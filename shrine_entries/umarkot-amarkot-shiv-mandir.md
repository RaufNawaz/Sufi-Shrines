## Overview

The Shiv Mandir at *Umarkot* (historically *Amarkot*), in the desert district of the same name in eastern Sindh, is among the oldest and most venerated Hindu temples in Pakistan, and the setting each year of one of the largest Hindu festivals in the country. Dedicated to *Shiva* and enshrining a much-revered *lingam*, it draws tens of thousands of pilgrims from across Sindh to the annual celebration of *Maha Shivaratri*, and it stands as a living centre of the Hindu devotional life that has flourished in this corner of the Thar borderlands for centuries.

## Antiquity and Sacred Legend

Tradition ascribes to the temple a very great age — some accounts speak of two thousand years and more — though such claims belong to legend rather than to documented history and are best treated with due caution. The origin story most often told is a pastoral one: a herdsman, it is said, noticed that one of his cows would slip away each day to a particular spot and there let down her milk of her own accord upon a stone. When the people investigated, they found the stone to be a *Shiva lingam*, self-manifested from the earth, and upon that spot the temple was raised. A further tradition holds that the lingam continued to grow over time, so that devotees marked its height to observe the increase — again a matter of devotional legend rather than record.

## The Temple and Interfaith Custom

The structure standing today is said to have been built roughly a century ago, and — in a detail often recounted as an emblem of Sindh's tradition of religious coexistence — the building is attributed to the piety and patronage of a local Muslim. Such interweaving of communities is characteristic of the region: Umarkot itself is celebrated in history as the birthplace, in 1542, of the Mughal emperor *Akbar*, born there while his fugitive father Humayun sheltered under the protection of the local Hindu Rana — a memory that underlines the district's long entanglement of faiths and rulers.

## Maha Shivaratri at Umarkot

The temple comes most fully to life at *Maha Shivaratri*, the great night of Shiva, when a festival lasting some three days transforms the town. Pilgrims from the length of Sindh, from the lower delta to the upper districts, converge upon the shrine, many staying for the duration of the celebrations; estimates of attendance run into the hundreds of thousands, making it one of the very largest religious gatherings of Pakistan's Hindu community. The festival is at once an act of collective worship and a great affirmation of the community's identity and continuity in the land of its ancestors.

## Legacy

The Umarkot Shiv Mandir is far more than an ancient building; it is a beating heart of Hindu devotion in Pakistan, where legend, landscape, and living faith meet. Its origin-tale of the milk-giving cow and the self-born lingam, its association with the tolerant history of the Sindhi borderland, and above all the vast annual pilgrimage that it sustains, together make it one of the most significant Hindu sacred sites in the country and a proud symbol of the endurance of Sindh's Hindu heritage.

  - From Edwin Oliver James, *The Tree of Life: An Archaeological Study*: the peepal, "as the abode of the Hindu Triad, Brahma, Vishnu and Shiva, it became peculiarly sacred equated with the highest concept of deity in Hinduism and so deeply laid in the ancient Indus valley civilization" (p. 9, ch. 3, folio not stated).

- **Suba Thatta described** (c. 042; book's words: "صوبه تهته" (p. 530), "درین ولایت" (p. 530), "درین صوبه" (p. 529); period: the author's time, c. 1107 AH, a Mughal suba; the capitals "در پاستان زمان"):
  - Capitals: "برهمن اباد نام شهری بزرگ تختگاه بود و قلعه ان هزار و چهار صد برج داشت هر یک بفاصله یک طناب"; after it "ادپور پائے تخت گردید", with many traces of towers and walls remaining; "پس ازان دیول اکنون تهته و دیبل دار الحکومت است" (p. 530).
  - Land revenue: "درین ولایت سوم بخش از کشاورز به گیرند" (p. 530).
  - Resources: "کان نمک و اهن فراوان"; at "در[OCR?] شش کروهے" a "کان سنگ زرد", cut to size for building (p. 530).
  - Boats: "اکثر مدار بر کشتی است", "از خورد و بزرگ چهل هزار خواهد بود" (p. 530).
  - Game and food: "گورخر", hare, "کوته پاچه", pig and fish; the diet rests "بر برنج و جغرات و ماهی" (p. 530). Dried fish ("قاق") is shipped to the ports, and fish oil ("روغن[OCR?]") is used on boats; a fish called "پلوه[OCR?]" comes from the salt sea into the Sind, is netted, and is found in no other country (p. 530). Curds keep four months; flowers and many fruits, "خاصه انبه", and small melons in the desert (p. 530).
  - The liver-eaters: "جگر خواره این دیار مشهورتر فی الاکناف است"; they take livers, especially children's, by spell, especially at mealtimes; "بعضی گویند" a trance comes on them; they share the liver with fellow-practitioners and the victim dies (p. 530). They make others like themselves by feeding them a piece and teaching the spell; they ride the hyena ("کفتار") and bring news from afar (p. 530). When one is caught the experts split the calf and take out "دانه بسان انار دانه", which is fed to the victim, who recovers (pp. 529–530). A liver-eater will not sink with a millstone tied to the neck nor burn in fire; to cure one, the experts brand both temples, fill the eyes with salt, hang the person in a house for forty days, give saltless food and recite spells (p. 529). Most are women, some men; "در هر بلاد می باشند و اما در بلاد تهته بیشتر باشند" (p. 529).
  - Peoples: north of the line from Multan and Uch to Thatta and Kach Makran, hard-rock hills, home of Baluch and "بعضے افغان"; to the south, from Uch to Gujarat, sand hills and the "بهتی", whose chief's seat is Jaisalmer, and "دیگر اقوام راجپوت[OCR?]"; from Bhakkar to Nasirpur and Amarkot, "سبوده[OCR?]" and "جاریجه" (p. 529).
  - River: "بزرگ ترین دریائے سند است"; merchants from Multan and Bhakkar ship goods by it to Thatta, and travellers and even "عساکر گران" go by river; large armies rarely go by land, because of the jungle and the lack of water (p. 529).
  - Extent: "طول این صوبه از بهکر تا کچ مکران دو صد و پنجاه کروه و عرض از قصبه بدین تا بندر لاهری صد کروه" (p. 529). Bounds: east "گجرات احمداباد", west "کچ مکران", north "بهکر", south "دریائے شور" (p. 529).
  - Administration and revenue: "سرکار تهته و سیوستان و نصیر پور و امرکوت چهار سرکار مشتمل بر پنجاه و هفت محال و پنج بندر و ده[OCR?] کرور[OCR?] و چهل و نه لک و هفتاد هزار دام داخل این صوبه است" (pp. 528–529).
  - Pass 1's Doubts: the book places Hinglaj in its suba Thatta section, but the Hinglaj row's province tag is balochistan; this sindh bullet is not meant to reach it.
