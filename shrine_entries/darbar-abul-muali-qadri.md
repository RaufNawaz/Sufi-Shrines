## Current site description (as of 10 October 2026)

*The row's `Description` cell exactly as it appears in `src/data/shrines-fallback.json` on 10 October 2026, untouched. Not yet checked against sources.*

Darbar Abul Muali Qadri is an active Muslim shrine in Lahore, built around the grave of Hazrat Syed Muhammad Khair ul Deen, widely known as Shah Abul Muali Qadri. The complex holds the tomb, a mosque, and a graveyard in which members of the saint's family are buried alongside ordinary and notable dead. It is looked after by the Government Auqaf Department together with the descendants of the shrine's hereditary custodians (*gaddī nashīn* in the surveyor's wording — the *sajjāda nashīn* line). Construction work at the site is ongoing, and between 2,000 and 2,500 people come daily.

## The site and its history
The account given to the surveyor states that in 980 Hijri, when Shah Abul Muali migrated from Depalpur, Okara, to Lahore, he settled at this spot; that during his lifetime the place became a centre for receiving his spiritual blessings; and that he had it constructed and repaired himself. Devotees relate that he was buried here after his passing, and that his tomb was raised at the site of his own seminary, near Mochi Darwaza, where he had earlier built a mosque and taught.

The survey traces custodianship from the saint to his sons and then to the hereditary custodians, names the first custodian as Hazrat Haider Ali Shah — also buried in the shrine — and states that "the kings of every era took care of this place," responsibility passing in the present era to the Auqaf Department. The survey further records that the land for Dyal Singh College in Lahore, close to the shrine, was given by the saint's descendants so that people could acquire education.

## The saint, as the survey relates him
Tradition holds that Shah Abul Muali was born on 10 Zil Hajj 960 Hijri in Kirman, Iran, the son of Syed Rehmatullah Kirmani, of the Kirmani Sadaat. His *kunyat* was Abul Muali, the name by which he is known; the survey also gives his title as Asadullah and describes him as Hassani Hussaini and of the Ahl al-Bayt. His father is described as a scholar and a poet in Urdu and Persian, who migrated from Iran and settled at Depalpur, Okara. Two of his father's brothers are named: Sheikh Dawood Bandgi Kirmani, whose shrine the survey places at Shergarh, and Syed Jalal ud Deen, whose shrine it places in Sindh.

The account states that after his father's death he was taken under the care of his uncle Dawood Bandgi, who was also his father-in-law; that he studied with him until the age of thirty; and that he migrated to Lahore at his uncle's command, establishing there a mosque and seminary near Mochi Darwaza where he led congregational prayers and taught. He is credited with having wells, mosques and *madrasas* built along his route, with writing books in Urdu and Persian, and with composing poetry, though the account is careful to say his focus was preaching. The survey states that he taught and memorised the Quran, possessed both exoteric and esoteric knowledge, and describes him as a dervish and a perfect scholar. Devotees relate that millions pledged allegiance to him, that kings came to him with requests, that he never distinguished between rich and poor, and that he kept many *chillas* (periods of seclusion) and nights of *dhikr* (remembrance of God). The account states that because of him millions of people entered the fold of Islam. He is said to have loved Sheikh Abdul Qadir Jilani and Mian Mir deeply. It states that he died on 16 Rabi ul Awal 1024 Hijri, during the reign of Shah Jahan, at the age of sixty-five.

Two marriages are recorded: the first to a daughter of Dawood Bandgi Kirmani, who died some time after the marriage, and the second to a daughter of Emperor Shah Jahangir, whose considerable wealth and lands the couple are said to have endowed entirely for charitable purposes. The survey gives him eight sons, buried beside him, one of whom it says was martyred fighting in a war against the Sikhs.

## Stories and *karāmāt*
The survey narrates several incidents, which it presents as widely known among devotees.

**The *majzūb* at the roadside.** The account relates that, while still searching for a spiritual master, the saint set out in 980 for the house of a companion, Sheikh Hamid Karishmi, in Delhi. On the way he spoke with a *majzūb* (a spiritually absorbed person) sitting by the road, who told him that the one he was wandering in search of was in his own home, and named his uncle Dawood Bandgi Kirmani. Astonished, he returned to his uncle, who answered with a verse to the effect of: "Wander and see the whole world, blister your feet, if you find anyone like us, then say." His request for allegiance was accepted, and after some days his uncle sent him back to Lahore with the instruction to spend his life in the path of Allah.

**Dreams of Sheikh Abdul Qadir Jilani.** It is said that whoever pledged allegiance at his hands would see Sheikh Abdul Qadir Jilani in a dream that very night, and that this was due to his *karāmāt* (miraculous graces).

**The rosary.** The survey cites the Mughal emperor Dara Shikoh — so styled in the account — as writing that he once accompanied Shah Abul Muali to the court of his teacher Sheikh Imam Niyamat Ali, where someone presented the saint with a rosary. Dara Shikoh relates that he silently wished it might be given to him; on their return to the seminary the saint handed him that same rosary, telling him to recite Darood on it daily so that the giver, the recipient and the saint would all share in the reward. He is said to have been amazed that his unspoken thought was known.

**The white turban.** In a second incident attributed to Dara Shikoh, he had never disclosed his love for Sheikh Abdul Qadir Jilani. One night, the account states, the Sheikh came to him in a dream and gave him a white turban. In the morning, presenting himself before Shah Abul Muali, he was given a white turban by the saint, who said that Sheikh Abdul Qadir Jilani had asked that it be given to him.

**The rain at the Eidgah.** The survey relates that during a drought at Lahore, when Hakim Afzal Agha was ruling, the Hakim gathered a very large crowd at the Eidgah outside the city for congregational prayer for rain, and came to invite the saint. Shah Abul Muali excused himself, saying he could not walk. The Hakim pressed him, saying that if the saint would not come he too would remain sitting there. At last the saint told him to go, and that it would certainly rain after two days. No rain fell for two days; on the third it rained heavily. When the Hakim returned to ask how he had known, the saint is said to have answered that had it rained on the day of the gathering, the arrogant among the worshippers might have taken it for their own wisdom and it would later have become a cause of sin — so Allah delayed it.

## Devotional life
There is a press of visitors daily. The custodians' descendants live at the shrine, and people travel from far away to receive *dum* (spiritual healing by breath and recitation) from them. The mosque inside the shrine holds the five daily prayers. A gathering is held daily at which *naʿt* (devotional poetry in praise of the Prophet) is recited, and *langar* (free food distribution) is continuous. Every Thursday, drums are beaten, gatherings are held, lamps are lit, and *niyāz* (consecrated offerings of food) is distributed. Devotees also recite Fateha at the graves.

Visitors come to pay their respects and to pray to Allah through the saint's intercession, and the survey reports that they say their prayers are answered there: that whatever the worry or sorrow they find peace only from here, because he had knowledge of the unseen, and that he was a *Wali Allah* (friend of God) from whose door Allah never lets anyone go empty-handed.

## The annual *ʿurs*
The *ʿurs* — the annual commemoration of the saint's death — is held on 15, 16 and 17 Rabi ul Awal. Its programme includes Chadar Poshi (the laying of the shroud), Mela Chiraghan (the festival of lights), a *mehfil* of *naʿt* shareef, dhol and dhamal, *qawwālī*, Ghusal (the ritual washing), and *langar*. The survey gives attendance as 200,000 to 250,000 people.

## Documentation
The surveyor uploaded nine photographs and one audio or video recording; no book or pamphlet was supplied. The survey states that many books have been written on the saint's life and *karāmāt*, that these are easily available, and that his own books are held in the libraries of well-known universities.

## A Second, Independent Source
A general compendium of Pakistani Sufi saints, consulted independently of the field survey, gives this figure's real name as Shah Khairuddin Muhammad — consistent with the survey's own "Hazrat Syed Muhammad Khair ul Deen" — with Abul Ma'ali recorded as his *kunyat* (byname), matching the survey exactly. It adds that he served as spiritual guide (*murshid*) to a companion of the Mughal prince Dara Shikoh — a somewhat different framing than the field survey's own account, which has Dara Shikoh himself directly receiving a rosary and a turban from the saint's hand. Both descriptions agree the two were connected; which is the more precise account is not resolvable from these two sources alone.

**The book's scope and the survey of Lahore**
- The commissioner "مسٹر ولیم کولڈسٹریم [OCR?]" ordered the author "کہ حالاتِ عمارات و مزارات و مقابر و مساجد نواحِ لاہور مفصل تحریر کروں" — to write a detailed account of the buildings, mazars, tombs and mosques of Lahore's environs. The author calls the task very hard "کیونکہ صدہا سال کے حالات بہم پہونچانے [OCR?] [illegible] مشکل", because the history of hundreds of years had to be gathered (p. 28). Elsewhere he says Lala Amin Chand's work on Lahore's buildings and people was cut short when "مسٹر[OCR?] ولیم[OCR?] گولڈ اسٹریم[OCR?]" left for Shahjahanabad (p. 734).
- The book "مشعرِ احوالِ مقابرِ بزرگانِ اہلِ اسلام وغیرہ عمارات و [illegible] و مراسمِ قدیمہ [illegible] لاہور" (reports on the tombs of Muslim elders, other buildings and the old customs of Lahore), "لیکن فی الاصل تواریخِ [OCR?] اولیا [illegible] ہے", but at root it is a history of saints (p. 29).
- Mufti Ghulam Sarwar had long sought "حالاتِ حضرات نواحیِ لاہور" and could not get them, "کیونکہ حضرات سجادہ نشیناں [illegible] اطلاعِ حال اپنا [illegible]" (p. 31).
- The survey proper opens under the heading "آغاز احوال قبور و مزارات و مقبرہ ہائے بزرگان لاہور اول[OCR?]" (p. 45).
- A forward reference: "مکانات اہل[OCR?] دہلی[OCR?] شہر لاہور میں تحریر یہ ہوگا انشاء اللہ تعالی" (the houses of the people[OCR?] of Delhi[OCR?] in the city of Lahore will be written up, God willing) (p. 292).
- The author cites Lala Amin Chand's *Safarnama* ("مطبوعہ کوہ نور"), its account of the city ("حال شہر لاہور کے بیان میں بہ صفحہ با دن[OCR?]"), and disputes it (p. 733).

**Pre-Mughal rulers and invasions**
- When Sabuktigin first invaded Hind, in "سن [illegible] سو ستہتر [OCR?] ہجری", "لاہور مین راجہ جی پال حاکم تہا" (p. 804). Sabuktigin, having won the Afghans over, "لاہور و ملتان کو اپنے قبضہ مین کر لیا" (p. 804).
- Jaipal, defeated, asked that a trusted man of Sabuktigin's come with him to Lahore to collect the tribute; he came with "نواب خیر اللہ خان [OCR?]". At his court "بطرف چپ چہتری اور بطرف راست برہمن کہتری"; the Chhatris urged him to keep his word, the Brahmins not to pay; Jaipal broke the agreement and imprisoned the envoy. Sabuktigin "لاہور پر چڑہ آیا"; the rajas of Delhi, Ajmer and Kalinjar[OCR?] helped Jaipal, but the Hindus were defeated (p. 804).
- In Sultan Mahmud's time "خاندان توار [OCR?] سی ایک راجہ اوسا [OCR?] لاہور و قنوج کا حاکم تہا" (p. 804).
- Mas'ud, facing the Turkmans (Tughril Beg), was "لاچار لاہور کے طرف روانہ ہوا"; his army looted the treasury at the Indus ("دریا سندہ") and put the blinded Muhammad back on the throne (p. 807).
- In Maudud's time the Hindu rajas besieged Lahore, "جو دار الخلافت اہل اسلام کا تہا", for "سات [OCR?] مہینہ" until the besieged drove them off (p. 807).
- "خسرو شاہ ولد بہرام [illegible] لاہور مین آکر بعالم مجبوری دار السلطنت بنایا"; he ruled "سات [OCR?] برس تک لاہور مین"; his son Khusrau Malik succeeded. The Ghurids took "یہہ ملک" from Khusrau "بہزار مکر و فریب", and "پہر یہان بہی سلطنت غوریان کی ہوئی" (pp. 808–809).
- From "کتاب خلاصۃ التواریخ": after "جہاں [illegible]", Lahore lay deserted ("ویران پڑا رہا") for a long time, until an amir of "سلطان سلوک[OCR?]" settled it again; "کامراں" is named in the damaged continuation (p. 430). Settlement then grew (p. 431).

**Mughal Lahore**
- When Humayun returns to Hindustan, "حاکم لاہور تاتار خان" comes out against him; Bairam Khan overcomes him off guard (p. 823). After Hemu takes Delhi, "اکبر نے لاہور مین کچہہ جمعیت بہم پہونچا کی دہلی پر یورش کی" (p. 824).
- Akbar's second enthronement after Humayun's death: "دوبارہ شہنشاہ حسن تخت نشینی[OCR?] کلاہور میں مسعقد ہوا" (garbled) (p. 648).
- "اکبر بادشاہ[OCR?]" turned to Lahore's settlement from the start of his reign, built a city and a "دولت خانہ", raised large buildings and stayed for a time (p. 431). Lahore is called the capital in Akbar's time: Malik Ali was kotwal in "عہد اکبر بادشاہ" and held "تمام کوتوالی ... دارالسلطنتہ لاہور" (p. 194; surrounding words heavily flagged: "تمر[OCR?] قرویہ[OCR?] حتماً[OCR?] طلوکہ[OCR?] ... مقلم[OCR?]"); also "ملک علی کوتوال لاہور[OCR?]" (p. 193). Akbar is said to have been in Lahore when the Gilani ancestor came: "ان ایام میں اکبر بادشاہ … لاہو یہ رہا[OCR?]" (heavily damaged) (p. 525).
- Khwaja Ayaz (Nur Jahan's father), his wife and the infant travelled with a caravan "اونکی ساتہہ وہ لاہور تک پہنچی"; there he took service and rose to "میر منشی" under Akbar (p. 827).
- Coin legends of "نور الدین جہانگیر ابن اکبر بادشاہ": the transcriber's summary of four illegible lines names mints including "لاہور [OCR?]" and "احمد آباد [OCR?]" (p. 828).
- After Mahabat Khan's victory, "نورجہان لاہور کیطرف بہاگ گئی" (p. 829). Jahangir, ill on his way back from Kashmir, came to Lahore and died there ("جب وہان سے واپس ہوکر لاہور مین آئے تو ... خیمہ زن ملک عدم ہوگئے"); date as printed "تاریخ [illegible] ماہ نومبر سنہ ۱۶۲[illegible]ء [OCR?] مطابق سنہ [illegible]۳۶ [OCR?] ہجری" (p. 830). He was buried "شہر [OCR?] لاہور کے شمال رویہ لب دریای راوی موضع شاہدرہ مین" (p. 830); the tomb is "دریای راوی کی کنارے پر شہر لاہور سے بطرف شمال بفاصلہ چار میل" (p. 855), and from its minarets "تمام شہر لاہور معہ مسجد بادشاہی و وزیر خان وغیرہ عمارات نظر آتی ہین" (p. 860).
- "شاہجہاں بادشاہ لاہور میں ٹھہرے[OCR?]", in the anecdote of Haji Nur (p. 200). Shah Jahan is said to have had "بہت سی عمارات واقع لاہور" built, and Nawab Wazir Khan was appointed "ماظم[OCR?] لاہور" (p. 738); Wazir Khan is "ناظم لاہور" again in "جب نواب صاحب ناظم لاہور رستے[OCR?]" (p. 749). "لاہور مین شالامار باغ" is Shah Jahan's memorial: "چشم روزگار نے کوئی ایسا باغ کم دیکھا ہوگا" (p. 833).
- After Aurangzeb's accession, "دارا شکوہ نے لاہور کو مراجعت کی", raised a larger army there, then crossed the "دریای سندہ" (p. 836). Dara's betrayer, after handing him over in Delhi, "لاہور کیطرف چلا گیا" and was killed on the road (p. 838).
- At the start of Alamgir's reign Nawab Khalil Allah Khan held "دارہ[OCR?] لاہور" and, with a mansab of "شش ہزاری", was appointed "صوبہ دار لاہور" (p. 497).
- The great buildings stood until "عہد عالمگیر"; great amirs built "بڑی بڑی عمارات سنگین"; Abu al-Hasan son of Asaf Khan's buildings were among Lahore's chief ornaments (p. 431).
- In the succession war after Aurangzeb, Muhammad Mu'izz al-Din, governor of Multan, came with a large army and stores "بمقام لاہور" to his father Sultan Mu'azzam Bahadur Shah, and both went on to Akbarabad (p. 845). "شاہ عالم شہر لاہور مین سنہ ۱۷۱۲ء [OCR?] مین فوت ہوا" (p. 846).

**Later Mughal governors (nazims and subadars)**
- Abdus Samad Khan was given the title "سیف الدولہ دلیر جنگ بہادر" and appointed to Lahore by "شاہ عالم" (heavily flagged); he arrived when his son Zakariya Khan was "تیرہ سال کا" (p. 714). Zakariya Khan was appointed "ناظم لاہور" through the Sayyid brothers' support, Abdus Samad Khan moving to "ناظم ملتان"; later he continues as "ناظم لاہور" under Muhammad Shah, "شوق عمارت انکو معہ[OCR?] کمال تہا" (p. 716).

- **Significance.** At the ruin of the sultanate the inhabitants scattered and "شاہ ابو المعالی کی اولاد شہر کے حصار کے اندر جا رہی اور محلہ ویران ہو گیا" — his **offspring moved inside the city's hisar** and the mohalla was left desolate (p. 87, folio 90). The rauza is the only structure in that earlier range that the book describes as a going concern in the author's present with both a mela and an urs (p. 87, folio 90). The tomb is also a bearing for its quarter: the tomb of Shah Kanhh[OCR?] is "شاہ ابو المعالی کے مقبرے کے شمال کی سمت موچی دروازے کے باہر کے علاقے میں" (p. 304, folio 307).

### Doubts the notes raise on this row

35. **darbar-abul-muali-qadri and abul-faiz-qalander-ali-suharwardi — ابوالخیر refused.** The only saint
    in that chunk is **ابوالخیر** at the Garhi Shahu compound (p. 365, folio 372). The index carries two
    superficially similar Lahore rows, "but the second element of the name differs in both cases, and the
    chunk gives no death date, silsila or street to test against"; **no id assigned**, and a human should
    decide whether ابوالخیر warrants a new row. The book gives **no year for Abul Khair's death**, only the
    age of "ایکسو پانچ برس", 105 years, and the reign of Muhammad Shah with the nizamat of "خان بہادر[OCR?]"
    printed twice over (p. 363, folio 370).
