## Current site description (as of 10 October 2026)

*The row's `Description` cell exactly as it appears in `src/data/shrines-fallback.json` on 10 October 2026, untouched. Not yet checked against sources.*

The shrine of Mian Muhammad Umar Baba lies at Chamkani, a village on the eastern outskirts of Peshawar about seven kilometres from the old city. Mian Umar (traditionally 1671-1776) was a Sufi of the Naqshbandi order — a scholar, poet, social reformer, and historian who is counted, together with Akhund Darweza Baba and the warrior-poet Khushal Khan Khattak, among the great literary and spiritual figures of the Mughal-era Pashtun frontier. Said to have been born near Lahore and to have lived to a great age, he attracted disciples from every rank of society; tradition holds that even Ahmad Shah Abdali (Durrani), the founder of the Afghan kingdom, visited him and became his devotee. Alam Faqri's *Tazkirah Awliya-e-Pakistan* gives a specific account of his death — in the month of Rajab, 1119 AH (approximately 1707-1708 CE) — and records his burial at the village of Chamkani, three miles from Peshawar, consistent with the shrine's location as given above; that date, however, falls roughly seven decades earlier than the 1776 death year given elsewhere in this entry, and the discrepancy is left unresolved here, with both figures reported rather than reconciled. His tomb at Chamkani grew into a shrine and khanqah that remains a place of resort for the surrounding population, drawing its largest crowds at the annual urs, held over the first Wednesday and Thursday of the Islamic month of Rajab, when devotees gather for sermons, collective dhikr, and na'at, and the langar (free kitchen) is served. Like a number of Sufi shrines in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, the site was targeted in militant attacks during the years of insurgency in the region, but it endures as an active centre of devotion. It complements the nearby shrine of the saint's contemporary Akhund Darweza Baba, also recorded in this dataset.

- indexed: "پیشاور [OCR?]" — printed locators "۸۵ - ۸۶ - ۸۷ - ۱۶۵ - ۵۱۲" (p. 39). *basis: index headword (brief item 1)*
- The name: "در پشاور که بکتب سلف فرشاور (۴) و پرشاور و بگرام نوشته اند" (p. 424); the editor's footnote (۴) to "فرشاور" is illegible (see Doubts). "پشاور شهر قدیم است که در کتب متقدمین پرشاور و فرشاور نیز نویسند" (p. 503).
- Mahmud's first campaign, "در سنه ۳۹۱": "راجه جیپال نیز بعزم مقابله رهگرا گشته در پشاور ... رسیده معرکه کارزار بیاراست" (p. 424). "پنج هزار کفار دران کارزار علف تیغ خونخوار نمود"; "راجه جیپال با پانزده نفر از پسر و برادر و خویش اسیر گشته بقتل رسید", printed as quoted (p. 424); see Doubts.
- "از بلده پشاور سه راه بکابل میرود" (pp. 502–501): the Nikshab road ("راه نیکشاب"), with many hard defiles and a long distance, armies passing with difficulty (p. 502); the "کهرپه[OCR?]" road, joining the highway at Jalalabad, with narrow defiles, hard passes, little water and Afghan raids ("دست بردے[OCR?] افغانان") (p. 502); "راه علی مسجد است و خیبر", running 18 kos ("هژده کروه") from the spring of Jamshid, "known as Jamrud", to "دهیکه[OCR?] کنار نیلاب", the Khyber hard going for two kos, yet easier than the others and used by armies and caravans; then 30 kos without hills from "دکه[OCR?]" to "ننله[OCR?]", and 40 kos with passes not very hard from "ننله[OCR?]" to Kabul (p. 501).
- "تومان بنگرام[OCR?] ان مشهور به پشاور جانب هندوستان است" (p. 504): its grapes, peaches and melons like Turan's, its heat, cold, seasons and rain like Hindustan's; its rice "سکهد اس[OCR?]" better than Bengal's; crops and grain plentiful (p. 504). "تمام[OCR?] این تومان بنگاه افغانان مهمند و غیر ذلک است", who "طریق مال گذاری و رعیت گیری ... دارند" (pp. 504–503).
- Dara sent khilats to "لشکرهائے پیشاور و کابل را که مهابت خان ایالت آنجا داشت" (p. 77). *basis: office mention*
- A bare march: after laying out the fort of Attock the court "متوجه پیشاور شدند", and "ازین منزل" a farman of counsel was issued to Hakim Mirza (p. 201). *basis: transit mention*
