## Overview

On the low, wind-scoured island of *Manora*, guarding the mouth of Karachi harbour, stands the Shri Varun Dev Mandir — a temple dedicated to *Varuna*, the ancient Vedic lord of the waters and the sea. It is believed to be the only temple to Varuna in Pakistan, and its weathered stonework, set almost within reach of the waves, makes it one of the most atmospheric and least-known of the country's Hindu shrines. For the Hindus of Sindh, and especially for seafaring and mercantile communities, it has long been a place to honour the deity who rules the ocean on which their fortunes depended.

## Varuna, Lord of the Waters

In the oldest stratum of Hindu tradition, Varuna is among the most exalted of the gods — guardian of cosmic order (*rita*), sovereign of the celestial ocean, and in later devotion the presiding deity of the seas, rivers, and rain. In the religious life of coastal and riverine Sindh, reverence for the water-god blended with the veneration of *Jhulelal*, the beloved Sindhi Hindu deity likewise associated with the waters of the Indus, so that the temple at Manora belongs to a distinctly Sindhi tradition of devotion to the divine in its aquatic form.

## History and Antiquity

The precise age of the temple is uncertain, and tradition claims for it a very great antiquity, with some accounts holding the site sacred for many centuries. According to one local tradition, Manora Island was purchased in earlier times by a wealthy sailor — remembered as *Bhojomal Nenshi Bhatia* — whose family is said to have commissioned a temple upon it. The structure that survives today is generally dated to the early twentieth century, with records pointing to construction or major rebuilding around 1917–18 and further embellishment, including its tilework, in the years around 1937–40. It is prudent to treat the temple's more ancient origins as traditional rather than documented, while its present fabric belongs to the last century or so.

## Decline and Preservation

For many decades the temple stood neglected and exposed to the elements. The salt-laden sea winds of Manora have eaten into its masonry and eroded the fine carvings that once adorned its walls, and by the early twenty-first century it had fallen into a dilapidated condition. Its plight has drawn the attention of heritage advocates and of Hindu community organisations in Pakistan, and there have been calls and efforts to conserve and restore the shrine as an important relic of Sindh's religious and architectural heritage.

## Legacy

The Shri Varun Dev Mandir is a rare and poignant survival — a temple to the god of the ocean standing on an island at the edge of the sea, its stones slowly yielding to the salt air that is itself the element of the deity it honours. As the only known temple of Varuna in Pakistan, and as a monument to the maritime and mercantile Hindu communities of old Karachi and Sindh, it holds a singular place among the sacred sites of the country, and its preservation is a matter of concern to all who value that shared heritage.

- **Suba Thatta described** (c. 042; book's words: "صوبه تهته" (p. 530), "درین ولایت" (p. 530), "درین صوبه" (p. 529); period: the author's time, c. 1107 AH, a Mughal suba; the capitals "در پاستان زمان"):
  - Capitals: "برهمن اباد نام شهری بزرگ تختگاه بود و قلعه ان هزار و چهار صد برج داشت هر یک بفاصله یک طناب"; after it "ادپور پائے تخت گردید", with many traces of towers and walls remaining; "پس ازان دیول اکنون تهته و دیبل دار الحکومت است" (p. 530).
  - Land revenue: "درین ولایت سوم بخش از کشاورز به گیرند" (p. 530).
  - Resources: "کان نمک و اهن فراوان"; at "در[OCR?] شش کروهے" a "کان سنگ زرد", cut to size for building (p. 530).
  - Boats: "اکثر مدار بر کشتی است", "از خورد و بزرگ چهل هزار خواهد بود" (p. 530).
  - Game and food: "گورخر", hare, "کوته پاچه", pig and fish; the diet rests "بر برنج و جغرات و ماهی" (p. 530). Dried fish ("قاق") is shipped to the ports, and fish oil ("روغن[OCR?]") is used on boats; a fish called "پلوه[OCR?]" comes from the salt sea into the Sind, is netted, and is found in no other country (p. 530). Curds keep four months; flowers and many fruits, "خاصه انبه", and small melons in the desert (p. 530).
  - The liver-eaters: "جگر خواره این دیار مشهورتر فی الاکناف است"; they take livers, especially children's, by spell, especially at mealtimes; "بعضی گویند" a trance comes on them; they share the liver with fellow-practitioners and the victim dies (p. 530). They make others like themselves by feeding them a piece and teaching the spell; they ride the hyena ("کفتار") and bring news from afar (p. 530). When one is caught the experts split the calf and take out "دانه بسان انار دانه", which is fed to the victim, who recovers (pp. 529–530). A liver-eater will not sink with a millstone tied to the neck nor burn in fire; to cure one, the experts brand both temples, fill the eyes with salt, hang the person in a house for forty days, give saltless food and recite spells (p. 529). Most are women, some men; "در هر بلاد می باشند و اما در بلاد تهته بیشتر باشند" (p. 529).
  - Peoples: north of the line from Multan and Uch to Thatta and Kach Makran, hard-rock hills, home of Baluch and "بعضے افغان"; to the south, from Uch to Gujarat, sand hills and the "بهتی", whose chief's seat is Jaisalmer, and "دیگر اقوام راجپوت[OCR?]"; from Bhakkar to Nasirpur and Amarkot, "سبوده[OCR?]" and "جاریجه" (p. 529).
  - River: "بزرگ ترین دریائے سند است"; merchants from Multan and Bhakkar ship goods by it to Thatta, and travellers and even "عساکر گران" go by river; large armies rarely go by land, because of the jungle and the lack of water (p. 529).
  - Extent: "طول این صوبه از بهکر تا کچ مکران دو صد و پنجاه کروه و عرض از قصبه بدین تا بندر لاهری صد کروه" (p. 529). Bounds: east "گجرات احمداباد", west "کچ مکران", north "بهکر", south "دریائے شور" (p. 529).
  - Administration and revenue: "سرکار تهته و سیوستان و نصیر پور و امرکوت چهار سرکار مشتمل بر پنجاه و هفت محال و پنج بندر و ده[OCR?] کرور[OCR?] و چهل و نه لک و هفتاد هزار دام داخل این صوبه است" (pp. 528–529).
  - Pass 1's Doubts: the book places Hinglaj in its suba Thatta section, but the Hinglaj row's province tag is balochistan; this sindh bullet is not meant to reach it.
