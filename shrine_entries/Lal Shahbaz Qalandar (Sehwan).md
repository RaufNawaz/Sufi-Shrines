## Overview

At Sehwan Sharif, in Sindh, stands the great shrine of Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar (c. 1177–1274) — perhaps the most ecstatically beloved saint in all of Pakistan, and the object of one of its largest and most exuberant pilgrimages. A wandering _qalandar_ — a mystic of the free, rapturous, rule-breaking kind — Lal Shahbaz drew Sindh into the world of Sufi devotion, and his memory is celebrated in the thunder of the _dhamal_, the trance-dance beaten out on the great drums of his shrine, and in the anthem _Dama Dam Mast Qalandar_, which has carried his name across the world. His golden-domed darbar is the heart of Sehwan, and its urs one of the defining festivals of the Sindhi year.

## The Life of the Saint

Sayyid Usman Marwandi — known to the world as Lal Shahbaz Qalandar — was born around 1177 at Marwand, in the Persian-speaking lands to the west (in the region of present-day Afghanistan/Iran), into a Sayyid family tracing descent from the Prophet through the Imams. Steeped from youth in learning and devotion, he took to the path of the wandering dervishes and travelled widely through the Muslim world — through Iraq, the Hejaz, and Central Asia — before coming to Sindh and settling at Sehwan, an ancient town with a deep pre-Islamic sacred history. There he lived as a _qalandar_, drawing crowds by his ecstatic sanctity, and there he died around 1274. He is called _Lal_ (“the Red”) for the crimson robe of his order, and _Shahbaz_ (“royal falcon”) for the soaring freedom of his spirit; and he is counted, in Sindhi and Punjabi tradition, among the “four friends” (_chaar yaar_) of the qalandari way, alongside such saints as Baba Farid and Bahauddin Zakariya, with whom legend links him in companionship.

## The Poetry and the Dhamal

Lal Shahbaz composed in Persian, and among the verses attributed to him is a ringing declaration of his identity as an intoxicated lover of God and devotee of the Prophet’s family:

حیدریم قلندرم مستم ٭ بندۂ مرتضیٰ علی ہستم

[“I am a Haidari, a qalandar, and intoxicated; I am the slave of Murtaza Ali.”]

But it is not chiefly his own verse that keeps his memory: it is the great devotional song made in his honour, the _Dama Dam Mast Qalandar_, whose refrain rings out wherever South Asian Sufi music is sung —

دَمادَم مست قلندر، علی دَم دَم دے اندر

[“At every breath, the intoxicated Qalandar! Ali is in every breath.”]

— and, above all, the _dhamal_: the ecstatic, whirling, drum-driven dance performed at his shrine, especially on Thursday evenings and at the urs, in which devotees lose themselves in rhythm and remembrance. To the beat of the huge _naqqara_ drums, before the blaze of lamps, the dhamal of Sehwan is one of the most electrifying expressions of devotion in the Muslim world.

## The Shrine and its Devotion

Lal Shahbaz’s shrine at Sehwan, with its gilded dome and mirrored interior, is among the most important pilgrimage sites in Pakistan, thronged year-round and overwhelmingly so at the annual urs, held in the Islamic month of Sha‘ban, when hundreds of thousands of devotees converge for days of dhamal, qawwali, and _langar_. The shrine has always been a strikingly inclusive place, drawing Muslims and Hindus alike and women in great numbers, and famed for a devotion that is joyous, bodily, and unrestrained. That openness has also made it a target: in February 2017 a suicide bombing at the shrine during the evening dhamal killed scores of worshippers — yet, in a gesture that became famous, the dhamal was danced again at the shrine the very next evening, in defiance of the terror. The shrine is administered by the Sindh Auqaf department and remains the beating heart of Sehwan.

## Legacy

Lal Shahbaz Qalandar embodies the ecstatic, boundary-crossing pole of South Asian Sufism — the qalandar who dances his way to God and gathers all comers, of every faith and station, into the rhythm of divine love. Through the song that bears his spirit and the dhamal of his shrine, his name is known far beyond Sindh, and his darbar at Sehwan remains one of the great living centres of devotion in Pakistan, a place where sanctity is celebrated not in silence but in drums, colour, and dance.
