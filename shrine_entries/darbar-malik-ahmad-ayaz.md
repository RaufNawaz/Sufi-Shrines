## Site and setting
Darbar Malik Ahmad Ayaz is an active Muslim shrine in the name of Malik Ahmad Ayaz, whom the survey identifies as "Slave of Mehmood Gaznavi - Governor Lahore"; the account given to the surveyor states that his tomb was built at this place after his death. The survey records the site's religion as Islam and its affiliation as "Ahl e Sunnat - Ghaznavi silsila" (*silsila*, a chain of spiritual transmission), reproduced as recorded.

The shrine stands in Shah Alam Market, which the survey describes as the largest market in Asia and reports as extremely crowded; the account given to the surveyor places the *mazār* (tomb, mausoleum) near Darbar Ali Hajveri Ganj Bakhsh. The site is small, and the surrounding congestion is reported to prevent many people from reaching it, gradually reducing both the figure's fame and the enthusiasm of visitors. Residents of this densely populated neighbourhood are largely unaware of the life of the figure buried there — a figure about whom, the survey states, Allama Iqbal, Kaniya Lal and other writers have written books.

## The account of Malik Ahmad Ayaz
The life narrated to the surveyor is long, and is given here in the account's own terms.

The survey relates that Malik Ayaz Ahmad was educated in Georgia in 993 AD, and that when "the infidels attacked your country" he was enslaved and taken for sale to the Baghdad slave market — the account notes that slave dealing was then the norm. Too weak to attract buyers, he was passed over until Mahmud Ghaznavi came through and asked his price. The minister questioned the purchase; the king answered that what he saw in the boy the minister could not see. The price asked was ten thousand dinars, greed having been raised in the seller's heart by the king's words; Mahmud paid it and enrolled him among his soldiers.

Tradition holds that the relation of master and slave turned into love, and that Ayaz spent his whole life in devotion to Mahmud. The account preserves the story of the melon: the king, feeding him a melon cut by his own hand, tasted it himself, found it bitter, and asked why Ayaz had said nothing; Ayaz replied that having had sweetness from his master's hand all his life, he had no cause to complain of one bitter piece.

The account states that Mahmud fought "almost 17 battles with Hindus", in which Ayaz "played the role of the mastermind", and that his faith was so firm that "he did not fall for the infidels". In one such story the king, attacking at the "Onkay temple", found the idol suspended in mid-air; Ayaz reasoned that a magnet in the temple held the clay figure up, and that demolishing the walls would bring it down of itself — which, the account says, is what happened, to the king's satisfaction.

The king's family and ministers, the survey relates, mocked the closeness between the two; when the minister tested them by summoning everyone at midnight to eat, Ayaz alone appeared fully prepared, the others arriving late or intoxicated and asleep. Mahmud made him his minister, and he continued always to regard himself as a slave, each night setting aside his costly clothes and taking out the old garments in which he had been brought for sale, so as to remember his past, sit alone and please his God. The last ten years of his life, tradition holds, were largely given to the remembrance of God (*dhikr*).

In Ayaz's last days, the account continues, Mahmud wished to hand his own place to him; Ayaz declined, asking that it go instead to one of his two sons, Saad, and the king relinquished his power. When Ayaz governed Lahore the city had been ruined by war, its systems of livelihood, food, trade and wages broken, and by his intelligence he gave it shape again and set it on its own feet. He fell suddenly ill and died; it is said he was poisoned out of resentment at the love shown him, and in some places it is said he died a natural death. The account gives his death as 8 August 1041, and states that his mausoleum was then built in Shah Alam Market.

## Memory, example and spiritual significance
Asked what should be remembered, the survey names the bond between Mahmud Ghaznavi and Malik Ahmad Ayaz — in its own words, "the love and romance" of king and slave — a slave sacrificing his life for his king, and loyalty so complete that he could answer for it before God on the Day of Judgement; poets and travellers of that era and of the present, it states, have written of these qualities. It adds that he is remembered as the person who gave Lahore a new shape, and known still because he "made a new map of Lahore".

On his teachings the survey is brief: he was generous, intelligent, loyal, hasty, daring, brave, kind and honest, a soldier and also a good and devoted person, who set the example of these qualities in his own life, and people were motivated by seeing it. The place is held spiritually important, the survey states, because he fought alongside the king, served the people, protected their lives and property and instilled the spirit of Islam in them; because after Mahmud Ghaznavi's death he left his residence; and because it was his habit to spend nights in the remembrance of God.

## Formation and later history of the site
The account given to the surveyor states that the tomb was built at this very place after the death of Malik Ayaz, that the Ghaznavi Sultanate built the Darbar, and that it was later maintained in good condition by "Raja Maharaja". The account then relates a period of loss: the water of the Darbar was polluted, and — in wording the transcriber flags as unclear — "the area of crop was stolen for the special place of your Darbar which was spoiled by Sikh Raja Ranjit Singh", after which only the present small plot remained. The survey elsewhere states that some of the site's space was lost during the Sikh era and some to the surrounding market.

## Observances, attendance and custodianship
A mosque named after the figure stands in the vicinity of the Darbar, where the five daily prayers are offered. Visitors come from distant places; the survey records that non-Muslims also visit and that some have made documentaries about the figure, his stories appearing in books. *Diyas* (oil lamps) and *prasād* (consecrated food) are offered daily, in the survey's terms.

The *ʿurs* (death anniversary observance) is held annually on 12, 13 and 14 Zil Hajj, its activities including the lighting of lamps, the changing of sheets, bathing, and the eating of food at the site. Attendance is given as 700 to 800 people daily and 40,000 to 50,000 at the annual *ʿurs*.

The shrine is under the supervision of the Department of Auqaf (Government Mehkama Auqaf), given consistently in the survey as both the present custodian and the body responsible for the site's current condition.
