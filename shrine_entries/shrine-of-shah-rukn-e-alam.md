## Overview

The shrine of Hazrat Shah Rukn-e-Alam (1251–1335) in Multan is at once the tomb of a great Suhrawardi saint and one of the supreme masterpieces of Islamic architecture in South Asia. Rukn-ud-Din Abul Fath — known by the honorific Rukn-e-Alam, “Pillar of the World” — was the grandson and spiritual heir of Bahauddin Zakariya, and under him the Suhrawardi order of Multan reached the height of its fame. His towering domed mausoleum, rising over the old citadel, is the emblem of Multan and among the most beautiful pre-Mughal buildings in the subcontinent.

## The Life of the Saint

Rukn-ud-Din was born in Multan in 1251, the grandson of Baha-ud-Din Zakariya and son of Sadr-ud-Din Arif, into the leading Suhrawardi family of the region. Raised in the great khanqah his grandfather had founded, he was schooled in the religious sciences and the discipline of the order, and in due course succeeded to its spiritual leadership, becoming the foremost shaykh of the Suhrawardis in India. He was held in the highest honour by the Delhi Sultanate — the Tughluq sultans in particular revered him — and he moved between Multan and the capital as a figure of immense spiritual and moral authority, interceding with rulers and guiding a vast body of disciples. He died in 1335 and was buried in Multan, in the mausoleum that is said to have been built originally for the Tughluq sultan Ghiyath-ud-Din and then given over to the saint.

## The Great Mausoleum

The tomb of Shah Rukn-e-Alam, raised in the early fourteenth century, is one of the glories of the age. Octagonal in plan, built of red brick banded with timber and dressed with carved and glazed blue-and-white tilework, it rises in three tapering tiers to a high white dome — a colossal structure that dominates the skyline of Multan from its perch on the mound of the old fort. It is counted among the earliest and finest monuments of the Tughluq style, a model that influenced tomb architecture across the region for generations, and it has been repaired and restored over the centuries, receiving in modern times awards for its conservation. For the people of Multan it is not only a shrine but the very symbol of their city.

## Devotion and Legacy

Shah Rukn-e-Alam’s shrine is a centre of continuous devotion, thronged by pilgrims who come to recite the Fatiha, seek intercession, and honour a saint whose blessing (_baraka_) is felt to guard the city. The daily prayers, qawwali, and the free kitchen are kept there, and the annual urs draws great crowds from across southern Punjab. As the heir of Bahauddin Zakariya and the most celebrated of the Multan Suhrawardis, Rukn-e-Alam carried the order to its zenith, and through his tomb — at once a place of pilgrimage and a wonder of architecture — the sanctity of the “City of Saints” finds its most visible expression.
