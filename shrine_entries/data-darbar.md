## Overview

Data Darbar, in the old heart of Lahore, is the greatest and most beloved shrine in Pakistan — the resting place of Hazrat Ali ibn Usman al-Hujwiri (c. 1009–1072/77), the eleventh-century saint and scholar known to all simply as _Data Ganj Bakhsh_, “the Master who bestows the treasure.” He was the first great Sufi to make his home in Lahore, the author of _Kashf al-Mahjub_, the earliest treatise on Sufism written in Persian, and, in the devotion of the Punjab, the very patron of the city, which is often called _Data ki Nagri_ — “the city of Data.” His tomb is more than a shrine: it is a vast devotional complex where prayer, learning, music, and above all the free kitchen (langar) come together in the daily life of hundreds of thousands, and it stands at the fountainhead of the whole tradition of Sufism in South Asia. To understand Data Darbar is to begin with the life of the saint whose blessing (baraka) is believed still to fill it.

## The Life of Ali Hujwiri

Ali Hujwiri was born around the turn of the eleventh century in Ghazni, in what is now Afghanistan, into a learned and pious family — his own epithets, _al-Jullabi_ and _al-Hujwiri_, recall the quarters of Ghazni from which he came. He grew into a scholar and mystic of the first rank, uniting deep intellectual study with an inward devotion, and in the manner of the seekers of his age he travelled widely across the Muslim world — through Iran, Iraq, Syria, and Central Asia — sitting at the feet of the masters of the day. His own spiritual guide was Abu’l-Fadl Muhammad al-Khuttali, who linked him to the “sober” Sufism of the school of Junayd of Baghdad, that tradition which insists that the mystical life be lived in full obedience to the Sharia and the example of the Prophet, rather than in intoxicated excess.

In time, in the middle of the eleventh century — by the usual reckoning around 1039 CE (431 AH), in the age of the Ghaznavids — Hujwiri came to Lahore, then a frontier city newly brought within the Muslim world, and there he settled. He built a small mosque, taught, preached, and served the poor, living in humility and voluntary poverty even though the doors of royal circles were open to him. His presence transformed the city: under his teaching Lahore became a centre of Sufi learning and devotion, and the seed he planted would flower into the whole later history of Islam in the north-western subcontinent. He died in Lahore between about 1072 and 1077 CE (465–469 AH), and his grave, from the first, drew those who had loved him.

## Kashf al-Mahjub

Hujwiri’s enduring gift to the world is his book. _Kashf al-Mahjub_ — “The Unveiling of the Veiled” — is the earliest formal treatise on Sufism composed in the Persian language, and it remains one of the classics of the tradition, studied wherever Sufism is studied. In it Hujwiri sets out the doctrines and disciplines of the mystical path: the nature of the soul and of knowledge, the meaning of sainthood, the practices and states of the Sufis, the sayings and lives of the earlier masters, and the etiquette of the seeker. Its teaching is of a piece with the man: a “sober” mysticism that weds spiritual devotion to ethical conduct, prayer, humility, and self-restraint, and holds that inward transformation matters more than any outward display of piety. At its heart lies the ideal of _faqr_ — spiritual poverty, the emptying of the self of ego, pride, and worldly craving — together with the remembrance of God (_dhikr_), sincere service to others, and the purification of the heart. Hujwiri urges the seeker to keep the company of a realised guide and warns sharply against the fraud who counterfeits sanctity or chases miracles, fame, and rank. Through this book, more even than through his tomb, the saint has gone on teaching for nine centuries.

## The Title "Data Ganj Bakhsh" and the Chishti Tribute

The name by which Hujwiri is universally known — _Data Ganj Bakhsh_ — was, by cherished tradition, bestowed upon him from beyond the grave by another of the giants of South Asian Sufism. It is told that Khwaja Moinuddin Chishti of Ajmer, the founder of the Chishti order in India, on his way into the subcontinent kept a forty-day retreat (_chilla_) at the foot of Hujwiri’s tomb in Lahore; and that on departing he uttered a Persian couplet in the saint’s praise that has become inseparable from his memory:

گنج بخشِ فیضِ عالم، مظہرِ نورِ خدا
ناقصاں را پیرِ کامل، کاملاں را رہنما

[“Bestower of the treasure, the bounty of the world, the manifestation of the light of God; for the imperfect, a perfect guide — and for the perfect, a guide.”]

From this verse comes the title _Ganj Bakhsh_ (“Bestower of Treasure”), and the affectionate _Data_ (“the Giver”) by which the Punjab has called him ever since; and because the Chishti master — himself the fountainhead of an entire order — bowed at his threshold, Hujwiri is remembered as the “teacher of teachers,” the saint from whom the later Sufis of India drew their light. Around his coming to Lahore, too, cluster the folk-miracles of a patron saint: the best-loved tells of a poor milk-seller whose meagre yield, at the saint’s blessing, miraculously increased — a small wonder that, in the telling, drew the people of the city to his teaching, and that still speaks of the compassion and generosity his shrine is felt to embody.

## The Making of the Shrine

From the saint’s grave grew, over a thousand years, the great complex of today. What began as a modest burial place beside his mosque became a site of pilgrimage almost at once; the first formal enclosure is said to have been raised by the Ghaznavid rulers, and through the centuries that followed the shrine was repaired, adorned, and enlarged by the powers of the age. Under the Delhi Sultanate and the Mughals its fame only grew — emperors such as Akbar and Shah Jahan honoured the saint and contributed to his darbar — and even in the Sikh period, under Maharaja Ranjit Singh, the shrine was maintained. By the nineteenth century it was already among the most revered landmarks of Lahore. After the creation of Pakistan in 1947 it passed into the care of the Punjab Auqaf Department, and in the 1980s it was transformed by a great expansion — wide marble courtyards, prayer halls, and facilities for the swelling multitudes of pilgrims — with renovation and enlargement continuing into the present century. The Data Darbar of today, with its gleaming courts and its towering mosque, is thus the work of many hands across nine hundred years, all gathered around a single grave.

## Devotion at the Darbar

Data Darbar is a place of ceaseless devotion, and the rhythms of worship there run day and night. The five daily prayers and the Friday congregation fill the grand mosque; devotees recite the Qur’an, offer personal and collective supplication (_dua_), lay cloths (_chadar_) and flowers upon the tomb, and sit in silent contemplation. Countless pilgrims come to fulfil vows or to seek blessing for health, livelihood, marriage, or success in examinations, in the belief that the saint is a mediator of God’s grace. At the heart of the shrine’s life is the _langar_ — the free kitchen — which feeds thousands every day, embodying the Sufi ethic that the love of God must be shown in generosity and service to His creatures; for the poor of Lahore, Data Darbar is not only a sanctuary of the spirit but a place of bread and belonging. On Thursday evenings the shrine fills for _qawwali_ and _sama_, the devotional music of the Sufis, and some among the devotees are moved to _dhamal_, the ecstatic rhythmic dance of trance and love. It is an inclusive space, open to men and women, rich and poor, scholar and labourer alike — one of the few places in the city where all classes meet on level ground. On an ordinary day the shrine draws some thirty to fifty thousand visitors, and on Thursdays and holy nights well over a hundred thousand.

## The Urs

The crowning event of the shrine’s year is the _urs_ of Data Ganj Bakhsh, held according to the Islamic lunar calendar in the month of Safar, over three days that fall by tradition around the 18th to the 20th. In the language of the Sufis the urs — literally “wedding” — celebrates not the saint’s death but his union with the Divine, and so it is kept in reverence and joy rather than mourning. During those days the darbar is transformed: the tomb and its courts are illuminated, special prayers and Qur’an recitations and _chadar_ ceremonies are held, and continuous qawwali and devotional assemblies fill the nights; the langar is expanded to feed tens of thousands, and the streets around the shrine take on the character of a great fair (_mela_), with stalls of sweets, books, and devotional wares. Pilgrims pour in not only from Lahore but from across Punjab and Pakistan, and the authorities commonly declare special arrangements to manage a gathering that can swell, over the three days, toward a million or more souls. Other seasons too raise the tide of devotion — the last ten nights of Ramadan, the Prophet’s birthday (Eid Milad-un-Nabi), and the other holy nights — but none approaches the scale and centrality of the urs, the defining festival of Data Darbar.

## The 2010 Bombing and the Shrine’s Endurance

The history of Data Darbar also carries the wounds of the present age. On the night of 1–2 July 2010, the shrine was struck by a twin suicide bombing that killed dozens of worshippers and injured many more — among the deadliest of a wave of militant attacks on the Sufi shrines of Pakistan in those years. It was an assault not merely on a building but on the whole plural, devotional, and tolerant world that shrines like Data Darbar embody, and that the attackers regarded as heresy. Yet the darbar endured. The wounded were carried out, the dead were mourned, and within days the pilgrims returned; the langar was cooked, the prayers resumed, and the qawwali was sung again. That refusal of ordinary devotees to let fear empty the shrine has become part of its meaning — a testimony to the resilience of a faith of love and service against the creed of violence.

## Custody and Administration

Unlike most of the shrines of the Punjab, Data Darbar has no hereditary keepers (_sajjada-nashins_). Since shortly after the creation of Pakistan it has been administered directly by the state, through the Punjab Auqaf and Religious Affairs Department, which manages the offerings (_nazrana_), the mosque, the security, and the vast operation of the langar. On the ground the daily life of the shrine depends on a small army of _khadims_ (attendants), imams, qawwals, cooks and langar workers, cleaners, and security staff, overseen by government administrators, and the complex is the subject of continual expansion and renovation projects. This state custodianship — exceptional among the great darbars — reflects both the enormous scale of Data Darbar and its standing as a national, and not merely a family, trust.

## The City of Data

More than any other figure, Ali Hujwiri made Lahore a holy city, and the whole later sacred landscape of the Punjab is bound, in devotion, to his threshold. He is remembered as the spiritual founder from whom the city takes one of its dearest names, _Data ki Nagri_, “the city of Data”; and tradition holds that no saint could settle in Lahore, or find his own path, without first paying homage at Hujwiri’s grave. The stories of the later masters bear this out. Khwaja Moinuddin Chishti, before he founded his order at Ajmer, kept his forty-day retreat at Data’s tomb and sealed his devotion in the famous couplet. The great Qadiri saint of the Mughal age, Mian Mir, is said to have kept a forty-day _chilla_ at Hujwiri’s khanqah, joining his own sanctity to that of the city’s patron. At the shrine of Bibi Pak Daman it is told that Hujwiri himself, on first coming to Lahore, would go each Thursday night to sit in reverence at the women’s graves. And even the modern poet-philosopher Muhammad Iqbal — who lies buried a little way off, by the Badshahi Mosque — is remembered in a beloved Lahori legend as having been visited one night by unseen guests, among them Data Ganj Bakhsh himself.

In this way Data Darbar is not merely one shrine among many but the source and centre of a whole network of holiness: the point from which, in the imagination of the Punjab, the sanctity of Lahore radiates outward to Bibi Pak Daman and Mian Mir, to Madho Lal Hussain at Baghbanpura, and beyond. To visit Data is, for many, to begin at the beginning — to salute the master before turning to his spiritual children. It is this that makes the darbar the true heart of the city: not only the busiest of its shrines and the largest of its kitchens, but the origin of its identity as a place under a saint’s protection, a city that belongs, in the end, to Data.

## Legacy

No shrine stands closer to the heart of Lahore than Data Darbar, and few figures loom larger in the spiritual history of the subcontinent than the saint it enshrines. Ali Hujwiri was the pioneer who carried the sober, learned Sufism of Baghdad into the Punjab; the author whose _Kashf al-Mahjub_ remains a lamp for seekers everywhere; and the “bestower of the treasure” at whose tomb the founder of the Chishti order himself sat in devotion. From his grave the whole later tradition of South Asian Sufism drew its beginning, and to it, still, the city brings its dead to be prayed over, its poor to be fed, its sick and sorrowful to seek relief, and its lovers of God to sing through the night. Bombed and rebuilt, endowed by emperors and thronged by the poorest, Data Darbar remains what it has been for nine centuries: the living heart of the city of Data, and one of the enduring wonders of the Muslim world.

### Doubts the notes raise on this row

**B1. `data-darbar` — the row's death year is the one this edition calls wrong.** The text gives
**465 AH** three times over in chronograms (p. 195, folio 193 as printed, chunk 018); the editor's
footnote opens "سال وفات میں اختلاف ہے۔ آقائے عبدالحی حبیبی نے کشف المحجوب کے اندرونی…" and breaks
off at "(باقی صفحہ آئندہ)" on that page, continuing into chunk 019, where the year is called
**"آپ کا سال وفات ۴۶۵ھ غلط ہے"** — wrong — and **490 AH [OCR?]** given as the point where the author
is last shown alive. The birth, **400 AH [OCR?]**, is called indeterminate. Both 490s and the 400 are
OCR-flagged. **The editor states no view of his own here, unlike his practice with Shah Daula and
Ayaz.** Actionable: the honest row states the traditional 465 AH, the edition's rejection of it, and
the 490 AH terminus, all three marked unresolved (pp. 195–197, folios 183 as printed / 184–185;
chunks 018, 019 — raised by two workers, the second having read the continuation the first could not).

**C32. `data-darbar`'s two presents must not be collapsed.** "بارہ برس" and "۸۲۷ برس" are the
**author's**, not the editor's, "and this is a 1974 edition of a nineteenth-century text. The dome's
date, the Auqaf note and the weekly vigil therefore belong to **two different presents**: the vigil
and the dome to the author's time, the mosque's demolition and the Auqaf plan to the editor's (1974).
**An entry must not write that the Thursday vigil, the Friday milad or the dome's condition are
current**, and must not present the 1292 AH figure as the book's own statement of its date — the chunk
gives 827 and 465 and does not add them" (pp. 194–195; chunk 018). The same caution applies to Mian
Wada's langar and Quran school and their named holder Hafiz Ahmad al-Din (p. 188), and to the relics
"اب تک قلعہ میں موجود ہیں" / "بقبضہ سرکار انگریزی" where **which Faqirs were alive when the author
wrote cannot be settled from the syntax** (pp. 256–259; chunk 025).

**C34. The book holds both an eleventh-century rival to `data-darbar`'s primacy and a footnote that
abolishes him.** Shaikh Ismail Muhaddith is the first caller to Islam in Lahore and dies **448 AH**;
Data Ganj Bakhsh arrives and dies **465 AH**. "Move Ismail to **980 AH** and Data becomes the earlier
figure by five centuries. **The book therefore contains, unreconciled, both an eleventh-century rival
to Data Darbar's primacy and a footnote that abolishes him. Record both; do not let an entry present
either as settled.**" The Ghausi notice the editor prefers is itself badly transcribed, flagged in four
places, "though the year itself is legible at two separate points in the footnote" (pp. 191–192, folios
189–190 as printed; chunk 018).

**The book's scope and the survey of Lahore**
- The commissioner "مسٹر ولیم کولڈسٹریم [OCR?]" ordered the author "کہ حالاتِ عمارات و مزارات و مقابر و مساجد نواحِ لاہور مفصل تحریر کروں" — to write a detailed account of the buildings, mazars, tombs and mosques of Lahore's environs. The author calls the task very hard "کیونکہ صدہا سال کے حالات بہم پہونچانے [OCR?] [illegible] مشکل", because the history of hundreds of years had to be gathered (p. 28). Elsewhere he says Lala Amin Chand's work on Lahore's buildings and people was cut short when "مسٹر[OCR?] ولیم[OCR?] گولڈ اسٹریم[OCR?]" left for Shahjahanabad (p. 734).
- The book "مشعرِ احوالِ مقابرِ بزرگانِ اہلِ اسلام وغیرہ عمارات و [illegible] و مراسمِ قدیمہ [illegible] لاہور" (reports on the tombs of Muslim elders, other buildings and the old customs of Lahore), "لیکن فی الاصل تواریخِ [OCR?] اولیا [illegible] ہے", but at root it is a history of saints (p. 29).
- Mufti Ghulam Sarwar had long sought "حالاتِ حضرات نواحیِ لاہور" and could not get them, "کیونکہ حضرات سجادہ نشیناں [illegible] اطلاعِ حال اپنا [illegible]" (p. 31).
- The survey proper opens under the heading "آغاز احوال قبور و مزارات و مقبرہ ہائے بزرگان لاہور اول[OCR?]" (p. 45).
- A forward reference: "مکانات اہل[OCR?] دہلی[OCR?] شہر لاہور میں تحریر یہ ہوگا انشاء اللہ تعالی" (the houses of the people[OCR?] of Delhi[OCR?] in the city of Lahore will be written up, God willing) (p. 292).
- The author cites Lala Amin Chand's *Safarnama* ("مطبوعہ کوہ نور"), its account of the city ("حال شہر لاہور کے بیان میں بہ صفحہ با دن[OCR?]"), and disputes it (p. 733).
- The afterword gives the book's scope as "چارون طرف لاہور کے جسقدر مزار ہین بزرگان سلف کی یادگار ہین اون سب کی حالات"; claims no book had yet been written or printed that covered "آثار [OCR?] قدیمہ لاہور" in full; and says it covers "بزرگان ہنود و اہل اسلام" and that its "صلح کلی" respects every religion (p. 872).

**Pre-Mughal rulers and invasions**
- When Sabuktigin first invaded Hind, in "سن [illegible] سو ستہتر [OCR?] ہجری", "لاہور مین راجہ جی پال حاکم تہا" (p. 804). Sabuktigin, having won the Afghans over, "لاہور و ملتان کو اپنے قبضہ مین کر لیا" (p. 804).
- Jaipal, defeated, asked that a trusted man of Sabuktigin's come with him to Lahore to collect the tribute; he came with "نواب خیر اللہ خان [OCR?]". At his court "بطرف چپ چہتری اور بطرف راست برہمن کہتری"; the Chhatris urged him to keep his word, the Brahmins not to pay; Jaipal broke the agreement and imprisoned the envoy. Sabuktigin "لاہور پر چڑہ آیا"; the rajas of Delhi, Ajmer and Kalinjar[OCR?] helped Jaipal, but the Hindus were defeated (p. 804).
- In Sultan Mahmud's time "خاندان توار [OCR?] سی ایک راجہ اوسا [OCR?] لاہور و قنوج کا حاکم تہا" (p. 804).
- Mas'ud, facing the Turkmans (Tughril Beg), was "لاچار لاہور کے طرف روانہ ہوا"; his army looted the treasury at the Indus ("دریا سندہ") and put the blinded Muhammad back on the throne (p. 807).
- In Maudud's time the Hindu rajas besieged Lahore, "جو دار الخلافت اہل اسلام کا تہا", for "سات [OCR?] مہینہ" until the besieged drove them off (p. 807).
- "خسرو شاہ ولد بہرام [illegible] لاہور مین آکر بعالم مجبوری دار السلطنت بنایا"; he ruled "سات [OCR?] برس تک لاہور مین"; his son Khusrau Malik succeeded. The Ghurids took "یہہ ملک" from Khusrau "بہزار مکر و فریب", and "پہر یہان بہی سلطنت غوریان کی ہوئی" (pp. 808–809).
- From "کتاب خلاصۃ التواریخ": after "جہاں [illegible]", Lahore lay deserted ("ویران پڑا رہا") for a long time, until an amir of "سلطان سلوک[OCR?]" settled it again; "کامراں" is named in the damaged continuation (p. 430). Settlement then grew (p. 431).

**Mughal Lahore**
- When Humayun returns to Hindustan, "حاکم لاہور تاتار خان" comes out against him; Bairam Khan overcomes him off guard (p. 823). After Hemu takes Delhi, "اکبر نے لاہور مین کچہہ جمعیت بہم پہونچا کی دہلی پر یورش کی" (p. 824).
- Akbar's second enthronement after Humayun's death: "دوبارہ شہنشاہ حسن تخت نشینی[OCR?] کلاہور میں مسعقد ہوا" (garbled) (p. 648).
- "اکبر بادشاہ[OCR?]" turned to Lahore's settlement from the start of his reign, built a city and a "دولت خانہ", raised large buildings and stayed for a time (p. 431). Lahore is called the capital in Akbar's time: Malik Ali was kotwal in "عہد اکبر بادشاہ" and held "تمام کوتوالی ... دارالسلطنتہ لاہور" (p. 194; surrounding words heavily flagged: "تمر[OCR?] قرویہ[OCR?] حتماً[OCR?] طلوکہ[OCR?] ... مقلم[OCR?]"); also "ملک علی کوتوال لاہور[OCR?]" (p. 193). Akbar is said to have been in Lahore when the Gilani ancestor came: "ان ایام میں اکبر بادشاہ … لاہو یہ رہا[OCR?]" (heavily damaged) (p. 525).
- Khwaja Ayaz (Nur Jahan's father), his wife and the infant travelled with a caravan "اونکی ساتہہ وہ لاہور تک پہنچی"; there he took service and rose to "میر منشی" under Akbar (p. 827).
- Coin legends of "نور الدین جہانگیر ابن اکبر بادشاہ": the transcriber's summary of four illegible lines names mints including "لاہور [OCR?]" and "احمد آباد [OCR?]" (p. 828).
- After Mahabat Khan's victory, "نورجہان لاہور کیطرف بہاگ گئی" (p. 829). Jahangir, ill on his way back from Kashmir, came to Lahore and died there ("جب وہان سے واپس ہوکر لاہور مین آئے تو ... خیمہ زن ملک عدم ہوگئے"); date as printed "تاریخ [illegible] ماہ نومبر سنہ ۱۶۲[illegible]ء [OCR?] مطابق سنہ [illegible]۳۶ [OCR?] ہجری" (p. 830). He was buried "شہر [OCR?] لاہور کے شمال رویہ لب دریای راوی موضع شاہدرہ مین" (p. 830); the tomb is "دریای راوی کی کنارے پر شہر لاہور سے بطرف شمال بفاصلہ چار میل" (p. 855), and from its minarets "تمام شہر لاہور معہ مسجد بادشاہی و وزیر خان وغیرہ عمارات نظر آتی ہین" (p. 860).
- "شاہجہاں بادشاہ لاہور میں ٹھہرے[OCR?]", in the anecdote of Haji Nur (p. 200). Shah Jahan is said to have had "بہت سی عمارات واقع لاہور" built, and Nawab Wazir Khan was appointed "ماظم[OCR?] لاہور" (p. 738); Wazir Khan is "ناظم لاہور" again in "جب نواب صاحب ناظم لاہور رستے[OCR?]" (p. 749). "لاہور مین شالامار باغ" is Shah Jahan's memorial: "چشم روزگار نے کوئی ایسا باغ کم دیکھا ہوگا" (p. 833).
- After Aurangzeb's accession, "دارا شکوہ نے لاہور کو مراجعت کی", raised a larger army there, then crossed the "دریای سندہ" (p. 836). Dara's betrayer, after handing him over in Delhi, "لاہور کیطرف چلا گیا" and was killed on the road (p. 838).
- At the start of Alamgir's reign Nawab Khalil Allah Khan held "دارہ[OCR?] لاہور" and, with a mansab of "شش ہزاری", was appointed "صوبہ دار لاہور" (p. 497).
- The great buildings stood until "عہد عالمگیر"; great amirs built "بڑی بڑی عمارات سنگین"; Abu al-Hasan son of Asaf Khan's buildings were among Lahore's chief ornaments (p. 431).
- In the succession war after Aurangzeb, Muhammad Mu'izz al-Din, governor of Multan, came with a large army and stores "بمقام لاہور" to his father Sultan Mu'azzam Bahadur Shah, and both went on to Akbarabad (p. 845). "شاہ عالم شہر لاہور مین سنہ ۱۷۱۲ء [OCR?] مین فوت ہوا" (p. 846).

**Later Mughal governors (nazims and subadars)**

Zakariya Khan was appointed "ناظم لاہور" through the Sayyid brothers' support, Abdus Samad Khan moving to "ناظم ملتان"; later he continues as "ناظم لاہور" under Muhammad Shah, "شوق عمارت انکو معہ[OCR?] کمال تہا" (p. 716).
- In the reign of "محمد شاہ بادشاہ" a "حاکم[OCR?] لاہور" is named in connection with "مال گزاری[OCR?]"; the rest is damaged (p. 372).
- Mir Momin Khan became "ناظم[OCR?]" of Lahore in the time of "محمد شاہ[OCR?]" (p. 172); administration of the subjects was good, "رعایا ہی لاہور اوست بند ولسیت[OCR?] بہت عن[OCR?]"; he was not autonomous ("اگرچہ یہ نخود مختار نہ سے تھے"), "نبج[OCR?] باں[OCR?] خان" being "اصلی ناظم لاہور"; most buildings between Mochi Gate and Kashmiri Bazaar are his memorials, "دروازہ موچی و جنوب باروبہ[OCR?] بازار کشمیری اکثر عمارات … یادگار انکی"; and "یہ آخری ناظم لاہور ہوے" — after him no nazim was appointed or came (p. 173). Set beside this, without reconciliation: Nawab Abdullah Khan appointed "ناظم لاہور" in "سنہ ۱۱۶۱[OCR?]" (p. 717); the "حاکم لاہور" of p. 579; "خواجہ عابد صوبہ دار لاہور" (p. 578).

## From Kanhaiya Lal, Tarikh-e Lahore

- **Saint.** A **Hasani Sayyid**: "یہ بزرگ یعنی علی گنج بخش ہجویری حسنی سید ہے", with the descent printed in full — "شیخ علی بن سید عثمان بن سید علی بن سید عبد الرحمان بن سید عبد اللہ [OCR?] بن سید ابو الحسن علی بن سید حسن بن سید زید شہید بن امام حسن بن علی مرتضی" (p. 291, folio 294). He came in the company of **Sultan Masud, son of Sultan Mahmud** — "یہ بزرگ سلطان مسعود سلطان محمود کے بیٹے کے ہمراہ لاہور مین آیا" — and "سنہ ۴۳۱ ہجری مین اس نے اپنے قدوم [OCR?] میمنت لزوم سے لاہور کو مشرف کیا اور سنہ ۴۶۵ مین مر گیا چونتیس برس برابر لاہور مین رہ کر ظاہری باطنی علم پہیلایا" — honoured Lahore with his arrival in **431 AH**, died in **465**, and spread outward and inward knowledge through **thirty-four years** in Lahore; **neither of these two year-numerals carries an `[OCR?]` flag** (p. 289, folio 292). His book: "کتاب کشف المحجوب بزبان فارسی تصوف کے علم مین اس نے ایسی لکہی ہے کہ اُسکا ثانی روئے زمین پر نہین ہے" — **Kashf al-Mahjub** in Persian on tasawwuf, with no equal on the face of the earth, and many books in **Arabic[OCR?] and Persian** (p. 291, folio 294).
- **Shrine / Darbar.** "یہ متبرک و قدیمی مقبرہ لاہور کے مشہور و مزین مزارون و مقبرون سے ہے دور دور سے مسلمان معتقد لوگ اس مزار کی زیارت کو آتے ہین" — among Lahore's famous and adorned mazars, with Muslim devotees coming from far for its ziyarat (p. 288, folio 291). Layout: the enclosure's door is **south**; inside, a stone-threshold gate to the west with a deorhi behind it; through it, from the eastern gate, a square courtyard with houses and dalans on all four sides; "جنوبی سمت مجاور رہتے ہین", the **mujawirs live on the southern side**; in the courtyard's middle an octagonal[OCR?] platform (ہشتمین [OCR?] چبوترہ) carrying the mazar — "چبوترہ سنگ مرمر کا ہے اوپر بہی فرش سنگ مرمر کا قبر بہی ایک ٹکڑہ سنگ مرمر کی بنی ہے", the platform of **marble**, the floor above it marble, **the grave made of a single piece of marble** — with a wooden lattice on all eight sides (pp. 289–290, folios 292–293).
- **A dated dome:** "پہلے اوپر مزار کے گنبد نہ تہا مگر سنہ ۱۲۴۷ [OCR?] ہجری مین مسمی نور محمد سماوہ [OCR?] نے یہ گنبد بنوایا تہا جو اب موجود ہے" — formerly there was **no dome**; in **1247[OCR?] AH** one **Nur Muhammad Samawa[OCR?]** had it built, round and very beautiful, with mirrors fitted below and above the lattice and the southern door kept (p. 290, folio 293).
- **A third date that does not agree with the other two, kept as printed.** A gilded inscription over the inner gate, "اندر کے دروازے کے سنہری یہ شعر لکہے ہین", three Persian couplets **every line of which is OCR-flagged**, beginning "خانقاہ علی ہجویری سے [OCR?]", under which stands "۳۷۶ [OCR?]" (p. 289, folio 292) — **a numeral that does not agree with the 431 and 465 printed on the same page, and is not reconciled**.

- **Legends & Miracles — chillas kept at the mazar by later saints:** "بہت سے بزرگون مثل خواجہ معین الدین چشتی اجمیری و خواجہ فرید پاک پٹنی وغیرہ نے یہان آکر چلے کاٹے اور فیض پایا تہا" (p. 289, folio 292). "اسکی لاکہون کرامتین کتابون مین درج ہین" — his lakhs of karamat are recorded in books; **none is narrated** (p. 289, folio 292).
- **Significance.** The author defers to books and then endorses them: "دارا شکوہ [OCR?] نے اپنی کتاب سفینۃ الاولیا اور مولانا جامی اپنی کتاب نفحات الانس مین اس بزرگ کی تعریف بحد کمال لکہتے ہین اور بیشک یہ بزرگ ایسا ہی ہوگا جیسی اسکی تعریف کتابون مین درج ہے" (p. 289, folio 292). The mazar is also used as a bearing for other sites: the Shia Imambara is placed "بیرون دروازہ بہاٹی … گنج بخش کے مزار پر انوار کے جنوب کی سمت کو" (p. 301, folio 304).
