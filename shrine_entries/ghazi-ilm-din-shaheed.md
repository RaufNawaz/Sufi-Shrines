## Overview

In Lahore lies the shrine of Ghazi Ilm Din Shaheed, a young carpenter revered across Pakistan not as a Sufi master or spiritual guide but for a single act: the killing, in 1929, of the publisher of a book judged blasphemous against the Prophet Muhammad. The field survey that is this entry's only source describes a shrine visited daily for prayer, vow-making and intercession, and calls him *Ghazi-e-Millat* and *Fakhr-e-Ummah* -- "warrior of the nation" and "pride of the community."

## Family and Early Life

According to the survey, Ghazi Ilm Din was born on 4 December 1908 in Lahore to a family of carpenters (*tarkhan*); his father's name is given as Taleh Mand. He had two brothers, one a fellow carpenter and one a government employee. The survey describes the family as widely respected by neighbours for its piety, with no history of dispute with anyone. Ilm Din received an early education at a neighbourhood madrasa before entering the family trade.

## The Blasphemy Case, as the Survey Narrates It

The survey dates the origin of the case to 1928, when a Hindu publisher named Rajpal issued a book, *Rangeela Rasool*, that the Muslim community of the time considered blasphemous. It states that a British magistrate sentenced Rajpal to only six months' imprisonment -- judged far too lenient -- and that a further appeal, heard by a magistrate named Dalip Singh, secured Rajpal's release on bail, further inflaming public anger. Two earlier attempts on Rajpal's life are recorded in the survey: on 24 September 1928 a man named Khuda Bakhsh tried and failed, and was sentenced to seven years; later a man named Abdul Aziz, said to have travelled from Afghanistan, struck the wrong man (Swami Satyanand, not Rajpal) at Rajpal's shop, and received fourteen years.

The survey then describes Ilm Din overhearing a public gathering recounting Rajpal's offence, and reports a dream in which an elder rebukes him for sleeping while his Prophet is insulted -- a dream his friend Sheeday says he shared the same night. The two are said to have drawn lots three times to decide who would act, with Ilm Din's name emerging each time. On 6 April 1929, per the survey, Ilm Din went to Rajpal's shop and killed him, then walked to Anarkali police station and turned himself in without expressing regret.

*Note: this entire episode is presented here exactly as the field survey states it -- the surveyor's own retelling of a well-known historical case, not checked here against other historical accounts of the same events.*

## Trial and Execution

The survey states that Muhammad Ali Jinnah represented Ilm Din at the High Court, and advised him against confessing so a defence could be mounted; Ilm Din is quoted as refusing, calling the killing the one deed of his life he took pride in. The High Court passed a death sentence on 7 July 1929, which the survey says Ilm Din welcomed, preferring the gallows to imprisonment. It states that Jinnah and Allama Muhammad Iqbal both sought clemency from the British government without success, and that Ilm Din was executed at Mianwali Jail on 31 October 1929.

## Funeral and Burial

The survey describes the body brought from Mianwali to Lahore under tight security, with funeral prayers led by Qari Shams-ud-Din, Imam of the Wazir Khan Mosque, on Railway Auqaf land, attended -- per the survey -- by an estimated 600,000 people. It states that Allama Iqbal and Allama Mehr-ud-Din Londkhori lowered the body into the grave, and quotes Iqbal as saying, on that occasion, "this carpenter's son has surpassed us all."

## Why the Shrine Is Venerated

The survey reports that visitors regard Ilm Din's act as proof of a station high enough that Allah answers prayers made through his intercession (*waseela*). It also records an account, attributed to elders who visited him in prison, that the Prophet appeared to him nightly to recite the Qur'an -- including an episode with the visiting scholar Pir Syed Meher Ali Shah, in which Ilm Din is said to have corrected a lapse in Meher Ali Shah's recitation of Surah Yusuf, prompting Meher Ali Shah to liken his imprisonment to that of the Prophet Yusuf.

## Devotion and Events

Per the survey, people come daily -- an estimated 400 to 600 visitors -- to light lamps, make vows (*mannat*), recite Fatiha and distribute langar; a space for two to three prayer rows sits beside the shrine. Thursday nights bring a dedicated Mehfil. The annual urs falls on 31 October (the date of the execution) and includes Mehfil-e-Naat, chadar-changing, ritual washing (*gusal*), langar, and collective prayer.

## Custodianship

The survey states the shrine is managed by a body called the Ghazi Ilam Din Shaheed Committee, with some matters handled by the Auqaf Department. It separately notes -- as a point some visitors raise -- that Rajpal was the book's publisher, while various accounts describe its author as an anonymous or pseudonymous figure, distinct from Rajpal himself.
