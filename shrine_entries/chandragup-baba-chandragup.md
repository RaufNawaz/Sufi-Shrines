## Overview

Chandragup, a mud volcano rising from the arid coastal hills of Hingol National Park in Lasbela District, is one of the most sacred halting-points on the annual Hinglaj Yatra, the pilgrimage that draws Hindu devotees across the Kirthar and Makran hills to the shrine of Hinglaj Mata. Known devotionally as Baba Chandragup, the cone is venerated as an abode associated with Shiva, and its periodic eruptions of cold, sulphurous mud are read by pilgrims as auspicious signs.

## The Hinglaj Yatra

During the annual four-day Hinglaj Yatra, held each April, pilgrims halt at Chandragup to fast, keep vigil through the night, and make offerings — coconuts, sweets, flags and coins pressed into the soft mud — before resuming their journey onward to the Hinglaj Mata Temple itself, the yatra's principal destination. The mountain's veneration is of unrecorded antiquity, attested only in the living pilgrimage tradition rather than in any dated textual source.

## A Natural Site Within a Devotional Landscape

Unlike most entries in this collection, Chandragup is not a constructed temple but a natural geological feature — one of several mud volcanoes in the Hingol region — whose sanctity derives entirely from its place within the Hinglaj pilgrimage route. It is filed here under "Hindu Temple" for consistency with the dataset's schema, though it is best understood as a sacred halting-place rather than a built shrine.

Press accounts of the pilgrimage add further devotional detail: an image of Shiva marks the volcano's flanks, and pilgrims traditionally ask the god's permission there before continuing on to visit Sati at the Hinglaj Mata shrine itself. The climb to the rim is reported as physically demanding — muddy and steep, with heat and dust causing some pilgrims to slip or faint from exhaustion — and coconuts are thrown into the crater as offerings and to make wishes. Reported attendance at the wider Hinglaj festival has varied sharply by year and source, from "more than 40,000 people" in 2019 to a reported 200,000 pilgrims across three days in 2025, and the standard academic treatment of the pilgrimage, Jürgen Schaflechner's *Hinglaj Devi: Identity, Change, and Solidification at a Hindu Temple in Pakistan* (Oxford University Press, 2018; Pakistan pbk. 2020), describes Hinglaj as "the most influential Hindu pilgrimage site in Pakistan," of which Chandragup is one station.

  - **Its function on the route is to grant or withhold permission to go on.** "the group sets off in
    the direction of Chandrakup, the mud volcano, where they will ask for permission to proceed to the
    shrine of Hinglaj Mata (Abadhut 1964, 47)" (p. 128, folio not stated). "many accounts even say that

  - **The preparation rite.** "The aguā gathers the pilgrims around the chaṛī and orders them to fast

    will be offered to Chandrakup the next day." (p. 141, folio not stated) "Before the group ascends
    the mud volcano, Rupalal instructs them on the proper proceedings at Chandrakup (Abadhut 1964,
    156–157). He says that to receive permission to continue their journey, they must worship
    Chandrakup and yell out their sins at the crater's edge. Only then will they be allowed to carry on
    with their journey. The pilgrims also bake a small cake (roṭ), which must be offered to the
    volcano." (p. 130, folio not stated) "After an emotional night in which many pilgrims worry acutely
    about their upcoming confessions at Chandrakup, the group begins their ascent (Abadhut 1964, 164)."

  - **The pūjā and the confession rite, in full.** "The next day, on the top of Chandrakup, the
    chaṛīdār performs the pūjā and tells the pilgrims to come forward one by one." (p. 141, folio not
    stated) "Once at the top and at the rim of the crater, the pilgrims must admit their sins, after
    which they should throw their offerings into the volcano. Before yelling out their sins, every
    pilgrim must state his or her name, caste, and village, as well as his or her father's,
    grandfather's, and great-grandfather's names (Shastri 1977, 42). The offerings consist of gāṁjā

  - **The sanction, as the sources give it.** "the offerings of those who are righteous, or of those
