## Overview

The Shree Ratneshwar Mahadev Temple is a historic cave shrine of *Shiva* near Clifton Beach in Karachi, celebrated as one of the oldest and most atmospheric Hindu sites in the city. Dedicated to Mahadev — the "Great God" Shiva — and said to be more than three centuries old, the temple is renowned for its subterranean setting beside the promenade known as the Jehangir Kothari Parade, and for the great gathering of pilgrims that it draws each year at the festival of *Shivratri*.

## A Cave by the Sea

In earlier times, according to the traditions of the shrine, the sea reached to the very walls of the temple, and the sanctuary was a cave set some thirty or forty feet into the water, with a spring of fresh water rising within it — a natural marvel of the kind that has long been held sacred in the Hindu imagination. The gradual retreat of the shoreline and the growth of the city have since left the cave inland, but the sense of a hidden, water-blessed sanctuary has remained central to its appeal. The adjoining Jehangir Kothari Parade, the seaside promenade built on land given to the city in 1919 by the Parsi philanthropist Jehangir Hormasji Kothari, today frames the approach to the temple.

## The Underground Temple

The shrine is described as an underground complex reached by staircases near the Kothari Parade, descending through several levels to the sanctuary below. Devotees speak of a marble courtyard on one of the lower floors and, on a level deeper still, of a tunnel said to lead towards the Mohatta Palace — a tradition of the sort common to old cave temples, and best regarded as an expression of devotional lore rather than of surveyed fact. Whatever the precise form of its chambers, the temple's descent into the earth, and its association with a subterranean spring, lend it an air of antiquity and mystery that few urban shrines can match.

## Shivratri and Devotional Life

The great occasion of the temple's year is *Shivratri*, the night sacred to Shiva, when many thousands of worshippers — by local reckoning as many as twenty-five thousand — throng to the shrine from across Karachi and beyond. On this and other festivals the underground sanctuary becomes the focus of the city's Shaiva devotion, with offerings, vigils and the chanting of the god's praises. Through the rest of the year it remains a quiet place of worship for the Hindu families of the Clifton neighbourhood.

## Preservation Struggles

The temple's survival has not been untroubled. In 2014, under a scheme to improve traffic at Clifton, the construction of underpasses and a flyover began near the shrine, and cracks appeared in the fabric of the cave. The Pakistan Hindu Panchayat brought the matter before the Supreme Court, which ordered the restoration of the temple — a further instance of the community's recourse to the law to protect its endangered heritage.

## Legacy

The Shree Ratneshwar Mahadev Temple stands among the most evocative of Karachi's surviving Hindu monuments: a cave sanctuary of Shiva that has kept its sanctity through the transformation of a fishing coast into a great metropolis. For the city's Hindus it is a living centre of pilgrimage and festival; for the wider public it is a reminder of the deep and continuing presence of Hindu devotion in the fabric of Karachi.

- "نوسر[OCR?] نام معبدیست منسوب بمهادیو": pilgrims there hear "اواز الات[OCR?] پرستش گری", "و کس نداند که از کجاست" (p. 507).
- In a cave in "تبت کلان" an ice image called "امرنات[OCR?]": when the moon comes out of conjunction an ice bubble appears and grows daily to "بده گز الهی" in fifteen days, shrinking as the moon wanes until nothing is left; "وان را پیکر مهادیو دانند و برامد کارها را دست مایه دانند" (p. 508).
- "در قصبه مول معبدیست منسوب بمهادیو": each year before the rains a creature "که بزبان هند سنگهه(۱) گویند" (editor's footnote on the name [illegible]), a little smaller than a pigeon, foul-smelling, white and black, settles on the temple, rolls about and dies; the townspeople gather, burn perfumes, and read the rains from its colours: "از سیاهی باریدگی و از سفیدی خشکی تفول کنند" (pp. 531–532).
- Among the rivers of suba Berar: "گنگ هندوستان را بمهادیو نسبت میدهند"; the Godavari ("گنگ گوتمی که ان را گوداوری گویند") is ascribed to Gautam, "که عابد مشهور بود" (pp. 537–538).

- **Suba Thatta described** (c. 042; book's words: "صوبه تهته" (p. 530), "درین ولایت" (p. 530), "درین صوبه" (p. 529); period: the author's time, c. 1107 AH, a Mughal suba; the capitals "در پاستان زمان"):
  - Capitals: "برهمن اباد نام شهری بزرگ تختگاه بود و قلعه ان هزار و چهار صد برج داشت هر یک بفاصله یک طناب"; after it "ادپور پائے تخت گردید", with many traces of towers and walls remaining; "پس ازان دیول اکنون تهته و دیبل دار الحکومت است" (p. 530).
  - Land revenue: "درین ولایت سوم بخش از کشاورز به گیرند" (p. 530).
  - Resources: "کان نمک و اهن فراوان"; at "در[OCR?] شش کروهے" a "کان سنگ زرد", cut to size for building (p. 530).
  - Boats: "اکثر مدار بر کشتی است", "از خورد و بزرگ چهل هزار خواهد بود" (p. 530).
  - Game and food: "گورخر", hare, "کوته پاچه", pig and fish; the diet rests "بر برنج و جغرات و ماهی" (p. 530). Dried fish ("قاق") is shipped to the ports, and fish oil ("روغن[OCR?]") is used on boats; a fish called "پلوه[OCR?]" comes from the salt sea into the Sind, is netted, and is found in no other country (p. 530). Curds keep four months; flowers and many fruits, "خاصه انبه", and small melons in the desert (p. 530).
  - The liver-eaters: "جگر خواره این دیار مشهورتر فی الاکناف است"; they take livers, especially children's, by spell, especially at mealtimes; "بعضی گویند" a trance comes on them; they share the liver with fellow-practitioners and the victim dies (p. 530). They make others like themselves by feeding them a piece and teaching the spell; they ride the hyena ("کفتار") and bring news from afar (p. 530). When one is caught the experts split the calf and take out "دانه بسان انار دانه", which is fed to the victim, who recovers (pp. 529–530). A liver-eater will not sink with a millstone tied to the neck nor burn in fire; to cure one, the experts brand both temples, fill the eyes with salt, hang the person in a house for forty days, give saltless food and recite spells (p. 529). Most are women, some men; "در هر بلاد می باشند و اما در بلاد تهته بیشتر باشند" (p. 529).
  - Peoples: north of the line from Multan and Uch to Thatta and Kach Makran, hard-rock hills, home of Baluch and "بعضے افغان"; to the south, from Uch to Gujarat, sand hills and the "بهتی", whose chief's seat is Jaisalmer, and "دیگر اقوام راجپوت[OCR?]"; from Bhakkar to Nasirpur and Amarkot, "سبوده[OCR?]" and "جاریجه" (p. 529).
  - River: "بزرگ ترین دریائے سند است"; merchants from Multan and Bhakkar ship goods by it to Thatta, and travellers and even "عساکر گران" go by river; large armies rarely go by land, because of the jungle and the lack of water (p. 529).
  - Extent: "طول این صوبه از بهکر تا کچ مکران دو صد و پنجاه کروه و عرض از قصبه بدین تا بندر لاهری صد کروه" (p. 529). Bounds: east "گجرات احمداباد", west "کچ مکران", north "بهکر", south "دریائے شور" (p. 529).
  - Administration and revenue: "سرکار تهته و سیوستان و نصیر پور و امرکوت چهار سرکار مشتمل بر پنجاه و هفت محال و پنج بندر و ده[OCR?] کرور[OCR?] و چهل و نه لک و هفتاد هزار دام داخل این صوبه است" (pp. 528–529).
  - Pass 1's Doubts: the book places Hinglaj in its suba Thatta section, but the Hinglaj row's province tag is balochistan; this sindh bullet is not meant to reach it.
