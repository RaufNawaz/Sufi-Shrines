## Overview

The shrine of *Hazrat Shah Daula Daryai* stands in the city of Gujrat, in the fertile plain between the Chenab and Jhelum rivers in the Punjab. It commemorates a Sufi saint of the Mughal period, remembered above all as a builder of bridges, wells and *sarais* (rest-houses) and as a benefactor of the poor, the traveller and the afflicted. His epithet *Daryai* — from *darya*, "river" — is traditionally explained by his works of construction across the region's torrential streams. For nearly four centuries the *darbar* has been a focus of popular devotion in central Punjab; it is also, more painfully, the site around which one of South Asia's most persistent and troubling folk traditions grew up — that of the so-called "Shah Daula's mice" (*chuhas*), children affected by microcephaly who were historically associated with the shrine. The account below treats the saint first, and then addresses that tradition factually and with the medical understanding it requires.

## The Life of the Saint

Reliable dates for Shah Daula are difficult to fix, and the sources differ; he is traditionally said to have lived roughly between 1581 and 1675, and to have settled in Gujrat in the early seventeenth century — some accounts placing this in the reign of the Mughal emperor Jahangir, others in that of Akbar. He is said to have travelled in his youth to the neighbourhood of Sialkot, where he became the disciple of a Suhrawardi *faqir* remembered as Shah Saidan Sarmast, serving at his master's *khanqah* with great devotion before being entrusted with its care. He later established his own *khanqah* at Gujrat, at the place formerly known as Garhi Shah Daula.

What is most consistently remembered of him is not doctrine but works of public benefit. He is credited with constructing bridges over seasonal watercourses — including, by tradition, a bridge over the Nala Dek — together with wells, mosques and rest-houses along the routes of travel, at a time when such infrastructure eased both trade and pilgrimage across the Punjab. He is also remembered for his *langar*, his charity to the destitute, and his care for animals and for people living with disabilities. In this he embodies a distinctively practical, service-oriented sanctity: a saint honoured less for miracles than for the tangible relief of hardship. His tomb, surmounted by a dome and set beside a spacious mosque, dates in its essentials from the seventeenth century, though the structure was substantially rebuilt in the late nineteenth century (traditionally 1898) and has been repaired and extended since. Since the mid-twentieth century the shrine has been administered by the Punjab Auqaf (Religious Affairs) Department, which now organises the annual three-day *urs* with arrangements for *langar*, sanitation and security, and with gatherings devoted to the saint's life and teaching.

## The "Chuhas" of Shah Daula: Folklore and the Medical Reality

No account of this shrine can avoid the tradition of the *chuhas* — a word meaning "mice" or "rats" in Punjabi — the name long given to microcephalic children associated with the *darbar*. This tradition must be approached with care, both because it concerns vulnerable and disabled human beings and because it has frequently been sensationalised. The essential facts, as documented by observers and scholars since the nineteenth century, are these.

Microcephaly is a medical condition in which the head and brain are abnormally small, typically accompanied by intellectual disability; it has recognised genetic and environmental causes and occurs in populations everywhere. At Gujrat, individuals with this condition came to be linked with Shah Daula's shrine through a folk belief — first recorded in print in January 1839 by the observer Shahamat Ali — that childless women who prayed for offspring at the *darbar* would be granted a child, the first of whom must, according to the legend, be dedicated to the shrine and would be born with the characteristic small head. Such dedicated children were called *chuhas* and were often maintained at, or associated with, the shrine.

From the mid-nineteenth century onward the phenomenon attracted European medical and ethnographic attention, and with it arose the sensational speculation that the small heads were produced deliberately, by binding or by fitting clamps or vessels to the skulls of infants. This claim has never been substantiated: no such instrument has ever been found, no such procedure has ever been witnessed, and medical authorities regard the intentional production of microcephaly in this manner as implausible without causing fatal injury. The modern consensus is that the *chuhas* were, and are, individuals with naturally occurring microcephaly whose condition was interpreted through the lens of local religious belief and who were, in effect, drawn into the economy of the shrine — historically, in some periods, being sent out as itinerant beggars. It should be stressed that the folk belief linking the condition to the saint's blessing or curse has no basis in fact and is not endorsed here; it is recorded only as a documented social phenomenon.

Reform came in the twentieth century. When the Auqaf Department assumed control of the shrine in the mid-twentieth century, it acted to forbid both the abandonment of such children to the *darbar* and the alleged deforming practices, and the population of *chuhas* associated with the site steadily declined. By the early twenty-first century observers reported that only one or two remained. The episode endures chiefly as a cautionary study for historians of medicine, disability and popular religion, in which a genuine medical condition, human vulnerability, exploitation and folk piety became tragically entangled.

## Legacy

Shah Daula Daryai is remembered in the Punjab as a saint of works and mercy, whose bridges and wells served the living and whose shrine remains a busy centre of devotion in Gujrat. The disquieting tradition of the *chuhas* that attached itself to his name in later centuries belongs to the social history that grew up around the shrine rather than to the saint himself, and is best understood today through the twin lenses of medical fact and compassion for those who were affected. The *darbar* continues to draw pilgrims, to feed the poor at its *langar*, and to hold its annual *urs* under Auqaf administration.
