## Overview

In the Shahi Bazaar of Kalat — the ancient hill-town of central Balochistan that was for centuries the seat of the Khanate of Kalat — stands a Hindu temple dedicated to the goddess Kali. Local tradition holds the shrine to be of great antiquity, with some accounts tracing its origins to the pre-Islamic Sewa dynasty period and claiming an age of some 1,500 years; no dated inscription or independent historical record has been located to confirm so early a founding, and the claim is best treated as devotional tradition rather than settled fact.

## A Living Shrine in a Historic Bazaar

The temple's presence within the working fabric of Kalat's old bazaar reflects a resident Hindu trading community that has occupied the town's commercial life alongside its Baloch and Brahui neighbours, a pattern of minority presence in Balochistan's interior towns that predates the modern state and has persisted, in much reduced form, into the present.

## Festival and Pilgrimage

The temple's clearest claim to wider recognition is its annual three-day Kali festival, held each January, which draws Hindu pilgrims from across Pakistan and, on occasion, from India — a notable instance of cross-border devotional travel to a small provincial shrine.

Independent confirmation of the temple's antiquity comes from the colonial-era *Ethnographic Survey of Baluchistan* (1913), which records the Kalat shrine as "probably the oldest place of Hindu worship" in the region and describes a perpetual lamp, or *jōt*, kept burning day and night, its oil historically supplied by the state itself. Press coverage of the temple's 2023 festival reports that it houses the second-largest Kali statue in Asia, and a 2017 profile found its rites then led by a young priest, Maharaj Sain Shamgir, before a congregation of about 35 worshippers, with Kalat city's own Hindu population put at some 200 households — of an estimated 100 families who had migrated elsewhere, roughly 80 were reported to have since returned. That same 2017 account also recorded a local priest's tradition that the city of Kalat itself takes its name from a Hindu ruler he called "Kalat Seva" — a separate oral tradition, distinct from the Sewa-dynasty founding narrative above, and not independently corroborated.

- **The chiefs of Baluchistan and Rustam Mirza's jagir** (c. 014; book's word: "بلوچستان" (p. 175); period: the emperor's Qandahar campaign, no year in the section; not called a suba, extent not stated — Pass 1: do not read it as the modern province): the Khankhanan's orders, "اگر کلان تران بلوچستان لوازم انقیاد و اطاعت بجا آورند و درین هم همراه شوند بهتر و الا بسزاے[OCR?] لایق رساند" (p. 175). Rustam Mirza's jagir: "ولایت ملتان و بلوچستان که زیاده از محصول قندهار بود" (p. 175).
