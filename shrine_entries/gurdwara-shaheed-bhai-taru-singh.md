## Overview

Gurdwara Shaheed Bhai Taru Singh, in the Naulakha bazaar quarter of the walled city of Lahore, marks one of the most harrowing and revered episodes in Sikh history: the martyrdom of *Bhai Taru Singh* (c. 1720–1745), the young cultivator who chose to have the skin of his scalp cut away rather than his sacred, uncut hair. His story stands at the heart of the eighteenth-century Sikh memory of persecution and steadfast faith, and the shrine that bears his name is a place of deep veneration for Sikhs across the world.

## The Life and Martyrdom of Bhai Taru Singh

Bhai Taru Singh was born around 1720 into a Sikh family of modest means and raised, after his father's early death, by his widowed mother alongside his sister, Bibi Tar Kaur. He took up farming at the village of Poola in the Kasur district of the Lahore region, growing crops and living simply. These were bitter years for the Sikhs of the Punjab: under the Mughal governor *Zakariya Khan*, a price was placed on Sikh heads and the community was driven into the forests and wastes, sustained by sympathisers in the villages. Bhai Taru Singh was one such sympathiser, quietly giving a share of his harvest to feed the Sikh bands who lived as fugitives.

He was denounced to the authorities — by tradition, betrayed by an informer — and arrested together with, in some accounts, his sister. Brought before Zakariya Khan at Lahore, he was offered the customary choice: embrace Islam, of which the outward sign would be the cutting of his *kesh* (the unshorn hair kept by observant Sikhs), or die. Bhai Taru Singh refused to cut his hair or renounce his faith. In an act of calculated cruelty, the governor is said to have ordered that his scalp be sliced from his skull with a blade, so that his hair could never be shorn nor grow again. This is believed to have taken place in the summer of 1745.

Sikh tradition relates that the martyr did not die at once, and that Zakariya Khan was soon afflicted by a torment of his own — an inability to pass water — that no physician could relieve. It was said he would find no ease until struck with the shoe of the man he had wronged; when Bhai Taru Singh's shoes were brought and struck against the governor's head, Zakariya Khan died in agony. Bhai Taru Singh, having outlived his persecutor, is said to have given up his life of his own will on 1 July 1745. His fortitude made him one of the great *shaheeds* (martyrs) of the Sikh Panth, remembered daily in the *Ardas*, the Sikh prayer that honours those who suffered for their faith.

## The Shrine

The gurdwara at Naulakha marks the place associated with his martyrdom, in the vicinity of the celebrated *Shahidganj* — the "treasury of martyrs" — where, through the eighteenth century, many Sikhs were put to death. The site has long been a focus of devotion and, in the colonial period, of legal and communal contest over its custody. Today the shrine preserves the memory of Bhai Taru Singh's sacrifice, and Sikh pilgrims visiting Lahore make their way to it to pay homage.

## Legacy

The martyrdom of Bhai Taru Singh has become one of the defining stories of Sikh identity — a parable of the *kesh* held dearer than life itself, and of faith unbroken by torture. His image, the young farmer whose scalp was taken rather than his principles, is invoked in sermon, art, and remembrance wherever Sikhs gather. The gurdwara in Lahore, standing on the soil where he suffered, is both a memorial to one man's courage and a monument to the endurance of a community through its darkest years.
