## Overview

At Ranmal Sharif, near Phalia in the Mandi Bahauddin region of the Punjab, stands the shrine of Hazrat Syed Muhammad Noushah Ganj Bakhsh (1552–1654) — the founder of the Naushahia branch of the Qadiri Sufi order. A saint of long life and wide influence, Noushah Qadiri established a spiritual line that spread across the Punjab and beyond and endures to this day, and his darbar at Ranmal Sharif remains an important centre of pilgrimage.

## The Life of the Saint

Muhammad Noushah — honoured as _Ganj Bakhsh_, “the bestower of treasure,” and _Noushah_, “the bridegroom” — was born in 1552. He took the Sufi path in the Qadiri order and, attaining a high spiritual station, founded his own branch, the _Naushahia_, which he propagated with great energy across the Punjab. He lived through a full century of the Mughal age, guiding a vast body of disciples and establishing a network of successors, and he is remembered both as a saint of powerful blessing and as an organiser of an enduring order. He died in 1654, full of years, and was buried at Ranmal Sharif, which became the seat of his line.

## The Shrine and the Naushahia Order

The shrine of Noushah Ganj Bakhsh at Ranmal Sharif is the mother-shrine of the Naushahia Qadiri order, kept by his descendants and successors, and a busy centre of pilgrimage. It maintains the customary devotions — prayer, qawwali and _naat_, and the free kitchen — and its annual urs draws great crowds of the order’s followers and of the wider devout. The Naushahia order he founded remains active across the Punjab and among the Punjabi diaspora, so that his shrine is the head of a living and widespread spiritual family.

## Legacy

Syed Muhammad Noushah Ganj Bakhsh is remembered as the founder of the Naushahia Qadiri order — one of the enduring Sufi lines of the Punjab — a saint whose long life and organising genius planted a spiritual family that has lasted nearly four centuries. His shrine at Ranmal Sharif remains its living heart and a major centre of Qadiri devotion in the region.

- Entry no. 30, printed p. 58, "حضرت نوشہ گنج بخش" in the contents (p. 15, folio 3); the entry itself

  gives his personal name as "حاجی محمد", against the archive's "Syed Muhammad Noushah Ganj Bakhsh".
  He also appears in full honorific as "حضرت حاجی محمد نوشہ گنج بخش" (p. 272, folio 260).
- A wali from birth and **khalifa of Shah Sulaiman Qadri** (the Bhalwal figure of entry no. 26);
  states of jazb, sukr, love and longing dominated him, and he was unequalled in asceticism, piety
  and karamat (p. 70, folio 58).
- **The order's origin stated plainly**: "طریقہ نوشاہیہ جس کے فقیر پنجاب میں ہزاروں ہیں۔ ان سے شروع
  ہوا" — the Naushahia path, whose faqirs in Punjab number in the thousands, began with him (p. 70,
  folio 58). Elsewhere the same order's rise in Punjab is attributed instead to **Shah Maruf Chishti
  wa Qadri** (entry no. 12): "انہیں کی ذات بابرکات سے پنجاب میں سلسلہ نوشاہیہ نے فروغ پایا" (p. 50,

- **Parents, both named on one printed page.** Father **Haji Ala al-Din**, himself a great elder who
  performed the Hajj seven times and was honoured with a vision of the Prophet; mother
  **Bibi Jiwani**, also a great worshipper and ascetic (p. 70, folio 58). Confirmed from the indexes:
  "جیونی بی بی (والدہ نوشاہ گنج بخش) | ۵۸" (p. 316, folio 304) and "علاؤ الدین (والد نوشاہ گنج

- **Education and bai'at.** Up to seventeen he completed the exoteric sciences, then renounced the
  world and stayed in the reed-bed of **ساندل بار** (Sandal Bar) in zikr, "مگر والدین نے ان کو وہاں
  نہ چھوڑا"; coming away, he was brought by Mulla Karim al-Din to Shah Sulaiman, took bai'at and in a
  few years reached completion (pp. 70–71, folios 58–59). **The editor corrects the place**:
  "ساندل بار کی بجائے گوندل بار ہونا چاہیے" — instead of Sandal Bar it should be **Gondal Bar**
  (p. 70, folio 58, حاشیہ 2).
- **Death — three readings, none reconciled.**

  - *Editor's footnote*: "حضرت نوشہ رح کا سال ولادت ۹۵۹ھ بمطابق یکم رمضان ہے… آپ کا صحیح سال وفات

- **Verse.** His Urdu and Punjabi verse was selected and edited by Sayyid Sharafat Naushahi as

### Doubts the notes raise on this row

**B6. `ranmal-sharif` — the book places the shrine in a different village from the row.** The editor
states the mazar is "اس وقت ساہنپال شریف ضلع گجرات پنجاب میں", at present at **Sahnpal Sharif, district
Gujrat**, and the *Tazkira-e Naushahi*'s author carries the nisba "ساہنپالوی" (p. 71, folio 59; chunk
006). The row places it at **Ranmal Sharif, Phalia**; **nothing in any chunk names Ranmal or Phalia.**
The figure is beyond doubt — Nausha Ganj Bakhsh, founder of the Naushahia Qadiri order — but the
death year differs by one from the row's 1654: the editor's own publication list gives
"حضرت حاجی محمد نوشہ گنج بخش قادری **ف ۱۶۵۳ء**" with no month, no Hijri year and no source (p. 307,
folio 295; chunk 029). And the name: the entry calls him "**شیخ حاجی محمد قادری**" where the row has
"Syed Muhammad Noushah Ganj Bakhsh"; the chunk gives him **no sayyid descent**, and his pir Shah
Sulaiman's family is claimed as Qureshi by the editor (p. 67, folio 55; chunk 005). "Syed" is
unsupported. **Must be resolved before the row cites this book on location.** (Raised by chunks 006,
023, 028, 029, 031.)

**C39. The `ranmal-sharif` disciples — four flat corrections in one chunk, two of the editor's own
preferred years flagged.** "مفتی صاحب کا مندرجہ سال وفات **۱۱۳۱ ھ غلط ہے**"; Shah Abdullah's death is
**1131** (author) or **1092** (editor); the editor's preferred years are themselves flagged in two of
the four cases — "**۱۰۹۴ ھ [OCR?]**" (p. 242) and "**۱۰۹۷ ھ [OCR?]**" (p. 243) — and his spelling of
the first disciple differs from the author's heading ("شیخ **مطا [OCR?]**" against "شیخ **مٹھا**",
pp. 240–241). Nanu's burial village is flagged, "موضع **کلاسکے [OCR?]** چیمہ ضلع گجرات"; **only
"ضلع گجرات پاکستان" is secure**. The chunk dates Naushah's own death only relatively — "ایک روز ان کی
وفات میں باقی تھا" when Shah Abdullah, "منصبدار ہفت [OCR?] ہزاری دربار عالمگیری", took bai'at — and
"**a human must decide which tradition the row follows before any of the four disciples' dates enter
it, and should check the row's 1654 against the 'Alamgiri court' placement of the deathbed bai'at. The
chunk itself does not date the court**" (pp. 240–243, folios 228–231; chunk 023). See N79, N80, N81.
