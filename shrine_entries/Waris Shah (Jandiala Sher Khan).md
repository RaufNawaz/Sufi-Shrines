## Overview

At Jandiala Sher Khan, in the Sheikhupura district of the Punjab, stands the mausoleum of Waris Shah (c. 1722–1798), the Sufi poet who gave the Punjab its national epic. His *Heer* — the great verse romance of Heer and Ranjha, completed in 1766 — is at once the supreme achievement of Punjabi poetry and a profound Sufi allegory of the soul’s love for God, and it has been sung, wept over, and quoted across the Punjab for two and a half centuries. Waris Shah’s tomb is honoured both as the shrine of a saint and as the resting place of the poet whom Punjabis revere as the “Shakespeare of the Punjabi language.”

## The Life of the Poet-Saint

Waris Shah was born around 1722 into a Sayyid family at Jandiala Sher Khan. He received a thorough religious and literary education, studying at Kasur, and belonged in spirit to the Chishti Sufi tradition, tracing his discipleship to the line of the saint Makhdoom of Kasur. Little of his life is recorded with certainty, and much is woven from legend — including the tradition that he composed *Heer* while living at a village called Malka Hans, in a small chamber beside a mosque, and that the poem grew out of, and transcended, an unfulfilled love of his own. He lived through the anarchic eighteenth century, between the collapse of Mughal power and the rise of the Sikhs, and died around 1798, to be buried at his native Jandiala Sher Khan.

## Heer Waris Shah

Waris Shah’s fame rests on a single work of genius. Taking up the old Punjabi love-legend of Heer, daughter of the Sials, and Ranjha, the herdsman who becomes a jogi for her sake, he retold it in some hundreds of stanzas of matchless Punjabi verse, completed in 1766. On its surface it is a tragic romance; beneath, it is a Sufi allegory in which the human lover’s longing is the soul’s longing for God, and the pain of separation the very engine of the spiritual path. Along the way the poem becomes an encyclopaedia of eighteenth-century Punjabi life — its trades and customs, its proverbs, its landscape and its faith — so that to know *Heer* is to know the Punjab. Its opening lines set love at the foundation of the world:

اوّل حمد خُدا دا ورد کیجے، عشق کیتا سُو جگ دا مُول میاں

[“First, let us utter the praise of God — who made love the very origin of the world.”]

Generations of Punjabis have known long passages of *Heer* by heart, and its verses are sung by professional *Heer*-singers in a distinctive traditional style; the poem is invoked in joy and in grief alike — most famously by the modern poet Amrita Pritam, who at the Partition cried out to Waris Shah himself to rise and witness the suffering of the Punjab.

## The Shrine

Waris Shah’s mausoleum at Jandiala Sher Khan is a place of pilgrimage for the devout and of homage for the lovers of Punjabi letters, and it has been developed over the years into a dignified complex befitting the poet’s stature. Devotees recite the Fatiha and seek blessing; singers come to perform *Heer* and the qawwali; and the annual urs gathers crowds who honour the poet-saint with music and remembrance. The shrine stands as a monument not only to a holy man but to the Punjabi language itself.

## Legacy

Waris Shah occupies a place in Punjabi culture that few poets hold in any language: his *Heer* is the canonical classic of the tongue, memorised, sung, and quoted by millions, and revered as scripture is revered. Through it he wove together the Sufi vision of divine love, the folk-romance of the Punjab, and the living speech of its people, and he made a single poem into a mirror of a whole civilisation. His tomb at Jandiala Sher Khan keeps the memory of the saint who was also the supreme poet of the Punjab.
