## Overview

Gurdwara Dera Sahib, known more fully as Gurdwara Dera Sahib *Patshahi Panjvin* — "of the Fifth Guruship" — stands in Lahore, just beyond the walls of the old city, and commemorates the place associated with the martyrdom in 1606 of Guru Arjan Dev, the fifth of the Sikh Gurus and the first to lay down his life for the faith. Rising in white and gold near the River Ravi, the shrine occupies an extraordinary architectural setting: it stands amid the great Mughal and Sikh monuments of Lahore, close by the Lahore Fort, the Badshahi Mosque, the Hazuri Bagh, and the *samadhi* of Maharaja Ranjit Singh. For Sikhs it is a place of profound solemnity, marking an event that transformed the character and history of their community.

## Guru Arjan Dev

Guru Arjan (1563–1606), son of the fourth Guru, Ram Das, and grandson of the third, Amar Das, presided over the Sikh Panth at a moment of remarkable growth and consolidation. It was he who completed the sacred tank and the central shrine at Amritsar — the Harmandir Sahib, later known as the Golden Temple — and who, in 1604, compiled the *Adi Granth*, the first authoritative scripture of the Sikhs, gathering the hymns of his predecessors together with those of Hindu and Muslim saints and installing the volume in the Harmandir with the venerable Baba Buddha as its first custodian. Under his guidance Sikhism flourished as an organised community with its own scripture, its own centre and a widening body of devotees.

## The Martyrdom of 1606

That very flourishing drew the suspicion of the Mughal state. The Emperor Jahangir, in his memoir the *Tuzuk-i-Jahangiri*, records his displeasure at the Guru's rising influence and at the many who were drawn to him, and complains further that Arjan had extended his blessing to the rebellious prince Khusrau, Jahangir's own son, then in flight through the Punjab. On these grounds the Guru was summoned to Lahore, and a heavy fine — or, failing it, punishment — was imposed.

Sikh tradition holds that the Guru refused to pay or to alter a word of the sacred scripture, and that, at the instigation of the Lahore financier Chandu Shah, he was subjected to days of torture: seated, it is recounted, upon a heated iron plate while burning sand was poured over him, and made to endure scalding water. Bearing these torments with serene equanimity, and declining every inducement to convert, Guru Arjan died in 1606 — his *shaheedi* (martyrdom) traditionally commemorated in the month of Jeth, in late May or early June. By tradition his body was given to the waters of the Ravi and was never recovered. He was the first Sikh Guru to be martyred, and his death is reckoned a watershed in Sikh history.

## The Shrine and its Setting

Sikh tradition records that the Guru's son and successor, Guru Hargobind, raised a memorial platform at the site of the martyrdom in the years that followed. It was under Maharaja Ranjit Singh, however, in the early nineteenth century, that the present *gurdwara* took its handsome form, embellished in the manner of Sikh sacred architecture, with a gilded dome that answers, across the Hazuri Bagh, the domes of the Badshahi Mosque. The proximity of the shrine to Ranjit Singh's own *samadhi* — his memorial tomb — binds it into the great ensemble of monuments that crowns this quarter of Lahore. Within, the *Guru Granth Sahib*, the scripture that Guru Arjan himself first compiled, is enshrined and reverently read, and the shrine functions as a living house of worship.

## Legacy

The martyrdom of Guru Arjan altered the course of Sikh history. His son, Guru Hargobind, on assuming the Guruship, is remembered as having girded himself with two swords, symbolising *miri* and *piri* — temporal and spiritual authority — and as having built the Akal Takht at Amritsar, thus arming and organising the community for its own defence. The pacific movement of Guru Nanak's early followers began, from this point, to take on the resolve and martial spirit that would mark later Sikh history. Gurdwara Dera Sahib thus stands not merely as a memorial to a single death, but as the birthplace of an idea — that faith may be defended, and that the willing acceptance of martyrdom is itself a form of victory. Since the Partition of 1947 the shrine has been maintained within Pakistan under the Evacuee Trust Property Board, and it remains a solemn and much-visited station for the Sikh pilgrims who come to Lahore, above all upon the anniversary of the Guru's martyrdom.

## From Kanhaiya Lal, Tarikh-e Lahore

- **Saint.** "گرو ارجن پانچواں گرو قوم سکھہ کا تھا" — Guru Arjan was the fifth Guru of the Sikh qaum (p. 206, folio 209). **No date of any kind** (not stated). "اگرچہ گرو ارجن دریا میں بوقت غسل مفقود ہو چکا تھا نعش اسکی جلائی نہیں گئی مگر یہ سمادہ یادگار کے طور پر بنائی گئی" — although he had disappeared in the river at the time of his bath and his corpse was not cremated, **this samadh was built by way of a memorial** (p. 206, folio 209). For the manner of his death: "اسکی وفات کا قصہ مفصل مندرج کتاب تاریخ پنجاب مولفہ بندہ ہے" (p. 206, folio 209).
- **Shrine / Darbar.** "یہ سمادہ روشنائی دروازے شہر لاہور کے سامنے واقع ہے" — opposite the **Roshnai Gate**; to its east, "بفاصلہ ایک رستہ[OCR?]", at the distance of one road, the wall of the fort; and toward the west ("غریب کی طرف", for غربی), at a remove lost to an `[illegible]`, the wall of Maharaja Ranjit Singh's samadh (pp. 205–206, folios 208–209). **Two phases:** "پہلے یہ مختصر مکان تھا مہاراجہ رنجیت سنگہ کے وقت یہ عالیشان مکان بنایا گیا" — formerly a small building; in **Ranjit Singh's** time the grand building was made; **no year for either phase** (p. 206, folio 209). Fabric: a pukhta chuna-gach enclosure with a parapet (منڈیر); two of its doors kept shut and all traffic through the great eastern door, on whose پیشانی letters are written "بخط گرمکھی", **in Gurmukhi script**, which the text goes on to call "تصویر[OCR?] یہ بھی گرو ارجن کی تحریر ہے"; to the south of the four walls the grand domed samadh building, pukhta chuna-gach; to its north a "سہ دالان" in whose middle bay "اسمیں گرنتہ صاحب بآداب تمام رکھا رہتا ہے", the **Granth Sahib** is kept with full reverence; the samadh's own chabutra pukhta chuna-gach, where the text **breaks off at an `[illegible]`** (p. 206, folio 209).
- **Practices & Events.** A great well with a baoli in one corner outside the dalan — "چاہ کلان با دلی[OCR?] یعنی چاہ پختہ وار", its stair running north to south down to the water, where a slab of red stone is set so that "وہان کھڑا ہوکر آدمی چاہ سے پانی لے سکتا ہے", a man standing there can draw water (p. 206, folio 209). Four man-height samadhs in another corner, one of them "لچھمی سرکار زوجہ مہاراجہ رنجیت سنگہ کی والدہ کی", of the mother of Lachhmi Sarkar, wife of Maharaja Ranjit Singh (p. 206, folio 209). "شمال کی طرف بھی چند کوٹھریان سکھوں کے آرام کے واسطے بنی ہین" — rooms on the north **for the Sikhs' rest** (p. 206, folio 209).
- **Practices & Events — a bhang house in the enclosure.** A chuna-gach building on the south, "اس مکان کو سکھوں کی دیگ[OCR?] کہتے ہین", called the Sikhs' *deg*[OCR?], because "اسمیں ایک کوڑہ[OCR?] پتھر کا زمین کے اندر گاڑا ہوا ہے اور ایک لکڑی موٹی جسکو ونڈ[OCR?] کہتے ہین بنگ کوٹنے کے واسطے پاس رکھی ہے", a stone mortar is sunk in the ground and a thick wooden pestle called *wand*[OCR?] kept beside it **for pounding bhang**, which the Sikhs grind and drink there (p. 206, folio 209).
- **Practices & Events — income from a dependent shrine.** Of Guru Hargobind's house at Mozang: "اب متعلق سمادہ گرو ارجن کے یہ مکان بھی ہے جو آمدنی اس جگہ کی وہی شخص لیتا ہے جو گرو ارجن کی سمادہ کا پجاری ہو" — that house is now attached to the **samadh of Guru Arjan**, and whatever income it has is taken by the same person who is **pujari of Guru Arjan's samadh** (p. 226, folio 229).
- **Significance.** The one Guru Arjan site in its range that is described as a building, and the anchor from which the Ranjit Singh samadh is located (pp. 205–206, folios 208–209). **It also carries a baoli**, the feature that collides with the archive's other Guru Arjan row (p. 206, folio 209).
- **Identification.** The notice that records the Mozang house's income says only "سمادہ گرو ارجن" and does not itself say which archive row that samadh is (p. 226, folio 229).

### Doubts the notes raise on this row

7. **The two Guru Arjan rows — gurdwara-dera-sahib and gurdwara-baoli-sahib-guru-arjan-dev-ji-lahore —
   are entangled across four ranges and one of the partials disagrees with another (see "Where the
   partials disagree").** In the contents, "سمادہ گورو ارجن [illegible]" at printed page ۲۰۸ could be
   either row and the word after the name is illegible (p. 3, folio 2); "مکان باولی صاحب[OCR?]" at printed
   page ۱۱۱ matches the Baoli row by title but sits inside the book's **first, Hindu, qism** begun at
   printed page ۹۶, and the name is flagged — the three-qism scheme places Sikh samadhs and dharamsalas in
   that same qism (p. 11, folio 6), so the section is neither evidence for nor against (p. 2). In the
   body, **everything about Guru Arjan in pp. 110–114 (folios 113–117) belongs to the baoli and its
   langar**; the book names no Dera Sahib and gives no death date for the Guru. **Do not carry any of it to
   the Dera Sahib row.** Guru Har Gobind appears only as the cause of the Baoli's seizure and attaches to
   **none** of the archive's four Hargobind rows (pp. 111–112, folios 114–115). The chunk that maps
   **gurdwara-dera-sahib** does so on locational bearings only — **outside** the city, opposite the
   Roshnai Gate, fort wall east and Ranjit Singh's samadh west (pp. 205–206, folios 208–209), which does
   not fit Dabbi Bazaar — **but the same site has a baoli** (p. 206, folio 209), exactly the feature that
   could pull it toward the Baoli row; **the book itself never uses either name**, and the mapping needs a
   human sign-off. And "سمادہ گرو ارجن" at p. 226 (folio 229) **cannot be mapped at all**: the Mozang
   building's income goes to the pujari of "گرو ارجن کی سمادہ" with **no location given**; record it as
   "the samadh of Guru Arjan" and **do not pick a row.**
