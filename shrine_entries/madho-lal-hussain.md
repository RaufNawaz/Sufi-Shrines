## Overview

In Baghbanpura, close by the Shalimar Gardens on the eastern edge of Lahore, stands the shrine of Hazrat Madho Lal Hussain — the joint name of two men, and the sign of one of the most extraordinary bonds in the spiritual history of the Punjab. Within it rest Shah Hussain (1538–1599), the great Malamati Sufi and father of the Punjabi _kafi_, and Madho Lal, the Hindu youth he loved and whose name he joined for ever to his own. Around the darbar has grown one of the largest festivals in the region, the Mela Chiraghan or “Festival of Lamps,” and from it has flowed a body of Punjabi mystical verse that ordinary people still sing four centuries on. The shrine is at once a place of ecstatic devotion, a monument of Punjabi language and poetry, and a beloved emblem of love that overleaps the boundaries of caste and creed. To understand it, one must begin with the strange and luminous life of Shah Hussain.

## The Life of Shah Hussain

Hussain was born in Lahore in 1538 into a family of weavers — his father, by tradition, a craftsman who stitched uniforms for the army of the Emperor Humayun. He was a child of unusual gifts and piety: he is said to have committed the Qur’an to memory by the age of seven, and by his mid-teens to have begun teaching recitation and preaching, leading the congregational prayers and the long Taraweeh prayers of Ramadan. For some twenty years he lived the life of an exact and disciplined scholar of the outward law, his devotion so evident that learned men sought his company and came away loving him. In his spiritual training he is remembered as a disciple of the Sufi master Bahlol (Bahlol Daryai) and as a devoted frequenter of the shrine of Data Ganj Bakhsh; there, the accounts say, he withdrew into a long spiritual retreat, keeping _chilla_ by the darbar of Lahore’s patron saint for some twelve years, absorbed in the discipline of divine love (_ishq-e-haqiqi_), while those who came to sit with him took to calling him, simply, “Baba.”

Then, around the age of thirty-two, everything changed. The scrupulous scholar became a _malang_ — a wandering, dishevelled ecstatic. He set aside the robes and the reputation of a preacher, and took up instead the crimson dress by which he is remembered as “Lal” (the Red) Hussain, dancing the _dhamal_ and carrying a flask in his hand that the scandalised took for wine. What had happened, in the language of the Sufis, was that the outward path of the _Shariat_ had opened, for Hussain, onto the burning inward path of love — and he chose to walk it in the most confronting way he could.

## The Way of Blame

Hussain belonged, in spirit, to the _Malamatiyya_ — the Sufis of the “path of blame,” who deliberately drew the censure of respectable society upon themselves in order to kill the ego’s hunger for esteem, hiding their inner sanctity beneath an outward show of impropriety. To dance and sing and carry a flask through the streets of Mughal Lahore was, for such a mystic, not a fall from grace but a discipline of self-effacement, a refusal of the world’s praise. Inevitably it drew fatwas and denunciations, and at last complaints reached the imperial court: some demanded that this man who seemed to mock the outward forms of religion be put to death.

The most famous story of his life belongs to this moment. Summoned before the ruler — the accounts name the Emperor Jahangir — and challenged over the wine he was said to carry, Hussain is said to have poured out his flask into the cups set before the court: and the liquid ran, in the first cup, as milk; in the second, as honey; and in the third, as the sacred water of Zamzam. The king and his courtiers, astonished, understood that they stood before no ordinary man. “Let him be,” they said; “he belongs to another world — let no one trouble him after this day.” The saint went his way, and the tale has been told ever since as a sign that what the world condemns in the friends of God it does not understand.

## Madho Lal: A Love Made Legend

The turning-point of Hussain’s later life, and the reason his name is joined to another’s, was his love for Madho Lal — a young man of a Hindu Brahmin family. The saint, it is said, was overcome at the sight of the boy’s beauty; when people objected that a Muslim faqir should be so taken with a Hindu youth, Hussain answered that his love was not carnal but a kind of wonder: “I do not love him — I envy his Creator, for how beautiful He has made him.” Slowly the two became inseparable, though Madho’s family and caste-fellows looked on the friendship with disapproval. Devotional tradition preserves a famous miracle of their bond: when Madho was bound by custom to make the pilgrimage of the Ganga Snan (the ritual bathing in the Ganges) and feared his parents’ anger, Hussain bade him take his hands, set his feet upon the saint’s own, and close his eyes — and in that instant Madho found himself at the distant bathing-place, to the astonishment of all. From then, the love was mutual and complete: the two could not bear to be parted.

So closely were their names woven together that Hussain gave the youth his own, telling him — when Madho worried that the world knew only “Lal Hussain” — that from that day people would speak Madho’s name before his own. And so it has been: the saint is remembered not as Shah Hussain alone but as _Madho Lal Hussain_, and the two lie buried side by side. In a land later torn by the divisions of religion, the shrine of a Muslim saint and his Hindu beloved, honoured under a single name, has become one of the Punjab’s most cherished symbols of a love that knows nothing of the frontiers of caste and creed — and, for the Sufi, a living parable of the soul’s love for God, in which the lover and the beloved at last become one.

## The Kafis

Shah Hussain is honoured as the first great master of the Punjabi _kafi_ — the short, sung mystical lyric that would become the very heart of Punjabi Sufi poetry, taken up after him by Bulleh Shah, Sultan Bahu, and Khwaja Farid. He is said not to have written his verses down himself; a court official, Dehan Bahar Khan, is remembered as having set them to paper as the saint sang them in his ecstasy. The kafis are brief and burning, cast in the voice of a woman — a bride, a spinner at her wheel, a girl longing for her absent love — for whom the Beloved is God, and separation from Him the only real grief. Almost all of them close with the poet naming himself in the third person, in a signature that has become famous in its own right: _Kahe Hussain, faqir Sain da_ (“Says Hussain, the humble faqir of the Lord”).

In his best-loved kafi the soul cries out in the voice of a daughter to her mother:

مائے نی میں کینوں آکھاں، دردِ وچھوڑے دا حال نی

[“O mother, to whom shall I tell the anguish of this separation?”]

In another, the whole of creation dissolves into the presence of the one Beloved:

رَبّا میرے حال دا محرم توں
اندر توں ہیں، باہر توں ہیں، رومَ رومَ وِچ توں

[“O Lord, You alone are the confidant of my condition — You are within me, You are without me, You are in my every pore.”]

And in another the ache of longing lengthens the very hours of the night:

سجّݨ بِن راتاں ہوئیاں وڈّیاں

[“Without the beloved, the nights have grown long.”]

And in a line that has passed into proverb, he sings of the truth that will not be silenced in the heart of the lover:

منہ آئی بات نہ رہندی اے

[“The word that has risen to the lips cannot be held back.”]

These songs, deceptively simple and endlessly deep, are still sung by qawwals and folk-singers across the Punjab, and it is largely through them that Shah Hussain lives — the man who abandoned the scholar’s certainties for the ecstatic’s, and made of the Punjabi language a vessel for the love of God.

## The Poet and His Age

Shah Hussain lived through the high noon of the Mughal Empire, in the reigns of Akbar and Jahangir, when Lahore was a great imperial capital adorned with gardens, mosques, and mansions. His was an age of ferment — of Akbar’s experiments in the reconciliation of religions and of popular unrest against the imperial order — and Punjabi tradition links the ecstatic faqir with the rebel folk-hero Dulla Bhatti, who rose against the Emperor’s revenue demands, casting Hussain among the friends of the poor and the defiant of the mighty. Among the learned and the holy of the city he had many companions and interlocutors, yet it is the texture of ordinary Punjabi life that fills his verse: the girl at her spinning-wheel (_charkha_), the bride adorned and waiting, the traveller on the long road, the lanes of the parental home that a daughter must one day leave. Into these homely images he poured the whole drama of the soul’s love for God and its grief at separation, so that the humblest villager and the deepest mystic could each find themselves in his song.

In choosing to sing in Punjabi rather than the Persian of the court, and in the voice of a woman rather than that of the scholar, Hussain did something momentous: he made the mother-tongue of the Punjab a vehicle of the highest spiritual expression, and founded the tradition of the mystical _kafi_ that Bulleh Shah, Sultan Bahu, Shah Sharaf, and Khwaja Farid would afterward carry to its heights. His Malamati freedom was itself a kind of statement — a rebuke, danced and sung in the red robe of the malang, to a religion of mere respectability and outward form. In his own person he fused the ecstatic and the poet, the lover and the critic, and left the Punjab a way of speaking to God that it has never since forgotten.

## Death and the Miracle of the Grave

Shah Hussain died in 1599. Foreseeing his end, the accounts say, he had sent Madho away — some say he had him enrolled in the army — knowing that his beloved could not bear to watch him die; and he left a strange instruction, that he be buried first on the very bank of the river Ravi, where the flood would in time carry the grave away. When the waters shifted the grave, he said, they should dig at the place where it came to rest, and if a rosary (_tasbih_), a water-pot (_lota_), and a prayer-mat (_jaanamaz_) were found in the earth, they would know they had found the true spot. He was buried by the Ravi, and Madho, told of his death only afterward, abandoned everything and returned, never again to leave the grave. Some years later the river flooded, as the saint had foretold; the grave was carried to Baghbanpura, where a _jogi_ (a Hindu ascetic) dwelt, and there, when the ground was opened, the rosary, the pot, and the prayer-mat were found exactly as Hussain had promised. On that spot Madho reburied him, and there he kept his long vigil, reciting his master’s kafis in the streets and at the tomb, until, decades later, he too died and was laid to rest beside the friend whose name he bore — as had been Hussain’s last wish.

## The Shrine

The darbar stands in Baghbanpura, in the shadow of the Mughal Shalimar Gardens, and spreads today over some twenty kanals of ground. At its heart lie the two graves — Shah Hussain’s and, beside it, Madho Lal’s — the object of a constant stream of pilgrims who come to recite the Fatiha, to make vows, and to sit in the presence of the saint of love. A mosque adjoins the shrine, where the five daily prayers are held, and around the complex has grown a large graveyard, for to be buried near Madho Lal Hussain is counted a great blessing, and space within it is hard to come by. The shrine took shape over the centuries after the saint’s death, was substantially rebuilt and enlarged in the nineteenth century, and was most recently renovated by the Punjab Auqaf Department, which administers it, in 2011. Every Thursday evening the tomb is lit with lamps and fills with visitors; on ordinary days, too, some eight to ten thousand people are said to pass through.

## Mela Chiraghan: The Festival of Lamps

The shrine’s crowning event is the Mela Chiraghan — the “Festival of Lamps” — the annual urs held in the spring, in the months of March and April, and reckoned among the greatest festivals of the Punjab. For three days the darbar and its surroundings blaze with countless lamps and candles, and the crowds swell from the daily thousands to well over a hundred thousand, drawn from far and wide. Each day carries its own character: one given to the fair itself, with _dhamal_ danced to the beat of the _dhol_; one to the lighting of the lamps (_chiraghan_) from which the festival takes its name; and one to the ceremonial changing of the _chadar_, the cloth spread over the grave — the whole accompanied by qawwali, by _langar_ (the free distribution of food), and by great gatherings of devotees. The festival has ancient associations with the neighbouring Shalimar Gardens, and it belongs as much to the shared popular culture of Lahore as to the shrine alone; for generations it has been one of the city’s defining occasions, a meeting of the sacred and the festive in which the memory of the poet-saint is renewed in light, music, and dance.

Historically the Mela Chiraghan was among the very largest fairs of the Punjab — at its height, it is said, rivalling even the great spring festival of Basant — and it was long celebrated in and around the Shalimar Gardens themselves, the lamps spilling out from the shrine into the royal garden. Under Sikh rule in the nineteenth century Maharaja Ranjit Singh and his court are remembered as joining in the festivities, a mark of how far the saint’s appeal reached beyond the Muslim community. In time the fair was drawn back within the shrine and its precincts, but it remains a vast and joyous occasion, binding the memory of the poet-saint to the civic life of Lahore and gathering Muslims and non-Muslims, the devout and the merely festive, into the same three nights of light.

## Devotion and Significance

The devotion at Madho Lal Hussain draws on everything the saint was: the ecstatic malang who danced where others prayed; the poet whose kafis put the love of God into the language of the people; and the lover whose bond with a Hindu youth became a byword for a love beyond division. Pilgrims come for the blessing of a saint famous for _ishq-e-haqiqi_, the “real love” of the Divine, and for the sense — as the surveyors of the shrine record the local belief — that Hussain “belonged to another world,” that his whole life was unlike that of ordinary men. Those who could not know him in the flesh, it is said, come to love him through his stories and his songs. His verse is sung at the shrine and across the Punjab; his festival is one of the largest in the land; and his joint name stands, in a country marked by communal division, as a quiet insistence that the highest sanctity and the deepest love recognise no barrier of caste or creed. In this the darbar is not only the tomb of a great saint but a living argument about what devotion, and what love, can mean.

## Legacy

Shah Hussain — Madho Lal Hussain — occupies a place all his own in the memory of the Punjab. He is honoured as the father of the Punjabi kafi and so as the fountainhead of a tradition of Sufi song that runs through Bulleh Shah and Sultan Bahu down to the present day; as a Malamati saint who dramatised, in his own scandalous freedom, the truth that the love of God is worth more than the good opinion of the world; and as the lover of Madho, whose story has made his shrine a symbol of harmony between Muslim and Hindu, and of a love that death itself could not part. Amid the crowds and the lamps of the Mela Chiraghan, and in every kafi sung in his voice, the poet-saint of Lahore lives on.

  servants and "ایک لاکھ پچپن ہزار" — one hundred and fifty-five thousand murids — of whom sixteen
  became famed khalifas, four titled Gharib, four Diwan, four Khaki and four Bilawal, with the
  burial place of each given: the Gharibs at Ratti Mittha next to Wazirabad, at لنگرہی [OCR?] in
  Wazirabad district, at جیلا پور [OCR?] in the Deccan, and beside Shah Husain's own grave; the
  Diwans — the first being Shaikh Madho — with Diwan Gorakh and Allah Diwan buried at Lahore and
  Diwan Bakhshi at Bijapur in the Deccan; the Khakis (Maula Bakhsh Khaki and Khaki Shah at Lahore,
  Khaki Shah at Wazirabad, Haidar Khaki in the Deccan); and the Bilawals (Shah Rang Bilawal, Budhu
  Bilawal and Shah Mast Bilawal at Lahore, Shah Bilawal in the Deccan) (p. 55, folio 43).
- **A named khalifa and a second-generation murid.** Entry ۲۳۰: Haji Jamiat's "بیعت بخدمت شاہ رنگ
  [OCR?] بلاول حضرت لال حسین لاہوری کے خلیفہ کی خدمت میں تھی" — his allegiance was to Shah
  Rang[OCR?] Bilawal, khalifa of Hazrat Lal Husain Lahori (p. 271, folio 259): a transmission line
  out of Shah Husain that the row can cite.
- **His writings**: two works have so far come to light — *Kafian* in Punjabi and *Risala-e Tahniyat*
  in Persian prose, presenting the problems of tasawwuf in an easy manner; the full text, edited and
  introduced by Muhammad Iqbal Mujaddidi, was printed in Majalla Maarif, Azamgarh, August 1970 and
  again in Sahifa, Lahore, July 1972 (p. 56, folio 44). Nine further sources are listed (pp. 56–57,
  folios 44–45), including Dara Shikoh's *Hasanat al-Arifin* pp. 18, 74, and the editor's own
  article "مادھو لال حسین" in the Urdu Daira-e Maarif-e Islamia of Punjab University. The
  bibliography repeats both: item ۲۲۲ "محمد اقبال مجددی : مادھو لال حسین مقالہ مشمولہ اردو دائرہ
  المعارف اسلامیہ دانش گاہ پنجاب لاہور" (p. 303, folio 291) and item ۱۰۳ "شاہ حسین لاہوری :
  تحقیقت[OCR?]۔ مرتبہ محمد اقبال مجددی مشمولہ مجلہ صحیفہ لاہور جولائی ۱۹۷۲ء" (p. 297, folio 285,
  title flagged); the editor's article list adds "۴- شاہ حسین لاہوری کی ایک غیر مطبوعہ فارسی تصنیف
  تہنیت۔ معارف اگست ۱۹۷۰ء" — an **unpublished Persian work titled "تہنیت"** known to him in
  manuscript, *Ma'arif*, August 1970 (p. 307, folio 295).

**Saint — Shaikh Madho, entry no. 24**
- Of Shaikh Husain Lahori's khalifas, "اپنے پیر روشن ضمیر کے محبوب و مطلوب تھے" — he was his pir's
  beloved and desired one (p. 64, folio 52).
- **Origin**: "قصبہ شاہدرہ میں ان کے باپ ماں بہ قوم کے برہمن تھے رہتے تھے" — his father and mother,
  Brahmins by caste, lived in **qasba Shahdara** (p. 64, folio 52).
- **Conversion**: "حضرت حسین ان کے حسن و جمال پر بدل و جان عاشق ہو گئے اور بجذب محبت اپنی طرف کھینچا"
  — Hazrat Husain fell in love with his beauty with heart and soul and drew him by the attraction of
  love; so Madho too came to him with sincere devotion "اور مشرف بہ اسلام ہوئے", and was honoured
  with Islam, and through training became a perfect wali (pp. 64–65, folios 52–53). Separately
  sourced by the editor: "میاں مادھو نے اسلام قبول کر لیا تھا" (Haqiqat al-Fuqara p. 82) (p. 65,
  folio 53, حاشیہ 1).
- **He became the shrine's mujawir**: "جب حسین رح نے وفات پائی۔ ان کے مزار پر مجاور رہے" — when
  Husain died he remained a *mujawir* at his mazar (p. 65, folio 53).
- **The joint shrine's one textual warrant**: the editor's footnote, sharpening the death to 22 Zi
  al-Hijja 1056 AH / 1646 CE, adds "اور اپنے شیخ کے احاطہ میں دفن ہوا" — **and he was buried within
  his shaikh's enclosure** (p. 65, folio 53, حاشیہ 2). That single clause is the book's warrant for
  the archive's joint shrine, and it is the **editor's**, not the author's.
- **The editor doubts Madho's very historicity** (chunk 005, entry no. 24, pp. 64–65). The argument
  itself sits under that chunk's *Arguments and interpretations* heading and is **not carried in this
  CSV** — read it on the page before the row is rewritten.

**Legends & Miracles**
- **Shrine practice recorded in a footnote**: Shaikh Muhammad Tahir Lahori (d. 1040 AH), an extremely
  shariah-bound khalifa of the Mujaddid Alf-e Sani, is quoted as saying "اگر مجھے علمائے ظاہر کے
  طعنوں کا خدشہ نہ ہوتا تو میں اکثر شیخ حسین کے مزار پر جا کر استمداد کرتا" — "If I did not fear the
  taunts of the exoteric ulama, I would often go to Shaikh Husain's mazar and seek help"

  accepted (p. 56, folio 44, حاشیہ 1).

### Doubts the notes raise on this row

**B14. `madho-lal-hussain` — three death years, a fourth numeral in the wrong place, and the joint
burial never stated.** Shah Husain: text **1008 AH**; *Miftah al-Arifin* **1013 AH**; editor concludes
**999 AH** (p. 55, folio 43 and حاشیہ 2). The chronogram quatrain closing the entry is printed with
"**۹۴۵ھ [OCR?]**" beside it (p. 56, folio 44) — the year the text gives for his **birth**; it must not
be read as a death date. Age at first instruction: seven in the text, ten in 955 AH per
*Haqiqat al-Fuqara* (p. 54, folio 42 and حاشیہ 4) (chunk 004). Madho: birth 983, death 1056, age 73 —
internally consistent, but against the editor's 999 for Shah Husain it leaves Madho as mujawir for 57
years, and everything about Madho rests on *Haqiqat al-Fuqara*, **the book the editor elsewhere calls
careless and uncritical** (chunk 005). **The book names شیخ مادھو as the first of Shah Husain's four
Diwan khalifas and his "معشوق و محبوب" and the editor's article is titled "مادھو لال حسین"
(pp. 55, 57; folios 43, 45), but no chunk states that the two are buried together or that a single
shrine holds them.** Confirm at Madho's own entry, printed p. 52.
