## Overview

At Hazarkhwani, on the edge of Peshawar, stands the mausoleum of Rahman Baba (Abdur Rahman Mohmand, c. 1653–1711) — the most beloved poet of the Pashto language and the great mystic voice of the Pashtuns. For three centuries his _Diwan_ has been the book most cherished after the Qur’an in countless Pashtun homes, its verses of love, humility, and the vanity of the world quoted as proverbs and sung by musicians from Peshawar to Kandahar. His shrine is the spiritual and literary heart of the Pashto-speaking world, and a traditional gathering place of poets and singers.

## The Life of the Poet-Saint

Abdur Rahman was born around 1653 near Peshawar, into the Mohmand tribe of the Pashtuns; he is remembered as a man of humble means and retiring temperament, a dervish who shunned the powerful and lived in poverty and prayer. Little of his outward life is documented — he was no courtier and left no chronicle of himself — but his verse reveals a soul of deep Sufi devotion, gentle, self-effacing, and wholly turned toward God. He belonged to the broad Sufi current of his time and place, and by tradition performed the Hajj; he died around 1711 and was buried at Hazarkhwani, where his shrine arose.

## The Poetry

Rahman Baba’s fame rests entirely on his _Diwan_ — a body of Pashto verse of unmatched sweetness and moral depth. His great themes are the love of God, the transience of the world, humility, tolerance, and the dignity of the poor; he preaches, again and again, that one should return kindness for cruelty and sow good rather than harm. One of his most quoted teachings runs, in sense:

[“Sow flowers, that your garden may bloom about you; the thorns you plant will wound your own feet.”]

His poetry is at once popular and profound — simple enough for the unlettered to carry in memory, deep enough to nourish the mystic — and it has done more than any other book to shape the moral imagination of the Pashtuns. His verses are sung to the _rabab_ and recited in gatherings, and his name is spoken with an affection given to no other Pashto poet.

## The Shrine

Rahman Baba’s mausoleum at Hazarkhwani has long been a centre of pilgrimage and of Pashto literary life, where poets and singers gather and an annual urs is held in his honour with music and _mushaira_ (poetic assembly) alongside the rites of devotion. The shrine was severely damaged by a bombing in 2009 — an attack on the tolerant, musical devotion it represents — but it was subsequently rebuilt, and pilgrims and lovers of his poetry returned. It remains the great shrine of the Pashto-speaking world.

## Legacy

Rahman Baba stands to Pashto as Bhittai to Sindhi and Bulleh Shah to Punjabi: the poet who gave his people’s language its deepest and most beloved voice. His gospel of love, humility, tolerance, and the emptiness of worldly pride has been for three centuries a moral compass for the Pashtuns, and his _Diwan_ a household treasure. His shrine at Peshawar — rebuilt after violence — endures as the hearth of Pashto poetry and the resting place of a saint whose verse still teaches a gentler way.
