## Overview

In the middle of Miani Sahib Graveyard in Lahore — one of the city's great burial grounds — stands the shrine of Hazrat Khawaja Feroz-ud-Din Gharib Nawaz Chishti Nizami, a Chishti Nizami Qadria saint remembered above all for a generosity so unstinting that he was called *Sakhi Lajpal*, "the generous one who never turns anyone away." The field survey that is this entry's only source gives no street address, no named section of the graveyard, and no coordinates — only that the shrine sits within Miani Sahib's grounds, surrounded on all four sides by the graves that make up the wider burial ground.

## The Life of Hazrat Khawaja Feroz-ud-Din

The survey is explicit that Hazrat Khawaja Feroz-ud-Din was his real name — not a title conferred later. It gives his birthplace as Kandhari Bazaar, in the Rang Mahal quarter of Lahore, and names his parents: his mother, Mehtab Bibi, described as an exceptionally devout woman who raised him carefully, and his father, Hassan Din, a poor and hardworking man already connected to the Chishti Qadria order, whose labour left him little time to oversee his son's education directly. On his birth year, the survey is genuinely unclear rather than silent: it states he was "born in the latter part of the 1900s (19th century)" — a phrase that contradicts itself, since the 1900s and the 19th century do not refer to the same decades. No other figure for his birth is given anywhere in the survey, so his birth year is left here as the source leaves it: stated, but internally inconsistent, and not resolvable from this material alone.

Coming of age, he pursued the righteous path on his own initiative. At fifteen he pledged allegiance (*baith*) to Hazrat Khawaja Sheikh Muhammad Mustafa Chishti-Nizami — an event the survey records as arriving through a dream, in which his future spiritual master appeared to him and said, "Your share is with us; come and take your share." He woke the next morning and travelled to his master's seat of learning on Barki Road, Lahore, to complete the pledge in person.

He never left his father's trade: the survey describes him working as a shoemaker in Rang Mahal Bazaar for the whole of his life, making and selling shoes, while living with deliberate simplicity. His nights, from Tahajjud until Fajr, were given to the remembrance of Allah; his days to his shop and, increasingly, to the stream of people who came to him. His spiritual master at one point asked him to visit only on Fridays rather than daily, out of concern for the length of the journey Feroz-ud-Din was making to reach him.

## Pledge of Allegiance and Succession

According to the survey, all of his religious learning came from his one spiritual master and guide, Hazrat Khawaja Sheikh Muhammad Mustafa Chishti-Nizami, whom he served closely for the rest of the older man's life. When Hazrat Khawaja Sheikh Muhammad Mustafa died in 1390 AH, he called Feroz-ud-Din to him in his final moments, conferred on him the cloak of succession (*khilafat*), and named him his spiritual successor. Thousands are said to have pledged allegiance to Feroz-ud-Din after he received this khilafat. He continued, the survey states, to mark his own master's annual Urs with honour throughout his life, without wanting elaborate invitation or attention drawn to the observance itself — a preference for a low profile that, per the survey, extended to his everyday manner: he asked disciples to visit on Fridays but treated anyone arriving on another day with the same humility.

He died suddenly of a liver ailment on Friday, 27 November 1981, and his disciples built his shrine at Miani Sahib, where — the survey says — a crowd of visitors is present at all times.

## The Shrine

The survey describes the shrine itself as built "very beautifully," using expensive marble and fine materials throughout its construction. Inside, it houses rare relics associated with sacred personalities from around the world, which his disciples are said to have brought back, preserved, and put on display over time — a detail the survey offers as part of what gives the shrine its splendour, distinct from the devotional life described below. The wider graveyard surrounds the shrine on every side.

## Devotional Life and the Annual Urs

A spiritual gathering (*mehfil*) — combining the remembrance of Allah (*zikr-e-Ilahi*) with praise of the Prophet (*Naat-e-Rasool*) — is held every few days, together with free food (*langar*). Thursdays draw people specifically to light lamps (*diyas*) and make vows (*manatein*). The survey estimates daily footfall at 750 to 1,000 visitors.

The annual Urs, per the survey, includes lamp-lighting, large-scale langar, the changing of the shrine's ceremonial cloth (*chadar tabdeeli*), ritual washing (*gusal*), further mehfilein, qawwali, and sustained zikr-o-azkar — drawing, the survey estimates, some 175,000 to 225,000 pilgrims. The survey does not, however, give the calendar date on which the Urs falls, despite describing its observances in detail.

A reciprocal relationship binds this shrine to that of Feroz-ud-Din's own master: his disciples attend Hazrat Khawaja Sheikh Muhammad Mustafa's Urs in large numbers, and in turn the disciples of Sheikh Muhammad Mustafa take an active part in organising Feroz-ud-Din's own Urs — a link the survey attributes to the scale of the following Feroz-ud-Din drew in in his own right after receiving khilafat.

## Custodianship

The shrine is maintained, per the survey, by his special disciples (*mureedeen-e-khas*) and their descendants, who took over its administration immediately after his death and continue to run it today; some workers are privately employed to attend to the shrine at all times.
