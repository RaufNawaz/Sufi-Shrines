## Overview

Shikarpur's only functioning Sikh gurdwara today is known locally as Gurdwara Sach Khand Sahib, sometimes rendered Gurdwara Sachkhand Hazoor Sahib — a modest, comparatively recent building serving the small Nanakpanthi Sikh community of this historic Sindhi trading city. It should not be confused with the much larger and more famous "Sach Khand" gurdwara of the Punjab, near Farooqabad/Chuharkana, which commemorates Guru Nanak's celebrated sugar-miracle with Bhai Mardana — a wholly different site, wrongly linked to Shikarpur in some casual accounts.

## Rebuilding After Partition

Like almost every gurdwara in Sindh, whatever pre-Partition Sikh place of worship once stood in Shikarpur was lost when the city's substantial Sikh and Hindu populations departed for India in 1947; its exact location and fate went unrecorded. The present gurdwara is a recent structure, built only within the last decade or so by the city's much-reduced Sikh community, and registered with the Evacuee Trust Property Board as a functional gurdwara on a plot of just over 3,000 square feet.

## Congregation and Practice

Shikarpur's resident Khalsa Sikh population numbers only a handful of families; the wider congregation drawing on the gurdwara is composed mainly of Nanakpanthi Sehajdhari Sikhs — Sindhi Hindus devoted to Guru Nanak who have long formed part of the region's syncretic religious landscape, and who learn Gurmukhi in order to read the Guru Granth Sahib. The Guru Granth Sahib is given daily *Prakash* (ceremonial opening) at the gurdwara, and a round-the-clock *langar* is maintained — a continuation of the communal-kitchen tradition central to Sikh gurdwaras everywhere. As of the mid-2010s the gurdwara's president and principal *sewadar*/granthi was Vikash Singh Khalsa, a Nanakpanthi convert to the Khalsa; under his leadership the gurdwara took part in a March 2017 meeting at Kandhkot that established the All Sindh Balochistan Gurdwara Management Committee, linking Shikarpur into the wider network of Sindh's surviving Sikh institutions.

## A Possible Second Site

Some sources also describe a separate or possibly older Shikarpur site called Gurdwara Sri Guru Nanak Sahib — locally rendered "Puj Udasian Samadha Ashram" — featuring a red-painted wooden *baradari*, the samadhs of past Udasi ascetics, and a banyan tree linked to a Guru Nanak legend. Whether this represents the same building as Sach Khand Sahib under an older name, an adjoining shrine within the same compound, or a genuinely separate site nearby is not clearly resolved in the available sources, and the question is noted here rather than resolved.

### Person-level material: Guru Nanak

- **Saint**
  - Back matter: "1. Guru Nanak (1469-1539)", "the first Guru, is believed to be the founder of Sikhism"; after lost text, "pilgrimages including Banaras, Hardwar, Mecca and Baghdad, challenging their rituals, dogma, and the caste system" (p. 314, folio 288). Birth at Talwandi/Nankana is in the Nankana Sahib group.
  - Nanak "spent a major portion of his life in present-day Pakistan. He was born here", first preached travelling this region, "and eventually settled in a small village which is now on the Pakistani side of the border" (p. 16, folio not stated).
  - Family: "Nanak belonged to the Bedi caste" (p. 100, folio 82); Iqbal Qaiser — "Baba Nanak had two sons, Sri Chand and Sri Lachman"; Sri Chand "became an ascetic and never married"; the lineage "is therefore preserved through his younger son", called Bedi (p. 44, folio 26). The Udasi sect "is believed to have been founded by the eldest son of Guru Nanak, Sri Chand", who, "Ignored by his father when it was time to appoint the next Guru", founded his own sect (p. 36, folio 18); Iqbal Qaiser calls it the first "anti-Guru" movement; Daleep says Sri Chand never accepted the Gurudom of Guru Angad Dev (p. 38, folio 20).
  - Author's reconstruction of his youth: Mehta Kalu calls the family "Bedis ... the ancient orators of the Vedas" and himself "an accountant"; Nanak herded buffaloes, which once entered a field of "Raj Kumar" [OCR?] (p. 63, folio 45; p. 64, folio 46); he was found hiding "in the growe [OCR?] [grove] of wax [OCR?] [waan] trees outside town" by his father and "his employer and friend, Rai Bular" (p. 64, folio 46); he sits at dusk under "this waan tree", cloths "tied as supplication" on its branches, "about two furlongs" from home, hearing the azaan and temple bells (p. 59, folio 41; p. 60, folio 42); he refuses the sacred thread ("janeu") proposed by his father and "the local priest of Rai Bhoi di Talwindi", which "caused a furore", and "Respectable Hindus started to avoid the Bedi" family (pp. 61–62, folios 43–44); his mentor is "Sayyed Hassan, a Muslim Sufi, who lived near his house", who argued "Islam teaches one to adopt the middle path" (p. 62, folio 44). Marriage to "Sulakhni", "the daughter of Moolchand Khatri from the village of Pakhoke near Gurdaspur"; "Soon after their marriage the young couple was blessed with a son who was named Sri Chand" (p. 67, folio 49); at Sultanpur he worked for "the Governor of Sultanpur as a manager of the Nawyh's [OCR?] [Nawab's] provision store", and a year after Sulakhni joined him "they were blessed with another son, whom they named Lachman Das" (p. 68, folio 50).
  - Author's reconstruction at Sultanpur: storekeeper to "the Governor of Sultanpur, Daulat Lodh[i] Khan"; nobles attacked him over "'There is no Hindu, no Muslim'" and alleged theft, but "when an audit was conducted, the accounts were shown to be in perfect order" (p. 106, folio 88); every night Mardana played the rubab while Nanak sang — a daily feature "for the rest of their [lives] and also for the Sikh community"; his audience "included both Hindus and Muslims" (p. 106, folio 88; p. 107, folio 89). He tells Lalo he "used to be in charge of the food store at the court of the Diwa[n] [OCR?] of Sultanpur" and quit to "take up this garb and travel the world in search of Truth" (pp. 104–105, folios 86–87); his garb deliberately mixed traditions — a red, not green, dervish's choga, a fakir's white belt, a cap partly covered by a turban, wooden sandals — "a conscious effort by Nanak to dilut[e] his religio[us] identity" (pp. 105–106, folios 87–88). "It is widely believed that it was in Sultanpur that the religious life of Nanak began" (p. 54, folio 36).
  - Travels: east "all the way to Bengal", south "to the island of Sri Lanka", north incl. "Kashmir, Nepal" and "Tibet", then west "to Mecca and returned to settle at Kartarpur"; mostly on foot, "about twenty-four years" (p. 54, folio 36); Nanak and Mardana "would not return to a sedentary life for twenty-four years" and "would travel thousands of kilometres, that too on foot" (p. 108, folio 90). Nanak "preferred to stay on the outskirts" of villages, "a recurrent theme", in tune with "moderation" and "a middle course" (p. 53, folio 35); in the reconstruction he prefers sleeping under trees to caravanserais (p. 120, folio 102). "Whenever he would visit an area he would visit its religious shrines, Muslim, Hindu and of any other denomination" (p. 37, folio 19).
  - Reconstructed journeys: the first goal is Saidpur, the city "with the grandest Hindu temples" (p. 108, folio 90); then "the historic city of Multan", "this holy city in the southern region of Punjab" (p. 122, folio 104) — he "was particularly fascinated" by Multan because "it signified the religious syncretism that he yearned for" (Mecca sacred only to Muslims, Benaras to Hindus, "Multan was revered by all"), and "was disappointed to see that the religious syncretism he had imagined was non-existent" (p. 123, folio 105); the caravanserai of "Sajjan and Gaijan [OCR?] [Gajjan]" near "the village of Makhdoom Pur Pahuran", where the innkeepers plot to rob and kill them, Nanak sings "The herons in their white feathers dwell in the sacred shrines of pilgrimage", Sajjan confesses, and the house becomes "a House of God" — "This was to become the first Gurdwara of the Sikh religion" (pp. 123–126, folios 105–108); a forest mound near Pakpattan where he meets Sheikh Kamal (p. 139, folio 121) and hears of Farid "while I was living in Sultanpur and also while I was in Lahore" (p. 141, folio 123); Sheikh Ibrahim's questioning on a mound "a few kilometres away from the city and the shrine" — "If I say I am a Hindu, I would be telling a lie. I am also not a Muslim", "a servant of God and a brother of true devotees" (p. 146, folio 128), the poem "Launch ye the boat of austere contemplation …" (pp. 148–149, folios 130–131); home via Lahore to Kanganpur after Bengal and Sri Lanka, where villagers take them for thugs "who had come from Kasur" and stone them (pp. 162–164, folios 144–146); Manakdeke; Sialkot with Maula Karar, where Nanak sends Mardana to buy "two paisas worth of truth and two paisas worth of untruth" and Karar's chits read "Life is false" and "Death is truth" (pp. 187–188, folios 161–162), then shrines around Sialkot (p. 189, folio not stated); Deepalpur, where he will not "go into to the city" for a temple bhajan (p. 207, folio 181); Nuri the leper (pp. 208–209, folios 182–183), "thirteen years" from home (p. 208, folio 182); the sea-god's cave temple with a ship's captain who turns out to be "a Jew" (p. 220, folio 194); a Shiva temple where he watches milk poured over the shiv ling "with scepticism", sits with "his feet dangling in the holy pond", tells the priest "I believe in Shiva. He is as much my guru as he is yours" and "My Shiva is the Supreme Creator", and sings the aarti "under a lone tree on the mountain facing the temple" with Mardana (pp. 240–245, folios not stated / 215–219); Nanak and Mardana leave Talwandi "towards the cold North" while Sulakhni and "her two sons Sri Chand and Lachman Das" watch (p. 239, folio 213); Makkah for the Hajj and Baghdad on the way back, and a finding that "there was no single form of Islam" — Bengal Muslims venerating "Devi Durga", caste in "South India", sleeping in the Kaaba in "Arabia", Shia paintings in "Baghdad" of the Prophet and "All. [OCR?] [Ali]" (p. 287, folio 261). "It is recorded in Sikh heritage that he came to the village of Kolachi and then visited the temple of a sea god for which he had to take a boat and travel to an island neighbouring Kolachi" (p. 229, folio not stated).
  - The author: Nanak "must have visited" Tibba Haji Deen (then Kothewal) "around the end of the fifteenth century" (p. 150, folio 132); in Benaras "there are legends that Nanak met Kabir but that too is historically not proven" (p. 153, folio 135).
  - Succession: "On his deathbed, he appointed not his son but his most loyal student as his spiritual successor" (p. 16, folio not stated); "one of his favourite disciples, who beca[me] famous as Guru Angad" (p. 100, folio 82); "Guru Nanak preferred Angad Dev over his son Sri Chand" (p. 273, folio not stated); Guru Angad "was given the title of Guru Angad by Guru Nanak when he appointed him as his successor bypassing his Progeny" (p. 314, folio 288); Nanak asked Angad Dev "to move to Khadur as opposed to Kartarpu! _yahib [OCR?] [Kartarpur Sahib] where Nanak was settled. Author's reconstruction: "Bhai Lehna" (also "Lebna" [OCR?]) of Khadur, "Born into an orthodox family", came to Kartarpur Sahib instead of "the temple of Durga where he was headed" (p. 297, folio 271) and "decided to collect the poetry of Nanak in written form" when Punjabi was "an oral language" with "no script" (damaged) (p. 299, folio 273); the sons disappointed him — "Lachman … had become a businessman", Sri Chand "highly inspired by the yogis" (p. 297, folio 271); Sri Chand objects that Lehna "still worships Durga in his house", and Sulakhni says "You should listen to your blood" (p. 300, folio 274); Nanak tells Sri Chand "it is not in your destiny to be my spiritual descendant. - **Significance** (the author's arguments)
  - Nanak "vehemently spoke against organised religion", "abhorred the concept of associating miracles with religious personalities", yet "his biography is nothing but a story of his miracles" (p. 16, folio not stated); "a couple of successors later 'Guruhood' became a family affair" (p. 17, folio not stated); he "was repulsed by the concept of making a larger-than-life figure out of a religious leader" and "comes across as a rationalist" (p. 112, folio 94; verse cited to Dhillon, p. vii, at p. 113, folio 95).
  - "Guru Nanak spoke vehemently against religious institutions and rituals. **Life (c. 042)**
- "دوازده کروهے بتاله برلب دریاے راوی مکان بابا نانک است که تا حال اولادش درانجا سکونت دارد" (p. 520).
- He was "قافله سالار مسالک حقیقت و مشعله دار طریق طریقت"; he composed "اشعار هندی" on the truth of God and set out divine unity "بعبارات واضحه و استعارات لایحه" (p. 520).
- Birth: "گویند" he was born "درسنه هزار و پانصد و بست و شش بکرماجیت مطابق هشتصد و هشتاد هجری درزمان سلطان بهلول لودی"; for the birthplace see Talwandi below (p. 520).
- "درخورد سالگی(۱) علامات کشف و کرامات و ایات خارق عادات از و بمنصه ظهور رسید" (editor's footnote variant: "ده سالگی") (p. 520).
- He travelled most of the world, then came to "قصبه بتاله", married ("کدخدا گردید"), and settled "دردهی از دیهات بتاله برآب دریاے راوی"; the village is not named (p. 520).
- "عالم عالم خلایق از اطراف ممالک آمده مرید شدند" (p. 520). "مردانه نام مطرب" sang his verses and drew people "در دام عقیدت" (p. 520).
- Death: "درعهد سلطنت سلیم شاه افغان درعمر مابین هفتاد و هشتاد سالگی" (p. 520).
- Succession: he had a son, "لکهمیداس نام خلف رشید", but "چون دولت معنوی نصیب او نبود" he passed him over and gave "لهنه نام کهتری عرف ترهن[OCR?]" the title Guru Angad and made him successor "دروقت نزع" (p. 520).

**His followers (c. 041, 045, 046)**
- "از مریدان و معتقدان بابا نانک اکثرے صاحب حال و مقبول المقال و اهل ریاضات و مستجاب الدعوات می باشند" (p. 519).
- "خلاصه عبادت اینطایفه مطالعه اشعار مرشد خویش است که بسرود و نغمه میگویند" (p. 519). Many have put away worldly ties; kin and stranger, friend and foe are the same to them, and they live "با دوستان یکرنگ و با دشمنان بی جنگ" (p. 519).
- "اعتقادی که این فریق بر مقتداے خویش دارند در طوایف دیگر کم نشان یافته میشود" (p. 519). Their master's name is always "ورد زبان"; they count serving travellers ("درخدمت صادر و وارد") as "عبادت عظمی" (p. 519). If someone arrives at midnight and speaks Baba Nanak's name, they treat him as brother and friend and serve him, even a stranger, a thief, a highwayman or an evildoer (p. 519).
- In the section "درویشان هند" (p. 567): "چهارمین صنف اوداسی از معتقدان بابا نانک", who worship the Creator "بطریق و ائین مقتدای خویش"; "خلاصه عبادت این گروه مطالعه اشعار مرشد خویش است که بسرود و نغمه میگویند و زمزمه دلفریب می برارند(۱)", and so "خود را و مستمعان را بهره ور و نصیبه ور می سازند" (p. 566). The chunk names no site, date, life event, tomb or gurdwara (p. 566).
- Forty kos north of the capital is Sambhal, with "هرمندر", an old temple; "و پیوست ان نانک متا جاے است که مریدان و معتقدان بابا نانک هجوم نموده نیایش بجا آورند" (p. 553). *basis: Nanak Mata, a non-archive site outside Pakistan, mapped person-level in c. 045 (brief item 5)*
