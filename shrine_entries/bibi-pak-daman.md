## Overview

In the heart of old Lahore, a short way from Empress Road and the old Qila Gujjar Singh, stands the shrine of Bibi Pak Daman — “the pure, chaste ladies” — one of the oldest and most venerated sacred sites in the city, and among the busiest, drawing many thousands of pilgrims each day and immense crowds through the month of Muharram. Within its enclosures lie the graves of six holy women, remembered for their piety and for the miracle by which they are said to have chosen death over dishonour, together with the grave of their devoted maidservant. For centuries the darbar has been a place of prayer, vow, and refuge — cherished above all by women — and it stands, across sectarian lines, as one of the clearest expressions in South Asian Islam of the veneration of female sanctity. Its story is inseparable from a question that has been debated for more than a thousand years, and to which we shall return: who, exactly, were the Bibis?

## The Saints: Who the Bibis Were

Two great traditions about the identity of the six women endure side by side, and the shrine holds meaning in both. The older and by far the more popular tradition — the one held by most pilgrims, and reflected in the shrine’s traditional founding in the first century of Islam — identifies the chief of the six as Sayyida Ruqayya bint Ali, a daughter of Hazrat Ali and sister of Hazrat Abbas the standard-bearer, and the wife of Muslim ibn Aqeel. With her, this tradition holds, were kinswomen and companions of the House of the Prophet (the Ahl al-Bayt) — among them women remembered as Bibi Hoor and Bibi Noor of the line of Abi Talib — six in all, together with a faithful camel-driver named Basheer. By this account the women were survivors of the tragedy of Karbala who made their way across the length of the Muslim world to Lahore, and the shrine is a memorial to the family of the Prophet. Because of this the darbar is a place of especially intense devotion for Shia Muslims and for all who love the Ahl al-Bayt, and its whole atmosphere turns toward the remembrance of Karbala.

A second tradition — advanced by the shrine’s own custodial family and by a line of scholars including Mufti Ghulam Sarwar (author of _Khazinat al-Asfiya_ and _Hadiqat al-Auliya_), Pir Ghulam Dastgir Nami, and Hafeez Ullah Manzar — holds that the six were the daughters of the great saint Sayyid Ahmad Tokhta Tirmidhi, “Murshid-e-Punjab,” and that they lived and died in Lahore in the early thirteenth century, at the time of the Mongol invasions. What the two traditions share is, in the end, greater than what divides them: reverence for six women of extraordinary holiness, the memory of their choice of death over dishonour, and the miracle by which the earth itself is said to have received them. Both, too, agree on the names by which the six are known — Bibi Haj, Bibi Taj, Bibi Noor, Bibi Hoor, Bibi Gauhar, and Bibi Shahbaz (or Shahnaz).

## The Journey from Karbala

In the popular telling, the story begins in the aftermath of Karbala in 61 AH. The women’s ordeal had begun even before that day: Sayyida Ruqayya was the wife of Muslim ibn Aqeel, the cousin and emissary whom Imam Hussain had sent ahead to Kufa, and who was betrayed and martyred there together with his young sons. After the martyrdom of Hussain himself and his companions on the plain of Karbala, the surviving members of the Prophet’s household were taken captive and, for a year, subjected to the cruelty of Yazid’s court — a captivity so harsh, the tradition holds, that the very sanctities of Islam were trampled. Hazrat Zain al-Abideen, the surviving Imam, is said to have charged Sayyida Ruqayya to depart with a few of the women, so that the faith and the lineage of the Prophet’s house might be carried to safety and continue.

She set out with her companions and, it is told, with the camel-driver Basheer — a man who had served in Yazid’s own palace and who, though he had once spoken harshly to the holy women, was bound to them by a hidden love, and who was trusted precisely because their destination was to be kept from strangers. They journeyed east across the length of the Muslim world until they reached the land of Hind. There, at Lahore, a Hindu raja — named in the local accounts as Mahabrand Bhatti — held sway, and scarcely a Muslim was to be found. As the women arrived, wonders are said to have followed: the idols of the temples toppled and their lamps went dark. The raja, disturbed, sent his son — remembered as Raja Kumar Bikrama Sahay — with soldiers to discover who these strangers were. When the prince came before the women he was overwhelmed by a radiant light and fell senseless; on recovering, he heard from their own lips the tale of Karbala, and was so moved that he embraced Islam, took a new name — remembered variously as Abdullah Shah Khaki or Shaikh Jamal — and remained among them as their servant. When his enraged father came with an army to seize the women, they lifted their hands in prayer, begging God to spare them exposure to strangers; the earth opened and drew them into itself, closing again and leaving only the edges of their veils above ground, over which their graves were later raised. From this — from their guarding of their honour to the last — comes the name by which they are known: Pak Daman, “the pure of hem.”

This is a tradition steeped in the devotion of Karbala, and on the frontispiece of one of its major histories stands the old Persian line of the lovers of the martyrs:

خوشا آنان کہ در خاک و خون غلطیدند

[“Blessed are those who rolled in dust and blood” — a verse of devotion to the martyrs of the House of the Prophet.]

## The Other Tradition: The Daughters of Sayyid Ahmad Tokhta Tirmidhi

The custodial and scholarly tradition tells a different, and no less devout, story. It holds that the six were the daughters of Sayyid Ahmad Tokhta Tirmidhi, a sayyid of the Junaidiya Sufi line whose spiritual descent is traced, through several generations, back to Imam Zain al-Abideen, and whose family had come from Tirmiz in Central Asia by way of Kech Makran to settle at last in Lahore. His curious title, “Tokhta,” is explained by a story of his early discipleship: when his own pir opened the door of his cell and found the young Sayyid Ahmad standing in patient attendance, he addressed him with the Turkish word for “standing” or “ever-present,” and the name stayed with him. He is said to have died around 602 AH — near the beginning of the reign of Sultan Qutb al-Din Aibak — and to be buried in the Lahore quarter still called Chilla Bibiyan, which takes its name from the spiritual retreats (chilla) of his daughters; his tomb is now enclosed within a property of a later custodial family. A Persian chronogram (tarikh) preserved in his memory hails him in verse:

سیّد احمد، شہِ برنا و پیر ٭ پیرِ ہادی، میرِ عالی جاہ

[“Sayyid Ahmad — king of the young and the old; a guiding master, a lord of lofty station.”]

His nobility, sanctity, and gift of miracles were remembered as hereditary in his line, and his descendants spread widely across the Punjab and beyond. Of his daughters, two — Bibi Haj and Bibi Taj — are described as having married, while the others lived as lifelong ascetics, “the Rabia of their age.” Through Bibi Haj, tradition traces a distinguished line of Suhrawardi saints, among them Sultan Hamiduddin Hakim, “Sultan al-Tarikeen,” who is said to have renounced a throne for the life of a faqir and to lie buried at Mubarak, near the old town of Tulamba. In this account it was during the Mongol onslaught — when the army of Chingez Khan, pursuing the fugitive Jalaluddin Khwarazm Shah, sacked Lahore and its people fought for two months before the city fell — that the women, alone in their hermitage outside the walls where their father had lived, prayed to be hidden from the invaders, and the earth enclosed them exactly as in the other telling. The two traditions thus meet in the same miracle even as they differ over the century and the names.

## The Long Debate

That the identity of the Bibis should be so disputed is itself part of the shrine’s history, and the arguments have been rehearsed by scholars for generations. Those who question the Karbala account marshal a formidable case. They observe that, according to the standard histories, the wife of Hazrat Ali who bore Abbas — Umm al-Banin — had four sons and no daughter at all, so that Ruqayya cannot have been her child; that the two women named Ruqayya bint Ali are traditionally commemorated elsewhere, one at a shrine in Damascus and another in Egypt, not in Lahore; and that none of the family of the Prophet who went with Imam Hussain is recorded to have abandoned him before the end — indeed, on the night of Ashura he is said to have released his companions from their oath, and all refused to leave. They point out, too, that in the first century of Islam Lahore lay wholly outside the Muslim world: Islam reached Sind only with the conquests of Muhammad bin Qasim around 93 AH, and Lahore itself came under Muslim rule only with Mahmud of Ghazni in the early eleventh century — so that no Muslim woman, still less a princess of the Prophet’s house, could plausibly have dwelt in Lahore in 61 AH. Finally they note that two of the six names — Gauhar and Shahbaz — are Persian, not Arabic, and are not to be found among the recorded daughters of Aqeel or of Ali.

Yet those who hold to the Karbala tradition answer that the histories are incomplete, that the pilgrims who have searched the shrines of the Arab lands report finding no grave of Ruqayya bint Ali there, and that the living devotion of centuries is itself a kind of testimony. It is striking that even Mufti Ghulam Sarwar — the very scholar who doubted that the women had come from Arabia after Karbala — did not doubt their holiness, writing that whatever their origin, the place is beyond question deeply blessed and its saints full of grace. This, in the end, is where the two traditions come to rest: divided over history, united in reverence.

## The Shrine Complex

The graves lie within enclosed courtyards in the heart of the old city. Tradition places the six Bibis in two adjoining walled enclosures — in the first, by the usual account, Bibi Haj, Bibi Taj, and Bibi Noor; in the second, Bibi Hoor, Bibi Gauhar, and Bibi Shahbaz — their tombs built up in lime and plaster. Counted among them is the grave of their maidservant, Bibi Halima, remembered affectionately as “Bibi Tanoori,” who is said to have been swallowed by the earth along with her mistresses. Within the complex, too, stands a domed masonry tomb of a later age — dated to the eleventh century of the Hijra (about 1600 CE) and belonging to Sayyid Jalaluddin Haider Bukhari, of the celebrated Bukhari sayyids of Uch — a sign of how holy men of later centuries sought to be buried in the women’s company. Around the shrine has gathered a wider sacred neighbourhood: the nearby quarter of Chilla Bibiyan holds the tomb of Sayyid Ahmad Tokhta Tirmidhi, and the devotional literature names other holy figures associated with the place, among them Hazrat Ali Rangrez and Hazrat Alif Shah. Endowments of land (waqf) were attached to the darbar by successive rulers, and its fabric has been rebuilt many times over the centuries; the most recent major renovation was carried out in 2024.

## Data Ganj Bakhsh and the Antiquity of the Shrine

One tradition above all is cited by devotees as proof of the shrine’s great antiquity. It is said that when Data Ganj Bakhsh — the eleventh-century saint Ali Hujwiri, author of _Kashf al-Mahjub_ and the patron saint of Lahore — first came to the city, he would go every Thursday night to the graves of the Bibis Pak Daman and sit in attentive reverence at a respectful distance, out of _adab_ (courtesy) before their sanctity. That so great a saint should honour the shrine in this way is taken to show that the Bibis’ graves were already ancient and holy in his day, and it binds the darbar to Data Darbar, the two shrines standing together at the head of Lahore’s sacred geography. A gentler tale from the same devotional tradition tells of an astrologer who boasted that he could read the hidden and the unseen; the elder Bibi had food prepared for the poor and for travellers and sent him a dish in which fine food lay concealed beneath a plain covering of dry khichri. When the astrologer scorned the humble rice, and then learned what lay beneath it, he was humbled — for a man who claimed knowledge of the unseen had not seen the feast under the khichri — and he became a disciple. Such stories express what the shrine has always meant to ordinary people: a place of hospitality, of humility, and of quiet wonder.

## Custodianship and the Sectarian Question

The shrine’s hereditary caretakers, the mujawirs, are traditionally held to descend from the convert prince — Abdullah Shah Khaki, or Shaikh Jamal — who is said to have built the first shrine over the women’s graves and to have served it for the rest of his life. In the modern era the darbar has been taken into the charge of the Punjab Auqaf Department, which administers it as a Sunni-managed shrine, and, given the scale of the crowds, government security and the Punjab Police are maintained on site at all times. The question of the Bibis’ identity carries a sectarian dimension that persists to this day: because the popular tradition makes the shrine a memorial of the Prophet’s martyred family, it is a focus of intense Shia devotion, while its formal administration and much of its custodial lineage are Sunni. Around this difference old disputes have gathered — over whether the graves are those of Sayyida Ruqayya and the Ahl al-Bayt or of a Punjabi saint’s daughters, and over who may claim the shrine — yet in practice Bibi Pak Daman is revered by Sunni and Shia alike, and the two devotions meet, without merging, in the same courtyards.

## Devotion and Events

Bibi Pak Daman is a living centre of devotion, alive around the clock. Pilgrims come to recite the Fatiha, to tie vows (mannat), and to seek the Bibis’ intercession (waseela) for healing, for children, and for relief from every kind of hardship; many recount cures received here after physicians had given up hope, and tell of the impossible made possible through the women’s grace. Women in particular find in the darbar a sanctuary — a place of comfort and of dignity — and it is above all a women’s shrine in the affection of the city. Every Thursday night the shrine fills for its largest weekly gathering, with langar (free food), qawwali, dhamal, and prayer, and a mosque attached to the shrine holds the five daily prayers. Its supreme season, however, is Muharram, when the sorrow and devotion of Karbala are remembered: enormous crowds converge on the darbar, and it is said that many walk barefoot in groups from their homes to reach it. Field surveys of the shrine record on the order of eight to twelve thousand visitors on an ordinary day, some two hundred and fifty thousand in a month, and — at the height of Muharram — numbers rising toward a million or more.

## A Shrine of Holy Women

In a sacred landscape crowded with the tombs of male saints — Data Ganj Bakhsh, Mian Mir, Madho Lal Hussain, and the rest — Bibi Pak Daman is something rare and precious: a great shrine whose sanctity is that of women, and whose devotion has always been bound up with the lives of women. It is to the Bibis that the mothers of Lahore have brought their deepest hopes and fears for generations — praying for children, for good marriages, for the safety of daughters, for healing, and for protection from harm — and it is women, above all, who fill its courtyards, tie their threads of vow at its screens, and find in it a refuge that the world outside does not always offer them. The very meaning of the shrine turns on a woman’s virtue: the six Bibis are honoured not for conquest or office or learning alone, but for a purity and courage so absolute that they are said to have chosen to be swallowed by the earth rather than be seen by strangers. In this the darbar gives to the women of the city a mirror of their own dignity — a place where female holiness stands at the very centre, and where the qualities most often asked of women, and most often unhonoured, are raised to the rank of the sacred. That a shrine of such scale and antiquity should be dedicated to women is, in the religious geography of the subcontinent, almost without parallel, and it is a large part of what makes Bibi Pak Daman beloved.

## Legacy

Whether one honours the six Bibis as women of the Prophet’s household who carried the memory of Karbala to the edge of the Muslim world, or as the daughters of a Punjabi saint who chose the earth over the gaze of strangers, the meaning of the shrine is the same: it stands for purity, for courage, and for a refusal to bow before injustice at the cost of one’s honour — and it has stood for these, in the devotion of Lahore, across more than a thousand years of retelling. Amid the debates of scholars and the changing life of the city, the darbar of Bibi Pak Daman endures as it always has: a place of refuge and of blessing, one of the deepest spiritual anchors of the old city, and a rare shrine where the sanctity honoured is that of holy women.

The popular name — "the mazar of the **ustad** of the Bibis" — is recorded alongside the actual occupant; **the book does not say the popular name is wrong**, it reports both. Cite it as the book does: the grave of Jalal al-Din Haidar, called by people the ustad's grave.
- **Burial 2, undated.** Appendix row 53: "**خواجہ ایوب قریشی لاہوری قدس سرہ** | لاہور | **قبرستان بی بی پاکدامناں**" (p. 290, folio 278).
- **Burial 3, dated 1274 AH — the author's own father.** "مفتی غلام محمد بن مفتی رحیم اللہ قریشی لاہوری"، "یہ بزرگ احقر غلام سرور مؤلف کتاب کے **والد ماجد** تھے"؛ "وفات حضرت کی سال **ایک ہزار دو سو چہتر** میں واقع ہوئی" (p. 232, folio 220); "اور **مدفن گورستان بی بی پاکدامنان** کی مادہ تاریخ ہے" (p. 233, folio 221). Appendix row 64 gives the same: "گورستان بی بی پاکدامناں | قبرستان بی بی پاکدامناں" (p. 290, folio 278), and the descent is established inside chunk 027 — "مفتی رحمت اللہ (۳) اور ان کے **مفتی رحیم اللہ** (۴) اور ان کے **مفتی غلام محمد** (۵) اور ان کا فرزند **غلام سرور** (۶) مؤلف کتاب ہے" (p. 277, folio 265). **Caution:** the wording of the second clause is broken — the chronogram ("خورشید دین محمد" in the preceding clause) and the burial place have been run together. The burial place is unambiguous; the syntax is not (chunk 022).
- Taken together: **two dated burials in the precinct 258 years apart plus one undated**, one of them explicitly *inside* the khanqah/enclosure rather than in the graveyard — direct evidence that the site functioned as a working burial ground across that span (pp. 183, 232–233, 289–290). **The appendix has no row for the Bibis themselves** (pp. 287–290); every reference to the site in that chunk is as a place where someone else is buried.

**Practices & Events**
- **The first mujawir is named.** The Lahore raja's son converts, is given the name **Jamal**, refuses to return to his old religion, and "**حضرات کی مزار پر مجاور ہو بیٹھا**" (pp. 247–248, folios 235–236).
- **A second body of mujawirs**, recruited from Mongol-era converts: "**چند آدمی یہ کرامت دیکھ کر مشرف باسلام ہوئے اور انہوں نے مجاوری اس مزار گوہر بار کی اختیار کر لی**" (p. 248, folio 236).
- **Conversions as the site's origin story.** "بہت سے لوگ **ان کے قدم کی برکت** سے مشرف باسلام ہوئے" (p. 247, folio 235).
- No urs, no annual observance, no offerings, no langar, no custodianship after the first mujawirs, and no building date anywhere in these chunks (pp. 183, 199–200, 232–233, 247–249, 289–291).

**Legends & Miracles**
- **Legend A (author's first account).** The Bibis were in Syria at Karbala; hearing of Imam Husain's coming, "**یہ بلا ہیں [OCR?] آئیں**", but the end had already come; so "**بخوف خاندان بنی امیہ** کے ہند کو روانہ ہوئیں اور **لاہور کے باہر آکر قیام کیا**" — **no date is given for the arrival** (p. 247, folio 235). The raja — "**مسمی لساؤ [OCR?] راجہ لاہور**" — sent courtiers of his darbar to tell them "**یہاں سے چلی جائیں**", but "جو شخص جاتا۔ **وہاں ہی مشرف باسلام ہو کر رہ جاتا**" (p. 247). Finally his son went, "اس نے بھی **اسلام کا خلعت** پہن لیا" (pp. 247–248). The Bibis then prayed "**ہم کو نامحرموں کی نظر سے بچا لے اور پیوند زمین کر لے**۔ چنانچہ **سب بیبیاں معہ خدام کے پیوند زمین ہو گئیں**" (pp. 247–248, folios 235–236).
- **Legend B (author's second account, from تذکرہ حمیدیہ).** "جب **چنگیز خان مغل** سے **شہزادہ جلال الدین خوارزمی** نے شکست کھائی اور ہند میں بھاگ آیا تو چنگیز خان کی فوج اس کے تعاقب میں پنجاب میں داخل ہوئی۔ **تمام ملک پنجاب انہوں نے غارت کر لیا۔ شہر لاہور کے لوگ دو مہینے تک ان کے ساتھ لڑتے رہے**۔ جب شہر فتح ہوا تو **افسر فوج نے حکم دیا کہ شہر کے لوگ سب کے سب قتل ہوں۔ بلکہ کوئی ذی جان حیوان بھی جانبر نہ ہو**۔ چنانچہ **ہزاروں انسان و حیوان قتل ہوئے**" (p. 248, folio 236). The Bibis were then "**شہر کے باہر اپنے صومعہ میں جہاں ان کا باپ رہتا تھا**"; they prayed "**الٰہی ہم کو پیوند زمین کر لے اور نامحرم مردوں کی صورت نہ دکھلا**"; and when the attackers "**دیوار توڑ کر مکان میں گھسے**" they found no living thing (p. 248, folio 236).
- **The two legends are incompatible and stay side by side**: the same karamat of absorption into the earth, with two different agents of death (a Lahore raja's embassy and his convert son; Chingiz Khan's army) and two different parentages.

**Significance — contradictions, voices, bibliography**
- **The author's own rejection of the Karbala origin.** "**یہ عام روایت لوگوں کی زبانی ہے** اور کتاب **تحفۃ الواصلین** میں بھی یہی مضمون لکھا دیکھا ہے۔ **مگر قیاس نہیں چاہتا کہ واقعہ کربلا کے وقت یہ عرب سے ہند میں آئی ہوں**۔ مگر **ان حضرات کی بزرگی و پر فیض ہونے میں شک نہیں کہ مکان نہایت متبرک ہے**" (p. 248, folio 236). He hedges the second account too: "یہ تقریر جو مشہور نہیں ہے۔ **شاید کوئی اس پر یقین نہ کرے گا**۔ مگر عجب نہیں ہے کہ ایسا ہوا ہو" (p. 248, folio 236).
- **Editor, voice 1 — the first account has no history in it.** "**واقعی یہ قیاس اور زبانی روایت ہے۔ جس کا تاریخ سے کوئی تعلق نہیں ہے**" (p. 248, folio 236, حاشیہ 1); and on the author's source, "**تحفۃ الواصلین کی حیثیت پر ہم شیخ اسمعیل محدث لاہوری کے تحت مفصل بحث کر چکے ہیں**" (p. 248, حاشیہ 2) — the discussion is elsewhere in the edition.
- **Editor, voice 2 — the sack date, rejected outright.** Author: "**واقعہ غارت و قتل لاہور کا چھ سو چودہ ہجری میں واقع ہوا تھا اور سید احمد تختہ کی وفات سنہ چھ سو دو میں ہوئی تھی**" (p. 249, folio 237). Editor: "**۶۱۴ ھ غلط ہے**۔ بلکہ یہ واقعہ **۶۳۹ ھ / ۱۲۴۱ ع** میں ہوا (نامی : رسالہ بی بی پاک دامناں ص ۳ [OCR?])" (p. 249, folio 237, حاشیہ 1). **Both readings must survive into any entry**: 614 AH is the author's, 639 AH / 1241 CE the editor's, and the editor's words for the author's figure are "غلط ہے".
- **Editor, voice 3 — naming this book's author as a source of the hearsay, and dating his change of mind.** "**بی بیاں پاک دامن کے بارے میں یہاں کے مجاوروں نے جو لا یعنی روایات مشہور کر رکھی تھیں**۔ **مولوی نور احمد چشتی مفتی غلام سرور لاہوری اور کنہیا لال** کی **بے اعتدالی** اور **سماعی روایات کے قلم بند کرنے** کی وجہ سے عرصہ دراز سے حقیقت بن چکی تھیں**۔ پھر **خود مفتی صاحب کو ۱۲۹۲ ھ میں جب تذکرہ حمیدیہ دستیاب ہوا تو ان روایات کو شک و شبہ کی نظروں سے دیکھنے لگے**۔ پہلی مرتبہ **پیر غلام دستگیر نامی** مرحوم نے **تاریخ جلیلہ** اور **رسالہ بی بیاں پاک دامن** لکھ کر انہیں حضرت **سید احمد توختہ ترمذی (ف ۶۰۲ ھ / ۱۲۰۵)** کی اولاد ثابت کیا اور **ہمارے نزدیک بھی یہ روایت قرین قیاس معلوم ہوتی ہے**" (p. 249, folio 237, حاشیہ 2). So the editor dates the author's own doubt to **1292 AH** and **sides with the Takhta Tirmizi descent** against the Karbala origin.
- **Editor, voice 4 — a general warning under the father's entry.** "ان **نسا عارفات** کے حالات کے لیے ملاحظہ ہو۔ نامی: **رسالہ بیان پاکدامناں** (**ان کی نسبت خاصی غلط بیانی کی گئی ہے**۔ ملاحظہ ہو **کتاب ہذا تحت بی بیاں پاکدامن**" (p. 199, folio 187, حاشیہ 2) — the editor holds that much of what is said about them is wrong, and names the modern tract he means.
- **Three voices to keep distinct** in any entry: the author's two transmitted legends; the author's own refusal of the Karbala origin coupled with his affirmation that "مکان نہایت متبرک ہے"; and the editor's verdict that the oral tradition has no history in it and that the author himself helped harden it into fact.
- **Editor's reading list for the shrine** (p. 249, folio 237): "(۱) **شہباز الدین [OCR?] : تذکرہ حمیدیہ** مرتبہ نامی (۲) **نامی : (رسالہ) بی بیاں پاک دامن** لاہور **۱۹۲۱ [OCR?] ع** (۳) ایضاً : **تاریخ جلیلہ** (۴) **ہاشمی فرید آبادی : تاریخ لاہور** جزو دوم ص **۵۰۹ [OCR?]** (۵) **کلیم محمد الدین : (رسالہ) بی بیاں پاک دامن** لاہور".
- **A dated monograph in the editor's numbered source list**, item ۲۳۴: "**نامی غلام دستگیر پیر : بی بیاں پاکدامن۔ لاہور ۱۹۳۵ء**" (p. 304, folio 292). Note this **1935** against the "۱۹۲۱ [OCR?] ع" the same editor prints at p. 249 for what may be the same tract by the same author; both as printed. The same author's *Bazurgan-e Lahore* (Lahore 1966), *Tarikh-e Jalila* (Lahore 1960) and *Sawanih Hazrat Shah Muhammad Ghaus Lahori* are items ۲۳۳، ۲۳۱، ۲۳۲ on the same page.
- **Locators.** Index: "**بی بیاں پاک دامن | ۱۸۷، ۲۳۵**" (p. 314, folio 302). Chunk 029 reads folio 235 (est. p. 247) as the entry proper and folio 187 (est. p. 199) as the cross-reference near the Husain Zanjani footnote, and marks that **an inference to check, not a finding**. Folio 235 sits in a run of women's names the index places at folios 235–243 — "زلیخا بی بی (والدہ نظام الدین اولیا) | ۸۲، ۲۳۹" (p. 318), "بی بی اولیاء | ۲۴۰" (p. 313), "راستی بی بی زوجہ صدر الدین عارف | ۲۴۰" (p. 317), "جمال خاتون | ۴۹، ۲۴۲" (p. 315), "بھاگی مائی، لاہوری | ۲۰، ۲۴۳" (p. 314) — but **that a section on women exists is inferred from the locators, not stated**. Folio 187 is shared with "حسین زنجانی | ۱۸۴ ، ۱۸۶ ، ۱۸۷", "حاج بی بی | ۱۸۷", "خضر خان | ۱۸۷", "بہاء الدین بن قطب الدین | ۱۸۷", "اسماعیل بن امام جعفر ؓ | ۱۸۷", "احمد بن علی سید ترمذی | ۱۸۷" (pp. 312–317).
- **Where the book's further material lies.** The numbered entries end at folio 223 (p. 235), but the index cites folios 225–228 (majzubs) and 232–270 (Lahore pirs, women at 235–243), so **at least forty-seven further printed pages of biographical matter follow entry no. 170**, est. pp. 237–282, and this row's material lies there (pp. 235, 312–318). Whether that continuation is the author's, an appendix by him, or the editor's supplement is (not stated). The book's numbering runs to at least **240** and its text to at least folio **264**: "مسلسل شمارہ ۔۔ ۲۴۰ ، صفحہ ۲۶۳ ۔ ۲۶۴" (p. 337, folio 325). Whether the Bibis have an entry in that range is not shown: the **ب** section of the *place* index (p. 333) has no "بی بی پاکدامن" line, and the persons index's ب section lies outside the chunks.

### Doubts the notes raise on this row

**B16. `bibi-pak-daman` — "پانچ لڑکیاں" against six names, twice, and it is the book's own
arithmetic.** The heading and the second list both give six — بی بی حاج، تاج، نور، حور، گوہر، شہباز —
while the text counts **five** in both genealogies (pp. 247–248, folios 235–236; chunk 024). The row's
principal figure is "The six Bibis". **Neither author nor editor remarks on the mismatch, and no name
is marked `[OCR?]`, so this is not a transcription artefact.** Two further cautions on the same row:
the 1016 AH burial of Jalal al-Din Haidar dates only the **subsidiary rauza**, and the book does not
say the popular name of that grave is wrong — an entry must not turn the juxtaposition into a
correction the book did not make, and "بدیوار حریم" cannot be resolved between "built into" and
"against" the enclosure wall (chunk 017); and the six names as a set appear in **the editor's table
only** (p. 291, folio 279; chunk 028).

**I11. `bibi-pak-daman` — where the book's own entry on the Bibis is has never been established.** The
editor cross-refers to it ("ملاحظہ ہو کتاب ہذا تحت بی بیاں پاکدامن", p. 199, folio 187; chunk 019) and
warns that "ان کی نسبت خاصی غلط بیانی کی گئی ہے" — considerable misstatement has been made about them.
Chunk 022 could not tell whether it falls before p. 168 or after p. 235; chunk 028 records that the six
names reach it only through the editor's table. **Chunk 029 locates it: "بی بیاں پاک دامن" is indexed
at folios 187 and 235 (p. 314, folio 302), the second of which lies past the end of the numbered
entries.** Evidence: read est. pp. 199 and 247. Two near-misses to keep off the row: "بی بی سیدہ پاک
دامن" is Miran Bhikha's mother, whose name the editor's own footnote replaces with **Bibi
Malikwah[OCR?]** — "do not map this to bibi-pak-daman" (p. 112, folio 100; chunk 010); and
"یہ بی بی پاک دامن دہلی میں رہتی تھی" is an epithet for **Bibi Awliya of Delhi** (p. 252, folio 240;
chunk 024). Also "محلہ چہل بی بیاں", the quarter of the **forty** bibis, where the father's grave is
placed, is not shown to be "بی بیاں پاکدامن", and forty does not agree with six (p. 200, folio 188;
chunk 019).

**The book's scope and the survey of Lahore**
- The commissioner "مسٹر ولیم کولڈسٹریم [OCR?]" ordered the author "کہ حالاتِ عمارات و مزارات و مقابر و مساجد نواحِ لاہور مفصل تحریر کروں" — to write a detailed account of the buildings, mazars, tombs and mosques of Lahore's environs. The author calls the task very hard "کیونکہ صدہا سال کے حالات بہم پہونچانے [OCR?] [illegible] مشکل", because the history of hundreds of years had to be gathered (p. 28). Elsewhere he says Lala Amin Chand's work on Lahore's buildings and people was cut short when "مسٹر[OCR?] ولیم[OCR?] گولڈ اسٹریم[OCR?]" left for Shahjahanabad (p. 734).
- The book "مشعرِ احوالِ مقابرِ بزرگانِ اہلِ اسلام وغیرہ عمارات و [illegible] و مراسمِ قدیمہ [illegible] لاہور" (reports on the tombs of Muslim elders, other buildings and the old customs of Lahore), "لیکن فی الاصل تواریخِ [OCR?] اولیا [illegible] ہے", but at root it is a history of saints (p. 29).
- Mufti Ghulam Sarwar had long sought "حالاتِ حضرات نواحیِ لاہور" and could not get them, "کیونکہ حضرات سجادہ نشیناں [illegible] اطلاعِ حال اپنا [illegible]" (p. 31).
- The survey proper opens under the heading "آغاز احوال قبور و مزارات و مقبرہ ہائے بزرگان لاہور اول[OCR?]" (p. 45).
- A forward reference: "مکانات اہل[OCR?] دہلی[OCR?] شہر لاہور میں تحریر یہ ہوگا انشاء اللہ تعالی" (the houses of the people[OCR?] of Delhi[OCR?] in the city of Lahore will be written up, God willing) (p. 292).
- The author cites Lala Amin Chand's *Safarnama* ("مطبوعہ کوہ نور"), its account of the city ("حال شہر لاہور کے بیان میں بہ صفحہ با دن[OCR?]"), and disputes it (p. 733).
- The afterword gives the book's scope as "چارون طرف لاہور کے جسقدر مزار ہین بزرگان سلف کی یادگار ہین اون سب کی حالات"; claims no book had yet been written or printed that covered "آثار [OCR?] قدیمہ لاہور" in full; and says it covers "بزرگان ہنود و اہل اسلام" and that its "صلح کلی" respects every religion (p. 872).

**Foundation legends** (two versions, kept side by side)
- In the Shah Kamal section, introduced "کہتے[OCR?] ہیں": Raja Ram Chand had three sons; "ایک لو[OCR?] نے [illegible] لاہور بنایا" (one, Lo[OCR?], built Lahore); a second, name lost in "[illegible: 4 words]", "قصور[OCR?] آباد کیا"; the third, "مسمی راو[OCR?]", founded Mauza Rawan. **Pre-Mughal rulers and invasions**
- When Sabuktigin first invaded Hind, in "سن [illegible] سو ستہتر [OCR?] ہجری", "لاہور مین راجہ جی پال حاکم تہا" (p. 804). Sabuktigin, having won the Afghans over, "لاہور و ملتان کو اپنے قبضہ مین کر لیا" (p. 804).
- Jaipal, defeated, asked that a trusted man of Sabuktigin's come with him to Lahore to collect the tribute; he came with "نواب خیر اللہ خان [OCR?]". At his court "بطرف چپ چہتری اور بطرف راست برہمن کہتری"; the Chhatris urged him to keep his word, the Brahmins not to pay; Jaipal broke the agreement and imprisoned the envoy. Sabuktigin "لاہور پر چڑہ آیا"; the rajas of Delhi, Ajmer and Kalinjar[OCR?] helped Jaipal, but the Hindus were defeated (p. 804).
- In Sultan Mahmud's time "خاندان توار [OCR?] سی ایک راجہ اوسا [OCR?] لاہور و قنوج کا حاکم تہا" (p. 804).
- Mas'ud, facing the Turkmans (Tughril Beg), was "لاچار لاہور کے طرف روانہ ہوا"; his army looted the treasury at the Indus ("دریا سندہ") and put the blinded Muhammad back on the throne (p. 807).
- In Maudud's time the Hindu rajas besieged Lahore, "جو دار الخلافت اہل اسلام کا تہا", for "سات [OCR?] مہینہ" until the besieged drove them off (p. 807).
- "خسرو شاہ ولد بہرام [illegible] لاہور مین آکر بعالم مجبوری دار السلطنت بنایا"; he ruled "سات [OCR?] برس تک لاہور مین"; his son Khusrau Malik succeeded. The Ghurids took "یہہ ملک" from Khusrau "بہزار مکر و فریب", and "پہر یہان بہی سلطنت غوریان کی ہوئی" (pp. 808–809).
- From "کتاب خلاصۃ التواریخ": after "جہاں [illegible]", Lahore lay deserted ("ویران پڑا رہا") for a long time, until an amir of "سلطان سلوک[OCR?]" settled it again; "کامراں" is named in the damaged continuation (p. 430). Settlement then grew (p. 431).

**Mughal Lahore**
- When Humayun returns to Hindustan, "حاکم لاہور تاتار خان" comes out against him; Bairam Khan overcomes him off guard (p. 823). After Hemu takes Delhi, "اکبر نے لاہور مین کچہہ جمعیت بہم پہونچا کی دہلی پر یورش کی" (p. 824).
- Akbar's second enthronement after Humayun's death: "دوبارہ شہنشاہ حسن تخت نشینی[OCR?] کلاہور میں مسعقد ہوا" (garbled) (p. 648).
- "اکبر بادشاہ[OCR?]" turned to Lahore's settlement from the start of his reign, built a city and a "دولت خانہ", raised large buildings and stayed for a time (p. 431). Lahore is called the capital in Akbar's time: Malik Ali was kotwal in "عہد اکبر بادشاہ" and held "تمام کوتوالی ... دارالسلطنتہ لاہور" (p. 194; surrounding words heavily flagged: "تمر[OCR?] قرویہ[OCR?] حتماً[OCR?] طلوکہ[OCR?] ... مقلم[OCR?]"); also "ملک علی کوتوال لاہور[OCR?]" (p. 193). Akbar is said to have been in Lahore when the Gilani ancestor came: "ان ایام میں اکبر بادشاہ … لاہو یہ رہا[OCR?]" (heavily damaged) (p. 525).
- Khwaja Ayaz (Nur Jahan's father), his wife and the infant travelled with a caravan "اونکی ساتہہ وہ لاہور تک پہنچی"; there he took service and rose to "میر منشی" under Akbar (p. 827).
- Coin legends of "نور الدین جہانگیر ابن اکبر بادشاہ": the transcriber's summary of four illegible lines names mints including "لاہور [OCR?]" and "احمد آباد [OCR?]" (p. 828).
- After Mahabat Khan's victory, "نورجہان لاہور کیطرف بہاگ گئی" (p. 829). Jahangir, ill on his way back from Kashmir, came to Lahore and died there ("جب وہان سے واپس ہوکر لاہور مین آئے تو ... خیمہ زن ملک عدم ہوگئے"); date as printed "تاریخ [illegible] ماہ نومبر سنہ ۱۶۲[illegible]ء [OCR?] مطابق سنہ [illegible]۳۶ [OCR?] ہجری" (p. 830). He was buried "شہر [OCR?] لاہور کے شمال رویہ لب دریای راوی موضع شاہدرہ مین" (p. 830); the tomb is "دریای راوی کی کنارے پر شہر لاہور سے بطرف شمال بفاصلہ چار میل" (p. 855), and from its minarets "تمام شہر لاہور معہ مسجد بادشاہی و وزیر خان وغیرہ عمارات نظر آتی ہین" (p. 860).
- "شاہجہاں بادشاہ لاہور میں ٹھہرے[OCR?]", in the anecdote of Haji Nur (p. 200). Shah Jahan is said to have had "بہت سی عمارات واقع لاہور" built, and Nawab Wazir Khan was appointed "ماظم[OCR?] لاہور" (p. 738); Wazir Khan is "ناظم لاہور" again in "جب نواب صاحب ناظم لاہور رستے[OCR?]" (p. 749). "لاہور مین شالامار باغ" is Shah Jahan's memorial: "چشم روزگار نے کوئی ایسا باغ کم دیکھا ہوگا" (p. 833).
- After Aurangzeb's accession, "دارا شکوہ نے لاہور کو مراجعت کی", raised a larger army there, then crossed the "دریای سندہ" (p. 836). Dara's betrayer, after handing him over in Delhi, "لاہور کیطرف چلا گیا" and was killed on the road (p. 838).
- At the start of Alamgir's reign Nawab Khalil Allah Khan held "دارہ[OCR?] لاہور" and, with a mansab of "شش ہزاری", was appointed "صوبہ دار لاہور" (p. 497).
- The great buildings stood until "عہد عالمگیر"; great amirs built "بڑی بڑی عمارات سنگین"; Abu al-Hasan son of Asaf Khan's buildings were among Lahore's chief ornaments (p. 431).
- In the succession war after Aurangzeb, Muhammad Mu'izz al-Din, governor of Multan, came with a large army and stores "بمقام لاہور" to his father Sultan Mu'azzam Bahadur Shah, and both went on to Akbarabad (p. 845). "شاہ عالم شہر لاہور مین سنہ ۱۷۱۲ء [OCR?] مین فوت ہوا" (p. 846).

**Later Mughal governors (nazims and subadars)**
- Abdus Samad Khan was given the title "سیف الدولہ دلیر جنگ بہادر" and appointed to Lahore by "شاہ عالم" (heavily flagged); he arrived when his son Zakariya Khan was "تیرہ سال کا" (p. 714). Zakariya Khan was appointed "ناظم لاہور" through the Sayyid brothers' support, Abdus Samad Khan moving to "ناظم ملتان"; later he continues as "ناظم لاہور" under Muhammad Shah, "شوق عمارت انکو معہ[OCR?] کمال تہا" (p. 716).
- In the reign of "محمد شاہ بادشاہ" a "حاکم[OCR?] لاہور" is named in connection with "مال گزاری[OCR?]"; the rest is damaged (p. 372).
- Mir Momin Khan became "ناظم[OCR?]" of Lahore in the time of "محمد شاہ[OCR?]" (p. 172); administration of the subjects was good, "رعایا ہی لاہور اوست بند ولسیت[OCR?] بہت عن[OCR?]"; he was not autonomous ("اگرچہ یہ نخود مختار نہ سے تھے"), "نبج[OCR?] باں[OCR?] خان" being "اصلی ناظم لاہور"; most buildings between Mochi Gate and Kashmiri Bazaar are his memorials, "دروازہ موچی و جنوب باروبہ[OCR?] بازار کشمیری اکثر عمارات … یادگار انکی"; and "یہ آخری ناظم لاہور ہوے" — after him no nazim was appointed or came (p. 173). Set beside this, without reconciliation: Nawab Abdullah Khan appointed "ناظم لاہور" in "سنہ ۱۱۶۱[OCR?]" (p. 717); the "حاکم لاہور" of p. 579; "خواجہ عابد صوبہ دار لاہور" (p. 578).
- Mir Mannu "بعہد محمد شاہ یہ نواب لاہور میں حاکم تھا"; the Sikhs had grown in number, and there was a general order to seize and kill them (p. 121). A Panjabi saying about him is illegible: "میر منو ساڈی[OCR?] [illegible] اسی[OCR?]" (p. 121).
- "بعدہ سنہ ۱۱۵۵[OCR?] میں صوبہ ملتان لاہور سکو شامل ہوا" (pp. 717–718).
- Under Nawab Abdullah Khan, "ناظم لاہور" from "سنہ ۱۱۶۱[OCR?]": "لاہور میں قحط پڑا", and he squeezed the people; proverb: "حکومت نواب عبداللہ[OCR?] نہ چکی[OCR?] رہی نہ چلہا[OCR?]" (p. 717).
- "خواجہ عابد صوبہ دار لاہور" attacked Charat Singh's garhi in "سن سترہ سو بیو کے ستہ[OCR?]"; "سکھوں کو فتح نصیب ہوئی" (p. 578).
- A Nawab ("نواب عارض[OCR?]", name damaged) came "کابل سے لاہور میں" (p. 722).
- The "حاکم اعلیٰ لاہور" is named as an office in Rajab Ali Khan's career (p. 141).

**Nadir Shah and Ahmad Shah Abdali**

## From Kanhaiya Lal, Tarikh-e Lahore

*Integrated 10 October 2026 from `entries/book_takeaways/tareekh_lahore.md` (reviewed: no). Every line below the heading is copied verbatim from that file; nothing was added or reworded. Page references are as the takeaways give them.*

- **Saints — antiquity asserted, origin denied.** "لاہور کے مزارات و مقابر اہل اسلام سے یہ مزارات نہایت قدیم اور متبرک کی مشہور ہیں" — among Lahore's mazars and tombs of the people of Islam these are famous as the most ancient and blessed; "اگرچہ ان کی قدمت نہایت ہے ہر کوئی قائل[OCR?] ہے مگر یہ امر کسی طرح سے ثابت نہیں ہوا کہ یہ بی بیاں لاہور میں کب آئیں اور کس ملک سے آئیں" — everyone acknowledges[OCR?] their great antiquity, but **it has in no way been proved when these Bibis came to Lahore or from what country** (p. 304, folio 307).
- **Saints — two accounts, one rejected and one preferred, kept side by side as the book keeps them.**
  - *The mujawirs' account, which the author rejects.* Orally, "زبانی مجاوروں", a thing is found "جسکو عقل مانتی ہی نہیں", which reason simply does not accept: that these **six girls (چہہ لڑکیاں)** — "بی بی ملچ[OCR?] ۔ بی بی تاج ۔ بی بی نور ۔ بی بی حور ۔ بی بی گوہر ۔ بی بی شہباز" — were daughters of **عقیل (Aqil)**, brother of "علی المرتضی پیغمبر صاحب کے داماد", and that when "واقع کربلا" befell Imam Husain they left **Mecca (مکہ)** at the time of that calamity, came to Hind, reached Lahore, settled, and died and were buried there (pp. 304–305, folios 307–308).
  - *The written account he prefers.* "مگر جو مصنف حدیقتہ الاولیا بحوالہ تذکرہ حاکمیہ[OCR?] لکہتا ہے وہ بات البتہ قرین قیاس ہے" — what the author of **حدیقتہ الاولیا** writes **on the authority of تذکرہ حاکمیہ[OCR?]** is indeed close to reason: that in the **sixth century Hijri (چہٹی صدی ہجری)** a person came from **Kirman (کرمان)**, "سید خدا پرست عابد زاہد ولی اللہ سید احمد تخنہ[OCR?] نام", a God-worshipping, devout, ascetic wali-ullah named **Sayyid Ahmad Takhna[OCR?]**, and settled in Lahore; in his house were the same six girls, "اور وہ چہوں تارک الدنیا مجرد عابدہ زاہدہ تہیں", all six world-renouncing, celibate, devout ascetics (p. 305, folio 308).
- **Saints — the father's own grave, and its fate.** "سنہ ۶۰۲[OCR?] ہجری میں سید احمد مر گیا" — Sayyid Ahmad died in **602[OCR?] AH** and was buried inside Lahore in "محلہ چہیل بہیویاں[OCR?]", where his grave still exists; formerly a large tomb stood over it, but "جب سنگ مرمر اسکا مہاراجہ رنجیت سنگ نے اتروا لیا تو مقبرہ گر گیا" — when **Maharaja Ranjit Singh had its marble taken down the tomb collapsed**; and "اسکے گرد و نواح کے قبرستان کو مسمار کرکے غلام محی الدین شاہ[OCR?] پیر زادہ رتا[OCR?] نے اپنی حویلی بنالی" — **Ghulam Muhiuddin Shah[OCR?] Pirzada Rata[OCR?]** demolished the surrounding graveyard and built his haveli; "وہ قبر اب ایک طویلہ کے اندر پختہ بنی ہوئی موجود ہے", the grave now stands, built pakka, **inside a stable (طویلہ)** (p. 305, folio 308).
- **Saints — why the daughters left the city, and the invasion dated.** After his death "اسکی لڑکیاں لاہور کے حصار سے باہر جا کر قیام پذیر ہو گئیں", they went outside **Lahore's hisar** and settled, remaining engaged in the worship of the Truth apart from people (p. 305, folio 308). "جب سنہ ۶۱۵ ہجری میں کفار مغل نے بتعاقب سلطان جلال الدین خوارزمی کے پنجاب پر لشکر کشی کی اور لاہور کی رعایا بجرم مقابلہ و مجادلہ کے قتل ہو لی[OCR?]" — in **615 AH**, printed **without a flag**, the infidel Mughals, in pursuit of **Sultan Jalal-ud-Din Khwarizmi**, led an army against the Punjab and the populace of Lahore was killed for the crime of resistance (p. 305, folio 308).
- **Shrine / Darbar — site and layout.** "یہ متبرک مزارات لاہور سے بفاصلہ دو میل بجانب کو مشہ گگنی[OCR?] واقع" — **two miles from Lahore**, in a direction whose name is flagged and damaged; the entrance is on the **west**, then a lane-like passage to a second gate, then north to the mazars (p. 306, folio 309). **Three enclosures:** "یہ مزارات تین احاطے میں" — an **outer enclosure** with the graves of "بی بی صاحبان کی خادماؤں", the Bibi Sahibans' maidservants, and a pakka domed mosque built by "نورا ایمان[OCR?] وہ اب اے[OCR?]" (unrecoverable); the **large second enclosure** with the graves of **Bibi Milch[OCR?], Bibi Taj and Bibi Nur**; the **third** with the graves of **Bibi Hur, Bibi Gauhar and Bibi Shahbaz**, built pakka lime-plaster; each enclosure "نہایت عمدہ مقطع پختہ چونہ گچ بنا ہوا ہے" (p. 306, folio 309).
- **Shrine / Darbar — a dated tomb in the corner.** In the corner of the eastern[OCR?] enclosure, a pakka domed tomb whose doorway is described through a damaged clause ("اسے اندر سے اسکا دروازہ نہ و ہے[OCR?]"), "وہ سنہ ۱۰۱۷ ہجری میں بنا تہا" — built in **1017 AH**, unflagged — "اور سید جلال الدین بخاری میران تہہ[OCR?] شاہ موج دریا بخاری کا بہائی اسمیں مدفون ہے" (p. 306, folio 309).
- **Shrine / Darbar — the contents locator, and the plural the book prints.** "مزارات بی بی پاک دامان" at printed page ۳۰۷, outside-the-city Islamic qism (p. 4, folio 3). - **Significance.** "اس مکان کو تمام اہل اسلام متبرک جانتے ہیں اور بدل و جان خدمت کرتے ہیں" — all the people of Islam hold this place blessed and serve it with heart and soul (p. 306, folio 309). This entry carries the range's sharpest act of source criticism: oral mujawir testimony set against a written tazkira, and the written one preferred, with the date and country of arrival left explicitly unproved (pp. 304–305, folios 307–308).

### Province-level material: punjab

*The book's `punjab` bullets, copied into every existing entry in the province (Rauf, 8 and 10 October 2026). They speak of the province, not of this shrine.*

- (book's word: "Punjab"; refers to: period not stated — "the early Muslim saints in general, and the Chishti masha'ikh in particular"; author's argument): "Not only for Gujarat, Bengal, but also for Uttar Pradesh, Punjab, and the Deccan does the significance of the early Muslim saints in general, and the Chishti masha'ikh in particular, mark a particular kind of South Asian spiritual authority that is not only political and religious but also cultural"; the authors describe "a spectrum of spiritually charged spaces or sites" that are "devotional loci for Indian Muslims from several classes, and also for Hindus" (p. 110, folio 97). Punjab is one of five regions named together; nothing specific to Punjab is said.
- (book's word: "the Punjab", which the book calls one of "two provinces"; refers to: Akbar's reign, the Fatehpur Sikri phase, which he "abandoned ... by 1585" — as the book states; author's argument): through Shaykh Salim and "Shaykh Farid ad-Din Ganj-i Shakar in the Punjab", "Through a twofold, redoubled Chishti loyalty, Akbar could anchor his imperial legitimacy in two provinces adjacent to Uttar Pradesh, the Punjab and Rajasthan, both of which also happened to be crucial to the political-military ambitions of his reign" (pp. 112–113, folios 99–100). The book's own unit is a Mughal-period "province"; its extent is not stated.
- (book's word: "Punjab"; refers to: "the eighteenth century"; book's historical prose): "The Nizami Chishtis came to prominence in the eighteenth century, principally in Delhi, the Deccan, and Punjab, through masters who were related to but in tension with local political leaders" (p. 119, folio 106).
- (book's word: "the Punjab", "Punjabi Hindus"; refers to: Shaykh Sulayman Taunsawi's time, "as British colonialism began to take hold of India" — Taunsawi is "(d. 1850)" on the same page; book's historical prose): Taunsawi "urged his followers to participate in resistance against Sikh aggression in the Punjab, and so, apparently for the first time in the annals of Chishti history, we have spiritual biographies drawn into partisanship on behalf of one political group (Muslim) over against another (non-Muslim)"; "Notable, however, is the effort of Shah Sulayman to maintain cordial relations with Punjabi Hindus even while he opposed their Sikh neighbors" (p. 121, folio 108). The supporting endnote (n. 10) cites M. Zameeruddin Siddiqi, "The Resurgence of the Chishti Silsilah in the Punjab during the Eighteenth Century" (p. 214, folio 203).
- (book's word: "the Punjab", "the southern Punjab"; refers to: Taunsawi "(d. 1850)", his successor Muhammad 'Aqil "(d. 1814)" and the latter's disciple Gul Muhammad Ahmadpuri "(d. 1827)" — as printed; book's historical prose): Taunsawi "consolidated the order by stressing learning through establishment of institutions: not only the academy called Dar al- 'Ulum at Taunsa on the upper Indus, but also other religious schools throughout the Punjab"; 'Aqil and Ahmadpuri "both of whom founded religious schools in the southern Punjab that provided free instruction and lodging to hundreds of students" (pp. 121–122, folios 108–109).
- (book's word: "the upper Punjab"; refers to: "the nineteenth century"; book's historical prose): the line from Shah Mirra "resurfaces in the nineteenth century in the figure of Hafiz Sayyid Muhammad 'Ali Khayrabadi, marking the expansion of the Nizami branch of Chishti Sufism through disciples of Shaykh Sulayman Taunsawi in the upper Punjab" (p. 71, folio 57).
- (book's word: "the Punjab"; refers to: "the colonial period"; author's argument): "If we were to follow only the dictates of political history and the criteria of social science, the test case for the Chishti Nizami Sulaymaniyya lineage in the colonial period might seem to be the battles over shrines in the Punjab"; the example given is Taunsa, and "Political power struggles internal to the Sulaymaniyya can be traced to British manipulation and also the rivalry of local Sikhs, Hindus, and Muslim reformers" (p. 124, folio printed "Ill" [text-layer?] [111]).
