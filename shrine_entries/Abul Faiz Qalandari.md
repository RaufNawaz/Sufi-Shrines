## Overview

On the Multan Road at Hanjarwal, on the south-western edge of Lahore, stands the shrine of Hazrat Abul Faiz Qalandar Ali Gilani Suhrawardi — a rare instance of a great Sufi shrine that belongs not to the distant past but to living memory. Abul Faiz Qalandar (died 1958) was at once a scholar, orator, author, and spiritual master: a distinguished pupil of the celebrated Ala Hazrat Ahmad Raza Khan of Bareilly, an heir to the Qadiri-Gilani and Suhrawardi orders, and the guide of thousands of disciples in twentieth-century Punjab. His darbar, kept by his own descendants beside the Jamia Masjid Suhrawardia, is a busy centre of devotion and learning, and it shows how the ancient traditions of Sufi Lahore continued to put out new life into the modern age. To understand the shrine is to follow the life of a man who joined the learning of the seminary to the ecstasy of the *qalandar*.

## The Life of the Saint

Abul Faiz Qalandar was born into a learned Gilani sayyid family of the Sialkot district in the last decades of the nineteenth century — by one careful account on Monday, 18 November 1885, at Kotli Loharan, and by another on 4 January 1895. Devotional tradition holds that his birth was foretold: a saint is said to have brought his grandfather, Jamal-ud-Din Gilani, the glad tidings that a grandson would be born in his house who should be named Abul Faiz Qalandar, and who would win great fame, serve the faith, and reach a lofty spiritual station — and so the grandfather named him. His father, Rasool Bakhsh Gilani, was himself a man of much learning, but the boy was able to keep him only some eight years before his father died; from him he had his first schooling, and thereafter he set out into the world of knowledge alone, often withdrawing for many days at a time into the wilderness.

In 1910 he came to Lahore, and for four years pursued the religious sciences at the Madrasa Shah Al-Maali. There, and in the years that followed, he mastered both the outward learning of the scholar and the inward knowledge of the mystic, until — in the phrase his devotees use — “his knowledge began to speak.” At the Jamia Shah Al-Maali he served as imam and preacher (khatib) and taught the memorisation and recitation of the Qur’an, and it was from this base of sober learning that he grew into one of the notable Sufi masters of his age. He died on 9 September 1958, and was buried at Hanjarwal, where his shrine now stands.

## Teachers and the Chain of Transmission

Abul Faiz Qalandar’s spiritual formation passed through the hands of several of the greatest figures of the age. He presented himself before Ala Hazrat Ahmad Raza Khan of Bareilly — the towering scholar of the Barelvi tradition — and, deeply impressed, took the oath of allegiance (bai‘at) at his hand, becoming counted among his special pupils; he would later write a commentary on the famous *Qasida Ghausiya*. He also received knowledge from Pir Mian Sher Muhammad of Sharaqpur, and drew great light from Hazrat Pir Meher Ali Shah of Golra Sharif. It was Meher Ali Shah, tradition says, who told him that his true portion lay in the Suhrawardi order, with Hazrat Ghulam Muhammad Suhrawardi, and bade him take allegiance there — which he did.

His lineage was thus doubly rich: his family descent as a Gilani sayyid is traced, through some twenty-three links, to Shaikh Abd al-Qadir Jilani of Baghdad, and his Sufi allegiance, through some nineteen links, to Shaikh Shihab al-Din Umar Suhrawardi, the great systematiser of the Suhrawardi way. Though he carried both the Gilani-Qadiri and the Suhrawardi inheritances, it was the Gilani-Suhrawardi path that he made his own. A cherished story tells that his master Ghulam Muhammad Suhrawardi once summoned him and said that his own murshid, Baba Jango Pir Shah Suhrawardi, had appeared to him in a dream, foretelling the coming of a young man who should be cared for, “for his manifestation will come about through my name.” Returning to Lahore, Abul Faiz kept his first spiritual retreat (chilla) at the shrine of Shaikh Hassan, known as Sohteli Suhrawardi; and when at last he met his master again, Ghulam Muhammad Suhrawardi told him, “You have made me happy; go, I am pleased with you.” To the end, Abul Faiz would say that whatever he was, he was fulfilling the word of Baba Jango Pir Suhrawardi — as though, he said, the saint were working within him.

## The Scholar and Author

Unusually among the qalandars, Abul Faiz was first and foremost a man of the pen. A recognised scholar of the Ahl-e-Sunnat tradition and a pupil of Ala Hazrat, he is listed among the notable disciples of Ahmad Raza Khan in the biographical compendia of the school, and he devoted much of his life to writing. His books — among them *Jamal-e-Rasool* (“The Beauty of the Messenger”), *Jamal-e-Ilahi* (“The Divine Beauty”), his commentary on the *Qasida Ghausiya*, and many other treatises, tracts, and poems — are steeped in the love of God and of the Prophet, and are still read with esteem. *Jamal-e-Rasool*, dedicated directly to the Prophet and composed in the memory of his own murshid, is a scholarly work saturated in *ishq-e-Rasool*, the passionate love of the Messenger that lay at the heart of his devotion, and he was known too for his devotional verse (naat) in the Prophet’s praise. In an age of religious controversy he wielded both the sermon and the book, and his learning was regarded by his followers as itself a kind of miracle.

## Teaching and Reform

Abul Faiz Qalandar is remembered above all as a reformer of souls — a master of *islah*, the correction of character — whose method was gentleness and example rather than censure. In a gathering, reproving those quick to condemn the faults of others, he is said to have taught: “Keep your own shirt clean and do not point at another’s dirty shirt; seeing your clean shirt, the man with the soiled one will mend himself of his own accord.” And again: “The time you would spend reforming others, spend it on reforming yourself.” He held that his knowledge was a trust to be spent in the service of others — “this is my duty, that I benefit people through what I know” — and his door was famously open: he used to say that whoever came to it never went away empty-handed, and that God does not turn away the prayer of one who comes to this threshold. It was for this union of learning, humility, and generosity that the great scholars of his day sought his company and, having met him, came to love him.

## Miracles

As with every saint, a body of miracle-stories (karamat) gathered about him, and they are still told at the shrine. Once, standing in the mosque as the rain began, he stretched his blessed hands out into it and, wetting them, remarked that he would be no true friend of God who could not number the drops that fall upon the earth. On a journey by train with his students, all the travellers stepped down at the end with their clothes soiled and their faces streaked with grime — all, that is, but the saint, whose face alone was clean; and when they asked how, he answered, “The dirt is subject to us; we are not subject to the dirt.” His students marvelled, too, that in writing his books he would cite other works of the scholars and the saints though those books were nowhere at hand; asked how this could be, he explained that when he began to write, all those books opened themselves before him. He was believed to know the outward and the inward alike, and to answer the need of those who came to him before ever they voiced it.

## A Life in a Changing Country

Abul Faiz Qalandar’s life ran through the last decades of British India and the birth of Pakistan, and he did not stand apart from the affairs of his people. He is remembered as having played a part in the politics of the age and in the Pakistan Movement, and, at the time of the First World War, as having roused a spirit of courage and steadfastness among the people. He belonged to that long line of Indian Sufis — reaching back to Bahauddin Zakariya of Multan, whose Suhrawardi order he inherited — who joined the inward path to an active concern for the community, and he laboured, as his followers put it, day and night for the faith through the middle of the twentieth century.

## The Suhrawardi Way

The order into which Abul Faiz Qalandar was ultimately drawn is one of the oldest and most influential in Islam. The Suhrawardiyya takes its name from Abu Najib al-Suhrawardi and, above all, from his nephew Shaikh Shihab al-Din Umar al-Suhrawardi of Baghdad, whose manual *‘Awarif al-Ma‘arif* became one of the classic guides of the Sufi path. In contrast to some of the more antinomian and ecstatic movements, the Suhrawardi way was known for its sobriety, its insistence on the outward law and on refined manners (*adab*), and its readiness to engage with rulers and society for the good of the community — a temper well suited to a scholar-saint. The order was carried into the Indian subcontinent in the thirteenth century, most famously by Bahauddin Zakariya of Multan, whose great shrine remains one of the anchors of Sufism in Pakistan, and it put down deep roots across the Punjab and Sind. In taking allegiance in this line, Abul Faiz Qalandar joined a chain of transmission seven centuries deep — even as he bore, in his Gilani descent, the blood and the blessing of the Qadiri order of Abd al-Qadir Jilani. His life thus gathered up two of the great rivers of Islamic spirituality, the Qadiri and the Suhrawardi, and carried them into twentieth-century Lahore.

## The Shrine

Abul Faiz Qalandar died on 9 September 1958, and his resting place at Hanjarwal Mor, on the Multan Road in Lahore, was raised into a shrine that his family has tended ever since. It stands within a wider sacred ground — beside the Hanjarwal graveyard and the shrine of Baba Pir Hanjar Wali Sarkar, where other holy men of the region also lie. Around the tomb has grown a small religious complex that reflects the saint’s own character as a scholar-Sufi: the Jamia Masjid Suhrawardia, where the five daily prayers are offered in congregation, together with a Madrasa Suhrawardia and a library at which students still read. Buried alongside the saint are his four sons; of one of them, Ejaz Ahmad Gilani Suhrawardi, who had gone into the film world, it is remembered that his father, disowning that path in his lifetime, said he could do no more for him than pray for his hereafter — yet at his death he too was laid to rest here. The shrine is not a government-managed darbar but private property, held and maintained by the Gilani-Suhrawardi family.

## The Living Lineage

What most distinguishes this shrine is that the spiritual line it commemorates is unbroken and still living. After Abul Faiz Qalandar, the seat (gaddi) passed to his successor Miraj-ud-Din Gilani Suhrawardi, who guided and initiated many disciples and who is buried beside his master; after him to Rasool Shah Gilani Suhrawardi, likewise buried at the shrine; and it is now held by the latter’s son, Shahid Rasool Shah Gilani Suhrawardi, who oversees the darbar and continues to accept the allegiance of new disciples. Pilgrims come not only to honour a saint of the past but to enter a living chain of guidance — to take *bai‘at* at the hand of the present successor and to be counted, as tens of thousands before them have been, among the disciples of the Suhrawardi way as it has been kept at Hanjarwal.

## Devotion and Events

The shrine draws a steady daily stream of visitors — up to some two thousand on an ordinary day — who come to recite the Fatiha, to seek the saint’s intercession (waseela), and to lay their needs before God at a threshold famed for never sending anyone away empty. Every Sunday the darbar holds gatherings and *milad* assemblies in honour of the Prophet, well attended, at which disciples also present themselves to the living successor. The great occasion of the year is the annual urs, held on the 11th and 12th of Rabi-ul-Awwal, when the shrine fills with as many as two hundred thousand devotees for days of qawwali and *dhol*, *langar* (the free distribution of food), the ceremonial washing (*ghusl*) of the tomb and the changing of its cloth (*chadar*). The adjoining mosque, madrasa, and library mean that the life of the shrine is one of learning and prayer as much as of festival, in keeping with the character of the scholar-saint it honours.

## Legacy

Abul Faiz Qalandar Ali Gilani Suhrawardi stands as a bridge between the classical world of the Punjabi Sufi orders and the modern age. In him the disciplined learning of Ala Hazrat’s school met the ecstatic freedom of the qalandar and the ordered devotion of the Suhrawardi silsila; he wrote and preached and reformed, and gathered thousands into a chain of guidance that his descendants carry still. His shrine at Hanjarwal — mosque, madrasa, library, and tomb together — is at once a place of pilgrimage, a seat of a living Sufi order, and a reminder that the sacred history of Lahore did not close with the great saints of the past but continued into the century just gone.
