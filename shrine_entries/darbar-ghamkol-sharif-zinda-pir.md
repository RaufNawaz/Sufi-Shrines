## Current site description (as of 12 October 2026)

*The row's `Description` cell exactly as it appears in `src/data/shrines-fallback.json` on 12 October 2026, untouched. Not yet checked against sources.*

Darbar-e-Aliya Naqshbandia Ghamkol Sharif lies in a secluded valley a few miles outside the town of Kohat in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, and is among the most important centres of the *Naqshbandi* Sufi order in modern Pakistan. It was founded in 1952 by the Naqshbandi master universally known as *Zinda Pir* (1912–1999), who was born in the nearby village of Jungle Khail. He had received spiritual authority (*khilafat*) around 1942 from his own guide, Baba Ji Muhammad Qasim of Mohra Sharif, who directed him to establish an independent centre; obedient to that instruction, Zinda Pir withdrew to the then-barren Ghamkol valley and there built, over the following decades, a great complex of mosque, seminary, and lodgings that grew into a thriving spiritual township. His following spread far beyond Pakistan, and the darbar developed an especially strong connection with the Pakistani community of Birmingham in the United Kingdom, where an associated mosque bears its name. Zinda Pir died on 21 March 1999 and is buried at the darbar, his tomb becoming a place of pilgrimage in its turn; his spiritual seat passed to his son, Pir Sayyid Badshah. Each year vast crowds of devotees gather at Ghamkol Sharif for the commemorative observances, making it one of the great living Sufi centres of the north-west.

Independent scholarly confirmation for the core outline comes from the anthropologist Pnina Werbner, who identifies the tariqa's specific branch name as *tariqa Naqshbandiyya Ghamkolia* and who, by the time of her fieldwork, found Zinda Pir's following grown into "a vast, transnational regional cult, stretching throughout Pakistan to the Gulf, Britain, Afghanistan and Southern Africa." She describes the lodge's daily rhythm — "truckloads of supplicants" arriving to seek the saint's blessing, swelling to "a veritable flood" on Fridays, holidays, and the *urs* — and the centrality of the *langar*, through which visitors are fed rather than asked for payment. Her fuller academic account appears in *Pilgrims of Love: The Anthropology of a Global Sufi Cult* (Hurst, 2004; Indiana University Press ed., 2016), the standard scholarly treatment of the darbar and its founder — though her 2009 conference paper on the subject dates the founding of the cult centre to 1948 rather than the 1952 given above, a discrepancy this entry has not resolved.

- **Saint**
  - **Zindapir is alive at the time of writing and Werbner interviewed him.** He is "the living saint
    of Ghamkol Sharif" (p. 111, folio 100), and the chapter writes of him throughout in the present
    tense (pp. 115–126, folios 104–115). He "stressed to me repeatedly" that the women's work "is all
    done out of muhabbat, the love of God" (p. 111, folio 100). **No death date is given anywhere in
    the book: (not stated).** Do not import 1999 from the archive row.
  - **No personal name, no birth date, no death date and no silsila.** All **(not stated)** across
    pp. 115–129 (folios 104–118). His order is referred to only as "Zindapir's Sufi order" and
    "Zindapir's cult" and **is never named** (pp. 116 and 124, folios 105 and 113). Do not import
    "Naqshbandi" from the archive row — the only silsila named anywhere in chunk 006 is the
    Naqshbandiyya, and it is named of a different man's pir at p. 98 (folio 87).
  - **Birthplace.** The core of the women volunteers "come from neighbouring Jungel Khel, the
    birthplace of Zindapir himself", "now a small town of 20,000 inhabitants" (p. 122, folio 111).
  - **Self-description as a faqir.** He "described himself as a faqir (an ascetic)": "A faqir is the
    friend of Allah. Even if he is offered one lakh [100,000] rupees or nothing to eat for Allah's
    sake, he would choose to go hungry" (p. 115, folio 104); "This is the way of a faqir. He fasts
    all day while making sure that everyone else is given food" (p. 115, folio 104).
  - **Ascetic and vegetarian.** "Zindapir, the saint of Ghamkol Sharif, is said never to sleep but to
    spend all his time meditating and praying" (pp. 113–114, folios 102–103). "The saint himself is a
    vegetarian who eats no meat and regards himself as the protector of all living creatures"
    (p. 118, folio 107); "He fasts all day and is a vegetarian while being the source of meat"
    (p. 118, folio 107).
  - **Physical description, dated to the fieldwork**: "an elderly man limping along slowly since his
    knees are painful and have been causing him some trouble" (p. 123, folio 112). He is "treated as
    a king or prince"; "Grown men tremble at his anger and sink into despair" (p. 123, folio 112).
  - **Household.** A son, whose wife runs the more exclusive of the two langars (p. 117, folio 106)
    and who built a water trough for wild animals (p. 118, folio 107); in 1991 "Zindapir's son's wife
    was undergoing an operation on her throat in England", news of its success arriving at 9.30 p.m.
    by telephone (p. 124, folio 113).
  - **Reputation on women.** "In the case of Zindapir, his reputation was quite immaculate" (endnote
    1, p. 127, folio 116).
  - **On the ʿulama:** "Allah will undoubtedly take the 'ulama to Paradise on the Day of Judgement.
    But Allah gives paradise to his faqirs on earth. I can give all the blessings of Allah" (p. 116,
    folio 105). **On generosity:** "I have to be generous because this [the lodge, or Mecca] is not
    my house. It is the house of Allah. I too am a guest here" (p. 116, folio 105) — Werbner reads
    this as "the close identification of the saint with Allah, his proximity to God" (p. 116,
    folio 105).

  - **Location, exactly as printed**: "Ghamkol Sharif, the lodge of Zindapir, in a little valley
    outside the town of Kohat in north western Pakistan" (p. 110, folio 99).
  - **Site and building history**: "The lodge was built outside any established settlement, very
    gradually, over many years, in what was previously an uncultivated valley" (p. 125, folio 114).
    **No founding date and no building dates anywhere: (not stated)** (pp. 115–126, folios 104–115).
  - Sacralised space: "the sacrifice takes place in a space which has been sacralised by continuous
    prayer" (p. 113, folio 102); "The lodge itself is a place —the remembrance of God, the incantation
    of God's name" `[text-layer?: apparently a dropped phrase, e.g. "a place of zikr—the remembrance

    individually and communally sung—echoes `continously [text-layer?: continuously]` through the
    valley" (p. 114, folio 103).
  - **A transnational regional cult.** "the network of satellite lodges distributed throughout
    Pakistan and Britain which provide langar for visitors" (pp. 115–116, folios 104–105); the ʿurs
    convoys come "throughout Pakistan and beyond it, including from Britain, where Zindapir's order
    extends" (p. 114, folio 103); "the branches in both countries send tribute to the shaikh himself"
    (p. 124, folio 113). "On the annual hajj, Zindapir distributes langar in Mecca" (p. 116,
    folio 105), that langar "organised and funded by his vicegerent in Britain—Sufi Abdullah… The
    money for the hajj langar is donated by British Pakistanis, while the pots and utensils are kept
    by disciples of Zindapir living in Mecca who are labour migrants working in Saudi Arabia"
    (p. 116, folio 105).
  - **Productive endowment.** "Ghamkol Sharif has its herds of cattle, goats and other livestock, its
    orange groves and fruit orchards, its vegetable gardens, as well as storage rooms for grain, rice
    and clarified butter, brought by supplicants or purchased with their donations" (p. 118,
    folio 107). "Gifts of camels and horses, and of strange and beautiful wild birds and animals, as
    well as of goats, sheep and buffalo are not unusual. Many are dedicated to the langar and cannot
    be used for any other purpose" (p. 118, folio 107).
  - **What the state has given, and what the saint refuses.** "Land, telephone lines, water, roads and
    transport have all been provided free through official channels, and Zindapir takes great care,
    in reality, not to offend politicians" (p. 121, folio 110). Against that, he refuses political
    involvement — "God is not elected, and to become a Sufi you don't need to be elected" (p. 119,
    folio 108); of politicians, "Because they tells lies [jhut]" (p. 119, folio 108) — yet prays for
    them when they come for duʿa (p. 119, folio 108). "he is regularly visited by top civil servants,
    army `brigadeers` [text-layer?: brigadiers] and generals, and even politicians. All partake of
    the langar" (p. 117, folio 106). "The Chief Minister of Azad Kashmir visited him several times,
    but Zindapir told me, he never asked his name" (p. 121, folio 110). **In 1991** Werbner "was told
    confidentially that one of the leading political figures in Pakistan had visited the lodge and
    had wanted to write Zindapir a cheque for a very large sum of money", which he refused (p. 120,
    folio 109). **No Auqaf department, no waqf and no state custodianship is mentioned for this lodge
    anywhere: (not stated).**
- **Practices & Events**
  - **The ʿurs is a three-day festival** whose peak is the saint's final duʿa (p. 125, folio 114); it
    is "the major three-day festival" (p. 110, folio 99).
  - **Whose ʿurs it is — stated once, and contradicted by silence elsewhere.** "The occasion of the
    sacrificial feast is the ‘urs, the commemoration of the death/rebirth of Zindapir's Sufi master,
    Baba Qasim" (p. 111, folio 100). **Chunk 007 covering pp. 115–129 states that what or whom the
    ʿurs commemorates is nowhere given there.** The two statements are kept side by side; see Doubts,
    where this collides with the archive row's own principal-figure field.
  - **The 1990 sacrifice, counts exactly as printed.** "In 1990 ninety-five goats, thirteen sheep, and
    seventeen cattle and buffalo were slaughtered over a three-day period, the equivalent of over
    3,000 kg of meat, distributed to some 20,000 or more pilgrims and supplicants" (p. 110, folio 99).
  - **The crowd figure, with the author's own caveat attached.** "The 'urs is said, somewhat
    hyperbolically, to draw 300,000 pilgrims to the lodge, all of whom are fed by the saint" (p. 116,
    folio 105). **Werbner marks the figure as hyperbolical in the same sentence.** It sits beside the
    "20,000 or more" of 1990 (p. 110, folio 99) and is not reconciled with it by the book.
  - **The langar is open twenty-four hours a day, all year.** "The langar at Ghamkol Sharif is open
    twenty-four hours a day all year to all supplicants and pilgrims, from the very poor to the most
    elevated and powerful in the land" (p. 117, folio 106). "The ‘urs is the high point of a
    continuous flow of food provided at the lodge by the saint all year round… a perpetual sacrifice,
    one that provides an apparently endless supply of sacrificial meat every day throughout the year"
    (p. 111, folio 100).
  - **There are two langars.** "One is an open, general langar, and the other is run by his son's
    wife. Many of the most respected guests are fed from this more exclusive langar, although
    chappati bread usually comes from the central langar" (p. 117, folio 106). The Sayyid women
    Werbner stayed with during the ʿurs "had brought their own food with them for the festival, and
    ate almost nothing from the central langar", denying any significance to the avoidance "beyond a
    matter of taste preferences" (p. 117, folio 106).
  - **Nothing is bought.** "All the animals slaughtered for the langar are freely given by pilgrims,
    disciples and supplicants—none is purchased" (p. 113, folio 102). The convoys "bear with them not
    only animals but sacks of flour, bags of rice, large containers of clarified butter (ghee) and
    money donated for the purchase of food for the langar" (p. 114, folio 103).
  - **Three weeks of volunteer labour and building before the ʿurs.** "For three weeks before the
    'urs both men and women begin arriving at the lodge to contribute their voluntary labour… Much of
    the building of the lodge takes place during this period, including the extension of water pipes,
    sewerage and electricity. New hostels are constructed for pilgrims" (p. 122, folio 111).
  - **Women's preparatory labour, itemised and class-marked.** "local high-class Sayyid women of
    wealthy families who claim descent from the Prophet, along with a large number of women from
    other respected families throughout Pakistan, clean and scrape thousands of clay dishes, hundreds
    of flat bread baskets, about twenty or thirty giant clay pots and an equivalent number of iron
    chappati stoves (loh)" (p. 110, folio 99); the stoves "accumulate a layer of rust over the year
    and need to be scraped and polished in readiness for the thousands of chappatis that are to be
    baked on them" (p. 110, folio 99); the women then "use heavy iron picks to dig up the dry earth in
    order to install the ovens" and "smear the surface around the ovens with mud" (p. 110, folio 99).
    "While the women wash, scrape and polish, the men invest their voluntary labour in piling up vast
    quantities of wood" (p. 111, folio 100). "The labour of the women is all voluntary, an act of
    khidmat, of public service" (pp. 110–111, folios 99–100); "The women explain that they work for
    sawab, merit, and for God's forgiveness, Allah mu‘af kare" (p. 111, folio 100).
  - **Who the women volunteers are.** Mostly "from the high-ranking Sayyid family, which is the
    largest, wealthiest and most important in Jungel Khel"; its members are "scattered throughout the
    world, occupying professional positions as doctors, engineers or pilots", hold "many of the top
    administrative positions in the town", "own large houses" and employ servants — yet take on "the
    most menial, dirty, unpleasant tasks" and sleep "on mats on the floor" (p. 122, folio 111). During
    the ʿurs "they also help supervise the visits of women supplicants to the saint" (p. 122,
    folio 111).
  - **Purity of the food.** "The food is pure, I was told, because it is cooked by men of pure heart
    who chant zikr (the incantation of God's name) as they cook" (p. 111, folio 100). The men who
    slaughter and cook "do so as a meritorious, freely given act, an act of selfless service
    (khidmat). All the labour in the lodge is freely given in the name of God" (p. 114, folio 103).
  - **Slaughter arrangements.** "At Ghamkol Sharif… animals are donated on the hoof and one of the
    disciples slaughters them, assisted by several companions. The same disciple acts as chief
    slaughterer for the lodge on all major festivals" (p. 117, folio 106) — against Britain, where
    slaughter "is entrusted to the Pakistani butcher, who slaughters it at the abattoir" (pp. 116–117,
    folios 105–106).
  - **The daily supplicant flow and the audience protocol.** "a constant daily flow of supplicants
    seeking healing for their ailments or divine blessings in their jobs, careers and marital
    fortunes" (p. 122, folio 111). The saint "takes in about ten at a time", instructs supplicants to
    perform their daily prayers, throws "a rolled-up, inscribed paper amulet in their general
    direction" or tells them to collect amulets "outside", then raises his hands in duʿa (p. 123,
    folio 112). "On their visits to his room women supplicants sit behind a low wooden barrier to
    prevent them from reaching too close to him" (p. 123, folio 112); both sexes "expose their faces,
    the women drawing their veils above their heads" (p. 123, folio 112).
  - **What the saint gives back.** "white chiffon headscarves, embroidered praying caps and perfumes",
    drawn "out of the treasure house of objects he accumulates in his room, buried in the silk and
    brocade cushions on which he reclines"; "salt and amulets to the supplicants… along with du'a and
    dam"; and money "to the poor and needy who come to him with requests for help" (pp. 121–122,
    folios 110–111). Elsewhere the list is "white cotton scarves and white embroidered hats for his
    disciples, gowns for his khalifa, amulets and salts for supplicants" (p. 125, folio 114).
  - **The order's festival calendar (in Britain, run from this lodge)**: the ʿurs; "eid milad-un nabi
    (the commemoration of the Prophet's birth/death)"; the monthly *gyarvin sharif*, "the eleventh of
    the month", "held in most of Zindapir's satellite lodges and mosques throughout his regional
    cult"; and daily distribution "during the month of Ramadan… at the mosque branches of Zindapir's
    Sufi order in Manchester and throughout Britain" (p. 116, folio 105). British langar "differs
    rather little in organisation and ideology from the langar in Pakistan, except for the fact that,
    as yet, it is not a perpetual sacrifice" (p. 124, folio 113).
  - **Plate captions belonging to this shrine.** Plate 5.1, "Cooking pots and wood piled high in
    anticipation of the ‘urs at Ghamkol Sharif", printed folio 101 (p. 8, folio not stated; p. 112,
    folio 101); Plate 5.2, "A pilgrim leads his sacrificial goat for the langar", folio 102 (p. 8,
    folio not stated; p. 113, folio 102); Plate 5.3, "Distributing the langar to the pilgrims", folio
    106 (p. 8, folio not stated). **Plates 5.2 and 5.3 do not name a place**; see Doubts before
    attaching them. **Neither caption is dated: (not stated).**
- **Legends & Miracles**
  - **Command over the wild**, attributed by Werbner to "the legends about the Zindapir": "He can cure
    poisonous snake bites, honey bees are said not to sting, and wild animals do not invade the lodge
    or attack its inhabitants" (p. 118, folio 107).
  - **Feeding the ants.** "Every morning he feeds the ants with the remains of his sweet morning tea.
    Killing ants and any other creatures, however minute, is prohibited within the lodge area. This is
    also true of his conduct during the hajj, I was told" (p. 118, folio 107). "A special water trough
    has been constructed by the saint's son for the wild animals that descend from the hills at night
    to drink from" (p. 118, folio 107).
  - **The founding myth of the lodge**, told by Zindapir: on the first journey to establish it he saw
    a companion carrying a sack of flour and told him, "What will you eat when the flour is finished?
    Throw it away and trust in Allah" (p. 120, folio 109). Werbner calls this "the paradigmatic tale
    which illuminates the place of the lodge as the sole source of God's boundless nurture" (p. 120,
    folio 109). "In his final supplication for the sake of the community, the saint recalls his own
    first arduous journey to the lodge. Indeed, he repeats the story of this legendary journey almost
    verbatim every year" (p. 125, folio 114).
  - **The refused three days of langar — two versions, printed side by side and not adjudicated.** In
    one, a great landowner from Sahiwal district offered all the food for the langar for three days in
    exchange for permission to heal by *dam*, and Zindapir answered: "If I let you provide the langar
    for three days I will make you a partner with God which would be shirk [polytheism, blasphemy]" —
    refusing even to make him a murid (p. 120, folio 109). In the other, the offer came from the
    Minister of Finance's uncle after Zindapir cured him of an incurable disease — "Pir Sahab cast dam
    (blew a prayer) on him and said: 'Let him eat from the langar's food and he will be cured'" — and
    the refusal ran: "You will provide for the langar for three days but what will happen after that?
    I cannot make you a partner (sharik) with God" (p. 120, folio 109). **Werbner presents these
    explicitly as two versions of one tale and does not choose between them.**
  - **The refusal of the Saudi politician's hospitality.** Invited to stay at a Saudi politician's
    home in Jedda, Zindapir replied: "When I visit the house of God, I am the guest only of Allah"
    (p. 120, folio 109).
  - **Healing by insight into hidden causes.** "The healing powers of the saint are grounded in a
    belief in his ability to see below the surface, to the occult and social causes of illnesses, the
    thoughts, feelings and accidental transgressions which have brought about pain, chronic illness,
    infertility, depression, business failures and so forth" (pp. 122–123, folios 111–112).
- **Significance**
  - **The lodge as a non-commodity economy.** "Hence the lodge is a space set apart from the commodity
    economy, and capitalist logic does not hold there" (p. 114, folio 103). Werbner sets the saint,
    who "asks for nothing except from Allah", against a cash figure for hired religious labour: "Only
    yesterday I paid a maulvi [Islamic scholar] 400 rupees [about £15] for giving a sermon at the
    mosque" (p. 116, folio 105).
  - **The index treats the lodge as a site of ʿurs ritual.** Headword `Ghamkol Sharif 100–15` (p. 250,
