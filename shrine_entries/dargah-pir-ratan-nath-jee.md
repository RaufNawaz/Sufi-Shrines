## Overview

The Dargah of Pir Ratan Nath, in the Jhanda Bazar of the walled city of Peshawar, is among the last living Hindu temples of that ancient frontier city. A shrine of the British colonial period, dedicated in practice to *Shiva* and centred upon the veneration of the yogi-saint *Ratan Nath*, it belongs to the tradition of the *Nath* order — the sect of ascetic yogis founded in the memory of Gorakhnath — and stands today as one of only a small handful of Peshawar's temples still in regular use, alongside the nearby Kali Bari and the historic Gorakhnath temple at Gor Khatri.

## The Nath Tradition

The temple's saint, Ratan Nath, belongs to the *Nath sampradaya*, the order of *kanphata* ("split-eared") yogis who trace their lineage to Gorakhnath and, before him, to Matsyendranath, the semi-legendary founder of the sect. The Naths, who flourished across northern India, Nepal, and the north-western frontier from roughly the early medieval centuries, practised a rigorous discipline of yoga and *hatha* austerity and revered Shiva as the *adi-nath*, the first lord and archetypal yogi. Peshawar and its region were an old stronghold of the order: the Gorakhnath temple within the Gor Khatri complex, and the ancient association of the nearby hill of *Tilla Jogian* across the Indus with Gorakhnath himself, mark this as Nath country of long standing.

Ratan Nath is remembered in tradition as a wandering seeker and teacher who carried the Nath message of peace, truth, and compassion across the frontier lands; his veneration extends beyond Peshawar to shrines in the wider region, including sites in Nepal. As with many figures of the order, the details of his life are wrapped in legend and cannot be fixed with confidence to a particular century, and the tradition should be received as sacred memory rather than documented biography.

## The Shrine at Jhanda Bazar

The dargah itself, in its present form, is a temple of the nineteenth century, raised in the Karimpura–Jhanda Bazar neighbourhood within the old walls of Peshawar when the city's Hindu mercantile community was numerous and prosperous. Its very name — *dargah*, a word borrowed from the vocabulary of the Muslim shrine — reflects the blended religious idiom of the frontier, where a Hindu yogi's shrine could be spoken of in the language of the Sufi tomb. The temple's chief festival is *Maha Shivratri*, the great night of Shiva, when devotees gather for worship.

After the Partition of 1947, when the greater part of Peshawar's Hindu population departed, most of the city's temples fell silent or were converted to other uses. The Dargah Pir Ratan Nath is remarkable for having survived as a functioning place of worship, tended by the small Hindu community that remained, and it is today reckoned one of the very few temples in daily use in the city, together with the Kali Bari.

## Legacy

The survival of the Dargah Pir Ratan Nath is significant out of all proportion to its modest size. It preserves, in a city more often associated in the modern imagination with the Khyber Pass and the turbulence of the frontier, the memory of a deep and ancient Hindu and yogic presence — the Nath yogis whose austerities once echoed across these hills. As a still-living shrine, it is both a place of worship for Peshawar's remaining Hindus and a fragile monument to the religious plurality of the old walled city. Efforts to document and protect the temple, including its listing among the heritage sites of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, reflect a growing recognition of its value.
