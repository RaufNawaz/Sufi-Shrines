## Overview

Panj Tirath — the "five bathing places" — is one of the most ancient sacred sites of Peshawar, a Hindu pilgrimage complex whose name records the five pools of water that lay at its heart. Set in the Hashtnagri quarter of the old city, it belongs to a stratum of the region's religious history far older than the Islamic and Sikh monuments that surround it, reaching back through the Hindu and Buddhist antiquity of the Peshawar valley. Long neglected and much reduced, it was declared a protected national heritage site in 2019, an acknowledgement of a sanctity that local memory traces to the age of legend.

## Antiquity and Legend

The site takes its name and its holiness from its five *tirthas*, the pools in which pilgrims bathed. Local tradition, recorded by Munshi Gopal Das in his *Tareekh-e-Peshawar* of 1874 and repeated by the Hindu community, connects Panj Tirath with the five sons of *Pandu* — the *Pandava* heroes of the *Mahabharata* — and holds that the site was hallowed by their presence, so that its origins are woven into the great epic geography of the subcontinent. Alongside this Hindu memory runs a Buddhist one: the celebrated archaeologists Sir Alexander Cunningham and Alfred Foucher identified Panj Tirath and its environs with places associated in ancient accounts with the Buddha, including the relic of his begging-bowl, which was long kept at Peshawar (the ancient *Purushapura*). The site thus stands at the confluence of the two great faiths that shaped the valley before Islam, and its sanctity is best understood as very old and layered, rather than fixed to any single founder.

## History

Like so many of the region's shrines, Panj Tirath passed through cycles of destruction and revival. It is recorded to have been damaged during the turbulence of the Durrani period in the eighteenth century, around 1747, and then rebuilt by the Hindus of Peshawar in 1834, in the years of Sikh ascendancy in the north-west, when worship was restored at the pools. Through the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries it remained an active centre of pilgrimage, its waters visited especially during the month of *Kartik* — the season, falling between late October and late November, when bathing at sacred *tirthas* carries particular merit in the Hindu calendar.

## Decline and Protection

The Partition of 1947 and the departure of most of Peshawar's Hindu population left Panj Tirath without a resident community to maintain it, and in the decades that followed the site was encroached upon and largely built over: much of its ground was absorbed into a public park, and its structures — two temples and a gateway among them — fell into dilapidation, some pressed into use as storerooms. In January 2019 the Directorate of Archaeology, acting under the Khyber Pakhtunkhwa Antiquities Act of 2016, declared Panj Tirath a protected national heritage site, a step welcomed by heritage advocates and the Hindu community alike, though the physical fabric of the complex remains fragile and in urgent need of conservation.

## Legacy

Panj Tirath is a witness to the deep and plural past of Peshawar — a city that has been in turn a Buddhist metropolis, a Hindu centre, a Mughal and Durrani stronghold, and a Sikh and British garrison. Its five pools, and the memory of the Pandavas and the Buddha that clings to them, connect the modern city to an antiquity measured in millennia. That the site survives at all, and now enjoys a measure of legal protection, offers some hope for the preservation of a heritage that belongs not to one community but to the long history of the region as a whole.
