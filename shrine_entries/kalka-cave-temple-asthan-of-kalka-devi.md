## Overview

The Kalka Devi Cave Temple is one of the most venerated Hindu shrines of Sindh, set within a natural cavern in the limestone hills of the Rohri plateau, about a mile south of the ruins of ancient *Aror* (Alor), the pre-Islamic capital of Sindh, near Sukkur. Dedicated to the goddess *Kalka Devi* — a form of *Kali* — the cave draws pilgrims from across Pakistan and, when circumstances allow, from India, and is remarkable for the reverence it commands among local Muslims as well as Hindus.

## Aror and the Sacred Cave

The setting is steeped in antiquity. Aror was the seat of the Rai and Brahman dynasties of Sindh and the capital of Raja Dahir at the time of the Arab conquest in the early eighth century; the shifting of the Indus away from the city in the historic period left its ruins stranded on the edge of the Rohri limestone escarpment. Within these hills, a natural cave has long been held sacred to the goddess. The present temple complex — the shrine chamber deep in the cave together with the rooms, halls and lodgings built around and before it — is generally attributed to the 1920s and to the patronage of a devotee named Hemraj Kewalramani, though the sanctity of the site is understood by its devotees to be immeasurably older. The built fabric blends Hindu iconography with the arches and ornament of regional Islamic architecture, a hallmark of Sindhi sacred building.

## Mythology and the Hinglaj Connection

According to the legend cherished at the shrine, the goddess *Kalka Devi* halted here, or made her appearance, while journeying to the great *Shakti Peetha* of *Hinglaj Mata* in the mountains of Balochistan — binding this cave into the wider sacred geography of Devi worship that stretches across Sindh and Balochistan. The temple's caretakers relate that two tunnels within the cave are said to lead, by hidden ways, to Hinglaj itself; such traditions, common to cave shrines, are best understood as expressions of devotional imagination rather than literal topography. The association with Hinglaj places Kalka Devi within the same current of pilgrimage that culminates in the *Hinglaj Yatra*, one of the most important observances of Pakistan's Hindu community.

## Devotional Life

The rhythm of worship at Kalka Devi is marked above all by the gathering of devotees on the evening of the first Monday of each month, and by far larger crowds at the great festivals of the goddess, when many rooms built around the cave serve to lodge pilgrims who arrive in their thousands. That the shrine is frequented by Muslims as well as Hindus — indeed, local accounts hold that Muslim visitors are often in the majority — reflects the shared reverence for holy places that has long characterised the Indus valley, where a sacred site may transcend the boundaries of formal religion.

## Legacy

As a living shrine of undoubted antiquity, the Kalka Devi Cave Temple is among the most significant Hindu sites in northern Sindh, complementing the great riverine temple of Sadhu Bela at nearby Sukkur. It has in recent years faced threats from illegal quarrying and hill-cutting in the Rohri hills, prompting appeals for its protection as both a religious and a heritage site. For the Hindu community of Sindh it remains a place of pilgrimage and continuity; for historians it is a tangible link to the deep pre-Islamic past of Aror and the enduring worship of the goddess in the land of the Indus.
