## Overview

The Shri Swaminarayan Mandir, rising behind its long frontage on M. A. Jinnah Road in the heart of Karachi, is the principal Hindu temple of Pakistan's largest city and the only surviving temple of the *Swaminarayan Sampradaya* — the devotional tradition founded in early nineteenth-century Gujarat — in the country. Spread across a walled compound of some thirty-two thousand square yards, it has for well over a century been both a place of worship and a refuge, and its story is woven into the history of Karachi itself.

## The Swaminarayan Tradition

The Swaminarayan movement takes its name from *Swaminarayan* (Sahajanand Swami, 1781–1830), the reformer and teacher who gathered a large following across Gujarat and Kutch and preached a disciplined, devotional Hinduism centred on the worship of Narayana, ethical conduct, and service. His followers spread with the trading networks of the Gujarati mercantile castes, and it was these communities — the *Lohana* and *Bhatia* merchants of Kutch and Kathiawar who had settled in the port of Karachi — who raised the temple to serve their spiritual needs. The temple is generally held to have been established around the middle of the nineteenth century, in the decades after Karachi passed under British rule and grew rapidly as a commercial port.

## The Temple and Its Life

Within its spacious precincts the mandir houses shrines to Swaminarayan and the deities of the tradition, together with halls, lodgings, and open courtyards that can accommodate large gatherings. It has long been the focal point of Hindu festival life in Karachi, most notably at *Janmashtami*, the celebration of Krishna's birth, and *Holi* and *Diwali*, when the compound fills with worshippers. The temple's ample grounds have also served secular and civic purposes over the years, hosting community functions and, at times, providing shelter in the crowded life of the city.

## Partition and the Refuge of 1947

The most poignant chapter in the temple's history came with the Partition of the subcontinent in 1947. As communal violence convulsed the region and hundreds of thousands of Hindus and Sikhs sought to leave the newly created Pakistan, the spacious compound of the Swaminarayan Mandir became a transit camp for refugees awaiting passage by ship from Karachi's port to India. Tradition records that *Muhammad Ali Jinnah*, the founder of Pakistan, visited the temple during this period and is said to have offered reassurance to those sheltering there. The episode is remembered as a moment when the great temple stood as a place of sanctuary amid the upheaval that reshaped the subcontinent.

## Legacy

Today the Shri Swaminarayan Mandir remains an active and well-attended temple, serving Karachi's Hindu minority and standing as one of the most visible symbols of the community's long presence in the city. Its survival — as the last Swaminarayan temple in Pakistan and one of the country's largest functioning Hindu shrines — makes it a landmark of both religious devotion and shared history, a reminder that the great port of Karachi has always been home to many faiths.

When one is caught the experts split the calf and take out "دانه بسان انار دانه", which is fed to the victim, who recovers (pp. 529–530). A liver-eater will not sink with a millstone tied to the neck nor burn in fire; to cure one, the experts brand both temples, fill the eyes with salt, hang the person in a house for forty days, give saltless food and recite spells (p. 529). Most are women, some men; "در هر بلاد می باشند و اما در بلاد تهته بیشتر باشند" (p. 529).
  - Peoples: north of the line from Multan and Uch to Thatta and Kach Makran, hard-rock hills, home of Baluch and "بعضے افغان"; to the south, from Uch to Gujarat, sand hills and the "بهتی", whose chief's seat is Jaisalmer, and "دیگر اقوام راجپوت[OCR?]"; from Bhakkar to Nasirpur and Amarkot, "سبوده[OCR?]" and "جاریجه" (p. 529).
  - River: "بزرگ ترین دریائے سند است"; merchants from Multan and Bhakkar ship goods by it to Thatta, and travellers and even "عساکر گران" go by river; large armies rarely go by land, because of the jungle and the lack of water (p. 529).
  - Extent: "طول این صوبه از بهکر تا کچ مکران دو صد و پنجاه کروه و عرض از قصبه بدین تا بندر لاهری صد کروه" (p. 529). Bounds: east "گجرات احمداباد", west "کچ مکران", north "بهکر", south "دریائے شور" (p. 529).
  - Administration and revenue: "سرکار تهته و سیوستان و نصیر پور و امرکوت چهار سرکار مشتمل بر پنجاه و هفت محال و پنج بندر و ده[OCR?] کرور[OCR?] و چهل و نه لک و هفتاد هزار دام داخل این صوبه است" (pp. 528–529).
