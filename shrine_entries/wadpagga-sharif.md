## Overview

Wadpagga Sharif is a village and shrine on the outskirts of Peshawar, in the historic vale that has for centuries been a meeting-ground of Pashtun, Persian, and Central Asian devotion. It is remembered above all as the seat of a family of *sayyids* — descendants of the Prophet Muhammad — of the *Bukhari* line, and as the resting place of the saint the local tradition calls *Ghazi Baba*. The very name of the place is said to preserve the memory of these holy men: *Wadpagga*, in the popular etymology, derives from words meaning "big turbans," for the *sayyids* who settled here wore the large turbans that marked their dignity, and the village took its name from them.

## The Sayyids of Wadpagga

According to the traditions of the family, the saint honoured at Wadpagga, Ghazi Baba, belonged to the *Bukhari sayyids*, that widely revered lineage which traces its descent through the great Suhrawardi saints of the medieval Punjab and Sindh — *Jalaluddin Surkh-Posh Bukhari* and his grandson *Makhdoom Jahaniyan Jahangasht* of Uch, both of whom are venerated at their own shrines elsewhere in this collection. Ghazi Baba is remembered as a descendant, some fifteen generations removed, of Syed Nasiruddin Mahmud, and thus as an inheritor of that Bukhari spiritual patrimony, which he carried into the Pashtun country around Peshawar. He is said to have been a contemporary of other saints of the valley, among them Mian Umar, remembered at the village of Chamkani, so that his life is placed within a whole network of holy men who flourished in the region in the later medieval and early modern centuries. Because the detailed record of his life survives chiefly in family and devotional tradition rather than in independent chronicle, the particulars of his dates and deeds are best received as the community remembers them, with the caution proper to hagiography.

## Devotional Life

The shrine of Ghazi Baba at Wadpagga is a site of pilgrimage, drawing devotees who come to offer *fatiha* at the tomb, to seek the intercession and blessing (*baraka*) of the saint, and to honour the wider circle of his descendants who are buried in the village. As at the other Bukhari shrines of the subcontinent, the devotion here is bound up with the prestige of *sayyid* descent — the belief that the family carries the light of prophetic lineage — and with the memory of a saint who brought that heritage from the Indus valley into the hills of the frontier. The rhythms of the shrine follow the familiar pattern of the Pashtun *ziyarat*: gatherings on the days of remembrance, the recitation of the Qur'an and of devotional poetry, and the quiet daily traffic of local people who bring their hopes and troubles to the saint's threshold.

## Legacy

Wadpagga Sharif is a characteristic example of the smaller sacred places that stud the countryside around Peshawar, where the great movements of Islamic mysticism — Suhrawardi, Qadiri, Naqshbandi — took root through the settlement of *sayyid* and saintly families who married into the local society and became, in time, the spiritual anchors of their villages. The shrine's link to the Bukhari lineage ties this frontier settlement to the far older centres of Suhrawardi devotion at Uch and Multan, and reminds the visitor that the religious landscape of the Peshawar valley was shaped not only by the celebrated poets and warriors of Pashtun tradition but also by these quieter dynasties of the holy. For the people of Wadpagga and the surrounding district, Ghazi Baba remains a protector and patron, and his turban-crowned forebears are honoured in the very name their descendants still bear.

- Mahmud's first campaign, "در سنه ۳۹۱": "راجه جیپال نیز بعزم مقابله رهگرا گشته در پشاور ... رسیده معرکه کارزار بیاراست" (p. 424). "پنج هزار کفار دران کارزار علف تیغ خونخوار نمود"; "راجه جیپال با پانزده نفر از پسر و برادر و خویش اسیر گشته بقتل رسید", printed as quoted (p. 424); see Doubts.
- "از بلده پشاور سه راه بکابل میرود" (pp. 502–501): the Nikshab road ("راه نیکشاب"), with many hard defiles and a long distance, armies passing with difficulty (p. 502); the "کهرپه[OCR?]" road, joining the highway at Jalalabad, with narrow defiles, hard passes, little water and Afghan raids ("دست بردے[OCR?] افغانان") (p. 502); "راه علی مسجد است و خیبر", running 18 kos ("هژده کروه") from the spring of Jamshid, "known as Jamrud", to "دهیکه[OCR?] کنار نیلاب", the Khyber hard going for two kos, yet easier than the others and used by armies and caravans; then 30 kos without hills from "دکه[OCR?]" to "ننله[OCR?]", and 40 kos with passes not very hard from "ننله[OCR?]" to Kabul (p. 501).
- "تومان بنگرام[OCR?] ان مشهور به پشاور جانب هندوستان است" (p. 504): its grapes, peaches and melons like Turan's, its heat, cold, seasons and rain like Hindustan's; its rice "سکهد اس[OCR?]" better than Bengal's; crops and grain plentiful (p. 504). "تمام[OCR?] این تومان بنگاه افغانان مهمند و غیر ذلک است", who "طریق مال گذاری و رعیت گیری ... دارند" (pp. 504–503).
- Dara sent khilats to "لشکرهائے پیشاور و کابل را که مهابت خان ایالت آنجا داشت" (p. 77). *basis: office mention*
- A bare march: after laying out the fort of Attock the court "متوجه پیشاور شدند", and "ازین منزل" a farman of counsel was issued to Hakim Mirza (p. 201). *basis: transit mention*
