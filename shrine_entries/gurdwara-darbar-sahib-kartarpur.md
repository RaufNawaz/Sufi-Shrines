## Overview

Gurdwara Darbar Sahib Kartarpur stands on the right bank of the River Ravi in Narowal District, in Pakistan's Punjab province, close to the frontier with India. It marks the place where Guru Nanak, founder of Sikhism, passed the final years of his life and where, in 1539, he died — making it one of the most venerated sites in the Sikh world and, in a real sense, the birthplace of the Sikh community as a settled, worshipping body. Its white domes, long visible across the fields from the Indian side of the border, became in 2019 the terminus of the celebrated Kartarpur Corridor, a rare visa-free pilgrimage passage that reunited the shrine with the devotees of a divided Punjab.

## Guru Nanak's Final Years

Guru Nanak is said to have founded the settlement of Kartarpur — "the abode of the Creator" — around 1504 on land beside the Ravi, and, after the long missionary journeys known as his *udasis*, to have settled there for the last years of his life; by many accounts he spent some eighteen years at the site. Here the wandering teacher became the founder of a living community. Setting aside the ascetic's road, he took up the life of a householder and farmer, and gathered around him a *sangat*, or congregation, of disciples.

It was at Kartarpur that the enduring institutions of Sikhism took shape. The Guru enjoined upon his followers the threefold discipline still summarised as *kirat karo* (to earn an honest living), *vand chhako* (to share one's earnings with the needy) and *naam japo* (to remember the divine Name). He instituted the *langar*, the communal kitchen where all — regardless of caste, creed or station — sat together in a single row, or *pangat*, to eat; and he established the daily rhythm of collective devotion, the singing of hymns at dawn and dusk. Kartarpur was thus the first Sikh commune and the model upon which every later gurdwara has been built.

## The Passing of the Guru

Before his death, Guru Nanak set aside his own sons in favour of a devoted disciple, Bhai Lehna, whom he renamed Angad — "of my own limb" — and installed as the second Guru, thereby founding the line of succession by spiritual merit rather than heredity that would run through ten Gurus. Guru Nanak died at Kartarpur, by tradition on 22 September 1539.

A cherished legend, preserved in the *Janamsakhi* tradition, surrounds his passing. His Hindu and Muslim followers, it is said, fell to disputing whether his body should be cremated or buried; but when the sheet covering him was lifted, only a heap of flowers remained. Each community took its share — the one to cremate, the other to inter — and each raised a memorial. To this day the shrine is understood to hold both a *samadhi* and a grave, side by side, a fitting emblem for a teacher who preached the unity beneath the divisions of religion.

## The Shrine

The original memorial by the Ravi was, over the centuries, repeatedly threatened by the shifting course of the river, which is traditionally said to have swept away earlier structures. The present white-domed gurdwara dates from the early twentieth century and is said to have been built with the patronage of the Maharaja of Patiala. In the run-up to 2019 the complex was extensively restored and enlarged, its precincts expanded to receive great numbers of visitors; by some reckonings it now ranks among the largest gurdwaras in the world. Within, the *Guru Granth Sahib* is installed and read, and *langar* is served to all who come.

## Pilgrimage and the Kartarpur Corridor

The Partition of 1947 left Kartarpur in Pakistan and the town of Dera Baba Nanak, only a few kilometres away, in India — near enough that Indian pilgrims long gathered at a raised viewing platform on their side of the border simply to gaze at the domes through binoculars. The idea of a dedicated corridor was aired as early as 1999, but it was for the 550th anniversary of Guru Nanak's birth that it finally came to pass. Foundation stones were laid on both sides in November 2018, and the corridor was inaugurated on 9 November 2019, when a first *jatha* of several hundred Indian pilgrims crossed. The passage allows Indian Sikhs to visit the shrine visa-free, returning the same day, and has been widely hailed as a rare gesture of goodwill between two often estranged neighbours.

## Significance Today

For Sikhs, Kartarpur is where their Guru laid down the pattern of a whole way of life — work, worship and shared bread — and where he closed his earthly journey. The opening of the corridor transformed a shrine long visible but unreachable into a place of living pilgrimage once more, drawing thousands across the border and restoring Kartarpur to its central place in the devotional geography of the Panth.

The book's "Kartarpur Sahib", "about an hour away from the city of Narowal", on the Ravi (p. 302, folio 276); once printed "Kartarpur Sahih" [OCR?] [Kartarpur Sahib] (p. 18, folio not stated). Other Kartarpurs in the book (Hargobind's, Harkrishan's, Har Rai's) are not identified with this row — see "Figures and sites not in the archive".

- **Saint** (Nanak's settlement here)
  - "Having spent twenty-four years of his life travelling, Nanak had finally settled down at a place called Kartarpur on the banks of the Ravi. Here he had started working on a piece of land that was given to him by one of his devotees"; he was joined by "his parents and also his wife and children"; devotees moved there; "Using Kartarpur as his base, Nanak now started travelling to nearby regions" (p. 288, folio 262). "He sent for his family from Talwindi [OCR?] [Talwandi] and started living at this small village called Kartarpur" (p. 296, folio 270).
  - "It had now been seventeen years that Nanak had been leading a sedentary life. Hundreds of devotees from neighbouring villages and beyond came to visit him every day"; kirtan every evening; "eventually a new hall was constructed which could host the huge gathering. This place was reserved for the evening prayers and was referred to as a Gurdwara by Nanak, where the Ultimate Guru resided" (p. 298, folio 272).
  - "The story of Nanak, the Gurus and us, converges at Kartarpur Sahih [OCR?] [Kartarpur Sahib] where Guru Nanak lay on his deathbed, breathing life into a new religi[on]" (p. 18, folio not stated; this line alone does not say which Kartarpur).
  - Back matter: "After his travels he settled at Kartarpur"; Guru Angad "became a disciple of Guru Nanak after he had settled at Kartarpur Sahib" (p. 314, folio 288).
  - Author's reconstruction: Nanak lives "in a simple hut that was built of wood and hay" and walks each dawn "towards the Ravi that flowed close to his hut", bathing there "for the past seventeen years" (p. 293, folio 267); he wishes he could have buried Mardana at "Kartarpur, his final abode" (damaged passage) (p. 293, folio 267); at the evening gathering Sulakhni and other women "prepared for the langar which concluded the evening", and Nanak tells her "that Muslims and even untouchables would be welcome to the langar", which at first "repulsed her" (p. 296, folio 270).
- **Shrine / Darbar** (travelogue, with Iqbal Qaiser, "a Sunday morning", year not stated)
  - Approach: "This is the border area; hence the roads are in a bad condition"; "a mud track towards the Ravi, which still flows next to the shrine"; on his first visit Iqbal Qaiser "had to walk from the main road to the gurdwara", "a road of about three kilometres", through partly flooded rice paddies (p. 302, folio 276).
  - At the gate policemen "sitting on a charpoy" drink tea; an old Muslim man and his wife "from a neighbouring village" walk in "without any trouble": "This was Kartarpur Sahib, the final resting abode of Guru Nanak and here, Muslims were allowed to enter." (p. 302, folio 276). A policeman recognises Iqbal Qaiser and waves the car in (pp. 302–303, folios 276–277).
  - "The main shrine was protected by a newly constructed wall." By the parking, "what appeared to be an airplane missile preserved in a glass case" with a marble plaque (see Legends); next to it "a well, covered with marble", whose plaque says it "was the well that was used by Guru Nanak to water his fields"; sugarcane and a peasant working the fields nearby (p. 303, folio 277).
  - Visitors take shoes off and cover heads "with skullcaps that were placed next to the entrance"; "The main structure was in the middle of a courtyard, while around it there were rooms meant to be used to house pilgrims." (p. 303, folio 277)
  - "the glass door that led into the main shrine, where the samadhi of Nanak was situated" was locked; "The main building was constructed around the samadhi, whereas the grave was on one side of the building" (p. 305, folio 279).
  - The grave: "There was a grave next to the main shrine", in the open; its description is OCR-damaged ("There was no tombonsiendgas [OCR?] its occupier"); "there was 2 [OCR?] [a] couplet etched on it" (p. 304, folio 278). Inderjit: "There is a grave of Nanak next to his samadhi" (p. 307, folio 281). The author puts it to Iqbal Qaiser that the grave might be Bhai Mardana's, "next to the samadhi of his Guru" (p. 304, folio 278) — see gurdwara-rori-sahib.
  - Iqbal Qaiser: "the original building was destroyed in a flood several years ago and this new one was built later"; the author: "The grave of Nanak became a casualty of the growing Muslim-Sikh antagonism." (p. 305, folio 279)
  - Iqbal Qaiser (partly OCR-damaged): on his first visit "this had not become a shrine [as] it is today"; it "used to be a centre for smugglers because of its proximity to the border. Drug addicts too had established a place for themselves" (pp. 307–308, folios 281–282).
  - Inderjit: "We established this gurdwara in 2001 … This is wher[e] [OCR?] we renovated the structure. We also prepared our first langar here at that time." (p. 308, folio 282). Works under way: "Right now there was only the shrine and [a] few rooms which were reserved for the caretakers. Soon there would be a langar hall as well as a pool" (OCR line order scrambled) (p. 309, folio not stated).
  - Architecture: Inderjit — "A lot of Sikhs from England and Canada have complained that the architecture of the shrine is not, strictly speaking, Sikh … it has a lot of features of Muslim architecture"; its arches "were plain. This was a feature of Muslim architecture. Sikh arches were usually three-cusped." (p. 309, folio not stated)
  - The border lies "towards the east, across the river"; the Ravi "sometimes crosses into India while at other points it comes back to Pakistan. Otherwise, the river is parched throughout the year"; had Radcliffe erred "by only a few centimetres, the shrine of Kartarpur Sahib would have ended up in India" (p. 307, folio 281).
- **Practices & Events**
  - Custodian: the Granthi "nowadays" is "Inderjit", who is "in the camps"; a young man, "probably a government official deputed here and most probably a Muslim" in the author's guess, says "No photography" (p. 305, folio 279). Inderjit's shed "had been constructed recently with the help of the Sikh Diaspora", with a double bed, a sofa set and a television showing Geo news of floods (pp. 305–306, folios 279–280).
  - Inderjit's family "belonged to a village in Faisalabad district. They weren't Sikhs but Nanak Panthi Hindus"; his garb "was that of a baptized Sikh, the Khalsa of Guru Gobind Singh"; at Partition his grandfather, on his way to India, was stopped "near the border by the Muslims of their village", who promised him security, and lived peacefully "till the war of 1965"; "In 1965, his family moved to Nankana"; he declined to discuss that period (p. 306, folio 280). The author: Inderjit "was now a government official and lived under its protection"; "the Pakistani establishment which regularly monitors the activities of Sikhs in Pakistan" (pp. 306–307, folios 280–281).
  - Muslim devotion: the Muslim couple pray at the grave; the man [OCR-damaged verb] "towards the feet of the grave, while the woman touched it with her hand and passed it over her head, blessing herself" (p. 304, folio 278). Inderjit: local Muslims "come here regularly and offer their fateha (first verse of the Quran, used as a prayer) just like they would at any Muslim shrine", and regard Nanak as a "[s]aint" (OCR "int,'") (p. 307, folio 281). Iqbal Qaiser: locals still came to the grave, presented offerings and "clean[ed] the shrine as a service", a tradition "Prevalent even before Partition" (pp. 307–308, folios 281–282).
  - Langar, per Inderjit: "rich Muslim families of the neighbouring villages provide us with monetary support to cook langar. This is their offering to the shrine"; "The wood that we require for cooking langar is provided to us by the Rangers"; Muslims at first "were hesitant to eat food cooked by Sikhs", but "Today, more Muslims eat food here compared to Sikhs." (p. 308, folio 282)
  - Across the border, behind a fence, "Indian pilgrims, who gather there to perform darshan of the shrine of Guru Nanak" through binoculars; "Hundreds of people gather there every day" (p. 308, folio 282).
  - "There are talks to declare this region a peace corridor", under which Indian pilgrims would enter "without a visa"; "The Pakistani government has given its approval to the Proposal; however the Indian government is not ready to implement the deal", saying it "would be used b[y] [OCR?] terrorists" — speaker apparently Iqbal Qaiser, OCR damaged (pp. 308–309, folio 282 / folio not stated).
- **Legends & Miracles**
  - The plaque by the parking: the missile "was dropped here by the Indian forces in 1971 but due to the miracle of Guru Nanak, it did not blow up" (p. 303, folio 277).
  - Local Muslims' "myth", as Inderjit tells it: "the river floods every twenty odd years and comes right up to the shrine. This is the river paying tribute to the shrine of Nanak." (p. 307, folio 281)
  - Inderjit: a "giant snake" "bigger than two humans combined"; snakes found in the fields are placed next to the shrine: "After paying their respects they return. All they want to do is to catch a sight of the shrine." (p. 309, folio not stated)
- **Significance**
  - The author: "Had Nanak supervised the construction of his own shrine, would he have allowed Muslim influences in the building, which the Sikh Diaspora now complained against?" (p. 310, folio 284)

### Person-level material: Guru Nanak

*The book's material headed "Guru Nanak (person-level: 18 rows)", copied into every existing entry that heading lists (ruling (a); Rauf, 10 October 2026). It is about the person, not about this shrine alone.*

General Nanak material not tied to a named archive site. Much of it is the AUTHOR'S RECONSTRUCTION, marked on each line.

- **Saint**
  - Back matter: "1. Guru Nanak (1469-1539)", "the first Guru, is believed to be the founder of Sikhism"; after lost text, "pilgrimages including Banaras, Hardwar, Mecca and Baghdad, challenging their rituals, dogma, and the caste system" (p. 314, folio 288). Birth at Talwandi/Nankana is in the Nankana Sahib group.
  - Nanak "spent a major portion of his life in present-day Pakistan. He was born here", first preached travelling this region, "and eventually settled in a small village which is now on the Pakistani side of the border" (p. 16, folio not stated).
  - Family: "Nanak belonged to the Bedi caste" (p. 100, folio 82); Iqbal Qaiser — "Baba Nanak had two sons, Sri Chand and Sri Lachman"; Sri Chand "became an ascetic and never married"; the lineage "is therefore preserved through his younger son", called Bedi (p. 44, folio 26). The Udasi sect "is believed to have been founded by the eldest son of Guru Nanak, Sri Chand", who, "Ignored by his father when it was time to appoint the next Guru", founded his own sect (p. 36, folio 18); Iqbal Qaiser calls it the first "anti-Guru" movement; Daleep says Sri Chand never accepted the Gurudom of Guru Angad Dev (p. 38, folio 20).
  - Author's reconstruction of his youth: Mehta Kalu calls the family "Bedis ... the ancient orators of the Vedas" and himself "an accountant"; Nanak herded buffaloes, which once entered a field of "Raj Kumar" [OCR?] (p. 63, folio 45; p. 64, folio 46); he was found hiding "in the growe [OCR?] [grove] of wax [OCR?] [waan] trees outside town" by his father and "his employer and friend, Rai Bular" (p. 64, folio 46); he sits at dusk under "this waan tree", cloths "tied as supplication" on its branches, "about two furlongs" from home, hearing the azaan and temple bells (p. 59, folio 41; p. 60, folio 42); he refuses the sacred thread ("janeu") proposed by his father and "the local priest of Rai Bhoi di Talwindi", which "caused a furore", and "Respectable Hindus started to avoid the Bedi" family (pp. 61–62, folios 43–44); his mentor is "Sayyed Hassan, a Muslim Sufi, who lived near his house", who argued "Islam teaches one to adopt the middle path" (p. 62, folio 44). Marriage to "Sulakhni", "the daughter of Moolchand Khatri from the village of Pakhoke near Gurdaspur"; "Soon after their marriage the young couple was blessed with a son who was named Sri Chand" (p. 67, folio 49); at Sultanpur he worked for "the Governor of Sultanpur as a manager of the Nawyh's [OCR?] [Nawab's] provision store", and a year after Sulakhni joined him "they were blessed with another son, whom they named Lachman Das" (p. 68, folio 50).
  - Author's reconstruction at Sultanpur: storekeeper to "the Governor of Sultanpur, Daulat Lodh[i] Khan"; nobles attacked him over "'There is no Hindu, no Muslim'" and alleged theft, but "when an audit was conducted, the accounts were shown to be in perfect order" (p. 106, folio 88); every night Mardana played the rubab while Nanak sang — a daily feature "for the rest of their [lives] and also for the Sikh community"; his audience "included both Hindus and Muslims" (p. 106, folio 88; p. 107, folio 89). He tells Lalo he "used to be in charge of the food store at the court of the Diwa[n] [OCR?] of Sultanpur" and quit to "take up this garb and travel the world in search of Truth" (pp. 104–105, folios 86–87); his garb deliberately mixed traditions — a red, not green, dervish's choga, a fakir's white belt, a cap partly covered by a turban, wooden sandals — "a conscious effort by Nanak to dilut[e] his religio[us] identity" (pp. 105–106, folios 87–88). "It is widely believed that it was in Sultanpur that the religious life of Nanak began" (p. 54, folio 36).
  - Travels: east "all the way to Bengal", south "to the island of Sri Lanka", north incl. "Kashmir, Nepal" and "Tibet", then west "to Mecca and returned to settle at Kartarpur"; mostly on foot, "about twenty-four years" (p. 54, folio 36); Nanak and Mardana "would not return to a sedentary life for twenty-four years" and "would travel thousands of kilometres, that too on foot" (p. 108, folio 90). Nanak "preferred to stay on the outskirts" of villages, "a recurrent theme", in tune with "moderation" and "a middle course" (p. 53, folio 35); in the reconstruction he prefers sleeping under trees to caravanserais (p. 120, folio 102). "Whenever he would visit an area he would visit its religious shrines, Muslim, Hindu and of any other denomination" (p. 37, folio 19).
  - Reconstructed journeys: the first goal is Saidpur, the city "with the grandest Hindu temples" (p. 108, folio 90); then "the historic city of Multan", "this holy city in the southern region of Punjab" (p. 122, folio 104) — he "was particularly fascinated" by Multan because "it signified the religious syncretism that he yearned for" (Mecca sacred only to Muslims, Benaras to Hindus, "Multan was revered by all"), and "was disappointed to see that the religious syncretism he had imagined was non-existent" (p. 123, folio 105); the caravanserai of "Sajjan and Gaijan [OCR?] [Gajjan]" near "the village of Makhdoom Pur Pahuran", where the innkeepers plot to rob and kill them, Nanak sings "The herons in their white feathers dwell in the sacred shrines of pilgrimage", Sajjan confesses, and the house becomes "a House of God" — "This was to become the first Gurdwara of the Sikh religion" (pp. 123–126, folios 105–108); a forest mound near Pakpattan where he meets Sheikh Kamal (p. 139, folio 121) and hears of Farid "while I was living in Sultanpur and also while I was in Lahore" (p. 141, folio 123); Sheikh Ibrahim's questioning on a mound "a few kilometres away from the city and the shrine" — "If I say I am a Hindu, I would be telling a lie. I am also not a Muslim", "a servant of God and a brother of true devotees" (p. 146, folio 128), the poem "Launch ye the boat of austere contemplation …" (pp. 148–149, folios 130–131); home via Lahore to Kanganpur after Bengal and Sri Lanka, where villagers take them for thugs "who had come from Kasur" and stone them (pp. 162–164, folios 144–146); Manakdeke; Sialkot with Maula Karar, where Nanak sends Mardana to buy "two paisas worth of truth and two paisas worth of untruth" and Karar's chits read "Life is false" and "Death is truth" (pp. 187–188, folios 161–162), then shrines around Sialkot (p. 189, folio not stated); Deepalpur, where he will not "go into to the city" for a temple bhajan (p. 207, folio 181); Nuri the leper (pp. 208–209, folios 182–183), "thirteen years" from home (p. 208, folio 182); the sea-god's cave temple with a ship's captain who turns out to be "a Jew" (p. 220, folio 194); a Shiva temple where he watches milk poured over the shiv ling "with scepticism", sits with "his feet dangling in the holy pond", tells the priest "I believe in Shiva. He is as much my guru as he is yours" and "My Shiva is the Supreme Creator", and sings the aarti "under a lone tree on the mountain facing the temple" with Mardana (pp. 240–245, folios not stated / 215–219); Nanak and Mardana leave Talwandi "towards the cold North" while Sulakhni and "her two sons Sri Chand and Lachman Das" watch (p. 239, folio 213); Makkah for the Hajj and Baghdad on the way back, and a finding that "there was no single form of Islam" — Bengal Muslims venerating "Devi Durga", caste in "South India", sleeping in the Kaaba in "Arabia", Shia paintings in "Baghdad" of the Prophet and "All. [OCR?] [Ali]" (p. 287, folio 261). "Nanak visited Uch Sharif on his way to Sindh, towards the end of his travels … He had now settled at Kartarpur in Punjab and would travel occasionally to neighbouring areas" (p. 278, folio 252). "It is recorded in Sikh heritage that he came to the village of Kolachi and then visited the temple of a sea god for which he had to take a boat and travel to an island neighbouring Kolachi" (p. 229, folio not stated).
  - The author: Nanak "must have visited" Tibba Haji Deen (then Kothewal) "around the end of the fifteenth century" (p. 150, folio 132); in Benaras "there are legends that Nanak met Kabir but that too is historically not proven" (p. 153, folio 135).
  - Succession: "On his deathbed, he appointed not his son but his most loyal student as his spiritual successor" (p. 16, folio not stated); "one of his favourite disciples, who beca[me] famous as Guru Angad" (p. 100, folio 82); "Guru Nanak preferred Angad Dev over his son Sri Chand" (p. 273, folio not stated); Guru Angad "was given the title of Guru Angad by Guru Nanak when he appointed him as his successor bypassing his Progeny" (p. 314, folio 288); Nanak asked Angad Dev "to move to Khadur as opposed to Kartarpu! _yahib [OCR?] [Kartarpur Sahib] where Nanak was settled. This was to avoid the ire of his sons" (p. 290, folio 264). Author's reconstruction: "Bhai Lehna" (also "Lebna" [OCR?]) of Khadur, "Born into an orthodox family", came to Kartarpur Sahib instead of "the temple of Durga where he was headed" (p. 297, folio 271) and "decided to collect the poetry of Nanak in written form" when Punjabi was "an oral language" with "no script" (damaged) (p. 299, folio 273); the sons disappointed him — "Lachman … had become a businessman", Sri Chand "highly inspired by the yogis" (p. 297, folio 271); Sri Chand objects that Lehna "still worships Durga in his house", and Sulakhni says "You should listen to your blood" (p. 300, folio 274); Nanak tells Sri Chand "it is not in your destiny to be my spiritual descendant. That has to be Bhai Lehna", walks by "the currents of the Ravi", returns "to the gurdwara he had constructed" where Lehna and "Baba Budha, another loyal disciple of Nanak" wait (p. 301, folio 275; the scene names no place), calls Lehna "my angad (of one's body)", has Baba Budha "apply saffron paste on Bhai Lehna's forehead", lays "five copper coins and a coconut at Lehna's feet", and announces "I have appointed Bhai Lehna, who is from now on to be known as Guru Angad, as my successor" (pp. 301–302, folios 275–276). Family in the reconstruction: "his wife and two sons" left in Punjab; Sulakhni "a simple woman who did not understand spiritual concepts" (p. 294, folio not stated); sons "Sri Chand" (also "Sai Chand" [OCR?], "Sc Chand" [OCR?]) and "Sri Lachman" (p. 295, folio 269); during his wanderings he "had no fixed routine … This is how he had travelled the four directions of the world" (p. 294, folio not stated).
- **Practices & Events**
  - Poetry quoted: the aarti ("The sky the salver, the sun and moon the lamps …", footnoted to Dhillon, *The First Sikh Spiritual Master*, "page 89"), which Iqbal Qaiser reads as God residing "in nature", calling Nanak "a naturalist" (p. 55, folio 37; p. 56, folio 38), and in another translation "In the salver of the firmament, / The sun and the moon shine as lamps" (pp. 243–244, folios 217–218); "O my merchant friend" (pp. 56–58, folio 40); "My Master is One / He is One, brother, the only One" (p. 60, folio 42); the janeu poem, "From the cotton of compassion, / Spin the thread of contentment" (pp. 61–62, folios 43–44); verses on "the word" (pp. 85–86, folios 67–68); "God is One, / The Path is One" (p. 144, folio 126); "As a beggar goes a-begging …" (p. 158, folio 140); on Karar's death, "Friendship with a money- monger / Js [OCR?] [Is] false" (p. 191, folio 165); "As hands or feet besmirched with slime, / Water washes white" (p. 192, folio 166); "His goodness cannot be priced or traded …" (p. 280, folio 254). Babur Bani, "the first poem of Nanak that I read", with "Coming in seventy-eight (1521 C.E), they will depart in Ninety sear, [OCR?] (1540 C_E.) [OCR?]" (pp. 113–114, folios 95–96).
  - The Guru Granth Sahib "contains the spiritual poetry of the various Sikh Gurus, including Cur [OCR?] [Guru] Nanak and other spiritual teachers like Bhagat Kabir, Baba Farid and Bhai Mardana" (p. 82, folio 64); later Gurus' poems are recorded "not in their own names but in that of Guru Nanak" (p. 98, folio 80); Guru Arjan "has the greatest number of hymns … followed by Guru Nanak" (p. 315, folio 289; Nanak's figure is illegible at p. 256, folio 230); Ram Rai "misquoted a poem by Guru Nanak" to appease Aurangzeb (p. 316, folio 290) — the quoted poem on "The clay of a Muslim's grave" is largely destroyed in OCR (p. 212, folio 186).
  - Travelogue: a Muslim storyteller at Gurdwara Sachkand calls him "a true devotee of God. He was a rightly guided soul" — "Our elders told us. They used to live with the Sikhs before Pakistan was made" (p. 75, folio 57); Nanak is still regarded as holy "particularly by these" Muslims [OCR?] of areas he visited (damaged) (p. 196, folio 170); Iqbal Qaiser's wife took down "a poster of Nanak", "thinking that it would be a sin to have a picture of a Sikh saint in a Muslim household"; family, students and friends call the author "a Hindu or a Sikh" (p. 69, folio not stated); the author covered "more than one thousand kilometres, visiting various sites associated with Nanak" on one Lahore trip [OCR?] (p. 192, folio 166).
- **Legends & Miracles**
  - The Naulakha Hazari shrine booklet (Auqaf): "Guru Nanak was born after Baba Naulakha Hazari prayed for his mother" (p. 31, folio 13).
  - "According to legend, Nanak once travelled to Mecca where he performed Hajj"; he slept with his feet toward the Kaaba, was rebuked, turned his feet "and as he did so, the Kaaba miraculously turned towards his feet"; the author doubts the miracle: "What is important in this story is Nanak's criticism of the concept of a 'sacred space'" (p. 82, folio 64).
  - Footnoted legend ("It is believed"): a trader told Nanak his mules carried "Just some mud"; his "sugar and gur (jaggery) had turned into mud"; when he repented "his mud became Sugar and gur once again" (p. 71, folio 53).
  - The Janamsakhis record a meeting between Nanak and Babur at Saidpur, where Babur asks for a blessing to conquer India and Nanak rebukes him (p. 114, folio 96); the encounter "was also interpreted to mark the beginning of this antagonism between the Mughals and the Sikhs" (p. 97, folio 79). Jahangir "was the great grandson of Emperor Babur who had incarcerated Guru Nanak" (p. 133, folio not stated).
  - Hamza Ghous at Sialkot (set-off legend narrative): the Sufi threatened to destroy the city after forty days of prayer; Nanak, under the berry tree, knocked on his door for days; thunder struck the dome and "Baba Guru Nanak saved the people of Sialkot from annibilation [OCR?] [annihilation]" (pp. 197–198, folios 171–172). Pasrur, "similar to that of Sialkot": Nanak humbles "an arrogant Muslim Sufi by the name of Mian Mitha", who threatens to "churn him"; Nanak will squeeze him "as one squeezes a lemon"; Mian Mitha comes barefoot, is answered "in Islamic iconography", touches and kisses [OCR? "hissed"] Nanak's feet and becomes his devotee; "This discourse is recorded in the Guru Granth Sahib" (pp. 200–201, folios 174–175; verses footnoted to MacAuliffe [OCR? "MacAulif Te"], *The Sikh Religion*, 1907, pp. 201–202, folios 175–176). The author: "pointless stories"; "His genius lies in the poetic use of words and not in miracles"; Iqbal Qaiser: "That is to show that our saint is more powerful than yours" (p. 199, folio 173).
  - Author's reconstruction: Nanak knew the innkeepers' intent "through divine knowledge", as Sajjan concludes (p. 126, folio 108); he knows Kamal is there "even though his eyes had been shut throughout the song" (p. 140, folio 122); the forest mound was where "the spirits and djinns of the forest resided" (p. 139, folio 121); he blesses hostile Kanganpur ("May the village of Kanganpur prosper!", p. 175, folio 149) and wishes hospitable Manakdeke would "never prosper" and its people "scatter", so that the one keeps its ill-will "within its walls" and the other spreads goodwill — Mardana: "Strange are the ways of the Guru!" (pp. 178–179, folio not stated / folio 153); the author later notes Manakdeke is "still a small village like Nanak hoped" (p. 178, folio not stated) and wonders "if Guru Nanak's prophecy is coming true" at expanding Kanganpur (p. 164, folio 146). "Karar's wife did not know that Nanak could not perform any miracles" (p. 190, folio 164).
- **Significance** (the author's arguments)
  - Nanak "vehemently spoke against organised religion", "abhorred the concept of associating miracles with religious personalities", yet "his biography is nothing but a story of his miracles" (p. 16, folio not stated); "a couple of successors later 'Guruhood' became a family affair" (p. 17, folio not stated); he "was repulsed by the concept of making a larger-than-life figure out of a religious leader" and "comes across as a rationalist" (p. 112, folio 94; verse cited to Dhillon, p. vii, at p. 113, folio 95).
  - "Guru Nanak spoke vehemently against religious institutions and rituals. **Life (c. 042)**
- "دوازده کروهے بتاله برلب دریاے راوی مکان بابا نانک است که تا حال اولادش درانجا سکونت دارد" (p. 520).
- He was "قافله سالار مسالک حقیقت و مشعله دار طریق طریقت"; he composed "اشعار هندی" on the truth of God and set out divine unity "بعبارات واضحه و استعارات لایحه" (p. 520).
- Birth: "گویند" he was born "درسنه هزار و پانصد و بست و شش بکرماجیت مطابق هشتصد و هشتاد هجری درزمان سلطان بهلول لودی"; for the birthplace see Talwandi below (p. 520).
- "درخورد سالگی(۱) علامات کشف و کرامات و ایات خارق عادات از و بمنصه ظهور رسید" (editor's footnote variant: "ده سالگی") (p. 520).
- He travelled most of the world, then came to "قصبه بتاله", married ("کدخدا گردید"), and settled "دردهی از دیهات بتاله برآب دریاے راوی"; the village is not named (p. 520).
- "عالم عالم خلایق از اطراف ممالک آمده مرید شدند" (p. 520). "مردانه نام مطرب" sang his verses and drew people "در دام عقیدت" (p. 520).
- Death: "درعهد سلطنت سلیم شاه افغان درعمر مابین هفتاد و هشتاد سالگی" (p. 520).
- Succession: he had a son, "لکهمیداس نام خلف رشید", but "چون دولت معنوی نصیب او نبود" he passed him over and gave "لهنه نام کهتری عرف ترهن[OCR?]" the title Guru Angad and made him successor "دروقت نزع" (p. 520).

**His followers (c. 041, 045, 046)**
- "از مریدان و معتقدان بابا نانک اکثرے صاحب حال و مقبول المقال و اهل ریاضات و مستجاب الدعوات می باشند" (p. 519).
- "خلاصه عبادت اینطایفه مطالعه اشعار مرشد خویش است که بسرود و نغمه میگویند" (p. 519). Many have put away worldly ties; kin and stranger, friend and foe are the same to them, and they live "با دوستان یکرنگ و با دشمنان بی جنگ" (p. 519).
- "اعتقادی که این فریق بر مقتداے خویش دارند در طوایف دیگر کم نشان یافته میشود" (p. 519). Their master's name is always "ورد زبان"; they count serving travellers ("درخدمت صادر و وارد") as "عبادت عظمی" (p. 519). If someone arrives at midnight and speaks Baba Nanak's name, they treat him as brother and friend and serve him, even a stranger, a thief, a highwayman or an evildoer (p. 519).
