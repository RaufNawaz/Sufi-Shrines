## Overview

Tilla Jogian — the "Hill of the Ascetics" — crowns the highest peak of the eastern Salt Range, rising to some 975 metres above sea level, in the Jhelum District of the Punjab. On its summit lie the ruins of an ancient temple and monastic complex that was, before 1947, the foremost centre of Hindu yogis in the whole of the Punjab. Cooler and greener than the parched country below, the hilltop has for many centuries been a retreat for renunciants seeking solitude, and its long association with the *Nath* order of yogis, with Guru Nanak, and with the lovers of Punjabi legend has made it one of the most storied heights in the region.

## History and the Nath Tradition

Tradition traces the sanctity of Tilla Jogian back some two thousand years and links its foundation to *Gorakhnath*, the revered yogi and organiser of the *Nath* tradition — though this popular chronology is internally inconsistent (scholarship usually places the historical Gorakhnath centuries later, c. 11th–12th century CE) and should be read as legend rather than settled history. It became the principal seat of the *Kanphata* yogis — the "split-eared" ascetics, so called from the great rings inserted through the cartilage of their ears at initiation — an order that looked to Gorakhnath as its master. On the hilltop the *jogis* maintained shrines, cells, and reservoirs of water, and the complex is said at its height to have sheltered hundreds of ascetics who came to meditate in its heights. The site formed the easternmost of a string of ancient Hindu sanctuaries strung along the Salt Range, a chain that also includes the temples of Katas Raj and, at its western end, the Amb temples above the Soon Valley.

Such was its renown as a place of Hindu spiritual power that it drew, in the early sixteenth century, *Guru Nanak*, the founder of the Sikh faith, who is remembered in tradition as having spent forty days in meditation upon the hill during his travels. The episode became part of the sacred memory of the site for Sikhs, adding another layer to a place already venerated by yogis and by Hindu pilgrims from across the Punjab.

## Legend and Literature

Tilla Jogian occupies a special place in Punjabi folklore through the tragic romance of *Heer and Ranjha*, immortalised in the eighteenth century by the Sufi poet *Waris Shah*. In the tale, Ranjha, parted from his beloved Heer, climbs to Tilla Jogian in his grief and there renounces the world: the ascetic *Bal Nath*, master of the hill, pierces his ears and initiates him into the order of the *jogis*, granting him *jog*, the discipline of renunciation. Through this episode the hill entered the imaginative life of the Punjab as the very emblem of the lover-turned-ascetic, and generations who never climbed its slopes knew its name from the recitation of Waris Shah's verses.

## Decline and Present State

The complex flourished until the Partition of 1947, when the departure of its Hindu and yogi custodians for India left it without a resident community. Deprived of care, the buildings fell into decay; the reservoirs silted, the shrines crumbled, and in the decades since the site has been steadily overgrown — by the early twenty-first century much of it lay beneath a spread of wild olive and scrub, its masonry slipping into ruin. Conservationists and writers have repeatedly warned that this remarkable monument, at once archaeological site, sacred summit, and literary landmark, risks being lost altogether without deliberate protection. Its remoteness has spared it the crowds that press upon lower shrines, but that same isolation has hastened its neglect.

## Legacy

Tilla Jogian gathers into one windswept hilltop several of the great strands of Punjabi religious and cultural history: the antiquity of the *Nath* yogis and their Kanphata order; the pilgrim memory of Guru Nanak; and the deathless romance of Heer and Ranjha. As one of the trio of Salt Range sanctuaries alongside Katas Raj and the Amb temples, it testifies to a landscape once dense with Hindu holiness. For the modern traveller it offers a rare and melancholy grandeur — ruined shrines amid olive groves, commanding a vast prospect over the Jhelum plains — and stands as a reminder of a plural sacred past that survives now chiefly in stone and in song.
