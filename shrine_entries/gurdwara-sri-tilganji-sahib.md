## Current site description (as of 12 October 2026)

*The row's `Description` cell exactly as it appears in `src/data/shrines-fallback.json` on 12 October 2026, untouched. Not yet checked against sources.*

Gurdwara Sri Tilganji Sahib stands on Masjid Road in the heart of Quetta, the capital of Balochistan. By Sikh tradition the site marks a visit by Guru Nanak, the first Guru, during one of his great journeys, or udasis: it is said that the Guru honoured the assembled sangat by distributing til — sesame seeds — as parshad, the sanctified offering, and that from this the shrine took its name, Tilganji deriving from til (sesame) and ganj (treasury or store). The gurdwara is a substantial domed building whose main gateway is said to resemble that of a mosque; within its precincts, in a pattern common to many gurdwaras in Pakistan after 1947, a government school — the Government Sandeman School — has long functioned, occupying land belonging to the shrine. Quetta was almost entirely destroyed in the catastrophic earthquake of 1935, so that the present fabric of the building, like much of the city, is unlikely to be of great antiquity, even if the sanctity of the site is older. The gurdwara is one of the principal Sikh shrines of the city and is to be distinguished from the Gurdwara Singh Sabha of Quetta, recorded separately in this dataset. Iqbal Qaiser's 1998 survey of Sikh shrines in Pakistan records that, in addition to the Sandeman School already functioning within the shrine's precincts, a new government school building had by then been constructed on vacant land belonging to the gurdwara, and that the Sangat of Quetta had filed suit in the lower courts seeking to recover possession of the property.

### Person-level material: Guru Nanak

General Nanak material not tied to a named archive site. Much of it is the AUTHOR'S RECONSTRUCTION, marked on each line.

- **Saint**
  - Back matter: "1. Guru Nanak (1469-1539)", "the first Guru, is believed to be the founder of Sikhism"; after lost text, "pilgrimages including Banaras, Hardwar, Mecca and Baghdad, challenging their rituals, dogma, and the caste system" (p. 314, folio 288). Birth at Talwandi/Nankana is in the Nankana Sahib group.
  - Nanak "spent a major portion of his life in present-day Pakistan. He was born here", first preached travelling this region, "and eventually settled in a small village which is now on the Pakistani side of the border" (p. 16, folio not stated).
  - Family: "Nanak belonged to the Bedi caste" (p. 100, folio 82); Iqbal Qaiser — "Baba Nanak had two sons, Sri Chand and Sri Lachman"; Sri Chand "became an ascetic and never married"; the lineage "is therefore preserved through his younger son", called Bedi (p. 44, folio 26). The Udasi sect "is believed to have been founded by the eldest son of Guru Nanak, Sri Chand", who, "Ignored by his father when it was time to appoint the next Guru", founded his own sect (p. 36, folio 18); Iqbal Qaiser calls it the first "anti-Guru" movement; Daleep says Sri Chand never accepted the Gurudom of Guru Angad Dev (p. 38, folio 20).
  - Author's reconstruction of his youth: Mehta Kalu calls the family "Bedis ... the ancient orators of the Vedas" and himself "an accountant"; Nanak herded buffaloes, which once entered a field of "Raj Kumar" [OCR?] (p. 63, folio 45; p. 64, folio 46); he was found hiding "in the growe [OCR?] [grove] of wax [OCR?] [waan] trees outside town" by his father and "his employer and friend, Rai Bular" (p. 64, folio 46); he sits at dusk under "this waan tree", cloths "tied as supplication" on its branches, "about two furlongs" from home, hearing the azaan and temple bells (p. 59, folio 41; p. 60, folio 42); he refuses the sacred thread ("janeu") proposed by his father and "the local priest of Rai Bhoi di Talwindi", which "caused a furore", and "Respectable Hindus started to avoid the Bedi" family (pp. 61–62, folios 43–44); his mentor is "Sayyed Hassan, a Muslim Sufi, who lived near his house", who argued "Islam teaches one to adopt the middle path" (p. 62, folio 44). Marriage to "Sulakhni", "the daughter of Moolchand Khatri from the village of Pakhoke near Gurdaspur"; "Soon after their marriage the young couple was blessed with a son who was named Sri Chand" (p. 67, folio 49); at Sultanpur he worked for "the Governor of Sultanpur as a manager of the Nawyh's [OCR?] [Nawab's] provision store", and a year after Sulakhni joined him "they were blessed with another son, whom they named Lachman Das" (p. 68, folio 50).
  - Author's reconstruction at Sultanpur: storekeeper to "the Governor of Sultanpur, Daulat Lodh[i] Khan"; nobles attacked him over "'There is no Hindu, no Muslim'" and alleged theft, but "when an audit was conducted, the accounts were shown to be in perfect order" (p. 106, folio 88); every night Mardana played the rubab while Nanak sang — a daily feature "for the rest of their [lives] and also for the Sikh community"; his audience "included both Hindus and Muslims" (p. 106, folio 88; p. 107, folio 89). He tells Lalo he "used to be in charge of the food store at the court of the Diwa[n] [OCR?] of Sultanpur" and quit to "take up this garb and travel the world in search of Truth" (pp. 104–105, folios 86–87); his garb deliberately mixed traditions — a red, not green, dervish's choga, a fakir's white belt, a cap partly covered by a turban, wooden sandals — "a conscious effort by Nanak to dilut[e] his religio[us] identity" (pp. 105–106, folios 87–88). "It is widely believed that it was in Sultanpur that the religious life of Nanak began" (p. 54, folio 36).
  - Travels: east "all the way to Bengal", south "to the island of Sri Lanka", north incl. "Kashmir, Nepal" and "Tibet", then west "to Mecca and returned to settle at Kartarpur"; mostly on foot, "about twenty-four years" (p. 54, folio 36); Nanak and Mardana "would not return to a sedentary life for twenty-four years" and "would travel thousands of kilometres, that too on foot" (p. 108, folio 90). Nanak "preferred to stay on the outskirts" of villages, "a recurrent theme", in tune with "moderation" and "a middle course" (p. 53, folio 35); in the reconstruction he prefers sleeping under trees to caravanserais (p. 120, folio 102). "Whenever he would visit an area he would visit its religious shrines, Muslim, Hindu and of any other denomination" (p. 37, folio 19).
  - Reconstructed journeys: the first goal is Saidpur, the city "with the grandest Hindu temples" (p. 108, folio 90); then "the historic city of Multan", "this holy city in the southern region of Punjab" (p. 122, folio 104) — he "was particularly fascinated" by Multan because "it signified the religious syncretism that he yearned for" (Mecca sacred only to Muslims, Benaras to Hindus, "Multan was revered by all"), and "was disappointed to see that the religious syncretism he had imagined was non-existent" (p. 123, folio 105); the caravanserai of "Sajjan and Gaijan [OCR?] [Gajjan]" near "the village of Makhdoom Pur Pahuran", where the innkeepers plot to rob and kill them, Nanak sings "The herons in their white feathers dwell in the sacred shrines of pilgrimage", Sajjan confesses, and the house becomes "a House of God" — "This was to become the first Gurdwara of the Sikh religion" (pp. 123–126, folios 105–108); a forest mound near Pakpattan where he meets Sheikh Kamal (p. 139, folio 121) and hears of Farid "while I was living in Sultanpur and also while I was in Lahore" (p. 141, folio 123); Sheikh Ibrahim's questioning on a mound "a few kilometres away from the city and the shrine" — "If I say I am a Hindu, I would be telling a lie. I am also not a Muslim", "a servant of God and a brother of true devotees" (p. 146, folio 128), the poem "Launch ye the boat of austere contemplation …" (pp. 148–149, folios 130–131); home via Lahore to Kanganpur after Bengal and Sri Lanka, where villagers take them for thugs "who had come from Kasur" and stone them (pp. 162–164, folios 144–146); Manakdeke; Sialkot with Maula Karar, where Nanak sends Mardana to buy "two paisas worth of truth and two paisas worth of untruth" and Karar's chits read "Life is false" and "Death is truth" (pp. 187–188, folios 161–162), then shrines around Sialkot (p. 189, folio not stated); Deepalpur, where he will not "go into to the city" for a temple bhajan (p. 207, folio 181); Nuri the leper (pp. 208–209, folios 182–183), "thirteen years" from home (p. 208, folio 182); the sea-god's cave temple with a ship's captain who turns out to be "a Jew" (p. 220, folio 194); a Shiva temple where he watches milk poured over the shiv ling "with scepticism", sits with "his feet dangling in the holy pond", tells the priest "I believe in Shiva. He is as much my guru as he is yours" and "My Shiva is the Supreme Creator", and sings the aarti "under a lone tree on the mountain facing the temple" with Mardana (pp. 240–245, folios not stated / 215–219); Nanak and Mardana leave Talwandi "towards the cold North" while Sulakhni and "her two sons Sri Chand and Lachman Das" watch (p. 239, folio 213); Makkah for the Hajj and Baghdad on the way back, and a finding that "there was no single form of Islam" — Bengal Muslims venerating "Devi Durga", caste in "South India", sleeping in the Kaaba in "Arabia", Shia paintings in "Baghdad" of the Prophet and "All. [OCR?] [Ali]" (p. 287, folio 261). "Nanak visited Uch Sharif on his way to Sindh, towards the end of his travels … He had now settled at Kartarpur in Punjab and would travel occasionally to neighbouring areas" (p. 278, folio 252). "It is recorded in Sikh heritage that he came to the village of Kolachi and then visited the temple of a sea god for which he had to take a boat and travel to an island neighbouring Kolachi" (p. 229, folio not stated).
  - The author: Nanak "must have visited" Tibba Haji Deen (then Kothewal) "around the end of the fifteenth century" (p. 150, folio 132); in Benaras "there are legends that Nanak met Kabir but that too is historically not proven" (p. 153, folio 135).
  - Succession: "On his deathbed, he appointed not his son but his most loyal student as his spiritual successor" (p. 16, folio not stated); "one of his favourite disciples, who beca[me] famous as Guru Angad" (p. 100, folio 82); "Guru Nanak preferred Angad Dev over his son Sri Chand" (p. 273, folio not stated); Guru Angad "was given the title of Guru Angad by Guru Nanak when he appointed him as his successor bypassing his Progeny" (p. 314, folio 288); Nanak asked Angad Dev "to move to Khadur as opposed to Kartarpu! _yahib [OCR?] [Kartarpur Sahib] where Nanak was settled. This was to avoid the ire of his sons" (p. 290, folio 264). Author's reconstruction: "Bhai Lehna" (also "Lebna" [OCR?]) of Khadur, "Born into an orthodox family", came to Kartarpur Sahib instead of "the temple of Durga where he was headed" (p. 297, folio 271) and "decided to collect the poetry of Nanak in written form" when Punjabi was "an oral language" with "no script" (damaged) (p. 299, folio 273); the sons disappointed him — "Lachman … had become a businessman", Sri Chand "highly inspired by the yogis" (p. 297, folio 271); Sri Chand objects that Lehna "still worships Durga in his house", and Sulakhni says "You should listen to your blood" (p. 300, folio 274); Nanak tells Sri Chand "it is not in your destiny to be my spiritual descendant. That has to be Bhai Lehna", walks by "the currents of the Ravi", returns "to the gurdwara he had constructed" where Lehna and "Baba Budha, another loyal disciple of Nanak" wait (p. 301, folio 275; the scene names no place), calls Lehna "my angad (of one's body)", has Baba Budha "apply saffron paste on Bhai Lehna's forehead", lays "five copper coins and a coconut at Lehna's feet", and announces "I have appointed Bhai Lehna, who is from now on to be known as Guru Angad, as my successor" (pp. 301–302, folios 275–276). Family in the reconstruction: "his wife and two sons" left in Punjab; Sulakhni "a simple woman who did not understand spiritual concepts" (p. 294, folio not stated); sons "Sri Chand" (also "Sai Chand" [OCR?], "Sc Chand" [OCR?]) and "Sri Lachman" (p. 295, folio 269); during his wanderings he "had no fixed routine … This is how he had travelled the four directions of the world" (p. 294, folio not stated).
- **Practices & Events**
  - Poetry quoted: the aarti ("The sky the salver, the sun and moon the lamps …", footnoted to Dhillon, *The First Sikh Spiritual Master*, "page 89"), which Iqbal Qaiser reads as God residing "in nature", calling Nanak "a naturalist" (p. 55, folio 37; p. 56, folio 38), and in another translation "In the salver of the firmament, / The sun and the moon shine as lamps" (pp. 243–244, folios 217–218); "O my merchant friend" (pp. 56–58, folio 40); "My Master is One / He is One, brother, the only One" (p. 60, folio 42); the janeu poem, "From the cotton of compassion, / Spin the thread of contentment" (pp. 61–62, folios 43–44); verses on "the word" (pp. 85–86, folios 67–68); "God is One, / The Path is One" (p. 144, folio 126); "As a beggar goes a-begging …" (p. 158, folio 140); on Karar's death, "Friendship with a money- monger / Js [OCR?] [Is] false" (p. 191, folio 165); "As hands or feet besmirched with slime, / Water washes white" (p. 192, folio 166); "His goodness cannot be priced or traded …" (p. 280, folio 254). Babur Bani, "the first poem of Nanak that I read", with "Coming in seventy-eight (1521 C.E), they will depart in Ninety sear, [OCR?] (1540 C_E.) [OCR?]" (pp. 113–114, folios 95–96).
  - The Guru Granth Sahib "contains the spiritual poetry of the various Sikh Gurus, including Cur [OCR?] [Guru] Nanak and other spiritual teachers like Bhagat Kabir, Baba Farid and Bhai Mardana" (p. 82, folio 64); later Gurus' poems are recorded "not in their own names but in that of Guru Nanak" (p. 98, folio 80); Guru Arjan "has the greatest number of hymns … followed by Guru Nanak" (p. 315, folio 289; Nanak's figure is illegible at p. 256, folio 230); Ram Rai "misquoted a poem by Guru Nanak" to appease Aurangzeb (p. 316, folio 290) — the quoted poem on "The clay of a Muslim's grave" is largely destroyed in OCR (p. 212, folio 186).
  - Travelogue: a Muslim storyteller at Gurdwara Sachkand calls him "a true devotee of God. He was a rightly guided soul" — "Our elders told us. They used to live with the Sikhs before Pakistan was made" (p. 75, folio 57); Nanak is still regarded as holy "particularly by these" Muslims [OCR?] of areas he visited (damaged) (p. 196, folio 170); Iqbal Qaiser's wife took down "a poster of Nanak", "thinking that it would be a sin to have a picture of a Sikh saint in a Muslim household"; family, students and friends call the author "a Hindu or a Sikh" (p. 69, folio not stated); the author covered "more than one thousand kilometres, visiting various sites associated with Nanak" on one Lahore trip [OCR?] (p. 192, folio 166).
- **Legends & Miracles**
  - The Naulakha Hazari shrine booklet (Auqaf): "Guru Nanak was born after Baba Naulakha Hazari prayed for his mother" (p. 31, folio 13).
  - "According to legend, Nanak once travelled to Mecca where he performed Hajj"; he slept with his feet toward the Kaaba, was rebuked, turned his feet "and as he did so, the Kaaba miraculously turned towards his feet"; the author doubts the miracle: "What is important in this story is Nanak's criticism of the concept of a 'sacred space'" (p. 82, folio 64).
  - Footnoted legend ("It is believed"): a trader told Nanak his mules carried "Just some mud"; his "sugar and gur (jaggery) had turned into mud"; when he repented "his mud became Sugar and gur once again" (p. 71, folio 53).
  - The Janamsakhis record a meeting between Nanak and Babur at Saidpur, where Babur asks for a blessing to conquer India and Nanak rebukes him (p. 114, folio 96); the encounter "was also interpreted to mark the beginning of this antagonism between the Mughals and the Sikhs" (p. 97, folio 79). Jahangir "was the great grandson of Emperor Babur who had incarcerated Guru Nanak" (p. 133, folio not stated).
  - Hamza Ghous at Sialkot (set-off legend narrative): the Sufi threatened to destroy the city after forty days of prayer; Nanak, under the berry tree, knocked on his door for days; thunder struck the dome and "Baba Guru Nanak saved the people of Sialkot from annibilation [OCR?] [annihilation]" (pp. 197–198, folios 171–172). Pasrur, "similar to that of Sialkot": Nanak humbles "an arrogant Muslim Sufi by the name of Mian Mitha", who threatens to "churn him"; Nanak will squeeze him "as one squeezes a lemon"; Mian Mitha comes barefoot, is answered "in Islamic iconography", touches and kisses [OCR? "hissed"] Nanak's feet and becomes his devotee; "This discourse is recorded in the Guru Granth Sahib" (pp. 200–201, folios 174–175; verses footnoted to MacAuliffe [OCR? "MacAulif Te"], *The Sikh Religion*, 1907, pp. 201–202, folios 175–176). The author: "pointless stories"; "His genius lies in the poetic use of words and not in miracles"; Iqbal Qaiser: "That is to show that our saint is more powerful than yours" (p. 199, folio 173).
  - Author's reconstruction: Nanak knew the innkeepers' intent "through divine knowledge", as Sajjan concludes (p. 126, folio 108); he knows Kamal is there "even though his eyes had been shut throughout the song" (p. 140, folio 122); the forest mound was where "the spirits and djinns of the forest resided" (p. 139, folio 121); he blesses hostile Kanganpur ("May the village of Kanganpur prosper!", p. 175, folio 149) and wishes hospitable Manakdeke would "never prosper" and its people "scatter", so that the one keeps its ill-will "within its walls" and the other spreads goodwill — Mardana: "Strange are the ways of the Guru!" (pp. 178–179, folio not stated / folio 153); the author later notes Manakdeke is "still a small village like Nanak hoped" (p. 178, folio not stated) and wonders "if Guru Nanak's prophecy is coming true" at expanding Kanganpur (p. 164, folio 146). "Karar's wife did not know that Nanak could not perform any miracles" (p. 190, folio 164).
- **Significance** (the author's arguments)
  - Nanak "vehemently spoke against organised religion", "abhorred the concept of associating miracles with religious personalities", yet "his biography is nothing but a story of his miracles" (p. 16, folio not stated); "a couple of successors later 'Guruhood' became a family affair" (p. 17, folio not stated); he "was repulsed by the concept of making a larger-than-life figure out of a religious leader" and "comes across as a rationalist" (p. 112, folio 94; verse cited to Dhillon, p. vii, at p. 113, folio 95).
  - "Guru Nanak spoke vehemently against religious institutions and rituals. At Hindu temples, he challenged the concept of aarti"; he associated with "people of lowercastes, terming them people of God", and criticised "high-caste landlords and government officials as exploiters" (p. 82, folio 64); his poems show "a disdain for [printed 'ior'] priests and Sufi pirs who manipulate the people for their own economic benefit", "a strong concept of individualism", "removing intermediaries and establishing a direct connect with God" (p. 280, folio 254); in the reconstruction his aim "was to end the exploitation of religion by priests and pandits" and to show "a direct path to God, one that doesn't travel through temples and shrines" (p. 237, folio 211), telling Benaras pandits "rituals would not help them attain salvation" (p. 238, folio not stated); he was "particularly averse" to the dervish's hashish and bhang (p. 288, folio 262).
  - "Nanak himself had been born into a Hindu household and was then educated in a Muslim milieu; however, he was neither a practising Muslim nor a Hindu"; he "offended both Hindus and Muslims by stating that there was no Hindu or Muslim" (p. 95, folio 77); the statement was questioned "at the court of Nawab Daulat Khan Lodhi at Sultanpur", "at the court of Malik Bhago in Eminabad" and at "Rai Bhoi Di Talwindi" (p. 144, folio 126). His advice to Bhai Bala and Bhai Mardana: "if one is a Muslim then one should be a good Muslim and if one is a Hindu then one should be a good Hindu" (p. 95, folio 77). "his entire movement was for the blurring of the religious divide", so religious identities "must have been … quite a social problem, for him to talk about it so much" (p. 266, folio 240); "Nanak was aware of these fluid boundaries … instead Sikhism, a distinct religion with its own exclusive boundaries, emerged out of Nanak's philosophy" (p. 310, folio 284).
  - The author tells Daleep "I believe that Nanak was influenced immensely by Islam"; Daleep: "seventy-five per cent of Nanak's teachings are similar to Islam", but not "influence" (p. 37, folio 19).
  - "Guru Nanak was a fakir or a Jogi who spent his entire lifetime in a few clothes" (p. 99, folio 81); the "Sikh" term "was derived by Nanak"; "the flame of the lamp of Nanak was passed from one Guru to the next" (p. 98, folio 80).
  - "Nanak incorporated several words which were not part of the Punjabi of the time … which is why Nanak's contribution as a poet to Punjabi is immense" (pp. 278–279, folios 252–253); named among Punjabi poets ridiculed by "the educated elite of Punjab" (p. 128, folio 110). At Uch: "He is after all as important a saint as any of these people" (p. 277, folio 251). "Some scholars have argued that the beauty of Nanak's message is that through his own example, he glorified peasantry" (p. 303, folio 277).
  - Method: "The only evidence we have is found within the Sikh religion and they are full of stories of miracles"; Iqbal Qaiser: "read what Nanak wrote at these places and try to get into his mind" (p. 286, folio 260). The author: "my depiction of Nanak in this book is not an accurate version of Nanak" but what he wants Nanak "to be like" (pp. 229–230, folio not stated / folio 204); the Nanak narrative "part of the book is fictionalised" (p. 17, folio not stated). The Janamsakhis are "Sikh texts on Nanak's life, written by his devotees after his death", "the primary sources", in "different versions" (p. 17, folio not stated); Meherban, son of Prithi Chand, "wrote a version of the Janamsakhi" (p. 43, folio 25).
  - The author, a Pakistani Punjabi, knew Nanak as "the founder of Sikhism" but found him "my own, being a Punjabi" yet "an alien, since he was a non-Muslim" (p. 15, folio not stated); he "truly discovered Guru Nanak" through Iqbal Qaiser's recitation of Babur Bani, in which Nanak "lays the blame of Babur's wrath at God's feet" and "challenges the divinity of the Divine" (pp. 15–16, folio not stated). Blurb: "not merely a story about gurdwaras", but "a re-telling of the story of Nanak the son, the poet, the wanderer, the father, the friend", tracing how "Nanak the poet became Guru Nanak the saint" (p. 2, folio not stated). He asks whether "Nanak's legacy in its true essence is also slowly disappearing" (p. 230, folio 204); in the Hassan Abdal scene Naju "just had a vague idea that the Sikhs of Punjab considered him to be their Prophet" (p. 264, folio 238).

The book's "Guru Nanak", "Nanak", "Nanak Dev", "the Guru". Person rule; no story is tied by the book to these sites except where given in the single rows above.
- **Saint** (author's Introduction and Notes)
  - The Janamsakhis are "cycles of tales purportedly drawn from the life of the first Guru of the Sikhs, Guru Nanak (f469-1539) [text-layer?] [1469-1539]" (p. 9, folio 9); Nanak's Bani "was compiled as part of the Adi Granth by Guru Arjan Dev, the fifth Guru, in 1604" (p. 9, folio 9).
  - Nanak was "no unworldly mystic" but "a householder in every sense of the word" who "brought a message of peace and harmony to believers of the mutually hostile religions of Islam and Hinduism" (p. 12, folio not stated); he taught that "all men and women were equal, irrespective of caste, creed, wealth, social standing and gender", and that a good life rested on honest labour, compassion, selfless service and prayer (pp. 12–13, folio not stated / 13).
  - "Guru Nanak's clear and emphatic disavowal of the miraculous" (printed "dear" [text-layer?] [clear]) (p. 11, folio 11).
  - The stories are placed "against the background of Guru Nanak's udasis (travels)" (p. 13, folio 13).
  - Two udasi schemes: "According to the Puratan Janamsakhi… he undertook five journeys (also known as udasis)": first "(1499-1506)", second "(1506-1513)", "Third ,ul.asi [text-layer?] [udasi] (1514-1518)", "Fourth tulasi [text-layer?] [udasi] (1519-1521)", "Fifth udasi (1523-1524)" (pp. 54–55, folios 60–61). "More modern accounts" give four, "which spanned rwency-three [text-layer?] [twenty-three] years": "(1499-1505)" central and eastern India; "(1506-1509)" southern India and Sri Lanka; "(1514-1516)" Gangetic plains to Tashkent and back via Kashmir; "(1518-1521)" Arab countries (p. 55, folio 61). The author: "Personally, I believe chat [that] he undertook four udasis and not five" (p. 56, folio 62).
  - Publisher's back-cover blurb: "The earliest known janamsakhi dates bade [text-layer?] [dates back] to 1658 nearly 120 years after the death of Guru Nanak in 1539"; "~one [text-layer?] [None] of the Janarnsakhis [text-layer?] [Janamsakhis] can claim to be the most authentic or authontatne [text-layer?] [authoritative] version" (p. 271, folio not stated).
- **Life, in Dhillon's retelling** (Talwandi material is also carried in the Nankana Sahib group below)
  - Birth "On 14 April 1469" to Kalyan Chand Das Bedi, "or Mehta Kalu as he was affectionately called", accountant of the Bhatti family under Rai Bular (pp. 17–18, folios 19–20); "Pandit Ha.rdyal [text-layer?] [Hardyal], the village astrologer" foretold greatness (p. 18, folio 20).
  - At thirteen he refused the janeu in "Mehta Kahis [text-layer?] [Mehta Kalu's]" courtyard: "it is our actions and our actions alone" that give position among men; "the beginning of a lifelong crusade against the performing of empty rites and rituals" (pp. 19–21, folios 21–23).
  - At sixteen he herded cattle (p. 23, folio 27); "became well acquainted with the dervish Sayyad Hussain and gained an intimate knowledge of the Sufi line of thought" (p. 24, folio 28).
  - "Nanak moved to Sultanpur (around 60 km from Talwandi)" as keeper of Nawab Daulat Khan's "modikhana (score [text-layer?] [store])"; bathing in "the Bein rivulet"; evening gatherings to hear his hymns (p. 44, folio 49).
  - "He got married on 24 September 1487 to the gencle [gentle], kind-hearted Sulakhini, daughter of Mul Chand and Chando Rani of the town of Batala" (p. 44, folio 49); sons "Sri Chand (8 September 1494) and Lakhmi Das (12 February 1497)" (p. 44, folio 49).
