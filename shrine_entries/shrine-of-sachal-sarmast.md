## Overview

At Daraza, near Ranipur in the Khairpur district of Sindh, lies the shrine of Sachal Sarmast (1739–1827) — “the Truthful, the Intoxicated One” — one of the boldest and most beloved of the Sufi poets of Sindh. Where Shah Abdul Latif is the serene ocean of Sindhi mysticism, Sachal is its ecstatic fire: a poet of dazzling daring who sang the unity of being in seven languages and who was called, for the reckless intensity of his verse, the _Second Mansur_ — after Mansur al-Hallaj, martyred for crying “I am the Truth.” His shrine at Daraza is a major centre of Sindhi devotion and music.

## The Life of the Saint

Sachal Sarmast was born in 1739 at Daraza into a Sayyid family of the Farooqi line; his given name was Abdul Wahab, but he became known as _Sachal_ — “the truthful” — and _Sarmast_ — “the intoxicated,” “the ecstatic.” Orphaned of his father young, he was raised and initiated by his uncle and spiritual guide, Khwaja Abdul Haq, and spent almost his entire life in his native Daraza, rarely travelling, absorbed in devotion and song. It is told that as a boy he was present when the great Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai passed through, and that the elder poet foretold his coming greatness — a meeting cherished as the passing of a torch from one master of Sindhi verse to the next. He lived to a great age and died in 1827.

## The Poetry

Sachal Sarmast poured out poetry in a remarkable range of tongues — Sindhi and Seraiki above all, but also Urdu, Persian, Punjabi, Balochi, and Arabic — earning him the title _Shair-e-Haft-Zaban_, “the poet of seven languages.” His theme, sung with unrestrained ardour, was the doctrine of _wahdat al-wujud_, the unity of all being in God, and the annihilation of the self in the divine reality; his verse breaks fearlessly through the forms of orthodoxy toward a naked declaration of union with the Beloved. It was this that won him the name of the “Second Mansur,” for like al-Hallaj he sang the identity of the lover and the Beloved without disguise. His poems are sung by the faqirs of his shrine and by folk-singers across Sindh, and their ecstatic boldness has made him a patron of all who prize love over law and truth over caution.

## The Shrine

Sachal Sarmast’s shrine at Daraza, ornamented in the glazed blue tilework of Sindh, is a busy centre of pilgrimage and, above all, of music: the singing of his poetry, often to the beat that stirs the _dhamal_, is central to its devotion. The annual urs draws great crowds from across Sindh for days of poetry, music, and celebration, in the exuberant Sindhi manner, alongside the rites of prayer and remembrance. The shrine keeps his memory as the ecstatic voice of Sindhi Sufism.

## Legacy

With Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai, Sachal Sarmast is one of the two great poet-saints of Sindh — the intoxicated counterpart to Bhittai’s sublime serenity — and among the most fearless voices of _wahdat al-wujud_ in all of South Asian Sufism. His multilingual verse, his ecstatic sincerity, and his refusal of every veil between the soul and God have kept him beloved for two centuries, and his shrine at Daraza remains a living hearth of the Sindhi Sufi song he raised to such heights.
