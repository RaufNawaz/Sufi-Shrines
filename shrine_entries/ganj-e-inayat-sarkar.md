## Overview

In the busy heart of modern Lahore, at Firdous Market in Gulberg — a quarter once known by the older name of Kabutar Pura — stands the shrine of Hazrat Allama Maulana Pir Muhammad Inayat Ahmad Naqshbandi Mujaddidi, known to his devotees as _Huzoor Ganj-e-Inayat Sarkar_, “the Treasure of Grace.” Unlike the ancient darbars of the city, this is a shrine of living memory: the saint died only in 2011, and the tomb was raised at his burial, on the very ground where he had lived, preached, and guided disciples for more than four decades. A Naqshbandi-Mujaddidi scholar-saint, orator, and author, Ganj-e-Inayat Sarkar drew thousands into his spiritual fold, and his line continues unbroken under his son and successor. His shrine shows how the deep tradition of Punjabi Sufism has carried on into the twenty-first century, at the centre of a great contemporary city.

## The Name "Ganj-e-Inayat"

The honorific by which the saint is known — _Ganj-e-Inayat Sarkar_ — carries its own meaning and sets him within an old Punjabi tradition. _Ganj_ is the Persian word for “treasure,” long bestowed upon the most beloved saints of Lahore and its region: Ali Hujwiri is _Data Ganj Bakhsh_, “the Bestower of the Treasure,” and Baba Farid of Pakpattan is _Ganj-e-Shakar_, “the Treasure of Sugar.” To call Pir Muhammad Inayat Ahmad “Ganj-e-Inayat” is thus to place him, in the affection of his devotees, in that same company — and the title turns gracefully on his own name, for _inayat_ means “grace” or “divine favour.” _Ganj-e-Inayat_ may be read as “the Treasure of Grace”: a saint understood as a store of blessing on which the needy may draw, and from whose door, it is said, no one returns empty-handed.

## The Life of the Saint

Muhammad Inayat Ahmad was born on 18 November 1937 in a village of the Kashmir valley, into a household known for both learning and piety. His father, Hazrat Mian Sohbat Ali, was himself a man of religion and a spiritual disciple in the line of Batala Sharif, and his parents — simple and devout — raised their son with care. So marked was the child from the first that, when news of his birth reached the saint Hazrat Syed Wilayat Ali Shah, he is said to have come to the house, laid his hand upon the infant in affection, and blessed him. Inayat Ahmad received his first instruction, and his knowledge of the Qur’an, at home.

In search of deeper learning he left Kashmir and travelled to Gujrat, in the Punjab, and the villages around it, studying under a number of the noted religious teachers of the age — among them Hazrat Abu al-Barakat Syed Muhammad Ahmad, Hazrat Syedna Tahir Alauddin Qadri Gilani, and the renowned orator Maulana Ghulam Ali Okarvi. It was at the seminary Ashraf-ul-Madaris in Gujrat that he found his own spiritual master: he took the oath of allegiance (bai‘at) at the hand of Hazrat Pir Syed Ismail Shah Bukhari — famous as _Karman Wali Sarkar_ — and studied and served under his mentorship, receiving from him both learning and the promise, often repeated, that one day he would become a _wali-ullah_, a friend of God. From this master he received his authorisation (khilafat) in the Naqshbandi-Mujaddidi order, becoming a _khalifa-e-mujaz_ of Karman Wali Sharif. He lived to the age of seventy-four, dying in 2011.

## His Order, Learning, and Teaching

Ganj-e-Inayat Sarkar belonged to the Naqshbandi-Mujaddidi order — the branch of the Naqshbandi way renewed by the great reformer Shaikh Ahmad Sirhindi (Mujaddid Alf-e-Sani), long noted for its strict adherence to the Sharia and its sober, God-centred discipline. This temper marked the saint through and through. His life was famously simple; he lived in a plain house and was remembered as a man of striking beauty and sincerity, such that (his devotees say) people would look on him and lose themselves in another world. He was rigorous in prayer, fasting, and the giving of _zakat_, and above all uncompromising in the doctrine of God’s oneness (_tauhid_): he counted the association of partners with God (_shirk_) as unbelief, and taught his followers to keep far from it. At the same time he held firmly to the Sufi conviction that the seeker needs a guide, and would say that until a person takes shelter with a _pir_ he cannot come onto the straight path, and that to be joined to a true spiritual lineage (_nisbat_) is a means of a person’s salvation in this world and the next. He was a teacher and an author as well as a guide: among his works is the _Kitab-us-Salat_, a book on the blessings of prayer, of the _durood_ (blessings on the Prophet), and of the Sufi path, written especially for the young — a sign of a saint who joined the pen and the pulpit to the practice of the heart.

## The Naqshbandi-Mujaddidi Way

The order to which Ganj-e-Inayat Sarkar belonged is among the most influential in the Muslim world, and its character helps explain his own. The Naqshbandiyya takes its name from Khwaja Baha-ud-Din Naqshband of Bukhara (d. 1389), and is distinguished among the Sufi paths by its preference for the silent, hidden remembrance of God (_dhikr-e-khafi_) over audible or ecstatic forms, and by its insistence that the mystical life be lived in strict conformity with the Sharia and in the midst of society rather than in withdrawal from it — expressed in the famous Naqshbandi principle of “solitude within the crowd.” The order was carried into India around 1600 by Khwaja Baqi Billah, and it was his disciple Shaikh Ahmad Sirhindi — revered as the _Mujaddid Alf-e-Sani_, the “Renewer of the Second Millennium” — who reshaped it into the Mujaddidi branch, laying a powerful stress on the purification of belief, the reaffirmation of God’s absolute oneness, and the reform of practice in accordance with the Prophet’s example. From Sirhindi’s Sirhind the Mujaddidi way spread across the subcontinent and beyond, becoming one of the great engines of Islamic renewal in South Asia.

It is this inheritance that one hears in Ganj-e-Inayat Sarkar’s own emphases: his rigor in prayer and fasting, his uncompromising insistence on _tauhid_ and his warnings against _shirk_, his union of sober scholarship with the guidance of a living master. In him the centuries-old Mujaddidi concern to wed the inward path to the outward law found a twentieth-century voice, and through his silsila — reaching back through his master Karman Wali Sarkar to the founders of the order, and forward now through his son — that chain of transmission continues in the Lahore of today.

## Lahore and the Making of the Shrine

In 1969 Ganj-e-Inayat Sarkar came to Lahore and settled at Firdous Market in Gulberg 3, in the quarter formerly called Kabutar Pura. That same year a mosque was raised at the spot that would become the heart of his darbar, and from it he taught, led prayer, and gathered a following. There he remained for some forty-two years — the whole second half of his life — preaching the faith and drawing disciples from near and far, until his death in 2011. Because he had lived and laboured on that ground for so long and had bound so many to himself as their spiritual guide, it was there that he was buried, and the shrine (darbar) was founded over his grave at the time of his burial. Beside it lies a graveyard in which, it is said, many sayyid families are laid to rest, for burial near the saint is counted a blessing.

## Miracles and Healing

Ganj-e-Inayat Sarkar was, in the eyes of his followers, a saint of evident spiritual power, and many stories of his grace are told at the shrine. It was said that one need not even speak one’s trouble to him: that on meeting him, people found their difficulties, their anxieties, and their spiritual afflictions (_bandish_) simply lifted, and that he would give to those who came before they had asked. Many recount healing (_shifa_) received at his hand or, after his death, at his shrine. One disciple related that he had suffered from the vomiting of blood, but that after coming to the saint and presenting himself in his presence he was never troubled by it again — healed, he believed, by the saint’s very gaze (_nazar_) — and that he bound himself to that door for life. It is such accounts, more than anything, that draw people to the shrine and keep them faithful to it.

## The Living Succession

What most distinguishes this shrine, like other recent darbars, is that the spiritual line it commemorates is living and directly continued. As his end drew near, Ganj-e-Inayat Sarkar is said to have transferred his spiritual succession openly and unmistakably: before a great gathering of his disciples he placed his blessed saliva (_luab_) into the mouth of his eldest son, Sahibzada Muhammad Umar Naqshbandi, and announced to the thousands present that he was leaving all that was his to his son. Sahibzada Muhammad Umar Naqshbandi is today the _sajjada-nashin_, the keeper of the shrine and heir to its spiritual authority. It is he who now takes the seat of guidance, and pilgrims come not only to honour the departed saint but to enter a living chain — to take the oath of allegiance at the hand of the successor and to receive the blessing (_faiz_) of the line.

## The Shrine and Its Devotions

The darbar stands within a small religious complex at Gulberg: the tomb, the mosque built in 1969 where the five daily prayers are offered in congregation, and the adjoining graveyard. It is not a government-administered shrine but private property, cared for by the saint’s own family and disciples. Each week, on Saturday, Sahibzada Muhammad Umar Naqshbandi holds his gathering at the shrine — presiding over assemblies of _naat_ (devotional poetry in praise of the Prophet), distributing _langar_ (free food), meeting the general public and his disciples, and admitting new followers to the oath of allegiance. The great occasion of the year is the annual urs, held on the 27th and 28th of Shaban, when the shrine takes on a heightened and vivid spiritual atmosphere: the blessed cloth (_chadar_) over the grave is ceremonially changed, the tomb is washed (_ghusl_) with rose-water and perfumes, gatherings of naat and _sama_ fill the nights, and _langar_ is distributed continuously to the crowds who come from far and wide. On an ordinary day the shrine receives some one to one-and-a-half thousand visitors, amounting to around thirty thousand in a month, while at the annual urs the number swells to fifty or sixty thousand who gather to share in the blessings.

## Legacy

The shrine of Ganj-e-Inayat Sarkar is a reminder that the making of saints and shrines is not only a matter of the distant past. Here is a holy man of the twentieth and twenty-first centuries — a Kashmir-born scholar of the Naqshbandi-Mujaddidi way, trained under the great teachers of his age, austere in doctrine yet warm in his care for the troubled — whose tomb, barely more than a decade old, has already become a fixed point of devotion in the heart of Lahore. In his simple life, his insistence on prayer and the oneness of God, his reputation for healing, and above all in the living succession he passed to his son, the darbar carries the ancient pattern of Punjabi Sufism forward into the present, gathering a new city’s hopes at a new saint’s door. In a metropolis of many millions, far from the quiet of the medieval khanqah, the darbar of Ganj-e-Inayat Sarkar stands as proof that the Punjab still makes its saints, and still knows how to gather at their thresholds.
