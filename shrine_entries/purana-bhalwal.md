## Overview

At Purana Bhalwal, the old settlement that gives its name to the modern town of Bhalwal in Sargodha District, stands the shrine of *Hazrat Shah Sulaiman Nuri*, a sixteenth-century Sufi of the *Qadiriyya* order who is remembered locally with the affectionate title *Sakhi Badshah*, "the generous king". The dargah is the spiritual heart of an otherwise unassuming corner of central Punjab, drawing devotees from the surrounding villages of the Chaj Doab, the fertile tract between the Jhelum and the Chenab.

## The Life of Shah Sulaiman Nuri

Shah Sulaiman Nuri is traditionally said to have lived a remarkably long life, from the early sixteenth century until 1604. According to the accounts preserved by his devotees, he was a scholar and ascetic trained in the *Qadiri* path, and his spiritual guide (*pir*) was Shah Maroof Khushabi of the nearby town of Khushab. It is related that his teacher, recognising his disciple's attainment, directed him to settle at Purana Bhalwal and to make it the centre of his teaching and service. There he is said to have spent the remainder of his life in devotion, instruction and the feeding of travellers and the poor — the quality of open-handed hospitality from which his epithet *Sakhi Badshah* is popularly derived.

As with many saints of this period, the details of his biography survive largely through oral tradition and later hagiography rather than contemporary record, and precise dates should be treated as traditional. He is said to have died at a great age on the night of the twenty-seventh of Ramadan, one of the most sacred nights of the Muslim calendar, a detail that has only deepened the reverence in which he is held.

## The Shrine and its Devotional Life

The shrine at Purana Bhalwal follows the familiar pattern of Punjab's smaller Sufi dargahs: a whitewashed tomb chamber sheltering the grave, enclosed within a courtyard where devotees gather to offer *Fatiha*, tie threads of intention, and distribute food. The saint's *urs* — the annual commemoration of his death anniversary, understood in Sufi thought as the soul's union with the Divine — is the principal event of the devotional year, when *qawwals* perform devotional song and langar (the free communal meal) is served to all who come.

Bhalwal itself grew as an agricultural market town under the canal colonies of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, and the shrine anchored the older village of Purana ("old") Bhalwal as the newer town expanded around the railway. For the cultivators of the district the saint remains a figure of intercession and blessing, invoked at times of illness, harvest and family need, in the manner characteristic of the living shrine culture of rural Punjab.

## Legacy

Shah Sulaiman Nuri belongs to the dense network of Qadiri and Chishti saints whose shrines dot the Chaj and Rechna Doabs, forming a sacred geography that shaped the religious life of Punjab long before modern boundaries were drawn. While he never attained the trans-regional fame of a Data Ganj Bakhsh or a Baba Farid, his dargah illustrates how Sufi devotion took root at the local level, giving small communities their own centre of blessing and continuity. The shrine remains in active use today, cared for by the descendants and devotees of the saint and, like many such sites, falling under the general oversight of the provincial Auqaf administration.

- (book's word: "پنجاب کے مالک فرنگی"; refers to: the day "جس روز راجہ رنجیت سنگھ مرا تھا", with "نو برس" more of "یہ سلطنت" — the book's "سکھوں کی سلطنت"; author's text (1875), reporting the majzub Taj Shah's saying): "سکھوں کی سلطنت کی خرابی کا حال پہلے ہی اس نے بیان کر دیا تھا ... نو برس اور یہ سلطنت رہے گی پھر پنجاب کے مالک فرنگی ہو جائیں گے". The unit here is the book's "سکھوں کی سلطنت", passing to "فرنگی" (p. 244, folio 232).
- (book's word: "پنجاب", "ملک پنجاب"; refers to: the book's own scope, written in the author's present; the closing chronograms read "۱۲۹۲ھ"; author's text (1875)): chaman 5 will hold "ان بزرگوں کا ذکر ... جو پنجاب میں عالم و فاضل صاحب نام گذرے ہیں" (p. 191, folio 179). Chaman 7 is headed "عَورات صالحات کے ذکر میں جو پنجاب میں گذر چکی ہیں" (p. 247, folio 235). A later section is headed "حالات بزرگان پنجاب جن کی وفات کا سن و تاریخ بپایہ ثبوت نہیں پہنچا" (p. 261, folio 249). In the closing, the author says his Persian *خزینۃ الاصفیا* was too large, so "شائقین ملک پنجاب کا یہ مطلب حاصل نہیں ہوتا تھا کہ کتنے بزرگ پنجاب کے ملک میں صاحب طریقت گذرے ہیں", a difficulty this short Urdu book removes (p. 280, folio 268).
- (book's word: "پنجاب"; refers to: the book's scope, chronogram "۱۲۹۲ھ"; quoted source: chronogram "از مفتی غلام صفدر صاحب لاہوری خلف مؤلف"): "جو خدا کے دوست تھے پنجاب میں   حال ان کا اس سے ہوتا ہے عیان" (p. 282, folio 270).
- (book's word: "پنجاب"; refers to: the scholars of Punjab "شیخ حامد سے لے کر حضرت شیخ بہاؤالدین زکریا ملتانی تک"; editor's footnote (modern)): Maulana Muhammad Aqil Lahori's *تحفۃ المسلمین* records Shaikh Hamid Qari's sayings and also gives "پنجاب کے اکابر علما کے حالات" across that span. The editor says its rare manuscript is in his own library (p. 278, folio 266).
