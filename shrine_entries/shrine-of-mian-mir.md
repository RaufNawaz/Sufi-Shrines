## Overview

In the Dharampura quarter of Lahore, once the garden-suburb of Baghbanpura (or Hashimpura), stands the shrine of Hazrat Mian Mir — Mir Mohammad of Sind — one of the greatest Sufi saints of the Mughal age and among the most beloved holy figures in the history of the city. A master of the Qadiri order, Mian Mir was revered in his own lifetime by beggars and emperors alike for the fierceness of his asceticism, the depth of his learning, and the breadth of a heart that welcomed Muslim, Sikh, Hindu, and Christian without distinction. For nearly four centuries his red-sandstone tomb has drawn pilgrims seeking peace, healing, and blessing, and because of his celebrated friendship with the Sikh Gurus it endures as one of Lahore’s living symbols of interfaith reverence. To understand the shrine is first to understand the life of the man buried within it, and so this account begins with the saint himself before turning to the tomb, its miracles, and the devotion that surrounds it.

## The Life of the Saint

Mian Mir was born in the sixteenth century in Sehwan (Sivastan) in Sind — the town lying, as the prince Dara Shikoh later noted, between the cities of Thatta and Bhakkar — into a devout and learned family. His father, Qazi Sain Datta, was a man of religious standing respected throughout Sind, and the family traced its lineage to the second Caliph of Islam, Umar al-Farooq, from which descent Mian Mir is often given the epithet “Farooqi.” His father died while he was still a child, and the decisive turn of his life came early: at about the age of twelve, with the consent and blessing of his widowed mother, the boy left home in search of inner enlightenment at the feet of some God-oriented soul, and for a time he wandered as a seeker. He was drawn to the inward path and was initiated into the Qadiri silsila — the order that traces its spiritual descent to Shaikh Abd al-Qadir Jilani of Baghdad — and from his shaykh he received training and, in time, the khilafat, the authority to guide others.

Around the age of twenty-five, with his teacher’s permission, Mian Mir came to Lahore and settled in the leafy neighbourhood of Baghbanpura on the edge of the city, beside the river Ravi. There he would remain for the rest of a very long life — by tradition some eighty-eight years — passing his days and nights in devotion, teaching, and the disciplined practice of the spiritual arts, until Lahore itself became inseparable from his name. He died in the city in 1635 (1045 AH). In his final illness, worn by age and by the gout that had crippled his legs, he is said to have refused treatment, explaining that he did not wish to be dulled into unconsciousness: he wished to meet death fully aware, and to feel it come. He had spent some sixty years in the same small cell, and it was there that he passed away.

## The Ascetic and His Way

Mian Mir’s contemporaries remembered him above all for the severity and the sincerity of his renunciation. In his hujra — the bare cell in which he lived — he spread nothing more than a piece of old sackcloth upon the floor, and upon that humble seat even the emperors of Hindustan did not hesitate to sit. He wore only coarse homespun khaddar, washed his own clothes with his own hands in the waters of the Ravi, and carried none of the outward marks of a Sufi master: no staff to lean upon in his age, and no patched cloak (khirqa) to advertise his station. Once, when a devotee brought him a staff and he had walked a few steps leaning upon it, he cast it away, saying, “He who has Allah for his support has no need of any artificial prop.” When the wealthy pressed gifts of money and goods upon him for his needs, he turned them away: “I stand in no need of these. I am not a beggar; I am rich, for my Provider looks after my needs — give them to the poor and the needy.” A dervish who begs from another beggar, he held, forfeits his trust in God.

He was a renowned adept of habs-e-dam, the disciplined regulation of the breath — a practice akin to the yogic pranayama — which he would carry through the night from the Isha prayer until the light of dawn, a discipline he passed on to his own successor. Though a man of deep and illumined learning who could expound the intricacies of the Qur’an and Hadith to the astonishment of the scholars of his day, he neither wrote prose nor composed verse, and showed no wish to keep letters or set down his own words; he held that when a dervish scatters his inner state upon paper the secret entrusted to him by God is unveiled to those unworthy to keep it. Ordinarily he was not eager even for visitors: he would welcome a guest, raise his hands in prayer and blessing for him, and then gently ask him to return to his own affairs.

## His Teaching

Mian Mir’s teaching, as his disciples preserved it, was as demanding as his life. He taught that a man is a composite of three things — _nafs_ (the ego), _dil_ (the heart), and _rooh_ (the soul) — and that the whole labour of the spiritual traveller is to bring these into equilibrium: to submit to the _Sharia_, to walk the path of _tariqat_ in search of _marifat_, and so to reach _haqiqat_, the Truth. He counselled his followers to fix the heart on God alone and to empty it of the love of the world; to live plainly, eating little, speaking little, and sleeping little; to meet honour and insult with the same evenness of soul; and to accept all weal and woe alike as a blessing from God. Above all he treated every human being, of whatever faith, as a creature of the one God — and he did not flatter power: he would, unmindful of royal displeasure, seek to dissuade the emperor and his sons from any act he judged unjust or unethical. In an age of magnificence, he made poverty and truthfulness his splendour.

## A Saint Who Wrote No Verse

Uniquely among the great figures of Punjabi Sufism, Mian Mir left behind no _diwan_ of poetry — a silence that was itself a kind of teaching. Yet verse gathered around him. Tradition records that when his disciples spoke of raising a fitting monument over his resting place, he forbade any dome or mausoleum, and is said to have uttered a Persian couplet on the vanity of such display:

صورتِ قبرم ز بعدِ مرگ ویرانہ خوشتر است
نیستی مانندِ من با خاک یکساں خوشتر است

[“The face of my grave, after death, is better left in ruins; for one like me, to be as nothing, level with the dust, is better.”]

His most devoted disciple at court, the Mughal prince Dara Shikoh, honoured him in Persian verse as the very pole of sainthood of the age — Mir Mohammad, he wrote, is the pathfinder for all the friends of God of this age; while the Qadiri line endures it shall lead others, so long as the universe abides. That a prince of the empire should write so of a barefoot dervish in sackcloth is itself a measure of the awe that Mian Mir commanded.

## Mian Mir and the Mughal Court

For all his withdrawal from the world, Mian Mir’s fame reached the very summit of the Mughal state. The Emperor Jahangir sought him out; Dara Shikoh records a long interview between the emperor and the saint, and relates that Mian Mir — “notwithstanding his great age and weakness” — took the trouble to come to him, and that the emperor, deeply moved, wrote to him both before and after their meeting, requesting to see him again. Such visitors the saint received seated on his sackcloth, offered a prayer for them, and then dismissed with courtesy to their own concerns.

His deepest imprint on the age, however, came through his disciples. His foremost successor was Mullah Shah Badakhshi, who carried on the master’s nightly practice of habs-e-dam and became the spiritual guide of Prince Dara Shikoh and, through him, of the emperor Shah Jahan’s brilliant daughter, the princess Jahanara. It was Dara Shikoh — the crown prince famed for seeking the common ground between Islam and the mystical traditions of India — who became Mian Mir’s most ardent admirer and who composed, in honour of the saint and his circle, the Persian hagiography _Sakīnat al-Auliyā_ (“The Tranquillity of the Saints”), today one of the principal sources for the saint’s life. The nearness of so many of the imperial family to Mian Mir’s circle carried, in the end, a tragic weight: when Dara Shikoh was defeated and put to death by his brother Aurangzeb in the war of succession, the prince and his wife Nadira Begum were laid to rest close to Mian Mir, and tradition holds that Aurangzeb drew upon stone from the complex in the raising of the great Badshahi Mosque of Lahore.

## The Mystical Court: Dara Shikoh and Jahanara

The circle that formed around Mian Mir and his successor Mullah Shah became, for one brief and luminous generation, the spiritual heart of the Mughal court. Prince Dara Shikoh, drawn through Mullah Shah into the Qadiri way, gave his life to the search for the unity underlying Islam and the mystical traditions of India. He gathered the lives and sayings of the saints in his _Safīnat al-Auliyā_ and _Sakīnat al-Auliyā_; he wrote the _Majma-ul-Bahrain_ — “The Mingling of the Two Oceans” — to show the deep harmony between Sufi and Indian metaphysics; and he caused the Upanishads to be rendered into Persian, a labour that would one day carry Indian thought to Europe. His sister, the princess Jahanara — Shah Jahan’s best-loved daughter — was herself a disciple of Mullah Shah and set down her own spiritual journey in the _Risala-i-Sahibiya_, one of the rare mystical works to come from the hand of a woman of the imperial house. Through these royal disciples the bare Qadiri cell beside the Ravi reached into the very centre of the empire.

Yet the same nearness marked the circle for tragedy. When the throne passed, in blood, to the austere and orthodox Aurangzeb rather than to his mystically minded elder brother, Dara Shikoh was condemned as a heretic and put to death in 1659. The liberal, seeking, syncretic spirit that Mian Mir and Mullah Shah had nourished was driven from the court — to survive thereafter not in palaces but at shrines such as Mian Mir’s, where it had first been kindled. That the prince and his wife were buried beside the saint they revered is a fitting close to the story: in death, the seeker of the union of faiths returned to the threshold of the master who had taught him that God’s door is open to all.

## The Golden Temple and a Friendship Across Faiths

The most famous tradition attached to Mian Mir binds his memory to the Sikh faith. He was a close friend of the fifth Sikh Guru, Arjan Dev, and of the sixth, Guru Hargobind, and by long-standing Sikh tradition it was Mian Mir whom Guru Arjan Dev invited to lay the foundation stone of the Harmandir Sahib — the Golden Temple — at Amritsar, so that the holiest shrine of the Sikhs might be founded by the hand of a Muslim saint. Later historians have weighed the episode carefully: the contemporary Persian chronicles do not mention it, and scholars such as Madanjit Kaur, in _The Golden Temple: Past and Present_, note the absence of early documentary proof, while others have argued that the political tensions of the age — the involvement of the Sirhindi court faction in the death of Guru Arjan Dev — would have made such a gesture difficult. Yet the objection does not really hold, for Mian Mir’s cordial and fraternal relations with the fifth Guru are well attested, and he belonged not to the Sirhindi (Naqshbandi) circle but to the Qadiri order. Whatever the historian concludes about the foundation stone itself, the tradition is cherished across communities as a luminous emblem of the friendship between Muslims and Sikhs in Punjab, and it continues to draw Sikh pilgrims to the saint’s memory. What all the sources agree upon is the temper of the man: Mian Mir received Sikhs, Hindus, and Christians with the same courtesy and reverence he showed his fellow Muslims, and treated them all as children of the one God.

## A Thread in Lahore’s Sufi Heritage

Mian Mir’s life also wove him into the deeper Sufi history of Lahore. He is said to have kept a forty-day retreat (chilla) at the khanqah of Data Ganj Bakhsh, Ali Hujwiri — the eleventh-century saint whose _Kashf al-Mahjub_ is among the earliest Persian treatises on Sufism, and whose shrine, Data Darbar, is the spiritual heart of the city. In going to sit in devotion at Data Sahib’s threshold, Mian Mir joined his own sanctity to that of Lahore’s patron saint, and later devotion has treasured the link as binding together the two greatest Sufi presences of the city across the span of five centuries.

## Miracles and Folklore

As with every great saint, a rich body of miracle-tales (karāmāt) gathered around Mian Mir, and they were recorded and retold well into the colonial era, when British collectors of Punjabi folklore took them down. Best known is the story of the dove: a hunter’s stone struck down a bird that had been sweetly praising God, and at Mian Mir’s touch it revived and flew away unharmed; when the hunter aimed once more, the stone turned back and wounded his own hand, until, repenting and vowing never again to harm a winged creature, he was healed. Devotees tell, too, of a black snake that came to the saint as he sat by the Ravi, circled him three times, and slipped quietly away; of a mute boy whom he taught to utter “Bismillah,” after which the child could speak; of a handkerchief he gave that healed the sick who used it in faith; and of food that appeared, in answer to his prayer, when there was nothing in the house to set before a guest.

Many such tales turn on his knowledge of hidden things and his power over sickness and death. A man came seeking his blessing for a journey to Khorasan; Mian Mir advised him not to go, for the way, he said, was closed — and so, unseen, it proved. Another came in anguish over a son choking and suffocating; the saint fell into a trance, called for a cup of water, breathed his recitation over it, and by it the child was relieved. To a certain visitor he said simply, “you may depart” — and within a few days the man breathed his last, the words proving true. His disciples cherished their own intimate wonders: Meerak Mohammad Lahori relates how, having taken up and chewed a discarded betel-leaf from the saint’s own mouth, he was carried away into so deep an ecstasy that his breast seemed near to bursting with felicity. Stories such as these, more than any doctrine, are what bound ordinary people to the saint, and they still draw the troubled and the hopeful to his shrine.

## Death and the Building of the Shrine

Mian Mir died in 1635, and though he had expressly forbidden any tomb over his grave, a mausoleum of red sandstone was raised at his resting place in Baghbanpura in the years that followed, during the lifetime of Dara Shikoh, who could not bear that his master should lie unmarked. It grew into a dignified monument in the Mughal manner, its chambers finished in fine stone, and around it, in time, were gathered the graves of others of the imperial circle who had loved him — Dara Shikoh himself, executed in 1659, and his wife Nadira Begum among them. Thus the barefoot dervish who owned nothing and had asked for nothing came to rest at the centre of a small city of tombs, his wish overruled by the devotion of a prince.

## The Shrine Through the Centuries

The tomb passed through the turbulence of later ages. In the Sikh period much of its valuable stone was carried off under the builders of Maharaja Ranjit Singh, who prized the material, and — by tradition — stone from the wider complex had earlier been drawn upon for imperial building under Aurangzeb; yet through every change of rule the shrine endured as a place of devotion. Under British rule it came under the care of the department of ancient monuments, and in 1985 it was formally declared a protected site by the Punjab Auqaf Department, which administers it to this day together with the shrine’s traditional sajjada-nashins, the hereditary custodians. The mausoleum stands in the Dharampura (Hashimpura) area of Lahore, its dome and gateway a familiar landmark, with an attached mosque and open courtyards to receive the crowds who gather there. Successive generations have repaired and adorned it, so that the austere saint who wished only for a ruined and level grave is honoured, instead, by one of the enduring monuments of the city.

## Devotion Today

The shrine of Mian Mir remains a living centre of popular piety. On ordinary days it draws on the order of one to three thousand visitors, who come to recite the Fatiha, tie vows, and seek the saint’s intercession for healing, for children, and for relief from hardship; many speak of the peace they feel within its walls and of prayers answered there. Qawwali is sung on Thursday evenings, the five daily prayers are held in the shrine mosque, and langar — free food — is distributed to visitors, sometimes twice a day. The shrine’s great annual occasion is the urs, held on 7 February, which marks the saint’s union with the Divine; the courtyards then fill with hundreds of thousands of devotees for days of qawwali, prayer, and communal feasting. Across the centuries — under Mughal, Sikh, and British rule, and in the Pakistan of today — the shrine has served as a shared space of devotion, remembered by writers from Dara Shikoh to Khushwant Singh as a place where the boundaries of community grew soft.

## Legacy

Mian Mir stands in memory as the very type of the Sufi ideal: a man who owned nothing and was sought by emperors; who wrote not a single verse and yet was praised in verse by a prince; who, rooted absolutely in his own faith, made his threshold a meeting-place for people of every religion. His disciples shaped the spiritual life of the Mughal court; his friendship with the Sikh Gurus became a legend of tolerance that Punjab still cherishes; his forty-day retreat at Data Darbar bound him to the patron saint of Lahore; and his tomb remains one of the anchors of the city’s sacred landscape. In honouring Mian Mir, Lahore remembers not only a saint of extraordinary austerity and learning, but a vision of holiness generous enough to hold an entire, plural world.

**[illegible]** خانقاہ کے اندر" and dated 1000 AH, while the editor quotes Dara Shikoh for 27 Rabi

اند**" — she never left Sehwan. **If the editor's quotation is right the Lahore burial cannot stand,

**The book's scope and the survey of Lahore**
- The commissioner "مسٹر ولیم کولڈسٹریم [OCR?]" ordered the author "کہ حالاتِ عمارات و مزارات و مقابر و مساجد نواحِ لاہور مفصل تحریر کروں" — to write a detailed account of the buildings, mazars, tombs and mosques of Lahore's environs. The author calls the task very hard "کیونکہ صدہا سال کے حالات بہم پہونچانے [OCR?] [illegible] مشکل", because the history of hundreds of years had to be gathered (p. 28). Elsewhere he says Lala Amin Chand's work on Lahore's buildings and people was cut short when "مسٹر[OCR?] ولیم[OCR?] گولڈ اسٹریم[OCR?]" left for Shahjahanabad (p. 734).
- The book "مشعرِ احوالِ مقابرِ بزرگانِ اہلِ اسلام وغیرہ عمارات و [illegible] و مراسمِ قدیمہ [illegible] لاہور" (reports on the tombs of Muslim elders, other buildings and the old customs of Lahore), "لیکن فی الاصل تواریخِ [OCR?] اولیا [illegible] ہے", but at root it is a history of saints (p. 29).
- Mufti Ghulam Sarwar had long sought "حالاتِ حضرات نواحیِ لاہور" and could not get them, "کیونکہ حضرات سجادہ نشیناں [illegible] اطلاعِ حال اپنا [illegible]" (p. 31).
- The survey proper opens under the heading "آغاز احوال قبور و مزارات و مقبرہ ہائے بزرگان لاہور اول[OCR?]" (p. 45).
- A forward reference: "مکانات اہل[OCR?] دہلی[OCR?] شہر لاہور میں تحریر یہ ہوگا انشاء اللہ تعالی" (the houses of the people[OCR?] of Delhi[OCR?] in the city of Lahore will be written up, God willing) (p. 292).
- The author cites Lala Amin Chand's *Safarnama* ("مطبوعہ کوہ نور"), its account of the city ("حال شہر لاہور کے بیان میں بہ صفحہ با دن[OCR?]"), and disputes it (p. 733).
- The afterword gives the book's scope as "چارون طرف لاہور کے جسقدر مزار ہین بزرگان سلف کی یادگار ہین اون سب کی حالات"; claims no book had yet been written or printed that covered "آثار [OCR?] قدیمہ لاہور" in full; and says it covers "بزرگان ہنود و اہل اسلام" and that its "صلح کلی" respects every religion (p. 872).

**Foundation legends** (two versions, kept side by side)
- In the Shah Kamal section, introduced "کہتے[OCR?] ہیں": Raja Ram Chand had three sons; "ایک لو[OCR?] نے [illegible] لاہور بنایا" (one, Lo[OCR?], built Lahore); a second, name lost in "[illegible: 4 words]", "قصور[OCR?] آباد کیا"; the third, "مسمی راو[OCR?]", founded Mauza Rawan. **Pre-Mughal rulers and invasions**
- When Sabuktigin first invaded Hind, in "سن [illegible] سو ستہتر [OCR?] ہجری", "لاہور مین راجہ جی پال حاکم تہا" (p. 804). Sabuktigin, having won the Afghans over, "لاہور و ملتان کو اپنے قبضہ مین کر لیا" (p. 804).
- Jaipal, defeated, asked that a trusted man of Sabuktigin's come with him to Lahore to collect the tribute; he came with "نواب خیر اللہ خان [OCR?]". At his court "بطرف چپ چہتری اور بطرف راست برہمن کہتری"; the Chhatris urged him to keep his word, the Brahmins not to pay; Jaipal broke the agreement and imprisoned the envoy. Sabuktigin "لاہور پر چڑہ آیا"; the rajas of Delhi, Ajmer and Kalinjar[OCR?] helped Jaipal, but the Hindus were defeated (p. 804).
- In Sultan Mahmud's time "خاندان توار [OCR?] سی ایک راجہ اوسا [OCR?] لاہور و قنوج کا حاکم تہا" (p. 804).
- Mas'ud, facing the Turkmans (Tughril Beg), was "لاچار لاہور کے طرف روانہ ہوا"; his army looted the treasury at the Indus ("دریا سندہ") and put the blinded Muhammad back on the throne (p. 807).
- In Maudud's time the Hindu rajas besieged Lahore, "جو دار الخلافت اہل اسلام کا تہا", for "سات [OCR?] مہینہ" until the besieged drove them off (p. 807).
- "خسرو شاہ ولد بہرام [illegible] لاہور مین آکر بعالم مجبوری دار السلطنت بنایا"; he ruled "سات [OCR?] برس تک لاہور مین"; his son Khusrau Malik succeeded. The Ghurids took "یہہ ملک" from Khusrau "بہزار مکر و فریب", and "پہر یہان بہی سلطنت غوریان کی ہوئی" (pp. 808–809).
- From "کتاب خلاصۃ التواریخ": after "جہاں [illegible]", Lahore lay deserted ("ویران پڑا رہا") for a long time, until an amir of "سلطان سلوک[OCR?]" settled it again; "کامراں" is named in the damaged continuation (p. 430). Settlement then grew (p. 431).

**Mughal Lahore**
- When Humayun returns to Hindustan, "حاکم لاہور تاتار خان" comes out against him; Bairam Khan overcomes him off guard (p. 823). After Hemu takes Delhi, "اکبر نے لاہور مین کچہہ جمعیت بہم پہونچا کی دہلی پر یورش کی" (p. 824).
- Akbar's second enthronement after Humayun's death: "دوبارہ شہنشاہ حسن تخت نشینی[OCR?] کلاہور میں مسعقد ہوا" (garbled) (p. 648).
- "اکبر بادشاہ[OCR?]" turned to Lahore's settlement from the start of his reign, built a city and a "دولت خانہ", raised large buildings and stayed for a time (p. 431). Lahore is called the capital in Akbar's time: Malik Ali was kotwal in "عہد اکبر بادشاہ" and held "تمام کوتوالی ... دارالسلطنتہ لاہور" (p. 194; surrounding words heavily flagged: "تمر[OCR?] قرویہ[OCR?] حتماً[OCR?] طلوکہ[OCR?] ... مقلم[OCR?]"); also "ملک علی کوتوال لاہور[OCR?]" (p. 193). Akbar is said to have been in Lahore when the Gilani ancestor came: "ان ایام میں اکبر بادشاہ … لاہو یہ رہا[OCR?]" (heavily damaged) (p. 525).
- Khwaja Ayaz (Nur Jahan's father), his wife and the infant travelled with a caravan "اونکی ساتہہ وہ لاہور تک پہنچی"; there he took service and rose to "میر منشی" under Akbar (p. 827).
- Coin legends of "نور الدین جہانگیر ابن اکبر بادشاہ": the transcriber's summary of four illegible lines names mints including "لاہور [OCR?]" and "احمد آباد [OCR?]" (p. 828).
- After Mahabat Khan's victory, "نورجہان لاہور کیطرف بہاگ گئی" (p. 829). Jahangir, ill on his way back from Kashmir, came to Lahore and died there ("جب وہان سے واپس ہوکر لاہور مین آئے تو ... خیمہ زن ملک عدم ہوگئے"); date as printed "تاریخ [illegible] ماہ نومبر سنہ ۱۶۲[illegible]ء [OCR?] مطابق سنہ [illegible]۳۶ [OCR?] ہجری" (p. 830). He was buried "شہر [OCR?] لاہور کے شمال رویہ لب دریای راوی موضع شاہدرہ مین" (p. 830); the tomb is "دریای راوی کی کنارے پر شہر لاہور سے بطرف شمال بفاصلہ چار میل" (p. 855), and from its minarets "تمام شہر لاہور معہ مسجد بادشاہی و وزیر خان وغیرہ عمارات نظر آتی ہین" (p. 860).
- "شاہجہاں بادشاہ لاہور میں ٹھہرے[OCR?]", in the anecdote of Haji Nur (p. 200). Shah Jahan is said to have had "بہت سی عمارات واقع لاہور" built, and Nawab Wazir Khan was appointed "ماظم[OCR?] لاہور" (p. 738); Wazir Khan is "ناظم لاہور" again in "جب نواب صاحب ناظم لاہور رستے[OCR?]" (p. 749). "لاہور مین شالامار باغ" is Shah Jahan's memorial: "چشم روزگار نے کوئی ایسا باغ کم دیکھا ہوگا" (p. 833).
- After Aurangzeb's accession, "دارا شکوہ نے لاہور کو مراجعت کی", raised a larger army there, then crossed the "دریای سندہ" (p. 836). Dara's betrayer, after handing him over in Delhi, "لاہور کیطرف چلا گیا" and was killed on the road (p. 838).
- At the start of Alamgir's reign Nawab Khalil Allah Khan held "دارہ[OCR?] لاہور" and, with a mansab of "شش ہزاری", was appointed "صوبہ دار لاہور" (p. 497).
- The great buildings stood until "عہد عالمگیر"; great amirs built "بڑی بڑی عمارات سنگین"; Abu al-Hasan son of Asaf Khan's buildings were among Lahore's chief ornaments (p. 431).
- In the succession war after Aurangzeb, Muhammad Mu'izz al-Din, governor of Multan, came with a large army and stores "بمقام لاہور" to his father Sultan Mu'azzam Bahadur Shah, and both went on to Akbarabad (p. 845). "شاہ عالم شہر لاہور مین سنہ ۱۷۱۲ء [OCR?] مین فوت ہوا" (p. 846).

**Later Mughal governors (nazims and subadars)**
- Abdus Samad Khan was given the title "سیف الدولہ دلیر جنگ بہادر" and appointed to Lahore by "شاہ عالم" (heavily flagged); he arrived when his son Zakariya Khan was "تیرہ سال کا" (p. 714). Zakariya Khan was appointed "ناظم لاہور" through the Sayyid brothers' support, Abdus Samad Khan moving to "ناظم ملتان"; later he continues as "ناظم لاہور" under Muhammad Shah, "شوق عمارت انکو معہ[OCR?] کمال تہا" (p. 716).
- In the reign of "محمد شاہ بادشاہ" a "حاکم[OCR?] لاہور" is named in connection with "مال گزاری[OCR?]"; the rest is damaged (p. 372).
- Mir Momin Khan became "ناظم[OCR?]" of Lahore in the time of "محمد شاہ[OCR?]" (p. 172); administration of the subjects was good, "رعایا ہی لاہور اوست بند ولسیت[OCR?] بہت عن[OCR?]"; he was not autonomous ("اگرچہ یہ نخود مختار نہ سے تھے"), "نبج[OCR?] باں[OCR?] خان" being "اصلی ناظم لاہور"; most buildings between Mochi Gate and Kashmiri Bazaar are his memorials, "دروازہ موچی و جنوب باروبہ[OCR?] بازار کشمیری اکثر عمارات … یادگار انکی"; and "یہ آخری ناظم لاہور ہوے" — after him no nazim was appointed or came (p. 173). Set beside this, without reconciliation: Nawab Abdullah Khan appointed "ناظم لاہور" in "سنہ ۱۱۶۱[OCR?]" (p. 717); the "حاکم لاہور" of p. 579; "خواجہ عابد صوبہ دار لاہور" (p. 578).
- Mir Mannu "بعہد محمد شاہ یہ نواب لاہور میں حاکم تھا"; the Sikhs had grown in number, and there was a general order to seize and kill them (p. 121). A Panjabi saying about him is illegible: "میر منو ساڈی[OCR?] [illegible] اسی[OCR?]" (p. 121).
- "بعدہ سنہ ۱۱۵۵[OCR?] میں صوبہ ملتان لاہور سکو شامل ہوا" (pp. 717–718).
- Under Nawab Abdullah Khan, "ناظم لاہور" from "سنہ ۱۱۶۱[OCR?]": "لاہور میں قحط پڑا", and he squeezed the people; proverb: "حکومت نواب عبداللہ[OCR?] نہ چکی[OCR?] رہی نہ چلہا[OCR?]" (p. 717).
- "خواجہ عابد صوبہ دار لاہور" attacked Charat Singh's garhi in "سن سترہ سو بیو کے ستہ[OCR?]"; "سکھوں کو فتح نصیب ہوئی" (p. 578).
- A Nawab ("نواب عارض[OCR?]", name damaged) came "کابل سے لاہور میں" (p. 722).
- The "حاکم اعلیٰ لاہور" is named as an office in Rajab Ali Khan's career (p. 141).

**Nadir Shah and Ahmad Shah Abdali**

## From Kanhaiya Lal, Tarikh-e Lahore

*Integrated 10 October 2026 from `entries/book_takeaways/tareekh_lahore.md` (reviewed: no). Every line below the heading is copied verbatim from that file; nothing was added or reworded. Page references are as the takeaways give them.*

*Attached as this book's claim, not as an identification (Rauf, 10 October 2026). The takeaways disagree with themselves about whether this material belongs to this row; the doubts below are shown as they stand.*

- **Saint.** Named among the great buildings of the city, "لاہور کی مشہور و معروف عالیشان عمارات میں سے" (p. 242, folio 245). Murid and khalifa of **Shaikh Khizr Sistani** in the **Qadiriyya**: "سلسلہ عالیہ قادریہ میں مریدو خلیفہ شیخ خضر سیستانی", "اور اُسی سے اس نے تکمیل پائی", and he received completion from him; he then "ہندوستان میں آکر لاہور میں سکونت پذیر ہوا", came to Hindustan and settled in Lahore (p. 242, folio 245). The book calls him **Lahori** and gives no birthplace and no other nisba (not stated). Character: "نہایت درجہ کا عابد و زاہد و متقی و خدا پرست تارک الدنیا" (p. 242, folio 245). Death "سنہ ۱۰۴۵[OCR?] ہجری عہد شاہجہان بادشاہ میں" (p. 242, folio 245); the chronogram over the tomb door carries the marginal figure "۱۰۴۵[OCR?]" and the text says the year of his death is obtained from it (p. 244, folio 247). No birth year, no family, no death date within the year beyond the month (not stated). The earlier range, by contrast, gives him **no death year and no date of any kind** (not stated) (p. 139, folio 142).
- **Saint — his relation to Dara Shikoh, on which the two ranges disagree.** One range names him as Dara Shikoh's "دادا مرشد", his **murshid's murshid**, with **Mulla Shah Qadri named as Dara Shikoh's own murshid in the same sentence** — do not flatten the two (p. 139, folio 142). The other prints Dara Shikoh as "پوتا خلیہ[OCR?] میان میر کا", Mian Mir's **grand-khalifa[OCR?]** — "یعنی ارادت اُسکی شیخ محمد المشہور ملا شاہ کے ہاتہہ پر تہی", his own *iradat* being at the hand of **Shaikh Muhammad famous as Mulla Shah** (p. 242, folio 245).
- **Saint — a Mughal prince wrote his life.** "سکینتہ الاولیا نام ایک کتاب بزبان فارسی دارا شکوہ شاہجہان بادشاہ کے بیٹے نے اسکے حالات میں لکہی ہے جسمیں ہزارون کرامتین اس بزرگ کی درج ہیں" — *Sakinat-ul-Auliya* by **Dara Shikoh**, recording thousands of his karamat (p. 242, folio 245); none is retold. A khadim of his, **Mir Inayatullah**, is the occupant of a neighbouring tomb: "صاحب مقبرہ کا نام میر عنایت اللہ تہا جو میان میر کا خادم تہا" (p. 264, folio 267).
- **Shrine / Darbar — patronage, and a contradiction about whose enclosure the village stands in.** In one range: when the governorship of Punjab was entrusted to Dara Shikoh in Shah Jahan's reign, "اُس نے عمارتین بہت کین پہلے چوک بنوایا پہر عالیشان عمارت اپنے دادا مرشد میان میر بالاپیر کے روضہ کی تعمیر کی" — first he had a chowk built, then the lofty building of the rauza of his *dada murshid* Mian Mir Bala Pir; then "پہر ملا شاہ قادری اپنے مرشد کا روضہ بنوایا جسکے حاطہ مین اب موضع میانمیر ایک گاؤن آبا دہی" — then the rauza of Mulla Shah Qadri, his own murshid, **in whose enclosure there is now the village Mauza Mian Mir**, a settlement; **grammatically the village lies in Mulla Shah's enclosure though it bears Mian Mir's name** (p. 139, folio 142). In the other range, Dara Shikoh gathered much stone and building material and **first built his own pir's rauza**, "جسکی چار دیواری میں اب موضع میان میر آباد ہے" — **in whose enclosure the village of Mian Mir is now settled** (p. 242, folio 245). **The two readings place the village in two different enclosures and are kept side by side; neither is chosen. No date for the rauza in either** (not stated) (p. 139, folio 142).
- **Shrine / Darbar — the fabric, as the author measured it** (pp. 243–245, folios 246–248): enclosure *pukhta chuna-gach* with qalbuti arches in all four walls, its height[OCR?] "پانچ گز کم[OCR?] نہیں"; door on the **south**; rauza on a grand chabutra "مربع ۱۹ ۔ ۱۹ ذرعہ طول و عرض کا", 19 by 19 *zar'a*; marble stair on the south; marble slabs[OCR?] set into the four faces; stone paving on top; the tomb itself "قدآدم بلند سنگ مرمر کی"; door panel marble, frame **red stone**; inside a marble floor inlaid with black "سنگ موسی[OCR?]"; three qalbuti arches with stone frames[OCR?] and the door in the fourth, southern one; the interior marble work "عمارت دارا شکوہ کے عہد میں بنائی گئی تہی"; glass-work with vine pattern and a large mirror on the flanks of the northern arch and in the dome ceiling; the mazar on a marble chabutra one foot high, its ta'wiz marble, "جسپر ہمیشہ غلاف قیمتی کپڑون کا پڑا رہتا ہے", on which a cover of costly cloths always lies; dome finial (کلس) also marble.
- **Shrine / Darbar — two seats of office built into the fabric.** On the west of the enclosure "ایک عالیشان دالان … مجاورین کی نشستگاہ بنا ہے", a grand dalan built as the **seat of the mujawirs**, and beside it a further building with a bukharcha[OCR?] in which "نشست خاص سجادہ نشین کی ہوتی ہے", the **special seat of the sajjada nashin** (p. 243, folio 246). A well fifty years in the making: "چونکہ اس چار دیواری کے اندر کوئی چاہ نہ تہا نور محمد ایاں والہ[OCR?] نے بمرور عرصہ پچاس برس کے یہ چاہ تیار کرایا تہا", with a bathhouse attached (p. 243, folio 246).

## From Zahur ul-Hasan Sharib, Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind

*Integrated 10 October 2026 from `entries/book_takeaways/tazkirah_awliya_pak_o_hind.md` (reviewed: no). Every line below the heading is copied verbatim from that file; nothing was added or reworded. Page references are as the takeaways give them.*

*Attached as this book's claim, not as an identification (Rauf, 10 October 2026). The takeaways disagree with themselves about whether this material belongs to this row; the doubts below are shown as they stand.*

**Three separate bodies of material in this range bear on this row, and the chunk workers placed them
differently. All three are recorded; the placement is unreconciled.**

1. **Chunk 015 maps the row** on the strength of باب 56, where Mian Mir appears as the Lahore murshid of
   Mulla Shah (pp. 292–294, folios 288–290). That material is set out below.
2. **Chunk 013 does not map باب 53, the book's own entry for حضرت میاں میر** (p. 269, folio 265), because
   the entry breaks off after the القاب heading and gives no shrine town, no death year, no silsila and
   no pir. Its material is recorded under "Figures and sites not in the archive".
3. **Chunk 014 does not map the unnamed Lahore saint of pp. 270–277** (folios 266–273), who resembles the
   row on three of the book's own statements but is **never named in those pages**. His material is
   likewise recorded under "Figures and sites not in the archive".

**Saint (from باب 56, chunk 015's mapping)**

- **He appears only as a murshid, and he is stationed only ever at Lahore.** Mulla Shah, moving from
  Kashmir, "کشمیر سے لاہور تشریف لائے۔ لاہور میں اپنی جستجو میں کامیاب نہ ہو سکے" — came from Kashmir
  to **لاہور** and could not succeed in his search there (p. 292, folio 288); on the road to Agra he
  learned Mian Mir's name from a stranger and "آپ کو افسوس ہوا کہ لاہور میں حضرت میاں میر سے کیوں نہ
  ملے" — regretted not having met him **in Lahore** (p. 292, folio 288); he returned, "آگرہ سے لاہور
  تشریف لائے… لاہور پہنچ کر حضرت میاں میر کی خدمت بابرکت میں حاضر ہوئے" (pp. 292–293, folios 288–289).
- **He withheld attention for three years, then set two conditions.** "حضرت میاں میر نے تین سال تک آپ کی
  طرف کوئی التفات نہیں برتا" — for **three years** he showed him no regard; then asked "کہاں رہتے ہو؟"
  and on "مسجد میں رہتا ہوں" told him "مسجد میں رہنا مناسب نہیں"; then asked "کیا کھاتے ہو؟" and on
  "بازار کی روٹی کھاتا ہوں" told him "بازار کی روٹی نہ کھانا چاہیے" (p. 293, folio 289, حاشیہ 2 = سکینۃ
  الاولیاء اردو ترجمہ ص 125). Only after both renunciations: "آخر کار حضرت میاں میر نے آپ کو بیعت سے
  مشرف فرمایا اور خرقہ خلافت عطا کیا" (p. 293, folio 289).
- **He named his disciple and predicted his own line through him.** "آپ کے پیرو مرشد حضرت میاں میر آپ کو
  'محمد شاہ' کے لقب سے پکارا کرتے تھے" (p. 292, folio 288); asked for whom he had prayed, he answered
  "ملا شاہ کے بارے میں' جس سے میرا طریقہ روشن ہو گا" (p. 293, folio 289).
- **His judgement on his disciple's austerity, quoted**: "ملا شاہ! جو ریاضت کرنے کی ہے' وہ مشائخ سابق
  میں سے کسی نے بھی نہیں کی" (p. 294, folio 290, حاشیہ 3 = سکینۃ الاولیاء اردو ترجمہ ص 119).
- **Death year: (not stated). Mazar: (not stated). Silsila: not stated of him directly**; the chunk
  places his khalifa in the Qadiri line — "قادری سلسلے کے بزرگوں میں آپ کو نمایاں درجہ حاصل ہے" (p. 294,
  folio 290) — which is the only order-name attached to this lineage in the range. The row's "1635" and
  "Alam Ganj (Dharampura)" are neither confirmed nor contradicted; its "Qadiri" is corroborated only at
  one remove.

**Shrine / Darbar**

- **Nothing.** No mazar, no burial locality, no urs, no custodianship, no endowment, no buildings
  (pp. 292–294, folios 288–290).
- The one institutional fact attaching to his seat is **a seasonal calendar**: with his permission Mulla
  Shah "گرمی کے موسم میں کشمیر چلے جاتے تھے اور جاڑے کے موسم میں لاہور آ کر اپنے پیرو مرشد کی خدمت میں
  رہتے" — summers in **کشمیر**, winters at **لاہور** in his pir's service (p. 294, folio 290).

**Practices & Events**

- **A pir's ban on residing in a mosque and on eating market bread**, imposed as the condition on which
  bai'at was finally granted; on the second the disciple "بازار کی روٹی کھانا چھوڑ دی' فاقہ کرنا شروع
  کیا" — gave up bazaar bread and **began to starve** (p. 293, folio 289, حاشیہ 2).
- **A term of service quantified**: "آپ اپنے پیرو مرشد کی خدمت میں تیس [OCR?] سال رہے" — **thirty
  [OCR?] years** in his pir's service (p. 293, folio 289), and three years of being ignored before the
  first question (p. 293, folio 289).

### Province-level material: punjab

*The book's `punjab` bullets, copied into every existing entry in the province (Rauf, 8 and 10 October 2026). They speak of the province, not of this shrine.*

- (book's word: "Punjab"; refers to: period not stated — "the early Muslim saints in general, and the Chishti masha'ikh in particular"; author's argument): "Not only for Gujarat, Bengal, but also for Uttar Pradesh, Punjab, and the Deccan does the significance of the early Muslim saints in general, and the Chishti masha'ikh in particular, mark a particular kind of South Asian spiritual authority that is not only political and religious but also cultural"; the authors describe "a spectrum of spiritually charged spaces or sites" that are "devotional loci for Indian Muslims from several classes, and also for Hindus" (p. 110, folio 97). Punjab is one of five regions named together; nothing specific to Punjab is said.
- (book's word: "the Punjab", which the book calls one of "two provinces"; refers to: Akbar's reign, the Fatehpur Sikri phase, which he "abandoned ... by 1585" — as the book states; author's argument): through Shaykh Salim and "Shaykh Farid ad-Din Ganj-i Shakar in the Punjab", "Through a twofold, redoubled Chishti loyalty, Akbar could anchor his imperial legitimacy in two provinces adjacent to Uttar Pradesh, the Punjab and Rajasthan, both of which also happened to be crucial to the political-military ambitions of his reign" (pp. 112–113, folios 99–100). The book's own unit is a Mughal-period "province"; its extent is not stated.
- (book's word: "Punjab"; refers to: "the eighteenth century"; book's historical prose): "The Nizami Chishtis came to prominence in the eighteenth century, principally in Delhi, the Deccan, and Punjab, through masters who were related to but in tension with local political leaders" (p. 119, folio 106).
- (book's word: "the Punjab", "Punjabi Hindus"; refers to: Shaykh Sulayman Taunsawi's time, "as British colonialism began to take hold of India" — Taunsawi is "(d. 1850)" on the same page; book's historical prose): Taunsawi "urged his followers to participate in resistance against Sikh aggression in the Punjab, and so, apparently for the first time in the annals of Chishti history, we have spiritual biographies drawn into partisanship on behalf of one political group (Muslim) over against another (non-Muslim)"; "Notable, however, is the effort of Shah Sulayman to maintain cordial relations with Punjabi Hindus even while he opposed their Sikh neighbors" (p. 121, folio 108). The supporting endnote (n. 10) cites M. Zameeruddin Siddiqi, "The Resurgence of the Chishti Silsilah in the Punjab during the Eighteenth Century" (p. 214, folio 203).
- (book's word: "the Punjab", "the southern Punjab"; refers to: Taunsawi "(d. 1850)", his successor Muhammad 'Aqil "(d. 1814)" and the latter's disciple Gul Muhammad Ahmadpuri "(d. 1827)" — as printed; book's historical prose): Taunsawi "consolidated the order by stressing learning through establishment of institutions: not only the academy called Dar al- 'Ulum at Taunsa on the upper Indus, but also other religious schools throughout the Punjab"; 'Aqil and Ahmadpuri "both of whom founded religious schools in the southern Punjab that provided free instruction and lodging to hundreds of students" (pp. 121–122, folios 108–109).
- (book's word: "the upper Punjab"; refers to: "the nineteenth century"; book's historical prose): the line from Shah Mirra "resurfaces in the nineteenth century in the figure of Hafiz Sayyid Muhammad 'Ali Khayrabadi, marking the expansion of the Nizami branch of Chishti Sufism through disciples of Shaykh Sulayman Taunsawi in the upper Punjab" (p. 71, folio 57).
- (book's word: "the Punjab"; refers to: "the colonial period"; author's argument): "If we were to follow only the dictates of political history and the criteria of social science, the test case for the Chishti Nizami Sulaymaniyya lineage in the colonial period might seem to be the battles over shrines in the Punjab"; the example given is Taunsa, and "Political power struggles internal to the Sulaymaniyya can be traced to British manipulation and also the rivalry of local Sikhs, Hindus, and Muslim reformers" (p. 124, folio printed "Ill" [text-layer?] [111]). The authors then set this political reading aside: "political contestation is only part of the narrative".
- (book's word: "the Punjab"; refers to: present day ("continues to be intoned"); ethnographic present): "The name Qalandar continues to be intoned in popular qawwali lyrics of the Punjab, and it has been appropriated by a mainstream Sufi group, the Indian Qalandari order centered at Kakori near Lucknow" (p. 36, folio 22). Which side of the border is not stated.
