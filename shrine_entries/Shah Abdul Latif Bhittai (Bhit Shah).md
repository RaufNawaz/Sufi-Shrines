## Overview

At Bhit Shah, in the Matiari region of Sindh, stands the shrine of Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai (1689–1752), the greatest poet of the Sindhi language and one of the supreme Sufi voices of the subcontinent. His life’s work, the _Shah Jo Risalo_ — a vast compendium of mystical poetry set to music — is to Sindh what the _Divan_ of Hafiz is to Persia: scripture, songbook, and national treasure at once. His tomb, built on the sandhill (_bhit_) from which he takes his name, is the cultural and spiritual heart of Sindh, and the nightly singing of his verse there, in the tradition of the _Shah jo Raag_, is among the most moving sounds in all of Sufi devotion.

## The Life of the Poet-Saint

Abdul Latif was born in 1689 into a Sayyid family of Sindh, descendants of a line of saints; his ancestor Shah Abdul Karim of Bulri was himself a noted mystic poet, and the boy grew up steeped in devotion and verse. As a young man he wandered for years with bands of yogis and ascetics through the deserts and mountains of Sindh and beyond, sharing the life of Hindu and Muslim mendicants alike — journeys that filled his poetry with the landscapes of Sindh and a profound sympathy across the boundaries of religion. In his later years he settled on a barren sandhill near the town of Hala, which grew into the settlement of Bhit Shah, and there he gathered disciples and musicians about him, composed and sang, and died in 1752.

## The Shah Jo Risalo

Bhittai’s poetry, gathered after his death as the _Shah Jo Risalo_, is organised into _surs_ — chapters named for the musical modes (_raags_) to which they are sung. Drawing on the folk-romances of Sindh — the tragic heroines Sassui, Marui, Sohni, Momal, and others — he made of their longing and suffering an allegory of the soul’s yearning for God, so that the desert wanderings of a lovelorn girl become the seeker’s journey to the Divine. His verse fuses the deepest mysticism of _wahdat al-wujud_ (the unity of being) with the very soil, speech, and song of Sindh, and it carries a fierce compassion for the poor and the suffering. Of his own poetry he is famously said to have insisted that it was no mere craft of words but a vehicle of the sacred:

جي تُو بيت ڀانئين، سي آيتون آهِن

[“What you take to be [mere] couplets are, in truth, verses/signs [of God].”]

The _Shah Jo Risalo_ is sung nightly at his shrine by hereditary singers (_raagi faqirs_) in the unbroken tradition of the _Shah jo Raag_, accompanied by the _tambooro_ — a music inseparable from the poetry itself.

## The Shrine

Shah Abdul Latif’s shrine at Bhit Shah, richly ornamented with the blue-and-white glazed tilework (_kashi_) of Sindh, is one of the most beautiful and most visited shrines in the province, and the town that has grown around it is a centre of Sindhi culture, music, and letters. Pilgrims come throughout the year; the singing of his verse continues night after night; and the annual urs, held in the month of Safar, draws vast crowds and hosts one of the great cultural gatherings of Sindh, with music, poetry, and celebration alongside the rites of devotion. The shrine is maintained by the Sindh Auqaf department.

## Legacy

Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai is at once the national poet of Sindh and one of the towering mystics of South Asia — the man who raised the Sindhi language to its highest expression and made of its folk-tales a scripture of divine love. His _Shah Jo Risalo_ is cherished as the soul of Sindh; his music lives in an unbroken tradition at his shrine; and his vision of a love that embraces all beings, across every division of creed and caste, remains a living force. Bhit Shah is not merely his tomb but the cultural heart of a people.
