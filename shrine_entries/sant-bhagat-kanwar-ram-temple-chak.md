## Current site description (as of 12 October 2026)

*The row's `Description` cell exactly as it appears in `src/data/shrines-fallback.json` on 12 October 2026, untouched. Not yet checked against sources.*

This temple at Chak, a town in Shikarpur district, is dedicated to the memory of Bhagat Kanwar Ram (1885–1939), one of the best-loved Sindhi Hindu saint-singers of the twentieth century. Born in the village of Jarwar in the Sukkur region, he became renowned for his rendition of the *Sur Prabhati*, sung in the early morning; the gramophone company HMV is said to have preserved a number of his devotional songs on record, spreading his fame across Sindh. Revered by Hindus and Muslims alike as a figure of piety and communal harmony, he was assassinated at Ruk railway station in the Sukkur area in November 1939 and is remembered as a martyr. The temple at Chak was raised in his name after his death and was rebuilt in 2006; its main hall holds canopies bearing images of Bhagat Kanwar Ram and of Sant Satram Das, the saint of the *Sacho Satram* tradition with whom his memory is closely linked. In a syncretic touch characteristic of Sindhi devotion, the *Guru Granth Sahib* is also installed within, so that the darbar is honoured by Hindu and Sikh worshippers together. Every year on 2 November his followers gather to mark the anniversary of his martyrdom, both at this shrine and at Ruk station where he was killed.

The temple stands specifically in the Lakhi Ghulam Shah taluka of Shikarpur district, roughly 24 km east of Lakhi Ghulam Shah town. Kanwar Ram — born 13 April 1885 at Tarachand's house in Jarwar, then part of Mirpur Mathelo tehsil, and a disciple of Sant Satram Das (1866-1910) — was also remembered for singing the verses of Sufi poets including Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai, Sachal Sarmast, Sami, and Kabir alongside his own devotional compositions. The Chak temple was first managed by Ladak Mal and his family and, following its 2006 rebuilding, has been maintained by Bal Chand Esrani, Tara Chand Thawani, and Prakash Lal Devani. A separate commemoration, distinct from both the Chak temple and the Ruk station gathering, has also been held at the Institute of Sindhology in Hyderabad, where in 2008 the university's vice-chancellor marked Kanwar Ram's 69th death anniversary by announcing a dedicated Bhagat Kanwar Ram Memorial Corner.

- **Suba Thatta described** (c. 042; book's words: "صوبه تهته" (p. 530), "درین ولایت" (p. 530), "درین صوبه" (p. 529); period: the author's time, c. 1107 AH, a Mughal suba; the capitals "در پاستان زمان"):
  - Capitals: "برهمن اباد نام شهری بزرگ تختگاه بود و قلعه ان هزار و چهار صد برج داشت هر یک بفاصله یک طناب"; after it "ادپور پائے تخت گردید", with many traces of towers and walls remaining; "پس ازان دیول اکنون تهته و دیبل دار الحکومت است" (p. 530).
  - Land revenue: "درین ولایت سوم بخش از کشاورز به گیرند" (p. 530).
  - Resources: "کان نمک و اهن فراوان"; at "در[OCR?] شش کروهے" a "کان سنگ زرد", cut to size for building (p. 530).
  - Boats: "اکثر مدار بر کشتی است", "از خورد و بزرگ چهل هزار خواهد بود" (p. 530).
  - Game and food: "گورخر", hare, "کوته پاچه", pig and fish; the diet rests "بر برنج و جغرات و ماهی" (p. 530). Dried fish ("قاق") is shipped to the ports, and fish oil ("روغن[OCR?]") is used on boats; a fish called "پلوه[OCR?]" comes from the salt sea into the Sind, is netted, and is found in no other country (p. 530). Curds keep four months; flowers and many fruits, "خاصه انبه", and small melons in the desert (p. 530).
  - The liver-eaters: "جگر خواره این دیار مشهورتر فی الاکناف است"; they take livers, especially children's, by spell, especially at mealtimes; "بعضی گویند" a trance comes on them; they share the liver with fellow-practitioners and the victim dies (p. 530). They make others like themselves by feeding them a piece and teaching the spell; they ride the hyena ("کفتار") and bring news from afar (p. 530). When one is caught the experts split the calf and take out "دانه بسان انار دانه", which is fed to the victim, who recovers (pp. 529–530). A liver-eater will not sink with a millstone tied to the neck nor burn in fire; to cure one, the experts brand both temples, fill the eyes with salt, hang the person in a house for forty days, give saltless food and recite spells (p. 529). Most are women, some men; "در هر بلاد می باشند و اما در بلاد تهته بیشتر باشند" (p. 529).
  - Peoples: north of the line from Multan and Uch to Thatta and Kach Makran, hard-rock hills, home of Baluch and "بعضے افغان"; to the south, from Uch to Gujarat, sand hills and the "بهتی", whose chief's seat is Jaisalmer, and "دیگر اقوام راجپوت[OCR?]"; from Bhakkar to Nasirpur and Amarkot, "سبوده[OCR?]" and "جاریجه" (p. 529).
  - River: "بزرگ ترین دریائے سند است"; merchants from Multan and Bhakkar ship goods by it to Thatta, and travellers and even "عساکر گران" go by river; large armies rarely go by land, because of the jungle and the lack of water (p. 529).
  - Extent: "طول این صوبه از بهکر تا کچ مکران دو صد و پنجاه کروه و عرض از قصبه بدین تا بندر لاهری صد کروه" (p. 529). Bounds: east "گجرات احمداباد", west "کچ مکران", north "بهکر", south "دریائے شور" (p. 529).
  - Administration and revenue: "سرکار تهته و سیوستان و نصیر پور و امرکوت چهار سرکار مشتمل بر پنجاه و هفت محال و پنج بندر و ده[OCR?] کرور[OCR?] و چهل و نه لک و هفتاد هزار دام داخل این صوبه است" (pp. 528–529).
