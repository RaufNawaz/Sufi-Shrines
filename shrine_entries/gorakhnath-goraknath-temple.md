## Overview

The Gorakhnath Temple stands within the historic walled precinct of Gor Khatri, in the heart of the old city of Peshawar in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa. Dedicated to Guru Gorakhnath, the great *yogi* and saint venerated as the founder of the *Nath* monastic order, it is one of the very few surviving and functioning Hindu temples in the city. Though the present temple is a nineteenth-century structure, it sits atop one of the most storied and continuously sacred pieces of ground in the subcontinent, a site whose religious associations stretch back some two thousand years. Closed for decades after Partition, the temple was reopened for worship in 2011 following a legal campaign by Pakistan's Hindu community, and its revival has made it a symbol of the region's layered spiritual heritage.

## Gor Khatri: A Sacred Ground Through the Ages

The temple's significance is inseparable from Gor Khatri itself, an elevated mound in Peshawar's old city that has been held sacred, in various traditions, from the Gandhara period through to the Mughal era. The name is popularly interpreted as "Grave of the *Kshatriya*", or of warriors, hinting at ancient funerary or heroic associations. Archaeological excavation, notably the vertical digs begun by Professor Farzand Ali Durrani in the early 1990s, has revealed a remarkable stratigraphy: successive layers of occupation descending some fifteen metres and spanning many civilisations, from the Indo-Greeks of the second century BCE through the Hindu Shahi and later periods, testifying to the site's extraordinary depth of history.

Gor Khatri was long a celebrated resort of the *Nath yogis*, and it is recorded that the Mughal founder Babur, drawn by curiosity about the *Shaiva* ascetics who dominated the place, visited Gor Khatri during his forays into the region. Under the Mughals the site was transformed: in 1641 Jahan Ara Begum, the daughter of Emperor Shah Jahan, built a grand caravanserai here, the Sarai Jahanabad, together with a mosque, a bath and wells for the comfort of travellers. In the nineteenth century, during Sikh rule, the precinct served as the residence and headquarters of the Italian mercenary general Paolo Avitabile, governor of Peshawar.

## Guru Gorakhnath and the Nath Tradition

The temple honours Guru Gorakhnath, one of the most influential figures of medieval Indian spirituality, traditionally held to have lived somewhere between the ninth and twelfth centuries CE and revered as a *mahasiddha* and the founder of the *Nath* order of *yogis*. He is remembered as the disciple of Matsyendranath, and the *Nath* tradition he shaped, with its emphasis on *hatha yoga*, asceticism and esoteric discipline, spread widely across the subcontinent and left a deep imprint on popular religion. Peshawar and the wider north-west retained strong *Nath* associations, and Gor Khatri, with its long yogic heritage, was a natural centre for devotion to Gorakhnath. Nearby, the city preserved other shrines linked to the tradition, including the *Dargah* of Pir Ratan Nath in Jhanda Bazaar, reflecting the enduring presence of the *Nath* current in Peshawar's religious life.

## Closure and Reopening

The present Gorakhnath Temple was built in the mid-nineteenth century, around 1851, within the Gor Khatri complex. The Partition of 1947 and the departure of much of Peshawar's Hindu population left the temple abandoned, and it remained closed to worship for some six decades, its future uncertain amid changing custody of the site. Its restoration came through the persistence of the Hindu community: in 2011, following a petition and litigation before the Peshawar High Court, the court ordered that the temple be reopened for worship and recognised as part of the site's heritage. The reopening, after roughly sixty years of silence, was widely reported as a significant moment for religious freedom and the preservation of minority heritage in Pakistan, allowing Hindus once again to worship at the ancient shrine.

## Significance Today

The Gorakhnath Temple at Gor Khatri is at once a living place of Hindu worship and a window onto the astonishing antiquity of Peshawar, a city where Buddhist, Hindu, *Nath* yogic, Mughal and Sikh histories are stacked one upon another. As one of the few operational Hindu temples in the region and a rare survival of the *Nath* tradition in Pakistan, its reopening restored a thread of continuity to a sacred ground that has drawn worshippers for two millennia, and it remains a cherished site both for devotees and for those who value the shared heritage of the frontier.
