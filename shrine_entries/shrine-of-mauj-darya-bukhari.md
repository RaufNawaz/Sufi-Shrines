## Current site description (as of 10 October 2026)

*The row's `Description` cell exactly as it appears in `src/data/shrines-fallback.json` on 10 October 2026, untouched. Not yet checked against sources.*

Tucked away on Edward Road near the old Customs House in Anarkali, the octagonal tomb of Hazrat Meeran Muhammad Shah Bukhari — popularly called "Mauj Darya," or "Wave of the River" — commemorates a saint born in 1533 in Uch Sharif, the son of Safi-ud-Din Bukhari of the Sadaat of that town, a descendant of Jalaluddin Surkh-Posh Bukhari whose genealogy is traced through Syed Jalal-ud-Din Jahaniyan Jahangasht, and an adherent of the Suhrawardi silsila founded by Bahauddin Zakariya of Multan. He became a hafiz-e-Quran in his youth and, according to popular tradition, earned his epithet by miraculously diverting the flood-prone River Ravi away from Lahore. The 2026 field survey derives the same title differently: he was called Mouj Darya, it says, for his open-handed generosity and for the state of spiritual ecstasy (*mouj*) in which his prayers were held to be answered at once. Both derivations stand here as they were given. He is remembered, too, as a missionary presence who drew many Hindus and Sikhs to Islam.

Above all he is remembered as a spiritual confidant of the Mughal emperor Akbar. The shrine's tradition relates that the emperor was foretold — through the saint's father — of a windstorm and a lit lamp that would mark the saint's presence at the fort of Chittor; when both signs appeared and the Rajput army surrendered, Akbar became his disciple. Alam Faqri's *Tazkirah Awliya-e-Pakistan* preserves an independent, extended telling of this same episode — giving the saint's given name as Miran Muhammad Shah, tracing his descent from Makhdoom Jahaniyan Jahangasht and his origins in Uch Sharif, and recounting at length how his prayer and intercession were credited with securing the fort's surrender to Akbar — corroborating, from a separate hagiographical compendium, the tradition already recorded at the shrine. Legend credits him with distributing to the poor a substantial reward Akbar granted him near Batala, and it is said that on the saint's arrival in Lahore the emperor gifted the land on which he settled; his darasgah — the seat of learning where he now lies buried — is said still to hold land documents bearing Akbar's own signature. He died in 1604, the date traditionally taken as the founding of the shrine.

He married twice. His first wife, Syedna Bibi Fatima Sani — remembered as "Bibi Killah," and popularly as Mai Fatima — bore him two sons and rests in a separate shrine facing his own, near the historic Jain Mandir; a second wife bore a third son, of whom tradition tells that he could take the form of a lion. The saint lies in a small graveyard in the company of his family, his spiritual successors and gaddi nasheen, and his cook, Allahu Samad — himself the subject of one of the shrine's most striking tales, in which the saint, to answer sceptics, had his cook sit unharmed in a burning cauldron. Other wonders are recounted of him: a cauldron of rice he blessed during a shortage that fed the people for nearly seven days; brackish irrigation water over which he prayed for local landowners, which turned sweet; and a tree planted in his lifetime — "the blessed tree" — said never to have needed cutting. His langar, devotees say, has continued under his descendants to this day. The survey adds that books about him written by Muslim, Hindu and Sikh authors alike are widely read.

Architecturally the tomb is unusual among Lahore's shrines for its octagonal plan, with a low dome, corner towers at each of its eight angles, and a mixture of recessed blind arches and plain panelled facades — a style thought to echo Multani funerary tradition. The shrine gained unwelcome national attention in 2018 when construction of the Orange Line Metro Train's underground Anarkali station demolished its mosque, langar khana and courtyard, with tunnelling reported to pass close to the saint's grave in apparent violation of heritage buffer-zone regulations — an episode that became a flashpoint in Lahore's debates over balancing infrastructure development against protection of religious heritage. Yet devotees hold that for all the government construction pressing around it, nothing has ever tarnished the shrine's beauty.

The shrine remains one of scores of Lahore shrines administered by the Punjab Auqaf and Religious Affairs Department (Mehkama Auqaf), and continues to draw a devoted following. Its upkeep, the survey relates, ran down a long line before that — from the emperor Akbar through the rulers who followed him and Maharaja Ranjit Singh, and through the saint's own descendants — before the department took it on. Daily devotion includes the recitation of the Fatiha, the lighting of diyas, the making of vows (mannat) and the presentation of niyaz, and the gatherings of Thursday night (Jummay Raat) remain the busiest of the week. The annual urs is held on the 17th to the 19th of Rabi-ul-Awal, with a Mela Chiraagha (festival of lights), the ceremonial washing (gusal) of the tomb and the changing of its chadar, and langar throughout; some 2,200 to 2,500 visitors are recorded on an ordinary day, rising to between 225,000 and 300,000 across the urs.
