## Overview

The Shaktipeeth Shri Hinglaj Mata Mandir is among the most revered and remote Hindu shrines in the world, set within a natural cave in the rugged wilderness of the Makran coast, in the Lasbela district of Balochistan. Cradled today within the dramatic canyons and mud-volcano landscape of Hingol National Park, along the Hingol River, the shrine is dedicated to the goddess Hinglaj Mata, also called *Hingula Devi* and, affectionately, *Nani Mandir*. It is one of the fifty-one *Shakti Peethas* venerated across the subcontinent, and one of only two situated in present-day Pakistan. The annual *Hinglaj Yatra*, one of the largest Hindu pilgrimages in the country, draws tens of thousands of devotees along a demanding desert route, sustaining a tradition of worship that reaches back well over a millennium.

## The Shakti Peetha Tradition and the Falling of the Head

The sanctity of Hinglaj is rooted in the pan-Indian legend of the *Shakti Peethas*. According to tradition, the goddess Sati, wife of Shiva, immolated herself in grief and outrage when her father Daksha insulted her husband. The bereaved Shiva, cradling her corpse, began the cosmic dance of destruction, the *tandava*, threatening the universe itself. To end his frenzy, Vishnu released his discus, the *Sudarshana Chakra*, which dismembered Sati's body; wherever a part fell to earth, a *Shakti Peetha* arose, charged with the presence of the goddess. At Hinglaj, tradition holds, it was Sati's head (or, in some accounts, the crown of her head, the *brahmarandhra*) that came to rest, an association that lends the site especial pre-eminence among the Peethas. The presiding goddess is known by names such as Kottari, and her guardian *Bhairava* is named Bhimalochana.

A second cycle of legend links Hinglaj to the epic hero Rama. After slaying the demon-king Ravana, who was a brahmin and a devotee of Shiva, Rama is said to have been burdened by the grievous sin of *Brahmahatya* (brahmin-slaying). Counselled that only a pilgrimage to Hinglaj could purify him, Rama, according to tradition, journeyed to the shrine to perform penance. The site is also sacred to the Aghori ascetics, who revere Hinglaj Mata as their patron goddess, and it features in Nath yogi and Charan bardic traditions alike.

## Pilgrimage and the Yatra

The *Hinglaj Yatra* is a pilgrimage of both physical hardship and profound devotion. Historically pilgrims crossed harsh desert terrain, and the journey retains its arduous, penitential character. The main pilgrimage now takes place over several days in April. Along the route and at the shrine, devotees perform a sequence of rituals at a constellation of sacred spots: they climb the *Chandragup* and Khandewari mud volcanoes, casting coconuts and offerings into the bubbling craters and voicing their prayers and vows; they bathe in the waters of the Hingol River; and they honour subsidiary sites such as Ganesh Deva, the *Brahma Kund*, and Guru Gorakh Nath's *dhuni*. Only after these observances do pilgrims approach the cave sanctuary itself, where the goddess is worshipped not as a carved idol but as a small mud-stone form daubed with sindoor, in keeping with the shrine's ancient, aniconic character.

## Interfaith Reverence and Modern Revival

One of the most striking features of Hinglaj is its cross-communal devotion. Despite the upheavals of Partition and the decline of Hindu institutions in Pakistan, the shrine has not merely survived but is venerated by local Muslims of the region, who know it as *Nani Mandir* or *Bibi Nani* and regard it as a place of blessing. This shared reverence has helped preserve the site through difficult times. In 1986 the Hinglaj Sheva Mandali, the temple committee, was established to organise and promote the annual pilgrimage, and improved road access through Hingol National Park in recent years has dramatically increased the numbers making the *yatra*, transforming what was once a perilous journey for the hardiest of devotees into a mass pilgrimage. The wider Hingol cultural landscape has been proposed for recognition on account of its natural and spiritual importance.

## Significance Today

Hinglaj Mata Mandir stands as the foremost living Hindu pilgrimage site in Pakistan and a shrine of the very first rank in the *Shakti Peetha* network. Its endurance, in a remote and challenging landscape and across the fault-lines of religion and history, has made it a powerful symbol of resilience for Pakistan's Hindu minority and a place of extraordinary sanctity for devotees of the Goddess throughout the subcontinent.
