## Overview

The Shaktipeeth Shri Hinglaj Mata Mandir is among the most revered and remote Hindu shrines in the world, set within a natural cave in the rugged wilderness of the Makran coast, in the Lasbela district of Balochistan. Cradled today within the dramatic canyons and mud-volcano landscape of Hingol National Park, along the Hingol River, the shrine is dedicated to the goddess Hinglaj Mata, also called *Hingula Devi* and, affectionately, *Nani Mandir*. It is one of the fifty-one *Shakti Peethas* venerated across the subcontinent, and one of only two situated in present-day Pakistan. The annual *Hinglaj Yatra*, one of the largest Hindu pilgrimages in the country, draws tens of thousands of devotees along a demanding desert route, sustaining a tradition of worship that reaches back well over a millennium.

## The Shakti Peetha Tradition and the Falling of the Head

The sanctity of Hinglaj is rooted in the pan-Indian legend of the *Shakti Peethas*. According to tradition, the goddess Sati, wife of Shiva, immolated herself in grief and outrage when her father Daksha insulted her husband. The bereaved Shiva, cradling her corpse, began the cosmic dance of destruction, the *tandava*, threatening the universe itself. To end his frenzy, Vishnu released his discus, the *Sudarshana Chakra*, which dismembered Sati's body; wherever a part fell to earth, a *Shakti Peetha* arose, charged with the presence of the goddess. At Hinglaj, tradition holds, it was Sati's head (or, in some accounts, the crown of her head, the *brahmarandhra*) that came to rest, an association that lends the site especial pre-eminence among the Peethas. The presiding goddess is known by names such as Kottari, and her guardian *Bhairava* is named Bhimalochana.

A second cycle of legend links Hinglaj to the epic hero Rama. After slaying the demon-king Ravana, who was a brahmin and a devotee of Shiva, Rama is said to have been burdened by the grievous sin of *Brahmahatya* (brahmin-slaying). Counselled that only a pilgrimage to Hinglaj could purify him, Rama, according to tradition, journeyed to the shrine to perform penance. The site is also sacred to the Aghori ascetics, who revere Hinglaj Mata as their patron goddess, and it features in Nath yogi and Charan bardic traditions alike.

## Pilgrimage and the Yatra

The *Hinglaj Yatra* is a pilgrimage of both physical hardship and profound devotion. Historically pilgrims crossed harsh desert terrain, and the journey retains its arduous, penitential character. The main pilgrimage now takes place over several days in April. Along the route and at the shrine, devotees perform a sequence of rituals at a constellation of sacred spots: they climb the *Chandragup* and Khandewari mud volcanoes, casting coconuts and offerings into the bubbling craters and voicing their prayers and vows; they bathe in the waters of the Hingol River; and they honour subsidiary sites such as Ganesh Deva, the *Brahma Kund*, and Guru Gorakh Nath's *dhuni*. Only after these observances do pilgrims approach the cave sanctuary itself, where the goddess is worshipped not as a carved idol but as a small mud-stone form daubed with sindoor, in keeping with the shrine's ancient, aniconic character.

## Interfaith Reverence and Modern Revival

One of the most striking features of Hinglaj is its cross-communal devotion. Despite the upheavals of Partition and the decline of Hindu institutions in Pakistan, the shrine has not merely survived but is venerated by local Muslims of the region, who know it as *Nani Mandir* or *Bibi Nani* and regard it as a place of blessing. This shared reverence has helped preserve the site through difficult times. In 1986 the Hinglaj Sheva Mandali, the temple committee, was established to organise and promote the annual pilgrimage, and improved road access through Hingol National Park in recent years has dramatically increased the numbers making the *yatra*, transforming what was once a perilous journey for the hardiest of devotees into a mass pilgrimage. The wider Hingol cultural landscape has been proposed for recognition on account of its natural and spiritual importance.

## Significance Today

Hinglaj Mata Mandir stands as the foremost living Hindu pilgrimage site in Pakistan and a shrine of the very first rank in the *Shakti Peetha* network. Its endurance, in a remote and challenging landscape and across the fault-lines of religion and history, has made it a powerful symbol of resilience for Pakistan's Hindu minority and a place of extraordinary sanctity for devotees of the Goddess throughout the subcontinent.

- **Legends & Miracles**
  - **The dismemberment of Sati, narrated at length as the myth "frequently heard at the shrine
    today"** (p. 55, folio not stated): the Goddess incarnates as Sati, daughter of king Daksha;
    marries Shiva against her father's will; Daksha performs "the aśvamedha (Skt.)" and invites every
    god "except Shiva"; Sati "jumped into the sacrificial fire"; Shiva kills Daksha, then dances "the
    tāṇḍava (Skt.), a frantic dance that threatened to destroy the whole world"; "the God Vishnu took
    his discus and cut Sati's body into many parts that fell out of Shiva's arms and scattered all
    over the subcontinent", and each landing place "became known as śākta pīṭha (Skt.), or seats of
    the Goddess's power." **A variant is recorded at the shrine:** "although Vishnu's intervention is
    sometimes omitted; in this version, the wild motion of Shiva's dance alone caused the body's
    dismemberment." (p. 55, folio not stated) The conclusion says the renovation foregrounds exactly
    this narrative: "the story of the Goddess Sati's dismembering and the dissemination of her body
    parts over the South Asian continent" (p. 325, folio not stated).
  - **Which part of Sati fell at Hinglaj — three answers, kept side by side.** (i) The brahmarandhra,
    "upper part of the head", per the Pīṭhanirṇaya (p. 55, folio not stated); glossed by Apte as "an
    aperture on the crown of the head through which the soul is said to escape on its leaving the
    body" (Apte 1992) (p. 98, folio not stated); at the image, "one of the globular stones on the
    eastern side is Sati's skull, which came to rest there after Vishnu cut her body into several
    pieces" (p. 229, folio not stated). (ii) The navel, per the Caṇḍīmaṅgala: Mukundarama "instead
    states that the navel rather than the Devi's brahmarandhra has fallen at Hinglaj (Sircar 1973,
    33)" (pp. 230–231, folio not stated); "since the womb (garbha) is directly underneath the sanctum
    it must have been the navel that has fallen in Hinglaj" (p. 231, folio not stated); and at the
    shrine today a minority argues "that since the womb (Hin. garbha), in the form of a tunnel into
    the mountain, is found under the image at Hinglaj, above it can only be a part of her belly"
    (p. 57, folio not stated). (iii) The womb: "One pilgrim mentioned that the island is the place
    where the Goddess's brahmarandhra is found, while at Hinglaj it is actually her womb (Hin. kokh)
    that fell to earth. He also added that this is why pilgrims can be reborn at Hinglaj and that he
    never saw anybody being born from a woman's head." (p. 260, folio not stated) **The author states
    which prevails:** "it is clear that the imaginary of the fallen skull is the prevailing one"
    (p. 231, folio not stated).
  - **The Hingol legend — the shrine's own foundation story, paraphrased by the author from three
    written sources** ("Abro 1991; Khatri 1973; Chhabria 2000", p. 229, folio not stated). "The demon
    Vichitra had two sons. One of them was called Hingol and the other was named Sindur." (p. 229,
    folio not stated) Sindur loots China; the people of China, "worshippers of Shiva", petition him;
    Shiva promises that "within three days, Ganesha would come and relieve them from their
    afflictions"; "After three days, `Ganesh`[text-layer?] [Ganesha] came indeed, and killed the
    tyrant Sindur." (pp. 229–230, folio not stated) Hingol "retreated to a mountain, raised his hands
    toward the sky, and stood on one toe for thousands of years"; Brahma grants that "he could not be
    killed in war, by any weapon, or by any human being and also not under the sky nor on the earth"
    (pp. 229–230, folio not stated). Parvati appears as a beautiful woman, is pursued from the
    Himalaya to the River Indus and into Sindhudesh, and "at the place where the shrine is located
    today, the Goddess absorbed him into her body" (p. 230, folio not stated). **The naming of the
    place, in the Goddess's own words as the book gives them.** Hingol's last wish: "You should remain
    here at this very place under my name and fulfill all wishes that people bring to you!"; the
    Goddess answers "From now on I will be called Hinglaj Devi, and this place should be my place of
    worship. I will stay here forever with Hingol." "Since this day, both of their faces are found
    inside the cave." (p. 230, folio not stated) **The author's own placing of it:** "This story,
    which is clearly a reference to the legend of Narasimha in the Bhāgavata Purāṇa, however, is
    rarely heard at the shrine today. Devotees often simply state that the other face is Shiva's or
    Rama's." (p. 230, folio not stated); "no particular myth had, as of the end of my research, fully
    solidified around this enigmatic part of the statue" (p. 230, folio not stated).
  - **The second face in the effigy, told two ways by the same priest.** "In 2009, Gopal Maharaj, the
    only permanent priest (Hin. pujārī) at the Hinglaj temple, explained that the puzzling second
    face found in the Goddess's effigy represents the demon Hingol, a local tyrant killed by the Devi
    in mythological times. Later, in 2012, the priest changed his explanation, insisting instead that
    the second face is that of a vīr (Hin. hero/army commander) of the Goddess Durga, who killed the
    demon Mahishasura." (p. 46, folio not stated) **Both years are cleanly printed.** "Maharaj, who
    gained his position as a pujārī through appointment by the local temple committee (the HSM) in
    2008, revises the story and connects the shrine to a broader Hindu discourse." (pp. 46–47, folio
    not stated) "In it, he sets aside the tyrant Hingol, who is a part of the local tradition, and
    changes him into the demon Mahishasura, a figure of the famous Devī Māhātmaya." (p. 47, folio not
    stated) Today, "the other round stone is often interpreted as the face of one of Durga's vīrs who
    helped her to kill the demon Mahishasura." (p. 229, folio not stated)
  - **Rama's pilgrimage — the charter narrative of the whole route.** The sin: the sage Kumbhodar
    refuses Rama's food because he is a "Brahmicide"; "by slaying the demon Ravana, Rama made himself
    guilty of 'brahm hatyā'... and thus committed one of the worst sins possible. To cleanse himself
    of this sin, the sage advises Rama to make a pilgrimage to Hinglaj Mata, the only place that can
    purify him." (p. 120, folio not stated) "The sage Vashishta advised Rama to go on the pilgrimage
    to Hinglaj, which was the only place where he could be purified of his horrible offense." (p. 76,
    folio not stated) The defeat: at the "Ungakhera Bherum Luk" pass, "the Goddess's army, which
    guards the entry to her empire, intercepts the group. A fight breaks out in which many of Rama's
    men die, and after twelve years of battle, the king from Ayodhya and his army must retreat."
    (pp. 120–121, folios not stated) The condition: "she responds that he must return to his first
    stop, Ram Bagh, and make the journey again as a simple pilgrim. So Rama leaves his entourage, his
    army, and vehicles behind and sets out to walk to the shrine, accompanied only by his closest
    friends." (p. 121, folio not stated) The promise that founds later pilgrimage: "Rama's men are
    devastated at not being allowed to accompany their lord to the Goddess's abode. The Devi thus
    promises them that their descendants will, at some point in time, all come back to do the
    pilgrimage." (p. 121, folio not stated) The water miracles: Hanuman's stomping "produces only a
    dry riverbed"; Lakshmana's arrow "only succeeds in detaching one of the hills"; "In agony, Sita
    places her palm down on the soil and thereby makes five wells appear, from which the group
    drinks." (p. 121, folio not stated) The purification and its monument: "the Devi grants him
    purification of his sin. To mark his completed yātrā, he carves the symbols of the sun and moon
    on the mountain opposite the temple." (p. 121, folio not stated) The same carvings are shown to
    pilgrims at the shrine: "the Aghori Baba shows the group the symbols of the sun and the moon,
    which, as he explains, were carved into the mountain by Ramachandra on his journey to Hinglaj
    (Abadhut 1964, 212)." (p. 132, folio not stated); "the Aghori Baba shows them the signs of the
    sun and moon inscribed on the mountains opposite the temple and explains that these were created
    by Ramachandra himself when he came to Hinglaj to cleanse himself of his sin of killing a
    Brahmin." (p. 142, folio not stated); "the imprint of a sun and a moon on the side of the
    mountain, supposedly etched there by Rama after he completed his journey to Hinglaj to receive
    purification for the sin of killing a Brahmin" — "The place is said to be unreachable by mortals
    and indeed looks quite inaccessible from the bottom of the valley, though it is possible to climb
    up there." (p. 257, folio not stated). **The Brahmin is not named**: **(not stated)**. **The
    textual source the book could not find:** "Jethamalani writes that Rama's pilgrimage to Hinglaj is
    found in the `Sat Koṭī Ānand Rāmā’ian` `[text-layer?]` [Sat Koṭī Ānand Rāmāʾian] (Urd.), a text
    taken from the fifth chapter of the `Yātrā Kānḍ` (Urd.), an addition to the canonized corpus of
    Valmiki's Rāmāyaṇa (Jethamalani 1994, 37). Unfortunately, I have not been able to locate this text
    anywhere." (p. 120, folio not stated); the paraphrase is assembled "from the bits and pieces I
    have gathered from a few different texts (cf. Jethamalani 1994, 37–38; Hart 1839, 79–80; Jareca
    n.d., 110–111)" (p. 120, folio not stated). A second unverified source: "Hart also claims that the
    account of his journey can be found in the `Hiṃgulā Purāṇ` (Hart 1839, 79), a fact that I cannot
    confirm, at least not with the version available to me." (p. 121, folio not stated) Hart's causal
    claim, reported without endorsement: "Hart even posits that the pilgrimage's attractiveness is
    based mainly on the legend that Rama himself visited the shrine." (p. 121, folio not stated) What
    survives today: "Notwithstanding the fact that parts of this story are now widely forgotten, its
    core—the walking pilgrimage by the king of Ayodhya and his entourage—are still often remembered by
    visitors to the Hinglaj Mata shrine." (p. 119, folio not stated); "What remains among visitors of
    the shrine today is the notion that Rama, an important god-king, had to come to Hinglaj as a
    simple pilgrim to rid himself of the sin of killing Ravana—another proof for the importance of the
    journey's kinesthetic experience (see also Cunningham 1975, 309)." (p. 122, folio not stated)
    "no figure was ever as present in the representations of the traditional pilgrimage to Hinglaj as
    Rama, the seventh incarnation of the God Vishnu." (p. 119, folio not stated)
  - **The ship turned to stone at "Goorab-i-Sung"**, quoted from Hart at length: "A wealthy Shet named
    Kowaljee freighted a vessel from Hindoostan to carry himself and a large party to Hinglaj… when
    the Goddess, justly indignant at his arrogance and presumption, transformed the boat and every
    soul on board into stone. (Hart 1839, 91)" (p. 114, folio not stated). The book reads it as a
    sanction on route choice, not only as a wonder: "the 'usual' way is considered to be the one over
    land and not the more comfortable sea route" (p. 118, folio not stated).
  - **The Brahmakshatriya charter: the Goddess grants and withholds at Hinglaj.** Dadhichi tells the
    five princes that "the five princes must go to the shrine of Hinglaj to be initiated into her
    tradition. So the princes fast, become initiated into the mantra of the Goddess Hinglaj, and then
    set out on the pilgrimage to the Goddess's abode." (p. 64, folio not stated) They "eventually
    ascend the mountain at Hinglaj, where they fast and meditate for another twelve years." (p. 64,
    folio not stated) She refuses to undo Parashurama's curse — "How could the words of the mighty
    Parashurama become a lie? But I will help you and do what is possible" — then "instructs the
    princes in worshipping her as their clan Goddess [Hin. kul'devī] and adds that he who enters her
    cave will gain the dharma of an ascetic. She furthermore advises them to always worship the
    Saraswat Brahmins." She promises that their descendants "would be prosperous and fortunate in the
    future" even after they forget the art of weaponry (p. 64, folio not stated). The author's gloss:
    "The pilgrimage of Hinglaj also plays a significant role within this plot: it is here where the
    divine and the mundane meet and where the humans can exercise some agency over their fate,
    eventually even altering the course of the curse." (p. 66, folio not stated) "The Goddess Hinglaj
    serves here as the community's creator and as the guarantor of 'Kshatriyadom.'" (p. 68, folio not
    stated)
  - **Mekan Dada and the milk miracle.** The Goddess, "disguised as a normal shepherd, wearing a
    Balochi dress and accompanied by a herd of goats she had created through her mantra power", cannot
    fill Mekan's cup, "so that a hundred thousand liters came out, but still she could not fill the
    glass" (pp. 77–78, folios not stated); she then grants him the guruship of all who come to her
    place (p. 78, folio not stated). His own statement of the ritual programme, which is the fullest
    inventory of the site's features in that chapter: "I will go to Chandrakup, I will climb up to
    Anil Kund, I will do the pūjā at your temple, I will put siṃdūr [Hin. vermilion] on the place
    where Rama sat down, and I will see the moon and the sun carved into stone there! I will receive
    all the knowledge and wisdom from this place and only then will I go home." (p. 78, folio not
    stated) — **a cave, Anil Kund, a temple with pūjā, "the place where Rama sat down", and a carved
    moon and sun. No measurements, no building date, no architect: (not stated).**
  - **The Charan signs.** A bell rings at the Hinglaj temple in Balochistan whenever a Charan boy is
    born — "Charans often say" (p. 70, folio not stated). A rock will bury the shrine if a Charan man
    fails to come before his 60th birthday (p. 70, folio not stated), restated in the Barhath epigraph:
    "From the moment a Charan is born, Mother Hinglaj waits for him to come for the darśan; if he
    leaves the world otherwise [without having visited the shrine], then a stone falls from the
    Mother's mountain." (BARHATH 2006, 9) (p. 172, folio not stated). The Goddess "grants Charan men
    the ability to write and compose stories and poems. She is their muse, and they imagine her sitting
    on a poet's tongue, quickening his creativity." (p. 70, folio not stated) The Devi "promised to
    incarnate as a Charan woman periodically in order to bring change and prosperity to the community"
    (p. 70, folio not stated). Returning alive was itself the miracle: "Homecomers were seen as a
    divine gift since, as the Charans say, the Goddess herself had blessed such pilgrims and allowed
    them to return safely from the brink of death." (p. 172, folio not stated); "those returning alive
    from the dangerous pilgrimage were often celebrated as gifts from the Goddess herself, who
    protected them on their way" (p. 321, folio not stated). Karni Mata's link: the Goddess appeared to
    Karni's mother "While Karni's father was undertaking the pilgrimage to Hinglaj" (p. 73, folio not
    stated).
  - **How the shrine came to be where it is, per Kamphorst.** "the Tumbel spread the Goddess's fame in
    Las Bela, and so Hinglaj decided to settle in a cave close to the Hingol River, where she has been
    worshipped by the Charan and other communities since as early as the ninth century (Kamphorst 2008,
    237)." (p. 71, folio not stated)
  - **The Muslim legends of the site, from three colonial reports.** "Colonel Wilford writes that the
    local Muslim community living around the temple believed that Eve fell from heaven, landed in one
    of the ponds inside the valley, and later settled there and became known as Bibi Nani or as Hinglaj
    (Wilford 1851, 259; Abro 1991, 66)." (p. 89, folio not stated) "Hart mentions that Muslims in the
    area claimed that Hazrat Ali, the son-in-law of the Prophet Mohammad, split a rock in two with his
    sword as he was on his way into the valley (Hart 1839, 94)." (pp. 89–90, folio not stated) "And one
    of Aurel Stein's informants claimed that Hinglaj, or Bibi Nani, is considered to be the wife of Ali
    (Stein 1943, 202)." (p. 90, folio not stated) "Today, such myths have virtually vanished from
    Hinglaj, and even though many recent Hindu texts note this element of Muslim worship at the shrine,
    no particular details are given." (p. 90, folio not stated) The Eve story is repeated as the Zikri
    claim: "The (Zikri) Muslim tradition at the shrine claims that this is the place where Eve fell
    from paradise and later became known as Hinglaj (Abro 1991, 66)." (p. 255, folio not stated) The
    Muslim miracle-claim on the stone itself: the grave of a saintly wife of `cAli`[text-layer?] [ʿAli],
    "miraculously translated to this spot" (p. 235, folio not stated, quoting Stein 1943, 202).
  - **Holdich on the shared shrine** (p. 92, folio not stated): "Here Hindoo and Muhammadan pilgrims
    alike resort, each claiming the divine protection of the presiding goddess or of the departed saint,
    according to the tenets of their faith; and neither recognising that the object of their veneration
    is probably the same goddess who was known to the Chaldeans under the same old-world name (Nana) a
    thousand years before the time of Abraham. (Holdich 1904, 45)" Holdich "describes her influence as
    extending 'from the Euphrates to the Ganges' (Holdich 1904, 45)" (p. 92, folio not stated).
  - **The Muslim woman priest of the Goddess, reported and then doubted by the author.** "According to
    Briggs, there exists a legend that a Muslim woman was the priest of the Goddess in Hinglaj in those
    days. By ritually applying the ṭīkā (Hin. ornamental or sectarian mark) on the forehead of the
    (Hindu) pilgrims, she turned all of them into Muslims. Thus, on their way back, the pilgrims had to
    be reconverted to their original faith—hence the branding (Briggs 1938, 106–110)." (p. 115, folio
    not stated) Schaflechner's verdict: "Due to the fact that no similar account is mentioned in any
    other sources, it is difficult to confirm Briggs' suggestion… Thus, the story of converting pilgrims
    into Muslims may simply be another in the huge corpus of unverifiable legends surrounding the aloof
    shrine of the Goddess." (p. 115, folio not stated)
  - **The Goddess has left the temple — two versions.** "He claimed that the Goddess actually no longer
    resides at the shrine because she has become annoyed with the crowd herself. He added that if one
    wanted to meet her, one must climb up to Chaurasi, a two-and-a-half-hour ascent into the mountains,
    to a freshwater pond called Anil Kund where she had taken refuge." (p. 188, folio not stated) — the
    speaker is "Another visitor from Karachi". "visitors at Hinglaj often claim that Anil Kund has now
    become the 'real' tīrtha, the present-day abode of the Goddess who retreated high up into the
    mountains to escape the tiresome noise and masses of people now overrunning her temple in the
    valley" (p. 255, folio not stated). And the island version: "the Goddess moves between the Hinglaj
    Valley and the island, where she resides for the largest part of the day, for seven 'pruhurs' (also
    from Hin. pahar, a three-hour period) (Hart 1839, 98)" (p. 260, folio not stated); the few who know
    of Astola "portray it as the 'actual' seat of the Goddess who nowadays only rarely resides at
    Hinglaj" (p. 260, folio not stated).
  - **Anil Kund carries the Ganges.** "at the Anil pond, the waters of the River Ganges are said to be
    flowing" (pp. 146–147, folios not stated). Fully: "Once Shiva saved the earth from destruction by
    taming the waters of the River Ganges with the help of his long locks of hair (Wilkins 2003, 467).
    He also used to come to the Hinglaj mountains for the solitude necessary for his meditation, and so
    some of the water from the Ganges also found its way from his hair into the hollow at Anil Kund."
    (p. 255, folio not stated) — attributed to "the local tradition". "all the holy rivers, including
    the Ganges, the Sarasvati, the Kaveri, and others, assembled together to meet and worship her.
    Altogether, nine hundred rivers came together here to form Anil Kund... In other accounts, the
    number of rivers convening at Anil Kund is eighty-four thousand—thus the alternate name, Chaurasi
    (Hin. `<caurāsī>`[text-layer?] [caurāsī] eighty-four)." (p. 256, folio not stated) — attributed to
    "Pilgrims from Gujarat". **The two counts are not reconciled.**
  - **The Goddess drops the boulder as a concession — with a rival origin for the same stone.** "In
    ancient times, munis and r̥ṣis (Hin. sages, wise men) would meditate in the northern mountain range.
    For this reason, common people could no longer visit Chaurasi since they did not dare disturb the
    sages. As a result, the pilgrims asked the Goddess to arrange some other way for them to receive the
    merit" (p. 259, folio not stated) — attributed to Gopal Maharaj. Against it: "Another story says
    that Guru Gorakhnath himself took the stone from somewhere around Anil Kund and brought it to this
    place (Jethamalani 1994, 8)." (p. 259, folio not stated)
  - **The Goddess closes the pond at a bereaved mother's demand**, told by Gopal Maharaj as his own
    eyewitness account: "After only fifteen days there was so much rain that a huge landslide filled the
    pond... it was only since a mother cried in front of the Goddess, because she had lost her child,
    that the pond disappeared." (p. 253, folio not stated). He explicitly rejects rival accounts: "People
    talk a lot about what has happened, but none of their stories are true" (p. 253, folio not stated).
  - **The Goddess's dream instruction, which ended a line of the author's research.** "A few days later,
    Gopal Maharaj … approached me and told me that I must stop my research on the shape of the image and

- **Significance**
  - **The book's own claim for the shrine's rank, in the author's voice.** The conclusion offers "a final
    look at the dramatic relationship between identity, change, and solidification for Pakistan's largest
    religious minority at its most important place of worship today." (p. 27, folio not stated) "HINGLAJ
    DEVI IN Balochistan has come to be one of the most important centers for Hindu spiritual practice in
    Pakistan over the past three decades." (p. 29, folio not stated) — **the case here is the text layer's
    rendering of small capitals and is not reliable evidence of the printed case; see Doubts.** "Today, many
    Pakistani Hindus share the opinion that Hinglaj occupies an outstanding position among the country's
    religious sites." (p. 270, folio not stated) It is "generally considered the largest active tīrtha sthān
    in Pakistan" (p. 279, folio not stated), and "for most people, the shrine is considered the oldest in the
    entire Hindu tradition, so ancient and important that Hindus even come from India to visit it" (p. 270,
    folio not stated) — **the author reports these as opinions held, not as his own datings.** "Hinglaj is
    not simply a particular, unique locality anymore; it stands in a synecdochical relationship to Hinduism
    as such" (p. 287, folio not stated). "Because of the shrine's central role, however, its examination
    offers meaningful insights into group formation and the Hindu body's relationship to the nation state."
    (p. 325, folio not stated) "this cacophony of voices—which conspicuously emerged after the shrine gained
    its central position for Pakistani Hinduism" (p. 187, folio not stated).
  - **Primacy among the śākta pīṭhas, and the two grounds devotees give.** "In the eyes of many Pakistani
    (and Indian) Hindus, Hinglaj is the oldest and thus the greatest of all śākta pīṭhas (Skt. seats of
    power) in the world." (p. 51, folio not stated) "The term pīṭha (Skt. seat, altar) specifically describes
    holy sites relating to Goddess worship all over South Asia" (p. 52, folio not stated). (i) "Pakistani
    devotees today claim that this primary position is because Hinglaj would be a 'xālis sthān' (Hin.), a
    'real' pilgrimage place, linked, for example, to its characteristic of being svayaṃbhū (Hin.; Skt.
    `svayambhū )`[text-layer?] [svayambhū], self-generated (this will be discussed in detail in Chapter 5)."
    (p. 52, folio not stated) (ii) "Others legitimize this claim by noting that Hinglaj (as Hiṅgulā,
    Hiṅgulāja, Hiṅgulāṭa) has been mentioned in various Sanskrit sources and thereby, is part of the real
    deal." (p. 52, folio not stated) The economy of citation: "In many such sources, the site in Balochistan
    appears as one of the śākta pīṭhas, also associated with the famous legend of the Goddess Sati found in
    the Mahābhārata. This mentioning in the Sanskrit corpus serves as 'symbolic capital' for the temple in
    Balochistan, frequently wielded by devotees in conversations about the shrine's legitimacy." (p. 52,
    folio not stated) The two modern grounds: "Hinglaj's ancient heritage (which Hindus and non-Hindus alike
    place within the same time frame as that of the Mohenjo-Daro civilization) and its mention in Sanskrit
    scriptures (cf. Motyani 2011, Ansari 2001)"; "these two labels—its age and its ties to
    Sanskrit—distinguish the place from other Hindu sites in Pakistan and endow it with the aura of a 'real'
    tīrtha sthān" (p. 270, folio not stated). **Which Sanskrit scriptures, and which passages: (not
    stated)** there.
  - **The Sanskrit attestation, source by source, with the datings the book gives and refuses to settle.**
    The Hevajra Tantra, Sādhanamāla and Kālikā Purāṇa, "the oldest known texts dealing with these pīṭhas",
    "do not include Hinglaj in their respective lists of four (Datta 2009, 157)" (p. 52, folio not stated).
    `Kulārṇava Tantra`: "Hiṅgulā" is found there and, "when allegedly quoting the Rudrayāmala—names eighteen
    pīṭhas, with 'Hiṅgulā' being third on the list (Sircar 1973, 18)" (p. 52, folio not stated); dated
    "earlier than 1000 CE" by P. V. Kane (Banerji 2007, 149) (p. 52, folio not stated), "between the eleventh
    and the fifteenth centuries" by Brooks (Brooks 2002, 347), and "between the thirteenth and the fourteenth
    centuries" by White (White 2003, 23) (p. 53, folio not stated). `Rudrayāmala`: composed "before 1052 CE"
    per Sircar (Sircar 1973, 17n4) but "not earlier than the thirteenth or fourteenth century" per White,
    during what he calls the "Tantric revival" (White 2003, 23); and decisively, "the currently available
    text of the Rudrayāmala Tantra itself does, in fact, mention only ten such pīṭhas as the principal holy
    sites of the Goddess. The name 'Hiṅgulā' is not one of them." (p. 53, folio not stated) `Kubjikā Tantra`:
    mentions Hinglaj "as the fifth of summa summarum forty-two pīṭhas (Sircar 1973, 19)", "usually supposed
    to be a fairly early work" per Sircar, "a late work" (twelfth to fourteenth centuries) per Bharati
    (Bharati 1965, 66n25), undated by Banerji (Banerji 2007, 146) (p. 53, folio not stated). The author's
    verdict: "tantric texts, therefore, prove fairly inaccurate for the attempt to date the occurrence of the
    name Hinglaj." (p. 53, folio not stated)
  - **Vāmana Purāṇa (VāmP), the possible earliest mention — and the author's refusal of the identification.**
    "At VāmP chapter 44, verse 47, it reads that Carcikā later roamed the earth only to eventually settle at
    'the best of all places—the Hingulata mountain' (Gupta 1968, 393)." (p. 53, folio not stated) Agrawala
    "assumes (a) that this line is, in fact, a reference to the mountain range in Balochistan and (b) that
    Hiṅgulāja is the 'Indianization' of a Nani cult at the same place (Agrawala 1964, 137)" (p. 53, folio not
    stated). **Schaflechner refuses (a):** "I cannot share Agrawala's conviction about the location of the
    shrine… a conclusive link between the text and the site in Balochistan as the location of the Hingula
    mountain is not given because the VāmP does not elaborate on where the aforesaid mountain is to be
    found." (pp. 53–54, folio not stated) Counter-evidence: "Brighenti points out the fact that another text,
    the early Pali Buddhist Kuṇālajātaka, locates the Hingula mountain in the Himalaya instead of toward the
    western side of the subcontinent (Brighenti 2016, 44)." (p. 54, folio not stated) VāmP datings: ninth to
    sixteenth centuries per scholars Rocher quotes, "most probably" the ninth per Hazara (Rocher 1986,
    240–241); "the first half of the seventh century" per G. P. Bhatt, "connecting it to the empires of
    Harṣavardhana and Mahendravarma Pallava" (Bhatt 2013, xiv); "late" per Mylius (Mylius 1988, 143) (p. 54,
    folio not stated). Verdict: "many scholars agree that the VāmP could indeed be of an early origin, thus
    making this possibly one of the first mentioning of the name Hiṅgulā." (p. 54, folio not stated)
  - **Brahmavaivarta Purāṇa (BVP).** Dating: the name was known to "Al Biruni (973–1048) and thus must be
    older than the eleventh century (Brown 1974, 23f.)", but "the extent to which this name also refers to
    the canonical text of the BVP as we find it today is questionable (Brown 1974, 24)"; Mylius calls it
    "fairly late" (Mylius 1988, 142); per Farquhar the relevant `Kr̥ṣṇajanmakhaṇḍa` "is an interpolation into
    the BVP", and "The general part about the pīṭhas must also be understood as a later interpolation,
    inserted not earlier than the fourteenth or fifteenth century (Sircar 1973, 6n3)." (p. 54, folio not
    stated)
  - **Devī Bhāgavata Purāṇa (DBh) — absent from the older list, present in a later one.** "The given list of
    108 names and places of worship in section VII, chapter 30, pp. 53–102 does not include the name Hinglaj.
    Only a later list in section VII, chapter 38, pp. 5–30 identifies Hiṅgulā as one of the śākta tīrthas.
    Even this list, however, is said to be a medieval interpolation (Sircar 1973, 107)." (pp. 54–55, folio not
    stated) Datings: core "likely from the eleventh or twelfth century" with the final version needing "four
    to five more centuries" (Brown 1992, 3); "between the eighth and twelfth centuries" per Beane, who
    "generally tends to date texts earlier than do other scholars" (Beane 1977, 62); "completed no later than
    1200 AD" per Hazara (p. 54, folio not stated). **The two page ranges are in an order the book does not
    explain — see Doubts.**
  - **Pīṭhanirṇaya (PN) — the text on which the shrine's primacy rests.** "In the Pīṭhanirṇaya (PN), the
    number of fifty-one śākta pīṭhas was first associated with the body parts of the Goddess Sati (Sircar
    1973, 4). In this list, Hinglaj is mentioned first, and thus the text is most frequently quoted as proof
    of the primary position of Hinglaj among all the śākta seats." (p. 55, folio not stated) What it supplies:
    "the name of the pīṭha (Hiṅgulā, Hiṅgulāṭa) as well as the names of the residing Goddess there (Koṭṭarī,
    Koṭṭavī, Koṭṭarīśā), the associated form of Shiva (Bhīmalocana), and most significantly, the part of
    Sati's body—the brahmarandhra (Skt. 'upper part of the head')—that is said to have fallen there (Sircar
    1973, 85)." (p. 55, folio not stated) Its date: "Sircar concludes that the PN must have been composed
    between the years 1690 and 1720 (Sircar 1973, 24). Thus, it cannot be definitely proven that claims to
    Hinglaj's primacy among the śākta pīṭha due to its association with the Goddess's brahmarandhra are older
    than the given time frame of the PN." (p. 56, folio not stated) **`1690` and `1720` are both cleanly
    printed.** The text is elsewhere dated "(seventeenth to eighteenth century)" (p. 229, folio not stated).
  - **Tantracūḍāmaṇi — the source today's Hinglaj literature cites, which the book disqualifies.** "The claim
    that the upper part of the Goddess's head fell at Hinglaj is frequently cited as evidence of the site's
    importance, with literature about Hinglaj mentioning the Tantracūḍāmaṇi as the source of this claim
