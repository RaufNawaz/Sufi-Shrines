## Overview

At Nurpur Shahan, in the Margalla Hills on the edge of Islamabad, stands the shrine of Hazrat Bari Imam — Sayyid Abdul Latif Kazmi (1617–1705) — the patron saint of the Islamabad-Rawalpindi region and one of the most venerated holy figures of the Pothohar. His darbar is the site of one of the largest annual urs festivals in the country, and for centuries pilgrims have climbed to Nurpur Shahan to seek the blessing of the saint whom the people of the capital regard as their guardian.

## The Life of the Saint

Sayyid Abdul Latif Shah, called _Bari Imam_ and _Bari Sarkar_, was born in 1617 into a Kazmi Sayyid family; tradition traces his descent to the Prophet through the Imams. From youth he was drawn to devotion, and after study and long spiritual wandering he took the Sufi path — associated with the Qadiri tradition — and settled at last at the village now called Nurpur Shahan, in the hills above the plain where Islamabad would one day rise. There he lived as an ascetic and guide, tending the poor, and gathered a wide following; he is remembered for a life of prayer, generosity, and miracle. He is said to have been a contemporary of the Mughal emperor Aurangzeb, who is remembered as honouring him. He died in 1705 and was buried at Nurpur Shahan.

## The Shrine and the Urs

The shrine of Bari Imam at Nurpur Shahan is a large complex and a ceaseless resort of pilgrims from the capital region and beyond. Its annual urs, held in the spring, is among the greatest religious festivals of Pakistan — a gathering of hundreds of thousands over several days, with qawwali, _dhamal_, _langar_, and a vast fair, drawing devotees, dervishes, and _malangs_ from across the country. The shrine keeps the daily prayers and the free kitchen, and it is bound up with the identity of Islamabad as the capital’s patron darbar. Like other great shrines it suffered a grievous suicide bombing in 2005, during the urs; yet the devotion endured, and the shrine remains thronged.

## Legacy

Bari Imam is the guardian saint of Pakistan’s capital region — a seventeenth-century Sayyid ascetic whose shrine at Nurpur Shahan has become, in the shadow of modern Islamabad, one of the country’s greatest centres of popular devotion. His urs is a defining festival of the Pothohar, and his memory endures as that of a saint of generosity and blessing watching over the capital from the Margalla Hills.
