## Current site description (as of 10 October 2026)

*The row's `Description` cell exactly as it appears in `src/data/shrines-fallback.json` on 10 October 2026, untouched. Not yet checked against sources.*

The shrine of Baba Shah Jamal, in the locality that now bears his name adjoining Ichhra and opposite Forman Christian College, commemorates Syed Shah Jamal, a Sufi of Hussaini Syed descent who traced his lineage to Jalaluddin Surkh-Posh Bukhari of Uch Sharif. The 2026 field survey names his father as Syed Ahmad Shah — the name this archive's entry for his brother, Baba Shah Kamal, gives independently — and adds that the family moved from Kashmir to Sialkot and from Sialkot to Lahore, where both brothers were born and where both are counted among the saints. It also accounts for his coming to Ichhra: he arrived to see Shah Kamal, whose shrine still stands close to his own, and pitched his camp a short distance from his brother's house. He belonged jointly to the Qadiriyya, Suhrawardiyya and Chishtiyya orders — a special disciple, tradition holds, of Sheikh Khokar Baig Suhrawardi — and lived in Lahore during the reign of the Mughal emperor Akbar, whose syncretic Din-i-Ilahi he is remembered for having resisted, working instead to draw people back towards orthodox Islamic practice. Devotees, the survey records, know him for his open-handedness as *Sakhi Sarwar* — a title that in the Punjab more usually belongs to Sultan Sakhi Sarwar of Dera Ghazi Khan, whose shrine this archive also holds; it is set down here as the survey gave it, and not as an identification of the two. Born, by the shrine's account, in 1587, he is generally held to have died in the later seventeenth century, though sources differ on the precise year: 1649 and 1671 both appear, the shrine's own tradition holding to 1671 and commemorating his death on the 4th of Rabi-ul-Sani. A separate compendium of Pakistani Sufi saints, Alam Faqri's *Tazkirah Awliya-e-Pakistan*, independently places his shrine on Achhra Road facing west, in Shah Jamal Colony, Lahore — consistent with this entry's own location — and traces his Suhrawardi lineage specifically to Bahauddin Zakariya of Multan.

The site itself, tradition says, was built and named "Damdama" by Shah Jamal in his own lifetime, and it was one of several stations of his austerities: besides his Damdama cell he kept chillas (forty-day retreats) at Sheikhupura, Shah Rehman, Shahdara Lahore and Chanpatpur in India. His death is remembered as inseparable from his devotions: emerging from a chilla, he died as the roof of his cell collapsed, and as people laboured to free him a voice from within is said to have commanded that he not be pulled out, but that his grave be raised over that very spot — which was done.

Legends gather thickly around the Damdama. The best known tells of a princess — Akbar's sister — who, annoyed that the tall tower overlooked her palace, demanded he lower it; the saint performed dhamal on its top floor, and storey after storey sank into the earth until only two remained, whereupon he predicted that her palace would not outlast his — fulfilled, it is said, when Sikh forces later destroyed it. Another tells of a cheating Hindu businessman to whom the saint gave a cantaloupe to feed his wife; twin sons followed, one of whom, named Sheikh Fakhr-ud-Din by Shah Jamal himself, became a devoted lifelong disciple — and the saint is also said to have saved that family by calling them outside moments before their roof collapsed. The survey adds that Sheikh Fakhr-ud-Din stayed in the saint's service all his life and that his grave lies inside the shrine.

Today the shrine is best known not for quiet contemplation but for its exuberant Thursday-night dhamal, when devotees, students, musicians and curious visitors gather from dusk until the early hours as dhol drummers — most famously the late Pappu Sain, whose successors continue the tradition — drive crowds into rhythmic, trance-like dancing, alongside qawwali, langar and the lighting of lamps. This weekly spectacle has made Shah Jamal one of the most written-about expressions of popular Sufi devotional culture in Punjab, and the crowds are considerable: on the order of 10,000 to 15,000 visitors a day, rising to 200,000-250,000 for the annual urs, held on the 3rd to the 5th of Rabi-ul-Sani with the ritual washing (gusal) of the tomb, the offering of the chadar, and drumming and dhamal.

The tomb itself is enclosed within a mosque of relatively modern construction, single-domed with four minarets, reflecting rebuilding rather than preserved Mughal fabric; an adjoining graveyard lies beside it. The shrine — of the Suhrawardi line and the Ahl-e-Sunnat tradition — has been in the care of the government's Auqaf Department since 1960; before that, the survey relates, its upkeep and adornment fell first to Shah Jamal himself, who oversaw the building in his own lifetime, and afterwards to the kings, maharajas and devotees who came after him. The current sajjada nashin, a descendant of the saint, continues to hold Khatam Khawajgan ceremonies. The site illustrates the continuing vitality of shrine-centred devotional practice in Lahore, and its shadows as well: the city's homeless rely on the shrine's langar and offerings, even as questions over noise, crowding and drug use in the vicinity — a real safety concern for pilgrims — have periodically drawn official scrutiny.
