## Overview

In the heart of old Lahore, a short way from Empress Road and the old Qila Gujjar Singh, stands the shrine of Bibi Pak Daman — “the pure, chaste ladies” — one of the oldest and most venerated sacred sites in the city, and among the busiest, drawing many thousands of pilgrims each day and immense crowds through the month of Muharram. Within its enclosures lie the graves of six holy women, remembered for their piety and for the miracle by which they are said to have chosen death over dishonour, together with the grave of their devoted maidservant. For centuries the darbar has been a place of prayer, vow, and refuge — cherished above all by women — and it stands, across sectarian lines, as one of the clearest expressions in South Asian Islam of the veneration of female sanctity. Its story is inseparable from a question that has been debated for more than a thousand years, and to which we shall return: who, exactly, were the Bibis?

## The Saints: Who the Bibis Were

Two great traditions about the identity of the six women endure side by side, and the shrine holds meaning in both. The older and by far the more popular tradition — the one held by most pilgrims, and reflected in the shrine’s traditional founding in the first century of Islam — identifies the chief of the six as Sayyida Ruqayya bint Ali, a daughter of Hazrat Ali and sister of Hazrat Abbas the standard-bearer, and the wife of Muslim ibn Aqeel. With her, this tradition holds, were kinswomen and companions of the House of the Prophet (the Ahl al-Bayt) — among them women remembered as Bibi Hoor and Bibi Noor of the line of Abi Talib — six in all, together with a faithful camel-driver named Basheer. By this account the women were survivors of the tragedy of Karbala who made their way across the length of the Muslim world to Lahore, and the shrine is a memorial to the family of the Prophet. Because of this the darbar is a place of especially intense devotion for Shia Muslims and for all who love the Ahl al-Bayt, and its whole atmosphere turns toward the remembrance of Karbala.

A second tradition — advanced by the shrine’s own custodial family and by a line of scholars including Mufti Ghulam Sarwar (author of _Khazinat al-Asfiya_ and _Hadiqat al-Auliya_), Pir Ghulam Dastgir Nami, and Hafeez Ullah Manzar — holds that the six were the daughters of the great saint Sayyid Ahmad Tokhta Tirmidhi, “Murshid-e-Punjab,” and that they lived and died in Lahore in the early thirteenth century, at the time of the Mongol invasions. What the two traditions share is, in the end, greater than what divides them: reverence for six women of extraordinary holiness, the memory of their choice of death over dishonour, and the miracle by which the earth itself is said to have received them. Both, too, agree on the names by which the six are known — Bibi Haj, Bibi Taj, Bibi Noor, Bibi Hoor, Bibi Gauhar, and Bibi Shahbaz (or Shahnaz).

## The Journey from Karbala

In the popular telling, the story begins in the aftermath of Karbala in 61 AH. The women’s ordeal had begun even before that day: Sayyida Ruqayya was the wife of Muslim ibn Aqeel, the cousin and emissary whom Imam Hussain had sent ahead to Kufa, and who was betrayed and martyred there together with his young sons. After the martyrdom of Hussain himself and his companions on the plain of Karbala, the surviving members of the Prophet’s household were taken captive and, for a year, subjected to the cruelty of Yazid’s court — a captivity so harsh, the tradition holds, that the very sanctities of Islam were trampled. Hazrat Zain al-Abideen, the surviving Imam, is said to have charged Sayyida Ruqayya to depart with a few of the women, so that the faith and the lineage of the Prophet’s house might be carried to safety and continue.

She set out with her companions and, it is told, with the camel-driver Basheer — a man who had served in Yazid’s own palace and who, though he had once spoken harshly to the holy women, was bound to them by a hidden love, and who was trusted precisely because their destination was to be kept from strangers. They journeyed east across the length of the Muslim world until they reached the land of Hind. There, at Lahore, a Hindu raja — named in the local accounts as Mahabrand Bhatti — held sway, and scarcely a Muslim was to be found. As the women arrived, wonders are said to have followed: the idols of the temples toppled and their lamps went dark. The raja, disturbed, sent his son — remembered as Raja Kumar Bikrama Sahay — with soldiers to discover who these strangers were. When the prince came before the women he was overwhelmed by a radiant light and fell senseless; on recovering, he heard from their own lips the tale of Karbala, and was so moved that he embraced Islam, took a new name — remembered variously as Abdullah Shah Khaki or Shaikh Jamal — and remained among them as their servant. When his enraged father came with an army to seize the women, they lifted their hands in prayer, begging God to spare them exposure to strangers; the earth opened and drew them into itself, closing again and leaving only the edges of their veils above ground, over which their graves were later raised. From this — from their guarding of their honour to the last — comes the name by which they are known: Pak Daman, “the pure of hem.”

This is a tradition steeped in the devotion of Karbala, and on the frontispiece of one of its major histories stands the old Persian line of the lovers of the martyrs:

خوشا آنان کہ در خاک و خون غلطیدند

[“Blessed are those who rolled in dust and blood” — a verse of devotion to the martyrs of the House of the Prophet.]

## The Other Tradition: The Daughters of Sayyid Ahmad Tokhta Tirmidhi

The custodial and scholarly tradition tells a different, and no less devout, story. It holds that the six were the daughters of Sayyid Ahmad Tokhta Tirmidhi, a sayyid of the Junaidiya Sufi line whose spiritual descent is traced, through several generations, back to Imam Zain al-Abideen, and whose family had come from Tirmiz in Central Asia by way of Kech Makran to settle at last in Lahore. His curious title, “Tokhta,” is explained by a story of his early discipleship: when his own pir opened the door of his cell and found the young Sayyid Ahmad standing in patient attendance, he addressed him with the Turkish word for “standing” or “ever-present,” and the name stayed with him. He is said to have died around 602 AH — near the beginning of the reign of Sultan Qutb al-Din Aibak — and to be buried in the Lahore quarter still called Chilla Bibiyan, which takes its name from the spiritual retreats (chilla) of his daughters; his tomb is now enclosed within a property of a later custodial family. A Persian chronogram (tarikh) preserved in his memory hails him in verse:

سیّد احمد، شہِ برنا و پیر ٭ پیرِ ہادی، میرِ عالی جاہ

[“Sayyid Ahmad — king of the young and the old; a guiding master, a lord of lofty station.”]

His nobility, sanctity, and gift of miracles were remembered as hereditary in his line, and his descendants spread widely across the Punjab and beyond. Of his daughters, two — Bibi Haj and Bibi Taj — are described as having married, while the others lived as lifelong ascetics, “the Rabia of their age.” Through Bibi Haj, tradition traces a distinguished line of Suhrawardi saints, among them Sultan Hamiduddin Hakim, “Sultan al-Tarikeen,” who is said to have renounced a throne for the life of a faqir and to lie buried at Mubarak, near the old town of Tulamba. In this account it was during the Mongol onslaught — when the army of Chingez Khan, pursuing the fugitive Jalaluddin Khwarazm Shah, sacked Lahore and its people fought for two months before the city fell — that the women, alone in their hermitage outside the walls where their father had lived, prayed to be hidden from the invaders, and the earth enclosed them exactly as in the other telling. The two traditions thus meet in the same miracle even as they differ over the century and the names.

## The Long Debate

That the identity of the Bibis should be so disputed is itself part of the shrine’s history, and the arguments have been rehearsed by scholars for generations. Those who question the Karbala account marshal a formidable case. They observe that, according to the standard histories, the wife of Hazrat Ali who bore Abbas — Umm al-Banin — had four sons and no daughter at all, so that Ruqayya cannot have been her child; that the two women named Ruqayya bint Ali are traditionally commemorated elsewhere, one at a shrine in Damascus and another in Egypt, not in Lahore; and that none of the family of the Prophet who went with Imam Hussain is recorded to have abandoned him before the end — indeed, on the night of Ashura he is said to have released his companions from their oath, and all refused to leave. They point out, too, that in the first century of Islam Lahore lay wholly outside the Muslim world: Islam reached Sind only with the conquests of Muhammad bin Qasim around 93 AH, and Lahore itself came under Muslim rule only with Mahmud of Ghazni in the early eleventh century — so that no Muslim woman, still less a princess of the Prophet’s house, could plausibly have dwelt in Lahore in 61 AH. Finally they note that two of the six names — Gauhar and Shahbaz — are Persian, not Arabic, and are not to be found among the recorded daughters of Aqeel or of Ali.

Yet those who hold to the Karbala tradition answer that the histories are incomplete, that the pilgrims who have searched the shrines of the Arab lands report finding no grave of Ruqayya bint Ali there, and that the living devotion of centuries is itself a kind of testimony. It is striking that even Mufti Ghulam Sarwar — the very scholar who doubted that the women had come from Arabia after Karbala — did not doubt their holiness, writing that whatever their origin, the place is beyond question deeply blessed and its saints full of grace. This, in the end, is where the two traditions come to rest: divided over history, united in reverence.

## The Shrine Complex

The graves lie within enclosed courtyards in the heart of the old city. Tradition places the six Bibis in two adjoining walled enclosures — in the first, by the usual account, Bibi Haj, Bibi Taj, and Bibi Noor; in the second, Bibi Hoor, Bibi Gauhar, and Bibi Shahbaz — their tombs built up in lime and plaster. Counted among them is the grave of their maidservant, Bibi Halima, remembered affectionately as “Bibi Tanoori,” who is said to have been swallowed by the earth along with her mistresses. Within the complex, too, stands a domed masonry tomb of a later age — dated to the eleventh century of the Hijra (about 1600 CE) and belonging to Sayyid Jalaluddin Haider Bukhari, of the celebrated Bukhari sayyids of Uch — a sign of how holy men of later centuries sought to be buried in the women’s company. Around the shrine has gathered a wider sacred neighbourhood: the nearby quarter of Chilla Bibiyan holds the tomb of Sayyid Ahmad Tokhta Tirmidhi, and the devotional literature names other holy figures associated with the place, among them Hazrat Ali Rangrez and Hazrat Alif Shah. Endowments of land (waqf) were attached to the darbar by successive rulers, and its fabric has been rebuilt many times over the centuries; the most recent major renovation was carried out in 2024.

## Data Ganj Bakhsh and the Antiquity of the Shrine

One tradition above all is cited by devotees as proof of the shrine’s great antiquity. It is said that when Data Ganj Bakhsh — the eleventh-century saint Ali Hujwiri, author of _Kashf al-Mahjub_ and the patron saint of Lahore — first came to the city, he would go every Thursday night to the graves of the Bibis Pak Daman and sit in attentive reverence at a respectful distance, out of _adab_ (courtesy) before their sanctity. That so great a saint should honour the shrine in this way is taken to show that the Bibis’ graves were already ancient and holy in his day, and it binds the darbar to Data Darbar, the two shrines standing together at the head of Lahore’s sacred geography. A gentler tale from the same devotional tradition tells of an astrologer who boasted that he could read the hidden and the unseen; the elder Bibi had food prepared for the poor and for travellers and sent him a dish in which fine food lay concealed beneath a plain covering of dry khichri. When the astrologer scorned the humble rice, and then learned what lay beneath it, he was humbled — for a man who claimed knowledge of the unseen had not seen the feast under the khichri — and he became a disciple. Such stories express what the shrine has always meant to ordinary people: a place of hospitality, of humility, and of quiet wonder.

## Custodianship and the Sectarian Question

The shrine’s hereditary caretakers, the mujawirs, are traditionally held to descend from the convert prince — Abdullah Shah Khaki, or Shaikh Jamal — who is said to have built the first shrine over the women’s graves and to have served it for the rest of his life. In the modern era the darbar has been taken into the charge of the Punjab Auqaf Department, which administers it as a Sunni-managed shrine, and, given the scale of the crowds, government security and the Punjab Police are maintained on site at all times. The question of the Bibis’ identity carries a sectarian dimension that persists to this day: because the popular tradition makes the shrine a memorial of the Prophet’s martyred family, it is a focus of intense Shia devotion, while its formal administration and much of its custodial lineage are Sunni. Around this difference old disputes have gathered — over whether the graves are those of Sayyida Ruqayya and the Ahl al-Bayt or of a Punjabi saint’s daughters, and over who may claim the shrine — yet in practice Bibi Pak Daman is revered by Sunni and Shia alike, and the two devotions meet, without merging, in the same courtyards.

## Devotion and Events

Bibi Pak Daman is a living centre of devotion, alive around the clock. Pilgrims come to recite the Fatiha, to tie vows (mannat), and to seek the Bibis’ intercession (waseela) for healing, for children, and for relief from every kind of hardship; many recount cures received here after physicians had given up hope, and tell of the impossible made possible through the women’s grace. Women in particular find in the darbar a sanctuary — a place of comfort and of dignity — and it is above all a women’s shrine in the affection of the city. Every Thursday night the shrine fills for its largest weekly gathering, with langar (free food), qawwali, dhamal, and prayer, and a mosque attached to the shrine holds the five daily prayers. Its supreme season, however, is Muharram, when the sorrow and devotion of Karbala are remembered: enormous crowds converge on the darbar, and it is said that many walk barefoot in groups from their homes to reach it. Field surveys of the shrine record on the order of eight to twelve thousand visitors on an ordinary day, some two hundred and fifty thousand in a month, and — at the height of Muharram — numbers rising toward a million or more.

## A Shrine of Holy Women

In a sacred landscape crowded with the tombs of male saints — Data Ganj Bakhsh, Mian Mir, Madho Lal Hussain, and the rest — Bibi Pak Daman is something rare and precious: a great shrine whose sanctity is that of women, and whose devotion has always been bound up with the lives of women. It is to the Bibis that the mothers of Lahore have brought their deepest hopes and fears for generations — praying for children, for good marriages, for the safety of daughters, for healing, and for protection from harm — and it is women, above all, who fill its courtyards, tie their threads of vow at its screens, and find in it a refuge that the world outside does not always offer them. The very meaning of the shrine turns on a woman’s virtue: the six Bibis are honoured not for conquest or office or learning alone, but for a purity and courage so absolute that they are said to have chosen to be swallowed by the earth rather than be seen by strangers. In this the darbar gives to the women of the city a mirror of their own dignity — a place where female holiness stands at the very centre, and where the qualities most often asked of women, and most often unhonoured, are raised to the rank of the sacred. That a shrine of such scale and antiquity should be dedicated to women is, in the religious geography of the subcontinent, almost without parallel, and it is a large part of what makes Bibi Pak Daman beloved.

## Legacy

Whether one honours the six Bibis as women of the Prophet’s household who carried the memory of Karbala to the edge of the Muslim world, or as the daughters of a Punjabi saint who chose the earth over the gaze of strangers, the meaning of the shrine is the same: it stands for purity, for courage, and for a refusal to bow before injustice at the cost of one’s honour — and it has stood for these, in the devotion of Lahore, across more than a thousand years of retelling. Amid the debates of scholars and the changing life of the city, the darbar of Bibi Pak Daman endures as it always has: a place of refuge and of blessing, one of the deepest spiritual anchors of the old city, and a rare shrine where the sanctity honoured is that of holy women.
