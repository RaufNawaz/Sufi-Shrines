## Overview

At Pakpattan, on the old bank of the Sutlej in southern Punjab, stands the shrine of Hazrat Baba Fariduddin Mas‘ud Ganjshakar (c. 1173–1266) — “Baba Farid,” one of the founding saints of the Chishti order in South Asia and among the most beloved holy figures of the Punjab. A disciple and spiritual heir of Khwaja Qutbuddin Bakhtiar Kaki of Delhi, and the master of the great Nizamuddin Auliya, Baba Farid made the town then called Ajodhan into a centre of Sufi devotion so important that it came to be renamed Pakpattan, “the Holy Ferry.” He was also a pioneer of Punjabi as a language of spiritual poetry, and verses attributed to him were later gathered into the Sikh scripture, the Guru Granth Sahib — a mark of how far his memory reaches across the communities of the Punjab. His shrine remains one of the greatest pilgrimage places of Pakistan, most of all at the annual celebration of the Bahishti Darwaza, the “Gate of Paradise.”

## The Life of the Saint

Farid was born around 1173 at Kothewal, near Multan, into a family of learned émigrés of Central Asian origin; his grandfather is said to have come from Kabul in the upheavals of the age. His mother, remembered as a woman of deep piety, first set him on the path of prayer, and he received a thorough religious education at Multan, where a chance meeting with Khwaja Qutbuddin Bakhtiar Kaki, the Chishti master of Delhi, changed his life. He followed Bakhtiar Kaki to Delhi, entered his discipleship, and gave himself to the severe austerities for which he became famous — long fasts and the arduous devotions of the Chishti way, including the _chilla-e-ma‘kus_, the forty-day “inverted” prayer suspended head-down in a well. It is from the sweetness that his long fasts are said to have miraculously produced — a pebble or a morsel turning to sugar in his mouth — that he earned the title _Ganjshakar_, “the Treasure of Sugar.”

On the death of his master he became the head (_qutb_) of the Chishti order and, seeking distance from the intrigues of the capital, settled at Ajodhan in the Punjab. There he lived in poverty and prayer, keeping an open _langar_ though his own family often went hungry, teaching, and drawing to himself disciples who would carry the Chishti way across India — above all Nizamuddin Auliya of Delhi, through whom Farid’s spiritual line became the greatest in the subcontinent. He died at Ajodhan in 1266.

## Teaching and Poetry

Baba Farid’s teaching was the pure Chishti gospel of love: of God to be sought through humility, poverty (_faqr_), patient endurance, and above all a boundless compassion that returned kindness for cruelty. He is remembered for counselling his disciples to answer harshness with gentleness — to give those who bring thorns the softest of replies. What sets him apart in the history of the Punjab is that he expressed this vision not only in the Persian of the learned but in the language of the common people, and he is honoured as one of the earliest and greatest poets of Punjabi. His _shlokas_ (couplets) have such authority that the Sikh Gurus included a body of them in the Guru Granth Sahib. Their tone is unmistakable — the ache of the soul for God, and the memory of death that makes the world small:

کاگا سب تن کھائیو، چُن چُن کھائیو ماس
دوے نیناں مت کھائیو، مینوں پِیا ملن دی آس

[“O crow, eat all my flesh, pick and eat it piece by piece — but do not eat these two eyes, for I still long to see my Beloved.”]

فریدا جے تُوں عقل لطیف ہیں، کالے لِکھ نہ لیکھ
آپنڑے گریوان مہ سِر نیواں کر ویکھ

[“Farid, if you are of subtle wit, write not against another’s account; bow your head into your own collar, and look there instead.”]

## The Shrine and the Bahishti Darwaza

Baba Farid’s tomb at Pakpattan grew, over the centuries, into a great shrine that has been honoured by every power to rule the Punjab — sultans, Mughals, and later rulers alike — and it remains among the most visited darbars in Pakistan. Its custodianship passed to a line of hereditary keepers, the Diwans of Pakpattan, descended from the saint’s family. The shrine’s most famous feature is the _Bahishti Darwaza_, the “Gate of Paradise” — a silver-plated door that is opened only once a year, during the saint’s annual urs in the Islamic month of Muharram; pilgrims believe that to pass through it is to be cleansed of sin, and for the days that it stands open a vast multitude presses through it in one of the largest gatherings of the Punjabi year. Qawwali, the free kitchen (_langar_), and the recitation of Farid’s verse fill the shrine, above all through the urs.

## Devotion and Legacy

For the people of the Punjab, Baba Farid is not a remote historical figure but a living presence — “Farid” is a household name, his sayings are proverbs, and his verses are sung by qawwals and folk-singers alike. Through his disciple Nizamuddin Auliya and the generations that followed, the Chishti order he helped root in India became the most widespread of all the Sufi paths of the subcontinent; through his Punjabi poetry he became a father of the language’s literature and a shared inheritance of Muslims and Sikhs; and through his shrine at Pakpattan he gave the Punjab one of its enduring centres of pilgrimage. Compassion, poverty, and the sweetness that gave him his name remain the marks of his memory.
