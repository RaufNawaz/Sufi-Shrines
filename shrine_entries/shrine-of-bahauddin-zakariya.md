## Overview

In the old city of Multan — the “City of Saints” — stands the shrine of Hazrat Baha-ud-Din Zakariya (c. 1170–1267), the saint who planted the Suhrawardi Sufi order in the soil of the subcontinent and made Multan one of the first great centres of Islamic mysticism in South Asia. A contemporary and friend of Baba Farid of Pakpattan, and like him a shaper of the spiritual map of the Punjab, Bahauddin Zakariya founded a khanqah at Multan whose influence reached across the region for centuries. His tomb, raised in the thirteenth century, is among the oldest and most revered Muslim shrines in Pakistan.

## The Life of the Saint

Baha-ud-Din Zakariya was born around 1170 near Multan, into a Qureshi family of Arab descent that had settled in the region. He pursued religious learning far afield — at Khurasan, at Bukhara, and in the Hejaz, where he is said to have studied at Medina — before travelling to Baghdad to enter the discipleship of Shaikh Shihab-ud-Din Abu Hafs Umar al-Suhrawardi, the great systematiser of the Suhrawardi order and author of the _‘Awarif al-Ma‘arif_. Under him Zakariya completed his training with remarkable speed and received the mantle of _khilafat_, with authorisation to carry the order to India. Returning to Multan, he established his khanqah and became the foremost Suhrawardi shaykh of the subcontinent.

Unlike the Chishtis, who embraced absolute poverty and kept their distance from the courts, Zakariya represented the Suhrawardi temper: he accepted endowments and gifts, managed considerable resources, engaged openly with rulers, and used his wealth in charity and hospitality on a grand scale. This difference of method — the two poles of early Indian Sufism — is often illustrated by his cordial contrast with his friend Baba Farid. He died at Multan in 1267, full of years and honour.

## The Suhrawardi Order in India

Bahauddin Zakariya’s lasting achievement was to root the Suhrawardi way in the Indian subcontinent. From his Multan khanqah the order spread through Sind and the Punjab, carried by his disciples and descendants — among them the poet-saint Fakhr-ud-Din Iraqi, who came to Multan and married into his family, and, in the next generations, his own grandson Shah Rukn-e-Alam. The Suhrawardi order he founded was marked by strict adherence to the Sharia, sober devotion, learning, and an active engagement with society and state — a pattern that made Multan a hub of both spiritual authority and worldly influence, and that shaped the religious life of the western Punjab and Sind for centuries.

## The Shrine

Zakariya’s mausoleum in the old fort area of Multan, built in the thirteenth century, is one of the earliest monuments of Islamic architecture in the subcontinent — a square tomb chamber surmounted by a hemispherical dome, much rebuilt after damage over the centuries (it was harmed, among other times, in the siege of Multan in 1848). It remains a working shrine and the seat of his descendants, the Qureshi _makhdooms_ of Multan, who have been among the most prominent religious and political families of the region. Pilgrims come throughout the year to recite the Fatiha, seek blessing, and honour the saint, and the shrine keeps the round of daily prayer, qawwali, and the free kitchen characteristic of the great darbars, rising to its height at the annual urs.

## Devotion and Legacy

Bahauddin Zakariya stands beside Baba Farid as one of the two founding fathers of Punjabi Sufism — the Suhrawardi to Farid’s Chishti — and his khanqah made Multan a wellspring of the tradition. Through his descendants and disciples, and through the later saints of Multan who trace to his line, his influence runs through the whole later history of Sufism in the western subcontinent. His shrine anchors Multan’s ancient identity as the “City of Saints,” and his memory endures as that of the master who first gave the Suhrawardi order an Indian home.

### Doubts the notes raise on this row

**B2. `shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya` — the row's date is the one the editor rejects.** Text and
chronogram give **666 AH** (≈ 1267 CE, the row's current figure); the editor rules for **661 AH**
(≈ 1262–63 CE). The birth is **578 AH** in text and chronogram against **566 AH / 1170 CE** in the
editor's footnote, with a birthplace (**Kot Kahror**) that appears only in the footnote. The editor's
pair gives a life of 95 lunar years and the text's 88; neither is stated as an age anywhere
(pp. 159–161, folios 147–149; chunk 015). Separately, the author's claim of descent from Baha al-Din
Zakariya in seventeen steps carries **no source and no footnote**, from an editor who corrected this
same family's pedigree twice elsewhere — cite it as a family claim (p. 232, folio 220; chunk 022).
