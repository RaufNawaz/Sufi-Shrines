## Overview

Sharada Peeth is the ruined but hallowed remnant of what was once among the most illustrious temples and centres of learning in the Indian subcontinent, standing beside the Neelum River in the village of Sharda, in the Neelum Valley of Azad Kashmir. Set at nearly 2,000 metres in the shadow of Mount Harmukh, revered by Kashmiri Pandits as an abode of Shiva, the site was dedicated to the goddess Sharada, the Kashmiri form of *Saraswati*, deity of learning, and gave her name both to the celebrated *Sharada* script and to Kashmir itself, long known as *Sharada Desh*, the "Land of Sharada". Simultaneously a temple, a fabled university, and one of the great *Shakti Peethas*, Sharada Peeth occupies a singular place in the cultural and religious memory of Kashmir.

## The Shakti Peetha and the Goddess of Learning

Sharada Peeth is counted among the *Shakti Peethas*, and by Kashmiri tradition it is enumerated among the eighteen *Maha Shakti Peethas*. According to the legend common to all these seats, the shrine marks a place where a part of the body of the goddess Sati fell to earth after Vishnu dismembered her corpse with his discus to end Shiva's grief-stricken dance; at Sharada, tradition holds, it was the goddess's right hand that descended, and here she is worshipped in her form as Sharada. The site's association with Saraswati fused devotion and scholarship in a manner rare among the Peethas, so that the goddess was venerated as the very source of knowledge, letters and wisdom. Its renown was such that the great teacher Adi Shankara, in his hymnic enumeration of the eighteen seats of the Goddess, named Sharada in Kashmir alongside Kamakshi at Kanchipuram and Vishalakshi at Varanasi.

## The Temple University and the Sharada Script

Between roughly the sixth and twelfth centuries CE, Sharada Peeth flourished as one of the foremost temple universities of the subcontinent, a rival in reputation to the great monastic seats of learning elsewhere in India. Tradition and later accounts describe a renowned library that drew scholars from great distances, and the site is associated in memory with luminaries such as the philosopher Adi Shankara, the historian Kalhana, author of the *Rajatarangini*, and the scholar Vairotsana. It was, above all, a pre-eminent centre of Sanskrit scholarship, where numerous important manuscripts were composed and copied.

The most enduring legacy of this learning is the *Sharada* script itself, which developed and spread from this centre and was named in the shrine's honour. For centuries the *Sharada* script was the principal medium for writing Sanskrit and Kashmiri in the region, and it remains sacred to Kashmiri Pandits, used in ritual and in the writing of horoscopes. So intimately was the goddess bound to the intellectual life of Kashmir that to be admitted to the shrine's precincts came to symbolise scholarly attainment.

## Decline, Partition and the Present

Sharada Peeth's decline unfolded over many centuries, its academic pre-eminence fading after the twelfth century amid the political upheavals that swept Kashmir. Earthquakes, neglect and the passage of time reduced the once-great complex to the massive stone shell that survives today, its walls of large ashlar blocks standing roofless within a walled enclosure. The Partition of 1947 and the ensuing division of Kashmir placed the shrine within Pakistan-administered territory, close to the Line of Control, and effectively cut it off from the Kashmiri Pandit community for whom it remained a spiritual lodestar.

In recent years the site has become a focus of renewed attention and appeals for conservation. Kashmiri Pandit organisations have campaigned for access and preservation, and there have been proposals for a pilgrimage corridor comparable to that opened at Kartarpur; on the Indian side of the Line of Control, a new Sharada temple was consecrated in Kupwara in 2023, seeking to rekindle the devotional link to the ancient seat.

## Legacy

Sharada Peeth endures as one of the most poignant and evocative sacred sites of the subcontinent, a ruin that once embodied the highest achievements of Kashmiri learning and piety. As the namesake of a script, a goddess and a land, and as a *Shakti Peetha* revered across generations, it remains a powerful emblem of Kashmir's ancient identity as the "Land of Sharada", even as its weathered stones await the care their history so richly merits.
