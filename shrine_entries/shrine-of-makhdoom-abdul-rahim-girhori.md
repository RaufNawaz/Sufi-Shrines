## Current site description (as of 10 October 2026)

*The row's `Description` cell exactly as it appears in `src/data/shrines-fallback.json` on 10 October 2026, untouched. Not yet checked against sources.*

The shrine of Makhdoom Abdul Rahim Girhori stands at Girhor Sharif, a village some eight kilometres west of Pithoro in Umarkot district, in the semi-desert borderlands of eastern Sindh. It honours a leading Naqshbandi mystic and scholar of eighteenth-century Sindh (1739–1778) who belonged to the Mangrio tribe and came to be known as *Girhori* after he settled at Girhor and established his *khanqah* there. He was among the foremost *khalifas* (deputies) of Khwaja Muhammad Zaman of Luari Sharif (also recorded in this dataset), the celebrated *Sultan al-Aulia* of the Sindhi Naqshbandis, under whom he was trained. A learned man, Abdul Rahim Girhori is remembered for his religious scholarship and ascetic life and is said to have composed works in Sindhi, Arabic and Persian. He is venerated as a *shaheed* (martyr): according to traditional accounts he was killed late in his life in a confrontation, and his tomb is honoured as that of a martyr-saint. Today the shrine complex is a place of pilgrimage that local sources note is frequented by both Muslims and Hindus, and an annual *urs* commemorates him. His memory forms part of the wider Naqshbandi devotional network that the disciples of Khwaja Muhammad Zaman spread across Sindh in the eighteenth century. Kalhoro's account further names his principal Sindhi, Arabic and Persian compositions — *Sharh Abayat Sindhi*, *Fath-al-Fazal*, *Risala Gul Numa* and *Kalam Girhori* among them — and gives a fuller version of the saint's death: tradition holds that he fell in battle against a rival Hindu ascetic, whom he killed along with destroying the ascetic's *marhi* (shrine), but was himself mortally wounded in the same encounter, an episode remembered as the origin of his shaheed status. The shrine's continuing local importance in more recent times is attested by a 2015 Dawn report naming Syed Asif Ali Shah as the custodian of the "Shaheed Abdul Raheem Girhori shrine" at the time of an armed attack on him that stirred sectarian unrest in the area.

- (book's word: "Sind"; refers to: "between the seventh and eleventh centuries", Hsuan Tsang in the seventh century, the Chach-nama in the eighth; book's historical prose, citing Irfan Habib): the Jat pastoralists "had been moving up from Sind into the Multan area between the seventh and eleventh centuries"; Hsuan Tsang "wrote of river groups in Sind who 'gave themselves exclusively to tending cattle and from this derive their livelihood,' 'have no masters,'" with "'an unfeeling temper'" and "'a hasty disposition'"; the Chach-nama "styled these groups 'Jatts', located them in the wastes of the Indus valley in Sind, and noted an absence among them of social hierarchy" (p. 224, folio 212); the essay sums this up as the Jats' "migration north from Sind to Punjab" (p. 227, folio 215).
- (book's word: "Sind"; refers to: "the eighth century"; book's historical prose, citing Y. Friedmann): "there is evidence that in the eighth century, when Arabs replaced Brahmans as rulers of Sind, the new rulers merely continued the earlier practice of requiring the Jats to associate themselves with dogs—unclean to Muslims as well as to Hindus—and in this way to affirm their lowly status" (p. 224, folio 212).
