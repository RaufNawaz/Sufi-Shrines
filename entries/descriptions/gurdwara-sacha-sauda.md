## Overview

Gurdwara Sacha Sauda, at Farooqabad — the town formerly called *Chuharkana* — in the Sheikhupura district of the Punjab, commemorates one of the best-loved stories of Guru Nanak's youth: the episode of the *sacha sauda*, the "true bargain." It is a tale that Sikhs recount to children and elders alike as the very seed of the faith's social teaching, and the shrine that marks its setting is a cherished stop on the pilgrim road through Pakistani Punjab.

Of the books consulted, one names the gurdwara itself. Haroon Khalid, travelling with the historian Iqbal Qaiser, writes that "At a little distance from the main road, was the Gurdwara Sacha Sauda. It was a splendid structure, recently renovated" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 71, folio 53), in the town he calls Farooqabad, historically "Chuhrkhana" or "Chuharkhana" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 63, 70–71, folios 45, 52–53). In his earlier *A White Trail* Khalid places a gurdwara "in Chuharkhana at the spot where the Guru fed the starving jogis", without giving it a name; there "Sacha Sauda" is the name of the deal, "a true deal", not of the building (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 182, folio not stated). Harish Dhillon's retelling of the Janamsakhis sets the story in the woods near "the nearby market town of Chuharkhana" and names no gurdwara at all (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 25, folio 29). The other books speak only of Guru Nanak, not of this place; Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi's account of the Guru names neither Farooqabad nor Chuharkana, and the one commercial notice in it, that "under pressure from his family he later became a trader and farmer", is attached to no place (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 408, folio 384).

## The Story of the True Bargain

According to the traditional accounts, the young Nanak — by the usual reckoning about eighteen years of age — was entrusted by his father, *Mehta Kalu*, with a sum of money, remembered as twenty rupees, and sent to a nearby town to strike a profitable deal, that he might learn the trade of a merchant and settle to a worldly living. On the road, accompanied by his lifelong companion *Bhai Mardana*, Nanak came upon a company of holy men — by some tellings hungry ascetics, by others the poor and the destitute — who had gone without food. Moved by their need, he spent the whole of his father's money on provisions to feed and clothe them, holding that no trade could be more honest or more profitable than the relief of the hungry.

When he returned empty-handed and was reproached by his father for squandering the money, Nanak answered that he had made the truest bargain of all — a *sacha sauda* — for he had spent it in the service of God's creatures. In the devotion of the Panth the episode is read as the first flowering of the principle that would become central to Sikhism: *langar*, the free community kitchen where all are fed without distinction of caste or creed, and *seva*, selfless service as an act of worship.

Khalid gives the story in brief in *A White Trail*: "According to the legend, when Baba Guru Nanak turned eighteen, his father asked him to take up a trade. He gave him twenty rupees", and Nanak left "the city (present day Nankana Sahib)" with Bhai Mardana (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 182, folio not stated). "Outside a place called Mandi Chuharkhana" he met starving *jogis* and bought them food with the money: "a Sacha Sauda, a true deal" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 182, folio not stated). Elsewhere in the book he refers to "the incident that came to be known as Sacha Sauda" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222).

In *Walking with Nanak* a footnote states the episode plainly: "This incident is known as Sacha Sauda (honest trade) and is one of the most important events from Nanak's early life" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 66, folio 48). The same book retells it at length in a reconstruction of Nanak's youth, a part of the book Khalid himself calls fictionalised (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 17, folio not stated). In it Mehta Kalu gives the "eighteen-year-old Nanak" "the hefty amount of twenty rupees" to visit "the market town of Chuharkhana about twelve kos ( forty-five kilometres) from here" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 63, folio 45). In "a jungle on the way" Nanak meets naked ascetics with matted hair: "Their leader was a wise man called Santrain", and the group "refers to itself as Nirbani" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 65, folio not stated). The scene also speaks of "the naked ascetics in the jungle of Chuhrkhana" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 61, folio 43), who "did not beg for food" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 62, folio 44). Santrain refuses the money as "against his religion", and Nanak tells it so: "when I reached Chuharkha [OCR?] [Chuharkhana] ... I bought food for the ascetics ... That was my honest trade". Mehta Kalu slaps him, and Rai Bular offers to adopt him (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 66, folio 48).

## The Bargain in Dhillon's Retelling

In Dhillon's retelling of the Janamsakhis the episode has a chapter of its own, "Sacha Sauda", which he glosses as "The true bargain." Mehta Kalu gives Nanak "twenty silver coins" and a list of goods to buy "from the nearby market town of Chuharkhana at wholesale prices" and sell in Talwandi (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 25, folio 29). Nanak and his companion Bala take "the path through the woods" instead of "the thoroughfare to Chuharkhana", and meet "dozens of sadhus in the forest", the Nagas, who have not been fed "in a week" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 26–32, folios 30–32).

In Chuharkhana Nanak buys from "the biggest wholesale grocer" "enough food to feed fifty starving sadhus", with vegetables, fruit and firewood, hires two cooks and two porters, and feeds the sadhus at "the grove of trees" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 33–35, folios 37–39). "Any dilution of my bargain will be a dilution of the truth", he says (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 34, folio 38), and feeding them is "a truly selfless service" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 35, folio 39).

At home Mehta Kalu confronts him and calls him a failure (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 36–37, folios 40–41). Rai Bular defends him: he "used the money to feed a whole akhara of famished sadhus", a bargain "true in the eyes of God" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 38, folio 42; p. 39, folio 43). Later in the chapter Pandit Hardyal speaks of Nanak as a sixteen-year-old (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 40, folio 44).

## The Shrine

Long afterward, Sikhs raised a gurdwara at the village associated with the story, near Chuharkana, and it grew into a popular place of pilgrimage drawing devotees from across the Punjab and beyond. With the Partition of 1947 the shrine passed into Pakistan and its congregation departed, but it was not forgotten. The building was renovated in 1993–94 with the support of the Sikh *sangat* (congregation) of the United Kingdom, and it has since been opened, under arrangement with the Government of Pakistan and the *Evacuee Trust Property Board*, to organised parties of Sikh pilgrims, especially on the great anniversaries of Guru Nanak's life.

Iqbal Qaiser, standing in the courtyard with Khalid, said of it: "This gurdwara was constructed to commemorate the event" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 81, folio 63). Khalid's *A White Trail* calls it "The second commemorating Gurdwara" of the story, set "at the spot where the Guru fed the starving jogis" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 182, folio not stated). Neither book gives the year it was built.

Khalid saw the gurdwara on a visit with Qaiser in "the monsoon season", during "Ramzan" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 70, folio 52). It stood within "a protective wall covered with barbed wire on top" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 71, folio 53). Through a portal lay a courtyard, "at the cent[re] of which stood a white gurdwara"; beside it were "a few waan trees enclosed by a small boundary", and gurdwara, courtyard and trees were "situated on an ancient mound, which was the highest point in and around the city" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 80, folio 62). The trees stand "in the middle of a marbled courtyard", "the last remnants of the jungle that used to exist here" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 81, folio 63). "It was under these trees that Nanak discoursed with the leader of the yogis", Qaiser told him (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 80, folio 62).

Inside was "a wooden palanquin in which the Guru Granth Sahib was placed", with "a couple of swords" before it (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 81, folio 63). Behind the gurdwara was a "new sukhasan", carpeted, its walls "done up in wood", with "a split air-conditioner", switched off "as there was no electricity", and a carved wooden bed. Asked whether the bed had been made in Amritsar, the caretaker Boota Singh answered, "It was made in Nankana Sahib" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 81, folio 63).

Qaiser remembered the place in a worse state. "I saw this gurdwara for the first time in 1993 when I was researching my book", he said; he had come with Ilyas Ghuman, and "Buffaloes were tied in the courtyard while drug addicts and gamblers had occupied the rest of the space" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 79, folio 61). A local man "referred to as Shah" — "I don't remember his real name" — tried to keep the place sacred and was beaten by the addicts and gamblers; Qaiser and Ghuman took him to hospital and filed a police report, and the matter reached "a few national newspapers". The Department of Auqaf (the printed text reads "Augaf") then came and "appointed him the official guard of this place", and "In subsequent years, the gurdwara was renovated" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 79, folio 61). Asked whether he had "played a major part in the renovation of this gurdwara, like in Shaheed Ganj", Qaiser answered, "Yes." (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 79, folio 61).

The renovation brought a road. After Qaiser's book, Khalid writes, "a debate raged about the renovation of a number of gurdwaras, one of which was the Gurdwara Sacha Sauda. Once the gurdwara was constructed, this new road also had to be made to connect it to Nankana Sahib" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 85, folio 67). The road, "constructed to facilitate Sikh pilgrims", takes "only about twenty minutes"; before it, pilgrims "had to travel to Sheikupura and then head towards Chuhrkhana" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 85, folio 67).

## Devotion and Practice

Khalid calls the gurdwara "one of the major shrines that is visited by pilgrim tourists who come to Nankana Sahib and Hassan Abdal" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 79, folio 61). He did not find it easy to enter. At the gate were "two security men sitting inside the gate while a policeman sat next to them with his gun resting on his shoulder"; Qaiser called them "new boys" who did not know him (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 79, folio 61), and a boy at the gate would not let them in until the guard had telephoned Boota Singh (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 80, folio 62).

"Boota Singh was the name of the sewadg [OCR?] [sewadar] … appointed here by the Augaf Departmen [OCR?] [Auqaf Department]". He was "about forty years old", "wearing a green shalwar kameez with a green turban", came with his youngest son, "about ten years old", and was "extremely shy" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 80, folio 62). "I have been working here for the past twelve years", he said (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 83, folio 65). Qaiser had "always come here with a jatha (a group of Sikhs)", and Boota Singh showed them round (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 80, folio 62). On the same page Khalid records a Boota Singh of Nena Kot saying "We are not from the Tribal Areas. We are Punjabis", and calls him "the first Pakistani Punjabi Sikh I had interacted with. Earlier, I had only encountered Pathan Sikhs who had moved to Punjab in the past few years" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 83, folio 65).

Before the Guru Granth Sahib, Qaiser "took out a ten rupee note and placed it there", walked round the palanquin and fanned the scripture with a "flying-whisk", and Khalid did the same (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 81, folio 63). Leaving the *sukhasan*, Boota Singh walked out backwards so that his back never faced the Guru Granth Sahib, and the visitors did likewise (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 82, folio 64). Khalid, for his part, told Boota Singh that "Sacha Sauda is already prominent" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 83, folio 65).

The episode the gurdwara remembers is the one to which Khalid traces the communal kitchen: "It is believed that the tradition of Langar began with Guru Nanak, when he fed the poor jogis at Mandi Chuharkhana" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 188, folio not stated). Bhandari's Persian *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, writing of Baba Nanak's followers in general and not of this place, says that they count the service of those who come and go as the greatest worship, and that whoever arrives at midnight speaking Baba Nanak's name is treated as a brother and a friend and served, even a stranger, a thief or a highwayman (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 519).

## Legends and Miracles

"According to legend", Khalid writes, yogis "were hanging from the trees while there were others who were sitting within an empty trunk when Nanak passed through"; the printed text of the passage is partly damaged (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 81, folio 63).

A Muslim storyteller at Gurdwara Sachkand, a different building in the same town, told Khalid that the yogis Nanak fed were meditating "on top of a mound. The mound is where the big gurdwara stands" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 74, folio 56). The same storyteller called Nanak "a true devotee of God. He was a rightly guided soul", adding: "Our elders told us. They used to live with the Sikhs before Pakistan was made" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 75, folio 57).

Another of Nanak's legends, which Khalid gives in a footnote in the same chapter, is also a story of trade. A trader told Nanak that his mules carried "Just some mud"; his "sugar and gur (jaggery) had turned into mud", and when he repented "his mud became Sugar and gur once again" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 71, folio 53). The footnote, as given here, does not tie the story to the town.

## Setting: Farooqabad

Driving into the town, Qaiser gave Khalid its new name, Farooqabad, and its old one, "Chuhrkhana". "This is still a mandi", he said, and he put it "About forty-five kilometres, I think" from Nankana Sahib "in a straight line" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 70, folio 52). *A White Trail* gives "Mandi Chuharkhana in district Sheikhupura, about forty-six kilometres from here", that is, from Nankana Sahib (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 182, folio not stated).

The new name, as Qaiser tells it, comes from the 1980s. In "the Zia era" a resident, "Farooq Dogar", was elected to the National Assembly "when we had non-party based elections in the eighties"; he supported the military dictator, was "killed over a personal feud", and the town was renamed from Chuhrkhana to Farooqabad in his honour (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 71, folio 53). In the same passage "Chuhr" is explained as the two bangles worn at the start and end of a set of regular bangles, "a Sanskrit word which means to lead" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 71, folio 53).

Writing of the province and not of this town, Khalid records that "When Maharaja Ranjit Singh became the sovereign of Punjab at the end of the eighteenth century he spent large amounts of money on the renovation and expansion of many gurdwaras", and "attached vast tracts of land to these gurdwaras, so that the revenues from the lands could facilitate their functioning" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 34, folio 16). Of the years after Partition he writes: "I have seen several gurdwaras and temples being taken over and destroyed by the migrants" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated). In *A White Trail* he notes that Gurdwaras that were built later by devotees at the places where Nanak stayed or performed miracles "still stand tall" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 168–169, folio not stated).

## Guru Nanak in the Wider Record

What the books say of Guru Nanak himself is said of the Guru, not of this site; it is gathered here because the gurdwara exists for his sake. Khalid gives his dates as 1469–1539, calling him "the founder of the Sikh religion" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 45, folio not stated), and Dhillon calls the Janamsakhis "cycles of tales purportedly drawn from the life of the first Guru of the Sikhs" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 9, folio 9). Dhillon has him born "On 14 April 1469" to Kalyan Chand Das Bedi, "or Mehta Kalu as he was affectionately called", accountant to the Bhatti family under Rai Bular (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 17–18, folios 19–20), and gives the date of his death as 22 September 1539 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 259, folio 287). Bhandari reports, as something that is said, a birth in the year 1526 of Bikramajit, corresponding to 880 of the Hijra, in the time of Sultan Bahlul Lodi, and a death in the reign of Salim Shah Afghan, at an age between seventy and eighty (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).

The years around the bargain appear in both retellings. In Dhillon's, Nanak refused the sacred thread at thirteen, "the beginning of a lifelong crusade against the performing of empty rites and rituals" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 19–21, folios 21–23); at sixteen he herded cattle (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 23, folio 27) and "became well acquainted with the dervish Sayyad Hussain and gained an intimate knowledge of the Sufi line of thought" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 24, folio 28). In Khalid's reconstruction, Mehta Kalu calls himself "an accountant" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 63, folio 45), Nanak herds buffaloes, and his father and "his employer and friend, Rai Bular" find him hiding in a grove of trees outside the town (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 64, folio 46); his mentor is "Sayyed Hassan, a Muslim Sufi, who lived near his house" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 62, folio 44). After the bargain, in Dhillon's telling, "Nanak moved to Sultanpur (around 60 km from Talwandi)" as keeper of Nawab Daulat Khan's stores (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 44, folio 49).

Bhandari's Persian notes that the signs of his gift showed early, signs of unveiling, of miracles and of wonders beyond the ordinary appearing from him in his childhood (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520), and that a minstrel named Mardana sang the Guru's verses and drew people into the snare of devotion (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).

Dhillon writes that Nanak "brought a message of peace and harmony to believers of the mutually hostile religions of Islam and Hinduism", that he taught that "all men and women were equal, irrespective of caste, creed, wealth, social standing and gender", and that a good life rested on honest labour, compassion, selfless service and prayer (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 12–13, folio not stated / 13). He also writes of "Guru Nanak's clear and emphatic disavowal of the miraculous" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 11, folio 11). Khalid writes that Nanak "abhorred the concept of associating miracles with religious personalities", yet "his biography is nothing but a story of his miracles" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 16, folio not stated), and that he associated with "people of lowercastes, terming them people of God" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 82, folio 64).

Dhillon sets out two schemes of the *udasis*, the Guru's journeys. "According to the Puratan Janamsakhi… he undertook five journeys", the last in 1523–1524 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 54–55, folios 60–61); "More modern accounts" give four, spanning twenty-three years (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 55, folio 61), and Dhillon himself holds that there were four (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 56, folio 62). Khalid gives the travels as about twenty-four years, mostly on foot (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 54, folio 36), and writes that "Whenever he would visit an area he would visit its religious shrines, Muslim, Hindu and of any other denomination" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 37, folio 19).

Muslim writers and singers have claimed the Guru too. Khalid writes that "many Muslims, acquainted with the teachings of Guru Nanak, are devoted to him too and visit his shrine" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 220, folio not stated), and that "Guru Nanak was a spiritual follower of Baba Fareed", who travelled to Pakpattan and "collected the poetry of the saint" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 188, folio not stated); in *In Search of Shiva* he writes that Baba Farid "became an inspiration for Guru Nanak when he visited the shrine during his travels" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated). Shemeem Burney Abbas writes that Baba Farid's "texts are revered among the Sikhs because he influenced the founder of their own religion, Baba Guru Nanak" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 87, folio not stated). Robert Rozehnal reports that a twentieth-century Chishti Sabiri shaykh, in certain lectures, describes "non-Muslim spiritual luminaries—Krishna, Rama, Sita, Lachman, and Guru Nanak—as believers in the one true God and religious leaders with 'Divine connection' (nisbat)" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 63, folio 53). Omar Kasmani, citing Michel Boivin, notes that the trope of the flower in milk is told also of "Guru Nanak, the spiritual leader and founder of Sikhism" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 195, folio 178, ch. 5 n. 17). At the shrine of the goddess Hinglaj, Jürgen Schaflechner records "the engraved footsteps of Guru Nanak" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 256, folio not stated).

## In Verse

None of the verse the books quote is tied to Farooqabad; it is given here as the poetry of the Guru the gurdwara remembers. Dhillon notes that Nanak's *Bani* "was compiled as part of the Adi Granth by Guru Arjan Dev, the fifth Guru, in 1604" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 9, folio 9). Bhandari says that the Guru composed Hindi verses on the truth of God, setting out divine unity in clear expressions and shining metaphors (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520), and that the worship of his followers centres on reading their master's verses, which they recite in song and melody (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 519). Abbas's glossary defines the *banī* by him: "Devotional verses such as those of Guru Nanak; verses of supplication, entreaty, humility, and reverence" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 179, folio not stated).

Khalid quotes the Guru's verse throughout *Walking with Nanak*, in published English translations. Among them is a poem he quotes under the opening 'O my merchant friend' (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 56–58, folio 40). The *aarti*, sung in praise of a God whom Qaiser reads as residing in nature, begins in one translation:

*The sky the salver, the sun and moon the lamps …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 55, folio 37)

and in another:

*In the salver of the firmament, / The sun and the moon shine as lamps* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 243–244, folios 217–218)

On the oneness of God:

*My Master is One / He is One, brother, the only One* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 60, folio 42)

*God is One, / The Path is One* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 144, folio 126)

The poem on the sacred thread, the *janeu*, which in Khalid's reconstruction the boy Nanak refused:

*From the cotton of compassion, / Spin the thread of contentment* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 61–62, folios 43–44)

On washing:

*As hands or feet besmirched with slime, / Water washes white* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 192, folio 166)

Other openings Khalid quotes: *As a beggar goes a-begging …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 158, folio 140); *Launch ye the boat of austere contemplation …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 148–149, folios 130–131); and *His goodness cannot be priced or traded …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 280, folio 254).

The Guru's verse is sung outside the gurdwaras too. Abbas quotes Abida Parvin: "I sing Shah Hussain, Bulle Shah, Mian Muhammad Bakhsh, Hazrat Khawaja Ghulam Farid, Sacchal Sarmast, Baba Farid, and Guru Nanak" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 54, folio not stated). In Sindh, Boivin records, "Sain Vasan Shah became famous for reciting the banis of Shri Guru Nanak and the kalams of the Sufis" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 68, folio 55), and he reports Ajwani's judgement that "Guru Nanak was the greatest poet of the Bhakti" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 90, folio 77).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **Nanak's age.** The site's account says "by the usual reckoning about eighteen years of age". Khalid has an "eighteen-year-old Nanak" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 63, folio 45) and has him sent out "when Baba Guru Nanak turned eighteen" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 182, folio not stated). In Dhillon's retelling Pandit Hardyal speaks of him as a sixteen-year-old (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 40, folio 44).
- **The sum.** The site's account and Khalid give twenty rupees (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 63, folio 45; Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 182, folio not stated); Dhillon gives "twenty silver coins" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 25, folio 29).
- **The errand.** The site's account has Nanak sent "to a nearby town to strike a profitable deal". Khalid's reconstruction sends him to "the market town of Chuharkhana about twelve kos ( forty-five kilometres) from here" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 63, folio 45); Dhillon has him sent with a list of goods to buy in Chuharkhana at wholesale prices and sell in Talwandi (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 25, folio 29).
- **His companion.** The site's account and *A White Trail* name Bhai Mardana (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 182, folio not stated). Dhillon names Bala (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 26–32, folios 30–32).
- **Whom he fed.** The site's account speaks of holy men, "by some tellings hungry ascetics, by others the poor and the destitute". Khalid has starving *jogis* (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 182, folio not stated), and in his reconstruction naked ascetics led by Santrain, calling themselves Nirbani, who "did not beg for food" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 65, folio not stated; p. 62, folio 44); Qaiser speaks of yogis (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 80, folio 62). Dhillon has Naga sadhus, unfed "in a week", "a whole akhara of famished sadhus" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 26–32, folios 30–32; p. 38, folio 42).
- **What he gave them.** The site's account says he spent the money on provisions "to feed and clothe them". Khalid's reconstruction has Santrain refuse the money and Nanak buy food (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 66, folio 48); Dhillon has food, vegetables, fruit and firewood, with cooks and porters (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 33–35, folios 37–39). Neither book mentions clothing.
- **Where he met them.** The site's account places the gurdwara "at the village associated with the story, near Chuharkana". *A White Trail* has the meeting "Outside a place called Mandi Chuharkhana" and the gurdwara "in Chuharkhana at the spot where the Guru fed the starving jogis" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 182, folio not stated). Khalid's reconstruction has "a jungle on the way" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 65, folio not stated); Qaiser puts the meeting under the trees in the gurdwara's courtyard (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 80, folio 62), and a local storyteller on the mound where the big gurdwara stands (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 74, folio 56). Dhillon has the sadhus in the woods off the road to Chuharkhana and the feeding at "the grove of trees" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 26–32, folios 30–32; pp. 33–35, folios 37–39).
- **The father's answer.** The site's account says Nanak "was reproached by his father". In Khalid's reconstruction Mehta Kalu slaps him and Rai Bular offers to adopt him (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 66, folio 48); in Dhillon's, Mehta Kalu calls him a failure and Rai Bular defends him (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 36–37, folios 40–41; p. 38, folio 42).
- **The distance from Nankana Sahib.** Khalid's reconstruction gives twelve *kos*, "forty-five kilometres" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 63, folio 45), and Qaiser "About forty-five kilometres, I think" in a straight line (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 70, folio 52); *A White Trail* gives "about forty-six kilometres" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 182, folio not stated).
- **The town's name.** The site's account writes Chuharkana. Khalid writes "Chuhrkhana" and "Chuharkhana" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 63, 70–71, folios 45, 52–53) and "Mandi Chuharkhana" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 182, folio not stated); Dhillon writes "Chuharkhana" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 25, folio 29).
- **The renovation and the custodians.** The site's account says the building was renovated in 1993–94 with the support of the Sikh *sangat* of the United Kingdom, and opened to pilgrims under arrangement with the Government of Pakistan and the Evacuee Trust Property Board. Qaiser says he first saw the gurdwara in 1993, occupied by buffaloes, addicts and gamblers, that the Department of Auqaf then appointed a guard, and that "In subsequent years, the gurdwara was renovated", with his own part in it (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 79, folio 61); Khalid has the *sewadar* Boota Singh appointed by the Auqaf Department (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 80, folio 62).
- **The Guru's dates.** Khalid and Dhillon give 1469–1539, Dhillon with a birth on 14 April 1469 and a death on 22 September 1539 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 17–18, folios 19–20; p. 259, folio 287). Bhandari gives Samvat 1526, 880 Hijri, in the reign of Bahlul Lodi, and a death under Salim Shah Afghan between the ages of seventy and eighty (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).
- **How many journeys.** Dhillon reports five *udasis* in the Puratan Janamsakhi and four in more modern accounts, and holds to four himself (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 54–55, folios 60–61; p. 56, folio 62). Khalid gives the travels as about twenty-four years (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 54, folio 36); Dhillon's modern scheme spans twenty-three (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 55, folio 61).

## Legacy

Gurdwara Sacha Sauda enshrines a story that is, in miniature, the whole ethic of Sikhism — that true profit lies in generosity, and that the service of the needy is the service of the divine. From the boy's "true bargain" the tradition traces the institution of *langar*, which to this day feeds millions in gurdwaras around the world without charge or condition. For Sikh pilgrims travelling in Pakistan, the shrine is a living link to the compassion of their founder; for the wider tradition, it is the place where a lesson in kindness became the cornerstone of a faith.

In Dhillon's retelling the boy's answer is the moral of the tale: "Any dilution of my bargain will be a dilution of the truth" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 34, folio 38). And Khalid, writing of the founder of the communal kitchen, ties it to this town: "It is believed that the tradition of Langar began with Guru Nanak, when he fed the poor jogis at Mandi Chuharkhana" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 188, folio not stated).
