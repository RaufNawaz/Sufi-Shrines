## Overview

The shrine of *Hazrat Imam Ali-ul-Haq* — known to the people of Sialkot simply and affectionately as *Imam Sahib* — stands in the heart of the old city of Sialkot in the Punjab, at the spot where the saint is traditionally believed to have died. He is venerated as a warrior-saint and martyr (*shahid*), one of the missionary holy men credited by local tradition with the coming of Islam to this corner of the upper Punjab, and his *darbar* is among the most important devotional sites of the city. It is essential at the outset to distinguish him clearly from the celebrated *Bari Imam* (Syed Abdul Latif Shah Kazmi) of Nurpur Shahan near Islamabad, with whom the similar honorific "Imam" sometimes causes confusion: the figure venerated at Sialkot is a separate personage belonging to the local history of that city. It must also be said plainly that the historical identity of the Sialkot saint is genuinely uncertain, and that the sources concerning him are thin and mutually contradictory; what follows therefore reports the traditions as traditions, and does not attempt to resolve them into a single confident biography.

Three older books in Persian and Urdu add their own testimony. Sujan Rai Bhandari of Batala, writing in Persian in the fortieth regnal year of Aurangzeb, writes that in the region of Sialkot lies the resting place of Imam Ali al-Haq, and that his tomb is now a place of visitation (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 516). Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti's *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* names him as a companion of the Zanjanis whose tomb in Sialkot is a place of pilgrimage (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 269). Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori's *Hadiqat al-Awliya* indexes him as Ali al-Haq of Sialkot (Ali al-Haq Sialkoti) (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 324, folio 312), and the bibliography of its modern edition lists a *Waqa'i Sialkot* on his martyrdom (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 299, folio 287). None of the three describes the buildings of the shrine or records an *ʿurs*. Where they differ from one another and from the traditions current at Sialkot, the differences are set out side by side below.

## The Saint and the Tradition

Two broad traditions circulate about Imam Ali-ul-Haq, and they cannot easily be reconciled. According to the more commonly repeated local account, he lived in the medieval period — often placed in the thirteenth or fourteenth century, and sometimes associated with the reign of the Delhi sultan Firuz Shah Tughlaq — and came as a preacher and holy warrior who is said to have brought a large part of the surrounding population to Islam, so transforming Sialkot into a predominantly Muslim town. In this telling he was ultimately martyred by an unbeliever; one version relates that he was struck down while at prayer in a mosque, another that he fell in battle against the local Hindu ruler.

A quite different and considerably older dating derives from a single source: the nineteenth-century Persian chronicle *Chār Bāgh-i-Panjāb* of Ganesh Das Wadhera. This account maintains that "Imam Ali al-Haq" led an army from Arabia to Sialkot around 970–971 CE, where he was killed in a battle with a local raja named Sahan Pal (who is said to have taken the title Salbahan), and that many of his companions perished with him; it further connects his death with a subsequent invasion of the Punjab by Sultan Sabuktigin of Ghazni. It is important to note that this tenth-century account rests on Ganesh Das alone and is corroborated by no other historical work, so it cannot be treated as established fact. Taken together, these divergent traditions — differing by some three or four centuries — mean that the saint's true dates, origins and circumstances remain unresolved. What is consistent across the accounts, and what matters most for the shrine's devotional life, is the memory of a holy man who came from afar, who is credited with the Islamisation of Sialkot, and who died a martyr on the spot now marked by his tomb.

## The Saint in the Older Books

Bhandari's *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, written in the reign of Aurangzeb, gives the saint a few lines within its description of Sialkot. It names him Imam Ali al-Haq, "son of Imam Zain al-Abidin", as the book prints it, and says that his resting place lies in that pleasant region (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 516). Bhandari tells the story of his coming as hearsay, introducing it with "it is said". He came to Hindustan from Arabia with many Muslims, with the intention of holy war; by chance they reached Sialkot, the name's reading uncertain in the printed text; there they fought the Hindus, and they attained the rank of martyrdom (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 516). The verb is plural: in Bhandari's telling the martyrdom belongs to the whole band, not to the Imam alone.

Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti's *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* places him in a different company. It counts him as the fourth companion of the Zanjanis, one whose tomb in Sialkot is a place of pilgrimage for God's people, the words describing the tomb uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 269). On the next page the book explains his name. Something, the words naming it lost in the printed text, "befell" him, and from this, it says, he became famous by the very name Imam Ali Lahiq, several words of the line being uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 270). What it was that befell, and so the story behind the name, cannot be read.

Lahori's *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, composed in Persian in 1875 and read in a modern Urdu edition, names him in its index, which carries the entry "Ali al-Haq Sialkoti", at 187, the *nisba* tying him to Sialkot (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 324, folio 312). The same number, 187, is given in that index for Bibiyan Pakdaman, Husain Zanjani, Haj Bibi and Khizr Khan (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 324, folio 312). A separate index entry, "Ali al-Haq Shah", at 223, is not cross-referenced to the Sialkot saint (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 324, folio 312).

The same edition's list of sources names a dated account of his martyrdom: item 141, a *Waqa'i Sialkot* by Muhammad Muqim, dated 1071 AH, on the martyrdom of Imam Ali al-Haq of Sialkot, edited by Dr Abdullah Chaghatai and published at Lahore in 1977 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 299, folio 287). Lahori's editor cites the same *Waqa'i Sialkot* of 1071 AH as his documentary evidence for the date 600 AH that he gives for Husain Zanjani (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 199). The book does not say what the *Waqa'i* itself reports of the Imam's dates.

## The Shrine and its Devotional Life

The *darbar* of Imam Sahib is not a single monumental tomb but a dense and atmospheric complex in the old city, described by visitors as a maze of narrow corridors and stairways opening onto several graves — those, by tradition, of unnamed companions who fell with the saint, together with the resting-places of later *pirs* and holy men who chose to be buried in his shadow. The tomb of the saint himself is approached through a mirrored gateway ornamented with Qur'anic inscriptions and geometric tilework, in the idiom of Punjabi shrine decoration, and the passages leading to it are often lined with prostrating and praying pilgrims.

As a martyr's shrine, the site carries the particular charisma that Punjabi popular religion attaches to the *shahid* — the martyr who is believed to be present and living, and whose intercession and *baraka* are sought by the faithful. Devotees come throughout the year to offer prayers, tie votive threads and seek blessings, and the shrine, administered like many of the province's major *darbars* under the auspices of the Punjab Auqaf Department, is the focus of an annual *urs* commemorating the saint. On such occasions the surrounding lanes fill with pilgrims, and the customary features of a Punjabi shrine festival — devotional music, the recitation of poetry, and the distribution of *langar* — accompany the gathering. Because Imam Sahib is regarded as the spiritual founder of Muslim Sialkot, his memory is woven into the civic and religious identity of the city itself, and local commemorations periodically recall his role in its history.

The older books bear witness to the tomb as a place of pilgrimage long before the present day. Bhandari, in the reign of Aurangzeb, writes that now his tomb, a place where divine lights are made manifest, is visited by small and great alike (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 516). Chishti calls it a place of visitation for God's people (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 269). Neither book describes the building, its custodians or its endowments, and none of the books consulted gives the date of an *ʿurs*.

## Setting: Sialkot

The passages below speak of the city of Sialkot, not of the shrine; none of them mentions the Imam.

Bhandari places the city in the Rachna doab, calling it a city of the ancients (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517). It was once called Shalkot, and its founding is attributed to a raja of the Pandavas' time whose printed name, perhaps Shal, cannot be read with certainty; the city and the raja are named in the Mahabharata, composed, the book says, some five thousand years before (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517). It is also called Salkot and attributed to Raja Salbahan, of whom a fort of fired brick is the memorial (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517). In former times it was the seat of government of the Punjab, its habitation stretching two or three *kos*; and in Bhandari's day it surpassed every other town of the suba in population (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517). When Lahore lay in ruins in early times, the city of Sialkot became the seat of government of this country, until Sultan Mahmud's conquest and Malik Ayaz's rebuilding of Lahore (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525).

Harish Dhillon, retelling the Janamsakhis of Guru Nanak, describes Sialkot as "a bustling city that was situated in the foothills of the Himalayas". Legend, he writes, has it founded by "Raja Sela, the uncle of the Pandavas", and rebuilt by "Raja Satya Vachan, when it became part of the kingdom of Kashmir"; "The earliest recorded mention of the city is found in ancient Greek texts going as far back as 322 B.C., where it is referred to as Sagala", and it was "famous for its silk" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 136, folio 151). Haroon Khalid's reconstruction of the city's past begins with another king: "Puran Bhagat was the son of the King of Sialkot, Raja Salivahan"; "It is believed that Sialkot was founded by this king"; "The well of Puran Bhagat was at a little distance from the city of Sialkot", and devotees raised a temple around it, "the most important temple in the ancient city of Sialkot" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 184, folio 158). Chishti, too, tells the Puran legend: Raja Salwan, the name uncertain in the printed text, held the seat of Sialkot, a word of the line being illegible (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 553); the well into which Puran was thrown, his hands and feet cut off, is still to be seen there (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 554); and when Puran came to Sialkot a dried-up garden revived on his arrival (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 557). The name Salbahan, which the site's account gives as the title of the raja who fought the saint, thus recurs in the books' stories of the city's founding king (Bhandari's Raja Salbahan, Khalid's Raja Salivahan); none of these books connects that king with the Imam.

Of the coming of Muslim rule and of the Sufis to the city, Dhillon writes that "Sialkot became a part of the Muslim Sultanate when Shahabuddin Muhammad Ghori captured Punjab in 1192", and that a garrison was left there (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 136, folio 151). Many Sufi missionaries, he adds, settled in the region, making it predominantly Muslim and "giving it a rich Sufi tradition", and the city was an important post on the trade route between West Asia and Delhi (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 137, folio 152). Bhandari tells of the same conqueror at the fort: Shihab al-Din of Ghur, failing at Lahore in the year 580 AH, came to Sialkot, repaired the old fort and left his army there (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517); in another passage he restored the fort, whose foundation was ancient, and Khusraw Malik with the Khokhars besieged it for a time without success (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 404). Under Akbar, Raja Man Singh, faujdar of Jammu and jagirdar of Sialkot, repaired the fort and improved the city; then Safdar Khan, in Jahangir's time, rebuilt the fort and its tower, and most later governors made repairs too (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517).

The site's account ties the saint's death to an invasion of the Punjab by Sultan Sabuktigin of Ghazni. The books consulted do not mention the saint in that connection, but two of them speak of Sabuktigin and the province. Kanhaiya Lal's history of Lahore writes that Sultan Mahmud and Sabuktigin of Ghazni attacked the Punjab when Raja Jaipal ruled the Punjab from Lahore (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 14, folio 9). Richard Eaton writes that "The pattern commenced in 986, when the Ghaznavid Sultan Sabuktigin (r. 977-97) attacked and defeated the Hindu Shahi raja who controlled the region between Kabul and northwest Punjab" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 110, folio 98). Of the city's countryside, Eaton notes that Babur "described Jat pastoralist groups in the Sind Sagar doab and Sialkot regions" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, pp. 224–225, folios 212–213).

The Sialkot of Bhandari's day was a city of crafts and learning. Its paper, the kinds called Mansinghi, silk (*hariri*) and *khass-i Jahangiri*, was white and durable and sent to other regions; its *karchikan* embroidery in silk and *kalabatun* went all over the world, and its *jamdhar*, *katari* and *barchhi* were well made (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517). Gardens surrounded it, among them the garden of Nazar Muhammad, whose further name, perhaps Bhutta, cannot be read with certainty (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517). A stream rising in the hills of Jammu flowed below the city, and when it flooded in the rains all the people of Sialkot, great and small, played in the water, naked but for a loincloth and carrying leather water-skins; anyone from the city who was far away, Bhandari writes, remembered this with pleasure (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 517–516). The buildings of the qanungos and of some others were, he writes, most pleasing and delightful (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517).

The city was, in Bhandari's words, a seat of learning and a gathering-place of scholars, a mine of excellence and a dwelling of the learned (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 516). Under Akbar, Maulana Kamal al-Din Husain left Kashmir and came to Sialkot in the year 971 AH, and established teaching there; under Shah Jahan Maulvi Abd al-Hakim spread the sciences, wrote glosses, and drew students to his blessed madrasa; his son Maulvi Abdullah ran the madrasa after him and was called the imam of his time (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 516). Annemarie Schimmel likewise calls Sialkot "one of the centers of Islamic scholarship during the Mogul period", where Ahmad Sirhindi studied (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 398, folio 367).

Other holy men of the city appear in the books. Shah Daula's first master lived in Sialkot (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 515), and in the volume edited by Pnina Werbner and Helene Basu, Shah Daulah himself is said to have left it: "Due to some disputes with the local people of Sialkot, he migrated from there and, after some years, moved to Gujrat" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 205, folio 194). Dhillon and Khalid both tell of the faqir Hamza Ghaus, whose curse was pronounced in "the centre of the market square" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 150, folio 165), and of whom Sialkot "was gripped by fear" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 197–198, folios 171–172).

Khalid found a modern city. "Sialkot has become one of the most important cities in Pakistan"; its business community "financed its own airport, which now handles international flights", and the highway, and on the way in he saw "several factories" and "a lot of mounds", "probably archaeological ruins, converted into graveyards" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 191, folio 165). "Sialkot is located right on the border", he writes, and "Historically, Sialkot provided Punjab with a connection to Kashmir" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 193, folio 167).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **His dates.** The local account places him in the thirteenth or fourteenth century, sometimes in the reign of Firuz Shah Tughlaq; Ganesh Das Wadhera's *Chār Bāgh-i-Panjāb*, as the site's account reports it, has him killed around 970–971 CE. Bhandari gives no date (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 516). Chishti counts him a companion of the Zanjanis (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 269). The *Waqa'i Sialkot* on his martyrdom is dated 1071 AH in the list of sources of Lahori's modern edition (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 299, folio 287).
- **Who he was.** The site's account calls him a missionary holy man and warrior-saint, in Ganesh Das's version the leader of an army from Arabia. Bhandari calls him "son of Imam Zain al-Abidin" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 516). Chishti calls him the fourth companion of the Zanjanis (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 269).
- **How he came to Sialkot.** Ganesh Das, as the site's account gives him, has him lead an army from Arabia to Sialkot; the local account has him come as a preacher and holy warrior. Bhandari has him come to Hindustan from Arabia with many Muslims for holy war and reach Sialkot by chance (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 516).
- **How he died.** One local version has him struck down by an unbeliever while at prayer in a mosque; another, that he fell in battle against the local Hindu ruler; Ganesh Das, that he was killed in battle with Raja Sahan Pal, and many of his companions with him. Bhandari says that the whole band fought the Hindus and attained martyrdom, the verb plural (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 516).
- **His name.** The site gives Imam Ali-ul-Haq, and Ganesh Das "Imam Ali al-Haq". Bhandari writes Imam Ali al-Haq (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 516), and Lahori's edition Ali al-Haq Sialkoti in its index and Imam Ali al-Haq Sialkoti in its list of sources (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 324, folio 312; p. 299, folio 287). Chishti gives the name as Imam Ali Lahiq and explains it from something that befell (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 270).
- **Who founded Sialkot** (of the city, not the shrine). Bhandari attributes it to a raja of the Pandavas' time whose printed name, perhaps Shal, cannot be read with certainty, and, under its other name of Salkot, to Raja Salbahan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517). Dhillon gives the legend of "Raja Sela, the uncle of the Pandavas", the city rebuilt by Raja Satya Vachan (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 136, folio 151). Khalid writes that it is believed to have been founded by Raja Salivahan (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 184, folio 158).
- **Babur and Sialkot** (of the city, not the shrine). Bhandari records Babur's order that Sialkot be laid waste and its people resettled at Bahlulpur (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 304). Dhillon writes that during "the Mughal Emperor Babur's third incursion into India", "Sialkot had offered no resistance and had been spared the usual bloody massacre and plunder" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 224, folio 246).

## Legacy

Imam Ali-ul-Haq occupies a place in Sialkot comparable to that of a patron saint: the figure to whom the city's Muslim character is popularly traced, and whose martyrdom sanctifies its very ground. That the details of his life are historically obscure — the sources disagreeing even on the century in which he lived — has done nothing to diminish his standing in local devotion, and may indeed be characteristic of the earliest layer of Punjabi Muslim sacred history, in which the memory of the warrior-saint who "opened" a region to Islam is preserved more in cult and story than in dated record. His shrine remains a living centre of pilgrimage and one of the defining landmarks of old Sialkot.

The older books show how long that memory has been written down. A *Waqa'i Sialkot* on his martyrdom is dated 1071 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 299, folio 287); Bhandari, in Aurangzeb's reign, found his tomb visited by small and great (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 516); and Chishti called it a place of pilgrimage for God's people (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 269).
