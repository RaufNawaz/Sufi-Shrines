## Overview

The Shrine of Makhdoom Nooh at Hala, a town some fifty-six kilometres north-east of Hyderabad in Sindh, honours one of the most revered saints and scholars of the province. Makhdoom Lutufullah, universally known as Makhdoom Nooh — and often as Makhdoom Sarwar Nooh — is generally dated to the sixteenth century, with tradition placing his birth around 1500-1505 and his death about 1590. A Sufi of the *Suhrawardi* order, honoured also with an Uwaisi spiritual lineage, he is remembered as the founder of the *Sarwari* branch of the order in Sindh, and above all as the first religious scholar of the region to render the Holy Quran into Persian, an achievement that secured his lasting fame.

He was buried near his native village close to Halakandi, but the shifting of the Indus twice compelled the relocation of his remains — to Mula Sanwani about 1601, and again, in 1777, to the site of new Hala, where his mausoleum now stands. The shrine is the spiritual heart of Hala, a town long associated with the *Makhdoom* saintly lineage, with Sufi devotional music and with the celebrated blue-and-white *Kashi* pottery of the region. It draws devotees throughout the year and hosts an annual *ʿurs* in the saint's memory.

Besides the compendium on which the site's own account draws, only one author among the books consulted here names the saint: Annemarie Schimmel, who does so in two of her works, each time briefly and each time by his place: as Makhdum Nuh "of Hala" in *Pain and Grace* (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 204, folio 204) and as the best-known name among the Suhrawardi leaders settled at Hala in *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 421, folio 390). None of the books describes the shrine itself, its building, its custodians or its *ʿurs*; what they add about Hala is said of the town, not of the shrine, and is gathered below as context.

## The Life of the Saint

The saint's real name is given as Lutufullah, and he is known by the title Makhdoom Nooh, and often as Makhdoom Sarwar Nooh. Tradition places his birth around 1500-1505 and his death about 1590, in the sixteenth century. A general compendium of Pakistani Sufi saints, Alam Faqri's *Tazkirah Awliya-e-Pakistan*, independently confirms his real name, which it spells Lutfullah, and his title as Makhdoom Nooh, and states that he died at the age of 87 — consistent with the dating above, which implies an age of roughly 85-90.

He belonged to the *Suhrawardi* order and is honoured also with an Uwaisi spiritual lineage. He is remembered as the founder of the *Sarwari* branch of the order in Sindh. His lasting fame rests above all on his scholarship: he is remembered as the first religious scholar of the region to render the Holy Quran into Persian.

Schimmel's study of two eighteenth-century mystical writers of Muslim India confirms the order and the region, and places him by his town. She calls him "the great Suhrawardi leader of Sind in the 17th century", printing his name as Makhdum Nuh of Hala, and the book's index lists him under the same place-name, as "Makhdiim[OCR?] Nuh of Hala" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 204, folio 204; Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 305, folio 305). She mentions him in a single sentence: in his work, she writes, the *dhikr* "itself is called a high priced pearl" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 204, folio 204). She does not name the work. This image of the *dhikr* as a pearl is all that any of the books consulted here reports of his own writing.

In *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* Schimmel again sets him among the Suhrawardis of Hala. Of Hala, where "mystical leaders of the Suhrawardiyya had settled", she writes that "the name of Makhdum Nuh in the seventeenth century is best known" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 421, folio 390). Her index, too, lists him with his century, as Makhdum Nuh of the seventeenth century (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 520, folio 489). Both of Schimmel's books thus place him in the seventeenth century, not the sixteenth; the difference is set out below under Where the Sources Disagree.

Neither book gives a year of birth or of death, the name Lutufullah, the title Sarwar, the Uwaisi lineage, the *Sarwari* branch, or the Persian rendering of the Quran; for these, the account above rests on the site's own description and its bibliography.

## The Shrine

The saint was first buried near his native village close to Halakandi. The Indus, shifting its course, twice compelled the relocation of his remains: first to Mula Sanwani, about 1601, and again, in 1777, to the site of new Hala, where his mausoleum now stands. Alam Faqri's compendium describes his shrine at "Hala Kandi" as a major place of pilgrimage.

The shrine is the spiritual heart of Hala. It draws devotees throughout the year and hosts an annual *ʿurs* in the saint's memory. The books consulted here record no date for the *ʿurs*, do not describe the mausoleum's building, and do not name its builder or its custodians.

## Setting: Hala

Hala, in the Matiari District, is a town long associated with the *Makhdoom* saintly lineage, with Sufi devotional music and with the celebrated blue-and-white *Kashi* pottery of the region. Several of the books speak of the town, though none of them mentions the shrine; what follows is about Hala, not about the shrine or the saint.

Schimmel names the town as a seat of the order to which Makhdoom Nooh belonged, a place where "mystical leaders of the Suhrawardiyya had settled" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 421, folio 390). On the same page she bears out the town's name for tilework. Describing the tomb of Shah Abdul Latif at Bhit Shah, she says it is "covered with blue and white tiles (typical of Hala)" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 421, folio 390).

Hala is also, in two of the books, the birthplace of the great Sindhi poet-saint Shah Abdul Latif. Schimmel writes that he "was born into a family of mystics in 1689 in Hala, near present-day Hyderabad-Sind" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 421, folio 390). Michel Boivin, who does not mention Makhdoom Nooh anywhere in his study, likewise records that Shah Abd al-Latif was born in Hala, in a Sayyid family (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 91, folio 78).

Shemeem Burney Abbas describes the country around the town. Kolhi and Bhil women, she writes, are devotees at the shrine of Shah Abdul Latif, and go there "primarily because the shrine is located in Hala, the heart of a fertile agricultural belt, where they can make a living working on the large feudal farms" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 46, folio not stated). Elsewhere in the same book she places that shrine in Bhit Shah, Sind, without naming Hala (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 50, folio not stated).

An older name for the town appears in Jürgen Schaflechner's study of the goddess Hinglaj, in an account of the goddess's origins that the book quotes: "Hala in Sindh was called Kohala in the former times, and the Goddess Hinglaj was once the queen of the Kohala or Kohana area." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 71, folio not stated). The book says nothing of Makhdoom Nooh or of his shrine, and draws no connection between the shrine and the goddess.

The country about the town was not always safe. In H. T. Sorley's study of Shah Abdul Latif and his times, the country between Sehwan and old Hala is remembered as dangerous: a people whose name is imperfectly printed, given as the Samej, "are described as a source of danger to traders for their robbing proclivities so that the country is not passable 'without a strong guard'" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 51, printed 42). Sorley names neither the saint nor the shrine.

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The saint's century.** The site's account dates him to the sixteenth century, with his birth around 1500-1505 and his death about 1590, and Alam Faqri's compendium gives his age at death as 87. Schimmel calls him "the great Suhrawardi leader of Sind in the 17th century" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 204, folio 204), and in *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* writes that "the name of Makhdum Nuh in the seventeenth century is best known" at Hala, her index again placing him in the seventeenth century (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 421, folio 390; Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 520, folio 489). Neither of Schimmel's books gives a year.
- **His name.** The site's account gives his real name as Lutufullah; Alam Faqri's compendium spells it Lutfullah. Schimmel uses only the title, Makhdum Nuh (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 204, folio 204; Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 421, folio 390).
- **Where the shrine stands.** The site's account places his mausoleum at new Hala, where his remains were brought in 1777 after two removals, and his first grave near Halakandi. Alam Faqri's compendium describes his shrine at "Hala Kandi" as a major place of pilgrimage.

## Legacy

Makhdoom Nooh is remembered in Sindh as one of the most revered saints and scholars of the province: the founder of the *Sarwari* branch of the *Suhrawardi* order there, and the first religious scholar of the region to render the Holy Quran into Persian. By the site's account, his remains were twice moved by the shifting Indus, the second time to new Hala in 1777, and his shrine remains the spiritual heart of the town, visited throughout the year and at his annual *ʿurs*. In Schimmel's *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, his is the best-known name among the Suhrawardi masters of Hala, and in her study of eighteenth-century mystical writing he stands as "the great Suhrawardi leader of Sind" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 421, folio 390; Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 204, folio 204).
