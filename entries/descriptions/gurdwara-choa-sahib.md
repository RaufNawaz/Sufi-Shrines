## Overview

Gurdwara Choa Sahib — its name meaning the "gurdwara of the exalted spring" — stands at the northern edge of the great sixteenth-century *Rohtas Fort*, a UNESCO World Heritage Site near Jhelum, close to the fort's Talaqi gate. It commemorates a tradition of *Guru Nanak* (1469–1539): Sikhs hold that the Guru, travelling with his companion *Bhai Mardana* during the fourth of his great journeys (*udasis*) and having spent forty days at the nearby *Tilla Jogian*, came to this waterless tract in the heat of summer.

None of the books consulted names Gurdwara Choa Sahib. One of them describes a gurdwara at Rohtas: Haroon Khalid's *Walking with Nanak* twice visits "Nanak's Gurdwara in Jhelum, [near] Rohtas Fort", once in the historian Iqbal Qaiser's memory and once on Khalid's own journey, but it never gives the gurdwara a name, and it is identified with this site by its place beside the fort (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 248, folio 222; p. 250, folio 224). Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* tells of the founding of the fort of Rohtas, but nowhere in it is Guru Nanak, a gurdwara or a spring named at Rohtas (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 268–269). Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi's history of Sufism in India names neither Rohtas, nor Dina, nor the Jhelum district. The other books speak of Guru Nanak, of Jhelum or of the Punjab at large; what they say is gathered below as being about the Guru or the place, not about this gurdwara.

## The Guru at Rohtas

The legend the gurdwara keeps is the Guru's spring. In the site's account, Guru Nanak and Bhai Mardana came to Rohtas in the heat of summer, after forty days at Tilla Jogian, and when Mardana lamented his thirst the Guru is said to have struck the ground with his staff and moved a stone, causing a natural spring to well up — the *choa* that gives the shrine its name. A further Sikh legend relates that Sher Shah Suri, builder of Rohtas Fort, thrice attempted to divert the spring uphill to supply his stronghold, and thrice his engineers failed.

Haroon Khalid, standing at the gurdwara, reflected on the place as it must have been: "When Nanak stayed at this place, there was no Rohtas fort, so this must have been a desolate space"; Nanak and Mardana, he writes, "sat here for days" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 250, folio 224). In his general account of the Guru's travels Khalid notes that Nanak "preferred to stay on the outskirts" of villages, "a recurrent theme" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 53, folio 35), and that "Whenever he would visit an area he would visit its religious shrines, Muslim, Hindu and of any other denomination" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 37, folio 19).

Bhandari's *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* gives the distance between the two places the site's account joins. Describing the suba of Lahore, it sets Tilla "بهفت کروهے قلعه رهتاس", seven *kos* from the fort of Rohtas (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 514). It says nothing of the Guru at either place.

The site's account places the visit in the fourth of the Guru's *udasis*. Harish Dhillon's retelling of the Janamsakhis sets out two schemes of these journeys. "According to the Puratan Janamsakhi… he undertook five journeys", the fourth in 1519–1521; "More modern accounts" give four, the fourth, in 1518–1521, to the Arab countries (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 54–55, folios 60–61; p. 55, folio 61). Dhillon himself writes: "Personally, I believe chat [that] he undertook four udasis and not five" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 56, folio 62). In his telling the fourth journey went by Multan, Sukkur, Thatta and Hinglaj across the Arabian Sea (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 194, folio 214), and on it the Guru was "dressed in blue robes, a colour considered sacred by the Muslims", with a staff, a prayer mat, the Quran and an ablution pot (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 194–195, folios 214–215). After Mecca, Medina and Baghdad he came home by way of Iran, Kabul, Peshawar, Kandahar, Hasan Abdal, Tilla Balnath, Katasraj and Saidpur to Kartarpur (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 224–225, folios 246–247). Dhillon does not name Rohtas on that road. The close of the journey, he writes, came around Babur's "third incursion into India", when he took a south-eastern route to the Punjab (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 224, folio 246).

## Bhai Mardana

The site's account remembers the Guru's companion, whose thirst brought forth the spring. Bhandari, writing in Persian of the Guru's life, names him: "مردانه نام مطرب", a singer named Mardana, who sang the Guru's verses and drew people "در دام عقیدت" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).

Khalid's reconstruction of the Guru's life, which he says is in part fictionalised (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 17, folio not stated), has Mardana play the *rubab* every night at Sultanpur while Nanak sang, a daily feature "for the rest of their [lives] and also for the Sikh community" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 106, folio 88; p. 107, folio 89). Nanak and Mardana "would not return to a sedentary life for twenty-four years" and "would travel thousands of kilometres, that too on foot" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 108, folio 90). In the same reconstruction, Mardana "knew by heart several folk stories of Hindu deities and Muslim saints that he would sing during different festivals of Punjab" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 121, folio 103). Khalid gives the Guru's advice to his two companions, Bhai Bala and Bhai Mardana: "if one is a Muslim then one should be a good Muslim and if one is a Hindu then one should be a good Hindu" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 95, folio 77). Mardana's poetry stands in the Guru Granth Sahib, which "contains the spiritual poetry of the various Sikh Gurus", Khalid writes, together with "other spiritual teachers like Bhagat Kabir, Baba Farid and Bhai Mardana" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 82, folio 64).

## The Gurdwara

The first commemorative structure, with a *sarovar* (sacred tank) and a place for the recitation of the *Guru Granth Sahib*, is attributed to Charat Singh; the present building dates from 1834 and was commissioned by *Maharaja Ranjit Singh*. Iqbal Qaiser's 1998 survey adds that Maharaja Ranjit Singh endowed the shrine with twenty-seven *ghumaon* of land and Rs 260.

Khalid writes of Ranjit Singh's patronage in general terms, not of this gurdwara: "When Maharaja Ranjit Singh became the sovereign of Punjab at the end of the eighteenth century he spent large amounts of money on the renovation and expansion of many gurdwaras", and he "attached vast tracts of land to these gurdwaras, so that the revenues from the lands could facilitate their functioning" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 34, folio 16). In *A White Trail* Khalid gives the Maharaja's dates as 1780–1839 and has him crowned "Ruler of Punjab" in 1801 (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 42, folio not stated; p. 207, folio not stated), and writes that he renovated "many Gurdwaras all over Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 208, folio not stated).

Khalid describes the gurdwara twice. The first account is Iqbal Qaiser's. Qaiser called "the trip I made to Nanak's Gurdwara in Jhelum, [near] Rohtas Fort" the most difficult of his journeys. With "a friend called Ilyas Ghuman", "in the month of June", he climbed a wall of the fort and saw the gurdwara "just under the shadow of the wall of the fort"; the two walked for hours through thorny shrubs, and when they reached it they "fell on the steps of the gurdwara and passed out". "An old man who was working there" brought them "an entire pot of water", told them of "a simpler route from the road", and warned of "quicksand and even wild animals" and of "dacoits who had taken refuge in the forest" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 248, folio 222).

The second is Khalid's own, on the way back from Lahore with Anam. They saw "a building with a dome on top", and there was "no doubt that this was a Sikh gurdwara". "A black corrosive powder had collected on the walls"; "This was an abandoned structure" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 249, folio 223). Close to, it was "a three storey building", "a beautiful structure located on top of a small mound", beside a forest "on the edge of which was the fort"; they climbed to the top "and walked around the dome" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 250, folio 224). Khalid gives no year for either visit.

## Devotion and Practice

Iqbal Qaiser's survey records that a fair was held here on the fifteenth of *Kattak Suddi*. The sources consulted record no other festival at the gurdwara.

Khalid's visit records the gurdwara's neighbours rather than its worship. Two men "wearing black shalwar kameez" came by motorcycle to tend "the Shia shrine" facing the gurdwara, and told the visitors to leave "since it was getting dark and this place was not safe"; Anam's search on the internet turned up the words "Two tourists beheaded" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 250, folio 224).

What the books say of the worship of Guru Nanak's followers is said of them in general. Bhandari, writing in the reign of Aurangzeb, finds that "خلاصه عبادت اینطایفه مطالعه اشعار مرشد خویش است که بسرود و نغمه میگویند", the sum of their worship being the verses of their master, which they sing (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 519). They count serving travellers, "درخدمت صادر و وارد", as "عبادت عظمی", the highest worship; whoever arrives at midnight speaking Baba Nanak's name they treat as a brother and a friend and serve, be he a stranger, a thief, a highwayman or an evildoer (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 519). They live "با دوستان یکرنگ و با دشمنان بی جنگ", and "اعتقادی که این فریق بر مقتداے خویش دارند در طوایف دیگر کم نشان یافته میشود": devotion such as theirs to their leader, he writes, is seldom found in other groups (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 519). "It is believed that the tradition of Langar began with Guru Nanak", Khalid writes in *A White Trail* (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 188, folio not stated). Expatriate Sikhs, he records, came to Pakistan for festivals "like Baisakhi and the birthday of Guru Nanak, which were attended by thousands of Indian pilgrims" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 214, folio not stated).

## Partition and Restoration

Long neglected after the Partition of 1947, the gurdwara was reopened to pilgrims around the time of Guru Nanak's 550th birth anniversary in 2019, and from 2020 a diaspora Sikh organisation undertook its rehabilitation and restoration. In January 2019 a delegation of the Walled City of Lahore Authority, led by Director General Kamran Lashari, inspected the gurdwara together with the nearby Janam Asthan Mata Sahib Kaur and directed the local administration to restore both sites; that August, seventy-two years after Partition, the Evacuee Trust Property Board formally reopened Gurdwara Choa Sahib for worship, its chairman Dr Aamer Ahmed presiding over a ceremony open to Sikh pilgrims “whether they come from India or any other part of the world.”

Khalid writes more widely of the fate of Sikh shrines in the Pakistani Punjab, not of this one. Under British rule, "As a result of sectarian divisions between the Sikh communities and the takeover of Punjab by the British, a large number of important Sikh Gurdwaras were being administered by Hindu mahants" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 189, folio not stated). After 1947 many such places were taken over and turned into homes, "like the hundreds of temples and Gurdwaras all over Pakistani Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 58, folio not stated). Of the migration that followed the division of the Punjab he writes: "I have seen several gurdwaras and temples being taken over and destroyed by the migrants"; where the population is largely indigenous, "such 'non-Muslim' religious spaces are better looked after", with exceptions (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated).

## Rohtas Fort

What follows is about the fort, not the gurdwara. Bhandari tells its founding in his history of Sher Khan. After the victory at Kanauj and the pursuit to Lahore, "شیر خان تهته[OCR?] تعلقه گکهران رفته برگشت و متصل کوه بالنا[OCR?] قلعه بنیا نهاد و برهتاس موسوم گردانیده": Sher Khan went into the country of the Gakhars, came back, founded a fort beside the hills of Balna, as the uncertain reading of the printed text gives the name, and called it Rohtas (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 268–269). He left "ده هزار سوار برائے سد راه لشکر بادشاهی و بالش[OCR?] [مالش?] گکهران[OCR?] در آنجا گذاشت", ten thousand horse to bar the road to the imperial army and to deal with the Gakhars (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 268). "آن قلعه را خلف او اسلام شاه باتمام رسانید": his successor Islam Shah brought the fort to completion (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 268). "بعد تنظیم و تنسیق مهمات آن دیار", once the affairs of that country were ordered, Sher Khan went to Agra, and "در سنه ۹۴۷[OCR?]" struck coin and had the *khutba* read in his own name as Sher Shah (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 268). Bhandari gives no year for the founding itself.

In his section on the Gakhars, Bhandari tells the same story from the other side. Sher Shah and Islam Shah "مدتی بتسخیر آن ولایت ترددات[OCR?] نمودند و قلعہ روہتاس را بر سر حد احداث کردند تا جماعت گکھران عاجز شدہ مطیع شوند": for a long time they strove to subdue that country, and they raised the fort of Rohtas on the frontier so that the Gakhars would be worn down and submit (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 239). But "اصلا کار پیشی نرفت", and in the end they made peace on condition that Sultan Sarang, the ruler there, came in (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 239). In this account Bhandari places Rohtas on the Gakhar border, between the Bahat and the Sindh.

Rohtas was the western end of Sher Shah's road and post. Bhandari describes sarais "هزار و پانصد کروه[OCR?] از بنگاله تا رهتاس پنجاب بمفاصله دو دو کروه", from Bengal to the Rohtas of the Punjab, two *kos* apart, each with two horses "که نام و اک چوکی[OCR?] [داک چوکی?] باشد", so that "دو سه روز خبر بنگاله برهتاس میرسید", news of Bengal reaching Rohtas in two or three days (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 266). With Sher Shah in his palace and the *naqqara* sounding, at a point where a word of the printed text is illegible, "تمامی سراها از بنگاله تا رهتاس پنجاب" beat their drums in turn (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 266).

Bhandari knows more than one Rohtas. He writes of the Punjab fort as "رهتاس پنجاب" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 266), and separately of a fort in Bihar held by Raja Chintaman (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 270) and of the dependencies of Rohtas around Sahsaram (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 273). He does not say in so many words that the fort founded beside the hills of Balna is the Rohtas of the Punjab; that reading rests on the name and on the narrative, which runs through Lahore, the Gakhars and the barrier against Kabul. Elsewhere, returning "از کابل … بهندوستان", an emperor made a halt "چون در حوالی رهتاس[OCR?] نزول اقبال واقع شد[OCR?]", in the neighbourhood of a Rohtas, and sent word through Khwaja Abul Hasan for Mahabat Khan to go on ahead, "و الا کار بجنگ میرسد"; Mahabat Khan went ahead and crossed the "دریای بهت" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 111). The name of the place is uncertain in the printed text there, and the passage says nothing of a fort.

## In Verse

The sources consulted give no verse of the spring or of Rohtas. They give the Guru's own verse, which Khalid quotes throughout *Walking with Nanak* in published English translations; the lines below belong to the Guru the gurdwara remembers, not to this place. Khalid's way to him began with a poem: the *Babur Bani*, "a long poem that Nanak wrote at the time the Mughal marauder Babur attacked Punjab, destroying the cities of Eminabad and Lahore on the way" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 15, folio not stated), "the first poem of Nanak that I read". Through Iqbal Qaiser's recitation of it, Khalid writes, he "truly discovered Guru Nanak": in it Nanak "lays the blame of Babur's wrath at God's feet" and "challenges the divinity of the Divine" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 15–16, folio not stated). Its line on the invaders' coming reads:

*Coming in seventy-eight (1521 C.E)* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 113–114, folios 95–96)

The year of their departure, which the line goes on to give, is damaged in the printed gloss.

The *aarti*, which Iqbal Qaiser reads as placing God "in nature" and which leads him to call Nanak "a naturalist", begins in one translation:

*The sky the salver, the sun and moon the lamps …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 55, folio 37)

and in another:

*In the salver of the firmament, / The sun and the moon shine as lamps* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 243–244, folios 217–218)

On the oneness of God:

*My Master is One / He is One, brother, the only One* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 60, folio 42)

*God is One, / The Path is One* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 144, folio 126)

The poem on the sacred thread, the *janeu*, which in Khalid's reconstruction the boy Nanak refused:

*From the cotton of compassion, / Spin the thread of contentment* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 61–62, folios 43–44)

On water and washing:

*As hands or feet besmirched with slime, / Water washes white* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 192, folio 166)

The song Nanak sings, in Khalid's reconstruction, to the innkeepers who meant to rob him and Mardana:

*The herons in their white feathers dwell in the sacred shrines of pilgrimage* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 123–126, folios 105–108)

Other openings Khalid quotes: *O my merchant friend* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 56–58, folio 40); *Launch ye the boat of austere contemplation …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 148–149, folios 130–131); *As a beggar goes a-begging …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 158, folio 140); and *His goodness cannot be priced or traded …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 280, folio 254).

Bhandari, writing in Persian, says that the Guru composed "اشعار هندی" on the truth of God and set out divine unity "بعبارات واضحه و استعارات لایحه" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520); an emperor heard from Guru Arjan, the reading of whose name is uncertain in the printed text, "اشعار هندی تصنیف بابا نانک که در معرفت حق گفته" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 164). Shemeem Burney Abbas's glossary defines the *banī* by him: "Devotional verses such as those of Guru Nanak; verses of supplication, entreaty, humility, and reverence" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 179, folio not stated). The verse is sung outside the gurdwaras too. Abbas quotes the singer Abida Parvin: "I sing Shah Hussain, Bulle Shah, Mian Muhammad Bakhsh, Hazrat Khawaja Ghulam Farid, Sacchal Sarmast, Baba Farid, and Guru Nanak" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 54, folio not stated). In Sindh, Michel Boivin records, "Sain Vasan Shah became famous for reciting the banis of Shri Guru Nanak and the kalams of the Sufis" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 68, folio 55). Abbas names the *doha*, a rhyming couplet, as the form in which "Poets such as Kabir and Nanak used it to write their mystical poetry" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 141, folio not stated).

## Setting: Jhelum and the Region

What follows is about Jhelum and the country around it, not the gurdwara. Bhandari, describing the rivers of the suba of Lahore, writes that the Bihat passes "پایان قصبه جهلم که درین محل شاهگذر[OCR?] واقع است", and that there "و جهلم نام می یابد": below the town of Jhelum, where a crossing stands, the river takes the name Jhelum (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 511).

Rizvi, writing of Babur's coming at the end of Guru Nanak's eastern travels, has the Guru return about 1520 "only to find the Panjab plunged into a number of significant political crises", and writes: "After two minor raids around the region between the Indus and Jhelum, Babur invaded a third time over the Panjab in 926/1520 reaching as far as Sialkot" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 409, folio 385).

On the page where he first sees the gurdwara, Khalid writes that Jhelum is some kilometres from his new home; the number of kilometres is illegible in the printed text (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 249, folio 223). In *In Search of Shiva* he tells of "a book which allegedly contains the letters of Alexander the Great", which he says were written after Alexander's death; in one, "an old Indian man visited him in Jhelum after his war with Porus, and told him of two talking trees, one male and the other female at a little distance from Jhelum", and Alexander "then visited the trees and indeed found them to be talking to each other" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 9, ch. 3, folio not stated). His companion, the travel writer Salman Rashid, adds: "I have heard of a bodhi tree in Islamabad and one near Jhelum" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 9, ch. 3, folio not stated).

## Guru Nanak in the Wider Record

What the books say of Guru Nanak himself is said of the Guru, not of this site; it is gathered here because the gurdwara exists for his sake. Khalid gives his dates as 1469–1539 and describes him as "the first Guru" who "is believed to be the founder of Sikhism" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 314, folio 288). Dhillon calls the Janamsakhis "cycles of tales purportedly drawn from the life of the first Guru of the Sikhs" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 9, folio 9), has the Guru born "On 14 April 1469" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 17–18, folios 19–20), and gives his death as 22 September 1539 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 259, folio 287). Bhandari reports, with a "گویند", a birth "درسنه هزار و پانصد و بست و شش بکرماجیت مطابق هشتصد و هشتاد هجری درزمان سلطان بهلول لودی", and a death "درعهد سلطنت سلیم شاه افغان درعمر مابین هفتاد و هشتاد سالگی" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). Khalid writes that Nanak "spent a major portion of his life in present-day Pakistan. He was born here", and "eventually settled in a small village which is now on the Pakistani side of the border" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 16, folio not stated). In *A White Trail* he writes that "there are several sites all over the country where Nanak stayed or performed miracles" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 168–169, folio not stated).

Bhandari, a historian of Batala writing in Persian, calls the Guru "قافله سالار مسالک حقیقت و مشعله دار طریق طریقت" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). He travelled most of the world, Bhandari writes, then came to "قصبه بتاله", married, and settled "دردهی از دیهات بتاله برآب دریاے راوی", in a village of Batala on the Ravi; and "عالم عالم خلایق از اطراف ممالک آمده مرید شدند", people in their multitudes coming from every land to become his disciples (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). Passing over his son "لکهمیداس نام خلف رشید", he made "لهنه نام کهتری عرف ترهن[OCR?]" his successor with the title Guru Angad (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). Khalid, too, writes that "On his deathbed, he appointed not his son but his most loyal student as his spiritual successor" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 16, folio not stated).

Dhillon calls Nanak "a householder in every sense of the word" who "brought a message of peace and harmony to believers of the mutually hostile religions of Islam and Hinduism" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 12, folio not stated); in his summary the Guru taught that a good life rested on honest labour, compassion, selfless service and prayer (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 12–13, folio not stated / 13). Dhillon has him say, after vanishing for a week in the stream at Sultanpur, "There is only one God. There is no Hindu, no Mussalman." (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 45–46, folios 50–51). Khalid writes that he "offended both Hindus and Muslims by stating that there was no Hindu or Muslim" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 95, folio 77), and that he associated with "people of lowercastes, terming them people of God" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 82, folio 64).

Several writers set the miracle stories against what they take to be the Guru's own view. Dhillon writes of "Guru Nanak's clear and emphatic disavowal of the miraculous" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 11, folio 11). Khalid writes that Nanak "abhorred the concept of associating miracles with religious personalities", yet "his biography is nothing but a story of his miracles" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 16, folio not stated), and that "His genius lies in the poetic use of words and not in miracles" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 199, folio 173). "The only evidence we have is found within the Sikh religion and they are full of stories of miracles", Khalid writes; Iqbal Qaiser's advice was to "read what Nanak wrote at these places and try to get into his mind" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 286, folio 260).

Muslim writers and singers have claimed the Guru too. Khalid writes that "many Muslims, acquainted with the teachings of Guru Nanak, are devoted to him too and visit his shrine" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 220, folio not stated), and his informant Nadeem, a Muslim painter, says "Guru Nanak is like a Muslim Sufi Saint" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 219, folio not stated). A Muslim tradition, reported by Iqbal Qaiser, holds that "Guru Nanak was born by the blessing of Baba Naulakh Hazari"; "The Sikhs don't accept it" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated). Baba Farid "became an inspiration for Guru Nanak when he visited the shrine during his travels" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated), and Abbas writes that Farid's "texts are revered among the Sikhs because he influenced the founder of their own religion, Baba Guru Nanak" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 87, folio not stated). Rizvi argues that "all literate Panjabis, both Hindus and Muslims, at the time of Guru Nanak were conversant with" ʿAttar, Rumi and Saʿdi (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 420, folio 396). Robert Rozehnal reports that a twentieth-century Chishti Sabiri shaykh, in certain lectures, describes "non-Muslim spiritual luminaries—Krishna, Rama, Sita, Lachman, and Guru Nanak—as believers in the one true God and religious leaders with 'Divine connection' (nisbat)" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 63, folio 53). Omar Kasmani, citing Boivin, notes that the trope of the flower in milk is told also of "Guru Nanak, the spiritual leader and founder of Sikhism" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 195, folio 178, ch. 5 n. 17). At the shrine of the goddess Hinglaj, Jürgen Schaflechner records "the engraved footsteps of Guru Nanak" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 256, folio not stated). Bhandari, too, knows the Guru's followers as wandering *darvishes*: "چهارمین صنف اوداسی از معتقدان بابا نانک", the fourth class of the dervishes of Hind being the Udasis, devotees of Baba Nanak, whose worship is to sing their master's verses (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 567; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 566).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **Who built Rohtas Fort.** The site's account calls Sher Shah Suri the builder of Rohtas Fort. Bhandari has Sher Khan found the fort and his successor Islam Shah complete it (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 268), and in his section on the Gakhars says that Sher Shah and Islam Shah together raised it on the frontier (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 239).
- **What the gurdwara is called.** The site's account names it Gurdwara Choa Sahib, the "gurdwara of the exalted spring". Khalid, the only one of the books consulted to describe a gurdwara at Rohtas, calls it "Nanak's Gurdwara in Jhelum, [near] Rohtas Fort" and gives it no name (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 248, folio 222).
- **Which journey brought the Guru here.** The site's account places the visit in the fourth of his *udasis*. Dhillon reports five *udasis* in the Puratan Janamsakhi, the fourth in 1519–1521, and four in more modern accounts, the fourth in 1518–1521, and holds to four himself (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 54–55, folios 60–61; p. 55, folio 61; p. 56, folio 62). His account of the fourth journey's return names Tilla Balnath and Katasraj but not Rohtas (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 224–225, folios 246–247). Khalid gives the travels as about twenty-four years (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 54, folio 36); Dhillon's modern scheme spans twenty-three (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 55, folio 61).
- **The Guru's dates.** The site's account, Khalid and Dhillon give 1469–1539 (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 314, folio 288), Dhillon with a birth on 14 April 1469 and a death on 22 September 1539 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 17–18, folios 19–20; p. 259, folio 287). Bhandari gives the birth in Samvat 1526, 880 Hijri, in the reign of Bahlul Lodi, and the death under Salim Shah Afghan, between the ages of seventy and eighty (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).

## Legacy

Gurdwara Choa Sahib keeps at Rohtas the memory of a spring, a thirsty companion and a Guru on the road. The books consulted do not name it, but one of them shows how near it came to being lost: an abandoned building with a black powder on its walls, reached by Iqbal Qaiser through thorns and heat, and by Khalid at dusk beside a forest he was told was not safe (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 248, folio 222; p. 249, folio 223; p. 250, folio 224). Its reopening in 2019, seventy-two years after Partition, returned it to the pilgrims for whom it was built. Khalid, writing of the many places in Pakistan tied to the Guru, says that "Gurdwaras that were built there later by the devotees still stand tall" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 168–169, folio not stated); and he asks, of the Guru himself, whether "Nanak's legacy in its true essence is also slowly disappearing" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 230, folio 204).
