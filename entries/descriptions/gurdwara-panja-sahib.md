## Overview

Gurdwara Panja Sahib lies in the small town of Hasan Abdal, in the Attock District of Pakistan's Punjab, some fifty kilometres north-west of Rawalpindi. It is among the most revered of Sikh shrines, celebrated above all for the great boulder it enshrines, upon which — according to devout tradition — the open right hand of Guru Nanak, the founder of Sikhism, is miraculously imprinted. The name of the shrine derives from this relic: *panja* means "the palm" or "the open hand." Set beside spring-fed pools whose clear water flows over the marked rock, Panja Sahib is at once a place of miracle, a memorial of the Guru's travels through the north-west, and, in more recent times, the scene of a famous act of self-sacrifice.

Haroon Khalid, who reported on the Baisakhi festival here on 13 April 2011 (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 158, folio not stated) and spells the town Hassan Abdal, opens his chapter with the setting: "Surrounded by mountains on one side and the entire city of Hassan Abdal on the other, the Gurdwara Panja Sahib is one of the most important Sikh shrines in the country" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 157, folio not stated). "A few pilgrims visit all year round to pay their respects to the shrine associated with the founder of the Sikh religion, Guru Nanak (1469-1539)" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 157, folio not stated). He glosses the name in the same way as the site's account: "Panja meaning palm, in this case Nanak's palm" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 161, folio not stated). In his earlier travelogue *Walking with Nanak*, Khalid and Iqbal Qaiser look down from the mountain of Wali Kandhari, whom Khalid calls Wali Qandhari, on the gurdwara in the plain below, "the grand Gurdwara of Panja Sahib" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 267, folio 241; p. 269, folio 243).

The older histories say less. Harish Dhillon's retelling of the Janamsakhis names Hasan Abdal only as a place Guru Nanak passed through on his way home (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 224, folio 246). Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi's account of Guru Nanak's travels does not name Hasan Abdal at all (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 409–418, folios 385–394). Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* records an imperial halt, two burials and a garden at Hasan Abdal, and says nothing of Guru Nanak or of the Panja (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 182).

## Guru Nanak and Wali Kandhari

The legend attached to the site is traditionally set during one of Guru Nanak's great missionary journeys, or *udasis*, in the early sixteenth century — often dated to around 1521 — as he travelled in the company of his devoted Muslim minstrel, Bhai Mardana. Weary and thirsty, the Guru and his companion halted at the foot of a hill near Hasan Abdal. Upon its summit dwelt a Muslim *pir*, or holy man, remembered as Wali Kandhari — his name recalling an origin in Kandahar — who controlled the only spring in the vicinity.

By the traditional account, Mardana, parched, was sent up the hill to ask for water, and was rebuffed; twice more he climbed, and twice more the *pir* refused, taunting the pair. Then, it is said, Guru Nanak touched a stone or scooped at the ground below, and a fresh spring welled up at the foot of the hill, even as the water at the summit ran dry. Enraged, Wali Kandhari is said to have hurled — or set rolling — a great boulder down upon the Guru. Nanak raised his open hand and stopped the descending rock, which yielded to his palm as if it were soft wax, leaving the imprint of his *panja* upon it. Humbled by the miracle, the *pir* is remembered as having bowed before the Guru. Whatever the historical kernel of the tale, its message — that spiritual grace outmatches worldly pride, and that mercy answers arrogance — has made it one of the best-loved episodes of Sikh sacred lore.

Khalid gives the story as it was told at the festival. "About five hundred years ago, on the same date, the 1st day of Vaisakh, Guru Nanak came here", with two companions, "Bhai Mardana – a Muslim, and Bhai Bala – a Hindu"; the place "was not a city at that time", and the Guru settled "under a peepal tree, the very spot where the Gurdwara was built later" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 159, folio not stated). Mardana was sent three times up the mountain to Wali Kandhari for water (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 159, folio not stated).

In *Walking with Nanak* the legend is told in reply to a question from Anam; the printed text does not make clear who is speaking. Nanak "arrived here along with Mardana", and the two sat under a peepal tree where the "gurdwara now stands". Mardana climbed three times to ask the hermit for water and was refused, the third time asking "in the name of God". Nanak then "removed a stone from the ground and water started to gush out"; the Wali's water disappeared, and he "hurled a rock at Nanak and Mardana, which Nanak stopped with his hand. The print of that hand remained on the rock" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 269–270, folios 243–244). In this telling Wali Kandhari is the hermit who "felt jealous of Guru Nanak" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 270, folio 244).

Iqbal Qaiser, looking down from the Wali's mountain, doubted the physics of the tale: "if a rock was thrown from here, how could it possibly reach" the gurdwara below, and why should Nanak have needed to stop it with his hand (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 269, folio 243). The printed text of his remark is damaged.

The image has travelled far from Hasan Abdal. In the gurdwara at Manora in Karachi, Khalid saw a poster of Gurdwara Panja Sahib and another of Nanak stopping with his hand a rock hurled at him from the top of a mountain where the Muslim saint was (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 228, folio 202).

Khalid's reconstruction of the town's history adds a source for the date. In it, the fakir Naju tells the Sikh general Hari Singh that Nanak "came to Hassan Abdal and spent a few days here", as "recorded in the Janamsakhis of Bhai Bala", and Hari Singh's *granthi* confirms that Nanak had visited Hasan Abdal on the first of Vaisakh, as the Janamsakhis of Bala record (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 263, folio 237). Local tradition in the same scene has Nanak staying a few days under "a peepal tree on the outskirts of the city" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 264, folio 238).

In Dhillon's retelling of the Janamsakhis, Hasan Abdal comes on the return from the fourth *udasi*: from Mecca, Medina and Baghdad, the Guru came home by way of Iran, Kabul, Peshawar, Kandahar, Hasan Abdal, Tilla Balnath, Katasraj and Saidpur to Kartarpur (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 224–225, folios 246–247). His text says only that Nanak "passed through Kandahar (Afghanistan) and Hasan Abdal (Pakistan)"; it tells no story of the place (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 224, folio 246). Of the two schemes of the *udasis* he sets out, the more modern one dates the fourth journey, to the Arab countries, to 1518–1521 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 55, folio 61).

## Another Story of the Hand: Naju and Kamma

Khalid sets against the legend a counter-narrative of his own, which he writes as a novelised chapter, "The Legend", and presents as his reconstruction rather than as a record. On "the eve of the Sikh invasion", with news of "Hari Singh Nalwa's approach", the people of the town flee; only the fakir Naju stays, and he is found "sitting at the shrine" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 260, folio 234). A month earlier Naju had watched "a Muslim mason called Kamma" carve the outline of his own hand into a rock, "like a signature that he was leaving for posterity" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 262–263, folios 236–237). Kamma "had been responsible for creating numerous carvings in the temples of Hassan Abdal"; his grandfather had become a Muslim "To avoid the jaziya tax that the Mughal Emperor Aurangzeb had reinitiated" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 262, folio 236).

In the reconstruction, Naju has only "a vague idea that the Sikhs of Punjab considered him to be their Prophet" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 264, folio 238). He gives the general the rock as bearing "the hand print of your Guru Nanak" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 263, folio 237). Hari Singh answers, "See how the hand of Nanak fits my hand perfectly", kisses the rock and lays gold coins on it; "Even while it was at the camp, the rock turned into a shrine" for "Muslims, Hindus and Sikhs" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 264, folio 238). He then orders "the rock to be placed there, under the tree", and a shrine built to mark the spot where Nanak and Mardana had rested (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 265, folio 239).

At the rock itself, years later, Khalid put his own hand on the print, "at that moment genuinely believing". He adds: "I knew this was a fictitious story dreamed up by a man called Naju the fakir to save his life, while the handprint" had been cut by a man called Kamma (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 271, folio 245).

## The Gurdwara

A deep imprint resembling a right hand is shown upon the rock, over which spring water continually flows, and pilgrims come to touch and reverence it. The place is said to have been named Panja Sahib by Hari Singh Nalwa, the renowned general of Maharaja Ranjit Singh's Sikh Empire, who is credited with raising the first *gurdwara* here in the early nineteenth century. The shrine that developed around the relic incorporates prayer halls and a *sarovar*, or sacred tank, fed by the springs, its waters gathering into pools where devotees bathe. The *Guru Granth Sahib* is installed within, and the customary *langar* is served. The setting — clear water, shade trees and the hill rising behind — has long been noted for its serenity.

Khalid gives two accounts of the building's history in the same chapter. In one, "This place became a site of devotion after the miracle of Nanak. It was then extended during the tenure of the Sikh ruler of Punjab, Maharaja Ranjit Singh (1780-1839)" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 159, folio not stated); in the other, the fading frescoes inside are "part of the original complex built by Maharaja Ranjit Singh" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 167, folio not stated). In *Walking with Nanak* a gurdwara was built here in the time of Maharaja Ranjit Singh, after which the old pool remained sacred but was given a new iconography drawn from Nanak's miracles (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 270, folio 244). Standing at the rock, Khalid notes that the gurdwara built in the nineteenth century was behind him; some words of the sentence are lost in the printed text (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 271, folio 245).

Writing of the province rather than of this site, Khalid records that "When Maharaja Ranjit Singh became the sovereign of Punjab at the end of the eighteenth century he spent large amounts of money on the renovation and expansion of many gurdwaras", and "attached vast tracts of land to these gurdwaras, so that the revenues from the lands could facilitate their functioning" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 34, folio 16). In *A White Trail* he says that Ranjit Singh renovated "many Gurdwaras all over Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 208, folio not stated).

As Khalid saw it in 2011, "The main shrine is a splendid grey building adorned with three white and yellow domes on the top". Colourful cloths were tied to a railing around it, and double-storey buildings on all four sides "contain rooms for the pilgrims to stay" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 159, folio not stated). The façade was pink, and the inside was closed off by an iron gate (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 158, folio not stated). "There are four entrances into the main shrine, one from all sides, symbolizing the openness of the religion" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 167, folio not stated). The interior was "decorated with gaudy glass patterns put up recently by the government of Pakistan as renovation work to cover the fading frescoes"; a wooden palanquin stood in the centre, a young Pathan Sikh read the Guru Granth Sahib, and pilgrims rested (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 167, folio not stated).

"The sacred pool said to have been created after the miracle of Nanak lies in the shadow of the main shrine"; Khalid calls it 'turquoise coloured' (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 160, folio not stated). White marble stairs lead into it, carrying the names of donors from Canada and the United States (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 160, folio not stated). "Water still gushes into this pool through a stream, flowing into the complex from under the protective walls recently raised by the Government of Pakistan", and newly built stairs lead down to the stream (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 161, folio not stated). The stream comes out behind the residential quarters and then flows out of the complex (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 170, folio not stated).

The rock faces the main shrine, inside the pool: "in a small niche whose protective arch is made of ivory, there is a grey stone", partly under water, with "a black mark in the middle of the rock that looks like a hand print". There is a window on each side, through which women offer their devotion, and "A marble plaque next to it describes its significance in Gurmukhi" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 160–161, folio not stated).

Behind the main shrine is the *kirtan* hall. At its entrances "statues of two Sikh soldiers stand guard holding spears, dressed up in yellow clothes and blue turbans"; the stage carries "a picture of this particular Gurdwara in the centre and that of Guru Gobind Singh on the top left corner", and behind the reader hangs "a picture of Guru Gobind Singh collecting money for Langar" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 169, folio not stated). A staircase leads underground to two spacious corridors where pilgrims without rooms live (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 169, folio not stated). "The Langar Hall is a newly built double storey construction, situated behind these residential quarters" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 170, folio not stated). An alley ends in an open space "surrounded by more buildings for the residence of the pilgrims", and a garden covered by tents gives makeshift rooms (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 166–167, folio not stated).

The pool, in Khalid's other book, is far older than the gurdwara. The speaker, whose name is partly lost in the printed text, cites "Salman Rashid's book The Salt Range and the Potohar Plateau" for the view that "this pool of water next to the gurdwara" is thousands of years old and was 'considered sacred even in the Buddhist era'. "The Chinese traveller Hiuen Tsang, in the 7th century CE", reportedly saw it as 'a sacred lake', a dip in which was believed to cure diseases (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 270, folio 244).

## The Saka of 1922

To the memory of the Guru's miracle, Panja Sahib later added the memory of martyrdom. On 30 October 1922, at the height of the Gurdwara Reform Movement, a train carrying Sikh prisoners — arrested during the *morcha*, or non-violent agitation, at Guru ka Bagh near Amritsar — was to pass through Hasan Abdal railway station. The local *sangat* wished to serve *langar* to the captives, but were told the train would not stop. Refusing to let their brethren pass unfed, Bhai Pratap Singh and Bhai Karam Singh, leading the congregation, lay down upon the rails, and others followed. The driver braked hard, but not before the engine had struck the foremost of them. Bhai Karam Singh, a man of about thirty, and Bhai Pratap Singh, some twenty-four years old, died of their injuries and were hailed as martyrs. This episode, the *Saka* Panja Sahib, entered Sikh memory as a supreme example of devotion to the duty of feeding and serving others, and until the Partition of 1947 an annual fair was held at the shrine in the martyrs' honour.

The books consulted do not describe the events of 1922. Two of them speak to their background, without naming this site. Khalid, writing of the province under British rule, says that "As a result of sectarian divisions between the Sikh communities and the takeover of Punjab by the British, a large number of important Sikh Gurdwaras were being administered by Hindu mahants" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 189, folio not stated). And Bhandari, writing of the followers of Baba Nanak, says that they count service of the traveller, of all who come and go, as the highest worship, and that whoever arrives at midnight speaking Baba Nanak's name is treated as a brother and a friend and served, even a stranger, a thief or a highwayman (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 519). "It is believed that the tradition of Langar began with Guru Nanak", Khalid writes (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 188, folio not stated).

## Baisakhi at Hasan Abdal

Khalid's chapter on the festival, "Baisakhi at Hassan Abdal", is the fullest account of the shrine's devotional life in these books (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 5, folio not stated). "For three days and nights in the month of April, everything changes here." The streets are cordoned off, "no Muslim is allowed to venture near the Gurdwara", and local schools close, their classrooms turned into hostels for the pilgrims (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 157, folio not stated). Pilgrims spend the 13th, 14th and 15th of April here, and most then go on to the other holy Sikh shrines; the market fills with "thousands of turbaned Sikhs", most of them Indian Punjabis (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 157, folio not stated).

Not all of them come from across the border. "Most of the Sikhs here are fair skinned and blue eyed Pathans, from the regions of Khyber-Pakthunkhwa and tribal areas and they are more alien to this place than the Indian Punjabi Sikhs"; a third group are clean-shaven Hindus from different regions of Pakistan (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 158, folio not stated). Some walk the tar road "without shoes as an expression of extreme respect" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 158, folio not stated). "According to local media reports, about seven thousand pilgrims have arrived for the festival this year- about two thousand from India and the rest from within Pakistan"; Khalid counted more than two hundred people in the line at the entrance (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 158, folio not stated).

In *Walking with Nanak*, too, it was "Baisakhi and the festival was only beginning", with Sikh pilgrims gathered to commemorate the formation of the Khalsa (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 271, folio 245). At Baisakhi, when thousands "descend upon this shrine, several also travel up to this Muslim shrine" of Wali Kandhari on the mountain above (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 267, folio 241). The festival calendar at the Wali's shrine records Baisakhi, which coincides with the festival at the shrine of Guru Nanak at the bottom of the mountain; the printed text here is badly damaged (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 269, folio 243). In a third book Khalid reports a boy named Amir as saying that Sikhs "come from India and other parts of the world to Hassan Abdal" on the occasion of Vaisakhi, and that they also visit the gurdwara at Mandi Mangat (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).

Entry is guarded. A police officer checks everyone's security card. "Even until last year", Khalid writes, a turban or head cover was enough, and those who looked more 'Muslim' were turned away only if they were Muslim; "This year however, the security has been intensified", and everyone needs a card from the Special Branch. "Even till recently, Muslims were allowed to pay their respects to the shrine of Baba Guru Nanak, much in line with the Sikh philosophy but not anymore" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 158, folio not stated). A television crew that threatened to broadcast its treatment was let through (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 158, folio not stated). Anyone may enter a gurdwara "according to the tradition, a practice that doesn't exist in Pakistan anymore though" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 167, folio not stated).

Inside, *kirtan* with harmonium and *tabla*, in Punjabi, carries across the complex (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 159, folio not stated). It "usually includes verses from the Sikh holy book, the Guru Granth Sahib", a tradition that goes "back to Nanak's Muslim devotee, Bhai Mardana" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 160, folio not stated). Listeners offer notes and coins to the musicians (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 169, folio not stated). At the Granth, devotees wave a fly-whisk, "a tradition that comes from Bhai Bala, the Hindu devotee of Guru Nanak"; the final chapters are read, marking the conclusion of the *Akhand Path* (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 168, folio not stated).

At the pool, men stand praying in the water, an old man swims, and children hang back on the stairs, "reluctant, perhaps due to the sacredness of the water" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 160, folio not stated). Volunteers pass buckets of pool water to wash the white marble floor of the courtyard — "a sacred duty called sewa" — and cook and serve at the Langar Hall (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 160, folio not stated). Devotees queue for 'the darshan of the stone', place a hand on the mark while pouring water over it, and some kiss it (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 160–161, folio not stated). A photograph in the book is captioned: "A pilgrim placing his hand on the hand print of Guru Nanak on the sacred rock at Gurdwara Panja Sahib, Hassan Abdal on the occasion of Baisakhi" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 109, folio not stated).

Khalid watched the same gesture in *Walking with Nanak*. A pilgrim in his sixties, in a saffron headscarf, put his hand on the mark and found that his hand too fitted Nanak's — 'Miracle!' — then laid a ten-rupee note on the rock, kissed it, poured water from the pool over it and cleaned it. Devotees queued behind him, hands reached out from the windows on either side of the rock, and women placed money on top of it (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 271, folio 245).

Some keep watch over the water. Surjeet Singh, a thirty-five-year-old Pathan Sikh from Peshawar whose family came from a village in the Khyber Agency, chases boys out of the pool — 'This is no place to play!' — as his way of performing *sewa* (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 161–162, folio not stated). His shirt reads 'Never forget 1984', with the image of Sant Jarnail Singh Bhindranwale (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 161–162, folio not stated); "This shirt represents the passion of Baisakhi", Khalid writes (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 164, folio not stated).

The procession, the *Nagar Kirtan*, has been drawn in. Avtar Kaur, fifty-five, from Amritsar, told Khalid: "I have always wanted to come to Pakistan. This is the land of Guru Nanak" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 168–169, folio not stated), and "here I have heard that the procession will not be allowed to leave the premises of this Gurdwara. That is sad" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 169, folio not stated). "Till a few years ago, the procession marched around the city of Hassan Abdal" as local Muslims looked on; now, "due to the growing threat from the Islamic extremists", it "remains within the protected confines of the Gurdwara" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 169, folio not stated).

"Usually, foreign pilgrims are accommodated in rooms, while Pakistanis are allowed to settle wherever there is space" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 169, folio not stated). In the underground corridors, older Indian pilgrims recalled 'the Khalistan days of insurgency': Harvinder Singh, sixty-five, whose shop in Lajpat Nagar in Delhi had been burnt, Jasdeep Singh, fifty-four, and Virinder Kaur, fifty-five, from a village in Amritsar District (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 170, folio not stated). Sikh boys served *doodh-soda* at a stall, and volunteers guarded the crowded Langar Hall (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 170, folio not stated). Maninder Singh, a forty-four-year-old photographer from Amritsar, said 'I actually belong to Pakistan': his parents came from a village in Gujrat and from Shah Alamgir, and on his visit for Guru Nanak's festival the year before he had taken a brick from his mother's ancestral house (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 171, folio not stated).

The festival is also a market. A T-shirt reading 'Never forget 1984' sold well at a stall behind the main shrine, kept by a boy from the south of the country, and a woman in a sari bargained over it — 'Two hundred only' … 'One-fifty. Final price.' — before confessing 'I don't understand this currency' (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 165–166, folio not stated). On sale were CDs of *Sikh Gurdwaras in Pakistan*, which Khalid calls "a government sanctioned propaganda documentary", a poster of all the functioning gurdwaras of Pakistan, and Ravinder Singh's latest collection of *kirtan* (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 166, folio not stated). From behind an iron gate a man threw *nimko* into the crowd (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 166, folio not stated). Vendors sold "head covers with Khalsa symbols, plates for aarti, posters of Hindu deities and Sikh Gurus", and "Most of these vendors are Pakistanis who travel all over the country throughout the year attending Hindu and Sikh festivals" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 166–167, folio not stated). One of them, Vishal Chand, said "I belong to the city of Multan but I live in Quetta (Baluchistan)"; almost all his goods "are from India, brought by their friends and relatives" on pilgrimage (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 167, folio not stated). At Nankana Sahib, Khalid notes that the T-shirt stalls were like those at Hasan Abdal, and that Vishal Nasir Chand had been selling religious goods at Hasan Abdal (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 229, folio not stated).

## The Sikhs of Hasan Abdal

Hasan Abdal is "home to about fifty to sixty Sikh families, who have moved here in the last few years from the tribal areas due to the rise of insurgency there, while the rest of the city is Muslim". Most of them speak only Pashto, and they "are mostly confined within the boundaries of the Gurdwara, protected by police officials, tall walls and barbed wires on all sides" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 157, folio not stated). Elsewhere in the same book Khalid writes that Sikh families from the tribal areas "moved to Peshawar, Hassan Abdal and Nankana Sahib, and started living at Gurdwaras" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 175, folio not stated).

## Setting: Hasan Abdal

Khalid's Hasan Abdal "is a historic city which has managed to retain its character", with narrow, curving streets and wooden balconies, and it sits on hillocks (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 157, folio not stated). A few Hindu temples survive but "are now being used as improvised houses or in some cases wandering grounds for stray animals" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 157, folio not stated). "The remains of a Mughal garden in a dilapidated state still survive facing the Gurdwara", a garden Khalid says is mentioned in the *Ain-i-Akbari*, written "during the reign of the third Mughal Emperor, Akbar (1542-1605) by Abu-Fazl" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 157, folio not stated).

Bhandari's *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* records a Mughal halt here. Returning from Kashmir by Pakhli and Damtur (the reading of both names is uncertain in the printed text), the emperor, with his retinue, his servants and his elephants, made his auspicious halt at Hasan Abdal (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 182). Two of his intimates who died on that road, Mir Fathullah Shirazi and, before him, Hakim Abu'l-Fath Gilani, were buried at Hasan Abdal (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 182). The royal standards rested there for some time and a delightful garden was laid out before the court left for Kabul (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 182). Bhandari says nothing here of Guru Nanak or the Panja.

Khalid's reconstruction of the town on the eve of the Sikh conquest takes Naju from "the street of the temple into the street of the Imambargah", past the old main bazaar. "This was the connecting town between Punjab and the Khyber"; its people abandoned it as the Sikhs advanced, Hari Singh Nalwa camped on the Grand Trunk Road outside, and the town stood on a small mountain (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 258–259, folios 232–233). The residents left for the town of Attock (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 260, folio 234). From the peepal tree Hari Singh could see "gardens that had been constructed by Emperor Jahangir" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 264–265, folios 238–239). In the same chapter Khalid has the Sikh soldiers arrive "from the Punjab", and writes that "For almost a thousand year[s] invaders had crossed into the Punjab from the West" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 258, folio 232).

Wali Kandhari's own shrine stands on the mountain above the gurdwara (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 267, folio 241). It is a separate Muslim shrine; at Baisakhi several of the pilgrims to the gurdwara travel up to it as well (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 267, folio 241).

## Guru Nanak in the Wider Record

What the books say of Guru Nanak himself is said of the Guru, not of this site; it is gathered here because the shrine exists for his sake. Khalid gives his dates as 1469–1539 (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 157, folio not stated), and so does Dhillon, who calls the Janamsakhis "cycles of tales purportedly drawn from the life of the first Guru of the Sikhs" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 9, folio 9). Dhillon has him born "On 14 April 1469" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 17–18, folios 19–20) and gives the date of his death as 22 September 1539 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 259, folio 287). Nur Ahmad Chishti's Urdu *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* gives the birth as 14–15 April 1469, at Talwandi in the district of Lahore, though the day, the year and the place-name are all uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 685); in a table elsewhere it puts the birth in the month of Katak, Samvat 1526 of Bikramajit, a reading that is also uncertain in print (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 515). Bhandari reports, as something people say, a birth in the year 1526 of Bikramajit, corresponding to 880 Hijri, in the time of Sultan Bahlul Lodi, and a death in the reign of Salim Shah Afghan, at an age between seventy and eighty (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).

Dhillon sets out two schemes of the *udasis*. "According to the Puratan Janamsakhi… he undertook five journeys", the last in 1523–1524 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 54–55, folios 60–61); "More modern accounts" give four, spanning twenty-three years, the fourth, to the Arab countries, in 1518–1521 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 55, folio 61). Dhillon himself holds that there were four (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 56, folio 62). Khalid gives the travels as some twenty-four years, mostly on foot, east to Bengal, south to Sri Lanka, north to Kashmir, Nepal and Tibet, and west to Mecca (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 54, folio 36). Nanak "preferred to stay on the outskirts" of villages, Khalid writes, and this is "a recurrent theme" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 53, folio 35); in Khalid's reconstruction he would rather sleep under trees than in caravanserais (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 120, folio 102). "Whenever he would visit an area he would visit its religious shrines, Muslim, Hindu and of any other denomination" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 37, folio 19).

His two companions in the Panja Sahib story have their own places in Sikh devotion. Khalid calls Bhai Bala "the Hindu devotee of Guru Nanak" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 168, folio not stated), and describes paintings, popular in Sikh hagiography, of "a serene Nanak listening to the songs of Bhai Mardana holding a rubab ... while Bhai Bala stands behind them holding a fly-whisk" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 160, folio not stated). In Bhandari's Persian, a singer named Mardana sang the Guru's verses and drew people into the snare of devotion (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).

The miracle at the heart of this shrine sits uneasily with what several writers say of the Guru. Dhillon writes of "Guru Nanak's clear and emphatic disavowal of the miraculous" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 11, folio 11). Khalid writes that Nanak "abhorred the concept of associating miracles with religious personalities", yet "his biography is nothing but a story of his miracles" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 16, folio not stated). Khalid records legends in which Nanak faces a Muslim Sufi: Hamza Ghous, who threatened to destroy Sialkot, and whose people Nanak saved (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 197–198, folios 171–172), and Mian Mitha of Pasrur, "an arrogant Muslim Sufi" who became Nanak's devotee (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 200–201, folios 174–175). Khalid calls such tales "pointless stories" and writes that "His genius lies in the poetic use of words and not in miracles"; Iqbal Qaiser's comment is, "That is to show that our saint is more powerful than yours" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 199, folio 173). In Dhillon's retelling the Guru tells Karori, who calls his cure a miracle, that "there is no such thing as a miracle" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 190, folio 209).

Another impression of the Guru's body is shown at the shrine of the goddess Hinglaj. At Hinglaj, Jürgen Schaflechner records "the engraved footsteps of Guru Nanak" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 256, folio not stated), and writes that "The hollowed-out footsteps of Guru Nanak connect Hinglaj to the Sikh and Nanak paṃth (Hin. sect) traditions" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 257, folio not stated).

Muslim writers and singers have claimed the Guru too. Khalid writes that "many Muslims, acquainted with the teachings of Guru Nanak, are devoted to him too and visit his shrine", (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 220, folio not stated), and his informant Nadeem, a Muslim painter, says "Guru Nanak is like a Muslim Sufi Saint" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 219, folio not stated). Robert Rozehnal reports that a twentieth-century Chishti Sabiri shaykh, in certain lectures, describes "non-Muslim spiritual luminaries—Krishna, Rama, Sita, Lachman, and Guru Nanak—as believers in the one true God and religious leaders with 'Divine connection' (nisbat)" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 63, folio 53). Shemeem Burney Abbas writes that Baba Farid's "texts are revered among the Sikhs because he influenced the founder of their own religion, Baba Guru Nanak" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 87, folio not stated). Scott Kugle places the Sufi poet Shah Hussayn in "the tradition of Kabir and Guru Nanak" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 329, folio 308, ch. 4 n. 51). Omar Kasmani, citing Michel Boivin, notes that the trope of the flower in milk is told also of "Guru Nanak, the spiritual leader and founder of Sikhism" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 195, folio 178, ch. 5 n. 17).

## In Verse

The shrine's own worship is sung. The *kirtan* at Panja Sahib "usually includes verses from the Sikh holy book, the Guru Granth Sahib" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 160, folio not stated), and Dhillon notes that Nanak's *Bani* "was compiled as part of the Adi Granth by Guru Arjan Dev, the fifth Guru, in 1604" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 9, folio 9). Bhandari, writing in Persian, says that the Guru composed verses in Hindi on the truth of God, setting out divine unity in clear words and shining metaphors (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520), and that his followers' worship centres on reading their master's verses, which they recite with song and melody (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 519). Abbas's glossary defines the *banī* by him: "Devotional verses such as those of Guru Nanak; verses of supplication, entreaty, humility, and reverence" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 179, folio not stated).

Khalid quotes the Guru's verse throughout *Walking with Nanak*, in published English translations. None of the lines he quotes is tied to Hasan Abdal; they are given here as the poetry of the Guru the shrine remembers. The *aarti*, sung in praise of a God whom Iqbal Qaiser reads as residing in nature, begins in one translation:

*The sky the salver, the sun and moon the lamps …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 55, folio 37)

and in another:

*In the salver of the firmament, / The sun and the moon shine as lamps* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 243–244, folios 217–218)

On the oneness of God:

*My Master is One / He is One, brother, the only One* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 60, folio 42)

*God is One, / The Path is One* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 144, folio 126)

The poem on the sacred thread, the *janeu*, which in Khalid's reconstruction the boy Nanak refused:

*From the cotton of compassion, / Spin the thread of contentment* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 61–62, folios 43–44)

On washing:

*As hands or feet besmirched with slime, / Water washes white* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 192, folio 166)

The song Nanak sings, in Khalid's reconstruction, to the innkeepers who meant to rob him:

*The herons in their white feathers dwell in the sacred shrines of pilgrimage* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 123–126, folios 105–108)

Other openings Khalid quotes: *Launch ye the boat of austere contemplation …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 148–149, folios 130–131); *As a beggar goes a-begging …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 158, folio 140); and *His goodness cannot be priced or traded …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 280, folio 254).

The first poem of Nanak that Khalid read was the *Babur Bani*, "a long poem that Nanak wrote at the time the Mughal marauder Babur attacked Punjab, destroying the cities of Eminabad and Lahore on the way" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 15, folio not stated). Khalid quotes from it the line 'Coming in seventy-eight (1521 C.E)' (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 113–114, folios 95–96).

The Guru's verse is sung outside the gurdwaras too. Abbas quotes Abida Parvin: "I sing Shah Hussain, Bulle Shah, Mian Muhammad Bakhsh, Hazrat Khawaja Ghulam Farid, Sacchal Sarmast, Baba Farid, and Guru Nanak" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 54, folio not stated). In Sindh, Boivin records, "Sain Vasan Shah became famous for reciting the banis of Shri Guru Nanak and the kalams of the Sufis" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 68, folio 55), and he reports Ajwani's judgement that "Guru Nanak was the greatest poet of the Bhakti" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 90, folio 77).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **When the Guru came.** The site's account sets the visit on an *udasi* in the early sixteenth century, "often dated to around 1521". Khalid's festival account says "About five hundred years ago, on the same date, the 1st day of Vaisakh" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 159, folio not stated); in his reconstruction a *granthi* cites the Janamsakhis of Bala for the first of Vaisakh, with no year (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 263, folio 237). Dhillon puts Hasan Abdal on the return from the fourth *udasi* (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 224–225, folios 246–247), which the modern scheme he summarises dates 1518–1521 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 55, folio 61).
- **Who came with him.** The site's account names Bhai Mardana alone. Khalid's festival account names "Bhai Mardana – a Muslim, and Bhai Bala – a Hindu" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 159, folio not stated); *Walking with Nanak* names Mardana only (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 269–270, folios 243–244).
- **Where he sat.** The site's account has the Guru and Mardana halt at the foot of the hill. Khalid has them under a peepal tree, "the very spot where the Gurdwara was built later" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 159, folio not stated); in his reconstruction the tree is on the outskirts of the city (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 264, folio 238).
- **How the spring rose.** The site's account says the Guru "touched a stone or scooped at the ground below". In *Walking with Nanak* he "removed a stone from the ground and water started to gush out" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 269–270, folios 243–244).
- **How old the pool is.** Khalid's festival account calls it the pool "said to have been created after the miracle of Nanak" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 160, folio not stated). In *Walking with Nanak* a speaker, citing Salman Rashid, says the pool is thousands of years old, was sacred in the Buddhist era and was seen by Hiuen Tsang in the seventh century (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 270, folio 244).
- **The boulder.** The site's account has Wali Kandhari hurl "or set rolling" a great boulder down upon the Guru. *Walking with Nanak* has him hurl a rock at Nanak and Mardana (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 269–270, folios 243–244); Iqbal Qaiser doubts that a rock thrown from the mountain could reach the gurdwara at all (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 269, folio 243).
- **The handprint.** The site's account gives the imprint "according to devout tradition" as the Guru's. Khalid's reconstruction, which he presents as his own, has it carved by the mason Kamma and passed to Hari Singh Nalwa by the fakir Naju, and Khalid writes that he knew the story to be "fictitious" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 262–263, folios 236–237; p. 264, folio 238; p. 271, folio 245).
- **Who built the gurdwara.** The site's account credits Hari Singh Nalwa with raising the first *gurdwara* in the early nineteenth century and with naming the place. Khalid's festival account says the site was extended under Maharaja Ranjit Singh (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 159, folio not stated) and that the original complex was built by Ranjit Singh (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 167, folio not stated). *Walking with Nanak* has a gurdwara built in Ranjit Singh's time (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 270, folio 244), while its reconstruction has Hari Singh order the rock placed under the tree and a shrine built (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 265, folio 239). None of the books says who named the place.
- **The Wali's name.** The site's account writes Wali Kandhari; Khalid writes Wali Qandhari (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 267, folio 241; Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 159, folio not stated).
- **Who may enter.** Khalid writes that "Even till recently" Muslims could pay their respects at the shrine, and that at his 2011 visit they no longer could (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 158, folio not stated); in *Walking with Nanak*, by contrast, Khalid himself put his hand on the print at the rock (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 271, folio 245). The books do not give the year of that visit.
- **A vendor's name.** Khalid calls the same trader "Vishal Chand" at Hasan Abdal (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 167, folio not stated) and "Vishal Nasir Chand" at Nankana Sahib (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 229, folio not stated).
- **The Guru's dates.** Khalid and Dhillon give 1469–1539, Dhillon with a birth on 14 April 1469 and a death on 22 September 1539 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 17–18, folios 19–20; p. 259, folio 287). Chishti gives the birth as 14–15 April 1469 at Talwandi in one place (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 685) and as Kartik of Samvat 1526 in a table (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 515); Bhandari gives Samvat 1526, 880 Hijri, in the reign of Bahlul Lodi, and a death under Salim Shah Afghan between the ages of seventy and eighty (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). Chishti places the death at Kartarpur in his table (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 515) and at Kiratpur on the Ravi elsewhere (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 685), the readings of both names uncertain in the printed text.
- **How many journeys.** Dhillon reports five *udasis* in the Puratan Janamsakhi and four in more modern accounts, and holds to four himself (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 54–55, folios 60–61; p. 56, folio 62). Khalid gives the travels as about twenty-four years (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 54, folio 36); Dhillon's modern scheme spans twenty-three (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 55, folio 61).

## Legacy

Since 1947 Panja Sahib has lain within Pakistan, cared for by the Evacuee Trust Property Board and the Pakistan Sikh Gurdwara Prabandhak Committee, and it remains one of the principal destinations for the Sikh *jathas* who travel from India and the diaspora on the great anniversaries — above all the Baisakhi festival, when the shrine draws large and joyful congregations. Uniting a miracle of the founder Guru with a modern tale of sacrifice, and a marked rock with living springs, Gurdwara Panja Sahib holds an honoured and enduring place among the sacred sites of the Sikh faith.

Khalid counts "Baisakhi at Hassan Abdal" among the few religious events 'kept alive by the foreign pilgrims' after Partition (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 176, folio not stated). Writing of Pakistan as a whole, he says that Nanak was born in present-day Nankana Sahib and died at Kartarpur, and that "there are several sites all over the country where Nanak stayed or performed miracles. Gurdwaras that were built there later by the devotees still stand tall" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 168–169, folio not stated). For other such places after the division of the Punjab he is less hopeful: "I have seen several gurdwaras and temples being taken over and destroyed by the migrants" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated).
