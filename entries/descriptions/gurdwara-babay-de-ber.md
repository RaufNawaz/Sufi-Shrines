## Overview

Gurdwara Babay De Ber — the "gurdwara of the elder's jujube tree," also known as Gurdwara Beri Sahib or Babe di Ber — stands in the old city of Sialkot, near the ancient fort, and marks a spot hallowed by the visit of *Guru Nanak Dev Ji* during his travels through the north-western Punjab. Its name derives from the *ber*, the jujube tree, beneath which the Guru is remembered to have sat, and a venerable tree, held by tradition to be more than five centuries old, still grows within the gurdwara's precincts as the sacred focus of the shrine. It is among the historic Sikh sites of Pakistan that have been restored and reopened for pilgrimage in recent years.

Of the books consulted, only one describes the place itself. Haroon Khalid's travelogue *Walking with Nanak* follows the Guru's route to a gurdwara in Sialkot that he calls "Baba Bair (the shrine of the Berry saint)", in a quarter "now known as the mohalla of Baba Bair" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 193–194, folios 167–168; p. 194, folio 168). Khalid never prints the name Babay De Ber; his Baba Bair is taken to be this gurdwara on the strength of the name and the city. Harish Dhillon's retelling of the Janamsakhis has the Guru set up camp at Sialkot "at the foot of an old beri (jujube) tree", but names no gurdwara there (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 137, folio 152). The other books speak of Guru Nanak or of Sialkot, not of this site, and what they say is given below as context for the Guru and the city.

## Guru Nanak and Hamza Ghaus

The tradition attached to the site concerns Guru Nanak's arrival at Sialkot, which he is said to have reached in the course of his great journeys, coming by way of Jammu. According to the account preserved in Sikh memory, he found the city gripped by fear, for it was believed to lie under the threat of destruction on account of a broken promise made to a Muslim holy man of Sialkot named *Hamza Ghaus*, who had withdrawn in anger to meditate and whose wrath the townsfolk dreaded. The Guru seated himself beneath a *ber* tree close to the place where Hamza was absorbed in his devotions and began to sing aloud the divine word.

The sound is said to have disturbed the meditation of Hamza Ghaus, who came out and demanded to know who this was, and why he should intercede for a city of liars and promise-breakers. To this Guru Nanak is remembered to have replied that it was not just to punish a whole city for the fault of a single man — a teaching of mercy and proportion that, in the telling, moved the holy man and lifted the shadow from Sialkot. The episode belongs to the recurring pattern of the *sakhis*, in which the Guru's travels bring him into dialogue with the Muslim and Hindu holy men of the places he visits, and in which his words turn anger and division towards compassion.

Dhillon retells the story as "The Faqir's Curse", the tenth chapter of his book, and places it on the Guru's second *udasi*, between Lahore and Bhatinda (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 136–159; p. 161, folio 177). In his telling Sialkot is a city "inhabited predominantly by Muslims", with a powerful Hindu trading presence (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 137, folio 152). Nanak "set up base at the foot of an old beri (jujube) tree" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 137, folio 152). The faqir Hamza Ghaus pronounces his curse in the centre of the market square (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 150, folio 165). In the end the faqir himself comes towards the *beri* tree, and the two sit "side by side in the shade of the tree" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 153–154, folios not stated). When the Guru left, almost the entire citizenry of Sialkot was there to see him on his way (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 159, folio 174).

Khalid tells the legend too, spelling the name Hamza Ghous. Sialkot "was gripped by fear" of the Sufi, who had told the people of Sialkot that he would pray for forty days; the quarrel had begun when a Hindu man broke a promise to give him his first-born son (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 197–198, folios 171–172). Hamza Ghous threatened to destroy the city when his forty days of prayer were done. Nanak, under the berry tree, knocked on his door for days; thunder struck the dome, and so, in Khalid's words, "Baba Guru Nanak saved the people of Sialkot" from annihilation (the last word is misprinted in the text) (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 197–198, folios 171–172).

The shrine of Hamza Ghous itself, Khalid writes, lies in an old graveyard not far from "the berry tree under which Nanak sat" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 199, folio 173).

Khalid does not believe the story, and says so. He calls such tales "pointless stories" and writes of Nanak that "His genius lies in the poetic use of words and not in miracles"; his companion Iqbal Qaiser comments: "That is to show that our saint is more powerful than yours" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 199, folio 173). He sets beside it a second legend from Pasrur, about thirty kilometres from Sialkot, which he calls "similar to that of Sialkot": there Nanak humbles "an arrogant Muslim Sufi by the name of Mian Mitha", who threatens to "churn him"; Nanak will squeeze him "as one squeezes a lemon"; Mian Mitha comes barefoot, is answered "in Islamic iconography", touches and kisses Nanak's feet (the printed text has "hissed") and becomes his devotee. "This discourse is recorded in the Guru Granth Sahib", Khalid notes (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 200–201, folios 174–175; pp. 199–200, folios 173–174).

## Nanak Under the Berry Tree

Khalid's book is in part a reconstruction of the Guru's travels, told as a story, and he says plainly that this part of the book is fictionalised (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 17, folio not stated). In that reconstruction Nanak and Mardana reach Sialkot on foot from Eminabad, and Nanak "spotted a [OCR?] berry tree not far from the city and decided to rest under it"; "There were a few scattered houses and shops around" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 185, folio 159). Sialkot, he writes, is "the city in the foothills of Jamin [OCR?] [Jammu]" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 185, folio 159).

The tree, in the reconstruction, is already a place of prayer. A young Hindu woman comes with her daughter, ties a cloth to a branch and circles the tree chanting "Vedic prayers"; her community holds trees to be sacred because "holy spirits resided in them", and she prays "for a son" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 185–186, folio 159 / folio not stated).

Here, too, Khalid sets the story of Maula Karar, "one vendor in the city of Sialkot", who tells Nanak of the various shrines of the place (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 188–189, folio 162 / folio not stated). Nanak and Karar talk "under the tree" all evening. Nanak sends Mardana into the city to buy "two paisas worth of truth and two paisas worth of untruth"; Karar's answer comes back on two slips of paper, which read "Life is false" and "Death is truth", and Mardana returns with them to "the berry tree" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 187–188, folios 161–162). Later, hiding from Nanak, Karar is bitten by a snake that was "escaping the harsh winter outside" and "died on the spot", and Nanak and Mardana take "part in the final rites" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 190, folio 164). "Karar's wife did not know that Nanak could not perform any miracles", Khalid adds (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 190, folio 164). On Karar's death he quotes the Guru's verse:

*Friendship with a money- monger / Is false* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 191, folio 165)

## The Gurdwara

The shrine that grew up about the sacred *ber* tree was built, in its later form, by one Natha Singh, and the complex came to include a garden, a *sarovar* or pool, and residential rooms for pilgrims and attendants. Like many Sikh shrines of Pakistan, it suffered in the disturbances that followed the demolition of the Babri Masjid in 1992, when a structure within the premises is said to have been pulled down by a mob. In the years that followed, however, the gurdwara's fortunes revived: access for Indian Sikh pilgrims was restored around 2012, and after renovation the shrine was formally reopened for worship, an event widely welcomed in the Sikh world as part of a wider effort to restore Pakistan's Sikh heritage.

Through all these vicissitudes the ancient *ber* tree has remained the heart of the site — a living link, in the eyes of the faithful, to the very afternoon on which the Guru sat in its shade. Pilgrims come to pay their respects before it and to make their devotions within the sanctum, where the *Guru Granth Sahib* is installed.

Khalid's account of his own visit, with Iqbal Qaiser, gives a different picture. They came from Eminabad by "the newly constructed motorway that connects the Grand Trunk Road with Sialkot" — "Nanak too must have followed a similar route", he writes (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 191, folio 165). Iqbal Qaiser asked a traffic policeman "where is Baba Bair (the shrine of the Berry saint)?", and he pointed "away from the ancient city of Sialkot", to a place "on top of a mound" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 193–194, folios 167–168). They drove "through the narrow streets of this mohalla (locality) which is now known as the mohalla of Baba Bair" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 194, folio 168). Khalid wrote in his diary: "Everybody seemed to know where Baba Bair is but do they also know that it is called Baba Bair because of Guru Nanak?" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 194, folio 168)

At the entrance a board "noted that the gurdwara was renovated in 2012 by the Augaf [OCR?] [Auqaf] Department". New tiles lay "on one side, rotting away"; the floor "had recently been completed and the building refurbished, but the gurdwara was far from ready to become functional" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 194, folio 168). Khalid had been told, through a few Sikhs living in Nankana Sahib, that the gurdwara was now functioning, and he found it "quite different" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 194, folio 168).

The building he describes is "an open structure", with "a veranda" whose pillars hold up the roof, and only one room, in the centre of the building, where the Guru Granth Sahib was (or had been) kept; the end of that sentence is damaged in the printed text. "The berry tree was behind the building" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 194, folio 168). It was "a thick tree, bending towards the ground. It looked ancient, if judged by its size." (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 195, folio 169)

Beneath the tree lay "a long grave … covered with a red sheet with Quranic verses on it", and there was "a red flag at the entrance". Iqbal Qaiser's verdict was short: "This grave is new" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 195, folio 169). When he had last come to the place, for his own book, "there was no such grave" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 196, folio 170).

A man of "about thirty years", apparently "hoping for a tip", showed them round. He said the place had been a gurdwara "abandoned in 1947", called the pictures of Hindu deities on its walls "frescoes", and pointed out floor tiles carrying the "names of Sikh devotees who had contributed towards the initial construction of this gurdwara" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 195, folio 169). On a wall were the words "'Ya Allah' and 'Ya Muhammad'", scribbled "with a piece of coal" where the Gurmukhi text had been wiped clean; "the building had been Islamised", Khalid writes, and there was a "newly built mosque next [to] the shrine" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 196, folio 170).

The precinct was not quiet. Men watched the visitors "looking at us suspiciously"; one group was "preparing bhang (cannabis)", another "in another corner was smoking dope"; and under the tree "where Nanak and Mardana sat" a few men were "playing cards, most probably gambling, but no money was visible as gambling is illegal in Pakistan" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 195, folio 169). From the roof tops of the city, Khalid notes, one can see the mountains of Jammu; the printed text of the sentence is damaged (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 194, folio 168).

## Baba Bair, the Saint of the Tree

The man who showed them round had his own story of the place. The grave, he said, is "the grave of Pir Bairi (the saint of berries)", "Very old", and "Guru Nanak also came to this saint and stayed with him … Guru Nanak realised that Pir Bairi was a powerful saint" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 195, folio 169). Khalid calls this "an apocryphal story" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 195, folio 169).

His own explanation runs the other way. After Partition the gurdwara was abandoned, but the place, "particularly the tree", stayed sacred because of Nanak, and in time a grave "of this non-existent saint was constructed" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 196, folio 170). Perhaps, he suggests, the tree was sacred even before Nanak came, and Nanak became one of the reasons for its sanctity. "Now this gurdwara has become a shrine to Baba Bair with the red flag at the entrance as his symbol" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 196, folio 170). On the same page Khalid writes that Nanak is still held holy, particularly by the Muslims of the areas he visited; the printed text there is damaged (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 196, folio 170).

Writing of Pakistan more widely, Khalid observes that the words "'Allah O Akbar'" painted on barren rocks are "more prevalent in places where there is a contested history, like in Sialkot, where there used [t]o be a major Sikh shrine" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 267, folio 241). He does not name the shrine.

## Devotion and Practice

The books consulted record no festival, *langar* or regular worship at this site. What Bhandari's Persian history, composed in the reign of Aurangzeb, says of the followers of Baba Nanak in general describes the devotion such places served. Most of Baba Nanak's disciples and devotees, he writes, are men of spiritual state whose word is accepted, given to austerities, and whose prayers are answered (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 519). The heart of their worship is the reading of their master's verses, which they sing (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 519). They live at one with their friends and without strife towards their enemies, and their master's name is always on their tongues; they count the service of those who come and go as the highest worship, and whoever arrives at midnight speaking Baba Nanak's name is treated as a brother and a friend and served, even a stranger, a thief, a highwayman or an evildoer (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 519). Among the dervishes of Hind he counts as a fourth class the Udasis, devotees of Baba Nanak who worship the Creator after their master's way (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 566).

Khalid writes that "It is believed that the tradition of Langar began with Guru Nanak" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 188, folio not stated), and that "many Muslims, acquainted with the teachings of Guru Nanak, are devoted to him too and visit his shrine" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 220, folio not stated). Neither remark is made of Sialkot.

## In Verse

Khalid quotes the Guru's verse throughout *Walking with Nanak*, in published English translations. Only the lines on Karar's death, given above, belong to the Sialkot story; the rest are given here as the poetry of the Guru the shrine remembers. The *aarti*, which Iqbal Qaiser reads as placing God in nature, begins in one translation:

*The sky the salver, the sun and moon the lamps …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 55, folio 37)

and in another:

*In the salver of the firmament, / The sun and the moon shine as lamps* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 243–244, folios 217–218)

On the oneness of God:

*My Master is One / He is One, brother, the only One* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 60, folio 42)

*God is One, / The Path is One* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 144, folio 126)

The poem on the sacred thread, the *janeu*, which in Khalid's reconstruction the boy Nanak refused:

*From the cotton of compassion, / Spin the thread of contentment* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 61–62, folios 43–44)

On washing:

*As hands or feet besmirched with slime, / Water washes white* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 192, folio 166)

The song Nanak sings, in Khalid's reconstruction, to the innkeepers who meant to rob him:

*The herons in their white feathers dwell in the sacred shrines of pilgrimage* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 123–126, folios 105–108)

Other openings Khalid quotes: *O my merchant friend* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 56–58, folio 40); *Launch ye the boat of austere contemplation …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 148–149, folios 130–131); *As a beggar goes a-begging …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 158, folio 140); and *His goodness cannot be priced or traded …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 280, folio 254). The first poem of Nanak that Khalid read was the *Babur Bani*, "a long poem that Nanak wrote at the time the Mughal marauder Babur attacked Punjab, destroying the cities of Eminabad and Lahore on the way" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 15, folio not stated); he quotes from it the line 'Coming in seventy-eight (1521 C.E)' (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 113–114, folios 95–96).

Bhandari, in Persian, says that the Guru composed Hindi verses on the truth of God, setting out divine unity in clear expressions and shining metaphors (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520), and that a singer named Mardana sang his verses and drew people into the snare of faith (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). Dhillon notes that Nanak's *Bani* "was compiled as part of the Adi Granth by Guru Arjan Dev, the fifth Guru, in 1604" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 9, folio 9), and Shemeem Burney Abbas's glossary defines the *banī* by him: "Devotional verses such as those of Guru Nanak; verses of supplication, entreaty, humility, and reverence" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 179, folio not stated). The Guru's verse is sung outside the gurdwaras too. Abbas quotes Abida Parvin: "I sing Shah Hussain, Bulle Shah, Mian Muhammad Bakhsh, Hazrat Khawaja Ghulam Farid, Sacchal Sarmast, Baba Farid, and Guru Nanak" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 54, folio not stated). In Sindh, Michel Boivin records, "Sain Vasan Shah became famous for reciting the banis of Shri Guru Nanak and the kalams of the Sufis" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 68, folio 55).

## Guru Nanak in the Wider Record

What the books say of Guru Nanak himself is said of the Guru, not of this site. Khalid gives his dates as 1469–1539, calling him "the founder of the Sikh religion" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 45, folio not stated); Dhillon calls the Janamsakhis "cycles of tales purportedly drawn from the life of the first Guru of the Sikhs", whose dates the printed text gives, misprinted, as 1469–1539 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 9, folio 9). Dhillon has him born "On 14 April 1469" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 17–18, folios 19–20) and gives the date of his death as 22 September 1539 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 259, folio 287). Bhandari reports, as something that is said, a birth in Samvat 1526, 880 Hijri, in the reign of Sultan Bahlul Lodi, and a death in the reign of Salim Shah Afghan, between the ages of seventy and eighty (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).

Dhillon sets out two schemes of the *udasis*. "According to the Puratan Janamsakhi… he undertook five journeys", the first of which, 1499–1506, ends at "Saidpur, Pasrur and Sialkot (all three in Pakistan)" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 54–55, folios 60–61; p. 54, folio 60). "More modern accounts" give four, spanning twenty-three years (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 55, folio 61). Dhillon himself holds that there were four (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 56, folio 62). In his retelling Sialkot recurs: on the second journey the Guru comes from Lahore to Sialkot and the faqir (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 124–134; pp. 136–159), and the third begins and ends there, the Guru having "trekked north from Sialkot" into Jammu and Kashmir, to "Sumeru, the legendary Mount Kailash" and Ladakh, before returning to Sialkot (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 191, folio 210). Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi's account of the Guru's life names the town once, in his itinerary (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 409, folio 385).

Khalid gives the travels as some twenty-four years, mostly on foot, east to Bengal, south to Sri Lanka, north to Kashmir, Nepal and Tibet, and west to Mecca (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 54, folio 36). Nanak "preferred to stay on the outskirts" of villages, Khalid writes, and this is "a recurrent theme" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 53, folio 35); in Khalid's reconstruction he would rather sleep under trees than in caravanserais (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 120, folio 102). "Whenever he would visit an area he would visit its religious shrines, Muslim, Hindu and of any other denomination" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 37, folio 19).

The family the Guru left behind is named differently in the books. In Khalid's reconstruction he married "Sulakhni", "the daughter of Moolchand Khatri from the village of Pakhoke near Gurdaspur", and their sons were Sri Chand and Lachman Das (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 67, folio 49; p. 68, folio 50). Dhillon has him marry on 24 September 1487 Sulakhini, daughter of Mul Chand and Chando Rani of the town of Batala, and gives the sons as "Sri Chand (8 September 1494) and Lakhmi Das (12 February 1497)" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 44, folio 49). Bhandari names a worthy son, Lakhmidas, whom the Guru passed over because spiritual wealth was not his portion, making a Khatri named Lehna, also known by a second name printed uncertainly (perhaps Tarhan), his successor as Guru Angad at the hour of death (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). Khalid, too, writes that "On his deathbed, he appointed not his son but his most loyal student as his spiritual successor" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 16, folio not stated).

The miracle at the heart of the Sialkot story sits uneasily with what several writers say of the Guru. Dhillon writes of "Guru Nanak's clear and emphatic disavowal of the miraculous" (the printed text has "dear" for "clear") (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 11, folio 11), and in his retelling the Guru tells Karori, who calls his cure a miracle, that "there is no such thing as a miracle" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 190, folio 209). Khalid writes that Nanak "abhorred the concept of associating miracles with religious personalities", yet "his biography is nothing but a story of his miracles" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 16, folio not stated). For Dhillon the Guru was "no unworldly mystic" but "a householder in every sense of the word" who "brought a message of peace and harmony to believers of the mutually hostile religions of Islam and Hinduism" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 12, folio not stated). Khalid records his advice to Bhai Bala and Bhai Mardana: "if one is a Muslim then one should be a good Muslim and if one is a Hindu then one should be a good Hindu" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 95, folio 77).

Muslim writers and singers have claimed the Guru too. Khalid's informant Nadeem, a Muslim painter, says "Guru Nanak is like a Muslim Sufi Saint" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 219, folio not stated). Robert Rozehnal reports that a twentieth-century Chishti Sabiri shaykh, in certain lectures, describes "non-Muslim spiritual luminaries—Krishna, Rama, Sita, Lachman, and Guru Nanak—as believers in the one true God and religious leaders with 'Divine connection' (nisbat)" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 63, folio 53). Abbas writes that Baba Farid's "texts are revered among the Sikhs because he influenced the founder of their own religion, Baba Guru Nanak" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 87, folio not stated). Omar Kasmani, citing Boivin, notes that the trope of the flower in milk is told also of "Guru Nanak, the spiritual leader and founder of Sikhism" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 195, folio 178, ch. 5 n. 17). In the Hinglaj valley, Jürgen Schaflechner records "the engraved footsteps of Guru Nanak" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 256, folio not stated). Rizvi, rejecting W.H. McLeod's view that the Sufism Nanak met in the Panjab was a debased one, argues that "all literate Panjabis, both Hindus and Muslims, at the time of Guru Nanak were conversant with" ʿAttar, Rumi and Saʿdi (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 420, folio 396).

## Setting: Sialkot

Dhillon's Sialkot is "a bustling city that was situated in the foothills of the Himalayas". Legend has it founded by "Raja Sela, the uncle of the Pandavas", and rebuilt by "Raja Satya Vachan, when it became part of the kingdom of Kashmir"; "The earliest recorded mention of the city is found in ancient Greek texts going as far back as 322 B.C., where it is referred to as Sagala", and it was "famous for its silk" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 136, folio 151). "Sialkot became a part of the Muslim Sultanate when Shahabuddin Muhammad Ghori captured Punjab in 1192", and a garrison was left there (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 136, folio 151). Many Sufi missionaries settled in the region, making it predominantly Muslim and "giving it a rich Sufi tradition", and the city was an important post on the trade route between West Asia and Delhi (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 137, folio 152).

Khalid's reconstruction of the city's past begins with another king: "Puran Bhagat was the son of the King of Sialkot, Raja Salivahan"; "It is believed that Sialkot was founded by this king"; "The well of Puran Bhagat was at a little distance from the city of Sialkot", and devotees raised a temple around it, "the most important temple in the ancient city of Sialkot" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 184, folio 158). Mardana, as a younger man, would roam the cities and villages of Punjab singing folk songs and legends, the most popular being Puran Bhagat's (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 184, folio 158).

Sujan Rai Bhandari of Batala, describing the suba of Lahore in Persian in the fortieth regnal year of Aurangzeb, places the city in the Rachna doab and calls it a city of the ancients (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517). It was once called Shalkot, and its founding is attributed to Raja Shal (the name and the word after it are printed uncertainly), a raja of the Pandavas' time named in the Mahabharata, a work some five thousand years before (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517). It is also called Salkot and attributed to Raja Salbahan, of whom a fort of fired brick is his memorial (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517). In former times it was the seat of government of the Punjab, its habitation stretching two or three *kos*; and now it surpasses every other town of the suba in population (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517). When Lahore lay in ruins in early times, the city of Sialkot became the seat of government of this region, until Sultan Mahmud's conquest and Malik Ayaz's rebuilding of Lahore (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525).

Bhandari tells of the fort's repairs. Shihab al-Din of Ghur, failing at Lahore in the year 580 Hijri, came to Sialkot, repaired the old fort and left his army there (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517); in another passage he restored the fort, whose foundation was ancient, and Khusraw Malik besieged it with the Khokhars for a time without success (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 404). Under Akbar, Raja Man Singh, faujdar of Jammu and jagirdar of Sialkot, repaired the fort and improved the city; then Safdar Khan, in Jahangir's reign, rebuilt the fort and its tower, and most later governors made repairs too (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517).

The city of Bhandari's day was a city of crafts and learning. Its paper, the Mansinghi, the silk paper and the Khass-e Jahangiri, was white and durable and sent to other regions; its *karchikan* embroidery in silk and *kalabatun* went all over the world, and its *jamdhar*, *katari* and *barchhi* were well made (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517). Gardens surrounded it, among them the garden of Nazar Muhammad, whose last name is printed uncertainly (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517). A stream rising in the hills of Jammu flowed below the city, and when it flooded in the rains all the people of Sialkot, great and small, played in the water, naked but for a loincloth and carrying leather water-skins; anyone from the city who was far away, Bhandari writes, remembered this with pleasure (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 517–516). The city was a seat of learning and a gathering-place of scholars, a mine of virtue and a home of the learned (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 516). Under Akbar, Maulana Kamal al-Din Husain left Kashmir and came to Sialkot in the year 971 Hijri and established teaching there; under Shah Jahan Maulvi Abd al-Hakim spread the sciences, wrote glosses, and drew students to his blessed *madrasa* (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 516). The buildings of the qanungos and of some others were, he writes, most pleasing and delightful (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517).

Annemarie Schimmel likewise calls Sialkot "one of the centers of Islamic scholarship during the Mogul period", where Ahmad Sirhindi studied (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 398, folio 367). In the volume edited by Pnina Werbner and Helene Basu, the saint Shah Daulah is said to have left the city: "Due to some disputes with the local people of Sialkot, he migrated from there and, after some years, moved to Gujrat" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 205, folio 194). Bhandari, too, notes that Shah Daula's first master lived in Sialkot (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 515).

The Guru's own lifetime saw Babur's invasions. Rizvi writes that Nanak returned from eastern India "only to find the Panjab plunged into a number of significant political crises", and that Babur "invaded a third time over the Panjab in 926/1520 reaching as far as Sialkot" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 409, folio 385). Bhandari records that when Daulat Khan and Ghazi Khan took Kalanaur and seized Sialkot from the emperor's men, Babur, encamped by the Chenab near Bahlulpur, gave the order that Sialkot be laid waste and its people resettled at Bahlulpur (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 304). Dhillon, by contrast, writes that during "the Mughal Emperor Babur's third incursion into India", "Sialkot had offered no resistance and had been spared the usual bloody massacre and plunder" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 224, folio 246).

Khalid found a modern city. "Sialkot has become one of the most important cities in Pakistan"; its business community "financed its own airport, which now handles international flights", and the highway, and on the way in he saw "several factories" and "a lot of mounds", "probably archaeological ruins, converted into graveyards" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 191, folio 165). "Sialkot is located right on the border", and "Historically, Sialkot provided Punjab with a connection to Kashmir"; the fields were flooded at the start of the monsoon, which Iqbal Qaiser put down to India releasing dam water, and days later shelling was reported on villages in the Sialkot district (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 193, folio 167). The author of *Tarikh Guru Khalsa*, Khalid notes, belonged to Sialkot (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 203, folio 177).

Of the fate of such places after 1947, Khalid writes, of the province rather than of this site, that hundreds of temples and gurdwaras "all over Pakistani Punjab" were taken over by refugees and turned into houses (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 58, folio not stated), and, in another book, "I have seen several gurdwaras and temples being taken over and destroyed by the migrants" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated). After the demolition of the Babri Mosque on 6 December 1992, he records, "Well-known Hindus living in the cities of Punjab had to hide for several months after the incident" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 22, folio not stated).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The name.** The site's account gives Gurdwara Babay De Ber, also Gurdwara Beri Sahib or Babe di Ber, the "gurdwara of the elder's jujube tree". Khalid's book calls the place "Baba Bair (the shrine of the Berry saint)" and its quarter the mohalla of Baba Bair (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 193–194, folios 167–168; p. 194, folio 168); the man who showed him round called the saint of the grave Pir Bairi, "the saint of berries" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 195, folio 169).
- **Where it stands.** The site's account places the gurdwara in the old city of Sialkot, near the ancient fort. In Khalid's account the policeman pointed "away from the ancient city of Sialkot", to a place on top of a mound (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 193–194, folios 167–168).
- **The Guru's road to Sialkot.** The site's account has him come by way of Jammu. In Khalid's reconstruction he walks from Eminabad (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 185, folio 159). In Dhillon's retelling he comes on his second journey, after Lahore (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 124–134; pp. 136–159); his third journey goes north from Sialkot into Jammu and returns to Sialkot (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 191, folio 210).
- **The faqir's grievance.** The site's account speaks of a broken promise made to Hamza Ghaus. Khalid specifies that a Hindu man broke a promise to give him his first-born son, and that the Sufi vowed to pray forty days before destroying the city (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 197–198, folios 171–172). Dhillon has the curse pronounced in the market square (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 150, folio 165).
- **How the Guru met him.** In the site's account the Guru sang the divine word beneath the tree, and the sound drew Hamza Ghaus out. In Khalid's telling of the legend Nanak knocked on his door for days, and thunder struck the dome (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 197–198, folios 171–172). In Dhillon's the faqir comes to the tree and the two sit side by side in its shade (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 153–154, folios not stated).
- **The spelling of the faqir's name.** The site's account and Dhillon write Hamza Ghaus (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 150, folio 165); Khalid writes Hamza Ghous (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 199, folio 173).
- **Why the tree is holy.** The site's account makes the tree sacred as the one beneath which the Guru sat. The man at the site told Khalid that the grave beneath it was that of Pir Bairi, a "Very old" saint whom Guru Nanak came to visit (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 195, folio 169); Khalid calls this apocryphal and the grave new, and suggests the tree may have been sacred before Nanak came (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 195, folio 169; p. 196, folio 170). In his reconstruction a Hindu woman already prays at the tree when Nanak arrives (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 185–186, folio 159 / folio not stated).
- **The state of the gurdwara.** The site's account says that after renovation the shrine was formally reopened for worship, and that pilgrims make their devotions in the sanctum where the *Guru Granth Sahib* is installed. Khalid saw the board recording a renovation in 2012 by the Auqaf Department, but found the gurdwara "far from ready to become functional", the building Islamised, a new grave under the tree and a new mosque beside it (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 194, folio 168; p. 195, folio 169; p. 196, folio 170). The books do not give the year of his visit.
- **Its history after 1947.** The site's account records damage after the demolition of the Babri Masjid in 1992 and a revival around 2012. The man at the site told Khalid the gurdwara was "abandoned in 1947" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 195, folio 169), and Khalid writes that after Partition it was abandoned (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 196, folio 170).
- **Who founded Sialkot.** Dhillon gives the legend of Raja Sela, uncle of the Pandavas, and a rebuilding by Raja Satya Vachan (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 136, folio 151). Khalid's reconstruction gives Raja Salivahan, father of Puran Bhagat (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 184, folio 158). Bhandari gives both a raja of the Pandavas' time named Shal and, under the name Salkot, Raja Salbahan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517).
- **When Ghur took the city.** Dhillon dates Shahabuddin Muhammad Ghori's capture of Punjab, and his garrison at Sialkot, to 1192 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 136, folio 151). Bhandari dates Shihab al-Din's repair of the Sialkot fort to the year 580 Hijri (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517).
- **Babur and Sialkot.** Dhillon writes that on Babur's third incursion Sialkot offered no resistance and was spared massacre and plunder (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 224, folio 246). Bhandari records Babur's order that Sialkot be laid waste and its people resettled at Bahlulpur (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 304). Rizvi has Babur's third invasion, in 926/1520, reach as far as Sialkot (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 409, folio 385).
- **The Guru's family.** Khalid's reconstruction has Sulakhni come from Pakhoke near Gurdaspur, daughter of Moolchand Khatri, and names the younger son Lachman Das (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 67, folio 49; p. 68, folio 50). Dhillon has Sulakhini come from Batala, daughter of Mul Chand and Chando Rani, and names the younger son Lakhmi Das (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 44, folio 49). Bhandari names the son Lakhmidas, written as one word (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).
- **The Guru's dates.** Khalid and Dhillon give 1469–1539, Dhillon with a birth on 14 April 1469 and a death on 22 September 1539 (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 45, folio not stated; Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 17–18, folios 19–20; p. 259, folio 287). Bhandari gives a birth in Samvat 1526, 880 Hijri, in the reign of Bahlul Lodi, and a death under Salim Shah Afghan between the ages of seventy and eighty (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).
- **How many journeys.** Dhillon reports five *udasis* in the Puratan Janamsakhi and four in more modern accounts, and holds to four himself (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 54–55, folios 60–61; p. 56, folio 62). Khalid gives the travels as about twenty-four years (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 54, folio 36); Dhillon's modern scheme spans twenty-three (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 55, folio 61).

## Legacy

Gurdwara Babay De Ber is one of several historic gurdwaras associated with Guru Nanak in the Pakistani Punjab that have drawn renewed attention as pilgrimage and heritage sites, and its story carries a particular moral weight. The encounter with Hamza Ghaus, and the Guru's plea that a whole community not be condemned for the sin of one, speaks directly to the ethic of compassion and shared humanity at the core of Guru Nanak's message. For the city of Sialkot, the shrine and its ancient tree are a reminder of a plural past in which the founder of the Sikh faith and a Muslim saint of the city met beneath the same sky; for pilgrims, it is a cherished station on the map of the Guru's travels through the land of the five rivers.

For Khalid the place is a lesson of another kind. "Everybody seemed to know where Baba Bair is", he wrote, but not that it bears the name because of Guru Nanak (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 194, folio 168); in his reading the place, and the tree above all, stayed sacred through Nanak after the gurdwara was abandoned, and in time a grave was made for a saint of its own (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 196, folio 170). Writing of Pakistan as a whole, he says that "there are several sites all over the country where Nanak stayed or performed miracles. Gurdwaras that were built there later by the devotees still stand tall" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 168–169, folio not stated).
