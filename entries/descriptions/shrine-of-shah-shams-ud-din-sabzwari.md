## Overview

In Multan, the ancient ‘City of Saints’, stands the shrine of Shah Shams-ud-Din Sabzwari — ‘Shah Shams’ or Pir Shams — a saint of the thirteenth century remembered as one of the most powerful and colourful holy figures of the region. Renowned for spectacular miracles and associated in tradition with the spread of a form of the faith through the Ismaili *daʿwa*, Shah Shams gave Multan one of its oldest and most storied shrines, and his memory is bound up with the very sun and heat of the city.

None of the books consulted here gives Shah Shams a notice of his own, and none of them uses the name Sabzwari. Two of them name a Shah Shams buried at Multan, each in a single line and each in a form that differs from the name the shrine bears. Sujan Rai Bhandari's *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* lists a Shams al-Din Tabrizi among the saints buried in Multan whose tombs are places of pilgrimage (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528). Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti's *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* mentions, in passing, a tomb of a Shah Shams in Multan, the second part of whose name cannot be read with certainty in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 717). Several of the modern books speak of a shrine of Shams Tabriz in Multan without saying whether it is this one. What follows keeps these strands apart.

## The Life and Legend of the Saint

Shah Shams is said to have come to the Indian subcontinent from Sabzwar, in the Persian lands, in the thirteenth century, as a preacher and missionary of extraordinary spiritual power. His life, more legend than chronicle, is famous above all for the miracle from which his name (*Shams*, ‘the sun’) is popularly explained: it is told that, having no fire to cook a fish, the saint commanded the sun itself to descend and roast it, and that the sun drew so near that Multan has been scorched by an intense heat ever since. Such tales made him one of the great wonder-working saints of the popular imagination.

In the histories he is connected with the Ismaili mission in Sindh and Multan, part of the complex religious currents of the medieval Indus valley, though he is venerated broadly across communities today. He died in Multan in the thirteenth century. The books consulted here give no year for his coming to Multan or for his death, and none of them tells the story of the sun.

## The Shrine and its Devotion

Shah Shams Sabzwari's shrine in Multan, a domed tomb by the old bank of the river, is among the oldest darbars of the city and a continuous centre of pilgrimage. Devotees come to seek the blessing of a saint famed for miraculous power, to recite the Fatiha, and to fulfil vows, and the annual *ʿurs* draws crowds in the customary manner of Multan's shrines, with qawwali and *langar*. The shrine forms part of the dense sacred landscape that has made Multan the ‘City of Saints’. The books consulted here give no date for the *ʿurs* and do not say who built the tomb.

## The Shams of Multan in the Older Histories

**Bhandari's list of the saints of Multan.** In its account of the suba of Multan, the *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, a Persian history composed in the reign of Aurangzeb, names the saints buried in the city and says that the tomb of each, a place where divine lights are made manifest, is a place of pilgrimage for the people (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528). Among them it names Shams al-Din Tabrizi; the book writes Tabrizi, not Sabzwari, and it says nothing more about him (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528). That this Shams al-Din is the saint of this shrine is the book's list read against the shrine's name, not something the book itself states; the name and the city are the only link. Of the city's other saints the book adds that many friends of God rest in that city in felicity (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528).

Elsewhere the same book speaks of a Shams al-Din Tabrizi whom it places in Rum with Maulana Rumi, not at Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 555). The book does not say whether the two are one man.

**Chishti's tomb of a Shah Shams.** Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti's *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* records that in the year 1158 there was a disturbance in Multan, in which Nawab Shahnawaz Khan was killed by a horseman of Diwan Kaura Mal, a name whose printed reading is uncertain (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 717). Shahnawaz Khan, the book says, was buried beside the tomb of a Shah Shams, a tomb it says still stands (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 717). The word printed after Shams is damaged (it may read Tira), and the reading of the whole name is uncertain; the book gives no other detail of the tomb. The same page records that the suba of Multan was joined to Lahore in the year 1155 (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 717); both years are uncertain in the printed text.

## Shams Tabriz of Multan in the Modern Books

Two modern scholars write of a shrine of Shams Tabriz in Multan. None of them names Sabzwari, and none of them says that the shrine they mean is this one; their words are given here as what they say about a Shams of Multan.

Annemarie Schimmel, reading a poem of Sachal, says that it speaks "of Shams-i Tabriz, Maulana Rumi's spiritual beloved who, according to the Indian tradition, was flayed alive by the mollas in Multan where his shrine is still venerated" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 175, folio 157). She ascribes the account to "the Indian tradition", not to herself. In her notes she adds: "About the mysterious figure of Shams see Vladimir Ivanow, 'Shams Tabriz of Multan.'" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 285, folio 267). Her bibliography gives that study as "Shums Tabrez of Multan", in a presentation volume published in Lahore by the University of the Punjab in 1955 (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 326, folio 308). She names no shrine building, tomb or date for this Shams.

Shemeem Burney Abbas, writing of a Hazrat Shams Tabriz, declines to settle who he was: "This may be the renowned Hazrat Shams Tabriz", the spiritual mentor of Maulana Rum, "or another local Sufi by the same name" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 193, folio not stated). She recalls that one of her students "did take me to the shrine of one Hazrat Shams Tabrizi in Multan", in a year that is lost in the copy consulted, and adds: "Multiple shrines to the same person are not unusual." (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 193, folio not stated). She does not name the shrine further.

## Setting

The books' wider accounts of Multan speak of the city, not of this shrine, but they help to place the currents with which tradition connects Shah Shams. Schimmel gives 711 as the year in which "the Arabs conquered Sind and the adjacent provinces northward up to Multan" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 375, folio 344). She writes of the Carmathians, "who ruled not only Bahrain but also northern Sind and Multan" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 98, folio 67), and records that "there, at Multan, Hallaj had been in touch with them, according to the tradition" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 113, folio 82). Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi writes that "Multan had become a dependency of the Fatimids of Egypt", describes Sultan Mahmud's blow to Ismaʿili power there, and notes that "by the middle of the eleventh century, sufism had penetrated into the areas surrounding Multan"; in 1175, he writes, Muʿizzu'd-Din Muhammad bin Sam seized the city (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India, vol. 1*, p. 128, folio 110).

Bhandari's history tells the same story from the conquerors' side. On Mahmud's fourth campaign, it says, the Qarmatian heretics who were in that country were taken captive (the word for the Qarmatians is uncertain in the printed text), and the book describes the punishments inflicted on them (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 417). Shihab al-Din, on his first campaign, took the province of Multan from the hands of the Qarmatians and left a deputy there (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 405).

The heat that the legend of the sun explains is a theme of the older accounts of the city as well. Of Multan's climate, Bhandari writes that the winter is mild, that the summer is excessively hot, and that there is little rain (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528). Of Mahmud's second campaign he writes that in such a season the sultan's soldiers could not bear the hot air of Multan and begged to go back, so that the sultan turned back to Ghazni (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 420–419). The book does not connect the heat with any saint.

The books also describe Multan in and around the thirteenth century, the age that tradition gives Shah Shams. Harish Dhillon writes that "During the twelfth century, many Sufi scholars made Multan their base", and that conversion "gained momentum in the early thirteenth century" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 166–167, folios 182–183). Omar Kasmani writes of the saint he calls Lal that "he is likely to have first arrived in Multan in 1263" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 188, folio 171). Carl W. Ernst and Bruce B. Lawrence call Multan "a strategic commercial center in the Punjab" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 80, folio 66). Dhillon adds that "By the time Nanak came to Multan, the city was famous as a major centre of Sufi learning and philosophy" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 167, folio 183), and Haroon Khalid that Multan "was known for the religious shrines of Muslim saints, and Jain and Hindu temples. It was a sacred space for people of all religious denominations" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 123, folio 105).

Visits to the tombs of Multan and its region appear in the books across the centuries. Bhandari records that in the year 835 the sultan reached Multan and made the round of the tombs of the shaikhs, the year uncertain in the printed text and no shaikh or tomb named (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 324). Abbas writes of performers who "sing around shrines in Multan and Sahiwal to earn a living", naming no shrine (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 199, folio not stated). And Khalid records the disapproval of a Tableeghi Jamaat devotee, Mussadiq, who says of the shrines of the region: "There are several shrines in the Multan region and in Sindh where people still practise Hinduism and give it the name of Islam", and "They light lamps, present offerings to the shrine and even bow down to the grave, an act of shirk" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 10, ch. 4, folio not stated). This is one informant's opinion of the region's shrines in general, not an account of this one.

## In Verse

Multan appears in the verse the books carry only in passing. In Naveed Alam's translation of Madho Lal Hussein, a bride's trousseau gathers the cloth of the whole subcontinent:

*"The dress / someone carried across the snows / all the way from Kashmir. The dress / someone brought from Gujarat… The dress bought in Multan"* (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 51, folio 51, kafi 29).

The *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori's book of the saints of the Punjab, carries a chronogram by the author's son, Mufti Ghulam Safdar, on the friends of God of the Punjab whose lives the book records:

جو خدا کے دوست تھے پنجاب میں
حال ان کا اس سے ہوتا ہے عیان

*A verse of the chronogram written for the book by the author's son; its subject is the saints of the Punjab whom the book describes* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 282, folio 270).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **His name.** The shrine's account calls him Shah Shams-ud-Din Sabzwari, from Sabzwar. The *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* names, among the saints buried in Multan, a Shams al-Din Tabrizi, writing Tabrizi, not Sabzwari (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528). The *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* speaks of the tomb of a Shah Shams whose second name is damaged in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 717).
- **Who the Shams of Multan was.** The *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* places a Shams al-Din Tabrizi in Rum with Maulana Rumi, not at Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 555), while listing a Shams al-Din Tabrizi among the saints buried at Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528). Schimmel reports "the Indian tradition" that Shams-i Tabriz, Rumi's beloved, died in Multan, where his shrine is venerated, and calls him "the mysterious figure of Shams" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 175, folio 157; p. 285, folio 267). Abbas leaves open whether the Shams of the shrine she visited in Multan is "the renowned Hazrat Shams Tabriz" or "another local Sufi by the same name" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 193, folio not stated). None of these sources equates the figure it speaks of with Shah Shams Sabzwari.
- **His death.** The shrine's account says only that he died in Multan in the thirteenth century. The tradition Schimmel reports of Shams-i Tabriz holds that he "was flayed alive by the mollas in Multan" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 175, folio 157); it is not said to be about this saint.
- **His dates.** The shrine's account places his life in the thirteenth century. None of the books consulted here gives a year for him.

## Legacy

Shah Shams Sabzwari endures in memory as one of the great wonder-working saints of medieval Multan, a figure of legend whose miracles, above all the calling-down of the sun, have entered the folklore of the region, and whose shrine remains one of the oldest and most visited in the city. He belongs to the founding age of Sufism in the Indus valley, and his darbar is a fixed point in Multan's ancient devotion. The older histories remember a Shams among the saints whose tombs made Multan a place of pilgrimage, and modern scholars leave open who the Shams of Multan was.
