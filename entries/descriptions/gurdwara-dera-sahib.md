## Overview

Gurdwara Dera Sahib, known more fully as Gurdwara Dera Sahib *Patshahi Panjvin* — "of the Fifth Guruship" — stands in Lahore, just beyond the walls of the old city, and commemorates the place associated with the martyrdom in 1606 of Guru Arjan Dev, the fifth of the Sikh Gurus and the first to lay down his life for the faith. Rising in white and gold near the River Ravi, the shrine occupies an extraordinary architectural setting: it stands amid the great Mughal and Sikh monuments of Lahore, close by the Lahore Fort, the Badshahi Mosque, the Hazuri Bagh, and the *samadhi* of Maharaja Ranjit Singh. For Sikhs it is a place of profound solemnity, marking an event that transformed the character and history of their community.

Two of the books consulted name the site. Haroon Khalid's *A White Trail* gives a chapter to the "Commemoration of Guru Arjan's martyrdom at Gurdwara Dera Sahib, Lahore" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 6, folio not stated), which he attended on "6th June 2011" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 196, folio not stated), and writes that the Guru was assassinated "in Lahore, where his now Gurdwara stands by the name of Gurdwara Dera Sahib" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 198, folio not stated). Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti's Urdu survey of Lahore, the *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, heads a section with an account of the building of the Dera of Guru Arjan Sahib, though most of what follows is illegible in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 516).

A third, Kanhaiya Lal's *Tarikh-e Lahore* of 1884, describes at length a *samadh* of Guru Arjan outside the city opposite the Roshnai Gate, between the fort and the *samadh* of Maharaja Ranjit Singh (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 205–206, folios 208–209). It never calls the building Dera Sahib, and the great well and *baoli* it records inside the enclosure leave open whether the description belongs here or to Lahore's other shrine of the Guru, Gurdwara Baoli Sahib; it is given below as Kanhaiya Lal's account, with that question beside it. The remaining books — Khalid's *Walking with Nanak* and *In Search of Shiva*, Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi's history of Sufism in India, Harish Dhillon's retelling of the Janamsakhis, Naveed Alam's translation of Shah Hussein and Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* — speak of Guru Arjan himself, not of this site, and are drawn on here for the Guru's life, death and memory.

## Guru Arjan Dev

Guru Arjan (1563–1606), son of the fourth Guru, Ram Das, and grandson of the third, Amar Das, presided over the Sikh Panth at a moment of remarkable growth and consolidation. It was he who completed the sacred tank and the central shrine at Amritsar — the Harmandir Sahib, later known as the Golden Temple — and who, in 1604, compiled the *Adi Granth*, the first authoritative scripture of the Sikhs, gathering the hymns of his predecessors together with those of Hindu and Muslim saints and installing the volume in the Harmandir with the venerable Baba Buddha as its first custodian. Under his guidance Sikhism flourished as an organised community with its own scripture, its own centre and a widening body of devotees.

Khalid's *Walking with Nanak* gives him as "5. Guru Arjan Dev (1563-1606, Guru from 1581 to 1606)" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 315, folio 289), and Dhillon as "Arjan Dev (Guru from 1581 to 1606)" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 240, folio 265). *A White Trail* calls him "the fifth Sikh Guru of Sikhism and the son of the fourth Sikh Guru, Guru Ram Das" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 198, folio not stated), appointed by his father as the next Guru (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 220, folio not stated). Bhandari's *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, a Persian chronicle composed under Aurangzeb, records the succession in a line: after Ram Das, it says, his son Guru Arjan became *sajjada-nishin* and, after twenty-five years, departed this life (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).

## The Succession and Prithi Chand

The books tell the succession as a contest with his elder brother. In *Walking with Nanak* Arjan is the brother of Prithi Chand (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 38, folio 20), preferred when "his father, Guru Ram Das, appointed his younger son" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 43, folio 25). Prithi Chand hid the letters Arjan wrote from Lahore to Ram Das, who "disowned Prithi Chand" and "A few days later … appointed Arjan as the next Guru" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 253–254, folio 227 / folio not stated). "Sikh tradition finds the root of the conflict to be Arja[n]'s trip to Lahore and the machinations of Prithi Chand", and Prithi Chand then "approached Mughal authorities to get him his rightful share" of the property, "and hence began the tumultuous relation … between the Mughals and the Sikhs" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 274, folio 248).

Chishti tells the same story with more detail. Guru Arjan produced two letters that had been withheld, hidden in Prithi Chand's robe, and Prithi Chand was deeply ashamed. Guru Ram Das, on the advice of one who from the beginning had seated each Guru on the *gaddi* with his own hand, made Arjan Guru, asked him for a fourth *pauri* so that the name of the four *pauris* should be remembered until the Day of Judgement, and, when Arjan composed it at once, seated him on the *gaddi* to stand in his own place; his fame spread within a short time (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 505). The verse he composed is given below, under *In Verse*.

In Chishti's account Prithi Chand bore him enmity to the utmost degree, and slandered Arjan before Jahangir even in Ram Das's lifetime, complaining through Jahangir's *diwan*, himself an enemy, while Nawab Wazir Khan defended Arjan as a man of God (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 499). Arjan admonished his brother again and again, and the harassment went on (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 506). Prithi Chand came against Amritsar with some Afghans whom the book calls, in a word printed uncertainly, agents of the state, intending to kill the Guru, and the Guru hid in a village whose printed name, perhaps Dauli, cannot be read with certainty, a few *kos* from Amritsar; the Afghans died on the road (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 507). Prithi Chand petitioned the court with Chandu Lal's support; a *mir* sent to arrest the Guru was killed near Ambala by four servants he had dismissed, the rumour spread that this had happened through the Guru's blessing, Chandu Lal was rebuked and the Guru held blameless (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 509).

## The Granth and the Community

The compilation of the scripture is the act the books credit to him most often. Rizvi writes that "The fifth Guru, Arjan Dev" "collected the inspiring hymns written by Guru Nanak and his disciples in the Sikh 'Bible,' the Adi-Granth which is also known as the Granth-Saheb or the Guru-Granth. He also went to the extent of incorporating the authentic poems of the fourteen bhaktas preceding Guru Nanak", calls this appendix "the most authentic anthology of the movement and a memorable collection of medieval devotional literature", and adds: "The Guru-Granth was completed at Amritsar in 1604" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 407, folio 383). The date string printed beside his name in Rizvi's text is damaged and cannot be read with confidence; the index gives "b. 1563 guru 1581-1606" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 466, folio 442). Rizvi also counts "more than a hundred s/okas [slokas] ascribed to Baba Farid Ganj-i Shakar in the Guru Garanth [Guru Granth], compiled by the fifth Sikh Guru, Arjan, in 1604" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 350, folio 328). Dhillon writes that Guru Nanak's Bani "was compiled as part of the Adi Granth by Guru Arjan Dev, the fifth Guru, in 1604" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 9, folio 9).

*A White Trail* names as a major contribution "the compilation into the Granth Sahib of the writings of all the previous Sikh Gurus and other non-Sikh saints" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 198, folio not stated); Baba Fareed's poetry, collected by Guru Nanak at Pak Pattan, "was later included in the Granth Sahib by Guru Arjan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 200, folio not stated). Chishti says that he gathered Baba Nanak's words into the Granth, the scripture of the Sikhs, as he explains it (some of his words uncertain in the printed text), and fixed some rules for the Sikh community (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 686).

*Walking with Nanak* follows the book's making. "Guru Arjan asked one of his followers to go to Lahore to bind the Adi Granth" — Bhai Bannu (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 39, folio 21). After the compilation he was summoned, through Prithi Chand, over its alleged "anti-Muslim and anti-Hindu content", and sent "Bhai Gurdas", who "clarified the misunderstanding" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 254, folio not stated). The Adi Granth "played a significant role in the development of a separate Sikh identity", one reason given being rumours that "Meherban, the son of Prithi Chand" was compiling his own (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 256, folio 230). Arjan "has the greatest number of hymns in the Guru Granth Sahib" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 315, folio 289), a figure the book gives elsewhere as "2218 [OCR?]" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 256, folio 230), and in the Granth's naming "Arjan becomes the fourth Nanak" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 98, folio 80).

The same book credits him with "the completion of the Amrit Sar the tank of nectar that was started by his father at Ramdaspur", after which "the Harmandir Sahib was constructed there" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 257, folio not stated), and with "the daswandh", "10% of their earning to the cause of the Guru" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 315, folio 289), collected "one-tenth of their earnings" through the Masands, "a tradition that was started by Guru Arjan" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 256–257, folio 230 / folio not stated). Dhillon adds that the Kartarpur near Jalandhar was founded "in 1594, by the fifth Guru, Arjan Dev, on land granted during the reign of the third Mughal Emperor, Jalal-ud-din Muhammad Akbar" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 175, folio "l92" [text-layer?] [192]).

## Disciples and an Emperor's Visit

Two of his followers are remembered at shrines Khalid visited. Iqbal Qaiser, the historian who guided Khalid, told him that the gurdwara at Mandi Mangat "was the house of Bhai Bannu, who was a devotee of the fifth Sikh guru, Guru Arjan" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated). "According to the Encyclopaedia of Sikh Literature", another shrine "is the shrine of Sahari Mal, a disciple of the fifth Sikh guru, Guru Arjan"; Khalid calls Sahari Mal "a Hindu (Sikhism as an institutionalized religion was not yet established)", while Haji Kamal, one of Khalid's informants, says "We know that Sahari Mal was a Sikh Jat but that doesn't matter to us" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).

Bhandari's chronicle records an imperial halt at the Guru's lodging. Crossing the Beas, the emperor came to the halting place of Guru Arjan, *sajjada-nishin* of Baba Nanak, who was famed in the knowledge of God, the name Arjan being uncertain in the printed text (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 164); the grammar leaves it open whether the words famed in the knowledge of God describe Arjan or Nanak. The emperor was pleased by the meeting and by hearing from his lips the Hindi verses Baba Nanak had composed, and Guru Arjan, counting it an honour, made a fitting offering (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 164). He then petitioned for the peasants: while the imperial armies were in the Punjab grain had been dear and the revenue demand had been raised, but now grain had become cheap and the peasants could not meet the revenue demand; at his request the *diwans* were ordered to lighten the demand on the peasants (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 164). The chronicle does not name the place of the meeting beyond the river.

## The Martyrdom of 1606

That very flourishing drew the suspicion of the Mughal state. The Emperor Jahangir, in his memoir the *Tuzuk-i-Jahangiri*, records his displeasure at the Guru's rising influence and at the many who were drawn to him, and complains further that Arjan had extended his blessing to the rebellious prince Khusrau, Jahangir's own son, then in flight through the Punjab. On these grounds the Guru was summoned to Lahore, and a heavy fine — or, failing it, punishment — was imposed.

Sikh tradition holds that the Guru refused to pay or to alter a word of the sacred scripture, and that, at the instigation of the Lahore financier Chandu Shah, he was subjected to days of torture: seated, it is recounted, upon a heated iron plate while burning sand was poured over him, and made to endure scalding water. Bearing these torments with serene equanimity, and declining every inducement to convert, Guru Arjan died in 1606 — his *shaheedi* (martyrdom) traditionally commemorated in the month of Jeth, in late May or early June. By tradition his body was given to the waters of the Ravi and was never recovered. He was the first Sikh Guru to be martyred, and his death is reckoned a watershed in Sikh history.

*Walking with Nanak* quotes the emperor's memoir: "In Goindwal which is on the river Beas, there was a Hindu named Arjan"; Khusro "happened to halt at a place where he was", and Arjan made "a finger-mark in saffron which the Indians call Qashga"; Jahangir had his property handed "to Murtaza Khan" and ordered that he be "dealt with the penal Laws of Yasa" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 252, folio 226). *A White Trail* quotes the same memoir, through Dalbir Singh Dhillon's *Sikhism: Origin and Development*: "this insignificant fellow (Guru Arjan) ... commanded that he should be put to death" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 198, folio not stated). In that book Khusrau, "Jahangir's rebellious son", "stopped over at the Langar of the Guru in Amritsar and sought blessings for his victory", and the *tilak* that was the blessing was "considered treason by the Emperor" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 199, folio not stated).

Chishti gives the meeting from a book whose name, printed perhaps as Liti R Bilas, cannot be read with certainty: Khusrau, the prince, son of the Emperor Jahangir, driven from Delhi, came to Tarn Taran in the district of Amritsar, and Arjan kept him three days and gave him the sum of five hundred rupees in cash (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 509).

*Walking with Nanak* gives the Sikh explanation: "The Sikhs believe that he was assassinated due to the connivance of one Chandu Shah who wanted to marry his daughter to Guru Arjan's son, but was refused" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 315, folio 289). Elsewhere it names him "a Hindu Khatri called Chandu Lal", Diwan of Lahore, allied with Prithi Chand (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 253, folio 227), and observes that "Mughal sources and particularly the Tuzk-e-Ja hangiri [OCR?] [Tuzk-e-Jahangiri]" are "glaringly silent about the role of Chandu Lal", Jahangir stating that Arjan "was punished because of his sympathies for his rebel son Khusro" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 255, folio 229). He "was summoned to Lahore by Emperor Jahangir" over his role "during the rebellion of Prince Khusro", appointed "Hargobind as the sixth Guru" and "was subsequently martyred" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 234, folio 208).

Chishti, too, traces Chandu Lal's enmity to a proposed match of his daughter with Arjan's son: it was declined after Sikhs wrote against it, and Arjan married his son to the daughters of two Sikhs (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 500). When Jahangir came to Lahore and Arjan did not present himself, Chandu Lal reported him as Arjan, the ruler of Amritsar; a *farman* summoned him, and he came to Lahore with five renowned Sikhs, the words for Amritsar and renowned being uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 509). Chishti names the Sikhs who came with him: Bhai Jetha, Bhai Barana, Bhai Langa, Bhai Ti…, Bahi Chand and Bhai Manj, all but the first printed uncertainly (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 501).

The accounts of the torture differ in place and length. *A White Trail* sets it "Outside the Alamgiri gate of the Lahore fort, the Guru was forced to sit in a cauldron of boiling water. Burning sand was poured on him" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 195, folio not stated). *Walking with Nanak* places it in the walled city: "there is a place near the Rang Mahal known as ... Khoi [OCR?] [Lal Khoi?], of the Red Well"; "It is believed that Arjan was kept in a room next to this well, which was the haveli of the Mughal Diwan of Lahore", and boiling sand (the word is damaged in the printed text) was poured on his head while he was "immersed in a cauldron" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 253, folio 227); "For five days the Guru was tortured" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 252, folio 226). In Chishti he had no audience; Chandu Lal took him to his *haveli* in the Hira Mandi quarter, imprisoned him alone and tortured him for a month (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 501–502), while one of his Sikhs recited aloud the *bani* composed by Guru Arjan, the Guru's own hymns (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 499).

"On the fifth day of the torture, the Guru requested a bath in the River Ravi, flowing next to the fort"; "Guru Arjan (1563-1606)" took a dip and did not emerge (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 195, folio not stated). *Walking with Nanak* gives the same as Sikh belief — he took "a dip in the waters of the Ravi" and "did not emerge from the water" — and also writes that "His body was then thrown into the river" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 253, folio 227). Kanhaiya Lal writes that although Guru Arjan had disappeared in the river at the time of his bath and his corpse was not cremated, this *samadh* was built by way of a memorial; for the manner of his death he refers the reader to his own history of the Punjab (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 206, folio 209). Chishti has him go with his Sikhs toward the river Ravi, die, and be cremated by Sikh *khadims* at that very place, where a shrine, printed perhaps as *mandir marhi*, was raised as a marker (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 502–503).

Chishti dates the death to a Friday, the fourth of the month of Jeth *sudi*, and also to the first of Jeth, given in both the Samvat and the Hijri eras, with the years lost to illegible words and several other words uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 502); in another place he gives the year as sixteen hundred and something of the Christian era, the rest illegible (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 686). His table of the Gurus gives the place of death as Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 515).

*Walking with Nanak* adds the place of the shrine to the story: "near Lahore Fort there is a small yellow structure which is now referred to [as] the samadhi" of Guru Arjan, and "It is believed that the Guru lost his life there" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 253, folio 227). *A White Trail* writes that he spent a lot of time in Lahore, "where he was assassinated and his remains interred" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated).

In Chishti's account, Guru Hargobind later told Jahangir that Arjan's *mala*, a thousand times finer than this one, was held by Chandu Lal, and a man in Lahore through whom Chandu Lal used to have something poured on the Guru's body, the word illegible in the printed text, later killed Chandu (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 513).

## The Gurdwara

Sikh tradition records that the Guru's son and successor, Guru Hargobind, raised a memorial platform at the site of the martyrdom in the years that followed. It was under Maharaja Ranjit Singh, however, in the early nineteenth century, that the present *gurdwara* took its handsome form, embellished in the manner of Sikh sacred architecture, with a gilded dome that answers, across the Hazuri Bagh, the domes of the Badshahi Mosque. The proximity of the shrine to Ranjit Singh's own *samadhi* — his memorial tomb — binds it into the great ensemble of monuments that crowns this quarter of Lahore. Within, the *Guru Granth Sahib*, the scripture that Guru Arjan himself first compiled, is enshrined and reverently read, and the shrine functions as a living house of worship.

Khalid gives the same history: "a small structure was raised at the spot by his son, Guru Hargobind to mark the samadhi of the Guru and it is still present there to this day", and "The present structure was first erected by Maharaja Ranjit Singh with further extensions made by the Sikh community in the early years of the twentieth century" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 200, folio not stated). "After partition, the Gurdwara came under the control of the Pakistan Government" through the Evacuee Trust Property Board, and "the ETPB regularly undertakes renovation work here"; "In 1996, rooms were built in the complex" for pilgrims "from India and other parts of Pakistan", and "This new hostel was named after Mian Meer" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 200, folio not stated).

Khalid describes it from the street: "Facing the fort is a small yellow building with a golden dome. A thick yellow wall protects it", with street vendors at the base of the wall, the place "teeming with visitors on any given day", and the Badshahi Mosque beside it (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 195, folio not stated). Of the garden beside it: "This is where the Sikh ruler of Punjab, Maharaja Ranjit Singh, used to meet his subjects. A Mughal baradari (a pavilion with twelve pillars) stands in the middle of the garden"; "All of these three places are famous with the tourists"; and "A giant gateway leading into the yellow building from here facing the garden is permanently locked" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 196, folio not stated).

Inside, after a last security check, the inner structure is the same yellow as the wall, and "A couple of rooms at the entrance have been taken over by the security officials" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 197, folio not stated). The Nishan Sahib "stands at the edge of the courtyard" on "a circular platform about five feet from the ground" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 197, folio not stated). The heart of the shrine is a small room "with windows on all sides, protected by iron bars"; in its centre "a small bulge which is covered by a velvet cloth ... This is the main attraction; the samadhi of Guru Arjan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 197–198, folio not stated). "A small door from the samadhi of Guru Arjan" leads to the Langar Hall, "divided into two sections", its mats laid "in four lines" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 201, folio not stated).

Chishti's section on the "Dera" of Guru Arjan survives only in fragments: a bazaar whose name is illegible, the word Roshnai (uncertain in the printed text), the building outside the fort, only a road from the outer wall of the fort; a door with gilded ornament and painted wood (both readings uncertain), a mosque facing it, and a large gateway; an inscription, its wording uncertain in the printed text, naming the Guru and a *sahibzada*; and lines in Gurmukhi of which only "੧ਓ[OCR?]" and "ਸਤਿਗੁਰ[OCR?]" can be read (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 516). In his section "Guru Arjan's Samadh" he places the *samadh* past the Roshnai gate, between the gate and the royal fort, to the west (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 498). On the next page of the description a Gurmukhi line naming the Guru is printed, much of it uncertain: ਗੁਰੂ ਅਰਜਨ[OCR?] [illegible] ਸੰਮਤੁ[OCR?] ੧੮੭੧੮[OCR?] ਸਾਵਨ[OCR?] ਸੁਦੀ[OCR?] ੧੫[OCR?] (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 517); and a figure labelled Guru Arjan, followed by an illegible word, is pictured in the niche of the *samadh* (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 520).

## Kanhaiya Lal's Samadh

Kanhaiya Lal's *Tarikh-e Lahore* describes a *samadh* of Guru Arjan, who was the fifth Guru of the Sikh *qaum*, without naming it Dera Sahib and without a date of any kind for the Guru (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 206, folio 209). Its position fits this site: it stands opposite the Roshnai Gate of Lahore, with the wall of the fort to its east, at the distance of one road (the word for road uncertain in the printed text), and to the west, at a distance lost in the printed text, the wall of Maharaja Ranjit Singh's *samadh* (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 205–206, folios 208–209). But the same enclosure has a *baoli*, the feature of Lahore's other shrine of the Guru, Gurdwara Baoli Sahib, and the book never uses either name; whether his description belongs to Dera Sahib has not been settled (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 206, folio 209).

He gives the building two phases: formerly a small building, and in Ranjit Singh's time the grand building was made — with no year for either (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 206, folio 209). The enclosure was of fired brick and lime with a parapet; two of its doors were kept shut and all traffic passed through the great eastern door, over which letters are written in Gurmukhi script; this too, the text says, is Guru Arjan's writing, a word before it, perhaps "picture", being uncertain in the printed text. To the south within the four walls stood the grand domed *samadh* building; to its north a three-bayed hall in whose middle bay the Granth Sahib is kept with full reverence; and the *samadh*'s own platform, of brick and lime, where the description breaks off in an illegible passage (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 206, folio 209).

In one corner outside the hall was a great well with a *baoli*, that is, a well of fired brick (the word *baoli* uncertain in the printed text), its stair running from north to south down to the water, where a slab of red stone is set so that a man standing there can draw water (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 206, folio 209). In another corner stood four *samadhs* of a man's height, one of them of the mother of Lachhmi Sarkar, wife of Maharaja Ranjit Singh; and there were also rooms on the north built for the Sikhs' rest (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 206, folio 209).

On the south was a lime-plastered building called the Sikhs' *deg* (the word uncertain in the printed text), because a stone mortar is sunk in the ground and a thick wooden pestle called *wand* (the word uncertain) kept beside it for pounding *bhang*, which the Sikhs grind and drink there (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 206, folio 209).

The same book records, in its notice of Guru Hargobind's house at Mozang, that that house is now attached to the *samadh* of Guru Arjan, and its income is taken by the *pujari* of Guru Arjan's *samadh* (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 226, folio 229). The notice does not say where that *samadh* is, so it may or may not refer to this shrine.

## Devotion and Practice

Since the Partition of 1947 the shrine has been maintained within Pakistan under the Evacuee Trust Property Board, and it remains a solemn and much-visited station for the Sikh pilgrims who come to Lahore, above all upon the anniversary of the Guru's martyrdom, observed in the month of Jeth.

Khalid's chapter is a report of that anniversary on 6 June 2011. "About three hundred pilgrims from India have come to Lahore for this event, while several hundred have come from other parts of the country"; the Indians stay "for three days", after which some return "while others will visit sacred shrines in Nankana Sahib and Hassan Abdal" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 196, folio not stated). Most pilgrims were Sikhs "bearing the 5 Ks", with a few clean-shaven "Nanak Panthi Sikhs" from "South Punjab and Sindh" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 197, folio not stated). The lawn became "a temporary camp for pilgrims who do not have rooms", "primarily Pathan-Sikh families who have come from Nankana Sahib, Hassan Abdal, Peshawar and the Tribal Areas", while Indian pilgrims and "other important guests" had rooms (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 201, folio not stated).

The courtyard was tented against "the intense heat of June" and carpeted, the congregation facing the Granthi, the recitation carried through "a small speaker" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 197, folio not stated). Men prayed to the Khanda on the Nishan Sahib, touched its base and kissed their hands or touched their eyes to the platform (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 197, folio not stated). In the *samadhi* room, whose "capacity [is] no more than five people", there were "About twenty attendees", and women recited from "pocket Granths" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 197–198, folio not stated). At the conclusion of the Akhand Path "bhog (community prayer) is performed"; "Waheguru"; "the entire community prostates [as printed] to the living Guru, the Granth Sahib", and Sikh boys served cold milk, juice and "freshly cut water melons" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 201, folio not stated). The *langar* was prepared by volunteers and employees, "an overwhelming majority of who seem to be low-caste Nanak Panthis from Southern Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 201, folio not stated).

For all its renown, Khalid found the approach closed. "Today, this place is deserted": the only road "is cordoned off by the police", behind a barricade "fortified with barbwire", and only people "carrying special cards, which have been made for them by the Evacuee Trust Property Board (ETPB)" may pass; "Many local tourists return disappointed", and at the metal-detector door the sign reads 'Out' while the 'In' gate is shut (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 196, folio not stated). On ordinary days "hundreds of local visitors" peek in, and the security men's answer is: "If you are a Muslim, you are not allowed." (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 196, folio not stated). Male and female police search the pilgrims, and bags pass "through an X-ray machine" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 197, folio not stated).

Khalid met members of Pakistan's own Sikh community at the shrine. Kartar Singh Kartar was "temporarily residing at this Gurdwara for his education and being given free accommodation and food at the shrine", and joked "I am a langur (a species of monkey)" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 196, folio not stated); he is "the most highly qualified Pakistani Sikh", hoping "to do my PhD in the Punjabi language" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 202, folio not stated). "32 year old Dr. Manoj Singh" is "the first MBBS doctor from the Pakistani Sikh community", "serving at the Ganga Ram Hospital" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 201–202, folio not stated). "25 year old Premjeet Singh, the first Pakistani Sikh to have been commissioned into the Pakistan Rangers", was allotted a room "because of his social standing", shared "with five other family members" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 202, folio not stated).

Kanhaiya Lal's account, if it is of this site, shows its nineteenth-century life: the Granth Sahib kept with full reverence in the hall, rooms for the Sikhs' rest, the well from which water was drawn, and the room where the Sikhs pounded and drank *bhang* (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 206, folio 209). It had a *pujari*, who also took the income of Guru Hargobind's house at Mozang (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 226, folio 229).

## Legends and Miracles

The books record the Guru's end as more than a death. "Legend has it that the Guru was lifted alive into the sky by God" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 195, folio not stated). In the Sikh belief that *Walking with Nanak* reports, when he took his dip in the Ravi and did not come out, "the Guru had performed a miracle and passed onto the other world" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 253, folio 227).

In the same Sikh account the Sufi Mian Mir asked leave "to bury alive his oppressors. The Guru refused" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 253, folio 227). *A White Trail* notes that "the friendship between Mian Meer and Guru Arjan is also the subject of several folk tales" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 200, folio not stated), and the pilgrims' hostel built at the gurdwara in 1996 bears Mian Meer's name (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 200, folio not stated).

Chishti's account carries a story of the Guru's protection: when a *mir* sent to arrest him was killed on the road near Ambala, the rumour spread that it had happened through the blessing of Guru Arjan (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 509).

## In Verse

The books carry little of Guru Arjan's own verse, and that only in Urdu script in Chishti's book, without an English version. Chishti prints the fourth *pauri* that, he says, Arjan composed at once when Guru Ram Das asked for it, on the day he was made Guru (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 505):

بھاگ ہویا گر سنت ملایا …
جن نانک داس تمہارے جی

The *pauri* composed by Guru Arjan when Guru Ram Das seated him on the *gaddi*: its opening and its closing words as Chishti prints them, with no translation given (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 505).

He also prints three of the Guru's verse letters, *gurbani* that he explains as letters in verse, written to Guru Ram Das, each with an Urdu gloss. The first opens with this line (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 503):

میرا من لوچے گورو درشن تائین

It is a line of longing for the sight (*darshan*) of the Guru. The second carries this line, in praise of the Guru's face:

تیرا مکھ سہاوا جی

The third carries a line, several of its words uncertain in the printed text, that sets even a moment's separation beside the Kali age (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 504).

The Guru's compilation drew poets to it, at least in later report. Naveed Alam, translating the Sufi poet Shah Hussein, writes: "According to some sources, Shah Hussein travelled to Amritsar to get his poetry included in the Granth Sahib being compiled by Guru Arjun Dev (1563–1605). He offered this kafi for inclusion. Though the account is unverifiable, the thematic content of this kafi might have lent itself to the speculation." (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 186, folio 186). The poetry of Baba Farid, collected by Guru Nanak at Pak Pattan, "was later included in the Granth Sahib by Guru Arjan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 200, folio not stated).

## Setting

The books describe the quarter of Lahore around the shrine. Khalid's *A White Trail* writes that "The Roshani gate, one of the thirteen gateways that lead into the ancient walled city of Lahore, is between the Alamgiri Gate and the Royal Mosque"; lamps "were lit all along the passageway ... hence the name Roshani gate, or the gate of lights" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 205, folio not stated). "Behind it is the Heera-Mandi bazaar", and the area is "also famous for the food street that runs parallel to the wall of the Badshahi Masjid" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 205, folio not stated). Chishti counts the gate, as the Roshnai Gate, among the twelve gates of Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 498).

The river beside which the Guru died has moved. The Ravi, which flowed next to the fort, "has changed its course, moving a few kilometres away" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 195, folio not stated). The city's Sikhs have thinned too: "it is now rare that one runs into a Sikh in this city, a city which was once ruled by a Sikh and the seat of their political strength" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 196, folio not stated). Lahore holds more of the Gurus' memory than this shrine: Guru Ram Das "was also born in present day Pakistan, in the city of Lahore", and Guru Hargobind "also spent a lot of time here" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated). *Walking with Nanak* adds that Chandu Lal was "appointed as the Diwan of Lahore by the Mughal authorities" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 253, folio 227), and that Khusro "rebelled against his father and moved towards Lahore" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 255, folio 229).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The Guru's dates.** The site's account gives 1563–1606, and so does *A White Trail*, "Guru Arjan (1563-1606)" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 195, folio not stated), and *Walking with Nanak*, with his guruship from 1581 (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 315, folio 289); Dhillon gives his guruship as 1581 to 1606 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 240, folio 265). Alam prints "(1563–1605)" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 186, folio 186). Rizvi's body text prints a damaged date string, and his index "b. 1563 guru 1581-1606" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 407, folio 383; p. 466, folio 442). Chishti's year is partly illegible, sixteen hundred and something of the Christian era (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 686). Bhandari gives no year, only that he died twenty-five years after succeeding his father (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). Kanhaiya Lal gives no date of any kind (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 206, folio 209).
- **The name of the Lahore official.** The site's account calls him Chandu Shah, "the Lahore financier", and so does one passage of *Walking with Nanak* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 315, folio 289). Another passage of the same book calls him Chandu Lal, a Khatri and Diwan of Lahore (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 253, folio 227), and Chishti calls him Chandu Lal (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 509).
- **Who was to blame.** The site's account has Jahangir act on the Guru's influence and his blessing of Khusrau, with the torture at Chandu Shah's instigation. *Walking with Nanak* sets the Sikh explanation, Chandu Lal's connivance with Prithi Chand, against the Mughal sources, which are silent about Chandu Lal and give the Guru's sympathy for Khusro as the cause (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 253, folio 227; p. 255, folio 229). Chishti puts Prithi Chand's slanders and Chandu Lal's report behind the summons (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 499; p. 509).
- **Where and how Khusrau met the Guru.** The site's account says Jahangir complained that Arjan had blessed the prince. Jahangir, as *Walking with Nanak* quotes him, sets the meeting at Goindwal on the Beas and the blessing as a saffron finger-mark (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 252, folio 226). *A White Trail* has the prince stop at the Guru's *langar* in Amritsar and receive a *tilak* (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 199, folio not stated). Chishti has him come to Tarn Taran, stay three days and receive five hundred rupees (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 509).
- **The penalty.** The site's account has a heavy fine or, failing it, punishment. Jahangir, as *Walking with Nanak* quotes him, gave the Guru's property to Murtaza Khan and ordered him dealt with by the laws of Yasa (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 252, folio 226).
- **Where he was tortured, and for how long.** The site's account gives days of torture without a place. *A White Trail* sets it outside the Alamgiri gate of the fort, the Guru going to the river on the fifth day (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 195, folio not stated). *Walking with Nanak* sets it in the Mughal Diwan's *haveli* by the Red Well near the Rang Mahal, for five days (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 253, folio 227; p. 252, folio 226). Chishti sets it in Chandu Lal's *haveli* in Hira Mandi, for a month (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 501–502).
- **The manner of the torture.** The site's account has a heated iron plate, burning sand and scalding water. *A White Trail* has a cauldron of boiling water and burning sand (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 195, folio not stated); *Walking with Nanak* has him immersed in a cauldron with boiling sand poured on his head (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 253, folio 227).
- **What became of his body.** The site's account has it given to the Ravi and never recovered. *Walking with Nanak* has it thrown into the river, and reports the Sikh belief that he went into the water and did not come out (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 253, folio 227); *A White Trail* has him take a dip and not emerge, records the legend that he was lifted alive into the sky (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 195, folio not stated), and elsewhere says his remains were interred in Lahore (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated). Kanhaiya Lal has him vanish in the river while bathing, his corpse never cremated (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 206, folio 209). Chishti has him cremated by Sikh *khadims* at the spot (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 502–503).
- **The first memorial.** The site's account and *A White Trail* have Guru Hargobind raise it (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 200, folio not stated). Chishti has the Sikh *khadims* who cremated the Guru raise a marker there (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 502–503). Kanhaiya Lal says only that it was at first a small building (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 206, folio 209).
- **The present building.** The site's account and Kanhaiya Lal give it to Ranjit Singh's time (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 206, folio 209). Khalid has it first erected by Ranjit Singh, with extensions by the Sikh community in the early twentieth century (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 200, folio not stated).
- **Its colour.** The site's account calls the shrine white and gold. Khalid calls it "a small yellow building with a golden dome" behind a yellow wall (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 195, folio not stated), and *Walking with Nanak* "a small yellow structure" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 253, folio 227).
- **Which Guru he was.** The site's account and the books make him the fifth Guru; *A White Trail* once calls him "the fourth Sikh Guru, Guru Arjan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 168, folio not stated), against "fifth" on its other pages (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 162, folio not stated; p. 198, folio not stated).
- **Whether Kanhaiya Lal's samadh is this site.** Its place, opposite the Roshnai Gate between the fort and Ranjit Singh's *samadh*, fits Dera Sahib; its *baoli* could point to Gurdwara Baoli Sahib; the book names neither (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 205–206, folios 208–209; p. 206, folio 209).
- **The spelling of the name.** The site's account spells him Arjan; Alam prints "Guru Arjun Dev" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 186, folio 186).

## Legacy

The martyrdom of Guru Arjan altered the course of Sikh history. His son, Guru Hargobind, on assuming the Guruship, is remembered as having girded himself with two swords, symbolising *miri* and *piri* — temporal and spiritual authority — and as having built the Akal Takht at Amritsar, thus arming and organising the community for its own defence. The pacific movement of Guru Nanak's early followers began, from this point, to take on the resolve and martial spirit that would mark later Sikh history. Gurdwara Dera Sahib thus stands not merely as a memorial to a single death, but as the birthplace of an idea — that faith may be defended, and that the willing acceptance of martyrdom is itself a form of victory.

The books agree on the turn. Rizvi: "After the death of the Fifth Guru, Arjan, at the hands of the Mughal Jahangir, the Sikhs dropped their pacifist policy and adopted their famous martial spirit" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 419, folio 395). *Walking with Nanak* calls the Guru "Regarded as the first martryr [OCR?] [martyr] of Sikhism" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 315, folio 289), crushed "in the fight between the Mughal 'elephants'" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 255, folio 229); the assassination is "the Karbala of Sikh history" and "precipitated the development of the Khalsa" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 255–256, folios 229–230). "The martyrdom of Guru Arjan provided the Sikh community with a cohesive motivation", though militarisation had begun, following Grewal, "during the period of the fifth Sikh Guru" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 232, folio 206; p. 234, folio 208). "He was the last Guru in the line of the Gurus who focused solely on spiritual matters" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 256, folio 230), and his assassination "marks the beginning of a long conflict between Mughal authorities and Sikh Gurus" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 315, folio 289). Tegh Bahadur "was the first Guru since Guru Arjan to compose hymns" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 134, folio 116).

*A White Trail* calls him "the first Sikh martyr, assassinated by the fourth Mughal Emperor, Jahangir (1569-1627)" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 162, folio not stated), and writes that "Guru Arjan's martyrdom is a low point in Sikh history" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 206–207, folio not stated). The memory reaches far beyond Lahore: Operation Blue Star was launched "on a day when thousands of Sikhs from all over Punjab had gathered at the shrine to commemorate the death anniversary of the fifth Sikh Guru, Guru Arjan", and "In a long list of martyrs who followed Guru Arjan", its victims "became the latest entry" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 162, folio not stated). His name is among those painted after Nanak's picture on the dome at Jhaman (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 51, folio 33).
