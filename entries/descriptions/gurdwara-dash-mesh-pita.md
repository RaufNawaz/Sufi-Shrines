## Overview

Among Sukkur's small number of surviving Sikh institutions stands the Gurdwara Dash Mesh Pita — its name invoking "Dashmesh Pita," a title of reverence for Guru Gobind Singh, the tenth Sikh Guru, meaning roughly "father of the Tenth [House]." Pakistan's Evacuee Trust Property Board lists it as a currently functional gurdwara occupying a plot of some 300 square yards in Sukkur. It is a distinct site from Sukkur's two much better-documented gurdwaras — Sadhu Bela, on its island in the Indus, and Gurdwara Pehli Patshahi (Jind Pir) near the Sukkur–Rohri bridge — both of which have detailed, independently recorded histories tied to Guru Nanak's travels, whereas Dash Mesh Pita's own founding story has not survived in any source located for this entry.

None of the books consulted for this article names the Gurdwara Dash Mesh Pita, and none of them places Guru Gobind Singh in Sukkur or in Sindh. What they hold is the story of the Guru himself. Haroon Khalid's *Walking with Nanak* and *A White Trail* tell his life and the founding of the Khalsa; the first has a chapter titled with his name (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 11, folio not stated), and the second a chapter on his birthday as it is kept at Nankana Sahib (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 5, folio not stated). Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti's *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* sets out his birth, parentage and death in a table (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 516) and writes of his wars and his granth. Michel Boivin, Harish Dhillon, Taufiq Rafat's selection of Bulleh Shah and the annotator of Charles Frederick Usborne's translation of Waris Shah name him in passing, and Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian chronicle speaks of a Guru Gobind Rai who may or may not be the same man. That material is gathered below as the background of the name the gurdwara bears. It is what the books say of the Guru, never of this site. Sukkur itself appears in the books only as a place, and what they say of the city and of the Sikhs of Sindh is set out at the end as context.

## The Gurdwara

No published account explains when the gurdwara was built, who established it, or what specific event or visit it commemorates; the honorific naming after Guru Gobind Singh suggests some historical or devotional association with the tenth Guru, but nothing more specific can currently be stated. A differently named gurdwara, "Dashmesh Darbar Singh Sabha," is documented at Jacobabad, roughly 100 kilometres away in a different district — a separate institution that should not be conflated with this Sukkur site, though the similarity of naming may be a source of past confusion.

As with several of Sindh's smallest gurdwaras, what can be confirmed rests on the Evacuee Trust Property Board's official register alone: the gurdwara's name, its location in Sukkur, and its functional status. Its history, founding date, and the size and character of its present congregation remain undocumented pending a local or community source.

The books consulted for this article leave that position as it was. None of them names the gurdwara, and none records for it a founding date, a builder, a custodian, an endowment or a festival. Khalid observes that the Guru's birthday and Lohri "had no historical significance to particular shrines on this side of the border" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 176, folio not stated).

## The Guru's Names and Titles

The title Dashmesh Pita does not occur in the books consulted, but the number it carries does. Chishti sets the Guru tenth in his list, with no place named for him (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 280). Elsewhere he calls him "دسوین گورو گوبند سنگہ صاحب", the tenth Guru Gobind Singh Sahib (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 680), and "دسواں گورو گوبند سنگھ خلف گورو تیج بہا[OCR?] اور آخری گورو", the tenth Guru Gobind Singh, son of Guru Tegh Bahadur, and the last Guru, the father's name uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 686).

Khalid calls him "the tenth Sikh Guru, Guru Gobind Singh" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 81, folio 63; Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 193, folio not stated), and, elsewhere, "The prince of princes is Guru Gobind Singh, at the end of the line, the ninth incarnation of Guru Nanak" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 101, folio 83). Boivin calls him "the last Sikh Guru, Guru Gobind Singh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 53, folio 40). The annotator of Usborne's Waris Shah calls him "Guru Gobind Singh, the tenth successor of Guru Nanak" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 20, folio 19). Rafat's selection spells the name 'Guru Govind Singh' (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 27, folio not stated).

Royal titles appear as well. Khalid writes that the Guru was "referred to as Sacha Padshah, the True King, and his court was referred to as Sacha Darbar, the True Court" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 99, folio 81). He names a book of the Guru's as the "Daswan Padshah ka Granth" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 100, folio 82), and Chishti describes a granth "دسوین بادشاہ کا", of the tenth Padshah, at the Shahid Ganj darbar (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 766).

## The Life of the Guru

What follows, up to the Setting, and the Legacy at the end, is told of Guru Gobind Singh himself. None of it is told of Sukkur or of this gurdwara: it is the history behind the gurdwara's name.

A table in Chishti's book gives the Guru's birth as "سمت ۱۷۲۳[OCR?] [illegible] سدی سات[OCR?]", the year as printed and part of the date illegible, at Patna, "مقام پٹنہ"; it names his father as "تیغ بہادر" and his mother as "گجری[OCR?]", the reading of her name uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 516). The modern books give his life as running from 1666 to 1708. Boivin prints "(1666–1708)" after his name (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 53, folio 40); the back matter of *Walking with Nanak* lists "10. Guru Gobind Singh (1666-1708, Guru from 1675 to 1708)" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 317, folio 291); and *A White Trail* gives the same years (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 162, folio not stated).

His father, in Khalid's account, appointed him heir "Before heading to Delhi" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 317, folio 291). "Guru Gobind Singh had become Guru at the age of nine in the year 1675" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 98, folio 80), and the book's chronology has "1675 - Guru Gobind Singh is appointed as the next Guru" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 313, folio 287). It was "actually the young Gobind Singh", Khalid writes, who urged his father to aid the Pandits (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 132–133). When Jaita recovered Tegh Bahadur's body, he "presented it to Guru Gobind Singh at Anandpur, who then cremated the body" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 132, folio 114). Chishti, too, makes the Guru the son of Tegh Bahadur, whom Aurangzeb killed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 846).

After "the assassination of his father Guru Tegh Bahadur", Khalid writes, "The gravest threat came from his uncle Ram Rai, who was the disgruntled son of Guru Har Rai". Ram Rai approached "Emperor Aurangzeb", and the Punjab chiefs "surrounded Guru Gobind's abode in Anandpur" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 96, folio 78).

Khalid describes a royal court. "He lived in a regal tent"; he was "in command of a standing army, whose weapons were procured through the offerings of devotees or through raids upon neighbouring villages", and kept "a war drum"; he was fond of war exercises and of hunting, and "adept in Persian, Arabic and Sanskrit" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 99, folio 81). In a footnote on the same page Khalid adds that the offering of weapons at the Guru's court "was a practice that was started by Guru Hargobind but then reinforced by Guru Gobind Singh" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 99, folio 81). Elsewhere he writes that the Guru "loved war epics, used to hunt regularly and took an active part in military exercises", that Hargobind's sword passed to him, and that he "can be seen as the true descendant of Guru Hargobind" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 233, folio 207). He was, in Khalid's words, "much more militaristically inclined than his father" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 137, folio 119), and the book's back matter calls him "Known as a warrior and a poet" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 317, folio 291).

Chishti names Bhai Mani Singh and Gulab Singh as in attendance on the Guru (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 768). Dhillon writes that Bhai Mani Singh, "an eighteenth-century Sikh scholar and martyr" to whom one set of the janamsakhis is supposedly attributed, "had been the childhood companion of Gobind Singh" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 9, folio 9).

The books place the Guru's seat in different places. For Khalid it is "Anandpur, a city that had been founded by his father" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 97, folio 79), where the chiefs surrounded his abode (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 96, folio 78). Chishti names "موضع امدپور [OCR?] علاقہ کوہستان" as often the Guru's residence, the place-name uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 768).

Bhandari's *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* speaks of a Guru Gobind Rai, son of Guru Tegh Bahadur. "الحال که این نسخه بتحریر می درآید", now, as this copy is being written, he says, Guru Gobind Rai has been *sajjada nashin* "ازمدت بست و دو سال", for a period of twenty-two years (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 519). He names Makhowal on the Sutlej, a reading uncertain in the printed text, as "مسکن گوروگوبند رائے", the dwelling of Guru Gobind Rai (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 513). The book calls him Gobind Rai, never Gobind Singh, and does not itself say that he is the Guru this gurdwara is named for; the passage is given here only as a pointer.

## The Khalsa

Khalid tells the founding of the Khalsa twice. In *Walking with Nanak*: "On the first of Vaisakh, 1699 (14 April 1699)", at "Anandpur, a city that had been founded by his father", five volunteers offered their heads, though "in reality, it was a goat's blood"; they became "the Panj Pyare or the five beloved", and the Guru gave the Khalsa the five Ks, which the book lists as kangi, kesh, kirpan, kachcha and kada, two of the words damaged in the printed text (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 97, folio 79). "He founded the Khalsa (The Pure Ones) in 1699, giving the Sikh community its distinct attire and identity" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 317, folio 291), and "when Guru Gobind Singh founded the Khalsa, he ordered his followers to keep a kirpan with them at all times" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 133, folio not stated).

In *A White Trail*: "On the 1st of Vaisakh 1699, thousands of Sikhs gathered at the city of Anandpur Sahib, East Punjab, India". The Guru asked for volunteers to give their lives and led away five, returning each time "with a blood soaked sword"; "In reality however, all were safe", and the five are "the Panj Pyare (the five beloved ones)", the first Khalsa (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 164, folio not stated). He made the five Ks compulsory, Kesh, Kanga, Kara, Kachera and kirpan, and gave the men the surname Singh ('lion') and the women Kaur ('lioness') "to dilute caste identities" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 165, folio not stated). A Sikh named Surjeet told Khalid: "Guru Gobind told us that we should keep a kirpan (sword) with us at all times ... Guru Gobind Singh founded the Khalsa on the occasion of Baisakhi, giving the Sikh community a separate identity" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 164, folio not stated).

In a passage damaged in the printed text, Chishti writes that the Guru introduced new ceremonies and the name سکھہ, itself damaged, and required that iron, "لوہر کا", be kept (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 686). Boivin writes of "the tenets of the Khalsa order as instituted by the last Sikh Guru, Guru Gobind Singh (1666–1708)", and, after Oberoi, that "It included a code of conduct, and only the official institution embodied by the guru was able to provide salvation through one's following the code" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 53, folio 40). Usborne's annotator puts it in two sentences: "The Sikhs became a militant body under Guru Gobind Singh, the tenth successor of Guru Nanak. They have a religious prohibition against shaving and smoking." (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 20, folio 19).

With the Khalsa came the end of the Masands. Khalid writes that the Guru abolished the Masand system when he established the Khalsa, for which devout Sikhs believe he purged Sikhism of corruption (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 182, folio 156); "The system of Masands was abolished by Guru Gobind Singh" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 257, folio not stated), and he "finally abolished" it "because of the corruption that had seeped into it" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 315, folio 289). The daswandh system, on the other hand, found its way "into the Khalsa of Guru Gobind Singh" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 256, folio 230).

## War and Loss

The books remember the Guru as a fighter. Rafat's selection of Bulleh Shah records that Zakaria Khan's son "enacted the general massacre of the Sikhs at Shaheed Ganj in Lahore in 1746, twelve years before Bulleh Shah's death, and perpetuated the resistence begun under Guru Govind Singh." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 27, folio not stated). Khalid calls him one who "was a warrior himself who challenged the mighty Mughal Empire and set up a Sikh army" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 176, folio not stated). On Lohri, he writes, "the Sikhs were able to defeat a large Mughal army under the command of the tenth Sikh Guru, Guru Gobind Singh, in a battle fought in Muktsar, India" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 193, folio not stated), and the Nihang order "was founded by Guru Gobind Singh as a fighting body of the Khalsa" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 210, folio not stated).

Chishti writes that the Guru, son of the Tegh Bahadur whom Aurangzeb killed, "تمام عمر اوسکی تون [OCR?] کے عوض لینے مین صرف کی", that his whole life was spent in taking requital, the word for what was requited uncertain in the printed text. Under him, Chishti goes on, the Sikhs became armed; he tells of two sons killed, and of exile; and he ends, "وہ اسی غم مین دیوانہ ہوکر مرگیا", that he went mad from that same grief and died (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 846). In another passage Chishti writes that the Guru fought عالمگیر, Alamgir, and "آخر کار مارا گیا", was killed in the end, in a year the printed text gives only in part, "[illegible] سواسی[OCR?] عیسوی" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 686). In a passage much damaged in the printed text, Chishti adds that the Guru ordered the Sikhs to drink bhang against fear, and that the faqirs made a habit of it; Chishti himself calls the account "قرین قیاس", plausible (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 768).

Of the Guru's sons, *Walking with Nanak* says: "He saw the death of all of his sons during his lifetime at the hands of the Mughal authorities" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 317, folio 291); its chronology has "1705 — All the sons of Guru Gobind Singh are assassinated by the Mughal forces" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 313, folio 287). *A White Trail* says "He had four sons all of whom were martyred fighting the Mughal army" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 167–168, folio not stated), and elsewhere that the Mughals assassinated the four sons (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 199, folio not stated). Khalid also reports that a warning of Amar Das is said to refer to the martyrdom of "Guru Teg Bahadur's grandsons (Guru Gobind Singh's sons)" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 273, folio not stated).

Khalid gives the Guru's own end as an assassination: "He was assassinated at the hands of Pathan horse traders in the yea[r] 1708" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 98, folio 80), and, in the chronology, "1708 - Guru Gobind Singh is assassinated by a Pathan horse trader" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 313, folio 287). Chishti's table gives his death as "سمت ۱۷۶۵[OCR?] [illegible] سدی نومی کاتک [illegible] اس [illegible] نگر", the year as printed and parts of the date and the place illegible (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 516). In the religious-historical reading Khalid reports, the Guru is cast with Tegh Bahadur as a victim of Aurangzeb (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 234, folio 208).

## The Guru and the Granth

Khalid credits the Guru with completing the scripture and with closing the line of the Gurus. He completed the Granth and "appointed it as the next Guru for eternity" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 317, folio 291). "According to tradition, the tenth Sikh Guru, Guru Gobind Singh, concluded the institution of Gurudom by declaring that the Guru Granth Sahib was the eternal Guru" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 81, folio 63). He is "credited with completing the compilation of the living Guru, the Gur[u] Granth Sahib"; he bowed before the book; and "Through this, Guru Gobind ended the long line of Gurus" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 100, folio 82; p. 101, folio 83). "Gobind Singh decreed th[at] the Adi Granth would be the Eterna[l] Guru … after he saw the death of all of his sons during his lifetime" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 273–274, folio not stated / folio 248). The book's chronology sets the declaration of the Guru Granth Sahib as the living Guru for eternity in 1708 (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 313, folio 287). In a passage damaged in the printed text, Khalid writes that the Shabd "had still not become the Eternal" guru: "This was done by Guru Gobind Singh" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 213, folio 187).

*A White Trail* puts the decision at the founding of the Khalsa: "In 1699, when Guru Gobind Singh founded the Khalsa, he also declared that no other person after him would succeed him as Guru" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 167–168, folio not stated). He instructed that the Granth Sahib be revered as the Guru, and after him "the Granth Sahib became the living Guru for eternity" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 220, folio not stated). "The first ever Akhand Path was arranged by Guru Gobind Singh, after compiling the final version of the Guru Granth Sahib. He made five readers read it to him as he stood at one place throughout the reading" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 168, folio not stated).

Chishti writes that Bhai Mani Singh put the granth into "نظم [OCR?]", verse (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 768). At the Shahid Ganj darbar he describes a granth "دسوین بادشاہ کا", of the tenth Padshah, on a *takhtposh*, and the other granth, which he calls "گرنتہ [OCR?] آدہ [OCR?]", a reading uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 766). Khalid's *In Search of Shiva* names the Guru as the author of a further book: the Nanghe Sadhus "refused to acknowledge the supremacy of the Guru Granth Sahib as the eternal guru and instead started worshipping the book written by Guru Gobind Singh known as the Dasant Granth" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).

## The Poet

Khalid calls the Guru a poet, but none of the books consulted quotes his verse. He writes that "Gobind Singh wrote various verses extolling the [Sodhi caste] and its ancient history", a passage whose reading is uncertain in the printed text (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 274, folio 248). He also retells a tale from the Guru's "Vichitr Natak which forms a portion of the Daswan Padshah ka Granth", in which the Guru explained how the Sodhis replaced the Bedis. The tale names Sodhi Rai, the Bedis at 'Kashi' and the king of 'Madar Des', and in it the Bedis vowed that they "would return the compliment in Kaliyuga when their descendant Nanak would raise the Sodhis to a prime position in the world" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 100, folio 82).

## How the Guru Is Remembered

The Guru's birthday is kept with martial games. Khalid writes that "on the occasion of his birthday his devotees pay tribute to him by enacting scenes of war exercises" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 99, folio 81). In *A White Trail*, which gives a chapter to the birthday at Nankana Sahib, he writes: "Engaging in sports on the occasion of Guru Gobind Singh's birthday has always been an essential feature". Traditionally these are "war games, including exhibitions of horse riding, swordsmanship, archery, spearing and the use of stick", because the Guru was a warrior himself (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 176, folio not stated). The songs and recitations at Nankana are "dedicated to the bravery of Guru Gobind Singh, with an emphasis on the valour of the Singh", and "all the recitations from the night are dedicated to the Guru and the significance of the Khalsa" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 177–178, folio not stated). The birthday is "celebrated only a few days before" Lohri (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 193, folio not stated).

Khalid's dates for it differ. The birthday was celebrated at Nankana Sahib on "10th of January 2011" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 172–173, folio not stated), but "11th January 2011 is when the actual birthday is, according to the Sikh calendar formulated by Pal Singh Purewal" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 179, folio not stated). Jathedar Puran Singh "celebrated the birthday of Guru Gobind Singh according to the previous calendar, whereas the SGPC celebrated it according to the new one" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 217, folio not stated).

His image recurs in other places Khalid visits. On the banner at Janamasthan he is "a young, good-looking man, wearing a silk gown and several pearl necklaces", with "a tiara adorned with beautiful jewels"; an eagle, a baaz, rests on his right hand, and "the quiver on his shoulder was full of arrows" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 94, folio 76). In *A White Trail* the Janamasthan banner shows him wearing "a shiny dress with a pearl necklace around his neck, and a turban that looks like a tiara" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 228, folio not stated). The dome at Jhaman shows "Guru Gobind Singh in battle and then Guru Gobind Singh along with his sons, Jujhar Singh and Fateh Singh" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 51, folio 33). There are pictures of him on the wall of Rajesh's gurdwara at Manora (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 230, folio 204), and two in the kirtan hall at Panja Sahib, one of them showing him "collecting money for Langar" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 169, folio not stated).

## Setting: Sukkur

The books say a little of the city, though nothing of this gurdwara. Jürgen Schaflechner names Sukkur as the city of the Sadh Bela island *tīrtha*, "in the city of Sukkur on an island in the River Indus" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 138, folio not stated), and Boivin tells the origin of Sadh Belo (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 68–69). In Harish Dhillon's retelling of the janamsakhis, the journey runs from Multan "to Sukkur from where he turned southwards along the river Indus" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 194, folio 214).

Boivin sets Sukkur at a corner of the Rohri–Sukkur–Shikarpur triangle (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 31, folio 18). It is the city of the 'Sukkur riots', which he dates "between December of 1939 and February of 1940"; their first victim was Bhagat Kanwar Ram, and there "the local Hindus opposed this rehabilitation since it was located in the harbour on the Indus River" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 61, folio 48). Writing of Sehwan, he contrasts it with Sukkur: "Contrary to other cities in Northern Sindh – for instance, Sukkur, following the Manzilgah riots – no communal violence had ever been registered" there (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 71, folio 58). He mentions, too, "an orphanage in Sukkur" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 104, folio 91), and that Dadi Dhan "was born in Sukkur, Sindh, and her father was a famous lawyer" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 115, folio 102).

H. T. Sorley counts Sukkur among "the four large towns of modern Sind", Karachi, Hyderabad, Sukkur and Shikarpur, whose rise towards the second half of the eighteenth century displaced older towns, among them Tatta, Nasarpur, Rohri, Sehwan and Kandiaro (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 30, folio 21). He quotes Major P. Goldney's report that Upper Sind, at the time of Nadir Shah's invasion of Hindustan and sack of Delhi, formed part of the Subah of Multan, and that its "Moghulee" portion, Sukkur, Bukkar and Shikarpur, was then annexed to the Durani kingdom (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 38–39, folios 29–30).

Bhandari's chronicle passes through Sukkur with the armies pursuing Dara. News reached them that Dara "از بهکر گذشته بست و پنجم محرم در سکهر نزول نمود", that having passed Bhakkar he had halted at Sukkur on the twenty-fifth of Muharram; Bhandari gives the distance from that stage to Sukkur as "شصت و سه کروه", sixty-three kos, on one side of the river and "قریب صد کروه", nearly a hundred kos, on the other (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 51). Shaikh Mir, Diler Khan and Qubad Khan crossed "بجانب سکهر", towards Sukkur. On 5 Safar Shaikh Mir came within a distance of Sukkur that the printed text gives only in part, "[illegible] ده کروسے سکهر"; because of "کثرت بیشه و تراکم اشجار خاردار و مراحل دشوارگذار", the thick jungle, the crowding of thorny trees and the hard stages of the road, many beasts perished and the baggage did not arrive, and on the sixth they reached Sukkur (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 51). Shaikh Mir stayed one day at Sukkur, and the zamindars told him "از بست و پنج کروسے سکهر راهی بجانب قندهار جدا می شود", that twenty-five kos from Sukkur a road branches off towards Kandahar; Saf Shikan Khan made an appointment to the *faujdari* of Sukkur, "فوج علی بیگ[OCR?] را بفوجداری سکهر", the appointee's name uncertain in the printed text (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 50).

## Setting: The Sikhs and the Nanakpanth in Sindh

The books describe the province, not this gurdwara, and say much of Sindh's followers of Nanak. Boivin writes that colonial rule brought a renewed religiosity "among the Sindhis, whether they were Muslim, Sikh or Hindu", performed in the darbar, a term "used by the Sikhs, the Muslims and the Hindus" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 37–38, folios 24–25). Of the census he reports that "Sindh's population statistics are not available in the first census of 1872", and that, in the figures he quotes from Hughes, Sindh had 1,718,688 Muslims, 393,092 Hindus, 5,034 Christians and "86,663 persons put in a category titled 'Other castes such as Sikhs, Parsis, Jews, etc.'" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 38, folio 25). He quotes Jethmal Parsram's summary of "the many religions that have flourished in Sindh, like Buddhism, Jainism and Hinduism. More recently, he added, two other religions had become dominant: Sikhism and Sufism" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 49, folio 36).

"Among the Lohanas and related mercantile groups, two main cults were followed: the Nanakpanth and the Daryapanth", Boivin writes (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 42–43, folios 29–30). "In Sindh, many Nanakpanthis belonged to a subgroup known as the Udasis" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 53, folio 40), the page on which he writes of the Khalsa order the Guru instituted. "In nineteenth-century Sindh, the Nanakpanth, or 'the path of Nanak', was probably the dominant religious path among the Hindu Sindhis" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 90, folio 77). The recomposition of the religious scene "is attested to by the creation and the development of several important Nanakpanthi darbars throughout Sindh", and "although some darbars had been created in the late eighteenth century, there was a renaissance of the Nanakpanthi darbars in the second half of the nineteenth century" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 53–54, folios 40–41); his conclusion speaks of "the building of many new Nanakpanthi darbars all over Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 199, folio 186). After the Singh Sabha process, he writes, "The Sindhi Nanakpanthis were thus not included in the Sikh community, and they identified themselves as being a Hindu religious sect" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 200, folio 187). "Due to the spread of the Nanakpanth in Sindh, the Hindu Sufis were very familiar with Sikh terminology" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 202, folio 189). In an endnote he records that "The Udasis of Sindh are coined as being a heterodox Sikh tradition" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 209, folio 196), and in another that "the pre-partition temples devoted to Jhulelal in Sindh were built with a dome, as were some Sikh samadhis" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 211, folio 198).

Schaflechner writes: "Many Lohanas in Sindh follow the teachings of Nanak, somewhat obscuring their religious affiliation for the colonial officers. Hence, in the 1881 census, Lohanas from Shikarpur and Hyderabad were counted as Sikhs, while ten years later, in the 1891 census, they were classified as Hindus" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 80, folio not stated). He writes that "many middle- and upper-class Hindus in Sindh are followers of the Nanak paṃth" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 257, folio not stated), and notes that "it is common to cover one's head before walking into a tikānā (Sin.), the temple containing the Guru Granth Sahib" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 286, folio not stated).

Khalid writes of Nanak Panthi Hindus, "thousands of such Sikhs", living "in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Punjab and Sindh in Pakistan" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 98, folio 80). The communities who refused the Khalsa and called themselves followers of Guru Nanak "still exist in Sindh and in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa. Baptised Sikhs look down upon them", and at Manora he saw them "now merging into the larger groups of Sikhs" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 230, folio 204). In *A White Trail* he writes that Nanak Panthi Sikhs "belong to the areas of South Punjab and Sindh", that their temples in Sindh and Southern Punjab hold portraits of Guru Nanak alongside Hindu deities, and that "They are found in very large numbers in the province of Sindh" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 197, folio not stated). Of Guru Nanak himself, Khalid writes that "Nanak visited Uch Sharif on his way to Sindh, towards the end of his travels, after he had already visited the East, South, North and West" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 278, folio 252), and reports Iqbal Qaiser's identification of the Clifton temple as "the temple which Nanak had visited on his tour of Sindh" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 217, folio 191).

## Legacy

Khalid presents the Guru as the one who brought Nanak's work to its end. "Guru Gobind is seen as someone who completed Nanak's mission"; "He codified the teachings of Nana[k] and founded the Khalsa" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 94, folio 76; p. 95, folio 77). The legacy that Hargobind began "was completed by Guru Gobind Singh" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 256, folio 230). The Guru concluded, Khalid writes, that "both Hinduism and Islam were flawed religious traditions", and that "any follower of Nanak who refused to obey the commandments of the subsequent Gurus was not a Sikh" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 96, folio 78). The book's blurb speaks of "the institutionalized Khalsa of Guru Gobind Singh" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 2, folio not stated), and *A White Trail* writes that "by the time of the tenth Guru" the movement "took up an institutionalized form … codified by the formation of the Khalsa" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 219–220, folio not stated).

The Khalsa he founded came to define Sikh identity in law. The Gurdwara Act of 1925, Khalid writes, legally defined that identity, "identifying it with the Khalsa of Guru Gobind Singh" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 36, folio 18). Communities that "refused to incorporate themselves into the Khalsa of Guru Gobind" call themselves followers of Guru Nanak instead (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 230, folio 204), and Nanak Panthis do not believe "in the Khalsa founded by Guru Gobind" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 197, folio not stated); they "don't believe in the Khalsa of Guru Gobind Singh" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 220, folio not stated). Inderjit's garb, Khalid notes, "was that of a baptized Sikh, the Khalsa of Guru Gobind Singh" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 306, folio 280).

Khalid traces later institutions and figures to him. The Damdami Taksal is "a Sikh religious organization founded by the tenth Sikh Guru, Guru Gobind Singh (1666-1708), to impart religious education to people" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 162, folio not stated). Bhindranwale "passionately claimed that the new generation of Sikhs do not respect the Sikh way of life as taught by Guru Gobind Singh" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 163, folio not stated), and "There is a view that Ranjit was a true follower of Guru Gobind Singh" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 208, folio not stated).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **His place in the line of Gurus.** The site's account calls him the tenth Sikh Guru. Chishti sets him tenth in his list (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 280) and calls him the tenth and the last Guru (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 686); Khalid calls him "the tenth Sikh Guru" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 81, folio 63); Boivin "the last Sikh Guru" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 53, folio 40). Usborne's annotator calls him "the tenth successor of Guru Nanak" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 20, folio 19), and Khalid, in another passage, "the ninth incarnation of Guru Nanak" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 101, folio 83).
- **His name.** The site's account and most of the books write Guru Gobind Singh. Rafat's selection writes 'Guru Govind Singh' (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 27, folio not stated). Bhandari writes of a Guru Gobind Rai, son of Guru Tegh Bahadur, and does not say that he is Gobind Singh (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 519; p. 513).
- **His death.** Khalid: assassinated in 1708 "at the hands of Pathan horse traders" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 98, folio 80), or, in the same book's chronology, "by a Pathan horse trader" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 313, folio 287). Chishti, in one passage: "آخر کار مارا گیا", killed in the end, in a year only partly legible (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 686); in another: "وہ اسی غم مین دیوانہ ہوکر مرگیا", he went mad from grief and died (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 846). Chishti's table gives the year of death as "سمت ۱۷۶۵[OCR?]" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 516); Boivin and Khalid give 1708 (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 53, folio 40; Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 162, folio not stated).
- **His sons.** *Walking with Nanak*: he saw the death of "all of his sons" at the hands of the Mughal authorities (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 317, folio 291), all of them assassinated by the Mughal forces in 1705 (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 313, folio 287). *A White Trail*: "four sons all of whom were martyred fighting the Mughal army" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 167–168, folio not stated), and, elsewhere, the four sons assassinated by the Mughals (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 199, folio not stated). Chishti: two sons killed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 846).
- **When the line of Gurus was closed.** *A White Trail* puts the declaration that no person would succeed him in 1699, at the founding of the Khalsa (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 167–168, folio not stated). *Walking with Nanak* has him decree the Adi Granth the eternal Guru "after he saw the death of all of his sons during his lifetime" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 273–274, folio not stated / folio 248), and dates the declaration to 1708 (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 313, folio 287).
- **Where he lived.** Khalid: Anandpur, where the chiefs surrounded his abode (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 96, folio 78). Chishti: "موضع امدپور [OCR?] علاقہ کوہستان", often his residence (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 768). Bhandari, of the Guru Gobind Rai he names: Makhowal on the Sutlej (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 513).
- **His birthday.** Khalid records the birthday celebrated at Nankana Sahib on 10 January 2011 (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 172–173, folio not stated), but the actual birthday, by Pal Singh Purewal's calendar, as 11 January 2011 (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 179, folio not stated); Jathedar Puran Singh kept it by the previous calendar and the SGPC by the new one (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 217, folio not stated).
- **Whether Sindh's followers of Nanak are Sikhs.** Boivin: after the Singh Sabha process the Sindhi Nanakpanthis "were thus not included in the Sikh community", and identified themselves as a Hindu sect (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 200, folio 187). Khalid calls the Nanak Panthi Hindus "thousands of such Sikhs" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 98, folio 80) and writes of Nanak Panthi Sikhs in Sindh (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 197, folio not stated). Schaflechner reports Lohanas counted as Sikhs in the census of 1881 and as Hindus in that of 1891 (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 80, folio not stated).
