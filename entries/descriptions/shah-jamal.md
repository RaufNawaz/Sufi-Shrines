## Overview

The shrine of Baba Shah Jamal, in the locality that now bears his name adjoining Ichhra and opposite Forman Christian College in Lahore, commemorates Syed Shah Jamal, a Sufi of Hussaini Syed descent who lived in Lahore in the reign of the Mughal emperor Akbar. The site is known as his *Damdama*, the building he raised and named in his own lifetime. Today it is best known for its exuberant Thursday-night *dhamal*, when dhol drummers drive crowds of devotees, students, musicians and curious visitors into rhythmic, trance-like dancing from dusk until the early hours; this weekly spectacle has made Shah Jamal one of the most written-about expressions of popular Sufi devotional culture in Punjab.

The books on Lahore and its saints know the site well, and for more than a century. Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori's tazkira of the saints of the Punjab, composed in 1875, gives him a notice of his own in its Suhrawardi chapter, headed "Hazrat Shah Jamal Suhrawardi Lahori", and supplies the reason the place carries its name: his mazar lies in Lahore adjoining mauza Ichhra, and it is called Shah Jamal's *damdama* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 185). Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti's *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, written as an account of the buildings, mazars, tombs and mosques of Lahore's environs (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 28), devotes a long chapter to him, headed with the account of Hazrat Shah Jamal Qadiri Suhrawardi (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 690), and describes the building, its keepers, its offerings and its *ʿurs* as he found them. Kanhaiya Lal's history of Lahore lists a tomb of Shah Jamal among the Muslim sites outside the city (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 4, folio 3). Scott Kugle, writing of Lahore's saints, mentions him in passing as the pir of a disciple, "whose tomb is still a popular pilgrimage place" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 207, folio 186), and Haroon Khalid records a legend told at the shrine (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 31, folio 13).

These sources do not always agree. They give his death in different years, trace his spiritual chain through different masters, name him by different orders, and count the storeys of the sunken *Damdama* differently. Each account is set out below as its source gives it, and the differences are gathered under "Where the Sources Disagree".

## The Life of the Saint

According to the account the shrine keeps, Syed Shah Jamal was of Hussaini Syed descent and traced his lineage to Jalaluddin Surkh-Posh Bukhari of Uch Sharif; this archive records his full name as Syed Shah Jamal Uddin Naqvi Bukhari. His father is reported to have been Syed Ahmad Shah, the name this archive's entry for his brother, Baba Shah Kamal, gives independently, and it is reported that the family moved from Kashmir to Sialkot and from Sialkot to Lahore, where both brothers were born and where both are counted among the saints. He is said to have come to Ichhra to see Shah Kamal, whose shrine still stands close to his own, and to have pitched his camp a short distance from his brother's house.

Lahori agrees that he was a Husaini Sayyid, and adds that his descendants still lived at Sialkot in his day: this Hazrat was a Sayyid Husaini, and his descendants live to this day in Sialkot (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 185). Lahori places the family's continuing line at Sialkot rather than at the Lahore shrine. Chishti's chapter, in a passage partly illegible in the copy consulted, likewise speaks of something of theirs still present at Sialkot (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 690).

Both books know the brother. Lahori writes that his second brother, Shah Kamal, was also devout and ascetic, a man of *jazb* and *shughl*, and the marvels of both are famous among most people (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 185). Lahori gives Shah Kamal no grave, no date and no place; his index lists him as Shah Kamal, brother of Shah Jamal Lahori (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 326, folio 314). Chishti calls them two true brothers, one Shah Jamal and the other Shah Kamal, and remarks on the fitness of their names, calling each name true to its bearer (the phrase uncertain in print) (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 690). Elsewhere Chishti places Shah Kamal's tomb adjoining the khanqah of Shah Jamal (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 661).

The shrine's account holds that he belonged jointly to the Qadiriyya, Suhrawardiyya and Chishtiyya orders, and that he was, tradition holds, a special disciple of Sheikh Khokar Baig Suhrawardi. He lived in Lahore during the reign of the Mughal emperor Akbar, whose syncretic Din-i-Ilahi he is remembered for having resisted, working instead to draw people back towards orthodox Islamic practice. A separate compendium of Pakistani Sufi saints, Alam Faqri's *Tazkirah Awliya-e-Pakistan*, traces his Suhrawardi lineage specifically to Bahauddin Zakariya of Multan.

Lahori sets out that line in eight steps, from Shah Jamal back to Baha al-Din Zakariya of Multan. In this telling Shah Jamal was the disciple of Shaikh Gada Beg, he of Shah Sharaf, he of Shah Ma'ruf, he of Ja'far al-Din, he of Ziya al-Din, he of Shaikh Jamal, he of Shaikh Arif Sadr al-Din, and he of his own father, Shaikh Baha al-Din Zakariya Multani (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 185).

Chishti gives the chain twice, both times running to Shaikh Shihab al-Din Suhrawardi and on to ʿAli. The chain of one Husain Shah runs from Shah Saad al-Dastar Posh[OCR?] through a Hazrat Asad Nabi, a reading uncertain in the printed text, then Hazrat Shah Jamal Sahib, then Hazrat Makhdum Kala Beg, also an uncertain reading, to Shah Sharaf, and on through Hazrat Shaikh Shihab al-Din Suhrawardi to ʿAli (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 610). In his chapter on Shah Jamal himself the chain reads, in part: a Hazrat Makhdum whose name is illegible; then Shah Sharaf; Shah Ma'ruf Shah; Mu'in al-Din; a Hazrat whose name, ending in -din, is partly illegible; Shah Shihab al-Din Suhrawardi; a Habib whose second name is illegible; Sari Saqati; Ma'ruf Karkhi; Habib Ajami; Da'ud Ta'i; Hasan Basri; and Murtaza ʿAli. Many of these names are uncertain readings in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 695).

Lahori counts him a Suhrawardi, placing him in that chapter of his book (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 18, folio 6), and Kanhaiya Lal calls him Shah Jamal Suhrawardi (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 169, folio 172). Chishti names him both Qadiri and Suhrawardi in his heading (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 690). Kugle, in a note on the Qalandars of present-day Lahore, groups Shah Jamal with Shah Hussayn and Miyan Mir among the great Qadiri saints of the city with whom the Qalandars are still associated (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 326, folio 305, ch. 4 n. 7).

Chishti speaks of his renown in his lifetime: the Hazrat's fame spread among poor and rich alike, and many came to him to gain benefit (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 691); the opening words of the sentence are uncertain in print. It is reported that devotees know him for his open-handedness as *Sakhi Sarwar*, a title that in the Punjab more usually belongs to Sultan Sakhi Sarwar of Dera Ghazi Khan, whose shrine this archive also holds; it is set down here as reported, and not as an identification of the two.

Born, by the shrine's account, in 1587, he is generally held to have died in the later seventeenth century, though sources differ on the precise year: 1649 and 1671 both appear, the shrine's own tradition holding to 1671 and commemorating his death on the 4th of Rabi-ul-Sani. Lahori gives a different year, written out in words: the fourteenth of Rabi al-Thani, 1050 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 185). Chishti's date is damaged in the copy consulted. He announces the date of the saint's death without a legible year (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 690), and later gives the day as a Thursday, the fourth of Rabi al-Thani, the year illegible (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 696).

His death is remembered as inseparable from his devotions: emerging from a *chilla*, he died as the roof of his cell collapsed, and as people laboured to free him a voice from within is said to have commanded that he not be pulled out, but that his grave be raised over that very spot, which was done.

## Disciples

Two of the books remember Shah Jamal chiefly through a disciple, Hassu Teli of Lahore. Kanhaiya Lal says of Shaikh Hassu Teli that he was the murid of Shah Jamal Suhrawardi (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 169, folio 172), and repeats it in a second passage (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 269, folio 272). Kugle writes that Hassu Tell "was also a Qadiri Sufi of Lahore and was a disciple of Shah Jamal (whose tomb is still a popular pilgrimage place)" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 207, folio 186); in Kugle's sentence the word Qadiri describes the disciple, not the master. Chishti's account of a miracle of Hasu Teli has the man renounce the world and go to a Hazrat whose name is printed as something like Sa Chamal; the reading of the name is uncertain in the printed text, and this may be Shah Jamal (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 609).

Lahori's index lists Shah Jamal Lahori on three printed pages, 168, 169 and 173, the last being his own notice (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 315, folio 303). Outside his own notice Lahori names him as the pir who set right the weights of a Lahore grain merchant, in the notice of that merchant (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, entry no. 119).

The shrine's own tradition tells of another disciple, Sheikh Fakhr-ud-Din. A cheating Hindu businessman was given a cantaloupe by the saint to feed his wife; twin sons followed, one of whom, named Sheikh Fakhr-ud-Din by Shah Jamal himself, became a devoted lifelong disciple, and the saint is also said to have saved that family by calling them outside moments before their roof collapsed. It is also reported that Sheikh Fakhr-ud-Din stayed in the saint's service all his life and that his grave lies inside the shrine. Chishti records a house bought for Shaikh Fakhr al-Din in the Chorhi Mori quarter, both words uncertain in print, and adds that the house is still known by the saint's name, as the house of Shah Jamal (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 691). He also records that Fakhr al-Din's descendants made an offering to the shrine in the name of the late Hazrat Shah Jamal, dedicating something whose name is illegible and the rent of a house and four shops, to Shaikh Ahmad Bakhsh (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 691).

## The Shrine

The site itself, tradition says, was built and named "Damdama" by Shah Jamal in his own lifetime, and it was one of several stations of his austerities. Lahori's notice confirms the name: his radiant mazar is in Lahore adjoining mauza Ichhra, and it is called Shah Jamal's *damdama* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 185). The modern edition of Lahori's book gives the place in its location table as mauza Ichhra in the author's day and Shah Jamal Colony, Ichhra, at present (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 289, folio 277). Alam Faqri's *Tazkirah Awliya-e-Pakistan* independently places the shrine on Achhra Road facing west, in Shah Jamal Colony, Lahore, consistent with this entry's own location.

Chishti locates the tomb, an exalted *maqbara* (the word uncertain in print), to the south of Lahore, toward the Sarai Goliyan Wali, near the village of Ichhra, its name printed in an uncertain form (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 690). Kanhaiya Lal places his tomb near mauza Ichhra in two passages whose wording differs: in one, his tomb and *madrasa* are near mauza Ijhra[OCR?] (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 169, folio 172); in the other, the word beside the tomb is printed as something like *madmah*, perhaps *damdama*, a reading uncertain in the printed text (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 269, folio 272). Kanhaiya Lal's own notice of the shrine survives, in the copy consulted, as a single damaged line at a break in the book's pagination, and its contents cannot be relied on (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 313, folio 316).

Chishti's account of the building, itself damaged in the copy consulted, speaks of labourers brought from the Sarai Golan Wali and paid double wages (the word uncertain in print), of a structure of several storeys, and of a haveli and an objection raised against the building (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 690). The shrine's tradition tells the same story at greater length: a princess, the sister of the emperor Akbar, objected that the tall tower overlooked her palace (see "Legends and Miracles"). Lahori's notice, too, describes the building and the storeys that sank, four of them in his account (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 185). Lahori's index places a Sultan Begum, sister of Akbar Badshah, on page 173, the page of Shah Jamal's notice, among the figures of Akbar's and Jahangir's Lahore indexed around it (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 319–326); what Lahori says of her is not recorded in the sources consulted here, and the link to the legend's princess is only a possibility.

Chishti describes the shrine as it stood in his day in close detail. The building was three-storeyed, though the reading is uncertain in print. There was a square *tharha* (platform) with a stair on the south side, a domed brick *hujra*, nine brick steps, *burjes*, and a brick *chabutra* ten *gaz* across with a niche for a lamp (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 693–694). The *tharha* served as the graveyard of Shaikh Amir Bakhsh Havaldar, and held the graves of Amir Bakhsh's mother, of Fateh Bibi and of Musammat Khadija, the last name uncertain in print (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 693). Chishti also lists a mosque, a *madrasa*, a well, a stair of seven steps, and a chamber printed as *hujra qalibi*, both readings uncertain; the enclosure was built by Shaikh Amir Bakhsh in a year of which only the words for twelve hundred can be read, a year in the 1200s, the rest illegible (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 694).

An inscription Chishti copied names the enclosure wall and its patrons. It records the *chardiwari* of His Exalted Honour Hazrat Shaikh Jamal Sahib Qadiri, may God sanctify his precious secret, raised in fulfilment of a vow (the word uncertain in print), and names Fazl Bakhsh and Shaikh Ghulam Mustafa, known as Nand[OCR?]; part of the text is omitted in the copy. It closes with the date of building, the fifth of Jumada al-Awwal of a year printed as 1299, the day, the month and the last two digits all uncertain in print (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 695). The enclosure measured six and a quarter by five and a quarter *gaz*, with four *burjis*; the *taʿwiz* over the grave was a handsome white; and the graves of the woman who swept the shrine and of Mir Taqi lay there too (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 695).

The tomb itself is today enclosed within a mosque of relatively modern construction, single-domed with four minarets, reflecting rebuilding rather than preserved Mughal fabric; an adjoining graveyard lies beside it. The shrine, of the Suhrawardi line and the Ahl-e-Sunnat tradition, has been in the care of the government's Auqaf Department since 1960. It is reported that before that its upkeep and adornment fell first to Shah Jamal himself, who oversaw the building in his own lifetime, and afterwards to the kings, maharajas and devotees who came after him.

## Keepers of the Shrine

Chishti's informant was the shrine's *sajjada nashin* in his day, Shaikh Amir Bakhsh, *sajjada nashin* of the *khanqah* of the late Hazrat Shah Jamal, whose father was Shaikh Roshan Ali; a Shah Sharaf is named as the father-in-law of Shaikh Roshan Ali (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 665). Chishti lists the members of the family: Shaikh Amir Bakhsh Havaldar (the title uncertain in print), Shaikh Ramzani, Sheikh Nathu[OCR?], Muhammad Azam[OCR?], Qudratullah, Mehr Bakhsh, Hafiz Amanullah, Saifullah, Lutfullah[OCR?] and Haidar Bakhsh (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 692–693). The birth of Ramzani is dated to a Hijri year of which only the words for twelve hundred can be read, a year in the 1200s (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 692). Of the regard in which the Shaikh family hold the saint, Chishti writes, in a sentence partly illegible, that the Shaikhs so revere Hazrat Shah Jamal that to this day none of them, it seems, will swear by him; the words that would settle the sense cannot be read (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 692).

The shrine was swept by a family of its own. Chishti names Musammat Subhan, a name uncertain in print, a sweeper of the Musalli community (also an uncertain reading) and a new Muslim, serving as sweeper; her daughter Karima[OCR?] was married to Hidayatullah, Karima's daughter was betrothed to Mir Taqi, his son was Qasim Ali, and Sultan Shah and Fateh Ali Shah, sons of Sayyid Ahmad son of Qasim Ali, are named after them (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 693). The sweepers' place was disputed. Amir Bakhsh and Nabi Bakhsh, a name uncertain in print, said that the two came there only on Thursdays, for the *chadhat*, the offerings; the income is put at about three hundred rupees a year; and they declared that they would appoint someone else as sweeper there, or sit there themselves (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 693).

The current *sajjada nashin*, a descendant of the saint, continues to hold *Khatam Khawajgan* ceremonies.

## Devotion and Practice

Today the shrine is best known not for quiet contemplation but for its exuberant Thursday-night *dhamal*, when devotees, students, musicians and curious visitors gather from dusk until the early hours as dhol drummers, most famously the late Pappu Sain, whose successors continue the tradition, drive crowds into rhythmic, trance-like dancing, alongside *qawwali*, *langar* and the lighting of lamps. The crowds are considerable: on the order of 10,000 to 15,000 visitors a day, rising to 200,000-250,000 for the annual *ʿurs*, held on the 3rd to the 5th of Rabi-ul-Sani with the ritual washing (*gusal*) of the tomb, the offering of the *chadar*, and drumming and *dhamal*.

Thursday was already the shrine's day in Chishti's time. He records Thursday offerings in cash and in kind, of which a share went to Shaikh Amir Bakhsh, and something fixed in the khanqah's name for the *ʿurs* and for repairs; the words are partly uncertain in print (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 692). Of the *ʿurs* he writes that it falls on the fourth of Rabi al-Thani; that hundreds come; that *halwa* is distributed; and that those who come are all people, Hindu and Muslim; several of these words, the date among them, are uncertain readings in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 692).

Besides his *Damdama* cell, the shrine's tradition holds, Shah Jamal kept *chillas* (forty-day retreats) at Sheikhupura, Shah Rehman, Shahdara Lahore and Chanpatpur in India. Chishti too lists places of his *chilla* where vows and the like were still offered: at Sheikhupura, where there is also a grave; at a place whose printed name, read as something like Bar Bhi Shah and ending in an illegible word, cannot be read with certainty; and at a place printed as Shah Madra, also an uncertain reading (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 693).

The site illustrates the continuing vitality of shrine-centred devotional practice in Lahore, and its shadows as well: the city's homeless rely on the shrine's *langar* and offerings, even as questions over noise, crowding and drug use in the vicinity, a real safety concern for pilgrims, have periodically drawn official scrutiny.

## Legends and Miracles

Legends gather thickly around the *Damdama*. The best known tells of a princess, Akbar's sister, who, annoyed that the tall tower overlooked her palace, demanded he lower it; the saint performed *dhamal* on its top floor, and storey after storey sank into the earth until only two remained, whereupon he predicted that her palace would not outlast his, a prophecy fulfilled, it is said, when Sikh forces later destroyed it.

Chishti relates the sinking of the building among the saint's marvels, and in his telling five storeys sank (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 691). Lahori's notice also tells of the sunken storeys, and counts four (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 185).

Haroon Khalid records a larger version of the saint's anger, told at the shrine: "at the shrine of Shahjamal in Lahore it is said that the saint buried an entire city under his foot in wrath long before it was discovered that the location of his shrine could be the site of a mound with archaeological importance" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 31, folio 13).

The stories of Sheikh Fakhr-ud-Din, the twin son of the Hindu businessman, and of the family saved from a falling roof, belong to the same body of tradition; they are told above, under "Disciples". So does the manner of his death, under the collapsing roof of his *chilla* cell. Lahori closes his account of the brothers with the remark that the marvels of both are famous among most people (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 185).

## Setting: Ichhra and the Canal

The books also speak of the place around the shrine, and what follows is about Ichhra and the locality, not about the shrine. Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian history, composed in the fortieth regnal year of Aurangzeb, knows Ichhra as a halting-place outside the city. After Dara Shikoh had left Lahore for Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 68), Khalilullah Khan and Raja Jai Singh[OCR?], ordered not to enter Lahore, marched on 11 Dhu al-Hijja and halted for a day at Ichhra, on that side of Lahore, to put the army in order; the place-name is uncertain in print (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 67). On the return, the emperor camped on the twenty-fourth of Muharram on the open ground of Ichhra near Lahore, received there the amirs who had been in the city, and on the twenty-fifth rode from Ichhra toward Lahore (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 65).

Chishti preserves a story of the village's sanctity in another saint's life. Mian Natha told Mian Mir that he used to sit in seclusion in mauza Ichhra, and left it because all the date palms there recited the *tasbih*, glorifying and praising God, which disturbed his concentration; the place-name and the word for the trees are uncertain in print (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 287).

The locality that took the saint's name lived through the violence of Partition. Haroon Khalid, who does not mention the shrine, records the memory of Mary, an 83-year-old widow whose family had "used to live near Shahjamal on the canal that now divides Lahore into two parts"; before Partition she played on its banks, and near Partition she saw "mutilated bodies floating in the water" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 132, folio not stated).

Lahori's book, in which Shah Jamal's notice stands, is an account of the saints of the Punjab. It closes with a chronogram for the year 1292 AH by the author's son, Mufti Ghulam Safdar, whose line names the book's subject, the friends of God in the Punjab and the account of them it makes plain:

جو خدا کے دوست تھے پنجاب میں
حال ان کا اس سے ہوتا ہے عیان

(Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 282, folio 270)

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The year and day of his death.** The shrine's account gives a birth in 1587 and holds to a death in 1671, commemorated on the 4th of Rabi-ul-Sani, while noting that 1649 also appears. Lahori gives the fourteenth of Rabi al-Thani, 1050 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 185). Chishti gives Thursday, the fourth of Rabi al-Thani, with the year illegible (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 696).
- **His descent.** The shrine's account makes him a Hussaini Syed descended from Jalaluddin Surkh-Posh Bukhari of Uch Sharif, and this archive records his name as Syed Shah Jamal Uddin Naqvi Bukhari. Lahori calls him a Sayyid Husaini and gives no Bukhari descent; the only line he traces runs back to Baha al-Din Zakariya of Multan (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 185).
- **Where his descendants are.** The shrine's account has the family come from Kashmir to Sialkot and from Sialkot to Lahore, and its present *sajjada nashin* is a descendant of the saint. Lahori says his descendants live to this day in Sialkot (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 185), and Chishti places something of theirs, in a damaged passage, at Sialkot (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 690); Chishti's *sajjada nashin* was Shaikh Amir Bakhsh, son of Shaikh Roshan Ali (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 665).
- **His pir and his chain.** The shrine's account makes him a special disciple of Sheikh Khokar Baig Suhrawardi, and Alam Faqri traces his Suhrawardi lineage to Bahauddin Zakariya of Multan. Lahori names his pir as Shaikh Gada Beg and runs the chain through Shah Sharaf, Shah Ma'ruf and Ja'far al-Din to Baha al-Din Zakariya Multani (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 185). Chishti, in one chain, sets a Hazrat Makhdum Kala Beg, an uncertain reading, between Shah Jamal and Shah Sharaf (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 610), and in another a Hazrat Makhdum whose name is illegible, then Shah Sharaf, Shah Ma'ruf and Mu'in al-Din, the last uncertain in print, running to Shihab al-Din Suhrawardi and on to ʿAli (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 695).
- **His order.** The shrine's account makes him jointly Qadiri, Suhrawardi and Chishti, and calls the shrine of the Suhrawardi line. Lahori places him in his Suhrawardi chapter (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 18, folio 6) and Kanhaiya Lal calls him Suhrawardi (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 169, folio 172). Chishti heads his chapter with both Qadiri and Suhrawardi (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 690), and the enclosure inscription he copied calls him Hazrat Shaikh Jamal Sahib Qadiri (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 695). Kugle counts him among the great Qadiri saints of Lahore (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 326, folio 305, ch. 4 n. 7).
- **The order of his disciple Hassu Teli.** Kanhaiya Lal names the master Shah Jamal Suhrawardi in the sentence that makes Hassu Teli his murid (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 169, folio 172); Kugle calls Hassu Tell "a Qadiri Sufi of Lahore" and a disciple of Shah Jamal (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 207, folio 186).
- **How many storeys sank.** The shrine's account has the storeys sink one after another until only two remained. Lahori counts four sunken storeys (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 185); Chishti counts five (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 691), and describes the building he saw as three-storeyed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 693–694).
- **What the saint's anger buried.** The shrine's account tells of the storeys of his own tower sinking into the earth; the version Khalid records at the shrine has the saint bury "an entire city under his foot in wrath" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 31, folio 13).
- **What stood beside the tomb.** Kanhaiya Lal places the tomb near Ichhra with a *madrasa* in one passage (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 169, folio 172) and with a word printed as something like *madmah*, perhaps *damdama*, an uncertain reading, in the other (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 269, folio 272); Lahori calls the site Shah Jamal's *damdama* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 185).
- **His places of retreat.** The shrine's account names *chillas* at Sheikhupura, Shah Rehman, Shahdara Lahore and Chanpatpur in India. Chishti lists Sheikhupura, a place read as something like Bar Bhi Shah followed by an illegible word, and a place printed as Shah Madra, both uncertain readings (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 693).
- **The day of the *ʿurs*.** The shrine's account holds the *ʿurs* on the 3rd to the 5th of Rabi-ul-Sani; Chishti gives the fourth of Rabi al-Thani (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 692).

## Legacy

Shah Jamal's *Damdama* has been a living shrine across every period the sources reach. Lahori knew it in 1875 by the name it still carries; Chishti found it kept by a *sajjada nashin*, swept by its own family of sweepers, receiving offerings every Thursday and attended by Hindus and Muslims alike at its *ʿurs*; Kugle, writing in 2007, calls the tomb "still a popular pilgrimage place" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 207, folio 186). Its legends, of a tower sunk by a dancing saint and an imperial princess rebuked, and its Thursday nights of drumming and *dhamal* have made it one of the most written-about expressions of popular Sufi devotional culture in Punjab, and the locality that grew up around it bears his name.
