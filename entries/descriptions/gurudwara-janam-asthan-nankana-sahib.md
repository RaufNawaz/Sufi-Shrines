## Overview

Gurudwara Janam Asthan, the *janam asthan* or "birthplace" shrine, stands at the very spot in the town of Nankana Sahib, in Pakistan's Punjab province some eighty kilometres south-west of Lahore, where Guru Nanak (1469–1539), the founder of the Sikh faith and the first of its ten Gurus, is traditionally held to have been born. For Sikhs the world over it is among the holiest of all *gurdwaras*, a place of origin comparable in devotional weight to Bethlehem for Christians or Mecca's Kaaba precinct for Muslims. The town itself takes its name from the Guru, and the great shrine at its heart, crowned with white domes and set beside a sacred pool, is at once a monument to a birth, a working house of worship, and one of the principal destinations of Sikh pilgrimage in the land of the faith's beginnings.

The books consulted call the shrine Gurdwara Janamasthan. For Haroon Khalid, who reported on the festivals of Nankana Sahib in his book *A White Trail*, it is "the main Gurdwara of the city where Nanak was born" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 183, folio not stated) and "the most famous place here", where the one main road of the city "begins or ends" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 186, folio not stated); "Gurdwara Janamasthan is situated where he was born" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated). In his travelogue *Walking with Nanak*, where he also spells it Janamsthan, the six gurdwaras of the town associated with Guru Nanak are headed by this one: "The grandest of them … a splendid structure spread over a vast area constructed over the place that was once the house of Mehta Kalu" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 90, folio 72; p. 91, folio 73). In a third book Khalid recalls researching "at the Gurdwara Janamasthan, Nankana Sahib" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).

The older histories name the town but not the shrine. Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi writes that "Guru Nanak was born in 1469 in the village of Talwandi later known as Nankana Sahib, about forty miles south-west of Lahore, now part of Pakistan" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 407, folio 383); his history names no gurdwara, building or shrine at Nankana Sahib. Harish Dhillon's retelling of the Janamsakhis speaks of "Nanakana Sahib (earlier called Talwandi)" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 175) and names no gurdwara there. Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, composed under Aurangzeb, records that Baba Nanak was born at Talwandi Rai Bhoi, the last part of whose printed name cannot be read with certainty, and that he lived there in his grandfather's house (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).

## Guru Nanak and the Birthplace

Guru Nanak was born, by tradition, in the year 1469 — a date commonly rendered as 15 April 1469 in the Common Era, though Sikhs observe his *Prakash Utsav* (birth celebration, also called *Gurpurab*) on the full-moon day of the month of Kartik, in October or November. His father, Mehta Kalu (Kalyan Das), was a village accountant; his mother was Mata Tripta. The settlement, then known as Rai Bhoi di Talwandi, lay within the estates of a local Muslim landholding family. Sikh tradition warmly remembers the chieftain Rai Bular Bhatti as among the first, alongside the Guru's elder sister Bebe Nanaki, to perceive in the child an extraordinary spiritual destiny. In time the town came to be called Nankana Sahib in the Guru's honour.

From this place Nanak would go out upon the long journeys known as his *udasis*, carrying a message that cut across the divisions of caste and creed — "there is no Hindu, there is no Musalman" — and teaching the worship of one formless God through honest living, remembrance of the divine Name, and service to others. That the founder of so far-reaching a tradition first drew breath here lends the site its abiding sanctity.

The books give the birth in several forms. Rizvi gives the year 1469, and his index the dates "b. 1469, d. 1539" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 407, folio 383; p. 466, folio 442); "Most of his life occurred during the reign of the Lodis" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 407, folio 383). Dhillon has him born at Talwandi "On 14 April 1469" to Kalyan Chand Das Bedi, "or Mehta Kalu as he was affectionately called", accountant to the Bhatti family under Rai Bular (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 17–18, folios 19–20), and in his telling the village astrologer, Pandit Hardyal, foretold the child's greatness (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 18, folio 20). The back matter of *Walking with Nanak* gives "Born Talwandi (now known as Nankana Sahib in Pakistan) on October 20, 1469" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 314, folio 288). Bhandari, introducing it as something that is said, dates the birth to Samvat 1526, corresponding to 880 Hijri, in the reign of Sultan Bahlul Lodi (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).

The place of birth was not always agreed. Khalid writes that the Janamsakhi writer Meherban "claims that Guru Nanak was born in Dera Chahal and not Rai Bhoi di Talwindi", while other Janamsakhi writers and Sikh scholars "are unanimous that Nanak was born in Rai Bhoi di Talwindi" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 43, folio 25). "The name of Rai Bhoi di Talwindi has been changed to Nankana Sahib in honour of the first Sikh Guru who was born there" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 31, folio 13).

Two stories of the birth point away from the Sikh shrine. Citing Harish Dhillon's *The First Sikh Spiritual Master*, Khalid writes that "Mata Triptaji, the mother of Guru Nanak, did not have a child for a long time after her marriage and she would regularly visit an ancient Hindu temple situated on top of a mound not very far from Rai Bhoi di Talwindi to pray for a child" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 30–31, folio 13). A booklet of the Auqaf department at the shrine of Naulakha Hazari states that "Guru Nanak was born after Baba Naulakha Hazari prayed for his mother" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 31, folio 13). Iqbal Qaiser, Khalid's guide, told him: "The Sikhs don't accept it but the Muslim tradition is that Guru Nanak was born by the blessing of Baba Naulakh Hazari. It is believed that Nanak's parents came to this shrine and the saint blessed them after which Nanak was born." (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).

Of the household, Rizvi writes: "The parents of Guru Nanak came from the Khattri community which was closely connected with the Afghan rulers in the fields of commerce and administration. Guru Nanak's father, Kalu, was a village accountant and as was customary with members of that profession he supplemented his income through agriculture" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 408, folio 384). Rizvi names neither the mother nor the wife. Khalid writes that "Nanak belonged to the Bedi caste" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 100, folio 82). Dhillon traces the Bedi family's service back a generation: Shiv Ram, head of a Bedi family, was accountant to Rai Bhoe, and was succeeded by his son Kalyan Chand Das Bedi (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 17, folio 19).

## The Childhood at Talwandi

Rizvi sketches the boy's schooling and youth. "When seven years old, Nanak was taken by his father to receive instruction in Hinduism", and two years later attempts were made to teach him Persian; "It would, however, seem that he learnt both Sanskrit and Persian. In those days Sa'di's Karima and the Gulistan, as well as general sufi verses were taught to both Hindu and Muslim youths" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 408, folio 384). "When about sixteen, Nanak was married by his parents and under pressure from his family he later became a trader and farmer. Nevertheless most of Nanak's time was spent with yogis who lived in the surrounding jungles" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 408, folio 384).

Dhillon's retelling gives the episodes of the Janamsakhis. At school the boy showed "a brilliance that had never before been seen in any child in Talwandi" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 18, folio 20). At thirteen he refused the sacred thread, the *janeu*, in his father's courtyard, saying that "it is our actions and our actions alone" that give a man his position; Dhillon calls it "the beginning of a lifelong crusade against the performing of empty rites and rituals" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 19–21, folios 21–23). At sixteen he herded cattle (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 23, folio 27), and he "became well acquainted with the dervish Sayyad Hussain and gained an intimate knowledge of the Sufi line of thought" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 24, folio 28). Talwandi "lay in the path of numerous mendicants, sadhus and holy men", who camped "in a grove of trees on the outskirts of the village" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 24, folio 28). The story of the *Sacha Sauda* begins with goods to be sold in Talwandi and ends in the forest and in Talwandi (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 25, folio 29; p. 38, folio 42). In Khalid's version of that tale, "According to the legend, when Baba Guru Nanak turned eighteen, his father asked him to take up a trade. He gave him twenty rupees", and Nanak left the city, "present day Nankana Sahib", with Bhai Mardana (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 182, folio not stated).

Khalid retells the same years as a novel, and says plainly that this part of his book is his own reconstruction: "my depiction of Nanak in this book is not an accurate version of Nanak" but what he wants Nanak "to be like" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 229–230, folio not stated / folio 204). In it Mehta Kalu keeps "the accounts of the ruler of Talwindi, Rai Bular Bhatti", his friend (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 22, folio 4), and is a Hindu of the 'warrior caste' (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 29, folio 11); Tripta presents herself to sadhus and fakirs in "the forest around Rai Bhoi di Talwindi" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 21, folio 3). The boy Nanak herds buffaloes, and when he hides in a grove of *waan* trees outside the town he is found by his father and "his employer and friend, Rai Bular" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 63, folio 45; p. 64, folio 46). At dusk he sits under a *waan* tree hung with cloths "tied as supplication", "about two furlongs" from home, hearing the *azaan* and the temple bells together (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 59, folio 41; p. 60, folio 42). He refuses the *janeu* proposed by his father and "the local priest of Rai Bhoi di Talwindi", which "caused a furore", and "Respectable Hindus started to avoid the Bedi" family (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 61–62, folios 43–44). His mentor is "Sayyed Hassan, a Muslim Sufi, who lived near his house", who argues that "Islam teaches one to adopt the middle path" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 62, folio 44). He meets ascetics and Sufis "on the outskirts of Talwindi" and spends time "at the abandoned mound on the outskirts of Talwindi" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 66, folio 48; p. 67, folio 49). His sister Bebe Nanaki and her husband Jai Ram visit Talwandi from Sultanpur (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 67, folio 49), and Mardana is his best friend from the town (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 106, folio 88).

The mound is still there. Iqbal Qaiser told Khalid that it "is an archaeological mound. The settlement there was destroyed after which Rai Bhoi di Talwindi was constructed within a boundary wall"; asked "So Nanak was born in the new Nankana?", he answered "Yes" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 91, folio 73). A young Nanak "used to spend a lot of time in conversation with yogis, sadhus and dervish who would pass through Talwindi" there, and "It is believed that in the years between the incident of Sacha Sauda and his migration to Sultanpur, Nanak started to frequent this place" — "That period of six to seven years" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 91, folio 73; p. 92, folio 74).

## Departures and Returns

From Talwandi Nanak went to Sultanpur, "around 60 km from Talwandi", as keeper of Nawab Daulat Khan's stores (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 44, folio 49); for that journey, Khalid writes, "he travelled from Nankana" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 54, folio 36). Dhillon dates his marriage: "He got married on 24 September 1487" to Sulakhini, "daughter of Mul Chand and Chando Rani of the town of Batala", and gives his sons as "Sri Chand (8 September 1494) and Lakhmi Das (12 February 1497)" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 44, folio 49). Khalid's reconstruction has the marriage to "Sulakhni", "the daughter of Moolchand Khatri from the village of Pakhoke near Gurdaspur" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 67, folio 49), and the second son named Lachman Das (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 68, folio 50). At Sultanpur, in Dhillon's telling, he vanished in the stream for a week and came out saying, "There is only one God. There is no Hindu, no Mussalman." (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 45–46, folios 50–51).

The first *udasi* in Dhillon's scheme passed "From Sultanpur… to Talwandi" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 56, folio 62). Nanak and Mardana came back to Talwandi in 1506 (the year is damaged in the printed text); "Mata Tripta felt great joy", and Nanak spent time daily with Rai Bhoe, who "looked upon" him "as his Guru" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 123, folio 137); Mehta Kalu was "a little in awe of his son" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 124, folio 138). After Pak Pattan, on the second journey, "Nanak travelled back to Talwandi"; the village "was now no longer an obscure little village but had become a place of pilgrimage", and Nanak sat by Rai Bular, who was sick and dying (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 172, folio 188). Later Kartarpur, "180 km from Nanakana Sahib (earlier called Talwandi) via Lahore", became a pilgrim town like Talwandi and Sultanpur (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 175; p. 176, folio 193).

Khalid's reconstruction gives the leave-takings. Nanak and Mardana leave the familiar streets of Talwandi while friends and families gather "at the edge of the town" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 239, folio 213); at an earlier parting Sulakhni and her two children, and Mehta Kalu and Mata Tripta, see them off (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 108, folio 90); "Years after they had left" Talwandi, both decide "that it was time to go back home" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 163, folio 145); Mehta Kalu is disappointed but silent when his son leaves again (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 107, folio 89). His route "back from Chuhrkhana to Nankana Sahib, which at that point in time was still known as Rai Bhoi di Talwindi", crossed "a vast jungle that existed then between Nankana and Chuhrkhana" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 86, folio 68). At the end, settled at Kartarpur, Nanak sends to Talwandi for his family (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 296, folio 270). His saying that there was no Hindu or Muslim was questioned by friends and family "at Rai Bhoi Di Talwindi", as it was at Sultanpur and Eminabad (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 144, folio 126).

## The Shrine and its Architecture

A shrine is believed to have marked the birthplace from an early period, associated by tradition with the Guru's descendants. The present complex owes its grandeur chiefly to the patronage of Maharaja Ranjit Singh, ruler of the Sikh Empire, who is recorded to have had the *gurdwara* rebuilt and richly endowed around 1819–20. Built in the characteristic idiom of Sikh sacred architecture, the shrine presents gleaming white façades, cusped arches, fluted domes and gilded finials. Within lies the inner sanctum enshrining the birthplace itself, where the *Guru Granth Sahib*, the scripture that Sikhs revere as their eternal living Guru, is installed and ceremonially read. A large *sarovar*, or sacred tank, adjoins the prayer halls, and pilgrims gather for *langar*, the free community kitchen that is a hallmark of every gurdwara. The complex is today listed as a protected heritage monument by the Government of Punjab, Pakistan.

None of the books consulted dates the building or names its builder. Writing of the province rather than of this site, Khalid records that "When Maharaja Ranjit Singh became the sovereign of Punjab at the end of the eighteenth century he spent large amounts of money on the renovation and expansion of many gurdwaras", and "attached vast tracts of land to these gurdwaras, so that the revenues from the lands could facilitate their functioning" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 34, folio 16); in *A White Trail* he says that Ranjit Singh renovated "many Gurdwaras all over Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 208, folio not stated). Rizvi, for his part, holds that "Guru Nanak, preoccupied with discovering and disseminating Truth, would not have cared about a lasting material memorial to himself and certainly nothing of this kind was left by him in his own country" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 413, folio 389). It is his own judgement, made against Sikh scholars who sought inscriptions to prove the Guru's journey to Baghdad, and it is all he says of memorials to the Guru.

The heart of the shrine is small. "The actual shrine is a small room in the middle of a spacious marble-floored courtyard. At the centre of this room is Thara Sahib, where the Guru Granth Sahib is recited. According to tradition, Guru Nanak was born here" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 90, folio 72). In *A White Trail* Khalid writes, "This room, where the Granth is being recited, is the room where Nanak was born." (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 189, folio not stated), and of an old employee, "Rajbir is standing inside the room where Guru Nanak was born" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 213, folio not stated). "Next to this shrine is a well, a board next to which says that it belonged to the household of Guru Nanak" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 90, folio 72); in *A White Trail* the well has an iron railing, and its board reads "This well was part of Guru Nanak's house." (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 189, folio not stated). A plate in *Walking with Nanak* shows Gurdwara Janamasthan as the place where Guru Nanak was born; its caption names no town (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 167, plate, folio not stated).

## The Gurdwara as Khalid Saw It

Khalid's books describe the complex as he and his companions found it. The approach in *Walking with Nanak* is through "the small market of Nankana": "Most of the shops were run by Muslims but there were a few Sikh-owned shops as well" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 91, folio 73). In *A White Trail* the railway station "is opposite the Gurdwara Janamasthan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 227, folio not stated), and Gurdwara Tambu Sahib is "a few hundred metres away" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 223, folio not stated). "Near the Gurdwara Janamasthan, next to the office of the Nazim, the former elected head of the district, is the Government College rechristened recently as Guru Nanak Government College" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 172, folio not stated). A "tall minaret right in front of Gurdwara Janamasthan", "the tallest in the city", with spiral stairs, rises from an unpainted grey mosque (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 187, folio not stated). Kartar, one of his informants, said of it: "It is a show of strength. They want to tell us who the real inhabitants of Nankana Sahib are." (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 187, folio not stated).

On the night of Lohri in January 2011 the main gate was closed and only a small door open; "two police officials sitting inside" let the non-Sikh visitors pass on Kartar's word (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 187, folio not stated). Inside, on the right, are buildings "where visitors stay when they come for the larger events", and a small garden with two walkways and street lights (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 187, folio not stated). Shoes are handed to a volunteer child in "a bar cage" and matched by numbered coins — "It reads '22'" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 187–188, folio not stated). There is a water niche for washing the feet, and two sinks (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 188, folio not stated).

"The Gurdwara is two steps higher from the ground. It has a gilded door, with depictions of Guru Nanak and scenes from a Langar" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 188, folio not stated). Two framed pictures hang in the passage: "Baba Fareed Ganjshakar" in a green shawl and "Baba Guru Nanak" in saffron; a railing divides the passages in and out, "useful when there are huge crowds" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 188, folio not stated). A *Nishan Sahib* stands with candles (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 189, folio not stated). In another book Khalid notes "a picture of Baba Farid accompanying the picture of Guru Nanak at the shrine of Guru Nanak at Nankana Sahib", without saying which of the town's gurdwaras he means (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).

Rooms in the courtyard are "allotted to Sikh pilgrims at the time of major festivals". A room behind the shrine belongs to the Granth: it has a bed, an air-conditioner "turned on during the summers for the Granth" and a blanket for the winter, and "No other activity can take place in this room" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 190–191, folio not stated).

Khalid's chapter on Guru Nanak's birthday describes more of the complex. There are "several blocks of hostels here, constructed during various eras", and, to the left of the main shrine, a separate building of communal washrooms, with stalls for laundry, public telephones, juices and food (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 228, folio not stated). "Only the pool at this Gurdwara is filled with water out of all the Gurdwaras of the city"; it deepens towards the centre, and police stand at its entrance (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 228, folio not stated). A web of lights hung over the façade, and a banner at the main entrance showed Guru Nanak on one side, Guru Gobind Singh on the other and the other Gurus between them (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 228, folio not stated). The gurdwara administration has its offices in the complex (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 229, folio not stated). The Langar Hall, which Khalid calls "newly created", is the last building before the boundary wall, "beyond which lies the Guru Nanak Government College"; the wall carries barbed wire, and "a large brown gate, which is a recent addition", is open only to officials (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 229, folio not stated). A locked room beside the washing area "stores raw material for Langar" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 229–230, folio not stated). The courtyard of the main shrine has two entrances, one at the front and one at the right side, facing the Langar Hall (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 230, folio not stated).

The building's image travels. On a poster at Panja Sahib of "all the functional Gurdwaras of Pakistan", Khalid saw "a large picture of Gurdwara Janamasthan in the middle, a long yellow building with a white outline" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 166, folio not stated).

## The Nankana Massacre of 1921

Nankana Sahib is remembered not only for a birth but for a bloodletting. In the early twentieth century, the movement to wrest control of the historic gurdwaras from hereditary custodians — many of them *mahants* of the Udasi order accused of corruption and misappropriation — gathered force as the Gurdwara Reform Movement. On 20 February 1921 a peaceful *jatha* (band) of Akali reformers led by Bhai Lachhman Singh Dharowali entered the Janam Asthan. The custodian, Mahant Narayan Das, had armed retainers waiting; the reformers were fired upon and cut down, and Bhai Lachhman Singh, it is recounted, was tied to a tree and burned. Scores of Sikhs were killed — traditional accounts speak of at least eighty-six martyrs, and some estimates run considerably higher. The *Saka* (massacre) at Nankana became a galvanising episode in the reform struggle, hastening the passage of the Sikh Gurdwaras Act of 1925 and the establishment of elected management of the shrines.

Khalid gives the background for the province: "As a result of sectarian divisions between the Sikh communities and the takeover of Punjab by the British, a large number of important Sikh Gurdwaras were being administered by Hindu mahants" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 189, folio not stated). His account of the day follows. About two hundred unarmed Sikhs, gathered at Gurdwara Tamboo Sahib, protested "the control of the Hindu priests (mahants) at major Gurdwaras like Janamasthan", and accused its *mahant*, "Narayan Das", of corruption; he was "alleged to have sold parts of the vast property connected with the Gurdwara" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 189–190, folio not stated). Narayan Das was guarded by "Muslim Pathans from the tribal areas" and joined by local Hindu supporters (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 190, folio not stated). Led by "Bhai Daleep Singh and Bhai Lachman Singh", the Sikhs entered and were fired on, "killing about a hundred people". Lachman Singh "was hung upside down on this tree and burnt"; Daleep Singh "was thrown inside a brick kiln, which once used to exist within the premises of the Gurdwaras". The British government acted against Narayan Das and his accomplices, some of whom were "sentenced to death" and others sent for life to "Kala Pani (Andaman Islands)", and the event "formed the basis of a larger movement" to take the major gurdwaras from the *mahants* (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 190, folio not stated).

The tree stands "next to the portico", with a plaque: "Sikh Martyrdom" – "20th February 1921 at Gurdwara Janamasthan, Nankana Sahib." (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 189, folio not stated). A second portico, with a glass room, "marks the spot where the martyrs from the deadly day of 20th February 1921 were cremated in one big pile"; its "golden palanquin was gifted to the Gurdwara by the former Chief Minister of Indian Punjab, Captain Amarinder Singh" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 191, folio not stated). Two paintings hang in the glass room: Baba Nanak with "Bhai Mardana and Bhai Bala", Mardana playing the *rubab* and Bala holding the fly-whisk, and the boy Nanak shaded by a cobra (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 191, folio not stated).

## The People of the Gurdwara

Khalid names those who kept the shrine. "Lalit Singh, a young man in his late thirties, the head Granthi at the Gurdwara"; "Lalit's family has been living here since the late seventies"; he "is considered to be the most learned (religious) person in this Sikh circle" and "leads the community prayers most of the time" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 177, folio not stated). "At 70, Rajbir is one of the oldest employees of the Gurdwara Janamasthan. He moved here in 1978, following a passionate speech by Dr. Jagjit Singh Chauhan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 213, folio not stated), who had invited Pakistani Sikhs to move to Nankana Sahib to take care of the gurdwaras; "Within two months, my cousin and I moved to Nankana", Rajbir recalled, and "There were hardly any Sikhs here at that time" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 213, folio not stated). Kartar's father "moved from Tirat Valley (Tribal Area) after the 1971 Indo-Pak war and settled here, inside Gurdwara Janamasthan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 183, folio not stated).

Kartar remembered an empty town: "There was no Sikh left here after partition. Our Gurdwaras became barren and were in a bad state". "Giani Pratap" came with an Indian pilgrimage party "sometime in the sixties" and stayed "to look after the Gurdwara"; he housed Kartar's family inside it for a time and made Kartar's father "a sewaadar (worker) at the Gurdwara" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 184, folio not stated). In *Walking with Nanak* Giani Pratap, "After looking at the dilapidated condition of the shrines here he decided to stay and look after these holy buildings. That was the first time someone had taken the initiative to take care of the gurdwaras in Nankana Sahib after Partition" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 90, folio 72).

Of the town's Sikhs as a whole, Khalid writes that they "are not indigenous people from the city, but recent migrants from Khyber-Pakhtunkhwa (KPK) where there is a considerable Sikh population" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 235, folio not stated). "Most of the newcomers were incorporated within the Gurdwaras, in rooms which were generally reserved for pilgrims", and their arrival gave "a confidence boost to the Sikhs who were until now dominated by the Muslim majority" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 176, folio not stated). In *Walking with Nanak* "the Sikh community in Nankana Sahib, which amounts to about two hundred-odd households", has Jatha Singh as its leader of several years (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 87, folio 69), and "Several people whom I interviewed at Nankana Sahib felt that PSGPC was not representative of the Sikh community in Pakistan" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 36, folio 18). Some of the young men speak Urdu "with a Pasthu accent", and "not all Sikhs here can speak Urdu or Punjabi" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 174, folio not stated).

## Guru Gobind Singh's Birthday and Lohri

Khalid's chapters on the festivals of 2011 are the fullest account of the shrine's devotional life in these books. On the night of Guru Gobind Singh's birthday in January, people walked to the gurdwara through near-freezing fog, and "The guard at the entrance is too cold to check for any suspicious people trying to enter" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 177, folio not stated). Lalit Singh played *tabla* on the stage "next to the shrine", with two women singing, one at the harmonium; teenage girls sang a song to Guru Gobind Singh; the evening "is dedicated to the children", who prostrated before the Granth Sahib and recited the lines they had learnt (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 177, folio not stated). The children wore white and "the 5 Ks for this special day, even though most of them have not been baptized as yet", their turbans orange, blue or black by group (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 178, folio not stated). Surjeet ran the children's show: recitation "from the holy book, the Guru Granth Sahib", songs to "the bravery of Guru Gobind Singh", and a fourteen-year-old girl's poem lamenting that the young are "cutting their hair and their turbans are being replaced with caps". Udham called out "Jo bole so nihal!", and the audience answered "Sat Sri Akal!" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 178, folio not stated). It went on until "eleven in the night" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 179, folio not stated). The festival had opened with a cricket tournament on the ground of Guru Nanak Government College, reserved for the Sikhs that day, and closed the next morning with a prize-giving and a communal prayer at a gurdwara Khalid does not name, the trophies brought "from the main shrine" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 172–173, folio not stated; pp. 179–180, folio not stated).

Lohri followed. The procession went from Tamboo Sahib to "the main Gurdwara – Gurdwara Janamasthan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 185–186, folio not stated), while Muslims in shops and on motorbikes watched "with burning curiosity" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 192, folio not stated). "About a hundred people" heard *kirtan* in a small room with a portico (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 189, folio not stated). After an hour of *kirtan*, Lalit Singh, "the head Granthi of the shrine", led the communal prayer; the Granth was carried on an elderly man's head around the shrine to its bedroom and covered with a blanket, which "marks the end of the prayer and the beginning of the Lohri festival" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 191, folio not stated). The community then gathered at "a flame in front of the main building" in the outer courtyard, "an integral part of the Lohri celebration" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 191–192, folio not stated).

Khalid sets the night in the history of the province. "Lohri like Vaisakhi or Basant, is an ancient festival of the Punjab that commemorates the change of season", kept "on the 13th of January, which is generally the last night of the month of Poh" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 192–193, folio not stated). After the creation of Pakistan "this indigenous festival of the Punjab died down" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 193, folio not stated). Kartar told him that Lohri and Guru Gobind Singh's birthday "have only begun in the last couple of years", made possible by the growth of the Sikh population through migration from the tribal areas: "Earlier, there was only a prayer at the Gurdwara." (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 193, folio not stated). "The celebration of Lohri at Nankana Sahib this year is an attempt to revive this cultural tradition of Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 193–194, folio not stated); "It is ironical though that this Punjabi tradition shunned by Punjab is now being revived by a group of Pathan men residing in Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 194, folio not stated).

## Sikh New Year

On the Sikh New Year of 14 March 2011, "Carrying a fly-whisk, Rajbir Singh stands in front of the palanquin that holds the Granth Sahib", concluding "the bhog, the communal prayer"; "Hundreds of devotees stand outside, following his lead" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 213, folio not stated). The community then moved "to the other pavilion that contains the golden palanquin", where, in "the glass chamber, a young boy is reading the last pages of the Granth. He is the final reader of the Akhand Path" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 214, folio not stated). Khalid does not name the gurdwara of this pavilion; the scene continues from the Janamasthan.

The congregation was mixed: "Pathan Sikhs, based in Nankana Sahib and clean shaven Sindhi devotees", "about three hundred people, half from each community" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 214, folio not stated). "42 year old Ajay Kumar from Sukkur, Sindh" told Khalid, "We are Sikhs but not Singhs"; his party, "a group of more than hundred people", had come "to hold prayers in memories of Baba Thara Singh" and had arranged the concluding *Akhand Path*, the *langar*, and the sweets and biscuits "as prasad" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 214, folio not stated). The festival, Khalid writes, "is not publicized as it is not only a religious event but also a political one, with no precedent in Pakistan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 215, folio not stated); Pakistani Sikhs keep the original Nanakshahi calendar, "This explains why March 14th was being celebrated as Sikh New Year here, while Indian Sikhs continue to celebrate New Year on April 13th" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 217, folio not stated).

That night six Muslim *qawwals* performed "at Gurdwara Janamasthan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 218, folio not stated). In brown *shalwar kameez* and Muslim caps, they sang first to "the Muslim Saint Baba Fareed" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 217–218, folio not stated). Kartar said: "This is the first time since partition that Muslim Qawwalis have been invited to perform at Nankana Sahib. We have called them from Faisalabad" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 218, folio not stated). Khalid judged that the performance "doesn't promise to revive" the tradition of Muslim *kirtan* singers "but is still a tribute to the years of interaction between the two communities at Sikh Gurdwaras" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 218, folio not stated).

## Guru Nanak's Birthday

The *Gurpurab* is the shrine's great day. "According to official estimates, ten to fifteen thousand people come to Nankana Sahib for this festival every year, which is celebrated during the last days of November"; in 2011, "According to media reports, only three thousand people are attending"; "This year it began on the 8th of November and will conclude today, on the 10th of November" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated). Pilgrims from India cross at Wagah, where representatives of the Evacuee Trust Property Board and the Pakistan Sikh Gurdwara Prabandhak Committee receive them and a *langar* is laid on, and they stay three days at Nankana (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 222–223, folio not stated). Three days of *Akhand Path* end in the *bhog*, followed by the *Nagar Kirtan*; richer pilgrims from Britain, America and Canada come by chartered plane and hire buses (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 223, folio not stated). Devotees walk barefoot (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 221–222, folio not stated); Mahtab Singh, seventy-two, "became a celebrity when his interview was broadcast on national television at the 2010 festival", vowing to go shoeless through his pilgrimage (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated).

"The main focus of the pilgrims is Gurdwara Janamasthan", with more police inside than at the entrance (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 227, folio not stated). "Everyone has been allotted a pass, even representatives from the media" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 228, folio not stated). A Geo television van and media cars broadcast live by satellite (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 221, folio not stated). A blue bus decorated with yellow flowers waited for the *Nagar Kirtan* round "the green belt at the centre of the complex", and traders "from other parts of the country" set up in tents (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 228, folio not stated). Meena Kumari, fifty-four, sold food beneath "Block 1, a residential hostel", stock she had bought "in bulk from Mardan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 228–229, folio not stated). T-shirt stalls sold "Never Forget 1984," "Singh is King," "Raj Karega Khalsa" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 229, folio not stated).

The *langar* served food "around the clock", "Like Gurdwara Tambu Sahib"; the floor was wet with "water, food, tea, and mud"; "Most of the volunteers are local Pathan Sikhs", and police patrolled the hall (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 229, folio not stated). Volunteers "performing sewa" washed the utensils in two large tubs (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 229–230, folio not stated). The courtyard was covered with tents and carpets where pilgrims slept: "The majority of these pilgrims are locals from Khyber Pakhthunkhwa and Sindh, however there are a few Indian families as well" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 230, folio not stated).

From a stage next to the shrine three men in white *shalwar kameez*, who "appear to be Muslims", sang *kirtan*, their heads covered with "a loose turban, a popular style in the villages of West Punjab"; a screen showed the proceedings, broadcast live through the internet "and viewed by millions of people world over. This is the first time it is being done from here." (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 230, folio not stated). For *darshan*, devotees queued behind "an orange robe", prostrated before the Guru Granth Sahib, went round clockwise, entered by a small door on the left, fanned the fly-whisk over the Book, left by a door on the right, and received "sujji ka halwa" as *prasad* (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 230, folio not stated).

The procession has been drawn in. A shopkeeper told Khalid that the round of the city "hasn't happened for two years now. There is a security threat. They will take out a small procession within the precincts of the Gurdwara, where it is safe" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 221, folio not stated). After the *Akhand Path* a decorated pickup with a *dhol* player handed out juice packets (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 231, folio not stated). Ten Sikh men in "two lines of five each" led the way, the front row in white *cholas* and saffron turbans carrying the *Nishan Sahib*, the row behind in saffron *cholas* and blue turbans with *kirpans* (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 231–232, folio not stated). The bus carried the Guru Granth Sahib in "a golden palanquin", devotees on the main gate threw red rose petals, and its halt at the starting point "concludes the Nagar Kirtan, marking an end to the religious rituals of the festival" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 232, folio not stated).

Security now shapes the day. At the 2011 festival there was "a heavy presence of police officials at all the entrances to the city. No car is permitted inside" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 220, folio not stated). Khalid's friend Nadeem, a Muslim painter returning "after seven years", found the main gate, once "made of iron bars, which permitted one to witness the activities inside", turned into "one big closed structure barring any view"; the two smaller gates beside it were shut; "The wall has been raised and protected by a barb wire on the top"; police "with guns stand at the gate, on top of walls and all the other higher structures at the complex"; and a new entrance had been opened at the side, some distance from the main gate (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 220, folio not stated). Pilgrims stood in two queues, women searched by female police in a small enclosure, men checked in the open and passing "two other security checks" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 220, folio not stated).

In *Walking with Nanak*, at "the annual celebration of Guru Nanaks birthday", a "huge banner" with pictures of all ten Sikh Gurus stood in front of the building, Nanak "wearing his saffron chola and raising his right hand in blessing" at one end and Guru Gobind Singh at the other (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 94, folio 76).

## Muslims and the Birthplace

A police official checked Nadeem's identity card: "No Muslims allowed". "But I came a few years ago", Nadeem said, and had no reply (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 220–221, folio not stated). Barefoot before the main gate, he raised his hand "in the direction of the shrine": "I will just say hello to the Baba from here then. Good bye, Baba Ji." (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 221, folio not stated). For Nadeem, "Guru Nanak is like a Muslim Sufi Saint" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 219, folio not stated), and Khalid writes that "many Muslims, acquainted with the teachings of Guru Nanak, are devoted to him too and visit his shrine" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 220, folio not stated).

Khalid himself learnt the shrine's etiquette as a visitor. He was sitting "with my back towards the main shrine when a Sikh man came up to me and told me to not sit with my back towards the main shrine" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 93, folio 75). In *In Search of Shiva* he recalls a scene from that earlier research: "A few years ago, when I was researching for my first book at the Gurdwara Janamasthan, Nankana Sahib", "a group of newly appointed government officials came and sat with the head priest" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated). One of them said: "We, Muslims, have always liked the Sikhs. They are closer to our religion ... It's the Hindus that we can never be friends with". Khalid heard it as spoken "in a patronizing manner, as if giving the priest permission to continue living at a place where he really did not belong" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated). When Khalid told him that "90 per cent, if not more, of Pakistanis today have Hindu origins", the official answered "No. That's not true", and gave his own caste as "Rajput Bhattis" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).

The regard for Nanak that Khalid met among Muslims at Gurdwara Sachkand, he writes, is not found among the Muslims of Nankana Sahib; Iqbal Qaiser explained that most of the town's Muslims migrated from the other side of the border after Partition, their attitudes "shaped by those experiences" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 76, folio 58). Some shopkeepers keep separate glasses for Muslim and non-Muslim customers (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 186, folio not stated); a restaurant owner, Aslam Chaudary, refused Sikh customers — "We do not have your utensils" — and complained, "We become prisoners within our own city", though not all traders resent the pilgrims who bring business to the town (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 226, folio not stated). At Guru Nanak Government College "there are only a few Sikh boys studying here; the majority of the students are Muslim" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 172, folio not stated).

## Guru Nanak in the Wider Record

What the books say of Guru Nanak's later life and teaching is said of the Guru, not of this site; it is gathered here because the shrine exists for his sake. Rizvi traces his itinerary through Sultanpur, Panipat, Delhi, eastern India and Assam, Saiyidpur, Ceylon, Kashmir, Mecca, Baghdad, Ajodhan, Multan, Gorakhtari and Kartarpur (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 409–418, folios 385–394). Dhillon sets out two schemes of the *udasis*: "According to the Puratan Janamsakhi… he undertook five journeys", the last in 1523–1524; "More modern accounts" give four, spanning twenty-three years (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 54–55, folios 60–61; p. 55, folio 61); Dhillon himself holds to four (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 56, folio 62). Khalid gives the travels as "about twenty-four years", mostly on foot, east to Bengal, south to Sri Lanka, north to Kashmir, Nepal and Tibet, and west to Mecca (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 54, folio 36). "Whenever he would visit an area he would visit its religious shrines, Muslim, Hindu and of any other denomination" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 37, folio 19).

Bhandari, writing in Persian under Aurangzeb, calls Baba Nanak a caravan-leader on the paths of truth and torch-bearer of the mystic way (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). After travelling most of the world he came to the town of Batala, married, and settled in a village of Batala on the Ravi; twelve kos from Batala on the bank of the Ravi, Bhandari writes, is the place of Baba Nanak, where his descendants still live (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). People came from every land to become his disciples (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). He died, Bhandari writes, in the reign of Salim Shah Afghan, between the ages of seventy and eighty (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). Dhillon gives the date of his death as 22 September 1539 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 259, folio 287).

His succession passed over his sons. Bhandari writes that Nanak had a worthy son named Lakhmidas, but because spiritual fortune was not his lot he gave a Khatri named Lehna the title of Guru Angad and made him his successor at the hour of death (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). "On his deathbed, he appointed not his son but his most loyal student as his spiritual successor", Khalid writes (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 16, folio not stated), and the Udasi sect "is believed to have been founded by the eldest son of Guru Nanak, Sri Chand" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 36, folio 18). Michel Boivin, writing of Sindh, names Sri Chand as "the eldest son of Guru Nanak who was not recognized as guru by the mainstream Sikh community", whose tradition the Udasis follow (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 53, folio 40). Rizvi writes that "posterity recognized him as the personification of the light of God. It came to be believed that the mingling of 'the light' of the Guru with that of Guru Angad was in accordance with God's Riza" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 419, folio 395).

His followers, in Bhandari's account, count the service of travellers, of those who come and go, as the highest worship; whoever arrives at midnight speaking Baba Nanak's name is treated as a brother and a friend and served, even a stranger, a thief or a highwayman (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 519). "It is believed that the tradition of Langar began with Guru Nanak", Khalid writes (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 188, folio not stated). Dhillon's Nanak was "no unworldly mystic" but "a householder in every sense of the word" who "brought a message of peace and harmony to believers of the mutually hostile religions of Islam and Hinduism" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 12, folio not stated). "It is said" that, asked by his Hindu and Muslim companions which religion to adopt, he told them to be true Muslims or honest Hindus: "That would be Nanak's religion" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 219, folio not stated).

Rizvi, a historian of Sufism, weighs Nanak's debt to the Sufis with care. "Our analysis of Guru Nanak's teachings in the light of sufi beliefs should not be construed as an assertion that the great Guru borrowed his ideas from sufis. It would indicate that Guru Nanak, through his own meditation, arrived at the same conclusions as had already been reached by sufis such as Rumi, Sa'di, 'Iraqi, Jami and Hafiz" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 420, folio 396). Against W.H. McLeod he argues that "all literate Panjabis, both Hindus and Muslims, at the time of Guru Nanak were conversant with" 'Attar, Rumi and Sa'di, "Certainly the Guru belonged to a literate family", and it is "unbelievable that he made 'informal contacts with ordinary Muslims' alone and not with the great sufi saints of his own days" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 420, folio 396). "Guru Nanak undoubtedly criticized sufis at several places as suggested by McLeod but he intended to denounce only impostors and charlatans who made a living out of mysticism" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 420, folio 396). Rizvi also writes that "Guru Nanak exhibited more equanimity and resignation in the divine will than Shaikh ʿAbduʾl-Quddus" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 368, folio 346), and records his visit to Shaikh Ibrahim at Ajodhan (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 351, folio 329).

Others make the same link through Baba Farid. "Guru Nanak was a spiritual follower of Baba Fareed. He traveled to Pak Pattan where he met the spiritual descendants of Baba Fareed and collected the poetry of the saint" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 188, folio not stated). Shemeem Burney Abbas writes that Baba Farid's "texts are revered among the Sikhs because he influenced the founder of their own religion, Baba Guru Nanak" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 87, folio not stated). Robert Rozehnal reports that a twentieth-century Chishti Sabiri shaykh, in certain lectures, describes "non-Muslim spiritual luminaries—Krishna, Rama, Sita, Lachman, and Guru Nanak—as believers in the one true God and religious leaders with 'Divine connection' (nisbat)" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 63, folio 53). Omar Kasmani, citing Boivin, notes that the trope of the flower in milk is told also of "Guru Nanak, the spiritual leader and founder of Sikhism" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 195, folio 178, ch. 5 n. 17).

His devotion reaches beyond the Sikhs. Khalid writes of Nanak Panthis, "Hindus who believe in the teachings of Guru Nanak, but not in the Khalsa", in whose temples in Sindh and southern Punjab "one will find portraits of Guru Nanak, along with Krishna, Kali Mata and other Hindu deities" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 197, folio not stated). Boivin notes "a growing similarity between the iconography of Guru Nanak and Jhulelal" in Sindh (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 54, folio 41). Jürgen Schaflechner records "the engraved footsteps of Guru Nanak" in the valley of the goddess Hinglaj, and writes that "The hollowed-out footsteps of Guru Nanak connect Hinglaj to the Sikh and Nanak paṃth (Hin. sect) traditions" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 256, folio not stated; p. 257, folio not stated). "Many Lohanas in Sindh follow the teachings of Nanak" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 80, folio not stated).

## Legends and Miracles

The legends below are told of the Guru, not of this shrine. The best known is the Kaʿba that turned. "According to legend, Nanak once travelled to Mecca where he performed Hajj"; he slept with his feet towards the Kaaba, was rebuked, turned his feet, "and as he did so, the Kaaba miraculously turned towards his feet"; Khalid comments, "What is important in this story is Nanak's criticism of the concept of a 'sacred space'" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 82, folio 64). Rizvi reads the same story through Sufi belief: "The legend that the Ka'ba or the mihrab of the principal mosque, moved in the direction of the Guru's feet need not be interpreted literally. The story is based on the sufi belief that great saints did not need to circumambulate the Ka'ba, for whichever way they looked it would appear before them" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 412, folio 388). In Dhillon's telling Nanak's feet were said to point towards "a place where God does not reside", and the *qazi* conceded that "God lives everywhere" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 198–201, folios 218–221).

The Janamsakhis' meetings with long-dead saints, Rizvi writes, express "the sufi and Nath belief that great saints did not die but remained accessible to important mystics in later ages through spiritual conversation" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 413, folio 389); "At Panipat Nanak also had discussions with the spirit of Shaikh Sharafu'd-Din Abu 'Ali Qalandar" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 409, folio 385). The debates with Naths and Siddhas on "the legendary Mount Sumeru or Maru" need not, he says, be accepted literally (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 411–412, folios 387–388). Taken prisoner in Babur's invasion, the Guru was freed because the conqueror was "so impressed with the Guru's hymns" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 410, folio 386); the Janamsakhis record a meeting between Nanak and Babur at Saidpur, where Babur asked a blessing to conquer India and Nanak rebuked him (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 114, folio 96).

Some writers set the miracles against the man. Dhillon writes of "Guru Nanak's clear and emphatic disavowal of the miraculous" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 11, folio 11), and in his retelling the Guru tells Karori, who calls his cure a miracle, that "there is no such thing as a miracle" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 190, folio 209). Khalid writes that Nanak "abhorred the concept of associating miracles with religious personalities", yet "his biography is nothing but a story of his miracles" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 16, folio not stated). Of the legends in which Nanak humbles a Muslim Sufi — Hamza Ghous at Sialkot, Mian Mitha at Pasrur — Khalid writes that they are "pointless stories" and that "His genius lies in the poetic use of words and not in miracles"; Iqbal Qaiser's comment is, "That is to show that our saint is more powerful than yours" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 197–198, folios 171–172; p. 199, folio 173).

At the shrine itself one image recalls a childhood marvel: a painting in the glass room shows the boy Nanak shaded by a cobra (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 191, folio not stated).

## In Verse

The shrine's worship is sung. Nanak's *Bani*, Dhillon writes, "was compiled as part of the Adi Granth by Guru Arjan Dev, the fifth Guru, in 1604" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 9, folio 9), and "the Guru Granth Sahib" carries also, in Khalid's words, the poetry of "other spiritual teachers like Bhagat Kabir, Baba Farid and Bhai Mardana" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 82, folio 64). Bhandari says that the Guru composed Hindi verses on the truth of God, setting out divine unity in clear words and shining metaphors (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520); that a singer named Mardana sang his verses and drew people into the snare of devotion (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520); and that the worship of his followers is the reading of their master's verses, which they sing to melody (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 519).

Rizvi quotes, in translation, the Guru's own hymn on the failure of miracle-workers against Babur's army:

*Millions of priests tried by their miraculous power to restrain the Emperor when they heard of his approach. He burned houses, mansions, and palaces; he cut princes to pieces, and had them rolled in the dust. No Mughal hath become blind; no priest hath wrought a miracle* (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 410, folio 386)

Khalid, who "truly discovered Guru Nanak" through Iqbal Qaiser's recitation of the *Babur Bani*, in which Nanak "lays the blame of Babur's wrath at God's feet" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 15–16, folio not stated), quotes the Guru's verse throughout *Walking with Nanak* in published English translations. None of the lines he quotes is tied to Nankana Sahib; they are given here as the poetry of the Guru the shrine remembers. The *aarti*, which Iqbal Qaiser reads as praising a God who resides "in nature", begins in one translation:

*The sky the salver, the sun and moon the lamps …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 55, folio 37)

and in another:

*In the salver of the firmament, / The sun and the moon shine as lamps* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 243–244, folios 217–218)

On the oneness of God:

*My Master is One / He is One, brother, the only One* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 60, folio 42)

*God is One, / The Path is One* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 144, folio 126)

The poem on the sacred thread, which in Khalid's reconstruction the boy refused at Talwandi:

*From the cotton of compassion, / Spin the thread of contentment* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 61–62, folios 43–44)

On washing:

*As hands or feet besmirched with slime, / Water washes white* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 192, folio 166)

The song Nanak sings, in Khalid's reconstruction, to the innkeepers who meant to rob him:

*The herons in their white feathers dwell in the sacred shrines of pilgrimage* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 123–126, folios 105–108)

Other openings Khalid quotes: *O my merchant friend* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 56–58, folio 40); *Launch ye the boat of austere contemplation …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 148–149, folios 130–131); *As a beggar goes a-begging …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 158, folio 140); and *His goodness cannot be priced or traded …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 280, folio 254). From the *Babur Bani* he quotes the line 'Coming in seventy-eight (1521 C.E)' (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 113–114, folios 95–96).

The Guru's language and forms are part of his legacy. "Nanak incorporated several words which were not part of the Punjabi of the time … which is why Nanak's contribution as a poet to Punjabi is immense" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 278–279, folios 252–253). Abbas writes of the *dohā*, a rhyming couplet in an old Prakrit and Hindi metre: "Poets such as Kabir and Nanak used it to write their mystical poetry" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 141, folio not stated); her glossary defines the *banī* by him: "Devotional verses such as those of Guru Nanak; verses of supplication, entreaty, humility, and reverence" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 179, folio not stated). Annemarie Schimmel, writing of the province, observes that in the Punjab the literary heritage of Panjabi was rarely continued by Muslims after 1800, and that the language "was used almost exclusively by the Sikhs as their literary medium, whereas the Panjabi Muslims resorted to Urdu" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 427, folio 396).

The Guru's verse is sung outside the gurdwaras too. Abbas quotes Abida Parvin: "I sing Shah Hussain, Bulle Shah, Mian Muhammad Bakhsh, Hazrat Khawaja Ghulam Farid, Sacchal Sarmast, Baba Farid, and Guru Nanak" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 54, folio not stated). In Sindh, Boivin records, "Sain Vasan Shah became famous for reciting the banis of Shri Guru Nanak and the kalams of the Sufis" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 68, folio 55), and he reports Ajwani's judgement that "Guru Nanak was the greatest poet of the Bhakti" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 90, folio 77). At Nankana Sahib itself, on the Sikh New Year of 2011, the *qawwals* invited to the Janamasthan sang first to Baba Farid (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 217–218, folio not stated), whose writings the Granth Sahib also contains (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 168, folio not stated).

## Setting: Nankana Sahib

Talwandi, in Dhillon's retelling, was "the village of Talwandi, which lay between the rivers Ravi and Chenab, directly in the path of the armies that had set their eyes on Delhi"; "Thirteen times was the village of Talwandi destroyed and thirteen times - like the fabled phoenix - it rose again from its ashes", and its last rebuilding was overseen by Rai Bhoe, a Bhatti Rajput who had become a Muslim (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 15–16, folios 17–18). Rai Bhoe's son was Rai Bular (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 17, folio 19). Writing of the province in Nanak's century, Rizvi says that "In the fifteenth century, the Panjab enjoyed a peaceful period due to Afghan rule", the Afghans resettling the land and "founding many new towns and promoting trade and commerce" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 408, folio 384).

"Originally called Rai-Bhoi-Di-Talwandi, this city was rechristened Nankana Sahib in honour of Guru Nanak"; because Nanak spent his early years here there are "several Gurdwaras in the city", all of which the pilgrims visit (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated). Khalid's *A White Trail* counts "seven Gurdwaras in Nankana Sahib, most of which are associated with incidents from the life of Baba Guru Nanak" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 192, folio not stated); *Walking with Nanak* says "There are a total of six gurdwaras associated with Guru Nanak at Nankana Sahib" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 90, folio 72). "All year round most of them remain locked, only to be opened at the time of the festival. There are security guards employed at the entrance to all of them." (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 227, folio not stated). It is "a city considered to be one of the holiest cities in the world for the Sikhs, as the founder of their religion, Nanak, was born here" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 183, folio not stated).

The mound of the old settlement "now rests" behind Gurdwara Janamsthan, "away from the hub of the new settlement" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 91, folio 73): a steep climb "with no proper path", "at least twenty metres from the ground", covered with shrubs of *karir*, with "shards of broken pottery and other mud utensils" underfoot (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 92, folio 74).

The modern town, as Khalid saw it, has "one main road that runs through the entire city"; "The entire city needs a major uplift. The buildings are old and the road has pot holes"; "Most of the buildings are from the late British or the early Pakistan era" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 186, folio not stated). From a roof he saw the minarets of mosques, the "small white domes of the Gurdwaras", and the "remains of a few Hindu temples", "cone like structures rising from houses abandoned at the time of partition" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 225, folio not stated). "During the Musharraf era (2001-2008), newly constructed roads and other government facilities made their way into the city. In 2005, Nankana Sahib was separated from Sheikhupura and made into an independent district, with this city becoming the district headquarters"; Sikhs abroad have asked for an international hotel and visa-free "holy city" status, so far without result (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 226, folio not stated). "Guru Nanak is celebrated all over Nankana Sahib. There is a Guru Nanak High School, a Guru Nanak grocery store and a Guru Nanak cloth shop, all run by Sikhs" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 172, folio not stated); Madan Singh, a former head of the PSGPC and a businessman of the town, runs "Guru Nanak High School, the only school in the country where the Granth Sahib is taught to Sikh children" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 203, folio not stated). Many Sikh families practise *hikmat*, traditional medicine (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 202, folio not stated), and "There has been a growing migration of Sikhs boys and men from Nankana Sahib to bigger cities like Lahore, Faisalabad, and Gujranawala" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 174, folio not stated).

The town's gurdwaras have known politics. "It was the November of 1985, Guru Nanak's birthday, a year after Operation Blue Star": "Thousands of Sikh pilgrims had come to Nankana from all over the world", and after the *Akhand Path* and *bhog* an American Sikh, Sardar Ganga Singh Dhillion, spoke for a separate Sikh state (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 215, folio not stated). The PSGPC "had not been formed as yet"; the SGPC, based in Amritsar, managed the Pakistani gurdwaras through the Indian High Commission in Islamabad, whose staff received pilgrims at the border and arranged their stay inside the gurdwaras (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 215–216, folio not stated). During the speech "the gate of the Gurdwara was closed" and "Counsellor B. Jain and First Secretary K. K. Khanna" were attacked, barely escaping alive; Madan Singh, "only 19 at that time", was among the attackers: "I was arrested along with twenty-five other people and held at Sheikhupura Jail for ten days" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 216, folio not stated). Khalid does not name the gurdwara. "The religious festivals at Nankana and other cities of Pakistan have gone back to being religious festivals" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 216, folio not stated).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The date of birth.** The site's account gives 1469, "commonly rendered as 15 April 1469", with the *Gurpurab* kept on the full moon of Kartik. Dhillon gives "On 14 April 1469" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 17–18, folios 19–20); the back matter of *Walking with Nanak* gives "October 20, 1469" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 314, folio 288); Rizvi gives the year 1469 only (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 407, folio 383); Bhandari, as something that is said, gives Samvat 1526, corresponding to 880 Hijri, in the reign of Bahlul Lodi (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).
- **The place of birth.** The site's account and every book place it at Talwandi, now Nankana Sahib; Khalid reports that the Janamsakhi writer Meherban "claims that Guru Nanak was born in Dera Chahal and not Rai Bhoi di Talwindi" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 43, folio 25).
- **The distance from Lahore.** The site's account puts Nankana Sahib "some eighty kilometres south-west of Lahore"; Rizvi puts it "about forty miles south-west of Lahore" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 407, folio 383).
- **The father's name.** The site's account names Mehta Kalu (Kalyan Das); Dhillon names Kalyan Chand Das Bedi, "or Mehta Kalu" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 17–18, folios 19–20); Rizvi names him only Kalu (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 408, folio 384).
- **The birth and a Muslim saint's prayer.** Khalid, citing Dhillon's *The First Sikh Spiritual Master*, has Mata Tripta pray for a child at an ancient Hindu temple on a mound near Talwandi (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 30–31, folio 13); an Auqaf booklet says he was born after Baba Naulakha Hazari prayed for his mother (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 31, folio 13); Iqbal Qaiser calls this "the Muslim tradition", which "The Sikhs don't accept" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).
- **The Guru's wife and sons.** Dhillon has Sulakhini, daughter of Mul Chand and Chando Rani of Batala, married on 24 September 1487, and the sons Sri Chand and Lakhmi Das (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 44, folio 49). Khalid's reconstruction has Sulakhni, daughter of Moolchand Khatri of Pakhoke near Gurdaspur, and the sons Sri Chand and Lachman Das (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 67, folio 49; p. 68, folio 50). Rizvi has him married "about sixteen", the wife unnamed (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 408, folio 384). Bhandari names the son passed over as Lakhmidas (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).
- **The date of death.** The site's account and Rizvi give 1539 (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 466, folio 442); Dhillon gives 22 September 1539 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 259, folio 287); Bhandari gives the reign of Salim Shah Afghan, at an age between seventy and eighty (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).
- **Who led the jatha of 1921.** The site's account names Bhai Lachhman Singh Dharowali; Khalid names "Bhai Daleep Singh and Bhai Lachman Singh" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 190, folio not stated).
- **How Lachhman Singh died.** The site's account says he was tied to a tree and burned; Khalid says he "was hung upside down on this tree and burnt" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 190, folio not stated).
- **How many died in 1921.** The site's account speaks of at least eighty-six martyrs, with some estimates higher; Khalid writes of "killing about a hundred people", from a gathering of about two hundred (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 189–190, folio not stated).
- **Who the mahants were.** The site's account calls them *mahants* of the Udasi order; Khalid calls them "Hindu priests (mahants)" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 189–190, folio not stated).
- **The guards of the mahant.** The site's account speaks of armed retainers; Khalid names "Muslim Pathans from the tribal areas" and local Hindu supporters (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 190, folio not stated).
- **How many gurdwaras Nankana has.** *A White Trail* counts seven (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 192, folio not stated); *Walking with Nanak* counts six associated with Guru Nanak (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 90, folio 72).
- **The size of the Sikh community.** *A White Trail* gives "about thirty to forty Sikh houses" ten years earlier and a sevenfold increase (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 174–175, folio not stated), "about two hundred houses" after about ten years (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 175, folio not stated), and, from Kartar, "only about fifty Sikh houses" a few years before against "almost two hundred to two hundred and fifty Sikh homes" now (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 193, folio not stated); *Walking with Nanak* gives "about two hundred-odd households" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 87, folio 69).
- **When the Gurpurab falls, and how many come.** The site's account sets it on the full moon of Kartik, in October or November, and speaks of "large numbers of Sikhs"; Khalid writes that it is "celebrated during the last days of November", that official estimates are ten to fifteen thousand a year, and that media reports counted three thousand in 2011, when it ran from 8 to 10 November (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated).
- **Who cared for the gurdwaras after Partition.** The site's account says the shrine "passed into the care of the state", today through the Evacuee Trust Property Board and the Pakistan Sikh Gurdwara Prabandhak Committee. Kartar says that "There was no Sikh left here after partition" and that Giani Pratap came in the sixties to look after the gurdwara (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 184, folio not stated); *Walking with Nanak* calls this "the first time someone had taken the initiative to take care of the gurdwaras in Nankana Sahib after Partition" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 90, folio 72); and in 1985, Khalid writes, the PSGPC had not yet been formed and the Amritsar SGPC managed the Pakistani gurdwaras through the Indian High Commission (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 215–216, folio not stated).
- **Nadeem's earlier visit.** Khalid's text has Nadeem returning "after seven years" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 220, folio not stated) and, a page later, saying "But I came a few years ago" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 220–221, folio not stated).
- **Who may enter.** Khalid writes that "many Muslims" devoted to Guru Nanak "visit his shrine" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 220, folio not stated); at the birthday festival Nadeem was turned away with "No Muslims allowed" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 220–221, folio not stated), while at Lohri the same year Khalid and his non-Sikh companions were let in on Kartar's word (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 187, folio not stated).
- **The spelling of the names.** The site's account writes Janam Asthan, Rai Bhoi di Talwandi and Lachhman Singh; Khalid writes Janamasthan and Janamsthan (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 183, folio not stated; Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 91, folio 73), Rai Bhoi di Talwindi (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 43, folio 25) and Rai-Bhoi-Di-Talwandi (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated), and Lachman Singh (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 190, folio not stated); Dhillon writes Rai Bhoe and Nanakana Sahib (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 15–16, folios 17–18; p. 175).

## Legacy

After the Partition of 1947, when the newly drawn border left Nankana Sahib within Pakistan and the great mass of Sikhs on the Indian side, the shrine passed into the care of the state. Today it is administered through the Evacuee Trust Property Board and the Pakistan Sikh Gurdwara Prabandhak Committee, which oversee its upkeep and the reception of pilgrims. Each year, and above all at the *Gurpurab* marking the Guru's birth, large numbers of Sikhs travel from India and across the diaspora to Nankana Sahib, where the town swells with congregations and the sound of *kirtan*. As the cradle of the faith and the site of one of its defining modern martyrdoms, Gurudwara Janam Asthan holds a singular place in the Sikh imagination — a home to which the community continually returns.

Khalid counts "Guru Nanak's birthday at Nankana Sahib" among the few religious events kept alive by foreign pilgrims after Partition (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 176, folio not stated). Sikhs from abroad come for festivals "like Baisakhi and the birthday of Guru Nanak, which were attended by thousands of Indian pilgrims" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 214, folio not stated), and pilgrim tourists "come to Nankana Sahib and Hassan Abdal" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 79, folio 61). Writing of Pakistan as a whole, Khalid says that "Guru Nanak was born in a city now called Nankana Sahib, Pakistan. He passed away in a small village called Kartarpur also in present day Pakistan", and that "Gurdwaras that were built there later by the devotees still stand tall" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 168–169, folio not stated). During the festival "The entire city looks like a mini-India during these few days" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 226, folio not stated), and when it is over, "Things in Nankana Sahib will soon calm down" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 232, folio not stated).

The birthplace is remembered far from it. At Manora in Karachi, Jeeta Singh holds a small festival "every November when the festival of Guru Nanak is celebrated at Nankana Sahib", and insists that Nanak never came to Manora: "He was from Punjab, Nankana Sahib" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 228, folio 202). Khalid, who found in Nanak a poet "my own, being a Punjabi" yet "an alien, since he was a non-Muslim" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 15, folio not stated), asks whether "Nanak's legacy in its true essence is also slowly disappearing" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 230, folio 204).
