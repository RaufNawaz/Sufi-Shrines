## Overview

The Ramapir Temple at Tando Allahyar, in the Sindh, is a Hindu shrine dedicated to *Ramdev Pir* — also called Ramapir or Baba Ramdev — a fifteenth-century saint of Rajasthan venerated across western India and Sindh as a worker of miracles and a champion of the poor and the low-caste. The temple is the focus of the annual *Ramapir Mela*, which is widely reckoned the second largest Hindu pilgrimage in Pakistan after the *yatra* to Hinglaj, and it is celebrated above all for the way its devotion reaches across the boundaries of community, drawing Muslims as well as Hindus into its festival.

Of the books consulted, only one names the temple, and only in a single sentence. Jürgen Schaflechner, writing of the Hindus of Pakistan, notes that "Many—especially in the rural areas—follow the teachings of local Sufi pīrs (Urd. spiritual guide) or adhere to the teachings of the fourteenth-century saint Ramdevji, whose main temple is located in the Sindhi city of Thando Allah Yar." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 13, folio not stated). He says nothing more of the temple or the saint. Schaflechner spells the town Thando Allah Yar and the saint Ramdevji; this article keeps Tando Allahyar and Ramdev Pir except in quotation. Michel Boivin's *The Hindu Sufis of South Asia* knows the saint as Rama Pir, Ramdeo and Ramdeo Pir and describes his cult in Sindh, but it never names Tando Allahyar and never names any temple of his; what it says is about the saint and his devotees, not about this temple. The other books consulted speak only of Sindh at large, and appear below as the setting of the cult.

## Ramdev Pir and the Legend of the Temple

Ramdev Pir is remembered in tradition as a ruler-saint of medieval Rajasthan, held by his devotees to have been an incarnation of Krishna, who used his powers to defend the humble and to dissolve the barriers of caste; his principal shrine stands at Ramdevra (Runicha) in the Jaisalmer region of Rajasthan, where he is said to have taken *samadhi* in the year 1459. His cult spread widely among both Hindus and the Muslim communities of the desert borderlands, so that he is honoured as a *pir* — a Sufi title — as readily as a Hindu saint.

According to the legend attached to the Tando Allahyar temple, a devotee carried an earthen lamp from the original shrine at Ramdevra, some four hundred and sixty kilometres to the north-east, across what is now the India–Pakistan frontier, and enshrined its flame here, founding the temple in his master's honour. The building itself dates from the British colonial period, traditionally placed in the mid-nineteenth century, around 1859.

## Rama Pir in the Books

Boivin places the saint among the folk heroes of the poor. Writing of the untouchable castes of Sindh, he says: "The untouchables venerated Devi as well as a specific category of divinities that Christopher Fuller described as 'divinized heroes' (Fuller 1992: 49–50). In Sindh, the most popular deified heroes were Rama Pir (Ramdeo or Ramdev), Pithoro Pir, Pabuji and Guga Pir." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 43, folio 30). He gives the three spellings in that order and gives the saint no dates, no place of burial and no temple.

He returns to the saint in discussing Pithoro Pir, and there gives the fullest account the books hold of Ramdev Pir's standing in Sindh: "Pithoro Pir is most of the time associated with soteriological figures. In this category, the saint most venerated is Ramdeo Pir, still known in Sindh as Rama Pir. Ramdeo Pir is also the object of widespread worship in Gujarat and Rajasthan. Although he is very present for the untouchable ones, he is also venerated by the Nathpanthis of Gujarat. In Sindh, he is today integrated into the temples dedicated to the pan-Indian divinities." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 192, folio 179).

The two cults, in Boivin's account, overlap without being equal: "The worship is not exclusive: the majority of Meghwars who venerate Pithoro Pir also venerate Ramdeo Pir. On the other hand, the reverse is less sure. The pir of Pithoro does not mention Ramdeo Pir among the companions with whom Pithoro Pir travelled." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 192, folio 179). What joins them, he suggests, is an ideal of holiness that can be seen in their images: "A most interesting point is the convergence of certain elements within these methods of worship, starting with the iconography. The figures of the two saints seem to draw with them a common model of holiness based on the renouncement." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 192, folio 179).

Boivin gives one example of the saint's place in a temple of Sindh, and it is in Karachi, not Tando Allahyar: "In Karachi, for example, a painting of Ramdeo Pir stands in the temple of Lakhshmi Narayan." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 192, folio 179). Schaflechner, too, attests devotion to the saint in Karachi, mentioning "the local Ram Pir temple" in the city's Narayanpur neighbourhood (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 163, folio not stated); that temple is a different place from the temple at Tando Allahyar.

Boivin's bibliography includes the study that heads this article's own list of sources, Dominique-Sila Khan's *Conversions and Shifting Identities: Ramdev Pir and the Ismailis in Rajasthan*, which it gives as published at Delhi by Manohar in 1997 (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 235, folio 222).

## The Temple

Beyond Schaflechner's sentence calling it the saint's "main temple" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 13, folio not stated), the books consulted say nothing of the temple at Tando Allahyar: they do not describe its building, name its founder or its custodians, or mention the Ramapir Mela. Schaflechner gives the saint no death year, no lineage and no festival (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 13, folio not stated). What is known of the temple's founding, its fair and its rites comes from the site's own account, given here as it stands.

## The Ramapir Mela

The temple's fame rests chiefly upon its great fair. Held on the first day of the month of *Bhadva* (falling in August or September), the Ramapir Mela is a pilgrimage of three days that draws thousands of the faithful from across Sindh and beyond. Its rites unfold in a rhythm of dawn flag-hoistings, barefoot processions, the singing of *bhajans*, offerings of coconuts and lengths of cloth, and the communal *langar*, or free kitchen, that feeds all comers without distinction.

What most strikes observers of the mela is its shared character. Although Hindu ritual predominates — the *aarti*, the offerings, the devotional song — the festival's appeal has never been confined to a single community. Muslim devotees, too, come to the shrine, offering *chadars* — the embroidered sheets laid upon the tombs of Sufi saints — and joining the processions, in a fusion of Hindu temple worship and the idiom of the Sufi *dargah* that is characteristic of the folk religion of Sindh. In a country where the Hindu minority is small and often anxious, the Ramapir Mela stands out as a rare and vivid expression of a devotional culture that both faiths can share.

## Devotional Life

Beyond the days of the mela, the temple serves the settled Hindu population of Tando Allahyar and the surrounding district, a community with deep roots in the agrarian society of lower Sindh. The cult of Ramdev Pir, with its emphasis on the dignity of the poor and the irrelevance of caste before the divine, has long held a special appeal for communities at the margins, and the temple remains a place where that message is renewed in song and ritual.

The books describe that constituency in Sindh at large. Boivin, as quoted above, finds the saint "very present for the untouchable ones" and venerated by the majority of the Meghwars (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 192, folio 179). Schaflechner names the teachings of Ramdevji beside those of the local Sufi *pirs* as what many follow, 'especially in the rural areas' (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 13, folio not stated).

## Setting

The books consulted describe the Hindu population of Sindh among whom the saint's cult lives; what follows is about the province, not the temple. Schaflechner calls Sindh "home to the majority of Pakistan's Hindus", whom he numbers at "between 6 and 10 million people—an estimate that varies widely according to the source", and he writes that "over 90 percent—belong to the underprivileged Hindu tribes or sub-castes, such as the Meghvar, the Bhil, and the Kohli, who frequently work as manual laborers for the powerful landowning elite in Sindh" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 13, folio not stated). Boivin agrees in substance: "In Pakistan, despite the mass migration, the Sindhi Hindus number several millions, with a majority of them belonging to the so-called scheduled castes, mostly Menghwars, Bhils and Kohlis" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 175, folio 162). "Since partition, the majority of the Pakistani Hindus of Sindh have belonged to the low castes. They are Dalits, as they are named by some, and some even claim they are not Hindu", he writes, and adds that "the Sufi legacy is still alive among the Dalits of Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 194, folio 181); among the Hindus of the province, he notes, "there is a debate among the Hindus to determine if the term 'Dalit' is relevant" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 187, folio 174). Haroon Khalid places most of the country's remaining non-Muslims among "Hindus living in the 'faraway' 'peripheral' regions of Southern Punjab, inner Sindh and Baluchistan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 12, folio not stated).

Several of these communities look east, across the present border, toward the Rajasthan of Ramdev Pir's own legend. Boivin writes that "Contrary to popular belief, there were many untouchables in Sindh", among them the "Bhils or Kohlis" and "the Menghwar, mostly settled around Umerkot", and that "The first census reports that many would have migrated from Gujarat or Rajasthan between 1843 and 1872" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 40, folio 27). Shemeem Burney Abbas, writing of the region's music, calls the Kolhis "the indigenous peasant communities of Sind who are sometimes migratory", adding that "together with the Bhils they are the native inhabitants of the Sind-Rajasthan continuum" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 46, folio not stated). "There is evidence of roving minstrels in the Sind-Rajasthan area," she writes (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 77, folio not stated), and her glossary describes the Manganhar as "Hereditary professional musicians in the Sind and Rajasthan" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 184, folio not stated).

The books also describe the common ground between Hindu and Muslim devotion in Sindh on which the site's account of the mela draws. Boivin recalls that "As Richard Burton noted as early as 1851, it is indeed the worship of the intercessors that constitutes the common religious idiom of Hindus and Muslims" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 51, folio 38), and that "In Sindh, we already know that a number of sacred figures have a dual religious identity, Muslim and Hindu. The Muslim identity is always related to Sufism, while the Hindu side usually belongs to the Nathpanth." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 187, folio 174). He writes that "the Hindus of Sindh are coming to the Sufi mausoleums since the nineteenth century" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 183, folio 170), and asks what that means: "The large majority of the Hindus used to pay a visit to the Sufi shrines, but does it mean they are Sufi? Of course not. It does mean that, for them, the Sufis are a part of their religious legacy" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 192–193, folios 179–180). He explains the province's openness by its position: "It is a fact that Sindh, because of its geographical location both as an isolated and a buffer zone, has hosted many unorthodox versions of normative religions, especially Hinduism and Islam." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 29, folio 16). Annemarie Schimmel finds Hindu participation in the Muslim mystical orders "more visible in Sind than in the Punjab", and notes that "Hindu writers wrote mystical poems in which they used the imagery of the Muslims" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 418, folio 387). Schaflechner writes that "The south of Pakistan, and especially Sindh, offers a whole range of salvific spaces that are frequently visited by different religious communities and form a network of pilgrimage routes or ritual journeys." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 107, folio not stated).

Both authors also qualify that picture. Schaflechner writes of "the common notion among Sindhis today that in the history of Sindh, there is no religious tension between the two communities—Muslims and Hindus. This notion of the harmonious coexistence of the religions, however, is merely a nationalist utopian ideal, as Aggarwal (2013) has shown" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 90–91, folio not stated). Boivin suggests that "the Hindu population of Pakistan, including Sindh, is more and more under the threat of the radical Muslims of the country" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 127, folio 114), and Khalid records that after the demolition of the Babri Mosque on 6 December 1992 "Hindu communities in Sindh and Quetta were attacked" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 22, folio not stated). Against this Schaflechner sets government funding for minority places of worship, which "is seen in a wave of restorations of temples across Sindh within the past decade"; "While in Sindh, I visited many temples that were renovated within the past decade, coinciding with changes in the political landscape" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 16, folio not stated). He does not say whether the temple at Tando Allahyar was among them.

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **When Ramdev Pir lived.** The site's account calls him a fifteenth-century saint who is said to have taken *samadhi* at Ramdevra in 1459. Schaflechner calls him "the fourteenth-century saint Ramdevji" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 13, folio not stated). Boivin gives him no dates (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 43, folio 30; p. 192, folio 179).
- **Where his main temple is.** The site's account places his principal shrine at Ramdevra (Runicha) in the Jaisalmer region of Rajasthan, from which the flame of the Tando Allahyar temple is said to have been carried. Schaflechner writes that his "main temple is located in the Sindhi city of Thando Allah Yar" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 13, folio not stated).
- **How he is worshipped in Sindh.** The site's account describes a temple dedicated to him, with its own fair. Boivin writes that "In Sindh, he is today integrated into the temples dedicated to the pan-Indian divinities", and gives as his example a painting of the saint in the temple of Lakhshmi Narayan at Karachi (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 192, folio 179).
- **What kind of holy figure he is.** The site's account makes him a ruler-saint, held by his devotees to be an incarnation of Krishna, and honoured as a *pir* as readily as a Hindu saint. Boivin counts "Rama Pir (Ramdeo or Ramdev)" among the "deified heroes" of Sindh, a category of divinities venerated by the untouchables (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 43, folio 30), and elsewhere calls him a saint among the "soteriological figures", whose model of holiness is "based on the renouncement" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 192, folio 179).
- **His name, and the town's.** The site's account calls him Ramdev Pir, Ramapir or Baba Ramdev, and the town Tando Allahyar. Boivin prints Rama Pir, Ramdeo, Ramdev and Ramdeo Pir (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 43, folio 30; p. 192, folio 179). Schaflechner prints Ramdevji, and spells the town Thando Allah Yar (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 13, folio not stated).
- **How harmonious Sindh's shared devotion has been.** The site's account calls the temple "a living emblem of Sindh's syncretic religious temperament" and speaks of "the older, more porous boundaries of devotion in the Indus lands". Writing of the province, not of this temple, Schaflechner calls the notion that Sindh's history knew no religious tension between Muslims and Hindus "merely a nationalist utopian ideal" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 90–91, folio not stated).

## Legacy

The Ramapir Temple at Tando Allahyar is at once a major centre of Hindu pilgrimage in Pakistan and a living emblem of Sindh's syncretic religious temperament. In the flame said to have been carried from Ramdevra, in the pilgrims who walk barefoot to its doors, and in the Muslim chadars laid alongside Hindu offerings, it preserves a vision of shared sanctity that has become increasingly precious. Second only to Hinglaj among the Hindu pilgrimages of the country, it testifies to the endurance of the faith and to the older, more porous boundaries of devotion in the Indus lands.
