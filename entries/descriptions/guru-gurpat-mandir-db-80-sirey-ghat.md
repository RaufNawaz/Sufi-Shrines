## Overview

On a plot known as DB-80 in the Sirey Ghat quarter of Hyderabad, Sindh, stands the Guru Gurpat Mandir — also called the Darbar of Baba Gurpat Sahib — a Nanakpanthi shrine, registered with Pakistan's Evacuee Trust Property Board as a functional mandir, built around a Sant revered as a twelfth-generation descendant, in the Jagiasi lineage, of Guru Nanak. Unlike many of the small, poorly documented sant-shrines of upper and central Sindh, Baba Gurpat Sahib's life and the founding of his darbar are recorded with unusual specificity, principally through community memory preserved by his present-day trust and reported in recent press coverage.

None of the books consulted for this article mentions the mandir, Baba Gurpat Sahib or the Sirey Ghat quarter. Jürgen Schaflechner's study of the goddess Hinglaj, which reports an attack on "a temple in Hyderabad" in March 2014, does not name the temple it means (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 17, folio not stated). What the books do carry is context, and it is set out below as context: what they say of the path of Guru Nanak in Sindh, of the Sindhi *darbar*, of the city of Hyderabad, and of the province around it. None of it is said of this darbar.

## The Life of Baba Gurpat Sahib

According to the darbar's own tradition, Gurpat Sahib was born into the Jagiasi lineage of Nanakpanthi Sants — spiritual descendants of Guru Nanak through Baba Sri Chand's Udasi line — in Punjab, and travelled to Sindh in the early nineteenth century. He is said to have settled first at Khairpur during the reign of the Talpur ruler Mir Murad Ali Khan, carrying letters of introduction and support from the Sikh court of Maharaja Ranjit Singh, before moving on to Hyderabad around 1830.

Among the relics he is said to have carried and enshrined were hair said to be from Baba Sri Chand, a Nanak Shahi–era rupee, and a ring attributed to Guru Gobind Singh — objects still spoken of at the darbar today. In the course of his ministry he is credited with founding several Jagiasu *tikanas* (seats) beyond Hyderabad, including at Khairpur, Halani, and Kandyaro, before his death in 1877 at the age of about seventy.

None of the books consulted records Baba Gurpat Sahib, his lineage, his relics or his seats. Michel Boivin's study of the Hindu Sufis of Sindh notes, of the province rather than of the Sant, that after 1857 "many Kayasht migrated to work in the Princely States, including the Khairpur emirate in Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 26, folio 13), and that under "the Talpur rule (1783–1843)" the Amils, the Hindu scribal class, "almost monopolized the function of kardar" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 44, folio 31).

## The Darbar and Its Trust

The present building at DB-80, Sirey Ghat is dated by community record to Vikram Samvat 1988 — 1931 in the Gregorian calendar — standing on a plot of some 595 square yards, and is maintained today as a registered charitable trust (*tikkano*) whose historic trusteeship is remembered under Bao Santokdas.

In addition to its worship hall, the darbar today also runs a modest vegetarian eatery and space for pilgrims to stay, with donors' names recorded on inscribed tiles — a continuation of the *langar*-adjacent hospitality long associated with Nanakpanthi and Sikh sant-shrines across Sindh.

## Community and Standing

The mandir forms part of a wider constellation of small Jagiasi and Nanakpanthi shrines scattered across the Dadu–Hyderabad belt of Sindh — the same milieu that produced the darbars of Sant Baba Bhagat Ram, Bhai Sant Thawan Das, and Gurdas Ram, all likewise registered with the Evacuee Trust Property Board. Its comparatively rich documentation — a named founder, a dated building, and a preserved lineage — makes it one of the better-attested sites in this cluster, even as the deeper historical record beyond community memory remains thin.

## The Path of Nanak in Sindh

The books say a good deal about the followers of Guru Nanak in Sindh, though nothing of this darbar. What follows is said of the province and its people.

**A dominant path.** "In nineteenth-century Sindh," Boivin writes, "the Nanakpanth, or 'the path of Nanak', was probably the dominant religious path among the Hindu Sindhis" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 90, folio 77). Among the Lohanas and related mercantile groups, he says, "two main cults were followed: the Nanakpanth and the Daryapanth", the second "based on the deification of the Indus River" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 42–43, folios 29–30). "In Sindh, many Nanakpanthis belonged to a subgroup known as the Udasis" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 53, folio 40). An endnote adds that "The Udasis of Sindh are coined as being a heterodox Sikh tradition, in one of the very few, if not the sole, academic papers devoted to the Nanakpanth in Sindh (Jatt 2016)" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 209, folio 196).

**Hindu or Sikh.** Schaflechner writes that "Many Lohanas in Sindh follow the teachings of Nanak, somewhat obscuring their religious affiliation for the colonial officers. Hence, in the 1881 census, Lohanas from Shikarpur and Hyderabad were counted as Sikhs, while ten years later, in the 1891 census, they were classified as Hindus (Lari 1994, 211f.)" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 80, folio not stated). Today, he observes, "many middle- and upper-class Hindus in Sindh are followers of the Nanak paṃth" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 257, folio not stated), and "it is common to cover one's head before walking into a tikānā (Sin.), the temple containing the Guru Granth Sahib" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 286, folio not stated). Boivin describes the outcome of the Singh Sabha process: "The Sindhi Nanakpanthis were thus not included in the Sikh community, and they identified themselves as being a Hindu religious sect" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 200, folio 187). He adds that "Due to the spread of the Nanakpanth in Sindh, the Hindu Sufis were very familiar with Sikh terminology", and that "in Sindh, the groups of Sufis and Nanakpanthis did not have a central organization ruling an encompassing body of followers" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 202, folio 189).

**Nanak Panthis today.** Haroon Khalid's *A White Trail* writes that the Nanak Panthi Sikhs "belong to the areas of South Punjab and Sindh", that their temples there hold portraits of Guru Nanak alongside Hindu deities, and that "They are found in very large numbers in the province of Sindh" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 197, folio not stated). In *Walking with Nanak* he writes that the communities who refused the Khalsa and called themselves followers of Guru Nanak "still exist in Sindh and in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa. Baptised Sikhs look down upon them"; at Manora he saw them "now merging into the larger groups of Sikhs" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 230, folio 204). He also records the tradition that "Nanak visited Uch Sharif on his way to Sindh, towards the end of his travels" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 278, folio 252).

**Nanak and the Sufi poets.** Boivin reports Jotwani's description of Shah Abdul Latif and Sachal as "the late participaters in the Bhakti movement which had a major impact in Sindh through the spread of Guru Nanak's teaching" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 91, folio 78).

## The Darbar in Sindh

The word by which this site is also known has a history of its own in the province. "In Sindh, another Persian word was mostly used, that of darbar", Boivin writes, and "In Sindh, the darbar was the sacred territory in which the Sufi culture was at work"; it was a term "used by the Sikhs, the Muslims and the Hindus" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 37–38, folios 24–25). British colonization, he argues, brought "a renewal of religiosity in relation to the spread of a composite middle class. It was marked by the emergence of the darbars, spreading a darbar culture" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 64, folio 51).

The Nanakpanthi darbars belong to that story. The religious recomposition of colonial Sindh "is attested to by the creation and the development of several important Nanakpanthi darbars throughout Sindh", and "although some darbars had been created in the late eighteenth century, there was a renaissance of the Nanakpanthi darbars in the second half of the nineteenth century" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 53–54, folios 40–41). His conclusion restates it: "Sindh was submitted to a religious recomposition in the late nineteenth century and early twentieth century, as is attested by the building of many new Nanakpanthi darbars all over Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 199, folio 186).

After Partition, Boivin writes, "The darbars in Sindh were most of the time preserved with different arrangements regarding their management", often with Indian *gaddi nashins* or followers paying keepers, and "despite the fluctuant diplomatic relations between Pakistan and India, it was always possible to travel across the border" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 68, folio 55). The darbars that the migrants founded in India, he notes, "are not centred on tombs, unlike those of Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 131, folio 118). In Sindh itself, Schaflechner records government funding seen "in a wave of restorations of temples across Sindh within the past decade" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 16, folio not stated).

## Setting: Hyderabad

The books speak of Hyderabad as a city, and say nothing of Sirey Ghat or of this darbar. What follows is said of the city.

**The Talpur capital.** Boivin calls Hyderabad "the late capital of the independent Sindh before the British conquest" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 30, folio 17). Of a Hindu holy man of the city he writes that Dalpat Sufi "founded an astano, a place of residence for a renunciant, in Hyderabad, on the Sipahimalani Lane. He is said to have attracted many followers, Muslims and Hindus, and the Talpur mirs are said to have looked after it"; after his death it "became a pilgrimage centre under the guidance of his successor, Keshavram Sipahimalani. The place was known as Guru Sehwani's place" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 118, folio 105). His own note qualifies this: "Apparently, he used to stay in a place in Hyderabad which can be called a darbar" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 209, folio 196). "The Sipahimalanis were a well-known Amil family of Hyderabad" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 119, folio 106).

**The Amils of Hyderabad.** Several of Boivin's Hindu Sufis came from the city. Hiranand Advani "was born in Hyderabad in a family of Khudabadi Amils" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 103, folio 90); Molchand Kripalani was "born in Hyderabad in 1887 in a Krishnaite family (Kripalani 1986: 3)" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 123, folio 110); Mulchand "studied up to class 6 at the Hiranand Academy in Hyderabad" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 59, folio 46); and "In 1901, aged 22, Rochaldas married and settled in Hyderabad to enrol as a student at the medical college of the city" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 56, folio 43). Boivin speaks of "the role played by the Hyderabadi Amils in the spread of the Sufi culture", while suggesting that the devotional centres of Sindh may have shifted north after the British conquest (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 60, folio 47). Brahmashankar Mishra spent years in Karachi and Hyderabad, yet "no formal relation between Sindh and the Hindu branch of the Naqshbandiyya has been found" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 26, folio 13).

**Theosophy.** Annie Besant, known in Sindh as Basanti Devi, "visited this city as well as Hyderabad, where she delivered a number of lectures", and "in Hyderabad, her speech was about Hindu religion (Sadhu Vaswani 2001: 31)" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 46–47, folios 33–34). Of one of the figures he follows, Boivin writes that "the printing house he founded in Hyderabad was named Blavatsky Printing" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 48, folio 35).

**Sufi shrines of the city.** Boivin names "Tando Jahaniyya in Hyderabad" as one of three Sufi traditions among the Hindu Sufis (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 60, folio 47). Qutub Ali Shah "was a famous Sufi from Hyderabad… His dargah is located in Jahaniyya, a neighbourhood in Hyderabad" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 95, folio 82), and its *sajjada nashin* came "from Pakistan twice, in 1957 and in 1980" to perform the *dastarbandi* ceremony for two of the figures Boivin follows (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 167, folio 154). Shemeem Burney Abbas records of one community of musicians that "Their pir (spiritual mentor) is Qutb Din Darya, whose shrine is called Tando Jahana and is in the Civil Lines at Hyderabad" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 71, folio not stated). Annemarie Schimmel uses the city as a landmark: Shah Abdul Latif was born "in Hala, near present-day Hyderabad-Sind" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 421, folio 390), and Jhok is "not far from Hyderabad" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 428, folio 397).

**A city of music and print.** For Abbas, Hyderabad is the centre of Sindhi devotional music on the air. The singer Taj Mastani left Sehwan for it "because it is a large urban center", and "affirmed that in Hyderabad she can earn a living from the radio" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 34, 63, folios not stated). "within areas such as Hyderabad or Faisalabad the local recording companies have assisted women musicians in earning a living from recording their Sufi lyrics with music" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 35, folio not stated). At Radio Pakistan Hyderabad, its director Sikander Baloch and music director Naseer Mirza told her that "they no longer have to go to melās at the Sufi shrines to hunt for talent" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 80, folio not stated). "there are two gharānās in Hyderabad—the Indore gharānā and the Gawalior gharānā" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 61, folio not stated), and the singer Zeena Bai "is buried in the Hyderabad graveyard where all the great musicians of the city are buried" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 62, folio not stated). "The Institute of Sindhology in Hyderabad, Sind, is one such source" for the documentation of Sindhi Sufi music (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 175, folio not stated).

The city is also a place of Sindhi publishing. "The Shah Waliul-lah Academy in Hyderabad, Sind, under the presidency of Maulana Ghulam Mustafa Qasimi, has published a number of Wallullah's works, as well as studies about him." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 403, folio 372). Schimmel's *As Through a Veil* lists the Sindhi Adabi Board of Hyderabad as the publisher of five Baloch volumes of 1960–1962 (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 314, folio 296), and Hyderabad as the place of Lilaram Watanmal's 1889 biography of Shah Abdul Latif (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 308, folio 290). Kasmani writes that "Starting in the 1960s, the Sindhi-language newspaper Daily Ibrat (published in Hyderabad) started to report the ʿurs at Sehwan in great detail." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 189, folio 172, ch. 1 n. 29).

**Pilgrims and violence.** Schaflechner names Hyderabad as a source of *mīlād-al-nabī* and *ʿāśūrah* visitors to Hinglaj (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 272, folio not stated) and of *ʿīd* and *muḥarram* pilgrims (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 291, folio not stated). He reports the attack on an unnamed temple in Hyderabad on 28 March 2014 (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 17, folio not stated), and quotes K. R. Malkani, "a Sindhi in exile who, as a Hindu, left Hyderabad after Partition and settled in New Delhi" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 87, folio not stated). Boivin recalls "the railway line which formerly connected Hyderabad (Sindh) to Barmer and Jodhpur" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 189, folio 176).

## Setting: Sindh

The books' accounts of the province give the wider frame in which the darbar's tradition places its founder, a Sant who came from the Punjab to the Talpur courts of Sindh. They are said of the province, not of this site.

**Under the Talpurs.** Boivin asks, "How was the society of Sindh organized in 1843?" "Given the political domination of the Talpurs, the Baluchis were at the top of local society"; and "Before 1843, Muslims were divided between being Sunnis and Shiites. The Sunnis were in the majority, but the sovereigns, the Talpur, were themselves Shiites" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 38–41, folios 25–28). "In the Hindu society of Sindh", he writes, the Lohanas "represented half of the total Hindu population" and were divided into Amils and Bhaibands, and "The panchayat system prevailed among the Hindus of Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 40–41, folios 27–28). Schaflechner quotes Burton that "a description of them [the Lohanas], therefore, will be a general portrait of the Sindh Hindoos" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 79, folio not stated). Schimmel records that the *hurr*, the disciples of the Pir Pagaro, were "gathered first to support Ahmad Brelwi's struggle against the Sikh (1829-31) and later actively joined in the struggle against the British" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 427, folio 396).

**Under the British.** "Sindhi continued to flourish after the British occupation in 1843", Schimmel writes, and "The country had always been proud of its mystical heritage. Did not Diwan Gidumal, the minister, give the invading Nadir Shah (1739) the most precious treasure Sind had to offer, some of the dust of its saints?" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 427, folio 396). Robert Rozehnal quotes Katherine Ewing that "British policy, especially in the Punjab and Sind, was to treat them in essentially the same way that landlords and tribal leaders were treated" — them being the hereditary saints (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 33, folio 23). Boivin reports Narsain's statement that "the integration of Sufism inside of the religious environment of the Hindu Sindhis dates back to the nineteenth century, and that the main actors involved were the Amils, the literary class" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 27, folio 14), and writes that "In the religiosity of Sindh, the devotional relationship of a follower with a spiritual Master was prevalent, rather than the focus being on affiliation to an institutionalized religion" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 51, folio 38).

**Partition.** "By early November of 1947, 246,000 Hindus had already left Sindh by sea, air or rail. As of 1 January 1948, it was estimated that nearly 500,000 people had sought refuge in India." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 62, folio 49). Schaflechner writes that "The departure of the Hindu elite and the influx of Muslim refugees (“Muhajirs”) from India dramatically altered the local demography" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 87, folio not stated). After Partition, Boivin writes, "Molchand was worried that it was difficult for Hindu Sindhi Sufis to pay a visit to the Sufi places in Sindh, now in Pakistan" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 124, folio 111).

**The Hindus of Sindh today.** "Although a large majority of Hindus have migrated to India from 1947 onwards, Sindh is the province of Pakistan with the most significant Hindu population." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 176–177, folios not stated and 164). Schaflechner calls Sindh "home to the majority of Pakistan's Hindus" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 13, folio not stated). Khalid writes that "The majority of the Hindu community is based in Sindh and Southern Punjab, where they have a proportionate representation from the lower and the upper castes" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 233, folio not stated). The formula *Sab ka malik ek hai*, Boivin observes, "has for some years been very popular among the Hindus of Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 186, folio 173).

**The older Sindh.** Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, composed under Aurangzeb, describes the Mughal suba of Thatta. Its greatest river, it says, is the Indus, by which merchants from Multan and Bhakkar ship goods to Thatta; it gives the suba's length as running from Bhakkar to Kach Makran (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529), and records that most traffic goes by boat (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 530).

**A song of the province.** Abbas records Alan Faqir reciting a line that he read as a statement about Sindh; her note explains that "'Sind is not the amir' means that Sind is not in the position of leadership or amirship" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 210–211, folios not stated):

*Punnu is not in the Kec / Sind is not the amir* (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 156, folio not stated)

Of his land he said in the same interview: "It has been looted—it has been plundered—it has been assaulted—but it goes on—it has not died—it will not die—it has existed for centuries and it shall exist—Sindhri is beaten—and yet Sindhri goes on" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 156–157, folios not stated); "Sindhri", her note explains, is "A name used for Sind in the poetic traditions" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 210–211, folios not stated).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **Hindu or Sikh.** The site's account calls the darbar a Nanakpanthi shrine registered as a mandir, and its Sant a spiritual descendant of Guru Nanak through Baba Sri Chand's Udasi line. Schaflechner records that Lohanas of Hyderabad who followed Nanak were counted as Sikhs in the census of 1881 and as Hindus in that of 1891 (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 80, folio not stated). Boivin writes that the Sindhi Nanakpanthis "identified themselves as being a Hindu religious sect" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 200, folio 187), and reports Jatt's description of the Udasis of Sindh as "a heterodox Sikh tradition" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 209, folio 196). Khalid calls the same communities Nanak Panthi Sikhs (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 197, folio not stated), and writes that "Baptised Sikhs look down upon them" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 230, folio 204).
- **The name of the seat.** The site's account calls the Sant's seats *tikanas* and the trust a *tikkano*, and spells the lineage both Jagiasi and Jagiasu. Schaflechner writes *tikānā*, "the temple containing the Guru Granth Sahib" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 286, folio not stated). Boivin writes *darbar*, a term "used by the Sikhs, the Muslims and the Hindus" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 37–38, folios 24–25).
- **The Sufi shrine of Tando Jahaniyya.** Boivin places the dargah of Qutub Ali Shah "in Jahaniyya, a neighbourhood in Hyderabad" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 95, folio 82). Abbas names Qutb Din Darya as the pir "whose shrine is called Tando Jahana and is in the Civil Lines at Hyderabad" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 71, folio not stated). Neither book says whether the two are one shrine.
- **How many Hindus live in Pakistan.** Schaflechner puts Pakistan's Hindus at "between 6 and 10 million people—an estimate that varies widely according to the source" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 13, folio not stated). Boivin writes that "the Sindhi Hindus number several millions, with a majority of them belonging to the so-called scheduled castes" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 175, folio 162). Khalid gives no figure, citing the census of 1998 for the Hindus as the country's largest minority (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 87, folio not stated).
- **How many Hindus left Sindh at Partition.** Boivin reckons the migrants "would have been closer to 0.8 million, if not less", while noting that "there is a kind of consensus that they numbered around 1 million" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 61, folio 48).
- **Caste in Sindh.** Boivin writes that "Frequently, the claim arises that there was no caste system in Sindh", and that "it is possible that Sindh hosted a specific social fabric", while the colonial power renewed that fabric (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 36–37, folio not stated / folio 24). Schaflechner quotes Thakur that the "[c]aste system is almost absent in Sind", and links that perception to Sindhi nationalist discourse, which Dalit organizations oppose (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 277–278, folio not stated).
- **Whether Sindh was free of communal strife.** Schaflechner calls the common notion of "the harmonious coexistence of the religions" in Sindh "merely a nationalist utopian ideal" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 90–91, folio not stated). Boivin writes that "even if it never reached the level of violence in Punjab, it is irrelevant to claim there was no communal violence in Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 62, folio 49), while arguing that "the Sufi culture still pervades Sindhi society" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 177, folio 164).

## A Note on the Sources

Much of what is recorded here rests on the darbar's own community tradition as related in recent press coverage rather than independent archival verification. The identification of the ETPB-registered "Guru Gurpat Mandir, DB-80 Sirey Ghat" with the Baba Gurpat Sahib darbar described in that coverage is highly probable — same name, same locality — but not explicitly cross-confirmed in a single authoritative source. Dates and figures should be read with this in mind.

The books cited above do not close that gap. None of them names the mandir, its Sant or its quarter; what they offer is the history of the Nanakpanth and the darbar in Sindh, and of Hyderabad and the province, against which the darbar's own account can be read.
