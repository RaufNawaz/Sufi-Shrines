## Overview

Wedged into the narrow space between the western wall of Lahore Fort and the eastern wall of Hazuri Bagh, close to the Alamgiri Gate, stands one of Lahore's most storied yet architecturally modest religious survivals: the Loh, or Lava, Temple. According to Hindu tradition, Lahore takes its name from Lava (also rendered Luv), twin son of Lord Rama and Sita in the Ramayana, who is said to have founded the city — known in Sanskrit as Lavapuri, the "City of Lava" — while his brother Kusha is traditionally credited with founding nearby Kasur.

Despite this ancient legendary association, historians including Sir Edward MacLagan date the surviving structure itself to the early nineteenth century, likely built during or shortly after Maharaja Ranjit Singh's conquest of Lahore Fort in 1799, making it a Sikh-period commemorative shrine to an ancient legend rather than an ancient building in its own right. Long neglected, the temple was restored between 2024 and 2026 and reopened to the public on 27 January 2026, although it does not currently host regular congregational worship.

Of the books consulted for this article, only one names the temple itself. Haroon Khalid, writing of Lahore's religious minorities, records: "There is a temple near the Alamgiri gate of Lahore fort, called the temple of Luv" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 71, folio not stated). The older histories of the city — Kanhaiya Lal's *Tarikh-e Lahore*, Nur Ahmad Chishti's *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori's *Hadiqat al-Awliya* and Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* — speak of the son of Rama, whom they call Lo or Lohu, only as the legendary founder of Lahore, and of the Fort only as a fort; none of them mentions a temple inside or beside it. What they say below is said of the figure and of the place, not of this building.

## Lava, Son of Rama

The temple's whole meaning rests on a founding legend. In the tradition the site's account follows, Lava, or Luv, is a twin son of Lord Rama and Sita, the founder of Lahore, which bore the Sanskrit name Lavapuri, the "City of Lava"; his twin brother Kusha is credited with founding Kasur. Khalid tells the same story of the twins: Luv and Kush are Ram's twin sons, born to Sita at Valmiki's ashram after she had been banished by Ram (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 71, folio not stated). He adds, in his own historical prose, that River Parsuni "is the old name of river Ravi, flowing next to the city of Lahore", and that "Lahore and Kasur are said to be named after Luv and Kush" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 71, folio not stated).

The brothers have a second place in Lahore's living Hindu memory. Khalid describes a portico painting at Neela Gumbad, in Lahore, that shows Valmiki "teaching his two pupils, Luv and Kush, next to river Parsuni" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 70, folio not stated).

The nineteenth-century Urdu histories of Lahore tell the legend in their own forms and names. Kanhaiya Lal's founding account names "مہاراجہ رام چندر او تار[OCR?] کے فرزند مسمی لو[OCR?]", the son of Maharaja Ram Chandar Avatar named Lo, as the founder, who called the city لوپور, Lopur (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 13, folio 8). He then cites the author of the خلاصۃ التواریخ as naming "لو اور کشو۔ دو فرزندان دلبند مہاراجہ رامچندر اوتار کے", Lo and Kasho, two beloved sons, Lo founding Lopur, that is, Lahore, and Kasho founding کشور یعنی قصور, that is, Kasur (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 14, folio 9). Over "صدہا بلکہ ہزار ہا سال", hundreds and indeed thousands of years, he writes, the name was worn down into Lahore (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 13–14, folios 8–9).

Kanhaiya Lal does not let this account stand alone. The same pages offer "لوہار پور" as a rival derivation of the name (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 13–14, folios 8–9), and he sets against the *Khulasat* the account of "شیخ احمد زنجانی صاحب رسالہ تحفۃ الواصلین": when Raja Deb Chand sat on the throne of Delhi, "اُس نے پنجاب کا علاقہ [illegible] برادر [illegible] لوہار چند کی جاگیر مین دیا", giving the region of the Punjab in *jagir* to a brother, Lohar Chand (the words around 'brother' are illegible in the printed text), who, once his hold on the Punjab was complete, made this city his capital (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 14, folio 9). The modern editor of the *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, in a footnote on another passage drawn from the same source, calls it "تحفۃ الواصلین ایک وضعی تذکرہ", a spurious *tazkira* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 191–192, folios 179–180).

Nur Ahmad Chishti's *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, written as a detailed account of the buildings, mazars, tombs and mosques of Lahore's environs (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 28), tells the legend twice, and differently. In the section on Shah Kamal, introduced with "کہتی[OCR?] ہین", 'they say', Raja Ram Chand had three sons: "ایک لو[OCR?] نے [illegible] لاہور بنایا", one, Lo, built Lahore; a second, whose name is lost in the damaged text, "قصور[OCR?] آباد کیا", founded Kasur; and the third, "مسمی راو[OCR?]", founded Mauza Rawan. The passage closes with "واللہ اعلم", 'God knows best' (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 661). In the book's dynastic history, by contrast, Raja Ramchandar had two sons, Kushu and Lohu, and "لوہو نی شہر لاہور آباد کیا", Lohu founded the city of Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 795).

Bhandari's *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, a Persian history composed in the reign of Aurangzeb, opens its description of the city in the suba of Lahore with the same attribution. "لاهور مصریست متقدمین برکنار دریاے راوی": it is an ancient city on the Ravi, and "آبادی ان را به لو خلف راجه رام چند نسبت میدهند", its founding is attributed to Lo, son of Raja Ram Chand (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525). He notes the variant forms of the name: "دربعضی تواریخ لهور و لهاور نیز مینویسند", in some histories it is also written Lahur and Lahawar (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525). Nothing is said there of a temple.

The name of Lava also travels far beyond Lahore. Jürgen Schaflechner, in his study of the goddess Hinglaj, records a caste-origin story told by his Sindhi interlocutors, and attributed also to Thakur, in which "Rama's son, called Lava, from whom the name Lohana ultimately developed", gave his name to the Lohana community (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 80, folio not stated). He attaches no temple, tomb, image or place to him.

## The Temple

The surviving temple is a modest building. It is a simple hexagonal brick structure of interconnected chambers around an inner sanctum, historically topped by a dome that has since been lost. It contains no surviving Ramayana carvings or frescoes, consistent with its comparatively late construction. Historians including Sir Edward MacLagan date it to the early nineteenth century, likely built during or shortly after Maharaja Ranjit Singh's conquest of Lahore Fort in 1799: a Sikh-period commemorative shrine to an ancient legend rather than an ancient building in its own right.

Khalid reports a different belief about its age: "It is believed that the original temple was built by Luv himself, whereas the current structure goes back to the Mughal era" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 71, folio not stated). He gives no builder and no date.

The uncertainty is old. Visiting in 2018, a *Dawn* correspondent found the then-unrestored temple littered with an empty water bottle and marked by graffiti, and noted that neither of the two historians she consulted could say who built the temple, when, what it once housed, or whether it was ever used for worship at all — questions she concluded "we can only speculate" about. The Walled City of Lahore Authority, which now manages the site, describes it in its own materials as "the temple of Raja Loh," a cluster of interconnected, open-to-sky chambers housing a memorial shrine.

The nineteenth-century histories of Lahore, which describe the Fort, are silent on the temple. Kanhaiya Lal describes the Fort, where the temple stands, at length, but names no temple inside it (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 338–339, folios 345–346); the Fort also appears in his survey of the city's gates, where no structure in or beside it is described (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 15, 19; folios 10, 14). The only *mandir* he names in the Fort is a renamed mosque: Ranjit Singh, he writes, renamed the Fort's Moti Masjid "موتی مندر", the Moti Mandir, and kept "سلطنت کا خزانہ", the treasury of the state, there, and in Kanhaiya Lal's own day the British government kept the treasury of the Punjab in the same building (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 339, folio 346). That building is not this temple. Lahori's *Hadiqat al-Awliya* records of the Fort only that it holds twenty-nine relics, kept "علیحدہ مکان میں", in a separate building, and says nothing of a temple or of any other structure there (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 259, folio 247). Chishti's *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* mentions the Fort of Lahore repeatedly without naming a temple in it (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 103, 179, 291, 292, 462, 516, 638, 653), and Bhandari, who credits Lo with the city's founding, is likewise silent on any temple to him (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525).

For much of the twentieth century the temple stood neglected and largely inaccessible within the Fort precincts. Between 2024 and 2026 it underwent careful restoration by the Walled City of Lahore Authority in partnership with the Aga Khan Cultural Service–Pakistan, funded by the United States Ambassadors Fund for Cultural Preservation, alongside related Sikh-era monuments nearby. It reopened to the public on 27 January 2026. It does not currently host regular congregational worship in the manner of Lahore's active mandirs, and the sources consulted record no festival or rite held there.

## Setting: The Fort and the Alamgiri Gate

The passages that follow are about Lahore and its Fort in general, not about the temple.

Bhandari traces the Fort back to the city's restorers. Lahore, he writes, fell into ruin over long ages, so that little trace of it was left, and "دار الحکومت این ولایت شهر سیالکوت گردید", the seat of government of the region passed to Sialkot; after Sultan Mahmud's conquest, Malik Ayaz rebuilt the city and built a *pakka* fort (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525). Akbar built "قلعه متین(۱) شهر پناه و دولتخانه", a strong fort, a city wall and a palace, the editor's footnote giving the variant "حصین[OCR?]"; Jahangir raised "عمارت عالیه که بالفعل موجود است", the lofty building still standing in Bhandari's day (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 524).

The Fort was also the scene of a small literary moment at court. On Akbar's second visit to Kashmir, in his thirty-seventh regnal year, which set out from Lahore in the rains, "در وقت عبور از دریاے راوی پایان قلعہ لاہور", at the crossing of the Ravi below the Fort of Lahore, the emperor asked about whom this verse had been written (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 179):

کلاه خسروی و تاج شاہی
بہر گل کی رسد حاشا و کلا

Bhandari quotes the couplet as the emperor's question; the book gives no translation of it (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 179).

The Fort was besieged more than once. In Bhandari's account of Shihab al-Din Ghuri's fifth campaign, "خسرو ملک تاب نیاورده درقلعه لاهور متحصن گردید", Khusraw Malik shut himself up in the Fort of Lahore (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 404). In Akbar's reign Muhammad Hakim Mirza besieged the Fort for twenty-two days while "راجه بهگونت داس صوبه دار لاهور" held it firm (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 203). Under Jahangir, the governor Dilawar Khan strengthened the Fort against the rebel prince Khusrau, and "شاهزاده هر چند سعی کرد قلعه لاهور بدست نیامد" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 146). In Chishti's account of the Sikh court, the killers of Maharaja Sher Singh met their end "بعد محاصرہ قلعہ لاہور و معرکہ عظیم", after the siege of the Fort of Lahore and a great battle (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 589). Chishti also records a building "روبروی تخت واقع قلعہ لاہور جنوب رویہ", facing the throne on the south side of the Fort, in which he himself saw a grave built, and which common people called "نگار خانہ" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 787).

The site's account ties the temple's building to Ranjit Singh's taking of Lahore in 1799. Khalid writes that in 1799 Ranjit Singh "was greeted enthusiastically by a population sick of the lawlessness of that time" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 207, folio not stated). Chishti records that when "لہنا سنگہ حاکم" began destroying the monuments of former kings, the elders of the city invited Ranjit Singh to "لاہور مین آکر قابض ہو", to come to Lahore and take possession of it (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 786).

The Alamgiri Gate, beside which the temple stands, takes its name from Aurangzeb. The domes of the Badshahi Masjid face "the Alamgiri gate of the Lahore Fort, named after the imperial title of the Mughal Emperor Aurangzeb" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 205, folio not stated). "The Roshani gate, one of the thirteen gateways that lead into the ancient walled city of Lahore, is between the Alamgiri Gate and the Royal Mosque", and it "was once reserved for the prince and the princess, who would go from the Fort to the city through this entrance" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 205, folio not stated). The gate also carries Sikh memory: Guru Arjan's torture took place "Outside the Alamgiri gate of the Lahore fort" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 195, folio not stated). The Ravi, which once flowed next to the Fort, "has changed its course, moving a few kilometres away" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 195, folio not stated). And the Fort itself, Khalid observes, "which was once the emblem of their power and wealth, is now a tourist destination. For twenty Pakistani rupees, one can visit it and even tour the private chambers of the mighty Mughal Emperor, Jahangir" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 195, folio not stated).

The temple's quiet sits within a harder history of Lahore's Hindu places. Khalid records that after "6th of December 1992", "Sitla Mandir at Shah Alami, Lahore, Jain Mandir at Anarkali, Lahore and Bhadra Kali Mandir at Thokar Niaz Baig, Lahore were a few prominent temples that were attacked" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 21–22, folio not stated), and that "Functional temples like Krishna Mandir on the Ravi road and Valmiki Mandir at Neela Gumbad, both in Lahore, were also burned down" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 22, folio not stated). The Valmiki temple at Neela Gumbad "serves a small community of Balmiki Hindus in Lahore, numbering only a few hundred, most of whom are Christians on record" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 66, folio not stated).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The founder's name.** The site's account calls him Lava, also rendered Luv; the Walled City of Lahore Authority speaks of "the temple of Raja Loh" and of Prince Loh. Khalid writes Luv (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 71, folio not stated); Schaflechner, Lava (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 80, folio not stated). Kanhaiya Lal names him "مسمی لو[OCR?]", Lo (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 13, folio 8); Bhandari, لو (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525); Chishti, Lo in one passage (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 661) and Lohu in another (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 795).
- **How many sons, and who founded Kasur.** The site's account has twin sons, Lava and Kusha, Kusha founding Kasur; Khalid also has twins, and says Lahore and Kasur are named after them (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 71, folio not stated). Kanhaiya Lal gives two sons, Lo and Kasho, Kasho founding Kasur (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 14, folio 9). Chishti gives three sons in one passage — Lo, a second whose name is lost who founded Kasur, and Rao, who founded Mauza Rawan (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 661) — and two, Kushu and Lohu, in another (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 795).
- **The city's first name.** The site's account gives the Sanskrit Lavapuri, the "City of Lava". Kanhaiya Lal gives لوپور, Lopur (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 13, folio 8), and offers "لوہار پور" as a rival derivation (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 13–14, folios 8–9); the account he takes from the *Tuhfat al-Wasilin* has the city made a capital by Lohar Chand, not founded by a son of Rama (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 14, folio 9). Bhandari reports the written forms Lahur and Lahawar (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525).
- **The age of the building.** The site's account, following historians including Sir Edward MacLagan, dates the surviving structure to the early nineteenth century, likely during or shortly after Ranjit Singh's conquest of the Fort in 1799. Khalid reports the belief that the original temple was built by Luv himself and says the current structure "goes back to the Mughal era" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 71, folio not stated). The *Dawn* correspondent of 2018 found that neither historian she consulted could say who built it or when.

## Legacy

The Loh Temple's reopening on 27 January 2026 drew renewed attention in both Pakistani and Indian media to a rare, tangible link between Lahore's Hindu mythological heritage and its physical urban fabric. The Walled City of Lahore Authority frames the restoration as part of a wider effort to showcase the Fort's cross-cultural heritage of Sikh and Hindu temples, Mughal mosques, and British-period structures alongside one another.

The legend the temple commemorates is older and wider than the building. Across three centuries of writing on Lahore, in Persian, in Urdu and in English, the city's founder appears again and again as Rama's son, under the names Lo, Lohu, Luv and Lava, even where the writers record no shrine to him at all; and in Khalid's words, "Lahore and Kasur are said to be named after Luv and Kush" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 71, folio not stated). The small hexagonal building by the Alamgiri Gate is where that story is given a place in stone.
