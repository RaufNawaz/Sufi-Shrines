## Overview

The shrine of *Hafiz Muhammad Jamal Multani* is among the most historically important Sufi mausolea of Multan, the "city of saints", and marks the resting place of the man traditionally credited with establishing the *Chishti* order firmly in the city. Born at Multan around 1747 (1160 AH) and dying in 1811, Hafiz Jamal was a disciple of *Khwaja Noor Muhammad Maharvi*, a leading Chishti master of the region, and became a renowned teacher and poet in his own right, composing in Arabic, Persian and Siraiki; his *Siharfi*, an acrostic poem of the Punjabi–Siraiki tradition, is his best-known literary work. His tomb stands near the Aam Khas Bagh outside the historic Daulat Gate, a fine piece of late-Mughal funerary architecture that still preserves original Mughal-era wall paintings. It remains an active centre of devotion, with an annual *urs*, and it forms part of the dense sacred geography of Multan alongside the great shrines of Bahauddin Zakariya, Shah Rukn-e-Alam and Shah Shams Sabzwari.

None of the books consulted here mentions the shrine itself: none describes the tomb, its paintings, its *urs* or its keepers. What they offer concerns the saint and his city. Carl W. Ernst and Bruce B. Lawrence, in their history of the Chishti order, give him one sentence: "Another successor of Nur Muhammad, Sulayman Taunsawi's contemporary Hafiz Muhammad Jamal (d. 1811), is also said to have taken part in resistance to Sikh attacks on Multan" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 121, folio 108). The phrase 'is also said to have' is theirs: they report the tradition and do not vouch for it. The modern Urdu edition of Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori's *Hadiqat al-Awliya* has an entry headed with the names "خواجہ محمد جمال / حافظ محمد جمال ملتانی" that appears to concern this saint, and its editor's footnote credits a Hafiz Muhammad Jamal Multani with the flourishing of the Chishti order in Multan (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 120, folio 108); what that footnote says is set out below, with its uncertainties.

## The Life of the Saint

By the account the site has long carried, Hafiz Muhammad Jamal Multani was born at Multan around 1747 (1160 AH) and died in 1811. He was a disciple of Khwaja Noor Muhammad Maharvi, a leading Chishti master of the region, and became a renowned teacher and a poet in his own right, writing in Arabic, Persian and Siraiki.

Ernst and Lawrence count him among Nur Muhammad's successors: he is "Another successor of Nur Muhammad" and "Sulayman Taunsawi's contemporary", and they give his death as 1811 (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 121, folio 108). The same sentence records, as something said of him rather than as established fact, that he "is also said to have taken part in resistance to Sikh attacks on Multan" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 121, folio 108). On the same page they write of Shaykh Sulayman Taunsawi, his contemporary, that he "urged his followers to participate in resistance against Sikh aggression in the Punjab" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 121, folio 108). Beyond that sentence the book names no tomb, no *urs* and no lineage for him other than the succession from Nur Muhammad.

The *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, composed in Persian in 1875 and read here in a modern Urdu edition, has an entry, no. 72, under the names "خواجہ محمد جمال / حافظ محمد جمال ملتانی", Khwaja Muhammad Jamal or Hafiz Muhammad Jamal Multani (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 120, folio 108). What the book is known here to say of him comes from the modern editor's footnote on that page, not from the author's own text. The footnote names him the third of the four chief *khalifas* of Khwaja Nur Muhammad Maharvi, called "صاحب قرب", those of nearness (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 120, folio 108). It gives him a death year in the hijri reckoning, 1226 AH, in a sentence that is the editor's judgement of his place in the history of the order: "ملتان میں سلسلہ چشتیہ کو حضرت حافظ محمد جمال ملتانی ف ۱۲۲۶ھ کی بدولت فروغ ہوا", in Multan the Chishtiya silsila flourished thanks to Hazrat Hafiz Muhammad Jamal Multani, d. 1226 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 120, folio 108, حاشیہ 5). The date is the editor's, not the author's.

How firmly that entry belongs to this saint is not settled. The book's index lists the name as "محمد جمال ملتانی", without the title Hafiz, against the page of the entry (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 328, folio 316), and Multan has had more than one Muhammad Jamal. The same index, against the same page, lists Nur Muhammad Maharvi, his father Hindal, a Qazi Muhammad Aqil, and the places Bahadurpur, Parhar, Maharan and Narawal (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 332, folio 320; p. 328; pp. 333–336); the index thus puts him on the same page as Nur Muhammad Maharvi and his circle, but does not itself say who Muhammad Jamal was.

## His Sayings Collected

The editor of the *Hadiqat al-Awliya* names three collections of the saint's *malfuzat*, his recorded sayings: *Gulzar-e Jamaliya* by Allama Abd al-Aziz Parharvi, *Anwar-e Jamaliya* by Ghulam Hasan, and *Asrar al-Kamaliya* by Zahid Shah, "آپ کے ملفوظات کے مجموعے ہیں", these are collections of his *malfuzat* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 120, folio 108, حاشیہ 5). For further reading the same footnote points to *Manaqib al-Mahbubin* and to Khaliq Ahmad Nizami's *Tarikh-e Mashaikh-e Chisht* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 120, folio 108, حاشیہ 5).

The bibliography at the end of *Sufi Martyrs of Love* lists works under a "Jamal al-Din Multani". One entry pairs "Jamal al-Din Multani and his disciple Khuda Bakhsh Multani" with Ghulam Husayn Multani, whose death it gives as 1265/1849, and the title *Anwar-i jamaliyya* (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 238, folio 228). Another lists *Anwar-i jamaliyya* again, compiled by 'Abd al-'Aziz Parharawi, with an edition from Multan (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 240, folio 230). A third, under Khuda Bakhsh Multani, notes: "It is noted here that the author studied with Wali Allah as well as with Jamal Multani", and dates Jamal Multani's death 1226/1811 (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 244, folio 234). The book itself never says that this Jamal al-Din Multani is the Hafiz Muhammad Jamal of its narrative; the shared death year and the shared title *Anwar-i jamaliyya* are the only ties between them.

## The Shrine

The tomb stands near the Aam Khas Bagh, outside the historic Daulat Gate of Multan. It is a fine piece of late-Mughal funerary architecture: a square chamber rising to a twelve-sided drum and a hemispherical dome. It is especially valued because it preserves original Mughal-era wall paintings, a rarity, since the murals of most such monuments have been lost to whitewash and renovation. Alam Faqri's compendium of the saints of Pakistan confirms that his shrine is in the city.

The books consulted here do not describe the tomb. None of them records who built it or when, its paintings, its custodians or any endowment.

## Devotion and Practice

The shrine remains an active centre of devotion, with an annual *urs* commemorating the saint and continuous visitation by those seeking blessing. The books consulted here give no date for the *urs* and describe no rite at this tomb.

What they say of shrine music in the city is said of Multan at large. Shemeem Burney Abbas, writing of the performers she studied, says that "They additionally sing around shrines in Multan and Sahiwal to earn a living", naming no shrine (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 199, folio not stated). Ernst and Lawrence tell of an earlier singer from the city: "One time a qawwal, Abu Bakr by name, came from Multan to visit Nizam ad-Din's teacher" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 171, folio 159).

## The Chishti Order in Multan

The claim for which Hafiz Jamal is chiefly remembered is that he established the Chishti order firmly in Multan. Alam Faqri's general compendium of Pakistani Sufi saints states plainly that Hafiz Muhammad Jamal Multani was "the first elder to lay the foundation of the Chishtia order" in Multan. The editor of the *Hadiqat al-Awliya* says that in Multan the Chishtiya silsila flourished thanks to him (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 120, folio 108, حاشیہ 5).

The same footnote credits the wider spread of the Chishtiya Nizamiya Fakhriya in the Punjab to his contemporary Khwaja Sulaiman Taunsvi: "پنجاب میں سلسلہ چشتیہ نظامیہ فخریہ کی زیادہ اشاعت حضرت خواجہ سلیمان تونسوی کے ذریعہ ہوئی" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 120, folio 108). Lahori's own text, opening its section on the Chishti masters, traces the spread of the order in Hind and the Punjab to Khwaja Mu'in al-Din Hasan Sijzi: "یہ سلسلہ ہند اور پنجاب میں حضرت خواجہ معین الدین حسن سجزی سے شائع ہوا" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 85, folio 73).

Ernst and Lawrence set the generation of Nur Muhammad's successors in a longer story. "The Nizami Chishtis came to prominence in the eighteenth century, principally in Delhi, the Deccan, and Punjab, through masters who were related to but in tension with local political leaders" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 119, folio 106). They also record a far older Chishti moment in the city: the visit of Qutb ad-Din to Multan, "a strategic commercial center in the Punjab", was the occasion on which Farid ad-Din pledged allegiance to the Chishti master (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 80, folio 66). Sujan Rai Bhandari's *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* likewise names Multan as the place where Qutbuddin Bakhtiyar Kaki, coming from Baghdad, met Shaikh Baha al-Din Zakariya (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 556). These passages speak of the order and the city, not of Hafiz Jamal or his tomb.

## In Verse

Hafiz Jamal is remembered as a poet in Arabic, Persian and Siraiki, and above all for his *Siharfi*, an acrostic poem of the Punjabi–Siraiki tradition. None of the books consulted here quotes a line of his verse or names his *Siharfi*; the poetry they do carry speaks of his city and his province.

Schimmel, writing of Punjabi poetry in general and not of Hafiz Jamal, places its flowering in his own age: "The golden age of Muslim Panjabi poetry is the late 17th and the 18th century, e.g., the time when the province was invaded one time after the other by Mahrattas, Sikhs, and, in between, by the liberating Muslim armies" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 17, folio 13). She suggests that it may be older: "In the Panjab, popular mystical poetry may have emerged as early as in the days of Fariduddin Shakarganj" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 16, folio 12). Abbas, writing of the Siraiki region, speaks of "the Siraiki belt around the Multan, Bahawalpur, Mianwali, and northern Sind areas" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 148, folio not stated).

For the vernacular mystical poets, Schimmel writes, the holy cities lie within: "Mecca and Medina (and for the Sindhi and Panjabi, also Multan) are inside the heart" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 178, folio 160). She reads a poem of Sachal as speaking "of Shams-i Tabriz, Maulana Rumi's spiritual beloved who, according to the Indian tradition, was flayed alive by the mollas in Multan where his shrine is still venerated" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 175, folio 157). And of Fakhruddin 'Iraqi, who in Multan attached himself to Baha'uddin Zakariya (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 383, folio 352), she writes: "The tender and intoxicating love poems that the Persian poet composed are still being sung by Pakistani musicians at the door of the master's tomb in Multan" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 384, folio 353).

The Punjabi kafis of Madho Lal Hussein, in Naveed Alam's English, name the city once, as the source of a bride's finery:

*The dress / someone carried across the snows / all the way from Kashmir. The dress / someone brought from Gujarat… The dress bought in Multan* (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 51, folio 51, kafi 29)

The *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, whose editor names Hafiz Jamal among Nur Muhammad's *khalifas*, closes with a chronogram by the author's son, Mufti Ghulam Safdar, encoding the year 1292 AH; its subject is the book's own, the saints of the Punjab:

جو خدا کے دوست تھے پنجاب میں
حال ان کا اس سے ہوتا ہے عیان

A chronogram on the saints of the Punjab whose lives the book records (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 282, folio 270).

## Setting: The City of Saints

The books' accounts of Multan speak of the city, not of this shrine, but they show the sacred landscape in which it stands. Bhandari, writing in Aurangzeb's reign, lists the saints buried in the city and adds that many more rest there: "بسیاری از اولیاے دران شهر سعادت بهر اسوده اند" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528). He also records a sultan who, coming to Multan, made the round of the tombs of its shaikhs, "در سنه ۸۳۵[OCR?] سلطان بملتان رسیده مزارات مشایخ را طواف نموده", the reading of the year uncertain in the printed text and no shaikh named (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 324).

Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi writes that "by the middle of the eleventh century, sufism had penetrated into the areas surrounding Multan" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 128, folio 110). Richard Eaton notes that many Muslim clans of the Punjab claim conversion by Baba Farid or by "his contemporary, Baha' al-Haqq Zakariya (d. 1263), whose tomb is in Multan city" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 227, folio 215). Schimmel writes that "Multan is the resting place of the great Suhrawardi saint Baha'uddin Zakariya (d. 1267)" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 286, folio 268). Haroon Khalid, describing the city in Guru Nanak's day, writes: "At that time Multan was one of the biggest cities of Punjab and was known for the religious shrines of Muslim saints, and Jain and Hindu temples. It was a sacred space for people of all religious denominations" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 123, folio 105). Harish Dhillon writes that "By the time Nanak came to Multan, the city was famous as a major centre of Sufi learning and philosophy" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 167, folio 183).

The Sikh attacks on Multan that Ernst and Lawrence connect with Hafiz Jamal's name belong to a longer conflict over the city, which the books record only in general terms. Haroon Khalid writes of the Sikh period that "uprisings led by Muslim leaders began in Kasur and Multan", and that the rebellion "was given a religious colour against the infidel Ranjit Singh" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 210, folio not stated). Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti's *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* records the fall of the city to Ranjit Singh: when he conquered Multan, Nawab Muzaffar Khan, "یہ[OCR?] نواب صاحبان والی ملتان", was killed, and members of his house were taken with their families to Lahore, where they lived in the author's day (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 130). Another passage of the same book, dated in words, tells of Kharak Singh sent with an army to conquer Multan, and of Multan conquered: "کھڑک سنگھ فتح ملتان پر بھیجا کی ملتان فتح ہوا" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 580). The editor of the *Hadiqat al-Awliya* counts it among the book's merits that its author recorded as an eyewitness what the notables of the Punjab and the tombs of its shaikhs suffered under the Sikhs, "مشائخ کے مقابر کی تباہی" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 33, folio 21). None of these passages names Hafiz Jamal or his shrine.

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **His death year.** The site's account and Ernst and Lawrence give 1811 (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 121, folio 108). The editor of the *Hadiqat al-Awliya* gives 1226 AH, in the hijri reckoning only (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 120, folio 108, حاشیہ 5). The bibliography of *Sufi Martyrs of Love* gives 1226/1811 for a "Jamal Multani" whom the book does not equate with its Hafiz Muhammad Jamal (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 244, folio 234).
- **His name.** The site's account calls him Hafiz Muhammad Jamal Multani, as do Ernst and Lawrence in their narrative (Hafiz Muhammad Jamal) (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 121, folio 108). The *Hadiqat al-Awliya* heads its entry with two forms, "خواجہ محمد جمال / حافظ محمد جمال ملتانی" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 120, folio 108), and indexes him as "محمد جمال ملتانی", without the title Hafiz (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 328, folio 316). The bibliography of *Sufi Martyrs of Love* has "Jamal al-Din Multani" and "Jamal Multani" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 238, folio 228; p. 244, folio 234).
- **His master's name.** The site's account names Khwaja Noor Muhammad Maharvi, and the *Hadiqat al-Awliya*'s footnote on Hafiz Jamal has Nur Muhammad Maharvi (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 120, folio 108). In the same book's entry on Khwaja Sulaiman Taunsvi, the same master's *nisba* is printed Majheel[OCR?], a reading uncertain in the printed text (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 123, folio 111). Ernst and Lawrence call him simply Nur Muhammad (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 121, folio 108).
- **The Chishti order in Multan.** The site's account credits Hafiz Jamal with establishing the order firmly in the city, and Alam Faqri calls him "the first elder to lay the foundation of the Chishtia order" in Multan. The editor of the *Hadiqat al-Awliya* says that the order flourished in Multan thanks to him (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 120, folio 108, حاشیہ 5). Ernst and Lawrence set an earlier Chishti event in the city, Farid ad-Din's pledge of allegiance to the Chishti master on Qutb ad-Din's visit to Multan (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 80, folio 66), and Bhandari has Qutbuddin Bakhtiyar Kaki meeting Baha al-Din Zakariya there (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 556).
- **The *Anwar-e Jamaliya* and its compiler.** The editor of the *Hadiqat al-Awliya* names *Anwar-e Jamaliya* as the work of Ghulam Hasan and Abd al-Aziz Parharvi as the author of a different collection, *Gulzar-e Jamaliya* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 120, folio 108, حاشیہ 5). The bibliography of *Sufi Martyrs of Love* lists *Anwar-i jamaliyya* as compiled by 'Abd al-'Aziz Parharawi (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 240, folio 230), and elsewhere sets the same title beside Ghulam Husayn Multani (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 238, folio 228); its subject there is "Jamal al-Din Multani", whom the book does not equate with Hafiz Muhammad Jamal.

## Legacy

Hafiz Muhammad Jamal Multani is remembered as the master through whom the Chishti order took firm root in Multan,. His tomb, with its rare surviving Mughal-era paintings, remains an active centre of devotion and part of the dense sacred geography of Multan alongside the great shrines of Bahauddin Zakariya, Shah Rukn-e-Alam and Shah Shams Sabzwari.

The books add two strands to that memory. Collections of his sayings are named by the editor of the *Hadiqat al-Awliya*: *Gulzar-e Jamaliya*, *Anwar-e Jamaliya* and *Asrar al-Kamaliya* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 120, folio 108, حاشیہ 5). And in the history of the order he is remembered alongside Sulayman Taunsawi as one of Nur Muhammad's successors, who "is also said to have taken part in resistance to Sikh attacks on Multan" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 121, folio 108).
