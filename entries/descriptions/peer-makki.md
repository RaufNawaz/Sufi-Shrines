## Overview

Standing close to the great mausoleum of Data Ganj Bakhsh, in the Walled City's Bhati Gate quarter, the shrine of Peer Makki commemorates Hazrat Syed Azizuddin al-Hassani wal-Hussaini, a scholar-mystic born in Baghdad — the son of Hazrat Peer Syed Abdullah, of a Hasani-Hussaini family of religious scholars that counted Junaid Baghdadi, Imam Ahmad bin Hanbal and Sheikh Abdul Qadir Jilani among its kin. He is said to have migrated to Mecca in 562 AH, at about the age of thirty, and to have spent twelve years there in ascetic seclusion — hence his popular title "Makki" — before journeying overland to India. Hagiographical accounts hold that he arrived in Lahore in 575 AH (around 1178 CE) by what tradition describes as divine instruction, and remained for some thirty-six years as a teacher of both the exoteric sciences of Islamic law and the esoteric path of the Junaidi Sufi lineage, tracing back to the classical Baghdad master Junayd. He is said to have died in 612 AH (1215 CE).

The shrine complex includes an adjoining mosque, the Hazrat Pir Makki Masjid, and is formally administered, like Data Darbar itself, by the Punjab Auqaf and Religious Affairs Department; its tradition is Syed and Ahl-e-Sunnat. Despite its proximity to one of Pakistan's most visited shrines, Peer Makki's own tomb receives markedly fewer pilgrims, giving it a more intimate, contemplative atmosphere.

Three of the books consulted name the shrine itself. Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori's *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, in its modern Urdu edition, gives him an entry of his own as Sayyid Shaikh Aziz al-Din Makki (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 18, folio 6), places his death in 612 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 201, folio 189), and lists his tomb among those still standing, on Ravi Road, in Pir Makki Street (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 290, folio 278). Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti's *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, a survey of the tombs and buildings of Lahore, describes the grave, its wells and its keeper, and records an *ʿurs* and a land grant; but Chishti found no account of the saint in any book and proposes, only tentatively, who he may have been (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 674; p. 675). Kanhaiya Lal's *Tarikh-e Lahore* lists the tomb of Pir Makki in its table of contents among the Muslim sites outside the city (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 5, folio 4). The books disagree with the site's account, and with one another, on where the tomb stands, on when the saint died, and on who he was; those differences are set out below.

## The Life of the Saint

His father, Hazrat Peer Syed Abdullah, was himself a saint and a scholar who taught in Baghdad, and Peer Makki had his first education from him — memorising the Qur'an, then learning what is reported as the knowledge of the soul and of the sect, seated in the same row as everyone else in his father's circle. Having taken the outward sciences as far as they went, he judged that he must go looking for the hidden knowledge, and in 562 AH, at thirty, he left Baghdad for Mecca. He spent twelve years there, hearing the pleas of those who came to him, helping them, keeping his nights in the Haram in the remembrance of God, and — it is reported — receiving the blessing of the Prophet many times. He began to know what was to come, and did not show it.

The migration to India, it is reported, was not his own idea. Worshipping in the Haram, he received the Prophet's command to go to Hind, where the people had fallen into associating partners with God, and to bring them back to the Sunna and to the oneness of God. He did not wish to leave the House of God, but the command was the command: he made his last Hajj, took some companions with him, and turned first to Baghdad, to tell his family and to say the Fatiha at his father's grave, before setting out. Two roads led to India, one through the dry desert and one by sea; the journey was long and dangerous and there was little to eat or drink, but he preached along the way, and people were moved enough to join his caravan. Something close to a year later he reached India and gave thanks. He came to Lahore in 575 AH and settled in the mosque that today carries his name beside his shrine, leading the prayers, teaching the Qur'an, and calling people away from polytheism.

Alam Faqri's *Tazkirah Awliya-e-Pakistan* preserves the same broad outline — his given name Azizuddin, his Baghdad origin, and the twelve years spent in seclusion at Mecca that earned him the epithet "Makki" — and adds that on first reaching Lahore he went directly to the shrine of Data Ganj Bakhsh before settling at the site that became his own; the detail reinforces, without resolving, the chronological tension between the two saints' conventional dates that is set out below.

The political part of his life is reported at length. Lahore was held by Khusrau Malik — reportedly the last of Mahmud of Ghazni's line, and the son of King Khusrau — who is described as pleasure-seeking and unjust; Muhammad Shahab-ud-Din Ghori was advancing on the city, having already taken Multan and Sialkot. When Khusrau Malik came to ask how long his rule would last, the saint told him plainly that it would end soon, that what God has written must come, and that he should turn to God and deal justly with his people. When Ghori's army arrived, the people and the king both ran to him; he told them to be patient and to repent, and said the danger would pass and return in six years. Ghori withdrew, and the saint's name went round the city — but the people soon returned to their old habits, and exactly six years later Ghori came again. Again the saint promised a respite, and said it was the last; again the army turned back, and this time people called him a liar, saying the old man only ever said such things and that Ghori left out of fear. When Ghori finally took Lahore, Khusrau Malik's men were killed or went over to him, and the king himself was taken prisoner and later killed. Ghori, it is reported, came to the saint's door and told him he had known he was there, and that everything he had said had been true.

He preached in Lahore for thirty-six years after that, and died in 612 AH; the quarter took his name from him, as the title Makki had come from his years in Mecca.

## The Saint in the Books

Lahori's *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, composed in 1875, gives the saint an entry with a date. Its table of contents lists him as entry 136, Sayyid Shaikh Aziz al-Din Makki (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 18, folio 6), and the author's text gives his death as 612 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 201, folio 189). The book's index files him as Aziz al-Din Makki, so that both elements of his name here, Azizuddin and Makki, stand in the index's own words; the same index lists three other men named Aziz al-Din, none of them called Makki (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 323, folio 311). The index also cites him on a second page, inside the book's run of Suhrawardi saints of Multan, and does not say why (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 323, folio 311).

It is the edition's modern editor who ties the book's Shaikh Aziz al-Din to the popular name. He writes that he has written a detailed article on Shaikh Aziz al-Din Makki Lahori, under the heading "Pir Makki", the name by which Lahore knows him, for the *Danishnama-e Shibh-e Qarra*, an encyclopaedia of the Subcontinent published in Tehran (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 350, folio 338). The identification of the *Hadiqat*'s saint with Peer Makki is therefore the editor's.

The editor also weighs the dates. He rejects a death year of 1048 AH which, he says, Nur Ahmad Chishti wrote down in the *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* (he cites its pages 601–607) without any reference, and calls it baseless (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 351, folio 339). Against it he finds the death year recorded by the author, Mufti Ghulam Sarwar, the more plausible, without calling it established: 612 AH, which he equates with 1215–16 CE, the Common Era span printed in reversed order (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 351, folio 339). Yet a footnote in the same edition, quoting the author's other book, the Persian *Khazinat al-Asfiya*, gives the same man's death as 796 AH, the reading of the year uncertain in the printed text (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 191, folio 189 as printed). The edition does not remark on the difference between the author's two books.

The editor's general verdict on what Lahore's historians had written about the saint is severe. They wrote down hearsay and did no research, he says, so that even the years of the conflict between the Ghurid and Ghaznavid sultans are given wrongly (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 350, folio 338). He includes the author's own *Khazinat al-Asfiya* in that charge (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 351, folio 339). The saint's entry thus stands, in this edition, against the background of the Ghurid and Ghaznavid struggle for Lahore that is also reported in the account of his life above.

Chishti's *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, as it is read here, says something different again. At the tomb he writes that the saint's circumstances are not known, and reports two opinions: some say he was a disciple of Hazrat Data Ganj Sahib, others that he came to Lahore in the company of Mahmud of Ghazni (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 674). The author adds that he found no account of him in any book he had read, though the words of this sentence are uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 674).

He then offers an identification, and hedges it. A book he calls the *Tazkira-e Fuqara* names a Hazrat Sa'd al-Din, whose epithet, perhaps Makki, cannot be read with certainty in the printed text, who came from Mecca the Exalted (the city's name is also uncertain in print), visited the *khanqah* of Hujwiri, stayed there in *iʿtikaf*, died, and was buried by the river at Shah Jahan's order, with a *maqbara* raised over him (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 675). Perhaps, Chishti writes, this is the same holy man (a word of the sentence is uncertain in print); the *maqbara* is not there, but if it has gone, that would be no wonder. He gives that saint's death as the 12th of Rabi al-Thani, the day uncertain and the year illegible in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 675).

## The Shrine

Kings maintained the shrine after him and added to its beauty, and it is reported that the Auqaf Department took responsibility for it in 1958. The shrine complex includes the adjoining Hazrat Pir Makki Masjid, the mosque in which, reportedly, the saint settled on reaching Lahore; its tradition is Syed and Ahl-e-Sunnat.

Chishti's survey of the tombs of Lahore describes the place as it stood in his day. It lies west of the *takia* he has just described, at the south-eastern corner of the garden the text calls the Committee Bagh; a brick wall rises above a man's height, and within it the grave of Hazrat Makki (the name uncertain in the printed text), built in brick and mortar, stands on a platform (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 674). There were two masonry wells, a *ghusl-khana*, and a platform marking a mosque (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 675). The keeper was a *faqir* woman, her age printed as 59, a reading that is uncertain, with two sons, Fateh Din and Karam Din (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 675). The shrine held land: cultivated land measured in *kanals*, the number unclear in the printed text, held free of revenue for as long as the *khanqah* stands (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 675). Elsewhere in the same book the shrine appears in passing beside its great neighbour: describing the garden of Zeb-un-Nisa, Chishti says its wall can be seen, like the places of Data Ganj Bakhsh Sahib and Pir Makki Sahib, in a passage whose printed text is partly uncertain (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 342).

Lahori's edition locates the tomb in its appendix of the saints' resting places as they stood when the edition was made. Row 49 gives Sayyid Shaikh Aziz al-Din Makki Lahori, may his secret be sanctified, at Lahore, Ravi Road, Pir Makki Street (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 290, folio 278). The site's own account places the shrine in the Bhati Gate area near Data Darbar; the two descriptions are not shown to conflict, and not shown to agree. The same appendix gives, under another row, a mosque in Anarkali whose name contains the word Makki; it is a different place (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 291).

Kanhaiya Lal's *Tarikh-e Lahore* of 1884 promises an entry on the tomb. Its table of contents lists the tomb of Pir Makki in the section on the Muslim sites outside the city, the page number printed beside it, 325, uncertain in the printed text; the entry itself is not among the passages of the book consulted here (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 5, folio 4).

## Devotion and Practice

Devotional custom in Lahore holds that pilgrims should pay their respects at Peer Makki's tomb before proceeding to Data Darbar, honouring him as a spiritual forerunner in the same sacred locale. Peer Makki's own tomb receives some 3,500 to 4,000 pilgrims on an ordinary day, though Thursday-night gatherings draw larger crowds, and the narrow street and surrounding Peer Makki Bazaar can make access difficult.

The annual urs, held on the 9th to the 11th of Rabi-ul-Awal, fills the quarter with gatherings and dhikr, drums and dhamal, qawwali, the ritual bathing of the shrine and the distribution of offerings, and draws on the order of 400,000 to 500,000 devotees.

Chishti, in his own day, records the *ʿurs* in the month of Rabi al-Awwal, on what the printed text, which garbles the month's name, appears to give as the twelfth of the month; *halwa* and *kheer* were served (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 675). He adds that in earlier days a great fair used to be held (the word for the fair uncertain in print), and that fewer people came in his own time (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 675).

## Legends and Miracles

A well-loved anecdote recalls that during Sultan Muhammad Ghori's 1178 siege of Lahore, the besieged governor Khusro Malik sought Peer Makki's prayers, and the saint assured him of six further years of safety. The fuller account reported of the two sieges, the twice-promised respite and Ghori's visit to the saint's door is given above.

The popular claim that he personally tutored Data Ganj Bakhsh sits awkwardly with the conventional dating of Ali Hujwiri's death around 1072, a century before Peer Makki's own arrival, and should be understood as devotional tradition rather than strict chronology. A tradition recorded at the shrine itself runs the other way about: when Ali Hujwiri was ordered to migrate to Lahore, he is said to have objected that "Peer Makki is present there, who is enough for Lahore" — only to arrive and find Peer Makki's funeral already underway. Like the tutoring claim, the story fits loosely with the dates, but it carries the shrine's essential teaching: that Lahore has never been without a saint of Allah.

Chishti knew yet another version of the two saints' bond, and reports it only as what some say: that Peer Makki was Data Ganj Bakhsh's disciple (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 674). The saint he tentatively identifies with Peer Makki likewise comes to Hujwiri's *khanqah* as a visitor (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 675).

## Setting: Ghaznavid and Ghurid Lahore

The books consulted tell the history of the city in which the saint's prophecies are reported to have been made, without naming him in it; what follows is about Lahore, not about the shrine.

Of the neighbouring shrine, and not of this one, Annemarie Schimmel writes that Lahore was "the capital of the Ghaznawids in India", where Hujwiri "died there in 1071", and that "His shrine, called that of Data Ganj Bakhsh, is still a popular place of pilgrimage in Lahore" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 119, folio 88). Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi has Hujwiri reach Lahore "in c. 1035, while Sultan Mas'ud I (1031-41) reigned in Ghazna" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 130, folio 112).

Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, written under Aurangzeb, gives the last Ghaznavid rulers of Lahore. Khusraw Shah, son of Bahram Shah, driven from Ghazni by the Ghurid sultan, took Lahore and governed the Punjab until his death in 555; his son, Sultan Khusraw Malik son of Khusraw Shah, succeeded him, and his rule in Lahore lasted twenty-eight years (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 405). Bhandari dates Shihab al-Din's third campaign, the one directed at Lahore, to the year 575, uncertain as printed, when Sultan Khusraw Malik, of the line of Sultan Mahmud of Ghazni, held the government of Lahore; the fort was nearly taken after siege and battle, and Khusraw Malik sent his son with an elephant as tribute and made peace (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 404). In the fifth campaign, in 580 (again uncertain in print), the sultan besieged Lahore again and Khusraw Malik shut himself in the fort (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 404); the book's account of the suba of Lahore says of the same year that Shihab al-Din came to take Lahore, besieged it, and could not master it (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 517). In the sixth, in 582 (also uncertain in print), Khusraw Malik submitted, was taken to Ghazni and died there, and the sultan set his own deputy over Lahore (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 404).

Kanhaiya Lal tells the end of the dynasty in the same terms: when the Ghurids drove the house from Ghazni, Khusrau Shah, Khusrau Malik's father, established his own rule in the Punjab, and with Khusrau Malik's surrender the rule of the Ghurid sultans in Hind began and that of the Ghaznavids came to an end (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 24, folio 19). Chishti, too, has Khusrau Shah son of Bahram make Lahore his capital, his son Khusrau Malik succeed him, and the Ghurids take the land from Khusrau by a thousand tricks and deceits (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 808–809). Rizvi dates the Ghurid seizure of Multan to 1175, when "Mu'izzu'd-Din Muhammad bin Sam seized Multan, establishing firm control and Sunni rule over the southern part of the Panjab" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 128, folio 110). Harish Dhillon, on the other hand, writes that Ghori "was not able to conquer Lahore" when he captured the Punjab in 1192 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 136, folio 151).

## Setting: Bhati Gate and the Name Makki

Bhati Gate, where the site's account places the shrine, is one of the twelve gates of the walled city: Chishti's list of them ends with the Bhati Gate and the Mori Gate (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 498), and Haroon Khalid writes of "the walled city of Lahore, of which Bhatti gate is one of the twelve entry points" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated). Kanhaiya Lal describes Bhati as the city's seventh gate and names no shrine in it (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 18, folio 13).

Two other names in Kanhaiya Lal's history echo this one and belong to other people. His contents list, apart from the tomb of Peer Makki, a tomb of Abd al-Razzaq Makki, the epithet uncertain in the printed text (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 4, folio 3). And he heads one passage with the thirteenth gate, the Zaki Gate known as the Makki Gate (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 20, folio 15): it is named for a Pir Zaki, a gate guard of Mughal times who was martyred at the gate; Kanhaiya Lal gives him no Syed descent, no date and no Sufi order, and presents the form Makki as a corruption of Zaki. In a later passage both the man's name and the gate's, Pir Zaki and Zaki Gate, are uncertain readings in the printed text (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 164, folio 167). The book does not connect either figure with Hazrat Syed Azizuddin.

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **Where the tomb stands.** The site's account places it in the Walled City's Bhati Gate quarter, close to Data Darbar. Lahori's edition gives Ravi Road, Pir Makki Street (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 290, folio 278). Chishti sets it west of a *takia*, at the south-eastern corner of the Committee Bagh (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 674). Kanhaiya Lal's contents list it among the sites outside the city (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 5, folio 4), and his account of Bhati Gate names no shrine there (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 18, folio 13).
- **When the saint died.** The site's account and the survey give 612 AH (1215 CE). Lahori's text gives 612 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 201, folio 189), and his editor calls 612 AH, or 1215–16 CE, the more plausible (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 351, folio 339). A footnote in the same edition quoting Lahori's *Khazinat al-Asfiya* gives 796 AH, the reading uncertain (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 191, folio 189 as printed). The editor reports that Nur Ahmad Chishti gave 1048 AH, and rejects it as baseless (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 351, folio 339). In Chishti's *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* as read here, the author gives no date for Peer Makki himself, and for the saint he tentatively identifies with him gives the 12th of Rabi al-Thani, the day uncertain and the year illegible (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 675).
- **Who he was and when he came.** The site's account and the survey make him Hazrat Syed Azizuddin of Baghdad, who reached Lahore from Mecca in 575 AH. Lahori's editor identifies the *Hadiqat*'s Shaikh Aziz al-Din Makki Lahori with Peer Makki (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 350, folio 338). Chishti writes that his circumstances are not known; some say he came with Mahmud of Ghazni; and Chishti himself suggests, with a "perhaps", a saint from Mecca who was buried by the river at Shah Jahan's order (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 674; p. 675).
- **Peer Makki and Data Ganj Bakhsh.** Popular claim has Peer Makki tutor Data Ganj Bakhsh; a tradition recorded at the shrine has Hujwiri arrive in Lahore to find Peer Makki's funeral under way; Alam Faqri has Peer Makki go first to the shrine of Data Ganj Bakhsh on reaching Lahore. Chishti reports that some call Peer Makki Data Ganj Bakhsh's disciple (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 674).
- **Hujwiri's death.** The site's account gives the conventional date of Ali Hujwiri's death as around 1072. Schimmel writes that he "died there in 1071" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 119, folio 88).
- **The date of the urs.** The site's account gives the 9th to the 11th of Rabi-ul-Awal. Chishti's printed text, which garbles the month's name, appears to give the twelfth of Rabi al-Awwal (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 675).
- **Ghori and Lahore.** The site's account dates Ghori's siege, in which the governor sought the saint's prayers, to 1178, and the survey has Ghori return exactly six years later and at last take the city, with Khusrau Malik taken prisoner and later killed. Bhandari dates the Ghurid campaigns against Lahore to 575, 580 and 582, the years uncertain in the printed text, and has Khusraw Malik submit in 582 and die at Ghazni (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 404). Dhillon writes that Ghori "was not able to conquer Lahore" when he captured the Punjab in 1192 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 136, folio 151). Lahori's editor holds that Lahore's historians wrote even the years of the Ghurid–Ghaznavid conflict wrongly (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 350, folio 338).
- **When the Auqaf took charge.** The field survey says the Auqaf Department took charge of the shrine in 1958. No book consulted gives a year.

## Legacy

Many writers — including, it is said at the shrine, authors from the Hindu and Christian communities — have written books about the saint that are still read with interest today. The books consulted here show how unsettled that literature has been: a tomb that Chishti could describe in detail while finding no account of its saint in any book (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 674); an identification and a date that Lahori's modern editor offers as the more plausible, against a footnote in his own edition that puts the same man nearly two centuries later (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 350, folio 338; p. 351, folio 339); and the editor's own judgement that Lahore's historians had written hearsay about him (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 350, folio 338). Through all of it the shrine has kept its name and its place beside Data Darbar, and the quarter, Peer Makki Bazaar and Pir Makki Street, carries the title Makki that his years in Mecca are said to have given him.
