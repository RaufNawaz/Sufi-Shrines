## Overview

Gurdwara Bal Lila Sahib stands at Nankana Sahib in the Punjab, some three hundred metres to the south-east of Gurdwara Janam Asthan, the birthplace of Guru Nanak (1469–1539). Its name — *Bal Lila*, "the play of childhood" — marks the open field where, according to Sikh tradition, the child Nanak gathered his companions and passed the days of his boyhood. It is among the cluster of shrines that make Nankana Sahib, the Guru's birth town, the foremost place of Sikh pilgrimage in present-day Pakistan, and it preserves the memory not of a miracle or a martyrdom but of something gentler: the ordinary yet luminous childhood of the founder of the faith.

Two of the books consulted describe the gurdwara itself, both by Haroon Khalid. In *Walking with Nanak* he calls it "Gurdwara Bal Lila", sets it "A few feet away from Gurdwara Patti Sahib", and writes: "According to Sikh tradition, this was once an open field where Guru Nanak used to play as a child" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 90, folio 72; p. 91, folio 73). In *A White Trail* it is "Gurdwara Balila", "near Gurdwara Janamasthan" and "Situated across the road from Gurdwara Patti Sahib", and "this Gurdwara is constructed to mark the spot where Guru Nanak used to play as a child" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 177, folio not stated). Writing of the Guru's birthday festival at Nankana, Khalid names it again as the place "where he played as a child" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated).

The other books do not mention the gurdwara. Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi's history speaks of Nankana Sahib only as the Guru's birthplace and names no gurdwara there of any kind (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 407, folio 383). Harish Dhillon's retelling of the Janamsakhis, Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* and Khalid's *In Search of Shiva* speak of Talwandi, of Nankana Sahib and of the Guru, but of no shrine at the place where he played. What they say is gathered below as being about the town and the Guru, not about this gurdwara.

## Guru Nanak's Childhood at Talwandi

Nankana Sahib was in the Guru's day the village of Rai Bhoi di Talwandi, held by Rai Bular Bhatti, the local landlord. It was here that Nanak was born and here that he spent his early years. The *janamsakhi* narratives — the traditional accounts of the Guru's life — dwell fondly on his boyhood, portraying a child unlike his fellows: drawn to contemplation, indifferent to games for their own sake, and given to gathering the other children around him not merely to play but to sit in stillness and turn their minds towards the divine Name. Tradition holds that the field of Bal Lila was the scene of these gatherings, where the young Nanak is remembered as a leader among his playmates and as a soul already absorbed in God.

Such stories are, of course, the pious recollections of a community looking back upon the childhood of its founder through the light of all that he later became. Whether taken as history or as devotional memory, they fixed this unremarkable patch of ground in the sacred geography of the Panth, and a shrine rose to honour it.

The books agree that the town of the Guru's birth was Talwandi. Rizvi places his birth in "the village of Talwandi later known as Nankana Sahib, about forty miles south-west of Lahore, now part of Pakistan" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 407, folio 383). Khalid gives him as "Born Talwandi (now known as Nankana Sahib in Pakistan) on October 20, 1469" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 314, folio 288), and writes that "The name of Rai Bhoi di Talwindi has been changed to Nankana Sahib in honour of the first Sikh Guru who was born there" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 31, folio 13). Talwandi, he says, is the place "where there are several gurdwaras marking events from his childhood" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 43, folio 25). In *A White Trail* he adds that Nanak spent his early years in the city, hence its "several Gurdwaras", all of which the pilgrims visit (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated).

Bhandari, writing in Persian in the fortieth regnal year of Aurangzeb, records the birth and the boyhood in one sentence:

درمکان تلونڈی رائے بهوئه[OCR?] سعادت ولادت یافته همانجا درخانه جد ابادری می بود

He was born at Talwandi Rai Bhoi, the reading of whose name is uncertain in the printed text, and lived there in his grandfather's house (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). Bhandari also writes that "درخورد سالگی(۱) علامات کشف و کرامات و ایات خارق عادات از و بمنصه ظهور رسید": that in his early years signs of spiritual unveiling and of wonders, marks beyond the ordinary course of things, appeared in him. The editor's footnote gives a variant reading, "ده سالگی", at the age of ten (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).

Dhillon's retelling has the Guru born "On 14 April 1469" to Kalyan Chand Das Bedi, "or Mehta Kalu as he was affectionately called", accountant of the Bhatti family under Rai Bular (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 17–18, folios 19–20). The village astrologer foretold greatness for the child, and at school he showed "a brilliance that had never before been seen in any child in Talwandi" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 18, folio 20). In Khalid's reconstruction Mehta Kalu keeps "the accounts of the ruler of Talwindi, Rai Bular Bhatti", who is his friend (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 22, folio 4), and calls himself "an accountant" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 63, folio 45). Khalid writes that "Nanak belonged to the Bedi caste" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 100, folio 82).

Before the birth, two accounts tell of a mother's prayers. Khalid, citing Dhillon's *The First Sikh Spiritual Master*, writes: "Mata Triptaji, the mother of Guru Nanak, did not have a child for a long time after her marriage and she would regularly visit an ancient Hindu temple situated on top of a mound not very far from Rai Bhoi di Talwindi to pray for a child" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 30–31, folio 13). In his reconstruction she presents herself to sadhus and fakirs in "the forest around Rai Bhoi di Talwindi" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 21, folio 3). A booklet of the Auqaf department at the shrine of Naulakha Hazari says instead: "Guru Nanak was born after Baba Naulakha Hazari prayed for his mother" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 31, folio 13). Iqbal Qaiser, Khalid's guide, told him the same tradition: "The Sikhs don't accept it but the Muslim tradition is that Guru Nanak was born by the blessing of Baba Naulakh Hazari. It is believed that Nanak's parents came to this shrine and the saint blessed them after which Nanak was born." (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated)

The best-remembered episode of the boyhood is the sacred thread. In Dhillon's retelling the boy, at thirteen, refused the *janeu* in his father's courtyard: "it is our actions and our actions alone" that give a man his position among men, and the refusal was "the beginning of a lifelong crusade against the performing of empty rites and rituals" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 19–21, folios 21–23). In Khalid's reconstruction the thread was proposed by his father and "the local priest of Rai Bhoi di Talwindi"; Nanak's refusal "caused a furore", and "Respectable Hindus started to avoid the Bedi" family (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 61–62, folios 43–44).

Both writers give the boy a Muslim teacher. Dhillon's Nanak "became well acquainted with the dervish Sayyad Hussain and gained an intimate knowledge of the Sufi line of thought" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 24, folio 28). Khalid's mentor is "Sayyed Hassan, a Muslim Sufi, who lived near his house", who argued that "Islam teaches one to adopt the middle path" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 62, folio 44).

The boy of the books was often out of doors. At sixteen, Dhillon writes, he herded cattle (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 23, folio 27). In Khalid's reconstruction he herded buffaloes, which once strayed into another man's field, the owner's name being uncertain in the printed text (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 63, folio 45; p. 64, folio 46), and he was found hiding in a grove of trees outside the town by his father and "his employer and friend, Rai Bular" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 64, folio 46). At dusk he sits under "this waan tree", its branches hung with cloths "tied as supplication", "about two furlongs" from home, where he can hear both the *azaan* and the temple bells (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 59, folio 41; p. 60, folio 42).

Talwandi, Dhillon writes, "lay in the path of numerous mendicants, sadhus and holy men", who camped "in a grove of trees on the outskirts of the village" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 24, folio 28). Khalid writes of an old mound outside the town where a young Nanak "used to spend a lot of time in conversation with yogis, sadhus and dervish who would pass through Talwindi"; "It is believed that in the years between the incident of Sacha Sauda and his migration to Sultanpur, Nanak started to frequent this place" — "That period of six to seven years" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 91, folio 73; p. 92, folio 74). In Khalid's reconstruction Nanak meets ascetics and Sufis "on the outskirts of Talwindi" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 67, folio 49) and spends time "at the abandoned mound on the outskirts of Talwindi" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 66, folio 48).

The Sacha Sauda story, the "true bargain", begins and ends at Talwandi. "According to the legend, when Baba Guru Nanak turned eighteen, his father asked him to take up a trade. He gave him twenty rupees", and Nanak left "the city (present day Nankana Sahib)" with Bhai Mardana (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 182, folio not stated). In Dhillon's retelling the goods were to be sold in Talwandi (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 25, folio 29), and the story closes in "the forest" and back at Talwandi (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 38, folio 42). In the version a Muslim storyteller told Khalid at Gurdwara Sachkand, "his father in Nankana Sahib became worried", and "At Nankana" the father asked what trade the son had done (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 74, folio 56; p. 75, folio 57).

From Talwandi the young man went to Sultanpur, "around 60 km from Talwandi", as keeper of Nawab Daulat Khan's stores (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 44, folio 49). He married, in Dhillon's account, on 24 September 1487, Sulakhini, the "daughter of Mul Chand and Chando Rani of the town of Batala" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 44, folio 49); in Khalid's reconstruction Sulakhni is "the daughter of Moolchand Khatri from the village of Pakhoke near Gurdaspur" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 67, folio 49). Bhandari has him travel most of the world, then come to the town of Batala, marry, and settle in a village of Batala on the Ravi (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).

He came home between his journeys. In Dhillon's retelling, "Nanak and Mardana finally returned to Talwandi" in 1506; "Mata Tripta felt great joy"; he spent time every day with Rai Bhoe, who looked upon him as his Guru (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 123, folio 137), and Mehta Kalu was "a little in awe of his son" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 124, folio 138). After Pak Pattan, "Nanak travelled back to Talwandi", which "was now no longer an obscure little village but had become a place of pilgrimage", and he sat by Rai Bular, who was sick and failing (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 172, folio 188). In Khalid's reconstruction Nanak and Mardana leave "the familiar streets of Talwindi" with friends and families gathered "at the edge of the town" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 239, folio 213), and years later Nanak sends for his family from Talwandi to Kartarpur (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 296, folio 270). His saying that there was no Hindu and no Muslim, Khalid writes, was questioned by friends and family "at Rai Bhoi Di Talwindi" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 144, folio 126).

## The Shrine and Its Sacred Tank

Beside the gurdwara lies a *sarovar*, the holy tank called Nanaksar, whose waters are held sacred by pilgrims. Tradition ascribes the original excavation of this pond, together with an early shrine, to Rai Bular, the Guru's own patron, who is remembered in Sikh devotion for the reverence he showed the boy Nanak. In later centuries the site drew the patronage of the Sikh state: the present building is traditionally associated with the mid-eighteenth century and the munificence of *Diwan Kaura Mal*, the influential minister of that era, and in the early nineteenth century Maharaja Ranjit Singh is said to have had the shrine renovated, the tank enlarged and properly lined with masonry, and generous grants of land settled upon it for its upkeep.

The books consulted record neither the tank nor the builder of the gurdwara. Of Ranjit Singh's patronage in general Khalid writes that "When Maharaja Ranjit Singh became the sovereign of Punjab at the end of the eighteenth century he spent large amounts of money on the renovation and expansion of many gurdwaras", and "attached vast tracts of land to these gurdwaras, so that the revenues from the lands could facilitate their functioning" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 34, folio 16). In *A White Trail* he writes that Ranjit Singh renovated "many Gurdwaras all over Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 208, folio not stated). Neither passage names Bal Lila.

Khalid's two books are the only ones to describe the gurdwara's condition. When he was at Nankana for *A White Trail*, the gurdwara was under repair: Surjeet, one of the Sikhs of the town, told him that no children's event would be held there that year "because the Gurdwara is being renovated" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 177, folio not stated).

## Devotion and Practice

The gurdwara is remembered for children's games. Khalid writes in *Walking with Nanak*: "Every year on the occasion of Lohri, sports competitions are arranged for children within the premises of this gurdwara, to mark the event" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 91, folio 73). In *A White Trail* Surjeet tells him: "Last year, we held a small event at Gurdwara Balila", for the younger children, with racing and tug of war; there would be none that year, because of the renovation (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 177, folio not stated). Surjeet and Udam speak Urdu "with a Pasthu accent", their families having "moved to Nankana a few years ago" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 174, folio not stated), and of the cricket tournament held for the birthday of Guru Gobind Singh Surjeet said: "For the first time, this year we have arranged this cricket tournament" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 176, folio not stated).

Of Lohri itself, Khalid writes that it is "an ancient festival of the Punjab that commemorates the change of season", celebrated "on the 13th of January, which is generally the last night of the month of Poh", with a village bonfire, dry fruit, folk songs, children collecting wood, and *kheer* cooled overnight (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 192–193, folio not stated). After the creation of Pakistan "this indigenous festival of the Punjab died down" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 193, folio not stated). An informant, Kartar, told him that Lohri and Guru Gobind Singh's birthday "have only begun in the last couple of years" at Nankana, made possible "by the increase in the Sikh population as a result of the migration from the tribal areas after the war"; "Earlier, there was only a prayer at the Gurdwara." (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 193, folio not stated). "The celebration of Lohri at Nankana Sahib this year is an attempt to revive this cultural tradition of Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 193–194, folio not stated); "It is ironical though that this Punjabi tradition shunned by Punjab is now being revived by a group of Pathan men residing in Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 194, folio not stated).

For most of the year, Khalid writes of Nankana's gurdwaras as a group, "All year round most of them remain locked, only to be opened at the time of the festival. There are security guards employed at the entrance to all of them." (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 227, folio not stated) The great festival is the Guru's birthday. "According to official estimates, ten to fifteen thousand people come to Nankana Sahib for this festival every year, which is celebrated during the last days of November"; in 2011, "According to media reports, only three thousand people are attending"; "This year it began on the 8th of November and will conclude today, on the 10th of November". It is called "Guru Nanak Jayanti or Gurpurab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated). Kartar told Khalid that "thousands of people come to Nankana Sahib from all over the world" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 182, folio not stated).

Khalid describes the festival of 2011. There was "a heavy presence of police officials at all the entrances to the city. No car is permitted inside" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 220, folio not stated), and devotees walked barefoot (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 221–222, folio not stated). Pilgrims from Delhi, Gujarat and Amritsar came by train, crossed at Wagah and stayed three days at Nankana; representatives of the Evacuee Trust Property Board and the PSGPC received them at the border, where a *langar* was arranged (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 222–223, folio not stated). Three days of *Akhand Path* ended in the *bhog*, followed by the *Nagar Kirtan*; richer pilgrims from Britain, America and Canada came by chartered plane and hired buses, and travel-company posters promised them "all" the gurdwaras in Pakistan (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 223, folio not stated). "The entire city looks like a mini-India during these few days" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 226, folio not stated). "Things in Nankana Sahib will soon calm down. Atleast till the next year…." (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 232, folio not stated)

In *Walking with Nanak*, Jeeta Singh of Manora tells Khalid that he holds a small festival "every November when the festival of Guru Nanak is celebrated at Nankana Sahib" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 228, folio 202). Khalid also writes that "There is a picture of Baba Farid accompanying the picture of Guru Nanak at the shrine of Guru Nanak at Nankana Sahib", without saying which gurdwara he means (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).

Bhandari describes the worship of Baba Nanak's followers in his own day: "خلاصه عبادت اینطایفه مطالعه اشعار مرشد خویش است که بسرود و نغمه میگویند", the sum of their worship being their master's verses, which they sing (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 519). They count service to the traveller, "درخدمت صادر و وارد", as the highest worship, and whoever arrives at midnight speaking Baba Nanak's name they treat as a brother and a friend and serve, even a stranger, a thief, a highwayman or an evildoer (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 519). "It is believed that the tradition of Langar began with Guru Nanak", Khalid writes (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 188, folio not stated).

## The Massacre of 1921 and After

The shrines of Nankana Sahib were caught up in the great struggle of the early twentieth century to reform the management of the gurdwaras, which had passed into the hands of hereditary custodians known as *mahants*. In February 1921 a peaceful body of reformers was massacred at Gurdwara Janam Asthan in one of the defining episodes of the Gurdwara Reform Movement. In the aftermath, tradition records, the mahants of Bal Lila Sahib chose to hand over the shrine voluntarily to the Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee, the body that thereafter managed the historic gurdwaras, in exchange for a maintenance allowance for their families.

The books consulted say nothing of the events of 1921 or of the mahants of Bal Lila. Writing of the Punjab as a whole, Khalid says that "As a result of sectarian divisions between the Sikh communities and the takeover of Punjab by the British, a large number of important Sikh Gurdwaras were being administered by Hindu mahants" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 189, folio not stated).

Since the Partition of 1947 the gurdwara, like its neighbours at Nankana Sahib, has stood in a land where few Sikhs remain, cared for by the Evacuee Trust Property Board and thrown open to the pilgrims who cross the border in their thousands each year — above all for the birth anniversary of Guru Nanak, when Nankana Sahib fills with song and the shrines of the Guru's childhood come alive again.

Khalid tells how the gurdwaras of the town were looked after again. Giani Pratap, "After looking at the dilapidated condition of the shrines here he decided to stay and look after these holy buildings. That was the first time someone had taken the initiative to take care of the gurdwaras in Nankana Sahib after Partition" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 90, folio 72). Kartar, in *A White Trail*, remembered: "Ours was one of the first families to move to Nankana Sahib ... There was no Sikh left here after partition. Our Gurdwaras became barren and were in a bad state"; Giani Pratap had come with an Indian pilgrimage party "sometime in the sixties" and stayed "to look after the Gurdwara" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 184, folio not stated).

Before the Pakistani committee existed, Khalid writes, the SGPC, "based in Amritsar", managed the gurdwaras of Pakistan through the Indian High Commission in Islamabad, whose staff received pilgrims at the border and arranged their stay "inside the Gurdwaras" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 215–216, folio not stated). At the Guru's birthday in November 1985, "a year after Operation Blue Star", an American Sikh spoke for a separate Sikh state, and during the speech the gate was closed and two officials, Counsellor B. Jain and First Secretary K. K. Khanna, were attacked, at a Nankana gurdwara the book does not name (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 215, folio not stated; p. 216, folio not stated). "The religious festivals at Nankana and other cities of Pakistan have gone back to being religious festivals" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 216, folio not stated).

A Sikh community has returned to the town, mostly from the north-west. Khalid counts "the Sikh community in Nankana Sahib, which amounts to about two hundred-odd households" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 87, folio 69). In *A White Trail* he writes that "these are not indigenous people from the city, but recent migrants from Khyber-Pakhtunkhwa (KPK) where there is a considerable Sikh population" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 235, folio not stated). "There were only about thirty to forty Sikh houses in Nankana ten years ago", families arriving "following the alienation after the wars of 1965 and 1971 as well as the demolition of Babri Mosque in 1992" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 174–175, folio not stated); "In about ten years, the population in Nankana Sahib increased to about two hundred houses" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 175, folio not stated). Kartar: "Just a few years ago, there were only about fifty Sikh houses, but now there are almost two hundred to two hundred and fifty Sikh homes" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 193, folio not stated). "Most of the newcomers were incorporated within the Gurdwaras, in rooms which were generally reserved for pilgrims" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 176, folio not stated).

Khalid writes: "Several people whom I interviewed at Nankana Sahib felt that PSGPC was not representative of the Sikh community in Pakistan" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 36, folio 18). Of the town's Muslims, Iqbal Qaiser says that "The majority of the Muslims in Nankana Sahib are" those "who migrated from the other side of the border following Partition", their attitudes "shaped by those experiences" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 76, folio 58).

## In Verse

The books carry no verse about the gurdwara. Khalid quotes the Guru's own verse throughout *Walking with Nanak*, in published English translations; the lines below are the poetry of the Guru the shrine remembers, not lines tied to Bal Lila.

The poem on the sacred thread, which in Khalid's reconstruction the boy Nanak refused at Talwandi:

*From the cotton of compassion, / Spin the thread of contentment* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 61–62, folios 43–44)

On the oneness of God:

*My Master is One / He is One, brother, the only One* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 60, folio 42)

The *aarti*, which Iqbal Qaiser reads as placing God "in nature" and which leads him to call Nanak "a naturalist", begins in one translation:

*The sky the salver, the sun and moon the lamps …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 55, folio 37)

and in another:

*In the salver of the firmament, / The sun and the moon shine as lamps* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 243–244, folios 217–218)

*God is One, / The Path is One* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 144, folio 126)

On washing:

*As hands or feet besmirched with slime, / Water washes white* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 192, folio 166)

The song Nanak sings, in Khalid's reconstruction, to innkeepers who meant to rob him and Mardana:

*The herons in their white feathers dwell in the sacred shrines of pilgrimage* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 123–126, folios 105–108)

From the *Babur Bani*, "the first poem of Nanak that I read", Khalid quotes the line that dates the invaders' coming:

*Coming in seventy-eight (1521 C.E)* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 113–114, folios 95–96)

Other openings Khalid quotes: *Launch ye the boat of austere contemplation …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 148–149, folios 130–131); *As a beggar goes a-begging …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 158, folio 140); and *His goodness cannot be priced or traded …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 280, folio 254).

Bhandari says that the Guru composed "اشعار هندی" on the truth of God, setting out divine unity "بعبارات واضحه و استعارات لایحه" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520), and that "مردانه نام مطرب", a singer named Mardana, sang his verses and drew people "در دام عقیدت" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). Shemeem Burney Abbas's glossary defines the *banī* by him: "Devotional verses such as those of Guru Nanak; verses of supplication, entreaty, humility, and reverence" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 179, folio not stated). The verse is sung beyond the gurdwaras. Abbas quotes the singer Abida Parvin: "I sing Shah Hussain, Bulle Shah, Mian Muhammad Bakhsh, Hazrat Khawaja Ghulam Farid, Sacchal Sarmast, Baba Farid, and Guru Nanak" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 54, folio not stated). In Sindh, Michel Boivin records, "Sain Vasan Shah became famous for reciting the banis of Shri Guru Nanak and the kalams of the Sufis" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 68, folio 55).

## Setting: Talwandi and Nankana Sahib

What follows is about the town, not the gurdwara. Dhillon's retelling opens with the village's history: "the village of Talwandi, which lay between the rivers Ravi and Chenab, directly in the path of the armies that had set their eyes on Delhi"; "Thirteen times was the village of Talwandi destroyed and thirteen times - like the fabled phoenix - it rose again from its ashes". Its last rebirth came under Rai Bhoe, a Bhatti Rajput who had converted to Islam (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 15–16, folios 17–18). Rai Bhoe's son was Rai Bular; Shiv Ram, head of a Bedi family, was accountant to Rai Bhoe, and was succeeded by his son Kalyan Chand Das Bedi (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 17, folio 19).

Iqbal Qaiser showed Khalid the mound where the older settlement stood: it "is an archaeological mound. The settlement there was destroyed after which Rai Bhoi di Talwindi was constructed within a boundary wall"; asked "So Nanak was born in the new Nankana?", he answered "Yes" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 91, folio 73). The mound "now rests" behind Gurdwara Janamsthan, "away from the hub of the new settlement" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 91, folio 73): a steep climb "with no proper path", "at least twenty metres from the ground", "covered with small shrubs of karir (capparis deciduas)", with "shards of broken pottery and other mud utensils" underfoot (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 92, folio 74).

"There are a total of six gurdwaras associated with Guru Nanak at Nankana Sahib", Khalid writes in *Walking with Nanak* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 90, folio 72). In *A White Trail* he writes: "There are seven Gurdwaras in Nankana Sahib, most of which are associated with incidents from the life of Baba Guru Nanak" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 192, folio not stated). He calls Nankana "a city considered to be one of the holiest cities in the world for the Sikhs, as the founder of their religion, Nanak, was born here" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 183, folio not stated). From a rooftop he saw mosque minarets and "small white domes of the Gurdwaras", and "remains of a few Hindu temples; cone like structures rising from houses abandoned at the time of partition" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 225, folio not stated).

The modern town is a district seat. "There is one main road that runs through the entire city"; "The entire city needs a major uplift. The buildings are old and the road has pot holes"; "Most of the buildings are from the late British or the early Pakistan era" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 186, folio not stated). "In 2005, Nankana Sahib was separated from Sheikhupura and made into an independent district, with this city becoming the district headquarters" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 226, folio not stated). Khalid gives it "a population of over a million" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 176, folio not stated). "Guru Nanak is celebrated all over Nankana Sahib. There is a Guru Nanak High School, a Guru Nanak grocery store and a Guru Nanak cloth shop, all run by Sikhs. The Muslims generally refrain from eulogizing Guru Nanak in this particular way" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 172, folio not stated).

Of the Punjab in the century of the Guru's birth the books speak differently. Rizvi writes: "In the fifteenth century, the Panjab enjoyed a peaceful period due to Afghan rule. The Mongol invasions between the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries had devastated portions of the Panjab and the Afghans had successfully contributed to its re-settlement and urbanization by founding many new towns and promoting trade and commerce" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 408, folio 384). Dhillon writes: "For over three hundred years, Islam and Hinduism existed side by side in the Punjab region in a state of constant conflict" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 16, folio 18). Bhandari records that in the time of Sultan Bahlul Lodi (Sikandar Lodi in a variant the editor gives in a footnote), when Tatar Khan was governor of Lahore, Rai Ram Deo Bhatti took the whole of the Punjab in farm from him, "پنج[OCR?] لک تنکه تمام پنجاب را از تاتارخان اجاره گرفت", the sum being uncertain in the printed text (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 522–523).

## Guru Nanak in the Wider Record

What the books say of Guru Nanak himself is said of the Guru, not of this site; it is gathered here because the shrine exists for his sake. Khalid gives his dates as 1469–1539 (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 314, folio 288). Dhillon calls the Janamsakhis "cycles of tales purportedly drawn from the life of the first Guru of the Sikhs", and gives the date of the Guru's death as 22 September 1539 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 9, folio 9; p. 259, folio 287). Bhandari reports, with a "گویند", a birth "درسنه هزار و پانصد و بست و شش بکرماجیت مطابق هشتصد و هشتاد هجری درزمان سلطان بهلول لودی", in Samvat 1526, 880 Hijri, in the time of Sultan Bahlul Lodi, and a death "درعهد سلطنت سلیم شاه افغان درعمر مابین هفتاد و هشتاد سالگی", under Salim Shah Afghan, between the ages of seventy and eighty (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). Khalid writes that Nanak "spent a major portion of his life in present-day Pakistan. He was born here", and "eventually settled in a small village which is now on the Pakistani side of the border" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 16, folio not stated). Meherban, son of Prithi Chand, "claims that Guru Nanak was born in Dera Chahal and not Rai Bhoi di Talwindi", while other Janamsakhi writers and Sikh scholars "are unanimous that Nanak was born in Rai Bhoi di Talwindi" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 43, folio 25).

Dhillon sets out two schemes of the *udasis*, the Guru's journeys. "According to the Puratan Janamsakhi… he undertook five journeys", the last in 1523–1524 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 54–55, folios 60–61); "More modern accounts" give four, spanning twenty-three years (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 55, folio 61). Dhillon himself holds that there were four (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 56, folio 62). Khalid gives the travels as about twenty-four years, mostly on foot (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 54, folio 36). "Whenever he would visit an area he would visit its religious shrines, Muslim, Hindu and of any other denomination" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 37, folio 19).

Bhandari calls him "قافله سالار مسالک حقیقت و مشعله دار طریق طریقت", the caravan-leader of the paths of truth and the torch-bearer of the mystic way, and writes that "عالم عالم خلایق از اطراف ممالک آمده مرید شدند", that people came from every land to become his disciples (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). He had a son, "لکهمیداس نام خلف رشید", but passed him over, and at the hour of death made Lehna his successor with the title Guru Angad (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). Khalid writes that "On his deathbed, he appointed not his son but his most loyal student as his spiritual successor" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 16, folio not stated).

Nanak's advice to his companions, as Khalid gives it, was that "if one is a Muslim then one should be a good Muslim and if one is a Hindu then one should be a good Hindu" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 95, folio 77). "Nanak himself had been born into a Hindu household and was then educated in a Muslim milieu; however, he was neither a practising Muslim nor a Hindu" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 95, folio 77). Dhillon has him say, after vanishing for a week in the stream at Sultanpur, "There is only one God. There is no Hindu, no Mussalman." (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 45–46, folios 50–51). Dhillon calls the Guru "a householder in every sense of the word", who "brought a message of peace and harmony to believers of the mutually hostile religions of Islam and Hinduism" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 12, folio not stated).

Several writers set the miracle stories against what they take to be the Guru's own view. Dhillon writes of the Guru's "emphatic disavowal of the miraculous" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 11, folio 11). Khalid writes that Nanak "abhorred the concept of associating miracles with religious personalities", yet "his biography is nothing but a story of his miracles" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 16, folio not stated), and that Nanak's genius, in his view, lay in the poetic use of words and not in miracles (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 199, folio 173). Khalid says plainly that the Nanak narrative "part of the book is fictionalised" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 17, folio not stated), and describes the Janamsakhis as "Sikh texts on Nanak's life, written by his devotees after his death", "the primary sources", in "different versions" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 17, folio not stated).

Muslim writers, singers and scholars have claimed the Guru too. Khalid writes that "many Muslims, acquainted with the teachings of Guru Nanak, are devoted to him too and visit his shrine" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 220, folio not stated), and his informant Nadeem, a Muslim painter, says "Guru Nanak is like a Muslim Sufi Saint" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 219, folio not stated). Baba Farid "became an inspiration for Guru Nanak when he visited the shrine during his travels" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated), and Abbas writes that Farid's "texts are revered among the Sikhs because he influenced the founder of their own religion, Baba Guru Nanak" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 87, folio not stated). Rizvi argues, against W. H. McLeod, that "all literate Panjabis, both Hindus and Muslims, at the time of Guru Nanak were conversant with" ʿAttar, Rumi and Saʿdi (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 420, folio 396). Robert Rozehnal reports that a twentieth-century Chishti Sabiri shaykh, in certain lectures, describes "non-Muslim spiritual luminaries—Krishna, Rama, Sita, Lachman, and Guru Nanak—as believers in the one true God and religious leaders with 'Divine connection' (nisbat)" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 63, folio 53). Omar Kasmani, citing Boivin, notes that the trope of the flower in milk is told also of "Guru Nanak, the spiritual leader and founder of Sikhism" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 195, folio 178, ch. 5 n. 17). Boivin observes "a growing similarity between the iconography of Guru Nanak and Jhulelal" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 54, folio 41). At the shrine of the goddess Hinglaj, Jürgen Schaflechner records "the engraved footsteps of Guru Nanak" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 256, folio not stated).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **Where the gurdwara stands.** The site's account sets it some three hundred metres south-east of Gurdwara Janam Asthan, and later calls the Janam Asthan "a few steps away". Khalid's *Walking with Nanak* places it "A few feet away from Gurdwara Patti Sahib" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 90, folio 72). His *A White Trail* places it "near Gurdwara Janamasthan" and "across the road from Gurdwara Patti Sahib" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 177, folio not stated).
- **The children's games.** *Walking with Nanak* says that sports competitions for children are held at the gurdwara "Every year on the occasion of Lohri" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 91, folio 73). In *A White Trail* Surjeet says a small event was held there "Last year" and none would be held that year, because of the renovation (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 177, folio not stated); Kartar says Lohri at Nankana has "only begun in the last couple of years" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 193, folio not stated).
- **How many gurdwaras Nankana Sahib has.** Six associated with Guru Nanak (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 90, folio 72); seven, most of them associated with his life (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 192, folio not stated).
- **The Guru's birth.** The site's account gives 1469. Khalid gives October 20, 1469 (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 314, folio 288). Dhillon gives 14 April 1469 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 17–18, folios 19–20). Bhandari, with a "they say", gives Samvat 1526, 880 Hijri, in the time of Bahlul Lodi (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).
- **The Guru's death.** The site's account and Khalid give 1539 (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 314, folio 288); Dhillon gives 22 September 1539 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 259, folio 287). Bhandari places it under Salim Shah Afghan, at an age between seventy and eighty (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).
- **Where he was born.** The site's account and all the books name Talwandi. Khalid reports that Meherban "claims that Guru Nanak was born in Dera Chahal", while other Janamsakhi writers and Sikh scholars are unanimous for Talwandi (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 43, folio 25).
- **How the birth was granted.** Khalid, citing Dhillon's *The First Sikh Spiritual Master*, writes that Mata Tripta prayed for a child at an ancient Hindu temple on a mound near Talwandi (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 30–31, folio 13). The Naulakha Hazari shrine booklet says Nanak was born after that saint prayed for his mother (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 31, folio 13); Iqbal Qaiser gives this as the Muslim tradition, which "The Sikhs don't accept" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).
- **Who held Talwandi.** The site's account says the village was held by Rai Bular Bhatti, and Khalid calls Rai Bular "the ruler of Talwindi" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 22, folio 4). Dhillon makes Rai Bhoe, Rai Bular's father, the man under whom Talwandi was last rebuilt (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 15–16, folios 17–18; p. 17, folio 19), and has Nanak spend time daily with Rai Bhoe on his return in 1506 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 123, folio 137), while Mehta Kalu is accountant of the Bhatti family under Rai Bular (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 17–18, folios 19–20).
- **The boy's Muslim teacher.** Dhillon names him "the dervish Sayyad Hussain" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 24, folio 28); Khalid names him "Sayyed Hassan, a Muslim Sufi, who lived near his house" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 62, folio 44).
- **The family of the Guru's wife.** Dhillon makes Sulakhini the daughter of Mul Chand and Chando Rani "of the town of Batala" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 44, folio 49). Khalid makes Sulakhni the daughter of Moolchand Khatri "from the village of Pakhoke near Gurdaspur" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 67, folio 49). Bhandari says the Guru married after he came to Batala (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).
- **The Punjab of the fifteenth century.** Rizvi writes that "the Panjab enjoyed a peaceful period due to Afghan rule" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 408, folio 384). Dhillon writes that Islam and Hinduism had existed side by side there for over three hundred years "in a state of constant conflict" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 16, folio 18).
- **How many journeys.** Dhillon reports five *udasis* in the Puratan Janamsakhi and four in more modern accounts, spanning twenty-three years, and holds to four himself (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 54–55, folios 60–61; p. 55, folio 61; p. 56, folio 62). Khalid gives the travels as about twenty-four years (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 54, folio 36).
- **The size of the Sikh community at Nankana.** "about two hundred-odd households" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 87, folio 69); thirty to forty houses ten years before, then about two hundred (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 174–175, folio not stated; p. 175, folio not stated); Kartar's fifty houses a few years before, then two hundred to two hundred and fifty (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 193, folio not stated).
- **When and how large the birthday festival is.** The site's account names the birth anniversary as the great pilgrimage. *A White Trail* gives official estimates of ten to fifteen thousand people every year, "during the last days of November", against media reports of three thousand in 2011, when the festival ran from 8 to 10 November (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated).
- **When Ranjit Singh's rule began.** The site's account places his renovation of the shrine in the early nineteenth century. *Walking with Nanak* has him become "the sovereign of Punjab at the end of the eighteenth century" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 34, folio 16), and elsewhere calls him the ruler of Punjab "in the nineteenth century" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 87, folio 69). *A White Trail* gives him the dates 1780–1839 and has him "crowned as the Ruler of Punjab" in 1801 (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 42, folio not stated; p. 207, folio not stated).

## Legacy

Bal Lila Sahib endures as a tender counterpoint to the grander shrines of Sikhism. Where others mark the Guru's teachings, his travels, or the sacrifices of the martyrs, this quiet field and its tank commemorate simply that the founder of the faith was once a child at play upon this ground, and that even then, in the telling of the Panth, the light within him was already shining. For the pilgrim who has bowed at the Janam Asthan a few steps away, a visit to Bal Lila Sahib completes the intimate geography of Guru Nanak's beginnings.

In Khalid's books the field is still a place for children's games. He records children's sports at Lohri within the gurdwara (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 91, folio 73), and Surjeet tells of races and tug of war there for the younger children (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 177, folio not stated). Writing of Pakistan as a whole, he says that "there are several sites all over the country where Nanak stayed or performed miracles. Gurdwaras that were built there later by the devotees still stand tall" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 168–169, folio not stated).
