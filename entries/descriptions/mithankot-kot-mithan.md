## Overview

At Kot Mithan (Mithankot), in the Rajanpur district on the edge of the Cholistan desert, lies the shrine of Khwaja Ghulam Farid (1845–1901) — the supreme poet of the Seraiki language and one of the last great masters of the classical Sufi *kafi*. A Chishti saint of the Kot Mithan line, Ghulam Farid poured the mysticism of *wahdat al-wujud* into verses of extraordinary tenderness, sung in the sweet Seraiki of the southern Punjab and steeped in the landscape of the Rohi, the great desert he loved. His shrine is the spiritual centre of the Seraiki belt, and his kafis are sung across it to this day.

The books bear out the poet more fully than the place. Carl Ernst and Bruce Lawrence call him "another great nineteenth-century Chishti" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 146, folio 134), and Robert Rozehnal "the nineteenth-century Chishti master Ghulam Farid" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 223, folio 215). Annemarie Schimmel describes Khwaja Farid as "a popular Siraiki poet of the late nineteenth century" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 286, folio 268); Michel Boivin as "a Siraiki-speaking author" whose verse is quoted in the devotions of Sindhis as well (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 91, folio 78); and Shemeem Burney Abbas follows his *kafis* into the concert repertoire of Abida Parvin (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 136, folio not stated). His discourses survive as a Persian *malfuzat*, compiled between 1893 and 1901 and translated into Urdu in 1979 (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 240, folio 230). Of the shrine itself, the books consulted say very little: one names him "of Kot Mithan Sharif" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 392), and another knows Kot Mithan a century before him as a town of learning (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 123, folio 111).

## The Life of the Poet-Saint

Ghulam Farid was born in 1845 at Chachran, near Kot Mithan, into a Sayyid Koreja family of the Chishti Sufi tradition; his father and, after him, his elder brother were the spiritual guides (*sajjada-nashins*) of their line, and on his brother’s death Ghulam Farid succeeded to the seat. Learned in Arabic and Persian and in the religious sciences, he was nonetheless a poet and mystic to the core, and he is famous for the years he spent in the wilderness of the Rohi (Cholistan), whose dunes, herders, and camel-caravans fill his poetry with an unmistakable local beauty. He was a contemporary and, in matters of the spirit, a critic of the Nawabs of Bahawalpur — remembered for counselling the ruler in the interest of the people. He died in 1901 at Chachran and was buried at Kot Mithan.

The scholarly books give his dates more than one way. Rozehnal prints "(1844–1901)" in his text (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 223, folio 215) and "(d. 1901)" in his bibliography (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 263, folio not stated). Ernst and Lawrence head their bibliography entry 'Ghulam Farid, Khwaja (d. 1319/1901)' (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 240, folio 230), but in their text write "Ghulam Farid (d. 1911)" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 146, folio 134). The index of Schimmel's *As Through a Veil* gives him "(d. 1901)" as well (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 353, folio 335). Both Ernst and Lawrence and Rozehnal call him a Chishti; none of these books names his pir, his birthplace, his place of death or his tomb.

He also took disciples. In the life of Khwaja Hasan Nizami, Ernst and Lawrence record that "at sixteen his brother made him a murid of Khwaja Ghulam Farid" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 127, folio 114).

The books spell his name in several ways. Ernst and Lawrence write Khwaja Ghulam Farid (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 127, folio 114), Ghulam Farid (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 146, folio 134), Khwaja Farid (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 225, folio 214) and Khawaja Farid (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 230, folio 219); Abbas writes Khawaja Ghulam Farid and Khawaja Farid (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 136, folio not stated; p. 149, folio not stated); Boivin and Rafat's selection of Bulleh Shah give the bare 'Ghulam Farid', with no honorific, no date and no place (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 171, folio 158; Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 236, folio 216). He should not be confused with a namesake in Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori's tazkira of the Punjab saints, Maulvi Ghulam Farid Lahori, who died in 1216 AH and is buried in the Miani graveyard at Lahore, a century earlier and in another city (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 222, folio 210).

## The Poetry

Khwaja Ghulam Farid’s fame rests on his *Diwan* of Seraiki *kafis* — hundreds of short mystical lyrics, cast (like those of Shah Hussain and Bulleh Shah before him) largely in the longing voice of a woman for her absent beloved, in which the Beloved is God and the desert of Rohi the very landscape of the soul. His verse unites the boldest metaphysics of the unity of being with the sweetest folk-idiom of the southern Punjab, and its refrains are known by heart across the Seraiki country. Most famous of all is his declaration that the Beloved is everything, the only reality:

میڈا عشق وی توں، میڈا یار وی توں

*You are my love, and You are my beloved; You alone are all.*

His kafis are sung to this day by the folk-singers and qawwals of the Seraiki belt, and he is honoured as the poet who gave that language its highest literary and spiritual expression.

Abbas, who studies the *kafis* as women sing them, sets the same woman's voice at the centre of his work: "The kāfis that are investigated here are the creation of the Siraiki Sufi poet Khawaja Ghulam Farid. The female narrator in the kāfīs mourns her separation from her beloved, who has gone to a distant land" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 148, folio not stated). That voice is borrowed from the romance of the Punjab: "Khawaja Farid additionally uses Hir's voice for that ultimate spiritual union or iśq-e haqīqī through the imagery of iśq-e majāzī (earthly love)" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 149, folio not stated). Schimmel, writing of the mystical poetry of the region at large and not of Ghulam Farid, observes that "In the province of Sind, as well as in Punjab, a special aspect of mystical appreciation of women can be observed that is rarely encountered in other parts of the Muslim world, i.e., the representation of the longing soul in the form of a woman", and that "The tragic heroines of the Sindhi and Panjabi folk tales—like Hir, Sassui, Sohni, and many others—represent the human soul in search of the beloved" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 465, folio 434).

One of his lyrics, as Abbas heard it sung, takes up the lovers Layla and Majnun. Abida Parvin sang a song of a black Layla at her Open University concert and "attributes the poetry to the Sufi poet Khawaja Ghulam Farid, who wrote in Siraiki" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 136, folio not stated). Abbas gives the burden of the poem in prose: "When folks asked Majnun about his black Layla, he retorted with an example from the Quran. He questioned them: If the pages from the Quran can be white but the ink be black, then what is so wrong for him to love a woman of color" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 137, folio not stated). "In fact, it is the poet Ghulam Farid who asks this question on behalf of Qays or Majnun" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 137, folio not stated).

The poet names himself in the lyric. Abbas prints his signature in the Siraiki twice, in roman letters and without a translation: *Vo la- akhe Ghulām Farīd-ā* (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 136, folio not stated), and the fuller line *Ākhe Ghulām Farīd-ā O jeh nāl dil aṛ pave O gorī hove yā kālī* (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 137, folio not stated).

Schimmel singles out a further theme of his work. In a note to *As Through a Veil* she directs the reader, "For the combination of exercise and interior pilgrimage in the work of Khwaja Farid, a popular Siraiki poet of the late nineteenth century", to Christopher Shackle's essay "The Pilgrimage and the Extension of Sacred Geography in the Poetry of Khwaja Ghulam Farid." (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, pp. 286–287, folios 268–269), which appeared in a volume edited by Attar Singh, *Socio-cultural Impact of Islam on India*, at Chandigarh in 1976 (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 339, folio 321). Pilgrimage and the sacred geography of the poems are named there as a subject; the book does not describe them further.

Of the *kafi* as a form, Naveed Alam, the translator of Shah Hussain, writes in general terms that "Kafis, the stanzaic poems couched in folkloric themes and techniques, were easy to understand and memorize, and thus served as the ideal means to disseminate the mystical teachings among the illiterate masses dependent on the oral transmission of knowledge. Even to this day, most Punjabis and Sindhis are more likely to have heard a kafi than read one" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 15, folio 15).

## The Discourses

Beside the poetry stands a book of his conversations. In their Persian bibliography Ernst and Lawrence list 'Isharat-i Faridi or Maqabis al-majalis', with references to Monzavi and one manuscript; they add that it was translated into Urdu by Wahid Bakhsh Siyal (Lahore: Islamic Book Foundation, 1399/1979), and that the discourses were compiled between 1893 and 1901 (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 240, folio 230). Rozehnal lists the same work under 'Farid, Khwaja Ghulam (d. 1901)', compiled 1893–1901 and translated from the Persian by Wahid Bakhsh Sial Rabbani (Lahore: al-Faisal, 1979/1993) (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 263, folio not stated), and renders its title in English: "the shaykh's lengthy introduction to the discourses (malfuzat) of the nineteenth-century Chishti master Ghulam Farid (1844–1901) titled Maqabis al-majalis [The Lessons of the Spiritual Assemblies]" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 223, folio 215).

The translator was a twentieth-century shaykh, and his introduction became a work in its own right. Ernst and Lawrence write: "In the lengthy Urdu introduction to his translation of the discourses (malfuzat) of another great nineteenth-century Chishti, Ghulam Farid (d. 1911), Capt. Wahid Bakhsh devotes more than a third of 225 pages to the exposition and defense of" *samaʿ* (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 146, folio 134). They draw on that introduction three times in their notes, once where it cites Ashraf 'Ali's *Khumkhana-i batin* (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 217, folio 206). Rozehnal, too, points the reader to "Wahid Bakhsh's introduction to Khwaja Ghulam Farid (compiled 1893–1901), Maqabis al-majalis", and names a chapter of the same work, 'The Taste of Sama'' (*Zauq-i sama'*), though that note gives the author's surname as 'Faridi' (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 261, folio 254).

An English rendering of the discourses also exists: Ernst and Lawrence list Christopher Shackle's *The Teachings of Khwaja Farid* (Multan, 1978) among the translations of Chishti *malfuzat* (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 225, folio 214).

## The Shrine

Khwaja Ghulam Farid’s shrine at Kot Mithan is the chief centre of pilgrimage of the Seraiki region, kept by his descendants and drawing devotees throughout the year for prayer, blessing, and the singing of his verse. The annual urs gathers great crowds for days of kafi-singing, qawwali, and *langar*, and the town of Kot Mithan is known above all as the resting place of the poet. His memory is inseparable from the cultural identity of the Seraiki people.

The books consulted add nothing about the tomb itself. Of all of them, only one ties the poet's name to the town, and that in a publisher's list printed at the end of Zahur ul-Hasan Sharib's tazkira, which advertises *Kalam-e Khwaja Ghulam Farid* by Khwaja Ghulam Farid of Kot Mithan Sharif (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 392). Ernst and Lawrence, Rozehnal, Schimmel, Boivin and Abbas name no tomb for him and set him neither at Mithankot nor in Rajanpur; none records the date of the building, its builder, the *ʿurs* or its custodians, which rest on the site's own account alone.

## Devotion and Practice

His verse is sung far beyond the town. In Abbas's account of women's performance of Sufi poetry, Abida Parvin's repertoire includes him (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 54, folio not stated), and he is among the spirits invoked at the outset of performances (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 99, folio not stated). Abida Parvin's improvised performance at Islamabad drew "references from Shah Abdul Latif's poetry and Khawaja Ghulam Farid's texts" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 151, folio not stated), and he holds two separate items, both in Siraiki, on the programme of that concert (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 101, folio not stated).

Abbas records, too, how a singer of his verse spoke of her gift: "Surraiya Multanikar, a Siraiki singer of Sufi poetry, in particular that of Khawaja Ghulam Farid, also confirmed in her interview with me that she is specially blessed by her pir, a Gilani Syed. She said that, because of his prayer, there is much sūr (melody) in her voice" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 199, folio not stated). His name stands in the title of a recording, *A Tribute to Great Mystics: … Khawaja Farid* (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 205, 212, 216, folios not stated). Elsewhere Abbas writes that "The singer invoked the Sufi poet Farid to create the mystical frame", with the refrain 'Yā-ā-r Farīd' (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 118, folio not stated); she does not say which Farid is meant, the setting is Siraiki, and she names Baba Farid of Pakpattan a few pages later, so the invocation may belong to either poet.

The qawwals sing him as well. Rozehnal names him, in the same clause as Mehr 'Ali Shah, among the poets of the qawwals' repertoire (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 229, folio 221).

His verse also crosses into the devotions of another community. Boivin, describing the Sindhi Hindu Sufis, writes that "Sometimes, Sufi poetry authored by non-Sindhis also played a liturgical role during the performances", and that "One can quote Bulhe Shah or Ghulam Farid, the latter being a Siraiki-speaking author, this being a language in which many Sindhis also composed poetry." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 91, folio 78). Elsewhere he notes that "Non-Sindhi poets are also quoted, including Bullhe Shah, Shah Bahu and Ghulam Farid" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 171, folio 158).

## Setting: Kot Mithan and the Siraiki Country

Kot Mithan was a place of teaching a century before Ghulam Farid. Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori's *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, in its entry on Khwaja Sulaiman Taunsvi, writes that in his early days he attended Qazi Muhammad Aqil in the *qasba* of Kot Mithan and acquired the outward sciences there (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 123, folio 111). The book's modern editor identifies the teacher as Khwaja Muhammad Aqil, of the Faruqi family, whose father was Muhammad Sharif and whose family title was Koreja, and who died on 14 Rajab 1229 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 120, folio 108). The same editor gives Taunsvi's birth as 1184 AH and his death as 1267 AH, and writes that the Chishti Nizami Fakhri line spread most widely in the Punjab through Hazrat Khwaja Sulaiman Taunsvi (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 120, folio 108). The book's index sends Mithankot, Taunsa and Dera Ghazi Khan to the same page (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 336, folio 324). It says nothing of a shrine at Kot Mithan or of Ghulam Farid, and gives no detail that would set this Kot Mithan apart from any other place of the name.

Sharib's tazkira likewise places the *madrasa* of Qazi Muhammad Aqil at Kot Mithan and Khwaja Muhammad Sulaiman's studies there, again without a word of a shrine (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 363). Ernst and Lawrence, writing of the order in the southern Punjab and without naming Kot Mithan, name a Muhammad 'Aqil (d. 1814) and his disciple Gul Muhammad Ahmadpuri (d. 1827), "both of whom founded religious schools in the southern Punjab that provided free instruction and lodging to hundreds of students" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, pp. 121–122, folios 108–109); the books do not say whether this is the same man.

The town's name appears once more, in the title of an early nineteenth-century river journey: Rozehnal cites the "Journal of Captain C.M. Wade's Voyage from Lodiana to Mithankot by the River Satlaj on His Mission to Lahore and Bahawalpur in 1832–1833" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 241, folio 234).

Of the language of the poems, the books speak in general terms. Boivin writes that "The Siraiki language is mainly spoken in Southern Punjab, with Multan as capital. Interestingly, this area was a part of Sindh during the Middle Ages" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 216, folio 203), and defines Siraiki as "a language spoken in Northern Sindh and Southern Punjab" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 220, folio 207). Haroon Khalid, travelling south, writes that "The boundary between Sindh and Punjab becomes a blur as we drive through Bahawalpur", that "This is where the cultures of Punjab and Sindh merge", and that Siraiki is "an amalgamation of the two languages"; his companion Iqbal Qaiser adds: "The Siraiki spoken in Multan is closer to Punjabi, while here it is more like Sindhi" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 276, folio 250). Schimmel, comparing the poets of Sind and the Punjab, writes that Siraiki "forms the transition to Panjabi; thus, the imagery of both, or rather all three, linguistic areas is very closely related" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 154, folio 154), and Abbas that the Sohni legend "is sung in Sufi poetry in all the regions of the Sind, the Siraiki-speaking area of the Panjab" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 126–127, folios not stated).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The year of his birth.** The site's account gives 1845. Rozehnal gives 1844, in the dates "(1844–1901)" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 223, folio 215). No other book consulted gives a birth year.
- **The year of his death.** The site's account gives 1901, and so do Rozehnal (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 263, folio not stated) and the index of Schimmel's *As Through a Veil* (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 353, folio 335). Ernst and Lawrence print it two ways: 'd. 1319/1901' in their bibliography (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 240, folio 230) and "Ghulam Farid (d. 1911)" in their text (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 146, folio 134).
- **The language of his verse.** The site's account calls him the supreme poet of Seraiki. Schimmel, Boivin and Abbas call him a Siraiki poet (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 286, folio 268; Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 91, folio 78; Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 136, folio not stated), and Ernst and Lawrence list his *Fifty Poems* under Siraiki (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 230, folio 219). Rafat's selection reports Asaf Khan's view, which "relates the diction of all the great Punjabi poets to Central Punjabi but accepts variations of vocabulary in Shah Husain, Ghulam Farid, etc." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 236, folio 216), and Rozehnal names him as a Punjabi poet in the qawwals' repertoire (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 229, folio 221).
- **The Koreja name.** The site's account places Ghulam Farid in a Sayyid Koreja family. Lahori's editor gives Koreja as the family title of Khwaja Muhammad Aqil of Kot Mithan, whom he places in the Faruqi family (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 120, folio 108). The book does not connect Muhammad Aqil with Ghulam Farid.
- **The Urdu translation of his discourses.** Ernst and Lawrence give the translator as Wahid Bakhsh Siyal and the imprint as Lahore: Islamic Book Foundation, 1399/1979, and give the work a second title, *Isharat-i Faridi* (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 240, folio 230). Rozehnal gives the translator as Wahid Bakhsh Sial Rabbani and the imprint as Lahore: al-Faisal, 1979/1993 (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 263, folio not stated), and in one note cites the work under the surname 'Faridi' (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 261, folio 254).

## Legacy

Khwaja Ghulam Farid is to Seraiki what Bhittai is to Sindhi and Bulleh Shah to Punjabi — the poet who raised his mother-tongue to the summit of mystical expression and made of its desert landscape an image of the soul’s love for God. His kafis remain among the most cherished poetry of the southern Punjab, and his shrine at Kot Mithan endures as the spiritual and cultural heart of the Seraiki belt.

His work has been carried into print and into English largely from Multan. Ernst and Lawrence list two English translations by Christopher Shackle, *The Teachings of Khwaja Farid* (Multan, 1978) and *Fifty Poems of Khawaja Farid* (Multan, 1983) (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 225, folio 214; p. 230, folio 219). Abbas cites *Kalām-e Farid*, edited by Kefi Jampuri (Multan: Bazm-e Saqafat) (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 209, 222, folios not stated); *Naghmā-e Sehrā*, translated by Kashafi Multani (Multan: Bazm-e Saqafat) (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 222, folio not stated); Shackle's *Fifty Poems of Khawaja Farid* (Multan: Bazm-e Saqafat) (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 207, 232, folios not stated); and M. Abdul Wasi's *Divān-e Farid Ma Dhorājāt* (Multan: Qadimi Kutab Khana, n.d.) (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 235, folio not stated).

His discourses kept a living Chishti readership through their translator. Rozehnal, writing of Wahid Bakhsh's own mission, reports that the will of his master Shahidullah Faridi says Wahid Bakhsh "has initiated halqa zikr and guidance in Bahawalpur" and "should spread the silsila in this place only", and reads it so: "Here Shahidullah effectively delineates the southern Punjab as Wahid Bakhsh's personal spiritual domain (wilayat)" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 90, folio 80).
