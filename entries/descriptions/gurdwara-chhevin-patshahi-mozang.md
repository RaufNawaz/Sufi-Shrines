## Overview

On Temple Road in the Mozang locality of Lahore stands Gurdwara Chhevin Patshahi, one of several Lahore-area shrines commemorating a visit by Guru Hargobind, the sixth Sikh Guru — hence "Chhevin Patshahi," the "Sixth Sovereignty." The site is bound up in Sikh tradition with the memory of Mata Kaulan (also remembered as Bibi Kaulan), a devoted follower associated with the Guru whose story is preserved in the devotional literature of the Mozang gurdwara tradition.

Two older Urdu books on Lahore describe a Sikh building east of Mozang that was held to be Guru Hargobind's. Kanhaiya Lal's history of Lahore, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, places it "موضع مزنگ کے شرق کی طرف", east of the village of Mozang, on the road that goes to the jail, and describes its enclosure, its samadhs, its sanctuary and its two Granths (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 225, folio 228; p. 226, folio 229). Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti's *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* calls it "یہ تکیہ گورو ہرگوبند صاحب کی", this *takia* of Guru Hargobind Sahib, and describes the same kind of compound beside Mozang (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 279; p. 280). Neither book uses the name Chhevin Patshahi for the building, and neither gives a date for the Guru's coming, for the building or for any rebuilding. That his building is this gurdwara rests on where he places it, for the word of his heading that is read as "sixth" is uncertain in the printed text (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 225, folio 228).

The other books consulted speak of Guru Hargobind, of Bibi Kaulan, of Mozang or of Sikh Lahore, not of this gurdwara. What they say is gathered below as the life of the Guru, the story of Bibi Kaulan and the setting of the place, not as the history of this building.

## Guru Hargobind at Mozang

The event commemorated here is traditionally dated to 1619. Kanhaiya Lal reports the Sikhs' own account of the building: "سکھوں کا بیان ہے کہ اس مکان میں گرو ہر گوبند چھٹا گرو سکھوں کا اکثر سکونت رکھتا تھا اس سبب سے اس کو سکھ اسکا ادب کرتے ہین". It is the Sikhs' statement, he writes, that Guru Hargobind, the sixth Guru of the Sikhs, mostly resided in this building, and for that reason the Sikhs honour it (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 226, folio 229). He gives this as what the Sikhs say, not in his own voice, and he gives no date for the Guru's stay (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 226, folio 229).

Chishti calls the place the Guru's *takia* (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 279). In his list of the ten *patshahis*, the sixth is Guru Hargobind's (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 280). In his account of the Guru's life he comes to Lahore: "جب یہاں لاہور میں گورو ہر گوبند آئے تو بمقام مزنگ[OCR?]…", when Guru Hargobind came here to Lahore, then at Mozang (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 513). The rest of that sentence is badly damaged in the printed text, and the place name in it is itself uncertain. What survives speaks of something done "بمقام مزنگ[OCR?] [illegible] وغیرہ کیا", of a *Badshahi* that is well known, "[illegible] بادشاہی مشہور ہے", and of a place that "متبرک معبد سکھان بن گیا", became a blessed place of worship of the Sikhs (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 513).

Haroon Khalid writes in *A White Trail* that Guru Arjan spent a lot of time in Lahore, and that "His son, Guru Hargobind, also spent a lot of time here" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated). He names no place in the city where the Guru stayed.

## The Gurdwara

An original structure is recorded as having been raised under Maharaja Ranjit Singh in the early nineteenth century, and the building seen today was rebuilt in 1926 under the patronage of Sardar Mehar Singh.

Kanhaiya Lal's description of the building in his day begins with its place: "یہ مکان موضع مزنگ کے شرق کی طرف سر راہ اُس سڑک کے ہے جو جیلخانہ[OCR?] کو جاتی ہے", this building lies east of the village of Mozang, on the road that goes to the jail (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 225, folio 228). It is a very spacious enclosure of brick and lime. Inside it, on the west, stand "چار پانچ سمام[OCR?]", four or five samadhs, built in brick. Within that is a second enclosure holding a brick well with a Persian wheel, "چاہ پختہ چرخی وار", and a "سبیل پانی پلانی کی", a *sabil* for giving water. Beside it is a small garden. There is a three-storey house for the mahant who holds the seat, "مکان سہ منزلہ گدی نشتین[OCR?] مہنت کا"; there are two *kothas* and an elaborate lime-plastered *chaubara*, which is the sitting place; there is a marble staircase; and there is a plastered brick platform three feet high (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 225, folio 228).

Above the platform is an arched doorway with a railing: "اسی کو مندر بابا صاحب کہتے ہین", this is what they call the Mandir Baba Sahib. On its walls are pictures of Guru Nanak and other Gurus, "تصویر گرو نانک و نانکٹ[OCR?] وغیرہ گروؤن کی کلھی[OCR?] ہوئی ہین"; one of the names and the verb are uncertain in the printed text. The ceiling is vaulted, set with mirrors and painted (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 226, folio 229). In the eastern *dalan*, "دو گرنتھہ باداب تمام رکھے ہین اور گرنتھون کے اوپر ریشمی سائبان تنا ہوا ہی": two Granths are kept there with all due reverence, with a silken canopy stretched over them (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 226, folio 229).

Chishti's account of the compound runs close to Kanhaiya Lal's. The entrance is on the south, and the enclosure is of brick (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 279). He places it with the words "شرق رویہ موضع مزنگ اور مقبرہ حضرت شاہ ابو اسحاق صاحب", naming the east side, the village of Mozang and the tomb of Hazrat Shah Abu Ishaq Sahib (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 279). On the west are four "مرتبان یعنی سمادھیں[OCR?]", *martaban*, that is, samadhs. He names those commemorated in them as "بھامی[OCR?] صوبا سنگھ", with a clause, "جو باپ انکا گدی نشین تھا", that speaks of a father who was the holder of the seat; Sajjan Singh; and "دھیان[OCR?] سنگھ" and his wife (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 279). The first name and the last are uncertain in the printed text.

He goes on: a brick well; a *musafir-khana*, a travellers' lodge, with doors "لب سڑک قصور", on the side of the Kasur road; "ایک مکان ثلثہ[OCR?] مسرلہ[OCR?] واسطے سکونت یہاں کی گدی", a house for the residence of the seat here, the words that describe the house being uncertain; and a brick *chaubara* with a stone floor and marble (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 280). Of the *chaubara* he writes: "اس چوبارہ کا نام دربار ہے انمیں تمام تصویریں[OCR?] سی ہوئی ہیں", this *chaubara* is named the Darbar, and in it are pictures throughout; the word for the pictures is uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 280).

Both writers speak of the time of Maharaja Ranjit Singh. Kanhaiya Lal writes: "مہاراجہ رنجیت سنگھ کے وقت اس مکان کی بڑی عزت تھی", in Ranjit Singh's time this building had great honour (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 226, folio 229). Chishti's sentence on the Maharaja's time, "یہ مکان سا[OCR?]", breaks off in a damaged passage, and what he says of the building then cannot be read (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 280).

By Kanhaiya Lal's day the building's revenue went elsewhere: its income, he writes, now goes to the *pujari* of Guru Arjan's samadh (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 226, folio 229). In the same part of his book many of the shrines he describes are held by their officiant in "مالکانہ قبضہ", proprietary possession, in his own name (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 200, 201). Haroon Khalid writes of Guru Arjan's samadhi that Guru Hargobind raised "a small structure" at the spot (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 200, folio not stated). He says nothing of any tie between that samadhi and a building at Mozang.

## Devotion and Practice

In the pre-1947 period the gurdwara sustained daily congregational worship, and it is recorded as having observed an annual Akhand Path, the unbroken reading of the Guru Granth Sahib, around Guru Hargobind's birth anniversary (Gurpurb).

Kanhaiya Lal records a monthly gathering: "ہر ایک چاندنی پانچویں تاریخ یہاں سکھوں کا مجمع ہوتا ہے کہ پرشاد تقسیم کیا جاتا ہے چراغان بھی ہوتا ہے". On the fifth day of every moon a gathering of Sikhs takes place here, *prashad* is distributed, and there is *chiraghan*, an illumination with lamps (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 226, folio 229). The two Granths under their silken canopy stood in the eastern *dalan* (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 226, folio 229).

Chishti also records a gathering at the place, "میلا جمع[OCR?]", a fair that assembles; the rest of his sentence is illegible, and he gives neither its day nor its occasion (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 280).

## The Life of Guru Hargobind

The books consulted tell the Guru's life without mentioning this site.

**Birth.** Chishti tells that a holy man, whose name cannot be read, was visited by Guru Arjan's wife with gifts, foretold "ایک ایسا فرزند ارجمند", a noble son, and said "اسکا نام ہرگوبند رکہنا", name him Hargobind (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 507). The child was born to "مائی گنگا صاحبہ", Mai Ganga Sahiba, on the day Guru Arjan went to "موضع ڈوالی", the village of Dawali (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 507); Chishti's table of the Gurus gives the birthplace as "موضع وڈالی[OCR?]" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 515). He relates that Prithi Chand bribed a nurse to poison the infant, and that poisoned milk was given instead to what the printed text calls "کسی کہنے[OCR?]", which died at once; the passage is garbled (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 508). Khalid's back matter, its broken lines read together, gives the Guru's years as 1595 to 1644, and his Guruship as 1606 to 1644 (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 315, folio 289). Dhillon likewise has "Har Gobind (Guru from 1606 to 1644)" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 240, folio 265).

**Accession.** Khalid writes that "On the 24 July 1606", after Arjan had appointed him, he decided to give *darshan*. The Akal Takht, "translated as the Eternal Throne", had been prepared by then; he dressed "as a royal", in "churidar pyjama, a saffron gown", an aigrette and a necklace, with "two swords". He "refused to take up the religious symbols of Guruhood": "My rosary shall be the sword belt and on my turban, I shall wear the emblem of royalty". This was *miri-piri*: one sword stood for "temporal power", the other for "spiritual prowess" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 231, folio 205). Elsewhere Khalid calls him "the first Guru to wear two swords on his person, one of which represented temporal power while the other spiritual power" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 315, folio 289). Chishti, too, has him come to Amritsar and build "اکال تخت[OCR?]", and wear two swords, "ایک … میری[OCR?] کی سے اور دوسرے میسر[OCR?] کی"; the two words that name what each sword stood for are uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 510).

**After Guru Arjan's death.** In Chishti's account the Guru was at Amritsar when the news of his father's death came. He mourned, swore "قسم ہائی مغلظہ", the most solemn of oaths, to take revenge on Chandu Lal, and ordered that no Sikh should speak Chandu Lal's name; whenever he was mentioned a Sikh should first say "پھٹکار یعنی لعنت", a curse, three times (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 503). Khalid names Chandu Lal as the man "appointed as the Diwan of Lahore by the Mughal authorities", and records that Chandu Lal "asked for Hargobind's hand for his daughter in marriage" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 253, folio 227). *A White Trail* says that Guru Hargobind "reared horses and encouraged his followers to keep arms in their defence after his father's death" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 199, folio not stated).

**The soldier-saint.** Khalid writes that, "Known as the 'soldier-saint' Guru Har Gobind began the process of militarisation of the Sikh community following the assassination of his father" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 315, folio 289). A saffron flag faced the Akal Takht, "to eventually become Nishan cael [OCR?] [Nishan Sahib?] a symbol of the Khalsa"; there was a large drum and "a hawk as a pet". He kept "fifty-two bodyguards" and "Sant Sipahi or saint soldiers", "five hundred soldiers to begin with", who were "not paid a salary but… exempt from paying the required tribute". The Persian historian Mohsin Fani gives "seven hundred cavaliers and sixty artillery men" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 232, folio 206). Through his *masands* devotees were told to offer "arms and horses" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 233, folio 207), and "To administer the Sikh community, Guru Hargobind had created the concept of the Sangat, which was then put under a Masand" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 181, folio 155). The offering of weapons at the Guru's court "was a practice that was started by Guru Hargobind but then reinforced by Guru Gobind Singh" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 99, folio 81).

**Jahangir and Gwalior.** In Chishti's telling Chandu Lal told Jahangir that the Guru "سچا بادشاہ کہلاتا ہے", is called the True King, and had built a throne. The emperor sent men to bring him; "بہائی بڈا[OCR?] اور بہائی گورداس", Bhai Gurdas and a second Bhai whose name is uncertain in the printed text, stayed at Amritsar as his deputies. At Delhi Jahangir asked him "کہو جی ہندو اچہے ہین یا مسلمان", say, are Hindus good or Muslims, and the Guru answered with Guru Arjan's lines on the different names by which people call on God, "کوئی بولے رام رام کوئی بولو خدا …" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 510). In a further episode a Sikh who came asking for "سچا بادشاہ ہمارا", our True King, was sent on by Jahangir to the Guru (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 511). Chandu Lal then arranged that he be held "قلعہ گوالیار میں اکتالیس[OCR?] روز", forty-one days in Gwalior fort, and wrote to the *thanadar* there to keep him in hardship (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 511). Imprisoned rajas were there too; the king dreamt, and a voice told him to release the Guru; Hargobind asked that "ان راجوں کو بھی", these rajas too, be released, and the king agreed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 512). Chishti goes on to the Guru's claim to Arjan's rosary and his claim for *qisas*, retaliation, against Chandu Lal: Chandu was handed over to him in chains, and each Sikh was to give him "پانچ پانچ جوتی", five blows of a shoe (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 513).

Khalid writes that "Guru Hargobind had been incarcerated by Emperor Jahangir" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 97, folio 79), and that he was imprisoned "in Gwalior jail on the orders of the Emperor" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 235, folio 209). He gives two explanations side by side: that he "had retained the money that [was] given him by the Court to be disbursed to his troops", and, in the Sikh version, that the Emperor "felt threatened" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 235, folio 209). Some claims have the Guru later in Jahangir's "employment, serving him in the Punjab", helping him "to overcome Tara Chand of Nalagarh" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 234–235, folios 208–209). The back matter of the book sums it up: in conflict at first with Jahangir, "who also incarcerated him in Gwalior", he later had "a cordial relationship" with the Emperor, "helping the Emperor in a couple of his military expeditions" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 316, folio 290). Khalid adds that Guru Amar Das's warning is said to refer to "the incarceration of Guru Hargobind" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 273, folio not stated).

**Battles and Kartarpur.** Under Shahjahan, Khalid writes, "Three battles… all of them were won by the Sikhs… Amritsar, Lahira and Kartarpur", after which the Guru "decided to settle at Kartarpur" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 235, folio 209). Chishti's table puts his death "سمت ۱۶۴۵[OCR?] بمقام کرتن[OCR?] پور ضلع ہشیارپور[OCR?]", at Kartarpur in the district of Hoshiarpur, with a birth year of "سمت ۱۶۵۱[OCR?]"; the death is printed with the lower year (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 515). A damaged passage attaches the year "سولہ سو چوالیس[OCR?] عیسوی", sixteen hundred and forty-four of the Christian era (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 686). Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* names Kiratpur on the Sutlej as the place "که گورو هرگوبند و گورو هرراے درانجا سکونت داشتند", where Guru Hargobind and Guru Har Rai lived (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 513).

**Succession.** Khalid writes that the Guru's oldest son, Baba Gurditta, died in his lifetime, and that he "appointed Har Rai, the younger son of Baba Gurditta" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 134, folio 116). Bhandari agrees that Har Rai's father, "گوردته", had died "در ایام حیات گورو هرگوبند", in Guru Hargobind's lifetime, so that Har Rai sat "بجاے جد بزرگوار", in the place of his noble grandfather; he names "تیغ بهادر خلف خورد هرگوبند", Tegh Bahadur, Hargobind's younger son (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 519), and he names the Guru as Guru Arjan's successor, "پس او گورو هرگوبند خلف" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). Khalid writes that Tegh Bahadur followed "the footsteps of his father, Hargobind" and kept "the sword of Guru Hargobind" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 137, folio 119), and that "Following the tradition of his grandfather Hargobind, Har Rai too kept horses and a force of twenty-two hundred fighters" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 210, folio 184). Chishti names him as father of the ninth Guru, Tegh Bahadur, and as the *pir* of the seventh (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 686), and lists him as "چہٹی[OCR?] گورو ہرگوبند[OCR?] صاحب", the sixth Guru (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 680).

**The Sikh greeting.** Chishti traces the Sikh salutation to the Guru. In a passage partly illegible, he names the emperor "عالمگیر[OCR?] ... اورنگ زیب[OCR?]" in connection with Guru Hargobind, and says that the Guru then ordered his Sikhs to ask God always for "فتح", victory, and "بجائے سلام یہی جملہ مقرر ہو گیا", in place of a greeting this phrase was fixed: "واہگورو جی کی فتح" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 685).

**Judgements.** Critics, Khalid writes, saw the Guru's militarism as "a departure from the teachings of the early Gurus", and "Hargobind did not even compose a single hymn" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 233, folio 207); he was the "first Guru to not have composed any single hymn" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 316, folio 290). "Gury [OCR?] [Guru] Hargobind remains one of the most controversial Sikh Gurus", Khalid concludes, and yet "He laid the foundations of the contemporary face of Sikhism" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 235, folio 209).

## Bibi Kaulan

Haroon Khalid's *In Search of Shiva* tells the story of Bibi Kaulan, but sets her shrine elsewhere. At the village of "Amar Sadhu", inside the Defence Housing Authority of Lahore, stands a gurdwara "Built in honour of the memory of the sixth Sikh guru, Guru Hargobind", which is "known as Gurdwara Bibi Kaulan" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).

In Khalid's telling Bibi Kaulan was a maid "employed by a Muslim trader who lived in Mozang", who had "bonded her against her wishes". Hearing that "the guru was encamped nearby", she pleaded to be rescued. "The guru took her along with him on his horse and they reached this spot where they rested for a while before they headed off to Amritsar" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated). The spot is the Amar Sadhu gurdwara; Mozang enters the story as the place where she was held.

The historian Iqbal Qaiser told Khalid the rest. In Amritsar she "insisted that he marry her". "The guru obviously couldn't marry her", and so "he ordered the construction of a pool in Amritsar near the Golden Temple and named it after her", decreeing that visitors to the Golden Temple "must first perform ablution at the pool of Bibi Kaulan". Qaiser added: "The story of the gurdwara is also affiliated to the guru's horse like that of the Muslim saint's horse" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).

Neither Kanhaiya Lal nor Chishti names Bibi Kaulan in his account of the building east of Mozang.

## Setting: Mozang and Sikh Lahore

Kanhaiya Lal and Chishti both speak of Mozang as a *mauza*, a village, east of which the Guru's building stood (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 225, folio 228; Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 279). Chishti, writing of the place in general, says: "یہ موضع مزنگ سابق آبادی بیرون شہر لاہور[OCR?] ایک محلہ منجملہ محلہ ہائے شہر لاہور تھا", this village of Mozang, a former settlement outside the city of Lahore, was one of the quarters of the city (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 115). On the same page he writes that the city of Lahore was once settled as far as this place, "قبل میں شہر لاہور اس [illegible] تک آباد تھا" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 115).

Chishti also gives a founder of the quarter, in a garbled passage: someone "مزنگ کی ہو جہ بانی محلہ مزنگ کا نہا[OCR?]", possibly a man called "فتح[OCR?] مزنگ", who came with the first Chaghtai kings, "جب پہلے شاہان چغتائی[OCR?] یہاں آئے تو یہ بھی ہمراہ آئے" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 207). Elsewhere he connects the settling of Mozang with Pir Shirazi Baloch, to whom Baloch people in Mozang came and went, and he places Miani "غرب رویہ موضع مزنگ", on the west of the village of Mozang (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 211).

Of the Sikh state in whose time the building had great honour, Kanhaiya Lal writes that Ranjit Singh ruled the Punjab for forty years, and held Kashmir and the Derajat as well, "اس مہاراجہ نے چالیس سال تک پنجاب میں حکومت کی" (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 217, folio 220). Khalid writes that when the Maharaja "became the sovereign of Punjab at the end of the eighteenth century he spent large amounts of money on the renovation and expansion of many gurdwaras", and "attached vast tracts of land to these gurdwaras, so that the revenues from the lands could facilitate their functioning" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 34, folio 16). In *A White Trail* he adds that, with the divisions among the Sikhs and the British takeover of the Punjab, "a large number of important Sikh Gurdwaras were being administered by Hindu mahants" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 189, folio not stated). None of these passages names the Mozang building.

Chishti marks the end of Sikh rule with a *qita* on the departure of Maharaja Dalip Singh from the Punjab, two of whose lines are these (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 592):

چون شمع[OCR?] پنجاب از پنجاب رفت
نو گلی از گلشن پنجاب رفت

*The verse laments Dalip Singh's going from the Punjab* (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 592).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **What the place remembers.** The site's account says the gurdwara commemorates a visit by Guru Hargobind, one halt among several in Lahore. Kanhaiya Lal reports the Sikhs' statement that the Guru "اکثر سکونت رکھتا تھا", mostly resided, in this building (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 226, folio 229). Chishti calls it the Guru's *takia* (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 279). Khalid writes that the Guru "also spent a lot of time" in Lahore, naming no place (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated).
- **When.** The site's account dates the event to 1619. Neither Kanhaiya Lal nor Chishti gives any date for the Guru's stay (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 226, folio 229; Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 513).
- **Ranjit Singh's part.** The site's account says an original structure was raised under Maharaja Ranjit Singh. Kanhaiya Lal says only that in Ranjit Singh's time the building "کی بڑی عزت تھی", had great honour (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 226, folio 229). Chishti's sentence on the Maharaja's time is damaged and cannot be read (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 280).
- **The road.** The site's account places the gurdwara on Temple Road. Kanhaiya Lal places it on the road that goes to the jail (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 225, folio 228). Chishti puts the doors of its travellers' lodge on the Kasur road (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 280). Both books place it east of Mozang (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 225, folio 228; Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 279).
- **How many samadhs.** Kanhaiya Lal counts "چار پانچ سمام[OCR?]", four or five (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 225, folio 228). Chishti counts four, and names those in them (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 279).
- **What was observed there.** The site's account records daily congregational worship before 1947 and an annual Akhand Path around Guru Hargobind's Gurpurb. Kanhaiya Lal records a gathering on the fifth day of every moon, with *prashad* and illumination (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 226, folio 229). Chishti records a fair, its occasion illegible (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 280).
- **Bibi Kaulan.** The site's account binds this gurdwara to the memory of Mata Kaulan. Khalid's *In Search of Shiva* places Gurdwara Bibi Kaulan at Amar Sadhu in the DHA, Lahore, and makes Mozang the place where she was held, not the place of her shrine (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated). Kanhaiya Lal and Chishti do not name her (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 226, folio 229; Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 280).
- **The Guru's birthplace.** Chishti's narrative gives "موضع ڈوالی" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 507); his table gives "موضع وڈالی[OCR?]" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 515).
- **The Guru's years.** Khalid gives 1595 to 1644, and Guruship from 1606 to 1644 (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 315, folio 289); Dhillon gives Guruship from 1606 to 1644 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 240, folio 265). Chishti's table gives a birth in "سمت ۱۶۵۱[OCR?]" and a death in "سمت ۱۶۴۵[OCR?]", the death printed with the lower year (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 515), and a damaged passage elsewhere gives 1644 (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 686).
- **Where the Guru settled and died.** Khalid says he settled at Kartarpur after his three battles (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 235, folio 209). Chishti's table puts his death at Kartarpur in the district of Hoshiarpur (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 515). Bhandari names Kiratpur on the Sutlej as where Guru Hargobind and Guru Har Rai lived (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 513).
- **Why he was imprisoned at Gwalior.** Chishti puts it down to Chandu Lal, who arranged forty-one days in Gwalior fort (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 511). Khalid gives two explanations: that the Guru had kept money the Court gave him for his troops, and, in the Sikh version, that the Emperor "felt threatened" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 235, folio 209).

## Legacy

Since Partition the gurdwara has been maintained, like most of Lahore's Sikh shrines, as part of Pakistan's protected Sikh heritage rather than as a site of resident congregational worship, though it remains distinguished among the city's several Chhevin Patshahi gurdwaras — including those at Chowmala/Bhati Gate and at Jhalian — each marking a separate halt in Guru Hargobind's travels.

Haroon Khalid, writing of Lahore in his own day, finds that "it is now rare that one runs into a Sikh in this city, a city which was once ruled by a Sikh and the seat of their political strength" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 196, folio not stated). Of Pakistani Punjab at large he writes of "the hundreds of temples and Gurdwaras all over Pakistani Punjab" that were taken over by refugees and converted into households (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 58, folio not stated), and in *In Search of Shiva* he records: "I have seen several gurdwaras and temples being taken over and destroyed by the migrants" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated). He does not name the Mozang gurdwara in either book.

The two older accounts preserve the building as it stood in the nineteenth century: a walled compound east of Mozang with its samadhs, its well and *sabil*, its garden and lodge, the painted Mandir Baba Sahib or Darbar, and the two Granths under a silken canopy, honoured by the Sikhs as the place where Guru Hargobind had lived (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 225, folio 228; p. 226, folio 229; Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 279; p. 280).
