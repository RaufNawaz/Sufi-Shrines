## Overview

In the heart of old Lahore, a short way from Empress Road and the old Qila Gujjar Singh, stands the shrine of Bibi Pak Daman — “the pure, chaste ladies” — one of the oldest and most venerated sacred sites in the city, and among the busiest, drawing many thousands of pilgrims each day and immense crowds through the month of Muharram. Within its enclosures lie the graves of six holy women, remembered for their piety and for the miracle by which they are said to have chosen death over dishonour, together with the grave of their devoted maidservant. For centuries the darbar has been a place of prayer, vow, and refuge — cherished above all by women — and it stands, across sectarian lines, as one of the clearest expressions in South Asian Islam of the veneration of female sanctity. Its story is inseparable from a question that has been debated for more than a thousand years, and to which we shall return: who, exactly, were the Bibis?

The written record bears out both the shrine's renown and the debate. Kanhaiya Lal's nineteenth-century history of Lahore opens its account by saying that among the city's Muslim mazars and tombs these are famous as the most ancient and blessed, "لاہور کے مزارات و مقابر اہل اسلام سے یہ مزارات نہایت قدیم اور متبرک کی مشہور ہیں", and in the same breath that it has in no way been proved when these Bibis came to Lahore or from what country (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 304, folio 307). Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori's *Hadiqat al-Awliya* (1875) gives the six women an entry carrying two incompatible accounts of who they were, and his modern editor adds a third voice that rejects the popular story outright (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 247, folio 235; p. 248, folio 236; p. 249, folio 237). Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti's *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, written as a survey of the buildings, mazars, tombs and mosques of Lahore's environs (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 28), devotes several pages to the "احوال مقبرہ عالیہ حضرت بی بی پاک دامناں[OCR?] صاحبہ[OCR?] رحمہ[OCR?]", the account of the exalted tomb of the Bibi Pak Daman, with its legend, its buildings and its graves (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 352).

Modern writers describe the shrine in the same terms its devotees do. Shemeem Burney Abbas, in her study of women's voices in Sufi ritual, writes: "Among the women who brought Islam to the subcontinent are the Bibi Pak Daman, or the Pure Women. These were six women who, according to tradition, belonged to the prophet Muhammad's family. Their shrines are in Lahore" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 161, folio not stated). Omar Kasmani calls it "Bibiyan-pak-daman, a shrine in Lahore dedicated to women saints and particularly revered by the Shiʿa" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 125, folio 108), and Haroon Khalid "the famous Muslim shrine, Bibi Pak Daman" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 48, folio not stated).

## The Saints: Who the Bibis Were

Two great traditions about the identity of the six women endure side by side, and the shrine holds meaning in both. The older and by far the more popular tradition — the one held by most pilgrims, and reflected in the shrine’s traditional founding date of about 63 AH, in the first century of Islam — identifies the chief of the six as Sayyida Ruqayya bint Ali, a daughter of Hazrat Ali and sister of Hazrat Abbas the standard-bearer, and the wife of Muslim ibn Aqeel. With her, this tradition holds, were kinswomen and companions of the House of the Prophet (the Ahl al-Bayt) — among them women remembered as Bibi Hoor and Bibi Noor of the line of Abi Talib — six in all, together with a faithful camel-driver named Basheer. By this account the women were survivors of the tragedy of Karbala who made their way across the length of the Muslim world to Lahore, and the shrine is a memorial to the family of the Prophet. Because of this the darbar is a place of especially intense devotion for Shia Muslims and for all who love the Ahl al-Bayt, and its whole atmosphere turns toward the remembrance of Karbala.

A second tradition — advanced by the shrine’s own custodial family and by a line of scholars including Mufti Ghulam Sarwar (author of *Khazinat al-Asfiya* and *Hadiqat al-Auliya*), Pir Ghulam Dastgir Nami, and Hafeez Ullah Manzar — holds that the six were the daughters of the great saint Sayyid Ahmad Tokhta Tirmidhi, “Murshid-e-Punjab,” and that they lived and died in Lahore in the early thirteenth century, at the time of the Mongol invasions. What the two traditions share is, in the end, greater than what divides them: reverence for six women of extraordinary holiness, the memory of their choice of death over dishonour, and the miracle by which the earth itself is said to have received them. Both, too, agree on the names by which the six are known — Bibi Haj, Bibi Taj, Bibi Noor, Bibi Hoor, Bibi Gauhar, and Bibi Shahbaz (or Shahnaz).

The older books print the six names in much the same form. Mufti Ghulam Sarwar's entry is headed "بی بی حاج و بی بی تاج و بی بی نور و بی بی حور و بی بی گوہر و بی بی شہباز" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 247, folio 235), and the same six stand in his modern editor's table of shrines: "مزارات حضرات بی بیاں پاکدامناں، (بی بی حاج، بی بی تاج، بی بی نور، بی بی حور، بی بی گوہر، بی بی شہباز)" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 291, folio 279). Yet the book's own text twice counts five, not six. Its first account says that Aqil had five daughters, "عقیل بن علی کی پانچ لڑکیاں تھیں" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 247, folio 235), and its second again speaks of five daughters, all five devout and ascetic, "ان کے پانچ لڑکیاں … اور پانچوں عابدہ و زاہدہ" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 248, folio 236). Neither the author nor his editor remarks on the mismatch. In the entry on the father, the same book arrives at six by another road: two daughters who married, and "اور چار لڑکیاں باقی ماندہ بی بی حور بی بی نور بی بی گوہر و بی بی شہباز", four who remained, women of great devotion and asceticism, "رابعہ عصر", the Rabia of their age, who died unmarried (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 199, folio 187).

Kanhaiya Lal's list matches it except in its first name, printed "بی بی ملچ[OCR?] ۔ بی بی تاج ۔ بی بی نور ۔ بی بی حور ۔ بی بی گوہر ۔ بی بی شہباز", and he calls them six girls, "چہہ لڑکیاں" (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 304–305, folios 307–308). Nur Ahmad Chishti arranges the household differently again. One of the women, he writes, is a daughter of "حضرت مرتضی علی کرم اللہ وجہہ", sister of "حضرت عباس[OCR?]", "موسوم باسم رقیہ المشہور بی بی حاج[OCR?]" — named Ruqayya and known as Bibi Haj — and "منکوحہ حضرت مسلم", the wife of Hazrat Muslim; with her were five daughters of Hazrat Aqil, "حضرت بی بی تاج، حضرت بی بی حور، حضرت بی بی نور، حضرت بی بی گوہر، حضرت بی بی شہر بانو[OCR?]", the sisters of Muslim (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 352). Later he calls Bibi Ruqayya[OCR?] "بنت جناب شاہ مرداں شیر یزداں … علی بن ابی طالب علیہم السلام کی ہین [OCR?]", the daughter of Ali ibn Abi Talib (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 363). He also records how she taught: "آپ تدریس پردہ میں بیٹھکر [OCR?] فرما کرتی تہیں", that she used to give instruction seated behind the veil (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 364).

## The Journey from Karbala

In the popular telling, the story begins in the aftermath of Karbala in 61 AH. The women’s ordeal had begun even before that day: Sayyida Ruqayya was the wife of Muslim ibn Aqeel, the cousin and emissary whom Imam Hussain had sent ahead to Kufa, and who was betrayed and martyred there together with his young sons. After the martyrdom of Hussain himself and his companions on the plain of Karbala, the surviving members of the Prophet’s household were taken captive and, for a year, subjected to the cruelty of Yazid’s court — a captivity so harsh, the tradition holds, that the very sanctities of Islam were trampled. Hazrat Zain al-Abideen, the surviving Imam, is said to have charged Sayyida Ruqayya to depart with a few of the women, so that the faith and the lineage of the Prophet’s house might be carried to safety and continue.

She set out with her companions and, it is told, with the camel-driver Basheer — a man who had served in Yazid’s own palace and who, though he had once spoken harshly to the holy women, was bound to them by a hidden love, and who was trusted precisely because their destination was to be kept from strangers. They journeyed east across the length of the Muslim world until they reached the land of Hind. There, at Lahore, a Hindu raja — named in the local accounts as Mahabrand Bhatti — held sway, and scarcely a Muslim was to be found. As the women arrived, wonders are said to have followed: the idols of the temples toppled and their lamps went dark. The raja, disturbed, sent his son — remembered as Raja Kumar Bikrama Sahay — with soldiers to discover who these strangers were. When the prince came before the women he was overwhelmed by a radiant light and fell senseless; on recovering, he heard from their own lips the tale of Karbala, and was so moved that he embraced Islam, took a new name — remembered variously as Abdullah Shah Khaki or Shaikh Jamal — and remained among them as their servant. When his enraged father came with an army to seize the women, they lifted their hands in prayer, begging God to spare them exposure to strangers; the earth opened and drew them into itself, closing again and leaving only the edges of their veils above ground, over which their graves were later raised. From this — from their guarding of their honour to the last — comes the name by which they are known: Pak Daman, “the pure of hem.”

This is a tradition steeped in the devotion of Karbala, and on the frontispiece of one of its major histories stands the old Persian line of the lovers of the martyrs:

خوشا آنان کہ در خاک و خون غلطیدند

*Blessed are those who rolled in dust and blood* — a verse of devotion to the martyrs of the House of the Prophet.

The older books tell the same story with differences of their own. In Mufti Ghulam Sarwar's first account, which he calls the common oral tradition, the women were daughters of Aqil, of the family of the Ahl al-Bayt, and were in Syria at the time of Karbala; hearing of Imam Husain's coming they set out, but the end had already come, and so, in fear of the house of Umayya, they left for Hind and settled outside Lahore, "بخوف خاندان بنی امیہ کے ہند کو روانہ ہوئیں اور لاہور کے باہر آکر قیام کیا". He gives no date for their arrival (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 247, folio 235). The raja of Lahore, whose name the printed text gives as "مسمی لساؤ [OCR?] راجہ لاہور", sent men of his court to tell them to leave, "یہاں سے چلی جائیں"; but whoever went stayed there as a Muslim, "وہاں ہی مشرف باسلام ہو کر رہ جاتا" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 247, folio 235). At last the raja's own son went, and he too put on the robe of Islam, "اس نے بھی اسلام کا خلعت پہن لیا" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 247–248, folios 235–236). The Bibis then prayed, "ہم کو نامحرموں کی نظر سے بچا لے اور پیوند زمین کر لے", to be kept from the sight of men outside their kin and joined to the earth, and so all of them, with their servants, became one with the earth: "چنانچہ سب بیبیاں معہ خدام کے پیوند زمین ہو گئیں" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 247–248, folios 235–236). Many people, the same account says, came to Islam through the blessing of their footsteps, "بہت سے لوگ ان کے قدم کی برکت سے مشرف باسلام ہوئے" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 247, folio 235).

Nur Ahmad Chishti's version, introduced with "کہتے ہیں", it is said, begins before Karbala and in Medina. Imam Husain sent the Bibis away from "مدینہ منورہ"; they would not leave him, and the order that sent them, "حکم مرتضوی", is said to have been seen in contemplation; they left two sons each with him, "ہمارے دو دو فرزند" — the passage is heavily damaged in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 352). After Karbala they travelled "حتی کہ لاہور میں آ پہنچیں", until they reached Lahore, and halted on a mound, "ایک ٹیلہ", with no settlement around it (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 352). The raja's people fell into disorder, saying "عرب ترک[OCR?] اولاد رسول اللہ کی آئی ہیں", that descendants of the Prophet had come (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 357). The women's lament is given in their own voice: "اول جدائی برادران اور واقعہ کربلا ہوا، اور پھر ملک بیگانہ[OCR?] حتی کہ کوئی ہماری بولی بھی نہیں سمجھتا" — first the parting from their brothers and the calamity of Karbala, then a strange land where no one understands even their speech — and again, "ہم غریب مسافر ستم رسیدہ اور بی خانمان، ظلم کشیدہ، از حد بیکس ہیں", that they are poor travellers, wronged and homeless, beyond all help (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 357).

In Chishti's telling the raja sent his heir, "سردار ولی عہد"; the raja's name, he says, is illegible in some historians and in others "مہابرن[OCR?]", and the printed text names his daughter, "اوسکی بیٹی کا نام بکرماجیت[OCR?]" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 357). With the women was a company the text numbers "سات سو چار[OCR?] آدمی" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 357). A single glance of attention from the elder Bibi, "بی بی صاحبہ کلاں", turned the raja's child, "راجہ کی لڑکی", to Islam, and all the Hindus rose in tumult (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 357). The women prayed, "یا الٰہی! زمین کو حکم دے کہ ہمکو امان دیوے", asking God to command the earth to give them refuge, and "تمام بی بیان و کنیزیں سما گئیں اور پوشیدہ ہو ئیں", all the Bibis and their maidservants were taken in and hidden; their seven companions, "سات[OCR?] اشخاص ہمراہیان", had first been sent home (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 357). Four hafiz stayed behind, "ابوالفتح ـ ابوالفضل ـ ابوالمکارم ـ سعد اللہ" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 358). The convert prince, the "کنور[OCR?]", was named "عبدالقدر[OCR?]", later "عبداللہ", or "محمد جمال[OCR?]"; at the tomb it was revealed to him, "الہام ہوا", that his marriage to a Hindu's daughter was lawful, and she became like the moon of the fourteenth night, "ماہ چہار دہم"; then all of them, six or seven thousand people, became Muslim, "سب کے سب، کہ چھ سات ہزار[OCR?] آدمی تھی، مسلمان ہو گئے", and land to the east and south of the khanqah, "مشرق و جنوب رویہ اس خانقاہ کی", was given to his descendants (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 358).

Shemeem Burney Abbas gives the tradition in a modern scholar's words: "Supposedly, they were the survivors of the Battle of Kerbala and came eastward to the subcontinent to take refuge. Upon arrival in Lahore, they engaged in missionary activity" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 161, folio not stated). She adds: "It is reported that seven hundred male scholars came with the Bibi Pak Daman to Hind, as the land was known then" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 161, folio not stated).

## The Other Tradition: The Daughters of Sayyid Ahmad Tokhta Tirmidhi

The custodial and scholarly tradition tells a different, and no less devout, story. It holds that the six were the daughters of Sayyid Ahmad Tokhta Tirmidhi, a sayyid of the Junaidiya Sufi line whose spiritual descent is traced, through several generations, back to Imam Zain al-Abideen, and whose family had come from Tirmiz in Central Asia by way of Kech Makran to settle at last in Lahore. His curious title, “Tokhta,” is explained by a story of his early discipleship: when his own pir opened the door of his cell and found the young Sayyid Ahmad standing in patient attendance, he addressed him with the Turkish word for “standing” or “ever-present,” and the name stayed with him. He is said to have died around 602 AH — near the beginning of the reign of Sultan Qutb al-Din Aibak — and to be buried in the Lahore quarter still called Chilla Bibiyan, which takes its name from the spiritual retreats (chilla) of his daughters; his tomb is now enclosed within a property of a later custodial family. A Persian chronogram (tarikh) preserved in his memory hails him in verse:

سیّد احمد، شہِ برنا و پیر
پیرِ ہادی، میرِ عالی جاہ

*Sayyid Ahmad — king of the young and the old; a guiding master, a lord of lofty station.*

His nobility, sanctity, and gift of miracles were remembered as hereditary in his line, and his descendants spread widely across the Punjab and beyond. Of his daughters, two — Bibi Haj and Bibi Taj — are described as having married, while the others lived as lifelong ascetics, “the Rabia of their age.” Through Bibi Haj, tradition traces a distinguished line of Suhrawardi saints, among them Sultan Hamiduddin Hakim, “Sultan al-Tarikeen,” who is said to have renounced a throne for the life of a faqir and to lie buried at Mubarak, near the old town of Tulamba. In this account it was during the Mongol onslaught — when the army of Chingez Khan, pursuing the fugitive Jalaluddin Khwarazm Shah, sacked Lahore and its people fought for two months before the city fell — that the women, alone in their hermitage outside the walls where their father had lived, prayed to be hidden from the invaders, and the earth enclosed them exactly as in the other telling. The two traditions thus meet in the same miracle even as they differ over the century and the names.

Mufti Ghulam Sarwar's *Hadiqat al-Awliya* gives the father an entry of his own. He was originally of Tirmidh and came to Hindustan by an unseen sign; "یہ بزرگ سید حسینی تھے", he was a Husaini sayyid, descended through Imam Ali Asghar son of Imam Zain al-Abidin son of Imam Husain; nobility, sainthood and the gift of wonders were his by inheritance, "شرافت و ولایت و کرامت ان کی موروثی تھی"; and his affiliation was with the Junaidi family, "توسل خاندان جنیدیہ سے تھا" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 199–200). The book's modern editor corrects this pedigree twice (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 199–200). The same entry says that he kept two of his daughters with him, "بی بی حاج و تاج دو لڑکیاں اپنے ہمراہ رکھتے تھے"; at Kech Makran he married Bibi Haj to a prince, Baha al-Din son of Sultan Qutb al-Din Qureshi Hakari, and on coming to Lahore married Bibi Taj to Shah Zaid, his own nephew (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 199, folio 187).

The book gives his death as 602 AH and makes his title the chronogram of that year: "اور مرشد پنجاب ۶۰۲ مادۂ تاریخ ہے" — the words *Murshid-e Punjab* yield 602 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 200, folio 188). It places his grave inside the city of Lahore: "اور مزار گوہر بار اندرون شہر لاہور محلہ چہل بی بیاں طویلہ حضرت غلام محی الدین کے زیارت گاہ خلق ہے", in the quarter it names محلہ چہل بی بیاں, in the stable of Hazrat Ghulam Muhi al-Din, where people visit it (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 200, folio 188). The author repeats the year 602, and his editor writes the name "سید احمد توختہ ترمذی (ف ۶۰۲ ھ / ۱۲۰۵)", dying in 602 AH / 1205 CE (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 249, folio 237). The book does not say whether the quarter where the father lies and the site of the six graves are one place (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 200, folio 188).

Kanhaiya Lal tells the fate of that grave. Sayyid Ahmad died in 602[OCR?] AH and was buried inside Lahore in "محلہ چہیل بہیویاں[OCR?]", where his grave still exists; a large tomb once stood over it, but "جب سنگ مرمر اسکا مہاراجہ رنجیت سنگ نے اتروا لیا تو مقبرہ گر گیا" — when Maharaja Ranjit Singh had its marble taken down, the tomb collapsed; and "اسکے گرد و نواح کے قبرستان کو مسمار کرکے غلام محی الدین شاہ[OCR?] پیر زادہ رتا[OCR?] نے اپنی حویلی بنالی", Ghulam Muhiuddin Shah[OCR?] Pirzada Rata[OCR?] demolished the graveyard around it and built his haveli there; the grave now stands, built *pakka*, inside a stable, "وہ قبر اب ایک طویلہ کے اندر پختہ بنی ہوئی موجود ہے" (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 305, folio 308).

The Mongol account, too, is older than the modern tracts. Mufti Ghulam Sarwar gives it as his second account, taken from the *Tazkira-e Hamidiya*: "سید احمد تختہ ترمذی جو لاہور کے بزرگوں میں سے قطب یگانہ و غوث زمانہ تھے", Sayyid Ahmad Takhta Tirmidhi, who among the elders of Lahore was the unique Qutb and the Ghaus of his age, was the father of the six; and the author notes that this account is not in his own *Khazinat al-Asfiya*, "اگرچہ کتاب خزینۃ الاصفیا مؤلفہ بندہ میں درج نہیں ہے" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 248, folio 236). In this account, "جب چنگیز خان مغل سے شہزادہ جلال الدین خوارزمی نے شکست کھائی اور ہند میں بھاگ آیا تو چنگیز خان کی فوج اس کے تعاقب میں پنجاب میں داخل ہوئی۔ تمام ملک پنجاب انہوں نے غارت کر لیا۔ شہر لاہور کے لوگ دو مہینے تک ان کے ساتھ لڑتے رہے" — when Prince Jalal al-Din Khwarazmi was defeated by Chingiz Khan and fled into Hind, Chingiz Khan's army entered the Punjab in pursuit and laid the whole country waste, and the people of Lahore fought them for two months. When the city fell, the commander ordered that every inhabitant be killed, and not even an animal spared, "افسر فوج نے حکم دیا کہ شہر کے لوگ سب کے سب قتل ہوں۔ بلکہ کوئی ذی جان حیوان بھی جانبر نہ ہو", and thousands of people and beasts were slain (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 248, folio 236).

The Bibis at that time were outside the city, in the cell where their father had lived, "شہر کے باہر اپنے صومعہ میں جہاں ان کا باپ رہتا تھا"; they prayed, "الٰہی ہم کو پیوند زمین کر لے اور نامحرم مردوں کی صورت نہ دکھلا", asking to be joined to the earth and not to be shown the faces of men outside their kin; and when the attackers broke through the wall and burst into the house, "دیوار توڑ کر مکان میں گھسے", they found no living thing (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 248, folio 236). In this version too the earth leaves a sign: "البتہ زنانے کپڑوں کے کنارے زمین کے باہر نظر آئے", only the edges of women's clothes were seen above the ground (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 248, folio 236). Some men who saw this wonder became Muslim and took up the service of the tomb: "چند آدمی یہ کرامت دیکھ کر مشرف باسلام ہوئے اور انہوں نے مجاوری اس مزار گوہر بار کی اختیار کر لی" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 248, folio 236). The author himself hedges it: "یہ تقریر جو مشہور نہیں ہے۔ شاید کوئی اس پر یقین نہ کرے گا۔ مگر عجب نہیں ہے کہ ایسا ہوا ہو" — this account is not well known, and perhaps no one will believe it, yet it would be no wonder if it had happened so (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 248, folio 236).

Kanhaiya Lal prefers the same account, on Mufti Ghulam Sarwar's authority: "مگر جو مصنف حدیقتہ الاولیا بحوالہ تذکرہ حاکمیہ[OCR?] لکہتا ہے وہ بات البتہ قرین قیاس ہے", what the author of the *Hadiqat al-Awliya* writes on the authority of the *Tazkira-e Hakimiya*[OCR?] is indeed close to reason (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 305, folio 308). In his retelling, in the sixth century of the Hijra a man came from Kirman, "سید خدا پرست عابد زاہد ولی اللہ سید احمد تخنہ[OCR?] نام", a God-worshipping, devout and ascetic friend of God named Sayyid Ahmad Takhna[OCR?], and settled in Lahore; in his house were the same six girls, "اور وہ چہوں تارک الدنیا مجرد عابدہ زاہدہ تہیں", all six world-renouncing, unmarried and devout (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 305, folio 308). After his death they went to live outside Lahore's walls, "اسکی لڑکیاں لاہور کے حصار سے باہر جا کر قیام پذیر ہو گئیں", and gave themselves to worship apart from people (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 305, folio 308). Then, in 615 AH, the infidel Mughals, in pursuit of Sultan Jalal-ud-Din Khwarizmi, led an army against the Punjab, and the people of Lahore were put to death for the crime of resistance (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 305, folio 308).

His telling of the miracle is the fullest: "سب نے ملکر دست وعا خدا کے حضور میں اٹہائے اور سکہا[OCR?] کہ یا الہی ہمکو زمین کلا[OCR?] بیو[OCR?] ندر کرلے چنانچہ ایسا ہی ہوا زمین جابجا سے پہٹ گئی اور وہ چہوں بی بیاں مع اپنی خادمہ عورتوں بی بی نور ہی[OCR?] وغیرہ کے زمین میں سما گئیں اور انکی اوڑہنیوں کے پلے ذرا ذرا سے زمین سے باہر رہ گئے". All of them together raised their hands in prayer before God, asking to be taken into the earth — the wording of the prayer is damaged in the printed text — and so it happened: the earth split in places, and all six Bibis, with their maidservants, Bibi Nur Hi[OCR?] and others, were absorbed into it, and the ends of their scarves remained just a little above the ground; after that, people built the graves (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 305–306, folios 308–309).

## The Long Debate

That the identity of the Bibis should be so disputed is itself part of the shrine’s history, and the arguments have been rehearsed by scholars for generations. Those who question the Karbala account marshal a formidable case. They observe that, according to the standard histories, the wife of Hazrat Ali who bore Abbas — Umm al-Banin — had four sons and no daughter at all, so that Ruqayya cannot have been her child; that the two women named Ruqayya bint Ali are traditionally commemorated elsewhere, one at a shrine in Damascus and another in Egypt, not in Lahore; and that none of the family of the Prophet who went with Imam Hussain is recorded to have abandoned him before the end — indeed, on the night of Ashura he is said to have released his companions from their oath, and all refused to leave. They point out, too, that in the first century of Islam Lahore lay wholly outside the Muslim world: Islam reached Sindh only with the conquests of Muhammad bin Qasim around 93 AH, and Lahore itself came under Muslim rule only with Mahmud of Ghazni in the early eleventh century — so that no Muslim woman, still less a princess of the Prophet’s house, could plausibly have dwelt in Lahore in 61 AH. Finally they note that two of the six names — Gauhar and Shahbaz — are Persian, not Arabic, and are not to be found among the recorded daughters of Aqeel or of Ali.

Yet those who hold to the Karbala tradition answer that the histories are incomplete, that the pilgrims who have searched the shrines of the Arab lands report finding no grave of Ruqayya bint Ali there, and that the living devotion of centuries is itself a kind of testimony. It is striking that even Mufti Ghulam Sarwar — the very scholar who doubted that the women had come from Arabia after Karbala — did not doubt their holiness, writing that whatever their origin, the place is beyond question deeply blessed and its saints full of grace. This, in the end, is where the two traditions come to rest: divided over history, united in reverence. The shrine’s own keepers speak in the same spirit: the custodial account recorded in 2026 closes by asking forgiveness for any errors, since no book survives from the holy women themselves, and all that is told of them has come down through later scholars and saints.

Mufti Ghulam Sarwar's own words are these: "یہ عام روایت لوگوں کی زبانی ہے اور کتاب تحفۃ الواصلین میں بھی یہی مضمون لکھا دیکھا ہے۔ مگر قیاس نہیں چاہتا کہ واقعہ کربلا کے وقت یہ عرب سے ہند میں آئی ہوں۔ مگر ان حضرات کی بزرگی و پر فیض ہونے میں شک نہیں کہ مکان نہایت متبرک ہے" — this is the common tradition on people's tongues, and he had seen the same written in the *Tuhfat al-Wasilin*; but reason does not allow that they came from Arabia to Hind at the time of Karbala; yet there is no doubt of their sanctity and grace, for the place is most blessed (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 248, folio 236).

His modern editor goes further. Of the first account he writes, "واقعی یہ قیاس اور زبانی روایت ہے۔ جس کا تاریخ سے کوئی تعلق نہیں ہے", that it is indeed conjecture and oral tradition with nothing of history in it, and he refers readers to his discussion of the *Tuhfat al-Wasilin* under the entry on Shaikh Ismail Muhaddith Lahori (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 248, folio 236). In a second note he lays the blame for the legend's hold on named writers, the book's own author among them: "بی بیاں پاک دامن کے بارے میں یہاں کے مجاوروں نے جو لا یعنی روایات مشہور کر رکھی تھیں" — the idle tales that the mujawirs here had spread about the Bibis — had through the immoderation of "مولوی نور احمد چشتی مفتی غلام سرور لاہوری اور کنہیا لال", and their setting down of hearsay in writing, long since become accepted fact; then, he writes, "خود مفتی صاحب کو ۱۲۹۲ ھ میں جب تذکرہ حمیدیہ دستیاب ہوا تو ان روایات کو شک و شبہ کی نظروں سے دیکھنے لگے", the Mufti himself, when the *Tazkira-e Hamidiya* came into his hands in 1292 AH, began to look on those tales with doubt (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 249, folio 237). The editor credits "پیر غلام دستگیر نامی" with first proving, in his *Tarikh-e Jalila* and his tract on the Bibis, that they were the offspring of Sayyid Ahmad Tokhta Tirmidhi, and adds that to him too this account seems close to reason, "ہمارے نزدیک بھی یہ روایت قرین قیاس معلوم ہوتی ہے" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 249, folio 237). Under the father's entry he warns that much has been misstated about these women of gnosis, "ان کی نسبت خاصی غلط بیانی کی گئی ہے", and points to Nami's tract (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 199, folio 187).

Kanhaiya Lal, writing in the same century, sets oral testimony against a written tazkira and prefers the written. What the mujawirs relate by word of mouth, "زبانی مجاوروں", is a thing that reason does not accept, "جسکو عقل مانتی ہی نہیں": that these six girls were daughters of Aqil, brother of "علی المرتضی پیغمبر صاحب کے داماد", who left Mecca at the time of the calamity of Karbala, came to Hind and reached Lahore, settled, and died and were buried there (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 304–305, folios 307–308). Yet he places the miracle of the earth within the account he calls close to reason (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 305–306, folios 308–309), and leaves the date and country of the women's coming unproved (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 304, folio 307).

For further reading the edition's editor lists five works on the shrine: "(۱) شہباز الدین [OCR?] : تذکرہ حمیدیہ مرتبہ نامی (۲) نامی : (رسالہ) بی بیاں پاک دامن لاہور ۱۹۲۱ [OCR?] ع (۳) ایضاً : تاریخ جلیلہ (۴) ہاشمی فرید آبادی : تاریخ لاہور جزو دوم ص ۵۰۹ [OCR?] (۵) کلیم محمد الدین : (رسالہ) بی بیاں پاک دامن لاہور" — the *Tazkira-e Hamidiya* as edited by Nami, Nami's own tract on the Bibis, his *Tarikh-e Jalila*, Hashimi Faridabadi's history of Lahore, and a tract on the Bibis by Kalim Muhammad al-Din (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 249, folio 237). His numbered list of sources dates Nami's tract differently: "نامی غلام دستگیر پیر : بی بیاں پاکدامن۔ لاہور ۱۹۳۵ء" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 304, folio 292).

## The Shrine Complex

The graves lie within enclosed courtyards in the heart of the old city. Tradition places the six Bibis in two adjoining walled enclosures — in the first, by the usual account, Bibi Haj, Bibi Taj, and Bibi Noor; in the second, Bibi Hoor, Bibi Gauhar, and Bibi Shahbaz — their tombs built up in lime and plaster. Counted among them is the grave of their maidservant, Bibi Halima, remembered affectionately as “Bibi Tanoori,” who is said to have been swallowed by the earth along with her mistresses. Within the complex, too, stands a domed masonry tomb of a later age — dated to the eleventh century of the Hijra (about 1600 CE) and belonging to Sayyid Jalaluddin Haider Bukhari, of the celebrated Bukhari sayyids of Uch — a sign of how holy men of later centuries sought to be buried in the women’s company. Around the shrine has gathered a wider sacred neighbourhood: the nearby quarter of Chilla Bibiyan holds the tomb of Sayyid Ahmad Tokhta Tirmidhi, and the devotional literature names other holy figures associated with the place, among them Hazrat Ali Rangrez and Hazrat Alif Shah. Endowments of land (waqf) were attached to the darbar by successive rulers, and its fabric has been rebuilt many times over the centuries; the most recent major renovation was carried out in 2024.

Kanhaiya Lal's history of Lahore describes the site as it stood in the nineteenth century. The tombs, he writes, lie two miles from Lahore, in a direction whose name is damaged in the printed text: "یہ متبرک مزارات لاہور سے بفاصلہ دو میل بجانب کو مشہ گگنی[OCR?] واقع" (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 306, folio 309). One enters from the west, follows a lane-like passage to a second gate, and turns north to the graves. They lie in three enclosures, "یہ مزارات تین احاطے میں": an outer enclosure holding the graves of the Bibis' maidservants, "بی بی صاحبان کی خادماؤں", and a *pakka* domed mosque whose builder's name cannot be recovered from the printed text; a large second enclosure with the graves of Bibi Milch[OCR?], Bibi Taj and Bibi Nur; and a third with those of Bibi Hur, Bibi Gauhar and Bibi Shahbaz. Each enclosure, "نہایت عمدہ مقطع پختہ چونہ گچ بنا ہوا ہے", is finely built in *pakka* lime plaster (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 306, folio 309). Many buildings belonged to this graveyard, he adds, and many had fallen for want of care: "عمارات اس گورستان کے متعلق بہت ہیں بہت سی عمارتیں بسبب عدم خبر گیری کے گر بہی گئی ہیں" (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 306, folio 309). His table of contents lists the site under the plural, "مزارات بی بی پاک دامان", among the Muslim sites outside the city (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 4, folio 3), and elsewhere he calls it a khanqah, placing the tomb-house of Shah Rahmatullah Qureshi "بی بی پاک دامنان کی خانقاہ کے شمال کی سمت کو", to the north of the khanqah of the Bibis (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 251, folio 254).

Mufti Ghulam Sarwar's author places the women outside the city, "لاہور کے باہر" and "شہر کے باہر" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 247, folio 235; p. 248, folio 236), and in his editor's table of 1974 the column for the site's present location reads "آبادی بی بیاں پاکدامناں محمد نگر", the settlement of the Bibis Pakdamanan at Muhammad Nagar (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 291, folio 279). The book's words for the precinct and its parts are "خانقاہ", the khanqah; "حریم", the sanctum or enclosure; "احاطہ", the enclosure; and "گورستان" or "قبرستان", the graveyard (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 183, folio 181; p. 289, folio 277; p. 290, folio 278). In both of the book's accounts the graves are first marked not by built tombs but by cloth: in the first, only some of the women's head-coverings remained outside to mark the graves, "صرف اوڑھنیوں کی کچھ قبروں کے نشان کے لیے باہر رہ گئی" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 247–248, folios 235–236); in the second, the edges of their clothes (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 248, folio 236).

Nur Ahmad Chishti gives the fabric a history. After the Bibis, he writes, Hindu rajas ruled for four hundred years, "چار سو سال تک راجہ ہائے[OCR?] ہنود"; then "سلطان محمود غزنوی" built a *pakka* wall and *dalans*; in the reign of Akbar, "بعہد بادشاہ اکبر بہت عمارات تیار ہوئیں اور قبرستان کے مقرر ہوا", many buildings were raised and the graveyard was laid out, and the grave of a "سید نور[OCR?]" of that time still stands (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 359). The old land of the shrine was continued by every ruler, and a further grant, whose wording is uncertain in the printed text, "چار جاہ[OCR?] مزروعہ اکبر[OCR?] فی ضلع بستی[OCR?]", went to the servants of the khanqah, "خدام خانقاہ"; under "خان بہادر و یعقوب خان، امیر خان بہادر" a house with a three-arched *dalan* was built for the mujawar "میان شیخ رضا[OCR?]", and it still stands (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 359). A five-doored *dalan* is attributed to "سلاطین محمود غزنوی" and has been repaired from time to time; the mosque was first built by Baba Khaki and rebuilt some years before he wrote by "بوڑا [OCR?] میانوا [OCR?]"; and a *van* tree, "ون", spreads over the Bibis' tomb (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 361).

He describes the graves themselves (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 362–363). On a raised platform of white marble with a lattice screen, "پنجرہ", lies "قبر مبارک جناب عصمت مآب بی بی حاج [OCR?] نام خاص اونکا بی بی رقیہ [OCR?]", the grave of Bibi Haj, whose own name was Bibi Ruqayya, always covered with a *ghilaf*; its white plaster is attributed to the Ghaznavid sultans, and he counts twelve black stones, "بارہ عدد سنگ سیاہ [OCR?]". On a lower, broken platform to the south lie "بیوی گوہر [OCR?]" and "بی بی شہباز [OCR?]", "صاحبزادیاں [OCR?] حضرت عقیل رضی اللہ عنہم کی", daughters of Hazrat Aqil; west of it, two maidservants whose names are unknown, "کنیزکاں [OCR?] نا معلوم الاسم"; and under the wall of the eastern *dalan*, "امتہ [OCR?] الفاطمہ [OCR?] تاج [OCR?]" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 363). The graves of the four hafiz who stayed with the women are said to lie to the west of a spot whose name is illegible (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 358).

## Burials in the Precinct

Later Muslims sought burial beside the Bibis, and the books name several. Two books date the domed tomb in the complex a year apart, one by its occupant's death and the other by its building. Mufti Ghulam Sarwar records that Sayyid Sultan Jalal al-Din Haidar son of Safi al-Din Bukhari, brother of Mauj Darya, died in 1016 AH and was buried by the wall of the Bibis' sanctum: "آخر سال ایک ہزار سولہ میں فوت ہو کر بدیوار حریم مزار بی بی پاک دامناں مدفون ہوئے۔ وہ روضہ اب تک موجود ہے اور دروازہ اس کا خانقاہ پاک دامناں کے اندر ہے اور لوگ اس کو مزار استاد بی بی پاک دامناں کہتے ہیں" — the tomb still stands, its door opens inside the khanqah of the Pak Daman, and people call it the tomb of the Bibis' teacher (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 183, folio 181). The book records the popular name beside the actual occupant without calling it wrong, and whether the tomb is built into the sanctum's wall or against it cannot be told from the wording. The editor's appendix repeats it: "سید سلطان جلال الدین حیدر بن صفی الدین بخاری | اندر خانقاہ بی بی پاکدامناں | اندرون احاطہ بی بیاں پاکدامناں" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 289, folio 277). Kanhaiya Lal sets in the corner of the eastern[OCR?] enclosure a *pakka* domed tomb, "وہ سنہ ۱۰۱۷ ہجری میں بنا تہا", built in 1017 AH, in which lies Sayyid Jalal al-Din Bukhari, brother of Shah Mauj Darya Bukhari (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 306, folio 309). Nur Ahmad Chishti places Sultan Jalal-ud-Din Haidar's tomb adjoining the khanqah, "بی بی پاکدامان [OCR?] کے خانقاہ کو متصل" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 107).

The *Hadiqat al-Awliya* names two more. Its appendix lists "خواجہ ایوب قریشی لاہوری قدس سرہ" in the graveyard of the Bibis, "قبرستان بی بی پاکدامناں", without a date (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 290, folio 278). And the author's own father lies there: Mufti Ghulam Muhammad son of Mufti Rahimullah Qureshi Lahori, "یہ بزرگ احقر غلام سرور مؤلف کتاب کے والد ماجد تھے", died in 1274 AH, "وفات حضرت کی سال ایک ہزار دو سو چہتر میں واقع ہوئی" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 232, folio 220). The book closes his entry with a chronogram, "خورشید دین محمد", run together in a broken clause with his burial in the graveyard of the Bibis, "اور مدفن گورستان بی بی پاکدامنان کی مادہ تاریخ ہے": the place of burial is plain, the syntax is not (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 233, folio 221; p. 290, folio 278). Between the burial of 1016 AH and that of 1274 AH lie two hundred and fifty-eight years; the book's appendix has no row for the Bibis themselves, and names the site only as the place where others are buried (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 289, folio 277; p. 290, folio 278).

Nur Ahmad Chishti, too, uses the shrine as a landmark for other graves. Syed Mahmud II was buried "لاہور میں متصل مزار بی بی پاکداماں[OCR?] صاحبہ کے", in Lahore beside the tomb of the Bibi Pak Daman (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 139); the goldsmiths' graveyard is "ملحقہ علاقہ گورستان بی بی پاکدامنان صاحبان", adjoining the graveyard of the Bibis (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 462); and Alif Shah, whom the devotional literature counts among the holy figures of the place, came with the Bibis themselves: "بوقت تشریف آوری حضرت بی بی پاکدامنان یہ حضرت انکی ہمراہ تشریف لائی[OCR?]" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 462). The same book describes Rajab Ali Khan's garden as lying toward a "خانقاہ حضرت بیوی[OCR?] صاحبان", the khanqah of the Bibi Sahiban, which may be this shrine; the book does not say so in as many words (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 137).

## Data Ganj Bakhsh and the Antiquity of the Shrine

One tradition above all is cited by devotees as proof of the shrine’s great antiquity. It is said that when Data Ganj Bakhsh — the eleventh-century saint Ali Hujwiri, author of *Kashf al-Mahjub* and the patron saint of Lahore — first came to the city, he would go every Thursday night to the graves of the Bibis Pak Daman and sit in attentive reverence at a respectful distance, out of *adab* (courtesy) before their sanctity. That so great a saint should honour the shrine in this way is taken to show that the Bibis’ graves were already ancient and holy in his day, and it binds the darbar to Data Darbar, the two shrines standing together at the head of Lahore’s sacred geography. A gentler tale from the same devotional tradition tells of an astrologer who boasted that he could read the hidden and the unseen; the elder Bibi had food prepared for the poor and for travellers and sent him a dish in which fine food lay concealed beneath a plain covering of dry khichri. When the astrologer scorned the humble rice, and then learned what lay beneath it, he was humbled — for a man who claimed knowledge of the unseen had not seen the feast under the khichri — and he became a disciple. Such stories express what the shrine has always meant to ordinary people: a place of hospitality, of humility, and of quiet wonder.

Shemeem Burney Abbas records the same devotion: "Pir Ali ibn Usman Data Ganj Bakhsh Hujwiri", who was himself a great Sufi mystic, "was a devotee of the shrines of the Bibi Pak Daman" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 161, folio not stated); "He offered prayers at their shrines and spent considerable time in meditation at the shrines" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 162, folio not stated). And she gives the antiquity in her own hedged words: "These shrines reportedly were there long before Data Sahib arrived in Lahore" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 162, folio not stated). Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi's history of Sufism in India, which does not mention the shrine, dates Hujwiri's arrival in Lahore to about 1035: Khattali, who had ordered his disciple Shaikh Husain Zinjani to move to the Punjab, later sent Hujwiri after him (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 130, folio 112). Another saint of Lahore is linked to the place in Nur Ahmad Chishti's book, which says that Mian Mir often went there in devotion (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 295).

## Custodianship and the Sectarian Question

The shrine’s hereditary caretakers, the mujawirs, are traditionally held to descend from the convert prince — Abdullah Shah Khaki, or Shaikh Jamal — who is said to have built the first shrine over the women’s graves and to have served it for the rest of his life. In the modern era the darbar has been taken into the charge of the Punjab Auqaf Department, which administers it as a Sunni-managed shrine, and, given the scale of the crowds, government security and the Punjab Police are maintained on site at all times. The question of the Bibis’ identity carries a sectarian dimension that persists to this day: because the popular tradition makes the shrine a memorial of the Prophet’s martyred family, it is a focus of intense Shia devotion — the shrine’s own devotional identity is recorded, simply, as Syed and Shia — while its formal administration and much of its custodial lineage are Sunni. Around this difference old disputes have gathered — over whether the graves are those of Sayyida Ruqayya and the Ahl al-Bayt or of a Punjabi saint’s daughters, and over who may claim the shrine — yet in practice Bibi Pak Daman is revered by Sunni and Shia alike, and the two devotions meet, without merging, in the same courtyards.

The older books name the first keepers. In Mufti Ghulam Sarwar's first account, the raja's son who converted was given the name Jamal, refused to return to his old religion, and sat down as mujawir at the women's tomb, "حضرات کی مزار پر مجاور ہو بیٹھا" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 247–248, folios 235–236). In his second account the first mujawirs were instead men who had seen the miracle in the Mongol sack and become Muslim (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 248, folio 236). Nur Ahmad Chishti gives the convert's descendants the land east and south of the khanqah (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 358), names a mujawar, Mian Shaikh Raza[OCR?], for whom a house was built (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 359), and reproduces a seal reading "مہر عظیم شاہ [OCR?] سجادہ نشین پاک دامنان [OCR?]", the seal of Azim Shah[OCR?], *sajjada nashin* of the Pak Daman (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 321). Kanhaiya Lal records no custodian, endowment or income for the site at all (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 306, folio 309), but says that "اس مکان کو تمام اہل اسلام متبرک جانتے ہیں اور بدل و جان خدمت کرتے ہیں", all the people of Islam hold this place blessed and serve it with heart and soul (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 306, folio 309).

The modern writers agree on the shrine's Shia character and its wider following. Omar Kasmani's description of it as "particularly revered by the Shiʿa" comes in a report, relayed at one remove, that a man named Murad was then based there (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 125, folio 108); a firmer later report places him only in Lahore (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 136, folio 119), and his index files the shrine under Shiʿism (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 223, folio 206). Haroon Khalid introduces Prem Gupta as "a 40 year old Hindu activist from Lahore", head of the Hindu Sudhar Sabha (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 46, folio not stated), whose family lives in Lahore near the shrine, and writes of him: "He visits the shrines often, which is associated with the Shiia sect of Islam, but is also visited by Sunni Muslims, all part of the religious syncretism that was once part of this land but is now vanishing" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 48, folio not stated).

## Devotion and Events

Bibi Pak Daman is a living centre of devotion, alive around the clock. Pilgrims come to recite the Fatiha, to tie vows (mannat), and to seek the Bibis’ intercession (waseela) for healing, for children, and for relief from every kind of hardship; many recount cures received here after physicians had given up hope, and tell of the impossible made possible through the women’s grace. Women in particular find in the darbar a sanctuary — a place of comfort and of dignity — and it is above all a women’s shrine in the affection of the city. Every Thursday night the shrine fills for its largest weekly gathering, with langar (free food), qawwali, dhamal, and prayer, and a mosque attached to the shrine holds the five daily prayers. Its supreme season, however, is Muharram, when the sorrow and devotion of Karbala are remembered: enormous crowds converge on the darbar, and it is said that many walk barefoot in groups from their homes to reach it. The shrine is reported to receive on the order of eight to twelve thousand visitors on an ordinary day, some two hundred and fifty thousand in a month, and — at the height of Muharram — numbers rising toward 1.2 million.

Music has a particular place here. Abbas writes: "Reshma, a renowned singer of Sufi poetry, informed me that she sings at the shrine of Bibi Pak Daman in Lahore whenever the Bibi summons her—it can be at any time, night or day" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 195, folio not stated). And: "Both Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan and Reshma, musicians discussed in this book, claimed to be devotees of the Bibi Pak Daman shrine in Lahore" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 211, folio not stated). The shrine is one of the sites of her fieldwork in Lahore (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 20, folio not stated).

None of the older books consulted records an *ʿurs*, a fair, offerings or a building date for the shrine. Kanhaiya Lal's account of the site records none (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 306, folio 309), and neither do Mufti Ghulam Sarwar's passages on it (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 183, 199–200, 232–233, 247–249, 289–291).

## A Shrine of Holy Women

In a sacred landscape crowded with the tombs of male saints — Data Ganj Bakhsh, Mian Mir, Madho Lal Hussain, and the rest — Bibi Pak Daman is something rare and precious: a great shrine whose sanctity is that of women, and whose devotion has always been bound up with the lives of women. It is to the Bibis that the mothers of Lahore have brought their deepest hopes and fears for generations — praying for children, for good marriages, for the safety of daughters, for healing, and for protection from harm — and it is women, above all, who fill its courtyards, tie their threads of vow at its screens, and find in it a refuge that the world outside does not always offer them. The very meaning of the shrine turns on a woman’s virtue: the six Bibis are honoured not for conquest or office or learning alone, but for a purity and courage so absolute that they are said to have chosen to be swallowed by the earth rather than be seen by strangers. In this the darbar gives to the women of the city a mirror of their own dignity — a place where female holiness stands at the very centre, and where the qualities most often asked of women, and most often unhonoured, are raised to the rank of the sacred. That a shrine of such scale and antiquity should be dedicated to women is, in the religious geography of the subcontinent, almost without parallel, and it is a large part of what makes Bibi Pak Daman beloved.

In her concluding chapter Abbas treats the Bibis as founders, not only as figures of devotion. "Since they were erudite in reading and interpreting the Quran and were conversant with the prophet Muhammad's hadīth, they established zikr assemblies for the incantation of the Quranic texts. They guided the lives of the laypeople" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 161, folio not stated). "Thus, the Bibi Pak Daman are said to have held mystical assemblies with male Sufi scholars. They involved the men in disseminating Islam among the lay non-Muslim populations in the Panjab. The women spoke to the male scholars and to their audiences from behind the veil" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 161, folio not stated). "The Bibi Pak Daman generated an interest in Islamic discourse with their audiences because of their eclectic approach in which mysticism (tassawaf) was a component" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 162, folio not stated). Her index files the Bibi Pak Daman under female mystics, beside Rabia al-Adawiyya alone (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 236, folio not stated), and at the same time among the Sufi saints of Pakistani and Indian *silsilas* (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 239, folio not stated). The teaching from behind the veil that she describes has its counterpart in Nur Ahmad Chishti's line that Bibi Ruqayya taught seated behind the *parda* (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 364).

Abbas's wider argument, made of shrines in general rather than of this one, explains why such a place matters to the women of a city: "In the major mosques in Pakistan, for instance, there is a small space where women can go and pray on Friday or on religious festivals such as the Eid. In the local mohallā mosques there is no possibility for a female to offer her ritual prayers. The domain is exclusively for male participation. Thus, the important spheres of religious and spiritual participation for women are the Sufi shrines" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 18, folio not stated). She also records, citing Tahir Lahori, women musicians who performed *hazri* at the Sufi shrines of Lahore, especially at the time of the *ʿurs* (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 90, folio not stated).

## Setting: The Punjab of the Two Legends

The two legends set the Bibis in two different Punjabs, and the books describe both, though not this site. Nur Ahmad Chishti, writing of the province, says that at Husain's death the lament of Karbala rose in the Punjab too, "ماتمِ حسین میں شورِ کربلا پنجاب میں برپا ہو گیا تھا" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 70). For the Mongol legend, Kanhaiya Lal, citing the *Tuhfat al-Wasilin*, writes that Jalal al-Din crossed the river of Sindh into the Punjab, "سندہ کا دریا اتر کر پنجاب میں دخل ہوا" (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 165, folio 168). Rizvi dates Jalal al-Din's defeat to November 1221, after which Chingiz Khan's absence let Iltutmish consolidate his power in the Punjab (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 152, folio 134). The editor of the *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, in his addenda, notes that Shihab al-Din Nasawi does not mention an attack by Chingiz Khan on Lahore at all: "شہاب الدین نسوی ... نے چنگیز خان کے لاہور پر حملے کا ذکر ہی نہیں کیا" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 352, folio 340).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **Whose daughters.** The popular tradition, as the current account gives it, makes the chief of the six Sayyida Ruqayya bint Ali, wife of Muslim ibn Aqeel, with kinswomen of the line of Abi Talib; the second tradition makes all six the daughters of Sayyid Ahmad Tokhta Tirmidhi. Mufti Ghulam Sarwar's first account makes them daughters of "عقیل بن علی" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 247, folio 235), his second the daughters of Sayyid Ahmad (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 248, folio 236). Nur Ahmad Chishti makes one, Ruqayya called Bibi Haj, a daughter of Ali and wife of Muslim, and the other five daughters of Aqil and sisters of Muslim (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 352). Kanhaiya Lal's mujawirs make all six daughters of Aqil, brother of Ali; his preferred account makes them Sayyid Ahmad's (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 304–305, folios 307–308; p. 305, folio 308).
- **How many.** The current account, Kanhaiya Lal and Abbas give six (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 304–305, folios 307–308; Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 161, folio not stated). Mufti Ghulam Sarwar heads his entry with six names but counts five daughters in both of its accounts (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 247, folio 235; p. 248, folio 236), and reaches six again, as two married and four unmarried, in the father's entry (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 199, folio 187). Chishti counts one daughter of Ali and five of Aqil (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 352).
- **The names.** The current account gives Bibi Haj, Taj, Noor, Hoor, Gauhar and Shahbaz or Shahnaz; the *Hadiqat al-Awliya* gives the same six with Shahbaz (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 247, folio 235). Kanhaiya Lal's first name is printed "بی بی ملچ[OCR?]" where the others have Bibi Haj (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 304–305, folios 307–308). Chishti's list of Aqil's daughters ends with "حضرت بی بی شہر بانو[OCR?]" where the others have Shahbaz (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 352), though his description of the graves names "بی بی شہباز [OCR?]" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 363).
- **Whether any married.** The current account and the *Hadiqat al-Awliya* say that Bibi Haj and Bibi Taj married, the book naming their husbands as Baha al-Din son of Sultan Qutb al-Din Qureshi Hakari and Shah Zaid (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 199, folio 187). Kanhaiya Lal calls all six unmarried, "مجرد" (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 305, folio 308). Chishti calls Ruqayya the wife of Muslim (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 352).
- **Whence and when they came, in the Karbala telling.** The current account has Imam Zain al-Abideen send Ruqayya away after a year's captivity at Yazid's court. Mufti Ghulam Sarwar's first account has them in Syria, arriving too late for Karbala and leaving for fear of the Umayyads (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 247, folio 235). Chishti has Imam Husain send them away from Medina before Karbala (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 352). Kanhaiya Lal's mujawirs have them leave Mecca (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 304–305, folios 307–308). The current account dates the founding to about 63 AH; Mufti Ghulam Sarwar gives no date for the arrival (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 247, folio 235); Kanhaiya Lal says the date and country of their coming have never been proved (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 304, folio 307).
- **Whence the father came, and his name.** The current account and the *Hadiqat al-Awliya* bring him from Tirmidh by way of Kech Makran (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 199–200; p. 199, folio 187); Kanhaiya Lal brings him from Kirman in the sixth century of the Hijra (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 305, folio 308). The current account and the *Hadiqat*'s editor call him Tokhta; the *Hadiqat*'s author writes "تختہ" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 248, folio 236; p. 249, folio 237); Kanhaiya Lal's text prints "تخنہ[OCR?]" (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 305, folio 308).
- **The father's quarter.** The current account calls it Chilla Bibiyan, named from the spiritual retreats of his daughters. The *Hadiqat al-Awliya* prints "محلہ چہل بی بیاں", a name that reads as the quarter of the forty bibis, and does not say whether it is the site of the six graves (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 200, folio 188). Kanhaiya Lal prints "محلہ چہیل بہیویاں[OCR?]" (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 305, folio 308). The current account says his tomb is enclosed within the property of a later custodial family; the *Hadiqat* places it in a stable (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 200, folio 188); Kanhaiya Lal says its marble was stripped under Ranjit Singh, the tomb fell, and a pirzada built his haveli over the graveyard, the grave standing inside a stable (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 305, folio 308).
- **The year of the Mongol sack.** The current account says the early thirteenth century. Mufti Ghulam Sarwar gives 614 AH, after the father's death in 602 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 249, folio 237); his editor writes that 614 AH is wrong, "۶۱۴ ھ غلط ہے", and gives 639 AH / 1241 CE, citing Nami (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 249, folio 237); Kanhaiya Lal gives 615 AH (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 305, folio 308). The same editor notes elsewhere that Nasawi does not mention a Mongol attack on Lahore (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 352, folio 340).
- **Who threatened the women.** In the Karbala telling a Hindu raja of Lahore and his men; in the second telling Chingiz Khan's army. Mufti Ghulam Sarwar gives both and leaves them side by side (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 247, folio 235; p. 248, folio 236).
- **The raja's name.** The current account: Mahabrand Bhatti. The *Hadiqat al-Awliya*: "لساؤ [OCR?]" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 247, folio 235). Chishti: illegible in some historians, "مہابرن[OCR?]" in others (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 357).
- **The convert.** The current account: the raja's son, Raja Kumar Bikrama Sahay, renamed Abdullah Shah Khaki or Shaikh Jamal. The *Hadiqat al-Awliya*: the raja's son, renamed Jamal (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 247–248, folios 235–236). Chishti: the printed text names the raja's daughter "بکرماجیت[OCR?]" and has the elder Bibi convert the raja's girl, while the convert prince is named Abd al-Qadir[OCR?], later Abdullah, or Muhammad Jamal[OCR?] (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 357; p. 358).
- **Who travelled with them.** The current account: a camel-driver named Basheer. Chishti: a company printed as seven hundred and four, seven companions sent home before the end, and four hafiz who stayed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 357; p. 358). Abbas: seven hundred male scholars, as reported (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 161, folio not stated).
- **The first keepers.** The current account: descendants of the convert prince. The *Hadiqat al-Awliya*: the convert Jamal in its first account, Mongol-era converts in its second (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 247–248, folios 235–236; p. 248, folio 236). The current account calls the Tirmidhi tradition that of the custodial family; the *Hadiqat*'s editor blames the mujawirs for the tales he rejects, and Kanhaiya Lal gives the Karbala account as the mujawirs' own (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 249, folio 237; Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 304–305, folios 307–308).
- **Where the shrine lies.** The current account: in the heart of old Lahore. The *Hadiqat*'s author: outside Lahore (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 247, folio 235). Kanhaiya Lal: two miles from Lahore (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 306, folio 309).
- **The plan of the graves.** The current account: two adjoining enclosures, Haj, Taj and Noor in one, Hoor, Gauhar and Shahbaz in the other. Kanhaiya Lal: three enclosures, the maidservants and a mosque in the outer, Milch[OCR?], Taj and Nur in the second, Hur, Gauhar and Shahbaz in the third (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 306, folio 309). Chishti: Bibi Haj alone on a raised marble platform, Gauhar and Shahbaz on a lower platform to the south, and a grave of Amat al-Fatima Taj under the eastern *dalan* wall (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 363).
- **The maidservants.** The current account: one, Bibi Halima, called Bibi Tanoori. Kanhaiya Lal: maidservants, Bibi Nur Hi[OCR?] and others (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 305–306, folios 308–309). Chishti: two whose names are unknown (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 363).
- **The domed tomb.** The current account dates it to the eleventh century of the Hijra, about 1600 CE. The *Hadiqat al-Awliya* gives its occupant's death as 1016 AH and records that people call it the tomb of the Bibis' teacher (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 183, folio 181). Kanhaiya Lal says it was built in 1017 AH (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 306, folio 309).
- **The mosque's builder.** Chishti: first built by Baba Khaki and rebuilt by "بوڑا [OCR?] میانوا [OCR?]" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 361). Kanhaiya Lal: built by a man whose name is damaged beyond reading (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 306, folio 309).
- **The fabric's history.** The current account: waqf land from successive rulers and many rebuildings, the latest in 2024. Chishti: a wall and *dalans* by Mahmud of Ghazni, buildings and the graveyard under Akbar (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 359). Kanhaiya Lal: many buildings fallen for want of care, and no endowment recorded (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 306, folio 309).
- **The date of Nami's tract.** The current bibliography: first published 1372 AH / 1953. The *Hadiqat*'s editor: Lahore 1921[OCR?] in one place, Lahore 1935 in another (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 249, folio 237; p. 304, folio 292).

## Legacy

Whether one honours the six Bibis as women of the Prophet’s household who carried the memory of Karbala to the edge of the Muslim world, or as the daughters of a Punjabi saint who chose the earth over the gaze of strangers, the meaning of the shrine is the same: it stands for purity, for courage, and for a refusal to bow before injustice at the cost of one’s honour — and it has stood for these, in the devotion of Lahore, across more than a thousand years of retelling. Amid the debates of scholars and the changing life of the city, the darbar of Bibi Pak Daman endures as it always has: a place of refuge and of blessing, one of the deepest spiritual anchors of the old city, and a rare shrine where the sanctity honoured is that of holy women.

The books, for all their quarrels, close on the same note. Mufti Ghulam Sarwar, who could not believe the women came from Arabia at the time of Karbala, wrote that "مکان نہایت متبرک ہے", the place is most blessed (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 248, folio 236); Kanhaiya Lal, who thought the mujawirs' story beyond reason, wrote that all the people of Islam serve it with heart and soul (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 306, folio 309); and Abbas, more than a century later, recorded a singer who goes there whenever the Bibi summons her, at any hour of the night or day (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 195, folio not stated).
