## Overview

Darbar Malik Ahmad Ayaz is an active Muslim shrine in the name of Malik Ahmad Ayaz, who is reported to have been the "Slave of Mehmood Gaznavi - Governor Lahore"; it is reported that his tomb was built at this place after his death. The site's religion is reported as Islam and its affiliation as "Ahl e Sunnat - Ghaznavi silsila" (*silsila*, a chain of spiritual transmission), reproduced as recorded.

The shrine stands in Shah Alam Market, which is reported to be the largest market in Asia and to be extremely crowded; the *mazār* (tomb, mausoleum) is reported to lie near Darbar Ali Hajveri Ganj Bakhsh. The site is small, and the surrounding congestion is reported to prevent many people from reaching it, gradually reducing both the figure's fame and the enthusiasm of visitors. Residents of this densely populated neighbourhood are largely unaware of the life of the figure buried there — a figure about whom, it is reported, Allama Iqbal, Kaniya Lal and other writers have written books.

The books consulted add a good deal to this reported picture, and contest some of it. Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori's *Hadiqat al-Awliya* gives him an entry of his own as 'شیخ ایاز لاہوری', calls him the slave and beloved of Shah Mahmud Ghaznavi, follows him from tutor to the young prince who governed the Punjab to a renouncer in the company of *faqirs*, and has him die in 450 AH and lie buried inside the city of Lahore (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 192–193). Kanhaiya Lal's *Tarikh-e Lahore* and Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* both make him the man who restored Lahore after Mahmud's conquest (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 23, folio 18; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525). Annemarie Schimmel's books on Islamic mysticism and its poetry know him as the Turkish officer whose devotion made the king "the slave of his slave" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 84, folio 74), and Nizami-i Arudi's *Chahar Maqala*, in Edward G. Browne's translation, tells of a carousal at which Mahmud bade him cut off his curls (Nizami-i Arudi, *Chahar Maqala*, p. 55, folio 37). The editor of Lahori's Urdu edition, for his part, rejects the tradition that Ayaz refounded Lahore and treats the romance of Mahmud and Ayaz as a literary growth of post-Seljuq times (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 191–193, folios 189–191 as printed); and the year of Ayaz's death is given three different ways.

## The Life of Malik Ahmad Ayaz

It is reported that Malik Ayaz Ahmad was educated in Georgia in 993 AD, and that when "the infidels attacked your country" he was enslaved and taken for sale to the Baghdad slave market — slave dealing being, reportedly, then the norm. Too weak to attract buyers, he was passed over until Mahmud Ghaznavi came through and asked his price. The minister questioned the purchase; the king answered that what he saw in the boy the minister could not see. The price asked was ten thousand dinars, greed having been raised in the seller's heart by the king's words; Mahmud paid it and enrolled him among his soldiers.

Tradition holds that the relation of master and slave turned into love, and that Ayaz spent his whole life in devotion to Mahmud. The story of the melon is also reported: the king, feeding him a melon cut by his own hand, tasted it himself, found it bitter, and asked why Ayaz had said nothing; Ayaz replied that having had sweetness from his master's hand all his life, he had no cause to complain of one bitter piece.

It is reported that Mahmud fought "almost 17 battles with Hindus", in which Ayaz "played the role of the mastermind", and that his faith was so firm that "he did not fall for the infidels". In one such story the king, attacking at the "Onkay temple", found the idol suspended in mid-air; Ayaz reasoned that a magnet in the temple held the clay figure up, and that demolishing the walls would bring it down of itself — which, reportedly, is what happened, to the king's satisfaction.

The king's family and ministers are reported to have mocked the closeness between the two; when the minister tested them by summoning everyone at midnight to eat, Ayaz alone appeared fully prepared, the others arriving late or intoxicated and asleep. Mahmud made him his minister, and he continued always to regard himself as a slave, each night setting aside his costly clothes and taking out the old garments in which he had been brought for sale, so as to remember his past, sit alone and please his God. The last ten years of his life, tradition holds, were largely given to the remembrance of God (*dhikr*).

In Ayaz's last days, it is further reported, Mahmud wished to hand his own place to him; Ayaz declined, asking that it go instead to one of his two sons, Saad, and the king relinquished his power. When Ayaz governed Lahore the city had been ruined by war, its systems of livelihood, food, trade and wages broken, and by his intelligence he gave it shape again and set it on its own feet. He fell suddenly ill and died; it is said he was poisoned out of resentment at the love shown him, and in some places it is said he died a natural death. His death is reported as 8 August 1041, and his mausoleum is reported to have been built then in Shah Alam Market.

## Origins and Career in the Histories

Bhandari's *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* tells another story of his origins, and introduces it with 'گویند', 'they say': 'این ایاز خلف والی کشمیر بود', this Ayaz was the son of the ruler of Kashmir. As a small child he went with his father to a hunting ground, where 'جمعی ازعیاران آدم دزد', a band of man-stealing rogues, seized him, carried him out of that country and sold him in Badakhshan to a merchant 'بقیمت خاطرخواه', at a price to their liking; and 'ازتقدیرات قادر مطلق شاهزاده بغلامی منسوب گشت', by the decrees of the Absolute Almighty the prince was given over to slavery (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 410).

The merchant, 'نظر برحسن وجمال ایاز', with an eye to Ayaz's beauty and grace, valued him above all his slaves and saw to his upbringing, and came 'بقصد تجارت ازبدخشان درغزنین', from Badakhshan to Ghazni to trade (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 410). In Ghazni word spread of a slave too beautiful to look at; great and small came to see him, likening him to the moon and, 'درحسن وخوبی به یوسف کنعان', in beauty and goodness to Yusuf of Canaan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 409–410). The Sultan summoned the merchant with him, bought Ayaz 'بقیمت گران انچه سوداگر میخواست بلکه زیاده بران', at a heavy price, what the merchant asked and more, and made him 'انیس انجمن خاص وجلیس مجلس اختصاص', the intimate of his private assembly and companion of his select gathering; 'اورا برتمامی خادم وحشم برگزیده', he preferred him above all his servants and retinue (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 409). 'وسلطان اگرچه چندین هزار غلام داشت اماسرامد ایشان ملک ایاز بود که سلطان درکمند عشق اوگرفتاری داشت': though the Sultan had many thousands of slaves, the foremost of them was Malik Ayaz, in the noose of whose love the Sultan was caught (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 410).

Bhandari sets beside this a different view, introduced with 'بعضی شعرا نوشته اند', some poets have written: 'ایاز اگرچه حسن صوری وجمال ظاهری چندان نداشت لیکن دراخلاق پسندیده طاق ... بود', that although Ayaz had no great outward beauty he was matchless in praiseworthy character, so that the Sultan's devotion was to his 'سیرت نیک خصایل', his good disposition, and not to his looks (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 408).

Nizami-i Arudi's anecdote of Sultan Mahmud and Ayaz, in Browne's translation, calls the Sultan's love for 'the Turk' 'well-known and famous'; the opening sentence is garbled in the copy consulted, and Ayaz's name before 'the Turk' cannot be read (Nizami-i Arudi, *Chahar Maqala*, p. 55, folio 37). Nizami's description of him can be read only in part in the copy consulted, and its reading is pieced together and uncertain: Ayaz was "not remarkably handsome, but was of sweet expression and olive complexion, symmetrically formed, graceful in his movements, sensible and deliberate in action, and mightily endowed with all the arts of pleasing" (Nizami-i Arudi, *Chahar Maqala*, p. 55, folio 37).

Lahori's contents list him as entry no. 130, 'شیخ ایاز لاہوری', in the fifth chapter, on 'miscellaneous families' (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 18, folio 6), and the entry says of him: 'یہ شخص شاہ محمود غزنوی کا غلام و محبوب تھا۔ حق جل شانہ نے اس کو دولت ظاہری و باطنی عطا کی تھی', he was the slave and beloved of Shah Mahmud Ghaznavi, and God gave him both outward and inward wealth (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 192–193).

Lahori then follows him through the reigns after Mahmud's. After Mahmud's death Masud took the throne; in his reign his son Majdud, 'جو نو جوان لڑکا تھا', a young boy, was made governor of the Punjab, and Ayaz was appointed his *atalik*, his tutor, and came with him. When Masud died and his son Maudud took the throne, Majdud rebelled and set up a separate sultanate in Hindustan. Maudud marched against him, and 'جب لاہور کا محاصرہ ہوا تو بروز عید ناگاہ مجدود بمرگ مفاجات مر گیا': when Lahore was besieged, on the day of Eid, Majdud suddenly died, and Maudud established his hold over the Punjab (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 193). The edition's editor dates Majdud's governorship to 427–433 AH, though the reading of these figures is uncertain in the printed text (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 191–193, folios 189–191 as printed).

Maudud tried hard to take Ayaz with him to Ghazni, 'مگر اس نے منظور نہ کیا اور تارک الدنیا ہو کر صحبت فقرا اختیار کی اور بزرگان لاہور سے فیض کامل حاصل کیا اور بے انتہا دولت جو اس کے پاس تھی، براہ خدا فقرا و غربا کو دے دی': but he did not agree; he renounced the world, took to the company of *faqirs*, received complete *faiz* from the elders of Lahore, and gave away the boundless wealth he had to the *faqirs* and the poor for God's sake (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 193).

'آخر سنہ چار سو پچاس ہجری میں وفات پائی اور شہر لاہور کے اندر دفن ہوا': he died in 450 AH and was buried inside the city of Lahore (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 193). The editor replaces the year with 449 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 193), which he gives as 1057 CE on the authority of Ibn Athir, and reports Firishta as placing the death "a few days after Maudud's death" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 191–193, folios 189–191 as printed).

## Governor of Lahore and the Resettlement of the City

The restoring of Lahore takes a large place in the books. Lahori records the tradition in full: 'یہ بزرگ بانی لاہور کا بھی شمار کیا جاتا ہے', this elder is also counted the founder of Lahore. When Sultan Mahmud Ghaznavi attacked Lahore and Raja Anangpal, son of Raja Jaipal, was overthrown and fled to Hind after the encounter, Mahmud's army plundered the city and all the subjects left — 'شہر میں کوئی متنفس باقی نہ رہا', not a breathing soul remained in the city. 'اس وقت ایاز نے سلطان سے اجازت لے کر اس شہر کو پھر آباد کیا اور رعایا کو دور دور سے طلب کر کے اس میں قیام پذیر کیا': then Ayaz, taking the sultan's permission, settled the city again and, summoning subjects from far and wide, settled them in it (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 193).

The edition's editor rejects this tradition outright (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 193). He calls the founding of Lahore "later writers' conjecture" and treats the romance of Mahmud and Ayaz as a literary growth of post-Seljuq times; in their place, citing Latif, he offers Ayaz as commander of the cantonment (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 191–193, folios 189–191 as printed).

Kanhaiya Lal's *Tarikh-e Lahore*, which writes the name 'ملک ایاز' each time and never adds 'احمد' (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 17, 18, 21, 23; folios 12, 13, 16, 18), tells the story through the city's walls and gates. After Mahmud Ghaznavi's sack, 'چند سال یہ شہر غیر آباد رہا آخر جب ملک ایاز[OCR?] پنجاب کے انتظام کے لئے نامور ہوا تو اُس نے شہر کو دوبارہ آباد کرنا چاہا': for some years the city stayed unpeopled, until at last Malik Ayaz, named to the administration of the Punjab, wished to people it again (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 17, folio 12).

The resettlement was licensed, not private: 'جب ملک ایاز بادشاہ کے حکم سے پنجاب کا حاکم بنا تو اُس نے اس شہر کی آبادی پر توجہ کی اور فرمان اجازت سلطان سے حاصل کیا', when Malik Ayaz became *hakim* of the Punjab by the king's order, he turned to peopling this city and obtained a *farman* of permission from the Sultan (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 23, folio 18). He went about it with such zeal — 'اس سرگرمی سے شہر بسایا کہ چند سال میں وہ ویرانہ علما و فضلا کا معدن بن گیا' — that in a few years that wilderness became a mine of *ulama* and the learned (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 23, folio 18). Kanhaiya Lal makes this resettlement the event by which he divides the city's history, and the setting into which Ali Hajveri arrives, the saint whose shrine is the reported landmark for this tomb (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 23, folio 18).

Three of Lahore's gates are tied to him in the book. The settlement began from the *mohalla* 'جسکو لاہوری منڈی کہتے ہیں', which is called Lahori Mandi, and 'سب سے اول یہی دروازہ تعمیر ہوا جسکا نام لاہوری دروازہ رکھا گیا', this gate was built first of all and was named the Lahori Darwaza (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 17, folio 12). The Mori Darwaza is his victory monument: Mahmud had breached the walls at that spot, and 'ایاز نے جب بہر شہر کو آباد کیا تو فتح کی یادگار کے طور اس جگہ دروازہ قائم کر دیا', when Ayaz settled the city again he set up a gate at that place as a memorial of the victory (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 18, folio 13). The Bhati Darwaza dates from his settlement too, the Bhat *qaum* settling inside it 'بعد آبادی ایاز کے', after Ayaz's settlement (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 18, folio 13). And yet, Kanhaiya Lal adds, 'ملک ایاز کی آبادی کے بعد بھی یہ شہر کبھی ترقی اور کبھی تنزل کی حالت میں رہا': even after Malik Ayaz's settlement the city remained now in a state of progress, now of decline (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 21, folio 16). The book gives no date for his governorship.

Bhandari says the same in brief. After Sultan Mahmud Ghaznavi conquered Hindustan, 'ملک ایاز که منظور نظر سلطان و درخوبی و فراست بے همتا بود', Malik Ayaz, the favourite of the Sultan's eye and without equal in goodness and discernment, turned to restoring Lahore; he 'قلعه پخته احداث نمود', raised a fort of baked brick, and the city was rebuilt (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525). Bhandari says nothing of his tomb.

## The Shrine

It is reported that the tomb was built at this very place after the death of Malik Ayaz, that the Ghaznavi Sultanate built the Darbar, and that it was later maintained in good condition by "Raja Maharaja". A period of loss is also reported: the water of the Darbar was polluted, and — in wording the transcriber flags as unclear — "the area of crop was stolen for the special place of your Darbar which was spoiled by Sikh Raja Ranjit Singh", after which only the present small plot remained. It is also reported that some of the site's space was lost during the Sikh era and some to the surrounding market.

A mosque named after the figure stands in the vicinity of the Darbar, where the five daily prayers are offered. The shrine is under the supervision of the Department of Auqaf (Government Mehkama Auqaf), consistently reported as both the present custodian and the body responsible for the site's current condition.

Of the books, only Lahori and Kanhaiya Lal speak of his grave, and neither names Shah Alam Market. Lahori has him buried inside the city of Lahore and names no quarter (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 193). Kanhaiya Lal's table of contents lists a 'مزار ملک ایاز' at printed page ۱۶۰[OCR?], within its Islamic section for inside the city, which opens with 'مسجد بادشاہی' at printed page ۱۴۱; by the book's own classification, then, the tomb is inside the city, unlike the shrines it lists from printed page ۲۳۶ onward (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 3, folio 2). The reading of that page number is uncertain, and the line stands out of sequence in the contents (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 2–5). Beyond that line, what the book has on him is the history of the city: it says nothing of his person, his death, his burial or his darbar, and does not mention Shah Alam Market (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 23, folio 18).

Lahori's book has, in an appendix, a line for 'شیخ ایاز لاہوری قدس سرہ', placed 'اندر شہر لاہور', inside the city of Lahore, at 'چوک رنگ محل' (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 289, folio 277). It may be the same site, but nothing in the book says so: the line gives a saint's honorific, no 'ملک' and no date, and a landmark other than Shah Alam Market. The book's index lists him as 'ایاز لاہوری | ۱۸۰', with neither 'Malik' nor 'Ahmad' (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 313, folio 301), and in the copy consulted its index of persons has no entry for a Malik Ayaz (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 330, folio 318).

Chishti and Kanhaiya Lal also list a mosque of a 'خواجہ ایاز'. Chishti's *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* has in its contents 'حال مسجد خواجہ ایاز', numbered ۷۲۵[OCR?] (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 6), and Kanhaiya Lal lists a 'مسجد خواجہ ایاز[OCR?]' at printed page ۲۸۱, a second Ayaz building, entered separately from the tomb (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 4, folio 3). The Khwaja Ayaz of Kanhaiya Lal's history is a noble of Shah Jahan's reign who served in the buildings department under Ali Mardan Khan (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 278, folio 281) and as *mir-e-imarat* of Shalamar Bagh (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 279, folio 282). Neither book says that this mosque is the one reported beside the Darbar.

## Devotion and Practice

Visitors come from distant places; it is reported that non-Muslims also visit and that some have made documentaries about the figure, his stories appearing in books. It is reported, in these terms, that *Diyas* (oil lamps) and *prasād* (consecrated food) are offered daily.

The *ʿurs* (death anniversary observance) is held annually on 12, 13 and 14 Zil Hajj, its activities including the lighting of lamps, the changing of sheets, bathing, and the eating of food at the site. Attendance is given as 700 to 800 people daily and 40,000 to 50,000 at the annual *ʿurs*.

The place is reportedly held spiritually important because he fought alongside the king, served the people, protected their lives and property and instilled the spirit of Islam in them; because after Mahmud Ghaznavi's death he left his residence; and because it was his habit to spend nights in the remembrance of God.

What is reported of his teachings is brief: he was generous, intelligent, loyal, hasty, daring, brave, kind and honest, a soldier and also a good and devoted person, who set the example of these qualities in his own life, and people were motivated by seeing it.

None of the books consulted describes the observances at the tomb.

## Legends and Stories

The books tell other stories of the king and his servant, each in its own setting.

Chishti's *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* has a temple story of its own. At Somnath the amirs advised Mahmud to take the rajas' crores; 'جب ایاز سے پوچہا', when Ayaz was asked, he held that breaking the idol was better, for the renown it would bring among Muslim kings, and God would give more; and the sultan broke it with his own hand (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 805). The reported temple story is set at the "Onkay temple", where Ayaz's reasoning about a magnet brought the idol down. The books do not say that the two are one episode.

The reported picture of Ayaz taking out, each night, the old garments in which he had been brought for sale stands beside a story that Schimmel tells from Rūmī. She counts among 'the finest allegories ever written' one by Rūmī, "who tells the story of Ayaz, Mahmud of Ghazna's favorite slave, who looked every morning at the out-worn shoes and shabby clothes he wore before Mahmud elected him for himself; it was necessary for him to remember his former lowly state so that he could appreciate the bounty that his lord had bestowed upon him" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 221, folio 190). The lesson, as Rūmī concludes it in her account, is that "man should likewise remember how weak he was created, and then he will recognize his nothingness before God" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 221, folio 190).

Schimmel retells the story of the Huma bird as well: "One day the Huma bird passed over Mahmud's army, and everybody rushed to be touched by the Huma's shade, which, in Persian tradition, conveys kingdom. Only Ayaz went into Mahmud's shade", "for here was his true kingdom: he is like the faithful, who does not seek glory and power from anything created but only from his eternally rich, beloved Lord" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 323, folio 292).

Nizami-i Arudi's anecdote, in Browne's translation, turns on obedience. Mahmud, Nizami writes, was a pious and God-fearing man, and "he wrestled much with his love for Ayaz so that he should not diverge" from 'the Path of the Law'. At a carousal, with the wine affecting him, Mahmud looked at 'the curls of Ayaz' and "lover-like he drew him to himself"; but the 'watchman' of the Qur'anic verse warned him not to mingle sin with love. Fearing that he could not withstand 'the hosts of Ayaz's locks', he gave Ayaz a knife and told him to cut off his curls 'in the middle' (Nizami-i Arudi, *Chahar Maqala*, p. 55, folio 37). Ayaz "doubled back his locks to get the measurement, executed the king's command, and laid the two tresses before Mahmud"; "this ready obedience became a fresh cause of love", and Mahmud gave him gold and jewels 'beyond his usual wont' before falling into a drunken sleep (Nizami-i Arudi, *Chahar Maqala*, p. 55, folio 37).

Next morning Mahmud 'saw the clipped tresses' and was seized by remorse. No courtier dared ask him why, until the Hajib 'Ali ibn Qarib, 'his Chief Chamberlain', sent 'Unsuri in to restore his temper (Nizami-i Arudi, *Chahar Maqala*, p. 56, folio 38). 'Unsuri 'extemporized' a quatrain, of which Browne's English reads in part:

*Why deem it shame a fair one's curls to shear, … When clipped the Cypress doth most trim appear* (Nizami-i Arudi, *Chahar Maqala*, p. 56, folio 38)

Mahmud had the poet's mouth filled twice with 'precious stones', and drank wine to those verses until nightfall, "whereby his melancholy was dissipated" (Nizami-i Arudi, *Chahar Maqala*, p. 56, folio 38). The anecdote stands in Nizami's Second Discourse, on poets, and its moral is his: "improvisation is the chief pillar of the Poetic Art" (Nizami-i Arudi, *Chahar Maqala*, p. 56, folio 38).

Bhandari also carries Zulali's dream version of the tale (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 408–409).

## Ayaz in Poetry

In the poetry Annemarie Schimmel describes, the pair became a model. "More surprising is the transformation of the great warrior king of Ghazna, Sultan Mahmud, into the model of the ideal lover", she writes in *As Through a Veil*. "This conqueror of northwest India is praised for his close relationship with his Turkish officer Ayaz, who appears as the exemplar of the 'servant.'" "But the king finally became 'the slave of his slave'" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 84, folio 74).

In *Pain and Grace* she returns to the theme: "the warrior king and conqueror of Northwest India, Mahmud of Ghazna, was completely enchanted by the obedience and loving veneration of his Turkish officer Ayaz so that he became 'the slave of his slave'" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 175, folio 175). She names the Persian theme as "Shah i gada, 'King and Beggar'", and notes that the word *niyaz* is "often used in the Mahmud Ayaz story for the sake of its fitting rhyme" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 175, folio 175).

In *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* she writes that "Mahmud of Ghazna, the warrior king of Afghanistan (d. 1030), famous in political history as the conqueror of northwest India, becomes in literary tradition the model of the lover, because of his inclination toward his slave Ayaz", and that "The story of Mahmud and Ayaz is a typical expression of this kind of love and shows, at the same time, how the poets of Iran used historical facts to point out eternal truth" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 323, folio 292). For the theme she refers to Gertrud Spies, *Mahmud von Ghazna bei Farīd ud-dīn ʿAṭṭār* (Basel, 1959), and adds that "several epics have been devoted to this topic, and lyrics abound in allusions to Ayaz" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 323, folio 292).

Bhandari, writing in Persian, points to this literature. 'قصه عشق ونیاز سلطان وحسن وناز ایاز مشهور هست[OCR?] ودراکثر کتب مسطور علی الخصوص مولانا زلالی': the tale of the Sultan's love and *niyaz* and of Ayaz's beauty and *naz* is famous and set down in most books, above all in Mawlana Zulali (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 409). Verses on this love are in the 'محمود نامه ودیگر رساله', the *Mahmud-nama* and other treatises; as Bhandari's sentence runs, the Sultan himself composed them, 'سوز درونی وراز نهانی خویش بعرصه ظهور آورده', bringing his inner burning and his hidden secret into view (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 409). In a digression on love he writes: 'بدرگاهش شاه ودرویش برابر ودرحضرتش خواجه وغلام همسر', at its court king and dervish are equal, and in its presence master and slave are peers (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 409).

## Setting: Shah Alam Market and Data Darbar

The reported landmark for the tomb is Darbar Ali Hajveri Ganj Bakhsh. Of that shrine, and not of this one, Schimmel writes: "His shrine, called that of Data Ganj Bakhsh, is still a popular place of pilgrimage in Lahore" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 119, folio 88); and "his tomb still provides a place of pilgrimage for the Panjabis" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 376, folio 345).

Haroon Khalid's *A White Trail* records a memory of Partition in Lahore that may concern the same bazaar, though the book does not mention the shrine and its 'Shah Alami' is not certainly the Shah Alam Market in which the shrine is reported to stand. From his house Bishna Ram saw "the burning of the Hindu dominated market of Shah Alami, still one of the largest wholesale markets in the city" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 67, folio not stated). According to Ram, "The Hindus had their shops in the front, while they lived at the back. Using the gutters, the Muslims entered that area and burned everything" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 67, folio not stated).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **His name.** The survey gives Malik Ahmad Ayaz, and also Malik Ayaz Ahmad and Malik Ayaz. Lahori names him 'شیخ ایاز لاہوری', and his contents and index give neither 'Malik' nor 'Ahmad' (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 18, folio 6; Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 313, folio 301). Kanhaiya Lal writes 'ملک ایاز' and never 'احمد' (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 17, 18, 21, 23; folios 12, 13, 16, 18), as Bhandari too writes 'ملک ایاز' (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 410). Schimmel prints only Ayaz, "his slave Ayaz" and "the Turkish officer Ayaz" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 323, folio 292).
- **His origin.** The survey has him educated in Georgia in 993 AD and enslaved when "the infidels attacked your country". Bhandari reports, as something 'they say', that he was the son of the ruler of Kashmir, seized as a child at a hunting ground and sold in Badakhshan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 410). Schimmel calls him Mahmud's "Turkish officer" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 84, folio 74), and Nizami's anecdote calls him 'the Turk' (Nizami-i Arudi, *Chahar Maqala*, p. 55, folio 37).
- **Where and how Mahmud bought him.** The survey places the sale in the Baghdad slave market, where he was too weak to attract buyers, and gives the price as ten thousand dinars. Bhandari has the merchant bring him from Badakhshan to Ghazni, where his beauty drew crowds, and the Sultan pay a heavy price, what the merchant asked and more (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 409–410).
- **His looks.** The survey's Ayaz was passed over as too weak. Bhandari's Ayaz was too beautiful to look at and likened to Yusuf of Canaan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 409–410), though Bhandari also reports poets who wrote that he had no great outward beauty and was loved for his character (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 408). Nizami's Ayaz, in lines only partly legible in the copy consulted, was "not remarkably handsome" but "mightily endowed with all the arts of pleasing" (Nizami-i Arudi, *Chahar Maqala*, p. 55, folio 37).
- **His office.** The survey makes him a soldier, Mahmud's minister and governor of Lahore. Lahori makes him *atalik*, tutor, to Majdud, who was governor of the Punjab (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 193); Kanhaiya Lal makes him *hakim* of the Punjab by the king's order (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 23, folio 18); Bhandari, the Sultan's intimate and the foremost of his slaves (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 409–410). Schimmel gives him no office but "his Turkish officer" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 175, folio 175), and the editor of Lahori's edition makes him commander of the cantonment (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 191–193, folios 189–191 as printed).
- **The order of their deaths.** The survey has Mahmud, in Ayaz's last days, wish to hand his own place to Ayaz, who asked that it go instead to 'one of his two sons', Saad; it also says that after Mahmud Ghaznavi's death Ayaz left his residence, and it gives Ayaz's death as 8 August 1041. Lahori has Ayaz outlive Mahmud and live on through the reigns of Masud and Maudud (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 193). Schimmel gives Mahmud's death as 1030 (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 323, folio 292).
- **The year of his death.** The survey gives 8 August 1041 and names no calendar era. Lahori gives 450 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 193). His editor gives 449 AH, 1057 CE, on the authority of Ibn Athir, and reports Firishta as placing the death "a few days after Maudud's death" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 191–193, folios 189–191 as printed). The survey's date agrees with neither year.
- **Where he is buried.** The survey places the mausoleum in Shah Alam Market, near Darbar Ali Hajveri Ganj Bakhsh. Lahori says only that he was buried inside the city of Lahore (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 193), and Kanhaiya Lal's contents class the 'مزار ملک ایاز' among the buildings inside the city (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 3, folio 2). Lahori's appendix places a 'شیخ ایاز لاہوری قدس سرہ' at 'چوک رنگ محل', a line the book does not tie to this tomb (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 289, folio 277).
- **The refounding of Lahore.** The survey remembers him as the man who gave Lahore a new shape and "made a new map of Lahore". Lahori counts him the founder of Lahore, who settled it again after Mahmud's army had emptied it (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 193); Kanhaiya Lal has him resettle it under a *farman* from the Sultan (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 23, folio 18); Bhandari has him restore it and raise a fort of baked brick (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525). Lahori's editor rejects the tradition outright and calls the founding "later writers' conjecture" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 191–193, folios 189–191 as printed).
- **The romance of king and slave.** The survey names "the love and romance" of king and slave as what should be remembered, and Schimmel's books trace the bond through mystical poetry (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 84, folio 74; Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 323, folio 292). Lahori's editor treats the romance as a literary growth of post-Seljuq times (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 191–193, folios 189–191 as printed).
- **The old clothes.** The survey has Ayaz take out his old garments each night. Rūmī's allegory, as Schimmel tells it, has him look "every morning at the out-worn shoes and shabby clothes he wore before Mahmud elected him for himself" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 221, folio 190).
- **Ayaz's counsel at a temple.** The survey's story is set at the "Onkay temple", where Ayaz reasoned that a magnet held the idol in the air and that demolishing the walls would bring it down. Chishti's is set at Somnath, where Ayaz advised breaking the idol rather than taking the rajas' crores, and the sultan broke it with his own hand (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 805). The books do not say that the two are one episode.

## Legacy

It is reported that what should be remembered is the bond between Mahmud Ghaznavi and Malik Ahmad Ayaz — in the words reported, "the love and romance" of king and slave — a slave sacrificing his life for his king, and loyalty so complete that he could answer for it before God on the Day of Judgement; poets and travellers of that era and of the present are said to have written of these qualities. He is also reported to be remembered as the person who gave Lahore a new shape, and known still because he "made a new map of Lahore".

For Schimmel, "The complete surrender of the Turkish officer Ayaz, for whom only Mahmud exists, results in perfect love of the king, who becomes, in a wonderful transformation, the slave of his slave. Ayaz is the symbol of the loving soul" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 323, folio 292). Kanhaiya Lal, for his part, makes his settlement of Lahore the turn from which the city's later history runs (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 23, folio 18).

His name also served as a figure of speech. Chishti's history says of Raja Hira Singh that 'مہاراجہ کلان اسکو اپنا ایاز خیال کرکے [illegible] فرزند کہا کرتی تہی', thinking him 'اپنا ایاز', one's own Ayaz, and calling him son (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 868).
