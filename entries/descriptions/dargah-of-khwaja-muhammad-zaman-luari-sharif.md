## Overview

The dargah of Luari Sharif, some fifteen kilometres from the town of Badin in the far south of Sindh, is the shrine of Khwaja Muhammad Zaman (1713–1775), one of the most revered Naqshbandi Sufis and Sindhi mystic poets of the eighteenth century. Luari Sharif is counted among the oldest and most important Sufi sites of southern Sindh.

None of the books consulted for this article describes the dargah itself: none names its tomb, its custodians or its *urs*. What they offer is of three other kinds, and each is given below for what it is. Michel Boivin's study of the Hindu Sufis of Sindh speaks of the place, Lowari Sharif, as a centre of Naqshbandi scholarship near Badin, without naming any saint or tomb there (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 22, folio 9; p. 228, folio 215). Annemarie Schimmel's *Pain and Grace* speaks of a Naqshbandi master of eighteenth-century Sind whom it calls 'Makhdum Muhammad Zaman' and 'Muhammad Zaman Lanwari', a figure who may be this saint, though the book never writes 'Luari', gives him no dates and never calls him Khwaja (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 306, folio 306). And Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, written in the reign of Aurangzeb, names Badin only as a point on the edge of the Mughal province of Thatta (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529). Everything else drawn from the books in this article speaks of Sindh, its Naqshbandis and its shrines at large, and is framed as such.

## The Life of the Saint

Born into a family of the Siddiqi lineage that claimed descent from the caliph Abu Bakr and had settled in Sindh in earlier centuries, Muhammad Zaman received his first instruction from his father, Shaikh Abdul Latif, a follower of the Naqshbandi order, before studying at Thatta, then the great seat of learning in Sindh. There he came under the influence of the Sufi master Khwaja Abul Masakin, whose disciple he became and from whom, tradition records, he received the honorific Sultan al-Aulia, "master of the saints".

Settling at Luari, he made the khanqah he established there — traditionally dated to 1737 — the foremost Naqshbandi centre of eighteenth-century Sindh after Thatta, a place of study from which his khalifas carried the order's teaching across the province. A separate compendium of Pakistani Sufi saints, Alam Faqri's *Tazkirah Awliya-e-Pakistan*, records that he founded a new town, also named Luari, after the original settlement became uninhabitable.

He was also a poet of note; a body of mystical verse in Sindhi is attributed to him, of which some eighty-five poems are said to survive. He died in January 1775 and was buried at Luari.

Alam Faqri's tazkira independently corroborates the saint's identity and location, recording him as Makhdoom Muhammad Zaman Naqshbandi, known by the title Khwaja Kalan, with his shrine at Luari Sharif, Badin, Sindh. The tazkira gives a year of death, but its digits cannot be read in the copy consulted and are not reproduced here.

## A Naqshbandi Master in Schimmel's Pain and Grace

Annemarie Schimmel's *Pain and Grace*, a study of two mystical writers of eighteenth-century Muslim India, speaks several times of a Sindhi master named Muhammad Zaman. It calls him "Makhdum Muhammad Zaman" and "Muhammad Zaman Lanwari", never Khwaja, and its index reads "Muhammad Zaman Lanwari, Makhdum 23, 208, 261" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 306, folio 306). The book names no Luari, no dargah, no *urs* and no title Sultan al-Aulia, and gives him no dates. Its spelling 'Lanwari' may be Schimmel's own transliteration of the place-name. That he is the saint of Luari rests on three things the book does say: his name, his place in Sind in the generation of Shah Abdul Latif, and his tie as master to the Sindhi scholar Abdur Rahim Girhori. On that understanding, and with that caution, what the book says of him is this.

He appears first as Girhori's master, in a Naqshbandi chain. Girhori, Schimmel writes, was "the khalifa of the famous saint Makhdum Muhammad Zaman who, in turn, was a disciple of" Mian Abu'l-qasim Naqshbandi (the printed form of the name is damaged, as Abu 'I]-qasim[OCR?]) "and Mian Muhammad Thattawi" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 27, folio 23). 'The famous saint' is the only honorific the book gives him. The relation is confirmed later, where Schimmel describes Girhori "following apparently the statements of his master Muhammad Zaman Lanwari" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 261, folio 261). In that sentence the order, printed Nagqshbandi[OCR?], is named for Girhori and for Dard, not explicitly for Muhammad Zaman himself.

His writings are referred to once, and indirectly: Girhori "commented upon his master's Sindhi apophtegmata in Arabic" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 28, folio 24). The book gives no title for the sayings or for the commentary. Of Girhori's own works it adds that most of his books "were, however, destroyed during the Afghan raids in Upper Sind in 1781" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 28, folio not stated), and it cautions that it would be wrong to regard Girhori "as a typical exponent of Sindhi Sufism" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 28, folio not stated).

Schimmel sets Muhammad Zaman against Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit on a single image, the eye-glass. "The positive evaluation of the eye-glass in Shah's verse contradicts the use of this image in the work of his younger compatriot Muhammad Zaman Lanwari, who explained outward science as an eye-glass that stands between the eye and the goal of seeing" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 208, folio 208). 'His younger compatriot' is the only indication of date the book gives. On a larger question of doctrine, the reality of the Prophet (*haqiqa muhammadiyya*) as mediator, Schimmel places him with Girhori and Dard, and Shah Abdul Latif outside them (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, pp. 260–261, folios 260–261).

## The Shrine

Muhammad Zaman was buried at Luari, where his tomb and the vaulted chambers of his successors form the shrine complex. The complex has at times been subject to restricted access, with a police post maintained at its entrance since the 1980s.

The books consulted add nothing on the fabric of the shrine, its builders, its custodians or its endowments. Boivin's index places the village as "Lowari Sharif (Badin, Sindh) 9" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 245, folio 232), and his account, given below, speaks of Lowari Sharif as a Naqshbandi centre rather than of a tomb. Of the shrines of Sindh at large, Schimmel evokes "a lonely saint's tomb" in a Sindhi village, covered with "the blue and white tiles" that "match the purity of the blue sky" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 263, folio 263); she is writing of the Sind countryside, not of Luari.

## Devotion and Practice

An annual urs commemorates Khwaja Muhammad Zaman and draws devotees from across the region. The books consulted give no date for it and describe no ritual at Luari.

Of the province at large, two practices recorded in the books are worth setting beside this. Schimmel writes that "still in our day the trees near Sindhi shrines are decorated with many pieces of cloth, preferably red, in the shape of little flags or tiny cradles, which are attached there for the sake of a vow, as it is usual almost everywhere in the Islamic world" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 168, folio 168). And Boivin writes of the place of music in Sindhi Sufism, while noting a Naqshbandi exception taken up in the next section: "Maybe because of the Sohrawardiyya's hold on Sindh, the Chishtiyya could not spread in Sindh as it did in the rest of South Asia. Notwithstanding, music appeared very early in the Sufism of Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 227–228, folios 214–215). Neither passage is said of Luari.

## Lowari Sharif and the Naqshbandis of Sindh

Michel Boivin's *The Hindu Sufis of South Asia* is the one book consulted that names the place itself. It spells it Lowari Sharif; the difference from 'Luari' follows from the book's own stated refusal of academic transliteration (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 12, folio not stated). Boivin names no saint, tomb, *urs* or custodian at Lowari Sharif, and Khwaja Muhammad Zaman is not named anywhere in his book. What he says is said of the place and of its scholars.

In the early eighteenth century, he writes, Naqshbandi treatises "were written in Sindh by Naqshbandi scholars from Lowari Sharif" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 22–23, folios 9–10): "Naqshbandi scholars from Lowari Sharif, a village close to Badin in Southern Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 22, folio 9). "The main author was Makhdum Muhammad Hashim (1692–1762), who was qazi in Thatta" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 22, folio 9). These scholars showed "that the Muslim Sindhis did not know the Quran", wrote paraphrases and then "a Sindhi translation of the Arabic Quran", and "condemned some rituals, especially coined as Hindu or Shia, as non-Islamic" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 22–23, folios 9–10). Boivin sets this against a wider picture: "Historical sources provide evidence that, before the eighteenth century, the people who identified themselves as Muslims were few", probably because the name was restricted to the *sayyids* and their dependents (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 22–23, folios 9–10).

In an annex on the Sufi orders of Sindh, Boivin gives the book's only other prose on the centre. The Naqshbandiyya, he writes, "came to Sindh from the sixteenth century onwards, with the coming of the Tarkhans and the Arghuns, two Central Asian dynasties that ruled Sindh", and has "several important centres in Sindh", especially that of Lowari Sharif (printed there as 'Lowari Shari'), "located in Southern Sindh, near Badin". Its later treatises on how to follow Islamic rules lead to the conclusion that "the Sindhis, even when they were officially Muslim, did not practise 'Orthodox Islam'". And "the official book devoted to the Naqshbandis of Lowari Sharif was written by a Hindu follower" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 228, folio 215). The book does not name that work or its author.

Of the Naqshbandis of Sindh as a whole, Boivin says more. "The Naqshbandis of Sindh followed the reforming enterprise led by Shah Waliullah in Delhi" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 42, folio 29). A Hindu branch of the order "is hardly the case in Sindh", and "the majority of the Naqshbandis in Sindh are strongly opposed to music and to the singing of devotional songs" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 228, folio 215). "In Sindh, save the Naqshbandis, Sufism is dominated by poetry expressing the concept of wahdat-e wujud" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 162, folio 149). The Sufis to whom Sindh's Hindu Sufis refer "all belong to the wujudi school of the Sufism of Sindh, and thus the Naqshbandis are excluded" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 108, folio 95).

Annemarie Schimmel, in *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, describes the same movement in southern Sind without naming Luari. "A strong Naqshbandiyya influence in the beginning of the eighteenth century, however, changed the spiritual climate of southern Sind to a certain extent"; Makhdum Muhammad Hashim of Thatta and his relatives attacked the Sufis "who defended the emotional side of religion", and "this Naqshbandi group contributed to promoting a more sober mystical piety" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 428, folio 397). Later, "In Bengal as in Sind, the Naqshbandiyya and some more militant mystically inspired groups tried to curb the overflowing emotionalism expressed in the lovely folk songs" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 432, folio 401). Yet in *Pain and Grace* she notes that "In Sind there were many pious who, by their recitation of the Mathnawi, 'kept the marketplace of union warm', and Rumi's work was quoted by stern Naqshbandis as much as by members of the more emotional orders" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 165, folio 165).

## Sind in the Poets' Verse

The books consulted quote no verse by Muhammad Zaman and none about him or about Luari; the eighty-five poems the site's account attributes to him are not reproduced in them. The verse they do carry from his Sind is that of his elder contemporary Shah Abdul Latif, against whose image of the eye-glass Schimmel sets him. Two passages, in Schimmel's translation, speak of the province itself.

In Sur Sarang the cloud of mercy, rising "from the desert area in Eastern Sind, the Thar Parkar", floats over the world, and the poet, coming home, prays:

*My Lord, be kind over dear Sind, / Friend! Sweet darling! You may make the whole world happy!* (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 259, folio 259)

In a lament from the Marui cycle, the poet mourns the passing of the heroes and the holy men of Sind; the printed text is damaged in several places, and the bracketed corrections are kept:

*Marui ts [OCR?] [is] no more in Malir, / and there ts [OCR?] [is] no Hamir— / Punhun is no longer in Kech, / nor ts [OCR?] [is] in Sind an Amir; ... There ts [OCR?] [is] in the convents no Pir* (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 235, folio 235)

The same lament still lives in performance. Shemeem Burney Abbas records the singer Alan Faqir reciting the line

*Punnu is not in the Kec / Sind is not the amir* (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 156, folio not stated)

and her endnotes explain that "'Sind is not the amir' means that Sind is not in the position of leadership or amirship", and that "These are all Alan Faqir's political interpretations of the female myths" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 210–211, folios not stated).

Schimmel also records an Arabic saying "which was very popular in 18th century Sind": "Affliction is for saintliness the same as are flames for the gold" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 222, folio 222).

## Setting: Badin, Thatta and Eighteenth-Century Sind

Badin appears in Bhandari's *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, a Persian history completed in the reign of Aurangzeb, only as a measure of the Mughal province of Thatta. Describing the suba in his own time, Bhandari writes that the suba ran two hundred and fifty kos in length, from Bhakkar to Kech Makran, and a hundred kos in breadth, from the qasba of Badin to the port of Lahari (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529). He bounds it by Gujarat Ahmadabad to the east, Kech Makran to the west, Bhakkar to the north, and the salt sea to the south (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529). The book's index lists Badin with the printed locator 60 (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 42). It never names Muhammad Zaman.

Bhandari's description of the suba, written of his own time, is the fullest picture the books give of the country round Badin. The greatest of its rivers is the Indus; merchants from Multan and Bhakkar ship goods by it to Thatta, and large armies rarely go by land, because of the jungle and the lack of water (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529). The country depends for the most part on its boats, of which, great and small, there are forty thousand (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 530). The diet rests on rice, curds and fish; dried fish is shipped to the ports, and fish oil is used on boats (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 530). Of the old capitals he writes that Brahmanabad was once a great capital whose fort had fourteen hundred towers, that Adpur became the seat after it, and that the seat of government is now Debal, which is now Thatta (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 530). The suba held four sarkars, Thatta, Siwistan, Nasirpur and Amarkot (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 528–529).

Bhandari also tells how Thatta passed under the Mughals. In the year 927, the reading of the year uncertain in the printed text, Mirza Shah Beg Arghun came from Qandahar with an army and took Thatta (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 172). Arghuns and then Tarkhans ruled until Mirza Jani Beg came to court with the Khankhanan in the year 1001 of the Hijra, the year printed in words (the reading uncertain), and the country was joined to the empire (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 171). Jani Beg was given the jagir of Thatta, and the port of Lahari, which lies on the side of Thatta, was made crown land (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 174). It was with these same Arghun and Tarkhan dynasties, Boivin writes, that the Naqshbandiyya first came to Sindh (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 228, folio 215).

The Sind of Muhammad Zaman's lifetime was a province in upheaval. Schimmel writes that "Sind was, since 1595, part of the Mughal Empire, and although representatives of the Delhi court came there, the indigenous rulers enjoyed much freedom" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 25, folio not stated). After Aurangzeb's death "the native Kalhoro dynasty took over the rule in Upper and later in Lower Sind, only to become the vassals of the invading Nadir Shah, who first conquered Sind and the Panjab before he reached Delhi in 1739"; the Kalhoros were overthrown by "the former disciples of their saintly ancestor, Adam Shah Kalhoro", and "The Talpurs, who thus came to power in 1781, ruled the country until it was conquered by the British in 1843" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, pp. 30–31, folio not stated). For all that, "in Sind, too, the 18th century can be called the most important period for the formation of mystical poetry and prose" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, pp. 30–31, folio not stated).

The holy men of that country held a place in its order. Boivin, writing of Sindh before the British conquest, notes that "In Sindh, the Sayyids had two characteristics. The majority of them were landowners (zamindar), but they were also pir"; that "The pirs of Sindh play the role of mediators between the tribes, and between the rulers and the governed"; and that "The power of the pir was reinforced in the eighteenth century when the Kalhora developed the irrigation system" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 38–41, folios 25–28). "In Sindh, the darbar was the sacred territory in which the Sufi culture was at work" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 37–38, folios 24–25). Jamal Malik, in Werbner and Basu's *Embodying Charisma*, writes that the holy-man cult is "particularly evident in the countryside, where major landlords and large-scale land concentrations are crucial features, especially in Punjab and Sindh", where "the local pîr represents the local landlord or is at least closely related to him" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 199, folio 188). Under the British, Katherine Ewing is quoted as saying, "British policy, especially in the Punjab and Sind, was to treat them in essentially the same way that landlords and tribal leaders were treated", that is, the hereditary saints (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 33, folio 23).

Sind's pride in its saints is told in a story that the books set in two different invasions. Schimmel, in *Pain and Grace*, relates that during Ahmad Shah Abdali's invasion Divan Gidumal, "the minister of the Kalhoro princes", offered the invader two bags which "contain the most valuable of Sind's gifts; they contain the holy dust from the tombs of numerous saints and Pirs of Sind" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 25, folio not stated). In *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* she asks: "Did not Diwan Gidumal, the minister, give the invading Nadir Shah (1739) the most precious treasure Sind had to offer, some of the dust of its saints?" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 427, folio 396). Of the province's shrines at large, Abbas writes that "In the province of Sind alone there are said to be more than a hundred thousand Sufi shrines" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 50, folio not stated). These passages speak of Sind, not of Luari.

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The saint's name and titles.** The site's account calls him Khwaja Muhammad Zaman, with the honorific Sultan al-Aulia, "master of the saints", received from Khwaja Abul Masakin. Alam Faqri's *Tazkirah Awliya-e-Pakistan*, as the site's account reports it, records him as Makhdoom Muhammad Zaman Naqshbandi, known by the title Khwaja Kalan. Schimmel's *Pain and Grace* calls him "Makhdum Muhammad Zaman" and "Muhammad Zaman Lanwari", never Khwaja, and gives him no honorific but 'the famous saint' (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 27, folio 23; p. 306, folio 306).
- **The name of the place.** The site's account has Luari and Luari Sharif. Boivin prints Lowari Sharif, and once 'Lowari Shari' (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 22, folio 9; p. 228, folio 215). Schimmel, if her Muhammad Zaman is this saint, gives the place-name only in the form 'Lanwari' (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 208, folio 208).
- **His teachers.** The site's account says he was taught first by his father, Shaikh Abdul Latif, and became at Thatta the disciple of Khwaja Abul Masakin. Schimmel makes Makhdum Muhammad Zaman a disciple of Mian Abu'l-qasim Naqshbandi (the printed name damaged) "and Mian Muhammad Thattawi" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 27, folio 23).
- **His writings.** The site's account calls him a poet of note, with a body of mystical verse in Sindhi of which some eighty-five poems are said to survive. Schimmel refers only to "his master's Sindhi apophtegmata", on which Girhori commented in Arabic, and gives no title (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 28, folio 24).
- **The dates of the Naqshbandi centre.** The site's account has the saint, born in 1713, establish his khanqah at Luari in 1737 (a traditional date) and make it the foremost Naqshbandi centre of Sindh after Thatta. Boivin writes that Naqshbandi treatises were written "by Naqshbandi scholars from Lowari Sharif" in the early eighteenth century, and names as "The main author" Makhdum Muhammad Hashim (1692–1762) (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 22–23, folios 9–10; p. 22, folio 9).
- **Makhdum Muhammad Hashim's place.** Boivin counts him among the scholars from Lowari Sharif, adding that he "was qazi in Thatta" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 22, folio 9). Schimmel calls him "Makhdum Muhammad Hashim of Thatta" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 428, folio 397).
- **Diwan Gidumal's gift.** Schimmel's *Pain and Grace* sets the gift of the saints' dust during Ahmad Shah Abdali's invasion (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 25, folio not stated); her *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* gives it to "the invading Nadir Shah (1739)" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 427, folio 396).

## Legacy

Luari Sharif is counted among the oldest and most important Sufi sites of southern Sindh, and the site's account remembers Khwaja Muhammad Zaman as one of the most revered Naqshbandi Sufis and Sindhi mystic poets of the eighteenth century, whose khalifas carried the order's teaching across the province.

The books consulted show the two sides of that legacy. For Boivin, Lowari Sharif stands for the Naqshbandi scholars who translated and paraphrased the Quran into Sindhi and who condemned rituals they judged non-Islamic (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 22–23, folios 9–10); yet "the official book devoted to the Naqshbandis of Lowari Sharif was written by a Hindu follower" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 228, folio 215). For Schimmel, the master she calls Muhammad Zaman Lanwari is remembered through his khalifa Girhori, who commented on his Sindhi sayings in Arabic, and through his image of outward science as an eye-glass standing between the eye and the goal of seeing, a counterpoint to Shah Abdul Latif's own verse (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 28, folio 24; p. 208, folio 208).
