## Overview

Gurdwara Khoohi Bhai Lalo, in the old town of Eminabad, marks the home and the narrow well (*khooi*) of *Bhai Lalo*, the honest carpenter with whom *Guru Nanak Dev Ji* lodged when he came to the town in the early sixteenth century. Bhai Lalo, traditionally said to have been born at Saidpur (Eminabad) in 1452 into a family of carpenters, became one of the most beloved of the Guru's early companions, and his house grew into a *dharamsala*, a gathering-place for the local followers of Guru Nanak. The site is inseparable from one of the most famous of all the *sakhis*: the story of *Malik Bhago*, a wealthy official of Eminabad who held a great feast and was affronted when the Guru chose the plain fare of the labouring carpenter over his own rich table. Summoned to explain himself, Guru Nanak is remembered to have taken Bhai Lalo's coarse bread in one hand and Malik Bhago's fine bread in the other and pressed them: from the honest man's bread there flowed milk, and from the oppressor's, blood — a sign, the Guru explained, that Lalo's food was earned by the sweat of honest toil, while Bhago's was wrung from the exploitation of the poor. The parable of the milk and the blood has ever since stood at the heart of the Sikh ethic of *kirat karni*, of earning one's bread by honest labour and sharing it with others. The gurdwara, named for the well that still survives, preserves this memory; since Partition it has been maintained as part of Pakistan's Sikh heritage and visited by pilgrims, usually in the same round as the nearby Chakki Sahib and Rori Sahib shrines.

The shrine remembers two figures, the Guru and the carpenter who was his host. Only one of the books consulted describes the gurdwara itself. Haroon Khalid, travelling through Pakistan in the footsteps of Guru Nanak with the historian Iqbal Qaiser, was led to it by old men of the town into an alley and found it locked; his book calls it Bhai Lalo di Khoi, and he identifies Saidpur, the town of the old stories, with Eminabad (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 115, folio 97; p. 116, folio 98). Harish Dhillon's retelling of the Janamsakhis tells the story of Lalo at length, but sets it at "Saidpur", which it places only "in Pakistan" and does not identify with Eminabad (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 54, folio 60). Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi's history of Sufism in India, which follows the Guru's life into the years of Babur's invasions, names neither Bhai Lalo nor Eminabad. Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* names a town and district spelled Aminabad but says nothing there of the Guru or of any shrine (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 176). The other books speak of Guru Nanak, of the town or of the province, never of this gurdwara, and what is drawn from them below is said of the figure or the place.

## Bhai Lalo

Bhai Lalo is the shrine's second figure, and the one who gives it its name. The site's account makes him a carpenter of Saidpur, born there in 1452 into a family of carpenters. Iqbal Qaiser's 1998 survey of Sikh shrines in Pakistan calls him “a carpenter of *Getaura* caste”. Speaking to Khalid of one Bhai Ram Singh, Qaiser said: "Bhai Ram Singh is of the carpenter caste and Bhai Lalo was also a carpenter", but he would not have Ram Singh taken for Lalo's descendant: "They shared the same caste. That's all." (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 117, folio 99)

Khalid's own account of Lalo is, by his own description, reconstruction: the part of his book that narrates the Guru's life, he writes, "is fictionalised" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 17, folio not stated). In it Lalo is a carpenter in Saidpur, older than Nanak, living in the poorest house in the city, a house with no window and nothing in it but a *charpoy*, a rolled-up mattress and a few clay utensils (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 102, 104, folios 84, 86). He "could not read or write but was eager for education": as a child he loitered outside the *havelis* of rich teachers to overhear their lessons in Persian and Arabic, while his father trained him as a carpenter (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 105, folio 87).

Dhillon's Lalo is a low-caste carpenter living in a "single room, thatched" shanty on the outskirts of Saidpur, who tells Nanak that people call him Lalo and that he is a carpenter (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 57, folio 63; p. 58, folio 64). He works "on a daily wage" for "Ramdev, the master carpenter", and cannot afford to miss a single day's work (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 63, folio 69). His poverty is drawn in a few objects: a single oil lamp, a loosely strung *charpoy*, a single thick *bajre ki roti*, a millet bread, and a single small onion (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 60, folio 66). When Dhillon brings the Guru back to Saidpur at the end of his travels, he calls it the town where Lalo, one of Nanak's first disciples, resided; the word "first" is uncertain in the printed text (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 224, folio 246).

Khalid counts the meeting of the two men among the great episodes of the Guru's life. The encounter between Nanak and Bhai Lalo, he writes, "is one of the most important" in the Janamsakhis, though the line in which he says so is damaged in the printed text (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 112, folio 94). And Lalo's name survives in the Guru's own verse: the *Babur Bani*, the poem of Babur's invasion, repeatedly addresses "O Lalo" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 113–114, folios 95–96).

## The Guru's Visit

In Khalid's reconstruction Saidpur is the first goal of the Guru's travels, the city "with the grandest Hindu temples" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 108, folio 90). Nanak and his companion Mardana come to Lalo's house, and Lalo serves them two dried-up *rotis* with water. He asks why Nanak did not go to Malik Bhago's feast, and Nanak explains his rejection of caste (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 109–111, folio not stated for p. 109, folios 92–93). Nanak tells Lalo of his past: he "used to be in charge of the food store at the court of the Diwa[n] [OCR?] of Sultanpur", and he had left it to "take up this garb and travel the world in search of Truth" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 104–105, folios 86–87). That garb, in Khalid's telling, mixed the dress of several traditions: a red dervish's *choga* rather than a green one, a fakir's white belt, a cap partly covered by a turban, and wooden sandals, which Khalid reads as "a conscious effort by Nanak to dilut[e] his religio[us] identity" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 105–106, folios 87–88).

At night Mardana played the *rubab* while Nanak sang, and "throngs of people headed towards Lalo's house"; Nanak stayed "for a few days" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 111, folio 93). In Khalid's reconstruction the same music had filled the evenings at Sultanpur, where every night Mardana played while Nanak sang, a practice that became a daily feature "for the rest of their [lives] and also for the Sikh community", with an audience that "included both Hindus and Muslims" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 106, folio 88; p. 107, folio 89).

Khalid's reconstruction brings Nanak back to Lalo more than once. On one journey Nanak and Mardana reached Sialkot "After a long walk from Eminabad where they had stopped to meet with Bhai Lalo" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 185, folio 159); on a later return Nanak "first went to Eminabad, where he met Bhai Lalo" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 189, folio not stated).

Dhillon's retelling also brings the Guru to Saidpur twice. The first time is early in the first of his journeys, from Sultanpur by way of Talwandi (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 56, folio 62). The second is at the end of the fourth, when he came home by Iran, Kabul, Peshawar, Kandahar, Hasan Abdal, Tilla Balnath and Katasraj to Saidpur, and on to Kartarpur (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 224–225, folios 246–247), taking a south-eastern route to the Punjab about the time of Babur's third incursion into India (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 224, folio 246).

In Dhillon's telling of the first visit, Lalo warns Nanak that by staying with him he risks being cast out "from your biradari". Nanak answers that "in God's eyes a man's worth is never measured in terms of his caste or social status or his wealth" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 59, folio 65). Lalo sings a *bhajan* of Kabir as he sweeps (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 62, folio 68), and Nanak asks him to lead "one of Bhagat Kabir's hymns" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 64, folio 70; p. 65, folio 71). At the hut Nanak himself sings, and the villagers gather (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 64, folio 70). Lalo marvels "at the prescience displayed by Nanak" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 63, folio 69).

## Malik Bhago's Feast: The Milk and the Blood

The site's account tells the story as it is remembered: Malik Bhago, a wealthy official of Eminabad, held a great feast, and was affronted when the Guru preferred the carpenter's plain fare; summoned to explain himself, Guru Nanak pressed the two breads, and from Lalo's flowed milk and from Bhago's blood.

Khalid gives the same story as the Janamsakhis tell it. When Nanak squeezed Malik Bhago's bread "blood started to ooze out of it", while Bhai Lalo's bread gave milk, and Malik Bhago, humbled, "realised Nanak's spiritual depth" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 112, folio 94). Khalid himself thinks such miracles were "inserted into the Janamsakhis to give Nanak a larger than-life personality" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 112, folio 94). In his own reconstruction Nanak puts the lesson into words, telling Bhago that his food "is prepared out of the blood of [y]our subjec[ts], while Bhai Lalo's food is cooked in the milk of honesty" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 111–112, folios 93–94).

Khalid's reconstruction also sets the scene of the feast. Malik Bhago lived in a "four-storey haveli known as the Shahi Qilla at Saidpur", was "a minister of the Rai of Saidpur", and was holding a wedding feast for "all Brahmins and members of the high castes" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 109, folio not stated). Writing in his own voice, Khalid says that Nanak's statement that there was no Hindu and no Muslim was questioned at three courts, one of them "at the court of Malik Bhago in Eminabad" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 144, folio 126).

In Dhillon's retelling Malik Bhago scorns what the Guru has eaten as "the coarse bajre ki roti from a wretched carpenter's hovel" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 71, folio 77). Nanak answers that Lalo eats only what "has been earned through hard labour", and that its ingredients are "the milk of human kindness and generosity" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 72, folio 78), while Bhago's twenty-one dishes are "the sweat and blood and tears of the suffering poor" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 71, folio 77). Bhago repents, and "Nanak moved on from Saidpur" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 72–73, folios 78–79).

## The Gurdwara

Iqbal Qaiser's 1998 survey identifies Bhai Lalo as “a carpenter of *Getaura* caste” whose house, once Guru Nanak had lodged there, was made into a gurdwara remembered as Bhai Lalu Di Khoi, the well from which the Guru had drunk still preserved within it. The same source records that a refugee family occupied the building after 1947 until 1989, when Sardar Surjit Singh Panesar purchased and restored it with funds raised by the Sangat abroad, at which time the well was covered over with a platform.

Khalid, citing Qaiser's survey, gives the history in his own words. "a Muslim migrant family" had lived inside the gurdwara; then "In 1989, a Sikh by the name of Sardar Surjit Singh Panesar bought the property of the shrine with money collected by the expat Sikh community and renovated the shrine", and it was opened for worship. Khalid prints the survey's title as *Historical Sikh Shrines of Pais [OCR?] [Pakistan]* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 116, folio 98).

Khalid came to the gurdwara through the town. Old men in a market guided him and Qaiser into an alley: "There was a lock at the entrance of the gurdwara. This was the site of the house of Bhai Lalo." What he found surprised him: "Far from being a worn down place that Nanak had visited, it was now in good shape. This is one of the most popular Sikh shrines in Pakistan and is visited by hundreds of devotees of Nanak." (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 115, folio 97) The lock was not a chance of the day. "This is how the gurdwara is at all times", Qaiser told him, and Khalid guessed that the keys were "probably with members of the Sikh comm[unity]" at the nearby Gurdwara Rori Sahib (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 116, folio 98).

The books consulted record no builder for the gurdwara, no date for its first construction, and no description of its fabric beyond the locked entrance Khalid found.

## Devotion and Pilgrimage

The gurdwara is a place of pilgrimage. At pilgrimage time, Khalid writes, several Sikhs come to "pay tribute to the memory of Guru Nanak and Bhai Lal[o]" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 116, folio 98). As Khalid and Qaiser walked through the town, passers-by wondered whether they were Sikh pilgrims themselves (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 115, folio 97). The site's account adds that pilgrims usually visit it in the same round as the nearby Chakki Sahib and Rori Sahib shrines.

The books speak more widely of the pilgrims who come from abroad. In *A White Trail* Khalid writes that expatriate Sikhs came to Pakistan for festivals "like Baisakhi and the birthday of Guru Nanak, which were attended by thousands of Indian pilgrims" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 214, folio not stated). And he lists Eminabad among the towns whose Sikh communities "Only recently" can "exert themselves in a way that was unthinkable earlier", going to university, many of them with cars of their own; the town's name is misprinted at that point in the book (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 88, folio 70).

The hospitality the shrine remembers is a theme the books return to. Sujan Rai Bhandari, writing in Persian under Aurangzeb, describes the followers of Baba Nanak as counting service to the traveller, to whoever comes and goes, as the highest worship; whoever arrives at midnight speaking Baba Nanak's name they treat as a brother and a friend and serve, even a stranger, a thief, a highwayman or an evildoer (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 519). Of their worship he writes that its sum is the reading of their master's verses, which they sing with song and melody (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 519). "It is believed that the tradition of Langar began with Guru Nanak", Khalid writes in *A White Trail* (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 188, folio not stated).

## In Verse

The poem most closely bound to Bhai Lalo is the Guru's *Babur Bani*. Khalid describes it as "a long poem that Nanak wrote at the time the Mughal marauder Babur attacked Punjab, destroying the cities of Eminabad and Lahore on the way" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 15, folio not stated), and the poem repeatedly addresses Lalo by name:

*O Lalo* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 113–114, folios 95–96)

It was "the first poem of Nanak that I read", Khalid writes, and it was through Iqbal Qaiser's recitation of it that he "truly discovered Guru Nanak": in it Nanak "lays the blame of Babur's wrath at God's feet" and "challenges the divinity of the Divine" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 15–16, folio not stated). Khalid quotes the line that dates the invaders' coming:

*Coming in seventy-eight (1521 C.E)* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 113–114, folios 95–96)

The line goes on to their departure "in Ninety", a year the printed gloss, damaged at that point, gives as 1540 [OCR?] (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 113–114, folios 95–96).

In Dhillon's retelling, the hymn the Guru sings at Lalo's hut opens with the words of the creed, *Ik Onkar Satnam …*, which Dhillon glosses 'there is only one God' (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 64, folio 70).

Khalid quotes the Guru's verse throughout *Walking with Nanak* in published English translations. The lines below are not tied to Eminabad; they are the poetry of the Guru the shrine remembers, sung, in Khalid's telling, to Mardana's *rubab*. The *aarti*, which Iqbal Qaiser reads as placing God "in nature" and which leads him to call Nanak "a naturalist", begins in one translation:

*The sky the salver, the sun and moon the lamps …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 55, folio 37)

and in another:

*In the salver of the firmament, / The sun and the moon shine as lamps* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 243–244, folios 217–218)

On the oneness of God:

*My Master is One / He is One, brother, the only One* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 60, folio 42)

*God is One, / The Path is One* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 144, folio 126)

The poem on the sacred thread, the *janeu*, which in Khalid's reconstruction the boy Nanak refused:

*From the cotton of compassion, / Spin the thread of contentment* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 61–62, folios 43–44)

On washing:

*As hands or feet besmirched with slime, / Water washes white* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 192, folio 166)

Another poem begins:

*O my merchant friend* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 56–58, folio 40)

The song Nanak sings, in Khalid's reconstruction, to the innkeepers who meant to rob him and Mardana:

*The herons in their white feathers dwell in the sacred shrines of pilgrimage* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 123–126, folios 105–108)

Other openings Khalid quotes: *Launch ye the boat of austere contemplation …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 148–149, folios 130–131); *As a beggar goes a-begging …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 158, folio 140); and *His goodness cannot be priced or traded …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 280, folio 254).

Bhandari says that the Guru composed verses in Hindi on the truth of God, setting out divine unity in clear expressions and shining metaphors (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520), and that a singer named Mardana sang the Guru's verses and drew people into the net of faith (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). Shemeem Burney Abbas's glossary defines the *banī* by him: "Devotional verses such as those of Guru Nanak; verses of supplication, entreaty, humility, and reverence" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 179, folio not stated). The verse is sung outside the gurdwaras too: Abbas quotes the singer Abida Parvin, "I sing Shah Hussain, Bulle Shah, Mian Muhammad Bakhsh, Hazrat Khawaja Ghulam Farid, Sacchal Sarmast, Baba Farid, and Guru Nanak" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 54, folio not stated), and in Sindh, Michel Boivin records, "Sain Vasan Shah became famous for reciting the banis of Shri Guru Nanak and the kalams of the Sufis" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 68, folio 55).

## Setting: Eminabad and Saidpur

What follows is about the town, not the gurdwara. Khalid gives the old name and the new: "When Babur took over the city of Saidpur, he renamed it Eminabad." (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 115, folio 97). On the next page he gives a legend of the name: a woman known as Eni offered Babur roasted grain when he had not eaten for days, shortly before he reached Saidpur, and the city was renamed "in her honour" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 116, folio 98). The reading of her name is uncertain in the printed text.

The town suffered in Babur's coming. Khalid writes that Babur destroyed "the cities of Eminabad and Lahore" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 15, folio not stated). Rizvi, writing of the Punjab as a whole, says that the Guru, coming back from eastern India about 1520, found "the Panjab plunged into a number of significant political crises"; Babur, after two minor raids, "invaded a third time over the Panjab in 926/1520 reaching as far as Sialkot" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 409, folio 385). Khalid writes that the Janamsakhis record a meeting between Nanak and Babur at Saidpur, in which Babur asks for a blessing to conquer India and Nanak rebukes him (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 114, folio 96). The encounter "was also interpreted to mark the beginning of this antagonism between the Mughals and the Sikhs" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 97, folio 79), and Khalid later calls Jahangir "the great grandson of Emperor Babur who had incarcerated Guru Nanak" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 133, folio not stated).

Khalid's reconstruction pictures Saidpur in Nanak's day. It had "magnificent houses and havelis" and huge temples crowned with gold tridents, with idols in every shop, Laxmi the most popular and then Ganesh (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 103, folio 85). "Saidpur was an ancient city", its temples said to have been built "thousands of years ago, long before the birth of Islam", and grown into complexes with rooms for pilgrims, leased shops, stables and "an army of servants" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 103, folio 85). There "the Brahmin pandits were the bosses"; offerings were kept, distributed or sold, and guards kept out "naked and starving beggars" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 103–104, folios 85–86). It was a Hindu-majority city with a few Muslim households, its Hindus mostly traders and its Muslims aristocrats; "one of the most important trading centres between the Chenab and the Ravi", with "some of the tallest temples in the Punjab and also the most beautiful courtesans" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 104, folio 86). It was in this city, in Khalid's telling, that Lalo's house was the poorest.

Khalid's Eminabad of today is a town of old temples and faded trade. "The cone like spires of ancient Hindu temples" rise among its houses, "beautiful, in contrast to the monolithic Muslim architecture that now dominates the city of Eminabad", and its dusty roads and worn houses showed that it had lost its economic strength; the top of the page that says so is badly damaged. "Once, it was an important trading centre on the Grand Trunk Road that connected with Lahore and then Delhi"; the highway has since moved south, and Gujranwala, not far away, has replaced Eminabad as a trading centre (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 115, folio 97). Of one temple he writes: "Despite being abandoned in 1947, the temple still looked sturdy"; its paint had worn off and a corrosive layer had formed. Such temples had been taken over by migrants from across the border after the creation of Pakistan, whose descendants stared back at him, and Khalid looked for signs of attack after the destruction of the Babri mosque in 1992 and "found none" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 115, folio 97). He "marvelled at how much of the historical architecture had stil[l] been preserved" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 116, folio 98). After their visit to Eminabad he and Qaiser went on to Sialkot (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 191, folio 165).

Gujranwala, the town that Khalid says replaced Eminabad as a trading centre, is placed by Abbas, with Faisalabad and Sahiwal, in "the heart of the Panjab" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 24, folio not stated). Maharaja Ranjit Singh, Khalid writes in *A White Trail*, started "from a small fief around Gujranwala, a city seventy-five kilometres away from the provincial capital" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 207, folio not stated), and in *Walking with Nanak* that Ranjit Singh allotted Baba Sahib Singh Bedi land "around the cities of Gujranwala and Lahore" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 44, folio 26).

Bhandari's Persian history, composed under Aurangzeb, names a town and *pargana* spelled Aminabad. The book does not connect it with Guru Nanak or with any shrine, and its identification with Eminabad rests on the spelling alone. In one passage Bhandari writes of the creation of the *pargana*, which, he says, had been formed a few years earlier out of Gujrat (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 176). In another, a man in Kashmir who posed as Umar Shaikh Mirza, son of Sulaiman Mirza, was brought before the emperor at the halt of Aminabad and was punished there (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 177). And he places Shah Daula's bridge over the Deg, on the highway five *kos* from Aminabad towards Lahore; the Deg, he writes, passes the villages of the *parganas* of Zafarwal, Pasrur, Aminabad and one more whose printed name, perhaps Hamingar, cannot be read with certainty, before it reaches the bridge (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 515; p. 516).

## Guru Nanak in the Wider Record

What the books say of Guru Nanak himself is said of the Guru, not of this site; it is gathered here because the shrine exists for his sake and for his host's. Khalid gives his dates as 1469–1539 and calls him "the first Guru", who "is believed to be the founder of Sikhism" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 314, folio 288). Dhillon gives the same years and calls the Janamsakhis "cycles of tales purportedly drawn from the life of the first Guru of the Sikhs" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 9, folio 9); he has the Guru born "On 14 April 1469" to Kalyan Chand Das Bedi, called Mehta Kalu (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 17–18, folios 19–20), and dying on 22 September 1539 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 259, folio 287). Bhandari reports, with a "they say", a birth in Samvat 1526, 880 Hijri, in the reign of Sultan Bahlul Lodi, and a death in the reign of Salim Shah Afghan, between the ages of seventy and eighty (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). He calls the Guru the leader of the caravan on the paths of truth and the bearer of the torch on the road of the spiritual way (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520), and places his house twelve *kos* from Batala on the bank of the Ravi, where his descendants still live (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). Khalid writes that Nanak "spent a major portion of his life in present-day Pakistan. He was born here", and "eventually settled in a small village which is now on the Pakistani side of the border" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 16, folio not stated).

Before his travels, Khalid and Dhillon both write, the Guru worked at Sultanpur. Dhillon writes that "Nanak moved to Sultanpur (around 60 km from Talwandi)" as keeper of Nawab Daulat Khan's store, bathing in the Bein and gathering people in the evenings to hear his hymns (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 44, folio 49). Vanishing for a week in the stream, he came back to say: "There is only one God. There is no Hindu, no Mussalman." (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 45–46, folios 50–51). "It is widely believed that it was in Sultanpur that the religious life of Nanak began", Khalid writes (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 54, folio 36). Dhillon has him set out "dressed… in a strange garb - an approximate combination of what the holy men of Hinduism and of Islam would wear" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 53, folio 59).

Dhillon sets out two schemes of the *udasis*, the Guru's journeys. "According to the Puratan Janamsakhi… he undertook five journeys", the first in 1499–1506 and the last in 1523–1524 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 54–55, folios 60–61); "More modern accounts" give four, the first in 1499–1505, spanning twenty-three years in all (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 55, folio 61). Dhillon himself holds that there were four (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 56, folio 62). On the first, in his telling, the Guru was at Haridwar "the day before Baisakhi ... in 1504" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 77, folio 85), facing "west, in the direction of Punjab" to water his fields (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 79, folio 87). Khalid gives the travels as about twenty-four years, mostly on foot (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 54, folio 36), and writes that Nanak and Mardana "would travel thousands of kilometres, that too on foot" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 108, folio 90). Nanak "preferred to stay on the outskirts" of villages, which Khalid calls "a recurrent theme" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 53, folio 35), and "Whenever he would visit an area he would visit its religious shrines, Muslim, Hindu and of any other denomination" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 37, folio 19).

The teaching the Lalo story carries is the one the books ascribe to the Guru at large. Dhillon calls Nanak "a householder in every sense of the word", and in his summary the Guru taught that "all men and women were equal, irrespective of caste, creed, wealth, social standing and gender", and that a good life rested on honest labour, compassion, selfless service and prayer (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 12, folio not stated; pp. 12–13, folio not stated / 13). Khalid writes that he associated with "people of lowercastes, terming them people of God", and criticised "high-caste landlords and government officials as exploiters" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 82, folio 64). His poems, Khalid says, show "a disdain for" priests and Sufi *pirs* "who manipulate the people for their own economic benefit", "a strong concept of individualism", and a wish for "removing intermediaries and establishing a direct connect with God" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 280, folio 254). "Some scholars have argued that the beauty of Nanak's message is that through his own example, he glorified peasantry" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 303, folio 277).

Nanak's advice to his two companions, as Khalid gives it, was that "if one is a Muslim then one should be a good Muslim and if one is a Hindu then one should be a good Hindu" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 95, folio 77); he "offended both Hindus and Muslims by stating that there was no Hindu or Muslim" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 95, folio 77). Bhandari, writing of the Guru's followers, says that their devotion to their leader is seldom found among other groups (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 519).

Several writers set the miracle stories, the milk and the blood among them, against what they take to be the Guru's own view. Dhillon writes of "Guru Nanak's clear and emphatic disavowal of the miraculous" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 11, folio 11), and in his retelling Nanak tells Karori, who calls his cure a miracle, that "there is no such thing as a miracle" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 190, folio 209). Khalid writes that Nanak "abhorred the concept of associating miracles with religious personalities", yet "his biography is nothing but a story of his miracles" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 16, folio not stated); that he "was repulsed by the concept of making a larger-than-life figure out of a religious leader" and "comes across as a rationalist" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 112, folio 94); and that "His genius lies in the poetic use of words and not in miracles" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 199, folio 173). Of the evidence he writes: "The only evidence we have is found within the Sikh religion and they are full of stories of miracles"; Qaiser's counsel was to "read what Nanak wrote at these places and try to get into his mind" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 286, folio 260).

On his deathbed, Khalid writes, the Guru "appointed not his son but his most loyal student as his spiritual successor" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 16, folio not stated), the disciple who became famous as Guru Angad (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 100, folio 82). Bhandari tells the same: the Guru passed over his worthy son Lakhmi Das because spiritual fortune was not his, and at the hour of death gave Lehna the title of Guru Angad (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). Dhillon names the successor "Guru Angad Dev" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 255, folio 282).

Muslim writers and singers have claimed the Guru too. Khalid writes that "many Muslims, acquainted with the teachings of Guru Nanak, are devoted to him too and visit his shrine" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 220, folio not stated), and his informant Nadeem, a Muslim painter, says "Guru Nanak is like a Muslim Sufi Saint" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 219, folio not stated). A Muslim storyteller at Gurdwara Sachkand called him "a true devotee of God. He was a rightly guided soul" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 75, folio 57). A Muslim tradition, reported by Iqbal Qaiser, holds that "Guru Nanak was born by the blessing of Baba Naulakh Hazari"; "The Sikhs don't accept it" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated). Baba Farid "became an inspiration for Guru Nanak when he visited the shrine during his travels" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated), and Abbas writes that Farid's "texts are revered among the Sikhs because he influenced the founder of their own religion, Baba Guru Nanak" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 87, folio not stated). Rizvi argues against the view that the Sufism the Guru met was debased, holding that "all literate Panjabis, both Hindus and Muslims, at the time of Guru Nanak were conversant with" the Persian poets 'Attar, Rumi and Sa'di (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 420, folio 396). Robert Rozehnal reports that a twentieth-century Chishti Sabiri shaykh, in certain lectures, describes "non-Muslim spiritual luminaries—Krishna, Rama, Sita, Lachman, and Guru Nanak—as believers in the one true God and religious leaders with 'Divine connection' (nisbat)" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 63, folio 53). Omar Kasmani, citing Boivin, notes that the trope of the flower in milk is told also of "Guru Nanak, the spiritual leader and founder of Sikhism" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 195, folio 178, ch. 5 n. 17). At the shrine of the goddess Hinglaj, Jürgen Schaflechner records "the engraved footsteps of Guru Nanak" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 256, folio not stated).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **Whether Saidpur is Eminabad.** The site's account writes "Saidpur (Eminabad)", and Khalid identifies the two: "When Babur took over the city of Saidpur, he renamed it Eminabad." (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 115, folio 97). Dhillon, who tells Lalo's story at Saidpur, says only that Saidpur is "in Pakistan" and does not name Eminabad (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 54, folio 60). Bhandari's Aminabad is tied to Eminabad by its spelling alone (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 176).
- **How the town got its name.** Khalid gives two accounts: that Babur renamed Saidpur on taking it (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 115, folio 97), and that the city was renamed in honour of a woman, Eni, who fed Babur roasted grain before he reached Saidpur (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 116, folio 98).
- **When the Guru came.** The site's account says the early sixteenth century. Dhillon brings him to Saidpur early in his first journey (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 56, folio 62), which the Puratan Janamsakhi dates 1499–1506 and more modern accounts 1499–1505 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 54–55, folios 60–61), and on which Dhillon has him at Haridwar in 1504 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 77, folio 85); and again at the end of the fourth, about the time of Babur's third incursion, with no year given (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 224–225, folios 246–247). Khalid's reconstruction makes Saidpur the first goal of the travels (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 108, folio 90) and brings Nanak back to Lalo later, giving no year (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 185, folio 159; p. 189, folio not stated).
- **Lalo's house.** The site's account says his house grew into a *dharamsala*, a gathering-place for the Guru's followers. Khalid's reconstruction calls it the poorest house in the city (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 102, 104, folios 84, 86). Dhillon's retelling puts it in a single-room thatched shanty on the outskirts of Saidpur (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 57, folio 63).
- **How Lalo's caste is described.** The site's account, after Iqbal Qaiser's 1998 survey, calls him a carpenter of *Getaura* caste. Qaiser, speaking to Khalid, says he was of the carpenter caste (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 117, folio 99). Dhillon calls him a low-caste carpenter (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 57, folio 63).
- **The milk and the blood.** The site's account tells the miracle as remembered: the Guru pressed the two breads, and milk flowed from Lalo's and blood from Bhago's. Khalid reports the same as the Janamsakhis' story (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 112, folio 94), and holds that such miracles were inserted into the Janamsakhis (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 112, folio 94). In Khalid's reconstruction Nanak puts it into words: Bhago's food is made from "the blood of [y]our subjec[ts]", Lalo's "cooked in the milk of honesty" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 111–112, folios 93–94). The sense given to the sign differs too. The site's account says Lalo's food was "earned by the sweat of honest toil"; in Dhillon's retelling Lalo's food is made of "the milk of human kindness and generosity", and it is Bhago's dishes that are "the sweat and blood and tears of the suffering poor" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 72, folio 78; p. 71, folio 77).
- **The building after Partition.** The site's account says in one place that since Partition the gurdwara has been maintained as part of Pakistan's Sikh heritage, and in another, after Qaiser's survey, that a refugee family occupied it from 1947 until 1989. Khalid, citing Qaiser, says that "a Muslim migrant family" had lived inside it before it was bought in 1989 (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 116, folio 98).
- **The well.** The site's account says in one place that the gurdwara is named "for the well that still survives", and that the well from which the Guru drank is still preserved within it; in another, after Qaiser's survey, that the well was covered over with a platform at the restoration of 1989.
- **The names of the shrine and of its source.** The site's account, after Qaiser's 1998 survey, gives the gurdwara's name as Bhai Lalu Di Khoi; Khalid writes "Bhai Lalo di Khoi"; the shrine's own name is spelled Khoohi and Khooi. The site's bibliography gives Qaiser's title as *Historical Sikh Shrines in Pakistan*; Khalid prints it as *Historical Sikh Shrines of Pais [OCR?] [Pakistan]* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 116, folio 98).
- **When Babur came.** Rizvi dates Babur's third invasion, which reached as far as Sialkot, to 926/1520 (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 409, folio 385). The line of the *Babur Bani* that Khalid quotes glosses the invaders' coming as 1521 (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 113–114, folios 95–96). Dhillon gives no year (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 224, folio 246).
- **The Guru's dates.** The site's account speaks only of the early sixteenth century. Khalid gives 1469–1539 (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 314, folio 288); Dhillon a birth on 14 April 1469 and a death on 22 September 1539 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 17–18, folios 19–20; p. 259, folio 287). Bhandari gives the birth in Samvat 1526, 880 Hijri, in the reign of Bahlul Lodi, and the death under Salim Shah Afghan, between the ages of seventy and eighty (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).
- **How many journeys.** Dhillon reports five *udasis* in the Puratan Janamsakhi and four in more modern accounts, and holds to four himself (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 54–55, folios 60–61; p. 56, folio 62). Khalid gives the travels as about twenty-four years (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 54, folio 36); Dhillon's modern scheme spans twenty-three (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 55, folio 61).

## Legacy

Gurdwara Khoohi Bhai Lalo keeps the memory of a carpenter's house in which, as the stories tell it, the founder of the Sikh faith chose honest bread over a rich man's table. Khalid found it "one of the most popular Sikh shrines in Pakistan", visited by "hundreds of devotees of Nanak" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 115, folio 97), and he counts the meeting it remembers as one of the most important in the Janamsakhis (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 112, folio 94). Lalo's name lives on, too, in the Guru's own lament for Babur's invasion, which turns again and again to "O Lalo" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 113–114, folios 95–96).

Its survival was not assured. From 1947 until 1989, the site's account says, a refugee family lived in the house; then Sardar Surjit Singh Panesar bought and restored it with money raised by Sikhs abroad (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 116, folio 98). Of other such places Khalid is less hopeful. Writing of another shrine in the province, Ram Thamman, he says that without the family that kept it, it "would have also been taken over by the refugees and converted into a household, like the hundreds of temples and Gurdwaras all over Pakistani Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 58, folio not stated); and of the country at large, "I have seen several gurdwaras and temples being taken over and destroyed by the migrants" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated). Yet he writes too that "there are several sites all over the country where Nanak stayed or performed miracles. Gurdwaras that were built there later by the devotees still stand tall" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 168–169, folio not stated).
