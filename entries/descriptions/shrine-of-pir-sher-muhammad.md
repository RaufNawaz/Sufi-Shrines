## Overview

At Sharaqpur Sharif, north-west of Lahore, stands the shrine of Hazrat Mian Pir Sher Muhammad Sharaqpuri (1865–1928) — a widely revered Sufi master of the Qadiri-Chishti tradition and one of the notable saints of the Punjab in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Known for his piety, his learning, and the great number of disciples he guided, Pir Sher Muhammad made Sharaqpur a centre of devotion and religious teaching, and his darbar remains an important place of pilgrimage in central Punjab.

None of the books consulted here mentions the shrine or Pir Sher Muhammad himself. What they offer is the town and the province. Two of them name Sharaqpur: Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori's *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, whose only saint of Sharaqpur is a different and much earlier man (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 121–122, folios 109–110), and Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti's *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, which mentions a grant of land in the Sharaqpur area (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 870). The rest of what follows from the books concerns the Punjab at large, the province in which the shrine stands, and is given as such: it describes the Sufi orders, the shrines and the poetry of the region, not this shrine.

## The Life of the Saint

Mian Sher Muhammad was born in 1865 at Sharaqpur into a devout family, and from his youth turned to the religious sciences and the Sufi path. Trained in both the outward learning of the seminary and the inward discipline of the orders, he became a spiritual guide of great reputation, uniting the Qadiri and Chishti lineages, and drew disciples from across the Punjab and beyond.

He was among the teachers whose light shaped the next generation of Punjabi Sufis — the saint Abul Faiz Qalandar Ali Suhrawardi of Lahore, for instance, is remembered as having received knowledge from him. He is remembered for a life of prayer, austerity, and service, and for the reforming, sober devotion he taught. He died in 1928 and was buried at Sharaqpur.

The books consulted here record none of this. They give no year of his birth or death, no teacher, no line of succession and no disciple, and the account above is the site's own.

## The Shrine and its Devotion

The shrine of Pir Sher Muhammad at Sharaqpur Sharif is a large complex kept by his successors, and a busy centre of pilgrimage. It maintains the five daily prayers, gatherings of *naat* and qawwali, and the free kitchen, and its annual *ʿurs* draws great crowds of devotees who come to honour the saint and seek his blessing.

The town of Sharaqpur Sharif takes its very identity from the darbar, and the saint's line has continued as its custodians and as respected religious figures.

The sources consulted do not record when or by whom the tomb was built, the date of the *ʿurs*, or the names of the custodians.

## Sharaqpur in the Books

Lahori's *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, composed in 1875, lists Sharaqpur in its index of places at two places in the book, "شرقپور | ۱۰۹ ، ۱۴۴" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 334, folio 322). Neither names Pir Sher Muhammad.

The first is the notice, entry no. 74, of Shaikh Muhammad Saeed Chishti Sabri Sharaqpuri, whose tomb the book places in Sharaqpur and whose death it gives as 1214 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 121–122, folios 109–110). The book's index lists him as "محمد سعید صابری شرقپوری | ۱۰۹" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 328, folio 316). He is not the saint of this shrine: he is a Chishti Sabiri of the same town, and his death and Pir Sher Muhammad's, which the site's account places in 1928, fall more than a century apart (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 121–122, folios 109–110).

The second mention is on a page that also names Makan Sharif, Rawalpindi, Peshawar and Gurdaspur, and on which Sharaqpur appears as the place of publication of a work cited there, the *Khazina-e Marifat* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 156, folio 144). Nothing on that page concerns a shrine.

The bibliography of the modern edition ties the town to the book twice more. One item is a manuscript of Lal Beg Lali's *Thamarat al-Quds* held at Sharaqpur, "لعلی، لعل بیگ : ثمرات القدس (لبعد ۱۰۱۷ھ) مملوکہ مولانا نصرت نوشاہی شرقپور پاکستان", in the possession of Maulana Nusrat Naushahi of Sharaqpur (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 294, folio 282). The other is the same *Khazina-e Marifat*, by Muhammad Ibrahim Qasuri, "محمد ابراہیم قصوری : خزینۂ معرفت شرقپور (س۔ن)", printed at Sharaqpur and undated (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 303, folio 291).

Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti's *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* names the Sharaqpur area once. Rani Jindan, it says, gave Bhai Budh Singh a jagir in the Sharaqpur area, "مبلغ پندرہ [OCR?] سو روپیہ کی جاگیر علاقہ شرقپور مین", to feed travellers at the samadhi of Jawahar Singh (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 870). The reading of the sum is uncertain in the printed text. The grant has nothing to do with the shrine.

## Setting: The Sufi Orders in the Punjab

The site's account places Pir Sher Muhammad in the Qadiri and Chishti traditions. The books say nothing of him, but several speak of those orders in the Punjab, the province as a whole.

Lahori wrote his *Hadiqat al-Awliya* for readers who wanted the saints of the Punjab in a short Urdu book, and he defined his Punjab as the whole country "دہلی سے پشاور تک جس قدر علاقہ اس وقت پنجاب کے ساتھ متعلق ہے", from Delhi to Peshawar, as it stood in his own day (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 37, folio 25). Its modern editor counts in it the lives of 244 Sufis of the Punjab, the figure uncertain in the printed text, in seven sections (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 31, folio 19).

Of the Qadiri line, Lahori writes that the first of it to come to the Punjab was Sayyid Muhammad Ghaus of Uch: "حضرت کے سلسلہ میں سے حضرت سید محمد غوث اوچی جیلی گیلانی اول پنجاب میں آئے". The same passage has him come by way of Khurasan to Multan and settle at Uch, after his ancestors left Baghdad in the time of Hulagu Khan; Lahori gives no year, and the year in the modern editor's footnote, "بقول علی اصغر گیلانی آپ ۸۸۷ھ میں اوچ میں داخل ہوئے", is the editor's (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 40, folio 28). Lahori writes that the Naushahi order spread in the Punjab through Shah Ma'ruf Chishti and Qadiri, or through Sayyid Mubarak Haqqani, named just before him; the text does not settle which (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 50, folio 38). Of Hazrat Naushah Ganj Bakhsh, he says: "طریقہ نوشاہیہ جس کے فقیر پنجاب میں ہزاروں ہیں۔ ان سے شروع ہوا", the Naushahi way, whose faqirs in the Punjab number in the thousands, began with him (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 70, folio 58). Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi, writing of the time of Guru Nanak, speaks of "the Qadiris who had made the Panjab the main centre of their activities" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 420, folio 396).

Of the Chishti order, Lahori opens his account with the statement "یہ سلسلہ ہند اور پنجاب میں حضرت خواجہ معین الدین حسن سجزی سے شائع ہوا", that it spread in Hind and the Punjab from Khwaja Muin al-Din Hasan Sijzi (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 85, folio 73). The modern editor adds in a footnote that its Nizami Fakhri branch spread in the Punjab chiefly through Khwaja Sulaiman Taunsvi: "پنجاب میں سلسلہ چشتیہ نظامیہ فخریہ کی زیادہ اشاعت حضرت خواجہ سلیمان تونسوی کے ذریعہ ہوئی" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 120, folio 108). Carl W. Ernst and Bruce B. Lawrence write that "The Nizami Chishtis came to prominence in the eighteenth century, principally in Delhi, the Deccan, and Punjab, through masters who were related to but in tension with local political leaders" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 119, folio 106). Taunsawi, they say, "consolidated the order by stressing learning through establishment of institutions", the academy at Taunsa and "other religious schools throughout the Punjab" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, pp. 121–122, folios 108–109); and in the nineteenth century the line resurfaces, "marking the expansion of the Nizami branch of Chishti Sufism through disciples of Shaykh Sulayman Taunsawi in the upper Punjab" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 71, folio 57). Michel Boivin writes that "The Chishtiyya is usually considered to be one of the most powerful Sufi orders in Northern India and Punjab" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 227, folio 214).

Richard M. Eaton observes that "Islam took root much more readily in Bengal or the Punjab than in upper India" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 14, folio 2). Christopher Shackle writes that the Muslim conquest of northern India carried the Persianate world to the Punjab, "where Persian remained the dominant literary language of the ruling elite through the Mughal period and beyond", and that a strong Sufi presence was quickly established there with the coming of figures of the main Sufi orders (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 11, folio x).

## Setting: The Punjab in the Saint's Century

The site's account sets Pir Sher Muhammad's life between 1865 and 1928. The books say a good deal about the Punjab of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, none of it about Sharaqpur.

Nur Ahmad Chishti records the British annexation: "۲۹[OCR?] مارچ سنہ ۱۸۴۹[OCR?]ء تمام ملک سلطنت انگریزی مین داخل ہو گیا", with the day and the year uncertain in the printed text; the book says "تمام ملک", the whole country, not the Punjab (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 592). Kanhaiya Lal, writing in 1884, calls Lahore "صوبہ پنجاب کا دار الحکومت", the capital of the province of the Punjab, where the Lieutenant-Governor held his court (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 60, folio 63); in the Sikh period, he says, no printed book came into the Punjab from Hindustan, and scribes copied books by hand for sale (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 42, folio 45).

Shackle writes that "Only with the British conquest of Panjab in the 1840s did the literary culture of the Sufi tradition come to be overlaid by new patterns of modernity": Persian gave way to Urdu and English, and publishers in Lahore brought the older Sufi poetry into print (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, pp. 11–12, folios x–xi). The lyrics of the Punjab's Sufi poets, he notes, were for the most part collected in printed form only in the late nineteenth century (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, pp. 9–10, folios viii–ix). Annemarie Schimmel writes that "In the Punjab, the literary heritage of Panjabi was rarely continued by Muslims after 1800", though "in the twentieth century a number of mystical treatises have been written and published in Panjabi"; the Panjabi Muslims, she says, turned to Urdu (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 427, folio 396).

Robert Rozehnal quotes Katherine Ewing on the colonial treatment of the hereditary saints: "British policy, especially in the Punjab and Sind, was to treat them in essentially the same way that landlords and tribal leaders were treated" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 33, folio 23). Jamal Malik, writing in the volume edited by Pnina Werbner and Helene Basu, says of the countryside of Punjab and Sindh: "In many cases, the local pîr represents the local landlord or is at least closely related to him" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 199, folio 188). Eaton quotes Major Aubrey O'Brien's "The Mohammedan Saints of the Western Punjab", of 1911, on the riverain folk: "Thus, all human beings require an intervener between them and God" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 216, folio 204).

Haroon Khalid describes the canal colonies of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, when "the British connected the five rivers of Punjab with each other through canals, turning several acres of jungle land into agricultural land", thousands of new villages were founded, and "farming families from all over Punjab were invited to settle" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated). He writes too that in the early twentieth century Muhammad Ilyas, founder of the Tableeghi Jamaat, "worked in undivided rural Punjab, exhorting Muslims to 'purify' their religious practices which he argued were corrupted due to the influence of the dominant Hindu culture" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 10, ch. 4, folio not stated).

## Setting: Devotion at the Shrines of the Punjab

The site's account speaks of *naat*, qawwali and the free kitchen at Sharaqpur. The books describe the music and practice of the Punjab's shrines in general terms. Regula Burckhardt Qureshi distinguishes the qawwali tunes of the Punjab from those of Uttar Pradesh by their rhythm: "Panjabi tunes tend to be rhythmically organized according to 'quality' or stress" (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 73, folio 53). Ernst and Lawrence write that "The name Qalandar continues to be intoned in popular qawwali lyrics of the Punjab" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 36, folio 22). Shemeem Burney Abbas defines *dhammāl* as "The vigorous ritual dance performed at Sufi shrines in Panjab and Sind" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 180, folio not stated).

Eaton writes that the writing of *taʿwidh*, amulets, for the people has not changed since the thirteenth century: "even now the successors of Baba Farid and his assistants, in common with those of other shrines in the Punjab, continue to write ta'widh for the masses" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 218, folio 206). And Schimmel notes that the Punjab and Sind "have also produced hymns extolling the great masters of the mystical orders", particularly ʿAbd al-Qadir Gilani (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 417, folio 386).

## The Punjab in Verse

No verse by or about Pir Sher Muhammad, and none about Sharaqpur, appears in the books consulted here. They do carry verse about the Punjab, the province in which the shrine stands.

Lahori's *Hadiqat al-Awliya* closes with chronograms giving the year of its completion, 1292 AH. One, by the author's son Mufti Ghulam Safdar Lahori, is on the book's scope, the saints of the Punjab:

جو خدا کے دوست تھے پنجاب میں
حال ان کا اس سے ہوتا ہے عیان

The lines are quoted as the book prints them, without a translation (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 282, folio 270).

Waris Shah's *Hir and Ranjha*, in Charles Frederick Usborne's English, opens with an invocation that praises Baba Farid of Pakpattan:

*When Shakarganj made his abode at Pakpattan, the Punjab was delivered of all its troubles.* (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 2, folio 1)

In the epilogue, on the page where the poet dates his poem to the year 1180 Hijri, Waris Shah laments the disorder of the country and turns to a single town:

*Of all the wretched Punjab I am most concerned for Kasur.* (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 56, folio 55)

Bulleh Shah's verse names the Punjab of the eighteenth century in the same troubled terms. In one *kafi*, in Shackle's translation, the twelfth century, which his note takes as the twelfth century of the Hijri era, 'has come with gaping mouth':

*The door of doom and torment has opened, and Panjab is in a bad state. It has been struck by fear of the pit of hell.* (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 52, folio 27)

Shackle's note reads the poem as referring to a separation of Bulleh Shah from Shah Inayat "due to the troubled conditions then prevailing in Panjab" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, pp. 165–166, folios 140–141). Taufiq Rafat renders another poem:

*Now hell has opened its bogs. / Punjab has gone to the dogs. / This hell is the nethermost hell.* (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 149, folio 129)

Nur Ahmad Chishti's account of the end of Sikh rule carries a *qitʿa* on the departure of Maharaja Dalip Singh, with the lines "چون شمع[OCR?] پنجاب از پنجاب رفت" and "نو گلی از گلشن پنجاب رفت"; the reading of one word is uncertain in the printed text, and the book gives no translation (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 592).

## Where the Sources Disagree

None of the books consulted mentions Pir Sher Muhammad or his shrine, so none of them disputes anything the site's account says of him. The disagreements below concern the province only.

- **Whether the Punjab's troubles ended with Baba Farid.** Waris Shah's verse says that when Shakarganj settled at Pakpattan "the Punjab was delivered of all its troubles". The book's later annotator replies: "Much as one would like to agree with Waris Shah this is not historically correct. The Punjab has had plenty of troubles even after Baba Farid honoured it by making it his home and his place of burial!" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 2, folio 1).
- **The Sufism of the Punjab in Guru Nanak's time.** W. H. McLeod, as Rizvi quotes him, held that "the Panjabi sufism of Guru Nanak's period had evidently departed radically from the classical pattern of Arab and Persian Sufism" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 419–420, folios 395–396). Rizvi answers that "all literate Panjabis, both Hindus and Muslims, at the time of Guru Nanak were conversant with" ʿAttar, Rumi and Saʿdi, and that McLeod "seems to be unaware of" Shaikh ʿAbd al-Quddus Gangohi, his disciple Shaikh Jalal of Thaneswar, and "the Qadiris who had made the Panjab the main centre of their activities" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 420, folio 396).

## Legacy

Pir Sher Muhammad Sharaqpuri is remembered as one of the influential Sufi masters of modern Punjab — a guide of many thousands whose teaching joined the Qadiri and Chishti ways and whose disciples carried his influence onward. His shrine at Sharaqpur Sharif remains a centre of the devotion he inspired, and his memory endures among the notable saints of the Lahore region.
