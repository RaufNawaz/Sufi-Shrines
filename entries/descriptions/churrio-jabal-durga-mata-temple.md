## Overview

The Churrio Jabal Durga Mata Temple crowns a rocky hill in the Nagarparkar region of Tharparkar District, in the far south-eastern corner of Sindh, amid the ancient granite of the *Karoonjhar* mountains. Dedicated to the goddess *Durga*, it is one of the most important Hindu pilgrimage sites in Pakistan, a place where the goddess is venerated as the protectress against evil and where the faithful come to immerse the ashes of their dead in waters held to be sacred. Set in a desert landscape of great beauty and antiquity, the temple is a living centre of devotion for the substantial Hindu population that still inhabits Tharparkar, one of the few districts of Pakistan where Hindus remain a large share of the people.

None of the books consulted for this article names the temple, Churrio Hill, Nagarparkar or the Karoonjhar range. Haroon Khalid's *A White Trail*, a journey among Pakistan's religious minorities, writes at length of the goddess and of the devotion paid to her in Punjab, and it calls her by many names, but it never names this site; the Durga temple it describes is at Bahawalnagar. The other books name Durga only in passing: as an image of a lion-riding goddess, a point of comparison, the deity of a pilgrim's youth, or the goddess of temples in Kashmir and of Hinglaj. What the books do carry is set out below as it stands: what they say of Durga herself, of the worship given her elsewhere, and, under Setting, of the Hindus of Sindh and of the old province of Thatta. None of it is said of this temple.

## Setting and Name

The temple stands upon *Churrio* Hill, one of the outcrops of the Karoonjhar range that rises abruptly from the Thar desert near the town of Nagarparkar. The name is a Sindhi word: *churrio* derives from the term for a *bangle*, and the surrounding villages are said to have been long associated with the craft of making the glass and lac bangles worn by the women of the region — so that the hill and its temple carry, in their very name, the memory of that local trade. The rugged Karoonjhar mountains in which the temple sits are themselves objects of reverence and pride, celebrated in Sindhi poetry and regarded as a natural and cultural treasure of the province.

The books consulted do not explain the name, and none quotes the Sindhi poetry of the Karoonjhar.

## Pilgrimage and Devotion

Churrio Jabal is above all a place of pilgrimage. Its greatest festival is *Maha Shivratri*, kept in the spring, when the temple draws enormous crowds — by some estimates as many as two hundred thousand pilgrims — who come from across Sindh and from Balochistan, Punjab, and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa for night-long vigils, the singing of *bhajans*, and the performance of rites at the shrine. A central act of devotion here is the immersion of the cremated ashes of the dead in the sacred waters associated with the site, a rite that makes Churrio Jabal a place of last farewells as well as of festival joy, and that binds families across the region to the goddess of the hill. Through the year, pilgrims climb the stone paths to the summit temple to offer worship to Durga and to seek her protection.

The books consulted record none of these rites at this temple. They do not mention its Maha Shivratri gathering, its pilgrims' numbers or the immersion of ashes there.

## History and Conservation

The precise age of the temple is not securely documented, and it is best described as ancient in the broad sense, its foundations lost in the long Hindu history of the Thar. What is certain is that, unlike so many temples elsewhere in Pakistan, Churrio Jabal survived the upheavals of Partition as a functioning place of worship, sustained by the large Hindu community of Tharparkar, where Hindus have continued to form a substantial portion — by many accounts approaching half — of the population. In recent years the site has become a focus of conservation concern, above all because of the threat that stone-quarrying and mining posed to the Karoonjhar range; in October 2023 the Sindh High Court declared the whole of the Karoonjhar mountains a protected monument under provincial law, prohibiting mining and directing the safeguarding of the area's cultural and ecological heritage, including its temples.

The books consulted give no date for the temple and name no founder, priest or custodian.

## The Goddess in the Books

The books that name Durga speak of the goddess, never of this temple.

**Her names and her mount.** Khalid's *A White Trail* calls her Durga Mata, Mata Durga and Sheranwali, and explains the last: "Durga is also known as Sheranwali or the 'One with the Tiger.' This is because she rides a tiger" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 32, folio not stated). Among the deity pictures in a Valmiki temple he notes "a picture of Durga, sitting on a tiger" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 80, folio not stated). In his *Walking with Nanak* the goddess's mount is a lion. At the Naulakha Hazari mound, Khalid and Iqbal Qaiser "concluded that this must have been a shrine dedicated to the Hindu goddess, Durga", because in the folk story Baba Naulakha Hazari "used to ride a lion, like Durga does in Hindu iconography" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 32, folio 14).

**Her forms.** *A White Trail* counts other goddesses as forms of Durga. Kali Mata is "an incarnation of Durga" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 39, folio not stated), and at Hinglaj the temple "honours an incarnation of Durga" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 32, folio not stated). Navratri, Khalid writes, "is a celebration associated with the worship of nine different incarnations of the goddess"; according to the priest Amog Lila, "One of her incarnations is of an adolescent girl. The Kunwari pooja is a worshipping of that incarnation" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 40, folio not stated). At Katas Raj, *Walking with Nanak* writes of "temples dedicated to the goddesses Kali and Durga" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 236, folio 210).

**A pilgrim of Durga.** Harish Dhillon's *Janamsakhis*, retelling the life of Guru Nanak, records that Lehna, before he became Nanak's devotee, "had ... been a worshipper of the Goddess Durga", and led pilgrims each year to Jwalamukhi, "now in Himachal Pradesh", during "the Navratras" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 246–247, folios 273–274). The place is in India, and the book ties the goddess to no site in Pakistan.

**Durga in a Persian history.** Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, composed in the reign of Aurangzeb, names Durga as the goddess of temples in its account of Kashmir and of Hinglaj in its account of Thatta. In Kashmir it describes a stone temple on the banks of a river whose printed name, perhaps Padmani, cannot be read with certainty, a river that comes from a district printed perhaps as Dakdu (the editor's footnote reads perhaps Dar Du); the temple, whose printed name is perhaps Asmaru, is ascribed to Durga, the book writes, and held to be very great (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 507). The reading of the river's, the district's and the temple's names is uncertain in the printed text. A second Kashmir temple, whose printed name is perhaps Pahnal, the book calls an idol-temple ascribed to Durga (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 508). The editor's footnote on that name cannot be read, being "covered by the source stamp" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 508). Hinglaj the book calls a place attributed to Durga (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529). The same book also names a goddess Bhavani at Nagarkot (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 518) and at Trikuta (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 512), but it does not equate her with Durga.

## Devotion to Durga Elsewhere

The worship of Durga that the books describe is never set in Tharparkar.

**Navratri.** *A White Trail* calls Navratri "a festival dedicated to Durga" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 31, folio not stated). Khalid's *In Search of Shiva* describes it as the festival "for which devotees stay up for nine consecutive nights praying to the goddess Durga" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated).

**Offerings of cloth.** Hindu devotees, Khalid writes, offer the goddess a head-cloth. In *In Search of Shiva*, Muslim groups at Ram Thamman present "chaddars at the shrine, like Hindus offer chunnis at the shrine of Durga" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated). In *A White Trail*, "Durga Mata is offered chunries bought from in and around the shrine" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 89, folio not stated). Neither book names the shrine it has in mind.

**Pilgrimage and its wishes.** Of the festival at Maryabad, *A White Trail* finds it "similar in many ways to the pilgrimage to the shrine of Durga during the festival of Navratri"; Hindu pilgrims call Durga 'Mata' and wear uniform shirts (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 88, folio not stated). At Maryabad, "Mother Mary takes the place of Mata Durga and the shawl replaces the chunri", and Hindu devotees believe "that by travelling to the shrine of Durga Mata all their wishes would come true" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 89, folio not stated). The shrine of Durga in this passage is not named.

**A Muslim Navratri.** In *In Search of Shiva*, Shia women from Chiniot perform 'Nauratre' at the shrine of Behlol, and Khalid argues: "This is a Muslim version of the Hindu festival Navratri for which devotees stay up for nine consecutive nights praying to the goddess Durga. The goddess is replaced by a saint here" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated).

**Her name called aloud.** The books carry no verse about Durga, but *A White Trail* records the call-and-response with which her name is raised at Holi in Multan. At the breaking of the *matka*, the boys shout 'Bolo bolo bolo Durga Mata ki' and the crowd answers 'Jai'; then 'Bolo Sheranwali ki', and again 'Jai' (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 26, folio not stated). The cry returns later in the same account: "Bolo bolo bolo Durga Mata ki." (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 29, folio not stated).

## Ordeals and Wonders at Durga's Temples in Kashmir

Bhandari's *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* relates two customs and one wonder at the Kashmir temples it ascribes to Durga. They are told of those temples, not of this one.

At the temple whose printed name is perhaps Pahnal (the reading is uncertain), a person seeking news of himself and of an enemy places two vessels of cooked rice inside, one for each; in the morning, the one whose vessel is covered with flowers and saffron will prosper, and the one whose vessel is full of straw and rubbish will be ruined (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 508). In a dispute where the right is hard to tell, each party is given a bird or a goat, and both animals are poisoned; the animal of the party in the right lives, and the other dies (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 508).

Of the temple whose printed name is perhaps Asmaru, the book says that on every *ashtami* a form comes into motion there (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 507). Two words of the line, the name of the day and the word for the form (printed perhaps as *machchh*), are uncertain in the printed text.

## Setting

The books say something of the Hindus of Sindh and a good deal about the old Mughal province of Thatta, and nothing about Tharparkar, Nagarparkar or the Karoonjhar. What follows is said of the province.

**The Hindus of Sindh today.** Khalid's *A White Trail* writes that most of Pakistan's remaining non-Muslims "are Hindus living in the 'faraway' 'peripheral' regions of Southern Punjab, inner Sindh and Baluchistan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 12, folio not stated). He cites the census: "according to the census of 1998, the Hindu minority is the largest minority in the country, with Christians in the second place, most of the Hindus are scattered in Sindh, Baluchistan, and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 87, folio not stated). In his Afterword he adds: "The majority of the Hindu community is based in Sindh and Southern Punjab, where they have a proportionate representation from the lower and the upper castes"; there they "are a part of the social fabric of the area", though he could not "delve into much detail about the experiences of the community from these regions" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 233, folio not stated). After the demolition of the Babri Mosque on "6th of December 1992", he records, "Hindu communities in Sindh and Quetta were attacked" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 22, folio not stated).

**Nanak Panthis.** Khalid's books describe a community between Hindu and Sikh in the province. Nanak Panthi Sikhs "belong to the areas of South Punjab and Sindh"; their temples there hold portraits of Guru Nanak alongside Hindu deities, and "They are found in very large numbers in the province of Sindh" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 197, folio not stated). *Walking with Nanak* counts "thousands of such Sikhs" living "in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Punjab and Sindh in Pakistan" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 98, folio 80), and writes that the followers of Guru Nanak who refused the Khalsa "still exist in Sindh and in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa. Baptised Sikhs look down upon them" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 230, folio 204).

**Shrines shared and contested.** In *In Search of Shiva*, an informant, Mussadiq, speaks of "several shrines in the Multan region and in Sindh where people still practise Hinduism and give it the name of Islam" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 10, ch. 4, folio not stated). Khalid argues that Tableeghi preachers "focus on southern Punjab and rural Sindh where syncretistic religion held sway" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 10, ch. 4, folio not stated), and cites Tahir Kamran that "the focus of the Deoband has been on South Punjab and Sindh where there is an ubiquitous saint and shrine culture" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated).

**The province of Thatta.** Bhandari's *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, written in Aurangzeb's reign, describes the Mughal province (*suba*) of Thatta (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 530). It reckons its length at two hundred and fifty *kos*, from Bhakkar to Kech Makran, and its breadth at a hundred *kos*, from the town of Badin to the port of Lahri; on the east it bordered Gujarat Ahmadabad (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529). Its four districts (*sarkars*) were Thatta, Siwistan, Nasirpur and Amarkot, comprising fifty-seven *mahals* and five ports, and the province's revenue the book gives as ten *krors*, forty-nine *lakhs* and seventy thousand *dams*, though the words for "ten" and "*kror*" are uncertain in the printed text (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 528–529). South of the line from Uch to Gujarat, the book writes, lay sand hills, the country of the Bhatti, whose chief's seat was Jaisalmer, and of other Rajput peoples; from Bhakkar to Nasirpur and Amarkot lived the Jareja and a people whose printed name, perhaps Sabuda, cannot be read with certainty (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529). The greatest river was the Sind, and large armies rarely went by land, because of the jungle and the lack of water (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529).

Bhandari records the old capitals of the province: Brahmanabad was a great city and a seat of rule, whose fort had fourteen hundred towers, each a rope's length (*tanab*) from the next; after it came Dewal, and now, he writes, Thatta and Debal are the seat of government (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 530). He notes mines of salt and iron in abundance, a yellow stone quarried for building, forty thousand boats great and small, and a diet resting on rice, curds and fish (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 530). In his Gujarat section, Kutch is a separate country, with Sind to its west (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 531).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The temple's festival.** The site's account names *Maha Shivratri*, kept in the spring, as the temple's greatest festival. The books consulted do not name this temple; where they speak of pilgrimage to a shrine of Durga, they set it in Navratri, "the pilgrimage to the shrine of Durga during the festival of Navratri" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 88, folio not stated), a festival "dedicated to Durga" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 31, folio not stated).
- **Durga's mount.** *A White Trail* has her ride a tiger: "she rides a tiger" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 32, folio not stated), and the Valmiki temple's picture shows her "sitting on a tiger" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 80, folio not stated). *Walking with Nanak* has her ride a lion, "like Durga does in Hindu iconography" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 32, folio 14).
- **Kali and Durga.** *A White Trail* calls Kali Mata "an incarnation of Durga" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 39, folio not stated). *Walking with Nanak* speaks of "temples dedicated to the goddesses Kali and Durga" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 236, folio 210). Bhandari's *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* names a goddess Bhavani at Nagarkot and Trikuta without equating her with Durga (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 518; *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 512).
- **The cloth offered to the goddess.** *In Search of Shiva* writes that Hindus "offer chunnis at the shrine of Durga" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated); *A White Trail* writes that Durga Mata "is offered chunries" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 89, folio not stated).

## Legacy

Churrio Jabal Durga Mata Temple is a powerful emblem of the enduring Hindu life of Sindh — a faith not merely remembered in ruins but still practised, in festival and in mourning, on a desert hilltop at the edge of the country. Together with the nearby Jain and Hindu monuments of Nagarparkar, it makes the Karoonjhar region one of the richest landscapes of religious heritage in Pakistan, and its continued vitality, and the recent moves to protect its mountains, offer a hopeful counterpoint to the losses suffered by sacred sites elsewhere.
