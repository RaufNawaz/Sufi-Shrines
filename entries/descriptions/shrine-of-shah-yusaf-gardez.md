## Overview

In the old city of Multan stands the shrine of Hazrat Shah Yusuf Gardez, one of the earliest Sufi saints of the region and, by tradition, a founding figure in the Islamisation of Multan. Coming from Gardez in what is now Afghanistan, he settled in Multan in the early twelfth century and founded the line of the Gardezi sayyids, long among the leading religious families of the city. His shrine, a low tomb famed for its exquisite blue tilework, is one of the most distinctive and revered monuments of the ‘City of Saints’.

Among the books consulted here, Sujan Rai Bhandari's *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* includes him in its list of the saints buried in Multan whose tombs are places of pilgrimage, and says nothing more about him (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528). Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori's *Hadiqat al-Awliya* has a notice on a Shah Gardez of Multan that may be this saint; it gives him no personal name, and the identification is uncertain (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 276, folio 264).

## The Life of the Saint

Sayyid Yusuf, called Shah Yusuf Gardez, is remembered as having come from Gardez to Multan around the early twelfth century, when Islam was still taking root in the region. Tradition places his arrival in the years around 1088 and his death in the first half of the 1100s.

A Sayyid of the family of the Prophet, he is honoured as one of the first great saints of Multan, a preacher whose presence helped make the city a centre of the faith even before the coming of the great Suhrawardi masters. He founded the Gardezi sayyid family, which would remain prominent in the religious and civic life of Multan for centuries. He died at Multan and was buried there.

None of the books consulted here gives a year for his arrival or for his death. The index of the *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* lists the name یوسف گردیزی, Yusuf Gardezi, which is taken to be his; the index entry itself carries neither the title Shah nor the name of Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 17).

## The Shrine and its Devotion

The shrine of Shah Yusuf Gardez is celebrated for its architecture as much as for its sanctity: a low, rectangular tomb sheathed in the brilliant blue-and-white glazed *kashi* tilework of Multan, it is one of the loveliest of the city's many shrines, and it stands among the oldest sacred monuments of the city. It is kept by the Gardezi family.

The tomb is a continuous place of pilgrimage, where devotees recite the Fatiha and seek the blessing of one of Multan's founding saints, and its annual *ʿurs* gathers the faithful in the customary manner. The books consulted here give no date for the *ʿurs* and do not say who built the tomb.

In its account of the suba of Multan, Bhandari's *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* includes Shah Yusuf Gardez in a list of the saints buried in the city whose tombs are places of pilgrimage; it says nothing more about him (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528). Of the city's saints beyond those it names, the book says: "بسیاری از اولیاے دران شهر سعادت بهر اسوده اند" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528).

## Shah Gardez of Multan in the *Hadiqat al-Awliya*

Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori's *Hadiqat al-Awliya* has a notice, entry no. ۲۴۲, on "حضرت شاہ گردیز ملتانی", Hazrat Shah Gardez of Multan, that may be this saint (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 276, folio 264). The identification is uncertain, and what follows is that notice's account of its own saint, not an established history of this shrine. The book prints no personal name for its saint, so nothing in it calls him Yusuf; the link rests only on the name Gardez and on the city of Multan; and the notice gives no year of death (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 276, folio 264). The book's index lists him as گردیز شاہی ملتانی, again an epithet and a city with no personal name, and the notice stands outside the block of pages the book gives to the saints of Multan (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 326, folio 314).

The notice speaks of him in a passage of praise: "ملتان کے بزرگوں میں سے یہ بزرگ صاحب تصرفات ظاہری و باطنی و مدارج صوری و معنوی قطب زمانہ فرد یگانہ فیاض کامل شیخ مکمل تھے" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 276, folio 264). It gives the location of the tomb in Multan in its own words: "حضرت کا قیام مدت مدید تک ملتان میں رہا اور وہاں ہی حضرت کا مزار ہے" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 276, folio 264). It says nothing of an *ʿurs*, a *sajjada nashin* or any buildings (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 276, folio 264).

Lahori relates a miracle of a handshake, and its end. The hands, he writes, emerged "اس راستے سے جو حسب الوصیت قبر کے تعویذ میں رکھا گیا تھا", by the passage that had been left in the grave's *ta'wiz* in accordance with his will (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 276, folio 264). Whether or not the miracle is credited, the sentence describes a passage deliberately left in the grave's *ta'wiz* at the saint's own testamentary instruction (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 276, folio 264).

Of his own day, Lahori writes that the family's *murids* still number in the thousands and that the saint's descendants live in Multan, honoured; one of them, Murad Shah, a man of honour in the colonial darbar, died in ۱۲۹۲ھ, the year in which the author was writing: "اس خاندان کے مرید لوگ اب تک ہزاروں موجود ہیں اور حضرت کی اولاد بھی صاحب عزت و حرمت ملتان میں رہتی ہے۔ چنانچہ ایک شخص مراد شاہ نام جو سرکار انگریز کے دربار میں بڑا معزز تھا۔ اس سال ۱۲۹۲ھ میں فوت ہوا ہے" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 276, folio 264).

## Setting

The books' wider accounts of Multan speak of the city, not of this shrine, but they help to place the tradition of an early saint there. Schimmel gives 711 as the year in which "the Arabs conquered Sind and the adjacent provinces northward up to Multan" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 375, folio 344). Rizvi writes that "Multan had become a dependency of the Fatimids of Egypt", that "by the middle of the eleventh century, sufism had penetrated into the areas surrounding Multan", and that Mu'izzu'd-Din Muhammad bin Sam seized Multan in 1175 (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India, vol. 1*, p. 128, folio 110). Dhillon writes that "During the twelfth century, many Sufi scholars made Multan their base", and that conversion "gained momentum in the early thirteenth century" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 166–167, folios 182–183).

Multan also had a place in the region's poetry and in its pilgrimage. Writing of the vernacular poets, Schimmel says that "Mecca and Medina (and for the Sindhi and Panjabi, also Multan) are inside the heart" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 178, folio 160). Bhandari has a passage on the tombs of the shaikhs of Multan, "در سنه ۸۳۵[OCR?] سلطان بملتان رسیده مزارات مشایخ را طواف نموده", in which the reading of the year is uncertain in the printed text and no shaikh or tomb is named (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 324). Shemeem Burney Abbas mentions in passing "the Muftis and Gardezis who belonged to Lahore and Multan", a mention about Multan in general, not about this shrine (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 26, folio not stated).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **His name.** The site's account calls him Sayyid Yusuf, known as Shah Yusuf Gardez. The index of the *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* has یوسف گردیزی, Yusuf Gardezi, with neither the title Shah nor Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 17). The *Hadiqat al-Awliya* notice that may concern him gives no personal name at all and calls him only "حضرت شاہ گردیز ملتانی" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 276, folio 264); its index has گردیز شاہی ملتانی (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 326, folio 314).
- **His dates.** The site's account, following tradition, places his arrival around 1088 and his death in the first half of the 1100s. The *Hadiqat al-Awliya* notice gives no year of death (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 276, folio 264), and the *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* gives no date for him (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528).

## Legacy

Shah Yusuf Gardez holds an honoured place as one of the earliest saints of Multan and the founder of the Gardezi sayyids, a pioneer of Sufism in the Indus valley whose coming, tradition holds, helped turn Multan toward the faith it would come to embody as the ‘City of Saints’. His tile-clad shrine remains a jewel of the old city and a living centre of devotion.
