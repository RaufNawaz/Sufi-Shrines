## Overview

At Nurpur Shahan, in the Margalla Hills on the edge of Islamabad, stands the shrine of Hazrat Bari Imam — Sayyid Abdul Latif Kazmi (1617–1705) — the patron saint of the Islamabad-Rawalpindi region and one of the most venerated holy figures of the Pothohar. His darbar is the site of one of the largest annual urs festivals in the country, and for centuries pilgrims have climbed to Nurpur Shahan to seek the blessing of the saint whom the people of the capital regard as their guardian.

Two of the books consulted here speak of him, and they speak of him differently. Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori's *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, composed in 1875, gives him an entry in its chapter on the Qadiri saints under the name Shah Latif Bari Qadiri (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 15, folio 3). It says that his holy *rauza* is famous, but it never names the place where it stands, and in the edition read here the name Bari Imam itself appears only in the title of a modern book that the edition's annotator cites (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 45, folio 33; p. 46, folio 34). The link between Lahori's Shah Latif and the shrine at Nurpur Shahan therefore rests on that title and on a meeting with the saint in the Pothohar, not on any statement in the text. The anthropologist Jürgen Wasim Frembgen, in his chapter on the *majzub* Mama Ji Sarkar in *Embodying Charisma*, does name the place: Barri Imam's shrine stands in "the nearby village of Nurpur Shahan, on the outskirts of Pakistan's capital city, Islamabad" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 151, folio 140). Frembgen spells the name Barri Imam throughout, and gives no other personal name for him (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 152, folio 141).

## The Life of the Saint

Sayyid Abdul Latif Shah, called *Bari Imam* and *Bari Sarkar*, was born in 1617 into a Kazmi Sayyid family; tradition traces his descent to the Prophet through the Imams. From youth he was drawn to devotion, and after study and long spiritual wandering he took the Sufi path — associated with the Qadiri tradition — and settled at last at the village now called Nurpur Shahan, in the hills above the plain where Islamabad would one day rise. There he lived as an ascetic and guide, tending the poor, and gathered a wide following; he is remembered for a life of prayer, generosity, and miracle. He is said to have been a contemporary of the Mughal emperor Aurangzeb, who is remembered as honouring him. He died in 1705 and was buried at Nurpur Shahan.

Frembgen gives the same span, 1617 to 1705 or 1706, and the same order: Mama Ji Sarkar "also belongs to the Qadiriyya silsila (spiritual genealogy), like his murshid (spiritual guide), Barri Imam (1617–1705/1706)" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 152, folio 141). He adds that Barri Imam never married: Mama Ji "remained unmarried just like Barri Imam, the saint whom he adopted as his spiritual guide" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 153, folio 142).

Lahori places him sixth among the saints of his Qadiri chapter (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 15, folio 3), and his notice is one of high praise: among the elders of the Punjab, it says, this Hazrat is famous as a great elder. Thousands of *khawariq* and *karamat* are famous of him; he was a great worshipper, an ascetic and a recluse, *mast* and *majzub*; and thousands of his *murids* reached the stages of completion (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 45, folio 33).

His inner blessing, Lahori writes, he received from Hazrat Hayat al-Mir Zinda Pir, whom he describes as one of the grandsons of Ghaus al-Azam, and as eternally living (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 45, folio 33). In another entry, that of Shaikh Bahlul Qadri, Lahori names a second master: besides Hayat al-Mir Zinda Pir, Shah Latif had also received *faiz* from Shaikh Nasir al-Din Qurashi Multani (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 50, folio 38).

The edition's annotator, Iqbal Mujaddidi, doubts the first of these masters. Hayat al-Mir, he writes, is not mentioned in the contemporary books of history or in the genealogies. Shah Abul Muali Lahori had recorded that of the sons of Shaikh Abdul Razzaq, son of Shaikh Abdul Qadir Jilani, Shaikh Jamal Allah was present in that era in the jungles of Bastam, but did not give him the sobriquet Hayat al-Mir; that name was first applied to Shaikh Jamal Allah by Ahmad Ali Astarabadi in the *Tazkira-e Muqimi*, a book the printed text dates, uncertainly, to 1172 AH, and for that sobriquet, he writes, there is no historical proof (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 45, folio 33, note 2).

## A Meeting in the Pothohar

The annotator's case for the saint's dates rests on two meetings with him. Sayyid Hasan Peshawari, he writes, met Shah Latif in the Pothohar in 1077 AH, a year the printed text gives uncertainly, and Shah Muhammad Ghaus Lahori, Sayyid Hasan Peshawari's son, met him in 1116 AH and wrote the meeting up himself (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 46, folio 34, note 3). He quotes Hasan Peshawari's account in Persian, from Mir Ghulam Kashmiri's *Khawariq al-Adat* of 1189 AH, in a manuscript owned by Maulana Sayyid Amir Shah Qadri of Peshawar, at a folio whose number, perhaps 8, is uncertain in the printed text (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 46, folio 34, note 3). In it Hasan Peshawari relates that when he reached the country of Pothohar he saw Sayyid Shah Latif Majzub, who bore the marks of great insight and met him with complete courtesy; a day or two passed, and he took his leave.

The witness himself is dated. In a note elsewhere in the edition the annotator gives Hasan Peshawari's birth: he was born in Jumada al-Akhir 1023 AH / 1614 CE at Mattha (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 73, folio 61, note 1), which accords with his meeting Shah Latif in the Pothohar in 1077 AH, a year whose printed reading is itself uncertain.

## The Shrine and the Urs

The shrine of Bari Imam at Nurpur Shahan is a large complex and a ceaseless resort of pilgrims from the capital region and beyond. Its annual urs, held in the spring, is among the greatest religious festivals of Pakistan — a gathering of hundreds of thousands over several days, with qawwali, *dhamal*, *langar*, and a vast fair, drawing devotees, dervishes, and *malangs* from across the country. The shrine keeps the daily prayers and the free kitchen, and it is bound up with the identity of Islamabad as the capital’s patron darbar. Like other great shrines it suffered a grievous suicide bombing in 2005, during the urs; yet the devotion endured, and the shrine remains thronged.

Neither book describes the shrine itself. Lahori says only that the saint's holy *rauza* is famous, without naming its place (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 45, folio 33). Frembgen places it at Nurpur Shahan (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 151, folio 140) but gives no account of its tomb, its custodians, its revenues or its buildings (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, pp. 145–162 and 163–179, folios 134–151 and 152–168).

What Frembgen does record is the *ʿurs* and the devotion that flows towards it from elsewhere. The festival, as he describes it, lasts five days and is preceded by a "traditional procession of devotees from Peshawar to Nurpur, organised by the descendants of the repentant Chan Agha and the later, better-known pir Agha Sayyid Mir Jani Shah (d. 1883)"; he speaks of "the week-long procession and…the subsequent five days of 'urs-celebrations" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 153, folio 142). He dates one *ʿurs* precisely: "During Barri Imam's ‘urs in 1994, in the first week of May, the anjuman was able to attract crowds of visitors to Mama Ji's shrine and to his former baithak"; and "During Barri Imam's ‘urs, both places of devotion were richly decorated and the event was successfully used to publicise the name and sacred power of Mama Ji Sarkar" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 164, folio 153). He gives no lunar date and no month for any other year (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 164, folio 153).

## Devotion beyond the Shrine

Frembgen's subject, Mama Ji Sarkar, took Barri Imam as his *murshid*, and the darbar of the disciple keeps the master present in several ways. "In a corner there is a model (dali) of Barri Imam's shrine, which is taken to Nurpur on the occasion of the 'urs" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 160, folio 149). In the *cheragh-khana* of Mama Ji's *langar*, five oil lamps "burn in honour of Mama Ji's murshid, Barri Imam", and devotees take their oil "as a remedy against any physical pain" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 159, folio 148). Barri Imam's shrine is twice pictured in Mama Ji's darbar: in a fresco by Sayyid Ishrat there is "a shrine in the background resembling that of Barri Imam", and among the mirror-mosaic motifs of 1990 is "the shrine of Barri Imam" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 159, folio 148).

Mama Ji himself, Frembgen relates, joined the annual procession at "around the age of 14–16", and afterwards "remained near Barri Imam's shrine for about ten or twelve years", "living for longer periods in the wilderness surrounding the village" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 153, folio 142). The book's index names Barri Imam among the sites at which it discusses the rituals of the *ʿurs*, alongside Ghamkol Sharif, the shrines of Gujarat and Mama Ji Sarkar (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 254, folio 243).

## Legends and Miracles

Lahori speaks of thousands of *khawariq* and *karamat* famous of him, but relates none (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 45, folio 33). Frembgen records one legend, told to him by Rahim Gul Motilala, "the oldest and most respected mujawir of Mama Ji's shrine": the saint Chan Agha Badshah "once insulted Barri Imam, not recognising his saintliness", and, on realising his mistake, "made a vow to journey every year by foot to the saint's dargah" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 153, folio 142). The procession from Peshawar, in Frembgen's account, is organised by the descendants of the repentant Chan Agha (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 153, folio 142).

## Books about the Saint

The annotator of the *Hadiqat al-Awliya* lists the writings on Shah Latif that he drew upon (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 46, folio 34). They are Shah Muhammad Ghaus Lahori's *Risala dar Kasb-e Suluk wa Bayan-e Marifat*, Peshawar 1284 AH; Mir Ghulam Kashmiri's *Khawariq al-Adat*, 1189 AH; Muqim Muhkam al-Din Hajrawi's *Durr al-Ajaib*, in translation, Lahore; Gul Hasan Shah's *Tazkira-e Ghausia*, Lahore, undated; Muhammad Qasim Rajori's *Hayat-e Bari Imam*, Rawalpindi; Manzur al-Haq Siddiqi's *Shah Latif Bari*, Lahore 1970; and his own article "Bari Shah Latif" in the *Danishnama-e Shibh-e Qarra*, Tehran.

The edition's general book list gives two of these again: Muhammad Qasim Rajori's *Hayat-e Bari Imam*, Rawalpindi, undated, and Manzur al-Haq Siddiqi's *Shah Latif Bari*, Lahore 1970 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 303, folio 291). The first carries the name Bari Imam in its title; the second presumably concerns the same saint, though the list does not say so.

## Setting

The books' wider accounts of Islamabad speak of the city, not of this shrine, but they show something of the capital around it. Frembgen calls Islamabad "Pakistan's capital city" and mentions a devotional print in Mama Ji's darbar naming 'Pir Mehr ‘Ali Shah (Golra/Islamabad)' (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 151, folio 140; p. 159, folio 148). Haroon Khalid notes that "Rawalpindi happens to be the twin city of Islamabad, the federal capital of the country" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 265, folio 239).

The capital is also a centre of patronage for Sufi music. Shemeem Burney Abbas writes that the singer Abida Parvin "lives in Islamabad and not in her hometown of Hyderabad, because Islamabad is the capital and she can negotiate business from there" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 80, folio not stated), and that "Lok Virsa, the Institute of Folk Heritage in Islamabad, contacted her through Radio Pakistan in Hyderabad" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 34, folio not stated). At a concert at the Open University in Islamabad, Abbas recalls, "this woman performer sang in a fiery mood and blasted the orthodox government of the time in the presence of its official bureaucracy by using the frames of Sufi poetry" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 101, folio not stated).

Islamabad has its place in the life of the Sufi orders and in religious publishing. Robert Rozehnal records that "Since 1989, The Association of Spiritual Training—founded by Wahid Bakhsh and administered by a number of his senior disciples—has published an English language journal titled The Sufi Path", based in Islamabad (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 130, folio 121), and that "Another female murid used to host a similar weekly halqa in Islamabad, but this practice has recently been discontinued" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 201, folio 193). Carl W. Ernst and Bruce B. Lawrence cite the publications and manuscripts of a Persian research centre in the city, the Markaz-i Tahqiqat-i Farsi-i Iran u Pakistan (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 234, folio 224; p. 235, folio 225), and Scott Kugle a study of Shah Hussayn issued from Islamabad by Lok Wirsa Isha'at Ghar in 1989 (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 340, folio 319).

Other currents run through the city as well. Khalid writes that "Al-Huda is one of the most prominent Pakistani religious revivalist organizations, based in Islamabad, which aims to educate women about the Quran and the Sunnah" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated); his informant, the travel writer Salman Rashid, tells him, "I have heard of a bodhi tree in Islamabad and one near Jhelum" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 9, ch. 3, folio not stated); and of the Baha'is he writes that Lahore and Islamabad are "two of the important cities as far as Baha'i representation is concerned" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 234, folio not stated).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- The year of his death. The current account gives 1705. Frembgen gives "1705/1706" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 152, folio 141). Lahori's own text gives 994 AH, a reading uncertain in the printed text (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 45, folio 33). His annotator rejects that year outright: this year of death, he writes, is much disputed (the word "much" is uncertain in the printed text), and he follows a family record that gives 1117 AH / 1706 CE, on the evidence of the meetings of 1077 AH and 1116 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 46, folio 34, note 3).
- His name. The current account calls him Sayyid Abdul Latif Kazmi and Bari Imam. Lahori calls him Shah Latif Bari Qadiri and, in the Persian account he quotes, Sayyid Shah Latif Majzub (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 15, folio 3; p. 46, folio 34, note 3). Frembgen calls him only Barri Imam and gives no personal name (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 152, folio 141).
- His masters. The current account names no master. Lahori names Hayat al-Mir Zinda Pir and, in another entry, Shaikh Nasir al-Din Qurashi Multani (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 45, folio 33; p. 50, folio 38). His annotator holds that the sobriquet Hayat al-Mir has no historical proof (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 45, folio 33, note 2).
- The place of his tomb. The current account and Frembgen place it at Nurpur Shahan (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 151, folio 140). Lahori says only that his *rauza* is famous, and names no place (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 45, folio 33).
- The five lamps at Mama Ji's *langar*. On the same page Frembgen says that they "burn in honour of Mama Ji's murshid, Barri Imam" and calls them "five oil lamps symbolising the Holy Family" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 159, folio 148).

## Legacy

Bari Imam is the guardian saint of Pakistan’s capital region — a seventeenth-century Sayyid ascetic whose shrine at Nurpur Shahan has become, in the shadow of modern Islamabad, one of the country’s greatest centres of popular devotion. His urs is a defining festival of the Pothohar, and his memory endures as that of a saint of generosity and blessing watching over the capital from the Margalla Hills.

His memory lives in other saints' houses too. Frembgen's account of Mama Ji Sarkar shows a disciple's darbar carrying Barri Imam's image on its walls, keeping lamps for him, and sending a model of his shrine to Nurpur on the occasion of the *ʿurs* (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 159, folio 148; p. 160, folio 149). And the written tradition about him continues: the *Hadiqat al-Awliya* and its annotator point to a line of books on Shah Latif that runs from Shah Muhammad Ghaus Lahori's Persian *risala* to a Rawalpindi life of Bari Imam and an article in a Tehran encyclopaedia (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 46, folio 34).
