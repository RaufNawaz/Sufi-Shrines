## Overview

The Shri Panchmukhi Hanuman Mandir, in the Soldier Bazaar quarter of Karachi, is one of the most remarkable and most ancient Hindu temples in Pakistan, famed for its extraordinary *panchmukhi* ("five-faced") image of the monkey-god *Hanuman*, which devotees hold to be *svayambhu*, self-manifested rather than made by human hands. Believed by tradition to be many centuries old, the temple was declared a protected site of national heritage under the Sindh Cultural Heritage (Preservation) Act of 1994, and it remains a busy centre of worship for Karachi's Hindu community.

None of the books consulted for this article describes the temple. None names Soldier Bazaar, and none speaks of the five-faced form of the god. Haroon Khalid's *A White Trail*, a book about Pakistan's religious minorities, speaks of "the monkey deity, Hanuman" but never names the Karachi temple (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 64, folio not stated); Jürgen Schaflechner's study of the Hindu temple of Hinglaj Devi writes of Hanuman only on the pilgrimage road to that goddess, in another place altogether (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 279–280, folio not stated). What the books do carry is set out below as it stands: what they say of Hanuman himself, in prose and in verse, and, under Setting, what they say of Karachi and of the Hindus of Sindh. None of it is said of this temple.

## The Five-Faced Image

Hanuman, the devoted servant of Rama and one of the most beloved deities of popular Hinduism, is here venerated in his rare *Panchamukha* form, bearing five faces that in the classical iconography represent Hanuman himself together with *Narasimha* (the man-lion), *Garuda* (the eagle), *Varaha* (the boar), and *Hayagriva* (the horse-headed form), each face turned to a direction and each embodying a divine power. What sets the Karachi image apart, in the belief of its devotees, is that it is a "natural" statue: not carved by any sculptor but revealed from the earth, which the faithful hold to make it unique among shrines of Hanuman.

Pilgrims come to offer worship before it, and the temple is especially thronged at *Hanuman Jayanti*, the deity's birth anniversary, as well as at *Holi* and *Diwali*. The books consulted record none of these festivals at this temple, and none describes its image, its priests or its rites.

## Antiquity and Tradition

The temple's origins are wreathed in legend and great antiquity. Devotional tradition holds that *Rama* himself passed this way during his years of exile, and that long afterwards the image of Hanuman was uncovered at the spot, whereupon a shrine was built to house it. The site is popularly said to be at least fifteen hundred years old, with some accounts reaching back further still; such datings are traditional and cannot be verified, but they express the deep sense of sanctity and age that the temple carries for its worshippers.

The books consulted neither confirm nor dispute these traditions. None gives a date for the temple, names a founder, or tells the story of the image's uncovering.

## Rediscovery and Renewal

In modern times the temple has passed through periods of difficulty, including the loss of part of its land, but it has also seen renewal. A programme of renovation was undertaken in 2012, supported by donations from local Hindus. In 2019, during construction and restoration work, a number of further images and artefacts were unearthed, reported to include several statues of Hanuman together with other sacred figures, a discovery that drew the interest of archaeologists and museum authorities and renewed public attention to the temple's long history. These finds, and the temple's protected status, have strengthened its standing as a heritage site of national importance.

The renovation belongs to a wider pattern that Schaflechner observed in the province, though he does not name this temple. Government funding for the places of minority communities, he writes, "is seen in a wave of restorations of temples across Sindh within the past decade", and "While in Sindh, I visited many temples that were renovated within the past decade, coinciding with changes in the political landscape" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 16, folio not stated).

## Hanuman in the Books

The books that name Hanuman speak of the god, never of this temple, and most of them only in passing.

Christopher Shackle, in the glossary to his translation of Bulleh Shah, lists the god among the legendary figures of the poems, as "Hanuman (Skt.,P. Hanūvant)", giving the Sanskrit and the Punjabi forms of the name (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 198, folio 173). Michel Boivin's index enters him as 'Hanuman (god)' (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 243, folio 230). Haroon Khalid calls him "the monkey deity, Hanuman", and writes that he is "depicted as a mighty warrior, the Indian version of Hercules" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 64, folio not stated).

Schaflechner names him as "Rama's close ally" in the stories of the pilgrimage to Hinglaj (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 119, 121, 122, folios not stated). Taufiq Rafat's selection of Bulleh Shah speaks, beside the poet's references to the Kurus and Pandus of the Mahabharata (a word imperfectly printed in the book), of "the Rama-Sita story of the 'Ramayana' in which Ravana and Hanuman figure as two poles of mystical knowledge." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 40, folio 20).

Images of the god appear in the books at other sites, none of them in Karachi. Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti's Urdu *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, describing the *samadh* of Ranjit Singh, records that in the southern niche there is an image of Hanuman in marble (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 570). At Chandrat, the same book lists him among the pictures on the walls, beside Ganesh (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 792). Khalid's *A White Trail* describes him painted on the wall of a Valmiki temple, "carrying an entire mountain on his hand while flying back to Lanka" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 80, folio not stated).

Boivin records Ramakrishna's absorption "in other incarnations such as Hanuman, Sita and Krishna" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 104, folio 91). Annemarie Schimmel's *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* carries the god's name only in its index, as a single headword with one locator and no context (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 516, folio 485).

## Legends of Hanuman

The books tell two legends in which Hanuman makes water flow, neither set in Karachi and neither connected with this temple.

On the pilgrimage road to Hinglaj, Schaflechner records that "The Bahur River, for example, is said to have been created by Hanuman's attempt to get water for Sita." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 119, 121, 122, folios not stated). Further along the same road, a *paṛāv*, a halting place in the Hinglaj valley, is contested in its dedication, and pilgrims, he reports, "even put a small Hanuman statue there!" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 279–280, folio not stated).

Khalid's *A White Trail* gives a second legend in the words of an informant. According to Advit's aunt, the Ram Thamman pool "was first dug by the monkey deity, Hanuman": he "created this ancient pool, digging with a shovel only two and a half times" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 64, folio not stated).

## Hanuman in Verse

The books carry Hanuman's name in the poetry of the Sufis, never in verse about this temple. The fullest line is Bulleh Shah's, in Christopher Shackle's English, where the poet recalls the epic of Rama:

*When Ravan the ten-headed abducted poor Sita, Hanuman burned Lanka. Ravan was completely humiliated, and finally you killed him* (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 141, folio 116)

In Shackle's notes the god appears under both his Sanskrit and his Punjabi names, Hanuman and Hanūvant (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 198, folio 173).

Annemarie Schimmel's *As Through a Veil*, paraphrasing a Muslim poet's folk verse in which all categories collapse, sets Hanuman in a list of paired opposites: "finally Hallaj and his judge, Shams-i Tabriz and the molla, and Abu Hanifa and Hanuman are one and the same, and there is no longer Turk, Hindu, or Peshawari." (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 176, folio 158). The name stands there as one term in the list, not as the subject of the poem.

Michel Boivin counts Sachal and Qutub Ali Shah among the "Sufis from nineteenth-century Sindh" who brought Krishna into their poetry (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 198, folio 185), and they named Hanuman's circle as well: "In his poetry, Sachal mentions different Hindu gods, such as Ram, Hanuman", Sita and especially Krishna (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 106, folio 93), and "Sachal mentions Hanuman while Qutub Ali Shah gives the names of Laxman and Sita, Ram and Krishna" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 107, folio 94).

## Setting

The books say a good deal about Karachi and about the Hindus of Sindh, and nothing about Soldier Bazaar or this temple. What follows is said of the city and the province.

**Hindu Karachi.** Haroon Khalid's *Walking with Nanak* describes a visit to Karachi's old temples. At Ram Bagh, he found, the area was now called Aram Bagh, the added letter, as he put it, neutralising its religious sense (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 222, folio not stated). The temple there had a painted Krishna "with his maidens" under its roof and "a peacock guarding the entrance"; most of the quarters behind it held Hindu households, and the temple was "much more elaborately decorated" and its community "much larger" than in Lahore (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 223, folio 197). An old man told him "This temple is one hundred and fifty years old", and Khalid remarked that "Hundred and fifty seemed to be the standard age of all temples here" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 223, folio 197). An officer said: "I don't know exactly how old this temple is but it is old… existed before Partition" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 223–224, folios 197–198). Along with the temple at Clifton, Khalid concluded, it "must have been a historical Hindu temple" in Guru Nanak's time (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 224, folio 198), and he cites Iqbal Qaiser's identification of the Clifton temple as "the temple which Nanak had visited on his tour of Sindh" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 217, folio 191).

At the Port Grand temple he found "a newly constructed mosque for tourists" built on top of it (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 224, folio 198), rooms "reserved for different deities", and a mural of "a male deity sitting [on] top of a giant fish", Jhule Lal (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 225, folio 199). "At the time of Partition, Karachi was a minor port city", he writes, which migrants from India made "the largest city of Pakistan" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 226, folio 200). Its Hindu fisherfolk "lived here before Kolachi became Karachi", and "these Hindu fisherfolk are now the only connection" between the city and the old village of Kolachi (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 225, folio 199; *Walking with Nanak*, p. 226, folio 200). A legend told to him explains the name: an old woman known as Mai Kolachi, whose son was killed by a fish, tore the fish apart and retrieved him, and the place came to be known as Kolachi (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 225, folio 199).

Khalid's *A White Trail* finds Karachi the supplier of Hindu worship elsewhere in Pakistan. Boys from Bahawalnagar "bought an idol of Durga from Karachi" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 31, folio not stated); a vendor's idols "have been brought from Karachi, where there are still a reasonable number of Hindus living or from India through illegal channels", and Navratri pilgrims come "from as far as Karachi" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 33, folio not stated). Hinglaj lies "about two hundred and fifty kilometres from the coastal city of Karachi" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 32, folio not stated).

In Schaflechner's *Hinglaj Devi*, Karachi is a city of violence against Hindus (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 18, folio not stated), the seat of the Nagnath and Jagnath *akhāṛās* (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 127, 138, 159, 207, 292–294, folios not stated), the staging city for Indian pilgrims (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 175, 180, 181, 183, folios not stated), the home of the Devipujak-Vagri community (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 306–312, 322, folios not stated), and the start of the road to the goddess, "about 240 kilometers long" from Narayanpur (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 158, folio not stated). The city's spread swallowed the old pilgrim road: "The ongoing growth of Karachi and the corresponding urbanization of the areas where the early stops occurred caused all but a few of the resting places to completely disappear." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 121, folio not stated).

**Colonial Karachi.** Michel Boivin writes of "the growth of new emporium in Sindh, especially Karachi", in the late nineteenth century (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 44, folio 31). Sir Charles Napier, "the conqueror and first governor of Sindh", built a Masonic lodge in Karachi in 1843, and "In Karachi, the Theosophical Society was founded in 1896" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 46, folio 33); "a printing press was brought to Karachi in the 1850s" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 48, folio 35).

**Partition.** Boivin estimates that in 1941 the Hindus "made up about 21 per cent of the total population of Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 61, folio 48), and that "By early November of 1947, 246,000 Hindus had already left Sindh by sea, air or rail." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 62, folio 49). The Hindu Sindhis "started leaving Sindh especially after the Karachi riots in January 1948" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 66, folio not stated). The narrative of Rochaldas, he writes, alludes to violence spread through Sindh that was "not perpetrated by the Muslim Sindhis, but by the Mohajirs who clearly wanted to take over the houses of the Hindus, at least in Karachi" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 70–71, folios 57–58). Schaflechner likewise describes the departure of the Hindu elite and the influx of Muslim refugees from India as having dramatically altered the province's demography (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 87, folio not stated).

**The Hindus of Sindh today.** Sindh, in Schaflechner's account, is "home to the majority of Pakistan's Hindus" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 13, folio not stated). Boivin writes that "Sindh is the province of Pakistan with the most significant Hindu population" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 176–177, folios not stated and 164), and that Ramdeo Pir, known in Sindh as Rama Pir, is "today integrated into the temples dedicated to the pan-Indian divinities" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 192, folio 179). Khalid writes that by the census of 1998 "the Hindu minority is the largest minority in the country" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 87, folio not stated), and that after the demolition of the Babri Mosque in December 1992 "Hindu communities in Sindh and Quetta were attacked" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 22, folio not stated).

**Hindus and the older Sindh.** Richard M. Eaton writes that in 711 Muslim navies "conquered and occupied the densely-populated Hindu—Buddhist society of Sind" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 30, folio 18). H. T. Sorley records, of the centuries after the mass conversions to Islam, "Hindus visiting Mussulman shrines and making votive offerings" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 175, folio 166), and observes that "Most of the musicians in Sind have been Hindus and not Mussulmans" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 231, folio 222). Shah Abdul Latif's stories, he argues, "are all Hindu in origin" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 269, folio 260). Christopher Shackle's notes to the *Risalo* describe the long sea voyages "undertaken by the Hindu traders of Sindh to locations like Aden, Gujarat, or Lanka" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 16, folio not stated), the custom of their wives of making offerings to the water deity for their husbands' safe return, and "The Hindu festival of lamps celebrated in the autumn, which marked the beginning of the trading season" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 324, folio not stated).

The *Risalo*'s best-known prayer is for the province itself. Shah Abdul Latif's rain-song, the Sarang, catalogues the places the rains reach, among them Umarkot, of which the verse says "Some have passed over Umarkot and have made the ground green", and closes, in Shackle's English, by asking God to make Sindh flourish forever:

*Dear friend and sweet beloved, make the whole world prosperous* (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 265, folio not stated)

Shackle's note calls that final invocation of God's blessings on Sindh "one of the most often quoted verses of the Risālo" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 338, folio not stated).

**Sufi Karachi.** Several of the books speak of the city's Sufi life. Regula Burckhardt Qureshi counts it, with Hyderabad in India, among "two important urban centres of Sufism", where the Qawwal Bachche lineage keeps its heritage (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 33, folio 13). Robert Rozehnal describes the Chishti order's teaching shaykh, who "leads weekly halqa (communal zikr sessions) on Thursday evenings" in Karachi (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 165, folio 157); Carl W. Ernst and Bruce B. Lawrence record that the Chishti master Zauqi Shah "was therefore present at the first meeting of the Muslim League in Karachi in 1907" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 96, folio 82). Omar Kasmani writes that after the annual fair at Sehwan, fakirs "follow other fakirs and proceed to shrines in Karachi" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 114, folio 97). Shemeem Burney Abbas records a Sidi congregation at Manghopir (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 70–71, folios not stated), and Annemarie Schimmel the alligator miracle "(as happened in Mango Pir near Karachi)" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 238, folio 207). Scott Kugle notes that Hajji Imdadullah went "from Karachi into exile in Mecca" after the uprising of 1857 (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 280, folio 259).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **Hanuman's standing beside Rama.** The site's account calls him "the devoted servant of Rama". Schaflechner calls him "Rama's close ally" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 119, 121, 122, folios not stated). Khalid has him "depicted as a mighty warrior, the Indian version of Hercules" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 64, folio not stated). Rafat's selection sets him with Ravana as "two poles of mystical knowledge" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 40, folio 20), and Schimmel's paraphrase of the mystical folk poets makes "Abu Hanifa and Hanuman" one and the same (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 176, folio 158).
- **The god's name.** The site's account and most of the books write Hanuman, and the site's account calls him the monkey-god; Khalid writes "the monkey deity, Hanuman" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 64, folio not stated); Shackle gives the Punjabi form Hanūvant beside the Sanskrit (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 198, folio 173).
- **How many Hindus live in Pakistan and Sindh.** Schaflechner puts Pakistan's Hindus at "between 6 and 10 million people—an estimate that varies widely according to the source", of whom "over 90 percent" belong to the underprivileged tribes and sub-castes (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 13, folio not stated). Boivin writes that "the Sindhi Hindus number several millions, with a majority of them belonging to the so-called scheduled castes" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 175, folio 162). Khalid gives no figure, but cites the census of 1998 for the Hindus as the country's largest minority (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 87, folio not stated).
- **How many Hindus left Sindh at Partition.** Boivin reckons the Hindus of 1941 at "exactly 0.9 million", and the migrants at "closer to 0.8 million, if not less", while noting "a kind of consensus that they numbered around 1 million" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 61, folio 48).
- **Whether Sindh was a land without communal strife.** Sorley finds in Sindh "a kind of rapprochement between the deeper mystical" ideas of Muslim and Hindu thought (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 225, folio 216). Schaflechner calls the common notion of "the harmonious coexistence of the religions" in Sindh "merely a nationalist utopian ideal" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 90–91, folio not stated). Boivin writes that "even if it never reached the level of violence in Punjab, it is irrelevant to claim there was no communal violence in Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 62, folio 49).

## Legacy

The Shri Panchmukhi Hanuman Mandir occupies a singular place among the Hindu shrines of Pakistan: a temple whose central image is held to be self-born, whose traditions reach back to the age of the *Ramayana*, and whose soil has yielded up sacred figures buried for generations. For the Hindus of Karachi it is a cherished house of worship and a point of continuity with a distant past; for the wider heritage of the city it is a monument that binds legend, devotion, and archaeology into a single enduring landmark.
