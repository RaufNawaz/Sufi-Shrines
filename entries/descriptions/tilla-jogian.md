## Overview

Tilla Jogian — the "Hill of the Ascetics" — crowns the highest peak of the eastern Salt Range, rising to some 975 metres above sea level, in the Jhelum District of the Punjab. On its summit lie the ruins of an ancient temple and monastic complex that was, before 1947, the foremost centre of Hindu yogis in the whole of the Punjab. Cooler and greener than the parched country below, the hilltop has for many centuries been a retreat for renunciants seeking solitude, and its long association with the *Nath* order of yogis, with Guru Nanak, and with the lovers of Punjabi legend has made it one of the most storied heights in the region.

The books bear out that reputation from several sides. Christopher Shackle, annotating the lyrics of Bulleh Shah, calls the hill the headquarters of the order of *Kanphat* yogis (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 187, folio 162, n. 10 on 122). Haroon Khalid calls it the most important pilgrimage site for jogis in the Punjab (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 21, folio 3). Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, in its account of the suba of Lahore, sets on the salt mountain the place of austerity of the jogi Bal Nath (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 514), and records a gathering there on the day of Shivrat (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 514–513). And in Waris Shah's romance of Heer and Ranjha, in Charles Frederick Usborne's English, Tilla is the hill to which Ranjha comes to take *jog* from Bal Nath, whom Usborne spells Balnath (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 31, folio 30).

## Gorakhnath and the Nath Yogis

Tradition traces the sanctity of Tilla Jogian back some two thousand years and links its foundation to *Gorakhnath*, the revered yogi and organiser of the *Nath* tradition — though this popular chronology is internally inconsistent (scholarship usually places the historical Gorakhnath centuries later, c. 11th–12th century CE) and should be read as legend rather than settled history.

Shackle's notes to Bulleh Shah place Gorakhnath at the head of the order the hill housed. Where the poet names 'guru Gorakh', Shackle explains "Gorakhnath, the head of the Kanphat yogis" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 187, folio 162, n. 12 on 122); the name on which the poem's initiate meditates is "Presumably the name of the yogis' guru Gorakhnath" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 187, folio 162, n. 11 on 122); and his glossary describes "the great yogi Gorakhnath, from whom Ranjha sought initiation as a yogi" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 198, folio 173). Shackle gives Gorakhnath no date and no lineage.

Michel Boivin, whose study names no Nath site in Sindh or in the Salt Range, describes the Nathpanthis as "the followers of Gorakhnath, who is believed to be an avatar of Shiva" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 77, folio 64). He observes that "in the Persian sources, the main Hindu figure is the jogi", a term "usually employed for the Nathpanthis, a Shivaite school of renunciants who were the followers of Gorakhnath" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 21, folio 8); he speaks of "an ascetic order founded by Gorakhnath" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 120, folio 107); and his glossary defines the jogis as "wandering Shivaite ascetics who meditate on One, usually affiliated with the Nathpanth" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 219, folio 206). In one tradition he reports, "Gorakhnath would have sent an invisible army to help Goga Pir against Rai Pithora" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 188, folio 175).

Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi's history of Sufism in India, which nowhere mentions Tilla Jogian, says of the Naths in general that they "lived in forests, wandered in towns and also established permanent monasteries" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 376, folio 354). Harish Dhillon notes that in the Puratan Janamsakhis the account of the anointment of Guru Angad has Gorakhnath "in conversation with Guru Nanak", and comments: "Historically, Gorakhnath lived and preached long before Nanak's time (in the eleventh century)" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 10, folio not stated).

Gorakhnath's name also reaches the cult of the goddess Hinglaj, the subject of Jürgen Schaflechner's study: "Tradition has it that Hinglaj was very fond of the Nath community, especially of Guru Gorakhnath. So the Goddess waited day after day in Thatta for Gorakhnath to come to meet her" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 205, folio not stated). Schaflechner records 'the dhūnī of Guru Gorakhnath' there (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 256, folio not stated) and writes that "The fireplace of Goraknath dates back to the long-standing tradition of Nath Yogis who made the journey to Hinglaj", citing Briggs's study of 1938 (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 256–257, folio not stated); "two texts describe the pilgrimage of Guru Gorakhnath to Hinglaj: the Gorakh Caritra and the Gorakhpurāṇa" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 257, folio not stated). What Boivin, Rizvi, Dhillon and Schaflechner say here is said of Gorakhnath and the Naths, not of this hill; Boivin and Rizvi name no Nath site in the Salt Range at all.

## The Seat of the Jogis

Tilla Jogian became the principal seat of the *Kanphata* yogis — the 'split-eared' ascetics, so called from the great rings inserted through the cartilage of their ears at initiation — an order that looked to Gorakhnath as its master. On the hilltop the *jogis* maintained shrines, cells, and reservoirs of water, and the complex is said at its height to have sheltered hundreds of ascetics who came to meditate in its heights. The site formed the easternmost of a string of ancient Hindu sanctuaries strung along the Salt Range, a chain that also includes the temples of Katas Raj and, at its western end, the Amb temples above the Soon Valley.

Shackle's note on the 'Tilla' of Bulleh Shah's lyric reads: "Tilla Jogian high up in the hills of District Jhelum was the headquarters of the order of Kanphat ('split-eared') yogis, who were initiated by having the cartilage of their ears pierced to allow the insertion of a wooden ring" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 187, folio 162, n. 10 on 122). It describes the order rather than the buildings: Shackle names no temple, building, date, custodian or endowment.

Bhandari's *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* places the hill on the salt mountain. "بالاے همین کوه بهفت کروهے قلعه رهتاس": on top of this same mountain, seven kos from the fort of Rohtas, is the place of austerity of the jogi Bal Nath (بالناتهه[OCR?] جوگی, the reading of the name uncertain in the printed text), called تله[OCR?], for which the editor's footnote gives the variant بله[OCR?]; the book gives its height in kos as چهار کروه بلند (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 514).

Waris Shah's romance, in Usborne's translation, calls the hill simply 'Tilla' and 'your hill of Tilla'; it never writes 'Tilla Jogian', and it names neither the Salt Range nor Gorakhnath. Beyond the name Tilla, its chief tie to this hill is a note by a later, unnamed annotator: "According to tradition, Balnath was the fourth Guru of 'Tilla Guruan' (the mound of the Gurus) on the banks of the river Jhelum" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 33, folio 32, note 1). The same note goes on, in the annotator's own present tense: "The place is still preserved, and the 'Jog Mat' or the cult of the wandering mystics, with its emphasis on pantheism, is still practised there" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 33, folio 32, note 1). It gives no building, structure, custodian, date or district.

The poem peoples the hill with Bal Nath's *chelas*, who keep the jogi's house and kitchen. When Ranjha arrives, "They were all engaged in religious contemplation and prayers. They were reading Gayan Gita Bhagvat and Bharat" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 31, folio 30). Their service runs for a fixed term, and they complain of it: "For eighteen years we have been serving him. We have given up all and live by begging. All day and night we remember God. Yet he does not give us Jog" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 32, folio 31). The annotator explains the guru by his Muslim counterpart: "Guru — the Hindu holy man, the teacher, the Hindu counterpart of the Muslim Murshid" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 33, folio 32, note 5).

## Festival and Practice

Bhandari records the one festival that any of these sources sets on the hill. On the appointed days, and above all on the day of Shivrat, "روز شیورات که روزه است منسوب بمهادیو[OCR?]", at the end of winter (دربرامد[OCR?] زمستان), the book says, "خلایق کثیر و طوایف جوگیان" gather there "و نمایش گری بکاری برند" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 514–513). The sources consulted record no other festival there.

Shackle's notes, which speak of yogis at large rather than of this hill, gloss the rest of the yogi's equipment as Bulleh Shah's lyrics present it: the ochre dress (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 165, folio 140); the bonfire and its ashes, for "Yogis sit in front of a bonfire (dhūāñ) and rub its ashes (bhabūt) on their bodies" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 190, folio 165, n. 3 on 137); the deerskin, skull-cup, matted hair and ashes (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 156, folio 131); the forehead mark (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 190, folio 165, n. 1 on 137); and the horn, bowl and beads (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, pp. 124, 141; folios 99, 116). He explains, too, that "The tenth door is the hidden aperture in the skull that is opened at the climax of the yogic process" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 173, folio 148, n. 2 on 63).

## Guru Nanak and the Hill

Such was its renown as a place of Hindu spiritual power that it drew, in the early sixteenth century, *Guru Nanak*, the founder of the Sikh faith, who is remembered in tradition as having spent forty days in meditation upon the hill during his travels. The episode became part of the sacred memory of the site for Sikhs, adding another layer to a place already venerated by yogis and by Hindu pilgrims from across the Punjab.

The books consulted add no account of Nanak's own stay on the hill. Haroon Khalid's reconstruction in *Walking with Nanak* brings the hill into the story by another road: Tripta accompanied her husband "to Tilla Jogian, the most important pilgrimage site for jogis in the Punjab. There the couple paid for the jogis' food for one day, as a result of which the jogis prayed all night to bless this young couple" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 21, folio 3).

## Ranjha Takes Jog

Tilla Jogian occupies a special place in Punjabi folklore through the tragic romance of *Heer and Ranjha*, immortalised in the eighteenth century by the Sufi poet *Waris Shah*. In the tale, Ranjha, parted from his beloved Heer, climbs to Tilla Jogian in his grief and there renounces the world: the ascetic *Bal Nath*, master of the hill, pierces his ears and initiates him into the order of the *jogis*, granting him *jog*, the discipline of renunciation. Through this episode the hill entered the imaginative life of the Punjab as the very emblem of the lover-turned-ascetic, and generations who never climbed its slopes knew its name from the recitation of Waris Shah's verses.

Waris Shah dates his poem "in the year 1180 Hijri" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 56, folio 55). In Usborne's English, Ranjha sets out for Tilla telling himself that "Balnath surely knows the way of salvation", and "after many days' journeying, Ranjha reached Tilla the hill on which Balnath lived" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 31, folio 30). He "bowed his head and placed a piece of gur" before the guru "as an offering, and clasped the feet of all the Jogis" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 31, folio 30). The annotator explains the gift: "Gur is raw sugar. It is well known as a ceremonial offering in the Punjab countryside" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 33, folio 32).

The poem sets caste against the vocation: "You Jats cannot attain Jog" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 31, folio 30), and "What can a Jat do with a beggars bowl or horn, whose heart is set only on ploughing?" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 32, folio 31). Ranjha's own name, the annotator notes, is a caste: "Ranjha is still a well-known caste of the Jats in the old Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 3, folio 2). Bal Nath sets out the conditions of *jog*: "You will have to dress as a Jogi, to wear dirty clothes, long hair, cropped skull and to beg your way through life. You will have to give up the pleasures of birth, to cease to rejoice when friends come or to grieve when they die. You will have to abstain from casting eyes on women. You will have to become divinely intoxicated by taking kand, mul, post, opium and other narcotic drugs" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 31, folio 30). A jogi must travel as well: "You will have to go on long pilgrimages to Jagannath, Godavari, Ganges and Jamna" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 31, folio 30). Ranjha, for his part, admits that the vocation is a means to his love: "Had I been only a lover of God, I should have sought only Him… Hir has captivated my heart. That is why I have become a Jogi" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 32, folio 31).

The rite of initiation is the fullest ritual description in the book. "They obeyed the orders of their Guru and brought Balnath the earrings as he had told them, and the razor wherewith to shave Ranjha. And the Guru took off Ranjha's clothes and having rubbed him in ashes and embraced him, made him sit by his side. Then he took the razor of separation and shaved him completely. Then he bored his ears and put earrings on him. He gave him the beggar's bowl, the rosary, the horn and the shell in his hands", and last he taught him the word Allah (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 32, folio 31). With the rite came a moral rule: "An old woman should be treated as your mother and a young woman as your sister" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 32, folio 31).

The annotator stresses what the scene shows of its time: "Here Ranjha, a Muslim, becomes a spiritual disciple of Balnath, a Hindu yogi. This may seem strange today, but was by no means unusual at the time" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 33, folio 32, note 4). In the same note he adds that "A Murshid is a spiritual teacher" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 33, folio 32, note 4).

The *chelas* turn on their guru: "They left the Jogi's house and kitchen. They pierced the Guru with their shameless taunts" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 32, folio 31). Bal Nath himself comes to regret his gift: "Verily I repent and am sorry for having given Jog to this youth. He has got the treasures of jog without spending a single farthing" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 32, folio 31). A protest in the poem names the hill again: "Had I known that you would try to keep me from my Love, I would never have set foot on your hill of Tilla" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 32, folio 31). At last "Ranjha made haste to leave Tilla and he collected strange herbs and potent roots from the woods" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 34, folio 33).

Ear-boring is the act that makes a jogi, and the poem returns to it again and again after Ranjha leaves the hill: "Perhaps he has obeyed my word and got his ears bored" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 40, folio 39); "He went to Tilla and got his ears bored and became a Jogi. He has today entered your village" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 41, folio 40); "Fate has shaved your beard and bored your ears" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 43, folio 42); "He has bored his ears and has become a Jogi for my sake" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 45, folio 44). At the end the marks are removed: "They took the Jogi's rings out of his ears" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 54, folio 53).

## Legends and Miracles

Waris Shah's guru commands by enchantment. When the *chelas* rebel, "Whereupon the Guru rebuked them and his anger blazed from his eyes. The Chelas instantly obeyed him, so powerful was the enchantment that the Guru laid upon them. All ill feeling vanished from their minds" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 32, folio 31).

At the hill, too, a prayer is answered aloud. Bal Nath "prayed and poured ashes over his body and plunged his soul into the deep waters of meditation"; then "there came a reply from the Darbar of God": "Hir has been bestowed on Ranjha and his boat has been taken ashore" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 33, folio 32).

The annotator denies the guru two powers. "Balnath obviously could not read Ranjha's thoughts", he writes, as jogis often claim to do (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 33, folio 32, note 6); and "Balnath apparently had no power to take back what he had given" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 34, folio 33, note 16). He also records a popular belief: "According to popular superstition in the Punjab countryside", Pirs, Fakirs and Jogis can, by the force of their piety, "make the Moving Finger of Destiny cancel what it has written" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 31, folio 30, note 7).

## In Verse

Bulleh Shah's lyric, in Shackle's translation, follows the initiate to Tilla step by step:

*Casting aside his drum, he goes toward Tilla, he sits and meditates on his name. He has his ears split and the earrings inserted, and is initiated by the guru* (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 123, folio 98)

The lyric then names the authority a Pir must satisfy:

*My Pir gains the approval of guru Gorakh. He keeps crying 'Hir, Hir.'* (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 124, folio 99)

Shackle glosses the yogic vocabulary of Bulleh Shah's verse: "The 'unstruck music' (anhad nād) is the mystical sound heard at the climax of the yogic process"; "The 'circle of nectar' (amrit manḍal) is another traditional image for the ecstatic state that is the goal of yoga"; and the phrase 'make the Ganges flow backward' (ulṭī gangā bahāio) is "an idiom meaning 'to do the opposite of the usual,' which here refers to the drawing up of psychic energy during the yogic process" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 155, folio 130). Again: "The 'fourth state' (cauthā pad, Skt. turīyāvasthā-) is a yogic term that denotes the state of liberation achieved through transcending the three guṇas that govern worldly existence" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 176, folio 151, n. 2 on 71).

Waris Shah's poem, in Usborne's English, defines *jog* in its own terms:

*Jog means to be dead while alive. One has to sing the song of nonentity using ones meagre body as a guitar. One's self has to be entirely absorbed. It is no child's play.* (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 31, folio 30)

*God has made his abode in this body of dust He is in every thing as a thread runs through the beads.* (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 31, folio 30)

The annotator reads the 'Song of Nonentity' through Sufi eyes: "this is the doctrine of annihilation in God (fana fillah) as practised by the Muslim Sufis, which has strong spiritual affiliations with the 'Nirvana' of the Hindus and Buddhists" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 33, folio 32, note 10). The poem also names a degree of *jog* called Chit Akas, reached "after passing through the stages of Bhut Kas and Juda Kas" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 32, folio 31), which the annotator glosses only as "The three different planes of Jog Philosophy" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 33, folio 32, note 13).

## Setting: The Salt Mountain

Bhandari's account of the salt mountain on which he places Tilla speaks of the range, not of the hill itself. In the Sind Sagar doab (دوابه سندساگر), rock salt (نمک سنگ) is dug at the foot of the hills near Shamsabad, famous above all other salts and called نمک سندهه[OCR?] because it is produced in that doab (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 514). The miners, called الاشه کش[OCR?], dig tunnels "زیاده از دو صد سیصد درعه[OCR?] عمیق"; each goes in naked with a lamp in his hand and a pick on his shoulder, cuts a lump of about سه من and carries it out on his back; he is paid, "از ناظمان این امر اجرت گرفته", and the men feel no fear. The tunnels, the book adds بحکمت الهی, are not hot in summer or cold in winter (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 514).

Two large tunnels near Shamsabad, کهوهره[OCR?] و کهیوره[OCR?], yield "هر سال چندان لکهه من نمک", and their revenue, with that of other places, "بسرکار بادشاهی ضبط می شود" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 514). Craftsmen make trays, dishes, covers and lamp-stands of salt; the گچ شیرین found next to it is used for whitewash inside the houses of the great; and the stone is also made into dishes and انجوره[OCR?] (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 514).

Bhandari also reports a Hindu belief. The Kahyala pool, "کهنتا[OCR?] چهه[OCR?] کولابی[OCR?]", whose depth no one can tell, is held to be an ancient temple; on great days such as the sun's entry into Aries, Hindus crowd there بقصد تغسل[OCR?]. They believe the earth has two eyes: the right is the pool of بهکر[OCR?] near Ajmer, the left this pool (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 514). He cites the *Zafarnama* and the *Akbarnama*, "درظفرنامه و اکبرنامه ان راکوه جوده نوشته اند"; Jud was رئیس قوم جنجوهه, whose descendants still live and rule at the foot of the hills in the parganas "کرچاک[OCR?] و نندنه[OCR?] و کهیاله(۱)[OCR?]", the editor giving کهتاز[OCR?] as a variant for the last (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 514).

Writing of the Sind Sagar doab as a whole, Richard Eaton notes: "Only in the extreme western Punjab, in the Sind Sagar doab, where the Janjua Rajputs and Ghakkars dominated, were the Jats conspicuously absent as zamindars" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 225, folio 213). Lukas Werth, whose chapter in Werbner and Basu's *Embodying Charisma* studies the anonymous saints' shrines of a village in the range to which he gives a pseudonym, sets that village "In the mountains dividing the plains of the Punjab from the slightly elevated plateau of the Potwar" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 88, folio 77), and writes of "the salt range where there is no irrigation" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 93, folio 82).

## Decline and Present State

The complex flourished until the Partition of 1947, when the departure of its Hindu and yogi custodians for India left it without a resident community. Deprived of care, the buildings fell into decay; the reservoirs silted, the shrines crumbled, and in the decades since the site has been steadily overgrown — by the early twenty-first century much of it lay beneath a spread of wild olive and scrub, its masonry slipping into ruin. Conservationists and writers have repeatedly warned that this remarkable monument, at once archaeological site, sacred summit, and literary landmark, risks being lost altogether without deliberate protection. Its remoteness has spared it the crowds that press upon lower shrines, but that same isolation has hastened its neglect.

Against this stands the present-tense note of Usborne's later, unnamed annotator: "The place is still preserved, and the 'Jog Mat' or the cult of the wandering mystics, with its emphasis on pantheism, is still practised there" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 33, folio 32, note 1).

For the fate of such places after the division of the Punjab, Haroon Khalid, writing of the country at large and not of this hill, says: "I have seen several gurdwaras and temples being taken over and destroyed by the migrants" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **Who initiated Ranjha.** The site's account names Bal Nath, master of the hill. Usborne's translation of Waris Shah agrees, and its annotator calls Bal Nath "the fourth Guru of 'Tilla Guruan'" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 33, folio 32, note 1). Shackle's glossary to Bulleh Shah instead names "the great yogi Gorakhnath, from whom Ranjha sought initiation as a yogi" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 198, folio 173), and Bulleh Shah's lyric says that the initiate "is initiated by the guru" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 123, folio 98).
- **Gorakhnath's date.** The site's account says that tradition traces the hill's sanctity back some two thousand years and links its foundation to Gorakhnath, while scholarship usually places him c. 11th–12th century CE. Dhillon puts him "in the eleventh century" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 10, folio not stated), while the Puratan Janamsakhis, as he reports them, set him in conversation with Guru Nanak.
- **Gorakhnath's place in the order.** The site's account calls him the organiser of the Nath tradition. Boivin speaks of "an ascetic order founded by Gorakhnath" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 120, folio 107) and reports the belief that he is an avatar of Shiva (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 77, folio 64); Shackle calls him "the head of the Kanphat yogis" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 187, folio 162, n. 12 on 122).
- **The name of the hill and where it stands.** The site's account gives Tilla Jogian, the "Hill of the Ascetics", on the highest peak of the eastern Salt Range in Jhelum District. Shackle has Tilla Jogian "high up in the hills of District Jhelum" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 187, folio 162, n. 10 on 122). Usborne's translation says only 'Tilla', and its annotator gives 'Tilla Guruan', "the mound of the Gurus", "on the banks of the river Jhelum" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 33, folio 32, note 1). Bhandari calls the place تله[OCR?], with the editor's variant بله[OCR?], and sets it on the salt mountain seven kos from the fort of Rohtas (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 514).
- **The ear-ring.** The site's account speaks of great rings through the cartilage of the ear; Shackle of the cartilage pierced "to allow the insertion of a wooden ring" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 187, folio 162, n. 10 on 122).
- **The hill since 1947.** The site's account has the complex abandoned at Partition, without a resident community, and its buildings in ruin. Usborne's annotator, writing in the present tense, says that "The place is still preserved" and that the 'Jog Mat' "is still practised there" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 33, folio 32, note 1).

## Legacy

Tilla Jogian gathers into one windswept hilltop several of the great strands of Punjabi religious and cultural history: the antiquity of the *Nath* yogis and their Kanphata order; the pilgrim memory of Guru Nanak; and the deathless romance of Heer and Ranjha. As one of the trio of Salt Range sanctuaries alongside Katas Raj and the Amb temples, it testifies to a landscape once dense with Hindu holiness. For the modern traveller it offers a rare and melancholy grandeur — ruined shrines amid olive groves, commanding a vast prospect over the Jhelum plains — and stands as a reminder of a plural sacred past that survives now chiefly in stone and in song.

Writers on the romance, who speak of the tale rather than of the hill, show how it lives on in song. Shackle writes: "The greatest of these local legends is the story of Hir and Ranjha, which is set in the world of the Jat pastoral tribes of western Panjab" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 20, folio xix). Annemarie Schimmel writes that the romance, "elaborated in more than a hundred versions in Panjabi, Urdu, Sindhi, and Persian, was given its final form in the late eighteenth century by Warith Shah. It is his verse which is sung, time and again, wherever Panjabis meet" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 171, folio 153), and that "The visitor who stays in a Panjabi village will certainly find somebody to sing for him the old romance of Hir Ranjha in the classical form given it by Warith Shah" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 414, folio 383). Shemeem Burney Abbas records that Waris Shah "is said to have recited it with great emotion among his speech communities in the Panjab", and that "Many renowned folk artists in the Panjab, such as Reshma, Zahida Parvin, Nazir Begum, Sabiha Khanum, Tufail Niazi, and Iqbal Bahu, have sung it" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 123, folio not stated). Scott Kugle compares its place in Punjabi devotion with that of Radha and Krishna: "Punjabi poets and their eager audiences poured into Hir and Ranjha the same erotic emotion that was invested in Radha and Krishna in regions closer to the Ganges. The story of Hir and Ranjha became a favorite Sufi device for conveying the value of erotic passion in spiritual life" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 228, folio 207). Bhandari, in his account of the suba of Lahore, already records that wonderful verses and designs on the love of Heer and Ranjha were composed and sung there (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 512–511).
