## Overview

The Katas Raj Temples (*Qila Katas*) form one of the most venerable Hindu sacred complexes in Pakistan, set amid the arid folds of the Salt Range in the Potohar Plateau, near the town of Choa Saidanshah in Chakwal District, Punjab. Perched at roughly 2,000 feet, the site clusters a group of ancient shrines around a spring-fed emerald pond, the *Katas kund*, whose waters Hindus revere as profoundly holy. A short distance to the south-west stands a second cluster known as the *Satghara*, or "seven temples", the architectural and ceremonial heart of the complex. Occupied across many centuries and many faiths, Katas Raj preserves layers of Buddhist, Hindu Shahi, Sikh and later occupation, making it at once a *tirtha* (place of pilgrimage), an archaeological palimpsest, and a poignant emblem of the subcontinent's shared heritage.

Three of the books consulted name the site itself. Haroon Khalid, who writes of it in two books, calls it 'Katas Raj or Killa Katas', or simply Katas, and gives a chapter to it under the title "Shivratri at Killa Katas" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 5, folio not stated; p. 45, folio not stated). He describes it as "a holy site for thousands of years" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 45, folio not stated), and in *Walking with Nanak* he calls Katas Raj, in the voice of his reconstruction of Guru Nanak's time, "the holiest of holy temples in Punjab", "a collection of temples located around a sacred pond" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 236, folio 210). Harish Dhillon's retelling of the *Janamsakhis*, the traditional lives of Guru Nanak, names 'Katasraj' among the shrines Nanak and Mardana visited (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 224, folio 246). Khalid places the complex "Situated near the small town of Kalar Kahar" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 45, folio not stated), and, in his later book, a few kilometres from Kallar Kahar on the Motorway (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 245, folio 219); the present account gives the nearby town as Choa Saidanshah, and both placings are set out below under the sources' disagreements.

Khalid's two visits frame most of what the books record. In *A White Trail* he reports the Shivratri of "2nd March 2011", when Hindu pilgrims from across Pakistan came to the pond (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 47, folio not stated). In *Walking with Nanak* he returns years later, "driving back to Islamabad after a weekend in Lahore", to look for the place where Guru Nanak is remembered at Katas (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 245, folio 219).

## Mythology and the Tears of Shiva

The sanctity of Katas rests above all upon the sacred pond and the legend attached to it. According to Puranic tradition, when the goddess Sati immolated herself, her consort Shiva was plunged into such inconsolable grief that he wandered the earth weeping. His tears, it is said, fell to form two sacred pools: one at Pushkar in Rajasthan, and the other here at Katas. The very name is popularly derived from the Sanskrit *kataksha*, meaning "tearful eyes" or "raining eyes", a poetic echo of the god's sorrow. The pond is thus held to be filled with the grief of Shiva himself, and bathing in its waters is believed to cleanse the pilgrim of sin.

Khalid gives the legend as the pilgrims and guides tell it. In *Walking with Nanak* the pool, "constructed in the shape of an eye", "was created when Lord Shiva shed a tear after his wife Sati passed away, or so the story goes", and the tear "could wash away the gravest of sins" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 236, folio 210); earlier in the same book the pond is "considered to have been filled up by a tear drop of Lord Shiva" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 21, folio 3). On his last visit he writes of "The sacred pond, believed to be the tear of Lord Shiva" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 246, folio 220).

In *A White Trail* the legend comes from Shakeel, a young guide at the site, who tells visitors that the Hindus believe the pond was formed from a tear Shiva shed at the death of Sati (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 43, folio not stated). Shakeel's version names a second pond, not at Pushkar but in India at Ajmer: the pond is "in the shape of an eye", from Shiva's tear, and "Another tear fall in Ajmer Sharif, India, where there is another ancient pond" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 43, folio not stated). He also warns: "No one knows how deep it is", and adds that "There are poisonous lizards and snakes living inside" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 43, folio not stated). He tells tourists, too, a story of Ganesh as "the son of the Hindu God, Shiva" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 42, folio not stated).

Beside the myth Shakeel keeps what Khalid calls his own "truth": that the whole place was once "under the sea", and that the pond was formed when the land emerged. As evidence he points to a leaf fossil, and to a "fauna fossil" in a cave "across the road from the complex", a cave with stalactites (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 43, folio not stated). Elsewhere Khalid names Katas with Hinglaj as an ancient Hindu temple and place of pilgrimage around which "Such myths have developed" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 64, folio not stated).

## The Pandavas and the Epic

Katas is also woven into the great epic tradition. According to legend, the region corresponds to the *Dvaitavana* of the *Mahabharata*, and the Satghara is traditionally identified as the place where the five Pandava brothers made their home during a portion of their twelve-year exile. It is here, tradition holds, that the celebrated dialogue of the *Yaksha Prashna* unfolded, when a *yaksha* guarding the waters posed a series of riddles to the Pandavas, testing the wisdom of Yudhishthira. Such associations gave the site enduring prestige among pilgrims across the subcontinent.

Khalid records the tradition in a different form: "It is believed that these temples were constructed by the Pandavs [OCR?] brothers when they were sent on a thirteen year exile", after the Kauravas had taken the throne, and "In the Mahabharata these temples are mentioned as sacred spaces" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 236, folio 210). In *A White Trail* the guide Shakeel dates the temples "from the time of the Mahabharata" and says the exiled Pandavas built them, a claim, Khalid writes, "confirmed by a blue board put up by the authorities here, noting the date of Mahabharata to be around 1500 BCE" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 44, folio not stated).

Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian history *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, in a passage about the Pandavas and not about Katas, carries the brothers through this part of the country at the end of their story. After Yudhishthira's abdication, at the coming of the age the book calls the *Kalijug*, the five brothers and Draupadi went through Bengal, the Deccan, Gujarat and Dwarka, and then toured the lands of Thatta (the name uncertainly read), Multan and Punjab, before going to the mountain of Badrinath (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 479). Bhandari says nothing more of these lands and names no temple in them.

## The Temple Complex and Architecture

The Satghara temples represent a distinctive regional building tradition, generally attributed to the Hindu Shahi (Odi Shahi) rulers who dominated the north-western subcontinent from roughly the seventh to the tenth centuries CE. The principal shrine, sometimes designated "Temple A" by surveyors, is dated to around the tenth century and executed in a Gandhara-influenced *Nagara* idiom, with squat proportions, trefoil-arched recesses and fluted decorative work characteristic of the Salt Range group. The temples are built of the local kanjur stone and were once richly plastered, some retaining traces of the fresco decoration that once enlivened their interiors.

The site's religious history reaches still deeper. The seventh-century Chinese pilgrim Xuanzang recorded a substantial Buddhist stupa in the vicinity, and the British archaeologist Alexander Cunningham, who surveyed the complex as Director-General of the Archaeological Survey of India in 1872, likewise documented Buddhist remains alongside the Hindu shrines, confirming a long sequence of sacred occupation. A *baradari* (pillared pavilion) and other later structures cluster near the pond, and small residential quarters attest to the site's role as a living pilgrimage centre well into the modern era.

Khalid's first description of the complex is a list: "a natural pond, fossils dating back to millions of years, ancient caves, an unexcavated Buddhist stupa, Hindu temples said to be thousands of years old, and a university which attracted scholars from other parts of the world" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 45, folio not stated). There are "several small temples built around the pond, dedicated to the different Hindu deities"; "an iron fence protects the complex", with "only one entrance", though a visitor can also get in by "climbing the mound" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 45, folio not stated).

The pond, in his account, is "turquoise" and "round". A rock divides off a small section of clear water "only three feet deep", and "Ancient buildings stand at the edge of the deeper end"; "The pond is clearly the lifeline of this complex. Numerous myths have developed around it" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 43, folio not stated). A photograph in the book carries the caption "The historical temple of Katas Raj with its emerald pool." (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 106, folio not stated).

The Shiva shrine stands "next to the pool": "a small light brown building, with a small dome at the top", with "a wooden door, covered with floral decorations" and a marble floor, and at its centre "the Shivling"; it is "a small room, which can only accommodate about ten people" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 52, folio not stated). The temples themselves are "the three tall cone shaped structures" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 44, folio not stated). Of their age Khalid sets two views side by side: the guide's, that they date "from the time of the Mahabharata", and his own, that "Experts say that these temples were constructed between the 7th and 10th century CE, when this area was under the influence of the Kashmiri kingdom" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 44, folio not stated). In *Walking with Nanak* he calls them the Kashmiri temples dedicated to Shiva, "built in the seventh and eighth century" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 246, folio 220).

The tallest building is a Sikh-period house: "the haveli of Hari Singh Nalwa, a general in the army of Maharaja Ranjit Singh (1780-1839)", "the tallest here, standing like a cube with a small dome at the top". Inside, "The artwork has been recently renovated with depictions of Hindu deities on the walls", among them Ganesh and Krishna "playing the flute while he flirts with the maidens" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 42, folio not stated). In *Walking with Nanak* the haveli of Hari Singh Nalwa stands in front, with the Shiva temples behind it (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 246, folio 220).

Next to the haveli is "a Buddhist stupa that has been partially excavated". Shakeel dates it "from the 3rd century BC, from the time of Taxila", and "More than half of the structure is still underground" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 43, folio not stated). In *Walking with Nanak* Khalid writes of a "half excavated stupa" that is "at least four thousand years old", with "the temple dedicated to Ram" beside it (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 246, folio 220).

Across the road, on a mound, an "ancient staircase" leads up to "a newly constructed single storey rectangular structure" which Shakeel calls "the site of a world famous ancient Hindu university" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 44, folio not stated). The building, put up "a few years ago", "acts as the Archaeology Department office" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 45, folio not stated). In *Walking with Nanak* Khalid likewise places "the offices of the Archaeology Department" and "a small hotel" across the road from the pond (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 246, folio 220).

## Al-Beruni and the Seat of Learning

The university on the mound carries one of the site's most repeated claims. According to Shakeel, "Al-Beruni, a Muslim scholar from the 11th century studied Hindu religion" there, and "calculated the radius of world" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 44, folio not stated). Khalid takes up the story in his own voice: "Al-Beruni came to India during the first half of the 11th century, after the Muslim invader Shah Mahmood Ghaznvi attacked Punjab. Al-Beruni arrived at this temple, which was known as a seat of learning at that time. He spent several years here" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 44, folio not stated).

## Guru Nanak at Katas

Katas holds significance for Sikhs as well as Hindus. Tradition records that Guru Nanak, the founder of Sikhism, visited the site, and a Gurdwara associated with his memory was later raised near the pond.

The books carry the visit in three forms. Dhillon's *Janamsakhis* has Nanak, on his return to the Punjab, visit with Mardana "the shrines of Tilla Balnath and Katasraj (both now in Pakistan)" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 224, folio 246); on the same page the book has him take a south-eastern route back to the Punjab at the close of his fourth journey, around the time of Babur's third incursion into India (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 224, folio 246). Khalid, in *A White Trail*, writes that Guru Nanak "(1469-1539) also came here, as it was a popular destination for ascetics at that time", and cites for it Iqbal Qaiser's *Historical Sikh Shrines* (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 45, folio not stated). In *Walking with Nanak* he writes that "historical records clearly state Nanak visited this sacred site" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 245, folio 219).

The third form is Khalid's own imagined scene, which he presents as reconstruction rather than record. He sets it at "the annual festival of Shivratri", on "a cold February night". Katas Raj, "Situated on the slope of the mountain", is a cluster of temples around the pond; the Shiva temple "at the edge of the pool" is "the most important temple here", and there are temples "dedicated to the goddesses Kali and Durga" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 236, folio 210). "Pilgrims from hundreds of kilometres away came here to purify their lives"; some devotees bathe the Shiva shrine "with milk" while others sing his praises, and offerings placed in the pond are carried off by a stream that runs into the mountains, a passage damaged in the printed text (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 236, folio 210). In "a small cave" sit "a few yogis, devotees of Lord Shiva", in meditation, while pilgrims climb to temples higher up (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 237, folio 211).

In that scene Nanak sits "inside the temple amongst the devotees", while Mardana waits on the neighbouring mountain, because "being a Muslim, Mardana would not have been allowed inside such a sacred Hindu space" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 237, folio 211). Khalid's reconstruction puts the temple "about fifty kos" from Talwandi, printed 'Talwindi' [OCR?], and has yogis whom Nanak had met there spend time at Katas (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 237, folio 211). Later in the same chapter he imagines Nanak at a Shiva temple that the book does not name or tie to Katas, where milk poured over the *lingam* runs out by "a small passageway" into a stream (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 240, folio not stated), the priest declares "Shiva has the power to destroy the world", Nanak calls it "one of the most sacred temples dedicated to Shiva" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 241, folio 215), and Nanak says: "My Shiva is the Supreme Creator" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 242, folio 216).

Katas enters Khalid's book once before Nanak himself. In his reconstruction of the lives of Nanak's parents, Tripta and her husband, on their way home from Tilla Jogian, "stopped at Katas Raj, where both of them took a dip in the holy pond" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 21, folio 3).

## The Search for Nanak's Gurdwara

Khalid's last visit is a search for the building that remembers Nanak. He had come "several times" before without seeing it. "A few years ago", when he came "to document the festival of Maha Shivratri", the local guide told him "there was no such building here"; yet, he writes, Iqbal Qaiser "had seen and photographed the place for his book" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 245, folio 219). This time, with his wife Anam, he stopped "to see Nanak's Gurdwara" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 245, folio 219).

Again a guide insisted: "There is no gurdwara here". Khalid telephoned Iqbal Qaiser, who told him, "It is next to the fort", and the only fort at Katas was one "built by Hari singh Nalwa" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 247, folio 221). The building he found "was at a little distance from the complex. It was not renovated as the rest of the complex was"; the path to it "had been neglected". It was "a big building, with several pillars that once supported a roof", but "The roof was now gone", and "a staircase leading nowhere" stood in it. It was "bigger than I had expected", and the guide told him: "We never knew what this building was" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 247, folio 221).

## Sikh Patronage

Under the Sikh Empire of Maharaja Ranjit Singh in the early nineteenth century, the complex received renewed patronage, with repairs and plastering to the principal structures attributed to the noble Sardar Gulab Singh.

Khalid's books tie the Sikh period at Katas instead to Hari Singh Nalwa, Ranjit Singh's general, whose haveli is the tallest building in the complex (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 42, folio not stated) and whose fort stands near the ruin of Nanak's gurdwara (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 247, folio 221). Of Ranjit Singh in general, and not of Katas, *Walking with Nanak* writes that when he "became the sovereign of Punjab at the end of the eighteenth century he spent large amounts of money on the renovation and expansion of many gurdwaras", and "attached vast tracts of land to these gurdwaras, so that the revenues from the lands could facilitate their functioning" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 34, folio 16). *A White Trail* calls him "the first Punjabi to rule Punjab", who renovated "many Gurdwaras all over Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 208, folio not stated).

## Partition, Neglect and Restoration

The Partition of 1947 severed the temples from their community of worshippers, and the site fell into prolonged neglect, its shrines decaying and its pond periodically depleted. In recent decades Katas has drawn renewed attention. In 2005 the Government of Pakistan undertook a programme of restoration, and cross-border pilgrimages by Hindu devotees from India have occasionally been permitted. Controversy has attended the site as well, notably concern over the alarming drop in the sacred pond's water level, attributed to industrial extraction by nearby cement factories, an issue that reached the country's Supreme Court.

Khalid tells the same history from the ground. Before Partition Katas was "a popular religious site for the Hindus", and "Thousands of pilgrims used to descend here from faraway places on the occasion of Shivratri" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 45, folio not stated). "This temple was abandoned as a result of partition", its buildings "falling into disrepair and the pond being polluted", and "a few cement factories opened in the surrounding area and started sucking the water out from here" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 45–46, folio not stated). No one guarded the place; "Several phone numbers and names are still engraved" on its walls, and the few Hindus who came did so "pretending to be tourists" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 46, folio not stated).

Prem Gupta, who first came in 2003, remembered: "There were boys jumping from the windows and alleys of temples into this sacred pond. They would leave juice boxes and chips wrappers in the water" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 46, folio not stated). "Things changed for the shrine in 2005, when the veteran Indian politician L.K Advani came here and expressed displeasure", and the Government of Pakistan began the work of renovation (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 46, folio not stated). "Major renovation work of the temple was undertaken by the Department in 2005": the pond was cleaned, the buildings "painted and restored", and "blue boards were raised" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 45, folio not stated). The Archaeology Department appointed Shakeel, "a 25 year old graduate" from Kalar Kahar, and a few others as guides when the site was renovated (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 42–43, folio not stated).

By the time of Khalid's later visit the pond "had recently been renovated" and was "brimming with water", free of the garbage he had seen "the last time", and "Muslim boys" were diving into it from the rooftops (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 246, folio 220). The water level "had started to fall" because "the cement factories around this area were sucking the groundwater"; after writers and journalists took it up, "the government woke from its slumber", and "The water level has now been restored" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 246, folio 220). The cement factories have changed the road as well: the National Highway Authority was widening the single-lane road to Katas, and "Cement factories dotting this area" had lobbied hard for it (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 245, folio 219).

The restoration did not hold everywhere. In 2011 there was "a small building, the only restaurant in the area", and Khalid notes that "Just a few years ago, this place would have refused to serve these people", so that "untouchability, at least here, has receded"; the pilgrims had "the only washroom the pilgrims can use", which closed "at eleven in the night" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 48–49, folio not stated). "There is no electricity". Haroon Sarab Diyal, who led the pilgrims from Peshawar, told him: "The authorities have not paid the bill of this temple for the past three months", and "Ever since the Indians have stopped coming, they have become negligent again. The temple is falling into disrepair" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 49–50, folio not stated).

## Shivratri and Pilgrimage

The great festival of Katas is Shivratri, the night of Shiva. Khalid describes it, as a festival and not as this site's alone, as "a Hindu festival dedicated to the worship of Lord Shiva" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 45, folio not stated), and writes: "The celebration of Shivratri, which literally means the 'the night of Shiva' includes the worship of Shiva throughout the night, as well as a symbolic marriage between him and his counterpart Parvati" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 51, folio not stated). Dhillon, writing of Maha Shivratri at Achal, near Batala in Gurdaspur, and not of Katas, calls it "the great night of Shiva" and the day "Shiva was married to Parvati" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 240–241, folio 265 / not stated).

After the restoration of 2005 the festival drew pilgrims from India. "In the subsequent year" the government invited Indian pilgrims for Shivratri: "About three hundred pilgrims came, which became national news". They stayed "for three days", with security, shelter and "free food", lodged "at the building at the site of the ancient university" in an area cordoned off. "This continued until 2008". After the Mumbai attacks of "between the 26th and 29th of November 2008", "Pilgrims didn't come to Katas for Shivratri in 2009" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 46, folio not stated). In 2010 Hindu organisations wrote to the Archaeology Department, the ETPB, the police and the Ministry of Minorities, and "did not receive any response"; even so, "About two thousand Hindus came to Katas from various cities of Pakistan unannounced, including Lahore, Rahim Yar Khan, Peshawar and Nowshera" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 46–47, folio not stated).

Khalid was at Katas for the Shivratri of 2011. In the afternoon pilgrims were preparing for the night, "for which about two thousand pilgrims would pour in from different parts of the country", and Hindu children "who have come from Gujranawala" were playing on the steps of the pond (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 42, folio not stated). Prem Gupta's banner read "'Welcome Yatrees (pilgrims)'" and bore a picture of Lord Shiva, and a canopy had been raised inside the complex (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 47, folio not stated). On the banner, "A snake is coiled around his neck. A third eye, which he can use to destroy the world, is visible on his forehead. The moon is in his hair" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 47, folio not stated).

Only "five officials" of the police stood at the gate, which Khalid reads as "an indication of the scant importance that Hindu pilgrims are accorded" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 47, folio not stated). A professor and students from the NCA were turned back ("Muslims are not allowed inside today," a police official said), while Hindu boys went in wearing a "Maha Shivratri" tag (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 47–48, folio not stated). About a hundred pilgrims came from Peshawar "in two large buses", led by Haroon Sarab Diyal, and more from Nowshera, Rahim Yaar Khan and Bahawalpur (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 49, folio not stated); once all the buses had come, "There are about a thousand people" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 49, folio not stated). A DSP at first refused to open the department's hall, citing "the security conditions of the country", and then opened one hall for the women, the children and the old (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 50–51, folio not stated).

The next morning the Archaeology Department's officials "find their offices invaded and the temples full of sleeping pilgrims", and junior officials threw the pilgrims' luggage, their *langar* utensils and their rice onto the road (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 52–53, folio not stated). "It is too cold to take a bath", so the pilgrims poured the pond's water over their heads, as "a substitute to performing a ritual ablution or ashnan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 53, folio not stated).

The books record no festival at Katas other than Shivratri. Khalid's reconstruction of Guru Nanak's visit is also set at Shivratri (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 236, folio 210). The sources consulted give no verse, hymn or inscription about Katas itself.

## Shiva in the Books

Some of what the books say of Shiva is said of the god in general, and is given here as context for the shrine of the *lingam* at the pond, not as a description of this site. Khalid writes that the Shivling "represents the potent force of Lord Shiv" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 52, folio not stated). In *In Search of Shiva*, his study of folk religious practices in Pakistan, which names no site of the Salt Range, he writes: "In Hinduism, Shiva is depicted as a lingam which is a phallus. In Hindu temples, the lingam is placed inside a structure called yoni, signifying the vagina. Together they represent the divine power of procreation" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated).

The same book, citing Mahadev Chakravarti's *The Concept of Rudra Śiva Through the Ages*, says that in post-Vedic literature "Shiva is known as the god of procreation" and that "Shiva is also worshipped as the god of cultivation" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated). It reports the view of the archaeologist Mark Kenoyer that seals from Mohenjo-daro showing a figure seated in a yogic posture "could actually be a prototype of Lord Shiva, one of the most important Hindu deities", and notes that "In Hinduism, Shiva worship and worship of a snake go hand in hand" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated). Its thesis is that the shrine practices Khalid met, "Even though Islamic in its garb", had roots in "the different spiritual traditions that developed around the cult of Lord Shiva" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 6, Introduction, folio not stated).

Bhandari's *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* names the god Mahadev. Every temple it ascribes to Mahadev lies outside Pakistan, in the parts of the empire the book treats as the subas of Kashmir, Gujarat and Berar, and none is in the Salt Range. Of one it writes that there is a temple ascribed to Mahadev whose name is printed as something like Nausar, a reading that is uncertain, where pilgrims hear a sound of worship, and no one knows where it comes from (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 507).

## Setting

The pilgrims Khalid met at Katas came from far away because the Hindus of Pakistan live, for the most part, far from the Salt Range. In *A White Trail* he writes, of the country and not of this site, that most of Pakistan's Hindus live "in the 'faraway' 'peripheral' regions of Southern Punjab, inner Sindh and Baluchistan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 12, folio not stated), and that "The majority of the Hindu community is based in Sindh and Southern Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 233, folio not stated). Of Central Punjab, where he did his research, he writes: "My perception is that the conditions under which members of the Hindu community live in Central Punjab are the harshest compared to the other regions" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 233, folio not stated). "A lot of Hindus in Punjab do this, passing off as Muslims or Christians by taking up non-Hindu names. This is a survival technique in a hostile environment" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 39, folio not stated).

Of the fate of other sacred buildings in the province after 1947, Khalid writes of "the hundreds of temples and Gurdwaras all over Pakistani Punjab" taken over by refugees and turned into houses (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 58, folio not stated), and in *In Search of Shiva*: "I have seen several gurdwaras and temples being taken over and destroyed by the migrants" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The nearby town.** The site's account places the complex near the town of Choa Saidanshah in Chakwal District. Khalid places it "near the small town of Kalar Kahar" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 45, folio not stated), and a few kilometres from Kallar Kahar on the Motorway (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 245, folio 219).
- **The age and builders of the temples.** The site's account attributes the Satghara temples to the Hindu Shahi (Odi Shahi) rulers of roughly the seventh to the tenth centuries CE, and dates the principal shrine to around the tenth century. Khalid gives the experts' dating as "between the 7th and 10th century CE, when this area was under the influence of the Kashmiri kingdom" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 44, folio not stated), and elsewhere calls them Kashmiri temples "built in the seventh and eighth century" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 246, folio 220). The guide Shakeel dates them "from the time of the Mahabharata", with a board at the site giving "around 1500 BCE" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 44, folio not stated).
- **The Pandavas at Katas.** The site's account has the Pandavas make their home at the Satghara during part of a twelve-year exile. Khalid reports the belief that the Pandavas built the temples, during "a thirteen year exile" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 236, folio 210); Shakeel too says the exiled Pandavas built them (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 44, folio not stated).
- **The second pond.** The site's account has Shiva's tears form two pools, one at Pushkar in Rajasthan and one at Katas. Shakeel puts the second tear at "Ajmer Sharif, India, where there is another ancient pond" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 43, folio not stated). The site's account speaks of Shiva's tears; Khalid speaks of a single tear (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 236, folio 210; p. 21, folio 3).
- **The age of the stupa.** The site's account says that Xuanzang recorded a substantial Buddhist stupa in the vicinity in the seventh century, and gives it no date of building. Shakeel dates the stupa "from the 3rd century BC, from the time of Taxila" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 43, folio not stated); *Walking with Nanak* calls it "at least four thousand years old" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 246, folio 220).
- **Whether the stupa is excavated.** *A White Trail* calls it "a Buddhist stupa that has been partially excavated" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 43, folio not stated), and two pages later lists "an unexcavated Buddhist stupa" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 45, folio not stated). *Walking with Nanak* calls it "half excavated" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 246, folio 220).
- **The Sikh-period builder.** The site's account attributes the Sikh-period repairs and plastering to Sardar Gulab Singh. Khalid's books name no Gulab Singh at Katas and tie the Sikh-period haveli and fort to Hari Singh Nalwa (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 42, folio not stated; Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 247, folio 221).
- **Where Nanak's gurdwara stands.** The site's account places the gurdwara associated with Guru Nanak near the pond. Khalid found it "at a little distance from the complex", next to the fort, unrestored and roofless, after guides had told him there was no gurdwara at Katas at all (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 247, folio 221).
- **The numbers at Shivratri, 2011.** Khalid first reports that "about two thousand pilgrims would pour in" for the night (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 42, folio not stated); once the buses had arrived he counts "about a thousand people" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 49, folio not stated).

## Legacy

Today the Katas Raj Temples stand as one of the most significant surviving monuments of Hindu civilisation in Pakistan, a place where myth, epic and archaeology converge. The complex embodies the deep antiquity of Hindu worship in the Salt Range and remains a treasured pilgrimage destination and heritage site, at once fragile and enduring, its weathered shrines still mirrored in the tears of Shiva.

The books add the shape of that survival. For Hindus of Pakistan it is still the place they come to at Shivratri, from Peshawar and Nowshera, Lahore, Bahawalpur and Rahim Yar Khan, even in years when no one answered their letters (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 46–47, folio not stated). For Khalid and his guide Iqbal Qaiser it keeps the memory of Guru Nanak's visit, in a ruined building that the site's own guides could not name (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 247, folio 221). And for its guides it remains a place of many stories at once, Shiva's tear beside an ancient sea, the Pandavas beside Al-Beruni, which, as Khalid writes of the pond, is "clearly the lifeline of this complex" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 43, folio not stated).
