## Overview

Immediately beside Lahore Fort and the Badshahi Mosque, on the spot where Maharaja Ranjit Singh's body was cremated, stands the samadhi built in his memory — one of the grandest funerary monuments of the Sikh Empire. Ranjit Singh, the "Lion of Punjab," founded and ruled the Sikh Empire from 1801 until his death on 27 June 1839. Though formally a samadhi rather than a constituted gurdwara, the site has long functioned as a living place of Sikh devotion, and the Evacuee Trust Property Board administers it as one of the most visited symbols of Sikh heritage remaining in Pakistan.

Three of the books consulted describe the monument itself. Kanhaiya Lal's history of Lahore, whose author was also the engineer charged with keeping the building standing, surveys it from the gateway to the gilded finial (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 217, folio 220; Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 222–223, folios 225–226). Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti's *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, a survey of the tombs and buildings around Lahore, gives the building a section of its own, headed "The Late Maharaja's Samadh" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 569), and a long account of the Maharaja's family, reign and court (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 576; Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 592). Haroon Khalid's *A White Trail* gives a chapter to the commemoration of the Maharaja's death anniversary at the samadhi in 2011 (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 6, folio not stated).

The other books speak of the man, not of the monument. Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori's *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, a tazkira of the saints of the Punjab, says nothing at all about the samadhi, the funeral or the year of his death, but it gives a scene at his deathbed, from the side of a Muslim faqir, and portrays him as a devotee of Lahore's majzubs (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 244, folio 232; Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 246, folio 234). Richard Eaton, and Haroon Khalid in *Walking with Nanak* and *In Search of Shiva*, mention him only as a ruler who endowed, built or confiscated elsewhere. All of this is given below as being about Ranjit Singh, not about his samadhi.

## The Maharaja's Forebears

Nur Ahmad Chishti introduces the Sikh *misls* in order to explain the Maharaja's family. The eighth misl was the Sukarchakia, as the uncertain print seems to read, and its sardar Charat Singh he calls the progenitor of Maharaja Ranjit Singh (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 576). His genealogy, as printed, begins in 1470 CE (the figure is uncertain) with one Kalu at a village whose printed name, perhaps Bandri Bothi, cannot be read with certainty; a son went to a village near Amritsar, perhaps Sansrahi, among the Sansi people (both readings uncertain), whose trade was banditry; and the family passed through villages printed uncertainly as Sandh and Kahali to Sukarchak in 1555, names and year again uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 577). It kept shops in salt and tobacco; one Bahara (the name is uncertain) became the *chela* of a Sikh, stopped shaving, and died in 1679, a year also uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 578).

Budh Singh, in Chishti's account, took the *pahul* at Amritsar, was a great robber (the word is uncertain in the printed text), rode a piebald mare, and died in 1716. The book then divides the line: from Naudh Singh, it says, began Ranjit Singh's family, and from a Singh whose printed name, perhaps Chanda Singh, cannot be read with certainty came the family of the Sandhanwalias (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 578). Naudh Singh was shot in 1742 CE, and Charat Singh built a fort, a *garhi* as the uncertain print seems to read, in the village of Gujranwala (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 578). Charat Singh became sardar, fought toward Jammu, and died at the age of forty-seven, his age uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 579). Of the Mughal side of these years Chishti records that Khwaja Abid, the *subadar* of Lahore, attacked Charat Singh's fort, and that victory fell to the Sikhs (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 578).

Mahan Singh succeeded as a boy of ten. In 1774 CE, a year uncertain in the printed text, he married the daughter of one Mahan Singh, ruler of a place perhaps Jind, a reading that cannot be made certain, and the printed line goes on, with an illegible word, to the birth of Maharaja Ranjit Singh; Mahan Singh died when one whose name is illegible in the printed text was twelve, and the mother sat on the *gaddi* (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 579). Lahori's tazkira names Mahan Singh, Ranjit Singh's father, as the first to seize the relic collection of Rasulnagar (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 257, folio 245).

## The Life of the Maharaja

Chishti dates the birth plainly: Ranjit Singh was born in 1780. The boy had smallpox, and the illness cost him one eye; a garbled line that follows says that someone killed someone's mother for misconduct, and leaves it uncertain who killed whom (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 579). Haroon Khalid gives his years as "Maharaja Ranjit Singh (1780-1839), the erstwhile ruler of Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 42, folio not stated).

Lahori's tazkira records one dated episode of his youth. After Mahan Singh's death, in 1211 AH, when Shah Zaman Durrani came from Kabul, Ranjit Singh in fear sent his best goods and the relics of Rasulnagar from Gujranwala to his mother-in-law Sada Kaur at the fort of Makerian (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 258, folio 246). He later pressed for them more than once and was refused; under stronger pressure she moved them to the fort of Chunda (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 258, folio 246). At last, when he had seized all her property, she gave them to his son Sher Singh, her grandson, who kept them by him until the day he was murdered (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 258, folio 246). The same page carries the collection on through Hira Singh and Jawahar Singh to Lahore Fort under the English (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 258). By Ranjit Singh's order, Lahori adds, the late Faqir Nur al-Din was appointed to subdue Chatti, and the Faqir family then bought a second collection for themselves (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 258, folio 246).

Lahore came to him, in Chishti's account, by the leave of Shah Zaman: Shah Zaman attacked in 1797 CE, and in 1798 he gave Ranjit Singh leave to take Lahore, and he took it; Kharak Singh's birth is recorded in the same passage, with fights at Gujrat and elsewhere (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 579). In another passage the book has Lahore's elders invite him when Lahna Singh, the *hakim*, began destroying the monuments of former kings, asking him to come to Lahore and take possession (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 786); the invitation was on condition that he restore the Shalamar garden, and the book calls this the reason for Maharaja Ranjit Singh's coming (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 786). Khalid's history runs: "Starting from a small fief around Gujranwala ... the Maharaja marched triumphantly into Lahore in 1799", and "In 1801, Ranjit Singh was crowned as the Ruler of Punjab by Sahib Singh Bedi, a descendant of Guru Nanak" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 207, folio not stated). In 1799, Khalid writes, he "was greeted enthusiastically by a population sick of the lawlessness of that time" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 207, folio not stated).

Chishti then follows the reign year by year as printed. In 1805 (the year is uncertain in the printed text) come Jaswant Rao Holkar and Amir Khan, and Holkar treats with the English; a treaty is made through Sir Charles Metcalfe Sahib Bahadur, the name uncertain in the print, in 1809, a year also uncertain; Kangra is taken after the siege by Amar Singh Gorkha (again an uncertain reading); and then Shah Shuja al-Mulk is pressed until the Maharaja, by straitening him, obtains the Koh-i-Nur jewel, in words the printed text leaves uncertain, a jewel the book says is now with the Queen of England in London; Multan follows in 1818, then the fort of Khyber, and in 1819, a year uncertain in the print, Kashmir was conquered; Jammu is granted to Gulab Singh and Suchet Singh, and Raja Sansar Chand is asked to marry his daughters to Raja Diwan Singh and Gulab Singh, both names uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 580). At the conquest of Multan, Chishti says elsewhere, Nawab Muzaffar Khan was martyred (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 130).

Kanhaiya Lal measures the reign in years: this Maharaja ruled in the Punjab for forty years, and besides the Punjab the country of Kashmir, the Derajat and other lands were in his possession (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 217, folio 220); for the reign itself he refers the reader to his own *Tarikh-e Punjab* (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 217, folio 220). His infantry, numbering, as printed, not less than seventy thousand, drew its pay through Bakhshi Bhagat Ram (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 96, folio 99). Khalid has his empire extend "from FATA (Federally Administered Tribal Areas) to Kashmir to Multan in the South and the River Sutlej", and records that he made a deal with the British (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 207, folio not stated). He adds: "In the land of Punjab which was for over a millenium [as printed] ruled by Afghans, Mughals and other non-Punjabis, Ranjit Singh was the first Punjabi to rule Punjab", and credits him with stability "in Punjab which lasted a good forty years" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 208, folio not stated). "There is a view that Ranjit was a true follower of Guru Gobind Singh", Khalid writes (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 208, folio not stated); and "In 1802, he employed a Nihang by the name of Akali Phula Singh to capture the city of Amritsar", Phula Singh bringing "two to three thousand Nihangs into the state army" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 210, folio not stated). "During his reign, Maharaja Ranjit Singh captured the Pathan regions of Khyber-Pakhthunkwa and the tribal areas, as a result of which several people converted to Sikhism" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 161–162, folio not stated).

## The Court

Chishti names the court's great men: Jamadar Khushal Singh, Faqir Aziz al-Din and Diwan Dina Nath, the last two names uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 581). Dhian Singh first served the Maharaja on an ordinary salary, and Khushal Singh rose at a time when the Maharaja was taken up with pleasure in the company of Moran the courtesan at the Saman Burj of Lahore Fort, a line in which one word is uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 582). Khushal Singh received havelis and gardens in Lahore and Amritsar, and Dina Nath built a large Shiva temple near a place in Lahore whose name is illegible in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 583). The Faqir family were known in Lahore as the family of the Faqir sahibs and the hakims (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 584), and the book tells their anecdote of the blind eye and the robe of honour, adding that the Maharaja, whose name is garbled in the printed line, placed complete trust in their word (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 585). Khalid names "Fakir Azizudin" as holding the offices of "Prime Minister and the Foreign Minister", and his Muslim generals "General Mohammad Sultan, General Elahi Baksh and General Ghaus Mohammad Khan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 207, folio not stated); the Fakir's "family still lives in Lahore in their ancestral house, located inside the walled city" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 207, folio not stated). Kanhaiya Lal records Khalifa Ghulam al-Madad[OCR?] seated beside him on an equal chair, and Qazi Nizam-ud-Din as the city's qazi at the conquest of Lahore (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 50, folio 53).

Hira Singh, in Chishti's account, was the Maharaja's favourite: the elder Maharaja, as the printed text gives the title, looked on him as an Ayaz of his own and used to call him son, in a line with an illegible word; and five rupees were put under Hira Singh's head every night while the Maharaja lived (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 868). The Hazuri family, Chishti adds, holds a sealed order of his, a *parwana* whose seal is engraved in Persian script with the words Akal Sahai Ranjit Singh (the first two uncertain in the printed text), dated the third of Safar al-Muzaffar, the year illegible (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 600).

Two of his attachments are told at length. The courtesan Moran, Kanhaiya Lal writes, held such favour that he never disobeyed her word, and coin was struck in her name (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 47, folio 50): it was current throughout the Punjab, and Moranshahi rupees and paisa, the name uncertain in the print, are still kept as keepsakes (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 179, folio 182). Chishti's version calls Mehr Muhkam a servant of Maharaja Ranjit Singh, the title garbled in the printed text, and says that the Maharaja fell in love with a courtesan, in a line whose epithets and whose spelling of her name are uncertain in the print; the Moranshahi rupee was current, and he built the village of Moranwala and the Moranwali mosque, which still stands (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 646; Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 647). Moran's own court of justice and darbar were held separately, and her word brought about Mehr Muhkam's ruin and the destruction of Nawan Kot (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 647). Chishti closes with a moral: Moran's grave is unknown, Ranjit Singh's name and trace were wiped out, as the uncertain print seems to say, and his sons were deposed from rule (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 648).

The second is Gul Begum. Kanhaiya Lal records his marriage to her, the *phere* taken and the procession going from Lahore to Amritsar (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 47–48, folios 50–51). Khalid writes: "Like the third Mughal Emperor Akbar, Maharaja Ranjit Singh was a secular ruler", who "constructed a mosque at a mohalla called Gul Begum in Lahore in memory of his Muslim wife"; and "Another Muslim wife of his, Jind Kaur, ordered the construction of a library" at Data Darbar (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 207, folio not stated).

## Lahore in His Reign

Kanhaiya Lal, writing of the city rather than the samadhi, makes Ranjit Singh its restorer: when his time came, it was as though the city's fortune woke and it began to be peopled again (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 32, folio 35), and in the forty years of his rule Lahore was well peopled once more (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 33, folio 36). Though none of the ruined outer quarters was resettled, the city within the walls was well peopled (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 22, folio 17), and his courtiers made large gardens on the outer ruins (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 33, folio 36). He kept the city wall in constant, daily repair (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 21, folio 16), dug a deep pukka ditch on all four sides and put at each gate a bridge and a second solid gate, so that no enemy could ever take the city (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 21, folio 16); the book lists the works again: he had double gates made, the ditch dug and an outer rampart built (the word is uncertain in the printed text), repaired the city wall and ordered the deserted quarters resettled (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 32, folio 35). Inside the Khizri Gate he kept two lions in cages, the gate guards answering for them; the cages went under the British, but the name Shiran Wala Darwaza stuck (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 20, folio 15). Chishti has Nura, who fed his wheat on milk, win access to the court and an appointment to the digging of Lahore's ditch (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 423).

He renamed the fort's Moti Masjid the Moti Mandir and kept the treasury of the state there; in Kanhaiya Lal's day the English government still kept the treasury of the Punjab province in it (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 339, folio 346). Under him, Chishti writes, it was proclaimed in the city that whoever brought out a *taʿzia* would lose his head, in a line partly uncertain and partly illegible in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 274).

## The Maharaja as Patron

Chishti, in his survey of Lahore's shrines, records many grants and gifts made by the Maharaja at other sites. Of the majzub Subah Shah it was said that the outward king was the Maharaja (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 85); all the pillars of the state, with the Maharaja, held him in regard (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 86); and the Maharaja and his successors down to Dalip Singh came every Saturday to a *munhara* and gave *nazr*, the *sajjada nishin* receiving four or five thousand rupees each Saturday (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 95). At Data Darbar there was a Qur'an that Ranjit Singh, the name uncertain in the print, had sent there from Peshawar at the conquest of Peshawar, and that had been kept there ever since (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 169), and the shrine received a thousand rupees a year, the figure uncertain in the print, and some sweets (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 171). Mauj Darya's shrine had forty rupees a month, again an uncertain figure (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 105); a well at Ghore Shah's site had been granted by Maharaja Ranjit Singh (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 432); and the samadh of Sri Chand at Tahli Sahib held revenue-free, from Ranjit Singh's time, a rupee a day and a well with land, in a line with two uncertain words (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 679). At the Mani Singh site a *sadabart* of twenty-five rupees a day ran under the elder Maharaja (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 768), and Bhai Sahib Singh was visited time and again by the Maharaja (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 770). Of Syed Ali Shah, Chishti writes that the Maharaja remained his heartfelt devotee all his life (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 707). Among his patronage Chishti records, too, a dera of *granthis*, set up in Samvat 1879 at a place printed as Bahaur, year and place both uncertain, with four hundred *granthis*, five standards, a *nagara* and five hundred men with *qawwals* (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 866).

Of the Shalamar garden, which he had been invited to restore, Chishti writes that he did so, and restored it so well that it shows to this day (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 787). The samadh at Shahid Ganj was raised in his reign: in Maharaja Ranjit Singh's time, Chishti writes, the whole building was completed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 767). Even in recording the endowment of the Wazir Khan mosque, Chishti praises him against his own grain: Maharaja Ranjit Singh, he writes, deserves praise for it in spite of his religious prejudice, in a sentence with uncertain words that breaks off in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 747–748). Kanhaiya Lal records the fund and *jagir* he gave to the Baoli Sahib (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 113, folio 116).

Khalid writes that "When Maharaja Ranjit Singh became the sovereign of Punjab at the end of the eighteenth century he spent large amounts of money on the renovation and expansion of many gurdwaras", attaching "vast tracts of land" so that revenues could fund them (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 34, folio 16); "During the tenure of Maharaja Ranjit Singh" Baba Sahib Singh Bedi was allotted "vast tracts of land around the cities of Gujranwala and Lahore", on which he founded Bedian (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 44, folio 26). The Panja Sahib gurdwara was "extended" in his tenure (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 159, folio not stated), its frescoes "part of the original complex built by Maharaja Ranjit Singh" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 167, folio not stated), and he "first erected" the present Dera Sahib structure (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 200, folio not stated). In *In Search of Shiva*, Iqbal Qaiser says of the gurdwara at Mandi Mangat that "The current structure was raised by Maharaja Ranjit Singh" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated), and Khalid writes that "during the tenure of Maharaja Ranjit Singh" the village of Maraka "was re-populated a few kilometres away from the ruins of the old one" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).

## The Maharaja as Despoiler

The same books record what he took. Chishti says that in Ranjit Singh's time red, black and marble stones were torn down all around Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 296), and that when the cantonments of his armies were built the bricks of old tombs and houses were pulled out, so that thousands of tombs, domes, mosques, gardens and old havelis that stood as memorials of the nobles of the past were lost, in a sentence with two uncertain words (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 431). At Jahangir's tomb, out of religious prejudice, the marble, amber and red-stone railings, worth lakhs of rupees, were taken to the Darbar Sahib at Amritsar and set in the Ram Bagh, though outwardly he was, in the uncertain words of the print, devoted in the highest degree to the kings of the past (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 855); Maharaja Ranjit Singh had the minaret lattices pulled out (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 859–860); and in his time all the *jagirs* of the *mujawirs* were confiscated, leaving only the garden (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 864). The marble slabs and lattices of Mian Mir's tomb went to a baradari at Amritsar (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 313); Zeb-un-Nisa's marble tomb was taken down for a baradari (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 637); and the marble of Shalamar's Sawan-Bhadon baradaris was set, by Maharaja Ranjit Singh's hard-heartedness, at the Ram Bagh in Amritsar (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 783). Shaikh Ashraf's tomb and mosque, Chishti writes, were demolished on the Maharaja's orders (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 159).

Lahori's tazkira gives a single example. Of the tomb of Pir Hadi Rahnuma, on the Anarkali–Mian Mir road, it says that the building was once of stone, and that in the time of the Sikhs Ranjit Singh had its stone pulled out (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 267, folio 255). Kanhaiya Lal adds the graves of Zakariya Khan, whose marble, worth lakhs of rupees, Maharaja Ranjit Singh had all taken off (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 91, folio 94); mosques used as powder magazines (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 80, 83, 84; folios 83, 86, 87); the demolition of Shah Sharaf's mosque and tomb for the Bhati Gate glacis, after which the coffin was reburied and a small pukka platform was built over it by the Maharaja's order (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 80–81, folios 83–84); and the expropriation of Mohalla Saudagran for the Guru Ram Das gurdwara (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 101, folio 104).

Beyond Lahore, Richard Eaton records that he seized the extensive holdings of the shrine at Pakpattan in 1810 and allowed its diwan and his retainers "only Rs. 1,000 a year for their maintenance, derived from the town duties of Pakpattan, in addition to a fourth share of four small villages nearby" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 231 and n. 59). Khalid reports, in Iqbal Qaiser's words, that "During the tenure of Maharaja Ranjit Singh, the Sikhs took over the mosque [at Shaheed Ganj], placed the Guru Granth Sahib inside it, converting it into a gurdwara. The Muslims could not do anything then" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 77, folio 59). He also gives, as things said, that "It is said that the Badshahi Masjid was used as a stable for horses", and that the Maharaja "removed the top structure of Emperor Jahangir's tomb and placed it in the Hazuri Bagh" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 207, folio not stated).

## The Maharaja and the Faqirs

Lahori's tazkira of the Punjab's saints sees the Maharaja through the majzubs of Lahore. Maharaja Ranjit Singh, ruler of the Punjab, it says, had complete faith in Mastan Shah Lahori and would give him thousands of rupees, which the majzub would not take (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 246, folio 234). Once he seated Mastan Shah on his own elephant to bring him to the fort, and the majzub jumped off it (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 246, folio 234).

Of the majzuba Mai Bhagi Lahori the tazkira says that Maharaja Ranjit Singh was her devoted follower, and that though she abused him he would not leave her; the appointment of a Hindu pandit to a *diwani* post at his darbar is credited to her word (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 255, folio 243). Chishti's account of her adds that he first offered her five hundred rupees as *nazr*, that she struck him with bricks more than once, and that in a year he names by a word uncertain in the printed text, perhaps Karkas, that is Samvat 1890 (the figure also uncertain), she refused Dhian Singh and Suchet Singh but came with Kharak Singh and stayed eleven days; a building at Kot Saeed was put up for ten thousand rupees, and her fame spread because the ruler was her devotee (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 790).

## His Death

The site's account gives his death as 27 June 1839, after which construction of the samadhi began the following month. Khalid gives his years as 1780–1839 (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 42, folio not stated), and writes that "The British took over soon after his death" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 208–209, folio not stated).

Kanhaiya Lal dates the death in a sentence whose numeral and month are uncertain in the printed text: in the end, he writes, the Maharaja died in 1896, in a month whose printed name cannot be read with certainty, the calendar era not stated (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 217, folio 220). Elsewhere he gives the death year as 1896 Bikrami, with the Baoli Sahib buildings still going up and left unfinished (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 113, folio 116); he also says the Maharaja fell severely ill in 1896 Bikrami, the figure uncertain in the print (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 112, folio 115); and in one passage he notes only that until the Maharaja's death the city grew more populous day by day (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 32, folio 35).

Lahori's tazkira gives no year for the death, but it gives the deathbed. Ill and near his end, the Maharaja sent for the majzub Taj Shah Lahori and asked him for his health. The faqir answered that death is for everyone, as his father and the faqir's father had died, and that sandalwood should be brought to burn him:

You too, the faqir told him, are bound to die, just as your father and mine have died; one word of his reply is uncertain in the printed text (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 245, folio 233).

Hearing this he lost hope, and as Taj Shah left the fort, Maharaja Ranjit Singh gave up his life (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 245, folio 233). The sandalwood is the faqir's phrase in the story, not a report of the rite. The book dates only the faqir's own death, to the year one thousand two hundred and sixty-one of the Hijra, 1261 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 245).

## After Him

Lahori's tazkira credits the same faqir with foreseeing the end of the Sikh kingdom on the day the Maharaja died: on that very day, it says, he had foretold the ruin of the Sikh kingdom, saying that it would last nine more years, and then the Firangis, the Europeans, would be masters of the Punjab (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 244, folio 232). It is the author's report of a saying, not a dated event.

Kanhaiya Lal writes that after Maharaja Ranjit Singh's death his own son, Maharaja Kharak Singh, sat on the throne of the government of the Punjab (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 221, folio 224), and Chishti that Maharaja Kharak Singh, his worthy son, sat in his seat of government over the Punjab (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 586). Chishti dates Kharak Singh's death and Sher Singh's accession to the same year, Samvat 1897 Bikrami, a figure uncertain in the print (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 723), and heads his pages on the period "Events after the Maharaja's Death" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 589–591). He calls the Sandhanwalias close kin of Rani Chand Kaur and the Maharaja (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 588), and has Dhian Singh tell Sher Singh that, God willing, he would soon be ruler of the Punjab (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 588). Sher Singh's killers were destroyed after a siege of Lahore Fort and a great battle (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 589). Lahori's tazkira records the death of Hira Singh, minister to Duleep Singh: on the day he was killed, it says, Raja Hira Singh fled on the pretext of a hunt, and the Sikhs pursued and killed him and made Jawahar Singh minister (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 245, folio 233). Chishti says he saw with his own eyes the heads of Raja Hira Singh and Pandit Jalla hung from a gutter-spout (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 868).

Kanhaiya Lal allows that after his death the city did suffer slight blows at the hands of the Sikh army itself (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 32, folio 35). Chishti carries the story through the defeat at Sobraon, the name uncertain in the print, the 1846 treaty and the Resident at Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 591), to the rising of 1848 and the annexation on 29 March 1849, when, he writes, the whole country entered the English dominion; all three figures are uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 592). On a day of December 1849 that is illegible in the print, Dalip Singh left Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 592), and the page carries a *qitʿa* on his departure, two of whose lines run:

چون شمع[OCR?] پنجاب از پنجاب رفت
نو گلی از گلشن پنجاب رفت

Chishti prints them on the departure of Duleep Singh (Dalip Singh in his text) (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 592). Khalid calls Duleep Singh "the last Maharaja of Punjab, before the British took over", and writes that "Punjab became a British colony almost immediately after his death eventually leading to its division at the time of Partition" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 209, folio not stated). When Duleep Singh was exiled and English rule was complete, Kanhaiya Lal notes, the troops left the Badshahi Mosque (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 141, folio 144).

## The Building of the Samadhi

Construction began the month after the Maharaja's death under his son Kharak Singh and was largely completed by 1848 under his youngest son, Duleep Singh, with final repairs funded by the British after their 1849 annexation of Punjab.

Kanhaiya Lal confirms the site of the cremation: he was burned where his samadh now stands (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 217, folio 220). He places the building precisely: this grand samadh stands outside the Roshnai Gate, against the city wall, wall to wall with the Badshahi Mosque, to the north (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 217, folio 220). From the neighbouring entry he gives its wall as lying to the west of the samadh of Guru Arjan, at a distance the printed text has lost (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 205–206, folios 208–209). His contents list it as the samadh of Maharaja Ranjit Singh, immediately after Shahid Ganj (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 4, folio 3); a second entry, for the samadh of a Maharaja Singh, ruler of the Punjab, whose name is illegible in the print, stands earlier in the list, and the book does not say whether it is the same monument (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 3, folio 2).

Beyond the site's own account, the sources consulted do not record when the building was begun or finished, or who built it. Chishti's contents open with an entry for the samadh of Maharaja Ranjit Singh (one word uncertain in the print), followed by lines on the Maharaja's life, his deeds, his ministers and the period after his death (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 4); his section on the building carries the margin heading "Raja Ranjit Singh's Samadh" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 569).

## The Fabric, as Kanhaiya Lal Surveyed It

At the monument's heart, beneath a marble pavilion inlaid with pietra dura stonework, a lotus-shaped marble urn holds the Maharaja's ashes. Architecturally the building is a striking synthesis of Sikh, Hindu and Islamic idioms: gilded fluted domes and cupolas rise above red sandstone doorways carved with images of Ganesha, Devi and Brahma, while surviving interior frescoes, painted between 1839 and 1849, have been reassessed by modern scholars as a high point of Sikh decorative art.

Kanhaiya Lal enters from the east. The eastern wall is pakka, with red stone at the doorway, five steps up, the steps and frame also of red stone (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 217, folio 220). Above the door are three images, of Shiv ji, Ganesh ji and a third whose name is uncertain in the printed text, made in stone and installed there (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 217, folio 220). In the cells of the eastern wall's first storey, shopkeepers keep shops (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 217, folio 220).

The samadh chamber stands on a grand platform about five feet high, faced with marble to the east and north and with brick to the south and west, and hollow beneath, like a cellar. Each of its four sides has three doors with marble frames, the middle one large and the two flanking it small; only the eastern middle door is kept open, with a large black stone slab before it (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 218, folio 221). Within is a roofed hall; at its centre stands a grand marble *baradari* with a *ghulam-gardish* running round all four sides; and at the centre of the *baradari* a marble bungalow whose columns are marble but whose dome is of brick (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 219, folio 222). The ceiling of the great dome carries mirrors and elaborate gilding (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 219–220, folios 222–223).

The *ghulam-gardish* is divided into eight bays, four at the corners and four on the sides, with brick floors and walls but remarkable glass work on the ceilings, the four corner ceilings gilded and the four side ones white (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 220, folio 223). A red stone stair in the south-west corner, of nineteen steps as the uncertain number is printed, rises to the second storey, which carries two balconies to each side, each of three doors, with a latticed bay of three openings between them, red stone railings in the balconies' lattices and marble railings within the openings (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 220, folio 223). A brick stair from the same corner, of twenty-two steps, again an uncertain number, leads to a large roofed terrace with small domelets along the parapets, four corner domelets, and at the centre of each wall a domed, elongated sitting-place of eight doors (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 220, folio 223). Above the great dome stands another lofty *baradari*, all of pakka brick, with three doors to each side, crowned by a round dome bearing a gilded finial, with four gilded domelets at its corners (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 221, folio 224).

Under the high plinth, in a clause much damaged in the printed text, large rooms are built whose doors lie beneath the northern wall, and people are living in them; the northern door and stair are now closed, and entry is only by the eastern door (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 221, folio 224). In that whole account Kanhaiya Lal records nothing on the samadh's custodians, offerings or anniversary (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 222, folio 225).

## The Fabric, as Chishti Surveyed It

Chishti's survey, many of whose words are uncertain in the printed text, begins at a two-storeyed gateway of red stone above, with floral work; the spaces on both sides are used as shops (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 569). Inside the east gate is a south-facing room, 3 *gaz* by 8 *gaz* as uncertainly printed, a stair facing north, and an upper door in a red stone frame, the courtyard floored with lime (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 570). South of the gate lies a five-arched *dalan* with brick columns, as the uncertain print reads, and a ceiling of painted, coloured lime plaster, and two rooms, in one of which the income of the place is collected (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 570). Further on he notes a *dalan* printed as *satta dara*, rooms, and a courtyard 45 *gaz* long and 44 *gaz* wide, both figures uncertain in the print, with what seems to be a mosque to the west, where the line breaks off (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 571).

The samadh itself, in Chishti's account, stands on a platform whose height the print gives, uncertainly, as two or one and a half *gaz*, with a cold chamber beneath, faced with flowered marble on the north and east (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 572). Above a marble course stands a two-storeyed brick building in white plaster, with twelve arched doorways on all four sides and, on the east, a marble image, perhaps of Ganesh, the name uncertain in the print (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 572). Inside is a marble *baradari* with the samadh, and over it two domes, one below and one above, a marble floor, a marble image of Devi Mai, and a ceiling of mirror-work (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 572). The large dome is gilded and set with mirrors, and beneath it is the Maharaja's square memorial under a covering; a door on the west leads to the cool chamber below (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 573). Four corner towers carry *kalas* finials; there is a large dome with a gilded finial and four small domes at the corners (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 573–574), and the book notes a gilded finial whose colour is white (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 574).

Elsewhere Chishti compares the inner dome of the samadh of Jhangar Shah with the Maharaja's, in a sentence whose words and sense are uncertain in the printed text; it seems to say that work of this kind, apart from the samadh of Maharaja Ranjit Singh, is found nowhere else (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 659). For the history after Jawahar Singh's death he sends the reader to the *Tawarikh-e Khalsa* and to his own account of the elder Maharaja's samadh (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 870).

## The Fabric Today

Haroon Khalid, attending the death anniversary of 2011, describes the samadhi facing Gurdwara Dera Sahib as "a beautiful white building, adorned with detailed frescoes and other art work" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 201, folio not stated), and on that day "A police official sits on top of the samadhi with his gun in front of him" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 201, folio not stated). He writes: "Just behind the samadhi of Guru Arjan, a splendid building, white in colour, stands four floors from the ground with a small dome on the top", with the minaret of the Badshahi Masjid behind (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 205, folio not stated). The samadhi "stands on top of another single storey building, painted yellow", in a courtyard "with rooms for the pilgrims on its sides" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 206, folio not stated). He notes: "Two loud speakers have been placed on top of the samadhi, facing the direction of the ground, erected opposite the mosque and the city, perhaps as a respect to the Muslim religion, a respect that is only expected of the religious minorities" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 206, folio not stated).

In the hall Khalid saw "a palanquin in the centre, constructed on a raised platform with its pillars made of white marble" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 206, folio not stated); it "marks the spot where the last remains of the Maharaja were buried. His cremation took place in the ground accompanying the building" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 209, folio not stated). "On a regular day, a portrait of the Maharaja is placed at the palanquin, dressed in a white robe, sporting a long white beard", next to that of "his youngest son, Dalip Singh, who also happened to be the last Maharaja of Punjab"; "Today, the portraits have been removed" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 209, folio not stated). The dome is "decorated with coloured glass", green, orange and red, in geometric patterns; niches hold frescoes; and the balcony walls show court scenes and "religious drawings from Hindu mythology", with "depictions of Krishna, Ganesh, Ram and Sita" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 209, folio not stated). The paintings "have faded", some have "disappeared all together", and visitors "have noted down their names and numbers thereby disfiguring it" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 209, folio not stated).

Modern scholarship has focused particular attention on the samadhi's ornamental programme: research by the art historian Dr Nadhra Shahbaz Naeem Khan uncovered guardian figures beneath whitewash on the building's eastern facade, though subsequent recoatings have since covered and lost this decoration again.

## Those Who Burned With Him

Around the Maharaja's urn, in the site's account, smaller urns commemorate the four queens and seven attendants who, according to the custom of sati, died on his funeral pyre.

Kanhaiya Lal counts the dead differently: on the marble platform within the marble bungalow stand the Maharaja's stone samadh at the centre, eleven samadhs of the eleven *ranis* who became *sati* with him, and two samadhs of two pigeons (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 219, folio 222). Khalid describes an adjacent hall with a raised platform bearing "thirteen small semi-spheres. These are the samadhis of the eleven women and two pigeons who also burned in the pyre of Maharaja Ranjit Singh", among them "his wives and concubines"; he calls the custom "sati", from "the ancient Rajput tradition" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 210, folio not stated).

## Images, Custodians and Repairs

In one of the chambers of the gate range, Kanhaiya Lal writes, a marble image of the eight-armed Devi (the word is uncertain in the print) stands with great reverence, throne and canopy all of marble, the Devi mounted on a marble lion (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 218, folio 221). He gives its history: the image had been kept by Rani Jindan, mother of Maharaja Duleep Singh, at a place in the fort whose printed name, perhaps the Saman Burj, cannot be read with certainty, and when she was exiled she sent it to this samadh; and a separate *pujari* is appointed for it (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 218, folio 221).

Chishti, too, finds images. In the southern niche is a marble image of Hanuman, and in the northern niche the image of a deity whose name is uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 570). In the dalan stands a movable marble *baradari*, one that can be carried wherever one wishes, measuring two and a quarter *gaz* by one and a half *gaz* on ten marble legs, all three figures uncertain in the print; it is connected, in a damaged line, with the departure from Lahore of a Rani whose printed name, perhaps Chand Kaur, cannot be read with certainty; inside it are an image of a *devi*, a bull and a marble lion, and the book goes on to an account of the *devi*'s bathing (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 570). Its custodian, Chishti writes, was Lala Narsingh Das, son of Bhagwan (both names uncertain in the print), the keeper of the whole place and then manager of the samadh, and now a servant attends there on his behalf (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 570). Today the Evacuee Trust Property Board administers the site.

Kanhaiya Lal was himself responsible for the building's survival. The *baradari*, he writes, first had eight marble columns, which cracked after a few years under the weight of the dome, and the matchless building came near to collapse (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 219, folio 222). The authorities, seeing its state, ordered the author, then Executive Engineer of the Buildings Division at Lahore, to secure it; he added eight brick columns to the eight, making sixteen, and bound the cracked ones with very strong iron rings (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 219, folio 222). He gives no year for the work and names no authority beyond those words.

## The Adjoining Samadhis

Adjoining smaller samadhis honour his son Kharak Singh and grandson Nau Nihal Singh. Kanhaiya Lal places their joint building to the west of Ranjit Singh's samadh, on the western edge of the same platform that lies beneath it (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 222, folio 225). Its foundation was laid after Ranjit Singh's death, in Kharak Singh's time; some work was done under Sher Singh; it stopped because of mutual killing and disorder; and the English brought it to completion, two words of the passage being uncertain in the print (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 222–223, folios 225–226).

Both books also describe, as separate monuments, the samadhs of the Maharaja's wives. Chishti heads one with the name of Mai Nakain, wife of Maharaja Ranjit Singh and mother of Maharaja Kharak Singh, the name and one other word uncertain in the print (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 664), and names another as a Kanwar, wife of Ranjit Singh, the rest of whose name is illegible (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 665). Kanhaiya Lal's entry on the samadh of Rani Nakain and Rani Chand Kaur records that after the deaths of Kharak Singh and Nau Nihal Singh, Rani Chand Kaur became mistress of the kingdom and was set on the throne by the Sandhanwalias, and that Raja Dhian Singh withdrew in anger to Jammu (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 443, folio 450).

## Devotion and Practice

Though formally a samadhi rather than a constituted gurdwara, the site has long functioned as a living place of Sikh devotion: the Guru Granth Sahib was installed and read there for decades after construction, and today it remains an active pilgrimage destination, especially around Ranjit Singh's death anniversary and the spring Vaisakhi season, when Sikh visitors, including delegations from India, gather to pay homage.

Khalid's chapter records one such day. In the hall were "about a hundred and fifty people, all men": "This is the conclusion of the Akhand Path held in the honour of Maharaja Ranjit Singh, also known as Sher-e-Punjab, or the Lion of Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 206, folio not stated). He dates it: "The occasion is the death anniversary of the ruler and the date 29th June 2011, twelve days after the death anniversary commemoration of Guru Arjan in the same complex"; the earlier pilgrims had left "and a set of new ones have arrived from India" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 206, folio not stated). After the ceremony came *langar* in two rows, of dal, rice, rotis and water, and tea "Boiled with sugar and ginger", "highly popular in East Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 210–211, folio not stated).

He spoke with the pilgrims. "Amanpreet Singh, 35, is a trader from Ludhiana", on his first visit to Pakistan: "The Maharaja was a great leader ... There was no crime" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 208, folio not stated). "54 year old Jaswant Singh" and a friend had come for the first time "from the city of Qadian in East Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 211, folio not stated).

## Legends and Miracles

Kanhaiya Lal explains the two smallest samadhs on the platform: the pigeons, at the burning of the body they came flying and fell into the pyre, and were burned with the Maharaja's corpse (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 219, folio 222). He reports it flatly, the two samadhs standing as its evidence; Khalid, too, counts the two pigeons among those who burned in the pyre (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 210, folio not stated).

Chishti tells a story of the Maharaja at the tomb of Mian Mir. He came to strip the tomb, his horse Laili bolted, and he fell and hurt his jaw (the word is uncertain in the print); now, he said, he had been punished for his disrespect, and he gave five hundred rupees as an offering, ordered the tomb whitewashed, and remained a devotee of the saint and respectful of the *mujawirs* (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 296–297). He also records the prayers of Subah Shah for the Maharaja (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 86).

The deathbed of Lahori's tazkira belongs here as well as to the history: the faqir who tells the ailing king to send for sandalwood, and the king who dies as the faqir walks out of the fort (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 245, folio 233), and the same faqir's prophecy of nine years (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 244, folio 232).

## Setting: Hazuri Bagh and the Roshnai Gate

The material in this section is about the place around the samadhi, not about the samadhi.

Khalid sets the scene: "The Hazuri Bagh is situated in the middle of these monuments, with a single storey pavilion placed symmetrically at the center of the garden while four lanes lead towards it" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 205, folio not stated). "The Roshani gate, one of the thirteen gateways that lead into the ancient walled city of Lahore, is between the Alamgiri Gate and the Royal Mosque"; lamps "were lit all along the passageway ... hence the name Roshani gate, or the gate of lights" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 205, folio not stated). The gate "was once reserved for the prince and the princess, who would go from the Fort to the city through this entrance" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 205, folio not stated). Behind it lies the Heera Mandi bazaar, "still one of the largest markets for traditional instruments in the city" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 205, folio not stated).

Kanhaiya Lal names Hazuri Bagh as a small open ground now called by that name, without mentioning the samadhi (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 138, folio 141). In his long description of the garden and its *baradari* he says that Ranjit Singh laid out the garden and sat at darbar on the *baradari*'s *shah-nashin*, again without mentioning the samadh (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 339–342, folios 346–349). Of the *baradari* in the garden, Chishti writes that Zeb-un-Nisa's marble tomb was taken down to build it, and that the baradari still stands in Hazuri Bagh as a memorial of the Maharaja, in a line partly illegible in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 637), while Khalid reports it as said that "The pavilion, which now stands in the middle of the ground, was part of the structure of Emperor Jahangir's tomb" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 207, folio not stated).

"For the city that the Maharaja once ruled", Khalid writes of the Lahore of 2011, "he is an alien, his memory kept alive not by people of the land he once ruled but by people from the other side of the border" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 206, folio not stated); it is now rare, he adds, "that one runs into a Sikh in this city, a city which was once ruled by a Sikh and the seat of their political strength" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 196, folio not stated).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The date of death.** The site's account gives 27 June 1839, and Khalid gives his years as 1780–1839 (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 42, folio not stated). Kanhaiya Lal gives the year as 1896, uncertainly printed, with no era and an uncertain month (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 217, folio 220), and elsewhere as 1896 Bikrami (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 113, folio 116); he also gives 1896 Bikrami as the year of a severe illness (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 112, folio 115). Lahori's tazkira gives no year.
- **The day of the anniversary.** The site's account gives the death as 27 June and has pilgrims gather around the death anniversary. Khalid attended the death anniversary on "29th June 2011" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 206, folio not stated), and calls it twelve days after the Guru Arjan commemoration, which he dates to "6th June 2011" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 196, folio not stated).
- **The taking of Lahore.** The site's account has him found and rule the empire from 1801. Chishti has Shah Zaman give him leave to take Lahore in 1798, and he took it (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 579). Khalid has him march into Lahore in 1799 and be crowned in 1801 (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 207, folio not stated).
- **Who died on the pyre.** The site's account has four queens and seven attendants. Kanhaiya Lal has eleven *ranis* (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 219, folio 222). Khalid has "eleven women", "his wives and concubines" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 210, folio not stated). Kanhaiya Lal and Khalid add two pigeons.
- **What marks his remains.** The site's account has a lotus-shaped marble urn holding the ashes. Kanhaiya Lal has a stone samadh at the centre (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 219, folio 222). Chishti has a square memorial under a covering beneath the gilded dome (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 573). Khalid has a palanquin on a raised platform that marks the spot where the last remains "were buried" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 209, folio not stated).
- **Where he was cremated.** The site's account and Kanhaiya Lal put the samadhi on the spot of the cremation (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 217, folio 220). Khalid writes that "His cremation took place in the ground accompanying the building" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 209, folio not stated).
- **The images at the door.** The site's account has red sandstone doorways carved with Ganesha, Devi and Brahma. Kanhaiya Lal has three stone images over the door, of Shiv ji, Ganesh ji and a third whose name is uncertain in the printed text (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 217, folio 220). Chishti has a marble image on the east, perhaps of Ganesh, the name uncertain in the print, Hanuman in the southern niche and a marble Devi within (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 570; Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 572). Khalid sees Krishna, Ganesh, Ram and Sita among the paintings (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 209, folio not stated).
- **The pavilion and its dome.** The site's account has a marble pavilion inlaid with pietra dura. Kanhaiya Lal's marble bungalow has marble columns but a brick dome (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 219, folio 222). Khalid's palanquin has pillars of white marble (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 206, folio not stated).
- **The great dome.** The site's account has gilded fluted domes. Kanhaiya Lal has the great dome's ceiling worked with mirrors and gilding (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 219–220, folios 222–223); Chishti a gilded dome set with mirrors (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 573); Khalid a dome "decorated with coloured glass" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 209, folio not stated).
- **How many storeys.** Chishti calls the brick building two-storeyed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 572). Kanhaiya Lal describes a second storey, a roofed terrace and a further *baradari* above the great dome (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 220, folio 223; Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 221, folio 224). Khalid's building "stands four floors from the ground" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 205, folio not stated).
- **Where it stands beside Dera Sahib.** Khalid has the samadhi facing Dera Sahib across a lawn (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 201, folio not stated), and four pages later "Just behind the samadhi of Guru Arjan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 205, folio not stated). Kanhaiya Lal puts its wall to the west of Guru Arjan's samadh (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 205–206, folios 208–209).
- **The Hazuri Bagh pavilion.** Chishti says it was built from the marble of Zeb-un-Nisa's tomb (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 637). Khalid reports it as said that it was part of the structure of Jahangir's tomb (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 207, folio not stated).
- **Spellings.** Chishti prints the name Ranjit Singh in the usual way and, in several places, in garbled forms uncertain in the printed text, one with the name itself garbled (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 585) and one with the title garbled (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 646). Lahori's tazkira prints his mother-in-law's name both as Sada Kaur and as Sada Kunwar (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 258).
- **His temper toward other faiths.** Khalid calls him, "Like the third Mughal Emperor Akbar", a secular ruler (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 207, folio not stated). Chishti attributes his stripping of Mughal tombs to religious zeal (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 855), and praises the Wazir Khan endowment in spite of his religious prejudice (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 747–748). Kanhaiya Lal both blames the Sikhs for the city's ruin, saying that they plundered and laid it waste, in a line with an illegible word (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 22, folio 17), and makes Ranjit Singh the restorer whose accession woke the city's fortune (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 32, folio 35).

## Legacy

The samadhi is one of the most visited symbols of Sikh heritage remaining in Pakistan, and the monument is listed by the Punjab Directorate General of Archaeology among the province's protected religious monuments of the Sikh period.

The books consulted leave a double portrait of the man it commemorates. To Kanhaiya Lal he was the ruler under whom Lahore revived (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 32, folio 35), though the same book holds the Sikhs responsible for the city's earlier ruin (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 22, folio 17). To Chishti he was at once the patron of Muslim shrines and the man who carried the marble of Mughal tombs to Amritsar. Lahori's tazkira remembers him through the faqirs he could not buy: the majzub who refused his thousands, the majzuba who abused him and whom he would not leave, and the faqir who told him to send for sandalwood. Khalid found his memory kept on his anniversary by pilgrims from across the border, at a samadhi where the Akhand Path is read in his honour and the *langar* served afterwards (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 206, folio not stated; Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 210–211, folio not stated).
