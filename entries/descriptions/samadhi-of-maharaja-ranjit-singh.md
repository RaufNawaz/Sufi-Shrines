## Overview

Immediately beside Lahore Fort and the Badshahi Mosque, on the spot where Maharaja Ranjit Singh's body was cremated, stands the samadhi built in his memory — one of the grandest funerary monuments of the Sikh Empire. Ranjit Singh, the "Lion of Punjab," founded and ruled the Sikh Empire from 1801 until his death on 27 June 1839. Though formally a samadhi rather than a constituted gurdwara, the site has long functioned as a living place of Sikh devotion, and the Evacuee Trust Property Board administers it as one of the most visited symbols of Sikh heritage remaining in Pakistan.

Three of the books consulted describe the monument itself. Kanhaiya Lal's history of Lahore, whose author was also the engineer charged with keeping the building standing, surveys it from the gateway to the gilded finial (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 217, folio 220; Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 222–223, folios 225–226). Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti's *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, a survey of the tombs and buildings around Lahore, gives the building a section of its own, headed "حال مکان سماد مہاراجہ رنجیت سنگھ سرگ باسی" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 569), and a long account of the Maharaja's family, reign and court (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 576; Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 592). Haroon Khalid's *A White Trail* gives a chapter to the commemoration of the Maharaja's death anniversary at the samadhi in 2011 (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 6, folio not stated).

The other books speak of the man, not of the monument. Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori's *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, a tazkira of the saints of the Punjab, says nothing at all about the samadhi, the funeral or the year of his death, but it gives a scene at his deathbed, from the side of a Muslim faqir, and portrays him as a devotee of Lahore's majzubs (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 244, folio 232; Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 246, folio 234). Richard Eaton, and Haroon Khalid in *Walking with Nanak* and *In Search of Shiva*, mention him only as a ruler who endowed, built or confiscated elsewhere. All of this is given below as being about Ranjit Singh, not about his samadhi.

## The Maharaja's Forebears

Nur Ahmad Chishti introduces the Sikh *misls* in order to explain the Maharaja's family. The eighth misl was "سوکر حکیا[OCR?]", and its sardar "چرت سنگھ" he calls "مورث اعلی مہاراجہ رنجیت سنگھ" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 576). His genealogy, as printed, begins "سن چودہ سو ستر[OCR?] عیسوی" with one "مسمی کالو" at "موضع بنڈری[OCR?] بوٹھی[OCR?]"; a son went to "موضع سانسرہی[OCR?]" near Amritsar, among "قوم سانہنسی[OCR?]", "جن کا پیشہ قزاقی تھا"; and the family passed through "موضع سندہ[OCR?]" and "موضع کہالی[OCR?]" to "موضع سکر حک[OCR?]" in "سن پندرہ سو پچین[OCR?]" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 577). It kept shops in salt and tobacco; one "بہارا[OCR?]" became the *chela* of a Sikh, stopped shaving, and died "سن سولہ سو اناسی[OCR?]" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 578).

Budh Singh, in Chishti's account, took the *pahul* at Amritsar, was "بڑا الٹیرا[OCR?]", rode a piebald mare, and died "سن سترہ سو سولہ". The book then divides the line: "نودہ سنگھ سے تو رنجیت سنگھ کا خاندان شروع ہوا اور چند ریہان[OCR?] سنگھ سے سندھانوالیان کا خاندان نکلا" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 578). Naudh Singh was shot in "سترہ سو بیالیس عیسوی", and Charat Singh built a fort ("گھڑی[OCR?]") in "موضع گجرانوالہ" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 578). Charat Singh became sardar, fought toward Jammu, and died "بعمر ہفتالیس[OCR?] سالہ", his age uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 579). Of the Mughal side of these years Chishti records that "خواجہ عابد صوبہ دار لاہور" attacked Charat Singh's fort and "سکھوں کو فتح نصیب ہوئی" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 578).

Mahan Singh succeeded as a boy of ten, "بعمر دہ سالہ". In "سترہ سو چہتر[OCR?] عیسوی" he married the daughter of "مہاں سنگھ والی جنید[OCR?]", and the printed line goes on "اور … مہاراجہ رنجیت سنگھ اوسکا [illegible] تولد ہوا"; Mahan Singh died when one whose name is illegible in the printed text was twelve, and the mother sat on the *gaddi* (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 579). Lahori's tazkira names "رنجیت سنگھ کے باپ مہان سنگھ" as the first to seize the relic collection of Rasulnagar (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 257, folio 245).

## The Life of the Maharaja

Chishti dates the birth plainly: "سن سترہ سو اسی میں رنجیت سنگھ پیدا ہوا". The boy had smallpox, and "اس عارضہ سے ایک آنکھ اوسکی جاتی رہی"; a garbled line that follows, "… اوسمو[OCR?] اسی والدہ کو باعث بد کاری مار ڈالا", leaves it uncertain who killed whom (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 579). Haroon Khalid gives his years as "Maharaja Ranjit Singh (1780-1839), the erstwhile ruler of Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 42, folio not stated).

Lahori's tazkira records one dated episode of his youth. After Mahan Singh's death, in 1211 AH, when Shah Zaman Durrani came from Kabul, Ranjit Singh in fear sent his best goods and the relics of Rasulnagar from Gujranwala to his mother-in-law Sada Kaur at the fort of Makerian: "مہان سنگھ کے مرنے کے بعد ۱۲۱۱ ہجری میں جب بادشاہ زمان درانی کابل سے آیا تو رنجیت سنگھ نے خوف کے مارے اچھا اچھا مال و اسباب اور یہ زیارتیں گوجرانوالہ سے اپنی ساس سدا کور کے پاس بقلعہ مکیریاں بھیج دیں" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 258, folio 246). He later pressed for them more than once and was refused, "رنجیت سنگھ باوجودیکہ چند بار ان کے لینے کے واسطے بضد ہوا۔ مگر اس نے نہ دیں"; under stronger pressure she moved them to the fort of Chunda (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 258, folio 246). At last, when he had seized all her property, she gave them to his son Sher Singh, her grandson, who kept them by him until the day he was murdered: "آخر جب کل مال سدا کنور کا راجہ رنجیت سنگھ نے چھین لیا تو اس نے یہ زیارات شیر سنگھ اپنے دوہتے رنجیت سنگھ کے بیٹے کو دے دیں اور وہ اپنے قتل کے دن اپنے پاس رکھتا تھا" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 258, folio 246). The same page carries the collection on through Hira Singh and Jawahar Singh to Lahore Fort under the English (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 258). By Ranjit Singh's order, Lahori adds, the late Faqir Nur al-Din was appointed to subdue Chatti, "بحکم رنجیت سنگھ فقیر نور الدین مرحوم چٹی کی تسخیر کے واسطے مامور ہوئے", and the Faqir family then bought a second collection for themselves (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 258, folio 246).

Lahore came to him, in Chishti's account, by the leave of Shah Zaman: "سن سترہ سو ستانویں عیسوی" Shah Zaman attacked, and in "سترہ سو اٹھانویں" he gave Ranjit Singh leave to take Lahore, and he took it; Kharak Singh's birth is recorded in the same passage, with fights at Gujrat and elsewhere (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 579). In another passage the book has Lahore's elders invite him when "لہنا سنگہ حاکم" began destroying the monuments of former kings, asking him to "لاہور مین آکر قابض ہو" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 786); the invitation was on condition that he restore the Shalamar garden, and the book calls this the "باعث تشریف آوری مہاراجہ رنجیت سنگہ" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 786). Khalid's history runs: "Starting from a small fief around Gujranwala ... the Maharaja marched triumphantly into Lahore in 1799", and "In 1801, Ranjit Singh was crowned as the Ruler of Punjab by Sahib Singh Bedi, a descendant of Guru Nanak" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 207, folio not stated). In 1799, Khalid writes, he "was greeted enthusiastically by a population sick of the lawlessness of that time" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 207, folio not stated).

Chishti then follows the reign year by year as printed. In "اٹھارہ سو پانچ[OCR?]" come "جسونت راؤ ہولکر" and "امیر خان", and Holkar treats with the English; a treaty is made through "سر چارلس مٹکلف[OCR?] صاحب بہادر" in "سن اٹھارہ سو نہ[OCR?]"; Kangra is taken after the siege by "امر سنگھ گورکہ[OCR?]"; and then "شاہ شجاع الملک" is pressed until the Maharaja "اوسکو تنگ کرکے جواہر کوہ نور[OCR?] لیوایا[OCR?]", a jewel the book says is now with "ملکہ معظمہ انگلستان" in London; Multan follows in "سن اٹھارہ سوا اٹھارہ", then "قلعہ خیبر", and "سن اٹھارہ سوا نویں[OCR?] میں ملک کشمیر فتح ہوا"; Jammu is granted to Gulab Singh and Suchet Singh, and Raja Sansar Chand is asked to marry his daughters to "راجہ دیوان سنگھ[OCR?] و گلاب سنگھ[OCR?]" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 580). At the conquest of Multan, Chishti says elsewhere, Nawab Muzaffar Khan "شہید ہوی" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 130).

Kanhaiya Lal measures the reign in years: "اس مہاراجہ نے چالیس سال تک پنجاب میں حکومت کی", and besides the Punjab "ملک کشمیر و دیرہ جات وغیرہ" were in his possession (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 217, folio 220); for the reign itself he refers the reader to his own *Tarikh-e Punjab* (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 217, folio 220). His infantry, "جسکی تعداد و ستر ہزار سے کم نہ تہی", drew its pay through Bakhshi Bhagat Ram (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 96, folio 99). Khalid has his empire extend "from FATA (Federally Administered Tribal Areas) to Kashmir to Multan in the South and the River Sutlej", and records that he made a deal with the British (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 207, folio not stated). He adds: "In the land of Punjab which was for over a millenium [as printed] ruled by Afghans, Mughals and other non-Punjabis, Ranjit Singh was the first Punjabi to rule Punjab", and credits him with stability "in Punjab which lasted a good forty years" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 208, folio not stated). "There is a view that Ranjit was a true follower of Guru Gobind Singh", Khalid writes (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 208, folio not stated); and "In 1802, he employed a Nihang by the name of Akali Phula Singh to capture the city of Amritsar", Phula Singh bringing "two to three thousand Nihangs into the state army" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 210, folio not stated). "During his reign, Maharaja Ranjit Singh captured the Pathan regions of Khyber-Pakhthunkwa and the tribal areas, as a result of which several people converted to Sikhism" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 161–162, folio not stated).

## The Court

Chishti names the court's great men: "جمعدار … خوشحال سنگھ اور فقیر عزیز الدین[OCR?] … و دیوان دینا ناتھ[OCR?]" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 581). Dhian Singh first served "بخدمت مہاراجہ رنجیت سنگھ … بہ تنخواہ معمولی", and Khushal Singh rose at a time when "مہاراجہ صاحب ہمراہ بیوہ[OCR?] ہی موراں طوائف با عیش و عشرت بمقام سمن برج قلعہ لاہور مشغول تھے" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 582). Khushal Singh received "لاہور و امرتسر میں حویلیاں اور باغ", and Dina Nath built "ایک بڑا شوالہ لاہور کی [illegible] کے پاس" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 583). The Faqir family were known in Lahore as "خاندان فقیر صاحبان اور حکیموں" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 584), and the book tells their anecdote of the blind eye and the robe of honour, adding that the Maharaja placed complete trust in their word: "مہاراجہ رحمت[OCR?] سکہ[OCR?] انکی قول و کلام پر … کمال اعتبار کہا[OCR?] تھا" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 585). Khalid names "Fakir Azizudin" as holding the offices of "Prime Minister and the Foreign Minister", and his Muslim generals "General Mohammad Sultan, General Elahi Baksh and General Ghaus Mohammad Khan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 207, folio not stated); the Fakir's "family still lives in Lahore in their ancestral house, located inside the walled city" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 207, folio not stated). Kanhaiya Lal records Khalifa Ghulam al-Madad[OCR?] seated beside him "برابر کرسی پر", on an equal chair, and Qazi Nizam-ud-Din as the city's qazi at the conquest of Lahore (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 50, folio 53).

Hira Singh, in Chishti's account, was the Maharaja's favourite: "مہاراجہ کلان اسکو اپنا ایاز خیال کرکے [illegible] فرزند کہا کرتی تہی", and five rupees were put under Hira Singh's head every night while the Maharaja lived (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 868). The Hazuri family, Chishti adds, holds a sealed order of his, "ایک پروانہ مہری مہاراجہ رنجیت سنگھ جسکی مہر میں اکال سہائی[OCR?] رنجیت سنگھ بخط فارسی کندہ", dated "سومین صفر المظفر سنہ [illegible]" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 600).

Two of his attachments are told at length. The courtesan Moran, Kanhaiya Lal writes, held such favour that he never disobeyed her word, and coin was struck in her name (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 47, folio 50): "تمام پنجاب میں اسکا سکہ جاری ہو گیا", and "مورانشاہی[OCR?]" rupees and paisa are still kept as keepsakes (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 179, folio 182). Chishti's version calls Mehr Muhkam "مہارا حد رنجیت سنگہ کا نوکر" and says "مہارا حد رنجیت سنگہ عالم سعید[OCR?] اشتہار مہدراں[OCR?] طوائف پر عاشق ہو گیا"; the "رویہ[OCR?] موراں شاہی" was current, and he built "موضع موراں والا" and "مسجد موراں والی", "اوسکی ابتک موجود ہے" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 646; Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 647). "موراں کا حکم یہ عدالت اور دربار علاحدہ قائم ہو تا تہا", and Moran's word brought about Mehr Muhkam's ruin and the "تخریب نوان کوٹ" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 647). Chishti closes with a moral: Moran's grave is unknown, "رنجیت سنگہ کا نام و نشان مسمو دیا[OCR?]", and his sons were "معزول الریاست" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 648).

The second is Gul Begum. Kanhaiya Lal records his marriage to her, the *phere* taken and the procession going from Lahore to Amritsar (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 47–48, folios 50–51). Khalid writes: "Like the third Mughal Emperor Akbar, Maharaja Ranjit Singh was a secular ruler", who "constructed a mosque at a mohalla called Gul Begum in Lahore in memory of his Muslim wife"; and "Another Muslim wife of his, Jind Kaur, ordered the construction of a library" at Data Darbar (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 207, folio not stated).

## Lahore in His Reign

Kanhaiya Lal, writing of the city rather than the samadhi, makes Ranjit Singh its restorer: "مہاراجہ رنجیت سنگھ کا زبانہ آیا تو گویا شہر کے بخت جاگے اور آبادی ہونے لگی" (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 32, folio 35), and "مہاراجہ رنجیت سنگھ کی چہل سالہ مدت علمداری میں یہ شہر لاہور بخوبی آباد ہو چکا تہا" (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 33, folio 36). Though none of the ruined outer quarters was resettled, "حصار کے اندرونی شہر کی آبادی اچھی طرح سے ہو گئی تھی" (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 22, folio 17), and his courtiers made "بڑے بڑے باغ" on the outer ruins (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 33, folio 36). He kept the walls in "ہمیشہ مرمت دیوار فصیل اور روزانہ" repair (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 21, folio 16), dug a deep pukka ditch on all four sides and put at each gate a bridge and a second solid gate, so that "غنیم کبھی شہر پہ متصرف نہیں ہو سکتا تھا" (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 21, folio 16); the book lists the works again as "دوہرے دروازے بنوائے خندق کھدوائی دھول کوٹ [OCR?] تعمیر کیا فصیل کی مرمت کی غیر آباد محلوں کی آبادی کا حکم دیا" (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 32, folio 35). Inside the Khizri Gate he kept two lions in cages, the gate guards answering for them; the cages went under the British, but the name Shiran Wala Darwaza stuck (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 20, folio 15). Chishti has Nura, who fed his wheat on milk, win access to the court and an appointment to the "تعمیر خندق لاہور" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 423).

He renamed the fort's Moti Masjid "موتی مندر" and kept "سلطنت کا خزانہ" there; in Kanhaiya Lal's day "سرکار انگریزی بہی صوبہ پنجاب کا خزانہ اُسی میں رکہتی ہے" (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 339, folio 346). Under him, Chishti writes, it was proclaimed in the city that whoever brought out a *taʿzia* would lose his head, "جو کوئی تعزیہ نکالے گا اسکا [OCR?] سر [illegible] ہوگا" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 274).

## The Maharaja as Patron

Chishti, in his survey of Lahore's shrines, records many grants and gifts made by the Maharaja at other sites. Of the majzub Subah Shah it was said that the outward king was the Maharaja, "طاہری[OCR?] بادشاہ مہاراجہ رنجیت سنگھ ہی" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 85); all the pillars of the state, with the Maharaja, held him in regard (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 86); and the Maharaja and his successors down to Dalip Singh came every Saturday to a *munhara* and gave *nazr*, the *sajjada nishin* receiving "چار پانچ ہزار روپیہ" each Saturday (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 95). At Data Darbar a Qur'an "ایک تو رنجیت[OCR?] سنگھ نے بوقت فتح پشاور در پشاور سے یہاں بھجوایا تب سے یہاں کہا ہوا ہے" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 169), and the shrine received "ایک ہزار[OCR?] روپیہ سالیانہ" and some sweets (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 171). Mauj Darya's shrine had "چالیس [OCR?] روپیہ ماہیانہ" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 105); a well at Ghore Shah's site "مہاراجہ رنجیت سنگھ نے عطا کیا تھا" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 432); and the samadh of Sri Chand at Tahli Sahib held "بعہد رنجیت سنگہ ایک کہ[OCR?] روپیہ یومیہ اور ایک چاہ معہ مکہ[OCR?] زمین" revenue-free (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 679). At the Mani Singh site a *sadabart* of "پچیس روپیہ یومیہ" ran under "مہاراجہ کلان" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 768), and Bhai Sahib Singh was visited "بار ہا" by the Maharaja (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 770). Of Syed Ali Shah, Chishti writes that "مہاراجہ صاحب تا حین حیات بالفت[OCR?] دلی معتقد ان حضرت کا رہا" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 707). Among his patronage Chishti records, too, a dera of *granthis*, "سمت [OCR?] اٹہارہ سو اناسی [OCR?] مین بمقام بہاور [OCR?]", with four hundred *granthis*, five standards, a *nagara* and five hundred men with *qawwals* (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 866).

Of the Shalamar garden, which he had been invited to restore, Chishti writes that he did so: "ایسا آباد کیا کہ اب بہی ظاہر ہے" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 787). The samadh at Shahid Ganj was raised in his reign, "پہر بعہد مہاراجہ رنجیت سنگہ یہہ تمام عمارت طیار ہوئی" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 767). Even in recording the endowment of the Wazir Khan mosque, Chishti praises him against his own grain: "آفرین کرنا چاہیے مہاراجہ رنجیت سنگھ کو کہ با وجود دیگر[OCR?] انکے تعصب[OCR?] مذہبی ..." (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 747–748). Kanhaiya Lal records the fund and *jagir* he gave to the Baoli Sahib (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 113, folio 116).

Khalid writes that "When Maharaja Ranjit Singh became the sovereign of Punjab at the end of the eighteenth century he spent large amounts of money on the renovation and expansion of many gurdwaras", attaching "vast tracts of land" so that revenues could fund them (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 34, folio 16); "During the tenure of Maharaja Ranjit Singh" Baba Sahib Singh Bedi was allotted "vast tracts of land around the cities of Gujranwala and Lahore", on which he founded Bedian (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 44, folio 26). The Panja Sahib gurdwara was "extended" in his tenure (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 159, folio not stated), its frescoes "part of the original complex built by Maharaja Ranjit Singh" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 167, folio not stated), and he "first erected" the present Dera Sahib structure (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 200, folio not stated). In *In Search of Shiva*, Iqbal Qaiser says of the gurdwara at Mandi Mangat that "The current structure was raised by Maharaja Ranjit Singh" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated), and Khalid writes that "during the tenure of Maharaja Ranjit Singh" the village of Maraka "was re-populated a few kilometres away from the ruins of the old one" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).

## The Maharaja as Despoiler

The same books record what he took. Chishti says that in Ranjit Singh's time red, black and marble stones "گرد و نواح لاہور کے" were torn down (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 296), and that when the cantonments of his armies were built the bricks of old tombs and houses were pulled out, so that "ہزار ہا مقابر و گنبد و مساجد و باغات و حویلیہائے[OCR?] قدیمہ جو یادگار امرائے سلف تھیں گم گشتیں[OCR?]" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 431). At Jahangir's tomb, "بباعث تعصب مذہبی", the marble, amber and red-stone railings worth "لاکہہا روپیہ" were set "بمقام دربار سری امرتسر در رام باغ", though outwardly he was "ارادتمند [OCR?] شاہان سلف بدرجہ کمال" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 855); the minaret lattices "رنجیت سنگہ مہاراجہ نے اکہڑوا ڈالی" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 859–860); and "بعہد رنجیت سنگہ تمام جاگیرین مجاورین کی ضبط ہوگئین", leaving only the garden (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 864). The marble slabs and lattices of Mian Mir's tomb went to a baradari at Amritsar (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 313); Zeb-un-Nisa's marble tomb was taken down for a baradari (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 637); and the marble of Shalamar's Sawan-Bhadon baradaris, "بسنگدلی مہاراجہ رنجیت سنگہ", was set at "رام باغ امرتسر" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 783). Shaikh Ashraf's tomb and mosque, Chishti writes, "مہاراجہ رنجیت سنگہ[OCR?] نو اس مقبرہ اور مسجد کو مسمار کرا دیا" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 159).

Lahori's tazkira gives a single example. Of the tomb of Pir Hadi Rahnuma, on the Anarkali–Mian Mir road, it says: "پہلے اس مقبرہ کی عمارت سنگین تھی۔ سکھوں کے وقت میں رنجیت سنگھ نے اس کا پتھر اکھڑوالیا" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 267, folio 255). Kanhaiya Lal adds the graves of Zakariya Khan, "جن پر لاکہوں روپیے کا سنگ مرمر تہا وہ سب مہاراجہ رنجیت سنگہ نے اُتروا لیا" (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 91, folio 94); mosques used as powder magazines (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 80, 83, 84; folios 83, 86, 87); the demolition of Shah Sharaf's mosque and tomb for the Bhati Gate glacis, after which the coffin was reburied and "مہاراجہ کے حکم سے چہوٹا سا چبوترہ پختہ اُسپر بنا دیا گیا" (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 80–81, folios 83–84); and the expropriation of Mohalla Saudagran for the Guru Ram Das gurdwara (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 101, folio 104).

Beyond Lahore, Richard Eaton records that he seized the extensive holdings of the shrine at Pakpattan in 1810 and allowed its diwan and his retainers "only Rs. 1,000 a year for their maintenance, derived from the town duties of Pakpattan, in addition to a fourth share of four small villages nearby" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 231 and n. 59). Khalid reports, in Iqbal Qaiser's words, that "During the tenure of Maharaja Ranjit Singh, the Sikhs took over the mosque [at Shaheed Ganj], placed the Guru Granth Sahib inside it, converting it into a gurdwara. The Muslims could not do anything then" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 77, folio 59). He also gives, as things said, that "It is said that the Badshahi Masjid was used as a stable for horses", and that the Maharaja "removed the top structure of Emperor Jahangir's tomb and placed it in the Hazuri Bagh" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 207, folio not stated).

## The Maharaja and the Faqirs

Lahori's tazkira of the Punjab's saints sees the Maharaja through the majzubs of Lahore. "مہاراجہ رنجیت سنگھ والی پنجاب کو کمال اعتقاد اس کی نسبت تھا۔ ہزاروں روپیہ وہ دیتا۔ مگر یہ نہ لیتا" — he had complete faith in Mastan Shah Lahori and would give him thousands of rupees, which the majzub would not take (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 246, folio 234). Once he seated Mastan Shah on his own elephant to bring him to the fort, and the majzub jumped off it: "ایک مرتبہ رنجیت سنگھ اپنے ہاتھی پر بٹھلا کر قلعے کو لیے جاتا تھا۔ یہ ہاتھی کے اوپر سے کود پڑا" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 246, folio 234).

Of the majzuba Mai Bhagi Lahori the tazkira says: "مہاراجہ رنجیت سنگھ اس کا کمال معتقد تھا۔ اگرچہ یہ اس کو گالیاں دیتی۔ مگر وہ اس کا پیچھا نہ چھوڑتا" — he was her devoted follower, and though she abused him he would not leave her; the appointment of a Hindu pandit to a *diwani* post at his darbar is credited to her word (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 255, folio 243). Chishti's account of her adds that he first offered her five hundred rupees as *nazr*, that she struck him with bricks more than once, and that in "کرکس[OCR?] والی سال یعنی سمت اٹہارہ سو نوی[OCR?]" she refused Dhian Singh and Suchet Singh but came with Kharak Singh and stayed eleven days; a building at "کوٹ سعید" was put up for "دس ہزار روپیہ", and her fame spread because the ruler was her devotee (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 790).

## His Death

The site's account gives his death as 27 June 1839, after which construction of the samadhi began the following month. Khalid gives his years as 1780–1839 (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 42, folio not stated), and writes that "The British took over soon after his death" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 208–209, folio not stated).

Kanhaiya Lal dates the death in a sentence whose numeral and month are uncertain in the printed text: "آخر ۱۸۹۶[OCR?] مہینہ ہگرمی[OCR?] میں مر گیا", the calendar era not stated (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 217, folio 220). Elsewhere he gives the death year as "سن ۱۸۹۶ بکرمی", with the Baoli Sahib buildings still going up and left unfinished (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 113, folio 116); he also says the Maharaja fell severely ill in "سن ۱۸۹۶[OCR?] بکرمی" (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 112, folio 115); and in one passage he notes only that "مہاراجہ کی وفات تک یہ شہر روز بروز آباد ہوتا گیا" (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 32, folio 35).

Lahori's tazkira gives no year for the death, but it gives the deathbed. Ill and near his end, the Maharaja sent for the majzub Taj Shah Lahori and asked him for his health. The faqir answered that death is for everyone, as his father and the faqir's father had died, and that sandalwood should be brought to burn him:

"مہاراجہ رنجیت سنگھ نے بحالت بیماری و آخری وقت کے ان کو بلایا اور اپنی صحت کے لیے عرض کی۔ جواب دیا کہ مرنا [OCR?] سب کے واسطے ہے۔ جس طرح تیرا اور میرا باپ مر گیا ہے۔ تو بھی مرنے والا ہے۔ چندن کی لکڑی تیرے جلانے کے لیے لانی چاہیے" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 245, folio 233).

Hearing this he lost hope, and he died as the faqir left the fort: "یہ سن کر وہ نا امید ہوا۔ جب تاج شاہ قلعے سے نکلا۔ مہاراجہ رنجیت سنگھ نے جان دے دی" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 245, folio 233). The sandalwood is the faqir's phrase in the story, not a report of the rite. The book dates only the faqir's own death, to "سال ایک ہزار دو سو اکسٹھ ہجری", 1261 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 245).

## After Him

Lahori's tazkira credits the same faqir with foreseeing the end of the Sikh kingdom on the day the Maharaja died: "سکھوں کی سلطنت کی خرابی کا حال پہلے ہی اس نے بیان کر دیا تھا۔ یعنی جس روز راجہ رنجیت سنگھ مرا تھا۔ اسی روز کہہ دیا تھا کہ نو برس اور یہ سلطنت رہے گی پھر پنجاب کے مالک فرنگی ہو جائیں گے" — nine more years, and then the "فرنگی" would be masters of the Punjab (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 244, folio 232). It is the author's report of a saying, not a dated event.

Kanhaiya Lal writes that "مہاراجہ رنجیت سنگہ کے مرنے کے بعد اُسکا حقیقی بیٹا مہاراجہ کہڑک سنگہ پنجاب کی حکومت کے تختہ پر بیٹھا" (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 221, folio 224), and Chishti that "مہاراجہ کھڑک سنگھ خلف الصدیق اونکی کرسی حکومت پنجاب پر بیٹھو" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 586). Chishti dates Kharak Singh's death and Sher Singh's accession to the same year, "سمت ۱۸۹۷[OCR?] بکرمی" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 723), and heads his pages on the period "ہمہ احوال بعد وفات مہاراجہ رنجیت سنگھ" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 589–591). He calls the Sandhanwalias "رشتہ دار قریبی رانی چند کور و مہاراجہ" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 588), and has Dhian Singh tell Sher Singh, "انشاء اللہ تعالیٰ عنقریب آپ حاکم پنجاب ہو نگے" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 588). Sher Singh's killers were destroyed "بعد محاصرہ قلعہ لاہور و معرکہ عظیم" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 589). Lahori's tazkira records the death of Hira Singh, minister to Duleep Singh: "جس روز راجہ ہیرا سنگھ مہاراجہ دلیپ سنگھ کا وزیر قتل ہوا تھا … راجہ ہیرا سنگھ بہ بہانہ شکار بھاگ نکلا۔ سکھوں نے اس کا تعاقب کر کے اس کو مار ڈالا اور جواہر سنگھ کو وزیر بنایا" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 245, folio 233). Chishti says he saw with his own eyes the heads of Raja Hira Singh and Pandit Jalla hung from a gutter-spout, "راقم الحروف نی بچشم خود دیکہا" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 868).

Kanhaiya Lal allows that "اس کے مرنے کے بعد البتہ خفیف صدمے خود سکھی فوج کے ہاتھ شہر کو پہنچے" (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 32, folio 35). Chishti carries the story through the defeat at "سبھراؤں[OCR?]", the 1846 treaty and the Resident at Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 591), to the rising of "سنہ ۱۸۴۸[OCR?]ء" and the annexation: "۲۹[OCR?] مارچ سنہ ۱۸۴۹[OCR?]ء تمام ملک سلطنت انگریزی میں داخل ہو گیا" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 592). On "[illegible] دسمبر سنہ ۱۸۴۹[OCR?]ء کو دلیپ سنگھ لاہور سے روانہ ہوا" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 592), and the page carries a *qitʿa* on his departure, two of whose lines run:

چون شمع[OCR?] پنجاب از پنجاب رفت
نو گلی از گلشن پنجاب رفت

Chishti prints them on the departure of Duleep Singh (Dalip Singh in his text) (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 592). Khalid calls Duleep Singh "the last Maharaja of Punjab, before the British took over", and writes that "Punjab became a British colony almost immediately after his death eventually leading to its division at the time of Partition" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 209, folio not stated). When Duleep Singh was exiled and English rule was complete, Kanhaiya Lal notes, the troops left the Badshahi Mosque (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 141, folio 144).

## The Building of the Samadhi

Construction began the month after the Maharaja's death under his son Kharak Singh and was largely completed by 1848 under his youngest son, Duleep Singh, with final repairs funded by the British after their 1849 annexation of Punjab.

Kanhaiya Lal confirms the site of the cremation: "اس جگہہ جلایا گیا جس جگہ اب اُسکی سمادہ بنی ہوئی" — he was burned where his samadh now stands (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 217, folio 220). He places the building precisely: "یہ عالیشان مکان سمادہ کا روشنائی دروازے باہر فصیل کے ملحق دیوار بدیوار مسجد بادشاہی کے بجانب شمال واقع ہے" — outside the Roshnai Gate, against the city wall, wall to wall with the Badshahi Mosque, to the north (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 217, folio 220). From the neighbouring entry he gives its wall as lying to the west of the samadh of Guru Arjan, at a distance the printed text has lost (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 205–206, folios 208–209). His contents list it as "سمادہ مہاراجہ رنجیت سنگھ", immediately after Shahid Ganj (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 4, folio 3); a second entry, "سمادہ مہاراجہ [illegible] سنگھ والی پنجاب", stands earlier in the list, and the book does not say whether it is the same monument (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 3, folio 2).

Beyond the site's own account, the sources consulted do not record when the building was begun or finished, or who built it. Chishti's contents open with "حال سمادہ[OCR?] مہاراجہ رنجیت سنگھ", followed by lines on the Maharaja's life, his deeds, his ministers and the period after his death (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 4); his section on the building carries the margin heading "حال سمادہ راجہ رنجیت سنگھ" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 569).

## The Fabric, as Kanhaiya Lal Surveyed It

At the monument's heart, beneath a marble pavilion inlaid with pietra dura stonework, a lotus-shaped marble urn holds the Maharaja's ashes. Architecturally the building is a striking synthesis of Sikh, Hindu and Islamic idioms: gilded fluted domes and cupolas rise above red sandstone doorways carved with images of Ganesha, Devi and Brahma, while surviving interior frescoes, painted between 1839 and 1849, have been reassessed by modern scholars as a high point of Sikh decorative art.

Kanhaiya Lal enters from the east. The eastern wall is pakka, with red stone at the doorway, five steps up, the steps and frame also of red stone (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 217, folio 220). Above the door "تین مورتیں مسنوتی[OCR?] شو جی و گنیش جی و بیر بہا[OCR?] کی پتہر میں بنا کر نصب کی گئی ہین" — three images, of Shiv ji, Ganesh ji and a third whose name is uncertain in the printed text, made in stone and installed there (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 217, folio 220). In the cells of the eastern wall's first storey, shopkeepers keep shops (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 217, folio 220).

The samadh chamber stands on a grand platform "جسکا ارتفاع پانچ فٹ کے قریب ہوگا", about five feet high, faced with marble to the east and north and with brick to the south and west, and hollow beneath "بطور تہہ خانے کے پولا", like a cellar. Each of its four sides has three doors with marble frames, the middle one large and the two flanking it small; only the eastern middle door is kept open, with a large black stone slab before it (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 218, folio 221). Within is a roofed hall; at its centre stands a grand marble *baradari* with a *ghulam-gardish* (غلام گردش) running round all four sides; and at the centre of the *baradari* a marble bungalow whose columns are marble "مگر اوپر کا گنبد خشتی ہے", but whose dome is of brick (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 219, folio 222). The ceiling of the great dome carries mirrors and elaborate gilding (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 219–220, folios 222–223).

The *ghulam-gardish* is "آٹھہ درجون میں منقسم", divided into eight bays, four at the corners and four on the sides, with brick floors and walls but remarkable glass work on the ceilings, the four corner ceilings gilded and the four side ones white (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 220, folio 223). A red stone stair in the south-west corner, of "انیس[OCR?] سیڑہیان", nineteen steps as the number is printed, rises to the second storey, which carries two balconies to each side, each of three doors, with a latticed bay of three openings between them, red stone railings in the balconies' lattices and marble railings within the openings (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 220, folio 223). A brick stair from the same corner, "بائیس[OCR?] زینہ", twenty-two steps, leads to a large roofed terrace with small domelets along the parapets, four corner domelets, and at the centre of each wall a domed, elongated sitting-place of eight doors (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 220, folio 223). Above the great dome stands "ایک اور عالیشان بلند بارہ دری", another lofty *baradari*, all of pakka brick, with three doors to each side, crowned by a round dome "جس پر کلس طلائی نصب ہے", bearing a gilded finial, with four gilded domelets at its corners (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 221, folio 224).

Under the high plinth, in a clause much damaged in the printed text, large rooms are built whose doors lie beneath the northern wall, and people are living in them; the northern door and stair "مگر اب وہ بند ہے آمد ورفت صرف شرقی دروازے سے ہوتی ہے" — it is now closed, and entry is only by the eastern door (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 221, folio 224). In that whole account Kanhaiya Lal records nothing on the samadh's custodians, offerings or anniversary (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 222, folio 225).

## The Fabric, as Chishti Surveyed It

Chishti's survey, many of whose words are uncertain in the printed text, begins at a two-storeyed gateway, "دو منزلہ یہ دروازہ", of red stone ("سنگ سرخ") above, with floral work; the spaces on both sides are used "بطور دوکان[OCR?]" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 569). Inside the east gate is a south-facing room, "تین[OCR?] گز" by "آٹھہ[OCR?] گز" as printed, a stair facing north, and an upper door in a red stone frame, the courtyard floored "فرش چونہ" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 570). South of the gate lies a "دالان پنجدری[OCR?]" with "ستون خشتی[OCR?]" and a ceiling "منقش رنگین چونہ گچ", and two rooms, in one of which the income of the place is collected (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 570). Further on he notes a "ستہ درہ" dalan, rooms, and a courtyard "طول ۴۵[OCR?] گز اور عرض ۴۴[OCR?] گز", with a "مسجد[OCR?]" to the west, where the line breaks off (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 571).

The samadh itself, in Chishti's account, stands on a platform "دو ڈیرہ[OCR?] گز" high, as printed, with a "سرد خانہ" beneath, faced with "سنگ مرمر گلکار" on the north and east (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 572). Above a marble course stands "عمارت خشتی" in white plaster, "دو منزلہ", with "بارہ در محرابی" on all four sides and, on the east, a marble "مورت گنین[OCR?]" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 572). Inside is a marble *baradari* with the samadh, and over it "دو گنبد زیر و بالا", a marble floor, a marble image of "دیوی مائی", and a ceiling of "محل آئینہ" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 572). The large dome is gilded ("طلائی") and set with mirrors, and beneath it is the Maharaja's square memorial under a covering; a door on the west leads to the cool chamber below (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 573). Four corner towers carry *kalas* finials; there is a large dome with a gilded finial and four small domes at the corners (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 573–574), and the book notes "کلس طلائی رنگ اسکا سفید" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 574).

Elsewhere Chishti compares the inner dome of the samadh of Jhangar Shah with the Maharaja's, in a sentence whose words and sense are uncertain in the printed text: "اور یہہ دنسع[OCR?] یعنی سچر[OCR?] سمادہ مہاراجہ رنجیت سنگہ کسی اور جگہہ میں نہیں" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 659). For the history after Jawahar Singh's death he sends the reader to "تواریخ خالصہ" and to "مہاراجہ کلان کی سمادہ کے حال" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 870).

## The Fabric Today

Haroon Khalid, attending the death anniversary of 2011, describes the samadhi facing Gurdwara Dera Sahib as "a beautiful white building, adorned with detailed frescoes and other art work" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 201, folio not stated), and on that day "A police official sits on top of the samadhi with his gun in front of him" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 201, folio not stated). He writes: "Just behind the samadhi of Guru Arjan, a splendid building, white in colour, stands four floors from the ground with a small dome on the top", with the minaret of the Badshahi Masjid behind (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 205, folio not stated). The samadhi "stands on top of another single storey building, painted yellow", in a courtyard "with rooms for the pilgrims on its sides" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 206, folio not stated). He notes: "Two loud speakers have been placed on top of the samadhi, facing the direction of the ground, erected opposite the mosque and the city, perhaps as a respect to the Muslim religion, a respect that is only expected of the religious minorities" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 206, folio not stated).

In the hall Khalid saw "a palanquin in the centre, constructed on a raised platform with its pillars made of white marble" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 206, folio not stated); it "marks the spot where the last remains of the Maharaja were buried. His cremation took place in the ground accompanying the building" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 209, folio not stated). "On a regular day, a portrait of the Maharaja is placed at the palanquin, dressed in a white robe, sporting a long white beard", next to that of "his youngest son, Dalip Singh, who also happened to be the last Maharaja of Punjab"; "Today, the portraits have been removed" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 209, folio not stated). The dome is "decorated with coloured glass", green, orange and red, in geometric patterns; niches hold frescoes; and the balcony walls show court scenes and "religious drawings from Hindu mythology", with "depictions of Krishna, Ganesh, Ram and Sita" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 209, folio not stated). The paintings "have faded", some have "disappeared all together", and visitors "have noted down their names and numbers thereby disfiguring it" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 209, folio not stated).

Modern scholarship has focused particular attention on the samadhi's ornamental programme: research by the art historian Dr Nadhra Shahbaz Naeem Khan uncovered guardian figures beneath whitewash on the building's eastern facade, though subsequent recoatings have since covered and lost this decoration again.

## Those Who Burned With Him

Around the Maharaja's urn, in the site's account, smaller urns commemorate the four queens and seven attendants who, according to the custom of sati, died on his funeral pyre.

Kanhaiya Lal counts the dead differently: on the marble platform within the marble bungalow "سمادہ سنگین مہاراجہ رنجیت سنگہ کی وسط میں بنی ہوئی ہے اور گیارہ سمادہیں اور گیارہ رانیون کی ہین جو مہاراجہ کے ساتھہ ستی ہوئی تھیں اور دو سمادہیں اُن دو کبوتروں کی ہین" — the Maharaja's stone samadh at the centre, eleven samadhs of the eleven *ranis* who became *sati* with him, and two samadhs of two pigeons (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 219, folio 222). Khalid describes an adjacent hall with a raised platform bearing "thirteen small semi-spheres. These are the samadhis of the eleven women and two pigeons who also burned in the pyre of Maharaja Ranjit Singh", among them "his wives and concubines"; he calls the custom "sati", from "the ancient Rajput tradition" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 210, folio not stated).

## Images, Custodians and Repairs

In one of the chambers of the gate range, Kanhaiya Lal writes, a marble image of the eight-armed Devi, "ہشت بہجی[OCR?] دیوی", stands with great reverence, throne and canopy all of marble, the Devi mounted "سنگ مرمر کے شیر پر", on a marble lion (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 218, folio 221). He gives its history: "یہ تصویر و دیوی جی کی رانی جندان والدہ مہاراجہ دلیپ سنگہ کے پاس شمن بیج[OCR?] میں رکھی تھی جب اُسکو جلا وطن کیا گیا تو اُس نے اس سمادہ پر سورت[OCR?] بہجوا دی تھی" — the image had been kept by Rani Jindan, mother of Maharaja Duleep Singh, and when she was exiled she sent it to this samadh, "اور پجاری علیحدہ مقرر ہے", and a separate *pujari* is appointed for it (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 218, folio 221).

Chishti, too, finds images. In the southern niche is "مورت ہنومان سنگ مرمر کی", and in the northern niche the image of a deity whose name is uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 570). In the dalan stands a movable marble *baradari*, "ایسی بارہ دری ہے کہ جہاں چاہیں ... لیجاویں[OCR?]", measuring "سوا دو گز[OCR?]" by "ڈیڑہ گز[OCR?]", on "دس[OCR?] پایہ" of marble, connected in a damaged line with the departure of a Rani from Lahore, "جب رانی چند[OCR?] لاہور سے رخصت ہوئی"; inside it are an image of a *devi*, a bull and a marble lion, and the book goes on to an account of the *devi*'s bathing (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 570). Its custodian, Chishti writes, was "محافظ تمام یہاں کا لالہ نرسنگھ داس[OCR?] پسر بھگوان[OCR?]", then "مہتمم سماد", and "اب اوسکی طرف سے ایک ملازم یہاں حاضر رہتا ہے" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 570). Today the Evacuee Trust Property Board administers the site.

Kanhaiya Lal was himself responsible for the building's survival. "پہلے اس بارہ دری کے آٹھہ ستون سنگ مرمر کے تھے … چند سال کے بعد ستون شق ہو گئے … قریب تہا کہ وہ لا ثانی عمارت گر جائے": the eight slender marble columns of the *baradari* cracked under the weight of the dome, and the building came near to collapse (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 219, folio 222). The "صاحبان عالیشان", seeing its state, ordered the author, then Executive Engineer of the Buildings Division at Lahore, to secure it; he added eight brick columns to the eight, "آٹھہ ستونون کے سولہ ستون ہو گئے", making sixteen, and bound the cracked ones with "نہایت مضبوط آ ہنی حلقے", very strong iron rings (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 219, folio 222). He gives no year for the work and names no authority beyond those words.

## The Adjoining Samadhis

Adjoining smaller samadhis honour his son Kharak Singh and grandson Nau Nihal Singh. Kanhaiya Lal places their joint building "مہاراجہ رنجیت سنگہ کی سمادہ سے بجانب غرب بنا ہوا ہے اور اُسی چبوترے کی غربی حد پر جو زیر سمادہ مہاراجہ رنجیت سنگہ ہے" — to the west of Ranjit Singh's samadh, on the western edge of the same platform that lies beneath it (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 222, folio 225). Its foundation was laid after Ranjit Singh's death, in Kharak Singh's time; some work was done under Sher Singh; it stopped "بسبب کشت وجون[OCR?] با ہمی و برچہ گردی[OCR?]", because of mutual killing and disorder; and "صاحبان انگریز نے با حتمام[OCR?] پہنچایا", the English brought it to completion (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 222–223, folios 225–226).

Both books also describe, as separate monuments, the samadhs of the Maharaja's wives. Chishti heads one "مسماۃ نکائین[OCR?] وجہ[OCR?] مہاراجہ رنجیت سنگھ والدہ مہاراجہ کھڑک سنگھ" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 664), and names another "[illegible] کنور[OCR?] زوجہ رنجیت سنگھ" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 665). Kanhaiya Lal's entry on the samadh of Rani Nakain and Rani Chand Kaur records that after the deaths of Kharak Singh and Nau Nihal Singh, Rani Chand Kaur became mistress of the kingdom and was set on the throne by the Sandhanwalias, and that Raja Dhian Singh withdrew in anger to Jammu (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 443, folio 450).

## Devotion and Practice

Though formally a samadhi rather than a constituted gurdwara, the site has long functioned as a living place of Sikh devotion: the Guru Granth Sahib was installed and read there for decades after construction, and today it remains an active pilgrimage destination, especially around Ranjit Singh's death anniversary and the spring Vaisakhi season, when Sikh visitors, including delegations from India, gather to pay homage.

Khalid's chapter records one such day. In the hall were "about a hundred and fifty people, all men": "This is the conclusion of the Akhand Path held in the honour of Maharaja Ranjit Singh, also known as Sher-e-Punjab, or the Lion of Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 206, folio not stated). He dates it: "The occasion is the death anniversary of the ruler and the date 29th June 2011, twelve days after the death anniversary commemoration of Guru Arjan in the same complex"; the earlier pilgrims had left "and a set of new ones have arrived from India" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 206, folio not stated). After the ceremony came *langar* in two rows, of dal, rice, rotis and water, and tea "Boiled with sugar and ginger", "highly popular in East Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 210–211, folio not stated).

He spoke with the pilgrims. "Amanpreet Singh, 35, is a trader from Ludhiana", on his first visit to Pakistan: "The Maharaja was a great leader ... There was no crime" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 208, folio not stated). "54 year old Jaswant Singh" and a friend had come for the first time "from the city of Qadian in East Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 211, folio not stated).

## Legends and Miracles

Kanhaiya Lal explains the two smallest samadhs on the platform: the pigeons "بوقت داغ[OCR?] دینے نعش کے چتا کے اندر اُڑتے ہوئے آ پڑے تھے اور مہاراجہ کی نعش کے ساتھہ بھی جل گئے تھے" — at the burning of the body they came flying and fell into the pyre, and were burned with the Maharaja's corpse (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 219, folio 222). He reports it flatly, the two samadhs standing as its evidence; Khalid, too, counts the two pigeons among those who burned in the pyre (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 210, folio not stated).

Chishti tells a story of the Maharaja at the tomb of Mian Mir. He came to strip the tomb, his horse "لیلی" bolted, and he fell and hurt his "جبڑوں[OCR?]"; "اب ہمکو سزا[OCR?] بےادبی[OCR?] کی ملی", he said, gave "پانسو روپیہ نذرانہ", ordered the tomb whitewashed, and remained a devotee of the saint and respectful of the *mujawirs* (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 296–297). He also records the prayers of Subah Shah for the Maharaja (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 86).

The deathbed of Lahori's tazkira belongs here as well as to the history: the faqir who tells the ailing king to send for sandalwood, and the king who dies as the faqir walks out of the fort (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 245, folio 233), and the same faqir's prophecy of nine years (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 244, folio 232).

## Setting: Hazuri Bagh and the Roshnai Gate

The material in this section is about the place around the samadhi, not about the samadhi.

Khalid sets the scene: "The Hazuri Bagh is situated in the middle of these monuments, with a single storey pavilion placed symmetrically at the center of the garden while four lanes lead towards it" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 205, folio not stated). "The Roshani gate, one of the thirteen gateways that lead into the ancient walled city of Lahore, is between the Alamgiri Gate and the Royal Mosque"; lamps "were lit all along the passageway ... hence the name Roshani gate, or the gate of lights" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 205, folio not stated). The gate "was once reserved for the prince and the princess, who would go from the Fort to the city through this entrance" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 205, folio not stated). Behind it lies the Heera Mandi bazaar, "still one of the largest markets for traditional instruments in the city" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 205, folio not stated).

Kanhaiya Lal names Hazuri Bagh as "چہوٹا سا میدان جسکو اب حضوری باغ کہتے ہین", without mentioning the samadhi (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 138, folio 141). In his long description of the garden and its *baradari* he says that Ranjit Singh laid out the garden and sat at darbar on the *baradari*'s *shah-nashin*, again without mentioning the samadh (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 339–342, folios 346–349). Of the *baradari* in the garden, Chishti writes that Zeb-un-Nisa's marble tomb was taken down to build it, "اب تک وہ بارہ دری حضوری باغ میں یادگار[OCR?] [illegible] مہاراج صاحب [illegible] موجود ہے" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 637), while Khalid reports it as said that "The pavilion, which now stands in the middle of the ground, was part of the structure of Emperor Jahangir's tomb" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 207, folio not stated).

"For the city that the Maharaja once ruled", Khalid writes of the Lahore of 2011, "he is an alien, his memory kept alive not by people of the land he once ruled but by people from the other side of the border" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 206, folio not stated); it is now rare, he adds, "that one runs into a Sikh in this city, a city which was once ruled by a Sikh and the seat of their political strength" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 196, folio not stated).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The date of death.** The site's account gives 27 June 1839, and Khalid gives his years as 1780–1839 (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 42, folio not stated). Kanhaiya Lal gives the year as "۱۸۹۶[OCR?]" with no era and an uncertain month (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 217, folio 220), and elsewhere as "سن ۱۸۹۶ بکرمی" (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 113, folio 116); he also gives 1896 Bikrami as the year of a severe illness (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 112, folio 115). Lahori's tazkira gives no year.
- **The day of the anniversary.** The site's account gives the death as 27 June and has pilgrims gather around the death anniversary. Khalid attended the death anniversary on "29th June 2011" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 206, folio not stated), and calls it twelve days after the Guru Arjan commemoration, which he dates to "6th June 2011" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 196, folio not stated).
- **The taking of Lahore.** The site's account has him found and rule the empire from 1801. Chishti has Shah Zaman give him leave to take Lahore in "سترہ سو اٹھانویں", and he took it (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 579). Khalid has him march into Lahore in 1799 and be crowned in 1801 (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 207, folio not stated).
- **Who died on the pyre.** The site's account has four queens and seven attendants. Kanhaiya Lal has eleven *ranis* (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 219, folio 222). Khalid has "eleven women", "his wives and concubines" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 210, folio not stated). Kanhaiya Lal and Khalid add two pigeons.
- **What marks his remains.** The site's account has a lotus-shaped marble urn holding the ashes. Kanhaiya Lal has a stone samadh, "سمادہ سنگین", at the centre (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 219, folio 222). Chishti has a square memorial under a covering beneath the gilded dome (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 573). Khalid has a palanquin on a raised platform that marks the spot where the last remains "were buried" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 209, folio not stated).
- **Where he was cremated.** The site's account and Kanhaiya Lal put the samadhi on the spot of the cremation (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 217, folio 220). Khalid writes that "His cremation took place in the ground accompanying the building" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 209, folio not stated).
- **The images at the door.** The site's account has red sandstone doorways carved with Ganesha, Devi and Brahma. Kanhaiya Lal has three stone images over the door, of Shiv ji, Ganesh ji and a third whose name is uncertain in the printed text (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 217, folio 220). Chishti has a marble "مورت گنین[OCR?]" on the east, Hanuman in the southern niche and a marble Devi within (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 570; Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 572). Khalid sees Krishna, Ganesh, Ram and Sita among the paintings (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 209, folio not stated).
- **The pavilion and its dome.** The site's account has a marble pavilion inlaid with pietra dura. Kanhaiya Lal's marble bungalow has marble columns "مگر اوپر کا گنبد خشتی ہے", a brick dome (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 219, folio 222). Khalid's palanquin has pillars of white marble (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 206, folio not stated).
- **The great dome.** The site's account has gilded fluted domes. Kanhaiya Lal has the great dome's ceiling worked with mirrors and gilding (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 219–220, folios 222–223); Chishti a gilded dome set with mirrors (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 573); Khalid a dome "decorated with coloured glass" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 209, folio not stated).
- **How many storeys.** Chishti calls the brick building "دو منزلہ" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 572). Kanhaiya Lal describes a second storey, a roofed terrace and a further *baradari* above the great dome (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 220, folio 223; Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 221, folio 224). Khalid's building "stands four floors from the ground" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 205, folio not stated).
- **Where it stands beside Dera Sahib.** Khalid has the samadhi facing Dera Sahib across a lawn (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 201, folio not stated), and four pages later "Just behind the samadhi of Guru Arjan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 205, folio not stated). Kanhaiya Lal puts its wall to the west of Guru Arjan's samadh (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 205–206, folios 208–209).
- **The Hazuri Bagh pavilion.** Chishti says it was built from the marble of Zeb-un-Nisa's tomb (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 637). Khalid reports it as said that it was part of the structure of Jahangir's tomb (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 207, folio not stated).
- **Spellings.** Chishti prints it "رنجیت سنگھ" and, in several places, in forms uncertain in the printed text such as "مہاراجہ رحمت[OCR?] سکہ[OCR?]" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 585) and "مہارا حد رنجیت سنگہ" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 646). Lahori's tazkira prints his mother-in-law both "سدا کور" and "سدا کنور" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 258).
- **His temper toward other faiths.** Khalid calls him, "Like the third Mughal Emperor Akbar", a secular ruler (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 207, folio not stated). Chishti attributes his stripping of Mughal tombs to religious zeal, "بباعث تعصب مذہبی" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 855), and praises the Wazir Khan endowment "با وجود دیگر[OCR?] انکے تعصب[OCR?] مذہبی" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 747–748). Kanhaiya Lal both blames the Sikhs for the city's ruin, "سکھوں نے لوٹ کر [illegible] برباد کر دیا" (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 22, folio 17), and makes Ranjit Singh the restorer whose accession woke the city's fortune (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 32, folio 35).

## Legacy

The samadhi is one of the most visited symbols of Sikh heritage remaining in Pakistan, and the monument is listed by the Punjab Directorate General of Archaeology among the province's protected religious monuments of the Sikh period.

The books consulted leave a double portrait of the man it commemorates. To Kanhaiya Lal he was the ruler under whom Lahore revived (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 32, folio 35), though the same book holds the Sikhs responsible for the city's earlier ruin (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 22, folio 17). To Chishti he was at once the patron of Muslim shrines and the man who carried the marble of Mughal tombs to Amritsar. Lahori's tazkira remembers him through the faqirs he could not buy: the majzub who refused his thousands, the majzuba who abused him and whom he would not leave, and the faqir who told him to send for sandalwood. Khalid found his memory kept on his anniversary by pilgrims from across the border, at a samadhi where the Akhand Path is read in his honour and the *langar* served afterwards (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 206, folio not stated; Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 210–211, folio not stated).
