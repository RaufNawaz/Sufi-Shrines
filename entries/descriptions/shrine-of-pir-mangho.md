## Overview

On the northern outskirts of Karachi lies Mangho Pir, one of the most unusual and atmospheric shrines in Pakistan, famous for three things that meet nowhere else: a medieval Sufi saint, a pool of sacred crocodiles, and hot sulphur springs believed to heal. The darbar of Pir Mangho (Hazrat Haji Syed Sakhi Sultan) is a centre of pilgrimage of great antiquity, and it is bound up with the culture of Karachi’s Sheedi community, the Afro-Pakistani descendants of East Africa, whose annual festival at the shrine is one of the city’s most distinctive traditions.

The books consulted here name the shrine, but in other spellings, and none of them gives the name Haji Syed Sakhi Sultan. Jürgen Schaflechner's study of the Hinglaj pilgrimage speaks of "the shrine of Magho Pir, which is now located in the northern part of Karachi" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 78, folio not stated), of "the dargāh (Urd.) of the Sufi saint Mango Pir, in northern Karachi" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 211, folio not stated), and of "the shrine of Mango Pir in Karachi" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 263, folio not stated); he gives no saint's name, no dates, no Sufi order, no custodian, no *ʿurs*, no ritual and no crocodiles. Shemeem Burney Abbas, writing of the women musicians of Sindh and of its Sidi community, calls it "Manghopir" and "The Mangopir shrine", and dedicates it to a different saint, Bava Gor (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 200, folio not stated). Annemarie Schimmel names "Mango Pir near Karachi" once, in a short parenthesis (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 238, folio 207). Michel Boivin uses the name "Mangho Pir" for the locality, in connection with a leper asylum, not the shrine (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 104, folio 91). Each of these identifications rests on the name and the place; none of the books says more about the saint than is set out below.

## The Saint

The saint of Mangho Pir is remembered as a holy man of the medieval centuries who settled at this spot in the hills above Karachi. The site's account names him Pir Mangho, Hazrat Haji Syed Sakhi Sultan. The books consulted here record no year of birth or death for him, no teacher and no Sufi order.

Abbas gives the shrine a different dedication: "The Mangopir shrine is associated with crocodiles and is dedicated to Bava Gor, the African Muslim saint" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 200, folio not stated). On the same page she places Bava Gor's shrine in India: "Bava Gor is the Abyssinian saint of the Sidi in Gujrat in India, where a shrine is dedicated to him, his brother Bava Habash, and his sister Mai Misra" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 200, folio not stated). She does not reconcile the two statements. Elsewhere she records that "In Karachi there is also a memorial shrine, and everywhere that Bava Gor stopped on his journey a shrine was created and each time one of the Sidis from his army remained in the place" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 73, folio not stated); she does not name that Karachi memorial shrine, and the sentence speaks of the city, not of Mangho Pir.

To Abbas's informant Qalandar Shah, the saint of the place is known by what lives in his pool: "Manghopir is the pir of the crocodiles" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 70, folio not stated).

Schaflechner names no saint of his own. He notes that an earlier writer whom he cites as Hart (Hart 1839) called the saint "Peer Moogha" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 263, folio not stated), and he raises, as a possibility, an older association of the shrine with Lalu Jasraj, a figure of the Kapri narratives he recorded (see Legends and Miracles below).

## The Shrine

Below the tomb lies a pond inhabited by crocodiles, held sacred and cared for by the shrine’s attendants; the eldest and largest is honoured with the title *Mor Sahib*, and pilgrims make offerings of meat, for the crocodiles are believed to be under the saint’s protection and their favour a sign of blessing. Abbas, too, describes the shrine as one that "is associated with crocodiles" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 200, folio not stated).

Nearby, hot sulphur springs rise from the earth, and bathing in their waters is believed to cure skin ailments and other diseases, so that the shrine is a place of healing as much as of prayer. Schaflechner mentions the springs as a natural phenomenon. In a comparison with Chandrakup, he writes: "Other examples of similar thermic phenomena also exist on this anticline, such as at the dargāh (Urd.) of the Sufi saint Mango Pir, in northern Karachi (Snead 1964, 549)." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 211, folio not stated) Elsewhere he speaks of "a hermitage near the hot springs" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 263, folio not stated).

The site's account places the shrine on the northern outskirts of Karachi, in the hills above the city; Schaflechner says only that it is "now located in the northern part of Karachi" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 78, folio not stated). The books consulted here do not describe the tomb or its buildings, and they record no founder, builder, custodian or endowment.

## Devotion and Practice

Mangho Pir holds a special place for the Sheedi community of Karachi, the descendants of Africans brought to the subcontinent over the centuries, who regard the saint as their own and the shrine as a spiritual home. Their annual *Sheedi Mela* at Mangho Pir, with its drumming, dance, and offerings to the sacred crocodiles, is a vivid survival of African-rooted culture in Pakistan and one of the most striking festivals of Karachi, binding the shrine to a living community and its heritage.

Abbas's informants describe this gathering, which they place in April. Qalandar Shah, speaking to the singer Kubra, said of the Sidis (the book's spelling of Sheedi): "They have a big congregation in Karachi at Manghopir—in April—Manghopir is the pir of the crocodiles—they have a large conference and Sidis from all over come there—they get a big press coverage—Have you been there?" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 70, folio not stated). Kubra answered that the festival lay outside her own circle: "No, that is in Karachi—we sing at weddings" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 71, folio not stated). Abbas adds that "The women musicians Karima and Kubra also explained that the Sidis have a huge melā of another pir at Manghopir in Karachi", and that "Qalandar Shah had talked about the annual festival at this shrine in his interview" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 71, folio not stated). April is the only date the books give for the festival; they give no day and no year.

The site's account also speaks of an annual *ʿurs*. The books consulted here give no date for it.

## Legends and Miracles

Tradition weaves the crocodiles and the springs into the miracles of the saint himself.

Schimmel's *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* touches on such a miracle only in passing. In a passage on a wonder involving an alligator she adds, in a parenthesis of ten words and without a note, "(as happened in Mango Pir near Karachi)" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 238, folio 207). She names no saint and gives no date. Her index lists the place as "Mango Pir" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 520, folio 489).

Schaflechner offers a conjecture of his own, and hedges it each time he makes it. "It seems rather likely that the shrine of Magho Pir, which is now located in the northern part of Karachi, was once associated with Lalu Jasraj (Burton 1851, 326)." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 78, folio not stated) In the narratives of the Kapri community that he recorded, Lalu Jasraj is "Rama's aguā (Hin. guide)" and "the founder of the Kapris" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 74, 78, folios not stated), and elsewhere "the chaṛīdār (Hin. stickbearer) of Rama and his entourage" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 209, folio not stated). In a note he sets out his reasoning: "Hart's description allows for an association of the mythological figure Lalu Jasraj with the shrine of Mango Pir in Karachi. Notwithstanding the fact that Hart calls the saint 'Peer Moogha,' knowing that it is said that Lalu Jasraj stayed at a hermitage near the hot springs makes the association possible (Hart 1839, 80)." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 263, folio not stated) The association is his suggestion, not a statement of fact. He is also cautious about how widely the Lalu Jasraj stories are known: "I came across this narrative mainly during my conversations with Dayaram Kapri from Gujarat… who claimed that this reference is found in the Hiṃgulā Purāṇ. Unfortunately, I was not able to locate the story there, and only a few of my other informants in Pakistan mentioned this myth about Liari, so it is questionable how widespread it is outside the Gujarati Kapri community." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 209, folio not stated)

## In Verse

The books consulted here quote no verse by the saint of Mangho Pir or about the shrine, and record no hymn, chronogram or inscription from it.

## Setting

The books' wider accounts of Karachi, of Sindh and of the Sidi community speak of the city, the province and the people, not of this shrine, but they help to place it.

**The Sidi community.** Abbas's glossary gives "Sidi Also known as Sheedi or Syedi in Sind and Gujrat" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 188, folio not stated). From her fieldwork among the Sidi she writes that they "were initially brought as slaves from Africa to work in the subcontinent of Pakistan and India. Although they are now free, some of them are attached to the feudal households in the Sind province of Pakistan. Their native language is Sindhi" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 67–68, folios not stated). Qalandar Shah told her that "the Africans are present in the Sind in large numbers—because of the slave trade—they were brought here in large numbers—and then—with the abolition of slavery—here too they were set free", and that "They had been coming continuously—through the ages" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 69, folio not stated). She records that "the Sidis in Sind and Kacch claim descent from Hazrat Bilal" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 167, folio not stated).

**Sidi music and ritual.** The drumming of the Sheedi festival has its context in what Abbas writes of the Sidi drum, the *mugarmān*, "held sacred by the Sidi communities in Sind, Baluchistan, Rajasthan, and Gujrat who use them in their shrines and for rituals" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 185, folio not stated). "In Sind," she writes, "the Sidis of African descent and the Manganhar musicians render the ceremonial singing of devotional narratives" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 169, folio not stated), and "Sidi women render ritual musical services in the households in the Sind" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 167, folio not stated).

**Karachi and its shrines.** Regula Burckhardt Qureshi names Karachi as one of "two important urban centres of Sufism", with Hyderabad in India, "where members of the same Qawwal Bachche lineage actively maintain their special Qawwali heritage" (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 33, folio 13). Omar Kasmani describes the city's shrines as a stage in the fakirs' round: after the three-day annual fair of Sehwan, fakirs "follow other fakirs and proceed to shrines in Karachi" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 114, folio 97). Haroon Khalid gives a legend of the city's name: the area was "known as the area of [Mai] Kolachi", after an old woman "known as Mai Kolachi" whose son "was killed [by] a fish"; she "tore apart the fish and retrieved" him, and the city "came to be known as Kolachi" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 225, folio 199). "At the time of Partition", he writes, "Karachi was a minor port city", and migrants from India made it "the largest city of Pakistan" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 226, folio 200).

**The locality and the road to Hinglaj.** Boivin mentions the locality of Mangho Pir once, for a charitable institution: "Among these were the leper asylum in Mangho Pir and an orphanage in Sukkur." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 104, folio 91) Schaflechner, whose conjecture about Lalu Jasraj ties the shrine to the Hinglaj pilgrimage, describes Karachi as the start of the road route to Hinglaj, "about 240 kilometers long" from Narayanpur (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 158, folio not stated), and writes that "The ongoing growth of Karachi and the corresponding urbanization of the areas where the early stops occurred caused all but a few of the resting places to completely disappear." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 121, folio not stated)

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **To whom the shrine is dedicated.** The site's account names the saint Pir Mangho, Hazrat Haji Syed Sakhi Sultan, a medieval holy man who settled at the spot. Abbas writes that "The Mangopir shrine is associated with crocodiles and is dedicated to Bava Gor, the African Muslim saint" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 200, folio not stated). Schaflechner names no saint, reports that Hart called him "Peer Moogha", and suggests that the shrine "was once associated with Lalu Jasraj" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 78, folio not stated; Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 263, folio not stated).
- **Where Bava Gor's shrine is.** On the same page on which she dedicates the Mangopir shrine to Bava Gor, Abbas writes that "Bava Gor is the Abyssinian saint of the Sidi in Gujrat in India, where a shrine is dedicated to him, his brother Bava Habash, and his sister Mai Misra" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 200, folio not stated).
- **The name of the place.** The site's account writes Pir Mangho and Mangho Pir. Schaflechner prints "Magho Pir" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 78, folio not stated) and "Mango Pir" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 211, folio not stated); Abbas prints "Manghopir" and "Mangopir" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 70, folio not stated; Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 200, folio not stated); Schimmel prints "Mango Pir" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 238, folio 207); Boivin prints "Mangho Pir" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 104, folio 91).

## Legacy

Pir Mangho is unlike any other shrine in Pakistan: a place where Sufi devotion, a pool of sacred crocodiles, healing sulphur springs, and the African-descended culture of the Sheedis come together. Ancient, strange, and beloved, the darbar remains a centre of pilgrimage, healing, and festival on the edge of the great city, and a monument to the many strands that weave through the popular religion of Sindh. The books that mention it carry those strands further: a dedication to Bava Gor, "the African Muslim saint", in Abbas's account, and in Schaflechner's a possible link to the Hindu pilgrimage to Hinglaj.
