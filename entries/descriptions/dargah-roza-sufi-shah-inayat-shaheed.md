## Overview

At Jhok Sharif, in the Sujawal region of Sindh, lies the shrine of Sufi Shah Inayat Shaheed (d. 1718) — a Sufi saint remembered not only for his mysticism but for a remarkable social experiment and a martyr's death. Shah Inayat gathered around him a community that farmed the land in common under the principle that the one who sows should eat, and this challenge to the entrenched order of landlords and rulers led, after a long siege, to his execution. He is honoured today as a saint, a martyr, and — in modern eyes — a pioneer of social justice, and his darbar at Jhok is a place of pilgrimage and remembrance.

The books bear out the outline of that story from several sides. Annemarie Schimmel, who devoted a study of her own to him, tells of his preaching, the denunciation at court, the siege of his *khanqah* and his execution "in January 1718" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 25, folio 21), and records that "Large armies were sent to Jhok, which surrendered after four months' siege" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 428, folio 397). Christopher Shackle, translating the *Risalo* of Shah Abdul Latif, calls him "the famous Sindhi" Sufi "martyr Shah Inayat of Jhok (d. 1718)" and finds his Persian verse quoted in that great Sindhi classic (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 325). Michel Boivin, writing of the Hindu Sufis of Sindh and their descendants in India, places "Sufi Shah Inayat's dargah" at Jhok Sharif and shows the dargah as a living centre of initiation, custodianship and song, finding that "most of the Hindu Sufis were in touch with Jhok, directly or indirectly, often through their own Sufi Master" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 219, folio 206; p. 60, folio 47).

## The Life of the Saint

Shah Inayat belonged to the tradition of *wahdat al-wujud*, the mysticism of the unity of being, and was schooled in the Sufi currents of his age, travelling in his search for knowledge before returning to his native Jhok in lower Sindh. There, in the early eighteenth century, he established a *khanqah* whose community held and cultivated land collectively, sharing its produce — a way of life summed up in the Sindhi maxim *jeko kheré, so khaé*, "whoever sows, eats." The gathering of the poor and the landless around him, and the implicit challenge his commune posed to the great landholders (*zamindars*) and to the Mughal-appointed authorities of the region, provoked their hostility. His settlement was besieged; after a long resistance he was taken by treachery and put to death in 1718. He is remembered ever after as *Shaheed*, the martyr of Jhok.

The books know him by several forms of the name. Schimmel writes "Shah Inayat of Jhok" and calls his establishment "his Khanqah Jhok" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 25, folio 21); the index of her *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* enters him, with the epithet that the site's name also carries, as Shah ʿInayat Shahid, "(d. 1718)" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 517, folio 486). Boivin writes "Sufi Shah Inayat" and "Shah Inayat of Jhok Sharif" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 219, folio 206; p. 124, folio 111), and one of Shemeem Burney Abbas's informants speaks of "Shah Inayat of Jhokevale", the Shah Inayat of the people of Jhok (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 63, folio not stated). Schimmel dates him in the title of her article on him as "A Sindhi Mystic of the early 18th century" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 293, folio 293).

Of his own spiritual descent the books say little. Boivin names his master: "Sayyid Shah Mehmud Shah al-Qadiri was the Master of Shah Inayat of Jhok Sharif." That master, he writes, was "a Sufi Master from Jhok Sharif" who had been buried in Bijapur, in Karnataka (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 124, folio 111). Boivin gives no birth or death date and no chain of succession for Shah Inayat himself, though he repeatedly ties the lodge at Jhok to the Qadiri order through its *sajjada nashins*, and the chart of Sufi orders in an annex to his book sets down "Jhok Sharif (Qadiriyya)" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 227, folio 214).

Boivin also counts him in a short list of masters, the "'three great Sufi teachers'", who are "Shah Inayat, Shah Abd al-Latif and Sachal Sarmast" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 49, folio 36). The name stands there without a place, and the company it keeps is the only sign that this Shah Inayat is meant. Boivin keeps him apart from an earlier poet with a similar name, "Shah Inat, a Sindhi Sufi poet of the seventeenth century", whom he dates 1623–1712 and calls Sayyid Inayat Allah Rizvi (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 105, folio 92; p. 211, folio 198); the nearness of that man's death year to 1718 does not make the two one.

## The Commune, the Siege and the Martyrdom

Schimmel's *Pain and Grace* gives the fullest account of his end among the books, and sets it in the politics of the province. "The first obvious sign of the struggle for power in the Lower Indus Valley becomes visible in 1717, when a mystical rebel was persecuted under the pretext that he tried to overthrow the established rule." She goes on: "The tragic story of Shah Inayat of Jhok, whose successful mystical preaching attracted many disciples from the mystical centers of the country, but also laborers from the neighboring big farms led his adversaries to denounce him at court; for during the confused situation in Delhi between the reign of Farrukh Siyar and that of Muhammad Shah it was easy to sow mistrust" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 25, folio 21). Two streams of followers thus came to him, adepts from the established centres and labourers from the great farms, and it was their coming, in her telling, that turned his enemies against him.

The siege followed. "The Kalhora clan from Upper Sind, particularly active in the struggle against Inayat, soon afterwards became independent in Sind. Shah Inayat was besieged in his Khanqah Jhok, and had to surrender to the overwhelming army that came from various places in Sind and Hind. He was executed in January 1718" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 25, folio 21). Later in the same book she gives the date again in a parenthesis: "Shah Inayat of Jhok (exec. 1718)" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 228, folio 228).

In *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* Schimmel tells the story a second time, placing Jhok as "not far from Hyderabad" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 428, folio 397). There the dargah draws followers away from its neighbours: "His way of life, spirituality, and justice attracted a large number, not only of Sufi adepts, but also, according to the sources, of slaves and husbandmen from the neighboring villages." "The Sufi sheikhs and the feudal lords in the country lost large numbers of disciples and workers, whereas the dargah in Jhok flourished." She records that "A considerable amount of land was distributed among" Shah Inayat's followers "after an unjustified attack on Jhok, led by the Sayyids of Bulrri" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 428, folio 397); she does not say who those Sayyids were beyond the name of their place, printed so.

Then came the charge and the end: "the pious mystic was accused of conspiracy against the Mogul throne, the easiest method of blackmail, since the throne was in constant danger after Aurangzeb's death. Large armies were sent to Jhok, which surrendered after four months' siege", and Shah Inayat "was executed in January 1718" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 428, folio 397). In *As Through a Veil* she writes that he was "called the first social reformer in Sindhi Islam, who was executed by the Moghul governor in early 1718" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 175, folio 157); the governor is not named. Shackle gives the same year, "(d. 1718)", and calls him "the martyred" Sufi saint (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 325; p. 321).

Schimmel is careful about how much of the commune can be known. To what extent Shah Inayat "really aimed at something like a land reform in the modern sense of the word cannot be detected from the sources, which are mostly unfriendly to him; positive evaluations, on the other hand, are strongly mixed with legends" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 428, folio 397). She places his case among others in which mystical leaders of the lower Indus valley took a political part: "This was not the first time that mystical leaders in Sind, or in other parts of the Muslim world, were engaged in politics or in social reforms" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 428, folio 397).

## The Shrine

The shrine of Sufi Shah Inayat at Jhok Sharif is a revered darbar of lower Sindh, drawing pilgrims who honour the saint and martyr. The books say little of its fabric: none of them describes the tomb, its building or its date. Boivin's glossary gives the place its most precise location: "Jhok Sharif: a village with an important Sufi Centre in Taluqa Mirpur Bathoro in Southern Sindh where Sufi Shah Inayat's dargah is." In the body of his book Jhok is "a great Sufi centre located in the extreme South of Sindh", and "Jhok, contrary to Daraza, is located in Southern Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 219, folio 206; p. 141, folio 128; p. 60, folio 47). Schimmel names "the dargah in Jhok" as an institution that drew disciples and workers away from the neighbouring sheikhs and lords (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 428, folio 397).

The custodians of the dargah are the *sajjada nashins*, the holders of the seat, and Boivin names several. A disciple "spent many years in Jhok Sharif, and he was initiated to the Qadiri tariqa by the sajjada nashin, Sufi Abd al-Sattar Shah", who set him to "cleaning the stable of his Master's mare" and, when he had reached "the highest spiritual degree, that of salvation", allowed him to give *nam*, the initiatory name, to disciples of his own (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 120, folio 107; pp. 120–121, folios 107–108). Elsewhere he writes of "Sufi Hazrat Abdul Sattar Shah of Jhok Sharif" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 101, folio 88), and his glossary makes the name a recurring one: "Abdul Sattar: the name of several Sufi masters at the dargah of Jhok Sharif." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 218, folio not stated). He also cites "the official website of Sufi Ataullah Sattari, the sajjada nashin of Jhok" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 128, folio 115). He gives no dates for any of them.

Jhok is, for Boivin, one of three seats in Sindh whose holders still keep the lineages of Hindu Sufis now in India: "They are the sajjada nashin of Daraza, of Tando Jahaniya and of Jhok Sharif. At each of them, a great Sufi poet is attached: Sachal Sarmast, Qutub Ali Shah and Nasir Faqir" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 27, folio 14). The poet who stands third in the list, against Jhok Sharif, is Nasir Faqir, though the book does not pair the two lists in so many words; Boivin elsewhere calls Nasir Muhammad Faqir "a Qadiri who was the disciple of the Master (murshid) of Jhok Sharif" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 141, folio 128), without naming that master.

The dargah's reach crosses the border. Boivin reports that "the official website devoted to Jhok Sharif claims to have a dargah in Bijapur which is affiliated with Jhok", and that under the heading 'affiliated dargahs' it names a Hazrat Soofi Abdul Malik; he adds at once that "I do not know what the relations were between Molchand and the sajjada nashin of Jhok" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 124, folio 111). Boivin tells of Molchand, who after Partition "was worried that it was difficult for Hindu Sindhi Sufis to pay a visit to the Sufi places in Sindh, now in Pakistan", and of the search for the Bijapur grave of Shah Inayat's master: "he decided to look for his grave" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 124, folio 111). Of the pilgrimage that grew from the claimed tie to Jhok, Boivin judges that "this attempt at creating a Muslim Sufi pilgrimage from Sindh in India is unique, but, unfortunately, it does not look to be very successful." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 128, folio 115).

## Devotion and Practice

The shrine's annual *ʿurs* gathers devotees for prayer, Sufi music, and remembrance. Of one Sindhi Sufi poet Boivin writes: "His Sufi poetry in Sindhi is voluminous and his verses (kafis) are still a part of the main poetry, which is sung in the urs of Jhok Sharif" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 59, folio 46). He gives no date, month or length for the *ʿurs*. Other Sindhi poetry is sung at Jhok too: the *kalam* of Dalpat Sufi is sung "in several dargahs located in Sindh and run by Muslim sajjada nashins, as for example in Jhok Sharif", and "It is also sung at Jhok Sharif in Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 118, folio 105).

Jhok's calendar travelled with its followers. The yearly *melo* at Bijapur is kept "every year from the 17th to the 19th of Moharram, as it is celebrated in Jhok Sharif" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 124, folio 111); Boivin does not say whether those days at Jhok are the *ʿurs* or whose memory they keep. One Hindu Sufi path counts Shah Inayat among the four masters whose death anniversaries (*versi*) it keeps: one of the four is "Shah Inayat (from Jhok Sharif)", and "the dates… are taken as sacred: the 11, the 15, the 17 and the 27" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 122, folio 109). The list of dates is not matched to the list of names, and nothing in the book says which day is Shah Inayat's.

A performance tradition carries his name. Naseer Mirza, speaking to Abbas of Sindhi poetry, says: "The sūng—the manānā—is done on the capṛi [castanets]—this is the tradition of Shah Inayat of Jhokevale—but we do not find this in Shah Latif's poetry", and, of Shah Latif, "his moods are more mellow" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 63, folio not stated).

Omar Kasmani, writing of the Sindhi publications of the state, which until the late 1960s hardly mentioned the saint or the shrine of Sehwan, records that "other saints of Sindh (especially Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai, Sachal Sarmast, and Shah Inayat) were regularly featured during this time, and their annual fairs were marked by special editions" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 70, folio 53). He names no place for this Shah Inayat; only the phrase "saints of Sindh" and his companions in the list point to the saint of Jhok.

Jhok was a place of initiation for many. Boivin writes that "It was at about the age of forty that Rakhiyyal Shah adopted the Sufi path and went to the dargah of Jhok Sharif, where he stayed under the guidance of his Master" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 59, folio 46), the master unnamed. Rohal Faqir "was a disciple of Shah Inayat from Jhok Sharif" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 107, folio 94); the guru Asardas "was himself a follower of the Sufi masters of Jhok Sharif", and Dalpat Sufi was "Himself a follower of Jhok Sharif" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 118, folio 105). Bedil, Boivin notes, "also visited Jhok Sharif and Daraza" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 97, folio 84). "It is interesting to observe," he concludes, "that the Sufi centre of Jhok Sharif plays a pivotal role since most of the Hindu Sufis were in touch with Jhok, directly or indirectly, often through their own Sufi Master", and "the two main Sufi traditions that were very popular among the Hindus were Daraza and Jhok." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 60, folio 47).

## Legends and Miracles

Schimmel warns that the favourable accounts of Shah Inayat "are strongly mixed with legends" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 428, folio 397), but the books themselves record few. The one vision they tell is of Jhok rather than of its saint: a disciple, as the published *kalam* of his own path relates, "from his early childhood on, he had visions involving Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani. In one of them, the founder of the Qadiriyya told him it was time for him to reach Jhok Sharif, to take nam from the Sufi Master" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 120, folio 107).

## In Verse

Shah Inayat wrote in Persian, and his verse is quoted in the *Risalo* of Shah Abdul Latif. Shackle's notes to his translation trace two Persian lines in it to him. The half-verse *bar khez ba-dih sāqī* is, he writes, "a half-verse from a ghazal by the martyred" Sufi saint Shah Inayat of Jhok, and he points the reader to "another quotation from this poet" elsewhere in the poem (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 321). The second is *sar dar qadam-e yār fdā shud chi ba-jā shud* (printed so, with *fdā* for *fidā*), "a verse from a poem by the famous Sindhi" Sufi "martyr Shah Inayat of Jhok (d. 1718)"; it is, Shackle adds, "one of the very few Persian quotations in the Risālo, but compare 2.49" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 325).

Schimmel, too, notes that Shah Abdul Latif "has quoted a Persian line usually connected with Shah Inayat", and that "It is possible that Shah Abdul Latif alluded to his fate in some of his verses" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 26, folio 22). She gives that line in English, citing it as Sohn. II:

*The head is sacrificed at the Friend's foot, / which is in order* (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 228, folio 228)

Schimmel does not print the Persian of this line, and neither book refers to the other, so whether her English renders the line Shackle transliterates is not stated; the two stand side by side here as each book gives them.

Another Persian verse was tied to his martyrdom by a Sindhi writer, Qani. Schimmel discusses a quatrain on the 'kitchen of love' attributed to Sarmad, "one of the Sufis of 'Hallajian succession'", though it is, she says, "much older"; and "It is typical that this very verse has been quoted by Qani", in his *Maqalat ash-shuʿara*, "in connection with the martyr-mystic Shah Inayat of Jhok" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, pp. 182–183, folios 182–183, footnote 41).

Verse about him followed his death. Schimmel writes that "his fate has inspired many Sufi poets, Hindus and Muslims alike, to write touching poetry"; "he becomes 'the Hallaj of Sind' due to his death at the hands of the establishment, and to his overwhelming mystical 'state.'" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, pp. 25–26, folios 21–22). Again: "Both Muslim and Hindu admirers have composed songs extolling him or describing his martyrdom"; among them was the Suhrawardi mystic Janullah of Rohri, "one of the outstanding Persian poets of Sind from the second half of the eighteenth century" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 428, folio 397).

The Sindhi poet Sachal Sarmast names him too. "Sachal also speaks of" Shah Inayat of Jhok, Schimmel writes (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 175, folio 157), and her translation of Sachal's litany of the afflicted includes the words "hast killed" ʿInayat "in the fighting arena"; her footnote identifies him as 'the martyr-mystic of Jhok' (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 426, folio 395).

At Jhok itself, one of the holders of the seat spoke in the same language of unity. Boivin reports that "Sufi Hazrat Abdul Sattar Shah of Jhok Sharif, speaks of jam wahdat jo, 'the cup of unity'", citing a work of Nasir Faqir of 1972 (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 101, folio 88).

## Setting

The epithet 'the Hallaj of Sind' draws on a long memory in the province. Schimmel writes that Hallaj traversed Sind "shortly after 900", and that "It is moving to listen to illiterate folk musicians in remote corners of Sind" who repeat his name in ecstatic songs, and "claim that he was the true lover who paid with his life for his love" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 175, folio 157). Boivin agrees: "In Sindh, Hallaj is a very popular figure, and many Sufi poets mention his name in their verses" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 102, folio 89). Of Sindhi poets at large, and not of Jhok, Schimmel adds that Hindu writers composed poems in the imagery of the Muslims and "devoted ballads to the fate of famous Sufi martyrs" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 418, folio 387).

The Sind in which he preached was a Mughal province in a time of change. Schimmel writes that "Sind was, since 1595, part of the Mughal Empire, and although representatives of the Delhi court came there, the indigenous rulers enjoyed much freedom" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 25, folio not stated). H. T. Sorley writes that "Sind was added to the Moghul Empire by Akbar in 1592" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 18, folio 9), and dates the rule of the Kalhora as governors from 1701 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 35, folio 26). Of the province's social order in that century Sorley writes that "The system of land tenure in Sind, so far as is known, has always been feudal", and that the Kalhora's deference to the priestly class "led to the extraordinary reverence for pirs, Sayids and holy men, which may be said to have been the predominant characteristic of eighteenth century Sind" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 163, folio 154); "The social religion of Sind was built up round the reverence of the murid for the murshid" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 166, folio 157). In southern Sind, Schimmel notes, a strong Naqshbandi influence at the beginning of the eighteenth century "changed the spiritual climate of southern Sind to a certain extent" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 428, folio 397). These passages speak of the province, not of Jhok.

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **Where Jhok lies.** The site's account places Jhok Sharif in the Sujawal region of Sindh, in lower Sindh. Boivin puts it in "Taluqa Mirpur Bathoro in Southern Sindh" and "in the extreme South of Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 219, folio 206; p. 141, folio 128). Schimmel says it "is not far from Hyderabad" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 428, folio 397).
- **Who came to him.** The site's account speaks of the poor and the landless. Schimmel's *Pain and Grace* names two streams, "many disciples from the mystical centers of the country, but also laborers from the neighboring big farms" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 25, folio 21); her *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* names Sufi adepts and "slaves and husbandmen from the neighboring villages" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 428, folio 397).
- **The land.** The site's account has the community hold and cultivate land collectively, under the maxim *jeko kheré, so khaé*. Schimmel records that land "was distributed among" his followers after an attack on Jhok, and holds that how far he aimed at a land reform "cannot be detected from the sources, which are mostly unfriendly to him" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 428, folio 397). She also observes that progressive writers tend to read figures such as Hallaj, Bishr al-Hafi and Shah Inayat of Jhok 'in the light of' modern social ideals (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 288, folio 270).
- **How the end came.** The site's account says that after a long resistance he was taken by treachery and put to death. *Pain and Grace* says that he "had to surrender to the overwhelming army that came from various places in Sind and Hind", and names the Kalhora as "particularly active in the struggle against Inayat" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 25, folio 21). *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* says that Jhok "surrendered after four months' siege", after he was accused of conspiracy against the Mughal throne (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 428, folio 397). *As Through a Veil* says he "was executed by the Moghul governor" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 175, folio 157).
- **The date.** The site's account gives 1718. Schimmel dates the persecution to 1717 and the execution to "January 1718" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 25, folio 21), to "January 1718" again (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 428, folio 397), and to "early 1718" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 175, folio 157); Shackle gives "(d. 1718)" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 325). The year agrees throughout; only the start of the persecution is set a year earlier.
- **The holder of the seat.** Boivin names the *sajjada nashin* who initiated a disciple as "Sufi Abd al-Sattar Shah" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 120, folio 107) and elsewhere names "Sufi Ataullah Sattari, the sajjada nashin of Jhok" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 128, folio 115); his glossary calls Abdul Sattar "the name of several Sufi masters at the dargah of Jhok Sharif" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 218, folio not stated). He gives no dates, and does not say whether these are one man or several.
- **The Bijapur dargah.** "the official website of Sufi Ataullah Sattari, the sajjada nashin of Jhok" claims Abd al-Malik's dargah in Bijapur as affiliated to Jhok, while "in the narrative found in Molchand's biography, there is no mention that Abd al-Malik's dargah in Bijapur is affiliated to Jhok Sharif" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 128, folio 115).
- **Boivin's article on Jhok.** Boivin's own bibliography lists his chapter 'The Sufi Centre of Jhok Sharif in Sindh (Pakistan)' as published at New York by Continuum in 2012, pages 94–109 (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 233, folio 220); Kasmani cites the same title from London, Continuum, pages 95–110, its first word damaged in the printed text (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 204, folio 187).
- **When Sind became Mughal.** Schimmel gives 1595 (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 25, folio not stated); Sorley gives 1592, under Akbar (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 18, folio 9).

## Legacy

Sufi Shah Inayat Shaheed holds a singular place among the saints of Sindh — a mystic of the unity of being whose commune of shared land and labour, and whose martyrdom in its defence, made him a saint of the poor and a byword for social justice. In the modern age his memory has taken on a further dimension: writers, poets, and social reformers have celebrated him as an early martyr for the rights of the peasant and the poor against feudal power, so that his shrine is honoured both as a place of mystical devotion and as a symbol of the struggle for justice. His darbar at Jhok Sharif keeps alive the memory of a holy man who joined the inward path to the cause of the landless, and who paid for it with his life.

The books show that double memory at work. Schimmel reports that he was "called the first social reformer in Sindhi Islam" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 175, folio 157), and that "his fate has inspired many Sufi poets, Hindus and Muslims alike, to write touching poetry", so that "he becomes 'the Hallaj of Sind'" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, pp. 25–26, folios 21–22); she also cautions that modern readers interpret him in the light of their own social ideals (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 288, folio 270). She devoted an article to him, published in the *Liber Amicorum* in honour of C. J. Bleeker at Leiden in 1969, pp. 151–70 (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 338, folio 320; Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 493, folio 462). Boivin wrote his own study of the Sufi centre of Jhok Sharif (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 233, folio 220), and in his book Jhok stands at the heart of a network that outlived Partition: "Again, the invocation of Jhok Sharif contributes to the construction of a network of sacred territories which are all located in Sindh." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 141, folio 128).
