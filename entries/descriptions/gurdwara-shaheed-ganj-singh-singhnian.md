## Overview

Gurdwara Shaheed Ganj Singh Singhnian, in the Naulakha Bazaar quarter of the walled city of Lahore, is among the most solemn of Sikh memorials in Pakistan. Its name — *Shahid Ganj*, "treasury of martyrs", and *Singhnian*, "of the Sikh women" — commemorates the many Sikhs, and above all the women and children, who were tortured and killed here during the great persecutions of the mid-eighteenth century. It stands within the cluster of martyrdom sites at Naulakha, close to the shrine of Bhai Taru Singh, that together mark one of the darkest chapters of Sikh history.

None of the books consulted uses the full name with *Singhnian*. Two of them write of a place called Shahid Ganj outside Delhi Gate, and it is on the name, the place and the account of the killings that their passages are read here as concerning this gurdwara. Haroon Khalid's travelogue *Walking with Nanak* gives a long history of a "Gurdwara Shaheed Ganj" in Naulakha Bazaar, told to him by the historian Iqbal Qaiser in his own words, from a small mosque to a court battle lasting generations and a rebuilding finished in 2004 (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 76–77, folios 58–59; p. 78, folio 60). The name in his book lacks the word *Singhnian* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 77, folio 59). Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti's nineteenth-century survey of Lahore, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, describes a mosque used by the Sikhs outside Delhi Gate and a Sikh *samadh*, each called Shahid Ganj, and sets the tomb of Mir Mannu in the same quarter (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 121; p. 122). A third book, Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori's *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, has an entry under the same name for a quite different kind of place, a burial ground of Muslim martyrs, which nothing in the book ties to this gurdwara (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 264, folio 252).

## Mir Mannu and the Persecution

After the Mughal–Afghan struggles of the 1740s, *Muin-ul-Mulk* — the governor of Lahore from 1748, known to the Sikhs as *Mir Mannu* — set himself to the extermination of the Sikh community. Mobile columns hunted the Sikhs who had taken refuge in the forests and wastes, and captives were brought in numbers to Lahore. The men, refusing conversion, were put to death; the women and children were consigned to a prison where they were set to grinding heavy quotas of grain by hand, starved and brutalised. Sikh tradition preserves the memory of mothers made to watch the killing of their children, and of their steadfast refusal to abandon their faith in exchange for their lives. The traditional accounts speak of enormous numbers of dead; while such figures are impossible to verify and are best understood as expressions of collective memory, the historical reality of a sustained and savage persecution under Mir Mannu is well attested.

Iqbal Qaiser, telling Khalid the story, names the same governor: "Thousands of them, men, women and children alike were slaughtered and thrown into a well here on the orders of the Governor of Lahore, Moin-ul-Mulk also known as Meer Manu" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 77, folio 59). He adds that a room was built there to hold prisoners before their execution (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 77, folio 59).

Chishti, writing of the Shahid Ganj quarter, records that "علاقہ شہید گنج" holds the tomb of Mir Mannu, and that in his time there was a general order to seize and kill the Sikhs (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 121). On the same page he places Mir Mannu's rule in the reign of the emperor Muhammad Shah: "بعہد محمد شاہ یہہ نواب لاہور مین حاکم تہا", in Muhammad Shah's time this nawab was ruler in Lahore; the order came, he explains, because the Sikhs had grown in number (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 121). He knew a Panjabi saying about the governor, but its words are illegible in the printed text beyond its opening, "میر منو ساڈی[OCR?] [illegible] اسی[OCR?]" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 121).

## The Martyrdom of the Singhnian

It is the constancy of the women — the *Singhnis* — that gives this gurdwara its name and its particular sanctity. In the retelling that every Sikh child learns, the women endured imprisonment, forced labour and the murder of their infants without renouncing the *Khalsa*, and their courage transformed a place of suffering into a place of honour. The site came to be venerated as a *shahid ganj*, and after the consolidation of Sikh power in the later eighteenth century a gurdwara was raised to enshrine the memory of the martyrs.

The ground itself, in Qaiser's telling, still held the dead. When the gurdwara was being rebuilt, "skeletons of children and adults would emerge", and Qaiser identifies them as the Sikhs killed on Meer Manu's orders (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 78, folio 60).

Chishti gives the naming of a Shahid Ganj in his own terms. He describes "ایک مکان کلاں سمادہ[OCR?] سکھو[OCR?] کی", a large building, a *samadh* of the Sikhs, raised because a governor whom the printed text names "میر[OCR?] مسعود[OCR?]", and calls "قاتل[OCR?] قوم سکہو[OCR?]", the killer of the Sikh people, had put Sikhs to death on that spot; and, he writes, "نام اس سمادہ[OCR?] کا شہید گنج رکہا", the *samadh* was given the name Shahid Ganj (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 122). The reading of the governor's name is uncertain in the printed text.

## The Gurdwara and its Memory

The Naulakha martyrdom sites became, in the colonial and post-colonial periods, focal points of Sikh commemoration and, at times, of communal contest over ownership. The Shaheed Ganj Singhnian is maintained today under the Evacuee Trust Property Board and is visited by Sikh pilgrims to Lahore, who come to pay homage alongside the neighbouring shrine of Bhai Taru Singh. The gurdwara's austerity is fitting: it is less an architectural showpiece than a site of remembrance, where the recitation of the *Guru Granth Sahib* and the ardas (prayer) keep alive the names and the example of those who died.

## The Place and the Mosque

Qaiser first told Khalid the story "when we drove around the railway station" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 76, folio 58). He is emphatic about where it lies: "No, this is not the walled city of Lahore. This [is] outside Delhi gate. This area is known as Naulakha Bazaar" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 76, folio 58; p. 77, folio 59). Prince Dara Shikoh, he says, had his Naulakha palace there (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 76, folio 58; p. 77, folio 59).

Qaiser describes the quarrel over the site as "an old issue that started about two centuries" before his telling, the last word of the phrase damaged in the printed text (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 76–77, folios 58–59). On a vacant plot there stood "a small mosque" from which "the Qazi would announce punishment to criminals"; "The mosque was small and was never used as a place of worship" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 76–77, folios 58–59). Under Maharaja Ranjit Singh, he says, the Sikhs "placed the Guru Granth Sahib inside it, converting it into a gurdwara. The Muslims could not do anything then" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 76–77, folios 58–59).

Chishti's survey describes a similar arrangement in his own day. Near "سرائی میاں محمد سلطان[OCR?]" outside Delhi Gate, he writes, stood two mosques; the Sikhs used one of them, the *Granth* ("گرنتھ[OCR?]") was read there, and the place was known by the name Shahid Ganj: "اس [illegible] نام شہید گنج[OCR?] مشہور[OCR?]" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 122).

## The Courts and the Riots

The dispute that Qaiser recounts ran on from British rule into Pakistan's. In the British period, he says, the Muslims applied to the courts for the building's return. "The Lahore High Court" asked for a living witness who had seen it used as a mosque, found none, and "decided in favour of the Sikhs"; the Sikhs then razed the building, and there followed "rioting between Muslims and Sikhs", which Qaiser lays at the door of the British policy of divide and rule (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 77, folio 59). Muhammad Ali Jinnah and Allama Iqbal, he says, became involved, and he has seen a photograph of Jinnah addressing a crowd "at the Badshahi Mosque at the time of this riot"; in the end "the gurdwara remained a gurdwara" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 77, folio 59).

After Partition, with the creation of the Auqaf Department, the question came back to the Lahore High Court, which ruled, "Around 1957-58", that the building "would remain a gurdwara" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 77, folio 59; p. 78, folio 60). In the late 1980s petitioners sought a review "again"; the judge said he would convert the site if they could produce "even one person who had prayed at the mosque", and none was produced (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 78, folio 60).

## The Rebuilding of 2004

"In about 1994-95, the Kar Sewa Committee of England" sought permission to renovate the gurdwara, and the government agreed (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 78, folio 60). Avtar Singh Panghedha asked Qaiser to supervise the construction, and, Qaiser says, "all the money, the contracts ... went through me" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 78, folio 60). When the newspapers reported the work, "the Muslims tried once again to create an issue" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 78, folio 60).

The opposition was turned, in Qaiser's account, by an ironsmith named Rana, who persuaded the local traders that the gurdwara "would attract foreign pilgrims"; the traders of Naulakha Bazaar became stakeholders in the project, and "this gurdwara was eventually constructed in 2004" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 78, folio 60). It was during this construction that the skeletons of children and adults came to light (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 78, folio 60).

## Devotion and Practice

In the site's account, the recitation of the *Guru Granth Sahib* and the ardas keep the martyrs' memory alive at the gurdwara. Chishti, describing the Shahid Ganj of his day, records that the *Granth* was read there and that "بہنگ[OCR?] بکثرت خرچ ہوتی ہی", bhang was consumed in quantity; and he adds that formerly, "بعہد مہاراجہ صاحب", in the Maharaja's time, the place had been a "فیض عام", a source of open bounty to all (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 123).

Khalid's book offers a general frame for that earlier patronage, said of the province and not of this site: "When Maharaja Ranjit Singh became the sovereign of Punjab at the end of the eighteenth century he spent large amounts of money on the renovation and expansion of many gurdwaras", and "attached vast tracts of land to these gurdwaras, so that the revenues from the lands could facilitate their functioning" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 34, folio 16).

## Another Shahid Ganj

Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori's *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, a tazkira of the saints of the Punjab completed in 1875, has an entry headed "مزار شہید گنج". It describes a mass grave of Muslim martyrs of the Ghaznavid period at "محلہ سادھواں", and its table of contents lists it, as entry 211, among the Punjab elders whose dates of death are not established (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 264, folio 252; p. 20, folio 8). The entry names no landmark beyond the mohalla, and whether this Shahid Ganj and the Sikh one at Naulakha are the same place cannot be settled from the book; it is noted here only because the two share a name.

Lahori's entry draws its history from the *Tuhfat al-Wasilin*: "جب سلطنت سلاطین غزنویہ کی پنجاب میں ہوگئی اور لاہور دار الحکومت قرار پایا", when the Ghaznavid sultans' rule was established in the Punjab and Lahore became its capital, it held for a long time, until strife among the Ghaznavids in the time of Shah Bahram left the government of the Punjab weak; Raja Anangpal then took Lahore, and there was Hindu rule until an army returned from Ghazni (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 264, folio 252). In a note on another entry, the modern editor of the *Hadiqat* calls that source "تحفۃ الواصلین ایک وضعی تذکرہ", a fabricated tazkira (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 191–192, folios 179–180).

## Setting: Lahore and the Punjab in Mir Mannu's Time

The books say a good deal about the governor and his years in general terms; none of what follows is said of this site. Lahori's modern editor dates the first assault of Ahmad Shah Abdali on the city: "احمد شاہ ابدالی کا لاہور پر پہلا حملہ (۱۱۶۱ھ) ۱۷۴۸ء کو ہوا" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 214, folio 202). Kanhaiya Lal's *Tarikh-e Lahore* tells how Jani Khan, with his brother Mir Muin al-Mulk, defeated Ahmad Shah Durrani, who went back to Kabul, and how "اسکے جانے کے بعد پنجاب کا انتظام میر معین الملک نے اپنے طور پر کیا", after his going Mir Muin al-Mulk ran the Punjab in his own way (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 270, folio 273).

Khalid's *In Search of Shiva* gives a modern reading of the governor's war. "Moin-ul-Mulk, popularly known as Meer Manu" "was the governor of Punjab" and was "constantly pestered by the Sikh insurgent groups Misl, which had sprung up all over Punjab"; "The Sikh groups did not control much land but because of their anti-status quo politics the lower rungs of the society supported and protected them" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated). Meer Manu, Khalid argues, "cast the battle between the state and insurgents as a war between Muslims and Sikhs, even though there were several Muslims in the Sikh groups and many non-Muslim soldiers in the army of Meer Manu" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated). He writes that Abdali had "agreed an annual tax stipulation which the governor was responsible of paying to him, for the right of governing Punjab", and that "Before Ahmad Shah could reach Lahore, the prize possession of Punjab, Meer Manu met him at a place near the city of Gujrat"; on a later raid, which he dates as "the 1751 attack by Ahmad Shah Abdali", the Afghan bypassed the governor and laid siege to the city: "For months the citizens were trapped within the walls of their city" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).

Chishti records what came after the governor's death: "جہان کسی سکہہ[OCR?] نے موقع پایا حاکم ہو بیٹہا", wherever a Sikh found the chance, he set himself up as ruler (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 719). The upheaval left its mark on the Muslim scholars of the province too: Lahori's editor writes of Ghulam Murtaza Qasuri that "آپ نے پنجاب میں سکھ گردی سے تنگ آکر پشاور ہجرت فرمائی", that, worn down by the Sikh disorders in the Punjab, he migrated to Peshawar (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 152, folio 140).

Khaled Ahmad's introduction to Taufiq Rafat's translations of Bulleh Shah sketches the same decades from the side of the Punjab's peasantry: "Marauding Sikh bands formed themselves into robber baronies and imposed their own system of extortion on the Punjab farmer", and Abdali's "treatment of the Punjab weakened the government in Lahore and delivered the various doabs into the hands of the Sikh" confederacy (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 27, folio 7). "As Bulleh Shah lay dying in 1758 at the age of 78, the rape of the Punjab was proceeding apace" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 28, folio 8). In a passage that runs from Banda Bairagi through Abdul Samad Khan, Zakriya Khan and Nadir Shah, the same introduction names 'Shahidganj in 1746'; the passage as quoted does not say which Shahid Ganj is meant or what happened there (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, pp. 42–43, folios 22–23).

Bulleh Shah, who died in 1758, left verses on the state of the province that his translators read as a witness to the times. In Christopher Shackle's translation, one *kafi* speaks of 'The twelfth century' that 'has come with gaping mouth' (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 52, folio 27), the twelfth century of the Hijri era, which Shackle's note equates with the eighteenth century C.E. (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, pp. 165–166, folios 140–141). It goes on:

*"The door of doom and torment has opened, and Panjab is in a bad state. It has been struck by fear of the pit of hell."* (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 52, folio 27)

And in Taufiq Rafat's:

*"Now hell has opened its bogs. / Punjab has gone to the dogs. / This hell is the nethermost hell." … "This century cramps our style."* (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 149, folio 129)

Rafat's translator's note explains its force: "The full bitterness of Bulleh Shah's 'Punjab has gone to the dogs' will only come through against this backdrop", a Punjab "being persistently rent by petty chiefs and marauders" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 16, folio xvi). Shackle notes another of the poet's verses as "Taken to be an allusion to the Sikhs, whose guerilla horsemen ultimately destroyed the Mughal authority in Panjab" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 193, folio 168).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **Where the gurdwara stands.** The site's account places it in the Naulakha Bazaar quarter of the walled city of Lahore. Qaiser says "this is not the walled city of Lahore" but outside Delhi Gate, in the area known as Naulakha Bazaar (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 76, folio 58; p. 77, folio 59); Chishti places his Shahid Ganj outside Delhi Gate, near a sarai whose name is uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 122).
- **The name.** The site's name is Shaheed Ganj Singh Singhnian, and its bibliography gives Shahid Ganj Singhnian. Khalid writes "Gurdwara Shaheed Ganj" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 77, folio 59), and Chishti only Shahid Ganj (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 122). Lahori uses the same name for a mass grave of Muslim martyrs of the Ghaznavid period, which may or may not be the same place (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 264, folio 252; p. 20, folio 8).
- **How the gurdwara began.** The site's account says a gurdwara was raised after the consolidation of Sikh power in the later eighteenth century to enshrine the memory of the martyrs. Qaiser says the building was a small mosque, never used for worship, into which the Sikhs placed the *Guru Granth Sahib* under Maharaja Ranjit Singh (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 76–77, folios 58–59). Chishti describes two mosques, one used by the Sikhs for reading the *Granth*, and separately a large Sikh *samadh* built where Sikhs had been killed and given the name Shahid Ganj (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 122).
- **Who ordered the killings.** The site's account names *Muin-ul-Mulk*, known as *Mir Mannu*; Qaiser names "Moin-ul-Mulk also known as Meer Manu" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 77, folio 59); Kanhaiya Lal writes Mir Muin al-Mulk (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 270, folio 273). Chishti names Mir Mannu for the general order against the Sikhs and for the tomb in the Shahid Ganj quarter (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 121), but in his account of the *samadh* the killer's name is printed as Mir Masud, the reading uncertain (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 122).
- **What was done there.** The site's account has the men put to death and the women and children imprisoned and set to grind grain. Qaiser has men, women and children alike slaughtered and thrown into a well, and a room built to hold prisoners before execution (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 77, folio 59).
- **When.** The site's account sets the persecution under Mir Mannu, governor from 1748. Khaled Ahmad's introduction names 'Shahidganj in 1746', without saying which Shahid Ganj is meant (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, pp. 42–43, folios 22–23). Chishti places Mir Mannu's rule in the reign of Muhammad Shah (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 121).
- **The building today.** The site's account calls the gurdwara austere, less an architectural showpiece than a site of remembrance. Qaiser says the old building was razed in the British period and the present gurdwara constructed in 2004 with the backing of the Kar Sewa Committee of England (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 77, folio 59; p. 78, folio 60).

## Legacy

In Sikh historical consciousness the martyrdoms at Lahore under Mir Mannu occupy a place beside the sacrifices of the Gurus themselves, invoked daily in the ardas that recalls "the Singhs and Singhnis who gave their heads but did not surrender their faith". Gurdwara Shaheed Ganj Singh Singhnian is the physical anchor of that memory in the city where it happened. For the study of eighteenth-century Punjab it testifies to the ferocity of the state's campaign against the early Khalsa; for the Sikh community it remains sacred ground.

The rebuilding had consequences beyond Naulakha. Afterwards, Khalid writes, "the Government of Pakistan woke up to the potential of Sikh pilgrimage tourism" and invited the Sikh community abroad "to invest in the renovation of any Sikh gurdwara in the country" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 79, folio 61). The long quarrel also became a byword: at a place the book calls Sachkand, Khalid and Qaiser name it as the outcome to be feared: "we could have a situation similar to Gurdwara Shaheed Ganj here?" — "Precisely." (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 76, folio 58).
