## Overview

Gurdwara Tambu Sahib stands in Nankana Sahib, a short distance east of the central shrine of Janam Asthan that marks the birthplace of *Guru Nanak Dev Ji*, founder of the Sikh faith. Its name comes from the Punjabi *tambu*, a tent, for the gurdwara grew up around a great *van* tree whose spreading, low-hanging branches formed a natural canopy — a living tent — beneath which the young Nanak is remembered to have taken shelter. It is one of the cluster of shrines in and around the holy town that preserve, site by site, the episodes of the Guru's boyhood.

Of the books consulted, two name the gurdwara itself, both by Haroon Khalid. *Walking with Nanak* calls it "Gurdwara Tambu Sahib" and places it "in between Janamsthan and Kiari Sahib and Malji Sahib" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 91, folio 73). *A White Trail* writes it both "Gurdwara Tamboo Sahib" and "Gurdwara Tambu Sahib", and puts it "a few hundred metres away from Gurdwara Janamasthan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 223, folio not stated); it reports the festival of Lohri there in January 2011 and the birthday of Guru Nanak, and recalls the gathering of 1921 that began within its grounds. Harish Dhillon's retelling of the Janamsakhis tells the story of the bargain that the gurdwara remembers but names no gurdwara at Talwandi, the old name of the town. The other books speak only of Guru Nanak, of Nankana Sahib, or of the Punjab, not of this place, and what they say is given below as being about the Guru or the town.

## The True Bargain and the Tent-Tree

The tradition attached to Tambu Sahib is bound up with one of the most beloved of the *sakhis*, the story of the *sacha sauda* or "true bargain." Nanak's father, Mehta Kalu, anxious that his contemplative son should learn a trade, gave him a sum of money and sent him to the market town of Chuharkana to buy goods and turn a profit. On the way Nanak encountered a company of hungry holy men, and, judging that no bargain could be truer than the feeding of the poor and the servants of God, he spent the whole sum on food for them and returned home empty-handed but content. Fearing his father's anger, the boy is said to have stopped short of the village and seated himself beneath the great *van* tree, whose branches enclosed him like a tent, until he was found and brought home. The gurdwara raised on the spot preserves the memory of that refuge. (A separate, often-conflated Nankana Sahib legend — of a tree's shadow standing still while all others moved — properly belongs to the nearby Gurdwara Mal Ji Sahib, not this site.)

Khalid gives the legend in *A White Trail*. "According to the legend, when Baba Guru Nanak turned eighteen, his father asked him to take up a trade. He gave him twenty rupees", and Nanak left "the city (present day Nankana Sahib)" with Bhai Mardana (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 182, folio not stated). He fed starving *jogis* with the money, and on his way back "hid under this tree"; his family found him there. "Now there are two Gurdwaras to commemorate this event. One is this Gurdwara, Tamboo Sahib" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 182, folio not stated). Elsewhere in the book the gurdwara marks the place "where he hid after returning from the incident that came to be known as Sacha Sauda" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated).

In *Walking with Nanak* Khalid writes that Nanak, "after he had spent all his father's money on a good deed", "looked for sanctuary in a group of waan trees that existed outside the town. Those trees still exist and are held sacred by Sikh devotees" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 86, folio 68; p. 87, folio 69). The road home, he writes, ran "back from Chuhrkhana to Nankana Sahib, which at that point in time was still known as Rai Bhoi di Talwindi", across "a vast jungle that existed then between Nankana and Chuhrkhana" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 86, folio 68).

The same book retells Nanak's youth in a reconstruction that Khalid himself calls fictionalised (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 17, folio not stated). In it Mehta Kalu calls himself "an accountant" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 63, folio 45), and Nanak is found hiding "in the growe [OCR?] [grove] of wax [OCR?] [waan] trees outside town" by his father and "his employer and friend, Rai Bular" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 64, folio 46). An earlier scene of the reconstruction, which does not name the site, has the boy sitting at dusk under "this waan tree", with cloths "tied as supplication" on its branches, "about two furlongs" from his home, hearing the call to prayer and the temple bells together (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 59, folio 41; p. 60, folio 42).

Dhillon's retelling sends the boy out with goods to be sold in Talwandi (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 25, folio 29), and his chapter on the bargain ends in "the forest" and back in "Talwandi" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 38, folio 42). Of Talwandi he writes that it "lay in the path of numerous mendicants, sadhus and holy men", who camped "in a grove of trees on the outskirts of the village" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 24, folio 28).

## The Name

Both of Khalid's books derive the name from the tree. *Walking with Nanak* says it is "derived from the fambu [OCR?] [tambu] (tent) like trees under which Nanak hid" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 87, folio 69). *A White Trail* says: "The word tamboo comes from the tent like cover of this tree. Tamboo means tent in Punjabi." (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 181, folio not stated). The tree itself, in that book, "is spread out like a tent", "an ancient tree, called Waan in Punjabi" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 181, folio not stated).

## The Gurdwara

A shrine was established beside the tree during the period of Sikh rule in the Punjab, about a furlong east of Janam Asthan, and its architecture echoes that of the other Nankana gurdwaras: a domed sanctum set within a square hall, decorated in the idiom of Sikh sacred building, though plainer in some of its detailing than its neighbours. It lies close to the gurdwaras of Mal Ji Sahib and of the fifth and sixth Gurus, so that the pilgrim moving among them traces a compact circuit of the Guru's early life. Within the sanctum the *Guru Granth Sahib* is installed, and the site is maintained, like the other shrines of the town, by the authorities responsible for Sikh heritage in Pakistan.

Khalid describes the place in both books. In *Walking with Nanak*: "Next to the trees is a small gurdwara, a yellow single storied structure. Behind it are two large hostels used by Sikh pilgrims at the time of the largest Sikh festival in Pakistan—the birthday of Guru Nanak, celebrated in November every year" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 87, folio 69). The gurdwara has a "blue gate" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 91, folio 73).

In *A White Trail* the tree is "protected by a boundary fence" with one gate, and shoes come off before entering (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 181, folio not stated). Behind the tree stands "a small mango coloured building with a small dome on the top", with a board reading "Gurdwara Tamboo Sahib." and the saffron *Nishan Sahib* in the courtyard (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 181, folio not stated). Describing the same building at the festival of Guru Nanak's birthday, he writes: "The shrine, which is a small yellow structure, stands in the middle of the ground with a white dome on the top" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 223, folio not stated). One of the book's photographs is captioned "Gurdwara Tambu Sahib at the time of sunset." (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 107, folio not stated). Among the plates of *Walking with Nanak* is one captioned "Gurdwara Tambu Sahib", which names no town; the caption also names "Sacha Sauda", the episode (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 167, plate).

Inside is "the Thara Sahib (a wooden pavilion which hosts the Granth Sahib)", and two swords lie there in blue velvet sheaths (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 185, folio not stated).

The grounds are large. Khalid found a single-storey building used by farmers, a tractor, and "vast land laying vacant on both sides of the shrine which is used for agriculture"; "A tall barb-wired wall" ran on all sides, with one gate from the main market, a board by the road in "English, Shahmukhi ... and Gurmukhi", and a second, blue gate "kept permanently locked for security reasons" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 181–182, folio not stated). "The area belonging to Gurdwara Tambu Sahib is the second largest in the city, making it the second most popular destination after Janamasthan", the ground "big enough to host an international cricket match" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 223, folio not stated).

## Custodians, Lands and Buildings

*Walking with Nanak* names the man in charge. Jatha Singh, "the self-appointed caretaker of the gurdwara", is "in-charge of the vast property associated with the gurdwara, which was allotted to it by the Sikh ruler of Punjab, Maharaja Ranjit Singh in the nineteenth century", and "receives millions of rupees in donations from Sikh expatriates residing in first world countries", to be spent "on the upliftment of the gurdwara and the Sikh community in Nankana Sahib" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 87, folio 69). Writing of the province and not of this gurdwara, Khalid adds that "When Maharaja Ranjit Singh became the sovereign of Punjab at the end of the eighteenth century he spent large amounts of money on the renovation and expansion of many gurdwaras", and "attached vast tracts of land to these gurdwaras, so that the revenues from the lands could facilitate their functioning" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 34, folio 16).

Jatha Singh kept his room "in one of the rooms of a hostel". When Khalid called on him he was arbitrating between two feuding Muslim families, and the guests were served "langar, vegetables along with lentils and hot rotts [OCR?] [rotis]" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 87, folio 69). Of the town's Sikhs Khalid writes, in the same passage, of "the Sikh community in Nankana Sahib, which amounts to about two hundred-odd households", whom Jatha Singh "has for several years played the role of a leader" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 87, folio 69).

*A White Trail* describes the newer buildings. By the entrance stands a newly built triple-storey residential hall, with two larger residential buildings raised from devotees' donations and "a single storey Langar Hall" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 223, folio not stated). "Three hostel blocks have been constructed here with the help of donations from pilgrims coming from Britain, Canada and America"; "A large amount has been donated by Kar Seva, an England based non-profit organization" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 223, folio not stated), and a yellow board on the Langar Hall reads: "This newly constructed building has been made by Kar Seva" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 224, folio not stated). "Shaheed Hall, next to the Langar Hall", also built from Kar Seva's donations and "Recently completed", is a "five storey building" whose roof is closed to pilgrims (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 224–225, folio not stated). "In the future, the Government of Pakistan plans to construct a Guru Nanak University in the vicinity of this complex", Khalid adds, a "persistent demand of the expat Sikh community" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 223–224, folio not stated).

## Devotion and Practice

The tree is the heart of the devotion. "Pilgrims tie pieces of cloth onto the branches of these stooping trees as a supplication", Khalid writes (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 87, folio 69). In *A White Trail* he describes the custom at Lohri. Kartar Singh Kartar tore a saffron cloth from inside the gurdwara and each person tied a piece to the tree; his Muslim friends tied cloth too, but kept their hands at their sides (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 181, folio not stated). According to Kartar, "by tying a cloth on the branches of this sacred tree, our wishes come true"; at Guru Nanak's birthday "People tie over each other's cloth" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 182, folio not stated). Candles were lit at the base of the *Nishan Sahib* (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 184, folio not stated).

The prayer that followed, which Khalid's account places in the gurdwara of the compound, was "a Sikh-only affair", with the other three doors closed. Kartar prostrated before the Thara Sahib and offered "a ten rupee note", "the highest one being that of fifty" among the offerings (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 185, folio not stated). The room filled to "about fifty people", "as many men as there are women"; the donations "will be used for the renovation of the Gurdwara". A *Granthi* "in his mid-sixties" read for half an hour, then came a community prayer with "Waheguru", and *Prasad* of naan and a sweet made in *desi ghee* (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 185, folio not stated). Candles were "held up in a circle around the arti that carries lit incense", and people "place their hand on top of the fire and rub it on their faces" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 185–186, folio not stated). From there the procession went on to Janamasthan (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 185, folio not stated).

Writing of the town's Lohri and not of this gurdwara alone, Khalid records that "Lohri like Vaisakhi or Basant, is an ancient festival of the Punjab that commemorates the change of season", celebrated "on the 13th of January" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 192–193, folio not stated). Kartar told him that Lohri and Guru Gobind Singh's birthday "have only begun in the last couple of years", made possible "by the increase in the Sikh population as a result of the migration from the tribal areas after the war"; "Earlier, there was only a prayer at the Gurdwara." (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 193, folio not stated). "It is ironical though", Khalid writes, "that this Punjabi tradition shunned by Punjab is now being revived by a group of Pathan men residing in Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 194, folio not stated).

At the birthday of Guru Nanak the gurdwara fills. "Most of the pilgrims from the developed countries stay at Gurdwara Tambu Sahib" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 223, folio not stated); Shaheed Hall is the "first choice of pilgrims from developed countries", though as their numbers have fallen it now houses many Indian pilgrims (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 224–225, folio not stated). Security is "lax" compared with Janamasthan: there are metal detectors and a few guards, but it is "fairly easy to walk in unnoticed" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 223, folio not stated). Food is served around the clock (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 229, folio not stated).

In the parking area stands a book stall of the "Dyal Singh Memorial Trust, a pre-partition trust based in Lahore", where Iqbal Qaiser, 58, sells his *Historical Sikh Shrines in Pakistan*: "This is the only time in the year my book sells … somewhere between twenty to thirty copies" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 224, folio not stated). Police stand on the roofs of the Langar Hall and the residential buildings (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 224, folio not stated). Arshad Malik, a constable of 35 with "eighteen years" in the police, on a twelve-hour duty since eight in the morning, told Khalid that "the entire police force of the city has been deployed at the Gurdwaras for these three days" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 225, folio not stated). He would not eat from the *langar*: "We are Muslims. We don't eat with the Sikhs or Hindus" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 225–226, folio not stated).

Writing of the festival in the town as a whole, Khalid gives the official estimate that "ten to fifteen thousand people come to Nankana Sahib for this festival every year, which is celebrated during the last days of November"; in 2011, "According to media reports, only three thousand people are attending", and "This year it began on the 8th of November and will conclude today, on the 10th of November" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated). It is called "Guru Nanak Jayanti or Gurpurab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated). Of the town's gurdwaras generally he writes: "All year round most of them remain locked, only to be opened at the time of the festival. There are security guards employed at the entrance to all of them." (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 227, folio not stated).

## The Gathering of 1921

*A White Trail* records one historical event at the gurdwara. On 20 February 1921 unarmed Sikhs from other districts "gathered at Gurdwara Tamboo Sahib", accused the *mahant*, and after a short prayer resolved to walk to Janamasthan (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 189–190, folio not stated). Writing of the Punjab in general, Khalid explains the background: "As a result of sectarian divisions between the Sikh communities and the takeover of Punjab by the British, a large number of important Sikh Gurdwaras were being administered by Hindu mahants" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 189, folio not stated).

## Setting: Nankana Sahib

What follows is said of the town, not of this gurdwara. Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi places Guru Nanak's birth in "the village of Talwandi later known as Nankana Sahib, about forty miles south-west of Lahore, now part of Pakistan" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 407, folio 383). Khalid writes that "The name of Rai Bhoi di Talwindi has been changed to Nankana Sahib in honour of the first Sikh Guru who was born there" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 31, folio 13), and that "Originally called Rai-Bhoi-Di-Talwandi, this city was rechristened Nankana Sahib in honour of Guru Nanak", so that Nanak's early years there account for "several Gurdwaras in the city" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated). It is "a city considered to be one of the holiest cities in the world for the Sikhs, as the founder of their religion, Nanak, was born here" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 183, folio not stated).

Bhandari's Persian history says that Baba Nanak was born at Talwandi Rai Bhoi (the last word uncertainly read), and lived there in his grandfather's house (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). Dhillon describes Talwandi as "the village of Talwandi, which lay between the rivers Ravi and Chenab, directly in the path of the armies that had set their eyes on Delhi": "Thirteen times was the village of Talwandi destroyed and thirteen times - like the fabled phoenix - it rose again from its ashes", and "Its last rebirth took place under the supervision of Rai Bhoe, a Bhatti Rajpur [text-layer?] [Bhatti Rajput], who had converted to Islam" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 15–16, folios 17–18).

Behind Gurdwara Janamsthan rises an old mound. Iqbal Qaiser told Khalid that "The settlement there was destroyed after which Rai Bhoi di Talwindi was constructed within a boundary wall" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 91, folio 73). "It is believed that in the years between the incident of Sacha Sauda and his migration to Sultanpur, Nanak started to frequent this place", talking with the yogis, *sadhus* and dervishes who passed through: "That period of six to seven years" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 91, folio 73; p. 92, folio 74).

The books count the town's gurdwaras differently. *Walking with Nanak* says: "There are a total of six gurdwaras associated with Guru Nanak at Nankana Sahib" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 90, folio 72); *A White Trail* says: "There are seven Gurdwaras in Nankana Sahib, most of which are associated with incidents from the life of Baba Guru Nanak" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 192, folio not stated). "Gurdwaras that were built there later by the devotees still stand tall", Khalid writes of the places across the country where Nanak stayed (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 168–169, folio not stated), and Kar Seva's name appears on "yellow dustbins and other boards that have been placed in different Gurdwaras of the city" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 223, folio not stated).

After Partition the town's gurdwaras stood empty. Khalid writes of Giani Pratap: "After looking at the dilapidated condition of the shrines here he decided to stay and look after these holy buildings. That was the first time someone had taken the initiative to take care of the gurdwaras in Nankana Sahib after Partition" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 90, folio 72). The informant Kartar told him: "There was no Sikh left here after partition. Our Gurdwaras became barren and were in a bad state" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 184, folio not stated). Most of the town's present Sikhs, Khalid writes, "are not indigenous people from the city, but recent migrants from Khyber-Pakhtunkhwa (KPK) where there is a considerable Sikh population" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 235, folio not stated), and "Most of the newcomers were incorporated within the Gurdwaras, in rooms which were generally reserved for pilgrims" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 176, folio not stated). He gives the numbers in more than one way: "There were only about thirty to forty Sikh houses in Nankana ten years ago" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 174–175, folio not stated), and "In about ten years, the population in Nankana Sahib increased to about two hundred houses" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 175, folio not stated).

## Guru Nanak in the Wider Record

What the books say of Guru Nanak himself is said of the Guru, not of this site; it is gathered here because the gurdwara exists for his sake. Khalid gives his dates as 1469–1539, calling him "the founder of the Sikh religion" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 45, folio not stated), and in *Walking with Nanak* has him "Born Talwandi (now known as Nankana Sahib in Pakistan) on October 20, 1469" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 314, folio 288). Dhillon calls the Janamsakhis "cycles of tales purportedly drawn from the life of the first Guru of the Sikhs" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 9, folio 9), and has him born "On 14 April 1469" to Kalyan Chand Das Bedi, "or Mehta Kalu as he was affectionately called", accountant to the Bhatti family under Rai Bular (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 17–18, folios 19–20); he gives the date of his death as 22 September 1539 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 259, folio 287). Bhandari reports, as something that is said, a birth in the year 1526 of Bikramajit, corresponding to 880 of the Hijra, in the time of Sultan Bahlul Lodi, and a death in the reign of Salim Shah Afghan, at an age between seventy and eighty (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). Khalid records that one Janamsakhi writer, Meherban, "claims that Guru Nanak was born in Dera Chahal and not Rai Bhoi di Talwindi", while the other Janamsakhi writers and Sikh scholars "are unanimous that Nanak was born in Rai Bhoi di Talwindi" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 43, folio 25).

In Dhillon's retelling, Nanak refused the sacred thread at thirteen, "the beginning of a lifelong crusade against the performing of empty rites and rituals" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 19–21, folios 21–23); at sixteen he herded cattle (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 23, folio 27) and "became well acquainted with the dervish Sayyad Hussain and gained an intimate knowledge of the Sufi line of thought" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 24, folio 28). In Khalid's reconstruction his mentor is "Sayyed Hassan, a Muslim Sufi, who lived near his house" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 62, folio 44). After the bargain, in Dhillon's telling, "Nanak moved to Sultanpur (around 60 km from Talwandi)" as keeper of Nawab Daulat Khan's stores (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 44, folio 49).

Bhandari calls him the caravan-leader of the paths of truth and the torch-bearer of the way of the *tariqa*, and writes that the signs of his gift showed early, signs of unveiling, of miracles and of wonders beyond the ordinary appearing from him in his childhood (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520), and that a minstrel named Mardana sang the Guru's verses and drew people into the snare of devotion (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). Of the Guru's followers he writes that they count the service of those who come and go as the greatest worship, and that whoever arrives at midnight speaking Baba Nanak's name is treated as a brother and a friend and served, even a stranger, a thief or a highwayman (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 519). Khalid writes that "It is believed that the tradition of Langar began with Guru Nanak" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 188, folio not stated).

Dhillon writes that Nanak "brought a message of peace and harmony to believers of the mutually hostile religions of Islam and Hinduism", that he taught that "all men and women were equal, irrespective of caste, creed, wealth, social standing and gender", and that a good life rested on honest labour, compassion, selfless service and prayer (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 12–13, folio not stated / 13). He also writes of "Guru Nanak's clear and emphatic disavowal of the miraculous" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 11, folio 11). Khalid writes that Nanak "abhorred the concept of associating miracles with religious personalities", yet "his biography is nothing but a story of his miracles" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 16, folio not stated).

Muslim writers and singers have claimed the Guru too. Khalid writes that "many Muslims, acquainted with the teachings of Guru Nanak, are devoted to him too and visit his shrine" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 220, folio not stated), and that "Guru Nanak was a spiritual follower of Baba Fareed" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 188, folio not stated); in *In Search of Shiva* he notes that "There is a picture of Baba Farid accompanying the picture of Guru Nanak at the shrine of Guru Nanak at Nankana Sahib", without saying which gurdwara he means (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated). Shemeem Burney Abbas writes that Baba Farid's "texts are revered among the Sikhs because he influenced the founder of their own religion, Baba Guru Nanak" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 87, folio not stated). Robert Rozehnal reports that a twentieth-century Chishti Sabiri shaykh, in certain lectures, describes "non-Muslim spiritual luminaries—Krishna, Rama, Sita, Lachman, and Guru Nanak—as believers in the one true God and religious leaders with 'Divine connection' (nisbat)" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 63, folio 53). Omar Kasmani, citing Michel Boivin, notes that the trope of the flower in milk is told also of "Guru Nanak, the spiritual leader and founder of Sikhism" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 195, folio 178, ch. 5 n. 17). At the shrine of the goddess Hinglaj, Jürgen Schaflechner records "the engraved footsteps of Guru Nanak" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 256, folio not stated).

## In Verse

None of the verse the books quote is tied to Tambu Sahib; it is given here as the poetry of the Guru the gurdwara remembers. Dhillon notes that Nanak's *Bani* "was compiled as part of the Adi Granth by Guru Arjan Dev, the fifth Guru, in 1604" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 9, folio 9). Bhandari says that the Guru composed Hindi verses on the truth of God, setting out divine unity in clear expressions and shining metaphors (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520), and that the worship of his followers centres on reading their master's verses, which they recite in song and melody (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 519). Abbas's glossary defines the *banī* by him: "Devotional verses such as those of Guru Nanak; verses of supplication, entreaty, humility, and reverence" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 179, folio not stated).

Khalid quotes the Guru's verse throughout *Walking with Nanak*, in published English translations. Among them, fitting a gurdwara that remembers a bargain, is a poem he quotes under the opening 'O my merchant friend' (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 56–58, folio 40). The *aarti*, sung in praise of a God whom Qaiser reads as residing in nature, begins in one translation:

*The sky the salver, the sun and moon the lamps …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 55, folio 37)

and in another:

*In the salver of the firmament, / The sun and the moon shine as lamps* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 243–244, folios 217–218)

On the oneness of God:

*My Master is One / He is One, brother, the only One* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 60, folio 42)

*God is One, / The Path is One* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 144, folio 126)

The poem on the sacred thread, the *janeu*, which in Khalid's reconstruction the boy Nanak refused:

*From the cotton of compassion, / Spin the thread of contentment* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 61–62, folios 43–44)

On washing:

*As hands or feet besmirched with slime, / Water washes white* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 192, folio 166)

Other openings Khalid quotes: *As a beggar goes a-begging …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 158, folio 140); *Launch ye the boat of austere contemplation …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 148–149, folios 130–131); and *His goodness cannot be priced or traded …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 280, folio 254).

The Guru's verse is sung outside the gurdwaras too. Abbas quotes Abida Parvin: "I sing Shah Hussain, Bulle Shah, Mian Muhammad Bakhsh, Hazrat Khawaja Ghulam Farid, Sacchal Sarmast, Baba Farid, and Guru Nanak" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 54, folio not stated). In Sindh, Boivin records, "Sain Vasan Shah became famous for reciting the banis of Shri Guru Nanak and the kalams of the Sufis" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 68, folio 55).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The spelling of the name.** The site's account writes Tambu Sahib, and the gurdwara is listed as Tambo Sahib. *Walking with Nanak* writes "Gurdwara Tambu Sahib" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 91, folio 73); *A White Trail* writes both "Gurdwara Tamboo Sahib" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 181, folio not stated) and "Gurdwara Tambu Sahib" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 223, folio not stated). The site's account names the tree *van*; Khalid writes "waan" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 86, folio 68) and "Waan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 181, folio not stated).
- **One tree or several.** The site's account speaks of "a great *van* tree", and *A White Trail* of "an ancient tree, called Waan in Punjabi", where Nanak "hid under this tree" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 181, folio not stated; p. 182, folio not stated). *Walking with Nanak* speaks of "a group of waan trees" and of "these stooping trees" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 86, folio 68; p. 87, folio 69).
- **Its distance from Janam Asthan.** The site's account puts the gurdwara "a short distance east" of Janam Asthan, "about a furlong east". *A White Trail* puts it "a few hundred metres away from Gurdwara Janamasthan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 223, folio not stated). In a reconstruction scene that does not name the site, *Walking with Nanak* puts a waan tree with cloths tied on its branches "about two furlongs" from Nanak's home (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 59, folio 41; p. 60, folio 42).
- **Who found the boy.** The site's account says he stayed under the tree "until he was found and brought home". *A White Trail* says his family found him there (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 182, folio not stated); Khalid's reconstruction in *Walking with Nanak* has his father and Rai Bular find him (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 64, folio 46).
- **The building.** The site's account describes "a domed sanctum set within a square hall". *Walking with Nanak* has "a yellow single storied structure" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 87, folio 69). *A White Trail* has, on one visit, "a small mango coloured building with a small dome on the top" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 181, folio not stated) and, on another, "a small yellow structure" with "a white dome on the top" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 223, folio not stated).
- **The hostels.** *Walking with Nanak* has "two large hostels" behind the gurdwara (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 87, folio 69). *A White Trail* has "Three hostel blocks", besides a triple-storey residential hall, two larger residential buildings and the five-storey Shaheed Hall (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 223, folio not stated; pp. 224–225, folio not stated).
- **Who keeps the gurdwara.** The site's account says it is maintained "by the authorities responsible for Sikh heritage in Pakistan". *Walking with Nanak* names Jatha Singh, "the self-appointed caretaker of the gurdwara", in charge of its property and its donations (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 87, folio 69).
- **The Sikhs of Nankana Sahib.** The site's account says that "Since Partition in 1947 the resident Sikh community of Nankana Sahib has been gone", and that the gurdwara is visited "chiefly by pilgrim *jathas*". *Walking with Nanak* speaks of "the Sikh community in Nankana Sahib, which amounts to about two hundred-odd households" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 87, folio 69). *A White Trail* describes resident Sikhs, most of them recent migrants from Khyber-Pakhtunkhwa, keeping Lohri at the gurdwara, and gives their numbers as "only about thirty to forty Sikh houses in Nankana ten years ago" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 174–175, folio not stated) and "about two hundred houses" after about ten years (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 175, folio not stated).
- **The number of gurdwaras in the town.** *Walking with Nanak* counts "a total of six gurdwaras associated with Guru Nanak at Nankana Sahib" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 90, folio 72); *A White Trail* counts "seven Gurdwaras in Nankana Sahib" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 192, folio not stated).
- **The Guru's birth.** Khalid gives "October 20, 1469" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 314, folio 288); Dhillon gives "14 April 1469" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 17–18, folios 19–20); Bhandari, reporting it as something that is said, gives Samvat 1526, 880 Hijri, in the reign of Bahlul Lodi (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). Khalid reports that Meherban placed the birth at Dera Chahal, against the other Janamsakhi writers' Rai Bhoi di Talwindi (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 43, folio 25).
- **The Guru's death.** Dhillon gives 22 September 1539 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 259, folio 287); Bhandari gives the reign of Salim Shah Afghan, at an age between seventy and eighty (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).

## Legacy

Since Partition in 1947 the resident Sikh community of Nankana Sahib has been gone, and Tambu Sahib, with the other shrines, is visited chiefly by pilgrim *jathas* who travel from India and the diaspora for the great anniversaries, above all the *Gurpurab* of Guru Nanak's birth, when the town fills with the faithful. The tent-tree of Tambu Sahib carries a lesson dear to the Sikh conscience — that the truest use of wealth is the service of others, the principle of *sacha sauda* — and the site endures as a gentle monument to the boy who thought the feeding of the hungry the best of all bargains.

Khalid's two books show the gurdwara as both of these things at once: a sacred tree that pilgrims and townspeople, Sikh and Muslim, hung with cloth, "held sacred by Sikh devotees" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 87, folio 69), and, with its grounds "the second largest in the city", "the second most popular destination after Janamasthan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 223, folio not stated), where the pilgrims of the Sikh diaspora sleep and the halls their donations built now stand.
