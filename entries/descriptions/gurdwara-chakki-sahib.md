## Overview

Gurdwara Chakki Sahib stands within the old town of Eminabad in Gujranwala District — the settlement known in Guru Nanak's day as Saidpur — and marks the place where a great millstone (*chakki*) associated with the Guru was kept. It commemorates one of the most dramatic episodes of Guru Nanak's life: his imprisonment during the invasion of the Punjab by the Mughal *Babur*, traditionally dated to around 1520-21, when Eminabad was sacked and its people carried off. The present shrine, a simple flat-roofed building within a brick-paved compound, preserves the memory of the millstone and of the Guru's witness against the cruelty of war. Since Partition it has stood without a resident congregation, cared for as part of Pakistan's Sikh heritage and visited by pilgrims on the great anniversaries, often together with the neighbouring Eminabad shrines of Rori Sahib and Bhai Lalo's well.

Only one of the books consulted describes the site itself. Haroon Khalid, travelling through Pakistan in the footsteps of Guru Nanak with the historian Iqbal Qaiser, was taken by Qaiser to a small locked house in Eminabad, which Qaiser described as the place where the Guru was held prisoner in Babur's time and made to work a stone grinder (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 116, folio 98). Khalid's book does not print the gurdwara's name; the link to the story of the millstone rests on Qaiser's words there. The same book retells, from the Janamsakhis, the story of the millstone that turned by itself at Saidpur, but it does not say where in the town the prison of that story stood (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 114, folio 96).

The other books speak of Guru Nanak, of the town or of the province, never of this gurdwara, and what is drawn from them below is said of the figure or the place. Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi's history of Sufism in India, which follows the Guru's life into the years of Babur's invasions, writes of a "Saiyidpur (Amanabad)" and never names Eminabad (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 410, folio 386). Harish Dhillon's retelling of the Janamsakhis brings the Guru to Saidpur twice but tells no story of a millstone (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 56, folio 62; pp. 224–225, folios 246–247). Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* names a town and *pargana* spelled "امن آباد", but says nothing there of the Guru or of any shrine (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 176).

## The Captivity at Saidpur

According to Sikh tradition the captives, Guru Nanak among them, were set to grinding corn with hand-mills to feed the conquering army; and it is remembered that the Guru's millstone turned of its own accord while he, pouring in the grain, sang the praises of the One God — the hymns lamenting the horrors visited upon the land that are preserved in Sikh scripture as the *Babar-vani*, the "utterances concerning Babur." The marvel is said to have astonished his guards and, when reported to Babur, to have brought the emperor himself to the Guru; moved by his words, Babur is remembered to have sought his blessing and, at the Guru's urging, to have freed many prisoners.

Khalid gives the story as the Janamsakhis tell it. Nanak was in Saidpur when the Mughal forces took the city; he was jailed with the other citizens and set to a stone grinder, which moved by itself "using his miraculous powers"; the guards told Babur, who sent for him (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 114, folio 96). The Janamsakhis, Khalid writes, record a meeting between Nanak and Babur at Saidpur, in which Babur asks for a blessing to conquer India and Nanak rebukes him (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 114, folio 96). The encounter "was also interpreted to mark the beginning of this antagonism between the Mughals and the Sikhs" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 97, folio 79). Writing of a later reign, Khalid calls the emperor Jahangir the "great grandson of Emperor Babur who had incarcerated Guru Nanak" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 133, folio not stated).

The Janamsakhis themselves, Khalid explains, are "Sikh texts on Nanak's life, written by his devotees after his death"; they are "the primary sources", and they survive in "different versions" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 17, folio not stated). Dhillon calls them "cycles of tales purportedly drawn from the life of the first Guru of the Sikhs" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 9, folio 9), and his publisher's note dates the earliest known janamsakhi to 1658, nearly 120 years after the Guru's death in 1539 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 271, folio not stated).

Rizvi tells the years of the invasions as history, of the province rather than of the town. Back from eastern India about 1520, Guru Nanak found "the Panjab plunged into a number of significant political crises"; Babur had opened unsuccessful negotiations with Ibrahim to annex west Panjab to Kabul, and "After two minor raids around the region between the Indus and Jhelum, Babur invaded a third time over the Panjab in 926/1520 reaching as far as Sialkot" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 409, folio 385). In the same account Rizvi names "Saiyidpur (Amanabad)" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 410, folio 386), and his index lists an "Amnabad" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 462, folio 438). The book does not identify either with Eminabad.

Dhillon's retelling brings the Guru to Saidpur early, on the first of his journeys, from Sultanpur by way of Talwandi (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 56, folio 62). He passes through it again on the way home from the fourth, by way of Iran, Kabul, Peshawar, Kandahar, Hasan Abdal, Tilla Balnath and Katasraj, and on from Saidpur to Kartarpur (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 224–225, folios 246–247). Dhillon sets that return at the time of Babur's third incursion into India, by a south-eastern route to the Punjab, and gives no year for it (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 224, folio 246).

## The Gurdwara

Iqbal Qaiser's 1998 survey records that fourteen *ghumaon* of land remain attached to the shrine, that two rooms surviving from Babur's own period still stand within it, and that a fair is held here at Visakhi, with the *Nishan Sahib* flying in the courtyard; the Encyclopaedia of Sikhism further notes that the shrine was administered by the Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee from 28 February 1922 until 1947, and that a hymn of Guru Nanak's preserved in the Guru Granth Sahib is read as evidence that he was himself present at Eminabad when Babur sacked it. Visiting in 2017, the journalist Haroon Khalid found the gurdwara's wooden door locked, the building identified only by a small plaque reading “Gurudwara Chakki Sahib, Eimanabad.”

In *Walking with Nanak* Khalid describes the place as he and Qaiser came to it in the town. Qaiser stopped before "a small house. It was an ordinary structure with a small door and a few steps leading to it". From behind its walls rose a pole wrapped in saffron cloth, with "a Khalsa flag" on top. "This too was locked." (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 116, folio 98)

Qaiser told him what the house had been: "This place was used as a prison at the time of Babur. Guru Nanak was imprisoned here and was forced to work on a stone grinder." (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 116, folio 98)

The books consulted record no builder for the gurdwara and no date for its first construction. They speak only in general of how such shrines were kept. Khalid writes that when Maharaja Ranjit Singh "became the sovereign of Punjab at the end of the eighteenth century he spent large amounts of money on the renovation and expansion of many gurdwaras", and that he "attached vast tracts of land to these gurdwaras, so that the revenues from the lands could facilitate their functioning" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 34, folio 16). In *A White Trail* he adds that, after the British took the Punjab, "a large number of important Sikh Gurdwaras were being administered by Hindu mahants" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 189, folio not stated). Neither passage names Chakki Sahib.

## Devotion and Pilgrimage

The site's account records the Visakhi fair held at the shrine, and the pilgrims who come on the great anniversaries, often visiting it in the same round as Rori Sahib and Bhai Lalo's well. In *A White Trail* Khalid writes, of Pakistan's Sikh shrines in general, that expatriate Sikhs came for festivals "like Baisakhi and the birthday of Guru Nanak, which were attended by thousands of Indian pilgrims" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 214, folio not stated). In *Walking with Nanak* he lists Eminabad among the towns whose Sikh communities "Only recently" can "exert themselves in a way that was unthinkable earlier", going to university, many of them with cars of their own; the town's name is misprinted at that point in the book (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 88, folio 70).

The books describe the worship of the Guru's followers in general terms. Sujan Rai Bhandari, writing in Persian under Aurangzeb, says of them: "خلاصه عبادت اینطایفه مطالعه اشعار مرشد خویش است که بسرود و نغمه میگویند", the sum of their worship being their master's verses, which they sing (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 519). Many of them, he writes, have put away worldly ties; kin and stranger, friend and foe are the same to them, and they live "با دوستان یکرنگ و با دشمنان بی جنگ" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 519). They count service to the traveller, "درخدمت صادر و وارد", as the highest worship, and whoever arrives at midnight speaking Baba Nanak's name they treat as a brother and a friend and serve, even a stranger, a thief, a highwayman or an evildoer (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 519). Of the Udasis, whom he counts among the devotees of Baba Nanak, he writes in the same words that the sum of their worship is the singing of their master's verses (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 566).

"It is believed that the tradition of Langar began with Guru Nanak", Khalid writes (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 188, folio not stated). Sikh hagiography, he notes, has a favourite scene of the Guru's own worship: "several paintings depicting a serene Nanak listening to the songs of Bhai Mardana holding a rubab ... while Bhai Bala stands behind them holding a fly-whisk" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 160, folio not stated). In Khalid's reconstruction of the Guru's years at Sultanpur, which he says is partly fictionalised (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 17, folio not stated), Mardana played the *rubab* every night while Nanak sang, a practice that became a daily feature "for the rest of their [lives] and also for the Sikh community" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 106, folio 88; p. 107, folio 89).

## The Miracle and Its Doubters

The miracle at the heart of this shrine is the millstone that turned by itself. The site's account gives it as tradition, and Khalid gives it as the Janamsakhis tell it (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 114, folio 96). At the house in Eminabad, Qaiser spoke of a prison and of forced labour at the grinder, without a miracle (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 116, folio 98). Asked by Khalid whether Nanak may never have met Babur at all, Qaiser answered, "Yes. Very much so." He calls the stories of Nanak meeting a Muslim saint, Malik Bhago and Babur "later constructions by his devotees" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 116, folio 98).

Several writers set the miracle stories against what they take to be the Guru's own view. Khalid writes that Nanak "abhorred the concept of associating miracles with religious personalities", yet "his biography is nothing but a story of his miracles" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 16, folio not stated). Nanak, he writes, "was repulsed by the concept of making a larger-than-life figure out of a religious leader" and "comes across as a rationalist" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 112, folio 94). Of other such stories, told at Sialkot and Pasrur, he writes: "His genius lies in the poetic use of words and not in miracles"; Qaiser's comment on them was "That is to show that our saint is more powerful than yours" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 199, folio 173). Of method, Khalid writes that "The only evidence we have is found within the Sikh religion and they are full of stories of miracles", and Qaiser's advice was to "read what Nanak wrote at these places and try to get into his mind" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 286, folio 260).

Dhillon writes of "Guru Nanak's clear and emphatic disavowal of the miraculous" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 11, folio 11). In one of his stories, when a cured man calls his cure a miracle, the Guru answers that "there is no such thing as a miracle" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 190, folio 209). Bhandari, by contrast, writes that signs of unveiling and wonders, "علامات کشف و کرامات و ایات خارق عادات", appeared in the Guru in his early years, at the age of ten in an editor's variant reading (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).

## In Verse

The poem most closely bound to Eminabad is the Guru's lament on Babur's invasion, the *Babar-vani* of the site's account, which Khalid calls the *Babur Bani*. The site's account notes that a hymn of Guru Nanak's in the Guru Granth Sahib is read as evidence that he was himself present at Eminabad when Babur sacked it. Khalid describes the poem as "a long poem that Nanak wrote at the time the Mughal marauder Babur attacked Punjab, destroying the cities of Eminabad and Lahore on the way" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 15, folio not stated). It was "the first poem of Nanak that I read", Khalid writes, and through Iqbal Qaiser's recitation of it he "truly discovered Guru Nanak": in it Nanak "lays the blame of Babur's wrath at God's feet" and "challenges the divinity of the Divine" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 15–16, folio not stated). Khalid quotes the line that dates the invaders' coming:

*Coming in seventy-eight (1521 C.E)* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 113–114, folios 95–96)

The line goes on to their departure, in a year whose printed gloss appears to read 1540; the words of that half are uncertain in the printed text (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 113–114, folios 95–96).

Khalid quotes the Guru's verse throughout *Walking with Nanak*, in published English translations. The lines below are not tied to Eminabad; they are the poetry of the Guru the shrine remembers, the praises of the One God that the site's account has him singing at the millstone. On the oneness of God:

*My Master is One / He is One, brother, the only One* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 60, folio 42)

*God is One, / The Path is One* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 144, folio 126)

The *aarti*, which Iqbal Qaiser reads as placing God "in nature", calling Nanak "a naturalist", begins in one translation:

*The sky the salver, the sun and moon the lamps …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 55, folio 37; p. 56, folio 38)

and in another:

*In the salver of the firmament, / The sun and the moon shine as lamps* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 243–244, folios 217–218)

The poem on the sacred thread, the *janeu*, which in Khalid's reconstruction the boy Nanak refused:

*From the cotton of compassion, / Spin the thread of contentment* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 61–62, folios 43–44)

On washing:

*As hands or feet besmirched with slime, / Water washes white* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 192, folio 166)

The song Nanak sings, in Khalid's reconstruction, to the innkeepers who meant to rob him and Mardana:

*The herons in their white feathers dwell in the sacred shrines of pilgrimage* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 123–126, folios 105–108)

Other openings Khalid quotes: *O my merchant friend* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 56–58, folio 40); *Launch ye the boat of austere contemplation …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 148–149, folios 130–131); *As a beggar goes a-begging …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 158, folio 140); and *His goodness cannot be priced or traded …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 280, folio 254).

Dhillon writes that the Guru's Bani "was compiled as part of the Adi Granth by Guru Arjan Dev, the fifth Guru, in 1604" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 9, folio 9), and that Nanak's "sermon was his Bani" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 228–229, folios 252–253). Khalid adds that later Gurus' poems are recorded "not in their own names but in that of Guru Nanak" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 98, folio 80), and that Nanak "incorporated several words which were not part of the Punjabi of the time … which is why Nanak's contribution as a poet to Punjabi is immense" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 278–279, folios 252–253).

Bhandari, writing in Persian, says that the Guru composed "اشعار هندی" on the truth of God, setting out divine unity "بعبارات واضحه و استعارات لایحه" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520), and that "مردانه نام مطرب", a singer named Mardana, sang his verses and drew people "در دام عقیدت" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). He also records that an emperor heard from Guru Arjan, who held Baba Nanak's seat and whose name is uncertain in the printed text, "اشعار هندی تصنیف بابا نانک که در معرفت حق گفته", Hindi verses composed by Baba Nanak on the knowledge of God (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 164).

Shemeem Burney Abbas places the Guru among the poets of devotion. "The poetry of Kabir and Nanak in the fifteenth century was an outcome of these movements and led to the founding of Sikhism" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 43, folio not stated). Of the *dohā*, "an old popular Prakrit and Hindi meter in which a couplet is put together where the two verses rhyme", she writes: "Poets such as Kabir and Nanak used it to write their mystical poetry" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 141, folio not stated). Her glossary defines the *banī* by him: "Devotional verses such as those of Guru Nanak; verses of supplication, entreaty, humility, and reverence" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 179, folio not stated). The verse is sung outside the gurdwaras too. Abbas quotes the singer Abida Parvin: "I sing Shah Hussain, Bulle Shah, Mian Muhammad Bakhsh, Hazrat Khawaja Ghulam Farid, Sacchal Sarmast, Baba Farid, and Guru Nanak" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 54, folio not stated). In Sindh, Michel Boivin records, "Sain Vasan Shah became famous for reciting the banis of Shri Guru Nanak and the kalams of the Sufis" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 68, folio 55), and he reports a new edition of a Sindhi poet's *kalam* in which "Guru Nanak's poetry has been kept" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 172, folio 159).

Of the language of that poetry, Annemarie Schimmel writes of the province as a whole that "Reliable tradition traces the use of the native language for mystical poetry both in Sind and in the Punjab back to the fifteenth century" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 415, folio 384), and that after 1800, when its literary heritage was rarely continued by Muslims, Panjabi "was used almost exclusively by the Sikhs as their literary medium, whereas the Panjabi Muslims resorted to Urdu" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 427, folio 396).

## Setting: Eminabad and Saidpur

What follows is about the town, not the gurdwara. Khalid gives the old name and the new: "When Babur took over the city of Saidpur, he renamed it Eminabad." (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 115, folio 97). On the next page he gives a legend of the name: a woman known as Eni offered Babur roasted grain when he had not eaten for days, shortly before he reached Saidpur, and the city was renamed "in her honour" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 116, folio 98). The reading of her name is uncertain in the printed text.

Khalid's Eminabad is a town of old temples and faded trade. "The cone like spires of ancient Hindu temples" rise among its houses, "beautiful, in contrast to the monolithic Muslim architecture that now dominates the city of Eminabad", and its dusty roads and worn houses showed that it had lost its economic strength. "Once, it was an important trading centre on the Grand Trunk Road that connected with Lahore and then Delhi"; the highway has since moved south, and Gujranwala, not far away, has replaced Eminabad as a trading centre (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 115, folio 97). Of one temple he writes: "Despite being abandoned in 1947, the temple still looked sturdy". Such temples had been taken over by migrants from across the border after the creation of Pakistan, and Khalid looked for signs of attack after the destruction of the Babri mosque in 1992 and "found none" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 115, folio 97). He "marvelled at how much of the historical architecture had stil[l] been preserved" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 116, folio 98). After their visit to Eminabad he and Qaiser went on to Sialkot (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 191, folio 165).

Khalid's reconstruction pictures Saidpur in Nanak's day, the city that in his telling was the first goal of the Guru's travels, the city "with the grandest Hindu temples" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 108, folio 90). It had "magnificent houses and havelis" and huge temples crowned with gold tridents, with idols in every shop, Laxmi the most popular and then Ganesh (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 103, folio 85). "Saidpur was an ancient city", its temples said to have been built "thousands of years ago, long before the birth of Islam", and grown into complexes with rooms for pilgrims, leased shops, stables and "an army of servants" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 103, folio 85). There "the Brahmin pandits were the bosses", and guards kept out "naked and starving beggars" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 103–104, folios 85–86). It was a Hindu-majority city with a few Muslim households, its Hindus mostly traders and its Muslims aristocrats; "one of the most important trading centres between the Chenab and the Ravi", with "some of the tallest temples in the Punjab and also the most beautiful courtesans" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 104, folio 86). Malik Bhago lived in a "four-storey haveli known as the Shahi Qilla at Saidpur", as "a minister of the Rai of Saidpur" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 109, folio not stated). Writing in his own voice, Khalid says that Nanak's statement that there was no Hindu and no Muslim was questioned "at the court of Malik Bhago in Eminabad", as it was at Sultanpur and at Rai Bhoi di Talwandi (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 144, folio 126).

Bhandari's Persian history names a town and *pargana* spelled "امن آباد". The book does not connect it with Guru Nanak or with any shrine, and the identification with Eminabad rests on the spelling alone. Bhandari writes of the creation of the *pargana* twice on one page. In one sentence: "چند سال پیش از گجرات پرگنہ امن آباد مقرر گردید", that the *pargana* was established "چند سال پیش", some years before; the sentence also names Gujrat. In the next he credits the creation of the town and *pargana* "موسوم بامن آباد" to a revenue official, Muhammad Amin, acting on imperial order (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 176). Elsewhere a man in Kashmir who posed as "عمر شیخ میرزا پسر سلیمان میرزا" was brought before the emperor "در منزل امن آباد", at the halt of that name, and was punished there (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 177). And he places Shah Daula's bridge over the Deg, on the highway, "پنجگروهی امن اباد جانب لاهور", five *kos* from it towards Lahore; the Deg, he writes, passes the villages of the *pargana* on its way to the bridge (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 515; p. 516).

Of the district, Abbas counts "Gujranwala, Faisalabad, and Sahiwal, which are the heart of the Panjab" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 24, folio not stated). Khalid writes in *A White Trail* that Maharaja Ranjit Singh started "from a small fief around Gujranwala, a city seventy-five kilometres away from the provincial capital" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 207, folio not stated), and in *Walking with Nanak* that Ranjit Singh allotted Baba Sahib Singh Bedi land "around the cities of Gujranwala and Lahore" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 44, folio 26).

## Guru Nanak in the Wider Record

What the books say of Guru Nanak himself is said of the Guru, not of this site; it is gathered here because the shrine exists for his sake. Khalid gives his dates as 1469–1539 (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 314, folio 288), and so does Dhillon (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 9, folio 9). Dhillon has him born "On 14 April 1469" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 17–18, folios 19–20) and gives the date of his death as 22 September 1539 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 259, folio 287). Bhandari reports, with a "گویند", a birth "درسنه هزار و پانصد و بست و شش بکرماجیت مطابق هشتصد و هشتاد هجری درزمان سلطان بهلول لودی", in Samvat 1526, 880 Hijri, in the reign of Sultan Bahlul Lodi, and a death "درعهد سلطنت سلیم شاه افغان درعمر مابین هفتاد و هشتاد سالگی", in the reign of Salim Shah Afghan, between the ages of seventy and eighty (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). Abbas's book prints his birth year twice, and both times the digits are lost from the printed text (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 48, folio not stated; p. 87, folio not stated). Khalid writes that Nanak "spent a major portion of his life in present-day Pakistan. He was born here", and "eventually settled in a small village which is now on the Pakistani side of the border" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 16, folio not stated). Bhandari places "مکان بابا نانک", Baba Nanak's place, twelve *kos* from Batala on the Ravi, where his descendants still lived (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).

Dhillon sets out two schemes of the *udasis*, the Guru's journeys. "According to the Puratan Janamsakhi… he undertook five journeys", the fourth in 1519–1521 and the last in 1523–1524 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 54–55, folios 60–61); "More modern accounts" give four, spanning twenty-three years, the last of them, to the Arab countries, in 1518–1521 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 55, folio 61). Dhillon himself holds that there were four (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 56, folio 62). Khalid gives the travels as about twenty-four years, mostly on foot (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 54, folio 36), and writes that "Whenever he would visit an area he would visit its religious shrines, Muslim, Hindu and of any other denomination" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 37, folio 19).

Dhillon calls Nanak "no unworldly mystic" but "a householder in every sense of the word", who "brought a message of peace and harmony to believers of the mutually hostile religions of Islam and Hinduism" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 12, folio not stated); in his summary the Guru taught that a good life rested on honest labour, compassion, selfless service and prayer (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 12–13, folio not stated / 13). Khalid writes that Nanak "offended both Hindus and Muslims by stating that there was no Hindu or Muslim", and that his advice to his companions was that "if one is a Muslim then one should be a good Muslim and if one is a Hindu then one should be a good Hindu" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 95, folio 77). Dhillon has him say, after vanishing for a week in the stream at Sultanpur, "There is only one God. There is no Hindu, no Mussalman." (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 45–46, folios 50–51). "Guru Nanak spoke vehemently against religious institutions and rituals", Khalid writes; he associated with "people of lowercastes, terming them people of God", and criticised "high-caste landlords and government officials as exploiters" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 82, folio 64). In *A White Trail* Khalid calls the movement that began with Nanak "a spiritual reformation movement" and "a rebellion against institutional religion" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 219, folio not stated).

Rizvi takes up the question of the Sufism the Guru knew. He quotes W. H. McLeod's view that "the Panjabi sufism of Guru Nanak's period had evidently departed radically from the classical pattern of Arab and Persian Sufism", in order to reject it (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 419–420, folios 395–396). Against it Rizvi argues that "all literate Panjabis, both Hindus and Muslims, at the time of Guru Nanak were conversant with" the poetry of 'Attar, Rumi and Sa'di, and that McLeod "seems to be unaware of" Shaikh 'Abdu'l-Quddus Gangohi of Shahabad, of his disciple Shaikh Jalal of Thaneswar, and of the Qadiris "who had made the Panjab the main centre of their activities" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 420, folio 396). Abbas writes that Baba Farid's "texts are revered among the Sikhs because he influenced the founder of their own religion, Baba Guru Nanak" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 87, folio not stated), and Khalid that Farid "became an inspiration for Guru Nanak when he visited the shrine during his travels" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).

Muslim writers and singers have claimed the Guru too. Khalid writes that "many Muslims, acquainted with the teachings of Guru Nanak, are devoted to him too and visit his shrine" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 220, folio not stated), and his informant Nadeem, a Muslim painter, says "Guru Nanak is like a Muslim Sufi Saint" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 219, folio not stated). A Muslim tradition, reported by Iqbal Qaiser, holds that "Guru Nanak was born by the blessing of Baba Naulakh Hazari"; "The Sikhs don't accept it" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated). Robert Rozehnal reports that a twentieth-century Chishti Sabiri shaykh, in certain lectures, describes "non-Muslim spiritual luminaries—Krishna, Rama, Sita, Lachman, and Guru Nanak—as believers in the one true God and religious leaders with 'Divine connection' (nisbat)" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 63, folio 53). Boivin finds "a growing similarity between the iconography of Guru Nanak and Jhulelal" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 54, folio 41), and reports the view that "it is a seductive hypothesis that the Bhakti could have permeated Sindh through the teaching of Guru Nanak" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 90, folio 77). Omar Kasmani, citing Boivin, notes that the trope of the flower in milk is told also of "Guru Nanak, the spiritual leader and founder of Sikhism" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 195, folio 178, ch. 5 n. 17). At the shrine of the goddess Hinglaj, Jürgen Schaflechner records "the engraved footsteps of Guru Nanak" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 256, folio not stated), which "connect Hinglaj to the Sikh and Nanak paṃth (Hin. sect) traditions" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 257, folio not stated).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **When Saidpur was sacked.** The site's account says the Guru's imprisonment is traditionally dated to around 1520-21. Rizvi dates Babur's third invasion, which reached as far as Sialkot, to 926/1520 (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 409, folio 385). The line of the *Babur Bani* that Khalid quotes glosses the invaders' coming as 1521 (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 113–114, folios 95–96). Dhillon sets the Guru's return through Saidpur at Babur's third incursion and gives no year (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 224, folio 246); his two schemes of the journeys end the fourth in 1521 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 54–55, folios 60–61; p. 55, folio 61).
- **What the site was.** The site's account says the gurdwara marks the place where a great millstone associated with the Guru was kept. Iqbal Qaiser told Khalid that the house "was used as a prison at the time of Babur", where Guru Nanak was imprisoned and forced to work on a stone grinder (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 116, folio 98).
- **How the millstone turned.** The site's account says that it is remembered that the Guru's millstone turned of its own accord while he poured in the grain and sang. The Janamsakhis, as Khalid gives them, have the grinder move by itself "using his miraculous powers" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 114, folio 96). Qaiser, at the house, says only that the Guru "was forced to work on a stone grinder" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 116, folio 98).
- **Whether the Guru met Babur, and what passed between them.** The site's account says the emperor came to the Guru, sought his blessing and, at the Guru's urging, freed many prisoners. In Khalid's summary of the Janamsakhis, Babur sent for the Guru, and at their meeting asked for a blessing to conquer India and was rebuked (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 114, folio 96). Qaiser holds that Nanak may very well never have met Babur, and calls such stories "later constructions by his devotees" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 116, folio 98).
- **The names of the town.** The site's account and Khalid write Eminabad and Saidpur (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 115, folio 97); the plaque the site's account reports reads "Eimanabad". Rizvi writes "Saiyidpur (Amanabad)" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 410, folio 386), and his index "Amnabad" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 462, folio 438). Bhandari's Persian spells the town "امن آباد" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 176). Khalid gives two accounts of the renaming: by Babur on taking the city (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 115, folio 97), and in honour of a woman, Eni, who fed him (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 116, folio 98).
- **Who created the pargana of Amanabad.** Bhandari, on one page, says in one sentence that the *pargana* was established some years before, in a sentence that also names Gujrat, and in the next that a revenue official, Muhammad Amin, established the town and *pargana* by imperial order (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 176).
- **The Guru's dates.** Khalid and Dhillon give 1469–1539 (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 314, folio 288; Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 9, folio 9), Dhillon with a birth on 14 April 1469 and a death on 22 September 1539 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 17–18, folios 19–20; p. 259, folio 287). Bhandari gives the birth in Samvat 1526, 880 Hijri, in the reign of Bahlul Lodi, and the death under Salim Shah Afghan, between the ages of seventy and eighty (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).
- **How many journeys.** Dhillon reports five *udasis* in the Puratan Janamsakhi and four in more modern accounts, and holds to four himself (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 54–55, folios 60–61; p. 56, folio 62). Khalid gives the travels as about twenty-four years (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 54, folio 36); Dhillon's modern scheme spans twenty-three (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 55, folio 61).
- **Whether the Guru worked wonders.** The site's account and the Janamsakhis, as Khalid gives them, tell of the self-turning millstone (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 114, folio 96), and Bhandari writes that wonders appeared in him in his early years (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). Dhillon writes of the Guru's "clear and emphatic disavowal of the miraculous" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 11, folio 11), and Khalid that Nanak "abhorred the concept of associating miracles with religious personalities" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 16, folio not stated).

## Legacy

Gurdwara Chakki Sahib preserves the memory of the millstone and of the Guru's witness against the cruelty of war, and through the *Babar-vani* it ties Eminabad to the Guru's hymns lamenting the horrors visited upon the land. For Khalid, the poem of that invasion was the way into the Guru himself. Born and brought up in Pakistani Punjab, he had known Guru Nanak as the founder of Sikhism, "Yet given the Muslim-dominant culture of Pakistani Punjab, there were no avenues available for me within the culture to explore his personality" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 15, folio not stated); he found the Guru "my own, being a Punjabi" and yet "an alien, since he was a non-Muslim", until Qaiser's recitation of the *Babur Bani* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 15–16, folio not stated).

Writing of Pakistan as a whole, Khalid says that "there are several sites all over the country where Nanak stayed or performed miracles. Gurdwaras that were built there later by the devotees still stand tall" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 168–169, folio not stated). Of other such places after the division of the Punjab he is less hopeful: "I have seen several gurdwaras and temples being taken over and destroyed by the migrants" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated). At Eminabad, the house Qaiser showed him still stood, small and locked, under its Khalsa flag (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 116, folio 98).
