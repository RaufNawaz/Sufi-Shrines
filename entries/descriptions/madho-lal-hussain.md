## Overview

In Baghbanpura, close by the Shalimar Gardens on the eastern edge of Lahore, stands the shrine of Hazrat Madho Lal Hussain — the joint name of two men, and the sign of one of the most extraordinary bonds in the spiritual history of the Punjab. Within it rest Shah Hussain (1538–1599), the great Malamati Sufi and father of the Punjabi *kafi*, and Madho Lal, the Hindu youth he loved and whose name he joined for ever to his own. Around the darbar has grown one of the largest festivals in the region, the Mela Chiraghan or “Festival of Lamps,” and from it has flowed a body of Punjabi mystical verse that ordinary people still sing four centuries on. The shrine is at once a place of ecstatic devotion, a monument of Punjabi language and poetry, and a beloved emblem of love that overleaps the boundaries of caste and creed. To understand it, one must begin with the strange and luminous life of Shah Hussain.

The books consulted know him by many spellings, and one of them knows him as two men. Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori's *Hadiqat al-Awliya* gives the saint and his beloved two separately numbered entries: no. 17, headed Shaykh Husain, known as Lal Husain Lahori (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 54–57, folios 42–45), and no. 24, listed as Shaykh Madho Lahori (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 15, folio 3). The joint name occurs there only inside the parenthesis of an index line, which gives Husain Lahori, Shah, with "Madho Lal Husain" in brackets (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 317, folio 305), and in the title of the editor's own encyclopaedia article, "Madho Lal Husain" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 303, folio 291). The book's link between the two men is a footnote in Husain's entry sending the reader to Madho's account, and the naming of the first *Diwan* as Shaykh Madho, the Hazrat's beloved (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 55, folio 43).

Naveed Alam, who translates his verse as *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, writes "Shah Hussein" and "Madho Lal Hussein" throughout, and begins with "The portrait of Shah Hussein (1538–99), popularly known as Madho Lal Hussein" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 6, folio 6). "Weaver, mystic, fakir, Shah Hussein continues to command great reverence in Punjab as a poet–saint more than 400 years after his death," he writes (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 6, folio 6), and: "His urs, or death anniversary, is still celebrated at his shrine in Baghbanpura, Lahore. In the inner sanctum of the shrine, buried right next to Hussein, is his young beloved and disciple, Madho (pronounced Madh-oh) Lal" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 6, folio 6). Scott Kugle, whose fourth chapter in *Sufis and Saints' Bodies* is called 'The Lips of Shah Hussayn' (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 8, folio not stated), writes "Shah Hussayn" and calls him "a Sufi who will become one of Lahore's patron saints" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 204, folio 183). Annemarie Schimmel writes "Madho Lal Husain" and records that he "is buried together with his Hindu beloved in a modest tomb near the Shalimar gardens in Lahore" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, pp. 170–171, folios 152–153).

## The Life of Shah Hussain

Hussain was born in Lahore in 1538 into a family of weavers — his father, by tradition, a craftsman who stitched uniforms for the army of the Emperor Humayun. He was a child of unusual gifts and piety: he is said to have committed the Qur’an to memory by the age of seven, and at fifteen to have begun teaching recitation (tajweed) and preaching, leading the congregational prayers and the long Taraweeh prayers of Ramadan. For some twenty years he lived the life of an exact and disciplined scholar of the outward law, his devotion so evident that learned men sought his company and came away loving him. In his spiritual training he is remembered as a disciple of the Sufi master Bahlol (Bahlol Daryai) and as a devoted frequenter of the shrine of Data Ganj Bakhsh; there, the accounts say, he withdrew into a long spiritual retreat, keeping *chilla* by the darbar of Lahore’s patron saint for some twelve years, absorbed in the discipline of divine love (*ishq-e-haqiqi*), while those who came to sit with him took to calling him, simply, “Baba.”

The books agree that he was born in Lahore and differ over the year. Zahur ul-Hasan Sharib writes that he was born in 945 AH in Lahore (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 249, folio 245), and Lahori's author gives the same year in words, nine hundred and forty-five (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 55–56, folios 43–44). Alam gives 1538 (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 6, folio 6). Kugle writes "He was born around 1539 C.E." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 206, folio 185, ch. 4 n. 7); Taufiq Rafat's selection of Bulleh Shah gives "Shah Husain (1539-1593 A.D.)" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 24, folio not stated), and Schimmel's *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* gives 1539–93 (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 415, folio 384). Nur Ahmad Chishti's *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* records the birth only in a chronogram by Pir Muhammad, part of whose line cannot be read (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 45), and in a verse of the author's own on the year of his birth, which opens as follows, its last word uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 46):

بسال مولودش از سروش آل[OCR?]

No year can be read from either. Kanhaiya Lal's *Tarikh-e Lahore* places him simply as a *salik* living in Lahore in Akbar's era (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 233, folio 236).

For his family the books give several accounts. Lahori's author writes that his grandfather, whose printed name, perhaps Kaljas Rai, cannot be read with certainty, was a Hindu who accepted Islam in the time of Firoz Shah Tughlaq; that his son Usman was a religious man who earned lawful sustenance by the work of weavers; and that Shaykh Husain was born in his house (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 54, folio 42). Kanhaiya Lal gives his caste as a weaver, a word of it uncertain in the printed text, and says that among his forebears one whose printed name, perhaps Kamas Rai, cannot be read with certainty was a Hindu who became Muslim in an era whose printed name cannot be read with certainty, and that this Husain was his grandson (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 233, folio 236). Sharib writes that his forebears were Kayasth, the word uncertain in the printed text, and makes the convert his father, whose printed name, perhaps Kalsan Rai, cannot be read with certainty, who accepted Islam in the reign of Sultan Firoz Shah, was named Shaykh Usman, and wove cloth; he gives no date for the conversion and does not name the mother (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 249, folio 245). Chishti gives the name by which the saint is known as Dhadha Husain, the first word uncertain in the printed text, and glosses Dhadha as a caste among the Rajputs; he traces the paternal descent to an ancestor whose printed name, perhaps Kalshatar, cannot be read with certainty, who became Muslim and was titled Shaykh al-Islam, gives the father's name as Usman, again printed uncertainly, and gives his trade as weaving (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 45).

Alam writes that "Hussein's father, a Rajput from the Kalsrai clan, converted to Islam during the Delhi Sultanate (1206–1526) and took on the Muslim name of Sheikh Usman" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 6, folio 6), and takes the mother's side from Dara Shikoh, who "calls him Hussein Dadha, using Hussein's matrilineal family name (his mother belonged to the Dadha clan of Rajputs)" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 8, folio 8); "Sheikh, the scholar, is also the family name of Hussein" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 181, folio 181). Kugle rejects the Rajput descent. "Hussayn belonged to a community of weavers, who had as a caste converted to Islam only a few generations before" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 228, folio 207, ch. 4 n. 52); "His family had joined Islam only a few generations before without claiming any Persian ancestry or educational ties." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 218, folio 197). Biographers like Ramakrishna, he writes, have made him a Rajput or Kayasth on the strength of later Urdu sources that put his family in the Dahda Rajput clan, but "these castes did not engage in weaving", and "The castes of weavers who converted to Islam in the medieval centuries had most likely been Shudras." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 329, folio 308, ch. 4 n. 52). He concludes: "The stories of Hussayn's 'noble' lineage are later accretions to make his image more acceptable to society, as are the stories of Madho's 'conversion' to Islam." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 329, folio 308, ch. 4 n. 52). Alam, for his part, says that the hagiographers "are not concerned with Hussein, the poet, or Hussein, the self-proclaimed weaver, but remain fixated on Hussein, the saint" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 8, folio 8).

The poems name the trade too. In Alam's English the weaver is addressed by name:

*O Hussein, the weaver! He earned no profit* (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 176, folio 176)

On the same page comes the poems' one run of biographical negatives:

*Never got engaged, never got married / Never fixed a date, never held a wedding / Neither a householder nor a traveller / Neither a man of faith nor an infidel / He is what he is* (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 176, folio 176)

Kugle quotes, from Shuja Alhaq's *Forgotten Vision*:

*O Friend, Hussayn is a weaver, no profit accrues to him / Neither was he betrothed nor married / Nor was any matrimony ceremony held for him* (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 328, folio 307, ch. 4 n. 26)

Of his schooling the accounts agree on a mosque and a teacher named Abu Bakr, and differ over his age. Lahori's author writes that at the age of seven, while he was reading the Quran with a learned man named Abu Bakr, Shaykh Bahlul happened to pass through that mosque, and his glance so fell on Husain that he became a perfect *wali* while still a child; the editor adds in a footnote that *Haqiqat al-Fuqara* instead has him become the pupil of Hafiz Abu Bakr at the age of ten, in 955 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 54, folio 42). Chishti has Hazrat Lal Husain ten years old (the reading uncertain in the printed text) when Bahlul came, memorising with Maulvi Abu Bakr and with the seventh *sipara* done, and puts his *maktab* in a mohalla of Lahore by the Taksali Gate and near the river, the description partly uncertain and partly illegible in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 50). Elsewhere he locates outside the Taksali Gate, the name again uncertain in the printed text, where the Takia of Sher Ali Shah now is, a mohalla that held in the time of the emperors Babur and Akbar the residence of Hazrat Husain's mother, and in its mosque, he writes, the late Hazrat Husain used to take his Quran lessons with Maulvi Abu Bakr, the teacher's name uncertain and some words illegible in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 101). Sharib sends him to the *maktab* at seven, where he memorised six *paras* from Hafiz Abu Bakr and read part of the *Tafsir-e Madarik* with Shaykh Saad Allah; but he could not keep his schooling up long (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 249, folio 245). Alam writes: "As a boy, Hussein joined the local mosque which also functioned as a madrasa, where memorizing the Quran by rote was the main method of learning" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 6, folio 6). For Kugle, his study as a youth "consisted of memorizing the Qur'an, learning Arabic, and studying scriptural and legal interpretation" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 206, folio 185).

How the boy came to finish the Quran is told as a wonder. Sharib relates that Bahlol announced he would recite the Quran that Ramadan, and the boy objected that this could not be, for he knew only six *paras* by heart; sent to the river, he met an elder, unnamed in the book, who had him pour water over his hands and, pleased, splashed water on his face, and by the blessing of it he recited the whole Quran that Ramadan (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 250, folio 246). After the Ramadan recitation Bahlol made him *imam* (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 250, folio 246). In Chishti's telling a *majzub* had ordered Bahlul to go from there by way of Lahore and show Husain the path of guidance, a word of the order uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 49); the boy had recited all he knew by the seventh day of the *tarawih*, and the river water and a prayer that he be made a gnostic *faqir* followed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 50), and he completed the Quran that Ramadan (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 51–52). Chishti dates Bahlul's coming, after Hazrat Pir Muhammad Sahib's *Haqiqat al-Fuqara* (the title uncertain in the printed text), with a line of verse that gives the year as nine hundred and fifty-five but names no era (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 52):

سال پنجاہ و پنج و نہ صد بود

Kugle gives another source of the gift: "Shah Hussayn himself is reported to have met the perennial spiritual guide Khidr in his boyhood. Khidr poured out some water from his jug into his own palm, and Hussayn drank from his hand. This transmission of spiritual blessing allowed him to finish memorizing the Qur'an with ease and eventually pushed him toward sainthood in his adult life." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 216, folio 195, ch. 4 n. 23).

Of his master the books say much. Lahori calls him the *khalifa* of Shaykh Bahlul Daryai, famous as a man of *hal*, *jazb*, longing, taste, *wajd* and *sama* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 54, folio 42), and in another entry records that Lal Husain showed Shaykh Musa Khokhar the utmost respect because they were pir-brothers (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 273, folio 261). Kanhaiya Lal writes that he became the *murid* of Shaykh Bahlol Qadri (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 233, folio 236). Sharib calls his pir Hazrat Shaykh Bahlol, the *qutb* of his age (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 249, folio 245), and writes that Bahlol made him a *murid* and honoured him with the *khirqa* of *khilafat*, then took his leave (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 250, folio 246). Chishti carries the chain of *bai'at* from Lal Husain to Hazrat Bahlul, and on to Hazrat Shah Latif Barri, then Shah Muhammad Muqim and Hazrat Hayat al-Mir Qadiri, a descendant of Hazrat Ghaus al-Azam, several of these names uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 46–47), and elsewhere names Hazrat Madho Lal Husain Sahib, a word before the name illegible, second among the *khalifas* of Hazrat Bilawal Daryai, after Hazrat Musa Khokhar, all three names printed uncertainly (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 417). Alam writes of "Sheikh Bahlol Dariai, who took him under his wing", a dervish who "had travelled across the wide expanse of the fifteenth-century Islamic world, including Arabia, Mesopotamia, Persia and Afghanistan" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, pp. 6–7, folios 6–7). Haroon Khalid reports his guide Iqbal Qaiser's view that Behlol, whose shrine is at 'Tiba Shah Behlol', "was the spiritual master of Shah Hussain" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated).

The years by Data Ganj Bakhsh are, in Sharib's account, an order from that master. Before leaving, Bahlol instructed him to present himself regularly at the luminous *mazar* of Hazrat Data Ganj Bakhsh (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 250, folio 246), and he gave attendance there with great regularity for twelve years, and in the end the recompense came to him (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 251, folio 247). Chishti too has him at dawn and *ishraq* at Hujwiri's khanqah and *mazar*, twelve years there (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 53). Kugle writes: "For the first half of his life, he lived as a mendicant student, wandering in the empty lands outside the city walls by day and returning at night to stay in the shrine of Lahore's patron saint", ʿAli Hujwiri, known as Data Ganj Bakhsh (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 206, folio 185).

The discipline of those years the books describe hour by hour. Sharib gives twenty-six years in worship, *riyazat* and *mujahida*, with a whole Quran completed daily (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 250, folio 246), and a timetable: nights in recitation; stillness at the riverbank and at Data Ganj Bakhsh's *mazar*; after the dawn prayer to the *maktab* for the *tafsir* lesson until Asr; *zikr* after Asr; *nafl* between Maghrib and Isha; and no variation even in illness (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 250–251, folios 246–247). Chishti calls him one who fasted always and stood in prayer all night (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 53), and later has him reciting the Quran until *chasht*, taking the *qailula*, teaching *tafsir* until Asr, and spending the night in the river (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 54). Kugle's portrait of the ascetic before the change: "He used to stand up to his waist in the Ravi River, lips blue from cold, while reciting the Qur'an all day." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 221, folio 200).

Then, around the age of thirty-two, everything changed. The scrupulous scholar became a *malang* — a wandering, dishevelled ecstatic. He set aside the robes and the reputation of a preacher, and took up instead the crimson dress by which he is remembered as “Lal” (the Red) Hussain, dancing the *dhamal* and carrying a flask in his hand that the scandalised took for wine. What had happened, in the language of the Sufis, was that the outward path of the *Shariat* had opened, for Hussain, onto the burning inward path of love — and he chose to walk it in the most confronting way he could.

The books tell the change as the work of one verse of the Quran. Lahori's editor, following *Haqiqat al-Fuqara*, writes that Husain, at first an orthodox Muslim, was reading the *Tafsir-e Madarik* with his teacher Shaykh Saad Allah Lahori, who died in 999 AH / 1590 CE, when the teacher explained the verse that the life of this world is nothing but sport and play, and Shah Husain understood the world to be mere play; he told his teacher that he needed *hal*, not *qal*, abandoned the student's life and took to the Malamati way (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 56, folio 44). The editor adds that Saad Allah himself often voiced the Malamati school, and quotes Nizam al-Din Ahmad's *Tabaqat-e Akbari* on him, that he walked the Sufi path in the Malamati manner (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 56, folio 44). Chishti has the *tafsir* with Shaykh Saad Allah, the *Madarik* and the *Nasafi* (both titles uncertain in the printed text), the verse of sport and play, dancing and a dispute (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 54–55); he left the madrasa dancing (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 55), and from that day, he writes, Husain took up the Malamati way so that people would hold him in aversion (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 56). In Sharib, after Shaykh Sa'dullah's exposition that the life of this world is sport and play, an ecstatic state came over him; he began to dance, threw his books into a well, was rid of beard and moustache, went about the lanes and bazaars intoxicated with a cup in his hand, frequented courtesans, and in sum had taken up the Malamati way, the word uncertain in the printed text (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 251, folio 247).

The English books put the turn later. Alam sets it "at the age of thirty-six, while studying with another respected scholar of the time, Sheikh Sa'ad Ullah" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 7, folio 7), cites the passages as Sura Al-Hadid 57:20 and Sura Ash-Sharh 94:1–4, and writes: "The words resonated so deeply with Hussein that he broke into a spontaneous dance of self-liberation"; and again, "In the middle of the prayer, Hussein broke into laughter and ran away when he recited these Quranic verses" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 7, folio 7). Kugle writes: "It was at the age of 36 that he experienced a dramatic spiritual opening", when the verse "The life of the world is nothing but play and pleasurable distraction" was recited (Q 6:32, echoed in 29:64 and 47:36), after which he resolved "to throw off constraints of ascetic piety, legal rectitude, and rational seriousness; he would instead lead his life as a child at play" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 206, folio 185). Sa'dullah, Kugle adds, "argued with him not to take literally" the verse, insisting that its tone was sarcastic (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 232, folio 211).

The master came to see for himself. In Sharib's chapter, headed for the episode "The Test", Bahlol heard at Chiniot that Husain drank, stayed intoxicated and had strayed from the *shariat*; he came to Lahore, saw him, and went back satisfied (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 251–252, folios 247–248). Kugle's Shaykh Bahlul "rushed to Lahore, ready to join Sa'dullah in urging him to return to 'the straight path'", but on seeing "Hussayn in ripped red clothes, dancing and drinking wine", he "realized that this was his personal path to religious sincerity. The Shaykh blessed him and returned to his home." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 232, folio 211). Chishti has Bahlul satisfied that Hazrat Husain had attained to God (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 56); after Bahlul's death, he writes, Husain remained in Lahore, as before, a resort of the common people, danced to the *chang* and *rabab* to conceal his state, and recited the Quran every night in the first watch, the wording in places uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 57).

## The Way of Blame

Hussain belonged, in spirit, to the *Malamatiyya* — the Sufis of the “path of blame,” who deliberately drew the censure of respectable society upon themselves in order to kill the ego’s hunger for esteem, hiding their inner sanctity beneath an outward show of impropriety. To dance and sing and carry a flask through the streets of Mughal Lahore was, for such a mystic, not a fall from grace but a discipline of self-effacement, a refusal of the world’s praise. Inevitably it drew fatwas and denunciations, and at last complaints reached the imperial court: some demanded that this man who seemed to mock the outward forms of religion be put to death.

The most famous story of his life belongs to this moment. Summoned before the ruler — the accounts name the Emperor Jahangir — and challenged over the wine he was said to carry, Hussain is said to have poured out his flask into the cups set before the court: and the liquid ran, in the first cup, as milk; in the second, as honey; and in the third, as the sacred water of Zamzam. The king and his courtiers, astonished, understood that they stood before no ordinary man. “Let him be,” they said; “he belongs to another world — let no one trouble him after this day.” The saint went his way, and the tale has been told ever since as a sign that what the world condemns in the friends of God it does not understand.

The red dress gave him his name, and the books say so in nearly the same words. Lahori writes that his path was *Malamati*, he wore red dress, and so became famous as Lal Husain; his editor glosses the Malamatiyya as the name of a silsila of free-spirited sufis (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 54, folio 42). Kanhaiya Lal writes that because he wore red clothing he received the title Lal Husain (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 233, folio 236). Kugle calls 'Lal Hussayn' the saint's own nickname, "alluding to his penchant for wearing only red clothing" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 328, folio 307, ch. 4 n. 24). Alam pictures "a rebel fakir dressed in all red, bearing a wine flask in one hand and an earthen bowl in another" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 7, folio 7), notes that "Shah Hussein is known to wander around in a red dress" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 180, folio 180), and reads the red into the poetry as "the colour of passion: lal (red)" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 13, folio 13).

*Malamati* is Alam's word for his path, and he glosses it more than once: "the promising young student turned malamati—one who seeks disgrace and censure—leading his pack of revellers through the town" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 7, folio 7); "as the prescribed norm of a malamati mystic" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 9, folio 9); "For a malamati, the ignominy ensures the grace" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 10, folio 10); and, in his notes to the poems, "Shah Hussein's humility cuts like an axe", "for he's a malamati, blameworthy, an outcast, a rebel, a transgressor of the bogus conventions of love, the one who breaks free" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 180, folio 180). "Hussein's malamati-style self-humiliation reaches its apogee in the line: I'm the bitch at your doorstep" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 11, folio 11). Haroon Khalid writes of "a wandering malamati Sufi in Lahore known by the name of Shah Hussain, a spectacular Punjabi poet" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated), and the introduction to Rafat's selection of Bulleh Shah hears in that later poet "The malamatia overtones, reminiscent of his great predecessor Shah Husain" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 24, folio not stated). Sharib joins the blame to a second word: he had adopted the Malamati way and his *mashrab* was *qalandarana* (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 254, folio 250). Chishti calls Hazrat Lal Husain's way *qalandarana*, with two words before it that cannot be read with certainty (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 610).

Two of the English books give him an order other than the Malamati. Kugle calls him "a Sufi of the Qadiri community and a Punjabi poet" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 202, folio not stated), writes that "He also joined the Qadiri Sufi lineage at a young age, in which he learned the value of poetic eloquence and ascetic rigor" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 206, folio 185, ch. 4 n. 7), and says that Shaykh Bahlul Darya'i initiated him into the Qadiri lineage at Chiniot, a village outside Lahore, when he was about ten years old; the Qadiris, he adds, had a reputation for being 'orthodox', but in Shah Hussayn's day they also included freethinking and antinomian characters (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 326, folio 305, ch. 4 n. 7). Kugle never calls him Malamati. He calls him a Qalandar, as a mode of life: "Shah Hussayn was one of these recklessly bold Sufis known by the label 'Qalandar,' whom we know as antinomian" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 203, folio 182); "Shah Hussayn's red clothes and his face shaved of facial hair marked him as a Qalandar." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 220, folio 199); "Shah Hussayn represents a long tradition in Sufism of Qalandar rebelliousness and homoerotic sensuality" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 229, folio 208). Schimmel calls him "the ecstatic mystic of the Qadiri order" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, pp. 170–171, folios 152–153), and Kanhaiya Lal, as above, gives a pir, Bahlol Qadri, rather than an order (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 233, folio 236).

What the blame looked like in the streets the books describe in lists. Kugle: "a widely known romance with another man, and he combined them with public forms of abandon: singing, drumming, dancing, wine drinking, shaving off all facial hair, trespassing divisions of social class, voluntary poverty. He displayed these practices in public to draw out from others their hypocrisy." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, pp. 231–232, folios 210–211). "He took to drinking wine" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 207, folio 186); "In addition to drinking wine in public, Shah Hussayn took to singing and playing musical instruments while dancing in the streets with his band of devoted friends and followers" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 207, folio 186), all "on very public display in the streets of sixteenth-century Lahore" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 206, folio 185). His reputation as a libertine, *laund*, and a drunk, *rind*, Kugle writes, was widely known (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 232, folio 211). Kugle also quotes a hagiographic description: "He wears red clothes, drinks wine openly, sings and plays music, and is surrounded by smooth-faced young men as beautiful as the moon. He dances with them in the street in front of his home. He shaves his beard off but lets his mustache grow long." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 233, folio 212, ch. 4 n. 57). In Alam's portrait "drunkenly he roams the streets of Lahore in the company of fellow carousers" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 6, folio 6). Sharib writes that he had a great fondness for dance and song, and that he kept himself hidden in the dress of *faqr*. He speaks of daily recitation in the morning, nights passed in laughter and weeping, and the use of intoxicating things as well (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 254, folio 250).

Chishti preserves his sayings on the word *faqr*, letter by letter: *f* for *faqr*, *faqa* and *fana*, poverty, hunger and annihilation; *q* for *qanaʿat*, contentment, and *qurb-e haqq*, nearness to God; *r* for *riyazat*, discipline, and *riza*, acceptance; and he records that the *sajjada nashin* holds a notebook in the saint's own hand on the five letters of *darvesh*, such as *d* for *dard-e dil*, pain of the heart, and *sh* for *shukr-e haqq*, thanks to God, and *sharm*, modesty (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 46).

Lahori's editor gives the life a different close. It is a fact, he writes, citing Abdullah Khweshgi Qasuri's *Maarij al-Wilayat*, that in his last years Shah Husain repented of all acts contrary to the *shariah*, became regular in fasting and prayer, and died in that state (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 56, folio 44). He quotes Shaykh Muhammad Tahir Lahori, who died in 1040 AH, an extremely *shariah*-bound *khalifa* of the Mujaddid Alf-e Sani: "If I did not fear the taunts of the exoteric ulama, I would often go to Shaikh Husain's mazar and seek help", and takes it both as confirmation of the repentance and as clear proof that Shah Husain was an orthodox Muslim whose *wilayat* was accepted (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 56, folio 44).

## Before the Police Chief, the Judge and the Emperor

Kugle tells the confrontation with authority as a story in three trials. "These actions inevitably brought Shah Hussayn in conflict with the authorities of Lahore: the police chief, the chief judge, and finally the emperor himself." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 233, folio 212). It opens in "a large public square" before the high walls of the Red Fort in Lahore, where crowds come to see the execution of "rebels against the Mughal emperor, highway robbers, and tax evaders"; "Suddenly, a very different sort of man is brought forward in chains" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 203, folio 182). "He wears ragged scarlet clothes. His head is shaved, and his beard is shaved clean. He walks into the public square, laughing without fear" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 204, folio 183). Kugle does not date the scene, and does not say that the sentence was carried out (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 204, folio 183).

The police chief of Lahore, the *kotwal* whom Kugle names Malik Ali (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 239, folio 218), had been asked to keep a watch on Hussayn's misdeeds (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 233, folio 212). He was administering a public punishment in the market when Hussayn, by the side of the road, was caught in flirtatious eye contact with a handsome young man who proved to be the police chief's own son, and the chief "arrested him and had him chained up, charging him with being a heretical innovator in religious matters" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 233, folio 212). He threatened Hussayn with impaling, "a gruesome pantomime of anal rape" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 204, folio 183). In the verse Kugle quotes, the threat runs:

*yours will be a disgraceful death, driven through with a spike / A spike that will go into you from behind / so that your soul will leave you from behind* (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 234, folio 213, ch. 4 n. 58)

Hussayn, "laughing like a woman", answered in verse:

*For what reason do you treat me like this / what sin have I committed, you stupid ass?* (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 233, folio 212)

*I may be a sinner in respect to the religious law / but how have I injured you or the king who sent you? / If my actions are a sin, then I sin against God / but I commit no crime against anyone, you or your king!* (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 233, folio 212)

His poetry, Kugle writes elsewhere, sounded "as laughter in the face of distress through which he confronted the police chief and other critics" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 225, folio 204). The hagiographic sources, he says, picture Shah Hussayn confronting the police chief with a miracle: "the chains kept falling from his ankles no matter how the police chief struggled to secure them." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 239, folio 218). The end of the story belongs to the same sources: "In the end, the police chief himself is impaled to death upon the command of the emperor for having failed him in some unrelated matter." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 239, folio 218). In the opening scene, too, drawn from "a biographical poem on the Sufi's life", "As the police drag him into the square, the chains keep falling off his wrists and ankles" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 204, folio 183).

The second trial was a judge's. "His case came before the Shaykh al-Islam, or chief religious scholar and jurist of the realm, Abd al-Nabi Sultanpuri. The judge approached Shah Hussayn as a 'heretic' rather than for sexual improprieties and questioned him about the pillars of Islam." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 239, folio 218). The answer, in Kugle's English: the first, belief in one God, both men keep; "The second and third are prayer and fasting, which I ignore openly"; the fourth and fifth, "pilgrimage and alms, which you ignore in secret!" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 239, folio 218, ch. 4 n. 66). The counter-charges against the judge were his ruling "that the hajj to Mecca was no longer obligatory for all Muslims" and the allegation that he avoided paying the *zakat* due on the wealth he had accumulated (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 240, folio 219).

Sharib tells the same answer of the *qazi* of Lahore, who saw him dancing to a drum and wished to reprimand him. Of the five pillars, the saint answered, the first, the profession of God's unity and of the Prophet's mission, we share; the second and third, prayer and fasting, I have abandoned; the fourth and fifth, *zakat* and *hajj*, you have abandoned (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 252, folio 248). On the next page, among the closing pages of the chapter, the *qazi* smiled at the answer and went away (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 253, folio 249).

The third trial was the emperor's, and the books that name him name Akbar. Sharib writes that Akbar summoned him to court after complaints of his *qalandarana mashrab*, questioned him, and dismissed him with honour and respect (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 253, folio 249). For Kugle, "The emperor called him to court, upon accusations of trickery and rebellion." "The royal inquisition centered on whether Shah Hussayn drank wine in public and whether he was miraculously able to turn wine into milk, water, tea, or punch." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 240, folio 219). According to the hagiographic record, he says, the saint was granted a miracle that turned the emperor's goblet of wine into water and other beverages, and the emperor was convinced (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 240, folio 219). "Akbar acknowledged Hussayn as a great saint and one of the pillars of Lahore's spiritual well-being" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 241, folio 220). Alam gives the legend another turn: "According to legend, the emperor was unimpressed by the wine-to-non-alcoholic-drinks miracle performed by Hussein when he was brought before Akbar for interrogation, and sent him to prison. While Hussein was in the dock, his likeness showed up in the harem attended upon by the royal ladies. Mystified by the miraculous feat, Akbar acknowledged Hussein's credentials as a saint, and set him free." (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, pp. 7–8, folios 7–8). His composite portrait from traditional sources has the saint who "breaks off the chains when officials approach to arrest him, baffles Akbar, the reigning emperor of the time, by appearing in his private chambers when he's supposed to be locked up", and who "turns wine into water, sherbet, milk, tea, vinegar and back to wine again" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 6, folio 6). "According to Muhammad Pir's Haqiqat al-Fuqra, when Emperor Akbar asked Hussein for any wish to be granted, Hussein requested he never be summoned to the royal court again" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 181, folio 181), which Alam offers as evidence of a 'note of anti-establishment'.

Chishti, citing *Haqiqat al-Fuqara*, gives the charge laid before Akbar: that he shaved off his beard, wore red clothes, drank wine and went about dancing, and laid claim to *wilayat*, some words uncertain or illegible in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 63). He names among the saint's devotees Prince Salim, with Khwaja Daulat Khan, the Khan-e Khanan and Abu al-Fazl (the last two names uncertain in the printed text) among others, says that the saint brought no petition to anyone, and that Akbar became his servant heart and soul (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 65); at the head of the miracles he sets Hazrat Nur al-Din Jahangir Badshah (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 59). Alam notes the silence of the court's own records: "There is no account of Hussein in the annals of Akbar, though the Mughals were known for their meticulous record-keeping", and "No mention of Hussein is made even in Tuzk-i-Jehangiri" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 8, folio 8).

A Mughal noble's errand is told three ways. Kugle: "As one of Akbar's chief nobles, Abd al-Rahim Khan-i Khanan, was traveling with an army toward Sindh, he stopped in Lahore to receive the blessing of Shah Hussayn. The Mughal armies successfully conquered Thatta in Sindh, and Hussayn's reputation spread." "The admiration of Mughal nobles protects him from local censure, and reciprocally, he becomes a saint who protects Mughal rule." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 241, folio 220). Chishti writes that the country of Thatta was granted to the Khan-e Khanan through the prayer of Hazrat Husain Lahori, a word uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 68). Alam sets one of the poems against the story: "According to some hagiographers, Hussein gave the blessing of a military victory to Khan-e-Khana when he came as a supplicant to the saint; however, this kafi points out something different: a caveat of mortality to the powerful" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 184, folio 184).

The rebel of the current account appears in Alam too. "Even hagiographers like Muhammad Pir and Chisti admit that Hussein kept eluding Akbar's police till he was finally nabbed at the hanging of 'Dullah Bhatti, though they make it sound as if he was attending the event as a mere spectator" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 15, folio 15). Writing of the Punjab in the saint's times, he points to "the relevant historical account of a rebellion brewing in Punjab led by Abdullah Bhatti, commonly known as 'Dullah Bhatti, who was captured and hanged in public on Akbar's orders" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 14, folio 14).

## Madho Lal: A Love Made Legend

The turning-point of Hussain’s later life, and the reason his name is joined to another’s, was his love for Madho Lal — a young man of a Hindu Brahmin family. The saint, it is said, was overcome at the sight of the boy’s beauty; when people objected that a Muslim faqir should be so taken with a Hindu youth, Hussain answered that his love was not carnal but a kind of wonder: “I do not love him — I envy his Creator, for how beautiful He has made him.” Slowly the two became inseparable, though Madho’s family and caste-fellows looked on the friendship with disapproval. Devotional tradition preserves a famous miracle of their bond: when Madho was bound by custom to make the pilgrimage of the Ganga Snan (the ritual bathing in the Ganges) and feared his parents’ anger, Hussain bade him take his hands, set his feet upon the saint’s own, and close his eyes — and in that instant Madho found himself at the distant bathing-place, to the astonishment of all. From then, the love was mutual and complete: the two could not bear to be parted.

So closely were their names woven together that Hussain gave the youth his own, telling him — when Madho worried that the world knew only “Lal Hussain” — that from that day people would speak Madho’s name before his own. And so it has been: the saint is remembered not as Shah Hussain alone but as *Madho Lal Hussain*, and the two lie buried side by side. In a land later torn by the divisions of religion, the shrine of a Muslim saint and his Hindu beloved, honoured under a single name, has become one of the Punjab’s most cherished symbols of a love that knows nothing of the frontiers of caste and creed — and, for the Sufi, a living parable of the soul’s love for God, in which the lover and the beloved at last become one.

The older Urdu books make Madho a Brahmin of Shahdara who became a Muslim. Lahori gives him his own entry, as one of Shaykh Husain Lahori's *khalifas* who was his illumined pir's beloved and desired one, and writes that his father and mother, Brahmins by caste, lived in the *qasba* of Shahdara (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 64, folio 52). Hazrat Husain, he writes, fell in love with his beauty with heart and soul and drew him by the attraction of love; Madho came to him with sincere devotion and was honoured with Islam, and through training became a perfect *wali* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 64–65, folios 52–53). The editor adds, from *Haqiqat al-Fuqara*, that Mian Madho had accepted Islam (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 65, folio 53). The editor of the Urdu edition also doubts whether Madho existed at all (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 64–65). Kanhaiya Lal writes that Madho was a beautiful Brahmin boy living at Shahdara, for whom Husain had the utmost love, and that in the end he too became a Muslim and Husain's *murid* (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 233, folio 236).

Sharib explains the joint name at the head of his chapter: he fell in love with a Brahmin boy named Madho, and the extremity of love joined the two names into one, so that instead of Shaykh Husain he came to be called Madho Lal Husain (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 249, folio 245). In his narrative, passing through Shahdara the saint's eye fell on a youth named Madho, a Brahmin boy of some sixteen years who was already married, and for sixteen years he circled Madho's house while Madho showed him no attention (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 252, folio 248). Then Madho began to come and go in his service and to share his pure wine; his household found it intolerable; he entered Islam, and he was named Mian Mahbub al-Haq but is famous as Madho (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 252, folio 248).

Chishti calls the pair lover and beloved (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 80). Hazrat Madho, he writes, was a beautiful boy, a Brahmin by caste, living in the *qasba* of Shahdara (the name uncertain in the printed text), and Husain circled his house at night (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 57). Chishti speaks of sixteen years (the number uncertain in the printed text) and of Husain made notorious before Madho turned to him, and writes that at last Madho became a Muslim outwardly as well (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 58); on the day of the Ganga miracle he became a Muslim with a sincere heart, a word uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 59). Husain gave him wine, by which he removed the dross of unbelief from Madho's heart, and at Babupura (the name uncertain in the printed text) made him a perfect *wali* through drink (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 60). Elsewhere in the same book, he was honoured with Islam when he was eighteen, and then reached annihilation in God (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 72).

Alam quotes Muhammad Pir: "Madho was very beautiful and eccentrically proud. He was a Brahmin's son… He was a Hindu, an infidel, a playful teaser, and cold-hearted" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 9, folio 9). Madho's home town, he writes, was Shahdara, where Jehangir's tomb stands (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 18, folio 18). Kugle writes: "He met a Brahmin youth named Madho and fell passionately in love with him" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 208, folio 187); the pairing was "a renegade Muslim saint of forty-odd years and a Brahmin teenager" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 208, folio 187). In the hagiographic verse he translates:

*Madho was wondrous in his beauty and his grace / A young man refined, noble Brahmin by descent* (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 209, folio 188, ch. 4 n. 11)

"the two were separated by age, class, and religion, and Madho looked askance at Hussayn as a social inferior, a source of caste impurity, and a general threat" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 209, folio 188). "Madho's Brahmin parents and his extended family did not accept this dangerous intimacy. They tried to hinder their meetings and to assassinate Hussayn." "It is not clear how the two managed to overcome this conflict with Madho's family and the Brahmins of Lahore." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 212, folio 191).

The courtship has its own wonders. Alam's sources have the saint persist and demonstrate "his mystical prowess by hearing what Madho says within the walls of his house and repeating the words verbatim" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 9, folio 9). A spy is "told to go back and verify the truth. He returns and finds two terrifying lions sitting face-to-face" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 9, folio 9); the scene he had first reported ends with the pair who "kept kissing each other like milk and sugar", and then 'two friends became one' (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, pp. 9–10, folios 9–10).

The festival of spring brought them together. Chishti writes that on Basant Panchami, near Holi, Madho threw colour and *gulal*, and that Husain kept Basant every year for the rest of his life (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 58). Sharib writes that on the occasion of Basant Madho brought *gulal* and threw it over him; an ecstatic state came over him and he began to dance, and thereafter he began to celebrate this festival (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 252, folio 248). Kugle: "Their separation was temporarily eased in the spring celebration of Basant, which gave the two a chance to interact personally"; "This holiday of frolic and play was perhaps the only time that two such different people could meet and speak. All conventions can be broken, all hierarchies can be overturned, and all restraints can be flouted." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 210, folio 189). The hagiographic poem he translates:

*Basant arrived, the day the world is flooded with joy / Hindus play Holi colors: music and wine, flirting and teasing* (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 211, folio 190, ch. 4 n. 14)

*Everyone gathers together that day without a care / the honorable mingle freely with the shameless* (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 211, folio 190, ch. 4 n. 14)

*From that joyous day onward Hussayn devoted / himself to play as if each day were Basant* (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 211, folio 190, ch. 4 n. 14)

Kugle does not tie Basant or Holi to any shrine or annual observance of the saint's own (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 211, folio 190, ch. 4 n. 14). Alam observes "whereas Hussein's participation in the Hindu festival of Holi fails to draw the authors' attention towards the spiritual syncretism of the lover and the beloved" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 8, folio 8).

Kugle reads the bond through the hagiography's own scenes. The first private meeting is in a hut "in the village of Babupur, far from Lahore", and the verse has "My head reels with desire to take you to Babupur" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 212, folio 191); Kugle nowhere says whether this Babupur is the Baghabanpur where the two later lived (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 215, folio 194). "Many Sufis held that a kiss on the lips between men was the vehicle for transmission of sacred power, rather than a routine expression of sexual desire." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 215, folio 194, ch. 4 n. 20). Some of Hussayn's followers "observed their sexual play through the keyhole and were troubled for a time by doubts about its propriety. The poet defends their sexual play as a 'spiritual initiation' in which Hussayn passed on to Madho the spark of divine love through touch" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 215, folio 194). "After this erotic initiation, Madho began to live with Hussayn at Baghabanpur on the outskirts of Lahore" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 215, folio 194), and they "lived together in complete intimacy" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 217, folio 196). Kugle sets beside this the version in Chishti's *Tahqiqat-i Chishti*: "So the saint, while drinking wine, embraced Madho and made him arrive at the truth (wasl bi-haqq), making Madho into a complete and perfected saint himself without any rigors or strenuous labors." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 327, folio 306, ch. 4 n. 22). "The erotic encounter of kissing and embracing is erased," he comments, the only trace of a sexual element being the term *wasl*, 'arriving' (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 327, folio 306, ch. 4 n. 22). "Although this account is clearly hagiographic, leaving no doubt that Shah Hussayn is a saint and will prevail, it describes this seduction in delightfully realistic tones." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 213, folio 192).

Kugle writes plainly of the attachment. "he never married, never raised children, never was the patriarchal leader of a household, and had a primary erotic attachment to another man who also never married" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 205, folio 184); he "appears to have been gay (if we can apply that term to premodern, non-European figures—but that is another debate altogether)" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 205, folio 184, ch. 4 n. 4); "most writers do not want to admit they were lovers, even though they lived together and Hussayn never married." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 217, folio 196, ch. 4 n. 26). "Shah Hussayn stands out from the background of Persianate Sufism, for he explicitly challenges patriarchal norms." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 231, folio 210). He marks the limit of the evidence too: "the texts never portray him as penetrating him anally or being penetrated, and the reasons for this lack of disclosure are not hard to understand" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 234, folio 213). Alam, glossing a poem, writes: "The disgrace here for proximity to the lover lends itself to the queer interpretation. In pursuit of Madho, Hussein moved across the river Ravi to the town of Shahdara. The two later cohabited together" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 181, folio 181). He reports that "Another modern scholar, Dr Syed Nazir Ahmed, calculates the thirty-eight-year age gap between the two", and finds a romance between them "repulsive to the mild taste" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 10, folio 10).

Of Madho's conversion the books hold opposite views. "Later biographers claim that Madho converted to Islam at the hand of Hussayn," Kugle writes, and calls the claim "another manifestation of the 'missionary position' that later writers used to dilute the passionate and sexual nature of their relationship"; "it is doubtful that he would continue to be known by his given, Hindu name (which is inscribed upon his tombstone today)" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 219, folio 198). Alam: "Hussein's young lover's subsequent conversion to Islam is treated as a necessary step towards the latter's salvation" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 8, folio 8).

The joined name the books explain in more than one way. For Alam, "The fact that Hussein literally fused his name with Madho's and came to be known as Madho Lal Hussein is not only testament to the enduring legacy of love, but also illustrates an essential tenet of Sufism, the merger of the lover and the beloved" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 6, folio 6). For Kugle, "So inseparable were they that Hussayn himself became known by the name of his lover" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 217, folio 196, ch. 4 n. 24); he warns that the nickname has led some writers to the false conclusion that Madho's own full name was 'Madho Lal', when 'Lal Hussayn' was the saint's nickname, and "After he fell in love, his lover's name was added to his own" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 328, folio 307, ch. 4 n. 24).

Whether the poems speak of Madho, the books do not agree. Alam writes: "Madho is addressed directly in only one kafi", "and even there it's open to interpretation since the word 'Lal' can also mean dear or precious one. Ditto for kafi 102 where Hussein mentions Lal in the penultimate line" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 10, folio 10). The kafi opens:

*Dear Lal! How can this life be trusted?* (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 43, folio 43)

The other two lines Alam points to are hedged in his own notes, where Lal might mean the colour red, the beloved, or Madho Lal ("Madho Lal or the colour red? I'd prefer the former") (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 185, folio 185):

*Forest and wilderness she searches / Haven't found Lal yet* (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 126, folio 126)

*Lal, I am inside out, that's my business, / that's my occupation* (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 138, folio 138)

The introduction to Rafat's selection holds that "Shah Husain's allusion to Madho Lal is indirect" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 45, folio 25), and its notes report that Dr. Nazir Ahmad "also notes that Shah Husain makes no reference to his beloved Madho Lal in his kafis" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 242, folio 222). Schimmel writes that he "had sung of his love for a Hindu boy in simple Panjabi verses, and his poems contain the traditional mystical vocabulary of longing and hope for annihilation" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 415, folio 384).

## The Kafis

Shah Hussain is honoured as the first great master of the Punjabi *kafi* — the short, sung mystical lyric that would become the very heart of Punjabi Sufi poetry, taken up after him by Bulleh Shah, Sultan Bahu, and Khwaja Farid. He is said not to have written his verses down himself; a court official, Dehan Bahar Khan, is remembered as having set them to paper as the saint sang them in his ecstasy. The kafis are brief and burning, cast in the voice of a woman — a bride, a spinner at her wheel, a girl longing for her absent love — for whom the Beloved is God, and separation from Him the only real grief. Almost all of them close with the poet naming himself in the third person, in a signature that has become famous in its own right: *Kahe Hussain, faqir Sain da* (“Says Hussain, the humble faqir of the Lord”).

In his best-loved kafi the soul cries out in the voice of a daughter to her mother:

مائے نی میں کینوں آکھاں، دردِ وچھوڑے دا حال نی

*O mother, to whom shall I tell the anguish of this separation?*

Alam calls it "Arguably the most popular kafi", and gives a literal translation of its first two lines (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 185, folio 185):

*O Mother, to whom can I tell / the state of separation's ache?* (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 185, folio 185)

Its Punjabi title survives in his book as "Maiy Ni Mein Kino Akhan", the title of Saleem Akhtar's Urdu edition (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 179, folio 179). Shemeem Burney Abbas finds the myths "brought out in the oral lore, such as in this kāfī from Shah Hussain, the Panjabi Sufi poet. Pathana Khan sings the narrative in Siraiki" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 151, folio not stated), and glosses two of its lines:

*O my mother, to whom shall I tell? / To whom narrate the agony of my separation?* (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 152)

Haroon Khalid heard it far from Lahore, at the shrine of Mai Heer in Jhang, where "a lone musician sang Shah Hussain (Punjabi Verses) on his harmonium": "O Mother, to whom should I now narrate these tales of my pains?" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated). The poem, he notes, "also had a reference to the love legend":

*I roamed all around searching for my Ranjhan / Little that I knew my Ranjhan was always with me* (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated)

In another, the whole of creation dissolves into the presence of the one Beloved:

رَبّا میرے حال دا محرم توں
اندر توں ہیں، باہر توں ہیں، رومَ رومَ وِچ توں

*O Lord, You alone are the confidant of my condition — You are within me, You are without me, You are in my every pore.*

And in another the ache of longing lengthens the very hours of the night:

سجّݨ بِن راتاں ہوئیاں وڈّیاں

*Without the beloved, the nights have grown long.*

And in a line that has passed into proverb, he sings of the truth that will not be silenced in the heart of the lover:

منہ آئی بات نہ رہندی اے

*The word that has risen to the lips cannot be held back.*

These songs, deceptively simple and endlessly deep, are still sung by qawwals and folk-singers across the Punjab, and it is largely through them that Shah Hussain lives — the man who abandoned the scholar’s certainties for the ecstatic’s, and made of the Punjabi language a vessel for the love of God.

The court scribe of the current account appears in three of the books as the keeper of a lost daily record. Sharib writes that Prince Salim, who sat on the throne with the title Jahangir and was his devotee, entrusted to Bahadur Khan the service of writing his *roznamcha* [OCR?] (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 253, folio 249). Alam: "Jehangir, Akbar's successor, is supposed to have appointed a person by the name of Bahar Khan to keep a daily log of Hussein's activities and sayings. No copy of his work, Baharia, is extant" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 8, folio 8); Chishti's claim to have used the *Baharia* Alam calls unverifiable (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 8, folio 8). Kugle: "The Mughal prince Salim (who matured to become Emperor Jahangir) ordered one of his courtiers, Bahar Khan, to attend to Shah Hussayn daily and write down his teachings and doings to preserve for posterity. This work is now lost" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 206, folio 185, ch. 4 nn. 5, 6). In his notes the work is named *Tadhkira-yi Bahariyya*, "which would make it a contemporary source from Shah Hussayn's own lifetime. However, this book no longer exists, even in manuscript form." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 326, folio 305, ch. 4 n. 5). Chishti has someone appointed to write down the saint's words, and a book of his life (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 59).

Two works in his name, Lahori's editor writes, have so far come to light: the *Kafian* in Punjabi and the *Risala-e Tahniyat* in Persian prose, presenting the problems of *tasawwuf* in an easy manner; its full text, edited and introduced by Muhammad Iqbal Mujaddidi, was printed in *Majalla Maarif*, Azamgarh, in August 1970 and again in *Sahifa*, Lahore, in July 1972 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 56, folio 44). The editor's own list of articles includes an article on an unpublished Persian work of Shah Husain's titled *Tahniyat*, in *Maarif*, August 1970 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 307, folio 295).

The poems were sung before they were written, and their text has a history of its own. Kugle calls them "oral tradition of his poetry that was originally sung before being written down" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, pp. 224–225, folios 203–204). The introduction to Rafat's selection writes that "Three hundred years later the works of the second great Punjabi poet, Shah Husain, would have been similarly lost had not another Sikh scholar, Dr. Mohan Singh Diwana collected the kafis from the bards and published them in the 1940's." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 47, folio 27), and that the 1882 Lahore volume of Bulleh Shah had mixed that poet's kafis with the work of the earlier poet Shah Husain (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 48, folio 28). Alam gives the figures: "Dr Mohan Singh Diwana, an early-twentieth-century Punjabi scholar, is acknowledged as the undisputed saviour of Hussein's kafis in written form. He discovered 138 kafis in a collection of Hindu, Muslim and Sikh mystical poetry by an unknown Sindhi editor. The collection was in Gurmukhi script and published in Lahore around 1900" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, pp. 16–17, folios 16–17); with "the twenty-five he found in a handwritten Gurmukhi manuscript in the Punjab University library (manuscript #374, dated 1804, the oldest textual record of Hussein's work)", they "made up the first Urdu script edition of Hussein's poetry published in 1942" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 17, folio 17). "Dr Nazir Ahmed, one of editors of Hussein's work, claims to have found a total of forty-eight kafis in the same manuscript; however, seven of them were repeated, which leaves forty-one kafis" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 186, folio 186). Two kafis he traces only to "Dr Mohan Singh Diwana's A Brief History of Punjabi Literature" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 187, folio 187). "As more editions followed, new kafis were discovered and contributed, the number of kafis rose, and the differences between the various versions of kafis, ranging from minor to major, started to emerge" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 17, folio 17). Alam warns that "The numbering of the kafis is not based on any authenticated preference of the poet", and follows the order of the APNA website (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 179, folio 179).

Not all of them are Punjabi. One, Alam notes, "is not in Punjabi but Persianized Hindi, similar to the brij bhasha of Amir Khusrau" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 183, folio 183), and another is a "Kafi in brij bhasha" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 184, folio 184). One is "A riddle-like kafi. Ten doors, a niche, a box, a bridal stage inside the box, and finally a dwarf elephant" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 186, folio 186); another "One of the most elusive kafis—if it could be called a kafi, since it does not conform to the typical stanzaic form. The red wedding dress, references to the various places of the Indian subcontinent (Kashmir, Gujarat, Multan) and then the image of Krishna with his flute—all add to the mystery." (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 182, folio 182). Of one more: "This kafi rhymes beautifully throughout (mooli, gandhooli, phooli, samjholi being the end words)" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 187, folio 187). The same introduction calls his compositions "kafis par excellence" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 49, folio 29) and writes that "Shah Hussain (1539-1593) tied himself to a raga-oriented metre" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 25, folio not stated); Asaf Khan, he reports, "relates the diction of all the great Punjabi poets to Central Punjabi but accepts variations of vocabulary in Shah Husain, Ghulam Farid, etc." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 236, folio 216).

For Kugle, "His poems (kafiyan) that rolled off his lips in Punjabi give the clearest view of his religious vision." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 222, folio 201); his "poetic output is so clearly Punjabi and draws so intensely from local Indian folklore and Hindu myth" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 218, folio 197). Kugle quotes them in Ghulam Yaqoob Anwar's translations, "adapting them slightly in English" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 328, folio 307, ch. 4 n. 37). Alam notes the names the poet gives God: "Hussein rarely uses Allah. If not Sain, then it is Rab, Maula, Ram. Actually, he uses Ram quite often", and "He even evokes Har, Shiva"; "Perhaps perceived as the god of nobles, sultans and emperors, Allah was not in vogue among the common folk Hussein chose to associate with" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 181, folio 181).

Whether the signature is always the poet's own is questioned. Rafat's selection reports: "Dr. Nazir in his introduction points out that self-reference in Shah Husain was frequently an interpolation of the qawwals which upset the prosodic balance of the poem." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 242, folio 222). Alam: "In almost every concluding line, Hussein addresses himself as fakir nimana (the lowly fakir) or fakir Sain da (the Lord's fakir)" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 16, folio 16). "In Arabic, fakir means poor, but in Punjabi, it's the mendicant, dervish, beggar, pauper. What kind of a fakir is Hussein? He is a fakir nimana—a fakir without ma'an or pride, worthless, wretched, of the lowest order, a lumpen proletariat" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 180, folio 180). In his translations the signature takes many forms: 'the beggar-wanderer' (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 48), 'the miserable mendicant' (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 73), 'the wretched fakir' (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 82), 'the wandering fakir' (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 104), 'God's fakir' (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 165), 'the helpless fakir' (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 174), and 'the lowly fakir', the phrase from which the volume takes its title (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, pp. 36, 49, 60, 92). Kugle's English gives it as *So says Hussain, / The mendicant of the Lord.* (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 223, folio 202, ch. 4 n. 38) and *So says Hussain, the Faqir.* (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 224, folio 203, ch. 4 nn. 41, 42). Christopher Shackle, editing Bulleh Shah, cites a Shah Husain text with "a quite different signature verse starting kahe Husain faqīr, 'Husain the fakir says.'" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 164, folio 139).

## In Verse

The poems that Alam translates speak, again and again, in the poet's own name and against himself. He refuses the title of master:

*One called Shah Hussein is a fakir, / don't call him a master / I'm not fond of falsehood* (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 134, folio 134)

He mocks his own pride:

*Why such self-importance, Hussein? / Who do you think you are? You are / a weed among the garden's fancy flowers* (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 59, folio 59)

And his age:

*Shah Hussein, you dotard, / gaps widen between your teeth* (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 64, folio 64)

He waits at the Lord's door:

*Shah Hussein lies at your doorstep, Sain, / yearning for your name* (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 46, folio 46)

Elsewhere he is "Shah Hussein stumbling at the very end" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 40, folio 40). The one property the verse gives him is a field:

*Blackbuck, you grazed / the borders of Shah Hussein's fields* (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 71, folio 71)

The speaking voice is a woman's, and in Alam's English a working weaver girl's: "This miserable one is caught in cotton" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 68, folio 68); "I'm the lowly cleaner at the esteemed house" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 111, folio 111); "Shah Hussein, I'm the dowry-less" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 136, folio 136).

*A weaver woman tied to the loom / I worked my spindles despite the detractors* (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 107, folio 107)

Kugle writes that "In his poetry, he often spoke in the voice of a woman, specifically a young woman just reaching the age of betrothal." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 222, folio 201). The poems turn on the choice "to spin and weave dutifully in the hope of finding a husband or to abandon the bounded courtyard of respectability", and on the dower: "She needs to earn the marriage, for her family will need to pay the dower to a groom." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, pp. 223–224, folios 202–203). Of his red rags as a bride's dress Kugle quotes:

*To hell with your white garb/ better is the rag of the faqir / only she is wedded who stands up/ to dance in his courtyard with abandon.* (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 329, folio 308, ch. 4 n. 50)

The heroine he most often becomes is Hir. "Though he never directly cites the name of Krishna's lover, Radha, he does use the name of the female heroine, Hir. He often cited the love epic of the Punjabi folk heroes Hir and her lover Ranjha, placing himself in the persona of the woman, Hir." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 228, folio 207, ch. 4 n. 50). In one poem, as Kugle quotes it, his identity with Hir is more explicit; it begins "Crying for my beloved, I myself became Ranjha", and ends:

*I have finally found the beloved I sought for so long / It was after meeting the sadhus, says Hussayn/ that the uncertainty has vanished.* (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 329, folio 308, ch. 4 n. 50)

Haroon Khalid quotes the image of becoming Ranjha in transliteration:

*Mahi mahi kook di mein ape Ranjhan hoyi / Ranjhan Ranjhan sab koi akho, Heer na akhon koi*

*Calling the name of my beloved I myself have become Ranjhan / Call me Ranjhan only now as I am no longer Heer* (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated)

In *Walking with Nanak* Khalid paraphrases the verse again, chanting the name of Ranjha "that I myself have become Ranjha, call me Ranjha" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 152, folio 134).

The poems end, too, on doctrine. The last kafi in Alam's book carries the hidden-treasure saying, its Arabic tags being "The Quranic attributes of Allah" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 187, folio 187), and then names a teacher:

*Each thing encompassed, he said, I was the hidden treasure* (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 177, folio 177)

*Says Hussein, this the incantation taught by Shah Jalal* (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 177, folio 177)

Shah Jalal is the only teacher the verse names; the book gives him no city, date or order (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 177, folio 177). One kafi, Alam notes, has become "one of the signature songs of Abida Parveen, the famous Pakistani singer of mystical poetry"; its refrain he gives as *Changi aan kay mandi aan/ mein sahib teri bandi aan* (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 183, folio 183).

The poems' text is not everywhere settled. Shackle, whose Bulleh Shah volume names him only as 'Shah Husain' and gives him no date or place, names four of its lyrics, 29, 63, 130 and 145, as doubtful in authorship because they are also given to Shah Husain (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 24, folio xxiii); of one he writes, "Like kāfī 29, it is also attributed to Shah Husain." (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 188, folio 163, n. 1 on 130). Of the first: "This poem is generally considered to be by Bullhe Shah and is included as such in R, Q, F, and J. Like kāfī 63, however, it is excluded from S as the work of Shah Husain." "Given the very uncertain early textual history of both poets, this rival attribution can hardly be regarded as definitive, and the poem is therefore included here." (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 164, folio 139). The Shah Husain text of that poem has the refrain *mitrāñ dī mijmānī kāran, dil dā lahū chhānīdā*, which Shackle's note renders 'To entertain my beloved, my heart's blood is filtered', followed by six verses only partly overlapping with Bulleh Shah's (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 164, folio 139).

The Hir of the poems is where the books begin his place in Punjabi letters. Shackle writes: "Although the story [of Hir and Ranjha] makes some appearances in the earlier Sufi poetry of Shah Husain, it is Bullhe Shah who properly exploits the rich potential for mystical allegory inherent in the relationship between Hir and her beloved" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 21, folio xx). Schimmel: "Allusions to this theme are found as early as the sixteenth century in the Panjabi verse of Madho Lal Husain" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, pp. 170–171, folios 152–153); "He also alluded to popular motifs, like the stories of Hir and of Sohni Mehanwal, and thus seems to inaugurate (or perhaps even continue from unknown beginnings) a tradition typical of the religious poetry of the two provinces: the insertion of folk tales into the context of mystical thought." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 415, folio 384); she names him again as the first known representative of that trend (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 419, folio 388). Khalid: "He for the first time, transformed the story of Heer Ranjha from a secular epic to a spiritual legend. He compared the love of Heer for Ranjha to that of a believer for his God, a theme that was subsequently picked up by Bulleh Shah and Waris Shah", and "Through his poetry, he introduced the concept of Wahadut-ul-Wajud, or monism, into the story which remains an essential part of Hindu philosophy and Islamic spirituality" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated). Schimmel also names him for a poetic topic he shares with the Turkish Bektashi Kaygusuz Abdal, the singing "of his insatiable appetite", which ridicules or reverses the classical topic of the mystic's insatiable thirst for more and more love (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 185, folio 167).

Writing of the Punjab and Sind at large, Schimmel observes: "In both provinces, a preference for the motif of spinning and weaving can be observed, a natural propensity in a cotton-growing country" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 417, folio 386), and "The tragic heroines of the Sindhi and Panjabi folk tales—like Hir, Sassui, Sohni, and many others—represent the human soul in search of the beloved" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 465, folio 434). Alam writes of the form: "Kafis, the stanzaic poems couched in folkloric themes and techniques, were easy to understand and memorize, and thus served as the ideal means to disseminate the mystical teachings among the illiterate masses dependent on the oral transmission of knowledge. Even to this day, most Punjabis and Sindhis are more likely to have heard a kafi than read one" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 15, folio 15).

## The Poet and His Age

Shah Hussain lived through the high noon of the Mughal Empire, in the reigns of Akbar and Jahangir, when Lahore was a great imperial capital adorned with gardens, mosques, and mansions. His was an age of ferment — of Akbar’s experiments in the reconciliation of religions and of popular unrest against the imperial order — and Punjabi tradition links the ecstatic faqir with the rebel folk-hero Dulla Bhatti, who rose against the Emperor’s revenue demands, casting Hussain among the friends of the poor and the defiant of the mighty. Among the learned and the holy of the city he had many companions and interlocutors, yet it is the texture of ordinary Punjabi life that fills his verse: the girl at her spinning-wheel (*charkha*), the bride adorned and waiting, the traveller on the long road, the lanes of the parental home that a daughter must one day leave. Into these homely images he poured the whole drama of the soul’s love for God and its grief at separation, so that the humblest villager and the deepest mystic could each find themselves in his song.

In choosing to sing in Punjabi rather than the Persian of the court, and in the voice of a woman rather than that of the scholar, Hussain did something momentous: he made the mother-tongue of the Punjab a vehicle of the highest spiritual expression, and founded the tradition of the mystical *kafi* that Bulleh Shah, Sultan Bahu, Shah Sharaf, and Khwaja Farid would afterward carry to its heights. His Malamati freedom was itself a kind of statement — a rebuke, danced and sung in the red robe of the malang, to a religion of mere respectability and outward form. In his own person he fused the ecstatic and the poet, the lover and the critic, and left the Punjab a way of speaking to God that it has never since forgotten.

Kugle sums the man up as "a theologian turned Sufi, a Sufi turned social rebel. He was a great poet in Punjabi and one of the most vivid Sufi personalities during the reign of the Mughal emperor Akbar" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 205, folio 184), and places him in a devotional line: "In his assertion that there were more commonalities than differences between Islamic and Hindu devotion, Shah Hussayn belongs to the tradition of Kabir and Guru Nanak. Like them, he found vernacular and folk poetry to be the best vehicle to expression this fusion of Sufi and bhakti ideals" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 329, folio 308, ch. 4 n. 51). His public life, Kugle writes, "brought him into conflict with the police, judges, and government, conflicts in which he ultimately prevailed" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 206, folio 185). The city's opinion of him ran two ways at once: "Though Shah Hussayn earned himself disrepute among the Muslim notables of Lahore, who saw him as a dissolute drunk … He began to earn the love and admiration of the common people as a saintly poet who was aloof from accruing worldly power through social status or religious reputation" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 208, folio 187); and, of the affair with Madho, "this open passion ruined his reputation throughout Lahore" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 210, folio 189). "Nobles began to patronize him, seeking blessings for their various endeavors." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 241, folio 220). He sets him, too, among practices of his time: the "oneness of being" was popularised in South Asia through *sama*, "listening to love poetry in a devotional setting that might erupt into ecstatic gesture or rapturous dance" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, pp. 230–231, folios 209–210), and the 'witness game', *shahid-bazi*, "has a long history in Persianate Sufism as a sign of surrender and renunciation" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 230, folio 209); "Playing the witness game was so popular in South Asia by the sixteenth century that a vocal minority of cautious and juridical Sufis began to write against it." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 231, folio 210). Of the Qalandars, Kugle writes: "They renounce productive work, refuse reproduction of the family, and resist the labor it takes to conform to religious custom and law. Those who oppose Qalandars see their lives as waste—wasting their time, living as parasites off productive society" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 221, folio 200).

Chishti names the companions of his *majlis*: Mian Shaban, Mian Ibrahim, Mulla Muhammad, Yaqub, Shah Jahan, Muhammad Islam, Shahab al-Din and Salih, most of the names uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 58–59). Among the holy men of the city one contemporary recurs. Chishti records from *Sair al-Arifin* that Hazrat Hassu Teli and Hazrat Madho Lal Hussain were of one age (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 610). Kanhaiya Lal tells of Hassu Teli, by caste a Teli and a native of Lahore, that "it is said" he had utter love for Hazrat Lal Husain and used to say, in one wording, that Hassu is Husain and Husain is Hassu and nothing of two remains (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 169, folio 172), and in another, "Hassu is Husain and Husain is Hassu; no duality remains" (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 269, folio 272). Khalid makes him a contemporary of Damodar (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated).

His name reached the Mughal literary record through Dara Shikoh. Kugle writes that "Hussayn was also held in high esteem by other Mughal nobles, including Dara Shikoh, who mentions Shah Hussayn in his book on spiritual boasts of the saints, Shathiyat, and in his memorial to the saints, Hasanat al-'Arifin" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 326, folio 305, ch. 4 n. 5); Schimmel too records that Dara Shikoh names him in his *Hasanat al-arifin* (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 415, folio 384), and Lahori's editor lists the *Hasanat al-Arifin* among the sources on him (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 56–57, folios 44–45). Alam's sources on his times include Najm Hosain Syed's play *Takht L'hore*, which, he writes, "extrapolates the socio-economic conditions of Punjab" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 14, folio 14). One account, which Alam himself calls unverifiable, takes him beyond Lahore: "According to some sources, Shah Hussein travelled to Amritsar to get his poetry included in the Granth Sahib being compiled by Guru Arjun Dev (1563–1605). He offered this kafi for inclusion. Though the account is unverifiable, the thematic content of this kafi might have lent itself to the speculation" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 186, folio 186).

## Death and the Miracle of the Grave

Shah Hussain died in 1599. Foreseeing his end, the accounts say, he had sent Madho away — some say he had him enrolled in the army — knowing that his beloved could not bear to watch him die; and he left a strange instruction, that he be buried first on the very bank of the river Ravi, where the flood would in time carry the grave away. When the waters shifted the grave, he said, they should dig at the place where it came to rest, and if a rosary (*tasbih*), a water-pot (*lota*), and a prayer-mat (*jaanamaz*) were found in the earth, they would know they had found the true spot. He was buried by the Ravi, and Madho, told of his death only afterward, abandoned everything and returned, never again to leave the grave. Some years later the river flooded, as the saint had foretold; the grave was carried to Baghbanpura, where a *jogi* (a Hindu ascetic) dwelt, and there, when the ground was opened, the rosary, the pot, and the prayer-mat were found exactly as Hussain had promised. On that spot Madho reburied him, and there he kept his long vigil, reciting his master’s kafis in the streets and at the tomb, until, decades later, he too died and was laid to rest beside the friend whose name he bore — as had been Hussain’s last wish.

The year of his death is the most disputed fact in the books. Lahori's author gives 1008 AH, in Shah Akbar's reign (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 55–56, folios 43–44), and closes the entry with a chronogram quatrain whose last line reads:

سال ترحیل شمع عشق حسین

The line speaks of the year of the departure of Husain, the candle of love; the figure printed beside it, 945 AH (uncertain in the printed text), is the year the text gives for his birth (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 56, folio 44). His editor reaches a different verdict by argument. All the local histories, he writes, give 1008 AH / 1599 CE, of which *Haqiqat al-Fuqara*, of 1041 AH, is the oldest; *Miftah al-Arifin*, composed seven years later in 1048 AH, gives 1013 AH, which makes the *Haqiqat al-Fuqara* figure doubtful; but the contemporary historian Badayuni recorded that while composing *Najat al-Rashid* in 999 AH / 1590 CE, "I learnt at Lahore that Shah Husain, hearing the song of a sweet-voiced woman singer, became beside himself and fell from the roof of a house and died" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 55, folio 43). Preferring the contemporary to the later *tazkira*-writers, the editor concludes that the correct year of death is 999 AH, not 1008 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 55, folio 43). Kanhaiya Lal writes that Lal Husain died in 1008 AH, the figure uncertain in the printed text, and was buried near Shahdara (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 233, folio 236). On the closing pages of his chapter Sharib writes that he died in 1008 AH, aged sixty-three, the numeral uncertain in the printed text (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 253, folio 249). Kugle gives "the saint's death in 1599 C.E. at the age of about 60" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 219, folio 198), and Alam 1599 (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 6, folio 6). The introduction to Rafat's selection gives 1593 three times (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 24, folio not stated; Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 25, folio not stated; Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 29, folio not stated), and so do Schimmel's two books, in the indexes of both and in the text of one (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 357, folio 339; Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 415, folio 384; Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 520, folio 489).

Chishti tells the death at length, crediting *Haqiqat al-Fuqara*. Under the heading "The Death of Hazrat Husain" he has the saint carried across the Ravi, and his words that he must go now, for God summons him to union, a word uncertain in the printed text; the age and the year of Akbar's reign cannot be read (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 69). He was buried at a place he had chosen himself, on Friday, the last day of the month of Jumada al-Thani, in the year 1008 AH, the last word of the number uncertain in the printed text, a date taken from *Haqiqat al-Fuqara*; Chishti gives a chronogram and a *qit'a* signed Chishtiya (the signature uncertain in the printed text), and writes that the mourning for Husain raised a clamour like that of Karbala in the Punjab (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 70). The chronogram's line reads:

گفت ہاتف کہ مستِ عشقِ ازل

In it the voice from the unseen speaks of one drunk with the love of pre-eternity.

The moving of the grave the books tell three ways. Kanhaiya Lal writes that some years after the burial near Shahdara the river came near the tomb, and that Madho brought his murshid's coffin out and buried it where the mazar now stands (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 233, folio 236); the book records the same thing again at the mazar of Shah Bilawal Qadri (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 235, folio 238). Chishti has the saint foretell it. After bathing he marked a place with a prayer-mat and a pillow, ordered greenery planted, said that his body would be taken to Babupura and buried there, that Madho would go away and return after twelve years, and that a faqir would remain there as *sajjada nashin*; a Mir Ishaq is named, all these readings uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 61). The Hazrat, he writes, greatly loved this place, Babupura (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 61). Then the Ravi flooded and the water reached Hazrat Husain's mazar, a word illegible in the printed text; the grave was found empty within; one Muhammad Salih found a bouquet of *rihan* flowers; and a voice ordered the burial at Babupura (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 71). Pir Muhammad's chronogram for it reads (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 71):

سدِ گل[OCR?] گورِ گل[OCR?] رو حسین[OCR?]

It names the grave of Husain, its words uncertain in the printed text. The reburial, Chishti writes, was here, where the holy mazar now stands (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 71). Kugle's account has no move at all: "Hussayn had prepared his own grave next to their home." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 219, folio 198).

## Madho After Hussain

The books follow Madho through the years apart and the years at the grave. Kugle: "Madho stayed with Hussayn and his companions for a few years and then asked permission to join the military service." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 219, folio 198). He left Lahore in the service of Raja Man Singh for three years in the army, fighting in Bengal and in the Deccan, which Kugle spells 'Dekkan'; he "returned to live with Hussayn until the saint's death"; afterwards he "joined the army again, journeying for twelve years throughout Hindustan" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 219, folio 198). "Finally, he returned to Lahore to live at the tomb of Hussayn for thirty-five more years. He lived in isolation (and was not known to have married)." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 219, folio 198). He became the spiritual successor of Hussayn's saintly authority, "and upon his death, Madho was buried directly beside Hussayn." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 219, folio 198).

Chishti gives him service with Raja Man Singh (the name uncertain in the printed text), to Delhi and, by Akbar's order, on a campaign in the Deccan (the name uncertain too), and has the Raja tell him that from that day he was his *murid* (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 60). After Husain's death Madho wept night and day, clasping the Hazrat's blessed grave; he was sent in a dream to Raja Man Singh for twelve years, on the principle that one under orders is excused, and the Raja said that he was Madho's servant, indeed his slave, and his *murid* and especial devotee; the author adds a note of caution on why (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 70), and the Raja died after twelve years (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 71). After thirteen full years Madho took his seat as *sajjada nashin* at the pure mazar (a word uncertain in the printed text), and came to look like Hazrat Husain (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 71).

Lahori writes that when Husain died Madho remained a *mujawir* at his mazar (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 65, folio 53), and Sharib that Hazrat Madho became his *sajjada nashin* (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 253, folio 249).

Madho's own dates the books give differently, and Alam gives none (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 18, folio 18). Lahori's author has him born in nine hundred and eighty-three, with 983 in the margin, and dead in one thousand and fifty-six, with 1056 in the margin, at the age of 73 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 65, folio 53). The editor sharpens the death to 22 Zi al-Hijja 1056 AH / 1646 CE, and adds that he was buried within his shaykh's enclosure; this clause, the editor's and not the author's, is the book's one statement that the two lie together (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 65, folio 53). Kanhaiya Lal writes that Madho died in 1057 AH, the figure uncertain in the printed text, and was buried in the same place (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 233, folio 236). Chishti gives his age as seventy-two years (the number uncertain in the printed text), a death on Monday, the twenty-second of Dhu al-Hijja (the day uncertain), in a year of which only "one thousand" can be read, the next word illegible, and the grave beside Hazrat Husain's mazar (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 72). For his birth Chishti gives a chronogram:

سالِ میلاد اور[OCR?] ز رومی عدد ۲۲[OCR?]
سہ[OCR?] ہشتاد[OCR?] و بود و بر نہ صد[OCR?]

Chishti does not work out the year it encodes (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 72).

Madho's tombstone, Kugle writes, bears his given, Hindu name today (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 219, folio 198). Kanhaiya Lal records a promise of his: the eastern gate of the enclosure is called the Bihishti Darwaza, the Gate of Paradise, because Shaykh Madho has decreed that whoever enters it will be of paradise (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 233, folio 236).

## The Shrine

The darbar stands in Baghbanpura, in the shadow of the Mughal Shalimar Gardens, and spreads today over some twenty kanals of ground. At its heart lie the two graves — Shah Hussain’s and, beside it, Madho Lal’s — the object of a constant stream of pilgrims who come to recite the Fatiha, to make vows, and to sit in the presence of the saint of love. A mosque adjoins the shrine, where the five daily prayers are held, and around the complex has grown a large graveyard, for to be buried near Madho Lal Hussain is counted a great blessing, and space within it is hard to come by. The shrine took shape over the centuries after the saint’s death — its custodians recall a first structure raised in the decades after 1600 and substantial construction achieved by the nineteenth century — and its most recent reconstruction was carried out in 2011 by the Punjab Auqaf Department, which administers it in the Sunni (Ahl-e-Sunnat) tradition. Every Thursday evening the tomb is lit with lamps and fills with visitors; on ordinary days, too, some eight to ten thousand people are said to pass through.

The books place it as the current account does. Chishti puts it north of *mauza* Baghbanpura and west of the Shalamar Bagh, two words uncertain and one illegible in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 45); Kanhaiya Lal puts it adjoining *mauza* Baghbanpura on the north (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 233, folio 236). Lahori's author says only that Shah Husain's pearl-scattering mazar is in Lahore (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 55–56, folios 43–44); the appendix of the Urdu edition gives both men rows of their own, Husain at Madho Lal Husain Street, Baghbanpura, and Madho, with the honorific *quddisa sirruhu al-aziz*, at the same street (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 287, folio 275). Alam calls it "the modest shrine of Shah Hussein in Baghbanpura near another dilapidated wonder of the Mughal era, the Shalimar Gardens" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, pp. 18–19, folios 18–19); Kugle, "His small tomb, shared with Madho" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 241, folio 220); Schimmel, "a modest tomb near the Shalimar gardens" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, pp. 170–171, folios 152–153). Kanhaiya Lal's contents call the site a *khanqah* (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 4, folio 3), and elsewhere he calls it a *mazar* and a *maqbara* (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 262–263, folios 265–266).

Kanhaiya Lal describes the khanqah of his day. It had a *pakka* enclosure; the great gate on the west was used for traffic, a south gate led toward Baghbanpura, there was a large north gate, and on the east the Bihishti Darwaza (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 233, folio 236). In the middle of the enclosure stood a large lime-plastered platform with *pakka* lattice on the parapets and four waist-high minarets at the corners; on it a second platform bore the *pakka* *taʿwiz* of Lal Husain's grave, always covered by a *ghilaf*; and on a further platform to its east Madho's grave, built *pakka* (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 234, folio 237). But for want of attention and repair many buildings had collapsed, and the enclosure wall had fallen in many places (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 234, folio 237).

Chishti walks through the whole precinct. South of the large western building were a *deorhi*, *dalans*, a white *chuna*-plastered *hujra*, and a marble slab recording the building as the work of one Ghulam Rasul Husain, the names uncertain and the rest illegible in the printed text; a place marked as the seat of the *gaddi*, where the *sajjada nashin* sits; a place of Hazrat Madho, a word illegible, five by three *gaz*, where, he writes, Hazrat Madho lived; a women's quarter with three *dalans*; and a well with a working *charkhi* (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 97). A walled area of a hundred and five *gaz* by a hundred *gaz*, the figures uncertain in the printed text, held a platform with ten graves, with that of Karam Husain, *sajjada nashin* (the name uncertain in the printed text), at its west end, and the graves of the *sajjada nashins* in order, Sain Wahid Shah, Azmat Shah, Abid Shah, Talib Shah, Sain Lal Din Shah and Haidar Shah (two of these names uncertain in the printed text), with Sain Ghulam Ali Shah among others nearby; he counts sixty or seventy graves there, the numbers uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 98). An eight-sided enclosure, built in a year that cannot be read with certainty in the printed text, had sloping *kanguras*, four corner seats, five arched doors on three sides and a half-circle door on the north (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 98). The enclosure was seventeen *gaz* square (the number uncertain in the printed text); within it the platform called the Maqam-e Takht, one and a half *gaz* high and twelve and a half *gaz* square (the figures uncertain), bore the blessed *taʿwiz* of Hazrat Husain, two feet, always covered with a *ghilaf*, a word illegible; to the east of Hazrat Husain's mazar is the luminous mazar of Hazrat Madho (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 99). Madho's grave had no *ghilaf* at the time of writing, though a *ghilaf* was laid on it on the day of the *urs* and of the fair; an upper level behind a marble door was always kept locked; and to the north, at a distance of a hundred *gaz* (the number uncertain in the printed text), stood an octagonal white *chiragdan*, four *gaz* high, in which a lamp was lit every day (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 99). Nearby, he notes, is Hazrat Sulaiman, whose mazar adjoins that of Madho Lal Husain, a word uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 348).

The mosque beside the shrine was built by a governor of Lahore. Kanhaiya Lal writes that to the west of the mazar of Madho Lal Husain stands this mosque built by Nawab Zakariya Khan, known as Khan Bahadur, *suba* of Lahore. To the east of the mosque were a large well with a wheel and two bathhouses; the courtyard floor was *pakka* but old and worn (the last word uncertain in the printed text), with a wall of a man's height round it; there were three *mihrabs*, and on the central one, in *thuluth* script in *kansi*-work on a sky-blue[OCR?] ground, the Bismillah and the Kalima (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 262, folio 265). The north *mihrab* names its era, the reign of Muhammad Shah, in a line whose first word is uncertain in the printed text:

خوہست [OCR?] در دور شاہ محمد شاہ

And it names its builder, the choicest of the emperor's court:

زبدۂ بارگاہ او نواب
زکریا خان صوبہ پنجاب

*The choicest of his court, Nawab Zakariya Khan, Suba of Punjab* (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 262–263, folios 265–266)

The verse calls him *suba* of the Punjab where the prose calls him *suba* of Lahore (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 262–263, folios 265–266). It gives the motive for the gift:

چاہ و مسجد ز خود بنا کہ منہ [OCR?]
عالی و خوب و خوشنما یکند [OCR?]
محض بہر خدا کند این کار
تا نمازی شود گماز [OCR?] گزار

*He built well and mosque of himself, lofty, fine and handsome; he does this work purely for God's sake, so that the worshipper may offer prayer* (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 263, folio 266)

The south *mihrab* places the mosque by the saint's *dargah* in so many words:

نزد درگاہ صاحب عرفان
واقف سر حضرت رحمان
آنکہ معروف شدہ بہ لال حسین
خاک نعلین اوست سرمہ عین

*Near the dargah of the master of gnosis, knower of the secret of the Merciful, he who has become known as Lal Husain, the dust of whose sandals is collyrium for the eye* (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 263, folio 266)

It records too that he newly raised a firm mosque and a firm well:

کرد احداث مسجد محکم
نیز خویش دو در چاہ مستحکم

And it closes with a chronogram for the building:

ز تاریخ او ہر کہ دو بید [OCR?] شمار
بداند ہزار و صدہ و چہل و چار

*One thousand one hundred and forty-four*, with the figure printed beneath it as 1144, uncertain in the printed text (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 263, folio 266)

Chishti copies the same south-arch inscription, with small differences of reading and a word illegible in the line on the dust of the saint's sandals, and gives a further line on the building of the mosque and the well (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 441):

مسجد و چاہ را انکو تعمیر

The mosque, he writes, opens towards the mazar of the Hazrat, the phrase partly illegible in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 440); it has doors and, perhaps, three wells, the figures uncertain in the printed text; and within the enclosure of Hazrat Madho Lal Hussain stands a *lasora* tree, the name uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 442). Kanhaiya Lal adds that now this mosque belongs to the tomb of Madho Lal Husain and is in the possession of the *sajjada nashin*; he gives the *sajjada nashin* no name, the transfer no date (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 263, folio 266).

Alam saw the graves in use: "The side-by-side graves of Hussein and Madho are always heaped with fresh rose and marigold garlands offered by the daily throngs of devotees" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 19, folio 19). Kugle, writing of the people of Lahore today, says that "even the custodians of his tomb, seem to have forgotten the erotic epic of his life story and the sexual nature of his love for Madho." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 241, folio 220).

## Khalifas and Custodians

The saint's following, as Lahori's book gives it from *Haqiqat al-Fuqara*, numbered nine thousand perfected servants and one hundred and fifty-five thousand *murids*, of whom sixteen became famed *khalifas*, in four groups of four, each with its burial place (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 55, folio 43). The Gharibs lie at Ratti Mittha next to Wazirabad, at a place in the Wazirabad district whose printed name, perhaps Langarhi, cannot be read with certainty, at another in the Deccan, perhaps Jilapur, equally uncertain, and beside Shah Husain's own grave (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 55, folio 43). The Diwans are Shaykh Madho, the first; Diwan Gorakh and Allah Diwan, buried at Lahore; and Diwan Bakhshi, at Bijapur in the Deccan. The Khakis are Maula Bakhsh Khaki and Khaki Shah at Lahore, Khaki Shah at Wazirabad and Haidar Khaki in the Deccan; the Bilawals, Shah Rang Bilawal, Budhu Bilawal and Shah Mast Bilawal at Lahore and Shah Bilawal in the Deccan (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 55, folio 43). Sharib also counts sixteen: of them four were called Shah Gharib, and four Diwan, that is, Madho Diwan, Gorakh Diwan, Bakhshi Diwan and Allah Diwan, and three Khaki, that is, Maula Bakhsh Khaki, Khaki Shah Wazirabadi and Haidar Bakhsh Khaki (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 253, folio 249); and four are called Bilawal, that is, Shah Rang Bilawal, Budhu Bilawal, Shah Bilawal and Shah Bilawal Dakkani (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 254, folio 250).

The line ran on through Shah Rang Bilawal. In Lahori's entry on Haji Jamiat, his allegiance was to Shah Rang Bilawal (the name uncertain in the printed text), *khalifa* of Hazrat Lal Husain Lahori (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 271, folio 259). Chishti names Hazrat Rang Bilawal (both words uncertain in the printed text) as servant, *khalifa* and *sajjada nashin* of Hazrat Madho Lal Husain Sahib (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 333), and in the chain of one Bibi Shah (the first word uncertain in the printed text) a link that runs from Rang Bilawal Sahib, whose name is printed uncertainly, to Hazrat Madho Lal Diwan (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 334).

Chishti names several *sajjada nashins* of the shrine. In the time of Maharaja Ranjit Singh it was Mian Sain Mahbub Shah (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 101). A seal on a testimony is that of Karam Husain, *sajjada nashin* of Madho Lal Husain, two words uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 321), and a division of the income of the *sajjadagi* names, for Madho Lal Husain, one Karam Hasan and Hasan Din, the names uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 318). One Shah, whose first name cannot be read, *sajjada nashin* of the mazar of Madho Lal Husain, was the leader (the word uncertain in the printed text) of the faqirs of Lahore, after which all the faqirs grew displeased (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 663). A deed names the servant and exemplar of the faqirs Sain Suli Shah (the name uncertain in the printed text), *sajjada nashin* of the blessed mazar of Janab Shah Lal Husain Sahib (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 780), and the same deed bars claims by the servants and others, the word uncertain in the printed text, of the *sajjada nashins* of Shah Ali Husain Sahib, or by anyone of the line of the Husain Shahi faqirs (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 781). One Jhuggi Shah served the mazar and built a house there at his own cost (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 777).

Kanhaiya Lal names the man who took the offerings in his day: the offering income of both *melas* was taken by Hassan Ali, *mujawir* of the khanqah (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 234–235, folios 237–238). None of the books consulted names the Punjab Auqaf Department or the renovation of 2011.

## Mela Chiraghan: The Festival of Lamps

The shrine’s crowning event is the Mela Chiraghan — the “Festival of Lamps” — the annual urs held on the 9th to the 11th of Shawwal — falling, in recent years, in the spring months of March and April — and reckoned among the greatest festivals of the Punjab. For three days the darbar and its surroundings blaze with countless lamps and candles, and the crowds swell from the daily thousands to between one hundred and one hundred and twenty-five thousand, drawn from far and wide. Each day carries its own character: one given to the fair itself, with *dhamal* danced to the beat of the *dhol*; one to the lighting of the lamps (*chiraghan*) from which the festival takes its name; and one to the ceremonial changing of the *chadar*, the cloth spread over the grave — the whole accompanied by qawwali, by *langar* (the free distribution of food), and by great gatherings of devotees. The festival has ancient associations with the neighbouring Shalimar Gardens, and it belongs as much to the shared popular culture of Lahore as to the shrine alone; for generations it has been one of the city’s defining occasions, a meeting of the sacred and the festive in which the memory of the poet-saint is renewed in light, music, and dance.

Historically the Mela Chiraghan was among the very largest fairs of the Punjab — at its height, it is said, rivalling even the great spring festival of Basant — and it was long celebrated in and around the Shalimar Gardens themselves, the lamps spilling out from the shrine into the royal garden. Under Sikh rule in the nineteenth century Maharaja Ranjit Singh and his court are remembered as joining in the festivities, a mark of how far the saint’s appeal reached beyond the Muslim community. In time the fair was drawn back within the shrine and its precincts, but it remains a vast and joyous occasion, binding the memory of the poet-saint to the civic life of Lahore and gathering Muslims and non-Muslims, the devout and the merely festive, into the same three nights of light.

Kanhaiya Lal gives the fullest account of the shrine's fairs in his time. Twice a year, he writes, a great spring *mela* is held at this khanqah (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 234, folio 237). The first is on the day of Basant, attended by Hindus and Muslims (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 234, folio 237). In Ranjit Singh's time the Maharaja came in person on Basant day; a silver bungalow was set up north and west of the enclosure; troops were drawn up in a double line from the Lahore Fort to the khanqah, some words of the description uncertain in the printed text; he held a *darbar* in the bungalow, taking *nazrs* from all the nobles and giving *khilats*, and offered five hundred rupees at the khanqah, the figure uncertain in the printed text (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 234, folio 237). Now that the Sikh kingdom is gone, he writes, it is an ordinary public *mela* (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 234, folio 237).

The second is the fair of lamps, which is still a large fair (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 234, folio 237). It was formerly held on the first of Rajab; now, he writes, it is no longer bound to the lunar month and falls in spring at the end of March (the month's name uncertain in the printed text), one night at the khanqah and the whole day in the Shalamar Bagh (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 234, folio 237). In March 1880 (the year uncertain in the printed text) the Deputy Commissioner made it a commercial *mela*; many merchants brought goods for sale, and the crowd stayed two days in the Shalamar Bagh (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 234, folio 237).

Sharib writes that every year the Mela Chiraghan is celebrated with great fanfare, and gives no month, date, duration or attendance (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 253, folio 249). Twelve folios later the same book prints another saint's flat prohibition of *sama*, dancing, sandal and lamps at the *urs* (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 266, folio 262). Schimmel fixes the date to a weekday: Madho Lal's anniversary, she writes, is "still celebrated in Lahore near the Shalimar Garden on the last Saturday in March", and is famous as the Mela Chiraghan, the 'Fair of Lamps' (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 415, folio 384). Alam counts the years: "The last week of March 2016 will mark the 428th urs of Shah Hussein, aka Madho Lal Hussein." (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 19, folio 19). The celebration, he writes, is commonly known as the Festival of Lamps (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 19, folio 19). He reads the female voice of the poems by the convention that "The urs of a saint is celebrated as a wedding, the union of the bride/mortal with the groom/divine" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 11, folio 11).

Abbas, who did fieldwork at the festival, gives it another name. A photograph in her book is captioned "Melā Chirāg Urs at Shah Hussain, Shalimar Gardens, Lahore" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 95, folio not stated), and her glossary defines "melā-cirāg Festival of Lights held annually at the Shalimar Gardens in Lahore, coinciding with the urs (death anniversary) of the Sufi poet Shah Hussain" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 185, folio not stated). She uses what she saw there to settle a question about another shrine: "However, I confirmed at the urs of Shah Hussain in Lahore that women sing before large male and female audiences and that there is no gender discrimination among the audiences. This strengthens the claim of my female informants at Sehwan Sharif that women performers sing before both female and male audiences" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 95–96, folios not stated). Kugle's single sentence on the festival does not use the word *ʿurs*: "His small tomb, shared with Madho, is the focus for a riotous celebration on the anniversary of his death, accompanied by frenzied drumming, ecstatic dance, and blazing fires." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 241, folio 220). Omar Kasmani's *Queer Companions* cites Richard Wolf's article "The Poetics of 'Sufi' Practice: Drumming, Dancing, and Complex Agency at Madho Lal Husain (and Beyond)", in *American Ethnologist* for 2006 (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 217, folio 200).

## Devotion and Significance

The devotion at Madho Lal Hussain draws on everything the saint was: the ecstatic malang who danced where others prayed; the poet whose kafis put the love of God into the language of the people; and the lover whose bond with a Hindu youth became a byword for a love beyond division. Pilgrims come for the blessing of a saint famous for *ishq-e-haqiqi*, the “real love” of the Divine, and for the sense — as the local belief is reported — that Hussain “belonged to another world,” that his whole life was unlike that of ordinary men. Those who could not know him in the flesh, it is said, come to love him through his stories and his songs. His verse is sung at the shrine and across the Punjab; his festival is one of the largest in the land; and his joint name stands, in a country marked by communal division, as a quiet insistence that the highest sanctity and the deepest love recognise no barrier of caste or creed. In this the darbar is not only the tomb of a great saint but a living argument about what devotion, and what love, can mean.

The books see in that devotion a patron of the city. Kugle writes, "To this day, Hussayn is seen as one of the patron saints of Lahore." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 241, folio 220). Abbas names Shah Hussain with Bibi Pak Daman, Data Ganj Bakhsh Hujwiri and Hazrat Mian Mir among her fieldwork sites in Lahore (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 20, folio not stated), and classes him among the "jamālī or aesthetic mystics" whose shrines stand against the *jalali* reputation of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 96, folio not stated). Kugle writes that "In Lahore today, the Qalandars (who completely abandon legal norms and social conventions and even gender distinctions) are still associated with the great" Qadiri saints of the city, "like Shah Hussayn, Shah Jamal, and Miyan Mir." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 326, folio 305, ch. 4 n. 7). Chishti closes his account of the saint's wonders by calling them countless, too many to write down in a lifetime (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 73); Kanhaiya Lal says only that his wonders are very famous among Muslims, and narrates none of them (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 233, folio 236).

## Legends and Miracles

The Ganga pilgrimage of the current account is told in three books. In Sharib, Madho's parents went to bathe in the Ganga and wished to take him, but the saint did not permit it (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 254, folio 250). He told Madho to close his eyes and place his foot on his own; when he opened his eyes he saw that they were standing on the bank of the Ganga; they bathed with the parents and returned to Lahore the same way, which the parents, on their return, confirmed (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 254, folio 250). Kugle: "Madho defied them and stayed back in Lahore, but Hussayn, in a miracle, took him from Lahore to Haridwar in one step." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 212, folio 191, ch. 4 n. 15); "This miracle may contain hints at reconciliation between Hussayn and Madho's family" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 212, folio 191). Chishti sets Madho's sincere conversion on that day (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 59).

Sharib gathers other wonders under the heading "Unveilings and Wonders". His companions asked for the books he had thrown down the well; he went to the well and told it to give the books back; at once the water rose, and the books were floating in it, not in the least wet (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 254, folio 250). Once the headman of the village of Mandiya (the name uncertain in the printed text) shut him and his companions in a room until it should rain; very well, he said, but feed us bread and chicken, and then we will pray (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 254, folio 250). After the meal he looked to the sky and asked what delay there was now; no sooner had he said it than clouds gathered and it rained heavily (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 254–255, folios 250–251).

Kugle retells, as "An apocryphal story", a contest with Shaykh Hassu, who teased Hussayn, "for I have never seen you in the court of the Prophet [Muhammad]!" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, pp. 207–208, folios 186, 187). The next day, "when Muhammad held his court in the spirit world, with all the prophets and saints in attendance including Shaykh Hassu, a child appeared" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, pp. 207–208, folios 186, 187); the child sat in the Prophet's lap, passed round the assembly, and "naughtily plucked some hairs from Shaykh Hassu's beard"; challenged again, "Shah Hussayn silently produced before him a hair from his very own beard", and Hassu conceded: "So it was you, was it? Ah well, it was as a child that you got the better of me" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, pp. 207–208, folios 186, 187). The beard, Kugle explains, "is an outward sign of piety for Muslim men and a way of signifying one's reputation" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 208, folio 187).

The poems themselves, in Alam's translation, narrate no miracle of any saint (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, pp. 68–178).

## The Sources of the Story

The books trace most of the story to one Persian poem, *Haqiqat al-Fuqara*. Kanhaiya Lal writes that Pir Muhammad wrote a Persian verse book on the saint's state called *Haqiqat al-Fuqara*, and that he recorded thousands of Husain's wonders (the number uncertain in the printed text) as an eyewitness (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 233, folio 236). Chishti names his source as the book *Haqiqat al-Fuqara* by Shaykh Pir Muhammad Sahib, whose name as a chronogram is Shaykh Mahmud, the name uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 45). Alam: "Haqiqat al-Fuqra (Truth of the Saints), written around 1660 by Sheikh Mahmud, commonly known as Muhammad Pir, established the trend for hagiography that continues till the present." "Another oft-cited source is the Tehqiqat-i-Chisti: Tarikh-i-Lahore ka Encyclopedia (Researches of Chisti: Encyclopedia of Lahore's History) by Noor Ahmed Chisti", and "The latter heavily relies on the former" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 8, folio 8). Kugle: "This hagiographic poem was composed about 1662, just sixty-two years after Shah Hussayn's death. The author's father had been a companion of Shah Hussayn's, while the author himself was a close attendant of his successor, Madho." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 326, folio 305, ch. 4 n. 6). Lahori's editor calls it, at 1041 AH, the oldest of the local histories (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 55, folio 43). Kugle says that the poem survives as an "unpublished original manuscript at Punjab University in Lahore" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 218, folio 197, ch. 4 n. 27); he found no evidence of a printing that Shuja Alhaq had claimed for Lahore in 1966, and doubts that it has ever been published (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 328, folio 307, ch. 4 n. 27). Parts of his own translation of it he had published earlier in Vanita and Kidwai's *Same Sex Love in India*, and smaller excerpts, he notes, had been translated by Christopher Shackle in 'Beyond Hindu and Turk: Crossing the Boundaries in Indo-Muslim Romance' (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 326, folio 305, ch. 4 n. 3).

Kugle also judges what the later books did with it. Chishti's *Tahqiqat-i Chishti* "describes Shah Hussayn and Madho, claiming to quote Haqiqat al-Fuqara"; "Most contemporary Urdu sources are based on Tahqiqat-i Chishti and repeat his erasure"; "Other Urdu sources mention nothing of Madho at all and also neglect Hussayn's poetic output"; and "even in the Persian collection of saints' stories composed in 1865", Lahori's *Khazinat al-Asfiya*, "Madho is mentioned only once, in an accusation against Hussayn that is never confirmed." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 327, folio 306, ch. 4 n. 22). In an Urdu translation of the list of offences in Chishti, he notes, "the allegation about dancing with handsome young men has been elided entirely." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 329, folio 308, ch. 4 n. 57). He explains the modern silence on the love as a matter of access: writers "have been unable to access the records that display it" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 218, folio 197).

Kugle's bibliography lists the modern studies: Shafqat Tanwir Mirza's *Shah Hussayn* (Islamabad: Lok Wirsa Isha'at Ghar, 1989), A. R. Luther and Ramakrishna's *Madho Lal Hussayn: Sufi Poet of the Punjab* (Lahore: Shaykh Mubarak Ali, 1982), Azra Waqar's article 'Devotional Songs of Shah Hussain Lahori' (1996), and M. Habibullah Faruqi and Mohan Singh Diwana's *Halat o Kafiyan Madho Lal Hussayn* (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 340, folio 319; Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 345, folio 324; Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 346, folio 325; Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 339, folio 318). The editions Alam uses include Muhammad Asif Khan's *Kafis: Shah Hussein* (Pakistan Punjabi Adabi Board, 2014), Syed Nazir Ahmed's *Poetry of Shah Hussein* (Babar Ali Foundation, 2014), Najm Hosain Syed's *Kafis of Madho Lal Hussein* (Suchet Kitab Ghar, 2014) and Saleem Akhtar's Urdu *Maiy Ni Mein Kino Akhan* (Book Home, 2010) (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 178, folio 178; Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 179, folio 179). Shackle and Kugle both list Ghulam Yaqoob Anwar's translation *The Paths Unknown*, published in Lahore by the Majlis Shah Hussain in 1966 (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 203, folio 178; Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 341, folio 320), and Shackle Maqbul Anvar Daudi's edition *Kahiā Shāh Husain neñ* (Ferozsons, 1990) (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 203, folio 178). Abbas lists *Kafian Shah Hussain*, edited by Maqbool Anwar Dawoodi, and *Kafiān*, translated by Abdul Majid Bhatti for the Institute of Folk Heritage (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 233, folio not stated; Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 225, folio not stated), and Rafat's selection cites Nazir Ahmad's *Kalam Shah Husain* (Packages Ltd., 1979) (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 242, folio 222).

## Setting

Lahore in his lifetime was a Mughal capital. Alam writes that "Akbar had temporarily shifted his capital to Lahore" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 7, folio 7); Kugle calls it "the Mughal regional capital of Lahor (Lahore in contemporary Pakistan)" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 202, folio not stated), and "a seasonal capital city and home of the Mughal court during its perennial rotation" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 233, folio 212). Abbas calls the Shalimar Gardens "Gardens built by Shah Jehan, the Mughal emperor" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 188, folio not stated), and Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, describing the course of the Ravi, has the royal canal from the river run to the Shalamar garden at Lahore (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 512). Of the neighbourhood Kugle writes that Baghabanpur lay "on the outskirts of Lahore (the neighborhood is now within the expanding city just next to the Shalimar gardens)" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 215, folio 194). Chishti, writing of the city, counts the Shalimar Bagh among Shah Jahan's memorials in Lahore, with the praise that the eye of the age has seldom seen such a garden (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 833); and of Baghbanpura he records that when Ahmad Shah Abdali came and Begumpura was plundered, the family whose story he is telling moved into the city and went back to Baghbanpura when he left (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 724). Writing of the Punjab's weaving castes, Kugle notes: "New Muslims (naw-musalman) from weaver castes converted to join the Islamic community in Punjab slightly later than in Hindustan, where the mystic poet Kabir displays the same kind of Sufi-bhakti synthesis a century earlier." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 329, folio 308).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The year of his birth.** The current account gives 1538. Alam gives 1538 too (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 6, folio 6); Kugle "around 1539 C.E." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 206, folio 185, ch. 4 n. 7); Rafat's selection 1539 (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 24, folio not stated); Schimmel 1539 (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 415, folio 384). Sharib and Lahori's author give 945 AH (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 249, folio 245; Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 55–56, folios 43–44). Chishti's chronogram cannot be read (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 45).
- **The year of his death.** The current account gives 1599, as do Kugle, "in 1599 C.E. at the age of about 60" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 219, folio 198), and Alam (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 6, folio 6). Lahori's author, Kanhaiya Lal and Sharib give 1008 AH, Kanhaiya Lal's figure printed uncertainly (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 55–56, folios 43–44; Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 233, folio 236; Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 253, folio 249), and Chishti a Friday at the end of Jumada al-Thani in the year 1008 AH, the last word of the number uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 70). Lahori's editor reports 1013 AH from *Miftah al-Arifin*, and himself concludes for 999 AH on Badayuni's report that the saint fell from a roof (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 55, folio 43). Rafat's selection gives 1593 three times (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 24, folio not stated; Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 25, folio not stated; Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 29, folio not stated), and Schimmel 1593 in both her books (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 357, folio 339; Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 520, folio 489).
- **His age at death, and the count of the ʿurs.** Kugle says about 60 (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 219, folio 198); Sharib sixty-three, the numeral uncertain in the printed text (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 253, folio 249); Chishti's figure cannot be read (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 69). Alam gives the saint's dates as 1538–99 and his death as "more than 400 years" before (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 6, folio 6), and calls the festival of March 2016 his "428th urs", printing both without comment (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 19, folio 19).
- **Who in his family converted, and when.** Lahori's author makes the convert his grandfather, whose printed name, perhaps Kaljas Rai, cannot be read with certainty, in the time of Firoz Shah Tughlaq (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 54, folio 42), and Kanhaiya Lal a forebear, perhaps Kamas Rai, the name uncertain in the printed text, whose grandson he was, in an era whose printed name cannot be read with certainty (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 233, folio 236). Sharib makes the convert his father, whose printed name, perhaps Kalsan Rai, cannot be read with certainty, under Sultan Firoz Shah (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 249, folio 245); Alam his father too, "during the Delhi Sultanate (1206–1526)" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 6, folio 6); Chishti an ancestor titled Shaykh al-Islam (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 45). Kugle says only that the family had joined Islam a few generations before (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 218, folio 197).
- **His caste and his father's trade.** The current account has a family of weavers, the father by tradition a craftsman who stitched uniforms for Humayun's army. Lahori has the father earn his living by the work of weavers (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 54, folio 42); Sharib has him weave cloth and calls the forebears Kayasth (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 249, folio 245); Chishti gives the father's trade as weaving and the name Dhadha, uncertain in the printed text, as a Rajput caste (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 45); Alam makes the father a Rajput of the Kalsrai clan and the mother a Dadha Rajput (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 6, folio 6; Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 8, folio 8). Kugle holds that the weaver castes "had most likely been Shudras" and that the stories of a noble lineage "are later accretions" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 329, folio 308, ch. 4 n. 52).
- **His age when the master came.** The current account says he had the Quran by heart by the age of seven. Lahori's author has Bahlul's glance fall on him at seven, while his editor reports *Haqiqat al-Fuqara*'s age of ten, in 955 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 54, folio 42); Chishti has him ten when Bahlul came, with the seventh *sipara* done (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 50); Kugle has him initiated at Chiniot at about ten (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 326, folio 305, ch. 4 n. 7); Sharib has him at seven in the *maktab* with only six *paras* by heart, the whole Quran coming to him that Ramadan by a blessing (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 249, folio 245; Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 250, folio 246).
- **The years at Data Ganj Bakhsh.** The current account gives some twelve years of *chilla* by the darbar, and Sharib and Chishti twelve years of attendance (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 251, folio 247; Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 53). Kugle has him sleep at the shrine "For the first half of his life" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 206, folio 185).
- **His age at the turn.** The current account says around thirty-two. Alam says "at the age of thirty-six" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 7, folio 7), and Kugle "at the age of 36" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 206, folio 185).
- **His order.** The current account calls him Malamati, and so do Lahori (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 54, folio 42), Chishti (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 56), Sharib, who adds a *qalandarana mashrab* (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 254, folio 250), Alam (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 7, folio 7) and Khalid (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated); Rafat's selection speaks only of "malamatia overtones" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 24, folio not stated). Kugle calls him Qadiri by lineage and a Qalandar by mode of life, and never Malamati (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 202, folio not stated; Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 220, folio 199); Schimmel calls him "the ecstatic mystic of the Qadiri order" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, pp. 170–171, folios 152–153); Kanhaiya Lal names his pir Shaykh Bahlol Qadri (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 233, folio 236).
- **The flask.** The current account has him carry a flask that the scandalised took for wine. Kugle writes "He took to drinking wine" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 207, folio 186); Alam has "a wine flask in one hand" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 7, folio 7); Sharib says he used intoxicating things (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 254, folio 250); Chishti has him give Madho wine and make him a *wali* through drink (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 60).
- **Which emperor, and what the wine became.** The current account names Jahangir, and has the flask run as milk, honey and the water of Zamzam. Sharib, Kugle and Alam name Akbar (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 253, folio 249; Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 240, folio 219; Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, pp. 7–8, folios 7–8). Kugle has the question whether he could turn wine "into milk, water, tea, or punch", and the goblet turn "into water and other beverages" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 240, folio 219); Alam's sources have him turn wine "into water, sherbet, milk, tea, vinegar and back to wine again" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 6, folio 6), and in Alam's legend Akbar was unimpressed by the miracle and sent him to prison, freeing him only after his likeness appeared in the harem (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, pp. 7–8, folios 7–8).
- **How the court let him be.** In the current account the court says "let no one trouble him after this day." In Alam's report of *Haqiqat al-Fuqara* the saint himself asked never to be summoned again (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 181, folio 181); in Sharib Akbar dismissed him with honour (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 253, folio 249); in Chishti Akbar became his servant (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 65).
- **Who asked him about the pillars of Islam.** Sharib names the *qazi* of Lahore, who smiled and went away (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 252, folio 248; Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 253, folio 249). Kugle names the Shaykh al-Islam Abd al-Nabi Sultanpuri (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 239, folio 218).
- **The Khan-e Khanan's victory.** Kugle has the noble stop at Lahore for the saint's blessing on the way to Sindh, and Thatta fall (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 241, folio 220); Chishti has Thatta granted to him by the saint's prayer (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 68). Alam reports the hagiographers' story and sets against it a kafi that offers "a caveat of mortality to the powerful" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 184, folio 184).
- **The scribe and his book.** The current account has a court official, Dehan Bahar Khan, set the kafis to paper as the saint sang them. Sharib has Prince Salim give Bahadur Khan the writing of the saint's *roznamcha* [OCR?] (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 253, folio 249); Alam has Jehangir appoint "Bahar Khan to keep a daily log of Hussein's activities and sayings", the *Baharia*, which is not extant (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 8, folio 8); Kugle has Salim order Bahar Khan to "write down his teachings and doings", in a work now lost (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 206, folio 185, ch. 4 nn. 5, 6).
- **The date of Haqiqat al-Fuqara.** Lahori's editor dates it 1041 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 55, folio 43); Alam "around 1660" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 8, folio 8); Kugle "about 1662" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 326, folio 305, ch. 4 n. 6).
- **Whether the signature is the poet's.** The current account says almost all the kafis close with the poet naming himself, and Alam says so "In almost every concluding line" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 16, folio 16). Rafat's selection reports Dr. Nazir Ahmad's view that the self-reference "was frequently an interpolation of the qawwals" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 242, folio 222).
- **Whether the poems name Madho.** Alam finds Madho addressed directly in one kafi, and possibly in two more, though 'Lal' may mean only "dear or precious one" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 10, folio 10). Rafat's selection calls the allusion "indirect" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 45, folio 25), and reports Nazir Ahmad's view that the kafis make no reference to Madho Lal at all (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 242, folio 222). Schimmel writes that he "had sung of his love for a Hindu boy in simple Panjabi verses" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 415, folio 384).
- **Who made Hir a mystical theme.** Khalid writes that he "for the first time" made the story a spiritual legend (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated). Schimmel says he "seems to inaugurate (or perhaps even continue from unknown beginnings)" the tradition (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 415, folio 384). Shackle says the story "makes some appearances" in his poetry, but that Bulleh Shah "properly exploits" its allegory (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 21, folio xx).
- **The authorship of four poems.** Shackle prints kafis 29, 63, 130 and 145 as Bulleh Shah's, though they are also attributed to Shah Husain and one edition excludes two of them as his; he holds the rival attribution not "definitive" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 24, folio xxiii; Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 164, folio 139).
- **The nature of the love.** The current account has the saint say that his love was not carnal but a kind of wonder: "I do not love him — I envy his Creator, for how beautiful He has made him." Kugle writes of "a primary erotic attachment to another man" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 205, folio 184), and that "most writers do not want to admit they were lovers" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 217, folio 196, ch. 4 n. 26). Alam finds a poem open to "the queer interpretation" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 181, folio 181), and reports Dr Syed Nazir Ahmed's view that, given the age gap, a romance between them is "repulsive to the mild taste" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 10, folio 10).
- **Madho's age, and the courtship.** The current account calls him a young man of a Hindu Brahmin family. Sharib makes him about sixteen and already married, with sixteen years of circling his house before he took notice (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 252, folio 248); Kugle "a Brahmin teenager", the saint "of forty-odd years" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 208, folio 187); Alam reports a thirty-eight-year gap between them (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 10, folio 10).
- **Whether Madho converted.** Lahori, Kanhaiya Lal and Chishti say he became a Muslim (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 64–65, folios 52–53; Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 233, folio 236; Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 58), Chishti once on the day of the Ganga miracle and once at eighteen (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 59; Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 72), and Sharib gives his Muslim name as Mian Mahbub al-Haq (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 252, folio 248). Kugle doubts it, since he is still known by his Hindu name, which is on his tombstone (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 219, folio 198), and Alam says that the hagiographers treat the conversion as "a necessary step towards the latter's salvation" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 8, folio 8). Lahori's editor doubts whether Madho existed at all (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 64–65).
- **Madho's name.** The current account calls the youth Madho Lal, and so do Alam, "Madho (pronounced Madh-oh) Lal" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 6, folio 6), and Rafat's selection (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 242, folio 222). Kugle calls it a false conclusion that Madho's full name was 'Madho Lal': 'Lal Hussayn' was the saint's own nickname (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 328, folio 307, ch. 4 n. 24).
- **The Ganga miracle.** In the current account Madho, bound by custom to make the Ganga Snan and fearing his parents' anger, took the saint's hands, set his feet on the saint's own and closed his eyes. In Sharib his parents wished to take him and the saint would not permit it; then Madho put his foot on the saint's, they bathed with the parents, and the parents confirmed it (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 254, folio 250). In Kugle "Madho defied them and stayed back in Lahore", and the saint took him "from Lahore to Haridwar in one step" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 212, folio 191, ch. 4 n. 15).
- **Where the two lived.** Kugle has Madho come to live with the saint "at Baghabanpur on the outskirts of Lahore" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 215, folio 194). Alam has the saint move "across the river Ravi to the town of Shahdara", where they later lived together (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 181, folio 181).
- **Babupur.** Kugle sets the first meeting in "the village of Babupur, far from Lahore", and nowhere says whether it is Baghabanpur (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 212, folio 191). Chishti names Babupura (the name uncertain in the printed text) as the place the saint loved and where he foretold his burial (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 61), and the voice after the flood orders the burial at Babupura (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 71); he makes Madho a *wali* at a place printed uncertainly as Babupura (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 60).
- **Madho's absence.** The current account says the saint sent Madho away before his death, some say into the army. Kugle says Madho himself "asked permission to join the military service", for three years in Bengal and the Deccan under Raja Man Singh, and had come back before the death (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 219, folio 198). Chishti has him serve Raja Man Singh at Delhi and on the Deccan campaign (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 60).
- **Madho after the death.** The current account has him return and never leave the grave. Kugle has him join the army again for twelve years, then live at the tomb for thirty-five more (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 219, folio 198). Chishti has him sent in a dream to Raja Man Singh for twelve years, and seated as *sajjada nashin* after thirteen (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 70; Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 71).
- **Madho's dates.** Lahori's author gives 983 to 1056 AH, aged 73 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 65, folio 53), and his editor 22 Zi al-Hijja 1056 AH / 1646 CE (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 65, folio 53); Kanhaiya Lal gives his death as 1057 AH, the figure uncertain in the printed text (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 233, folio 236); Chishti gives the twenty-second of Dhu al-Hijja, a Monday, at an age of seventy-two that is uncertain in the printed text, the year illegible (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 72). Alam gives no dates for him (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 18, folio 18).
- **The first grave and the moving of it.** The current account has the saint ask to be buried on the bank of the Ravi, the flood carry the grave to Baghbanpura where a *jogi* dwelt, and a rosary, a water-pot and a prayer-mat mark the true spot. Kanhaiya Lal has him buried near Shahdara, and Madho move the coffin when the river came near (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 233, folio 236). Chishti has him carried across the Ravi and buried where he had chosen; the flood found the grave empty, a bouquet of *rihan* flowers was found, and a voice ordered the burial at Babupura (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 69; Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 70; Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 71), the place having been marked beforehand with a prayer-mat and a pillow, both words uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 61). Kugle has the saint prepare his own grave "next to their home", with no move (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 219, folio 198).
- **The scale of the shrine.** The current account describes some twenty kanals, with a mosque and a large graveyard. Schimmel calls it "a modest tomb" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, pp. 170–171, folios 152–153), Alam "the modest shrine" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, pp. 18–19, folios 18–19), and Kugle "His small tomb" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 241, folio 220).
- **The festival's date.** The current account gives the 9th to the 11th of Shawwal, falling in recent years in March and April, for three days. Kanhaiya Lal gives the first of Rajab formerly, and in his day the end of March, one night at the khanqah and a day in the Shalamar Bagh (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 234, folio 237); Schimmel "the last Saturday in March" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 415, folio 384); Alam "The last week of March" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 19, folio 19). Sharib gives no month (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 253, folio 249).
- **The festival's place and name.** The current account has the lamps long spill from the shrine into the Shalimar Gardens, and the fair later drawn back within the precincts. Kanhaiya Lal has the lamp fair spend its day in the Shalamar Bagh (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 234, folio 237); Abbas places her "Melā Chirāg Urs" at the Shalimar Gardens (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 95, folio not stated; Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 185, folio not stated); Schimmel "near the Shalimar Garden" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 415, folio 384). Alam calls the ʿurs itself the Festival of Lamps (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 19, folio 19), Abbas has the festival "coinciding with the urs" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 185, folio not stated), and Kanhaiya Lal counts it as one of two spring fairs, without the word *ʿurs* (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 234, folio 237).
- **Ranjit Singh and Basant.** The current account remembers Ranjit Singh and his court at the Mela Chiraghan, and says the Mela at its height rivalled the festival of Basant. Kanhaiya Lal has the Maharaja come in state on Basant day, to a Basant fair held at the khanqah itself (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 234, folio 237).
- **The khalifas.** Lahori's book counts sixteen in four groups of four, among them four Khakis and Shah Mast Bilawal (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 55, folio 43). Sharib also says sixteen, but names three Khakis, and among the Bilawals Shah Bilawal and Shah Bilawal Dakkani (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 253, folio 249; Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 254, folio 250).

## Legacy

Shah Hussain — Madho Lal Hussain — occupies a place all his own in the memory of the Punjab. He is honoured as the father of the Punjabi kafi and so as the fountainhead of a tradition of Sufi song that runs through Bulleh Shah and Sultan Bahu down to the present day; as a Malamati saint who dramatised, in his own scandalous freedom, the truth that the love of God is worth more than the good opinion of the world; and as the lover of Madho, whose story has made his shrine a symbol of harmony between Muslim and Hindu, and of a love that death itself could not part. Amid the crowds and the lamps of the Mela Chiraghan, and in every kafi sung in his voice, the poet-saint of Lahore lives on.

The books bear out that place in different registers. The introduction to Rafat's selection calls him "the second great Punjabi poet" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 47, folio 27) and sets him with Baba Farid as Bulleh Shah's predecessors (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 35, folio 15). Haroon Khalid names Shah Hussain with Baba Farid, Guru Nanak, Bulleh Shah and Waris Shah among the figures who have "become a part of" his own existence (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 151, folio 133), and among the Punjabi poets whose poetry "the educated elite of Punjab ends up disowning, in fact, ridiculing" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 128, folio 110).

His name is carried by institutions and recordings. A Majlis Shah Hussain in Lahore published Anwar's translation of the kafis in 1966 and Najm Hosain Syed's *Recurrent Patterns in Punjabi Poetry* in 1968 (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 341, folio 320; Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 346, folio 325); none of the books says more of it. The editor of Lahori's Urdu edition signs himself lecturer in history at Shah Husain College, Lahore (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 306, folio 294). Abbas lists an audiocassette from the Institute of Folk Heritage, Islamabad, *A Tribute to Great Mystics: Bulleh Shah, Shah Hussain, and Khawaja Farid* (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 205, 212, 216, folios not stated), and records him first in Abida Parvin's list, "I sing Shah Hussain, Bulle Shah…" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 54, folio not stated), and as an item, "Shah Hussain / Panjabi", on the programme of a concert in Islamabad (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 101, folio not stated).
