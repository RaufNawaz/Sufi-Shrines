## Overview

The Shrine of Shah Abdul Karim stands at Bulri, the town in Tando Muhammad Khan district that bears his name — Bulri Shah Karim — and honours one of the earliest and most influential of the Sindhi Sufi poets. Shah Abdul Karim (1536–1623) is remembered as a mystic of simple, laborious life and as a pioneer of Sindhi devotional verse, and as the forebear of Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai, the poet of Bhit, who is said to have overseen the building of the shrine complex about 1741. The dargah remains a vibrant place of devotion, and its annual *urs* draws thousands of pilgrims.

The books consulted for this article speak of him far more as a poet and an ancestor than as the saint of a shrine. Only one of them describes the shrine itself: H. T. Sorley, in his 1940 study of Shah Abdul Latif, writes of the "famous Sayid Abdul Karim" that his *durgah* "was at Bulri" and "is still a great place of yearly pilgrimage for its Shah Karim fair (mero) held on the saint's" *ʿurs* day (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 180–181, folios 171–172); it is the only *ʿurs* named in his book. Christopher Shackle, Annemarie Schimmel and Michel Boivin, writing of his poetry and of his descendant, say nothing of a tomb, a shrine, an *ʿurs* or custodians at Bulri. Their material is about the man and his verse, and it is given here as that.

Schimmel calls him "the most important figure in the next century", and writes that "in his Persian malfuzat 93 verses in Sindhi are preserved, which contain all the ingredients of later mystical poetry" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 161, folio 161). Shackle introduces him as a man "who had been a noted spiritual teacher in his own right" and who also composed pioneering Sufi poems in Sindhi (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 9). Sorley records that his name "is still held in reverence in Lower Sind" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 212, folio 203).

## The Life of the Saint

Shah Abdul Karim (1536–1623) was born into a Sayyid family of Matiari but spent most of his life at Bulri, from which he takes his epithet. Alam Faqri's *Tazkirah Awliya-e-Pakistan* adds that he was himself a disciple (*murid*) of Makhdoom Nooh of Hala before settling and preaching at Bulri.

The books carry his years in different degrees of precision. Schimmel gives both, "(1536-1623)" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 161, folio 161); Shackle gives his death, "(d. 1623)" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 9), and so does the index of Schimmel's *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, which prints the year 1623 beside his name (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 510, folio 479). Sorley gives no death year at all, only a floruit: "circa A.D. 1600" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 181, folio 172). Beyond Alam Faqri's account of his discipleship, none of the sources consulted names a teacher, an order or a chain of spiritual descent for him.

His names vary across the books. Sorley calls him "Sayid Abdul Karim" and "Shah Karim"; Boivin "Shah Abd al-Karim, or Shah Karim" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 91, folio 78); Schimmel "Shah Abdul Karim of" Bulri, with the place-name printed as 'Bulrri' (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 161, folio 161). Shackle writes plainly of "Shah Abdul Karim (d. 1623) of Bulri in southern Sindh" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 9).

Sorley marks a gap in what is known of the family. "It is not known how the family had migrated to Bulri or why, in the generations immediately preceding Shah Abdul Latif's birth, it had returned to the vicinity of Matiari" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 181, folio 172).

## Ancestor of the Poet of Bhit

He is remembered above all as the forebear of Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai, though the sources count the generations between them differently. The site's account calls Shah Abdul Latif his celebrated great-great-grandson. A separate source, Alam Faqri's *Tazkirah Awliya-e-Pakistan*, instead calls him Bhittai's great-grandfather (three generations, not four); the two are recorded here as given, without picking one generational count over the other.

The books divide the same way. Shackle writes: "His great-great-grandfather Shah Abdul Karim (d. 1623) of Bulri in southern Sindh, who had been a noted spiritual teacher in his own right, also composed pioneering" Sufi "poems in Sindhi" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 9). Sorley says it twice and in agreement: "His ancestor in the fourth generation before him was the famous Sayid Abdul Karim" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 180–181, folios 171–172), and "The poet's great-great-grandfather was the Shah Karim whose name is still held in reverence in Lower Sind" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 212, folio 203). Schimmel, in *Pain and Grace*, calls him "Shah Abdul Latif's great-grandfather" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 161, folio 161), and earlier in the same book writes, without counting the generations, that Shah Abdul Latif's "father, Shah Habib, was a descendant of one of the first noted poets in his mother tongue, Shah Abdul Karim of" Bulri (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 29, folio 25). In *As Through a Veil* she again names him only as the great-grandfather of Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 171, folio 153), and in *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* simply as Abdul Latif's ancestor (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, pp. 420–421, folios 389–390). Boivin writes of Shah Abdul Latif: "He was the great-grandson of Shah Abd al-Karim, or Shah Karim." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 91, folio 78).

## The Shrine

Tradition holds that Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai himself stayed two years at Bulri and supervised the building of the shrine complex, laying the foundation of the tomb and its three-domed mosque about 1741 (1154 AH). None of the books consulted for this article gives a date or a builder for the tomb or the mosque.

Sorley is the only one of them to describe the shrine. In his words the saint's *durgah* "was at Bulri" in a *taluka* whose name is damaged in the printed text, "of the Hyderabad district", and it "is still a great place of yearly pilgrimage for its Shah Karim fair (mero) held on the saint's" *ʿurs* day (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 180–181, folios 171–172). His index enters Bulri once, at the page where the saint appears (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 433), and the saint under both his names (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 439; Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 440). Sorley places Bulri in the Hyderabad district, where the site's account today places it in Tando Muhammad Khan district.

The sources consulted record nothing of the shrine's custodians, endowments or later repairs.

## Devotion and Practice

The dargah remains a vibrant place of devotion, and its annual *urs* draws thousands of pilgrims. Sorley gives the yearly gathering its Sindhi name: the Shah Karim fair, the *mero*, held on the saint's *ʿurs* day, which makes Bulri, in his words, "a great place of yearly pilgrimage" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 180–181, folios 171–172). Neither he nor any other source consulted gives the date of the *ʿurs* in the calendar.

## The Poet

He was among the first to weave the folk romances of the land — *Sasui and Punhun*, *Sohni and Mehar*, *Umar and Marui*, *Leela and Chanesar* — into poetry expressing the soul's longing for the Divine, a method later brought to its summit by his celebrated great-great-grandson, Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai.

Schimmel credits him with exactly that step: "He seems to be the first one to use allusions to Sindhi folk tales in his verses, thus to Sassui" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 161, folio 161). Her illustration is a verse that names Bhambhore:

*Nobody ever took with himself / two things at once from Bhambhore, / Yearning for the beloved, / and attachment with one's world* (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 162, folio 162)

Her note takes it from Motilal Jotwani's collection of his verses, where it is numbered XVII. In *As Through a Veil* she sets him in a line of three: in Sind, she writes, "the range of folk tales utilized for mystical purposes is even wider"; early allusions appear "however veiled, in Qadi Qadan's verse"; they "become more obvious in Shah 'Abdul Karim of" Bulri's "short verses, and reach full bloom in the" *Risalo* "of his great-grandson, Shah 'Abdul Latif of Bhit" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 171, folio 153). In *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* she writes that the poems of Abdul Latif's ancestor Shah Abdul Karim, or those of Shah Inat, in the seventeenth century "are closely related to Latif's poetry" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, pp. 420–421, folios 389–390).

## The Record of His Sayings

His sayings and verses were first gathered by a disciple, Mir Daryai Tharawi, in the Persian work *Bayan al-Arifin*, completed in 1630, seven years after the poet's death. Shackle, who does not name the work or date it, writes: "These were recorded soon after Karim's death in a lengthy Persian memoir composed by a disciple and are known to have been treasured by Shah Latif" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 9). Schimmel calls the work his "Persian malfuzat" and counts in it "93 verses in Sindhi" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 161, folio 161). Boivin's bibliography lists a printed edition under a title that names Bulri: "*Shah Karim Bulri wari jo kalam bayan al-arifin va tanbiyya al-ghafaliyyan*, ed. Doctor Omar Muhammad Daudpota, Bombay, Qayyimah Press, 1937/1356" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 232, folio 219), the only item in that bibliography dated in both the Christian and the Hijri calendars.

## In Verse

Schimmel quotes two of his verses whole. One is a picture of village life:

*Like a jar poised on woman-water-carrier, / and a bird on the water, / Our beloved in the same way / has been close to our soul* (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 162, folio 162)

Her note gives it as number XII of the same collection. The other is on the swan, an image his descendant also used:

*A swan feeds on pearls, / he dives deep down, / He who plays in the shallows* (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 168, folio 168)

The fourth line, whose verb is damaged in the printed text, calls that one 'only an ordinary bird'. Schimmel's comment: "The imagery of the swan must have been popular in Sind for a long time, for Abdul Karim uses it more than a century before his descendant Latif" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 168, folio 168).

A third fragment survives inside a line of Shah Abdul Latif's. Of a verse of his descendant's, Schimmel writes: "The second line is a quotation from Shah Abdul Karim who then continues: The beloved is not separated from you, turn only your face inside" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 206, folio 206); her note cites Jotwani's number XIX.

Boivin traces a phrase to him: *tuhnjo Allah hekro*, 'Your God is One', "which first appears in Shah Karim's verses and then in Shah Abd al-Latif" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 100, folio 87). He finds in him, too, the figure of the *jogi* that his descendant would develop: "we can find it [the figure of the jogi] in Shah Karim's poetry, but it is fully expressed in Shah Abd al-Latif's verses." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 105, folio 92).

Schimmel names further motifs that begin with him. "The motif of the thief is found already in the poems of Shah Latif's ancestor, Shah Abdul Karim" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 197, folio 197). "A verse similar to Shah's saying is already found in Shah Abdul Karim's poetry" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 200, folio 200), her note citing Jotwani's number XXXVI. And of verse that mocks the *madrasa* curriculum: "It was probably Shah Latif's ancestor Abdul Karim who invented this type of poetry, which became very popular in later times" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 202, folio 202); she names Sachal Sarmast and Bedil of Rohri among those who carried it on (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 202, folio 202).

Boivin, in a passage on Qazi Qazan, whose poetry he calls "the oldest known Sufi poetry from Sindh", writes that of it "only seven verses (baits) were known, and only since they were quoted by Shah Karim in his own kalam" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 171, folio 158). The passage names him only as Shah Karim, with no place or dates; it is given here as a pointer, since Boivin uses the same short name for the saint of Bulri elsewhere in his book, but it does not say in so many words that this is the same man.

## Legends and Miracles

Tradition holds that Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai stayed two years at Bulri and supervised the building of his ancestor's shrine, laying the foundation of the tomb and its three-domed mosque about 1741 (1154 AH).

Schimmel records a different kind of story, which takes him far from Bulri: "an anecdote relates that Latif's ancestor, Shah Abdul Karim, was taught 'the one crooked word', e.g., OM, by an ascetic in Ahmadabad" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 213, folio 213). She tells it only to set aside a Hindu reading of the symbolism of the letters *alif* and *mim* in Shah Abdul Latif's verse, judging that "this seems too far fetched, and has no parallels in Shah Latif's poetry" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 213, folio 213).

## Setting

The books place him at the head of a Sindhi tradition. Shackle, cautioning against reading Shah Abdul Latif as an unlettered genius, names Shah Abdul Karim and Shah Inat Rizvi (d. 1711) as his forerunners, and concludes: "There was, therefore, already an established culture in Sindh of vernacular" Sufi "poetry, although this tends to be overshadowed by Shah Latif's unique reputation in the literary histories" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 9). Schimmel writes of the province in general that "It seems that in Sind, as in other parts of the Muslim world, the Sufis are responsible for the development of the spoken language into a literary one" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 161, folio 161).

Of the district in which the site's account places Bulri, Boivin's book mentions only another figure, at another place: "Mohsin Shah, the gaddi nashin of Tando Saindad near Tando Muhammad Khan", "a Sufi who was affiliated to the Qalandariyya" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 123, folio 110). It says nothing of Bulri.

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **How many generations from Shah Abdul Latif.** The site's account calls Shah Abdul Latif his great-great-grandson; Alam Faqri's *Tazkirah Awliya-e-Pakistan*, as the site's account reports it, calls him Bhittai's great-grandfather. Shackle (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 9) and Sorley (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 180–181, folios 171–172; Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 212, folio 203) count four generations. Schimmel (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 161, folio 161; Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 171, folio 153) and Boivin (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 91, folio 78) count three. Schimmel elsewhere calls Shah Abdul Latif's father simply his descendant, without a count (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 29, folio 25).
- **His dates.** The site's account and Schimmel give 1536–1623 (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 161, folio 161); Shackle (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 9) and the index of *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 510, folio 479) give only the death year, 1623; Sorley gives no death year and only a floruit of about 1600 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 181, folio 172).
- **The district.** The site's account places Bulri in Tando Muhammad Khan district; Sorley places the *durgah* in a *taluka* of the Hyderabad district (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 180–181, folios 171–172).
- **The family and Matiari.** The site's account says he was born into a Sayyid family of Matiari and spent most of his life at Bulri. Sorley writes that it is not known how the family came to Bulri, or why it later returned to the vicinity of Matiari (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 181, folio 172).
- **Who first used the folk tales.** The site's account has him among the first to turn the folk romances to mystical ends. Schimmel's *Pain and Grace* says he "seems to be the first one" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 161, folio 161); her *As Through a Veil* finds earlier, veiled allusions in the verse of Qadi Qadan (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 171, folio 153).
- **The worth of his verse.** Schimmel calls him "the most important figure in the next century" and writes that his verses "contain all the ingredients of later mystical poetry" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 161, folio 161). Sorley writes that "Though some poetry is attributed to Shah Karim and a few of his verses are believed to be incorporated in the Risalo", nothing "of outstanding merit" by him has survived (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 212, folio 203).
- **Whose verses are in the *Risalo*.** Sorley reports the belief that a few of Shah Karim's verses are incorporated in Shah Abdul Latif's *Risalo* (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 212, folio 203); on another page he attributes verses inserted in the *Risalo* to Inayat (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 184, folio 175), and does not bring the two statements together. Schimmel writes that "Shah Latif has inserted some of his ancestor's lines into his own poetry" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 162, folio 162).
- **The modern study of his verse.** Schimmel's three books cite a monograph, *Shah Abdul Karim*, by Motilal Jotwani, New Delhi, 1970 (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 292, folio 292; Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 327, folio 309; Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 482, folio 451). Boivin lists *Shah Abdul Karim – A Mystic Poet of Sindh* by "Motwani, Motilal", Karachi, 1986 (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 236, folio 223). Shackle refers to "Jotwani 1996" for a complete translation of his Sindhi verses (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 25).

## Legacy

His verse outlived him in his descendant's hands. Sorley writes that "the Koran, the Masnawi of Jalaluddin" Rumi, "and the Sindhi verses of Shah Karim were always in the poet's hands" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 183, folio 174), and says it again, of the same three books, that they "were constantly in his hands" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 212–213, folios 203–204). Shackle likewise counts his Sindhi verses among the three books Shah Latif kept constantly by him, beside the Qur'an and Rumi's *Masnavī* (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 23), and makes him one of the grounds for rejecting the picture of Shah Abdul Latif as an unlettered genius (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 9). Schimmel writes that "Shah Latif has inserted some of his ancestor's lines into his own poetry" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 162, folio 162).

He has been the subject of modern study. Schimmel's books cite a monograph by Motilal Jotwani, *Shah Abdul Karim* (New Delhi, 1970), which *As Through a Veil* gives with the publisher, Kumar Brothers (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 327, folio 309; Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 283, folio 265); Boivin lists a study titled *Shah Abdul Karim – A Mystic Poet of Sindh*, published in Karachi by Sindi Kitab Ghar and Indus Publications in 1986 (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 236, folio 223); and Shackle notes that "Jotwani 1996 includes a complete translation of Shah Karim's Sindhi verses" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 25).
