## Overview

Gurdwara Panjvin and Chhevin Patshahi — the gurdwara "of the Fifth and Sixth Kingships" — stands in Nankana Sahib near Gurdwara Tambu Sahib, on the road leading to the central shrine of Janam Asthan. Unlike most of the town's shrines, which commemorate the boyhood of *Guru Nanak Dev Ji*, this gurdwara honours two of his successors: the fifth Guru, *Arjan Dev*, and the sixth Guru, *Hargobind*, whose visits to the birthplace of the founder are remembered here. It is thus a reminder that Nankana Sahib drew not only the devotion of later pilgrims but the presence of the living Gurus themselves.

Of the books consulted, one names the gurdwara itself: Haroon Khalid's *A White Trail*, which calls it "Gurdwara Panjvin and Chevin Patshahi", "the Gurdwara of the fifth and sixth Guru", and describes its buildings, the relic it keeps and the devotions of its pilgrims (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 227, folio not stated). Khalid's other books, *Walking with Nanak* and *In Search of Shiva*, Harish Dhillon's retelling of the Janamsakhis, Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian history *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* and Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi's history of Sufism in India speak of the two Gurus, of Nankana Sahib or of the old village of Talwandi, but not of this gurdwara. What they say is gathered below as the lives of the Gurus and the setting of the town, not as the history of this building.

## The Visits of the Fifth and Sixth Gurus

The gurdwara marks the tradition that both Guru Arjan Dev and Guru Hargobind came to the town of Guru Nanak's birth. Guru Hargobind — revered as the master of *miri* and *piri*, the union of temporal and spiritual authority — is remembered to have visited Nankana Sahib in the month of *Har* in Samvat 1670, corresponding to 1613 CE, while returning from a journey to Kashmir. In memory of his stay, his followers are said to have established a *Gur Mela*, a fair, at the very spot where he had rested, and the site came in time to honour the fifth Guru as well. Within the enclosure there are accordingly two shrines: that of Guru Arjan Dev, built plainly and without a dome, and that of Guru Hargobind, surmounted by a dome, the two standing together within a common boundary wall.

Khalid records a different memory of the place, in which father and son came together. Guru Arjan and his son Guru Hargobind, on pilgrimage to Nankana Sahib, left their horses outside the city, tied them "at this particular spot to a Waan tree", and walked "about seven hundred metres on foot" to the shrine of Gurdwara Janamasthan "as a mark of respect" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 227, folio not stated). The gurdwara, in this telling, stands where the Gurus dismounted, at the edge of the town, so that they might enter the birthplace of the founder on foot.

## The Gurdwara

The paired shrines of the fifth and sixth Gurus form one of the quieter corners of the Nankana Sahib complex, less frequented than the great shrine of Janam Asthan but valued by pilgrims who make the full round of the town's sacred sites. Their situation beside Tambu Sahib places them within the compact eastern cluster of gurdwaras, and their modest architecture — the domeless sanctum of Guru Arjan set beside the domed sanctum of Guru Hargobind — gives visible form to the succession of the Gurus. As with the other shrines of Nankana Sahib, the *Guru Granth Sahib* is installed within, and the site is cared for by the bodies charged with Sikh heritage in Pakistan.

Khalid places the gurdwara about two hundred metres from Gurdwara Tambu Sahib, towards the railway station, marked by "a brown board on the left" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 227, folio not stated). He calls it "a fairly small Gurdwara". Its main shrine is a double-storey building "in the traditional mango yellow colour", with a *Nishan Sahib* beside it and a single-storey block of rooms allotted to pilgrims; within, a *Thara Sahib* holds the *Guru Granth Sahib*, with a fly-whisk (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 227, folio not stated).

The gurdwara keeps a relic of the visit. A small room above the shrine is "also regarded as sacred", and in it stands a glass showcase "containing a piece of the Waan tree that was used by the Gurus to tie their horses" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 227, folio not stated).

## Devotion and Practice

The sources consulted record no date of observance particular to this gurdwara beyond the *Gur Mela* that, by tradition, Guru Hargobind's followers founded at the spot where he rested. Khalid describes the devotions of a visit. An old Sikh writes his name on the platform of the *Nishan Sahib*; pilgrims prostrate, go round clockwise and fan the Book; and devotees touch the glass of the showcase that holds the piece of the Waan tree, for blessings (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 227, folio not stated).

Writing of the town's gurdwaras in general, and in the same chapter, Khalid adds: "All year round most of them remain locked, only to be opened at the time of the festival. There are security guards employed at the entrance to all of them." (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 227, folio not stated). The festival is the birthday of Guru Nanak, "Guru Nanak Jayanti or Gurpurab": "According to official estimates, ten to fifteen thousand people come to Nankana Sahib for this festival every year, which is celebrated during the last days of November", though in 2011, "According to media reports, only three thousand people are attending" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated). Pilgrims from India come by train across the border at Wagah and wait at Nankana three days; there are three days of *Akhand Path*, and the *Nagar Kirtan* follows the *bhog* on the final day, after which the pilgrims go on to other cities (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 222–223, folio not stated). "The entire city looks like a mini-India during these few days" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 226, folio not stated).

## The Fifth Guru, Arjan Dev

What follows is said of Guru Arjan, not of this gurdwara. Khalid's *A White Trail* calls him "the fifth Sikh Guru of Sikhism and the son of the fourth Sikh Guru, Guru Ram Das", appointed by his father as the next Guru (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 198, folio not stated; p. 220, folio not stated). *Walking with Nanak* gives his dates in its back matter as "5. Guru Arjan Dev (1563-1606, Guru from 1581 to 1606)" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 315, folio 289), and Dhillon gives "Arjan Dev (Guru from 1581 to 1606)" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 240, folio 265). Bhandari's Persian history, written under Aurangzeb, names him as the son and successor of Guru Ram Das, who died after twenty-five years on the seat: "بعد ان گورو ارجن خلف او سجاده نشین گشته بعد مدت بست و پنج سال رخت هستی بربست" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).

His succession was contested. Khalid writes that he was preferred when "his father, Guru Ram Das, appointed his younger son" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 43, folio 25). His elder brother Prithi Chand hid Arjan's letters from Lahore to their father, who "disowned Prithi Chand" and "A few days later … appointed Arjan as the next Guru" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 253–254, folio 227 / folio not stated). "Sikh tradition finds the root of the conflict to be Arja[n]'s trip to Lahore and the machinations of Prithi Chand", Khalid writes; Prithi Chand then "approached Mughal authorities to get him his rightful share" of the property, "and hence began the tumultuous relation … between the Mughals and the Sikhs" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 274, folio 248).

Guru Arjan built and gathered. Khalid credits him "with the completion of the Amrit Sar the tank of nectar that was started by his father at Ramdaspur", after which "the Harmandir Sahib was constructed there" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 257, folio not stated). He introduced "the daswandh", "10% of their earning to the cause of the Guru" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 315, folio 289), collected through the *Masands*, "a tradition that was started by Guru Arjan" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 256–257, folio 230 / folio not stated). Dhillon writes that Guru Nanak's *Bani* "was compiled as part of the Adi Granth by Guru Arjan Dev, the fifth Guru, in 1604" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 9, folio 9), and that the Kartarpur near Jalandhar, a town he places in India, "was founded much lacer [later], in 1594, by the fifth Guru, Arjan Dev, on land granted during the reign of the third Mughal Emperor, Jalal-ud-din Muhammad Akbar" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 175, folio "l92" [text-layer?] [192]).

Bhandari records an imperial visit to the Guru. After an affair near Batala the emperor crossed the Beas and came to the halting place of Guru Arjan, whom the book calls the successor of Baba Nanak: "بعد نهضت ازانجا از دریاۓ بیاه عبور فرموده بمنزل گورو ارجن[OCR?] سجاده نشین بابا نانک که در معرفت الهی مشهور بود تشریف فرمودند" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 164). The closing words of the clause speak of one renowned in the knowledge of God, and the sentence does not make clear whether they describe Guru Arjan or Baba Nanak. The emperor was pleased, the history says, by the meeting and by hearing from the Guru's own tongue the Hindi verses that Baba Nanak had composed on the knowledge of God, "از ملاقات او و اصغاۓ اشعار هندی تصنیف بابا نانک که در معرفت حق گفته از زبان او شنیده"; and the Guru, counting the visit an honour, presented a fitting offering: "گورو ارجن[OCR?] سرافرازے خویش دانسته پیشکش لایق گذرانید" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 164).

The Guru used the audience to plead for the peasants. While the imperial armies were quartered in the Punjab grain had been dear, "از نزول عساکر منصوره در پنجاب نرخ غله گران بود", and the assessment had been raised; now grain had grown cheap, "غلات ارزانی[OCR?] رو[OCR?] آورده", and "رعایا از عهده اداۓ جمع نمیتوانند برآمد", the peasants could not meet the demand (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 164). At his petition the high revenue officers were ordered to lighten the assessment, "بحسب التماس او بدیوانیان عظام حکم شد که بحساب ده دوازده[OCR?] انده[OCR?] جمع بر رعایا تخفیف دهند", the revenue collectors were to deal with the peasants accordingly, and the history closes the episode with a verse on the care of subjects (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 164).

Disciples of the fifth Guru are remembered elsewhere in the Punjab. Khalid writes that "Guru Arjan asked one of his followers to go to Lahore to bind the Adi Granth", Bhai Bannu (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 39, folio 21); his guide Iqbal Qaiser told him that a gurdwara at Mandi Mangat "was the house of Bhai Bannu, who was a devotee of the fifth Sikh guru, Guru Arjan" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated). "According to the Encyclopaedia of Sikh Literature", another shrine Khalid visited "is the shrine of Sahari Mal, a disciple of the fifth Sikh guru, Guru Arjan" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated). Khalid adds that "the friendship between Mian Meer and Guru Arjan is also the subject of several folk tales" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 200, folio not stated).

## The Martyrdom of Guru Arjan

Khalid's *Walking with Nanak* calls Guru Arjan "the first martryr [OCR?] [martyr] of Sikhism", "assassinated by Emperor Jahangir in the year 1606 for his alleged support to Khusro, the eldest son of Jahangir"; "The Sikhs believe that he was assassinated due to the connivance of one Chandu Shah who wanted to marry his daughter to Guru Arjan's son, but was refused" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 315, folio 289). Khalid quotes the emperor's own memoir: "In Goindwal which is on the river Beas, there was a Hindu named Arjan"; the rebel prince Khusro "happened to halt at a place where he was"; Arjan made "a finger-mark in saffron which the Indians call Qashga"; his property was handed "to Murtaza Khan", and he was to be "dealt with the penal Laws of Yasa" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 252, folio 226). *A White Trail* tells the same story from the other side: Khusrau, "Jahangir's rebellious son", "stopped over at the Langar of the Guru in Amritsar and sought blessings for his victory", and the mark of blessing was "considered treason by the Emperor" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 199, folio not stated); it quotes Jahangir's memoir, through a book by Dalbir Singh Dhillon, "this insignificant fellow (Guru Arjan) ... commanded that he should be put to death" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 198, folio not stated).

"For five days the Guru was tortured" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 252, folio 226). In the walled city of Lahore, Khalid writes, "there is a place near the Rang Mahal known as ... Khoi [OCR?] [Lal Khoi?], of the Red Well"; "It is believed that Arjan was kept in a room next to this well, which was the haveli of the Mughal Diwan of Lahore"; immersed in a cauldron, he had something boiling poured on his head (the word, read as sand, is damaged in the printed text), and "His body was then thrown into the river"; "near Lahore Fort there is a small yellow structure which is now referred to [as] the samadhi of Guru Agjan [OCR?] [Arjan]. It is believed that the Guru lost his life there" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 253, folio 227). The traditional Sikh explanation, Khalid writes, is "the connivance of a Hindu Khatri called Chandu Lal", the Diwan of Lahore, in league with Prithi Chand (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 253, folio 227), whereas "Mughal sources and particularly the Tuzk-e-Ja hangiri [OCR?] [Tuzk-e-Jahangiri]" are "glaringly silent about the role of Chandu Lal", Jahangir stating that Arjan "was punished because of his sympathies for his rebel son Khusro" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 255, folio 229).

Sikh belief, as Khalid gives it, tells the end as a miracle: on the fifth day the Guru took "a dip in the waters of the Ravi" and "did not emerge from the water" — "the Guru had performed a miracle and passed onto the other world". Mian Mir, the tradition continues, asked the Guru's leave "to bury alive his oppressors. The Guru refused" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 253, folio 227). *A White Trail* writes that "Guru Arjan spent a lot of time in the same city [Lahore], where he was assassinated and his remains interred" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated), and that his son raised "a small structure" at the spot of his samadhi (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 200, folio not stated).

The martyrdom marks a turning in the books' telling of Sikh history. Khalid writes that Arjan was "crushed in the fight between the Mughal 'elephants'" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 255, folio 229), that the assassination is "the Karbala of Sikh history" and "precipitated the development of the Khalsa" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 255–256, folios 229–230), and that "The martyrdom of Guru Arjan provided the Sikh community with a cohesive motivation" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 232, folio 206; p. 234, folio 208). "He was the last Guru in the line of the Gurus who focused solely on spiritual matters" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 256, folio 230). *A White Trail* calls him "the first Sikh martyr, assassinated by the fourth Mughal Emperor, Jahangir (1569-1627)" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 162, folio not stated), and "Guru Arjan's martyrdom is a low point in Sikh history" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 206–207, folio not stated). Operation Blue Star, Khalid writes, was launched "on a day when thousands of Sikhs from all over Punjab had gathered at the shrine to commemorate the death anniversary of the fifth Sikh Guru, Guru Arjan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 162, folio not stated).

## The Sixth Guru, Hargobind

What follows is said of Guru Hargobind, not of this gurdwara. *Walking with Nanak* gives his dates in a line damaged in the printed text, read as "6. Guru Hargobind (1595-" … "1644, Guru from 1606 to 1644)" [OCR?] (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 315, folio 289); Dhillon gives "Har Gobind (Guru from 1606 to 1644)" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 240, folio 265). Bhandari names him as Guru Arjan's successor, "پس او گورو هرگوبند خلف", in a sentence that breaks off (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). His father "was summoned to Lahore by Emperor Jahangir" over his role "during the rebellion of Prince Khusro", appointed "Hargobind as the sixth Guru" and "was subsequently martyred" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 234, folio 208).

"Known as the 'soldier-saint' Guru Har Gobind began the process of militarisation of the Sikh community following the assassination of his father", Khalid writes; he was "the first Guru to wear two swords on his person, one of which represented temporal power while the other spiritual power" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 315, folio 289). He gave *darshan* "On the 24 July 1606", when "the Aka/ [OCR?] [Akal] Takht, translated as the Eternal Throne, had also been prepared by then"; he came dressed "as a royal", in "churidar pyjama, a saffron gown", an aigrette, a necklace and "two swords", and "refused to take up the religious symbols of Guruhood". His words, as Khalid gives them, were: "My rosary shall be the sword belt and on my turban, I shall wear the emblem of royalty". This is "miri-piri": one sword for "temporal power", the other for "spiritual prowess" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 231, folio 205).

Khalid describes the court the sixth Guru kept: a saffron flag facing the Akal Takht, "to eventually become Nishan cael [OCR?] [Nishan Sahib?] a symbol of the Khalsa", a large drum, and "a hawk as a pet" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 232, folio 206). He had "fifty-two bodyguards", and "Sant Sipahi or saint soldiers", "five hundred soldiers to begin with", who were "not paid a salary but… exempt from paying the required tribute"; "Mohsin Fani, a Persian historian from the seventeenth [century]" gives "seven hundred cavaliers and sixty artillery men" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 232, folio 206). Through his *Masands* devotees were told to offer arms and horses (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 233, folio 207). "To administer the Sikh community, Guru Hargobind had created the concept of the Sangat, which was then put under a Masand" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 181, folio 155). *A White Trail* puts it simply: "Guru Arjan's son, Guru Hargobind, reared horses and encouraged his followers to keep arms in their defence after his father's death" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 199, folio not stated). The offering of weapons at the Guru's court "was a practice that was started by Guru Hargobind but then reinforced by Guru Gobind Singh" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 99, folio 81).

His relations with the Mughals turned more than once. Jahangir "also incarcerated him in Gwalior", and later there was "a cordial relationship with the Gun [OCR?] [Guru] helping the Emperor in a couple of his military expeditions" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 316, folio 290). Under Shahjahan, "Three battles… all of them were won by the Sikhs… Amritsar, Lahira and Kartarpur", after which he "decided to settle at Kartarpur" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 235, folio 209). Bhandari places him on the Sutlej at Kiratpur, the place "که گورو هرگوبند و گورو هرراے درانجا سکونت داشتند", where he and Guru Har Rai lived (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 513). *A White Trail* adds that "His son, Guru Hargobind, also spent a lot of time here [Lahore]" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated).

He chose his grandson to follow him. His eldest son, "Baba Gurdin [OCR?] [Baba Gurditta]", died in his lifetime, and he "appointed Har Rai, the younger son of Baba Gurditta" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 134, folio 116). Bhandari says the same: Gurditta had died "در ایام حیات گورو هرگوبند", so that Har Rai sat in the place of his grandfather, "بجاے جد بزرگوار", and he names "تیغ بهادر خلف خورد هرگوبند", Tegh Bahadur, Hargobind's younger son (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 519). The sword passed to "his SON, Guru Tegh Bahadur", and then to Guru Gobind Singh (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 233, folio 207); "Following the tradition of his grandfather Hargobind, Har Rai too kept horses and a force of twenty-two hundred fighters" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 210, folio 184).

Khalid's verdict is divided. Critics call the militarism "a departure from the teachings of the early Gurus", and note that "Hargobind did not even compose a single hymn" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 233, folio 207). "Gury [OCR?] [Guru] Hargobind remains one of the most controversial Sikh Gurus"; "He laid the foundations of the contemporary face of Sikhism" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 235, folio 209).

Another of the sixth Guru's stories, told of a different shrine, also turns on a horse. In the village of Amar Sadhu, now inside the DHA in Lahore, a gurdwara "Built in honour of the memory of the sixth Sikh guru, Guru Hargobind" is "known as Gurdwara Bibi Kaulan" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated). Bibi Kaulan, a maid bonded to a Muslim trader of Mozang, heard that "the guru was encamped nearby" and begged for rescue: "The guru took her along with him on his horse and they reached this spot where they rested for a while before they headed off to Amritsar" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated). Iqbal Qaiser told Khalid that "The story of the gurdwara is also affiliated to the guru's horse like that of the Muslim saint's horse" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).

## The Gurus and the Scripture

No verse is quoted in the books consulted that belongs to this gurdwara or to either of its Gurus by name; what they record is the fifth Guru's work as the gatherer of the scripture. Khalid writes that Guru Arjan "began the compilation of Guru Granth Sahib" and "has the greatest number of hymns in the Guru Granth Sahib" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 315, folio 289), "the highest number of hymns in the Guru Granth Sahib" at "2218 [OCR?]" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 256, folio 230). In the Granth's naming, "Arjan becomes the fourth Nanak" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 98, folio 80). The Adi Granth "played a significant role in the development of a separate Sikh identity" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 256, folio 230). When it was complete the Guru was summoned over its alleged "anti-Muslim and anti-Hindu content" and sent "Bhai Gurdas", who "clarified the misunderstanding" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 254, folio not stated).

The scripture he compiled carries a Muslim saint's poetry. Baba Fareed's poetry, Khalid writes, "was later incorporated into the Guru Granth Sahib, when it was first compiled by the fifth Sikh Guru, Guru Arjan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 188, folio not stated); collected by Guru Nanak at Pak Pattan, it "was later included in the Granth Sahib by Guru Arjan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 200, folio not stated). A major contribution of the Guru was "the compilation into the Granth Sahib of the writings of all the previous Sikh Gurus and other non-Sikh saints" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 198, folio not stated). Bhandari's account of the imperial visit turns on poetry too: what pleased the emperor was the Hindi verse of Baba Nanak, recited by the Guru himself (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 164).

The sixth Guru, by contrast, is remembered by Khalid as the "first Guru to not have composed any single hymn" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 316, folio 290). Tegh Bahadur, his son, "was the first Guru since Guru Arjan to compose hymns" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 134, folio 116).

## Setting: Nankana Sahib

What follows is said of the town, not of this gurdwara. Rizvi places Guru Nanak's birth in "the village of Talwandi later known as Nankana Sahib, about forty miles south-west of Lahore, now part of Pakistan" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 407, folio 383). Khalid writes that "The name of Rai Bhoi di Talwindi has been changed to Nankana Sahib in honour of the first Sikh Guru who was born there" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 31, folio 13), and that "Originally called Rai-Bhoi-Di-Talwandi, this city was rechristened Nankana Sahib in honour of Guru Nanak", Nanak's early years there accounting for "several Gurdwaras in the city" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated). It is "a city considered to be one of the holiest cities in the world for the Sikhs, as the founder of their religion, Nanak, was born here" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 183, folio not stated). Bhandari writes that Baba Nanak "درمکان تلونڈی رائے بهوئه[OCR?] سعادت ولادت یافته همانجا درخانه جد ابادری می بود", that he was born at Talwandi Rai Bhoi and lived there in his grandfather's house (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).

Dhillon describes "the village of Talwandi, which lay between the rivers Ravi and Chenab, directly in the path of the armies that had set their eyes on Delhi": "Thirteen times was the village of Talwandi destroyed and thirteen times - like the fabled phoenix - it rose again from its ashes", and "Its last rebirth took place under the supervision of Rai Bhoe, a Bhatti Rajpur [text-layer?] [Bhatti Rajput], who had converted to Islam" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 15–16, folios 17–18). By the end of Nanak's life, in his telling, Talwandi "was now no longer an obscure little village but had become a place of pilgrimage" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 172, folio 188). Behind Gurdwara Janamsthan rises an old mound; Iqbal Qaiser told Khalid that "The settlement there was destroyed after which Rai Bhoi di Talwindi was constructed within a boundary wall" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 91, folio 73).

The books count the town's gurdwaras differently: "There are a total of six gurdwaras associated with Guru Nanak at Nankana Sahib" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 90, folio 72); "There are seven Gurdwaras in Nankana Sahib, most of which are associated with incidents from the life of Baba Guru Nanak" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 192, folio not stated). Rizvi names no gurdwara at Nankana Sahib.

Of the modern town Khalid writes: "There is one main road that runs through the entire city"; "Most of the buildings are from the late British or the early Pakistan era" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 186, folio not stated). From a rooftop he saw mosque minarets and the "small white domes of the Gurdwaras", the "remains of a few Hindu temples", and the railway station (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 225, folio not stated). "In 2005, Nankana Sahib was separated from Sheikhupura and made into an independent district, with this city becoming the district headquarters" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 226, folio not stated).

After Partition the gurdwaras of the town stood empty. Khalid writes of Giani Pratap: "After looking at the dilapidated condition of the shrines here he decided to stay and look after these holy buildings. That was the first time someone had taken the initiative to take care of the gurdwaras in Nankana Sahib after Partition" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 90, folio 72). His informant Kartar told him: "There was no Sikh left here after partition. Our Gurdwaras became barren and were in a bad state" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 184, folio not stated). Most of the town's present Sikhs, Khalid writes, "are not indigenous people from the city, but recent migrants from Khyber-Pakhtunkhwa (KPK) where there is a considerable Sikh population" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 235, folio not stated), and "Most of the newcomers were incorporated within the Gurdwaras, in rooms which were generally reserved for pilgrims" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 176, folio not stated). *Walking with Nanak* speaks of "the Sikh community in Nankana Sahib, which amounts to about two hundred-odd households" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 87, folio 69).

The town's pilgrimages have known violence. "It was the November of 1985, Guru Nanak's birthday, a year after Operation Blue Star": "Thousands of Sikh pilgrims had come to Nankana from all over the world" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 215, folio not stated). During a speech for a separate Sikh state "the gate of the Gurdwara was closed", and "Counsellor B. Jain and First Secretary K. K. Khanna" were attacked, barely escaping alive (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 216, folio not stated). "The religious festivals at Nankana and other cities of Pakistan have gone back to being religious festivals" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 216, folio not stated).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The name.** The gurdwara is listed as Panjvi Chati Patshahi, and the site's account calls it Gurdwara Panjvin and Chhevin Patshahi. *A White Trail* writes "Gurdwara Panjvin and Chevin Patshahi" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 227, folio not stated).
- **Who came, and when.** The site's account remembers a visit by Guru Hargobind in the month of *Har*, Samvat 1670 (1613 CE), on his return from Kashmir, and says the site "came in time to honour the fifth Guru as well". *A White Trail* has Guru Arjan and his son Guru Hargobind come together, on pilgrimage, and gives no date (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 227, folio not stated).
- **What happened at the spot.** The site's account says Guru Hargobind rested here and his followers founded a *Gur Mela* where he had rested. *A White Trail* says the Gurus tied their horses here to a Waan tree and walked about seven hundred metres on foot to Janamasthan (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 227, folio not stated).
- **The buildings.** The site's account describes two shrines within a common boundary wall: Guru Arjan Dev's, built plainly and without a dome, and Guru Hargobind's, surmounted by a dome. *A White Trail* describes "a fairly small Gurdwara" whose main shrine is a double-storey building "in the traditional mango yellow colour", with a *Nishan Sahib*, a single-storey block of pilgrims' rooms and a small room above the shrine (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 227, folio not stated).
- **Its place in the town.** The site's account sets the gurdwara near Tambu Sahib "on the road leading to the central shrine of Janam Asthan", within the compact eastern cluster. *A White Trail* puts it about two hundred metres from Tambu Sahib "towards the Railway station" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 227, folio not stated).
- **Whether it alone does not remember Guru Nanak.** The site's account says that "most of the town's shrines" commemorate Guru Nanak's boyhood. *A White Trail* says: "This Gurdwara is the only one in the city, which is not in memory of Guru Nanak" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 227, folio not stated).
- **The Sikhs of Nankana Sahib.** The site's account says the gurdwara has stood "without a local congregation" since 1947. *Walking with Nanak* speaks of "about two hundred-odd households" of Sikhs in the town (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 87, folio 69). *A White Trail* gives "only about thirty to forty Sikh houses in Nankana ten years ago" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 174–175, folio not stated), "about two hundred houses" after about ten years (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 175, folio not stated), and, from Kartar, "only about fifty Sikh houses" a few years earlier and "almost two hundred to two hundred and fifty Sikh homes" now (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 193, folio not stated).
- **Pilgrims at the birthday festival.** *A White Trail* gives the official estimate of "ten to fifteen thousand people" every year and, for 2011, media reports of "only three thousand" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated).
- **Why Guru Arjan died.** *Walking with Nanak* gives the Sikh belief that he died through "the connivance of one Chandu Shah" whose daughter's marriage to the Guru's son was refused (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 315, folio 289), and the traditional explanation of "a Hindu Khatri called Chandu Lal", Diwan of Lahore, allied with Prithi Chand (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 253, folio 227); Jahangir's memoir says he "was punished because of his sympathies for his rebel son Khusro", and is silent on Chandu Lal (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 255, folio 229). *A White Trail* has Khusrau seek the Guru's blessing at the *langar* in Amritsar, "considered treason by the Emperor" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 199, folio not stated). The name of the Diwan is given as Chandu Shah (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 315, folio 289) and as Chandu Lal (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 253, folio 227).
- **How Guru Arjan's life ended.** In one account given by *Walking with Nanak*, "His body was then thrown into the river"; the samadhi near Lahore Fort marks where "It is believed that the Guru lost his life"; and Sikh belief holds that he went into the Ravi and "did not emerge from the water", having "performed a miracle" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 253, folio 227). *A White Trail* says he was assassinated in Lahore and "his remains interred" there (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated).
- **The fifth or the fourth Guru.** *A White Trail* calls Guru Arjan the fifth Sikh Guru (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 162, folio not stated; p. 188, folio not stated; p. 198, folio not stated), but on one page "the fourth Sikh Guru, Guru Arjan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 168, folio not stated).
- **Why Guru Hargobind was imprisoned.** *Walking with Nanak* gives two explanations: that he "had retained the money that [was] given him by the Court to be disbursed to his troops", and, in the Sikh version, that the Emperor "felt threatened" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 235, folio 209). Some claims, it adds, have him in Jahangir's "employment, serving him in the Punjab" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 234–235, folios 208–209).
- **The size of Guru Hargobind's force.** *Walking with Nanak* gives "five hundred soldiers to begin with", and, from Mohsin Fani, "seven hundred cavaliers and sixty artillery men" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 232, folio 206).
- **When the militarisation began.** *Walking with Nanak* says Guru Hargobind "began the process of militarisation of the Sikh community" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 315, folio 289); it also reports P. S. Grewal's view that it began under Guru Arjan and gained impetus under Hargobind, and that militarisation had started "during the period of the fifth Sikh Guru" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 232, folio 206; p. 234, folio 208).
- **Guru Nanak's birth.** Khalid gives "October 20, 1469" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 314, folio 288); Dhillon gives "On 14 April 1469" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 17–18, folios 19–20). Khalid reports that the Janamsakhi writer Meherban "claims that Guru Nanak was born in Dera Chahal and not Rai Bhoi di Talwindi", while other writers "are unanimous that Nanak was born in Rai Bhoi di Talwindi" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 43, folio 25).
- **The number of gurdwaras in the town.** *Walking with Nanak* counts six associated with Guru Nanak (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 90, folio 72); *A White Trail* counts seven (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 192, folio not stated).

## Legacy

Since 1947 the gurdwara, like its neighbours, has stood without a local congregation, coming to life chiefly when pilgrim *jathas* arrive for the great anniversaries. Its dedication to the fifth and sixth Gurus lends the sacred geography of Nankana Sahib a fuller shape: the town is not only the cradle of Guru Nanak but a place touched by the later Gurus who carried his message forward — Arjan Dev, the compiler of the scripture and the first martyr of the faith, and Hargobind, who first girded the sword of temporal power. In honouring their visits, Gurdwara Panjvin and Chhevin Patshahi joins the beginning of the Sikh story to its unfolding.

In Khalid's account the gurdwara keeps that succession in a single object: a piece of the tree to which father and son tied their horses before they walked, on foot and "as a mark of respect", into the birthplace of the first Guru (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 227, folio not stated). It is, he writes, "the only one in the city, which is not in memory of Guru Nanak" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 227, folio not stated) — a shrine in Nanak's town to the Guru who gathered the scripture and the Guru who first bore the sword.
