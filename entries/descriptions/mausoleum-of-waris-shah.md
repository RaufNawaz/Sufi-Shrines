## Overview

At Jandiala Sher Khan, in the Sheikhupura district of the Punjab, stands the mausoleum of Waris Shah (c. 1722–1798), the Sufi poet who gave the Punjab its national epic. His *Heer* — the great verse romance of Heer and Ranjha, completed in 1766 — is at once the supreme achievement of Punjabi poetry and a profound Sufi allegory of the soul’s love for God, and it has been sung, wept over, and quoted across the Punjab for two and a half centuries. Waris Shah’s tomb is honoured both as the shrine of a saint and as the resting place of the poet whom Punjabis revere as the “Shakespeare of the Punjabi language.”

The books gathered here say a great deal about the poet and his poem, and nothing about his tomb. The chief witness is Waris Shah himself, in Charles Frederick Usborne's English translation of the poem, which is printed with the numbered notes of a later, unnamed annotator. Near its close the poet writes of himself that "Waris Shah lives at Jandiala and is the pupil of the Saint of Kasur" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 57, folio 56). Annemarie Schimmel, who spells his name Warith Shah, credits him with the final form of a romance "elaborated in more than a hundred versions in Panjabi, Urdu, Sindhi, and Persian" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 171, folio 153), and Shemeem Burney Abbas writes that "now, whenever a reference is made to the Hir-Ranjha legend, it is mainly to the Waris Shah text" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 120, folio not stated). None of the books consulted names a tomb, a grave, an urs or a custodian for him, and none of them writes "Jandiala Sher Khan".

## The Life of the Poet-Saint

Waris Shah was born around 1722 into a Sayyid family at Jandiala Sher Khan. He received a thorough religious and literary education, studying at Kasur, and belonged in spirit to the Chishti Sufi tradition, tracing his discipleship to the line of the saint Makhdoom of Kasur. Little of his life is recorded with certainty, and much is woven from legend — including the tradition that he composed *Heer* while living at a village called Malka Hans, in a small chamber beside a mosque, and that the poem grew out of, and transcended, an unfulfilled love of his own. He lived through the anarchic eighteenth century, between the collapse of Mughal power and the rise of the Sikhs, and died around 1798, to be buried at his native Jandiala Sher Khan.

Usborne's translation twice names the poet's home. A line in the body of the poem runs: "Otherwise his wrath will descend on us like a sudden invasion of Ahmad Shahs and God save Jandiala (the birthplace of the poet)" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 43, folio 42). At the end the poet says that he "lives at Jandiala" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 57, folio 56). The translation prints "Jandiala" alone, never "Jandiala Sher Khan", and it speaks of his birth and his residence there, not of his burial.

Of his family it has only a boast in the mouth of Luddan the ferryman: "Even the son of a Pir like Waris", he declares, "we will not take into our boat for nothing", and a note explains: "This is a reference to Waris Shah, the poet himself." (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 6, folio 5). A study of the poet that Abbas cites, by S. Hamidullah Hashimi, is titled *Syed Waris Shah* (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 206, 224, folios not stated).

The "Saint of Kasur" whose pupil he calls himself is named only in the annotator's note on that line, and the name is damaged in the printed text: what can be read is 'Murtaza of Kasur', with a reference back to the fourth note of the Introduction (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 57, folio 56). The introduction to Taufiq Rafat's selection from Bulleh Shah names Bulleh Shah's teacher and makes Waris Shah his other pupil: "Khawaja Hafiz Ghulam Murtaza Qasuri trained two rebellious personalities; the other was Waris Shah, the author of Punjabi's greatest romance in verse, 'Hir'." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 22, folio not stated). Later it repeats the claim more cautiously, and without naming the teacher: Waris Shah "was a contemporary and is said to have studied religion together with Bulleh Shah from the same teacher" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 38, folio 18). Christopher Shackle, translating Bulleh Shah, calls him the older poet's younger contemporary and the author of "the famous narrative treatment in the Hīr" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 21, folio xx), and Abbas writes that "Bulle Shah, who was a contemporary of Waris Shah, also worked with the Hir-Ranjha myth in Panjabi" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 120, folio not stated).

Kasur is the one town for which the poet says he fears. In the Epilogue, in Usborne's English:

*Of all the wretched Punjab I am most concerned for Kasur. I am concerned for my faith and conscience even as Moses was frightened on Holy Sinai.* (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 56, folio 55)

When the poem was done, he writes, "Having finished the story I presented it to my master for his acceptance." (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 57, folio 56). The page does not say whether this master is the Saint of Kasur or another man.

Of his Sufi allegiance the annotator writes: "It is not unusual for a person to owe allegiance to more than one sect of the Sufis. This is the case with Waris Shah." (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 2, folio 1, note 2). The note stands in a passage on the Qadiriyya of Abdul Qadir Gilani and the Chishtiyya of Shakarganj, but it does not say which sects were his, and the translation names no order or chain for him anywhere. The poet claims the help of one saint in his own voice: "With the aid of Shakarganj", he writes, "I have conferred this benefit on the world" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 57, folio 56). His Invocation, the annotator reports, credits another with power over fate: Waris Shah himself, "in speaking of Mohiuddin Abdul Qadir Gilani in the 'Invocation' says that he can score out even the writing of the Pen of Destiny" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 31, folio 30, note 7). The same note records a belief of the countryside: "According to popular superstition in the Punjab countryside", Pirs, Fakirs and Jogis can, by the force of their piety, "make the Moving Finger of Destiny cancel what it has written" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 31, folio 30, note 7).

The tradition of a love of his own has a name in Usborne's translation. A line of the poem calls on a woman directly:

*Bhag Bhari, help me to meet my Ranjha.* (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 45, folio 44)

Usborne's own note, as the annotator reports it, calls this "an apostrophe to the woman with whom the poet" Waris Shah fell in love; the annotator adds that, as earlier notes explained, this "need not necessarily have been her real name" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 45, folio 44). Further on he ventures, of a turn in the tale, that the poet "must have wished for some such miracle for himself and Bhag Bhari as well, but it does not seem to have happened." (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 51, folio 50).

The poem bears witness to the disorder of his century. Of the troubles its Epilogue describes, the annotator writes: "The latter half of the eighteenth century, to which Waris Shah is referring here, marked the decline of the Mughal Empire in India, and was a period of great confusion and disturbance… Waris Shah's description of the conditions of his day is probably not exaggerated." (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 57, folio 56, note 1). The invader rides through the poem's similes, "as if armies from Kandahar had swept over the Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 7, folio 6), and "today the Punjab has fallen into the hands of Kandaharis" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 47, folio 46), a line the annotator takes as "probably a reference to the invasion of Ahmad Shah Abdali, whose capital was Kandahar" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 48, folio 47, note 5). Of such lines he writes that "The Punjab has often been the battleground of invading forces from the North", and that the poet may have been thinking of Ahmad Shah Abdali, "who, in 1748, began a series of invasions of India through the Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 8, folio 7, note 8). Schimmel, writing of the province rather than the poet, calls the late seventeenth and the eighteenth century "The golden age of Muslim Panjabi poetry", "the time when the province was invaded one time after the other by Mahrattas, Sikhs, and, in between, by the liberating Muslim armies" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 17, folio 13).

## Heer Waris Shah

Waris Shah’s fame rests on a single work of genius. Taking up the old Punjabi love-legend of Heer, daughter of the Sials, and Ranjha, the herdsman who becomes a jogi for her sake, he retold it in some hundreds of stanzas of matchless Punjabi verse, completed in 1766. On its surface it is a tragic romance; beneath, it is a Sufi allegory in which the human lover’s longing is the soul’s longing for God, and the pain of separation the very engine of the spiritual path. Along the way the poem becomes an encyclopaedia of eighteenth-century Punjabi life — its trades and customs, its proverbs, its landscape and its faith — so that to know *Heer* is to know the Punjab. Its opening lines set love at the foundation of the world:

اوّل حمد خُدا دا ورد کیجے،
عشق کیتا سُو جگ دا مُول میاں

*First, let us utter the praise of God — who made love the very origin of the world.*

Generations of Punjabis have known long passages of *Heer* by heart, and its verses are sung by professional *Heer*-singers in a distinctive traditional style; the poem is invoked in joy and in grief alike — most famously by the modern poet Amrita Pritam, who at the Partition cried out to Waris Shah himself to rise and witness the suffering of the Punjab.

The poet tells how the poem came to be written. Near its opening, in Usborne's English, he records a request:

*My friends came to me and said, 'Write for us afresh the forgotten story of the Love of Hir.' So we have written it right cunningly and plucked a new rose in the Garden of Poetry.* (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 2, folio 1)

At the end he reports the task done: "By the Grace of God I have fulfilled the request of my dear friends." (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 57, folio 56). No patron, payment or occasion is named. Abbas notes that the poem's devotional frame is an invocation of Baba Farid of Pakpattan (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 123, folio not stated). Usborne's English of the Invocation includes the line:

*When Shakarganj made his abode at Pakpattan, the Punjab was delivered of all its troubles.* (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 2, folio 1)

The annotator will not let the line pass: "Much as one would like to agree with Waris Shah this is not historically correct. The Punjab has had plenty of troubles even after Baba Farid honoured it by making it his home and his place of burial!" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 2, folio 1).

The Epilogue gives the poet's own date:

*When love came to me I felt a desire to write this story in verse. I wrote it in the year 1180 Hijri in the southern country.* (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 56, folio 55)

The year is printed only as "1180 Hijri", in Latin letters, with no equivalent in the Common Era. The annotator supplies one: a tragic incident, he suggests, "may have been present in Waris Shah's mind when he wrote his Hir in 1766" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 12, folio 11, note 4). Beside a second reckoning on the last page, "the year 120 of the Bikramajit era", he writes: "These two dates do not exactly correspond, but the poem was written in AD. 1766." (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 57, folio 56, note 2). Elsewhere he speaks of Waris Shah, "who wrote his poem in" a year whose reading is uncertain in the printed text and appears to be 1768, for whom Nadir Shah's invasion of 1739 was still "a recent memory" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 33, folio 32, note 2); and he writes that Ahmad Shah Abdali "broke the Mahratta power at the third Battle of Panipat" in 1761, "five years before Hir was written", without stating the year that results (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 44, folio 43, note 8). Schimmel dates the poem later. Writing of Ali Haydar, she says: "Shortly after his death the classical work of Panjabi literature was composed: Warith Shah's version of Hir Ranjha (around 1794)" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 17, folio 13); and again, "It was given its classical form by Warith Shah (completed around 1794)." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 419, folio 388).

Of the place of writing the poet says:

*When I produced the tale among learned men it became known to the world. Waris, those who have recited the Holy Kalima have attained salvation. Kharal Hans is a well known place. Here I composed this story.* (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 56, folio 55)

For "the southern country" of the Epilogue, Usborne's note, as the annotator gives it, reads: "Lammnan Des is the present Montgomery district of the Punjab." (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 57, folio 56, note 2). The annotator elsewhere speaks of "the Montgomery area (now renamed Sahiwal)" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 10, folio 9, note 3). The translation never names Jandiala as the place where the poem was composed.

The story was old when Waris Shah took it up. Abbas writes that "The Hir-Ranjha myth existed for a long time before Waris Shah", who "made it famous in the eighteenth century", and that "The myth was probably sung in the folk traditions for several centuries" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 120, folio not stated). Shackle calls it the greatest of the local legends: "The greatest of these local legends is the story of Hir and Ranjha, which is set in the world of the Jat pastoral tribes of western Panjab" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 20, folio xix). Waris Shah's is the version "which tells the whole story of how Ranjha leaves his family home in search of Hir" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 21, folio xx). Haroon Khalid compares his rewriting with a famous retelling of the Ramayana: "just the way Valmiki's Ramayana became Tulsidas's after he rewrote the epic, this legend also became Waris Shah's when he rewrote it in the eighteenth century. Today it is also referred to as Waris Shah's Heer" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated). He reports a claim to history as well: "According to Damodar's version, which was then supported by Waris Shah, Heer Ranjha is based on an actual story that Damodar saw unfold in front of his eyes" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated).

Of what Waris Shah brought to the tale, Abbas writes: "Waris Shah's text is used for a variety of reasons, the strongest one being his creation of Hir's character. Through her he challenges patriarchy, the clergy, and issues of the socioeconomic caste system" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 120–121, folios not stated). Of Heer's people she writes that "The Jats of the Panjab are zamindars (landowners), known for practicing widow-marriage" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 121, folio not stated), and that "In devising Hir's character Waris Shah seems to have banked on the caste inclinations of the Jats, for Hir belonged to the family of the powerful Siyal Jats of Jhang" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 121, folio not stated). Her glossary calls the Siyal the "Clan name of Hir's family; powerful landowners of the Panjab" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 188, folio not stated), and the Kheṛās "A powerful clan of landowners in the Panjab mentioned in the Hir-Ranjha myth" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 183–184, folios not stated). Of his learning she writes that "Sufi poets like Waris Shah and others were well read in Persian and Arabic literatures", and that "if Waris Shah was using sources such as Ahmed Gujjar's creation of the text, as is claimed, then the poets who created the narrative were quite erudite in Persian and Arabic literary sources" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 155, folio not stated).

His ear was close to the speech of women. *Nī*, Abbas explains, "is an intimate, feminine-gender term in Panjabi, which women use to address one another. The fact that Waris Shah uses this in women's discourse reveals his sensitivity to feminine speech forms" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 210, folio not stated); and "There are references to the potters (kahārs) in Hir's laments in Waris Shah's Hir-Ranjha narrative; I have observed this through my association with the landowning Syeds in Burewala, Multan" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 202, folio not stated). His stanza is the *bait*: "The form is adopted in Panjabi Sufi poetry and signifies a stanza of four lines. Sultan Bahu and Waris Shah use the bait form in their poetry, such as the Abiāt-e Bahu and Waris Shah's Hir" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 178, folio not stated).

The annotator's glosses map that encyclopaedia of Punjabi life, often in the present tense of his own day. "Ranjha is still a well-known caste of the Jats in the old Punjab." (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 3, folio 2). "Spinning parties — the Punjabi name is 'tarinjan' or 'bhora' — were a famous institution of the Punjab village" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 4, folio 3, note 14), and "A spinning-wheel (charkha) used to be the inseparable companion of every woman, old or young, in the rural Punjab." (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 17, folio 16, note 1). Of a dawn at the mosque: "This is how a morning begins even now in a Punjab village." (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 5, folio 4, note 4). Judging by their names, he notes, some of Heer's girl friends are Hindus, and "This symbolises the atmosphere of the village in the old Punjab, where the village community as a whole was regarded as one social unit irrespective of caste or creed" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 20, folio 19, note 12). Of her people he writes that "the Sial tribe has always been more than usually considerate towards its women. It is one of the few tribes in the Punjab which allows women to inherit property under customary law." (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 20, folio 19, note 13), and Abbas says the same of the Siyal (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 121, folio not stated). "It may appear strange that a Muslim family should depend on Hindu Brahmans for fixing a marriage but this was by no means unusual in the old Punjab." (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 26, folio 25, note 1). "A traditional Punjab marriage still retains the symbolism of 'marriage by capture'." (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 28, folio 27, note 5).

His glosses run on through house and season. "Old houses in the Punjab countryside had a big courtyard with a number of living-rooms and bedrooms round it. A number of brothers living with their parents as a joint family was quite common." (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 16, folio 15, note 7). "Sawan and Bhadon are the two rainy months of the year in the old Punjab." (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 26, folio 25, note 2). "The Gujars are a famous tribe of the old Punjab who keep animals and sell their milk." (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 19, folio 18, note 3). The tuft of the turban "is the well-known 'turrah', beloved of the rural aristocracy of the Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 11, folio 10, note 7). "Gur is raw sugar. It is well known as a ceremonial offering in the Punjab countryside." (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 33, folio 32, note 3). The Arsi "was one of the favourite ornaments of a bride in the old Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 26, folio 25, note 7); "Brides in the old Punjab wore coloured (and colourful) garments. White clothes, by tradition, were worn by widows, particularly among the Hindus" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 28, folio 27, note 4); and "After the 'mangni' or betrothal, a girl does not see her fiancé until marriage" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 48, folio 47, note 6). "'Dandasa' is, or used to be, the equivalent of lipstick in the rural Punjab." (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 48, folio 47, note 2). "Jugglers and their bears are still a familiar sight in the old Punjab." (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 40, folio 39, note 16).

A vow of thanksgiving in the poem reads:

*Oh poet Waris Shah! If I hear that my lover is returning, I will offer sweet cakes and light my lamps with rich butter for oil.* (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 41, folio 40)

On the same page the annotator notes: "Lighting lamps in mosque even with ordinary oil is still a well-known way of expression of gratitude to God for His favours. Punjabi mothers still promise lamp-lighting in mosques when they pray for their children" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 41, folio 40, note 5).

The poem's Punjabi appears, in Roman letters, in a modern book too. Haroon Khalid, who calls him "Waris Shah, the Sufi poet from the eighteenth century", quotes the line of "his famous work Heer" that describes the arrival of Heer's *baarat*: "Jevein log nigahein te raatan thaman dhol marde te rang lawande ne" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 56, folio not stated). He sets it beside the festivities at Ram Thamman during Baisakhi.

The Five Pirs who help the lovers are, for Shackle, a mark of this version: their "miraculous interventions on behalf of Ranjha are a notable feature of Varis Shah's Hīr" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 186, folio 161, n. 2 on 122). The annotator is less approving: the Five Pirs, "or Saints", as Waris Shah presents them, "seem to be concerned more with the success of an illicit love affair than with any idea of social morality!" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 16, folio 15, note 10).

## The Poet in His Poem

Waris Shah writes himself into his story. In Usborne's English he breaks in as a named commentator: "(Quoth the poet: 'This is not a boat but a marriage procession.')" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 6, folio 5); "See what a net of deceit Hir, the Jat girl, has spread" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, pp. 9–10, folios 8–9); "Ask Waris the poet, he knows this well." (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 12, folio 11); "Waris, if you trust in God, he will give you your portion of the good things of this world." (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 18, folio 17); "Qazis have no fear of God and eat the bread of iniquity" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 26, folio 25). Two of his asides:

*(Poet Waris: 'None can withstand when eyes fight eyes in the tourney of love.')* (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 8, folio 7)

*Waris, I shall be called the Hir of Ranjha in Heaven, in the assembly of Fatima, the daughter of the Holy Prophet.* (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 17, folio 16)

His name even enters a curse:

*May the curse of the Poet Waris Shah fall upon you, Hir, for you have robbed us of our dear brother-in-law.* (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 22, folio 21)

The annotator explains the habit: "Waris Shah, like most Persian and Urdu poets who have written poetical romances, has a habit of making a personal appearance on the stage whenever it pleases him, and even entering into conversation with his characters at will" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, pp. 17–18, folios 16–17, note 9). He does not always approve. "The poet's intervention as a commentator at this stage, however traditional in character, is utterly" unnecessary, he writes of one aside (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 10, folio 9, note 2), and of another, "Here the poet has jumped quite unnecessarily into the fray… even though on the wrong side" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 22, folio 21, note 5). Elsewhere he defends him: "This is more an expression of sympathy with Hir in her distress on the part of Waris Shah than a personal opinion, although he certainly seems to be angry with this particular Qazi." (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 27, folio 26, note 22); "Here Waris Shah is acting merely as a mouthpiece of popular opinion or sentiment" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 13, folio 12, note 9). Of the poem's learning he remarks that Arabic verbs, alas, are as "mysterious today as they were in Waris Shah's days" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 40, folio 39, note 17).

The poet makes large claims for his work, and repeats them:

*Poets, you should determine the worth of any poem. I have let my horse loose in the arena. Other poets have wasted their efforts in writing on petty themes. I have composed a grand poem.* (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 56, folio 55)

Elsewhere he writes that "Only the poet's poetry remains" in "everlasting remembrance, for no one has written such a beautiful Hir" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 55, folio 54).

*There are none so fortunate as the Kheras, no one so full of sin as the Poet Waris and there is no spell like that of his poetry.* (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 39, folio 38)

*Waris Shah, your name will be famous, if God be kind.* (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 57, folio 56)

On the last page he adds that "Waris Shah has shed the light of his genius over all the faithful in the world." (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 57, folio 56). Of one such boast the annotator writes: "Self-approbation is quite common among Persian and Urdu poets. As compared with most of them Waris Shah is modest — and true." (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 40, folio 39, note 18). Beside the boast stands the penitent:

*I have no capital of good works. Of what can I be proud? I have no hope without Thy grace. I am only a poor sinner. Without the favour of the Prophet I am helpless.* (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 56, folio 55)

He closes asking pardon: "God, overlook the fault of poor Waris Shah" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 57, folio 56), and "If I have let fall a word in ignorance may God forgive me." (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 57, folio 56).

It is the author, not an editor, who declares that the poem has an inner sense:

*Oh wise man, you should note that there is a secret under the guise of my words. I have written this Hir with care and meditation… I have planted a flower to give a sweet savour.* (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 56, folio 55)

And on the last page: "I have written it as a parable." (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 57, folio 56).

*The story of true lovers is like the scent of a rose in a garden. He who reads it with love in his heart will be able to separate the true from the false.* (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 57, folio 56)

*Waris Shah is anxious to see God's face even as Hir longed for her lover.* (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 57, folio 56)

Schimmel reads him the same way: "Warith Shah has used this story as a vehicle for mystical thought, and interspersed it with mystical explanations" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 17, folio 13). "Since the tale was known to everybody, the poet has the heroes recite short verses, which are commonly interpreted in a mystical sense." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 419, folio 388). Khalid traces the spiritual reading of the tale to Shah Hussain, whose theme was "subsequently picked up by Bulleh Shah and Waris Shah" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated); Schimmel, writing of the poetry of Sind and the Punjab at large, sees in Madho Lal Husayn's allusions to Hir what "seems to inaugurate (or perhaps even continue from unknown beginnings) a tradition typical of the religious poetry of the two provinces: the insertion of folk tales into the context of mystical thought" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 415, folio 384).

## In Verse: The Mystical Key

Schimmel quotes, in another's English, "the mystical interpretation given by Warith Shah" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 420, folio 389): an allegorical key to the romance, twenty-two lines long. In it, "Our soul is the tragic heroine" Heer, and the key goes on:

*Our body is the lover Ranjha, / Our spiritual preceptor is the Yogi Balmath,* (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 420, folio 389)

Further down the key reads:

*The secret chamber is the grave, / Saida Khera is the angel of death.* (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 420, folio 389)

*Reason is the flute / that to us the music of Divine wisdom conveys.* (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 420, folio 389)

His signature line, as she prints it:

*Warith Shah says: Those alone cross over / who have silently prayed to the Lord, / and ever devotedly recited His glorious Name.* (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 420, folio 389)

Her gloss on the whole begins 'Hir is our soul', and goes on: "one of the characteristics of Sindhi and Panjabi poetry is that the longing soul is always depicted as a woman; this typical aspect of Indian Sufi poetry was inherited from Hinduism" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 420, folio 389). Writing of the folk tales of both provinces, she adds that "The tragic heroines of the Sindhi and Panjabi folk tales—like Hir, Sassui, Sohni, and many others—represent the human soul in search of the beloved" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 465, folio 434).

## The Shrine

Waris Shah’s mausoleum at Jandiala Sher Khan is a place of pilgrimage for the devout and of homage for the lovers of Punjabi letters, and it has been developed over the years into a dignified complex befitting the poet’s stature. Devotees recite the Fatiha and seek blessing; singers come to perform *Heer* and the qawwali; and the annual urs gathers crowds who honour the poet-saint with music and remembrance. The shrine stands as a monument not only to a holy man but to the Punjabi language itself.

The books consulted add nothing to this account of the mausoleum. None of them describes the tomb or its complex, and none records the date of the urs, who built the complex, or the family that keeps it. Usborne's translation, the only one of them to name the poet's home, has him born and living at Jandiala, never buried there (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 43, folio 42; p. 57, folio 56).

## Recitation and Song

The poem was made to be heard. "Waris Shah composed Hir as an epic poem to be sung to music in public contexts. He is said to have recited it with great emotion among his speech communities in the Panjab" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 123, folio not stated). Abbas is careful about the poet himself: "Although it is commonly believed that he was a great musician, I have not found any evidence of that in the literature" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 123, folio not stated). The poet names three kinds of people who will carry the poem on: those who copy it, those who recite it and those who read it. "May he who reads or copies my poor efforts derive pleasure", he writes, and:

*May God pardon him who copies these words and give his bounty to those who recite it. May its readers enjoy the book.* (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 57, folio 56)

The tale was sung in the Punjab before him. Sujan Rai Bhandari, writing of the suba of Lahore in his own time, about 1107 AH, records that the people of the Punjab compose wonderful verses and designs on the love of Heer and Ranjha and sing them (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 512–511). Kanhaiya Lal, in the preface to his history of Lahore, calls Heer and Ranjha the story that is, in his words, on the tongue of good and bad, small and great, rich and poor in the land of the Punjab. Many books on it existed in Punjabi, he writes, but none but Punjabis could profit from them (the first word of that clause is uncertainly read), and that is why he wrote his own version in Persian (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 10, folio 5).

The modern books speak of the same song. "It is his verse which is sung, time and again, wherever Panjabis meet." (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 171, folio 153). "The visitor who stays in a Panjabi village will certainly find somebody to sing for him the old romance of Hir Ranjha in the classical form given it by Warith Shah in the late eighteenth century." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 414, folio 383). In *Pain and Grace* she writes that "even today a Panjabi will be deeply touched when this moving story is sung" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 17, folio 13). Khalid, in conversation, asks: "Isn't Heer Ranjha part of Punjabi culture? It is the most celebrated folk story here. For centuries it has been sung and dramatized" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated).

Abbas follows the song into the age of recording: "there is now substantial recorded material of Waris Shah's Hir available, both on audiocassettes and videos sung in the folk traditions", and "Many renowned folk artists in the Panjab, such as Reshma, Zahida Parvin, Nazir Begum, Sabiha Khanum, Tufail Niazi, and Iqbal Bahu, have sung it" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 123, folio not stated). Among her sources are the recordings *Hir: Waris Shah*, issued in Islamabad by the EMI Recording Co. (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 215, folio not stated), and an edition of the *Hir* by Ahmed Gujjar from the Institute of Folk Heritage, Islamabad (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 218, folio not stated). Writing of the Punjabi wedding rather than of the poet, she says that Hir's lament as she is carried off in the palanquin "is traditionally sung at almost every Panjabi wedding at the time of the bride's departure to her husband's home" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 124, folio not stated). Where she quotes the Hir songs at length from the renderings of Tufail Niazi and Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan, she names only the singers and does not name Waris Shah at all (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 136–154, folios not stated).

The song is not welcome everywhere. Khalid's informant Iqbal Qaiser told him that "in a lot of villages, the recitation of Waris Shah's Heer is not allowed. People believe that if the sounds of the verses fall on the ears of young girls, they too will elope like Heer" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated). At another shrine, that of Sahari Mal, Khalid saw that "a young man had put on Waris Shah's 'Heer' on his mobile phone while a group of men was playing cards in a corner" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).

## Setting: Sheikhupura District

The books that name the district speak of it, not of Jandiala Sher Khan. Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti records that Rani Jindan was first sent to the fort of Sheikhupura (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 591), and then to the fort of Chunar Garh (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 592). Usborne's annotator, in a note, writes of the Ghazi Pir, "buried at the foot of the hillock near Shah Kot in District Sheikhupura in West Pakistan", and counts Sheikhupura among the five districts whose people "even now congregate annually at this shrine" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 29, folio 28, note 4); the book draws no connection between Ghazi Pir and Waris Shah. Haroon Khalid notes that, before the new road, Sikh pilgrims "had to travel to Sheikupura and then head towards Chuhrkhana" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 85, folio 67).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **His dates.** The site's account gives c. 1722–1798: born around 1722, died around 1798. Usborne's translation gives neither year. Schimmel's index dates him only by century, "late 18th cent." (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 364, folio 346). Abbas prints a year of birth for him, but its digits cannot be read in the text available (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 120, folio not stated). A 1971 study by R. K. Kuldip, listed in Shackle's bibliography, carries other dates in its title, *Waris Shah 1730–1790*; they are Kuldip's dates, not Shackle's (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 202, folio 177).
- **When the poem was written.** The site's account says *Heer* was completed in 1766. The poet writes "in the year 1180 Hijri" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 56, folio 55). His annotator gives 1766 (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 12, folio 11, note 4), conceding beside the Bikramajit year that the two dates "do not exactly correspond" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 57, folio 56, note 2); elsewhere he gives a year that appears to read 1768, the reading uncertain in the printed text (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 33, folio 32, note 2); and elsewhere again he puts the poem five years after the Battle of Panipat of 1761 (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 44, folio 43, note 8). Schimmel gives "around 1794" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 17, folio 13; Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 419, folio 388), and elsewhere only "the late eighteenth century" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 171, folio 153; Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 414, folio 383).
- **Where it was written.** The site's account gives the tradition that he composed it at a village called Malka Hans, in a small chamber beside a mosque. In the poem he writes, "Kharal Hans is a well known place. Here I composed this story", and says he wrote it "in the southern country", which Usborne glosses as "the present Montgomery district of the Punjab" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 56, folio 55; p. 57, folio 56, note 2).
- **His Sufi allegiance.** The site's account places him in spirit in the Chishti tradition, tracing his discipleship to the line of the saint Makhdoom of Kasur. Usborne's annotator says only that he owed "allegiance to more than one sect of the Sufis", without naming them (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 2, folio 1, note 2); the poet claims "the aid of Shakarganj" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 57, folio 56); and, by the annotator's report, his Invocation praises Abdul Qadir Gilani (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 31, folio 30, note 7). The other books consulted name no order for him.
- **His teacher.** The site's account has him study at Kasur in the line of the saint Makhdoom of Kasur. Usborne's translation calls him "the pupil of the Saint of Kasur", the annotator's note naming the saint in a damaged form that ends 'Murtaza of Kasur' (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 57, folio 56). The introduction to Rafat's selection names "Khawaja Hafiz Ghulam Murtaza Qasuri", teacher also of Bulleh Shah (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 22, folio not stated), and elsewhere says only that Waris Shah "is said to have studied religion together with Bulleh Shah from the same teacher" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 38, folio 18).
- **His birthplace and his grave.** The site's account has him born at Jandiala Sher Khan and buried there. Usborne's translation names "Jandiala (the birthplace of the poet)" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 43, folio 42) and says that he "lives at Jandiala" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 57, folio 56); it never writes "Jandiala Sher Khan", and it attaches no grave to the place.
- **Legend or history.** The site's account calls the tale "the old Punjabi love-legend". Abbas calls it a myth that "existed for a long time before Waris Shah" and was "probably sung in the folk traditions for several centuries" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 120, folio not stated). Khalid reports Damodar's version, "which was then supported by Waris Shah", in which the story "is based on an actual story that Damodar saw unfold in front of his eyes" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated).
- **The poet and his annotator on the Punjab's past.** The poet says that when Shakarganj settled at Pakpattan the Punjab "was delivered of all its troubles"; the annotator answers that "this is not historically correct" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 2, folio 1). A simile in the poem runs "as if the armies of the Punjab had fallen upon Hindustan" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 7, folio 6); the annotator calls it "an imaginary situation", since "in the past the armies of the Punjab are known to have defended Hindustan on more than one occasion rather than to have fallen upon it at any time", and suggests that "The poet's flight of fancy may have been inspired by a mistaken sense of local patriotism." (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 8, folio 7, note 2).
- **His name.** The site's account, Usborne's translation, Rafat, Abbas and Khalid write Waris Shah. Schimmel writes "Warith Shah" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 17, folio 13; Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 171, folio 153; Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 414, folio 383). Shackle writes "Varis Shah" in his own prose (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 21, folio xx).
- **The translator's name.** Schimmel names him "Charles Frederick Usborne" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 420, folio 389). Abbas lists the translation as C. L. Osborne, *The Adventures of Hir and Ranjha* (London: Peter Owen) (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 205, 230, folios not stated).
- **Jalalpuri's book on the poet.** A note in Rafat's selection gives it as *Muqamat-e-Waris Shah*, published by Aina Adab, Lahore, in 1972, its author "Jalalpuri, Ali Abbas", the printed title being damaged (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 244, folio 224). Abbas lists Abbas Jalalpuri's *Muqamat-e Waris Shah*, spelling the title two ways, from the Hamid Raza Press, Lahore (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 206, 210, 226, folios not stated).

## Legacy

Waris Shah occupies a place in Punjabi culture that few poets hold in any language: his *Heer* is the canonical classic of the tongue, memorised, sung, and quoted by millions, and revered as scripture is revered. Through it he wove together the Sufi vision of divine love, the folk-romance of the Punjab, and the living speech of its people, and he made a single poem into a mirror of a whole civilisation. His tomb at Jandiala Sher Khan keeps the memory of the saint who was also the supreme poet of the Punjab.

The books measure that place in their own ways. For Shackle, Waris Shah's version is the yardstick of the legend: certain passages in one of Bulleh Shah's lyrics "are less characteristic of the Sufi lyric than of narrative treatments of the legend, like the famous Hīr by Varis Shah, and may be insertions by later imitators", and the closing section of that lyric "is hardly intelligible without quite close familiarity with the story as" Waris Shah tells it (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 186, folio 161, n. 1 on 122). The introduction to Rafat's selection calls the *Hir* "Punjabi's greatest romance in verse" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 22, folio not stated), and notes how the critics have favoured him over Bulleh Shah: "A 1979 book of criticism by Darshan Singh Maini, *Studies in Punjabi Poetry* examines Waris Shah but omits Bulleh Shah." and "Mr. Jalalpuri has already published an extremely readable book on Waris Shah" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 51, folio 31).

Writing of the tale rather than of the poet, Scott Kugle says that "Punjabi poets and their eager audiences poured into Hir and Ranjha the same erotic emotion that was invested in Radha and Krishna in regions closer to the Ganges. The story of Hir and Ranjha became a favorite Sufi device for conveying the value of erotic passion in spiritual life" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 228, folio 207). Haroon Khalid names Waris Shah with Baba Farid and Bulleh Shah among those "who spread the message of peace and brought several religious communities together instead of shedding blood", the "heroes" he would set in place of a poster's 'Muslim heroes' (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 14, ch. 8, folio not stated). Yet, he writes in *Walking with Nanak*, "the educated elite of Punjab ends up disowning, in fact, ridiculing" the poetry of Baba Farid, Guru Nanak, Shah Hussain, Waris Shah and Bulleh Shah (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 128, folio 110). In *A White Trail* he is named again, in Kartar's list of those whose language is Punjabi (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 202, folio not stated).

A shelf of studies has grown around him. Besides Hashimi's *Syed Waris Shah*, from the Majlis-e Panjabi Adab, Faisalabad (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 206, 224, folios not stated), Abbas cites Sant Sekhon's *The Love of Hir and Ranjha*, from the Panjab Agricultural University, Ludhiana (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 206, 232, folios not stated). Shackle lists his own essay, "Transitions and Transformations in Vāris Shāh's Hīr", in *The Indian Narrative: Perspectives and Patterns*, edited by C. Shackle and R. Snell (Wiesbaden: Harrassowitz, 1992), pages 241–263 (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 204, folio 179), and Kuldip's book of 1971, *A Critical Appreciation of the Poet and His Only Heer*, in whose title a bracketed judgement adds that it is "in fact mostly containing translations of Bullhe Shah" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 202, folio 177). Of the English versions, Schimmel writes: "The best translation is Charles Frederick Usborne, Hir Ranjha", in the edition of Mumtaz Hasan (Karachi, 1966) (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 420, folio 389).
