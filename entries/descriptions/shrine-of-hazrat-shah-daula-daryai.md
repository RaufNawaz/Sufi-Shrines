## Overview

The shrine of *Hazrat Shah Daula Daryai* stands in the city of Gujrat, in the fertile plain between the Chenab and Jhelum rivers in the Punjab. It commemorates a Sufi saint of the Mughal period, remembered above all as a builder of bridges, wells and *sarais* (rest-houses) and as a benefactor of the poor, the traveller and the afflicted. His epithet *Daryai* — from *darya*, "river" — is traditionally explained by his works of construction across the region's torrential streams. For nearly four centuries the *darbar* has been a focus of popular devotion in central Punjab; it is also, more painfully, the site around which one of South Asia's most persistent and troubling folk traditions grew up — that of the so-called "Shah Daula's mice" (*chuhas*), children affected by microcephaly who were historically associated with the shrine. The account below treats the saint first, and then addresses that tradition factually and with the medical understanding it requires.

Three books speak of the saint and his tomb directly, each from a different century and a different vantage. Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* describes him, in its account of the suba of Lahore and the town of Gujrat, as once the slave of a man of Sialkot who became the town's great builder and benefactor, and records that near that city his tomb is a place of visitation for people of every class (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 515–514). Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori's *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, a nineteenth-century register of the saints of the Punjab, gives him entry no. 124 under the name Shah Daula Daryai Gujrati (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 18, folio 6), with a spiritual chain, a death date in a chronogram, and a picture of his household in his lifetime. And Jamal Malik's chapter in Pnina Werbner and Helene Basu's *Embodying Charisma* prefaces its reading of Ahmad Nadîm Qâsimî's Urdu story 'Bain' ('Dirge'), which is set at this shrine, with a historical sketch of the saint and his cult drawn from colonial and *malfûzât* sources; the volume also carries a photograph captioned "The shrine of Shâh Daulah" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 8, folio not stated).

The three books spell the name in their own ways. Bhandari writes Shah Daula with a final *alif*; Lahori's entry likewise ends the name in *alif*, while the editor's source list and index in the same edition end it in *he*, as Daulah (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 317, folio 305); Malik writes "Shâh Daulah", and inside Qâsimî's story the saint is "Sân'în Daule Shâh" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 210, folio 199).

## The Life of the Saint

Reliable dates for Shah Daula are difficult to fix, and the sources differ; he is traditionally said to have lived roughly between 1581 and 1675, and to have settled in Gujrat in the early seventeenth century — some accounts placing this in the reign of the Mughal emperor Jahangir, others in that of Akbar. He is said to have travelled in his youth to the neighbourhood of Sialkot, where he became the disciple of a Suhrawardi *faqir* remembered as Shah Saidan Sarmast, serving at his master's *khanqah* with great devotion before being entrusted with its care. He later established his own *khanqah* at Gujrat, at the place formerly known as Garhi Shah Daula.

## Descent and Early Years

Malik gives the saint a birth name: "Shâh Daulah—whose birth name was Qâsim —lived in Mughal times and died at an advanced age around AH 1676 in Gujrat" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 205, folio 194). His descent, Malik writes, is contested: "His ancestors were Afghans, but it is also claimed that he was a Sayyid (descendant of the Prophet)" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 205, folio 194). Of his youth he writes: "After a turbulent childhood he became supervisor of the treasury of a local official, and also worked as a mendicant and an ordinary labourer for some time" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 205, folio 194).

Bhandari's account, which he introduces as what is said, begins differently. At first, he writes, Shah Daula was the slave of a resident of Sialkot whose name cannot be read with certainty in the printed text. He loved the faqirs and served Hazrat Mian Sayyidna devotedly; the editor's footnote gives a variant, perhaps Sayyidnam, which cannot be read with certainty (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 515).

## The Master and the Turning

In Bhandari's telling, the change in Shah Daula came at his master's deathbed. When Mian Sayyidna was about to die, he cast a grace-bearing glance on Shah Daula; Shah Daula's state changed, and the chamber of his inner self was lit with the lights of gnosis. He then left Sialkot and settled in Gujrat (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 515). Bhandari adds that because his heart was so illumined, treasures of the unseen became manifest to him (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 515).

Malik's sketch gives a longer apprenticeship and a quarrel: "He then spent twelve years at the feet of a Suhrawardi Sufi, but it is recorded too that he was initiated into the Qadiri order" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 205, folio 194). "Due to some disputes with the local people of Sialkot, he migrated from there and, after some years, moved to Gujrat", Malik continues, 'in obedience to divine instructions' (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 205, folio 194).

## The Spiritual Chain

Lahori sets the saint at the end of a long chain that runs back, through Rajan Qattal of Uch, to Baha al-Din Multani. Shah Daula, he writes, was the disciple and *khalifa* of Shaikh Sina Sarmast; he of Shah Monga; he of Shah Kabir; he of Shaikh Shahr Allah, a name whose reading is uncertain in the printed text; he of Shaikh Yusuf; he of Pir Burhan; he of Shaikh Sadr al-Din; he of Shaikh Badr al-Din; he of Shaikh Ismail Qureshi; he of Shaikh Sadr al-Din Rajan Qattal Uchi; he of Shaikh Rukn al-Din Abu al-Fath Multani; he of Shaikh Sadr al-Din Arif; and he of Shaikh Baha al-Din Multani (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 184). He adds that the saint also received complete *faiz* from the Chishti family, whom he calls the people of paradise (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 184).

Lahori counts him among the famous saints of the Punjab (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 186), a man of lofty stations and marvels, of *jazb*, *ishq*, *zuhd* and *riyazat*, who gathered in himself both outward and inward gifts (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 184).

## The Saint's Household

Lahori pictures the saint's establishment in his lifetime as a royal one: a court like the court of kings, with thousands of servants, horses and palanquins waiting at his door (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 184). The crowd of those in need, he adds, was there at every hour (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 184).

Bhandari describes the same throng and the saint's open hand. In his lifetime multitudes upon multitudes came from every quarter for *ziyarat* and made abundant offerings in cash and kind; he gave visitors and petitioners more than they had brought, a generosity such that a whole lifetime of Hatim's generosity would not come to a tenth of a tenth of it (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 515). Malik, from his sources, records the breadth of the saint's following and a refusal: he was popular among "Muslims as well as Hindus, became famous for his miracles and received large gifts" in kind, "but refused to accept land grants" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 205, folio 194).

## The Death of the Saint

The books give the year of his death in several ways. Lahori writes that he died in the year 1075 AH, and that the chronogram of his death is the phrase *Khuda Dost*, Friend of God (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 186). The edition's modern editor, in a footnote on the same entry, gives instead a death in 1087 AH / 1676 CE, with both figures uncertain in the printed text, citing Mushtaq Ram Gujrati's *Karamat Nama*, owned by Sayyid Sharafat Naushahi (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 184, folio 182). Bhandari dates the death by the reign of Alamgir: at last, in the seventeenth regnal year of Alamgir, he departed to the world of permanence, the ordinal uncertain in the printed text (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 515). Malik's sketch says he "died at an advanced age around AH 1676 in Gujrat" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 205, folio 194). The site's own account gives 1675.

## The Builder of Bridges

What is most consistently remembered of him is not doctrine but works of public benefit. He is credited with constructing bridges over seasonal watercourses — including, by tradition, a bridge over the Nala Dek — together with wells, mosques and rest-houses along the routes of travel, at a time when such infrastructure eased both trade and pilgrimage across the Punjab. He is also remembered for his *langar*, his charity to the destitute, and his care for animals and for people living with disabilities. In this he embodies a distinctively practical, service-oriented sanctity: a saint honoured less for miracles than for the tangible relief of hardship.

Bhandari makes this the heart of his account. Shah Daula settled in the *qasba* of Gujrat, built tanks, wells and mosques, and bridged the Chenab, which came down from the hills and used to damage the town (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 515). He raised buildings and bridges in many places, above all a bridge on the highway over the river Deg, five *kos* from Eminabad in the direction of Lahore, a work so solid that no man of wealth could raise a building as strong (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 515). The bridge became a landmark: in his description of the Deg's course, Bhandari has the river pass below Shah Daula's bridge, which stands on the highway (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 516).

Bhandari credits the saint with the making of the town itself. Gujrat, he writes, had never been much of a place until Shah Daula settled there (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 515), and his settling there made the town grow in people and prosperity (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 515).

Malik reads the saint's two titles through the same works. During his life, he writes, the saint devoted himself to "works of public utility" and to the construction of religious buildings, and this is why he was called *Daulah*, which Malik glosses 'wealth'; his building of bridges, especially, earned him the second title, glossed 'one affiliated to water' (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 205, folio 194). At that point in the text the printed form of the second title is uncertain; elsewhere in the chapter it is *Daryâ'î*.

## The Shrine

His tomb, surmounted by a dome and set beside a spacious mosque, dates in its essentials from the seventeenth century, though the structure was substantially rebuilt in the late nineteenth century (traditionally 1898) and has been repaired and extended since. Since the mid-twentieth century the shrine has been administered by the Punjab Auqaf (Religious Affairs) Department, which now organises the annual three-day *urs* with arrangements for *langar*, sanitation and security, and with gatherings devoted to the saint's life and teaching.

Lahori, writing in the 1870s, calls the tomb a living place of pilgrimage: his mazar in the city of Gujrat, Punjab, is a place of pilgrimage for the people (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 186). Bhandari had placed it near the town as a place of visitation for people of every class (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 515–514).

Malik gives the reconstruction one date: the shrine "had been reconstructed (1898)" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 206, folio 195). It is the only date for the building in his chapter, which names no founder, no *sajjada nashin* and no custodian family.

## Endowment and Administration

The shrine, Malik writes, held no land: "the lack of regular income at the dargâh, since no land was attached to it, resulted in the holy men's total dependence on the alms and offering of the followers", and colonial authors also suspected the followers of trafficking in women, a suspicion Malik reports from Ibbetson and Maclagan (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 206, folio 195). He quotes the editor of the *Indian Antiquary* on "the absence of landed property in possession of the band, or any recognized right to succession to the leadership, and the entire dependence on the earnings" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 206, folio 195).

Of the modern administration he writes of "this Sufi tomb, which was taken over by the Pakistan Auqaf Department in the 1960s" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 204, folio 193). The takeover, in his reading, did not displace the older authority on the spot: "even the nationalisation policy of the Auqaf Department since the 1960s could not totally change the perceptions deeply rooted in the minds of the rural population and the authority exercised by the shrine administrator, the mujâwir, who ideally represents state power" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 206, folio 195).

## Fertility, Justice and Punishment

Malik sums up the shrine's reputation in a phrase: "The sanctuary of Shâh Daulah thus has a particular reputation—fertility, justice and punishment" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 206, folio 195). He observes too that "the dargâh is a female domain, at least in quantitative terms—in contrast to mosques, which are frequented by men" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 208, folio 197), while adding elsewhere that "the power in shrines is usually held by men" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 214, folio 203).

## Devotion and Practice

The site's account describes an annual three-day *urs* under Auqaf administration, with *langar*. The books consulted give no date, month or duration for the *urs*.

Malik, writing of the shrine's older practice, describes the vow of a child. "The custom used to be to leave the child, as soon as it was weaned, at Shah Daulah's khanqah [Sufi lodge], as an offering to him"; after the saint's death, devotees "would make a vow either to present the child when born or make an offering to the shrine" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 205, folio 194). He analyses the exchange at the tomb as "a kind of a bargain between the parents and the mujâwir, like the mahr (bridewealth): the parents are declared dwellers of paradise (jannatî—without intercession of any authority) by the shrine administrator. In response they leave the child—the present (ni‘mat) —as a trust (amânat) at the dargâh" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 211, folio 200).

## The "Chuhas" of Shah Daula: Folklore and the Medical Reality

No account of this shrine can avoid the tradition of the *chuhas* — a word meaning "mice" or "rats" in Punjabi — the name long given to microcephalic children associated with the *darbar*. This tradition must be approached with care, both because it concerns vulnerable and disabled human beings and because it has frequently been sensationalised. The essential facts, as documented by observers and scholars since the nineteenth century, are these.

Microcephaly is a medical condition in which the head and brain are abnormally small, typically accompanied by intellectual disability; it has recognised genetic and environmental causes and occurs in populations everywhere. At Gujrat, individuals with this condition came to be linked with Shah Daula's shrine through a folk belief — first recorded in print in January 1839 by the observer Shahamat Ali — that childless women who prayed for offspring at the *darbar* would be granted a child, the first of whom must, according to the legend, be dedicated to the shrine and would be born with the characteristic small head. Such dedicated children were called *chuhas* and were often maintained at, or associated with, the shrine.

From the mid-nineteenth century onward the phenomenon attracted European medical and ethnographic attention, and with it arose the sensational speculation that the small heads were produced deliberately, by binding or by fitting clamps or vessels to the skulls of infants. This claim has never been substantiated: no such instrument has ever been found, no such procedure has ever been witnessed, and medical authorities regard the intentional production of microcephaly in this manner as implausible without causing fatal injury. The modern consensus is that the *chuhas* were, and are, individuals with naturally occurring microcephaly whose condition was interpreted through the lens of local religious belief and who were, in effect, drawn into the economy of the shrine — historically, in some periods, being sent out as itinerant beggars. It should be stressed that the folk belief linking the condition to the saint's blessing or curse has no basis in fact and is not endorsed here; it is recorded only as a documented social phenomenon.

Malik's chapter, drawing on colonial sources, records the same tradition in the words of its time. The shrine, he writes, "is particularly known for its Chûhâs or Rat-children, who are without understanding or the power of speech. After some initial training they are passed on to a faqîr (religious mendicant) and made to beg in the name of Shâh Daulah" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 205, folio 194). A further passage describes a band or order of faqirs living off these children and "the convenient existence of an important shrine" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 206, folio 195). Malik reports, without endorsing it, the colonial suspicion that the children were "artificially produced after their birth as ordinary infants", citing Ibbetson and Maclagan (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 206, folio 195). He also reports a physiological explanation from Sharif Kunjahi's modern life of the saint: "the bad conscience of parents, particularly mothers, set free certain chemical reactions that caused this peculiarity" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 206, folio 195).

Malik argues that the cult is late. Because it is absent from "contemporary and even later malfûzât and biographical accounts" of the saint, he concludes: "It therefore seems likely that this cult arose only later, by the end of the nineteenth century—after the shrine had been reconstructed (1898)" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 206, folio 195).

Reform came in the twentieth century. When the Auqaf Department assumed control of the shrine in the mid-twentieth century, it acted to forbid both the abandonment of such children to the *darbar* and the alleged deforming practices, and the population of *chuhas* associated with the site steadily declined. By the early twenty-first century observers reported that only one or two remained. The episode endures chiefly as a cautionary study for historians of medicine, disability and popular religion, in which a genuine medical condition, human vulnerability, exploitation and folk piety became tragically entangled.

## Legends and Miracles

The legend behind the *chuhas*, as Malik quotes it from colonial sources, runs: "Shah Daulah, like other saints, could procure the birth of a child for a couple desiring one, but the first child born in response to his intercession would be a Chuha—brainless, small-headed, long-eared and rat-faced" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 205, folio 194). "After the saint's death the miracle continued, but in a modified way": where a promised gift was withheld, "the spirit of the offended saint so worked on the parents that the next child was born a Chuha, and all subsequent children as well, until the original vow was fulfilled" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 205, folio 194). These are the words of the legend as the colonial sources recorded it, not a description of any person.

Malik attributes to the same colonial sources a second miracle: "through his charismatic or miraculous powers (karâmât) Shâh Daulah was able to control female infanticide, which was widespread in Jammu State at the time" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 205, folio 194).

Bhandari's single marvel is an inward one: because the saint's heart was so illumined, treasures of the unseen became manifest to him (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 515). Lahori calls him a man of marvels and of *jazb*, ecstatic attraction (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 184).

## The Shrine in Fiction: Qâsimî's 'Bain'

Ahmad Nadîm Qâsimî's Urdu story 'Bain' ('Dirge'), as Malik reads it, "deals with the activities in and around the dargâh (court, shrine) of Shâh Daulah Daryâ'î in Gujrat" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 204, folio 193), although the story does not mention the *chuha* cult, to "which Nadîm's story does not, however, refer directly" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 204, folio 193). What follows is the story's world, not a report of the shrine.

In the story the shrine "had become an institution of justice, symbolised by the holy hand of Shâh Daulah, which, in order to judge, would rise from the grave" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 207, folio 196). Malik gives the passage in full: "The upper part [sar bâne kî taraf se] of the holy grave opened and the Pîr stretched out his holy hand [dast-e mubârak]. The culprit then was drawn to the shrine [mazâr sharîf] from wherever he was, laid his neck into the holy hand of the Sân'înjî [the pîr] and died on the spot [dher honâ]" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 210, folio 199). "Therefore, nobody dared to neglect Sân'în Daule Shâh's orders" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 210, folio 199).

The *mujâwir* rules through a dream. He demands that the girl Rano recite at the *dargâh* for three days, which "Shâh Daulah had ordered in a dream" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 207, folio 196); otherwise, the villagers are warned, "Sân'în Daule Shâhjî would put all villagers to fire and ash" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 209, folio 198).

The *urs* frames the story's crisis: "the girl's maturity or sexualisation and the marriage proposal coincide with the anniversary of the holy man's death, ‘urs, the unification of the pîr with God" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 209, folio 198), and "During the ‘urs the girl falls victim to the corrupted society" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 211, folio 200). The shrine's kitchen and bath-house become the scene of defiance: Rano flings the skirt her parents bring "into the burning coals of the public kitchen (langar)", and her father later throws the *mujâwir*'s shroud "into the burning coals of the fire heating the water for the great ablution (ghusl)" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 212, folio 201).

Malik's verdict on what the story shows is severe: "It shows that the shrine cult has become a falsehood void of any mysticism, or, in other words, an expression of corrupt political power" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 208, folio 197), and "fear is the prime motivational force inducing the villagers and adepts to be loyal to the moral authority emanating from the shrine" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 210, folio 199).

## In Verse

The books carry little verse on Shah Daula. What they hold is a chronogram. Lahori's entry closes the saint's life with a two-word *tarikh*, *Khuda Dost*, Friend of God, which he gives as encoding the year of death, 1075 AH; the phrase *Khuda Dost*, in his words, is the chronogram (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 186).

The same book closes with a verse chronogram of its own, by Mufti Ghulam Safdar Lahori, which gives the year 1292 AH. It speaks of the scope of the whole book, the saints of the Punjab, and not of this saint:

جو خدا کے دوست تھے پنجاب میں
حال ان کا اس سے ہوتا ہے عیان

*A chronogram on the book's own subject, the saints of the Punjab; the book gives no English.* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 282, folio 270)

## The Saint in Writing

The books point to a small literature on the saint. The *Hadiqat al-Awliya*'s editor catalogues, as his source for the death date, item no. 64, a *Karamat Nama* of 1132 AH on the life of Shah Daula Daryai Gujrati, by Mushtaq Ram Gujrati, owned by Sayyid Sharafat Naushahi (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 296, folio 284). The same list has item no. 235, a *Tazkira* of Shah Daula Gujrati by M. S. Nasim, published in Lahore in 1970 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 304, folio 292).

Malik names his own sources as "historical and biographical accounts as well as on malfûzât literature (namely, the imputed speeches and utterances of holy men)", among them Ibbetson's *Glossary* and the work of Sharîf Kunjâhî (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 205, folio 194). Kunjâhî's book, *Hadrat Shâh Daulah Daryâ'î Gujrâtî: Hayât o ta'lîmât*, was published in Lahore in 1985 by the Punjab Auqaf Department's imprint, Markaz Ma'ârif Awliyâ Mahkamah Awqâf Punjâb (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 217, folio 206). It is in this title alone that Malik's chapter calls the saint *Gujrâtî*.

## Setting: Gujrat

The books that describe the town of Gujrat speak of the place, not of the shrine.

Bhandari places it in the Chaj doab between the Chenab and the Bihat, which he calls the Chunhat doab: Gujrat, he writes, is a *qasba* there. It was settled under the emperor Akbar, and villages were taken out of the pargana of Sialkot to make it a pargana of its own (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 515). Under the heading "Founding of Gujrat and Amanabad" he tells how the pargana was made: all this land had been part of Sialkot, and the Jats and the Raich held sway over it (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 177). In the regnal year 37 or, by another account, 42, as the emperor was travelling from Lahore to Kashmir, a body of Gujars came to him after the Chenab crossing to complain of oppression by the Jats and the Raich, a name uncertain in the printed text (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 176–177). The villages of their *zamindari* were separated off as a pargana named Gujrat (the name uncertain in the printed text), and a small fort and a town were founded (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 176). Bhandari does not name Shah Daula in this passage.

The town in Bhandari's own day was a market of the province, a resort of every kind of people and a gathering place for the goods of every land and the rare things of the age. Rare swords and *jamdhars* were made there, and more *chikan* work was done there than in Sialkot; the district bred Iraqi horses, some worth up to ten thousand rupees (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 514).

Bhandari also sets an imperial episode in the neighbourhood. When the prince Khusrau rebelled and was turned back at the Chenab, he was seen at the ford at dawn, and Mir Abu al-Qasim Khan and the eunuch Hilal Khan, who were in the neighbourhood of Gujrat, seized him and carried him to Gujrat (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 146–145). He says nothing there of the saint or the shrine.

Usborne's annotator to Waris Shah ties the town's name to a tribe: "The Gujars are a famous tribe of the old Punjab who keep animals and sell their milk. They also engage in agriculture on a large scale. A number of Punjab towns, such as Gujranwala, Gujrat and Gujar Khan are named after the Gujar tribe" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 19, folio 18, note 3). Haroon Khalid's informant Iqbal Qaiser dates the town's rise to Akbar: "Gujrat became an important city during the tenure of Akbar. Before that it was a small village. One of Akbar's wives is also buried here" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated). Khalid's account of the Punjab after the Mughals also has the governor Meer Manu waiting 'near the city of Gujrat' (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9).

Gujrat has other saints. Khalid writes of a crow saint who "seemed to have quite a following in this historic city", and of the city's major shrines put "on high alert" after a blast in Sindh (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated). A poster reproduced in *Embodying Charisma* names a 'Sain Kanwan Wali (Gujrat)' (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 160, folio 149).

The Chenab country around the town is the land of Punjabi romance. Shemeem Burney Abbas begins the tale of Sohni with a potter "who lived in the Gujrat district of the Panjab" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 125, folio not stated), and places Ranjha's clan at Takht Hazara, "now Gujrat District of Pakistan" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 122, folio not stated). Usborne's annotator likewise has Sohni belong to Gujrat, "on the river Chenab", the town's name garbled in the printed edition (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 12, folio 11, note 5).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The year of death.** The site's account gives 1675, with a birth year of 1581. Lahori gives 1075 AH, with the chronogram *Khuda Dost* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 186). His modern editor gives 1087 AH / 1676, both figures uncertain in the printed text, citing Mushtaq Ram Gujrati's *Karamat Nama* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 184, folio 182). Bhandari gives the seventeenth regnal year of Alamgir, the ordinal uncertain in the printed text (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 515). Malik gives "around AH 1676" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 205, folio 194).
- **His master's name.** The site's account names Shah Saidan Sarmast. Lahori names Shaikh Sina Sarmast (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 184). Bhandari names Hazrat Mian Sayyidna, with an editor's variant, perhaps Sayyidnam, which cannot be read with certainty (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 515). Malik names no master, speaking of twelve years "at the feet of a Suhrawardi Sufi" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 205, folio 194).
- **His order.** The site's account makes his master a Suhrawardi *faqir*. Lahori traces his chain to Baha al-Din Multani through Rajan Qattal of Uch, and adds complete *faiz* from the Chishti line (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 184). Malik gives a Suhrawardi teacher but records "too that he was initiated into the Qadiri order" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 205, folio 194).
- **His early life.** The site's account has him travel in his youth to the neighbourhood of Sialkot to become a disciple. Bhandari says he was first the slave of a resident of Sialkot (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 515). Malik says he became supervisor of a local official's treasury and worked as a mendicant and labourer (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 205, folio 194).
- **Why he left Sialkot.** The site's account has him entrusted with his master's *khanqah* before establishing his own at Gujrat. Bhandari has him leave Sialkot after his dying master's glance transformed him (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 515). Malik has him leave "Due to some disputes with the local people of Sialkot", moving to Gujrat after some years in obedience to divine instructions (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 205, folio 194).
- **His descent.** Malik gives both accounts himself: Afghan ancestors, and the claim that he was a Sayyid (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 205, folio 194).
- **The great bridge.** The site's account names, by tradition, a bridge over the Nala Dek. Bhandari names a bridge over the river Deg on the highway five *kos* from Eminabad toward Lahore, and a bridge on the Chenab at Gujrat (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 515).
- **Where the tomb stands.** The site's account and Lahori place it in the city of Gujrat (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 186). Bhandari places it near the town (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 515–514).
- **When the chuha tradition began.** The site's account says the folk belief was first recorded in print in January 1839 by Shahamat Ali. Malik, from its absence in the early lives of the saint, judges that the cult "arose only later, by the end of the nineteenth century—after the shrine had been reconstructed (1898)" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 206, folio 195).
- **The cause of the children's condition.** The site's account holds that they had naturally occurring microcephaly and that deliberate deformation was never substantiated. Malik reports the colonial suspicion that they were "artificially produced after their birth as ordinary infants", and Kunjahi's explanation that the bad conscience of parents set free "certain chemical reactions that caused this peculiarity" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 206, folio 195).
- **What the Auqaf takeover changed.** The site's account dates the takeover to the mid-twentieth century and says the Department forbade the abandonment of children, after which their number declined. Malik dates it to the 1960s and writes that it "could not totally change the perceptions deeply rooted in the minds of the rural population and the authority exercised by the shrine administrator" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 206, folio 195).
- **The spelling of the name.** Lahori's entry ends the name in *alif* and his editor's lists end it in *he* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 317, folio 305); Bhandari ends it in *alif* (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 515); Malik writes "Daulah", and in one sentence the second title's printed form is uncertain where elsewhere it is *Daryâ'î* (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 205, folio 194).

## Legacy

Shah Daula Daryai is remembered in the Punjab as a saint of works and mercy, whose bridges and wells served the living and whose shrine remains a busy centre of devotion in Gujrat. The disquieting tradition of the *chuhas* that attached itself to his name in later centuries belongs to the social history that grew up around the shrine rather than to the saint himself, and is best understood today through the twin lenses of medical fact and compassion for those who were affected. The *darbar* continues to draw pilgrims, to feed the poor at its *langar*, and to hold its annual *urs* under Auqaf administration.

The books add three witnesses across three centuries. For Bhandari, writing in the fortieth regnal year of Aurangzeb, the saint was the man who made Gujrat grow in people and prosperity, the builder of a famous bridge on the Lahore highway, and the saint whose tomb drew people of every class (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 515). For Lahori in the 1870s he was a saint of the Punjab whose tomb was a place of pilgrimage and whose death was remembered in a chronogram (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 186). For Malik, writing on Qâsimî's story in a volume of 1998, his shrine had a particular reputation for "fertility, justice and punishment" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 206, folio 195), and had become the setting of one of Urdu fiction's critiques of shrine power.
