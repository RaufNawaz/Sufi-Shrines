## Overview

Gurdwara Rori Sahib stands at Eminabad, the old town near Gujranwala in the Punjab that was once known as *Saidpur*, and it is among the most evocative of all the shrines that mark the earthly travels of Guru Nanak (1469–1539), the founder of the Sikh faith. Its name comes from the Punjabi word *rori* — the gravel, pebbles, and coarse grit of the ground — for tradition holds that it was here, on a bed of pebbles, that the Guru rested during his sojourn in the town. The gurdwara belongs to a wider constellation of sacred sites clustered around Eminabad, each preserving the memory of an episode from Guru Nanak's life, and together they make the town one of the most important centres of Sikh pilgrimage in present-day Pakistan.

The shrine remembers two figures: the Guru and his companion Bhai Mardana, the Muslim minstrel who travelled with him. Only one of the books consulted describes the gurdwara itself. Haroon Khalid, travelling with the historian Iqbal Qaiser, visited it and recorded Qaiser's account of its name and its history (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 116–117, folios 98–99; p. 118, folio 100). Khalid identifies the gurdwara he describes as Rori Sahib only through Qaiser's explanation of the name (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 118, folio 100). The older histories say nothing of a gurdwara at Eminabad. Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi records that Guru Nanak and Mardana were at Saiyidpur, which he glosses as Amanabad, when it was sacked, but he names no gurdwara and no site of any kind there (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 410, folio 386). Harish Dhillon's retelling of the Janamsakhis sets several of its stories at Saidpur and never names Eminabad or Rori Sahib (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 56, folio 62; p. 224, folio 246). Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* names Mardana but no shrine (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).

Khalid's book also describes a second "Gurdwara Rori Sahib", at Jhaman near the border of Lahore and Kasur. It is a different place, and nothing told of it belongs to the Eminabad shrine (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 48, folio 30).

## Guru Nanak at Eminabad

Eminabad occupies a cherished place in the *janamsakhi* literature — the traditional narratives of Guru Nanak's life. Popular memory often links this shrine to the story of the Guru lodging with *Bhai Lalo*, a low-caste carpenter of honest livelihood, in preference to the lavish hospitality of the wealthy official Malik Bhago — though this episode is most specifically tied by local tradition to the nearby Gurdwara Khuhi Bhai Lalo. In the best-loved telling, the Guru is said to have squeezed the simple bread of Bhai Lalo's honest labour in one hand and the rich food of Malik Bhago in the other, so that milk flowed from the first and blood from the second — a parable, in the devotion of the Panth, of the difference between righteous earnings and wealth wrung from others. Whether recounted as history or as teaching-story, the episode fixed Eminabad in Sikh memory as a place where Guru Nanak honoured the dignity of honest work.

To Rori Sahib itself attaches the memory of the Guru seated in meditation upon the rough pebbled ground, and it is this humble scene of devotion — the saint at prayer, indifferent to comfort — that the shrine was raised to preserve. The town is also associated with the turbulence of Babur's invasion: tradition holds that Guru Nanak witnessed the sack of Saidpur by the Mughal armies around 1520–21, an experience that many hold inspired the anguished hymns known as the *Babarvani*, in which the Guru lamented the suffering visited upon the innocent.

Rizvi tells the episode as history. Back from eastern India about 1520, Guru Nanak found "the Panjab plunged into a number of significant political crises"; Babur, after two minor raids, "invaded a third time over the Panjab in 926/1520 reaching as far as Sialkot" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 409, folio 385). Then, Rizvi writes, "Guru Nanak and Mardana witnessed the massacre of the inhabitants of Saiyidpur (Amanabad) and were taken captive" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 410, folio 386). Dhillon's retelling has the same capture as legend: at Saidpur, on the way home from the Guru's fourth journey, "legend has it" that Mardana too was taken prisoner with him (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 224, folio 246). Dhillon places this at the time of Babur's "third incursion into India" and gives no year for it (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 224, folio 246).

Khalid writes that the Janamsakhis record a meeting between Nanak and Babur at Saidpur, in which Babur asks for a blessing to conquer India and Nanak rebukes him (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 114, folio 96). The encounter "was also interpreted to mark the beginning of this antagonism between the Mughals and the Sikhs" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 97, folio 79), and Khalid later calls Jahangir "the great grandson of Emperor Babur who had incarcerated Guru Nanak" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 133, folio not stated).

Dhillon's retelling brings the Guru to Saidpur early, on the first of his journeys, from Sultanpur by way of Talwandi (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 56, folio 62), and he passes through it again on the way home from the fourth, coming by Hasan Abdal, Tilla Balnath and Katasraj to Saidpur and on to Kartarpur (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 224–225, folios 246–247). In Dhillon's telling of the Lalo story Mardana has his own part. At first he refuses Lalo's millet *roti*, and eats it only when Nanak calls it *prasad* (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 60, folio 66; p. 61, folio 67); later it is Mardana who carries Lalo's *roti* to Bhago's house (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 70, folio 76; p. 71, folio 77).

Khalid's own reconstruction of Nanak's life, which he says is partly fictionalised (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 17, folio not stated), makes Saidpur the first goal of the Guru's travels, the city "with the grandest Hindu temples" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 108, folio 90). In it Nanak tells Lalo that he "used to be in charge of the food store at the court of the Diwa[n] [OCR?] of Sultanpur" and had left it to "take up this garb and travel the world in search of Truth" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 104–105, folios 86–87). His dress mixed the dress of several traditions: a red dervish's *choga* rather than a green one, a fakir's white belt, a cap partly covered by a turban, and wooden sandals, which Khalid reads as "a conscious effort by Nanak to dilut[e] his religio[us] identity" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 105–106, folios 87–88). Writing in his own voice, Khalid says that Nanak's statement that there was no Hindu and no Muslim was questioned "at the court of Nawab Daulat Khan Lodhi at Sultanpur", "at the court of Malik Bhago in Eminabad" and at Rai Bhoi di Talwandi (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 144, folio 126).

## The Name of the Shrine

Iqbal Qaiser gave Khalid an explanation of the name. Rori Sahib, he said, is not where Nanak worked the grindstone but "the place where he was picked up by the guards", a small mound outside Eminabad where there were pebbles, "hence the name Rori Sahib". "Nanak was sitting here with Mardana at the time of his arrest" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 118, folio 100). Part of Qaiser's sentence describing the pebbles is garbled in the printed text.

## The Shrine and Its Architecture

The gurdwara that survives today owes its grandeur to the era of the Sikh Empire, having been built during the reign of *Maharaja Ranjit Singh* (1801–1839), whose patronage adorned so many of the sites connected with the Gurus. The central building is an imposing structure of finely cut brickwork rising through several storeys, crowned by a large central dome (*gumbad*) flanked by smaller domed pavilions (*chhatris*) in the manner characteristic of Sikh sacred architecture. A room at the rear, roofed with an especially graceful ribbed white lotus dome and ringed by a circumambulatory verandah, is said to enclose the very patch of pebbled earth on which the Guru rested.

Khalid describes the approach as he found it. The gatekeeper knew Iqbal Qaiser; empty grounds lay on either side of the path, and "a few police officials lazed on a charpoy" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 116–117, folios 98–99). The doorway is "a splendid construction. A verse from the Guru Granth Sahib was carved on top of it with an artistic effect of bricks" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 117, folio 99).

Qaiser named its designer as "Bhai Ram Singh", Principal of the "Mayo School of Arts in Lahore", who designed "the Indian gallery in Buckingham Palace". Ram Singh came from a family of carpenters, and he created "the effect of woodcutting from the use of bricks". "It was constructed around 1922-23. Before that, it was a small shrine. Only this gateway was constructed by Bhai Ram Singh." (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 117, folio 99)

Visitors took off their shoes at the portal. "The main shrine was an ordinary room which too was locked." Beside the gateway was a small courtyard with several rooms, where "A few Pathan Sikh children were playing". Qaiser explained: "They are the caretakers of this Sikh gurdwara. They have moved here from Nankana Sahib." (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 117, folio 99)

In the ground beside the gurdwara stood a further structure, with buffaloes wallowing in the pools around it: "That was also a part of the gurdwara." It must have been a hall of some kind, the book says; the word that names the hall is uncertain in the printed text. The protective wall around the site is recent, built only a few years before his visit, though the line that says so is damaged (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 118, folio 100).

## Partition and After

Like most Sikh shrines in the western Punjab, Rori Sahib was left on the Pakistani side of the border when the subcontinent was divided in 1947, and its congregation departed in the great and grievous migration of that year. The building is reported to have been damaged by fire in the disorder that followed Partition, and for decades it stood largely neglected. In more recent years, with the easing of pilgrimage across the border and the growing attention paid to Sikh heritage in Pakistan, the shrine has again received visitors — Sikh *jathas* (pilgrim parties) travelling from India and from the worldwide diaspora — and efforts have been made toward its care and conservation.

Iqbal Qaiser remembered what it had been like: "Oh it was horrible. The structure was standing as it is but there [was] no protection. A bazaar for the sale of buffaloes used to be set up [at] the premises of the gurdwara." And now: "It was after my book was published that this gurdwara came into the limelight. Now it is fully restored. There are security officials here at all times and Sikh pilgrims [come] here every year." (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 118, folio 100)

Khalid records that the gurdwara's caretakers are Pathan Sikhs who moved there from Nankana Sahib (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 117, folio 99). Elsewhere in the same book, he lists Eminabad among the towns whose Sikh communities "Only recently" can "exert themselves in a way that was unthinkable earlier": going to university, and many of them owning cars (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 88, folio 70). The town's name is misprinted at that point in the book.

## Bhai Mardana

The gurdwara's second figure is the Guru's companion. Rizvi writes that at Sultanpur, while Nanak worked in Daulat Khan's commissariat, "There the minstrel, Bhai Mardana, a favourite friend of the Guru's, joined him", and that "Soon afterwards, accompanied by Bhai Mardana, Nanak left Sultanpur" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 409, folio 385). Rizvi gives Mardana no date of death, no tomb and no place of burial (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 409, folio 385; p. 410, folio 386).

Khalid calls him Nanak's "Muslim companion" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 16, folio not stated; p. 54, folio 36), and writes of "Bhai Bala and Bhai Mardana, a Hindu and a Muslim respectively and both companions of Nanak" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 95, folio 77). In *A White Trail* he explains the name: "Bhai being a title given to him due to the exalted status he enjoys in the religion and Mardana being his real name"; in paintings Mardana is shown "holding a rubab (a traditional stringed instrument)" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 160, folio not stated). Mardana's poetry is in the Guru Granth Sahib, which contains the "poetry of several non-Sikh saints including Baba Farid, Bhai Mardana and Bhagat Kabir" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 82, folio 64; p. 317, folio 291). *A White Trail* counts him among the "Muslim Sufis whose poetry has been included in the Sikh holy book", with Pipa Ji, Bheekhan and Sata Balwand (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 200, folio not stated).

Bhandari, writing in Persian, counts Mardana a minstrel among Baba Nanak's intimates: "اشعار این مقرب درگاه افریدگار درسرود و نغمه بائین دلفریب گفته مردم را در دام عقیدت می آورد" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). In his account of the Guru's life, "مردانه نام مطرب", a singer named Mardana, sang the Guru's verses and drew people "در دام عقیدت" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).

Much of what Khalid says of Mardana is, by his own account, reconstruction (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 17, folio not stated). In it Mardana is "a Muslim Mirasi (a community of genealogists and bards)", Nanak's best friend from Talwandi, who joined him at Sultanpur and "had ignited within Nanak a passion fo[r] music"; Nanak's family "regarded Mardana as an untouchable Mirasi" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 106, folio 88). He grew up travelling with his clan, singing the bravery of local chieftains for gifts, and knew "folk stories of Hindu deities and Muslim saints" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 120–121, folios 102–103). He worried that "music was un-Islamic, a criticism that came from the more puritanical sects of Islam" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 122, folio 104). As a younger man he "would roam the cities and villages of Punjab, singing folk songs and legends", Puran Bhagat's among the most popular (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 184, folio 158), and he wanted to see Puran Bhagat's temple at Sialkot "not due to his religious convictions but because of the memory associated with the legend" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 185, folio 159).

The friendship, in Khalid's telling, was close and unceremonious. Mardana regards Nanak "as his mentor and Guru", but the relationship "was not constrained by formalities", and he "could sometimes blackmail his friend" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 123, folio 105). "He had accepted Nanak as his eternal guru … For Nanak, Mardana was a friend and an equal, never a devotee" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 187, folio 161). He wore "a white chola", so that a shopkeeper took him for "a fakir or a dervish" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 187, folio 161). Sheikh Kamal calls him "a Mirasi bard" who "plays the rubab while Nanak sings" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 143, folio 125), and Nanak describes him as "born in the house of a Muslim Mirasi family" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 243, folio 217). Every night at Sultanpur Mardana played the *rubab* while Nanak sang, a practice that became a daily feature "for the rest of their [lives] and also for the Sikh community" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 106, folio 88; p. 107, folio 89).

The reconstruction follows him along the road. He weeps at a song he thinks "directed against him" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 125, folio 107). He notices "a lot of similarity between the symbolism of Nanak and Sheikh Farid" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 141, folio 123), and he goes with Nanak to Baba Farid's shrine (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 147–148, folios 129–130). At Kanganpur he sits "under a berry tree" tuning his *rubab*, and then he and Nanak are stoned (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 162–164, folios 144–146). At Sialkot Nanak sends him to Maula Karar to buy "two paisas worth of truth and two paisas worth of untruth" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 187–188, folios 161–162). At Deepalpur he hopes "they will have langar for us", worries over Nanak's "exhaustion and homesickness", and recoils from Nuri the leper (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 207–209, folios 181–183). Waiting on the mountain at Katas Raj, he resolves to stop travelling after "a long trip to the East and South", because "separation from family" had taken its toll (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 236–238, folios 210–211 / folio not stated). On the northern journey he feels "like a young man again", with "the soul of a wanderer" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 239, folio 213).

Dhillon's retelling gives Mardana the same place at the Guru's side. "Accompanied by his friend Mardana, the rabaab player", Nanak set out on his travels (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 53, folio 59; p. 54, folio 60). Mardana went with him east "all the way to Assam" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 109, folio not stated), and to Haridwar (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 78, folio 86), Prayag (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 102, folio 113), Lahore (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 126, folio 140), Sialkot (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 137–158), Multan (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 169–170, folios 185–186), Mecca, Medina and Baghdad (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 194, 196, 204, folios 214, 216, not stated), and "Tilla Balnath and Katasraj" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 224, folio 246). Dhillon gives him a "perpetual hunger" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 56, folio 62), feet "perpetually suffering from foot sores" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 84, folio 93), and a homesickness that sometimes overcame him: "Often he would sob into his pillow" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 110, folio 122). Turned away from house to house in an inhospitable village, he asks why two villages received opposite blessings (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 113–114, folios 125–126; pp. 120–121, folios 132–133). At Sialkot he is sent to the faqir, given two slips, and helped by Moola (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 151–158, folios 166–173). His parents settled at Kartarpur (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 176, folio 193), and he and Nanak "had spent a lifetime on the road" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 184, folio 203).

Other stories of Mardana are set elsewhere. Iqbal Qaiser tells one from Ghavindi, where Mardana, told not to beg, came back hungry from a family celebrating the birth of a son; the child "died by an act of God", and the family sought Nanak's forgiveness (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 56, folio 38). At the Ghavindi shrine are "two plinths meant to represent the seats of Guru Nanak and Bhai Mardana. Nanak's was higher than Mardana's" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 54, folio 36). Qaiser reads the Janamsakhis as stripping Nanak of "all his human characteristics" while leaving Mardana prey to human weakness, the two "probably used as symbols for religious and worldly life" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 56, folio 38). At Jhaman, at the other gurdwara of the same name, "It is believed that Nanak, along with his companion Mardana, rested here under a tree. Nanak made a rubab for his friend out of the branches" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 48, folio 30). In the legend of Panja Sahib, Mardana climbs three times to Wali Qandhari for water (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 269–270, folios 243–244; Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 159, folio not stated). Old shop paintings at Kanganpur bear text that "confirmed that they were of Guru Nanak and Bhai Mardana" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 164, folio 146).

Of his death the books give more than one account. Khalid writes that "Mardana had passed away in Baghdad while they were on the way back from the Hajj. Nanak did not have a fixed companion anymore" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 288, folio 262). Elsewhere he writes that "no grave marks the spot where Mardana was buried. According to one story, he passed away in Baghdad but there is another story which says that Mardana returned to Punjab with Nanak and died here in Kartarpur. It was, however, his last wish that no shrine be constructed over his grave"; he asks whether the grave beside Nanak's *samadhi* at Kartarpur may be "the grave of Bhai Mardana", and the no-shrine story "added later on" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 304, folio 278). Dhillon says only that Mardana, "whose multifaceted relationship with the Guru defied a label or a name", died before Nanak (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 257, folio 285).

## Devotion and Music

Khalid traces the sung worship of the gurdwaras to Mardana: "The tradition of kirtan comes from Guru Nanak's Muslim companion, Bhai Mardana. Ever since his time, his descendants as well as other Muslim kirtan performers have performed at Sikh Gurdwaras". That tradition "was disrupted at partition" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 218, folio not stated). Sikh hagiography has a favourite scene: "several paintings depicting a serene Nanak listening to the songs of Bhai Mardana holding a rubab ... while Bhai Bala stands behind them holding a fly-whisk" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 160, folio not stated).

Dhillon gives the music its moment in the stories. Mardana would "take up his rabaab and provide an accompaniment for the divinely inspired verses that Nanak sang", and the Guru's call to him was "Mardana, Bani aaye hai - I have inspiration for a Bani" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 228–229, folios 252–253). At the tomb in Baghdad Mardana tuned his *rabaab* and struck up the accompaniment to his master's singing (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 206, folio 227).

Bhandari describes the worship of Baba Nanak's followers in the same terms: "خلاصه عبادت اینطایفه مطالعه اشعار مرشد خویش است که بسرود و نغمه میگویند", the sum of their worship being their master's verses, which they sing (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 519). They count service to the traveller, "درخدمت صادر و وارد", as the highest worship, and whoever arrives at midnight speaking Baba Nanak's name they treat as a brother and a friend and serve, even a stranger, a thief, a highwayman or an evildoer (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 519). "It is believed that the tradition of Langar began with Guru Nanak", Khalid writes (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 188, folio not stated).

## In Verse

The poem most closely tied to Eminabad is the Guru's *Babur Bani*, which Khalid describes as "a long poem that Nanak wrote at the time the Mughal marauder Babur attacked Punjab, destroying the cities of Eminabad and Lahore on the way" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 15, folio not stated). It was "the first poem of Nanak that I read", Khalid writes. Through Iqbal Qaiser's recitation of it he "truly discovered Guru Nanak": in it Nanak "lays the blame of Babur's wrath at God's feet" and "challenges the divinity of the Divine" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 15–16, folio not stated). Khalid quotes the line that dates the invaders' coming:

*Coming in seventy-eight (1521 C.E)* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 113–114, folios 95–96)

The line goes on to their departure, in a year whose printed gloss is damaged (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 113–114, folios 95–96).

Khalid quotes the Guru's verse throughout *Walking with Nanak*, in published English translations. The lines below are not tied to Eminabad; they are the poetry of the Guru the shrine remembers, sung, as the books tell it, to Mardana's *rubab*. The *aarti*, which Iqbal Qaiser reads as placing God "in nature", begins in one translation:

*The sky the salver, the sun and moon the lamps …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 55, folio 37)

and in another:

*In the salver of the firmament, / The sun and the moon shine as lamps* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 243–244, folios 217–218)

On the oneness of God:

*My Master is One / He is One, brother, the only One* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 60, folio 42)

*God is One, / The Path is One* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 144, folio 126)

The poem on the sacred thread, the *janeu*, which in Khalid's reconstruction the boy Nanak refused:

*From the cotton of compassion, / Spin the thread of contentment* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 61–62, folios 43–44)

On washing:

*As hands or feet besmirched with slime, / Water washes white* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 192, folio 166)

The song Nanak sings, in Khalid's reconstruction, to the innkeepers who meant to rob him and Mardana:

*The herons in their white feathers dwell in the sacred shrines of pilgrimage* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 123–126, folios 105–108)

Other openings Khalid quotes: *Launch ye the boat of austere contemplation …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 148–149, folios 130–131); *As a beggar goes a-begging …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 158, folio 140); and *His goodness cannot be priced or traded …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 280, folio 254).

Bhandari, writing in Persian, says that the Guru composed "اشعار هندی" on the truth of God, setting out divine unity "بعبارات واضحه و استعارات لایحه" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). Shemeem Burney Abbas's glossary defines the *banī* by him: "Devotional verses such as those of Guru Nanak; verses of supplication, entreaty, humility, and reverence" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 179, folio not stated). The verse is sung outside the gurdwaras too. Abbas quotes the singer Abida Parvin: "I sing Shah Hussain, Bulle Shah, Mian Muhammad Bakhsh, Hazrat Khawaja Ghulam Farid, Sacchal Sarmast, Baba Farid, and Guru Nanak" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 54, folio not stated). In Sindh, Michel Boivin records, "Sain Vasan Shah became famous for reciting the banis of Shri Guru Nanak and the kalams of the Sufis" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 68, folio 55).

## Setting: Eminabad and Saidpur

What follows is about the town, not the gurdwara. Khalid gives the old name and the new: "When Babur took over the city of Saidpur, he renamed it Eminabad." (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 115, folio 97). On the next page he gives a legend of the name: a woman known as Eni offered Babur roasted grain when he had not eaten for days, shortly before he reached Saidpur, and the city was renamed "in her honour" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 116, folio 98). The reading of her name is uncertain in the printed text.

Khalid's Eminabad is a town of old temples and faded trade. "The cone like spires of ancient Hindu temples" rise among its houses, "beautiful, in contrast to the monolithic Muslim architecture that now dominates the city of Eminabad", and its dusty roads and worn houses showed that it had lost its economic strength. "Once, it was an important trading centre on the Grand Trunk Road that connected with Lahore and then Delhi"; the highway has since moved south, and Gujranwala, not far away, has replaced Eminabad as a trading centre (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 115, folio 97). Of one temple he writes: "Despite being abandoned in 1947, the temple still looked sturdy". Such temples had been taken over by migrants from across the border after the creation of Pakistan, and Khalid looked for signs of attack after the destruction of the Babri mosque in 1992 and "found none" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 115, folio 97). He "marvelled at how much of the historical architecture had stil[l] been preserved" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 116, folio 98). After their visit to Eminabad he and Qaiser went on to Sialkot (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 191, folio 165).

Khalid's reconstruction pictures Saidpur in Nanak's day. It had "magnificent houses and havelis" and huge temples crowned with gold tridents, with idols in every shop, Laxmi the most popular and then Ganesh (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 103, folio 85). "Saidpur was an ancient city", its temples said to have been built "thousands of years ago, long before the birth of Islam", and grown into complexes with rooms for pilgrims, leased shops, stables and "an army of servants" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 103, folio 85). There "the Brahmin pandits were the bosses", and guards kept out "naked and starving beggars" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 103–104, folios 85–86). It was a Hindu-majority city with a few Muslim households, its Hindus mostly traders and its Muslims aristocrats; "one of the most important trading centres between the Chenab and the Ravi", with "some of the tallest temples in the Punjab and also the most beautiful courtesans" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 104, folio 86). Malik Bhago lived in a "four-storey haveli known as the Shahi Qilla at Saidpur", as "a minister of the Rai of Saidpur", and held a wedding feast for "all Brahmins and members of the high castes" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 109, folio not stated).

Bhandari's Persian history names a town and *pargana* spelled "امن آباد". The book does not connect it with Guru Nanak or with any shrine, and the identification with Eminabad rests on the spelling alone. In one passage Bhandari writes of the creation of the *pargana*: "چند سال پیش از گجرات پرگنہ امن آباد مقرر گردید" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 176). In another, a man in Kashmir who posed as "عمر شیخ میرزا پسر سلیمان میرزا" was brought before the emperor "در منزل امن آباد", at the halt of that name, and was punished there (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 177). And he places Shah Daula's bridge over the Deg, on the highway, "پنجگروهی امن اباد جانب لاهور", five *kos* from it towards Lahore (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 515).

## Guru Nanak in the Wider Record

What the books say of Guru Nanak himself is said of the Guru, not of this site; it is gathered here because the shrine exists for his sake. Khalid gives his dates as 1469–1539 (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 314, folio 288), and so does Dhillon, who calls the Janamsakhis "cycles of tales purportedly drawn from the life of the first Guru of the Sikhs" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 9, folio 9). Dhillon has him born "On 14 April 1469" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 17–18, folios 19–20) and gives the date of his death as 22 September 1539 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 259, folio 287). Bhandari reports, with a "گویند", a birth "درسنه هزار و پانصد و بست و شش بکرماجیت مطابق هشتصد و هشتاد هجری درزمان سلطان بهلول لودی", and a death "درعهد سلطنت سلیم شاه افغان درعمر مابین هفتاد و هشتاد سالگی" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). Khalid writes that Nanak "spent a major portion of his life in present-day Pakistan. He was born here", and "eventually settled in a small village which is now on the Pakistani side of the border" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 16, folio not stated).

Dhillon sets out two schemes of the *udasis*. "According to the Puratan Janamsakhi… he undertook five journeys", the last in 1523–1524 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 54–55, folios 60–61); "More modern accounts" give four, spanning twenty-three years (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 55, folio 61). Dhillon himself holds that there were four (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 56, folio 62). Khalid gives the travels as about twenty-four years, mostly on foot (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 54, folio 36), and writes that Nanak and Mardana "would travel thousands of kilometres, that too on foot" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 108, folio 90). Nanak "preferred to stay on the outskirts" of villages, which Khalid calls "a recurrent theme" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 53, folio 35), and "Whenever he would visit an area he would visit its religious shrines, Muslim, Hindu and of any other denomination" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 37, folio 19).

Nanak's advice to his two companions, as Khalid gives it, was that "if one is a Muslim then one should be a good Muslim and if one is a Hindu then one should be a good Hindu" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 95, folio 77). Nanak "offended both Hindus and Muslims by stating that there was no Hindu or Muslim" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 95, folio 77). Dhillon has him say, after vanishing for a week in the stream at Sultanpur, "There is only one God. There is no Hindu, no Mussalman." (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 45–46, folios 50–51). Khalid writes that he associated with "people of lowercastes, terming them people of God", and criticised "high-caste landlords and government officials as exploiters" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 82, folio 64). Dhillon calls Nanak "a householder in every sense of the word" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 12, folio not stated), and in his summary the Guru taught that a good life rested on honest labour, compassion, selfless service and prayer (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 12–13, folio not stated / 13).

Several writers set the miracle stories against what they take to be the Guru's own view. Dhillon writes of "Guru Nanak's clear and emphatic disavowal of the miraculous" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 11, folio 11). Khalid writes that Nanak "abhorred the concept of associating miracles with religious personalities", yet "his biography is nothing but a story of his miracles" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 16, folio not stated), and that "His genius lies in the poetic use of words and not in miracles" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 199, folio 173).

Muslim writers and singers have claimed the Guru too. Khalid writes that "many Muslims, acquainted with the teachings of Guru Nanak, are devoted to him too and visit his shrine" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 220, folio not stated), and his informant Nadeem, a Muslim painter, says "Guru Nanak is like a Muslim Sufi Saint" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 219, folio not stated). A Muslim tradition, reported by Iqbal Qaiser, holds that "Guru Nanak was born by the blessing of Baba Naulakh Hazari"; "The Sikhs don't accept it" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated). Baba Farid "became an inspiration for Guru Nanak when he visited the shrine during his travels" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated), and Abbas writes that Farid's "texts are revered among the Sikhs because he influenced the founder of their own religion, Baba Guru Nanak" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 87, folio not stated). Robert Rozehnal reports that a twentieth-century Chishti Sabiri shaykh, in certain lectures, describes "non-Muslim spiritual luminaries—Krishna, Rama, Sita, Lachman, and Guru Nanak—as believers in the one true God and religious leaders with 'Divine connection' (nisbat)" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 63, folio 53). Omar Kasmani, citing Boivin, notes that the trope of the flower in milk is told also of "Guru Nanak, the spiritual leader and founder of Sikhism" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 195, folio 178, ch. 5 n. 17). At the shrine of the goddess Hinglaj, Jürgen Schaflechner records "the engraved footsteps of Guru Nanak" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 256, folio not stated).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **When Saidpur was sacked.** The site's account says the Guru witnessed the sack "around 1520–21". Rizvi dates Babur's third invasion, which reached as far as Sialkot, to 926/1520 (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 409, folio 385). The line of the *Babur Bani* that Khalid quotes glosses the invaders' coming as 1521 (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 113–114, folios 95–96). Dhillon gives no year (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 224, folio 246).
- **Why the shrine is called Rori Sahib.** The site's account says that the Guru rested here on a bed of pebbles, and that the shrine preserves the memory of the Guru seated in meditation on the pebbled ground. Iqbal Qaiser says it is "the place where he was picked up by the guards", a mound of pebbles where Nanak was sitting with Mardana "at the time of his arrest" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 118, folio 100).
- **Whether the capture is history or legend.** Rizvi states it as history: Nanak and Mardana "were taken captive" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 410, folio 386). Dhillon gives it as legend (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 224, folio 246). Khalid reports that the Janamsakhis record a meeting at Saidpur at which Nanak rebuked Babur (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 114, folio 96).
- **When the gurdwara was built.** The site's account says it was built during the reign of Maharaja Ranjit Singh, as a building of several storeys. Iqbal Qaiser says of it: "It was constructed around 1922-23. Before that, it was a small shrine. Only this gateway was constructed by Bhai Ram Singh"; Khalid found the main shrine "an ordinary room" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 117, folio 99).
- **Its state after 1947.** The site's account says the building was damaged by fire after Partition and long neglected. Qaiser says "The structure was standing as it is" but unprotected, with a buffalo market held on the premises, and that it is "now fully restored" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 118, folio 100).
- **The names of the town.** The site's account and Khalid write Eminabad and Saidpur (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 115, folio 97). Rizvi writes "Saiyidpur (Amanabad)" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 410, folio 386). Bhandari's Persian spells the town "امن آباد" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 176). Khalid gives two accounts of the renaming: by Babur on taking the city (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 115, folio 97), and in honour of a woman, Eni, who fed him (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 116, folio 98).
- **Where Mardana died.** Khalid writes in one place that Mardana died in Baghdad on the way back from the Hajj (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 288, folio 262), and in another that one story has him die in Baghdad and another at Kartarpur (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 304, folio 278). Dhillon says only that he died before Nanak (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 257, folio 285). Rizvi gives no death, tomb or burial (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 409, folio 385).
- **The Guru's dates.** The site's account, Khalid and Dhillon give 1469–1539, Dhillon with a birth on 14 April 1469 and a death on 22 September 1539 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 17–18, folios 19–20; p. 259, folio 287). Bhandari gives the birth in Samvat 1526, 880 Hijri, in the reign of Bahlul Lodi, and the death under Salim Shah Afghan, between the ages of seventy and eighty (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).
- **How many journeys.** Dhillon reports five *udasis* in the Puratan Janamsakhi and four in more modern accounts, and holds to four himself (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 54–55, folios 60–61; p. 56, folio 62). Khalid gives the travels as about twenty-four years (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 54, folio 36); Dhillon's modern scheme spans twenty-three (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 55, folio 61).

## Legacy

Gurdwara Rori Sahib endures as a quiet monument to the earliest chapter of the Sikh story, when Guru Nanak walked the roads of the Punjab teaching the oneness of God and the equality of all people. Its very name — a shrine of pebbles — carries the lesson of the tradition it commemorates: that holiness is found not in comfort or wealth but in humility and honest devotion. For the Sikhs of the world it remains a treasured link to their founder, and for the town of Eminabad it is the enduring sign of a visitor whose memory has outlasted empires.

It is also a shrine of the friendship between the Guru and his Muslim minstrel, who, in Qaiser's account, sat with him on this mound when the guards came (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 118, folio 100). Writing of Pakistan as a whole, Khalid says that "there are several sites all over the country where Nanak stayed or performed miracles. Gurdwaras that were built there later by the devotees still stand tall" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 168–169, folio not stated). For other such places after the division of the Punjab he is less hopeful: "I have seen several gurdwaras and temples being taken over and destroyed by the migrants" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated).
