## Overview

At Pakpattan, on the old bank of the Sutlej in southern Punjab, stands the shrine of Hazrat Baba Fariduddin Mas‘ud Ganjshakar (c. 1173–1266) — "Baba Farid," one of the founding saints of the Chishti order in South Asia and among the most beloved holy figures of the Punjab. A disciple and spiritual heir of Khwaja Qutbuddin Bakhtiar Kaki of Delhi, and the master of the great Nizamuddin Auliya, Baba Farid made the town then called Ajodhan into a centre of Sufi devotion so important that it came to be renamed Pakpattan, "the Holy Ferry." He was also a pioneer of Punjabi as a language of spiritual poetry, and verses attributed to him were later gathered into the Sikh scripture, the Guru Granth Sahib — a mark of how far his memory reaches across the communities of the Punjab. His shrine remains one of the greatest pilgrimage places of Pakistan, most of all at the annual celebration of the Bahishti Darwaza, the "Gate of Paradise."

The books bear out that standing from many sides. Richard Eaton gives two whole essays of his *Essays on Islam and Indian History* to this one shrine, "The Political and Religious Authority of the Shrine of Baba Farid" and "Court of Man, Court of God: Local Perceptions of the Shrine of Baba Farid, Pakpattan, Punjab" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 9), and calls it "perhaps the most famous such tomb-shrine in the Punjab" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 16). The devotees whose depositions he read saw it as "the supreme shrine of the Punjab, to which others were subordinate" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, pp. 241–242). Robert Rozehnal, whose *Islamic Sufism Unbound* opens with the shrine, calls Baba Farid "Pakistan's most famous Sufi saint" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 26, folio 15) and "the patron saint of Pakistan" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 215, folio 207), and writes that since Partition "Pakpattan now stands as the unrivaled center of a distinctly Pakistani Sufism" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 36, folio 26). Carl Ernst and Bruce Lawrence set the tomb beside Ajmer and Delhi on a pilgrimage itinerary (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 98, folio not stated), and Haroon Khalid, arriving in the town, found that "The shrine of Baba Farid is the focal point of the city of Pakpattan" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 154, folio not stated).

On the years of his birth and death, on his family, on how he came by his name and on much else, the sources do not agree. Their readings are given side by side in the course of the article and gathered under "Where the Sources Disagree" below.

## The Life of the Saint

Farid was born around 1173 at Kothewal, near Multan, into a family of learned émigrés of Central Asian origin; his grandfather is said to have come from Kabul in the upheavals of the age. His mother, remembered as a woman of deep piety, first set him on the path of prayer, and he received a thorough religious education at Multan, where a chance meeting with Khwaja Qutbuddin Bakhtiar Kaki, the Chishti master of Delhi, changed his life. He followed Bakhtiar Kaki to Delhi, entered his discipleship, and gave himself to the severe austerities for which he became famous — long fasts and the arduous devotions of the Chishti way, including the *chilla-e-ma‘kus*, the forty-day "inverted" prayer suspended head-down in a well. It is from the sweetness that his long fasts are said to have miraculously produced — a pebble or a morsel turning to sugar in his mouth — that he earned the title *Ganjshakar*, "the Treasure of Sugar."

On the death of his master he became the head (*qutb*) of the Chishti order and, seeking distance from the intrigues of the capital, settled at Ajodhan in the Punjab. There he lived in poverty and prayer, keeping an open *langar* though his own family often went hungry, teaching, and drawing to himself disciples who would carry the Chishti way across India — above all Nizamuddin Auliya of Delhi, through whom Farid’s spiritual line became the greatest in the subcontinent. He died at Ajodhan in 1266.

The year of his birth differs from book to book. Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori's *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, which gives him entry no. 43 of its Chishti chapter (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 16, folio 4), says that he was born in 583 AH at mauza Khotwal in the Multan district, and brought up from Multan (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 88, folio 76 [marker 86]). The edition's editor, in a footnote on the same page, calls a birth year of 582 AH entirely wrong, notes that *Sair al-Auliya* gives 569 AH and that *Fawaid al-Fawad*, which says Baba Sahib lived 93 years, gives 571 AH counted back, and concludes that *Fawaid al-Fawad* is to be preferred to *Sair al-Auliya*, so that 571 AH is the correct year of his birth (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 88, folio 76 [marker 86]). The readings of the three numerals in that footnote are uncertain in the printed text, and the author's own 583 is not among the years the editor weighs. Zahur ul-Hasan Sharib's *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind* prints two years and chooses neither: he adorned this world, it says, in 575 AH, and some have written his birth as 569 AH (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 56, folio 52). Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi writes that "Shaikh Farid was born in 569/1173-74 or 571/1175-76" and prefers neither (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 157, folio 139). Taufiq Rafat's selection of Bulleh Shah gives "(1175-1265)" in two places (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 25, folio not stated; Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 29, folio not stated) and "(1173-1265)" in two others (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 30, folio 10; Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 242, folio 222). Haroon Khalid writes that Baba Farid "was born in the year 1173 CE" at the village of Tibba Haji Deen, formerly Kothewal (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 150, folio 132), and Harish Dhillon gives his years as 1173–1266 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 171, folio 187).

The village of his birth is spelled three ways in Lahori's book: Khotwal in the author's sentence (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 88, folio 76 [marker 86]), a form perhaps to be read Kathwal, uncertain in the printed text, in the editor's footnote on the family (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 86–87, folios 74–75), and, in a later annotation, Kahtwal, of which it says that the correct name of the qasba is Kothewal (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 345, folio 333). That annotation names no saint itself; it refers to an article by Muhammad Aslam whose title, "Baba Farid's Ancestral Home", makes the link to Baba Farid (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 345, folio 333). Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* gives his birthplace as the qasba of Khotwal, near Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 526). At Tibba Haji Deen a shrine official told Khalid's guide, the historian Iqbal Qaiser, "This is Kothewal. Kothewal was its old name" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).

The family's origins are told at length, and contested. In Lahori's text his father, Jamal al-Din Sulaiman, said there to be a nephew of Sultan Mahmud Ghaznavi, came from Kabul to Lahore in the time of Sultan Shihab al-Din Ghori, resided for a time (the word uncertain in the printed text) in qasba Kasur, then went to Multan and married Qursum Khatun, daughter of Mulla Wajih al-Din Khujandi; three sons were born of her, Shaikh Azz al-Din Mahmud, Farid al-Din Masud and Najib al-Din Mutawakkil, of whom the text says that all three brothers were unique in their age (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 86–87, folios 74–75 [markers 84–85]). The ancestral chain is said to reach Sultan Ibrahim b. Adham through seventeen links and Umar ibn al-Khattab through thirty, and is printed in full (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 87, folio 75 [marker 85]). After Farrukh Shah, king of Kabul, died and the kings of Ghazni took Kabul, sovereignty left the family though its progeny stayed on; then, when Chingiz Khan overcame the Sultan of Khwarazm and calamity struck Kabul, his grandfather was martyred in the fighting, and Shaikh Jamal al-Din Sulaiman came away to Hindustan (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 88, folio 76 [marker 86]).

The edition's editor rejects almost all of this. The migration story transmitted in *Sair al-Auliya*, *Mirat al-Asrar*, *Maarij al-Wilayat* and *Sair al-Arifin* is not correct: Qazi Shuaib came to Hindustan before 1175 CE, about forty years before the Mongol occupation, indeed at the time of the Ghuzz invaders (the name uncertain in the printed text) in 552 AH / 1157 CE (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 87, folio 75 [marker 85]). Mulla Wajih al-Din Khujandi is mentioned by no contemporary tazkira-writer, his name appearing only in four later books; and the Mufti's transmitted genealogy, he says, is proved wrong, and a corrected chain is printed from *Sair al-Aqtab* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 87, folio 75). The editor adds that after Qazi Shuaib's death Shaikh Jamal al-Din Sulaiman was appointed qazi of a qasba between Multan and Ajodhan whose printed name, perhaps Kathwal, cannot be read with certainty, and which, the editor says, is now an ordinary qasba known as the Jhawli of the Mashaikh, that name also uncertain in the printed text; there he was buried (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 86–87, folios 74–75).

Sharib's account runs differently again. It traces his paternal genealogy to the Commander of the Faithful, Hazrat Umar ibn al-Khattab, and places him in the family of Farrukh Shah, king of Kabul; his highest ancestor was martyred in the battle of Kabul, and his grandfather, Hazrat Qazi Shuaib Faruq, came with three sons and his goods to Lahore, went from Lahore to Kasur, was appointed qazi of Kahtwal and settled there. His father was Shaikh Jamal al-Din Sulaiman; his mother Bibi Qursum Khatun, daughter of Maulana Wajih al-Din, whose nisba, perhaps Najandi, is uncertain in the printed text; his elder brother Azz al-Din Mahmud, the first name also uncertain in the printed text; his younger brother Hazrat Najib al-Din Mutawakkil, who was, Sharib says, his murid and khalifa; and his sister Hajira, famous by the title Jamila Khatun (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 56, folio 52). Rizvi writes that "Shaikh Faridu'd-Din's ancestors came from Kabul, but migrated to the Panjab in the middle of the twelfth century" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 156, folio 138), driven by "The orgy of Ghazna's destruction by 'Ala'u'd-Din Husain of Ghur in 1150-51, followed by the Ghuzz invasion six years later" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 156–157, folios 138–139). In his account it was "Baba Farid's father, Qazi Shu'aib" who "went to Lahore and later moved to Qasur, south-east of Lahore", and "The Ghaznawid Sultan of the Panjab made him the Qazi of Kahtwal, a town in Multan, between Maharan and Ajodhan"; there "Qazi Shu'aib married and had three sons, 'Izzu'd-Din Mahmud, Faridu'd-Din and Najibu'd-Din" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 157, folio 139). Bhandari names him the son of Jalal al-Din Sulaiman, of the lineage of Farrukh Shah of Kabul, the father's name printed there as Jalal al-Din rather than Jamal al-Din (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527).

His mother has an entry of her own in Lahori's chapter on righteous women, no. 194, headed with her name as Bibi Qursum Khatun, mother of Farid al-Din Ganj Shakar, which says of her that she was the gnostic of her age and one whose prayers were answered, and that whatever she said with her blessed tongue came to pass (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 250, folio 238). Rizvi, who never gives her name, writes that "The Shaikh's father was a scholar, but it was his mother, an exceedingly pious woman, who prayed nightly at lengthy vigils, who exerted the greater influence on the future sufi saint" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 157, folio 139). Annemarie Schimmel writes that he "had been influenced in his early religious life by his pious mother" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 377, folio 346), and names "Farid Ganj-i Shakar's mother in India" among the women of whom "there is no doubt that many elderly women in the families contributed to the spiritual formation of some of the great Sufi leaders" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 461, folio 430). In Thanvi's account as Ernst and Lawrence give it, his mother is Bibi Sharifa: "Bibi Sharifa became a widow at an early age and had no children besides Farid ad-Din. Until her dying breath, she continued to remember God, and was considered to be a great perfected saint" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 135, folio 122).

His brother Najib al-Din Mutawakkil appears in Lahori as Shaikh Farid al-Din's brother, whom the woman saint Bibi Fatima Sam used to call her own brother, and as the family's courier between Hansi and Ajodhan (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 250, folio 238); Rizvi calls him "Shaikh Najibu'd-Din Matwakkil (Trusting in God)" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 169, folio 151), and Nur Ahmad Chishti's *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* names the brother Shaikh Najib al-Din Mutawakkil (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 229). Of his own name Sharib writes that it was Masud; that Hazrat Farid al-Din Attar bestowed on him the name Farid al-Din; and that it is also said that Farid al-Din is a title granted from the divine court (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 57, folio 53).

In Sharib's telling his father died before he came to his senses. He was taught at home, memorised the Quran at twelve, and at fifteen came to Multan, where he read the famous book of fiqh *Nafi* with Maulana Minhaj al-Din Tirmizi and acquired the religious sciences; he then went to Qandahar, stayed five years, and attained high competence in tafsir, hadith, fiqh, sarf, nahw and logic (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 58, folio 54). He was sitting with his *Nafi* in a mosque in the Sarai Halwai quarter, where Minhaj al-Din Tirmizi gave his lessons, when Khwaja Qutb al-Din Bakhtiar Kaki came to Multan and entered the same mosque. After the prayer the Khwaja asked Masud what he was reading; the book *Nafi*, came the answer; the Khwaja asked whether he knew that his benefit would come from this *Nafi*; and the young man replied that his benefit would come from kissing the Khwaja's feet, and laid his head on the Khwaja's feet (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 58, folio 54). When the Khwaja left for Delhi he refused him leave to follow and ordered him to continue his studies, and the disciple went with him up to thirty stages, the numeral uncertain in the printed text (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 58–59, folios 54–55). Sharib dates the *bai'at* to 590 AH, when he was fifteen years old (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 59, folio 55). His studies done, he came to Delhi in 595 AH and lived in a *hujra* near the Ghazni gate (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 59, folio 55).

Rizvi gives the scene another age and doubts its place. "At the age of eighteen, Baba Farid settled in Multan to undertake further studies and joined a seminary at the mosque of Maulana Minhaju'd-Din Tirmizi. There he met Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki and asked to become his disciple. According to Jamali, Baba Farid was initiated by the Khwaja at Multan and was advised to complete his education there"; but "Jamali adds that the Baba then visited Qandahar to acquire further knowledge, but it is more likely that Baba Farid reached Delhi shortly after the Khwaja's arrival and was initiated into the distinguished group of sufis residing in the Khwaja's" *jama'at-khana* (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 157, folio 139). Bhandari has it that in Multan he met Khwaja Qutb al-Din Muhammad Bakhtiyar, went with him to Delhi and became his disciple; and that, some say, on the Khwaja's instruction he went from Multan to Qandahar and Sistan to study before rejoining him in Delhi (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 526). Ernst and Lawrence write that "The visit of Qutb ad-Din to Multan (a strategic commercial center in the Punjab) became the occasion" for his pledge of allegiance to the Chishti master, and give no year for it (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 80, folio 66).

Sharib sends him on long travels — to Iraq, Syria, Siwistan, Ghazni, Bukhara, Qandahar and a further place whose printed name cannot be read with certainty — and has him benefit from the company of masters whose list runs from Shaikh Shihab al-Din Umar Suhrawardi through Hazrat Shaikh Baha al-Din Zakariya to Shaikh Farid al-Din Muhammad Attar Nishapuri (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 59, folio 55). In Delhi, Rizvi writes, "Baba Farid lived in a small cell near the jama'at-khana and, under guidance, performed severe ascetic exercises. His austerities so impressed Khwaja Mu'inu'd-Din during his visit to Delhi, that he took a special interest in Baba Farid, prophesied his later fame and asked Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din to join him in prayer for his disciple's future greatness" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 157–158, folios 139–140). Lahori says that in renunciation, austerity, knowledge, practice, unveiling and miracle he outstripped everyone of the Chishti family, with perfect occupation in *zauq*, *shauq*, love, *wajd* and *sama* — and that his fast was perpetual and tree leaves his *iftar*, his days given to instructing the people and his nights to worship (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 86, folio 74 [marker 84]). Sharib quotes him, from the Urdu translation of *Rahat al-Qulub*, saying that for twenty years he stood in contemplation, never sitting, his feet swollen and bleeding; and that once he kept a *roza-e tai*, a fast unbroken by night, once did a *chilla-e ma'kus*, an inverted *chilla*, and once kept the *roza-e Da'udi*, the fast of David (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 60, folio 56). Ernst and Lawrence write that "His reputation for vigils, fasting, and penances extended back to his boyhood when he used to be regarded as" crazy for the severity of his observances, and that "Customarily he broke his fast by eating stale bread" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 81, folio 67).

The inverted *chilla* is described most fully by Rizvi. He asked "if he could perform a chilla which involved spiritual exercises and fasting for forty days. The Khwaja finally permitted him to perform a chilla-i ma'kus (inverted chilla)"; the details, obtained through Badru'd-Din Ghaznawi, were that "it required a man to tie a rope around his feet and remain suspended in a well, head down, for forty days and nights, while both fasting and praying. The Baba found a lonely mosque in Uch and, taking the mu'azzin into his confidence performed the chilla-i ma'kus" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 158, folio 140). The master's hesitation, Rizvi explains, "sprang from the fact that such devotional exercises gave the devotee unnecessary publicity" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 158, folio 140), and he later writes that "the first Indian sufi to perform it was Baba Farid" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 364, folio 342). Ernst and Lawrence's translation of the *Akhbar al-akhyar* locates it in the well of the Hajj mosque of Ucch: "For 40 days they would suspend him from a branch of the tree that overhung the well every night. At daybreak they would pull him out" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 168, folio 156). Scott Kugle writes that "He perpetuated in South Asia the Central Asian Sufi practice of the 'inverted prayer'", which "consisted of a spiritual retreat for prayer by hanging by one's feet from a rafter or in a dry well, remaining in an inverted state of contemplation from daybreak to sunset, often continued as an isolated retreat" for forty days in succession, and that "Though Farid al-Din never made this arduous exercise obligatory for his followers, it was integral to his own spiritual practice, and he saw it as crucial to forcefully dispel selfishness through such muscular ascetic practices" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 260, folio 239). Schimmel: "He performed extremely difficult ascetic practices, among them the chilla ma'kusa, hanging upside down in a well and performing the prescribed prayers and recollections for forty days" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 377, folio 346). Ernst and Lawrence also report Thanvi's caution: "Baba Farid was reputed to have practiced this unusual form of meditation. How could this be justified? Thanvi concludes that if it is performed as a practice stipulated for drawing a person closer to God then it is not allowable, but if it is done in order to cure some moral defect, then it may be permitted" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 135, folio 122).

From Delhi he went to Hansi. Lahori writes that after receiving the *khirqa* of *khilafat* from Khwaja Qutb al-Din Bakhtiar Kaki Aushi Dehlvi he settled at Hansi, then came to Ajodhan (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 88, folio 76 [marker 86]); and, from the side of the majzubs, that he left Delhi for Hansi for the sake of a majzub, Mian Saranga Hansvi, who wept that now the Hazrat sat on the seat of guidance, the crowds of people kept him even from the sight of him; the Hazrat was moved and, for his sake, left Delhi for Hansi; the majzub died in the year 646 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 236, folio 224). Rizvi writes: "After the Baba's fame in Delhi became an obstacle to prayer and meditation, he left for Hansi in the Hisar district. He was therefore absent from Delhi at the time of the Khwaja's death, arriving five days after the event" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 158, folio 140); and "It was mainly his deep affection for Shaikh Jamalu'd-Din which prompted Baba Farid to stay in Hansi for a period of twelve years. To Baba Shaikh Jamal was his jamal (beauty)" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 171, folio 153). Disciples of the Chishti Sabiri order told Rozehnal that "the saints Baba Farid Ganj-i Shakkar and 'Ali Ahmad Sabir both underwent twelve years of spiritual training before completing suluk" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 81, folio 71).

The inheritance he came to Delhi to receive is itemised in Sharib. Qutb al-Din Bakhtiar Kaki gave him his own prayer-mat and staff, and said that he would entrust Farid's *amanat*, the *sajjada*, the *dastar* and the *na'lain* that had reached him hand to hand from the pirs of Chisht, to Qazi Hamid al-Din Nagori, and that when Farid came from Hansi to his grave on the fifth day after his death, the Qazi would hand this trust of the pirs over to him. In accordance with that order Qazi Hamid al-Din Nagori and Badr al-Din Ghaznavi handed over all the *tabarrukat*; he wore his pir's *khirqa*, prayed two rak'ats on the *musalla* and stayed in his pir's house (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 59, folio 55). Rizvi: "In accordance with the Khwaja's will, Qazi Hamidu'd-Din Nagauri gave Baba Farid relics from the Khwaja, including his" *khirqa*, turban, stick and wooden sandals; "Although this implied that the Baba was the Khwaja's successor in Delhi, the Baba's asceticism and total withdrawal from the world precluded any conflict with Shaikh Badru'd-Din" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 158, folio 140) — and elsewhere: "His successor in Delhi was Shaikh Badru'd-Din, who came from Ghazna" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 156, folio 138). Bhandari says that at his death Khwaja Qutb al-Din ordered that the *khirqa*, the staff and whatever else had come to him from his pir be given to Farid, who came from Hansi to Delhi, took the trust, went back, and settled at Patan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 526). Schimmel writes that "because of the politically confused situation he left the capital and settled in the Punjab on the river Sutlej" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 377, folio 346).

## At Ajodhan

The town he chose is named by Sharib as Ajodhan, which nowadays is famous by the name of Pak Pattan (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 59, folio 55), and by the *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* as the place Ajodhan, the name uncertain in the printed text, now famous as Pak Pattan, where he hid himself from people among a folk who denied the dervishes, under a *karir* tree (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 229). Ernst and Lawrence's translation of the *Akhbar al-akhyar* says: "After wandering from city to city, he at last settled in Ajodhan. At that time its inhabitants were harsh-mannered people who worshiped the world and had no faith in dervishes" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 167, folio 155). Eaton has him arrive "sometime in the early thirteenth century" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 217), and, citing *Siyar al-auliya'*, writes that "Baba Farid resided in Ajudhan only sixteen or twenty-four years" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 228, n. 43). Rizvi, who calls the town only Ajodhan, has him there "from about 1236 until his death", and writes: "His long stay on the Satlaj, along one of the main routes from Multan to Lahore and Delhi, was a spiritually rewarding experience" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 158, folio 140). Kugle notes that "Shaykh Farid al-Din lived far from the major urban areas where other Chishti masters were increasingly settling" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, pp. 259–260, folios 238–239). In Haroon Khalid's words, he "had shifted the centre from Delhi to Ajodhan", believing the order "should distance itself from the imperial authority" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated); and "Baba Farid moved from Delhi to Pakpattan because he did not want to associate with the rulers there. His madrassa was similar to poorly funded public schools, where the students could not even afford a proper meal" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 282, folio 256).

His life there is drawn in close detail. Rizvi: "Shaikh Faridu'd-Din lived near the Jami' mosque, in a small house of mud walls covered with a thatched roof. He had strictly forbidden the use of burnt bricks for the buildings which, according to Chishti tradition, were not simple enough for an ascetic's dwelling. The door remained open until midnight as a welcome to visitors" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 159, folio 141). "Among his few possessions, the Baba had a small rug which he used by night as a blanket, but which hardly covered him. During the day it was used as a sitting rug. Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din's stick rested behind his head as a pillow" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 159, folio 141). "His food consisted of wild fruit and millet bread. Abstaining from nourishment during the day, in the evening he broke his fast by taking sherbet. A bowl of it would be brought to the Baba and often mixed with dried grapes. Of this he drank never more than half, dividing the rest among his visitors. After prayers, two pieces of bread smeared with ghee were taken to Baba Farid. One was given away, the other he himself ate, sharing it with his favourite disciples" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 159, folio 141). Sharib's account runs close to it: he broke his fast from one cup of sharbat with something in it, perhaps raisins, the word uncertain in the printed text, drinking a little and sharing out the rest; of two buttered loaves he ate one and broke the other for the company; when he first came to Ajodhan he and his dependants lived on *pilu* and *vela*[OCR?]; his murids carried the begging bowl round for years; there was no salt in the food; and such was his trust in God that whatever came in was spent (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 63, folio 59). His clothing was tattered, and his one blanket was so small that covering his feet uncovered his head (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 63, folio 59). Ernst and Lawrence's translation adds that "Outside the town there was a grove of karil trees. One was especially thick, and Farid ad-Din used to spend his time meditating under that tree", and that "It was there in Ajodhan that his children were born. Often they suffered pangs of starvation and underwent hardships" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 167, folio 155); Nizam ad-Din remembered that "That night when I ate delah or the fruit of the karil tree was like a festival to me; it was a gift of grace" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 167, folio 155). Ernst and Lawrence quote another writer's judgement that he "was forced by his circumstances to permit his disciples to circulate a begging bowl and collect food" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 20, folio 5).

Ajodhan did not welcome him. Sharib writes that the qazi of Ajodhan was his fierce opponent, but his opposition did not work and he went on guiding (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 59–60, folios 55–56). Rizvi: "Although he selected a lonely place to reside, Baba Farid was harassed by local officers of the Qazi of Ajodhan and by the rudeness of the people of the town. This was prompted mainly by the Qazi's hostility towards the sufi movement" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 158, folio 140); "On one occasion the Qazi requested the 'ulama' of Multan to issue a fatwa (legal decree) against anyone who lived in a mosque, was known as a dervish, listened to music or danced. The 'ulama' refused to comply, after discovering that such a fatwa would be used against the Baba" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 158–159, folios 140–141); "Nevertheless, official oppression continued, and an attempt was made on his life by a qalandar. At a later date, the Shaikh became convinced that a Muslim magician was employing witchcraft to kill him" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 159, folio 141). Sharib records that the son of Shihab al-Din the sorcerer worked magic on him and that he forgave him his offence (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 60, folio 56). Rizvi rejects a modern reading of the choice of Ajodhan: "The Baba did not, therefore, select Ajodhan in order to reform or convert 'backward Hindu tribes', as Nizami seeks to show, for it would appear that there was a great deal of work to be done among the Muslims of Ajodhan. Rather, through an example of sanctity and austerity, Baba Farid sought to reform the Muslims in the area of north-west India" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 159, folio 141).

Lahori tells of a land dispute that reached the royal governor. The saint bought a plot of land from a man, paying a price, for inclusion in the *khanqah*. Months later a second claimant filed suit at Dipalpur before the royal governor; the Hazrat was summoned in person or by proxy and sent an attendant, and witnesses were demanded to prove that the land had been the seller's property. Wearied, he said that this broken-neck should ask the land himself, and that if the land did not bear witness they would send witnesses. The governor came in person, mounted, and thousands of onlookers gathered (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 88–89, folios 76–77 [markers 86–87]); what followed is told under Legends and Miracles below.

Kings and their gifts he kept at a distance. Eaton writes that he "assiduously avoided contact with the mundane world of the court and its ministers", and quotes Barani's report of his warning to a Sufi leaving Ajudhan for Delhi: "Have nothing to do with maliks and amirs, and beware of their intimacy as dangerous; no darwesh ever kept up such intimacy, but in the end found it disastrous" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 219, n. 12). Ernst and Lawrence: "Farid ad-Din later refused land grants that were offered to him, and routinely gave away all money and other gifts brought to his lodge. As a consequence, his family is said to have literally faced starvation on numerous occasions" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 81, folio 67). Ernst and Lawrence note that he never performed the hajj: "Qutb ad-Din, Farid ad-Din, Nizam ad-Din, and Nasir ad-Din all failed to fulfill this critical obligation of Islam. It must be quickly noted, however, that they espoused poverty and, therefore, were not technically able to go on the pilgrimage" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 81, folio 67). Schimmel calls him the stern ascetic "who gave no thought to any worldly needs and refused all governmental grants for his family and his disciples" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 383, folio 352). Rizvi tells of one offer: "Ulugh Khan himself visited Shaikh Farid and offered a farman of four villages to the Shaikh and a cash payment for dervishes. The Shaikh returned the farman but accepted the money for distribution among the dervishes" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 162, folio 144). Sharib tells the same refusal of a different giver: Sultan Nasir al-Din visited him on his way to Uch and Multan and afterwards sent a *farman* for villages, their number uncertain in the printed text, and some cash; he returned the villages' deed and accepted the cash, spending it on the dervishes (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 61, folio 57). Rizvi, for his part, writes that "Ulugh Khan prevented Sultan Nasiru'd-Din from seeing Baba Farid in 650/1252-53 when the Sultan was marching towards Multan", and that "Ulugh Khan was too discreet to request the Shaikh to pray for his succession to the throne" but "paid close attention to the Shaikh's utterances for a propitious omen" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 162, folio 144). Sharib reports that it is said he gave the tidings of crown and throne to one whose printed name, perhaps Ulugh Khan, cannot be read with certainty, and that he did become king, as Ghiyath al-Din Balban (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 61–62, folios 57–58); and that Balban married his daughter, Bibi Nahirza, the name uncertain in the printed text, to him (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 61, folio 57). Rizvi writes that "Later Chishti records give an exaggerated account of the intimate relationship between Baba Farid and Sultan Balban (1266-87)", and of this story: "According to the Jawahar-i Faridi, after his accession Balban married his daughter to the Shaikh, but this is hardly historical as the Shaikh died before Ulugh Khan became Sultan Balban of the Delhi Sultanate" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 162, folio 144). Lahori's list of sources includes an essay by Muhammad Aslam asking whether any daughter of Sultan Balban, the name uncertain in the printed text, was betrothed to Baba Sahib (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 89–90, folios 77–78 [markers 87–88]). Asked for a letter to Sultan Ghiyas ad-Din Balban on a petitioner's behalf, he wrote, in Ernst and Lawrence's translation, "I entrust this man's fate to God and then to you. If you give him something, God is the giver but you will be thanked for it" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 170, folio 158); the *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* quotes a *ruq'a* of his to Sultan Ghiyath al-Din in Arabic with a translation (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 233).

Rizvi makes his refusal the saving of the order: "Baba Farid, through his total rejection of government patronage, averted the crisis threatening the Chishti order and succeeded in convincing his disciples that the wisest course was not to accept any form of land grant. Holiness could only be obtained, said the Baba, by ignoring princes"; yet "non-involvement in government service was not compulsory for ordinary disciples, and applied only to khalifas" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 244, folio 224). He records too that "The verses recited by Baba Farid before Ulugh Beg, reminded him of Firidun's previous rule of justice and advocated a high degree of tolerance" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 244, folio 224); that when an *'amil* asked him to intercede with the *wali* of Ajodhan, "Baba Farid sent a message to the wali requesting that he treat the 'amil kindly. However, the Shaikh was unsuccessful and the wali refused to heed his advice" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 163, folio 145); and that his last years were hard: "As a result of Sher Khan's hostility towards Baba Farid, merchants and wealthy supporters appear to have stopped sending gifts to the sufis at the jama'at-khana. This action impoverished Baba Farid, his family and other members of his religious community, during the last years of his life, and they all were reduced to near starvation" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 163, folio 145).

Yet the crowds came. "Baba Farid was tremendously popular at this time. The soldiers of Sultan Nasiru'd-Din's army also visited him. The large crowd of men concerned him, so he left his house. A sleeve from his shirt was hung from the roof of his dwelling facing the street. Crowds of people came to kiss the sleeve and in the process it was torn to shreds. Baba Farid went to the mosque and on his instructions his disciples formed a circle around him, so his devotees could not come too close. A farrash (menial servant) broke the circle of protectors and falling at the Shaikh's feet, kissed them" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 162–163, folios 144–145). Amir Khwurd's grandfather, Saiyid Muhammad bin Mahmud Kirmani, a merchant on the road between Kirman and Lahore, would call on him at Ajodhan, became his disciple, "finally settled in Ajodhan and for about eighteen years loyally served his pir" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 23, folio 9). In Eaton's account the saint already had two constituencies in his lifetime: the full-time residents of the convent, "initiated into the Chishti order, who lived a communal life of a strongly ascetic nature", and the masses who received amulets (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 217). Kugle notes "elliptical textual references to discussions between Shaykh Farid al-Din and some Hindu Yogis who visited his retreat in the Punjab", which he calls "these tantalizing fragments of evidence" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 264, folio 243); Rizvi writes that "The topics discussed at the jama 'at-khana gatherings of Baba Farid were of great interest to visiting Siddhas whose beliefs were founded on Hatha Yoga" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 345, folio 323).

He married and had a large family. Rizvi: "Having had two or three wives, the Baba's family was large. Some of his children died of starvation in infancy, but five sons and three daughters survived and outlived him" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 167, folio 149). Sharib names four wives — Bibi Nahirza, the daughter of Sultan Ghiyath al-Din Balban; Bibi Kulsum, mother of Shaikh Nasr Allah; Bibi Sharo; and Bibi Sakre, all but Kulsum's name uncertain in the printed text — and five sons and three daughters, with a further son, Shaikh Abd Allah, who died in childhood, the phrase uncertain in the printed text (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 61–62, folios 57–58). Schimmel writes that "He did not care for his wives or children, eight of whom survived" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 378, folio 347), and Ernst and Lawrence that "it is Farid ad-Din who poignantly attests to the tension peculiar to Muslim saints, the tension between bearing responsibility for one's family and abandoning all to God" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 81, folio 67). Rizvi names the sons: "The eldest, Nasiru'd-Din", who "lived as an agriculturalist"; "Baba's second son, Shihabu'd-Din"; "The third, Badru'd-Din Sulaiman" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 167, folio 149); "his fourth son, Nizamu'd-Din, who preferred the life of a soldier"; and "Baba Farid's fifth son, Shaikh Ya'qub, was a malamati and died in obscurity" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 168, folio 150). Of the soldier, "According to Amir Khwurd, Nizamu'd-Din was killed fighting against a Mongol army which had attacked Ajodhan. Later sources refer to his death during 'Ala'u'd-Din Khalji's invasion of Ranthambhor in 1301" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 168, folio 150). Khwaja Ya'qub is quoted on the *muwahhid*: "The muwahhid is he whose main concern is good action. Whatever he does aims at seeking divine grace. Water does not drown him and fire doesn't burn him" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 397, folio 373). "It was mainly due to Shaikh Badru'd-Din that the descendants of Baba Farid spread throughout all of India, and became a significant part of the sufi movement in the sub-continent" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 167, folio 149).

Of the daughters Rizvi writes that "Bibi Sharifa, who was widowed at a very early age and did not remarry, was the most pious", that "Her father often stated that if women could have become khalifas he would not have hesitated in making her one", and that "His third daughter was married to one of her father's disciples, Shaikh Badru'd-Din Ishaq" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 168, folio 150). Sharib names the daughters Bibi Mastura, Bibi Sharifa and Bibi Fatima (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 61–62, folios 57–58), and in his chapter on Sabir says that Baba Farid married his daughter Khadija Begum, known as Sharifa, to Sabir at his mother's insistence (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 80, folio 76). Ernst and Lawrence name "a prominent daughter of Farid ad-Din Ganj-i Shakar", Bibi Fatimah, through whose elder son the Faridi blood line runs, and write that "the descendants of Farid ad-Din Ganj-i Shakar (the Faridis) have shaped the continuation of Chishti identity" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 126, folio 113). Ernst and Lawrence, through Thanvi, attach a like saying to Bibi Sharifa the mother: "Her son, Baba Farid, used to say," that "If khilafat and sajjada [deputyship and successorship] could be given to a woman, then undoubtedly she was worthy of getting the khilafat" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 135, folio 122). So numerous did his line become, Eaton notes, "that even in the seventeenth century Baba Farid himself was called 'the Second Adam'" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 230, n. 51).

He died at Ajodhan in the month of Muharram. Ernst and Lawrence's translation tells that after the evening prayers in congregation he fell unconscious, asked on waking whether he had prayed, and said the prayers a second and then a third time, asking 'Who knows what may happen?'; at the end he cried out, 'O Living one, O Eternal one!', "and gave up his soul to God" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 169, folio 157). The *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* tells of the night prayer said three times and of the words *Ya Hayy, Ya Qayyum*, printed uncertainly, repeated thrice (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 232–233), and Sharib that he died saying "*Ya Hayy, Ya Qayyum*" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 62, folio 58). The year is given variously as 664, 663, 667, 670 and 634 AH and as 1265, 1266 and 1295 CE, as set out under "Where the Sources Disagree" below. Kugle, in a note on Sabir, gives the place where he died as "in Pakpattan in Pakistan", near which Sabir's cave retreat is preserved (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 331, folio 310, ch. 5 n. 30). Ernst and Lawrence give his age at death as 95 (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 169, folio 157); Eaton writes of "Baba Farid, who died at the age of ninety in A.D. 1265" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 241).

Rizvi tells how the grave came to lie inside the town. His fourth son Nizamu'd-Din "was with Balban's army in Patiali (approximately two hundred kilometres east of Delhi). When he reached Ajodhan, the coffin was being taken for burial outside the city walls. He suggested to his brothers that it be brought inside so that pilgrims could also receive spiritual guidance from members of Baba Farid's descendants. His advice was taken" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 168, folio 150). Sharib records how poor the house was at the end: when he died there was no money in the house for a shroud, and the door of the house was broken up and its bricks set in the grave (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 63, folio 59).

## Disciples and Succession

Rizvi calls Nizamuddin Auliya "Baba Farid's most outstanding khalifa" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 168, folio 150), and dates his discipleship closely: "In 655/1257-58 Nizamu'd-Din left Delhi for Ajodhan"; "Nizamu'd-Din was immediately initiated and his head shaved"; he made three visits in all, and "In Jumada I 663/February 1265, the Shaikh made his third and final visit to the Baba" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 174, folio 156); "He received his khilafat-nama from Baba Farid on 13 Ramazan 663/29 June 1265" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 175, folio 157). Baba Farid "offered him a bed in the jama'at-khana" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 174, folio 156). Schimmel writes that "After his third visit to Pakpattan, he was appointed khalifa in Delhi", and that Nizamuddin was Farid's outstanding khalifa, "whom he met in 1257" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 379, folio 348). Bhandari writes that Nizamuddin came to the qasba of Ajodhan at the age of twenty and became the disciple of Shaikh Farid al-Din Ganjshakar, and that having obtained the key to the treasury of inner meaning he was given leave for Delhi to guide the people (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 556). Kugle: "Near the end of his life, Shaykh Farid al-Din delegated his authority to a young follower", Nizam al-Din; "Farid al-Din charged him to become the embodiment of love, through whom the comforting shade of divine mercy would pour forth in the capital city of Delhi, infusing all corners of the Islamic empire in India" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 260, folio 239). Regula Burckhardt Qureshi's study of qawwali calls Baba Farid, in her glossary, "spiritual guide of Nizamuddin Auliya" (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 262, folio 242).

The training was severe. "The method by which Baba Farid trained Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya' to take up his position as successor in the order became engraved in the latter's memory" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 175, folio 157), and Baba Farid justified it: "the pir is the mashshata (hairdresser) of the murids (disciples)" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 176, folio 158). His parting words, in Rizvi: "'You will be a tree under whose shadow the people will find rest… you should strengthen your spirits by devotion…I have handed over all these things to you for at the time of my death you will not be present.'" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 175, folio 157). Lahori tells that after eating the cowpeas Nizam al-Din had bought by pawning his turban, he blessed the disciple's kitchen, saying that he had asked God that seventy-three maunds of salt be spent in his guest-house every day (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 95, folio 83 [marker 93]). Nizamuddin later found himself unable to abolish prostration before the pir, because of "the traditions followed by Shaikh Qutbu'd-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki and Shaikh Faridu'd-Din, who had both permitted prostration" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 239, folio 219). He drew up his own disciples' diplomas "according to a strict procedure that Farid ad-Din Ganj-i Shakar employed" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 194, folio 182).

How the tokens of succession reached Nizamuddin is told several ways. Kugle: "Upon his death in 1265, Farid al-Din sent his staff, sandals, and other tokens of his authority to Nizam al-Din in Delhi" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 260, folio 239). Sharib writes that when Nizam al-Din Auliya came to Ajodhan after his death, his son-in-law Badr al-Din Ishaq gave him the robe in accordance with his will (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 62, folio 58). Ernst and Lawrence's translation tells that "When Nizam ad-Din was laid in his grave, they covered his body with the cloak that he had received from Farid ad-Din and they put under his blessed head the prayer mat of the Shaykh" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 176, folio 164).

His khalifas are counted differently. Rizvi: "Later authorities give a long list of the khalifas of Baba Farid, but Amir Khwurd lists only seven. According to Baba himself, the khalifas were chosen in three different ways. The most outstanding were those whom God inspired the Shaikh to select. These were the Rahmani khalifas. The second category were those personally chosen by the Shaikh because of their own merits. The third group consisted of those disciples whom the Shaikh accepted as successors on the recommendation of others" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 168, folio 150). Schimmel: "Fariduddin invested seven khalifas with spiritual power; among his disciples, Jamal Hanswi and Nizamuddin must be counted as his special friends" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 379, folio 348). Sharib notes that some have written that his khalifas numbered more than fifty thousand (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 62, folio 58).

His deputies carried signed documents, countersealed at Hansi. Lahori writes that it was his custom that every khalifa sent to a country carried a *farman* over Farid's own signature, to be sealed by Khwaja Jamal al-Din Hansvi at Hansi before he set out (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 90, folio 78 [marker 88]). Sharib, in a chapter that places Baba Farid at Ajodhan and citing *Siyar al-Auliya*, has the same rule: if Shaikh Jamal sealed the *khilafat-nama* it was reckoned authentic, and if he did not, Baba Sahib would not accept it either, saying that what Jamal has torn we cannot stitch (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 84, folio 80). Rizvi explains the rule by a forgery threat: "The Baba took care that his khilafat-namas were quite accurate and not forged as a disciple whom he considered unworthy of receiving a khilafat-nama threatened the Baba that he would prepare a similar document and use it for enlisting his own disciples" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 166–167, folios 148–149); "Maulana Badru'd-Din Ishaq who prepared copies of the khilafat-nama was required to sign them, and they were later endorsed by the Baba's senior disciple, Shaikh Jamalu'd-Din, who lived at Hansi" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 167, folio 149). Badr al-Din Ishaq was also "the steward of his jama'at-khana" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 170, folio 152). Jamal al-Din had a copy of the *'Awarifu'l-Ma'arif* "from Baba Farid at the time of receiving his khilafat-nama", which passed down to his grandson through Nizamuddin (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 196, folio 178). In Jamal al-Din's house, Sharib relates, there was a *kaniz*, a chaste woman, whom Baba Farid addressed as Mother of the Faithful; asked by him what their Jamal was doing, she said that since meeting Baba Sahib he had wholly abandoned village, means, estate and the occupation of preaching, girding himself to starvation and labour, and Baba Sahib, delighted, gave praise to God and wished him joy (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 84–85, folios 80–81). To Jamal al-Din's minor son Burhan al-Din Sufi he gave the *khirqa* of *khilafat*, the *musalla* and the staff (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 85, folio 81). Bhandari records at Hansi the tomb of Shaikh Jamal al-Din, khalifa of Shaikh Farid al-Din Ganjshakar (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 554).

Sabir is told of in conflicting ways. In Lahori, Ala al-Din Ali Ahmad was given the title Sabir for twelve years' service in the kitchen without eating an unpermitted morsel, and was made the *qutb* of Delhi by a *farman* written for him (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 90, folio 78 [marker 88]); when Jamal al-Din tore up the first *farman*, Farid said that what Jamal has torn Farid cannot stitch, and later wrote Sabir a *farman* of custodianship, for Kaliyar in his own hand (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 91, folio 79 [marker 89]). Sharib's chapter on Sabir, which places Baba Farid at Ajodhan, says that he entrusted Sabir with distributing the *langar*; that over twelve years he ate nothing of it; and that when Baba Farid learned this he said that Ala al-Din Ahmad was *sabir*, patient, and the title stuck (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 78, folio 74). Sabir had come to Ajodhan with his mother and lived with his *mamun*, his maternal uncle, Baba Farid (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 78, folio 74). Entrusted with the *wilayat* of Delhi, with the instruction to have his certificate sealed at Hansi first (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 78, folio 74), Sabir rode into Jamal al-Din's *khanqah* at Hansi still mounted on his palanquin (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 78–79, folios 74–75); Jamal al-Din tore the certificate up and did not seal it (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 79, folio 75); at Ajodhan Baba Farid said that what Jamal has torn Farid cannot stitch, and then entrusted him with the *wilayat* of Kaliyar, signed the deed himself, and this time did not send him to Jamal al-Din (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 79–80, folios 75–76). Kugle tells it otherwise: Sabir's "ability to channel this force into performing miracles was so audacious that Shaykh Farid al-Din was wary of granting him leadership of the community and exposing the wider public to his spiritual heat. Instead, he sent the young Shaykh Sabir out to the wilderness" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 260, folio 239). He calls Sabir "a follower and nephew of Shaykh Farid al-Din 'Ganj-i Shakar'" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 259, folio 238), and reports that Sabir's followers "regarded him as the true inheritor of Shaykh Farid al-Din's saintly authority and the only one of his followers to adhere closely to the saint's rigorous concentration on bodily control" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 261, folio 240). Rizvi writes that in later sources "the sufi whose khilafat-nama, Shaikh Jamalu'd-Din had torn was Sabir, and he was also depicted as the nephew of Baba Farid", and calls these stories legendary (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 172, folio 154). Ernst and Lawrence report that Shaykh 'Ali ibn Ahmad Sabir "is said to have been the Shaykh 'Ali Sabir who is briefly mentioned in Siyar al-awliya' as a disciple of Shaykh Farid ad-Din Ganj-i Shakar. No less an authority than Shaykh 'Abd al-Haqq, however, questions the conflation of the two names and persons" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 74, folio 60).

Through these two the order divided. "The centuries-old division between the two branches stems from the time of Farid ad-Din Ganj-i Shakar, and it is his two disciples 'Ala' ad-Din 'Ali Sabir and Nizam ad-Din Awliya', after whom these two branches are named" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 131, folio 118); "Both the major sublineages of the Chishtis derive from him" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 167, folio 155). Kugle: "not all Chishtis were followers of Nizam al-Din, even if the majority were, for Nizam al-Din was not the only follower of the passionate and ascetic Shaykh Farid al-Din" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 260, folio 239). In Qureshi's table of the Chishti hierarchy, Baba Farid stands below Qutbuddin Bakhtiyar-e-Kaki and above the pair Ala'uddin Sabir and Nizamuddin Auliya (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 101, folio 81); in the Chishti Sabiri *shajara* that Rozehnal prints, "19. Farid ad-Din Mas'ud Ganj-i Shakkar (d. 1265)" is followed by 'Ala ad-Din 'Ali Ahmad Sabir, "eponymous founder of the Chishti Sabiri order" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 15, folio 4). Ernst and Lawrence count him the third of "the five human pillars on whom the Indian extension of the Chishti order was built" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 80, folio 66), one of "the three Chishti giants, Farid ad-Din, Nizam ad-Din, and Nasir ad-Din, who complete the first cycle of the Chishtiyya" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 183, folio 171), and write that "Shaykh Farid was in the first generation of Indian-born masters of the Chishti order" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 111, folio 98).

Other disciples pass through the books. Shams al-Din Turk was told at his leave-taking to settle at Panipat because its *wilayat* had been entrusted to him; and Baba Farid declared his own *khilafat* insufficient for him, saying that his attaining grace and perfection depended on another guide, and sent him to Sabir at Kaliyar (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 92, folio 88). Sabir sent away Hasan the qawwal with a few gular figs, which he brought to Baba Sahib at Ajodhan; Baba Sahib shared the figs among those present, and whoever ate of them was rid of his grossness and gained inner light (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 82–83, folios 78–79). Lahori records that a grandson, Shaikh Ala al-Din, received the Hindu Sohan Majzub, called Ajodhani Pak Pattani, into Islam (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 236, folio 224), and that Shah Kaku Chishti of Lahore was of the progeny of Hazrat Khwaja Farid al-Din Ganj Shakar (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 99–100, folios 87–88 [markers 97–98]). Generations later, Burhan ad-Din Gharib asked of Nizam ad-Din "the gift of being under the direct spiritual observation (nazar) of Farid ad-Din Ganj-i Shakar" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 192, folio 180).

## Teaching and Poetry

Baba Farid’s teaching was the pure Chishti gospel of love: of God to be sought through humility, poverty (*faqr*), patient endurance, and above all a boundless compassion that returned kindness for cruelty. He is remembered for counselling his disciples to answer harshness with gentleness — to give those who bring thorns the softest of replies. What sets him apart in the history of the Punjab is that he expressed this vision not only in the Persian of the learned but in the language of the common people, and he is honoured as one of the earliest and greatest poets of Punjabi. His *shlokas* (couplets) have such authority that the Sikh Gurus included a body of them in the Guru Granth Sahib. Their tone is unmistakable — the ache of the soul for God, and the memory of death that makes the world small:

کاگا سب تن کھائیو، چُن چُن کھائیو ماس
دوے نیناں مت کھائیو، مینوں پِیا ملن دی آس

*"O crow, eat all my flesh, pick and eat it piece by piece — but do not eat these two eyes, for I still long to see my Beloved."*

فریدا جے تُوں عقل لطیف ہیں، کالے لِکھ نہ لیکھ
آپنڑے گریوان مہ سِر نیواں کر ویکھ

*"Farid, if you are of subtle wit, write not against another’s account; bow your head into your own collar, and look there instead."*

Rizvi gives the teaching in his own summary: "To Baba Farid, sufism was a strenuous exercise leading one to a pious life within the society in which one lived. It demanded humility, modesty, patience, fortitude and a cleansing of the heart from all conceit. Self-abnegation involved careful concern for any offence to another, and in the event of such a situation, sincere and copious apologies were demanded" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 164, folio 146). "Five hundred aphorisms of Baba Farid were collected", and Rizvi prints sixty of those Amir Khwurd selected, which "give some insight into the Baba's broadly based, humanitarian teachings" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 164, folio 146). Among them is a parable of renunciation: "The zakat of Sharia'a [OCR?] [Shari'a] was five dirhams out of 200, the zakat of Tariga [Tariqa] involved the payment of 195 dirhams out of 200 and the retention of only five dirhams and the zakat of Hagiqa [Haqiqa] entailed the payment of everything, while retaining nothing" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 164, folio 146). "He encouraged education in his disciples and considered the 'ulama' to be nobler than the common people, though he disliked their self-satisfied indifference to others"; the *faqirs*, he asserted, were superior to the *'ulama'* "and occupied the same place among them as the full moon amongst a constellation of stars" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 164, folio 146). The *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* quotes the saying in Arabic, that the 'ulama are the noblest of people and the faqirs the noblest of the noble, and that the faqir among the 'ulama is like the full moon among the stars, together with another, that harm lies in contrivance and safety in submission (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 233). "Fasting was the first half of the way along the sufi path, said the Baba, and prayers and pilgrimages the other" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 174, folio 156).

Asked whether to give up study for prayer, he answered, in Rizvi's paraphrase, that the disciple should "continue both, as the two were necessary for the life of a dervish and he would then see which would emerge as the most dominant" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 174, folio 156); in Ernst and Lawrence's translation, "Do both till one predominates" — though they warn at once that "This anecdote is not found in Fawa'id al-fu'ad. It may or may not be true" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 87, folio 73). Sharib sets out his teachings at length: six kinds of *tauba*, of heart and tongue, eye, ear, hand, foot and *nafs*; that a dervish who seeks the eminence of this base world is no dervish but one who defames dervishes and an apostate from the *tariqa*; that love has a hundred stations, the first being patience under affliction from the Friend; that the *kulah* may not be worn until one guards the four worlds of eye, ear, tongue, and hand and foot; and four questions put to seven hundred pirs of the *tariqa*, all of whom answered alike — the wisest is he who abandons sins, the most alert he whom nothing troubles, the richest the contented, the neediest he who abandons contentment (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 64–65, folios 60–61). Among his sayings Sharib prints one that calls *tasawwuf* the name for pure friendship with the Lord, one word uncertain in the printed text (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 65, folio 61), and calls his teachings a priceless treasury of *tasawwuf* (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 64, folio 60).

His prayers became remedies. Sharib gives *aurad* and *waza'if* in his name: Sura Baqara recited between the *sunnats* and *farz* of the dawn prayer for three days fulfils a purpose; Sura An'am recited seventy times fulfils a need; the words declaring that there is no god but You, glorifying You and confessing oneself among the wrongdoers free one from grief and worry; and frequent recitation of a prayer that calls on God, in the name of the Merciful, as everlasting in might, dominion and permanence, possessor of majesty, generosity, bounty and giving, loving, lord of the glorious Throne, and doer of whatever He wills, removes straitness in livelihood (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 66, folio 62). Ernst and Lawrence write that "Farid ad-Din Ganj-i Shakar is the source of specific zikr formulas, including some in Indian languages" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 45, folio 31), and quote one, from Awrangabadi: "Say *wuhi hi* upwards, *hi hi* to the left side of the breast, *hin hi* toward the heart." These phrases, they add, "belong to a long tradition of adapting" hatha yoga mantras to Islamic themes and Sufi practices (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 47, folio 33). Nizamuddin traced his own amulet-writing to him, recalling "the fact that Shaikh Faridu'd-Din once gave me a pot of ink and ordered me to write amulets for the people" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 193, folio 175), and Farid had himself asked his master about amulets, receiving the answer that "the fulfilment of desires belonged to no-one; the amulets contained God's name and His words, and could be given to the people" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 154–155, folios 136–137).

He loved *sama'*. Sharib writes that he was very fond of *sama'* (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 62–63, folios 58–59), and gives his saying that *sama'* is the heart's ease and sets in motion the people of love who swim in the sea of love (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 65, folio 61). Asked about its lawfulness, "a topic disputed among the 'ulama", he replied, in Ernst and Lawrence's translation, "Praise be to God!" and then: "One group has been burnt to ashes, while the other is still arguing" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 170, folio 158). Ashraf Jahangir Simnani counted him among the Indian shaykhs who favoured music and "experienced empathetic ecstasy and danced" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 57, folio 43). Once, Rizvi writes, "the Baba wished to partake of sama' although no musician was present", and had the letters of Qazi Hamidu'd-Din Nagauri read to him instead, falling at once into an ecstatic swoon (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 216, folio 198). Usborne's annotator to Waris Shah writes that "The Chishtiyya sect of Sufis, which counts Fariduddin Shakarganj among its leaders, regards music (Sama) as a permissible means of experiencing spiritual exaltation" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 19, folio 18, note 1 to Ch. 12).

Whether he wrote books is disputed. Sharib calls *Fawaid al-Salikin* his famous book, in which he collected his pir's *malfuzat*, and says of the *risalas* *Maujud Wujud*, *Guftar* and *Ilahi Nama* that they too are said to be his (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 63, folio 59). Lahori's editor declares *Fawaid al-Salikin*, *Asrar al-Auliya* and *Rahat al-Qulub* — the first the malfuzat of Qutb al-Din Bakhtiar Kaki said to be compiled by Baba Sahib, the second compiled by Shaikh Badr al-Din Ishaq, the third by Khwaja Nizam al-Din Auliya — all merely fabricated and falsely attributed (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 89, folio 77 [marker 87]). Rizvi lists the same three among works "embroidered by anonymous authors who were bereft of either a feeling for history or a first-hand knowledge of the lives of their heroes" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 18, folio 4). Ernst and Lawrence translate Nizam ad-Din's testimony that neither Farid ad-Din nor Qutb ad-Din nor any Chishti saint before them wrote a book (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 178, folio 166), record that "Some of the sayings [malfuzat] of Shaykh Farid ad-Din Ganj-i Shakar have been recorded by Shaykh Nizam ad-Din Awliya in a letter" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 169, folio 157), and list two works only attributed to him, the *Ganj-i asrar* and the *Risala-i 'irfani* (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 241, folio 231). Rizvi says Nizamuddin had tried to write down what he heard from Baba Farid and had found himself unable to write a word (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 19, folio 5).

Most of the books call him a poet, though in which tongues, and how surely, is argued. Sharib writes that he was also a poet (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 63, folio 59). Rizvi: "Baba Farid was himself a scholar and wrote excellent poetry in Arabic, Persian and the local Hindawi dialect. He recited the Qur'an perfectly, popularized the study of the" *'Awarifu'l-Ma'arif* and lectured on the *Lawa'ih* of Qazi Hamidu'd-Din Nagauri (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 164, folio 146). "Most of the Hindawi songs recited at sama' gatherings held during this period have been lost, but a few verses that have survived have been ascribed to Shaikh Hamidu'd-Din Nagauri and Baba Farid" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 349, folio 327); "The Siyaru'l-Auliya' quotes a verse composed by Baba Farid in the Multani dialect whose precise meaning is difficult to decipher" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 350, folio 328). Haroon Khalid writes that "Baba Farid is considered to be the first poet of the Punjabi language", who "wrote for the people in a language that they could understand" when the elite used Persian and Arabic, and who "first uses the symbolism of a wife being abandoned by her husband" for devotee and deity (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 153, folio 135); and, again, "Perhaps the greatest contribution of Baba Farid is not in religion but in literature. He is believed to be the first poet of Punjabi" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated). Rafat calls him "The 12th century poet Baba Farid, the first of the Punjabi language extant" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 47, folio 27), and writes that "Baba Farid's slokes are called kafis" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 49, folio 29). Dhillon quotes the judgement that "Farid laid the basis for a vernacular Punjabi literature" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 172, folio 188). Shemeem Burney Abbas reports that he "is said to be the creator of the kāfī form in which the human soul is treated as a female and contextualized in the imagery of the medieval institution of marriage" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 148, folio not stated). Schimmel is more careful: "In the Panjab, popular mystical poetry may have emerged as early as in the days of Fariduddin Shakarganj, the Chishti saint of Pakpattan" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 16, folio 12); "Fariduddin Ganj-i Shakar of Pakpattan is credited with having used a kind of Old Panjabi for his mystical songs" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 415, folio 384); and "It is possible that Fariduddin composed a few lines of poetry in the local dialect, which would place him in the chain of those mystics who helped disseminate Sufi teachings in popular songs, thus influencing the population, particularly the women, who used to sing these simple verses while doing their daily work" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 379, folio 348).

Rizvi gives the sense of two of his *dohas*, preserved with a Persian rendering in Mir 'Abdu'l-Wahid Bilgarami's *Saba'-Sanabil*; both attack false initiation and outward asceticism (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 350, folio 328):

*You give a cap as a mark of initiation, and this indicates that you are presumptuous* (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 350, folio 328)

*You shave your head but what you should do is to cut lust from the heart* (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 350, folio 328)

Of the *shlokas* in the Guru Granth Sahib the sources tell different histories. Rafat writes that he "would have been without trace had not the Sikh saint Guru Nanak taken his slokes from a descendant of the poet two centuries later. Farid is a poet of the Granth and has survived with the sacred book of the Sikh religion" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 47, folio 27); and, in a note: "Guru Nanak (1469-1538) collected the great first poet's work from his heirs and wrote it down in his note-books", and "Guru Arjun (1553-1606) put together the first copy of the Holy Granth out of these notebooks and thus secured Baba Farid from obscurity. Today he is the most important poet of the Granth, with more slokes than any other selected poet" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 242, folio 222). In Dhillon's retelling Nanak "spent many days there holding discussions with him", and "Later, he personally included 134 hymns composed by Baba Farid in the Adi Granth" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 171, folio 187). Khalid writes that the scribe of Sheikh Ibrahim, Farid's descendant, compiled "all the poems of Baba Farid" and handed them to Nanak, and that "It was through this channel that the poems of Baba Farid found their place in the Guru Granth Sahib" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 149, folio 131); in *A White Trail* he has Nanak go "to Pak Pattan, the city of Baba Fareed, to collect his poetry from his descendants", the poems later included in the Granth by Guru Arjan (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 200, folio not stated). Abbas writes that "To this day some of Shaikh Fariduddin Ganj-e Shakar's" best Panjabi poetry is preserved in the Gurmukhi script in the sacred texts of Sikhism (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 49, folio not stated). Rizvi doubts the authorship itself: "Controversy surrounds more than a hundred" *slokas* ascribed to Baba Farid Ganj-i Shakar in the Guru Granth, compiled by the fifth Sikh Guru, Arjan, in 1604 (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 350, folio 328); "Some scholars assert they were composed by Baba Farid himself but the language indicates they were the work of his successors who may have rewritten some of Baba Farid's original slokas into a more intelligible Multani. Some of these slokas are even ascribed to Kabir" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 350–351, folios 328–329); and his own conclusion is that "A careful analysis of Baba Farid's slokas in the Guru Granth would tend to suggest they were not composed by one individual. Therefore it is wrong to ascribe them either to Shaikh Ibrahim or another of Baba Farid's descendants, known as Farid Sani. They represent the teachings of Baba Farid through the years from his own time to the fifteenth century and were therefore composed by a number of different descendants, all using Farid as their nom de plume" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 351, folio 329).

His voice lives on in later poets. Rafat writes that "The tradition already existed in relation to Baba Farid and a number of Sikh gurus wrote poetry using 'Farida' as vocative" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 240, folio 220), and that "Bulleh Shah does not mention either Rumi or Baba Farid, classics in whose technical and intellectual traditions he was steeped. Two of his Kafis are exact quotations from their work" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 37, folio 17). He finds in Farid and Bulleh Shah the same confessional vein, "so pronounced in Baba Farid and Bulleh Shah" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 30, folio 10), and notes that East Punjab's Sikh scholars passed over Bulleh Shah "in favour of Baba Farid and Hashim Shah, both integrally related to their religion and politics" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 51, folio 31). Rozehnal heard his own Persian verse sung at the *'urs*: "The three hour evening sama' session was filled with ancient Hindi songs of Amir Khusrau and the Persian songs of Hazrat Baba Farid himself" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 232, folio 224). In a scene Khalid reconstructs, qawwals sing what he calls "Sheikh Farid's most popular qawwali" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 138, folio 120):

*I did not sleep with my love tonight / And every bit of my body aches* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 138, folio 120)

## The Shrine

Baba Farid’s tomb at Pakpattan grew, over the centuries, into a great shrine that has been honoured by every power to rule the Punjab — sultans, Mughals, and later rulers alike — and it remains among the most visited darbars in Pakistan. Its custodianship passed to a line of hereditary keepers, the Diwans of Pakpattan, descended from the saint’s family.

The older Urdu and Persian books name the tomb in a line. Lahori writes that his illuminated *rauza* at Pak Pattan is a place of pilgrimage; a Persian chronogram follows, both of its couplets ending on the same words, which name Farid a peerless one (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 89, folio 77 [marker 87]). Sharib writes that his radiant mazar at Pak Pattan is a fountainhead of graces and blessings (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 62, folio 58). Bhandari places the tomb in the city of Patan alias Ajodhan, sarkar Dipalpur, east of Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527). The *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* says that the Hazrat's radiant mazar, the word for radiant uncertain in the printed text, stands at Pak Pattan (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 233). Qureshi's table of the Chishti order gives him "d. 1266, Pakpattan" (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 101, folio 81), and Usborne's annotator to Waris Shah writes that "Shakarganj is the saint of Pakpattan" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 57, folio 56) and that Baba Farid "honoured it by making it his home and his place of burial" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 2, folio 1). Ernst and Lawrence name the town of the tomb seven times, as "Ajodhan (modern Pakpattan)" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 105, folio 92) and as "Pakpattan/Ajodhan" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 99, folio 86), but describe no building.

Eaton dates the complex from the death. "In 1265 Baba Farid died, and with his death began the career of the vast shrine complex based on his tomb, in time encompassing a mosque, a welfare kitchen", the *langar khana*, and a number of related buildings (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 218). When the saint came, Eaton writes, "a Jami mosque had already been established there", with a resident qadi and a Muslim governor at nearby Dipalpur (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 217), and the mosque, "now part of the shrine complex", by its large size "attests to the presence of a substantial Muslim population at that time" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 223). The grandest building is not the saint's own: "soon after 'Ala' ad-Din's death in 1335", the grandson's, "Sultan Muhammad commissioned two engineers to construct what proved to be one of the finest examples of Tughluq architecture in the subcontinent. It is also the most imposing structure in the entire shrine complex, dwarfing by far Baba Farid's own tomb" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 221); its date "comes from an inscription on the tomb itself—Safar, 737, which corresponds to Sept./Oct., A.D. 1336" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 221, n. 19). In his second essay Eaton describes the shrine itself as "a magnificent marble edifice constructed in the fourteenth century" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 239), and the colonial court at Montgomery as some 25 miles down a dusty road "from the hallowed marble domes of Pakpattan" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 257); he does not reconcile the two descriptions.

Nur Ahmad Chishti, who had himself been there some twenty years before he wrote, the number uncertain in the printed text, describes the building as of middling height, the words uncertain in the printed text, lower than the ground of the town, with two doors (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 233). One door, now called by a name that cannot be read with certainty in the printed text, he explains by a dream of Nizam-ud-Din's in which the Prophet said that whoever entered it would be safe; lakhs upon lakhs of people came there, as the author saw for himself, and the crowd was kept in order by beating; devotees came from all the land of Hindustan, the Punjab and elsewhere (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 234).

Haroon Khalid's two books describe the shrine as he saw it. "The shrine of Baba Farid is located on the top of a mound that overlooks the entire city"; "The madrassah of Baba Farid was located on the mound where he is buried"; "It seems as if the entire city of Pakpattan has developed around this shrine. All roads lead here. The name of every other shop or business entity either includes Baba Farid or his title Ganj Shakar" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated). Stairs lead up to "an open courtyard with a new mosque on one side and the shrine of Baba Farid in front", with "a huge line of men" waiting; "This was a modest building". "Behind the shrine is a splendid structure rising several metres higher. This is where the progeny of Baba Farid is buried. The graves of his female family members are covered with purdah. Here too women are not allowed". "Facing the shrine is an ancient Karir tree", and "Behind the qawwals was the langar hall, offering free food to all" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated). In *Walking with Nanak* the shrine is the highest point of the area; it was "an abandoned mound, probably carrying within it the secrets of an ancient city, when Farid moved here with his students. Now it is a shrine spread over a vast tract of land", and the shops on the approach are named after Baba Farid or "his title Shakarganj" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 154, folio not stated); "the shrine of Sheikh Farid at Pakpattan, one of the biggest centres of the Sufi Chistiyan school of thought in all of South Asia" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 143, folio 125). In his reconstruction of the town in Nanak's day the shrine "was in one corner" of a courtyard, beside a tomb "which contained the graves of the descendants of the saint" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 142, folio 124). Khalid reads the modest tomb and the grander building of the progeny as showing "the transition that occurred after the death of Baba Farid" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).

Rozehnal describes "The regional shrine complex in the small pilgrimage town of Pakpattan—120 miles southwest of Lahore", which "has for centuries served as a central marker in the regional history of Punjab" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 35, folio 25); "Since the thirteenth century, the tomb of the saint, Baba Farid, has linked the local population to a wider universe of Islamic piety and Chishti Sufi spiritual hierarchy" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 35, folio 25). He names "the narrow stairwell at the extreme north end of the shrine, a passageway typically reserved for the Diwan and his personal guests" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, pp. 36–37, folios 26–27), "the northern gate" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 38, folio 28), "a large stage in the center of the courtyard" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, pp. 37–38, folios 27–28) and "the inner sanctum of the tomb" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 219, folio 211). "This is especially true at Pakpattan where thousands of pilgrims are pressed into an extremely cramped area. As a result, murids generally avoid lingering in the shrine for extended periods of time" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 217, folio 209). Books are sold "in the myriad bookstalls that flank the country's major Sufi shrines, most prominently in the narrow lanes surrounding the tomb complex of Baba Farid, the Chishti saint, in Pakpattan" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 130, folio 121).

Around the shrine there grew a family of others. Eaton: "small memorial shrines to Baba Farid began appearing, scattered throughout the countryside of central Punjab", and "what is significant is that they were built not by the Tughluq sultans as were the main structures of the Ajudhan complex, but by the common people themselves" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 222). Amir Khurd, a contemporary of the grandson 'Ala' ad-Din, wrote that "in the countryside around Ajudhan, Dipalpur, and in the hills toward Kashmir, the people out of love and belief have built structures and tombs in the name of his shrine…and they go to these villages for alms and devotions" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 222, n. 25). The devotees saw the shrine as "occupying one in a network of sacred wilayats, or territories under the spiritual authority of one or another pir (saint), which dot all of North India", these shrines being "related to each other in terms of a ranked hierarchy, with the shrine of Baba Farid being the supreme shrine of the Punjab, to which others were subordinate" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, pp. 241–242); "this ranked hierarchy was ritually acted out according to an unwritten etiquette governing the attendance of shrine rituals by the sajjada-nishins of other shrines" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 242). One sajjada-nishin listed among its subordinates the shrines of Shaikh Jamal al-Din of Hansi, of Nizam al-Din Auliya' in Delhi, Piran Kaler, Golra Sharif, Taunsa Sharif, Uch Sharif, Kasur and Panipat, among others — a single witness's claim (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, pp. 241–242, n. 12). "Although one shrine located in the nearby town of Dipalpur had been given land by an earlier Diwan of Pakpattan and for this reason saw itself as subordinate to Baba Farid's shrine, most of the 'daughter' shrines of Baba Farid in the Punjab were founded by family descendants of Baba Farid, and hence they looked upon the shrine at Pakpattan as their spiritual superior" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 242, n. 13). Among such shrines in Pakpattan itself is that of Baba Farid's son-in-law, Badr al-Din Ishaq, which the Diwan visited at its own *'urs* (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 255).

The shrine has known violence and the hand of the state. "A couple of years prior to our visit there was a blast at the entrance of the shrine killing six people", Khalid writes; women were searched by female police officials; and of all such shrines he notes that photography was strictly prohibited, and that "Earlier these shrines would remain open throughout the night providing shelter to all, but now they are shut at midnight on the order of the government" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated). He names "Baba Farid's at Pakpattan" among the Sufi shrines that have "come under attack" in "a wave of Islamic purism" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 278, folio 252).

## Custodians, Patrons and the State

The seat passed at once to a son. Sharib writes that his son Badr al-Din Sulaiman became his *sajjada nashin* (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 62, folio 58). Eaton: "the position of prime successor to Baba Farid, later called the 'diwan,' fell to his son, Badr ad-Din Sulaiman (1265–81)", whose accession "immediately set a pattern of hereditary religious leadership at the shrine" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 219); "although every diwan of the shrine down to the present has been the eldest son of his predecessor, Baba Farid's own first and second sons were passed over in favour of Badr ad-Din, who was the third son", and he alone received the office not from his predecessor but "directly by the Chishti elders in Chisht, western Afghanistan" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 219, n. 9). The title itself, Eaton writes, belongs to the court: "the special title of Baba Farid's chief successor, a personage who at other shrines was designated simply sajada nishin ('one who sits on the prayer carpet'), at Pakpattan was and is 'diwan,' a term taken directly from the lexicon of Indo-Islamic royal courts, and possibly alluding to the man's revenue-collecting function mentioned above" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 230). In the depositions "the shrine's religious leader was a direct lineal descendant of the saint and was believed to be Baba Farid's living representative" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 239). Ernst and Lawrence write: "Examples of shrines in Pakistan comparable to Ajmer include Baba Farid's tomb and its custodians" at Pakpattan, the *diwans*, *gaddi nishins* or *sajjada nishins* (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 116, folio 103).

The shrine kept a ranked society. In descending rank, Eaton writes, there was the diwan, the diwan's family, the khalifas and shrine functionaries, the Chishti caste, the clan leaders, and the common Jat agriculturalists (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 236). "At the bottom of the shrine's social hierarchy, then, were the peasant murids, 'we smaller people,' belonging for the most part to Jat agricultural clans in southwestern Punjab. At the top, of course, was the Diwan, the supreme patron of all ritual functions at the shrine and the chief dispenser of Baba Farid's blessings. In between these was an endogamous clan of family descendants of Baba Farid, the Chishti clan" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 248). "So numerous were these descendants that they literally formed a separate zamindar caste in the area, the 'Chishti' caste, possessing both economic privileges and ritual status vis-a-vis the local clans" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 230). A colonial assessment report described the Chishtis as "a semi-religious Mussalman tribe" of "considerable local influence," who "are not working agriculturalists, but depend for cultivation entirely on tenants" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 230, n. 52).

The clans were bound to the family by marriage and oath. Eaton counts, from the *Jawahir-i Faridi* of 1623, thirteen alliances between the Khokhars and the shrine, in which "seven of the Khokhar brides were daughters of clan" chiefs, and "three brides went to the diwans themselves and six went to sons of various diwans", the earliest being "a Khokhar bride given to a son of Diwan Ahmad Shah, who was diwan of the shrine from 1452 to 1474" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 232, n. 60); "Of five exchanges involving the Bhattis, two brides were daughters of Bhatti chiefs and three grooms were sons of diwans, the earliest alliance dating back to the late 1400s" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 232, n. 61). "Chishti daughters were evidently kept within the caste", and "the kinship relations of the diwan and the clans immediately call to mind those of the Mughal court and its subordinate Rajput clans, except that in the case of the diwans religious as well as political patronage was involved" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 232). "The Khokhars, Bhattis, Dhudhis, and Hans are found in the environs of Pakpattan, and all the clans take bai'at with this family and have become murids" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 230, n. 53); in Eaton's words, "the clans swore allegiance not to Baba Farid but to his family—i.e., the Chishti caste of Pakpattan" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 230). The same source claims that the clans "are the possessors of dignity in the environs of Pakpattan. They can place ten thousand cavalry and foot soldiers in his [Baba Farid's] service" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 231, n. 55), though "we do not hear that the diwan's Jat murids were actually called upon to do battle in service of the shrine" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 231). The *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* tells of one Abdullah Khokhar who went to the *gaddi nashins* of the mazar of Hazrat Baba Farid al-Din Ganj Shakar at Pak Pattan[OCR?] to offer his daughter and was refused (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 161). Living pirs in the villages carried the saint's grace onward; a cultivator-and-pir of Dipalpur told the court, "I am Watuzai Pathan and my ancestor of the 16th degree higher up was a disciple of Baba Farid. I am personally a pir of my tribe" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 242, n. 14).

The rulers of Delhi came to the shrine the saint had kept from them. The Khalji sultans "do not seem to have been interested in the shrine", but "the whole picture changed with the advent of the Tughluq period (1321–98)" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 220). Ibn Battuta reported that Sultan Muhammad ibn Tughluq had bestowed the city of Ajudhan on the shrine, which Eaton thinks "seems to be the earliest reference to the court's alienation of local revenues in favour of the shrine's support" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 220, n. 16); in the brief successorship of Diwan Mu'izz ad-Din (1335–38) the same sultan granted an endowment for the shrine's public kitchen (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 221, n. 22); and Mu'izz ad-Din himself "was even called to Delhi by Muhammad ibn Tughluq, placed in government service, and sent to Gujarat as deputy governor", while "The diwan's brother, meanwhile, was appointed to the office of Shaikh al-Islam of India" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 221, nn. 20–21). "Never were the affairs of the shrine more firmly welded to court interests. The shrine's leaders were now under Delhi's control, and the shrine became even more dependent economically upon court patronage" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 221). Barani recorded that Sultan Firuz Tughluq repaired the tomb of Baba Farid and "granted robes of honour to his descendants and confirmed them in possession of their villages and lands." (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 221, n. 23). In October 1398 Timur, in the midst of his plunder of northern India, visited the shrine (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 228, n. 45); "Akbar, in March 1571, opened his sixteenth regnal year in Pakpattan, where he implored strength at Baba Farid's shrine" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 229, n. 46). "No major ruler passed by the area without showing deference to its spiritual power" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 228). Ernst and Lawrence write that Akbar drew at Fatehpur Sikri on "the spiritual blessing that derived from his ascetic patron, Shaykh Farid ad-Din Ganj-i Shakar in the Punjab" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 112, folio 99), and Rizvi that Muhammad bin Tughluq "was deeply devoted to the descendants of Shaikh Hamidu'd-Din Nagauri and Baba Farid" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 194, folio 176).

Shah Jahan's *farman* of 1629 is the fullest endowment document the books quote. "The sacred town of Pak Pattan with all its dependencies is by old agreement held in grant from the preceding emperors for the 'Langar' expenses of the shrine", held "by Shekh Mohammad, Sujjadah Nasheen of the shrine, a descendant of the Baba"; "the proceeds thereof are applied to his own maintenance and to that of the Durveshes and Khadims attached thereto, as well as to the feed of Travellers and the repair and adornment of the building"; and the order runs, "Continue the whole 'Muhal' [i.e., pargana] in endowment to the shrine." (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 229, n. 47). In return, "the diwans of Pakpattan performed several functions for the Mughal government. Above all, on certain tracts of land they received the government's share of all crops on which revenue was levied in kind, whereas the tax on cash crops such as cotton, indigo, or tobacco had to be paid in cash and went straight to revenue officials without passing the diwan" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 229, n. 48). Eaton offers, while warning that "there is no corroborating evidence to this effect", that "these circumstances suggest that the diwans might have been promoters of peasant agriculture" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 229).

As Mughal power failed, the shrine armed. "Formerly, in the history of medieval Punjab, the Diwans of Pakpattan had frequently played important political roles, enjoying close relations with the sultans and Mughal emperors of Delhi. And during the decline of Mughal authority, the Diwans' political roles even outweighed their religious roles" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 243). A shrine functionary deposed that from the early 1700s the Diwans "were inclined to make conquests, to maintain armies, and to build forts. They themselves stopped giving spiritual education and employed Maulvis, Imams, Khatibs and Mubilighs for the purpose" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, pp. 243–244, n. 18). Eaton records that "in 1757 Diwan 'Abd as-Subhan (1752–66) gathered an army of his Jat murids, attacked the raja of Bikaner, and thereby expanded the shrine's territorial holdings for the first time to the east of the Sutlej" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 231, n. 56). "Supported mainly by his Wattu murids, the diwan successfully defended a Sikh attack on Pakpattan around 1776 in which Hira Singh was killed, and then pursued the retreating Sikhs with four thousand cavalry, killing a great number of them" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 231, nn. 57–58). Then, "In 1810 the shrine's extensive holdings were seized by Ranjit Singh, and the diwans of Pakpattan, their brief period of political independence now at an end, reverted to their former status of political intermediaries" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 231); "Ranjit Singh allowed the diwan and his retainers only Rs. 1,000 a year for their maintenance, derived from the town duties of Pakpattan, in addition to a fourth share of four small villages nearby", under "much worse terms than under the Mughals" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 231, n. 59). The *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* names the *sajjada nashin* at the time of Maulvi Fakhr-ud-Din's yearly visits, Hazrat Diwan Muhammad Subhan (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 756).

Under the British the succession came to court. "At times, the British interfered directly in the administration of local shrines such as" Pakpattan, "going so far as to mediate succession disputes in colonial courts" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 33, folio 23). Eaton's second essay is built on one such case: "In December 1934, Sa'id Muhammad, who was the religious head of the shrine of Baba Farid, died, leaving behind him his eleven-year-old son, Qutb al-Din, as his chosen successor. Only four months later, however, a local group of devotees at the shrine, who like Sa'id Muhammad were genealogical descendants of Baba Farid, filed a legal plaint in the nearby District Court in which they not only disputed Qutb al-Din's claim to the shrine's leadership, but substituted a rival claimant", Ghulam Rasul (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 238). "Between 6 April 1935, when Ghulam Rasul filed his plaint, and 2 December 1938, when a District Court judge decided the case in favour of the defendant, Qutb al-Din, no less than 159 oral depositions were filed on behalf of the plaintiff and 172 on behalf of the defendant, covering in all over 836 typed pages. In addition, both sides filed nearly three hundred documents with the Court" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 238); the record is the Lahore High Court's Regular First Appeal No. 93 of 1939, *Dewan Ghulam Rasul vs. Ghulam Qutab-ud-din*, in five volumes (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 239, n. 3). Eaton writes that "the essential purpose of the trial of 1935–8 was to judge the plaintiff's claim that the prerogative to select a new Diwan belonged to the Chishti clan and not to the previous Diwan" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 249). The defendant "claimed that only a former Diwan could choose his successor": his father had placed a turban on the boy's head "during the course of the Bihishti mela of 1933", and on the fortieth day after his father's death the *sajjada-nishin* of the Ajmer shrine placed the turban on his head again; the plaintiff "claimed that a new Diwan must be elected by the Chishti clan", and representatives of the whole clan turbaned him (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 255, n. 61; Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 255, n. 62). "Since the plaintiff was not given access to the shrine for his dastar-bandi ceremony, this aspect of the rite had to be omitted in his case" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 256, n. 63). A point of law was whether the late Diwan "received a dream in which Baba Farid revealed to him that his son Qutb al-Din should succeed him as Diwan" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 243), and Judge Guru Datta ruled: "I decide that the defendant has sufficiently succeeded in proving that Diwan Said Muhammad had received revelation and that the defendant's nomination was made on the strength of that" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 243, n. 17). "Neither side in this case argued that the murids should have had any say in the matter" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 249); a villager put it, "The murids were never consulted, they only said Amin to the biradri's election" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 249, n. 40), and another, "I am a follower of the Gaddi Nashin whosoever may be occupying it" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 258, n. 70). Ernst and Lawrence record that "a debate raged in Chishti circles in the 1930s over whether or not the British could compel a minor who had become diwan at Pakpattan to obtain education at a British college in Lahore", and that "All the sajjada nishins from other major Chishti shrines in North India protested 'that the sajjada nishin should be a Darwesh and has no need of receiving the education which is imparted to the sons of rich families.'" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 116, folio 103).

In Pakistan the state has a hand in the festival. Ernst and Lawrence write that "Government manipulation, as in Pakistan's control of the 'urs of Baba Farid, may present an institutional, ideological face to this practice that many" mystics would resist or resent (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 157, folio 145). Rozehnal names the shrine's chief custodian in 2001, "Diwan Maudud Mas'ud Chishti", and a member of his family, "Badr Moeenuddin, the Diwan's maternal uncle" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 39, folio 29); that year, a newspaper reported, the Diwan argued with the Awqaf Department, "insisting that the shrine should be paid an annuity of 1,500,000 rupees rather than the 150,000 rupees they were promised" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 242, folio 235). A disciple spoke of "a six-hundred-and-fifty-year-old tradition" of custodianship broken by the Awqaf takeover (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 41, folio 31). "The current guardians of the shrine are from the family of Baba Farid and they are not only one of the richest but also one of the most powerful families of the region", Khalid writes, and his progeny "received gifts and awards from different rulers" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated); in Khalid's reconstruction of Nanak's time, the power of the descendants grew until they "became major feudal lords and political players of the region", and Sheikh Ibrahim, "the twelfth successor of Baba Farid", held the position of Diwan, finding it frustrating, and "abhorred the institutionalisation of spirituality as was being practised in the name of his family" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 142, folio 124).

## Devotion and Practice

The shrine’s most famous feature is the *Bahishti Darwaza*, the "Gate of Paradise" — a silver-plated door that is opened only once a year, during the saint’s annual urs in the Islamic month of Muharram; pilgrims believe that to pass through it is to be cleansed of sin, and for the days that it stands open a vast multitude presses through it in one of the largest gatherings of the Punjabi year. Qawwali, the free kitchen (*langar*), and the recitation of Farid’s verse fill the shrine, above all through the urs.

The *'urs* falls on the fifth of Muharram, the day of his death. The Chishti *'urs* calendar in Ernst and Lawrence's appendix opens with "Muharram / 5. Farid ad-Din Mas'ud Ajodhani [Ganj-i Shakar] (d. 664/1265)" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 159, folio 147), and the Chishti Sabiri calendar Rozehnal prints lists "Baba Farid Ganj-i Shakkar (5 Muharram): at the saint's tomb in Pakpattan" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 215, folio 207). Ernst and Lawrence describe it as "the multi-day annual festival celebrating his 'marriage' with God", at which "cowrie shells (once used as the smallest units of currency) are thrown over the crowd, to imitate the moment when Shaykh Farid ad-Din went into ecstasy, on realizing that Nizam ad-Din Awliya' had just attained a status rare even among the great ones, the nearly unprecedented state of becoming the beloved of God Himself" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 18, folio 3). For the order that Rozehnal followed it is a "weeklong 'urs retreat" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 217, folio 209). The festival is old: Amir Khusrau "mentioned the annual 'urs or death-date celebration on the occasion of the fiftieth anniversary of the shaikh's death and described in detail the 'urs celebration for the year 1315: the pilgrimage of pious persons to Ajudhan, the recitation of the saint's wonderful deeds, and the entertainments performed by an ensemble of darweshes" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 219, n. 10). The *Jawahir-i Faridi*, completed in 1623, dates all the shrine's major rituals to the generation after the saint's death: the *dastar bandi*, the regularizing of ecstatic singing at the shrine, the public kitchen from which the diwan directed the distribution of food and sweets on formal occasions, and the opening of "the southern door (shisha darwaza) of Baba Farid's tomb on the occasion of the shrine's annual 'urs celebration, allowing the masses of common devotees to pass by the shrine's sanctum sanctorum, ritually entering heaven" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 219, n. 11; Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 240, n. 8).

At the festival the Diwan opens the Gate. Rozehnal: "This famous southern door of the saint's tomb remains closed throughout the year until the evening of the anniversary when it is opened by the Diwan, a direct lineal descendant of the saint and the shrine's official representative (sajjada nishin)"; passing through it is "a direct, salvational encounter with Baba Farid's living grace (baraka), a ritual reenactment of the saint's eternal union ('urs, or 'marriage') with God" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 36, folio 26). Eaton calls it the shrine's "carefully defined formula for achieving religious transcendence" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 236): "that day the Diwan personally unlocked and opened the gate, passed through it, and was then followed by throngs of murids estimated to number between fifty and sixty thousand"; "All through the night and in states of intense religious ecstasy, the devotees filed through this 'Gate of Heaven' and past Baba Farid's flower-bedecked tomb, thereby both commemorating and ritually reenacting the ascension of their patron saint to heaven" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 254). The crowd figure comes from the Montgomery District gazetteer of 1884, and the year it describes is not stated (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 254, n. 56). A deponent of August 1938 gave the order of passage: "Dewan Sahib first opens the Bahishti Gate… he enters the gate and thereafter his companions pass through that gate. The companions number about 100-125. After that, the pass-holders pass through it, they are about 400-500; after that, the darveshes of the Hujra [i.e., resident mystics], who number 1,000—1,200. After them the plaintiff, Ghulam Rasul and his party, pass through the Bahishti Gate, after that the sajjadanashins of other institutions pass, and so on." (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 254, n. 58). "The great masses of common devotees, included among the 'and so on' in the above quote, were allowed to enter through the gate only after the other dignataries had done so" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 254). Lieutenant F. Mackeson's journal of Captain C. M. Wade's voyage of 1832–33 records that "numbers of pilgrims, both Hindus and Mussalmans, come to visit the shrine, and all who pass through this doorway are considered saved from the fires of perdition. The door-way is about two feet wide, and cannot be passed without stopping, and the apartment itself is not capable of containing thirty people crowded together"; "A superlative heaven is allotted to those who are first to enter the tomb on the day mentioned. The rush for precedence may, therefore, be better imagined than described"; and, as the pilgrims come out "after having rubbed their foreheads on the foot of the saint's grave, the air resounds with their shouts of Farid! Farid!" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 254, n. 57). Rozehnal heard the pilgrims' cry as "Ya Farid, Haq Farid!" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 36, folio 26). Thus, Eaton writes, "because only the Diwan opened the gate, it was only through his agency that devotees gained access to Baba Farid, and only through the saint's agency that they gained access to Heaven" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 254). The *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* explains one of the tomb's two doors by Nizam-ud-Din's dream of the Prophet saying that whoever entered it would be safe (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 234).

Turbans and sugar flow outward from the Diwan. A sixty-year-old villager deposed that "pechas [turbans] are distributed at the annual urs only by [the] sajjadanashin and nobody else. At that time, [the] sajjadanashin has hundreds of pechas with him. He must distribute those pechas personally." (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 256, n. 67). In 2001 "the Diwan mounted a large stage in the center of the courtyard to distribute saffron-soaked strips of cloth known as pechas to the assembled masses" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, pp. 37–38, folios 27–28). For Eaton "the distribution of sugar represented the outward flow of Baba Farid's bounty and blessings", linking Diwan and murids to "their spiritual ancestor, Baba Farid, whose best-known epithet, Ganj-i Shakar, or 'treasury of sugar,' was associated with legends concerning the saint's severe austerities" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 240). The *dastar* of succession is "a special saffron-coloured turban reserved only for the Diwan", which "was physically touched to the saint's tomb before being placed on the candidate's head" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 256, n. 63). When the Diwan went to the *'urs* of Badr al-Din Ishaq's shrine he was carried on a palanquin, and gave the descendant there 100 rupees in cash, a horse and a silver saddle (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 255; Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 255, n. 60).

The kitchen has fed pilgrims since the first successor. "Located in the shrine's courtyard just opposite Baba Farid's tomb, the langar was a vast operation, daily providing physical sustenance to the many pilgrims who came to pay respects and devotions at the shrine"; one former kitchen worker estimated before the court that 240 to 320 lbs, 3 or 4 *mans*, of wheat were used there every day, with *dal* in proportion (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 240, n. 5). Khalid found "the langar hall, offering free food to all" behind the qawwals (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).

Offerings and vows bind devotee and saint. Already in his lifetime "Huge crowds gathered daily at Baba Farid's convent to receive ta'widh", usually "a scrap of paper on which were written the names of God or some Qur'anic verses", and "even now the successors of Baba Farid and his assistants, in common with those of other shrines in the Punjab, continue to write ta'widh for the masses" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 218); and devotees would offer the shrine a gift called *futuh*, typically of sweets, of which "even in Baba Farid's own day the gift swelled to enormous proportions" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 218). Eaton writes from the depositions that "murids regularly made religious vows or 'contracts' with Baba Farid, as a mediator between them and God, in hopes of receiving any number of concrete favours, such as female fertility, good crops, relief from illness, etc." (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 239). A cultivator of Lyallpur District said, "We give nazranas to Dewan Sahib out of love, respect and regard for the Pir and to invoke his blessings for the good of the murid" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 239, n. 2); "Some murids testified that, having made the pious visit to Pakpattan, they would 'earmark' their nazrana to go either in support of the shrine's public kitchen, or to the Diwan. Others gave their nazrana to the Diwan when he visited them in their villages" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 239, n. 4). Today there is "always a rush outside the shrine, where there are several shops selling religious paraphernalia", and devotees buy "chaddar, flowers and incense sticks to be presented to the caretakers of the shrine", for "It is considered inauspicious to visit the grave of a saint without a gift" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated). Not every devotee approved: "one old cultivator" declared, "doing sajda [prostration] to the tomb of Baba Farid under Mohammadan Law is not permissible" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 257, n. 68).

Qawwali sounds there at every hour. "At the shrine of Baba Farid, the most important Chisti Sufi in Pakistan, there are qawwals performing at all times of the day"; a group sat "under the shade of a building across from the Karir tree", "one man on the harmonium and one on tabla while the rest of them clapped", and "Aptly the musicians were singing verses of Baba Farid" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated). A young man began "whirling on his knees in a state of trance", another devotee "threw notes of ten rupees in the air", and "Here women and men sat together" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated). Abbas names it as a Chishtiyya shrine where "the same ritual in qawwālī discourse is followed" and where "this is an exclusively male domain, women's presence at such performances is rare" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 84, folio not stated). Of Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan she writes that "He was himself a devotee of the Sufi saint and performed at his shrine in Pakpattan Sharif" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 89, folio not stated), and in a note that names only the place and "the shrine" she records that the scholar Lorraine Sakata, accompanying a musician to Pakpattan Sharif, "observed the performance sitting behind a pillar where the men could not see her" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 203, folio not stated). The Chishti Sabiri order Rozehnal studied keeps its own *sama'* "in a room adjacent to the shrine", where "the shaykh sits directly opposite the performers" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 225, folio 217); "throughout the 1970s Shaykh Shahidullah Faridi pointedly refused to allow either the Sabri Brothers or Nusrat Fateh 'Ali Khan to perform at the annual 'urs in Pakpattan" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 230, folio 222); and Wahid Bakhsh wrote of the 1990 *'urs* that "On the night of the 'urs the qawwals sang special Persian and Hindi ghazals for our silsila people because they know what our taste is. On the arrival of the Diwan Sahib, the qawwali reverted to mob entertainment", and that "During mob qawwali we fixed our attention on the saint himself who made up for all the deficiency" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 232, folio 224). Khalid's *A White Trail* names it among the "famous Muslim shrines" where a *dandiya* group has played (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 63, folio not stated). Orthodox critics, as *In Search of Shiva* reports them, argue that Sufis "like Baba Farid" condoned qawwali "because they wanted to win over Hindus into Islam", and that "However today there is no need for qawwali" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).

The grave chamber is closed to women. "Women are not allowed inside the chamber where the grave is located"; instead there is "a verandah adjacent to the shrine, from where they can catch a glimpse of the grave" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated). A woman disciple from Peshawar told Rozehnal: "At Pakpattan Sharif women cannot even enter [the inner sanctum of the tomb]. The most you can do is touch and kiss the threshold" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 219, folio 211). In 2001 the male disciples of the order sat in rows before the main door of the tomb "as the female disciples looked down from the rooftop balcony above" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 37, folio 27). Usborne's Waris Shah measures a great crowd against "the Fairs of Pakpattan or Nigah or Rattan or Thamman, where women flock to kiss the tomb of the saint and attain the achievement of their desires" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 24, folio 23) — a sentence that names the place but not the saint.

The pilgrims come from near and far. On the eve of the *'urs* in 2001 "tens of thousands of devotees inundated the provincial town of Pakpattan" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 36, folio 26). Eaton: "It is in Pakpattan, a town that lay within easy reach of only several days' travel in the Punjab, that God's grace was made concrete and accessible, that Heaven and Earth met" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 247). After Partition, when its Pakistani disciples could no longer easily travel to Ajmer, the Chishti Sabiri order "transferred the 'urs of Muin ad-Din to the shrine of Baba Farid in Pakpattan" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 215, folio 207); in Pakpattan, as in Allahabad and Karachi, "the silsila maintains separate living compounds for men and women adjacent to the saint's tomb" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 216, folio 208). Its disciples offer communal prayers inside the *dargah* and sit afterwards "in silent contemplation, facing the saint's tomb", but avoid *zikr* there: "At Pakpattan we do not do anything extra because Baba Sahab's influence is so great and so powerful" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 218, folio 210). A woman disciple described "a typical day at the 'urs in Pakpattan Sharif", from morning prayer "at the women's compound or at the shrine" to *muraqaba* at the shrine and preparing "for the qawwali" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 219, folio 211). Wahid Bakhsh's letter records "a full week of spiritual exercises (mujahada) at the shrine (mazar) comprising three strenuous hours in the morning from 8 am until 11 am, and three hours in the evening, from 5 to 8 pm" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 232, folio 224). At the *'urs* of 20 July 1977 Shahidullah Faridi answered the charge of idolatry: "we come here because these relics have an association with a saintly person like Baba Sahib. We come here because they are signs of Allah" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 212, folio 204). Ernst and Lawrence record a nineteenth-century meditation circuit in which "Farid ad-Din in Pakpattan" was the third station after Qutb ad-Din in Delhi and Mu'in ad-Din in Ajmer (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 71, folio 57), and Hasan Nizami's "trip on foot (partially) to Pakpattan in emulation of the saint's trip to find his master Farid" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 127, folio 114). Malaysian disciples who could not travel gathered in Kuala Lumpur on the evening of the *'urs*, "Listening to recordings of sama' on a small cassette player" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 43, folio 33).

Hindus and Sikhs have come too. The journal of 1832–33 records "both Hindus and Mussalmans" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 254, n. 57). Khalid writes that "Once Baba Farid found a place in the Guru Granth Sahib, his personality and shrine became holy for the followers of Guru Nanak as well. Every year, hundreds of Sikhs and Hindus travel to Pakpattan to pay homage to the shrine of Baba Farid" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 153, folio 135), and also that "For years now Sikhs have petitioned the Pakistani government to grant them visas to attend the urs of Baba Farid but in vain" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated). Rozehnal writes that "In the absence of other religious communities—the Hindu, Jain, or Sikh 'Other'—Pakpattan is no longer a site of intercommunal conflict or cooperation. Instead, the shrine is now marked as exclusively Muslim space" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, pp. 35–36, folios 25–26).

His day is kept far from Pakpattan as well. At the shrine of Nizamuddin Auliya in Delhi, Qureshi lists among its anniversaries those "of the Saint's own spiritual guide, Baba Farid" (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 116, folio 96). In Lahore the silk-worker Shaikh Faiz Bakhsh kept seventeen *'urs* a year, among them Khwaja Farid al-Din's, and at each had food cooked in plenty and given out to the poor and needy (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 125, folio 113). At Nankana Sahib, Khalid writes, the qawwals "begin with a devotional song, expressing love towards the Muslim Saint Baba Fareed. The Sikh community, which feels a strong affinity towards the Muslim Saint, rejoices" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 218, folio not stated).

## The Night of 1 April 2001

Rozehnal's book opens with a disaster at the *'urs*. "On the night of April 1, 2001 more than one hundred Pakistani men lost their lives, crushed to death in a mob stampede at the tomb of one of South Asia's most renowned Sufi saints: Baba Farid ad-Din Mas'ud Ganj-i Shakkar (d. 1265)" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 29, folio not stated). He was there: "I too was there that night, accompanying a select group of Chishti Sabiri Sufi disciples (murids)" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 37, folio 27). It was "Late in the night of April 1, 2001—the fifth day of Muharram, the first month of the Muslim lunar calendar", "the eve of Baba Farid's death anniversary ('urs)" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 36, folio 26).

The victims had "ascended the narrow stairwell at the extreme north end of the shrine"; "an impatient crowd of several thousand onlookers broke through a weak police cordon and descended upon them from above"; and "a police contingent posted at the entrance point made the fateful decision to close and lock the doors, sealing off the only means of escape" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, pp. 36–37, folios 26–27). "Pilgrims had become martyrs, crushed to death." (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 37, folio 27). A boy of the order died: "A fourteen-year-old boy from the group was not so fortunate, however, perishing amid the melee", the son of a prominent devotee and "the great-grandson of the prominent Chishti Sabiri master, Shaykh Muhammad Zauqi Shah (1877–1951)" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 38, folio 28). The book gives the dead as "more than one hundred" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 29, folio not stated); the reporter Tahir Jehangir wrote in *The Friday Times*, "The newspapers reported over forty, but I can vouch for the fact that there were many more" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 37, folio 27).

The blame was laid in two directions. The state's account was that "the Diwan had delayed the public ceremonies and the opening of the bihishti darwaza by four hours while bickering over the annual payment allotted to him from the Awqaf Department", and the next day "the lead article in the national Urdu newspaper Jang quoted the district commissioner who insisted that this financial quarrel precipitated the crisis" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 39, folio 29); "Local politicians and police attributed the chaos, confusion, and deaths to the greed of the local shrine authorities and the lawlessness of an unruly, uneducated mob" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 48, folio 38). The custodians' account, given by Badr Moeenuddin in *The Nation*, "blames poor crowd control and security arrangements as the underlying cause of the stampede", noting that "the tragedy took place some two hours after the Diwan's arrival for the door-opening ceremony" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 39, folio 29); "The heirs of Pakpattan's holy man, by contrast, condemned these same administrators for overturning established crowd control policies and shirking responsibility" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 48, folio 38).

The disciples read the deaths as martyrdom. "With a firm belief that nothing accidental happens in proximity to a saint's tomb, murids reasoned that the loss of life must be sacralized and understood in terms of martyrdom (shahadat)" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 41, folio 31); "Murids now commemorate the 'marriage' ('urs) of both the saint and the young boy with the God. In both cases, death has been transformed into martyrdom" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 234, folio not stated). "A number of disciples narrated dreams that they experienced in the days leading up to the 'urs: visions of Chishti Sabiri shaykhs, the death of family members and, in the case of one female murid, an attack by a black goat" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 43, folio 33). Rozehnal judges that "The scope and scale of the tragedy of 2001 seem to be unprecedented in Pakpattan's seven-hundred-year history. Given the massive crowds that attend the shrine's annual 'urs commemoration, however, accidents are not uncommon", and quotes Miles Irving in 1911: "Some thirty years ago there was an unfortunate occasion on which several lives were lost by the crush of the Door of Paradise, and before British rule, it is said, this was of yearly occurrence." (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 242, folio 235). "The Pakpattan tragedy of April 1, 2001 quickly receded from Pakistan's media spotlight" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 234, folio not stated).

## Legends and Miracles

His birth was marked by a sign, as Sharib tells it. There was doubt about the Ramadan moon; a *buzurg* staying there said that a child would be born that night at the qazi's house, Farid's father's, and that if it drank milk the fast should not be kept; that night he was born, did not drink, and the people fasted (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 56–57, folios 52–53). Rizvi tells of his mother's piety at work: "a thief, entering the house glanced at the praying woman and was instantly blinded. The robber implored the Shaikh's mother to restore his eyesight. She did so, and the blindness was removed. The following day, the thief returned with his family to the Shaikh's house and was converted to Islam" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 157, folio 139) — "one of the rare conversions through early Chishti influence, although the circumstances which prompted it, according to the story, were miraculous" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 425, folio not stated).

How he came by the name Ganjshakar is told in many ways. In Sharib's first reason, walking to his pir in Delhi through the mud after fasting seven days, his foot slipped and a little mud entered his mouth; by God's command it became sugar, and his pir said that God had made his being sugar and would keep him sweet for ever (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 57, folio 53). In his second, a merchant carrying sugar on bullocks denied it, saying it was salt, not sugar, and found salt in every sack at his destination; he came seeking a prayer, was told that it would become sugar, and it did become sugar (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 57, folio 53). Rizvi gives the pebbles: "overpowered by incessant fasting for three days, the Baba placed some pebbles in his mouth. These immediately changed into sugar. Baba Farid, believing that this had been the work of the devil, spat them out… Finally, overpowered by extreme hunger he ate some pebbles which had become sugar so that he could continue praying. Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din approved his action, telling him that whatever was received from the unseen world must necessarily be good" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 158, folio 140), adding that "The reason why the Shaikh came to be known as Ganj-i Shakar (Store of Sugar) is described in different stories in various hagiological works" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 158, folio 140). Ernst and Lawrence's translation tells the pebbles under Qutb ad-Din's direction and then "another well-known story about how he came to have this epithet", the sugar merchant whose load turned to salt and back (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 168, folio 156). Schimmel: "His constant fasting was miraculously rewarded—even pebbles turned into sugar, hence his surname Ganj-i shakar", 'sugar treasure'; "Whether this charming legend is true or not", she adds, it shows the importance he placed on ascetic exercises (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, pp. 377–378, folios 346–347). Bhandari writes that earth turned to sugar under his gaze, and so he became famous as Ganj-i Shakar (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 526). Khalid notes "several myths as to how the saint earned this title—one of them being that he was extremely soft-spoken" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).

Several books tell of a single pomegranate seed. Rizvi: Jalalu'd-Din Tabrizi "had with him a pomegranate, and broke it, offering it to Baba Farid. As the Baba was fasting he refused to eat it", but after his guest had left he picked up a seed and in the evening ate it; "Instantly his heart was illuminated and he lamented not having eaten the whole fruit. However in Delhi Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din consoled the Baba saying that in each pomegranate there was only one seed endowed with spiritual blessings and that he had had it" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 157, folio 139). In Sharib the seed was left on the ground when Jalal al-Din Tabrizi met him at Kahtwal; he put it in his turban, broke his fast with it, felt *inshirah*, and his pir told him that everything had been in that one seed (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 60, folio 56). Another chapter of the same book, which places him only at Kahtwal, the name uncertain in the printed text, calls him the qazi's boy, living in the *jami'* mosque there (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 35, folio 31), and has the pir tell Masud at Delhi that the pomegranate seed that was his portion had come to him, and that he should be at peace (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 36, folio 32). Ernst and Lawrence write only that "once he is alleged to have consumed a single seed of a pomegranate brought by Jalal ad-Din Tabrizi, with astonishing results" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 81, folio 67). Rizvi adds that the townsfolk of Kahtwal "came to believe he was deranged, and they spoke so to Shaikh Jalalu'd-Din Tabrizi of Baba Farid and what to them were his strange habits" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 157, folio 139).

Heaven, the stories say, marked him early. Gharib Nawaz prayed that God accept their Farid and raise him to the rank of the most perfect dervish, and a voice came from the unseen: Farid was accepted, and he would be the unique one of the age (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 60–61, folios 56–57). Gharib Nawaz told Qutb Sahib that he had brought a great royal falcon into his net, whose nest would be the Sidrat al-Muntaha, the lote-tree of the furthest boundary (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 61, folio 57).

At Ajodhan the land itself bore witness. When the claimant to the plot of land asked it, the land made no sound; when an attendant asked on the Hazrat's behalf, a voice came from the land, saying that it had belonged to the man who sold it to Farid and was now Khwaja Farid's property. The governor punished the claimant severely; riding away after the judgement his horse stumbled and he fell, his neck broke at that moment and he died; God preserve us, the book adds, from the wrath of the saints (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 88–89, folios 76–77 [markers 86–87]). Lahori declines to tell the rest of his miracles, thousands of which are recorded in histories — the eager reader, it says, may look for them in the *Khazinat al-Asfiya* — giving only that one (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 88, folio 76 [marker 86]).

Some stories turn on his anger. In Sharib, a dervish asked three times for the comb on his prayer-mat and at last said that if he gave him the comb it would bring him blessing; the saint told him to go and not meddle in his state, for he had cast him and his blessing into running water; and outside Ajodhan the dervish bathed in the river, could not come out, and drowned (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 66, folio 62). Rizvi tells it too — "Baba Farid replied that he had thrown in the river whatever prosperity was wished on him. Going to bathe in a neighbouring shallow river, the dervish was drowned" — and rejects it as "incompatible with more factual evidence which depicts the kindliness of both Baba Farid and Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya'" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 248, folio 228). When a qalandar was set on striking a disciple, "the Baba asked him to strike the wall instead. The qalandar complied and the wall fell down" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 160–161, folios 142–143). He begged five travellers not to cross the desert; "Ignoring his advice, they proceeded to travel along the desert route. Shortly afterwards, news was brought that the four had died from heat exhaustion and the fifth, who finally reached a well, drank so much water that he also died" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 164, folio 146). Six dervishes who asked for provisions were given the dates before him; displeased, they threw them away, and saw that the dates had turned to pure gold (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 66, folio 62).

He knew what was hidden. Of a hundred *tankas* of which 'Arif kept fifty, "the Baba smiled and said that 'Arif had already divided it on a brotherly basis, rather than giving him the entire amount entrusted to him" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 172, folio 154). Nizam ad-Din told of him at prayer, prostrate under his garment, describing an unseen visitor — "is he a Turk of medium size with slightly yellowish complexion?" — and sending him off "to go away before he is disgraced", whereupon "The man took to his heels and disappeared" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 82, folio 68). Rizvi writes: "The Shaikh made nineteen visits to Baba Farid at Ajodhan, and on each occasion the Baba prayed that there might be a further visit. On the nineteenth he refrained from his usual prayer, and a few months before the Baba's death Shaikh Najibu'd-Din died" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 170, folio 152). His spiritual realm had a frontier: he "replied that the limit of his spiritual influence was at a certain water tank and that beyond it began the area of Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din whose prayers he should attain. The musician acted on the Baba's advice and safely completed his journey" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 211, folio 193). Lahori tells of the chairs at Multan, when he sat with Khwaja Baha al-Din: asked how far his striving had brought him, the answer was that if the word were spoken the chairs would fly; the chairs rose, and a hand was laid on them with the words that this talk had been by way of conversation, not of instruction, and that they should stay where they were (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 160, folio 148); the text does not make clear which of the two saints spoke and which restrained the chairs. Of the two, Bhandari writes that they were together for some time (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528).

The starving child is a hard story. In Ernst and Lawrence's translation one of his wives came to him with the words "Khwaja, today a son of ours is at the point of death from starvation", and the shaykh, bowing his head, replied 'What can Mas'ud do?', and told her, if it be God's will and the boy dies, to tie a rope around his feet and throw him out (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 81, folio 67). "The story is grim but probably exaggerated: Several of the saint's children did live to manhood and, in fact, were a credit to their ascetic father, whom they emulated in many respects" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 81, folio 67). Schimmel marks it as legend: "When he was informed that a child had died—so legend has it—he said": "If fate has so decreed and he dies, tie a rope around his feet and throw him out and come back!" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 378, folio 347).

His mother vanished on the road. Lahori: on the way to Ajodhan, in a waterless wilderness, she felt thirst; when water was brought two hours later she was no longer under the tree, and a search of the whole forest found no trace of her. Two years later some bones resembling human bones were found, and these were put in a bag tied shut; when the Hazrat opened the bag he found it empty, without a single bone, and it was understood that this too was one of the divine mysteries. The disappearance of this chaste woman took place in 643 AH; so she has no grave (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 251, folio 239). He ordered much food to be cooked and fed to the dervishes, and prayers said for her forgiveness (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 251, folio 239). Rizvi tells the story in the same shape: "When Baba Farid settled at Ajodhan he asked his brother, Shaikh Najibu'd-Din Mutwakkil, to bring his mother who was then living at either Hansi or Delhi, to Ajodhan to live with him" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 425, folio not stated); "Presuming them to be those of his mother who had fallen prey to a lion or some other beast, the Shaikh collected them in a bag and took them to Ajodhan. When the bag was opened, to the astonishment of the two brothers, it was empty"; "The tragedy was related to Baba Farid and the latter appropriately had prayers said and food distributed to the poor for the repose of his mother's soul" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 426, folio 402).

His relics worked wonders. "Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya' related that even a hair from the beard of Baba Farid was used by him as an amulet": "That hair embodied a great blessing. Whenever a sick person approached me for an amulet, I gave the hair to him. He would keep it for a few days and be cured" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 240, folio 220). Once it could not be found when a friend's child was ill, and the child died; "The moral of this story is that the hair could not be found as the child was destined to die" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 240–241, folios 220–221). Kugle writes of his "inner spiritual heat and force that could either propel or singe others, depending upon the sincerity of their approach toward him" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 260, folio 239). Eaton writes that the custodian and the building both carry the saint's power: "Both the Diwan and the shrine itself, a magnificent marble edifice constructed in the fourteenth century, are perceived to be carriers of Baba Farid's baraka"; "Hence both the Diwan and the shrine functioned as intermediaries for an intermediary, as on-going vehicles of the saint's mediative power" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 239). Ibn Battuta's account of his visit in 1334 shows, to Eaton, "that Baba Farid, the saint, was now clearly identified with the physical shrine complex, and that his (its) fame had spread as far as Egypt" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, pp. 221–222).

He is said to go on speaking after death. He appeared to Nizam ad-Din in a dream: "You have remarked that even if there is nothing in the house, one is expected to show perfect courtesy to whomever comes. How then has it happened that someone has gone from your house with a heavy heart?" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 173, folio 161). Muhammad Ghaws, the Shattari master, dreamt that "he found himself in one of the highest celestial realms. There he came face-to-face with Farid ad-Din Ganj-i Shakar", who explained that he shared that station with Bayazid, Junayd and Zu'n-Nun (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 75, folio 61). A Persian manuscript of Qadi Shir Muhammad Pakpattani, the *Chishtiyya-i bihishtiyya*, treats "Farid's postmortem miracles, khalifas, and testimony of Baha' al-Din Zakariyya" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 236, folio 226). In the Chishti Sabiri memory Rozehnal gathered, Shahidullah Faridi "had a vivid dream in which his status as khalifa was confirmed by the patron saint himself" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 76, folio 66): "Baba Sahab himself was standing at the door of his mazar. The people were shouting at him, 'Please, we want to become your disciples (murids). Make us your disciples!'", and Baba Sahab ordered him to make them murids (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, pp. 76–77, folios 66–67). Wahid Bakhsh, passing the tomb, thought that Baba Sahab had not left many books to read: "At that moment a voice came to me; it was the voice of Baba Sahab. He said, 'You are my books!'" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 174, folio 166). Disciples drew a parallel from his own life: "Several disciples recounted a similar story about the saint Baba Farid Ganj-i Shakkar who also met his future wife in a prescient dream" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 79, folio 69). A child who opened a closed door found a saintly person who said, "'No, no. Let her come. She is my daughter.'", and was told afterwards that the man was Baba Sahab (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 80, folio 70). A Malaysian pilgrim of 1979 was told that "in Pakpattan nobody's requests are rejected", and in the shrine received illuminations and fell: "I sensed that I was unconscious, but I could still hear people talking around me" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 157, folio 149). Of Shahidullah Faridi at the shrine at the time of the *'urs*, as people came out of the Gate of Heaven, a narrator recalls the words "These are the real universities, these places. Real knowledge is given to you through the attention of the saint, through inspiration." (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 220, folio 212).

Rizvi dismisses some legends of the family: "Their myths related to the performance of supernatural feats such as flying through the air and to the conversion of huge numbers of Hindus, the latter being quite contrary to the Chishti mission" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 27, folio 13). In Waris Shah's *Hir*, as Usborne gives it, Shakarganj is one of the Five Pirs who meet Ranjha — "First came Khwaja Khizr, lord of all the waters, then Shakarganj, the holy saint of Pakpattan" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 10, folio 9) — and gives him a kerchief (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 11, folio 10); at the crisis "at midnight he remembered the Five Pirs. He kissed the handkerchief of Shakarganj and took the ring of Lal Shahbaz" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 50, folio 49). Shackle names among the Five Pirs "Farid Shakarganj of Pakpattan", and notes that they "are believed collectively to possess special powers" and that "Their miraculous interventions on behalf of Ranjha are a notable feature of Varis Shah's Hīr" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 186, folio 161, n. 2 on 122). Omar Kasmani records that Lal is said to have met him, one of three such figures, at Chothanbi, and adds: "Their shrines are spread across southern Punjab in Pakistan." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 188, folio 171, ch. 1 n. 17).

## In Verse

Waris Shah opens his romance of Hir and Ranjha with Baba Farid. In Usborne's English the Invocation runs:

*Praise also to the beloved Pir Chishti Shakarganj. When Shakarganj made his abode at Pakpattan, the Punjab was delivered of all its troubles.* (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 2, folio 1)

The book's annotator objects: "Much as one would like to agree with Waris Shah this is not historically correct. The Punjab has had plenty of troubles even after Baba Farid honoured it by making it his home and his place of burial!" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 2, folio 1). At the poem's close Waris Shah credits the saint with the work itself, "With the aid of Shakarganj", writing that "I have conferred this benefit on the world" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 57, folio 56). Abbas notes that Waris Shah gives the story "a formulaic initiation for the poem, with an ode to God and to Love, obedience to the prophet Muhammad and to his four companions, and more specifically to Pir Chishti Shakarganj or Baba Farid of Pakpattan" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 123, folio not stated).

The qawwals of the Chishti order sing his name in Amir Khusrau's Hindi song, which Qureshi transcribes. Its second verse runs "Sadqa Baba Ganj-e-Shakar ka / Rakh le laj hamari (Nijam Piya)", which she translates (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 46, folio 26):

*In the name of Ganj-e-Shakar / Protect our honour (Nijam, Beloved)* (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 46, folio 26)

In another song, "Ganj-e-Shakar ke rup men, Mahbub piyara sowat hai" (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 51, folio 31):

*In the likeness of Ganj-e-Shakar [see note 6 above], the dear Beloved is asleep* (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 51, folio 31)

Qureshi observed that the stanza naming him "is certain to yield offerings, since it constitutes an appeal to the saint in the very name of his own spiritual guide, Baba Farid", the saint being Nizamuddin (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 187, folio 167), and that the song, "both text and music, is part of the Qawwal Bachche's special heritage. It is widely sung within the entire Chishti" order (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 47, folio 27).

A qawwali in the United Kingdom that Abbas describes opens by invoking him. She transliterates the line "Sir devĩ te vafā na mangĩ-ehī pīr Farīd dā dāse" and translates (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 88, folio not stated):

*Give thy head, expect no loyalty in return— / O, this is Farid, the Saint's wisdom* (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 10, folio not stated)

Another line of the same performance, "Yār Farīd ābād thī vaṇ o jhok-rab phir vīrān basāe", she renders as running text:

*O lover Farid, alive were those habitations once—may God bring the wilderness to life again!* (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 88, folio not stated)

"Had it not been for the very sacred frame at the beginning, when his ensemble invoked Baba Farid, a researcher might have taken the performance for a secular love song where a lover seeks a beloved and serenades her" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 89, folio not stated).

When Sher Khan turned against him, Baba Farid is said to have recited:

*'Alas! You have no knowledge about me; / When you would gain knowledge about me, you would repent.'* (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 163, folio 145)

The older books carry verse about him. The *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* prints a versified Chishti chain in which he follows Qutb al-Din and precedes Mahbub-e Ilahi, with these lines (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 36–37):

از کمال نور[OCR?] رخ و ہمچون فرید الدین شدی

صد ہزاراں [illegible] شکر بار[OCR?] آمدے

They speak of the perfection of light in a face, of becoming like Farid al-Din, and of sugar showered by the hundred thousand; the second line is partly illegible in the printed text. The book also prints a chronogram with this line (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 38):

بامدادِ[OCR?] فرید الدین شکر گنج

The line names Farid al-Din Shakar Ganj; its first word is uncertain in the printed text. After giving his death year as 663 AH, the numerals uncertain in the printed text, the book prints a *qit'a-e tarikh*, a chronogram verse, by a Mufti Ghulam Sarwar, the name uncertain in the printed text, which turns on the words *ganj*, treasure, *shakar*, sugar, and *ganj-e shakar* (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 234–235). The Persian chronogram that follows Lahori's notice of the tomb ends both its couplets on the same words, which name Farid a peerless one (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 89, folio 77 [marker 87]). The saying on Jamal al-Din's torn certificate, that what Jamal has torn Farid cannot stitch, comes down in three forms: in Lahori with Farid named (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 91, folio 79 [marker 89]), in Sharib's chapter on Sabir with Farid named and another form of the verb (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 79, folio 75), and in another chapter of Sharib in the first person, what Jamal has torn we cannot stitch (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 84, folio 80).

Poets have made him a measure. Schimmel notes that in Panjabi there are *bārahmāsa* about Fariduddin Ganj Shakar of Pakpattan (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 287, folio 269), and that a biographer of Mir Dard wrote that the mountain of Dard's patience would have made Sheikh Farid Ganj-i Shakar "bite his fingers from amazement like sugarcane" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 411, folio 380). The Mughal grandee Bayram Khan "is said to have composed" a four-line poem on Farid's name (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 168, folio 156).

## Other Places Linked to Baba Farid

Several places beyond the Pakpattan shrine are linked to him in the books; none of them is this shrine.

Khalid places his birthplace at Tibba Haji Deen, the old Kothewal, and found there that the birthplace house "is preserved", its courtyard holding graves of the saint's ancestors "back seven generations", that "Baba Farid's parents are also buried here", and that the family of the caretaker Ghulam Shabbir "has been serving this shrine for the past seven generations"; nearby is "another shrine dedicated to the childhood of Baba Farid", a well within a house under an iron cage, on whose railing devotees tie thread (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 150, folio 132). "Because of his presence, this well and its water became sacred" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 150, folio 132). "The shrine at Tiba Haji Deen was older than the shrine of Baba Farid at Pakpattan, but it was less important. The importance, in this case, was determined by the number of followers each shrine had" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 145, folio 127). In *In Search of Shiva* pilgrims walk "barefoot on the dusty roads of the village, out of respect for the holy village"; "The well was covered by an iron cage, on which devotees had tied saffron-coloured threads", and a shrine official said, "Drinking water from this well will cure all your problems!" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated). In the birth-house "In an open courtyard were seven graves", which an old caretaker said were those of "Baba Farid's father, grandfather, uncles, and siblings" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).

Chistian, Khalid writes, is "a small city which was established by Baba Farid's son and named after his school of thought, according to legend" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 154, folio not stated); a local book, the *Tajul Auliya*, says it was founded by his grandson, and that in a jungle outside an old fort "there was a spot where Baba Farid had spent some time in meditation. Later when the saint settled at Pakpattan, he asked his grandson, Tajuddin Chisti to populate the place of his meditation" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated). Tibba Nanaksar, with its gurdwara, lies "about five kilometres from the shrine of Baba Farid", and a descendant of the saint, "Baba Fateh Ullah Shah Noori Chisti", has a shrine there (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 155, folio 137); Iqbal Qaiser told Khalid that pressure "even by the Diwan of the shrine of Baba Farid" led the government to renovate its gurdwara (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 156, folio 138).

In Lahore the *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* tells that he came for ascetic practice and sat on the mound now called Farid Astana, refusing to sit where his pir had sat and saying that he would keep his *chilla* at the side of his feet (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 228). Kanhaiya Lal's history of Lahore names Khwaja Farid Pak Pattani among the elders who came to the mazar of Data Ganj Bakhsh and kept *chillas* there (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 289, folio 292); its table of contents lists a Chilla of Baba Farid Ganj Shakar in Lahore, the name and page uncertain in the printed text (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 5, folio 4), and it uses a *takiya* of Baba Farid Ganj Shakar, the first word uncertain in the printed text, in Lahore as a landmark (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 443, folio 450). Lahori places outside Delhi Gate the mazar of Shah Kaku Chishti, of his progeny (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 99–100, folios 87–88 [markers 97–98]).

Further afield, Ernst and Lawrence record that "one chillah of Baba Farid abuts the shrine of Khwaja Mu'in ad-Din in Ajmer, while another associated with Farid is found outside the tomb of Qutb ad-Din in Delhi" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 109, folio 96), and a staff "which is called the staff" of Farid ad-Din Ganj-i Shakar held at Khuldabad (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 218, folio 207). Rizvi writes that Gisu Daraz ordered a disciple "to perform forty days continuous meditation and penance in the Delhi cell of Baba Farid. The cell was later incorporated into the tomb of Shaikh Qutbu'd-Din" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 276, folio 256). Qureshi describes the Chilla of Baba Farid in Delhi as a working qawwali venue — "The Chilla Mahfil is perhaps the most exclusive or intimate Qawwali event at Nizamuddin Auliya" (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 164, folio 144) — and as her example of "any other locality having an associational link with a saint (such as the Chilla of Baba Farid near the Nizamuddin Auliya shrine)" (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 132, folio 112). Dhillon writes that the city of Faridkot, then known as Mokhalpur, was named after him by its ruler Raja Mokhal, and that the "Baba Sheikh Farid Agman Purb Mela" is held "each year for three days, from 21 to 23 September, commemorating his arrival in" the city (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 172, folio 188). At Uch, Khalid saw him on a banner at Jalaludin's shrine "in a simple white dress with a green shawl on his shoulders" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 282, folio 256).

Near Kallar Kahar in the Salt Range there is a spring and a cave shrine of a "Baba Farid", which Lukas Werth describes without saying whether this is the saint of Pakpattan. "A locally well-known spring near Kallar Kahar is also associated with a shrine: the spring of Baba Farid" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 95, folio 84). The local tradition tells that he asked girls carrying water for a drink; "they teased him and said: ‘The water is bitter.' Three times he repeated his request, with the same answer, at which point he turned the lake's sweet waters bitter (its waters are salty)"; "Then he disappeared into the mountain above, to a lonely place whence nowadays his spring emerges, flowing with sweet water containing healing powers" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, pp. 95–96, folios 84–85). "He is said to have vanished into a cave near the top of the mountain, above the spring, where there is also a small shrine, accessible only after some half-hour's climb" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 96, folio 85).

## Setting

Pakpattan was a river town long before the saint. Eaton places the shrine "on the right bank of the Punjab's most southeasterly river, the Sutlej, roughly halfway between Ferozepore and Bahawalpur" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, pp. 216–217): "The town in which the shrine is located, known since the sixteenth century as Pakpattan, is the ancient city of Ajudhan", and "As the principal ferry point on the Sutlej river, this town served from ancient times as a major nexus for east-west trade between the Delhi region and Multan" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 217). Bhandari's Persian places it in the sarkar of Dipalpur and calls it Patan alias Ajodhan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527). Ernst and Lawrence write that "One had to go to the desert to reach the tomb of Farid ad-Din at Pakpattan/Ajodhan, or that of Hamid ad-Din at Nagaur in Rajasthan" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 99, folio 86), and that the itinerary of Ajmer, Delhi and Pakpattan was "followed in 1352 by Shaykh Zayn ad-Din Shirazi, the main successor to Burhan ad-Din Gharib in Khuldabad" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 98, folio not stated). Khalid writes of the Pakpattan that had once been Ajodhan as "a small insignificant city", next to which "on top of an abandoned mound Baba Farid used to teach his students", while the Sutlej "snaked through" it (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 141, folio 123); in his own day the town lay "a journey of about four hours" by road from Lahore (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated), and Rozehnal puts it "120 miles southwest of Lahore" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 35, folio 25). Khalid also writes of "another shrine of phallic offerings also referred to as Aban Shah near Pakpattan" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The year of his birth.** The site's account gives c. 1173. Lahori's text gives 583 AH; its editor rejects 582 AH, notes 569 AH from *Sair al-Auliya*, and chooses 571 AH from *Fawaid al-Fawad* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 88, folio 76 [marker 86]). Sharib gives 575 AH, and 569 AH as what some have written (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 56, folio 52). Rizvi gives "569/1173-74 or 571/1175-76" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 157, folio 139). Rafat gives 1175 twice (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 25, folio not stated; Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 29, folio not stated) and 1173 twice (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 30, folio 10; Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 242, folio 222). Khalid gives 1173 CE (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 150, folio 132), and Dhillon 1173 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 171, folio 187).
- **The year of his death.** The site's account gives 1266, as do Qureshi (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 101, folio 81), Omar Kasmani (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 188, folio 171, ch. 1 n. 17) and Dhillon (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 171, folio 187). Lahori gives 5 Muharram 664 AH, and his editor affirms it (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 89, folio 77 [marker 87]). Rizvi gives "5 Muharram 664/17 October 1265" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 158, folio 140). The *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* gives 663 AH, the numerals uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 234). Bhandari gives Saturday 5 Muharram 667 AH, at Patan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 526). Sharib gives 5 Muharram 670 AH, and 664 AH as what some have written (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 62, folio 58). Ernst and Lawrence print "d. 664/1265" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 159, folio 147), 1265 elsewhere (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 17, folio 2), and "5 Muharram 634 A.H." (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 169, folio 157). Eaton (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 218), Kugle (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 260, folio 239), Schimmel (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 8, folio 4; Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 377, folio 346; Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 353, folio 335) and Rafat (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 242, folio 222) give 1265. Rozehnal gives 1265 in his text (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 29, folio not stated) and 1295 in his index (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 276, folio 270).
- **His age at death.** Ernst and Lawrence give 95 (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 169, folio 157); Eaton ninety (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 241); Lahori's editor cites *Fawaid al-Fawad* for 93 years (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 88, folio 76 [marker 86]).
- **His century.** Qureshi calls him a "major thirteenth-century saint of the Chishti order in India" (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 263, folio 243), and Rozehnal "his renowned thirteenth-century predecessor" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 175, folio 167); Rafat calls him "The 12th century poet" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 47, folio 27), and Khalid calls him a mystic "from the twelfth century" in one book (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated) and "a Muslim Saint from the twelfth century" in another (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 188, folio not stated), while also writing that he settled at Ajodhan "in the thirteenth century" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
- **The name of his birthplace.** The site's account gives Kothewal. Lahori's book prints Khotwal (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 88, folio 76 [marker 86]), a form perhaps to be read Kathwal, uncertain in the printed text (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 86–87, folios 74–75), and Kahtwal corrected to Kothewal (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 345, folio 333). Sharib and Rizvi have Kahtwal (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 56, folio 52; Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 157, folio 139); Bhandari Khotwal (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 526); Khalid Tibba Haji Deen, formerly Kothewal (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 150, folio 132).
- **Who came from Kabul, and who was Qazi Shuaib.** The site's account says his grandfather came from Kabul. Lahori's author has his father, Jamal al-Din Sulaiman, come from Kabul to Lahore, his grandfather having been martyred (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 88, folio 76 [marker 86]); Lahori's editor has Qazi Shuaib come to Hindustan in 552 AH / 1157 CE (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 87, folio 75 [marker 85]). Sharib makes Qazi Shuaib his grandfather and the qazi of Kahtwal (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 56, folio 52); Lahori's editor makes Jamal al-Din Sulaiman, after Qazi Shuaib's death, the qazi there (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 86–87, folios 74–75); Rizvi makes Qazi Shu'aib his father and the qazi of Kahtwal (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 157, folio 139).
- **His father's name.** Lahori and Sharib give Jamal al-Din Sulaiman (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 86–87, folios 74–75 [markers 84–85]; Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 56, folio 52); Bhandari prints Jalal al-Din Sulaiman (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527); Rizvi names the father Qazi Shu'aib (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 157, folio 139).
- **His mother's name, and who Bibi Sharifa was.** Lahori and Sharib name his mother Qursum Khatun (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 250, folio 238; Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 56, folio 52), and Lahori's editor says her father is named by no contemporary writer (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 87, folio 75). In Thanvi's account as Ernst and Lawrence give it, his mother is Bibi Sharifa, of whom "Her son, Baba Farid" said that she would have been worthy of *khilafat* (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 135, folio 122). Rizvi leaves his mother unnamed and makes Bibi Sharifa his daughter, of whom "Her father" said the same (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 168, folio 150); Sharib also lists a daughter Bibi Sharifa (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 61–62, folios 57–58).
- **His age, and the place, of his initiation.** Sharib has him come to Multan at fifteen (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 58, folio 54) and take *bai'at* in 590 AH, aged fifteen (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 59, folio 55); Rizvi has him settle in Multan at eighteen, reports Jamali's account of initiation at Multan, and thinks it "more likely" that he was initiated at Delhi (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 157, folio 139). Bhandari has him meet the Khwaja at Multan and become his disciple at Delhi (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 526).
- **Whether he followed his master to Delhi at once.** The site's account says that after meeting Khwaja Qutbuddin Bakhtiar Kaki at Multan he followed him to Delhi. Sharib says the Khwaja refused him leave to follow and ordered him to go on with his studies, that he went to Qandahar for five years, and that he came to Delhi in 595 AH (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 58–59, folios 54–55; Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 58, folio 54; Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 59, folio 55). Rizvi reports Jamali's account of a visit to Qandahar and thinks it "more likely" that he reached Delhi shortly after the Khwaja (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 157, folio 139); Bhandari has him go with the Khwaja to Delhi, and reports that some say he first studied at Qandahar and Sistan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 526).
- **Who succeeded Qutbuddin Bakhtiar Kaki.** The site's account says that on his master's death Farid became the head of the order, and Schimmel calls him Bakhtiyar Kaki's successor (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 377, folio 346); Rizvi says the relics implied he was the Khwaja's successor in Delhi, yet "His successor in Delhi was Shaikh Badru'd-Din, who came from Ghazna" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 156, folio 138; Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 158, folio 140).
- **Where he performed the inverted *chilla*.** Rizvi places it in a lonely mosque at Uch (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 158, folio 140), and Ernst and Lawrence's translation in the well of the Hajj mosque of Ucch (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 168, folio 156). Khalid reports the tradition that "he hung himself inside a well not far from the shrine" at Tibba Haji Deen, and a shrine official's denial: "Baba Farid did not hang inside the well, as it is believed" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated). Schimmel says that he performed it (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 377, folio 346); Ernst and Lawrence, through Thanvi, that he "was reputed to have practiced" it (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 135, folio 122). The timing differs too: Rizvi has him suspended "for forty days and nights" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 158, folio 140); Ernst and Lawrence's translation has him suspended "every night" and pulled out at daybreak (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 168, folio 156); Kugle describes the practice as "remaining in an inverted state of contemplation from daybreak to sunset" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 260, folio 239).
- **How he came by the name Ganjshakar.** The site's account: a pebble or a morsel turned to sugar in his mouth during his fasts. Sharib gives two reasons, mud turned to sugar and a merchant's sugar turned to salt and back, and chooses neither (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 57, folio 53). Rizvi (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 158, folio 140) and Schimmel (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, pp. 377–378, folios 346–347) give the pebbles; Ernst and Lawrence the pebbles and the merchant (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 168, folio 156); Bhandari earth turned to sugar under his gaze (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 526); Khalid a myth that he was "extremely soft-spoken" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
- **The renaming of Ajodhan and its meaning.** The site's account gives Pakpattan as "the Holy Ferry"; Eaton "('the holy ferry')", the name dating "by the sixteenth century" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 228); Schimmel "the ferry of the pure", the name in use "ever since" his time (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 377, folio 346); Khalid has the name changed after Baba Farid settled there (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 141, folio 123).
- **Where Pakpattan lies.** The site's account puts it on the old bank of the Sutlej in southern Punjab, and Eaton halfway between Ferozepore and Bahawalpur (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, pp. 216–217); Dhillon calls Pak Pattan "a town near Sirhind" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 171, folio 187).
- **How long he lived at Ajodhan.** Eaton, citing *Siyar al-auliya'*, says "only sixteen or twenty-four years" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 228, n. 43); Rizvi has him there "from about 1236 until his death" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 158, folio 140).
- **Who offered him villages.** Rizvi says Ulugh Khan offered a *farman* of four villages (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 162, folio 144); Sharib that Sultan Nasir al-Din, having visited him, sent a *farman* for villages whose number is uncertain in the printed text (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 61, folio 57). Rizvi also says that Ulugh Khan prevented Sultan Nasiru'd-Din from seeing him in 650/1252-53 (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 162, folio 144).
- **The marriage to Balban's daughter.** Sharib states that Balban married his daughter to him, and names her first of four wives (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 61, folio 57); Rizvi calls the story "hardly historical as the Shaikh died before Ulugh Khan became Sultan Balban" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 162, folio 144).
- **His wives and children.** Sharib names four wives (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 61–62, folios 57–58); Rizvi says "two or three wives" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 167, folio 149). Rizvi and Sharib give five sons and three daughters, Sharib adding a further son, Shaikh Abd Allah; Schimmel says eight children survived (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 378, folio 347).
- **The tokens that passed to Nizamuddin.** Kugle: sent "Upon his death in 1265" to Delhi (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 260, folio 239). Rizvi: handed over in his lifetime, "for at the time of my death you will not be present" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 175, folio 157). Sharib: the robe given at Ajodhan after his death by his son-in-law, under his will (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 62, folio 58).
- **The number of his khalifas.** Seven according to Amir Khwurd, as Rizvi reports (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 168, folio 150), and to Schimmel (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 379, folio 348); more than fifty thousand according to what "some have written", as Sharib reports (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 62, folio 58).
- **Sabir.** Lahori says Farid made him *qutb* of Delhi and later gave him a *farman* for Kaliyar (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 90, folio 78 [marker 88]; Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 91, folio 79 [marker 89]), and Sharib that he gave him the *wilayat* of Kaliyar, and makes Baba Farid his maternal uncle (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 79–80, folios 75–76; Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 78, folio 74). Kugle says Farid was wary of granting him leadership and sent him to the wilderness, and calls him a nephew (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 260, folio 239; Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 259, folio 238). Rizvi calls the torn certificate and the nephew stories legendary (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 172, folio 154), and Ernst and Lawrence report Shaykh 'Abd al-Haqq's doubt that Sabir is the disciple named in *Siyar al-awliya'* (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 74, folio 60).
- **His writings.** Sharib calls *Fawaid al-Salikin* his famous book and lists three *risalas* said to be his (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 63, folio 59). Lahori's editor (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 89, folio 77 [marker 87]) and Rizvi (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 18, folio 4) call *Fawaid al-Salikin*, *Asrar al-Auliya* and *Rahat al-Qulub* spurious; Ernst and Lawrence give Nizam ad-Din's testimony that he wrote no book and list two works only attributed to him (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 178, folio 166; Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 241, folio 231).
- **Whether he composed in Punjabi.** The site's account calls him one of the earliest and greatest poets of Punjabi, and Khalid "the first poet of the Punjabi language" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 153, folio 135). Schimmel writes that "It is possible that Fariduddin composed a few lines of poetry in the local dialect" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 379, folio 348). Rizvi credits him with poetry in Arabic, Persian and Hindawi (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 164, folio 146).
- **The *shlokas* in the Guru Granth Sahib.** The site's account calls them his, and Rafat calls him "the most important poet of the Granth" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 242, folio 222). Rizvi concludes that they "were not composed by one individual" but by "a number of different descendants, all using Farid as their nom de plume" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 351, folio 329). Rafat (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 242, folio 222), Khalid (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 200, folio not stated) and Rizvi (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 350, folio 328) have Guru Arjan compile them into the Granth, Rizvi in 1604; Dhillon has Guru Nanak "personally" include "134 hymns composed by Baba Farid in the Adi Granth" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 171, folio 187). Rizvi counts "more than a hundred" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 350, folio 328).
- **The building and the ground.** Nur Ahmad Chishti describes the tomb as lower than the ground of the town (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 233); Khalid as "located on the top of a mound that overlooks the entire city" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated) and as the highest point of the area (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 154, folio not stated).
- **Which building is the great one.** Eaton calls the grandson 'Ala' ad-Din's tomb "the most imposing structure in the entire shrine complex, dwarfing by far Baba Farid's own tomb" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 221), and elsewhere calls the shrine itself "a magnificent marble edifice constructed in the fourteenth century" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 239). Khalid calls the saint's shrine "a modest building" and the burial place of his progeny behind it "a splendid structure" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
- **Whether every power honoured the shrine.** The site's account says the shrine has been honoured by every power to rule the Punjab. Eaton writes that the Khalji sultans "do not seem to have been interested in the shrine" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 220), and that in 1810 its holdings "were seized by Ranjit Singh" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 231); of the saint himself Eaton writes that he "assiduously avoided contact with the mundane world of the court and its ministers" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 219).
- **The name of the Gate.** The site's account calls it the *Bahishti Darwaza*, a silver-plated door. Eaton calls it "the southern door (shisha darwaza)" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 219, n. 11), the "Gate of Paradise" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 236) and the "Bahishti Gate" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 254); Rozehnal "the narrow Gate of Heaven (bihishti darwaza)" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 36, folio 26). The *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* gives the door a name of its own, which cannot be read with certainty in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 234).
- **Where the Chilla of Baba Farid in Delhi stands.** Qureshi places it "near the Nizamuddin Auliya shrine" (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 132, folio 112) and, in her glossary, attached to Humayun's tomb (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 262, folio 242).
- **Ibn Battuta's visit.** Eaton dates it to 1334 (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, pp. 221–222); Ernst and Lawrence to "around the year 1340" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 105, folio 92).
- **Who comes to the shrine now.** Khalid writes that "Every year, hundreds of Sikhs and Hindus travel to Pakpattan" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 153, folio 135), and also that Sikhs have petitioned for visas to attend the *'urs* "but in vain" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated); Rozehnal that "the shrine is now marked as exclusively Muslim space" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, pp. 35–36, folios 25–26).
- **Women at the qawwali.** Abbas calls qawwali at the Chishtiyya shrines, this one among them, "an exclusively male domain" where "women's presence at such performances is rare" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 84, folio not stated); Khalid, at the qawwali in the courtyard, writes "Here women and men sat together" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
- **The court case of the 1930s.** Eaton's introduction calls it "a 1938 court dispute" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 16); his essay runs it from 6 April 1935 to 2 December 1938 (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 238), with the appeal record dated 1939 (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 239, n. 3). The colonial assessment report he quotes is "An 1897 British Assessment Report" in his text and dated 1896 in his note (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 230, n. 52).
- **The dead of 2001.** Rozehnal gives "more than one hundred" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 29, folio not stated); the newspapers, as Tahir Jehangir reports them, "over forty" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 37, folio 27). The state blamed the Diwan's four-hour delay over the Awqaf annuity (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 39, folio 29); the Diwan's family blamed crowd control, the tragedy coming "some two hours after the Diwan's arrival" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 39, folio 29).
- **Conversion.** Eaton reports that "the vast majority" of the Jat and Rajput groups that became Muslim in the medieval period "claim to have been converted either by Baba Farid or by his contemporary, Baha' al-Haqq Zakariya (d. 1263)" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 227), and Schimmel that "A number of Punjabi tribes still claim to have been converted by Fariduddin Ganj-i Shakar" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 381, folio 350). Eaton doubts the claim, among his grounds the shortness of the saint's stay (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 228); Rizvi rejects the view that he went to Ajodhan to convert "'backward Hindu tribes'" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 159, folio 141) and calls mass conversion "quite contrary to the Chishti mission" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 27, folio 13).

## Legacy

For the people of the Punjab, Baba Farid is not a remote historical figure but a living presence — "Farid" is a household name, his sayings are proverbs, and his verses are sung by qawwals and folk-singers alike. Through his disciple Nizamuddin Auliya and the generations that followed, the Chishti order he helped root in India became the most widespread of all the Sufi paths of the subcontinent; through his Punjabi poetry he became a father of the language’s literature and a shared inheritance of Muslims and Sikhs; and through his shrine at Pakpattan he gave the Punjab one of its enduring centres of pilgrimage. Compassion, poverty, and the sweetness that gave him his name remain the marks of his memory.

Eaton places the tomb among the first holy places of Muslim India: the tombs "of Mu'in ad-Din Chishti at Ajmer, Nizam ad-Din Auliya' at Delhi, and Baba Farid at Pakpattan—became the first Muslim holy places within India", which meant "that South Asian Muslims were no longer compelled to look exclusively to the Middle East for spiritual inspiration" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 236). Pakpattan is one of the three "enormous cults", beside Ajmer and Delhi, on which the order's wealth and power centred (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 265). For the devotees "The shrine was Baba Farid": "many murids referred to the saint and his shrine in the same terms, sometimes defining themselves as murids of Baba Farid, and sometimes referring to themselves as murids of the gaddi, or shrine" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 241). "The shrine, on the other hand, has been there all along, sustaining the powerful baraka of the saint through its line of diwans" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 228). A landholder of Ferozepore District told the court, "As the Sajjada Nashin in office is a representative of Baba Farid, the same sanctity and reverence is shown to the Gaddi Nashin as is shown to Baba Farid" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 247, n. 32). In Eaton's words, "this saint has remained for many centuries an immensely popular 'invisible friend' among millions of villagers inhabiting the southwestern Punjab" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 238), and Rozehnal quotes Eaton's judgement that "The shrine of Baba Farid in Pakpattan provides a striking example of how Islam, the religion par excellence 'of the Book,' has been in one instance mediated among common villagers most of whom were illiterate" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 35, folio 25).

Many clans claim him as the one who made them Muslim. Witnesses before the court named "the Wattu, the Siyal, the Dogar, the Kathia, the Bhatti, and the Tiwana" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 241, n. 9). A cultivator of Lyallpur said, "Our Tiwana tribe was converted by Baba Farid" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 241, n. 10); another, "Baba Farid converted us Bhattis into Islam. Since that time we are the followers of the gaddi and respect the gaddinashin [i.e., sajjada-nishin, or Diwan] in office" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 241, n. 11); and a Wattu villager, "Khewa, one of my ancestors, was converted into Islam by Baba Farid, and since that time we have been following the gaddi in dispute" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 248, n. 35). His cult went with Punjabis far from home: the Mughal officer Mirza Nathan in Bengal was a "faithful disciple" of Farid al-Din Shakarganj, "probably because the writer's ancestors had come from the Punjab where Baba Farid's cult was especially prominent" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 265, n. 10).

He belongs to more than one faith. "The same Shaykh Farid ad-Din is so highly respected by Sikhs that they have incorporated his Punjabi verses for their holy scripture, the Guru Granth Sahib" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 18, folio 3). Abbas: "Although Baba Farid" was "a Muslim Sufi saint who wrote devotional poetry in Panjabi, his texts are revered among the Sikhs because he influenced the founder of their own religion" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 87, folio not stated); "Thus, he is a revered Sufi among the Sikhs as well as among his Muslim devotees" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 89, folio not stated). "Being part of the Granth, Baba Fareed is also given a special status in the Sikh religion and revered like the Gurus" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 188, folio not stated). Khalid writes that Nanak "like Farid, also chose Punjabi as his medium of expression" and was "impressed by the fact that Baba Farid lived a life of simplicity and shunned violence" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 153, folio 135), and that "There is a picture of Baba Farid accompanying the picture of Guru Nanak at the shrine of Guru Nanak at Nankana Sahib" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated). Rizvi explains the Janamsakhis' meeting of Nanak and Farid by "the sufi and Nath belief that great saints did not die but remained accessible to important mystics in later ages through spiritual conversation" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 413, folio 389), and rejects the suggestion that Nanak met a namesake descendant as "far-fetched" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 413, folio 389). Khalid asks "why our heroes cannot be people like Baba Farid, Waris Shah and Bulleh Shah, who spread the message of peace and brought several religious communities together instead of shedding blood" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 14, ch. 8, folio not stated).

His line reaches far. Shaykh Salim Chishti of Fatehpur Sikri belongs to "the Faridi line" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 111, folio 98), and Hazrat Inayat Khan was "from the lineage of Farid ad-Din and Nizam ad-Din" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 18, folio 3). Kugle finds him the only South Asian saint in the ascension oath that Khidr teaches Muhammad Ghawth, sworn "by the Intimacy of Farid al-Din [Ganj-i Shakar] Ajhodani" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 159, folio 138). For the Chishti Sabiri order of today, "the contemporary silsila places the tomb of Shaykh Baba Farid ad-Din Mas'ud Ganj-i Shakkar in Pakpattan at the center of Sufi sacred geography in Pakistan" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 206, folio 198); one of its shaykhs, Shahidullah Faridi, took the name Faridi "to mark his special relationship with the saint in Pakpattan" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 77, folio 67), and its murids contemplate him daily (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 206, folio 198). "Sufi shrines such as Pakpattan serve as vital centers of popular piety—nodal points of local identity and culture." (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 47, folio 37). Ernst and Lawrence's headnote calls him "an extraordinary ascetic, noted for his controlled ecstasy; yet unlike Qutb ad-Din, he was also a loyal father and husband", whose life yields "the three characteristics of pious Chishtis: eschewing all stable income, engaging in meditation, often abetted by music, and training worthy disciples" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 167, folio 155). Schimmel quotes the judgement that "no saint excelled him in his devotions and penances." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 378, folio 347).

He has drawn a long literature. Lahori's editor lists seventeen sources on him, among them *Fawaid al-Fawad*, Amir Khurd's *Sair al-Auliya*, *Akhbar al-Akhyar*, K. A. Nizami's *Life and Times of Sh. Farid-u-Din* (Aligarh, 1955) and Muslim Nizami's *Anwar al-Faridiya*, printed at Pak Pattan in 1965 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 89–90, folios 77–78 [markers 87–88]). Ernst and Lawrence give him twelve entries in their European-language bibliography, the earliest an account by Munshi Mohana Lala in the *Journal of the Asiatic Society of Bengal* of 1836 (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 223, folio 212), and list Miles Irving's article on the shrine in the *Journal of the Panjab Historical Society* of 1911–12 beside Eaton's essay on its authority (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 227, folio 216). Khaliq Ahmad Nizami's biography of the saint is listed by Schimmel (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 293, folio 293), by Rizvi in its Aligarh edition of 1955 (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 454, folio 430) and by Rozehnal in its Delhi edition of 1973 (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 241, folio 234). Shackle lists Gurbachan Singh Talib's *Baba Sheikh Farid Shakar Ganj* (New Delhi: National Book Trust, 1974) (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 204, folio 179), and Rafat cites Muhammad Asaf Khan's study of the provenance of his poetry, published by the Pakistan Punjabi Adabi Board, Lahore, in 1978 (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 242, folio 222). For the Chishti Sabiri order the book about him is Wahid Bakhsh Sial Rabbani's *Maqam-i Ganj-i Shakkar* (Lahore: al-Faisal Publishers, 1994), in which Rabbani translated "the discourses of Farid al-Din Ganj-i Shakkar" into Urdu (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 111, folio 102; Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 250, folio 243).
