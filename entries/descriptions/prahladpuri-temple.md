## Overview

The Prahladpuri Temple stood upon a raised platform at the southern tip of the great fort of Multan, immediately beside the shrine of the Sufi saint Bahauddin Zakariya — an eloquent juxtaposition of Multan's Hindu and Muslim sacred pasts on the same commanding mound. Dedicated to the deity *Narasimha*, the man-lion incarnation of Vishnu, and named for the devotee-prince *Prahlada*, it was among the most storied Hindu temples of the Indus plain, and in popular tradition it is remembered as a place from which the festival of *Holi* took its origin. Today the temple survives only as a ruin, its destruction in the late twentieth century having severed a line of worship that legend traced back to the beginnings of the world's ages.

Two books by Haroon Khalid describe the temple, though neither uses the name Prahladpuri. In *Walking with Nanak* it is "The temple of Prahlad Bhagat", "one of the most important Hindu temples" of Multan, with the shrine of Bahauddin Zakariya "located beside the temple" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 123, folio 105). In *A White Trail*, whose chapter "Holi at Multan" is given to the city and its Hindus (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 5, folio not stated), it is the temple built "in the memory of Prahlad" and "the historical Prahlad Temple" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 20, folio not stated; p. 24, folio not stated). Both books identify it by its place beside Bahauddin Zakariya's shrine and by Prahlada's name; neither names Narasimha, the deity to whom the temple is dedicated. The other books consulted speak of Multan, of its Hindus, or of the Narasimha myth, but not of this temple; what they say is given below as the setting of the temple, not as an account of it.

## Mythology and Origins

The temple belongs to the great cycle of the *Narasimha* legend, one of the best-known narratives of Vaishnava devotion. As the story is told, the *asura* king *Hiranyakashipu* had won a boon that made him all but invincible and demanded to be worshipped as a god, but his own son *Prahlada* remained steadfastly devoted to Vishnu. Enraged, the king sought to destroy the boy, whose faith was at last vindicated when Vishnu burst forth from a pillar in the royal court in the form of *Narasimha*, half-man and half-lion, to slay the tyrant at a threshold that was neither inside nor out, at a twilight that was neither day nor night — circumventing every term of the boon. According to local tradition it was Prahlada himself who raised the temple at Multan in thanksgiving, enshrining an image of Narasimha upon the spot.

Khalid's account of the temple's beginning differs. In *A White Trail* he writes: "His devotees built a temple in the memory of Prahlad at the spot where he supposedly killed his father – on a mound outside the city, which later became a popular spiritual site with mystics and saints" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 20, folio not stated). Hiranyakashipu, in his telling, is "the tyrant who is believed to have ruled the city of Multan thousands of years ago" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 19, folio not stated), and the city "continues to move forward unaware of its ancient past and the mythical association that it has with the story of Hiranyakashipu and Prahlad Bhagat" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 20–21, folio not stated). A photograph in the same book carries the caption "Celebrating Holi in the city of Hiranyakashipu and Prahlad Bhagat." (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 108, folio not stated).

The man-lion himself appears elsewhere in Khalid's book, far from Multan: in the Sri Valmiki shrine at Bahawalnagar he saw "a picture of Narasimha, tearing Hiranyakashipu apart" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 36, folio not stated). Jürgen Schaflechner, writing on the goddess Hinglaj, names the myth only in passing, as "the famous myth of Narasimha, canonized in the Bhāgavata Purāṇa" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 229, folio not stated), and of another story he writes that it is one "which is clearly a reference to the legend of Narasimha in the Bhāgavata Purāṇa" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 230, folio not stated). Neither passage concerns Multan or this temple.

Bound to this is the tradition that connects Multan with the origins of *Holi*. The bonfire of *Holika* — the demoness sister of Hiranyakashipu, who sought to burn the child Prahlada but was herself consumed while he emerged unharmed — is remembered in the spring festival of colour and fire, and Multan's temple was long held to be the very stage of that deliverance. Whatever weight one gives to these legends as history, they made Prahladpuri a site of unusual resonance in the sacred geography of the subcontinent.

## The Temple and the Mound

Khalid makes the temple the reason the mound became holy. Because the mound was linked with the temple, he writes, "the area came to be considered holy", and it drew Muslim mystics, among them Bahauddin Zakariya, "whose shrine came to be situated next to the temple" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 20, folio not stated). In *Walking with Nanak* he sets the two side by side in a city that was, in Guru Nanak's day, "one of the biggest cities of Punjab and was known for the religious shrines of Muslim saints, and Jain and Hindu temples. It was a sacred space for people of all religious denominations" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 123, folio 105).

The order of the two sites has since reversed. The temple, Khalid writes, "now lies in obscurity under the shadow of this massive shrine" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 20, folio not stated), and he calls it, "even though in shambles now", "an anomaly next to the shrine, an unacceptable act of treason in 'Muslim' Pakistan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 20, folio not stated).

## History and Destruction

For centuries the temple was a living centre of worship, repaired and rebuilt across the vicissitudes of Multan's turbulent history. During the siege of Multan in 1848, in the campaigns that preceded the British annexation of the Punjab, a shell struck a powder magazine within the fort and the ensuing explosion is said to have carried away the temple's roof; in the aftermath the East India Company held the fort directly for some years before restoring the shrines to their communities around 1852. The building suffered again amid communal violence in 1881, when it was set alight, only to be renovated once more by Multan's Hindu community.

Its end came in December 1992. In the wave of reprisals across Pakistan that followed the demolition of the Babri Masjid at Ayodhya in India, a mob attacked and destroyed the Prahladpuri Temple and its adjoining *dharamshala*, reducing the historic structure to rubble. The site of the temple is now held by the Evacuee Trust Property Board, the state body that administers the properties of communities displaced at Partition, and proposals for its restoration have been discussed from time to time without a full rebuilding having been achieved.

Khalid gives the temple's recent history in other terms. "The temple stands even today, a worn down building with several cracks in its walls. It has remained abandoned ever since partition, occasionally becoming victim to the passion of fanatic mobs like the one in 1992" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 20, folio not stated). Of the days after the demolition of the Babri Mosque, dated in his book to the "6th of December 1992", he writes more generally that "Well-known Hindus living in the cities of Punjab had to hide for several months after the incident" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 22, folio not stated). One of his informants in Multan, Parvati Devi, "a 44 year old classically trained professional singer from Multan, who is also a social activist", recalls a mob in those days that had "destroyed the Valmiki temple" near her house and wanted to burn her alive (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 17, 21, folio not stated); afterwards she "took up a Muslim name, Shaila Bibi" and stopped appearing on radio and television (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 21, folio not stated). Her account names the Valmiki temple, not the Prahlad temple.

## The Temple Today

Khalid's informants describe who holds the temple and what has been built on its ground. According to Parvati Devi, the temple "comes under the jurisdiction of the Awqaf department (a government institution that looks after all the historical Muslim shrines and mosques of the country)", and she says: "It is our demand that the temple be restored and given to the Hindus of the city who are its real owners" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 24, folio not stated). She recalls that "in 2006 and 2008" the authorities of Bahauddin Zakariya's shrine "extended the shrine into the premises of the historical Prahlad Temple by constructing a place for ablutions first and then a Langar Hall" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 24, folio not stated).

The matter reached the courts. Khalid records that "25 year old Usman Chaudary, President of the RAS (Organisation for Religious and Sexual Minorities) society filed a petition in the High Court", that "the Court passed a stay order against any further construction on the premises of the temple", and that the RAS Society "has petitioned that the temple be handed back to the Hindu community in the city" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 24, folio not stated). The book does not say how the petition ended.

## Holi at Multan

Khalid's chapter on Holi follows the festival that tradition ties to this temple into the Multan of his own day; the celebrations he describes are held in a Hindu locality and before the city's press club, not at the temple. The "thirty odd women" who burned the Holika, he writes, "are now the only link that the city has with that story"; these Hindus "lived here before Multan became a part of Pakistan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 21, folio not stated). The locality where he watched the festival, "situated next to the Railway station, is one of several areas where the Hindu community of the city is located"; the community numbers "little more than a thousand", and "there isn't a centralized celebration"; the settlement has "about a hundred houses", "only about ten to twelve belonging to Hindu families" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 23, folio not stated). According to Parvati Devi, "There were several historical temples in Multan from the pre-partition days. All of them have become ruins now", and the community has turned other spaces "into make-shift temples" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 24, folio not stated).

The public festival, in Khalid's account, had lapsed and returned. "As recently as 2010" festivals including Holi "were not celebrated in the city"; that year Usman organised "a token celebration of Holi in front of the Multan Press Club" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 24–25, folio not stated). Holi, "even though on a small scale is now celebrated every year in Multan" and is "regularly featured in the local media"; Usman has been alleged to be "an Indian agent", and "More than once he along with his friends has been physically attacked" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 25, folio not stated).

## Setting: Multan

The books' accounts of Multan speak of the city, not of the temple, but they show the place in which it stood. Harish Dhillon, retelling the Janamsakhis of Guru Nanak, calls Multan one of the oldest cities in South-East Asia, a city where three rivers merge before the Indus and "a commercial hub for national and international trade, including with Persia, Central Asia and the Arab world" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 165, folio 181). He writes that "During the time of the Mahabharata, it was the capital of the Tuganta kingdom", that legend has Alexander struck by an arrow there, that "For the next thousand years, Multan was ruled by Hindu and" Buddhist kings, and that the Chinese traveller Heun Tsang, as the book spells him, visited it in A.D. 641; he calls it the city of gold, its Suraj temple "Claimed to be by far the biggest temple in the subcontinent" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 165, folio 181). In the eleventh century, he writes, Mahmud of Ghazni damaged the Suraj temple and destroyed "the giant idol", and in time the town became studded with mosques as it had once been with Hindu temples (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 166, folio 182). By the time Nanak came to Multan, "the city was famous as a major centre of Sufi learning and philosophy" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 167, folio 183).

Annemarie Schimmel gives 711 as the year in which "the Arabs conquered Sind and the adjacent provinces northward up to Multan" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 375, folio 344). Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi writes that "Multan had become a dependency of the Fatimids of Egypt", that "by the middle of the eleventh century, sufism had penetrated into the areas surrounding Multan", and that "in 1175 Mu‘izzu'd-Din Muhammad bin Sam seized Multan" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 128, folio 110). Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, under the heading "Suba Dar al-Aman Multan", says of the city that it is one of the ancient cities and has a fort of brick; he adds that besides the saints he names, many saints lie at rest in that blessed city (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528).

The nineteenth-century wars that the temple's own history records touched the city as a whole. Carl Ernst and Bruce Lawrence note that Hafiz Muhammad Jamal (d. 1811), a successor of Nur Muhammad, "is also said to have taken part in resistance to Sikh attacks on Multan" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 121, folio 108). Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti's *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* records the Sikh conquest of Multan in a year written out in words, apparently as eighteen hundred and eighteen, with Kharak Singh sent against the city (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 580); when Ranjit Singh conquered Multan, he writes, Nawab Muzaffar Khan was killed, and others of the family, Sarfraz Khan among them by an uncertain reading, were captured with their households and came to Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 130). In a year printed uncertainly as 1848, Diwan Mulraj, son of Sawan Mal, governor of Multan, raised a disturbance and had Agnew Sahib Bahadur with another sahib, and Kahan Singh, the new governor of Multan, killed; the readings of both names are uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 592). Chishti traces the troubles of that year from Multan through Hazara and four hard battles to a date printed, uncertainly, as 29 March 1849, when, as he puts it, the whole country passed into the English dominion, and he closes with a *qitʿa* on the departure of Maharaja Dalip Singh, one of whose lines speaks of his going from the Punjab (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 592).

Multan also enters the verse of the region, though never this temple. In Naveed Alam's translation of a kafi of Madho Lal Hussein, the city is named as the place where one item of a bride's trousseau was bought:

*The dress / someone carried across the snows / all the way from Kashmir. The dress / someone brought from Gujarat… The dress bought in Multan* (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 51, folio 51).

Schimmel writes of the vernacular poets of Pakistan that for them "Mecca and Medina (and for the Sindhi and Panjabi, also Multan) are inside the heart" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 178, folio 160).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **Who built the temple.** The site's account gives the local tradition that "it was Prahlada himself who raised the temple at Multan in thanksgiving". Khalid writes that "His devotees built a temple in the memory of Prahlad" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 20, folio not stated).
- **What happened on the spot.** The site's account tells that Vishnu, as Narasimha, slew Hiranyakashipu, and that Prahlada raised the temple in thanksgiving "upon the spot". Khalid places the temple "at the spot where he supposedly killed his father", the "he" being Prahlad (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 20, folio not stated).
- **Where the mound lay.** The site's account sets the temple "at the southern tip of the great fort of Multan". Khalid sets it "on a mound outside the city" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 20, folio not stated).
- **To whom the temple belongs.** The site's account dedicates the temple to Narasimha and names it for Prahlada. Khalid calls it "The temple of Prahlad Bhagat" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 123, folio 105) and the temple "in the memory of Prahlad" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 20, folio not stated), and does not name Narasimha in connection with it.
- **When worship ceased.** The site's account has the temple as "a living centre of worship" for centuries, renovated by Multan's Hindu community after 1881, until its destruction in December 1992 "severed a line of worship". Khalid writes that it "has remained abandoned ever since partition" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 20, folio not stated).
- **Whether the building still stands.** The site's account says the 1992 mob reduced "the historic structure to rubble" and that the temple "survives only as a ruin". Khalid writes that "The temple stands even today, a worn down building with several cracks in its walls", the 1992 mob being one of those to which it has "occasionally" fallen victim (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 20, folio not stated).
- **Who holds the site.** The site's account says the temple's site "is now held by the Evacuee Trust Property Board". Khalid's informant Parvati Devi says the temple "comes under the jurisdiction of the Awqaf department" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 24, folio not stated).

## Legacy

The ruins of Prahladpuri remain among the most poignant of Pakistan's lost Hindu monuments, a fragment of the deep Hindu antiquity of Multan — a city whose very name is bound in classical sources to a famed sun-temple, and whose fort mound has borne temples and shrines in almost unbroken succession for millennia. That the remains of the Narasimha temple and the tomb of a great Muslim saint share the same eminence is itself a lesson in the layered religious history of the Indus valley. For the Hindu communities of Pakistan and for pilgrims and scholars abroad, Prahladpuri endures as a place of memory: a temple whose legend gave the subcontinent one of its most joyous festivals, and whose fate records one of the harder chapters of the region's recent past.

For Khalid the temple's legacy is its claim on the city's memory, and the claim of the city's Hindus on the temple. Multan, he writes, goes on "unaware of its ancient past" and of its tie to the story of Hiranyakashipu and Prahlad Bhagat (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 20–21, folio not stated); the demand his informant puts to him is that the temple "be restored and given to the Hindus of the city who are its real owners" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 24, folio not stated).
