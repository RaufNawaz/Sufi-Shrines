## Overview

At Sehwan Sharif, in Sindh, stands the great shrine of Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar (c. 1177–1274) — perhaps the most ecstatically beloved saint in all of Pakistan, and the object of one of its largest and most exuberant pilgrimages. A wandering *qalandar* — a mystic of the free, rapturous, rule-breaking kind — Lal Shahbaz drew Sindh into the world of Sufi devotion, and his memory is celebrated in the thunder of the *dhamal*, the trance-dance beaten out on the great drums of his shrine, and in the anthem *Dama Dam Mast Qalandar*, which has carried his name across the world. His golden-domed darbar is the heart of Sehwan, and its urs one of the defining festivals of the Sindhi year.

The books consulted bear out that fame from many sides. Omar Kasmani, whose book takes the saint as its whole subject, calls him "Pakistan's most beloved figure of devotion, whose tomb-shrine in Sehwan, a town of about a hundred thousand residents, draws millions each year" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 20, folio 3), and sums up the town in one sentence: "Sufi by renown, Shivaite in heritage, and increasingly Shiʿi in following, Sehwan is in many ways Pakistan at its complex best." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 20, folio 3). H. T. Sorley, in his study of Shah Abdul Latif, names him "the famous Lal Shahbaz, or Jivelal", the 'living Pir', "whose shrine at Sehwan attracts a great gathering of both Mussulmans and Hindus every year in a strange mixture of worship" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 257, folio 248). For Annemarie Schimmel, his settling at Sehwan is where the mystical history of Sindh begins: "From the day that Lal Shahbaz Qalandar settled in Sehwan in the 13th century … Sind had produced an immense number of mystics, whose shrines are scattered all through the Indus Valley" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 25, folio 21). Michel Boivin makes the town one of the three case studies of his chapter on shared shrines: "Sehwan Sharif, a main Sufi pilgrimage centre of Sindh, where the darbar of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar is located" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 179, folio 166).

Kasmani gives the measure of the figure in a few lines: "Saints, even if ubiquitous, are divisive figures in Pakistan, though none is more vexing than the saint in question" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 20, folio 3), and "Lal Shahbaz Qalandar is a plurivocal figure and a ubiquity hard to miss. He embodies a historical continuum and bridges multiple and oftentimes contradicting genealogies attributed to him" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, pp. 57–58, folios 40–41). "Lal and Sehwan are inseparable figures, always in narration." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 165, folio 148).

## The Life of the Saint

Sayyid Usman Marwandi — known to the world as Lal Shahbaz Qalandar — was born around 1177 at Marwand, in the Persian-speaking lands to the west (in the region of present-day Afghanistan/Iran), into a Sayyid family tracing descent from the Prophet through the Imams. Steeped from youth in learning and devotion, he took to the path of the wandering dervishes and travelled widely through the Muslim world — through Iraq, the Hejaz, and Central Asia — before coming to Sindh and settling at Sehwan, an ancient town with a deep pre-Islamic sacred history. There he lived as a *qalandar*, drawing crowds by his ecstatic sanctity, and there he died around 1274. He is called *Lal* (“the Red”) for the crimson robe of his order, and *Shahbaz* (“royal falcon”) for the soaring freedom of his spirit; and he is counted, in Sindhi and Punjabi tradition, among the “four friends” (*chaar yaar*) of the qalandari way, alongside such saints as Baba Farid and Bahauddin Zakariya, with whom legend links him in companionship.

The books stress, in their own words, how little of this life is documented. Kasmani writes that "historical references to Lal are few and far between, a patchwork at best of brief mentions found in other saints' biographical texts", and that "he evades hagiographical hold and historical record. Mystery suits him better than history" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 62, folio 45); elsewhere, "the history of the saint of Sehwan is at best a patchwork of brief references, some found in sparse historical accounts, some in other saints' hagiographies." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 165, folio 148). He sends readers to Michel Boivin for "a detailed biography of the saint (in French)" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 184, folio 167). Sorley notes that Jethmal Parsram gave "some account of him and his companions" in his book *Sind and its Sufis*, "but the facts are meagre" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 299, folio 290), and judges that between "the thirteenth century when Lal Shahbaz flourished and the eighteenth century" there is "very little that can be said to be securely established in the way of verified historical fact" about the Sufi poets of Sindh (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 299, folio 290).

Even his name is contested. Kasmani writes that "his followers cannot quite agree on his doctrinal persuasion, Sufi affiliations, not even his actual name, Sayyid Usman Marwandi. The saint is better known by the three-part epithet Lal Shahbaz Qalandar." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 35, folio 18), and that "matters like the saint's origin or real name… are actively debated in Sehwan" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 62, folio 45). In a note he records that "Custodian families in Sehwan take the position that the saint's actual name was most likely Shah Hasan and not Usman, the latter commonly associated with the Sunni", and that "The title Lal Shahbaz as reference to the saint is first recorded in late seventeenth-century textual sources" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 184, folio 167, intro n. 33). Sorley gives the personal name once, as Sayid Usman Marwandi, with the word 'Shah' printed before Marwandi in a damaged line, a man "who became famous as" Lal Shahbaz "with a popular shrine at Sehwan" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 298, folio 289); he also quotes Jethmal Parsram's form of the name, 'Kalandar Lal Marwandi' (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 299, folio 290). Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi writes instead that "Lal Shahbaz's real name was Mir Saiyid 'Usman and he was a native of Sehwan in the Sind" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 328, folio 306), and gives no 'Marwandi'.

The two halves of the epithet are explained in more than one way. Rizvi: "Often he wore a red dress, hence his name lal (red). Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya gave him the title 'Shahbaz,' the noblest species of falcon." (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 328, folio 306). Kasmani: "Lal (red, dear one) or Laʿl (ruby, gem), though different words and both ascribed to the saint, are indistinguishable in Sehwan since there is no difference in pronunciation." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 182, folio not stated, intro n. 5). As one fakir learned it, Kasmani writes, "red was the color associated with Lal, whose variant Laʿl means ruby, or that Shah-baz (or royal falcon) referred to a miracle attributed to the saint." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 64, folio 47). Boivin records "Mast qalandar", the name of the song, used once as a name for the saint himself (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 141, folio 128).

Kasmani, following Boivin, writes that "he is most likely to have been born in Marwand, near Tabriz (in present-day Iran), and his lineage through Jafar al-Sadiq's son Ismaʿil makes him an important figure of the Ismaʿili line of the Shiʿi faith", adding: "This also means that he is a descendant of Ali and thus related to the Family of the Prophet (ahl-e bayt)" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 62, folio 45). Boivin's one biographical statement is a negative one: "Despite his fame, Lal Shahbaz was not Sindhi since he was born in Persia and his alleged diwan is composed in Persian." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 213, folio 200, Ch. 4 n. 4). Rizvi, as above, makes him a native of Sehwan.

Kasmani gives his death once, in his own voice, as "d. 1274 ce", and calls him "a thirteenth-century mystic known for his radical rejection of Islamic religious institutions, norms, and practices." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 20, folio 3); everywhere else he gives only the century. The Library of Congress cataloguing heading printed on his book's copyright page gives '1177–1274' (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 5, folio not stated); Kasmani himself never states a birth year. Schimmel's index to *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* prints his death as 1262 (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 520, folio 489), and the index to *As Through a Veil* gives only '13th cent.' (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 357, folio 339). Sorley, Rizvi, Boivin, Abbas and Schaflechner give no year at all.

Sorley places him in a wave of migration: "From the thirteenth century onwards there was a great immigration into Sind of learned and religious-minded men from the centre of the Muslim world. Lal Shahbaz, who came with several companions, some of whom made names for themselves elsewhere in India, was a man of this kind." (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 302, folio 293). The companions are not named. Schimmel, in *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, sets him in "the same period during which" Muʿīnuddīn Chishtī and Bahāʾuddīn Zakariyā were preaching divine love and union, as the representative of "Another aspect of mystical life" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354).

The dates of his coming are given several ways, and Kasmani joins none of them. "Also unsettled is the year of his arrival in Sehwan, of which 1272 ce is considered to be more reliable" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 62, folio 45); a note adds only "The other date is 1251" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 188, folio 171, ch. 1 n. 13). Following Boivin, Kasmani writes that "he is likely to have first arrived in Multan in 1263", and that he stopped at Makran, Lahut-lamakan, Panjgur and Pasni in Balochistan and at Naing, Lake Manchhar and Sehwan in Sindh (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 188, folio 171, ch. 1 n. 14). "He later settled in Sehwan for the last year or two of his life" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 62, folio 45), and the stories of his burial place it "a year after he arrived in town" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 163, folio 146). Of the town he came to, Kasmani writes that "Sehwan at this time was also under the influence of the Ismaʿilis", which might explain his arrival or his choice to settle there (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 188, folio 171, ch. 1 n. 16), and that "Sehwan at the time of the saint's arrival was dominated by followers of Shiva as well as Daryapanthis, who divinized the Indus River" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 195, folio 178, ch. 5 n. 8).

The books do not agree on his spiritual descent. Rizvi introduces him in a run of qalandars "Among the Suhrawardiyyas" and makes him an exception to his master's rule: "Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din disliked qalandars intensely and certainly never encouraged their unwelcome visits. Lal Shahbaz was an exception and was adopted as his disciple." (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 328, folio 306). Kasmani reports that "Lal bears at least three genealogies — Sufi, Shiʿi, and Shivaite", and that "Lal's Shiʿi descent through the Ismaʿili line is well established; however, he is also claimed through Suhrawardi and Qadiri Sufi registers." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 35, folio 18). "In Sufi terms, Lal is claimed by both Suhrawardi and Qadiri orders and is popularly regarded as one of a quartet of mystics, chahar-yar or 'four friends.'" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 62, folio 45). "For many fakirs in Sehwan, Lal is an original thirteenth-century qalandar, who they believe was initiated at the hands of the order's founding figure, Jamal al-Din Savi of Damascus." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 62, folio 45); yet "historical sources do not confirm that Lal observed chahar-zarb or the fourfold shave typical of the early qalandars" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 62, folio 45). In his notes: "According to the Suhrawardi Sufi tradition, the saint was initiated in Multan by Bahaʾuddin Zakaria", and custodians in Sehwan "claim a loose affiliation with some Qadiri strands though no formal initiations into the order are made." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 184, folio 167, intro n. 34).

Boivin names a master for him in Sindh, with his own hedge: at the Tando Ahmad Khan entrance, he writes, stand shrines to Shiva, to Raja Vir and to "Shah Sadr, who is said to have been the Sufi Master of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar in Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 185, folio 172). He names a successor, too: "Sayyid Ali Sarmast, the successor of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar", from whom Qutub Ali Shah descended on the maternal side (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 56, folio 43). The only order-name Boivin attaches to him in his text is that of a path at Sehwan, where "a Hindu disciple of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar initiates Muslims to the path (tariqa) of Qalandariyya shahbaziyya" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 20, folio 7); a chart in his annex sets Sehwan Sharif under the Qalandariyya, and he comments that "The Qalandariyya is not so widespread over it, but in the case of Sindh, it reflects the radiation of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar and the pilgrimage centre of Sehwan Sharif" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 227, folio 214).

Others place him outside the orders altogether. Rizvi: "Following the Shari'a held no interest for Lal Shahbaz who like the malamatis, indulged in ecstatic dancing." (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 328, folio 306). Haroon Khalid writes that he "belonged to the school of thought of Malamti Sufis, who do not believe in any form of organised religion and consider themselves to be beyond the pale of any religious law. Both Bahaudin and Farid would have had serious differences with Lal Shahbaz Qalandar of Sehwan Sharif" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 282, folio 256), and in another book calls him "the malamati saint, Shahbaz Qalandar" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).

Kasmani writes that he "was a celibate figure and thus without issue" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 82, folio 65). The fakirs of Sehwan make him the type of celibacy: "Because he [the saint] did not marry either, he had cast it [his loins] in iron" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 139, folio 122). The legal consequence is set out under the custodians below. Sorley calls him a *kalandar* who won a great reputation for sanctity both in his lifetime and after his death (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 299, folio 290).

Sorley states that "He was the author of a number of works used for the instruction of youth in a later age, as noted by Burton." (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 298, folio 289), without naming them there. In his account of the Sindhi madressa syllabus he names, as its first books, the *Mizan-i-sarf* "by Lal Shahbaz, the famous scholar and saint of Sehwan"; then the *Ajnas* or *Munshaib*, on the rules for forming the increased derivatives, and a work on the verbs whose title is damaged in the printed text, both of them, he says, by Lal Shahbaz and written in Persian; and then the *Akd*, also by Lal Shahbaz, on the permutation of letters, in Arabic mixed with a second language whose name is damaged in the print (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 223, folio 214). No other source consulted mentions these works, and the attribution rests on Sorley alone. Writing of Sindh at large, Sorley records that in the time of the Talpurs the province possessed six 'madressas', one of them at Sehwan (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 225, folio 216).

## Many Names, Many Faiths

Kasmani describes a saint who is many people's: "As a descendant of Ali, he especially attracts Pakistan's Shiʿa. In the guise of a Shiva incarnate, the Hindus revere him. Muhammad, Ali, or Ram, his memory spans religious divides, extends far out from the town of his burial, and is traceable all over Pakistan" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, pp. 57–58, folios 40–41). "Hindus consider him to be a Shivaite ascetic who epitomizes a particular ideal of ascetic renunciation" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 62, folio 45); "Lal, the saint of Sehwan, is a placeholder for Ali, if not an avatar of Ali himself" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 156, folio 139); and "the saint is known as Jhule Lal, a name originally directed at the river deity of the Indus" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 162, folio 145). "Lal, I would argue, is a figure of historial abundance, evading as often as he recurs in Shiʿi, Sufi, or Hindu guises." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 63, folio 46).

Boivin makes Sehwan his type case of a shrine shared under two names: "In Sehwan Sharif, for example, there is only one place of worship but the saint is denominated Lal Shahbaz Qalandar by Muslims and Raja Bharthari (or Raja Vir) by Hindus." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 50, folio 37). Elsewhere: "In Sehwan Sharif, Lal Shahbaz Qalandar is said to be Raja Barthari, or Raja Vir." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 178, folio 165); and he is "the Sufi saint of Sehwan Sharif who is worshipped by the local Hindus as Raja Barthari, a main character of the Nathpanth." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 200, folio 187). Boivin glosses neither Hindu name and gives no source for them. Of this kind of shrine he writes: "In other places, there is only a tomb, but the Hindus give the saint a distinct identity… In this configuration, there is only one annual fair, in which the Hindus play a leading ritual role." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 178, folio 165).

The name Jhulelal comes up again and again, and Boivin declines to go further than the name: "It is tempting to think of a connection, or even of a fusion, between Lal Shahbaz Qalandar and Jhulelal, but none of the sources allow for us to go that far. That said, both are today called Jhulelal." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 216, folio 203, Ch. 7 n. 5). At Tando Ahmad Khan a local follower told him of "a 'Hindu dargah' devoted to Lal Shahbaz Qalandar, here worshipped as Jhulelal" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 184, folio 171); "While the figure of Lal Shahbaz is dominant, insofar as the shrine is a replica of the Sehwan one, he has been given the identity markers usually attributed to Jhulelal." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 193–194, folios 180–181). Sorley, for his part, once writes of "the shrine of the Kalandar Shah Lal Baz (or Udero Lal) at Sehwan" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 168, folio 159), and his index refers 'Udero Lal' to Lal Shahbaz (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 441); he gives no ground for the equation, and it rests on that one parenthesis and that one cross-reference. His 'Jivelal', glossed 'the living Pir', is a further form whose relation to Jhulelal he does not explain (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 257, folio 248).

## The Shrine

Lal Shahbaz’s shrine at Sehwan, with its gilded dome and mirrored interior, is among the most important pilgrimage sites in Pakistan.

Kasmani traces the shrine's growth. "the earliest mention of the saint's grave occurs in a fourteenth-century account of the North African traveler Ibn Battuta (d. 1369)" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 81, folio 64). "If one is to follow Boivin's (2011, 30) stage-wise process of the shrine's historical transformation, a first mausoleum was in place as early as 1357 and another was built around 1585" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 81, folio 64); "Such developments suggest how the shrine grew from a nondescript grave site to a center of public pilgrimage", and "The shrine's rise in prominence during the Mughal period and especially its architectural development would have been concurrent with its ascending material prospects" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 81, folio 64). He adds that "the shrine, whether formerly a temple or not, evidences a rich history of Hindu association and patronage" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 36, folio 19).

The building now standing is recent. "the present-day structure was built in the early 1990s, several rounds of repairs prior to and since have been undertaken by the Department of Auqaf" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 82, folio 65). "Plans for the construction of the new mausoleum were eventually executed during the early 1990s during the premiership of Benazir Bhutto… a much larger structure would replace the old Sindhi mausoleum" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 70, folio 53). "The shape of the shrine's golden dome and the form of its minarets replicate those of the mausoleums of Shiʿi imams in Iran and Iraq" — "a curious development, especially when it comes at the hands of a predominantly Sunni state" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 71, folio 54). Kasmani offers an explanation, marked as conjecture: "It might well have been influenced by the Shiʿi devotion of the Bhuttos since both father and daughter were personally involved in architectural and development plans." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 71, folio 54).

Kasmani draws on an undated construction and survey report, 'Redevelopment of the Mazar of Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar at Sehwan Sharif', which he consulted "in the personal collection of Michel Boivin in Chambéry, France, in July 2013" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, pp. 187–188, folios 170–171, ch. 1 n. 11); "It can be assumed that the document was prepared between 1989 and 1992, corresponding with the first government of Benazir Bhutto." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 189, folio 172, ch. 1 n. 33). Its declared models were "Khomeini's Square in Isfahan, Iran, the Alhambra in Granada, Spain, and the Taj Mahal in Agra, India, in addition to shrines of imams and saints in Iran and Iraq" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 189, folio 172, ch. 1 n. 33), and Kasmani reads the result as "the architectural shift from the vernacular to the 'truly Islamic' — as the shrine's construction report describes it" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 71, folio 54).

Kasmani describes the complex as he saw it. The mausoleum has an "elaborate blue-tiled facade" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 21, folio 4). The courtyard is of "white marble" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 25, folio 8), "long rectangular", flanked along its length by "shaded arcades" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 26, folio 9), where "many of the visitors, especially women and poorer families, spent the night" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 104, folio 87). The durbar is "the octagonal audience hall where hundreds circled the tomb of Lal" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 101, folio not stated). Its two prayer corners "weren't really mosques but semiprivate sections reserved for ritual prayer, peripheral spaces of the shrine durbar", set in the space left over where an octagonal layout meets a square plan, and raised enough above the ground to give "an uninterrupted view of the saint's tomb" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 78, folio 61). "A separate room was designated for Shiʿi prayer in an adjacent part of the shrine" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 80, folio 63). He notes "the tall and heavily carved wooden doors of the durbar", with "A metallic lock… wrapped in a red silken cloth" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 83, folio 66), the three doors of the durbar (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 85, folio 68), "the drum and the metal disc placed just where the entrance portal gives way to the open courtyard" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 83, folio 66), "the large donation boxes" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 84, folio 67), and the "secondary entrances", with "a tiny shop right across one of" them (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 21, folio 4). His plan of the shrine, drawn by Yves Ubelmann (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 105, folio 88), labels "Lal's tomb", the "Durbar", the "Courtyards", the "Outer yard", the "Qadimi 'alam" and the "Markets" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 106, folio not stated).

"What used to be an old public square — featuring the qadimi ʿalam, an old structure commemorating the battle of Karbala — was now paved and enclosed with a low wall, creating an outer yard, remodeled as part of an ever-extending shrine complex" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 105, folio 88). "Such ongoing architectural and spatial modification of the shrine, especially at the hands of its administration, was already compromising its organic relation with the bazaar and had greatly altered the place's nightlife" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 105, folio 88).

"Today, hundreds of funerary sheets drape the tomb of the saint, so numerous that no mortal has ever seen the surface of the tomb itself, it is widely claimed in Sehwan." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 163, folio 146). "Citing this legend, many fakirs and prominent sayyid figures in Sehwan do not refer to the shrine as a tomb; instead they call it the throne of Lal (takht)." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 163, folio 146). At the centre of the durbar "stood Lal's tomb-shrine lined with silver, bedecked with silks and rose petals, and from where the saint as emperor (shahanshah or the King of kings) was said to hold court and grant audience" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 77, folio not stated). Shemeem Burney Abbas records a relic that hung over it: "the guluband or heart-shaped necklace that belonged to Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar and that hung over his tomb in Sehwan" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 20, folio not stated), and one of her plates is captioned "Guluband of Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar, Sehwan Sharif" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 164, folio not stated).

"The constant and highly elaborate remodeling of Sehwan's shrine from the 1990s until its latest and ongoing expansion in 2018 is proof of its increasing importance. It also reveals the faith of the state in its economic worth and value" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 72, folio 55). Between 2009 and 2013, "The shrine's facade and its interior had been remodeled; the marble floor of the courtyard was newer and whiter than it was in 2009" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 102, folio 85); by 2018 "the shrine had turned grander in scale; its facades had become more ornate than before, the dome more golden than ever" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 105, folio 88). A room where healing took place has since been demolished to make way for the growing shrine (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 88, folio 71), and "Only in later architectural modifications of the shrine was a prayer room introduced" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 99, folio 82). Kasmani names no architect, contractor or cost.

The rebuilding also took things away. "when, in a 2013 redesign of the tomb hall, the customary five lamps of the saint were not accommodated, it disenfranchised a local Hindu spiritual figure. His twice daily service of lighting lamps, which I had observed in years prior to the renovations, was no longer possible at the shrine. This forms part of a gradual but systematic fading away of the shrine's Hindu character at the hands of the department" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 72, folio 55). Similar measures, he writes, included the removal of the doorbells and of "what some locals have described as a Shiva lingam-like object from the shrine" during early repairs in the 1970s (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 72, folio 55); "Whether such decisions were premeditated or taken arbitrarily is hard to tell" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 72, folio 55). "Explicitly Sunni inscriptions like names of the first four caliphs were introduced in the tomb hall in 2013. These, however, were no longer present when I visited the shrine in 2018, possibly removed when parts of the tomb hall were repaired after the suicide attack of 2017." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 189, folio 172, ch. 1 n. 35).

Whether the shrine stands on a Hindu sanctuary is reported, not settled. Kasmani gives the belief and its rival: "Many in Sehwan believe that where the shrine of Lal now stands is where the high temple of Shiva once stood." "In a different version, however, the location of his tomb marks the stretch of land where Lal is said to have rested on his arrival in town." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 163, folio 146). "His site of burial, the present-day shrine at Sehwan, is thought to replace an ancient Shiva sanctuary." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 35, folio 18). In his notes he weighs it: "Available archaeological evidence does not confirm the claim (see Collinet 2008). However, historians believe that overlaps are likely" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 185, folio 168, intro n. 35), and "Annemarie Schimmel (2003, 355) mentions the presence of a Shiva lingam close to the saint's tomb" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 189, folio 172, ch. 1 n. 34).

Schimmel's own words, in *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, are firmer, and she gives no source for them: the saint settled "at the site of the old Shiva sanctuary at the west bank of the lower Indus", and "A lingam close to the actual tomb proves the Shivait background of some of the rites." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 386, folio 355). Boivin is more guarded: "Many years back, it is said that there was a lingam inside the dargah." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 178, folio 165). Haroon Khalid, without naming the saint, writes of "the shrine of Sehwan Sharif, one of the most important Muslim shrines in Sindh, which was once a Hindu temple dedicated to Lord Shiva" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 32, folio 14).

Inside the durbar is "a little grave in a cove-like corner of the space, which had been rather oddly incorporated since the redesign of the tomb-hall sometime in the 1990s", of which one woman "viewed herself as the lone guardian of a forgotten grave" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 79, folio 62); "The minor shrine in question is the tomb of one of the earliest disciples of Lal." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 190, folio 173, ch. 2 n. 9), the disciple unnamed. A photograph of 2012 shows "the two domes of the shrines of Lal and Bodlo" from the old fortress (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 161, folio 144). Boivin records that the sultan Muhammad Shah Tughluq, who came to Sindh in 1351, "died shortly afterwards in Sonda on the banks of the Indus, and he was buried in Sehwan Sharif, near Lal Shahbaz Qalandar's shrine." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 189, folio 176). He also records the tomb of the Hindu faqir Mulchand: "Because most of his followers had migrated to India, they arranged a tomb (maqbaro) when Mulchand was still alive in a compound located at the western side of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar's shrine (mazar). The place is also known as Mulan Faqir jo asthan." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 75, folio 62). Of the Hindu lamp-lighter Lal Das, he writes that "He himself had disciples who raised a small dargah not far from his Master's" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 181, folio 168).

The saint's memory reaches beyond the walls. "The graveyard is one among several other locations that bespeak the memory of the saint. One of these is the Indus River." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 162, folio 145). "The other major site associated with the saint's memory is the garden of Lal, or Lal-bagh, the wooded grove where Baba-Akram once established a fakir dwelling… A few kilometers south of the city, the area is known to have been the contemplative environs where the saint camped before making his way into the town." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 162, folio 145). "Revered trees in the grove and natural springs in the area bespoke the saint's miracles and formed part of a whole constellation of sacred sites in and around Sehwan that pilgrims were sure to visit." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 58, folio 41). Kasmani names Chothambi and the natural springs of Lal-bagh among them (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 195, folio 178, ch. 5 n. 7), and one spring by name: citing the *Tarikh Mazhar Shahjahani*, he writes that "on the eve of the festival Shivratri, every Hindu man and woman of Sehwan would bathe at Chhoti Nali, one of the springs associated with the saint on the outskirts of town" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 185, folio 168, intro n. 36).

## Custodians, Lodges and the State

The shrine is administered by the Sindh Auqaf department and remains the beating heart of Sehwan.

Kasmani traces the custodianship back through colonial records. "In the period following the eighteenth century, British colonial records document duties and services at the shrine being shared between Hindu and Muslim groups. More prominent in such records are lengthy court disputes regarding its revenues, especially the issue of the shrine's custodianship involving two notable sayyid lineages of Sehwan, the Lakkiyari and the Sabzwari" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, pp. 81–82, folios 64–65). "These families continue to hold a significant influence in the town, though their claims on the shrine and its revenues, both immediate and ancillary, can be documented only as far back as the colonial period" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 82, folio 65). Each has its own account of itself, which Kasmani reports without endorsing: "Their ancestors, as the Sabzwari claim, were select disciples who accompanied Lal on his journey from Iran; the Lakkiyari, from the neighboring hills of Lakki, assert that they descend from the saint's primary followers who received the journeying ascetic on the outskirts of Sehwan" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 82, folio 65). "Rivalries endure in Sehwan to this day" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 82, folio 65), and, as Kasmani reports from Boivin, the rift between the two families dates back to the British period (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 190, folio 173, ch. 2 n. 5). A third claim belongs to the fakirs: "Many fakirs in Sehwan claim that the shrine's original heirs were in fact ascetic followers of the saint." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 190, folio 173, ch. 2 n. 2). Kasmani thanks, among others, Sayyid Mehdi Raza Shah Sabzwari, "one of the traditional custodians of the shrine at Sehwan", and Sayyid Hasan Shah Khatdhani, "a prominent spiritual figure of Sehwan" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 15, folio xiv).

Boivin describes the same two lineages from the side of the lodges: "two lineages dominate the system, both being Sayyids. The Lakkiyaris are usually accepted as being the head of the system, but the Sabzwaris also share the symbolic power since the shrine itself came under government control in the 1960s." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 180, folio 167).

Kasmani sets this shrine's story inside the national one. A countrywide survey of religious endowments was under way in the 1950s, but, he writes, "it would take another decade before these shrines would formally come to be institutions of the state, eventually organized under the Department of Auqaf and governed via the Ministry of Religious" Affairs (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 68, folio 51). "It was through a federal ordinance in 1959 that the Pakistani state finally began to implement its writ broadly by assuming custodianship of sites that were considered waqf (pl. auqaf), an inalienable endowment" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, pp. 68–69, folios 51–52), the "West Pakistan Waqf Properties Ordinance (1959)" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 183, folio 166, intro n. 17). The ordinance "made it possible for all nonprivate endowments that were not solely charitable to be taken legally into the hands of the state" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 69, folio 52), and "shrine takeovers were not without conflict. Some involved long legal battles with groups and families affected by nationalization, and a few such court appeals lasted well into the 1980s." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 68, folio 51).

For Sehwan he gives the year three times: "the shrine's takeover from its traditional custodians in 1960" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 28, folio 11); "The shrine of Sehwan, according to one report, was taken over by the state of Pakistan on June 24, 1960." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 61, folio 44), the report unnamed; and the local sayyids' "control over the shrine was replaced by the state in June 1960" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 79, folio 62). Boivin says only "in the 1960s" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 180, folio 167). The saint's celibacy mattered here: "A shrine without legal heirs could not be deemed familial inheritance, which in fact was a key line of argument in the modern state's takeover of the shrine from its traditional sayyid custodians" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 82, folio 65).

The motive, as Kasmani reads it, was power and money: "Displacing such authority and gaining possession of shrine resources promised not only new financial means for the state but also guaranteed curbing the influence of local powerhouses" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 68, folio 51). Those who lost were "sayyids, that is, families that claim descent from the Prophet Muhammad, and who held control over shrine revenues at least since the colonial period", and also local Hindu patrons long tied to the shrine's services and ceremonies (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 28, folio 11); "State control and interest have also effectively disenfranchised local Hindu families who once enjoyed a greater role in similar services at the shrine" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, pp. 83–84, folios 66–67).

"while the nationalization of shrines has dramatically reduced their roles at the shrine, limiting it to the ceremonial, former custodian families continue to hold considerable leverage through other forms of participation" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 89, folio 72): they seek a say in its upkeep by offering advice to the state, sitting on shrine committees and helping to organise the annual ceremonies (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 89, folio 72). "a quick survey of the markets surrounding the shrine revealed that a large number of rented shops in the market adjacent to the shrine are owned by various members of the custodian families." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 190, folio 173, ch. 2 n. 15). "most sayyids in town traced their descent to the primary disciples of the saint. A long history of attachment to the shrine and its revenues, social privilege, and official favors spanning centuries have enabled these lineages to prosper in both economic and political terms." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 89, folio 72).

Above all they kept the lodges. Kasmani writes of their patronage of the system of fourteen *kafis*, "a network of the town's primary and ancient fakir lodges believed to have been established by the saint himself" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 89, folio 72): "With the shrine out of their hands, Sehwan's sayyids and custodian families are able to retain part of their historically principal roles by virtue of their positions as patrons and masters of fakirs at such lodges." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 89, folio 72). "Only one in a system of fourteen kafis of Sehwan is owned and headed by a nonsayyid" *sajjadah-nashin* (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 191, folio 174, ch. 2 n. 16). The lodges are "fraternities whose histories are tied to Lal's earliest disciples. Many of these lodges are in fact named after such figures and run by local elite, most of whom also trace their lineage to contemporaries of the saint" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 126, folio 109), though "The exact number of secondary fakir lodges, however, is hard to determine in Sehwan" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 128, folio 111).

Boivin counts differently: "Sehwan Sharif hosts about twenty kafis, or Sufi lodges, of which two are Hindu." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 193, folio 180). "Two kafis are currently managed by Hindus. The first is the kafi of Lal Das and the second is that managed by Ramdas (or Ramchand), which is Mulchand Kafi." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 180, folio 167). He names the whole arrangement himself: in Sehwan the Hindus "are integrated into the system of the kafis, what I call the Sehwani system", and "The Sehwani system is made up of a number of Sufi lodges, settled all around Lal Shahbaz Qalandar's darbar." "There are complex relationships of authority and dependence between the different holders of the Sufi lodges." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 180, folio 167). He explains the word: "In Sehwan Sharif, the place where the faqirs stay, usually known as a khanaqah, is named kafi. The origin of the use of this word is unknown, but maybe that it is related to the fact that it signifies a place where poetical verses, or kafis, are sung." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 211, folio 198, Ch. 2 n. 4). He names, too, "the Doda Mard Haqani Kafi, a Sufi lodge devoted to an alleged disciple of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar in Sehwan Sharif", where a portrait of Ali is shown (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 122, folio 109).

Mulchand Kafi, where Mulchand's tomb stands, "is situated to the east of the darbar of Lal Shahbaz and it adjoins the kafi of Doda Mard Haqani." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 180, folio 167). "Although Hindus are very few in number in Sehwan today, it should be noted that Mulchand Kafi works to a certain extent as a place of community worship." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 180, folio 167). One of its buildings "is used to store different artefacts that are paraded during Lal Shahbaz's mehndi procession." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 125, folio 112). "Concerning the cult of Lal Shahbaz proper, although it is true that the kafi is open to the public mainly on the occasion of the urs, certain ceremonies of the cycle of life are always realized there." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 180, folio 167).

"Among hundreds of shrines in Sindh, the Herald reported in 1972, the shrine of Sehwan earned the most revenues and its annual fair drew bigger crowds than any other shrine" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 70, folio 53). "The state collects pilgrim donations offered to saints at shrines and also generates revenue from waqf properties through work contracts, rents, and leases, which it claims to repurpose for the maintenance and reconstruction of old shrines and other religious endowments." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 189, folio 172, ch. 1 n. 26). "Monetary offerings to the saint are either collected in donation boxes or distributed among shrine workers on duty." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 190, folio 173, ch. 2 n. 11). "Many of the contract workers seen washing the shrine floor would have found entry into the town's most important economic network through an exchange of favors between the Auqaf and the sayyids." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 84, folio 67). Kasmani gives no sum or budget.

Kasmani credits the state with making Sehwan national. "For most of Pakistan's early years, and especially prior to the arrival of Auqaf-led administration, the shrine at Sehwan had enjoyed a largely regional appeal. It drew mostly Sindhi and Baloch pilgrims from around the region" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 69, folio 52). "A direct and steady investment in hard infrastructure such as the organization of saints' fairs, the upkeep of mausoleums and their facilities, and road and rail networks to sites of pilgrimage served to enhance the vitality of devotional centers like Sehwan" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, pp. 61–62, folios 44–45). "special trains like the Jhule Lal Express were initiated to facilitate the annual journey of pilgrims connecting Sehwan with Lahore"; the superhighway of the early 1980s joined the country's major towns; and under Zulfiqar Ali Bhutto, "It was during his term as prime minister that Sehwan Developmental Authority was established" — "As a follower of the saint, the Sindhi politician's private association with the shrine ensured a personal interest in the development of the town" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 70, folio 53). Boivin, too, speaks of "the growing fame of the saint of Sehwan Sharif, especially since Bhutto's rule in the 1970s" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 193, folio 180).

The press followed. "Sindhi publications of the state hardly mention the saint or the shrine of Sehwan until the late 1960s" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 70, folio 53); "Only from the 1960s onward do Urdu and Sindhi newspapers offer detailed reportage on the annual fair of Sehwan" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 70, folio 53), and "Starting in the 1960s, the Sindhi-language newspaper Daily Ibrat (published in Hyderabad) started to report the ʿurs at Sehwan in great detail." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 189, folio 172, ch. 1 n. 29). "State institutions like the Department of Culture or the Sindhi Adabi Board routinely publish books and brochures dedicated to the saint's life as well as to Sehwan's history and heritage" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 188, folio 171, ch. 1 n. 20). And the fair became a platform: "In his address to the annual conference at the saint's fair in November 1969, Sindhi nationalist leader G. M. Syed (2012) traced features of shrine ritual and fakir practices to Shivaite forms." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 185, folio 168, intro n. 39).

Kasmani's reading of the whole is pointed: "What makes the modern story of governing shrines infinitely more intriguing in the case of Sehwan is how, despite the explicitly Sunni-Islamic disposition and agenda of the state, its infrastructures have systematically furthered a historically Shivaite and an ascendant Shiʿi site of devotion" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 72, folio 55). "It is not exactly surprising that Sehwan came to be adopted as a site of communal identification and resistance politics for the Pakistani Shiʿa during the 1990s, a decade synonymous with an intensification of sectarian violence and political division in the country" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 71, folio 54). "Lal's unresolved history explains why his shrine, along with its relics, revenues, services, and ceremonies, has remained riddled by different claims. It has historically attracted long-drawn-out disputes of authority involving fakirs, princely patrons, sayyid elite, traditional custodians, and, more recently, the Pakistani state." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 36, folio 19).

## Devotion and Practice

Thronged year-round, the shrine fills overwhelmingly at the annual urs. The shrine has always been a strikingly inclusive place, drawing Muslims and Hindus alike and women in great numbers, and famed for a devotion that is joyous, bodily, and unrestrained.

The books describe that crowd. "Devotees come from a whole spectrum of social, religious, and ethnic backgrounds. If Sehwan attracts the poorest of the poor, Lal's followers have also included the country's former prime ministers and its top fashion models and photographers." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 20, folio 3). The courtyard was where "fakir women found refuge, spirits surrendered to the sound of drums, and Hindu pilgrims brushed shoulders with Shiʿa devotees, but also where locals simply hung out: tea sellers and vendors of all kinds solicited customers, and children played" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 104, folio 87). "At Sehwan's shrine… one could find women, khwaja-sara, and men crossing paths in many places and on most occasions. Yet there were exceptions." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 78, folio 61). Jürgen Schaflechner writes that "Today, local and translocal Hindu and Muslim communities alike visit this shrine regularly, and both take part in the worship of the saint" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 107–108, folio not stated).

"Pilgrims kissing Lal's tomb, women singing to the saint, men's shirtless torsos in flagellation, sick and seemingly possessed bodies, screaming, losing composure, and then regaining it— all of these are daily occurrences in Sehwan. Making offerings, ingesting substances, touching relics, dealing spirits" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 37, folio 20). In one account given to Kasmani: "I held the jali [metal latticework around the tomb] and made him [the saint] my murshid [master]." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 119, folio 102). "His devotees believe that many prayers are answered at the shrine and many wishes granted" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 93–94, folios not stated). Abbas calls him "a jalālī pir, a mystic with intense supernatural energy" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 57, folio not stated), and his shrine "a jalālī shrine" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 197, folio not stated), as against the shrines of "jamālī or aesthetic mystics" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 96, folio not stated). Sorley, writing of Sindh in general, records that "The Sufi fakirs of Sind belonged to the two great orders of Jelali and Jemali darwishes" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 299, folio 290).

Each day begins with the *ghusl*. Kasmani watched it "around 3:30 a.m. on a Friday morning in November 2011" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 82, folio 65): "the daily ceremony of ghusl or the ritual bathing of the saint's" tomb (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 83, folio 66). Present were "Department of Auqaf employees, contract workers and cleaners, a handful of fakirs, visitors, and pilgrims. A policeman tried to keep things in order. The junior manager of the shrine arrived with keys to the shrine." There were *khadims*, some employed by the state, and others on contract. "A fakir from a prominent lodge of the Lakkiyaris walked in with a large bundle wrapped in red cloth. Another one, representing a secondary shrine in the town, carried a big basket filled with rose petals" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 83, folio 66).

The Lakkiyaris supply the chief item of the tomb's dressing, the *dastar*: "It is the Lakkiyaris… who provide this most important item for the tomb's dressing. It is prepared with fresh leaves at one of their kafis (fakir lodges) located just outside the shrine premises. A representative is present every morning to deliver the dastar and to collect the old one, which is then taken back to the lodge and placed at a minor shrine" there; "This changing of the dastar is one example of a greater system of exchange of favors that continues between the state and the sayyid elite of Sehwan" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 84, folio 67). "Ornamented jewels, decorative flowers, and peacock feathers add the final touch to the dressing of the crown of the saint, finished by the use of perfumed oils" and sprays (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 84, folio 67). "Occasionally, wealthy pilgrims also contribute gifts of ornamental value for the dressing of the tomb." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 84, folio 67).

"As the cleaning drew to a close, the Auqaf manager walked over to the three doors of the durbar and sprinkled rose water on those witnessing the ritual from outside." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 85, folio 68). "There was a loud wave of chants as people flocked to the tomb to touch and kiss the freshly dressed tomb of the saint. They brought new covers and fresh petals and offered sweets and money to the" saint (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 85, folio 68). For Kasmani the keys are the point: "That it is performed by a junior manager of the department who possesses the keys to the shrine is an everyday staging of state power, a reminder that the shrine of Sehwan is no longer familial inheritance but public asset" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 83, folio 66).

"only those men assigned the task of washing are allowed to enter at this time" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 84, folio 67); "The ghusl or bathing of the saint is considered an intimate event that requires privacy. The presence of women inside the durbar during the ceremony is discouraged." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 190, folio 173, ch. 2 n. 8). "The ritual on Friday morning is more elaborate and considered auspicious, given the religious significance of the day." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 190, folio 173, ch. 2 n. 6). The ceremony has narrowed: "certain services were discontinued; for instance, the cleaning and lighting of five lamps (chiraghi) was no longer observable by 2013. During my last visit in 2018, the ritual had become a completely private affair." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 190, folio 173, ch. 2 n. 10).

The lamps had their own servants. Boivin records that "Lal Das was a Hindu from Kashmir who came to make the pilgrimage to Sehwan Sharif. He devoted nearly forty years of his life to serving Lal Shahbaz Qalandar. He was in charge of lighting the lamps (chiraghi wala) before extinguishing himself 'in the smell of sanctity' in 1993." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 180–181, folios 167–168).

The evening belongs to the drums. Boivin: "In Sehwan Sharif, where his shrine stands, the dhamal is performed every day at sunset. It is performed by professional drummers in the main courtyard and hundreds of devotees start to dance to reach ecstasy. Also, a number of women participate, but in a different manner." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 140, folio 127). Kasmani calls it "the evening dhamal, a daily ritual of drumming" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 101, folio not stated): "This is when hundreds move to the tune of reed and drums every evening in the shrine courtyard" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 110, folio 93), and "the evening performance of dhamal (ritual drumming) was carefully segregated" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 78, folio 61). Abbas: "Faqīrs (devotees who wear orange robes) perform the ritual dhammāl (dance) every evening at the Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar shrine in Sehwan Sharif", and "The large drums (naubats), which are played for the dhammāl, can be seen lying in the large courtyard of the shrine during the day" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 93, folio not stated). "The passionate whirling dance of the faqīrs in a circle is said to express the immense jalāl (energy) of Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar." (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 93–94, folios not stated).

"Both men and women participate in the dhammāl. Women perform the dance in a certain section of the courtyard. Many times, families will bring women who are emotionally disturbed to participate in the dhammāl, which devotees claim has healing powers" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 66, folio not stated), and in the bazaar outside "one can buy cassettes of healing music for emotionally disturbed persons" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 66, folio not stated). Her informants at Radio Pakistan in Hyderabad "compare the dhammāl of the faqīrs at this shrine with that of the whirling dervishes of Maulana Rumi in Konya" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 93, folio not stated). Boivin adds: "The renunciants (faqirs) of Bodlo Bahar, the main follower of Lal Shahbaz who has a shrine nearby, come once a month, and they perform a beautiful whirling dance in their red skirts." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 140, folio 127).

With the dhamal comes *haziri*. "Each evening in the white marble courtyard of the shrine, women from across the country experience haziri (lit. presence; see fig. 2.3). Responding to the sounds of drum and reed, cohabiting spirits are believed to become present during a ritual performance, leaving hundreds of women in bouts of exhilaration." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 87, folio 70). "Once the performance of dhamal concludes, family members help loved ones recover from the exhausting experience of haziri." "It is also for their abilities to discern good from bad spirits that fakirs are often summoned to watch over women's passages during haziri." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 87, folio 70).

The sung word has its place too. The shrine is one of five where "qawwālī is performed every evening after the prayers" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 42, folio not stated), and *sufiānā-kalām* "is a feature of the shrines in Sind, especially those of Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar and Shah Abdul Latif at Bhit" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 85, folio not stated).

The day ends at midnight. "A little after midnight, as the high doors of the shrine would be ritually closed, people would now be asked to withdraw from the courtyard" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 105, folio 88); "as the doors of the shrine were drawn, an elaborate prayer was held, which was led by the Auqaf manager" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 114, folio 97). A fakir's "intermittent but regular chants" were "a regular feature at the opening of the shrine doors as well as its closing at midnight" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 83, folio 66). The week has its own peak: "The streets of Sehwan were gradually recovering from the Thursday night frenzy characteristic of saints' shrines in Pakistan" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 82, folio 65).

Abbas records that "at the Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar shrine at Sehwan Sharif, the woman faqiriānī or caretaker gives out water to the devotees" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 160, folio not stated), and that "women participated actively in the support services at the Lal Shahbaz Qalandar shrine in Sehwan: a female muridīānī gave water to the devotees" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 20, folio not stated). She frames the act: "In the Islamic tradition giving water to the thirsty is a savab (charitable deed). The act of giving water to the thirsty is eulogized in the oral Sufi traditions, especially in relation to the Battle of Kerbala" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 160, folio not stated). Around the relic over the tomb she saw a rite of women: "women devotees held up glasses of water to seek ritual blessings from the guluband", and "The glasses of water were then shared with their kinswomen and men. To orthodox Muslims this may seem to be a fetish, but devotees draw strength from the ritual" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 20, folio not stated). Her guide on her first visit was a senior librarian at the Institute of Sindhology: "Although an orthodox Muslim, he was a devotee of the Sufi saint" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 20, folio not stated).

Kasmani describes a photograph trade. For 50 rupees a pilgrim could have himself set into a collage of saintly figures, sites and symbols, and of political personalities besides (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 20, folio 3). "Pilgrims in Sehwan were routinely caught in prayer-like postures at the shrine, posing with the saint as it were", facing "not his tomb as was customary, but the camera" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 20, folio 3). "Digitally manipulated intimacies with the saint are all too common when it comes to pilgrim photographs in Sehwan" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 54, folio 37), and in such pictures "Lal is additionally featured flying above in his popular falcon form" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 21, folio 4).

## The Urs and the Mehndi

The annual urs is held in the Islamic month of Sha‘ban, when hundreds of thousands of devotees converge for days of dhamal, qawwali, and *langar*.

Sorley speaks of it in his own voice and in the present tense: "The gathering at his tomb on his saint-day produced and still produces" strange sights; "Religion and superstition are mingled and Hindus and Muslims assemble together to do honour to" his memory (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 299, folio 290). He quotes Jethmal Parsram: in Sehwan, where Lal Shahbaz "(also called Kalandar Lal Marwandi) lived and died", there gather "during the annual fair thousands of Hindus and Muhammadans" camped round the tomb (the word is damaged in the printed text), "mixing freely, and singing the Sufi songs that ever melt the heart. Wrong things have also entered into the celebration of these holy days, but that is what always happens." (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 299, folio 290).

The books name the occasion several ways. Kasmani: "Death anniversaries of saints are celebrated in Pakistan, marking their union with the divine. These are called ʿurs (Arabic for wedding) or simply mela in Sehwan, meaning fair." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 187, folio 170, ch. 1 n. 1). One of his interlocutors "uses the Urdu word shadi instead of the Arabic ʿurs, both meaning wedding" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 188, folio 171, ch. 1 n. 23): "The fakir told me that he had received an invitation to this saint's wedding [shadi]." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 66, folio 49). Kasmani's fair is "the three-day annual fair" of Sehwan (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 114, folio 97), and he gives it no month. Boivin gives its days: "Lal Shahbaz's urs in the Islamic calendar, 18, 19 and 20 Shaban" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 146–147, folios 133–134). Schimmel writes that the 'fair' of Sehwan, "celebrated between Shawwal 18 and 20", "has become notorious for its rather illicit events" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 386, folio 355). Abbas's women informants say that "during the urs celebrations, which last for more than a week, it is almost impossible to walk in the narrow streets of the city" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 56, folio not stated).

During the fair the grove of Lal-bagh fills: its "measured distance from Sehwan made it ideal for fakir seclusion except at the time of the annual fair, when a temporary bazaar would sprawl over. Visiting bands of fakirs and large groups of pilgrims would camp on its grounds." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 58, folio 41). The state depends on its working relationship with the town's elite, Kasmani writes, to organise the year's great events, among them "the town's annual ritual processions during the time of the saint's fair as well as in the holy month of Muharram" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 84, folio 67). After "the culmination of the three-day annual fair", many fakirs "proceed to Lahut" in Balochistan, or follow others to the shrines of Karachi (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 114, folio 97).

The heart of the urs, in Boivin's account, is the *mehndi*, the henna of a wedding: "henna or mehndi symbolizes the marriage between the Sufi and God, which is to say that it represents death as their union" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 147, folio 134). "The protocol of the urs reflects the balance at which the communities involved in the distribution of the charisma have arrived. Each procession is organized upon the same pattern. It is composed of singers, musicians and dancers. A designated person wears the silk chador, which is deposited on the tomb of the qalandar, and places a tray on which the henna (mehndi) is located." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 182, folio 169). "The procession is the reproduction of the classic procession of marriage. Each time, the procession runs through the narrow streets of the town while the 'clients' of each party grow more and more numerous. The roofs of the houses are filled with onlookers who do not want to miss this show." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 183, folio 170).

There are three processions, on three days: "on the 18th of Shaban, it is the procession led by the Lakiyyaris, on the 19th it is the one led by Ramchand and on the 20th is that of Lal Gul." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 181, folio 168), Lal Gul being Lal Das's "alleged son" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 181, folio 168). "In Sehwan, the mehndi is led on the first day by the dominant group of Sayyids, the descendants of the Prophet Muhammad, and on the second and third days by Hindus who belong to two distinct families." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 147, folio 134). Elsewhere he writes: "They performed the mehndi on the second day of the urs, the nineteenth day of Shaban." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 58, folio 45). Schaflechner, citing Boivin, says that "two Hindu families play a crucial role in the arrangement of the henna (Hin. mehaṁdī) ceremony at the shrine" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 108, folio not stated).

Who the two Hindu families were Boivin gives in more than one form: "The Navani family in Sehwan Sharif was one of the two Hindu families with the Kanungos who used to perform mehndi since centuries during the urs of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 58, folio 45); Dalpat Sufi "was born in Sehwan Sharif in the Mirani family, one of the two Hindu families whose members performed the mehndi ceremony during the urs of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar. The other Hindu family was Mulchand Faqir's one, the Navanis." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 118, folio 105). His fullest account gives the order before and after Partition: "Before partition, the first procession was led by Gul Muhammad Lakkiyyari. On the second day, it left the house of the 'Meranis' and 'Nevanis' under the direction of Mulchand Faqir. Finally, on the third day, it started from the house of the Kanungos. Now, the eldest son of Gul Muhammad, Murad Shah, has replaced him to lead the mehndi of the first day. The two groups leading the following mehndis have changed. After the death of Mulchand Faqir in 1962, the duty of the mehndi was transmitted to his cousin, Mohan Lal, who then passed it on to his son, Ramchand, in 1981. The Kanungos migrated to India in the same year and the burden fell to Lal Das, a Hindu from Kashmir. It is today his son Lal Gul who fulfils this obligation." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 183, folio 170).

Another chapter tells the second-day succession differently. Mulchand's "first successor was Chandmal Chandrani, a local Hindu from Sehwan. But, in 1971, he decided to migrate to India, and his son Kanya Lal, who had a vegetable shop in the Shahi Bazar in Sehwan, refused to take care of the Mulchand Kafi. Mohan Lal, who was Mulchand's nephew, finally took care of it. Also, he took on the duty of performing the mehndi during the urs of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 125, folio 112). "Mohan Lal passed away in 1981 and his son Ramchand began to perform the mehndi; he is still in charge. Ramchand is an Amil, but his mother was a Thakur." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 125, folio 112). And again: "the sajjada nashin is Ramchand, Mulchand's nephew, and he performs the mehndi ritual every year, leading a procession from Mulchand Kafi up to Lal Shahbaz Qalandar's darbar." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 193, folio 180). Of the third day he writes: "On the third day, the mehndi was led by the Kanungos, but in 1962 they decided to emigrate to India and settled in Ulhasnagar", and "In Sehwan Sharif, another Hindu named Lal Das then became mehndibardar." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 147, folio 134).

Mulchand himself (Boivin gives his dates as 1883–1962) "was not initiated by a local Sufi Master. He was thus independent from the local Sufis, and his only connection with Sufism in Sehwan was directly through Lal Shahbaz Qalandar." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 59, folio 46; p. 55, folio 42). "Mulchand nevertheless performed the mehndi ceremony of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar in Sehwan until his death" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 59, folio 46), and "Until his death in 1962, Mulchand lived as a recluse, probably leaving his dwelling place only for performing the mehndi of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 71, folio 58). His family "was perfectly integrated in the dominant groups of the town through the main ritual role they were playing in the mehndi procession during the annual festival devoted to Lal Shahbaz Qalandar" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 71, folio 58). "Finally, Mulchand Faqir is known in Sehwan as a mehndibardar." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 126, folio 113).

At Mulchand Kafi, "The main event occurring in Mulchand Kafi is the procession of the mehndi for the urs of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar, which starts from the kafi." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 126, folio 113). Before it, Boivin writes, "two main events are arranged. First is the dastarbandi of Ramchand. As we saw before, this is the coronation, when the ceremonial turban (pag) is put on his head. It is followed by a musical session with musicians and dancers from Punjab. The apex of the listening to spiritual music is the singing of the famous song devoted to Lal Shahbaz Qalandar, Dam dama mast qalandar. The third is the preparation of the mehndi under a canopy." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 126, folio 113); he announces two events and lists a third. The kafi is otherwise quiet: "The only time in a year when it plays a social and religious role is the time of the mehndi procession, on the 19th Shaban." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 127, folio 114). Its keeper does not act like other masters of lodges: "Usually, the sajjada nashin stays for welcoming the murids, and provides them advice and amulets (tawiz). Ramchand did not play such a role. And no faqir stays in Mulchand Kafi." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 126, folio 113). For Lal Gul's procession, "there is a musical session in a house, with the presence of the head of the Lakkiyyaris, Murad Shah" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 181, folio 168).

A caste of saffron-workers has its own office: "The Khuhnbatis, as saffron specialists, were attributed a priestly function in the ceremony of the mystical nuptials of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar." Their name "comes from the Sindhi word khuhnbo, which means saffron" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 181, folio 168). The state sets the order of things: "For all categories of people involved in sharing the charisma, the urs is also a moment of prime importance. It is at this moment that the position of each one is reaffirmed. These positions, be they honorary or real, can be watched during the implementation of a protocol imposed by the State." "This protocol, as well as the distribution of tasks between confessions, has not undergone fundamental change since the partition, with one exception." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 181, folio 168) — the exception being the Lal Kunwar, below. "No timetable is given for the custom of mehndi. It proceeds according to the document of the Awqaf manager in relation to the procession of the matamis." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 183, folio 170). A photograph in Boivin's book is captioned "Asan Gul, a Hindu sajjada nashin in Sehwan Sharif" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 182, folio 169); he does not name him in his text.

Boivin's judgement on why Hindus keep these roles: "In a Pakistan under the pressure of Islamist extremists, it may seem surprising that Hindus can still play a ritual role in the Sufi set-up of Sehwan Sharif. This resilience can be explained primarily by the desire to preserve a historic heritage. But beyond this integrative dynamic of Sufism, it must be understood that the Hindus do not exercise this function as Hindus, but as Hindu Sufis. This is obvious from the fact that the Hindu processions cannot be distinguished from the Muslim ones, and furthermore, nothing especially Hindu can be observed in these devotional events." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 183, folio 170).

Kasmani notes a rite now gone: some locals, citing the figure of the bride of Lal, claim that a Hindu custom of ritual marriage with the saint was part of the fair's ceremonies until the middle of the twentieth century (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 191, folio 174, ch. 2 n. 28). Boivin's account of the Lal Kunwar is told among the legends below.

The shrine has drawn other Sufis. "Once, Rakhiyyal Shah (1846–1940), a high-level Sufi of the Qadiriyya, came to pay homage to Lal Shahbaz Qalandar." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 59, folio 46). Of the Hindu Sufi Sobhraj Faqir, Boivin writes that he "was often in company with Mulchand Faqir and they were both fond of dhamal, as they used to perform together. Sobhraj wanted to stay for the ending of his life in Sehwan Sharif, which he did." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 179, folio 166).

## Poetry and Song

Lal Shahbaz composed in Persian, and among the verses attributed to him is a ringing declaration of his identity as an intoxicated lover of God and devotee of the Prophet’s family:

حیدریم قلندرم مستم
بندۂ مرتضیٰ علی ہستم

*I am a Haidari, a qalandar, and intoxicated; I am the slave of Murtaza Ali.*

Boivin is more guarded about the Persian corpus, writing of "his alleged diwan" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 213, folio 200, Ch. 4 n. 4), but records his *kalams* being sung. At the first celebration of Mulchand's *versi*, in 1953, "All night long, he sang kalams of Sufi poets of Sindh, such as Bedil, Bekas and also Lal Shahbaz Qalandar." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 82, folio 69). Abbas reports a verse said to be his own, on the dance: "Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar is reported to have said of the dhammāl" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 65, folio not stated):

*For the sake of the love of the friend / I dance over fire / Sometimes I roll in the dust / And sometimes I dance on the thorns* (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 65, folio not stated)

The reported lines end:

*I am not afraid of this disrepute / To dance in every bazaar* (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 65, folio not stated)

But it is not chiefly his own verse that keeps his memory: it is the great devotional song made in his honour, the *Dama Dam Mast Qalandar*, whose refrain rings out wherever South Asian Sufi music is sung:

دَمادَم مست قلندر،
علی دَم دَم دے اندر

*At every breath, the intoxicated Qalandar! Ali is in every breath.*

Kasmani tells how the song left the shrine. "In the summer of 1969, a folk-devotional number, 'O Lal meri,' customarily performed by female pilgrims, was adopted from the shrine itself to be musically arranged for a Punjabi film." It was composed by Ashiq Hussain, and Noor Jahan, "Pakistan's most iconic singer", was asked to sing it; soon the country was abuzz with its chorus, 'dam-a-dam mast Qalandar', which Kasmani glosses *every breath is intoxicated with Qalandar* (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 61, folio 44). "The song, which featured twice in the same film, sparked a short but impactful trend in Punjabi cinema of the time. Lal would make a number of appearances in several films. Each time he was projected in his unique capacity to fly", and was shown to rescue "the female protagonist in distress". "This would effectively turn one of his minor miracles into a most persevering image: a falcon-saint in flight." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 61, folio 44). "At least six films between 1969 and 1975 featured songs in praise of the saint." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 188, folio 171, ch. 1 n. 12). The film's makers followed the song to its source: "The Bukhari brothers, the director-producer duo responsible for the film number… would go on to organize pilgrimages from Lahore to Sehwan, reserving entire trains for their annual journeys." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 61, folio 44).

Abbas gives the song a double dedication: "The lyric, although dedicated to Ali, is also dedicated to Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar", and prints the line *Alī dam dam de andar*, glossed *Ali's name is the healing breath* (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 57, folio not stated). Her glossary gives *mast qalandar* as "Intoxicated mendicant; a melody sung to Ali, the fourth caliph of Islam" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 185, folio not stated). Writing of the wider region, she calls it "the famous 'Mast Qalandar' dhun" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 47–48, folios not stated), "a tune reported to be sung all along the Rajasthan, Sind, Baluchistan, and Iran continuum": "Almost every folksinger in the region can sing it" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 57, folio not stated). For Boivin it is "the famous song devoted to Lal Shahbaz Qalandar, Dam dama mast qalandar", the climax of the music before the Hindu mehndi procession (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 126, folio 113).

And above all there is the *dhamal*: the ecstatic, whirling, drum-driven dance performed at his shrine, especially on Thursday evenings and at the urs, in which devotees lose themselves in rhythm and remembrance. To the beat of the huge *naqqara* drums, before the blaze of lamps, the dhamal of Sehwan is one of the most electrifying expressions of devotion in the Muslim world.

The devotion crosses languages. Schimmel, in *As Through a Veil*, notes that "examples for Lal Shahbaz and Pir Murad of Thatta are also found in Balochi because Baloch tribes migrate in winter into the Indus Valley" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 287, folio 269). Sorley brings the saint into his book through that Baluchi poetry, in a line he quotes from Dames's *Popular Poetry of the Baloches*:

*also Shahbaz, the generous to his friends, a firm embankment erected by the Ruler of the Faithful.* (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 257, folio 248)

The poem says only 'Shahbaz'; the identification is Sorley's: "The Shahbaz is of course the famous Lal Shahbaz" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 257, folio 248).

In Punjabi verse he is one of the Five Pirs. Christopher Shackle's notes to Bulleh Shah's lyrics name "Lal Shahbaz Qalandar of Sehwan" among them, and describe the Five Pirs' collective powers and their interventions on Ranjha's behalf (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 186, folio 161, n. 2 on 122). Usborne's English version of Waris Shah's poem names among the Pirs "Shahbaz Qalandar, the holy saint of Uch" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 10, folio 9); in it the Pirs give Ranjha gifts, "Lal Shahbaz a ring" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 11, folio 10), and later Ranjha "kissed the handkerchief of Shakarganj and took the ring of Lal Shahbaz" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 50, folio 49). The book's later annotator writes that "Lal Shahbaz Qalandar is buried in Sind" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 11, folio 10) and calls him "Shahbaz Qalandar of Sind" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 16, folio 15); neither the poem nor the annotator names Sehwan, and whether the poem's saint of Uch is the saint of Sehwan the book does not settle.

## The Women Singers

Abbas, who studied the women musicians of the Sufi shrines, finds Sehwan their great market. "Sehwan, the historic city built within a large fortress from the time of Alexander the Great, is flooded with large numbers of female musicians who converge on the city to earn a living" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 56–57, folios not stated); "Most of these women also sing at the Sufi shrines such as Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar in Sehwan, where they make a living. Here, they find an audience at any time of the night or day that pays them for their performance" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 33, folio not stated). "most of the women musicians come from the Panjab and the neighborhoods of Sind itself. They perform in every nook and cranny and attract huge audiences" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 57, folio not stated). "Performers like Reshma sing the melody in their high-pitched, full-throated voices at the shrine without the support of microphones" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 57, folio not stated). Her informants differ on when they come: "Female informants in the city of Sehwan Sharif confirmed that many female performers come from the Panjab during the urs of Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar and sing at the shrine with the utmost devotion. Their audiences are men as well as women. My female informants did not have much information about women singers performing at other times of the year. However, professionals at Radio Pakistan in Hyderabad reported that women musicians come to the shrine only at the time of the urs celebrations" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 95, folio not stated).

One tradition of women singers survives, in Sindh, only here, on the testimony of Naseer Mirza: "These were gypsy women who only sang at the Shahbaz Qalandar shrine—certainly, that is the only place in the Sind now where the loṭevālīs come—to sing there… no loṭevālī passes by these shrines—except at Qalandar Lal Shahbaz" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 61, folio not stated). They "come to the Shahbaz Qalandar shrine with a loṭa—and sing there—and make a livelihood on a rupee, two rupee—the alms they get there" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 61, folio not stated).

Careers began in the courtyard. Reshma told Abbas: "I first started at the Shahbaz Qalandar shrine—"; "Every year we go to the Shahbaz Qalandar shrine—"; "I was young at the time when I went there and made a mannat—for my brother" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 58, folio not stated). "I go there every year… I go even now—I just returned from there—"; "The Sufi Shahbaz Qalandar calls for me at least three or four times—I go there and sing for him—I sing for my master—he listens to my songs—" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 61, folio not stated). She sang there "in Siraiki, when she was discovered by the media. This was during an urs of the mystic in the sixties" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 94, folio not stated). "Some of the women, like Zeena Bai, who sang as roving minstrels at the Lal Shahbaz Qalandar shrine, evolved into trained classical and semiclassical musicians in Hyderabad" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 63, folio not stated), and Taj Mastani, who "belongs to Sehwan Sharif where the Lal Shahbaz Qalandar shrine is located", moved to Hyderabad "because it is a large urban center" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 34, 63, folios not stated).

The melody Reshma sang is printed by Abbas with its English facing:

*Lāj merī pat rakio bhalā / Jhule Lālan, / Sindhṛi dā, / Sehwan dā, / Sakhi Shahbaz Qalandar*

*O, keep my prestige / O Lalan, protector of the cradle, / O thou of Sind, / O thou of Sehwan, / Benevolent Shahbaz Qalandar* (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 94, folio not stated)

The melody "honored Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar's jalāl or spiritual powers as a bestower of children", and Reshma "believes that Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar has blessed her" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 94, folio not stated). "Hazrat Lal Shahbaz is venerated in the lyric as the bestower of children" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 57, folio not stated); of two of its lines the original survives only incompletely, but the English reads:

*Thou giver of children to mothers / Thou giver of brothers to sisters* (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 57, folio not stated)

The performers "also sing poetic texts that glorify the karāmāt (mystical prowess) of Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 57, folio not stated). Schimmel, in *As Through a Veil*, found the same petition in the women's songs of Sindh: "in Sind, where the women often use endearing diminutives (Piral, Ghauthal) in addressing the saints, we find poems in honor of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar of Sehwan, who is often implored when a woman wants a son" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 287, folio 269).

## Legends and Miracles

Rizvi writes that "A large number of miracles are said to have been performed by him and even his tomb in Sind is known for innumerable miraculous feats." (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 328, folio 306), on the authority of the *Ma'ariju'l-Wilayat*. Kasmani sets out what the legends do: "He fights the old order with miraculous conduct, strikes down his foes, and lays the foundation for new communities and institutions" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 165, folio 148), and "In the turning upside down of a fortress, in his emasculating impact in acts of sex, in the floating metaphor of the brimming cup, in his prevailing over the river as well as in his takeover of territory is a remaking of place: the place of Lal." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 164, folio 147).

"As a single most defining image of the saint, the flight of Lal that the icon represents — preserved in textual accounts and transmitted in oral legends — is more than a record of a one-time miraculous event." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 53, folio not stated). Kasmani counts the flight among his "minor miracles" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 61, folio 44), and, as one fakir learned it, the name Shah-baz "referred to a miracle attributed to the saint" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 64, folio 47).

"Legends had it that the saint himself had camped in these woods prior to his arrival in the city proper, thus earning the grove its name, Lal-bagh, the garden of Lal." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 58, folio 41). In legend the town's holy men did not welcome him. Kasmani relates that "According to one legend, the holy men of the town felt threatened by his decision and demanded that he prove the merit of his spiritual achievements. Lal asked them to join him for a prayer at the river. As the men arrived, they were astonished to see that Lal had spread open his prayer mat on the surface of the river itself while remaining afloat. None were able to follow suit, and so they quietly acquiesced." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 162, folio 145).

Another form of the story he quotes from "a special publication to mark the saint's fair in 1968": "They sent to him a bowl of milk filled up to its brim. This was an implicit sign that this city is already filled with people of Allah [saints]. There is no space left for you in the city… Qalandar Shahbaz [Lal] floated a flower in the bowl and had it sent across to them. His response therefore was that just as this flower has made its place in the bowl full of milk, for me too a similar place will emerge." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, pp. 162–163, folios 145–146). In a note he reports Boivin's view that "the flower in milk is a key trope in Sufi narratives of the Indus valley and also beyond and can be witnessed in accounts of other saintly figures like Pir Shams of the Ismaʿilis and also Guru Nanak" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 195, folio 178, ch. 5 n. 17).

Two harder stories follow. "In one legend, when the saint expressed his wish to settle down, the owner refused to give up his claim on the property." Lal, the legend goes, struck him dead with a single blow of his staff. "When questioned about the incident, the saint declared that he had merely killed a dog. Unconvinced, the family of the deceased man dug up the grave to indeed find a dead dog." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 163, folio 146). "In yet another version, he first arrived in the quarter of the city that at the time was inhabited by sex workers. On the night of his arrival, the women and their male clients were overcome by an extraordinary aura. None of the men that night, it is said, were able to perform sexually… The charisma of a powerful saint had started to take effect." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 163, folio 146). Kasmani declines to choose: "Whether one accepts that he first settled at a careful distance from the then walled city or that he arrived in the quarter of the sex workers, the alternate character of his choice of dwelling confirms a spatial inversion that is characteristic of antinomian piety" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 195, folio 178, ch. 5 n. 16).

Amma, Kasmani's "fakir interlocutor at the shrine", told him, as other locals did, that "when the time came to bury the saint, the mullahs of the city decided to give him a funerary bath. As they began undressing him, there were layers and layers of cloth tied to his groin. Every time they would remove a layer, another would surface. That the truth of his genitals was never revealed is proof for many that the saint was fiercely celibate. Then came a mysterious voice that asked for the body to be veiled from all sides. When the curtains were finally lifted, a miraculous tomb was already in place." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 163, folio 146).

The fullest of the legends is told of his beloved fakir Bodlo, who, the story goes, would call out the saint's name every day as he swept the alleys of Sehwan. "Irked by the constant nuisance, the prince residing in the overlooking fortress ordered that Bodlo be killed. But each time Bodlo was killed, he would return to the streets the next morning… After six failed attempts, the prince ordered that Bodlo be dismembered…" "As Lal set foot in the city, he called out for his beloved fakir. Returning the call of the master, each and every piece of Bodlo's butchered body rose from the skulls of those who had consumed him." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, pp. 160–161, folios 143–144). "So furious was Lal that to invoke divine punishment, he turned his cup over, causing in effect the fortress and its environs to be turned on their head." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 161, folio 144). "In another version of the same legend, he used his staff instead of the cup to beckon divine punishment. Such details vary across narrations" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 195, folio 178, ch. 5 n. 6). The place is "remembered as the inverted city or the fortress of the infidels" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 161, folio 144), the *ulta sheher*, *kafir-qila* or *ulta qila*, and "The solemn memory of Lal's wrath was etched in its topography, where he was believed to have turned on its head what was once an ancient settlement" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 156, folio 139). The ruler is not named and no date is given.

A saying gives Bodlo precedence. "'Whoever comes to me must first go to Bodlo,' Lal is believed to have said, or so goes the saying in Sehwan" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 127, folio 110); "Most pilgrims therefore make their first offerings at Bodlo, making the otherwise small shrine a destination of great importance in Sehwan" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 127, folio 110). Local legend tells, too, that Bodlo's she-camel, a gift from Lal, would leave the lodge each evening to collect food for its fakirs (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 193, folio 176, ch. 4 n. 13), and that "since fakirs of the other lodges had colluded with each other and killed Bodlo's camel, Lal ordered that they henceforth be marked with a seal to distinguish them from fakirs of Bodlo." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 194, folio 177, ch. 4 n. 35).

Boivin gives the dhamal a legend of its own, as tradition and not as his own claim: tradition says that he practised an ecstatic, gyratory dance "in which he saw the supreme form of meditation (zikr), a position that brought him closer to Jalal al-Din Rumi (d. 1275), the celebrated founder of the brotherhood of the whirling dervishes. Through the dhamal, Lal Shahbaz Qalandar reached the final stage of absorption into God (fana fi Allah)." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 140, folio 127). The Hindu share in the mehndi has its origin story: "According to local tradition, Meru and Nao were two brothers who used to take care of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar by providing food for him. Very grateful to them, Lal Shahbaz decided they would lead the mehndi ritual after his death, when his own urs would be celebrated." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 58, folio 45). Schaflechner, citing Boivin, has it that "According to the legend, it was Shahbaz himself who arranged this process and appointed, in addition to the local Muslim elite, two Hindu families to take part in it" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 108, folio not stated). And the henna is said to bear the saint's touch: "It is said that the act of depositing the chador on the grave amounts to putting the fiancée in her wedding clothes. It is also said that, the next morning, palms and fingerprints are visible in the henna that was deposited the day before. This suggests that, during the night, the qalandar coated his hands with henna and is therefore ready to be united to his divine lover." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 182–183, folios 169–170).

Boivin gives two irreconcilable versions of a woman married to the grave. Burton's: "In the middle of the nineteenth century, Richard Burton mentioned the following custom: 'Every year, a daughter of the Khonbati caste (saffron flower dyers) was married to the grave, with music, dance and all the solemnities due to her' (Burton 1851: 211). This custom, known as Lal Kunwar (the fiancée of the red), disappeared during the partition in 1947." "Burton specifies, however, that the bride was released after a year, though she had to remain forever unmarried." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 181, folio 168). The rival: "according to other sources, the young Lal Kunwar belonged to the caste of Kanungos. The origin of the custom comes from the fact that a young woman of the caste had fallen in love with Lal Shahbaz. The latter being a renouncer, she threw herself from a balcony at his feet, thus sacrificing her life for him. Lal Shahbaz would have been touched by this sacrifice and he would have authorized that, after his death, a young virgin of the caste should be married to his grave. The Lal Kunwar lived for a year in a room where she was venerated as a devi, a goddess. When she was released, she was forced to accept celibacy." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 182, folio 169). Boivin then sets the story aside: "Despite a relatively thorough investigation, with Pakistani and Indian informants, both Muslim and Hindu, it was not possible to obtain evidence of this chain of events." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 182, folio 169).

For Kasmani's fakirs the saint still governs their lives. "for most of my interlocutors, fakirs or otherwise, arriving in and departing from Sehwan was always contingent upon Lal's approval" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 150, folio 133); "fakirs believe that saints summon them" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 117, folio 100). Murad spoke of marriage with the saint: "'When Lal [the saint] contracts a nikah with someone, that someone is then his wife'" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 138, folio 121), and Zaheda held "that a fakir woman, owing to her gender, had the distinct potential to be close to the saint in a way that would not be possible for male fakirs" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, pp. 111, 138, folios 94, 121). Amma told of the saint's correction: "It was in the durbar that the saint said, 'I will bend your foot!' My foot got sprained for the first time in the durbar… Then a second time, a third time, and then my foot got burned." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 91, folio 74). She told, too, of a prayer granted, which Kasmani reports without judging (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 94, folio 77; p. 96, folio 79).

Two fairies from "the fabled mountains of Qaf (Koh-Qaf)" "had once arrived in Sehwan in their amorous pursuit of Lal" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 154, folio 137). Shah-Bibi told Kasmani: "There exists an atmosphere where all are present: the ones made of light, made of fire, angels — of the entire universe. Friends/allies of Allah, prophets, all of them . . . every qutb, ghaws, wali, pir, fakir [categories of saintly figures]. Let me tell you this one thing, that this is the main tower!" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 156, folio 139), her 'main tower' being the English for a telecommunications mast. "Sehwan, according to her description, was one among three vertebrae that hold the earth in place, Karbala and the Hijaz being the other two" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 155, folio 138). "Locals also boast that Sehwan is a sacred geography with 125,000 shrines or that it has forever been a place of pilgrimage." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 164, folio 147).

## The Attack of 2017

The shrine's openness has also made it a target: in February 2017 a suicide bombing at the shrine during the evening dhamal killed scores of worshippers — yet, in a gesture that became famous, the dhamal was danced again at the shrine the very next evening, in defiance of the terror.

Kasmani heard the news in Berlin on "February 16, 2017": "Just hours before, and barely meters away from where Zaheda and I had first crossed paths, a suicide bomber had struck the courtyard of Sehwan's shrine. More than eighty people were killed." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 28, folio 11). "It fit the tragic pattern of recent years whereby key Sufi shrines in Pakistan have periodically been targeted by hardline militants and radical Islamist networks." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 28, folio 11). He notes that more than twenty-five shrines had been targeted since 2005 (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 184, folio 167, intro n. 19). Boivin: "a huge terrorist attack took place in the darbar of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar in Sehwan Sharif on 16 February 2017. It was during the dhamal and ninety people were killed; more than 300 were injured" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 217, folio 204, Ch. 7 n. 6). Neither book names the attackers. Amma "had witnessed the bombing and survived it too" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 99, folio 82), and "Some of the unidentified bodies from the deadly attack at the shrine in 2017 had been laid to rest" in the graveyard on the town's northern edge (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 160, folio 143). Boivin thinks it likely that the Hindu dargah of Tando Ahmad Khan was closed for a time afterwards: "it is difficult not to think that the Hindu dargah would have been temporarily closed after the terrible terrorist attack which occurred in Sehwan Sharif in February of 2017" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 186, folio 173).

The shrine changed after it. "The administrative and security apparatuses of the shrine had been entirely revamped in the aftermath of the suicide attack of 2017. Just walking into the shrine was now an ordeal. There were numerous security checks, and police and security cameras were everywhere… With the arrival of intelligence officials, the order of authorities had become more complex, and everyday decisions regarding the shrine were filtered through multiple channels. Offerings to the saint were thoroughly screened, and metal detectors were placed at all entrances." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 98, folio 81). "The shrine now had a section of the police force dedicated to its protection." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 98, folio 81). By 2018 the entrances had been pushed further out (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 105, folio 88), and the daily bathing of the tomb "was now a completely private affair; visitors were no longer allowed in the courtyard to witness the auspicious ceremony in the early hours." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 98, folio 81). The tightening had begun earlier: "A great deal of what I had observed in 2009 was already changing as early as 2011. A terror attack at Lahore's most prominent shrine had led to security measures being beefed up at major Sufi sites across the country." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 104, folio 87).

## Pilgrim Roads

Sehwan is a station on longer roads. "Lal Shahbaz is also the starting point for the pilgrimage to Lahoot la Makan, the abode of the saint Shah Noorani in the Khuzdar district in Balochistan." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 108, folio not stated). In 2010 Schaflechner met "a Punjabi pilgrim in Lahoot la Makan who had just arrived from Sehwan Sharif and confirmed that this side of the pilgrimage route is still frequented" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 147, folio not stated). He also tells of Raj Kumar, "one of the walking pilgrims from Narayanpur" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 191, folio not stated), who "walked annually to the" ʿurs of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar, "where, according to his own account, he had gone for eleven subsequent years. Raj frequently went together with members of the Christian and Hindu paṃcāyat from his neighborhood. Eventually he stopped going to Sehwan and instead started to go to Hinglaj." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 160–161, folios not stated). In his own words: "We set out walking from Liari [on our way to Sehwan Sharif] and kept our luggage on a camel, [and also] rations [Engl.], water . . . It was my first year." He goes on: "'Man, forget Qalandar! Again we are climbing the mountains for Noorani. The Mother's [Hinglaj] road is all straight. It is by road [Engl.].'" "So the Qalandar was out [lit.: died] and I left from there [to Hinglaj]." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 160, folio not stated). "The foot journey from Liari to Sehwan meant a long and grueling trek through the Pab mountain chain, a relatively arduous and exhausting path in comparison to the route to Hinglaj along the highway." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 161, folio not stated); "Raj Kumar and his fellow travelers changed not only their route but even their final destination in 2004" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 161, folio not stated).

Haroon Khalid writes of the Muslim Nanghe and Udasi sadhus: "In the Pakistani context, their devotion is centred on the shrine of the malamati saint, Shahbaz Qalandar. However, they spend their lifetime travelling from one shrine to the next, following urs celebrations" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated). His image travels too. Khalid records posters of him on sale in the Uch Sharif market (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 277, folio 251) and his picture on the banner at Jalaludin's shrine, and names him at Uch as one of three saints said to have meditated in the mosque rooms (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 282, folio 256; p. 283, folio not stated). In Rawalpindi, in the *langar* of Mama Ji's darbar, "Other religious posters include portraits of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar (Sehwan) and Sain Kanwan Wali (Gujrat)" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 160, folio 149). In Gujarat, Abbas found the Sidi dhammāl lasting "seven nights, in the manner of the dhammāl at the Lal Shahbaz Qalandar shrine" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 74, folio not stated).

## Among the Hindu Sufis of India

Boivin follows the saint to India with the Sindhi Hindus who left at Partition. For them he was one of the "three main Sufi traditions of Sindh: Lal Shahbaz Qalandar in Sehwan Sharif, Daraza and Tando Jahaniyya in Hyderabad." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 60, folio 47). Jethmal Parsram gave him the third part of a small book (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 49, folio 36). A Hindu Sufi's Persian breviary names him among "Bayazid Bistami, Junaid, Mansur Hallaj, Shams Tabrizi, Lal Shahbaz Qalandar, Attar, Rumi and even Bu Ali Qalandar" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 121, folio 108). In R. M. Hari's record of the sayings of Rochaldas, Lal Shahbaz Qalandar "is the symbol of ishq haqiqi", as Majnun is of *ishq majazi* (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 166, folio 153). A daily puja in India "ends with the jai (praise) formula devoted to the gods, including Guru Nanak, Jhulelal as well as Mast qalandar, that is, Lal Shahbaz Qalandar." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 141, folio 128). In a Dalit hagiography the pir of Pithoro "quotes the names of the Lal Shahbaz Qalandar, Puran Bhagat and Jhulelal" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 192, folio 179).

The urs crossed the border by a dream. "Shortly before his death, Rochaldas had a dream in which Lal Shahbaz Qalandar appeared. He pointed out that although the Hindu Sindhis had forgotten him, Lal Shahbaz always watched over them in India. He asked him to celebrate his urs, as at Sehwan." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 146, folio 133). "It seems that the first celebration took place in 1959, two years after the death of Rochaldas, who had left orders." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 146, folio 133). "For a few years, two distinct urs were celebrated: Lal Shahbaz's urs in the Islamic calendar, 18, 19 and 20 Shaban, and Rochaldas's versi. One year, the two anniversaries fell at the same time. From then on, it was decided to celebrate both at the same time, or, that is to say, for the versi of Rochaldas." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 146–147, folios 133–134). In Rochaldas's darbar at Ulhasnagar, the alam "was added to the darbar after Rochaldas had a dream in which Lal Shahbaz Qalandar asked him to celebrate his urs every year, as it was celebrated at Sehwan Sharif in Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 81, folio 68): "a small square courtyard, enclosed by four walls, at the top of which floats the red banner of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 81, folio 68). "The darbar is not decorated with any tower or dome, maybe except one considered the pole of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar, which is high and thus can be seen from far away" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 80, folio 67), and within it "The transition from the first level to the second level is carried out by a representation of the darbar of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 133, folio 120).

There the dhamal "was adapted: it is a simple beating of drums practised in front of the samadhi, which plays the rhythm of the devotional song Mast qalandar devoted to Lal Shahbaz" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 140, folio 127); "The ritual has gone on unchanged since 1952." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 141, folio 128). "the dhamal performed in Ulhasnagar is different. It is reduced to the drumming, while in Sehwan, the drumming is played for the ecstatic dance of the devotees." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 145, folio 132). "The purpose of the ritual is clearly to make the darbar of Rochaldas a territory that extends from an original sacred territory which is located in Sindh, the holy city of Sehwan Sharif." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 141, folio 128). The Kanungos who had led the third day's mehndi at Sehwan settled in Ulhasnagar, "where the son of Rochaldas, Hari, asked them to lead the mehndi procession in the darbar" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 147, folio 134). "Gandaram (d. 2008), the eldest of the Kanungos, inherited the charge" on his father's death in 1948, "but in the 1980s he was transferred to Bangalore, far from Ulhasnagar"; his brother Murlidhar lived in Baroda, the youngest, Lachhman, "was not allowed", and "Thus, it was the son of Hari, Damodar, who led the mehndi from that point on." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 147, folio 134). "At that time, the mehndi procession went as far as the river. There, a puja was performed just as it is in the shrine, ending with Hari Om Jhulelal." "The mehndi of Lal Shahbaz makes it possible to maintain a direct link through the ritual with the lost territory of Sindh." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 147, folio 134).

The Indian rites have since thinned. The mehndi procession was simplified "since the mehndibardar left Ulhasnagar" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 152, folio 139); after the Ayodhya Affair "the main impact was the retreat and/or disappearance of major Sufi rituals, especially the dhamal, the alam and the mehndi", and "The mehndi procession is performed inside Rochaldas's darbar, while previously, at the time of the Kanungos, it was made from the darbar to the river." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 201, folio 188). In Mulchand's case in India, by contrast, Boivin writes that "even his relationship with Sehwan Sharif and Lal Shahbaz Qalandar is not displayed" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 75, folio 62).

In Sindh itself, at Tando Ahmad Khan, Boivin found "a replica of the darbar of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar in Sehwan Sharif, though settled in a different religious environment" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 184, folio 171): "the Hindu dargah of Tando Ahmad Khan hosts Lal Shahbaz Qalandar's tomb, surrounded by Hindu temples, also Sufi astanas, and with many inscriptions referring to an array of religious traditions, including the Nanakpanth." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 184, folio 171). "It can be framed as a spiritual pilgrimage from Shiva to Lal Shahbaz, who is finally merged through the space's agency in Jhulelal." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 186, folio 173).

## Setting: The Town of Sehwan

What follows speaks of the town and its region, not of the shrine. Kasmani places it in "the ancient pilgrimage town of Sehwan in Pakistan's Sindh Province" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 19, folio 2), "hardly 300 kilometers north of Karachi", "a four-hour drive to the town" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 23, folio 6). "According to the provisional data of the 2017 census, the population of greater Sehwan including neighboring villages was about 269,291 people, out of which 104,239 were town residents." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 182, folio not stated, intro n. 6).

Boivin places Sehwan Sharif "in central Sindh where Vicholi was spoken (a Sindhi dialect which was officially proclaimed to be the official Sindhi language by the British)" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 30, folio 17), and Schaflechner writes of "the mountains of Sehwan" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 113, folio not stated). Of the poet Bedil, Boivin writes: "In his teens, he [Bedil] went to Sehwan Sharif, where he is reported to have been vouchsafed spiritual visions" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 97, folio 84); and Alan Faqir "is reported to have sung on the buses and trains between Rohri and Sehwan Sharif in his youth" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 78, folio not stated).

The name carries the town's past. "The name Sehwan is itself a remnant of the older Shiv-vahan (the village of Shiva)." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 36, folio 19). A seventh-century account, Kasmani writes, shows it as a stronghold of the Shivaite Pashupatas before the Arab conquest at the start of the eighth century; "Arab geographers have called it Sadusan and Siwistan"; and Alexander Cunningham "identifies Sehwan as Sindomana", holding that the Greek name Saindhuwan, the abode of the Saindhavas, "is the origin of the name Sehwan" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 185, folio 168, intro n. 38). Abbas calls it "the historic city built within a large fortress from the time of Alexander the Great" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 56–57, folios not stated).

Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* knows Sehwan and Siwistan as a fort and a region, never as a shrine. The book's first capture of the fort of Sehwan comes under the Sammas, when Mirza Shah Beg, the governor of Qandahar, took Bhakkar fort, and afterwards took the fort of Sehwan as well and returned to Qandahar (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 172). In the Khankhanan's Thatta campaign, the army first conquered the fort of Sehwan and then moved on (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 175). In Dara's flight, Muhammad Salih Tarkhan (the last name is uncertainly read), faujdar and commandant of the fort of Siwistan, wrote that Dara had come near the fort, and the author judges that had he blocked the way, Dara would probably have been taken (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 50). And in his account of the suba of Thatta, Siwistan is one of its four sarkars: the sarkar of Siwistan, a dependency of this suba, lying on the bank of the Indus (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529).

Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi records a different religious foundation in the town, not this shrine: Sultan Muhammad bin Tughluq appointed Makhdum-i Jahaniyan the Shaikhu'l-Islam and made him "the head of a khangah [khanqah] in Siwistan (Sehwan) known as the Khanqah-i Muhammadi to which several villages were attached" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 299, folio 277).

Before Partition, Boivin writes, "the Hindus made up about half of the total population, and the town hosted two main Hindu temples. The first one was devoted to Shiva and settled in the north-eastern part of the town. The other one was devoted to Jhulelal and was established on the north-western side of the town. From 1947 onwards, almost all of the Hindu population migrated to India." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 179–180, folios 166–167). "All the Thakurs, the priestly caste of Jhulelal, left Sindh to settle in India, especially in Bombay and Ulhasnagar. In Sehwan, they could have been the most powerful Hindu group, and in Bombay, there is still a Sehwani panchayat which has published several directories of the Sehwanis in India and overseas since partition." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 180, folio 167). He calls the town "a very reputed centre of Sufi pilgrimage" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 113, folio 100), and speaks of "the strong Sufi ambiance of the city" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 161, folio 148). Of Mulchand's decision to stay, he writes: "Contrary to other cities in Northern Sindh – for instance, Sukkur, following the Manzilgah riots – no communal violence had ever been registered. Maybe this is due to the spirit of toleration which prevailed in Sehwan" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 71, folio 58); and yet "the large majority of the Hindu population of Sehwan migrated to India" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 71, folio 58).

The town has grown again since. "Prospects of economic growth and promises of development attracted new migration to Sehwan, especially from the less urban and impoverished parts of the country. The new settlers had very material and observable impacts on the urban and social character of the city" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 70, folio 53). "New migrants claimed the southern part of the city lying below the old highway, which until then had consisted mainly of graveyards and old cemeteries." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 189, folio 172, ch. 1 n. 30).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **His birthplace.** The site's account has him born at Marwand, in the region of present-day Afghanistan/Iran. Kasmani: "most likely" born "in Marwand, near Tabriz (in present-day Iran)" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 62, folio 45). Boivin: "born in Persia" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 213, folio 200, Ch. 4 n. 4). Rizvi: "a native of Sehwan in the Sind" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 328, folio 306).
- **His birth year.** The site's account gives c. 1177. In the books it appears only in the Library of Congress cataloguing heading printed in Kasmani's volume, '1177–1274'; Kasmani himself gives none (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 5, folio not stated).
- **His death year.** The site's account gives c. 1274, as does Kasmani, once (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 20, folio 3). Schimmel's index to *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* gives 1262 (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 520, folio 489); the index to *As Through a Veil* gives only the thirteenth century (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 357, folio 339); Sorley says only that he flourished in the thirteenth century (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 299, folio 290).
- **When he came to Sehwan.** The site's account gives no year. Kasmani prints 1272 as "more reliable", 1251 as "The other date", a first arrival at Multan in 1263, settlement at Sehwan "for the last year or two of his life", and burial "a year after he arrived in town", and joins none of them (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 62, folio 45; p. 188, folio 171, ch. 1 n. 13; p. 163, folio 146).
- **His road to Sindh.** The site's account has him travel through Iraq, the Hejaz and Central Asia. Kasmani, following Boivin, has him reach Multan first and stop at Makran, Lahut-lamakan, Panjgur and Pasni in Balochistan and at Naing and Lake Manchhar in Sindh (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 188, folio 171, ch. 1 n. 14).
- **His name.** The site's account: Sayyid Usman Marwandi. Sorley: Sayid Usman Marwandi (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 298, folio 289), and, quoting Jethmal Parsram, 'Kalandar Lal Marwandi' (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 299, folio 290). Rizvi: "Mir Saiyid 'Usman", with no Marwandi (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 328, folio 306). Sehwan's custodian families, as Kasmani reports them: "most likely Shah Hasan and not Usman" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 184, folio 167, intro n. 33).
- **Why he is called Lal.** The site's account: for the crimson robe of his order. Rizvi: "Often he wore a red dress, hence his name lal (red)" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 328, folio 306). Kasmani: Lal, "red, dear one", or Laʿl, "ruby, gem", both ascribed to him (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 182, folio not stated, intro n. 5).
- **Why he is called Shahbaz.** The site's account: for the soaring freedom of his spirit. Rizvi: a title given him by Bahauddin Zakariya, "the noblest species of falcon" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 328, folio 306). Kasmani, from what a fakir learned: the name "referred to a miracle attributed to the saint" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 64, folio 47).
- **His order and master.** The site's account makes him a wandering *qalandar* of "the qalandari way". Rizvi sets him "Among the Suhrawardiyyas" as Bahauddin Zakariya's disciple (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 328, folio 306). Kasmani reports Suhrawardi and Qadiri claims, the fakirs' belief in an initiation by Jamal al-Din Savi, and the custodians' "loose affiliation with some Qadiri strands" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 62, folio 45; p. 184, folio 167, intro n. 34). Boivin names Shah Sadr as the master he "is said to have" had in Sindh (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 185, folio 172), and a path of "Qalandariyya shahbaziyya" at Sehwan (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 20, folio 7). Khalid calls him a Malamti Sufi (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 282, folio 256).
- **The four friends.** The site's account counts him among the "four friends" alongside such saints as Baba Farid and Bahauddin Zakariya, linked by legend in companionship. Kasmani gives the quartet, *chahar-yar*, without naming its other members (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 62, folio 45). Khalid writes that "Both Bahaudin and Farid would have had serious differences with Lal Shahbaz Qalandar" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 282, folio 256). Rizvi writes that Bahauddin "disliked qalandars intensely" but adopted Lal Shahbaz as his disciple (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 328, folio 306).
- **Where he is buried.** The site's account and most books: Sehwan. Rizvi places his tomb only "in Sind", and Sehwan only as his birthplace (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 328, folio 306). Usborne's Waris Shah calls him "the holy saint of Uch" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 10, folio 9), while its annotator writes that he "is buried in Sind" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 11, folio 10).
- **His other names.** Kasmani: "Jhule Lal, a name originally directed at the river deity of the Indus" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 162, folio 145). Boivin: Raja Bharthari or Raja Vir, and Raja Barthari (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 50, folio 37; p. 178, folio 165); both he and Jhulelal "are today called Jhulelal", but Boivin refuses to infer a fusion (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 216, folio 203, Ch. 7 n. 5). Sorley: 'Jivelal', glossed 'the living Pir' (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 257, folio 248), and "Shah Lal Baz (or Udero Lal)" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 168, folio 159). The site's account makes none of these identifications.
- **The poems.** The site's account says he composed in Persian and quotes a couplet attributed to him. Boivin speaks of "his alleged diwan" in Persian (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 213, folio 200, Ch. 4 n. 4). Sorley credits him with grammar books for the instruction of youth (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 298, folio 289; p. 223, folio 214), which no other source mentions.
- **Whose song Dama Dam is, and what it says.** The site's account calls it "the great devotional song made in his honour" and renders the refrain "At every breath, the intoxicated Qalandar! Ali is in every breath." Kasmani has it begin as 'O Lal meri', a number "customarily performed by female pilgrims", taken into a film in 1969, and glosses its chorus, 'dam-a-dam mast Qalandar', as meaning that every breath is intoxicated with Qalandar (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 61, folio 44). Abbas: "although dedicated to Ali, is also dedicated to Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar", with the line *Alī dam dam de andar* glossed "Ali's name is the healing breath" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 57, folio not stated); her glossary calls it "a melody sung to Ali" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 185, folio not stated). Boivin: "the famous song devoted to Lal Shahbaz Qalandar" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 126, folio 113).
- **When the dhamal is danced.** The site's account: at his shrine, "especially on Thursday evenings and at the urs". Boivin: "every day at sunset" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 140, folio 127), and in a note "every day before the sunset" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 210, folio 197, Ch. 1 n. 18). Kasmani and Abbas: every evening (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 110, folio 93; Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 93, folio not stated).
- **The drums.** The site's account names them *naqqara*. Abbas calls the dhamal drums "naubats" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 93, folio not stated); Kasmani writes of "the drum and the metal disc" at the entrance (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 83, folio 66).
- **The month and length of the urs.** The site's account: Sha‘ban, for "days" of dhamal, qawwali and *langar*. Boivin: 18, 19 and 20 Shaban (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 146–147, folios 133–134). Schimmel: the 'fair' "celebrated between Shawwal 18 and 20" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 386, folio 355). Kasmani: "three-day", with no month (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 114, folio 97). Abbas's informants: "more than a week" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 56, folio not stated).
- **How many come.** The site's account: hundreds of thousands at the urs. Kasmani: the tomb-shrine "draws millions each year" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 20, folio 3). Jethmal Parsram, quoted by Sorley: "thousands of Hindus and Muhammadans" during the annual fair (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 299, folio 290). Boivin: "hundreds of devotees" at the daily dhamal (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 140, folio 127).
- **The attack of 2017.** The site's account: February 2017, during the evening dhamal, "scores" killed. Kasmani: "February 16, 2017", the courtyard struck, "More than eighty people were killed" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 28, folio 11). Boivin: "16 February 2017", "during the dhamal", "ninety people were killed; more than 300 were injured" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 217, folio 204, Ch. 7 n. 6).
- **When the state took the shrine.** Kasmani: "in 1960" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 28, folio 11), "according to one report" on "June 24, 1960" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 61, folio 44), and "in June 1960" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 79, folio 62), under an ordinance of 1959 (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, pp. 68–69, folios 51–52). Boivin: "in the 1960s" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 180, folio 167).
- **What stood on the site.** The site's account speaks of the town's "deep pre-Islamic sacred history". Schimmel: he settled "at the site of the old Shiva sanctuary", and a lingam by the tomb "proves the Shivait background of some of the rites" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 386, folio 355). Khalid: the shrine "was once a Hindu temple dedicated to Lord Shiva" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 32, folio 14). Boivin: "it is said that there was a lingam inside the dargah" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 178, folio 165). Kasmani: "Available archaeological evidence does not confirm the claim" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 185, folio 168, intro n. 35), and in another local version the tomb marks where Lal rested on his arrival (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 163, folio 146).
- **The district.** The site's record places Sehwan Sharif in Jamshoro District. Schaflechner: "Sehwan Sharif in the Dadu district" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 107, folio not stated).
- **How many lodges.** Kasmani: a system of fourteen *kafis*, the number of secondary lodges "hard to determine" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 89, folio 72; p. 128, folio 111). Boivin: "about twenty kafis", two of them Hindu (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 193, folio 180).
- **Where Mulchand's tomb stands.** Boivin puts it in a compound "at the western side" of the shrine (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 75, folio 62), and Mulchand Kafi, where the tomb is, "to the east of the darbar" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 180, folio 167).
- **The Hindu mehndi families.** Boivin names them as the Navanis and the Kanungos (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 58, folio 45), as the Miranis and the Navanis (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 118, folio 105), and, for the second day before Partition, as the 'Meranis' and 'Nevanis', with the Kanungos on the third (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 183, folio 170). Schaflechner names neither family (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 108, folio not stated).
- **The succession at Mulchand Kafi.** Boivin makes Mohan Lal Mulchand's "nephew" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 125, folio 112) and his "cousin" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 183, folio 170); Ramchand Mohan Lal's son (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 125, folio 112) and "Mulchand's nephew" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 193, folio 180); one chapter has a first successor, Chandmal Chandrani, who left in 1971 (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 125, folio 112), the other passes the duty straight to Mohan Lal on Mulchand's death in 1962 (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 183, folio 170).
- **When the Kanungos left.** Boivin gives 1962 (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 147, folio 134) and, in another chapter, 1981, "the same year" Ramchand took the second day's duty (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 183, folio 170).
- **The Lal Kunwar.** Burton, as Boivin quotes him: a daughter of the Khonbati caste, married to the grave every year and released after a year; the custom ended at Partition in 1947 (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 181, folio 168). Boivin's "other sources": a virgin of the Kanungo caste, after a woman of that caste threw herself at the saint's feet; Boivin found no evidence of that chain of events (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 182, folio 169). Kasmani's locals: a Hindu custom of marriage with the saint, kept until the mid-twentieth century (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 191, folio 174, ch. 2 n. 28). Boivin renders the name "the fiancée of the red"; Kasmani, the bride of Lal.
- **The woman who gives water.** Abbas calls her "the woman faqiriānī or caretaker" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 160, folio not stated) and "a female muridīānī" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 20, folio not stated); her plate is captioned "Serving devotees: Murīdīānī at Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar, Sehwan Sharif" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 161, folio not stated).
- **When the women singers come.** Abbas's women informants in Sehwan knew of them during the urs and "did not have much information" about other times; professionals at Radio Pakistan said they come "only at the time of the urs celebrations" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 95, folio not stated); elsewhere Abbas writes that they find an audience "at any time of the night or day" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 33, folio not stated).
- **When Reshma was discovered.** Abbas: "in the sixties" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 94, folio not stated) and "the late sixties" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 56, folio not stated).

## Legacy

Lal Shahbaz Qalandar embodies the ecstatic, boundary-crossing pole of South Asian Sufism — the qalandar who dances his way to God and gathers all comers, of every faith and station, into the rhythm of divine love. Through the song that bears his spirit and the dhamal of his shrine, his name is known far beyond Sindh, and his darbar at Sehwan remains one of the great living centres of devotion in Pakistan, a place where sanctity is celebrated not in silence but in drums, colour, and dance.

The books read that legacy in their own terms. For Kasmani, "Muhammad, Ali, or Ram, his memory spans religious divides" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, pp. 57–58, folios 40–41), and the plural ideas of the social and the divine that figures like Lal and Sehwan bear "will always be unsettling in a Muslim-national present that rests on a severance from its" polytheistic Hindu past (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 28, folio 11). "The saint is at the forefront of a struggle for spatial and imaginative dominion. He is more than a world-rejecting, miracle-making figure… Lal's portrayal is deeply implicated in the social and political life of the town." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 165, folio 148). For Boivin, the rites carried to India keep a tie to Sindh for its Hindus abroad: "The mehndi of Lal Shahbaz makes it possible to maintain a direct link through the ritual with the lost territory of Sindh." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 147, folio 134). Schimmel, less admiring, writes that around his sanctuary "strange groups" of 'lawless' Sufis, *malang*, gathered, dressed in black, "and the mystical current connected with Sehwan often shows Sufism in its least attractive aspects." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 386, folio 355).

He has his own literature. Abbas cites a monograph, Inam Muhammad's *Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar of Sehwan*, published at Jamshoro by the Institute of Sindhology (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 199, 229, folios not stated). Kasmani records the film *Der rote Sufi: Rausch und Ekstase in Pakistan*, made by Martin Weinhart with the ethnologist Jürgen W. Frembgen for Westdeutscher Rundfunk in 2011 (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 189, folio 172, ch. 1 n. 31), and observes that "Whether Shiʿi or Shivaite, Sunni or Sufi, Sehwan, despite its prominence, has only haltingly attracted the interest of studies on Pakistan." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 44, folio 27). In a note on the same page, Schimmel says of Peter Mayne's *Saints of Sind* (London, 1956) that it "gives a vivid and often ironical account of the customs of so-called saints; yet his brilliantly written book contains much valuable information about little-known facts of saint worship and mystical thought in the Indus Valley." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 386, folio 355).
