## Overview

The shrine of Hazrat Shah Rukn-e-Alam (1251–1335) in Multan is at once the tomb of a great Suhrawardi saint and one of the supreme masterpieces of Islamic architecture in South Asia. Rukn-ud-Din Abul Fath — known by the honorific Rukn-e-Alam, “Pillar of the World” — was the grandson and spiritual heir of Bahauddin Zakariya, and under him the Suhrawardi order of Multan reached the height of its fame. His towering domed mausoleum, rising over the old citadel, is the emblem of Multan and among the most beautiful pre-Mughal buildings in the subcontinent.

The books consulted know him chiefly as a shaikh of Multan who moved among sultans. Zahur ul-Hasan Sharib opens his chapter on him with the words "حضرت شیخ رکن الدین ابو الفتح خورشید سپہر ہدایت ہیں", he is the sun of the sky of guidance (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 113, folio 109). Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori's *Hadiqat al-Awliya* gives him a notice of his own, entry no. 105, under the name "شیخ رکن الدین ابو الفتح بن شیخ صدر الدین عارف بن شیخ بہاء الدین زکریا ملتانی" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 164–166, folios 152–154). Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi's history of Sufism in India records a hundred villages granted for his khanqah, sums of hundreds of thousands of *tankas* from a sultan on each visit to Delhi, a palanquin filled with petitions to the throne, and a visit from Ibn Battuta in 1333 (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 231, folio 211; p. 232, folio 212; p. 233, folio 213). The people, Lahori and Sharib both write, called him *Qibla-e Hajat*, the Qibla of needs, because no one in need left him empty-handed (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 166, folio 154; Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 116–117, folios 112–113).

## The Life of the Saint

Rukn-ud-Din was born in Multan in 1251, the grandson of Baha-ud-Din Zakariya and son of Sadr-ud-Din Arif, into the leading Suhrawardi family of the region. Raised in the great khanqah his grandfather had founded, he was schooled in the religious sciences and the discipline of the order, and in due course succeeded to its spiritual leadership, becoming the foremost shaykh of the Suhrawardis in India. He was held in the highest honour by the Delhi Sultanate — the Tughluq sultans in particular revered him — and he moved between Multan and the capital as a figure of immense spiritual and moral authority, interceding with rulers and guiding a vast body of disciples. He died in 1335 and was buried in Multan, in the mausoleum that is said to have been built originally for the Tughluq sultan Ghiyath-ud-Din and then given over to the saint.

## His Names

The books give him several names. Sharib writes that his grandfather named him: "آپ کے دادا حضرت بہاؤ الدین زکریا رحمۃ اللہ علیہ نے آپ کا نام رکن الدین رکھا"; his *kunya* was Abu al-Fath, and "آپ کا لقب 'فضل اللہ' ہے", his *laqab* is Fazl Allah (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 113, folio 109). Sharib does not use the title Rukn-e-Alam. Lahori's contents call him "شیخ رکن الدین ابوالفتح" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 17, folio 5), and elsewhere the book names him "شیخ رکن الدین ابوالفتح سہروردی" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 252, folio 240) and, as a link in a chain of masters, "شیخ رکن الدین ابوالفتح ملتانی" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 184). The editor of Lahori's book uses the title, "حضرت شاہ رکن عالم", in a note (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 165, folio 153, حاشیہ 1).

Rizvi calls him "Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din Abu'l Fath" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 230, folio 210), and once, in a saying reported by Shaikh Nasir al-Din Chiragh Dihlawi, 'Shaikhu'l-Islam Ruknu'l Haqq Wa'd-Din' (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 234, folio 214). Rizvi's index has two headwords that it does not join, "Ruknu'd-Din Multani, Shaikh (d. 1334—35)" and "Ruknu'd-Din Abu'l-Fath" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 482, folio 458), and the same book names a different man of almost the same name, "Saiyid Ruknu'd-Din Abu'l-Fath, the son of Saiyid Hamid Kabir", of Uch (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 304, folio 282). Michel Boivin writes "Shah Rukn-e Alam" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 187, folio 174); Robert Rozehnal, "Shaykh Rukn ad-Din Abul Fatah Suhrawardi" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 57, folio 47); and Shemeem Burney Abbas, "Rukunuddin Shah Alam" and "Shaikh Rukunuddin Multani" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 20, folio not stated; p. 193, folio not stated).

## Birth and Family

His mother was a saint in her own right. Lahori writes "حضرت کی والدہ کا نام بی بی راستی تھا جو اپنے وقت میں رابعہ وقت تھیں", that his mother's name was Bibi Rasti and that she was the Rabia of her time (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 166, folio 154). In a separate notice of her Lahori calls her "والدہ شیخ رکن الدین ابوالفتح سہروردی کی تھی۔ عابدہ و زاہدہ و حافظہ و متقیہ کہ ہر روز ایک مرتبہ قرآن ختم کرتی", a worshipper, an ascetic, a *hafiza* and a God-fearing woman who completed the Quran once every day; she was a disciple of his grandfather, and she died in 695 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 252, folio 240). The book records no grave for her. Sharib likewise writes "آپ کی والدہ ماجد بی بی راستی رابعہ عصر اور حافظہ کلام اللہ تھیں' وہ حضرت بہاء الدین زکریا رحمۃ اللہ علیہ ملتانی کی مرید تھیں", the Rabia of the age and a *hafiza* of the Quran, a *murid* of Bahauddin Zakariya Multani, whose practice was "روزانہ ایک قرآن ختم کرنا", to complete one Quran daily (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 113, folio 109). Rizvi, who does not name her, writes that "The latter's mother was a most pious lady" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 230, folio 210).

His grandfather foretold him before his birth. Lahori tells that when the pregnant Bibi Rasti came into Bahauddin Zakariya's presence "وہ تعظیم کو اٹھ کھڑے ہوتے", he would rise in respect, "بخلاف عادت بزرگان", against the custom of the elders, and when asked why he said "یہ تعظیم تیرے واسطے نہیں بلکہ اس شخص کے واسطے ہے جو تیرے بطن عفت میں ہے کہ وہ ایک آفتاب اوج ولایت و ماہتاب برج شرافت ہو گا", that the respect was not for her but for the one in her womb, who would be a sun at the height of sainthood and a moon of nobility (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 166, folio 154). Sharib gives the saying as "یہ تعظیم اس بچے کی ہے۔ جو تیرے پیٹ میں ہے۔ وہ چراغ خاندان و شمع دو دمان کا ہو گا", this respect is for the child in your womb, who will be the lamp of the family and the candle of two houses, and adds that the child was then "سات ماہ کے اپنی والدہ کے شکم مبارک میں", seven months in the womb (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 113, folio 109). Rizvi too records a prenatal prophecy by his grandfather (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 230–231, folios 210–211).

Sharib dates his birth: "آپ نے 9 رمضان 649ھ کو بروز جمعہ اس عالم کو زینت بخشی", on Friday, 9 Ramadan 649 AH (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 113, folio 109). The Arabic source that Lahori's editor quotes spells out his birth year as "سنۃ سبع و اربعین و ستمایۃ", 647 AH; in the editor's own Urdu the figure is uncertain in the printed text (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 165, folio 153).

His father, in Lahori, Rizvi and most of Sharib, is Shaikh Sadr al-Din Arif, Bahauddin Zakariya's son. Rizvi writes "The successor of Shaikh Sadru'd-Din was his son, Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din Abu'l Fath" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 230, folio 210), and calls him Bahauddin Zakariya's "grandson" in so many words (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 236, folio 216). Sharib writes "آپ حضرت شیخ بہاء الدین زکریا رحمۃ اللہ علیہ ملتانی کے پوتے ہیں", he is the grandson of Shaikh Bahauddin Zakariya Multani, and names the father in his narrative as "حضرت شیخ صدر الدین عارف" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 113, folio 109; p. 114, folio 110); but the rubric of the same chapter that gives the father's name reads "آپ کے والد ماجد کا نام رکن الدین [OCR?] ہے", which would make father and son namesakes, the reading uncertain in the printed text (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 113, folio 109). The book does not reconcile the two.

He had a brother, whom Sharib names "شیخ عماد الدین اسمٰعیل" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 115–116, folios 111–112). Rizvi's text names the brother "Shaikh Maulana 'IImu'd-Din [OCR?]", the reading uncertain in the printed text, and his footnote adds that the *Siyar al-Auliya* gives the brother's name as Maulana 'Imad al-Millat wa'd-Din Isma'il, 'Imad al-Din being, it seems, his title (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 231, folio 211, footnote 4). Sharib records a question of doctrine that the brother put to him: "حضرت محمد مصطفٰی صلی اللہ علیہ وسلم کی ہجرت میں کیا حکمت تھی؟", what was the wisdom in the Prophet's *hijra*? Rukn-ud-Din's answer turned on the "اصحاب صفہ", and Nizam al-Din Auliya's on the poor of Medina who could not otherwise reach the Prophet's company (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 115, folio 111).

He had no children. Lahori: "حضرت لاولد تھے" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 166, folio 154); Sharib: "آپ کے کوئی اولاد نہیں تھی" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 116, folio 112).

## Upbringing, Discipline and Succession

Sharib writes that his "تعلیم و تربیت", his education and upbringing, were under his grandfather's supervision (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 114, folio 110). He was bound to prayer and fasting from the age of seven, prayed in congregation, kept the night prayer of *tahajjud* and the voluntary prayers of *ishraq* and *chasht*, fasted in the Ashura of Muharram besides Ramadan, and practised silent and spoken *zikr*, *muraqaba* and *muhasaba*; "کشف قلوب' طے ارض و طے لسان میں دس سال کی عمر سے ممتاز تھے' کمالات صوری و معنوی آپ کو پچیس سال کی عمر سے حاصل تھے", he was distinguished in the reading of hearts and in the folding of earth and tongue from the age of ten, and possessed of the outward and inward perfections from the age of twenty-five (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 116, folio 112). Of his table Sharib writes "خوراک آپ کی کم تھی' لیکن کھانا اچھا کھاتے تھے", he ate little, but ate well (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 116, folio 112).

His succession was marked by a turban. Sharib tells that at four years old he found his grandfather's "دستار" lying on the leg of the bed and, at play, put it on his own head; his father Sadr al-Din Arif forbade him, and Bahauddin Zakariya answered "بابا صدر الدین! تم اس کو منع نہ کرو' کیوں کہ اس نے دستار بطور حق کے اپنے سر پر رکھی ہے' میں نے یہ دستار اس کو دی", do not forbid him, for he has put the turban on his head as of right; I have given him this turban. Then "اپنے والد کی وفات کے بعد جب آپ سجادہ نشین ہوئے تو آپ نے وہی دستار سر پر رکھی اور حضرت شیخ شہاب الدین سہروردی رحمۃ اللہ علیہ کا خرقہ جو آپ کو والد سے ملا تھا' زیب تن فرمایا", when he became *sajjada nashin* after his father's death he put on that same turban and wore the *khirqa* of Shaikh Shihab al-Din Suhrawardi which had come to him from his father (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 114, folio 110). Rizvi records the turban at the age of four (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 230–231, folios 210–211), and at the succession "he wore the same turban and Shaikh Sadru'd-Din's khirga [OCR?] [khirqa]" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 231, folio 211).

Sharib makes him "آپ اپنے والد ماجد حضرت شیخ صدر الدین عارف کے مرید اور خلیفہ اعظم ہیں", the *murid* and chief *khalifa* of his father, who after his father's passing became *sajjada nashin* and gave himself to teaching, *talqin* and guidance (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 114, folio 110). Lahori writes that on his father's death he sat on the seat of guidance: "حضرت کو دولت ظاہری و باطنی نصیب ہوئی۔ سلاطین وقت حضرت کی خدمت کو اپنا فخر جانتے تھے", he was granted outward and inward wealth, and the sultans of the time counted his service their pride (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 166, folio 154). Lahori's editor, from the *Lataif-e Ashrafi*, gives the length of his tenure: "و اقام فی سجادہ جدہ لبعد ابیہ اثنتین و خمسین سنۃ", fifty-two years on his grandfather's *sajjada*, after his father (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 165, folio 153, حاشیہ 1). Nur Ahmad Chishti's *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* names him instead a disciple of "شیخ بہاؤ الدین ذکریا ملتانی" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 154), and, in a chain whose reading is damaged, sets a "رکن عالم" between Nasir al-Din and Sadr al-Din Arif (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 399). Boivin writes that "Shah Rukn-e Alam was none other than the grandson and successor of Baha al-Din Zakariyya", both of them "members of the Sohrawardi order" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 187, folio 174).

Rizvi adds that he may have had a disciple whom others give to his father: "As he was about fourteen years old when Shaikh Sadru'd-Din died, many biographers believe he was in fact the disciple of Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din, the son of Shaikh Sadru'd-Din", of Amir Husain (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 226, folio 206).

## Sultans and Delhi

The sultans of Delhi honoured him with money and ceremony. Sharib writes "سلطان علاء الدین آپ کا بہت معتقد تھا' آپ جس دن دہلی میں رونق افروز ہوتے تو وہ دو لاکھ ٹنکہ بطور شکرانہ آپ کی خدمت میں بھیجتا اور جس روز دہلی سے روانہ ہوتے پانچ لاکھ ٹنکہ پیش کرتا' اس کی درخواست منظور کرکے جب آپ اس سے ملنے جاتے تو وہ تیسرے دروازے پر آپ کا استقبال کرتا": Sultan Ala al-Din sent two lakh *tankas* as a thanksgiving on the day he arrived in Delhi and presented five lakh *tankas* on the day he left, and when he granted the sultan's request and went to meet him, the sultan received him at the third gate (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 115, folio 111). Sultan Qutb al-Din likewise "بہت اعتقاد رکھتا تھا اور آپ کی بہت تعظیم و تکریم کرتا تھا", held him in great faith and honoured him greatly (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 115–116, folios 111–112). Rizvi gives the same sums: "In the reign of Sultan 'Ala'u'd-Din Khalji, Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din visited Delhi twice. The Sultan himself went to welcome him and rode back with him to the city. During each visit he paid 200,000 tankas at the Shaikh's arrival and 500,000 tankas at his departure. The Shaikh always distributed the entire amount among Delhi's population and naturally his visits were a source of great jubilation to the people" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 231, folio 211). Lahori's editor, citing Jamali's *Sair al-Arifin*, writes "سلطان علاؤ الدین خلجی آپ کا بڑا معتقد تھا۔ حضرت اس کی زندگی میں دہلی بھی گئے … سلطان نے نذر پیش کی", that Ala al-Din Khalji was his great devotee, that he went to Delhi in the sultan's lifetime, and that the sultan presented a *nazr* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 165, folio 153, حاشیہ 1).

He was a broker of petitions to the throne. On his palanquin, Rizvi writes, "there was a pile of papers. Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din mentioned that they were petitions from the needy and had been given to him so that he could recommend them to the Sultan" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 232, folio 212). And "whenever Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din went to the court of Sultan Qutbu'd-Din, the people of Delhi would place petitions inside the Shaikh's takht-i rawan (a type of palanquin similar to a moveable throne)… The petitions would then be brought in and, after having read them, the Sultan would write sympathetic replies. According to a sufi tradition, the Shaikh would remain at court until all the requests had been granted", the sultan meanwhile squatting respectfully at his feet (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 232, folio 212).

A sultan tried to set him against Nizam al-Din Auliya, and failed. Rizvi: "His three visits to the capital during Sultan Qutbu'd-Din's reign were, however, sponsored by the Sultan who wished that Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din's presence might eclipse that of Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya'. But the close friendship between the two great Shaikhs frustrated the Sultan's hopes and he could not arouse the tiniest jealousy between them" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 231, folio 211). Sharib counts the same visits, "دو بار سلطان علاء الدین کے عہد میں اور تین بار سلطان قطب الدین کے عہد میں", twice in Ala al-Din's reign and three times in Qutb al-Din's (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 114, folio 110). In Qutb al-Din's reign, Sharib writes, Nizam al-Din came out from his khanqah as far as "حوض خاص علائی [OCR?]" to receive him, and they met "اس وقت کی جامع مسجد میں", in the Jami Masjid of the time (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 114, folio 110). When the sultan asked who had received him first, he answered "سب سے پہلے اس شخص نے استقبال کیا' جو اہل شہر میں بہترین شخص ہے", the first to receive me was the best man of the city, meaning Nizam al-Din (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 116, folio 112); Rizvi: "Unhesitatingly the Shaikh replied that the leading figure amongst the élite was the one who had met him first, that is, Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 231, folio 211).

Rizvi records his infirmity and his courtesy together: "Although Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din had a serious problem with his leg, he ordered his men to lift him from his palanquin so he could show his respect for Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din, but the latter prevented him from doing so" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 231, folio 211). On "the tenth Zu'lhijja, the day when pilgrimages to Mecca were performed", with Nizam al-Din ill, "Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din decided instead to visit the Shaikh in order to gain from him the blessings of a hajj" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 232, folio 212). Sharib writes that he came from Multan to Delhi to visit Nizam al-Din in his illness, led his funeral prayer, and afterwards "آپ ملتان تشریف لے گئے", returned to Multan (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 115, folio 111).

A gift passed between the two shaikhs. Sharib tells that after a meal at Nizam al-Din's, "حضرت نظام الدین اولیاء رحمۃ اللہ علیہ کے خاص خادم اقبال نے ریشمی کپڑوں کے چند تھان اور سو اشرفیاں ایک باریک کپڑے میں رکھ کر آپ کے قدموں پر رکھیں' آپ نے قبول نہیں کیا۔ حضرت محبوب الٰہی نے وہ تھان اور اشرفیاں آپ کے بھائی شیخ عماد الدین اسمٰعیل کے سپرد کیں", Nizam al-Din's own servant Iqbal laid several bolts of silk and a hundred *ashrafis*, wrapped in a fine cloth, at his feet; he would not accept them, and Nizam al-Din handed them to his brother Imad al-Din Ismail (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 115, folio 111). Rizvi: "Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din's servant, Iqbal, presented Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din with several pieces of fine cloth and a purse of gold coins from his master, but the latter would not accept them and asked his brother, Maulana 'IImu'd-Din [OCR?] ['Ilmu'd-Din] to accept them instead" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 232, folio 212).

With the Tughluqs his relations were close. "Unlike his relations with the Chishti sufis of Delhi, those between Sultan Muhammad bin Tughluq and Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din were most cordial", Rizvi writes (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 232, folio 212). In the pages that follow Rizvi writes of the Sultan that "Marching towards Multan he was met by Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 233, folio 213), and that "The Sultan awarded Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din one hundred villages for the upkeep of his khangah [OCR?] [khanqah]" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 233, folio 213); he does not date the grant or name the villages. Of a battle he writes: "In a ruse to confuse the enemy the Sultan placed Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din's brother, Shaikh 'Imadu'd-Din, who closely resembled himself, under the royal canopy. 'Imadu'd-Din was killed" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 233, folio 213).

## The Massacre at Multan

When Multan rebelled against Muhammad bin Tughluq, the saint is remembered as having saved the city. Lahori's editor, citing Isami's *Futuh al-Salatin*, writes "ملتان میں کشلو خان نے بغاوت کی اور محمد تغلق نے اسے شکست دے کر ملتان میں خون کی ندیاں بہانے کا حکم دیا تو حضرت شاہ رکن عالم ننگے پاؤں بادشاہ کے پاس سفارش کے لیے گئے": when Kishlu Khan rebelled in Multan, and Muhammad Tughluq, having defeated him, ordered rivers of blood to flow in Multan, Hazrat Shah Rukn-e-Alam went barefoot to the king to intercede (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 165, folio 153, حاشیہ 1).

Rizvi opens the episode with "In 1327-28, Bahram Aiba Kishlu Khan, the Governor of Multan," (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 232, folio 212), and tells it in two versions. The Sultan "flayed the Qazi alive and ordered a general massacre of the population. According to 'Isami, Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din retreated into meditation for seven days, while the inhabitants were massacred; and emerging from isolation, he then interceded with the Sultan in an effort to save further lives. This story, however, would appear apocryphal. Barani says the Shaikh approached the Sultan soon after he learnt of his intention to massacre the population, and that the Sultan relented on his request for clemency" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 233, folio 213).

## The Death of Ghiyath al-Din Tughluq

Rizvi tells how the saint left a pavilion shortly before it fell on a sultan. He "went to receive Sultan Ghiyasu'd-Din Tughluq at the village of Afghanpur, about five miles from the city. He lunched with the Sultan in a specially constructed palace and left to perform afternoon prayers at the suggestion of Muhammad bin Tughlugq [OCR?] [Tughluq], the Sultan's son. After the Shaikh left, the palace collapsed and Sultan Ghiyasu'd-Din was crushed to death under its roof" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 232, folio 212). Rizvi draws no miraculous inference from it. It is to this same Sultan Ghiyath-ud-Din that the site's account attributes the first building of the mausoleum.

## His Khanqah and His Wealth

His khanqah drew visitors from far away. "Following the tradition set by his father and grandfather, the Shaikh's khangah [OCR?] [khanqah] was a busy rendezvous for distinguished visitors from many countries west of-the Indus" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 233, folio 213). "Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din's fame reached as far as Alexandria, being spread by merchants who visited Multan, and Ibn Battuta was recommended to see him. In 1333, after reaching Multan Ibn Battuta stayed with one of the Shaikh's disciples", and the conversation Ibn Battuta had with the shaikh, Rizvi writes, reached even to political matters (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 233, folio 213).

Sharib describes the house from within. A *murid* "خانقاہ کے حجرہ میں عبادت میں مشغول تھا", was occupied in worship in a cell of the khanqah, and Rukn-ud-Din ruled that the *hajj* was not obligatory on him "کیوں کہ وہ ایک فقیر ہے", because he was a *faqir* (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 119, folio 115). Makhdum Jahaniyan, Sharib writes, "ملتان میں آپ شیخ رکن الدین ابو الفتح کی اجازت سے مدرسے میں رہتے تھے", lived at Multan in the *madrasa* by Shaikh Rukn-ud-Din Abu al-Fath's permission (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 134, folio 130). The shaikh had a boat of his own: when there was friction between Makhdum Jahaniyan's father and Shaikh Jamal al-Din Khandan, "انہوں نے آپ کو اپنی خاص کشتی پر سوار کرا کے اونچہ بھجوایا", he put the young man on his own boat and sent him to Uch, and on receiving the message "آپ کے والد اسی وقت حضرت شیخ جمال الدین خنداں کے پاس گئے اور ان سے بغل گیر ہوئے", the father went at once to Jamal al-Din Khandan and embraced him (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 134, folio 130). A devotee, Sharib writes, "کو آپ کے یہاں سے چار قرص ملا کرتے تھے", used to receive four loaves from his house (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 119, folio 115).

What came in went out again. Sharib writes "جو نذرانہ آتا' آپ اس کو خرچ کر دیتے تھے۔ اس میں سے کچھ نہیں بچاتے تھے۔ سلطان علاء الدین جب آپ دہلی آتے لاکھوں ٹنکے آپ کو پیش کرتا' آپ وہ سب رقم خرچ کر دیتے تھے", whatever offering came he spent, saving nothing of it, and when Sultan Ala al-Din presented lakhs of *tankas* on his visits to Delhi he spent the whole sum (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 117, folio 113). Hence his name among the people: "جو حاجت مند آپ کے پاس آتا' خالی ہاتھ نہیں جاتا' چونکہ آپ لوگوں کی حاجت پوری کرتے تھے۔ اس واسطے لوگوں میں آپ 'قبلہ حاجات' مشہور ہوئے", no needy person went away empty-handed, and so he became famous among the people as the *Qibla-e Hajat* (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 116–117, folios 112–113). Lahori says the same: "دنیا اور دین کا حاجت مند جو ان کی خدمت میں آتا خالی نہ جاتا۔ اس لیے لوگ ان کو قبلہ حاجات کہتے تھے", whoever came to him in need of this world or the next did not go away empty, and so people called him *Qibla-e Hajat* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 166, folio 154).

He defended a shaikh's wealth in so many words. Shaikh Nasir al-Din Chiragh Dihlawi related that when he visited Delhi *qalandars* asked him for sherbet and another class of dervishes, whose name is uncertain in the printed text, for money, and that he satisfied them and said: "The leaders of the community should possess three things. Firstly, they should have property in order to satisfy their demands…"; secondly knowledge, to hold scholarly discussions with the *ulama*; and thirdly *hal*, mystical enlightenment, to impress dervishes. To which Shaikh Nasir al-Din "added that money was not necessary—only knowledge and hal were essential" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 234, folio 214). Rizvi reads the exchange as a sign of the ideological differences between him and his Chishti counterparts, and writes that "Qalandars were intolerable to Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din but the attitude of his grandson, Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din, was different. He tolerated them as he considered that their persistent demands and those of other visitors justified sufi Shaikhs possessing wealth" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 236, folio 216).

Sharib tells of a rich man who became his *murid* and repented, and on whom he bestowed a *kulah*, a cap. "ایک درویش نے اس پر اعتراض کیا", a dervish objected, and he answered "اے عزیز! اگر وہ بوجہ ایک کلاہ کے گناہ سے باز آجائے اور اس کی جہت [OCR?] سے بخشا جائے تو میں کس لئے اس کو کلاہ نہ دوں", if by means of one cap he gives up sin and is forgiven on its account, why should I not give him a cap? (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 117, folio 113). Of his humility Sharib writes "کسر نفسی کا یہ عالم تھا کہ جب پالکی میں نکلتے تو آپ کے دونوں ہاتھ باہر نکلے ہوئے ہوتے", when he went out in his palanquin both his hands hung outside it, so that perhaps the hand of some forgiven person might touch his and he too be forgiven; and he took great care of "علماء' فضلاء اور درویشوں", scholars, learned men and dervishes, honouring and entertaining them (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 117, folio 113).

## His Teaching

His teaching survives at second hand. Rizvi writes: "Shaikh 'Abdu'l-Haqq mentions a number of works containing the teachings of Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din, including his Malfuzat; none of these however still exist" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 233, folio 213). Lahori's editor finds it in his *khalifas'* works, the *Fatawa-e Sufiya* of Fazl Allah bin Ayyub, the *Majma al-Akhbar* and the commentary *Kanz al-Ibad*, and adds "آپ کے ملفوظات کا ایک خطی نسخہ پروفیسر قریشی احمد حسین احمد صاحب گجرات کے کتب خانہ میں ہے", that a manuscript copy of his *malfuzat* is in the library of Professor Qureshi Ahmad Husain Ahmad (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 165, folio 153, حاشیہ 1).

Rizvi summarises it. He "exhorted his disciples to abandon cruelty, oppression, avarice and greed, as he believed such vices rendered human beings to the level of beasts. Self-purification came only through humility and prayer", citing "'I do not exculpate myself. Lo! the (human) soul enjoineth unto evil, save whereon my Lord hath mercy. Lo! My Lord is Forgiving and Merciful.'" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 233, folio 213). He taught that "purification of the self in the final analysis depended on divine grace", on the verse "'Had it not been for the grace of Allah and His mercy unto you, not one of you would ever have grown pure.'", and the mark of divine grace and mercy lay in an insight into one's own faults (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 234, folio 214).

Sharib, under the heading of his teachings, gives two: on "صورت اور صفت", form and attribute, that judgement rests on the attribute and not the form, and that its verification appears only in the hereafter, where each person is gathered in the form that answers to his attribute; and on "اعمال پر متابعت", that the limbs be held back, in word and deed, from what the *sharia* forbids or dislikes, and that idle gatherings and the company of "بطالوں", idlers, be avoided (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 117, folio 113; pp. 117–118, folios 113–114).

## Disciples and Successors

Lahori's editor names five *khalifas*: Maulana Zahir al-Din, Sultan Hamid al-Din Hakim, Shaikh Fakhr al-Din Iraqi, Makhdum Jahaniyan Jahangasht Bukhari and Shaikh Sama al-Din Dehlvi (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 165, folio 153, حاشیہ 1). Sharib announces six "ممتاز خلفاء", eminent *khalifas*, and names Sayyid Jalal al-Din Bukhari, called Makhdum Jahaniyan Jahangasht; Shaikh Usman Sayyah; Haji Shaikh Sadr al-Din Arif; Haji Chiragh Din; and Shaikh Hamid al-Din, called Sultan al-Tarikin, whose last name is uncertain in the printed text (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 116, folio 112). Elsewhere he names another, "حضرت شیخ قوام الدین" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 135, folio 131).

Shaikh Usman Sayyah, Rizvi writes, "Shaikh 'Usman Sayyah of Sunnam", was enrolled "near Kilukhari when the latter was performing prayers on the banks of the Jumna", taught "the 'Awarifu'l-Ma'arif" at Multan, and on returning from seven years' travel was "given the honour of being presented with Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din's own garment and turban" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 235, folio 215).

His best-known disciple was Makhdum Jahaniyan. Rizvi writes that "Later he obtained initiation into the Suhrawardiyya order from Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 299, folio 277); Rozehnal, that Makhdum-i Jahaniyan "completed his training under Shaykh Rukn ad-Din Abul Fatah Suhrawardi and Nasir ad-Din Chirag-i Delhi" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 57, folio 47); Chishti makes him the master of "سید جلال الدین بخاری" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 154). Sharib gives the relation in detail. The shaikh conferred the *khirqa* of *khilafat* on him in person, and another *khirqa* "عالم خواب میں پہنا اور بیدار ہونے کے بعد وہ سر پر پایا", worn in a dream and found on his head on waking (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 135, folio 131). When Makhdum Jahaniyan held the office of *Shaikh al-Islam* and the seat of the Khanqah-e Muhammadi at Sehwan, "حضرت شیخ رکن الدین ابو الفتح رحمۃ اللہ علیہ کے حکم سے ملازمت سے مستعفی ہو گئے", he resigned the post at Shaikh Rukn-ud-Din's command (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 136, folio 132). Once he lay down below the threshold, the "دہلیز", so that the shaikh might step down upon him, at which "انہوں نے آپ کا ہاتھ پکڑا اور اپنا سینہ آپ کے سینے سے ملایا", the shaikh took his hand and pressed him to his breast (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 137, folio 133, حاشیہ 12); and once he bathed in the water that ran off from the shaikh's *ghusl*, and when the shaikh learned of it "آپ کو نعمت خاص سے سرفراز فرمایا", he honoured him with a special favour (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 137–138, folios 133–134, حاشیہ 13).

## His Death and the Succession

He prepared his own death. Lahori writes that for three months beforehand he shut himself in his *hujra* day and night, "سوائے ادائے نماز پنجگانہ فریضہ کے باہر نہ آتے", coming out only for the five obligatory prayers; then, after the afternoon prayer on 16 Rajab 735 AH, he called Maulana Zahir al-Din and "حکم دیا کہ سامان تجہیز و تکفین مہیا کرے", ordered him to make ready what was needed for his burial and shroud; and he led the sunset prayer as *imam* and died in the prostration of the last *rak'a* of the supererogatory prayers (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 166, folio 154). Sharib: "وفات سے تین ماہ قبل گوشہ تنہائی اختیار کر لی تھی۔ 16' رجب 735ھ کو جوار رحمت میں داخل ہوئے", three months before his death he took to seclusion, and on 16 Rajab 735 AH he entered the neighbourhood of mercy (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 116, folio 112). The Arabic that Lahori's editor quotes gives instead the night of Friday, 9 Jumada al-Awwal 735 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 165, folio 153). Rizvi writes that "Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din died in 735/1334-35" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 234, folio 214); Boivin gives "(d. 1335)" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 187, folio 174).

After him the seat passed sideways, and was contested. Lahori: "ان کی وفات کے بعد محمد اسماعیل ان کے بھائی کی اولاد سجادہ نشین ہوئی", after his death Muhammad Ismail, of his brother's offspring, became *sajjada nashin* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 166, folio 154). Sharib: "آپ کی وفات کے بعد آپ کے بھائی حضرت عماد الدین اسمٰعیل آپ کے سجادہ نشین ہوئے", after his death his brother Imad al-Din Ismail became his *sajjada nashin* (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 116, folio 112). Rizvi gives two accounts side by side: "According to Jamali, his nephew, Shaikh Isma'il, succeeded him. Ibn Battuta says that Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din nominated his grandson, Shaikh Hud, as his successor, but his nephew challenged the claim. The dispute was laid before Muhammad bin Tughluq who gave his verdict in favour of Shaikh Hud. After some time, the governor of Sind accused Shaikh Hud of misappropriating the income of Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din's khangah [OCR?] [khanqah] for his own personal use. The Sultan ordered the governor to seize the entire property and the Shaikh was reduced to a miserable condition" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 234, folio 214). Shaikh Hud was then "executed on a trumped-up charge of attempting to mastermind a Mongol invasion of India, using as a war-cry the ill-treatment of Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya's descendants by the Sultan" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 234, folio 214). "As discussed in the previous chapter, after the death of Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din the order declined in Multan only to be rejuvenated in Uch" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 299, folio 277).

## The Great Mausoleum

The tomb of Shah Rukn-e-Alam, raised in the early fourteenth century, is one of the glories of the age. Octagonal in plan, built of red brick banded with timber and dressed with carved and glazed blue-and-white tilework, it rises in three tapering tiers to a high white dome — a colossal structure that dominates the skyline of Multan from its perch on the mound of the old fort. It is counted among the earliest and finest monuments of the Tughluq style, a model that influenced tomb architecture across the region for generations, and it has been repaired and restored over the centuries, receiving in modern times awards for its conservation. For the people of Multan it is not only a shrine but the very symbol of their city.

The books speak of the tomb as a place of pilgrimage rather than as a building. Lahori: "روضہ منورہ حضرت کا ملتان میں زیارت گاہ خلق ہے", his luminous *rauza* at Multan is a place of pilgrimage for the people (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 166, folio 154). Sharib: "مزار پر انوار ملتان میں مرجع خاص و عام ہے", the luminous *mazar* at Multan is a resort of high and low (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 116, folio 112). Shemeem Burney Abbas, who counts Multan among her places of fieldwork, "Hazrat Bahauddin Zakariya and Rukunuddin Shah Alam in Multan" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 20, folio not stated), writes: "I have visited the shrine of Rukunuddin several times, adjacent to the shrine of Hazrat Bahauddin Zakariya in Multan" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 193, folio not stated).

None of the books consulted describes the fabric of the tomb, names its builder, gives the year in which it was built, or names its custodians, endowment or *ʿurs*.

## Devotion and Practice

Shah Rukn-e-Alam’s shrine is a centre of continuous devotion, thronged by pilgrims who come to recite the Fatiha, seek intercession, and honour a saint whose blessing (*baraka*) is felt to guard the city. The daily prayers, qawwali, and the free kitchen are kept there, and the annual urs draws great crowds from across southern Punjab.

Abbas names him last in a list of five shrines at which *qawwali* is held nightly after the prayers (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 42, folio not stated); the phrase stands as "Bahauddin Zakariya, and Rukunud-din Shah Alam", and whether 'Shah Alam' there completes his name or names a further shrine is not certain. Sharib records a night vigil at his grave: the subject of one of his chapters, on the night of Eid, after visiting another grave, "پھر صدر الدین عارف اور [OCR?] حضرت رکن الدین ابو الفتح کے مزارات پر حاضر ہوئے", then presented himself at the graves of Sadr al-Din Arif and Hazrat Rukn al-Din Abu al-Fath; the sentence does not name the town, and a word joining the two names is uncertain in the printed text (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 133, folio 129). Abbas counts him in her own family's descent, the second name in her account of her mother's ancestry (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 25, folio not stated).

## Legends and Miracles

Sharib relates that when the Mughals came near Multan and the people asked him for prayers, he went into *muraqaba* and then said "مغل چلے گئے", the Mughals have gone; on reaching the river they scattered, and when he was asked why he said "خدا تعالٰی نے فرشتوں کا لشکر بھیج دیا اور ان کو منتشر کر دیا", God Most High sent an army of angels and scattered them (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 119, folio 115).

The same pages tell of loaves in Mecca. The devotee who used to receive four loaves from his house went to Mecca and complained that there he received none. A dervish told him "آپ ہر جمعہ کی رات کو یہاں آتے ہیں", that the shaikh comes here every Friday night, and showed him the spot; he found and saluted him, and from then on "چار قرض [sic] اور دو پیالے سالن برابر ملتے رہے", four loaves and two bowls of curry reached him without fail (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 119, folio 115).

A *murid* dreamed that he was told to go on *hajj*; the shaikh, on seeing him, said at once "وہ خواب شیطان کی وجہ سے تھا۔ مشغولی سے محروم کرنا چاہتا ہے", that dream came from Satan, who wishes to deprive you of your devotions (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 119, folio 115).

Rizvi records a prophecy sealed by an obeisance: "The Sultan kissed the Shaikh's feet and he in turn prophesied victory. The battle took place near Abuhar" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 233, folio 213).

In Sindh his name enters a birth legend that Boivin records. Boivin writes: "The legend of his birth is always identical, except that sometimes, as for example the case with the gaddi nashin, the Sufi Master was not Baha al-Din Zakariyya, but Shah Rukn-e Alam (d. 1335), another later saint of Multan." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 187, folio 174).

## A Shaikh Rukn al-Din in Sujan Rai's History

Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* mentions a "شیخ رکن الدین", and in places a "شیخ رکن الدین ملتانی", who may be this saint. The identification rests on the name and the Multan setting: the book never calls him Rukn-e-Alam, and in most of these passages gives him no tomb, date or descent. What follows is the book's, given as pointers only.

The book names a Shaikh Rukn al-Din Multani among the holy men of Ala al-Din's reign: "سلطان العارفین شیخ قطب الدین و قدوة الاصفیا شیخ نظام الدین اولیا و زبدة الواصلین شیخ صدر الدین عارف و شیخ رکن الدین ملتانی در زمان او بودند" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 361). It names a shaikh of this name, in the form "شیخ رکن الدین ابو الفتح سهروردی", as the one from whom Makhdum Jahaniyan "نیز خلافت یافت", also received *khilafat* (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527). In its account of the suba of Multan it heads its list of the saints who "دران شهر سعادت بهر اسوده اند و مزار مظهر انوار هریک زیارت گاه خلایق است", saints buried in Multan whose tombs are each a place of pilgrimage, with a Shaikh Rukn al-Din, there given as the son of Sadr al-Din Arif (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528).

In the book's history of the sultans the name recurs. Under Muhammad Tughluq, Malik Bahram, "برادر خوانده سلطان تغلق شاه", rebelled "در ملتان"; the sultan came to Multan from Daulatabad, Bahram was taken after a short fight and killed, and "سلطان خواست که اهل ملتان را قتل عام نماید به شفاعت شیخ الاسلام شیخ رکن الدین قدس الله سره نجات داده به دهلی مراجعت نمود": the sultan wished to put the people of Multan to a general massacre, they were spared at the intercession of the Shaikh al-Islam Shaikh Rukn al-Din, and the sultan returned to Delhi (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 347). Under Qutb al-Din Mubarak Shah, who was hostile to Nizam al-Din Auliya, the sultan favoured "شیخزاده جام را که از مخالفان مشایخ[OCR?] بود" and summoned Shaikh Rukn al-Din Multani to himself, "شیخ رکن الدین ملتانی را [illegible] نزد خود طلبیده نشسته"; a word before the summons is illegible, so that whether the summons was an honour is not clear (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 357). When Alf Khan and Zafar Khan besieged Multan in Ala al-Din's time, Jalal al-Din's sons came out and met the besiegers "بوسیله دانائی اسرار کونی و آگهی جویائے رموز سفیدی و سیاهی زبدة العارفین قدوة الواصلین شیخ رکن الدین قدس سره", through the mediation of a Shaikh Rukn al-Din (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 368–367); Alf Khan treated them with honour and brought them to Delhi, where the sultan had both princes and their companions blinded and imprisoned; the book does not say whether the shaikh's mediation carried any promise (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 367).

The book also tells, as one version among several, of a sultan's death in a falling pavilion: "شیخ رکن الدین ملتانی براے ملاقات سلطان در ان قصر رفته بود برمز و ایما[OCR?] بجهت زود[OCR?] بر خاستن میگفت اما سلطان تفهم[OCR?] نکرد چون شیخ برخاست قصر بر نشست", Shaikh Rukn al-Din Multani had gone into the pavilion to meet the sultan and urged him by hint and sign to rise quickly; the sultan did not understand, and when the shaikh rose the pavilion collapsed. The book introduces this with "بعضے مینویسند", some write, and calls a different account, in which Nizam al-Din figures, "انچه تحقیق پیوسته", the verified one (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 351).

## In Verse

The books consulted carry no verse by Shah Rukn-e-Alam, no poem written about him, and no inscription or chronogram from his tomb. Such verse as they give touches him only through his city and through the books that keep his memory.

The *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, which holds his notice, closes with chronograms giving the year of its own completion, 1292 AH; one, by the author's son Mufti Ghulam Safdar, is on the book's subject, the friends of God in the Punjab:

جو خدا کے دوست تھے پنجاب میں
حال ان کا اس سے ہوتا ہے عیان

*A chronogram for the completion of the book, on its subject, the saints of the Punjab* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 282, folio 270)

Multan's own fame enters the verse of Madho Lal Hussain, in Naveed Alam's translation, as the place of one item in a bride's trousseau:

*The dress / someone carried across the snows / all the way from Kashmir. The dress / someone brought from Gujarat… The dress bought in Multan* (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 51, folio 51, kafi 29)

## Setting: Multan

The books' wider accounts of Multan speak of the city, not of this shrine. Rizvi writes that "by the middle of the eleventh century, sufism had penetrated into the areas surrounding Multan" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 128, folio 110). Carl Ernst and Bruce Lawrence call the city "a strategic commercial center in the Punjab" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 80, folio 66), and record that "One time a qawwal, Abu Bakr by name, came from Multan to visit Nizam ad-Din's teacher" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 171, folio 159). Harish Dhillon writes that "By the time Nanak came to Multan, the city was famous as a major centre of Sufi learning and philosophy" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 167, folio 183), and Haroon Khalid that "At that time Multan was one of the biggest cities of Punjab and was known for the religious shrines of Muslim saints, and Jain and Hindu temples. It was a sacred space for people of all religious denominations" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 123, folio 105). Annemarie Schimmel writes that for the vernacular poets "Mecca and Medina (and for the Sindhi and Panjabi, also Multan) are inside the heart" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 178, folio 160).

The governors of Multan were bound up with the Tughluqs. Chishti's book records that "غازی بیگ تغلق صوبہ دار ملتان", the governor of Multan, raised an army, killed Khusrau Malik and took the throne as Ghiyas-ud-Din (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 814); Bhandari has Ghazi al-Malik march on Delhi "به اتفاق بهرام حاکم ملتان", together with Bahram, governor of Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 354). Kanhaiya Lal's history of Lahore, writing of the province, has Muhammad Tughluq behead "بہرام [OCR?] نام حاکم ملتان", a governor of Multan named Bahram, because a Tatar army had entered the Punjab at his signal (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 27, folio 30). None of these passages mentions the saint.

Bhandari, describing the suba of Multan in his own day, writes that it is "از شهرهاے پاستان است و قلعه خشتین دارد", among the ancient cities, with a fort of brick, that people of every kind live there, and that besides the saints he names "بسیاری از اولیاے دران شهر سعادت بهر اسوده اند", many saints rest in that blessed city (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528). Abbas writes of the city's shrines in her own day that musicians "perform in the family shrines of the Syeds" of Multan and Bahawalpur, supported by their large landholdings, and that the Syeds "arrange annual rituals such as the Eid and the Muharram ceremonies marking the tragedy of Kerbala"; she names no shrine (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 168–169, folios not stated).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The year of his birth.** The site's account gives 1251. Sharib gives Friday, 9 Ramadan 649 AH (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 113, folio 109). The Arabic quoted by Lahori's editor spells out 647 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 165, folio 153).
- **The date of his death.** The site's account gives 1335, as does Boivin (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 187, folio 174). Lahori's text and Sharib give 16 Rajab 735 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 166, folio 154; Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 116, folio 112); the Arabic quoted by Lahori's editor gives the night of Friday, 9 Jumada al-Awwal 735 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 165, folio 153). Rizvi gives 735/1334-35, and his index "(d. 1334—35)" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 234, folio 214; p. 482, folio 458).
- **His father's name.** The site's account, Lahori and Rizvi name Sadr al-Din Arif (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 164–166, folios 152–154; Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 230, folio 210), as does Sharib's narrative (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 114, folio 110); Sharib's rubric for the father gives "رکن الدین [OCR?]" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 113, folio 109).
- **Whose disciple he was.** Sharib makes him the *murid* and chief *khalifa* of his father (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 114, folio 110); Chishti, a disciple of Bahauddin Zakariya (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 154).
- **Whose successor he was.** The site's account calls him Bahauddin Zakariya's grandson and spiritual heir, and Boivin his "grandson and successor" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 187, folio 174). Rizvi makes him his father's successor (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 230, folio 210), Sharib his father's *sajjada nashin* (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 114, folio 110), and Lahori's editor has him sit fifty-two years on his grandfather's *sajjada* after his father (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 165, folio 153, حاشیہ 1).
- **The *khirqa* of his accession.** Sharib: the *khirqa* of Shaikh Shihab al-Din Suhrawardi, which had come to him from his father (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 114, folio 110). Rizvi: "Shaikh Sadru'd-Din's khirga [OCR?] [khirqa]" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 231, folio 211).
- **His brother's name.** Sharib: Imad al-Din Ismail (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 115–116, folios 111–112). Rizvi's text gives a name uncertain in the printed text, read as 'Ilmu'd-Din, and his footnote the *Siyar al-Auliya*'s 'Imadu'l-Millat wa'd-Din Isma'il, 'Imad al-Din being, Rizvi suggests, a title (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 231, folio 211, footnote 4).
- **His brother's fate.** Sharib has the brother, Imad al-Din Ismail, succeed him (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 116, folio 112). Rizvi has a brother, Shaikh 'Imad al-Din, killed under the royal canopy as the Sultan's double (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 233, folio 213).
- **Who succeeded him.** Lahori: Muhammad Ismail, of his brother's offspring (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 166, folio 154). Sharib: his brother Imad al-Din Ismail (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 116, folio 112). Rizvi: according to Jamali, his nephew Shaikh Isma'il; according to Ibn Battuta, his grandson Shaikh Hud, nominated by him and confirmed by Muhammad bin Tughluq against the nephew's challenge (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 234, folio 214).
- **Whether he had descendants.** Lahori and Sharib say that he had no children (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 166, folio 154; Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 116, folio 112). Ibn Battuta, as Rizvi reports him, speaks of "his grandson, Shaikh Hud" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 234, folio 214).
- **His welcome at Delhi.** Sharib: when he went to meet Sultan Ala al-Din, the sultan received him at the third gate (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 115, folio 111). Rizvi: "The Sultan himself went to welcome him and rode back with him to the city" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 231, folio 211).
- **Nizam al-Din's gift.** Sharib: he refused it and Nizam al-Din handed it to his brother (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 115, folio 111). Rizvi: he refused it and himself asked his brother to accept it (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 232, folio 212).
- **The massacre at Multan.** Lahori's editor, citing Isami: he went barefoot to the king to intercede (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 165, folio 153, حاشیہ 1). Rizvi, citing Isami: he retreated into meditation for seven days while the inhabitants were massacred, and then interceded; citing Barani: he approached the Sultan as soon as he learned of the intended massacre, and the Sultan relented. Rizvi calls Isami's story apocryphal (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 233, folio 213). Bhandari's history, if its Shaikh Rukn al-Din is this saint, has the massacre averted by his intercession after Malik Bahram's revolt (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 347).
- **The fall of the pavilion.** Rizvi: he left at the suggestion of the Sultan's son to perform the afternoon prayer, and the palace collapsed after he had gone; Rizvi names no agency (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 232, folio 212). Bhandari, in one version he reports, has a Shaikh Rukn al-Din Multani, who may be this saint, urge the sultan by signs to rise, and the pavilion fall as the shaikh rose; Bhandari himself calls another account the verified one (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 351).
- **His writings.** Rizvi: of the works containing his teachings, including his *Malfuzat*, "none of these however still exist" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 233, folio 213). Lahori's editor: a manuscript of his *malfuzat* is in a professor's library (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 165, folio 153, حاشیہ 1).
- **His *khalifas*.** Lahori's editor names five, among them Fakhr al-Din Iraqi, Sultan Hamid al-Din Hakim and Shaikh Sama al-Din Dehlvi (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 165, folio 153, حاشیہ 1). Sharib announces six and names five, among them Shaikh Usman Sayyah, Haji Shaikh Sadr al-Din Arif and Haji Chiragh Din, and elsewhere a Shaikh Qawam al-Din (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 116, folio 112; p. 135, folio 131). Both lists name Makhdum Jahaniyan.
- **His order.** The site's account, Rozehnal, Boivin and Lahori call him Suhrawardi (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 57, folio 47; Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 187, folio 174; Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 252, folio 240). Sharib never calls him Suhrawardi and names no order for him; the one order-bearing statement in his chapter is the inherited *khirqa* of Shihab al-Din Suhrawardi (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 114, folio 110).

## Legacy

As the heir of Bahauddin Zakariya and the most celebrated of the Multan Suhrawardis, Rukn-e-Alam carried the order to its zenith, and through his tomb — at once a place of pilgrimage and a wonder of architecture — the sanctity of the “City of Saints” finds its most visible expression.

The books carry his memory in their own registers. To Lahori and Sharib he is the *Qibla-e Hajat*, whose tomb at Multan is a place of pilgrimage (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 166, folio 154; Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 116, folio 112). To Rizvi he is the shaikh whose death marked the decline of the order in Multan (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 299, folio 277). In Sindh, Boivin records, a birth legend sometimes makes him the master in place of his grandfather (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 187, folio 174). Lahori's editor lists a book devoted to him, Nur Ahmad Khan Faridi's "تذکرہ حضرت شاہ رکن عالم ملتانی ؒ۔ ملتان ۱۹۶۱ء", published at Multan in 1961 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 304, folio 292).
