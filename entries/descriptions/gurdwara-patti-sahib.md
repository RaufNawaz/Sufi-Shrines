## Overview

Gurdwara Patti Sahib, also known as Gurdwara Maulvi Patti, lies in Nankana Sahib midway between the central shrine of Janam Asthan, which marks the birthplace of *Guru Nanak Dev Ji*, and Gurdwara Bal Lila, along the road that runs from the town centre towards the railway station. It commemorates the place remembered in Sikh tradition as the village school where the child Nanak was sent to learn, and where, according to the *sakhis*, he astonished his teacher with the depth of his understanding. The gurdwara takes its name from the *patti* — the wooden writing-tablet on which children of the Punjab once learned their letters — and it is counted among the well-kept shrines of the holy town.

Two of Haroon Khalid's books describe the gurdwara at first hand. In *Walking with Nanak* he calls it a small gurdwara that "is only a few metres away from Gurdwara Janamasthan. It was here that Guru Nanak got his early education", and here, he writes, Nanak wrote his first poem, "which is recorded in the Guru Granth Sahib" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 89, folio not stated). In *A White Trail* he places it "only a few hundred metres away" from the Janamasthan (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 192, folio not stated) and across the road from Gurdwara Balila (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 177, folio not stated), and says that "Gurdwara Patti Sahib is constructed where Guru Nanak learned Hindi and then Sanskrit from his teacher as a child" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 192, folio not stated), "where he received his earliest education" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated). Both books also record the Sikh families who have made their homes in its rooms, and *A White Trail* describes the Lohri fire lit in its courtyard in January 2011.

The other books consulted do not name the gurdwara. Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi's history of Sufism, Harish Dhillon's retelling of the Janamsakhis and Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian chronicle *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* speak of Guru Nanak's boyhood at Talwandi, the village later called Nankana Sahib, and of his schooling there, but none of them names a school building or a shrine. What they say is gathered below as the record of the Guru's early life and of the town, not as a record of this gurdwara.

## The Schooling of Guru Nanak

In the Punjab of the fifteenth century, a *patti* was the smooth wooden board on which a pupil, using a reed pen and home-made ink, would practise the letters of the alphabet under the eye of a teacher. Tradition relates that Nanak was taken as a young boy to the village schoolmaster to be taught in this way. The most celebrated account tells that, rather than merely copying his letters, the child composed and recited an acrostic upon the alphabet — a poem in which each verse begins with a successive letter — expounding in it deep truths concerning God and the soul, and so amazed his teacher that the master confessed the boy had nothing more to learn from him.

This composition is preserved in Sikh scripture: the *Patti Likhi*, set in *Rag Asa*, is recorded in the *Guru Granth Sahib* under Guru Nanak's name, and it is to this acrostic that the gurdwara's name ultimately refers. The *sakhis* add further episodes of the Guru's early education — his study of Punjabi and accounts, of Sanskrit with a Hindu *pandit*, and of Persian with a Muslim *maulvi*, named in some tellings Qutbuddin — through all of which the recurring theme is the same: that the learning of the schools was surpassed by the inward wisdom already present in the child. The alternative name Maulvi Patti preserves the memory of this Muslim teacher, and of a schooling that crossed the community lines of the age.

Khalid tells the story as it is told at the gurdwara. In *Walking with Nanak* he meets Jasdeep, a young Pathan Sikh who lives at Patti Sahib and was "educated … in Sikh precepts at this gurdwara" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 90, folio 72), and gives the tale that Jasdeep "must have heard": that Nanak composed his first poem "in the same building" and "impressed his teacher, Pandit Gopal" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 90, folio 72). Khalid quotes lines of the poem in the same passage (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 90, folio 72). In *A White Trail* the lessons given at the site are in Hindi and then Sanskrit (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 192, folio not stated).

The older accounts of the schooling name no school. Rizvi writes: "When seven years old, Nanak was taken by his father to receive instruction in Hinduism." Two years later, he continues, attempts were made to teach him Persian (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 408, folio 384); he names no school and no building. In Dhillon's retelling of the Janamsakhis the boy showed at school "a brilliance that had never before been seen in any child in Talwandi" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 18, folio 20). Bhandari, writing in Persian under Aurangzeb, says that in Nanak's boyhood the signs of a holy life showed themselves: "درخورد سالگی(۱) علامات کشف و کرامات و ایات خارق عادات از و بمنصه ظهور رسید", that in childhood signs of spiritual unveiling, of wonders and of marvels beyond the ordinary appeared in him; his editor's footnote gives the variant reading "ده سالگی", at the age of ten (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).

Khalid sets the schooling in the meeting of two faiths. "Nanak himself had been born into a Hindu household and was then educated in a Muslim milieu; however, he was neither a practising Muslim nor a Hindu" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 95, folio 77). Writing of the province rather than of Nanak's own lessons, Rizvi holds that "all literate Panjabis, both Hindus and Muslims, at the time of Guru Nanak were conversant with" the Persian poets 'Attar, Rumi and Sa'di (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 420, folio 396). Annemarie Schimmel, describing the Muslim poets of the countryside, writes that in the plains of Sind and the Punjab they "wrote tender little poems or instructive Golden Alphabets to instil love for him into the souls of the villagers", the beloved of those alphabets being the Prophet (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 222, folio 204). The alphabet poem was thus a form that Muslim and Sikh poets of the Punjab both knew, though Schimmel does not speak of Guru Nanak.

## The Boy at Talwandi

What the books say of Nanak's boyhood beyond the schoolroom is said of the Guru and of Talwandi, not of this site. Bhandari records that Baba Nanak was born at Talwandi Rai Bhoi and lived there in his grandfather's house: "درمکان تلونڈی رائے بهوئه[OCR?] سعادت ولادت یافته همانجا درخانه جد ابادری می بود" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). Dhillon gives the birth "On 14 April 1469" to Kalyan Chand Das Bedi, "or Mehta Kalu as he was affectionately called", accountant to the Bhatti family under Rai Bular, and has the village astrologer, Pandit Hardyal, foretell the child's greatness (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 17–18, folios 19–20; p. 18, folio 20). The back matter of *Walking with Nanak* gives "Born Talwandi (now known as Nankana Sahib in Pakistan) on October 20, 1469" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 314, folio 288). Khalid writes that "Nanak belonged to the Bedi caste" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 100, folio 82).

At thirteen, in Dhillon's telling, the boy refused the sacred thread, the *janeu*, in his father's courtyard, saying that "it is our actions and our actions alone" that give a man his position among men; Dhillon calls it "the beginning of a lifelong crusade against the performing of empty rites and rituals" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 19–21, folios 21–23). At sixteen he herded cattle (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 23, folio 27), and he "became well acquainted with the dervish Sayyad Hussain and gained an intimate knowledge of the Sufi line of thought" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 24, folio 28). Talwandi "lay in the path of numerous mendicants, sadhus and holy men", who camped "in a grove of trees on the outskirts of the village" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 24, folio 28).

Khalid retells the same years as a novel, and says plainly that this part of his book is his own reconstruction: "my depiction of Nanak in this book is not an accurate version of Nanak" but what he wants Nanak "to be like" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 229–230, folio not stated / folio 204). In it Mehta Kalu calls the family "Bedis ... the ancient orators of the Vedas" and himself "an accountant", and the boy herds buffaloes (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 63, folio 45; p. 64, folio 46). At dusk he sits under a *waan* tree hung with cloths "tied as supplication", "about two furlongs" from home, hearing the *azaan* and the temple bells together (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 59, folio 41; p. 60, folio 42). He refuses the *janeu* proposed by his father and "the local priest of Rai Bhoi di Talwindi", which "caused a furore", and "Respectable Hindus started to avoid the Bedi" family (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 61–62, folios 43–44). His mentor is "Sayyed Hassan, a Muslim Sufi, who lived near his house", who argues that "Islam teaches one to adopt the middle path" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 62, folio 44). He meets ascetics and Sufis on the outskirts of the town and spends time at the abandoned mound there (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 66, folio 48; p. 67, folio 49). "It is believed that in the years between the incident of Sacha Sauda and his migration to Sultanpur, Nanak started to frequent this place" — "That period of six to seven years" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 91, folio 73; p. 92, folio 74).

## The Gurdwara

The shrine that grew up on the site of the remembered schoolhouse stands today as a substantial and well-maintained building, notable among the Nankana Sahib gurdwaras for its good state of repair at a time when many Sikh shrines in Pakistan have suffered from the absence of a resident congregation. Set on the road between the two greatest shrines of the town, it forms a natural station on the circuit that pilgrims follow among the sites of the Guru's childhood, and its sanctum houses the *Guru Granth Sahib*, before which the visiting faithful make their devotions.

Khalid describes it as he found it on the night of Lohri in January 2011. "Unlike Gurdwara Janamathsan, there is no security at this particular shrine." An arch leads in to "a double-storey yellow structure with a white dome", raised on a platform, and a *Nishan Sahib*, the Sikh flagstaff, stands with candles, lamps and incense (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 192, folio not stated). Its rooms "have been taken over by local Sikhs with Kartar's family being one of them" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 192, folio not stated); Kartar is one of Khalid's informants among the Sikhs of the town. Of the colour, Khalid remarks elsewhere, writing of the province and not of this gurdwara, that "yellow was commonly used for buildings in Punjab during the older days" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 197, folio not stated).

None of the books consulted dates the present building or names its builder.

## A Refuge and a School

As with the other shrines of Nankana Sahib, the living community of worshippers departed for India at Partition in 1947, and Patti Sahib is now visited chiefly by pilgrim *jathas* who come on the great anniversaries, above all the *Gurpurab* of Guru Nanak's birth, when the town fills with visitors from India and the Sikh diaspora. Between these occasions it is maintained by the authorities charged with the care of Sikh heritage in Pakistan.

Khalid's books record a second community that came later. "During the early days of Sikh migration to Nankana Sahib after the War, of 1965 and 1971, a lot of Sikh families from" Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and the Tribal Areas, he writes, "found refuge in Gurdwara Patti Sahib" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 90, folio 72). There "a teacher by the name of Giani Pratap started teaching young Sikh" children "the Gurmukhi alphabet and the Guru Granth Sahib"; Giani Pratap was an Indian citizen, Khalid writes, "who visited Pakistan sometime during the sixties for a Pilgrimage to Nankana Sahib" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 90, folio 72). "After looking at the dilapidated condition of the shrines here he decided to stay and look after these holy buildings. That was the first time someone had taken the initiative to take care of the gurdwaras in Nankana Sahib after Partition" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 90, folio 72). The house of the boy's first lessons became, in this account, a schoolroom again, where the children of refugee families learnt the script of the Guru's hymns.

Jasdeep, the young Pathan Sikh whom Khalid met there, lived at the gurdwara and had been "educated … in Sikh precepts at this gurdwara" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 89, folio not stated; p. 90, folio 72). In *A White Trail* Kartar, whose family had rooms at Patti Sahib, told Khalid of the years before: "There was no Sikh left here after partition. Our Gurdwaras became barren and were in a bad state". Giani Pratap, he said, came with an Indian pilgrimage party "sometime in the sixties", stayed "to look after the Gurdwara", housed Kartar's family inside it for a time, and made Kartar's father "a sewaadar (worker) at the Gurdwara"; Kartar does not name the gurdwara, and Giani Pratap "died recently in Lahore" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 184, folio not stated).

Writing of the town's Sikhs as a whole, Khalid says that they "are not indigenous people from the city, but recent migrants from Khyber-Pakhtunkhwa (KPK) where there is a considerable Sikh population" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 235, folio not stated). Families came "following the alienation after the wars of 1965 and 1971 as well as the demolition of Babri Mosque in 1992" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 174–175, folio not stated), and "Most of the newcomers were incorporated within the Gurdwaras, in rooms which were generally reserved for pilgrims"; their arrival gave "a confidence boost to the Sikhs who were until now dominated by the Muslim majority" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 176, folio not stated). Some of the young men speak Urdu "with a Pasthu accent", and "not all Sikhs here can speak Urdu or Punjabi" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 174, folio not stated). Kartar called Punjabi "the language of Baba Guru Nanak", and remembered a professor at Nankana Sahib who "commented that Punjabi is a language of the unrefined" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 202, folio not stated).

Teaching in the Guru's script and scripture continues elsewhere in the town. Madan Singh, a former head of the Pakistan Sikh Gurdwara Prabandhak Committee and a businessman of Nankana Sahib, runs "Guru Nanak High School, the only school in the country where the Granth Sahib is taught to Sikh children" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 203, folio not stated). Khalid himself grew up, he writes, in a Punjab where Punjabi was fined at his school "even in the playground", and where "the educated elite of Punjab ends up disowning, in fact, ridiculing anything Punjabi", the poetry of Baba Farid and Guru Nanak among it (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 128, folio 110).

## Lohri at Patti Sahib

Khalid's *A White Trail* gives the fullest account of an occasion kept at the gurdwara. On the night of Lohri in January 2011 the second Lohri fire of the town was burnt here, "where Kartar also happens to live" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 192, folio not stated). Kartar broke a wooden window into the pile; oil was poured over it and lit; children added branches; and *kheer* was served in steel cups and bowls (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 192–193, folio not stated). Afterwards the older boys went round the homes of the community for more *kheer* (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 194, folio not stated). Muslims in the shops and on motorbikes watched the procession through the town "with burning curiosity" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 192, folio not stated).

Khalid sets the night in the history of the province. "Lohri like Vaisakhi or Basant, is an ancient festival of the Punjab that commemorates the change of season", kept "on the 13th of January, which is generally the last night of the month of Poh"; traditionally it means a village bonfire, dry fruit, folk songs, children collecting wood, and *kheer* cooled overnight (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 192–193, folio not stated). After the creation of Pakistan "this indigenous festival of the Punjab died down", and "the Sikh and the Hindu minorities of central Punjab also stopped this celebration" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 193, folio not stated). Kartar told him that Lohri and Guru Gobind Singh's birthday "have only begun in the last couple of years", made possible by the growth of the Sikh population through migration from the tribal areas: "Earlier, there was only a prayer at the Gurdwara." (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 193, folio not stated). "The celebration of Lohri at Nankana Sahib this year is an attempt to revive this cultural tradition of Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 193–194, folio not stated); "It is ironical though that this Punjabi tradition shunned by Punjab is now being revived by a group of Pathan men residing in Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 194, folio not stated).

## The Gurpurab

The great day of the town is the *Gurpurab* of Guru Nanak's birth. The books consulted do not describe Patti Sahib on that day, but Khalid writes that because Nanak spent his early years in the city there are "several Gurdwaras in the city", all of which the pilgrims visit (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated), and that travel-company posters promise pilgrims "all" the gurdwaras of Pakistan (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 223, folio not stated). "According to official estimates, ten to fifteen thousand people come to Nankana Sahib for this festival every year, which is celebrated during the last days of November"; in 2011, "According to media reports, only three thousand people are attending"; "This year it began on the 8th of November and will conclude today, on the 10th of November" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated). Pilgrims from India cross at Wagah, where representatives of the Evacuee Trust Property Board and the Pakistan Sikh Gurdwara Prabandhak Committee receive them and a *langar* is laid on, and they stay three days at Nankana (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 222–223, folio not stated). Three days of *Akhand Path* end in the *bhog*, followed by the *Nagar Kirtan*; richer pilgrims from Britain, America and Canada come by chartered plane and hire buses (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 223, folio not stated). Devotees walk barefoot (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 221–222, folio not stated). During the festival "The entire city looks like a mini-India during these few days" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 226, folio not stated).

## Guru Nanak in the Wider Record

What the books say of the Guru's later life is said of him, not of this site; it is gathered here because the shrine exists for his sake. From Talwandi Nanak went to Sultanpur, "around 60 km from Talwandi", as keeper of Nawab Daulat Khan's stores (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 44, folio 49); for that journey, Khalid writes, "he travelled from Nankana" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 54, folio 36). Dhillon dates his marriage to 24 September 1487, to Sulakhini, "daughter of Mul Chand and Chando Rani of the town of Batala", and gives his sons as "Sri Chand (8 September 1494) and Lakhmi Das (12 February 1497)" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 44, folio 49). At Sultanpur, in Dhillon's telling, he vanished in the stream for a week and came out saying, "There is only one God. There is no Hindu, no Mussalman." (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 45–46, folios 50–51).

Dhillon sets out two schemes of the journeys, the *udasis*: "According to the Puratan Janamsakhi… he undertook five journeys", the last in 1523–1524, while "More modern accounts" give four, spanning twenty-three years (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 54–55, folios 60–61; p. 55, folio 61); Dhillon himself holds to four (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 56, folio 62). Khalid gives the travels as "about twenty-four years", mostly on foot, east to Bengal, south to Sri Lanka, north to Kashmir, Nepal and Tibet, and west to Mecca (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 54, folio 36). "Whenever he would visit an area he would visit its religious shrines, Muslim, Hindu and of any other denomination" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 37, folio 19).

Bhandari calls Baba Nanak "قافله سالار مسالک حقیقت و مشعله دار طریق طریقت", and records that after travelling most of the world he came to the town of Batala, married, and settled in a village of Batala on the Ravi (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). People came from every land to become his disciples, "عالم عالم خلایق از اطراف ممالک آمده مرید شدند" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). He died, Bhandari writes, "درعهد سلطنت سلیم شاه افغان درعمر مابین هفتاد و هشتاد سالگی", in the reign of Salim Shah Afghan, between the ages of seventy and eighty (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520); Dhillon gives the date as 22 September 1539 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 259, folio 287). He had a son, "لکهمیداس نام خلف رشید", but, Bhandari writes, because spiritual fortune was not his lot, the Guru passed him over and at the hour of death gave a Khatri named Lehna the title of Guru Angad (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). "On his deathbed, he appointed not his son but his most loyal student as his spiritual successor", Khalid writes (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 16, folio not stated). In Khalid's reconstruction it is Lehna who "decided to collect the poetry of Nanak in written form", at a time when Punjabi was "an oral language" with "no script" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 299, folio 273).

Dhillon's Nanak was "no unworldly mystic" but "a householder in every sense of the word" who "brought a message of peace and harmony to believers of the mutually hostile religions of Islam and Hinduism" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 12, folio not stated), and taught that "all men and women were equal, irrespective of caste, creed, wealth, social standing and gender" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 12–13, folio not stated / 13). His followers, in Bhandari's account, count service of the traveller, "درخدمت صادر و وارد", as "عبادت عظمی", the highest worship (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 519). "It is believed that the tradition of Langar began with Guru Nanak", Khalid writes (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 188, folio not stated).

Others trace his debt to the Sufis. "Guru Nanak was a spiritual follower of Baba Fareed. He traveled to Pak Pattan where he met the spiritual descendants of Baba Fareed and collected the poetry of the saint" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 188, folio not stated). Shemeem Burney Abbas writes that Baba Farid's "texts are revered among the Sikhs because he influenced the founder of their own religion, Baba Guru Nanak" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 87, folio not stated). Robert Rozehnal reports that a twentieth-century Chishti Sabiri shaykh, in certain lectures, describes "non-Muslim spiritual luminaries—Krishna, Rama, Sita, Lachman, and Guru Nanak—as believers in the one true God and religious leaders with 'Divine connection' (nisbat)" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 63, folio 53). Omar Kasmani, citing Michel Boivin, notes that the trope of the flower in milk is told also of "Guru Nanak, the spiritual leader and founder of Sikhism" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 195, folio 178, ch. 5 n. 17).

His devotion reaches beyond the Sikhs. Khalid writes of Nanak Panthis, "Hindus who believe in the teachings of Guru Nanak, but not in the Khalsa", in whose temples in Sindh and southern Punjab "one will find portraits of Guru Nanak, along with Krishna, Kali Mata and other Hindu deities" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 197, folio not stated). For Khalid's friend Nadeem, a Muslim painter, "Guru Nanak is like a Muslim Sufi Saint" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 219, folio not stated). Boivin notes "a growing similarity between the iconography of Guru Nanak and Jhulelal" in Sindh (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 54, folio 41). Jürgen Schaflechner records "the engraved footsteps of Guru Nanak" in the Hinglaj valley (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 256, folio not stated), and writes that "Many Lohanas in Sindh follow the teachings of Nanak" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 80, folio not stated).

## Legends and Miracles

The legends below are told of the Guru, not of this shrine. "According to legend, Nanak once travelled to Mecca where he performed Hajj"; he slept with his feet towards the Kaaba, was rebuked, turned his feet, "and as he did so, the Kaaba miraculously turned towards his feet"; Khalid comments, "What is important in this story is Nanak's criticism of the concept of a 'sacred space'" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 82, folio 64). In Dhillon's telling the *qazi* conceded that "God lives everywhere" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 198–201, folios 218–221).

Two stories of the birth point away from the Sikh tradition. A booklet of the Auqaf department at the shrine of Naulakha Hazari states that "Guru Nanak was born after Baba Naulakha Hazari prayed for his mother" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 31, folio 13), and Iqbal Qaiser, Khalid's guide, told him: "The Sikhs don't accept it but the Muslim tradition is that Guru Nanak was born by the blessing of Baba Naulakh Hazari." (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).

A footnoted tale in *Walking with Nanak*, introduced with "It is believed", tells of a trader who told Nanak that his mules carried "Just some mud": his sugar and *gur* turned to mud, and when he repented they became sugar and *gur* once again (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 71, folio 53). Of the legends in which Nanak humbles a Muslim Sufi, at Sialkot and at Pasrur, Khalid writes that they are "pointless stories" and that "His genius lies in the poetic use of words and not in miracles" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 199, folio 173).

Some writers set the miracles against the man. Dhillon writes of "Guru Nanak's clear and emphatic disavowal of the miraculous" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 11, folio 11), and in his retelling the Guru tells Karori, who calls his cure a miracle, that "there is no such thing as a miracle" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 190, folio 209). Khalid writes that Nanak "abhorred the concept of associating miracles with religious personalities", yet "his biography is nothing but a story of his miracles" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 16, folio not stated). The story told at Patti Sahib is of another kind: its marvel is a poem.

## In Verse

The gurdwara remembers a poem: the *Patti Likhi* of the site's account, the first poem that Khalid says Nanak wrote here, "which is recorded in the Guru Granth Sahib" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 89, folio not stated). Nanak's *Bani*, Dhillon writes, "was compiled as part of the Adi Granth by Guru Arjan Dev, the fifth Guru, in 1604" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 9, folio 9), and Nanak's "sermon was his Bani" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 228–229, folios 252–253). The scripture carries also, in Khalid's words, the poetry of "other spiritual teachers like Bhagat Kabir, Baba Farid and Bhai Mardana" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 82, folio 64), and the poems of later Gurus are recorded "not in their own names but in that of Guru Nanak" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 98, folio 80). Bhandari says that the Guru composed "اشعار هندی" on the truth of God, setting out divine unity "بعبارات واضحه و استعارات لایحه" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520), and that a singer named Mardana, "مردانه نام مطرب", sang his verses (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520); the worship of his followers, he writes, is the reading of their master's verses: "خلاصه عبادت اینطایفه مطالعه اشعار مرشد خویش است که بسرود و نغمه میگویند" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 519).

Khalid quotes the Guru's verse throughout *Walking with Nanak* in published English translations. None of the lines below is tied to Patti Sahib; they are given here as the poetry of the Guru whose schooling the gurdwara remembers. The poem on the sacred thread, which in Khalid's reconstruction the boy refused at Talwandi, begins:

*From the cotton of compassion, / Spin the thread of contentment* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 61–62, folios 43–44)

On the oneness of God:

*My Master is One / He is One, brother, the only One* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 60, folio 42)

*God is One, / The Path is One* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 144, folio 126)

The *aarti*, which Iqbal Qaiser reads as praising a God who resides "in nature", begins in one translation *The sky the salver, the sun and moon the lamps …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 55, folio 37), and in another:

*In the salver of the firmament, / The sun and the moon shine as lamps* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 243–244, folios 217–218)

On washing:

*As hands or feet besmirched with slime, / Water washes white* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 192, folio 166)

Other openings Khalid quotes are *O my merchant friend* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 56–58, folio 40), *As a beggar goes a-begging …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 158, folio 140) and *His goodness cannot be priced or traded …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 280, folio 254).

The Guru's language and forms are part of his legacy. "Nanak incorporated several words which were not part of the Punjabi of the time … which is why Nanak's contribution as a poet to Punjabi is immense" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 278–279, folios 252–253). Abbas writes of the *dohā*, "an old popular Prakrit and Hindi meter in which a couplet is put together where the two verses rhyme": "Poets such as Kabir and Nanak used it to write their mystical poetry" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 141, folio not stated). Her glossary defines the *banī* by him: "Devotional verses such as those of Guru Nanak; verses of supplication, entreaty, humility, and reverence" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 179, folio not stated). The verses are sung outside the gurdwaras too: Abbas quotes Abida Parvin: "I sing Shah Hussain, Bulle Shah, Mian Muhammad Bakhsh, Hazrat Khawaja Ghulam Farid, Sacchal Sarmast, Baba Farid, and Guru Nanak" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 54, folio not stated), and in Sindh, Boivin records, "Sain Vasan Shah became famous for reciting the banis of Shri Guru Nanak and the kalams of the Sufis" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 68, folio 55).

## Setting: Nankana Sahib

Rizvi describes the town of Nanak's birth as "the village of Talwandi later known as Nankana Sahib, about forty miles south-west of Lahore, now part of Pakistan" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 407, folio 383); his history names no gurdwara there. Talwandi, in Dhillon's retelling, "lay between the rivers Ravi and Chenab, directly in the path of the armies that had set their eyes on Delhi"; "Thirteen times was the village of Talwandi destroyed and thirteen times - like the fabled phoenix - it rose again from its ashes", and its last rebuilding was overseen by Rai Bhoe, a Bhatti Rajput who had become a Muslim (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 15–16, folios 17–18). Writing of the province in Nanak's century, Rizvi says that "In the fifteenth century, the Panjab enjoyed a peaceful period due to Afghan rule" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 408, folio 384).

"Originally called Rai-Bhoi-Di-Talwandi, this city was rechristened Nankana Sahib in honour of Guru Nanak" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated). *A White Trail* counts "seven Gurdwaras in Nankana Sahib, most of which are associated with incidents from the life of Baba Guru Nanak" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 192, folio not stated); *Walking with Nanak* says "There are a total of six gurdwaras associated with Guru Nanak at Nankana Sahib" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 90, folio 72). "All year round most of them remain locked, only to be opened at the time of the festival. There are security guards employed at the entrance to all of them." (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 227, folio not stated). It is "a city considered to be one of the holiest cities in the world for the Sikhs, as the founder of their religion, Nanak, was born here" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 183, folio not stated).

The modern town, as Khalid saw it, has "one main road that runs through the entire city"; "Most of the buildings are from the late British or the early Pakistan era" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 186, folio not stated). From a roof he saw the minarets of mosques, the "small white domes of the Gurdwaras", the remains of a few Hindu temples, and the railway station (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 225, folio not stated). "In 2005, Nankana Sahib was separated from Sheikhupura and made into an independent district, with this city becoming the district headquarters" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 226, folio not stated). "Guru Nanak is celebrated all over Nankana Sahib. There is a Guru Nanak High School, a Guru Nanak grocery store and a Guru Nanak cloth shop, all run by Sikhs" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 172, folio not stated). In *Walking with Nanak* "the Sikh community in Nankana Sahib, which amounts to about two hundred-odd households", has Jatha Singh as its leader of several years (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 87, folio 69); Kartar told Khalid in *A White Trail* that "Just a few years ago, there were only about fifty Sikh houses, but now there are almost two hundred to two hundred and fifty Sikh homes" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 193, folio not stated). Iqbal Qaiser explained to Khalid that "The majority of the Muslims in Nankana Sahib are" those who migrated from the other side of the border after Partition, their attitudes "shaped by those experiences" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 76, folio 58).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **Where the gurdwara stands.** The site's account places it midway between Janam Asthan and Gurdwara Bal Lila, on the road from the town centre to the railway station. *Walking with Nanak* puts it "only a few metres away from Gurdwara Janamasthan" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 89, folio not stated); *A White Trail* puts it "only a few hundred metres away" from the Janamasthan (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 192, folio not stated) and across the road from Gurdwara Balila (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 177, folio not stated).
- **The teacher.** The site's account speaks of the village schoolmaster, a Hindu *pandit* and a Muslim *maulvi*, named in some tellings Qutbuddin, the *maulvi* giving the gurdwara its other name. *Walking with Nanak* names the teacher whom the boy impressed as "Pandit Gopal" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 90, folio 72). None of the books names a Muslim teacher or the name Maulvi Patti.
- **What was taught.** The site's account names Punjabi and accounts, Sanskrit, and Persian. *A White Trail* says that here Nanak "learned Hindi and then Sanskrit from his teacher" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 192, folio not stated). Rizvi has him taken at seven "to receive instruction in Hinduism", and taught Persian two years later, and names no school (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 408, folio 384).
- **The Muslim mentor of his youth.** Dhillon names "the dervish Sayyad Hussain" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 24, folio 28); Khalid's reconstruction names "Sayyed Hassan, a Muslim Sufi, who lived near his house" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 62, folio 44).
- **Who lives at the gurdwara.** The site's account says the community of worshippers left at Partition and that the shrine is now visited chiefly by pilgrim *jathas* and kept by the authorities. *Walking with Nanak* says that Sikh families from Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and the Tribal Areas "found refuge in Gurdwara Patti Sahib" after the wars of 1965 and 1971, and that Jasdeep lives there (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 90, folio 72); *A White Trail* says its rooms "have been taken over by local Sikhs with Kartar's family being one of them" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 192, folio not stated).
- **Its care and upkeep.** The site's account calls it well kept and in good repair, maintained by the authorities in charge of Sikh heritage. Khalid writes that Giani Pratap, an Indian pilgrim of the 1960s, stayed on after seeing "the dilapidated condition of the shrines here", the first after Partition to look after the gurdwaras of the town (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 90, folio 72).
- **Security.** *A White Trail* says that "there is no security at this particular shrine" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 192, folio not stated), and later, of the town's gurdwaras, that "There are security guards employed at the entrance to all of them." (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 227, folio not stated).
- **When and why the Sikh families came.** *Walking with Nanak* dates the migration to after the wars of 1965 and 1971 (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 90, folio 72); *A White Trail* adds the demolition of the Babri Mosque in 1992 (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 174–175, folio not stated).
- **How many gurdwaras Nankana has.** *A White Trail* counts seven (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 192, folio not stated); *Walking with Nanak* counts six associated with Guru Nanak (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 90, folio 72).
- **The size of the Sikh community.** *Walking with Nanak* gives "about two hundred-odd households" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 87, folio 69); in *A White Trail* Kartar gives about fifty houses a few years before and "almost two hundred to two hundred and fifty Sikh homes" now (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 193, folio not stated).
- **The date of birth.** Dhillon gives "On 14 April 1469" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 17–18, folios 19–20); the back matter of *Walking with Nanak* gives "October 20, 1469" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 314, folio 288).
- **The age at which the signs appeared.** Bhandari's text says the signs of a holy life showed in Nanak's childhood, "درخورد سالگی"; his editor's footnote gives the variant "ده سالگی", at ten (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).
- **The journeys.** Dhillon gives five *udasis* after the Puratan Janamsakhi and four after "More modern accounts", spanning twenty-three years, and holds to four himself (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 55, folio 61; p. 56, folio 62); Khalid gives "about twenty-four years" of travel (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 54, folio 36).
- **The date of death.** Dhillon gives 22 September 1539 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 259, folio 287); Bhandari gives the reign of Salim Shah Afghan, at an age between seventy and eighty (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).
- **The son passed over.** Dhillon names the younger son Lakhmi Das (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 44, folio 49); Bhandari names the son passed over "لکهمیداس" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).
- **The spelling of the names.** The site's account writes Janam Asthan and Bal Lila; Khalid writes Janamasthan (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 89, folio not stated), Janamathsan (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 192, folio not stated) and Balila (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 177, folio not stated).

## Legacy

Patti Sahib enshrines one of the tenderest and most characteristic of the Guru Nanak narratives: the schoolboy who taught his teacher, whose first recorded verses were a meditation on the letters of the alphabet turned towards the divine. In the geography of Nankana Sahib it takes its place among the shrines that mark, step by step, the unfolding of the Guru's early life, and it carries a particular meaning for a faith that has always prized both learning and the transcendence of mere learning. That the school is remembered as belonging to a Muslim *maulvi* lends the site an added resonance in the plural religious world of the medieval Punjab from which Sikhism arose.

The books add a modern chapter to that story. The building that remembers the Guru's first lessons sheltered Pathan Sikh families who came to Nankana Sahib after the wars of 1965 and 1971, and there Giani Pratap taught their children the Gurmukhi alphabet and the Guru Granth Sahib (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 90, folio 72). In January 2011 its courtyard held one of the town's Lohri fires, part of a revival that Khalid describes as "an attempt to revive this cultural tradition of Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 193–194, folio not stated). Khalid, who found in Nanak a poet "my own, being a Punjabi" yet "an alien, since he was a non-Muslim" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 15, folio not stated), asks whether "Nanak's legacy in its true essence is also slowly disappearing" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 230, folio 204).
