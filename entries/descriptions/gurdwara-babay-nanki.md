## Overview

Gurdwara Babay Nanki commemorates *Bebe Nanaki* (also Bibi Nanaki), the elder sister of Guru Nanak and, in Sikh tradition, the very first of his followers — the first Sikh. The shrine associated with her lies at Dera Chahal, a village in the Lahore district some twenty kilometres south-east of the city and close to the present India–Pakistan border; it marks the ancestral village of Guru Nanak's maternal family, where Nanaki is traditionally held to have been born. (The location recorded in this dataset as "Lahore" refers to this village in Lahore district rather than the walled city itself.)

Of the books consulted, one describes the gurdwara itself. Haroon Khalid, in *Walking with Nanak*, visits it in the company of Iqbal Qaiser and calls it both "Gurdwara Janamasthan Bebe Nanaki" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 35, folio 17) and Gurdwara Bebe Nanaki, standing in the village of Dera Chahal, "located on the Bedian road, now flanked between DHA Phase Five, Six and Seven" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 34, folio 16). He spells her name both Bebe Nanaki and 'Bebe Nanki'. The same book once speaks of a 'Gurdwara Dera Chahal' in a sentence too damaged to read with confidence; it appears to mean this gurdwara, but the link is not certain (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 43). Harish Dhillon's *Janamsakhis* gives a fuller picture of Nanaki herself but sets her at Sultanpur throughout and never mentions Dera Chahal or its gurdwara; what it says is said of the woman, not of this site.

## Bebe Nanaki, the First Sikh

Bebe Nanaki was born, by tradition, around 1464 and was older than her brother by some five years. From his earliest childhood she is said to have perceived in Nanak a spiritual greatness that others overlooked, and she became his steadfast supporter and protector. Sikh tradition holds her to be the first to recognise him as a teacher of divine truth — and thus the first *Gursikh* — and credits her with encouraging his devotional use of music, the practice of *kirtan* that remains central to Sikh worship.

She was married to *Jai Ram*, an official (a Khatri of the Palta or Uppal clan) in the service of *Daulat Khan Lodi*, the governor at Sultanpur. It was through this connection that the young Nanak came to Sultanpur and took employment at Daulat Khan's *modikhana*, the government store — a period during which, at the river Bein, he is said to have received the revelation that inaugurated his mission. Nanaki's household at Sultanpur thus became one of the cradles of the nascent faith. She is believed to have died in 1518, at Sultanpur, in what is now Indian Punjab.

## Her Birth at Dera Chahal

Khalid gives the tradition that ties her to this spot. The gurdwara, he writes, is named after Guru Nanak's elder sister: "According to tradition, she was born in a house that was situated where the gurdwara now stands" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 34, folio 16). Mata Tripta, their mother, "belonged to this historical village of Dera Chahal", and he records a tradition about her brother's name: "According to tradition, Nanak was named after his sister Nanaki" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 34, folio 16).

Several of Khalid's scenes of Nanaki's life belong to his own reconstruction of Nanak's story rather than to his travels. In that reconstruction, Tripta, five months pregnant, goes to "her maternal home, a village called Dera Chahal at a little distance from Lahore"; "about six years after their wedding" a daughter is born there, and her grandmother suggests the name Nanaki "since the child was born in her maternal house, Nanaka in Punjabi" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 29, folio 11).

## Sister and Protector

Both books show Nanaki standing between the young Nanak and their father's displeasure. In Dhillon's retelling she restrains her father when he berates Nanak (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 38, folio 42). In Khalid's reconstruction she brings her father "a glass of hot milk mixed with almonds" and defends her brother: "He is a very sensible person" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 63, folio 45). On the next page she goes on, in a passage whose opening words are damaged in print, to say of their Nanak: "He is sensitive and observant" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 64, folio 46).

In Khalid's account she then "got married and moved to Sultanpur" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 66, folio 48). It was she who arranged her brother's marriage, having "already identified a girl", Sulakhni (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 67, folio 49). Her husband, Jai Ram, was well established "in the court of Nawab Daulat Khan", the governor of Sultanpur (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 67, folio 49; p. 68, folio 50), and Nanak went to live with them (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 68, folio 50). Later in the book Khalid recalls that Nanak "had been sent to Sultanpur by his father on the request of" Bebe Nanaki and her husband (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 106, folio 88).

Dhillon tells the same turn of events. Nanki, Nanak's sister, and "her husband Jai Ram", "a revenue official in the employment of Nawab Daulat Khan Lodhi, the governor of Sultanpur", arrange for Nanak to come to Sultanpur (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 43, folio 48). Dhillon's Lahore chapters add that Daulat Khan Lodhi, by the time of Nanak's second journey, "had risen in rank to become the governor of Lahore and the adjoining areas" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 124, folio 138); that is said of Jai Ram's patron, not of Nanaki or of Dera Chahal.

## The First Believer

Dhillon gives Nanaki a decisive place in the story of Nanak's call. When Nanak disappears in the river, she is the "one other person" besides Daulat Khan who believes he is alive: "'My brother will return safe'" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 46, folio 51). Of the disciples who gathered round him at Sultanpur, Dhillon writes, "the most ardent of these disciples were his sister Nanki and her husband Jai Ram" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 53, folio 59).

Dhillon tells of her joy at her brother's returns from his journeys, in one passage joined with Jai Ram's (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 124, folio 138; p. 172, folio 188; p. 193, folio "2 13"). He also writes that "Nanki, though saddened by her brother's decision, did not attempt" to sway him (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 174, folio 191); on another occasion, at her plea, he put off his departure (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 194, folio 214).

Khalid's book follows Nanak on the road to her. In it Nanak "decided to visit his sister, Nanaki, at Sultanpur", stopping on the way at Dera Chahal, at 'Jaman' and at a third place whose printed name is damaged and appears to read Ghavindi (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 54, folio 36). In Iqbal Qaiser's telling of the same journey, Nanak was going "to visit his sister, brother-in-law and his friend Nawab Daulat Khan" Lodhi (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 55, folio 37).

## Sulakhni and Sri Chand

Khalid's reconstruction also gives Nanaki a place in her brother's household after his marriage. "Bebe Nanki was particularly sympathetic towards Sulakhni" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 294, folio not stated). She "decided to look after her eldest son", that is, Sulakhni's son Sri Chand; "Bebe Nanki did not have a child of her own, which made the decision to temporarily adopt" him easier, and Sri Chand followed "the spiritual pursuits of his father, encouraged by Bebe Nanki" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 295, folio 269). The printed text gives the boy's name in a damaged form; the reading Sri Chand is the evident one.

## Her Death

Dhillon records her death in plain terms: "Nanki came down with a mysterious ailment and breathed her last in 1518", at an age that the printed text gives in a damaged form, apparently sixty-four; Jai Ram, "not able to sustain the loss, also passed away" three days later (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 194, folio 214). The year 1518 is clear and agrees with the date the site's account gives. Dhillon names her again among those who died before Nanak (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 257, folio 285).

## Dera Chahal and the Gurdwara

Dera Chahal was the village of Guru Nanak's *nanake* — his mother's family — and the Guru is remembered to have visited it on more than one occasion. The gurdwara here honours it as the *janamasthan*, the birthplace, of Bebe Nanaki, and stands within the settlement as a modest but cherished shrine. Like the other Nanak-related sites of the Lahore region, it was largely abandoned after 1947 and passed into the care of the Evacuee Trust Property Board, though it has continued to receive Sikh visitors, especially on pilgrimages that take in the great shrines of Nankana Sahib and Kartarpur.

Khalid gives the gurdwara's origin in a sentence: "Since this was Nanak's maternal home he often came here. Later, his followers built a gurdwara here to mark the spot" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 34, folio 16). He names no founder and no date of building. Its later patron, in his account, was Maharaja Ranjit Singh: "When Maharaja Ranjit Singh became the sovereign of Punjab at the end of the eighteenth century he spent large amounts of money on the renovation and expansion of many gurdwaras", this one among them, and he "attached vast tracts of land to these gurdwaras, so that the revenues from the lands could facilitate their functioning" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 34, folio 16).

Asked whether the gurdwara stands "on the exact spot where Nanak's maternal family home used to be", Iqbal Qaiser answered "Yes"; he described the building as it once was: the gurdwara "was one big hall with a small" balcony "opening towards the centre of the hall from the first floor" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 38, folio 20). Of one part of the grounds Qaiser said that the area "used to be the langar hall"; "Now it is a lawn with a few rooms around it" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 35, folio 17).

## The Gurdwara Today

Khalid came on a morning in "the month of June", leaving at six in the morning and spending "about two hours here" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 40, folio 22). "The white dome of the gurdwara was visible from afar"; around the old village stand the new houses of former farmers who had sold their land to developers (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 33, folio 15). "A saffron flag rose from behind the wall testifying that there were members of the Sikh community living here." Behind it lay "a pool of water where buffaloes wallowed. It was once the sacred pool of this shrine. Next to it was a mosque which was being expanded" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 34, folio 16).

A young priest, Daleep Singh, led the visitors in (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 35, folio 17). Inside, he bowed before the Guru Granth Sahib, "placed in a wooden Thara Sahib"; Khalid and Qaiser did not bow (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 37, folio 19). As they talked, a sermon from the mosque's loudspeaker drowned the conversation (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 36, folio 18). Ghulab Singh, "the first Sikh police officer in the revamped traffic police of Lahore", stays at the gurdwara when he is in Lahore, and his official bike stood in the courtyard (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 35, folio 17).

## Renovation

Iqbal Qaiser told Khalid how the building came to be restored: "There was a lot of pressure on the Government of Pakistan from Sikhs living in Canada, England and America to renovate this gurdwara after my book came out." He went on: "It was in a horrible state when Meraj Khalid ordered its renovation. Even then, only its external structure was spruced up. The gurdwara remained shut to the Sikh community. Only recently has the government allowed pilgrims to visit this gurdwara when they come to Pakistan for festivals. Now on those occasions, prayers are offered here" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 33, folio 15).

Malik Meraj Khalid was a son of the village, "born in 1916 to a poor agricultural family of this village". A politician of the PPP, he was twice "National Speaker", once Chief Minister of Punjab and, "between November 1996 and February 1997", interim Prime Minister. "One of his first orders as the Prime Minister was the approval of funds to renovate this historical gurdwara at his village", Khalid writes, "though he was not Sikh" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 33–34, folios 15–16).

## The Land Dispute

Agricultural land associated with the gurdwara is now the subject of a legal fight. Ghulab Singh filed a case against the Evacuee Trust Property Board in the Lahore High Court "for the alleged sale of agricultural land associated with Gurdwara Janamasthan Bebe Nanaki"; a few days before Khalid's visit, the Supreme Court of Pakistan "took cognisance of the case" and asked the construction companies "to pay Rupees 986 million as surety while the hearings proceeded" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 35, folio 17). "Behind the once-sacred pool now occupied by buffaloes is the agricultural land in dispute", Khalid writes, and from the window a new apartment block was in view (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 36, folio 18).

Daleep Singh alleged that documents proved corruption at the Board, and that "Asif Hashmi (former head of the ETPB) has earned billions in his tenure of five years"; Khalid adds: "In April 2016 Asif Hashmi was arrested in Dubai" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 35, folio 17). These are the priest's allegations as Khalid reports them.

## Bhai Bannu and the Granth

Iqbal Qaiser gave Khalid one further tradition of the gurdwara. Bhai Bannu, sent by Guru Arjan to Lahore to bind the Adi Granth, "came and stayed at this gurdwara" and there prepared another copy "without the Guru's permission", adding verses that the Guru rejected; "one of the movements also started from this particular gurdwara", Qaiser said (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 39, folio 21). On the same page Khalid notes, of the city rather than the village, that "The Kashmiri binders who used to reside in Lahore were known for their meticulous binding" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 39, folio 21).

## Legends

One story of Nanak gives his sister the moment of discovery. In the Sachkand storyteller's version of the tale of mud turned into sugar, as Khalid records it, "when his sister opened the bags on the mules she found that the mud had actually been changed into sugar and gur" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 75, folio 57). Nothing in the passage ties the story to Dera Chahal.

## Devotional Significance

For Sikhs, Bebe Nanaki embodies the ideals of insight, devotion and selfless love, and she holds a place of special affection as the woman who first understood the Guru's message. Her shrine at Dera Chahal, though far less frequented than the major gurdwaras, carries a particular resonance for pilgrims who wish to honour the family that nurtured Guru Nanak and the sister whose faith preceded even his ministry.

The sources consulted record no annual festival of her own at the gurdwara. Qaiser's account is that pilgrims who come to Pakistan for festivals are now allowed to visit, and that prayers are offered here on those occasions (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 33, folio 15).

## Setting

What the books say of the country around Dera Chahal speaks of Lahore and its edges, not of the gurdwara. Khalid notes that "The Bedian road is the old route to Amritsar", and that Bedian itself was founded "near the city of Lahore" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 38, folio 20; p. 44, folio 26). The apartment block he saw from the gurdwara's window was among those "built recently to cater to the growing demands of urban Lahore" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 36, folio 18).

In another book Khalid describes the same suburban advance in general terms. The Defence Housing Authority is "the neatest and most expensive residential area of Lahore"; "Recently constructed, the DHA sprawls across a vast tract of land, eating into the small historic villages in the area", villages that, "Congested and inflicted with poverty", have been "trapped within a wall" after selling their land to the housing authority (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated). He does not name Dera Chahal there.

Of the Sikh presence in the city, Khalid writes in *A White Trail* that "it is now rare that one runs into a Sikh in this city, a city which was once ruled by a Sikh and the seat of their political strength" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 196, folio not stated); and of the province after 1947 he speaks of "the hundreds of temples and Gurdwaras all over Pakistani Punjab" taken over by refugees and turned into households (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 58, folio not stated). In *Walking with Nanak* he records Iqbal Qaiser's view of Sikh architecture in Lahore: "like the Sikh religion, which picks up from both Hinduism and Islam, Sikh architecture draws inspiration from both Hindu and Muslim architecture", the domes of Muslim building having been made "smaller" by Sikh builders (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 309–310, folio not stated / folio 284).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **Visitors after 1947.** The site's account says the gurdwara was largely abandoned after 1947 but "has continued to receive Sikh visitors, especially on pilgrimages that take in the great shrines of Nankana Sahib and Kartarpur". Iqbal Qaiser, as Khalid reports him, says that even after its renovation "The gurdwara remained shut to the Sikh community" and that "Only recently has the government allowed pilgrims to visit this gurdwara when they come to Pakistan for festivals" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 33, folio 15). Khalid himself saw a saffron flag "testifying that there were members of the Sikh community living here" and was received by a resident priest (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 34, folio 16; p. 35, folio 17).
- **Her age.** The site's account says that she was born around 1464 and was some five years older than her brother, and that she died in 1518. Dhillon gives the same year of death and her age at death as sixty-four, though the printed figure is damaged (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 194, folio 214).
- **Her place among the first followers.** The site's account calls her the very first of Nanak's followers, the first to recognise him as a teacher of divine truth. Dhillon names her and her husband together: "the most ardent of these disciples were his sister Nanki and her husband Jai Ram" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 53, folio 59); and when Nanak vanishes in the river, he has Daulat Khan share her faith that he lives (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 46, folio 51).
- **Jai Ram's post.** The site's account calls Jai Ram an official in the service of Daulat Khan Lodi, the governor at Sultanpur. Dhillon makes him "a revenue official" of Daulat Khan Lodhi (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 43, folio 48); Khalid says he was well established "in the court of Nawab Daulat Khan" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 67, folio 49).
- **The names.** The site's account calls her Bebe Nanaki or Bibi Nanaki, and the gurdwara goes by the name Gurdwara Babay Nanki. Khalid writes both Bebe Nanaki and 'Bebe Nanki', and calls the gurdwara "Gurdwara Janamasthan Bebe Nanaki" and Gurdwara Bebe Nanaki (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 34, folio 16; p. 35, folio 17), and perhaps also 'Gurdwara Dera Chahal' (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 43). Dhillon calls her Nanki (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 43, folio 48).

## Legacy

The veneration of Bebe Nanaki affirms the honoured place of women in the Sikh tradition from its very origin, and the gurdwara at Dera Chahal — one of two principal sites associated with her, the other being at Sultanpur Lodhi in India — preserves that memory on Pakistani soil. Its continued existence within Lahore district adds to the region's remarkable concentration of sites tied to the life of Guru Nanak and his circle.

The books show that memory under pressure and in renewal at once. Khalid found the sacred pool given over to buffaloes and the gurdwara's former lands in dispute before the courts, but also a building restored on the orders of a Prime Minister born in the village who was not himself a Sikh, a priest in residence, the Guru Granth Sahib installed, and pilgrims again admitted on festival days (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 33–34, folios 15–16; p. 35, folio 17; p. 37, folio 19).
