## Overview

At Purana Bhalwal, the old settlement that gives its name to the modern town of Bhalwal in Sargodha District, stands the shrine of *Hazrat Shah Sulaiman Nuri*, a sixteenth-century Sufi of the *Qadiriyya* order who is remembered locally with the affectionate title *Sakhi Badshah*, 'the generous king'. The dargah is the spiritual heart of an otherwise unassuming corner of central Punjab, drawing devotees from the surrounding villages of the Chaj Doab, the fertile tract between the Jhelum and the Chenab.

None of the books consulted describes the shrine at Purana Bhalwal itself. Its tomb, its courtyard and its yearly gatherings are known here only from the site's own account; what the books record is the saint. Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori's *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, composed in 1292 AH, 1875 CE, as an account of the saints of Punjab, gives him an entry of his own, numbered 26 and headed "شاہ سلیمان قادری", Shah Sulaiman Qadri, and calls him a *khalifa* of Shah Maruf Chishti (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 66–67, folios 54–55). The book's modern editor corrects its author three times in that one entry: on the name of the place, on the saint's family and on the year of his death (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 66–67, folios 54–55). It is the editor's correction of the place name, not the author's own text, that makes the place Bhalwal (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 66–67, folios 54–55, حاشیہ 3). Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti's *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* names him once, as "شاہ سلیمان نوری", Shah Sulaiman Nuri, in a spiritual pedigree that runs from Shah Maruf Chishti through him to Naushah Ganj Bakhsh (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 467).

## The Life of Shah Sulaiman Nuri

Shah Sulaiman Nuri is traditionally said to have lived a remarkably long life, from the early sixteenth century until 1604. According to the accounts preserved by his devotees, he was a scholar and ascetic trained in the *Qadiri* path, and his spiritual guide (*pir*) was Shah Maroof Khushabi of the nearby town of Khushab. It is related that his teacher, recognising his disciple's attainment, directed him to settle at Purana Bhalwal and to make it the centre of his teaching and service. There he is said to have spent the remainder of his life in devotion, instruction and the feeding of travellers and the poor — the quality of open-handed hospitality from which his epithet *Sakhi Badshah* is popularly derived.

As with many saints of this period, the details of his biography survive largely through oral tradition and later hagiography rather than contemporary record, and precise dates should be treated as traditional. He is said to have died at a great age on the night of the twenty-seventh of Ramadan, one of the most sacred nights of the Muslim calendar, a detail that has only deepened the reverence in which he is held.

## Family and Birthplace

Lahori's entry opens with the saint's father and his village. The author writes: "ان کا باپ منگو قوم موچی موضع بھیلووال [OCR?] میں رہتا تھا": his father, Mangu, of the Mochi caste, lived in the village of Bhelowal, a name whose reading is uncertain in the printed text (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 66–67, folios 54–55).

The editor's footnotes correct both halves of that sentence. Of the place he writes "بھیلووال کی بجائے بھلوال ہونا چاہیے": instead of Bhelowal it should be Bhalwal (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 66–67, folios 54–55, حاشیہ 3). Of the family he writes that the saint's father was Shaikh Abdullah, known as Mian Mangu, of the Qureshi caste, and that "آپ موچی نہیں تھے", he was not a Mochi; for the Qureshi genealogy he cites Sharafat Naushahi's *Sharif al-Tawarikh*, volume 1, page 865, and Umar Bakhsh Rasulnagri's *Manaqibat-e Naushahi* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 66–67, folios 54–55, حاشیہ 2). The book thus carries two accounts side by side: the author's Mochi father named Mangu, and the editor's Qureshi father, Shaikh Abdullah, called Mian Mangu.

## His Master and His Disciple

The site's account makes Shah Maroof Khushabi his *pir*. Lahori calls him a *khalifa* of Shah Maruf Chishti (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 66–67, folios 54–55), and in another passage speaks of "شاہ معروف چشتی و قادری", Shah Maruf, Chishti and Qadiri, the subject of his twelfth entry (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 50, folio 38). Chishti's *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* records that Maruf Chishti held authority to initiate in two orders, "قادریہ و چشتیہ", the Qadiriyya and the Chishtiyya (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 467).

Lahori records that Shah Maruf marked Sulaiman out while he was still a child (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 66–67, folios 54–55). The site's account, for its part, relates that his teacher, recognising his disciple's attainment, sent him to settle at Purana Bhalwal.

The disciple who carried the line on was, in Lahori's account, Haji Muhammad Naushah Ganj Bakhsh, who was brought to Shah Sulaiman by Mulla Karim al-Din (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 70–71, folios 58–59). Lahori's own entry on that disciple is headed "شیخ حاجی محمد قادری المشہور بحضرت نوشہ گنج بخش", and says that the Naushahi way began with him: "طریقہ نوشاہیہ جس کے فقیر پنجاب میں ہزاروں ہیں۔ ان سے شروع ہوا", the thousands of its *faqirs* in Punjab being those of the author's own day (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 70, folio 58).

The same chain appears in *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, in the second *shajra*, or spiritual pedigree, of one Bhage Shah: "… حضرت حاجی محمود شاہ نوشاہ گنج بخش اونکو[OCR?] شاہ سلیمان نوری اونکی حضرت معروف چشتی" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 467). Here Shah Sulaiman Nuri stands between Naushah Ganj Bakhsh and Hazrat Maruf Chishti, and the disciple is named Haji Mahmud Shah rather than Haji Muhammad.

Of the spread of the Naushahi order Lahori also writes, in a passage on the lifetime of Shah Maruf, "انہیں کی ذات بابرکات سے پنجاب میں سلسلہ نوشاہیہ نے فروغ پایا": the Naushahi order spread in Punjab through him. The sentence names Sayyid Mubarak Haqqani just before, and the text does not settle whether 'his' means Shah Maruf or Sayyid Mubarak (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 50, folio 38).

## Teaching, Listening and Ecstasy

Lahori's entry describes the saint's way of devotion. He listened to *sama*, and in *wajd* he would fall so senseless "کہ مرگ کی حالت تک پہنچ جاتے", that he reached the condition of death (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 67, folio 55). Thousands, the book says, entered his *bai'at* and reached high degrees, and his whole life was spent guiding students (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 67, folio 55).

## His Death

The site's account gives his death as 1604, on the night of the twenty-seventh of Ramadan. Lahori's author gives the year in words, "سال ایک ہزار پینسٹھ", with ۱۰۶۵ in the margin: 1065 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 67, folio 55, حاشیہ 1). The editor rejects it: "آپ ۲۷ رمضان ۱۰۱۲ھ میں فوت ہوئے (عمر بخش: مناقبات نوشاہی قلمی)، ۱۰۶۵ھ غلط ہے", he died on 27 Ramadan 1012 AH, citing Umar Bakhsh's *Manaqibat-e Naushahi*, and 1065 AH is wrong (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 67, folio 55, حاشیہ 1). The editor's 1012 AH is the Hijri year in which 1604 falls; the author's 1065 AH is not.

## The Name in the Books

The names the site uses are not all in the books. Lahori's entry heads him Shah Sulaiman Qadri and gives neither 'Nuri' nor 'Sakhi Badshah'; *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* calls him Shah Sulaiman Nuri (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 66–67, folios 54–55; Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 467). The index of *Hadiqat al-Awliya* lists a "سلیمان بھلوالی", Sulaiman of Bhalwal, and on a separate line a "سلیمان قادری لاہوری شاہ"; the first carries the place and the second the order, and neither line carries both, so which of them leads to this entry is not settled (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 319, folio 307). The same index keeps several other Sulaimans apart from these two (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 319, folio 307).

## The Shrine and its Devotional Life

The shrine at Purana Bhalwal follows the familiar pattern of Punjab's smaller Sufi dargahs: a whitewashed tomb chamber sheltering the grave, enclosed within a courtyard where devotees gather to offer *Fatiha*, tie threads of intention, and distribute food. The saint's *urs* — the annual commemoration of his death anniversary, understood in Sufi thought as the soul's union with the Divine — is the principal event of the devotional year, when *qawwals* perform devotional song and *langar* (the free communal meal) is served to all who come.

The books say nothing of the shrine's building, its founding, its custodians or the date of its *urs*. They do speak of the traditions the gathering draws on. Regula Burckhardt Qureshi, writing of *qawwali* in general and not of this shrine, distinguishes the tunes of its two principal regions, Uttar Pradesh and a Panjab she does not divide between India and Pakistan: "A predominantly quantitative emphasis characterizes tunes from Uttar Pradesh, while Panjabi tunes tend to be rhythmically organized according to 'quality' or stress", the drum's articulation of the metre being "more strongly accentual than in Uttar Pradesh" (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 73, folio 53). Annemarie Schimmel, writing of the mystical literature of Sind and Punjab, notes that "Both provinces have also produced hymns extolling the great masters of the mystical orders", particularly ʿAbd al-Qadir Gilani, in imagery that "could easily be understood by everybody in the village" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 417, folio 386).

Bhalwal itself grew as an agricultural market town under the canal colonies of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, and the shrine anchored the older village of Purana ("old") Bhalwal as the newer town expanded around the railway. For the cultivators of the district the saint remains a figure of intercession and blessing, invoked at times of illness, harvest and family need, in the manner characteristic of the living shrine culture of rural Punjab.

## Setting

The books that describe the country around Bhalwal speak of the province, not of the town. Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, describing the Mughal suba of Lahore, divides it into five *doabs*, the tracts between its rivers, and names the doab between the Chenab and the Bihat "چنتهه[OCR?]", the reading uncertain in the printed text, with "چونهت" for Gujrat (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 511; p. 515). In that suba, he writes, "اکثر مدار اب یاری[OCR?] چاه است": irrigation depended mostly on wells, each worked by a wheel of "سیصد و شصت چوب" and more than a hundred small pots turned by a pair of oxen, while the *kharif* and *rabi* harvests and the cheapness of grain depended on the rain (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 510).

The canal colonies came later. Haroon Khalid writes of the province as a whole that "the British connected the five rivers of Punjab with each other through canals, turning several acres of jungle land into agricultural land", that "thousands of new villages, referred to as chaks were established, and farming families from all over Punjab were invited to settle", and that its former inhabitants "were primarily nomads", unable "to prove ownership over land": "Their land was snatched away and allotted to these migrants" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated).

Of the order the site names, Lahori writes that the first of the Qadiri line to come to Punjab was "حضرت سید محمد غوث اوچی جیلی گیلانی", who came by way of Khurasan to Multan and settled at Uch, after his ancestors had left Baghdad in the time of Hulagu Khan; the book gives no year, and the editor's footnote adds, on the authority of Ali Asghar Gilani, that he entered Uch in 887 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 40, folio 28).

*Hadiqat al-Awliya*, the book that keeps the fullest record of Shah Sulaiman, closes with chronograms for its own completion. One, by Mufti Ghulam Safdar Lahori, whom the book calls the author's "خلف", sets out its scope in Punjab:

جو خدا کے دوست تھے پنجاب میں
حال ان کا اس سے ہوتا ہے عیان

*A chronogram couplet on the book's scope, the saints of Punjab; the closing chronograms read ۱۲۹۲ھ, 1292 AH* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 282, folio 270).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The year of his death.** The site's account gives 1604, on the night of the twenty-seventh of Ramadan. Lahori's author gives 1065 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 67, folio 55, حاشیہ 1). His editor gives 27 Ramadan 1012 AH, citing Umar Bakhsh's *Manaqibat-e Naushahi*, and calls 1065 AH wrong (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 67, folio 55, حاشیہ 1).
- **His father and caste.** Lahori's author names the father Mangu, of the Mochi caste (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 66–67, folios 54–55). His editor names him Shaikh Abdullah, known as Mian Mangu, of the Qureshi caste, and says he was not a Mochi, citing *Sharif al-Tawarikh* and *Manaqibat-e Naushahi* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 66–67, folios 54–55, حاشیہ 2). The site's account does not name his father.
- **The name of the place.** The site's account gives Purana Bhalwal. Lahori's author writes "بھیلووال [OCR?]", Bhelowal; his editor says it should be Bhalwal (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 66–67, folios 54–55, حاشیہ 3).
- **The saint's name and title.** The site's account calls him Hazrat Shah Sulaiman Nuri and Sakhi Badshah. Lahori heads his entry "شاہ سلیمان قادری" and gives neither Nuri nor Sakhi Badshah (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 66–67, folios 54–55). *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* calls him "شاہ سلیمان نوری" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 467).
- **His master and his order.** The site's account names his *pir* as Shah Maroof Khushabi of Khushab and places him in the Qadiri path. Lahori calls him a *khalifa* of Shah Maruf Chishti (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 66–67, folios 54–55), and elsewhere calls Shah Maruf "چشتی و قادری" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 50, folio 38). *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* names him "حضرت معروف چشتی", with authority in both the Qadiriyya and the Chishtiyya (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 467).
- **The name of his disciple.** Lahori names the disciple Haji Muhammad Naushah Ganj Bakhsh (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 70–71, folios 58–59), and heads his entry "شیخ حاجی محمد قادری المشہور بحضرت نوشہ گنج بخش" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 70, folio 58). *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* names him "حضرت حاجی محمود شاہ نوشاہ گنج بخش" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 467).

## Legacy

Shah Sulaiman Nuri belongs to the dense network of Qadiri and Chishti saints whose shrines dot the Chaj and Rechna Doabs, forming a sacred geography that shaped the religious life of Punjab long before modern boundaries were drawn. While he never attained the trans-regional fame of a Data Ganj Bakhsh or a Baba Farid, his dargah illustrates how Sufi devotion took root at the local level, giving small communities their own centre of blessing and continuity. The shrine remains in active use today, cared for by the descendants and devotees of the saint and, like many such sites, falling under the general oversight of the provincial Auqaf administration.

The books place him at a turning in the history of the orders of Punjab. In Lahori's account he is the link between Shah Maruf Chishti and Naushah Ganj Bakhsh, from whom the Naushahi way began (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 66–67, folios 54–55; pp. 70–71, folios 58–59; p. 70, folio 58), and *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* keeps him in the same place in Bhage Shah's pedigree (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 467).

Of the hereditary saints of the province in general, not of this shrine, Robert Rozehnal quotes Katherine Ewing's *Arguing Sainthood*: "British policy, especially in the Punjab and Sind, was to treat them in essentially the same way that landlords and tribal leaders were treated", the hereditary saints being those who "were seen primarily in terms of their economic power", and the policy one of securing "the loyalty of the pirs, landlords, and chiefs by reinforcing their economic positions and educating them in the British tradition" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 33, folio 23).
