## Overview

In Multan stands the shrine of Hazrat Syed Musa Pak Shaheed — a revered Qadiri saint of the sixteenth century and the ancestor of the Gilani (Musa Pak) sayyids, one of Multan’s most eminent religious families. Honoured with the titles *Pak* (“the pure”) and *Shaheed* (“the martyr”), Musa Pak carried the Qadiri order of Abd al-Qadir Jilani in Multan and founded a spiritual line that remained influential in the city for centuries. His darbar is among the notable shrines of the ‘City of Saints’.

Among the books consulted here, Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori's *Hadiqat al-Awliya* gives him a full entry, no. 15, headed "Sayyid Musa Pak Shaheed" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 52–53, folios 40–41). It supplies his fuller name and his father's, a famous disciple in Shaikh Abdul Haq Muhaddith Dehlvi, a printed work of his own, and a martyrdom at the hands of the Langah, and it calls his tomb in Multan a place of pilgrimage (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 53, folio 41). Its author and its modern editor disagree on the year of his death. Sujan Rai Bhandari's *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* names a Shaikh Musa Gilani in its list of the saints buried in Multan, and says nothing more (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528). None of the books describes the building, its *ʿurs* or its custodians.

## The Life of the Saint

Syed Musa, called Musa Pak Shaheed, was a Sayyid of the Gilani line — descendants of the great Baghdad saint Shaikh Abd al-Qadir Jilani — who flourished in Multan in the sixteenth century. Learned and devout, he was a shaykh of the Qadiri order and a guide of many disciples, and he is remembered for a life of piety and for the sanctity that earned him the epithet *Pak*, ‘the pure’. Tradition honours him as a *shaheed*, a martyr. Through him and his descendants the Qadiri-Gilani presence was firmly established in Multan, and the Musa Pak Gilani family became one of the leading religious and landed houses of the region. He was buried in Multan, where his shrine arose.

Lahori calls him the beloved son of Sayyid Hamid Ganj Bakhsh Gilani Uchi, and writes that when he had completed his training before his father he was given the title Jamal al-Din Abul Hasan (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 52–53, folios 40–41). In a later entry, no. 141, the book gives his name at full length, as Sayyid Jamal al-Din Abu al-Hasan Musa Pak Shaheed Gilani Multani, the worthy son of Shaikh Sayyid Hamid Gilani, and praises his father as the exemplar of the noble Sayyids and the choicest of the great shaikhs (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 206, folio 194). The book's index lists him as Musa Pak Shaheed Multani (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 330, folio 318).

Of his inner life Lahori writes that he had a special *nisbat* with the spirituality of Ghaus al-Azam, so that its presence was constant with him, and that hundreds of times he was blessed with visions of the Prophet, waking and sleeping. His whole life, the book says, passed in austerity, struggle, worship, teaching and instruction (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 53, folio 41).

The book does not speak with one voice on how he came to his father's seat. Its text says that Sayyid Jamal al-Din Musa succeeded to the *masnad* after his father's death (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 48, folio 36), while the editor's note to his own entry records a dispute over that same succession lasting 'long years' (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 53, folio 41).

## His Disciple Abdul Haq Muhaddith Dehlvi

Great *ulama*, Lahori writes, became his disciples, and Shaikh Abdul Haq Muhaddith Dehlvi too was his sincere disciple. The editor dates that attachment precisely, to 6 Shawwal 985 AH / 1577 CE, and adds that the shaikh gave his disciple special attention (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 53, folio 41). The book tells the same relation from the other side in its entry no. 141, where Abdul Haq's *bai'at* is at Musa Pak's hand (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 206, folio 194).

The same entry follows the disciple onward: after the saint's death Abdul Haq went to Shaikh Abd al-Wahhab, the *khalifa* of Shaikh Ali Muttaqi, and received from him *faiz* and a *khirqa-e tabarruk* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 206, folio 194). The editor dates that next stage: Abdul Haq reached the Hijaz in 996 AH and stayed there until 999 AH, passing almost all of that time with Abd al-Wahhab (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 206, note 1).

## His Writings

A work of his own, *Taisir al-Thaqalain*, on litanies and observances (*awrad* and *ma'mulat*), was printed at Ferozepur in 1309 AH, as the editor notes (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 53, folio 41, note 2). The edition's bibliography lists it again, as Musa Pak Shaheed Multani's *Taisir al-Thaqalain*, Ferozepur 1309 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 300, folio 288). For his life the editor points further to *Bahr al-Sarair*, on his conditions and practices, in a manuscript owned by Maulvi Hakim Allah Bakhsh Ansari; to the *Akhbar al-Akhyar*; and to Badayuni's *Muntakhab al-Tawarikh* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 53, folio 41, note 2).

## His Martyrdom and Its Date

Lahori's author is the one source here that tells how he died: he was martyred in the year 1001 AH at the hands of the ill-wishers of the Langah tribe (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 53, folio 41). The book says nothing more of the circumstances, and in its later entry the word Shaheed, martyr, stands in his name without explanation (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 206, folio 194).

The edition's editor rejects the author's year outright: the year 1001 AH, he writes, is wrong. He gives instead the year 998 AH, which Shaikh Abdul Haq Muhaddith Dehlvi, the saint's own disciple, derived from a chronogram on his death, preserved in a manuscript *bayaz* in the Punjab University library (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 53, folio 41, note 1). The chronogram is a phrase of three words:

شمع جمع سیادت

*The chronogram on the saint's death, by Shaikh Abdul Haq Muhaddith Dehlvi, which the editor reports as yielding 998 AH.* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 53, folio 41, note 1)

A third reading can be drawn from the book, though the book does not draw it. Its text has Abdul Haq go on to Abd al-Wahhab after the saint's death, and its editor dates Abdul Haq's arrival in the Hijaz to 996 AH; read together, the two statements would place the death before 996 AH. This is an inference from two sentences, one the author's and one the editor's, and the book does not say that the two events followed one another directly (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 206, note 1).

## The Shrine and its Devotion

The shrine of Syed Musa Pak in Multan is kept by his Gilani descendants and is a continuous place of pilgrimage, where devotees come to recite the Fatiha, seek intercession, and honour the saint. It maintains the customary devotions of a Multan darbar — prayer, qawwali, and the free kitchen — and its annual *ʿurs* gathers the faithful. It forms part of the great constellation of shrines that gives Multan its identity.

Lahori's account bears witness to the tomb as a place of pilgrimage in his own day: his *rauza* in Multan is a place of pilgrimage for God's creatures (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 53, folio 41). He says nothing of the building, its *ʿurs* or its keepers, and neither does his later entry (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 206, folio 194). The books consulted here do not record who built the tomb, or when, or the date of the *ʿurs*.

In its account of the suba of Multan, Bhandari's *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* includes Shaikh Musa Gilani in its list of the saints buried in the city, and says nothing more about him (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528). The name is taken to be this saint's on the strength of the name Musa and the city alone: the book calls him Shaikh, not Sayyid, and gives him neither Pak nor Shaheed (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528). Beyond those it names, the book says, many saints lie at rest in that blessed city (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528).

## A Former Grave at Uch

Haroon Khalid's travelogue records, at Uch Sharif and not at Multan, a structure that may bear on this saint. The Gilani shrine there, he writes, "is situated next to a fifteenth century structure, where the grave of Moosa Pak Shaheed once existed", and "According to a board next to the structure, put up by the Department of Archaeology, his body was later shifted to Multan"; the building is "octagonal, constructed in typical Multani fashion with blue tiles. In a dilapidated condition now" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 278, folio 252). Khalid himself adds of this Moosa Pak Shaheed, "It's unclear who he was" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 278, folio 252). The link to the Multan shrine rests on the name and on the board's statement that the body went to Multan; the Uch structure is a separate building from the shrine described here.

## Setting

The books' wider accounts of the Qadiri order, of the Langah and of Multan speak of the order, the tribe and the city, not of this shrine, but they help to place the saint's story.

Lahori's account of the coming of the Qadiri line to the Punjab says that Sayyid Muhammad Ghaus Uchi Jili Gilani was its first to arrive, after his ancestors had left Baghdad in the time of Hulagu Khan. He came to Multan by way of Khurasan and settled at Uch; the editor adds, citing Ali Asghar Gilani, that he entered Uch in 887 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 40, folio 28). This is said of the order, not of Musa Pak's own household. Rizvi, writing of the age of Guru Nanak, speaks of "the Qadiris who had made the Panjab the main centre of their activities" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 420, folio 396). Schimmel writes that Sind and the Punjab "have also produced hymns extolling the great masters of the mystical orders", naming Abd al-Qadir Gilani above them all (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 417, folio 386).

The Langah, at whose hands Lahori's author has the saint die, appear in Bhandari's history of Multan as a tribe that once ruled the city. In those days, Bhandari writes, the Langah rose in revolt in Multan and raised the banner of sovereignty, though the printed word for their revolt cannot be read with certainty (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 324). He tells how the chief of the Langah, whose printed name, perhaps Rai Sanhra, cannot be read with certainty, seized the fort of Multan from Shaikh Yusuf Qureshi by a ruse and took a royal title, and he lists the Langah sultans who followed (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 296–297). Their rule ended with the Arghun conquest, after which, Bhandari writes, Multan was so ruined that it occurred to no one that it would ever be peopled again (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 294). He does not connect the Langah with the saint.

Bhandari also names a later governor of Multan with a like name: Shaikh Musa Gilani, the man who held Multan for Dara Shikoh and submitted to the pursuing emperor (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 66). The reading of the name is uncertain in the printed text, and the book does not say whether this governor was of the saint's family.

Of the city in Guru Nanak's time, Haroon Khalid writes that "At that time Multan was one of the biggest cities of Punjab and was known for the religious shrines of Muslim saints, and Jain and Hindu temples. It was a sacred space for people of all religious denominations" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 123, folio 105). Harish Dhillon writes that "By the time Nanak came to Multan, the city was famous as a major centre of Sufi learning and philosophy" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 167, folio 183).

Shemeem Burney Abbas describes, without naming any shrine or family, the place of the Syed houses in the city's devotional life: "Musicians such as Surraiya Multanikar are associated with the powerful landowning Syeds of Multan and Bahawalpur who claim descent from the prophet Muhammad's family. Their training is supported by the Syed households, and these musicians perform in the family shrines of the Syeds. This is partly because of the large landholdings where the affluent Syeds support the shrines of their ancestors. They arrange annual rituals such as the Eid and the Muharram ceremonies marking the tragedy of Kerbala" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 168–169, folios not stated).

Multan had its place in the region's poetry as well. Writing of the vernacular poets, Schimmel says that "Mecca and Medina (and for the Sindhi and Panjabi, also Multan) are inside the heart" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 178, folio 160). In Madho Lal Hussein's verse the city appears as a market, in a bride's trousseau:

*The dress / someone carried across the snows / all the way from Kashmir. The dress / someone brought from Gujarat… The dress bought in Multan* (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 51, folio 51, kafi 29)

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The year of his death.** The site's account gives no year, placing him in the sixteenth century. Lahori's author gives 1001 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 53, folio 41). His editor calls 1001 AH wrong and gives 998 AH, from Shaikh Abdul Haq Muhaddith Dehlvi's chronogram on the saint's death (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 53, folio 41, note 1). The book's account of Abdul Haq, read with the editor's date for his arrival in the Hijaz, would put the death before 996 AH, though the book does not say so (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 206, note 1).
- **The succession to his father.** Lahori's text has Sayyid Jamal al-Din Musa succeed to the *masnad* after his father's death (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 48, folio 36); the editor's note records a dispute of 'long years' over that succession (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 53, folio 41).
- **His name and title.** The site's account calls him Syed Musa Pak Shaheed. Lahori gives "Sayyid Musa Pak Shaheed" as the heading of his entry (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 52–53, folios 40–41) and, in a later entry, the fuller Sayyid Jamal al-Din Abu al-Hasan Musa Pak Shaheed Gilani Multani (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 206, folio 194). Bhandari's list of the saints of Multan has Shaikh Musa Gilani, Shaikh rather than Sayyid, with neither Pak nor Shaheed (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528). Khalid writes "Moosa Pak Shaheed", and of him "It's unclear who he was" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 278, folio 252).
- **Where he was first buried.** The site's account has him buried in Multan, where his shrine arose, and Lahori places his tomb in Multan (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 53, folio 41). An Archaeology Department board at Uch Sharif, as Khalid reports it, says that the grave of a Moosa Pak Shaheed once lay in a fifteenth-century structure there and that his body was later shifted to Multan (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 278, folio 252).

## Legacy

Syed Musa Pak Shaheed is remembered as a chief bearer of the Qadiri order in Multan and the founder of the distinguished Musa Pak Gilani line — a saint whose purity and martyrdom won him lasting veneration, and whose family carried his influence through the later history of the city. His shrine remains a revered darbar of the ‘City of Saints’.

The books add to that memory a famous disciple and a written legacy: Lahori counts Shaikh Abdul Haq Muhaddith Dehlvi among his disciples, and his editor records the printing of the saint's *Taisir al-Thaqalain* at Ferozepur in 1309 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 53, folio 41; p. 53, folio 41, note 2). In Lahori's own day his tomb in Multan was a place of pilgrimage (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 53, folio 41).
