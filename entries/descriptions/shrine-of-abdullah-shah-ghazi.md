## Overview

On a low hill in Clifton, overlooking the Arabian Sea in the heart of Karachi, stands the shrine of Hazrat Abdullah Shah Ghazi, the patron saint of the city, whose green-domed darbar is among the most visited and most beloved sacred places in Pakistan’s largest metropolis. Tradition places him in the eighth century and holds him to be a descendant of the Prophet through the line of Imam Hasan. For the people of Karachi he is the guardian of the city, believed to shield it from storm and calamity, and his shrine is a ceaseless resort of pilgrims of every kind.

The books consulted here say little about the saint himself. None of them gives his dates, his lineage or an account of his life, and none describes the building. What they record is the shrine in recent times: Haroon Khalid calls it "the most famous shrine in the city" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated), records the attack on it in 2010 (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated), and names it among the Sufi shrines of Pakistan that have come under attack (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 278, folio 252). Omar Kasmani's study of the fakirs of Sehwan quotes a woman who petitioned at "Abdullah Shah [a shrine in Karachi]", which may be this shrine; the book gives no "Ghazi" and no Clifton, and its words do not exclude another Karachi shrine of the same short name (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 119, folio 102).

## The Life of the Saint

The historical record of Abdullah Shah Ghazi is thin and wrapped in legend, but the tradition is strong and old. He is remembered as a Sayyid of the family of the Prophet, by the usual account a descendant of Imam Hasan, who came to Sindh in the early Islamic centuries: the eighth century, in the age when Islam was first spreading in the region through Muhammad bin Qasim and after. He came, the tradition holds, as a merchant, a warrior (*ghazi*) and a preacher.

He is said to have settled by the sea near what became Karachi, and to have died there, some traditions holding that he was martyred. His grave on the Clifton hill became a place of blessing, and over the centuries grew into the great shrine that now bears his name.

The books consulted here record no year of birth or death for him, no teacher and no Sufi order, and none of them tells the story of his coming to Sindh. Kasmani's book prints no "Ghazi", no Clifton, no dates, no order, no custodian and nothing about the fabric of the shrine (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 119, folio 102).

## The Patron Saint of Karachi

Abdullah Shah Ghazi is woven into the very identity of Karachi. It is widely believed that his presence protects the coastal city from cyclones and disaster, and that the sea has never brought ruin upon Karachi because the saint keeps watch over it from his hill. Khalid calls it "the most famous shrine in the city" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).

## Devotion and Practice

His shrine draws an immense and continuous cross-section of the city, rich and poor, of every sect and background, who come to recite the Fatiha, tie vows, distribute food, and share in the qawwali and the *dhamal* that fill its evenings, above all on Thursdays and at the annual *ʿurs*. The books consulted here give no date for the *ʿurs*.

Kasmani quotes a woman, Zaheda, who may have brought her petitions here. Her family had moved to Karachi in the early 2000s, when her husband found work in the army, and it was there that she "got acquainted with the shrines of the Sindh region" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 119, folio 102). She tells of going from shrine to shrine without the answer she sought: "It may have been possible perhaps that someone else would give me something; that I would go to another shrine and he [that saint] would have given me something. I have wandered through numerous saints' places. I have also tried two or three times at Abdullah Shah [a shrine in Karachi]. I even broke down into tears" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 119, folio 102). The words in square brackets are the book's own. The book's words name only "Abdullah Shah" in Karachi; the link to this shrine rests on that name, and the book does not exclude another Karachi shrine called by it (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 119, folio 102).

Khalid recalls a news broadcast from "sometime ago", giving no date, in which "the anchor, a self-proclaimed vigilante, entered the shrine of Abdullah Shah Ghazi in Karachi ... and secretly filmed people consuming hashish", and then "began harassing the caretaker of the shrine for allowing such a practice" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated). Khalid found "the apologetic response of the caretaker" a disappointment: "under pressure, in front of the camera, the caretaker failed to explain the cultural context of the shrine and promised to monitor the situation better in the future" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated). That is Khalid's own judgement of the episode; he gives neither the channel nor the caretaker's name.

## The Attack of 2010

Like other great shrines, it has been a target of violence: a suicide bombing struck it in 2010. Khalid gives the date and the toll: "On 6 October 2010 the shrine of Abdullah Shah Ghazi in Karachi was attacked killing eight people" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated). In another book he names "Abdullah Shah Ghazi's in Karachi" among the Sufi shrines that have "come under attack" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 278, folio 252).

The devotion of Karachi has never faltered, and the shrine, since rebuilt and expanded with its landmark green dome, remains thronged. The books consulted here do not describe the rebuilding or say who carried it out.

## Setting

The books' wider accounts of Karachi and of Sindh speak of the city and the province, not of this shrine, but they help to place it.

The current account of the saint sets his coming in the age of Muhammad bin Qasim. Rizvi dates that conquest, writing that "In 710 Muhammad bin Qasim al-Saqafi began his advance through Makran and Baluchistan with the conquest of Sind between 711 and 712" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India, vol. 1*, p. 127, folio 109). He adds that of the early Arab period "no such developments in Sind are recorded" as the spread of Sufism seen further north and west, and that "Early Arab conquerors settled their families in large numbers in the various towns of Sind" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India, vol. 1*, p. 127, folio 109). Shemeem Burney Abbas writes that "The Sufi mystical traditions came to the subcontinent of Pakistan and India through the Sind province", and that after Muhammad bin Qasim's conquest "a long line of Sufi mystics came to the region through Baluchistan, Mekran, Multan, and Sind itself" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 50, folio not stated).

Of the place itself, Khalid notes that "A large part of Clifton is constructed on reclaimed land" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 217, folio 191). He also gives a legend of the city's name, told to him by a companion: the area was "known as the area of [Mai] Kolachi", after an old woman "known as Mai Kolachi" whose son "was killed [by] a fish"; she "tore apart the fish and retrieved" him, and the city "came to be known as Kolachi" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 225, folio 199). "At the time of Partition", he writes, "Karachi was a minor port city", and migrants from India made it "the largest city of Pakistan" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 226, folio 200).

The devotions described in the current account have their context in the books' accounts of the city and the province. Regula Burckhardt Qureshi names Karachi as one of "two important urban centres of Sufism", with Hyderabad in India, "where members of the same Qawwal Bachche lineage actively maintain their special Qawwali heritage" (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 33, folio 13). Abbas defines the *dhammal* as "The vigorous ritual dance performed at Sufi shrines in Panjab and Sind" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 180, folio not stated). Kasmani describes Karachi's shrines as a stage in the fakirs' round: after the three-day annual fair of Sehwan, fakirs "follow other fakirs and proceed to shrines in Karachi" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 114, folio 97). Of the Karachi shrines, the only one his book names is "Abdullah Shah" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 119, folio 102).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The saint's name.** The site's account and Khalid call him Abdullah Shah Ghazi (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated). Kasmani's informant names only "Abdullah Shah [a shrine in Karachi]", with no "Ghazi" and no Clifton, and the book does not exclude another Karachi shrine of the same short name (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 119, folio 102).
- **The attack of 2010.** The site's account speaks of a suicide bombing in 2010 and gives no date or toll. Khalid writes that "On 6 October 2010 the shrine of Abdullah Shah Ghazi in Karachi was attacked killing eight people", and does not say how it was attacked (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).

## Legacy

Abdullah Shah Ghazi is the spiritual guardian of Karachi: a saint of the earliest Islamic centuries whose shrine on the Clifton hill has become the sacred heart of a modern megacity of many millions. In him a coastal metropolis of migrants and strangers finds a common patron and a place of refuge, and his darbar remains one of the most vibrant centres of popular devotion in Pakistan.
