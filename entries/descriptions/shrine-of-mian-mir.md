## Overview

In the Dharampura quarter of Lahore, once the garden-suburb of Baghbanpura (or Hashimpura), stands the shrine of Hazrat Mian Mir — Mir Mohammad of Sindh — one of the greatest Sufi saints of the Mughal age and among the most beloved holy figures in the history of the city. A master of the Qadiri order, Mian Mir was revered in his own lifetime by beggars and emperors alike for the fierceness of his asceticism, the depth of his learning, and the breadth of a heart that welcomed Muslim, Sikh, Hindu, and Christian without distinction. For nearly four centuries his red-sandstone tomb has drawn pilgrims seeking peace, healing, and blessing, and because of his celebrated friendship with the Sikh Gurus it endures as one of Lahore’s living symbols of interfaith reverence. To understand the shrine is first to understand the life of the man buried within it, and so this account begins with the saint himself before turning to the tomb, its miracles, and the devotion that surrounds it.

The books that speak of him each name him in their own way. Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori's *Hadiqat al-Awliya* gives him an entry of his own, headed with the name Shaikh Muhammad Mir, famous as Mian Mir Qadiri Lahori (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 60–62, folios 48–50). Its index sends the reader from Mir Muhammad to Mian Mir (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 329, folio 317), and its modern editor explains that he was called Mir Muhammad and bore the titles Mian Mir and Shah Mir, *Mian* being used in the local language in the sense of 'sahib', as a mark of respect (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 61, folio 49, note). Outside his own entry the book usually calls him by the epithet Bala Pir, as Mian Mir Bala Pir (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 69, 78, 222, 242, 254–255, 288), and it keeps him apart from another Bala Pir: its entry no. 199 names Sayyid Muhammad Bala Pir Gilani, a grandfather of Miran Muhammad Shah's wife, and entry no. 200, his sister's, names Mian Mir Bala Pir Lahori, two different men in the book's own telling (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 254, folio 242). Kanhaiya Lal's *Tarikh-e Lahore*, in an account whose bearing on this shrine is not beyond doubt (the question is set out with his description of the tomb, below), prints the name as Shaikh Muhammad Mir, famous as Mian Mir Bala Pir Lahori, and counts his tomb among the famous and splendid buildings of Lahore (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 242, folio 245). Among the English-language writers, Scott Kugle, Carl Ernst and Bruce Lawrence, and Robert Rozehnal spell him Miyan Mir (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 326, folio 305, ch. 4 n. 7; Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 65, folio 51; Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 72, folio 62), and Haroon Khalid's *A White Trail* calls him "Mian Meer, the Muslim Sufi Saint of Lahore" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 200, folio not stated).

A note in Taufiq Rafat's selection of Bulleh Shah, citing Khushwant Singh's *History of the Sikhs* (Princeton, 1963), says: "The founder of the Qadiriya order in Lahore was Mian Mir (d. 1635) who had come from Sehwan Sharif in Sind." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 240, folio 220). Annemarie Schimmel speaks of "the Qadiriyya, represented in Lahore by Mian Mir, who had come there from Sind, together with his mystically inclined sister Bibi Jamal." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 392, folio 361). Khalid writes of "Mian Meer (1550-1635), whose shrine now attracts thousands of devotees in Lahore" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 195, folio not stated).

## The Life of the Saint

Mian Mir was born in the sixteenth century in Sehwan (Sivastan) in Sindh — the town lying, as the prince Dara Shikoh later noted, between the cities of Thatta and Bhakkar — into a devout and learned family. His father, Qazi Sain Datta, was a man of religious standing respected throughout Sindh, and the family traced its lineage to the second Caliph of Islam, Umar al-Farooq, from which descent Mian Mir is often given the epithet “Farooqi.” His father died while he was still a child, and the decisive turn of his life came early: at about the age of twelve, with the consent and blessing of his widowed mother, the boy left home in search of inner enlightenment at the feet of some God-oriented soul, and for a time he wandered as a seeker. He was drawn to the inward path and was initiated into the Qadiri silsila — the order that traces its spiritual descent to Shaikh Abd al-Qadir Jilani of Baghdad — at the hands of the master whom the shrine’s tradition remembers as Shaikh Siyustani, from whom he received training and, in time, the khilafat, the authority to guide others.

Lahori names both parents: his father, Qazi Sain Ditta as the book spells him, son of Qazi Qalandar Faruqi, and his mother, Bibi Fatima, daughter of Qazi Qadan. He was born, he writes, at Sewistan; and his father died when he was seven (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 60, folio 48). The book's index enters the mother as Fatima Bibi, mother of Mian Mir (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 325, folio 313). Lahori counts four more brothers, Mian Qazi, Qazi Usman, Qazi Tahir and Qazi Muhammad, and two sisters, Bibi Badi and Bibi Jamal Khatun, the second of whom has an entry of her own in the book's chapter on righteous women (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 62, folio 50). Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti's survey of the tombs and shrines of Lahore, the *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, opens its account under the heading "Mian Mir's Light-Filled Tomb" and gives the names in a form of its own: the name it gives as the Hazrat's is Qazi Sain Ditta, Lahori's name for the father, and the mother is Fatima, daughter of Qazi Qadan, the last name uncertain in the printed text; it names the brothers Qazi Tawakkul (the name printed uncertainly), Qazi Usman and Qazi Tahir, and speaks of two sisters, the word again uncertain (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 284).

On the year of his birth the books divide. Lahori, on the authority of the author of the *Safīnat al-Auliyā*, gives the year 960, with his death in 1045 AH at the age of eighty-seven, his arrival at Lahore at twenty-five and sixty years' residence there (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 61–62, folios 49–50); the book's modern editor dates the birth instead to 957 AH / 1550 CE, on Dara Shikoh's authority in the *Sakīnat al-Auliyā* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 61, folio 49, note). Chishti has him born in the city of Sistan in the year nine hundred and fifty-seven of the hijra, the reading uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 284). Khalid gives the span 1550-1635 (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 195, folio not stated).

Lahori tells the seeker's path differently from the shrine's tradition. At twelve, he writes, the boy finished his religious education and took *bay'a* in the Qadiri silsila at his own mother's hand; only later, and with her permission, did he go to Shaikh Khizr Sistani for its completion, and after the *khirqa* of *khilafat* his pir directed him to Lahore, where he settled (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 61, folio 49). The book makes him the khalifa of Shaikh Khizr Sistani and gives his whole chain of discipleship back through Qutb al-Afaq Sayyid Abdul Razzaq to Abdul Razzaq's father, Abdul Qadir Gilani (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 60, folio 48). Shemeem Burney Abbas, following Rizvi, counts his mother among "the saintly mothers of Shaikh Abdul Haqq Muhaddis Dehlavi, Khawaja Baqi Billah, and Mian Mir, who played a role in making their sons great Sufis" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 192, folio not stated).

Chishti calls him murid and khalifa of Shaikh Khizr Sistani, the last name printed uncertainly, in the exalted Qadiriyya (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 284). In his telling the young man sought a pir in the Kohistan of Sistan (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 284), waited three days and nights, the numeral uncertain in the printed text, took *bay'a* with Shaikh Khizr, and after the completion of his *wilayat* set out for Lahore with the leave of his enlightened pir (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 285). Kanhaiya Lal, too, makes him murid and khalifa of Shaikh Khizr Sistani in the Qadiriyya, from whom he received completion, after which he came to Hindustan and settled in Lahore; the book calls him Lahori and gives no birthplace (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 242, folio 245). At Lahore, Lahori's editor adds, he studied the current sciences with Mawlana Saad Allah Lahori and Akhund Nemat Allah (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 61, folio 49, note), the same Saad Allah whom the editor names elsewhere as Shah Husain's teacher (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 56, folio 44).

Around the age of twenty-five, with his teacher’s permission, Mian Mir came to Lahore and settled in the leafy neighbourhood of Baghbanpura (also recorded as Khanpura) on the edge of the city, beside the river Ravi. There he would remain for the rest of a very long life — by tradition some eighty-eight years — passing his days and nights in devotion, teaching, and the disciplined practice of the spiritual arts, until Lahore itself became inseparable from his name. He died in the city in 1635 (1045 AH) — the date held by the Auqaf Department and the great majority of sources, though a few place his death in 1645. In his final illness, worn by age and by the gout that had crippled his legs, he is said to have refused treatment, explaining that he did not wish to be dulled into unconsciousness: he wished to meet death fully aware, and to feel it come. He had spent some sixty years in the same small cell, and it was there that he passed away.

The books that give a year for his death give 1045 AH or 1635. Lahori gives 1045 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 61–62, folios 49–50), and Kanhaiya Lal 1045 of the hijra, in the reign of Shah Jahan, the figure uncertain in the printed text (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 242, folio 245), though another passage of the same book gives him no date of any kind (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 139, folio 142). Rafat prints "d. 1635" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 240, folio 220), as do Ernst and Lawrence (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 65, folio 51) and the index of Schimmel's *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 521). Chishti gives the hour and the day, the time of the Zuhr prayer on the seventh of Rabi al-Awwal, but the year is illegible in the printed text; and he gives the saint's age as ninety-eight years, the reading uncertain (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 284).

## The Ascetic and His Way

Mian Mir’s contemporaries remembered him above all for the severity and the sincerity of his renunciation. In his hujra — the bare cell in which he lived — he spread nothing more than a piece of old sackcloth upon the floor, and upon that humble seat even the emperors of Hindustan did not hesitate to sit. He wore only coarse homespun khaddar, washed his own clothes with his own hands in the waters of the Ravi, and carried none of the outward marks of a Sufi master: no staff to lean upon in his age, and no patched cloak (khirqa) to advertise his station. Once, when a devotee brought him a staff and he had walked a few steps leaning upon it, he cast it away, saying, “He who has Allah for his support has no need of any artificial prop.” When the wealthy pressed gifts of money and goods upon him for his needs, he turned them away: “I stand in no need of these. I am not a beggar; I am rich, for my Provider looks after my needs — give them to the poor and the needy.” A dervish who begs from another beggar, he held, forfeits his trust in God.

He was a renowned adept of habs-e-dam, the disciplined regulation of the breath — a practice akin to the yogic pranayama — which he would carry through the night from the Isha prayer until the light of dawn, a discipline he passed on to his own successor. Though a man of deep and illumined learning who could expound the intricacies of the Qur’an and Hadith to the astonishment of the scholars of his day, he neither wrote prose nor composed verse, and showed no wish to keep letters or set down his own words; he held that when a dervish scatters his inner state upon paper the secret entrusted to him by God is unveiled to those unworthy to keep it. Ordinarily he was not eager even for visitors: he would welcome a guest, raise his hands in prayer and blessing for him, and then gently ask him to return to his own affairs.

The books add to the picture of his austerity. Lahori writes that he never slept at night; that his practice of *habs-e-dam*, its printed form uncertain, was such that a whole night passed in a single breath; that he broke his fast only after a week; and that sometimes, in absorption, a month at a time passed without his eating (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 61, folio 49). Chishti, too, has him awake all night, and speaks of his holding of the breath, *habs-e nafs* (the reading uncertain), a night passed in one or two breaths and later in four, on the confirmation of his disciple Hazrat Mullah Shah (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 285).

Rafat's note sums up the man in a sentence: "Ascetic and shy of courtly favour, he was extremely popular with people of all religions." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 240, folio 220). Kanhaiya Lal describes him in a string of epithets of devotion and renunciation: a worshipper, an ascetic and a God-fearing man of the highest degree, devoted to God, one who had left the world (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 242, folio 245).

## His Teaching

Mian Mir’s teaching, as his disciples preserved it, was as demanding as his life. He taught that a man is a composite of three things — *nafs* (the ego), *dil* (the heart), and *rooh* (the soul) — and that the whole labour of the spiritual traveller is to bring these into equilibrium: to submit to the *Sharia*, to walk the path of *tariqat* in search of *marifat*, and so to reach *haqiqat*, the Truth. He counselled his followers to fix the heart on God alone and to empty it of the love of the world; to live plainly, eating little, speaking little, and sleeping little; to meet honour and insult with the same evenness of soul; and to accept all weal and woe alike as a blessing from God. Above all he treated every human being, of whatever faith, as a creature of the one God — and he did not flatter power: he would, unmindful of royal displeasure, seek to dissuade the emperor and his sons from any act he judged unjust or unethical. In an age of magnificence, he made poverty and truthfulness his splendour.

The books preserve a few of his rulings in his own words. When news of his sister's wonder with the grain reached him, he said that the display of *karamat* is forbidden and that God is the giver of sustenance (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 255, folio 243); the story is told below, with the conditions he set his disciple Mullah Shah before he would accept him. Chishti records that he was a contemporary of Shah Abul Maali (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 125), and a written exchange with Mian Mir Lahori, the name printed uncertainly, on the question of *wahdat al-wujud* (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 389).

## A Saint Who Wrote No Verse

Uniquely among the great figures of Punjabi Sufism, Mian Mir left behind no *diwan* of poetry — a silence that was itself a kind of teaching. Yet verse gathered around him. Tradition records that when his disciples spoke of raising a fitting monument over his resting place, he forbade any dome or mausoleum, and is said to have uttered a Persian couplet on the vanity of such display:

صورتِ قبرم ز بعدِ مرگ ویرانہ خوشتر است
نیستی مانندِ من با خاک یکساں خوشتر است

*The face of my grave, after death, is better left in ruins; for one like me, to be as nothing, level with the dust, is better.*

His most devoted disciple at court, the Mughal prince Dara Shikoh, honoured him in Persian verse as the very pole of sainthood of the age — Mir Mohammad, he wrote, is the pathfinder for all the friends of God of this age; while the Qadiri line endures it shall lead others, so long as the universe abides. That a prince of the empire should write so of a barefoot dervish in sackcloth is itself a measure of the awe that Mian Mir commanded.

Verse gathered at his tomb as well. Lahori writes that his rauza in Lahore is a place of pilgrimage for God's creatures, and follows the words with a Persian chronogram quatrain that opens:

میر دنیا و دیں میاں میر ست

The opening line is a praise of Mian Mir that plays on his name, *Mir* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 62, folio 50); only its opening is quoted here, and the year it yields is not given.

Kanhaiya Lal records a chronogram set over the door of the tomb, from which, he says, the year of the saint's death is obtained; and in the margin beside it stands the figure 1045, uncertain in the printed text (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 244, folio 247).

Chishti's account of the saint carries two *qitas*: one by Mufti Ghulam Sarwar, and a second whose lead-in names Dara Shikoh's book, the *Sakīnat al-Auliyā*, and a Fath Allah whose further name is illegible, so that who composed it is unclear (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 284).

## Mian Mir and the Mughal Court

For all his withdrawal from the world, Mian Mir’s fame reached the very summit of the Mughal state. The Emperor Jahangir sought him out; Dara Shikoh records a long interview between the emperor and the saint, and relates that Mian Mir — “notwithstanding his great age and weakness” — took the trouble to come to him, and that the emperor, deeply moved, wrote to him both before and after their meeting, requesting to see him again. Such visitors the saint received seated on his sackcloth, offered a prayer for them, and then dismissed with courtesy to their own concerns.

Of Shah Jahan, Chishti writes that the emperor too came into his presence several times (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 289). He tells a story of Shah Jahan and Shah Bilawal in which Mian Mir refuses an offering that Shah Bilawal accepts. Shah Bilawal says of him, in words partly printed uncertainly or illegible, that Mian Mir dwells in the realm of the divine attributes and the world of detachment and solitude, and that his attention is not turned toward the world at all; Mian Mir, speaking to the emperor, likens the perfect *wali* to a river, and his words about himself are partly illegible; and Shah Jahan gives thanks for such perfect saints (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 185).

His deepest imprint on the age, however, came through his disciples. His foremost successor was Mullah Shah Badakhshi, who carried on the master’s nightly practice of habs-e-dam and became the spiritual guide of Prince Dara Shikoh and, through him, of the emperor Shah Jahan’s brilliant daughter, the princess Jahanara. It was Dara Shikoh — the crown prince famed for seeking the common ground between Islam and the mystical traditions of India — who became Mian Mir’s most ardent admirer and who composed, in honour of the saint and his circle, the Persian hagiography *Sakīnat al-Auliyā* (“The Tranquillity of the Saints”), today one of the principal sources for the saint’s life. The nearness of so many of the imperial family to Mian Mir’s circle carried, in the end, a tragic weight: when Dara Shikoh was defeated and put to death by his brother Aurangzeb in the war of succession, the prince and his wife Nadira Begum were laid to rest close to Mian Mir, and tradition holds that Aurangzeb drew upon stone from the complex in the raising of the great Badshahi Mosque of Lahore.

Of Dara Shikoh's book the others speak with one voice. Lahori writes that Prince Dara Shikoh wrote a whole book, the *Sakīnat al-Auliyā*, on his wonders and *karamat* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 61, folio 49). Kanhaiya Lal writes that the Persian *Sakīnat al-Auliyā* of Dara Shikoh, son of Shah Jahan, records thousands of his *karamat*, though he retells none of them (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 242, folio 245). For Schimmel the work "is a fine biography of Mian Mir and the Qadiriyya in Lahore" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 392, folio 361). Lahori's apparatus lists, as the sources for the saint's life, the *Safīnat al-Auliyā* at pp. 69–73, the *Sakīnat al-Auliyā* in its Tehran printing of 1965, Abdul Hamid Lahori's *Badshah Nama*, juz 1, at six separate pages, Kanbo's *Amal-e Salih* 3/357–360, Qani Thathawi's *Tuhfat al-Kiram* and Jahangir's *Tuzuk* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 61, folio 49).

How near Dara Shikoh stood to Mian Mir the books tell in different ways. Rafat's note says that "He nevertheless counted among his disciples Prince Dara Shikoh" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 240, folio 220). Schimmel calls Mian Mir "Jihanara's and Dara's first mystical guide" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 464, folio 433) and writes that "Mian Mir's disciple and successor, Molla Shah Badakhshi, introduced the prince to the saint." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 392, folio 361). In one passage Kanhaiya Lal makes Mian Mir Dara Shikoh's *dada murshid*, his murshid's murshid, and names Mullah Shah Qadri in the same sentence as the prince's own murshid (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 139, folio 142); in another he calls Dara Shikoh Mian Mir's grand-khalifa, the word uncertain in the printed text, his own *iradat* having been at the hand of Shaikh Muhammad, famous as Mullah Shah (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 242, folio 245). Chishti makes Dara, son of Shah Jahan, a murid of Mullah Shah who had a perfect *iradat* toward Mian Mir (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 289, 291).

Ernst and Lawrence describe the Qadiri line that Dara Shikoh chose to claim: "in presenting the Qadiriyya, Dara bypasses the lineage traced" by 'Abd al-Haqq, "acknowledging only that line of Qadiri affiliation traceable through 'Abd al-Qadir II to" 'Abd Allah Bhiti "to Miyan Mir (d. 1635) and then to his own preceptor, Mulla Shah (d. 1660)" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 65, folio 51).

## The Mystical Court: Dara Shikoh and Jahanara

The circle that formed around Mian Mir and his successor Mullah Shah became, for one brief and luminous generation, the spiritual heart of the Mughal court. Prince Dara Shikoh, drawn through Mullah Shah into the Qadiri way, gave his life to the search for the unity underlying Islam and the mystical traditions of India. He gathered the lives and sayings of the saints in his *Safīnat al-Auliyā* and *Sakīnat al-Auliyā*; he wrote the *Majma-ul-Bahrain* — “The Mingling of the Two Oceans” — to show the deep harmony between Sufi and Indian metaphysics; and he caused the Upanishads to be rendered into Persian, a labour that would one day carry Indian thought to Europe. His sister, the princess Jahanara — Shah Jahan’s best-loved daughter — was herself a disciple of Mullah Shah and set down her own spiritual journey in the *Risala-i-Sahibiya*, one of the rare mystical works to come from the hand of a woman of the imperial house. Through these royal disciples the bare Qadiri cell beside the Ravi reached into the very centre of the empire.

Yet the same nearness marked the circle for tragedy. When the throne passed, in blood, to the austere and orthodox Aurangzeb rather than to his mystically minded elder brother, Dara Shikoh was condemned as a heretic and put to death in 1659. The liberal, seeking, syncretic spirit that Mian Mir and Mullah Shah had nourished was driven from the court — to survive thereafter not in palaces but at shrines such as Mian Mir’s, where it had first been kindled. That the prince and his wife were buried beside the saint they revered is a fitting close to the story: in death, the seeker of the union of faiths returned to the threshold of the master who had taught him that God’s door is open to all.

Schimmel names Mian Mir as the first mystical guide of "Shah Jihan's eldest daughter Jihanara" and of "her luckless brother Dara Shikoh" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 464, folio 433), and writes of the princess: "Her master Molla Shah thought her worthy to be his successor, though this did not materialize" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 394, folio 363). Abbas adds of the prince that "Akbar's great-grandson Dara Shikoh, a devotee of Hazrat Mian Mir, a Qadiriya Sufi mystic of Lahore, also patronized Hindu mystics (saniyasis)" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 197, folio not stated).

## Mullah Shah and the Line of Succession

Zahur ul-Hasan Sharib's *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind* meets Mian Mir only as the Lahore pir of Mullah Shah, never as a shrine: in these pages it names no mazar, no death year and no order for him, and the Qadiri name it gives belongs to his disciple (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 292–294, folios 288–290). The book has a chapter of its own for a Hazrat Mian Mir, which gives a birthplace in Sind and a birth year but breaks off before it says anything of a shrine, and the book never joins that chapter to these pages (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 269, folio 265).

Mullah Shah, as Sharib tells it, came from Kashmir to Lahore and could not succeed in his search there (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 292, folio 288). On the road to Agra he learned Mian Mir's name from a stranger and regretted that he had not met him in Lahore (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 292, folio 288); he returned from Agra and, reaching Lahore, presented himself before Mian Mir (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 292–293, folios 288–289).

Then the master tried him. For three years Mian Mir showed him no regard. Then he asked where he lived, and when told that he lived in a mosque, said that living in a mosque was not fitting; he asked what he ate, and when told that it was bazaar bread, said that bazaar bread ought not to be eaten (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 293, folio 289, note 2). The disciple gave up bazaar bread and began to starve (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 293, folio 289, note 2); and only after both renunciations did Mian Mir at last honour him with *bay'a* and give him the *khirqa* of *khilafat* (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 293, folio 289). Sharib takes the exchange from the Urdu translation of the *Sakīnat al-Auliyā*.

Mian Mir called his disciple by a title of his own, Muhammad Shah (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 292, folio 288). Asked for whom he had prayed, he answered that it was for Mullah Shah, through whom his way would shine, a prayer which, as Sharib presents it, foretold his own line through Mullah Shah (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 293, folio 289). Of his disciple's austerity he said, in words the book again takes from the Urdu *Sakīnat*, that none of the shaikhs of the past had performed such *riyazat* (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 294, folio 290, note 3). Sharib counts the disciple's service at thirty years, the number uncertain in the printed text (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 293, folio 289), and says that with his pir's permission Mullah Shah spent the summers in Kashmir and the winters at Lahore in his service (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 294, folio 290). Of the disciple it writes that he holds a distinguished rank among the elders of the Qadiri order (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 294, folio 290).

Lahori, too, names Mullah Shah Qadiri as his khalifa (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 242, folio 230), and shows the line running on in Lahore. Pir Burhan, who came from Bukhara in Akbar's time, took Qadiri *fayz* from the Lahore elders Mian Mir and Shah Bilawal (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 270, folio 258). The chain of Shaikh Abdullah Shah Baloch joined him through four intermediaries (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 82, folio 70 [marker: 80]). In its entry no. 152 the book sets out a Qadiri chain running down to 1213 AH: Sayyid Bhawan Shah, murid of Shah Bilaq (the name uncertain in the printed text), murid of Shah Abd al-Rashid Lahori, murid of Shaikh Muhsin Shah, murid of Shaikh Muhammad, famous as Mullah Shah, murid of Mian Mir Lahori (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 221–222, folios 209–210). In Lahori's telling, then, Mian Mir is the root of a Qadiri lineage still living in Lahore, not merely a seventeenth-century grave.

Not every order honoured the line. From the *Tazkira-e Naushahi*, which Lahori names as his source, he relates that Hafiz Tahir attended Mullah Shah Qadiri, Mian Mir Bala Pir's khalifa, for some years in pursuit of inner knowledge, but got no benefit there, lost his faith, turned from Islam itself, put on the *zunnar* and went begging among Hindu faqirs, until Naushah restored him (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 242, folio 230). Lahori neither endorses nor softens the story; it is the Naushahi tradition's view of the Mian Mir line, not his own judgement.

Rafat's note on Shah Enayat says that he "was a successor to the Qadiriya 'gaddi' of Mian Mir" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 42, folio 22). Chishti, naming Mullah Shah as Dara Shikoh's murshid, refers the reader to his account of Mian Mir, where he has already been spoken of (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 131), and at another place speaks of a murid and khalifa, a word between them illegible, of Hazrat Mian Mir Bala Pir (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 291). He writes that his murids were thousands of people (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 289); he names a faqir, Abd al-Haqq, a murid of Mian Mir Bala Pir (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 689); and he records, in a damaged passage, how Pir Ambari, described as a khadim connected through a Mullah with Hazrat Mian Mir, came by the name Amri, itself printed uncertainly (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 442). The *shajra* of one Mehr Shah, as Chishti gives it, runs back through Miran Shah Said to Hazrat Mian Mir Bala Pir, and he adds that the remaining names of the chain stand in his account of Mian Mir, where the saint's life is told (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 227). Kanhaiya Lal names a khadim of the saint, Mir Inayatullah, as the occupant of a neighbouring tomb (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 264, folio 267).

## Bibi Jamal Khatun and the Saint's Family

Of all his family, the books speak most of his sister Bibi Jamal Khatun. Lahori gives her an entry of her own. The Rabia of her time in renunciation, she received the Qadiri *nisbat* from her full brother Mian Mir Bala Pir Lahori and from their mother (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 254, folio 242). Dara Shikoh, Lahori adds, wrote her life in detail in both his books, the *Sakīnat al-Auliyā* and the *Safīnat al-Auliyā* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 254, folio 242). Abbas, following Rizvi, recalls Bibi Jamal Khatun, "sister of Mian Mir, who, according to Dara Shikoh, was the Rabia of her times" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 192, folio not stated), and Schimmel writes that "The sister of Jihanara's and Dara's first mystical guide Mian Mir, Bibi Jamal Khatun (d. 1639), was one of the outstanding saints of the Qadiriyya order during its formative period in the Punjab." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 464, folio 433).

On her death and burial Lahori and his editor part company. Lahori writes that she died in 1000 AH and lies buried inside a khanqah in the city of Lahore, its name illegible in the printed text (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 255, folio 243). The editor answers that the Mufti's year of 1000 is not right, for Dara Shikoh wrote 1057. He then quotes page 131 of the *Sakīnat al-Auliyā* on her age, by then past sixty, and on the day of her death, a Tuesday (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 255, folio 243, note 1). By that quotation she lived in Sehwan, her native place, and never left it, and died on 27 Rabi al-Awwal 1057 AH; the editor's quotation thus runs against the Lahore burial as well as against the year.

Chishti's register of the graves at the shrine enters the mazar of Bibi Jamal Khatun, sister of Hazrat Mian Mir, against a year read as 1055, uncertain in the printed text, and calls her sister of Mian Mir and mother of Hazrat Muhammad Sharif, the first sajjada-nashin. A second row, for a Bibi Jamal or Wajamal, sister of Mian Mir Sahib (both readings uncertain), describes a grave formerly *pakka*, with a tomb, a garden and a mosque, since razed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 325). In the table of graves compiled by Lahori's modern editor, the column for 1875 places her inside the enclosure of Mian Mir's khanqah, and the column for 1974 between the Karachi railway line and Miani Sahib village (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 291, folio 279).

## The Golden Temple and a Friendship Across Faiths

The most famous tradition attached to Mian Mir binds his memory to the Sikh faith. He was a close friend of the fifth Sikh Guru, Arjan Dev, and of the sixth, Guru Hargobind, and by long-standing Sikh tradition it was Mian Mir whom Guru Arjan Dev invited to lay the foundation stone of the Harmandir Sahib — the Golden Temple — at Amritsar, so that the holiest shrine of the Sikhs might be founded by the hand of a Muslim saint. Later historians have weighed the episode carefully: the contemporary Persian chronicles do not mention it, and scholars such as Madanjit Kaur, in *The Golden Temple: Past and Present*, note the absence of early documentary proof, while others have argued that the political tensions of the age — the involvement of the Sirhindi court faction in the death of Guru Arjan Dev — would have made such a gesture difficult. Yet the objection does not really hold, for Mian Mir’s cordial and fraternal relations with the fifth Guru are well attested, and he belonged not to the Sirhindi (Naqshbandi) circle but to the Qadiri order. Whatever the historian concludes about the foundation stone itself, the tradition is cherished across communities as a luminous emblem of the friendship between Muslims and Sikhs in Punjab, and it continues to draw Sikh pilgrims to the saint’s memory. What all the sources agree upon is the temper of the man: Mian Mir received Sikhs, Hindus, and Christians with the same courtesy and reverence he showed his fellow Muslims, and treated them all as children of the one God.

Rafat's note states the tradition as history: "There is evidence that the Qadiriya mystical school of Lahore founded by Mian Mir had begun a close sympathetic link with the Sikhs when they had yet to adopt their anti-Muslim posture. Guru Arjun was a follower of the great Qadiriya mystic and had got him to lay the foundation stone of the Hariman-dir temple at Amritsar." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, pp. 41–42, folios 21–22). Khalid's *A White Trail* says likewise that "Mian Meer, another Muslim saint laid the foundation of one of the holiest Sikh shrines of the world, the Golden Temple" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 167, folio not stated); it calls him "a close associate of Guru Arjan" and adds that "the friendship between Mian Meer and Guru Arjan is also the subject of several folk tales" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 200, folio not stated).

The same note of Rafat's records, as hearsay, a hostility at court. Of Shaikh Ahmad Sirhindi it says: "His dislike of the Qadiriya saint Mian Mir is also said to have prompted him to speak to the king against him." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 42, folio 22).

Khalid's books carry, too, the Sikh memory of the Gurus' ordeals. In *Walking with Nanak*: "It is believed that the Guru [Hargobind] was released after the Muslim saint Mian Mir, who was also a friend of Guru Arjan, interceded on behalf of Guru Hargobind" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 235, folio 209); and, in the Sikh version, "his friend, the Muslim saint of Lahore, Mian Mir, came to him and asked for permission to bury alive his oppressors. The Guru refused" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 253, folio 227). In *A White Trail* Guru Arjan Dev's "Muslim Sufi friend", incensed at the Guru's torture, asks whether he should destroy the oppressors, and "The Guru replied that he should not" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 195, folio not stated).

A damaged sentence in Chishti's survey names Hazrat Mian Mir together with a bhagat whose name, read as Chhajju Bhagat, is itself doubtful in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 244).

## A Thread in Lahore’s Sufi Heritage

Mian Mir’s life also wove him into the deeper Sufi history of Lahore. He is said to have kept a forty-day retreat (chilla) at the khanqah of Data Ganj Bakhsh, Ali Hujwiri — the eleventh-century saint whose *Kashf al-Mahjub* is among the earliest Persian treatises on Sufism, and whose shrine, Data Darbar, is the spiritual heart of the city. In going to sit in devotion at Data Sahib’s threshold, Mian Mir joined his own sanctity to that of Lahore’s patron saint, and later devotion has treasured the link as binding together the two greatest Sufi presences of the city across the span of five centuries.

Kugle, writing of the Qalandars, places him among the great Qadiri saints of the city: "In Lahore today, the Qalandars (who completely abandon legal norms and social conventions and even gender distinctions) are still associated with the great" Qadiri "saints of the city, like Shah Hussayn, Shah Jamal, and Miyan Mir." (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 326, folio 305, ch. 4 n. 7). In Lahori's *Hadiqat* he and Shah Bilawal are the Lahore elders from whom Pir Burhan took Qadiri *fayz* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 270, folio 258).

## Miracles and Folklore

As with every great saint, a rich body of miracle-tales (karāmāt) gathered around Mian Mir, and they were recorded and retold well into the colonial era, when British collectors of Punjabi folklore took them down. Best known is the story of the dove: a hunter’s stone struck down a bird that had been sweetly praising God, and at Mian Mir’s touch it revived and flew away unharmed; when the hunter aimed once more, the stone turned back and wounded his own hand, until, repenting and vowing never again to harm a winged creature, he was healed. Devotees tell, too, of a black snake that came to the saint as he sat by the Ravi, circled him three times, and slipped quietly away; of a mute boy whom he taught to utter “Bismillah,” after which the child could speak; of a handkerchief he gave that healed the sick who used it in faith; and of food that appeared, in answer to his prayer, when there was nothing in the house to set before a guest.

Many such tales turn on his knowledge of hidden things and his power over sickness and death. A man came seeking his blessing for a journey to Khorasan; Mian Mir advised him not to go, for the way, he said, was closed — and so, unseen, it proved. Another came in anguish over a son choking and suffocating; the saint fell into a trance, called for a cup of water, breathed his recitation over it, and by it the child was relieved. To a certain visitor he said simply, “you may depart” — and within a few days the man breathed his last, the words proving true. His disciples cherished their own intimate wonders: Meerak Mohammad Lahori relates how, having taken up and chewed a discarded betel-leaf from the saint’s own mouth, he was carried away into so deep an ecstasy that his breast seemed near to bursting with felicity. Stories such as these, more than any doctrine, are what bound ordinary people to the saint, and they still draw the troubled and the hopeful to his shrine.

The older books assert his wonders more often than they tell them. Kanhaiya Lal, on Dara Shikoh's authority, speaks of thousands and retells none (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 242, folio 245); Chishti writes that his *karamat* are beyond counting, and sets down only a few (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 285).

Two of them tell a wonder worked at the tomb itself. Lahori quotes Shah Muhammad Ghaus's own *Risala-e Ghausia* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 78, folio 66 [marker: 76]). When he reached Lahore in search of the truth, Shah Muhammad Ghaus writes, he lodged for the night at the exalted maqbara of Mian Mir; in a dream Mian Mir Bala Pir appeared to him and, with complete attention, bestowed a *shughl* on him; in the morning he rose and went to Shaikh Hamid Lahori and asked him, too, for some *shughl*; and Shaikh Hamid laughed and said that the very *shughl* bestowed on him that night by Mian Mir Bala Pir was enough for him. Chishti tells the same first night at the exalted maqbara of Hazrat Mian Mir, from the same *Risala-e Ghausia*: the saint came in a dream and gave a *shughl*, that is a *wazifa*, and Shaikh Hamid Lahori, by way of *kashf*, said that it was enough (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 177).

In his sister's entry Lahori tells the one wonder in these books on which the saint himself passes judgement. There were some two *man* of wheat in the household; the Bibi put her hand into it, and for a year it fed the faqirs, the poor, the dervishes of the khanqah and its dependants every day, as though the two *man* had become several hundred (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 254–255, folios 242–243). When the news reached Mian Mir Bala Pir, he said that the display of *karamat* is forbidden, and that God is the giver of sustenance; what need was there to obtain grain in this way? (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 255, folio 243).

Kanhaiya Lal gives a saying of the saint about his khadim Mir Inayatullah, to whom his murshid gave the title Miskin Shah Amri, because no one knew where he got his food. When someone described his state to Mian Mir, he said that this Miskin is Amri: his food comes to him by the *amr*, the command, of God, and he has no need to earn it (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 264, folio 267).

Chishti's story of Shah Gada adds a tale of knowledge at a distance. There Mian Mir Sahib sends Qazi Afzal, the name uncertain in the printed text, for *ziyarat* (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 702); and a Hazrat Shah Mir, possibly but not certainly the same saint, knows from afar what has befallen Qazi Muhammad Fazl, intercedes for him, asking kindness for the Qazi as his innocent servant (the words printed uncertainly), and is handed the Qazi's goods and horse to give away in the way of God (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 702–703).

A modern story, too, is set at the shrine. Robert Rozehnal, from the telling of "another senior male murid" in Bahawalpur on 2 May 2001, records that "Once Captain Wahid Bakhsh Sahab and Hazrat Shahidullah Sahab went to visit the shrine of the saint Miyan Mir in Lahore". A *majzub* there (the book's *majhzub*) asked Shahidullah his hometown; he answered "My hometown is London"; the *majzub* laughed and said "No, no. Your hometown is Medina!", and "repeated this many times" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 72, folio 62).

## Death and the Building of the Shrine

Mian Mir died in 1635, and though he had expressly forbidden any tomb over his grave, a mausoleum of red sandstone was raised at his resting place in Baghbanpura in the years that followed, during the lifetime of Dara Shikoh, who could not bear that his master should lie unmarked. It grew into a dignified monument in the Mughal manner, its chambers finished in fine stone, and around it, in time, were gathered the graves of others of the imperial circle who had loved him — Dara Shikoh himself, executed in 1659, and his wife Nadira Begum among them. Thus the barefoot dervish who owned nothing and had asked for nothing came to rest at the centre of a small city of tombs, his wish overruled by the devotion of a prince.

Kanhaiya Lal tells the building twice. In one passage, when the governorship of the Punjab was entrusted to Dara Shikoh in Shah Jahan's reign, he raised many buildings, first a chowk, then the lofty building of the rauza of his *dada murshid* Mian Mir Bala Pir; and then the rauza of Mullah Shah Qadri, his own murshid, in whose enclosure, as the sentence runs, the village of Mauza Mian Mir now stands, though the village bears Mian Mir's name (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 139, folio 142). In the other, Dara Shikoh gathered much stone and building material and first built his own pir's rauza, within whose walls the village of Mian Mir is now settled (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 242, folio 245). Neither passage dates the rauza. Chishti, too, says that Dara Shikoh built the exalted rauza (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 289), and says again that he built the Hazrat's rauza, the word printed uncertainly (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 291).

Lahori, in his entry for Mullah Shah Badakhshi, writes that Dara Shikoh had the rauza built at a cost of several lakh rupees, and Ranjit Singh had its stones taken down entirely and carried off to Amritsar (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 69, folio 57); his editor gives Kanhaiya Lal's history of Lahore as the authority (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 69, folio 57, note 3). Because the sentence stands in Mullah Shah's entry, its words for his rauza may mean Mullah Shah's rauza rather than Mian Mir's; the wording does not settle whose tomb was stripped.

Dara Shikoh's devotion, Kanhaiya Lal writes, reached further. He meant to build, from the chowk of Dara Shah (the name printed uncertainly) to Mian Mir's maqbara, a road of red stone so clean and pure that he could go and come barefoot every day to pay his *salam*, and that none but the prince should travel on it. The plan was cut short when Alamgir Aurangzeb overcame his father and imprisoned him and had his brother Dara Shikoh killed (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 139, folio 142). Chishti speaks of a red-stone road from the holy rauza to the fort of Lahore, walked barefoot (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 291).

Kanhaiya Lal adds that at the completion of the building much stone was left over, and that Aurangzeb ordered that a Jami Masjid be built from the stone on hand (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 139, folio 142).

Of the prince's household, Rafat's note says that Dara Shikoh's "wife lies buried near his mausoleum" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 240, folio 220). Chishti's register places the tomb of Nadiran Begum, the name printed uncertainly, opposite on the east, and of that tomb says something, in a damaged sentence, about Mian Mir's own lifetime (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 324).

## The Tomb in Kanhaiya Lal's Description

The fullest description of the building is Kanhaiya Lal's, and it is given here as his account; whether all of it belongs to this shrine is not beyond doubt. His table of contents gives the nearest tomb as the maqbara of Mian Munir, Munir rather than Mir, a reading uncertain in the printed text (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 4, folio 3), while the account itself prints Shaikh Muhammad Mir, famous as Mian Mir Bala Pir Lahori (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 242, folio 245); and elsewhere in the book "Mian Mir" is only the name of the cantonment, with no saint, grave, mazar, urs or custodian named there.

In Kanhaiya Lal's measurement the enclosure is *pukhta*, of *chuna-gach*, with *qalbuti* arches in all four walls; its height, a reading uncertain in the printed text, is not less than five *gaz*, and its door is on the south. The rauza stands on a grand *chabutra*, square, nineteen by nineteen *zar'a*, with a marble stair on the south, marble slabs set into its four faces (the word uncertain in the printed text) and stone paving on top. The tomb itself is of marble, a man's height tall; its door panel is of marble and its frame of red stone; inside, a marble floor is inlaid with black stone, *sang-e musa* (the name printed uncertainly); three *qalbuti* arches have stone frames (the word again uncertain), and the door stands in the fourth, the southern one. The interior marble work, he writes, was built in Dara Shikoh's time. Glass-work in a vine pattern and a large mirror adorn the flanks of the northern arch and the ceiling of the dome. The grave stands on a marble *chabutra* a foot high, its *ta'wiz* of marble, on which a cover of costly cloths always lies; and the finial of the dome, the *kalas*, is of marble too (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 243–245, folios 246–248).

The shrine's offices are built into its fabric. On the west of the enclosure a grand *dalan* is built as the seat of the *mujawirs*, and beside it stands a further building with a *bukharcha* (the word uncertain in the printed text) in which is the special seat of the sajjada-nashin (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 243, folio 246). Because there was no well within the walls, a Nur Muhammad, his second name uncertain in the printed text, had one made over the course of fifty years, with a bathhouse beside it (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 243, folio 246).

The mosque, too, was built across two reigns: its stone-faced base was laid in Dara Shikoh's time and the rest was built under Alamgir (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 243, folio 246). The war of succession stopped the work — Shah Jahan imprisoned, Dara Shikoh killed, so that the building stood idle for some years — and only on a petition to Alamgir by a man of the same family, his name, perhaps Mattu Sultan, printed uncertainly, was the building taken up again and finished, though not to the finish Dara Shikoh had intended (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 242–243, folios 245–246). Even the glass-work belongs to a later age: an Englishman in a merchant's service, his name uncertain in the printed text, had it made, and had the outside whitewashed as well (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 245, folio 248).

## The Precinct and Its Graves

Lahori's appendix sites several graves by reference to Mian Mir's. His own stands at Mian Mir, near a railway station whose name is illegible in the printed text; Khwaja Bihari's lies adjoining Mian Mir's rauza, and is found today in the *qasba* of Mian Mir, opposite his maqbara; and Mullah Shah Qadiri's lies outside Mian Mir Bala Pir's mazar, and is found today inside *mauza* Mian Mir (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 287–288, folios 275–276). The locality itself thus bears his name, as the *qasba* or the *mauza* of Mian Mir. In the body of the book, too, Khwaja Bihari's mazar adjoins Mian Mir's rauza (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 66, folio 54), and Mullah Shah's lies in Lahore, outside Mian Mir Bala Pir's mazar; the book continues as a fact of its own day: the village of Mian Mir Abad now stands within the enclosure of his mazar (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 69, folio 57).

Chishti's register of graves places them by the rauza and its walls, opposite Hazrat Mian Mir's rauza and on the western side (the word uncertain) of his enclosure wall (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 325). It names khadims of the shrine, Mullah Jan Muhammad Gujar and Hazrat Ibrahim Rose, so far as the names can be read, with years read as 1048 and 1040 (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 325), and Hafiz Allah Yar and Sain Mir Shah, from among the khadims, with years read as 1265 and 1259 (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 324), every figure uncertain in the printed text. Three of his murids are buried by the rauza: Haji Sulaiman, with a year read as 1031; Shaikh Abu al-Karim, the name printed uncertainly, with 1021; and Shaikh Abu al-Khair, with 1018, every year again uncertain; and the register says of them that all were accomplished murids of Hazrat Mian Mir (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 325). It records a burial ground of the late Muhammad Sharif's kin, its graves of various dates, adjoining the khanqah and tomb of Khwaja Bahar, their names unknown. Of Mullah Shah's garden, against a year read as 1078, it says that Dara Shikoh built this garden and tomb of Mullah Shah, the rest of the sentence, partly illegible, naming Hazrat Muhammad Sharif, the sajjada-nashin; that zamindars now live in the garden; and that the whole village is called Mian Mir (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 325).

## The Chilla in Anarkali

Chishti gives a separate account, headed "House of Mian Mir's Chilla", to a house in Anarkali known as Mian Mir's chilla (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 254), and his table of contents lists, besides it and an account of the saint's house, a heading for the mazars connected with him, its first and last words printed uncertainly (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 2). The house stands in Sadr Bazaar Anarkali, beside a merchant's kothi and shops; its rent goes to those same faqirs; and it has a running well (the word uncertain), an arched way, a structure like a mosque (also uncertain), a *tah khana* that is an old building, trees, and a well (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 254). Here one or two lamps are lit every day, and here, in his lifetime, Hazrat Mian Mir would stay in retreat, so far as the damaged words can be read. And Chishti reports what he learned from the mouth of the saint's sajjada-nashin: that the saint spent nights here, that the house was once a garden, and that his death took place at this very place, the last word printed uncertainly (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 255). Elsewhere he has Qazi Muhammad Afzal, called Fazl Gada, visit the saint at the place in Anarkali where his chilla now is (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 701).

## The Shrine Through the Centuries

The tomb passed through the turbulence of later ages. In the Sikh period much of its valuable stone was carried off under the builders of Maharaja Ranjit Singh, who prized the material, and — by tradition — stone from the wider complex had earlier been drawn upon for imperial building under Aurangzeb; yet through every change of rule the shrine endured as a place of devotion. Under British rule it came under the care of the department of ancient monuments, and in 1985 it was formally declared a protected site by the Punjab Auqaf Department, which administers it to this day together with the shrine’s traditional sajjada-nashins, the hereditary custodians. The mausoleum stands in the Dharampura (Hashimpura) area of Lahore, its dome and gateway a familiar landmark, with an attached mosque and open courtyards to receive the crowds who gather there. Successive generations have repaired and adorned it, so that the austere saint who wished only for a ruined and level grave is honoured, instead, by one of the enduring monuments of the city.

Chishti returns, in other pages, to Ranjit Singh's stripping and to the Maharaja's repentance (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 296–297, 313).

Shemeem Burney Abbas, who visited the shrine in her fieldwork, found there the feature she had seen at Data Darbar, the extension and renovation of the shrine into a mosque with Friday congregational prayer (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 20–21, folios not stated): "I noticed the same feature at the Hazrat Mian Mir" … "shrine in Lahore, which I visited this summer". "The shrine had fallen to pieces almost a decade ago. It has been recently restored and is also used as a mosque" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 21, folio not stated).

## Devotion Today

The shrine of Mian Mir remains a living centre of popular piety. On ordinary days it draws on the order of one to three thousand visitors, who come to recite the Fatiha, tie vows, and seek the saint’s intercession for healing, for children, and for relief from hardship; many speak of the peace they feel within its walls and of prayers answered there. Qawwali is sung on Thursday evenings, the five daily prayers are held in the shrine mosque, and langar — free food — is distributed to visitors, sometimes twice a day. The shrine’s great annual occasion is the urs, held on 7 February, which marks the saint’s union with the Divine; the courtyards then fill with hundreds of thousands of devotees for days of qawwali, prayer, and communal feasting. Across the centuries — under Mughal, Sikh, and British rule, and in the Pakistan of today — the shrine has served as a shared space of devotion, remembered by writers from Dara Shikoh to Khushwant Singh as a place where the boundaries of community grew soft.

Kanhaiya Lal knew a fuller calendar. Five melas were held at the mazar each year: one the annual urs, the word read with uncertainty, when the crowd stayed two days and two nights, and four in the rains, on the four Wednesdays of the month of Sawan. A big mela was held there in the time of the Sikhs too, but in his day the gathering was the more prosperous, because the people of Lahore proper, of the Mian Mir cantonment and of the villages gathered in great numbers on the day of the mela, and much income came from the offerings (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 245, folio 248).

Pilgrims and seekers of many kinds have come to the tomb. Khalid writes of a shrine that "now attracts thousands of devotees in Lahore" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 195, folio not stated). Shah Muhammad Ghaus spent his first night in Lahore there and received his *shughl* in a dream (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 78, folio 66 [marker: 76]; Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 177), and Fazl Shah, as far as a badly damaged line can be read, often went to the mazar (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 336). Kugle finds the Qalandars of Lahore still associated with the saint (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 326, folio 305, ch. 4 n. 7).

## Setting: Mian Mir in the Map of Lahore

The saint's name runs through the topography of Lahore. Lahori places one tomb outside Lahore near Mian Mir's road (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 44, folio 32), and another on the edge of the road that runs from Anarkali to Mian Mir (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 267, folio 255). Chishti, too, speaks of a road running from Lahore to Mian Mir (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 360).

In Kanhaiya Lal's Lahore the name belongs above all to the cantonment. He records that the Anarkali cantonment was given up and the cantonment set in the plain of Mian Mir (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 286–287, folios 289–290); that it was founded in the plain of Mian Mir, for the breadth of its land, with a Sadr Bazaar of Mian Mir, great barracks and countless kothis costing lakhs (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 34, folio 37); and his contents list the church of the Mian Mir cantonment (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 5, folio 4). At none of these places does the book name a saint, a grave or a custodian, and it nowhere says there that the place is named after anyone. He sets other tombs by the cantonment and the road: Miskin Shah Amri's lies on the west side of the Mian Mir cantonment (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 263, folio 266), and Sayyid Jan Muhammad Huzuri's adjoins the Mian Mir road on the west (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 265, folio 268).

Writers on the city at large give the wider setting, and what follows speaks of Lahore, not of the shrine. Kugle calls Lahore "a seasonal capital city and home of the Mughal court during its perennial rotation" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 233, folio 212), and describes "Baghabanpur on the outskirts of Lahore (the neighborhood is now within the expanding city just next to the Shalimar gardens)" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 215, folio 194); he writes of the neighbourhood, not of Mian Mir. Abbas calls the Shalimar Gardens "Gardens built by Shah Jehan, the Mughal emperor" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 188, folio not stated). Chishti's own survey was commissioned: the commissioner ordered him to write a detailed account of the buildings, mazars, tombs and mosques of Lahore's environs (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 28). Of a site at Hashim Pura, in his account of the city's former extent, he writes that Lahore once reached that far (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 298). He records that in Ranjit Singh's time red, black and marble stones were torn down around Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 296), and that when the cantonments of Ranjit Singh's armies were built the bricks of old tombs and houses were pulled out, so that thousands of old tombs, domes, mosques, gardens and havelis that were the memorials of the nobles of the past were lost (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 431).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **His name.** The site's account calls him Hazrat Mian Mir, Mir Mohammad of Sindh. Lahori heads his entry Shaikh Muhammad Mir, famous as Mian Mir Qadiri Lahori (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 60–62, folios 48–50), and his editor gives Mir Muhammad, with the titles Mian Mir and Shah Mir (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 61, folio 49, note). Kanhaiya Lal's account prints Shaikh Muhammad Mir, famous as Mian Mir Bala Pir Lahori (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 242, folio 245), but his table of contents has the maqbara of Mian Munir, with Munir for Mir, a reading uncertain (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 4, folio 3).
- **Where he was born.** The site's account gives Sehwan (Sivastan) in Sindh, between Thatta and Bhakkar. Lahori has Sewistan (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 60, folio 48); Chishti the city of Sistan (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 284); Rafat "Sehwan Sharif in Sind" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 240, folio 220); Schimmel only Sind (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 392, folio 361). Kanhaiya Lal gives no birthplace and calls him Lahori (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 242, folio 245).
- **When he was born.** The site's account says the sixteenth century. Lahori gives 960 on the authority of the *Safīnat al-Auliyā* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 61–62, folios 49–50); his editor, on Dara Shikoh's authority, 957 AH / 1550 CE (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 61, folio 49, note); Chishti nine hundred and fifty-seven of the hijra, the reading uncertain (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 284); Khalid 1550 (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 195, folio not stated).
- **How long he lived.** The site's account says by tradition some eighty-eight years. Lahori says eighty-seven (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 61–62, folios 49–50); Chishti ninety-eight, the reading uncertain (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 284).
- **When he died.** The site's account gives 1635 (1045 AH), "the date held by the Auqaf Department and the great majority of sources, though a few place his death in 1645", and holds the urs on 7 February. Lahori gives 1045 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 61–62, folios 49–50); Kanhaiya Lal 1045, the figure uncertain, in the reign of Shah Jahan, with a chronogram over the tomb door that yields the year (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 242, folio 245; p. 244, folio 247); Rafat, Ernst and Lawrence, and Schimmel's index 1635 (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 240, folio 220; Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 65, folio 51; Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 521); Khalid 1635 (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 195, folio not stated). Chishti gives the Zuhr prayer on 7 Rabi al-Awwal, the year illegible (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 284).
- **Who initiated him.** The site's account has him leave home at about twelve with his widowed mother's blessing and receive initiation at the hands of the master whom the shrine's tradition remembers as Shaikh Siyustani. Lahori has him take *bay'a* in the Qadiri silsila at twelve at his own mother's hand, and only later go, with her permission, to Shaikh Khizr Sistani for its completion (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 61, folio 49). Chishti and Kanhaiya Lal make him murid and khalifa of Shaikh Khizr Sistani (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 284; Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 242, folio 245). Ernst and Lawrence trace the line Dara Shikoh acknowledged to him through 'Abd al-Qadir II and 'Abd Allah Bhiti (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 65, folio 51).
- **His brothers.** Lahori names four more brothers, Mian Qazi, Qazi Usman, Qazi Tahir and Qazi Muhammad (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 62, folio 50); Chishti names Qazi Tawakkul (the name uncertain), Qazi Usman and Qazi Tahir (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 284).
- **His breath.** The site's account has him hold the discipline of the breath from the Isha prayer until dawn. Lahori says that a whole night passed in a single breath (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 61, folio 49); Chishti, that a night passed in one or two breaths, and later in four (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 285).
- **Where he died.** The site's account has him die in the small cell where he had spent some sixty years. Chishti reports from the shrine's sajjada-nashin that his death took place at the Anarkali house known as his chilla, once a garden (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 255).
- **Where he kept his chilla.** The site's account speaks of a forty-day retreat at the khanqah of Data Ganj Bakhsh. Chishti describes a house in Sadr Bazaar Anarkali known as the chilla of Hazrat Mian Mir, where he stayed in retreat in his lifetime (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 254; p. 255; p. 701).
- **Dara Shikoh's relation to him.** The site's account calls the prince his most devoted disciple at court, with Mullah Shah as the prince's spiritual guide. Rafat counts Dara among his disciples (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 240, folio 220); Abbas calls him "a devotee of Hazrat Mian Mir" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 197, folio not stated); Schimmel makes Mian Mir the prince's first mystical guide, to whom Mullah Shah introduced him (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 464, folio 433; p. 392, folio 361). Kanhaiya Lal makes him the prince's *dada murshid*, his murshid's murshid, in one passage (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 139, folio 142) and the prince his grand-khalifa, the word uncertain, in another (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 242, folio 245); Chishti makes Dara a murid of Mullah Shah with a perfect *iradat* toward Mian Mir (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 289, 291); Ernst and Lawrence name Mullah Shah as Dara's "own preceptor" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 65, folio 51).
- **Jahanara's guide.** The site's account makes the princess a disciple of Mullah Shah, reached through Dara Shikoh. Schimmel calls Mian Mir "Jihanara's and Dara's first mystical guide" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 464, folio 433), and Mullah Shah her master (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 394, folio 363).
- **Guru Arjan Dev and the foundation stone.** The site's account calls Mian Mir the Guru's close friend, gives the foundation stone as Sikh tradition, sets out the doubts of Madanjit Kaur and others, and holds that the objection does not really hold. Rafat writes that "Guru Arjun was a follower of the great Qadiriya mystic and had got him to lay the foundation stone of the Hariman-dir temple at Amritsar" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, pp. 41–42, folios 21–22); Khalid, that Mian Meer "laid the foundation" of the Golden Temple (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 167, folio not stated), and that he was "a close associate of Guru Arjan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 200, folio not stated).
- **The offer against the Guru's oppressors.** In *Walking with Nanak* Mian Mir asks "permission to bury alive his oppressors" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 253, folio 227); in *A White Trail* he asks whether he should destroy them (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 195, folio not stated).
- **The order of Dara Shikoh's buildings.** The site's account has the mausoleum raised in the years after 1635, during Dara Shikoh's lifetime. Kanhaiya Lal, in one passage, has the prince, as governor of the Punjab, build first a chowk, then Mian Mir's rauza, then Mullah Shah's (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 139, folio 142); in the other, he first builds his own pir's rauza (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 242, folio 245). Neither passage gives a date.
- **What the tomb is made of.** The site's account calls it a mausoleum of red sandstone. Kanhaiya Lal describes a tomb of marble, its door frame of red stone and its interior marble work built in Dara Shikoh's time (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 243–245, folios 246–248).
- **The red-stone road.** Kanhaiya Lal has Dara Shikoh intend a red-stone road from the chowk of Dara Shah, the name uncertain, to the maqbara, a plan cut short by Aurangzeb's victory (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 139, folio 142). Chishti speaks of a red-stone road from the rauza to the fort of Lahore, walked barefoot (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 291).
- **Whose enclosure holds the village.** Kanhaiya Lal, in one passage, puts the village of Mauza Mian Mir in the enclosure of Mullah Shah Qadri's rauza (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 139, folio 142); in the other, within the walls of the rauza of the prince's own pir (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 242, folio 245). Lahori, in Mullah Shah's entry, puts Mian Mir Abad within the enclosure of his mazar, which in that entry reads as Mullah Shah's (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 69, folio 57). Chishti has zamindars living in Mullah Shah's garden and the whole village called Mian Mir (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 325).
- **The stone and the great mosque.** The site's account gives as tradition that Aurangzeb drew upon stone from the complex for the Badshahi Mosque. Kanhaiya Lal writes that much stone was left over at the completion of the building, and that Aurangzeb ordered a Jami Masjid built from the stone on hand (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 139, folio 142).
- **The Sikh-period stripping.** The site's account says that much of the tomb's valuable stone was carried off under Ranjit Singh's builders. Lahori writes that Ranjit Singh had the stones of his rauza taken down entirely, and carried to Amritsar, in a sentence that stands in Mullah Shah's entry and may mean Mullah Shah's rauza (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 69, folio 57).
- **Where the shrine stands.** The site's account sets it in Dharampura (Hashimpura), once the suburb of Baghbanpura, also recorded as Khanpura. Lahori's appendix places Mian Mir's grave at Mian Mir, near a railway station whose name is illegible, in a locality called the *qasba* or the *mauza* of Mian Mir (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 287–288, folios 275–276); his editor's table puts Bibi Jamal Khatun's grave, in its column for 1875, inside the enclosure of Mian Mir's khanqah, and in its column for 1974 between the Karachi railway line and Miani Sahib village (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 291, folio 279). Kanhaiya Lal's account of the two rauzas gives no quarter for either (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 139, folio 142).
- **The urs.** The site's account holds the urs on 7 February, with hundreds of thousands of devotees and days of qawwali. Kanhaiya Lal records five melas a year: the urs, when the crowd stayed two days and two nights, and four on the Wednesdays of Sawan (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 245, folio 248).
- **The state of the building.** The site's account has the shrine endure through every change of rule, repaired and adorned by successive generations. Abbas writes that "The shrine had fallen to pieces almost a decade ago" and had been recently restored and put to use as a mosque (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 21, folio not stated).
- **Nadira Begum's tomb.** The site's account has the prince and his wife laid to rest close to Mian Mir after the war of succession. Rafat says only that his wife "lies buried near his mausoleum" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 240, folio 220). Chishti's register places her tomb on the east, in a damaged sentence that speaks of Mian Mir's own lifetime (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 324).
- **When Bibi Jamal Khatun died.** Lahori gives 1000 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 255, folio 243); his editor, quoting Dara Shikoh, 27 Rabi al-Awwal 1057 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 255, folio 243, note 1); Chishti's register a year read as 1055 (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 325); Schimmel 1639 (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 464, folio 433).
- **Where Bibi Jamal Khatun lies.** Lahori places her inside a khanqah in Lahore whose name is illegible (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 255, folio 243); his editor's quotation from Dara Shikoh has her living in Sehwan and never leaving it (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 255, folio 243, note 1); the editor's table, in its column for 1875, inside the enclosure of Mian Mir's khanqah (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 291, folio 279); Chishti's register enters her mazar at the shrine, and a second grave, formerly *pakka*, since razed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 325).

## Legacy

Mian Mir stands in memory as the very type of the Sufi ideal: a man who owned nothing and was sought by emperors; who wrote not a single verse and yet was praised in verse by a prince; who, rooted absolutely in his own faith, made his threshold a meeting-place for people of every religion. His disciples shaped the spiritual life of the Mughal court; his friendship with the Sikh Gurus became a legend of tolerance that Punjab still cherishes; his forty-day retreat at Data Darbar bound him to the patron saint of Lahore; and his tomb remains one of the anchors of the city’s sacred landscape. In honouring Mian Mir, Lahore remembers not only a saint of extraordinary austerity and learning, but a vision of holiness generous enough to hold an entire, plural world.

The books carry that memory forward in their own ways. For Lahori he is the root of a Qadiri line that ran on in Lahore through Mullah Shah and his successors (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 221–222, folios 209–210). For Rafat, after Khushwant Singh, he was "extremely popular with people of all religions" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 240, folio 220). For Khalid, his friendship with Guru Arjan Dev lives on in folk tales, and a pilgrims' hostel built at Dera Sahib in 1996 "was named after Mian Meer" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 200, folio not stated).
