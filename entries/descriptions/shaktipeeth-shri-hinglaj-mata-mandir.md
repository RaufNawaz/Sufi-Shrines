## Overview

The Shaktipeeth Shri Hinglaj Mata Mandir is among the most revered and remote Hindu shrines in the world, set within a natural cave in the rugged wilderness of the Makran coast, in the Lasbela district of Balochistan. Cradled today within the dramatic canyons and mud-volcano landscape of Hingol National Park, along the Hingol River, the shrine is dedicated to the goddess Hinglaj Mata, also called *Hingula Devi* and, affectionately, *Nani Mandir*. It is one of the fifty-one *Shakti Peethas* venerated across the subcontinent, and one of only two situated in present-day Pakistan. The annual *Hinglaj Yatra*, one of the largest Hindu pilgrimages in the country, draws tens of thousands of devotees along a demanding desert route, sustaining a tradition of worship that reaches back well over a millennium.

The fullest account of the shrine in the books consulted is Jürgen Schaflechner's *Hinglaj Devi*, a study of this one temple built on "my intensive fieldwork and textual research between 2009 and 2015" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 14, folio not stated). He writes of "the temple of Hinglaj in Balochistan" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 13, folio not stated) and of "the Hingol National Park, in which the shrine is located" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 295, folio not stated), and he calls its goddess Hinglaj Devi, Hinglaj Mata, Mata Hinglaj, the Mother and the Devi (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 64–70, 150, folios not stated). Among Muslims, he records, the site and the Goddess are Bibi Nani, Nani Hinglaj or simply Nani (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 24, 89–91, 108, folios not stated). The English signboard at the site reads 'Mata Mandir' and 'Mata Hinghlaj [sic] Mandir', the *sic* being Schaflechner's own (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 284, folio not stated); in Charles Masson's English of 1842 the place is Hinglátz (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 91, folio not stated), and the Sanskrit texts know it as Hiṅgulā, Hiṅgulāja and Hiṅgulāṭa (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 52, folio not stated). None of the names these books print is exactly the temple's present full title, Shaktipeeth Shri Hinglaj Mata Mandir.

Schaflechner holds that "HINGLAJ DEVI IN Balochistan has come to be one of the most important centers for Hindu spiritual practice in Pakistan over the past three decades" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 29, folio not stated). It is "generally considered the largest active tīrtha sthān in Pakistan" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 279, folio not stated), and he reports the opinion, without making it his own, that "for most people, the shrine is considered the oldest in the entire Hindu tradition, so ancient and important that Hindus even come from India to visit it" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 270, folio not stated). His conclusion speaks of "Pakistan's largest religious minority at its most important place of worship today" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 27, folio not stated).

Older and broader books know Hinglaj chiefly as a mountain and a goal of pilgrims. Annemarie Schimmel calls it "the sacred mountain Hinglaj in Balochistan" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 29, folio 25) and "an ancient Shiva sanctuary" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 159, folio 159), and writes that it is often mentioned as 'the goal' in Sindhi mystical poetry (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 421, folio 390); she names no temple, no goddess and no festival there. Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, in its account of Thatta, gives Hinglaj as seventy kos from Thatta, between north and west, near the salt sea, a place attributed to Durga (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529). Haroon Khalid places Hinglaj "in Baluchistan, about two hundred and fifty kilometres from the coastal city of Karachi", where "a Hindu temple honours an incarnation of Durga" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 32, folio not stated). Michel Boivin reports a Theosophical claim of Parsram that universal wisdom was kept "in Hinglaj to be exact, which is a pilgrimage centre located in the Sindhi-speaking area of Baluchistan" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 50, folio 37).

## The Goddess and the Falling of the Head

The sanctity of Hinglaj is rooted in the pan-Indian legend of the *Shakti Peethas*. According to tradition, the goddess Sati, wife of Shiva, immolated herself in grief and outrage when her father Daksha insulted her husband. The bereaved Shiva, cradling her corpse, began the cosmic dance of destruction, the *tandava*, threatening the universe itself. To end his frenzy, Vishnu released his discus, the *Sudarshana Chakra*, which dismembered Sati's body; wherever a part fell to earth, a *Shakti Peetha* arose, charged with the presence of the goddess. At Hinglaj, tradition holds, it was Sati's head (or, in some accounts, the crown of her head, the *brahmarandhra*) that came to rest, an association that lends the site especial pre-eminence among the Peethas. The presiding goddess is known by names such as Kottari, and her guardian *Bhairava* is named Bhimalochana.

Schaflechner gives the same story as the myth most often heard at the shrine today. The Goddess incarnates as Sati, daughter of king Daksha, and marries Shiva against her father's will; Daksha performs the horse sacrifice, the *aśvamedha*, and invites every god except Shiva; Sati "jumped into the sacrificial fire"; Shiva kills Daksha and dances "the tāṇḍava (Skt.), a frantic dance that threatened to destroy the whole world"; and "the God Vishnu took his discus and cut Sati's body into many parts that fell out of Shiva's arms and scattered all over the subcontinent", each landing place becoming a *śākta pīṭha*, a seat of the Goddess's power (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 55, folio not stated). A variant is told at the shrine as well, "although Vishnu's intervention is sometimes omitted; in this version, the wild motion of Shiva's dance alone caused the body's dismemberment" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 55, folio not stated). The renovation of the temple in recent decades, he argues, puts exactly this narrative in the foreground: "the story of the Goddess Sati's dismembering and the dissemination of her body parts over the South Asian continent" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 325, folio not stated).

Which part of Sati fell at Hinglaj is answered three ways in his book. The Sanskrit *Pīṭhanirṇaya* names the *brahmarandhra*, the "upper part of the head" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 55, folio not stated), which Apte's dictionary glosses as "an aperture on the crown of the head through which the soul is said to escape on its leaving the body" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 98, folio not stated); at the image itself, "one of the globular stones on the eastern side is Sati's skull, which came to rest there after Vishnu cut her body into several pieces" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 229, folio not stated). The Bengali *Caṇḍīmaṅgala* of Mukundarama instead "states that the navel rather than the Devi's brahmarandhra has fallen at Hinglaj" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 230–231, folio not stated), and a minority at the shrine argues "that since the womb (Hin. garbha), in the form of a tunnel into the mountain, is found under the image at Hinglaj, above it can only be a part of her belly" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 57, folio not stated). A third answer came from a pilgrim, who said that at Hinglaj "it is actually her womb (Hin. kokh) that fell to earth. He also added that this is why pilgrims can be reborn at Hinglaj and that he never saw anybody being born from a woman's head" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 260, folio not stated). Schaflechner judges that "it is clear that the imaginary of the fallen skull is the prevailing one" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 231, folio not stated).

The *Pīṭhanirṇaya* supplies "the name of the pīṭha (Hiṅgulā, Hiṅgulāṭa) as well as the names of the residing Goddess there (Koṭṭarī, Koṭṭavī, Koṭṭarīśā), the associated form of Shiva (Bhīmalocana), and most significantly, the part of Sati's body—the brahmarandhra (Skt. 'upper part of the head')—that is said to have fallen there" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 55, folio not stated). Those names, he adds, may point to the shape of the image itself: "The names 'Koṭṭarī,' 'Koṭṭavī,' and 'Koṭṭari' refer to a naked woman in Sanskrit, which again would hint at the possibility of an anthropomorphic figure—that of a naked woman (maybe giving birth) seen in the stone clusters (Skt. piṇḍī) and worshipped as the shrine's sanctum sanctorum" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 98, folio not stated).

The name itself is explained twice. One explanation relates it to "the Sanskrit word 'hiṅgula' (also hiṅguli, hiṅgulu), meaning 'vermillion' or 'cinnabar.'" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 57, folio not stated), and ties it to the Sati story: "It is said that as a pious, married Hindu woman, Sati wore vermillion (Hin. 'siṃdūr') in her hair, and, hence, after the upper part of her head fell in Balochistan, the place became known as Hiṅgula, which eventually was changed to Hinglaj" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 57, folio not stated). A second lead is that "the term hiṅgu (Skt.; Hin. also, hīṃg or hiṃgu) also means asafoetida, a plant used frequently in cooking and found in the desert area around Hinglaj" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 98, folio not stated).

## Hingol and the Second Face

The shrine has its own foundation legend, which Schaflechner paraphrases from three written sources, the works of Abro, Khatri and Chhabria (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 229, folio not stated). "The demon Vichitra had two sons. One of them was called Hingol and the other was named Sindur." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 229, folio not stated). Sindur loots China; its people, worshippers of Shiva, petition the god, who promises that "within three days, Ganesha would come and relieve them from their afflictions", and Ganesha comes and kills the tyrant (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 229–230, folio not stated). Hingol "retreated to a mountain, raised his hands toward the sky, and stood on one toe for thousands of years", and Brahma grants that "he could not be killed in war, by any weapon, or by any human being and also not under the sky nor on the earth" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 229–230, folio not stated). Parvati appears to him as a beautiful woman and is pursued from the Himalaya to the Indus and into Sindhudesh, and "at the place where the shrine is located today, the Goddess absorbed him into her body" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 230, folio not stated).

The naming of the place follows in the Goddess's own words, as the book gives them. Hingol's last wish is: "You should remain here at this very place under my name and fulfill all wishes that people bring to you!" The Goddess answers: "From now on I will be called Hinglaj Devi, and this place should be my place of worship. I will stay here forever with Hingol." And "Since this day, both of their faces are found inside the cave." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 230, folio not stated). Schaflechner places the tale himself: "This story, which is clearly a reference to the legend of Narasimha in the Bhāgavata Purāṇa, however, is rarely heard at the shrine today. Devotees often simply state that the other face is Shiva's or Rama's." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 230, folio not stated). He adds that "no particular myth had, as of the end of my research, fully solidified around this enigmatic part of the statue" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 230, folio not stated).

The temple's own priest told the story of the second face two ways. "In 2009, Gopal Maharaj, the only permanent priest (Hin. pujārī) at the Hinglaj temple, explained that the puzzling second face found in the Goddess's effigy represents the demon Hingol, a local tyrant killed by the Devi in mythological times. Later, in 2012, the priest changed his explanation, insisting instead that the second face is that of a vīr (Hin. hero/army commander) of the Goddess Durga, who killed the demon Mahishasura." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 46, folio not stated). In the second telling "he sets aside the tyrant Hingol, who is a part of the local tradition, and changes him into the demon Mahishasura, a figure of the famous Devī Māhātmaya" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 47, folio not stated), and today "the other round stone is often interpreted as the face of one of Durga's vīrs who helped her to kill the demon Mahishasura" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 229, folio not stated).

## The Goddess of Clans and Peoples

Hinglaj is a clan and caste goddess, a *kul'devī*, for several communities. "members of the Brahmakshatriya, Charan, Kapri, and Lohana communities in particular have produced ritual manuals, mytho-historical narratives, travelogues, video documentations, institutions, and other material that has influenced the course of events at Hinglaj lately" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 58, folio not stated). "Hinglaj is the clan Goddess (Hin. kul'devī) of the Dewanis, a Sindhi Lohana family from Las Bela" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 274, folio not stated), and "Many of these Lasi Lohana communities worship the Goddess Hinglaj as their clan Goddess" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 82, folio not stated). Before the present temple committee, "certain castes and communities, like the Brahmakshatriyas, the Charans, and the Kapris, used to visit the shrine of Hinglaj and worship the Goddess as patron or clan deity. Other local communities living in Sindh and in Balochistan, both Hindu and (Zikri) Muslim, also claim an ancient connection with the desert shrine." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 320, folio not stated).

The Charan bards claim the closest bond. "The Charan in particular claim to have a special bond with Hinglaj in Balochistan, and the pilgrimage to her shrine at some point in one's life is mandatory for many of them" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 69, folio not stated). "Charans often say that when a boy is born into the community, a bell rings at the Hinglaj temple in Balochistan. Furthermore, it is the responsibility of every Charan male to visit the Goddess at Hinglaj before his 60th birthday; otherwise a rock will tumble down into the sacred valley, eventually burying the shrine." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 70, folio not stated). She is their *ādiśakti*, "the primordial energy that has proliferated through their community for many generations" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 172, folio not stated). According to Charan narratives "Hinglaj herself was once a Charani", indeed "the first of countless incarnations of the Goddess in this world" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 70, folio not stated); yet that place in the circle of Charan goddesses "is also contested among Charans today. While some are proud to include Hinglaj into this circle of incarnations… others are of the opinion that Hinglaj is the ādiśakti, the primordial divine power that can only ever partially incarnate into this mundane world." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 71, folio not stated).

Charan writers also make Hinglaj a historical woman, in three accounts Schaflechner keeps side by side. Samaur traces her to the 'gaur'viyā cāraṇ' branch from Thatta, gives "her father's name was Haridas", and reports that the Charan and Udasin renunciants therefore held "that the temple in Thatta was as important as the main shrine in Balochistan" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 71, folio not stated). P. P. Payaka, as reproduced, holds that "the Goddess Hinglaj was once the queen of the Kohala or Kohana area. In this account, Hinglaj is portrayed as a Charani and the leader of the Tumbel Charan, whom she guided from Sindh to the Makran", and that "she remained a virgin all her life and was well versed in the religious texts" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 71, folio not stated). Kamphorst gives "the woman's name was 'Charani Deval'", in the eighth century (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 71, folio not stated).

For the Kapri community, "anyone who emerges from Hinglaj's cave in Balochistan is automatically initiated into the group and can assume the title 'Kapri.'" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 75, folio not stated). Depavat writes that "everyone who comes out of the Goddess's womb (Hin. garbha) also attains the title 'kāpaḍiyā'", and Kamphorst that "In this way all travellers become religious brothers and assume the title Kapadiya" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 75, folio not stated). Among the Brahmakshatriyas, "the Brahmakshatriya branch in particular explains its genealogy through the intervention of the Goddess in Balochistan" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 59, folio not stated). "The shrine of Hinglaj is one of the main religious centers for the Devipujak community of Karachi." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 311, folio not stated), and they "describe Hinglaj as their Hajj. For the community, it is the most important place in Hindu cosmology and ought to be visited by all Hindus once in their lifetime." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 311, folio not stated).

Muslims of the region venerate her too. Present-day Zikris "see Hinglaj as a Balochi 'pīr' (Urd. saint) who is a part of their distinct culture and who has the power to fulfill wishes." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 90, folio not stated), and "consider Hinglaj to be the grave of one of Prophet Mohammad's family members" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 325, folio not stated), without naming the family member. His book explains "how the veneration of Hinglaj as the pīr 'Bibi Nani' within certain Muslim traditions allows her to exert considerable power within modern-day Sindhi nationalist discourses" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 24, folio not stated).

## The Goddess in the Sanskrit Texts

"In the eyes of many Pakistani (and Indian) Hindus, Hinglaj is the oldest and thus the greatest of all śākta pīṭhas (Skt. seats of power) in the world." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 51, folio not stated). Devotees give two grounds. Some say Hinglaj is "a 'xālis sthān' (Hin.), a 'real' pilgrimage place", linked to its being *svayaṃbhū*, self-generated; "Others legitimize this claim by noting that Hinglaj (as Hiṅgulā, Hiṅgulāja, Hiṅgulāṭa) has been mentioned in various Sanskrit sources and thereby, is part of the real deal." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 52, folio not stated). "This mentioning in the Sanskrit corpus serves as 'symbolic capital' for the temple in Balochistan, frequently wielded by devotees in conversations about the shrine's legitimacy." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 52, folio not stated). Hindus and non-Hindus alike, he notes, place its heritage "within the same time frame as that of the Mohenjo-Daro civilization" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 270, folio not stated).

Schaflechner tests that claim text by text. The *Hevajra Tantra*, *Sādhanamāla* and *Kālikā Purāṇa*, "the oldest known texts dealing with these pīṭhas", "do not include Hinglaj in their respective lists of four" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 52, folio not stated). The *Kulārṇava Tantra*, quoting the *Rudrayāmala*, names eighteen *pīṭhas* with 'Hiṅgulā' third on the list (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 52, folio not stated), and is dated before 1000 CE by P. V. Kane, between the eleventh and fifteenth centuries by Brooks and between the thirteenth and fourteenth by White (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 53, folio not stated). Yet "the currently available text of the Rudrayāmala Tantra itself does, in fact, mention only ten such pīṭhas as the principal holy sites of the Goddess. The name 'Hiṅgulā' is not one of them." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 53, folio not stated). The *Kubjikā Tantra* names Hinglaj as the fifth of forty-two *pīṭhas* (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 53, folio not stated). His verdict on this group is that "tantric texts, therefore, prove fairly inaccurate for the attempt to date the occurrence of the name Hinglaj." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 53, folio not stated).

The *Vāmana Purāṇa* may hold the earliest mention: "At VāmP chapter 44, verse 47, it reads that Carcikā later roamed the earth only to eventually settle at 'the best of all places—the Hingulata mountain'" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 53, folio not stated). Agrawala took the line for the mountain range in Balochistan and Hiṅgulāja for the 'Indianization' of a Nani cult at the same place; Schaflechner demurs: "I cannot share Agrawala's conviction about the location of the shrine" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 53–54, folio not stated), and Brighenti notes that the Pali *Kuṇālajātaka* "locates the Hingula mountain in the Himalaya instead of toward the western side of the subcontinent" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 54, folio not stated). Still, "many scholars agree that the VāmP could indeed be of an early origin, thus making this possibly one of the first mentioning of the name Hiṅgulā." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 54, folio not stated).

The *Brahmavaivarta Purāṇa* "mentions Hinglaj (under the name Hiṅgulā), stating that a devotee reaches the end of the rebirth circle by seeing the idol of Durga at Hinglaj on the eighth day after the new moon in the month āśvin (Skt.)" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 54, folio not stated). The verse runs: *Hiṅgulāyāṃ tathā 'ṣṭamyāmiṣe māsi site śubhe/śrīdurgāpratimāṃ dr̥ṣṭvā karoti janmakhaṇḍanam//* (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 98, folio not stated). Sen had translated it as the "pilgrimage of vermillion"; Schaflechner thinks that rendering "inaccurate as the rest of the chapter in the BVP clearly describes the merits of visiting certain holy places" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 98, folio not stated). The general passage on the *pīṭhas* in that Purāṇa is, on Sircar's view, "a later interpolation, inserted not earlier than the fourteenth or fifteenth century" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 54, folio not stated). In the *Devī Bhāgavata Purāṇa*, the list of 108 places in section VII, chapter 30 "does not include the name Hinglaj. Only a later list in section VII, chapter 38, pp. 5–30 identifies Hiṅgulā as one of the śākta tīrthas. Even this list, however, is said to be a medieval interpolation" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 54–55, folio not stated).

The text on which the shrine's primacy rests is the *Pīṭhanirṇaya*: "In the Pīṭhanirṇaya (PN), the number of fifty-one śākta pīṭhas was first associated with the body parts of the Goddess Sati (Sircar 1973, 4). In this list, Hinglaj is mentioned first, and thus the text is most frequently quoted as proof of the primary position of Hinglaj among all the śākta seats." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 55, folio not stated). But "Sircar concludes that the PN must have been composed between the years 1690 and 1720", so "it cannot be definitely proven that claims to Hinglaj's primacy among the śākta pīṭha due to its association with the Goddess's brahmarandhra are older than the given time frame of the PN." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 56, folio not stated). Modern literature on Hinglaj cites the *Tantracūḍāmaṇi* for the fall of the head (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 56, folio not stated), a work Banerji ascribes to Purnananda Giri around 1571; "If these dates are correct, the association of Hinglaj with the Goddess's brahmarandhra could be as late as the sixteenth century." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 56, folio not stated), and "The reference to the site in an older Tantracūḍāmaṇi, often mentioned in today's literature about Hinglaj, cannot be seen as conclusive." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 57, folio not stated). From the image of Hiṅghalāja at Khajuraho, Desai concludes "that the word Hiṅghalāja could not have originated earlier than the eleventh century" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 98, folio not stated).

Schaflechner sums up: "the first mention of the name Hinglaj in the Sanskrit literature can only be estimated to have occurred between the ninth and fifteenth centuries. The association with the Goddess Sati's brahmarandhra, however, and the notion of Hinglaj as the primary pīṭha first appeared in the Pīṭhanirṇaya, which, according to Sircar, dates to sometime between 1690 and 1720" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 57, folio not stated). Elsewhere he writes that "it can only be assumed that the earliest mention of her must have occurred sometime between the tenth and the fifteenth centuries." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 96, folio not stated).

The age of the cult is estimated more boldly by others. Tambs-Lyche thought it might have begun in the fifth century, and "a rough estimate between the fifth and seventh century could be an accurate guess" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 71–72, folios not stated). Schaflechner's own summary is that the cult "was already active between the seventh and ninth centuries, possibly incorporating rituals that might have already existed at the shrine of Hinglaj/Nani in Balochistan. Unfortunately, no definite proof is available." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 74, folio not stated). "Following research on the Charan community, however, Kamphorst estimates that the shrine must have had some religious importance since at least the ninth century." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 96–97, folio not stated). "Such estimates, however, are merely based on oral histories and hence cannot be conclusively verified." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 97, folio not stated).

Hinglaj anchors a wider map of goddesses. "the Goddess Hinglaj is understood by some as the first incarnation, the ādiśakti, and thus the center for many variations of Goddess worship in western India"; "these various local incarnations became anchored to the shrine of Hinglaj and further interpreted in its light" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 74, folio not stated). White pairs Hinglaj with Kamakhya in eastern India as "geographical and mythological mirror[s]" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 211–212, folios not stated). Ibrahim Shah names three *śākta pīṭhas* in Pakistan, and Schaflechner agrees "that Hinglaj is the most frequented of these three" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 97, folio not stated).

## The Cave Temple

Schaflechner's description of the temple is the fullest in the books consulted. "A couple of hundred meters after the Kali Mata shrine, the confining walls of the Hinglaj Mata temple on the southern side of the valley spring into view. The walls are built on a stone platform leading into a natural cave that was apparently carved out of the mountain by a river ages ago. Only the northern and eastern walls are made of stone bricks and attach to the naturally formed hollow producing the foundations of the temple" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 223, folio not stated). "The cave forms a natural arch about 9 meters high at its peak and about 64 meters in length." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 225, folio not stated). "The current stone walls inside the cave's confines support no roof since the overhanging arch of the cave forms a natural shelter from sun and rain." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 225, folio not stated). The priest Gopal Maharaj told a Dawn interviewer in December 2010: "Every temple in the world is constructed with bricks and stones, but if you look at the roof of this place, you will realize that it has naturally generated. No one constructed it." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 200, folio not stated).

The earliest physical description Schaflechner quotes is Masson's, of 1842: "The sacred locality is called Hinglátz. It is understood to be consecrated to Parbatí, the goddess of nature, the universal mother, &c., or Diana, the moon, &c. By Máhommedans, by whom it is alike revered, the shrine is considered as one of Bíbí Nánī, the lady Nání, or the motherly lady." Masson goes on: "There is a small mat or temple at Hinglátz, but the chief attractions appear to be natural objects, as a kand, or reservoir of water; a well, of unfathomable depth, above the mat; and the semblance the mural disposition of the rock presents, in a certain spot, to that of a fortress. There are also said to be figures of the sun and the moon hewn on the rock, in an accessible site." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 91, folio not stated).

How old the enclosure is cannot be settled. A photograph in Marc Aurel Stein's account of 1943 shows "that the basic stone structure had already been built in the mid-twentieth century" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 225, folio not stated), while the earliest colonial sources, Wilford, Hart and Goldsmid, "do not mention any stable stone structure; Hart and Goldsmid instead describe a 'mud edifice'" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 225, folio not stated). Nabi Bux Khan Baloch, travelling in 1961, put the stone building at fifty or sixty years old, and "These accounts suggest that the confining walls must be roughly a century old." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 225, folio not stated). "There is neither a foundation stone nor is any inscription visible today that could help to determine the building's exact age." No one at the temple could date the walls, "Thus, the exact age of the enclosure remains unknown." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 225, folio not stated). He records a tradition he does not vouch for: "I often heard that the walls were built gradually over time with stones from the valley by pilgrims and mendicants. The veracity of this claim, however, cannot be determined." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 225, folio not stated).

The building has been altered within living memory. On the eastern side "a set of stairs constructed around 1998 to replace a simple stone heap" leads to "the inner courtyard of the temple where larger groups can gather for the pūjā or bhajans"; "The HSM demolished the divider between the two rooms around 1998 in order to create a wider space to accommodate the crowds"; "During the HSM's renovations in the late 1990s, the walls were cleaned and the inside of the temple was completely covered with tiles"; and "At the end of the 1990s, an additional room was built opposite the entrance for the residing pujārī, Gopal Maharaj. During the melā, this place is used for storing drinking water." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 225, folio not stated). In the past the inner space was divided into two rooms, "one where music was played and the other where food was stored" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 225, folio not stated). "Older photographs and videotapes show that pilgrims who successfully reached the shrine would write their names and addresses on the walls. Inside the temple, many names were visible written in Gujarati, Nastaliq, English, and Bengali." The renovation "also obliterated many traces of the temple's history, complicating a historical survey." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 225, folio not stated). "While many buildings inside the Hinglaj Valley were financed by the provincial government of Balochistan, according to the HSM, the reconstruction work at the temple was supported mainly by donations from the Hindu community." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 226, folio not stated). The book names no endowment of any kind.

## The Image of the Goddess

The current account of the shrine says that the goddess is worshipped not as a carved idol but as a small mud-stone form daubed with sindoor. Schaflechner measures it. "The image of Hinglaj is located on the far west side of the temple, where the arch of the natural cave inside which the temple is nestled reaches down to meet the ground. Now considered to be the main image of the Goddess, the representation rests on an oblong stone of approximately 1 meter by 1.5 meters sitting on the far west side of a wide platform about 1 meter off the ground. The platform itself is around 5.5 to 7.5 meters wide, but it is irregular instead of rectangular in shape" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 226, folio not stated). The platform "was originally built on the foundations of a small rock or mud hill (Hin. miṭṭī kā ṭīlā), the age of which is uncertain" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 247, folio not stated).

"The Goddess's image seems to rest on an extension of the cave wall, under which there is a small channel", and "It is this alleged connection of the oblong stone with the cave wall that has endowed the image with the characteristic of svayaṃbhū" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 227, folio not stated): the image is held to have arisen of itself. "Inside the temple, the sanctum, an oblong stone with some raised mounds atop it, along with a small rift in the mountain wall called the garbha (Hin. womb), are also considered to be svayaṃbhū." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 200, folio not stated). The whole cluster can be read as "an anthropomorphic figure lying flat with its head toward the east and its legs toward the west", formed of "several connected stones or piṃḍ (Hin. lump, a ball, stones that are worshipped as images of the Goddess)" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 231, folio not stated). Two round stones now carry faces: "Representations of eyes and noses have also been attached to the two round stones today to bestow the rocks with human faces. Over the course of time, pilgrims have also brought women's ornaments, crowns, and umbrellas to decorate the image." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 229, folio not stated). "In recent decades, these stones have been covered with layer after layer of vermilion paste (Hin. siṃdūr)" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 232, folio not stated), and "the figure's alleged body parts from the head downward are always covered with red and orange cloth, and thus its full shape is no longer visible" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 232, folio not stated).

"The single most important change made to the Goddess's image was certainly the tiling of the oblong stone, which led to the subsequent backgrounding of the visual impression of a Muslim gravestone." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 261, folio not stated). "While Hart, Goldsmid, and Stein describe the sanctum as a rectangular stone resembling the form of a Muslim grave with some 'diminutive domes' (Goldsmid 1863, 208), on top, the tiling of the oblong stone shifted the attention from the structure as a whole to the stone heaps on top, now emphasized as comprising the Goddess's image." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 261, folio not stated). Stein had recorded the Muslim reading of the same stone: "Muhammadan pilgrims who also visit Hinglaj take the stone block, which a carved railing bearing various sacred emblems surrounds, as marking the grave of a saintly wife of" Ali, "miraculously translated to this spot" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 235, folio not stated). Before the present committee there were thus "two possible modes of interpreting the image": "one interpretation sees the site as the gravestone of a saintly figure in Islam (interchangeably the grandmother, nanny, or wife of Ali), and the other interpretation sees it as the (lying) representation of the Hindu Devi or the Goddess Nani" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 235, folio not stated).

Two inscriptions have been read on the image, and they are not the same. A Hindi inscription on the back of the oblong stone is now partly buried "under concrete and tiles. Today, only the name of the donor, who apparently helped construct the image's foundation, is visible." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 227, folio not stated); it "must have appeared between 1943 and 1961 when Khan Baloch's party visited the shrine" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 227, folio not stated). A second, different inscription, transliterated by Ishtiaq Ansari, "must have been produced after Khan Baloch's visit in 1961" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 227, folio not stated); it read: "I have taken refuge in the fold of the Mother Shri Hinglaj. You keep my sins safe, my [second] birth was successful." The text now legible reads: "Following the order of Shri Matavala Baba, Jagat Janani ordered to build this lion's throne for the Mother Hinglaj." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 264, folio not stated). Khan Baloch also found the inscription "directly in front of two engraved footsteps of a holy man", and "the two footprints he mentions are still clearly visible directly in front of the writing" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 227, folio not stated). "an archeological survey of the site would be necessary to uncover the exact age of the structure around the image" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 227, folio not stated).

"The platform holding the oblong stone structure and the image of Hinglaj separates the inside of the temple into two parts: the western, elevated part is mostly used for pūjā and ār'tī, and the lower, eastern part is for housing bigger groups of pilgrims during the festivals." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 228, folio not stated). "For a long time, this dhūnī or yajña kuṃḍ (Hin.), into which ritual offerings to the Goddess are given, was in the center of the oblong stone until the 1990s when it was shifted to the front of the image." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 233, folio not stated), and "Recently, a large yajña kuṃḍ has been built in the yard in front of the temple." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 233, folio not stated). A conical stone that Hart's companions called 'Sadasheo', a *śiva liṅga*, is gone: "This liṅga was removed by the HSM in the 1990s and thrown into the Sharan Kund (Hin. refuge-pond), which, before it dried up, was directly in front of the Hinglaj temple." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 238, folio not stated).

## The Womb of the Goddess

Beneath the image runs the *garbha*, the womb. Two doors "each around 90 centimeters high" lead into the tunnel and "form the entrance and exit to the Goddess's garbha (Hin. womb) mentioned above, a U-shaped tunnel forming a semicircle underneath the image and opening up in the temple yard" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 228, folio not stated). "At its tallest, this tunnel is no more than 90 centimeters high, and at certain points, it is barely large enough for a person to fit through." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 247, folio not stated). Visitors "must ask the pujārī or the volunteer workers at the shrine" to open its doors (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 248, folio not stated).

Its fabric is partly natural and partly built. "one can clearly distinguish between the manmade work—however old it might be—and the natural cleavage in the stone of the mountain walls"; one wall is "a stone and brick wall that, according to the resident pujārī, was built to stabilize the mud heap"; and "This crevice in the rock appears to be the oldest part of the tunnel, which is located directly underneath, or very close to the oblong stone on which the image of the Goddess rests" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 249, folio not stated). Goldsmid already found a semicircular tunnel, so "the 'U' shape of the tunnel can be dated to at least the mid-nineteenth century" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 250, folio not stated). "In the middle of the twentieth century, the allegedly natural tunnel looping beneath the image of the Goddess had already been supported and stabilized with man-made means." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 251, folio not stated). "From videotapes made around the end of the 1980s, one can see that the HSM later extended the tunnel on both sides to create the full 'U' shape under the platform." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 251, folio not stated). "No member of the HSM I spoke with knew the age of the masonry or of the construction around the tunnel, and some were reluctant to talk about the organization's involvement in the process." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 248, folio not stated). Schaflechner crawled through it "over a dozen times" but promised not to publish his photographs (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 265, folio not stated).

The passage is the heart of the pilgrimage. "These narratives connect to the central ritual practice at the shrine in which pilgrims crawl through a small tunnel, signifying the womb of the Goddess from which they are born again. Before a pilgrim is permitted to enter this tunnel, he or she has to follow a rigid pattern of purification and worship." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 57, folio not stated). It is "one of the oldest practices at the shrine and, in fact, the climax of the journey now as well as then", "the rebirthing ritual in which the devotee enters a tunnel underneath the image of the Goddess" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 321, folio not stated). The passage "becomes a performative act of rebirth out of the womb of the Goddess, sometimes even followed with mimicry of cries and gestures of newborn babies" and "has the power to cleanse Hinglaj's followers of all their prior sins" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 321, folio not stated); "every visitor to Hinglaj is reborn from the womb of the Goddess and thus gains the ātmā of a newborn child" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 178, folio not stated). Pilgrims enter it with a chosen partner: at Kanda Devi they choose "their śaraṇ bhāī/bahan—the partner with whom they will enter the Mother's garbha" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 159, folio not stated).

Two novels, one Bengali and one Hindi, describe the rite. In Abadhut's Bengali novel, "Early the next morning, the pilgrims proceed to the shrine of Hinglaj, where they bathe and put on fresh clothes to enter the womb of the Mother", and at the shrine "they find only a small altar (bedī) on which a lamp is burning" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 131, folio not stated). As leader of the group Abadhut enters first; "After entering the tunnel, he has a vision of the Goddess who laughingly gazes at him, and only a few minutes later, he exits the passage on the other side." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 131–132, folios not stated). The Aghori Baba "advises him never in his whole life to speak of what he has seen in the tunnel" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 132, folio not stated). In Devadatta Shastri's Hindi novel, the sanctum is "a cave around fifty feet high containing a vedī (Hin. a place for sacrifices). On the vedī a lamp is burning."; the pilgrims "crawl on all fours through a small door on one side of the vedī leading to a tunnel into the mountain. The semicircular tunnel leads to another door on the other side of the platform." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 142, folio not stated). They were forbidden "to cross the stream toward the cave during the day" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 142, folio not stated); when they emerged, "Aghori Baba, who had followed them, garlands him with a mālā from the vedī, and the ritual is complete" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 142, folio not stated).

## The Valley and Its Stations

The temple stands in a valley of springs, ponds and caves. "Today, the point at which the Hinglaj Valley begins is clearly marked by a wall built by the HSM in 1996." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 217, folio not stated). Schaflechner names "the Goddess's tunnel (Hin. garbha)" and "one of the pilgrim shelters" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 180, folio not stated), "Anil Kund, a pond atop one of the hill chains in Chaurasi" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 180–181, folio not stated), Til Kund (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 181, folio not stated), "the River Aghor" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 173, folio not stated), a Brahma Kund "named after Brahma" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 255, folio not stated), Kali Kund and Kir Kund (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 254–255, folio not stated), and "the Kali Mata temple, the main location for the bali" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 296, folio not stated). Until his death in the late 1990s the caretaker Dost Mohammad "guided the pilgrims through this steep and challenging landscape, explaining the various sites along the way" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 255, folio not stated).

Sharan Kund "was once a pool lying directly in front of the temple of Hinglaj", "the last place for a purification bath before the pilgrims entered into the garbha of the Goddess" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 251, folio not stated). "Devotees would submerge three times under the fresh water together with their chosen śaraṇ'bhāi/bahan (Barhath 2006, 31) before they would be permitted to enter the Goddess's abode." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 251, folio not stated). Other writers call it 'Kanyar Kun' and 'Hinglaj Kund' (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 264, folio not stated). "while in 2009 it was still obvious that a large pool had once been at this place, by 2011, the area was covered over with concrete, making evident the HSM's lack of any intention to dig it up again" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 251, folio not stated).

New shrines rise in the valley year by year. "Some years ago, Gujarati Hindus from Karachi began constructing a small booth for Melari (Meladi) Mata in the heart of the valley. By 2013, that booth had become a small concrete temple, tiled in a manner similar to Hinglaj and housing a statue of the Goddess about one and a half meters tall." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 271, folio not stated). "Around the beginning of the millennium, another group of visitors had a vision of Momai Mata a short distance up the mountains on the way to Chaurasi, where they later constructed a wooden edifice that has been expanded over the years." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 271, folio not stated). "almost every year, pilgrims make additions or revisions to the different temples, statues, and ritual places" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 271, folio not stated). In 2012, when the front courtyard was being cemented, "workers found a new cave toward the east of the temple that had been buried under the sand" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 265, folio not stated). Hart was told of a place called 'sath durwazu', "which contains the private rooms of the Goddess. To spit on or defile this area is considered a serious transgression" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 263, folio not stated).

The crowds have changed the valley itself. A visitor told Schaflechner: "Before the Hingol River was flowing, but now it stopped. The water at Ashapura, the water at Kali Mata . . . everything dried out due to the crowds." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 188, folio not stated). In his own words, "the crowds are also responsible for major ecological changes inside the valley. The masses hinder the 'real' pilgrims from their spiritual experience and are even responsible for the drying up of sacred ponds." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 189, folio not stated).

## Custodians and the Temple Committee

Before 1986 the shrine was in Zikri hands. "The Zikris, in fact, were in charge of the shrine for many years, until in 1986 when the organization was taken over by the HSM, the current Hindu temple committee. Before the 1980s, Dost Mohammad was the main caretaker of Hinglaj, and his descendants still work today for the HSM as cooks, guides, and drivers." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 90, folio not stated). "The family of Dost Mohammad, who had been the caretakers of the shrine for many generations, could not recall a time when a female was in charge at Hinglaj." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 115, folio not stated). In 2012 the tour to Chaurasi "was still a family tradition and was guided by his son, Hussain" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 265, folio not stated).

"The Hinglaj Sheva Mandali (Urd. Hinglāj Śevā Manḍalī; in the following also HSM) was established in order to promote an annual pilgrimage to the shrine. It was created on January 5, 1986, in Bela (Las Bela district), Balochistan, and continues to be the main organization serving the temple and its pilgrims" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 82, folio not stated). "The establishment of an annual gathering at Hinglaj was the idea of one person, Vasrup Chand Tulsi, who helped found the HSM for the express purpose of publicizing and organizing the pilgrimage" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 83, folio not stated). The committee "has since its inception consisted mainly of Lohanas from the district of Las Bela" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 82, folio not stated), and "The Las Bela Lohana community, which forms the majority of the Hinglaj temple committee, the Hinglaj Sheva Mandali, exerts special influence on the narratives and practices at the shrine today." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 80, folio not stated).

"As the main organizational body in charge, the HSM has for many years been overseeing the renovation of temples, the building of bridges, and the construction of community places inside the valley." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 324, folio not stated). "In recent years, the HSM gradually improved the infrastructure around the temple by constructing shelters, water tanks, and roads in and near the valley. Such projects were made possible by financial support from private national and international Hindu donors together with grants from the Balochistan government." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 323, folio not stated). "Their ability to disseminate information inside and outside the valley, links with international devotees, and connections in the provincial and the federal government together have endowed the temple committee with the power to perpetuate its own version of the shrine's history and rituals." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 324, folio not stated). One associate put it this way: "all arrive, but it is a difference of the class one is traveling in." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 324, folio not stated).

The temple has one resident priest. "In 2009, Gopal Maharaj, the only permanent priest (Hin. pujārī) at the Hinglaj temple" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 46, folio not stated) "gained his position as a pujārī through appointment by the local temple committee (the HSM) in 2008" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 46–47, folio not stated). "Since Maharaj is the only permanent pujārī at the shrine and thus an authority for many visitors, his words and his interpretation of the shrine's mythology have a strong impact on views held by the pilgrims." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 47, folio not stated). Before Hinglaj he served elsewhere, in his own words "the pujārī at the Makli temple in Thatta" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 253, folio not stated).

The state has shaped the shrine three times over. The valley "undoubtedly gained prominence from the construction of the MCH", the Makran Coastal Highway (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 260–261, folio not stated). A dam planned on the Hingol would have flooded the roads to the shrine; the committee argued that "these sites were not like common temples and could not simply be relocated", and its spokesman said, "This is a site of pilgrimage which is place specific." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 217, folio not stated). And "The building of a bridge over the River Hingol also modified the spatial practices and definitively influenced the choosing of a date for the main melā in the Hindu calendar." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 262, folio not stated).

## Pilgrimage and the Yatra

The *Hinglaj Yatra* is a pilgrimage of both physical hardship and profound devotion. Historically pilgrims crossed harsh desert terrain, and the journey retains its arduous, penitential character. The main pilgrimage now takes place over several days in April. Along the route and at the shrine, devotees perform a sequence of rituals at a constellation of sacred spots: they climb the *Chandragup* and Khandewari mud volcanoes, casting coconuts and offerings into the bubbling craters and voicing their prayers and vows; they bathe in the waters of the Hingol River; and they honour subsidiary sites such as Ganesh Deva, the *Brahma Kund*, and Guru Gorakh Nath's *dhuni*. Only after these observances do pilgrims approach the cave sanctuary itself, where the goddess is worshipped not as a carved idol but as a small mud-stone form daubed with sindoor, in keeping with the shrine's ancient, aniconic character.

The order of these stations binds. "many accounts even say that it is not possible to enter the Goddess's valley without having made offerings there" at Chandrakup, as Schaflechner spells it (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 210, folio not stated), and "many pilgrims today believe that Hinglaj's temple may only be entered after visiting Chandrakup and making proper offerings" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 212, folio not stated). In a list of 1994, the temple is the place "where the yagya", the *havan* and the *darśan* of Hinglaj are performed (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 203, folio not stated). Formerly, after the sacrifice to Kali, "pilgrims were not allowed to proceed immediately to the Hinglaj shrine, located just a few hundred meters away. Instead, they were required to return to Ashapura paṛāv, where the group would wait until nightfall; only then could they proceed to Hinglaj" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 222, folio not stated).

How far back the pilgrimage goes is uncertain. "From the sources available, it is evident that an elaborate pilgrimage system to the shrine has existed at least since the fourteenth century." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 145, folio not stated). "Although the precise age of the shrine will remain unknown until proper archeological research is conducted at the site, it is safe to say that Hinglaj has attracted pilgrims from all over the subcontinent for several centuries." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 266, folio not stated). He lists the journeys the sources record: Hamir Gohil "in the year 1301 CE" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 113, folio not stated); "an aristocrat from Rajasthan, Raja Bij Singh, went to Hinglaj in the year 1499 CE" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 112, folio not stated); Raja Todarmal and Raja Bharimal in 1508 CE according to Chhabria, or arriving at Thatta in 1558 CE according to Ansari, "Both authors apparently refer to the same group of travelers but give different dates for their arrival." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 112, folio not stated); and "the journeys of Raja Madhu Singh (in 1792) and Raja Jagat Singh (in 1801)" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 113, folio not stated). Inscriptions in Thatta, on the eastern route, "suggest that a network of ritual journeys has been in place since the fourteenth century", though such references "leave significant gaps, which make it impossible to conclusively speak about a continuous transmission of practices before the middle of the nineteenth century" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 320, folio not stated).

Bhandari, writing in Aurangzeb's reign, stresses the hardship. He writes of the difficulty of the desert road, the scarcity of water and the robbery of the Bhil, which make the place hard for anyone to reach; only some faqirs, naked from head to foot (a word of the phrase is uncertainly read), enduring hunger and thirst, reach it and perform worship there; and the journey takes more than fifteen days, with great hardship (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529).

Schimmel's pilgrims are Hindu ascetics on foot. Shah Abdul Latif's Sur Khéhori[OCR?], which may read Khahori, "is written in praise of the Yogis who walk from the village of Ganji Takar near present-day Hyderabad to the sacred mountain Hinglaj (an ancient Shiva sanctuary in Balochistan) and suffer every possible discomfort" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 159, folio 159). Another route is told in Sur Ramakali: "Latif tells that the cara-van of the Swamis has come to Qalat (23), and from there takes the way to Hinglaj (28 ff)" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 221, folio 221). The pilgrims are called "Sami (swdmi[OCR?] [swami]), Weéragi[OCR?], Kapari, but also Nang, 'naked', or simply Babu" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 220, folio 220), marked by "'slit ears' and matted hairs" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 220, folio 220), and resemble the Nath Yogis of Kabir's poetry, "though worshippers of Shiva", who "appear as the true monotheists" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 220, folio 220). Shah Abdul Latif himself, Schimmel writes, "spent three years with a group of yogis, wandering through the country, up to the sacred mountain Hinglaj in Balochistan" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 29, folio 25). Boivin, writing of Shah Abdul Latif and the Nathpanthi jogis, says: "He probably travelled with them on a number of Hindu pilgrimages, including Dwarka and Hinglaj." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 92, folio 79). And Harish Dhillon's retelling of the Janamsakhis brings Guru Nanak here: from Thatta he "went west to Hinglaj", where he met "a group of pilgrims who had embarked on the Haj" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 194, folio 214).

In 1946, in Abadhut's novel, the gathering place was "the Nagnath akhāṛā in Karachi", from which "members of the local Nath community guide pilgrims once or twice a year to the shrine of Hinglaj Mata" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 127, folio not stated). "People from all parts of India—such as Gujarat, Rajasthan, or Bengal—meet there to await the beginning of the journey that will carry them into the desert of Balochistan. After a month, enough people have gathered to comprise a pilgrimage party, and cameleers are arranged from the area around the Hinglaj shrine to guide the group" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 127, folio not stated). The length of the journey is reckoned very differently: "According to Briggs, the journey took twenty-four days (Briggs 1938, 107); however, according to Hotchand, it took two and a half months (Hotchand 1982, 48); and Jethamalani goes as far as to say that the whole trip took six months" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 117, folio not stated). Writers count the stops differently too: thirteen stops and twenty-five places of worship in Chhabria, thirteen and twenty-four in Jareca, eighteen stops and twenty-four places of *pūjā* in Kshatriya (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 263, folio not stated). "Notwithstanding the fact that there existed a sea route to Hinglaj, not much has been passed down concerning this mode of transportation." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 146, folio not stated).

The danger was real. "For centuries, the shrine of Hinglaj was famous for being extremely difficult to reach. Its location in the arid desert of Balochistan, far away from any urban settlements, made the journey exceptionally demanding. Many reports document the deaths of pilgrims either coming to or returning from the shrine." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 29, folio not stated). "as little as thirty years ago, only a handful of people actually knew the exact location of the shrine" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 82, folio not stated); members of Karachi's 4x4 Off-Road Club "only found the shrine after a few years of searching" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 100, folio not stated). A purity mark once burnt onto the forearm is still sought by a few, "However, due to hygiene concerns, they did not have it burnt onto their forearm but asked instead for the mark of a cold stamp." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 117, folio not stated).

Those who came home earned a title. "The phrase 'this is our hajj' ('yah hajj hai hamārā') is by far the most common answer to the question about why people have the incentive to travel (whether they drive or walk) to the shrine." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 155, folio not stated). "the epithet 'Hinglaji,' equivalent to the title 'Hajji,' is bestowed upon people who have successfully accomplished the journey. Today, however, this title is mainly found in India" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 155, folio not stated). "In Pakistan, the term yātrā is used mostly by Hindu pilgrims. The Urdu equivalent, ziyārat, is rarely applied in conjunction with Hinglaj." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 146, folio not stated). One devotee set a hard measure: "Of 1,000 Pakistanis who go to Hinglaj only 50 do it properly. They are the only Hinglajis [here]." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 171, folio not stated).

## The Highway and the Festival

The road changed everything. "Before the construction of the Makran Coastal Highway (MCH)—the highway that coincidentally linked Hinglaj to the outside world—the journey to the Goddess's remote abode in the desert of Balochistan frequently presented a lethally dangerous undertaking for her devotees" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 25, folio not stated). "The temple's location, although far from any urban environment, was now comfortably reachable by car, and as a result, after 2001, the site quickly developed into a stage for one of the biggest annual Hindu festivals in Pakistan." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 30, folio not stated). The road "unified the different routes into the valley, causing many pilgrimage stops to slip into disuse" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 322, folio not stated), yet it also brought "not only the modernization of transportation to the shrine, but in contrast also a renaissance of the walking practice to Hinglaj" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 26, folio not stated). In just three decades, he writes, the *yātrā* "evolved from a highly strenuous bodily experience and a once-in-a-lifetime undertaking to an annual sedentary festival that now represents an established event within the Pakistani-Hindu calendar" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 323, folio not stated). Before that, "there appears to have been a period of stagnancy from around the mid-1960s until the mid-1980s—a time when the shrine was scarcely visited." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 152, folio not stated).

The main festival falls in spring: "most pilgrimage groups choose to set out in April, just before the big melā (Hin. festival) that falls in the month of phālgun or caitra in the Hindu calendar" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 154, folio not stated). "In recent years, the melā has officially begun with the visit to Chandrakup on the first day and ended with a collective havan (Hin. ceremony), in front of the Hinglaj temple on the third day" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 281, folio not stated). Schaflechner counts four main festivals at the shrine: the two *ʿīds*, Muharram and the spring *melā*, while "Festivals like Holi or Diwali do not play any significant role since they are not public holidays in Pakistan." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 277, folio not stated). Haroon Khalid describes a poster at Bahawalnagar calling pilgrims to the annual pilgrimage, and writes: "Thousands of Hindu pilgrims go there every year in October, making it one of the largest Hindu festivals in the country" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 32, folio not stated).

The crowds come from every class: "The Kohlis, together with other scheduled castes, such as the Meghvar or the Bhil, form the biggest part of the visitors at the Hinglaj festival, which also reflects the overall population of Hindus in Sindh, and more than 90 percent originate from these communities." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 154, folio not stated). Henry Pottinger put the annual pilgrims early in the nineteenth century in the thousands; Captain Hart "estimated the number of pilgrims per year to be between five and six thousand" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 113, folio not stated); Seth Hotchand writes that in 1805–1806 members of his family travelled there "along with 2,000 other pilgrims" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 113–114, folio not stated). Now "Within just a couple of decades, the local environment has had to adapt to soaring numbers of visitors—from merely a few thousand a year to over forty thousand on a single weekend in the spring." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 261, folio not stated); in April 2010 "over forty thousand people visited the shrine", according to the committee's ticket sales (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 84, folio not stated).

The festival is run at a cost. "the entry fee of 250 rupees (in 2012) in particular provoked resentment and protest among visitors, ultimately prompting a response by the HSM to abolish the ticket system in 2013" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 290, folio not stated). Paying pilgrims wore an ochre plastic wristband, the *bhag'vā paṭṭī*, introduced "in 2008" according to one passage (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 219, folio not stated), and "the HSM engaged many young men who worked shifts during the day and night checking these wristbands to ensure that no one without it snuck by" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 290–291, folio not stated). "During the 2012 melā, every pilgrim had to pay an additional 100 rupees at a checkpoint when approaching Chandrakup, one of the main stops on the way to Hinglaj. This money, however, was to be paid to the police and not to the HSM." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 291, folio not stated). The *bhanḍārā*, the community kitchen, "feeds—according to the HSM—around forty thousand people on the main days of the April festival" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 291, folio not stated), water is trucked in from the Hingol because the stream in the valley "does not carry enough water for the huge crowds", and generators give the festival its electricity (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 291, folio not stated).

The festival is also a stage. "During these festivals, many Hindu welfare organizations are also active inside the valley, advertising their philanthropic work for the community." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 313, folio not stated). A 1987 announcement invited the whole Hindu community of the country, 'mulk bhar kī hindū barādarī', to the *yātrā* (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 100, folio not stated). Rites of passage are performed here: the *upanayana saṃskāra* and the *guru dakṣiṇā* "are traditionally performed in and around Hinglaj" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 275, folio not stated), and for Lasi Lohana communities the sacred thread "is bestowed on the young at the River Hingol" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 82, folio not stated).

For devotees in India, the border is the obstacle. "While Hinglaj devotees in Pakistan today can easily experience her shrine, due to the challenges and costs of obtaining a visa, her worshippers in India must often still rely on texts, videos, or the accounts of the few who have had the chance to cross the border for the ritual journey." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 26, folio not stated). "There are many such proxy shrines for Hinglaj in India. Some of them were established long before the separation of the subcontinent, and others were the result of the rigid visa policy put in place afterward" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 167, folio not stated). In 2010 Schaflechner travelled with a party from Gujarat led by Dayaram Kapri; television crews interviewed Dayaram, the organiser Virsimal Dewani and the priest Gopal Maharaj, and "When the men, one after the other, were asked during the interviews what the temple's main sanctum sanctorum shows, all three gave conflicting answers." One said the womb of the Goddess, another the upper part of her head, the third "the face of the Goddess Durga together with her main army officer" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 48, folio not stated). Dewani turned to the author: "You come from a university, you will find out what the image really stands for!" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 48, folio not stated).

Books have shaped the pilgrimage as much as roads. The Bengali novel *Marutīrtha Hiṃlāj*, The Desert Pilgrimage to Hinglaj, published in 1957 by Abadhut, whom the book identifies as Dulalchandra Mukhopadhyay, "together with its 1959 movie adaptation helped to make the journey known to a broader audience." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 320, folio not stated). "The 1959 film, in particular, cast Hinglaj into popular culture and helped foster the pilgrimage's extremely arduous image." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 126, folio not stated). "In the 1980s and 1990s, only two books about Hinglaj were published. In the decade after 2001, five works about the Goddess were published, three of them in 2001, the same year as the completion of the first part of the MCH." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 101, folio not stated).

## Sacrifice and Its Ban

"Today, the Goddess Hinglaj is generally understood as a vegetarian deity who only accepts non-animal offerings from her worshippers." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 285, folio not stated). Yet "Some colonial sources, however, also show that until recently, it was customary for many people to eat the sacrificed animals at the Kali shrine located just some meters before the temple of Hinglaj", and "Some Sindhi Hindu authors, however, claim this to be a tradition alien to Hinglaj, brought to the shrine by foreigners" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 285, folio not stated). "After 2001, when the shrine rose in importance as a unifying signifier for the entire Pakistani-Hindu community, the critical role of vegetarianism became increasingly emphasized at Hinglaj by the HSM." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 285, folio not stated).

"The HSM officially banned animal sacrifice inside the valley in 2004" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 294, folio not stated). Schaflechner argues that the ban began in practical difficulties and only later took on religious meaning: "Only after the ban was put into effect was the prohibition increasingly reinterpreted as a" religious decision "and became an important part of the tradition." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 47, folio not stated); "Functional yet ultimately contingent events prompted the ban inside the valley. But once the temple committee adopted and enforced the prohibition, this debarment was retroactively established as a typical (read: natural) characteristic of worship at the shrine, clashing with other interpretations of the practice, such as the Devipujak-Vagri's cosmology." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 325, folio not stated). A substitute has appeared, "a mere symbolic offering of a goat to Kali by cutting into the animal's ear, allowing a few drops of blood to spill, and then setting it free inside the valley" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 311–312, folio not stated). But sacrifice did not simply stop: a rooster sacrifice was witnessed at the Kali Mata temple in 2010 and in later years (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 294, folio not stated), and "members of the HSM secretly continued sacrificing goats in the mountains for the Goddess Kali" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 296, folio not stated). A visitor justified the ban otherwise: "Well, this is a nature reservoir, it is not allowed to kill animals here." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 295, folio not stated).

For the Devipujak-Vagris of Karachi the offering is a duty. "At every visit, however, each member of the Devipujak community is obliged to give" a rooster or a male goat "to the Goddess Kali, or else risk her wrath. For the Vagris, Kali is the guard" of the Goddess Hinglaj, "and thus they must appease Kali before making their way on to Hinglaj. For the Devipujaks, without Kali's blessing, the whole" *darśan* "at Hinglaj is in vain." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 311, folio not stated). They hold that "every time a festival is organized at Hinglaj and the Mother is deterred from her sacrifice, she causes accidents and disease among the pilgrims, thereby taking her own sacrifice" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 294, folio not stated); a Devipujak said in 2012, "Since the Hinglaj Sheva Mandali has prohibited animal sacrifice, every year there are some accidents... In one way or another, the Mother takes her gift" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 312, folio not stated). They trace the taste for blood to the Goddess's battle with demons, a story that "closely parallels the story of the Goddess's struggle with the demon Raktabija" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 310, folio not stated). One of them, Bhao Tulsi, went "exactly once in his life, in the mid-1970s", holding that "the journey to Hinglaj should only be made once in the lifetime", while the young men of his community "have been to Hinglaj, and most have been several times" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 311, folio not stated).

## Legends and Miracles

A second cycle of legend links Hinglaj to the epic hero Rama. After slaying the demon-king Ravana, who was a brahmin and a devotee of Shiva, Rama is said to have been burdened by the grievous sin of *Brahmahatya* (brahmin-slaying). Counselled that only a pilgrimage to Hinglaj could purify him, Rama, according to tradition, journeyed to the shrine to perform penance. The site is also sacred to the Aghori ascetics, who revere Hinglaj Mata as their patron goddess, and it features in Nath yogi and Charan bardic traditions alike.

Schaflechner gathers the story of Rama "from the bits and pieces I have gathered from a few different texts" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 120, folio not stated). In one telling the sage Kumbhodar refuses Rama's food, and "To cleanse himself of this sin, the sage advises Rama to make a pilgrimage to Hinglaj Mata, the only place that can purify him." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 120, folio not stated); in another, "The sage Vashishta advised Rama to go on the pilgrimage to Hinglaj, which was the only place where he could be purified of his horrible offense." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 76, folio not stated). At a pass, "the Goddess's army, which guards the entry to her empire, intercepts the group. A fight breaks out in which many of Rama's men die, and after twelve years of battle, the king from Ayodhya and his army must retreat." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 120–121, folios not stated). The Goddess tells him "he must return to his first stop, Ram Bagh, and make the journey again as a simple pilgrim. So Rama leaves his entourage, his army, and vehicles behind and sets out to walk to the shrine, accompanied only by his closest friends." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 121, folio not stated). "The Devi thus promises them that their descendants will, at some point in time, all come back to do the pilgrimage." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 121, folio not stated). On the way Hanuman's stomping brings only a dry riverbed and Lakshmana's arrow only splits a hill, until "In agony, Sita places her palm down on the soil and thereby makes five wells appear, from which the group drinks." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 121, folio not stated). Then "the Devi grants him purification of his sin. To mark his completed yātrā, he carves the symbols of the sun and moon on the mountain opposite the temple." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 121, folio not stated).

The carvings are still pointed out. In Abadhut's novel "the Aghori Baba shows the group the symbols of the sun and the moon, which, as he explains, were carved into the mountain by Ramachandra on his journey to Hinglaj" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 132, folio not stated); Schaflechner describes the imprint on the mountainside: "The place is said to be unreachable by mortals and indeed looks quite inaccessible from the bottom of the valley, though it is possible to climb up there." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 257, folio not stated). He could not find the text Jethamalani names as the source of the story, the *Sat Koṭī Ānand Rāmāyaṇa* (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 120, folio not stated), and "Hart even posits that the pilgrimage's attractiveness is based mainly on the legend that Rama himself visited the shrine." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 121, folio not stated). "no figure was ever as present in the representations of the traditional pilgrimage to Hinglaj as Rama, the seventh incarnation of the God Vishnu." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 119, folio not stated).

The Goddess punishes pride. Hart heard of "A wealthy Shet named Kowaljee" who chartered a ship to carry his party to Hinglaj, "when the Goddess, justly indignant at his arrogance and presumption, transformed the boat and every soul on board into stone." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 114, folio not stated). For Schaflechner the tale marks the land route as the proper one (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 118, folio not stated).

The Brahmakshatriyas tell how five princes, fleeing the curse of Parashurama, came here. The sage Dadhichi tells them that "the five princes must go to the shrine of Hinglaj to be initiated into her tradition", and they "eventually ascend the mountain at Hinglaj, where they fast and meditate for another twelve years." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 64, folio not stated). The Goddess answers: "How could the words of the mighty Parashurama become a lie? But I will help you and do what is possible"; she adds "that he who enters her cave will gain the dharma of an ascetic" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 64, folio not stated). "The Goddess Hinglaj serves here as the community's creator and as the guarantor of 'Kshatriyadom.'" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 68, folio not stated).

To Mekan Dada she came "disguised as a normal shepherd, wearing a Balochi dress and accompanied by a herd of goats she had created through her mantra power", and could not fill his cup, "so that a hundred thousand liters came out, but still she could not fill the glass" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 77–78, folios not stated). His vow names the whole round of the valley: "I will go to Chandrakup, I will climb up to Anil Kund, I will do the pūjā at your temple, I will put siṃdūr [Hin. vermilion] on the place where Rama sat down, and I will see the moon and the sun carved into stone there!" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 78, folio not stated).

According to Kamphorst, "the Tumbel spread the Goddess's fame in Las Bela, and so Hinglaj decided to settle in a cave close to the Hingol River, where she has been worshipped by the Charan and other communities since as early as the ninth century" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 71, folio not stated). For the Charans, returning alive was itself her gift: "Homecomers were seen as a divine gift since, as the Charans say, the Goddess herself had blessed such pilgrims and allowed them to return safely from the brink of death." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 172, folio not stated).

Some say the Goddess has left her temple. A visitor from Karachi told Schaflechner that "the Goddess actually no longer resides at the shrine because she has become annoyed with the crowd herself", and that one must climb two and a half hours up to Anil Kund, "where she had taken refuge" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 188, folio not stated). Hart was told that she moves between the valley and an island, "where she resides for the largest part of the day, for seven 'pruhurs'" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 260, folio not stated). At Anil Kund, local tradition holds, "some of the water from the Ganges also found its way from his hair into the hollow at Anil Kund", Shiva having come to these mountains to meditate (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 255, folio not stated); pilgrims from Gujarat say that "Altogether, nine hundred rivers came together here to form Anil Kund", while in other accounts the rivers are "eighty-four thousand—thus the alternate name, Chaurasi" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 256, folio not stated).

A great stone in the valley has two origins. Gopal Maharaj says that sages once meditated in the mountains so that common people dared not disturb them, and "the pilgrims asked the Goddess to arrange some other way for them to receive the merit" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 259, folio not stated); "Another story says that Guru Gorakhnath himself took the stone from somewhere around Anil Kund and brought it to this place" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 259, folio not stated). Tradition also "has it that Hinglaj was very fond of the Nath community, especially of Guru Gorakhnath. So the Goddess waited day after day in Thatta for Gorakhnath to come to meet her" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 205, folio not stated).

Gopal Maharaj told of the end of Sharan Kund as a miracle: after a child drowned there, "After only fifteen days there was so much rain that a huge landslide filled the pond... it was only since a mother cried in front of the Goddess, because she had lost her child, that the pond disappeared." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 253, folio not stated). And he stopped the author's research: "he told me that the Goddess had come to him in a dream and instructed him to tell me not to conduct any further inquiries on the topic because my work was disturbing her sleep." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 201, folio not stated). "This divine intervention put an end to my research into the sanctum sanctorum." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 201, folio not stated).

The Goddess protects the road. "There are many wild animals on the way, but the Mother always helps, and never has anyone been hurt. Nothing has ever happened to us. This is because we have the power of the triśūl, the chaṛī, and the jhoṃk, the flame that burns all the time in the rath. This flame is like our friend; it is sent by the Mother." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 164, folio not stated). A pilgrim named Giresh walked for his sister: "I came since I wanted my sister to have a child. I had already come twice, once on a bus and then on a motorbike, but my Mother told me that I must come on foot. Then [the Goddess] heard my wish and my sister had a child. This is why I am now walking to her shrine." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 153, folio not stated). Indian pilgrims see her hand in their visas: "She is the one who influences the employees working in the Pakistani embassy to issue visas, or who causes airport security personnel to get distracted at the right time." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 173, folio not stated). "There are many legends about pilgrims disappearing in the tunnel", which Schaflechner doubts (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 265, folio not stated). On the road, too, the Goddess Ashapura "used to sit there to welcome the pilgrims", and "a statue of Ashapura was erected there by a Gujarati businessman" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 220, folio not stated); and "camels are not allowed to cross into the valley since their feet would swell from the presence of the Goddess" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 221, folio not stated).

Her power has been carried to India by dreams. Anoop Dan dreamt that "since her shrine in Balochistan was no longer reachable, the site of Temra Rai should be visited by Charans in its place" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 167, folio not stated); the head of a Delhi temple said his guru was told "to build her temple here in India, because there is no proper worship [pūjā arcanā] in Pakistan" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 170, folio not stated). Schaflechner sets beside such claims two rumours he calls false, that the Taliban had blasted the cave temple and that three pilgrims from Rajkot were killed on the road in 2006: "Both stories turned out to be sheer fabrications." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 170, folio not stated).

Wilford's informants described a rite before the temple: they "strip naked, and rolling themselves upon the rough pavement they, like madmen, call out" to the Mother to cleanse them (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 234–235, folio not stated). Khalid names Hinglaj with Katas Raj as an ancient Hindu temple and pilgrimage with which "Such myths have developed" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 64, folio not stated).

## Hinglaj in Verse and Song

In Sindhi mystical poetry Hinglaj is a goal. Schimmel writes that Shah Abdul Latif "perhaps reached the sacred mountain Hinglaj in Balochistan, which is often mentioned as" 'the goal' in that poetry (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 421, folio 390). In his Sur Ramakali the journey recurs: "Again, the journey towards the East (3) and that to Hinglaj are mentioned (10), and eventually the saints are re-ported to have reached Kabul, the distant place beyond the hills (14)" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 223, folio 223); "Burning in the fire of love (17) they set out for Hinglaj (20 ff.)" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 234, folio 234). Elsewhere she writes that "the seeker who was enchanted by the Yogis, the true saints, tries to follow them to the inaccessible heights of Mount Hinglaj" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 178, folio 178).

Schimmel notes that the poet's yogis travel west, yet are said to go east. "even though Mount Hinglaj, the 'spiritual goal' is geographically located west of Sind in the Las Bela region, his Yogis' journey was still 'towards the East'" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 221, folio 221). "East is, for Shah, the eternal spiritual homeland, which is alluded to also in the very name of Sur Purab, 'East'. He follows the old spiritual tradition of the Ex Oriente lux-type" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 220, folio 220). Schimmel says it again elsewhere: "even when the pilgrims travel to Mt. Hinglaj, which is situated west of Sind, they are said to" 'go East', "i.e., to find illumination." (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 288, folio 270).

The poet also turns the pilgrimage inward. In Schimmel's translation:

*Those who are happy when sitting, the head put on their knees, / Yogis make the pilgrimage, they have reached divine essence (uluhiyyat)* (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 227, folio 227)

And again:

*the Kaparis have not seen Kabul and Kashmir, / Those who make the journey of truth, sitting they meet the guru* (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 230, folio 230)

Schimmel glosses: "True journey is safar dar watan, interior journey, as Sassui was told at the end of her road. The foreign kingdoms are in the seeker's heart" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 230, folio 230).

Sachal Sarmast's poetry, Boivin notes, names "a number of sacred Hindu places, such as Hinglaj, Kasi, or Kashi (Banaras), Sri Lanka and the Ganga" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 106, folio 93).

For the Charans, Hinglaj is the source of verse itself. The Goddess "grants Charan men the ability to write and compose stories and poems. She is their muse, and they imagine her sitting on a poet's tongue, quickening his creativity." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 70, folio not stated). Schaflechner sets a Charan saying from Barhath at the head of a chapter: "From the moment a Charan is born, Mother Hinglaj waits for him to come for the darśan; if he leaves the world otherwise [without having visited the shrine], then a stone falls from the Mother's mountain." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 172, folio not stated). The stories and the poetry of the bards, he writes, perpetuate "a continuous history" that ties local goddesses to the shrine in Balochistan (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 74, folio not stated).

## Interfaith Reverence and Modern Revival

One of the most striking features of Hinglaj is its cross-communal devotion. Despite the upheavals of Partition and the decline of Hindu institutions in Pakistan, the shrine has not merely survived but is venerated by local Muslims of the region, who know it as *Nani Mandir* or *Bibi Nani* and regard it as a place of blessing. This shared reverence has helped preserve the site through difficult times. In 1986 the Hinglaj Sheva Mandali, the temple committee, was established to organise and promote the annual pilgrimage, and improved road access through Hingol National Park in recent years has dramatically increased the numbers making the *yatra*, transforming what was once a perilous journey for the hardiest of devotees into a mass pilgrimage. The wider Hingol cultural landscape has been proposed for recognition on account of its natural and spiritual importance.

Masson's account of 1842 is, for Schaflechner, "the first explicit textual mention of the name 'Bibi Nani' available to me and is one of the primary references to a Muslim influence at the shrine within colonial literature." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 91, folio not stated). Masson thought the Muslims might "have preserved the ancient name NANAIA, that of the goddess of the old Persians, and the Bactrians" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 91, folio not stated). Holdich wrote in 1904: "Here Hindoo and Muhammadan pilgrims alike resort, each claiming the divine protection of the presiding goddess or of the departed saint, according to the tenets of their faith; and neither recognising that the object of their veneration is probably the same goddess who was known to the Chaldeans under the same old-world name (Nana) a thousand years before the time of Abraham." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 92, folio not stated).

Colonial writers recorded the Muslim legends of the site. "Colonel Wilford writes that the local Muslim community living around the temple believed that Eve fell from heaven, landed in one of the ponds inside the valley, and later settled there and became known as Bibi Nani or as Hinglaj" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 89, folio not stated). "Hart mentions that Muslims in the area claimed that Hazrat Ali, the son-in-law of the Prophet Mohammad, split a rock in two with his sword as he was on his way into the valley" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 89–90, folio not stated). "And one of Aurel Stein's informants claimed that Hinglaj, or Bibi Nani, is considered to be the wife of Ali" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 90, folio not stated). Briggs reported a legend "that a Muslim woman was the priest of the Goddess in Hinglaj in those days", who by applying the *ṭīkā* turned the pilgrims into Muslims, so that they had to be reconverted on their way back; Schaflechner thinks the story "may simply be another in the huge corpus of unverifiable legends surrounding the aloof shrine of the Goddess." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 115, folio not stated).

"Today, such myths have virtually vanished from Hinglaj, and even though many recent Hindu texts note this element of Muslim worship at the shrine, no particular details are given." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 90, folio not stated). "Zikri Muslim pilgrims still visit the shrine today" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 90, folio not stated), and the shrine is "widely frequented" on Baqra Eid, on the Prophet's birthday and on Ashura (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 272, folio not stated). But "The lack of any current mytho-historic narratives at the shrine told from a Zikri perspective relates to an increasing 'Hinduization' inside the valley through which any kind of (Zikri) Muslim symbol is increasingly marginalized." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 90, folio not stated). "Due to their lack of control over the renovations, the Zikri community has had few chances to establish their interpretation of the image at the shrine." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 262, folio not stated). The committee's work, Schaflechner concludes, is "not only parts in a process of 'Hinduization' but also represent steps in a process of (Lasi) 'Lohanaization' as they literally set in stone a particular parochial interpretation of Hinglaj and the beliefs and practices of her followers." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 325, folio not stated).

Sindhi nationalists claim the valley too. "The valley of Hinglaj is also located in this area and served the ancient Sindhis as a place of contemplation. This is why—according to Abro—Hinglaj can also be called a 'thought evolution site'" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 94, folio not stated). Boivin's Parsram held that universal wisdom was preserved in Sindh, "in Hinglaj to be exact" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 50, folio 37).

## Setting

The books describe the province around the shrine, not the shrine itself, in these passages. Schaflechner notes that "Unlike many other rivers in Balochistan, the Hingol flows throughout the year" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 215, folio not stated), and that the mud cones of the region last because of Balochistan's low rainfall (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 211, folio not stated). "After the first part of the Makran Coastal Highway (N 10) was completed in 2001, the area around the River Hingol became connected to the National Highway 25, a heavily traveled road that links Karachi with Quetta" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 83, folio not stated). Indian pilgrims "must obtain an additional visa allowing travel to Balochistan, a province that has been in a state of a latent insurgency since the establishment of Pakistan" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 169, folio not stated). Of the bomb blast at the shrine of Lahoot la Makan he writes that "a terrible bomb blast in November 2016, killing more than fifty people, has curbed the growing influx of visitors and has created a volatile situation for Balochistan's shrines" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 108, folio not stated).

Haroon Khalid, looking out from Manora, sees "the Makran mountain range, the boundary line between Sindh and Baluchistan" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 228, folio 202), and writes that most of Pakistan's remaining non-Muslims are Hindus of the "faraway" "peripheral" regions, among them Baluchistan (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 12, folio not stated). Schimmel counts "the hill slopes of Balochistan" among the places where the folk tales of Sind are set (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 161, folio 161). Boivin places Hinglaj in "the Sindhi-speaking area of Baluchistan" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 50, folio 37).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **Where the shrine stands.** The site's account places it in the Lasbela district, within Hingol National Park. Schaflechner confirms the park, "in which the shrine is located" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 295, folio not stated), but names no district. Schimmel puts Mount Hinglaj "west of Sind in the Las Bela region" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 221, folio 221). Bhandari places it, in his account of Thatta, seventy kos from Thatta between north and west near the salt sea (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529). Boivin places it in "the Sindhi-speaking area of Baluchistan" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 50, folio 37). Khalid gives about 250 kilometres from Karachi (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 32, folio not stated).
- **Whose shrine it is.** The site's account dedicates it to the goddess Hinglaj Mata. Masson's source had it consecrated to Parbatí (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 91, folio not stated); Schimmel calls it "an ancient Shiva sanctuary" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 159, folio 159); Bhandari calls it a place attributed to Durga (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529), and Khalid a temple that "honours an incarnation of Durga" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 32, folio not stated). Muslims have seen in it the grave of a saintly wife of Ali (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 235, folio not stated), the place where Eve fell (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 89, folio not stated), or the grave of one of the Prophet's family members (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 325, folio not stated).
- **Which part of Sati fell here.** The site's account gives the head, or the crown of the head, the *brahmarandhra*. The *Pīṭhanirṇaya*, as Schaflechner reports it, gives the *brahmarandhra* (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 55, folio not stated); the *Caṇḍīmaṅgala* gives the navel (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 230–231, folio not stated); a pilgrim gave the womb (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 260, folio not stated).
- **How many seats of the Goddess there are, and how many in Pakistan.** The site's account counts fifty-one *Shakti Peethas*, two of them in Pakistan. In the texts Schaflechner reviews, the *Kulārṇava Tantra* lists eighteen, the *Kubjikā Tantra* forty-two and the *Pīṭhanirṇaya* fifty-one (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 52, folio not stated; Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 53, folio not stated; Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 55, folio not stated). Ibrahim Shah names three in Pakistan (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 97, folio not stated).
- **The names of the Goddess and her Bhairava.** The site's account gives Kottari and Bhimalochana. The *Pīṭhanirṇaya* gives Koṭṭarī, Koṭṭavī and Koṭṭarīśā, and Bhīmalocana as the form of Shiva (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 55, folio not stated).
- **When the name first appears.** Schaflechner gives the ninth to the fifteenth century (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 57, folio not stated) and, elsewhere, the tenth to the fifteenth (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 96, folio not stated). Desai holds that the form Hiṅghalāja cannot be older than the eleventh century (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 98, folio not stated). The fall of the head first appears in the *Pīṭhanirṇaya*, dated by Sircar between 1690 and 1720 (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 56, folio not stated), or perhaps by 1571 if the *Tantracūḍāmaṇi* is its source (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 56, folio not stated).
- **How old the worship is.** The site's account says it reaches back well over a millennium. Schaflechner finds an elaborate pilgrimage system "at least since the fourteenth century" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 145, folio not stated); Kamphorst puts the shrine's importance at least in the ninth century (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 96–97, folio not stated); Tambs-Lyche suggests the fifth to seventh (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 71–72, folios not stated).
- **Who told Rama to go to Hinglaj.** The site's account says only that he was counselled. One telling names the sage Kumbhodar (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 120, folio not stated), another the sage Vashishta (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 76, folio not stated).
- **Whose is the second face.** The demon Hingol, in the written legend and in Gopal Maharaj's account of 2009; a *vīr* of Durga, in his account of 2012 (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 46, folio not stated); Shiva's or Rama's, in what devotees often say (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 230, folio not stated).
- **Who Hinglaj was as a woman.** A Charani of Thatta whose father was Haridas (Samaur); the queen of Kohala and leader of the Tumbel Charan (Payaka); Charani Deval of the eighth century (Kamphorst) (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 71, folio not stated). Some Charans count her among the incarnations of the Goddess; others hold her the primordial power that never fully incarnates (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 71, folio not stated).
- **What the garbha is.** "a small rift in the mountain wall called the garbha (Hin. womb)" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 200, folio not stated), or "the garbha, the tunnel underneath the sanctum" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 201, folio not stated).
- **The Goddess's form.** The site's account calls her worshipped as a small mud-stone form daubed with sindoor. Schaflechner describes an oblong stone about a metre by a metre and a half, now tiled, bearing stones with attached faces and layers of vermilion (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 226, folio not stated; Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 229, folio not stated); Hart, Goldsmid and Stein saw a rectangular stone like a Muslim grave (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 261, folio not stated).
- **When the temple was renovated.** "the end of the 1990s" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 227, folio not stated) against "The HSM's construction work in the early 1990s" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 248, folio not stated).
- **When the highway came.** The first part linking the valley to Karachi "was completed and in use by 2001", the whole road from Liari to Gabd "in 2003" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 83, 85, folio not stated), and elsewhere "at the beginning of the new millennium" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 319, folio not stated). The site's account says only "in recent years".
- **When the ticket system began.** The wristband was introduced "in 2008" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 219, folio not stated); "Before 2009, there had been another ticketing system in place" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 209, folio not stated).
- **When the festival is.** The site's account and Schaflechner give April, in the month of *phālgun* or *caitra* (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 154, folio not stated). Khalid gives October (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 32, folio not stated). The *Brahmavaivarta Purāṇa* promises its merit on the eighth day of *āśvin* (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 54, folio not stated).
- **How many come.** The site's account says tens of thousands. Pottinger wrote of thousands early in the nineteenth century and Hart of five to six thousand a year (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 113, folio not stated); the committee counted over forty thousand in April 2010 (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 84, folio not stated); Khalid writes of thousands every year (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 32, folio not stated).
- **How long the journey took.** Twenty-four days (Briggs), two and a half months (Hotchand), six months (Jethamalani) (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 117, folio not stated); more than fifteen days, in Bhandari (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529).
- **When Todarmal and Bharimal came.** 1508 CE in Chhabria, 1558 CE at Thatta in Ansari (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 112, folio not stated).
- **How many rivers make Anil Kund.** Nine hundred, or eighty-four thousand (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 256, folio not stated).
- **Who placed the great stone.** The Goddess, at the pilgrims' request, in Gopal Maharaj's telling; Guru Gorakhnath, in another (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 259, folio not stated).
- **Whether the Goddess takes blood.** She is now "generally understood as a vegetarian deity"; colonial sources record the eating of sacrificed animals at the Kali shrine; Sindhi Hindu authors call that a foreign custom (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 285, folio not stated). The Devipujak-Vagris hold the offering obligatory (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 311, folio not stated).
- **Whether Shah Abdul Latif went there.** Schimmel writes that he "spent three years" with yogis "up to the sacred mountain Hinglaj" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 29, folio 25), hedges both the duration and the itinerary in a later restatement (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 219, folio 219), and writes that he "perhaps reached" it (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 421, folio 390); Boivin says he "probably" went (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 92, folio 79).
- **Which way the pilgrims travel.** West of Sind on the map, but "towards the East" in the poetry (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 221, folio 221).
- **The Muslim community at the shrine.** The site's account says local Muslims venerate it and that their reverence has helped preserve it. Schaflechner records that the Zikris were its custodians until 1986 (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 90, folio not stated), that the Muslim myths "have virtually vanished" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 90, folio not stated), and that their reading of the image has been covered by the renovation (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 262, folio not stated).

## Significance Today

Hinglaj Mata Mandir stands as the foremost living Hindu pilgrimage site in Pakistan and a shrine of the very first rank in the *Shakti Peetha* network. Its endurance, in a remote and challenging landscape and across the fault-lines of religion and history, has made it a powerful symbol of resilience for Pakistan's Hindu minority and a place of extraordinary sanctity for devotees of the Goddess throughout the subcontinent.

Schaflechner explains the shrine's new place by its remoteness. "the location of the shrine in a remote desert region, outside urban settlements, is certainly a significant reason for the temple's increasing public visibility" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 271, folio not stated). "Due to such insecurities, the shrine of Hinglaj represents a safe haven for undisturbed Hindu celebrations. Conveniently located close to a highway, yet far enough from urban centers and possible threats, Hinglaj provides a remote stage for public displays of Hindu faith and draws worshippers from all corners of the country." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 319, folio not stated). Though an official of the Jinnah Institute called Hinglaj in 2012 a 'high risk for catastrophe', "it is one of the few places in Pakistan that offers a relatively secure public space for a massive Hindu festival to occur." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 50, folio not stated). "Hinglaj is not simply a particular, unique locality anymore; it stands in a synecdochical relationship to Hinduism as such" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 287, folio not stated).

The interest of Indian pilgrims raises its standing at home. "Because many are accustomed to looking to India in terms of religious authority, this valorization of the shrine is refreshing for the community" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 270, folio not stated), and "Indian groups at the shrine are frequently treated with respect and curiosity and get regular attention from local and national television channels broadcasting all over the country." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 270, folio not stated). Yet no single account of the place has prevailed: "Its remote location is also partially responsible for the fact that no single prevailing narrative evolved over time." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 29, folio not stated). "Today, the tradition of Hinglaj finds itself at an intricate crossroads. On the one hand, it is rooted in an ancient history; on the other hand, the site has seen some of its most disruptive changes within only a few decades." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 195, folio not stated).
