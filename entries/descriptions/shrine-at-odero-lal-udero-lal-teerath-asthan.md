## Overview

The shrine at Odero Lal — also spelt Udero Lal — in the village of the same name near Tando Adam in Sindh, is among the most remarkable sacred places in Pakistan: a single sanctuary revered and used in common by both Hindus and Muslims, where a Hindu temple and a Muslim tomb stand together under one roof and are tended by caretakers of both faiths. To the Sindhi Hindus the saint of the shrine is *Jhulelal*, understood as an incarnation of *Varuna*, the ancient deity of the waters; to the Muslims he is a holy man whom they call *Sheikh Tahir*, or simply, in a name both communities honour, *Zinda Pir* — "the living saint." The shrine is one of the great living symbols of the shared religious culture of Sindh, and of the reverence, common to both traditions there, for the life-giving waters of the Indus.

Of the books consulted, Michel Boivin's *The Hindu Sufis of South Asia* is the one that describes the site itself. He calls it "the darbar of Udero Lal", "the main darbar devoted to Jhulelal" and "the huge sanctuary close to Hyderabad where Hindus and Muslims worship a dual figure: Jhulelal/Shaykh Tahir" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 184, folio 171), and he writes that "the main centre of the Daryapanth is in Udero Lal, the name given to the village which houses a vast sanctuary dating back to the seventeenth century" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 42–43, folios 29–30). For him the darbar is the best example of a sacred place where "the saint has a double identity, Muslim and Hindu" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 178, folio 165). Boivin spells the Muslim saint's name Shaykh Tahir; this article keeps Sheikh Tahir except in quotation.

Annemarie Schimmel knows the figure but names no place for him. In *Pain and Grace* she calls him "Shaikh Tahir, called by the Hindus Lal Udero" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 28, folio 24), and in *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* her index sends the reader from "Tahir, Sheikh" to "Lal Udero" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 526, folio 495), so that both her books join the two names that the shrine joins; neither describes a shrine, a village or a festival. H. T. Sorley, writing of Sindh in the eighteenth century, uses the name Udero Lal only once, and then of a different saint at a different place: "the shrine of the Kalandar Shah Lal Baz (or Udero Lal) at Sehwan" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 168, folio 159). That identification is his alone; it is set out below among the points on which the sources disagree.

## The Legend of Jhulelal

The story that gives the shrine its meaning is set in a time of persecution. According to Sindhi Hindu tradition, a tyrannical ruler — remembered as *Mirkh Shah* of Thatta — commanded the Hindus of Sindh to abandon their faith and accept Islam. In their fear the Hindus gathered on the banks of the Indus and prayed to *Varuna*, the lord of the river, for deliverance; and the god answered that he would be born among them as a child, at *Nasarpur*, to save them. The saviour so promised is *Jhulelal*, whose birth the Sindhi Hindus still celebrate at *Cheti Chand*, the festival that marks the Sindhi new year and is kept as his birthday. In the devotion of Sindh, Jhulelal is the protector of his people and the spirit of the river itself, and Odero Lal is honoured as the centre of his cult.

Boivin gives the tradition in a shorter and somewhat different form: "According to tradition, Udero Lal emerged from the river to help the Hindus persecuted by a Muslim ruler in the mid-tenth century." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 42, folio 29). He does not name the ruler. Tradition also has an end for the story at the shrine itself: Jhulelal "is said to have disappeared in a well set up in the darbar of Udero Lal" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 186, folio 173), and Boivin reads the presence of a well in another Sindhi shrine as "strongly reminiscent of Jhulelal" for that reason (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 186, folio 173).

## Jhulelal, Udero Lal and Sheikh Tahir

The figure of the shrine carries many names, and the books trace them in different ways. Boivin is cautious about the Hindu figure's origins: "Jhulelal was probably a modern figure of the Vedic god Varun, the god of the Indus River. He is briefly mentioned in historical sources" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 54, folio 41). He does not say which sources. Of the darbar he writes that "The Hindus worship Jhulelal, who they claim to be an avatar of the Vedic God Varun." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 178, folio 165), and in the colonial period he places "an attempt made to create a centralized path devoted to Jhulelal, then known as Amar Lal or Udero Lal, in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 54, folio 41).

Omar Kasmani, citing Boivin, gathers the names of the river cult in a single note: "According to Boivin (2012b, 43), the Indus was worshipped under the name Udero Lal, depicted as an incarnation of Varuna, the Vedic deity of waters. Other references include Amar Lal, Darya Shah, Darya Nath, Zinda pir, Shaykh Tahir, and the Quranic figure (Khwaja) Khizr." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 185, folio 168, intro n. 37). He adds that "In most historical accounts, it is the figure of Udero Lal who is conflated with Khizr in Sindh" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 195, folio 178, ch. 5 n. 9).

The Muslim tradition of the site tells that the saint interred here was born a Hindu named Odero Lal and embraced Islam, becoming known as Sheikh Tahir; some identify the figure with *Khwaja Khizr*, the deathless guide associated in Islamic lore with the waters. Boivin records two further Muslim accounts. One gives the saint a Sufi lineage: "a Sufi genealogy has been built – if in an unknown period", in that "a tazkira refers to him as a disciple of the same Bahauddin Zakariyya already mentioned" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 51, folio 38); Boivin does not name the *tazkira*. The other concerns his order: "The Muslims venerate a Sufi, Shaykh Tahir, who could have been a Sohrawardi." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 178, folio 165). The hedge is Boivin's own.

Schimmel speaks of Sheikh Tahir as a mystic rather than as a god of the river. In *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* she writes: "A strange fact—more visible in Sind than in the Punjab—is the way Hindus shared in the Muslim orders. Some saints were claimed by both communities, like Sheikh Tahir," whom the Hindus, she goes on, called Lal Udero (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 418, folio 387). "This sixteenth-century mystic was one of the most ardent defenders of the unity of being", she continues, and he saw God 'even in a camel' (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 418, folio 387); her index, however, places him in the seventeenth century (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 520, folio 489). Her source for the identification is Mir ʿAli Shir Qaniʿ's *Tuhfat al-kiram*, in its Hyderabad (Sind) edition of 1957 (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 418, folio 387). In *Pain and Grace* she makes him her named case of the shared saint: "The most famous case is that of Shaikh Tahir, called by the Hindus Lal Udero, who is venerated by members of both communities, and about whose ecstatic 'states' strange legends are told" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 28, folio 24). She does not tell the legends there.

## Legends and Miracles

Beside the Hindu legend of the river's deliverer, one story of Sheikh Tahir himself reaches the books, and it is the same story in both of Schimmel's: that he saw God in a camel. In *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* it is the measure of his devotion to the unity of being (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 418, folio 387). In *Pain and Grace*, at the far end of the book, she treats it with open irony: "when Shaikh Tahir saw God even in a camel behind which he started running we reach the border between the lofty and the ridiculous" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 251, folio 251). The judgement is Schimmel's, not the tradition's; her footnote there again names Qani‘'s *Tuhfat ul-kiram*, in a Sindhi translation, as the source (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 251, folio 251).

## The Sanctuary

Boivin dates the sanctuary to the seventeenth century and calls it "vast" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 42–43, folios 29–30). He notes that "Although there are several temples dedicated to Udero Lal", the village of Udero Lal is the main centre of the cult (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 42–43, folios 29–30); the other temples are not named. He locates the darbar "about 50 kilometres north of Hyderabad" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 54, folio 41).

Of its fabric he records one fact, in a note on the domes of Sindhi temples: "the pre-partition temples devoted to Jhulelal in Sindh were built with a dome, as were some Sikh samadhis. The main site known as the darbar of Udero Lal hosts many domes (Boivin and Rajpal 2018)." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 210, folio 197, Ch. 2 n. 5). He does not count the domes. Within the darbar is the well into which Jhulelal is said to have disappeared (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 186, folio 173).

The plan of the sanctuary answers to its two communities. "In Udero Lal, for example, to which I shall return, the Hindus venerate Udero Lal in a chapel of the sanctuary, and the Muslims worship Shaykh Tahir in another chapel of the same sanctuary (Boivin and Rajpal 2018)." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 51, folio 38). Boivin's general description fits it: "although there is a single sanctuary, the Hindus worship the saint with a specific name and in a specific chapel, while the Muslims give him another name and worship him in another chapel" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 50–51, folios 37–38). Elsewhere he describes the type more fully: "It is a sacred place hosting one or two tombs and catafalques, and the saint has a double identity, Muslim and Hindu. The best example is the darbar of Udero Lal." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 178, folio 165).

## A Shared Sanctuary

A beloved legend accounts for the shrine's dual character: when Hindus and Muslims disputed over the saint, a voice is said to have come from the heavens commanding that the shrine be made acceptable to both — one part a temple, the other a *dargah* — for the saint belonged to all.

And so it remains. Within the complex, Hindus perform *puja* and *aarti* while Muslims offer their prayers at the tomb; an ever-burning lamp and the veneration of the nearby Indus are shared by both, and the custodianship of the shrine is divided between the two communities. Few places express so directly the interwoven devotional life of Sindh.

Boivin describes the division in its offices and its festival: "The annual fair is performed separately, and there is a gaddi nashin for the Hindus and a sajjada nashin for the Muslims." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 178, folio 165). He names neither incumbent. He also cautions against too easy a picture: "Such a scenario should not be idealized since the groups interact very little, and many disputes arise regularly." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 178, folio 165). The sharing, in his account, runs one way: "In this case, this is a shared space, but only the Hindus pay a visit to Shaykh Tahir's tomb. The Muslims do not go to the space devoted to Jhulelal." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 178, folio 165).

Boivin dates one fair: "In this darbar, during the annual fair in April of 2016, there was a huge poster staging Shah Abd al-Latif and Jhulelal, with the epigram: Sab ka malik ek hai." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 186, folio 173). He writes that the formula "has for some years been very popular among the Hindus of Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 186, folio 173). It was in the same month that he began "a new research project on the darbar of Udero Lal" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 184, folio 171), and with a team "toured in Southern Sindh to map out the different shrines devoted to Jhulelal in Southern Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 184, folio 171).

## Devotion and Practice

In Boivin's account the darbar is the main centre of the Daryapanth, the river cult which he places among the Lohanas and related mercantile groups. "It is based on the deification of the Indus River. … The Daryapanth was managed by a priestly caste, the Thakurs. Most of the rituals were related to changes in the level of the Indus during a given year." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 42, folio 29). He judges that "The Daryapanth is in all probability the oldest of the two" cults, the other being the Nanakpanth (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 42, folio 29).

The Thakurs, he writes, were the priests of Jhulelal in general, not of this shrine alone: "The Thakurs were the priests of Jhulelal. In Sindh, they were settled in different cities but they migrated to India from 1947 onwards. Here, the Thakurs came from Sehwan Sharif." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 210, folio 197, Ch. 2 n. 6). He is careful over the name: "In Sindhi, the Rajut Thakur is written with a long 'a', while the Jhulelal Thakur is written with a short 'a'." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 217, folio 204, Ch. 7 n. 7). In Sehwan itself the Thakurs ran "Jhulelal's temple in the northwestern part of the town" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 71, folio 58).

In the colonial period the darbar was a meeting place for the Hindu community of Sindh. Boivin records "a meeting organized by several Hindu panchayats of Sindh at Udero Lal, in the main darbar devoted to Jhulelal" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 54, folio 41), which he dates only to the last decade of the nineteenth century and sets beside the attempt to centralise the Daryapanth (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 54, folio 41). He gives neither its year nor its outcome.

Two devotional formulas of Jhulelal appear in Boivin's book: the inscription "Jhulelal bera par (May Jhulelal safely guide our nave)" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 186, folio 173), and the devotional songs to Jhulelal called *panjro*, which fill "the last four pages" of a devotional book he examines (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 158, folio 145). That book "starts with Guru Nanak and Jhulelal, here named Darya Shah (and Zinda Pir in the caption of the image)" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 156, folio 143). Boivin also notes "a growing similarity between the iconography of Guru Nanak and Jhulelal" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 54, folio 41).

## Setting

The books set the shrine's double devotion within a wider pattern in Sindh. Boivin sorts the ways the Hindus of Sindh are bound to Sufism into three kinds of site, each with its example: a Sufi tomb "visited both by Muslims and Hindus" (Bhit Shah), a saint with "a double identity, Muslim and Hindu" (Udero Lal), and "a Sufi darbar … managed by the Hindus" (Pithoro Pir) (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 177–178, folios 164–165). "In Sindh, we already know that a number of sacred figures have a dual religious identity, Muslim and Hindu. The Muslim identity is always related to Sufism, while the Hindu side usually belongs to the Nathpanth." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 187, folio 174). He recalls that "As Richard Burton noted as early as 1851, it is indeed the worship of the intercessors that constitutes the common religious idiom of Hindus and Muslims" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 51, folio 38). Among the Lohanas and related mercantile groups, he writes, "two main cults were followed: the Nanakpanth and the Daryapanth" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 42–43, folios 29–30).

Sorley, writing of the province in the eighteenth century, records the river's place in the common religion of Sindh. The cult of Khwajo Khizr was "linked up with the worship of the river Indus and supported by Muslim and Hindu alike", and Khizr is "called in Sind Zinda Pir" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 177, folio 168). Quoting Longworth Dames, he adds: "In Sind under the name of Khwajo Khizr this saint has always exercised a great power and has in popular superstition become somehow identified with the spirit of the waters and … with the deity of the river Indus", and "The Daryapanthis of Sind are believers in the sanctity of the River God or the God of Waters, and the belief affects Hindu and Muslim alike" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 301, folio 292). Sorley also notes, of the province at large, "Hindus visiting Mussulman shrines and making votive offerings" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 175, folio 166). Kasmani observes that "Offerings to the Indus River can still be documented in the wider region of Sindh" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 195, folio 178, ch. 5 n. 11).

The name Jhule Lal reaches beyond this shrine. Kasmani, whose book names no temple of the figure and describes no site outside Sehwan, writes that "Jhule Lal, the modern chant directed at the saint, was once a call to the river deity of the Indus worshipped by the Hindu Daryapanthis." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 36, folio 19), and that "in the figure of Lal, the Quranic river saint Khizr meets an Indic fish god." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 162, folio 145). A pilgrim train carried the name: "special trains like the Jhule Lal Express" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 70, folio 53). Haroon Khalid describes a temple mural at Port Grand of "a male deity sitting [on] top of a giant fish", with water beneath, whom he names as "the famous Hindu deity also revered in certain Muslim traditions—Jhule Lal" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 225, folio 199); the temple he describes is not this shrine.

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **Where Udero Lal is venerated.** The site's account and Boivin place the cult of Udero Lal at the village of that name; Boivin calls it "the main centre of the Daryapanth" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 42–43, folios 29–30). Sorley gives Udero Lal as another name for the Qalandar of Sehwan: "the shrine of the Kalandar Shah Lal Baz (or Udero Lal) at Sehwan" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 168, folio 159), and his index repeats the equation (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 441). He describes no separate shrine of Udero Lal.
- **Where the shrine lies.** The site's account places it near Tando Adam; Boivin places it "about 50 kilometres north of Hyderabad" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 54, folio 41) and calls it "the huge sanctuary close to Hyderabad" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 184, folio 171).
- **The deliverance legend.** The site's account names the persecutor as Mirkh Shah of Thatta and has Varuna promise to be born as a child at Nasarpur. Boivin's tradition has Udero Lal emerge "from the river to help the Hindus persecuted by a Muslim ruler in the mid-tenth century", naming no ruler and no birthplace (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 42, folio 29).
- **Who the saint was.** In the site's Muslim tradition he was born a Hindu named Odero Lal who embraced Islam and became Sheikh Tahir, and some identify him with Khwaja Khizr. Boivin reports a *tazkira* making Udero Lal a disciple of Bahauddin Zakariyya (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 51, folio 38) and calls Shaykh Tahir a Sufi who "could have been a Sohrawardi" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 178, folio 165), while judging Jhulelal "probably a modern figure of the Vedic god Varun" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 54, folio 41). Kasmani, after Boivin, makes Udero Lal the name under which the Indus itself was worshipped (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 185, folio 168, intro n. 37). Schimmel calls Sheikh Tahir a mystic of the unity of being whom the Hindus called Lal Udero (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 418, folio 387).
- **When Sheikh Tahir lived.** Schimmel's text calls him a "sixteenth-century mystic" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 418, folio 387); her index places him in the seventeenth century (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 520, folio 489). Boivin's tradition sets Udero Lal's emergence from the river in the mid-tenth century (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 42, folio 29).
- **How far the sanctuary is shared.** The site's account has both communities venerating the lamp and the river together, the boundary between them dissolving in shared reverence. Boivin writes that only the Hindus visit Shaykh Tahir's tomb, that the Muslims do not go to the space devoted to Jhulelal, that the annual fair is performed separately, and that "the groups interact very little, and many disputes arise regularly" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 178, folio 165).
- **The camel story.** Schimmel gives the same anecdote two readings: in *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* it shows Sheikh Tahir as "one of the most ardent defenders of the unity of being" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 418, folio 387); in *Pain and Grace* it marks "the border between the lofty and the ridiculous" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 251, folio 251).

## Legacy

The shrine at Odero Lal has endured as a symbol of tolerance and common humanity, a place where the boundary between Hindu and Muslim dissolves in shared reverence for a single saint and a single sacred river. In a subcontinent whose modern history has too often been marked by communal division, Udero Lal stands as testimony to an older and more generous pattern of coexistence, and it continues to draw pilgrims of both faiths — Sindhi Hindus above all, for whom it is a spiritual homeland, and Muslims who honour the *Zinda Pir*. Its festivals, and especially the celebration of Cheti Chand, keep alive a devotion that has bound the peoples of Sindh together for centuries.

After Partition the cult of Jhulelal travelled with the Sindhi Hindus to India, and Boivin follows it there. Jhulelal became "the isht devta of the Hindu Sindhis of India" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 70, folio 57) and "the Sindhi god of India par excellence" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 174, folio 161); Boivin speaks of "the consecration of Jhulelal as isht devta, or community God, of the Hindu Sindhis of India" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 156, folio 143) and of "Jhulelal as the embodiment of the Sindhi culture in India" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 157, folio 144). Before Partition, he notes, "Jhulelal was already worshipped by some segments among the Hindus of Sindh, but as we saw before, they were in majority Nanakpanthis" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 158, folio 145). The change had a cost: the turn to Jhulelal's worship "can be seen as a kind of soft hinduization of the Sindhi legacy in India" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 159, folio 146), and "With the spread of the Jhulelal's apotheosis, room for the Sufi legacy was reduced among the Hindu Sindhis" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 201, folio 188). In Ulhasnagar a temple of Jhulelal "had recently been built by Sehwani Thakurs" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 83, folio 70). Boivin's own work with Bhavna Rajpal traces the same path in its title, "From Udero Lal in Sindh to Ulhasnagar in Maharashtra: Partition and Memories across Borders in the Tradition of Jhulelal" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 233, folio 220).
