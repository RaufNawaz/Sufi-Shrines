## Overview

At Uch Sharif rests Sayyid Jalaluddin Bukhari (1308–1384), universally known by his honorifics *Makhdoom-i-Jahaniyan* — 'the Master of the World' — and *Jahangasht*, 'the World-Traveller.' Grandson of Jalaluddin Surkh-Posh Bukhari, he was among the most learned and widely revered Suhrawardi saints of fourteenth-century India, a tireless traveller and preacher whose authority was sought by sultans and commoners alike. His shrine is one of the principal darbars of Uch Sharif and a major centre of pilgrimage in the southern Punjab.

The histories and studies consulted for this article fill out that portrait in unusual detail, and in places contradict it. Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori's *Hadiqat al-Awliya* gives him a full notice, with the night of the week on which he was born and the festival on which he died, and places his *roza* at Uch as a place of pilgrimage (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 169, folio 157). Zahur ul-Hasan Sharib gives him a whole chapter, from his birth at Uch to his death, with his teachings, his sayings and the wonders told of him (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 133–142, folios 129–138). Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi prints his birth, his death and his burial: "Saiyid Jalalu'd-Din Bukhari died on 10 Zu'lhijja 785/3 February 1384 and was buried in Uch" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 303, folio 281). Rizvi writes that after the death of Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din the Suhrawardi order "declined in Multan only to be rejuvenated in Uch" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 299, folio 277), and Annemarie Schimmel writes that "Ucch was, for a while, the center of the Suhrawardiyya, thanks chiefly to the active and pious Jalaluddin Makhdum-i Jahaniyan" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354). The books differ over the year of his death, over his father's name, over how often and how far he travelled, over the orders into which he was initiated, and over whether he lies at Uch or at Multan.

A further difficulty runs through the whole record. His grandfather, Jalaluddin Surkh-Posh Bukhari, bore the same name, and several books speak of a Jalal Bukhari, or join the title Makhdum Jahaniyan to that name, in ways that do not settle which of the two men they mean; others give the title to a saint of Multan, or the epithet Pir Jahanian to a man of another name. What each of those books says is given below as that book's own account, with its uncertainty beside it, and the question is set out under 'Where the Sources Disagree'.

## The Life of the Saint

Born at Uch in 1308 into the Bukhari sayyid family founded by his grandfather Surkh-Posh, Jalaluddin was schooled in the religious sciences and the Suhrawardi discipline and grew into a scholar-saint of vast reputation. He earned the title *Jahangasht* — 'he who has roamed the world' — through his extraordinary travels: he journeyed across the Muslim world, performed the Hajj many times, and visited the great centres of learning and sanctity of his age, gathering knowledge and spiritual connection wherever he went. Honoured as *Makhdoom-i-Jahaniyan*, he was held in the highest esteem by the rulers of the Delhi Sultanate, particularly in the Tughluq age, and his counsel and blessing were widely sought. He died in 1384 and was buried at Uch, where his shrine became a focus of devotion.

Sharib makes him one of the Husaini sayyids, of a respected family of Bukhara: "آپ سادات حسینی سے ہیں۔ بخارا کے ایک معزز خاندان سے تھے" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 133, folio 129). He names his father as سید احمد کبیر and his grandfather as شیخ جلال الدین سرخ بخاری (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 133, folio 129), and his younger brother as سید صدر الدین المعروف بہ راجو قتال (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 137, folio 133). Rizvi gives the same line in English: "He was a grandson of Saiyid Jalalu'd-Din Surkh (Red), a disciple of Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya. Saiyid Jalalu'd-Din Surkh had three sons: Saiyid Ahmad Kabir, Saiyid Baha'u'd-Din and Saiyid Muhammad. Saiyid Jalalu'd-Din Bukhari and his younger brother, Raju Qattal, were the sons of Saiyid Ahmad Kabir" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 299, folio 277). Lahori's heading to his notice names the same three generations (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 168–169, folios 156–157), and the editor's appendix to it makes the line one of succession: "ان کے جانشین ان کے فرزند سید احمد کبیر تھے", the grandfather's successor was his son Sayyid Ahmad Kabir (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 349, folio 337).

Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* names another father. Its genealogy reads "خوابگاه شیخ جلال پور سید محمود بن سید جلال بخاری مشهور بمخدوم جهانیانست": the man whose resting-place is at Uch, Shaykh Jalal, son of Sayyid Mahmud, son of Sayyid Jalal Bukhari, is known as Makhdum Jahaniyan; and Bhandari calls him "مرید و خلیفه پدر بزرگوار خود است", the disciple and khalifa of his own revered father (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527). Bhandari does not call the grandfather Surkh-Posh, does not place his tomb at Uch and gives him no date (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527).

The editor's appendix to Lahori's notice is about the father, not the son. "مخدوم جہانیاں جہاں گشت بخاری کے والد گرامی سید احمد کبیر الدین بھی ایک بزرگ صوفی تھے": the revered father of Makhdum Jahaniyan Jahangasht Bukhari, Sayyid Ahmad Kabir al-Din, was also a venerable Sufi. Of his training it adds "وہ شیخ جمال خنداں رو کی خدمت میں بھی حاضر رہتے تھے۔ مخدوم احمد کبیر کی باطنی تربیت شیخ صدرالدین عارف ملتانی نے کی تھی": he attended Shaykh Jamal Khandan Ru, and his inward training was given by Shaykh Sadr al-Din Arif Multani (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 349, folio 337).

The same editor dates the father's death twice, and differently. A footnote to the notice makes Sayyid Ahmad Kabir a murid of Shaykh Sadr al-Din Arif who "۷۱۳ھ [OCR?] کے بعد انتقال کیا", died after 713 AH, the reading of the year uncertain in the printed text, and cites two pages of Muhammad Ayub Qadri's *Makhdoom Jahaniyan Jahan Gasht Bukhari* for it (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 168, folio 156, حاشیہ 1). The appendix says instead: "مخدوم سید احمد کبیر کا سال وفات تذکروں میں نہیں ملتا۔ ہم نے بعض قرائن و قیاسات کی بنیاد پر حدود ۷۳۱ھ/۱۳۳۰۔۳۱ء قرار دیا ہے". The year of his death, the editor writes, is not found in the tazkiras, and he has put it at about 731 AH / 1330–31 CE "on the basis of certain indications and conjectures", pointing to his own Danishnama article "احمد کبیر الدین" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 349, folio 337). The second date is, by the editor's own account, a conjecture.

The books date the son's birth closely, though not alike. Lahori gives it as "چہارم شعبان المعظم شب شنبہ سنہ سات سو سات", the night of Saturday, 4 Shaban, in the year 707 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 169, folio 157). Sharib writes "آپ کی ولادت باسعادت اوچہ میں 15' شعبان 707ھ کو واقع ہوئی", born at Uch on 15 Sha'ban 707 AH, citing the *Salik al-Salikin* and the Urdu translation of the *Akhbar al-Akhyar* (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 133, folio 129, حاشیہ 1). Rizvi has "Saiyid Jalalu'd-Din, the Makhdum-i Jahaniyan, was born on 15 Sha'ban 707/9 February 1308" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 299, folio 277), and Michel Boivin prints his life dates as '(1308–84)' (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 56, folio 43). Bhandari puts the birth on Shab-i Barat: "شب برات هفتصد و هفت هجری بعرصه وجود آمد", on the night of Barat in the year 707 Hijri he came into existence (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527).

Sharib traces his schooling. He began it at Uch, which Sharib spells here "اونچہ", under "علامہ قاضی بہاء الدین"; after the qazi's death he came from Uch to Multan to finish the *Hidaya* and the *Bazdawi*, "ہدایہ و بزدوی", under "شیخ موسیٰ اور مولانا مجد الدین"; and then "مدینہ جا کر مزید تعلیم حاصل کی", he went to Medina and studied further (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 134, folio 130). Rizvi writes that "he received a formal religious education, and then became his father's disciple" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 299, folio 277).

Sharib records "آپ نے شادی کی تھی", that he married, but names no wife and gives no number of children (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 137, folio 133); a youngest son of four years appears in one of the stories told of him (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 140–141, folios 136–137). Rizvi names a son, Saiyid Mahmud Nasiru'd-Din, who "too lived in Uch and attracted disciples from Multan and neighbouring areas. He had a very large family and is believed to have died in 847/1443-44" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 304, folio 282), and his index lists a 'Muhammad b. Makhdum Jahaniyan' (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 476, folio 452).

Of his death the books give more than one year. Lahori writes "دہم ماہ ذی الحجہ روز عید اضحیٰ سنہ سات سو پچاسی", the tenth of the month of Zil-Hijja, the day of Eid al-Adha, in the year 785 AH, and his editor's footnotes dispute neither this date nor that of the birth (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 169, folio 157). Rizvi gives the same day and year with its Common Era equivalent, 3 February 1384, and buries him in Uch (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 303, folio 281); his index has him "(b. 1308, d. 1384)" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 474, folio 450). Bhandari, too, puts his death on the Id-i Qurban of 785, a Wednesday, in the words "چهارشنبه عید قربان هفتصد و هشتاد و پنج اخشیجی پیکر برانداخت", he cast off his elemental frame; he prints no era word with this year, as he does with the year of the birth (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527). Sharib gives a later year: "کچھ روز بیمار رہ کر 10' ذی الحجہ 788ھ کو آپ رحمت حق میں پیوست ہوئے", after some days' illness he passed into God's mercy on 10 Zi al-Hijja 788 AH, again on the authority of the *Salik al-Salikin* (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 137, folio 133, حاشیہ 10). On another page Boivin prints '(d. 1383)' (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 189, folio 176), and Schimmel gives '(d. 1383)' in her text and 'd. 1382-83' in her index (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354; Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 518, folio 487). Carl Ernst and Bruce Lawrence call him only "the famous fourteenth-century Suhrawardi saint of the Punjab" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 95, folio 81).

## His Names and Titles

Sharib gives his name, kunya and titles together. "آپ کا نام سید جلال الدین حسین ہے": his name is Sayyid Jalal al-Din Husain; his kunya is ابو عبداللہ; his laqab is "مخدوم جہانیاں جہاں گشت"; and some, Sharib adds, call him simply Makhdum Jahaniyan (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 133, folio 129). The chapter opens by calling him "حضرت سید جلال الدین بخاری سیاح بادیہ تجرید [OCR?] و تفرید ہیں", a traveller of the wilderness of *tajrid* and *tafrid*, the reading of the first of the two words uncertain in the printed text (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 133, folio 129). Rizvi's fullest form is "Saiyid Abu 'Abdu'llah Jalalu'd-Din Husain bin Ahmad Bukhari, better known as the Makhdum Jahaniyan" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 22, folio 8); elsewhere he writes "the Makhdum Jahaniyan, Saiyid Jalal Bukhari" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 24, folio 10) and 'Makhdum-i Jahaniyan Saiyid Jalal Bukhari' (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 251, folio 231), and his index gives the headword 'Makhdum Jahaniyan, Saiyid Abu ‘Abdu'llah Jalalu’d-Din Husain b. Ahmad Bukhari' (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 474, folio 450).

Lahori heads his notice, entry no. 107, "سیّد جلال الدین بخاری الملقّب بمخدوم جہانیاں جہاں گرد بن سیّد احمد کبیر بن سیّد جلال الدین شیر شاہ میر سُرخ بخاری اوچی": Sayyid Jalal al-Din Bukhari, entitled Makhdum Jahaniyan Jahan-gard, son of Sayyid Ahmad Kabir, son of Sayyid Jalal al-Din Sher Shah Mir Surkh Bukhari of Uch (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 168–169, folios 156–157). The heading's second title is جہاں گرد, the world-roamer; the book's index of persons has instead "مخدوم جہانیاں جہاں گشت بخاری" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 330, folio 318). The notice gives him a chain of praise-titles: "چراغ خاندان مظہر انوار ربانی مطلع تجلیات سبحانی کاشف رموز طریقت ہادی طرائق حقیقت صاحب ارشاد پیشوائے اوتاد" — the lamp of the family, the manifestation of the lights of the Lord, the dawning-place of the epiphanies of the All-Glorious, the unveiler of the mysteries of the Path, the guide on the ways of Reality, the master of guidance, the leader of the *awtad* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 168, folio 156).

The books gloss the titles differently. Rizvi writes "the Makhdum-i Jahaniyan (Lord of the Mortals)" and 'the Jahangasht (World Traveller)' (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 299–300, folios 277–278), and explains the second: "So well-travelled did he become that Saiyid Jalalu'd-Din was called the Jahangasht (World Traveller)" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 300, folio 278). Schimmel renders the first 'whom all the inhabitants of the world serve' (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354). Sharib tells a story of how the first title was conferred, given under 'Legends and Miracles' below.

Other books join the title to the name in their own ways. Boivin writes 'Jalal al-Din Bukhari Makhdum-i Jahaniyyan Jahangasht' (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 56, folio 43) and "Sayyid Jalal al-Din Bukhari, better known under the name of Makhdum Jahaniyyan Jahangasht" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 189, folio 176), and indexes him under 'Jahangasht' (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 244, folio 231). Ernst and Lawrence have 'Makhdum-i Jahaniyan Jahangasht' (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 93, folio 79) and 'Jalal ad-Din Bukhari Makhdum-i Jahaniyan' (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 95, folio 81); Robert Rozehnal 'Sayyid Jalal ad-Din Bukhari Makhdum-i Jahaniyan' (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 57, folio 47); Schimmel 'Jalaluddin Makhdum-i Jahaniyan', to which her index sends the bare title (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 520, folio 489); and Christopher Shackle, in a note to Bulleh Shah, 'Jalal ud Din Makhdum Jahaniyan of Uch' (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 186, folio 161, n. 2 on 122). Waris Shah's romance of Hir and Ranjha, in Charles Frederick Usborne's English, has "Sayyid Jalal of Bukhara, whom men also call Makhdum Jahanian" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 11, folio 10), and later the shorter 'Makhdum Jahanian' (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 50, folio 49) and bare 'Jahanian' (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 52, folio 51); the title Jahangasht never appears in it, nor Uch Sharif, nor any date or order. Bhandari's form is "مشهور بمخدوم جهانیانست", known as Makhdum Jahaniyan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527).

The epithet is not his alone in Lahori's book. Its index gives 'Pir Jahanian' to a Kamal Bukhari (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 326, folio 314; Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 316, folio 304), and so do two of its notices. Shah Kamal Bukhari, the maternal grandfather of Muhammad Nur, is 'famous as Pir Jahanian', with a mazar at qasba Chunian (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 51, folio 39), and a later notice speaks of "پیر سید کمال المشہور پیر جہانیاں" of Chunian, of the Bukhari sayyids, "سادات بخاری", with living descendants there (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 266, folio 254). The book gives this Pir Jahanian the name Kamal, not Jalal al-Din, and a mazar at Chunian, not Uch; whether he is another saint who shared the sobriquet, or a second site claimed for the saint of Uch, these passages do not say.

Sharib spells the saint's town three ways: "اوچہ" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 133, folio 129), "اونچہ" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 134, folio 130) and "اوچ" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 146, folio 142).

## Masters and Initiations

Lahori sets out four stages of his formation, in order. He took his first *bai'at* at the hand of his father, Sayyid Ahmad Kabir, and received from him the *khirqa* of *khilafat* of the Suhrawardi path; he then drew inward *faiz* from Shaykh Sadr al-Din, famous as Muhammad Ghaus; at Sadr al-Din's direction he became the murid of Shaykh Rukn al-Din Abu al-Fath Multani, completed his course and received the khirqa of khilafat from him; and he then made the Hajj, and at Mecca stayed with the Shaikh al-Islam, Shaykh Afif al-Din Abdullah Matri, for outward and inward benefit (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 168, folio 156). A footnote in the edition names him among the "نامور خلفا", the famous khalifas, of Shah Rukn-e Alam, with Maulana Zahir al-Din, Sultan Hamid al-Din Hakim, Shaykh Fakhr al-Din Iraqi and Shaykh Sama al-Din Dehlvi (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 165, folio 153, حاشیہ 1). Another quotes him on the teaching of Shaikh al-Arif at Multan, "شیخ العارف ہر مبتدی و منتہی کو بلا کسی امتیاز کے تعلیم دیتے تھے": Shaikh al-Arif taught every beginner and every advanced student without any distinction (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 161, folio 149, حاشیہ 1).

Sharib's account runs along the same line and goes further. "آپ اپنے والد ماجد سے سلسلہ سہروردی میں بیعت ہوئے": he took bai'at from his father in the Suhrawardi silsila (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 135, folio 131). Then, "پھر آپ نے اپنے چچا حضرت سید صدر الدین المعروف بہ محمد غوث سے خرقہ پہنا۔ حضرت شیخ رکن الدین ابو الفتح رحمۃ اللہ علیہ سے بھی آپ نے خرقہ خلافت پہنا": he put on the khirqa from his uncle, Hazrat Sayyid Sadr al-Din, known as Muhammad Ghaus, and from Hazrat Shaykh Rukn al-Din Abu al-Fath, God's mercy be upon him, he also put on the khirqa of khilafat (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 135, folio 131). He took bai'at and khilafat, Sharib adds, in fourteen *khanwadas*: "آپ نے چودہ خانوادوں میں بیعت اور خلافت حاصل کی" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 135, folio 131, حاشیہ 4).

Sharib records khirqas of other kinds as well. He claimed the *khirqa-yi siyadat*, the robe of his sayyid descent, from Hazrat Ali, "کہ جو خرقہ ان کو سرور عالم حضرت محمد مصطفیٰ صلی اللہ علیہ وسلم سے ملا تھا", the khirqa that had come to Ali from the Prophet; and he wore the khirqa of Baha al-Din Zakariya, which he had received from his father (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 135, folio 131). Two khirqas came to him in dreams, from Rukn al-Din Abu al-Fath and from Nizam al-Din Auliya, and each was found on his head when he woke (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 135, folio 131). On his return he received a further khirqa of khilafat from Nasir al-Din Chiragh Dehlvi (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 137, folio 133).

A footnote lists, without stories, the men from whom the khirqa came to him: Shaykh Qawam al-Din, the khalifa of Rukn al-Din Abu al-Fath; Qutb al-Din Munawwar; Nasir al-Din Chiragh Dehlvi; "شیخ مکہ امام عبداللہ یافعی" … "شیخ مدینہ حضرت عبداللہ المطری", the shaykh of Mecca, Imam Abdullah Yafi'i, and the shaykh of Medina, Hazrat Abdullah al-Matri; a qutb of Aden whose name is uncertain in the printed text; Shaykh Ishaq Kazaruni; Shaykh Amin al-Din; Shaykh Hamid al-Din; a khalifa of Shihab al-Din Suhrawardi, Shaykh Sharaf al-Din Mahmud Shah, whose name is uncertain in the printed text; Sayyid Ahmad Rifa'i; Shaykh Najm al-Din Kubra; Hazrat Khizr; and Shaykh Nur al-Din, among others (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 135–136, folios 131–132, حاشیہ 5).

Rizvi, too, has him become his father's disciple and take initiation from Rukn al-Din, and then adds a second order: "he received a formal religious education, and then became his father's disciple. Later he obtained initiation into the Suhrawardiyya order from Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 299, folio 277), and "After his return to Uch, Saiyid Jalalu'd-Din Bukhari went to Delhi and was initiated personally into the Chishtiyya order by Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 299, folio 277). Rizvi makes him the man who began in India the practice of initiation into more than one order: "The practice seems to have been started in India by the Suhrawardi, Makhdum Jahaniyan Saiyid Jalal Bukhari who, as we shall see, also obtained initiation in the Chishtiyya order" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 292, folio 272). Elsewhere he calls him 'the famous Suhrawardi' (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 429, folio 405) and 'the celebrated Suhrawardi saint' (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 22, folio 8).

Rozehnal, quoting the *Sirat-i Zauqi*, says that Jalal ad-Din "completed his training under Shaykh Rukn ad-Din Abul Fatah Suhrawardi and Nasir ad-Din Chirag-i Delhi. Thus both the Suhrawardi and Chishti systems were followed in the family." (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 57, folio 47). Ernst and Lawrence call him a Suhrawardi "who also had close ties to the Chishtiyya" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 95, folio 81). Bhandari, after making him his father's disciple and khalifa, writes "از شیخ رکن الدین ابو الفتح سهروردی نیز خلافت یافت", he received khilafat also from Shaykh Rukn al-Din Abu al-Fath Suhrawardi, and "در دهلی شیخ نصیر الدین چراغ دهلی را دیده فراوان فیض اندوخت", at Delhi he saw Shaykh Nasir al-Din Chiragh-i Dihli and gathered abundant grace (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527). Boivin calls him "the great Sohrawardi Sufi" and "one of the masters of the second Sohrawardiyya period" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 56, folio 43).

## The Hijaz and the Travels

Lahori follows the stay at Mecca with a wider journey: "بقدم تجرید سیر تمام روئے زمین کی کی", he toured the whole face of the earth on the foot of detachment, drawing *faiz* from hundreds of guiding saints (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 168, folio 156). Rizvi reports a like figure from Jamali: "According to Jamali, during his travels the Saiyid was met by more than three hundred important sufis" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 299, folio 277).

Sharib gives the Hijaz years a length and an end: "چھ سال مدینہ اور مکہ میں رہ کر 752ھ میں وطن واپس ہوئے", after six years in Medina and Mecca he returned home in 752 AH (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 137, folio 133, حاشیہ 7). On that journey he met "سید صدر الدین کماش [OCR?]" and "شیخ حمید الدین بن محمود الحنی [OCR?] سمرقندی", both names uncertain in part in the printed text (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 137, folio 133, حاشیہ 8). At Medina, leading the prayer in the Prophet's mosque in the imam's absence, "آپ بپاس ادب اس جگہ سے جہاں سرور عالم صلی اللہ علیہ وسلم کھڑے ہوتے تھے۔ ایک صف پیچھے کھڑے ہوئے": out of reverence he stood one row behind the place where the Prophet used to stand (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 136, folio 132).

Rizvi doubts the wider travels. "Although it is believed that the Saiyid went abroad on three different occasions, the validity of such an assertion is doubtful. His travels mentioned in the Safar Nama-i Makhdum Jahaniyan (Book of the Travels of Makhdum Jahaniyan) are mostly legendary and all of a later date" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 300, folio 278). He also writes that "It seems likely that for most of the reign of Sultan Muhammad bin" Tughluq "the Saiyid lived abroad and his" khanqah "was administered by his brother" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 299–300, folios 277–278). The wonders that the *Mahbubiyya* attaches to his journeys are given under 'Legends and Miracles'.

## The Sultans of Delhi

Three books record that Sultan Muhammad bin Tughluq gave him office. Sharib writes "سلطان محمد تغلق شاہ کے عہد میں آپ کو منصب شیخ الاسلامی و مسند خانقاہ محمدی پیش کیا گیا۔ آپ نے عہدہ قبول فرمایا۔ کچھ عرصے تک منصب شیخ الاسلامی و مسند خانقاہ محمدی پر سیوستان اور اس کے مضافات میں فائز رہ کر اپنے فرائض بحسن و خوبی انجام دیتے رہے": in the reign of Sultan Muhammad Tughluq Shah the office of Shaikh al-Islam and the seat of the Khanqah-i Muhammadi were offered to him; he accepted, and for a time held them at Siwistan and its dependencies, discharging his duties well; he then resigned them at the order of Rukn al-Din Abu al-Fath (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 136, folio 132). Rizvi: "It would seem that Sultan Muhammad bin Tughluq appointed him the Shaikhu'l-Islam and made him the head of a" khanqah "in Siwistan (Sehwan) known as the Khanqah-i Muhammadi to which several villages were attached. However, Saiyid Jalalu'd-Din did not remain there long" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 299, folio 277). Boivin: "He was named shaykh al-islam by the sultan of Delhi Muhammad bin Tughluq." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 56, folio 43).

Under Muhammad's successor, Firuz, the books bring him often to the court. Sharib writes "سلطان فیروز تغلق آپ کا معتقد و منقاد تھا۔ اس کے عہد حکومت میں آپ کئی دفعہ اوچہ سے دہلی تشریف لائے": Sultan Firoz Tughluq was his devotee and obedient to him, and in his reign he came several times from Uch to Delhi; Sharib's footnote cites the Urdu translation of the *Akhbar al-Akhyar* (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 137, folio 133, حاشیہ 9). Rizvi writes: "During the time that Sultan Firuz Shah Tughluq ruled in Delhi the Saiyid remained mainly at Uch, only visiting the capital periodically. One of his known visits took place between 8 Rabi' II 781/24 July 1379 and Muharram 782/April 1380" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 300, folio 278), and elsewhere he dates that visit to the day: "The account dates from Sunday 8 Rabi' II 781/24 July 1379 and ends on Tuesday 17 Muharram 782/23 April 1380, during the time of Shaikh's visit to Delhi." (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 22, folio 8).

Rizvi describes the Sultan's regard. "the Sultan gave Saiyid Jalalu'd-Din Bukhari a royal reception and continued to visit him in his" khanqah "throughout his stay. So great was the Saiyid's hold with the Sultan that all his recommendations were granted"; and "His assistance was also sought by the governors of Sind" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 301, folio 279). A letter that Rizvi quotes in S.H. Askari's translation says of the Sultan: "When for some days his holiness, Syed Jalalu'd-Din (Bukhari) may his resting place be blessed! came to him, he met him and derived many benefits from his association with the Syed"; and "He learnt to say the 'Chasht' or the morning prayer and 'Tahajjud' (night prayer) from the saintly saint after the latter's arrival (at Delhi from Uch, near Multan)" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 258, folio 238).

He used that regard for others. Rizvi relates that Sultan Firuz, advised by the *'ulama*, summoned "Shaikh Sharafu'd-Din from Bihar to account for his conduct. A farman was issued authorizing this, but was cancelled at the request of Makhdum-i Jahaniyan Saiyid Jalal Bukhari" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 251, folio 231); "It would, however, seem that the strong recommendations of Makhdum-i Jahaniyan, which the Sultan and the 'ulama' were unable to ignore, saved Shaikh Sharafu'd-Din from the axe" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 251–252, folios 231–232). "The abolition of about twenty-eight taxes by Sultan Firuz Shah largely imposed on towns throughout the empire may be ascribed to the influence of the Saiyid" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 301, folio 279).

The books credit him with the peace of Thatta. Rizvi: "In 763/1362 accompanied by a large army Sultan Firuz marched on Thatta"; "The Sammah ruler, Jam Jauna bin Babinya (756/1355—770/1369), sought to arrange peace-terms through Saiyid Jalalu'd-Din. It was finally agreed that Jam's son, Mani, should rule as a tributary of Sultan Firuz of Delhi. The peace was generally hailed as a triumph for Makhdum Jahaniyan" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 301, folio 279). Boivin dates the siege otherwise and calls the peacemaker a saint of Multan: "In 1367, his successor, Firuz Shah Tughluq, returned to lay siege to Thatha; Jam Juna I Samma would have to ask for the intercession of a saint of Multan", Sayyid Jalal al-Din Bukhari, "better known under the name of Makhdum Jahaniyyan Jahangasht (d. 1383)" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 189, folio 176).

Sharib tells of an intercession with the Sultan's minister. Khan Jahan, the wazir of Firoz, had imprisoned the son of a *muharrir*, a clerk, and the saint "انیس بار سفارش کرنے تشریف لے گئے", went nineteen times to plead for him and was not received. The twentieth time Khan Jahan sent word, "اے سید تم کو غیرت نہیں آتی", O Sayyid, have you no shame? He answered: "اے عزیز! میں جتنی بار آتا ہوں' مجھ کو ثواب ملتا ہے۔ میں چاہتا ہوں کہ مظلوم کو تیرے ہاتھ سے چھڑاؤں' تاکہ تو بھی درجہ ثواب کو پہنچے", O dear one, every time I come I earn merit; I wish to free the wronged man from your hand, so that you too may reach the rank of merit. Khan Jahan came to him "نگے سر' گلے میں رسی ڈالے ہوئے", bareheaded, with a rope round his neck, asked his pardon and became his murid, and "محرر کے بیٹے کو خلعت اور گھوڑا دے کر رہا کر دیا", released the clerk's son with a robe of honour and a horse; and when he offered the saint a *nazrana*, "آپ نے وہ نذرانہ محرر کے بیٹے کو دے دیا", he gave that offering to the clerk's son. Sharib's footnote cites the *Salik al-Salikin* (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 138, folio 134, حاشیہ 14).

In two passages on one page where Rizvi names him by the title alone, with no personal name, he is the measure of other men's standing at Delhi. "At that time the Makhdum Jahaniyan had gone to Delhi. The Sultan and his nobles used to pay their respects to the famous Suhrawardi and great glamour surrounded his personality. The Baba suggested to Shaikh Ahmad that he should become the disciple of the great Shaikh, but Ahmad refused to leave his pir"; and "At that time Makhdum Jahaniyan also happened to visit Delhi. He called on Ahmad-i Khattu and hugged him, telling him he reminded him of his friend, Baba Ishaq" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 429, folio 405).

## Teachings and Rulings

Rizvi gives his rulings. "A notorious puritan, Makhdum Jahaniyan strongly opposed the tradition which had developed around the Shab-i Barat festival which was held in the evening of the middle of Sha'ban, the eighth month in the Muslim Calendar, when fire works were exploded, graves were white-washed and lighted lamps were placed on them" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 302, folio 280). Muslims, he taught, "should pray and give the money, which otherwise would be used on crackers and lamps, to the poor"; "Only in India, he added, was there such a blatant disregard of the holy law, for such innovations were not to be found in Ghazna, Khurasan or Arabia"; "He also sternly disapproved of giving Allah such Hindi names as Thakur (Lord), Dhani (Rich) and Kartar (Creator)"; and he advised the *a'imma* "not to accept any payment which came from illegal sources of revenue" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 302, folio 280).

His rule on sufis and rulers set the direction of the visit. "Makhdum Jahaniyan urged that dervishes, sufis and the 'ulama' visit rulers, government officials and the rich in order to elicit assistance for the deprived and justified his own visits to Sultan Firuz on these grounds. Dervishes, however, should never allow such people to visit them at their" khanqahs; "if this did occur, the time should be beneficially used by preaching on the significance of the Shari'a" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 302, folio 280). Of royal stipends he ruled that "according to the books of Fatwas, it was makruh (disapproved) however it was not invalidated by the Shari'a" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 301, folio 279). "The Saiyid preferred to call his disciples Akhi (brothers)", and Rizvi traces the usage: "Saiyid Jalalu'd-Din in fact borrowed the ideal both from the Akhi system, contained in the Anatolian craft guilds, and the Akhi and Futuwwa organizations among dervish brotherhoods in Khurasan and Transoxiana" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 303, folio 281). In the *Khulasatu'l-Alfaz*, Rizvi writes, he taught that "beginners on the mystic path should not concern themselves with trying to understand the philosophy of the Wahdat al-Wujud as it was for experts only" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 300, folio 278).

Sharib sets out his teachings under named heads, on the *khirqa*, on *sabr*, on *taqwa*, on *mashikhat*, and on *shari'at*, *tariqat* and *haqiqat*, with a list of twenty sayings and a *wird* (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 138–140, folios 134–136). Whenever he took a murid, he would say "میں صرف وکیل ہوں", I am only a *wakil* (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 138, folio 134). He distinguished two kinds of khirqa: "خرقہ دو طرح کا ہے۔ خرقہ تصوف اور خرقہ تشبہ خرقہ تصوف خرقہ صحبت ہے اور خرقہ تشبہ خرقہ تبرک کو کہتے ہیں' اس میں صحبت کی شرط نہیں": the *khirqa-yi tasawwuf* is the robe of companionship, *suhbat*, while the *khirqa-yi tashabbuh* is the khirqa of *tabarruk*, of blessing, in which companionship is not a condition (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 138, folio 134).

He laid down three conditions for being a shaykh at all: "مشیخیت کے لئے تین شرطیں ہیں۔ اگر یہ تینوں نہ ہوں تو مشیخیت درست نہیں۔ اول شرط یہ ہے کہ وہ تین علم یعنی شریعت' و حقیقت کا عالم ہو۔ دوسری شرط یہ ہے کہ اس زمانے کے علماء اس کو قبول کریں اور اس کے معتقد مرید ہوں۔ تیسری شرط یہ ہے کہ اس کو سوائے حق تعالیٰ کے اور کوئی طلب نہ ہو". There are three conditions for shaykhhood, and without all three it is not valid: first, that he be learned in the three sciences, that is, in *shari'at* and *haqiqat*; second, that the *'ulama* of his age accept him and become his believing murids; third, that he seek nothing but God Most High. The first condition, as printed, promises three sciences and names two (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 139, folio 135). His daily *wird* included the words "یاحیی یاقیوم اسم اعظم ہے", *Ya Hayy, Ya Qayyum* is the Greatest Name (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 140, folio 136).

Sharib describes his devotions in numbers: "دن و رات میں پانسو رکعت نماز پڑھنا آپ کا معمول تھا۔ آپ ساتوں قرات کے قاری تھے۔ آخری عمر میں بھی سو رکعت نماز پڑھتے تھے۔ ذکر جہر بہت کرتے تھے۔ لباس آپ کا معمولی تھا": five hundred *rak'ats* of prayer by day and night were his custom; he was a reciter in all seven *qira'at*; even in his last years he prayed a hundred rak'ats; he performed much loud *dhikr*; and his dress was plain (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 137, folio 133). Of the *sama'* Sharib records both opinions and chooses neither: "سماع سنتے تھے۔ بعض کہتے ہیں کہ سماع نہیں سنتے تھے", he listened to sama'; some say he did not (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 137, folio 133, حاشیہ 11). His humility reached to the turban: "آپ میں انکساری اس درجہ تھی کہ ٹوپی کے ساتھ نماز پڑھتے تھے' آپ فرماتے تھے کہ عمامہ خاص مردوں کے لئے ہے اور میں مرد نہیں ہوں", he prayed in a cap, saying that the turban is for men alone, and "I am not a man" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 138, folio 134).

## Writings and Discourses

Schimmel calls him "a prolific writer in almost all religious fields" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354). Rizvi describes four collections of his teachings. The Delhi discourses were "the work of his disciple Abu 'Abdu' llah 'Ala'u'd-Din 'Ali bin Sa'd bin Ashraf bin 'Ali al-Quraishi al-Husaini. The Makhdum Jahaniyan encouraged the author in his undertaking and clarified particular points he could not understand. The work was called the Khulasatu'l-Alfaz Jami'u'l-'Ulum." Then: "Another of the Makhdum Jahaniyan's disciples, Makhdumzada 'Abdu'llah, compiled a summary of discourses delivered by the great saint on different occasions, but these lack the pithiness of those featured in the Khulasatu'l-Alfaz. The work is entitled the Siraju'l-Hidaya." And: "The Khazinat al-Fawa'id al Jalalaliyya, composed by Ahmad bin Ya'qub in 752/1351 and the Khazinah-i Jalali by Abu'l Fazl bin Ziya' are compendiums of the teachings of the Makhdum Jahaniyan which, however, lack the personal touches contained in malfuzat." (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 22, folio 8). He returns to the *Khulasatu'l-Alfaz* and its doctrine later in the book (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 300, folio 278).

A fifth work tells of his family: "The Mahbubiyya by Najmu'd-Din Yusuf bin Ruknu'd-Din Muhammad Nia'mu'llah Gardezi covers various stories in the life of the Makhdum Jahaniyan, Saiyid Jalal Bukhari, and the latter's son, grandson and great-grandson." (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 24, folio 10). The son, grandson and great-grandson are not named there. Rizvi's bibliography lists two anonymous manuscripts, a *Manaqib-i Makhdum-i Jahaniyan*, Ivanow 1210, and a *Safar Nama-i Makhdum Jahaniyan* in the India Office, D.P. 1107 (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 448, folio 424).

The list of sources compiled by the editor of Lahori's book, Muhammad Iqbal Mujaddidi, includes a manuscript of a collection of the saint's sayings in his own possession, "تاج الدین احمد بربنی : سراج الہدایہ (ملفوظات حضرت مخدوم جہانیاں بخاری[OCR?]) مملوکہ محمد اقبال مجددی", the *Siraj al-Hidaya*, the *malfuzat* of Hazrat Makhdum Jahaniyan Bukhari, listed under the name of Taj al-Din Ahmad Barbani, owned by Muhammad Iqbal Mujaddidi, the saint's name within the parentheses uncertain in the printed text (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 292, folio 280). He lists, too, two later books on him: "سخاوت مرزا : تذکرہ مخدوم جہانیاں۔ دکن ۱۹۶۲ء", Sakhawat Mirza's *Tazkira-yi Makhdum Jahaniyan*, Deccan, 1962 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 302, folio 290), and "محمد ایوب قادری : مخدوم جہانیاں جہان گشت بخاری ؒ۔ کراچی ۱۹۶۳ء", Muhammad Ayub Qadri's *Makhdum Jahaniyan Jahan Gasht Bukhari*, Karachi, 1963 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 303, folio 291). The editor names nine further sources on him (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 169, folio 157). For the town as a whole his list includes Ali Asghar Gilani's genealogies of the shaykhs of Uch, "علی اصغر گیلانی : شجرۃ الانوار (انساب مشائخ اوچ) ۱۱۹۳ھ کتابخانہ دانشگاہ پنجاب", dated 1193 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 294, folio 282).

## Influence and the Bukhari Line

Makhdoom Jahaniyan Jahangasht carried the prestige of the Uch Bukhari sayyids to its height. A prolific guide with a wide following, he strengthened the Suhrawardi presence across the Punjab and Sindh, and his descendants and disciples spread the *Jalali* Bukhari line far and wide — a lineage of sayyids who would remain religiously and politically prominent for centuries and whose branches reached into many regions of the subcontinent. His memory is bound to that of his grandfather Surkh-Posh and to the whole flowering of sanctity that made Uch Sharif a spiritual capital of the south.

Rizvi writes of the Suhrawardiyya in his time that the order "spread from Uch to Gujarat, the Panjab and Kashmir even extending its influence to the Delhi of Sultan Firuz Shah" Tughluq (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 299, folio 277). "His successor was his brother, Sadru'd-Din, who achieved fame under his nicknames, Raju, and Qattal (Slayer), for his militant evangelism" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 303, folio 281), and the Suhrawardi silsila "spread further through both the sons and grandsons of Makhdum Jahaniyan and Saiyid Raju Qattal" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 303–304, folios 281–282). Lahori's book, too, records in a neighbouring notice, entry no. 109, that his *sajjada* passed to his brother and not to his son (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 168 area).

Sharib names two of his khalifas: "قاضی عبدالمالک المعروف بہ شاہ اجل اور سید صدر الدین المعروف بہ راجو قتال جو آپ کے چھوٹے بھائی ہیں", Qazi Abd al-Malik, known as Shah Ajal, and Sayyid Sadr al-Din, known as Raju Qattal, his younger brother (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 137, folio 133). Rizvi's Shaikh Qiwamu'd-Din "went to Uch where he obtained initiation from Saiyid Jalalu'd-Din Makhdum Jahaniyan of the Suhrawardiyya order" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 293, folio 273).

Rizvi follows his disciples further afield. "A more famous Suhrawardiyya sufi in the region was Shaikh Rajgiri, to whom Makhdum Jahaniyan gave the title 'Akhi.'"; a native of Daryabad in Awadh, Akhi was given the Makhdum's khirqa "when he first settled at Kanauj"; and "Kalpi became such a centre due to the prestige of Shaikh Siraj Sukhta, a favourite Imam of Makhdum Jahaniyan" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 306, folio 284). In Kashmir "the Suhrawardiyya order was revived in Kashmir by Saiyid Muhammad Isfahani, a disciple of Makhdum Jahaniyan", and "Saiyid Jamalu'd-Din, a sufi of the Makhdum Jahaniyan's order, made a spectacular impact on the sufi scene in Kashmir" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 312, folio 290). On a page where Rizvi gives only the name Saiyid Jalal Bukhari, which this saint shared with his grandfather, Shaikh Yusuf Budh "was initiated into the Suhrawardiyya order by Saiyid Jalal Bukhari and Shaikh Raju Quttal" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 295, folio 275).

Three books bring a Simnani to him. Ernst and Lawrence have Simnani move, after Transoxiana, "on to the Punjab to spend time with the Suhrawardi saint Makhdum-i Jahaniyan Jahangasht" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 93, folio 79). Rizvi writes: "Then taking the route through Multan and Uch to India, Simnani lived for a period with Makhdum Jahaniyan" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 287, folio 267). Sharib has Ashraf Jahangir Simnani reach Uch, اوچ, where he "حضرت مخدوم جہانیاں جہاں گشت کے روحانی فیوض و برکات سے مستفید و مستفیض ہوئے", drew benefit from the spiritual grace and blessings of Hazrat Makhdum Jahaniyan Jahangasht (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 146, folio 142).

Other chapters of Sharib's book mention him in passing, and they are given here as they stand, as passing mentions. They make him a khalifa of Rukn al-Din (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 116, folio 112). They call a Shaykh Qawam al-Din "جو حضرت مخدوم جہانیاں کے مرید اور خلیفہ ہیں' اپنے زمانے کے مشہور بزرگ تھے", the murid and khalifa of Hazrat Makhdum Jahaniyan and a famous elder of his time, though that page gives this Qawam al-Din no town, no mazar, no year of death and no silsila (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 183, folio 179). They make Shah Ajmal his murid and khalifa in the silsilas "سلسلہ چشتیہ جمانیہ[OCR?] و قادریہ و سہروردیہ میں", the Chishti, Qadiri and Suhrawardi, with a word after the Chishti whose reading is uncertain in the printed text (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 191, folio 187). They call Qutb Alam "مخدوم جہانیاں جہاں گشت حضرت سید جلال الدین بخاری کے پوتے", the grandson of Makhdum Jahaniyan Jahangasht Hazrat Sayyid Jalal al-Din Bukhari (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 190, folio 186), and Shah Alam the great-grandson of "مخدوم جہانیاں جہاں گشت کے بیٹے حضرت سید جلال الدین بخاری", Hazrat Sayyid Jalal al-Din Bukhari, the son of Makhdum Jahaniyan Jahangasht (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 195, folio 191).

Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti calls Sain Qutb Shah "سید حسینی بخاری اولاد حضرت مخدوم جہانیان", a Husaini Bukhari sayyid of the line of Hazrat Makhdum Jahaniyan (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 235), and places the saint himself, by name only, in the spiritual genealogy of the Rasul Shahi faqirs of Lahore, "نقل شجرہ فقرای رسول شاہی موجودین لاہور", as the master of "سید براہو[OCR?] بخاری" and the disciple of a Hazrat Sayyid whose name is illegible (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 154). In Lahori's book he stands seventh among the ancestors of Mauj Darya of Lahore, with Jalal al-Din Surkh-Posh ninth (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 181), and in the chain of Mian Wada "سید جلال الدین مخدوم جہانیاں" stands beside "شیخ رکن الدین ابوالفتح ملتانی" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 188).

Later saints claimed descent from him. Boivin writes that "Qutub Ali Shah was a descendant in direct line from the great Sohrawardi Sufi Jalal al-Din Bukhari Makhdum-i Jahaniyyan Jahangasht (1308–84), who was one of the masters of the second Sohrawardiyya period, then based in Utch near Bahawalpur in Punjab." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 56, folio 43). Ernst and Lawrence write that the biography of Zauqi Shah lists "each of his male ancestors for 36 generations going back to the Prophet Muhammad. Among these forebears was Jalal ad-Din Bukhari Makhdum-i Jahaniyan, the famous fourteenth-century Suhrawardi saint of the Punjab, who also had close ties to the Chishtiyya", and that "After the fifteenth century, this lineage fell into obscurity until it suddenly reemerged into history during the British Raj" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 95, folio 81).

Two books speak of a following of naked ascetics. Rizvi writes: "The custom and ideas of the Qalandariyya and the Nath yogis also had considerable impact on the beliefs of a section of the followers of Makhdum Jahaniyan Saiyid Jalal Bukhari. Unlike other sufis they adopted Shi'ism and in the same manner as the Madaris went naked and to warm themselves sat before fires. Although their hair was unmatted, they shaved their heads, beards, moustaches and eyebrows and spent their time wandering from place to place"; "When they came into the order they offered all their material goods to their preceptors, and in return were given a cap and the spiritual tree of the order which was worn on their heads or hung round their necks"; and he calls them 'Jalali saints' (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 342, folio 320). Rizvi's name for their master joins the grandson's title to the name Jalal that both men bore, and his account is given here as his own; the question is among those set out below. Bhandari writes "درویشان ملنگ و دوداے سراپا برهنه از مریدان ان جناب درهرجانب هستند و ذکر جلی مینمایند": *malang* dervishes, and others the text calls دوداے, naked from head to foot, are among the murids of that honoured man in every quarter, and perform the loud *dhikr* (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527).

## The Shrine

The shrine of Makhdoom Jahaniyan Jahangasht at Uch Sharif is a busy darbar amid the town's famous complex of tombs, kept by his descendants. It stands close to the celebrated tiled mausolea of Uch, including that of his descendant Bibi Jawindi, in one of Pakistan's richest concentrations of sacred architecture.

Three books place his grave at Uch. Lahori writes "روضہ بمقام اوچ زیارت گاہ خلق ہے", his *roza* at the place Uch is a place of pilgrimage for the people, and gives it no quarter, no date of building and no custodian (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 169, folio 157). Rizvi buries him in Uch, but nowhere describes the tomb, its builder or its date, its later custodians, an *ʿurs* or a sajjada-nashin (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 303, folio 281). Bhandari writes "درقصبه اوچ خوابگاه", his resting-place is in the town of Uch (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527).

Others do not, or place him elsewhere. Sharib's chapter records his death but never his grave, and what it gives of Uch is his birth there, his *i'tikaf* in its Jami Masjid and his journeys from Uch to Delhi (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 133, folio 129; Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 137, folio 133; Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 141, folio 137). Boivin names a base, not a tomb, "then based in Utch near Bahawalpur in Punjab" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 56, folio 43), and on another page calls him "a saint of Multan" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 189, folio 176). Rozehnal calls him "the famous fourteenth-century Suhrawardi master of Multan" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 57, folio 47). Usborne's later annotator, glossing the Jalal of Bukhara of Waris Shah's romance, writes that "Jalal Bukhari, who originally came from Uch, is also buried at Multan" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 11, folio 10, note 2), and in another note calls him Makhdum Jahanian 'of Multan' (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 16, folio 15, note 10). Ernst and Lawrence place him in the Punjab and name no tomb, no Uch Sharif and no year of death (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 93, folio 79), and Schimmel names no shrine, site or tomb (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354).

Of his *khanqah* at Uch Rizvi records one visit. "Mir Saiyid 'Ali on his way to Kashmir passed through Uch and visited the" khanqah "of the Makhdum Jahaniyan. When Saiyid Makhdum Jahaniyan was informed of the visit of the Hamadani Saiyid, without taking much notice he stated merely that Hamadan (All Knowing) was none but the Great Knower of the Invisible World, that is, God the Most High" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 313, folio 291). Sharib sets one of his stories in "اوچہ کی جامع مسجد", the congregational mosque of Uch (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 141, folio 137, حاشیہ 16).

Bibi Jawindi, whom the site's account calls his descendant, appears in Rozehnal's book in another relation: "the wife of the renowned fourteenth-century Suhrawardi shaykh, Sayyid Jalal ad-Din Bukhari (1303–1383)" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 21, folio 10). Rozehnal gives this Sayyid Jalal ad-Din no title, and his dates stand beside the site's 1308–1384 for this saint; which Jalal he means is set out under 'Where the Sources Disagree'.

## Devotion and Practice

The shrine is visited year-round by pilgrims seeking the blessing of the 'Master of the World.' It maintains the customary devotions — prayer, qawwali, and the free kitchen — and its annual urs draws crowds from across the region. The sources consulted record no date for the *ʿurs*.

The books record devotions attached to him rather than to his tomb. Sharib tells of a cure prescribed by way of another saint's grave. When the governor of Uch had been driven mad, and his mother brought him and asked pardon, "آپ نے اس کو ہدایت فرمائی کہ سومرہ کو غسل دے کر اور کپڑے پہنا کر حضرت جمال الدین خنداں کے مزار پر لے جائے۔ اور پھر آپ کے پاس لائے": the saint told the governor's mother to bathe Sumra, clothe him, take him to the mazar of Hazrat Jamal al-Din Khandan, and then bring him back; "اس کی ماں نے ایسا ہی کیا", his mother did so, and after Sumra's apology "آپ کی دعا سے وہ بالکل اچھا ہو گیا", through the saint's prayer he was entirely restored (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 141, folio 137, حاشیہ 16). Sharib does not say in which town that mazar stands (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 141, folio 137).

The saint, in Rizvi's account, condemned the grave customs of the Shab-i Barat, the whitewashing of graves and the lamps placed on them (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 302, folio 280). Rizvi's Jalali following received on joining the order "a cap and the spiritual tree of the order which was worn on their heads or hung round their necks" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 342, folio 320), and Bhandari's naked dervishes among his murids perform the *dhikr-i jali*, "ذکر جلی مینمایند" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527).

## Legends and Miracles

Sharib tells how he came by his title. On a night of Eid he kept vigil at the mazar of Baha al-Din Zakariya, and then presented himself at the mazars of "صدر الدین عارف اور [OCR?] حضرت رکن الدین ابو الفتح"; at each he asked for an *idi*, an Eid gift, "ہر جگہ عیدی مانگی", and from each the title Makhdum Jahaniyan was conferred on him (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 133–134, folios 129–130, حاشیہ 2). Sharib does not name the town of the three tombs on that page.

The stories begin in childhood. At seven, given "چھوارے", dates, from a "خوان" set before Shaykh Jamal al-Din Khandan, he ate them "مع گٹھلیوں کے", stones and all, and explained "اس میں بھی برکت ہے اور پھینکنا بے ادبی ہے": there is blessing in this too, and to throw it away is disrespect (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 134, folio 130). Of his embrace a saying ran: "آپ جب کبھی کسی سے معانقہ فرماتے تو جو نعمت اس کے پاس ہوتی' اسی وقت جذب کر لیتے", whenever he embraced anyone he drew into himself at that moment whatever gift that person had; and Sharib explains it at once, with his own "یعنی", that is: "آپ اس قدر توجہ اور خدمت سے کام لیتے کہ وہ شخص بے اختیار ہو کر آپ کو اپنی ہر نعمت دے دیتا", he showed such attention and service that the person, unable to help himself, would give him every gift he had (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 134–135, folios 130–131, حاشیہ 3). Sharib's source here is the Urdu translation of the *Akhbar al-Akhyar*.

At Medina, on the authority of the *Siyar al-Arifin*, he said at the grave "السلام علیک یا جدی", peace be upon you, O my grandfather, and an answer came to him from there: "وعلیک السلام یا ولدی", and upon you be peace, O my son (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 136, folio 132, حاشیہ 6).

One of Sharib's stories is set at Uch. "ایک سال ماہ رمضان میں آپ نے اوچہ کی جامع مسجد میں اعتکاف فرمایا۔ ایک دن اوچہ کا حاکم جس کا نام سومرہ تھا' جامع مسجد میں آیا۔ آپ کے پاس بہت لوگ جمع ہو گئے' اس نے سب کو جامع مسجد سے باہر نکال دیا": one Ramadan he kept *i'tikaf* in the Jami Masjid of Uch; the governor of Uch, whose name was Sumra, came into the mosque, where many people had gathered about the saint, and turned them all out (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 141, folio 137, حاشیہ 16). The saint said "اے سومرہ! کیا تو دیوانہ ہو گیا ہے کہ درویشوں کو تنگ کرتا ہے", O Sumra, have you gone mad, that you trouble the dervishes; and, Sharib writes, "آپ کا یہ فرمانا تھا کہ سومرہ دیوانہ ہو گیا", no sooner had he said it than Sumra went mad (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 141, folio 137). The cure that followed, by way of the mazar of Jamal al-Din Khandan, is told under 'Devotion and Practice'. Sharib cites the *Salik al-Salikin* for the story.

At Delhi, on the authority of the Persian *Tarikh-i Firishta*, people distressed by drought asked for his prayer, and "اتنی بارش ہوئی کہ لوگوں نے عاجز ہو کر بارش کے بند ہونے کی آپ سے دعا کرائی' آپ نے دعا کی اور بارش رک گئی": so much rain fell that the people, overwhelmed, asked him to pray for it to stop; he prayed, and the rain stopped (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 141, folio 137).

One story turns on a moment's distraction. His youngest son, a child of four, played near his prayer-mat during the *chasht* prayer and drew his attention. Afterwards he said to سید شمس الدین, Sayyid Shams al-Din: "مجھے اس لڑکے کا زندہ رہنا مشکل نظر آتا ہے' کیوں کہ عین نماز میں میری طبیعت اس کی طرف مائل تھی", it seems hard to me that this boy will live, for in the very midst of the prayer my heart inclined towards him; and "ظہر کے وقت اس لڑکے کو بخار ہوا اور اسی رات اس کا انتقال ہو گیا", at the time of the noon prayer the boy fell into a fever, and that same night he died (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 140–141, folios 136–137).

In a neighbouring chapter Sharib tells of the saint of Panipat that he reached Delhi "بقوت روحانی ایک ساعت میں", by spiritual power in a single hour, found Makhdoom Jahaniyan "حالت نزع میں", in the throes of death, prayed for him, and "اپنی عمر کے چند سال ان کو دے کر", gave him some years of his own life, and returned to Panipat in a moment (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 131, folio 127). The same event is told once earlier, as Firoz Shah's information (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 129, folio 125, حاشیہ 7). Lahori's notice, too, records a miracle connected with a *sanad* at Medina; its details are not given in the material consulted for this article (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 168–169, folios 156–157).

Rizvi reports the wonders of the *Mahbubiyya* and disowns them in the same paragraph. Its stories "should not be accepted literally"; in them the Saiyid "even performed the rare and hazardous journey to China and Mongolia, at the same time converting the local inhabitants en route"; they are "stock-in-trade anecdotes of Saiyid Jalalu'd-Din Bukhari's encounter with yogis, all whom he ultimately converted to Islam"; and "the Saiyid travelled through Ceylon where, according to the author, Islam had failed to penetrate. There again he was reported to have converted a yogi" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 300, folio 278). Of the saints of Uch generally he writes that "all the stories dealing with three generations of these 'sufis in Uch recount conflicts with Hindu Yogis. Although the ultimate success in the performance of supernatural feats is ascribed to the Suhrawardis, that of the yogis is not to be underestimated. Habitually, the stories end with the Islamization of the vanquished in various types of competitions" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 304, folio 282).

Conversions are ascribed to him. "It is believed that the Sumirahs of the lower Sind who were Isma'ilis, adopted Sunnism under the influence of the Saiyid. A Jat chief of Sind, later named 'Abdu'llah, is also said to have embraced Islam through a supernatural feat performed by Makhdum Jahaniyan" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 301, folio 279).

Rizvi sets a death-bed scene at Uch. "The Hindu, Nawahun, visited Makhdum Jahaniyan on his death-bed and while praying for his recovery stated that the Saiyid was the seal of the saints, just as the Prophet Muhammad was the seal of the prophets"; Nawahun was "the darogha of Uch", whom Rizvi's footnote explains as "A revenue official entrusted to keep accounts in Persian" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 301, folio 279). The Saiyid "concluded that Nawahun's statement amounted to a protestation of faith"; in what followed, Saiyid Raju "retorted that the Shaikh was dishonest and would die for it. Instantly Shaikh Muhammad was seized with a violent stomach-ache from which he died. Nawahun was handed over to Saiyid Raju who again refused to embrace Islam and was executed" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 302, folio 280).

His blessing reached through his disciples. "The Sultan sought the protection of Shaikh Siraj Sukhta, a disciple of the Makhdum Jahaniyan, who gave him his shirt to wear. The holy shirt cured the Sultan"; the Sultan was Qadir Shah (1410-35), ruler of Kalpi (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 340, folio 318). Of his Jalali following Rizvi writes, after the *Dabistan-i Mazahib*: "Jalali saints who had achieved a certain standard of perfection like the Haidaris would swallow snakes saying they were 'Ali's fish and scorpions were called 'Ali's prawns" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 342, folio 320).

In the romance of the Punjab he is one of the Five Pirs who come to the aid of the lover. Shackle's note to Bulleh Shah names "Jalal ud Din Makhdum Jahaniyan of Uch" among them, and credits the Five Pirs together with special powers and with miraculous intervention for Ranjha in Varis Shah's *Hir*; the note gives him no date, tomb or order (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 186, folio 161, n. 2 on 122).

## In Verse

The sources consulted quote no verse of his own, and no Persian or Urdu verse about him. His place in poetry is in the romance. Waris Shah's *Hir and Ranjha*, in Usborne's English, names a Sayyid Jalal of Bukhara 'whom men also call Makhdum Jahanian' among the holy men whose help Ranjha seeks; the sentence begins on the page before:

*Multan, and Sayyid Jalal of Bukhara, whom men also call Makhdum Jahanian. Ranjha saw by their countenances that they were holy men and besought their help.* (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 11, folio 10)

In the same passage his gift is a dagger, 'Sayyid Jalal a dagger' (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 11, folio 10), and a later page returns to it:

*and he grasped the dagger given him by Makhdum Jahanian* (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 50, folio 49)

Two pages on, in the fight with the lion, the poem gives the cudgel to Jahanian and the dagger to Sayyid Jalal:

*Then Ranjha took the cudgel of Jahanian and thrust it into the side of the lion and he drove the dagger of Sayyid Jalal Bokhari into his belly.* (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 52, folio 51)

The book does not reconcile the two pages. Its annotator lists him among the 'spiritual celebrities' of the Five Pirs (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 16, folio 15, note 10). The romance gives him no date, no order and no seat at Uch Sharif, and never the title Jahangasht.

Bulleh Shah's lyrics, in Shackle's translation, invoke the Five Pirs twice (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 122; Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 136), and a note of Shackle's on the Five Pirs names among them 'Jalal ud Din Makhdum Jahaniyan of Uch' (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 186, folio 161, n. 2 on 122).

## Setting: Uch Sharif

The books' accounts of the town speak of Uch as a whole, not of this shrine. Rozehnal, visiting "the nearby town of Uch Sharif" in April 1996, "spent a day wandering through the magnificent blue and white tiled mosques and desolate tombs of this ancient center of learning, trade, and pilgrimage built on a hill overlooking the confluence of the Sutlej and Chenab rivers"; of its tombs he names only Bibi Jawindi's (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 21, folio 10). He places it as "an ancient city located seventy miles southwest of Multan in Pakistan" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 210, folio 202); Schimmel places 'Ucch' 'northeast of Multan', with no district or province (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354); Boivin has 'Utch near Bahawalpur in Punjab' (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 56, folio 43) and indexes it 'Utch (Bahawalpur, Punjab)' (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 251, folio 238). Shemeem Burney Abbas calls Uch Gilanian "the city of saints in Bahawalpur", where Bulle Shah's family, Syeds, had settled and where he was born (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 107, folio not stated); she quotes the words "Thou art an inhabitant of Uch city [Uch literally means high/great]" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 104, folio not stated), and records Muhammad Bakhsh, of a hereditary family of qawwals at Kasur, saying "We came with Bulle Shah's family from Uch Sharif, and we have learned his poetry from our ancestors, who have always sung here" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 112, folio not stated). Bahawalpur, with Multan, is in her account the seat of the landowning Syeds who "support the shrines of their ancestors" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 168–169, folios not stated).

Rizvi tells a foundation legend of the town, of Shaikh Safiu'd-Din Kaziruni, who was ordered "to mount a camel and travel in whatever direction the animal led him; he was then to remain where the camel finally halted"; the camel halted "in the middle of a desert", and "the Shaikh founded a town later called Uch". Rizvi's verdict: "Although this story would appear mythical, it is typical of medieval tales relating to sufis." (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 129, folio 111). He tells, too, of a flying contest with a yogi at Uch (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 129–130, folios 111–112).

Uch was also a seat of the Gilani Qadiris. Rozehnal calls Shaykh Muhammad al-Husseini al-Jilani "the founder of the first Qadiriyya khanaqah at Uch", who, "A native of Turkey", "traveled to Khurasan and then Multan before settling with his family in Uch" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 259, folio 252); his son 'Abd al-Qadir Jilani Thani (d. 1533) is "the famous Qadiri saint of Uch Sharif", yet "there seems to be no extant archive of the saint, no textual record to document his life and legacy" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 210, folio 202). Lahori writes "حضرت کے سلسلہ میں سے حضرت سید محمد غوث اوچی جیلی گیلانی اول پنجاب میں آئے", that of the Qadiri line Sayyid Muhammad Ghaus Uchi Jili Gilani was the first to come to the Punjab, by way of Khurasan to Multan, settling at Uch; the modern editor's footnote adds "بقول علی اصغر گیلانی آپ ۸۸۷ھ میں اوچ میں داخل ہوئے", a date that is the editor's and not the author's (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 40, folio 28). Schimmel writes that "It was in Ucch that the first missionaries of the Qadiriyya settled in the late fifteenth century; from there this order spread into the Subcontinent, where it soon gained a firm footing and was carried to Indonesia and Malaysia" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354), and on another page that "the order was introduced in the late fourteenth century" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 278, folio 247). Rozehnal, in the same sentences in which he makes Makhdum-i Jahaniyan a master of Multan, calls Baha ad-Din Zakaria "the well-known Suhrawardi saint of Uch Sharif" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 57, folio 47). For further reading on the town Rozehnal points to Mas'ud Hasan Shahab's *Khitta-i pak Uch* (Bahawalpur: Urdu Academy, 1967/1993) and to the second volume of Rizvi's history (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 259, folio 252).

Chishti's *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* records the standing of the sayyids of Uch. The سادات اوچ are famous, he writes, and where a dispute over descent arises and they accept a man as صحیح النسب, the matter is closed: "پہر کسکی مجال نہین کہ دم مار سکے" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 526–527). The four sons of Hazrat Muhammad Ghaus "بمقام اوچ شریف آسودہ ہین", lie at Uch Sharif beside the tomb of Shah Muhammad Ghaus, and some of Abd al-Qadir Sani's descendants are "صاحب سجادہ اوچ مین" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 527). The elders of the Faqir family lived at Uch Sharif Sayyidan and served the sayyids of Uch (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 584). In the time of a Nawab whose title is printed نواب خان بہادر[OCR?], the reading uncertain, Chishti relates, the Nawab had at Uch Sharif a boil that would not heal; he went to the sayyids of Uch Sharif for prayer, and they named Ghulam Muhyi-ud-Din, the reading of whose name is uncertain, as a skilled physician, "حکیم حاذق"; the Nawab brought him to Lahore and was cured (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 584). According to "کتاب نسب نامہ حضرت پیر محمد شاہ صاحب", a Gilani ancestor came "اوچ شریف سے … بعہد اکبر شاہ", from Uch Sharif in the time of Akbar Shah (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 525).

Bhandari's history knows Uch as a town, "قصبه اوچ" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527), on the roads of armies. Shihab al-Din's first campaign took it and left a deputy there, and his second came again to Multan and Uch and went on to Gujarat (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 405); Mirza Pir Muhammad crossed the Sind and took Uch and Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 333); and on one march toward Jodhpur, Uch appears as "a dependency of Multan" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 285). In his account of suba Thatta, the province runs from "حدود ملتان و اوچ" to Thatta and Kach Makran (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529).

Haroon Khalid, driving to Uch Sharif, writes that "The boundary between Sindh and Punjab becomes a blur as we drive through Bahawalpur", and that "This is where the cultures of Punjab and Sindh merge. Some of the roadside hotels are called Punjab Hotel, while others are Sindh Hotel"; Iqbal Qaiser adds that "The Siraiki spoken in Multan is closer to Punjabi, while here it is more like Sindhi" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 276, folio 250). Khalid found the older city on a mound, with the 'massive havelis' "of the current caretakers of the shrine, the Gilanis and the Makhdooms" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 276, folio 250); the group of 'thirteenth and fourteenth century shrines' was reached through the market, and Iqbal Qaiser remarked, "This place has changed since the last time I came here" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 281, folio 255); on the mound were graves "which were covered arbitrarily with the blue and white Multani tiles that had been recovered from these historical shrines" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 285, folio not stated). The "River Sutlej, the lost river of Pakistan, used to flow from here before it was lost forever after the Indus Water Treaty was signed between India and Pakistan in 1960", and "Nanak must have seen these buildings intact with a mighty river flowing next to it" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 281–282, folios 255–256). Of Guru Nanak's traces in the town Khalid found a well "hidden within the field, shielded by a grove of trees. It was abandoned and juice tetrapaks and wrappers were floating in the black water", and beside it a grassy mound of which Iqbal Qaiser said, "This was the Gurdwara of Nanak" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 286, folio 260). Khalid places Nanak's visit to the town on his way to Sindh, "after he had settled down" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 278, folio 252), and in his reconstruction of it writes that "Uch Sharif was one of the most famous Muslim shrines in Southern Punjab and Nanak had arrived here only a day ago, travelling from Kartarpur" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 288, folio 262).

Khalid writes of the region that "The southern region of Punjab, where Uch Sharif lies, has more traces of religious and cultural syncretism compared to the northern and central region, where there is a greater prevalence of education and hence religious intolerance" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 277, folio 251). He reports the view of Ayesha Siddiqa that organisations of the Ahl-e-Hadith school "have seeped into the social fabric of southern Punjab, a region that has historically rejected such strands of Islam and adopted a syncretistic version of religion. Such Sufi shrines are a symbol of that strand of Islam", and of Tahir Kamran that the first step of such organisations is "to target such shrines and their guardians, who have become immensely rich feeding off the oppressed classes" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 284, folio 258).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **Which Jalal: the grandson or the grandfather.** The site's account distinguishes this saint, Sayyid Jalaluddin known as Makhdoom Jahaniyan Jahangasht, from his grandfather Jalaluddin Surkh-Posh Bukhari. Sharib's table of contents lists a chapter as "باب ۲۲ حضرت سید جلال الدین بخاری ۱۲۹", a name both men share (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 15); within it the subject is born at Uch in 707 AH, takes bai'at from his father in the Suhrawardi silsila and bears the laqab Makhdum Jahaniyan Jahangasht (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 133, folio 129; Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 135, folio 131). Bhandari's genealogy names the grandfather only as a bare "سید جلال بخاری": it does not call him Surkh-Posh, does not place his tomb at Uch and gives him no date (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527). Shackle's identification rests on the title alone: "Jalal ud Din Makhdum Jahaniyan of Uch", with no date, tomb or order (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 186, folio 161, n. 2 on 122).
- **Which Jalal: Rizvi's compound name.** Rizvi joins the grandson's title to the shared name as 'Makhdum Jahaniyan Saiyid Jalal Bukhari' (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 292, folio 272), and under that name he describes the Shi'i, naked, fire-sitting 'Jalali saints' and their snake-swallowing (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 342, folio 320). The site's account speaks of a *Jalali* Bukhari line spread by his descendants and disciples. On two other pages Rizvi gives only "Saiyid Jalal Bukhari" or "the Makhdum Jahaniyan" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 295, folio 275; Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 429, folio 405).
- **Which Jalal: Bibi Jawindi's husband.** Rozehnal's "Sayyid Jalal ad-Din Bukhari (1303–1383)" is the husband of Bibi Jawindi and is given no title (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 21, folio 10); the site's account gives this saint 1308–1384, and makes Bibi Jawindi his descendant.
- **Which Jalal: the romance.** In Usborne's English the romance's 'Sayyid Jalal of Bukhara' is one "whom men also call Makhdum Jahanian" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 11, folio 10); the book never uses the title Jahangasht, never names Uch Sharif as his seat, and gives no date or order.
- **A second Pir Jahanian.** Lahori's book gives the epithet 'Pir Jahanian' to a Shah Kamal Bukhari with a mazar at qasba Chunian (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 51, folio 39) and to "پیر سید کمال المشہور پیر جہانیاں" of Chunian (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 266, folio 254). The site's Makhdoom Jahaniyan is Sayyid Jalaluddin of Uch.
- **His father.** Lahori (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 168–169, folios 156–157), Sharib (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 133, folio 129) and Rizvi (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 299, folio 277) name Sayyid Ahmad Kabir. Bhandari's genealogy makes him the son of Sayyid Mahmud (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527). Rizvi names a son of the saint Saiyid Mahmud Nasiru'd-Din (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 304, folio 282).
- **His father's death.** The editor of Lahori's book gives it in a footnote as after 713 AH, the reading of the year uncertain (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 168, folio 156, حاشیہ 1), and in his appendix as about 731 AH / 1330–31 CE, by conjecture (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 349, folio 337).
- **His birth.** The site's account gives 1308, as do Rizvi (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 299, folio 277) and Boivin (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 56, folio 43). For the day, Lahori gives the night of Saturday, 4 Shaban 707 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 169, folio 157); Sharib and Rizvi give 15 Sha'ban 707 (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 133, folio 129, حاشیہ 1; Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 299, folio 277); Bhandari gives Shab-i Barat, 707 Hijri (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527). Rozehnal's Sayyid Jalal ad-Din Bukhari was born in 1303 (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 21, folio 10).
- **His death.** The site's account gives 1384. Lahori gives 10 Zil-Hijja 785 AH, the day of Eid al-Adha (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 169, folio 157); Rizvi 10 Zu'lhijja 785/3 February 1384 (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 303, folio 281); Bhandari a Wednesday, the Id-i Qurban, 785, with no era word (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527). Sharib gives 10 Zi al-Hijja 788 AH (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 137, folio 133, حاشیہ 10). Boivin gives '(1308–84)' on one page and '(d. 1383)' on another (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 56, folio 43; Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 189, folio 176); Schimmel gives 1383 in her text and 1382-83 in her index (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354; Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 518, folio 487); Rozehnal's Sayyid Jalal ad-Din Bukhari died in 1383 (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 21, folio 10).
- **Where he lies.** The site's account buries him at Uch, as do Lahori (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 169, folio 157), Rizvi (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 303, folio 281) and Bhandari (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527). Usborne's annotator says that Jalal Bukhari "originally came from Uch" and "is also buried at Multan" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 11, folio 10, note 2), and calls Makhdum Jahanian 'of Multan' (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 16, folio 15, note 10). Boivin calls him "a saint of Multan" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 189, folio 176) and Rozehnal a "master of Multan" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 57, folio 47), where Shackle has 'of Uch' (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 186, folio 161, n. 2 on 122). Sharib records no grave (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 137, folio 133).
- **His orders.** The site's account names the Suhrawardi discipline. Rizvi adds a Chishti initiation from Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din at Delhi (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 299, folio 277); Sharib a khirqa of khilafat from Nasir al-Din Chiragh Dehlvi and bai'at in fourteen khanwadas (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 137, folio 133; Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 135, folio 131, حاشیہ 4); Rozehnal training under Nasir ad-Din Chirag-i Delhi (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 57, folio 47); Bhandari grace from Nasir al-Din at Delhi (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527); Ernst and Lawrence "close ties to the Chishtiyya" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 95, folio 81). Lahori's four stages end with his teacher at Mecca and his tour of the earth, and do not name Nasir al-Din (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 168, folio 156).
- **His teacher in the Hijaz.** Lahori has him stay at Mecca with Shaykh Afif al-Din Abdullah Matri (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 168, folio 156); Sharib's list calls Abdullah al-Matri the shaykh of Medina, and Imam Abdullah Yafi'i the shaykh of Mecca (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 135–136, folios 131–132, حاشیہ 5).
- **His travels.** The site's account has him journey across the Muslim world and perform the Hajj many times. Lahori has him make the Hajj and then tour the whole face of the earth (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 168, folio 156); Sharib gives six years in Medina and Mecca and a return in 752 AH (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 137, folio 133, حاشیہ 7). Rizvi writes that the belief in three journeys abroad is "doubtful" and that the travels of the *Safar Nama* "are mostly legendary and all of a later date" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 300, folio 278).
- **The meaning of his titles.** The site's account renders *Makhdoom-i-Jahaniyan* 'the Master of the World' and *Jahangasht* 'the World-Traveller'. Rizvi has 'Lord of the Mortals' and 'World Traveller' (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 299–300, folios 277–278); Schimmel 'whom all the inhabitants of the world serve' (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354). Rizvi derives Jahangasht from his travels (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 300, folio 278); Sharib has the title Makhdum Jahaniyan conferred at three mazars on an Eid night (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 133–134, folios 129–130, حاشیہ 2). Lahori's heading gives the second title as جہاں گرد (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 168–169, folios 156–157), where his index has جہاں گشت (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 330, folio 318).
- **The peace of Thatta.** Rizvi dates Sultan Firuz's march on Thatta to 763/1362 (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 301, folio 279); Boivin dates the siege at which Jam Juna sought the saint's intercession to 1367 (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 189, folio 176).
- **The Sultan at his khanqah.** Rizvi has Sultan Firuz visit him in his khanqah throughout his stay (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 301, folio 279), and on the next page gives his rule that dervishes "should never allow such people to visit them" at their khanqahs (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 302, folio 280).
- **Sama'.** Sharib writes that he listened to sama', and that some say he did not (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 137, folio 133, حاشیہ 11).
- **His successor.** The site's account has the shrine kept by his descendants. Rizvi names his brother Sadru'd-Din, Raju Qattal, as his successor (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 303, folio 281), and Lahori's book records that his sajjada passed to his brother, not his son (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 168 area).
- **A grandson and a son of the same name.** Sharib calls Qutb Alam the grandson of "مخدوم جہانیاں جہاں گشت حضرت سید جلال الدین بخاری" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 190, folio 186), naming the saint himself so; a later chapter gives the name حضرت سید جلال الدین بخاری to the son of Makhdum Jahaniyan Jahangasht (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 195, folio 191).
- **The relics in the romance.** In Waris Shah's *Hir and Ranjha* his gift is a dagger (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 11, folio 10), and "he grasped the dagger given him by Makhdum" Jahanian (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 50, folio 49); in the lion fight the cudgel is Jahanian's and the dagger Sayyid Jalal Bokhari's (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 52, folio 51).
- **The compiler of the Siraj al-Hidaya.** Rizvi names Makhdumzada 'Abdu'llah as the compiler of the *Siraju'l-Hidaya* (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 22, folio 8); the list of sources in Lahori's book gives a *Siraj al-Hidaya* of his sayings under the name Taj al-Din Ahmad Barbani (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 292, folio 280).
- **The Panipat saint's gift of years.** Sharib tells the story twice, and the printed text reads صحبت in the first telling and صحت in the second (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 129, folio 125, حاشیہ 7; Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 131, folio 127).
- **Zakariya at Uch, Makhdum-i Jahaniyan at Multan.** In one passage Rozehnal calls Baha ad-Din Zakaria the saint "of Uch Sharif" and Makhdum-i Jahaniyan the master "of Multan" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 57, folio 47); the site's account puts this saint at Uch.
- **Where Uch lies.** Rozehnal puts it "seventy miles southwest of Multan" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 210, folio 202); Schimmel 'northeast of Multan' (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354).
- **The Qadiriyya at Uch.** Schimmel has the first Qadiri missionaries settle in Ucch "in the late fifteenth century" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354), and elsewhere has the order introduced "in the late fourteenth century" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 278, folio 247); the editor of Lahori's book, citing Ali Asghar Gilani, has Sayyid Muhammad Ghaus enter Uch in ۸۸۷ھ (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 40, folio 28).

## Legacy

Makhdoom Jahaniyan Jahangasht is remembered as one of the great Suhrawardi saints of medieval India — the learned, far-travelled 'Master of the World' whose authority spanned the Sultanate and whose lineage shaped the religious landscape of the Punjab and Sindh for generations. His shrine at Uch Sharif keeps alive the memory of the age when Uch, with Multan, was a beating heart of Sufism in the subcontinent.

The books carry that memory in their own terms. To Schimmel he is the reason Ucch was 'for a while, the center of the Suhrawardiyya' (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354); to Rizvi, the man in whose time the order, declining at Multan, was renewed at Uch (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 299, folio 277), who began in India the practice of initiation in more than one order (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 292, folio 272), who brokered the peace of Thatta and is credited with the remission of taxes (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 301, folio 279), and who, as a puritan, attacked the lamps and whitewash of the Shab-i Barat graves (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 302, folio 280). To Ernst and Lawrence he is the illustrious forebear among a modern saint's thirty-six generations of ancestors (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 95, folio 81); to Boivin, the ancestor in direct line of Qutub Ali Shah (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 56, folio 43); and to Chishti, the forebear of Sain Qutb Shah, a Husaini Bukhari sayyid (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 235). On Qutub Ali Shah and the Jahaniyyan lineage Boivin points to V. J. Matai's master's thesis, *Jahaniyyan khandan* (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 236, folio 223), "submitted in 1972, recently reprinted by the Institute of Sindhology" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 210, folio 197, Ch. 1 n. 15).
