## Overview

At Sial Sharif, in the Sargodha district of the Punjab, stands the shrine of Hazrat Khwaja Muhammad Shamsuddin Sialvi (1799–1883), a leading Chishti saint of the nineteenth century whose khanqah became one of the most influential centres of the order in the region. Heir to the great Chishti revival that flowed from Taunsa Sharif, Shamsuddin Sialvi guided a wide following and trained saints of the first rank, among them Pir Meher Ali Shah of Golra, and his darbar at Sial Sharif remains a major place of pilgrimage in central Punjab.

The books consulted here say very little about him, and none of them names the shrine or the settlement of Sial Sharif. Carl W. Ernst and Bruce B. Lawrence's *Sufi Martyrs of Love* mentions him in a single clause, as the direct pir of Meher Ali Shah, and gives his year of death as 1882 rather than 1883 (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 124, folio 111). Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori's *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, written in 1875, has no entry under his name in its index (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 320). The other passages gathered below speak of the Chishti line in the Punjab and of the Sargodha district and the city of Sargodha; they are context for the saint and his country, not accounts of this shrine.

## The Life of the Saint

Khwaja Muhammad Shamsuddin was born in 1799 at Sial Sharif into a devout family, and from his youth pursued the religious sciences and the Sufi path. He was initiated in the Chishti order and became a spiritual heir within the line of Khwaja Sulaiman Taunsvi, rising to be among the foremost Chishti shaykhs of his age. From his khanqah at Sial Sharif he taught, guided disciples, and kept a great *langar*, and his spiritual authority drew seekers from across the Punjab.

Among those who took his guidance was Pir Sayyid Meher Ali Shah of Golra Sharif, through whom the Sial Sharif line reached into the next generation of Punjabi Sufism. Ernst and Lawrence, who spell the names Mihr 'Ali Shah and Shams ad-Din Siyal, record the bond in these words: "Mihr 'Ali Shah's direct pir was Shams ad-Din Siyal (d. 1882)" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 124, folio 111). The clause names no shrine, no place and no order, and says nothing of his succession (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 124, folio 111).

He died in 1883 and was buried at Sial Sharif. Ernst and Lawrence give the year of his death as 1882 (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 124, folio 111). None of the books consulted here records his year of birth, his teachers or the place of his burial.

## The Shrine and its Devotion

The shrine of Shamsuddin Sialvi at Sial Sharif is a large complex kept by his successors, who have continued as its *sajjada-nashins* and as prominent religious figures. It maintains the daily prayers, gatherings of *naat* and qawwali, and the free kitchen, and its annual *urs* gathers great crowds of devotees. The town of Sial Sharif takes its identity from the darbar, a continuing seat of Chishti teaching and devotion.

The books consulted here do not describe the shrine. They give no date for the *urs*, do not say who built the tomb or when, and do not name its *sajjada-nashins*.

## The Chishti Line in the Punjab

The site's account places Shamsuddin Sialvi within the line of Khwaja Sulaiman Taunsvi. The books do not speak of Sial Sharif, but several of them describe the Chishti revival in the Punjab to which that line belongs. Their statements concern the order and the province, not this shrine.

In a footnote to the modern edition of the *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, its editor, Muhammad Iqbal Mujaddidi, writes that the wider spread of the Chishti Nizami Fakhri line in the Punjab came through Hazrat Khwaja Sulaiman Taunsvi. The same footnote gives Taunsvi's birth as 1184 AH and his death as 1267 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 120, folio 108).

Ernst and Lawrence write that "The Nizami Chishtis came to prominence in the eighteenth century, principally in Delhi, the Deccan, and Punjab, through masters who were related to but in tension with local political leaders" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 119, folio 106). Of Shaykh Sulayman Taunsawi, whom they date "(d. 1850)", they say that he "consolidated the order by stressing learning through establishment of institutions: not only the academy called Dar al- 'Ulum at Taunsa on the upper Indus, but also other religious schools throughout the Punjab" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, pp. 121–122, folios 108–109). They add that he "urged his followers to participate in resistance against Sikh aggression in the Punjab", and that "Notable, however, is the effort of Shah Sulayman to maintain cordial relations with Punjabi Hindus even while he opposed their Sikh neighbors" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 121, folio 108). Elsewhere they describe "the expansion of the Nizami branch of Chishti Sufism through disciples of Shaykh Sulayman Taunsawi in the upper Punjab" in the nineteenth century (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 71, folio 57).

On the page where they name Shams ad-Din Siyal, Ernst and Lawrence turn to the later history of this lineage: "If we were to follow only the dictates of political history and the criteria of social science, the test case for the Chishti Nizami Sulaymaniyya lineage in the colonial period might seem to be the battles over shrines in the Punjab". Their example is Taunsa, and they write that "Political power struggles internal to the Sulaymaniyya can be traced to British manipulation and also the rivalry of local Sikhs, Hindus, and Muslim reformers"; they then add that "political contestation is only part of the narrative" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 124, folio 111).

Michel Boivin writes in general terms that "The Chishtiyya is usually considered to be one of the most powerful Sufi orders in Northern India and Punjab" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 227, folio 214). Of the qawwali that the site's account names among the shrine's gatherings, Regula Burckhardt Qureshi writes, of the music of the region as a whole, that "tunes from the two principal regions of the Qawwali tradition, Uttar Pradesh and Panjab, can be distinguished mainly by their rhythmic setting", and that "Panjabi tunes tend to be rhythmically organized according to 'quality' or stress" (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 73, folio 53). Neither book mentions Sial Sharif.

## Setting

Sial Sharif is a settlement in the Sargodha district, distinct from the city of Sargodha. The books that mention Sargodha speak of the district or the city, and none of them names Sial Sharif, its shrine or its saint.

In a chapter of *Embodying Charisma*, Jamal Malik writes of the Punjab that "The cult of the holy man (pîr) and the associated folk piety is particularly evident in the countryside, where major landlords and large-scale land concentrations are crucial features, especially in Punjab and Sindh. In many cases, the local pîr represents the local landlord or is at least closely related to him" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 199, folio 188). In the same chapter he refers to "the tomb to which his ancestors were attached" in the Sargodha district, without naming the tomb, the village or Sial Sharif (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 203, folio 192).

The district also has a place in the Punjab's most famous romance. Annemarie Schimmel, retelling the plot of Hir and Ranjha, writes: "Ranjha, the son of a landlord of the Sargodha district, falls in love with Hir, the daughter of the ruler of Djang." She says nothing further about the district or about any shrine in it (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 419, folio 388).

Of the city of Sargodha, several modern writers give glimpses. Omar Kasmani writes of Zaheda, whose family lived there, that her "stay at the shrine of Se-hwan at the expense of Sargodha, a city in the Punjab where her family lived, demanded explaining"; she stayed at Sehwan "for months at a stretch, a thousand or so kilometers away from home" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 26, folio 9). Her husband Zafar "lived in Sargodha" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 113, folio 96). Haroon Khalid recalls that "in my ancestral house in Sargodha, the arches were three-cusped, hence Sikh, even though we were a Muslim family" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 309, folio not stated). In another book, an uncle of his from Sargodha tells of "another fakir in Sargodha who roams around the city all day long surrounded by dogs, and refuses to talk to any human being. Like Peer Abbas, he offers his food to the dogs first" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated). Khalid also writes that sectarian conflict at Eid Milad-Un-Nabi processions "remains a problem particularly in cities like Sargodha, Faisalabad and Dera Ismael Khan, where ultra-orthodox Sunni organizations have a large support base", though "until now these incidents have been minor" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The year of his death.** The site's account gives his dates as 1799–1883 and says he died in 1883. Ernst and Lawrence give "(d. 1882)" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 124, folio 111).
- **The form of his name.** The site's account calls him Hazrat Khwaja Muhammad Shamsuddin Sialvi. Ernst and Lawrence call him Shams ad-Din Siyal, without Muhammad or a title (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 124, folio 111).

## Legacy

Khwaja Shamsuddin Sialvi holds an important place in the Chishti revival of nineteenth-century Punjab, a master whose khanqah at Sial Sharif carried the tradition forward and trained saints, above all Meher Ali Shah of Golra, who would shape the religious life of the region into the twentieth century. It is as Meher Ali Shah's own master that he appears in Ernst and Lawrence's history of the Chishti order (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 124, folio 111). His shrine remains a great Chishti centre of central Punjab.
