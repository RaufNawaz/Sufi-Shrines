## Overview

The Dargah of Pir Ratan Nath, in the Jhanda Bazar of the walled city of Peshawar, is among the last living Hindu temples of that ancient frontier city. A shrine of the British colonial period, dedicated in practice to *Shiva* and centred upon the veneration of the yogi-saint *Ratan Nath*, it belongs to the tradition of the *Nath* order — the sect of ascetic yogis founded in the memory of Gorakhnath — and stands today as one of only a small handful of Peshawar's temples still in regular use, alongside the nearby Kali Bari and the historic Gorakhnath temple at Gor Khatri.

None of the books consulted for this article names the temple in the Jhanda Bazar, describes its building, or records anything of its custodians or its festival. What they offer is of two kinds. Two of them speak of the figure. Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi's history of Sufism in India names a 'Ratannath' among the yogis of the *Nath* movement, a contemporary of Gorakhnath and "the subject of many miraculous tales", though it tells none of the tales and gives him no place, no date and no shrine (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 354, folio 332). Michel Boivin's study of the Hindu Sufis of South Asia mentions, in a single endnote, a *dargah mandir* devoted to a 'Pir Baba Ratannath' and places it in Delhi, not in Peshawar (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 214, folio 201, Ch. 4 n. 6). Whether Rizvi's Ratannath, Boivin's Pir Baba Ratannath and the saint of the Peshawar temple are one and the same is not settled by either book. The rest of what the books say concerns Peshawar, its Hindu and Sikh communities and the frontier province around it; it is given below as context about the city and the region, not as testimony about this temple.

## Ratan Nath and the Nath Tradition

The temple's saint, Ratan Nath, belongs to the *Nath sampradaya*, the order of *kanphata* ('split-eared') yogis who trace their lineage to Gorakhnath and, before him, to Matsyendranath, the semi-legendary founder of the sect. The Naths, who flourished across northern India, Nepal, and the north-western frontier from roughly the early medieval centuries, practised a rigorous discipline of yoga and *hatha* austerity and revered Shiva as the *adi-nath*, the first lord and archetypal yogi. Peshawar and its region were an old stronghold of the order: the Gorakhnath temple within the Gor Khatri complex, and the ancient association of the nearby hill of *Tilla Jogian* across the Indus with Gorakhnath himself, mark this as Nath country of long standing.

Rizvi's history gives that picture of the frontier a wider frame. Writing of the Nath movement as a whole, he says: "From the eleventh century the Nath yogis began to spread throughout northern India, and from their centre at Peshawar moved to all parts of Central Asia and Iran, at the same time influencing both qalandars and sufis" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 355, folio 333). The sentence speaks of the Naths and of Peshawar as their centre; it names no building in the city, and no temple of Ratan Nath.

Ratan Nath is remembered in tradition as a wandering seeker and teacher who carried the Nath message of peace, truth, and compassion across the frontier lands; his veneration extends beyond Peshawar to shrines in the wider region, including sites in Nepal. As with many figures of the order, the details of his life are wrapped in legend and cannot be fixed with confidence to a particular century, and the tradition should be received as sacred memory rather than documented biography.

## Ratan Nath in the Books

Rizvi spells the name 'Ratannath' and gives him no 'Pir' and no 'Baba'. The whole of what his main text says of him is one clause, set inside a list of "Other names associated with the Nath cult": "Ratannath, a contemporary of Gorakhnath, and the subject of many miraculous tales" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 354, folio 332). The tales themselves are named and never told; Rizvi records no place for him, no dargah, no dates and no order beyond the Nath cult.

Rizvi returns to the figure in a footnote of his own, as a correction: "In fact Ratannath, a contemporary of Gorakhnath, was a very important figure of the movement" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 376, folio 354, footnote 1). The footnote stands against a statement in his main text in the same place, that "Baba Ratan Haijji [Hajji] was identified with Gorakhnath" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 376, folio 354). Read together, the two passages appear to set Ratannath, the Nath yogi, apart from a Baba Ratan styled *Hajji*; the footnote again names no Peshawar, no Jhanda Bazar, no dargah, no dates and no lineage.

Rizvi's index lists 'Ratannath, 332' among its run of Nath names, beside Dandanath, Dayanath, Dharamanath, Gahininath, Guganath, Minanath, Nimanath, Nivrittinath and Parasnath, again with no 'Pir', no 'Baba', no Peshawar and no date (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 482, folio 458). The same index carries a work headed 'Risala-i Rataniyya, 320', which may be related to one or other of these figures; the index does not tie it to Ratannath (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 482, folio 458).

The Baba Ratan styled *Hajji* is a different figure in Rizvi's pages, and the book does not connect him with this temple. Rizvi writes of a 'Shaikh Baba Ratan or Hajji Baba Ratan', "Born before the birth of Muhammad", made a companion of the Prophet, given *Abu'l-Riza* as his *kunniya*, said to come from Tabarhinda or Bhatinda in the Panjab, given 700/1300 as the year of his death, and buried in the Punjab (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 342, folio 320). The index lists him as 'Baba Ratan, Hajji, 320, 354', with no Nath, no Peshawar and no Jhanda Bazar (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 461, folio 437). Some traditions join the two figures into one; Rizvi's footnote reads as keeping them apart, and nothing in the books places the *Hajji* at Peshawar.

Boivin names the figure once, in an endnote on the 'paradoxical' terminology of shrines that are at once Muslim and Hindu in name: "On this 'paradoxical' terminology, see also the discussion on the 'dargah mandir' devoted to Pir Baba Ratannath in Delhi (Bouillier and Khan 2009: 571 passim)." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 214, folio 201, Ch. 4 n. 6). His index repeats the location, 'Pir Baba Ratannath, Delhi 201 n.6' (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 247, folio 234). Boivin says nothing about Peshawar, Gor Khatri or the Jhanda Bazar, and gives the Delhi shrine no dates, no custodian and no order.

The study to which Boivin points carries the figure's divided identity in its very title. His bibliography lists it as "Bouillier, Véronique and Dominique-Sila Khan, 'Hajji Ratan or Baba Ratan's Multiple Identities', *Journal of Indian Philosophy*, 37, 2009, pp. 559–95" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 233, folio 220). Boivin gives no account of its contents. Three readings of Boivin's note remain open, and the book settles none: that the same figure is honoured at a separate *dargah mandir* in Delhi, that the Delhi Pir Baba Ratannath is a different figure, or that the Peshawar shrine has been placed in the wrong city.

## The Shrine at Jhanda Bazar

The dargah itself, in its present form, is a temple of the nineteenth century, raised in the Karimpura–Jhanda Bazar neighbourhood within the old walls of Peshawar when the city's Hindu mercantile community was numerous and prosperous. Its very name — *dargah*, a word borrowed from the vocabulary of the Muslim shrine — reflects the blended religious idiom of the frontier, where a Hindu yogi's shrine could be spoken of in the language of the Sufi tomb.

Boivin's endnote, though it speaks of a shrine in Delhi and not of this one, names the same doubled vocabulary: a *dargah mandir*, a shrine called by the Muslim word for a saint's tomb and the Hindu word for a temple at once, a usage he counts among the 'paradoxical' terms of the shared shrine culture he studies (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 214, folio 201, Ch. 4 n. 6).

After the Partition of 1947, when the greater part of Peshawar's Hindu population departed, most of the city's temples fell silent or were converted to other uses. The Dargah Pir Ratan Nath is remarkable for having survived as a functioning place of worship, tended by the small Hindu community that remained, and it is today reckoned one of the very few temples in daily use in the city, together with the Kali Bari.

The sources consulted record no builder, no founding year, no endowment and no line of custodians for the temple.

## Devotion and Practice

The temple's chief festival is *Maha Shivratri*, the great night of Shiva, when devotees gather for worship. None of the books consulted describes the festival at this temple, or any rite performed here.

Haroon Khalid's account of Pakistan's religious minorities shows Peshawar's Hindus keeping the same festival elsewhere. He records that Hindus from Peshawar came to Katas in 2010 (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 47, folio not stated), and that at Shivratri in 2011 "Hindu pilgrims from Peshawar have arrived in two large buses", about a hundred people (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 49, folio not stated); the musical band, too, "have also come from Peshawar" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 51, folio not stated). Four marriages solemnised at Katas that Shivratri, an informant told him, were of "girls from Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and FATA, who suffered during the floods of 2010" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 51, folio not stated). Of the city's other great Hindu festival, one of his informants, Prem Gupta, says: "If you want to see the lights of Diwali, go to Peshawar" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 49, folio not stated). Khalid notes that the names of his informants may have been changed. Of the province as a whole he writes that "Hindu festivals are celebrated with much pomp in these areas" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 49, folio not stated). None of these passages names the Dargah of Pir Ratan Nath.

## Setting: Peshawar and the Frontier

Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, a history composed in the reign of Aurangzeb, describes Peshawar as an old city with older names. In one place it speaks of Peshawar as the place which the books of earlier writers call Farshawar, Parshawar and Bagram; the editor's footnote to Farshawar cannot be read in the printed text (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 424). In another it says that Peshawar is an ancient city, which the books of the earlier writers also write as Parshawar and Farshawar (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 503).

Bhandari sets at Peshawar the battle of Mahmud's first campaign, in the year 391: Raja Jaipal, in his words, set out to meet the invader and, reaching Peshawar, gave battle. The book says that five thousand of the raja's men fell to the sword in that fight, and that Raja Jaipal was taken captive with fifteen of his sons, brothers and kinsmen and was put to death (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 424).

In his account of the suba of Kabul, Bhandari writes that from the city of Peshawar three roads run to Kabul (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 502–501). The first is the Nikshab road, with many hard defiles and a long distance, over which armies pass with difficulty (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 502). The second is a road whose printed name, perhaps Kharpa, cannot be read with certainty, which joins the highway at Jalalabad, with narrow defiles, hard passes, little water and raids by Afghans (the word for the raids is uncertainly read) (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 502). The third, the road of Ali Masjid and the Khyber, runs eighteen kos from the spring of Jamshid, known as Jamrud, to a place on the bank of the Nilab whose printed name is uncertain; the Khyber is hard going for two kos, yet the road is easier than the others and is the one used by armies and caravans. Beyond it, Bhandari counts thirty kos without hills from a halt whose printed name, perhaps Dakka, is uncertain, to another whose name is also uncertain, and forty kos, with passes not very hard, from that second halt to Kabul (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 501).

Of the country round the city Bhandari writes that the district of Bangram (the name uncertainly read), known as Peshawar, lies on the side of Hindustan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 504). Its grapes, peaches and melons are like those of Turan, while its heat and cold, its seasons and its rain are like those of Hindustan; its rice, of a kind whose printed name cannot be read with certainty, is better than that of Bengal, and crops and grain are plentiful (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 504). The whole district (the first word uncertainly read) is the home of the Mahmand Afghans and others, who keep to the ways of paying revenue and of living as subjects (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 504–503). Twice more the city appears in his narrative of Mughal affairs: Dara sent robes of honour to the armies of Peshawar and Kabul, whose governorship Mahabat Khan held (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 77); and after the fort of Attock had been laid out the court set out for Peshawar, and from that stage a farman of counsel was sent to Hakim Mirza (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 201). Bhandari names no temple and no yogi at Peshawar in any of these passages.

Rizvi, in a passage on a place called Gorakhtari, writes that "McLeod rightly locates the site in Peshawar" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 413, folio 389). Harish Dhillon's retelling of the Janamsakhis, the traditional lives of Guru Nanak, brings the Guru to the city on his return from Baghdad: "He visited Kabul before reaching Peshawar" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 224, folio 246).

Peshawar appears in the literature of the Sufis too. Robert Rozehnal records that Zauqi Shah's *Sirr-i dilbaran*, a "comprehensive encyclopedia of Sufi terminology", was "Begun in Peshawar in 1923… compiled over a number of years. The text was first published by the silsila in 1951 and is now in its fifth edition (1985)." (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 106, folio 97). Zauqi Shah moved "between Agra, Jaipur, Bombay, Peshawar, Hyderabad, Ajmer, and, finally, Karachi" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 62, folio 52). A woman disciple "originally from the northwest frontier town of Peshawar", interviewed in Lahore in 2000, contrasted the shrines of her home region with Pakpattan: "I am quite used to the culture of segregation when it comes to the saints and the shrines. In the frontier, we have so many shrines where you can walk into the saint's shrines. Women can go inside, we can touch the graves. At many places there is a separate side for the women. It is not like this at Pakpattan Sharif" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 219, folio 211). She names no Peshawar shrine.

Annemarie Schimmel, describing the boldest mystical verse of the Panjab and Sind, paraphrases its vision of a oneness in which every boundary of birth and creed falls away: "the Sufi is no longer Arab, Hindu, Turk, or Peshawari; eventually Hallaj and the judge who condemned him, the lover and the theologian, are seen as nothing but different manifestations of the one divine reality" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 417, folio 386). In another book she gives the same thought in shorter form: "there is no longer Turk, Hindu, or Peshawari." (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 176, folio 158). These are her paraphrases of the poets, not their verse, and they speak of no particular shrine; the books consulted quote no verse by or about Ratan Nath.

Khalid's books give the modern city and province a human face. Peshawar, he writes, is "a city on the western border of Pakistan"; the elders of one Sikh informant, Surjeet Singh, moved there from a village in the Khyber Agency "for better economic opportunities" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 161, folio not stated), and Sikh refugees from the tribal areas "moved to Peshawar, Hassan Abdal and Nankana Sahib, and started living at Gurdwaras, just like the migrants before them" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 175, folio not stated). After the attacks of 1971, another informant, Tarvinder Kaur, told him, her family "fled to Peshawar, where there was a considerable Sikh population", staying "for a few months" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 184, folio not stated). A man named Diyal "teaches comparative religion at Peshawar University" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 50, folio not stated).

Of the province he writes that "since partition, the province of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (KPK) has been home to a large proportion of religious minorities who have lived there rather peacefully. These are primarily Hindus, who form the largest minority", and that "Thousands of them are part of the main stream society, visible often in the economic and political fabric" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 49, folio not stated). Khyber Pakhtunkhwa is among the provinces where, he writes, "most of the Hindus are scattered" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 87, folio not stated). Of the time of Partition he says that "unlike Punjab the riots were less intense due to the influence of the Indian National Congress there. A lot of Hindus and Sikhs continued living in their ancestral lands even after the creation of Pakistan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 49, folio not stated); and, elsewhere, that "At the time of partition, unlike Punjab, this area was spared bloodshed primarily due to the influence of the Sarhadi (Frontier) Gandhi – Abdul Ghaffar Khan", so that "several Hindus and Sikhs stayed rooted in Khyber-Pakhtunkhwa because of the comparatively peaceful political situation here" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 162, folio not stated). Reaching further back, he writes that "During his reign, Maharaja Ranjit Singh captured the Pathan regions of Khyber-Pakhthunkwa and the tribal areas, as a result of which several people converted to Sikhism" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 161–162, folio not stated).

In *Walking with Nanak*, Khalid notes that Nanak Panthi Hindus, "thousands of such Sikhs", live "in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Punjab and Sindh in Pakistan" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 98, folio 80), and that the independent "followers of Guru Nanak" who refused the Khalsa "still exist in Sindh and in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 230, folio 204).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **Where the figure's dargah stands.** The site's account places the Dargah of Pir Ratan Nath in the Jhanda Bazar of Peshawar, and says his veneration extends to shrines in the wider region, including sites in Nepal. Boivin places the *dargah mandir* devoted to Pir Baba Ratannath in Delhi (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 214, folio 201, Ch. 4 n. 6), and his index gives 'Pir Baba Ratannath, Delhi' (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 247, folio 234); he does not mention Peshawar. Rizvi places Ratannath nowhere (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 354, folio 332).
- **The figure's name and style.** The site's account calls him Pir Ratan Nath, and Baba Pir Ratan Nath. Rizvi writes 'Ratannath', with no 'Pir' and no 'Baba' (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 354, folio 332; p. 482, folio 458). Boivin writes 'Pir Baba Ratannath' (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 214, folio 201, Ch. 4 n. 6).
- **When he lived.** The site's account says the details of his life cannot be fixed with confidence to a particular century. Rizvi makes Ratannath "a contemporary of Gorakhnath" in both his main text and his footnote, without giving a date for either (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 354, folio 332; p. 376, folio 354, footnote 1).
- **When the Naths spread.** The site's account has the Naths flourish across northern India, Nepal and the north-western frontier from roughly the early medieval centuries. Rizvi writes that "From the eleventh century the Nath yogis began to spread throughout northern India", from their centre at Peshawar (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 355, folio 333).
- **Who Ratan was.** The site's account treats him as a Hindu yogi-saint of the Nath order. Rizvi's main text says that "Baba Ratan Haijji [Hajji] was identified with Gorakhnath" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 376, folio 354), while his footnote insists that "In fact Ratannath, a contemporary of Gorakhnath, was a very important figure of the movement" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 376, folio 354, footnote 1). Rizvi also describes a Muslim 'Shaikh Baba Ratan or Hajji Baba Ratan', a companion of the Prophet who died in 700/1300 and lies buried in the Punjab (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 342, folio 320), and the study Boivin cites is titled 'Hajji Ratan or Baba Ratan's Multiple Identities' (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 233, folio 220). Neither book ties the *Hajji* to the Peshawar temple.
- **The name of the place at Gor Khatri.** The site's account speaks of the Gorakhnath temple at Gor Khatri. Rizvi writes of a place called Gorakhtari, which, he says, "McLeod rightly locates" in Peshawar (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 413, folio 389).
- **Peshawar's Hindus after 1947.** The site's account says that the greater part of Peshawar's Hindu population departed after Partition, leaving a small community. Khalid, writing of the province rather than the city, says that "A lot of Hindus and Sikhs continued living in their ancestral lands even after the creation of Pakistan", and that Hindus are the province's largest minority (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 49, folio not stated).
- **Why the frontier was spared in 1947.** Khalid gives two reasons on two pages: the influence of the Indian National Congress (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 49, folio not stated), and the influence of Abdul Ghaffar Khan, the Frontier Gandhi (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 162, folio not stated).

## Legacy

The survival of the Dargah Pir Ratan Nath is significant out of all proportion to its modest size. It preserves, in a city more often associated in the modern imagination with the Khyber Pass and the turbulence of the frontier, the memory of a deep and ancient Hindu and yogic presence — the Nath yogis whose austerities once echoed across these hills. As a still-living shrine, it is both a place of worship for Peshawar's remaining Hindus and a fragile monument to the religious plurality of the old walled city. Efforts to document and protect the temple, including its listing among the heritage sites of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, reflect a growing recognition of its value.

The books add a thread to that memory. Rizvi, who calls Peshawar the centre from which the Naths moved into Central Asia and Iran (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 355, folio 333), counts Ratannath "a very important figure of the movement" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 376, folio 354, footnote 1); and the *dargah mandir* of a Pir Baba Ratannath, which Boivin knows in Delhi, carries in its name the same meeting of Muslim and Hindu vocabulary as the Peshawar temple's own (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 214, folio 201, Ch. 4 n. 6).
