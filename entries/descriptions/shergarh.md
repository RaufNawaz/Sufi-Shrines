## Overview

At Shergarh, in the Okara district of the Punjab, stands the shrine of Hazrat Daud Bandagi Kirmani (1513–1575), a revered Qadiri saint of the sixteenth century who did much to spread the Qadiri order in the Punjab. A contemporary of the Mughal emperors Humayun and Akbar and of the great Lahori saints of his age, Daud Bandagi made Shergarh a centre of devotion, and his darbar remains a place of pilgrimage in central Punjab.

Several of the books name the tomb at the town. Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori, writing in 1875, says that his pearl-scattering mazar at the place Sher Garh is a place of pilgrimage for God's creatures, and that every year a great crowd gathers at his mazar (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 49, folio 37). Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti speaks of a Hazrat Daud Bandagi, whose mazar is at Shergarh (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 179). Haroon Khalid, travelling in the Punjab, writes: "One of the places we stopped at was Shergarh, about an hour from Lahore, where we witnessed the festivities at the annual festival of Daud Bandagi Kirmani" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).

The older books do not all call him by the name the shrine bears today. Lahori never prints "Bandagi"; he calls him Shaikh Daud Kirmani of Shergarh and gives his toponymic name in two forms. Zahur ul-Hasan Sharib calls him simply Hazrat Shaikh Daud of Shergarh, and Kanhaiya Lal's history of Lahore Khwaja Daud of Sher Garh. They agree that he was a master at Shergarh from whom later masters took their path: Shah Abul Muali of Lahore in the accounts of Lahori, Sharib and Chishti, and Shah Abu Ishaq in those of Kanhaiya Lal and Chishti. Lahori dates his death to 982 AH. These differences are set out side by side below.

## The Life of the Saint

Daud, honoured with the title *Bandagi* ("servitude," marking his devotion) and the epithet *Kirmani* for his family's origins, was born in 1513. He gave himself to religious learning and the Sufi path, and became a shaykh of the Qadiri order (the line of Shaikh Abd al-Qadir Jilani of Baghdad) at a time when that order was taking firm root in the subcontinent. From his base at Shergarh he taught, guided disciples, and spread the Qadiri way across the Punjab, living through the turbulent early Mughal decades. He was among the notable saints of the generation of Shah Hussain of Lahore, part of the flowering of Punjabi Sufism in the sixteenth century. He died in 1575 and was buried at Shergarh.

Lahori gives him a full notice, the ninth in the Qadiri chapter of his *Hadiqat al-Awliya* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 48–49, folios 36–37). His table of contents names him Shaikh Daud Jahniwal Shergarhi (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 15, folio 3), while the heading of the notice itself reads Shaikh Daud Chuniwal Shergarhi, the reading of that word uncertain in the printed text (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 48). Elsewhere the book calls him simply Shaikh Daud Kirmani Shergarhi (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 58, folio 46, note 1) and, with the place-name spelled differently, Shaikh Daud Kirmani Shergarhi again (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 293, folio 281).

Lahori traces his descent as a Kirmani sayyid. On the authority of the *Shajarat al-Anwar* he carries the line to Imam Musa Kazim: Sayyid Daud, son of Sayyid Fath Allah Kirmani, son of Sayyid Mubarak, son of a Sayyid whose name is printed, uncertainly, as Sayyid Qamis Allah, son of Safi al-Din Adam Kirmani, son of Sayyid Taqi al-Din Ahmad, and so on to Musa Mubarqa, son of Imam Musa Raza (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 48–49, folios 36–37). He then records a correction from another genealogy, which runs through Sayyid Muhammad Aaraji, son of Sayyid Musa Mubarqa, son of Imam Muhammad Taqi, son of Imam Ali Musa Raza, because Musa Mubarqa was no son of Imam Raza (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 49, folio 37).

The family's coming to the Punjab is told, on the authority of Dara Shikoh's *Safinat al-Auliya*, as a story of two stages. His father, Sayyid Fath Allah, came first from Arabia to Hind and settled at Haibatpur Patti, and then moved to the *qasba* of Chunian. When the father died, Sayyid Daud was born four months after his death. He took a full share of the exoteric sciences from Maulana Ismail Lahori, and then turned to the inner sciences (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 49, folio 37).

In Lahori's account he was famous among the Qadiri elders of the Punjab as a master of *hal* and *qal*, of *shariat* and *tariqat*. He was at worship from evening to morning and never slept the whole night, passing it now standing, now prostrate, now bowing, now seated. From the abundance of his austerity a special *nisbat* with Ghaus al-Azam arose in him, and by Ghaus al-Azam's inner permission he presented himself to Sayyid Hamid Ganj Bakhsh, reached completion, and took the *khirqa* of *khilafat* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 48, folio 36). He was one of the two perfect *khalifas*, of Sayyid Hamid Ganj Bakhsh, the other being Sayyid Sher Shah Multani (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 48, folio 36). Chishti's chain of Shah Bilawal likewise runs from Hazrat Daud Bandagi back through a Sayyid Hamid to Ghaus al-Azam (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 179).

Lahori gives his death in 982 AH, with two chronograms for it (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 49, folio 37), set out under "In Verse" below. Sharib dates his death only relatively, in his account of the disciple: Shah Abul Muali came to Lahore after the passing of his pir, in a year printed as 1110 AH, a reading the printed text leaves in doubt (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 257, folio 253).

## His Disciples and Successors

The disciple through whom he is best remembered is Shah Abul Muali of Lahore. Lahori makes Abul Muali his nephew and *murid*, who spent thirty-two years with him (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 57, folio 45), and in a note names the saint of Shergarh as Abul Muali's uncle, father-in-law and pir, and as the subject of Abul Muali's *Naghmat-e Daudi* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 58, folio 46, note 1).

Sharib tells the story from the disciple's side. Abul Muali's father had already settled the family at Shergarh, and Abul Muali was born there (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 255, folio 251). He went from Delhi to Shergarh and presented himself in the service of Hazrat Shaikh Daud (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 256, folio 252). He was the *murid* and distinguished *khalifa* of Hazrat Shaikh Daud (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 256, folio 252). The condition of his training was that he live always with his pir, who told him that so he would gain what he could not gain elsewhere in a whole lifetime (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 256, folio 252). In the same pages a *majzub*'s Persian names the master as Shaikh Daudi (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 256, folio 252).

Chishti, in his account of Shah Abul Maali, calls the murshid at Sher Garh Hazrat Shaikh Daud, the name printed uncertainly, from whom the disciple set out, and says that at his halts on the road the disciple faced towards Sher Garh (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 479–480).

A second line of succession runs through Shah Abu Ishaq. Kanhaiya Lal writes that Shah Abu Ishaq was, in the Qadiriya silsila, the *murid* and *khalifa* of Khwaja Daud of Sher Garh; the place-name is uncertain in the printed text, and the words that follow it in the sentence cannot be recovered (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 283, folio 286). Chishti gives the same link in the chain of Shah Bilawal: Shaikh Shams-ud-Din, then Shah Abu Ishaq, the disciple of Hazrat Daud Bandagi (both words of the name printed uncertainly), whose mazar is at Shergarh, then Sayyid Hamid, and so back to Ghaus al-Azam (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 179).

## The Shrine

The shrine of Daud Bandagi Kirmani at Shergarh is a centre of pilgrimage kept by his successors, where devotees come to recite the Fatiha and seek the saint's blessing. The place takes its sacred identity from the darbar of the saint who made it a seat of the Qadiri order.

Lahori's pearl-scattering mazar at Sher Garh is a place of pilgrimage for God's creatures (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 49, folio 37). Chishti speaks of the lofty tomb of Hazrat Daud Bandagi (the last word printed uncertainly), in a passage that also names the tombs of Shah Abul Maali and Pir Dastgir, and of a mazar at Shergarh, the place-name printed uncertainly; the same passage names the mazar of a Sayyid Jalal al-Din, followed by an illegible word and a place printed uncertainly as Kothal, together with a Sayyid Rahmat Allah (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 124). How these tombs stand to one another the passage, as it survives, does not make clear.

Khalid saw "the footprints of the saint engraved on a small marble platform" beside the grave (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).

Sharib's account names Shergarh only as a place of residence, birth and initiation, never as the site of a shrine (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 255–256, folios 251–252). The sources consulted do not record who built the tomb, when, or what endowments it held.

## Devotion and Practice

The shrine maintains the customary devotions (prayer, qawwali, and the free kitchen), and its annual urs gathers the faithful of the region. Lahori, in the nineteenth century, records that every year a great crowd gathers at the mazar, though he gives the gathering no name and no date (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 49, folio 37). In our own day Khalid witnessed "the festivities at the annual festival of Daud Bandagi Kirmani" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated); the year of his visit is not stated.

Khalid's account dwells on a *malang* he watched there. "Halting at the threshold of the shrine, he said a silent prayer. He touched the base of the entrance and entered". His eyes were "smeared in kohl"; he wore "a vertical mark on his forehead", "a glass earring on his right ear", "thin black clothes" and "a thick bangle on his right ankle", with his head shaved and no facial hair (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated). "Circling around the grave of the saint Daud Bandagi, the malang stopped at a spot where he kissed the footprints of the saint engraved on a small marble platform" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).

Khalid's guide, the historian Iqbal Qaiser, explained that *malangs* "only drape a shawl when they are in public ... When they are with other malangs they wear nothing but a small lungi" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated). Of the footprints he said: "If you go to Jain temples there are footmarks of their prominent priests which become objects of veneration. This is exactly like that", and "There has to be some sort of an inspiration from that particular Jain sect as well" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated). Khalid himself asked "They are Nanghe sadhus, aren't they?", and wrote that the *malang* "looks like a Digamabar Jain monk" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).

Leaving the town, Khalid met more ascetics: "Driving out of Shergarh towards Tibba Haji Deen, we drove past a small group of ascetics walking barefoot on the road". The eldest wore "a black shawl", "a green turban", and "a belt of bells ... around his waist" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated).

## Legends and Miracles

Sharib relates that the master read his disciple's state by *kashf* and consoled him with the words, in Persian, that today or tomorrow he would bring Abul Muali into the holy and exalted presence, and that he should wait for the hour and the time, the word for "hour" being printed uncertainly (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 256, folio 252).

A note in Lahori's book, sourcing the family of Hujra, relates an anecdote from the *Maqamat-e Daudi* of Abdul Baqi, whose second name, perhaps Haji, is printed uncertainly, a manuscript owned by Muhammad Iqbal Mujaddidi: during Hazrat Daud Kirmani's period of *i'tikaf* Hazrat Baha al-Din Hajrawi came to meet him, and because of the *i'tikaf* the meeting could not take place (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 47, folio 35, note 4). The note names him only as Hazrat Daud Kirmani, with no place, date or order.

## In Verse

The sources consulted carry no verse of his own. What survives in verse is his death, in the two chronograms Lahori gives for the year 982 AH. The first is the phrase from which, he says, the date of the death appears:

پیر حق پرست

A chronogram on the saint's death, given by Lahori (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 49, folio 37).

The second is the chronogram recorded in Abdul Haq's *Akhbar al-Akhyar*:

مشتاق منان

A second chronogram on the same death, which Lahori takes from the *Akhbar al-Akhyar* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 49, folio 37).

His disciple Shah Abul Muali wrote of him in the *Naghmat-e Daudi* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 58, folio 46, note 1); the sources consulted quote none of it.

## Setting

Lahori's history of the Qadiri order in the Punjab, which speaks of the order and the province and not of Shergarh, holds that of the Qadiri line Sayyid Muhammad Ghaus of Uch was the first to come to the Punjab, coming by way of Khurasan to Multan and settling at Uch; the editor's note, not the author, gives the year of his entry into Uch as 887 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 40, folio 28).

Khalid places Shergarh "about an hour from Lahore", on the road to Tibba Haji Deen (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated). Sharib, in his account of Shah Abul Muali, gives in a footnote a migration in stages across four generations that ends at this town: from Kirman to Hindustan in 796 AH, then to Uch, then to a place printed as Daud Jal near Multan, then to one printed as Singhra, and at last to Shergarh, the readings of both names uncertain in the printed text (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 255, folio 251, note 1).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **His name.** The shrine's account calls him Daud Bandagi Kirmani, as do Khalid (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated) and, for "Bandagi", Chishti, whose reading of the word is uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 179). Lahori never prints "Bandagi" and calls him Shaikh Daud Kirmani of Shergarh (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 293, folio 281). Sharib never gives him "Bandagi" or "Kirmani" and calls him Hazrat Shaikh Daud (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 256, folio 252). Kanhaiya Lal calls him Khwaja Daud (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 283, folio 286).
- **His toponymic name.** Lahori's table of contents reads Jahniwal (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 15, folio 3); the heading of his notice reads Chuniwal, its reading uncertain in the printed text (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 48). The same notice has his father move to the *qasba* of Chunian (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 49, folio 37).
- **The year of his death.** The shrine's account gives 1575. Lahori gives 982 AH, with two chronograms (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 49, folio 37). Sharib dates it only as before Abul Muali's coming to Lahore in a year printed as 1110 AH, a reading the printed text leaves in doubt (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 257, folio 253).
- **His descent.** The *Shajarat al-Anwar*, as Lahori reports it, carries the line through Musa Mubarqa as a son of Imam Musa Raza; the other genealogy Lahori follows makes Musa Mubarqa a son of Imam Muhammad Taqi, on the ground that Musa Mubarqa was no son of Imam Raza (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 48–49, folios 36–37).
- **How the family reached the Punjab.** Lahori, after the *Safinat al-Auliya*, has his father come from Arabia to Hind, settle at Haibatpur Patti and move on to Chunian (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 49, folio 37). The footnote in Sharib's account of Shah Abul Muali has a migration from Kirman to Hindustan in 796 AH, by way of Uch and the Multan country, ending at Shergarh after four generations (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 255, folio 251, note 1).
- **His title in the order.** Lahori and Sharib call him Shaikh (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 58, folio 46, note 1; Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 256, folio 252); Kanhaiya Lal calls him Khwaja (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 283, folio 286); Lahori's genealogy calls him Sayyid (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 48–49, folios 36–37).
- **The footprints.** Iqbal Qaiser likens the footprints at the grave to the venerated footmarks of Jain priests and looks for a Jain inspiration (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated). No other source consulted mentions them.

## Legacy

Daud Bandagi Kirmani is remembered as one of the saints who firmly planted the Qadiri order in the sixteenth-century Punjab, a devout shaykh of the age of Akbar whose shrine at Shergarh has kept his memory for four and a half centuries. He belongs to the great generation of Punjabi Sufism, and his darbar remains a living centre of the devotion he inspired.

His memory was kept in writing early. The modern edition of Lahori's book lists, as item 23 of its sources Abdul Baqi Haji Qadiri's *Maqamat-e Daudi*, an account of Shaikh Daud Kirmani Shergarhi, dated 1056 AH and owned by the editor himself, Muhammad Iqbal Mujaddidi (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 293, folio 281). Lahori's notice names seven works on him: Abul Muali Shah Lahori's *Naghmat-e Daudi*; Abdul Baqi b. Jan Muhammad Haji Qadri's *Maqamat-e Daudi*, of 1056 AH, copied in 1201 AH, in a manuscript owned by Muhammad Iqbal Mujaddidi; Abdul Qadir Badayuni's *Muntakhab al-Tawarikh*, Nawal Kishore edition, at 287–291; Nizam al-Din Ahmad's *Tabaqat-e Akbari*, at a page printed uncertainly as 11; Abdul Haq's *Akhbar al-Akhyar*, at 193–195; Ghausi's *Azkar-e Abrar*, or *Gulzar-e Abrar*, at 207; and Dara Shikoh's *Safinat al-Auliya*, at 193 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 49, folio 37).

Through his disciples his name runs on in the chains of later masters: through Shah Abul Muali, who spent thirty-two years at his side (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 57, folio 45), and through Shah Abu Ishaq, Shaikh Shams-ud-Din and Shah Bilawal (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 179; Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 283, folio 286).
