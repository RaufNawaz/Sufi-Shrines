## Overview

At Ranmal Sharif, near Phalia in the Mandi Bahauddin region of the Punjab, stands the shrine of Hazrat Syed Muhammad Noushah Ganj Bakhsh (1552–1654) — the founder of the Naushahia branch of the Qadiri Sufi order. A saint of long life and wide influence, Noushah Qadiri established a spiritual line that spread across the Punjab and beyond and endures to this day, and his darbar at Ranmal Sharif remains an important centre of pilgrimage.

None of the books consulted here names Ranmal Sharif or Phalia as the place of the saint's tomb. What they offer is the saint himself, his order and his disciples. Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori's *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, a tazkira of the saints of the Punjab composed in 1875 and read here in a modern annotated Urdu edition, gives him a full entry headed Shaikh Haji Muhammad Qadiri, known as Hazrat Naushah Ganj Bakhsh (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 70–72, folios 58–60), with his parents, his training, his pir, a Persian chronogram on his death and a circle of disciples; and the book's modern editor states that his holy mazar is at present at Sahnpal Sharif, district Gujrat, Punjab (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 71, folio 59, note). Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti's *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* carries a chapter headed "The Account of Hazrat Naushah" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 483), whose damaged passage on his burial names district Shahpur and Sahn Pal in district Gujrat (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 483–484). Kanhaiya Lal's history of Lahore names Nau Shah Ganj Bakhsh only at the head of a Naushahi spiritual pedigree, and says nothing of the site (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 304, folio 307). Everything below drawn from the books is therefore said of the saint, his order or the town, not of the shrine at Ranmal Sharif.

## The Life of the Saint

Muhammad Noushah — honoured as *Ganj Bakhsh*, “the bestower of treasure,” and *Noushah*, “the bridegroom” — was born in 1552. He took the Sufi path in the Qadiri order and, attaining a high spiritual station, founded his own branch, the *Naushahia*, which he propagated with great energy across the Punjab. He lived through a full century of the Mughal age, guiding a vast body of disciples and establishing a network of successors, and he is remembered both as a saint of powerful blessing and as an organiser of an enduring order. He died in 1654, full of years, and was buried at Ranmal Sharif, which became the seat of his line.

Lahori's book lists him in its contents as Hazrat Naushah Ganj Bakhsh, entry no. 30 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 15, folio 3), and its persons index gives his personal name as Haji Muhammad, in brackets after Naushah Ganj Bakhsh (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 332, folio 320). Elsewhere the book names him in full honorific as Hazrat Haji Muhammad Naushah Ganj Bakhsh (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 272, folio 260). Lahori calls him a wali from birth and the khalifa of Shah Sulaiman Qadri; states of *jazb* and *sukr*, of love and longing, dominated him, and he was unequalled in asceticism, piety and *karamat* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 70, folio 58).

**His parents.** Lahori names both on one page. His father, Haji Ala al-Din, was himself a great elder who performed the Hajj seven times and was honoured with a vision of the Prophet; his mother, Bibi Jiwani, was also a great worshipper and ascetic (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 70, folio 58). The book's indexes list them as Bibi Jiwani, mother of Naushah Ganj Bakhsh (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 316, folio 304) and Ala al-Din, father of Naushah Ganj Bakhsh (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 323, folio 311). Chishti, citing a work he calls *Manaqib-e Naushahiya*, gives the family's old home as the village of Kahliyanwali, the father as Shaikh Ala al-Din and the mother as Mulla Bi Jiwan, the names of the village and of the mother both printed uncertainly (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 486).

**His birth and boyhood, as Chishti tells them.** The father left three months into the pregnancy, saying that the child would be a champion of the faith (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 486), and returned from the journey to Mecca when the child was four (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 487). Hazrat Shah Sulaiman Sahib, the *qutb* of the age, foretold the birth (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 486–487) and advised the family (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 487–488). Chishti names a brother, Shaikh Ismail (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 488). At twelve the boy was taken for twenty-two years old (the number printed uncertainly), so strong was he, and he fought in many battles (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 488–489). He then withdrew into a vast, empty wilderness, fasting without break, and was fed with milk each night (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 489–490); he studied at the village of Hakothara (the name printed uncertainly) near Nowshera and memorised the Quran in a single night, where the printed text breaks off (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 490).

**His training and initiation, as Lahori tells them.** Up to the age of seventeen he completed the exoteric sciences; then he renounced the world and stayed in zikr in the reed-bed of Sandal Bar, but his parents would not leave him there. Coming away, he was brought by Mulla Karim al-Din to Shah Sulaiman, took *bai'at*, and in a few years reached completion (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 70–71, folios 58–59). The book's editor corrects the place: instead of Sandal Bar it should be Gondal Bar (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 70, folio 58, note 2).

**The founding of the order.** Lahori states it plainly: the Naushahia path, whose faqirs in the Punjab number in the thousands, began with him (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 70, folio 58). The same book names the order the Naushahia Qadiria family (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 272, folio 260). In another entry, however, it ascribes the order's rise in the Punjab to another figure (see below, under the disagreements).

**His death.** The current account gives 1654. Lahori himself gives 1103 AH, with the chronogram *pir-e fayyaz*, 'the bountiful pir' (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 71, folio 59). His modern editor rejects that year in a footnote: his birth fell on 1 Ramadan 959 AH, and his correct year of death is 12 Rabi al-Awwal 1064 AH, 1103 AH is wrong, as Sayyid Sharafat Naushahi has proved by research in his *Sharif al-Tawarikh* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 71, folio 59, note 2). The same editor's own list of his publications gives yet another date, describing the collected verse as that of Hazrat Haji Muhammad Naushah Ganj Bakhsh Qadri, who died in 1653 CE (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 307, folio 295). Lahori also records a *bai'at* the saint accepted when he was ill and one day remained before his death, without giving the year (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 241, folio 229).

**A family wedding.** Chishti tells the story of the marriage of Hazrat Naushah's daughter, in which the bride's side demanded a hundred rupees of *lagan*, after the custom of the Muslims of the Punjab (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 484).

## The Shrine and the Naushahia Order

The shrine of Noushah Ganj Bakhsh at Ranmal Sharif is the mother-shrine of the Naushahia Qadiri order, kept by his descendants and successors, and a busy centre of pilgrimage. It maintains the customary devotions — prayer, qawwali and *naat*, and the free kitchen — and its annual urs draws great crowds of the order’s followers and of the wider devout. The Naushahia order he founded remains active across the Punjab and among the Punjabi diaspora, so that his shrine is the head of a living and widespread spiritual family.

The books consulted record nothing of the village of Ranmal, its urs, its custodians or its fabric. The place they give for the saint's tomb is another. Lahori's modern editor writes that the mazar is at present at Sahnpal Sharif in district Gujrat (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 71, folio 59, note); the same editor describes the author of the order's chronicle *Tazkira-e Naushahi* as Hafiz Sayyid Muhammad Hayat Rabbani Naushahi Sahnpalvi (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 71, folio 59, note 1). Chishti's passage on the burial is damaged in the printed text, with several words illegible and several place-names printed uncertainly. It names a mazar in district Shahpur and a mazar at Sahn Pal in district Gujrat, near names read as Sher Shah and Rasul Nagar, and whose mazar each clause names is not clear (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 483–484).

Lahori's index names a holder of the order's *sajjada*: Pir Muhammad, Sajjada Naushahi (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 315, folio 303). In the editor's time the manuscripts of the order were mostly held in a private Naushahi library at Sahanpal, Gujrat (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 292–295, folios 280–283; Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 243, folio 231).

## Devotion and Practice

The current account records prayer, qawwali and *naat*, the free kitchen and the annual urs at Ranmal Sharif. The books speak of the order's devotions, not of this site's.

**The *sama* of the order.** Writing in his own present, in the 1870s, Lahori describes the ecstatic *sama* of the order under the entry for Abdul Rahman Pak Naushahi, whom he calls the great khalifa of Noushah Ganj Bakhsh (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 80, folio 68 [marker 78]).

Let it not stay hidden, he writes, that everyone in the exalted Naushahi order is a person of ecstasy and *sama*, of longing, relish and intoxication (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 81, folio 69 [marker 79]).

But the faqirs of the Pak Rahman line become the most intoxicated at *sama*: they do not cool until a rope is put round their feet and they are hung upside down and dance so for an hour or two; failing that they are dragged along the ground, and the rope is not untied until they come to themselves (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 81, folio 69 [marker 79]). Chishti, too, speaks of the Naushahi followers, in the singular and the plural, and of their qawwali and their *hal*, their state of ecstasy (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 466).

**Grain at his door.** A party of Hindu faqirs, among whom the apostate Hafiz Tahir had joined, passed his house one day. The saint ordered wheat to be given to every one of them; all received it except Hafiz Tahir, for the grain had run out (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 242, folio 230).

**A deathbed initiation and a teaching.** Of Shah Abdullah, whom the book describes as a *mansabdar* of seven thousand at the court of Alamgir (the "seven" printed uncertainly), Lahori writes that he came to the saint when Haji Muhammad was ill, with one day left before his death, and the saint accepted his *bai'at* and gave him leave for *zikr* and spiritual practice (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 241, folio 229). When Shah Abdullah insisted on becoming a *majzub*, the saint answered that to remain detached amid attachments is the work of men, and that becoming a *majzub* is easy. He gave way only when Shah Abdullah invoked God between them (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 241, folio 229).

## The Saint's Circle

**His khalifas.** Chishti names two renowned khalifas: Faqir Abdul Rahman, called Pak Rahman, and Pir Muhammad Sachyar, the last name printed uncertainly (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 484). Lahori says of Abdul Rahman Pak Naushahi that his pir's favour to him was shown to no one else (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 80, folio 68 [marker 78]), and that his radiant mazar at the village of Bharri, a name printed uncertainly, is a place of pilgrimage. The author gives his death as 1153 AH; the editor, from three family manuscripts, *Lataif-e Gul Shahi*, *Bayaz-e Qadri* and *Kitab al-Fawaid*, gives 4 Muharram 1115 AH, the year printed uncertainly, which he calls reliable, and his birth as 995 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 81, folio 69 [marker 79], note 1).

**A chain of succession.** Under entry 231 Lahori sets out a five-step Naushahi Qadiri chain that ends in the saint: Fazl Shah, from Rahman Shah, from Muhammad Siddiq, from Shah Farid Lahori, from Pir Muhammad Sachyar, from Haji Muhammad Naushah Ganj Bakhsh (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 272, folio 260). Beside it stand Hazrat Shah Kanthha Naushahi, the name printed uncertainly (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 272–273), and Hazrat Shah Farid Naushahi, a murid of the same Shaikh Pir Muhammad Sachyar (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 274, folio 262). Of this line Lahori says that most of the family's murids are in the city of Lahore, and that it was materially supported by the Sikh state through Raja Dina Nath (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 273, folio 261). Chishti gives the same Fazl Shah line — Rahman Shah, Muhammad Siddiq, Shah Farid and Hazrat Bajibar, both names printed uncertainly — ending with their enlightened pir, Hazrat Naushah Ganj Bakhsh (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 337). He also closes Shah Kamaita's spiritual pedigree with Hazrat Naushah Sahib (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 349), and sets Bhage Shah's second pedigree directly below Shah Sulaiman Nuri (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 467).

**Four God-absorbed disciples.** The sixth section of Lahori's book, "The Sixth Garden", on the madmen and the God-absorbed, opens at p. 236 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 236, folio 224), and four of its *majzubs* were Noushah Ganj Bakhsh's disciples, each carrying the *nisba* Naushahi. The book introduces them in turn as Shaikh Mittha Majzub Naushahi, a murid of Hazrat Haji Muhammad Naushah Ganj Bakhsh (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 240, folio 228); Sayyid Shah Abdullah Majzub Naushahi (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 241, folio 229); Nanu Majzub Naushahi, likewise a murid of Haji Muhammad Naushah Ganj Bakhsh (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 242, folio 230); and Hafiz Tahir Majzub Naushahi (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 242, folio 230).

Of Shaikh Mittha the book says that absorption so mastered him that he wandered the wilderness, spoke with beasts and birds, and whoever he turned his attention upon became a *majzub* too (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 240, folio 228). Of Nanu, whose real name, the editor says, was Namak, though among the people he became known as Nanu (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 242, note 1), the book tells that, hearing that on the Day of Judgement all men save the Prophet would be beardless, he bought tweezers and plucked out every hair of beard and moustache (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 242, folio 230). After his pir's death he left for the wilderness, gave up living among people, and there highway robbers killed him in the year 1135 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 242, folio 230). The editor gives his burial place as the village of Kalaske Chima, district Gujrat, Pakistan, the name of the village uncertain in the printed text (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 242, folio 230, note 2).

## Legends and Miracles

Lahori says his *karamat* have no end, and that they are set out in detail in the *Tazkira-e Naushahi* of Muhammad Hayat (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 71, folio 59). He relates two of them through his disciples.

**The worn shirt.** When Shah Abdullah would not be dissuaded from the way of the *majzub*, the saint, left no choice, gave him a shirt he had worn for some days and told him to put it on, and as soon as he wore it the angelic realm was disclosed to him (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 241, folio 229).

**Hafiz Tahir at the door.** When the party of Hindu faqirs came for grain, the saint called the stranger by name, telling Hafiz Tahir to come to him, for his share was with the saint; he ordered his servant to take off his shirt and break the sacred thread at his neck; after which Hafiz Tahir became a Muslim anew, and with a single look the saint brought him to God (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 243, folio 231).

## In Verse

Lahori closes his entry with a Persian chronogram on the saint's passing:

حضرت حاجی محمد پیر نوشہ گنج بخش
آنکہ شد مشہور در اہل جہاں اجلال او
کرد چوں آخر عروج از فرش بر عرش بریں
پیر فیاض است ہم اہل سخاوت سال او

The book gives no translation of the chronogram; its final line carries *pir-e fayyaz*, 'the bountiful pir', the phrase in which the author reads the year 1103 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 72, folio 60; Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 71, folio 59).

Lahori's editor records that his Urdu and Punjabi verse was selected and edited by Sayyid Sharafat Naushahi as *Ganj-e Sharif* and published by Dar al-Mu'arrikhin, Lahore, in 1975 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 71, folio 59). The editor's own list of publications gives it as *Ganj-e Sharif*, the collected Urdu verse of Hazrat Haji Muhammad Naushah Ganj Bakhsh Qadri (d. 1653 CE), compiled by Sayyid Sharafat Naushahi with a foreword by Muhammad Iqbal Mujaddidi, beside a 64-page study of the life and work of Sayyid Sharafat Naushahi, Lahore, 1971 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 307, folio 295). The books consulted quote none of the saint's own lines.

## The Order's Own Record

The Naushahi family's manuscripts were the order's archive, and Lahori's editor draws on them throughout. He lists nine sources for the Naushahia line (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 71, folio 59): Razi al-Din Qazi Ganjahi, *Risala Tuhfa-e Razwiya*, within Mirza Ahmad Beg Lahori's *Risala al-Ijaz*; Ahmad Beg Mirza Lahori, *Risala al-Ijaz*, 1107 AH; Sadaqat Muhammad Mah Ganjahi, *Thawaqib al-Manaqib*, 1126 AH; Hafiz Muhammad Hayat, *Tazkira-e Naushahi*, 1146 AH; Pir Kamal b. Shaikh Usman Lahori, *Sahaif-e Qudsiya*, 1186 AH; Imam Bakhsh Barqandazi Jalandhari, *Mirat al-Ghafuri*, 1186 AH; Muhammad Ashraf Manjri, *Kanz al-Rahmat*, 1220 AH, printed at Lahore in 1330 AH; Ghulam Muhi al-Din Faqir Lahori, *Tashrif al-Fuqara*, about 1248 AH [OCR?]; and Sayyid Sharafat Naushahi's own *Sharif al-Tawarikh*, 1355–1394, three volumes of seven thousand pages, in manuscript in its author's possession. All but the last two, the editor says, are owned by Sayyid Sharafat Naushahi.

The *Tazkira-e Naushahi*, by Hafiz Sayyid Muhammad Hayat Rabbani Naushahi Sahnpalvi (d. 1173 AH), was composed in 1146 AH and is in origin an annotated copy of Mirza Ahmad Beg Lahori's *Risala al-Ijaz* with additions, holding only the affairs of the Naushahia elders; four manuscripts are in Sharafat Naushahi's library and two in the Punjab University Library (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 71, folio 59, note 1). The author himself cites it by name as a written source (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 242, folio 230), and the editor cites a copy as Muhammad Hayat Naushahi's *Tazkira-e Naushahi*, a manuscript owned by Sayyid Sharafat Naushahi, Gujrat (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 243, folio 231). The edition's bibliography adds the *Risala al-Ijaz*, on the lives of the shaikhs of the Naushahia line, of 1107 AH, owned by Maulana Sayyid Sharafat Naushahi of Sahan Pal, Gujrat (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 292, folio 280), and Naushahi works such as the *Manaqibat-e Naushahi* by Umar Bakhsh Rasulnagri, the *Bayaz-e Qadri* by Ghulam Qadir Naushahi, and Gul Muhammad Naushahi's *Lataif-e Gul Shahi* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 294, folio 282). Which of these treat Noushah Ganj Bakhsh himself the book does not say.

## Setting: Phalia

Haroon Khalid, writing of the town and not of the shrine, records the story he had heard that Phalia "was named after Alexander's legendary horse Bucephalus— Phalus became Phalia", and that the horse's grave was there (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated). His companion Salman Rashid disputed it: "The horse is not buried here ... He was buried near Jalalpur on the other side of the river's bank where his grave was swept by the flood only a few months after"; "Coins dating back to the Indo-Greek era were discovered from the pahari of the city of Phalia, giving birth to the rumour"; and "This version became popular during the colonial era when British officers roamed all over the country collecting oral history" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated). The old mound, Rashid said, now has "a government office there along with a school", and Iqbal Qaiser relayed an old shopkeeper's report: "That pahari has been sold by the department for nine crore rupees. Now there will be a shiny, new shopping plaza there" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **Where the saint is buried.** The current account places his shrine at Ranmal Sharif, near Phalia. Lahori's editor writes that his mazar is at present at Sahnpal Sharif, district Gujrat (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 71, folio 59, note). Chishti's damaged passage names a mazar in district Shahpur and a mazar at Sahn Pal, a name printed uncertainly, in district Gujrat, without making clear whose each is (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 483–484).
- **The year of his death.** The current account gives 1654. Lahori gives 1103 AH, with the chronogram *pir-e fayyaz* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 71, folio 59). His editor's footnote gives 12 Rabi al-Awwal 1064 AH and calls 1103 AH wrong (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 71, folio 59, note 2). The editor's own list of publications gives 1653 CE (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 307, folio 295).
- **His birth.** The current account gives 1552. Lahori's editor gives 1 Ramadan 959 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 71, folio 59, note 2).
- **His name.** The current account calls him Syed Muhammad Noushah Ganj Bakhsh. Lahori heads his entry Shaikh Haji Muhammad Qadiri, known as Hazrat Naushah Ganj Bakhsh (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 70–72, folios 58–60), and the index gives his personal name as Haji Muhammad (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 332, folio 320).
- **Who began the order.** The current account and Lahori's entry on him make him its founder: the Naushahia path began with him (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 70, folio 58). In his entry on Shah Maruf Chishti wa Qadri, Lahori writes that through his blessed person the Naushahia line flourished in the Punjab; the pronoun may refer to Shah Maruf or to Sayyid Mubarak Haqqani, named just before (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 50, folio 38).
- **Where he withdrew as a young man.** Lahori writes Sandal Bar; his editor says it should be Gondal Bar (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 70, folio 58, note 2). Chishti speaks of a vast, empty wilderness and of study at the village of Hakothara, a name printed uncertainly, near Nowshera (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 489–490; Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 490).
- **His mother's name.** Lahori gives Bibi Jiwani (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 316, folio 304); Chishti Mulla Bi Jiwan, a reading printed uncertainly (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 486).
- **The chain through Pir Muhammad Sachyar.** Lahori names the link above the saint Pir Muhammad Sachyar and the link below him Shah Farid Lahori (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 272, folio 260); Chishti's version of the same chain reads Hazrat Bajibar and Shah Farid, all printed uncertainly (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 337), while his list of khalifas has Pir Muhammad Sachyar, again printed uncertainly (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 484).
- **Shaikh Mittha's death and name.** The author gives 1115 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 240–241). The editor writes that 1115 is wrong and 1076 is right by the family traditions, spelling the name, in a form printed uncertainly, differently from the author's Shaikh Mittha (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 241, folio 229, note 1).
- **Shah Abdullah's death.** The author gives 1131 AH, in figures and in words (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 241). The editor says that the family sources make it 1092 AH, and that the year Mufti Sahib enters, 1131 AH, is therefore wrong (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 241, folio 229, note 2).
- **Nanu's death.** The author gives 1135 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 242). The editor notes that the *Khazinat al-Asfiya* gives 1132, calls both years wrong, and gives the year of his martyrdom in the family sources as 1094 AH, a figure printed uncertainly (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 242, folio 230, note 2).
- **Hafiz Tahir's death.** The author gives 1136 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 243). The editor says that Hafiz Tahir Naushahi's year of death, 1136 AH, is also wrong, and that by Sayyid Sharafat Naushahi's research the correct year should be 1097 AH, a figure printed uncertainly (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 243, folio 231, note 1).
- **Pak Rahman's death.** The author gives 1153 AH; the editor gives 4 Muharram 1115 AH, a figure printed uncertainly, from three family manuscripts (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 81, folio 69 [marker 79], note 1).

## Legacy

Syed Muhammad Noushah Ganj Bakhsh is remembered as the founder of the Naushahia Qadiri order — one of the enduring Sufi lines of the Punjab — a saint whose long life and organising genius planted a spiritual family that has lasted nearly four centuries. His shrine at Ranmal Sharif remains its living heart and a major centre of Qadiri devotion in the region.

Already in the 1870s Lahori counted the faqirs of the Naushahia path in the Punjab in the thousands (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 70, folio 58), and he found the order's murids numerous in Lahore (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 273, folio 261). Kanhaiya Lal's history of Lahore, which speaks of a Lahore tomb and not of this site, carries the order's spiritual pedigree up to the saint through the custodian of Shah Kanhh[OCR?]'s tomb outside Mochi Gate: Jawahar Shah, *khadim* of Alahi Shah, he of Gami Shah, he of Muhammad Zaban[OCR?], he of Shah Ismat al-Mand[OCR?], he of Shaikh Abdul Rahman known as Pak Rahman, he of Nau Shah Ganj Bakhsh (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 304, folio 307). At that tomb, Kanhaiya Lal records, Jawahar Shah in the year 1294 AH, the figure printed uncertainly, rebuilt the tomb anew and made the mosque *pakka*; Jawahar Shah has become *sajjada nashin*; and a dalan, a kothri and a well for the faqirs' comfort, stand within the enclosure (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 304, folio 307).
