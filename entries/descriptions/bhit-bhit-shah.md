## Overview

At Bhit Shah, in the Matiari region of Sindh, stands the shrine of Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai (1689–1752), the greatest poet of the Sindhi language and one of the supreme Sufi voices of the subcontinent. His life’s work, the *Shah Jo Risalo* — a vast compendium of mystical poetry set to music — is to Sindh what the *Divan* of Hafiz is to Persia: scripture, songbook, and national treasure at once. His tomb, built on the sandhill (*bhit*) from which he takes his name, is the cultural and spiritual heart of Sindh, and the nightly singing of his verse there, in the tradition of the *Shah jo Raag*, is among the most moving sounds in all of Sufi devotion.

The books consulted bear out that standing, though most of them take the poet, rather than his tomb, as their subject. Christopher Shackle, whose English translation of the *Risalo* appeared in the Murty Classical Library of India, introduces it as "a large collection of Sindhi lyrical poetry by the eighteenth-century" Sufi "poet Shah Abdul Latif (1689–1752) of Bhit, near Hyderabad in modern-day Pakistan" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 8), a work "universally acknowledged to be the greatest classic of Sindhi literature in both Sindh itself and other parts of Pakistan, as well as among the Sindhi émigré population in India and the wider diaspora" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 8, folio not stated). H. T. Sorley, whose study of the poet and his times first appeared in 1940, opens it by saying that the collection "is the only classic which the language of Sind has yet produced in the realm of deeply imaginative literature" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 7, folio vii). Annemarie Schimmel calls him "the greatest mystical poet of the Lower Indus Valley" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 2, folio xii), and writes in another book that "The most outstanding master of popular Sufi poetry in Pakistan is, no doubt," Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit, adding: "He is not the first in the long line of Sindhi mystical poets." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 420, folio 389). Michel Boivin describes "a Sindhi Sufi poet who is acclaimed by all Sindhis as the greatest poet of Sindh and the Sindhi language" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 91, folio 78).

The tomb, for its part, stands in the books as a meeting place of communities. Boivin takes "Shah Abd al-Latif's darbar in Bhit Shah" as his example of the most common kind of shared sacred place, in which "a sacred place is a Sufi tomb which is visited both by Muslims and Hindus" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 178, folio 165). Jürgen Schaflechner writes that the poet's pilgrimage "connects the shrine of Hinglaj Devi with his own dargāh in Bhit Shah (Sindh), and many legends exist telling of the connection between the saint and the Goddess." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 108, folio not stated). Shemeem Burney Abbas records that his shrine "is known in the region as a jamālī (aesthetic) shrine" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 167, folio not stated), for the falsetto in which his *faqirs* sing his verse there.

## The Life of the Poet-Saint

Abdul Latif was born in 1689 into a Sayyid family of Sindh, descendants of a line of saints; his ancestor Shah Abdul Karim of Bulri was himself a noted mystic poet, and the boy grew up steeped in devotion and verse. As a young man he wandered for years with bands of yogis and ascetics through the deserts and mountains of Sindh and beyond, sharing the life of Hindu and Muslim mendicants alike — journeys that filled his poetry with the landscapes of Sindh and a profound sympathy across the boundaries of religion. In his later years he settled on a barren sandhill near the town of Hala, which grew into the settlement of Bhit Shah, and there he gathered disciples and musicians about him, composed and sang, and died in 1752.

The books are frank about how little of this life is documented. Shackle warns that "The largely hagiographic accounts of his life are of the usual limited use in constructing a fully detailed biography" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 9). Schimmel writes: "Contrary to Mir Dard, Shah Latif did not leave any notes about his life or the development of his mystical theories; besides a few dry facts we know about him merely some legends of more or less authentic character. But his poetry is enough to make us acquainted with his personality" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 151, folio not stated). Boivin agrees that "Many legends were framed about him, although it is still difficult to trace his historical character." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 91, folio 78). Sorley found that the period of the poet's life "is totally devoid of any reference to Sind in the East India Company's records", and that in the Bombay Record Office he could trace only one reference to any event in Sind during those years, "and that event is a trivial one" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 28–29, printed 19–20). Of the European travellers, only Captain Alexander Hamilton, who came in 1699, wrote while the poet lived: "He is the only European traveller whose narrative is actually contemporary with the life of Shah Abdul Latif" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 90, folio 81).

Most of the books give his years as 1689 and 1752, with differing confidence. Shackle prints them without qualification in his introduction (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 8), while the Library of Congress record printed in the same volume reads "approximately 1689-approximately 1752" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 5). Sorley hedges them twice: "His dates are not established with complete certainty. But they may be taken as extending from 1689 to 1752" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 172); and "The exact dates of the poet's birth and death are not known. But the evidence is in favour of his having been born in 1689 and having died in 1752" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 179, folio 170). His chronological appendix then sets down the birth in 1689 and the death, at Bhit, in 1752, without a hedge (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 201, folio 192). Schimmel gives the same years without qualification, writing that "It is Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit (1689-1752) who died ten years before Shah Waliullah left this valley of tears" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 24, folio 20), and so does Boivin (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 91, folio 78). Sorley also measures the poet's age against the events of his century. When Hamilton visited Laribunder in 1699, "Shah Abdul Latif was a boy of about eight or nine years of age" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 90, folio 81); "When Aurangzeb died Shah Abdul Latif was a youth of eighteen years of age" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 179, folio 170); "He was fifty years old when Nadir Shah sacked Delhi and made Sind tributary to Persia"; and "Shah Abdul Latif died at the age of sixty-three" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 180, folio 171). Schaflechner prints two other pairs of dates, set out below under the disagreements.

On his family, Shackle explains that the title Shah "indicates his status as a Sayyid claiming direct descent from the prophet Muhammad" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, pp. 8–9), and that he "belonged to one of the many lineages of hereditary" Sufi "saints established in the countryside of Sindh, where they have always enjoyed great prestige and power as pīrs or holy men with a special spiritual authority" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 8). Sorley writes: "He belonged to a notable Alite Sayid family of Matiari. Hence he was the scion of the most illustrious religious house of Sind tracing a connexion to Herat" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 180, folio 171). "The poet was, therefore, born to a position of dignity and power as one of the privileged class", and "He was, as an Englishman might say, born with a silver spoon in his mouth" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 181, folio 172). Schimmel adds that "about his mother's family the traditions are not unanimous", though it is said that he, like Mir Dard, was a sayyid whose lineage went back to the seventh imam, Musa al-Kazim (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 29, folio 25). Abbas records that both Muslim and non-Muslim devotees venerate his shrine because "he was a Syed, who claimed descent from the prophet Muhammad through his daughter Fatima and son-in-law and cousin Ali ibn Abi Talib" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 50, folio not stated).

The books agree that his forebear was Shah Abdul Karim of Bulri, but count the generations differently. Shackle calls Shah Abdul Karim (d. 1623) of Bulri the poet's great-great-grandfather (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 9). Sorley writes that "His ancestor in the fourth generation before him was the famous Sayid Abdul Karim", who flourished about A.D. 1600 and "whose durgah was at Bulri" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 180–181, folios 171–172), and elsewhere that "The poet's great-great-grandfather was the Shah Karim whose name is still held in reverence in Lower Sind" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 212, folio 203). Boivin has him one generation closer: "He was the great-grandson of Shah Abd al-Karim, or Shah Karim. He was born in Hala in a Sayyid family." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 91, folio 78); and Schimmel's *As Through a Veil* likewise calls the poet Shah Abdul Karim's great-grandson (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 171, folio 153). In *Pain and Grace* she writes that "Shah Abdul Latif was born into a family of mystics who lived near Hala; his father, Shah Habib, was a descendant of one of the first noted poets in his mother tongue, Shah Abdul Karim of" Bulri (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 29, folio 25).

Sorley names the poet's father in three ways. "the poet's father, Sayid Habib Shah, was living at a place called Hala Haweli"; later "his father, Shah Habib, went to live in a" nearby "village called Kotri" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 181, folio 172); and a footnote records that "His father Habib Khan died in" 1742 "and was buried in Bhit a few paces from where later in" 1754 the poet's own tomb was erected, the digits of both years being imperfectly printed (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 183, folio 174, footnote 1). Shackle does not name the father, but notes that one verse of the *Risalo* "is supposed to be a response by Shah Latif to his father's appeal in 12.75" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 331).

Sorley keeps his life within a small compass. "He spent nearly all his life living in a very restricted locality in the northern part of what is now the Hyderabad district. In that locality he resided successively in villages called Hala Haweli, Kotri and Bhit Shah" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 180, folio 171). Hala Haweli was in his day "now in ruins, not far from Matiari", and Kotri was in ruins too (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 181, folio 172); Sorley's index lists Hala Haweli apart from Hala (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 435). Schimmel's *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* puts his birth at Hala itself: he "was born into a family of mystics in 1689 in Hala, near present-day Hyderabad-Sind," (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 421, folio 390).

Of his learning, Shackle writes that he was "a member of the rural elite who were trained in the Islamic sciences as transmitted through Arabic and Persian, the standard languages of education" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 9), and that "All the sources agree that he kept three books with him as his primary sources of continual inspiration: the Qur'an, from whose verses he so frequently quotes in Arabic; Rumi's great Persian Masnavī; and the Sindhi verses of his ancestor Shah Karim" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 23). Sorley gives the three books as tradition: "Tradition says that the Koran, the Masnawi of Jalaluddin" Rumi "and the Sindhi verses of Shah Karim were constantly in his hands", and adds: "The general truth of this assertion need not be doubted. But though it may explain some of the characteristic literary and scholarly qualities of the Risalo, it does nothing to account for the poetic excellence of the verses" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 213, folio 204). On his schooling Sorley argues both ways. "How far Shah Abdul Latif was an educated man has proved a great puzzle to scholars. The popular tradition is that he had no regular education but taught himself everything", but "Popular tradition in this respect is, however, unreliable. His poems show clearly an acquaintance with Arabic and Persian far beyond the ordinary accomplishments of his time", so that "it is clear that his education was neither superficial nor contemptible" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 183, folio 174). In a later chapter, though, he calls it "quite certain" that the poet was not a learned man "in the narrow scholastic sense" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 224, folio 215). He names one teacher: "One of his preceptors was a Niir Muhammad Bhatti of whose scholastic ability we are singularly ignorant" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 212, folio 203), whom a quotation from Lilaram Watanmal on the same page calls Niir Mahomad. Schimmel rejects the claim that he could not read: "Mir Ali Shir Qani' claims in his two major historical works that the mystical poet was ummi, 'illiterate;' but that is impossible to accept when one enters into the complicated web of thoughts displayed in the poems, and decorated with Arabic quotations, or allusion to Persian mystical poetry" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 29, folio 25).

Shackle also cautions that "It would however be misleading to see his poetry as the entirely original product of an unlettered genius" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 9). Shah Abdul Karim had written Sindhi poems a century before him, and his older contemporary Shah Inat Rizvi (d. 1711) of Nasarpur, another pīr with whom he "is also known to have been in contact", left a collection "strikingly similar in scope"; "There was, therefore, already an established culture in Sindh of vernacular" Sufi "poetry, although this tends to be overshadowed by Shah Latif's unique reputation in the literary histories" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 9).

His growing following brought him into conflict with the powerful. In Sorley's account, "The independent ways of the young ascetic and contemplative were certain to arouse the jealousy of the orthodox" hierarchy, "especially as the fame of the young man with his saintly manner of living was becoming known and was attracting to him a number of murids. At this stage of his life, therefore, he found himself ranged against the Sayids of Matiari who succeeded for a time in enlisting the support of" Nur Muhammad Kalhoro, "then the most powerful man in Sind". Nur Muhammad, "however, became later convinced of the sincerity and worth of this remarkable young man and attached himself to him in friendship" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 182, folio 173); and "it is related that" the prince, "from whom he had become estranged, won back the poet's favour by presenting him with a fine copy of the Masnawi" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 183, folio 174). Schimmel tells the same reconciliation without naming the prince: Rumi's Mathnawi "served the Kalhoro prince to gain back the sympathies of Shah Abdul Latif" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 165, folio 165).

"A friend of his younger days was one Mirza Moghul Beg, who, in 1713, died an untimely death at the hands of robbers and whose daughter he eventually married" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 182, folio 173). Sorley never names her. "He left no heir, and the oral tradition, which in this respect there is no reason to disbelieve, records the decorum and dignity of his family life" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 181, folio 172). Sorley draws his character from the same tradition: "a man of quiet and unassuming disposition", with "a gentleness of manner and speech, an innate gentlemanliness, a bent of kindness, compassion and generosity"; "He is said to have hated cruelty and to have been unable to bear to beat an animal or cause physical pain to man or beast" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 180–181, folios 171–172). From oral tradition comes his likeness: "the poet in his prime was a well-set-up, handsome, bearded man of average height with fine black eyes, an intelligent face, a noble forehead and the solemn look of a deep-thinking and penetrating mind overcast with thought" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 181, folio 172). Shackle adds that "In popular iconography, Shah Latif is often depicted in the yogic posture of sitting with his head supported by his knees" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 335).

The books report his answer to the question of sect in more than one form. Sorley: "It is stated of Shah Abdul Latif that, when questioned whether he was a Sunni or a Shia, he replied that he was between Sunni and Shia", to which Sorley adds his own inference: "In reality he was more a Shia than a Sunni, because he intended at a later stage of his life to make the pilgrimage not to Mecca but to Kerbela" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 175, folio 166). The journey was never made: he "was dissuaded from doing so by the insistence of his followers that he should not desert them. Instead of going to Kerbela Shah Abdul Latif is said to have composed the Sur Kedaro which deals with the story of Hassan and Husain" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 183, folio 174). Shackle, who calls it "an interesting anecdote" and takes it from Advani (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 26, n. 29), gives it another ending: the poet first "replied that he was in between the two, and when told there was nothing in between, he gave the perfect" Sufi "response by stating, 'I am that nothing'" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, pp. 22–23). Schimmel writes that "he is related to have called himself 'something between Sunni and Shii Islam;'" and reads it as the tradition of *tashayyu' hasan*, "the loving attitude towards Muhammad's family" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, pp. 268–269, folios 268–269).

No book gives him a spiritual master. Shackle, prefacing it with "It appears that", writes that he was not formally affiliated in a chain of spiritual descent to any of the great Sufi orders, and is "therefore generally classed as an Uvaisi" Sufi, "the term given to those whose spiritual initiation comes directly from divine inspiration without any saintly human intermediary" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 9). Schimmel reports the same as tradition, "It is said that Shah Latif was an" *uwaisi* "mystic, e.g., that he had no formal spiritual master, and hence did not belong to any of the acknowledged chains of initiation", and qualifies it at once: "Yet, he quotes with approval an Arabic saying about the necessity of the mystical guide: 'Who walks on the Path without shaikh?'", and "continues his instructions to Sassui with the famous Sufi saying: 'Who has no shaikh, his shaikh is Satan'" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 191, folio 191). "He certainly did not call his listeners to rebellion against the outward forces, or to the formation of a closely knit mystical community. His mysticism is individualistic, and has as its center only one story: that of the return of the loving soul to her Lord" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 187, folio 187). Boivin calls him "a Muslim, a Sayyid, who was a wujudi, preaching throughout his poetry the Oneness of God." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 178, folio 165).

Schimmel places younger poets beside him. Of Abdur Rahim Girhori, his younger contemporary, she writes: "A verse by him expresses also his admiration for Shah Abdul Latif, whom he may have met when still a child" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 28, folio 24), and Sachal Sarmast "met him when still a boy" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 30, folio 26). Of Shah Inayat of Jhok she writes: "It is possible that Shah Abdul Latif alluded to his fate in some of his verses; in any case he has quoted a Persian line usually connected with Shah Inayat" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 26, folio 22). In her translation the line runs:

*The head is sacrificed at the Friend's foot, / which is in order* (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 228, folio 228)

The rest of his life was spent at Bhit. Sorley writes that "he cut himself off from the old ties and founded a village of his own at a place called Bhit", which he glosses as 'sandhill', "so called from its being built on sandhills, in the true ascetic spirit of the religious eremite"; and "It was characteristic of the man" that "he helped to build the village with his own hands" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 182, folio 173). Sorley gives no year for the founding. Shackle: "he later settled in the desert near Hyderabad in lower Sindh at Bhit, a place now known in his honor as Bhitshah ('Shah's Dune')" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 10). Schimmel: "He then settled down in Bhit, not far from his hometown, and gathered a number of disciples around him." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 421, folio 390); and "Later Latif settled in Bhit, where he died without issue in 1752, leaving behind him the collection of thirty Sindhi poems" that became the *Risalo* (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 29, folio 25).

Sorley names the circle around him. "The amanuenses whose help was invoked to record the poems as delivered were named Tamar and Hashim. A great friend of the poet was called Bilal", and "another was called Inayat, himself a poet," some of whose verses stand in the *Risalo* as it exists today (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 184, folio 175). Schimmel tells of "A little joke about Shah Latif's friend and servant" in one of the Surs, in which "he, though 'stinking', 'a glutton', 'ugly,'—to mention only a few of his epithets—becomes purified in the rose-water-like company of the master" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 160, folio 160). "The last years of his life were spent in a halo of sanctity in which the reverence of his followers for him played an important part. Thousands would come to listen to the magic of his poetry and to enjoy the beauty of its message" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 183, folio 174).

Of his end Sorley writes: "It is recorded that shortly before his death he retired into solitude. Coming out again he performed his ablutions, put on a white sheet and ordered the singing of songs." When the music stopped, "he fell into a reverie of divine contemplation" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 183, folio 174). Sorley sees in the life a pattern: "The poet's own life displays the successive stages he went through from poet to sage, ending finally in sainthood" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 214, folio 205). It was lived at a turn of history: "He stood at the parting of the ways between the rule of the Moghuls and that of the Kalhora." (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 172), and "Shah Abdul Latif is the last of the great medieval poets" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 214, folio 205). Yet his verse "contains little or no reference to the current political events which he had witnessed. He was interested not in the transient phenomena of his age but in eternal verities" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 180, folio 171).

## Among the Yogis

The books dwell at length on his years among the yogis. Shackle writes that three surs of the *Risalo* are "devoted to one of most remarkable themes in the Risālo, the wandering yogis who traversed Sindh during their pilgrimage to the shrine of the goddess at Hinglaj in Balochistan", and that "As a young man, Shah Latif is believed to have spent time with these yogis" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 20, folio not stated). Schimmel is more definite in one place: "Shah Abdul Latif spent three years with a group of yogis, wandering through the country, up to the sacred mountain Hinglaj in Balochistan" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 29, folio 25). In another she is more cautious: "Shah Abdul Latif had spent some time—probably three years—in the company of wandering Yogis, and it is said that he visited with them many of the places mentioned in the Risalo. That may or may not be true—but the deep impression which the Yogis left in his heart cannot be denied" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 219, folio 219). In *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* she writes that "In his early years Latif wandered through the country in the company of a group of yogis and perhaps reached the sacred mountain Hinglaj in Balochistan", a place often named in Sindhi mystical poetry as 'the goal' (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 421, folio 390).

Boivin writes that "he was very fascinated by the jogis, considering them the models of renunciation" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 91, folio 78). "He probably travelled with them on a number of Hindu pilgrimages, including Dwarka and Hinglaj. The technical vocabulary used by Shah Latif to name the jogis gives evidence that they were Nathpanthis, and that they were therefore an important religious community in eighteenth-century Sindh." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 92, folio 79). "The jogi, a vernacular form of yogi, is usually the model of renunciation in Sindhi Sufi poetry, especially in Shah Abd al-Latif's work" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 120, folio 107).

Schaflechner, whose book is a study of the Hinglaj shrine, gives the fullest itinerary, each time as report. He writes that, "according to Sindhi history, Shah Abdul Latif (1689–1753) had visited Hinglaj with a group of Kanphata Yogis at the beginning of the eighteenth century." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 108, folio not stated). Elsewhere: "It is said that Latif went to Hinglaj accompanying a group of 'kāpṛī' on their way to the shrine in Balochistan (Agha 2004, 446). Moreover, following the visit to the shrine, the poet continued to wander with the kāpṛīs for three more years, visiting other places like Dwarka (Dwarka district, Gujarat), Lakhpat (Kutch), Lahoot (Khuzdar), and Jaisalmer (Rajasthan) (Agha 2004, 427)." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 95, folio not stated). "Latif's experiences are documented in one part of his magnum opus, the Risalo, in a section called Sur Rāmkalī (Agha 2004, 427)." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 95, folio not stated). The journey still sends pilgrims on the road. Putu, a Kohli pilgrim whom Schaflechner met at Hinglaj, told him: "Long ago Shah Abdul Latif also came here with a group of Yogis. He came here to ask the Mother to help him. See, we are also Yogis when we come here. Those who want something from the Mother are all called Yogis or saṃnyāsīs." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 154–155, folios not stated). "Since the Sindhi poet Latif also walked to Hinglaj, Putu saw it as his duty (Hin. farz) to go on this pilgrimage." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 155, folio not stated); he and the other men in his group "claimed that one of the main reasons for their visit to Hinglaj was its praise in the poetry of Bhittai." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 101, folio not stated).

The journey is read in opposite ways. Schaflechner writes: "This section has been interpreted in various—often diametrically opposed—ways, thereby validating the worldviews of orthodox (Sindhi) Muslims and Sindhi nationalists alike. While Sindhi nationalists look upon Latif as the ideal Sindhi—the perfect example for the tolerant and 'syncretic' history of Sindh—other, more orthodox writers aim to explain away Latif's journey as 'youthful recklessness' (Agha 2004, 427) with the goal of distancing the poet from any possible Hindu influence." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 95, folio not stated). "In such orthodox discourses, Latif's eventual break with the group of ascetics is attributed to a religiously animated disagreement (Agha 2004, 427)." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 96, folio not stated); while for the nationalists "Latif's peaceful journey with Hindu nomads is thus paradigmatic for the liberal character of 'the Sindhi.'" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 96, folio not stated).

How far he travelled is itself in question. Shackle says the many places named in the *Risalo* "support the claim that Shah Latif traveled extensively as a young man" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 10). Sorley is sparing: "For a person of that age he was well travelled. It is true that he did not travel far, but he visited some of the lands adjoining Sind and traversed a great part of Sind in his quest for religious truth" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 181–182, folios 172–173). He quotes the longer itinerary of the Sindhi scholar Gurbuxani, "Gurbuxani says that he visited the places of pilgrimage in Sind and Lakhpat," and on to Hinglaj, Kabul and Kandahar, and doubts it: "But I do not know on what" authority "he holds that Shah Abdul Latif went so far" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 182, folio 173, footnote 1). An informant of Abbas, Shaikh Ghulam Hussain, remembered the journeys as nights of song: "In the case of Shah Latif you will find at least fifty places where he used to spend the nights. Audiences would come there and sit there at night—there would be a paṛāo [a camp]—they would make a fire—and they would have a mehfil—and when he sang his narratives, there would be faqīrs and dervishes with him" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 51, folio not stated). The singer Abida Parvin told her of another journey: "Shah Abdul Latif—he went to Rajasthan—and the person there is a Rajasthani—the Shah took very simple words from him… although the individual from this region is not literate—the Shah acquired great wisdom from him—from the people" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 52, folio not stated).

The yogis live on in the verse. The poem Shah Abdul Latif called *Ramkali* Sorley translates under the title 'The Holy Men', and writes: "That poem, while ostensibly referring to Hindu Jogis, Bairagis, Adesis and other religious beggars, is much wider in its appeal, as is apparent from the passages that can refer only to a belief in Islam." It was typical of the poet's "eclecticism that he should lump all religious mendicants together in this way." (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 300, folio 291). In Sorley's version the poem counts the ascetics' practice day by day over twelve days. On the seventh:

*They washed their threads upon the seventh day, / The Sayid says, and stood with folded hands / Before God Indescribable.* (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 359, folio 350)

On the eleventh day there was fulfilment, the poem says,

*… / Of all Bairagis practised. Holy shrines / The Jogis visited and where there shines / The seat of worship.* (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 360, folio 351)

Schimmel notes that the first known Sindhi example of a poem counting the days of a week or a month is in Sur Ramakali (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 282, folio 264). The same poem in Sorley's English calls the listener to the wanderers' dwellings, and questions the would-be ascetic:

*Come, let us look on the abodes of them / Who pass the day and night in wandering* (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 360–361, folios 351–352)

*Why dost thou bore thine ears / If cold thou canst not bear ?* (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 362, folio 353)

Abbas writes that in this Sur "there are innumerable references to mendicants (faqirs) who are ascetic with slit ears and who wear large rings in their ears" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 51, folio not stated), and Schaflechner that "there are repeated references to the Kapris' slit ears in the section Sur Rāmkalī" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 101, folio not stated). Shackle writes that sur 18 "praises the various groups of Hindu yogis who wandered from one place to another in Sindh or beyond as ideal practitioners of the spiritual life" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 334, folio not stated); of the yogi's horn the verse says, in his English, "There is nothing like it in the north, in Sindh, or in Hind" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 244, folio not stated). Schimmel adds that he "praises also the instruments of the Yogis for their bewitching power" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 283, folio 265).

He also brings the yogis into Islam. Schimmel: "How much Shah Latif has islamized his Yogis becomes clear from verse 32, where he praises the Shiva-followers 'whose leader is Ali.'" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 221, folio 221). Sorley's version has:

*For Sanyasis the path's Ali's field. / They are seated with dust on their heads / Where the plain of Lord Ali's revealed* (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 364, folio 355)

And he warns the yogi against being made an object of worship. In Shackle's translation:

*Do not let yourself be worshiped, oh yogi, but maintain your yoga. Oh master, to make people your servants is a great fault* (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 237)

Another verse on the same page reads "Do not let yourself be worshiped, oh yogi, curses on people's devotion… keep your mouth from pretense" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 237). Schimmel quotes a verse of Sur Asa that measures Muslim and Hindu alike by sincerity:

*You are a liar in infidelity—don't call yourself an infidel: / … / The caste-mark is for those, who are true in their paganism* (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 191, folio 191)

She glosses it: "whether Muslim or Hindu, monotheist or idol worshipper, man has to be sincere and not to make claims to sanctity which he cannot realize" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 191, folio 191). "Shah Abdul Latif, as did his Panjabi contemporaries and his successors in Sind, accepted the Hindus who were striving on the way to perfection as true pilgrims in the eternal Light" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 267, folio 267).

## The Shah Jo Risalo

Bhittai’s poetry, gathered after his death as the *Shah Jo Risalo*, is organised into *surs* — chapters named for the musical modes (*raags*) to which they are sung. Drawing on the folk-romances of Sindh — the tragic heroines Sassui, Marui, Sohni, Momal, and others — he made of their longing and suffering an allegory of the soul’s yearning for God, so that the desert wanderings of a lovelorn girl become the seeker’s journey to the Divine. His verse fuses the deepest mysticism of *wahdat al-wujud* (the unity of being) with the very soil, speech, and song of Sindh, and it carries a fierce compassion for the poor and the suffering. Of his own poetry he is famously said to have insisted that it was no mere craft of words but a vehicle of the sacred:

جي تُو بيت ڀانئين، سي آيتون آهِن

[“What you take to be [mere] couplets are, in truth, verses/signs [of God].”]

Shackle's translation carries the poet's claim in these words:

*What you consider to be poems are divine verses. They direct the mind toward the beloved* (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 109)

His note explains: "In this famous line, Shah Latif claims for his poems (S. baita) a status comparable to the verses of the Qur'an (S. āyatūṉ)" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 326). In his introduction he offers, with his own hedges, a further reading: the contrast "might be seen as an indirect claim for the status of the Risālo as a 'Qur'an in the Sindhi language'… Equally, it might well be said the Risālo is one of those very rare instances in the literary history of South Asia of a genuinely integral Indo-Islamic creation" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 24).

The *Shah Jo Risalo* is sung nightly at his shrine by hereditary singers (*raagi faqirs*) in the unbroken tradition of the *Shah jo Raag*, accompanied by the *tambooro* — a music inseparable from the poetry itself.

How the verses came to be written down is told in several ways. Shackle writes that "Shah Latif's verses were" first "extemporized orally in speech or song, then unsystematically recorded in writing by disciples", and that the *Risalo* "emerged gradually from various collections of the verses Shah Latif had produced on many" occasions; the cult his disciples kept at the shrine preserved "the collection of poetry by Shah Latif and others that was carefully assembled in the historic manuscript known as the Ganj, dated a.h. 1207 (1792 CE)" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 10). Shackle's bibliography lists an edition of it, Mumtaz Mirza's *The Ganj of Shah Abdul Latif Bhitai*, issued at Hyderabad in 1994 by the Bhitshah Cultural Centre Committee (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 348). Sorley doubts the tradition that the poet wrote nothing: "Tradition says that he did not write his poems at all but that they were collected and transcribed by amanuenses. This passes the bounds of credibility", although "The poems were certainly later collated by scribes and formed into a regular collection which exists in several manuscript forms" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 183–184, folios 174–175). "Thus the poems as we know them today are not identical with the verses that came from the lips of Shah Abdul Latif himself as he recited them." They are the verses as later "written down by others, altered by them, and edited by them." (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 233, folio 224). Among the signs of careless compilation he counts "the insertion of short verses by other poets, as for instance Inayat, Lakhmir and" Junejo (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 233, folio 224). Abbas reports another tradition of the text's survival: "Mai Naimat, a maidservant of Shah Abdul Latif, from whose memory his entire Risālo is said to have been reconstructed" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 21, folio not stated).

The word *Risalo* itself names the form. Schimmel: "That was the technical term for collections of poetry which are arranged according to the musical modes" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 29, folio 25); Shah ʿInat's collection was "the first to be arranged in 19 Surs, and was therefore called a Risalo" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 162, folio 162). Schimmel writes that the *Risalo* "consists (in the excellent Bombay edition) of thirty chapters, Sur, which deal with different topics" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 154, folio 154), each of which "is named according to the musical mode in which it is sung, and which points to the contents" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 154, folio 154). A Hyderabad edition of 1974 has a different and longer sequence, of which "Some of the chapters are not found in the other editions" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 154, folio 154, footnote 5). Each chapter ends with a *waʾi*, "that is, a longer poem with one main line which is repeated, in singing, after every line of the poem" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 154, folio 154). Abbas: "Altogether, there are thirty sūrs in the Risālo, and at least ten are dedicated to female heroines, or sūrmīs" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 120, folio not stated); in Sindhi, she adds, "risālo means 'the message.'" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 197–198, folios not stated). Sorley writes: "The poems of the Risalo are set to rags and raginis of the generic types described. These types have of course many local variations. Many of the modal raginis to which some of the surs are sung are Sindhi variants of Hindustani generic forms." (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 231, folio 222); even the poems not named after a mode are sung in a form suited to their subject, "the time of the year or the time of day", "when the song should be sung" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 232, folio 223). Sorley's own translation follows an abridgement, the Muntakhab: "The Muntakhab consists of twenty-seven poems of very unequal length in a variety of metres." (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 234, folio 225).

The folk-romances are the core of it. Schimmel's *As Through a Veil* describes the method: "Since the original tales were known to everyone, he usually begins his poetical renderings with the most dramatic moment in order to describe, with remarkable psychological insight, the moods of the soul on its way to the beloved." (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 171, folio 153). Sohni "swims every night through the Indus to meet her beloved on an island, until the jar which she uses as a kind of life-vest is shattered and she is drowned" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 171, folio 153); Sassui follows "her beloved Punhun through burning deserts and over terrifying hills" and at last "is completely transformed into Love" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, pp. 171–172, folios 153–154); Marui is "the ideal soul", and Nuri "a historical figure, the consort of the Sindhi ruler Jam Tamachi in the fifteenth century", "though a simple fishermaid" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 172, folio 154). "Some of these stories have a historical basis and can be dated back to the 14th and 15th centuries; those belong to Shah's favorite topics" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 154, folio 154). Sorley counts five love stories in the Muntakhab and a sixth, "The story of Sorath and King Diach" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 271, folio 262), and his glossary calls Sasui, as he spells her, "a Brahman girl from Bhambhor" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 431, folio 422). Abbas writes that "Shah Abdul Latif's entire Risālo is based on these narratives, and he evokes mystical poetry for his tragic heroines to become symbols of otherworldly love" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 120, folio not stated), heroines "drawn cross culturally from the centuries-old pre-Islamic myths of the region that he blended into the Islamic mystical framework" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 47, folio not stated). "The male equivalent of the sūrmī is a sūrma, which means a hero; someone who has prowess. Therefore, Shah intends his sūrmis to be perceived as women with prowess" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 120, folio not stated). The singer Alan Faqir gave her his own reason for the poet's choice of tales: "he does not talk of Hir-Ranjha—of Layla-Majnun—because they have no graves—Shah only wrote of that which could be verified" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 157, folio not stated).

The tale of the minstrel and the king is the one in which music itself works the wonder. Boivin: "In 'Sur Sorath', a much-acclaimed chapter of the poetical work the Shah jo Risalo, the minstrel Bijal defies Rao Diach, the king of Junagarh. The power of his lyre (chang) is such that the queens start crying and the forts begin to crumble." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 92, folio 79). "Shah Latif attributes a kind of magic power to him." and "The figure of the 'mendicant minstrel', the charan, is dominant throughout Shah Latif's poetry." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 92, folio 79). For Schimmel the story "becomes for Shah Latif an allegory of the absolute power of the mystical guide for whose sweet songs the initiate gladly sacrifices everything, including his life." (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 283, folio 265).

On the faith the poems express the books differ in emphasis. Boivin writes that "His emphasis on the wahdat-e wujud furthered his resonance among different populations because it indicated that distinctions, especially religious, were disposable in the face of unity." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 92, folio 79), but notes that "The expression wahdat-e wujud is not included in the lexicon, of course, because it was not used by Shah Abd al-Latif." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 100, folio 87); the word he used was *wahdat*, in Sindhi *hekro*, as in "tuhnjo Allah hekro, 'Your God is One'" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 100, folio 87). Sorley writes that for the poet God "and man are things apart", the divine reached only by long struggle (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 237, folio 228), and that "Thus love for Shah Abdul Latif is merely the means of approaching the divine. It is not a description of the divine itself." (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 240, folio 231). "The religion of the Risalo is thoroughly Islamic.", he writes, counting in it at least thirty-four appellations of God, several of them from the Ninety-Nine Names (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 268, folio 259). Schimmel ends her chapter by calling "the Islamic contents of the Risalo" "undeniably strong": the God of the *Risalo* "is a living and loving God; not a philosophical or mystical principle, but rather the Creator, the Merciful and Maj-estic, the First and the Last, and always the Lord as revealed through the Quran" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 262, folio 262). She names the reading she opposes: "The way Shah Abdul Latif unites his descriptions of Sufis and Yogis by explaining the wondrous life of the Hindu ascetics with Quranic terminology has induced many of his Hindu admirers and interpreters to regard his poetry as a perfect blending of the two religious traditions of India" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 236, folio not stated). She also notes that in the *Risalo* "None of the great" orders "is mentioned, nor any of the sanctuaries to which the inhabitants of Sind flocked at many occasions" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 190, folio not stated).

Rumi runs all through it. "Among his influences, Jalal al-Din Rumi was so dominant that his poetry is sometimes called the Masnawi of Sindh." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 92, folio 79). "Quotations from Rumi permeate the Sindhi verses of the eighteenth-century poet" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 358, folio 327). Schimmel singles out other bold strokes. His verse "calls the reader to admire Iblis as the one true lover and to follow his example—a verse that has caused considerable puzzlement to the commentators" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 226, folio 195); he "advised the true Sufi to throw his cap into the fire instead of boasting with it" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 265, folio 234); and he compared the Prophet "to the cloud that sends down the life-giving rain to the thirsty fields, quickening what seemed to be dead" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 248, folio 217). With him, *pūrab* "always denotes the 'mystical' East, the home of the soul" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 288, folio 270).

The language is hard even for Sindhis. Sorley calls him "the first great exponent of the imaginative use of the Sindhi language" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 215, folio 206). Schimmel writes that the style of the *Risalo* "is extremely difficult and offers problems even to those whose mother tongue is the complicated and melodious Sindhi" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 152, folio 152). Boivin finds the proof in the glossaries: "more than two decades after the first publication of Shah jo Risalo in 1866, Sindhi literati thought it necessary to publish Shah's works so that his poetry could be more accessible to the people." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 100, folio 87). "The first glossary was published in 1890 by Lilaram Watanmal Lalwani in a book he wrote in English, with the title" *The Life, Religion, and Poetry of Shah Latif*, and "In 1913, he [Mirza Qalich Beg] published in Sindhi the first book totally devoted to Shah Abd al-Latif's technical lexicon." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 100, folio 87).

The printed text has its own history. The German missionary Ernest Trumpp made "the first print of the famous collection of poetry" at Leipzig in 1866, from a manuscript of "more than 1200 pages" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 152, folio 152). Boivin: "In Sindh, a printing press was brought to Karachi in the 1850s, but the main event in relation to Sufism came with the printing in 1866 of the Shah jo Risalo" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 48, folio 35); the work "was 'canonized' by the British in the middle of the nineteenth century, and later on re-appropriated by the Sindhi intelligentsia" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 212, folio 199, Ch. 3 n. 13). Schimmel's *As Through a Veil* is harsh on Trumpp, who "made some poisonous remarks about one of the masterpieces of classical Sindhi poetry", the *Risalo*, "which he edited with great pains although he thoroughly disliked anything that smacked of mysticism." (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 11, folio not stated). Under British rule, Shackle writes, the "Risālo was pressed into service for the examinations prescribed by the new education syllabus" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 13). Schimmel lists Lilaram Watanmal's book of 1889 and Sorley's of 1940 among the studies that followed, and beneath them "an almost limitless amount of speeches, articles, booklets, and devotional literature was published, mainly in Sindhi" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 153, folio 153); "Even after partition, both the Sindhi Hindus in India and the Sindhi Muslims in Pakistan continued their work, which resulted in a number of editions and analyses" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 152, folio 152). Schimmel also records an Urdu verse translation by Sheikh Ayaz (Hyderabad, 1963) and Elsa Kazi's English selection (Hyderabad, 1965) (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, pp. 421–422, folios 390–391, footnote 75).

## The Shrine

Shah Abdul Latif’s shrine at Bhit Shah, richly ornamented with the blue-and-white glazed tilework (*kashi*) of Sindh, is one of the most beautiful and most visited shrines in the province, and the town that has grown around it is a centre of Sindhi culture, music, and letters. Pilgrims come throughout the year; the singing of his verse continues night after night; and the annual urs, held in the month of Safar, draws vast crowds and hosts one of the great cultural gatherings of Sindh, with music, poetry, and celebration alongside the rites of devotion. The shrine is maintained by the Sindh Auqaf department.

Shackle writes that Bhitshah "is the site of the" magnificent "tomb constructed in his honor by the local ruler Ghulam Shah Kalhoro, where the date of his death is recorded as 14 Safar a.h. 1166 (1752 CE)" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 10). "Although he left no son to assume responsibility for the Bhitshah shrine, his disciples ensured that it became the center of his cult" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 10); Shackle names no custodial family, and gives no date for the building.

Sorley gives the building a date and a craftsman. "An imposing tomb was built for his body by Ghulam Shah" Kalhoro in 1754, "according to the tradition", in the year that Nur Muhammad Kalhoro died (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 183, folio 174), and at Ghulam Shah's expense (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 183, folio 174, footnote 1). "The fine mausoleum built for the poet's body by Ghulam Shah" Kalhoro "was ornamented by a well-known mason of the time, one Idan from Sukkur." (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 185, folio 176). The poet's father lies near it, "buried in Bhit a few paces from where" the tomb was later raised (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 183, folio 174, footnote 1). The mausoleum, Sorley writes, "has become a kind of holy shrine which still draws its crowds" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 185, folio 176), and a visitor of 1882 describes "the wide courtyard where I kept my memorable vigil" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 185, folio 176, footnote 2). "At Bhit are still preserved several relics of him": his long walking stick, "his turban, a piece of cloth he wore, and the beggar's bowl of coconut husk from which he ate and drank frugally" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 181, folio 172). Sorley's chronological appendix, whose printed years are damaged in places, sets the flight and death of Nur Muhammad Kalhoro in 1754, during Ahmad Shah Durani's invasion of Sind (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 201, folio 192), the civil war that ended in Ghulam Shah Kalhoro's ascendancy in 1757–8 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 201, folio 192), and Ghulam Shah's death in 1771 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 202, folio 193); Sorley does not comment on how these dates stand to the tomb's.

Schimmel describes the building from a visit. "Shah Abdul Latif is buried in a beautiful mausoleum in Bhit Shah near Hala, north east of Hyderabad/Sind"; "His shrine, crowned by a graceful minaret, was erected by the Kalhoro prince Ghulam Shah Kalhoro in 1765"; "it is covered with blue and white tiles as are typical of Hala district; they show flower motifs, and the pillars at the corners seem to grow out of large tulips"; and "The ceiling of the entrance hall shows the traditional workmanship of this area: between the wooden framework small brownish-olive-yellowish rectangular tiles are set in, a style found in many saints' tombs in Sind". Her verdict: "Although not outstanding as a work of architecture the building and the adjacent graveyard impress the visitor by their peculiar charm" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 151, folio not stated). In *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* she writes again that the tomb is "covered with blue and white tiles (typical of Hala)" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 421, folio 390).

The dome carries verse. "One of the Persian chronograms composed at his death and inscribed at the northern side of the dome says:"

*When he became intoxicated by union from the goblet of 'Come back' (Sura 89/17). / The hidden inspirer said: 'The year of his journey was Ridwan-i Haqq,' (God's satisfaction) = 1165* (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, pp. 29–30, folios 25–26)

Schimmel gives the chronogram's value as 1165 and does not convert it to a Christian year.

Others lie within its walls. Abbas writes that "A woman was the caretaker of the tombs of Shah Abdul Latif's female relatives who were buried in a compound of the shrine" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 20, folio not stated); she names neither the woman nor the relatives. Boivin speaks of a "local sajjada nashin", without naming him, in noting that "while Shah Abd al-Latif's poetry was highly praised, his darbar at Bhit Shah is not mentioned as a reference point, and no link has been mentioned between the local sajjada nashin and the faqirs" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 60–61, folios 47–48). In Schimmel's book of 1976 a guest house for the visitors to the *ʿurs* has recently been built, and "A cultural center is being developed" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 151, folio not stated); publications on the poet carry the imprint of the "Shah Abdul Latif Bhitshah Cultural Centre Committee" of Hyderabad (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 348; Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 232, folio 219). None of the books consulted records an endowment for the shrine.

## Devotion and Practice

The music of the shrine is the poet's own. Shackle writes that his disciples' cult included "the elaborate tradition of musical performance of his poetry that he himself had devised" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 10). Schimmel: "Shah Latif was an excellent musician, who even invented a new type of tambura (drone instrument), and whose blending of traditional Indian raga and Sindhi folk tunes enhances the beauty of his verses which ought to be sung, not analyzed, to enjoy their full charm" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 29, folio 25). Abbas describes the singing as she found it: "Even today, his faqīrs sing his traditional poetry at the shrine, in the melodies that he composed. They sing his poetry in the falsetto, imitating a woman's voice. The ritual of singing Shah's poetry to his melodies has been performed every evening since the shrine has been there" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 96, folio not stated). "It is precisely for this reason that his shrine is known as the jamālī (aesthetic) shrine" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 50, folio not stated); her endnote explains that "Jamālī mysticism is attained through intuition and creativity, such as by Shah Abdul Latif, a poet and musician. In contrast to this, jalālī mysticism is achieved through rigor and discipline" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 197, folio not stated). "Shah's faqīrs at his shrine in Bhit Shah are renowned for singing the vāī in the falsetto" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 189, folio not stated). Her informant Sikander Baloch told her: "Shah's poetry is kind of melancholic—that is why the faqīrs at his shrine wear black", and "his poetry is sung throughout the night at his shrine—there are maqāms in his poetry when certain sections are sung at particular times of the night—his poetry is sung on the tambūrā" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 63, folio not stated). She counts Bhit Shah among five shrines where "qawwālī is performed every evening after the prayers", the performances continuing "well past midnight" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 42, folio not stated), and writes that "In the Sind, performers like Abida Parvin are invited to sing at Shah Abdul Latif's shrine" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 55, folio not stated).

The books name different days for the regular gathering. Schimmel: "Every Thursday evening, till late at night, the few dervishes who still continue the tradition of Shah Abdul Latif sing his poetry inside the compound of the mausoleum" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 151, folio not stated). Her *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* likewise has the dervishes at his tomb performing his music on Thursday nights, "and the melodies are well known all over Sind" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 421, folio 390). Sorley gives Friday: "Since the poet's death the poems have been recited weekly on Fridays in the mausoleum which has become a kind of holy shrine which still draws its crowds", gatherings he calls 'wakes' (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 185, folio 176). He quotes Dayaram Gidumal's account of one in 1882, when "The deepest silence", broken now and then by a hearty *Allahu*, "prevailed in the wide courtyard where I kept my memorable vigil with more than a hundred men, women and children. The subdued and tranquil look of these people", their simple habits and gentle demeanour, "their strong faith and their erect but humble attitude of mind made a very great impression upon me and inspired me with a most profound veneration and admiration for the poet saint who was their recognized spiritual teacher and whose burning words and breathing thoughts moved them to tears" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 185, folio 176, footnote 2).

Of the *ʿurs* Schimmel writes: "To accomodate at least part of the innumerable visitors who flock to Bhit Shah in the days of the" *ʿurs*, "when the memory of the mystic's death is celebrated in the month of Safar, a guest house has recently been built. A cultural center is being developed" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 151, folio not stated). She gives no day and no count of the crowd. Boivin: "The Hindus belonging to different paths and castes come every day and for the annual urs. They go to the tomb, circumambulating around it, touching it, and then attend the Shah jo rag, the singing of Shah's poetry by the local faqirs." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 178, folio 165). Omar Kasmani notes that, until the late 1960s, the state's Sindhi publications featured the saints of Sindh while ignoring Sehwan's: "other saints of Sindh (especially Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai, Sachal Sarmast, and Shah Inayat) were regularly featured during this time, and their annual fairs were marked by special editions" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 70, folio 53). Abbas cites the proceedings of a Shah Abdul Latif conference held for one of his *ʿurs* celebrations, whose number is not legible in the title as printed (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 219, folio not stated).

Hindus and Muslims both come to him. Boivin: "Known as ziyarat, both Hindus and Muslims bring textiles to put on the tomb, or they only touch and kiss the tomb for obtaining blessings from the buried saint. This is the case of the visit to the dargah by the great mystic poet Shah Abd al-Latif." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 50, folio 37). Abbas writes of the Kolhi and Bhil peasants: "Their women are ardent devotees at Muslim shrines such as those of Shah Abdul Latif at Bhit Shah. They go there primarily because the shrine is located in Hala, the heart of a fertile agricultural belt, where they can make a living working on the large feudal farms" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 46, folio not stated), and gives a second reason: "his poetry is linguistically diverse and is tolerant of other beliefs" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 50, folio not stated). She saw them herself: "I was standing in the shrine courtyard when I suddenly saw a train of graceful, colorfully dressed women in their ghāgrās (long skirts). They were all clad in flowered veils. They walked toward the shrine exit and hastened to pickups parked by the roadside in which their menfolk waited to drive them back" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 134, folio not stated).

The book of his verse is itself revered. "Among musician communities in the Sind, Shah's Risālo is treated with great respect because of its sacred Quranic context, and the book is not allowed to lie around on the floor" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 51, folio not stated).

Beyond the shrine, the verse travelled by work and by song. Schimmel: "They could sing Shah Abdul Latif's poetry when ploughing or going out for fishing, picking cotton or spinning it, and thus the central concepts of Islamic mysticism—Love of God and the Prophet, and trust in the eternal divine wisdom—became part and parcel of their daily lives" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 269, folio 269). She quotes Richard Burton, a century and a quarter earlier: "The learned praise it for its beauty, and are fond of hearing it recited to the sound of the guitar. Even the unlearned generally know select portions by heart, and take the trouble to become acquainted with their meaning" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 152, folio 152). Abbas records that the Manganhar women of Alan Faqir's family "would sing the arifānā-kalām of Shah Latif", finishing with 'and so says Shah Latif—O Latif, I say your sanā [praise]' (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 79, folio not stated). Alan Faqir himself "claimed to be a faqīr of the Bhit Shah shrine" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 78, folio not stated), and said of the poet: "Shah Latif—he is my mentor—he is my murshid" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 157, folio not stated). Abida Parvin "initiated the performance with a mystical invocation from Shah Abdul Latif's poetry in Sindhi" at a concert in Islamabad (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 113, folio not stated), and a film of folk performers in Kacch "demonstrates that Shah Abdul Latif's poetry of the Risālo is sung there in the Sindhi language" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 155, folio not stated). One tradition is absent: of the roving women singers called *loṭevālī*, the broadcaster Naseer Mirza told Abbas, "I have not seen such a tradition at Bhit Shah" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 61, folio not stated).

Among the Hindu Sufis of India whom Boivin studies, the poet is honoured but used differently. "it is amazing to observe that, while the poetry of the greatest Sufi poet, Shah Abd al-Latif, is sung during satsangs, there is no ritual use during the different ceremonies. Furthermore, his poetry, which is praised by everyone as being the quintessence of Sindhi poetry and literature, does not provide data for any prayer" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 108, folio 95). "On 23 May 1957, Rochaldas comes to Poona to meet him, and Sadhu Vaswani asks him to deliver a sermon. His sermon is a comment on Shah Abd al-Latif's verses, completed by quotations from the Jap Sahib." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 165, folio 152). At another shrine's annual fair in April 2016, "there was a huge poster staging Shah Abd al-Latif and Jhulelal, with the epigram: Sab ka malik ek hai" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 186, folio 173). Boivin cites a booklet that traces an Indian darbar's spiritual genealogy back to "the Sufis of Sindh, including Shah Latif, Bedil, Rohal Shah and others" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 68, folio 55).

## Legends and Miracles

Sorley prefaces the stories with a dismissal and then tells them: "Many stories of the miraculous order are told of him. But no serious-minded person will attach any value to them." (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 180, folio 171). The first concerns his schooling: "One typical story of this kind is that when he was being taught the alphabet he would not go beyond the letter Alif, which is the symbol of God and unity." (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 180, folio 171). Schimmel sets the story in a wider pattern: "Many legends tell that a saint was illiterate and knew only the alif: that is said about the Turkish folk poet Yunus Emre, the Panjabi poet Bullhe Shah, and our Abdul Latif as well" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 208, folio 208); the story "is commonplace in the legends of Muslim saints from Turkey to India" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 157, folio 139).

A legend that Sorley takes from Dayaram Gidumal tells how the *Risalo* nearly perished at its birth. When Tamar and Hashim brought him the finished collection, "Shah Abdul Latif turned over the pages and remarked of the frequent laments of Sasui that the work was simply a safety valve for the peccant humours of the flesh and flung it suddenly into the Kirar lake. His secretaries expostulated with him and he allowed them to rewrite the whole from memory." Sorley judges that "it can be no more than a stupid magnification of some trivial incident. But it is more likely to be a legend intended to build up the tradition of the inspired delivery of the poems." (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 184, folio 175).

Another, from the same source, explains why he left no heir. The Shah's wife, while with child, longed one day for a certain kind of fish, and one of his followers was sent to find it. "The Shah happened to enquire about this follower and learning the cause of his absence observed" — "When the embryo is such a trouble to my fakirs, what will the full blown adult be ? May such a blossom be nipped in the bud !" — and "The child, we are told, in consequence was still-born to the great grief of the mother and the Shah left no heir surviving him." (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 184–185, folios 175–176). Sorley calls it a fairy tale, whose object is "to account for the poet's childlessness." (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 185, folio 176).

A third binds him to the man who built his tomb: "Indeed tradition likes to add that the birth of Ghulam Shah" Kalhoro "was the result of the blessing of Shah Abdul Latif." (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 182, folio 173).

Schimmel tells of his meeting with the young Sachal Sarmast, and undercuts it in the same breath: "There is a story that once when a boy came to his convent, the mystic immediately recognized that he would one day" 'open the lid from those things' "that he had kept secret." "The anecdote may have been derived from the pen name Sachal used in his Persian poetry, namely," *Ashikar*, 'Open' (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 424, folio 393).

At Hinglaj, legend gives him a miracle and a flight. "Legend has it that after making a long and difficult journey, Latif had reached the shrine of the Goddess and was offering the Mother some milk from his palm. In this moment, the image came alive, and the Mother drank from the hands of the saint. This evoked the envy of the Yogis, who then decided to kill him. Latif fled the scene and disappeared into the earth only to re-emerge at Thatta, some 350 kilometers away." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 108–109, folio not stated).

None of the books consulted tells of a miracle worked at the tomb.

## In Verse

The books quote the *Risalo* in several English versions: Shackle's prose, Sorley's verse renderings of 1940 and Schimmel's translations. A selection of short passages follows, in the translators' own words.

Several of the books quote the rain-song. Schimmel describes the cloud of mercy, "beginning from the desert area in Eastern Sind, the Thar Parkar", seen floating "from Africa to China", until the poet, "coming closer and closer to his home region", prays:

*My Lord, be kind over dear Sind, / Friend! Sweet darling! You may make the whole world happy!* (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 259, folio 259)

Shackle's translation of the Sarang closes with a prayer that God make Sindh flourish forever, and: "Dear friend and sweet beloved, make the whole world prosperous" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 265). His note says that the final "invocation of God's blessings on Sindh is one of the most often quoted verses of the Risālo" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 338). Bhit itself appears in the poem's rain verses, among the places the rain reaches: in Shackle's English the rains fill the sky "and have come rejoicing to Bhit", with flashes of lightning on the north wind, and "The greenery is fragrant, and grass is piled in heaps" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 261). For Schimmel the Sarang is the finest of the songs that "speak of the poet's hope for Muhammad's intercession" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 223, folio 205): the rain "brings fertility to the parched soil, milk to the cows, bread to the poor", and "Out of this description Shah Latif turns to a short, touching song in which the Prophet, once more the cloud of mercy, is praised because he will protect his community at Doomsday" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 224, folio 206). In Sorley's English the lightning of the rain crosses the Prophet's tomb:

*Across the Holy Prophet's tomb / The lightning streaks did swiftly leap. / They smiled in kindness on the scene / And filled the stream with water deep* (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 343, folio 334)

The poems speak often of tombs and doors. Sorley's translation prescribes a visit to the Prophet's tomb:

*Let Mecca be thy port or no, / Delay not, urge thy vessel on. / Repeat at Lord Muhammad's tomb / The holy words of God and doom* (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 335, folio 326)

It speaks of the round of doors:

*No door like Thine is. I have seen / Of doors my round completely* (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 254, 373; folios 245, 364)

*Why go ye then and bow yourselves / In front of others, why go ye ?* (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 321, folio 312)

And it condemns the worship of pirs. Sorley introduces the passage: "But his puritanical religion is brought out clearly in the scathing passages in which he refers to false practices, and to lapses" from the rigour of asceticism (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 300, folio 291). He prints the lines twice, in his discussion and again, at greater length, among the stanzas of his translation on the Jogis and the Adesis (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 363, folio 354), and names no sur for either printing:

*But of the Lord alone true worship is . / There's none of pir or prophet. They do sin / Who worship pirs and worse than these are they / Who worship idols, those poor luckless folk, / From path misled who grasped untruthfulness. / Self worship not, thou faithful, pure of heart.* (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 300, folio 291)

Burial in the right ground is a kind of life:

*My corse, Lord, convey / To my home that in graveyard of Marus / In fixed peace I may stay. / Perchance in my death I'd be living, / Were my corse in Malir.* (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 428, folio 419)

The journey that matters is the inward one:

*Go with thine heart towards thy love. / Cease, Sasui, wanderings of thy feet. / Ask not the sand how lies the path. / To travel soul-fully is meet.* (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 380, folio 371)

Caste is no bar. The poet signs this verse in the line before it asks why anyone should inquire into the caste of those who come, and says that all who came were welcomed:

*There came fresh message yester night / From Rano. Won is our behest. / The Giver gave it, says Latif.* (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 414, folio 405)

Yet Sasui feels her caste as a burden:

*Mother, my caste is a slur / In the minds of the men of Kech* (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 393, folio 384)

Separation is the master theme. "There is hardly a poem in the Risalo that does not preach this doctrine", Sorley writes, quoting Sur Kalyan:

*The seas of separation roll / And drown each single separate soul.* (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 292, folio 283)

The love of the heroines is dated to before the world:

*Time was when God, the One and the Eternal, spake / Unto the souls and thundered : ' Am I not your Lord ? ' / Then, even then, to Suhini had there come a love / And longing for the neatherd.* (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 406, folio 397)

Schimmel translates the cry of the desert heroine:

*O voice in the desert—as if the grouse were crying . . . / cry from the water's depths—it is the sigh of love. / O voice in the desert, like sound of a violin- / It is the song of love—only the people unaware took it / to be a woman's song.* (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 331, folio 300)

She glosses it: "This mystery of transformation from chaste human love to the summit of divine love remains one of the central themes of great poetry in Iran and its neighboring countries" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 331, folio 300). On the unity behind all things she quotes:

*One castle, a hundred thousand doors, / Windows without number- / Wherever I look, there the Beloved confronts me.* (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 187, folio 169)

And on the letter alif, "cipher for Allah":

*Those who remember the line which begins with alif: / 'There is no goal but Him in both worlds,' [that is what] they think* (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 158, folio 140)

One line, Schimmel writes, has "become almost proverbial in Sindhi" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 241, folio 241):

*Thou makest the leaf drown in the stream, and makest the stone swimming* (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 241, folio 241)

The martyrs of Karbala have a whole Sur. Boivin: "Another neglected issue addressed by Shah Latif is the tragedy of Karbala. In the Shah jo Risalo, there is another chapter (sur) of special interest: Sur Kedaro. The whole chapter is devoted to the martyrdom (shahadat) of the third Shia imam, Husayn." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 92, folio 79). "Shah Latif is the first Sindhi poet to write a marsiyyo in Sindhi, and after him, the genre was adopted by many other poets." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 93, folio 80, endnote 6). Its four parts run from "the coming of Moharram, the month of mourning for the martyrdom of Husayn" to "their union with God after death" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 93, folio 80). Sorley's version opens the month of mourning:

*Muhurram's holy month is come, / The Princes' day of woe.* (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 347, folio 338)

It asks the dyer to dye the mourner's clothes "In sober mourning black", and goes on:

*They wandered forth to cruel doom / When fate with bloodshed came. / Because of their untimely end / I put myself to shame.* (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 347, folio 338)

Schimmel notes that the Kedaro "takes up the motif of nuptials for the death of the martyrs" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 285, folio 267):

*The houris wind rose chains; flower decorations for the martyrs!* (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 285, folio 267)

Work songs are in it too. Abbas quotes four lines of the cotton-spinning songs, the *kapāītī*:

*Not withstanding the spinners / Carded the strands well / And spun out very fine yarn / Accumulated wealth, O mother* (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 152, folio not stated)

Sorley notes that the song he calls the 'Cotton Spinner' is set against the festival of Id (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 145, folio 136). A crew sets out, and vows to the Prophet before sailing; the verse begins:

*They tied their boats with hawsers fast, / So doth Latif the Poet tell.* (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 334, folio 325)

The poet signs his verses inside them. In Shackle's English the signature varies within a single Sur, "Through divine favor she crossed the waves, says Latif" against "Love destroys the force of the current, says Shah" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 99); in Sorley's, once, the full name:

*Abdul Latif it is / Who makes these harmonies.* (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 356, folio 347)

## Reception and Reputation

Sorley makes the poet the measure of the faith of his province. "The real form and meaning of Islam in Sind is not to be found in these ebullitions of popular and incredible fanaticism, but is to be read in the beautiful spiritual poems of Shah Abdul Latif. It will be most unfair to judge Sind by the former and forget the latter." (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 169, folio 160). The poetry, "while itself in the true tradition of Islamic mysticism, nevertheless exercises over Hindus in Sind a spell almost as complete as that exercised by it over Muslims" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 175, folio 166); its message "lies deep in the hearts of all classes of Sind's population, Muslim and Hindu, lettered and unlettered" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 178–179, folios 169–170). Sorley finds one reason in the music: "Most of the musicians in Sind have been Hindus and not Mussulmans. Possibly in this circumstance may be found another reason why the Islamic poetry of Shah Abdul Latif exercises so strong a spell over the non-Muslim inhabitants of the land.", for "the musical forms in which the poems are sung are part of the Hindu heritage of India" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 231, folio 222). Boivin: "Shah is the symbol of Sindhi culture for Muslims as well as for Hindus, in Pakistan as well as in India." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 92, folio 79).

Sorley ranks him highest in Sind and lower beside the Persian masters. He is "the greatest man whom Sind has yet produced in the realm of imaginative art" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 178, folio 169); but beside the Persian masters, "The work is fine but it is on an infinitely lower plane of achievement. Excellent in a humble way though it be, it is not in the same class" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 252–253, folios 243–244). And yet "he can be fully as effective as any of the great Persian poets. Many of his shorter passages would not disgrace them" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 253–254, folios 244–245). "the genius of the Sindhi poet is essentially lyrical. It is neither philosophic nor discursive" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 254, folio 245). "It is extremely doubtful whether poetry like Shah Abdul Latif's can ever again be composed in Sind." (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 214, folio 205). Schimmel, for her part, writes of Sorley that "some of his usually correct statements must be verified and deepened in the light of history of religions" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 153, folio 153).

His reputation is contested. Shackle writes that Pakistani scholarship's readings are "tied to local preoccupations, as when Shah Latif is too narrowly seen as an authentic spokesman of the Sindhi folk tradition or as an advocate of Sindhi nationalism", while Indian scholars "naturally tend to view Shah Latif as one of the many great premodern poet saints who helped to construct the national identity of modern India" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 13); his own corrective is "the common emphasis upon the Islamic character of Shah Latif's poetry" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 14). Schimmel quotes the scholar Rashdi: "Shah was the crown of the Sufis, Shah was a folk poet, Shah was the master of ragas, Shah was a patriot, Shah was a congressman, Shah was a Muslim Leaguer, Shah was Rumi, Shah was Goethe, Shah has produced Hir, Shah has made alive Marui—in short, Shah is the medicine for every illness, Shah is the remedy for every pain. That means, the same thing that has been done to Ghalib and Iqbal we have done to Shah Sahib" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 153, folio 153). She draws the moral: "there is always the danger that great poetry will be interpreted by every reader according to his own feelings, and also according to certain opportunist or even political tendencies; and since contradictions cannot be excluded in the verse of a great poet, let alone a religious, or mystically minded, poet, everyone finds in him what he has sought" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 153, folio 153).

Schaflechner traces his place in Sindhi nationalism. The movement's narratives, in Verkaaik's list, are built on "Sindhi language, the poetry of [national poet] Shah Abdul Latif, the Indus, the peasant or hari, and above all, Sufism and mysticism" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 88, folio not stated), and "According to another publication by Verkaaik, it was in fact G. M. Syed who held primary responsibility for generating Bhittai's role as the most important poet of Sindh (Verkaaik 2004, 33)." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 101, folio not stated). He quotes Abdul Qadir Mangi: "Our ancient civilization Mohen-jo-Daro was, indeed, great. So is our saint-poet Shah Abdul Lateef Bhitai in his art and thought. He is our identity even in this modern age. Every Sindhi, of whatever faith he may be, believes in the greatness of Bhitai. From a Kohli Bheel to a Syed, every one considers Bhitai his spiritual guide." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 95, folio not stated), and comments: "Shah Latif is here described as the embodiment of Sindhi identity that 'even' remains in modern times. The poet is exalted as being the idol of every Sindhi, transgressing every distinction of religion, class or caste." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 95, folio not stated). The singer Alan Faqir read the heroines politically; Abbas writes that he "told me in his interview that Sassi and Marvi in Shah's poetry also represent the land pillaged by conquerors and invaders. He claimed that when he sang Shah's poetry he did it with a passion that would convey the exploitation of the land" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 196, folio not stated). Among the lines he recited was "Punnu is not in the Kec / Sind is not the amir" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 156, folio not stated); a lament of the poet's in Schimmel's translation includes the line 'Punhun is no longer in Kech' (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 235, folio 235).

The Sindhi Hindus who went to India took him with them. Boivin writes that, before Partition, the Sindhi Hindus "shared their passion for Sufi poetry with Muslim Sindhis and others, especially through Shah Abd al-Latif 's Shah jo Risalo" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 14, folio not stated). Ram Panjwani's book on the poet appeared in 1945, "thus before migrating to India", and was "republished in India in 1981" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 157, folio 144). Panjwani named him first of the 'trinity of the Sindhi poets' (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 91, folio 78), and Boivin quotes his image of the three: "He (Shah Abd al-Latif) brings the kettle on the boil, Sachal takes the lid off from it, while Sami passes on the beverage to the rank and file of the people so that they may be brought to the intense awareness of God even when engaged in the day-to-day affairs of life" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 160, folio 147). Boivin also reports Panjwani's quotation of the poet on who a Sufi is: "A Sufi belongs to no sect. No one can label him. He fights with his own selves and divulges nothing. He extends help even to those who are hostile to him" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 160, folio 147). He objects to one Indian reading: "Unfortunately, there is no comprehensive argumentation supplying his claim that 'Shah Abdul Latif (was) a late participant in the Bhakti movement' (Jotwani 1996: 121)." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 88, folio 75). Schimmel, for her part, finds that "the Hindu commentators on" his "poetry failed to understand the importance of the Prophet" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 308, folio 290).

## Setting: Bhit and Its Country

Sorley, drawing on Mirza Kalich Beg and Gurbuxani, describes the place as it was in the poet's time: "Bhit itself, though unattractive as a residence, was set in the midst of striking scenery. It was close to the Kirar lake which was fed by the Ali Ganj or Pahar canal", and "There were several small sheets of water near by with trees and a certain richness of greenery not common in the arid land of Sind except in the immediate vicinity of the river or its large riverain branches" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 182, folio 173). Shackle glosses Kirar as "the name of a depression near" the poet's place of residence at Bhit, which fills with water in the rainy season (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 338).

The books' wider pictures of Sind are of the province, not of this place. Sorley sums up the country for which the poems were made: "It was a land of cultivators and herdsmen, keepers of camels, buffaloes, sheep, goats and oxen, of tombs and Sayids, of dust and heat, of the annual rise and fall of the waters of the Indus but inefficiently controlled by a crude system of irrigation. For the common folk living in such a country Shah Abdul Latif composed his verses" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 265, folio 256). "It was the Kalhora's practice of deferring to the priestly class that led to the extraordinary reverence for pirs, Sayids and holy men, which may be said to have been the predominant characteristic of eighteenth century Sind." (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 163, folio 154). "In the province of Sind alone there are said to be more than a hundred thousand Sufi shrines" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 50, folio not stated). Schimmel tells how, during an invasion "which took place during Shah Abdul Latif's life time", the minister Divan Gidumal offered the invader two bags that "contain the most valuable of Sind's gifts; they contain the holy dust from the tombs of numerous saints and Pirs of Sind" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 25, folio not stated). And, glossing a verse in which a merchant's wife binds cloth on a sacred tree, she writes that "still in our day the trees near Sindhi shrines are decorated with many pieces of cloth, preferably red, in the shape of little flags or tiny cradles, which are attached there for the sake of a vow, as it is usual almost everywhere in the Islamic world" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 168, folio 168).

Sorley warns against reading the poems as a map: "There is surprisingly little of Sind topography in the Risalo." "His place names might be any place names for all the value they possess as guides to definite localities" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 266, folio 257).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **His dates.** The site's account gives 1689–1752, as do Shackle (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 8), Schimmel (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 24, folio 20) and Boivin (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 91, folio 78). The Library of Congress record in Shackle's volume reads "approximately 1689-approximately 1752" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 5), and Sorley says the dates "are not established with complete certainty" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 172). Schaflechner prints "(1690–1751)" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 90, folio not stated) and, elsewhere, "(1689–1753)" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 108, folio not stated).
- **The year of his death in the Hijri reckoning.** Shackle writes that the date recorded at the tomb is "14 Safar a.h. 1166 (1752 CE)" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 10). The Persian chronogram on the dome, as Schimmel gives it, comes to 1165 (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, pp. 29–30, folios 25–26).
- **When the tomb was built.** Shackle names Ghulam Shah Kalhoro as builder and gives no date (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 10). Sorley gives 1754, "according to the tradition", the year of Nur Muhammad Kalhoro's death (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 183, folio 174), while his own chronology puts Ghulam Shah's ascendancy in 1757–8 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 201, folio 192). Schimmel gives 1765 (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 151, folio not stated).
- **How many generations from Shah Abdul Karim.** The site's account calls Shah Abdul Karim of Bulri his ancestor. Shackle (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 9) and Sorley (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 212, folio 203) make him the poet's great-great-grandfather; Boivin (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 91, folio 78) and Schimmel's *As Through a Veil* (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 171, folio 153) make the poet his great-grandson. Shackle dates Shah Abdul Karim's death to 1623 (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 9); Sorley has him flourishing about A.D. 1600 (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 180–181, folios 171–172).
- **Where he was born and where his family lived.** The site's account says a Sayyid family of Sindh. Boivin (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 91, folio 78) and Schimmel's *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 421, folio 390) say he was born in Hala; *Pain and Grace* says his family "lived near Hala" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 29, folio 25). Sorley calls the family "a notable Alite Sayid family of Matiari" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 180, folio 171) and has the father living at Hala Haweli, a ruined village near Matiari, and then at Kotri (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 181, folio 172).
- **The father's name.** Sorley gives Sayid Habib Shah, Shah Habib and Habib Khan (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 181, folio 172; Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 183, folio 174, footnote 1); Schimmel gives Shah Habib (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 29, folio 25); Shackle does not name him.
- **Where Bhit Shah lies.** The site's account places it in the Matiari region, "near the town of Hala". Schimmel has "Bhit Shah near Hala, north east of Hyderabad/Sind" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 151, folio not stated); Shackle has Bhit "near Hyderabad in lower Sindh" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 10); Abbas writes that "the shrine is located in Hala" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 46, folio not stated).
- **The ground at Bhit.** The site's account speaks of "a barren sandhill". Sorley agrees the place was "unattractive as a residence" and built on sandhills, but says it was "set in the midst of striking scenery", near the Kirar lake, with water, trees and "a certain richness of greenery" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 182, folio 173).
- **How far he travelled.** The site's account says he wandered for years with yogis "through the deserts and mountains of Sindh and beyond". Schimmel gives three years, up to Hinglaj (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 29, folio 25), "probably three years" in her later chapter (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 219, folio 219); Schaflechner, after Agha, has three more years after Hinglaj, to Dwarka, Lakhpat, Lahoot and Jaisalmer (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 95, folio not stated). Sorley writes that "he did not travel far" and doubts Gurbuxani's itinerary (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 181–182, folios 172–173).
- **His education.** Popular tradition, as Sorley reports it, held that "he had no regular education"; Sorley first rejects that (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 183, folio 174), then calls him no learned man "in the narrow scholastic sense" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 224, folio 215). Schimmel rejects Qani's claim that he was "illiterate" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 29, folio 25).
- **What he answered when asked his sect.** In Sorley he "replied that he was between Sunni and Shia", and Sorley infers he was "more a Shia than a Sunni" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 175, folio 166). In Shackle he goes on to say "'I am that nothing'" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, pp. 22–23). Schimmel reads the answer as the tradition of "the loving attitude towards Muhammad's family" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, pp. 268–269, folios 268–269).
- **Who restored the lost text.** In the legend Sorley reports, the secretaries Tamar and Hashim rewrote the whole *Risalo* from memory after he threw it into the Kirar lake (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 184, folio 175). Abbas reports that it was Mai Naimat, his maidservant, "from whose memory his entire Risālo is said to have been reconstructed" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 21, folio not stated).
- **When the singing is held.** The site's account speaks of nightly singing. Abbas says it is performed "every evening since the shrine has been there" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 96, folio not stated). Schimmel names Thursday evenings (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 151, folio not stated; Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 421, folio 390). Sorley says the poems have been "recited weekly on Fridays in the mausoleum" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 185, folio 176).
- **Whether every Sur is named for its mode.** The site's account calls the *surs* "chapters named for the musical modes". Schimmel writes that each Sur "is named according to the musical mode in which it is sung" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 154, folio 154). Sorley says the poems are arranged by their musical settings though not all of them are named after the tunes to which they are sung (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 229, folio 220).
- **How many Surs, and how many for the heroines.** Schimmel counts thirty Surs in the Bombay edition and a longer sequence in the Hyderabad edition of 1974 (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 154, folio 154). Abbas counts thirty, of which "at least ten" are for the heroines (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 120, folio not stated); her glossary says nine (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 187, folio not stated); an endnote says ten and lists seven (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 198, folio not stated).
- **The unity of being.** The site's account says his verse "fuses the deepest mysticism of *wahdat al-wujud*". Boivin calls him "a wujudi" and speaks of "His emphasis on the wahdat-e wujud", but notes that he did not use the expression (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 92, folio 79; Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 100, folio 87). Sorley writes that for the poet God "and man are things apart" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 237, folio 228).
- **The beauty of the building.** The site's account calls the shrine "one of the most beautiful and most visited shrines in the province". Schimmel finds it "not outstanding as a work of architecture", though the building and its graveyard "impress the visitor by their peculiar charm" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 151, folio not stated).
- **The Bombay edition's year.** Schimmel's *Pain and Grace* dates Kalyan Adwani's Bombay edition to 1957 (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 4, folio not stated); *As Through a Veil* (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 311, folio not stated) and *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 331, folio 300) give 1958; Boivin lists an Advani edition of 1955 (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 229, folio not stated).
- **The first biography and glossary.** Schimmel dates Lilaram Watanmal's book to 1889 (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 152, folio 152; Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 308, folio 290). Boivin dates Lalwani's first glossary to 1890 (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 100, folio 87).
- **Mirza Qalich Beg's lexicon.** Boivin dates it to 1913 (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 100, folio 87); Sorley's bibliography lists a work by Mirza Kalich Beg under 1914, its title damaged in the printed text (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, pp. 191–192, folios 182–183).
- **Trumpp's date.** Schimmel's *Pain and Grace* (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 152, folio 152) and Boivin (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 48, folio 35) date Trumpp's printing to 1866; *As Through a Veil* dates his remarks on the *Risalo* he edited to 1867 (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 11, folio not stated).
- **The invader who received the saints' dust.** *Pain and Grace* sets the gift in Ahmad Shah Abdali's invasion (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 25, folio not stated); *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* says it was given to "the invading Nadir Shah (1739)" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 427, folio 396).

## Legacy

Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai is at once the national poet of Sindh and one of the towering mystics of South Asia — the man who raised the Sindhi language to its highest expression and made of its folk-tales a scripture of divine love. His *Shah Jo Risalo* is cherished as the soul of Sindh; his music lives in an unbroken tradition at his shrine; and his vision of a love that embraces all beings, across every division of creed and caste, remains a living force. Bhit Shah is not merely his tomb but the cultural heart of a people.

The books measure the endurance in their own ways. Schimmel writes that his poetry "would certainly not have attracted the Sindhi speaking people for more than two centuries, had he only dwelt upon highly mystical topics" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 168, folio 168). Shackle notes that his use of the vernacular, like Bullhe Shah's, "has certainly helped to ensure their continuing popularity across religious boundaries and modern national frontiers today" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 12). Sorley, in 1940, wrote that the poems "still bind Sind with a powerful spell of love and admiration", but that "Modern delight in the poems is of a different genre entirely… Though the ideas in the poems are still believed they are not believed in quite the same way or for quite the same reason" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 215, folio 206). Boivin ends on a question: "Except for some monuments such as the Shah jo Risalo by the Sufi Shah Abd al-Latif, what will be left of the wonderful Sufi poetry in Sindhi among the new generations?" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 34, folio 21).

Sorley dedicated his book to the people of Sind, in verse that calls the poet "your lowly hamlet bard" and "your humble minstrel" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 9), and looks to a land at peace:

*The drums of peacefulness are struck : / Your land a full abundance yields.* (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 9, folio not stated)

His study closes: "This may be all delusion and word-weaving. But if it is merely delusion, the delusion is a very pleasant one, shared by many, like Shah Abdul Latif," whose "verses will continue to delight mankind." (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 297, folio 288).
