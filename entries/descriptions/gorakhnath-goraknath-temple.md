## Overview

The Gorakhnath Temple stands within the historic walled precinct of Gor Khatri, in the heart of the old city of Peshawar in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa. Dedicated to Guru Gorakhnath, the great *yogi* and saint venerated as the founder of the *Nath* monastic order, it is one of the very few surviving and functioning Hindu temples in the city. Though the present temple is a nineteenth-century structure, it sits atop one of the most storied and continuously sacred pieces of ground in the subcontinent, a site whose religious associations stretch back some two thousand years. Closed for decades after Partition, the temple was reopened for worship in 2011 following a legal campaign by Pakistan's Hindu community, and its revival has made it a symbol of the region's layered spiritual heritage.

None of the books consulted names the present temple. Two of them speak of a place at Peshawar whose name matches Gor Khatri. Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi's history of Sufism in India describes a place in Peshawar it calls Gorakhtari, a Buddhist site taken over by yogis that "In the reign of Akbar and Jahangir" was "an important yogi centre of pilgrimage" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 413, folio 389); it never calls the place a temple, names no deity there, and does not name Gorakhnath in that passage. Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, describing Peshawar, records that near the city Kor Khatri, the jogis' temple, was famous, and that it was demolished in the reign of Shah Jahan Badshah (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 503); Bhandari, too, does not name Gorakhnath. Everything else the books say bears on Gorakhnath himself, on the *Nath* yogis, or on Peshawar and its province, and is given below as being about them, not about the temple.

## Gor Khatri: A Sacred Ground Through the Ages

The temple's significance is inseparable from Gor Khatri itself, an elevated mound in Peshawar's old city that has been held sacred, in various traditions, from the Gandhara period through to the Mughal era. The name is popularly interpreted as "Grave of the *Kshatriya*", or of warriors, hinting at ancient funerary or heroic associations. Archaeological excavation, notably the vertical digs begun by Professor Farzand Ali Durrani in the early 1990s, has revealed a remarkable stratigraphy: successive layers of occupation descending some fifteen metres and spanning many civilisations, from the Indo-Greeks of the second century BCE through the Hindu Shahi and later periods, testifying to the site's extraordinary depth of history.

Gor Khatri was long a celebrated resort of the *Nath yogis*, and it is recorded that the Mughal founder Babur, drawn by curiosity about the *Shaiva* ascetics who dominated the place, visited Gor Khatri during his forays into the region. Under the Mughals the site was transformed: in 1641 Jahan Ara Begum, the daughter of Emperor Shah Jahan, built a grand caravanserai here, the Sarai Jahanabad, together with a mosque, a bath and wells for the comfort of travellers. In the nineteenth century, during Sikh rule, the precinct served as the residence and headquarters of the Italian mercenary general Paolo Avitabile, governor of Peshawar.

Rizvi's account of the place comes in his discussion of Guru Nanak's travels: "After his return from the Middle East, Guru Nanak seems to have remained in the Panjab, occasionally visiting Ajodhan, Multan and a place called Gorakhtari." He goes on: "According to Trumpp the existence of such a town has not been substantiated by modern geographers. McLeod rightly locates the site in Peshawar. Babur mentions his visit to a place which according to Persian script is either 'Gorkhattari' or perhaps 'Gorakhtari.'" Of the place's earlier history he writes: "According to Dani the spot was associated with 'the tower of Buddha's bowl.' Like other Buddhist sites occupied by yogis, it became a centre for their activities. In the reign of Akbar and Jahangir it was an important yogi centre of pilgrimage" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 413, folio 389). For these statements Rizvi cites A. H. Dani's *Peshawar, historic city of the Frontier* (1969) and his own Hindi study of the *Babur Nama* (1965) (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 413, folio 389).

Rizvi's book also carries a picture of Babur's visit. Its list of illustrations names "Gorakhattri" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 10, folio not stated), and the plate itself is captioned "Babur visiting Gorkhattri" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 392, folio not stated); the caption says nothing more about the place. His index lists the place twice, under "Gorakhtari" and "Gorkhattari" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 466, folio 442). The identification of Rizvi's Gorakhtari with Gor Khatri rests on the name, the city, and McLeod's location of the site in Peshawar as Rizvi reports it.

Bhandari, writing in the reign of Aurangzeb, gives the place a single sentence in his description of Peshawar: near it, Kor Khatri, the jogis' temple, was famous; it was demolished in the reign of Shah Jahan Badshah (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 503). The same sentence goes on to say that today it is a most delightful *tirth*, and that jogis, sannyasis, bairagis and other dervishes have built pleasing buildings around a tank that is fed by a spring, and live there (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 503). The first word of that clause is uncertain in the printed text, and the book does not make clear whether this present-day account describes Kor Khatri itself or another place by a spring-fed tank. Bhandari names neither Gorakhnath nor any deity at Kor Khatri.

## Guru Gorakhnath and the Nath Tradition

The temple honours Guru Gorakhnath, one of the most influential figures of medieval Indian spirituality, traditionally held to have lived somewhere between the ninth and twelfth centuries CE and revered as a *mahasiddha* and the founder of the *Nath* order of *yogis*. He is remembered as the disciple of Matsyendranath, and the *Nath* tradition he shaped, with its emphasis on *hatha yoga*, asceticism and esoteric discipline, spread widely across the subcontinent and left a deep imprint on popular religion. Peshawar and the wider north-west retained strong *Nath* associations, and Gor Khatri, with its long yogic heritage, was a natural centre for devotion to Gorakhnath. Nearby, the city preserved other shrines linked to the tradition, including the *Dargah* of Pir Ratan Nath in Jhanda Bazaar, reflecting the enduring presence of the *Nath* current in Peshawar's religious life.

## Gorakhnath in the Books

What follows is said of Gorakhnath and of the *Nath* yogis in general; none of these passages names the temple or Gor Khatri.

Rizvi gives the fullest account. Of the figure himself he writes: "Gorakhnath, Matsyendra's disciple, believed to have been born from the sweat of Shiva's breast, saved his master, in whose service he had earlier lost an eye. Much mystery and legend also surround Gorakhnath's personality, but we know he wrote some treatises in Sanskrit" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 353, folio 331). He gives no date of birth or death and no place of burial. He sets Gorakhnath at the head of the order's lineage beside a god: "The transmission of the Nath doctrine however is founded on the trinity: Shiva, Matsyendranath and Gorakhnath" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 354, folio 332), and "The Adi Nath, the First Lord of the Naths, is the Shiva of the Hindus" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 353, folio 331). Of the wider company of saints he is more guarded: "The traditions venerate nine Naths and eighty-four Siddhas but this does not imply the historical authenticity of these figures" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 354, folio 332).

Rizvi credits Gorakhnath with the order of the split-eared yogis: "The framework of the order of the Kanphata (Split Ear Yogis) or the Naths founded by Gorakhnath serves to illustrate the latter's remarkable organizational capacity and foresight" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 354, folio 332). He describes the *Kanphata* initiation rite as still current (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 354, folio 332), and says of the Naths that they "lived in forests, wandered in towns and also established permanent monasteries" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 376, folio 354), naming no monastery. Their goal was bodily mastery: "The perfect yogi can transform his body according to his will and is therefore free of all diseases and death. Siddhas, such as the disciples of Gorakhnath and others are believed to have achieved the practical aspects of this philosophy while in the foothills of the Himalayas and came to be known as the jivan-mukta (liberated while living)" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 357, folio 335).

On Gorakhnath's writings Rizvi distinguishes what survives from what was later attached to his name. "Of the Sanskrit treatises by Gorakhnath, unlike the Hatha-Yoga, the Goraksha-Sataka has survived and has been edited and translated into English" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 354, folio 332). "Gorakhnath's disciples were the authors of a number of treatises on magic, alchemy and left-handed occultism and Hatha-Yoga" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 353–354, folios 331–332). The seventeenth-century *Goraksha-Bijay* was "based on the Sanskrit work said to have been composed by Gorakhnath" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 374, folio 352), and Rizvi's footnote gives its author: "Written by Shaikh Faizu'llah" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 374, folio 352, footnote 3).

Rizvi summarises Gorakhnath's teaching at length. Ultimate Reality "could not be conceived by logical reasoning; it was a super sensuous, super intellectual, direct experience in the state of samadhi (trance)" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 355, folio 333). "Sat-Cit-Ananda-Murti (One who reveals Himself as Being, Consciousness and Bliss), Goraknath [OCR?] [Gorakhnath] believed was the highest form of God, the self-manifestation of the Formless and Manifestationless One—Brahma, Shiva, Parmatma, Parmeswara, the holiest names of the Nameless One" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 355, folio 333). "To Gorakhnath and the Siddhas the phenomenal cosmic system was not false or illusory" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 355, folio 333). "Adhars, according to Gorakhnath, are the main sources of the vital and psychical functions which have to be controlled" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 356–357, folios 334–335). And "According to Gorakhnath the Absolute Truth realized in the highest spiritual experience is above the concept of bhava (existence) and abhava (negation of existence)" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 361, folio 339).

Rizvi records what the yogis claimed for their master, and what they kept hidden. "They considered Brahma, Vishnu and Mahesh to be both angels and disciples of Gorakhnath. They also claimed all the prophets and apostles as his disciples. To yogis the Prophet Muhammad was a pupil and disciple of Gorakhnath but they concealed this belief for fear of Muslim retribution" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 376, folio 354), a passage he draws from the *Dabistan-i Mazahib*. In the same place he writes that "Baba Ratan Haijji [OCR?] [Hajji] was identified with Gorakhnath and it was asserted that the Prophet had learnt Yoga through the Baba" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 376, folio 354). Elsewhere he sets Gorakh beside the awaited Mahdi of Muslim belief: "a yogi becomes a Siddha only after meeting Gorakh, a counterpart of the Mahdi. Both Gorakh and Siddhas exhibit the distinctive features of the Mahdi" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 393, folio 369).

Michel Boivin, whose study of Hindu Sufis names no temple, no Peshawar and no Gor Khatri, describes the Nathpanthis as "the followers of Gorakhnath, who is believed to be an avatar of Shiva" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 77, folio 64). He notes that "in the Persian sources, the main Hindu figure is the jogi", a term "usually employed for the Nathpanthis, a Shivaite school of renunciants who were the followers of Gorakhnath" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 21, folio 8), and speaks of "an ascetic order founded by Gorakhnath" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 120, folio 107). One tradition he reports gives Gorakhnath a part in the legend of Goga Pir: "According to another version, Gorakhnath would have sent an invisible army to help Goga Pir against Rai Pithora" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 188, folio 175).

Christopher Shackle, annotating the lyrics of Bulleh Shah, calls him "Gorakhnath, the head of the Kanphat yogis" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 187, folio 162, n. 12 on 122), and explains the name on which the poem's initiate meditates as "Presumably the name of the yogis' guru Gorakhnath" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 187, folio 162, n. 11 on 122). In the romance of Hir and Ranjha, as Shackle summarises it, "Ranjha goes to the great yogi Gorakhnath for initiation" and returns transformed from a herdsman into a yogi to win back Hir (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 21, folio xx); his glossary describes "the great yogi Gorakhnath, from whom Ranjha sought initiation as a yogi" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 198, folio 173). He also glosses the *Kanphat* ear-piercing rite that Gorakhnath heads (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 187, folio 162, n. 10 on 122). Shackle gives Gorakhnath no date and no lineage.

Harish Dhillon notes that in the Puratan Janamsakhis the account of the anointment of Guru Angad has Gorakhnath "in conversation with Guru Nanak", and comments: "Historically, Gorakhnath lived and preached long before Nanak's time (in the eleventh century)" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 10, folio not stated).

Gorakhnath's name reaches the cult of the goddess Hinglaj in Balochistan, the subject of Jürgen Schaflechner's study: "Tradition has it that Hinglaj was very fond of the Nath community, especially of Guru Gorakhnath. So the Goddess waited day after day in Thatta for Gorakhnath to come to meet her" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 205, folio not stated). Schaflechner tells of "all the food that Hinglaj had prepared in anticipation of his arrival" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 206, folio not stated), and of a boon attributed to Lakhavat: the piled *khic'ṛī* turned to limestone, and "anyone who returned from Hinglaj wearing a necklace of these limestone pebbles would be freed from the fetters of this world" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 206, folio not stated). In the valley he records 'the dhūnī of Guru Gorakhnath' (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 256, folio not stated) and writes that "The fireplace of Goraknath dates back to the long-standing tradition of Nath Yogis who made the journey to Hinglaj", citing Briggs's study of 1938 (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 256–257, folio not stated); "two texts describe the pilgrimage of Guru Gorakhnath to Hinglaj: the Gorakh Caritra and the Gorakhpurāṇa" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 257, folio not stated). "Another story says that Guru Gorakhnath himself took the stone from somewhere around Anil Kund and brought it to this place" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 259, folio not stated). Schaflechner spells the name both Gorakhnath and Goraknath, as the temple's own name does.

## Gorakhnath and the Sufis

Rizvi treats the Naths as a movement in continuous exchange with the Sufis, and gives Peshawar a place in it: "From the eleventh century the Nath yogis began to spread throughout northern India, and from their centre at Peshawar moved to all parts of Central Asia and Iran, at the same time influencing both qalandars and sufis" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 355, folio 333). He names no building at Peshawar in that sentence.

His strongest evidence of that exchange is the *Rushd-Nama* of the Chishti Shaikh 'Abdu'l-Quddus, in which Gorakhnath's name stands for God: "Of the many verses in the Rushd-Nama there are six references to either 'Gorakhnath,' 'Shri Gorakh,' 'Nath' and 'O! Nath.' As in many Nath texts, these words at five different places throughout the work imply Ultimate Reality and Absolute Truth, while in the sixth place, the word refers to the Perfect Siddha or Perfect Man" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 358, folio 336). "All the verses by Shaikh 'Abdu'l-Quddus relating to the simile of the mirror and the polishing of the heart are based on ideas expressed by Ibn al-'Arabi and Gorakhnath" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 363, folio 341).

## In Verse

No book consulted gives verse composed at or about the temple. The verse they carry is about Gorakhnath, or speaks in his name.

Bulleh Shah's lyric of the lover turned yogi, in Shackle's translation, names the authority a Pir must satisfy:

*My Pir gains the approval of guru Gorakh.* (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 124, folio 99)

Rizvi reports that "A number of verses in Panjabi and Hindi also attributed to him were written by successive generations of disciples using the name Gorakhnath in order to establish a spiritual link with their master" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 353, folio 331), the same practice of borrowing a master's name that he finds in the verse attributed to Baba Farid (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 353, folio 331). Among them are the verses of the Hindi *Gorakhbani*: "Some verses ascribed to Gorakhnath in the Hindi Gorakhbani challenge qazis for mechanically crying the name 'Muhammad'" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 361, folio 339).

The Chishti Shaikh 'Abdu'l-Quddus turned Gorakh's own voice upon the yogis. "In the words of Gorakh, Shaikh 'Abdu'l-Quddus warns yogis that wearing rings in their ears and rosaries of Eleocarpus ganitrus around their necks, or the recitation of sakhi and sabad (Nath poetry), fails to make one a yogi" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 360, folio 338).

The city's own name enters the mystical poetry of the Punjab and Sindh only as a word for one of the identities the lover leaves behind. Annemarie Schimmel, paraphrasing that verse, writes that "there is no longer Turk, Hindu, or Peshawari" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 176, folio 158), and in another book that "the Sufi is no longer Arab, Hindu, Turk, or Peshawari; eventually Hallaj and the judge who condemned him, the lover and the theologian, are seen as nothing but different manifestations of the one divine reality" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 417, folio 386).

## Closure and Reopening

The present Gorakhnath Temple was built in the mid-nineteenth century, around 1851, within the Gor Khatri complex. The Partition of 1947 and the departure of much of Peshawar's Hindu population left the temple abandoned, and it remained closed to worship for some six decades, its future uncertain amid changing custody of the site. Its restoration came through the persistence of the Hindu community: in 2011, following a petition and litigation before the Peshawar High Court, the court ordered that the temple be reopened for worship and recognised as part of the site's heritage. The reopening, after roughly sixty years of silence, was widely reported as a significant moment for religious freedom and the preservation of minority heritage in Pakistan, allowing Hindus once again to worship at the ancient shrine.

The books consulted do not record the temple's building, its closure or its reopening.

## Setting: Peshawar and the Frontier

What follows speaks of Peshawar and its province, not of the temple.

Bhandari calls Peshawar an ancient city whose name older books also write in two other forms, Parshawar and Farshawar: Peshawar, he writes, is an old city which the earlier books also write as Parshawar and Farshawar (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 503). He describes the district known as Peshawar, a *tuman* on the side of Hindustan whose name before "known as Peshawar" is uncertainly read, as having grapes, peaches and melons like Turan's and heat, cold, seasons and rain like Hindustan's, with rice better than Bengal's and crops and grain in plenty (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 504). He counts three roads from the city to Kabul (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 502–501): the Nikshab road, with many hard defiles, along which armies pass with difficulty (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 502); a second road, joining the highway at Jalalabad, with narrow defiles, hard passes, little water and Afghan raids (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 502); and the road of Ali Masjid and the Khyber, harder going for two kos yet easier than the others and used by armies and caravans (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 501).

Harish Dhillon, retelling the Janamsakhis, has Guru Nanak on his return from Baghdad: "He visited Kabul before reaching Peshawar" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 224, folio 246).

Haroon Khalid, writing in *A White Trail* of the province's religious minorities, says that "since partition, the province of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (KPK) has been home to a large proportion of religious minorities who have lived there rather peacefully. These are primarily Hindus, who form the largest minority", and that "Hindu festivals are celebrated with much pomp in these areas" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 49, folio not stated). He accounts for their staying in two ways on two pages: at Partition "unlike Punjab the riots were less intense due to the influence of the Indian National Congress there. A lot of Hindus and Sikhs continued living in their ancestral lands even after the creation of Pakistan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 49, folio not stated); and "At the time of partition, unlike Punjab, this area was spared bloodshed primarily due to the influence of the Sarhadi (Frontier) Gandhi – Abdul Ghaffar Khan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 162, folio not stated). He names the province among those where "most of the Hindus are scattered" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 87, folio not stated).

Khalid meets Peshawar's Hindus away from home. In 2011, at the Shivratri festival at Katas, "Hindu pilgrims from Peshawar have arrived in two large buses", about a hundred people, and the musical band "have also come from Peshawar" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 49, folio not stated; Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 51, folio not stated); Hindus from Peshawar had come to Katas in 2010 as well (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 47, folio not stated). One of his informants, Prem Gupta, tells him: "If you want to see the lights of Diwali, go to Peshawar" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 49, folio not stated).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The name of the place.** The site's account calls it Gor Khatri and gives the popular reading "Grave of the *Kshatriya*", or of warriors. Rizvi writes "a place called Gorakhtari", and reports that Babur's Persian script reads "either 'Gorkhattari' or perhaps 'Gorakhtari'" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 413, folio 389); his list of illustrations has "Gorakhattri" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 10, folio not stated) and his plate caption "Gorkhattri" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 392, folio not stated). Bhandari writes Kor Khatri (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 503).
- **Whether the place exists, and where.** As Rizvi reports them, Trumpp held that "the existence of such a town has not been substantiated by modern geographers", while McLeod "rightly locates the site in Peshawar" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 413, folio 389). The site's account places Gor Khatri in the old city of Peshawar.
- **What the place was before the yogis.** The site's account holds the mound sacred "from the Gandhara period through to the Mughal era", with layers from the Indo-Greeks through the Hindu Shahi. Rizvi, following Dani, associates the spot with "the tower of Buddha's bowl" and calls it one of the "Buddhist sites occupied by yogis" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 413, folio 389).
- **The yogis' place under the Mughals.** The site's account calls Gor Khatri a celebrated resort of the *Nath yogis*, has Babur visit it "drawn by curiosity about the *Shaiva* ascetics who dominated the place", and says that under the Mughals the site was transformed when, in 1641, Jahan Ara Begum, daughter of Shah Jahan, built the Sarai Jahanabad there. Rizvi says only that Babur "mentions his visit", and calls the place an important yogi centre of pilgrimage under Akbar and Jahangir (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 413, folio 389). Bhandari says that Kor Khatri, the jogis' temple, was demolished in the reign of Shah Jahan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 503).
- **Gorakhnath's date.** The site's account places him traditionally between the ninth and twelfth centuries CE. Dhillon puts him "in the eleventh century", while the Puratan Janamsakhis, as he reports them, set him in conversation with Guru Nanak (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 10, folio not stated). Rizvi gives no date for Gorakhnath, and dates the Naths' spread from Peshawar "From the eleventh century" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 355, folio 333).
- **Gorakhnath's standing.** The site's account calls him a *mahasiddha*, the founder of the *Nath* order and the disciple of Matsyendranath. Rizvi agrees that he was "Matsyendra's disciple", reports the belief that he was "born from the sweat of Shiva's breast" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 353, folio 331), and calls the *Kanphata* the order "founded by Gorakhnath" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 354, folio 332). Boivin reports the belief that he "is believed to be an avatar of Shiva" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 77, folio 64). Shackle calls him "the head of the Kanphat yogis" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 187, folio 162, n. 12 on 122).
- **Peshawar's Hindus after 1947.** The site's account speaks of "the departure of much of Peshawar's Hindu population" at Partition. Khalid, writing of the province rather than the city, says that "A lot of Hindus and Sikhs continued living in their ancestral lands even after the creation of Pakistan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 49, folio not stated).
- **Why the province was spared at Partition.** Khalid gives the influence of the Indian National Congress (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 49, folio not stated) and, on another page, the influence of Abdul Ghaffar Khan (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 162, folio not stated).

## Significance Today

The Gorakhnath Temple at Gor Khatri is at once a living place of Hindu worship and a window onto the astonishing antiquity of Peshawar, a city where Buddhist, Hindu, *Nath* yogic, Mughal and Sikh histories are stacked one upon another. As one of the few operational Hindu temples in the region and a rare survival of the *Nath* tradition in Pakistan, its reopening restored a thread of continuity to a sacred ground that has drawn worshippers for two millennia, and it remains a cherished site both for devotees and for those who value the shared heritage of the frontier.

George Weston Briggs's *Gorakhnath and the Kanphata Yogis*, the standard study of the order, stands in the bibliographies of two of the books consulted: Boivin cites the 1998 Motilal Banarsidass printing of the work first published in 1938 (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 234, folio 221), and Schaflechner cites it for the Nath yogis' journey to Hinglaj (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 256–257, folio not stated). Rizvi's bibliography adds S. Raghaw's *Gorakhnath aur unka yug* (Delhi, 1963) (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 449, folio 425) and A. K. Banerjea's *Philosophy of Gorakhnath* (Gorakhpur, no date) (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 450, folio 426).
