## Overview

Gurdwara Sri Kiara Sahib stands amid the fields on the eastern side of Nankana Sahib, the town in the Punjab that was the birthplace of *Guru Nanak Dev Ji*, founder of the Sikh faith. Its name comes from the Punjabi *kiara*, meaning a field or plot of cultivated ground, for the gurdwara marks the very field where one of the best-loved miracles of Guru Nanak's boyhood is remembered to have taken place. Set a little apart from the great shrines at the centre of the town, Kiara Sahib belongs to the intimate landscape of sites in and around Nankana Sahib that together preserve the memory of the Guru's early years.

Two of the books consulted name the gurdwara, both of them by Haroon Khalid. In his travelogue *Walking with Nanak*, where the name is spelt Kiari Sahib, the gurdwara is "said to mark the spot where Guru Nanak's buffaloes spoilt the fields of a local farmer who is reported to have complained to Nanak's father" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 91, folio 73). In *A White Trail*, his book on Pakistan's religious minorities, "Gurdwara Kiara Sahib" at Nankana Sahib is the place "where his cattle destroyed the fields of a farmer while he slept" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated). That is all the books say of this gurdwara itself. The others name no gurdwara at Nankana Sahib: Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi's history names the town only as "the village of Talwandi later known as Nankana Sahib, about forty miles south-west of Lahore, now part of Pakistan" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 407, folio 383); Harish Dhillon's retelling of the Janamsakhis speaks of "Nanakana Sahib (earlier called Talwandi)" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 175); and Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, of the town, records only that Baba Nanak was born at Talwandi Rai Bhoi and lived there in his grandfather's house (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). What these books say of the Guru's boyhood, of Talwandi and of the modern town is gathered below as context: it is said of the Guru and of the place, not of this gurdwara.

## The Miracle of the Field

The tradition attached to Kiara Sahib belongs to the cycle of *sakhis*, the accounts of Guru Nanak's life, that centre on his childhood at what was then the village of Rai Bhoi di Talwandi, later renamed Nankana Sahib in his honour. As a boy, Nanak was set to tend the family's cattle, and — as the stories tell — he would let the animals graze while he himself sat absorbed in meditation, heedless of the world about him. On one such day the buffaloes are said to have strayed into a neighbour's standing crop, and the aggrieved peasant hurried to *Rai Bular Bhatti*, the local landlord, to complain that young Nanak's herd had ruined his field.

When the field was inspected, however, the crop was found whole and unharmed, as though nothing had touched it. The onlookers took this for a miracle, and the plot was thereafter revered as *Kiara Sahib*, "the honoured field." For the Sikh tradition the episode is one of a series of signs by which Rai Bular, a Muslim landlord, came to recognise the extraordinary nature of the child in his village, and it is remembered less as a marvel of crops than as an early disclosure of the Guru's sanctity and of the divine favour that rested upon him.

Khalid's two books tell the story more briefly, and differently. In *Walking with Nanak* it is the buffaloes that "spoilt the fields of a local farmer", and the farmer is "reported to have complained to Nanak's father" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 91, folio 73). In *A White Trail* the animals are cattle, they "destroyed the fields of a farmer", and the boy was asleep (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated). Neither book says what was found when the field was inspected, and neither names the farmer.

The herding itself is told in the other retellings of the Guru's youth, though none of them ties it to this field. In Dhillon's retelling of the Janamsakhis, at sixteen the boy herded cattle (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 23, folio 27). In *Walking with Nanak* Khalid also retells Nanak's youth as a novel, a part of the book he himself calls fictionalised (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 17, folio not stated); there Mehta Kalu calls the family "Bedis ... the ancient orators of the Vedas" and himself "an accountant", and the boy herds buffaloes, which once enter a field belonging to "Raj Kumar" [OCR?] (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 63, folio 45; p. 64, folio 46). In the same pages the boy is found hiding in a grove of *waan* trees outside the town by his father and "his employer and friend, Rai Bular" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 64, folio 46). Of this reconstruction Khalid writes that "my depiction of Nanak in this book is not an accurate version of Nanak" but what he wants Nanak "to be like" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 229–230, folio not stated / folio 204).

That the boy showed signs of sanctity is an old claim. Bhandari, writing in Persian under Aurangzeb, records that in his childhood signs of spiritual unveiling and of wonders, marvels beyond the ordinary course of things, became manifest in him; the editor of the printed text gives a variant reading that puts this at the age of ten (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). In Dhillon's retelling, the village astrologer, Pandit Hardyal, foretold the child's greatness (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 18, folio 20), and at school the boy showed "a brilliance that had never before been seen in any child in Talwandi" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 18, folio 20).

## Rai Bular and the Village

The landlord of the story belongs to the family that rebuilt the village. Dhillon writes that Talwandi's last rebuilding "took place under the supervision of Rai Bhoe", a Bhatti Rajput who had converted to Islam (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 15–16, folios 17–18), and that Rai Bhoe's son was Rai Bular; Shiv Ram, head of a Bedi family, was accountant to Rai Bhoe and was succeeded by his son Kalyan Chand Das Bedi (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 17, folio 19), "or Mehta Kalu as he was affectionately called", the Guru's father, accountant of the Bhatti family under Rai Bular (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 17–18, folios 19–20). In Khalid's reconstruction Mehta Kalu keeps "the accounts of the ruler of Talwindi, Rai Bular Bhatti", his friend (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 22, folio 4), and is a Hindu of the 'warrior caste' (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 29, folio 11).

Dhillon's retelling returns to the family later. When Nanak and Mardana came back to Talwandi from the first of his journeys, Nanak spent time daily with Rai Bhoe, who "looked upon" him "as his Guru", and Mehta Kalu was "a little in awe of his son" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 123, folio 137; p. 124, folio 138). Later, after Pak Pattan, "Nanak travelled back to Talwandi"; the village "was now no longer an obscure little village but had become a place of pilgrimage", and Nanak sat by Rai Bular, who was sick and dying (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 172, folio 188).

## The Gurdwara

A shrine was raised in later times to mark the sacred field, and the present building is associated with the great works of gurdwara construction carried out in the decades before Partition. It was reconstructed by *Sant Gurmukh Singh* — remembered by the epithet *Sevavale*, "the one devoted to service," for his lifelong labour of building and restoring Sikh shrines — in the years leading up to 1947. The gurdwara comprises a square, domed sanctum set upon a raised plinth, with a circumambulatory verandah about it, in the characteristic idiom of Punjabi gurdwara architecture, and it stands amid open ground that still recalls the agricultural setting of the original miracle.

None of the books consulted describes the building, dates it or names its builder. Khalid places it. Of "a total of six gurdwaras associated with Guru Nanak at Nankana Sahib" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 90, folio 72), he puts Kiari Sahib with Malji Sahib as "the furthest from Gurdwara Janamsthan" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 91, folio 73). Writing of Rai Bhoi di Talwindi, "which later became Nankana Sahib", he says that it is the place "where there are several gurdwaras marking events from his childhood" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 43, folio 25).

Since the Partition of 1947, when the Sikh population of west Punjab migrated to India, Kiara Sahib — like the other shrines of Nankana Sahib — has stood in a land with few resident worshippers, cared for by the bodies responsible for Sikh heritage in Pakistan and visited chiefly by pilgrims who cross the border on the great anniversaries. On the *Gurpurab* marking Guru Nanak's birth, and on other occasions when *jathas* of pilgrims travel to Nankana Sahib, the gurdwara receives the devotions of the faithful who come to walk the ground of the Guru's childhood.

Elsewhere Khalid's books describe the gurdwaras of the town as a group. "There are seven Gurdwaras in Nankana Sahib, most of which are associated with incidents from the life of Baba Guru Nanak" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 192, folio not stated). "All year round most of them remain locked, only to be opened at the time of the festival. There are security guards employed at the entrance to all of them." (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 227, folio not stated). The name of the Kar Seva is on yellow dustbins and other boards "placed in different Gurdwaras of the city" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 223, folio not stated). Kartar, one of Khalid's informants, remembered the years after Partition: "There was no Sikh left here after partition. Our Gurdwaras became barren and were in a bad state" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 184, folio not stated). Giani Pratap, who in Kartar's account came with an Indian pilgrimage party "sometime in the sixties" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 184, folio not stated), "After looking at the dilapidated condition of the shrines here he decided to stay and look after these holy buildings. That was the first time someone had taken the initiative to take care of the gurdwaras in Nankana Sahib after Partition" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 90, folio 72).

Of the province, Khalid writes that "When Maharaja Ranjit Singh became the sovereign of Punjab at the end of the eighteenth century he spent large amounts of money on the renovation and expansion of many gurdwaras", and "attached vast tracts of land to these gurdwaras, so that the revenues from the lands could facilitate their functioning" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 34, folio 16); in *A White Trail* he says that Ranjit Singh renovated "many Gurdwaras all over Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 208, folio not stated). Of the years after 1947 he writes, of the country as a whole, "I have seen several gurdwaras and temples being taken over and destroyed by the migrants" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated).

## Devotion and Practice

Khalid names Kiara Sahib in his chapter on Guru Nanak's birthday at Nankana Sahib, in a passage on the town's gurdwaras. "Originally called Rai-Bhoi-Di-Talwandi, this city was rechristened Nankana Sahib in honour of Guru Nanak"; because Nanak spent his early years here there are "several Gurdwaras in the city", all of which the pilgrims visit (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated). The festival, which he also calls "Guru Nanak Jayanti or Gurpurab", is large: "According to official estimates, ten to fifteen thousand people come to Nankana Sahib for this festival every year, which is celebrated during the last days of November"; in 2011, "According to media reports, only three thousand people are attending", and "This year it began on the 8th of November and will conclude today, on the 10th of November" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated).

The pilgrims from India, by Khalid's account, come by train from Delhi, Gujarat and Amritsar, cross at Wagah, where representatives of the Evacuee Trust Property Board and the Pakistan Sikh Gurdwara Prabandhak Committee receive them and a *langar* is laid on, and stay three days at Nankana (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 222–223, folio not stated). Three days of *Akhand Path* end in the *bhog*, and the *Nagar Kirtan* follows on the final day; the pilgrims then go on to other cities, richer pilgrims from Britain, America and Canada come by chartered plane and hire buses, and travel companies' posters promise "all" the gurdwaras in Pakistan (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 223, folio not stated). Devotees walk barefoot (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 221–222, folio not stated), and there is "a heavy presence of police officials at all the entrances to the city. No car is permitted inside" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 220, folio not stated). "The entire city looks like a mini-India during these few days" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 226, folio not stated).

The pilgrims come from abroad above all. Khalid counts "Guru Nanak's birthday at Nankana Sahib" among the events that foreign pilgrims kept alive after Partition (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 176, folio not stated), and Sikhs from abroad come to Pakistan for festivals "like Baisakhi and the birthday of Guru Nanak, which were attended by thousands of Indian pilgrims" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 214, folio not stated). Pilgrim tourists "come to Nankana Sahib and Hassan Abdal" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 79, folio 61). At Manora in Karachi, Jeeta Singh holds a small festival "every November when the festival of Guru Nanak is celebrated at Nankana Sahib" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 228, folio 202).

The Sikhs who live in the town today are mostly newcomers. Khalid writes that they "are not indigenous people from the city, but recent migrants from Khyber-Pakhtunkhwa (KPK) where there is a considerable Sikh population" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 235, folio not stated), and that "Most of the newcomers were incorporated within the Gurdwaras, in rooms which were generally reserved for pilgrims" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 176, folio not stated). In *Walking with Nanak* "the Sikh community in Nankana Sahib, which amounts to about two hundred-odd households", has had Jatha Singh as its leader for several years (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 87, folio 69).

## Setting: Nankana Sahib

Talwandi, in Dhillon's retelling, was "the village of Talwandi, which lay between the rivers Ravi and Chenab, directly in the path of the armies that had set their eyes on Delhi"; "Thirteen times was the village of Talwandi destroyed and thirteen times - like the fabled phoenix - it rose again from its ashes" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 15–16, folios 17–18). It "lay in the path of numerous mendicants, sadhus and holy men", who camped "in a grove of trees on the outskirts of the village" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 24, folio 28). Writing of the province in Nanak's century, Rizvi says that "In the fifteenth century, the Panjab enjoyed a peaceful period due to Afghan rule", the Afghans resettling the land and "founding many new towns and promoting trade and commerce" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 408, folio 384).

"The name of Rai Bhoi di Talwindi has been changed to Nankana Sahib in honour of the first Sikh Guru who was born there" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 31, folio 13). The mound of the old settlement "now rests" behind Gurdwara Janamsthan, "away from the hub of the new settlement" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 91, folio 73). Iqbal Qaiser, Khalid's guide, told him that it "is an archaeological mound. The settlement there was destroyed after which Rai Bhoi di Talwindi was constructed within a boundary wall" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 91, folio 73). It is a steep climb "with no proper path", "at least twenty metres from the ground", covered with shrubs of *karir*, with "shards of broken pottery and other mud utensils" underfoot (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 92, folio 74). A young Nanak, Khalid writes, "used to spend a lot of time in conversation with yogis, sadhus and dervish who would pass through Talwindi" there (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 91, folio 73). His route "back from Chuhrkhana to Nankana Sahib, which at that point in time was still known as Rai Bhoi di Talwindi", crossed "a vast jungle that existed then between Nankana and Chuhrkhana" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 86, folio 68).

The modern town, as Khalid saw it, has "one main road that runs through the entire city"; "The entire city needs a major uplift. The buildings are old and the road has pot holes"; "Most of the buildings are from the late British or the early Pakistan era" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 186, folio not stated). From a roof he saw the minarets of mosques, the "small white domes of the Gurdwaras", and the "remains of a few Hindu temples", "cone like structures rising from houses abandoned at the time of partition" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 225, folio not stated). "In 2005, Nankana Sahib was separated from Sheikhupura and made into an independent district, with this city becoming the district headquarters" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 226, folio not stated). It is "a city considered to be one of the holiest cities in the world for the Sikhs, as the founder of their religion, Nanak, was born here" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 183, folio not stated). "Guru Nanak is celebrated all over Nankana Sahib. There is a Guru Nanak High School, a Guru Nanak grocery store and a Guru Nanak cloth shop, all run by Sikhs" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 172, folio not stated).

Khalid found the town's Muslims less warm to the Guru than those he met elsewhere. The regard for Nanak among Muslims at Gurdwara Sachkand, he writes, is not found among the Muslims of Nankana Sahib; Iqbal Qaiser explained that most of them migrated from the other side of the border after Partition, their attitudes "shaped by those experiences" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 76, folio 58). In a third book Khalid notes "a picture of Baba Farid accompanying the picture of Guru Nanak at the shrine of Guru Nanak at Nankana Sahib", without saying which of the town's gurdwaras he means (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).

## Guru Nanak in the Wider Record

What the books say of Guru Nanak himself is said of the Guru, not of this site; it is gathered here because the gurdwara exists for his sake. Dhillon calls the Janamsakhis "cycles of tales purportedly drawn from the life of the first Guru of the Sikhs" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 9, folio 9), and the back cover of his book notes that "The earliest known janamsakhi" dates to 1658, nearly 120 years after the Guru's death (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 271, folio not stated). Khalid calls them "Sikh texts on Nanak's life, written by his devotees after his death", "the primary sources", in "different versions" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 17, folio not stated).

Dhillon has him born "On 14 April 1469" to Kalyan Chand Das Bedi (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 17–18, folios 19–20); the back matter of *Walking with Nanak* gives "Born Talwandi (now known as Nankana Sahib in Pakistan) on October 20, 1469" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 314, folio 288). Bhandari, introducing it as something people say, dates the birth to Samvat 1526, corresponding to 880 Hijri, in the reign of Sultan Bahlul Lodi (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). Khalid writes that the Janamsakhi writer Meherban "claims that Guru Nanak was born in Dera Chahal and not Rai Bhoi di Talwindi", while other Janamsakhi writers and Sikh scholars "are unanimous that Nanak was born in Rai Bhoi di Talwindi" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 43, folio 25). Two stories of the birth point beyond the Sikh tradition. Citing Dhillon's *The First Sikh Spiritual Master*, Khalid writes that Mata Tripta "would regularly visit an ancient Hindu temple situated on top of a mound not very far from Rai Bhoi di Talwindi to pray for a child" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 30–31, folio 13), and a booklet of the Auqaf department states that "Guru Nanak was born after Baba Naulakha Hazari prayed for his mother" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 31, folio 13). Iqbal Qaiser told him: "The Sikhs don't accept it but the Muslim tradition is that Guru Nanak was born by the blessing of Baba Naulakh Hazari." (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).

The years of the boyhood are the years of the field. In Dhillon's retelling, at thirteen Nanak refused the sacred thread, the *janeu*, in his father's courtyard, saying that "it is our actions and our actions alone" that give a man his position; Dhillon calls it "the beginning of a lifelong crusade against the performing of empty rites and rituals" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 19–21, folios 21–23). At sixteen he herded cattle (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 23, folio 27), and he "became well acquainted with the dervish Sayyad Hussain and gained an intimate knowledge of the Sufi line of thought" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 24, folio 28). In Khalid's reconstruction the boy sits at dusk under a *waan* tree hung with cloths "tied as supplication", "about two furlongs" from home, hearing the *azaan* and the temple bells together (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 59, folio 41; p. 60, folio 42); he refuses the *janeu*, which "caused a furore", and "Respectable Hindus started to avoid the Bedi" family (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 61–62, folios 43–44); and his mentor is "Sayyed Hassan, a Muslim Sufi, who lived near his house" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 62, folio 44). The story of the *Sacha Sauda*, the true bargain, begins in Dhillon with goods to be sold in Talwandi (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 25, folio 29); in Khalid's version, "According to the legend, when Baba Guru Nanak turned eighteen, his father asked him to take up a trade. He gave him twenty rupees", and Nanak left "the city (present day Nankana Sahib)" with Bhai Mardana (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 182, folio not stated).

From Talwandi Nanak went to Sultanpur, "around 60 km from Talwandi", as keeper of Nawab Daulat Khan's stores (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 44, folio 49). Dhillon dates his marriage: "He got married on 24 September 1487" to Sulakhini, "daughter of Mul Chand and Chando Rani of the town of Batala", and gives his sons as "Sri Chand (8 September 1494) and Lakhmi Das (12 February 1497)" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 44, folio 49). At Sultanpur, in Dhillon's telling, he vanished in the stream for a week and came out saying, "There is only one God. There is no Hindu, no Mussalman." (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 45–46, folios 50–51). Dhillon sets out two schemes of the *udasis*, the Guru's journeys: "According to the Puratan Janamsakhi… he undertook five journeys" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 54–55, folios 60–61), while "More modern accounts" give four, spanning twenty-three years (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 55, folio 61); Dhillon himself holds to four (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 56, folio 62). Khalid gives the travels as "about twenty-four years", mostly on foot, east to Bengal, south to Sri Lanka, north to Kashmir, Nepal and Tibet, and west to Mecca (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 54, folio 36). "Whenever he would visit an area he would visit its religious shrines, Muslim, Hindu and of any other denomination" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 37, folio 19). At the end, settled at Kartarpur, Nanak sends to Talwandi for his family (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 296, folio 270).

Bhandari calls Baba Nanak a caravan-leader on the paths of truth and torch-bearer of the mystic way (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). Twelve *kos* from Batala on the bank of the Ravi, he writes, is the place of Baba Nanak, where his descendants still live; and people came in multitudes from every land to become his disciples (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). He died, Bhandari writes, in the reign of Salim Shah Afghan, between the ages of seventy and eighty (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520); Dhillon gives the date of his death as 22 September 1539 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 259, folio 287). Bhandari writes that Nanak had a worthy son named Lakhmidas, but passed him over and made a Khatri named Lehna, with the title Guru Angad, his successor at the hour of death (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). "On his deathbed, he appointed not his son but his most loyal student as his spiritual successor", Khalid writes (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 16, folio not stated). Michel Boivin, writing of Sindh, names Sri Chand as "the eldest son of Guru Nanak who was not recognized as guru by the mainstream Sikh community", whose tradition the Udasis follow (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 53, folio 40).

His followers, in Bhandari's account, count service of the traveller, of all who come and go, as the highest worship; whoever arrives at midnight speaking Baba Nanak's name is treated as a brother and a friend and served, even a stranger, a thief or a highwayman (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 519). "It is believed that the tradition of Langar began with Guru Nanak", Khalid writes (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 188, folio not stated). Dhillon's Nanak was "no unworldly mystic" but "a householder in every sense of the word" who "brought a message of peace and harmony to believers of the mutually hostile religions of Islam and Hinduism" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 12, folio not stated). "It is said" that, asked by his Hindu and Muslim companions which religion to adopt, he told them to be true Muslims or honest Hindus: "That would be Nanak's religion" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 219, folio not stated). Khalid, who once found Nanak "my own, being a Punjabi" yet "an alien, since he was a non-Muslim" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 15, folio not stated), writes that he "vehemently spoke against organised religion" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 16, folio not stated) and associated with "people of lowercastes, terming them people of God" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 82, folio 64). "Some scholars have argued that the beauty of Nanak's message is that through his own example, he glorified peasantry" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 303, folio 277).

Others tie him to the Sufis. Rizvi, against W.H. McLeod, argues that "all literate Panjabis, both Hindus and Muslims, at the time of Guru Nanak were conversant with" 'Attar, Rumi and Sa'di (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 420, folio 396). "Guru Nanak was a spiritual follower of Baba Fareed. He traveled to Pak Pattan where he met the spiritual descendants of Baba Fareed and collected the poetry of the saint" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 188, folio not stated). Shemeem Burney Abbas writes that Baba Farid's "texts are revered among the Sikhs because he influenced the founder of their own religion, Baba Guru Nanak" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 87, folio not stated), and that "Reformers and mystics like Kabir" and Guru Nanak "were certainly influenced by Sufism" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 48, folio not stated). Robert Rozehnal reports that a twentieth-century Chishti Sabiri shaykh, in certain lectures, describes "non-Muslim spiritual luminaries—Krishna, Rama, Sita, Lachman, and Guru Nanak—as believers in the one true God and religious leaders with 'Divine connection' (nisbat)" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 63, folio 53). Omar Kasmani, citing Boivin, notes that the trope of the flower in milk is told also of "Guru Nanak, the spiritual leader and founder of Sikhism" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 195, folio 178, ch. 5 n. 17).

His devotion reaches beyond the Sikhs. Khalid writes of Nanak Panthis, "Hindus who believe in the teachings of Guru Nanak, but not in the Khalsa", in whose temples in Sindh and southern Punjab "one will find portraits of Guru Nanak, along with Krishna, Kali Mata and other Hindu deities" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 197, folio not stated), and that "many Muslims, acquainted with the teachings of Guru Nanak, are devoted to him too and visit his shrine" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 220, folio not stated). Boivin notes "a growing similarity between the iconography of Guru Nanak and Jhulelal" in Sindh (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 54, folio 41). Jürgen Schaflechner records "the engraved footsteps of Guru Nanak" in the valley of the goddess Hinglaj, and writes that "The hollowed-out footsteps of Guru Nanak connect Hinglaj to the Sikh and Nanak paṃth (Hin. sect) traditions" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 256, folio not stated; p. 257, folio not stated); "Many Lohanas in Sindh follow the teachings of Nanak" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 80, folio not stated).

## Legends and Miracles

The field of Kiara Sahib is one miracle among many told of the Guru; the others below are told of him, not of this place. The best known is the Kaʿba that turned. "According to legend, Nanak once travelled to Mecca where he performed Hajj"; he slept with his feet towards the Kaaba, was rebuked, turned his feet, "and as he did so, the Kaaba miraculously turned towards his feet"; Khalid comments, "What is important in this story is Nanak's criticism of the concept of a 'sacred space'" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 82, folio 64). In Dhillon's telling Nanak's feet were said to point towards "a place where God does not reside", and the *qazi* conceded that "God lives everywhere" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 198–201, folios 218–221). Another legend, which Khalid gives in a footnote, turns on goods spoilt and restored: a trader told Nanak that his mules carried "Just some mud"; his "sugar and gur (jaggery) had turned into mud", and when he repented "his mud became Sugar and gur once again" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 71, folio 53).

Some writers set the miracles against the man. Dhillon writes of "Guru Nanak's clear and emphatic disavowal of the miraculous" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 11, folio 11), and in his retelling the Guru tells Karori, who calls his cure a miracle, that "there is no such thing as a miracle" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 190, folio 209). Khalid writes that Nanak "abhorred the concept of associating miracles with religious personalities", yet "his biography is nothing but a story of his miracles" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 16, folio not stated). Of the legends in which Nanak humbles a Muslim Sufi, at Sialkot and Pasrur, he writes that they are "pointless stories" and that "His genius lies in the poetic use of words and not in miracles"; Iqbal Qaiser's comment is, "That is to show that our saint is more powerful than yours" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 199, folio 173). For his method Qaiser advised: "read what Nanak wrote at these places and try to get into his mind" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 286, folio 260).

## In Verse

None of the verse the books quote is tied to Kiara Sahib or to Nankana Sahib; it is given here as the poetry of the Guru the gurdwara remembers. Dhillon notes that Nanak's *Bani* "was compiled as part of the Adi Granth by Guru Arjan Dev, the fifth Guru, in 1604" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 9, folio 9). Bhandari says that the Guru composed verses in Hindi on the truth of God, setting out divine unity in clear words and shining metaphors; that a singer named Mardana sang his verses and drew people into the snare of devotion (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520); and that the whole of his followers' worship is the reading of their master's verses, which they recite with song and melody (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 519).

Khalid, who "truly discovered Guru Nanak" through Iqbal Qaiser's recitation of the *Babur Bani*, in which Nanak "lays the blame of Babur's wrath at God's feet" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 15–16, folio not stated), quotes the Guru's verse throughout *Walking with Nanak* in published English translations. The *aarti*, which Qaiser reads as praising a God who resides "in nature", calling Nanak "a naturalist" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 55, folio 37; p. 56, folio 38), begins in one translation:

*The sky the salver, the sun and moon the lamps …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 55, folio 37)

and in another:

*In the salver of the firmament, / The sun and the moon shine as lamps* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 243–244, folios 217–218)

On the oneness of God:

*My Master is One / He is One, brother, the only One* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 60, folio 42)

*God is One, / The Path is One* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 144, folio 126)

The poem on the sacred thread, which in Khalid's reconstruction the boy refused at Talwandi:

*From the cotton of compassion, / Spin the thread of contentment* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 61–62, folios 43–44)

On washing:

*As hands or feet besmirched with slime, / Water washes white* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 192, folio 166)

The song Nanak sings, in Khalid's reconstruction, to innkeepers who meant to rob him:

*The herons in their white feathers dwell in the sacred shrines of pilgrimage* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 123–126, folios 105–108)

Other openings Khalid quotes: *O my merchant friend* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 56–58, folio 40); *Launch ye the boat of austere contemplation …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 148–149, folios 130–131); *As a beggar goes a-begging …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 158, folio 140); and *His goodness cannot be priced or traded …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 280, folio 254).

The Guru's language and forms are part of his legacy. "Nanak incorporated several words which were not part of the Punjabi of the time … which is why Nanak's contribution as a poet to Punjabi is immense" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 278–279, folios 252–253). Abbas writes of the *dohā*, a rhyming couplet in an old Prakrit and Hindi metre: "Poets such as Kabir and Nanak used it to write their mystical poetry" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 141, folio not stated); her glossary defines the *banī* by him: "Devotional verses such as those of Guru Nanak; verses of supplication, entreaty, humility, and reverence" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 179, folio not stated). Annemarie Schimmel, writing of the province, observes that Panjabi "was used almost exclusively by the Sikhs as their literary medium, whereas the Panjabi Muslims resorted to Urdu" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 427, folio 396).

The Guru's verse is sung outside the gurdwaras too. Abbas quotes Abida Parvin: "I sing Shah Hussain, Bulle Shah, Mian Muhammad Bakhsh, Hazrat Khawaja Ghulam Farid, Sacchal Sarmast, Baba Farid, and Guru Nanak" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 54, folio not stated). In Sindh, Boivin records, "Sain Vasan Shah became famous for reciting the banis of Shri Guru Nanak and the kalams of the Sufis" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 68, folio 55), and he reports Ajwani's judgement that "Guru Nanak was the greatest poet of the Bhakti" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 90, folio 77).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **To whom the farmer complained.** The site's account has the peasant hurry to Rai Bular Bhatti, the local landlord. *Walking with Nanak* says the farmer "is reported to have complained to Nanak's father" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 91, folio 73). *A White Trail* does not say (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated).
- **Whether the crop was harmed.** The site's account says the crop was found "whole and unharmed", and that this was taken for a miracle. *Walking with Nanak* has the buffaloes "spoilt the fields of a local farmer" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 91, folio 73); *A White Trail* has the cattle "destroyed the fields of a farmer" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated). Neither book records an inspection or a restoration.
- **What the boy was doing.** The site's account has him "absorbed in meditation". *A White Trail* has the cattle destroy the fields "while he slept" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated).
- **The animals.** The site's account speaks of the family's cattle and has buffaloes stray into the crop; *Walking with Nanak* names buffaloes (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 91, folio 73); *A White Trail* says cattle (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated).
- **The name.** The site's account and *A White Trail* write Kiara Sahib (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated); *Walking with Nanak* writes Kiari Sahib (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 91, folio 73).
- **How Rai Bular is described.** The site's account calls him "the local landlord" and "a Muslim landlord". Khalid's reconstruction calls him "the ruler of Talwindi, Rai Bular Bhatti" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 22, folio 4). Dhillon makes him the son of Rai Bhoe, a Bhatti Rajput who had converted to Islam and under whom Talwandi was rebuilt (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 15–16, folios 17–18; p. 17, folio 19).
- **How many gurdwaras Nankana has.** *A White Trail* counts seven (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 192, folio not stated); *Walking with Nanak* counts six associated with Guru Nanak (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 90, folio 72).
- **The date of birth.** Dhillon gives "On 14 April 1469" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 17–18, folios 19–20); the back matter of *Walking with Nanak* gives "October 20, 1469" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 314, folio 288); Bhandari, introducing it as something people say, gives Samvat 1526, corresponding to 880 Hijri, in the reign of Bahlul Lodi (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).
- **The place of birth.** The site's account and every book place it at Talwandi, now Nankana Sahib; Khalid reports that the Janamsakhi writer Meherban "claims that Guru Nanak was born in Dera Chahal and not Rai Bhoi di Talwindi" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 43, folio 25).
- **The birth and a Muslim saint's prayer.** Khalid, citing Dhillon's *The First Sikh Spiritual Master*, has Mata Tripta pray for a child at an ancient Hindu temple on a mound near Talwandi (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 30–31, folio 13); an Auqaf booklet says he was born after Baba Naulakha Hazari prayed for his mother (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 31, folio 13); Iqbal Qaiser calls this "the Muslim tradition", which "The Sikhs don't accept" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).
- **The signs of childhood.** Bhandari's text dates the first signs of wonders to the Guru's childhood; the editor's variant reading puts them at the age of ten (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).
- **The Guru's wife and sons.** Dhillon has Sulakhini, daughter of Mul Chand and Chando Rani of Batala, married on 24 September 1487, and the sons Sri Chand and Lakhmi Das (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 44, folio 49). Khalid's reconstruction has Sulakhni, "the daughter of Moolchand Khatri from the village of Pakhoke near Gurdaspur", and the sons Sri Chand and Lachman Das (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 67, folio 49; p. 68, folio 50). Bhandari names the son passed over as Lakhmidas (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).
- **How many journeys.** Dhillon reports five *udasis* in the Puratan Janamsakhi and four in more modern accounts, spanning twenty-three years, and holds to four himself (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 54–55, folios 60–61; p. 55, folio 61; p. 56, folio 62). Khalid gives the travels as about twenty-four years (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 54, folio 36).
- **The date of death.** Dhillon gives 22 September 1539 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 259, folio 287); Bhandari gives the reign of Salim Shah Afghan, at an age between seventy and eighty (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).
- **Who cared for the gurdwaras after Partition.** The site's account says the shrines of Nankana Sahib have been cared for by the bodies responsible for Sikh heritage in Pakistan. Kartar says that "There was no Sikh left here after partition" and that the gurdwaras "became barren and were in a bad state" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 184, folio not stated); *Walking with Nanak* says that Giani Pratap's decision to stay was "the first time someone had taken the initiative to take care of the gurdwaras in Nankana Sahib after Partition" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 90, folio 72).

## Legacy

Kiara Sahib is one of the cluster of gurdwaras that make Nankana Sahib the holiest town of the Sikh world after Amritsar, each marking a remembered moment of the Guru's early life — his birth, his schooling, his play, and the miracles of his boyhood. The field of the untouched crop speaks to a theme dear to Sikh devotion: that the greatness of Guru Nanak was manifest from his earliest years, and that even a landlord of another faith was moved to acknowledge it. For the pilgrim, to stand at Kiara Sahib is to enter the pastoral world of the *sakhis*, where a boy lost in the remembrance of God let his cattle wander, and the very fields bore witness to his sanctity.

Khalid calls Nankana Sahib "one of the holiest cities in the world for the Sikhs, if not the holiest" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 221–222, folio not stated). Writing of Pakistan as a whole, he says that "Guru Nanak was born in a city now called Nankana Sahib, Pakistan. He passed away in a small village called Kartarpur also in present day Pakistan", and that "Gurdwaras that were built there later by the devotees still stand tall" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 168–169, folio not stated). When the festival is over, "Things in Nankana Sahib will soon calm down" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 232, folio not stated). Khalid's own book on the Guru, its blurb says, is "not merely a story about gurdwaras" but a retelling of "Nanak the son, the poet, the wanderer, the father, the friend" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 2, folio not stated), and he asks whether "Nanak's legacy in its true essence is also slowly disappearing" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 230, folio 204).
