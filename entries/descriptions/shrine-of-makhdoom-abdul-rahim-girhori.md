## Overview

The shrine of Makhdoom Abdul Rahim Girhori stands at Girhor Sharif, a village some eight kilometres west of Pithoro in Umarkot district, in the semi-desert borderlands of eastern Sindh. It honours a leading Naqshbandi mystic and scholar of eighteenth-century Sindh (1739–1778) who belonged to the Mangrio tribe and came to be known as *Girhori* after he settled at Girhor and established his *khanqah* there. He is venerated as a *shaheed* (martyr), his tomb honoured as that of a martyr-saint, and today the shrine complex is a place of pilgrimage that local sources note is frequented by both Muslims and Hindus, with an annual *urs* in his memory.

None of the books consulted here names the shrine, Girhor Sharif, his tomb, his *urs* or his custodians; Annemarie Schimmel's *Pain and Grace* does not state his burial place even in its account of his death. What the books offer is the man: the scholar-mystic, the disciple of Makhdum Muhammad Zaman, the ascetic, the commentator on the Qur'an in Sindhi verse, and the saint who died attacking a Hindu shrine. Everything below that comes from the books is about Abdul Rahim Girhori himself, or about the country around Umarkot, not about the shrine at Girhor.

Schimmel introduces him as "Another Naqshbandi mystic of Sind, and of quite a different formation", "Makhdum Abdur Rahim Girhori (1739-1778)" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 27, folio 23). Her *As Through a Veil* calls him "The Naqshbandi mystic" Abdur Rahim Girhori, "who was killed when destroying a Shiva temple (1778)" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 224, folio 206), and indexes him with the death year 1778 (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 353, folio 335). Her *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* likewise gives "(d. 1778)" in its index (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 515, folio 484), but names a different order for him in its running text, as set out below.

## The Life of the Saint

Abdul Rahim was among the foremost *khalifas* (deputies) of Khwaja Muhammad Zaman of Luari Sharif (also recorded in this dataset), the celebrated *Sultan al-Aulia* of the Sindhi Naqshbandis, under whom he was trained. Schimmel uses the same word for his position and traces the chain one link further back: Girhori was "the khalifa of the famous saint Makhdum Muhammad Zaman who, in turn, was a disciple of" Mian Abu'l-qasim Naqshbandi (the name is damaged in the printed text) "and Mian Muhammad Thattawi" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 27, folio 23). Elsewhere she names his master Muhammad Zaman Lanwari (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, pp. 260–261, folios 260–261).

She repeats his affiliation in her own characterisations. He is "the militant Naqshbandi Sufi Abdur Rahim Girhori" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 201, folio 201); "even an orthodox Naqshbandi mystic in Sind, like Girhori" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 205, folio 205); and "Shah's younger contemporary", Shah Abdul Latif's, "like Dard a" Naqshbandi "mystic" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 261, folio 261). Of his link with Bhit she writes: "A verse by him expresses also his admiration for Shah Abdul Latif, whom he may have met when still a child" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 28, folio 24).

A learned man, Abdul Rahim Girhori is remembered for his religious scholarship and ascetic life. Schimmel sets his learning apart from the legal scholarship of his fellow Naqshbandis at Thatta: Girhori, "contrary" to "the Naqshbandis of Thatta, was little interested in legal problems; the energetic man even threw an ink" stand "at a scholar's head while attacking the 'dry trees of Makhdum Muhammad Hashim's school.'" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 27, folio 23). Of his religion and his ascetic life she writes: "He completely relied upon Quran and tradition, and led a perfectly ascetic life, with long periods of fasting, which were made even more difficult since he used to take a purgative every third day" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 27, folio 23). In *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* she describes ascetics who "not only fasted and restricted their diet to the absolute minimum but, in addition to that, took a purgative on every third day", and supports the statement with a reference to Umar Muhammad Daudpota's edition of the *Kalam-i Girhori*, p. 23 (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 147, folio 116).

## His Writings

He is said to have composed works in Sindhi, Arabic and Persian. Zulfiqar Ali Kalhoro's account names his principal compositions, *Sharh Abayat Sindhi*, *Fath-al-Fazal*, *Risala Gul Numa* and *Kalam Girhori* among them.

Schimmel describes the same range of work: "Besides, he was a good writer who commented upon his master's Sindhi apophtegmata in Arabic, and composed poetical commentaries of several suras of the Quran. Most of his books were, however, destroyed during the Afghan raids in Upper Sind in 1781" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 28, folio 24). The finest of them, in her judgment, is "his rhymed commentary on Surat al-Kauthar, the favorite theme of mystical veneration of the Prophet", and his mystical teaching on the Prophet is "in some way reminiscent of Dard's deep mystical love of the Prophet" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 28, folio 24).

In *As Through a Veil* she returns to the same commentary as an example of "some highly complicated" *naʿtiyya* "poetry in the Sindhi language" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 224, folio 206). Surat al-Kauthar "was, besides" Aḍ-Ḍuḥā "and An-Najm, always a favorite of those poets who eulogized the Prophet" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 224, folio 206), and "Girhori's commentary takes up many earlier elements; thus he has God say, among other things:" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 224, folio 206)

*So much gift did I not give to anyone but you* (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 224, folio 206)

The Divine address runs on through Jesus, the Torah and Joseph (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 224, folio 206), and closes:

*A small quantity only of your love, O Ahmad, has reached Zulaikha … Hell is at your door supplicating like a beggar* (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 225, folio 207)

In her notes to the same book she cites him once more, "Cf. a similar statement by the Sindhi mystic Girhori in his 'Commentary Surat al-kauthar,'", referring the reader to an article of her own on commentaries and translations of the Qur'an in Sindhi (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 294, folio 276).

## His Thought

Schimmel quotes his teaching in several places. She quotes a saying of his on the perfect mystics, whom he, "as often by Shah, called Kapari, e.g., Yogi, a special group of Sanyasin"; the key words of the saying are damaged in the printed text (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 201, folio 201). He quotes the Arabic saying *ad-dunya sihr*, "'The world is magic'" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 205, folio 205).

On the Prophet, he reads the *haqiqa muhammadiyya*, "the archetype of Muhammad", as "a kind of mediator between the Divine and the human sphere", "following apparently the statements of his master Muhammad Zaman Lanwari" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, pp. 260–261, folios 260–261). Schimmel places Girhori and Mir Dard on the side of those who interpret the Prophet in this way, and Shah Abdul Latif outside it (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 261, folio 261). In *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* she writes that "Exalted descriptions of Muhammad as the Perfect Man, who is like the dawn between the divine light and the darkness of human existence, can be read in the theoretical works of some Sindhi Sufis, for example, the eighteenth-century Suhrawardi mystic" Abdur Rahim Girhori, "who paid with his life for his missionary zeal" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 418, folio 387).

She marks one use of the Qur'an that separates him from Shah Abdul Latif: "the poet has not, as his compatriot Abdur Rahim Girhori did, applied the words of Sura 57/4 'And He is with you wherever you are' to Sassui's journey in the desert" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 242, folio 242).

## His Death

He is venerated as a *shaheed* (martyr): according to traditional accounts he was killed late in his life in a confrontation. Kalhoro's account gives a fuller version: tradition holds that he fell in battle against a rival Hindu ascetic, whom he killed along with destroying the ascetic's *marhi* (shrine), but was himself mortally wounded in the same encounter, an episode remembered as the origin of his *shaheed* status.

Schimmel tells it as a martyrdom sought: "Girhori followed the truly mystical ideal of being killed on the way towards the beloved … he wanted to be a true martyr, shedding his blood for the defense of the true faith. Thus, he set out with 72 companions to destroy the Shiva idol in Hathungo, District Khairpur, and was killed there as he had hoped" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 28, folio 24). In *As Through a Veil* he is the mystic "who was killed when destroying a Shiva temple (1778)", the temple's place unnamed (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 224, folio 206), and in *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* the mystic "who paid with his life for his missionary zeal" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 418, folio 387).

## The Shrine

The shrine stands at Girhor Sharif, some eight kilometres west of Pithoro in Umarkot district, where he had settled and established his *khanqah*, and his tomb is honoured as that of a martyr-saint. Today the shrine complex is a place of pilgrimage that local sources note is frequented by both Muslims and Hindus, and an annual *urs* commemorates him. The shrine's continuing local importance in more recent times is attested by a 2015 Dawn report naming Syed Asif Ali Shah as the custodian of the "Shaheed Abdul Raheem Girhori shrine" at the time of an armed attack on him that stirred sectarian unrest in the area.

The books consulted record no building, builder, date of construction, *urs* day or custodian for the shrine.

## Setting

Michel Boivin's study of the Hindu Sufis of Sindh does not mention Girhori or Girhor Sharif, but it describes the country around them. Pithoro, the nearby town, appears in it as the seat of another saint's tomb: "The tomb of Pithoro Pir is located in the hamlet of Pithoro, between Mirpur Khas and Umarkot, on the railway line which formerly connected Hyderabad (Sindh) to Barmer and Jodhpur, in Rajasthan. As for many other saints, the site of their sanctuary bears the name of the saint." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 189, folio 176). Of that saint's country he writes: "The area of Umarkot where he lived had been a place of rough combat between two groups of Rajputs, the Somras and the Sodhas" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 188, folio 175), and "The area of Umarkot was at the centre of conflicts of local, regional and central interests" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 189, folio 176). Of the Sodhas he writes that "Their chief, Parmar Sodha, would have been established as king (rano) at Umarkot" and that "The Gazetteer of 1907 describes them as worshippers of Shiva" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 191, folio 178). He also notes that "Another important untouchable group was made of the Menghwar, mostly settled around Umerkot" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 40, folio 27).

Other books touch the town. Jürgen Schaflechner records a walking pilgrim group from "a village near Umerkot in Sindh" who covered "around 600 kilometers" on foot to the goddess's temple at Hinglaj (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 152, folio not stated). Shemeem Burney Abbas gives Umarkot as the seat of the vizier Rano in the Mumal myth (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 185, folio not stated). Sujan Rai Bhandari's Mughal history, which spells the town Amarkot, relates that after the desert crossing the emperor's party halted at the fortress of Amarkot, whose ruler received them with the duties of service, and that Prince Muhammad Akbar was born there on the fifth of Rajab of the year 949 [OCR?] (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 284).

On the order he belonged to, Boivin writes that the Naqshbandiyya "came to Sindh from the sixteenth century onwards, with the coming of the Tarkhans and the Arghuns, two Central Asian dynasties that ruled Sindh", that it has "several important centres in Sindh", among them Lowari Sharif, "located in Southern Sindh, near Badin", and that "the majority of the Naqshbandis in Sindh are strongly opposed to music and to the singing of devotional songs" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 228, folio 215). "The Naqshbandis of Sindh followed the reforming enterprise led by Shah Waliullah in Delhi" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 42, folio 29), and "In Sindh, save the Naqshbandis, Sufism is dominated by poetry expressing the concept of wahdat-e wujud" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 162, folio 149). Schimmel writes that "A strong Naqshbandiyya influence in the beginning of the eighteenth century, however, changed the spiritual climate of southern Sind to a certain extent", Makhdum Muhammad Hashim of Thatta and his relatives attacking the Sufis "who defended the emotional side of religion", so that "this Naqshbandi group contributed to promoting a more sober mystical piety" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 428, folio 397). It was this Muhammad Hashim's school whose "dry trees" Girhori attacked.

His books were lost in the troubles that closed the Kalhoro period. Schimmel writes that the Kalhoros were overthrown by "the former disciples of their saintly ancestor, Adam Shah Kalhoro", and that "The Talpurs, who thus came to power in 1781, ruled the country until it was conquered by the British in 1843" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, pp. 30–31, folio not stated); 1781 is also the year she gives for the Afghan raids in Upper Sind in which most of Girhori's books were destroyed (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 28, folio 24).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **His order.** The site's account, *Pain and Grace* and *As Through a Veil* call him a Naqshbandi (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 27, folio 23; Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 224, folio 206). *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* calls him "the eighteenth-century Suhrawardi mystic" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 418, folio 387).
- **His death.** Kalhoro's account, as the site gives it, has him fall in battle against a rival Hindu ascetic, whom he killed while destroying the ascetic's *marhi*, before dying of his own wounds. *Pain and Grace* has him set out "with 72 companions to destroy the Shiva idol in Hathungo, District Khairpur", and be killed there (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 28, folio 24). *As Through a Veil* has him killed "when destroying a Shiva temple", naming no place (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 224, folio 206). *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* says only that he "paid with his life for his missionary zeal" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 418, folio 387).
- **His master's name.** The site's account calls his master Khwaja Muhammad Zaman of Luari Sharif; *Pain and Grace* prints "Makhdum Muhammad Zaman" in one place (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 27, folio 23) and "Muhammad Zaman Lanwari" in another (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, pp. 260–261, folios 260–261).
- **The printed edition of his verse.** All three of Schimmel's books cite Umar Muhammad Daudpota's *Kalam-i Girhori* of 1956, but give its place differently: Karachi (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 295, folio 295); Karachi, at the Matbaʿ al-ʿArab (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 318, folio 300); and "Hyderabad, Sind, 1956. In Sindhi." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 475, folio 444).

## Legacy

His memory forms part of the wider Naqshbandi devotional network that the disciples of Khwaja Muhammad Zaman spread across Sindh in the eighteenth century. Schimmel's three books all cite Umar Muhammad Daudpota's *Kalam-i Girhori*, published in 1956 (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 295, folio 295; Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 318, folio 300; Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 475, folio 444).

Schimmel adds at once, after telling of his death: "It would be wrong, however, to regard Girhori as a typical exponent of Sindhi Sufism" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 28, folio 24). On the whole, she continues, there was "a common basis of understanding, by Muslim and Hindu alike, of the mystical message of Islam," in H. T. Sorley's words, and "a number of Hindus became devoted disciples of Muslim saints" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 28, folio not stated).
