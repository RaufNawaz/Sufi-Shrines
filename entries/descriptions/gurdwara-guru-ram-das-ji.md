## Overview

Gurdwara Janam Asthan Guru Ram Das stands in the Chuna Mandi quarter of the Walled City of Lahore, upon the site traditionally revered as the birthplace and childhood home of Guru Ram Das (1534–1581), the fourth of the ten Sikh Gurus and the founder of the city of Amritsar. Set along the old *Shahi Guzargah*, the "royal road" that ran from the Delhi Gate to the Lahore Fort, near the Fort itself and the Begum Shahi Mosque, the shrine joins Lahore's several great gurdwaras in binding the city to the early history of the Sikh faith.

Of the books consulted, one describes the gurdwara itself: Kanhaiya Lal's Urdu history of Lahore, *Tarikh-e Lahore*. He opens his account with the place: "جس مقام پر گورامداس صاحب مذہب سکہی کے جانشین چہارم کا جنم ہوا", the place where Guru Ram Das, the fourth successor of the Sikh religion, was born, "اور اسی جگہ پرورش پائی", and where he was reared as well (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 101, folio 104). He calls the building "یہ عبادت گاہ سکہی مذہب سے متعلق ہی", a house of worship of the Sikh faith, and writes that Sikhs "اس متبرک مکان کا کمال ادب کرتے ہین", showing the blessed house the utmost reverence, while Hindus high and low also attend it and hold the place extremely pure (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 101, folio 104).

The other books speak of the Guru, not of the gurdwara. Haroon Khalid writes in *A White Trail* that "Guru Ram Das, the fourth Sikh Guru, was also born in present day Pakistan, in the city of Lahore" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated), and Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti's Urdu *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* gives "لاہور" as his birthplace in a table (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 515); neither names the shrine. Khalid's *Walking with Nanak* gives the Guru a chapter of his own, and tells his life without mentioning the Lahore site. What these books say of the Guru is gathered below as his life, not as the history of this building.

## The Life of Guru Ram Das

Ram Das was born in the Chuna Mandi bazaar of Lahore in 1534, in a family of modest means, and was known in his boyhood as Bhai Jetha. Orphaned young, he came under the influence of Guru Amar Das, the third Guru, whose daughter he married and whom he served with such devotion and humility that he was in time chosen to succeed him. As the fourth Guru he is remembered above all for founding the settlement that grew into Amritsar — first excavating the sacred pool, the *Amrit Sarovar*, from which the city takes its name — and for composing the *Lavan*, the four hymns that form the heart of the Sikh marriage rite and are sung at every *Anand Karaj* to this day. His verses occupy a cherished place in the *Guru Granth Sahib*, the Sikh scripture.

That the founder of Amritsar should himself have been born in Lahore gives this shrine its particular resonance, joining the two cities most closely associated with the early Gurus.

Khalid's list of the Gurus gives him as "4. Guru Ram Das (1534-1581, Guru from 1574 to 1581)", and adds: "Born in 1534 Guru Ram Das became the first Sodhi Guru"; the Gurudom thereafter "remained within the Sodhi family till Guru Gobind singh" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 315, folio 289). Harish Dhillon's retelling of the Janamsakhis gives the same span of office, "Ram Das (Guru from 1574 to 1581)", naming him among the later Gurus whose stewardship Baba Budha witnessed (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 240, folio 265).

Chishti's table records the birth in the month of Kartik, "[illegible] کاتک سمت [illegible] بوقت چار گہڑی[OCR?] دن", at four *gharis* of the day; the day and the year are illegible in the printed text. The same table gives the birthplace as Lahore, the father as "ہرداس[OCR?]" and the mother as "[illegible] کنور[OCR?]", her first name lost (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 515). In his narrative Chishti names "ہر داس" and describes the family as "کھتری قوم سود ہی ساکن لاہور", Khatris of the Sodhi line and residents of Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 498). Khalid, citing P. S. Grewal's "Nanak's Doctrine and the Feudalization of the Sikh Gurudom", calls Jetha "a Sodhi Khatri, an ancient caste of kings", and notes that "Amar Das himself belonged to the Khatri Bhalla caste" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 272, folio 246).

Kanhaiya Lal places the family's home on this spot: "خاص جائے سکونت گورامداس کے ماباپ کی اسجگہ پر تہی", the particular residence of Ram Das's mother and father was at this place. Then, "سبب انقلاب زمانہ", by a turn of the times, they left Lahore for "گوبندوال", Goindwal, and "وہان ہی شادی گورامداس کی امرداس کی لڑکی کے ساتہہ ہو گئی", there his marriage with Amar Das's daughter took place (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 101, folio 104). He gives no date for the birth, the departure or the marriage (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 101, folio 104).

Khalid tells the boyhood differently. "Jetha's parents had died at a young age" and his grandmother raised him; as a child "he sold boiled grain and wheat in the village of Basarke near Amritsar", where Amar Das first saw him (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 272, folio 246). He followed Amar Das to "Khadur", where he looked after "the langar at the darbar of the Guru", and later followed him to "Goindwal" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 272–273, folio 246 / folio not stated). His own family complained of his "servile attitude" towards his in-laws (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 273, folio not stated). Chishti, too, has him serve "بارہ برس", twelve years, in the *langar*, and speaks of the "نعمت", the favour, that he received (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 498).

## Marriage and Succession

In Khalid's telling, Guru Amar Das's wife recommended "the young man Jetha" as a husband for their younger daughter, "Bibi Bhani", and the Guru "was relieved" to learn that he was a Sodhi Khatri (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 272, folio 246). Chishti names the wife "بی بی بہانی", and records her request that the honour of the Guruship, "یہ شرف گورو ای[OCR?]", should stay in their offspring; he gives the couple three sons (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 498). His account of the marriage itself is garbled in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 498).

Khalid writes that Amar Das "put his two sons-in-law and his two sons through a few tests", and that "All Sikhs accepted the choice … except his eldest son Mohar [OCR?]" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 274, folio 248). In *A White Trail* he puts it plainly: "Guru Amar Dass selected his son-in-law, Guru Ram Das, as his successor" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 220, folio not stated). Sujan Rai Bhandari, writing in Persian in the reign of Aurangzeb, records that at his death Amar Das seated "رامداس عرف سودهی داماد خود", Ramdas known as Sodhi, his own son-in-law, in his place, although he had children of his own, and that "او هفت سال سجده گاه مریدان بود", for seven years he was the object of his disciples' prostration (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).

Chishti names him in two forms. One passage reads "چوتہی گورو رام داس خلف گورو امرداس[OCR?]" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 680). The other names "گورو امر داس کا داماد", the son-in-law of Guru Amar Das, adding "اوسکی اولاد سوڈہی[OCR?] کہلاتی ہی", his descendants are called Sodhi; the place and the year given with it, "[illegible] پور" and "سولہ[OCR?] سو [illegible] عیسوی", are only partly legible (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 686).

"Guru Ram Das became the first Sodhi [Guru]" [OCR?], Khalid writes, and by the time of Gobind Singh it "was understood" that the Guruship would pass only within the Sodhi caste (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 274, folio 248). "After Ram Das, the succession of Guruhood became a family affair in which the son of the Guru was preferred over others" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 273, folio not stated).

## Ramdaspur and Amritsar

The site's account remembers the Guru as the founder of the settlement that grew into Amritsar. Khalid gives the story of the land. "When Amar Das appointed Ram Das as his successor over his sons, he too asked Ram Das to move to Ramdaspur" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 291, folio 265). In one passage, "Ram Das moved to K[…] [OCR?]", the name of the place cut off in the printed text, a "small town that he had founded on the land that was gifted [to] his wife by the Mughal Emperor Akbar when he met Guru Amar Das"; there "the Guru started the construction of the Amrit Sar", and to fund it "the Guru introduced the system o[f] Masand" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 274, folio 248). Elsewhere Akbar "allotted a huge tract of land to the daughter of Amar Das, Bibi Bhani, the wife of Guru Ram Das instead" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 292, folio 266).

In another place "Guru Ram Das had a jagir where he laid the foundation of the ... [Ramdaspur]", which "eventually became Amritsar", and he was "responsible for the establishment of the Masand system" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 254, folio not stated); it was he who started the tank at Ramdaspur (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 257, folio not stated). Khalid's list of the Gurus calls him "known for starting the construction of the Harminder Sahib". He appointed "his Masand or deputies in different regions" to spread the message and "collect offerings", and "the revenue of the seat of the Guru began to increase under him" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 315, folio 289).

Chishti, too, ties the Guru to the city. He places "چوتہی بادشاہی گورو رام داس کی امرتسر میں", the fourth *Padshahi*, that of Guru Ram Das, at Amritsar (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 280); and of Amritsar he writes, in a phrase partly uncertain in the printed text, "گورو رام داس کی لگی[OCR?] مشہور" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 500); and he names "گورو رام داس[OCR?]" beside his son Arjan in his Amritsar passage (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 686).

Khalid reads the grant of land as a turning point. "with this event also begins the process of feudalisatio[n] [of] Guruhood. All subsequent Gurus after Ram Das were huge land[owners]" [OCR?] (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 274, folio 248).

## The Choice of Guru Arjan

The Guru's own succession divided his sons. Khalid writes that he appointed his younger son, Arjan, rather than Prithi Chand (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 43, folio 25). In *A White Trail*, too, Ram Das appointed his son, Guru Arjan (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 220, folio not stated). Chishti calls Arjan the Guru's "فرزند خورد", his younger son, made "ولی عہد سجادہ نشین", heir and successor to the seat, to the anger of Prithi Chand (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 499). Chishti's account then turns to Arjan's letters, which were intercepted (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 503–504), and to "موضع گوبندوال جو متصل ضلع امرتسر ہے", the village of Goindwal near the district of Amritsar (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 505).

Lahore has its part in the story. In Khalid's account the Guru sent Arjan to Lahore for a wedding and, on discovering Prithi Chand's deception, called him back and appointed him Guru (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 253–254, folio 227 / folio not stated). Sikh tradition, Khalid writes, traces the quarrel between the brothers to "Arja[n]'s trip to Lahore" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 274, folio 248). The conflict arose "between the Guru's two sons, Prithi Ch[and] and Arjan", over an "immense property", "essenti[ally] the entire city of Ramdaspur and the area around it" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 274, folio 248), and bypassing Prithi Chand began "a conflict that was to last for several generations after him" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 315, folio 289). Near Lahore, Khalid notes, "The Bedian road is the old route to Amritsar", and Prithi Chand's rival shrine would lie "on the route from Lahore to Amritsar" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 38, folio 20; p. 44, folio 26).

Chishti's table places the Guru's death at Goindwal in the district of Amritsar, "سمت [illegible] … گوئندوال[OCR?] ضلع امرتسر", the year illegible (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 515). Dhillon and Khalid end his Guruship in 1581 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 240, folio 265; Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 315, folio 289).

## The Shrine and Maharaja Ranjit Singh

The Guru's childhood home is said to have survived on this spot until the era of the Sikh Empire. Tradition relates that Maharaja Ranjit Singh, the ruler of the Sikh state at Lahore, was asked to raise a fitting shrine at the site — according to the account, at the request of his wife Maharani Datar Kaur, during the celebrations that attended the birth of their son Kharak Singh in 1801. The Maharaja agreed, acquiring the surrounding plots and building the gurdwara that stands, much altered, today. Its situation within the dense fabric of the old city, hard by the Fort and the imperial mosque of the Mughals, speaks to the layered history of Lahore, where the monuments of Mughal, Sikh, and later ages stand shoulder to shoulder.

Kanhaiya Lal tells the founding in his own way. "پہلے اسجگہ چہوٹا سا مکان تہا", there was formerly a small house on the spot. Then, "مہاراجہ رنجیت سنگہ کی اخیر عملداری مین", in the last years of Maharaja Ranjit Singh's rule, the Guru's Sikhs turned their attention to it, and "مہاراجہ کے حکم سے محلہ سوداگران کے مکانات جو اس کے ساتہ ملحق تہے لے گئے": by the Maharaja's order the houses of Mohalla Saudagran adjoining it were taken. The land was enlarged, the foundation laid, and the building "ایکسال کے عرصے مین تیار ہو گیا", was ready within one year (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 101, folio 104). He says nothing of any payment for the houses, and gives no year (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 101, folio 104).

He places the gurdwara "بازار چوک متی[OCR?] کے جنوبی لین مین", in the southern lane of a bazaar whose name is uncertain in the printed text, with "سرِ بازار ایک عالیشان دروازہ پختہ", a lofty brick gateway on the bazaar, and a stair in the *deorhi*, the entrance hall (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 101, folio 104). Within is an upper courtyard, with a well in its north-west corner and a *bel* tree; in the courtyard "ایک علم یعنی چہنڈا کہڑا کیا گیا ہی", a standard, that is a flag, has been raised; and on the east is a stepped jharokha of ornamented brick (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 101, folio 104).

"یہ عالیشان عمارت دربار امرتسر کی قطع پر ہی ہوئی ہے", Kanhaiya Lal writes: the stately building is made on the pattern of the Darbar of Amritsar (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 101–102, folios 104–105). It has four doors on its four sides, and "بیچ مین اگرچہ گنبد نہین ہی", though there is no dome at its centre, it has four two-storeyed *shah-nashins* (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 102, folio 105).

Writing of Lahore's Sikh buildings in general, and not of this one, Khalid reports Iqbal Qaiser's lecture on Sikh architecture in the city: "like the Sikh religion, which picks up from both Hinduism and Islam, Sikh architecture draws inspiration from both Hindu and Muslim architecture"; domes "initially part of the Muslim buildings" were made "smaller" by the Sikhs, and Sikh shrines carry "graphic depictions of Sikh Gurus and also sometimes Hindu deities (in older buildings)" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 309–310, folio not stated / folio 284).

Of the Maharaja's patronage across the province, Khalid writes: "When Maharaja Ranjit Singh became the sovereign of Punjab at the end of the eighteenth century he spent large amounts of money on the renovation and expansion of many gurdwaras", and "attached vast tracts of land to these gurdwaras, so that the revenues from the lands could facilitate their functioning" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 34, folio 16). In *A White Trail* he says that Ranjit Singh renovated "many Gurdwaras all over Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 208, folio not stated). Neither passage names this gurdwara, and Kanhaiya Lal records no endowment, land or income for it (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 102, folio 105).

## Worship in the Nineteenth Century

Kanhaiya Lal describes the gurdwara at worship. An elaborate floor covering stays spread, and "گرنتہہ صاحب کمال آداب سے رکہا رہتا ہی", the Granth Sahib is kept there with the greatest reverence. Sikhs come in numbers and perform "جبین سائی[OCR?]", prostration, and "دو وقت روز مرہ گرنتہہ پڑہا جاتا ہی", the Granth is read twice daily, before large gatherings (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 102, folio 105).

There is singing, and not only by Sikhs: "قوال موحدانہ کافیان گا کر لوگون کو محظوظ کرتے ہین", *qawwals* delight the people by singing monotheist *kafis* (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 102, folio 105). A larger assembly comes round regularly: "آٹہوین روز انوار[OCR?] کے دن تیسرے پہر بڑا مجمع زن و مرد کا ہوتا ہی", on the eighth day, on the day the printed text gives as "انوار", in the third watch, a great gathering of men and women takes place. A selection from the Gurus' Granths is read and "شبد توحید آمیز گائے جاتے ہین", *shabads* imbued with the oneness of God are sung; "جلسہ لایق دید ہوتا ہی", the gathering is worth seeing (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 102, folio 105).

He records one dated occasion, though he does not give its year. "ابہی[OCR?] کے ہولیون کے دنون مین سنگہ سبہا کے ممبرون[OCR?] نے یہان بڑا جلسہ کیا تہا … تمام مندر اور بازار مین روشنی کی اور تمام رات شبد گاتے رہے تہے": recently, in the days of the Holi festival, members of the Singh Sabha held a great assembly here, lit up the whole temple and the bazaar, and sang *shabads* all night (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 102, folio 105). The reading of the opening word and of the members' title is uncertain in the printed text.

He names the custodians of his day: "فی الحال پجہاری اس گردوارے کا جیت سنگہ اور معتمد و معاون حلقہ کا ایک شخص کنہیا لال ہے", the *pujari* of the gurdwara at present is Jeet Singh, and the trusted agent of the circle is a man named Kanhiya Lal. His verdict on the two is lost in the printed text: "حقیقت مین یہ [illegible] شخص ہین" (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 102, folio 105). He records no endowment, land, income or annual festival for the gurdwara (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 102, folio 105).

## Partition and Its Aftermath

The Partition of 1947 fell heavily upon the Sikh shrines of Lahore. Amid the communal violence that swept the city, some eighteen Sikhs are reported to have been killed within the premises of this gurdwara — one of many such tragedies that emptied the western Punjab of its Sikh population almost overnight. In the decades that followed, the shrine, like the other historic gurdwaras of Pakistan, passed into the care of the Evacuee Trust Property Board.

In recent years the gurdwara has been reopened to worship and to pilgrimage, and the birthday of Guru Ram Das draws Sikh visitors to the narrow lanes of Chuna Mandi to honour the Guru at the place of his birth. Its careful upkeep forms part of the wider, if uneven, effort to preserve and restore Pakistan's Sikh heritage.

The books consulted do not describe the gurdwara in 1947 or after. Khalid writes of the city's eve of Partition that "Tara Singh, a prominent Sikh leader at that time, took out his kirpan on the stairs of the Punjab Assembly in Lahore and said that the time to take revenge for the Gurus had come" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 199, folio not stated). Of Lahore today he writes that "it is now rare that one runs into a Sikh in this city, a city which was once ruled by a Sikh and the seat of their political strength" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 196, folio not stated).

Of Sikh and Hindu sites after 1947, Khalid writes that without the care of one family the temple of Ram Thamman "would have also been taken over by the refugees and converted into a household, like the hundreds of temples and Gurdwaras all over Pakistani Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 58, folio not stated). In *In Search of Shiva* he adds: "I have seen several gurdwaras and temples being taken over and destroyed by the migrants" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated). Neither passage names this gurdwara.

## Setting: The Gurus' Lahore

The city where Ram Das was born kept its hold on his line. Khalid writes that "Guru Arjan spent a lot of time in the same city, where he was assassinated and his remains interred", and that Guru Hargobind "also spent a lot of time here" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated). Arjan "was summoned to Lahore by Emperor Jahangir" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 234, folio 208) and died there; Chandu Lal, Khalid adds, had been "appointed as the Diwan of Lahore by the Mughal authorities" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 253, folio 227).

Lahore had drawn the Gurus before him. In Dhillon's retelling of the Janamsakhis, Guru Nanak came to the city when Daulat Khan Lodhi governed it, and Duni Chand, the *amil*, the revenue collector, of Lahore, became his disciple and gave away his seven lakh rupees (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 124, folio 138; p. 134, folio 148). In Khalid's account Nanak and Mardana reached Lahore on their way home (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 163, folio 145). Later in the line, Guru Har Rai "along with his forces, reached Lahore" and met Dara Shikoh there after the prince's defeat by Aurangzeb (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 210–211, folios 184–185); Bhandari has the Guru leave Dara's camp at Lahore in the same way as others had slipped away (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 76).

Bhandari, writing under Aurangzeb, calls Lahore "شهریست بزرگ و مصریست سترگ", a great city and a mighty metropolis, where "درهر کوچه و بازار مساجد بسیار از بسیار است", there are mosques beyond counting in every lane and bazaar (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 524). Chishti counts "شہر لاہور کے بارہ دروازہ ہی[OCR?]", the twelve gates of the city of Lahore, the Delhi Gate, "دہلی دروازہ", among them (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 498). Khalid writes that "The Roshani gate, one of the thirteen gateways that lead into the ancient walled city of Lahore, is between the Alamgiri Gate and the Royal Mosque" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 205, folio not stated).

Ranjit Singh came to the city in 1799, Khalid writes, and "was greeted enthusiastically by a population sick of the lawlessness of that time"; he was crowned ruler of the Punjab in 1801 (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 207, folio not stated). Kanhaiya Lal writes that "اس مہاراجہ نے چالیس سال تک پنجاب مین حکومت کی", the Maharaja ruled the Punjab for forty years (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 217, folio 220). Kharak Singh, the son whose birth the site's account ties to the gurdwara, followed him on the throne: "مہاراجہ رنجیت سنگہ کے مرنے کے بعد اُسکا حقیقی بیٹا مہاراجہ کہڑک سنگہ پنجاب کی حکومت کے تختہ پر بیٹہا", after Ranjit Singh's death his own son, Maharaja Kharak Singh, took the throne of the Punjab (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 221, folio 224). Chishti, too, has Kharak Singh sit on "کرسی حکومت پنجاب" at his father's death (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 586). The Sikh state ended with Dalip Singh, who, Chishti writes, left Lahore in December of a year printed as 1849, the reading uncertain and the day illegible (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 592).

## In Verse

None of the books consulted quotes the Guru's own hymns, the *Lavan* among them. Khalid notes that his poetry is in the *Guru Granth Sahib* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 317, folio 291), and Kanhaiya Lal records the verse that was sung at this gurdwara in his day, the *shabads* of the Gurus and the monotheist *kafis* of the *qawwals*, without quoting any of it (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 102, folio 105).

The verse the books do carry speaks of the Punjab in the century before the Maharaja built here. It is given as context for the province, not as verse about the Guru or the site. Bulleh Shah, who died in 1758 (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 28, folio 8), lived through what Taufiq Rafat calls a time when "the Punjab was being persistently rent by petty chiefs and marauders"; "The full bitterness of Bulleh Shah's 'Punjab has gone to the dogs' will only come through against this backdrop" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 16, folio xvi). The lines, in Rafat's English, read:

*Now hell has opened its bogs. / Punjab has gone to the dogs. / This hell is the nethermost hell.* (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 149, folio 129)

Christopher Shackle translates another lyric: *The door of doom and torment has opened, and Panjab is in a bad state.* (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 52, folio 27). Shackle reads a verse in a third lyric as "Taken to be an allusion to the Sikhs, whose guerilla horsemen ultimately destroyed the Mughal authority in Panjab" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 193, folio 168).

Chishti's account of the end of the Sikh state carries a *qita* on Dalip Singh's departure from Lahore, which includes these lines:

چون شمع[OCR?] پنجاب از پنجاب رفت
نو گلی از گلشن پنجاب رفت

The lines are printed so in Chishti's book, the reading of one word uncertain; they are left untranslated here (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 592).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The bazaar.** The site's account places the gurdwara in Chuna Mandi. Kanhaiya Lal places it "بازار چوک متی[OCR?] کے جنوبی لین مین", in the southern lane of a bazaar whose name is uncertain in the printed text (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 101, folio 104).
- **What stood on the spot.** The site's account says the Guru's childhood home survived on this spot until the era of the Sikh Empire. Kanhaiya Lal writes that his parents' residence was at this place, and that before the gurdwara was built "پہلے اسجگہ چہوٹا سا مکان تہا", there was a small house here; he does not say that it was the same house (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 101, folio 104).
- **When the gurdwara was built, and at whose wish.** The site's account has Ranjit Singh build it at the request of Maharani Datar Kaur, during the celebrations for Kharak Singh's birth in 1801. Kanhaiya Lal sets the building "مہاراجہ رنجیت سنگہ کی اخیر عملداری مین", in the last years of the Maharaja's rule, when the Guru's Sikhs turned their attention to the spot, the work done by the Maharaja's order and finished within a year; he gives no year and does not name Datar Kaur (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 101, folio 104).
- **How the land was obtained.** The site's account says the Maharaja acquired the surrounding plots. Kanhaiya Lal says that by his order the houses of Mohalla Saudagran adjoining the spot "لے گئے", were taken, and mentions no payment (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 101, folio 104).
- **Where the Guru grew up.** The site's account and Kanhaiya Lal have him born and reared at this place (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 101, folio 104). Khalid has him raised by his grandmother after his parents' early death, and selling boiled grain as a child in the village of Basarke near Amritsar (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 272, folio 246).
- **His parents, and how he came to Amar Das.** The site's account calls him orphaned young, and Khalid writes that his parents died at a young age (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 272, folio 246). Kanhaiya Lal has his parents leave Lahore for Goindwal, where he was married to Amar Das's daughter (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 101, folio 104). Khalid has Amar Das first see him at Basarke, and Jetha follow the Guru to Khadur and then to Goindwal (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 272–273, folio 246 / folio not stated).
- **His relation to Amar Das, in Chishti.** One of Chishti's lists styles him "خلف گورو امرداس[OCR?]" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 680); another calls him "گورو امر داس کا داماد", Amar Das's son-in-law (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 686). Bhandari and Khalid call him the son-in-law (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520; Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 220, folio not stated).
- **How many sons.** Chishti gives the Guru and Bibi Bhani three sons (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 498). Khalid speaks of the dispute "between the Guru's two sons, Prithi Ch[and] and Arjan" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 274, folio 248).
- **Whose land Ramdaspur stood on.** The site's account has the Guru found the settlement. Khalid writes in one place that the town stood on land "gifted [to] his wife by the Mughal Emperor Akbar" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 274, folio 248), and in another that Akbar allotted the land to Bibi Bhani (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 292, folio 266); in a third, "Guru Ram Das had a jagir where he laid the foundation" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 254, folio not stated). Khalid also has Amar Das ask Ram Das to move to Ramdaspur on appointing him (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 291, folio 265), and elsewhere has Ram Das move to a small town that he had founded himself (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 274, folio 248).
- **The gates of the walled city.** Writing of the city, not of this site, Chishti counts twelve gates (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 498), and *In Search of Shiva* calls Bhatti Gate "one of the twelve entry points" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated); *A White Trail* calls the Roshani Gate "one of the thirteen gateways" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 205, folio not stated).

## Legacy

For the Sikh community, Gurdwara Janam Asthan Guru Ram Das is doubly precious: it marks the earthly beginning of the Guru who gave Sikhism its holiest city and its wedding liturgy, and it stands as a reminder that Lahore — for so long a great centre of Sikh power and devotion — was also his cradle. Amid the crowded bazaars of the Walled City, the shrine keeps alive the memory of a boy born here in 1534 whose life would reshape the faith and give the world Amritsar.

The Guru's image appears elsewhere too. Khalid finds his name among the figures on the frescoes of the dome at Jhaman (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 51, folio 33), and Chishti records him among the Gurus pictured in the niche of a *samadh* he describes (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 520). Khalid's *A White Trail* counts him among the "eclectic religious preachers" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 199, folio not stated), and *Walking with Nanak* gives the fourth Guru a chapter under his own name, "Guru Ram Das" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 11, folio not stated).
