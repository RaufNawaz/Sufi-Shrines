## Overview

Tucked away on Edward Road near the old Customs House in Anarkali, the octagonal tomb of Hazrat Meeran Muhammad Shah Bukhari — popularly called "Mauj Darya," or "Wave of the River" — commemorates a Suhrawardi saint of the Sadaat of Uch Sharif who became, in Lahore, a spiritual confidant of the Mughal emperor Akbar. He died in 1604, the date traditionally taken as the founding of the shrine, and he lies in a small graveyard in the company of his family, his spiritual successors and gaddi nasheen. The shrine remains one of scores of Lahore shrines administered by the Punjab Auqaf and Religious Affairs Department (Mehkama Auqaf), and continues to draw a devoted following, with its gatherings of Thursday night and an annual *ʿurs* in the month of Rabi-ul-Awal.

Three older books of Lahore give the saint and his tomb a full record. Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori's *Hadiqat al-Awliya* (1875) gives him an entry of his own, headed with his name as Miran Muhammad Shah, known as Sayyid Mauj Darya Bukhari Lahori, and his wife a second entry among the holy women of the Punjab (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 181–182, folios 179–180; p. 254, folio 242). Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti's *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, a survey of the tombs and buildings of Lahore's environs, opens a section on the tomb of Hazrat Miran Sayyid Muhammad Shah Mauj Darya Bukhari and returns to his family, his successors and his descendants across many later pages (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 101). Kanhaiya Lal's *Tarikh-e Lahore* (1884) treats him twice: once as the founder of a whole quarter of the city, and once, at length, under the heading of his tomb, which it counts among the famous and well-known buildings of Lahore (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 79, folio 82; p. 281, folio 284).

The three books do not always agree — on the year and day of his death, on the size and the place of Akbar's grant, on the names of his wives and the mothers of his sons, and on where the tomb stands in relation to the landmarks of their day. Their accounts are set out below as each gives them, and every difference is gathered in the section on where the sources disagree.

## The Life of the Saint

The saint was born in 1533 in Uch Sharif, the son of Safi-ud-Din Bukhari of the Sadaat of that town, a descendant of Jalaluddin Surkh-Posh Bukhari whose genealogy is traced through Syed Jalal-ud-Din Jahaniyan Jahangasht, and an adherent of the Suhrawardi silsila founded by Bahauddin Zakariya of Multan. He became a hafiz-e-Quran in his youth.

Mufti Ghulam Sarwar gives the chain of his descent in full: Miran Muhammad Shah, son of Sayyid Safi al-Din, son of Sayyid Nizam al-Din, son of Sayyid Ilm al-Din the Second, son of Jalal al-Din, son of Sayyid Ilm al-Din the First, son of Sayyid Nasir al-Din, son of Sayyid Jalal al-Din Makhdoom Jahaniyan, son of Sayyid Ahmad Kabir al-Din, son of Sayyid Jalal al-Din Mir Surkh Bukhari; and he adds that sanctity, nobility and the gift of miracles were his by inheritance (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 181). Nur Ahmad Chishti calls him Hazrat Muhammad Shah, son of Sayyid Safi al-Din the Elder, known as Mauj Darya Sayyid Bukhari, of the progeny of Hazrat Mir Sayyid Jalal al-Din, known as Pir Surkh, the last word uncertain in the printed text, and says he was famous at Uch for his renunciation, scrupulousness, piety and miracles, and was there the holder of the seat of the exalted khanqah of his forefathers, the word exalted uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 101). Elsewhere he traces him instead to the progeny of Sayyid Sultan Jalal al-Din Haidar Surkh, the last word uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 106). Kanhaiya Lal counts him among the Sayyids of Uch — an elder of the Sayyids of Uch famous in Akbar's reign as a God-fearing man of sanctity and miracles — and names his forebear Sayyid Jalal-ud-Din Mir Surkh Bukhari, who lived at Uch, where his tomb stands (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 279, folio 282).

His name is given in several forms. The *Hadiqat al-Awliya* names him in full, in the entry on one of his disciples, as Hazrat Miran Muhammad Shah Mauj Darya Suhrawardi Lahori (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 269, folio 257). Kanhaiya Lal's first account calls him Sayyid Miran Shah Bukhari, whom they also call Mauj Darya (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 79, folio 82). In Chishti's lithographed text the epithet is printed in several damaged forms, among them, as far as they can be read, Mauj Darya Sanjar (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 157) and Mauj Darya Sanjhar (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 523).

According to popular tradition, he earned his epithet by miraculously diverting the flood-prone River Ravi away from Lahore. A different derivation of the same title is also reported: he was called Mouj Darya for his open-handed generosity and for the state of spiritual ecstasy (*mouj*) in which his prayers were held to be answered at once. Both derivations stand here as they were given. None of the older books consulted explains the name.

Above all he is remembered as a spiritual confidant of the Mughal emperor Akbar. The shrine's tradition relates that the emperor was foretold — through the saint's father — of a windstorm and a lit lamp that would mark the saint's presence at the fort of Chittor; when both signs appeared and the Rajput army surrendered, Akbar became his disciple. Alam Faqri's *Tazkirah Awliya-e-Pakistan* preserves an independent, extended telling of this same episode — giving the saint's given name as Miran Muhammad Shah, tracing his descent from Makhdoom Jahaniyan Jahangasht and his origins in Uch Sharif, and recounting at length how his prayer and intercession were credited with securing the fort's surrender to Akbar — corroborating, from a separate hagiographical compendium, the tradition already recorded at the shrine.

The *Hadiqat al-Awliya* tells it plainly: he first lived at Uch; from there Akbar summoned him to Chittorgarh and asked his prayer for the conquest of the fort (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 181). Chishti likewise has him come to Lahore after Akbar's Chittorgarh campaign, at the emperor's request (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 101–102), and says in another place that with his help the fort was taken; the fort's printed name, perhaps Huzur Garh, cannot be read with certainty (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 362). Kanhaiya Lal writes that Akbar was his great devotee (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 279, folio 282). None of the books gives a year for the summons.

Legend credits him with distributing to the poor a substantial reward Akbar granted him near Batala, and it is said that on the saint's arrival in Lahore the emperor gifted the land on which he settled. The books give the grant in more detail, and in different sums.

In the *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, after the fort fell Akbar gave him a *jagir* of one lakh rupees in the Punjab, with the city of Tibala inside it (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 181). He took it unwillingly: he accepted the *jagir* with reluctance and set a *langar* running (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 181).

Kanhaiya Lal gives two figures in two places. In his account of the quarter the saint founded, the emperor, in perfect devotion, made over to him as *waqf* a *jagir* of two lakh rupees for the expenses of the dervishes of the khanqah, with the qasba of Batala within it, the word for its release uncertain in the printed text (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 79, folio 82). In his account of the tomb, a *jagir* of one lakh twenty-five thousand in the region of Batala stood released in the saint's own name, the name Batala uncertain in the printed text (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 279, folio 282). Chishti's figure is lost in the printed text, which speaks of a tract worth some lakhs of rupees with the number illegible, and he says its income was spent on faqirs (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 102).

His darasgah — the seat of learning where he now lies buried — is said still to hold land documents bearing Akbar's own signature. The older books report such documents too, each at a remove. The modern editor of the *Hadiqat al-Awliya* adds in a footnote that Sayyid Muhammad Latif wrote that the *farman* by which the *jagir* was granted is preserved with the family to this day, with Akbar's seal impressed on it (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 181). The "today" of that sentence is Latif's, in the nineteenth century; the editor relays the report and does not say he saw the document (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 181–182, folios 179–180 as printed). Chishti says that Akbar's documents were with the saint's descendants, and that if the government wished to see them, they could show them, though several words of the sentence are uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 102); and later that an imperial *farman* of Akbar in the saint's name, its wording uncertain in the printed text, was shown to the writer himself, held at a place whose printed name, perhaps Watala, cannot be read with certainty (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 362).

Kanhaiya Lal makes him the first settler of the place where his tomb stands: at this place, first of all, Sayyid Miran Shah Bukhari, whom they also call Mauj Darya, came and settled in Akbar's reign — and calls him an elder possessed of miracles, devout and ascetic (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 79, folio 82). The *Hadiqat al-Awliya* says that he lived mostly in Lahore and there kept the work of a shaikh alive (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 182). Chishti adds that, with a second household at a place whose printed name, perhaps Danala, cannot be read with certainty, he lived sometimes there and sometimes in Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 104–105).

He is remembered, too, as a missionary presence who drew many Hindus and Sikhs to Islam.

The books name disciples of his. The *Hadiqat al-Awliya* says of Shah Abdul Razzaq Makki that he was a *murid* of Shah Mauj Darya Bukhari; he came to Lahore, took service with Akbar, renounced the world, and from Hazrat Miran Muhammad Shah Mauj Darya Suhrawardi Lahori he received such grace that he became the *qutb* of the age. The book gives the disciple's death as the year 1084, without naming the era (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 269, folio 257). Kanhaiya Lal, writing of Shaikh Abdul Razzaq Makki of the Nila Gumbad, says he reached such perfection that Mauj Darya himself honoured him (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 286, folio 289). Chishti says that Sayyid Abdul Razzaq's first allegiance was to him, one word of the phrase being uncertain in the printed text, that he worshipped nightly at the saint's tomb, and that his own tomb was ordered in a dream (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 157). Kanhaiya Lal also names a Sayyid Abd al-Razzaq of Dola Wari as one of the *khalifas* of Sayyid Mauj Darya Bukhari (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 80, folio 83); the book does not say whether he is the same man.

He died in 1604. The *Hadiqat al-Awliya* gives his death as the year 1014 AH, and closes with a chronogram on it (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 182). Kanhaiya Lal, from the book *Khazinat al-Asfiya*, gives 11 Rabi-ul-Awwal 1013 AH, the year uncertain in the printed text (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 279, folio 282; p. 280, folio 283); his first account of the saint gives no year at all (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 79, folio 82). Chishti gives 17 Rabi-ul-Awwal 1013 AH, the day and the year uncertain and one word illegible in the printed text, and adds that this was a year before the death of Akbar (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 105).

Chishti alone says where he died: at a place whose printed name begins Khan and is otherwise illegible, some *kos* from a place whose printed name, perhaps Danala, cannot be read with certainty; people regard the mosque where his body was washed as blessed too; and his son Sayyid Safi-ud-Din brought the body to Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 105).

## Wives and Sons

He married twice. His first wife, Syedna Bibi Fatima Sani — remembered as "Bibi Killah," and popularly as Mai Fatima — bore him two sons and rests in a separate shrine facing his own, near the historic Jain Mandir; a second wife bore a third son, of whom tradition tells that he could take the form of a lion.

The *Hadiqat al-Awliya* names the mother of his two elder sons as Sayyida Pak Amina, known as Bibi Kalan, daughter of Sayyid Abd al-Qadir III (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 182). In its chapter on the holy women of the Punjab the same book gives a separate entry to Bibi Fatima Sayyida, known as Bibi Bari: this lady, it says, was the wife of Hazrat Miran Muhammad Shah Mauj Darya Bukhari and the daughter of Sayyid Abd al-Qadir III, son of Sayyid Abd al-Wahhab, son of Sayyid Muhammad Bala Pir Gilani (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 254, folio 242). The book gives the two women different given names and the same father, with epithets that both mean "elder", and nowhere says whether one woman or two is meant (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 182; p. 254, folio 242). Its modern editor lists Bibi Fatima, wife of Shaikh Mauj Darya Bukhari Lahori, among the figures whose accounts are found in this book alone, no earlier writer having recorded them (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 32, folio 20), and in his table of shrines calls her Bibi Fatima Sayyida, known as Bibi Waddi (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 291, folio 279). Her death is given as 1016 AH: the lady died in the year 1016, and her tomb is outside the tomb of Miran Muhammad Shah (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 254, folio 242).

Kanhaiya Lal does not name her. He says that Bari Bibi, the Elder Lady, was the title of the wife of the founder of the quarter, and that the quarter was also called the quarter of Bibi Waddi after her, the name uncertain in the printed text (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 79, folio 82). Chishti calls her the senior wife, Bibi Sahiba Kalan, and places the tomb of Bibi Kalan, wife of Hazrat Mauj Darya Bukhari, whom they call Bibi Waddi, near the *kothi* of a Mr McLeod and that of a merchant, Nabi Bakhsh; the names Waddi, McLeod and Nabi Bakhsh are all uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 105). He gives a detailed account of her later in the book (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 349).

Chishti records that the senior wife objected to a second marriage, and that the junior wife lived at a place whose printed name, perhaps Danala, cannot be read with certainty, where there were havelis (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 104–105). The saint's present *sajjada nashin*, he says, named the two wives as Mai Waddi Sahiba and Mai Nur Rang Bibi, the second name uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 396). The *Hadiqat al-Awliya* names the third son's mother only in a damaged phrase, which speaks of another wife, a Bibi whose name is printed in a form that may read Forang (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 182), and her name cannot be recovered with certainty from the printed text (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 182, folio 180 as printed).

The *Hadiqat al-Awliya* names three sons: Sayyid Safi al-Din, named for his grandfather, and Sayyid Baha al-Din, both born of Bibi Kalan, and Shah Shihab al-Din, born of the other wife. Shihab al-Din settled at Tibala, where his descendants are; Safi al-Din's descendants live in Lahore (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 182). The book's modern editor adds a fourth son, Fath Allah Shah, who is absent from the author's text and is taken from an anonymous manuscript by way of Nami (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 182, folio 180 as printed); the index lists him as Fath Shah, son of Mauj Darya Bukhari (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 325). By Chishti's informant, Safi al-Din, the elder son, was born of Mai Waddi, and both Shah Shihab al-Din and Sayyid Baha al-Din of the second wife, the readings of both names uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 396).

Chishti gives an account of the third son, Hazrat Shah Shihab al-Din, son of Hazrat Mauj Darya Bukhari, whose epithet is printed in uncertain forms, perhaps Jafra and Bahra, and says the epithet is explained in the father's section. The boy was put to school at four years, four months and four days, by the custom of the law of Muhammad (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 393). He slapped his teacher and recited the whole Quran; his father was greatly pleased, and offered two *rakʿat* of thanks (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 394).

The household's division outlived the saint. Chishti relates that when a Sayyid Mustafa brought the body of Sayyid Shihab al-Din for burial, the first name and Shihab al-Din's epithet uncertain in the printed text, Safi-ud-Din refused it a place in his father's tomb: only those born of the chaste womb of Bibi Kalan may be buried in this tomb — and he was buried elsewhere, at a place whose name is damaged in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 106). Kanhaiya Lal says that the grave of the son Shahab-ud-Din is near a village printed, uncertainly, as Bhugiwal (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 280, folio 283).

Kanhaiya Lal records a brother, Sayyid Jalal al-Din Bukhari, one word of the phrase that names him uncertain in the printed text, buried in a domed tomb of baked brick built in 1017 AH, in the corner of an enclosure at Bibi Pak Daman (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 306, folio 309). Chishti, describing the tomb of Sultan Jalal al-Din Haidar Bukhari, writes of its third grave in a damaged phrase that connects it in some way with Mauj Darya Bukhari (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 362).

His langar, devotees say, has continued under his descendants to this day. Kanhaiya Lal says that some of his descendants live in Lahore and some at the qasba of Batala (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 79, folio 82), and more fully that his descendants, Sayyid Husain Shah and others, live in Lahore and trace their line to him through Sayyid Safi-ud-Din, and the rest live at the qasba of Batala and trace their line through Shahab-ud-Din (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 281, folio 284). Chishti names his own informants in Lahore as Sayyid Husain Shah and Sayyid Pir Shah, with Asghar Ali of Shihab-ud-Din's line, the names Husain Shah and Asghar Ali uncertain in the printed text, and puts the rest of that line at the second household (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 104). He says he gathered the family's record because many wished to know it, and lists the Sayyids of sound descent in Lahore, among them Sayyid Asghar Ali (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 399).

## The Shrine

The tomb stands on Edward Road near the old Customs House in Anarkali. The *Hadiqat al-Awliya* says only that his lofty tomb in Lahore is a place of pilgrimage for the people, without naming a quarter (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 182). Its modern editor's appendix of shrines gives the address: Edward Road, Lahore, opposite the A.G. Office (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 289, folio 277).

Kanhaiya Lal places it in two ways. In his account of the quarter, the tomb of Sayyid Mauj Darya, founder of the quarter, still stands there, close to Langar Khan's quarter and near Mazang (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 76–79, folios 79–82); and in the middle of that ruined quarter, he says, now stands the house of McLeod, the former Lieutenant Governor, the name McLeod uncertain in the printed text (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 79, folio 82). In his account of the tomb, he says that it adjoins the eastern wall of McLeod Sahib, Lieutenant Governor of the Punjab, at Sadr Bazar Anarkali, one word of the phrase uncertain in the printed text (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 280, folio 283). Chishti gives the same neighbour: the tomb lies next to the *kothi* of Mr McLeod in the Anarkali bazaar, several words of his phrase uncertain or illegible in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 101).

Architecturally the tomb is unusual among Lahore's shrines for its octagonal plan, with a low dome, corner towers at each of its eight angles, and a mixture of recessed blind arches and plain panelled facades — a style thought to echo Multani funerary tradition.

Kanhaiya Lal and Chishti both say it was built in the saint's lifetime, by Akbar. Kanhaiya Lal says that at the very spot where the tomb is, his house stood and a public *langar* ran continually, and the present tomb was built in his lifetime by Akbar's order (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 279, folio 282). Chishti says that Akbar insisted and had this tomb built for him, the word for his insistence uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 103).

Kanhaiya Lal describes it as octagonal, with its door on the south, standing on a platform of baked brick; over the door is a niche carrying, in a bold hand, an inscription in Persian saying that the sacred tomb of the choicest of those who have attained, the exemplar of the gnostics, accepted at the court of the Creator, Miran Sayyid Muhammad Shah Mauj Darya Bukhari, may God illumine his resting place, was built in the reign of Akbar Badshah; and there are verses on each of its eight sides (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 280, folio 283). Chishti copies the same inscription with a different epithet, accepted at the court of God, *Izad* in place of *Bari*, followed by *wali*, a word uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 107). He sets the building on a high mound (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 103), with its entrance on the south, a *dalan* and two small rooms, the sitting place of the trustees; an inscription in brick work like tile-work, the word for the tile-work uncertain in the printed text; four brick lattices; a form whose description begins with the word for eight, uncertain in the printed text, with a *burj* and *burjiyan*, towers large and small; and verses over the doors, one to eight (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 107). About thirty years before he wrote, the number uncertain in the printed text, he says, an engraver of Lahore named Shanku whitewashed the tomb, his name, his trade and his town all uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 103).

The saint lies in a small graveyard in the company of his family, his spiritual successors and gaddi nasheen, and his cook, Allahu Samad. Kanhaiya Lal counts eleven graves inside the tomb: one that of Miran Muhammad Shah himself, and ten of his sons Safi-ud-Din and others and of the rest of his offspring, the number ten uncertain in the printed text (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 280, folio 283). Outside, on a platform, is the grave of a great-grandson, Zind Ali, son of Sayyid Abd al-Rahim, son of Safi-ud-Din, son of Miran Muhammad Shah, the first name uncertain in the printed text, of whom too people say that he was a saint and a man of God (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 280, folio 283). Chishti also counts eleven graves, the number uncertain in the printed text: the saint's, those of his sons Sayyid Safi al-Din Ahmad and Sayyid Baha al-Din, of the grandson Sayyid Abd al-Rahim, son of Sayyid Safi al-Din, of the wives, and of five grandsons; by one wall he names Rahman, father of Shanku; the sons' names and these last two are uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 103). The grave of Sayyid Ibrahim, a grandson of the saint by an uncertain reading of the printed text, lies under a tree (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 107), and there are further graves by that of Sayyid Abd al-Rahman (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 108). The small tomb of the servant Mian Murid was built by order of Akbar, with two graves near the road which goes to the village of Marang, the name uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 106).

Within the enclosure Kanhaiya Lal saw a mosque of baked brick, dwellings for the servants of the khanqah, a well and other buildings, all of baked brick (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 280, folio 283). Chishti names an old mosque, with a year printed in the margin, 1243 [OCR?], and over its arch the name Abdur Rahman, an officer of the army (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 107); the *kalima*; a small mosque called after one Ismail, the name uncertain in the printed text; a well; trees; and *hujras* (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 108). The *Hadiqat al-Awliya* distinguishes the khanqah from the house: in one story the saint is at that moment in the courtyard of the khanqah, while a tree stands in the courtyard of his house (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 254, folio 242).

The shrine gained unwelcome national attention in 2018 when construction of the Orange Line Metro Train's underground Anarkali station demolished its mosque, langar khana and courtyard, with tunnelling reported to pass close to the saint's grave in apparent violation of heritage buffer-zone regulations — an episode that became a flashpoint in Lahore's debates over balancing infrastructure development against protection of religious heritage. Yet devotees hold that for all the government construction pressing around it, nothing has ever tarnished the shrine's beauty.

## Endowment, Langar and Custodians

It is reported that its upkeep ran down a long line before the Auqaf Department took it on — from the emperor Akbar through the rulers who followed him and Maharaja Ranjit Singh, and through the saint's own descendants.

The *langar* begins, in the *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, with the *jagir* itself, and ran in two towns: the saint's *langar* ran in two places, Lahore and Tibala, and hundreds of the poor and destitute ate there (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 181). Chishti counts three: by the tomb in Lahore; at a place whose printed name begins Khan and is otherwise illegible, apparently beside a stream; and at a place whose printed name, perhaps Sabanwala, cannot be read with certainty, in the region of Batala — and he says the descendants of the *langar*'s servants still lived in his day (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 102–103). Kanhaiya Lal's public *langar* ran at the house on the site of the tomb (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 279, folio 282).

Under the Sikhs the khanqah drew a small allowance, which had stopped by Kanhaiya Lal's day: in Maharaja Ranjit Singh's time forty rupees a month were received for the expenses of this khanqah; now nothing at all is received (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 280, folio 283).

Chishti records the succession to the seat: Sayyid Safi al-Din became *sajjada nashin*, then Hazrat Shams Shah, Qamar Shah, Sayyid Hayat Shah and a further Shah whose name is lost, the names Safi al-Din and Qamar Shah uncertain in the printed text; in his own lifetime the saint handed over to Safi al-Din the revenue-free grants of Lahore, the passage partly illegible (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 397). In one damaged passage he writes that the descendants are not the owners of the income of the place and that there is no *sajjada nashin* of the saint's line (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 107); yet he took part of his account from a Shah whose first name is illegible in the printed text, the present *sajjada nashin* of Mauj Darya Bukhari (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 396).

## Devotion and Practice

The shrine continues to draw a devoted following. Daily devotion includes the recitation of the Fatiha, the lighting of diyas, the making of vows (mannat) and the presentation of niyaz, and the gatherings of Thursday night (Jummay Raat) remain the busiest of the week.

The annual urs is held on the 17th to the 19th of Rabi-ul-Awal, with a Mela Chiraagha (festival of lights), the ceremonial washing (gusal) of the tomb and the changing of its chadar, and langar throughout; some 2,200 to 2,500 visitors are recorded on an ordinary day, rising to between 225,000 and 300,000 across the urs. Kanhaiya Lal records the *ʿurs* in the nineteenth century: at this blessed place an *ʿurs* is held every year and a great multitude gathers (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 280, folio 283). His first account of the saint records no *ʿurs*, fair or custodian (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 79, folio 82). Chishti says that Sayyid Abdul Razzaq worshipped nightly at the tomb (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 157).

## Legends and Miracles

The cook, Allahu Samad, is himself the subject of one of the shrine's most striking tales, in which the saint, to answer sceptics, had his cook sit unharmed in a burning cauldron. Chishti tells of Mian Murid, the servant who leapt into the *tandoor*, whose small tomb Akbar ordered built (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 106); the book does not say whether he is the cook of the shrine's tale.

Other wonders are recounted of him: a cauldron of rice he blessed during a shortage that fed the people for nearly seven days; brackish irrigation water over which he prayed for local landowners, which turned sweet; and a tree planted in his lifetime — "the blessed tree" — said never to have needed cutting. Chishti attaches the sweet water to his grandson Sayyid Abdur Rahim, a great saint and a worker of miracles, two of the words uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 106).

The *Hadiqat al-Awliya* tells a story of a tree in which the saint is not the wonder-worker but the questioner. In his wife's entry, the story turns on a *ber* tree in the courtyard of her house while he is in the courtyard of the khanqah; Miran Muhammad Shah asked where she had got this bounty, and she said that this wealth was her ancestral inheritance (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 254, folio 242). Chishti knows a tree at her house that could be seen from the khanqah of Hazrat Mauj Darya Bukhari (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 351), and in the same place tells of a miracle of a *chadar*, after which, he says, the Bukhari and Gilani Sayyids no longer married one another (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 351).

Kanhaiya Lal states the saint's reputation without recounting an incident: he was famous for sanctity and miracles (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 279, folio 282). It is also reported that books about him written by Muslim, Hindu and Sikh authors alike are widely read.

## In Verse

Mufti Ghulam Sarwar closes his notice with a Persian chronogram on the saint's death, printing the year 1014 AH twice within it:

چوں محمد شاہ ملک عاقبت
در مقام قرب حقانی نشست
از حضور ۱۰۱۴ھ آمد عیان تاریخ او
نیز سرور گفت مرد حق پرست ۱۰۱۴ھ

A chronogram on the death of Muhammad Shah, with the year 1014 AH set against it twice in the printed text (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 182).

Kanhaiya Lal prints in full a Persian quatrain on the saint's death taken from *Khazinat al-Asfiya*, the Persian book that Mufti Ghulam Sarwar names as his own (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 280, folio 268), with the year 1013 beneath it, the reading uncertain in the printed text. It names him Hazrat Miran Muhammad Shah and calls him a Suhrawardi *pir* (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 280, folio 283), in the line:

سہروردی پیر شیخ با صفا

Chishti knows the same verse: he notes that Mufti Ghulam Sarwar entered the saint's account in his own book, and quotes a *qitʿa* that calls him a Suhrawardi *pir* in the same words, its line with the year illegible in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 105). He also prints a chronogram for Hazrat Sayyid Ali Imam Pir and Hazrat Mauj Darya Bukhari, the word Pir uncertain in the printed text, most of which cannot be read (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 399).

The tomb itself carries verse. Kanhaiya Lal says there are verses on each of its eight sides (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 280, folio 283). Chishti copies verses from the doors, one to eight. The seventh is too damaged in the printed text to set out as verse; what can be read names a Sayyid Rahmat Shah Bukhari. The eighth is a chronogram, which ends:

بگفتا [OCR?] روضہ والا مقدس

In the margin stands the year 1253, the reading uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 107).

## Setting

The saint came to Lahore in Akbar's reign. Chishti's survey of the city records that Akbar turned to the settlement of Lahore from the start of his reign, built a city and a palace there, raised large buildings and stayed for a time (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 431). The quarter Kanhaiya Lal credits to the saint stayed settled, he says, to the end of the Chughtai sultanate, and was ruined in the time of the Sikh plunder and the attacks of Ahmad Shah (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 79, folio 82); part of its land, he adds, went into the roads (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 79, folio 82). His book carries the quarter by name, as Mohalla Mauj Darya, in its table of contents (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 2).

Of Anarkali itself, Haroon Khalid writes that the market "is named after the legendary lover of the Mughal Emperor Jahangir", and that the area "over the years has become the largest tyre market in the city" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 66, folio not stated).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The meaning of the name.** Popular tradition derives "Mauj Darya" from his diverting the River Ravi away from Lahore; the 2026 field survey from his generosity and the state of ecstasy (*mouj*) in which his prayers were answered. The older books consulted give no derivation.
- **The year and day of his death.** The site's account gives 1604. The *Hadiqat al-Awliya* gives 1014 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 182). Kanhaiya Lal gives the eleventh of Rabi-ul-Awwal, 1013 AH, the year uncertain in the printed text (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 279, folio 282), and in his first account no year at all (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 79, folio 82). Chishti gives the seventeenth of Rabi-ul-Awwal, 1013 AH, both numbers uncertain in the printed text, a year before Akbar's death (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 105). The site holds its *ʿurs* on the 17th to the 19th of Rabi-ul-Awal.
- **When the tomb was built.** The site's account takes his death in 1604 as the traditional founding date of the shrine. Kanhaiya Lal says the present tomb was built in his lifetime by Akbar's order (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 279, folio 282), and Chishti that Akbar insisted on building it for him (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 103); the inscription both copy says only that it was built in Akbar's reign (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 280, folio 283; Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 107).
- **The wording of the inscription.** Kanhaiya Lal reads the epithet as accepted at the court of the Creator, *Bari* (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 280, folio 283); Chishti as accepted at the court of God, *Izad*, followed by *wali*, a word uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 107).
- **The size, form and place of Akbar's grant.** The site's account speaks of a substantial reward near Batala, which the saint gave to the poor. The *Hadiqat al-Awliya* gives a *jagir* of one lakh rupees in the Punjab containing the city of Tibala (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 181). Kanhaiya Lal gives in one place a *jagir* of two lakh rupees made over as *waqf* for the dervishes of the khanqah, containing the qasba of Batala (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 79, folio 82), and in another a *jagir* of one lakh twenty-five thousand in the region of Batala, released in the saint's own name (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 279, folio 282). Chishti's figure is illegible (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 102).
- **Akbar's documents.** The site's account places land documents bearing Akbar's signature at the darasgah where he is buried. The *Hadiqat al-Awliya*'s editor reports, from Sayyid Muhammad Latif, a *farman* under Akbar's seal preserved with the family (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 181). Chishti says the documents were with the descendants (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 102), and that the *farman* he was shown was held at a place whose printed name, perhaps Watala, cannot be read with certainty (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 362).
- **The towns of the family.** The *Hadiqat al-Awliya* settles the third son and his line at Tibala (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 182); Kanhaiya Lal puts that line at the qasba of Batala (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 281, folio 284); Chishti puts the second household, and the rest of the line, at a place whose printed name, perhaps Danala, cannot be read with certainty (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 104). None of the books says whether these are one place.
- **How many *langars*.** The *Hadiqat al-Awliya* gives two, at Lahore and Tibala (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 181); Chishti three, at Lahore and two other places (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 102–103); Kanhaiya Lal names one, at the house on the site of the tomb (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 279, folio 282). The site's account says his *langar* continues under his descendants to this day.
- **The first wife's name.** The site's account calls her Syedna Bibi Fatima Sani, remembered as "Bibi Killah" and popularly as Mai Fatima. The *Hadiqat al-Awliya* names the mother of the two elder sons Sayyida Pak Amina, known as Bibi Kalan (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 182), and gives a separate entry to his wife Bibi Fatima Sayyida, known as Bibi Bari (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 254, folio 242), whom its editor calls Bibi Waddi (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 291, folio 279); the book does not say whether these are one woman or two. Kanhaiya Lal gives only her title, Bari Bibi, the Elder Lady (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 79, folio 82). Chishti calls her Bibi Kalan, known as Bibi Waddi (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 105), and his informant Mai Waddi Sahiba (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 396).
- **The mothers of the sons.** The site's account gives the first wife two sons and the second wife a third. The *Hadiqat al-Awliya* makes Safi al-Din and Baha al-Din the sons of Bibi Kalan and Shihab al-Din the son of the other wife (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 182). Chishti's informant makes Safi al-Din the son of Mai Waddi and both Shihab al-Din and Baha al-Din the sons of the second wife (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 396). The *Hadiqat al-Awliya*'s editor adds a fourth son, Fath Allah Shah, from an anonymous manuscript (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 182, folio 180 as printed).
- **Where the first wife lies.** The site's account puts her in a separate shrine facing his own, near the Jain Mandir. The *Hadiqat al-Awliya* puts her tomb outside his (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 254, folio 242). Its editor's table puts that grave, outside the tomb of Miran Muhammad Shah, on Lake Road, Old Anarkali, while his appendix puts the saint's own tomb on Edward Road (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 291, folio 279; p. 289, folio 277); the table names the saint by his personal name only. Chishti puts her tomb near the houses of McLeod and of the merchant Nabi Bakhsh (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 105), while also counting wives among the eleven graves of the saint's tomb (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 103).
- **Where the tomb stands.** The site's account gives Edward Road near the old Customs House in Anarkali; the *Hadiqat al-Awliya*'s editor, Edward Road opposite the A.G. Office (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 289, folio 277). Kanhaiya Lal places it in one account near Langar Khan's quarter and Mazang, with McLeod's house in the middle of the quarter (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 76–79, folios 79–82; p. 79, folio 82), and in the other against the eastern wall of McLeod's property at Sadr Bazar Anarkali (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 280, folio 283). Chishti places it next to McLeod's house in Bazar Anarkali (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 101).
- **The burning cauldron.** The site's account tells of the cook Allahu Samad sitting unharmed in a burning cauldron. Chishti tells of Mian Murid, the servant who leapt into the *tandoor* (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 106).
- **The sweet water.** The site's account credits the saint's prayer with turning brackish water sweet; Chishti attaches the sweet water to his grandson Sayyid Abdur Rahim (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 106).
- **His forebear's name.** The site's account names Jalaluddin Surkh-Posh Bukhari; the *Hadiqat al-Awliya* and Kanhaiya Lal, Sayyid Jalal al-Din Mir Surkh Bukhari (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 181; Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 279, folio 282). Chishti gives him once as Pir Surkh, the reading uncertain (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 101) and once as Sayyid Sultan Jalal al-Din Haidar Surkh, the last word uncertain (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 106).
- **A seat or none.** Chishti writes in one damaged passage that there is no *sajjada nashin* of the saint's line (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 107), and elsewhere names a present *sajjada nashin* and a line of successors (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 396; p. 397). The site's account speaks of gaddi nasheen buried beside the saint, and of the Auqaf Department as the shrine's present administrator.

## Legacy

Kanhaiya Lal's verdict is that this khanqah is among the famous and well-known places of Lahore (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 281, folio 284). His name passed to a quarter of the city, which Kanhaiya Lal calls after him and his wife alike (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 79, folio 82), and to a line of Sayyids in Lahore who still traced their descent to him in the nineteenth century (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 281, folio 284; Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 399). Shah Abdul Razzaq Makki, whom the *Hadiqat al-Awliya* makes the *qutb* of his age by the saint's grace, was his disciple (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 269, folio 257); and his wife, whom the *Hadiqat al-Awliya*'s editor counts among the figures recorded in no earlier book, is remembered as a saint in her own right (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 32, folio 20).

In the twenty-first century the demolition of its mosque, langar khana and courtyard for the Orange Line in 2018 made the shrine a flashpoint in Lahore's debates over development and heritage. Books about the saint by Muslim, Hindu and Sikh authors are widely read, and the crowds of its *ʿurs* run to the hundreds of thousands.
