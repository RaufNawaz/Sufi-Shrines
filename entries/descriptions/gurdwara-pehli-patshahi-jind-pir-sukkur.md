## Overview

Gurdwara Pehli Patshahi, popularly called Jind Pir, stands on the Sukkur side of the great bridge that carries the Lahore–Karachi railway across the Indus between Sukkur and Rohri. By tradition it marks a spot graced by Guru Nanak, the first Guru (*Pehli Patshahi*), during his third journey (*udasi*) through Sindh; it was from here, the tradition holds, that he crossed to the river islet later famous as Sadh Belo (a separate entry in this dataset). The gurdwara is thus a characteristic monument of Sindh's syncretic Nanakpanthi tradition, distinct both from the island temple of Sadh Belo and from the other Sikh sites of the Sukkur–Rohri conurbation. Much of its detailed history survives only in oral and community tradition.

None of the books consulted for this article names a Gurdwara Pehli Patshahi at Sukkur, and none of them ties Guru Nanak to a shrine called Jind Pir. One of them, Michel Boivin's *The Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, describes a darbar "on the bank of the Indus River in Sukkur" known as Jinda Pir, built in the last decade of the nineteenth century for a Hindu cult of Khwaja Khizr; it may be this site, or a shrine related to it, but the book never calls that darbar a gurdwara and never names Guru Nanak in connection with it (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 54, folio 41). That passage is set out below as a pointer, beside the older account of a Jinda Pir shrine on the Sukkur bank that this site's own description already records. The rest of what the books hold is about Guru Nanak himself, about his journey through Sindh, about the cities of Sukkur and Rohri, and about the followers of Nanak and the worshippers of the river in Sindh. It is gathered here as the background of the site, and every passage of it is framed as being about the figure or the place, never about this gurdwara.

## The Gurdwara

Its first custodian is said to have been an Udasi *sadhu* of the Jhulelal devotion, and the shrine passed into the keeping of the Nanakpanthi community of Sindh — Hindus and Sikhs together — who honour Guru Nanak without a firm confessional boundary. Within, the *Guru Granth Sahib* is installed and its *prakash* performed, while images are also venerated, and a lamp said to have been kindled by the Guru himself is kept burning day and night, before which pilgrims bow.

The books consulted add nothing to this account of the building. None of them records for the gurdwara a founding date, a builder, a custodian, an endowment or a festival, and none mentions the lamp, the images or the crossing to Sadh Belo.

## A Darbar Called Jinda Pir

Independent, gazetteer-derived scholarship attests that the name "Zinda Pir" (or "Jinda Pir") also belongs to an older riverine shrine at Sukkur, at which Hindus and Muslims worshipped jointly until the late nineteenth century, when — per the 1919 Gazetteer of Sindh — the Hindu congregation "abandoned their claim and set up a shrine of their own to Jinda Pir on the Sukkur bank"; whether that shrine and this Sikh gurdwara are the same structure, two structures sharing one name, or otherwise related is not established by any source consulted, and neither account confirms the other.

Boivin's history of the Hindu Sufis gives a closely parallel account of a Jinda Pir at Sukkur. Writing of the religious recomposition of colonial Sindh, he says: "A new darbar was built on the bank of the Indus River in Sukkur, this coming to be known as Jinda Pir. It was engendered by a will of the Hindus to develop a distinct Hindu cult of Khwaja Khizr, also known as Zinda Pir, after they had been expelled from the main sanctuary located on an island in the Indus River." He dates it only to "the last decade of the nineteenth century" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 54, folio 41). He does not say who expelled the Hindus, and he does not name the island sanctuary.

What Boivin describes is a darbar of a Hindu cult of Khwaja Khizr. He does not call it a gurdwara, and he does not name Guru Nanak in connection with it. The name, the city and the riverbank match this site; the spelling differs, Jinda Pir in the book against Jind Pir in the site's name. Whether Boivin's darbar is this gurdwara is not settled by anything the book says, and the identification is offered here only as a possibility.

The same page of Boivin's book carries the wider story into which he sets the darbar. There he writes of "a growing similarity between the iconography of Guru Nanak and Jhulelal" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 54, folio 41), and, across the two pages, of "the attempt made to create a centralized path devoted to Jhulelal, then known as Amar Lal or Udero Lal", with "an attempt to impose a specific script, the Khudawadi", and "a meeting organized by several Hindu panchayats of Sindh at Udero Lal" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 53–54, folios 40–41). The book's index gathers at that one page, folio 41, the entries for Khwaja Khizr, Amar Lal, Jhulelal, the Gurmukhi and Khudawadi scripts, the Halani darbar, Varun the Vedic god, and the Nanakpanth (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 239, 242, 244, 246, 251–252, folios 227, 229, 231, 233, 238–239).

The index lists the name twice over. "Jinda Pir 41" appears as a headword of its own and again under darbars (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 241, 244, folios 228, 231), while "Zinda Pir 41" stands as a separate headword at the same folio, with no cross-reference between the two (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 252, folio 239). Elsewhere Boivin gives the name Zinda Pir to another figure: describing a book of poetry, he writes that "The book starts with Guru Nanak and Jhulelal, here named Darya Shah (and Zinda Pir in the caption of the image)" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 156, folio 143).

## Devotion and Practice

The books record no ritual at this gurdwara. What they say of the worship of Nanak's followers in Sindh is set out here as context.

Jürgen Schaflechner writes that "Many upper-caste Hindus in Sindh, especially from the Lohana community, refer to Nanak and his teachings (Thakur 1997, 141), and it is common to cover one's head before walking into a tikānā (Sin.), the temple containing the Guru Granth Sahib" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 286, folio not stated). Haroon Khalid describes the shrines of the Nanak Panthis: "Inside their temples in Sindh and Southern Punjab, one will find portraits of Guru Nanak, along with Krishna, Kali Mata and other Hindu deities" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 197, folio not stated). He adds that "Guru Nanak is mostly depicted wearing saffron coloured clothes" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 213, folio not stated), and that "It is believed that the tradition of Langar began with Guru Nanak" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 188, folio not stated).

Boivin finds the image of the Guru beside other holy figures in the darbars of the Sindhi Hindu Sufis. On Rochaldas's wall hang "pictures of Guru Nanak, Qutub Ali Shah, Sai Baba, Ramachandra and Krishna" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 134, folio 121); a jai formula names "the gods, including Guru Nanak, Jhulelal as well as Mast qalandar" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 141, folio 128); and Sachal's portrait shows him "in a pose that could be reminiscent of Guru Nanak's iconography" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 151, folio 138). The Guru's verse is sung there too: "Sain Vasan Shah became famous for reciting the banis of Shri Guru Nanak and the kalams of the Sufis." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 68, folio 55).

Sujan Rai Bhandari, writing in Persian in the reign of Aurangzeb, describes the worship of the followers of Baba Nanak as song: the sum of this group's worship is the reading of their master's verses, which they recite in song and melody (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 519). Their master's name is always on their tongue, and they count the service of travellers, of those who come and go, as the greatest worship. If someone arrives at midnight and speaks Baba Nanak's name, Bhandari writes, they treat him as brother and friend and serve him, even a stranger, a thief, a highwayman or an evildoer (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 519).

## The Guru's Journey Through Sindh

The site's tradition places Guru Nanak here on his third *udasi*. The books tell the Guru's journeys differently, and none of them names this spot.

Harish Dhillon, retelling the janamsakhis, sets out two schemes. "According to the Puratan Janamsakhi", the Guru made five journeys, which Dhillon dates (1499-1506), (1506-1513), (1514-1518), (1519-1521) and (1523-1524) (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 54–55, folios 60–61). "More modern accounts" give four, spanning twenty-three years: central and eastern India (1499-1505); southern India and Sri Lanka (1506-1509); the Gangetic plains to Tashkent and back through Kashmir (1514-1516); and the Arab countries (1518-1521) (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 55, folio 61). Dhillon himself believes there were four journeys and not five (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 56, folio 62).

In Dhillon's retelling it is the fourth journey that passes through Sukkur. Its itinerary runs through Multan, Sukkur, "Thatta (now in Sindh)", Hinglaj, and across the Arabian Sea (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 194, folio 214): from Multan they went "to Sukkur from where he turned southwards along the river Indus" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 194, folio 214). On this journey the Guru was "dressed in blue robes, a colour considered sacred by the Muslims", and carried a staff, a prayer mat, a Quran and an ablution pot (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 194–195, folios 214–215). The journey went on to Mecca, Medina and Baghdad, and returned by way of Iran, Kabul, Peshawar, Kandahar, Hasan Abdal, Tilla Balnath, Katasraj and Saidpur to Kartarpur (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 224–225, folios 246–247). Dhillon's third journey, by contrast, went north from Sialkot into Jammu and Kashmir, to "Sumeru, the legendary Mount Kailash" and Ladakh (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 191, folio 210).

Haroon Khalid places the Sindh journey late in the Guru's life: "Nanak visited Uch Sharif on his way to Sindh, towards the end of his travels, after he had already visited the East, South, North and West" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 278, folio 252). He had by then, Khalid writes, settled at Kartarpur and would travel occasionally to neighbouring areas (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 278, folio 252). Khalid also reports that "It is recorded in Sikh heritage that he came to the village of Kolachi and then visited the temple of a sea god for which he had to take a boat and travel to an island neighbouring Kolachi" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 229, folio not stated), and that Iqbal Qaiser's *Historical Sikh Shrines of Pakistan* identifies the Clifton temple as "the temple which Nanak had visited on his tour of Sindh" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 217, folio 191).

Further along Dhillon's route lies Hinglaj, where, Schaflechner writes, the Guru's footprints are still shown: "the engraved footsteps of Guru Nanak" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 256, folio not stated), and "The hollowed-out footsteps of Guru Nanak connect Hinglaj to the Sikh and Nanak paṃth (Hin. sect) traditions" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 257, folio not stated).

## The Life of Guru Nanak

What follows is told of Guru Nanak himself. None of it is told of Sukkur or of this gurdwara. Khalid warns that the part of his book that narrates Nanak's life "is fictionalised" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 17, folio not stated), and Dhillon calls the janamsakhis "cycles of tales purportedly drawn from the life of the first Guru of the Sikhs" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 9, folio 9).

**Birth and youth.** Dhillon gives the Guru's birth "On 14 April 1469", to Kalyan Chand Das Bedi, "or Mehta Kalu as he was affectionately called", accountant to the Bhatti family under Rai Bular (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 17–18, folios 19–20). Khalid's back matter lists him as "1. Guru Nanak (1469-1539)", "the first Guru, is believed to be the founder of Sikhism" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 314, folio 288), and Khalid writes that "Guru Nanak was born in a city now called Nankana Sahib, Pakistan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 168–169, folio not stated). Bhandari gives the year in other reckonings, and as report rather than his own finding: they say, he writes, that the Guru was born in the year 1526 of Bikramajit, corresponding to 880 of the Hijra, in the time of Sultan Bahlul Lodi (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). Shemeem Burney Abbas prints a birth year for him twice, but in both places the digits are lost from the printed text (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 48, folio not stated; p. 87, folio not stated).

At thirteen, in Dhillon's telling, he refused the sacred thread: "it is our actions and our actions alone" that give a man his position, the beginning of "a lifelong crusade against the performing of empty rites and rituals" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 19–21, folios 21–23). He "became well acquainted with the dervish Sayyad Hussain and gained an intimate knowledge of the Sufi line of thought" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 24, folio 28). Bhandari writes that in his early years the signs of unveiling and of miracles and wonders beyond the ordinary appeared from him; the editor's footnote gives a variant reading, at the age of ten (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).

**Sultanpur.** "Nanak moved to Sultanpur (around 60 km from Talwandi)" as keeper of Nawab Daulat Khan's store, bathed in the Bein rivulet, and gathered people in the evenings to hear his hymns (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 44, folio 49). He vanished into the stream for a week and came out with the words: "There is only one God. There is no Hindu, no Mussalman." (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 45–46, folios 50–51). "It is widely believed that it was in Sultanpur that the religious life of Nanak began" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 54, folio 36). He set out "dressed… in a strange garb - an approximate combination of what the holy men of Hinduism and of Islam would wear" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 53, folio 59).

**Marriage and sons.** Dhillon writes that he married "on 24 September 1487", Sulakhini, "daughter of Mul Chand and Chando Rani of the town of Batala", and that their sons were "Sri Chand (8 September 1494) and Lakhmi Das (12 February 1497)" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 44, folio 49). In Khalid's reconstruction she is "Sulakhni", "the daughter of Moolchand Khatri from the village of Pakhoke near Gurdaspur", and the sons are Sri Chand and Lachman Das (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 67, folio 49; p. 68, folio 50). Bhandari writes that the Guru came to the town of Batala and married there, and settled in one of the villages of Batala on the water of the Ravi, which he does not name (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). He adds that twelve kos from Batala, on the bank of the Ravi, is the place of Baba Nanak, where his descendants live to this day (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).

**The travels.** Khalid writes that the Guru went east "all the way to Bengal", south "to the island of Sri Lanka", north to Kashmir, Nepal and Tibet, then west "to Mecca and returned to settle at Kartarpur", mostly on foot, for "about twenty-four years" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 54, folio 36). He "preferred to stay on the outskirts" of villages (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 53, folio 35), and "Whenever he would visit an area he would visit its religious shrines, Muslim, Hindu and of any other denomination" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 37, folio 19). Bhandari writes that people came in their multitudes from every land and became his disciples, and that a minstrel named Mardana sang his verses and drew people into the snare of devotion (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).

**Succession and death.** "On his deathbed, he appointed not his son but his most loyal student as his spiritual successor" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 16, folio not stated). Bhandari names the son passed over as Lakhmi Das, a worthy son, and the successor as a Khatri named Lahna, whom the Guru titled Guru Angad and made his successor at the hour of his death; Lahna's byname, perhaps Tarhan, cannot be read with certainty in the printed text (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). Dhillon calls the successor "Guru Angad Dev" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 255, folio 282) and gives the Guru's death as "(The date: 22 September 1539.)" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 259, folio 287). Bhandari places it in the reign of Salim Shah Afghan, at an age between seventy and eighty (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).

**Shri Chand and the Udasis.** The site's first custodian, by its own account, was an Udasi. The books trace the Udasis to the Guru's elder son. Boivin names Shri Chand as "the eldest son of Guru Nanak who was not recognized as guru by the mainstream Sikh community", whose tradition the Udasis follow (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 53, folio 40). Khalid writes that the Udasi sect "is believed to have been founded by the eldest son of Guru Nanak, Sri Chand", who, "Ignored by his father when it was time to appoint the next Guru", founded his own sect (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 36, folio 18), and, in another book, that "the sect called Nanghe Sadhu derives its origin from the eldest son of Guru Nanak, Shri Chand" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 17, ch. 11, folio not stated). Bhandari counts among the dervishes of Hind a fourth class, the Udasis, devotees of Baba Nanak, who worship the Creator in the way of their master (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 567).

## Legends of the Guru

The legends below are told of Guru Nanak in the books, not of this site. The site's own tradition, that the Guru kindled the lamp still kept burning here and crossed from this spot to Sadh Belo, is not found in them.

Omar Kasmani records, in a note, a trope the Guru shares with the saints of the Indus: "According to Michel Boivin, the flower in milk is a key trope in Sufi narratives of the Indus valley and also beyond and can be witnessed in accounts of other saintly figures like Pir Shams of the Ismaʿilis and also Guru Nanak, the spiritual leader and founder of Sikhism (email message to author, September 1, 2014)" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 195, folio 178, ch. 5 n. 17).

At Mecca, in Dhillon's retelling, the Guru slept with his feet towards the Kaaba and, rebuked, asked that they be turned towards a place where God does not reside; the qazi conceded that "God lives everywhere" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 198–201, folios 218–221). In the version Khalid reports, "the Kaaba miraculously turned towards his feet"; Khalid doubts the miracle and writes: "What is important in this story is Nanak's criticism of the concept of a 'sacred space'" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 82, folio 64).

At Haridwar, Dhillon writes, the Guru faced west and threw water to reach "my newly ploughed fields there", mocking a ritual (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 79–80, folios 87–88); at the Kumbh Mela he taught that "it is our souls, the real sinners, that we must wash and not our bodies" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 106, folio 117); and in Baghdad he sang the *azaan* until his hearers knelt to pray (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 206–209, folios 227–230). When Karori called his cure a miracle, the Guru answered that "there is no such thing as a miracle" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 190, folio 209).

The Naulakha Hazari shrine's booklet, Khalid reports, holds that "Guru Nanak was born after Baba Naulakha Hazari prayed for his mother" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 31, folio 13). Iqbal Qaiser tells him: "The Sikhs don't accept it but the Muslim tradition is that Guru Nanak was born by the blessing of Baba Naulakh Hazari." (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).

## In Verse

None of the books quotes verse sung at this gurdwara. They do quote the Guru's own verse, in English, and Haroon Khalid's *Walking with Nanak* carries the most. The excerpts below are the Guru's, in the translations Khalid prints.

Of the *aarti*, the ritual of lamps, Khalid quotes the line "The sky the salver, the sun and moon the lamps …", footnoting it to Dhillon's *The First Sikh Spiritual Master*; his companion Iqbal Qaiser reads it as God residing in nature, and calls Nanak "a naturalist" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 55, folio 37). Later in the book, in another translation, the Guru sings the same *aarti* under a lone tree facing a Shiva temple:

*In the salver of the firmament, / The sun and the moon shine as lamps* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 243–244, folios 217–218).

On the oneness of God:

*My Master is One / He is One, brother, the only One* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 60, folio 42).

*God is One, / The Path is One* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 144, folio 126).

On the sacred thread he refused:

*From the cotton of compassion, / Spin the thread of contentment* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 61–62, folios 43–44).

On cleansing:

*As hands or feet besmirched with slime, / Water washes white* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 192, folio 166).

On the death of Karar, the money-lender of Sialkot, the first word of the second line damaged in the printed text:

*Friendship with a money- monger / Js [OCR?] false* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 191, folio 165).

In Khalid's reconstruction the Guru answers the plotting innkeepers with a hymn that opens, *The herons in their white feathers dwell in the sacred shrines of pilgrimage* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 123–126, folios 105–108), and at Pakpattan he sings a poem that begins *Launch ye the boat of austere contemplation …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 148–149, folios 130–131). Another poem Khalid quotes begins *His goodness cannot be priced or traded …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 280, folio 254). Of the Babur Bani, "the first poem of Nanak that I read", he quotes a prophetic line whose dates are partly damaged in the printed text: 'Coming in seventy-eight (1521 C.E), they will depart in Ninety sear, [OCR?] (1540 C_E.) [OCR?]' (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 113–114, folios 95–96).

Bhandari, in Persian, describes the Guru's poetry rather than quoting it. He writes that Baba Nanak composed Hindi verses on the truth of God, and set out the unity of God in plain words and shining metaphors (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). He records that the emperor heard from Guru Arjan, a name uncertain in the printed text, Hindi verses composed by Baba Nanak on the knowledge of God (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 164).

Shemeem Burney Abbas places the Guru in the line of the *dohā*, "an old popular Prakrit and Hindi meter in which a couplet is put together where the two verses rhyme": "Poets such as Kabir and Nanak used it to write their mystical poetry" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 141, folio not stated). Her glossary defines the *bani* by him: "Devotional verses such as those of Guru Nanak; verses of supplication, entreaty, humility, and reverence" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 179, folio not stated). And she quotes the singer Abida Parvin: "I sing Shah Hussain, Bulle Shah, Mian Muhammad Bakhsh, Hazrat Khawaja Ghulam Farid, Sacchal Sarmast, Baba Farid, and Guru Nanak" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 54, folio not stated).

## Setting: Sukkur and Rohri

The books say a little of the two cities the bridge joins, though nothing of this gurdwara.

Boivin sets Sukkur at a corner of the Rohri–Sukkur–Shikarpur triangle (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 31, folio 18) and tells the origin of Sadh Belo, the island the site's tradition links to the Guru's crossing (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 68–69). Schaflechner names Sukkur as the city of the Sadh Bela island *tīrtha*, "in the city of Sukkur on an island in the River Indus" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 138, folio not stated). Sukkur is also, for Boivin, the city of the 'Sukkur riots', dated "between December of 1939 and February of 1940", whose first victim was Bhagat Kanwar Ram, and where "the local Hindus opposed this rehabilitation since it was located in the harbour on the Indus River" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 61, folio 48). Writing of Sehwan, he contrasts it with Sukkur: "Contrary to other cities in Northern Sindh – for instance, Sukkur, following the Manzilgah riots – no communal violence had ever been registered" there (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 71, folio 58). He mentions "an orphanage in Sukkur" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 104, folio 91), and that Dadi Dhan "was born in Sukkur, Sindh, and her father was a famous lawyer" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 115, folio 102).

Bhandari's chronicle passes through Sukkur with the armies pursuing Dara Shikoh. News reached them that Dara, having passed Bhakkar, had halted at Sukkur on the twenty-fifth of Muharram (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 51). Shaikh Mir, Diler Khan and Qubad Khan crossed towards Sukkur; because of the thick jungle, the crowding of thorny trees and the hard stages of the road, many beasts perished and the baggage did not arrive, and on the sixth of Safar they reached Sukkur (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 51). Shaikh Mir stayed a day there, and the zamindars told him that twenty-five kos from Sukkur a road branches off towards Kandahar (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 50).

Across the river, Rohri was the home of Hindu and Muslim mystics. Boivin writes that Rochaldas was "born in 1879 in Rohri, in the north of Sindh, to a family that his hagiographers call modest" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 55, folio 42), and once found himself "wandering high in the sky above the port on the Indus at Rohri" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 57, folio 44). Bedil was "a Sufi born in Rohri", and Rochaldas was born "in Rohri, like Bedil, but seven years after his demise" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 97–98, folios 84–85). Sain Vasan Shah, the singer of Guru Nanak's banis, has a darbar there: "Vasan Shah's darbar is located in Ulhasnagar, while another one is in Rohri, on the bank of the Indus River" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 209, folio 196). Annemarie Schimmel writes: "Among them Bedil of Rohri (d. 1872) should be mentioned as a prolific, though not very outstanding, writer in five languages." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 427, folio 396). She names, too, "the SuhrawardI mystic Janullah of Rohri, one of the outstanding Persian poets of Sind from the second half of the eighteenth century" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 428, folio 397), and her *As Through a Veil* indexes the poet as "Bedil Rohriwaro (d . 1872)" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 351, folio 333).

Abbas records the town as a stop on a singer's road: Alan Faqir "is reported to have sung on the buses and trains between Rohri and Sehwan Sharif in his youth. He also sang at the dargahs (shrines) in the Sind." (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 78, folio not stated). Schaflechner reports the Sindhi writer Badar Abro's claim that Sindhis once lived in the valleys between Rohri and east Balochistan, the 'real Sindh' (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 94, folio not stated).

## Setting: Khizr, the River and the Followers of Nanak in Sindh

The books describe the province, not this gurdwara. Two of its threads meet in the site's history as the current account and Boivin give it: the worship of the river and of Khwaja Khizr, and the Nanakpanth.

**The river cult.** "Among the Lohanas and related mercantile groups, two main cults were followed: the Nanakpanth and the Daryapanth", Boivin writes. The Daryapanth "is in all probability the oldest of the two. It is based on the deification of the Indus River", and is run by "a priestly caste, the Thakurs" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 42–43, folios 29–30). "The Thakurs were the priests of Jhulelal. In Sindh, they were settled in different cities but they migrated to India from 1947 onwards" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 211, folio 198). "Jhulelal was already worshipped by some segments among the Hindus of Sindh, but as we saw before, they were in majority Nanakpanthis" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 158, folio 145). Kasmani writes that "In most historical accounts, it is the figure of Udero Lal who is conflated with Khizr in Sindh" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 195, folio 178, ch. 5 n. 9), and that "Offerings to the Indus River can still be documented in the wider region of Sindh" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 195, folio 178, ch. 5 n. 11).

**The Nanakpanth.** "In nineteenth-century Sindh, the Nanakpanth, or 'the path of Nanak', was probably the dominant religious path among the Hindu Sindhis" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 90, folio 77), and "In Sindh, many Nanakpanthis belonged to a subgroup known as the Udasis" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 53, folio 40). Boivin finds it "a seductive hypothesis that the Bhakti could have permeated Sindh through the teaching of Guru Nanak" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 90, folio 77). He writes that "although some darbars had been created in the late eighteenth century, there was a renaissance of the Nanakpanthi darbars in the second half of the nineteenth century", and the recomposition of the religious scene "is attested to by the creation and the development of several important Nanakpanthi darbars throughout Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 53–54, folios 40–41). His conclusion speaks of "the building of many new Nanakpanthi darbars all over Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 199, folio 186). In Sindh, he writes, the darbar was a term "used by the Sikhs, the Muslims and the Hindus" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 37–38, folios 24–25).

After the Singh Sabha process, Boivin writes, "The Sindhi Nanakpanthis were thus not included in the Sikh community, and they identified themselves as being a Hindu religious sect" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 200, folio 187). In an endnote he adds that "The Udasis of Sindh are coined as being a heterodox Sikh tradition" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 209, folio 196), and that "the pre-partition temples devoted to Jhulelal in Sindh were built with a dome, as were some Sikh samadhis" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 211, folio 198). "Due to the spread of the Nanakpanth in Sindh, the Hindu Sufis were very familiar with Sikh terminology" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 202, folio 189).

Schaflechner writes: "Many Lohanas in Sindh follow the teachings of Nanak, somewhat obscuring their religious affiliation for the colonial officers. Hence, in the 1881 census, Lohanas from Shikarpur and Hyderabad were counted as Sikhs, while ten years later, in the 1891 census, they were classified as Hindus" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 80, folio not stated). He adds that "many middle- and upper-class Hindus in Sindh are followers of the Nanak paṃth" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 257, folio not stated).

Khalid writes of Nanak Panthi Hindus, "thousands of such Sikhs", living "in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Punjab and Sindh in Pakistan" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 98, folio 80). The communities who refused the Khalsa and called themselves followers of Guru Nanak "still exist in Sindh and in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa. Baptised Sikhs look down upon them", and at Manora he saw them "now merging into the larger groups of Sikhs" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 230, folio 204). In *A White Trail* he writes that Nanak Panthi Sikhs "belong to the areas of South Punjab and Sindh", and that "They are found in very large numbers in the province of Sindh" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 197, folio not stated).

**After Partition.** Of the shrines the Hindus left behind, Boivin writes: "The darbars in Sindh were most of the time preserved with different arrangements regarding their management", often with gaddi nashins or followers in India paying keepers, and "despite the fluctuant diplomatic relations between Pakistan and India, it was always possible to travel across the border" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 68, folio 55).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **What the shrine called Jind Pir or Jinda Pir is.** The site's account calls it a Sikh gurdwara, Gurdwara Pehli Patshahi, marking a spot graced by Guru Nanak. Boivin describes a darbar called Jinda Pir on the Indus bank at Sukkur as the seat of "a distinct Hindu cult of Khwaja Khizr, also known as Zinda Pir", never calls it a gurdwara and never names Guru Nanak with it (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 54, folio 41). Whether the two are one place is not established.
- **When it began.** The site's tradition ties it to Guru Nanak's journey through Sindh. Boivin dates the darbar Jinda Pir to "the last decade of the nineteenth century" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 54, folio 41). The current account, citing the 1919 Gazetteer of Sindh, places the founding of a Jinda Pir shrine on the Sukkur bank in the late nineteenth century.
- **Why the Hindus left the older shrine.** The current account, citing the 1919 Gazetteer, says that the Hindu congregation "abandoned their claim and set up a shrine of their own to Jinda Pir on the Sukkur bank". Boivin says the Hindus built the new darbar "after they had been expelled from the main sanctuary located on an island in the Indus River" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 54, folio 41).
- **The name.** The site's name is Jind Pir; the current account gives the older shrine as "Zinda Pir" (or "Jinda Pir"); Boivin's text writes Jinda Pir, and his index carries "Jinda Pir" and "Zinda Pir" as separate headwords at the same folio, without a cross-reference (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 241, 244, folios 228, 231; p. 252, folio 239). Boivin gives the name Zinda Pir to Khwaja Khizr (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 54, folio 41), and reports it, in an image caption, for Jhulelal, named Darya Shah in the text (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 156, folio 143).
- **Which journey brought the Guru to Sukkur.** The site's tradition: the third *udasi*. Dhillon's retelling: the fourth, which went on to Thatta, Hinglaj and Mecca (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 194, folio 214); his third went north to Kashmir and Ladakh (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 191, folio 210). Khalid: the Sindh journey came "towards the end of his travels, after he had already visited the East, South, North and West", when he had settled at Kartarpur (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 278, folio 252).
- **How many journeys, and how long.** The Puratan Janamsakhi, as Dhillon reports it, counts five *udasis*; "More modern accounts" count four, over twenty-three years; Dhillon believes there were four (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 54–55, folios 60–61; p. 56, folio 62). Khalid gives the travels as "about twenty-four years" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 54, folio 36).
- **Whether Sindh's followers of Nanak are Sikhs.** The current account speaks of the Nanakpanthi community as "Hindus and Sikhs together". Boivin: after the Singh Sabha process the Sindhi Nanakpanthis "were thus not included in the Sikh community", and identified themselves as a Hindu sect (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 200, folio 187). Khalid calls the Nanak Panthi Hindus "thousands of such Sikhs" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 98, folio 80) and writes of Nanak Panthi Sikhs in Sindh (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 197, folio not stated). Schaflechner reports Lohanas counted as Sikhs in the census of 1881 and as Hindus in that of 1891 (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 80, folio not stated).
- **The Guru's dates.** Dhillon: born 14 April 1469, died 22 September 1539 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 17–18, folios 19–20; p. 259, folio 287). Khalid: 1469–1539 (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 314, folio 288). Bhandari: born, 'they say', in the year 1526 of Bikramajit, corresponding to 880 of the Hijra, under Sultan Bahlul Lodi, and died in the reign of Salim Shah Afghan at an age between seventy and eighty (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).
- **His wife and sons.** Dhillon: Sulakhini, of the town of Batala; sons Sri Chand and Lakhmi Das (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 44, folio 49). Khalid: Sulakhni, of the village of Pakhoke near Gurdaspur; sons Sri Chand and Lachman Das (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 67, folio 49; p. 68, folio 50). Bhandari names one son, Lakhmidas, whom the Guru passed over (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).
- **Miracles.** Bhandari writes that signs of unveiling and miracles appeared from the Guru in his early years (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). Dhillon writes of "Guru Nanak's clear and emphatic disavowal of the miraculous", the word printed "dear" in his text (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 11, folio 11). Khalid writes that the Guru "abhorred the concept of associating miracles with religious personalities", yet "his biography is nothing but a story of his miracles" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 16, folio not stated).
- **The Mecca story.** Dhillon: the qazi is answered in words, and concedes that "God lives everywhere" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 198–201, folios 218–221). The legend Khalid reports: the Kaaba itself "miraculously turned towards his feet" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 82, folio 64).

## Legacy

The books remember Guru Nanak as a teacher who crossed the line between Hindu and Muslim, and the site's description of a shrine kept by Hindus and Sikhs together, where the Granth and images are honoured side by side, belongs to that memory.

Dhillon writes that Nanak "brought a message of peace and harmony to believers of the mutually hostile religions of Islam and Hinduism" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 12, folio not stated). Khalid writes that "Nanak himself had been born into a Hindu household and was then educated in a Muslim milieu; however, he was neither a practising Muslim nor a Hindu" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 95, folio 77); that "his entire movement was for the blurring of the religious divide" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 266, folio 240); and that "Nanak was aware of these fluid boundaries … instead Sikhism, a distinct religion with its own exclusive boundaries, emerged out of Nanak's philosophy" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 310, folio 284). "The movement that began with Nanak was a spiritual reformation movement"; "if anything, Nanak's movement was a rebellion against institutional religion" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 219, folio not stated). "It is said" that, asked which religion to adopt, he told his Hindu and Muslim companions to be true Muslims or honest Hindus: "That would be Nanak's religion" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 219, folio not stated). Khalid adds that "many Muslims, acquainted with the teachings of Guru Nanak, are devoted to him too and visit his shrine", and a Muslim painter tells him: "Guru Nanak is like a Muslim Sufi Saint" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 220, folio not stated; p. 219, folio not stated).

Sufis claimed him too. Shemeem Burney Abbas writes: "Although Baba Farid was a Muslim Sufi saint who wrote devotional poetry in Panjabi, his texts are revered among the Sikhs because he influenced the founder of their own religion, Baba Guru Nanak" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 87, folio not stated). Khalid writes that Baba Farid "became an inspiration for Guru Nanak when he visited the shrine during his travels" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated). Robert Rozehnal records a twentieth-century Chishti Sabiri shaykh who, "In certain lectures", describes "non-Muslim spiritual luminaries—Krishna, Rama, Sita, Lachman, and Guru Nanak—as believers in the one true God and religious leaders with 'Divine connection' (nisbat)" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 63, folio 53). Boivin writes that, for the Hindu Sufis of Sindh, "the message of the Sufis can also be found through Guru Nanak, Mirabai and Kabir, as well as in other sants' poetry" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 160, folio 147).

Bhandari, in the age of Aurangzeb, praised the Guru's followers in Persian: of the disciples and devotees of Baba Nanak, he writes, most are men of spiritual state, whose word is accepted, given to austerities, and whose prayers are answered (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 519). They live, he writes, of one colour with friends and without war with enemies, and the faith this group has in its leader is seldom found among other peoples (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 519).
