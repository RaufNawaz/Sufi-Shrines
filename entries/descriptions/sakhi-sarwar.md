## Overview

In the town of Sakhi Sarwar (Nigaha), at the foot of the Sulaiman range in the Dera Ghazi Khan district, stands the shrine of Sultan Sakhi Sarwar — a saint of the twelfth century honoured across an extraordinary breadth of communities, and known by the loving title *Lakhdata*, “the giver of hundreds of thousands,” for his boundless generosity. For centuries his cult was one of the most widespread in the western Punjab, drawing not only Muslims but Hindus and Sikhs, and his shrine at Nigaha remains a major place of pilgrimage in the south-west of the province.

Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori's *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, composed in 1875, names the place. He relates that the saint pitched his tent at the foot of the mountain, where mauza Nigaha is now settled and where the Hazrat's *roza* also is, and that it was there that he was martyred, in 577 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 197; p. 198). Sujan Rai Bhandari's *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, a Persian work composed in the fortieth regnal year of Aurangzeb, describes the tomb of Sultan Sarwar forty kos west of Multan at the foot of the hills, without naming Nigaha or Dera Ghazi Khan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528). Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti's *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* tells the saint's life at length from the stories of his devotees, though its own subject is a *chauki* of Sakhi Sarwar in Lahore which, the author says, is not his tomb (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 255). Haroon Khalid, writing in this century, names "the shrine of Sakhi Sarwar in Dera Ghazi Khan" among the places where Baisakhi is still kept (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 63, folio not stated), and records the bombing of the shrine in 2011 (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).

None of these books gives a date for the building of the tomb, names a builder or patron, or gives the date of an *ʿurs*.

## The Life of the Saint

Sayyid Ahmad Sultan, called Sakhi Sarwar (“the Generous Chief”), lived in the twelfth century; tradition places his origins in the region of Multan/Shahkot, and his life is wrapped in legend more than documented history. He is remembered as a saint of miraculous power and open-handed charity, and after his death his shrine at the place now called Sakhi Sarwar became the centre of a devotional cult of remarkable reach and endurance.

Lahori heads his notice with the name Sayyid Ahmad, known as Sakhi Sarwar Sultan, and lists it the same way in his contents (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 197–198, folios 195–186 as printed [corrected: 185–186]; p. 18, folio 6). He places the saint by region: he is of the ancient *mashaikh* and the great *awliya* of the region of Multan (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 197). Chishti gives the name in the same way: his name was Sayyid Ahmad and his father's name Zain al-Abidin (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 256). Bhandari, too, names the father, Sayyid Zain al-Abidin, father of Sultan Sarwar (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528).

On the authority of the author of *Tashrif al-Shurafa*, Lahori traces a pedigree of twenty-one steps to Ali: Sayyid Ahmad bin Sayyid Zain al-Abidin bin Sayyid Umar bin Sayyid Abd al-Latif bin Sayyid Baha al-Din bin Sayyid Ghiyas al-Din bin Sayyid Baha al-Din bin Sayyid Salah al-Din bin Sayyid Zain al-Abidin bin Sayyid Isa bin Sayyid Salih bin Sayyid Abd al-Ghani bin Sayyid Jalil bin Sayyid Khair al-Din bin Sayyid Zia al-Din bin Sayyid Daud bin Sayyid Abd al-Jalil Rumi bin Sayyid Ismail bin Imam Jafar Sadiq bin Imam Muhammad Baqir bin Imam Zain al-Abidin bin Imam Husain bin Ali (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 197). His index lists a Sayyid Abd al-Latif as the saint's grandfather (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 322, folio 310).

His father, in Lahori's account, came from Arabia to Hind because of some dissension and settled in the Multan area at a village whose printed name, perhaps Kahr Si Kot, cannot be read with certainty. Being devout, ascetic and a keeper of night vigils, he was given a wife by the headman of the village: the *muqaddam*, named Pira, gave him his daughter Bibi Ayesha in marriage. From her the Hazrat was born, a *wali* from birth (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 197).

Chishti tells it with other names and distances. Zain al-Abidin came by way of the Multan district to a village whose printed name, perhaps Shah Sakot, cannot be read with certainty, ten kos south of Multan (the number, too, is printed uncertainly); the *numberdar* of the place became his *murid* and built a mosque; after a death in the household, told in a damaged passage, the elder daughter, whose name is printed uncertainly (perhaps Aisha), was married to him (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 256). After two[OCR?] years a son was born, Sakhi Sarwar, known as Sayyid Ahmad, the name Ahmad written in the margin and uncertain (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 256–257), and a brother, Sayyid Abd al-Ghani, known by a byname that cannot be read with certainty, when he was ten years old, the age too printed uncertainly (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 257). Chishti speaks of two wives in his father's household, one of them the mother of Hazrat Sayyid Ahmad (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 258). Bhandari places the father four kos south of Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528).

Bhandari describes the saint's youth in the language of the Persian *tazkira*: in early youth he mastered the commanding self, the *nafs-e ammara*, by hard austerities and devotions beyond what can be borne, and the chamber of his inner being was lit by the lamp of mystic knowledge (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 527–528).

After his father's death, Lahori writes, the son's fame spread and people came to him in droves. His maternal cousins, with whom he owned half the village, grew jealous and tormented him, and he left. At Baghdad he received inward grace in the service of Ghaus al-Azam, of Shaikh al-Shuyukh Shihab al-Din Suhrawardi and of Shaikh Maudud Chishti, and returned to Hind perfected (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 197). Back in the Punjab, he lived for a while at Dhaunkal and then came back to Kahr Si Kot, a name printed uncertainly (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 197).

Chishti names his authorities and the saint's lineage of teaching. His account is what he has heard from the Bharai[OCR?] people. In a book whose title, perhaps *Miraj al-Awliya*, is printed uncertainly, the saint is a disciple of Khwaja Maudud Chishti, the word for disciple also uncertain (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 266); and he received the blessing of *wilayat* from Hazrat Khwaja Bahauddin Zakariya Multani as well, and his family was Chishti and Suhrawardi (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 267).

Lahori records his marriage into the house of the governor: the governor of Multan gave him his daughter in marriage, and a son named Siraj al-Din was born of her (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 197). Lahori's index adds a daughter, Aisha, daughter of Sakhi Sarwar (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 321, folio 309). Bhandari names his wife Bi Bai and his young son Mian, followed by a name printed uncertainly, perhaps Rata (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527); Chishti names her Bibi Bai Sahiba and the son, six months old, Sayyid Raj (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 265).

## The Martyrdom

Lahori's saint was warned of a plot and left the village secretly with his brother Abd al-Ghani, his wife and his young son Siraj al-Din. At the foot of the mountain, where Nigaha now stands and where his *roza* is, he pitched a tent in the very midst of a waterless wilderness (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 197). Then the jealous brothers, learning that he had gone, gathered a great company, rode after him, and at that place martyred the Hazrat together with his dependants. This took place in 577 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 198).

Bhandari gives the killers another name: by a chance that befell, a fight broke out with the Khokhar people, the word before whose name, perhaps Jast, is printed uncertainly, and Sultan Sarwar and his brother Mian Dhoda[OCR?] were martyred. His wife, Bi Bai, could not bear the separation and died, and his young orphaned son died too; they were buried at the foot of the hills, and the tomb became known as the tomb of the martyr (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527).

Chishti's telling is the fullest. The family set out from the village of Karsi Kot for Nigahi, both names printed uncertainly, at a distance of some kos, the number unreadable, from a place whose printed name, perhaps Banaye, cannot be read with certainty; beyond that lay jungle (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 264). A rider on the plumed mare kills a number of the enemy, printed uncertainly, perhaps seventy-two, and is martyred; the saint, at prayer while Shaikh Garha holds his horse, kills the army; then he turns to an old Khokhar (the word printed uncertainly), tells him that he is his killer, and bids him come and martyr him. He orders Shaikh Garha and the *mukabbir* to bury him (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 265).

Of the household's end, Chishti relates that Bibi Bai Sahiba refused to remarry, and the grave opened and took her; her six-month-old son Sayyid Raj was swallowed after the grave answered that there was no need, for their *fatiha* would be said in house after house, the last words printed uncertainly (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 265). He names the burial place of the father's family as Karsi Kot, a name printed uncertainly, where Hazrat Zain al-Abidin, Mai Aisha (perhaps; this name too is uncertain), Sayyid Mahmud and Sayyid Sohrab lie, a place of pilgrimage for the people (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 264).

Chishti's year of death is a word printed uncertainly, perhaps the sixth century Hijri, reckoned from the end of Ghaznavid rule in a passage that is damaged in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 267).

## The Shrine

The shrine complex at Sakhi Sarwar (Nigaha), set in the hills, comprises the saint’s tomb together with a mosque and associated buildings, and it has drawn pilgrims for centuries. The darbar remains the defining landmark of its town.

Bhandari, writing in the reign of Aurangzeb, places it: forty kos west of Multan, at the foot of the hills of Baluchistan, on the side (the word is oddly printed) of the river Sind, is the resting-place of Sultan Sarwar (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528). On another page he puts the road from Multan at more than forty kos (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527).

Lahori records a spring at the place. By the blessing of the saint's step God made a spring flow there out of the desert, which exists to this day (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 197). Chishti, too, tells of a spring that broke out there, the words printed uncertainly, where the saint made his ablution (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 266).

Bhandari tells how the tomb's custodians came to it. Three men, one blind, one a leper and one impotent, took up attendance at the tomb and were cured; because the first thing the blind man saw was what the book calls *kah o kalang* (both words printed uncertainly), the three became known by that name, and their descendants who keep the tomb are called Kah o Kalang to this day (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527).

Chishti's account of the shrine's servants runs through Shaikh Garha, who held the saint's horse. He brings people from the village of Kotli as sweepers of the *khanqah* (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 265), and receives the prayer-mat, the ewer and the banner, all three words printed uncertainly; the Dholis, a name also uncertain, claim descent from him; and he speaks of the Bharai people, who are called Sakhi, both names again uncertain (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 266).

## Devotion and Practice

His devotees — the *Sultanias* or *Sarwarias* — carried his banners in seasonal pilgrimages, and until modern times his following notably crossed the boundaries of religion, with Hindus and Sikhs of the Punjab honouring him alongside Muslims; the Sikh tradition itself preserves references to the *Sakhi Sarwar* pilgrims. This shared devotion made him one of the great examples of the composite religious culture of the old Punjab. Devotees come to seek blessing, fulfil vows, and honour the *Lakhdata*, and the annual pilgrimage and urs are great occasions of music, offering, and celebration in the regional manner.

Bhandari describes the pilgrimage of his own day. Multitudes come from every region and corner of the world, give *nazrs* and ask for their wishes, which are granted; the crowds are greatest especially at the beginning of winter (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527). The whole road from the city of Multan to the tomb, a distance of more than forty kos, is full of people coming and going (the last word printed uncertainly), and the crowd on it is beyond what writing can set down (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527). The tomb, he says, became a place of pilgrimage after the healing of a Qandahar merchant's camel: from that time the noble tomb became a place of pilgrimage for the people (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527).

Chishti speaks of hundreds of thousands of people at his tomb (the figure printed uncertainly), and of a caravan of the black blanket from a place whose printed name, perhaps Dwara, cannot be read with certainty, in black caps, which stops in Lahore (printed as Laho) and goes on toward a place whose name cannot be read, some kos from Multan (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 256). In Lahore, at the sarai west of Jahangir's tomb, when the company of Hazrat Sakhi Sarwar sets out (the word for the company is printed uncertainly) it halts there for a night, and for ten days there is great festivity (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 861). He says that in every village a house of the Hazrat has been built, and that a *takiya*, the word printed uncertainly, still stands in a quarter of a city he does not name (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 266).

Haroon Khalid names the shrine among "a few other Muslim villages and cities in Pakistan where this festival [Baisakhi] is still celebrated, despite it being considered a 'non-Muslim' celebration" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 63, folio not stated). It is also among the Sufi shrines that a man named Nadeem has visited "on the occasion of urs" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 219, folio not stated).

Like other major shrines, it has been touched by the violence of recent years — a suicide bombing struck a procession of its pilgrims in 2011 — yet the devotion has continued. Khalid writes: "On 3 April 2011 the shrine of Sakhi Sarwar in Dera Ghazi Khan was bombed killing around fifty people" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).

## Legends and Miracles

Chishti's book, drawing on what the Bharai people tell, carries a long cycle of the saint's miracles, set in his village and in Multan.

In the village, goats are revived by divine power, with the words that the offering, a word printed uncertainly, has been accepted at the court of God, and the goat is given as a thanks-offering; the villagers say that these are sorcerers (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 258). When the land is divided he answers that it does not matter, it is their right, and the wheat he holds as a *faqir's* trust thrives while his brothers' is very poor, the word printed uncertainly (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 258–259). He kills a lion with his wooden shoe (the word printed uncertainly), gives the heap away in the way of God to the *lagi* people, and the story turns to the government's revenue and the road to Multan (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 259).

At Multan the ruler, whose printed name, perhaps Gahar Sahan, cannot be read with certainty, sees an army and finds a man with a shade over him; sealed vessels left empty, a water-pot and another vessel whose name is printed uncertainly, are filled by the grace of God (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 260). There is food of paradise; the brothers are jailed; the saint is dismissed with a splendid robe of honour, and says that all the prisoners in the jail are his brothers, so that all are freed and the *nazr* is shared; and forty fakirs, the number printed uncertainly, ask for *iftar* (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 261). The robe is cut into rags, and the horse is eaten and revived: by divine power the horse rose up alive and whole. Zain al-Abidin refuses such a king, and the whole city was in uproar (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 262).

The princess is offered; the answer, addressed to the king, sets his royal rank against the family's own station, in words that cannot be read with certainty (perhaps that they are Arabs). Then all the *awliya* and *aqtab* come with two thousand banners, the number and the word printed uncertainly, from the village of Kahrsi, also uncertain, to the gate of Multan; in a contest of archery a khan whose name cannot be read misses, and a Hazrat named Shah … Sahib, the middle of his name unreadable and partly in the margin, hits; and the *milni* follows (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 263). The lagis are shamed, and Mai Aisha Sahiba, her name printed uncertainly, turns something whose printed name, perhaps *jowar* millet, cannot be read with certainty into fruit (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 264).

Bhandari's miracles are those of the tomb: the healing of the Qandahar merchant's camel, which made the tomb a place of pilgrimage, and the cure of the three men who became its custodians (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527). Lahori's is the spring that broke from the desert at Nigaha and still flowed in his day (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 197).

## In Verse

Lahori prints a Persian chronogram on the saint:

سید و سرور و سخی احمد
بود در ملک سروری والی
رفت چوں از جہاں بخلد بریں
شد زمین از وجود او خالی
سال تاریخ وصل آں سرور
گفت سرور کہ سرور عالی

The words that carry the date are the poem's last two, *sarwar-e ali*; the book prints no year beside them (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 198).

Chishti, too, prints a *qita*, the word printed uncertainly, under the heading "Chronogram of Sakhi Sarwar's Death", which joins the names Sarwar and Sakhi Ahmad with the title *Sultan-e Alam*; the word that carries its year is partly illegible (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 267).

## Sakhi Sarwar in Lahore

Chishti's chapter on the saint begins in Lahore, not at Nigaha. His table of contents lists "The *Chauki* of Sakhi Sarwar" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 2), and the chapter is headed as the account of the *chauki* of Sakhi Sarwar, known as Lakh Data (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 255).

He describes a building shaped like a *kotha*, with a wooden door that was white and has turned black, and niches for lamps, standing south of the sarai of Muhammad Shafi in the Anarkali Sadr Bazaar, with unbaked houses west of the road from the slaughterhouse to a bazaar whose printed name, perhaps Chamaran, cannot be read with certainty (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 255). It was built in his name by Bharai people, the name printed uncertainly, out of worldly desire; otherwise there is no true place of the Hazrat's retreat here. Its owners are six Bharai men, the name again uncertain, whose names are printed in a garbled list (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 255).

## Dhonkal

Two of the books tie the saint's name to a place called Dhonkal, which is not the shrine at Nigaha. Lahori says that the saint lived for a time at Dhaunkal before returning to Kahr Si Kot (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 197). Bhandari describes Dhonkal, the name printed uncertainly, twelve kos from Sialkot, a place associated with Sultan Sarwar and always a place of pilgrimage; in summer people come from every region and offer *nazrs*, and for two months there is a great throng there (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 516). Neither book says that its Dhonkal is the other's.

## Setting

Bhandari's account of the suba of Multan, in which he places the tomb, gives the region's history as he knew it. In the time of the Langahs, Sultan Husain Langah granted the country from Karorkot to Mankot in *jagir* to Malik Sohrab and other Baloch who had come to him from Kech Makran; and in the reign of Akbar, Raja Todar Mal, the chief *diwan*, confirmed that country to the Baloch and set it as a strong outpost and barrier between Khurasan and Hindustan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525). These are statements about the suba and its Baloch borderland, the hills of Baluchistan at whose foot he places the tomb, not about the shrine itself.

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **When he died.** The current account places him in the twelfth century. Lahori dates his martyrdom to 577 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 198). Chishti gives a year printed uncertainly, perhaps the sixth century Hijri, reckoned from the end of Ghaznavid rule, in a damaged passage (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 267). Bhandari gives no year.
- **Whose name is Ahmad.** Lahori's notice and Chishti both make the saint Sayyid Ahmad and his father Zain al-Abidin (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 197; Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 256). One line of Lahori's index keeps Sayyid Ahmad, in brackets, as the saint's own name (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 319, folio 307), while another makes Ahmad his father, listing Ahmad as the father of Sakhi Sarwar (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 312, folio 300).
- **Aisha, mother or daughter.** Lahori's notice makes Bibi Ayesha, daughter of the headman Pira, the saint's mother (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 197); Chishti likewise marries the headman's elder daughter, whose printed name may be Aisha, to the father (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 256). Lahori's index lists Aisha, daughter of Sakhi Sarwar (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 321, folio 309).
- **Where the family settled.** The current account places his origins in the region of Multan/Shahkot. Lahori names the village, perhaps Kahr Si Kot (the reading is uncertain), in the Multan area (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 197). Chishti brings the father to a village printed uncertainly, perhaps Shah Sakot, ten kos south of Multan (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 256), and names the family's burial place Karsi Kot, also uncertain (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 264). Bhandari places the father four kos south of Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528).
- **His masters.** Lahori: Ghaus al-Azam, Shihab al-Din Suhrawardi and Maudud Chishti, at Baghdad (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 197). Chishti: a *murid* of Khwaja Maudud Chishti according to the *Miraj al-Awliya*, who also received *wilayat* from Bahauddin Zakariya Multani, his family Chishti and Suhrawardi (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 266; p. 267).
- **His wife and son.** Lahori: the wife is the daughter of the governor of Multan, and the son is Siraj al-Din (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 197). Bhandari: the wife is Bi Bai and the son Mian, followed by a name printed uncertainly, perhaps Rata (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527). Chishti: the wife is Bibi Bai Sahiba and the son Sayyid Raj; in his story the ruler of Multan offers a princess (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 265; p. 263).
- **His brother.** Lahori: Abd al-Ghani, who fled with him (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 197). Chishti: Sayyid Abd al-Ghani, known by a byname that cannot be read with certainty (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 257). Bhandari: Mian Dhoda[OCR?], martyred with him (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527).
- **Who killed him.** Lahori: his jealous brothers, who rode after him to Nigaha (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 198). Bhandari: the Khokhar people, the word before the name printed uncertainly, in a fight (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527). Chishti: an army that he destroys at prayer, after which he bids an old Khokhar, the word printed uncertainly, to martyr him (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 265).
- **How his wife and son died.** Lahori: martyred with him, among his dependants (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 198). Bhandari: the wife died of grief at the separation, and the orphaned son died too (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527). Chishti: the wife, refusing to remarry, was taken by the opening grave, and the infant son swallowed after it (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 265).
- **How far from Multan.** Bhandari gives forty kos (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528), and on another page more than forty kos (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527).
- **Who keeps the tomb.** Bhandari: the descendants of three cured men, called Kah o Kalang (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527). Chishti: Shaikh Garha and the people he brought from Kotli as sweepers, with the Dholis claiming descent from him (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 265; p. 266).
- **The chronogram.** Lahori's verse names the saint Sayyid, Sarwar and Sakhi Ahmad and gives its date in the words *sarwar-e ali* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 198). Chishti's carries the names Sarwar and Sakhi Ahmad and the title *Sultan-e Alam* (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 267).
- **The bombing of 2011.** The current account: a suicide bombing struck a procession of its pilgrims in 2011. Khalid: "On 3 April 2011 the shrine of Sakhi Sarwar in Dera Ghazi Khan was bombed killing around fifty people" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).

## Legacy

Sultan Sakhi Sarwar is remembered as one of the most widely venerated saints of the old western Punjab — the open-handed *Lakhdata* whose cult, crossing the lines of Muslim, Hindu, and Sikh, embodied the shared sacred world of the region before the divisions of modern times. His shrine at Nigaha endures as a major centre of pilgrimage and as a monument to that older, composite devotion.

The books bear out the reach of the name. Chishti, in Lahore, finds a *chauki* of the saint in Anarkali, a halt for his pilgrims at a Lahore sarai, and a house of the Hazrat in every village (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 255; p. 861; p. 266); Bhandari, writing in the reign of Aurangzeb, describes crowds on the forty kos from Multan beyond what writing can set down (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527); and Khalid, in this century, finds Baisakhi still kept at the shrine (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 63, folio not stated).

- General established histories of the Sakhi Sarwar cult and the shared religious culture of the Punjab.
- Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya* (Persian, composed 1292 AH / 1875 CE; read in an Urdu edition annotated by محمد اقبال مجددی and issued by تصوّف فاؤنڈیشن).
- Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* (dated 1867 for the original, the date unconfirmed).
- Sujan Rai Bhandari of Batala, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* (Persian, composed regnal year 40 of Aurangzeb; 1918 Delhi lithograph edited by Zafar Hasan).
- Haroon Khalid, *A White Trail: A journey into the heart of Pakistan's religious minorities* (westland ltd, 2013; first e-book edition 2013).
- Haroon Khalid, *In Search of Shiva: A Study of Folk Religious Practices in Pakistan* (New Delhi: Rupa Publications India, first impression 2015).
