## Overview

In the town of Sakhi Sarwar (Nigaha), at the foot of the Sulaiman range in the Dera Ghazi Khan district, stands the shrine of Sultan Sakhi Sarwar — a saint of the twelfth century honoured across an extraordinary breadth of communities, and known by the loving title *Lakhdata*, “the giver of hundreds of thousands,” for his boundless generosity. For centuries his cult was one of the most widespread in the western Punjab, drawing not only Muslims but Hindus and Sikhs, and his shrine at Nigaha remains a major place of pilgrimage in the south-west of the province.

Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori's *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, composed in 1875, names the place. He relates that the saint pitched his tent at the foot of the mountain "جہاں اب موضع نگاہا آباد ہے اور روضہ بھی حضرت کا اسی مقام پر ہے", where mauza Nigaha is now settled and where the Hazrat's *roza* also is, and that it was there that he was martyred, in 577 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 197; p. 198). Sujan Rai Bhandari's *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, a Persian work composed in the fortieth regnal year of Aurangzeb, describes the tomb of "سلطان سرور" forty kos west of Multan at the foot of the hills, without naming Nigaha or Dera Ghazi Khan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528). Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti's *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* tells the saint's life at length from the stories of his devotees, though its own subject is a *chauki* of Sakhi Sarwar in Lahore which, the author says, is not his tomb (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 255). Haroon Khalid, writing in this century, names "the shrine of Sakhi Sarwar in Dera Ghazi Khan" among the places where Baisakhi is still kept (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 63, folio not stated), and records the bombing of the shrine in 2011 (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).

None of these books gives a date for the building of the tomb, names a builder or patron, or gives the date of an *ʿurs*.

## The Life of the Saint

Sayyid Ahmad Sultan, called Sakhi Sarwar (“the Generous Chief”), lived in the twelfth century; tradition places his origins in the region of Multan/Shahkot, and his life is wrapped in legend more than documented history. He is remembered as a saint of miraculous power and open-handed charity, and after his death his shrine at the place now called Sakhi Sarwar became the centre of a devotional cult of remarkable reach and endurance.

Lahori heads his notice "سید احمد المشہور بسخی سرور سلطان", Sayyid Ahmad, known as Sakhi Sarwar Sultan, and lists it in his contents as "سید احمد المشہور سخی سرور سلطان" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 197–198, folios 195–186 as printed [corrected: 185–186]; p. 18, folio 6). He places the saint by region: "قدمائے مشائخ و کبرائے اولیائے خطہ ملتان سے ہیں", he is of the ancient *mashaikh* and the great *awliya* of the region of Multan (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 197). Chishti gives the name in the same way: "نام ان حضرت کا سید احمد ہی اور باپ کا نام زین العابدین", his name was Sayyid Ahmad and his father's name Zain al-Abidin (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 256). Bhandari, too, names the father: "سید زین العابدین پدر سلطان سرور" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528).

On the authority of the author of *Tashrif al-Shurafa*, Lahori traces a pedigree of twenty-one steps to Ali: Sayyid Ahmad bin Sayyid Zain al-Abidin bin Sayyid Umar bin Sayyid Abd al-Latif bin Sayyid Baha al-Din bin Sayyid Ghiyas al-Din bin Sayyid Baha al-Din bin Sayyid Salah al-Din bin Sayyid Zain al-Abidin bin Sayyid Isa bin Sayyid Salih bin Sayyid Abd al-Ghani bin Sayyid Jalil bin Sayyid Khair al-Din bin Sayyid Zia al-Din bin Sayyid Daud bin Sayyid Abd al-Jalil Rumi bin Sayyid Ismail bin Imam Jafar Sadiq bin Imam Muhammad Baqir bin Imam Zain al-Abidin bin Imam Husain bin Ali (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 197). His index lists a Sayyid Abd al-Latif as the saint's grandfather, "عبد اللطیف سید جد سخی سرور" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 322, folio 310).

His father, in Lahori's account, came from Arabia to Hind "بسبب کسی تفرقہ کے", because of some dissension, and settled at "کہر سی کوٹ [OCR?]" in the Multan area, a name whose reading is uncertain in the printed text. Being devout, ascetic and a keeper of night vigils, he was given a wife by the headman of the village: "اس گاؤں کے مقدم مسمی پیرا نے اپنی لڑکی بی بی عائشہ ان کے نکاح میں دے دی", the *muqaddam* of that village, named Pira, gave him his daughter Bibi Ayesha. From her the Hazrat was born, "ولی مادر زاد", a *wali* from birth (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 197).

Chishti tells it with other names and distances. Zain al-Abidin came by "صلع[OCR?] ملتاں" to "موضع شاہ[OCR?] سکوٹ[OCR?]", "ملتاں سی دس[OCR?] کوس جنوب رویہ", ten kos south of Multan; the *numberdar* of the place became his *murid* and built a mosque; after a death in the household, told in a damaged passage, the elder daughter, "سمات[OCR?] عالیشہ[OCR?]", was married to him (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 256). After two[OCR?] years a son was born, "سخی سرور المعروف سید [margin: احمد[OCR?]]" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 256–257), and a brother, "سید عبد الغنی المشہور نمانں[OCR?] مہودا[OCR?]", when he was "بدس[OCR?] برس" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 257). Chishti speaks of two wives in his father's household, one of them the mother of Hazrat Sayyid Ahmad (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 258). Bhandari places the father four kos south of Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528).

Bhandari describes the saint's youth in the language of the Persian *tazkira*: in early youth he mastered the "نفس اماره" by "ریاضات شاقه و عبادات مالایطاق", and "بانوار شمع عرفان شبستان باطن روشنی گرفت" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 527–528).

After his father's death, Lahori writes, the son's fame spread and people came to him in droves. His maternal cousins, "جن کے ساتھ یہ نصف گاؤں کے مالک تھے", with whom he owned half the village, grew jealous and tormented him, and he left. At Baghdad he received inward grace in the service of Ghaus al-Azam, of Shaikh al-Shuyukh Shihab al-Din Suhrawardi and of Shaikh Maudud Chishti, and returned to Hind perfected (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 197). Back in the Punjab, "پنجاب میں پہنچ کر چندے بمقام دھونکل سکونت کی پھر کہر سی کوٹ [OCR?] میں آئے", he lived for a while at Dhaunkal and then came back to Kahr Si Kot (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 197).

Chishti names his authorities and the saint's lineage of teaching. His account is what is "مسموع", heard, from the Bharai[OCR?] people; in "کتاب معراج[OCR?] الاولیاء[OCR?]" the saint is a "مرید[OCR?]" of "خواجہ مودود چشتی" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 266); and "آپ نے حضرت خواجہ بہاوالدین ذکریا ملتانی سے بھی نعمت ولایت حاصل کی اور خاندان آپ کا چشتیہ و سہروردیہ تھا", he received the blessing of *wilayat* from Hazrat Khwaja Bahauddin Zakariya Multani as well, and his family was Chishti and Suhrawardi (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 267).

Lahori records his marriage into the house of the governor: "صوبہ ملتان نے اپنی لڑکی ان کے نکاح میں دی۔ اس سے ایک بیٹا سراج الدین نامی پیدا ہوا", the governor of Multan gave him his daughter in marriage, and a son named Siraj al-Din was born of her (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 197). Lahori's index adds a daughter, "عائشہ بنت سخی سرور", Aisha, daughter of Sakhi Sarwar (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 321, folio 309). Bhandari names his wife "بی بائی" and his young son "میان را[OCR?] تا[OCR?]" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527); Chishti names her "بی بی بائی صاحبہ" and the son, six months old, "سید راج" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 265).

## The Martyrdom

Lahori's saint was warned of a plot and left the village secretly, "مع بھائی عبدالغنی و زوجہ سراج الدین، پسر خرد سال", with his brother Abd al-Ghani, his wife and his young son Siraj al-Din. At the foot of the mountain, where Nigaha now stands and where his *roza* is, he pitched a tent "عین ویرانے بے آب میں", in the very midst of a waterless wilderness (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 197). Then: "پیچھے ان کے جب حاسد بھائیوں نے خبر پائی کہ وہ چلے گئے تو مجمع کثیر کر کے ان کے پیچھے سوار ہوئے اور اس مقام پر آکر حضرت کو مع لواحقین شہید کر دیا", the jealous brothers, learning that he had gone, gathered a great company, rode after him, and at that place martyred the Hazrat together with his dependants. "یہ واقعہ سنہ پانسو ستتر ہجری میں واقع ہوا", this took place in 577 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 198).

Bhandari gives the killers another name: "باتفاقی که رو داد بقوم جست[OCR?] کهوکهر آویزش گردید سلطان سرور و میان دهودا[OCR?] برادرش درجه شهادت یافتند", he and his brother Mian Dhoda[OCR?] were martyred in a fight with the "جست[OCR?] کهوکهر" people. His wife "بی بائی" could not bear the separation and died, and his young orphaned son died too; "در دامن کوه مدفون شدند و بمزار شهید شهرت گرفت", they were buried at the foot of the hills, and the tomb became known as the tomb of the martyr (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527).

Chishti's telling is the fullest. The family set out from "موضع کرسی کوٹ[OCR?]" for "مقام نگاہی[OCR?]", "سانٹ[OCR?] کوس" from "بنایے[OCR?]", and beyond that lay jungle (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 264). A rider on the "کلگی گھوڑی" kills "بہتر[OCR?]" and is martyred; the saint, at prayer while "شیخ گاڑھا" holds his horse, kills the army; then he turns to an old "کھوکھر[OCR?]" and says: "تو ہمارا قاتل ہے آ اور مجکو شہید کر", you are our killer; come and martyr me. He orders "شیخ گاڑھا اور مکبر" to bury him (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 265).

Of the household's end, Chishti relates that "بی بی بائی صاحبہ" refused to remarry, and the grave opened and took her; her six-month-old son "سید راج" was swallowed after the grave answered "کچھ ضرورت نہیں فاتحہ ہمارا گھر گھر[OCR?] ہوے گا" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 265). He names the burial place of the father's family as "کرسی کوٹ[OCR?]", where "حضرت زیں العابدیں اور مائی عایتہ[OCR?] اور سید محمود اور سید سہراب" lie, "زیارتگاہ حلق اللہ", a place of pilgrimage for the people (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 264).

Chishti's year of death is "ششمہ[OCR?] ہجری", reckoned from the end of Ghaznavid rule in a passage that is damaged in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 267).

## The Shrine

The shrine complex at Sakhi Sarwar (Nigaha), set in the hills, comprises the saint’s tomb together with a mosque and associated buildings, and it has drawn pilgrims for centuries. The darbar remains the defining landmark of its town.

Bhandari, writing in the reign of Aurangzeb, places it: "چهل کروهے ملتان غرب رویه دامن کوه بلوچستان الطرف دریاے سنده خوابگاه سلطان سرور است", forty kos west of Multan, at the foot of the hills of "بلوچستان", "الطرف" (as printed) the river Sind, is the resting-place of Sultan Sarwar (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528). On another page he puts the road from Multan at more than forty kos (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527).

Lahori records a spring at the place. By the blessing of the saint's step God "وہاں ریگستان میں سے پانی کا چشمہ جاری کر دیا جو اب تک ہے", made a spring flow there out of the desert, which exists to this day (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 197). Chishti, too, tells of a spring, "وہاں سے چشمہ آب[OCR?] نکلا[OCR?]", where the saint made his ablution (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 266).

Bhandari tells how the tomb's custodians came to it. Three men, one blind, one a leper ("مبروص") and one impotent ("نامرد"), who "مجاورت مزار اختیار کرده", took up service at the tomb and were cured; because the first thing the blind man saw was "کاه[OCR?] و کلنگ[OCR?]", the three became known by that name, "و اولاد انها را که مجاورت مزار دارند تا حال کاه و کلنگ نامند", and their descendants who keep the tomb are called Kah o Kalang to this day (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527).

Chishti's account of the shrine's servants runs through Shaikh Garha, who held the saint's horse. He brings people from "موضع کوٹلی" as "جاروب کش خانقاہ", sweepers of the *khanqah* (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 265), and receives the "مصلا[OCR?]", "آفتابہ[OCR?]" and "نشان[OCR?]"; the "ڈھولی[OCR?]" claim descent from him; and he speaks of "بھرائی[OCR?] لوگ جو سخی[OCR?] کہلاتی ہیں", the Bharai people who are called Sakhi (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 266).

## Devotion and Practice

His devotees — the *Sultanias* or *Sarwarias* — carried his banners in seasonal pilgrimages, and until modern times his following notably crossed the boundaries of religion, with Hindus and Sikhs of the Punjab honouring him alongside Muslims; the Sikh tradition itself preserves references to the *Sakhi Sarwar* pilgrims. This shared devotion made him one of the great examples of the composite religious culture of the old Punjab. Devotees come to seek blessing, fulfil vows, and honour the *Lakhdata*, and the annual pilgrimage and urs are great occasions of music, offering, and celebration in the regional manner.

Bhandari describes the pilgrimage of his own day. Multitudes come "از اطراف ممالک و اکناف گیتی", from every region and corner of the world, give *nazrs* and ask for their wishes, which are granted; the crowds are greatest "علی الخصوص درابتدا ایام زمستان", especially at the beginning of winter (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527). The whole road is full of travellers: "از شهر ملتان تا مزار که فاصله زیاده از چهل کروه واقع است تمام راه آدم آمد و رفی[OCR?] شود", and the crowd on it is "بقدرے ... که بتحریر راست نیاید", beyond what writing can set down (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527). The tomb, he says, became a place of pilgrimage after the healing of a Qandahar merchant's camel: "ازان زمان ان مزار بزرگوار زیارت گاه خلایق گشت", from that time the noble tomb became a place of pilgrimage for the people (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527).

Chishti speaks of "اپکی[OCR?] مزار پر لکھہ[OCR?] ہا[OCR?] آدمی", hundreds of thousands of people at his tomb, and of "ایک قافلہ کالی کملی کا", a caravan of the black blanket, from "دوارہ[OCR?]", in black caps, which stops in "لاہو" and goes on toward "لگا[OCR?] ہا[OCR?]", some kos from "ملتاں" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 256). In Lahore, at the sarai west of Jahangir's tomb, "جب حضرت سخی سرور کا سنگ [OCR?] جاتا ہے تو ایک رات یہاں مقام کرتا ہے اور دس روز یہاں رہتی [OCR?] دہوم دہام ہوتی ہے", when the company of Hazrat Sakhi Sarwar sets out it halts there for a night, and for ten days there is great festivity (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 861). He says that "ہر گاؤں[OCR?] میں مکان حضرت کا بنا ہوا ہے[OCR?]", in every village a house of the Hazrat has been built, and that a "مکان تکیہ[OCR?]" still stands in a "محلہ" of a city he does not name (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 266).

Haroon Khalid names the shrine among "a few other Muslim villages and cities in Pakistan where this festival [Baisakhi] is still celebrated, despite it being considered a 'non-Muslim' celebration" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 63, folio not stated). It is also among the Sufi shrines that a man named Nadeem has visited "on the occasion of urs" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 219, folio not stated).

Like other major shrines, it has been touched by the violence of recent years — a suicide bombing struck a procession of its pilgrims in 2011 — yet the devotion has continued. Khalid writes: "On 3 April 2011 the shrine of Sakhi Sarwar in Dera Ghazi Khan was bombed killing around fifty people" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).

## Legends and Miracles

Chishti's book, drawing on what the Bharai people tell, carries a long cycle of the saint's miracles, set in his village and in Multan.

In the village, goats are revived "قدرت الہی سے", by divine power, with the words "تمہارا بیرو[OCR?] درگاہ الہی میں قبول ہوا", and the "بکرا" is given "بطور شکرانہ", as a thanks-offering; the villagers say "یہ جادو گر ہیں", these are sorcerers (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 258). When the land is divided he answers "کچھ مضائقہ نہیں تمہارا حق ہے", and the wheat he holds as "امانت فقیر" thrives while his brothers' is "ہمایت[OCR?] ناقص" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 258–259). He kills a lion with his "کفش جوبی[OCR?]", gives the heap "فی سبیل اللہ" to the "لاگی لوگ", and the story turns to the "محاصل سرکار" and the road to Multan (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 259).

At Multan the ruler, "حاکم ملتاں مسمی گہر[OCR?] سہاں[OCR?]", sees an army and finds a man with a "سایہ" over him; sealed vessels left empty, "دوان[OCR?] خالی" and "لوٹہ خالی", are filled by "اللہ کی عناست[OCR?]" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 260). There is "طعام بہشتی", food of paradise; the brothers are jailed; the saint is dismissed "معہ خلعت فاخرہ", with a splendid robe of honour, and says "یہ سب قیدی جیل کے ہمارے بھائی ہیں", all these prisoners are our brothers, so that all are freed and the *nazr* is shared; and "جالیس[OCR?]" fakirs ask for *iftar* (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 261). The robe is cut "لتہ لتہ", into rags, and the horse is eaten and revived: "قدرت الہی سے وہ گھوڑا صحیح و سالم زندہ ہو کر اوٹھہ کہڑا[OCR?] ہوا", by divine power the horse rose up alive and whole. Zain al-Abidin refuses "ایسے بادشا", and "تمام شہر میں شور پڑ گیا", the whole city was in uproar (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 262).

The princess is offered; the answer is "اے بادشاہ ہم عرب[OCR?] بہر[OCR?] اور تم بادشاہ ہو". Then "تمام اولیا اقطاب", all the *awliya* and *aqtab*, come with "دو ہزار[OCR?] نشان[OCR?]" from "موضع کہرسی[OCR?]" to "دروازہ ملتان", the gate of Multan; in a contest of archery "ن خان[OCR?]" misses and "حضرت شہ[OCR?] د لیو[OCR?] [margin] صاحب" hits; and the *milni* follows (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 263). The lagis are shamed, and "مائی عا پسہ[OCR?] صاحبہ" turns "چوار[OCR?]" into fruit (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 264).

Bhandari's miracles are those of the tomb: the healing of the Qandahar merchant's camel, which made the tomb a place of pilgrimage, and the cure of the three men who became its custodians (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527). Lahori's is the spring that broke from the desert at Nigaha and still flowed in his day (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 197).

## In Verse

Lahori prints a Persian chronogram on the saint:

سید و سرور و سخی احمد
بود در ملک سروری والی
رفت چوں از جہاں بخلد بریں
شد زمین از وجود او خالی
سال تاریخ وصل آں سرور
گفت سرور کہ سرور عالی

The words that carry the date are "سرور عالی"; the book prints no year beside them (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 198).

Chishti, too, prints a "قطعہ[OCR?]" under the heading "تاریخ وفات سخی سرور صاحب", a chronogram of the death of Sakhi Sarwar Sahib, which joins the names "سرور و سخی احمد" and "سلطان عالم"; the word that carries its year is partly illegible (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 267).

## Sakhi Sarwar in Lahore

Chishti's chapter on the saint begins in Lahore, not at Nigaha. His table of contents lists "حال چوکی سخی[OCR?] سرور سلطان" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 2), and the chapter is headed "احوال مکان چوکی سخی[OCR?] سرور المشہور بہ[OCR?] لکہہ[OCR?] داتا", the account of the *chauki* of Sakhi Sarwar, known as Lakh Data (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 255).

He describes a building shaped like a *kotha*, with a wooden door, "جو سفید تھا سیاہ ہو گیا ہی", and niches for lamps, standing "مقام صدر بازار انارکلی کی سرائے[OCR?] محمد شفیع کی جنوب رویہ", south of the sarai of Muhammad Shafi in the Anarkali Sadr Bazaar, with unbaked houses west of the road from the "قصاب خانہ" to "بازار جماراں[OCR?]" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 255). It was built in his name by "پہرائی[OCR?] لوگوں" "بطمع نفسانی", out of worldly desire, "ورنہ کوئی اصلی مکان اعتکاف حضرت کا نہیں", otherwise there is no true place of the Hazrat's retreat here; its owners are six "پہرا[OCR?]" men, "وہی چہہ آدمی مالک اس مکان کی ہیں", whose names are printed in a garbled list (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 255).

## Dhonkal

Two of the books tie the saint's name to a place called Dhonkal, which is not the shrine at Nigaha. Lahori says that the saint lived for a time "بمقام دھونکل" before returning to Kahr Si Kot (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 197). Bhandari describes "دوازده کروه سیالکوت دهونکل[OCR?] مکانی است منسوب سلطان سرور", twelve kos from Sialkot, a place associated with Sultan Sarwar, "دایما زیارتگاه خلایق", always a place of pilgrimage; in summer people come from every region and offer *nazrs*, "نذورات می گذرانند", and "تا دو ماه دران مکان هجوم بسیار می شود", for two months there is a great throng there (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 516). Neither book says that its Dhonkal is the other's.

## Setting

Bhandari's account of the suba of Multan, in which he places the tomb, gives the region's history as he knew it. In the time of the Langahs, "سلطان حسین لنکاه" granted "از کرورکوت تا مانکوت" in *jagir* to "ملک سهراب و غیره بلوچان که از کچ مکران نزد او رسیده بودند", Malik Sohrab and other Baloch who had come to him from Kech Makran; and in the reign of Akbar, "راجه تودرمل دیوان اعلی ان ولایت را بر بلوچان مسلم داشته درمیان خراسان و هندوستان یزک محکم مقرر ساخت و درحدود هر دو کشور سد باستحکام انداخت", Raja Todar Mal confirmed that country to the Baloch and set it as a strong outpost and barrier between Khurasan and Hindustan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525). These are statements about the suba and its Baloch borderland, the "دامن کوه بلوچستان" at whose foot he places the tomb, not about the shrine itself.

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **When he died.** The current account places him in the twelfth century. Lahori dates his martyrdom to 577 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 198). Chishti gives "ششمہ[OCR?] ہجری", reckoned from the end of Ghaznavid rule, in a damaged passage (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 267). Bhandari gives no year.
- **Whose name is Ahmad.** Lahori's notice and Chishti both make the saint Sayyid Ahmad and his father Zain al-Abidin (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 197; Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 256). One line of Lahori's index keeps "(سید احمد)" as the saint's own name (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 319, folio 307), while another makes Ahmad his father: "احمد والد سخی سرور" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 312, folio 300).
- **Aisha, mother or daughter.** Lahori's notice makes Bibi Ayesha, daughter of the headman Pira, the saint's mother (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 197); Chishti likewise marries the headman's elder daughter, "سمات[OCR?] عالیشہ[OCR?]", to the father (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 256). Lahori's index lists "عائشہ بنت سخی سرور", Aisha, daughter of Sakhi Sarwar (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 321, folio 309).
- **Where the family settled.** The current account places his origins in the region of Multan/Shahkot. Lahori names the village "کہر سی کوٹ [OCR?]" in the Multan area (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 197). Chishti brings the father to "موضع شاہ[OCR?] سکوٹ[OCR?]", ten kos south of Multan (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 256), and names the family's burial place "کرسی کوٹ[OCR?]" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 264). Bhandari places the father four kos south of Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528).
- **His masters.** Lahori: Ghaus al-Azam, Shihab al-Din Suhrawardi and Maudud Chishti, at Baghdad (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 197). Chishti: a *murid* of Khwaja Maudud Chishti according to the *Miraj al-Awliya*, who also received *wilayat* from Bahauddin Zakariya Multani, his family Chishti and Suhrawardi (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 266; p. 267).
- **His wife and son.** Lahori: the wife is the daughter of the governor of Multan, and the son is Siraj al-Din (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 197). Bhandari: the wife is "بی بائی" and the son "میان را[OCR?] تا[OCR?]" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527). Chishti: the wife is "بی بی بائی صاحبہ" and the son "سید راج"; in his story the ruler of Multan offers a princess (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 265; p. 263).
- **His brother.** Lahori: Abd al-Ghani, who fled with him (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 197). Chishti: "سید عبد الغنی المشہور نمانں[OCR?] مہودا[OCR?]" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 257). Bhandari: Mian Dhoda[OCR?], martyred with him (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527).
- **Who killed him.** Lahori: his jealous brothers, who rode after him to Nigaha (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 198). Bhandari: the "جست[OCR?] کهوکهر" people, in a fight (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527). Chishti: an army that he destroys at prayer, after which he bids an old "کھوکھر[OCR?]" to martyr him (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 265).
- **How his wife and son died.** Lahori: martyred with him, among his dependants (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 198). Bhandari: the wife died of grief at the separation, and the orphaned son died too (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527). Chishti: the wife, refusing to remarry, was taken by the opening grave, and the infant son swallowed after it (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 265).
- **How far from Multan.** Bhandari gives "چهل کروهے", forty kos (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528), and on another page "زیاده از چهل کروه", more than forty kos (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527).
- **Who keeps the tomb.** Bhandari: the descendants of three cured men, called Kah o Kalang (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527). Chishti: Shaikh Garha and the people he brought from Kotli as sweepers, with the Dholis claiming descent from him (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 265; p. 266).
- **The chronogram.** Lahori's verse reads "سید و سرور و سخی احمد" and gives its date in "سرور عالی" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 198). Chishti's carries "سرور و سخی احمد" and "سلطان عالم" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 267).
- **The bombing of 2011.** The current account: a suicide bombing struck a procession of its pilgrims in 2011. Khalid: "On 3 April 2011 the shrine of Sakhi Sarwar in Dera Ghazi Khan was bombed killing around fifty people" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).

## Legacy

Sultan Sakhi Sarwar is remembered as one of the most widely venerated saints of the old western Punjab — the open-handed *Lakhdata* whose cult, crossing the lines of Muslim, Hindu, and Sikh, embodied the shared sacred world of the region before the divisions of modern times. His shrine at Nigaha endures as a major centre of pilgrimage and as a monument to that older, composite devotion.

The books bear out the reach of the name. Chishti, in Lahore, finds a *chauki* of the saint in Anarkali, a halt for his pilgrims at a Lahore sarai, and a house of the Hazrat in every village (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 255; p. 861; p. 266); Bhandari, writing in the reign of Aurangzeb, describes crowds on the forty kos from Multan beyond what writing can set down (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527); and Khalid, in this century, finds Baisakhi still kept at the shrine (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 63, folio not stated).
