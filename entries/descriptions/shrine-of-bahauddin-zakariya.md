## Overview

In the old city of Multan — the “City of Saints” — stands the shrine of Hazrat Baha-ud-Din Zakariya (c. 1170–1267), the saint who planted the Suhrawardi Sufi order in the soil of the subcontinent and made Multan one of the first great centres of Islamic mysticism in South Asia. A contemporary and friend of Baba Farid of Pakpattan, and like him a shaper of the spiritual map of the Punjab, Bahauddin Zakariya founded a khanqah at Multan whose influence reached across the region for centuries. His tomb, raised in the thirteenth century, is among the oldest and most revered Muslim shrines in Pakistan.

Of the books consulted for this account, Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori's *Hadiqat al-Awliya* opens its chapter on the Suhrawardis with him (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 17, folio 5), and Zahur ul-Hasan Sharib's *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind* gives him a chapter of his own (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 48–52, folios 44–48). Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi writes that the other disciples of Shihab-ud-Din Suhrawardi "were eminent in their own right, but the continuous history of the silsila can be traced only through the khalifas of Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 220, folio 202). Annemarie Schimmel calls him "the Suhrawardi master" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 8, folio 4), and Christopher Shackle, annotating the lyrics of Bulleh Shah, counts him among the Five Pirs and calls his tomb in Multan "a major center of pilgrimage" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 186, folio 161, n. 5 on 122). Haroon Khalid writes of him as the saint "who is now considered the patron saint of Multan" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 282, folio 256), and of his shrine as one "which has become the symbol of the Muslim city of Multan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 20, folio not stated). Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* sets his tomb inside the fort of Multan, beneath a lofty dome of brick and lime (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528).

## The Life of the Saint

Baha-ud-Din Zakariya was born around 1170 near Multan, into a Qureshi family of Arab descent that had settled in the region. He pursued religious learning far afield — at Khurasan, at Bukhara, and in the Hejaz, where he is said to have studied at Medina — before travelling to Baghdad to enter the discipleship of Shaikh Shihab-ud-Din Abu Hafs Umar al-Suhrawardi, the great systematiser of the Suhrawardi order and author of the *‘Awarif al-Ma‘arif*. Under him Zakariya completed his training with remarkable speed and received the mantle of *khilafat*, with authorisation to carry the order to India. Returning to Multan, he established his khanqah and became the foremost Suhrawardi shaykh of the subcontinent.

The books know him by many names. The *Hadiqat al-Awliya* calls him "شیخ الشیوخ بہاء الدین زکریا قریشی ملتانی" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 158), and elsewhere prints the name as Bahar al-Din, "شیخ الشیوخ خواجہ بہار الدین زکریا" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 17, 24); its editor records an interlinear gloss in his manuscript, "پیشِ نظر نسخہ میں 'بہار' کے نیچے 'تابندہ' لکھا ہوا ہے" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 158–159, folios 146–147, حاشیہ 1). Bhandari too, in one passage, prints the name as "شیخ بهار الدین ذکریا" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 556). Sharib heaps titles on him, among them "پیر لاثانی", peerless pir, and "مخزن سخا", treasury of generosity, and writes that "اکابر اولیاء میں آپ کا شمار ہے", he is counted among the greatest of the *awliya* (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 48, folio 44). His name, Sharib says, was بہاء الدین and his *kunya* ابو محمد, Abu Muhammad, though "بعض نے آپ کی کنیت ابو البرکات بھی لکھی ہے", some also wrote it Abu al-Barakat, and the book prefers neither (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 49, folio 45, حاشیہ 8). At Bukhara, he adds, he became famous as Bahauddin Farishta (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 50, folio 46), and in the dream in which he received his robe the Prophet calls him "شیخ بہاء الدین غوث العالم" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 51, folio 47). Shackle explains Bahaval Haq, the name under which Bulleh Shah's lyric invokes him, as the "title given to the Sufi saint Baha ud Din Zakariya of Multan" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 198, folio 173). Richard Eaton calls him both "Shaikh Baha' ad-Din Zakariya" and "Baha' al-Haqq Zakariya" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 222; p. 227), and Taufiq Rafat's selection of Bulleh Shah spells him Bahauddin Zakria (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 39, folio 19).

Lahori writes that his grandfather Kamal al-Din Ali Shah Qureshi came from Mecca to Khwarazm and from there to Multan: "پہلے ان کے جدّ بزرگوار کمال الدین علی شاہ قریشی مکہ معظمہ سے خوارزم میں آئے۔ وہاں سے ملتان میں آکر قیام کیا" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 160, folio 148). Sharib's account of the family's route is close to it: his "جد امجد", Maulana Shaikh Kamal al-Din Aliya Shah[OCR?], a dervish-natured elder and an honoured member of the Quraish, "مکہ سے سکونت ترک کرکے خوارزم میں رہے' پھر خوارزم سے ملتان تشریف لائے اور وہاں مستقل سکونت اختیار کی", left Mecca, lived in Khwarazm, then came from Khwarazm to Multan and settled there permanently (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 48, folio 44, حاشیہ 6), where many became his devotees (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 49, folio 45). Sharib gives the reason for the choice: "ملتان اس زمانہ میں اسلامی علوم و فنون کا مرکز تھا", Multan in that age was a centre of the Islamic sciences and arts (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 48, folio 44). Bhandari gives the line in Persian as "این شیخ پور شیخ وجیه الدین محمد بن شیخ کمال الدین علی شاه قریشی" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528).

On the family's descent Sharib prints four accounts. He writes that "آپ قریشی' اسدی' ہاشمی تھے", Qurashi, Asadi, Hashimi (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 48, folio 44, حاشیہ 1); that "یہ بھی کہا جاتا ہے", it is also said, that he was of the progeny of "مہیار[OCR?] بن اسود بن مطلب بن اسد بن عبد العزیٰ[OCR?] بن قصی" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 48, folio 44, حاشیہ 2); that some accepted only "القرشی الاسدی" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 48, folio 44, حاشیہ 3); and that some wrote, in Persian, "سلسلہ او باسد قریش کہ جد مادری حضرت امیر المومنین علی کرم اللہ وجہہ منتہی می شود", that his lineage ends at Asad of Quraish, the maternal grandfather of Ali (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 48, folio 44, حاشیہ 4). Then Sharib settles the question: "آپ ہاشمی ہیں اور آپ کے ہاشمی ہونے میں کوئی شک و شبہ نہیں", he is Hashimi and in his being Hashimi there is no doubt whatever (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 48, folio 44, حاشیہ 5). Nur Ahmad Chishti's *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* names him in a chain of transmission with the same three *nisbas*, "خواجہ مخدوم بہار[OCR?] الحق زکریا[OCR?] ملتانی قرشی[OCR?] اسدی ہاشمی" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 404).

His father, Lahori writes, was Shaikh Wajih al-Din, born in that house, who married the daughter of Maulana Husam al-Din Tirmizi, resident at mauza Kot Karor (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 160, folio 148). Sharib also names the father as شیخ وجیہہ الدین, Shaikh Wajih al-Din, and the mother as "بی بی فاطمہ", Bibi Fatima (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 49, folio 45). Of her father he gives two accounts and prefers neither. In the first he is Maulana Husam al-Din Tirmizi, who came from his homeland, Tirmiz, to "قلعہ کوٹ کڑور[OCR?]", the fort of Kot Karor, when the Tatar plundering began (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 49, folio 45, حاشیہ 7). In the second, introduced with "یہ بھی کہا جاتا ہے", he is Hazrat Isa, of the progeny of Ghaus al-Azam Shaikh Jilani, resident at "ہامہ[OCR?]", who married her to Shaikh Wajih al-Din when he visited there. Both accounts end with the family at Kot Karor (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 49, folio 45).

The books do not agree on the year of his birth. Sharib gives the very day: "آپ صبح کے وقت' جمعہ کے دن 27 رمضان 566ھ کو کوٹ کڑور میں پیدا ہوئے", he was born in the morning, on a Friday, 27 Ramazan 566 AH, at Kot Karor (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 49, folio 45). The editor of the *Hadiqat al-Awliya* gives the same year and place, "حضرت بہاء الدین زکریا ملتانیؒ ۵۶۶ھ / ۱۱۷۰ء میں کوٹ کہروڑ (ملتان) میں پیدا ہوئے", born in 566 AH / 1170 CE at Kot Kahror (Multan), following Nur Ahmad Faridi's *Tazkira-e Hazrat Baha al-Din Zakariya Multani* on Sayyid Jalal Bukhari's authority (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 158, folio 146, حاشیہ 1). Lahori's own text, however, has him born in Wajih al-Din's house in 578 AH, "پانچ سو اٹھتر", gives the year twice by chronogram, and names no birthplace (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 160–161, folios 148–149). Bhandari has "درسال پانصد و شصت و پنج هجری درکوت کرور", in 565 AH, at Kot Karor (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528). The index to Rizvi's history gives "b. 1182-83" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 461, folio 437), and Khalid prints his dates as 1170-1267 (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 20, folio not stated).

Sharib tells of a child set apart from birth. "آپ مادر زاد ولی تھے", he writes: he was born a *wali*, and as an infant would stop suckling to listen when his father recited the Quran (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 49, folio 45; p. 49, folio 45, حاشیہ 9). His father died when he was twelve (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 49, folio 45). Sharib has him memorise the Quran "سات سال کی عمر میں سات قرات کے ساتھ", at the age of seven and with the seven readings (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 50, folio 46, حاشیہ 10); Lahori has the Quran memorised at twelve (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 160, folio 148).

The accounts of his studies follow different routes. In Lahori's account he went after his father's death to Bukhara for *sarf*, *nahw*, hadith and *tafsir*; then to the Haramain, with five years at Medina; then to Bait al-Maqdis; and then to Baghdad (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 160, folio 148). In Sharib's he went first to Khurasan and spent seven years in the outward lessons (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 50, folio 46, حاشیہ 10); then to Bukhara, where he completed his learning and "وہاں آپ 'بہاء الدین فرشتہ' کے نام سے مشہور ہوئے"; then to Medina, where he took hadith from Shaikh Kamal al-Din Muhammad Yamani, one of the great *muhaddithin*, "اجازت نامہ بھی حاصل کیا", obtaining an *ijazat-nama* as well, and stayed five years (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 50, folio 46). He met the dervishes of "خراسان' بخارا' مکہ' مدینہ اور بیت المقدس", Khurasan, Bukhara, Mecca, Medina and Bait al-Muqaddas (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 50, folio 46). Bhandari says that his father died in his childhood, and that he studied the formal sciences, travelled through "ایران و توران" and reached Baghdad (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528).

At Baghdad he took *bai'at* from Shaikh Shihab-ud-Din Umar Suhrawardi and, in Lahori's words, "اٹھارہ روز کے عرصہ میں خرقہ خلافت حاصل کیا اور ملتان کو مامور ہوئے", received the *khirqa* of *khilafat* within eighteen days and was posted to Multan, a speed the book gives as a mark of distinction (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 160, folio 148). Sharib calls him "آپ حضرت شیخ شہاب الدین عمر سہروردی رحمۃ اللہ علیہ کے مرید اور خلیفہ ہیں", the murid and khalifa of Shaikh Shihab-ud-Din Umar Suhrawardi (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 48, folio 44), and places the *bai'at* at Baghdad (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 50, folio 46). He quotes Nizam al-Din Auliya that the disciple stayed "ستر[OCR?] روز", seventy days, the reading of the number uncertain in the printed text, in his master's service and received much bounty; the other murids resented it, and the shaikh answered them: "تم مثل لکڑی کے تر ہو اور زکریا مثل لکڑی خشک کے ہے' پس آگ خشک لکڑی کو جلد پکڑتی ہے" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 50, folio 46, حاشیہ 11). Bhandari has him become the disciple of "شیخ شهاب الدین سهروردی" and receive "پایه خلافت" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528), and Chishti likewise names him a disciple of "حضرت شیخ شہاب الدین سہروردی" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 154). After the investiture, Sharib writes, his pir "آپ کو ملتان جانے کی تاکید فرمائی", enjoined him to go to Multan (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 51, folio 47).

Lahori sets out the spiritual chain that ran to him. "آغاز اس سلسلہ عالیہ کا حضرت سید الطائفہ جنید بغدادی سے ہے", the line begins with Junaid Baghdadi, and runs through Mamshad Alu Dinawari, Rudaim[OCR?], Ali Rudayari[OCR?], Ahmad Aswad Dinawari, Abu Abdullah Khafif, Abu al-Qasim Gurgani, Abu Bakr Nassaj, Shaikh Ahmad Ghazali, the brother of Imam Muhammad Ghazali, Ziya al-Din Abu al-Najib Suhrawardi and Wajih al-Din Suhrawardi to Shihab-ud-Din Umar Suhrawardi, and from him to Bahauddin Zakariya (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 158, folio 146).

On the road to Multan, Sharib relates, he met a learned qalandar, Sayyid Abd al-Quddus of Mosul, who had put on the qalandars' dress at the grave of سید جمال الدین مجرد; "آپ نے ان کا قلندرانہ جامہ اتروایا اور عالم جذب سے ان کو عالم سلوک کو پہنچایا", he had the qalandari garment taken off and brought him from *jazb* to *suluk* (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 51, folio 47). Shaikh Jalal al-Din Tabrizi travelled with him as far as Nishapur, where they parted (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 51, folio 47). In another chapter Sharib gives the cause: he rebuked Jalal al-Din for not naming Shihab-ud-Din Suhrawardi before Farid al-Din Attar, and, displeased, separated from him at Nishapur (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 35, folio 31, حاشیہ 6). Rizvi tells it differently: on the road from Baghdad to Nishapur Bahauddin "would constantly pray and meditate", while his companion "would visit local dervishes and spend time sightseeing", and at Nishapur they parted (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 217, folio 199).

At Multan, Sharib writes, "آپ نے ملتان پہنچ کر مستقل سکونت اختیار فرمائی", he took up permanent residence (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 51, folio 47). Bhandari says that he came from Baghdad to Multan and settled there, and that many became his disciples (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528). The dervishes already in the city, by Sharib's account, did not want him there, and the story of how he answered them is told below (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 51, folio 47).

## Sultans and the Office of Shaikh al-Islam

The *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, citing Khaliq Ahmad Nizami's *Salatin-e Dehli ke Mazhabi Rujhanat*, records that "حضرت کے سلطان شمس الدین التمش کے ساتھ اچھے تعلقات تھے۔ سلطان قباچہ کے مقابلہ میں آپ نے التمش کی حمایت کی تھی اور التمش کی درخواست پر شیخ الاسلام کا عہدہ بھی قبول کر لیا تھا": he was on good terms with Sultan Shams al-Din Iltutmish, supported him against Sultan Qabacha, and at Iltutmish's request accepted the office of Shaikh al-Islam (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 159, folio 147). Sharib gives the office a headed section of its own, "عہدہ", and tells how it came to him: Sultan Shams al-Din Altamsh removed Najm al-Din Sughra[OCR?] from the office of Shaikh al-Islam as the punishment for a false slander, and "نجم الدین صغریٰ کے بجائے آپ شیخ الاسلام مقرر ہوئے", in Najm al-Din's place Bahauddin was appointed Shaikh al-Islam (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 51–52, folios 47–48, حاشیہ 14).

Other writers make the court part of his character. Haroon Khalid writes that he "rose to importance because of his association with the rulers in Delhi" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 282, folio 256). Schimmel writes that "he was willing to mix freely with members of the ruling classes", just as Abu Hafs Umar Suhrawardi "himself had served the caliph an-Nasir" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 383, folio 352). Of the Suhrawardis in general Rizvi writes: "They accepted state patronage, received huge gifts from merchants and artisan guilds, and treasured riches as future assets" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 236, folio 216).

At Delhi, Sharib and Rizvi both relate, he sat in judgement at a trial. Sharib tells it at length. When the complaint against Shaikh Jalal reached Sultan Iltutmish, "جب سلطان التمش کے پاس شکایت پہنچی تو اس نے تمام مشاہیر اور مشائخ کو دہلی بلایا۔ دو سو سے زیادہ اولیائے کرام دہلی آئے۔ جمعہ کی نماز کے بعد سب مسجد منار میں جمع ہوئے": more than two hundred *awliya* came to Delhi, and after the Friday prayer all assembled in the Masjid Minar. The Sultan let the accuser name the arbitrator, the ثالث, and the accuser chose Bahauddin, counting on the old estrangement: "ان کو خیال تھا کہ حضرت بہاء الدین زکریا رحمۃ اللہ علیہ کی آپ سے نیشاپور میں کشیدگی ہو گئی تھی" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 36, folio 32, حاشیہ 9; p. 36, folio 32). When the accused entered, "سب آپ کی تعظیم کے لئے کھڑے ہو گئے", and Bahauddin lifted the accused saint's shoes in his own hands and declared "میرے اوپر واجب ہے کہ شیخ جلال کی خاک پا کو اپنی آنکھوں کا سرمہ بناؤں' کیوں کہ وہ سات برس تک سفر و خضر[OCR?] میں میرے پیر و مرشد کی خدمت میں مقیم رہے ہیں", it is obligatory on me to make the dust of Shaikh Jalal's feet the kohl of my eyes, because for seven years, in travel and in residence, he stayed in the service of my pir and murshid; the Sultan said "جب ثالث نے اتنی عزت کی تو پھر معاملہ طے ہو گیا", when the arbitrator has shown such honour the matter is decided; and Bahauddin then examined the woman, who confessed the whole plot (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 36–37, folios 32–33, حاشیہ 9).

Rizvi tells the same story. Bahauddin was made chairman of the *mahzar*, he writes, because of "the rivalry between Shaikh Jalalu'd-Din and Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya"; yet he "ran to receive him and carry his shoes", saying that Jalalu'd-Din "had served his pir for seven years and therefore it was fitting for him to use the dust from Shaikh Jalalu'd-Din's feet as an eye-wash" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 218, folio 200). Rizvi glosses the *mahzar* as "An assembly of 'ulama' and sufis to decide an important dispute; literally the word means either a royal court or any document attested by witnesses" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 146, folio 128, footnote 4). He gives it no date.

## Wealth and Its Critics

Unlike the Chishtis, who embraced absolute poverty and kept their distance from the courts, Zakariya represented the Suhrawardi temper: he accepted endowments and gifts, managed considerable resources, engaged openly with rulers, and used his wealth in charity and hospitality on a grand scale. This difference of method — the two poles of early Indian Sufism — is often illustrated by his cordial contrast with his friend Baba Farid.

The books are plain about his riches. Lahori writes that "دولت ظاہری و باطنی ان کے نصیب ہوئی", outward and inward wealth was his portion (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 160, folio 148). Rizvi speaks of him, in his own voice, as "himself extremely rich" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 146, folio 128, footnote 6), and as "mystically prompted by the teachings of his pir, who also had vast wealth" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 147, folio 129). Schimmel calls him "the well-to-do landlord", "who looked after the needs of his family and never failed to keep a supply of grain in his house", and adds that "His accumulation of wealth was sufficient to make him the target of accusations by other Sufis" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 383, folio 352).

The question was put to him in writing by Hamid ad-Din Suwali Nagauri. Carl Ernst and Bruce Lawrence write of him: "He had extensive correspondence with Shaykh Baha ad-Din Zakariyya on the question of poverty and wealth." (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 184, folio 172). Hamid ad-Din's first letter sets out the question, that by the teaching of the Shaikhs and of the Quran and hadith "the property of this world is supposed to be an obstacle to the attainment of union with God. Yet it has been heard that some of the Shaykhs of our time enjoy a handsome share of it, and at the same time perform miracles." The letter is addressed to Bahauddin, and the wealthy Shaikhs are left unnamed. The second records that he did not answer, or did not satisfy: "This humble creature has sent a letter to Shaykh Baha ad-Din and requested him to resolve certain difficulties. For whatever reason your honor did not reply, or if you did reply, what you said was not satisfactory." The answer, the letter goes on, came instead from God (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 184, folio 172).

Rizvi tells the same quarrel from the other side. "In order to ascertain the attitude of the Suhrawardi order towards wealth, Shaikh Hamidu'd-Din wrote a number of letters to Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya… Although the latter replied, Shaikh Hamidu'd-Din was not completely satisfied with the answers received", and the two met at Delhi during the *mahzar* held on the charges against Jalal al-Din Tabrizi (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 146, folio 128). Rizvi gives Bahauddin's defence in his own reported words: "Although wealth is a serpent, someone who has learnt the incantation to overcome the venom, need not have any fear from the serpent"; and, when pressed, "Your achievement as a dervish is not endowed by such beauty and elegance as to be immune from the evil eye, but our achievements as dervishes are endowed with such great beauty as to gain no harm even if the whole of the world's blackness were applied to its face" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 146–147, folios 128–129). Rizvi, following the *Sururu's-Sudur*, twice records that he lost the exchange: "Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din was speechless", and "Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya Suhrawardi was unable to reply" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 147, folio 129). The quarrel outlived the letters. "Controversy over the acquisition of material wealth by members of the Suhrawardi order annoyed one of Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din's sons, who visited Ajmer", Rizvi writes; finding that Hamid ad-Din did not attend the Friday congregational prayers, the son used this as a pretext to attack him, and "gained some support from a group of 'ulama' who demanded that the Shaikh fulfil his religious duties" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 147, folio 129). The son is not named.

Sharib's chapter carries the saint's own sayings on wealth. The chapter breaks off just after announcing the saint's verse, and the three pages that follow, which carry that verse, these sayings and several of the stories told below, are taken to be its close from their contents: the verse is signed with the name بہاء الدین ملتانی and the stories are set in Multan, though the printed text does not say on any one of these pages whose chapter it is (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 52, folio 48; p. 53, folio 49). In them he says that "مال دنیا کتنا ہی کیوں نہ ہو' تاہم قلیل ہے اور سانپ کی صحبت اس شخص کو نقصان پہنچاتی ہے' جو افسوں اس کا نہ جانتا ہو' میرے نزدیک مال دنیا کی کوئی حقیقت نہیں ہے", however much worldly wealth there is, it is still little, and the company of a snake harms the one who does not know its charm; in my view worldly wealth has no reality; and that "فقیروں کے نزدیک عدم اور وجود مال دنیا کا یکساں ہے' نہ جانے کا غم' نہ آنے کی خوشی", for faqirs the absence and the presence of worldly wealth are the same, no grief at its going, no joy at its coming (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 53, folio 49).

## The Khanqah at Multan

Lahori draws the scale of the household. "ملتان میں آکر شاہان وقت اور خاص و عام حضرت کی بیعت میں داخل ہوئے اور دولت ظاہری و باطنی ان کے نصیب ہوئی۔ ہزاروں خادم حضرت کی بارگاہ فلک پایگاہ پر حاضر رہتے": at Multan the kings of the time and high and low alike entered his *bai'at*, and thousands of attendants were present at his sky-high court; and "لاکھوں آدمی حضرت سے مستفید طریقہ سہروردیہ ہوئے", lakhs benefited from the Suhrawardi way through him (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 160, folio 148).

Sharib describes the life of the house. "آپ کے یہاں زراعت اور تجارت بڑے پیمانے پر ہوتی تھی' ہر حال میں خدا کا شکر ادا کرتے تھے' آپ کا لنگر عام تھا' آپ بہت مخیر تھے' دنیا سے بے پروا تھے": at his place agriculture and trade were carried on on a large scale; he gave thanks in every condition; his *langar* was open to all; he was very charitable and unconcerned with the world. He lived with simplicity and contentment, ate very little but strong food, and greatly honoured the *ulama*, the great *mashaikh* and his guests (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 52, folio 48, حاشیہ 17). He completed one reading of the Quran every night, and "تحمل و بردباری' ذوق و شوق' بے ہوشی اور استغراق آپ کی نمایاں خصوصیات تھیں" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 52, folio 48). He fasted much in early life, but "آخری عمر میں روزانہ روزہ نہ رکھتے تھے", in his last years he did not fast daily (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 52, folio 48, حاشیہ 17).

Rizvi's khanqah is a more guarded place. The food in his khanqah, he writes, "was abundant, but was offered to only a select few. Often the Shaikh ordered his servants to pay fixed amounts in charity to those he considered deserving and only the sum he specified was paid" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 236, folio 216). Yet "Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya was a lively host and followed literally the Qur'anic injunction: 'eat of what is pure and act righteously.'" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 237, folio 217). Schimmel writes that "As opposed to the open table in the poor Chishti khanqahs," he "was more formal and had fixed hours for visitors who were invited to partake in meals" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 383, folio 352).

Rizvi describes the khanqah's economy: "The prosperity of Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya's khanqah depended mainly on gifts from merchants and trade guilds, for even local governors were often short of funds. As many merchants traded outside India, to a large extent, they were independent of the government and could not be prevented from giving futuh" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 242, folio 222).

The house had its rules. "Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya did not encourage his disciples to perform sajda. They greeted him with: as-salam alaikum (Peace be with you). He also expected his disciples to finish their obligatory religious duties first and to greet him afterwards" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 240, folio 220). "Although Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya had insisted during his lifetime that sufis join one order only, from the fourteenth century sufis were often initiated into both the Chishtiyya and the Suhrawardiyya" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 292, folio 272). Rizvi, citing no source, doubts that people came to it for cures: "Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya was concerned only with the élite and it is unlikely that people visited his" khanqah "for faith cures. By contrast, the Chishti jama'at-khanas were filled with people wanting amulets" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 240, folio 220).

In Rizvi's account wandering dervishes were not welcome. "Qalandars were intolerable to Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 236, folio 216). "Mongol domination of Central Asia and Iran facilitated the movement of the Qalandariyyas and the Jawaliqi groups from Turkey and Egypt to India. Passing through Multan, they often came into conflict with Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya", Rizvi writes, "but such experiences were counteracted by the generosity of Baba Farid" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 325, folio 303). He states the dislike absolutely, "Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din disliked qalandars intensely and certainly never encouraged their unwelcome visits", and records two exceptions in the same place: Fakhr-ud-Din Iraqi, who "even after his initiation by Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya he continued to live as a wandering dervish", and Lal Shahbaz, whom he adopted as his disciple and named (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 328, folio 306).

Accounts of music in his house pull two ways. Sharib writes that "آپ کو سماع کا شوق تھا", he was fond of *sama'* (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 52, folio 48). Rizvi writes that "Baha'u'd-Din was opposed to poetry and music, but he tolerated its indulgence by Fakhru'd-Din Ibrahim" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 224, folio 204). Ernst and Lawrence report a singer's memory of his gatherings: "One time a qawwal, Abu Bakr by name, came from Multan to visit Nizam ad-Din's teacher." He told how he had been reciting in *sama'* before Shaikh Baha ad-Din and reached the line "The snake of desire has stung my heart." "I could not recall the second half of the couplet, but the Shaykh did." (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 171, folio 159). Ernst and Lawrence give this Shaikh Baha ad-Din no place or dates of his own; that he is the saint of Multan rests on his name and on the singer's coming from Multan. The qawwal went on: "And then he began to extol the virtues of Shaykh Baha ad-Din, saying that in his hospice devotion to God was such that even the slave girls recited the names of God while grinding grain. In this fashion he rambled on, but his words did not make any lasting impression on the heart of the young Nizam ad-Din" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 171, folio 159). Rizvi has the same memory of a khanqah "where even his slave girls performed constant zikr", without saying there where the khanqah stood (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 173, folio 155). Shemeem Burney Abbas, citing M. Wahid Mirza's *Life and Works of Amir Khusrau*, which rests in turn on Firishta, names another singer: "On authority from Firishta he mentions one qawwāl named Abdullah, who came to that city from Turkey (Rum) and enlivened the assemblies of Shaikh Bahauddin Zakariya, the father of Shaikh Sadruddin of Multan" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 193, folio not stated).

## His Death

He died at Multan in 1267, full of years and honour.

The books give the year in many ways. Lahori's text gives "سال چھ سو چھیاسٹھ", the year 666 AH, gives ۶۶۶ھ twice by chronogram, and adds "اور ملتان میں مدفون ہوئے", and he was buried at Multan (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 160–161, folios 148–149). His editor rules otherwise: "حضرت شیخ بہاء الدین زکریا ملتانیؒ نے ۶۶۱ھ میں وصال فرمایا۔ سال وفات میں اختلاف ہے۔", he died in 661 AH, and the sources differ on the year, the *Lataif-e Ashrafi* giving 666, the *Akhbar al-Akhyar* 661 and the *Gulzar-e Abrar* 666[OCR?]; and he concludes "لیکن ان میں ہمارے نزدیک صحیح ۶۶۱ھ ہے", but in our view 661 AH is correct (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 159, folio 147, حاشیہ 1). The publisher's catalogue in the same volume prints his dates as "۵۶۶۔ ۶۶۱", with no era marker (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 353).

Sharib gives the day: "آپ 7 صفر 666ھ کو بروز جمعرات جوار رحمت میں داخل ہوئے", he died on Thursday, 7 Safar 666 AH (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 52, folio 48, حاشیہ 15), and "آپ کی عمر (100) سو سال کی تھی", his age was a hundred years (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 52, folio 48). Bhandari has the same day of the month and another year, "هفتم ماه صفر سال ششصد و شصت هجری", 7 Safar 660 AH, and says that he died on reading a sealed letter (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528). Rizvi writes that "Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din died on 7 Safar, 661/21 December, 1262" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 212, folio 194), says again that he "died in December 1262" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 226, folio 206), and prints "d. 1262" in his index (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 461, folio 437). Eaton gives 1263 (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 227), and Schimmel's *Pain and Grace* "(d. c. 1265)" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 8, folio 4). Her *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* gives "d. ca. 1262" in its text (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 383, folio 352) and "d. ca. 1267" in its index (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 512, folio 481), while her *As Through a Veil* gives 1267 three times (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 104, folio 94; p. 286, folio 268; p. 351, folio 333). Michel Boivin and Omar Kasmani also print "(d. 1267)" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 187, folio 174; Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 188, folio 171, ch. 1 n. 17). Khalid writes only that "Bahaudin too belonged to the thirteenth century" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 286, folio 260).

## Writings and Sayings

One book of his survives, the *Kitab al-Awrad*, the editor of the *Hadiqat al-Awliya* writes, commented on by Maulana Ali bin Ahmad Ghori, a murid of Shah Rukn-e-Alam, as the *Kanz al-Ibad fi Sharh al-Awrad*; a copy written at Samarqand in 854 AH, in the hand of Shams al-Din Ahmad bin Maulana Sadr al-Din, is in his own library. That copy, he argues, settles a question: "ڈاکٹر مولوی محمد شفیع کے اس شبہہ کی تردید ہوتی ہے کہ یہ اوراد شیخ شہاب الدین سہروردی کے ہیں", Dr Maulvi Muhammad Shafi's doubt, that these *awrad* are Shihab-ud-Din Suhrawardi's, is refuted, for the commentator has "صاف طور پر بہاء الحق والشرع والدین لکھا ہے" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 158–159, folios 146–147, حاشیہ 1).

His sayings were gathered too. The same editor names the *Khulasat al-Arifin*, of which an Urdu translation was printed at Lahore in 1909, and an unknown, unpublished *Malfuzat* once in the library of the late Maulvi Muhammad Shams al-Din, which "ملک لطیف مرحوم نے رسالہ صحیفہ لاہور ۱۹۷۰ء [OCR?] میں شائع کر دیا تھا", the late Malik Latif published in *Sahifa*, Lahore, in a year printed 1970 whose reading is uncertain in the printed text, the copy being now in Malik Sahib's library (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 159, folio 147, حاشیہ 1). The edition's own book list dates the same article differently: "لطیف ملک : ملفوظات حضرت بہاء الدین زکریا ملتانی صحیفہ لاہور جنوری ۱۹۷۱ء", January 1971 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 305, folio 293). The list also records a manuscript of the *Khulasat al-Arifin* copied in 1033 AH and privately held at Lahore (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 293, folio 281), and the Samarqand copy of the *Kanz al-Ibad*, "مکتوبہ در سمرقند ۸۵۴ھ مملوکہ محمد اقبال مجددی" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 294, folio 282).

The literature on him is gathered in the same pages. The editor's note on Lahori's notice lists among its authorities Dara Shukoh's *Safinat al-Auliya*, the *Fawaid al-Fawad*, the *Akhbar al-Akhyar*, the *Lataif-e Ashrafi*, Sharaf al-Din Qureshi's *Jami al-Karamat* in Sayyid Farzand Ali's Urdu translation (Bahawalpur, 1915), Abd al-Qadir Thatthvi's *Hadiqat al-Auliya* of 1016 AH as edited by Sayyid Husam al-Din Rashidi (Sindhi Adabi Board, Hyderabad Sindh, 1967), Shamim Zaidi's *Ahwal wa Athar*, printed at Rawalpindi, and Makhdum Muhammad Hashim Thatthvi's *Bayaz*, a manuscript in the Ganj Bakhsh library of the Markaz-e Tahqiqat-e Farsi Iran wa Pakistan at Rawalpindi (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 159–160, folios 147–148). The book list adds Nur Ahmad Khan Faridi's "تذکرہ حضرت بہاء الدین زکریا ملتانیؒ۔ ملتان ۱۹۵۴ء" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 304, folio 292) and gives Shamim Zaidi's *Ahwal wa Athar* the imprint Rawalpindi, 1975 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 298, folio 286); the publisher's catalogue in the same volume gives the book "احوال و آثار حضرت بہاء الدین زکریا ملتانی" to another author, "حمید اللہ شاہ ہاشمی" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 353). The editor also owns a rare manuscript of the *Tuhfat al-Muslimin* of Maulana Muhammad Aqil Lahori, "جس میں انہوں نے شیخ حامد سے لے کر حضرت شیخ بہاؤالدین زکریا ملتانی تک پنجاب کے اکابر علما کے حالات بھی لکھے ہیں", in which the lives of the great scholars of the Punjab are written, from Shaikh Hamid back to Bahauddin Zakariya (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 278, folio 266, حاشیہ 2).

Among the sayings Sharib records, in the same closing pages of his chapter, is a definition of renunciation: "زہد تین چیزیں ہیں' جس میں یہ نہیں وہ زاہد نہیں", *zuhd* is three things, and whoever lacks them is no ascetic: to know the truth of the world and let it go; to serve the Lord and keep to his *adab*; and to long for and seek the hereafter. Safety, he adds, lies in little food, the soul's safety in leaving people, and the faith's safety in the prayer of the Best of Creation (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 53, folio 49).

## The Suhrawardi Order in India

Bahauddin Zakariya’s lasting achievement was to root the Suhrawardi way in the Indian subcontinent. From his Multan khanqah the order spread through Sindh and the Punjab, carried by his disciples and descendants — among them the poet-saint Fakhr-ud-Din Iraqi, who came to Multan and married into his family, and, in the next generations, his own grandson Shah Rukn-e-Alam. The Suhrawardi order he founded was marked by strict adherence to the Sharia, sober devotion, learning, and an active engagement with society and state — a pattern that made Multan a hub of both spiritual authority and worldly influence, and that shaped the religious life of the western Punjab and Sindh for centuries.

The books measure that reach. In Schimmel's words, "An even greater Suhrawardiyya impact on Muslim religious life was made by" Bahauddin Zakariya Multani (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 383, folio 352), and in another book she describes the order he led: "The Suhrawardiyya, not as austere in their practices as the early Chishtiyya", sent their members throughout the country and "gained disciples even in Eastern Bengal" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 8, folio 4). Rafat, introducing Bulleh Shah, sets the order's character against another's: "The Suhrawardiya tradition of patronage is diametrically opposed to that of the Qadiriya who practise asceticism and are given to assimilation of local cults." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 39, folio 19). Eaton writes that of the Jat and Rajput groups that became Muslim in the medieval period, "the vast majority of them claim to have been converted either by Baba Farid or by his contemporary, Baha' al-Haqq Zakariya (d. 1263), whose tomb is in Multan city" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 227).

Spiritual chains ran back to him. In the *Hadiqat al-Awliya* he stands at the head of the chain of Shah Jamal and of that of Shah Daula, of eight and thirteen steps (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 185; p. 184), of Mian Wada's silsila of thirteen steps (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 188), and of a Suhrawardi chain of nine steps from the Lahore scholar Shaikh Hamid Qari Suhrawardi, which ends "حسام الدین متقی … شیخ صدرالدین … شیخ بہاؤالدین بن زکریا ملتانی" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 278, folio 266). Chishti makes him the pir of "حضرت صدر الدین عارف" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 399), and the source of the "نعمت ولایت" of Sakhi Sarwar (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 267).

Sharib lists his khalifas. Besides his son, he names five "مقتدر خلفاء", eminent khalifas who were not his sons: "حضرت سید جلال الدین سرخ بخاری", Hazrat Sayyid Jalal al-Din Surkh Bukhari; "حضرت سید عثمان معروف بہ لعل شہباز سندی[OCR?]", Hazrat Sayyid Usman known as Lal Shahbaz; "حضرت شیخ حسن افغان", Hazrat Shaikh Hasan Afghan; "حضرت شیخ فخر الدین ابراہیم عراقی", Hazrat Shaikh Fakhr al-Din Ibrahim Iraqi; and "حضرت سید صدر الدین احمد معروف بہ سید حسین", Hazrat Sayyid Sadr al-Din Ahmad known as Sayyid Husain. He gives none of them a place, a date or a shrine (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 52, folio 48, حاشیہ 16).

Sharib writes that the first of them, Jalal al-Din Surkh Bukhari, "ملتان آئے۔ حضرت بہاء الدین زکریا رحمۃ اللہ علیہ کے مرید ہوئے", came to Multan and became his murid, and that he "ان ہی سے خرقہ خلافت پایا' اپنے پیرو مرشد کے حکم سے اوچہ میں سکونت اختیار کی", received the *khirqa* of *khilafat* from him and took up residence at Uch by his pir's order (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 133, folio 129). Rizvi: "Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya had many famous disciples, one was Saiyid Jalalu'd-Din Bukhari, also called Jalal Surkh (Red)" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 223, folio 203), and he names Bahauddin again as the pir behind the Uch line, in the clause "a disciple of Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 299, folio 277). Schimmel: "One of Zakariya's disciples, Sayyid Jalaluddin Surkhpush", settled in Uch (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354). His robe passed down another family: Makhdum Jahaniyan "حضرت شیخ بہاء الدین زکریا رحمۃ اللہ علیہ کا خرقہ اپنے والد سید احمد کبیر سے پہنا", wore Bahauddin Zakariya's *khirqa*, received from his own father Sayyid Ahmad Kabir (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 135, folio 131). Robert Rozehnal, writing of the family of Zauqi Shah, records that their patriarch Sayyid Jalal Gul Surkh, coming from Bukhara, stayed "for some time in the hospice (khanaqah) of the well-known Suhrawardi saint of Uch Sharif, Baha ad-Din Zakaria", and that "A close affiliation with the Suhrawardi order was a hallmark of the family through the generations" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 57, folio 47).

Of Hasan Afghan Rizvi writes: "One of Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din's disciples, Hasan Afghan, was illiterate. The Shaikh was proud of Hasan Afghan and used to say that if God asked him what he had brought from the world, he would present Hasan as a gift" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 223, folio 203). Of Lal Shahbaz, Kasmani writes that "According to the Suhrawardi Sufi tradition, the saint was initiated in Multan by" Bahauddin Zakariya (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 184, folio 167, intro n. 34); Rizvi, as above, has Bahauddin adopt him as his disciple and name him (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 328, folio 306); but Khalid writes that "Both Bahaudin and Farid would have had serious differences with Lal Shahbaz Qalandar of Sehwan Sharif" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 282, folio 256).

His name reaches into Sindh and into devotions beyond Sufism. Boivin writes of Pithoro Pir that "they recognize that Pithoro Pir was a Muslim Sufi initiated by Bahauddin Zakariyya", and that "a tazkira refers to him as a disciple of the same Bahauddin Zakariyya already mentioned", of Udero Lal (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 51, folio 38). Of Pithoro Pir he writes elsewhere: "Thus, nobody challenges the fact that the saint was a Sufi." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 178, folio 165). But he hedges it in his summing-up: "there is only one concrete link with Sufism: Pithoro Pir would have been initiated to Sufism by Baha al-Din Zakariyya. Such an element is incorporated into an oral tradition, and it can be related to a kind of legitimization of Pithoro's charisma." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 192, folio 179).

Some disciples are disputed. Bhandari writes that "شیخ عراقی و میر حسینی از و فیض برگرفتند", naming Shaikh Iraqi and Mir Husaini as his students (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528). Rizvi notes that "Jamali describes him in connection with the disciples of Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya", and that "Modern scholars also describe him as Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din's disciple", of an Amir Husain; but he argues on chronology that Husain "must have been born about 671/1272-73, while Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya died in December 1262, ten years earlier" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 226, folio 206). In the *Kanzu'r-Rumuz*, Rizvi adds, "Husaini praised the Shaikhs, Shihabu'd-Din Suhrawardi, Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya and Sadru'd-Din" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 226, folio 206). The books do not say whether Bhandari's میر حسینی is Rizvi's Amir Husain.

## Fakhr-ud-Din Iraqi

"The main reason for Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya's fame in the scholarly world, however," Rizvi writes, "was due to the achievements of his disciple, Shaikh Fakhru'd-Din Ibrahim, popularly known as 'Iraqi" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 224, folio 204). Iraqi came with a party of wandering qalandars, who "reached Multan where they stayed with Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din"; when the party broke up in a storm, Iraqi returned to the Shaikh's khanqah and "began living in a cell which the Shaikh had assigned him, as a spiritual retreat" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 224, folio 204). Schimmel's *Pain and Grace* says that Iraqi, "one of the leading Persian mystical poets", "stayed with him for 25 years" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 8, folio 4); her *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, that the poet "lived in his entourage for nearly twenty-five years", attached himself to him at Multan and dedicated to him 'some impressive' *qasidas* (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 383, folio 352).

Both writers tell of a quarrel over the disciple's poetry. Rizvi writes that Bahauddin's other disciples "were shocked to learn that this ghazal was being sung in local taverns to the accompaniment of the harp and zither. They complained to the Shaikh", and he ruled for Iraqi (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 224, folio 204). Schimmel tells the story in the words of Arberry's translation, introducing it with 'The story goes': Iraqi sat ten days admitting nobody and sang his verse on the eleventh; the brothers complained, the story explaining that their order followed the rule of Shihab-ud-Din Suhrawardi, whose favoured pupil Bahauddin was, and that "Suhrawardi's rule was that the devotee should occupy himself only with the recitation of the Koran and the expounding of tradition"; the Shaikh answered "that this was prohibited to them, but not to him"; and on hearing the poem sung in the bazaar and the taverns he declared "His affair is complete," and invested him with "the mystic robe" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, pp. 383–384, folios 352–353). Schimmel's own verdict is: "Baha'uddin, with the spiritual insight of a true saint, acknowledged Iraqi's greatness and true love." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 384, folio 353).

Rizvi sets out the investiture in order. Bahauddin "declared 'Iraqi's training to be complete. He ordered him to give up retirement and meditation, presented him with his own" *khirqa* "to wear and later gave him his daughter in marriage. Before he died, Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din appointed him his khalifa" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 224–225, folios 204–205). Then, Rizvi writes, "after his death, the jealousy of other disciples forced 'Iraqi to leave Multan about 1263. It would appear he had been there since about 1239" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 225, folio 205). Schimmel writes that soon after Bahauddin's death Iraqi left Multan for Konya, "where he met Sadruddin Qonawi and perhaps Jalaluddin Rumi" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354), and in *As Through a Veil* that he "lived for some years in Konya after his master, Baha'uddin Zakariya of Multan, had died in 1267" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 104, folio 94).

## Baba Farid and Other Contemporaries

Of Baba Farid, Bhandari writes "با شیخ فرید الدین گنجشکر فراوان دوستی داشت و مدتی باهم بودند" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528). Sharib counts him among the *mashaikh* whose company Farid profited from (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 59, folio 55, note 13), and preserves an exchange between them. Bahauddin wrote "میان ما و شما عشق بازی است", between us and you is love-play, and Farid replied "میان ما و شما عشق ہست و بازی نیست", between us and you is love, and no play (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 63–64, folios 59–60, note 20). Schimmel calls him "a contemporary" of Farid, says that the two men "were separated by only a few hundred miles", and sets them against each other as two styles of life (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 383, folio 352). She also names him with Muin al-Din Chishti, in "the same period during which" the two "were preaching divine love and union" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354), and names him with Farid in a list of five saints who "tell of the spiritual glory of the thirteenth century" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, pp. 310–311, folios 279–280).

Their spheres had a frontier. Eaton quotes Farid telling a traveller: "from here [Ajudhan] to such-and-such a village is in my charge, and from such-and-such a reservoir is the frontier of Shaikh Baha' ad-Din Zakariya [beyond which] is in his charge"; his source is the *Siyar al-arifin*, which Eaton calls "an early-sixteenth-century hagiography" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 222, n. 26). Rizvi has the same line drawn at a water tank, beyond which "began the area of Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din whose prayers he should attain" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 211, folio 193). He is a witness even in a Pakpattan text about Farid: Ernst and Lawrence describe the *Chishtiyya-i bihishtiyya* as "On Farid's postmortem miracles, khalifas, and testimony of Baha' al-Din Zakariyya." (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 236, folio 226).

The Chishti Khwaja Qutb al-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki, leaving Baghdad, "travelled through Khurasan to reach Multan. There he established a friendship with Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 153, folio 135). Bhandari, too, has him come from Baghdad to Multan, where "بملتان وارد شده شیخ بهار الدین ذکریا را دریافت", he met Bahauddin, before going on to Delhi in Iltutmish's reign (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 556).

Rizvi writes that "Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya, not well-known for his praise of other sufis, was a great admirer of Shaikh Ahmad", the weaver Shaikh Ahmad Naharwani (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 216, folio 198). Sharib quotes him on an Ahmad: "اگر احمد کی مشغولی وزن کی جائے تو دس صوفیوں کی مشغولی کے برابر ہو گی", if Ahmad's absorption were weighed it would equal that of ten Sufis (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 46, folio 42).

## Family and Descendants

Sharib writes that "آپ کے ساتھ [OCR?] لڑکے تھے", he had sons, the number lost in the printed text, and names seven: "شیخ صدر الدین عارف، شیخ برہان الدین، شیخ ضیاء الدین، شیخ علاء الدین، شیخ شہاب الدین، شیخ قدوۃ الدین، شیخ شمس الدین" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 52, folio 48). Rizvi writes that "Shaikh Sadru'd-Din had six brothers" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 220, folio 202), and Lahori's notice records that he had seven sons and that his estate was divided (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 158–161). Lahori elsewhere names شیخ صدر الدین عارف as his son (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 252, folio 240), and Abbas calls him "the father of Shaikh Sadruddin of Multan" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 193, folio not stated).

His son Sadr al-Din Arif succeeded him. "آپ کے لڑکے حضرت شیخ صدر الدین عارف آپ کے خلیفہ اور سجادہ نشین تھے", Sharib writes, his son was his khalifa and *sajjada nashin*, and lists the five eminent khalifas apart (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 52, folio 48, حاشیہ 16). He says it again at the head of the son's own chapter, where Sadr al-Din is "اپنے والد ماجد حضرت بہاء الدین زکریا ملتانی رحمۃ اللہ علیہ کے فرزند' مرید' خلیفہ اور جانشین", his father's son, murid, khalifa and successor, who "اپنے والد … سے بیعت ہوئے اور انہیں سے خرقہ خلافت پایا", took *bai'at* from his father and received from him the *khirqa* of *khilafat* (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 74, folio 70). Rizvi: "He appointed his own son, Sadru'd-Din, known as 'Arif (Gnostic), his khalifa" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 220, folio 202). Bhandari names the son "شیخ صدر الدین عارف" and gives his death "درسنه هفتصد و نه هجری", in 709 AH (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528). Schimmel writes that "his sons, unlike the sons of most of the early Chishti saints, followed in his path; the succession in the Suhrawardiyya became, generally, hereditary." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 383, folio 352).

The inheritance is told in two ways. In Sharib's account, "والد کی وفات کے بعد (70) ستر لاکھ ٹنکہ [OCR?] آپ کے حصہ میں آیا جو آپ نے فقراء کو تقسیم کر دیا", after his father's death seventy lakh *tanka*, the unit's reading uncertain in the printed text, came to Sadr al-Din's share, and he distributed it to the *fuqara* (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 74, folio 70). Sharib prints the heir's reasoning, which begins "میرے والد دنیا پر غالب تھے' اس لئے مال و زر کی موجودگی ان کو کوئی نقصان نہ پہنچا سکتی تھی" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 74–75, folios 70–71, حاشیہ 2). Rizvi writes that Sadr al-Din "alone inherited from his father property worth 700,000 tankas. According to Jamali, he immediately gave the entire amount to the poor, for like his father he believed he was unable to spend it judiciously", and concludes that "it seems clear that wealth accumulated by Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din did not long remain with his sons" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 220, folio 202).

His son's wife was a disciple of his in her own right. The *Hadiqat al-Awliya* writes of her as "زوجہ شیخ صدر الدین عارف بن شیخ بہاؤ الدین زکریا ملتانی", who "نسبت ارادت اپنے خسر بہاؤ الدین زکریا کے ساتھ رکھتی تھی", held her bond of discipleship to her father-in-law; through her, "بہت سی عورات کو فیض نسبت سہروردیہ اس کی ذات با برکات سے پہنچا", the Suhrawardi *nisbat* reached many women (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 252, folio 240). She died, the book says, in "سنہ چھ سو پچانوے ہجری", 695 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 252). Sharib names her Bibi Rasti and calls him her pir and her father-in-law (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 113, folio 109).

Sharib tells how he foresaw his grandson, rising for his pregnant daughter-in-law and saying "یہ تعظیم اس بچے کی ہے۔ جو تیرے پیٹ میں ہے۔ وہ چراغ خاندان و شمع دو دمان کا ہو گا" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 113, folio 109), and that he supervised Rukn al-Din's "تعلیم و تربیت", his education and upbringing (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 114, folio 110). Rizvi too writes that he "prophesied his prominence while he was still in his mother's womb" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 230, folio 210). Both tell of a turban. Rizvi: "at the age of four, Ruknu'd-Din donned Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din's turban, and although his father objected strongly, Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din approved of the child's action" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 231, folio 211); Sharib gives his words, "بابا صدر الدین! تم اس کو منع نہ کرو" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 114, folio 110). The turban became a mark of succession: "After Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din succeeded his father, he wore the same turban" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 231, folio 211). Boivin calls Shah Rukn-e-Alam "the grandson and successor of Baha al-Din Zakariyya" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 187, folio 174).

His descendants appear across the later history of Multan and Lahore. Bhandari tells how the people of Multan, their province left without a ruler, raised to rule "شیخ یوسف قریشی را که از اولاد شناور دریاۓ حقیقت عارف معارف طریقت سر حلقه اولیاۓ مخدوم العالم شیخ بهاؤالدین ذکریا قدس الله سره بود", Shaikh Yusuf Qureshi, of his descendants (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 297). Shaikh Yusuf struck coin and read the *khutba* in his own name "در ۸۵۵ [OCR?]" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 297), reigned "دو سال" before Rai Sanhra[OCR?] of the Langahs seized him, and escaped to Sultan Bahlul Lodi at Delhi, who married his own daughter to "شیخ عبد الله خلف شیخ مذکور"; Bahlul's army was then sent against Multan "بتحریک شیخ یوسف" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 296; p. 297). Bhandari's verdict on his rule is that "درویشی را بجهانبانی همرنگ و ولایت را بسلطنت هم سنگ گردانید و قلوب رعایا و برایا بخود رام ساخت" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 297). He also records that Sultan Firuz Shah, on reaching his capital, gave "شیخ صدرالدین از اولاد شیخ بهاءالدین ذکریا قدس الله سره" the title of Shaikh al-Islam and a place near himself, "و خطاب شیخ الاسلامی داده بر تقرب خویش جا داده" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 344). And within his account of Prince Muhammad Sultan's governorship of Multan he tells of Sadr al-Din's *halala* marriage to a daughter of Sultan Shams al-Din and of his refusal to give her back to the prince (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 385–386).

Rizvi writes that the alleged war-cry of Shaikh Hud was "the ill-treatment of Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya's descendants by the Sultan" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 234, folio 214).

Lahori writes that Shah Rahmatullah Qureshi, "حضرت شیخ بہاء الدین زکریا ملتانی کی اولاد سے ملتان سے لاہور میں آیا", came from Multan to Lahore from among his descendants, and that the family lived in the author's day in Lahore and in mauza Dholanwal on the *nazr* of their murids, one of them, بہادر شاہ, being known to the author personally (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 271, folio 259). Chishti too counts a Shah Rahmat Ullah Qureshi "اولاد حضرت شیخ بہاوالدین ذکریا ملتانی مین" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 329). Sharib says of Shaikh Hamza Dharsawi, "آپ حضرت شیخ بہاء الدین زکریا ملتانی رحمۃ اللہ علیہ کی اولاد ہیں", he is of his descendants (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 234, folio 230).

Lahori himself claims descent from him. His pedigree runs back through Mian Kalan, Shaikh Qutb al-Din and Shaikh Shihab al-Din Anwar to "Shaikh Bahar al-Din Zakariya Multani" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 24, folio 12), but it is printed twice in the book and the two printings differ, at three links and in the number of generations (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 24, folio 12; p. 232, folio 220). The editor disputes one link and gives the book itself as the genealogy's source (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 25, folio 13, حاشیہ 1); the descent is the author's family claim. The book names a Makhdum Mufti Shaikh Muhammad Qureshi, known as Mian Kalan, as "the first elder of this family of Muftis who came from Multan and settled in Lahore" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 27, folio 15), and of Mian Kalan it writes "جو شہر ملتان سے حسب الطلب بادشاہ وقت کے لاہور میں آئے۔ افتار [OCR?] پر ممتاز ہوئے", that he came from Multan to Lahore at the summons of the king of the time and was distinguished in the giving of *fatwas*, and that "ذکر و شغل ان کا طریق آبائی سہروردیہ پر تھا" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 232, folio 220). Whether the two passages mean the same man is not certain. Kanhaiya Lal's history of Lahore makes a like claim, without giving a source, for the family of the Lahore mufti Shaikh Kamal-ud-Din Qureshi: his father, "شیخ مخدوم المشہور پیر امیان[OCR?]", was "محمد تغلق بادشاہ کیوقت ملتان سے بلایا گیا اور عہدہ افتا پر مامور ہوا", summoned from Multan under Muhammad Tughlaq and appointed to the office of *ifta*, and "یہہ بزرگ شیخ بہاوالدین زکریا کی اولاد مین سے تہا" (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 52, folio 55). Shemeem Burney Abbas writes that she had listened to "the narratives of older women in the family" as they spoke of "my maternal family's descent from Hazrat Bahauddin Zakariya" and Rukunuddin (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 25, folio not stated).

## The Shrine

Zakariya’s mausoleum in the old fort area of Multan, built in the thirteenth century, is one of the earliest monuments of Islamic architecture in the subcontinent — a square tomb chamber surmounted by a hemispherical dome, much rebuilt after damage over the centuries (it was harmed, among other times, in the siege of Multan in 1848). It remains a working shrine and the seat of his descendants, the Qureshi *makhdooms* of Multan, who have been among the most prominent religious and political families of the region.

Bhandari places the tomb within the fort: "درون قلعه خوابگاه ... واقع است و برمزار مظهر انوار گنبد بلند از خشت و چونه برافراخته اند", the tomb is inside the fort, under a lofty dome of brick and lime (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528). Others say that he lies at Multan. Lahori writes "اور ملتان میں مدفون ہوئے" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 160–161, folios 148–149); Usborne's annotator explains the 'Zakaria' of Waris Shah's poem as Baha-ud-Din Zakaria of Multan, a famous saint buried in that city (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 11, folio 10); Eaton writes of him as the saint "whose tomb is in Multan city" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 227); Schimmel, that "Multan is the resting place of the great Suhrawardi saint Baha'uddin Zakariya (d. 1267)" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 286, folio 268); and Khalid, that "He had a madrassa in Multan, where he is now buried", teaching "the children of the local Nawab and other important officials. It was an elite school of its era" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 282, folio 256).

The shrine stands beside a Hindu temple. Khalid writes of "the shrine of Bahauddin Zakariya", "one of the most popular Muslim shrines", lying beside the temple of Prahlad Bhagat in Multan (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 123, folio 105). In *A White Trail* he writes that the saint, one of the Muslim mystics drawn to the holy mound, "came and settled next to the temple", and that his shrine "came to be situated next to the temple"; he writes, too, of "the fluidity of the religious traditions that once existed between these two shrines" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 20, folio not stated). Khalid also records a dispute. According to the singer and activist Parvati Devi, whom Khalid quotes, the shrine authorities built "in 2006 and 2008" first "a place for ablutions" and then "a Langar Hall for devotees visiting the shrine" on the premises of the Prahlad Temple, and a stay order of the High Court followed on Usman Chaudary's petition (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 24, folio not stated).

The tomb has had its keepers and its petitioners. Chishti's *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* tells that when the Khan-e Khanan "ملتان مین پہونچا تو حضرت خواجہ بہاوالدین[OCR?] ذکریا ملتانی کی خانقاہ پر حاضر ہوا", he presented himself at the khanqah, and names its *sajjada nashin* in a line of praise, "شیخ کبیر[OCR?] بالا پیر[OCR?] روشن ضمیر پیر سجادہ نشین مزار حضرت مخدوم" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 68). Sharib tells of a scholar who wished for a grave there. Maulana Hussam al-Din, "جب وہ ملتان میں تھے تو ایک دن وہ حضرت بہاء الدین زکریا رحمۃ اللہ علیہ کے مزار سے واپس ہو رہے تھے", was one day returning from the mazar when he conceived the wish "کہ وہ اپنی قبر کے واسطے ایک قطعہ زمین مانگیں", to ask for a plot of ground for his own grave. Sadr al-Din Arif knew it by *kashf* and told him: "مجھے ایک قبر کی جگہ آپ کو دینی مشکل نہیں' اور نہ عذر ہے' لیکن آپ کے واسطے حضرت سرور عالم صلی اللہ علیہ وسلم نے ایک جگہ بدایوں میں تجویز فرمائی ہے' آپ کا مدفن اس جگہ ہوگا", it is not difficult for me to give you a grave-place, nor do I object, but for you the Prophet has chosen a place at Badaun, and there you will be buried (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 76, folio 72).

Rizvi dates the end of the khanqah's prosperity by an event. He writes, of the execution of Shaikh Hud and the seizure of the khanqah's property: "This marked the end of the prosperity of Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya's khanqah in Multan. The Suhrawardi silsila, however, started flourishing in other areas, while Multan became a mere shadow of its former glory" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 235, folio 215; p. 234, folio 214).

None of the books consulted gives the date of the *ʿurs*, the name of the builder of the tomb or the year in which it was built.

## Devotion and Practice

Pilgrims come throughout the year to recite the Fatiha, seek blessing, and honour the saint, and the shrine keeps the round of daily prayer, qawwali, and the free kitchen characteristic of the great darbars, rising to its height at the annual urs.

The poetry of his disciple is still sung there. Schimmel writes that "The tender and intoxicating love poems that the Persian poet composed are still being sung by Pakistani musicians at the door of the master's tomb in Multan." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 384, folio 353). Khalid writes that "Thousands of devotees visit this shrine every day oblivious to the religious syncretism of which this shrine is a product" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 20, folio not stated). His image travels beyond Multan: Khalid saw posters of him sold in the market at the Gilani shrine in Uch Sharif (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 277, folio 251), and on the banner at Jalaludin's shrine there found him "dressed like a rich man, with pearls around his neck and a fancy gown" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 282, folio 256). At Uch he is named as one of three saints said to have meditated in the rooms of a mosque, a story Khalid calls apocryphal (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 283, folio not stated).

The tomb was a station for travelling saints. Sharib writes of Shaikh Jamali that "ملتان میں آپ حضرت بہاء الدین زکریا ملتانی رحمۃ اللہ علیہ کے مزار پر انوار پر حاضر ہوئے اور شیخ صدر الدین سے ملاقات کی", in Multan he presented himself at the luminous mazar of Hazrat Baha al-Din Zakariya Multani and met a Shaikh Sadr al-Din, whom the book does not further identify; Multan is one stop on a journey that ran from the Haramain to Egypt, Baghdad and other lands as far as Khurasan (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 222, folio 218; p. 222, folio 218, حاشیہ 3). In another chapter Sharib records that "ایک سال عید کی رات کو آپ حضرت بہاء الدین زکریا رحمۃ اللہ علیہ کے مزار مبارک پر یاد الٰہی میں مشغول رہے", one year, on the night of Eid, the subject of that chapter spent the night at his mazar in the remembrance of God; the sentence does not name the town (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 133, folio 129).

An emperor came to ask for help. Bhandari records that, with the imperial camp near Multan in the pursuit of Dara Shikoh, the emperor went on "نهم محرم الحرام" to "بزیارت روضه منوره فیض امین[OCR?] عارف صمدانی قطب ربانی شیخ بهاء الدین ذکریا ملتانی قدس الله سره العزیز", and there "استمداد همت و استفاضه انوار توجه نمودند", sought the help of the saint's spiritual power and the outpouring of his lights (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 65). At the visit, "بوقت امدن و رفتن از روشنائی جیب مراد مساکین برآوردند[OCR?]", he seems to have given to the poor as he came and went, though the sense of a phrase in the sentence is not settled (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 65). The sentence gives no year; in the book's order the visit falls after the camp three kos from Multan on "هفتم محرم" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 66) and before the departure from Multan on "دوازدهم محرم" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 65).

Bulleh Shah's lyric imagines a pilgrimage to the tomb, though Shackle doubts the verse is his (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 186, folio 161, n. 5 on 122); the lines are given below.

## Legends and Miracles

Sharib tells how he received his robe in a dream. He saw a house of light with the Prophet present, his pir standing respectfully, and many *khirqas* hanging on a rope; he was summoned, his hand was taken, and the Prophet said "اے عمر! اس خرقہ کو شیخ بہاء الدین غوث العالم کو پہنا دو", O Umar, clothe Shaikh Bahauddin Ghaus al-Alam in this *khirqa*. In the morning his pir called him, and "آپ نے مکان اسی طرح کا پایا' جیسا کہ خواب میں دیکھا تھا", he found the house exactly as in the dream; the same *khirqa* was taken down and put on him by hand, the pir disclaiming any authority of his own: "یہ سب خرقے سرور عالم ﷺ کے ہیں' میں تو درمیان میں صرف ایک واسطہ ہوں' بے اجازت سرور عالم ﷺ کے کسی کو نہیں پہنا سکتا" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 50–51, folios 46–47, حواشی 12–13).

As a schoolboy, Sharib writes, he claimed to remember the primordial covenant: "جس وقت خداوند تعالیٰ نے الست بربکم فرمایا تھا' اس وقت سے لے کر اب تک کے واقعات مجھے یاد ہیں", I remember what has happened from the moment God said *Am I not your Lord?* until now (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 49, folio 45, حاشیہ 9).

The dervishes of Multan, Sharib relates, were displeased at his coming and tried to have him leave. They sent him a cup filled with milk, to say "اس شہر میں دوسرے کی گنجائش نہیں ہے", there is no room for another in this city. He placed a flower on the milk and sent the cup back, meaning that his place in the city would be "جیسے کہ پھول دودھ پر ہے", as a flower is on milk; they were astonished and became his devotees (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 51, folio 47). Sharib's book tells the same exchange of milk and flower, with the same claim that a town has room for one saint only, at Panipat between two other saints (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 94, folio 90). Harish Dhillon's retelling of the Janamsakhis sets a like story at Multan with Guru Nanak, "The Bowl of Milk and the Jasmine Petal": at the gates stands "an earthen bowl, filled to the very brim with milk", and Nanak lays a jasmine petal on it (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 169–170, folios 185–186). Dhillon names none of the Sufis in that story and does not connect it with this saint.

In the closing pages of Sharib's chapter, under *kashf o karamat*, is a story of hail. When Jalal al-Din Surkh Bukhari came to Multan and pined for the weather of Bukhara, "آپ کو یہ بات کشف سے معلوم ہو گئی", the saint knew it by *kashf*. He sent a *khadim* to the mosque with orders to roll up the prayer rows and sweep the courtyard; a cloud appeared, heavy rain fell, and hailstones, and "مسجد سے باہر نہ بارش ہوئی اور نہ اولے", outside the mosque there was neither rain nor hail. At Zuhr he came smiling and asked "کہئے سید! اولے ملتان کے بہتر ہیں یا برف بخارا کا", to which the Sayyid answered "اس صورت میں تو اولے ملتان کے سو درجہ بہتر ہیں" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 53–54, folios 49–50). The same day he conferred the *khirqa* of *khilafat* on the Sayyid: "آپ نے ان کو اسی روز خرقہ خلافت سے مشرف فرمایا" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 54, folio 50).

The same pages tell of a ship. Khwaja Kamal al-Din Masud Shirazi, caught in a storm at sea, called on him, and "آپ جہاز پر نمودار ہوئے' سب مسافروں نے آپ کو بخوبی دیکھا", the saint appeared on the ship and all the passengers saw him plainly; he gave them tidings of safety, and the ship reached Aden well. The passengers gave a third of their goods to Khwaja Kamal al-Din, "بطور نذرانہ و شکرانہ", as an offering and a thanksgiving, and he sent all of it, with half of his own jewels, to Multan by his nephew, who on arriving recognised the saint at once "اور اسی لباس میں پایا' جس میں انہوں نے آپ کو جہاز پر دیکھا تھا", in the very dress in which he had seen him on the ship. "وہ مال و جواہرات آپ نے تیس دن کے اندر لوگوں کو تقسیم کر دیا' اس مال کی قیمت ستر لاکھ ٹکہ[OCR?] تھی": he distributed the goods and jewels to the people within thirty days, and their value was seventy lakh *tanka* (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 54, folio 50, note 19).

Chishti tells of an offering returned. The Khan-e Khanan, at the khanqah in Multan, gave "دو سو روپیہ نذرانہ", two hundred rupees as an offering, which was accepted and then returned the next morning: in a dream, a holy figure whose name is uncertain in the printed text, "حضرت جلدی[OCR?]", had told the *sajjada nashin* that Thatta had already been granted "حسب الدعا[OCR?] مے[OCR?] حضرت حسین لاہوری" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 68).

In Sindh he is remembered for a miraculous birth. Boivin writes: "One Madan had asked for the intercession of Baha al-Din Zakariyya (d. 1267) to obtain a son, when the famous Sufi of Multan was touring in Sindh. Baha al-Din offered him a small end (pithu) of a dried date. At the moment his wife tasted the blessed piece, she became pregnant. The son whom they had was named Pithoro, or the name of the date end. Thereafter, Pithoro became the disciple of Baha al-Din." He adds that "The legend of his birth is always identical, except that sometimes, as for example the case with the gaddi nashin, the Sufi Master was not Baha al-Din Zakariyya, but Shah Rukn-e Alam (d. 1335), another later saint of Multan." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 187, folio 174).

Bhandari, who says that he died on reading a sealed letter, closes his notice: "بسا داستان شگرف حیرت افزا ازان حضرت برزبان صغار و کبار است" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528).

In Waris Shah's romance he is one of the Five Pirs, and in their list of gifts to Ranjha Usborne's English gives him "a thick blanket" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 11, folio 10). Later in the poem a Pir Bahauddin, named with no Zakariya, no Multan, no date and no order, so that the identification with this saint is uncertain, works a wonder for Ranjha. Ranjha prayed that the Five Pirs would "bless my enterprise and make the way easy", and Pir Bahauddin, the translation says, "shook the earth, and the way was opened unto Ranjha, and a voice spoke", saying "Jat, arise, go on your way. Why are you sleeping? The way has been opened for you." (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 50, folio 49).

Guru Nanak's hagiography has him meet the saint at Multan. Rizvi writes: "At Ajodhan and Multan, Guru Nanak was reported to have had discussions with Baba Farid and Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya… The suggestion is far-fetched although Guru Nanak did undoubtedly enjoy the company of sufis in these towns" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 413, folio 389). Khalid writes that "A similar story of Nanak meeting Bahaudin Zakariya is also found in Nanak's hagiography", and dismisses it: "Bahaudin too belonged to the thirteenth century, almost a hundred years before Nanak" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 286, folio 260).

## In Verse

The saint wrote verse himself. "آپ شاعر بھی تھے", Sharib writes, and he has a famous *manqabat* in honour of Ghaus al-Azam Shaikh Abd al-Qadir Jilani, "جس کے چند اشعار ذیل میں پیش کئے", of which a few verses are given below (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 52, folio 48). The chapter breaks off there, and the verses stand on the next page, which is taken to be the chapter's close: six couplets with the refrain "محی الدین جیلانی", among them the lines "معلا[OCR?] جب سبحانی مقدس قطب ربانی …" and "مدد یا شاہ جیلانی بریں افتادہ حیرانی …" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 53, folio 49). The poem closes on the couplet that carries his name:

سنگ درگاہ جیلانی بہاء الدین ملتانی
لقائے[OCR?] دین سلطانی محی الدین جیلانی

*Baha al-Din Multani is a stone of the threshold of Jilani* (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 53, folio 49)

The gloss given with the couplet renders its first line only.

Fakhr-ud-Din Iraqi wrote for him. Rizvi quotes a devotional verse of Iraqi's that names him as the guide of the world:

*If thou shouldst ask of the world 'Who is the guide of men?' / Thou wilt hear from heaven no other answer than 'Zakariyya'.* (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 225, folio 205)

Schimmel sets "Fakhruddin 'Iraqi's poems for his master Baha'uddin Zakariya Multani" as the classical parallel to the popular poems composed in honour of saints in Sindhi, Panjabi and Balochi (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 287, folio 269).

The Punjabi poets remembered him too. Rafat writes of Bulleh Shah that "His reverential reference to Multan as a holy city and to Bahauddin Zakria, the founder of the Suhrawardiya school is frequent." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 39, folio 19). In one of Bulleh Shah's lyrics, in Shackle's translation, Hir calls Ranjha to the tomb:

*Come, Ranjha, let us go to Multan, to pay homage to the great saint Bahaval Haq. Let us gain our desire immediately. My heart longs for the lord* (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 123, folio 98)

It is the only pilgrimage to a named saint's place anywhere in the 157 lyrics of Shackle's volume, and Shackle doubts it: "This invitation to visit his tomb in Multan, which is a major center of pilgrimage, strikes a strange note, and the verse may be a later interpolation." (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 186, folio 161, n. 5 on 122).

The same lyrics invoke him among the Five Pirs, "Five great saints who are believed collectively to possess special powers", whom Shackle names as "the legendary Khwaja Khizr, Farid Shakarganj of Pakpattan, Baha ud Din Zakariya of Multan, Jalal ud Din Makhdum Jahaniyan of Uch, and Lal Shahbaz Qalandar of Sehwan." (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 186, folio 161, n. 2 on 122). The Pirs guard the beloved:

*The Five Pirs protect my beloved, they search for him among people* (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 122, folio 97)

And they are propitiated:

*I belong to the yogi, after propitiating the Five Pirs.* (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 136, folio 111)

In Waris Shah's *Hir and Ranjha*, as Usborne's English renders the passage, the Five Pirs come to Ranjha: "Then Shahbaz Qalandar, the holy saint of Uch", and Zakaria, saint of Multan, "and Sayyid Jalal of Bukhara, whom men also call Makhdum Jahanian." (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 10, folio 9; p. 11, folio 10). The annotator lists him among the Five Pirs' "spiritual celebrities" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 16, folio 15, note 10).

## Setting: Multan

The books' wider accounts of Multan speak of the city, not of this shrine, but they show the place to which he came. Schimmel gives 711 as the year in which "the Arabs conquered Sind and the adjacent provinces northward up to Multan" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 375, folio 344). Rizvi writes that "Multan had become a dependency of the Fatimids of Egypt", that "by the middle of the eleventh century, sufism had penetrated into the areas surrounding Multan", and that "in 1175 Mu'izzu'd-Din Muhammad bin Sam seized Multan" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 128, folio 110). Dhillon writes that "During the twelfth century, many Sufi scholars made Multan their base", that conversion "gained momentum in the early thirteenth century" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 166–167, folios 182–183), and that "By the time Nanak came to Multan, the city was famous as a major centre of Sufi learning and philosophy" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 167, folio 183). Of Nanak's time Khalid writes: "At that time Multan was one of the biggest cities of Punjab and was known for the religious shrines of Muslim saints, and Jain and Hindu temples. It was a sacred space for people of all religious denominations" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 123, folio 105).

Bhandari, describing the suba of Multan in his own day, writes of it "از شهرهاے پاستان است و قلعه خشتین دارد" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528); "هرگونه مردم درو اباد", people of every kind live there, and the goods of every land are bought and sold; its winter is mild, its summer "گرما افراط دارد", and it has little rain; and, besides the saints he names, "بسیاری از اولیاے دران شهر سعادت بهر اسوده اند" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528). He records that under Balban, when Prince Muhammad Sultan governed Multan, Amir Khusrau and Amir Hasan were in the prince's service there, and that the prince twice sent to Shiraz for Sa'di, wishing to build a khanqah for him at Multan and endow it with villages; Sa'di could not come because of his age, and the book does not say whether the khanqah was built (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 386). He also notes a later Sultan's visit, "در سنه ۸۳۵[OCR?] سلطان بملتان رسیده مزارات مشایخ را طواف نموده", in which the reading of the year is uncertain in the printed text and no shaikh or tomb is named (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 324).

Multan held a place in the poets' devotion as well. Schimmel writes that for the vernacular poets "Mecca and Medina (and for the Sindhi and Panjabi, also Multan) are inside the heart" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 178, folio 160), and Rafat writes that in the eyes of Bulleh Shah Takht Hazara, the birthplace of Ranjha, attains the same status as "Multan as the seat of the Suhrawardiya school of mysticism" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, pp. 38–39, folios 18–19). Khalid recalls an older sanctity: Hiranyakashipu is "the tyrant who is believed to have ruled the city of Multan thousands of years ago" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 19, folio not stated).

Nur Ahmad Chishti's history records that in سنہ ۱۸۴۸[OCR?]ء, as it prints the year, "دیوان مولراج پسر ساون مل ناظم ملتان" raised a disturbance and had اگنیو[OCR?] صاحب بہادر, another sahib and the new *nazim* of Multan killed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 592). Chishti says this of the city and says nothing there of the shrine.

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The year of his death.** The site's account gives 1267. Lahori's text gives 666 AH, twice by chronogram (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 160–161, folios 148–149); his editor gives 661 AH, noting that the *Lataif-e Ashrafi* and the *Gulzar-e Abrar* have 666, the latter's figure uncertain in the printed text, and the *Akhbar al-Akhyar* 661 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 159, folio 147, حاشیہ 1). Sharib gives Thursday, 7 Safar 666 AH, at the age of a hundred (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 52, folio 48, حاشیہ 15). Bhandari gives 7 Safar 660 AH (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528). Rizvi gives 7 Safar 661 / 21 December 1262 (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 212, folio 194). Eaton gives 1263 (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 227). Schimmel gives c. 1265 in *Pain and Grace* (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 8, folio 4), ca. 1262 in the text and ca. 1267 in the index of *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 383, folio 352; p. 512, folio 481), and 1267 in *As Through a Veil* (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 104, folio 94). Boivin, Kasmani and Khalid give 1267 (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 187, folio 174; Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 188, folio 171, ch. 1 n. 17; Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 20, folio not stated).
- **The year and place of his birth.** The site's account gives around 1170, near Multan. Sharib gives Friday, 27 Ramazan 566 AH, at Kot Karor (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 49, folio 45). Lahori's editor gives 566 AH / 1170 CE at Kot Kahror (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 158, folio 146, حاشیہ 1), but Lahori's text gives 578 AH and no place (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 160–161, folios 148–149). Bhandari gives 565 AH at Kot Karor (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528). Rizvi's index gives 1182-83 (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 461, folio 437); Khalid gives 1170 (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 20, folio not stated).
- **Where his seat was.** The site's account and nearly every book place him at Multan. Rozehnal calls him "the well-known Suhrawardi saint of Uch Sharif", and in the same passage calls Makhdum-i Jahaniyan "the famous fourteenth-century Suhrawardi master of Multan" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 57, folio 47).
- **Whether Multan is in the Punjab or in Sind.** The site's account sets him in the Punjab. Schimmel's *Pain and Grace* sets Multan "at the border of Sind and the Panjab" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 8, folio 4), and has him establish the Suhrawardi centre "at the northernmost border of the country, in Multan", the country there being Sind (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 25, folio 21).
- **His descent.** The site's account calls the family Qureshi, of Arab descent. Sharib gives four accounts, Qurashi, Asadi and Hashimi; of the progeny of Mahyar[OCR?] bin Aswad; only al-Qurashi al-Asadi; and of Asad of Quraish, the maternal grandfather of Ali; and then rules that he is Hashimi beyond doubt (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 48, folio 44, حاشیہ 1; p. 48, folio 44, حاشیہ 5).
- **His mother's father.** Lahori names Maulana Husam al-Din Tirmizi of Kot Karor (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 160, folio 148). Sharib gives Husam al-Din Tirmizi and also, as "it is also said", Hazrat Isa of the progeny of Shaikh Jilani, and does not choose (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 49, folio 45).
- **His kunya.** Sharib gives Abu Muhammad, and reports that some wrote Abu al-Barakat (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 49, folio 45, حاشیہ 8).
- **When he learned the Quran.** Sharib: at seven, with the seven readings (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 50, folio 46, حاشیہ 10). Lahori: at twelve (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 160, folio 148).
- **How long he stayed with his master.** The site's account says he completed his training with remarkable speed. Lahori gives eighteen days to the *khirqa* of *khilafat* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 160, folio 148). Sharib, quoting Nizam al-Din Auliya, gives seventy days in his master's service, the number uncertain in the printed text (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 50, folio 46, حاشیہ 11).
- **His place in the order.** The site's account calls the Suhrawardi order one "he founded", and Rafat calls him "the founder of the Suhrawardiya school" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 39, folio 19). Lahori traces the line from Junaid Baghdadi through Shihab-ud-Din Umar Suhrawardi to him (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 158, folio 146); Sharib makes him the murid and khalifa of Shihab-ud-Din (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 48, folio 44); and in the story Schimmel quotes he is Shihab-ud-Din's "favoured pupil" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 384, folio 353).
- **A Qadiri threshold.** Sharib makes him a Suhrawardi murid and khalifa (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 48, folio 44), and prints, as his, a *manqabat* to Abd al-Qadir Jilani whose last couplet calls Baha al-Din Multani "a stone of the threshold of Jilani" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 53, folio 49). The book does not reconcile the two.
- **How he became Shaikh al-Islam.** The *Hadiqat al-Awliya* has him accept the office at Iltutmish's request, after supporting him against Qabacha (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 159, folio 147). Sharib has him appointed in place of Najm al-Din Sughra[OCR?], removed for a false slander (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 51–52, folios 47–48, حاشیہ 14).
- **Why he parted from Jalal al-Din Tabrizi.** Sharib: he rebuked Jalal al-Din for not naming Shihab-ud-Din Suhrawardi before Farid al-Din Attar (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 35, folio 31, حاشیہ 6). Rizvi: he prayed and meditated on the road while Jalal al-Din visited dervishes and went sightseeing (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 217, folio 199).
- **His table.** The site's account speaks of hospitality on a grand scale. Sharib writes that his *langar* was open to all (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 52, folio 48, حاشیہ 17). Rizvi writes that the food "was offered to only a select few" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 236, folio 216). Schimmel writes that he kept fixed hours for invited visitors, as opposed to the open Chishti table (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 383, folio 352).
- **Who came to him.** Lahori: the kings of the time and high and low alike, and lakhs who benefited (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 160, folio 148). Rizvi: he "was concerned only with the élite", and Rizvi thinks it unlikely that people came to him for faith cures (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 240, folio 220).
- **What sustained the house.** The site's account speaks of endowments and gifts. Sharib speaks of agriculture and trade on a large scale (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 52, folio 48, حاشیہ 17); Rizvi, of gifts from merchants and trade guilds (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 242, folio 222); Schimmel, of a well-to-do landlord (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 383, folio 352).
- **Music and poetry.** Sharib: he was fond of *sama'* (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 52, folio 48). Rizvi: he "was opposed to poetry and music", tolerating them in Iraqi alone (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 224, folio 204). Ernst and Lawrence and Abbas report qawwals in his gatherings (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 171, folio 159; Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 193, folio not stated).
- **Lal Shahbaz Qalandar.** Sharib lists him among the five eminent khalifas (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 52, folio 48, حاشیہ 16); Rizvi has Bahauddin adopt him and name him (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 328, folio 306); Kasmani reports a tradition that he was initiated at Multan by him (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 184, folio 167, intro n. 34). Khalid writes that both Bahaudin and Farid "would have had serious differences" with him (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 282, folio 256).
- **The answer to Hamid ad-Din.** Rizvi says that Bahauddin replied, though not to Hamid ad-Din's satisfaction (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 146, folio 128). The letter Ernst and Lawrence quote says that he did not reply, or replied unsatisfactorily (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 184, folio 172).
- **The estate.** Sharib gives seventy lakh *tanka* as Sadr al-Din's share (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 74, folio 70), and the identical sum, spelled differently, as the value of the offering from the ship (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 54, folio 50). Rizvi has Sadr al-Din alone inherit property worth 700,000 *tankas* (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 220, folio 202). Lahori's notice records seven sons and the division of the estate (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 158–161).
- **Iraqi's years at Multan.** Schimmel gives twenty-five years (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 8, folio 4) and nearly twenty-five (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 383, folio 352); Rizvi, from about 1239 to about 1263 (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 225, folio 205). Rizvi has him forced out by the other disciples' jealousy (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 225, folio 205); Schimmel has him leave for Konya soon after the master's death (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354).
- **Iraqi's investiture.** Rizvi: the master declared his training complete, gave him his own *khirqa*, then his daughter, and before he died made him khalifa (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 224–225, folios 204–205). Schimmel's story: on hearing Iraqi's poem sung in the bazaar and the taverns he declared "His affair is complete," and invested him with the mystic robe (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, pp. 383–384, folios 352–353).
- **Amir Husain.** Bhandari names میر حسینی among his students (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 528). Rizvi argues that the Amir Husain whom Jamali and modern scholars call his disciple was born ten years after his death (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 226, folio 206). The books do not say that the two are one man.
- **Pithoro Pir's master.** Boivin's legend gives Baha al-Din Zakariyya; sometimes, as with the *gaddi nashin*, it gives Shah Rukn-e Alam instead (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 187, folio 174).
- **The Five Pirs.** Shackle names Khwaja Khizr, Farid of Pakpattan, Baha ud Din Zakariya of Multan, Jalal ud Din Makhdum Jahaniyan of Uch and Lal Shahbaz Qalandar of Sehwan (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 186, folio 161, n. 2 on 122). Usborne's Waris Shah has "Shahbaz Qalandar, the holy saint of Uch", Zakaria of Multan, and Sayyid Jalal of Bukhara, called Makhdum Jahanian (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 10, folio 9; p. 11, folio 10).
- **Two items in the literature on him.** The editor of the *Hadiqat al-Awliya* dates Malik Latif's publication of the *Malfuzat* in *Sahifa* to 1970, the year uncertain in the printed text (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 159, folio 147, حاشیہ 1); the book list dates it January 1971 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 305, folio 293). The book list gives the *Ahwal wa Athar* to Shamim Zaidi (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 298, folio 286); the publisher's catalogue gives a book of that title to Hamid Allah Shah Hashmi (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 353).
- **Lahori's own descent.** The author's pedigree from the saint is printed twice and the two printings differ at three links and in the number of generations (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 24, folio 12; p. 232, folio 220).

## Legacy

Bahauddin Zakariya stands beside Baba Farid as one of the two founding fathers of Punjabi Sufism — the Suhrawardi to Farid’s Chishti — and his khanqah made Multan a wellspring of the tradition. Through his descendants and disciples, and through the later saints of Multan who trace to his line, his influence runs through the whole later history of Sufism in the western subcontinent. His shrine anchors Multan’s ancient identity as the “City of Saints,” and his memory endures as that of the master who first gave the Suhrawardi order an Indian home.

The books carry that memory in several traditions at once. Khalid calls him the saint "who is now considered the patron saint of Multan" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 282, folio 256). Shackle's Bulleh Shah and Usborne's Waris Shah count him among the Five Pirs (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 186, folio 161, n. 2 on 122; Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 16, folio 15, note 10). Kasmani names him, with Fariduddin Ganjshakr and Jalaluddin Surkhposh Bukhari, among "The other three figures" of a group he elsewhere calls "a quartet of mystics, chahar-yar or 'four friends'", and writes: "Their shrines are spread across southern Punjab in Pakistan." Chothanbi, a site between Lal-bagh and Sehwan that pilgrims visit, "is believed to be their place of meeting" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 188, folio 171, ch. 1 n. 17; p. 62, folio 45). In Sindh the legend of Pithoro Pir traces that saint's birth and discipleship to him (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 187, folio 174), and Nanak's hagiography, as Khalid and Rizvi report it, has Guru Nanak meet him at Multan, a meeting both writers reject (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 286, folio 260; Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 413, folio 389). Rizvi's judgement is that the continuous history of the Suhrawardi silsila in India "can be traced only through the khalifas of Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 220, folio 202).
