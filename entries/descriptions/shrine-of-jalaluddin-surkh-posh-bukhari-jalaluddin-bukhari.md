## Overview

At Uch Sharif, the ancient town near the confluence of the Chenab and the Sutlej in southern Punjab, lies the shrine of Sayyid Jalaluddin Surkh-Posh Bukhari (1192–1291), the saint who made Uch one of the great centres of Sufism in the subcontinent and the founding father of the celebrated Bukhari sayyids. 'Surkh-Posh', the Red-Robed, came from Bukhara to settle at Uch, where he propagated the Suhrawardi order and founded a spiritual dynasty whose descendants — among them Makhdoom Jahaniyan Jahangasht — would shape the religious history of the region for centuries. His shrine anchors the remarkable cluster of tombs that has made Uch Sharif a place of pilgrimage and a treasury of Islamic architecture.

The histories and studies consulted for this article fill out that outline and, in places, contradict it. Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori's *Hadiqat al-Awliya* makes him a khalifa of Bahauddin Zakariya, gives him a year of birth, a death date exact to the day and five sons, and places his mazar at Uch (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 162–163, folios 150–151). Zahur ul-Hasan Sharib brings him from Bukhara to Multan, where he became Bahauddin Zakariya's *murid*, and then to Uch at his pir's command (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 133, folio 129). Annemarie Schimmel calls him "the forefather of a long line of devout mystics and theologians" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354), and Haroon Khalid, visiting Uch Sharif, found the shrine of 'Pir Jalaludin Bokhari' the goal of almost every visitor there (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 282, folio 256). The books differ over whether his migration ended at Uch or at Multan, over the year of his death, over the number and names of his sons, and over the epithet itself, which several of them give as Surkh alone, without Posh.

A further difficulty runs through the whole record. His grandson, whom the site's account names Sayyid Jalaluddin known as Makhdoom Jahaniyan Jahangasht, bore the same name, and several books speak of a Jalal Bukhari in terms that do not settle which of the two they mean. What each of those books says is given below as that book's own account, with its uncertainty beside it, and the question is set out under 'Where the Sources Disagree'.

## The Life of the Saint

Sayyid Jalaluddin was born in 1192 at Bukhara, in Central Asia, into a family of Sayyids tracing descent from the Prophet. Migrating southward in the age of the Mongol upheavals, he came by way of Bhakkar in Sindh and settled at last at Uch, which he made the base of his mission. He is remembered as 'Surkh-Posh' for the red garment he habitually wore, and as a propagator of the Suhrawardi way, connected to the great Multan line of Bahauddin Zakariya. From Uch he preached and guided, and he founded the family of the Bukhari sayyids that would become one of the most influential religious lineages of the Punjab and Sindh. He died at Uch in 1291, full of years, and was buried there.

Lahori's *Hadiqat al-Awliya* gives him entry no. 103 and dwells on his descent. It makes him a khalifa of Bahauddin Zakariya and ties the standing he held in Hindustan to his lineage: in Hindustan, that land like paradise, all the sayyids, shaikhs, amirs and sultans treated him with deference because of the soundness of his descent — a precedence, as the book presents it, resting on pedigree rather than on office or wealth (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 162, folio 150). The notice sets out a genealogy of ten links, by name, back to Imam Muhammad Taqi, and records the branching of two families from it: Sayyid Ali Asghar, the saint's seventh ancestor, had two sons, Sayyid Abd Allah and Sayyid Ismail, and from Abd Allah's descendants came the family of the Bukhari sayyids, from Ismail's the sayyids whose name is printed as perhaps Bhakhari, its reading uncertain (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 162, folio 150). For the saint's early life Lahori names his source as the author of *Mazhar-e Jalali* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 162, folio 150).

The books trace his road out of Bukhara in different ways. Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi writes that "Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya had many famous disciples, one was Saiyid Jalalu'd-Din Bukhari, also called Jalal Surkh (Red)", and gives the route the site's account follows, with a reason for its last stage: "He first migrated from Bukhara to Bhakkar, however local jealousy forced him to migrate to Uch. He remained there until his death" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 223, folio 203). These three sentences, for which Rizvi cites a single source, are the whole of what his book says of the saint in his own right: it prints no date for him and names no tomb, shrine building or khalifa (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 223, folio 203). His index, which files this saint under 'Surkh', sends the shorter 'Jalalu’d-Din Bukhari' to Makhdum Jahaniyan (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 470, folio 446).

Sharib, in a chapter whose subject is the saint's grandson, brings him to Multan first: the grandfather, whom it calls Shaikh Jalal al-Din Surkh Bukhari, migrated from his homeland, Bukhara, to Multan, became Bahauddin Zakariya's *murid* and received from him the *khirqa* of *khilafat*, and took up residence at Uch by his pir's order (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 133, folio 129). In Sharib's telling, then, the settlement at Uch was an instruction from Multan.

A later note in Lahori's book gives the migration a year and the same end as Sharib: in 635 AH / 1227 CE, as the years are printed, he migrated from Bukhara to Multan (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 349, folio 337). That note names Multan, not Uch, gives no year of death, and leaves the word 'Posh' out of his name (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 349, folio 337). Schimmel's sentence on him reads: "One of Zakariya's disciples, Sayyid Jalaluddin Surkhpush" ('Red-dressed', d. 1292) "from Bukhara, settled in Ucch, northeast of Multan, and is the forefather of a long line of devout mystics and theologians" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354). Kanhaiya Lal's history of Lahore, which names him only as the forebear of Mauj Darya Bukhari, says that he lived at Uch and that his tomb is there, calling him Sayyid Jalal al-Din Mir Surkh Bukhari (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 279, folio 282). Kanhaiya Lal gives him no date and no silsila (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 279, folio 282).

Of the Mongol age into which the site's account sets his migration, the books speak only of the Punjab at large and do not name him. Richard Eaton writes that Mongol pressures "forced waves of Turkish settlers into the subcontinent, many of them settling permanently in the urban centres of the Punjab, such as Lahore, Dipalpur, and Multan" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 217, folio 205), and that Balban's garrisoning of the Punjab's cities drew in "thousands of refugees fleeing before the advancing Mongols—artisans, merchants, petty officials, and the like" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, pp. 222–223, folios 210–211). Rizvi writes that "The Mongol invasions between the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries had devastated portions of the Panjab" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 408, folio 384), and that in that age the Qalandariyyas and Jawaliqis who passed through Multan "often came into conflict with Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 325, folio 303). Eaton, who names Zakariya as Baha' al-Haqq Zakariya and gives his death as 1263, writes of him "whose tomb is in Multan city" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 227, folio 215).

Lahori dates the saint's life exactly, and here, unusually for this book, the editor adds no correcting footnote: born in the year 595, died on 19 Jumada al-Awwal 690, his mazar at Uch (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 163, folio 151). The year 690 AH corresponds to the site's 1291; Lahori's year of birth, 595, stands beside the site's 1192. The notice also carries a chronogram, which gives the date 690 AH twice (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 163, folio 151). Omar Kasmani's note on "Jalaluddin Surkhposh Bukhari (d. 1291) of Uchh" gives the same year as the site (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 188, folio 171, ch. 1 n. 17), while Schimmel gives 1292, both in her text and in her index (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354; Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 518, folio 487). Sharib gives neither a birth nor a death year (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 133, folio 129). Khalid says only that "Jalaludin had lived in the thirteen century", and, in a passage whose printed text is damaged, that he had died before "the end of the century" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 285, folio not stated).

## His Names

The epithet by which the site's account knows him, Surkh-Posh, the Red-Robed, for the red garment he habitually wore, appears in only some of the books, and in varying forms. Schimmel prints 'Sayyid Jalaluddin Surkhpush' and glosses the epithet as 'Red-dressed' (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354). Kasmani writes 'Jalaluddin Surkhposh Bukhari' and 'Uchh' (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 188, folio 171, ch. 1 n. 17). Khalid's travelogue has 'Syed Jalaludin Surkh Posh Bokhari' (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 276, folio 250), and beside it the shorter 'Pir Jalaludin Bokhari' (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 282, folio 256) and 'Syed Jalaludin Bokhari' (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 285, folio not stated). Rizvi prints 'Jalal Surkh (Red)' and never the longer form (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 223, folio 203), and indexes him as "Saiyid Jalalu’d-Din Surkh (Bukhari), 203, 277" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 483, folio 459).

The Urdu and Persian histories give him fuller names. Lahori heads his notice Sayyid Jalal al-Din Sher Shah, titled Mir Surkh Bukhari Uchi (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 162–163, folios 150–151), and his table of contents lists Sayyid Jalal al-Din Sher Shah Surkh Bukhari (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 17, folio 5). The index of the same book files him under the epithet Surkh Bukhari Uchi, with the personal name Jalal al-Din Sher Shah in parentheses, the reading of that name uncertain in the printed text (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 318, folio 306), and his later notices give him the nisba Mir Surkh Bukhari Uchi (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 168, folio 156). Kanhaiya Lal has Sayyid Jalal al-Din Mir Surkh Bukhari (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 279, folio 282). Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti calls him Mir Sayyid Jalal al-Din known as Pir Surkh, the epithet uncertain in the print (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 101), and on another page gives a different form, Sayyid Sultan Jalal al-Din Haidar Surkh, the last word again uncertain (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 106). Sharib's form, Shaikh Jalal al-Din Surkh Bukhari, lacks the word Posh (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 133, folio 129).

Two books give him only the name Jalal and the nisba of Bukhara. Waris Shah's romance of Hir and Ranjha, in Charles Frederick Usborne's English, says 'Sayyad Jalal of Bokhara' (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 50, folio 49) and 'Sayyid Jalal Bokhari' (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 52, folio 51), and its later annotator 'Jalal Bukhari' (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 11, folio 10, note 2); Surkh-Posh never appears there, nor Uch Sharif as his seat, nor any date or order. Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* has only Sayyid Jalal Bukhari, without the epithet (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527).

## Uch Sharif and the Bukhari Sayyids

Through Jalaluddin Surkh-Posh and his descendants, Uch Sharif became a spiritual capital of the southern Punjab — rivalling Multan as a seat of the Suhrawardi order and producing a succession of saints of the first rank. Chief among his descendants was his grandson, Sayyid Jalaluddin known as Makhdoom Jahaniyan Jahangasht, one of the most revered Suhrawardi saints of the fourteenth century, and the line continued through generations of sajjada-nashins who carried great religious and worldly authority. The town they made holy is today a dense and evocative landscape of shrines and tombs — among them the exquisite tiled mausolea of Bibi Jawindi and others — and it is counted among the most important heritage sites of Pakistan.

Lahori, on the authority of reports he calls sound, gives the saint five sons and names their mothers. The first two, Sayyid Ali and Sayyid Jafar, were both sons of the daughter of the king of Bukhara; Jafar settled in Bukhara and never came to Hind. The third, Sayyid Ahmad Kabir, was the son of Bibi Fatima, daughter of Sayyid Badr al-Din Bhakkari, the reading of whose name is uncertain in the printed text. The fourth was Sayyid Sadr al-Din Muhammad Ghaus, and the fifth, Sayyid Baha al-Din Ahmad, famous as Muhammad Masum, is named with his mother, Bibi Tahira Sayyida (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 163, folio 151). Lahori thus records three wives, a royal marriage, and a branch of the family that stayed in Central Asia.

Sharib gives three sons, named and ranked: Sayyid Ahmad Kabir the eldest, Sayyid Baha al-Din, and the youngest, Sayyid Sadr al-Din, known as Muhammad Ghaus (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 133, folio 129). Sharib names no order for the saint himself; the Suhrawardi affiliation his chapter does name belongs to the grandson's *bay'a* from the saint's son (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 135, folio 131). Rizvi, too, gives three sons: "He was a grandson of Saiyid Jalalu'd-Din Surkh (Red), a disciple of Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya. Saiyid Jalalu'd-Din Surkh had three sons: Saiyid Ahmad Kabir, Saiyid Baha'u'd-Din and Saiyid Muhammad" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 299, folio 277). On the same page Rizvi writes of the Suhrawardiyya in the time of Saiyid Jalalu'd-Din Bukhari, Makhdum-i Jahaniyan, whose birth he gives as 15 Sha'ban 707/9 February 1308, that the order "spread from Uch to Gujarat, the Panjab and Kashmir even extending its influence to the Delhi of Sultan Firuz Shah" Tughluq (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 299, folio 277).

Bhandari names the saint only in the genealogy of Makhdum Jahaniyan, where he stands as grandfather: the man buried at Uch, Shaikh Jalal, son of Sayyid Mahmud, son of Sayyid Jalal Bukhari, is known as Makhdum Jahaniyan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527). Bhandari thus makes Sayyid Mahmud the son through whom the line ran to Makhdum Jahaniyan, a name that appears in none of the other books' lists of the saint's sons; he gives the grandfather no tomb, no place, no date and no epithet (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527).

In two later notices Lahori names him only as the head of the Uch Bukhari line: as the grandfather of Makhdoom Jahaniyan Jahangasht in the heading of entry no. 107 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 168, folio 156), and as the ancestor of Sayyid Haji Abd al-Wahhab Bukhari Dehlvi in entry no. 114 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 176, folio 174 as printed). A later note in the book, naming a father as Sayyid Jalal Surkh Bukhari, refers back to his notice by its number, 103 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 349, folio 337). Two other histories know him as an ancestor. Kanhaiya Lal names him as the forebear of Mauj Darya Bukhari (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 279, folio 282). Chishti, on the page where he calls Mauj Darya the sajjada-nashin of his forefathers' khanqah, writes of a hazrat descended from Mir Sayyid Jalal al-Din known as Pir Surkh, and of a mazar at Uch, which he here calls Uch Miran (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 101). Elsewhere Chishti writes of the descendants of Sayyid Sultan Jalal al-Din Haidar Surkh, the last word uncertain in the print (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 106), and in a sequence of names on a later page a Sayyid Jalal al-Din Bukhari stands just before Hazrat Sayyid Rukn al-Din Abu al-Fath Multani (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 154).

Two books connect Bibi Jawindi with a Jalal Bukhari. Khalid writes that the tomb of Bibi Jawindi, whom he calls Bibi Jiwindi, "belonged to the great granddaughter of Syed Jalaludin Bokhari" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 285, folio not stated). Robert Rozehnal, writing of the same tomb, calls Bibi Jawindi "the wife of the renowned fourteenth-century Suhrawardi shaykh, Sayyid Jalal ad-Din Bukhari (1303–1383)" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 21, folio 10); his dates are not the site's dates for this saint, and the question of which Jalal is meant is taken up below.

Of the town's later standing in the Suhrawardi order, the books speak of Uch rather than of this saint. Schimmel writes that "Ucch was, for a while, the center of the Suhrawardiyya, thanks chiefly to the active and pious Jalaluddin Makhdum-i Jahaniyan", 'whom all the inhabitants of the world serve', "a prolific writer in almost all religious fields" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354). Michel Boivin speaks of "the second Sohrawardiyya period, then based in Utch near Bahawalpur in Punjab" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 56, folio 43). Kasmani, naming the saint as the third of three figures who met Lal at Chothanbi, adds: "Their shrines are spread across southern Punjab in Pakistan." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 188, folio 171, ch. 1 n. 17).

## The Shrine

The shrine of Jalaluddin Surkh-Posh Bukhari at Uch is a working darbar and the ancestral seat of the Bukhari sayyids. Standing amid the celebrated monuments of Uch Sharif, the shrine is at once a place of living devotion and a link to the deep medieval history of Sufism in the subcontinent.

Lahori and Kanhaiya Lal place his grave at Uch but say nothing of its building. Lahori writes that his mazar is at Uch (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 163, folio 151), and nowhere in his account of the saint does he describe the shrine's fabric, its *ʿurs*, its custodians or its economy. Kanhaiya Lal says that his tomb is at Uch and gives no description of it (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 279, folio 282). Chishti speaks of the mazar at Uch Miran (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 101). Sharib records no mazar, *ʿurs*, custodianship or endowment for him (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 133, folio 129), and neither Rizvi nor Schimmel names a tomb (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 223, folio 203; Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354). One book buries him elsewhere. Usborne's annotator, glossing the romance's Jalal of Bukhara, writes: "Jalal Bukhari, who originally came from Uch, is also buried at Multan" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 11, folio 10, note 2); the romance itself never calls him Surkh-Posh or gives Uch as his seat.

The one description of the shrine in the books consulted is Khalid's, from a visit on which Iqbal Qaiser was with him. The pages of his travelogue that describe the shrines on the mound do not themselves name the town; Uch Sharif is clear from the shrines he names and from his later 'shrine of Jalaludin Bokhari at Uch Sharif'. He writes: "The shrine of Pir Jalaludin Bokhari was also situated on this mound. It was protected by a thick wall with barbed wire on top, which was constructed recently to protect it from terrorist attacks". "Almost all the visitors here, who numbered in the hundreds, were heading towards this shrine"; there was an "old Banyan tree in the centre"; and a banner "depicted Pir Jalaludin, Bahauddin Zakariya, Baba Farid and Lal Shahbaz Qalandar. Once again there was no Nanak" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 282, folio 256).

A mosque stands "next to the shrine, within the same compound", and Khalid calls it "a historical building" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 283, folio not stated). Of the interior, in a passage whose printed lines are damaged and run into one another, he writes: "Women were not allowed here either. It was a dark room", with "wooden pillars" "intricately carved with floral designs"; there were "several graves here, of people who must have paid hefty amounts of money to get buried next to the saint"; and "The grave of the saint" was "enclosed in glass" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 283, folio not stated).

A board of the Archaeology Department, as Khalid reports it, records that Baha-ul-Halim taught "at the madrassa that used to run at the shrine of" Jalaludin Surkh Posh Bokhari (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 285, folio not stated). In the market at the Gilani shrine, Khalid saw a poster of Pir Jalaludin on sale (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 277, folio 251).

## Devotion and Practice

The shrine is visited throughout the year by pilgrims who come to honour the founder of the line and to seek his blessing. It keeps the round of prayer, qawwali, and the free kitchen, and its annual urs draws devotees from across the region. The books consulted record no date for the *ʿurs*.

Khalid's account is of the visitors' devotion as he saw it. "Women it seemed were not allowed as they looked on from the boundary" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 283, folio not stated). A man showed him "three rooms" where, he said, Baba Farid, Bahauddin Zakariya and Lal Shahbaz Qalandar had meditated; Khalid writes, "I was sure that this was an apocryphal story". The man put a green shawl "with Quranic verses on it" round Khalid's neck and offered him water; expecting to be asked for a donation, Khalid walked off (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 283, folio not stated). People stood by the grave "for blessings"; another man waited "with a green box"; and devotees "offered their donations to the saint" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 283, folio not stated).

## Guru Nanak and the Shrine

Khalid's travelogue brings the shrine into the story of Guru Nanak. "According to Sikh tradition, Nanak was invited here [Uch Sharif] by the saint Syed Jalaludin Surkh Posh Bokhari when he met him in Kandahar, on the way back from Haj" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 276, folio 250). The visit, as he places it, took place on Nanak's way to Sindh, "after he had settled down" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 278, folio 252).

Khalid himself doubts the tradition. The Sikh "tale of Nanak and Jalaludin meeting" cannot hold, he argues, for "Jalaludin had lived in the thirteen century"; in a heavily damaged passage, Iqbal Qaiser suggests that Nanak may have met a descendant "of the saint but not Jalaludin" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 285, folio not stated). He writes that "once Nanak had been invited to visit this place at the behest of the saint himself but today there is no trace of his heritage" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 277, folio 251).

Khalid then imagines the visit. "Uch Sharif was one of the most famous Muslim shrines in Southern Punjab and Nanak had arrived here only a day ago, travelling from Kartarpur"; "At the shrine of Jalaludin Bokhari at Uch Sharif, Nanak saw an overwhelming number of dervish and ascetics", some "selling beads", some selling the leaves of trees "as sacred entities with magical properties", some pretending "to practise black magic", and others selling prayers; and Nanak sat "under the shadow of the banyan tree which was in the centre of the courtyard" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 288, folio 262). In the reconstruction a jogi prepares *bhang*; near the entrance is "a man selling bhangwale pakore (fritters with the addition of cannabis)"; and a jogi "carrying an ektara" sings in praise of the intoxicant, "one of the main attractions of a festival like this" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 287, folio 261). Nanak says, "Mardana, play Raag Ashta Padya", forgetting that his companion was his best friend's son; the hymns Khalid then gives are drawn, his note says, from Singh's translation, *Hymns of the Gurus* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 289, folio 263).

Of Nanak's traces in the town itself, Khalid found little. Iqbal Qaiser asked him, "Do you want to see the well of Nanak?", and warned, "There is nothing left there anyway"; the well "was hidden within the field, shielded by a grove of trees. It was abandoned and juice tetrapaks and wrappers were floating in the black water", and beside it was a grassy mound: "This was the Gurdwara of Nanak", and "Now nothing remained" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 286, folio 260). Of the river, he writes that the "River Sutlej, the lost river of Pakistan, used to flow from here before it was lost forever after the Indus Water Treaty was signed between India and Pakistan in 1960", and that "Nanak must have seen these buildings intact with a mighty river flowing next to it" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 281–282, folios 255–256).

## Legends and Miracles

Lahori's notice, which names its source for the saint's early life (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 162, folio 150), also tells of a miracle of his childhood; its details are not given in the material consulted for this article.

Sharib's chapter on Bahauddin Zakariya names a Hazrat Sayyid Jalal al-Din Surkh Bukhari among Zakariya's khalifas, with no place, no date and no shrine (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 52, folio 48), and has a man of that name receive the *khirqa* of *khilafat* at Multan after the miracle of the hail, again with no town of burial, no year of death and no silsila (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 53–54, folios 49–50). The name and the link to Zakariya resemble this saint's, and the *khirqa* agrees with the chapter in which Sharib names him as a grandfather; but the book itself gives nothing more to tie these passages to this saint.

In Waris Shah's romance a Jalal of Bukhara lends his name to two relics, a cudgel and a dagger, and his dagger becomes a weapon against a lion; the passages are given under 'In Verse'.

## In Verse

Waris Shah's *Hir and Ranjha*, in Usborne's English, counts a Jalal of Bukhara among its five Pirs, and names him in the same sentence as Makhdum Jahanian:

*he smelt the sweet savour that came from the cudgel of Sayyad Jalal of Bokhara, -and he grasped the dagger given him by Makhdum Jahanian.* (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 50, folio 49)

Two pages later it is Jalal's dagger that is used against the lion:

*he drove the dagger of Sayyid Jalal Bokhari into his belly.* (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 52, folio 51)

The cudgel is Jalal's and the dagger Makhdum Jahanian's in the first passage; in the second the dagger is Jalal's. The inversion is the book's own. The romance gives this Jalal no epithet, no seat, no date and no order; the only thing in it that sets him apart from Makhdum Jahanian is that the two are named side by side as separate members of the five.

Lahori's notice on the saint carries a chronogram that gives the date 690 AH, the year of his death, twice (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 163, folio 151); the verse itself is not quoted in the material consulted for this article.

## Setting: Uch Sharif

The books' accounts of the town speak of Uch as a whole, not of this shrine. Rozehnal, who visited "the nearby town of Uch Sharif" in April 1996, "spent a day wandering through the magnificent blue and white tiled mosques and desolate tombs of this ancient center of learning, trade, and pilgrimage built on a hill overlooking the confluence of the Sutlej and Chenab rivers"; of its tombs he names only Bibi Jawindi's (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 21, folio 10). He places it as "an ancient city located seventy miles southwest of Multan in Pakistan" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 210, folio 202); Schimmel places 'Ucch' 'northeast of Multan', and gives no district or province (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354). Usborne's annotator describes it as an ancient town "in Bahawalpur, now part of West Pakistan", and adds: "It is famous for its Gilani and Bukhari Saints, who hailed originally from Gilan (Iraq) and Bukhara (Central Asia) respectively" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 11, folio 10, note 2).

Shemeem Burney Abbas calls the place "the city of saints in Bahawalpur", writing of Bulleh Shah's family as "Syeds who had settled in Uch Gilanian, the city of saints in Bahawalpur, where Bulle Shah was born" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 107, folio not stated). She quotes the words "Thou art an inhabitant of Uch city [Uch literally means high/great]", and, in prose, that "Bulle Shah's mentor Inayat Shah, he--he belonged to Uch city" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 104, folio not stated). At Kasur, Muhammad Bakhsh, of a hereditary family of qawwals, told her: "We came with Bulle Shah's family from Uch Sharif, and we have learned his poetry from our ancestors, who have always sung here" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 112, folio not stated). Bahawalpur, with Multan, is in her account the seat of the landowning Syeds who "support the shrines of their ancestors" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 168–169, folios not stated).

Rizvi tells a foundation legend of the town in connection with Shaikh Safiu'd-Din Kaziruni. In it an order "to mount a camel and travel in whatever direction the animal led him; he was then to remain where the camel finally halted" ends with a halt "in the middle of a desert", where "the Shaikh founded a town later called Uch"; Rizvi's verdict is: "Although this story would appear mythical, it is typical of medieval tales relating to sufis." (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 129, folio 111). He also tells of a flying contest with a yogi at Uch (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 129–130, folios 111–112), and observes that all the stories of three generations of the sufis of Uch 'recount conflicts with Hindu Yogis' (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 304, folio 282).

The town's other great family is that of the Gilani Qadiris. Rozehnal calls Shaykh Muhammad al-Husseini al-Jilani "the founder of the first Qadiriyya khanaqah at Uch", and writes: "A native of Turkey, Shaykh Muhammad traveled to Khurasan and then Multan before settling with his family in Uch" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 259, folio 252). His son 'Abd al-Qadir Jilani Thani (d. 1533) is "the famous Qadiri saint of Uch Sharif", yet "there seems to be no extant archive of the saint, no textual record to document his life and legacy" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 210, folio 202). Lahori, writing of the arrival of the Qadiri line in the Punjab, says that the first of that line to come to the Punjab was Hazrat Sayyid Muhammad Ghaus Uchi Jili Gilani, and has him come by way of Khurasan to Multan and settle at Uch; the modern editor's footnote, citing Ali Asghar Gilani, gives his entry into Uch as 887 AH, a date that is the editor's and not the author's (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 40, folio 28). Chishti, following a genealogy he names as the book of the genealogy of Hazrat Pir Muhammad Shah Sahib, writes of a Gilani ancestor who came from Uch Sharif in the time of Akbar Shah (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 525), and of an ancestor who came to Multan, was given a man's daughter, named Fatima, in marriage, was presented with the territory of Uch, the name uncertain in the print, and settled there (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 526). Schimmel writes that "It was in Ucch that the first missionaries of the Qadiriyya settled in the late fifteenth century; from there this order spread into the Subcontinent, where it soon gained a firm footing and was carried to Indonesia and Malaysia" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354), but elsewhere that "the order was introduced in the late fourteenth century" into the subcontinent (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 278, folio 247). Rozehnal, against the site's account, calls Bahauddin Zakariya himself "the well-known Suhrawardi saint of Uch Sharif" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 57, folio 47).

Chishti's *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* records the reputation of the sayyids of Uch. The sayyids of Uch are famous, he writes, and where a dispute over descent arises and they accept a man as of sound descent the matter is closed, and no one then dares say a word (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 526–527). The four sons of Hazrat Muhammad Ghaus lie at Uch Sharif, beside the tomb of Shah Muhammad Ghaus, and some of Abd al-Qadir Sani's descendants hold the *sajjada* at Uch (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 527). The elders of the Faqir family lived at Uch Sharif Sayyidan and served the sayyids of Uch (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 584). Chishti also tells a story of the time of a Nawab whose title is printed as perhaps Nawab Khan Bahadur, the reading uncertain: at Uch Sharif the Nawab had a boil on his cheek that would not heal, went to the sayyids of Uch Sharif for prayer, and was directed by them to Ghulam Muhyi-ud-Din, the reading of whose name is uncertain, as a skilled physician; the Nawab took him to Lahore and was cured. Chishti attributes the account to Hazrat Shah Sardar, son of Pir Muhammad Shah Gilani (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 584).

Bhandari's history knows Uch as a town on the roads of armies (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527). Shihab al-Din's first campaign took it and left a deputy there, and his second came again to Multan and Uch and went on to Gujarat (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 405); Mirza Pir Muhammad crossed the Sind and took Uch and Multan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 333); and on one march toward Jodhpur, Uch appears as "a dependency of Multan" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 285). In his account of suba Thatta, the province runs from the borders of Multan and Uch to Thatta and Kach Makran (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529).

Khalid, driving to Uch Sharif, writes that "The boundary between Sindh and Punjab becomes a blur as we drive through Bahawalpur", and that "This is where the cultures of Punjab and Sindh merge. Some of the roadside hotels are called Punjab Hotel, while others are Sindh Hotel"; the language, Siraiki, is "an amalgamation of the two languages", and Iqbal Qaiser adds that "The Siraiki spoken in Multan is closer to Punjabi, while here it is more like Sindhi" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 276, folio 250). Boivin, too, notes that "The Siraiki language is mainly spoken in Southern Punjab, with Multan as capital. Interestingly, this area was a part of Sindh during the Middle Ages" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 216, folio 203). Khalid found the older city on a mound, with the 'massive havelis' "of the current caretakers of the shrine, the Gilanis and the Makhdooms" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 276, folio 250); the group of 'thirteenth and fourteenth century shrines' was reached through the market, and Iqbal Qaiser remarked, "This place has changed since the last time I came here" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 281, folio 255). On the mound were several graves "which were covered arbitrarily with the blue and white Multani tiles that had been recovered from these historical shrines" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 285, folio not stated).

For further reading on the town, Rozehnal points to Mas'ud Hasan Shahab's *Khitta-i pak Uch* (Bahawalpur: Urdu Academy, 1967/1993) and to the second volume of Rizvi's *A History of Sufism in India* (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 259, folio 252).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **Which Jalal: the indexes and chapter headings.** The site's account distinguishes this saint from his grandson, Sayyid Jalaluddin known as Makhdoom Jahaniyan Jahangasht, and several books name a Jalal Bukhari in terms that could fit either. Rizvi indexes this saint as "Saiyid Jalalu’d-Din Surkh (Bukhari), 203, 277" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 483, folio 459), but sends the shorter form to the grandson: 'Jalalu’d-Din Bukhari, see Makhdum Jahaniyan' (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 470, folio 446). Sharib's table of contents lists chapter 22, beginning on page 129, as Hazrat Sayyid Jalal al-Din Bukhari, a name both men share (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 15); within it this saint appears only as the grandfather of its subject (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 133, folio 129). Bhandari's Sayyid Jalal Bukhari is a bare name in Makhdum Jahaniyan's genealogy, with no epithet, tomb or date (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527). In two later notices Lahori names him only as the head of the Uch Bukhari line (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 168, folio 156).
- **Which Jalal: a fourteenth-century Jalal ad-Din Bukhari.** Carl Ernst and Bruce Lawrence write that Qiwam ad-Din "is best known for his spiritual initiation, as a Chishti through Chiragh-i Dihli, and as a Suhrawardi through Jalal ad-Din Bukhari (d. 1383)", and that Sa'd ad-Din compiled his master's discourses after the *Khizana-yi Jalali*, "in the style of a Suhrawardi malfuzat text of Jalal ad-Din Bukhari, The Jalalian Treasury" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 71, folio 57); they name neither Uch nor Surkh-Posh nor Makhdum. Rozehnal's "Sayyid Jalal ad-Din Bukhari (1303–1383)", the husband of Bibi Jawindi, is likewise given no epithet (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 21, folio 10). Their dates stand against the site's 1192–1291 for this saint; Ernst and Lawrence name no place, and neither book uses the epithet Surkh-Posh.
- **Which Jalal: the five Pirs of the romance.** Waris Shah's *Hir and Ranjha* names 'Sayyad Jalal of Bokhara' and Makhdum Jahanian in one sentence as two of its five Pirs (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 50, folio 49); it gives Jalal no epithet, seat, date or order, and that sentence is the only thing in the book that sets its Jalal apart from Makhdum Jahanian.
- **Which Jalal met Guru Nanak.** Sikh tradition, as Khalid reports it, has Syed Jalaludin Surkh Posh Bokhari meet Nanak at Kandahar and invite him to Uch (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 276, folio 250). Khalid answers that "Jalaludin had lived in the thirteen century", and Iqbal Qaiser suggests that Nanak met a descendant "of the saint but not Jalaludin" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 285, folio not stated).
- **Where his migration ended, and where he lies.** The site's account has him settle, die and be buried at Uch. Lahori's notice puts his mazar at Uch (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 163, folio 151), Kanhaiya Lal his tomb (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 279, folio 282), and Chishti a mazar at Uch Miran (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 101); Rizvi has him remain at Uch until his death (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 223, folio 203), and Schimmel has him settle in Ucch (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354). A later note in Lahori's book brings his migration from Bukhara to Multan and names no Uch (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 349, folio 337). Sharib has him come to Multan and move to Uch at his pir's order (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 133, folio 129). Usborne's annotator says that he "originally came from Uch" and "is also buried at Multan" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 11, folio 10, note 2).
- **The road from Bukhara.** The site's account brings him by way of Bhakkar in Sindh to Uch. Rizvi agrees on Bhakkar and adds that "local jealousy forced him to migrate to Uch" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 223, folio 203). Sharib and the later note in Lahori's book take him from Bukhara to Multan instead, the latter in 635 AH / 1227 CE as the years are printed (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 133, folio 129; Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 349, folio 337).
- **His dates.** The site's account gives 1192–1291. Lahori gives his birth in the year 595 and his death on 19 Jumada al-Awwal 690, the year that corresponds to the site's 1291 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 163, folio 151). Kasmani gives 1291 (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 188, folio 171, ch. 1 n. 17); Schimmel gives 1292, in her text and in her index (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354; Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 518, folio 487). Rizvi prints no date for him at all (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 223, folio 203), Sharib gives no birth or death year (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 133, folio 129), and Khalid says only that he lived in the thirteenth century (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 285, folio not stated).
- **His sons.** Lahori gives five: Sayyid Ali, Sayyid Jafar, Sayyid Ahmad Kabir, Sayyid Sadr al-Din Muhammad Ghaus and Sayyid Baha al-Din Ahmad, called Muhammad Masum (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 163, folio 151). Sharib gives three: Sayyid Ahmad Kabir, Sayyid Baha al-Din and Sayyid Sadr al-Din, known as Muhammad Ghaus (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 133, folio 129). Rizvi gives three: Saiyid Ahmad Kabir, Saiyid Baha'u'd-Din and Saiyid Muhammad (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 299, folio 277). Bhandari makes Sayyid Mahmud the son through whom the line passed to Makhdum Jahaniyan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527).
- **His order.** The site's account makes him a propagator of the Suhrawardi order. The books make him a khalifa, *murid* or disciple of Bahauddin Zakariya (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 162, folio 150; Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 133, folio 129; Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 223, folio 203; Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354), but none of those that give him the epithet Surkh or Surkh-Posh names his order: Rizvi does not use the word Suhrawardiyya of him (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 223, folio 203), and the Suhrawardi affiliation Sharib names is his grandson's (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 135, folio 131). The Suhrawardi Jalal ad-Din Bukhari of Ernst and Lawrence and of Rozehnal is the man whose identity is in question above.
- **His epithet.** The site's account calls him Surkh-Posh, the Red-Robed, for a red garment. Schimmel glosses Surkhpush as 'Red-dressed' (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354); Rizvi has 'Jalal Surkh (Red)' only (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 223, folio 203); Sharib's form has no Posh (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 133, folio 129), nor has the later note in Lahori's book (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 349, folio 337); Lahori's notice has Mir Surkh (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 162–163, folios 150–151); and Chishti has 'Pir Surkh' and, on another page, Sayyid Sultan Jalal al-Din Haidar Surkh, the last word uncertain in the print (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 101; Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 106).
- **Bibi Jawindi.** Khalid calls her "the great granddaughter of Syed Jalaludin Bokhari" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 285, folio not stated); Rozehnal calls her the wife of "Sayyid Jalal ad-Din Bukhari (1303–1383)" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 21, folio 10).
- **The relics in the romance.** In Waris Shah's *Hir and Ranjha* the cudgel is Jalal's and the dagger Makhdum Jahanian's (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 50, folio 49); two pages later the dagger is Jalal's (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 52, folio 51).
- **Bahauddin Zakariya's seat.** The site's account speaks of the Multan line of Bahauddin Zakariya; Sharib has the saint come to Multan to become his *murid* (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 133, folio 129), and Eaton puts the tomb of Baha' al-Haqq Zakariya, as he names him, "in Multan city" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 227, folio 215). Rozehnal calls Zakariya "the well-known Suhrawardi saint of Uch Sharif" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 57, folio 47).
- **Where Uch lies.** The site's account sets Uch near the confluence of the Chenab and the Sutlej, and Rozehnal on a hill overlooking it (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 21, folio 10). Rozehnal puts it "seventy miles southwest of Multan" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 210, folio 202); Schimmel puts it 'northeast of Multan' (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354).
- **The Qadiriyya at Uch.** Schimmel has the first Qadiri missionaries settle in Ucch "in the late fifteenth century" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354), and elsewhere has the order introduced into the subcontinent "in the late fourteenth century" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 278, folio 247). Lahori's editor, citing Ali Asghar Gilani, has Sayyid Muhammad Ghaus enter Uch in 887 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 40, folio 28).

## Legacy

Jalaluddin Surkh-Posh Bukhari holds a foundational place in the spiritual history of the Punjab and Sindh: the Bukhara-born saint who rooted the Suhrawardi way at Uch and fathered a dynasty of saints whose influence endured for centuries. Through his descendants — above all Jahaniyan Jahangasht — his line shaped the religion and society of the region, and through the shrines of Uch Sharif his memory is bound to one of the great sacred landscapes of Pakistan.

The books carry that memory in their own ways. To Schimmel he is "the forefather of a long line of devout mystics and theologians" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354); to Kanhaiya Lal, the ancestor of Mauj Darya Bukhari (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 279, folio 282); and on the banner Khalid saw at his shrine he stands beside Bahauddin Zakariya, Baba Farid and Lal Shahbaz Qalandar (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 282, folio 256). Lahori's notice records a standing in Hindustan that rested on his lineage (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 162, folio 150), and Chishti's book records the authority of the sayyids of Uch in questions of descent (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 526–527).

Khalid sets the shrine in the southern Punjab of his own day. "The southern region of Punjab, where Uch Sharif lies, has more traces of religious and cultural syncretism compared to the northern and central region, where there is a greater prevalence of education and hence religious intolerance" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 277, folio 251). At the shrine itself he wrote: "Standing at this shrine and observing such blatant religious oppression, it became clear to me why such extremist organisations were becoming popular in this region" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 284, folio 258). He reports the view of Ayesha Siddiqa, who "belongs to Bahawalpur, which is only about seventy kilometres from here", that organisations of the Ahl-e-Hadith school "have seeped into the social fabric of southern Punjab, a region that has historically rejected such strands of Islam and adopted a syncretistic version of religion. Such Sufi shrines are a symbol of that strand of Islam", and Tahir Kamran's, that such organisations are popular with the young because "they offer an explanation for their current oppression", and that their first step is "to target such shrines and their guardians, who have become immensely rich feeding off the oppressed classes" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 284, folio 258).
