## Overview

At Hazarkhwani, on the edge of Peshawar, stands the mausoleum of Rahman Baba (Abdur Rahman Mohmand, c. 1653–1711) — the most beloved poet of the Pashto language and the great mystic voice of the Pashtuns. For three centuries his *Diwan* has been the book most cherished after the Qur’an in countless Pashtun homes, its verses of love, humility, and the vanity of the world quoted as proverbs and sung by musicians from Peshawar to Kandahar. His shrine is the spiritual and literary heart of the Pashto-speaking world, and a traditional gathering place of poets and singers.

The books consulted say little of him, and of the mausoleum itself only one sentence. Haroon Khalid, writing of Pakistan's folk religious practices, records that "In March 2009 the popular shrine of Rahman Baba was attacked in Peshawar resulting in the death of over 100 people" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated). No book consulted names Hazarkhwani, describes the tomb's building, or records its *ʿurs*. Annemarie Schimmel writes of the poet, not the tomb: Abdur Rahman, "lovingly called Rahman Baba", was a mystic of the Chishti order, one "who lived during the Aurangzeb period (d. 1709) and is usually regarded as the best mystical poet in Pashto." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 430, folio 399). Her death year differs from the one given above, and the two are set side by side below.

## The Life of the Poet-Saint

Abdur Rahman was born around 1653 near Peshawar, into the Mohmand tribe of the Pashtuns; he is remembered as a man of humble means and retiring temperament, a dervish who shunned the powerful and lived in poverty and prayer. Little of his outward life is documented — he was no courtier and left no chronicle of himself — but his verse reveals a soul of deep Sufi devotion, gentle, self-effacing, and wholly turned toward God. He belonged to the broad Sufi current of his time and place, and by tradition performed the Hajj; he died around 1711 and was buried at Hazarkhwani, where his shrine arose.

Schimmel names his order, calling him a mystic of the Chishti order (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 430, folio 399). She places him in the reign of Aurangzeb and gives his death as 1709, in her running text and again in her index, where he stands as Abdur Rahman (Rahman Baba), d. 1709 (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 430, folio 399; p. 510, folio 479). She gives the retiring life as legend, and in so many words: "As legend has it, he led a life of withdrawal and seclusion, and his poetry reflects a sadness and ascetic feeling that are not too frequent in the mystical poets of this period." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 430, folio 399).

She names a second Pashto poet of his order: "a remarkable poet tried to express mystical teachings in Pashto. This poet was Khwaja Muhammad Bangash", who was, like Abdur Rahman, a member of the Chishti order (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 432, folio 401). She gives no tomb, no Peshawar and no Hazarkhwani for Rahman Baba.

## The Poetry

Rahman Baba’s fame rests entirely on his *Diwan* — a body of Pashto verse of unmatched sweetness and moral depth. His great themes are the love of God, the transience of the world, humility, tolerance, and the dignity of the poor; he preaches, again and again, that one should return kindness for cruelty and sow good rather than harm. One of his most quoted teachings runs, in sense:

[“Sow flowers, that your garden may bloom about you; the thorns you plant will wound your own feet.”]

His poetry is at once popular and profound — simple enough for the unlettered to carry in memory, deep enough to nourish the mystic — and it has done more than any other book to shape the moral imagination of the Pashtuns. His verses are sung to the *rabab* and recited in gatherings, and his name is spoken with an affection given to no other Pashto poet.

Schimmel adds a note of her own on the verse's mood, in the words of the legend quoted above: "his poetry reflects a sadness and ascetic feeling that are not too frequent in the mystical poets of this period." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 430, folio 399). The books consulted quote none of his lines, in Pashto or in English.

His verse has reached readers abroad in selection. In a note on Pashto mystical poetry in another of her books, Schimmel writes that "Good examples are found in H . G . Raverty, Selections from the Poetry of the Afghans; James Darmesteter, Chants populaires des Afghans, and Jens Enevoldsen, Selections from Rahman Baba" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 278, folio 260). Her bibliography dates Enevoldsen's volume to 1977, from the publisher Paul Kristensen; the place of publication is damaged in the printed text (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 319, folio 301). That book says nothing else of him, and nothing of his tomb.

## The Shrine

Rahman Baba’s mausoleum at Hazarkhwani has long been a centre of pilgrimage and of Pashto literary life, where poets and singers gather and an annual urs is held in his honour with music and *mushaira* (poetic assembly) alongside the rites of devotion. The shrine was severely damaged by a bombing in 2009 — an attack on the tolerant, musical devotion it represents — but it was subsequently rebuilt, and pilgrims and lovers of his poetry returned. It remains the great shrine of the Pashto-speaking world.

Khalid gives the attack its month and its toll: "In March 2009 the popular shrine of Rahman Baba was attacked in Peshawar resulting in the death of over 100 people" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated). It is the one sentence on the shrine in the books consulted; none of them records who built the mausoleum, when, or how it was rebuilt, and none gives the date of the *ʿurs*.

## Setting

The books say more of Peshawar and its country than of the poet's tomb, and what follows is about the place, not the shrine.

Sujan Rai Bhandari's *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, a Persian history composed in the fortieth regnal year of Aurangzeb, writes that Peshawar is an old city which the earlier books also write as Parshawar and Farshawar (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 503). It describes the district known as Peshawar as having grapes, peaches and melons like those of Turan, heat, cold, seasons and rain like those of Hindustan, rice better than Bengal's, and crops and grain in plenty (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 504). Of its people it says that the whole district (the first word is uncertainly read) is the home of the Mohmand Afghans and others; the Mohmand are the tribe into which, in the current account, Rahman Baba was born (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 504–503). The same history records three roads from Peshawar to Kabul: by Nikshab, hard and long; by a road joining the highway at Jalalabad, with narrow defiles, little water and Afghan raids; and by Ali Masjid and the Khyber, easier than the others and used by armies and caravans (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 502–501).

Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi writes of the city's religious past long before the poet that "From the eleventh century the Nath yogis began to spread throughout northern India, and from their centre at Peshawar moved to all parts of Central Asia and Iran, at the same time influencing both qalandars and sufis" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 355, folio 333). Rizvi does not name Rahman Baba.

Of the frontier's shrines in general, Robert Rozehnal quotes a woman disciple from Peshawar, contrasting her home with Pakpattan: "In the frontier, we have so many shrines where you can walk into the saint’s shrines. Women can go inside, we can touch the graves. At many places there is a separate side for the women. It is not like this at Pakpattan Sharif" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 219, folio 211). She names no Peshawar shrine. And in Schimmel's paraphrase of the boldest mystical verse of the Panjabi and Sindhi poets, Peshawar stands among the identities the Sufi leaves behind: "the Sufi is no longer Arab, Hindu, Turk, or Peshawari; eventually Hallaj and the judge who condemned him, the lover and the theologian, are seen as nothing but different manifestations of the one divine reality." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 417, folio 386).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- The year of his death. The current account gives it as around 1711; Schimmel gives 1709, in her running text and in her index alike (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 430, folio 399; p. 510, folio 479).
- His retiring life. The current account gives his humble, retiring temperament as how he is remembered; Schimmel gives his life of withdrawal and seclusion as legend: "As legend has it" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 430, folio 399).
- His Sufi affiliation. The current account places him in the broad Sufi current of his time and place, naming no order; Schimmel calls him a mystic of the Chishti order (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 430, folio 399; p. 432, folio 401).
- The 2009 attack. The current account speaks of a bombing that severely damaged the shrine; Khalid speaks of an attack in March 2009 resulting in the death of over 100 people (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).

## Legacy

Rahman Baba stands to Pashto as Bhittai to Sindhi and Bulleh Shah to Punjabi: the poet who gave his people’s language its deepest and most beloved voice. His gospel of love, humility, tolerance, and the emptiness of worldly pride has been for three centuries a moral compass for the Pashtuns, and his *Diwan* a household treasure. His shrine at Peshawar — rebuilt after violence — endures as the hearth of Pashto poetry and the resting place of a saint whose verse still teaches a gentler way.

Schimmel's judgement, that he "is usually regarded as the best mystical poet in Pashto", is the books' own measure of that standing (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 430, folio 399).
