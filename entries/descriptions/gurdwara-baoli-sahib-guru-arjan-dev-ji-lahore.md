## Overview

Gurdwara Baoli Sahib stands in the crowded Dabbi Bazaar at the very heart of the walled city of Lahore, where its precinct once adjoined the Loha, Kesera and Guru bazaars. It commemorates a visit of *Guru Arjan Dev*, the fifth Sikh Guru, to Lahore in 1599, and takes its name from the *baoli* — a roofed stepwell — that the Guru is remembered as having had sunk at this spot to provide water for the people of the quarter. As a shrine tied to Guru Arjan — the compiler of the *Adi Granth* and the first martyr of the Sikh faith, whose principal Lahore memorial is the Gurdwara Dera Sahib by the Fort — Baoli Sahib is a significant station on the map of Sikh Lahore. Today the site is also known as Baoli Bagh, its surroundings laid out as a garden.

Of the books consulted for this article, only one describes a Baoli Sahib at all: Kanhaiya Lal's Urdu history of Lahore, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, first published in 1884. Its long account of the house of Baoli Sahib, as its table of contents names it, the last word uncertain in the printed text (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 4, folio 3), gives the building's situation among the bazaars, its fabric, its custodians and income, a founding story and a story of loss and recovery. The book names among the building's neighbours the bazaar of the iron-sellers and the Kasera bazaar of the vessel-sellers, as the site's account names the Loha and Kesera bazaars (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 108, folio 111). What follows reports that account as Kanhaiya Lal's own: a later passage of the same book speaks of a house of a baoli placed elsewhere in the walled city, and whether both passages describe this gurdwara is not settled (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 147, folio 150; p. 148, folio 151). The other books consulted do not mention the site. They speak of Guru Arjan himself, of Lahore, and of the Punjab, and what is drawn from them below is said of the Guru, the city or the province, not of this gurdwara.

## Guru Arjan Dev

Guru Arjan was "the fifth Sikh Guru of Sikhism and the son of the fourth Sikh Guru, Guru Ram Das" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 198, folio not stated), appointed as the next Guru by his father (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 220, folio not stated). Haroon Khalid's *Walking with Nanak* gives his dates as 1563 to 1606, and his years as Guru as 1581 to 1606 (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 315, folio 289); Harish Dhillon likewise has him Guru "from 1581 to 1606" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 240, folio 265). Naveed Alam, translating the verse of Shah Hussein, prints his dates as 1563–1605 and spells him Guru Arjun Dev (Alam, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 186, folio 186). Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, composed under Aurangzeb, records that after Guru Ram Das, Guru Arjan, his son, became his successor and died after twenty-five years (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).

He was the younger brother of Prithi Chand, and was preferred when "his father, Guru Ram Das, appointed his younger son" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 43, folio 25). Lahore stands at the root of that succession. Khalid relates that Prithi Chand hid Arjan's letters from Lahore to Ram Das, who then disowned Prithi Chand and, a few days later, appointed Arjan as the next Guru (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 253–254, folio 227 / folio not stated); "Sikh tradition finds the root of the conflict to be Arja[n]'s trip to Lahore and the machinations of Prithi Chand", who then approached the Mughal authorities for his share of the property, "and hence began the tumultuous relation … between the Mughals and the Sikhs" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 274, folio 248). Khalid writes, too, that Guru Ram Das "was also born in present day Pakistan, in the city of Lahore", that "Guru Arjan spent a lot of time in the same city, where he was assassinated and his remains interred", and that Guru Hargobind "also spent a lot of time here" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated).

His great work was the scripture. Dhillon writes that Guru Nanak's *bani* "was compiled as part of the Adi Granth by Guru Arjan Dev, the fifth Guru, in 1604" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 9, folio 9). Khalid counts among his major contributions "the compilation into the Granth Sahib of the writings of all the previous Sikh Gurus and other non-Sikh saints" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 198, folio not stated), and notes that the poetry of Baba Fareed, collected by Guru Nanak at Pak Pattan, "was later included in the Granth Sahib by Guru Arjan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 200, folio not stated). He himself "has the greatest number of hymns in the Guru Granth Sahib" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 315, folio 289), a number Khalid gives as 2218, the figure uncertain in the printed text (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 256, folio 230); in the Granth's naming, "Arjan becomes the fourth Nanak" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 98, folio 80). The binding of the book was Lahore work: "Guru Arjan asked one of his followers to go to Lahore to bind the Adi Granth", Bhai Bannu (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 39, folio 21), and "The Kashmiri binders who used to reside in Lahore were known for their meticulous binding" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 39, folio 21). After the compilation the Guru was summoned over its alleged "anti-Muslim and anti-Hindu content", and sent Bhai Gurdas, who "clarified the misunderstanding" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 254, folio not stated). The Adi Granth "played a significant role in the development of a separate Sikh identity", one reason Khalid gives being rumours that "Meherban, the son of Prithi Chand" was compiling a scripture of his own (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 256, folio 230).

Other institutions are credited to him. He introduced "the daswandh", "10% of their earning to the cause of the Guru" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 315, folio 289), collected through the Masands, "a tradition that was started by Guru Arjan" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 256–257, folio 230 / folio not stated). He is credited "with the completion of the Amrit Sar the tank of nectar that was started by his father at Ramdaspur", after which "the Harmandir Sahib was constructed there" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 257, folio not stated). Dhillon writes that the Kartarpur near Jalandhar was founded in 1594 by the fifth Guru, Arjan Dev, "on land granted during the reign of the third Mughal Emperor, Jalal-ud-din Muhammad Akbar" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 175).

Bhandari's history records a meeting between an emperor and the Guru. As the imperial camp left Lahore for the Deccan, the emperor crossed the Beas and came to the halting-place of Guru Arjan, the name uncertain in the printed text, successor of Baba Nanak, described as famous in divine gnosis — though the sentence as printed leaves open whether that last phrase describes the Guru or Baba Nanak (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 164). The emperor was pleased with the meeting and with hearing from the Guru's own mouth the Hindi verses that Baba Nanak had composed on the knowledge of God; Guru Arjan, counting it an honour, presented a fitting *peshkash* (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 164). He then made a petition: while the imperial armies were in the Punjab grain had been dear and the assessment had been raised, but now grain had become cheap and the peasants could not meet it. On his request the officers of revenue were ordered to grant the peasants a reduction (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 164).

The Guru's name runs also through the Punjabi Sufi tradition. Alam records that "According to some sources, Shah Hussein travelled to Amritsar to get his poetry included in the Granth Sahib being compiled by Guru Arjun Dev (1563–1605). He offered this kafi for inclusion. Though the account is unverifiable, the thematic content of this kafi might have lent itself to the speculation" (Alam, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 186, folio 186). Khalid notes that "the friendship between Mian Meer and Guru Arjan is also the subject of several folk tales" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 200, folio not stated). Annemarie Schimmel, writing of the Qadiri order, places Mian Mir in Lahore: "the Qadiriyya, represented in Lahore by Mian Mir, who had come there from Sind, together with his mystically inclined sister Bibi Jamal" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 392, folio 361).

## The Guru's Death in Lahore

Guru Arjan died in Lahore. Khalid writes that he "was summoned to Lahore by Emperor Jahangir" over his role "during the rebellion of Prince Khusro", that he appointed "Hargobind as the sixth Guru", and that he "was subsequently martyred" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 234, folio 208). In Khalid's other account, Khusrau, "Jahangir's rebellious son", "stopped over at the Langar of the Guru in Amritsar and sought blessings for his victory", and the *tilak* the Guru gave him was "considered treason by the Emperor" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 199, folio not stated). Khalid quotes Jahangir: "In Goindwal which is on the river Beas, there was a Hindu named Arjan"; Khusro "happened to halt at a place where he was", and Arjan made "a finger-mark in saffron which the Indians call Qashga"; his property was handed "to Murtaza Khan", and he was to be "dealt with the penal Laws of Yasa" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 252, folio 226). In *A White Trail* the same memoir is quoted as calling him "this insignificant fellow" and ordering that he be put to death (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 198, folio not stated).

Bhandari's history tells the Lahore side of Khusrau's revolt, the setting of these events, without mentioning the Guru. Khusrau and Hasan Beg Badakhshi plundered along the road, burning *sarais* and seizing horses, until they reached Lahore (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 146). Dilawar Khan, the governor of Lahore, would not meet the prince and made the fort fast, and however the prince tried, the fort of Lahore did not fall to him (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 146). News of Khusrau's capture reached the emperor in the garden of Kamran Mirza at Lahore, in a year printed as 1015 of the Hijra, the reading uncertain (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 145). From Kamran Mirza's garden to the gate of the palace Khusrau's companions were impaled in two rows, crowds came out of the city to watch, and the prince was led on an elephant between the stakes and imprisoned in the fort (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 145).

Of the Guru's death Khalid writes that "For five days the Guru was tortured" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 252, folio 226). In the walled city, he goes on, "there is a place near the Rang Mahal" named for a red well, its name damaged in the printed text; "It is believed that Arjan was kept in a room next to this well, which was the haveli of the Mughal Diwan of Lahore"; boiling sand, the word damaged in the printed text, was poured on his head while he was immersed in a cauldron, and "His body was then thrown into the river"; and "near Lahore Fort there is a small yellow structure which is now referred to [as] the samadhi of Guru Agjan [OCR?] [Arjan]. It is believed that the Guru lost his life there" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 253, folio 227). The traditional Sikh explanation of his death lies in "the connivance of a Hindu Khatri called Chandu Lal", Diwan of Lahore, allied with Prithi Chand (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 253, folio 227); the back matter of the same book says that "The Sikhs believe that he was assassinated due to the connivance of one Chandu Shah who wanted to marry his daughter to Guru Arjan's son, but was refused" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 315, folio 289). Khalid observes that the Mughal sources, and the emperor's memoir in particular, are "glaringly silent about the role of Chandu Lal", Jahangir stating that Arjan was punished for his sympathies with his rebel son (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 255, folio 229).

Sikh belief, as Khalid relates it, holds that on the fifth day the Guru took a dip in the waters of the Ravi and did not emerge — that "the Guru had performed a miracle and passed onto the other world"; and that Mian Mir asked leave "to bury alive his oppressors. The Guru refused" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 253, folio 227).

For Khalid, Arjan was "crushed in the fight between the Mughal 'elephants'" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 255, folio 229), and the assassination is "the Karbala of Sikh history", which "precipitated the development of the Khalsa" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 255–256, folios 229–230). "The martyrdom of Guru Arjan provided the Sikh community with a cohesive motivation" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 232, folio 206); "He was the last Guru in the line of the Gurus who focused solely on spiritual matters" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 256, folio 230); and "Guru Arjan's martyrdom is a low point in Sikh history" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 206–207, folio not stated).

## The Founding of the Baoli

In the tradition preserved by the community, a devotee named *Bhai Chhajju Bhagat*, together with a Pathan merchant, laid a purse of gold *mohars* at the Guru's feet and asked that it be spent for religious purposes; the Guru had a *baoli* sunk at this spot to provide water for the people of the quarter. Iqbal Qaiser's 1998 survey of Sikh shrines gives the same origin story and specifies that the dispute was resolved before the Guru in Samvat 1616, while Majid Sheikh's reporting for *Dawn* fixes the disputed purse at "282 gold mohars".

Kanhaiya Lal tells the founding differently, and gives the attribution as report rather than as his own assertion: this baoli is famous as built by Guru Arjan, fifth successor of the Sikh religion, in the time of the Emperor Jahangir (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 110, folio 113). His story is of a treasure found in the ground. A Sikh of Guru Arjan sold his house on the spot to a woman for three hundred rupees. Digging to rebuild, she found a hoard, among it a gilded and jewelled canopied bed, and offered it to the seller as his ancestor's. He refused it: when the land was sold, whatever lay inside the land was sold with it, and this wealth is not his by right. The case went to the Qazi and then before the emperor, who, pleased with the honesty of both parties and because the householder was a Sikh of Guru Arjan, sent the treasure to the Guru to be spent on a good work; and Guru Arjan resolved that a baoli be built with this wealth (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 110–111, folios 113–114).

The Guru then set a guard over his well: a Sikh named Langahiya, the reading of the name uncertain in the printed text, was appointed with four other Sikhs to protect the baoli (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 111, folio 114). The Guru himself sometimes stayed there; and immediately to the south, in a building of the bazaar, Guru Arjan's *langar* used to be held, that building being as it were part of the house of the baoli itself (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 111, folio 114). Kanhaiya Lal gives no date for the Guru's death.

## Loss and Recovery

The stepwell later suffered in the turbulence of the eighteenth century: it is said to have been filled in and built over during the governorship of Nawab Yahya Khan, at the instigation of Diwan Jaspat Rai. The Department of Archaeology's 1962 memoir records instead that a mosque stood on the site between the era of the Sikh Gurus and the reign of Ranjit Singh, a detail found neither in Qaiser's account nor in the Nawab Yahya Khan narrative, and not reconciled with either.

Kanhaiya Lal sets the loss a century earlier, in the reign of Shah Jahan, and ties it to a mosque. After Guru Har Gobind's clash with the Qazi and the emperor, all the Guru's property in Lahore was confiscated, the word uncertain in the printed text, and handed to the Qazi, the baoli and the site of the *langar* among it. The Qazi, out of his personal enmity, built a mosque on the site of the *langar*, killed Langahiya and his four companions and threw them into the baoli, closed the well and built houses over it, and from that day the baoli was entirely lost (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 112, folio 115).

It was rediscovered and restored during the reign of *Maharaja Ranjit Singh*. Majid Sheikh's reporting adds that Maharaja Ranjit Singh's restoration followed his own recovery from illness after his astrologers advised him to bathe in the baoli's water, an account the Department of Archaeology's 1962 memoir independently records.

Kanhaiya Lal tells the recovery as a dream, and prints two versions of it together. In a Bikrami year printed as 1896, the reading uncertain, during Ranjit Singh's severe illness, an aged Sikh, the word for his age uncertain in the printed text, made it known that Guru Arjan had come to him in a dream, and had said that at such a place lay the Guru's own baoli and *langar-khana*, buried by the Qazi under a mosque; the Sikh was to tell Maharaja Ranjit Singh to dig out that baoli and bathe in its water, and he would be well. In the same breath the author records the variant: some say that the Guru came in the Maharaja's own dream and gave the command (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 112, folio 115). At last the baoli appeared, and the Maharaja bathed in its water and was restored to health (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 113, folio 116).

The rebuilding followed. Under Ranjit Singh, Kanhaiya Lal writes, the houses over the well were levelled and the mosque of the Qazi's time that had been built on the site of the *langar-khana* was demolished, and shops, sitting-places and *kothras* were built where it had stood (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 113, folio 116). The building was paid for by the state: the order came that one month's salary of the Khalsa, that is of the state, be deducted to raise this lofty building, and the deduction raised seventy-two thousand rupees, the figure uncertain in the printed text. Work began in a Bikrami year printed as 1890, the reading uncertain, went on slowly for five years, and was unfinished at the Maharaja's death (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 113, folio 116).

The site's account dates the work differently: Ranjit Singh in 1834 had a new building raised and a *sarovar* (sacred tank) dug, and the gurdwara passed under the management of the Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee in 1927.

## The Building in 1884

Kanhaiya Lal describes the house of Baoli Sahib as it stood in his day. This blessed building is among the famous places of worship of the Sikhs of Lahore, and the largest in extent of them all (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 108, folio 111). It stood among the bazaars, between the Kasera bazaar, the Lohati bazaar and a third whose printed name cannot be read with certainty. To the east lay the bazaar of the iron-sellers, its printed name, perhaps Topi, uncertain; to the north, the direction itself uncertain in the printed text, a bazaar whose printed name, perhaps Goti, cannot be read with certainty; and to the south the Kasera bazaar of the sellers of vessels. The great door was on the west, up three steps, the number uncertain in the printed text (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 108, folio 111).

He walks through it in order. An arched gateway with two *chaukis* opened on *dalans* north and south of the *deorhi*, with a *shah-nashin* above it lit by three *dariche* (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 108, folio 111). Beyond lay a very wide open court with a wheel-well, sets of three *kothris*, twelve upper *kothris* on the north, two *bel* trees and a *pipal* (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 109, folio 112). On the south stood the building of the Baoli itself, as it were three very wide *dalans* in one, floored with matting, the word uncertain in the printed text, with *shah-nashins* above (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 109, folio 112).

In the third *dalan* stood a *takhtposh* with a small *palang*, on which the Granth Sahib was kept with great honour inside costly coverings (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 109, folio 112). The stair of the baoli lay behind a door that was usually locked and opened when needed, and a second great door on the bazaar was always shut (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 109, folio 112).

The descent to the water was long. Thirty-six steps, the number uncertain in the printed text, led down to an intermediate courtyard (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 109, folio 112), and thirty-five more, again uncertain, led on to the water, where an arched door of red stone was set in the wall of the well, its threshold, the word uncertain in the printed text, beneath the water (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 110, folio 113). Above, a small masonry tank with seven steps on the west of the upper courtyard had long lain dry, and the wheel that once filled it from the well no longer turned (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 110, folio 113).

## Custodians, Endowment and Income

Kanhaiya Lal traces the custodianship through three hands. The Maharaja gave the building to Bhai Nihal Singh, with an annual *jagir* of eight thousand rupees in return for the building's service. It then passed into the charge of Bhai Jawahar Singh Kartarpuria, who took the monthly rents of the shops (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 113, folio 116). At the time of writing the serving officer was a white-bearded Sikh named Bhai Khazan Singh, appointed to the service and care of the building (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 113–114, folios 116–117).

The endowment was commercial. One hundred shops on both bazaar sides belonged to the building, the figure and one word describing the shops uncertain in the printed text (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 113, folio 116). The enclosure itself served the bazaar as a warehouse: the people of the bazaar had rented all the *kothris* inside the enclosure for their goods; cattle were kept inside; and many kept their cash and goods there under lock and paid rent. The building of the Roghan Mandi, among its dependencies, also paid rent (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 110, folio 113).

When Jawahar Singh's debts sank the income, the state provided a substitute: fifty rupees a month, the figure uncertain in the printed text, released by the government for the servants' salaries, lamp-wicks and *karah prashad*, paid monthly from the Mahkama Khafifa, all expenses, flooring included, to be met from it. A further two and a half rupees a month, the figure uncertain in the printed text, went to the drummers and one further party, its name illegible in the printed text, who came every day at dawn to make the offering (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 113, folio 116).

The offerings had dwindled. The offerings to the Granth Sahib were formerly never less than two rupees a day, and now brought no cash at all. What a raja or a *sardar* offered was taken by Bhai Jawahar Singh, and the servants got nothing of it; but the sugar-drops, sweets and coppers offered to the Granth Sahib were the right of the *pujaris* (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 114, folio 117).

## Devotion and Practice

In Kanhaiya Lal's day the house of Baoli Sahib served as a place of assembly: whenever a gathering of any kind took place in the Hindu religion, this was the building chosen (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 108, folio 111). A large *naqara* in the eastern *kothri* of the third *dalan* was played at two times of the day (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 109, folio 112). The Granth Sahib lay in its coverings in the same *dalan*, *karah prashad* was provided for from the state's monthly grant, and the drummers came each dawn to make the offering (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 113, folio 116). He records no annual fair or gathering at the building and no date for one.

The site is tied also to the scripture itself. Majid Sheikh's reporting adds that it was at Gurdwara Baoli Sahib, not elsewhere in Dabbi Bazaar, that the original Adi Granth manuscript (the Kartarpur *pothi*) was kept in the custody of the Sodhi family, carried in grand procession to the Lahore Fort under Ranjit Singh's patronage, and restored to the Sodhi family after the British annexation of 1849.

The principal commemoration of the Guru's martyrdom in Lahore, as Khalid records it, is held at the other shrine: one of the chapters of *A White Trail* is titled "Commemoration of Guru Arjan's martyrdom at Gurdwara Dera Sahib, Lahore" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 6, folio not stated). Khalid recalls, too, that Operation Blue Star was launched "on a day when thousands of Sikhs from all over Punjab had gathered at the shrine to commemorate the death anniversary of the fifth Sikh Guru, Guru Arjan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 162, folio not stated).

## The Neighbouring Mosque

A second passage of *Tarikh-e Lahore*, in the book's account of a mosque, tells of a dispute between a baoli and its Muslim neighbours. Kanhaiya Lal places this house of a baoli adjoining the Sunehri Masjid in Kashmiri Bazaar, and names no guru, no founder and no date for it (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 147, folio 150; p. 148, folio 151). It is reported here because the Faqirs who pleaded for the mosque speak of Baoli Sahib, where the Granth Sahib is kept; whether this is the same Baoli Sahib is uncertain.

The story is told entirely from the mosque's side. The Bhais of the Baoli, a party whose name is uncertain in the printed text, and the Akalis petitioned the Maharaja that the mulla of this mosque gave the call to prayer in a loud voice and it fell in their ears; this mosque too, they said, should remain in their possession along with the Baoli, or be demolished, for the neighbourhood of Muslims with the Guru's Sikhs was not desirable (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 147, folio 150). The Maharaja ordered at once that the mulla be expelled and the Granth placed there. The Akalis took possession, the Guru's seat was set in the mosque and the Granth placed in it, and the income of the mosque's shops was confiscated and merged into the Baoli's estate (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 147, folio 150).

The Muslims of the city were deeply grieved, and went in a body to the house of Faqir Aziz al-Din and Nur al-Din, that through them a petition for the mosque's release might be put to the Maharaja (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 147, folio 150). The Faqirs argued that the mullas of the mosques of all the Punjab do not give the call aloud, let alone that the mulla of a mosque beside Baoli Sahib, where the Granth Sahib is kept, should give it (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 148, folio 151). The settlement: the mosque was to be handed back to the mulla as before, a bond taken from him that he would give no call to prayer, and the rent of the mosque's shops was to remain confiscated. The Muslims counted even this a gain and regained the mosque, but the shops were lost from their hands (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 148, folio 151).

The confiscation outlived the Sikh state. Under English rule too the shops stayed confiscated, under the head of *nazul*, and petitions and lawsuits were refused on the ground that the property had been confiscated since the Maharaja's time and should not now be released (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 148, folio 151). After thirty-two years, the number uncertain in the printed text, the case was decided under Egerton, Lieutenant-Governor of the Punjab: the mosque's shops, whose rent brought five hundred rupees a year, were released by the government, and a committee of Muslim notables was formed to collect and spend the money (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 148, folio 151).

## Setting: Lahore of the Emperors and the Maharaja

Bhandari's history, composed under Aurangzeb, describes the city in which the Guru's baoli was sunk. Lahore, it says, is an ancient city on the bank of the Ravi (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525). Akbar built its fort, city wall and palace, Jahangir raised buildings still standing in the author's day, and princes and amirs built mansions, the city growing further under Shah Jahan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 524). It was a great and mighty city: craftsmen of every land lived there, and in every street and bazaar there were very many mosques (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 524). Jahangir's palace in the city, the imperial *daulatkhana*, was built of solid masonry at a cost given as eight lakh rupees, the figure uncertain in the printed text (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 135).

Wells, in Bhandari's account, were the life of the province. Of the suba of Lahore he writes that most irrigation depended on wells, worked by a wheel of three hundred and sixty pieces of wood and more than a hundred small pots, turned by a pair of oxen; the word for irrigation is uncertain in the printed text (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 510). In the fourteenth year of his reign, the number uncertain in the printed text, Jahangir ordered along the highway from Agra to Lahore a tall *minar* at every *kos*, a baked-brick well at every two *kos*, the number uncertain in the printed text, and two rows of trees (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 136).

The Sikh Maharaja's Lahore is the setting of the rebuilding. Khalid writes that "When Maharaja Ranjit Singh became the sovereign of Punjab at the end of the eighteenth century he spent large amounts of money on the renovation and expansion of many gurdwaras", and "attached vast tracts of land to these gurdwaras, so that the revenues from the lands could facilitate their functioning" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 34, folio 16). In 1799 Ranjit Singh "was greeted enthusiastically by a population sick of the lawlessness of that time"; he built a mosque at a *mohalla* called Gul Begum in Lahore; and Fakir Azizudin's "family still lives in Lahore in their ancestral house, located inside the walled city" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 207, folio not stated). Kanhaiya Lal writes that under Ranjit Singh's rule the whole region of the Punjab came under him (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 112, folio 115).

Of the city's Sikh buildings Khalid reports the historian Iqbal Qaiser's view, given in a lecture on Sikh architecture in Lahore: "like the Sikh religion, which picks up from both Hinduism and Islam, Sikh architecture draws inspiration from both Hindu and Muslim architecture"; domes "initially part of the Muslim buildings" were made smaller by the Sikhs, and Sikh shrines carry "graphic depictions of Sikh Gurus and also sometimes Hindu deities (in older buildings)" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 309–310, folio not stated / folio 284).

Of the city now Khalid writes that "it is now rare that one runs into a Sikh in this city, a city which was once ruled by a Sikh and the seat of their political strength" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 196, folio not stated). Of the province, he writes that without one family's care a temple elsewhere "would have also been taken over by the refugees and converted into a household, like the hundreds of temples and Gurdwaras all over Pakistani Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 58, folio not stated), and, of the country at large, "I have seen several gurdwaras and temples being taken over and destroyed by the migrants" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated). None of these sentences names this gurdwara.

Kanhaiya Lal's history is itself part of the city's memory of its families. The scholar Shemeem Burney Abbas, who traces her maternal ancestry to the old walled city of Lahore, writes that "Mufti Ghulam Sarwar, one of my ancestors, is mentioned together with my paternal grandfather, Mufti Muzaffar Din, in Kanhyalal's history of Lahore" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 193, folio not stated).

## In Verse

No verse by or about Guru Arjan or the baoli is quoted in the books consulted. Bhandari records only that the Guru recited to the emperor the Hindi verses of Baba Nanak, without giving them (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 164). The verse the books do carry speaks of Lahore and of the Punjab.

Bhandari sets one Persian couplet at the fort of Lahore. As the emperor set out on his second journey to Kashmir, crossing the Ravi below the fort, he asked about whom this verse had been written (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 179):

کلاه خسروی و تاج شاہی
بہر گل کی رسد حاشا و کلا

The book gives the question and not the answer, and the couplet is not translated in the sources consulted.

The site's account sets the loss of the baoli in the turbulence of the eighteenth century. The poets of that century in the Punjab wrote of its ruin. Bulleh Shah, in Christopher Shackle's translation:

*The twelfth century has come with gaping mouth. The door of doom and torment has opened, and Panjab is in a bad state. It has been struck by fear of the pit of hell.* (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 52, folio 27)

Shackle explains that the twelfth century of the Hijri era corresponds to the eighteenth century of the common era, and that the poem's allusive verses appear to refer to a separation of Bulleh Shah from his master "due to the troubled conditions then prevailing in Panjab" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, pp. 165–166, folios 140–141). In Taufiq Rafat's translation of another poem:

*Now hell has opened its bogs. / Punjab has gone to the dogs. / This hell is the nethermost hell.* (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 149, folio 129)

Rafat writes that "The full bitterness of Bulleh Shah’s ‘Punjab has gone to the dogs’ will only come through against this backdrop" of a province "being persistently rent by petty chiefs and marauders" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 16, folio xvi). Waris Shah, who dates his romance of Hir and Ranjha in the year 1180 of the Hijra, closes it with a lament for the land, in Charles Frederick Usborne's English:

*Of all the wretched Punjab I am most concerned for Kasur.* (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 56, folio 55)

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **How the baoli was founded.** The site's account has Bhai Chhajju Bhagat and a Pathan merchant lay a purse of gold *mohars* at the Guru's feet, to be spent for religious purposes; Qaiser, as the site's account reports him, has a dispute resolved before the Guru; Majid Sheikh puts the purse at 282 gold *mohars*. Kanhaiya Lal has a treasure found under a house that a Sikh of Guru Arjan sold to a woman for three hundred rupees, a case taken to the Qazi and the emperor, and the treasure sent by the emperor to the Guru, who resolved to build a baoli with it (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 110–111, folios 113–114).
- **When it was founded.** The site's account dates the Guru's visit to 1599, and Qaiser, as it reports him, resolves the dispute in Samvat 1616. Kanhaiya Lal gives the baoli, as report, to the time of the Emperor Jahangir (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 110, folio 113).
- **When and how the baoli was lost.** The site's account has it filled in and built over in the eighteenth century, under the governorship of Nawab Yahya Khan at the instigation of Diwan Jaspat Rai. The 1962 memoir, as the site's account reports it, has a mosque on the site between the era of the Gurus and Ranjit Singh's reign. Kanhaiya Lal has it lost under Shah Jahan, when the Guru's Lahore property passed to the Qazi, who built a mosque on the site of the *langar*, killed its five guards, threw them into the well and built houses over it (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 112, folio 115).
- **How it was recovered.** Majid Sheikh and the 1962 memoir, as the site's account reports them, have Ranjit Singh's astrologers advise him, in his illness, to bathe in the baoli's water. Kanhaiya Lal has an aged Sikh dream that Guru Arjan told him where the baoli lay, and records in the same breath that some say the Guru appeared in the Maharaja's own dream (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 112, folio 115).
- **The date of the rebuilding.** The site's account has Ranjit Singh raise a new building and dig a *sarovar* in 1834. Kanhaiya Lal prints the year of the Maharaja's illness and the dream as Bikrami 1896, the start of the building as Bikrami 1890, both readings uncertain in the printed text, and says it was built slowly over five years and left unfinished at the Maharaja's death (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 112, folio 115; p. 113, folio 116).
- **Where the baoli stands.** The site's account places it in Dabbi Bazaar, its precinct adjoining the Loha, Kesera and Guru bazaars. Kanhaiya Lal's first account places it among bazaars with the iron-sellers' bazaar to the east, a bazaar whose name is uncertain in the printed text to the north, and the Kasera bazaar to the south (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 108, folio 111); his second places a house of a baoli adjoining the Sunehri Masjid in Kashmiri Bazaar (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 147, folio 150), and in that passage names no quarter for Baoli Sahib at all (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 148, folio 151).
- **Guru Arjan's dates.** *Walking with Nanak* gives 1563–1606 (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 315, folio 289), as does *A White Trail* (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 195, folio not stated); Alam prints 1563–1605 (Alam, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 186, folio 186). Khalid and Dhillon give his years as Guru as 1581 to 1606 (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 315, folio 289; Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 240, folio 265); Bhandari has him die twenty-five years after succeeding his father (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).
- **His place in the line.** Khalid calls him the fifth Sikh Guru throughout *A White Trail* (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 198, folio not stated), but once "the fourth Sikh Guru, Guru Arjan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 168, folio not stated).
- **Why he died.** Sikh tradition, as Khalid reports it, lays his death to the connivance of Chandu Lal, Diwan of Lahore, allied with Prithi Chand (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 253, folio 227), or to one Chandu Shah, whose daughter Guru Arjan's son was refused in marriage (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 315, folio 289). Jahangir's memoir, as Khalid reads it, says he was punished for his sympathies with the rebel prince Khusro (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 255, folio 229).
- **What became of his body.** *Walking with Nanak* has it thrown into the river, and reports the Sikh belief that he went into the Ravi and did not emerge (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 253, folio 227); *A White Trail* says that he was assassinated in Lahore "and his remains interred" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated).

## Legacy

The baoli's later history is one of decline. By 2015 the baoli itself had been reduced to "a mere small opening in an open space," with only a few traditional healers still drawing water from it as the water table fell, and by 2018 the same reporter, Majid Sheikh, separately flagged the demolition of the gurdwara structure itself as an unaddressed grievance. Kanhaiya Lal had already found, in 1884, the courtyard tank long dry and the wheel that fed it from the well no longer turning, while the well itself still held water at the foot of its two long flights of steps, behind its door of red stone (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 110, folio 113).

What remains is the memory the books carry: of a well sunk by the fifth Guru in the city where he would die, of a *langar* held beside it, of its loss and of a Maharaja's dream that brought it back. As a shrine tied to Guru Arjan — the compiler of the *Adi Granth* and the first martyr of the Sikh faith — Baoli Sahib keeps its place on the map of Sikh Lahore, beside Gurdwara Dera Sahib by the Fort.
