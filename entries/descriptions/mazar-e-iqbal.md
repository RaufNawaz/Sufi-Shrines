## Overview

Mazar-e-Iqbal is the tomb of Allama Muhammad Iqbal (1877–1938) — poet, philosopher, jurist, and the spiritual visionary honoured across Pakistan as its ideological father, the Mufakkir-e-Pakistan. It stands in the Hazuri Bagh, the walled garden that lies between the great Badshahi Mosque and the Lahore Fort, in the monumental heart of the old city and within sight of the Minar-e-Pakistan. Though it is not a Sufi shrine in the traditional sense but the resting place of a modern thinker, Pakistanis approach it as a place of pilgrimage and national homage: visitors come to recite the Fatiha, to honour the man whose verse and vision gave the Muslims of South Asia the idea of a homeland of their own, and to stand at one of the most revered sites in the country. To understand the tomb is, above all, to understand the life, the poetry, and the thought of the man who lies within it — and so the account below begins with the poet himself before turning to the monument, its rituals, and its place in the life of the nation.

The books consulted for this article speak of Iqbal as a poet and a thinker, and never of his tomb. Annemarie Schimmel's *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* is the only one of them to say where he lies, and it does so in a parenthesis, in a passage about another place: Iqbal, it notes, "is buried in Lahore" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 270, folio 239). None of them describes the mausoleum, its builders or its ceremonies. What they give instead is a portrait of the man: of his verse, of his devotion to the Prophet, of his quarrel with the Sufi masters of his own day and his debt to those of the past, and of the scholars and translators who carried his work abroad. That portrait is set out below beside the site's own account, and where the two differ, both are given.

## Birth and Boyhood in Sialkot

Muhammad Iqbal was born on 9 November 1877 in the city of Sialkot, in the Punjab, into a family descended from Kashmiri Brahmins who had embraced Islam some generations before and settled in the plains — a descent the tomb's records still register, in the old style, as "Sheikh/Brahman." His father, Sheikh Noor Muhammad, was a tailor of modest means but of unusual spiritual depth — a man remembered for his piety and his love of the Qur'an. Devotional tradition preserves a story that before Iqbal's birth his father dreamed of a radiant white bird descending from the sky, sought after by many hands, that at last settled in his own lap; he woke convinced that a remarkable child was coming to his house. It was Sheikh Noor Muhammad who first shaped his son's inner life, urging him to recite the holy book slowly and with attention, as though it had been revealed for him to understand — a counsel that left in Iqbal a lifelong intimacy with the Qur'an.

Near the family home the boy was placed in the care of the learned Sayyid Mir Hassan, an imam and teacher of rare gifts, who taught him Arabic, Persian, and Urdu and quickened in him a hunger to understand rather than merely to memorise. Recognising the extraordinary promise of his pupil, Mir Hassan guided him onward to the Scotch Mission (Christian) High School in Sialkot, where the young Iqbal is said to have completed two classes in a single year. It was in these years, too, that — at his elders' wish and against his own inclination — he was married as a boy of about sixteen to Karim Bibi, of a family from Gujrat. The marriage, entered so young, would not endure happily, but it set the pattern of a life in which Iqbal's deepest energies were always drawn away from domestic settledness toward learning, poetry, and the cause of his people.

Of these early years the books consulted say little. Michel Boivin prints the poet's dates as 1877–1938, as the site's account does (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 102, folio 89), but his explanatory note describes him as "a poet born in Lahore" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 212, folio 199); the difference is set out under Where the Sources Disagree. Schimmel's *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* prints his death year, 1938, three times (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 107, folio 76; p. 148, folio 117; p. 518, folio 487) and his birth year nowhere, speaking of him only as a thinker 'in our day', 'in the twentieth century', 'a modern thinker' (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, pp. 170, 186, 231, 337, 518).

## Lahore, Europe, and the Making of a Scholar

From Sialkot, Iqbal moved to Lahore to study at Government College, then the foremost seat of learning in the province. There he read philosophy and English literature and came under the influence of the Orientalist Sir Thomas Arnold, a teacher whose learning and friendship shaped him profoundly and who later drew him toward Europe. Even as a student Iqbal's poetry began to circulate, and when some of his verses were shown to the celebrated Delhi master Dagh Dehlvi, that connoisseur of Urdu is said to have marvelled that whoever had written them was no ordinary poet. After taking his degree Iqbal joined the Oriental College, Lahore, in 1899 as a reader in Arabic and taught philosophy, already known in the literary gatherings (mushairas) of the city for verse of unusual power. From this period, too, dates his long association with his devoted household servant Ali Baksh, who served him faithfully to the end and who, after the poet's death, would relate the private wonders of his master's life.

In 1905 Iqbal sailed for Europe, where he spent three transforming years. He read philosophy at Trinity College, Cambridge; he qualified as a barrister at Lincoln's Inn in London; and he took a doctorate at the University of Munich with a dissertation on *The Development of Metaphysics in Persia*. In Europe he mastered Western philosophy at first hand — encountering the currents of thought associated with Nietzsche, Bergson, and others — and he saw, too, the spiritual emptiness he believed lay beneath the material power of the modern West. He returned to India in 1908 with his mastery of two civilisations deepened and with a settled conviction: that the Muslims of the world had fallen from greatness through the loss of their own inner vitality, and that they must be reawakened — not by imitating Europe, but by recovering the dynamism, faith, and selfhood of their own tradition.

Boivin's note records the doctorate in the same terms: "Muhammad Iqbal was a poet born in Lahore, who was granted a PhD by the University of Munich" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 212, folio 199, Ch. 3 n. 14). The dissertation itself appears in two of the bibliographies consulted, with different details. Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi lists "The development of metaphysics in Persia, London, 1908." (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 452, folio 428). Schimmel's *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* lists it as *The Development of Metaphysics in Iran*, published at Cambridge in 1908 (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 481, folio 450).

## Career, Family, and Public Life

Back in Lahore, Iqbal took up the practice of law at the bar, and taught for a time, but he was candid that the law was for him a means and not an end: he practised, he said, only so far as it secured his independence and left him free to serve his nation with his true instruments, which were poetry and thought. His domestic life settled into a new pattern after the estrangement of his first marriage: he married Sardar Begum and Mukhtar Begum in the years around 1910–1913, and became the father of children — among them his son Javid, to whom the great Persian poem *Javid Nama* is addressed — whom he raised with tender care. In 1922 the British Crown knighted him, and he became "Sir Muhammad Iqbal," though the title sat lightly on a man who lived simply and counted his books and his verse his truest companions.

Iqbal did not hold himself aloof from public affairs. He served as a member of the Punjab Legislative Council and took part in the Round Table Conferences convened in London to debate the constitutional future of India, lending to practical politics the moral authority of a thinker whose deepest labour lay elsewhere. His boldness of thought sometimes drew fire: when, in his lectures and addresses, he argued that the inherited interpretation of Islamic law must be rethought for the modern age, some among the orthodox turned against him and issued condemnations — a controversy that still attaches to his English lectures, *The Reconstruction of Religious Thought in Islam*. Through his last years his health declined, and in his final illness he is said to have refused heavy treatment, explaining that he did not wish to be dulled into unconsciousness but to meet death fully aware. He died in Lahore on 21 April 1938 — the date given in the standard biographies and observed nationally, though the tomb's own records preserve a variant date of 21 September, a discrepancy that remains unreconciled. To his people he is known by a constellation of titles that between them capture the range of the man: Shair-e-Mashriq, the Poet of the East; Hakeem-ul-Ummat, the Sage of the Community; and Mufakkir-e-Pakistan, the Thinker of Pakistan.

The title the Crown conferred stayed with him in the scholarship. Carl Ernst and Bruce Lawrence speak of "the great twentieth-century modernist Sir Muhammad Iqbal" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 157, folio 145), and Schimmel heads him 'Iqbal, Sir Muhammad' in her bibliography to *As Through a Veil* (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 325, folio 307) and 'Iqbal, Sir Muhammad (d. 1938)' in the index to *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 518, folio 487).

He could also bestow a title. Of the writer Khwaja Hasan Nizami, Ernst and Lawrence write: "Some of his first articles were published under the name Sayyid Muhammad 'Ali Hasan Nizami, but a newspaper editor in Amritsar published one of his pieces simply under the name Hasan Nizami; then the poet Muhammad Iqbal started to call him Khwaja" ('Master') Hasan Nizami, "a title with ancient resonance in the Chishti tradition" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 128, folio 115).

## The Poet of the East

Iqbal is, before all else, one of the supreme poets of the Urdu and Persian languages, and his verse is the true foundation of his fame. His Urdu collections — *Bang-e-Dara* ("The Call of the Marching Bell," 1924), *Bal-e-Jibril* ("Gabriel's Wing," 1935), and *Zarb-e-Kalim* ("The Rod of Moses," 1936) — stand beside his great Persian works, among them *Asrar-e-Khudi* ("The Secrets of the Self"), *Rumuz-e-Bekhudi* ("The Mysteries of Selflessness"), *Payam-e-Mashriq* ("The Message of the East"), and the visionary *Javid Nama*, in which the poet ascends through the heavens in the company of Rumi. Together these are gathered for readers as the *Kulliyat-e-Iqbal*, the collected works, and it is from a copy of this collection that his poetry is most widely read today. His verse ranges from the tenderest devotion to the most demanding philosophy, and its lines are known by heart, quoted in ordinary speech, and sung across the subcontinent.

Schimmel's bibliography in *As Through a Veil* lists the Lahore printings of the poems with their years: the *Asrar-e-Khudi* in 1915, the *Rumuz-e-Bekhudi* in 1917, the *Payam-e-Mashriq* in 1923, the *Bang-e-Dara* in 1924, the Persian *Zabūr-i ʿAjam* in 1927, the *Javid Nama* in 1932, *Musafir* in 1934, the *Bal-e-Jibril* in 1935, *Pas che bāyad kard* in 1936 and the *Armaghān-i Ḥijāz* in 1938 (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 325, folio 307; p. 326, folio 308). In *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* she writes of "his poetical works, which appeared from 1915 until 1935" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 354, folio 323). The title of his Urdu diwan, she explains, names the part he took for himself: he "describes his own role as that of the caravan bell that helps guide the travelers to the central sanctuary in Mecca", as the title *Bang-e-Dara* indicates (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 226, folio 208).

His name closes the very title of Schimmel's history of Urdu letters, which Pnina Werbner and Helene Basu's bibliography prints as *Classical Urdu Literature from the Beginning to Iqbal* (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 218, folio 207) and her own as *Classical Urdu Literature from the Beginnings to Iqbal*, published in Jan Gonda's *History of Indian Literature* (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 337, folio 319).

From his early period comes the children's morning prayer that generations have sung at school assembly:

لب پہ آتی ہے دعا بن کے تمنا میری
زندگی شمع کی صورت ہو خدایا میری

*My longing comes to my lips as a prayer: O God, may my life be like a candle — that it may give light to the world.*

From the same early collection comes the anthem that first made his name a household word, *Tarana-e-Hindi*:

سارے جہاں سے اچھا ہندوستاں ہمارا
ہم بلبلیں ہیں اس کی، یہ گلستاں ہمارا

*Better than the entire world is our land; we are its nightingales, and it is our garden.*

And in a famous ghazal of *Bang-e-Dara* the longing that runs through all his work — the yearning of the servant for the vision of God — finds one of its most quoted forms:

کبھی اے حقیقتِ منتظر نظر آ لباسِ مجاز میں
کہ ہزاروں سجدے تڑپ رہے ہیں مری جبینِ نیاز میں

*O awaited Reality, appear but once in the garb of the material world; for a thousand prostrations writhe in longing upon my brow of supplication.*

## From the Homeland to the Ummah

Iqbal's vision widened over time from the love of the homeland to the love of the whole Muslim community, and finally to a summons addressed to all humanity before God. In *Bang-e-Dara* the long and daring poem *Shikwa* ("The Complaint") gives voice to a believer's plaintive reproach to God at the fallen state of the Muslims, and its companion *Jawab-e-Shikwa* ("The Answer to the Complaint") supplies the divine reply — not consolation but a challenge, calling the community to make itself worthy of its inheritance once more. Recited from public platforms, these poems electrified their hearers and remain among the most famous in the language. The same summons rings through *Tulu-e-Islam* ("The Dawn of Islam"), whose lines became a kind of watchword for Muslim revival:

یقیں محکم، عمل پیہم، محبت فاتحِ عالم
جہادِ زندگانی میں ہیں یہ مردوں کی شمشیریں

*Firm certainty, ceaseless action, and a love that conquers the world — these are the swords of true men in the struggle of life.*

The *Shikwa* tested its translators. Taufiq Rafat, recalling his apprenticeship as a translator, writes that he had once translated five or six short Urdu poems by contemporaries as an assignment, and "had made an aborted effort at rendering Iqbal's" *Shikwa* and *Jawab-i-Shikwa*: "That was the sum total of my experience" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 12, folio xii).

In poems such as *Khizr-e-Rah* ("Khizr, the Guide of the Way") he turned the same searching gaze on the poverty and bondage of his age, on labour and capital and empire, insisting that the message of faith was also a demand for justice in the world of men.

Schimmel notes the same concern in his use of an old figure: Farhad, the luckless lover, has become in modern progressive writing "from Iqbal to Nazim Hikmet the representative of the oppressed working class" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 253, folio 235). Another old figure, Sultan Mahmud, transformed into the ideal lover, the king who "finally became" 'the slave of his slave', remains, she notes, a topic of Iqbal's poetry (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 84, folio 74).

## The Philosophy of Khudi

At the centre of Iqbal's thought lies the idea of *khudi* — "selfhood," or the realisation of the ego — which he expounded first in the Persian *Asrar-e-Khudi* and its companion *Rumuz-e-Bekhudi*. By khudi Iqbal meant not selfishness but the awakening, disciplining, and strengthening of the individual self through love, action, and nearness to God, until the human being becomes a conscious co-worker in the unfolding of creation. Against what he saw as the passivity and world-weariness of a decayed mysticism — a Sufism that counselled the annihilation of the self — and against the soulless materialism he had observed in the modern West, Iqbal preached striving, self-mastery, and the forging of the *mard-e-momin*, the complete believer who unites inward faith with outward power. He took the great Persian master Jalaluddin Rumi as his guide and cast much of his profoundest thought in Persian so that it might address the entire Muslim world. His most famous couplet fixes the height to which he believed the self could rise:

خودی کو کر بلند اتنا کہ ہر تقدیر سے پہلے
خدا بندے سے خود پوچھے، بتا تیری رضا کیا ہے

*Raise your selfhood so high that, before issuing each decree, God Himself asks His servant: 'Tell me — what is it that you wish?'*

And of that complete believer, in whom the human will becomes an instrument of the divine, he wrote in *Bal-e-Jibril*:

ہاتھ ہے اللہ کا بندۂ مومن کا ہاتھ
غالب و کار آفریں، کار کشا، کار ساز

*The hand of the true believer is the hand of God — triumphant and creative, resolving all difficulty, accomplishing all things.*

Schimmel's studies give the doctrine its setting in the older vocabulary of the mystics. In our time, she writes, "it was Muhammad Iqbal, who never tired of describing the long way of development from a drop to the ocean, from sperm to man, and eventually to the Perfect Man, the crown and apex of creation" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 337, folio 306). Writing of Shabistari's *Gulshan-i raz*, she says: "Its form and content inspired Muhammad Iqbal to compose his Gulshan-i raz-i jadid," 'The New Rose Garden of Mystery', "published in 1927; in this poem he follows Shabistari's notions and offers his own solutions to the questions of the Perfect Man (one of his central ideas), of God, and of destiny" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 312, folio 281). In *As Through a Veil* she calls the same poem an interesting modern response to Shabistari in the 1920s, which Iqbal appended to his Persian diwan *Zabūr-i ʿAjam* (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 247, folio 229).

In her study of Mir Dard, Schimmel sets Iqbal's reading of the Quranic trust against Dard's. Dard takes the trust to be speech, "not, as many mystics did, as love, or as the capacity of choosing between good and evil, nor, as Iqbal held, as the possibility of individuation" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 110, folio 108).

Of predestination, Schimmel explains that the *jabr mahmud*, the 'higher predestination' as Iqbal calls it, can be achieved not simply by accepting a given situation "but by actively conforming with God's will through love and prayer" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 229, folio 198; p. 230, folio 199). A page earlier the same book glosses the phrase, without reference to him, as 'agreeable constraint' (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 228, folio 197).

Schimmel reads his best-known slogan in a mystical light: "the claim of a modern thinker like Iqbal that he would" 'call a people to spiritual resurrection' "gains special significance; he assumes, in a sense," the role of the *qutb* (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 231, folio 200; p. 233, folio 202). "In fact, the three angelic figures Sarosh, Israfil, and Gabriel occur very frequently in his poetry." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 233, folio 202).

## The Eagle and the Call to Action

If any single image belongs to Iqbal, it is the *shaheen* — the eagle or royal falcon — his emblem of the free, austere, self-reliant spirit that scorns comfort, keeps no store, and makes its home upon the heights. Against every temptation to ease and every limit set upon human aspiration, he summoned the young especially to a life of soaring effort:

نہیں تیرا نشیمن قصرِ سلطانی کے گنبد پر
تو شاہیں ہے، بسیرا کر پہاڑوں کی چٹانوں میں

*Your nest is not on the dome of the royal palace; you are an eagle — make your dwelling in the crags of the mountains.*

ستاروں سے آگے جہاں اور بھی ہیں
ابھی عشق کے امتحاں اور بھی ہیں

*Beyond the stars there are yet other worlds; there are still more trials of love to be met.*

Nowhere is his union of art and thought more complete than in *Masjid-e-Qurtuba* ("The Mosque of Córdoba"), composed after he visited the great mosque in Spain — a sustained meditation on time, love, and the deathless work of the man of God, and widely held to be among the finest poems in the Urdu language.

Another of his emblems is the tulip. "In Iqbal's work," Schimmel writes, the tulip, *lāla*, "represents the independent, vigorous plant that appears in the middle of the desert, resembling in its shape and color the flames which Moses saw on Mt. Sinai; it is seen as a positive contrast to the traditional rose which lives in well-trimmed gardens, and thus becomes a symbol of man's striving against heavy odds to realize himself" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, pp. 253–254, folios 235–236).

## In Praise of the Prophet

Schimmel, who gives his devotion to the Prophet the fullest of her treatments of Iqbal, introduces him as "a modern Muslim poet who has most eloquently praised Muhammad although he has never written a formal" *naʿt* "in traditional style" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 226, folio 208). She sets him beside "a medieval Turkish folk poet" as a modern instance of the same devotion, within her argument that "the very name of the Prophet was often praised for its baraka" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 192, folio 174). In the *Asrar-e-Khudi*, written in 1915, he claims:

*All our glory is from the name of Muhammad* (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 192, folio 174)

A few years earlier he had sung in *Jawab-e-Shikwa*:

*Light the world, too long in darkness, / With Muhammad's radiant name.* (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 192, folio 174)

Even his early Urdu poems, such as *Jawab-e-Shikwa*, "contain verses in praise of the Prophet" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 226, folio 208). In the *Asrar-e-Khudi*, "his first revolutionary Persian mathnawi", "he has devoted some of his finest lines to Muhammad"; in the *Rumuz-e-Bekhudi*, "the poetical collection which translates Iqbal's sociopolitical ideals into Persian verse", the Prophet appears "as the lamp in the darkness of existence" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 227, folio 209). His last verses are devoted to the Hijaz, "the country which he never visited" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 227, folio 209), and among them stands the line:

*For us Mustafa is enough* (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 227, folio 209)

In the *Javid Nama* he had gone even further, and there "the daring line appears":

*You can deny God, but you cannot deny the Prophet!* (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 227, folio 209)

Schimmel quotes the line again in *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, where "Muhammad Iqbal stressed the importance of the Prophet in his daring statement", and traces it to verse 608 of the *Javid Nama* (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 258, folio 227).

For Iqbal the Prophet was a leader in this world as well as the next. "Iqbal sees Muhammad as the political leader who is able to guide people toward their destiny even in this world", Schimmel writes, a view reflected "in the mirror of the complaint of Abu Jahl, one of his grimmest enemies, who defends earthrootedness, narrow patriotism and distinction of races and castes" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 228, folio 210). He defines the Prophet's 'more-than-human state' in a passage of the *Javid Nama*, "in a poem which he puts in the mouth of Hallaj, whose hymn in honor of the Prophet forms the actual beginning of mystical prophetology", a poem turning on the epithet *ʿabduhu*, 'His slave' (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 228, folio 210). Her summary: "Iqbal's prophetology combines elements of both a modern and a traditional world view: Muhammad is both the political leader of his community and the scent of the rosa mystica" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 229, folio 211).

One episode of his last years belongs here, and Schimmel takes it from Iqbal's own letter. 'The Arabian friend', as he calls the Prophet, like many Indian Muslim poets before him, "is praised in a poem which Iqbal wrote in 1936 in the hope of being cured from an ailment, as he tells in a letter. He had been advised to write the poem by a dream apparition of Sir Sayyid Ahmad Khan: the healing quality of the Burda is remembered" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 226, folio 208). Of al-Būṣīrī's *Burda* itself, Schimmel notes elsewhere, writing of the city and not of the poet: "Copies are still being sold in the shops of Lahore and elsewhere." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 256, folio 225).

## The Reconstruction of Religious Thought

Iqbal set out his ideas not in verse alone but in searching prose. His English lectures, gathered as *The Reconstruction of Religious Thought in Islam*, remain among the most ambitious modern attempts to rethink Islamic philosophy in dialogue with modern science, evolutionary biology, and Western metaphysics. In them he argued for the reopening of *ijtihad* — independent reasoning in the interpretation of Islamic law — so that the community might meet the demands of a changing world without losing its spiritual core; he reflected on the nature of the self, of time, of prayer, and of religious experience. It was these bold arguments that drew the censure of some traditional scholars in his lifetime, and the book continues to be studied, taught, and debated wherever the future of Islamic thought is discussed.

The books consulted cite the lectures in several printings. Schimmel lists them as *Six Lectures on the Reconstruction of Religious Thought in Islam*, Lahore, 1930 (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 481, folio 450), and, in *As Through a Veil*, as published at Lahore by Ashraf, "1930 and often" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 325, folio 307). Robert Rozehnal recommends them in the Lahore edition of Sh. Muhammad Ashraf dated 1934/1982: "For the clearest exposition of Muhammad Iqbal's thought, see his classic The Reconstruction of Religious Thought in Islam" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 251, folio 244; p. 270, folio 264). Rizvi's bibliography gives "The reconstruction of religious thought in Islam, rep. Lahore, 1944." (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 452, folio 428).

Schimmel finds in the lectures a debt to the medieval saints. The difference between what Nathan Söderblom and Friedrich Heiler defined as 'mystical' and 'prophetic' religiosity, she writes, "has been very clearly understood and expressed by the saints of medieval Islam, whom Iqbal follows in the fifth chapter of his Reconstruction of Religious Thought in Islam" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 235, folio 204). In *As Through a Veil* she shows where he found it. Against ʿAbdul Quddūs Gangohī's saying that the true mystic "would never like to return", she writes: "In this remark about Muhammad's ascension and his return in order to work in the world, a modernist thinker like Muhammad Iqbal found the difference between 'mystical' and 'prophetical' religion expressed in a nutshell" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 20, folio 10).

## Iqbal, Rumi, and the West

Iqbal's thought was forged in a lifelong dialogue between two worlds. His spiritual master across the centuries was Jalaluddin Rumi, the thirteenth-century Persian mystic, whom he took as his guide in the fullest sense: in the *Javid Nama*, Iqbal's great Persian poem of a journey through the heavens, it is Rumi who leads the poet — as Virgil led Dante — through the spheres and into the presence of the divine. From Rumi, Iqbal drew his conviction that love (*ishq*) is the engine of the self and of the cosmos, a force higher than mere intellect.

Yet Iqbal had also mastered European philosophy at first hand, and much of his originality lies in how he answered it. He admired the dynamism of Western thought — its science, its energy, its worship of will and action — while rejecting what he saw as its spiritual bankruptcy. Nietzsche's vision of the *Übermensch*, the superman who creates his own values in a world without God, fascinated and repelled him: Iqbal's own ideal, the *mard-e-momin*, is likewise a figure of immense will and self-affirmation, but one whose power is surrendered to God and disciplined by love, not a lonely idol of the self. From Bergson he took a vivid sense of time as living duration and of the universe as an unfinished, creative becoming, which he wove into his dynamic understanding of *khudi*. And he turned a fierce moral gaze on the political forms of the modern West — on a narrow territorial nationalism that set brother against brother, and on an imperial capitalism that ground down the poor and the colonised. His counsel to the East was neither to imitate Europe nor to shun it, but to take its fire while keeping its own soul: to marry action to faith, and knowledge to love, so that the reawakened Muslim might be at once modern and whole.

Schimmel traces how the bond with Rumi was formed. She counts Iqbal among the readers who take Rumi's 'I died as mineral' passage as a statement of evolution, a reading that reached him through the Indian scholar Maulana Shibli, "in his Urdu biography of Rumi (1903), which deeply influenced Iqbal's ideas" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 353, folio 322). "In the Muslim world, Muhammad Iqbal gave this idea of constant development a new interpretation in his poetical works" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 354, folio 323). "Iqbal was deeply influenced by Maulana and inserted many of his lines into his own works", chiefly into his *mathnawis*, which he wrote in the same metre as his master's *Mathnawi* (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 358, folio 327). Her verdict: "Muhammad Iqbal, no doubt, understood best the dynamic force behind Rumi's experiences, though he highlights only one of the numerous aspects of the great lover", and "in many respects his interpretation appeals more to the modern mind than many of the traditional interpretations" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 359, folio 328).

In *As Through a Veil* Schimmel quotes Rumi on Intellect and Love:

*When Intellect looks still for a camel to perform the pilgrimage, / Love has already reached the hill Safa* (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 273, folio 255)

These are "ideas which Iqbal has taken up in his lyrical" poetry, she writes, "particularly in the Payam-i Mashriq" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 274, folio 256). In the *Payam-e-Mashriq*, too, stands "the poem which contrasts Avicenna and Rumi", with "the bookworm that dwells in Ibn Sina's pages and therefore lacks real experience of life and love" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 250, folio 232). Avicenna, condemned by Sana'i as "the paragon of love-less intellectualism", "is regarded this way in Persian verse to our day (e.g., in Iqbal's work)" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 77, folio 67).

Of the ideal at the centre of his thought, Schimmel writes that "Iqbal's ideas of the Perfect Man as he who has developed his individual possibilities to the fullest bloom are derived from these theories rather than from the Nietzschean superman" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 304, folio 273). In the same book she observes that Mir Dard in the eighteenth century equated God with light, "an idea that finds a faint echo in some sentences of Iqbal as well" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 294, folio 263).

## The Javid Nama

The *Javid Nama*, which the site's account calls visionary, is for Schimmel his 'most intriguing literary work': "In the twentieth century, Muhammad Iqbal has chosen the type of the heavenly journey in the company of the spiritual preceptor (in his case the spirit of Rumi) for his most intriguing literary work" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 250, folio 219). Her bibliographies date it to Lahore, 1932 (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 481, folio 450; Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 326, folio 308).

The poem is built from the old materials of the mystics. Of the Prophet's saying "I have a time with God in which no created being has access, not even Gabriel who is pure spirit.", Schimmel writes: "Iqbal, in his modern interpretation of mystical symbols, understands this tradition as a reference to the moment at which" 'the infidel's girdle', namely 'serial' time, "is torn and the mystic establishes direct contact with God in a person-to-person encounter" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 251, folio 220). Of Hallaj's *Tawasin*, Schimmel writes that "the title has been imitated, in our time, by Muhammad Iqbal, who in his account of his spiritual journey", the *Javid Nama*, invented the 'tawasin of the Prophets', "dwelling places of the prophetic spirits in the Sphere of Moon" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 448, folio 417). A section of the poem bears the title 'Tāsīn of Muhammad' (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 309, folio 291). Writing of Bayezid, Schimmel notes that Iqbal took over Rumi's story of the Zoroastrian in the *Javid Nama*, of 1932, "where it serves to assert the spiritual strength of the Islamic religion and the true Muslims" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 81, folio 50). And in the poem's scene in Jupiter-Heaven, Schimmel writes, "Iqbal has even treated Hallaj as a sort of medieval forerunner of himself and has emphasized his dynamic concept of love and faith as an ideal for every free Muslim" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 107, folio 76).

The poem travelled far. Schimmel's bibliography records its translations: into English by Shaikh Mahmud Ahmad (Lahore, 1961) and by A. J. Arberry (London, 1966), into Italian by Alessandro Bausani in 1955, into French by Eva Meyerovitch in 1962, and by Schimmel herself into German verse in 1957 and Turkish prose in 1958 (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 481, folio 450). In *As Through a Veil* she quotes it in Arberry's translation (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 309, folio 291).

## Iqbal and the Sufis

The site's account sets Iqbal against "a decayed mysticism". The scholars of Sufism consulted here draw a more divided picture, and the quarrel comes first. Schimmel writes that Hijazi "has put his finger on the danger of the exaggerated veneration of the spiritual master", the *shaykh* or *pir*, "what Muhammad Iqbal has called" 'pirism', "which means the absolute sway of the leader over his followers and the attendant exploitation of ignorant peasants and villagers" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 53, folio 22). She returns to it: "though mystically minded, a modernist like Iqbal considered Pirism one of the most dangerous aspects of Islam, a wall that closes off large groups of Muslims from a new and vital interpretation of Islamic values" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 269, folio 238), a remark she sets directly beside Atatürk's abolition of the dervish orders in Turkey in 1925; and more than once, she writes, Iqbal attacked the 'Pirism' and backwardness of the so-called 'spiritual leaders' of the Indo-Pakistani Subcontinent (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, pp. 436–437, folios 405–406). In a chapter by Malik in Werbner and Basu's collection the word appears again: "Muslim modernists like Muhammad Iqbal have referred to these developments in ideological terms as pirism" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 200, folio 189).

He was as hard on a certain kind of verse. Of the poetry of *wahdat al-wujud*, with its imagery of indifference to ritual, Schimmel writes that "the sharp reaction of a modern critic like Iqbal to this kind of Sufi poetry, with its sweeping generalizations which seemed to leave no room for the truly Islamic values, can be understood and appreciated" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 178, folio 160). Hali's verdict on mystical poetry "was taken over and emphasized by Iqbal, who claimed that mystical poetry after Hafiz might prove utterly dangerous for the morals of a people"; yet he admitted in the *Javid Nama*, at lines 727–728:

*If the purpose of poetry is to form men, / Poetry is the heir of prophecy* (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 233, folio not stated)

Schimmel insists that the attack be read with its counterweight: "yet a close study of his work makes it clear that he himself follows quite closely the Sufi thought of the classical period" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 437, folio 406). As a thinker, she writes, Iqbal turned from a Hegelian and Ibn ʿArabi pantheism "to what seemed to him true Islam. In fact, he relied heavily upon the heritage of the great masters of early Sufism" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 437, folio 406). And "as much as he detested the kind of enthralling" 'otherworldly' "mysticism that he thought was hidden behind Hafiz's beautiful verses, he himself used the whole fabric of Sufi imagery in his poetry, though he often reinterpreted it" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 437, folio 406).

His debts were also literary. "Even a modern writer of completely different mental stature, Muhammad Iqbal, admits his indebtedness to Bedil's poetry for the formation of his thought", Schimmel writes in *Pain and Grace* (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 15, folio 11). In *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* he stands immediately after Bedil in a run of poets criticising Sufi gluttony: "Muhammad Iqbal (d. 1938) made the fake" *murid* explain that God, though "closer than the jugular vein" (Sura 50:16), "is not closer to man than his stomach" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 148, folio 117).

Hallaj stayed with him from his youth onward. "Muhammad Iqbal (d. 1938), who in his youth described the great mystic as a pantheist—as he had seen him through hundreds of Persian, Urdu, and Panjabi poems—later recognized Hallaj's strong personal religious commitment and saw in him one of the few who had attained to an experience of the divine higher than that of ordinary people." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 107, folio 76). "He recognized that Hallaj had called the slumbering Muslims to a personal realization of the truth, thus coming into conflict with the religious authorities who were afraid of any ardent witness for the living God." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 107, folio 76). Schimmel adds that "his treatment of Hallaj, with whose work he became acquainted through Louis Massignon's books, shows an extraordinary insight into the phenomenon of the enthusiastic mystic" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 437, folio 406). Michel Boivin, drawing on Schimmel, writes that Iqbal "was formerly a critic of Hallaj, but finally thought that ana al-haqq meant the 'Infinite entering into the loving embrace of the Finite'" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 102, folio 89). Hallaj's name, Schimmel writes in *As Through a Veil*, "looms large in high Persian and Urdu poetry" from ʿAttar to Ghalib and Iqbal (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 176, folio 158).

The Sufis' Satan, too, lives on in his work. "The manifold aspects of Satan developed throughout the centuries by the Sufis have been echoed, in our day, in the work of Muhammad Iqbal, whose multicolored picture of Satan is quite unusual." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 227, folio 196). "He sees in him the lover as well as the intellectual, the monotheist and the spirit of evil who longs to be overcome by the perfect man", faithful to the Prophetic tradition 'My shaytan has become Muslim', "and to be broken in order to find salvation. Thus, he will eventually prostrate himself before man, who proves stronger than he. On the other hand, Iqbal has also employed the more common image of Satan the seducer, the materialist, and the destroyer." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 227, folio 196). His Satan, she notes, sadly complains that it is much too easy for him to seduce people (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 224, folio 193; p. 225, folio 194), and he follows Rumi on the one-eyedness of Iblis (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 226, folio 195). In *Pain and Grace* she writes that the Hallajian satanology persisted "until they were once more brought into a fascinating system by Muhammad Iqbal" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 211, folio 211).

Prayer and longing are his themes too. "True prayer is constant. It cannot be limited to a number of rak'as and portions of the Koran, but permeates man's whole being—an idea that in our day has been expressed once more by Muhammad Iqbal, who follows Rumi's example." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 186, folio 155). These ideas "were taken up once more by Muhammad Iqbal, who often speaks of the miraculous power of true prayer, which ends in a complete conformity of the human to the divine will, in a change and sublimation of man's hopes and anguish" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 191, folio 160). Schimmel borrows a phrase from his *Zabūr-i ʿAjam*, "like us, a prisoner of Desire", citing the poem and A. J. Arberry's translation of it, *Persian Psalms* (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 170, folio 139). "Iqbal has interpreted the song of the nightingale in the context of his philosophy of unfulfilled union and longing—only longing gives the soul bird the capacity to sing", and "Longing is the highest state the soul can reach, for it results in creativity, whereas union brings about silence and annihilation." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 338, folio 307). In *Pain and Grace* she refers to what Iqbal, "following Ghazzali's interpretation of infinite longing, has proclaimed in our day in the Indo-Muslim world" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 179, folio 179).

## A Pilgrim's Plea

Ernst and Lawrence close their study of Chishti Sufism with a poem of Iqbal's. They thank Shantanu Phukan "for his superb translation of the Urdu poem by Iqbal that serves as a coda to the book" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 14, folio not stated), and the last note of their seventh chapter identifies it as 'Iltija-ye Musafir', from Iqbal's *Kulliyat* in an undated Aligarh edition, in Phukan's translation (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 218, folio 207). They introduce it so: "Among its ironic traces is the tribute of the great twentieth-century modernist Sir Muhammad Iqbal. Despite his reservations about the medieval, anti-progressive legacy of institutional Sufism, his poet's heart produced this ghazal" ('A Pilgrim's Plea') "in praise of the paragon of Chishti spirituality, Shaykh Nizam ad-Din Awliya'" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 157, folio 145).

The ghazal is printed in full in English across two pages. Two of its lines turn on the circling of a grave:

*A step around your grave's a step into the life within.* (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 157, folio 145)

and, of a saint the poem does not name:

*Grant that I step, just once, around his tomb.* (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 157, folio 145)

The translation is printed without its Urdu original, and the page gives no date or place of composition and no collection title (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 157, folio 145).

## The Idea of Pakistan

Iqbal's enduring place in the history of the subcontinent rests, finally, on his political vision. In his presidential address to the annual session of the All-India Muslim League at Allahabad in 1930, he argued that the Muslims of the north-west of India — the Punjab, the North-West Frontier, Sindh, and Baluchistan — formed a distinct nation with its own culture, law, and moral order, and that their destiny would be best secured in a consolidated, self-governing Muslim state within or without the British Empire. This address is remembered as the intellectual seed from which the demand for Pakistan would grow. In the last years of his life Iqbal corresponded closely with Muhammad Ali Jinnah, the Quaid-e-Azam, urging him to return from London to lead the Muslims of India and pressing upon him the case for a separate homeland; their exchange, the famous "Iqbal–Jinnah letters," is treasured as a founding document of the Pakistan Movement. Iqbal's concern was never merely territorial: he insisted that a true Islamic polity must also be a just one, freeing the poor and the labouring from exploitation, and he saw the proposed state as a place where the ethical vision of his faith might be lived out. He did not live to see it — he died in 1938, nine years before Pakistan came into being in August 1947 — yet the new nation enshrined him as its ideological founder, and its highest honours and its very self-image are bound up with his name.

The books consulted add their own measures of that place. Boivin's note counts Iqbal among "the greatest Muslim poets in the Indian subcontinent, and one of the founding fathers of Pakistan" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 212, folio 199). Rozehnal, writing of a rhetorical move by Wahid Bakhsh, says that he "is no different from a host of other Muslim reformers and revivalists, including Jamal ad-Din al-Afghani (d. 1897), Sir Sayyid Ahmad Khan (d. 1898), Muhammad Iqbal (d. 1938), Bediuzzaman Said Nursi (d. 1960), and even Mawlana Mawdudi (d. 1941)" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 120, folio 111). In *Pain and Grace* Iqbal is one of two writers named in a passage Schimmel quotes from Rashdi on political appropriation (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 153, folio 153).

Haroon Khalid, on the word of the historian Iqbal Qaiser, a different man from the poet, writes that Allama Iqbal and Jinnah "got involved as they tried pacifying the Muslims" in the Shaheed Ganj riots (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 77, folio 59), a case that, Khalid adds, the Lahore High Court heard in the British era and again "Around 1957-58" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 77, folio 59; p. 78, folio 60). Writing of Lahore's history, Khalid's *A White Trail* records that Sir Zaffarullah Khan "drafted the Lahore Resolution which the Muslim League presented in Lahore in March 1940" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 211–212, folio not stated).

## The Tomb

Iqbal was laid to rest on a raised platform in the Hazuri Bagh, in the narrow but hallowed ground between the steps of the Badshahi Mosque and the wall of the Lahore Fort — a site of the utmost symbolic dignity, at the ceremonial heart of Mughal Lahore. His mausoleum was raised in the years after his death and completed around 1951, its design a dignified fusion of Afghan and Moorish–Mughal styles. Built chiefly of red sandstone, it is grave and elegant rather than grand; the fine marble within and the two headstones of lapis lazuli are traditionally said to have been gifts from Afghanistan, whose rulers claimed a special reverence for the poet who had sung of the Muslim world beyond India's borders. Verses of Iqbal's own composition are inscribed within. As a protected national monument the tomb is maintained by the Government of Punjab, and a ceremonial guard — furnished by the Pakistan Rangers — is posted at its entrance; the changing of the guard is itself a small daily ceremony that visitors gather to watch. Its placement is deliberate and eloquent: the poet of Islamic revival rests forever between the greatest mosque and the greatest fort of the old Mughal capital, and beneath the shadow, close by, of the Minar-e-Pakistan raised to mark the demand for the country he imagined.

The books consulted do not describe the mausoleum. They say nothing of its builders, its stones or its custodians, and they do not record the text of the verses inscribed within. Schimmel alone says where Iqbal lies, and she does so while writing of something else. "Several" *maqāms*, places, "may bear the name of the same saint", she observes, and "a few years ago a maqam of Muhammad Iqbal (who is buried in Lahore) was erected in the garden of" Rumi's tomb in Konya (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 270, folio 239). She gives no year for the memorial at Konya, and the index of her book files under his name that foreign *maqam* alone (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 518, folio 487). The poet who took Rumi as his guide is thus remembered by a second *maqam* in the garden of his master's tomb, while the books say nothing of the first.

## A Place of Homage and Devotion

Standing where it does — hemmed by the Badshahi Mosque, the Lahore Fort, and the Minar-e-Pakistan — the mausoleum lies within the single most visited heritage precinct in the country, and it draws a great and constant stream of Pakistani and foreign visitors — some forty to fifty thousand a day — and on national days and the anniversaries of the poet the numbers swell toward a hundred thousand. Many come first to see the mosque and the fort and the gardens, and turn aside to recite the Fatiha at Iqbal's grave; others come expressly to pay homage. On Pakistan's national days and on Iqbal's death anniversary, floral wreaths and a ceremonial chadar are laid upon the grave — as they are on religious and celebratory occasions through the year — and the Army mounts a parade, rendering a formal guard of honour and ceremonial salute (salami). Because Iqbal was at once a devout Muslim thinker and a poet of vast popular reach, his tomb has gathered, too, a quiet devotional aura not unlike that of the older shrines of the city: visitors recite the Fatiha there as at a darbar, and folk memory preserves stories of the poet's nearness to the saints.

## Legends and Stories

The best-loved of the folk stories of Iqbal's nearness to the saints, related by his faithful servant Ali Baksh, tells of a night when the poet bade him fetch lassi (a yoghurt drink) for guests who were coming, though it was the dead of night and the bazaar was shut; setting out, the servant found the lane bathed in an unearthly light and an old man with a luminous face selling lassi where no shop had ever stood, who took no payment, saying, "I have an old account with Iqbal." When the servant later pressed his master to explain, Iqbal is said to have revealed, under a promise of secrecy kept until his death, that the guests had been Khwaja Moinuddin Chishti of Ajmer, and the seller of lassi none other than Data Ganj Bakhsh Ali Hujwiri, the patron saint of Lahore. Such stories, whatever the historian may make of them, bind the modern thinker to the ancient sacred landscape of the city and are part of why ordinary people cherish his memory as they do that of a saint.

The saint of that story's ending belongs, in the books consulted, to the city itself. Writing of Lahore, Schimmel says that Hujwiri "died there in 1071" and that "His shrine, called that of Data Ganj Bakhsh, is still a popular place of pilgrimage in Lahore." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 119, folio 88). Scott Kugle calls ʿAli Hujwiri "Lahore's patron saint" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 206, folio 185). These passages describe the saint and his city, not the poet's tomb.

Haroon Khalid heard a different story of the poet among the saints from Ghulam Muhammad, a dervish at a crow shrine in Gujrat: "Allama Iqbal also once came to see the saint. One of his devotees told him that the Allama wants to speak to him." 'All of them are Allamas for me,' replied the saint, referring to his crows (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated).

## Iqbal Day and National Memory

Pakistan keeps Iqbal's memory as a kind of national trust. His death anniversary on 21 April, and above all Iqbal Day on his birthday, 9 November, are observed across the country with ceremonies, seminars, and recitations of his verse; his poetry is set to music and sung, his lines are quoted by leaders and inscribed on public buildings, and his works are taught in every school. At the mausoleum, heads of state and visiting foreign dignitaries lay wreaths, and the site serves as a place of national remembrance as much as of private prayer. Whole institutions and an entire field of scholarship — "Iqbaliyat" — are devoted to his thought, and his poetry has been translated into many languages, carrying the Poet of the East to readers far beyond the subcontinent. Because his verse gave the Pakistan Movement much of its emotional and moral vocabulary, the tomb is not a relic of the past but a living focus of the nation's self-understanding, where the memory of the man and the idea of the country are renewed together.

Ernst and Lawrence mark one limit of that musical life: "In Pakistan, by contrast, qawwali became the one musical genre with a clear Muslim identity, overshadowing even the sung poetry of Muhammad Iqbal." (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 149, folio 137).

His image travels on the country's everyday surfaces. Haroon Khalid saw a Pakistani flag at the Kallar Kahar cable car bearing "a picture of Mickey Mouse on the white section (reserved for minorities) and Allama Iqbal and Jinnah on the green section" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated). At a school in Makhdoom Pur Pahuran, "On the external wall of his office there were badly painted pictures of Jinnah and Allama Iqbal" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 129, folio 111).

## Translators and Scholars

The bibliographies of the books consulted show how far the poet's work travelled, and who carried it. Schimmel's notes name Reynold A. Nicholson's *The Secrets of the Self* as the translation of the *Asrar-e-Khudi*, and Arberry's as the translation of the *Javid Nama* (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 309, folio 291); Arberry also translated part of the *Zabūr-i ʿAjam* as *Persian Psalms* (Lahore, 1948) (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 481, folio 450).

Among the studies they list are Hafeez Malik's edited volume *Muhammad Iqbal: Poet-Philosopher of Pakistan* (New York, 1971) (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 485, folio 454); Rais Ahmad Jaʿfri's Urdu study *Iqbāl aur ʿishq-i rasūl* (Lahore: Ashraf, 1956) (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 326, folio 308; Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 482, folio 451); Alessandro Bausani's articles "Satana nell'opera filosofico-poetica di Muhammad Iqbal", in the *Rivista degli studi orientali* 30 (1957), and on the *Gulshan-i raz-i jadid*, in the *Annali* of the Istituto Universitario Orientale of Naples (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 472; Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 315, folio 297); and Bürgel's "The Pious Rogue: A Study in the Meaning of qalandar and rend in the Poetry of Muhammad Iqbal", in *Edebiyât* 4, no. 1 (1979) (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 317, folio 299).

Schimmel's own studies of him are listed too: *Gabriel's Wing: A Study into the Religious Ideas of Sir Muhammad Iqbal* (Leiden, 1963), "Die Gestalt des Satan in Muhammad Iqbal's Werk" in *Kairos* (1963), and "The Idea of Prayer in the Thought of Iqbal" in *Moslem World* 48, no. 3 (1958) (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 492, folio 461), as well as an article on the Prophet in his thought in *Islamic Studies* for 1962 (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 493, folio 462; Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 338, folio 320).

## Setting

The books consulted say nothing of the Hazuri Bagh itself. What they offer is the neighbourhood and the city around it, and it is given here as context about the place, not as fact about the tomb. Haroon Khalid's *A White Trail* writes that "The Roshani gate, one of the thirteen gateways that lead into the ancient walled city of Lahore, is between the Alamgiri Gate and the Royal Mosque"; lamps "were lit all along the passageway ... hence the name Roshani gate, or the gate of lights". "Behind it is the Heera-Mandi bazaar, the area reserved for the prostitutes of the city before they dispersed all over Lahore, after a crackdown on them during the Islamist government of General Zia-ul-Haq"; but "the musicians remain. This is still one of the largest markets for traditional instruments in the city", and the area is "also famous for the food street that runs parallel to the wall of the Badshahi Masjid" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 205, folio not stated). The River Ravi, which flowed next to the fort, "has changed its course, moving a few kilometres away" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 195, folio not stated). And "the Parsi community buries its dead in a graveyard next to the Minar-e-Pakistan facing the ancient walled city of Lahore" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 144, folio not stated).

Of the mosque and the fort between which the tomb stands, Kanhaiya Lal's history of Lahore records that at the end of Sikh rule, with the British annexation of the Punjab, the troops left the Badshahi Mosque (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 141, folio 144). Khalid's *Walking with Nanak*, writing of the fort at Rohtas, says that it "was not the kind of fort that was found in Lahore, where only royalty stayed" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 249, folio 223).

Of the city's place in Muslim history, Schimmel writes that "Lahore became the first center of Persian-inspired Muslim culture in the Subcontinent—the name of Hujwiri, who composed his famous Persian treatise on Sufism in this town, has already been mentioned; his tomb still provides a place of pilgrimage for the Panjabis." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 376, folio 345).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **Where he was born.** The site's account has him born in the city of Sialkot on 9 November 1877. Boivin's note calls him "a poet born in Lahore" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 212, folio 199), while printing the same years, 1877–1938, elsewhere (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 102, folio 89).
- **The day of his death.** The site's account gives 21 April 1938, the date of the standard biographies and of the national observance, and notes that the tomb's own records preserve a variant date of 21 September. The books consulted give the year alone, 1938 (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 102, folio 89; Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 120, folio 111; Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 226, folio 208; Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 107, folio 76).
- **The span of the poetry.** The site's account dates *Zarb-e-Kalim* to 1936. Schimmel's *As Through a Veil* lists *Pas che bāyad kard* in 1936 and the *Armaghān-i Ḥijāz* in 1938 (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 325, folio 307; p. 326, folio 308). Her *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* says that his poetical works appeared "from 1915 until 1935" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 354, folio 323).
- **The doctoral dissertation.** The site's account gives a doctorate from the University of Munich with a dissertation on *The Development of Metaphysics in Persia*, and Boivin agrees on the Munich PhD (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 212, folio 199). Rizvi lists the book as "The development of metaphysics in Persia, London, 1908." (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 452, folio 428). Schimmel lists it as *The Development of Metaphysics in Iran*, Cambridge, 1908 (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 481, folio 450).
- **The lectures' title and date.** The site's account names *The Reconstruction of Religious Thought in Islam* and gives no date. Schimmel lists *Six Lectures on the Reconstruction of Religious Thought in Islam*, Lahore, 1930 (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 481, folio 450; Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 325, folio 307). Rozehnal cites the Lahore edition of Sh. Muhammad Ashraf, 1934/1982 (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 251, folio 244). Rizvi cites a Lahore reprint of 1944 (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 452, folio 428).
- **Iqbal and Sufism.** The site's account sets him against "a decayed mysticism", a Sufism that counselled the annihilation of the self. Schimmel reports his attacks on 'Pirism' but writes that "a close study of his work makes it clear that he himself follows quite closely the Sufi thought of the classical period" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 437, folio 406). Ernst and Lawrence speak of "his reservations about the medieval, anti-progressive legacy of institutional Sufism" and of a ghazal he wrote in praise of Shaykh Nizam ad-Din Awliya' (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 157, folio 145).
- **The Perfect Man and Nietzsche.** The site's account says that Nietzsche's *Übermensch* "fascinated and repelled him" and that the *mard-e-momin* is "likewise a figure of immense will and self-affirmation". Schimmel writes that Iqbal's ideas of the Perfect Man "are derived from these theories rather than from the Nietzschean superman" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 304, folio 273).
- **The meaning of jabr mahmud.** *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* glosses the phrase as Iqbal's 'higher predestination' (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 229, folio 198) and, a page earlier and without reference to him, as 'agreeable constraint' (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 228, folio 197).
- **Bausani's article.** For Alessandro Bausani's article on the *Gulshan-i raz-i jadid*, *As Through a Veil* dates its volume of the *Annali* to 1958 (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 315, folio 297); *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* dates the same volume to 1959 (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 472).
- **The titles of Schimmel's own studies.** Werbner and Basu print *Classical Urdu Literature from the Beginning to Iqbal* (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 218, folio 207); *As Through a Veil* prints *Classical Urdu Literature from the Beginnings to Iqbal* (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 337, folio 319). *As Through a Veil* names her article "The Place of the Prophet of Islam in Iqbal's Thought" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 338, folio 320); *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* names it "The Place of the Prophet in Iqbal's Thought" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 493, folio 462).

## Legacy

More than a grave, Mazar-e-Iqbal is a meeting-place of literature, faith, and national identity. It honours a man who reawakened a people through poetry, who dared to rethink his religious tradition for the modern age, and who imagined into being a country he would never see. His verse is recited from schoolrooms to state ceremonies; his philosophy of *khudi* remains a living argument about the self, freedom, and faith; his image of the *shaheen* still summons the young to a life of striving. And his tomb, in the shadow of the Badshahi Mosque and within sight of the Minar-e-Pakistan, endures as one of the places where Pakistan goes to remember who it is, and who it once hoped to become.

Iqbal's legacy reaches, too, into the scholarship of Sufism itself. Annemarie Schimmel, whose books supply most of what is said of him above, traces her own study of a mystical writer of eighteenth-century Muslim India back to him: "my interest in Khwaja Mir Dard (1721-1785)", she writes, "developed during my studies on Muhammad Iqbal's work" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 1, folio not stated).
