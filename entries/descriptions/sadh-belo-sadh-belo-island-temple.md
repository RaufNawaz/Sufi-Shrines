## Overview

Sadh Belo is a serene temple complex occupying a wooded island in the River Indus, set between the twin cities of Sukkur and Rohri in Sindh. One of the most important living centres of Hindu worship in the province, it is intimately bound to the *Udasi* tradition, the ascetic order that traces its lineage to Sri Chand, elder son of Guru Nanak, and so stands at a confluence of Hindu and Sikh devotion. Spread across two interconnected islets, the complex encompasses temples, shrines, *samadhis* (memorial tombs of holy men), gardens, a communal kitchen and living quarters, all of it founded in the early nineteenth century amid the palms and greenery that give the island its name.

Two of the books consulted name the site, both under the spelling Sadh Bela. Michel Boivin, in his study of the Hindu Sufis of Sindh and their migration to India, counts "Sadh Bela, on another island" among the remarkable sanctuaries of the Rohri–Sukkur–Shikarpur country and calls it "the famous Udasi temple in Sadh Bela", created "thanks to a gift of land" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 31, folio 18). Jürgen Schaflechner, in his study of the goddess Hinglaj, describes it as "a tīrtha, as he is told, in the city of Sukkur on an island in the River Indus", and records the hospitality of its abbot to a pilgrim on the road to Hinglaj (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 138, folio not stated). Neither book names Baba Bankhandi Maharaj, gives a foundation date, or describes the buildings; what the other books consulted say of Sukkur, Rohri, the Nanakpanthi and *Udasi* communities and the province of Sindh is set out below as context, and is said of those places and communities, not of this island.

## History and the Coming of Baba Bankhandi

The founding of Sadh Belo is attributed to the *Udasi* saint Baba Bankhandi Maharaj, born Balchand Sharma, whose origins are variously traced, according to differing accounts, to Kero Khetar near Delhi or to Nepal. As a young spiritual seeker, still in his teens, he is said to have arrived in Sukkur, then a flourishing trading hub of the subcontinent, in the year 1823. Drawn to the tranquil, forested island in the Indus, he settled there and made it a place of meditation and worship, and it came to be known as *Sadhu Belo*, meaning "the woods of the sage". Under Baba Bankhandi and the succession of *gaddi nashin* (spiritual successors) who followed him, the island grew into a substantial religious establishment.

The complex enshrines a broad and characteristically syncretic array of devotion. Baba Bankhandi is credited with establishing places of worship dedicated to Hindu deities including Annapurna, Hanuman, Ganesh and Shiv Shankar, while also making provision for the veneration of the *Guru Granth Sahib* and the *Bhagavad Gita* side by side, an expression of the inclusive *Udasi* ethos. Much of the architecture visible today, including the principal temple, was raised somewhat later; the main shrine is generally dated to the closing years of the nineteenth century, built under an eighth successor in the lineage, Sant Harnam Das, and further embellished in subsequent decades.

Boivin's one statement on the temple's origin sets it among the works of princely patrons. Writing of the amirs of Khairpur, he observes that "the amirs of Khairpur were themselves staunch Shia Muslims, and they built beautiful imambaras in the nineteenth century, but it did not prevent them from being generous patrons for all of the religious buildings as, for instance, the famous Udasi temple in Sadh Bela which was created thanks to a gift of land" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 31, folio 18). He gives no year for the gift, no extent of land and no name for the giver or the receiver.

## The Udasi Tradition and the Island Complex

The distinctive character of Sadh Belo flows from its rootedness in the *Udasipanth*. This religious and ascetic movement grew from the teachings of Sri Chand, the elder son of Guru Nanak, and blended devotional streams in a manner that resists neat sectarian classification, honouring Sikh scripture and Hindu deities alike, and cultivating a tradition of renunciation, pilgrimage and hospitality. Sadh Belo became one of the pre-eminent seats of *Udasi* religious life in Sindh.

The island itself is arranged to serve both worship and community. The principal island, Sadh Belo, houses the temples, verandahs and the *langar*-style kitchen that fed pilgrims and residents, while the adjoining island, sometimes called Deen Belo, contains *samadhis*, a park and the Rishi Nol *mandir*. Reached by boat across the Indus, the complex offered pilgrims a self-contained sacred world amid the river, its white temples and shaded courtyards forming a striking contrast to the surrounding waters.

Boivin places the island twice. In his survey of the shrines of northern Sindh, speaking of the country on "both sides of the Indus River", he writes: "It suffices to quote some remarkable sanctuaries such as Khwaja Khizr, on an island, Sadh Bela, on another island, and the dargah of Sachal Sarmast" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 31, folio 18). Later he speaks of "the Sadh Belo settled in the island in the Indus River, in Sukkur" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 69, folio 56). His index carries the site under both spellings, one after the other, as "Sadh Bela (sanctuary)" and "Sadh Belo" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 248, folio 235). Schaflechner, too, sets it "in the city of Sukkur on an island in the River Indus" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 138, folio not stated).

## The Nanakpanth and the Udasis in Sindh

What the books say of the *Udasis* and of the wider Nanakpanth in Sindh is said of the province and its communities, not of this island, but it is the world to which the temple belongs. Boivin writes that "In Sindh, many Nanakpanthis belonged to a subgroup known as the Udasis" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 53, folio 40), and that "In nineteenth-century Sindh, the Nanakpanth, or 'the path of Nanak', was probably the dominant religious path among the Hindu Sindhis" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 90, folio 77). Among the Lohanas and related mercantile groups, he says, "two main cults were followed: the Nanakpanth and the Daryapanth" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 42–43, folios 29–30).

He describes a religious recomposition of the province in the colonial period, attested "by the creation and the development of several important Nanakpanthi darbars throughout Sindh", and adds that "although some darbars had been created in the late eighteenth century, there was a renaissance of the Nanakpanthi darbars in the second half of the nineteenth century" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 53–54, folios 40–41). The word *darbar* itself, he notes, was "used by the Sikhs, the Muslims and the Hindus" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 37–38, folios 24–25). After the Singh Sabha process, "The Sindhi Nanakpanthis were thus not included in the Sikh community, and they identified themselves as being a Hindu religious sect" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 200, folio 187). In an endnote he records that "The Udasis of Sindh are coined as being a heterodox Sikh tradition, in one of the very few, if not the sole, academic papers devoted to the Nanakpanth in Sindh (Jatt 2016)" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 209, folio 196).

Schaflechner writes that "Many Lohanas in Sindh follow the teachings of Nanak, somewhat obscuring their religious affiliation for the colonial officers. Hence, in the 1881 census, Lohanas from Shikarpur and Hyderabad were counted as Sikhs, while ten years later, in the 1891 census, they were classified as Hindus (Lari 1994, 211f.)" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 80, folio not stated). He adds that "many middle- and upper-class Hindus in Sindh are followers of the Nanak paṃth" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 257, folio not stated), and that "it is common to cover one's head before walking into a tikānā (Sin.), the temple containing the Guru Granth Sahib" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 286, folio not stated).

Haroon Khalid found the Nanak Panthi temples of Sindh and southern Punjab holding portraits of Guru Nanak alongside Hindu deities, and writes that these communities "are found in very large numbers in the province of Sindh" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 197, folio not stated). In a later book he writes that the communities who refused the Khalsa and called themselves followers of Guru Nanak "still exist in Sindh and in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa. Baptised Sikhs look down upon them" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 230, folio 204).

## Devotion and Practice

The island remains the focus of an annual festival that gathers Hindus from across Sindh and beyond, when boats ferry pilgrims to the island for days of worship, music and communal meals, and its kitchen has long fed pilgrims and residents alike.

Boivin's account of the riots at Sukkur in the colonial period preserves the river crossing as the pilgrims' way to the island: "the local Hindus opposed this rehabilitation since it was located in the harbour on the Indus River, from which the pilgrims reached the sacred site of Sadh Belo" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 61, folio 48). He gives no date for any festival and names no custodian.

Schaflechner records the one act of the temple's life that his study carries: its hospitality to a pilgrim bound for the goddess Hinglaj. The pilgrim, Shastri, whose own account Schaflechner cites, "uses the time to go to Sadh Bela, a tīrtha, as he is told, in the city of Sukkur on an island in the River Indus". "There the mahaṃt (Hin. superior of a monastery, abbot) of Sadh Bela grows very fond of Shastri and provides him with supplies and advice for his pilgrimage." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 138, folio not stated) The advice was practical: "The abbot instructs him to keep many onions in case of a drinking-water shortage; he advises that the onion's juice would help stave off nausea and dizziness as well as provide liquid (Shastri 1977, 8)." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 138, folio not stated) A note pairs the same onion advice with a second pilgrim's account, "Abadhut (1964, 79)" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 148, folio not stated). The abbot is not named, and the visit is not dated beyond Shastri's journey.

## Partition, Survival and Pilgrimage

The Partition of 1947 dispersed much of Sindh's Hindu population and cast uncertainty over its many shrines, yet Sadh Belo endured as one of the most significant functioning Hindu sites in Pakistan. It has continued to be maintained by its custodians and to draw devotees, and its survival has made it a cherished anchor for the Hindu community of the region and a rare instance of continuity across the rupture of Partition.

Of the province at large, Boivin writes that "By early November of 1947, 246,000 Hindus had already left Sindh by sea, air or rail. As of 1 January 1948, it was estimated that nearly 500,000 people had sought refuge in India" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 62, folio 49), and that the Hindu Sindhis "started leaving Sindh especially after the Karachi riots in January 1948" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 66, folio not stated). Of the shrines they left behind, he says that "The darbars in Sindh were most of the time preserved with different arrangements regarding their management", often with Indian *gaddi nashins* or followers paying keepers, and that "despite the fluctuant diplomatic relations between Pakistan and India, it was always possible to travel across the border" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 68, folio 55).

The island's name also crossed into India. Writing of the Sindhi darbars there, Boivin names "Sadh Belo – which was transferred from Sukkur to Mumbai (Maharashtra)" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 68, folio 55), and he observes the break in built form between the two: "In the Mumbai Sadh Belo, for example, it is difficult to see any architectural continuity with the Sadh Belo settled in the island in the Indus River, in Sukkur" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 69, folio 56).

On the Pakistani side, Schaflechner writes of Sindh as "home to the majority of Pakistan's Hindus" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 13, folio not stated), and of government funding for the places of minority communities, seen "in a wave of restorations of temples across Sindh within the past decade" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 16, folio not stated). He does not say whether Sadh Bela was among them.

## Setting

Sukkur and Rohri appear in several of the books, though none of these passages speaks of the island. Boivin treats them, with Shikarpur, as corners of a triangle of northern Sindh rich in sanctuaries (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 31, folio 18). He dates the "Sukkur riots" to "between December of 1939 and February of 1940", and notes that they "occurred beyond the city, in other cities such as Rohri, and also in other parts of Sindh, such as in Lakki" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 61, folio 48). Elsewhere he contrasts Sehwan with Sukkur: "Contrary to other cities in Northern Sindh – for instance, Sukkur, following the Manzilgah riots – no communal violence had ever been registered" there (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 71, folio 58).

Rohri, in Boivin's pages, is the birthplace of two Sufis: Rochaldas, "born in 1879 in Rohri, in the north of Sindh, to a family that his hagiographers call modest" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 55, folio 42), and the poet Bedil, "a Sufi born in Rohri" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 97–98, folios 84–85). Annemarie Schimmel writes that "Among them Bedil of Rohri (d. 1872) should be mentioned as a prolific, though not very outstanding, writer in five languages" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 427, folio 306 as printed), and names "the SuhrawardI mystic Janullah of Rohri, one of the outstanding Persian poets of Sind from the second half of the eighteenth century" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 428, folio 397). Shemeem Burney Abbas reports that the singer Alan Faqir "is reported to have sung on the buses and trains between Rohri and Sehwan Sharif in his youth" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 78, folio not stated).

The Janamsakhi traditions, in Harish Dhillon's retelling, bring Guru Nanak from Multan "to Sukkur from where he turned southwards along the river Indus" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 194, folio 214). And Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, composed in the fortieth regnal year of Aurangzeb, records Sukkur as a halt in the pursuit of Dara Shikoh: news reached the pursuing armies that Dara, having passed Bhakkar, had halted at Sukkur on the twenty-fifth of Muharram (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 51). The Mughal commanders who crossed towards Sukkur lost many beasts to what the chronicle calls the thickness of the jungle, the press of thorny trees and the hard stages of the road; the baggage did not come up, and on the sixth of Safar they reached Sukkur (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 51). Shaikh Mir stayed a day at Sukkur, where the zamindars told him that twenty-five kos from Sukkur a road branches off towards Qandahar; and a faujdar of Sukkur was appointed (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 50).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The name.** The site's account gives Sadh Belo, and *Sadhu Belo*, "the woods of the sage". Schaflechner spells it Sadh Bela throughout (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 138, folio not stated). Boivin uses both: "Sadh Bela" where he names it among the sanctuaries and the patrons' temples (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 31, folio 18), and "Sadh Belo" when he writes of the pilgrims, the riots and the transfer to Mumbai (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 61, folio 48; p. 68, folio 55); his index prints the two spellings as separate entries (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 248, folio 235).
- **Where the island stands.** The site's account sets it between the twin cities of Sukkur and Rohri. Boivin writes of "the island in the Indus River, in Sukkur" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 69, folio 56), and Schaflechner of "the city of Sukkur on an island in the River Indus" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 138, folio not stated).
- **How the temple came to be.** The site's account has Baba Bankhandi Maharaj arrive in 1823, settle on the island and found the place of worship, with the main temple raised under his eighth successor, Sant Harnam Das, at the close of the nineteenth century. Boivin, who names neither man, gives the temple as an example of the patronage of the Shia amirs of Khairpur, "created thanks to a gift of land" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 31, folio 18), without a date or the giver's name.
- **What kind of place it is.** The site's account calls it a Hindu temple complex of the *Udasi* tradition. Boivin calls it an "Udasi temple" and, in his index, a "sanctuary" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 31, folio 18; p. 248, folio 235); Schaflechner calls it a "tīrtha" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 138, folio not stated).
- **The head of the house.** The site's account speaks of a line of *gaddi nashin* who succeeded Baba Bankhandi. Schaflechner's account of the pilgrim's visit names its head by the office of *mahaṃt*, which he glosses as "superior of a monastery, abbot", and gives no name (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 138, folio not stated).
- **Sadh Belo after 1947.** The site's account presents the island as having endured in Pakistan, maintained by its custodians, a rare instance of continuity across Partition. Boivin writes that Sadh Belo "was transferred from Sukkur to Mumbai (Maharashtra)" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 68, folio 55), and finds no architectural continuity between the Mumbai Sadh Belo and the island (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 69, folio 56).

## Legacy

Sadh Belo stands as a testament to Sindh's plural religious heritage and to the enduring vitality of the *Udasi* tradition on the banks of the Indus. As both a place of active pilgrimage and a monument to the syncretic devotion of Baba Bankhandi and his successors, the island temple remains one of the most beloved and evocative sacred sites of Hindu Sindh, its shrines rising from the river much as they have for two centuries. Its name now stands in two places: on the island in the Indus at Sukkur and in the Sadh Belo of Mumbai, to which, as Boivin records, it was transferred (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 68, folio 55; p. 69, folio 56).
