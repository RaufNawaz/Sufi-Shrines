## Overview

The shrine of Hazrat Sultan Bahu (1630–1691) at Garh Maharaja, in the Jhang region of the Punjab, honours one of the greatest of the Punjabi Sufi poet-saints and the founder of the Sarwari Qadiri order. Sultan Bahu wrote prolifically in Persian, but he is loved above all for his Punjabi *abyat* — quatrains that end each line with the long, sighing exhalation of the divine name, “Hoo” — which are sung across the Punjab in a soaring traditional style. His shrine, twice moved and rebuilt as the river Chenab shifted its course, is a major centre of pilgrimage in central Punjab.

None of the books consulted for this article names Garh Maharaja. Zahur ul-Hasan Sharib's *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind* places the saint's mazar at Shorkot, "آپ کا مزار شور کوٹ میں واقع ہے" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 316, folio 312), and Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori's *Hadiqat al-Awliya* places his tomb in the Jhang region, fifteen kos from the place where the Ravi and the Chenab meet (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 265, folio 253). These two collections of saints' lives (*tazkiras*) carry most of what is told of the man. Lahori's author gives him a short notice in his closing section on the saints of the Punjab whose year of death he could not establish (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 20, folio 8), and the book's modern editor adds, in his own notes, a death date from a manuscript colophon, the name of the saint's father in the saint's own words, and a chain of masters reaching back to Shaykh Abd al-Qadir Jilani (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 265, folio 253). Sharib tells the life at length: his birth and family at Shorkot, his search for a master, his books and his teaching, his sons and his successors, his death, and the wonders told of him (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 313, folio 309; p. 316, folio 312; p. 320, folio 316).

The English-language studies know him as a poet. Annemarie Schimmel quotes and reads the opening stanza of his Golden Alphabet (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 416, folio 385; Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 160, folio 142); Shemeem Burney Abbas records his verses as they were sung in performance (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 102, folio not stated); and Naveed Alam, translating Shah Hussain, and Taufiq Rafat's selection of Bulleh Shah name him among the Punjabi Sufi poets of their tradition (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 15, folio 15; Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 45, folio 25). None of them gives him a shrine, or a place narrower than the Punjab.

## The Life of the Saint

Bahu was born in 1630 in the Shorkot region of Jhang, into an Awan family; his father was an official of the Mughal administration and his mother, Bibi Rasti, a woman of great piety who is herself remembered as a saint and to whom tradition credits his very name — “Bahu,” “with Hoo,” the one who is with God.

Sharib opens his notice with the words "حضرت سلطان باہو منظور جناب کبریا ہیں", that Hazrat Sultan Bahu is one accepted of the Divine Presence. He was, Sharib writes, of the Awan tribe, "آپ قبیلہ اعوان سے تھے", and his line of descent reaches Hazrat Ali through twenty-nine intermediaries, "انتیس واسطوں سے" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 313, folio 309).

His father, in Sharib's account, was Bayazid Muhammad, "آپ کے والد ماجد کا نام بایزید محمد ہے", who held the rank of *mansabdar* in Kohistan on behalf of the Emperor Shahjahan, "وہ شہنشاہ شاہجہاں کی طرف سے کوہستان میں منصب دار تھے"; Sharib's note names the *Manaqib-e Sultani* (مناقب سلطانی) as his source (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 313, folio 309, حاشیہ 1). The father was a *hafiz* of the Quran, a scholar of his time and strict in the *shariat* (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 313, folio 309). From Kohistan he came to Multan and settled there; when orders were issued to send him back to Kohistan he refused, and from Multan he came to Shorkot, made his home there, received a *jagir* there, and there he died: "کوہستان سے ملتان آ کر اقامت گزین ہوئے۔ ان کو دوبارہ کوہستان بھیجے جانے کے احکام نافذ ہوئے۔ لیکن انہوں نے انکار کر دیا۔ ملتان سے وہ شور کوٹ تشریف لائے اور وہاں سکونت اختیار کی۔ شورکٹ میں ان کو جاگیر ملی" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 313, folio 309, حاشیہ 2). Sharib gives the grant no extent, no revenue and no grantor.

Lahori's editor, quoting the saint's own book, gives the father a different name. "آپ نے اپنی ایک تصنیف کلید التوحید میں اپنے والد کا نام بازید عرف اعوان تحریر کیا ہے": in one of his works, the *Kalid al-Tawhid*, the saint wrote his father's name as Bazid known as Awan. The editor quotes the Persian, "فقیر باہو ولد بازید عرف اعوان ساکن قلعہ شور کوٹ": so the saint describes himself, Faqir Bahu son of Bazid known as Awan, resident of Shorkot fort, writing in the time of Aurangzeb, whose titles in the passage are uncertain at three points in the printed text (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 265, folio 253, حاشیہ). The editor's copy is a manuscript, "کلید التوحید خطی ذخیرہ مولانا غلام محی الدین قصوری مخزونہ کتاب خانہ گنج بخش راولپنڈی": the *Kalid al-Tawhid* in manuscript, from the collection of Maulana Ghulam Muhyi al-Din Qasuri, kept in the Ganj Bakhsh library at Rawalpindi (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 265, folio 253, حاشیہ).

Of his mother Sharib writes "آپ کی والدہ ماجدہ کا نام بی بی راستی تھا۔ وہ اپنی بزرگی اور پرہیزگاری کے لیے مشہور ہیں": her name was Bibi Rasti, and she is famous for her sanctity and her abstinence (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 313, folio 309). The saint was born, Sharib says, at Shorkot in 1039 AH, "آپ شور کوٹ میں 1039ھ میں پیدا ہوئے", and he gives no day or month (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 313, folio 309). Rafat's selection of Bulleh Shah gives the year of his birth as 1631 (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 25, folio not stated), and Alam's translation of Shah Hussain as 1629 (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 15, folio 15); Abbas writes only that he was born in the Panjab (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 204, folio not stated).

Sharib, too, connects the name with the mother. "آپ کا نام باہو ہے۔ آپ اپنے نام پر فخر کرتے تھے اور خوش ہوتے تھے کہ آپ کے نام میں "ہوا"[OCR?] آتا ہے": his name is Bahu, and he was proud of it and glad that his name holds the word given there as ہوا, a reading uncertain in the printed text, on which the saying turns. Sharib then quotes the saint's own words, giving the عین الفقر زبدۃ العارفین as his source: "میری والدہ پر اللہ تعالی کی رحمت ہو کر انہوں نے میرا نام "باہو" رکھا ہے", God's mercy be on my mother, that she named me 'Bahu' (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 313, folio 309, حاشیہ 3). Schimmel gives the name another origin. In *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* she calls him "the first great Panjabi mystical poet", who got his surname because each line of his Golden Alphabet ends with the exclamation *hu*, 'He' (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 416, folio 385).

Sharib dates the beginning of his wonders to his infancy. While he was still at the breast, in Ramadan, he would not drink milk by day, as though he were keeping the fast: "شیر خوارگی کے زمانے میں رمضان میں آپ نے دن کو دودھ نہیں پیا' گویا دن کو روزہ رکھتے تھے" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 313–314, folios 309–310). He was, Sharib adds, a saint from the womb, "آپ مادر زاد ولی تھے" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 316, folio 312), and his first teaching and training were under his mother's supervision, "آپ کی ابتدائی تعلیم و تربیت آپ کی والدہ کی نگرانی میں ہوئی" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 314, folio 310).

Lahori's author counts him among the saints of the Punjab and describes him as a man of ecstasy and intoxication, of love and devotion, of sincerity and purity: "یہ بزرگ بزرگان پنجاب میں سے صاحب جذب و سکر و عشق و محبت و صدق و صفا تھے". His fame in the Punjab, the author writes, is complete, and there is no one who does not acknowledge his sanctity: "ان کی شہرت پنجاب میں کمال ہے اور کوئی ایسا شخص نہیں جو ان کی بزرگی کا قائل نہ ہو" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 264, folio 252).

## The Search for a Master

Drawn from youth to the inward life, Bahu received initiation in the Qadiri order at the hand of Sayyid Abdul Rahman of Delhi, and, tradition holds, attained his spiritual completion with extraordinary swiftness.

Sharib tells the way to that initiation in stages, and the first is set at the court of the Prophet. "امام الاولیاء حضرت علی کرم اللہ وجہہ نے آپ کو دربار نبوی صلی اللہ علیہ وسلم میں پیش کیا۔ حضرت محمد مصطفے صلی اللہ علیہ وسلم نے آپ کو بیعت سے سرفراز فرمایا": Hazrat Ali presented him at the court of the Prophet, and the Prophet honoured him with *bai'at*; "پھر غوث الاعظم میران محی الدین حضرت عبدالقادر جیلانی رحمۃ اللہ علیہ کے سپرد فرمایا", and then gave him into the charge of Abd al-Qadir Jilani (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 314, folio 310). There, Sharib writes, he obtained stations "جو بیان سے باہر ہیں", beyond telling (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 314, folio 310). Sharib marks a note at this point, but the note names no source.

When he was then urged to take *bai'at* with a perfect shaykh, "کسی با کمال شیخ", he objected that after *bai'at* at the Prophet's hand one should not take it from another. His mother answered: "یہ دنیا عالم اسباب ہے اور اس دنیا میں بھی کسی کامل شیخ کے دست حق پر ست پر بیعت کرنا ضروری ہے", this world is a world of means, and in this world too *bai'at* at the hand of a perfect shaykh is needful. "پھر آپ میرے لئے مرشد کافی ہیں", he replied, then you are murshid enough for me; and she told him "عورتوں کو اجازت نہیں کہ وہ کسی کو بیعت کریں", that women are not permitted to take anyone's *bai'at*. Asked where a murshid was to be sought, "آپ کی والدہ ماجدہ نے مشرق کی طرف اشارہ کیا", his mother pointed to the east (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 314, folio 310).

The first shaykh he came to was Hazrat Shah Habibullah, "حضرت شاہ حبیب اللہ", who laid down a condition: "یہ ضروری ہے کہ طالب حق پہلے مال و متاع سے فارغ ہو لے پھر اس راہ میں قدم رکھے", the seeker of truth must first be free of goods and possessions, and then set foot upon this path. "گھر آئے' مال و متاع سے فارغ ہوئے": he went home and divested himself of his goods and property (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 314–315, folios 310–311). Sharib does not say what the property was or what became of it. Shah Habibullah then sent him on: "جس نعمت کے تم مستحق ہو' وہ ہمارے پاس نہیں ہے۔ البتہ ہم تم کو منزل کا نشان بتائے دیتے ہیں… تم میرے شیخ حضرت شیخ عبدالرحمان قادری کی خدمت با برکت میں جاؤ اور ان کا دامن تھام لو" — the blessing you deserve is not with us, but we will show you the sign of the destination; go into the blessed service of my shaykh, Hazrat Shaykh Abdul Rahman Qadiri, and hold fast to the hem of his robe (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 315, folio 311).

"حضرت شاہ حبیب اللہ کی ہدایت کے موافق آپ دہلی تشریف لائے": as Shah Habibullah had directed, he came to Delhi. There Hazrat Sayyid Abdul Rahman Qadiri learned of his coming by *kashf*, "حضرت سید عبدالرحمان قادری رحمۃ اللہ علیہ کو بذریعہ کشف آپ کی آمد کی اطلاع ہوئی", and sent a man to bring him (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 315, folio 311). "وہ آپ کو خلوت میں لے گئے اور مدارج سلوک ذرا دیر میں طے کرا دیئے۔ بیعت سے مشرف ہوئے اور خرقہ خلافت سے سرفراز ہوئے": the master took him into seclusion and brought him through the stages of the path in a moment, and he was honoured with *bai'at* and with the *khirqa* of *khilafat* (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 315, folio 311). Sharib gives the master two honorifics, Shaykh in Shah Habibullah's words and Sayyid in his own narrative (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 315, folio 311).

In Delhi, Sharib relates, he would walk the bazaars, and whoever he looked upon he made God-attained in a moment: "دہلی کے بازاروں میں گھومتے اور جس پر نگاہ ڈالتے اس کو ذرا دیر میں خدا رسیدہ بنا دیتے تھے" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 315, folio 311). His pir summoned him and said "ہم نے تمہیں خاص نعمت عطاء کی اور تم نے اس خاص نعمت کو عام کر دیا", we gave you a special blessing and you have made that special blessing common (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 315, folio 311). He answered that he had wished to put the special blessing he had received to the test, to learn how precious it was and what its nature: "حضرت سے جو خاص نعمت مجھے حاصل ہوئی' اس کی آزمائش منظور تھی کہ مجھے کس قدر نعمت گراں مایہ حاصل ہوئی اور اس کی ماہیت کیا ہے". And the pir "مزید نعمت سے آپ کو مالا مال کیا", enriched him with further blessing (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 316, folio 312).

Lahori's editor gives a chain of ten links from the saint back to Shaykh Abd al-Qadir Jilani, passing through a Burhanpuri: "تلقین از سید عبد الرحمان و سید عبد الرحمٰن تلقین از سید عبد الجلیل و ہو از سید عبد البقا و ہو از سید عبد الستار و ہو سید عبد الفتاح و ہو سید نجم الدین برہان پوری و ہو محمد صادق و ہو از سید عبد الجبار و ہو سید عبد الرزاق و ہو از حضرت شیخ عبد القادر جیلانی" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 265, folio 253). Its first link is a Sayyid Abd al-Rahman.

He founded his own branch of the order, the Sarwari Qadiri, which spread widely after him.

The books name the order differently. Sharib, whose note cites the *Manaqib-e Sultani*, gives the name of his way as the saint himself gave it, the Qadiriyya Masruriyya: "آپ کے طریقے کا نام "قادریہ مسروریہ" ہے' جیسا کہ آپ نے خود فرمایا" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 316, folio 312, حاشیہ 6). Lahori's author calls him a faqir of great rank in the Qadiri silsila, "قادریہ سلسلے میں یہ حضرت بڑے مرتبے کے فقیر ہیں" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 265, folio 253), and Schimmel writes of "the Qadiriyya order, to which Sultan Bahu belonged" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 161, folio 143). None of the books consulted uses the name Sarwari.

Through his khalifas, Sharib writes, his silsila spread: "آپ کے مشہور خلفاء حسب ذیل ہیں۔ حضرت نورنگ[OCR?] (کھتران)[OCR?]' حضرت ملامعانی[OCR?] بلوچستانی' حضرت مومن شاہ گیلانی (سندھ) ان خلفاء سے آپ کا سلسلہ پھیلا". He names three, the second called Balochistani and the third of Sindh; the printed text is uncertain in two of the names and in one of the two regions (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 316, folio 312). Sharib names no *sajjada nashin*.

## Household, Livelihood and Death

From Delhi he returned to Shorkot and gave himself to teaching, instruction and guidance: "دہلی سے شور کوٹ تشریف لائے اور تعلیم و تلقین اور رشد و ہدایت میں مشغول ہوئے" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 316, folio 312). Sharib names no *khanqah* built for him there, no date for his return and no patron.

Sharib says that he had no outward livelihood: "بظاہر آپ کا کوئی ذریعہ معاش نہیں تھا۔ دو مرتبہ جاگیر والی زمین پر کھیتی باڑی کی' لیکن فصل نہیں کاٹی۔ توکل پر گزارہ کرتے تھے" — outwardly he had no means of livelihood; twice he cultivated the land of the *jagir* and did not reap the crop; he lived on *tawakkul*, trust in God (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 316, folio 312). One of the wonders told of him finds him at the plough (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 320–321, folios 316–317).

"آپ کی چار بیویاں تھیں۔ ان چاروں بیویوں سے آٹھ لڑکے پیدا ہوئے": he had four wives, and from the four were born eight sons. The youngest, سلطان حیات محمد, died in childhood, and Sharib then lists the others, "سلطان نور محمد' سلطان ولی محمد' سلطان لطیف محمد' محمد[OCR?]' سلطان صالح محمد' سلطان اسحاق محمد' سلطان فتح محمد' سلطان شریف احمد", one name uncertain in the printed text, so that nine names stand against the eight sons of his count; his note gives the *Manaqib-e Sultani*, at its pages 43 and 44, as the source (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 316, folio 312, حاشیہ 5).

He died in 1691 and was buried in his homeland.

The tazkiras give the date by the Hijri calendar. Sharib writes "آپ یکم جمادی الثانی 1102ھ کو جوار رحمت میں داخل ہوئے": he passed into the divine mercy on the first of Jumada al-Thani 1102 AH; he gives no age at death (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 316, folio 312). Lahori's author, writing in 1875, gives no year at all. He sets the saint's notice, as entry number ۲۱۳ (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 264–265, folios 252–253), in the closing section of his book, under the heading "حالات بزرگان پنجاب جن کی وفات کا سن و تاریخ بپایہ ثبوت نہیں پہنچا", the saints of the Punjab whose year and date of death are not established (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 20, folio 8; p. 261, folio 249). His modern editor supplies the year, "حضرت سلطان باہو کا سال وفات ۱۱۰۲ ھ ہے" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 265, folio 253, حاشیہ ۱), from a manuscript colophon that gives the hour but not the day: "سیوم پاس شب جمعہ شہر جمادی الثانی ۱۱۰۲ (ہجری) فرمودند", the third watch of the night of Friday, in the month of Jumada al-Thani 1102 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 265, folio 253). The same year stands in the editor's list of sources, "ف ۱۱۰۲ھ" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 292, folio 280). Neither book gives a Common Era equivalent.

The English-language books give Common Era years that do not all agree. Schimmel's *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* and the index of her *As Through a Veil* give his death as 1691 (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 416, folio 385; Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 362, folio 344), as does Rafat's selection (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 25, folio not stated); Alam gives 1690 (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 15, folio 15); and Schimmel's *Pain and Grace* gives 1691 in one place and 1694 in another (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 17, folio 13; p. 208, folio 208). Michel Boivin calls him "an important seventeenth-century Sufi from Punjab" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 121, folio 108).

## The Abyat and His Teaching

Sultan Bahu’s Punjabi poetry takes the form of *si-harfi* and, most famously, the *abyat* — quatrains in which every line closes on the ecstatic syllable *Hoo* (ھُو), the sound of the breath and of the divine name, so that the poems are literally sung on the rhythm of remembrance (*dhikr*). Their theme is the burning of divine love (*ishq*), the primacy of the inward reality over outward form, and the indispensable role of the spiritual guide (*murshid*).

The books bear out the refrain. Lahori's author writes that his sayings and verses in the Punjabi language are very famous, and that at their close comes the word *hū*, as he puts it: "ان کے قول و ابیات و اشعار پنجابی زبان میں بہت مشہور ہیں۔ جن کے اخیر میں ہُو کا لفظ آتا ہے" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 264, folio 252). Abbas writes that the end word *hū* marks Sultan Bahu's poetry (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 204, folio not stated). Schimmel's *Pain and Grace* speaks of "the short ecstatic poems of Sultan Bahu (d. 1691)", the most famous of them composed in the traditional form of the *siharfi*, the 'Golden Alphabet' (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 17, folio 13), and her *As Through a Veil* takes his Golden Alphabet as its example of the abecedarian poem (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 165, folio 147).

Abbas's glossary names his form as the *bait*: "Sultan Bahu and Waris Shah use the bait form in their poetry, such as the Abiāt-e Bahu and Waris Shah's Hir" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 178, folio not stated). Rafat's selection puts it in a sentence: "Sultan Bahu (1631-1691) accepted the restraint of the bait." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 25, folio not stated). The same book is cool towards the *siharfi*. "A favourite device with Sultan Bahu", its 'method of beginning a line', it writes, "has contributed nothing to the intrinsic quality of the Punjabi poetry; it has indeed introduced a needless and tedious mannerism" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 50, folio 30).

The most famous of all begins:

اَلف اللہ چنبے دی بُوٹی، مُرشد مَن وِچ لائی ھُو

*Alif — Allah — is a jasmine plant that my master has planted within my heart, Hoo.*

Schimmel quotes the whole of this first stanza in English in *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*: "The first verse of Sultan Bahu's (d. 1691) poem goes:" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 416, folio 385)

*Alif: Allah is like the jasmine plant which the preceptor planted in my heart—o Hu! / By water and the gardener of negation and positive statement it remained near the jugular vein and everywhere—o Hu! / It spread fragrance inside when it approached the time of blossoming—o Hu! / May the efficient preceptor live long, says Bahu, who planted this plant—o Hu!* (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 416, folio 385 – p. 417, folio 386)

Her footnote at this point cites Maqbool Elahi's translation of the *Abyat*, published at Lahore in 1967 (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 416, folio 385). In *As Through a Veil* the stanza opens "Alif: Allah is a jasmine bud which the preceptor has planted in my heart" and closes "May the efficient preceptor live long, says Bahu, who planted this plant", with the refrain 'O Hu!' (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 160, folio 142). There she sets it beside the North Indian Chishti mystic Masʿud Bakk of the late fourteenth century: "the Panjabi poet Sultan Bahu some 300 years later begins his Golden Alphabet with the lines" of the jasmine (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 160, folio 142).

Schimmel reads the stanza as a lesson in remembrance, "These lines point to the recollection of the divine name, planted in the mystic's heart by his Pir", and the water of negation and affirmation as the *lā* and *illā* of the profession of faith in the *dhikr*, combined with the allusion to Sura 50:16 (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 417, folio 386), the reference she gives in *As Through a Veil* for the jugular vein (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 160, folio 142). In that book the *dhikr* of the profession of faith, the negative and the positive assertion "as used in the Qadiriyya order, to which Sultan Bahu belonged", "is the water that makes the 'plant' God grow in man's heart" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 161, folio 143); in *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* it is "the jasmine tree watered by the" *lā* and *illā*, the two parts of the profession of faith used in *dhikr*, "as Sultan Bahu of the Punjab sang" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 199, folio 168; p. 200, folio 169).

In another he sings that a single lesson of love outweighs all the learning of the schools, and that the heart in which the Beloved dwells needs no other pilgrimage. Bahu’s verse is at once fiercely devotional and gloriously musical, and the singing of his abyat — with the long-drawn “Hoo” — is one of the characteristic sounds of Punjabi Sufi devotion.

## In Verse

Beyond the opening stanza, the books carry his verse in English, and two of his texts in transliteration as they were sung.

Naveed Alam, introducing his translation of Shah Hussain, counts Sultan Bahu among the Punjabi Sufi poets who 'looked askance at scholars', and quotes him:

*You read and read, became a learned scholar, a seeker of gold / One hundred thousand books you read, but the cruel self didn't die / Without dispossession no one killed the thief within* (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 15, folio 15)

His note gives the source as the *Complete Abyat of Sultan Bahu*, edited by Muhammad Sharif Sabir (Fairfax, VA: Academy of Punjab in North America, n.d.), the tenth *bait*, lines 2–4, in Alam's own translation (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 179, folio 179).

Abbas records him sung. In her account a singer "sang the following text from Sultan Bahu in Siraiki", and Abbas transliterates it with her own marks for the vowels drawn out in the singing. Among its lines are these:

*Ilm paṛhe-e aśrāf na-a thīnḍā / Pītal dā soṇā-ā mū-ū-l nā theve-e / Shūm t sakha kadhā-a na theve-e / Alī binā-ā imān nahĩ Bāhū-ū-ū / Toṛe dafan vic Medīnā hū-ū*

*One does not become noble from reading books / Thou cannot buy gold for the price of copper / The miser will never become generous / Without Ali there is no faith-O Bahu / For i-t is buried far in Medina hu* (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 102, folio not stated)

In a second text the singer "derives her speech from Sultan Bahu" to speak of the 'caretakers of the faith', comparing them with the genuine believer:

*Paṛh paṛh ilm hazār kitāb- ālim hoe sāre hū / Ik harf iśq dā na paṛh jāṇan / Bhulan phiran bicāre hū*

*They read books and think they are scholars / They can't read a word of love / The poor souls are lost* (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 103, folio not stated)

*Lakh nigāh je ālim vekhe kise kandhī na caṛhī hū / Hik nigāh je aśiq vekhe lakh hazār tare hū*

*Thousands of scholars have we seen but none ever carried them on their shoulders / But when we see even a single lover, he appears like a myriad stars* (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 103, folio not stated)

Schimmel's *Pain and Grace* gives two of his sayings on the first letter, the *alif*:

*Who has studied the alif, needs not read the chapter b* (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 208, folio 208)

Writing of the study of that first letter, she adds that "Sultan Bahu in the Panjab uses exactly the same expression in his Golden Alphabet" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 209, folio 209); and elsewhere, "Sultan Bahu teaches in his Golden Alphabet:"

*True salvation belongs to those who put a mim to the alif!* (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 213, folio 213)

In an endnote of *As Through a Veil*, Schimmel sets the first verse of his *Abyat* beside a couplet from Angelus Silesius's *Cherubinischer Wandersmann* (I, 81), in a comparison with German Baroque mysticism (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 280, folio 262).

He wrote in Persian too. Sharib prints eight Persian lines of his in praise of Abd al-Qadir Jilani, beginning "شاہ میراں ہست ثانی شہ امیر" and ending "خاکپائے شاہ میراں راہ دیں"; the printed text of the eight lines is uncertain in six places, and they are not quoted here (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 317, folio 313). Schimmel, writing of Sind and the Punjab at large, observes that "Both provinces have also produced hymns extolling the great masters of the mystical orders", above all Abd al-Qadir Gilani (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 417, folio 386).

## His Books

A prolific author, he is credited with a great many works in Persian on the mystical path; and he composed the Punjabi verses that made him immortal.

Lahori's author writes that his works on *tawhid*, the oneness of God, are many, in Arabic and in Persian, and that he himself had seen three or four of them: "تصانیف عربی و فارسی میں بمضامین توحید بہت ہیں۔ چنانچہ تین چار کتابیں مؤلف کی نظر سے گذری ہیں". He names none of them. He calls the saint a great and deeply learned scholar, "حضرت بڑے عالم متبحر تھے", who united the outward and the inward sciences, "ظاہر و باطن علوم کے حضرت جامع تھے" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 265, folio 253). His Persian *diwan* too, the author says, is "نہایت مطبوع", a word that can mean either that it was printed or that it was well received: "ان کا فارسی دیوان بھی نہایت مطبوع ہے" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 265, folio 253).

Sharib makes him unlettered: "آپ لکھے پڑھے نہیں تھے' اس کے باوجود آپ بہت سی کتابوں کے مصنف ہیں", he could not read or write, and yet he is the author of many books (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 316, folio 312). Sharib lists twenty-four of them: "عین الفقر' گنج الاسرار' کلید التوحید' نور الہدی' محبت الاسرار' شمس العارفین' اورنگ شاہی' اسرار قادری' توفیق ہدایت' مجالستہ النبی' تیغ برہنہ' رسالہ روحی' قرب دیدار' کلید جنت' محک الفقر کبیر' محک الفقر صغیر' مفتاح العاشقین' کشف الاسرار' امیر الکونین' جامع الاسرار' عین الاسرار' قطب الاقطاب' محکم الفقراء حجتہ الاسرار" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 317, folio 313). Among them is the *Kalid al-Tawhid*, the book in which, Lahori's editor reports, the saint named his father. Of the poetry Sharib writes "آپ شاعر بھی تھے۔ "دیوان باہو" اور "ابیات باہو" آپ کی یادگار ہیں": he was a poet as well, and the *Diwan-e Bahu* and the *Abyat-e Bahu* are his memorials (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 317, folio 313).

Abbas writes that he wrote books on mysticism, all of them in Arabic and Persian except his *ābīāt* and *sihārfī*, which are in Panjabi (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 204, folio not stated).

## The Teaching in His Books

Sharib lets the saint speak for himself. He quotes, rather than summarises, passages on the world (دنیا), the kinds of murshid (مرشد کے اقسام), the faqir (فقیر), listening to music (سماع), perpetual remembrance (ذکر دوام), poverty (فقر), following the Prophet (سرور عالم صلی اللہ علیہ وسلم کی مطابعت), sayings (اقوال) and litanies (اوراد و وظائف), and he footnotes each to one of the saint's own books in Urdu translation (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 317–320, folios 313–316).

Among the kinds of murshid, the third attains perfection in rank and office, "مراتب و مناصب", and by that advancement reaches the rank of Pharaoh; the passage comes from the گنج الاسرار (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 317–318, folios 313–314).

He is as severe on the pir who lacks inward power and on the pir who leads by inheritance. "جو پیرو مرشد قوت باطنی نہ رکھے' ہر وقت مرید کی خبر گیری نہ کرے' اسے گناہ و معصیت سے روک نہ سکے اور مرید کی جاں کنی کے وقت اللہ تعالی اور رسول اللہ صلی اللہ علیہ وسلم سے دعا اور عرض نہ کرے… اسے پیرو مرشد نہ کہنا چاہیے": the pir who has no inward power, who does not watch over his disciple at every moment, who cannot hold him back from sin, and who does not pray and plead for him before God and the Prophet in his death agony, should not be called a pir. And one who leads "اپنی شیخ زادگی کے بھروسے", on the strength of being a shaykh's son, without following the Prophet, "خود بھی گمراہ ہو گا اور دوسروں کو بھی گمراہ کرے گا", will go astray himself and lead others astray; the passage comes from the کشف الاسرار (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 319, folio 315, حاشیہ 13). He adds: "پیری و مرشدی کوئی معمولی کام نہیں… حرص و حسد کا انجام آخر خواری و ذلت ہے": to be a pir and murshid is no ordinary work, and the end of greed and envy is disgrace (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 319, folio 315).

Under the heading of *sama*, Sharib quotes the saint against listening to music: "جو شخص کہ راگ سنتا ہے' اس کا دل مردہ اور نفس زندہ ہوتا ہے اہل سرور مردہ دل زندہ نفس ہوتے ہیں", whoever listens to *rag*, his heart is dead and his self alive; the people of pleasure are dead of heart and alive of self. The passage comes from the گنج الاسرار in Urdu translation (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 318, folio 314, حاشیہ 10).

Perpetual remembrance he defines as the hidden *dhikr*, and he likens it to salt: "ذکر دوام سے ذکر خفی مراد ہے… اسم اللہ سے وجود میں اس طرح جاری ہوتا ہے' جس طرح نمک ماکول و مشروب میں سرایت کر جاتا", through the name of Allah it runs through the whole being as salt pervades what is eaten and drunk (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 318, folio 314). The rest of the passage is too uncertain in the printed text to quote. Sharib also prints a gloss on the letters of the word *faqr*, in three readings that do not agree (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 319, folio 315).

On generosity he writes: "سخاوت کرنے سے خلق اللہ کا حق ادا ہوتا ہے اور معلوم ہوتا ہے کہ صاحب مال کو خدا کے مقابلہ میں اپنے مال کی کہاں تک محبت ہے", by giving, the due of God's creatures is paid, and it is shown how far the man of wealth loves his wealth against God; and "دنیا اور سیم و زر' فقیر عارف باللہ کے غلام ہیں", the world, silver and gold are the slaves of the faqir who knows God (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 319, folio 315).

For litanies Sharib gives, from the کشف الاسرار, a sura whose name is uncertain in the printed text, سورہ مزل[OCR?], and the دعاء سیفی, each with the conditions under which it takes effect (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 320, folio 316).

## The Shrine

Sultan Bahu’s shrine has a history bound to the wandering of the Chenab: the river’s floods forced the tomb to be moved and rebuilt more than once, and the present shrine at Garh Maharaja is a large and much-visited complex.

The books say little of the tomb. Lahori's author places it by its distance from the rivers: "حضرت کا روضہ جھنگ کے علاقہ میں پندرہ کوس مقام سے جہاں دریائے راوی و چناب ملتے ہیں۔ واقع ہے", the saint's *rauza* lies in the Jhang region, fifteen kos from the place where the Ravi and the Chenab meet (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 265, folio 253). His text names neither Garh Maharaja nor Shorkot; Shorkot appears in the book only in the editor's note, as the fort where the saint lived, in the saint's own words from the *Kalid al-Tawhid* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 265, folio 253). Sharib states plainly that his mazar is at Shorkot (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 316, folio 312); he writes the name as شور کوٹ and, twice, as شورکٹ, and never as گڑھ مہاراجہ (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 313, folio 309; p. 316, folio 312).

Sharib tells one event in the history of the grave, and tells it as a miracle: "آپ کی وفات کے ستر سال بعد جب چناب میں طغیانی آئی تو آپ کا تابوت قبر سے نکالا گیا' سب لوگ یہ دیکھ کر حیران ہوئے کہ پورا جسم مبارک صحیح و سالم تھا" — seventy years after his death, when the Chenab was in flood, his coffin was taken out of the grave, and all who saw it were astonished that the whole blessed body was sound (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 320, folio 316). He does not say where the body was then laid, who ordered the grave opened, or what became of the first grave.

The Sarwari Qadiri order he founded maintains his memory and his teaching.

The books consulted say nothing of the shrine's buildings or their builders, its custodians, its endowments or its *urs*, and Sharib names no *sajjada nashin* (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 316, folio 312). The only land Sharib connects with the family is the *jagir* that the father received at Shorkot, and the *jagir* land that the son twice cultivated and did not reap (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 313, folio 309; p. 316, folio 312).

## Devotion and Practice

It keeps the round of daily prayer, qawwali and the singing of his abyat, and the free kitchen, and it draws its greatest crowds at the annual urs, held in the spring, when devotees gather in vast numbers from across the Punjab to sing his verses and seek his blessing.

The books record the life of his poetry in performance rather than at the shrine. Abbas lists him among the spirits invoked at the outset of performances (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 99, folio not stated), and two items on the programme of a concert at Islamabad bear his name, "Sultan Bahu / Panjabi and Siraiki" and "Sultan Bahu, Shah Latif, and Bulle Shah / Siraiki, Sindhi, Panjabi, and Urdu" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 101, folio not stated). Schimmel names a Panjabi *siharfi* 'in honor of Sultan Bahu', verse composed for the saint, in the same sentence as a *barahmasa* of Pakpattan (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 287, folio 269).

Of his following, Lahori's author writes that the grace of his path flowed so freely that thousands of people benefited, "فیض طریقت اس قدر جاری تھا کہ ہزاروں لوگ مستفید ہوئے" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 265, folio 253).

## Legends and Miracles

Several of the wonders Sharib tells have been told above: the infant who would not drink milk by day in Ramadan, the *bai'at* at the court of the Prophet, the master who knew of his coming by *kashf*, the stages of the path passed in a moment, and the glance that made passers-by in the bazaars of Delhi God-attained (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 313–314, folios 309–310; p. 314, folio 310; p. 315, folio 311). Under the heading "کشف و کرامات" he sets the body found incorrupt when the grave was opened after the flood, seventy years after the burial (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 320, folio 316).

He tells too how the saint turned earth to gold. A man who had come to ask something of him found him at the plough, and was set to plough in his place while the saint went aside to relieve himself. Returning, "وہ ڈھیلے جن سے طہارت کی تھی' زمین پر دے مارے' زمین سونا ہو گئی": he struck upon the ground the clods with which he had cleansed himself, and the earth turned to gold. He told the man "جتنا سونا چاہو' لے جاؤ", take as much gold as you wish, and "وہ بہت سونا لے کر کامیاب و کامران واپس ہوا", the man went home with much gold, his errand fulfilled (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 320–321, folios 316–317).

Lahori's author carries the saint's following beyond people. After the thousands who benefited from the grace of his path, he writes, "بلکہ اس جنگل کے جس قدر پرند ہیں۔ وہ بھی یا ہُو کا ذکر کرتے ہیں": and more, all the birds of that wild country, they too make the *dhikr* of یا ہُو (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 265, folio 253).

## The Sources of His Life

Sharib and Lahori's editor both point to a life of the saint called the *Manaqib-e Sultani*. Sharib's notes cite it for the father's office and settlement, for the sons and for the name of the order (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 313, folio 309, حاشیہ 1; p. 316, folio 312, حاشیہ 6), and cite the saint's own books, in Urdu translation, for his name and his teaching (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 313, folio 309, حاشیہ 3; p. 318, folio 314, حاشیہ 10).

Lahori's editor lists three works on him. The first is "سلطان حامد : مناقب سلطانی (احوال و مناقب سلطان باہو)", Sultan Hamid's *Manaqib-e Sultani*, on the life and virtues of Sultan Bahu, a manuscript in the Sherani collection (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 265, folio 253, حاشیہ). The second is its Urdu translation, printed at Lahore, "مناقب سلطانی اردو ترجمہ مطبوعہ لاہور", and the third Bilal Zubairi's "تذکرہ اولیائے جھنگ", at its pages ۱۱۴ to ۱۲۶ (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 266, folio 254), which the editor's general list of sources dates to Lahore, 1968 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 301, folio 289). The same list enters the *Kalid al-Tawhid* from the Qasuri collection in the Ganj Bakhsh library, and the *Manaqib-e Sultani* again, as "حامد سلطان : مناقب سلطانی (احوال حضرت سلطان باہو ف ۱۱۰۲ھ)" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 292, folio 280).

The English-language books cite the editions and translations of the *Abyat* that they used. Schimmel's *Pain and Grace* lists "Bahoo, Sultan, Abyat, ed. and transl. Maqbul Ilahi, Lahore 1967." (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 295, folio 295); *As Through a Veil*, under the name Sultan Bahoo, the *Abyat* edited and translated by Maqbool Elahi, Lahore: Ashraf, 1967 (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 341, folio 323); and *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* Maqbool Elahi's translation, Lahore, 1967 (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 496, folio 465). Abbas cites the *Abiāt-e Bahu* edited by Maqbool Anwar Dawoodi, Lahore: Ferozesons (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 220, folio not stated), and Alam the *Complete Abyat of Sultan Bahu* edited by Muhammad Sharif Sabir (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 179, folio 179). Shackle's bibliography to Bulleh Shah lists two translations of 1998: Jamal J. Elias's *Death Before Dying: The Sufi Poems of Sultan Bahu*, from the University of California Press at Berkeley, and J. R. Puri and K. S. Khak's *Sultan Bahu*, from Radhasoami Satsang Beas at Dera Baba Jaimal Singh (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 203, folio 178). Alam explains the first title: "Death before dying, a common refrain in the Sufi poetry… Jamal Elias chose it as the title for his translations of Sultan Bahu's poetry which was published by the University of California Press" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 182, folio 182).

## Setting

The books that speak of the country around Shorkot speak of the river and the district, not of the shrine. Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* names Shorkot only as the northern boundary of the suba of Multan, "شمالی شورکوت" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525), and in its index (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 27); it does not mention the saint. Its account of the rivers has the Chenab join the Bihat "نزدیک قصبه جهنگ سیالان مسکن مسماة هیر که در عشق رانجها مشهور است", near the town of Jhang Siyalan, the home of Hir, who is famed for her love of Ranjha (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 511).

Abbas, writing of Waris Shah's *Hir*, calls Hir "the beautiful and witty daughter of Cucak Siyal, the powerful Jat chief of Jhang Siyal, which is a vast agricultural territory situated to the southwest of Lahore in Pakistan" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 121–122, folios not stated), and adds that "Although the myth is several hundred years old, villagers in the district of Jhang do identify Hir's tomb to this day" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 205, folio not stated). Haroon Khalid writes of "Jhang, the city where the legendary lovers, Heer and Ranjha, are buried in a single grave. Their shrine has now become a religious pilgrimage site", and adds that "The story was first written by a poet from Jhang called Damodar Das Arora during the reign of the Mughal Emperor Akbar" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated). Of the river he writes that "The Chenab, in the folk culture of Punjab, is known as the river of love" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 9, ch. 3, folio not stated).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The year of his birth.** The site's account gives 1630, in the Shorkot region of Jhang. Sharib gives 1039 AH, at Shorkot (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 313, folio 309); Rafat's selection gives 1631 (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 25, folio not stated); Alam gives 1629 (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 15, folio 15). Abbas says only that he was born in the Panjab (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 204, folio not stated).
- **The year and day of his death.** The site's account gives 1691, as do Rafat's selection (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 25, folio not stated), Schimmel's *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 416, folio 385; p. 526, folio 495) and the index of *As Through a Veil* (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 362, folio 344). Alam gives 1690 (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 15, folio 15). Schimmel's *Pain and Grace* gives 1691 in one place and 1694 in another (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 17, folio 13; p. 208, folio 208). Sharib gives the first of Jumada al-Thani 1102 AH (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 316, folio 312). Lahori's author gives no year and counts him among the saints whose date of death is not established (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 20, folio 8); his editor gives 1102 AH, from a colophon that names the third watch of the night of Friday in Jumada al-Thani but no day of the month (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 265, folio 253). Neither tazkira gives its Hijri date a Common Era equivalent.
- **Where he lies.** The site's account places the shrine at Garh Maharaja, in the Jhang region. Sharib places the mazar at Shorkot and never names Garh Maharaja (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 316, folio 312). Lahori's author places the tomb in the Jhang region, fifteen kos from the meeting of the Ravi and the Chenab, and names neither Garh Maharaja nor Shorkot (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 265, folio 253).
- **The moving of the tomb.** The site's account has the tomb twice moved and rebuilt as the Chenab shifted its course, its floods forcing the move more than once. Sharib records one opening of the grave, seventy years after his death, when the Chenab was in flood: the coffin was taken out and the body found sound. He tells it as a miracle and says nothing of a reburial or a rebuilding (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 320, folio 316).
- **The name of his order.** The site's account makes him the founder of the Sarwari Qadiri order. Sharib gives the name of his way, on the saint's own authority, as "قادریہ مسروریہ", the Qadiriyya Masruriyya (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 316, folio 312, حاشیہ 6). Lahori's author calls him a faqir of great rank in the Qadiri silsila (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 265, folio 253), and Schimmel speaks of the Qadiriyya order to which he belonged (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 161, folio 143). None of the books uses the name Sarwari.
- **His father.** The site's account makes his father an official of the Mughal administration and does not name him. Sharib names him Bayazid Muhammad, a *mansabdar* in Kohistan on behalf of Shahjahan, who refused to be sent back there and received a *jagir* at Shorkot (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 313, folio 309). Lahori's editor, quoting the saint's *Kalid al-Tawhid*, gives the name as "بازید عرف اعوان", Bazid known as Awan (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 265, folio 253, حاشیہ).
- **How he got his name.** The site's account says that tradition credits his mother with the name, “Bahu,” “with Hoo”. Sharib quotes the saint's own words that his mother named him, and says he rejoiced at a word in his name whose reading is uncertain in the printed text (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 313, folio 309, حاشیہ 3). Schimmel writes that he got his surname because each line of his Golden Alphabet ends with the exclamation *hu* (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 416, folio 385).
- **His master, and the way to him.** The site's account has him initiated by Sayyid Abdul Rahman of Delhi. Sharib sets before this a *bai'at* at the court of the Prophet and his handing over to Abd al-Qadir Jilani (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 314, folio 310), and a first shaykh, Shah Habibullah (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 314–315, folios 310–311); he prints the Delhi master's name both as "حضرت شیخ عبدالرحمان قادری" and as "حضرت سید عبدالرحمان قادری" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 315, folio 311). Lahori's editor begins the chain of masters with "سید عبد الرحمان" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 265, folio 253).
- **His learning and his books.** The site's account calls him a prolific author of works in Persian. Lahori's author speaks of many works in Arabic and Persian and calls him a great and deeply learned scholar (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 265, folio 253). Sharib says he could not read or write, and yet lists twenty-four books (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 316, folio 312; p. 317, folio 313). Abbas writes that all his books are in Arabic and Persian except his *ābīāt* and *sihārfī*, which are in Panjabi (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 204, folio not stated).
- **His verse form.** The site's account names the *si-harfi* and, most famously, the *abyat*, which it calls quatrains. Schimmel's *Pain and Grace* says that the most famous of his poems are in the *siharfi* form, the Golden Alphabet (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 17, folio 13). Abbas names his form as the *bait* (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 178, folio not stated); Rafat's selection says that he "accepted the restraint of the bait" and calls the *siharfi* his favourite device (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 25, folio not stated; p. 50, folio 30).
- **The worth of the verse.** The site's account calls his verse at once fiercely devotional and gloriously musical. Schimmel writes that his verses "do nothing but develop ideas well known to earlier Sufis", though "his approach is worth mentioning as typical of the way rural mystics spread the ideas of Sufism." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 416, folio 385). Rafat's selection says that his *siharfi* method has "introduced a needless and tedious mannerism" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 50, folio 30).
- **The preceptor in the verse.** The site's account names the indispensable role of the spiritual guide as one of his themes, and his most famous verse speaks of the master who planted the jasmine; in Schimmel's English the stanza ends "May the efficient preceptor live long, says Bahu, who planted this plant—o Hu!" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 416, folio 385 – p. 417, folio 386). Rafat's selection counts him as one of the two predecessors of Bulleh Shah who "had no personal reference to make to an actual preceptor-beloved" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 45, folio 25).
- **Music.** The site's account says the shrine keeps *qawwali* and the singing of his abyat, and Abbas records his texts sung in performance (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 102, folio not stated; p. 103, folio not stated). Sharib quotes the saint's own teaching, under the heading of *sama*, that whoever listens to *rag* has a dead heart and a living self (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 318, folio 314, حاشیہ 10).
- **The first verse in English.** The site's account renders the opening as "Alif — Allah — is a jasmine plant that my master has planted within my heart, Hoo." *As Through a Veil* has "Alif: Allah is a jasmine bud which the preceptor has planted in my heart" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 160, folio 142); *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* has "Alif: Allah is like the jasmine plant which the preceptor planted in my heart—o Hu!" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 416, folio 385).
- **The number of his sons.** Sharib counts eight sons by his four wives, but names nine, the youngest of them dead in childhood (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 316, folio 312, حاشیہ 5).
- **The manuscript of the Manaqib-e Sultani.** Lahori's editor gives its number in the Sherani collection as ۲۴۷ / ۳۲۵۲ [OCR?] in one note (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 265, folio 253, حاشیہ) and as ۲۹۷/۳۲۵۲[OCR?] in his list of sources (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 292, folio 280).

## Legacy

Sultan Bahu ranks with Shah Hussain, Bulleh Shah, and Waris Shah among the immortals of Punjabi poetry, and his abyat — with their unmistakable refrain of “Hoo” — are among the most widely sung of all Sufi verses in the language. As poet, prolific author, and founder of an enduring order, he shaped the spiritual life of central Punjab, and his shrine at Garh Maharaja remains a great centre of the devotion he did so much to inspire.

The books place him in a line of Punjabi poets. Rafat's selection names "the Punjabi sufi poets Baba Farid, Shah Hussain, Sultan Bahu, who were his predecessors", the predecessors of Bulleh Shah (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 45, folio 25), and Alam sets him among the poets who looked askance at scholars (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 15, folio 15). Schimmel, for whom he is the first great Panjabi mystical poet, judges that his approach is typical of the way rural mystics spread the ideas of Sufism (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 416, folio 385). Writing of the province as a whole, she calls the late 17th and the 18th century "The golden age of Muslim Panjabi poetry" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 17, folio 13), and writes that while the urban poets of India praised the Prophet in grandiose hymns, "their contemporaries in the plains of Sind and the Punjab wrote tender little poems or instructive Golden Alphabets to instil love for him into the souls of the villagers" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 222, folio 204).

His verse has been read in other traditions too. In the introduction to a Hindu Sufi's breviary that Boivin describes, "there are interesting parallels drawn with Sultan Bahu's poetry, he being an important seventeenth-century Sufi from Punjab, Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani, and, not surprisingly, the Guru Granth Sahib, from which copious abstracts are given" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 121, folio 108). Boivin also notes, citing Ladkani, that "Non-Sindhi poets are also quoted, including Bullhe Shah, Shah Bahu and Ghulam Farid"; the 'Shah Bahu' there may be this poet, though the book does not say so (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 171, folio 158). Schimmel sets him beside Angelus Silesius (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 280, folio 262), and his poems have reached English readers under the title *Death Before Dying* (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 203, folio 178).
