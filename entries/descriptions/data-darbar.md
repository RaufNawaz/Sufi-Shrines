## Overview

Data Darbar, in the old heart of Lahore, is the greatest and most beloved shrine in Pakistan — the resting place of Hazrat Ali ibn Usman al-Hujwiri (c. 1009–1072/77), the eleventh-century saint and scholar known to all simply as *Data Ganj Bakhsh*, “the Master who bestows the treasure.” He was the first great Sufi to make his home in Lahore, the author of *Kashf al-Mahjub*, the earliest treatise on Sufism written in Persian, and, in the devotion of the Punjab, the very patron of the city, which is often called *Data ki Nagri* — “the city of Data.” His tomb is more than a shrine: it is a vast devotional complex where prayer, learning, music, and above all the free kitchen (langar) come together in the daily life of hundreds of thousands, and it stands at the fountainhead of the whole tradition of Sufism in South Asia. To understand Data Darbar is to begin with the life of the saint whose blessing (baraka) is believed still to fill it.

The books that describe the shrine all speak of its standing. Kanhaiya Lal's history of Lahore counts this blessed and ancient tomb among the famous and adorned mazars of the city, to which Muslim devotees come from far for its *ziyarat* (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 288, folio 291). Annemarie Schimmel writes that his "tomb in Lahore is still a place of pilgrimage for the people" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 8, folio 4), and again: "His shrine, called that of Data Ganj Bakhsh, is still a popular place of pilgrimage in Lahore." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 119, folio 88). Scott Kugle calls it "the shrine of Lahore's patron saint" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 206, folio 185); Omar Kasmani, "Lahore's most prominent shrine complex" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 65, folio 48); Haroon Khalid, "the most important shrine of Lahore" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated), and, in another book, "one of the most popular Sufi Shrines in the city, revered by people from different religions" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 207, folio not stated).

The older accounts set it by the Bhati gate. Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori's *Hadiqat al-Awliya* places the grave in the saint's own khanqah, and gives its present location as outside the Bhati Gate (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 289, folio 277). Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti's *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* sets the khanqah outside the same gate, on its west side (the gate's name is uncertain in the printed text), on ground that was formerly a graveyard (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 165). Kanhaiya Lal lists the mazar of Data Ganj Bakhsh Lahori, the last word uncertainly printed, among the buildings outside Lahore connected with Islam (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 4, folio 3). Khalid places "the shrine of the eleventh century Sufi, Ali Hujwiri" "near the walled city of Lahore" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 207, folio not stated), and Robert Rozehnal "in the old city of Lahore" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 206, folio 198). Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian history says simply that the tomb is in Lahore: in this city, it says, lies the resting place of Mir Ali Hujwiri, the choicest of the great saints (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 524).

The shrine goes by many names in these books. Rozehnal never calls it Data Darbar: it is "the tomb complex of his patron saint 'Ali Hujwiri", "Hujwiri's shrine" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 75, folio 65) and "al-Hujwiri's tomb", and to the disciples he interviewed its saint is "Hazrat Data Sahab", "Data Sahab" or "Data Ganj Bakhsh" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 207, folio 199). A woman interviewed by Ewing says simply, "I went to Data Sahib" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 178, folio 167). Shemeem Burney Abbas uses "Data Sahib" and "Data Darbar" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 162, folio not stated; pp. 20–21, folios not stated); Sharib's tazkira names it the mazar of Hazrat Data Ganj Bakhsh, twice calling it the radiant mazar (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 250, folio 246); and Chishti's table of contents gives it a heading of its own, on the tomb of Data Ganj Bakhsh Lahori, the word Data uncertainly printed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 1).

## The Life of Ali Hujwiri

Ali Hujwiri was born around the turn of the eleventh century in Ghazni, in what is now Afghanistan, into a learned and pious family — his own epithets, *al-Jullabi* and *al-Hujwiri*, recall the quarters of Ghazni from which he came. He grew into a scholar and mystic of the first rank — a Sunni of the Hanafi school — uniting deep intellectual study with an inward devotion, and in the manner of the seekers of his age he travelled widely across the Muslim world — through Iran, Iraq, Syria, and Central Asia — sitting at the feet of the masters of the day. His own spiritual guide was Abu’l-Fadl Muhammad al-Khuttali, who linked him to the “sober” Sufism of the school of Junayd of Baghdad, that tradition which insists that the mystical life be lived in full obedience to the Sharia and the example of the Prophet, rather than in intoxicated excess.

The books add a birthplace, a family and a pedigree. Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi writes: "As his name implies, Hujwiri was born in the Ghazna suburb, about 1009" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 130, folio 112). Zahur ul-Hasan Sharib says that he was born in Ghazni, without giving a year; that his forefathers were residents of Ghazni; that his grandfather's name was Ali; and that his father's name was Usman (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 21, folio 17; p. 21, folio 17, note 1). The editor of the 1974 Urdu edition of Lahori's *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, following Muhammad Musa Hakim Amritsari's *Muqaddama Kashf al-Mahjub*, gives his year of birth as 400 AH, a year he himself calls indeterminate, the figure uncertain in the printed text (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 196).

On the names he bears from Ghazni, Chishti records Hujwir, the name uncertainly printed, and Jullab as mohallas of Ghaznin (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 163). Sharib explains, without citing a source, that Hujwir is not the name of a city but of a quarter of Ghazni, and that is why he is called Ali Hujwiri (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 21, folio 17). Chishti gives his father as Hazrat Usman ibn Ali Jalali Ghaznawi, the last name uncertainly printed, his madhhab as that of the Imam-e Azam of Kufa, and his silsila as the Junaidi (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 163); Schimmel notes that "Hujwiri praised Abu Hanifa the Kufian" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 201, folio 201).

The Urdu accounts make him a sayyid of the line of Imam Hasan. Lahori's text makes him a Hasani sayyid and sets out the descent: Makhdoom Ali Ganj Bakhsh Hujwiri bin Sayyid Usman bin Sayyid Ali bin Abd al-Rahman bin Shah Shuja bin Abu al-Hasan Ali bin Husain Asghar bin Sayyid Zaid Shahid bin Imam Hasan bin Ali, a genealogy the edition's editor contests (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 194). Kanhaiya Lal, also calling him a Hasani sayyid, prints the descent in full: Shaikh Ali bin Sayyid Usman bin Sayyid Ali bin Sayyid Abd al-Rahman bin Sayyid Abd Allah (a name uncertainly printed) bin Sayyid Abu al-Hasan Ali bin Sayyid Hasan bin Sayyid Zaid Shahid bin Imam Hasan bin Ali Murtaza (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 291, folio 294). Chishti, too, calls Hazrat Ali Makhdoom Ganj Bakhsh Hujwiri a Hasani sayyid, and gives yet another line: Hazrat Ali Ganj Bakhsh bin Shaiban bin Ali bin Sakar bin Sad bin Abd al-Rahman bin Sayyid Abd al-Fath Hujwiri bin Salam al-Waris bin Sayyid Hasan bin Sayyid Zaid Shahid bin Hazrat Imam Hasan bin Ali al-Murtaza, in which Shaiban, Sakar, Abd al-Rahman, Abd al-Fath, Salam and al-Waris are uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 172). Sharib says only that he was of the Hasani sayyids, the line running through a few intermediaries whose number he does not give, to Zaid Shahid, son of Imam Hasan (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 21, folio 17).

Sharib relates that having finished the outward sciences he turned to the inward (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 22, folio 18). He names as his teacher (*ustad*) Shaikh Abu al-Qasim, whose saying on attending a murshid he quotes: a murshid is one who is a diver, not one of a second kind whose printed name, perhaps *muttasil*, cannot be read with certainty; one should take *bai'at* only if one finds strength in oneself, and if not, both will be ruined (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 22, folio 18, note 4). Rizvi draws the teachers from the saint's own book: "He studied under several sufi masters, some of whom he names in his Kashf al-Mahjub were Abu'l-Qasim Gurgani, Khwaja Muzaffar, Abu'l-‘Abbas Ahmad bin Muhammad al-Ashqani. However, Khattali of Syria was his main teacher", that is, "Abu'l-Fazl Muhammad bin al-Hasan Khattali, a disciple of Husri (d. 371/981-82) of the school of Junaid" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 130, folio 112). Schimmel makes him "One of Qushayri's colleagues, who had attended his meetings and had visited almost every leading Sufi of his time" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 119, folio 88), and a contemporary of ʿAbdullah-i Ansari, "whose work, like that of Hujwiri, was written in part in his Persian mother tongue" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 120, folio 89).

His initiation is traced through a short chain. In Lahori's account he took *bai'at* with Shaikh Abu al-Fazl bin Hasan Khuttali Junaidi, who had taken it with Shaikh Husri, who had taken it with Shaikh Abu Bakr Shibli; and when his training was complete his pir gave him leave to guide the people of Hind (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 194). Sharib makes him the murid of Shaikh Abu al-Fazl bin Hasan Khuttali, and continues that Khuttali was the murid of a Hazrat whose printed name, perhaps Khizri, cannot be read with certainty, and he of Shaikh Shibli (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 22, folio 18, note 6). Kanhaiya Lal runs the chain from Khwaja Abu al-Fazl bin Hasan Khuttali through Shaikh Ali Haqiri, both names uncertainly printed, and on through Shibli and Junaid of Baghdad to Ali al-Murtaza (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 291, folio 294); Chishti's chain, ending likewise with Ali al-Murtaza, is uncertain at several points in the printed text and names no order (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 172).

His travels are given in two lists. Sharib writes that he travelled through Khurasan, Transoxiana, Marw, Azerbaijan and other lands, where he met many darwishes and drew benefit from the spiritual graces of Shaikh Abu al-Qasim Gurgani, Shaikh Abu Sa'id Abu al-Khair and Shaikh Abu al-Qasim Qushairi (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 22, folio 18, note 7). Rizvi writes that "Even after settling in Lahore, Hujwiri kept in contact with sufis in Transoxiana, Khurasan and Syria" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 130, folio 112).

Sharib gives him the titles Sultan al-Tariqat, Ganj al-Haqiqat, Burhan al-Shariat (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 21, folio 17), and sums up his character: he was the *qutb* of the age, engaged in zikr and fikr, *muraqaba* and *muhasaba*, *ibadat* and *riyazat*; he was clean of worldly taint; and his *tasarrufat* are countless (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 23, folio 19). Chishti calls him *qutb al-aqtab*, the pole of poles, the first word uncertainly printed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 164).

## The Coming to Lahore

In time, in the middle of the eleventh century — by the usual reckoning around 1039 CE (431 AH), in the age of the Ghaznavids — Hujwiri came to Lahore, then a frontier city newly brought within the Muslim world, and there he settled. He built a small mosque, taught, preached, and served the poor, living in humility and voluntary poverty even though the doors of royal circles were open to him. His presence transformed the city: under his teaching Lahore became a centre of Sufi learning and devotion, and the seed he planted would flower into the whole later history of Islam in the north-western subcontinent. He died in Lahore between about 1072 and 1077 CE (465–469 AH), and his grave, from the first, drew those who had loved him.

He went to Lahore, Rizvi, Chishti and Sharib agree, at his pir's command; two of them add that he objected. Rizvi writes that his pir "asked a young disciple … to follow Husain Zinjani" to the Panjab, and that "Hujwiri objected on the grounds that already an eminent sufi was there. His pir, however, ordered him to obey" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 130, folio 112). In Chishti's account he was told by his pir to go to Lahore although his pir-bhai Husain Zanjani was already there (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 163). Sharib tells the command as a dream, for which he footnotes no source: he came to Hindustan by his murshid's command; one night his pir appeared to him in a dream and told him: you have been made the *qutb* of Lahore, Lahore has been entrusted to you, go to Lahore. He objected in the dream that Khwaja Hasan Zanjani was already there, so that his own going to Lahore in that man's presence would be of no use, and the pir replied that there was no need for argument and that he should set out quickly (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 22, folio 18).

All three tell of the funeral that met him. "Hujwiri reached Lahore after dark. In the morning Shaikh Husain Zinjani's coffin was carried out for burial—he had died during the night" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 130, folio 112). Chishti has him reach the city at night, meet the funeral of Hazrat Husain Zanjani the next morning, and see to the shrouding and the burial (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 163). In Sharib he stayed outside the city because night had fallen, and entering in the morning saw a bier and learned that it was the funeral of Shaikh Hasan Zanjani; now he understood what the expediency in his pir's command had been (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 23, folio 19, note 9). Lahori's editor separates two men of that name: the brother-in-the-path named in the *Fawaid al-Fuad* is a different person from the well-known Shaikh Husain Zanjani, who died in 600 AH (a year uncertain in the printed text) and is buried at Chah Miran (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 196).

When he came, and with whom, the books tell differently. Kanhaiya Lal writes that he came to Lahore in the company of Sultan Masud, son of Sultan Mahmud, and that he honoured Lahore with his auspicious arrival (the word for arrival is uncertain in the printed text) in 431 AH, died in 465, and spread outward and inward knowledge through thirty-four years in Lahore (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 289, folio 292). Sharib sets two accounts side by side and judges neither: some think that he came with the army of Sultan Masud son of Sultan Mahmud Ghaznavi, while others disagree and say he came in the company of Shaikh Ahmad Jamawi[OCR?] Sarakhsi and Shaikh Abu Sa'id Hujwiri (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 22–23, folios 18–19, note 8). Rizvi offers a date by inference: "It would seem that Hujwiri probably reached Lahore in c. 1035, while Sultan Mas‘ud I (1031-41) reigned in Ghazna" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 130, folio 112). Bhandari's *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, which writes his name Mir Ali Hujwiri and calls him one who held learning and sainthood in a single embrace (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 523–524), says instead that he came from Ghazni with Sultan Mahmud and died in Lahore, and that the Sultan credited the conquest of Lahore to the blessing of his arrival (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 523). Schimmel writes: "He later came to Lahore, the capital of the Ghaznawids in India, and he died there in 1071." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 119, folio 88).

Of his life in the city, Sharib writes that he took permanent residence in Lahore and occupied himself in teaching, *talqin* and guidance (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 23, folio 19). Chishti has him stay where the khanqah is and build a small mosque at his own expense (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 163). Lahori describes the city he came to, at that time a resort of scholars and men of learning, and what he did there: coming to Lahore, he stirred it with learning and spiritual mastery; he taught students by day and instructed seekers by night, and the book counts the thousands transformed by him: through him thousands of the ignorant became learned, thousands of unbelievers Muslims, thousands of the misguided rightly guided, thousands of madmen sane, thousands of the imperfect perfect, and thousands of sinners righteous (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 194). Of his years in the city Lahori's author writes that he lived in Lahore all his life (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 195).

Rizvi, by contrast, gives him a life that went on reaching far beyond Lahore: "He made long trips at least twice during his lifetime, each for more than two years. When Khattali died in 1065, Hujwiri was by his side. After 1067, Hujwiri returned to Lahore, remaining there until his death" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 130, folio 112). Schimmel's *As Through a Veil* speaks of him as the one "who spent his last years in Lahore" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 61, folio 51).

Nor was the city, in Rizvi's reading of the saint's own book, a welcoming one: "The Kashf al-Mahjub suggests that in the eleventh century a number of mystics and scholars who had settled in Lahore were strongly hostile to the views of its author." An unnamed "expert in Qur'anic commentaries, sharply disagreed with his interpretation of" *fana'* and *baqa'*; "Some Lahore sufis believed that gnosis emanated from inspiration; Hujwiri disparagingly called this view ‘Brahmanical.’"; and "These developments so distressed Hujwiri that he considered himself a ‘captive among uncongenial folk’ in Lahore." Rizvi's footnote adds, citing Nicholson, that "The original does not indicate that he was actually imprisoned" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 131, folio 113). Elsewhere Rizvi recalls "Hujwiri's disappointment with certain sufis of his own time, whom he called Hululis", and "his assessment of current Brahmanical influence on sufi thought in Islam" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 345, folio 323).

The mosque he built is remembered in every account. Lahori writes that he built his house and his mosque himself, and they stood when the author wrote; its mihrab was then somewhat askew towards the south compared with other mosques, and the ulama objected to it (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 194). Sharib tells the same: after settling in Lahore he began to have a mosque built, and the ulama objected that it did not face the qibla, for its mihrab appeared to face south; he answered none of the objections, and the work went on (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 27, folio 23). Chishti, following Dara Shikoh's *Safinat al-Awliya*, records that the qibla inclined to the south, and that the ulama of Lahore objected (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 163–164). How the dispute ended belongs among his miracles.

## His Death and Its Dates

Lahori's author gives the year of his death as 465 AH, saying that he died at the end of that year and was buried in the courtyard of his own khanqah (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 195). He seals the year with three Persian chronograms, each printed with 465 against it (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 195).

علی غزنوی آن شاہ ہجویر
سراپا نور روشن ماہ ہجویرے [OCR?]

*The first of the three chronograms on his death; its last word is uncertain in the printed text.* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 195)

عیاں تاریخ او چوں ماہ گفتم
علی ہجویری عالی جاہ گفتم

*The second chronogram, printed with the year 465.* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 195)

چو جستم از خرد تاریخ سالش
عیاں شد کاشف دین ارتحالش

*The third chronogram, printed with the year 465; its phrase Kashif-e Din, revealer of the faith, is also the subject of a chronogram that Chishti records.* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 195; Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 165)

The same page carries a footnote that begins by saying that the year of death is disputed, and breaks off at the foot of the page (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 195). On the next page the edition's editor rejects the author's year outright, under the heading "Recent Research on Ali Hujwiri": the year 465 AH, he writes, is wrong, for the internal evidence of the *Kashf al-Mahjub* shows that he was still alive in 490 AH, a figure uncertain in the printed text, as Abd al-Hai Habibi has argued; and the broken footnote completes the thought: on that evidence the book's composition has been dated to 490 AH, so that he was alive until then (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 196, folio 184). The evidence is the date at which his own book was composed, not the date of his death: the editor's claim is that he was alive in 490 AH [OCR?], and he names no year of death of his own. The publisher's catalogue at the back of the same edition still prints the older dates for Sayyid Ali Hujwiri, 400–465, with no era marked (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 353). Among the authorities the edition lists is Abd al-Hai Habibi's article on the date of his death in the *Oriental College Magazine*, dated February 1940 [OCR?] in one place (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 196–197) and February 1960 in the edition's book list (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 305, folio 293).

Chishti too finds the accounts at odds. Jami's *Nafahat al-Uns*, as he reports it, gives 495 AH; the *Tazkirat al-Asfiya* gives 465 AH; and the year he takes from Dara Shikoh's *Safinat al-Auliya* is illegible in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 165). He prints chronograms as well: one on the word *sardar*, chief, yielding 495; qitas that Mufti Ghulam Sarwar sent for his book, one giving 464, one on the word *sarwar*, leader, giving 466, and one on *kashif-e din*; and one by Maulvi Ahmad Bakhsh Chishti, his name uncertainly printed, on *sardar* and *zewar-e Lahore*, the ornament of Lahore. Every one of these figures is uncertain in the printed text. His account, he says, was written from the *Safina* and other books (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 165).

Kanhaiya Lal gives 465, thirty-four years after an arrival in 431 (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 289, folio 292). Sharib prints two years side by side and takes no side: he journeyed to the abode of the hereafter in 465 AH, citing the *Safinat al-Awliya* (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 23, folio 19, note 10); and in the view of some his passing was in 456 AH, citing the *Kashf al-Asrar* (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 23, folio 19, note 11). He gives neither day nor month nor his age at death (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 23, folio 19).

Rizvi sets three positions in one paragraph, one of them read off the tomb itself: "According to the tablet on his tomb, the saint died in 465/1072-73. Nicholson believed that Hujwiri died sometime between 465 and 469/1076—77. However, the Kashf al-Mahjub recorded Khwaja ‘Abdullah Ansari, who died in 1089, as amongst the contemporary saints. It would seem, therefore, that Hujwiri died some time after that date" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 130, folio 112). His index accordingly heads the saint "(d. after 1089)" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 468, folio 444).

The English-language studies print a run of Common Era years. Schimmel gives "(d.1071)" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 8, folio 4); "Hujwiri (d. circa 1071)" and "he died there in 1071" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 37, folio 6; p. 119, folio 88), with "(d. ca. 1071)" in that book's index (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 516, folio 485) and "(d. c. 1071)" in the index of another (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 355, folio 337). Carl Ernst and Bruce Lawrence print four different years and reconcile none: "Shaykh 'Ali Hujwiri (d. 1074)" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 22, folio 7), "Hujwiri (d. ca. 1071)" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 52, folio 38), "'Ali Hujwiri (d. 1072)" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 138, folio 125), and "(d. 465/1073)" in their Persian bibliography (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 236, folio 226). Rozehnal prints "'Ali Hujwiri (d. 1072)" once (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 72, folio 62) and "(d. 1074)" four times (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 171, folio 163; p. 206, folio 198; p. 263, folio not stated; p. 276, folio 270). Regula Qureshi dates him only by his century, as "eleventh-century" (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 103, folio 83).

## Kashf al-Mahjub

Hujwiri’s enduring gift to the world is his book. *Kashf al-Mahjub* — “The Unveiling of the Veiled” — is the earliest formal treatise on Sufism composed in the Persian language, and it remains one of the classics of the tradition, studied wherever Sufism is studied. In it Hujwiri sets out the doctrines and disciplines of the mystical path: the nature of the soul and of knowledge, the meaning of sainthood, the practices and states of the Sufis, the sayings and lives of the earlier masters, and the etiquette of the seeker. Its teaching is of a piece with the man: a “sober” mysticism that weds spiritual devotion to ethical conduct, prayer, humility, and self-restraint, and holds that inward transformation matters more than any outward display of piety. At its heart lies the ideal of *faqr* — spiritual poverty, the emptying of the self of ego, pride, and worldly craving — together with the remembrance of God (*dhikr*), sincere service to others, and the purification of the heart. Hujwiri urges the seeker to keep the company of a realised guide and warns sharply against the fraud who counterfeits sanctity or chases miracles, fame, and rank. Through this book, more even than through his tomb, the saint has gone on teaching for nine centuries.

The books concur on its primacy. Rizvi: "Hujwiri's Kashf al-Mahjub is also the first known manual of sufism written in Persian. Composed towards the end of his life, the work draws upon the vast source material available in Arabic and is a most authoritative exposition of sufism according to Junaid's school"; and "Hujwiri however, is not merely a compiler. Firstly, he explains the doctrines of various schools, then he relates and assesses them. His comments form the most authoritative classification of sufi thoughts and practices" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 131, folio 113). "Of his many works only his greatest, the Kashf al-Mahjub, has survived," Rizvi writes in his account of the Sufi literature of the age, where the saint stands between Qushairi and Ghazali (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 80, folio 64). For Schimmel it is "the first Persian treatise on Islamic mysticism" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 8, folio 4) and "the first major treatise on Sufi theories in Persian" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 61, folio 51), part of a Persian mystical literature that "developed first in the Eastern Lands of the Caliphate, namely in Herat and northwest India, in the second half of the eleventh century" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 61, folio 51). His "important innovation", she writes, rendering the title 'Unveiling of the Hidden', is that he wrote it in Persian "and thus ushered in a new period in mystical literature" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 119, folio 88).

Schimmel's judgement runs across a page: the book is "A monument of early Persian and noteworthy for its expressiveness", one that "contains much interesting information rarely found in other sources"; its author's "inclination toward systematization sometimes goes too far", yet "it is singularly valuable in its approach and its balanced discussion" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 119, folio 88; p. 120, folio 89). The praise she quotes within it, that the book is one "which belongs to the valid and famous books", is Jami's (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 119, folio 88). She places its writing in Lahore, the city of "Hujwiri, who composed his famous Persian treatise on Sufism in this town" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 376, folio 345), and finds it among the books it became fashionable to buy in the Delhi of Nizamuddin, as Barani lists them (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 379, folio 348). Kanhaiya Lal calls it a book in Persian on the science of *tasawwuf* that has no equal on the face of the earth, and adds that he wrote many books in Arabic [OCR?] and Persian (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 291, folio 294).

Its English title varies from book to book. Ernst and Lawrence call it "the Sufi classic *The Unveiling of the Concealed*" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 138, folio 125). Rozehnal calls it "the famous Persian treatise on Sufism written by the eleventh-century master 'Ali Hujwiri (d. 1072)", glossing the title as 'The Unveiling of the Secrets' (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 72, folio 62), and elsewhere "al-Hujwiri's famous tome titled Kashf al-mahjub [The Unveiling of the Concealed]" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 111, folio 102).

The scholars read it in a handful of editions. Reynold A. Nicholson's English translation in the Gibb Memorial Series, first published in 1911, is the one most of them cite: Schimmel in its London reprint of 1959 (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 480, folio 449), with "3d ed . 1959, and often" in another of her books (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 325, folio 307); Qureshi in a 1970 printing from Luzac in London (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 270, folio 250); Ewing in a 1976 reprint issued at Lahore by the Islamic Book Foundation (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 193, folio 182); Ernst and Lawrence in a 1976 Luzac reprint (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 204, folio 193); and Rozehnal as "Translated by Reynold A. Nicholson. Leiden: E. J. Brill, 1911." (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 264, folio 258). Rizvi's bibliography lists "Hujwiri, Kashfu'l-Mahjub, Lahore, 1923, English tr. R.A. Nicholson, London, 1936." (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 442, folio 418). The Persian text, Schimmel notes, was edited by V. A. Zhukovsky at Leningrad in 1926 and reprinted at Tehran (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 480, folio 449); the list of authorities in Lahori's edition cites Zhukovsky's introduction to his edition, printed at Moscow, with a second printing at Tehran (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 196–197).

The book lives on in the translations of Sufi teachers. Wahid Bakhsh Sial Rabbani, Rozehnal records, translated it from Persian into Urdu with a commentary (Lahore: al-Faisal Publishers, 1995), and his English version, *The Kashful Mahjub ('Unveiling the Veiled'): The Earliest Persian Treatise on Sufism*, was published by disciples at Kuala Lumpur in 1997 (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 246, folio 239). The publisher of Lahori's edition runs a department named for the saint, the Shaikh Ali bin Usman Hujwiri Department, and advertises three editions of the book: a Persian one, the Tehran text, edited by Ali Qawim; an Urdu one, the Moscow text, in a translation by Sayyid Muhammad Faruq al-Qadiri with a foreword by Hakim Muhammad Musa Amritsari; and an English one, the Lahore text, Nicholson's translation, with a foreword by Hazrat Shahidullah Faridi (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 354).

## His Other Works, and the Poet

The *Kashf al-Mahjub* names other books of his own. Rizvi lists them from it: the Minhaj al-Din, "a work on sufi practices, containing a detailed account of the Ahl-i Suffa and a full biography of Husain bin Mansur al-Hallaj"; the Asrar al-khiraq wa'l-ma‘unat, "a work on the patched garments worn by sufis"; the Kitab-i fana' wa baqa', written, as the author relates, ‘in the vanity and rashness of youth’; "A work of which no title is given, in which the sayings of al-Hallaj are explained"; the Kitab al-Bayan li-Ahl al-‘Iyan, "a work on the union with God"; the Bahr al-Qulub; the Ri'ayat li-Huquq Allah, on Divine Unity; and "A work on faith of which the title is not specified" — a numbered list whose later numerals are lost in the printed text (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 131, folio 113). Sharib names six: the *Minhaj al-Din*, *al-Bayan li-Ahl al-Iyan*, *Asrar al-Khiraq wa al-Mu'unat*, *Kashf al-Asrar*, *al-Riayat li-Huquq Allah* and the *Kashf al-Mahjub*, the second, third and fifth printed in forms that cannot be read with certainty (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 23, folio 19, note 12). Schimmel recalls a lost one: in "a book written in the mid-eleventh century", no longer extant, he, "in spite of all his admiration for Hallaj, felt obliged to declare him" 'not firmly settled' (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 104, folio 73).

The *Kashf al-Asrar* is a book in dispute. Sharib lists it among his works and leans on it more than on any other source, citing its Urdu translation in nine of the twenty footnotes to his chapter (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 29, folio 25). Lahori's editor removes it from his works: the *Kashf al-Asrar*, he writes, is not his composition but a forged book merely attributed to him (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 196). The publisher's catalogue of the same edition attaches it to him again, advertising Urdu versions of the *Kashf al-Asrar* as the work of Hazrat Data Ganj Bakhsh Lahori (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 354).

He was also a poet. Sharib writes that he was a poet, that his *takhallus* was Ali, and that he had a diwan — and quotes the saint's own words, that he had composed many verses and poems, and a diwan as well (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 23, folio 19, note 13). Sharib prints a Persian ghazal of seven couplets attributed to him, too damaged in the printed text to quote (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 24, folio 20). In Shemeem Burney Abbas's fieldwork a qawwal at Kasur, Muhammad Bakhsh, lists his poetic sources: "We use poetic texts from Hazrat Muinuddin Chishti, Baba Ganj Shakar, Data Ganj Bakhsh, Amir Khusrau, and of course we mostly sing Baba Bulle Shah's poetry" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 111, folio not stated).

Of the path of blame, Rizvi writes, "Hujwiri sums it up like this: 'In true love there is nothing sweeter than blame, because blame of the Beloved makes no impression on the lover's heart: he heeds not what strangers say, for his heart is ever faithful to the object of his love.'" and the summary ends with a line of verse, in the Nicholson English through which Rizvi reads him:

*'Tis sweet to be reviled for passion's sake.* (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 59, folio 43)

## His Teaching

Sharib draws five headed sections and ten sayings from his writings: on self-interest, which lifts the blessing from any work; on the company of the Sufis, with a fourfold analysis of the motives of those who seek it, ending on those who hide their own afflictions in the Sufis' correction and wear their garb; on the struggle against the self, which the Prophet held greater than jihad; on the *faqir*; on ten things that devour ten; and on eight sayings chosen from Luqman Hakim (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 24–27, folios 20–23).

On rulers his counsel is severe: a faqir should reckon acquaintance with kings and rulers as acquaintance with venomous snakes and great dragons, because once a faqir gains a king's favour let him know that his provision and viaticum are ruined; and putting a Turkish cap on your head gains you no *faqiri* (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 25, folio 21, note 16). On wealth: reckon wealth a torment and give it to the starving as *sadaqa*, for in the end worms will eat it in the grave, whereas if you give it away they will remain your friends (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 26–27, folios 22–23). For a *wird* he prescribes: take the meanings of *Ya Habib* and *Ya Latif*, among the Most Beautiful Names of God, into your being and make them your *wird* (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 27, folio 23, note 19).

Rizvi quotes the *Kashf al-Mahjub*, in Nicholson's English, on the four Righteous Caliphs as Sufi exemplars: Abu Bakr "a paragon of voluntary poverty to the sufis" who "taught them to renounce all their material goods for Islam"; Umar, whose patched gown was "a symbol of his self-denial", teaching that "spiritualists outwardly were a part of mankind, but inwardly their hearts clung to God"; Usman's life "the best of all possible examples in resignation during a crisis"; and Ali "their Shaikh (leader or teacher) in both the theory and practice of sufism" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 41, folio 25). From the same book come the saying of Uways al-Qarani that "the heart of the solitary one was free from thoughts of others" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 41, folio 25); the prayer of al-Baqir, "O my God and my Lord, night has come, and the power of monarchs has ceased" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 42–43, folios 26–27); a conversation between a Bedouin and Hasan on patience, ending "This is the mark of true sincerity" and "Association with the wicked produces suspicion of the good" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 44, folio 28); and the report that "According to Hujwiri, Shaqiq wrote a number of books on Sufism but none are in existence" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 51, folio 35).

Rizvi quotes his glosses on his predecessors. Of a saying of Abu'l Fawaris he comments: "The eminent have eminence until they see it, and the saints have saintship until they see it," that is, "whoever regards his eminence loses its reality, and whoever regards his saintship loses its reality" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 61, folio 45). Of annihilation and subsistence: "In annihilation there is no love or hate, and in subsistence there is no consciousness of union or separation. Some wrongly imagine that annihilation signifies loss of essence and destruction of personality, and that subsistence indicates the subsistence of God in Man; both these notions are absurd." (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 68, folio 52). Of the spirit: "The spirit is created and is under God's command. Anyone who holds another belief is in flagrant error and cannot distinguish what is non-eternal from what is eternal. No saint, if his saintship be sound, can possibly be ignorant of the attributes of God." (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 77, folio 61). He judged which schools were sound: "According to Hujwiri the sufi sects named after the above Masters were approved ones. He gives a detailed description of each in the Kashf al-Mahjub" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 77, folio 61).

On the life of the khanqah Rizvi makes him Suhrawardi's model. "Like Hujwiri, Suhrawardi divided the people of khanqahs into two: residents and travellers", and Suhrawardi's section on ethics "was a marked improvement over related chapters in Hujwiri's Kashf al-Mahjub" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 109, folio 91). "According to Hujwiri, sufi Shaikhs prescribed a probationary period to novices of three years before instructing them in the real secrets of the Path", the first year "devoted to serving the people; the second, to the service of God and the third to guarding the heart" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 117, folio 99). Of the patched cloak he held that its wearing "was practised by Muhammad and his companions" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 120, folio 102), and he read it as an allegory, which Schimmel quotes thus: "Its collar is annihilation of intercourse [with men], its two sleeves are observance and continence, its two gussets are poverty and purity, its belt is persistence in contemplation, its hem is tranquillity in [God's] presence, and its fringe is settlement in the abode of union." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 134, folio 103; Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 120, folio 102). The forty-day retreat, the *chilla* that later saints would keep at his own tomb, is "derived, as Hujwiri says, from the forty-day fast of Moses, when he hoped for a vision from God" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 134, folio 103).

Schimmel quotes him at length. On the name of the Sufi: "Some assert that the Sufi is so called because he wears a woollen garment (jama-i suf), others that he is so called because he is in the first rank (saff-i awwal), others say it is because the Sufis claim to belong to the ashab-i Suffa (the people of the Bench who gathered around the Prophet's mosque)", while others derive it from the word for purity (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 45, folio 14). On love: "Love is concord, and the lover has but one duty in the world, namely to keep the commandment of the beloved, and if the object of desire is one, how can discord arise?" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, pp. 46–47, folios 15–16). On learning: "Knowledge is immense and life is short: therefore it is not obligatory to learn all the science . . . but only so much as bears upon the religious law" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 48, folio 17). On two approaches to God: "There is a difference between one who is burned by His Majesty in the fire of love and one who is illuminated by His Beauty in the light of contemplation." and "There is a difference between one who meditates upon the Divine acts and one who is amazed at the Divine Majesty; the one is a follower of friendship, the other is a companion of love." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 37, folio 6). On the people of blame, more critically than Sulami: "The ostentatious men purposely act in such a way as to win popularity, while the malamatl purposely acts in such a way that people reject him. Both have their thought fixed on mankind and do not pass beyond that sphere" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 118, folio 87).

On repentance: "The penitent is a lover of God, and the lover of God is in contemplation of God: in contemplation it is wrong to remember sin, for recollection of sin is a veil between God and the contemplator"; and of his threefold division of repentance Schimmel writes, "This tripartition is not found elsewhere." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 141, folio 110). On poverty, which she endorses in her own voice: "Its form is destitution and indigence, but its essence is fortune and free choice" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 153, folio 122); and on the dervish, "Dervishhood in all its meanings is a metaphorical poverty", and "Then he is the Way, not the wayfarer, i.e., the dervish is a place over which something is passing, not a wayfarer following his own will." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 154, folio 123). Of contentment she judges him "probably right in his statement that" it "is a result of love, inasmuch as the lover is content with what is done by the beloved" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 157, folio 126); "True sobriety is defined by Hujwiri as" 'reaching the goal' (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 160, folio 129).

She quotes his classification of lovers, which Ghazzali adopted: "Those who regard the favor and beneficence of God toward them and are led by that regard to love the Benefactor, and those who are so enraptured by love that they reckon all favors as a veil and by regarding the Benefactor are led to [consciousness of] His favors" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 163, folio 132); his threefold unification, "God's unification of God, i.e. His knowledge of His Unity; God's unification of His creatures, i.e. His decree that a man shall pronounce Him to be one, and the creation of unification in his heart; man's unification of God, i.e. their knowledge of the Unity of God" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 177, folio 146); his definition of mystics by their attitude to the ritual prayer, "some hold that it is a means of obtaining the presence of God; others regard it as a means of obtaining absence" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 183, folio 152); his correction of a misattributed saying, "the more correct version" of the words of Muhammad ibn Wasi', "I never saw anything without seeing God therein" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 178, folio 147); and the sentence that opens her whole discussion of sainthood, "You must know that the principle and foundation of Sufism and knowledge of God rest on saintship" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 230, folio 199).

On contemplation, Schimmel's *As Through a Veil* notes that the *Kashf* "discusses the two approaches to the world: some mystics state, 'I never saw anything without seeing God therein,' which points to an advanced stage of contemplation because he sees the agent only, while the statement of others, 'I never see anything except God,' shows that he, fully enraptured, sees only the agent" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 248, folio 230); and she quotes its law of love, "It is a law that when the lover advances, the beloved retires. If the lover is satisfied with love alone, then the beloved draws near. In truth, the lover has honor only while he has no desire of union." — a theme that "forms the topic of most Persian works on love" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 251, folio 233). To "looking toward the young men" he devoted, Schimmel writes, "a weighty paragraph": "Looking at youth and associating with them are forbidden practices and anyone who declares this to be allowable is an unbeliever. The traditions brought forward in this matter are vain and foolish." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 321, folio 290; Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 250, folio 232). Schimmel also notes "Hujwiri's biting remarks about those who make their patched frocks almost an object of worship" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 203, folio 203), and Ewing cites him for the stance of blame, "drawing blame onto oneself as a way of removing self-conceit on the path of spiritual development" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 183, folio 172).

He was also a transmitter. A prayer of Sari al-Saqati, Schimmel notes, "has been preserved by Hujwiri" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 162, folio 131); she cites him for a saying of Sumnun al-Muhibb (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 236, folio 218), and, without comment, for the imagery of wine and the tavern (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 254, folio 236); and he records a saying of an unnamed master of his own to a disciple: "To traverse distance is child's play: henceforth pay visits by means of thought; it is not worth while to visit any person, and there is no virtue in bodily presence" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 237, folio 206). Rizvi quotes from his book the saying of Ahmad ibn Khazruya that "the way to God is like the blazing sun; do thou seek thyself, for when thou hast found thyself thou art come to thy journey's end, inasmuch as God is too manifest to admit of His being sought" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 53, folio 37).

## Hujwiri and Samaʿ

His shrine is known for its music, and the books record what he himself thought of *samaʿ*. Ernst and Lawrence count him with Gisu Daraz, 'Ali Hamadani and Muhammad Nur Allah among the four Indian writers whom Mole cites as important theorists of *samaʿ* (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 49, folio 35). He "ably summarizes the several conflicting views toward" it "that were voiced by contemporary and earlier Sufis, but he severely criticizes sensual gratification through empathetic ecstasy"; when a contemporary traditionist told him he had written a book on its permissibility, "the Lahori shaykh replies: 'It is a great calamity to religion that the Imam should have made lawful an amusement which is the root of all immorality.'" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 52, folio 38). Yet "he goes on to admit that he himself practices" it, and "To the extent that Hujwiri affirms empathetic ecstasy, he is constrained by the well-attested hadith of the Prophet" that bids the reciter of the Qur'an weep, "and if you cannot weep, then you should try to weep" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 52, folio 38).

He records a rebuke against himself, from a "venerable shaykh who indulged Hujwiri's desire for" music and then told him that "A time will come when this music will be no more to you than the croaking of a raven" and to "Take care not to accustom yourself to this, lest it grow (to be) part of your nature and keep you back from higher things." Perhaps with that reprimand in mind, Ernst and Lawrence suggest, he closes his book "with a petitionary prayer requesting divine forgiveness for his sins of the past" in *samaʿ* (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 52, folio 38). He was himself "the disciple of a shaykh disinclined to" it (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 53, folio 39), and "Hujwiri, for instance, is often seen to be Indian because he died and was buried in Lahore. Yet he lived prior to the Sultanate period, and his reflections on" it "are closer to the non-Indian than to the Indian, especially the Chishti, attitude" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 52, folio 38). Their verdict on his importance is this: "Almost despite himself, Hujwiri gives some of the claims made on behalf of" *samaʿ*, "and for the first time links the topic of" it "to the discussion of the grades of ecstasy"; and that linkage "made possible the later and distinctive Chishti contribution to" its theory (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 53, folio 39). They call his posture "equivocal" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 205, folio 194), and make him the model of method for Mas'ud Bakk, who "Like Hujwiri, … proceeds from a general description of hearing and its role in Islamic revelation" to the practice itself (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 55, folio 41).

Rizvi ranks him above Suhrawardi: "the Shaikh was unable to excel Hujwiri's unique means of defending sama'", he writes of Suhrawardi (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 110–111, folios 92–93). For Qureshi, writing on qawwali, he supplies the very definition of *samaʿ*, which "comprises first and foremost that which is heard, the 'divine message which stirs the heart to seek God'" (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 102, folio 82); he is her authority for "placing the entire practice of sama' firmly within the hierarchical structure of spiritual authority" (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 103, folio 83); and he is among the writers whose "principles laid down between the eleventh and fourteenth centuries" still "contribute to an amazing extent to the concepts in use today" (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 126, folio 106). Sharib, for his part, quotes his warning to beginners, the word itself uncertainly printed: a beginner should not go near *samaʿ* and should keep away from it (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 27, folio 23).

## The Title "Data Ganj Bakhsh" and the Chishti Tribute

The name by which Hujwiri is universally known — *Data Ganj Bakhsh* — was, by cherished tradition, bestowed upon him from beyond the grave by another of the giants of South Asian Sufism. It is told that Khwaja Moinuddin Chishti of Ajmer, the founder of the Chishti order in India, on his way into the subcontinent kept a forty-day retreat (*chilla*) at the foot of Hujwiri’s tomb in Lahore; and that on departing he uttered a Persian couplet in the saint’s praise that has become inseparable from his memory:

گنج بخشِ فیضِ عالم، مظہرِ نورِ خدا
ناقصاں را پیرِ کامل، کاملاں را رہنما

[“Bestower of the treasure, the bounty of the world, the manifestation of the light of God; for the imperfect, a perfect guide — and for the perfect, a guide.”]

From this verse comes the title *Ganj Bakhsh* (“Bestower of Treasure”), and the affectionate *Data* (“the Giver”) by which the Punjab has called him ever since; and because the Chishti master — himself the fountainhead of an entire order — bowed at his threshold, Hujwiri is remembered as the “teacher of teachers,” the saint from whom the later Sufis of India drew their light. Around his coming to Lahore, too, cluster the folk-miracles of a patron saint: the best-loved tells of a poor milk-seller whose meagre yield, at the saint’s blessing, miraculously increased — a small wonder that, in the telling, drew the people of the city to his teaching, and that still speaks of the compassion and generosity his shrine is felt to embody.

Sharib tells the story in the same shape. When Khwaja Moinuddin Chishti — Hazrat Khwaja Moinuddin Hasan Chishti Sanjari in his spelling — came to Lahore, he kept *itikaf* at Hujwiri's blessed mazar, and on leaving recited the couplet given above, which Sharib prints in the same words, without its vowel marks and commas; and from that day he became famous as Data Ganj Bakhsh (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 21–22, folios 17–18, note 3). He is, Sharib writes, called by the *laqab* Data Ganj Bakhsh (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 21, folio 17), and some call him simply Ganj Bakhsh (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 22, folio 18). Rizvi records only that the title was posthumous: "Later Muslims posthumously conferred on Shaikh Hujwiri the title, Data Ganj Bakhsh, ‘Distributor of (Unlimited) Treasure’" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 130, folio 112). Rozehnal glosses it otherwise: "As the saint's moniker, Data Ganj Bakhsh ('The Lavish Giver'), suggests al-Hujwiri's tomb is renowned as a place of intense power—an open and accessible outlet for spiritual grace." (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 207, folio 199). Chishti notes that the name Ganj Bakhsh became famous, and that Nawab Mir Momin Khan, who had perfect devotion to the saint, held his resting place greater than the highest paradise (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 164, 173).

The name takes many forms even within one book. Lahori's entry on him is headed with the name Shaikh Ali Makhdoom Jalali Ghaznawi Hujwiri, styled Data Ganj Bakhsh (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 194–195, folios 192–193), and his table of contents lists it as Shaikh Ali Makhdoom, known as Data Ganj Bakhsh (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 18, folio 6); his appendix has Jullabi where the heading has Jalali (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 289, folio 277); his index lists him under Ganj Bakhsh Dana, Ali bin Usman Hujwiri (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 326, folio 314), with a cross-reference to Ganj Bakhsh Lahori (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 324, folio 312). The same book gives the epithet Ganj Bakhsh to another man altogether, Haji Muhammad Naushah Ganj Bakhsh (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 240, 242). Chishti speaks of the radiant mazar of Hazrat Shah Mir Ali Hujwiri (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 170). Rizvi gives him in full as "Abu'l-Hasan ʿAli bin ʿUsman bin ʿAli al-Ghaznawi al-Jullabi al-Hujwiri" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 80, folio 64), and Abbas as "Pir Ali ibn Usman Data Ganj Bakhsh Hujwiri" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 161, folio not stated). In the indexes of Rizvi and of Qureshi the title "Data Ganj Bakhsh" stands only as a cross-reference to Hujwiri (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 464, folio 440; Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 283, folio 263).

The books agree that the founder of the Chishtiyya in India kept his retreat at this tomb, and differ on its details. Rizvi: "His tomb has always been greatly venerated by sufis and Muslims alike. Among early mystics who undertook hard ascetic exercises in Lahore at the Shaikh's tomb was Khwaja" Moinuddin Chishti, "the founder of the leading Indian order, the Chishtiyya" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 130, folio 112); elsewhere he writes that the Khwaja "reached Lahore and stayed near the tombs of Shaikh Husain Zanjani and Shaikh ‘Ali Hujwiri", and rules that "Fawaidu'l Fu‘ad's statement appears to be correct and it is likely that the Khwaja stayed near the two tombs" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 139, folio 121; p. 139, folio 121, footnote 3). Lahori writes that the Khwaja came from Ajmer to Lahore to keep a *chilla* at the holy tomb, lodging with Sayyid Yaqub Zanjani at its beginning and end (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 200–201, folios 188–189); the editor dates this arrival to about 588 AH in one note and about 585 AH in another three pages earlier (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 200, note 2; p. 198, note 1). Chishti has him meet the Zanjani elders at the mazar of Sayyid Ali Ganj Bakhsh, a word after the name illegible, and then leave for Ajmer, the abode of good (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 270). Kanhaiya Lal names him first among the saints who came here, kept *chillas* and received grace (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 289, folio 292).

What he received there is told as a grant. Ernst and Lawrence: "One must also consider the 'station of Mu'in ad-Din' outside the shrine of Hujwiri in Lahore, where the first Indian Chishti master was granted the dominion of all of India, for himself and his successors" — a site of the class of "sites of heroic asceticism, particularly the location of the 40-day retreat" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 109, folio 96). Rozehnal: "According to tradition Mu'in ad-Din Chishti Ajmeri (d. 1236), the first Indian Chishti master, was granted the dominion (wilayat) of all of South Asia, for himself and his successors, at al-Hujwiri's tomb." (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 207, folio 199). Chishti describes, beside the grave of a mujawir, a platform, the station of Hazrat Khwaja Moinuddin bin Hasan Sanjari Chishti, perhaps of his *chilla*, the word uncertain in the printed text, under a brick dome (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 167).

## The Making of the Shrine

From the saint’s grave grew, over a thousand years, the great complex of today. What began as a modest burial place beside his mosque became a site of pilgrimage almost at once; the first formal enclosure is said to have been raised by the Ghaznavid rulers, and through the centuries that followed the shrine was repaired, adorned, and enlarged by the powers of the age. Under the Delhi Sultanate and the Mughals its fame only grew — emperors such as Akbar and Shah Jahan honoured the saint and contributed to his darbar — and even in the Sikh period, under Maharaja Ranjit Singh, the shrine was maintained. By the nineteenth century it was already among the most revered landmarks of Lahore. After the creation of Pakistan in 1947 it passed into the care of the Punjab Auqaf Department, and in the 1980s it was transformed by a great expansion — wide marble courtyards, prayer halls, and facilities for the swelling multitudes of pilgrims — with renovation and enlargement continuing into the present century. The Data Darbar of today, with its gleaming courts and its towering mosque, is thus the work of many hands across nine hundred years, all gathered around a single grave.

The grave lies, Lahori writes, in the courtyard of his own khanqah (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 195). Kanhaiya Lal records royal Qur'ans kept at the mazar: the kings of old, he writes, also showed this mazar the utmost reverence, and Qur'ans written by the hands of Sultan Ibrahim Ghaznavi, Sultan Shams-ud-Din Altamsh and other kings are present at the mazar (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 288–289, folios 291–292). In the age of Akbar, Chishti records, the pakka grave of Shaikh Sulaiman, a mujawir, was built, though the phrase that dates it is partly illegible and uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 167). Of the Mughal princess Zeb-un-Nisa Lahori can report only what was said: it is commonly told that Zeb-un-Nisa, the princess, was building in the vicinity, a building whose wall, the report goes, kept collapsing (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 270, folio 258).

The Sikh period left its mark in two ways. Kanhaiya Lal writes that the Maharaja, though he greatly respected the mazar and sent thousands of rupees for its interior [OCR?], left not one of the outer buildings standing, having their stones, doors and foundations [OCR?] taken out of the ground, so that only the mazar's own building remained (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 289, folio 292). Nearby, the tomb and mosque of Shah Ashraf, which stood in its vicinity, all fell in the oppression [OCR?] of the Sikhs (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 289, folio 292). Chishti records a different side of the same rule: the first of the great Qur'ans at the shrine came from Ranjit Singh at the conquest of Peshawar (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 169); a hujra or haveli of a Mian whose printed name, perhaps Abuman Jan, cannot be read with certainty was tied to him (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 170); and the mosque and *takia* to the north of the grave were tied to the Sarkar of Maharaja Ranjit Singh (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 171).

The dome is the work of private patrons. Lahori writes that there was formerly no dome over the grave; Haji Nur Muhammad Sadhu built it twelve years before the author wrote, and Mian Muhammad Jan, *ra'is* of Amritsar, paid for its repair and whitewashing; may God reward them both, he adds (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 195). Kanhaiya Lal writes that formerly there was no dome; in 1247 [OCR?] AH one Nur Muhammad Samawa [OCR?] had it built, round and very beautiful, with mirrors fitted below and above the lattice (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 290, folio 293). Chishti describes it as gilded, and records chronograms on the building by Nur Muhammad Chishti, one of them ending with words on the tomb of our bountiful Makhdoom and a year that begins 12, the rest illegible, and by Mufti Ghulam Sarwar on the building of this mosque (the word mosque uncertainly printed), naming the saint as Sayyid al-Din Ganj Bakhsh, the Ghaznawi (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 171). The domed building, he writes, was rebuilt anew on its former foundation, with hujras, a decorated wall, a *deorhi* and the arched doorway of the mosque, and a chronogram ending with the year 1268, uncertainly printed, the words before it illegible (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 167).

The accounts of the saint's mosque do not agree. Lahori's author knew it still standing (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 194). Chishti writes that in 1269 AH, a year uncertainly printed, Gulzar Shah rebuilt it anew on the same foundation (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 163). Kanhaiya Lal, placing it at the enclosure's south-west corner, writes that Hazrat built it himself, and it has now been rebuilt, in 1868 [OCR?] AD (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 290, folio 293). The editor of Lahori's 1974 edition writes in a note that, to enlarge the mosque, the old one has been demolished and a new one built; a stone marks the line of the old mihrab, and the Auqaf Department is planning a further extension; he gives no date for either the demolition or the plan (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 194, folio 192, note 4).

The modern complex, as Abbas describes it from her fieldwork, belongs to a wider movement: "In recent years, with the coming into power of orthodox Islamic governments in Pakistan, there has been a trend to extend and renovate the shrines of Sufis such as Hazrat Data Ganj Bakhsh Hujwiri in Lahore into mosques and places of worship"; "Data Darbar has been extended to almost ten times its original size and has been remodeled with the most expensive Italian marble. Huge congregations of devotees perform the Friday prayers in the shrine courtyard" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 20, folio not stated; pp. 20–21, folios not stated). She names no government and no year.

## The Shrine as the Older Histories Describe It

Kanhaiya Lal walks the reader through the enclosure. Its door faces south; inside, to the west, is a gate with a stone threshold and a *deorhi* behind it; through it, from the eastern gate, lies a square courtyard with houses and dalans on all four sides, and the mujawirs live on the southern side. In the middle of the courtyard an octagonal [OCR?] platform carries the mazar: the platform is of marble, the floor above it is marble, and the grave itself is made of a single piece of marble — with a wooden lattice on all eight sides (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 289–290, folios 292–293). The dome over it keeps the southern door (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 290, folio 293). Outside the enclosure to the south are many pakka mazars, a mosque, and many sitting-places of the mujawirs (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 291, folio 294).

Chishti adds other details. Of the radiant mazar of Hazrat Shah Mir Ali Hujwiri he notes the marble and the carved stonework, both words uncertainly printed, the marble *taʿwiz*, and a date, 1265 AH, also uncertain, given with a haveli (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 170). He records a pigeon house with many pigeons, and twelve graves outside to the east and the south (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 171). The Qur'an dalan had been widened, and the siparas were kept in a chest, read by whoever comes (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 167). Rizvi notes a tablet on the tomb that gives the year of the saint's death as "465/1072-73" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 130, folio 112). Around the khanqah, Chishti adds, on what had been a graveyard, a man whose name is damaged in the printed text had many tombs demolished, though many graves remain (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 165).

## Qur'ans, Inscriptions and Endowments

The shrine was a house of Qur'ans. Kanhaiya Lal's royal copies are kept on *rahls*, book-rests, in the rooms of the courtyard, and people go on reading them (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 290, folio 293). Chishti lists the large Qur'ans, each a *gaz* long: the first from Ranjit Singh at Peshawar's conquest; the second from Shaikh Ghulam al-Din, holder of an office in Kashmir whose printed title cannot be read with certainty, father of Nawab Shaikh Imam al-Din; one from a Kashmiri merchant living in Lahore; a fourth from Mian Ghulam al-Husain, a Lahore calligrapher; one written in musk, whose history is not known; a sixth, in Multani hand, from the Nawab of Multan; and a seventh, an old one (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 169). He records another attributed to a Muhammad Khan of Gujranwala and Ahmadgarh, the entry partly illegible and uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 167). Haroon Khalid writes that Jind Kaur "ordered the construction of a library at the shrine of the eleventh century Sufi, Ali Hujwiri and gifted all the Qurans she had in her possession to it" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 207, folio not stated).

A *waqf* inscription records that a copy in the hand of Muhammad Hadi, known as Mumin al-Mulk Ala al-Daula Jafar Khan, Bahadur, Nasir Jang, was made an endowment for the mazar of Mir Ali Hujwiri at Lahore, the exemplar of the wayfarers; it is dated 1236 AH, the year given in figures and in words, and opens by invoking the grace of God before naming the donor; several of his titles, the saint's name and the year are uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 167). It runs on over two more pages, listing Qur'ans and a salaried reciter sent to other mazars (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 168–169).

The inscriptions Chishti records include the *kalima*, a Persian verse naming Makhdoom Ali, and the *basmala* (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 166). Over the inner gate Kanhaiya Lal records a gilded inscription, three Persian couplets that open with the khanqah of Ali Hujwiri, every line of them uncertain in the printed text, with the number 376, itself uncertain, beneath — a number that does not agree with the 431 and 465 printed on the same page (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 289, folio 292).

## Graves in the Precinct

The precinct holds other graves, old and new. Chishti notes two graves beside the saint's, apparently, in a phrase uncertain in the printed text, those of the saint's brothers, and, in a damaged passage, a line naming Zahir al-Daula, a brother of Sultan Mahmud Ghaznavi, with a word before his title that cannot be read with certainty (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 170).

Beside the saint's enclosure stands that of Nawab Shaikh Imam-ud-Din. Kanhaiya Lal reaches it by a path from the northern dalan; it stands wall to wall with the saint's enclosure on the east and south, and is very elaborately built. The Nawab's grave, its title uncertainly printed, is of marble, and Persian couplets on the tomb's southern wall, a lament that the world will go on long after us, begin with this line:

دریغا کہ بے ما بسے روزگار

The tomb was built after his death by his son, Nawab Ghulam Mahbub Subhani, both of whose last names are uncertainly printed, and a man named Ahmad al-Din Bakhsh, his name uncertain and the word after it illegible, made the grave; and chronogram verses stand on its stone (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 290, folio 293; p. 291, folio 294).

چونکہ نواب شیخ امام الدین
شد ز دنیا و رو بخلد نہاد…

*The opening of the chronogram verses on the stone of Nawab Shaikh Imam-ud-Din's grave.* (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 290, folio 293; p. 291, folio 294)

Chishti describes the same tomb: square, unroofed over the grave, with dalans, hujras, a *balakhana*, an iron grille and a wooden cage; a quatrain in the dotted hand of Mirza Imam Wardi (the word for dotted uncertainly printed), signed by him as a humble mote and dated 1275, the figure uncertain; and a chronogram on the grave of Imam al-Din Khan, with an epithet that cannot be read with certainty (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 169). On a brick platform beside it he records a marble grave naming Nawab Shaikh Imam al-Din, with the date 2 Shaban 1225 AH, given in figures and in words, both uncertainly printed, though what event the date marks is unclear in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 170).

A modern burial belongs to the story of the saint's book. Two English brothers named Lennard, Ernst and Lawrence record, "had converted to Islam in 1936 after reading the Sufi classic *The Unveiling of the Concealed*, by 'Ali Hujwiri (d. 1072)", then "left their well-to-do English family and taken the Muslim names Faruq Ahmad and Shahid Allah", and "traveled to the Middle East and India in search of a Sufi master"; "Faruq Ahmad died in February 1945, and was buried within the tomb precincts of his patron saint Hujwiri (Data Ganj Bakhsh) in Lahore" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 138, folio 125). Rozehnal, following the *Tarbiyat al-'ushshaq*, gives the date — he "died in Lahore on February 13, 1945 after a prolonged bout of pneumonia" — and the price of such a grave: "there were insufficient funds to fulfill Faruq Ahmad's desire to be buried in the compound of Hujwiri's shrine, and an alternative location was hastily arranged", and that, as the burial was about to commence, "a stranger suddenly arrived" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 75, folio 65). Rozehnal writes elsewhere, spelling the name differently, that "to this day, Faruq Ahmed lies buried in a small, subterranean room within his patron saint's tomb complex" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 207, folio 199).

## Custody and Administration

Unlike most of the shrines of the Punjab, Data Darbar has no hereditary keepers (*sajjada-nashins*). Since shortly after the creation of Pakistan it has been administered directly by the state, through the Punjab Auqaf and Religious Affairs Department, which manages the offerings (*nazrana*), the mosque, the security, and the vast operation of the langar. On the ground the daily life of the shrine depends on a small army of *khadims* (attendants), imams, qawwals, cooks and langar workers, cleaners, and security staff, overseen by government administrators — in 2026 the Auqaf secretary Dr Ehsan Bhutta, director-general Shahid Kalyani, and shrine administrator Muhammad Ali Khan — and the complex is the subject of continual expansion and renovation projects, with further works in progress as of 2026. This state custodianship — exceptional among the great darbars — reflects both the enormous scale of Data Darbar and its standing as a national, and not merely a family, trust.

The older histories speak of mujawirs. Kanhaiya Lal counts more than seventy of them, who divide the income from the offerings in equal shares (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 291, folio 294). Chishti records their property beyond the enclosure: the house at the grave of Pir Ghaib, also in the possession of the mujawirs of Hazrat Data Ganj Bakhsh, whose income they take (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 281); and, at Bakhshi Kanhiya Lal's garden, south of the tomb of Hazrat Pir Ali Ganj Bakhsh Hujwiri in its eastern corner, a pomegranate plot and a bed of ground in the possession of the mujawirs of Hazrat Data Ganj Bakhsh, land that was the mujawirs' own property, held without rent under a written statement, and a *kotha* built by the mujawirs of Data Ganj Bakhsh Sahib and let to a shopkeeper whose trade is uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 345). He also tells how, on the day of an urs he himself was given a yellow *basanti* turban before thousands through Hasan Ali Shah, *sajjada nashin*, whose name is uncertainly printed, with flowers and cowries from the present treasury of the Hazrat's mazar; the book does not make clear which shrine's *sajjada nashin* is meant (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 78). None of the books consulted here describes the shrine's langar. Lahori's editor, writing for the edition of 1974, notes the Auqaf Department's plan to enlarge the mosque (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 194, folio 192, note 4).

The shrine's economy has a study of its own. Kasmani's bibliography lists Linus Strothmann's doctoral thesis, "Managing Piety at the Shrine of Data Ganj Bukhsh, Lahore, Pakistan" (Freie Universität Berlin, 2012), and his book *Managing Piety: The Shrine of Data Ganj Bakhsh* (Karachi: Oxford University Press, 2016); her text cites the book for "the distribution of shrine revenues and the economic dimension of a Sufi shrine in Lahore" without naming the shrine there (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 216, folio 199; p. 189, folio 172, ch. 1 n. 26).

## Devotion at the Darbar

Data Darbar is a place of ceaseless devotion, and the rhythms of worship there run day and night. The five daily prayers and the Friday congregation fill the grand mosque; devotees recite the Qur’an, offer personal and collective supplication (*dua*), lay cloths (*chadar*) and flowers upon the tomb, and sit in silent contemplation. Countless pilgrims come to fulfil vows or to seek blessing for health, livelihood, marriage, or success in examinations, in the belief that the saint is a mediator of God’s grace. At the heart of the shrine’s life is the *langar* — the free kitchen — which feeds thousands every day, embodying the Sufi ethic that the love of God must be shown in generosity and service to His creatures; for the poor of Lahore, Data Darbar is not only a sanctuary of the spirit but a place of bread and belonging. On Thursday evenings the shrine fills for *qawwali* and *sama*, the devotional music of the Sufis, and some among the devotees are moved to *dhamal*, the ecstatic rhythmic dance of trance and love. It is an inclusive space, open to men and women, rich and poor, scholar and labourer alike — one of the few places in the city where all classes meet on level ground. The devotional culture of the darbar is continuous with the Barelvi-inflected piety of the Punjab, in which the veneration of the saints and the seeking of their intercession stand at the centre of religious life. On an ordinary day the shrine draws some thirty to fifty thousand visitors, and on Thursdays and holy nights well over a hundred thousand.

Sharib's tazkira calls the radiant mazar in Lahore a fountainhead of graces and blessings (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 23, folio 19). Chishti speaks of wishes fulfilled there (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 164). Schimmel's index of subjects sets pilgrimage "to Data Ganj Bakhsh" beside pilgrimage to Mecca (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 538, folio 507). Zindapir, in a speech Pnina Werbner records, made the shrine his proof that saints outrank kings: "The tombs of kings do not get izzat [honour]; only those of awliya… Like Data Ganj Baksh in Lahore [the shrine of the eleventh-century Sufi saint Hujweri]. If you go to the Emperor's tomb [Shah Jahangir's] you will find no people there. But at Data Ganj Baksh there are thousands of people all the time" — the words in square brackets are Werbner's, not his (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 120, folio 109).

The books record what individual devotees do there. In Haroon Khalid's reconstruction in *Walking with Nanak*, when someone suggested that Tripta "should also offer a chadar (sheet) at the shrine of Ali Hajveri in Lahore, she did so" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 21, folio 3). A woman named Rabi'a, whose daughter was under another pir's treatment, told Ewing: "When she began to get treatment from the Kharian pir, I went to Data Sahib [Lahore's most important Sufi shrine]"; there, "I told my dream to a faqir [a beggar-ascetic] there", and he read it: "[In the dream] I saw that someone closed a water pipe by filling it with dust. Baba [the faqir] said, ‘Your daughter's birth canal is closed… You should bring your daughter before me once'" — the bracketed glosses being Ewing's insertions into the transcript (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 178, folio 167). Omar Kasmani tells of Baba: "Then, once the fair was over, Baba decided to board a bus, arriving at Data Darbar, Lahore's most prominent shrine complex. It was here that, according to Baba, they first found mentorship under the tutelage of a fakir." Baba stayed about a year: "Another year would pass and even though the dreams from the village weren't really forgotten, the visions had stopped." (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 65, folio 48). Khalid names it among the famous Muslim shrines at which Ghulam Ali's *dandiya* group has played (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 63, folio not stated).

The Chishti Sabiri order of Rozehnal's study makes the shrine its one exception. Its rule is "Do not go to any mazar which is not in our silsila" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 207, folio 199), and "A notable exception is made, however, for the shrine of Sayyid 'Ali ibn 'Uthman al-Hujwiri (d. 1074) in the old city of Lahore" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 206, folio 198). Their shaykh Wahid Bakhsh wrote in a letter: "Although Hazrat Data Sahab, may God have mercy upon him, is not in the line of our silsila directly, he is great enough to know what you need. Moreover, he is very much associated with the masha'ikh of our silsila. He is very kind to us." (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 207, folio 199). The saint is one of five figures on whom the disciples meditate daily in *muraqaba-i shaykh*, "for fifteen to thirty minutes on a daily basis" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 206, folio 198). At the tomb they mark their respect: "When we go to shaykhs other than our silsila, we recite Fatiha twice… But if you go to Data Sahab, you recite it only once because you are in his silsila. This is done out of respect." (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 208, folio 200). A woman disciple described her visit: "You should say, in your heart, 'I am sitting in front of Data Sahab. Data Sahab is sitting there, and I am sitting here.' Then you just close your eyes, and take long, slow, deep breaths… When I go to Data Sahab I sit there for ten to fifteen minutes of meditation and receive an answer to all my problems." (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 208, folio 200). Contemplation there is "an intimate conversation between the Sufi disciple and the patron saint, alive in his tomb" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 207, folio 199). Yet "Murids do not go to the mazar [of al-Hujwiri] collectively. They go privately or as a family, but not as a group." (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 208, folio 200).

## Thursdays and Fridays

The week has long had its fixed nights. Lahori's author, writing in his own present, describes a weekly vigil and a weekly *milad* on two consecutive days, kept by devotees of two cities: even now, he writes, every Thursday the devotees of Lahore and Amritsar gather at the mazar and stay awake all night, from evening to morning, in *durud* and *naat*; then on every Friday, by day, there is a *milad*, and high and low alike are blessed in greeting the saint (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 195). As the authority for the Thursday round he cites Dara Shikoh, who said that whoever lights a lamp at his mazar for forty Thursdays in succession will obtain from God whatever he asks (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 195); the footnote keyed to the quotation gives Dara Shikoh's name and nothing more (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 196). His editor brings the congregation up to his own day: since the Partition of India in 1947, devotees have come from Lahore and every part of Pakistan, and from Afghanistan, and pilgrims from other countries too — a national and international congregation in place of the Amritsar half of the author's (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 196, note 3). Against the author's figure of 827 years elapsed, the editor writes that some nine and a quarter centuries have now passed, about 925 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 196, note 4).

Chishti, too, writes of the Thursday crowd, and of a special gathering every month (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 164), and of the forty days or forty Thursdays: whoever can attend his blessed mazar for forty days or forty Thursdays will obtain from God whatever he wishes, followed by a testimony in the first person whose writer the book leaves unclear (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 164–165). Kanhaiya Lal records that every Thursday night many people keep vigil and remain all night in worship — and a *mela* on the eighth [OCR?] day, on Friday (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 291, folio 294). The family of Bakhshi Kanhiya Lal, Chishti notes, sat in their garden by the shrine on Fridays and at the urs (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 345).

In the Chishti Sabiri order of Rozehnal's study, "Disciples who live in Lahore regularly visit al-Hujwiri's shrine on Thursday evenings before attending the communal halqa-i zikr, and they often repeat the journey on Sundays as well." — and "it was Shaykh Wahid Bakhsh who established this weekly pilgrimage, instigating a new ritual practice that Chishti Sabiris in Lahore continue today" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 207, folio 199; p. 208, folio 200). On Fridays, Abbas writes, "Huge congregations of devotees perform the Friday prayers in the shrine courtyard" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 20–21, folios not stated).

## Retreats at the Tomb

From early on the grave was a place to keep the forty-day retreat. Lahori writes that Shah Husain — the saint of Madho Lal Hussain's shrine at Baghbanpura — kept several *chillas* at the "pearl-scattering" mazar of Ali Makhdoom Ganj Bakhsh Hujwiri and reached completion (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 54, folio 42). In Chishti's account Bahlul tells Husain that here in Lahore is the radiant mazar of Hazrat Pir Makhdoom Ali Ganj Bakhsh Hujwiri; whoever comes there finds the way; Husain is to stay at its grace-bestowing door, the word uncertainly printed, Hujwiri being his patron and guide (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 52); and Husain kept his prayers at the khanqah for twelve years (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 53). Kugle tells how Shah Hussayn "lived as a mendicant student, wandering in the empty lands outside the city walls by day and returning at night to stay in the shrine", for "the first half of his life" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 206, folio 185).

Kanhaiya Lal names the great visitors who kept *chillas* at the mazar: many elders, among them Khwaja Moinuddin Chishti of Ajmer and Khwaja Farid of Pakpattan, came here, kept *chillas* and received grace (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 289, folio 292). Chishti adds Mian Wadda, who on Sayyid Mahmud's advice kept one *chilla*, that is forty days, in retreat, and was told that something complete would be granted him, the word for it illegible; he did so, Chishti writes, and complete purity was attained (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 449). He tells of Sa'd-ud-Din of the *Tazkira-e Fuqara*, who came from Mecca to visit the saint's khanqah in Lahore, the name damaged in the printed text; of Shah Dami[OCR?] Shah's *chilla* at the shrine; of Fakhr-ud-Din, who spent most of his time at the khanqah (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 675; p. 756); and, in a damaged sentence naming only Hazrat Data Sahib, of Farid seeking his help, the end of the sentence illegible (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 228).

Sharib tells of a later saint ordered by his pir to attend the radiant mazar regularly (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 250, folio 246); he kept the duty for twelve years, very strictly, between the dawn prayer and the maktab, and found stillness in two places, on the bank of the river and at the mazar of Hazrat Data Ganj Bakhsh, where he found calm and singleness of mind (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 250, folio 246; pp. 250–251, folios 246–247). Sharib does not name the town in that passage, though the shrine he names is Data Ganj Bakhsh's (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 250–251, folios 246–247).

## Qawwali and Hazri

The music that fills the shrine has its own history in Abbas's account. Citing a writer she calls Lahori, she names "notable musicians such as Ustad Bare Ghulam Ali, Ustad Chote Ghulam Ali, Niaz Hussain Shami, Fateh Ali Mubarik, and Dina Qawwal, who performed hazrī in terms of singing mystical texts at the urs of Data Ganj Bakhsh in Lahore. He also mentions women musicians who performed hazrī at the Sufi shrines in Lahore, especially at the time of the urs" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 90, folio not stated). She writes of "the Chishtiyya Sufi shrines in Pakistan, such as Hazrat Data Ganj Bakhsh Hujwiri in Lahore and Baba Fariduddin Ganj-e Shakar at Pakpattan Sharif", where "the same ritual in qawwālī discourse is followed. Since this is an exclusively male domain, women's presence at such performances is rare" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 84, folio not stated). At such shrines, she writes, the urs qawwali "is always initiated with a qaul or saying of the prophet Muhammad", 'Mun kunto Maulā fā Alī-un Maulā', in Arabic, and "During an urs performance at the Chishtiyya shrines the poetry is focused on Ali, on the saint buried at that particular shrine, and on his spiritual lineage" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 84–85, folios not stated).

## The Urs

The crowning event of the shrine’s year is the *urs* of Data Ganj Bakhsh, held according to the Islamic lunar calendar in the month of Safar, over three days that fall by tradition around the 18th to the 20th. In the language of the Sufis the urs — literally “wedding” — celebrates not the saint’s death but his union with the Divine, and so it is kept in reverence and joy rather than mourning. During those days the darbar is transformed: the tomb and its courts are illuminated, special prayers and Qur’an recitations and *chadar* ceremonies are held, and continuous qawwali and devotional assemblies fill the nights; the langar is expanded to feed tens of thousands, and the streets around the shrine take on the character of a great fair (*mela*), with stalls of sweets, books, and devotional wares. Pilgrims pour in not only from Lahore but from across Punjab and Pakistan, and the authorities commonly declare special arrangements to manage a gathering that can swell, over the three days, to between one and one and a half million souls. Other seasons too raise the tide of devotion — the last ten nights of Ramadan, the Prophet’s birthday (Eid Milad-un-Nabi), and the other holy nights — but none approaches the scale and centrality of the urs, the defining festival of Data Darbar.

The older histories give the urs its dates in Safar, with a second fair on the Last Wednesday. Kanhaiya Lal puts the great *mela*, that is the urs, on the 18th [OCR?] of Safar, and a second *mela* on the day of the Last Wednesday, both drawing people from far (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 291, folio 294). Chishti gives the urs on the twentieth and twenty-second of Safar, both numbers uncertainly printed, and a *mela* on the last Wednesday of Safar (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 171). Lahori mentions no annual urs at all; the observance he records is weekly (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 195).

At the urs, Abbas writes, the sexes are separated: "in the popular culture of today, whether at the shrine or in the concert, it is usually a mixed group, except during an urs such as at Data Ganj Bakhsh in Lahore, when the shrine establishment segregates the women's sections. Women may be hidden from men's view by a curtain, and in some shrines they sit behind a curtain set up between them and the musicians" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 90, folio not stated). Khalid names it among the Sufi shrines that one Nadeem has visited "on the occasion of urs" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 219, folio not stated). Not every devotee comes: a senior disciple of the Chishti Sabiri order told Rozehnal, "We do not even go for the 'urs", adding "We only go for one-day attendance." (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 208, folio 200).

## Legends and Miracles

One miracle settled the dispute over his mosque. Sharib relates, citing the *Safinat al-Awliya*, that when the mosque was finished he summoned the ulama and the mashaikh, all prayed there and he led the prayer; then he asked them to consider where the Kaaba lay, and he turned his attention to it, the veils were lifted, and all saw the Kaaba before them and the mosque's direction true (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 28, folio 24, note 20). Lahori and Chishti tell the same miracle in answer to the same objection (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 194; Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 163–164). In Sharib's chapter on the saint it is the only wonder narrated; of the rest he says only that his *tasarrufat* are countless (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 23, folio 19). Kanhaiya Lal says the same in another way, that his lakhs of *karamat* are recorded in books, and narrates none (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 289, folio 292).

He is remembered as appearing at his own tomb. In Sharib's account of the saint who served there twelve years, a luminous form appeared, asked whether he knew who it was, and said: "I am Ali Hujwiri"; this, it said, was the reward of the service he had done for twelve years; and it enriched him with inward bounty, granted him *wilayat*, and made him drunk with the wine of unity (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 251, folio 247). Chishti records a vision of Shah Husain's in which, for his twelve years' service, he is a perfect *wali*, the word *wali* uncertainly printed, and prints its couplet (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 53–54).

The disciples of Rozehnal's study say he still appears. "When you visualize Data Sahab, you see Data Sahab. Many times he will appear to you and give you messages." They "compare the spiritual energy (faizan) that radiates from the saint's tomb during muraqaba to an intense electric current surging through the body" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 208, folio 200). The burial of Faruq Ahmad has its own wonders: "Throughout the time of the funeral, a light rain fell from that cloud of mercy only above that spot in Lahore" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 75, folio 65), and workers who later came to move the grave "discovered that his body was still fresh and had not decomposed—one of the well-known tropes of sainthood", so that "Faruq Ahmad's tomb was indeed left undisturbed" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 76, folio 66).

He spoke of such wonders himself. Rizvi quotes his account of the hierarchy of the saints: "(God) has made the Saints the governors of the universe"; "Through the blessing of their advent the rain falls from heaven, and through the purity of their lives the plants spring up from the earth, and through their spiritual influence the Muslims gain victories over the unbelievers"; "Among them there are four thousand who are concealed and do not know one another"; and the graded officers, three hundred Akhyar, forty Abdal, seven Abrar, four Awtad and three Nuqaba, and "one, called Qutb or Ghaws. All these know one another and cannot act save by mutual consent." — closing, "I myself—God be praised!—have had ocular experience (khabar-i 'iyan) of this matter." (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 115–116, folios 97–98). His book tells, too, of Hasan of Basra corrected by God in a dream: "The same night, Hasan dreamed that he saw God and said to Him: ‘O Lord, wherein does Thy good pleasure consist?' and that God answered: ‘O Hasan, you found My good pleasure, but did not know its value: if ... you had said your prayers after Habib, and if the rightness of his intention had restrained you from taking offence at his pronunciation, I should have been well pleased with you'" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 45, folio 29).

## The 2010 Bombing and the Shrine’s Endurance

The history of Data Darbar also carries the wounds of the present age. On the night of 1–2 July 2010, the shrine was struck by a twin suicide bombing that killed dozens of worshippers and injured many more — among the deadliest of a wave of militant attacks on the Sufi shrines of Pakistan in those years. It was an assault not merely on a building but on the whole plural, devotional, and tolerant world that shrines like Data Darbar embody, and that the attackers regarded as heresy. Yet the darbar endured. The wounded were carried out, the dead were mourned, and within days the pilgrims returned; the langar was cooked, the prayers resumed, and the qawwali was sung again. That refusal of ordinary devotees to let fear empty the shrine has become part of its meaning — a testimony to the resilience of a faith of love and service against the creed of violence.

Haroon Khalid gives a different count: "On 1 July 2010 three bomb blasts rocked the most important shrine of Lahore, Data Darbar, resulting in the death of five people and injuring more than hundred and seventy-five" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated). In another book he names "Data Ganj Baksh in Lahore" among the Sufi shrines that have "come under attack" as "a wave of Islamic purism has spread over Pakistan due to the influence of the Taliban" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 278, folio 252). Kasmani, who elsewhere calls Data Darbar "Lahore's most prominent shrine complex", writes, without naming the shrine or dating the attack, that "A terror attack at Lahore's most prominent shrine had led to security measures being beefed up at major Sufi sites across the country", a change she had seen at Sehwan by 2011 (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 104, folio 87).

## The City of Data

More than any other figure, Ali Hujwiri made Lahore a holy city, and the whole later sacred landscape of the Punjab is bound, in devotion, to his threshold. He is remembered as the spiritual founder from whom the city takes one of its dearest names, *Data ki Nagri*, “the city of Data”; and tradition holds that no saint could settle in Lahore, or find his own path, without first paying homage at Hujwiri’s grave. The stories of the later masters bear this out. Khwaja Moinuddin Chishti, before he founded his order at Ajmer, kept his forty-day retreat at Data’s tomb and sealed his devotion in the famous couplet. The great Qadiri saint of the Mughal age, Mian Mir, is said to have kept a forty-day *chilla* at Hujwiri’s khanqah, joining his own sanctity to that of the city’s patron. At the shrine of Bibi Pak Daman it is told that Hujwiri himself, on first coming to Lahore, would go each Thursday night to sit in reverence at the women’s graves. And even the modern poet-philosopher Muhammad Iqbal — who lies buried a little way off, by the Badshahi Mosque — is remembered in a beloved Lahori legend as having been visited one night by unseen guests, among them Data Ganj Bakhsh himself.

In this way Data Darbar is not merely one shrine among many but the source and centre of a whole network of holiness: the point from which, in the imagination of the Punjab, the sanctity of Lahore radiates outward to Bibi Pak Daman and Mian Mir, to Madho Lal Hussain at Baghbanpura, and beyond. To visit Data is, for many, to begin at the beginning — to salute the master before turning to his spiritual children. It is this that makes the darbar the true heart of the city: not only the busiest of its shrines and the largest of its kitchens, but the origin of its identity as a place under a saint’s protection, a city that belongs, in the end, to Data.

The books bear out the bond with Bibi Pak Daman: Abbas, who calls him "himself a great Sufi mystic", writes that he "was a devotee of the shrines of the Bibi Pak Daman" and "offered prayers at their shrines and spent considerable time in meditation at the shrines" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 161, folio not stated; pp. 161–162, folios not stated). Kugle calls him "Lahore's patron saint" and calls Shah Hussayn, too, "one of Lahore's patron saints", without reconciling the two (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 206, folio 185; p. 204, folio 183). Pir Makki, Chishti records, is said by some to have been his disciple (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 674); and in Jahangir's time the elders of Sayyid Chiragh Shah were, in a sentence several of whose words are uncertain in the printed text, teachers and maulvis connected with the khanqah of Hazrat Ali Hujwiri in a way the text leaves unclear (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 207).

The grave was the city's landmark, the point from which other sites were found. Lahori places the tomb of Pir Ghazi, known as Pir az Ghaib, with reference to it: on the far side of Ali Makhdoom Ganj Bakhsh Hujwiri's tomb, the mazar stands on a high platform (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 270, folio 258); Chishti sets the grave of Pir Ghaib with the shrine of Hazrat Data Ganj Bakhsh, the word for shrine uncertainly printed, to its south and west (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 281). Qutb Shah settled outside the Bhati Gate, beside the mazar of Hazrat Data Ganj Bakhsh Sahib (its epithet uncertainly printed), in the house of Sain Jali Shah Majzub, and Chishti mentions an unbaked-brick *kotha* belonging to Hazrat Data Sahib (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 237). Kanhaiya Lal uses the mazar as a bearing for the Shia Imambara, which stands outside the Bhati Gate, south of Ganj Bakhsh's radiant mazar (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 301, folio 304).

The city has other saints, and not every order visits them. The Chishti Sabiri disciples of Rozehnal's study say: "There are many mazars of unknown shaykhs in Lahore. Some of them are qalandars [a roaming sect of antinomian Sufis] and can only give their visitors what they have—that is, they make them qalandars as well. In our silsila we avoid this." (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 207, folio 199). Lahori's own book holds a rival claim to the saint's primacy in the city: its text makes Shaikh Ismail Muhaddith the first caller to Islam in Lahore, dying in 448 AH, where Data Ganj Bakhsh dies in 465; a footnote of the editor, preferring the notice of Ghausi, moves Ismail to 980 AH and makes Data the earlier figure by five centuries (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 191–192, folios 189–190 as printed).

## Setting: The City of Lahore

The books frame the city of his day as a Ghaznavid one. "Lahore became the first center of Persian-inspired Muslim culture in the Subcontinent", Schimmel writes, "the name of Hujwiri, who composed his famous Persian treatise on Sufism in this town, has already been mentioned; his tomb still provides a place of pilgrimage for the Panjabis." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 376, folio 345). Rizvi, writing of the province, says that "the annexation of the Panjab by Mahmud of Ghazna and its incorporation into his empire prompted many sufis to settle in the area" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 130, folio 112). The city's literary life in that age shows in Browne's translation of Nizami-i Arudi's *Chahar Maqala*, whose annotation says of the Ghaznavid panegyrist Mas'ud-i Sa'd-i Salman that "he himself was born at Lahore, of which, in several passages in his poems, he speaks as his native place" (Nizami-i Arudi, *Chahar Maqala*, p. 135, folio 117).

Bhandari's account of the suba of Lahore describes the city: an ancient city on the bank of the Ravi, whose founding is attributed to Lav (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525); after Sultan Mahmud's conquest Malik Ayaz rebuilt the city and built a pakka fort (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525). It is a great city and a mighty metropolis, he writes; craftsmen of every land live there, and in every lane and bazaar there are mosques beyond counting (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 524). Besides Hujwiri, he writes, others close to the Eternal Lord lie at rest in that city, and on its outskirts there are many delightful gardens and thousands of joy-giving flower beds (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 523).

The gate by which the shrine stands is one of the old city's twelve. Chishti lists them, the Bhati gate among them (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 498), and Khalid writes of "the walled city of Lahore, of which Bhatti gate is one of the twelve entry points" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 12, ch. 6, folio not stated). Chishti's history of the city's monuments sets Kanhaiya Lal's account of the Sikh years in a wider frame: when the cantonments of Ranjit Singh's armies were built, the bricks of old tombs and houses were pulled out, and thousands of old tombs, domes, mosques, gardens and havelis that were memorials of the nobles of old were lost, two of the words uncertainly printed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 431).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The year of his death.** The site's account gives c. 1072/77, "between about 1072 and 1077 CE (465–469 AH)". Lahori's author gives 465 AH, with three chronograms (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 195); his editor calls 465 wrong and shows him alive in 490 AH [OCR?], naming no year of death (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 196, folio 184); the publisher's catalogue of the same edition prints 400–465 with no era (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 353). Chishti gives 495 from Jami and 465 from the *Tazkirat al-Asfiya*, with chronograms yielding 495, 464 and 466, every figure uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 165). Kanhaiya Lal gives 465 (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 289, folio 292). Sharib gives 465 AH and, as the view of some, 456 AH (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 23, folio 19). Rizvi reports the tomb tablet's 465/1072-73 and Nicholson's "between 465 and 469/1076—77", and himself concludes that he died some time after 1089 (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 130, folio 112; p. 468, folio 444). Schimmel gives 1071, at times with a circa (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 8, folio 4; Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 119, folio 88; Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 355, folio 337). Ernst and Lawrence give 1074, ca. 1071, 1072 and 465/1073 (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 22, folio 7; p. 52, folio 38; p. 138, folio 125; p. 236, folio 226). Rozehnal gives 1072 once and 1074 four times (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 72, folio 62; p. 206, folio 198).
- **The year of his birth.** The site's account gives c. 1009, as does Rizvi, "about 1009" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 130, folio 112). Lahori's editor gives 400 AH [OCR?] and calls it indeterminate (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 196). Sharib gives no year (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 21, folio 17).
- **When he came to Lahore, and with whom.** The site's account gives around 1039 CE (431 AH), in the age of the Ghaznavids. Kanhaiya Lal gives 431 AH, in the company of Sultan Masud, son of Sultan Mahmud (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 289, folio 292). Sharib reports that some say he came with Sultan Masud's army and others with Shaikh Ahmad Jamawi[OCR?] Sarakhsi and Shaikh Abu Sa'id Hujwiri (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 22–23, folios 18–19, note 8). Rizvi infers "c. 1035, while Sultan Mas‘ud I (1031-41) reigned in Ghazna" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 130, folio 112). Bhandari has him come from Ghazni with Sultan Mahmud (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 523).
- **Whether he stayed in Lahore.** Lahori's author writes that he lived in Lahore all his life (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 195), and Kanhaiya Lal of thirty-four years in Lahore (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 289, folio 292). Rizvi has him make at least two long journeys of more than two years each, stand by Khattali's side at his death in 1065, and return to Lahore after 1067 (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 130, folio 112). Schimmel says he "spent his last years in Lahore" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 61, folio 51).
- **The Zanjani whose funeral he met.** Chishti names him Husain Zanjani (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 163), as does Rizvi, "Shaikh Husain Zinjani" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 130, folio 112); Sharib names him Shaikh Hasan Zanjani (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 23, folio 19, note 9). Lahori's editor holds that the brother-in-the-path of the *Fawaid al-Fuad* is a different person from the Shaikh Husain Zanjani buried at Chah Miran (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 196).
- **The first saint of Lahore.** The site's account calls him "the first great Sufi to make his home in Lahore". Chishti, Sharib and Rizvi all have an eminent Sufi, Zanjani, already in the city when he arrived (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 163; Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 22, folio 18; Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 130, folio 112). Lahori's text makes Shaikh Ismail Muhaddith the first caller to Islam in Lahore, dying in 448 AH, and its editor moves him to 980 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 191–192, folios 189–190 as printed).
- **His pedigree.** Lahori's text runs the line through Shah Shuja, Abu al-Hasan Ali and Husain Asghar to Zaid Shahid (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 194); Kanhaiya Lal through Sayyid Abd Allah (uncertainly printed), then Sayyid Abu al-Hasan Ali and Sayyid Hasan (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 291, folio 294); Chishti through Shaiban and Sakar (both uncertainly printed) and Sad (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 172), while also naming his father Hazrat Usman ibn Ali Jalali Ghaznawi, the last name uncertainly printed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 163). Lahori's editor contests the genealogy (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 194).
- **His teachers and his chain.** The site's account names Abu'l-Fadl Muhammad al-Khuttali as his guide. Between Khuttali and Shibli, Lahori puts Shaikh Husri (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 194), as does Rizvi (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 130, folio 112); Sharib puts a Hazrat whose printed name, perhaps Khizri, cannot be read with certainty (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 22, folio 18, note 6); Kanhaiya Lal puts Shaikh Ali Haqiri, the name uncertainly printed (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 291, folio 294). Sharib names Shaikh Abu al-Qasim as his *ustad* (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 22, folio 18, note 4); Rizvi names Gurgani, Khwaja Muzaffar and al-Ashqani beside his "main teacher" Khattali "of Syria" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 130, folio 112); Schimmel names no teacher, only his attendance at Qushayri's meetings (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 119, folio 88).
- **His silsila.** The site's account ties him to the school of Junayd, and Chishti calls his silsila the Junaidi (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 163). Abbas counts his shrine among "the Chishtiyya Sufi shrines in Pakistan" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 84, folio not stated). Rozehnal writes that he "is remembered as an honorary Chishti Sabiri", while his order's shaykh says he "is not in the line of our silsila directly" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 207, folio 199), and a disciple says "you are in his silsila" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 208, folio 200). Ernst and Lawrence place him in no lineage and do not call him a Chishti (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 54, folio 40); Sharib names no order (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 21–29, folios 17–25).
- **The title and its meaning.** The site's account renders *Data Ganj Bakhsh* as "the Master who bestows the treasure", *Ganj Bakhsh* as "Bestower of Treasure" and *Data* as "the Giver", and derives the title from Khwaja Moinuddin Chishti's couplet, as Sharib does (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 21–22, folios 17–18, note 3). Rizvi gives "‘Distributor of (Unlimited) Treasure’" and says only that "Later Muslims posthumously conferred" it (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 130, folio 112); Rozehnal gives 'The Lavish Giver' (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 207, folio 199).
- **Khwaja Moinuddin Chishti at the tomb.** The site's account has him keep a forty-day *chilla* "on his way into the subcontinent", before he founded his order at Ajmer, and Chishti has him go on from the mazar to Ajmer, the abode of good (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 270). Lahori has him come from Ajmer to Lahore to keep the *chilla* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 200–201, folios 188–189), its editor dating the arrival to about 588 AH in one note and about 585 AH in another (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 200, note 2; p. 198, note 1). Sharib calls his stay *itikaf* (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 21–22, folios 17–18, note 3); Rizvi speaks of "hard ascetic exercises" at the tomb and, elsewhere, of his staying "near the tombs" of Zanjani and Hujwiri (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 130, folio 112; p. 139, folio 121). The dominion granted to him there is "all of India" for Ernst and Lawrence (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 109, folio 96) and "all of South Asia" for Rozehnal (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 207, folio 199).
- **The wording of the couplet.** The site's account prints it with the vowel marks of the *izafat* and with commas; Sharib prints the same words without them (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 21–22, folios 17–18, note 3).
- **The saint's mosque.** Lahori's author says it still stood (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 194). Chishti has it rebuilt on the same foundation by Gulzar Shah in 1269 AH, the year uncertainly printed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 163). Kanhaiya Lal has it rebuilt in 1868 [OCR?] AD (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 290, folio 293). Lahori's editor has it pulled down and a new mosque built, with a stone to mark the old mihrab (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 194, folio 192, note 4).
- **The dome.** Lahori has it built by Haji Nur Muhammad Sadhu twelve years before the author wrote, and repaired by Mian Muhammad Jan of Amritsar (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 195). Kanhaiya Lal has it built in 1247 [OCR?] AH by Nur Muhammad Samawa [OCR?] (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 290, folio 293). Chishti records chronograms by Nur Muhammad Chishti on the building, one ending with a year that begins 12, the rest illegible (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 171).
- **The gate inscription.** Kanhaiya Lal prints 376, itself uncertain, under the gilded couplets over the inner gate, a number that does not agree with the 431 and 465 he prints on the same page (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 289, folio 292).
- **Ranjit Singh and the shrine.** The site's account says that under Maharaja Ranjit Singh "the shrine was maintained". Kanhaiya Lal says that he respected the mazar and sent thousands of rupees for its interior [OCR?], but stripped every one of its outer buildings to the foundations, leaving only the mazar's own building (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 289, folio 292). Chishti records a great Qur'an given by him, and a mosque and *takia* tied to his sarkar (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 169; p. 171).
- **The keepers of the shrine.** The site's account says that the shrine has no hereditary keepers and that the state has administered it since shortly after 1947. Kanhaiya Lal counts more than seventy mujawirs who divide the offerings in equal shares (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 291, folio 294); Chishti records houses and land held by the mujawirs (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 281; p. 345), and a *sajjada nashin* at an urs, of a shrine he leaves unclear (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 78).
- **The date of the urs.** The site's account gives three days around the 18th to the 20th of Safar. Kanhaiya Lal gives the 18th [OCR?] of Safar, with a second fair on the Last Wednesday (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 291, folio 294); Chishti gives the twentieth and twenty-second of Safar, both uncertainly printed, with a fair on the last Wednesday of Safar (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 171). Lahori records no annual urs, only a weekly vigil and *milad* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 195).
- **Where the shrine stands.** The site's account sets it "in the old heart of Lahore", and Rozehnal "in the old city of Lahore" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 206, folio 198). Lahori places it outside the Bhati Gate (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 289, folio 277), Chishti outside the Bhati Gate, the name uncertainly printed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 165), Kanhaiya Lal among the buildings outside Lahore (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 4, folio 3), and Khalid "near the walled city of Lahore" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 207, folio not stated).
- **The patron saint of Lahore.** The site's account makes Hujwiri "the very patron of the city". Kugle calls him "Lahore's patron saint" and Shah Hussayn "one of Lahore's patron saints" (Kugle, *Sufis and Saints' Bodies*, p. 206, folio 185; p. 204, folio 183).
- **Women at the shrine.** The site's account calls it "an inclusive space, open to men and women". Abbas writes that at the urs "the shrine establishment segregates the women's sections" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 90, folio not stated), and that at such Chishtiyya shrines qawwali is "an exclusively male domain" where "women's presence at such performances is rare" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 84, folio not stated).
- **The bombing of 2010.** The site's account describes, on the night of 1–2 July 2010, "a twin suicide bombing that killed dozens of worshippers". Khalid writes of "three bomb blasts" on 1 July 2010, "resulting in the death of five people and injuring more than hundred and seventy-five" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).
- **The Kashf al-Asrar.** Sharib counts it among his works (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 23, folio 19, note 12) and cites its Urdu translation in nine of his twenty footnotes (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 29, folio 25). Lahori's editor removes it from his works, calling it merely attributed to him and forged (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 196); the publisher's catalogue of the same edition attributes it to Hazrat Data Ganj Bakhsh Lahori (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 354).
- **The English title of his book.** The site's account renders it "The Unveiling of the Veiled", and Wahid Bakhsh's English version is titled 'Unveiling the Veiled' (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 246, folio 239). Ernst and Lawrence give *The Unveiling of the Concealed* (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 138, folio 125), as does Rozehnal in one place, while in another he gives 'The Unveiling of the Secrets' (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 111, folio 102; p. 72, folio 62). Schimmel gives 'Unveiling of the Hidden' (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 119, folio 88).
- **Zhukovsky's edition.** Schimmel gives it as published at Leningrad in 1926 (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 480, folio 449); the list of authorities in Lahori's edition gives it as printed at Moscow (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 196–197).
- **Habibi's article on his death.** The list of authorities in Lahori's edition dates it February 1940 [OCR?] in one place (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 196–197) and February 1960 in another, spelling the saint's title Dana for Data (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 305, folio 293).

## Legacy

No shrine stands closer to the heart of Lahore than Data Darbar, and few figures loom larger in the spiritual history of the subcontinent than the saint it enshrines. Ali Hujwiri was the pioneer who carried the sober, learned Sufism of Baghdad into the Punjab; the author whose *Kashf al-Mahjub* remains a lamp for seekers everywhere; and the “bestower of the treasure” at whose tomb the founder of the Chishti order himself sat in devotion. From his grave the whole later tradition of South Asian Sufism drew its beginning, and to it, still, the city brings its dead to be prayed over, its poor to be fed, its sick and sorrowful to seek relief, and its lovers of God to sing through the night. Bombed and rebuilt, endowed by emperors and thronged by the poorest, Data Darbar remains what it has been for nine centuries: the living heart of the city of Data, and one of the enduring wonders of the Muslim world.

The books measure his legacy in his book's long life. For Schimmel he is the earliest instance of "the mystics who had settled in the Subcontinent as early as the 11th century" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 8, folio 4). For Rizvi he is the authority who classified Sufism and the saint whose grave drew the founder of the Chishtiyya (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 130–131, folios 112–113). Ernst and Lawrence find that later *tazkiras* "took their cue from the eleventh-century theorist of Persian Sufism, Shaykh 'Ali Hujwiri", who "classified the Sufi orders into 12, linking each to a famous early Sufi master, despite the fact that there was seldom a correspondence between these early ascetics and the well-known Sufi orders" of later times (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 38, folio 24); they speak elsewhere of "Hujwiri's theoretical list of ten approved and two heretical orders" and of "the list of ten acceptable Sufi schools or teachings compiled by Hujwiri in the eleventh century, which he acknowledged was a theoretical construct based on individual masters, not a current institutional reality" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 72, folio 58). They make him, too, the first witness to a long lament: he "quoted the truism of Abu al-Hasan of Fushanja: that Sufism was in the beginning a reality without a name, but it had become over time a name without a reality. Hujwiri offers no commentary on this startling observation" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 22, folio 7).

Among living Sufis his book is still read in a set order. In the Chishti Sabiri order of Rozehnal's study his writings head a list of five premodern authors that are "especially popular" among today's disciples, with al-Ghazali, Ibn al-'Arabi, al-Suhrawardi and Rumi (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 171, folio 163); a senior disciple put it third in the order's course of reading: "Then you go still deeper with Kashf al-mahjub [a translation of the Persian classic by al-Hujwiri]. That delves into all the deeper levels of Sufism" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 173, folio 165). Rozehnal credits Nicholson's translation with sparking Shahidullah Faridi's interest in Sufism (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 72, folio 62). The edition of Lahori's book gathers the older literature on the saint in a list of fifteen authorities: Attar's *Tazkirat al-Awliya*, in a Tehran edition; Amir Hasan Sijzi's *Fawaid al-Fawad*; Ali Mahmud Jandar's *Durar-e Nizami*; Muhammad Yaqub bin Usman Ghaznavi's *Risala Abdaliya*, in a British Museum manuscript; Jami's *Nafahat al-Uns*; Ahmad Zanjani's *Tuhfat al-Wasilin*, noted as no longer extant; Abu al-Fazl's *Ain-e Akbari*; Abd al-Samad's *Akhbar al-Asfiya*, at Islamia College Peshawar; Lal Beg Bakhshi's *Samarat al-Quds*, in a manuscript owned by Maulana Nusrat Naushahi of Sharaqpur; Ghausi Mandwi's *Azkar-e Abrar*; Dara Shikoh's *Safinat al-Awliya*, in a Lucknow printing; Zhukovsky's edition of the *Kashf al-Mahjub*; Habibi's article on the date of his death; Muhammad Musa Amritsari's *Muqaddama Kashf al-Mahjub*, in the translation of Abu al-Hasanat Sayyid Muhammad Ahmad Qadri (Lahore, 1393 AH [OCR?]); and Muhammad Aslam's article on Data Ganj Bakhsh's coming to Lahore, in *Tarikhi Maqalat* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 196–197). A Persian life of the saint, Muhammad 'Alim's *Ahwal-i 'Ali Hujwiri*, whose fourth to sixth chapters "concern his authority over India and the Punjab, and the visit of Mu'in al-Din Chishti there", survives, Ernst and Lawrence record, in a single manuscript at Gulra (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 236, folio 226). Kanhaiya Lal defers to the books, Dara Shikoh's *Safinat al-Awliya* and Jami's *Nafahat al-Uns*, and then endorses them: Dara Shikoh (the name uncertainly printed) in his *Safinat al-Awliya* and Maulana Jami in his *Nafahat al-Uns* praise this elder to the utmost, he writes, and doubtless he was just as the books describe him (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 289, folio 292).
