## Overview

At Kasur, south of Lahore, lies the shrine of Hazrat Bulleh Shah (Sayyid Abdullah Shah Qadiri, c. 1680–1757), the towering Punjabi Sufi poet whose *kafis* are among the best-loved verses ever composed in the language. Heir to the tradition of Shah Hussain and forerunner of Waris Shah, Bulleh Shah gave Punjabi mysticism some of its most piercing and rebellious poetry — verse that scorned the hypocrisy of the learned, dissolved the boundaries of caste and creed, and sang of a love for God that overturns the self. His tomb at Kasur is a place of pilgrimage and, still more, his poetry a living presence, sung by qawwals and folk-singers and, in our own day, by pop musicians across South Asia.

The books that speak of his tomb agree on the town. Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori, writing in 1875, says that his pearl-scattering mazar at Kasur is a place of pilgrimage for the people (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 82, folio 70 [marker: 80]). Zahur ul-Hasan Sharib writes that his mazar is at Kasur (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 343, folio 339), and his translator Christopher Shackle that "His later life was apparently spent in Kasur, which is the site of his tomb" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, pp. 8–9, folios vii–viii). On much else (the year of his death, the meaning of his name, the road that brought him to his master, the look of the tomb itself) they differ, and the differences are set out side by side below.

## The Life of the Saint

Bulleh Shah was born around 1680, into a respected Sayyid family; his father was a learned and pious man, an imam and teacher, and the family settled in the Kasur region of the Punjab. Well educated in Arabic and Persian and in the religious sciences, Bulleh Shah might have become a scholar of the orthodox kind — but he was drawn instead to the burning inward path, and became the disciple of Shah Inayat Qadiri, a Sufi master of Lahore of the Qadiri-Shattari line, who was by trade an *arain* (a gardener-cultivator) rather than a high-born sayyid. That a proud Sayyid should bow to a master of humble caste scandalised his relations, and the tension became one of the great themes of his verse: Bulleh Shah defiantly proclaimed that love, not lineage, was the only nobility. He lived through the turbulent decades of the Mughal decline and the invasions of the eighteenth century, and died at Kasur around 1757 — where, tradition holds, the orthodox at first refused him burial in the common graveyard, so fierce had been his attacks on their hypocrisy.

Shackle warns at the outset that "remarkably few details of his life can be reliably established. Neither the exact date nor the precise place of his birth is known for certain." (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 8, folio vii). The only birth year in his volume stands, with a question mark, in its Library of Congress line: "Bullhe Shāh, 1680?-1758?, author." (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 5, folio not stated). Khaled Ahmad, in his introduction to Taufiq Rafat's selection, is more definite: "Abdullah Shah was born in 1680 A.D. in Uch Gilanian", "the city of Saints in Bahawalpur, in the family of a pious Syed." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 22, folio not stated). Shemeem Burney Abbas agrees: "His original name was Abdullah Shah, and his family were Syeds who had settled in Uch Gilanian, the city of saints in Bahawalpur, where Bulle Shah was born" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 107–108, folios not stated). A note in Rafat's selection adds that "Bulleh Shah is said to be a descendant of the founder and was born in Uch Gilanian", the founder being Abdul Qadir Gilani, and observes that "The hagiographer of Bulleh Shah has, thus, an immaculate background against which to present him to posterity." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 235, folio not stated). Lahori, too, calls him a sayyid, Sayyid Bulleh Shah (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 219, folio 207).

Sharib names the father as Sayyid Darvesh Sakhi Muhammad and traces the family's movements in three stages: the father lived at Uch, left it for Malkwal, and went on from Malkwal to settle at a place whose printed name, perhaps Pandu, cannot be read with certainty (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 340, folio 336). A story told to Haroon Khalid by his guide Iqbal Qaiser speaks of "the village of Pandoke where Bulleh Shah's father worked as an Imam in the local mosque" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated). Khaled Ahmad writes that "His father moved to a village of Qasur, a district of Lahore division today, where the six-year old Abdullah received a strictly orthodox religious training." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 22, folio not stated), and Abbas that "The family migrated to Kasur when Bulle Shah was six years old" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 108, folio not stated).

His schooling, in Sharib's account, began under his father, a learned man of his time, with a command of Arabic and Persian; then he reached Kasur and attended Hafiz Ghulam Murtaza, from whom he had further instruction, and Kasur appears here as his place of study, three pages before it appears as the place of his tomb (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 340, folio 336). Khaled Ahmad writes that "No one knows how the boy made his inner journey from received knowledge to an intensely felt heterodox devotional religion", but that the hagiographic *tazkiras* narrate that "his teacher Khawaja Hafiz Ghulam Murtaza Qasuri trained two rebellious personalities; the other was Waris Shah" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 22, folio not stated). Shackle reports a reading of a phrase in one poem as a reference to "Bullhe Shah's hometown of Kasur, which was famed for the learning of the local Islamic scholar Maulana Ghulam Murtaza", and goes on: "Rather than study with this great scholar, Bullhe Shah gave his allegiance to Shah Inayat, from whom he received the word (kalmā) of his Sufi teaching." (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 155, folio 130).

Sharib then turns to the search for a guide: having finished with the outward sciences, he longed for the inward ones, which cannot be had without a perfect guide, and he was taken up with that search (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 340, folio 336). He relates that, asleep under a tree, the young man saw Sayyid Abd al-Hakim, his ancestor in the fifth generation, seated on a throne flying in the air; the throne came down, the ancestor asked who he was, and he answered that he was a sayyid (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 340, folio 336). On the next page he gives Sayyid Abd al-Hakim his own name and his father's; the elder asks for water, is given milk, drinks a little of the bowl and returns the rest, and on drinking it he was carried out of himself and his breast was illumined; on parting the elder says that he had held something in trust for him and has today handed it over, assigns him ten rupees a day and orders him to seek a *murshid-e kamil* (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 341, folio 337). Whether this meeting continues the dream or is a separate waking episode is not clear from the account as it stands. Sharib also has the father, after *muraqaba*, tell his son that the elder graces the village of Sanda, locating a living elder at the village of Sanda by contemplation alone; where Sanda lies is not said (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 341, folio 337). When he set out for Shah Inayat, the father gladly gave his permission and gave him some rupees and a *dastar* to present to Hazrat Inayat Allah Qadiri (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 341, folio 337).

Lahori tells the approach to Shah Inayat quite differently, inside his entry on Khwaja Abd al-Khaliq, whom he places as founder of the Uwaisiya. The Khwaja went with Sayyid Bulleh Shah and his own brother Gul Sher Muhammad, intending *bai'at*, to Shaikh Abd al-Hakim Qadiri at Talamba [OCR?]; after *muraqaba* the shaikh took Gul Sher Muhammad as his disciple, telling Sayyid Bulleh Shah that the portion of his lot lay with Shah Inayat Qadiri, and that if he went to Qasur he would receive his share (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 219). In this telling his bond with Shah Inayat is the result of another shaikh's refusal and referral, and the passage gives no date, no shrine and no poetry.

Sharib has him sent to Hazrat Shah Inayat Allah Qadiri and initiated at his hand in Lahore, honoured with *bai'at* and afterwards given the *khirqa-e khilafat*; the pir set a price on the initiation, a condition in four items of five hundred rupees each (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 342, folio 338). Lahori makes him the *khalifa* of Hazrat Shah Inayat Qadiri Lahori, living in the *qasba* of Kasur (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 81, folio 69 [marker: 79]). After a time in his pir's service, Sharib says, he settled on the bank of the Chenab, given to worship and spiritual exertion, and then took up residence at Kasur again, busied in teaching, instruction and preaching (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 342–343, folios 338–339). Shackle adds that "Bullhe Shah remained unmarried and left no direct descendants." (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 8, folio vii). Annemarie Schimmel, who never names Kasur, says only that "he lived near Lahore" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 419, folio 388).

The order he belonged to is given in several ways. Lahori heads his notice Sayyid Bulleh Shah Qadiri Shattari (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 16, folio 4) and, at the entry itself, Sayyid Bulleh Shah Qadiri Shattari Qasuri (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 81–82, folios 69–70 [markers: 79–80]). Shackle's glossary speaks of the "Sufi order with which Shah Inayat and Bullhe Shah were affiliated", the Qadiri (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 199, folio 174), while his introduction attaches the Shattari to the master alone, "the Shattari order with which Shah Inayat was affiliated" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 24, folio xxiii). Rafat writes that "Although he clearly expressed himself as being in the Qadiriya tradition to which his preceptor Shah Inayat belonged, Bulleh Shah cuts across the mystical politics of his times." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 39, folio 19), and that "The times of Shah Enayat and Bulleh Shah were therefore hard times for the Qadiriya order." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 241, folio 221). Khaled Ahmad names the Qadiriya only for the master and says of the disciple that "His allegiance to Shah Enayat was his only concession to form which also provided him a mooring in the Islamic tradition. In nothing else does he recognise discipline" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 24, folio not stated). Iqbal Qaiser told Khalid simply, "Bulleh Shah was a Qadri." (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated). Sharib gives the nisba Qadiri only to the pir (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 341–342, folios 337–338).

The caste question runs through every account. "The discrepancy between Bullhe Shah's own high status as a Sayyid and the lower rank of Shah Inayat as an Arain is a prominent theme of the poet's hagiography." (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 165, folio 140). Abbas puts it that "Whereas Bulle Shah was himself a Syed, he chose to become the disciple of Shah Inayat, who did not belong to his socioeconomic caste but is said to have been of arāĩ descent" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 108, folio not stated). In one of the poems Shackle translates, the poet's sisters and his brothers' wives reproach him: "stop clinging to the Arain's skirt. Why have you brought disgrace on the family of the Prophet and the descendants of Ali?" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 113, folio 88). In the same poem he answers:

*May anyone who calls us a Sayyid suffer the punishments of hell. / Swings are set up in paradise for anyone who calls us an Arain.* (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 113, folio 88)

Rafat's version of the scene has the women of the household tell him "it's a sin to condemn the prophet's kin. Those who acknowledge them, you tell, will get the one-way ticket to hell." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 93, folio 73), and he replies:

*Does sanctity deserve such scorn? / It's God's carelessness where one is born. … / My caste? I've none. I'm just a man.* (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 93, folio 73)

Elsewhere in Rafat's selection he asks "What ecstatic being is here who is so drenched in oneness for caste he has no care?" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 111, folio 91). Shackle, for his part, judges one kafi on the theme to be "a later hagiographic elaboration than 31 of the familiar theme of the disparity in outward status between the Sayyid poet and his revered master from the lower-ranking Arain caste. Its genuineness is anyway open to question on stylistic grounds" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 183, folio 158).

His times were violent. Shackle sets his life "at a time of great social upheaval in Panjab", when the authority of the declining Mughal empire was under challenge from armed Sikh rebellion and from local notables, and notes that these unsettled conditions are only occasionally reflected in the poetry, whose "main focus is on his spiritual guide" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 8, folio vii). One kafi speaks of the century itself, a Hijri century which Shackle's note glosses: "The twelfth century of the Hijri era corresponds to the eighteenth century C.E. (1100 A.H. = 1688 C.E.)." (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, pp. 165–166, folios 140–141):

*The twelfth century has come with gaping mouth. / The door of doom and torment has opened, and Panjab is in a bad state. / It has been struck by fear of the pit of hell.* (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 52, folio 27)

Khaled Ahmad dates his life by its events: "When the last great Mughal ruler Aurangzeb died in 1707 Bulleh Shah was 27 years old" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 26, folio not stated), and he "was fated to see a second invasion of the Punjab by Ahmad Shah Abdali in 1748 and then a third in 1752," (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 27, folio 7). Rafat writes that "In the years to come, when plunder became routine in the Punjab, Bulleh Shah had no choice of factions." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, pp. 42–43, folios 22–23), and, of him at sixty-six, that "he was to die twelve years later, a disenchanted man, witness to three more Afghan invasions of extreme severity under Ahmad Shah Abdali." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 43, folio 23). Schimmel groups him with Shah Abdul Latif in Sind and with Shah Waliullah, Mir Dard and Mazhar Janjanan in Delhi as "a witness of the epoch of great political disasters that occurred, with only brief interruptions, in the northwestern part of the Subcontinent after Aurangzeb's death in 1707", adding: "Like these mystics, he found peace in the inner world of love and surrender, singing his mystical songs in order to console himself and his friends in the times of external sufferings and afflictions." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 419, folio 388).

The year of his death is the most disputed fact of his life. Sharib gives his death in 1171 AH, with no month, day or age (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 343, folio 339); the tomb's own chronogram, as Shackle reports it, gives the same Hijri year; Lahori's text gives 1180 AH and his editor a seal of 1181 AH; and the English books range from 1752 to 1758. Every reading is set out under the sources' disagreements below.

## The Poetry

Bulleh Shah’s fame rests wholly on his *kafis* — short, sung Punjabi lyrics of astonishing directness and power. In them he mocks the empty learning of the mullah and the pandit, tramples the divisions of Hindu and Muslim, high and low, and insists that the seeker must lose the self entirely in the Beloved. His most famous kafi asks the question at the heart of all mysticism — who am I? — and answers by refusing every label of religion and identity:

بُلھیا! کی جاݨاں میں کوݨ
نہ میں مومن وِچ مسیتاں، نہ میں وِچ کُفر دِیاں ریتاں

*Bulleh! What do I know of who I am? / I am not the believer in the mosque, nor am I in the ways of unbelief.*

Rafat's selection prints the poem in full in Ramakrishna's translation, opening "Bullha, what do I know who I am?" and closing "Bullha, who is the true master?" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 243, folio 223), and says that "His famous kafi 'Ki janan mein koan' has attained the status of a proverb." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, pp. 36–37, folios 16–17). In Shackle's version the speaker is "neither an Arab nor from Lahore, nor an Indian from the city of Nagaur" and says "Nor do I live in Nadaun" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 112, folio 87). Khaled Ahmad holds that "There is no doubt that the earliest heterodox tradition he imbibed was that of the Persian mystic poet Rumi (1207-1273 A.D.). His most rebellious kafi, and the most popular, 'Ki janan mein kaun', is almost a literal translation of a ghazal from Diwan-i-Shams Tabriz." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 22, folio not stated). Rafat goes further: two of his kafis, 'Ke janan mein koan' and 'Neh wi doongi', are exact quotations from the work of Rumi and Baba Farid (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 37, folio 17). Shackle, of one famous kafi, writes that "It has often been observed this famous kāfī is modeled on Rumi's great Persian ghazal beginning cih tadbīr ai musalmānān kih man ḳhud-rā namīdānam", which he renders "What can I do, oh Muslims, for I do not know myself. I am not a Christian nor a Jew, not a Zoroastrian nor a Muslim." (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 167, folio 142).

The refusal of labels comes again and again:

*I am not a Hindu, nor a Muslim. I have forsaken pride and become unsullied. / I am not a Sunni, nor a Shia. I have adopted the path of peace toward all.* (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 58, folio 33)

"Bullha is not a Shia or a Sunni, neither a learned scholar nor a magician or philosopher." (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 46, folio 21). And again:

*I am not twice-born, nor am I thrice-born. / I do not recognize pure and impure.* (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 131, folio 106)

Elsewhere he turns on dry scholarship with the cry that has become proverbial, insisting that a single letter of love outweighs a library of knowledge:

عِلموں بس کریں او یار

*Enough of learning, my friend / Only one letter 'A' is all you need* (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 15, folio 15)

Naveed Alam quotes those lines in his translation of Madho Lal Hussein's verses, in the same run as Sultan Bahu, attributing them "(Bulleh Shah, 1680–1758)" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 15, folio 15), and Rafat's introduction lists 'Ilmon bas kareen O yar' among his kafis (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, pp. 36–37, folios 16–17). The same quarrel with the schoolroom runs through his self-portraits. Shackle's translation has him called a dunce:

*The mullah taught me the lesson, but I could not get beyond alif. / I suffered a beating with his slippers* (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 102, folio 77)

Schimmel gives the same complaint in her own English:

*The mollas have made me read lessons, / They have not gone beyond the alif!* (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 158, folio 140)

In *Pain and Grace* she renders it "The mollas have made me read lessons, they have not reached beyond the alif" and ties it to a legend that a saint "was illiterate and knew only the alif: that is said about the Turkish folk poet Yunus Emre, the Panjabi poet Bullhe Shah, and our Abdul Latif as well" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 208, folio 208). He calls himself a fakir: "Bullhe Shah is a fakir of the lord; he disturbs those who are awake" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 79, folio 54); "Bullhe Shah is a poor fakir; he has departed, proclaiming to the sound of the drum" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 141, folio 116). Khalid quotes the image of the mystic as a dog before God: "Dogs are awake at night, dogs are superior to you [believers]." (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated).

His verse fuses the imagery of Punjabi village life — the spinning-wheel, the beloved’s lane, the tale of Heer and Ranjha — with the boldest mystical claims, and it is this union of the homely and the sublime, the rebellious and the tender, that has kept it alive on the tongues of the Punjab for nearly three centuries. Schimmel describes his forms as traditional, the *siharfi* or 'Golden Alphabet', the *baramah* of the lovers' feelings through the twelve months, and others, and says that "he has made use of the whole mystical vocabulary and imagery which had been inherited from classical times", adding to their beauty by combining them "with the rural imagery of his homeland so that they could be understood by every villager and ploughman and sung by the large masses who would flock to the shrines of the saints" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 17, folio 13). "Bullhe Shah is, like all the folk poets, a defender of the unity of Being" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 17, folio 13). For Rafat the spinning-wheel is the poems' basic metaphor, the charkha "to which the career of a rural woman was permanently linked… whatever she spins would become a part of her dowry", and the monologue built on it is "unsurpassed in Indian poetry in its sustained anatomy of a physical object" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 46, folio 26). Shackle refers the spinning theme in Shah Abdul Latif's poetry back to him, ‘like Bullhe Shah’ (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 339). Schimmel notes that the romance of Heer and Ranjha "was used by Bullhe Shah, as well as by his younger contemporary Hashim Shah of Amritsar, for their mystical meditations." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 419, folio 388); Abbas, that "Bulle Shah, who was a contemporary of Waris Shah, also worked with the Hir-Ranjha myth in Panjabi" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 120, folio not stated); Khalid, that Shah Hussain's spiritual reading of Heer was "a theme that was subsequently picked up by Bulleh Shah and Waris Shah" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 16, ch. 10, folio not stated).

Much of the poetry is spoken in a woman's voice: "I, Bullha, am the slave girl of the lord" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 110, folio 85); "Ranjha is a yogi and I am his yogini" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 142, folio 117); ‘I'm just a sweepress.’ (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 187, folio 167). Its language shifts with its theme: "Like other kāfīs on Hindu themes, this poem is written in Hindi, the characteristic language of the yogis." (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 155, folio 130), and one kafi "is actually a ghazal in the hazaj meter of four feet", in "fairly standard Urdu" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 166, folio 141). Schimmel adds that "In addition to being the leading poet of Panjabi Sufism, he also composed a number of Persian prose treatises, comparable to those of Mir Dard." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 419, folio 388).

The kafi, in Shackle's description, "is designed for singing in qawwali and also lends itself to solo singing by amateur devotees and professional performers", and "The final verse of the kāfī is regularly marked by the poet's signature." (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 13, folio xii; p. 14, folio xiii). Rafat's introduction writes that "In this quality of voice rather than verse he is very close to the vacana tradition of Kannada Bhakti poets from 1100 A.D. to 1200 A.D." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 31, folio 11). A note in his selection calls him the most frequently quoted poet in Punjabi conversation, "his lines are essentially non-poetic in terms of rhythm", the vocative 'Bullha' making "for precise assimilation in common speech" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, pp. 239–240, folios 219–220). The same note warns: "Very often people pretend to quote Bulleh Shah with a vocative of his name but the lines are their own, not Bulleh Shah's." The line 'Bullhya, hor wi neenva ho', for one, "is purely popular fabrication and is totally non-poetic" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 240, folio 220). Shackle's Risalo calls the kafi the primary genre of Sufi poetry in Panjabi (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 14), and pairs him with Sachal Sarmast as the poets who "so frequently invoked" the phrase *anā 'l-ḥaqq*, which Shah Latif preferred to approach obliquely (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 326). Shackle, glossing a pun between *cullhā*, 'hearth', and the name Bullha, uses such wordplay as a mark of his hand (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 179, folio 154). Rafat does not spare him: "Much is made of Bulleh Shah's mysticism. As in Rumi, it tends to limit him as a poet and causes him to repeat himself." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 43, folio 23).

Which poems are his best known, the books answer differently. Khaled Ahmad names 'Teray Ishq nachaya', "perhaps the most popular of his Kafis today" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 24, folio not stated). Shackle says of one kafi that "The natural appeal of this kāfī across religious and national boundaries has helped it become probably the best known of all Bullhe Shah's lyrics." (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 183, folio 158), of two more that each is "one of Bullhe Shah's most famous compositions" and "one of Bullhe Shah's most famous kāfīs" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 185, folio 160; p. 193, folio 168), and of another that it "is best known by this second verse of the refrain ending with the syllables thaīā thaīā, which are used by teachers of dancing to mark the rhythm of the steps." (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 182, folio 157).

How much of this is his is itself uncertain. Writing in 1875, Lahori credits him with thousands of Punjabi *abyat* and *kafis* on themes of *tauhid*, which qawwals still sing in the assemblies of the faqirs (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 82, folio 70 [marker: 80]). Rafat counts "Bulleh Shah's 156 extant and authenticated kafis" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 43, folio 23), and describes Faqir Mohammad's 1960 edition as holding 156 kafis besides *dohras*, *athvaras*, *siharfis* and *baramahas* (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 48, folio 28). Shackle writes that "The present volume includes 157 of these lyrics", the uncertain transmission making it hard to be sure of the number of authentic items (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 13, folio xii); that "Absolute authenticity in the precise wording of any poem generally agreed to be by Bullhe Shah is hardly to be expected", though "the corpus that has come down to us provides ample evidence of a powerfully coherent poetical and mystical imagination" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 14, folio xiii); and that his lyrics, like those of the other Sufi poets of the Panjab, were never kept by an organized faith community and were for the most part recorded "and assembled in printed collections only in the late nineteenth century" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, pp. 9–10, folios viii–ix). He lists Panjabi editions from Ruhtaki's *Qānūn-e ‘ishq* (Lahore, 1889) and Qasuri's *Kāfīhā-e Hazrat Bullhe Shāh* (Qasur, 1896) to Faqir's *Kulliyāt-e Bullhe Shāh* (Lahore, 1960) and later (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 202, folio 177); Lahori's editor used the *Kulliyat* of Faqir Muhammad Faqir, listed undated and in Punjabi (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 304, folio 292), and Schimmel the *Diwan* "ed. Faqir M. Faqir, Lahore 1960." (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 295, folio 295). Schimmel also cites an undated Lahore chapbook commentary on his kafis, *Qānūn-e ʿishq* (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 333, folio 315), and records attempts to "transplant some Bektashi verses into Panjabi and declare them to be compositions of Bullhe Shah" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 169, folio 151). Shackle argues that hagiography itself has added to the corpus: one poem "has not been included in any of the subsequent standard editions of Bullhe Shah's poetry, unlike the similarly sourced 108, which fulfills a more satisfying hagiographic need." (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 176, folio 151).

## The Shrine

Bulleh Shah’s mazar at Kasur, once (by tradition) a grave the orthodox had shunned, is today a revered shrine visited by pilgrims and lovers of his poetry alike, and the town of Kasur is known above all as the place where he lies. The shrine keeps the familiar devotional life of a Punjabi darbar — the recitation of the Fatiha, qawwali, the offering of vows — and it draws its greatest crowds at the annual urs, when his kafis are sung through the night. In an age that has rediscovered him, his tomb has become a place of pilgrimage not only for the pious but for musicians, students, and admirers of Punjabi letters.

Shackle gives the tomb's one inscription: "It bears a Persian chronogram that gives the year of his death as A.H. 1171 according to the Muslim calendar, equivalent to 1757 or 1758 C.E." (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, pp. 8–9, folios vii–viii). The text of the chronogram is not given. His glossary calls Kasur only the poet's hometown, "Kasur (Qasūr) hometown of Bullhe Shah." (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 199, folio 174), and for the building as it now stands he points to published photographs "showing the radically modernized appearance of Bullhe Shah's shrine in Kasur today." (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 23, folio xxii).

Khaled Ahmad opens his introduction to Rafat's selection at the tomb, in the afternoon heat. "The afternoon sun beat down on the concrete courtyard of the mausoleum. It exacted penance from the barefoot votaries" for a distant injustice "to the poet whose dead body had lain out in the sun in 1758 as the mullahs disputed his right to be buried in a communal grave-yard. Today the same cemetery, where orthodoxy had refused him burial-ground, is mostly in ruins, encroached upon by a fast-growing town whose hard-pressed denizens defecate on its collapsed graves." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 21, folio not stated). He tells the refusal as fact and gives no source for it. "The tomb of Bulleh Shah in Qasur and the area around it is today the only place free of collective refuse and the privileged of the city pay handsomely to be buried in the proximity of the man they had once rejected." Their graves carry "stone canopies adorned with mosaic paintings of low taste, of the sort seen on the back of trucks", while the poet lies serenely inside the dilapidated building of his tomb, its broken stone filigree work "proclaiming his indifference to the well-heeled buried around him." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 21, folio not stated). Inside, "the Auqaf Department has allowed mediocre Urdu verse composed by the poetasters of Qasur to festoon the walls in colourful banners. A local grandee has contributed a framed couplet of execrable taste in which his name appears more prominently than the verse he has concocted. Bulleh Shah's own poetry is conspicuously absent in this ambience of bourgeois profanity." He closes on the grave itself: "Lying under layers of tinsel and flower, the poet communes with the cats that sleep leaning against his grave on the cool floor." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 22, folio not stated).

Abbas, who did fieldwork at the shrine, reached it by car "through extremely narrow streets, encountering large horse-driven carriages (ṭangās)", and found that "the shrine is located in the heart of the city" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 105, folio not stated). "Like most shrines, Bulle Shah's tomb is in the center of a brick courtyard covered with a concrete canopy. The crypt is covered with green and red sheets decorated with gold tinsel. The devotees bring these as offerings". "There are several small tombs surrounding the main tomb, all within the same compound. Here, the female and male relatives of the Sufi poets are buried. Devotees light candles at these tombs and offer fātehā (prayer)" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 106, folio not stated). The street outside "was colorfully decorated with shops that sell all kinds of artifacts such as marigolds, rose petals, candles, and small earthenware lamps called divās, which are filled with mustard oil and have a cotton wick in them. They are lit at the shrine" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 105–106, folios not stated); "The shops displayed incense sticks, prayer beads, and silver-plated bangles with Quranic verses inscribed on them that devotees take to the shrine, or home as souvenirs. The shops also sell sweets that the devotees buy as tabarruk, nazar, or niāz (food for distribution in the shrine)" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 106, folio not stated).

None of the books consulted names the builder of the tomb or the date of its building, a *sajjada nashin* or other custodian beyond the Auqaf Department and the shrine's qawwals, or the date of the *ʿurs*.

## Devotion and Practice

Abbas lists Bulleh Shah's shrine first among the five she names where *qawwali* is sung nightly (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 42, folio not stated), and she counts it among those that "are said to be of the jamālī or aesthetic mystics. The latter Sufis were poets who wrote narratives that could be sung to music. In fact, some were musicians themselves" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 96, folio not stated). She came "two days before the start of the fasting month of Ramazan", setting out "from Lahore on a beautiful spring afternoon", and "arrived at the shrine around five o'clock in the evening" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 105, folio not stated). The singers were "the qawwāls of the shrine, Muhammad Bakhsh and his paternal uncle Karam Bakhsh", with a young man said to be a nephew and two other musicians, playing "a harmonium (an accordion), a ḍholak (a double-sided drum shaped like a barrel), and a pair of tambourines" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 105, folio not stated). Their timetable, in their own words: "We sing here twice a day. We sing in the morning from eight to ten, and then throughout the evening"; on her visit they "sang the entire evening, until after the evening prayers" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 112, folio not stated; p. 111, folio not stated).

The qawwals claim a long descent at the shrine. "They said they had inherited them orally from their ancestors who had been qawwāls at the shrine for the last three centuries" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 106, folio not stated). Muhammad Bakhsh told her: "The saint will not let us leave this shrine, and even if we do go away to other shrines to sing for the urs, we always return here." and "We came with Bulle Shah's family from Uch Sharif, and we have learned his poetry from our ancestors, who have always sung here" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 111–112, folios not stated). They "claim to do the same, that is, sing his poetry to his melodies", and "They assert that they are the guardians of Bulle Shah's oral poetry, some of which has not yet found its way into the written texts." (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 96, folio not stated). Abbas gives all of this as the qawwals' claim. The women of their family do not sing there: "Our women have never sung at the shrine. Perhaps, almost a century ago, they would go to the Syed households on special invitation. They would only sing among women. They sang Baba Bulle Shah's poetry for the Syed women" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 112, folio not stated). She found that "the Bulle Shah qawwāls sing the Sufi poet's Panjabi poetry in the Panjabi ang (style), where they create the female myths in their lyrics" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 151, folio not stated).

At her request they sang six compositions on the female voice, all in Panjabi, five of them female narratives that "The musicians claimed" were Bulle Shah's; "Only one qawwālī was about Muhammad the Prophet; the other five were narratives about different stages of Bulle Shah's spiritual growth as a mystic and his relationship with his spiritual mentor, Shah Inayat" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 106, folio not stated; p. 107, folio not stated). "All together, including the musicians, there were fifty women and men in the shrine" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 106, folio not stated). "Women devotees who until then were performing their rituals of prayer or lighting candles and incense sticks at the poet's shrine or elsewhere joined the assembly to listen to the qawwāls." Then "Male devotees in the shrine made a small circle in front of the musicians and started to dance in rhythm with the music. Later, some hijṛās (eunuchs) also joined the group"; "The hijṛas at Bulle Shah had ghungrū tied to their ankles." Abbas writes: "Bulle Shah's was the only shrine where I encountered the hijṛās. Most probably they are traditionally linked to the shrine because of Bulle Shah's own life history of having lived among the dancing girls for several years" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 106, folio not stated), a link she offers as her own inference. She presents the evening as an instance of how, "Outside the urs context, and particularly in shrines not associated with the Chishtiyya, the qawwālī performance may not necessarily follow a strict ritualistic pattern", for "Shrine performances and qawwālī routines may differ from shrine to shrine" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 84, folio not stated).

Khaled Ahmad heard the qawwals too: "The qawwals constantly sing his rebuking lines: 'Tamba chook-chook mandi javain'", while, "Their feet burning, the votaries run toward the shade of the graves of the city's rich", whose "gaudy presence lends an edge to the lines shouted by a hoarse group of qawwals" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 21, folio not stated). He records no *ʿurs* or visitor numbers.

The singing is old. In 1875 Lahori wrote that his Punjabi verse was "to this day sung by qawwals in the assemblies of the faqirs" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 82, folio 70 [marker: 80]). Kanhaiya Lal's history of Lahore, in a sentence damaged in the printed text, records the same of the city: in the Punjabi language the *kafis* of Bulleh Shah and the *barah-mah* and *abyat* are sung in *kalawant* assemblies, the readings of the poet's name and of *kalawant* uncertain in the printed text (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 144, folio 147). The clause around it that would link him with Kasur and with another saint is broken and cannot be read. Lahori describes the saint's own practice: the practice of *sama* and *wajd* before his eyes day and night, and marvels often issuing from him involuntarily (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 81, folio 69 [marker: 79]). Sharib writes of his music, *sama* and poetry: he had a great love of *sama*, knew the art of music well and was a poet too; and he adds that he loved his pir with a rapturous love (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 343, folio 339). Khaled Ahmad writes that "Aware of the Qadiriya custom of Sama and devotional dance, Bulleh Shah took to the sarangi, the stringed instrument associated with the wandering dervish, and began to sing and dance." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 24, folio not stated). Khalid notes that "Bulleh Shah used the word 'love' to describe his emotion towards his teacher" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 42, folio 24).

His songs travel far beyond Kasur. Michel Boivin writes that "Sometimes, Sufi poetry authored by non-Sindhis also played a liturgical role during the performances. One can quote Bulhe Shah or Ghulam Farid" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 91, folio 78); at Dadi Dhan's *versi* in 2012, "Although the majority of the songs were in Sindhi, there was also a composition of Bullhe Shah and another one of Mirabai" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 149, folio 136); he observes of Panjwani's 1959 anthology that "Some Sufis are not Sindhi, such as the famous Punjabi poet, Bulhe Shah, but they are very few" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 158, folio 145); and in a Siraiki anthology "Non-Sindhi poets are also quoted, including Bullhe Shah, Shah Bahu and Ghulam Farid" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 171, folio 158). Khalid records that Ghulam Ali's *dandiya* group has played at "Bulleh Shah's, Data Darbar's and Baba Fareed Shakar Ganj's, all of which are famous Muslim shrines" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 63, folio not stated). Abida Parvin told Abbas, "I sing Shah Hussain, Bulle Shah, Mian Muhammad Bakhsh, Hazrat Khawaja Ghulam Farid, Sacchal Sarmast, Baba Farid, and Guru Nanak" and "and there were mystics like Baba Bulle Shah—the Sufis never come out of this canvas" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 54, folio not stated); at an Islamabad concert she blended his Panjabi into a text of Sultan Bahu, closing on "Ve mī- Bulle-ā rab unh nū mildā ate dilī- sacī- achī- nū", which Abbas gives as "O gentle Bulle, God is found only by those who are true and noble of heart" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 102, folio not stated). "Pop groups such as Ali Hyder and ensembles who sing Bulle Shah's poetry with electronic instruments sing substantial Sufi poetry" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 80, folio not stated); young musicians "use the resources of Sufi poetry such as that of Bulle Shah for their lyrics" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 174, folio not stated); and "Khusrau's and Bulle Shah's mystical poetry is sung at weddings in Pakistan" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 209, folio not stated). Shackle notes his present circulation through well-known Sikh and Muslim singers "or through the popular selections issued by Indian and Pakistani publishers" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 9, folio viii).

## Legends and Miracles

Of the refusal of burial, the current account speaks as tradition and Khaled Ahmad as an event of 1758 (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 21, folio not stated). Sharib's chapter gathers most of the wonders. Besides the dream of the flying throne and the milk that lit his breast, which he had no sooner drunk than he was carried out of himself (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 341, folio 337), he tells, under the heading "Help from the Unseen", of a despairing young man who sat on the bank of the Ravi resolved to drown himself. A masked horseman arrived, asked him to hold his horse and guard his things while he bathed, handed over four things, a purse of five hundred rupees, a pair of gold bangles, his clothes and the horse, and went into the river and never came out. When the young man went to the city, people cried out that the clothes and the horse were Shah Inayat Allah's (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 342, folio 338). The four things answer to the four items of the price set on his initiation; Sharib draws no conclusion, and says only that Shah Inayat Allah smiled on seeing him (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 342, folio 338).

Under the heading "Miracle" Sharib tells how a field's owner found the boy asleep with a cobra shading him with its hood. The owner reported the boy dead; the father came with some men, the snake vanished and the boy sat up, and the field proved indeed greener than before (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 343, folio 339). Then Jiwan Khan made over the whole field to the saint's father as an offering (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 343, folio 339).

Several of the books tell of a break with his master and a return, and no two tell it alike. In Sharib, his pir grew displeased over some matter and stripped him of his *wilayat*; he went to Gwalior, to the mazar of Shaikh Muhammad Ghaus Gwaliyari, where he received spiritual graces and gained mastery in the art of music, and after some days his pir was pleased with him again and restored his *wilayat* to him (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 343, folio 339). Shackle tells "the most popular tale of Bullhe Shah, of how he once fell out of favor with his guide, allegedly for being too outspoken in his criticisms of formal Islam, and was dismissed from his company. Since Bullhe Shah knew of Shah Inayat's fondness for music and dancing, he is then said to have gone away and taken lessons with a dancing girl, then reappeared in Lahore one day, himself dressed as a dancing girl. There he danced and sang in front of Shah Inayat, who was won over by his performance and readmitted him to his favor" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, pp. 18–19, folios xvii–xviii). He reads one rupture differently, as "a separation of Bullhe Shah from Shah Inayat due to the troubled conditions then prevailing in Panjab." (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 165, folio 140), and the poems lament it:

*With the departure of Inayat Qadiri, / my story has again become difficult* (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 104, folio 79)

And the poems sing the return through dance:

*Bullha, let us go and sit at the gate of Shah Inayat, who made me dress in green and red. / When I started dancing, I found my way to him* (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 111, folio 86)

"Bullha, the lord has made me dance, and the sound is very loud" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 115, folio 90); "I have stripped off my blanket and dance, making him hurl astonished looks at me." (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 52, folio 27); "Bullha, it is indeed a strange thing for a woman to start dancing with her veil on." (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 51, folio 26). The reproach is quoted too: "Satan makes her dance in the courtyard. Talk to her and make her stop." (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 116, folio 91). Shackle notes that "The image of Bullhe Shah dancing is a popular one in modern iconography" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 24, folio xxiii). No miracle of any kind is attributed to him in Shackle's volume.

The shrine's qawwals sing the same story. "The first narrative that the qawwāls sang was from Bulle's legendary reconciliation with his mentor, Shah Inayat": "Bulle Shah worked hard for several years to win back his murshid", and "he went and lived among musicians for almost seven years and became their apprentice. After learning the musical arts, he returned to Shah Inayat's company and won him back by playing music and dancing before him." In the song "Bulle Shah portrays himself as a dancing girl who narrates how she won back her beloved through dance. Hence, it is the disciple who is represented as the dancing girl here" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 108, folio not stated; p. 109, folio not stated). Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan told Abbas that he "went away to spend twelve years among the dancing girls" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 99, folio not stated). Khalid writes that he "worked as the servant of a dancing girl for twelve years", which he reads as "an act clearly meant to criticize the divisions of caste in society": "Bulleh Shah was a Syed, and the dancing girls, like the eunuchs, occupy the lowest rungs of society" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated). Abbas adds that "There are several legends in the oral lore of the qawwāls at the shrine about Bulle Shah defying the mores of his family and community to continue his discipleship of Shah Inayat despite the fact that he was a Syed himself." (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 108, folio not stated).

Khaled Ahmad's version runs otherwise again. "Hagiographers tell of a dramatic encounter between the two in Lahore, one outdoing the other in a display of miraculous powers." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 22, folio not stated); he does not say what the powers were or who prevailed. "It is said that Shah Enayat turned him out of his house after this." At the reunion Shah Enayat, approaching a bedraggled Bulleh Shah, asked whether he was not Bullah, and was told he was not Bullah but Bhulla, as the printed text is read; ‘Bhulla meant lost.’ (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 24, folio not stated). He also warns that "Tazkiras are significantly revisionist on this point. They are condemnatory of Bulleh Shah's revolt against orthodox creed through Shah Enayat's disapproval of his disciple's deviationist poetry." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 23, folio not stated).

Iqbal Qaiser told Khalid a story of flight. After his father's death "the chaudris of the village asked Bulleh Shah to take his place but he refused"; threatened with a beating, "he fled from the village and headed towards Kasur", and took refuge in a gurdwara in the village of Daftu, saying "I am in the house of God. Let's see who can take me from here." The Sikhs refused to put him out, and "Eventually after protracted discussions, Bulleh Shah was allowed to proceed to Kasur"; Iqbal Qaiser calls the gurdwara "of immense importance" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).

One tradition has him put to death. Schimmel quotes the litany of the Sindhi poet Sachal Sarmast:

*Thou hast made to be slaughtered Bullhe Shah, Jacfar to / be drowned in the sea,* (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 426, folio 395)

and answers it in a footnote on the same page: "The traditional sources say nothing about the martyrdom of Bullhe Shah." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 426, folio 395).

## Setting

The town around the shrine has its own history in the books, which speak of Kasur and not of the tomb. Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi places "Qasur, thirty miles south-east of Lahore" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 28, folio 14), and elsewhere tells that Baba Farid's father "moved to Qasur, south-east of Lahore" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 157, folio 139); Abbas writes that "Kasur is seventy miles from Lahore" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 86, folio not stated), and Khalid calls it "the twin city of Lahore" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 163, folio 145). Nur Ahmad Chishti's *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* records that one of Ram Chand's sons, his name illegible in the printed text, founded Qasur (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 661), and in another place that Kushu founded Kashur, the town now known as Qasur, before taking the *gaddi* at Ajodhya (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 795); Khalid notes that "Lahore and Kasur are said to be named after Luv and Kush" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 71, folio not stated). Chishti also records that Sikhs gathered at Kasur, many were killed, and the town was badly damaged (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 718). Rafat writes that the Sikh bands "under Banda Bairagi (1670-1716) were to lay waste the whole of the province excluding Lahore and Qasur" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, pp. 42–43, folios 22–23).

For Rafat, "If Takht Hazara, Multan and Prem Nagar are idylls, the city of Qasur where he lived is a kind of inferno." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 40, folio 20); "Bulleh Shah lived and sang in the inferno of Qasur" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 47, folio 27), and two *dohras* "make direct reference to the city after the rise of the Pathans, the chaos of moral and political values in it and the consequent suffering it visited upon the common man." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 40, folio 20). In the verse it is the town of an absent beloved:

*You are gone, friend, far away beyond my reach. / Many a day Kasur has missed your smile.* (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 149, folio 129)

On the same page the whole province is lost:

*Now hell has opened its bogs. / Punjab has gone to the dogs. / This hell is the nethermost hell.* (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 149, folio 129)

Rafat's translator's note reads the line against its time, when "the Punjab was being persistently rent by petty chiefs and marauders": "The full bitterness of Bulleh Shah's 'Punjab has gone to the dogs' will only come through against this backdrop" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 16, folio xvi). Waris Shah, whom the *tazkiras*, as Khaled Ahmad reports them, make his fellow pupil, turns to the same town in the epilogue to his *Hir*, where he dates the poem "in the year 1180 Hijri":

*Of all the wretched Punjab I am most concerned for Kasur.* (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 56, folio 55)

Lahori records at Kasur the graves of another family altogether, a Qasuri lineage of Naqshbandi-Mujaddidi *ulama* from Sindh who died in 1202, 1270 and 1295 AH; they are a different burial ground from Bulleh Shah's (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 151–152, folios 139–140).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- The year of his death. The current account gives c. 1757 (with a birth around 1680). Lahori's author gives his death in 1180 AH; his editor, following Dr Maulvi Muhammad Shafi's *Auliya-e Kasur*, rejects it because Shafi saw a manuscript of *awrad* at Ferozepur bearing the seal of Bulha Shah Qadiri dated 1181 AH, and writes that it appears from this that Baba Sahib was alive up to 1181 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 82, folio 70 [marker: 80]; p. 81, folio 69 [marker: 79]). Sharib gives 1171 AH (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 343, folio 339). Shackle gives ‘d. 1758’ and reports the tomb's Persian chronogram as A.H. 1171, "equivalent to 1757 or 1758 C.E. (the later year is somewhat more probable)", with the Library of Congress line ‘1680?-1758?’ (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 8, folio vii; pp. 8–9, folios vii–viii; p. 5, folio not stated); his Risalo also has "his Panjabi contemporary Bullhe Shah (d. 1758)" (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 12). Khaled Ahmad heads his section "Bulleh Shah 1680-1758" and writes "As Bulleh Shah lay dying in 1758 at the age of 78", and Rafat "Bulleh Shah died in 1758." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 21, folio not stated; p. 28, folio not stated; p. 48, folio 28). Alam's quotation gives "(Bulleh Shah, 1680–1758)" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 15, folio 15). Schimmel gives "Bullhe Shah's personality (d. 1752)" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 17, folio 13) and "Bullhe Shah (1680-1752)", with 1752 again in her index (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 419, folio 388; p. 513, folio 482), where the same year stands against Shah Abdul Latif two pages earlier, so that this reading needs checking against the printed page; the index of *As Through a Veil* gives "Bullhe Shah (d. 1754)" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 351, folio 333). Abbas writes that "Bulle Shah is said to have lived approximately from" one year to another in Kasur, but both years are illegible in the copy consulted (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 107, folio not stated).
- His birth. The current account and Khaled Ahmad give 1680, Khaled Ahmad at Uch Gilanian (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 22, folio not stated), as does Abbas for the place (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 107–108, folios not stated); Alam and Schimmel give 1680 (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 15, folio 15; Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 419, folio 388). Shackle holds that neither the date nor the place of birth is known for certain, and prints the year only as ‘1680?’ (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 8, folio vii; p. 5, folio not stated). Rafat's own half of the introduction gives an age at a given year, not a birth year (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 43, folio 23). The shrine's qawwals say the family came ‘from Uch Sharif’ (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 111–112, folios not stated).
- His name. The current account calls him Sayyid Abdullah Shah Qadiri. Sharib makes Abdullah Shah his name and Bulleh Shah his *kunya* and *takhallus* (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 340, folio 336; p. 343, folio 339). Shackle explains ‘Bullhe Shah’ as "the honorific form proper to a Sayyid descendant of the prophet Muhammad" and ‘Bullha’ as "the familiar form of his given name, Abdullah" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 8, folio vii). Rafat derives the name by declension: "'Bulleh Shah' is derived from Abdullah Shah which is non-declinable; from Bulleh it becomes Bullah" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 240, folio 220). Lahori gives no personal name at all; his editor holds that the present pronunciation is Bulhe or Bulha and that his seal, reading Bulha Shah Qadiri 1181 AH, also supports the present pronunciation Bulha (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 81, folio 69 [marker: 79]). The books spell him ‘Bullhe Shah’ (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 8, folio vii; Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 17, folio 13), ‘Bulle Shah’ (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 107, folio not stated) and ‘Bulhe Shah’ (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 91, folio 78).
- How he came to Kasur. The current account has the family settle in the Kasur region; Khaled Ahmad and Abbas have the family move there when he was six (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 22, folio not stated; Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 108, folio not stated). Sharib has the father move from Uch to Malkwal to a third place, and the son come to Kasur to study, settle by the Chenab after his initiation, and only then take up residence at Kasur (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 340, folio 336; pp. 342–343, folios 338–339). Iqbal Qaiser's story has him flee to Kasur as a grown man after his father's death at Pandoke (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).
- His teacher at Kasur. Sharib and Khaled Ahmad have him study with Hafiz Ghulam Murtaza (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 340, folio 336; Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 22, folio not stated); Shackle writes that "Rather than study with this great scholar", he gave his allegiance to Shah Inayat (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 155, folio 130).
- The road to Shah Inayat. Sharib's guide is Sayyid Abd al-Hakim, an ancestor of the fifth generation seen on a flying throne and met over a bowl of milk, after which the son is initiated at Lahore (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 340, folio 336; p. 341, folio 337; p. 342, folio 338). Lahori's is a living Shaikh Abd al-Hakim Qadiri at Talamba [OCR?], who declines him and sends him to Qasur to find his share with Shah Inayat (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 219).
- His order. The current account places Shah Inayat in the Qadiri-Shattari line. Lahori gives Bulleh Shah himself the nisba Qadiri Shattari (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 16, folio 4); Shackle's glossary makes both men Qadiri, while his introduction gives the Shattari to Shah Inayat only (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 199, folio 174; p. 24, folio xxiii); Rafat places him in the Qadiriya, Khaled Ahmad names that order only for the master (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 39, folio 19; p. 22, folio not stated); Sharib gives the nisba to the pir alone (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 341–342, folios 337–338).
- Shah Inayat's caste. The current account makes him by trade an *arain*, a gardener-cultivator; Abbas writes that he "is said to have been of arāĩ descent" and defines the *arain* in her text as "gardeners or small cultivators who worked on their own land" but in her glossary as "A caste of landowners in the Panjab" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 108, folio not stated; p. 178, folio not stated).
- The break with his master. Sharib: the pir took away his *wilayat* over some matter, he went to Gwalior and the mazar of Muhammad Ghaus, and was restored after some days (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 343, folio 339). Shackle: dismissed, allegedly for outspoken criticism of formal Islam, he is said to have taken lessons with a dancing girl and returned in a dancing girl's dress in Lahore; or, in a note on another poem, separated by the troubles of the Panjab (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, pp. 18–19, folios xvii–xviii; p. 165, folio 140). The shrine's qawwals: almost seven years among musicians (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 109, folio not stated); Abbas herself: several years among the dancing girls (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 106, folio not stated); Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan: twelve years among the dancing girls (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 99, folio not stated); Khalid: twelve years as a dancing girl's servant (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated). Khaled Ahmad: expelled after a contest of miracles in Lahore, with the *tazkiras* blaming Shah Enayat's disapproval of his poetry (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 22, folio not stated; p. 24, folio not stated; p. 23, folio not stated).
- The burial. The current account gives the refusal of burial as tradition; Khaled Ahmad tells it, without a source, as an event of 1758 (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 21, folio not stated).
- A martyr's death. Sachal Sarmast's litany has Bulleh Shah slaughtered; Schimmel answers that "The traditional sources say nothing about the martyrdom of Bullhe Shah." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 426, folio 395).
- Where Kasur lies. The current account puts it south of Lahore; Rizvi, thirty miles south-east (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 28, folio 14); Abbas, seventy miles from Lahore (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 86, folio not stated; p. 105, folio not stated). Schimmel says only that he lived near Lahore (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 419, folio 388).
- The tomb's fabric. Khaled Ahmad: a concrete courtyard and a dilapidated building with broken stone filigree (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 21, folio not stated). Abbas: a tomb in a brick courtyard covered with a concrete canopy (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 106, folio not stated). Shackle: photographs showing a radically modernized shrine (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 23, folio xxii).
- His politics. The current account calls him a voice of the ordinary human heart against every orthodoxy and every tyranny. Shackle holds that his social critique "represents only a very small, if vividly expressed, part of his poetry" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, pp. 15–16, folios xiv–xv); Khaled Ahmad that "Bulleh Shah never directly criticised the rulers and whatever reference to the contemporary situation occurs in his poetry is random and oblique", though "he appears to be deeply bruised by the upheavals taking place in the Punjab" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 26, folio not stated); Rafat that he "cannot be designated a conscientious objector par excellence against the political despotism of his times simply because he treated the subject in passing" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 40, folio 20).
- Rumi in his verse. Shackle finds in one poem "a unique reference in Bullhe Shah's poetry to the great Sufi teacher and poet Jalal ud Din Rumi" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 177, folio 152); Rafat writes that "Bulleh Shah does not mention either Rumi or Baba Farid, classics in whose technical and intellectual traditions he was steeped." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 37, folio 17).
- The size of the corpus. Lahori: thousands of *abyat* and *kafis* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 82, folio 70 [marker: 80]); Rafat: 156 authenticated *kafis* (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 43, folio 23); Shackle: 157 lyrics in his volume, the number of authentic items uncertain (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 13, folio xii).

## Legacy

Bulleh Shah is, with Shah Hussain, Sultan Bahu, and Waris Shah, one of the pillars of classical Punjabi poetry, and perhaps the most beloved of them all. His insistence that love is higher than law, that the divisions of religion and caste are veils over a single truth, and that the self must be annihilated in God, has made him a voice of tolerance and of the ordinary human heart against every orthodoxy and every tyranny. His kafis are sung today from village shrines to concert stages, and his shrine at Kasur remains a fixed point of Punjabi devotion — the resting place of the rebel-saint who taught the Punjab to sing its way to God.

The books rank him as high. Schimmel writes that "Bullhe Shah (1680-1752) is considered the greatest of the Panjabi mystical poets" and that "His poetry was so highly esteemed that he was surnamed" ‘the Rumi of Punjab’ (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 419, folio 388); in *Pain and Grace* his personality "looms large in the Panjabi poetry of the 18th century, so much so that he has even been called the 'Rumi of Panjab.'" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 17, folio 13), and Panjabi mystical folk poetry "reached its apex with Bullhe Shah during exactly the same period" as Shah Abdul Latif (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 154, folio 154). In *As Through a Veil* she pairs him with the Sindhi Sachal Sarmast: "with their highly ecstatic songs", the two "are the most typical exponents" of the tendency she is tracing (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, pp. 176–177, folios 158–159). Shackle calls him "rightly regarded as the greatest master of the mystical Sufi lyric in Panjabi" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 8, folio vii), and, in his Risalo, writes that the use of the vernacular by Shah Latif and Bullhe Shah "has certainly helped to ensure their continuing popularity across religious boundaries and modern national frontiers today", setting Shah Latif's harder-to-pin-down manner against "the more familiar outspoken style" of his Panjabi contemporary (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 12; p. 23). Khalid names him with Baba Farid and Waris Shah among those "who spread the message of peace and brought several religious communities together instead of shedding blood" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 14, ch. 8, folio not stated), and calls him "one of the most celebrated Sufis from Punjab" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated); the relation of "Bulleh Shah and Shah Inayat" is his figure for the bond of disciple and master (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 42, folio 24). He also counts him among the Punjabi poets whom "the educated elite of Punjab ends up disowning, in fact, ridiculing" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 128, folio 110).

His reputation has been made and remade. Shackle lists the twentieth century's readings of him, "an exponent of universal spiritual truths owing as much to Vedanta as to Sufism", an authentic spokesman of the Panjabi folk tradition, a social revolutionary, "or simply a Romantic poet avant la lettre" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 12, folio xi), and offers his volume as a corrective: "The present volume may help correct the biases that have too often continued to distort" understanding of him (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, pp. 12–13, folios xi–xii). He judges the studies of him in English to be ones "none of which is at all comprehensive or very critical" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 24, folio xxiii). Rafat's introduction complains that "The celebrated East Punjab journal Punjab: Past and Present has not featured an article on Bulleh Shah over a period of 20 years." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 51, folio 31), and that the Punjab still awaits a scholarly "assessment of its greatest poet, a tryst that may never take place given the regional antecedents in the realm of letters" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 49, folio 29). Lahori's editor lists, among the sources for his notice, a *Sawanih-e Baba Bulhe Shah* by Ahmad Husain Ahmad Qureshi then in press with the Department of Auqaf, Lahore, Dr Muhammad Shafi's *Auliya-e Kasur*, and two works of his own in preparation, a *Tarikh-e Kasur* and a *Hayat-e Shah Inayat Qadri* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 82, folio 70 [marker: 80]).
