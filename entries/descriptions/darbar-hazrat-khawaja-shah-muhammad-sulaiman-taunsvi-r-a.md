## Overview

At Taunsa Sharif, in the Dera Ghazi Khan region of southern Punjab, stands the shrine of Hazrat Khwaja Muhammad Sulaiman Taunsvi (c. 1770–1850), the great Chishti saint honoured as *Pir Pathan* and remembered as a chief reviver of the Chishti order in the Punjab. His khanqah at Taunsa became one of the most important centres of Chishti learning and devotion in the nineteenth century, sending out disciples who spread the order across the region, and his darbar remains a major place of pilgrimage in the south-west of the province.

The books that speak of him agree that his grave is at Taunsa. Zahur ul-Hasan Sharib writes that "آپ کا مزار پر انوار تونسہ میں فیوض و برکات کا سرچشمہ ہے", that his luminous mazar at Taunsa is a fountainhead of effusions and blessings (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 365, folio 361). Carl W. Ernst and Bruce B. Lawrence, who treat the shrine as an institution at some length, write that "the shrine of Sulayman at Taunsa on the upper Indus is remote, and difficult of access" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 99, folio 86). Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori, writing in 1875, gives him a full entry in his *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, and says that it was by his pir's command that he went to Taunsa at all (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 123, folio 111). On his dates, the name of his order, and the meaning of the revival he is credited with, the sources differ, and the differences are set out side by side below.

## The Life of the Saint

Khwaja Muhammad Sulaiman was born around 1770 in the Taunsa region, into a Pathan family (whence his title *Pir Pathan*), and gave himself from youth to religious learning and the Sufi path. He was initiated in the Chishti-Nizami order and became the spiritual heir of his master Khwaja Nur Muhammad Maharvi's line, rising to be among the foremost Chishti shaykhs of his age. From his khanqah at Taunsa he taught, guided a vast following, and kept a great *langar*, and he is credited with reinvigorating the Chishti tradition in the Punjab at a time of political upheaval. His disciples carried his influence widely: the Chishti revival of the nineteenth-century Punjab, including the lines that led to Sial Sharif and Golra Sharif, owes much to his khanqah. He died in 1850 and was buried at Taunsa Sharif.

Sharib heads his chapter simply "حضرت خواجہ محمد سلیمان رحمۃ اللہ علیہ" and writes "آپ کا نام محمد سلیمان ہے", giving him neither the title Shah nor the name Taunsvi; his honorific line calls him "حضرت خواجہ محمد سلیمان مقتدائے ارباب یقین ہیں", the exemplar of the people of certainty (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 363, folio 359). Lahori heads his entry "خواجہ محمد سلیمان چشتی", and his modern editor's annotation "خواجہ محمد سلیمان چشتی تونسوی" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 348, folio 336). Ernst and Lawrence call him "Shaykh Sulayman Taunsawi" in their running text, and also Shah Sulayman, Shaykh Sulayman, and Sulayman Tawnsawi (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 121, folio 108; p. 238, folio 228).

On his family, Sharib writes that "آپ کے پردادا عمر خاں اور آپ کے دادا عبدالوہاب افغانی جعفریہ قبیلہ سے تھے", that his great-grandfather Umar Khan and his grandfather Abd al-Wahhab were Afghans of the Ja'fariya tribe (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 363, folio 359), and names his father: "آپ کے والد ماجد کا نام زکریا خان ہے" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 363, folio 359). Lahori's editor gives the same line of descent: "شاہ سلیمان تونسوی بن زکریا بن عبدالوہاب بن عمر خاں کی ولادت ۱۱۸۴ھ بمقام گڑ گوجھی[OCR?] میں ہوئی", that Shah Sulaiman Taunsvi, son of Zakariya, son of Abd al-Wahhab, son of Umar Khan, was born in 1184 AH at Gar Gojhi [OCR?] (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 123, folio 111), and the index of Lahori's book glosses Zakariya as his father (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 318, folio 306).

Lahori writes that "اصل وطن ان کا پہاڑی ملک میں موضع گڑ گوجھی[OCR?] تھا", that his original home was the village of Gar Gojhi [OCR?] in the hill country (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 123, folio 111). The editor quotes the saint's own malfuzat, *Nafi al-Salikin*: "خانہ خود کہ در کوہ است واسم آں گڑ کوچی[OCR?] است کہ مسافت از تونسہ سہ کروہ می شود", his own house in the hills, named Gar Kochi [OCR?], three *kuroh* from Taunsa (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 123, folio 111). Sharib writes "آپ نے گڑ گوجی[OCR?] میں 1184ھ میں اس عالم کو زینت بخشی", that he adorned this world at Gar Goji [OCR?] in 1184 AH (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 363, folio 359). The name of the village is uncertain in every printing and spelled more than one way; what the printed texts agree on is a place in the hills, three *kuroh* from Taunsa.

Sharib relates that "چار سال کی عمر میں آپ کے والد کا سایہ آپ کے سر سے اٹھ گیا", that his father's shadow was lifted from him at the age of four (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 363, folio 359). He was brought up and taught under his mother's supervision. He read fifteen *paras* of the Quran with ملا یوسف جعفر and the remaining fifteen with میاں حسن علی, completing the Quran, and from the same teachers Attar's "پند نامہ" and Sa'di's "گلستان" and "بوستان", among other books. He then went to Langh, printed لانگھ [OCR?], where he read further Persian literature with مولوی ولی محمد, and from Langh to Kot Mithan, where he entered the *madrasa* of Qazi Muhammad Aqil, finished the Arabic textbooks in a short time and completed "تحصیل علوم ظاہری", the acquisition of the outward sciences (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 363, folio 359). Lahori too says that he first acquired outward learning at qasba Kot Mithan in the service of Qazi Muhammad Aqil, and only then came to his pir (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 123, folio 111).

Sharib relates that while he was living at Kot Mithan, Khwaja Nur Muhammad Maharvi came to Uch: "آپ کا کوٹ مٹھن میں قیام تھا تو حضرت خواجہ نور محمد مہاروی رحمۃ اللہ علیہ اچ تشریف لائے… آپ اچ جا کر ان کی خدمت میں حاضر ہوئے". He went to Uch, was honoured with *bai'at*, and "اس وقت آپ کی عمر سولہ سال کی تھی", he was then sixteen years old (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 364, folio 360). Afterwards "آپ اپنے پیرو مرشد کے پاس مہار آتے جاتے رہے' کبھی کبھی ایسا بھی ہوا کہ آپ پیدل ہی مہار روانہ ہو گئے", he kept going to and from his master at Mahar, and sometimes set out for Mahar on foot (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 364, folio 360). His pir "ریاضت و مجاہدات ذکر جہر اور پاس انفاس کی تعلیم فرمائی", taught him austerities and spiritual struggle, *zikr-e jahr* (vocal remembrance) and *pas-e anfas* (watching the breath), and taught him books of *tasawwuf*, among them "آداب الطالبین' عشرہ کاملہ" and "فصوص الحکم" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 364, folio 360).

At twenty-two he received the robe of succession, by the Prophet's own permission as Sharib tells it: "آپ کے پیرو مرشد نے سرور عالم صلی اللہ علیہ وسلم کی اجازت سے آپ کو خرقہ خلافت عطا فرمایا' اس وقت آپ کی عمر بائیس سال کی تھی" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 364, folio 360). After the passing of حضرت مولانا فخر الدین فخر جہاں he went to Delhi, and "چالیس روز تک ان کے مزار مبارک پر معتکف رہے' چہلم کی فاتحہ کے بعد مہار واپس آئے", he kept a forty-day retreat at that master's tomb and returned to Mahar after the fortieth-day *fatiha* (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 364, folio 360).

Lahori names him among the renowned khalifas of his pir, whose name is printed there as Khwaja Nur Muhammad Majheel [OCR?] Chishti (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 123, folio 111); in the neighbouring entry on Hafiz Muhammad Jamal the same book calls the pir Nur Muhammad Maharvi (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 120, folio 108). Ernst and Lawrence write that "he himself was the major successor to Nur Muhammad Maharvi (d. 1790)" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 121, folio 108), and that Nur Muhammad "was himself the fourth in line of succession from Shah Kalim Allah" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 123, folio 110). The chain they print runs from Kalim Allah Jahanabadi (1142/1729) through Nizam ad-Din Awrangabadi (1142/1730), Fakhr ad-Din Dihlawi (1199/1784) and Nur Muhammad Maharvi (1205/1790) to Sulayman Taunsawi (1267/1850) (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, pp. 123–124, folios 110–111). Sharib's own book carries, in a footnote to an earlier chapter, a list of the khalifas of خواجہ نور محمد مہاروی that includes the name "حضرت خواجہ محمد سلیمان", with no town, no nisba and no date; the book does not itself join that name to the subject of its later chapter, which states that he took *bai'at* from Nur Muhammad Maharvi and received the robe of succession from him (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 348, folio 344; p. 364, folio 360).

Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti's *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* names him among those who came after his pir, whose name is printed there as Nur Muhammad Mahl, in a passage whose printed text is uncertain at several words, and records that he became so famous "کہ مرجع ہی [OCR?] بنا دیا گیا" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 758).

The move to Taunsa was not his own choice. Lahori writes that "حسب الحکم پیر روشن ضمیر کے قصبہ تونسہ میں جاکر قیام پذیر ہوئے", that by the command of his enlightened pir he went and took up residence at the qasba of Taunsa (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 123, folio 111). Sharib agrees: "آپ کے پیر روشن ضمیر نے آپ کو تونسہ میں رہنے کا حکم دیا" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 364, folio 360), and "اپنے پیرو مرشد کے مطابق آپ نے تونسہ میں سکونت اختیار کی۔ وہاں ایک جھونپڑی ڈال کر رہنے لگے", that in accordance with his master's word he settled at Taunsa and began living there in a hut (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 364, folio 360). Lahori's editor writes that he left his own town and by his murshid's command began the work of guidance and instruction at Taunsa (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 123, folio 111).

Sharib describes a passionate love for his pir and a renunciation without equal, "ترک و تجرید", and writes that "سوائے ایک لنگی کے آپ کے پاس اور کچھ نہ تھا", he owned nothing but a *lungi*, and a mat was spread in his *hujra* on which he sat to worship (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 365, folio 361). He was a scholar as well as a mystic: "قرآن' حدیث اور فقہ میں دست گاہ حاصل تھی", he had mastery of the Quran, hadith and *fiqh* (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 365, folio 361), which Lahori's editor also says (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 123, folio 111). He loved *sama'*: "آپ اہل سماع و صاحب شوق و ذوق تھے", and "بعض اوقات حالت رقص و وجد میں اشک بار ہوتے تھے", at times, in the state of dance and ecstasy, he wept (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 365, folio 361).

Sharib writes that "آپ کے دونوں صاحب زادے' خواجہ گل محمد اور خواجہ درویش محمد آپ کی حیات میں وفات پا گئے", that both his sons, Khwaja Gul Muhammad and Khwaja Darvesh Muhammad, died in his lifetime; the book names no wife (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 365, folio 361). He gives the death as "آپ 7 صفر 1267ھ کو جوار رحمت میں داخل ہوئے", 7 Safar 1267 AH, and states no age at death (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 365, folio 361). Lahori's author gives "نویں ماہ صفر روز پنجشنبہ سنہ ایک ہزار دوسو سٹھ", the ninth of Safar, a Thursday, in the year 1260, while the chronogram four lines later in the same entry carries ۱۲۶۷ [OCR?], and the editor states "وفات ۱۲۶۷ھ میں ہوئی", death in 1267 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 124, folio 112; p. 120, folio 108). Ernst and Lawrence print "(d. 1850)" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 33, folio 19; p. 121, folio 108) and "Sulayman Taunsawi (1267/1850)" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 124, folio 111), and head his entries in their Persian bibliography "Sulayman Tawnsawi (d. 1267/1850)" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 240, folio 230).

## The Shrine

The shrine of Sulaiman Taunsvi at Taunsa Sharif is a large complex, richly built and kept by his successors, and a busy centre of pilgrimage in southern Punjab. The town of Taunsa Sharif takes its name and its identity from the darbar, which has remained, through the saint's successors, a seat of Chishti teaching and devotion.

Sharib ties the growth of the town to the saint's coming: "وہاں ایک جھونپڑی ڈال کر رہنے لگے۔ عبادت' ریاضت اور مجاہدہ میں مشغول رہتے تھے۔ رفتہ رفتہ تونسہ کو ترقی ملی", he began living there in a hut, occupied in worship, austerity and struggle, and gradually Taunsa came to prosper (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 364–365, folios 360–361). Ernst and Lawrence place him there in life as "the living master, Shaykh Sulayman in Taunsa", to whom a disciple returned from a circuit of tombs (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 71, folio 57).

Lahori's editor, Sharib, and Ernst and Lawrence all speak of a school he founded at Taunsa. Lahori's editor writes "یہاں ایک مدرسہ قائم کیا۔", that he established a *madrasa* here (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 123, folio 111). Sharib gives its purpose and its teaching: "آپ نے تونسہ میں ایک دارالعلوم قائم کیا۔ علوم شریعت سے بہت لوگ ناواقف تھے۔ اس دارالعلوم کا مقصد علوم شریعت کی تعلیم تھا۔ آپ بھی اس دارالعلوم میں خاص خاص طلباء کو سلوک و احسان کی کتب کا درس دیتے تھے' رفتہ رفتہ یہ دارالعلوم بڑھتا گیا اور ایک اچھا خاصا اسلامی علوم کا مرکز بن گیا". He founded a *dar al-ulum* at Taunsa because many people were ignorant of the sciences of the *shari'a*; he himself taught books of the mystical path to select students there; and it grew into a considerable centre of Islamic learning (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 364, folio 360). Ernst and Lawrence write that "He consolidated the order by stressing learning through establishment of institutions": "not only the academy called" Dar al-'Ulum "at Taunsa on the upper Indus, but also other religious schools throughout the Punjab, linking each to instruction in the mystical insights of the Nizami Chishti branch of Sufism" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 121, folio 108). None of the three gives a founding year, an endowment or a number of students for the Taunsa school.

Ernst and Lawrence write that "we can say with confidence that future scholarship will benefit from the library built up and retained at his principal lodge in Taunsa on the upper Indus" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 121, folio 108); they give no catalogue or count of its holdings. They describe one manuscript from it, a calendar of the death anniversaries of the saints: "It is common to find short calendars of death anniversaries of saints of a particular order, as in a manuscript in the Nizami-Chishti shrine of Taunsa, compiled at the end of the eighteenth century and arranged according to the months of the Muslim lunar calendar" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 104, folio 91), and they print it as the appendix of their book. "The calendar from this shrine is not strictly equivalent to the direct lineage of the chain (silsila) of masters", they write; "Not only does the list include extra figures outside the Chishti lineage, like the first four caliphs; it also contains some relatively obscure undated ancillary figures who are related by family to the main Chishti shaykhs of this lineage" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 104, folio 91).

Lahori, writing in his own present, says that "بعد وفات ان کے اب شیخ الہ بخش حضرت کے پوتے مسند آرائے مسند مشیخیت ہیں", that after his death Shaikh Allah Bakhsh, the Hazrat's grandson, now adorns the seat of the shaykh (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 124, folio 112), and the book's index glosses Allah Bakhsh as his *sajjada nashin* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 313, folio 301). The editor adds that "اپنے دادا کی طرح خواجہ الہ بخش تونسویؒ روحانی اصلاح و تربیت کے کام میں سرگرم رہے۔ حقیقت یہ ہے کہ انیسویں صدی میں چشتیہ سلسلہ کی رونق ان کے دم قدم سے تھی", that like his grandfather Khwaja Allah Bakhsh Taunsvi was active in spiritual reform and training, and that the splendour of the Chishti order in the nineteenth century was owing to him; he dates him born 1231 AH and died 1319 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 124, folio 112). Sharib agrees: "آپ کی وفات کے بعد آپ کے پوتے حضرت اللہ بخش آپ کے سجادہ نشین ہوئے", after his death his grandson Hazrat Allah Bakhsh became his *sajjada nashin*, the seat passing over a generation because both sons had died before him (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 365, folio 361). Ernst and Lawrence date the grandson "(d. 1901)" in their text and "(d. 1319/1901-2)" in their Persian bibliography (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 124, folio 111; p. 238, folio 228).

Ernst and Lawrence class Taunsa among the branch shrines of Ajmer in one practice, hereditary custodianship under the title *gaddi nishin*: "Variations of this practice can be traced from the principal Chishti shrine in Ajmer to branch shrines, like Taunsa on the west bank of the Indus" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 111, folio 98; pp. 110–111, folios 97–98). They name it again beside Ajmer as "the tomb-shrine of Shah Sulayman Tawnsawi", one of two shrines "discussed at length by David Gilmartin" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 116, folio 103).

The succession was later contested. "Consider the shrine at Taunsa, which witnessed a conflict between two of the branches of succession from Shaykh Sulayman, one of them linked to his grandson Allah Bakhsh (d. 1901), the other to Mihr 'Ali Shah (d. 1937). Political power struggles internal to the Sulaymaniyya can be traced to British manipulation and also the rivalry of local Sikhs, Hindus, and Muslim reformers" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 124, folio 111). The authors do not let that be the whole story: "But political contestation is only part of the narrative" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 124, folio 111).

None of the books consulted gives a date for the building of the tomb, a builder, an endowment, or a figure for the number of pilgrims.

## Devotion and Practice

The shrine maintains the daily prayers, qawwali and *naat*, and the free kitchen, and its annual urs draws great crowds. The books consulted do not record the date of the *'urs*.

Lahori describes the *langar* as it was in the saint's lifetime: "ہزاروں طالبان خدا وہاں حضرت کی خدمت میں مشرف ہوئے اور ہزاروں مسافر ومسکین وغربا دو وقتہ حضرت کے لنگر سے کھانا کھاتے", thousands of seekers of God were honoured in his service there, and thousands of travellers, the destitute and the poor ate two meals a day from the Hazrat's *langar* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 123, folio 111).

Lahori writes that "غرض وہ قبول حاصل ہوا کہ اس زمانہ میں کسی کو حاصل نہ تھا", that he attained such acceptance as no one else had in that age, and that "ایک لاکھ سے زیادہ حضرت کے مرید ہوئے اور سینکڑوں کفار وفجار نے توبہ کی", more than a lakh became his disciples, and hundreds of unbelievers and sinners repented (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 123, folio 111).

Sharib sets out his daily discipline, to which, he says, he held without fail: "آپ اپنے معمولات کے سختی سے پابند تھے۔ کسی حالت میں بھی اپنے معمولات میں فرق نہیں آنے دیتے تھے". After the night prayer of *tahajjud* came vocal *zikr*; after the dawn prayer a short rest in the *hujra*; then out to "رشد و ہدایت", guidance of others; a midday rest after the meal, always; after the noon prayer, recitation of the Quran; then the afternoon prayer; after the sunset prayer, "ذکر جہر میں ایک پہر گذارتے", a watch of the night spent in vocal *zikr*; then the night meal, the *'isha* prayer with the congregation, and rest in the *hujra* (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 365, folio 361).

Sharib records that his life passed in worship, austerity, guidance and instruction, and that he insisted the purpose of human life is the worship of God, often reciting "وما خلقت الجن والانس الا لیعبدون" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 365–366, folios 361–362). He held that following the Prophet is indispensable for worldly and religious advancement; enjoined his hearers to shun "جھوٹ' غیبت' بے ایمانی' لالچ' حسد' غرور اور تکبر", lying, backbiting, dishonesty, greed, envy, vanity and pride; condemned the love of the world and the company of worldly people, "جہاں تک ممکن ہو سکے' دنیا داروں کی صحبت سے بچنا چاہیے"; and commended humility, "اپنے کو ادنٰی اور سب سے بدتر سمجھنا چاہیے" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 366, folio 362). Among his sayings: that none can be accepted at God's court without following the *shari'a* outwardly and inwardly; that Muslims should keep away from "غیر مشروع امور"; and that "صفائی قلب اور روحانی ترقی کے لئے اتباع شریعت از بس ضروری ہے", that for purity of heart and spiritual progress following the *shari'a* is altogether necessary (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 366, folio 362).

Ernst and Lawrence write that "It is, above all, the combination of spiritual valence and political activism that characterizes Shaykh Sulayman" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 121, folio 108). "He urged his followers to participate in resistance against Sikh aggression in the Punjab, and so, apparently for the first time in the annals of Chishti history, we have spiritual biographies drawn into partisanship on behalf of one political group (Muslim) over against another (non-Muslim)"; and at once: "Notable, however, is the effort of Shah Sulayman to maintain cordial relations with Punjabi Hindus even while he opposed their Sikh neighbors" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 121, folio 108).

## Legends and Miracles

Sharib tells a story about the source of the *langar*'s wealth. A merchant, a devotee of the saint, "وہ ہندوستان آتا' مال فروخت کرتا اور پھر واپس چلا جاتا۔ جب واپس ہوتا تو آپ کی خدمت با برکت میں حاضر ہوتا' نذرانہ پیش کرتا' دعا کا طالب ہوتا اور چلا جاتا", came to Hindustan, sold his goods and went back, and on his way back would attend on the saint, present an offering, ask for a prayer and leave (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 366, folio 362). Once, seeing a great deal of offerings coming in, "اس کو یہ دیکھ کر خیال آیا کہ یہ سب لنگر اور داد و دہش دنیا داروں کے روپے سے ہے' اگر سامنے نذرانے نہیں آئیں گے تو یہ سب کام ختم ہو جائے گا", the thought came to him that all this *langar* and giving ran on the rupees of worldly people, and if the offerings stopped the whole work would come to an end (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 366–367, folios 362–363).

The saint, as Sharib tells it, answered with a precedent. When سلطان غیاث الدین had held the same suspicion about the *langar* and largesse of حضرت نظام الدین اولیاء, the sultan, he says, "اس نے ممانعت کر دی تھی کہ اس کے دربار کا کوئی آدمی حضرت نظام الدین اولیاء رحمۃ اللہ علیہ کی خدمت میں نہ جائے اور نہ کوئی نذرانہ وغیرہ پیش کرے", he forbade anyone of his court to attend on Nizam al-Din or to present any offering; and Nizam al-Din, learning of it, "دونا خرچ کرنے کی تاکید فرمائی' خادم کو ہدایت فرمائی کہ حجرہ کے طاق میں سے ضرورت کے موافق لے لیا کرے", ordered the spending doubled and told his servant to take what was needed from the niche of the *hujra* (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 367, folio 363).

Then, under the heading of *kashf* and *karamat*, Sharib relates that the saint "اس سوداگر کے خطرے سے بذریعہ کشف آگاہ ہوئے", knew the merchant's misgiving by inner unveiling. His prayer-mat was fetched from the *hujra*, the merchant lifted it, and "اس کو یہ دیکھ کر حیرت ہوئی کہ اس کے نیچے ایک دریا ہے اور اس کی تین دھاریں ہیں' ایک دھار اشرفی کی' دوسری دھار روپیوں کی اور تیسری دھار جواہرات کی", he was amazed to see beneath it a river with three currents, one of gold *ashrafis*, one of rupees and one of jewels. The mat fell from his hand, and he came out and begged forgiveness (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 367, folio 363).

Sharib relates that an old woman came "چیختی چلاتی اور روتی ہوئی", screaming and weeping, saying that her only son was dead. A servant suggested "ممکن ہے کہ اس کو سکتہ ہو گیا ہو", that the boy might only be in a swoon. The saint found him dead, with no pulse anywhere, and at the servant's request looked closely: "آپ کی توجہ قلبی سے اس کی نبض میں حرکت ہوئی' دوبارہ دیکھنے پر نبض اچھی طرح چلنے لگی' وہ لڑکا اٹھ کر بیٹھ گیا", through the attention of his heart the pulse stirred, at a second look it was beating well, and the boy sat up; "اس واقعہ کے بہت دنوں بعد تک وہ لڑکا بقید حیات رہا", the boy lived for a long time after (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 367–368, folios 363–364). The boy then "آپ کی خدمت میں رہنے لگا' آپ نے پانی بھرنے کی خدمت اس کے سپرد فرمائی", stayed in the saint's service, and was given the duty of drawing water (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 367, folio 363). Sharib gives neither story a source.

## In Verse

The books consulted quote no verse of the saint's own and no couplet about him, but they record that his death and his life were put into verse. Lahori's entry carries a chronogram on his death, which the printed text gives as encoding the year ۱۲۶۷ [OCR?], 1267 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 124, folio 112). Chishti's *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* carries a passage headed "تاریخ وفات حضرت خواجہ سلیمانصاحب", a chronogram on the death of Hazrat Khwaja Sulaiman, connected with "مولوی عبد الحمیر [OCR?] صاحب چاچڑ [OCR?] والی"; no year can be read in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 758).

Ernst and Lawrence list several lives of him in verse in their Persian bibliography: 'Abd al-Rahman Khayrpuri's *Hilya-i mubarak*, a verse biography (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 237, folio 227); Muhammad Husayn Pishawari's *Raz-i Hazrat-i Khwaja Muhammad Sulayman*, a biography in seventy-one verses (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 237, folio 227); a *Manaqib-i Sulaymani* in verse by Khuda Bakhsh Tawnsawi (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 238, folio 228); and the *Fayd-i Muhtawi* or *Muraqqa'-i Sulaymani*, a *mathnawi* verse biography by Musa Buzdar, a disciple of Hafiz Ghulam Shah Sadid al-Din Chishti (d. 1379/1960) (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 238, folio 228). Even his robe of succession had its poem: "Ishaq Afghan (ca. 1292/1875) wrote in verse Qamis-i sharif on the pedigree of the khirqa of Sulayman, plus Manaqib-i Sulaymani" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 238, folio 228). The book does not say where the *khirqa* itself is kept.

## His Order and His Disciples

The current account calls his order the Chishti-Nizami. Lahori's editor names it more fully: "پنجاب میں سلسلہ چشتیہ نظامیہ فخریہ کی زیادہ اشاعت حضرت خواجہ سلیمان تونسوی کے ذریعہ ہوئی", in the Punjab the wider spread of the Chishtiya Nizamiya Fakhriya order came about through Hazrat Khwaja Sulaiman Taunsvi (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 120, folio 108). Ernst and Lawrence derive a branch from him: "One of the main branchings within the Chishtiyya, for instance, is the Nizami-Chishtiyya, derived from Shaykh Nizam ad-Din Awliya' (d. 1325); a sub-subbranch is the" Sulaymaniyya, "linked to Shaykh Sulayman Taunsawi (d. 1850)", and "Hence a master of the latter subbranch would be properly identified as a Sulaymani-Nizami-Chishti shaykh" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 33, folio 19); elsewhere they speak of "the Chishti Nizami Sulaymaniyya lineage in the colonial period" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 124, folio 111). Sharib attaches no order-name to him at all, naming only his pir and the practices he was taught (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 364, folio 360).

Lahori writes that "شیخ محمد یار و حافظ محمد علی و مولوی محمد علی ثانی تین خلیفے حضرت کے مقرب و محبوب و محرم اسرار تھے", that Shaikh Muhammad Yar, Hafiz Muhammad Ali and Maulvi Muhammad Ali Thani were three khalifas close to him, beloved, and sharers of his secrets, out of countless others (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 124, folio 112). Of the second, his editor adds that Hafiz Muhammad Ali, known as Muharram Ali Khairabadi (1192–1266), was among Taunsvi's earliest khalifas, and that "خیرآباد میں ان کی خانقاہ علم و فضل کا مرکز تھی", his khanqah at Khairabad was a centre of learning (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 124, folio 112). Sharib writes "آپ کے خلفاء بہت ہیں", that his khalifas are many, and names as the famous among them "شیخ محمد یار' حافظ محمد علی خبر آیادی[OCR?]' مولوی محمد علی' میاں عبداللہ شاہ" (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 365, folio 361).

Ernst and Lawrence write that Khayrabadi "became one of the major successors of Shaykh Sulayman" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 71, folio 57), and that the line resurfaces in the nineteenth century "in the figure of Hafiz Sayyid Muhammad 'Ali Khayrabadi, marking the expansion of the Nizami branch of Chishti Sufism through disciples of Shaykh Sulayman Taunsawi in the upper Punjab" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 71, folio 57). Their Persian bibliography names two more men as "khalifa of Sulayman": Muhammad Fadil Shah Mirpuri, author of *Za'faran* and *Nafi' al-rasikhin* (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 238, folio 228), and Muhammad Allah Bakhsh Tawnsawi, whose *Ghaza' al-muhibbin wa samm al-mu'anidin* was compiled by Nur Muhammad Mak'hadi in 1314/1896-97 (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 240, folio 230). Another disciple, Ahmad 'Ali Khayrabadi, wrote the *Qasr-i 'arifan*, a general history of Sufi orders and Indian dynasties, translated into Urdu by Muhammad Baqir (Lahore, 1965) (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 235, folio 225).

Two of his khalifas have entries of their own in Lahori's book among the saints of Lahore: Shaikh Haji Ramzan Lahori, and, through Haidar Shah and Khair Shah, the Lahore line of the following entry (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 125–126, folios 113–114). The index of Lahori's book also lists three further men called Taunsvi, Sami' Allah, Abd al-Rahman and Abd al-Rahim, all at printed page 113; it does not say how they were related to him (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 319, folio 307; pp. 321, 322).

The lineage travelled far. Ernst and Lawrence name "Sufi Saheb (d. 1916), the Chishti responsible for introducing Chishti Nizami Sulaymaniyya into South Africa" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 124, folio 111). They also write that "When he was eleven his father made him a murid of Shah Allah Bakhsh Taunsawi", of Khwaja Hasan Nizami (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 127, folio 114); they do not say there whether this is the same Allah Bakhsh they call Shaykh Sulayman's grandson.

The same authors describe a pattern of schooling in the southern Punjab: "The same pattern of educational effort was followed by his successor Muhammad 'Aqil (d. 1814), and the latter's disciple Gul Muhammad Ahmadpuri (d. 1827), both of whom founded religious schools in the southern Punjab that provided free instruction and lodging to hundreds of" students (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, pp. 121–122, folios 108–109). The figure of hundreds of students belongs to those schools, not to Taunsa.

## Books about the Saint

Ernst and Lawrence take him as their example of how the Persian literature of the Chishtis went on multiplying late, "right up to the end of the nineteenth century (see, for example, the numerous biographies of Shaykh Sulayman Taunsawi)" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 16, folio not stated). They write too that "numerous scholars cite him as a founding figure of the Chishti recuperation in eighteenth century North India", but that "we lack a complete picture of him" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 121, folio 108).

Their Persian bibliography lists thirteen works under his name. Besides the verse lives named above, they include Ghulam Muhammad Chhachari's *Manaqib-i Sulaymani*, compiled in 1256/1840, whose Bahawalnagar manuscript has an appendix dated 1287/1870 "recording the author's later visits to Taunsa and the death of the shaykh"; and Yar Muhammad's *Intikhab-i manaqib-i Sulaymani* (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 237, folio 227). On the following page come Nizam al-Din Ahmad Bada'oni's *Rahat al-'ashiqin*; Najm al-Din Nagawri's *Manaqib-i mahbubayn*, lives of the saint and of Nur Muhammad Maharawi completed in 1278/1862 and printed at Lahore in 1312; Khuda Bakhsh Tawnsawi's *Muntakhab al-asrar*, "Composed ca. 1287-88/1870-72"; a *Manaqib-i Sulaymani* by Nabi Bakhsh; and a modern study, Muhammad Husayn Lillahi's *Hazrat Khwaja Muhammad Sulayman Tawnsawi awr un ke khulafa*, published at Lahore by the Islamic Book Foundation in 1399/1979, "A Karachi University Ph.D. dissertation" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 238, folio 228). Three collections of his sayings, *malfuzat*, follow: a *Malfuzat-i sharif* compiled by Ghulam Haydar; the *Nafi' al-salikin*, compiled by Imam al-Din Ghulam Shah in 1267/1851-52; and the *Muntakhab al-manaqib*, compiled by Yar Muhammad Dhawqi ibn Taj Muhammad Pakpattani (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 240, folio 230). The grandson Allah Bakhsh Tawnsawi has a book of his own in the list, the *Shama'il-i gharib nawaz* (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 238, folio 228).

Lahori's editor appends his own list of sources on the saint, with imprints: *Nafi al-Salikin* (Lahore, 1285 AH); *Khatam-e Sulaimani* (Lahore, 1335 AH); Muhammad al-Din Sufi's *Zikr-e Habib*; *Manaqib-e Hafiziya*; Amir Bakhsh's *Anwar-e Shamsiya* (Lahore, 1335 AH); Din Muhammad's *Ahtiram al-Asfiya* or *Nishat-e Hafiziya* (Aligarh, 1931); *Nafa'at* [OCR?] *al-Mahbub fi Ihya al-Qulub*; and Khaliq Ahmad Nizami's *Tarikh-e Mashaikh-e Chisht* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 124, folio 112). In an earlier footnote he names *Nafi al-Salikin*, *Khatam-e Sulaimani*, *Intikhab-e Manaqib-e Sulaimani* and *Manaqib al-Mahbubin* as his sources for the saint's dates (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 120, folio 108). The editor also points to his own article on the saint in the *Danishnama*, "جس میں آپ کے ملفوظات کے مختلف مجموعوں کا تعارف کروایا گیا ہے", in which the various collections of his *malfuzat* are introduced (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 348, folio 336).

## Setting

Lahori's editor places Taunsa for his readers: "تونسہ ڈیرہ غازی خاں سے ۴۰ کوس کے فاصلے پر ایک گاؤں ہے۔", Taunsa is a village forty *kos* from Dera Ghazi Khan (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 123, folio 111). Ernst and Lawrence set it on the Indus: "branch shrines, like Taunsa on the west bank of the Indus" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 111, folio 98), and "his principal lodge in Taunsa on the upper Indus" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 121, folio 108).

Taunsa was also a place where Chishti books were copied. Ernst and Lawrence print, as the appendix's source, a manuscript of Muhammad Chishti's *Adab al-muridin* "copied in Taunsa ca. 1790" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 159, folio 147), and in their bibliography record a manuscript of *Adab al-talibin* copied in "Tawnsah", probably after 1790, now in Carl Ernst's personal collection, the gift of Khalil al-Rahman Dawoodi of Lahore (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 243, folio 233).

Richard M. Eaton's essays name "Taunsa Sharif in Dera Ghazi Khan District" once, in a list of subordinate shrines given in a court deposition of the 1930s; they name no saint and say nothing more, and the identification with this shrine rests on the place-name alone (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 242, n. 12).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The year of his death.** The current account gives 1850, as do Ernst and Lawrence, who print "(d. 1850)" and "1267/1850" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 33, folio 19; p. 124, folio 111). Sharib gives 7 Safar 1267 AH (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 365, folio 361). Lahori's author writes the year in words as 1260, on 9 Safar, a Thursday; the chronogram in the same entry carries 1267, its numeral uncertain in the printed text; and the editor states 1267 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 124, folio 112; p. 120, folio 108).
- **The day of his death.** Sharib gives the seventh of Safar (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 365, folio 361); Lahori's author the ninth (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 124, folio 112).
- **His birth.** The current account gives c. 1770, in the Taunsa region. Lahori's editor and Sharib give 1184 AH, at a village in the hills whose name is printed Gar Gojhi, Gar Kochi and Gar Goji, every time with its reading uncertain; the editor's quotation from *Nafi al-Salikin* puts it three *kuroh* from Taunsa (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 123, folio 111; Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 363, folio 359).
- **The name of his order.** The current account calls it Chishti-Nizami. Lahori's editor names it the Chishtiya Nizamiya Fakhriya (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 120, folio 108). Ernst and Lawrence name a Sulaymaniyya sub-branch after him and call its masters Sulaymani-Nizami-Chishti (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 33, folio 19). Sharib gives no order-name (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 364, folio 360).
- **A Chishti revival.** The current account remembers him as a chief reviver of the Chishti order and an architect of the Chishti revival in the nineteenth-century Punjab. Ernst and Lawrence write: "Rather than categorize him as part of the so-called Chishti revival, we prefer to locate him in the third cycle of the Chishti Nizamiyya, a historical period" running from the eighteenth to the twenty-first centuries; the same page reports that "numerous scholars cite him as a founding figure of the Chishti recuperation in eighteenth century North India" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 121, folio 108).
- **His pir's name.** Lahori's entry on the saint prints the pir as Nur Muhammad Majheel, its reading uncertain (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 123, folio 111); the same book's neighbouring entry, Sharib, Ernst and Lawrence and the current account call him Nur Muhammad Maharvi (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 120, folio 108; Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 364, folio 360; Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 121, folio 108); *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* prints Nur Muhammad Mahl (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 758).
- **Muhammad 'Aqil.** Lahori and Sharib make Qazi Muhammad Aqil of Kot Mithan the teacher under whom he finished his outward studies before he came to his pir (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 123, folio 111; Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 363, folio 359). Ernst and Lawrence, in the passage that follows their account of Shaykh Sulayman, speak of "his successor Muhammad 'Aqil (d. 1814)" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, pp. 121–122, folios 108–109), and three pages later of a master "Muhammad 'Aqil (d. 1813)" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 124, folio 111). None of the books says whether these are the same man.
- **His chief khalifas.** Lahori names three intimates, Shaikh Muhammad Yar, Hafiz Muhammad Ali and Maulvi Muhammad Ali Thani (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 124, folio 112); Sharib names four famous khalifas, adding Mian Abdullah Shah (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 365, folio 361).
- **The grandson's death.** Lahori's editor dates Allah Bakhsh's death 1319 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 124, folio 112); Ernst and Lawrence give "(d. 1901)" in their text and 1319/1901-2 in their bibliography (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 124, folio 111; p. 238, folio 228).
- **The manuscript copied at Taunsa.** Ernst and Lawrence name the manuscript copied at Taunsa around 1790 as *Adab al-muridin*, "copied in Taunsa ca. 1790" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 159, folio 147), and in their bibliography as *Adab al-talibin*, copied there "probably after" 1790 (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 243, folio 233). They do not say whether the two are one manuscript.

## Legacy

Khwaja Muhammad Sulaiman Taunsvi stands among the principal architects of the Chishti revival in the nineteenth-century Punjab, the "Pir Pathan" whose khanqah at Taunsa nourished a generation of saints and spread the order across the region. His shrine remains one of the great Chishti centres of southern Punjab, and his memory endures as that of a reviver of the tradition.

Lahori, writing in 1875, judged that he had won such acceptance as no one else in his age (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 123, folio 111), and his editor credits him with the wider spread of the Chishti order in the Punjab (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 120, folio 108). Ernst and Lawrence, declining the word revival, still make him the head of a named branch of the Nizami Chishtis that reached as far as South Africa, the founder of a school at Taunsa and of others across the Punjab, and the subject of the numerous biographies they give as their example of the late growth of Chishti writing in Persian, and they commend the library at his lodge to future scholars (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 33, folio 19; p. 124, folio 111; p. 121, folio 108; p. 16, folio not stated).
