## Overview

The Shri Laxmi Narayan Mandir is a Hindu temple in Karachi remarkable for its situation: it stands beneath the Native Jetty Bridge, on the banks of the Chinna Creek, the only temple in the city set directly upon the tidal waters that open onto the Arabian Sea. Reputed to be some two hundred years old, it is dedicated to *Lakshmi* and *Narayan* — the goddess of fortune and the preserving god *Vishnu* — and it is among the oldest continually operating temples of Karachi, cherished above all for the seaside rites that its unique location makes possible.

Of the books consulted for this article, one appears to name the temple, and only in a single sentence. Michel Boivin's study of the Hindu Sufis of Sindh, discussing the deified hero Ramdeo Pir, writes: "In Karachi, for example, a painting of Ramdeo Pir stands in the temple of Lakhshmi Narayan." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 192, folio 179). He gives no address, no custodian, no date and no description beyond the painting, and the sentence is taken to refer to this temple only because it names a temple of Lakshmi-Narayan in Karachi; if Boivin meant another Karachi temple of that dedication, the passage is not about this one. No book names the Native Jetty Bridge or the Chinna Creek, describes the temple's rites by the water, or records its festivals or the court case that saved it. What the other books carry is set out below as it stands: what they say of the goddess Lakshmi, and, under Setting, what they say of Karachi and of the Hindus of Sindh. None of that is said of this temple.

## Lakshmi-Narayan and Worship by the Sea

The pairing of Lakshmi and Narayan is one of the most beloved in the Hindu tradition, expressing the union of prosperity and divine sustenance. At Karachi the temple's devotion has been shaped by its place at the water's edge. Overlooking the creek where it meets the sea, the shrine is a favoured site for those rituals that require flowing or tidal water — most importantly the immersion of the ashes of the dead, the *asthi visarjan* by which Hindus commit the remains of the departed to sacred waters. For a community far from the great river-*ghats* and coastal *tirthas* of India, the temple on the Chinna Creek has offered an irreplaceable link to the wider ritual geography of the faith. Within the sanctuary, alongside the principal images, devotees also honour *Hanuman* and *Sai Baba* of Shirdi.

## The Painting of Ramdeo Pir

The one detail of the temple's interior that a book records is a painting. Boivin mentions it as an example: "In Karachi, for example, a painting of Ramdeo Pir stands in the temple of Lakhshmi Narayan." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 192, folio 179). He spells the goddess's name Lakhshmi. He says nothing of where in the temple the painting hangs, how old it is, or who placed it there.

The sentence belongs to Boivin's account of the deified heroes whom the Hindus of Sindh shared with their Muslim neighbours. "In Sindh, the most popular deified heroes were Rama Pir (Ramdeo or Ramdev), Pithoro Pir, Pabuji and Guga Pir" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 42–43, folios 29–30). On the same page as the Karachi painting he writes that Ramdeo Pir is "still known in Sindh as Rama Pir … In Sindh, he is today integrated into the temples dedicated to the pan-Indian divinities" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 192, folio 179); the painting in the Karachi temple of Lakshmi-Narayan is his instance of that. The case of Pithoro Pir, discussed on the same page, shows in his words "the variety of the negotiations between Sufism and Hinduism in Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 192, folio 179).

Boivin frames such figures within a wider pattern. "In Sindh, we already know that a number of sacred figures have a dual religious identity, Muslim and Hindu. The Muslim identity is always related to Sufism, while the Hindu side usually belongs to the Nathpanth." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 187, folio 174). He sorts the ways in which the Hindus of Sindh are related to Sufism into three: "the most common", a Sufi tomb "visited both by Muslims and Hindus"; the "also relatively common" saint with "a double identity, Muslim and Hindu"; and "a Sufi darbar … managed by the Hindus" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 177–178, folios 164–165). "The large majority of the Hindus used to pay a visit to the Sufi shrines, but does it mean they are Sufi? Of course not. It does mean that, for them, the Sufis are a part of their religious legacy" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 192–193, folios 179–180).

A painted image in a Hindu temple of Sindh has a further context in Boivin's notes. An endnote records that wall paintings of Krishna in Hindu temples "could mean there was a kind of Krishnaite revival in the nineteenth century among the Sindhi Hindus (Kalhoro 2014: 238–44)" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 213, folio 200). None of this is said of the Karachi temple.

## Lakshmi in the Books

The books that name the goddess speak of her in other places, never of this temple, and none of them names her consort Narayan with her. Haroon Khalid's *A White Trail* describes, on the wall of a Valmiki temple, "Laxmi, the spouse of Lord Vishnu", pictured "resting on a lotus flower" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 80, folio not stated). His *Walking with Nanak*, in a scene the author reconstructs, has Tripta visit "the Laxmi temple" in Talwindi at sunrise (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 21, folio 3). In Saidpur, the same book records, every shop kept idols, and "Laxmi" was the most popular (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 103, folio 85).

Boivin writes that "the Muslim Sufis of Sindh were mostly attracted by the Vishnuite school of Hinduism" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 107, folio 94). Among the Hindus themselves, "Krishna's worship had already developed in late-nineteenth-century Sindh among the Amils", and "the Vallabhachari sect was accepted by some members of the Amil community" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 135, folio 122). "A number of Sufis from nineteenth-century Sindh, such as Sachal, Qutub Ali Shah and Bedil, themselves include Krishna in their Sufi poetry … despite the fact that Sindh was Muslim dominated, the Krishnaite Bhakti was influential among wujudi Sufis" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 198, folio 185).

## The Temple by the Creek

The temple's setting is at once its glory and its vulnerability. Tucked beneath the approaches of the Native Jetty Bridge, it looks out across the water, and the sea has long lapped close to its precincts. Its festivals mark the turning of the devotional year: *Ganesh Chaturthi*, the birthday of Ganesha, is celebrated here, together with the spring festival of *Holi* and *Raksha Bandhan*, the observance of the bond between brothers and sisters. On these days the modest shrine fills with the Hindu families of Karachi, for whom it is a place of gathering as much as of worship.

The books consulted record none of these festivals at this temple, and none describes its building, its priests or its rites by the water. The nearest they come to the worship of water in Sindh is Boivin's account of the province's Hindu trading castes: "Among the Lohanas and related mercantile groups, two main cults were followed: the Nanakpanth and the Daryapanth". The Daryapanth "is in all probability the oldest of the two. It is based on the deification of the Indus River", and it was served by "a priestly caste, the Thakurs" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 42–43, folios 29–30). "In nineteenth-century Sindh, the Nanakpanth, or 'the path of Nanak', was probably the dominant religious path among the Hindu Sindhis" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 90, folio 77). An endnote adds that "The Thakurs were the priests of Jhulelal. In Sindh, they were settled in different cities but they migrated to India from 1947 onwards" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 211, folio 198).

## Under Threat

The very features that make the temple precious have placed it in jeopardy. The development of the port and the construction that has crowded the shoreline near the site have threatened both its access to the seawater essential for its rituals and the integrity of the structure itself. In September 2012, after a petition brought by the local Hindu community, the Sindh High Court issued an order restraining the Karachi Port Trust from demolishing the temple. The episode drew wide attention to the fragility of the city's surviving Hindu heritage and to the community's determination to defend it.

The books do not mention the 2012 order, but they describe both the restoration of Hindu places in the province and the pressures on its Hindus. Jürgen Schaflechner, writing of Sindh, records that government funding for minority community places "is seen in a wave of restorations of temples across Sindh within the past decade", and that "While in Sindh, I visited many temples that were renovated within the past decade, coinciding with changes in the political landscape" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 16, folio not stated). In his book Karachi is, among much else, a city of violence against Hindus (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 18, folio not stated). Boivin writes that "the Hindu population of Pakistan, including Sindh, is more and more under the threat of the radical Muslims of the country" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 127, folio 114), and Khalid that after the demolition of the Babri Mosque in December 1992 "Hindu communities in Sindh and Quetta were attacked" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 22, folio not stated).

## Setting

The books say a good deal about Karachi and about the Hindus of Sindh, and nothing about the Native Jetty Bridge or this temple. What follows is said of the city and the province.

**Temples of Karachi.** Haroon Khalid's *Walking with Nanak* describes a visit to the city's old temples; the book identifies none of them with this one. At Ram Bagh, he found, the area was now called Aram Bagh, the added letter, as he put it, neutralising its religious sense (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 222, folio not stated). The temple had a painted Krishna "with his maidens" under its roof and "a peacock guarding the entrance"; an old man told him "This temple is one hundred and fifty years old", and Khalid remarked that "Hundred and fifty seemed to be the standard age of all temples here". The temple was "much more elaborately decorated", and its community "much larger", than in Lahore (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 223, folio 197). An officer said: "I don't know exactly how old this temple is but it is old… existed before Partition" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 223–224, folios 197–198). Along with the temple at Clifton, Khalid concluded, it "must have been a historical Hindu temple" in Guru Nanak's time (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 224, folio 198), and he cites Iqbal Qaiser's identification of the Clifton temple as "the temple which Nanak had visited on his tour of Sindh" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 217, folio 191).

At a temple within the Port Grand complex he found "a newly constructed mosque for tourists" built right on top of it (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 224, folio 198). He describes separate entrances for women and men, food being prepared, "probably… an offering from a rich Hindu", stairs leading into water "blackened by all the industrial waste", fishermen's boats, pigeons fed with rice, rooms "reserved for different deities", and a mural of "a male deity sitting [on] top of a giant fish", Jhule Lal (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 225, folio 199). The book does not name the deity of that temple.

**The port city.** "At the time of Partition, Karachi was a minor port city", Khalid writes, which migrants from India made "the largest city of Pakistan"; the port itself was "a modest structure, a minor tower… A clock is placed within the tower", a symbol of "colonial rule" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 226, folio 200). Its Hindu fisherfolk "lived here before Kolachi became Karachi" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 225, folio 199), and "these Hindu fisherfolk are now the only connection" between the city and the old village of Kolachi (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 226, folio 200). A legend told to him explains the name: an old woman known as Mai Kolachi, whose son "was killed [by] a fish", "tore apart the fish and retrieved" him, and the place came to be known as Kolachi (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 225, folio 199). In a collection of essays on Sufi devotion, the quay of "Karachi port" is where a disciple about to sail to Iraq saw his master's face in the water (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 154, folio 143).

**Hindu Karachi.** Khalid's *A White Trail* finds Karachi the supplier of Hindu worship elsewhere in Pakistan. Boys from Bahawalnagar "bought an idol of Durga from Karachi" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 31, folio not stated); a vendor's idols "have been brought from Karachi, where there are still a reasonable number of Hindus living or from India through illegal channels", and Navratri pilgrims come "from as far as Karachi" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 33, folio not stated). In Schaflechner's *Hinglaj Devi*, Karachi is the seat of the Nagnath and Jagnath *akhāṛās* (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 127, 138, 159, 207, 292–294, folios not stated), the staging city for Indian pilgrims (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 175, 180, 181, 183, folios not stated), the home of the Devipujak-Vagri community (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 306–312, 322, folios not stated), and the start of the road to the goddess Hinglaj, "about 240 kilometers long" from Narayanpur (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 158, folio not stated). The city's growth swallowed the old pilgrim road: "The ongoing growth of Karachi and the corresponding urbanization of the areas where the early stops occurred caused all but a few of the resting places to completely disappear." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 121, folio not stated).

**Colonial Karachi.** Boivin writes of "the growth of new emporium in Sindh, especially Karachi", after which the Lohanas invested in land and "became powerful landowners, locally known as zamindars, at the end of the nineteenth century" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 43–44, folios 30–31). Sir Charles Napier, "the conqueror and first governor of Sindh", built "a Masonic lodge in 1843 in Karachi", and "In Karachi, the Theosophical Society was founded in 1896" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 46, folio 33); its founders were "Dhunjishaw, Jamshed Nusserwanji and Dayaram Jethmal" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 46, folio 33). "In Sindh, a printing press was brought to Karachi in the 1850s, but the main event in relation to Sufism came with the printing in 1866 of the Shah jo Risalo" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 47–48, folios 34–35).

**Partition.** Boivin estimates that "In 1941, the Hindus made up about 21 per cent of the total population of Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 61, folio 48), and that "By early November of 1947, 246,000 Hindus had already left Sindh by sea, air or rail." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 62, folio 49). The Hindu Sindhis "started leaving Sindh especially after the Karachi riots in January 1948" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 66, folio not stated). The narrative of the Hindu Sufi Rochaldas, he writes, alludes to violence spread through Sindh that was "not perpetrated by the Muslim Sindhis, but by the Mohajirs who clearly wanted to take over the houses of the Hindus, at least in Karachi" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 70–71, folios 57–58). Schaflechner writes that "The departure of the Hindu elite and the influx of Muslim refugees (“Muhajirs”) from India dramatically altered the local demography" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 87, folio not stated). After 1947, Boivin records, "The darbars in Sindh were most of the time preserved with different arrangements regarding their management" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 68, folio 55).

**The Hindus of Sindh today.** Sindh, in Schaflechner's account, is "home to the majority of Pakistan's Hindus" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 13, folio not stated). Boivin writes: "Although a large majority of Hindus have migrated to India from 1947 onwards, Sindh is the province of Pakistan with the most significant Hindu population." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 176–177, folios not stated and 164). Khalid writes that by the census of 1998 "the Hindu minority is the largest minority in the country" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 87, folio not stated), and that "The majority of the Hindu community is based in Sindh and Southern Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 233, folio not stated). Schaflechner notes that "many middle- and upper-class Hindus in Sindh are followers of the Nanak paṃth" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 257, folio not stated), and Boivin that the formula *Sab ka malik ek hai* "has for some years been very popular among the Hindus of Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 186, folio 173).

**The older province.** Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian history, composed under Aurangzeb, describes the Mughal province of Thatta: its greatest river is the Sind, by which merchants of Multan and Bhakkar ship their goods down to Thatta (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529); its southern bound is the salt sea (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529); and its life turns on boats, which carry most of its traffic, and of which, small and great, there would be forty thousand (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 530). Its diet rests on rice, curds and fish (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 530).

**Sind in song.** Shemeem Burney Abbas records the singer Alan Faqir's words about the province: "It has been looted—it has been plundered—it has been assaulted—but it goes on—it has not died—it will not die—it has existed for centuries and it shall exist—Sindhri is beaten—and yet Sindhri goes on" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 156–157, folios not stated). "Sindhri", her note explains, is "A name used for Sind in the poetic traditions" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 210–211, folios not stated). Among the lines he recited:

*Punnu is not in the Kec / Sind is not the amir* (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 156, folio not stated)

Her notes explain that "'Sind is not the amir' means that Sind is not in the position of leadership or amirship", and that "These are all Alan Faqir's political interpretations of the female myths" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 210–211, folios not stated).

**Sufi Karachi.** Regula Burckhardt Qureshi counts the city, with Hyderabad in India, among "two important urban centres of Sufism", where members of the Qawwal Bachche lineage "actively maintain their special Qawwali heritage" (Qureshi, *Sufi Music of India and Pakistan*, p. 33, folio 13). Robert Rozehnal writes that "In Karachi, the order's current teaching shaykh, Siraj 'Ali Muhammad, leads weekly halqa (communal zikr sessions) on Thursday evenings." (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 165, folio 157). Abbas records the Sidi congregation held in April "in Karachi at Manghopir" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 70–71, folios not stated), and Annemarie Schimmel the alligator miracle "(as happened in Mango Pir near Karachi)" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 238, folio 207). Schimmel writes that the Hindu share in the Muslim orders is "more visible in Sind than in the Punjab": "Hindu writers wrote mystical poems in which they used the imagery of the Muslims; they composed poems in honor of the Prophet" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 418, folio 387).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The goddess's name.** The site's account writes Laxmi Narayan and Lakshmi and Narayan. Boivin prints "the temple of Lakhshmi Narayan" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 192, folio 179). Khalid writes "Laxmi" alone, without Narayan (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 80, folio not stated; Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 21, folio 3).
- **How many Hindus live in Pakistan and Sindh.** Schaflechner puts Pakistan's Hindus at "between 6 and 10 million people—an estimate that varies widely according to the source", of whom "over 90 percent" belong to the underprivileged tribes and sub-castes (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 13, folio not stated). Boivin writes that "the Sindhi Hindus number several millions, with a majority of them belonging to the so-called scheduled castes" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 175, folio 162). Khalid gives no figure, but cites the census of 1998 for the Hindus as the country's largest minority (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 87, folio not stated).
- **How many Hindus left Sindh at Partition.** Boivin reckons the Hindus of 1941 at "exactly 0.9 million", and the migrants at "closer to 0.8 million, if not less", while noting "a kind of consensus that they numbered around 1 million" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 61, folio 48).
- **Whether Sindh knew caste.** Schaflechner quotes Thakur that the "[c]aste system is almost absent in Sind", a perception he links to the Lohanas and to nationalist portrayals of Sindh as "a place of religious harmony and tolerance" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 277–278, folio not stated). Boivin writes that "Frequently, the claim arises that there was no caste system in Sindh", and that "Contrary to popular belief, there were many untouchables in Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 36–37, folio not stated / folio 24; Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 40, folio 27).
- **Whether Sindh was a land without communal strife.** Verkaaik, as Schaflechner quotes him, writes that "both Sindhi Hindus and Muslims were influenced by mysticism and religious tolerance. This was the Sindhi essence that both Hindus and Muslims shared" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 89, folio not stated). Schaflechner himself calls the notion of "the harmonious coexistence of the religions" in Sindh "merely a nationalist utopian ideal" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 90–91, folio not stated). Boivin writes that "even if it never reached the level of violence in Punjab, it is irrelevant to claim there was no communal violence in Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 62, folio 49).
- **The Lohanas' share of Sindh's Hindus.** Boivin writes that the Lohanas "represented half of the total Hindu population" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 40–41, folios 27–28). Schaflechner quotes McMurdo that "The Lowannas or Lohannas compose the great body of Hindus in Sindh", and Thakur that they form "the general body of the Sind Hindus" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 79, folio not stated; Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 80, folio not stated).

## Legacy

The Shri Laxmi Narayan Mandir endures as a small but singular monument: a living temple that has kept its place on the Karachi waterfront through two centuries of change, and that continues to serve the ritual needs of a community whose presence in the city long predates the creation of Pakistan. Its survival, secured in part through the courts, stands as a testament to the resilience of Karachi's Hindus and to the enduring importance of the sea in their devotional life.

In the scholarship, the temple's one appearance is as a witness to the shared religious culture of Sindh: a painting of Ramdeo Pir, one of the deified heroes of Sindh, standing in a Karachi temple of Lakshmi-Narayan (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 192, folio 179).
