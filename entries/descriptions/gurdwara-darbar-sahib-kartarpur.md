## Overview

Gurdwara Darbar Sahib Kartarpur stands on the right bank of the River Ravi in Narowal District, in Pakistan's Punjab province, close to the frontier with India. It marks the place where Guru Nanak, founder of Sikhism, passed the final years of his life and where, in 1539, he died — making it one of the most venerated sites in the Sikh world and, in a real sense, the birthplace of the Sikh community as a settled, worshipping body. Its white domes, long visible across the fields from the Indian side of the border, became in 2019 the terminus of the celebrated Kartarpur Corridor, a rare visa-free pilgrimage passage that reunited the shrine with the devotees of a divided Punjab.

Harish Dhillon, retelling the *Janamsakhis*, places it with some precision: "Kartarpur, which Guru Nanak founded and where he breathed his last on 22 September 1539, is at a distance of 80 km from Lahore and 180 km from Nanakana Sahib (earlier called Talwandi) via Lahore and only about 2 km from the Indo-Pak border on the right bank of river Ravi" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 175, folio "l92" [text-layer?] [192]). The shrine, he adds, stands out because there is no habitation around it, and the nearby railway station bears its name, Darbar Sahib Kartarpur, on the Lahore–Chak Amru line, in Shakkar Garh, district Narowal (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 175, folio "l92" [text-layer?] [192]; p. 176, folio 193). Dhillon is careful to distinguish it from "the Kartarpur town that today flourishes about 15 km from Jalandhar", which he says was founded in 1594 by Guru Arjan Dev (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 175, folio "l92" [text-layer?] [192]).

Haroon Khalid, who visited with the historian Iqbal Qaiser, calls it Kartarpur Sahib, "about an hour away from the city of Narowal", on the Ravi (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 302, folio 276), and at the gate he wrote: "This was Kartarpur Sahib, the final resting abode of Guru Nanak and here, Muslims were allowed to enter." (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 302, folio 276). In another book he says simply that Guru Nanak "passed away in a small village called Kartarpur also in present day Pakistan" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 168, folio not stated). Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi's history of Sufism in India records that "Guru Nanak died at Kartarpur in 1539" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 418, folio 394), but describes no building there.

## Guru Nanak's Final Years

Guru Nanak is said to have founded the settlement of Kartarpur — "the abode of the Creator" — around 1504 on land beside the Ravi, and, after the long missionary journeys known as his *udasis*, to have settled there for the last years of his life; by many accounts he spent some eighteen years at the site. Here the wandering teacher became the founder of a living community. Setting aside the ascetic's road, he took up the life of a householder and farmer, and gathered around him a *sangat*, or congregation, of disciples.

In Dhillon's retelling, Nanak went to "the region between the rivers Ravi and Beas", and on the banks of the Ravi chose land for his permanent home. A farmer of Pakhoke village, Ajit Randhawa, "along with a group of other farmers, pledged this piece of land", and "the foundations of a new settlement, Kartarpur (the abode of God) were laid" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 175, folio "l92" [text-layer?] [192]). People "of every faith and religion" came, and the place soon took on the look of a pilgrim town, like Talwandi and Sultanpur before it; before the Guru set out again, his own parents and Mardana's came to live there (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 176, folio 193). The settlement was not yet his last home. After "a few months in Kartarpur, Nanak resumed his third journey" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 184, folio 203), and in Dhillon's account it was at Kartarpur that the fourth and last *udasi* ended (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 225, folio 247). There the Guru laid aside the pilgrim's clothes and took the garb of an ordinary householder (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 227, folio 251).

Khalid tells it in similar terms. "Having spent twenty-four years of his life travelling, Nanak had finally settled down at a place called Kartarpur on the banks of the Ravi. Here he had started working on a piece of land that was given to him by one of his devotees"; he was joined by "his parents and also his wife and children", devotees moved there too, and "Using Kartarpur as his base, Nanak now started travelling to nearby regions" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 288, folio 262). Elsewhere: "He sent for his family from Talwindi [OCR?] [Talwandi] and started living at this small village called Kartarpur" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 296, folio 270). In the back matter of the same book: "After his travels he settled at Kartarpur" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 314, folio 288). Khalid's itinerary has Nanak visit Uch on his way to Sindh towards the end of his travels, when "He had now settled at Kartarpur in Punjab and would travel occasionally to neighbouring areas" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 278, folio 252); that passage does not itself say which Kartarpur is meant. Dhillon too has the Guru, in his last years, cross the Ravi south-east to Achal, a village near Batala in Gurdaspur district, for the festival of Maha Shivratri (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 240–241, folio 265 / not stated).

The Persian *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* of Sujan Rai Bhandari of Batala, composed in the fortieth regnal year of Aurangzeb, tells of the settling without naming the place: after travelling most of the world, Baba Nanak came to the town of Batala, married, and settled in a village of Batala on the Ravi (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). The same page records a place of Baba Nanak twelve *kos* from Batala on the bank of the Ravi, where, it says, his descendants still live (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). Bhandari gives the place no name, and his account is not tied to this site; it is given here as a pointer only.

It was at Kartarpur that the enduring institutions of Sikhism took shape. The Guru enjoined upon his followers the threefold discipline still summarised as *kirat karo* (to earn an honest living), *vand chhako* (to share one's earnings with the needy) and *naam japo* (to remember the divine Name). He instituted the *langar*, the communal kitchen where all — regardless of caste, creed or station — sat together in a single row, or *pangat*, to eat; and he established the daily rhythm of collective devotion, the singing of hymns at dawn and dusk. Kartarpur was thus the first Sikh commune and the model upon which every later gurdwara has been built.

Dhillon names three great traditions established at Kartarpur: *seva*, or selfless service; *kirtan*, or religious singing; and *langar*, the free kitchen (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 228, folio 252). At Kartarpur, he writes, these traditions became firmly entrenched (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 229, folio 253). The Guru's compositions were sung at the appropriate times of the day to the accompaniment of Mardana's *rabaab* (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 229, folio 253). In the *langar*, everyone who could drew water, ground wheat, gathered wood, cooked, served and scrubbed, and the kitchen came to foster the spirit of equality, humility and brotherhood (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 229, folio 253); people brought whatever they could for the evening meal (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 184, folio 203). By the end of the Guru's travels the settlement "had by now grown into a full-fledged township" of people from different social backgrounds, castes and creeds (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 227, folio 251), yet its people were by no means members of a religious or monastic order: each went about an ordinary, everyday occupation (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 227, folio 251). The Guru and his disciples worked the fields; there were *sevadars* with bullock-carts, and a *dharamshala* whose newly built wing nearly collapsed in the monsoon until Lehna led an overnight repair (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 252–254, folios not stated / 281).

Dhillon calls the settlement a *dera*, a camp or settlement. Duni Chand of Lahore was among its greatest contributors (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 134, folio 148); a Brahmin of one of the stories came to the place where the *dera* was being built (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 178, folio 195); and disciples flocked there and made their homes in it (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 240, folio 265). Its people were of two kinds: "the original inhabitants of Kartarpur, who had come and settled here, when the Guru had first earmarked the site", and new settlers from the follower communities along the routes of his *udasis* (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 230, folio 254).

Khalid's account of these years is partly his own reconstruction, which he marks as such. "It had now been seventeen years that Nanak had been leading a sedentary life. Hundreds of devotees from neighbouring villages and beyond came to visit him every day"; there was *kirtan* every evening, and "eventually a new hall was constructed which could host the huge gathering. This place was reserved for the evening prayers and was referred to as a Gurdwara by Nanak, where the Ultimate Guru resided" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 298, folio 272). In the reconstruction Nanak lives "in a simple hut that was built of wood and hay" and walks each dawn "towards the Ravi that flowed close to his hut", where he had bathed "for the past seventeen years" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 293, folio 267). At the evening gathering his wife Sulakhni and other women "prepared for the langar which concluded the evening", and when Nanak told her "that Muslims and even untouchables would be welcome to the langar", the idea at first "repulsed her" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 296, folio 270). In the same reconstruction Nanak wishes he could have buried Mardana at "Kartarpur, his final abode"; the passage is damaged in the printed text (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 293, folio 267).

## The Passing of the Guru

Before his death, Guru Nanak set aside his own sons in favour of a devoted disciple, Bhai Lehna, whom he renamed Angad — "of my own limb" — and installed as the second Guru, thereby founding the line of succession by spiritual merit rather than heredity that would run through ten Gurus. Guru Nanak died at Kartarpur, by tradition on 22 September 1539.

Rizvi writes: "Guru Nanak died at Kartarpur in 1539. Before his death he appointed Lehna his successor" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 418, folio 394). He describes the succession in the language of a Sufi discipleship: "Although Lehna came from a rich family, after becoming the Guru's disciple, in accordance with sufi traditions, he was made to perform such humiliating tasks as carrying loads of wet grass" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 418, folio 394). His index gives the Guru's dates as born 1469 and died 1539 (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 466, folio 442).

In Dhillon's retelling Lehna, on pilgrimage to Jwalamukhi, reached Kartarpur and stayed, working in the fields, at a construction site and in the *langar* (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 248–249, folio not stated / 276); he was ordained "the second Guru of the Sikhs, Guru Angad Dev" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 255, folio 282). Khalid records that Guru Angad "became a disciple of Guru Nanak after he had settled at Kartarpur Sahib" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 314, folio 288), and that "On his deathbed, he appointed not his son but his most loyal student as his spiritual successor" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 16, folio not stated). "Guru Nanak preferred Angad Dev over his son Sri Chand" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 273, folio not stated), and he asked Angad Dev to move to Khadur rather than stay at Kartarpur Sahib, where Nanak was settled, "to avoid the ire of his sons" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 290, folio 264).

Khalid's reconstruction of the succession has Bhai Lehna of Khadur, "Born into an orthodox family", come to Kartarpur Sahib instead of "the temple of Durga where he was headed" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 297, folio 271). The Guru's sons have disappointed him: Lachman has become a businessman, and Sri Chand is "highly inspired by the yogis" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 297, folio 271). Sri Chand objects that Lehna "still worships Durga in his house", and Sulakhni urges her husband, "You should listen to your blood" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 300, folio 274). Nanak tells Sri Chand that "it is not in your destiny to be my spiritual descendant. That has to be Bhai Lehna", walks by the currents of the Ravi, and returns "to the gurdwara he had constructed", where Lehna and Baba Budha wait (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 301, folio 275). He calls Lehna "my angad (of one's body)", has Baba Budha apply saffron paste to Lehna's forehead, lays five copper coins and a coconut at his feet, and announces: "I have appointed Bhai Lehna, who is from now on to be known as Guru Angad, as my successor" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 301–302, folios 275–276).

Bhandari's Persian account, composed under Aurangzeb, records the same choice. Baba Nanak had a worthy son named Lakhmi Das, but because spiritual fortune was not his lot the Guru passed him over, gave a Khatri named Lahna, known by a second name the print gives uncertainly as perhaps Tarhan, the title of Guru Angad, and made him his successor at the moment of death (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). Bhandari dates the death to the reign of Salim Shah Afghan, at an age between seventy and eighty (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).

A cherished legend, preserved in the *Janamsakhi* tradition, surrounds his passing. His Hindu and Muslim followers, it is said, fell to disputing whether his body should be cremated or buried; but when the sheet covering him was lifted, only a heap of flowers remained. Each community took its share — the one to cremate, the other to inter — and each raised a memorial. To this day the shrine is understood to hold both a *samadhi* and a grave, side by side, a fitting emblem for a teacher who preached the unity beneath the divisions of religion.

Dhillon tells the end in his own way. Nanak lay dying under a tree on the bank of the river Ravi, just outside Kartarpur (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 257, folio 285). His Muslim followers claimed the body for burial — "He is ours, our peer" — and his Hindu followers claimed it for cremation (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 257–260, folios 285–288; p. 258, folio 286). The flowers laid beside the dying Guru by Muslims and by Hindus were both found unwithered the next day (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 258–260, folios 286–288). Dhillon gives the date in a bracket: "(The date: 22 September 1539.)" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 259, folio 287). "Even in death, Nanak had underscored the lesson that he had taught all his life: All human beings are equal" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 260, folio 288).

Khalid opens *Walking with Nanak* with Kartarpur: the story of Nanak, the Gurus and the author himself converges at Kartarpur Sahib, "where Guru Nanak lay on his deathbed" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 18, folio not stated); that line alone does not say which Kartarpur. He also writes that Nanak spent much of his life in present-day Pakistan and "eventually settled in a small village which is now on the Pakistani side of the border" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 16, folio not stated).

## The Gurdwara

The original memorial by the Ravi was, over the centuries, repeatedly threatened by the shifting course of the river, which is traditionally said to have swept away earlier structures. The present white-domed gurdwara dates from the early twentieth century and is said to have been built with the patronage of the Maharaja of Patiala. In the run-up to 2019 the complex was extensively restored and enlarged, its precincts expanded to receive great numbers of visitors; by some reckonings it now ranks among the largest gurdwaras in the world. Within, the *Guru Granth Sahib* is installed and read, and *langar* is served to all who come.

Khalid's account describes the shrine as he found it on a Sunday morning with Iqbal Qaiser, some years before the corridor; the year of the visit is not given. "This is the border area; hence the roads are in a bad condition", he writes, and a mud track leads "towards the Ravi, which still flows next to the shrine". On his first visit Iqbal Qaiser had had to walk the three kilometres from the main road to the gurdwara, through partly flooded rice paddies (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 302, folio 276). At the gate policemen sat on a *charpoy* drinking tea, and an old Muslim man and his wife from a neighbouring village walked in "without any trouble"; a policeman recognised Iqbal Qaiser and waved the car through (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 302, folio 276; pp. 302–303, folios 276–277).

"The main shrine was protected by a newly constructed wall." Beside the parking stood what appeared to be a missile preserved in a glass case with a marble plaque, and next to it a well covered with marble, whose plaque said it "was the well that was used by Guru Nanak to water his fields"; sugarcane grew nearby, and a peasant was working the fields (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 303, folio 277). Visitors took off their shoes and covered their heads "with skullcaps that were placed next to the entrance". "The main structure was in the middle of a courtyard, while around it there were rooms meant to be used to house pilgrims." (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 303, folio 277)

The glass door "that led into the main shrine, where the samadhi of Nanak was situated" was locked. "The main building was constructed around the samadhi, whereas the grave was on one side of the building" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 305, folio 279). "There was a grave next to the main shrine", in the open, with a couplet etched on it; the printed description of the grave is damaged, and the couplet is not given (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 304, folio 278). The *granthi*, Inderjit, told Khalid: "There is a grave of Nanak next to his samadhi" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 307, folio 281). Khalid put it to Iqbal Qaiser that the grave might instead be Bhai Mardana's, "next to the samadhi of his Guru" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 304, folio 278).

Iqbal Qaiser told him that "the original building was destroyed in a flood several years ago and this new one was built later"; Khalid adds: "The grave of Nanak became a casualty of the growing Muslim-Sikh antagonism." (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 305, folio 279). On Iqbal Qaiser's first visit the place had not yet become the shrine it is today: it "used to be a centre for smugglers because of its proximity to the border. Drug addicts too had established a place for themselves" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 307–308, folios 281–282). Inderjit dated the present establishment: "We established this gurdwara in 2001", when, he said, they renovated the structure: "We also prepared our first langar here at that time." (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 308, folio 282). At the time of Khalid's visit there was only the shrine and a few rooms reserved for the caretakers; a *langar* hall and a pool were soon to come (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 309, folio not stated).

The building's style was itself disputed. Inderjit said: "A lot of Sikhs from England and Canada have complained that the architecture of the shrine is not, strictly speaking, Sikh"; it has many features of Muslim architecture, and its arches "were plain. This was a feature of Muslim architecture. Sikh arches were usually three-cusped." (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 309, folio not stated). Khalid asked: "Had Nanak supervised the construction of his own shrine, would he have allowed Muslim influences in the building, which the Sikh Diaspora now complained against?" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 310, folio 284)

The frontier lies to the east, across the river. The Ravi, Khalid writes, "sometimes crosses into India while at other points it comes back to Pakistan. Otherwise, the river is parched throughout the year", and had Radcliffe erred "by only a few centimetres, the shrine of Kartarpur Sahib would have ended up in India" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 307, folio 281).

## The Granthi

The *granthi* "nowadays", Khalid writes, is Inderjit, who was "in the camps"; a young man whom Khalid took to be a government official, and probably a Muslim, told him "No photography" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 305, folio 279). Inderjit's shed had been built recently with the help of the Sikh diaspora, and held a double bed, a sofa set and a television showing the news of floods (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 305–306, folios 279–280). His family "belonged to a village in Faisalabad district. They weren't Sikhs but Nanak Panthi Hindus"; he himself wore the garb of a baptised Sikh of the Khalsa. At Partition his grandfather, on his way to India, was stopped near the border by the Muslims of their village, who promised him security, and the family lived there peacefully until the war of 1965, when it moved to Nankana; Inderjit would not talk about that time (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 306, folio 280). Khalid notes that Inderjit was now a government official living under its protection, and speaks of "the Pakistani establishment which regularly monitors the activities of Sikhs in Pakistan" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 306–307, folios 280–281).

## Devotion and Practice

The devotion Khalid saw was not only Sikh. The Muslim couple who had walked in at the gate prayed at the grave; the woman touched it with her hand and passed it over her head, "blessing herself" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 304, folio 278). Inderjit said local Muslims "come here regularly and offer their fateha (first verse of the Quran, used as a prayer) just like they would at any Muslim shrine", and regard Nanak as a saint, the word being damaged in the printed text (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 307, folio 281). Iqbal Qaiser added that local people still came to the grave, brought offerings and cleaned the shrine as a service, a tradition "Prevalent even before Partition" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 307–308, folios 281–282).

The *langar*, Inderjit said, depended on the neighbours: "rich Muslim families of the neighbouring villages provide us with monetary support to cook langar. This is their offering to the shrine", and "The wood that we require for cooking langar is provided to us by the Rangers". At first Muslims "were hesitant to eat food cooked by Sikhs", but "Today, more Muslims eat food here compared to Sikhs." (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 308, folio 282)

What Khalid's *granthi* described is the institution the site's own account traces to the Guru. "It is believed that the tradition of Langar began with Guru Nanak", Khalid writes elsewhere (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 188, folio not stated). Bhandari, writing of the Guru's followers in general and not of this place, says that they count service of the traveller, of whoever comes and goes, as the highest worship, and that whoever arrives at midnight speaking Baba Nanak's name is treated as a brother and a friend and served, even a stranger, a thief or a highwayman (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 519).

## Pilgrimage and the Kartarpur Corridor

The Partition of 1947 left Kartarpur in Pakistan and the town of Dera Baba Nanak, only a few kilometres away, in India — near enough that Indian pilgrims long gathered at a raised viewing platform on their side of the border simply to gaze at the domes through binoculars. The idea of a dedicated corridor was aired as early as 1999, but it was for the 550th anniversary of Guru Nanak's birth that it finally came to pass. Foundation stones were laid on both sides in November 2018, and the corridor was inaugurated on 9 November 2019, when a first *jatha* of several hundred Indian pilgrims crossed. The passage allows Indian Sikhs to visit the shrine visa-free, returning the same day, and has been widely hailed as a rare gesture of goodwill between two often estranged neighbours.

Khalid saw the far side of that divide before the corridor opened. Across the border, behind a fence, "Indian pilgrims, who gather there to perform darshan of the shrine of Guru Nanak" looked through binoculars, and "Hundreds of people gather there every day" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 308, folio 282). "There are talks to declare this region a peace corridor", under which Indian pilgrims would enter without a visa; "The Pakistani government has given its approval to the Proposal; however the Indian government is not ready to implement the deal", saying it would be used by terrorists. The speaker appears to be Iqbal Qaiser, and the printed text is damaged here (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 308–309, folio 282 / folio not stated).

## Legends and Miracles

Several stories attach to the site. The plaque beside the parking says that the missile in its glass case "was dropped here by the Indian forces in 1971 but due to the miracle of Guru Nanak, it did not blow up" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 303, folio 277). Inderjit told Khalid of a belief among local Muslims, which Khalid calls a myth: "the river floods every twenty odd years and comes right up to the shrine. This is the river paying tribute to the shrine of Nanak." (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 307, folio 281). He spoke too of a "giant snake" "bigger than two humans combined", and said that snakes found in the fields are placed beside the shrine: "After paying their respects they return. All they want to do is to catch a sight of the shrine." (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 309, folio not stated)

Dhillon retells stories set in the early settlement. In the story of a Brahmin and his "kitchen" at the *dera*, a cubit of consecrated ground was dug and bones were found; the Brahmin relented and asked to share in the *langar* (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 178–182, folios 195–199; bones p. 180, folio 197). Moola Keer, "One of the earliest settlers in Kartarpur", covered for a guest who had stolen his wife's jewellery, and the thief confessed after the *kirtan* (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 230–237, folios 254–261; p. 236 folio not stated). And at the end, the flowers laid beside the dying Guru by both communities were found unwithered (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 258–260, folios 286–288).

The flower has its own life in the region's stories. Omar Kasmani, citing Michel Boivin, notes that the trope of the flower in milk, "a key trope in Sufi narratives of the Indus valley", is told also of "Guru Nanak, the spiritual leader and founder of Sikhism" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 195, folio 178, ch. 5 n. 17); he ties it to no place.

## In Verse

The books quote no verse composed at Kartarpur or inscribed there, and the couplet Khalid saw etched on the grave is not given in his text (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 304, folio 278). But the settlement, in both Dhillon's and Khalid's accounts, was a place where the Guru's verse was sung daily: at the appropriate times of the day to Mardana's *rabaab*, in Dhillon's telling (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 229, folio 253), and as *kirtan* every evening, in Khalid's (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 298, folio 272). Dhillon writes that Nanak's "sermon was his Bani" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 228–229, folios 252–253), and that the *Bani* "was compiled as part of the Adi Granth by Guru Arjan Dev, the fifth Guru, in 1604" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 9, folio 9). In Khalid's reconstruction it is at Kartarpur that Bhai Lehna decides to collect the poetry of Nanak in written form, at a time when Punjabi was an oral language with no script; the passage is damaged in the printed text (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 299, folio 273).

Bhandari, in Persian, says the Guru composed verses in Hindi on the truth of God and set out divine unity in clear expressions and shining metaphors (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). A singer named Mardana sang his verses and drew people into the net of faith (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). Of the Guru's followers he writes that the sum of their worship is the reading of their master's verses, which they sing with song and melody (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 519).

Khalid quotes the Guru's verse throughout *Walking with Nanak*, in published English translations. None of the lines he quotes is tied to Kartarpur; they are given here as the poetry of the Guru who sang at this place. The *aarti*, in which Iqbal Qaiser reads a God residing in nature, calling Nanak "a naturalist", begins in one translation:

*The sky the salver, the sun and moon the lamps …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 55, folio 37)

and in another:

*In the salver of the firmament, / The sun and the moon shine as lamps* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 243–244, folios 217–218)

On the oneness of God:

*My Master is One / He is One, brother, the only One* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 60, folio 42)

*God is One, / The Path is One* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 144, folio 126)

The poem on the sacred thread, the *janeu*, which in Khalid's reconstruction the boy Nanak refused:

*From the cotton of compassion, / Spin the thread of contentment* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 61–62, folios 43–44)

On washing:

*As hands or feet besmirched with slime, / Water washes white* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 192, folio 166)

Other openings Khalid quotes: *O my merchant friend* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 56–58, folio 40); *Launch ye the boat of austere contemplation …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 148–149, folios 130–131); *As a beggar goes a-begging …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 158, folio 140); and *His goodness cannot be priced or traded …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 280, folio 254). The first poem of Nanak that Khalid read was the *Babur Bani*, "a long poem that Nanak wrote at the time the Mughal marauder Babur attacked Punjab, destroying the cities of Eminabad and Lahore on the way" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 15, folio not stated), in which, he writes, Nanak "lays the blame of Babur's wrath at God's feet" and "challenges the divinity of the Divine" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 15–16, folio not stated). He quotes from it the line 'Coming in seventy-eight (1521 C.E)' (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 113–114, folios 95–96).

The verse has travelled beyond the gurdwaras. Shemeem Burney Abbas defines the *banī* by him: "Devotional verses such as those of Guru Nanak; verses of supplication, entreaty, humility, and reverence" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 179, folio not stated); she notes that Kabir and Nanak used the *dohā*, a rhyming couplet in an old Prakrit and Hindi metre, "to write their mystical poetry" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 141, folio not stated), and quotes the singer Abida Parvin: "I sing Shah Hussain, Bulle Shah, Mian Muhammad Bakhsh, Hazrat Khawaja Ghulam Farid, Sacchal Sarmast, Baba Farid, and Guru Nanak" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 54, folio not stated). In Sindh, Michel Boivin records, "Sain Vasan Shah became famous for reciting the banis of Shri Guru Nanak and the kalams of the Sufis" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 68, folio 55), and he reports Ajwani's judgement that "Guru Nanak was the greatest poet of the Bhakti" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 90, folio 77). Khalid writes that Nanak brought into his poetry words that were not part of the Punjabi of his time, "which is why Nanak's contribution as a poet to Punjabi is immense" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 278–279, folios 252–253). Annemarie Schimmel, writing of the province after 1800, observes that Panjabi "was used almost exclusively by the Sikhs as their literary medium, whereas the Panjabi Muslims resorted to Urdu" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 427, folio 396).

## Guru Nanak in the Wider Record

What the books say of Guru Nanak's life before Kartarpur is said of the Guru, not of this site; it is gathered here because the shrine exists for his sake. Dhillon calls the *Janamsakhis* "cycles of tales purportedly drawn from the life of the first Guru of the Sikhs" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 9, folio 9), and has the Guru born "On 14 April 1469" to Kalyan Chand Das Bedi, the accountant Mehta Kalu (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 17–18, folios 19–20). Khalid gives his dates as 1469–1539 and calls him "the first Guru" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 314, folio 288); "Guru Nanak was born in a city now called Nankana Sahib, Pakistan. He passed away in a small village called Kartarpur also in present day Pakistan. He spent most of his time here, and there are several sites all over the country where Nanak stayed or performed miracles. Gurdwaras that were built there later by the devotees still stand tall" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 168–169, folio not stated). Bhandari reports, prefacing it with "it is said", a birth in the year 1526 of Bikramajit, corresponding to 880 AH, in the time of Sultan Bahlul Lodi (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).

Dhillon has the Guru married "on 24 September 1487" to Sulakhini, daughter of Mul Chand and Chando Rani of the town of Batala, and gives the births of their sons, Sri Chand on 8 September 1494 and Lakhmi Das on 12 February 1497 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 44, folio 49). In Khalid's reconstruction Sulakhni is "the daughter of Moolchand Khatri from the village of Pakhoke near Gurdaspur" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 67, folio 49), and Iqbal Qaiser tells him that the Guru's two sons were Sri Chand and Sri Lachman, that Sri Chand "became an ascetic and never married", and that the lineage "is therefore preserved through his younger son" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 44, folio 26). The Udasi sect "is believed to have been founded by the eldest son of Guru Nanak, Sri Chand", who, "Ignored by his father when it was time to appoint the next Guru", founded his own (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 36, folio 18). Boivin too names Shri Chand as "the eldest son of Guru Nanak who was not recognized as guru by the mainstream Sikh community", whose tradition the Udasis follow (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 53, folio 40).

Dhillon sets out two schemes of the *udasis*. "According to the Puratan Janamsakhi… he undertook five journeys", the first in 1499–1506 and the last in 1523–1524 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 54–55, folios 60–61); "More modern accounts" give four, spanning twenty-three years: 1499–1505, 1506–1509, 1514–1516 and 1518–1521 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 55, folio 61). Dhillon himself holds that there were four (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 56, folio 62). In his telling the fourth journey went by Multan, Sukkur, Thatta and Hinglaj, across the Arabian Sea to Mecca, Medina and Baghdad, and home by Iran, Kabul, Peshawar, Kandahar, Hasan Abdal, Tilla Balnath, Katasraj and Saidpur to Kartarpur (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 194, folio 214; pp. 224–225, folios 246–247). Khalid gives the travels as some twenty-four years, mostly on foot, east to Bengal, south to Sri Lanka, north to Kashmir, Nepal and Tibet, and west "to Mecca and returned to settle at Kartarpur" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 54, folio 36).

Rizvi sets the Guru's return in its politics: coming back from eastern India, he found "the Panjab plunged into a number of significant political crises", as Babur invaded a third time "over the Panjab in 926/1520 reaching as far as Sialkot" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 409, folio 385). Rizvi also quotes, in order to reject it, W. H. McLeod's judgement in *Guru Nanak and the Sikh religion* that "the Panjabi sufism of Guru Nanak's period had evidently departed radically from the classical pattern of Arab and Persian Sufism" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 419–420, folios 395–396); against it he argues that "all literate Panjabis, both Hindus and Muslims, at the time of Guru Nanak were conversant with" ʿAttar, Rumi and Saʿdi (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 420, folio 396).

Muslim writers and teachers have claimed the Guru too. Abbas writes that Baba Farid's "texts are revered among the Sikhs because he influenced the founder of their own religion, Baba Guru Nanak" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 87, folio not stated), and Khalid that Farid "became an inspiration for Guru Nanak when he visited the shrine during his travels" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated). Robert Rozehnal reports that a twentieth-century Chishti Sabiri shaykh, in certain lectures, describes "non-Muslim spiritual luminaries—Krishna, Rama, Sita, Lachman, and Guru Nanak—as believers in the one true God and religious leaders with 'Divine connection' (nisbat)" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 63, folio 53). Khalid writes that "many Muslims, acquainted with the teachings of Guru Nanak, are devoted to him too and visit his shrine" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 220, folio not stated), and his informant Nadeem, a Muslim painter, says "Guru Nanak is like a Muslim Sufi Saint" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 219, folio not stated). Among Hindus, the Nanak Panthis "are Hindus who believe in the teachings of Guru Nanak, but not in the Khalsa" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 197, folio not stated) — the community from which, Khalid learned, Kartarpur's own *granthi* came (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 306, folio 280). Far to the south-west, at the shrine of the goddess Hinglaj, Jürgen Schaflechner records "the engraved footsteps of Guru Nanak" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 256, folio not stated).

Several writers set the Guru against the miracles told of him. Dhillon writes of "Guru Nanak's clear and emphatic disavowal of the miraculous" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 11, folio 11), and describes him as "a householder in every sense of the word" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 12, folio not stated) — the life the *Janamsakhis* place at Kartarpur. Khalid writes that Nanak "abhorred the concept of associating miracles with religious personalities", yet "his biography is nothing but a story of his miracles" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 16, folio not stated). "Some scholars have argued that the beauty of Nanak's message is that through his own example, he glorified peasantry" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 303, folio 277), Khalid notes in his Kartarpur chapter, beside the well said to have watered the Guru's fields.

## Setting

Writing of the province rather than of this site, Khalid records that "When Maharaja Ranjit Singh became the sovereign of Punjab at the end of the eighteenth century he spent large amounts of money on the renovation and expansion of many gurdwaras" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 34, folio 16). Of the years after 1947 he writes, of Pakistan generally and framed by the division of the Punjab: "I have seen several gurdwaras and temples being taken over and destroyed by the migrants" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 8, ch. 2, folio not stated). On the day he visited Inderjit, the television was "covering the floods in Sindh and lower Punjab" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 306, folio 280).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The founding.** The site's account says the Guru founded Kartarpur around 1504. Dhillon gives no year; his retelling sets the founding after the end of the second *udasi* and a few months before the third (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 172, folio 188; p. 175, folio "l92" [text-layer?] [192]; p. 184, folio 203), journeys which the modern scheme he summarises dates 1506–1509 and 1514–1516 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 55, folio 61). Khalid has Nanak settle at Kartarpur after twenty-four years of travelling (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 288, folio 262).
- **The name.** The site's account renders Kartarpur "the abode of the Creator"; Dhillon, "the abode of God" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 175, folio "l92" [text-layer?] [192]).
- **How long he lived there.** The site's account says some eighteen years. Khalid's reconstruction speaks of seventeen years of sedentary life (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 298, folio 272; p. 293, folio 267).
- **How long he travelled.** Khalid gives about twenty-four years (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 54, folio 36; p. 288, folio 262); Dhillon's modern scheme spans twenty-three (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 55, folio 61), and his Puratan scheme has five journeys, not four (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 54–55, folios 60–61).
- **Which son was passed over.** The site's account says the Guru set aside his own sons. Bhandari names Lakhmi Das as the son passed over (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520); Khalid names Sri Chand (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 273, folio not stated; p. 301, folio 275).
- **Where Lehna was going.** Dhillon has Lehna on pilgrimage to Jwalamukhi when he reached Kartarpur (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 248–249, folio not stated / 276); Khalid's reconstruction has him headed for "the temple of Durga" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 297, folio 271).
- **The numbering of Guru Angad.** The site's account and Dhillon make Angad the second Guru (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 255, folio 282). Rizvi writes "As the First Guru he was known as Guru Angad (1539-52)" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 419, folio 395).
- **How the death is dated.** The site's account gives 22 September 1539 "by tradition", and Dhillon gives the same day (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 259, folio 287); Rizvi gives the year 1539 (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 418, folio 394). Bhandari gives no year, dating the death to the reign of Salim Shah Afghan, at an age between seventy and eighty (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).
- **Where he died.** The site's account and Rizvi place the death at Kartarpur (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 418, folio 394). Dhillon has the Guru die under a tree on the bank of the Ravi, "just outside Kartarpur" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 257, folio 285).
- **The flowers.** In the site's account, when the sheet was lifted only a heap of flowers remained, and each community took its share. In Dhillon's retelling the flowers laid beside the dying Guru by Muslims and Hindus were both found unwithered the next day (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 258–260, folios 286–288).
- **Whose grave.** The site's account has a *samadhi* and a grave side by side, raised by the two communities. Inderjit calls the grave Nanak's (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 307, folio 281); Khalid suggests it might be Bhai Mardana's (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 304, folio 278).
- **Any memorial at all.** Rizvi records the Guru's death at Kartarpur but mentions no *samadhi*, tomb or building there, and elsewhere states flatly that Guru Nanak left no memorial (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 413, folio 389).
- **The present building.** The site's account dates the present gurdwara to the early twentieth century, under the patronage of the Maharaja of Patiala, with earlier structures traditionally swept away by the river. Iqbal Qaiser told Khalid that the original building was destroyed in a flood "several years ago" and a new one built later (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 305, folio 279); Inderjit said "We established this gurdwara in 2001" and renovated the structure then (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 308, folio 282).
- **The Guru's wife.** Dhillon calls her Sulakhini, of the town of Batala (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 44, folio 49); Khalid's reconstruction calls her Sulakhni, of the village of Pakhoke near Gurdaspur (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 67, folio 49).
- **The younger son's name.** Dhillon and Bhandari call him Lakhmi Das (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 44, folio 49; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520); Khalid calls him Lachman Das and Sri Lachman (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 68, folio 50; p. 44, folio 26).

## Legacy

For Sikhs, Kartarpur is where their Guru laid down the pattern of a whole way of life — work, worship and shared bread — and where he closed his earthly journey. The opening of the corridor transformed a shrine long visible but unreachable into a place of living pilgrimage once more, drawing thousands across the border and restoring Kartarpur to its central place in the devotional geography of the Panth.

Khalid's book about the Guru opens with Kartarpur: the story of Nanak, he writes, converges at Kartarpur Sahib (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 18, folio not stated); and at the shrine itself he asks what the Guru would have made of the building raised over him (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 310, folio 284). He found Kartarpur a place shared across the line drawn in 1947: Muslims from the neighbouring villages praying at the grave, paying for the *langar*, and eating at it in greater numbers than Sikhs (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 304, folio 278; p. 308, folio 282). Dhillon draws the same lesson from the Guru's death there: "Even in death, Nanak had underscored the lesson that he had taught all his life: All human beings are equal" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 260, folio 288).
