## Overview

At Mozang Chungi on Queens Road, Lahore, not far from Fatima Jinnah Medical University, lies the shrine of Shah Inayat Qadiri — one of the most historically consequential Sufi figures of Mughal Punjab, not chiefly for his own writings, considerable though they were, but as the spiritual guide (*murshid*) of Bulleh Shah, arguably the most celebrated Punjabi Sufi poet of all.

The books that speak of him know him first as that guide. Christopher Shackle, translating Bulleh Shah's lyrics, calls him "noted Sufi master Shah Inayat (d. 1728)" and writes that the most important "focus of Bullhe Shah's love is his spiritual guide, Shah Inayat" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 8, folio vii; p. 18, folio xvii). Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori, writing in 1875, enters him as "شاہ عنایت قادری شطاری لاہوری" and says that his radiant *mazar* is in Lahore (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 75–76, folios 63 and 74; p. 76, folio 74), and Lahori's modern editor gathers his full name from his own writings, with his teachers and his works. Several of the other books name him only through his disciple; most give him no tomb, some no date, and one places him at Uch. Their differences are set out side by side at the end.

## The Life of the Saint

Born around 1643 in Kasur into a farming family of the Arain community, Shah Inayat trained under a Shattari Sufi teacher — named as Muhammad Ali Raza Shattari, and also reported as Hazrat Shah Raza Qadri Shattari; the two names are close but not identical, so both are recorded rather than one being silently preferred. It is reported that his given name was Abul-Muarif Hanfi Qadri and that he had memorised the Qur'an by age five, and he is reported to bear the titles Sheikh-ul-Mashaikh, Shah-e-Lahore, and Mufakkir-e-Pakistan.

Lahori's editor, Muhammad Iqbal Mujaddidi, takes his full name from two of the saint's own works, *Zail al-Aghlat* and *Ghayat al-Hawashi*: "حافظ ابوالمعارف محمد عنایت اللہ حنفی قادری شطاری". He names his father as Pir Muhammad; his teachers in the exoteric sciences as Maulana Sayyid Abu al-Nasr, called Sayyid Ilyas, and Maulvi Abdul Hadi Lahori; and those from whom he took inner benefit as Shah Ali Raza Faruqi, a great-grandson of the Mujaddid Alf-e Sani, whose death he gives as 1141 AH / 1728 CE, and Shaikh Muhammad Sultan Bukhari. After 1111 AH, the editor writes, he attached himself to Shah Raza Qadri Shattari Lahori, who died in 1118 AH / 1707 CE (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 75–76, folios 63 and 74, حاشیہ 1). Lahori's own text makes him one of Shah Raza Qadri Lahori's *khalifas*, of great rank, comprehensive in the exoteric and the esoteric sciences (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 75, folio 63).

Kanhaiya Lal's history of Lahore gives him a different master. Writing of Shah Zia Qadri, whose ancient and blessed *mazar* he places in Mohalla Nasbi [OCR?] within the Taksali Darwaza, he says that this elder was of the "خاندان[OCR?] شطاریہ[OCR?] قادریہ[OCR?]", a man of utmost worship and asceticism whose line continued after him, "چنانچہ یہ شاہ عنایت اللہ اسی کا مرید تہا", and so this Shah Inayat Allah was his *murid* (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 144, folio 147). The reading of all three words naming that order is uncertain in the printed text, and Kanhaiya Lal gives this Shah Inayat Allah no place, no date and no shrine of his own; the year 1126 [OCR?] that he records on the same page is Shah Zia Qadri's death, not his. At Shah Zia Qadri's *mazar*, he adds, "ہر سال میلا بہی ہوتا ہی صوفی لوگ جمع ہوکر سماع سنتے ہین اور وجد کرتے ہین", every year a *mela* is held, and Sufis gather to hear *sama* and go into ecstasy (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 144, folio 147).

After friction with Kasur's ruling family — some of which, it is reported, local tradition attributes to the ill intent of the ruler toward his wife, described as unusually beautiful and of noble family; this is reported as a local account rather than settled fact — Shah Inayat migrated to Lahore. Lahori names the ruler: "اول سکونت ان کی قصبہ قصور میں تھی۔ پھر بسبب اس کے کہ حسین خان حاکم قصور سے ان کی رنجیدگی ہو گئی۔ لاہور میں آ کر قیام پذیر ہوئے", his first residence was the *qasba* of Kasur; then, because he fell out with Husain Khan, the ruler of Kasur, he came and settled at Lahore (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 76, folio 74). The edition's book list describes him in the same order of places, "of Kasur, then of Lahore": "حیات شاہ عنایت قادری قصوری ثم لاہوری" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 295, folio 283).

In Lahore he served as imam of the Oonchi Masjid near Bhati Gate and founded his own madrasa, teaching students without regard to caste or class. Lahori writes that "ہنگامہ مشیخت ان کی زندگی تک گرم رہا", that the contest of his shaykhhood stayed hot as long as he lived (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 76, folio 74). Shackle sets him in the city: a note to one lyric explains that "The city that is the seat of royal authority in Panjab (hence called taḳhat lahor) is here celebrated as the home of Shah Inayat" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 185, folio 160, n. 1 on 120). Khaled Ahmad, introducing Taufiq Rafat's selection of Bulleh Shah, writes that "Shah Enayat of Lahore, the beloved preceptor of Bulleh Shah, was a successor to the Qadiriya 'gaddi' of Mian Mir", and so could not have inclined to an overt opposition to the spiritual traditions of the Sikhs (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 42, folio 22). Zahur ul-Hasan Sharib, too, places him at Lahore, under the heading ‘لاہور میں آمد’ (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 341, folio 337).

He combined the Qadiri and Shattari silsilas in his own practice. Lahori's list of entries names him "شاہ عنایت قادری شطاری" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 16, folio 4), and the editor's full name for him carries both orders. Shackle's notes give both, without reconciling them: "Shah Inayat was affiliated to the Qadiri order of Sufis" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 180, folio 155, n. 1 on 94), and his glossary speaks of the Qadiri "Sufi order with which Shah Inayat and Bullhe Shah were affiliated" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 199, folio 174); yet his introduction names only the Shattari, writing that the doctrine of self-obliteration in the master, *fanā fī 'l-shaiḳh*, "was given particular importance within the Shattari order with which Shah Inayat was affiliated" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 24, folio xxiii). Khaled Ahmad names only the Qadiriya: "Shah Enayat, whose life spanned the Mughal rule from Aurangzeb to Mohammad Shah, belonged to the Qadiriya School of Muslim mysticism in India" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 22, folio not stated). He adds: "His scholarship of Arabic and Persian was overlaid with an acceptance of the mystical traditions of the people of the subcontinent", an openness that was ironical, he says, since the creed of Sheikh Abdul Qadir of Baghdad, the Hanbali founder of the School, "was originally opposed to innovative adjustments of this sort" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 22, folio not stated). The introduction speaks of "the Qadiriya tradition to which his preceptor Shah Inayat belonged" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 39, folio 19), and a note closes: "The times of Shah Enayat and Bulleh Shah were therefore hard times for the Qadiriya order." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 241, folio 221). Sharib gives him the nisba ‘قادری’ and names no other order (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 341–342, folios 337–338).

Three of the books name his caste. Lahori writes "حضرت قوم کے باغبان تھے۔ اولاد اب بھی ان کی لاہور میں ہے", that he was of the Baghban, the gardener caste, and that his descendants are in Lahore still (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 76, folio 74). Shackle's glossary defines the *arain* as "the caste of vegetable gardeners to which Shah Inayat belonged" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 198, folio 173). Shemeem Burney Abbas writes that he "is said to have been of arāĩ descent. The arāĩ in the Panjab were gardeners or small cultivators who worked on their own land" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 108, folio not stated).

He authored several Persian works of mystical philosophy, including the *Dastur-ul-Amal* (also reported independently) and *Lata'if-e-Ghaibiya*. Lahori's editor writes that sixteen of his works have been traced, of which *Ghayat al-Hawashi* is the bulkiest (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 75–76, folios 63 and 74, حاشیہ 1). Shackle, glossing one lyric, speaks of "his written Persian treatises", which he does not name (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 180, folio 155, n. 2 on 91). The introduction to Rafat's selection reports, as Dr. Lajwanti's claim, that "Shah Enayat Qadiri in his book 'Dastur-ul-Amal' points to the Hindu origin of the mystical doctrines and their recrudescence in Greek thought centuries later." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 44, folio 24), and a note records that "Dr. Lajwanti examines in detail Shah Enayat's publication Dastur-ul-Amal." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 241, folio 221). The same note attributes a doctrine to him, in words it quotes from Ramakrishna's *Punjabi Sufi Poets*: "The saint believed in the Hindu origin of Islamic mysticism", knowledge that "he believed, was carried by the Greek soldiers of Alexander the Great of Greece, from where it was borrowed by the mystics of Islam." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 241, folio 221).

Of his teaching, Khaled Ahmad writes that the saint practised *haqiqat*, direct knowledge (the word printed ‘bagigat’ [OCR?]), "under the careful camouflage of sophisticated Persian metaphor, was dismayed by the rough vernacular expression of his disciple and advised him to desist from railing against orthodox postures upheld by the political might of the Mughal court. Bulleh Shah did not listen" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 23, folio not stated). Of one kafi he adds that "The last couplet, not translated", affirmed Bulleh Shah's allegiance to the *haqiqi* aspects of Shah Enayat (the word printed ‘baqiqi’ [OCR?]), "while the rest of the poem obstinately resisted the advice that he should pay homage to the Syed paramountcy in religion." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 23, folio not stated). The introduction sets this within its argument that "During Aurangzeb's period, the Qadiriya mystics in Lahore were on the defensive, careful not to offend an orthodoxy inclined to take severe punitive measures against heretics." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 42, folio 22).

The year of his death is disputed. The current account gives around 1728, and Shackle "Shah Inayat (d. 1728)", the only date his volume gives him (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 8, folio vii). Lahori gives ۱۱۴۱ھ [OCR?] (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 76, folio 74). His editor rejects it: "شاہ عنایت کا سال وفات کسی معاصر تذکرہ نویس نے نہیں لکھا۔ پہلی مرتبہ مفتی صاحب نے ہی ۱۱۴۱ھ بغیر کسی سند کے لکھ دیا۔ جسے بعد کے مؤرخ نقل کرتے رہے", no contemporary writer of *tazkiras* recorded Shah Inayat's year of death; the Mufti was the first to write 1141 AH, without any authority, and later historians went on copying it (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 75–76, folios 63 and 74, حاشیہ 1). His evidence is a letter to Shah Inayat from a contemporary scholar close to him, Haji Muhammad Sharif Qasuri, who died in 1153 AH: quoting one of the saint's works, the writer put after his name "سلمہ اللہ تعالیٰ", the formula used of a living man, and sealed it "العبد الفقیر محمد شریف ۱۱۴۸ ہجری". From this the editor concludes that Shah Inayat was alive as late as 1148 AH and died approximately after ۱۱۵۰ھ / ۱۷۳۷ء [OCR?] (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 75–76, folios 63 and 74, حاشیہ 1). The title of the editor's own life of the saint repeats his dating, "حیات شاہ عنایت قادری قصوری ؒ (ف بعد ۱۷۳۷ء)" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 307, folio 295). None of Sharib, Rafat or Abbas gives him a year of death.

## Bulleh Shah and His Wider Circle

His acceptance of Bulleh Shah as a disciple became one of the most retold episodes in Punjabi Sufi lore, precisely because it crossed rigid social boundaries: Bulleh Shah came from a Syed family of higher perceived status, yet sought out and remained devoted to a teacher of humbler, land-tilling background — facing, it is reported, religious decrees (*fatwas*) from his own family over the choice, which he is said to have answered only "what I have seen in him, you cannot perceive." This relationship, immortalised in Bulleh Shah's own verses, continues to be cited in Pakistan as an example of Sufism's disregard for caste hierarchy.

Shackle speaks of "the lower rank of Shah Inayat as an Arain" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 165, folio 140) and of "his revered master from the lower-ranking Arain caste" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 183, folio 158). A note to another lyric makes the master a figure from the romance of Heer: "Just as Ranjha was despised by Hir's family, so too was Shah Inayat disparaged for being a member of the Arain caste." (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 195, folio 170, n. 4 on 151).

Lahori states twice that Shah Inayat was Bulleh Shah's *pir*. In Bulleh Shah's own notice he is named as the poet's *pir* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 81, folio 69 [marker: 79]); and in the notice of another saint he tells how Shaikh Abd al-Hakim Qadiri at Talamba [OCR?] declined to take Bulleh Shah as his disciple and sent him on, saying "تیرے نصیب کا بجزہ [OCR?] شاہ عنایت قادری کے پاس ہے", that the portion of his lot lay with Shah Inayat Qadiri (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 219, folio 207). That passage gives no date, no shrine and no order beyond Qadiri.

Sharib, in his chapter on Bulleh Shah, also has the young man reach his master by referral. Sayyid Abd al-Hakim told him "تمہارا حصہ حضرت شاہ عنایت اللہ قادری رحمۃ اللہ علیہ کے پاس ہے' ان کے پاس جاؤ اور اپنا حصہ لو", your share is with Hazrat Shah Inayat Allah Qadiri; go to him and take your share (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 341, folio 337). He asked his father's leave, "حضرت عنایت اللہ قادری رحمۃ اللہ علیہ سے بیعت کی اجازت", and "لاہور پہنچ کر آپ حضرت عنایت اللہ قادری کی خدمت میں حاضر ہوئے", reaching Lahore, he attended at Inayat Allah Qadiri's service (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 341, folio 337). Sharib prints the master's name with *Allah*, as ‘شاہ عنایت اللہ قادری’ and ‘عنایت اللہ قادری’ (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 341–342, folios 337–338).

The master set a price on the initiation. In Sharib's account he said he would give *bai'at* on condition that the young man first bring "پانسو روپے نقد' پانسو روپے کا ایک گھوڑا' پانسو روپے کے طلائی کنگنوں کی ایک جوڑی اور پانسو روپے کی ایک پوشاک", five hundred rupees in cash, a horse worth five hundred rupees, a pair of gold bangles worth five hundred rupees and a suit of clothes worth five hundred rupees (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 342, folio 338). The four things are later delivered exactly, and then "آپ کو بیعت سے مشرف فرمایا اور بعدہ خرقہ خلافت عطاء کیا", he honoured him with *bai'at* and afterwards gave him the *khirqa* of succession (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 342, folio 338). Shackle puts the gift in another form: "Bullhe Shah gave his allegiance to Shah Inayat, from whom he received the word (kalmā) of his Sufi teaching." (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 155, folio 130).

The books tell of a break between master and disciple, and give it different causes. Abbas states it most fully: "after several years of association Shah Inayat excluded Bulle Shah from his spiritual company. Shah Inayat showed his disapproval of Bulle Shah's poetry, which he considered to be too outspoken against the priestly class. Shah Inayat dissociated himself from Bulle Shah for his outright criticism of issues of gender, class, and caste. Furthermore, he objected to Bulle's ridicule of institutionalized religion. Shah Inayat had to detach himself from his rebellious disciple because he was afraid of the political forces of the time" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 108, folio not stated); which forces, she does not say. She adds that "He knew that Shah Inayat was fond of music" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 109, folio not stated). The introduction to Rafat's selection gives a political reason, reading the story as an account of "Shah Enayat's early rejection of Bulleh Shah as a follower when the latter began to sing his rebuking lines against the orthodoxy." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 42, folio 22). Sharib says that the master withdrew the disciple's *wilayat* and later restored it (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 343, folio 339). Shackle reports "the most popular tale", in which Bulleh Shah "reappeared in Lahore one day, himself dressed as a dancing girl. There he danced and sang in front of Shah Inayat" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, pp. 18–19, folios xvii–xviii; p. 19, folio xviii); Rafat's book has no dancing girl. Khaled Ahmad writes: "It is said that Shah Enayat turned him out of his house after this. Aware of the Qadiriya custom of Sama and devotional dance, Bulleh Shah took to the sarangi, the stringed instrument associated with the wandering dervish, and began to sing and dance." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 24, folio not stated). The kafi 'Teray Ishq nachaya', he says, "expresses his desolation and helplessness in the face of his unavoidable passion and his alienation from Shah Enayat." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 24, folio not stated). A note of Shackle's reads a line of one famous kafi as "Symbolizing the gloom created by the master's departure." (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 182, folio 157, n. 2 on 105).

Waris Shah is reported to have been a second prominent disciple from this period, alongside Hazrat Hafiz Tabraiz, Maulana Jami, Deewan-e-Hafiz, and Deewan-e-Shams, names whose connection to him is not explained.

## In Bulleh Shah's Verse

Bulleh Shah names his master again and again: the name stands inside the lyrics of Shackle's translation at least twenty times, and the introduction to Rafat's selection observes that "The most common reference to Shah Enayat in Bulleh Shah's kafis comes at the end of a whole train of suffering caused by separation." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 45, folio 25). In Shackle's translation the master is where the search ends:

*I have found Shah Inayat.* (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 44, folio 19)

*I got caught in its whirlpools, but Shah Inayat delivered me to the other side.* (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 46, folio 21)

*With every breath I have remembered Inayat.* (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 52, folio 27)

He holds the secret, and he is the stone that transmutes:

*If Shah Inayat reveals the secret, then all mysteries are solved.* (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 62, folio 37)

*Bullha, Shah Inayat is a master mystic, he is the lord of my heart. I am iron and he is the philosopher's stone.* (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 73, folio 48)

One poem places him in the city:

*Baghdad is the place of the Pir of Pirs; our guide is in royal Lahore* (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 122, folio 97)

Within four lines of that, the same poem has:

*Shah Inayat has set out his hooks, and without being seen he tugs the line* (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 122, folio 97)

He is the poet's faith and his very being:

*Shah Inayat is my religion, the one whom we accept in this world and the next* (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 90, folio 65)

*My whole being became Inayat; then it got the name Bullha* (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 93, folio 68)

*Leaving my parents, I have clung to your skirt, oh my lord Shah Inayat.* (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 115, folio 90)

The poems call him by his order's name, in absence and in longing:

*With the departure of Inayat Qadiri, my story has again become difficult* (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 104, folio 79)

*Come, Inayat Qadiri, my heart desires you* (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 127, folio 102)

And they go to his gate, a gate of the poem rather than of a building:

*Bullha, let us go and sit at the gate of Shah Inayat* (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 111, folio 86)

The master, in the end, is the lord who shows himself:

*As Shah Inayat, you show yourself to Bullha all the time.* (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 146, folio 121)

*See how my lord Shah Inayat flirts with me.* (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 148, folio 123)

*Bullha, he lets himself be found. Shah Inayat is discovered.* (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 150, folio 125)

Shackle's notes explain the play on his name, "There is a play on the literal meaning, 'grace,' of the name (ināit, Ar. ‘ināyat) of Bullhe Shah's master, Shah Inayat." (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 173, folio 148, n. 7 on 59), and his glossary has "INAYAT (Ar. ‘ināyat 'favor') Bullhe Shah's spiritual guide Shah Inayat." (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 199, folio 174). He identifies an epithet, "Oh you with a great family" (*vaḍ parvāriā*), as Shah Inayat (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 165, folio 140); reads one line as "I.e., the divine light entered Shah Inayat." (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 186, folio 161, n. 3 on 121); and closes another poem on "This profession of the mystical identity between disciple and the master standing directly before him" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 183, folio 158, n. 7 on 106).

Taufiq Rafat's English gives the same master under the names Inayat and Inayat Shah. In 'The Follower', with the name printed in lower case:

*Love has brought me to the door of inayat [OCR?], dressed like a whore. — I click my heels, and leap in glee.* (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 101, folio 81)

In 'Come See Me Once in a While':

*The flames engulfing my house, Bulleh, only love can douse. Inayat calls up the aisle* (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 149, folio 129)

In 'I'm Waiting':

*I'm waiting, pass this way once more, Inayat Shah, my eyes are sore with looking for you at the door.* (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 185, folio 165)

*Who will lead me to the place of my master, so by his grace he may my heart and body restore.* (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 185, folio 165)

In 'The Load' and 'The Story of Creation':

*not Bulleh, but Inayat must steer.* (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 189, folio 169)

*Only the proper guide can lead you to His side; and unless you choose Inayat, you will lose.* (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 205, folio 185)

Elsewhere the guide goes unnamed: "then help there will be none till you call on Bulleh's guide." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 155, folio 135). The introduction quotes, in Roman letters and with the printed text damaged, a line in which the master is the revealer of secrets, "Bajb vaseelay likbiya na jaey/ Shab Enayat bbed bataey" [OCR?], read as 'Bajh vaseelay likhiya na jaey / Shah Enayat bhed bataey' (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 46, folio 26), and adds that "Shah Enayat is made to be the source of mystical secrets" and that "it is only in Rumi" that a true parallel is found to Bulleh Shah's invocation of his master at the end of the kafi (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 45, folio 25). Rafat's own note explains the figure for a foreign reader: "Bulleh Shah's frequent references to his mentor and guide Inayat Shah can, therefore, be quite puzzling to a Western reader unless he has some background knowledge of this convention. The poet as a saint's disciple is a common figure in sufi poetry; he has no counterpart in Western literature," (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 14, folio xiv). A note reports Nazir Ahmad's observation that Shah Husain makes no reference to his beloved Madho Lal in his kafis, whereas "Bulleh Shah's invocation of Shah Enayat is constant." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 242, folio 222).

Abbas records a song that Bulleh Shah made for his master and that now belongs to the wedding:

*Merā pīyā ghar āyā o lālnī (Celebrate thou, for my beloved has come home)* (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 208, folio not stated)

It was "composed by Bulle Shah, the Panjabi Sufi poet, for his mentor, Shah Inayat, is also sung as a wedding song" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 208, folio not stated).

## Family

Shah Inayat died around 1728 and is buried at Mozang alongside two of his sons. It is reported that a daughter is buried alongside him as well, and that the two sons were Zamanaullah, described as a Sufi poet who left no descendants, and Zahidullah, through whom the family line continued. Lahori, writing in 1875, says that his descendants are in Lahore still (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 76, folio 74).

## The Shrine

His shrine, with its adjoining mosque, remains an active site of pilgrimage. Administration is reported to have passed from the family's own descendants to the Auqaf Department.

Lahori says only that his radiant *mazar* is in Lahore (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 76, folio 74). His editor's appendix of the present locations of the saints' resting places lists "عنایت شاہ قادری شطاری لاہوری قدس سرہ" at Lahore, on "شاہراہ فاطمہ جناح", Shahrah-e Fatima Jinnah (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 288, folio 276); the appendix does not say how that road stands to Mozang Chungi or Queens Road. None of the other books consulted describes the tomb. Shackle names no tomb, shrine, *urs* or successor for him, and never names Mozang; Rafat's book gives him no tomb, no burial place and no year of death, and the "door" and the "place" of its poems are figures of speech in a lyric; Sharib gives him no *mazar* and no locality within Lahore; and Abbas gives him no tomb, shrine or *urs*. The sources consulted do not record the builder of the tomb or the date of its building.

Nur Ahmad Chishti's *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, in a damaged passage, records on a platform south-east of the mosque at the tomb of Pir Hadi Rahnuma "ایک عنایت اللہ قادری کی جو مرشد حضرت [illegible] پناہ[OCR?] صاحب کی ہیں جسکا مقبرہ قصبہ [illegible] زمین واقع ہی", one Inayat Allah Qadiri, murshid of a Hazrat whose name is illegible, with a *maqbara* in a *qasba* whose name is also illegible; it then gives a death, "وفات [illegible] کی سنہ [illegible] سو اکاسی[OCR?] میں ہوئی", in a year ending in eighty-one, without making clear whose death is meant (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 221). Whether this Inayat Allah Qadiri is the saint of this shrine is not established.

## Devotion and Practice

The annual urs, observed 25-27 Jamadi al-Thani, draws thousands of devotees for nights of *zikr* and qawwali. The shrine's ordinary devotional life is reported to include five daily prayers at the attached mosque, a weekly Mehfil-e-Milad with langar on Thursdays, and a corner set aside for lighting oil lamps, and visitors are reported to number roughly 300-500 daily, rising to 1,500-3,000 on Thursday nights and 40,000-60,000 during the urs.

His memory is also sung far from his own tomb. At the shrine of Bulleh Shah, the qawwals whom Abbas recorded sang six compositions, of which five were "narratives about different stages of Bulle Shah's spiritual growth as a mystic and his relationship with his spiritual mentor, Shah Inayat. The qawwāls sang about Bulle Shah the murid (disciple) and Shah Inayat the mentor (murshid) through female and male representations" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 107, folio not stated). Abida Parvin, in her own Urdu prose narration as Abbas records it, says: "Bulle Shāh ke murśid Ināyat Shāh vo śahr-- Uch śahr ke rehne vāle thε", which Abbas gives as "Bulle Shah's mentor Inayat Shah, he--he belonged to Uch city" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 104, folio not stated). In the sung line, as Abbas translates it:

*Thou art an inhabitant of Uch city [Uch literally means high/great]* (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 104, folio not stated)

Abbas's book places him at Uch and nowhere else.

## Legends and Miracles

Khaled Ahmad reports that "Hagiographers tell of a dramatic encounter between the two in Lahore, one outdoing the other in a display of miraculous powers." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 22, folio not stated); he does not say what the powers were. After the expulsion, he writes, "Seeing that the heterodoxy of Bulleh Shah had a divine source, his teacher finally took him back. The reunion was dramatic." Shah Enayat, approaching a bedraggled Bulleh Shah, asked whether he was not Bullah, and was told he was not Bullah but Bhulla, as the damaged printed text is read; ‘Bhulla meant lost.’ (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 24, folio not stated).

In Sharib's chapter on Bulleh Shah the master's possessions are recognisable in the town, where people say "یہ پوشاک اور گھوڑا تو حضرت شاہ عنایت اللہ کا ہے", these clothes and this horse are Hazrat Shah Inayat Allah's (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 342, folio 338). In the same chapter the master withdraws his disciple's *wilayat* and later restores it, as though it were a grant that could be revoked; Sharib names no document, seal or witness (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 343, folio 339).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The year of his death.** The current account gives around 1728, and Shackle "Shah Inayat (d. 1728)" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 8, folio vii). Lahori gives ۱۱۴۱ھ [OCR?] (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 76, folio 74). His editor says that no contemporary recorded the year, that 1141 AH was first written by Lahori without authority, and that a sealed letter of 1148 AH shows the saint alive, so that he died approximately after ۱۱۵۰ھ / ۱۷۳۷ء [OCR?] (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 75–76, folios 63 and 74, حاشیہ 1; p. 307, folio 295). In the same footnote the editor gives 1141 AH / 1728 CE as the death year of a different man, Shah Ali Raza Faruqi (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 75–76, folios 63 and 74, حاشیہ 1). Rafat, Sharib and Abbas give no year; Khaled Ahmad says his life spanned the Mughal rule from Aurangzeb to Mohammad Shah (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 22, folio not stated).
- **His teacher.** The current account records two names, Muhammad Ali Raza Shattari from earlier sourcing and Hazrat Shah Raza Qadri Shattari from the field survey. Lahori makes him a *khalifa* of Shah Raza Qadri Lahori (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 75, folio 63); his editor names exoteric teachers in Maulana Sayyid Abu al-Nasr and Maulvi Abdul Hadi Lahori, inner guides in Shah Ali Raza Faruqi and Shaikh Muhammad Sultan Bukhari, and, after 1111 AH, Shah Raza Qadri Shattari Lahori (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 75–76, folios 63 and 74, حاشیہ 1). Kanhaiya Lal makes a Shah Inayat Allah the *murid* of Shah Zia Qadri (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 144, folio 147). Khaled Ahmad calls him a successor to the Qadiriya *gaddi* of Mian Mir (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 42, folio 22).
- **His order.** The current account and Lahori give Qadiri and Shattari together (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 16, folio 4). Shackle names the Qadiri in his notes and glossary and the Shattari alone in his introduction (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 180, folio 155, n. 1 on 94; p. 199, folio 174; p. 24, folio xxiii). Rafat's book names only the Qadiriya (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 22, folio not stated; p. 39, folio 19); Sharib gives only the nisba ‘قادری’ (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 341–342, folios 337–338). Kanhaiya Lal gives his master's line as Shattariya Qadriya, the reading uncertain in the printed text (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 144, folio 147).
- **His name.** The current account calls him Shah Inayat Qadiri, and the field survey gives his given name as Abul-Muarif Hanfi Qadri. Lahori's editor gives "حافظ ابوالمعارف محمد عنایت اللہ حنفی قادری شطاری" (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 75–76, folios 63 and 74, حاشیہ 1), and the appendix inverts it as ‘عنایت شاہ’ (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 288, folio 276). Sharib prints Shah Inayat Allah Qadiri (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, pp. 341–342, folios 337–338). Rafat's book writes Shah Enayat, Shah Inayat, Shah Enayat Qadiri and Inayat Shah (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 42, folio 22; p. 39, folio 19; p. 44, folio 24; p. 14, folio xiv), and Abida Parvin, as Abbas records her, Inayat Shah (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 104, folio not stated).
- **Where he came from.** The current account has him born in Kasur, and Lahori gives his first residence as the *qasba* of Kasur (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 76, folio 74). Abida Parvin, in Abbas's record, says he belonged to Uch (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 104, folio not stated).
- **Why he left Kasur.** The current account speaks of friction with Kasur's ruling family, and the field survey of a local tradition about the ruler's intent toward his wife. Lahori says that he fell out with Husain Khan, the ruler of Kasur (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 76, folio 74).
- **His caste.** The current account calls his family farmers of the Arain community, of land-tilling background. Lahori calls him a Baghban, a gardener (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 76, folio 74); Shackle calls the Arain "the caste of vegetable gardeners" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 198, folio 173); Abbas says he "is said to have been" of *arain* descent and defines the *arain* in her text as gardeners or small cultivators who worked their own land (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 108, folio not stated), but in her glossary as "A caste of landowners in the Panjab" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 178, folio not stated).
- **Where he lies.** The current account places the shrine at Mozang Chungi on Queens Road. Lahori's editor's appendix gives Shahrah-e Fatima Jinnah (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 288, folio 276). Lahori's text says only Lahore (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 76, folio 74).
- **His writings.** The current account names the *Dastur-ul-Amal* and *Lata'if-e-Ghaibiya*. Lahori's editor speaks of sixteen traced works and names *Zail al-Aghlat* and *Ghayat al-Hawashi* (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, pp. 75–76, folios 63 and 74, حاشیہ 1); Rafat's book names the *Dastur-ul-Amal* only, through Dr. Lajwanti (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 44, folio 24); Shackle mentions Persian treatises without naming them (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 180, folio 155, n. 2 on 91).
- **How Bulleh Shah came to him.** Lahori: Shaikh Abd al-Hakim Qadiri at Talamba [OCR?] declined him and sent him to Shah Inayat (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 219, folio 207). Sharib: Sayyid Abd al-Hakim told him his share was with Shah Inayat Allah Qadiri, and he went to Lahore with his father's leave (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 341, folio 337).
- **The break with Bulleh Shah.** Abbas: disapproval of outspoken poetry and fear of the political forces of the time (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 108, folio not stated). Rafat's book: an encounter of miraculous powers in Lahore, then expulsion, which the introduction reads as a rejection for singing against orthodoxy (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 22, folio not stated; p. 24, folio not stated; p. 42, folio 22). Sharib: a withdrawal of *wilayat* later restored (Sharib, *Tazkirah Awliya-e Pak o Hind*, p. 343, folio 339). Shackle: the popular tale of the return in a dancing girl's dress (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 19, folio xviii).

## Legacy

Shackle makes the master the key to the disciple's poetry: the most important "focus of Bullhe Shah's love is his spiritual guide, Shah Inayat, now as the present source of his joy, now as the absent cause of his pain, now as the master who is mysteriously both different from and identical to his adoring disciple." (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 18, folio xvii). The signature verse of the kafi, he writes, often proclaims Bulleh Shah's powerful identification "with his spiritual master, Shah Inayat, who is frequently alluded to as 'the lord'" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 14, folio xiii), and the poems are "the love between the human and the divine as lived out between the poet and his lord, Shah Inayat" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 22, folio xxi). Behind that bond stands the institution Shackle describes in general terms: "Besides transmission of distinctive spiritual disciplines within the circle of disciples formed around a master (called Shaikh or Pir), the Sufi message reached a wider audience through poetry." (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 11, folio x). For Khaled Ahmad, the *murshid* (printed ‘mursbid’ [OCR?]) "is called upon to provide solace to a Bulleh Shah deeply hurt by the socio-political realities of his times" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 45, folio 25).

His own life has its literature in Urdu. Lahori's editor, Muhammad Iqbal Mujaddidi, lists a *Hayat-e Shah Inayat Qadri* of his own as in press (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 82, folio 70 [marker: 80]), and the edition's book list carries a life of Shah Inayat as "زیر تکمیل ۱۹۷۴ء", under completion in 1974 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 295, folio 283). The titles reported for him, Sheikh-ul-Mashaikh, Shah-e-Lahore and Mufakkir-e-Pakistan, and the thousands who come for his urs, record a saint remembered in his own right as well as through the verse of his disciple.
