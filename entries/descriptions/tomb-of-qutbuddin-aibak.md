## Overview

Tucked amid the crowded lanes of Aibak Road in the Anarkali quarter of Lahore, a modest garden tomb marks the burial place of Sultan Qutb ud-Din Aibak, founder of the Mamluk, or "Slave," dynasty and the first ruler of the Delhi Sultanate. A slave who rose through service to the Ghurid ruler Muhammad Ghori, he established independent rule based in Lahore after Ghori's death, laying the foundations of Muslim dynastic government in the subcontinent, and he is remembered too as the patron who commissioned Delhi's Qutb Minar. He died in Lahore in November 1210 after a fall from his horse while playing *chaugan*, an early form of polo. The tomb is not a Sufi shrine: it hosts no *ʿurs*, no pilgrimage tradition and no ongoing devotional practice, and it is maintained as a protected historical monument, valued chiefly for its symbolic role marking the thirteenth-century founding moment of Muslim dynastic rule in South Asia, centred, briefly, on Lahore itself.

The tomb's physical history has been anything but continuous. Historians dispute whether a substantial mausoleum ever stood over the grave in the medieval period; for long the site was a simple, informal grave hemmed in by the houses of the Anarkali bazaar; and the structure visitors see today is essentially a reconstruction of the 1970s, though one official account dates it to 1990.

The books consulted add a good deal to this picture. Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti's survey of the tombs and buildings of Lahore, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, is the only one of them to describe the grave itself: he places it in the Sadr Bazaar of Anarkali, records a lost two-storey dome of marble that old people remembered, and says the Maharaja of his day demolished the buildings and sent their stone to Amritsar (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 271). Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* gives the fullest account of the life: the purchased slave made viceroy, his campaigns, his accession at Lahore, his generosity, his name and his death at *chaugan* (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 400; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 401–402). Kanhaiya Lal's *Tarikh-e Lahore* places his death at Lahore and explains his title Lakh Bakhsh, but names no grave for him (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 25, folio 20). Naveed Alam's translation of Madho Lal Hussein, Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi, Shemeem Burney Abbas and Annemarie Schimmel mention him in passing, and the year of his death is given in more than one way.

## The Life of the Sultan

Aibak was born around 1150 in Turkestan and sold into slavery as a child. He rose through service to the Ghurid ruler Muhammad Ghori, and came to command Ghurid territories in northern India following the decisive Second Battle of Tarain in 1192.

Bhandari calls him plainly 'سلطان قطب الدین ایبک غلام زرخرید سلطان شهاب الدین نیابتاً حکومت هندوستان داشت', the Sultan's purchased slave, governing Hindustan as his deputy (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 401). After the victory at 'نراین', he writes, Sultan Shihab al-Din left 'ملک قطب الدین ایبک را که غلام برگزیده او بود درقصبه کهرام هفتاد کروهے دهلی نایب سلطنت': his chosen slave was made viceroy at the *qasba* of Kuhram, seventy *kos* from Delhi (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 402). Rizvi's history of Sufism tells it differently: "Mu‘izzu’d-Din Muhammad bin Sam had conquered Delhi and, departing for Ghazni, left his favourite slave, Qutbu’d-Din Aibak, as head of his forces in the occupied city" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 139, folio 121).

As deputy, 'نیابتاً', after the Sultan went back to Ghazni, Bhandari continues, he 'دراسرع اوقات قلعه دهلی ومیرته تسخیر نموده بهرجا حکام نصب گردانید', in the shortest time took the forts of Delhi and Meerut and set governors in every place; in the second year he took 'قلعه کول', and then the forts of Gwalior, Budaun 'ودیگر قلاع', and other forts (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 401–402). He marched on Gujarat and took revenge on 'راۓ بهیم دیو گجراتی' for the Sultan's earlier defeat, 'انتقام شکست سلطان که سابقاً بتحریر درامده', plundered the country, returned to Delhi and 'خطبه وسکه بنام سلطان رواج داد', put the *khutba* and the coin into currency in the Sultan's name (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 401). In the Sultan's tenth campaign, against the Khokhars near Lahore, 'ملک قطب الدین نیز از دهلی بخدمت سلطان رسیده باتفاق یکدیگر گوشمال انجماعت ... کردند': Malik Qutb al-Din too came from Delhi to the Sultan's service, and together they chastised that tribe (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 401).

Rizvi describes the occupation: "Occupying troops stationed at Indraprastha near Delhi under Qutbu’d-Din Aibak were forced to make several invasions in the Doab and the regions around Ajmer to consolidate their power" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 140, folio 122). In the same account he records that "Qutbu’d-Din had appointed Saiyid Husain Mashhadi as the darogha (military governor) of the area. The Saiyid served the Khwaja with great devotion" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 140, folio 122).

His captains carried the conquest east. In Bhandari's words, 'در بلاد بنگاله آغاز ظهور اسلام از ملک محمد بختیار که از امراے بزرگ سلطان قطب الدین ایبک بود گردیده وازان زمان آن ولایت درتصرف[OCR?] سلطان دهلی گشت': Islam in Bengal began with Malik Muhammad Bakhtiyar, one of Aibak's great amirs, and from that time the province was in the hands of the Sultan of Delhi (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 208). Chishti tells of a general who sent his booty from Bihar 'اپنے مالک کی پاس دہلی مین', to his master at Delhi, of a 'مست ہاتہی', a rutting elephant, and of the governorship of Bihar and an order to take Bengal (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 809).

Delhi was his seat. Bhandari records that 'سلطان قطب الدین ایبک و سلطان شمس الدین التمش در قلعه رائے پتهورا می بودند', he and Iltutmish lived in Rai Pithora's fort (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 561), and that 'ازان تاریخ شهر دهلی دارالخلافت سلاطین مسلمین مقرر گردیده', from then on Delhi was the capital of the Muslim sultans (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 401). He is remembered as the patron who commissioned Delhi's Qutb Minar, and Alam's note calls him "known for the famous Qutb Minar" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, pp. 184–185, folios 184–185). Schimmel, writing of the death of Muʿinuddin in 1236, places it "not far from the beautiful mosque" that Aibak, whom she calls 'of Delhi', "had erected in Ajmer" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 377, folio 346).

## The Accession

After Ghori's assassination in 1206, Aibak established independent rule based in Lahore. Chishti's history gives Muhammad Ghori's death as 'سن بارہ سو چہہ [OCR?]', the year twelve hundred and six, and says he died without a son; 'قطب الدین حاکم دہلی', Qutb al-Din the ruler of Delhi, then claimed Hindustan (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 809). Elsewhere Chishti sets the accession in 'چھ سو دو[OCR?]', six hundred and two, with the figure ۶۰۲ in the margin, when he 'تخت دہلی پر جلوس فرمایا', took his seat on the throne of Delhi; before that, he writes, 'ملک ہندوستان ... بے چراغ تھا', the land of Hindustan was without a lamp (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 267). Kanhaiya Lal states his status plainly: Shahab-ud-Din Ghori 'دہلی کا تخت سلطان [illegible] قطب الدین ایبک اپنے غلام کے سپرد کر دیا', handed the throne of Delhi to Qutb al-Din Aibak, his own slave (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 24, folio 19).

Bhandari puts the moment of sovereignty at Lahore: 'بعد ازانکه سلطان درجه شهادت یافت از دهلی برامده درلاهور رسید اصالتاً سریراراۓ خلافت گشت ویازدهم ربیع الاول سنه ۶۰۳[OCR?] سکه وخطبه بنام خود کرد'. After the Sultan attained martyrdom, he came out of Delhi, reached Lahore and took the throne in his own right, and on the eleventh of Rabiʿ al-Awwal of the year 603 had the coin struck and the *khutba* read in his own name; the reading of the year is uncertain in the printed text (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 401). 'سلطان غیاث الدین محمود[OCR?] برادرزاده سلطان شهاب الدین از فیروزه کوه', Sultan Ghiyas al-Din Mahmud, Shihab al-Din's nephew, sent him from Firozkoh 'چتر وتجملات بادشاهی', the parasol and the trappings of kingship, and 'بخطاب سلطانی مخاطب ساخت', addressed him with the title of Sultan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 400–401).

## The Contest with Yildiz

His rival for the Ghurid inheritance was Taj al-Din Yildiz, whom the books spell variously. Bhandari calls 'تاج الدین یلدوز' one of Shihab al-Din's 'بندهاۓ خاص', his special slaves, who had been given the name of sultan in Ghazni after Shihab al-Din's death; a dispute arose between the two 'برسر لاهور', over Lahore, and they fought a pitched battle: 'بالآخر تاج الدین تاب مقاومت نیاورده منهزم گشته درکرمان اقامت ورزید وسلطان مظفر ومنصور شده باستقلال کمال بسلطنت پرداخت'. At last Taj al-Din could not hold out, was routed and settled in Kirman, and the Sultan, victorious, gave himself to rule in complete independence (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 400).

In Kanhaiya Lal's history Yalduz, having seized Ghazni, 'پھر پنجاب کو آیا', came on to the Punjab to take the throne of Hind from Qutb al-Din (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 25, folio 20). Aibak defeated him in the field and pursued him to Ghazni, and then 'قطب الدین وہاں سے لاہور کو آ گیا', Qutb al-Din came from there to Lahore (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 25, folio 20). Chishti, for his part, has him defeat 'الدوز [OCR?]' and be crowned at Ghazni, where he grew 'آرام طلب', a lover of ease, and was driven back (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 809).

## His Rule and His Household

Chishti says he ruled 'بہت لیاقت سی', with great ability, and lived five years after his master, the reading of the number being uncertain (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 809). Bhandari reckons the whole of his government thus: 'مدت بست سال حکومت هندوستان نمود ازانجمله سلطنت غزنین چهار سال', he governed Hindustan for twenty years, the sultanate of Ghazni among them for four (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 400). Rizvi's index gives the bracket "(1206-1210)" after his name (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 481, folio 457).

Iltutmish, who would follow him, was his slave before he was his son-in-law. Rizvi writes that "Sultan Qutbu'd-Din Aibak, also a former slave, during a visit to Ghazna after his Anhilwara expedition in 1196, took a fancy to Iltutmish and was able to buy him in Delhi, and between 1197 or 1198 to 1206 Iltutmish rose to prominence" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 153, folio 135, footnote 2). In Bhandari's account, 'اورا سلطان قطب الدین ایبک به[OCR?] لک جیتل خریده بفرزندی گرفته دختر خود را درمناکحت او درآورده بود': Aibak bought him for a lakh of *jitals*, adopted him and married him to his daughter. 'بعد فتح گوالیار امارت انجا باو ارزانی داشت', after the conquest of Gwalior he granted him its command; then he gave him 'برن[OCR?]' and its region and, step by step, Budaun, until Iltutmish rose to be *amir al-umara* and 'خط آزادی از سلطان یافت', received his letter of manumission from the Sultan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 399). Shemeem Burney Abbas, recounting her own ancestors' appointment as muftis of Lahore under "the Muslim slave king Shamsuddin Iltutmish", adds that "Qutubuddin Aibek was himself a slave king" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 25, folio not stated).

He was succeeded eventually by his son-in-law Iltutmish. The books differ on what came between. Bhandari writes that 'سلطان مرحوم پسر صلبی که سزاوار جهانبانی تواند بود نداشت', the late Sultan had no son of his own body fit to rule; the nobles therefore seated 'آرام بخش پسرخوانده سلطان', Aram Bakhsh, the Sultan's adopted son and the only heir, 'درسنه ۶۰۷[OCR?] درخطه دل کشاۓ لاهور', in the year 607 in the delightful region of Lahore, as Sultan Aram Shah (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 400); but 'از سلطان آرام شاه امور خلافت تمشیت نیافت', under Aram Shah the affairs of state did not prosper (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 399). Chishti calls 'آرام' his son, deposed after a year by 'شمس الدین', who was Aibak's slave and son-in-law (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 809). Kanhaiya Lal names his successor on the throne of Delhi as 'سلطان شمس الدین التمش' (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 25, folio 20), and Abbas has Iltutmish ascend the throne of Delhi "after the death of his master, Qutubuddin Aibek" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 25, folio not stated).

A different Qutbuddin must not be confused with him. On the same page as her mention of Aibak's mosque, Schimmel writes of Qutbuddin Bakhtiyar Kaki of Farghana, who died in 1235 and whose "rather modest compound in Merauli near the Qutb Minar in Delhi is usually filled with pious pilgrims" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 377, folio 346); her index lists him separately (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 513, folio 482).

## Lakh Bakhsh: His Names and His Giving

Bhandari explains the name Aibak: 'چون انگشت خنصر او شکسته بود ازینجهت اورا ایبک گفتندے', because his little finger was broken, he was called Aibak (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 401).

His title Lakh Bakhsh, the giver of lakhs, is explained by his largesse. Kanhaiya Lal ties it to Lahore: there he 'بہت [illegible] رعایا کو [illegible] خوش کیا اور کثرت داد و دہش سے سلطان لکھ بخش خطاب لیا', pleased the subjects and, by the abundance of his giving, took the title Sultan Lakh Bakhsh (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 25, folio 20). Bhandari gives a long passage in praise of his giving, 'دست جود وسخایش گرد افلاس ازچهره مفلسان برافشاندی', which ends 'چون لکها باهل احتیاج انعام دادی ازینجهت درهند اورا لکهه بخش گفتندی': because he gave lakhs to the needy, in Hind they called him Lakh-bakhsh (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 400). A couplet in his praise follows:

باهمت او حوصله دریا تنگ
بارفعت او مرتبه گردون پست

The couplet closes Bhandari's praise of the Sultan's generosity (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 400).

The books write his name in several forms: 'قطب الدین ایبک' in Kanhaiya Lal and Bhandari (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 24, folio 19; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 401), "Sultan Qutub-uddin Aibak" in Alam's note (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, pp. 184–185, folios 184–185), Qutbu’d-Din Aibak in Rizvi (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 139, folio 121), Qutubuddin Aibek in Abbas (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 25, folio not stated) and Aybek in Schimmel (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 377, folio 346).

## Death at Chaugan

He died in Lahore in November 1210 after a fall from his horse while playing *chaugan*, an early form of polo. Kanhaiya Lal tells it in one line: 'اور لاہور ہی کے مقام پر گیند کھیلتے ہوئے گھوڑے سے گر کر مر گیا', and at Lahore itself, playing ball, he fell from his horse and died; he gives no year (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 25, folio 20). Bhandari has 'درسنه [illegible] درچوگان بازی از اسپ افتاده گوۓ زندگی بجولانگاه آخرت رسانید', that he fell from his horse at *chaugan* and died; the year cannot be read, and Bhandari names no place of death (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 400). Chishti gives the year as 'سن بارہ سو دس [OCR?]', twelve hundred and ten, the reading flagged as uncertain (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 809).

Naveed Alam's translation of Madho Lal Hussein recalls the death in a note on the game itself: "The game was brought to the subcontinent by the Central Asian armies. Sultan Qutub-uddin Aibak, known for the famous Qutb Minar, died playing polo in Lahore in AD 1210." (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, pp. 184–185, folios 184–185). The verse is Hussein's own, a *kafi* that watches a polo match and does not name the Sultan:

*Let me stand and watch for a few days / the polo tournament. Who wins, who loses, / whose brisk horse makes a clever move? … Let's see who hits the ball / across the field. The crowd cheers and jeers* (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 113, folio 113, kafi 90)

## The Tomb

Chishti's survey of Lahore's tombs is the only book consulted that describes the grave. He places it 'دروازہ لاہوری کے باہر بطرف شمال', outside the Lahori Gate on the north side, east of a 'گودام [OCR?] شراب', a liquor godown, 'واقعہ صدر بازار انار کلی', situated in the Sadr Bazaar of Anarkali. In his day a 'قصاب [OCR?] خانہ', a butcher's house, stood on its land, and 'طوائف لوگوں', courtesans, occupied it (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 271).

He records what had been lost. Under Sikh rule there stood 'عمارات عالیشان سنگ [illegible]', lofty buildings of stone; 'مہاراجہ نے سب مسمار کرکے روانہ امرتسر کر دین', the Maharaja demolished them all and sent them off to Amritsar; and there remained 'ایک قبر خشتی [OCR?] بلند چبوترہ پر', a single brick grave on a raised platform. Old people, he writes, had seen a 'گنبد دو منزلہ سنگ مرمر کا', a two-storey dome of marble, unmatched anywhere round Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 271).

Chishti judged that no building near Lahore was older than this grave, because Qutb al-Din was '[illegible] سی', and because 'سلطنت عوریہ یہاں چغتائی [OCR?] سے بہت مدت پہلے تھی', the Ghurid sultanate was here long before the Chaghatai; for the fuller story he refers the reader to 'تواریخ ہند', the histories of Hind. He left more unsaid on purpose: because of the courtesans, what might have been written, '[illegible] لکھا جاوے قلم انداز کیا گیا', was omitted (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 271–272).

The modern accounts tell the story another way. Punjab's Directorate General of Archaeology states that the grave was "originally... only one grave without any tomb or structure," that a marble tomb was later "pulled down by Ranjit Singh," and that the department itself "built the present tomb over the grave in 1990." Earlier still, according to Dawn's "Harking Back" column, Sultan Iltutmish had ordered "a simple small room, one barely able to enclose the grave": at first a mud room, it was later bricked in during Akbar's reign. For long the site was a simple, informal grave hemmed in by the houses of the Anarkali bazaar.

The same column dates the present mausoleum's actual construction to 1972, ordered by Prime Minister Zulfiqar Ali Bhutto after the Bangladesh war, who is quoted remarking that Aibak "was a great and kind ruler and deserved the respect of the poor"; the design was led by the architect Kamil Khan Mumtaz. Daily Times likewise places the rebuilding "during the 1970s," rather than in 1990 as the Archaeology Department's own page states. The structure visitors see today is essentially this 1970s reconstruction by Pakistan's Department of Archaeology and Museums, deliberately evoking early Sultanate-period style within a small walled garden.

The other books are silent on the tomb. Kanhaiya Lal's history of Lahore names no grave for him, and its table of contents lists no tomb of his (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 2–5); Bhandari names no burial (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 400); and Rizvi, Abbas, Schimmel and Alam mention none.

## The Tomb Today

Unlike Lahore's Sufi shrines, the tomb hosts no *ʿurs*, no pilgrimage tradition and no ongoing devotional practice. It is maintained purely as a protected historical monument, and none of the books consulted records any observance at the grave.

## Setting: Anarkali and Ghurid Lahore

What the books say of the quarter and the city is context, not a description of the tomb. Kanhaiya Lal's *Tarikh-e Lahore* knows Anarkali only as a cantonment and a bazaar (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 34–35, folios 37–38). Haroon Khalid's *A White Trail* says that the Anarkali market "is named after the legendary lover of the Mughal Emperor Jahangir", that the area "over the years has become the largest tyre market in the city", and that "The Pak Tea House, just a few shops away, once famed for its gatherings of the leading intelligentsia of the city, is now on its way to becoming yet another tyre shop" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 66, folio not stated).

Aibak's Lahore was a city the Ghurids had only lately taken. Bhandari tells how 'خسروشاه بن بهرام شاه', driven from Ghazni by the Ghurids, took Lahore and died there in 555 (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 405); his son Khusraw Malik held it for twenty-eight years, 'مدت حکومت او درلاهور بست وهشت سال', while Shihab al-Din attacked Lahore again and again (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 405). In the campaign Bhandari dates 582, Khusraw Malik submitted and was taken to Ghazni, where he died, and the Sultan 'ودرلاهور نایب خود نصب کرد', set his own deputy in Lahore (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 404). Kanhaiya Lal marks the same turn: with Khusrau Malik's surrender, 'سلاطین غوریہ کی سلطنت کا ہند میں آغاز ہوا اور غزنویہ حکومت با اختتام پہنچی', the rule of the Ghurid sultans in Hind began and the Ghaznavid government came to its end (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 24, folio 19).

After Aibak's death Lahore was briefly a royal seat again: his heir Aram Shah was enthroned there, and when Iltutmish came, Aram Shah moved 'از لاهور', from Lahore, toward Delhi, drew up his ranks and fled 'باندک محاربه', after little fighting (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 399). In Kanhaiya Lal's account Yalduz rose again in Iltutmish's reign, took Multan and came to Lahore 'اور اُس نے دل کھول کر پنجاب کو غارت کیا', and plundered the Punjab freely (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 25, folio 20).

Chishti's general account of the city sets the loss of old Lahore's monuments in the Sikh period, which is the setting for his report of this tomb's demolition. In Ranjit Singh's time, he writes, red, black and marble stones 'گرد و نواح لاہور کے', in the neighbourhood of Lahore, were torn down (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 296); when the cantonments of Ranjit Singh's armies were built the bricks of old tombs and houses were pulled out, and 'ہزار ہا مقابر و گنبد و مساجد و باغات و حویلیہائے[OCR?] قدیمہ جو یادگار امرائے سلف تھیں گم گشتیں[OCR?]', thousands of tombs, domes, mosques, gardens and old mansions that were memorials of the nobles of the past were lost (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 431).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The year of his death.** The current account gives November 1210. Chishti gives 'سن بارہ سو دس [OCR?]', 1210, the reading flagged as uncertain (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 809), and Alam's note gives "AD 1210" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, pp. 184–185, folios 184–185). Kanhaiya Lal gives no year (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 25, folio 20), and in Bhandari the year is illegible (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 400). Schimmel's index prints "(d. 1206)" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 523). Rizvi's index prints "(1206-1210)", a span of rule, and gives no death date (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 481, folio 457). Abbas prints a year at this point that cannot be read in the copy consulted (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 25, folio not stated).
- **Where he died.** The current account, Kanhaiya Lal and Alam place his death at Lahore (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 25, folio 20; Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, pp. 184–185, folios 184–185). Bhandari tells the fall at *chaugan* but names no place (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 400). Rizvi places him at Delhi and Ghazni only and has no Lahore for him (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 481, folio 457).
- **Where Ghori left him.** Rizvi has Muhammad bin Sam leave him "as head of his forces in the occupied city" of Delhi (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 139, folio 121). Bhandari has him made viceroy at Kuhram, seventy *kos* from Delhi, taking Delhi himself afterwards (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 401–402).
- **When and where he became sovereign.** The current account has him establish independent rule based in Lahore after Ghori's assassination in 1206. Chishti has him take his seat on the throne of Delhi in 'چھ سو دو[OCR?]', 602 (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 267), and elsewhere has Ghori die in 1206 and Aibak, ruler of Delhi, claim Hindustan (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 809). Kanhaiya Lal has Ghori hand him the throne of Delhi (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 24, folio 19). Bhandari has him come from Delhi to Lahore and strike coin and read the *khutba* in his own name on 11 Rabiʿ al-Awwal 603, the year's reading uncertain (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 401).
- **Yildiz and Ghazni.** Bhandari has Yildiz contest Lahore, lose a pitched battle and settle in Kirman, and gives Aibak four years of the sultanate of Ghazni (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 400). Kanhaiya Lal has Aibak defeat Yalduz, pursue him to Ghazni and come back to Lahore (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 25, folio 20). Chishti has him crowned at Ghazni, grow 'آرام طلب', a lover of ease, and be driven back (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 809).
- **How long he ruled.** Chishti says he lived five years after his master, the number's reading uncertain (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 809). Bhandari says he governed Hindustan for twenty years (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 400). Rizvi's index brackets his name with 1206 and 1210 (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 481, folio 457).
- **His successor.** The current account has him succeeded eventually by his son-in-law Iltutmish. Bhandari has the nobles seat Aram Bakhsh, his adopted son, at Lahore as Aram Shah, saying the Sultan had no son of his own body (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 400). Chishti calls 'آرام' his son, deposed after a year by Shams al-Din (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 809). Kanhaiya Lal names Iltutmish as his successor on the throne of Delhi with no reign between (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 25, folio 20), and Abbas has Iltutmish ascend "after the death of his master" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 25, folio not stated).
- **How he acquired Iltutmish.** Rizvi has him buy Iltutmish in Delhi, having taken a fancy to him on a visit to Ghazna after the Anhilwara expedition of 1196 (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 153, folio 135, footnote 2). Bhandari has him buy Iltutmish for a lakh of *jitals*, adopt him and marry him to his daughter, and names no place (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 399).
- **Where he was called Lakh Bakhsh.** Kanhaiya Lal ties the title to his largesse at Lahore (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 25, folio 20). Bhandari says that 'درهند', in Hind, he was called Lakh-bakhsh (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 400).
- **What once stood over the grave.** The Directorate General of Archaeology says it was originally only a grave without any tomb or structure, and that a marble tomb was later pulled down by Ranjit Singh. Dawn's column has Iltutmish order a simple small room, first of mud and bricked in under Akbar. Chishti reports lofty buildings of stone under Sikh rule, demolished by the Maharaja and sent to Amritsar, and a two-storey marble dome that old people remembered (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 271). The current account adds that historians dispute whether a substantial mausoleum ever stood over the grave in the medieval period.
- **When the present tomb was built.** The Directorate General of Archaeology says its department built the present tomb in 1990. Dawn's column dates the construction to 1972, under Zulfiqar Ali Bhutto, and Daily Times places the rebuilding in the 1970s.

## Legacy

The tomb is valued chiefly for its symbolic role, marking the thirteenth-century founding moment of Muslim dynastic rule in South Asia, centred, briefly, on Lahore itself. Bhandari makes the same claim for Delhi: from his time, 'ازان تاریخ شهر دهلی دارالخلافت سلاطین مسلمین مقرر گردیده', Delhi was fixed as the capital of the Muslim sultans (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 401). Chishti, for whom the land of Hindustan had been 'بے چراغ' before him (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 267), held his grave to be older than any other building near Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 271–272).

Aibak, according to Dawn's column, had also wanted Lahore's dead buried together, designating land south of Mozang village as a graveyard for "at least 150,000 dead," the origin of the present Miani Sahib graveyard.

Bhutto's remark, as Dawn reports it, that Aibak "was a great and kind ruler and deserved the respect of the poor" stands beside the older memory of the Lakh Bakhsh, whose giving Bhandari praises in prose and verse (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 400).
