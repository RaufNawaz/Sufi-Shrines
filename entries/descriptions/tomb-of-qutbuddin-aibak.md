## Overview

Tucked amid the crowded lanes of Aibak Road in the Anarkali quarter of Lahore, a modest garden tomb marks the burial place of Sultan Qutb ud-Din Aibak, founder of the Mamluk, or "Slave," dynasty and the first ruler of the Delhi Sultanate. A slave who rose through service to the Ghurid ruler Muhammad Ghori, he established independent rule based in Lahore after Ghori's death, laying the foundations of Muslim dynastic government in the subcontinent, and he is remembered too as the patron who commissioned Delhi's Qutb Minar. He died in Lahore in November 1210 after a fall from his horse while playing *chaugan*, an early form of polo. The tomb is not a Sufi shrine: it hosts no *ʿurs*, no pilgrimage tradition and no ongoing devotional practice, and it is maintained as a protected historical monument, valued chiefly for its symbolic role marking the thirteenth-century founding moment of Muslim dynastic rule in South Asia, centred, briefly, on Lahore itself.

The tomb's physical history has been anything but continuous. Historians dispute whether a substantial mausoleum ever stood over the grave in the medieval period; for long the site was a simple, informal grave hemmed in by the houses of the Anarkali bazaar; and the structure visitors see today is essentially a reconstruction of the 1970s, though one official account dates it to 1990.

The books consulted add a good deal to this picture. Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti's survey of the tombs and buildings of Lahore, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, is the only one of them to describe the grave itself: he places it in the Sadr Bazaar of Anarkali, records a lost two-storey dome of marble that old people remembered, and says the Maharaja of his day demolished the buildings and sent their stone to Amritsar (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 271). Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* gives the fullest account of the life: the purchased slave made viceroy, his campaigns, his accession at Lahore, his generosity, his name and his death at *chaugan* (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 400; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 401–402). Kanhaiya Lal's *Tarikh-e Lahore* places his death at Lahore and explains his title Lakh Bakhsh, but names no grave for him (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 25, folio 20). Naveed Alam's translation of Madho Lal Hussein, Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi, Shemeem Burney Abbas and Annemarie Schimmel mention him in passing, and the year of his death is given in more than one way.

## The Life of the Sultan

Aibak was born around 1150 in Turkestan and sold into slavery as a child. He rose through service to the Ghurid ruler Muhammad Ghori, and came to command Ghurid territories in northern India following the decisive Second Battle of Tarain in 1192.

Bhandari calls him plainly Sultan Qutb al-Din Aibak, the purchased slave of Sultan Shihab al-Din, governing Hindustan as his deputy (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 401). After the victory at Narain, he writes, Sultan Shihab al-Din left Malik Qutb al-Din Aibak, his chosen slave, as viceroy at the *qasba* of Kuhram, seventy *kos* from Delhi (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 402). Rizvi's history of Sufism tells it differently: "Mu‘izzu’d-Din Muhammad bin Sam had conquered Delhi and, departing for Ghazni, left his favourite slave, Qutbu’d-Din Aibak, as head of his forces in the occupied city" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 139, folio 121).

As deputy, after the Sultan went back to Ghazni, Bhandari continues, he took the forts of Delhi and Meerut in the shortest time and set governors in every place; in the second year he took the fort of Kol, and then the forts of Gwalior, Budaun and others (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 401–402). He marched on Gujarat and took revenge on Rai Bhim Dev of Gujarat for the Sultan's earlier defeat, which the book has already recorded, plundered the country, returned to Delhi and put the *khutba* and the coin into currency in the Sultan's name (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 401). In the Sultan's tenth campaign, against the Khokhars near Lahore, Malik Qutb al-Din too came from Delhi to the Sultan's service, and together they chastised that tribe (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 401).

Rizvi describes the occupation: "Occupying troops stationed at Indraprastha near Delhi under Qutbu’d-Din Aibak were forced to make several invasions in the Doab and the regions around Ajmer to consolidate their power" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 140, folio 122). In the same account he records that "Qutbu’d-Din had appointed Saiyid Husain Mashhadi as the darogha (military governor) of the area. The Saiyid served the Khwaja with great devotion" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 140, folio 122).

His captains carried the conquest east. In Bhandari's words, Islam in Bengal began with Malik Muhammad Bakhtiyar, one of Aibak's great amirs, and from that time the province was in the hands of the Sultan of Delhi; the word for 'in the hands of' is uncertain in the printed text (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 208). Chishti tells of a general who sent his booty from Bihar to his master at Delhi, of a rutting elephant, and of the governorship of Bihar and an order to take Bengal (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 809).

Delhi was his seat. Bhandari records that he and Iltutmish lived in Rai Pithora's fort (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 561), and that from then on Delhi was the capital of the Muslim sultans (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 401). He is remembered as the patron who commissioned Delhi's Qutb Minar, and Alam's note calls him "known for the famous Qutb Minar" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, pp. 184–185, folios 184–185). Schimmel, writing of the death of Muʿinuddin in 1236, places it "not far from the beautiful mosque" that Aibak, whom she calls 'of Delhi', "had erected in Ajmer" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 377, folio 346).

## The Accession

After Ghori's assassination in 1206, Aibak established independent rule based in Lahore. Chishti's history gives Muhammad Ghori's death as the year twelve hundred and six (1206), a reading uncertain in the printed text, and says he died without a son; Qutb al-Din, the ruler of Delhi, then claimed Hindustan (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 809). Elsewhere Chishti sets the accession in six hundred and two, a reading uncertain in the printed text, with the figure 602 in the margin, when he took his seat on the throne of Delhi; before that, he writes, the land of Hindustan was without a lamp (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 267). Kanhaiya Lal states his status plainly: Shahab-ud-Din Ghori handed the throne of Delhi to Sultan Qutb al-Din Aibak, his own slave, a word of the title being illegible in the printed text (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 24, folio 19).

Bhandari puts the moment of sovereignty at Lahore. After the Sultan attained martyrdom, he came out of Delhi, reached Lahore and took the throne in his own right, and on the eleventh of Rabiʿ al-Awwal of the year 603 had the coin struck and the *khutba* read in his own name; the reading of the year is uncertain in the printed text (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 401). Sultan Ghiyas al-Din Mahmud, Shihab al-Din's nephew, the name Mahmud uncertain in the printed text, sent him from Firozkoh the parasol and the trappings of kingship and addressed him with the title of Sultan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 400–401).

## The Contest with Yildiz

His rival for the Ghurid inheritance was Taj al-Din Yildiz, whom the books spell variously. Bhandari calls Taj al-Din Yalduz one of Shihab al-Din's special slaves, who had been given the name of sultan in Ghazni after Shihab al-Din's death; a dispute arose between the two over Lahore, and they fought a pitched battle. At last Taj al-Din could not hold out, was routed and settled in Kirman, and the Sultan, victorious, gave himself to rule in complete independence (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 400).

In Kanhaiya Lal's history Yalduz, having seized Ghazni, came on to the Punjab to take the throne of Hind from Qutb al-Din (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 25, folio 20). Aibak defeated him in the field and pursued him to Ghazni, and then Qutb al-Din came from there to Lahore (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 25, folio 20). Chishti, for his part, has him defeat a rival whose printed name, perhaps Alduz, cannot be read with certainty, and be crowned at Ghazni, where he grew a lover of ease and was driven back (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 809).

## His Rule and His Household

Chishti says he ruled with great ability, and lived five years after his master, the reading of the number being uncertain (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 809). Bhandari reckons the whole of his government thus: he governed Hindustan for twenty years, the sultanate of Ghazni among them for four (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 400). Rizvi's index gives the bracket "(1206-1210)" after his name (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 481, folio 457).

Iltutmish, who would follow him, was his slave before he was his son-in-law. Rizvi writes that "Sultan Qutbu'd-Din Aibak, also a former slave, during a visit to Ghazna after his Anhilwara expedition in 1196, took a fancy to Iltutmish and was able to buy him in Delhi, and between 1197 or 1198 to 1206 Iltutmish rose to prominence" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 153, folio 135, footnote 2). In Bhandari's account, Aibak bought him for what the printed text gives, not quite legibly, as a lakh of *jitals*, adopted him and married him to his daughter. After the conquest of Gwalior he granted him its command; then he gave him a district whose printed name, perhaps Baran, cannot be read with certainty, and its region and, step by step, Budaun, until Iltutmish rose to be *amir al-umara* and received his letter of manumission from the Sultan (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 399). Shemeem Burney Abbas, recounting her own ancestors' appointment as muftis of Lahore under "the Muslim slave king Shamsuddin Iltutmish", adds that "Qutubuddin Aibek was himself a slave king" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 25, folio not stated).

He was succeeded eventually by his son-in-law Iltutmish. The books differ on what came between. Bhandari writes that the late Sultan had no son of his own body fit to rule; the nobles therefore seated Aram Bakhsh, the Sultan's adopted son and the only heir, as Sultan Aram Shah, in the year 607 in the delightful region of Lahore, the reading of the year uncertain in the printed text (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 400); but under Aram Shah the affairs of state did not prosper (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 399). Chishti calls Aram his son, deposed after a year by Shams al-Din, who was Aibak's slave and son-in-law (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 809). Kanhaiya Lal names his successor on the throne of Delhi as Sultan Shams al-Din Iltutmish (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 25, folio 20), and Abbas has Iltutmish ascend the throne of Delhi "after the death of his master, Qutubuddin Aibek" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 25, folio not stated).

A different Qutbuddin must not be confused with him. On the same page as her mention of Aibak's mosque, Schimmel writes of Qutbuddin Bakhtiyar Kaki of Farghana, who died in 1235 and whose "rather modest compound in Merauli near the Qutb Minar in Delhi is usually filled with pious pilgrims" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 377, folio 346); her index lists him separately (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 513, folio 482).

## Lakh Bakhsh: His Names and His Giving

Bhandari explains the name Aibak: because his little finger was broken, he was called Aibak (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 401).

His title Lakh Bakhsh, the giver of lakhs, is explained by his largesse. Kanhaiya Lal ties it to Lahore: there, in a sentence partly illegible in the printed text, he pleased the subjects and, by the abundance of his giving, took the title Sultan Lakh Bakhsh (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 25, folio 20). Bhandari gives a long passage in praise of his giving, in which the hand of his generosity brushed the dust of poverty from the faces of the poor, and which ends: because he gave lakhs to the needy, in Hind they called him Lakh-bakhsh (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 400). A couplet in his praise follows:

باهمت او حوصله دریا تنگ
بارفعت او مرتبه گردون پست

The couplet closes Bhandari's praise of the Sultan's generosity (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 400).

The books write his name in several forms: Qutb al-Din Aibak in Kanhaiya Lal and Bhandari (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 24, folio 19; Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 401), "Sultan Qutub-uddin Aibak" in Alam's note (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, pp. 184–185, folios 184–185), Qutbu’d-Din Aibak in Rizvi (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 139, folio 121), Qutubuddin Aibek in Abbas (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 25, folio not stated) and Aybek in Schimmel (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 377, folio 346).

## Death at Chaugan

He died in Lahore in November 1210 after a fall from his horse while playing *chaugan*, an early form of polo. Kanhaiya Lal tells it in one line: at Lahore itself, playing ball, he fell from his horse and died; he gives no year (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 25, folio 20). Bhandari has it that he fell from his horse at *chaugan* and carried the ball of his life to the field of the hereafter; the year cannot be read, and Bhandari names no place of death (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 400). Chishti gives the year as twelve hundred and ten (1210), the reading flagged as uncertain (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 809).

Naveed Alam's translation of Madho Lal Hussein recalls the death in a note on the game itself: "The game was brought to the subcontinent by the Central Asian armies. Sultan Qutub-uddin Aibak, known for the famous Qutb Minar, died playing polo in Lahore in AD 1210." (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, pp. 184–185, folios 184–185). The verse is Hussein's own, a *kafi* that watches a polo match and does not name the Sultan:

*Let me stand and watch for a few days / the polo tournament. Who wins, who loses, / whose brisk horse makes a clever move? … Let's see who hits the ball / across the field. The crowd cheers and jeers* (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 113, folio 113, kafi 90)

## The Tomb

Chishti's survey of Lahore's tombs is the only book consulted that describes the grave. He places it outside the Lahori Gate on the north side, east of a liquor godown, situated in the Sadr Bazaar of Anarkali. In his day a butcher's house stood on its land, and courtesans occupied it; the words for the godown and the butcher are both uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 271).

He records what had been lost. Under Sikh rule there stood lofty buildings of stone, the kind of stone illegible in the printed text; the Maharaja demolished them all and sent them off to Amritsar; and there remained a single grave on a raised platform, of brick by an uncertain reading. Old people, he writes, had seen a two-storey dome of marble, unmatched anywhere round Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 271).

Chishti judged that no building near Lahore was older than this grave, because of something said of Qutb al-Din in words the printed text leaves illegible, and because the Ghurid sultanate was here long before the Chaghatai, a name uncertain in the printed text; for the fuller story he refers the reader to the histories of Hind. He left more unsaid on purpose: because of the courtesans, what might have been written was omitted, the opening of that sentence being illegible (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 271–272).

The modern accounts tell the story another way. Punjab's Directorate General of Archaeology states that the grave was "originally... only one grave without any tomb or structure," that a marble tomb was later "pulled down by Ranjit Singh," and that the department itself "built the present tomb over the grave in 1990." Earlier still, according to Dawn's "Harking Back" column, Sultan Iltutmish had ordered "a simple small room, one barely able to enclose the grave": at first a mud room, it was later bricked in during Akbar's reign. For long the site was a simple, informal grave hemmed in by the houses of the Anarkali bazaar.

The same column dates the present mausoleum's actual construction to 1972, ordered by Prime Minister Zulfiqar Ali Bhutto after the Bangladesh war, who is quoted remarking that Aibak "was a great and kind ruler and deserved the respect of the poor"; the design was led by the architect Kamil Khan Mumtaz. Daily Times likewise places the rebuilding "during the 1970s," rather than in 1990 as the Archaeology Department's own page states. The structure visitors see today is essentially this 1970s reconstruction by Pakistan's Department of Archaeology and Museums, deliberately evoking early Sultanate-period style within a small walled garden.

The other books are silent on the tomb. Kanhaiya Lal's history of Lahore names no grave for him, and its table of contents lists no tomb of his (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 2–5); Bhandari names no burial (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 400); and Rizvi, Abbas, Schimmel and Alam mention none.

## The Tomb Today

Unlike Lahore's Sufi shrines, the tomb hosts no *ʿurs*, no pilgrimage tradition and no ongoing devotional practice. It is maintained purely as a protected historical monument, and none of the books consulted records any observance at the grave.

## Setting: Anarkali and Ghurid Lahore

What the books say of the quarter and the city is context, not a description of the tomb. Kanhaiya Lal's *Tarikh-e Lahore* knows Anarkali only as a cantonment and a bazaar (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 34–35, folios 37–38). Haroon Khalid's *A White Trail* says that the Anarkali market "is named after the legendary lover of the Mughal Emperor Jahangir", that the area "over the years has become the largest tyre market in the city", and that "The Pak Tea House, just a few shops away, once famed for its gatherings of the leading intelligentsia of the city, is now on its way to becoming yet another tyre shop" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 66, folio not stated).

Aibak's Lahore was a city the Ghurids had only lately taken. Bhandari tells how Khusraw Shah son of Bahram Shah, driven from Ghazni by the Ghurids, took Lahore and died there in 555 (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 405); his son Khusraw Malik held it for twenty-eight years, while Shihab al-Din attacked Lahore again and again (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 405). In the campaign Bhandari dates 582, Khusraw Malik submitted and was taken to Ghazni, where he died, and the Sultan set his own deputy in Lahore (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 404). Kanhaiya Lal marks the same turn: with Khusrau Malik's surrender, the rule of the Ghurid sultans in Hind began and the Ghaznavid government came to its end (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 24, folio 19).

After Aibak's death Lahore was briefly a royal seat again: his heir Aram Shah was enthroned there, and when Iltutmish came, Aram Shah moved from Lahore toward Delhi, drew up his ranks and fled after little fighting (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 399). In Kanhaiya Lal's account Yalduz rose again in Iltutmish's reign, took Multan and came to Lahore, and plundered the Punjab freely (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 25, folio 20).

Chishti's general account of the city sets the loss of old Lahore's monuments in the Sikh period, which is the setting for his report of this tomb's demolition. In Ranjit Singh's time, he writes, red, black and marble stones in the neighbourhood of Lahore were torn down (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 296); when the cantonments of Ranjit Singh's armies were built the bricks of old tombs and houses were pulled out, and thousands of tombs, domes, mosques, gardens and old mansions that were memorials of the nobles of the past were lost, in a sentence some of whose words are uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 431).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The year of his death.** The current account gives November 1210. Chishti gives twelve hundred and ten, 1210, the reading flagged as uncertain (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 809), and Alam's note gives "AD 1210" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, pp. 184–185, folios 184–185). Kanhaiya Lal gives no year (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 25, folio 20), and in Bhandari the year is illegible (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 400). Schimmel's index prints "(d. 1206)" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 523). Rizvi's index prints "(1206-1210)", a span of rule, and gives no death date (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 481, folio 457). Abbas prints a year at this point that cannot be read in the copy consulted (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 25, folio not stated).
- **Where he died.** The current account, Kanhaiya Lal and Alam place his death at Lahore (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 25, folio 20; Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, pp. 184–185, folios 184–185). Bhandari tells the fall at *chaugan* but names no place (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 400). Rizvi places him at Delhi and Ghazni only and has no Lahore for him (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 481, folio 457).
- **Where Ghori left him.** Rizvi has Muhammad bin Sam leave him "as head of his forces in the occupied city" of Delhi (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 139, folio 121). Bhandari has him made viceroy at Kuhram, seventy *kos* from Delhi, taking Delhi himself afterwards (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, pp. 401–402).
- **When and where he became sovereign.** The current account has him establish independent rule based in Lahore after Ghori's assassination in 1206. Chishti has him take his seat on the throne of Delhi in six hundred and two (602), a reading uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 267), and elsewhere has Ghori die in 1206 and Aibak, ruler of Delhi, claim Hindustan (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 809). Kanhaiya Lal has Ghori hand him the throne of Delhi (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 24, folio 19). Bhandari has him come from Delhi to Lahore and strike coin and read the *khutba* in his own name on 11 Rabiʿ al-Awwal 603, the year's reading uncertain (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 401).
- **Yildiz and Ghazni.** Bhandari has Yildiz contest Lahore, lose a pitched battle and settle in Kirman, and gives Aibak four years of the sultanate of Ghazni (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 400). Kanhaiya Lal has Aibak defeat Yalduz, pursue him to Ghazni and come back to Lahore (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 25, folio 20). Chishti has him crowned at Ghazni, grow a lover of ease, and be driven back (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 809).
- **How long he ruled.** Chishti says he lived five years after his master, the number's reading uncertain (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 809). Bhandari says he governed Hindustan for twenty years (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 400). Rizvi's index brackets his name with 1206 and 1210 (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 481, folio 457).
- **His successor.** The current account has him succeeded eventually by his son-in-law Iltutmish. Bhandari has the nobles seat Aram Bakhsh, his adopted son, at Lahore as Aram Shah, saying the Sultan had no son of his own body (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 400). Chishti calls Aram his son, deposed after a year by Shams al-Din (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 809). Kanhaiya Lal names Iltutmish as his successor on the throne of Delhi with no reign between (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 25, folio 20), and Abbas has Iltutmish ascend "after the death of his master" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 25, folio not stated).
- **How he acquired Iltutmish.** Rizvi has him buy Iltutmish in Delhi, having taken a fancy to him on a visit to Ghazna after the Anhilwara expedition of 1196 (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 153, folio 135, footnote 2). Bhandari has him buy Iltutmish for a lakh of *jitals*, adopt him and marry him to his daughter, and names no place (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 399).
- **Where he was called Lakh Bakhsh.** Kanhaiya Lal ties the title to his largesse at Lahore (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 25, folio 20). Bhandari says that in Hind he was called Lakh-bakhsh (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 400).
- **What once stood over the grave.** The Directorate General of Archaeology says it was originally only a grave without any tomb or structure, and that a marble tomb was later pulled down by Ranjit Singh. Dawn's column has Iltutmish order a simple small room, first of mud and bricked in under Akbar. Chishti reports lofty buildings of stone under Sikh rule, demolished by the Maharaja and sent to Amritsar, and a two-storey marble dome that old people remembered (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 271). The current account adds that historians dispute whether a substantial mausoleum ever stood over the grave in the medieval period.
- **When the present tomb was built.** The Directorate General of Archaeology says its department built the present tomb in 1990. Dawn's column dates the construction to 1972, under Zulfiqar Ali Bhutto, and Daily Times places the rebuilding in the 1970s.

## Legacy

The tomb is valued chiefly for its symbolic role, marking the thirteenth-century founding moment of Muslim dynastic rule in South Asia, centred, briefly, on Lahore itself. Bhandari makes the same claim for Delhi: from his time, Delhi was fixed as the capital of the Muslim sultans (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 401). Chishti, for whom the land of Hindustan had been without a lamp before him (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 267), held his grave to be older than any other building near Lahore (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 271–272).

Aibak, according to Dawn's column, had also wanted Lahore's dead buried together, designating land south of Mozang village as a graveyard for "at least 150,000 dead," the origin of the present Miani Sahib graveyard.

Bhutto's remark, as Dawn reports it, that Aibak "was a great and kind ruler and deserved the respect of the poor" stands beside the older memory of the Lakh Bakhsh, whose giving Bhandari praises in prose and verse (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 400).
