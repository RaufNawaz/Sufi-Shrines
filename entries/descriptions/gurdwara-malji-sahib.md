## Overview

Gurdwara Sri Mal Ji Sahib stands in Nankana Sahib, near the railway station and close to Gurdwara Kiara Sahib, and commemorates one of the miracles of *Guru Nanak Dev Ji's* childhood. Its name comes from the *mal* — a shady tree, also called *van* or *jal* — beneath which the young Nanak, tending his father's cattle, once lay asleep in the heat of the day. According to the *sakhi*, a great cobra spread its hood over the sleeping boy's face to shade him from the sun. Nankana Sahib is the old Talwandi, the town in which Guru Nanak (1469–1539), whom Khalid calls "the first Guru", was born (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 314, folio 288), and the gurdwara is one of the several there that mark events from his childhood (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 43, folio 25).

Two of the books consulted name the gurdwara itself, both by Haroon Khalid. In *Walking with Nanak* he tells its legend and places it, with Kiari Sahib, furthest from the shrine of the Guru's birthplace (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 91, folio 73). In *A White Trail* he calls it "Mal Ji Sahib" and puts it about five hundred metres from Gurdwara Janam Asthan, near the railway station (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 191, folio not stated). A 1962 register of the Department of Archaeology, Khan Mohammad Waliullah Khan's *Sikh Shrines in West Pakistan*, likewise treats it as a real and distinct site, and gives its own account of the founding legend.

None of the other books names the gurdwara. Harish Dhillon's retelling of the Janamsakhis, Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* and Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi's history of Sufism speak of Talwandi and of the Guru's youth there, but none mentions this shrine (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 407, folio 383). What they say is gathered below as the history of the Guru and of his town, not of the gurdwara.

There is a second gurdwara of the same name, at Kanganpur in Kasur District. The 1962 register distinguishes the two in the same entry, and Khalid's *Walking with Nanak* prints a plate of "Gurdwara Malji Sahib at Kanganpur", a different site (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 169, plate). Nothing told of the Kanganpur shrine belongs to the gurdwara at Nankana Sahib.

## The Legend of the Cobra

The site's account tells the story in full. The young Nanak, tending his father's cattle, lay asleep beneath the *mal* tree in the heat of the day. The local landlord *Rai Bular*, riding through his fields with his attendants, came upon the sleeping child and saw that a great cobra had spread its hood over Nanak's face to shade him from the sun, withdrawing quietly as the men approached. This wonder — remembered together with the miracle of the untouched crop at nearby Kiara Sahib — is said to have convinced Rai Bular of the boy's spiritual greatness, so that he became a devoted follower of Guru Nanak and later granted him half of his estate.

The 1962 register tells it with a different emphasis. In its account Guru Nanak was resting under the tree while herding cattle, and Rai Bular first noticed that the tree's shadow stayed fixed over the sleeping Guru rather than moving with the sun; only afterwards, and separately, did he see the cobra shading him.

Khalid gives the story as a belief: "It is believed that Guru Nanak once fell asleep while his buffaloes were grazing nearby at the spot that Gurdwara Malji Sahib was constructed." When the sun fell on his face, "Miraculously, a cobra emerged at that time and extended its hood, protecting the Guru from the sun while he slept" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 91, folio 73). His version names no witness and says nothing of a fixed shadow. In *A White Trail* he tells of the boy Nanak, asleep while grazing buffaloes, shaded by a cobra's hood: "There is a Gurdwara about five hundred metres from Gurdwara Janamasthan that commemorates this spot. It is called Mal Ji Sahib, and is situated near the Nankana Sahib Railway station." (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 191, folio not stated). Later in the same book he names "Gurdwara Malji Sahib" as the place "where he encountered a snake" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated).

## The Gurdwara

A gurdwara was first built on the spot by *Diwan Kaura Mall* and later renovated in the time of *Maharaja Ranjit Singh*. It is larger and more ornate than Kiara Sahib. A central dome crowns it, with domed kiosks at the corners and porches along the hall. Its interior is faced with old ceramic tiles, each some four inches square and each depicting a cobra in memory of the miracle. The 1962 register records the gurdwara in its entry "Gurdwara Mal Ji Sahib", with a plate.

The books place it in the town. *A White Trail* puts it "about five hundred metres from Gurdwara Janamasthan" and near the railway station (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 191, folio not stated). *Walking with Nanak* sets it, with Kiari Sahib, "the furthest from Gurdwara Janamsthan" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 91, folio 73); the site's account likewise places it close to Kiara Sahib, the two forming the eastern cluster of the Nankana Sahib shrines.

No book gives a date for Diwan Kaura Mall's building or for the renovation. Of Ranjit Singh's patronage in general, not of this gurdwara, Khalid writes that "When Maharaja Ranjit Singh became the sovereign of Punjab at the end of the eighteenth century he spent large amounts of money on the renovation and expansion of many gurdwaras", and that he "attached vast tracts of land to these gurdwaras, so that the revenues from the lands could facilitate their functioning" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 34, folio 16). In *A White Trail* Khalid calls Ranjit Singh "the first Punjabi to rule Punjab", who renovated "many Gurdwaras all over Punjab" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 208, folio not stated), crowned "Ruler of Punjab" in 1801 (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 207, folio not stated). Khalid also writes that after the British took over the Punjab, and with the sectarian divisions among the Sikhs, "a large number of important Sikh Gurdwaras were being administered by Hindu mahants" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 189, folio not stated).

Since Partition the shrine has been maintained as part of Pakistan's Sikh heritage and is visited by pilgrims on the great anniversaries. Khalid writes of the town's gurdwaras as a group that "All year round most of them remain locked, only to be opened at the time of the festival. There are security guards employed at the entrance to all of them." (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 227, folio not stated). He records that the shrines of Nankana Sahib were in a dilapidated state when Giani Pratap decided to stay and look after them: "That was the first time someone had taken the initiative to take care of the gurdwaras in Nankana Sahib after Partition" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 90, folio 72). From the roof of a building in the city he saw "small white domes of the Gurdwaras" among the minarets of the mosques, and the railway station (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 225, folio not stated).

## The Boyhood of Guru Nanak at Talwandi

The gurdwara remembers a boy herding cattle in the fields of his town. What the books say of that boyhood is said of the Guru and of Talwandi, not of this gurdwara.

Dhillon, retelling the Janamsakhis, has Nanak born "On 14 April 1469" to Kalyan Chand Das Bedi, "or Mehta Kalu as he was affectionately called", accountant to the Bhatti family under Rai Bular; the village astrologer, Pandit Hardyal, foretold greatness for the child (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 17–18, folios 19–20; p. 18, folio 20). In Dhillon's account the Bhatti family was that of Rai Bhoe and his son Rai Bular, and the head of the Bedi family, Shiv Ram, was accountant to Rai Bhoe, succeeded in that office by his son Kalyan Chand Das Bedi (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 17, folio 19). At school the boy showed "a brilliance that had never before been seen in any child in Talwandi" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 18, folio 20). At thirteen he refused the sacred thread, the *janeu*, in his father's courtyard, saying that "it is our actions and our actions alone" that give position among men; Dhillon calls it "the beginning of a lifelong crusade against the performing of empty rites and rituals" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 19–21, folios 21–23). At sixteen, in Dhillon's telling, he herded cattle (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 23, folio 27), and he "became well acquainted with the dervish Sayyad Hussain and gained an intimate knowledge of the Sufi line of thought" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 24, folio 28). Talwandi, Dhillon writes, "lay in the path of numerous mendicants, sadhus and holy men", who camped in a grove of trees on the outskirts of the village (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 24, folio 28). The story of the *Sacha Sauda* begins with goods to be sold in Talwandi and ends in the forest and in Talwandi (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 25, folio 29; p. 38, folio 42).

Khalid's *Walking with Nanak* tells the same years in a reconstruction that he says is partly fictionalised (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 17, folio not stated). In it Mehta Kalu keeps "the accounts of the ruler of Talwindi, Rai Bular Bhatti", his friend (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 22, folio 4), and is "a kshatriye", printed in a damaged form, "(warrior caste) Hindu" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 29, folio 11). He calls his family "Bedis ... the ancient orators of the Vedas" and himself "an accountant" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 63, folio 45; p. 64, folio 46); elsewhere Khalid writes in his own voice that "Nanak belonged to the Bedi caste" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 100, folio 82). The boy herds buffaloes, which once stray into a field whose owner's name is uncertain in the printed text, and he is found hiding in a grove of trees outside the town by his father and "his employer and friend, Rai Bular" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 63, folio 45; p. 64, folio 46). At dusk he sits under 'this waan tree', its branches hung with cloths "tied as supplication", "about two furlongs" from home, listening to the call to prayer and the temple bells (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 59, folio 41; p. 60, folio 42).

The reconstruction tells the *janeu* story too. Nanak refuses the sacred thread proposed by his father and "the local priest of Rai Bhoi di Talwindi", which "caused a furore", and "Respectable Hindus started to avoid the Bedi" family (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 61–62, folios 43–44). His mentor is "Sayyed Hassan, a Muslim Sufi, who lived near his house", who teaches that "Islam teaches one to adopt the middle path" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 62, folio 44). He meets ascetics and Sufis on the outskirts of Talwandi and spends time at the abandoned mound there (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 67, folio 49; p. 66, folio 48). Outside the reconstruction, Khalid reports that the young Nanak "used to spend a lot of time in conversation with yogis, sadhus and dervish who would pass through Talwindi", on that mound: "It is believed that in the years between the incident of Sacha Sauda and his migration to Sultanpur, Nanak started to frequent this place", a period of six to seven years (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 91, folio 73; p. 92, folio 74).

Bhandari's Persian history records his birth and boyhood in the town in one line: Baba Nanak "درمکان تلونڈی رائے بهوئه[OCR?] سعادت ولادت یافته همانجا درخانه جد ابادری می بود", born at Talwandi Rai Bhoi and living there in his grandfather's house (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). The reading of the town's name is uncertain in the printed text. Bhandari adds that "درخورد سالگی" — in childhood — "علامات کشف و کرامات و ایات خارق عادات از و بمنصه ظهور رسید": signs of inner vision and of wonders beyond the ordinary appeared in him. The editor's footnote gives a variant reading, "ده سالگی", at the age of ten (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).

## Stories of His Birth

Other stories cluster around his birth. Khalid, citing Harish Dhillon's *The First Sikh Spiritual Master*, writes that "Mata Triptaji, the mother of Guru Nanak, did not have a child for a long time after her marriage and she would regularly visit an ancient Hindu temple situated on top of a mound not very far from Rai Bhoi di Talwindi to pray for a child" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 30–31, folio 13). In the reconstruction Tripta presents herself to sadhus and fakirs in "the forest around Rai Bhoi di Talwindi" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 21, folio 3). A booklet published by the Auqaf department at the shrine of Baba Naulakha Hazari says instead that "Guru Nanak was born after Baba Naulakha Hazari prayed for his mother" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 31, folio 13), and Khalid's companion, the historian Iqbal Qaiser, reports the same tradition: "The Sikhs don't accept it but the Muslim tradition is that Guru Nanak was born by the blessing of Baba Naulakh Hazari. It is believed that Nanak's parents came to this shrine and the saint blessed them after which Nanak was born." (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated). Khalid also reports that Meherban, son of Prithi Chand, "claims that Guru Nanak was born in Dera Chahal and not Rai Bhoi di Talwindi", while other Janamsakhi writers and Sikh scholars "are unanimous that Nanak was born in Rai Bhoi di Talwindi" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 43, folio 25).

## The Life of the Guru

The rest of the Guru's life is told far from Nankana Sahib, but the books return to Talwandi again and again. Khalid's *Walking with Nanak* gives him a single entry in its back matter: "Born Talwandi (now known as Nankana Sahib in Pakistan) on October 20, 1469"; "Guru Nanak (1469-1539)", "the first Guru, is believed to be the founder of Sikhism" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 314, folio 288). Khalid writes that Nanak "spent a major portion of his life in present-day Pakistan. He was born here", first preached travelling this region, "and eventually settled in a small village which is now on the Pakistani side of the border" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 16, folio not stated). Dhillon calls the Janamsakhis "cycles of tales purportedly drawn from the life of the first Guru of the Sikhs" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 9, folio 9), and the publisher's blurb on his book says that the earliest known Janamsakhi dates to 1658, "nearly 120 years after the death of Guru Nanak in 1539" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 271, folio not stated). Bhandari reports, with a "گویند", "they say", that Baba Nanak was born "درسنه هزار و پانصد و بست و شش بکرماجیت مطابق هشتصد و هشتاد هجری درزمان سلطان بهلول لودی", in the Bikrami year 1526, corresponding to 880 Hijri, in the time of Sultan Bahlul Lodi (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). Bhandari calls him "قافله سالار مسالک حقیقت و مشعله دار طریق طریقت", the leader of the caravan on the roads of truth and the torch-bearer of the mystic way (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).

From Talwandi, Dhillon writes, "Nanak moved to Sultanpur (around 60 km from Talwandi)" as keeper of Nawab Daulat Khan's *modikhana*, or store, bathing in the Bein rivulet and holding evening gatherings at which his hymns were heard (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 44, folio 49). "It is widely believed that it was in Sultanpur that the religious life of Nanak began", Khalid writes (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 54, folio 36). In Dhillon's retelling Nanak vanished into the stream for a week and was found on its bank saying: "There is only one God. There is no Hindu, no Mussalman." (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 45–46, folios 50–51). In Khalid's reconstruction the nobles of Sultanpur attacked him over the saying "'There is no Hindu, no Muslim'" and accused him of theft, but "when an audit was conducted, the accounts were shown to be in perfect order" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 106, folio 88). That saying, Khalid writes in his own voice, was questioned at the court at Sultanpur, at Malik Bhago's court in Eminabad, and by friends and family at Rai Bhoi di Talwandi itself (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 144, folio 126).

Dhillon has him marry on 24 September 1487 Sulakhini, "daughter of Mul Chand and Chando Rani of the town of Batala", and gives the births of his sons as "Sri Chand (8 September 1494) and Lakhmi Das (12 February 1497)" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 44, folio 49). Khalid's reconstruction names her "Sulakhni", "the daughter of Moolchand Khatri from the village of Pakhoke near Gurdaspur"; "Soon after their marriage the young couple was blessed with a son who was named Sri Chand" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 67, folio 49), and at Sultanpur "they were blessed with another son, whom they named Lachman Das" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 68, folio 50). Bhandari puts the marriage after the travels: the Guru journeyed through most of the world, then came to "قصبه بتاله", the town of Batala, married ("کدخدا گردید"), and settled "دردهی از دیهات بتاله برآب دریاے راوی", in one of the villages of Batala on the Ravi, which the book does not name (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).

The journeys took him far. Khalid sums them up: east "all the way to Bengal", south "to the island of Sri Lanka", north to Kashmir, Nepal and Tibet, then west "to Mecca and returned to settle at Kartarpur", mostly on foot, over "about twenty-four years" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 54, folio 36). Nanak set out from Talwandi with Mardana and came back to it between journeys; for Sultanpur "he travelled from Nankana" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 54, folio 36). Dhillon sets out two schemes of the *udasis*, the journeys. "According to the Puratan Janamsakhi… he undertook five journeys", from 1499 to 1524 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 54–55, folios 60–61); "More modern accounts" give four, spanning twenty-three years (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 55, folio 61); Dhillon himself holds that there were four (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 56, folio 62). Nanak "preferred to stay on the outskirts" of villages, which Khalid calls "a recurrent theme" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 53, folio 35), and "Whenever he would visit an area he would visit its religious shrines, Muslim, Hindu and of any other denomination" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 37, folio 19).

Dhillon brings him home to Talwandi twice. After the first journey, "Nanak and Mardana finally returned to Talwandi" in 1506; his mother, Mata Tripta, "felt great joy", and he spent time every day with Rai Bhoe, who "looked upon" Nanak "as his Guru" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 123, folio 137), while Mehta Kalu was "a little in awe of his son" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 124, folio 138). After Pak Pattan, "Nanak travelled back to Talwandi". The town "was now no longer an obscure little village but had become a place of pilgrimage", and he sat by Rai Bular, who was sick and failing fast (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 172, folio 188). Kartarpur, in its turn, became a town of pilgrims "like Talwandi" and Sultanpur (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 176, folio 193). In Khalid's reconstruction Nanak and Mardana leave "the familiar streets" of Talwandi with friends and families gathered "at the edge of the town" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 239, folio 213), and at the end Nanak sends for his family from Talwandi to Kartarpur (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 296, folio 270). On his road home from Chuhrkhana, Khalid writes, Nanak crossed "a vast jungle that existed then between Nankana and Chuhrkhana" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 86, folio 68).

Bhandari says that "عالم عالم خلایق از اطراف ممالک آمده مرید شدند", that crowds upon crowds came from every land to become his disciples, and that "مردانه نام مطرب", a singer named Mardana, sang his verses and drew people "در دام عقیدت", into the snare of devotion (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). He places "مکان بابا نانک", the place of Baba Nanak, twelve *kos* from Batala on the bank of the Ravi: "دوازده کروهے بتاله برلب دریاے راوی مکان بابا نانک است که تا حال اولادش درانجا سکونت دارد", where his descendants live to this day (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).

The books that speak of the succession agree that the Guru passed over his sons. Bhandari writes that he had a son, "لکهمیداس نام خلف رشید", but "چون دولت معنوی نصیب او نبود", since spiritual fortune was not his lot, he gave the title of Guru Angad to "لهنه نام کهتری عرف ترهن[OCR?]", a Khatri named Lehna, and made him successor "دروقت نزع", at the hour of death (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). Khalid writes that "On his deathbed, he appointed not his son but his most loyal student as his spiritual successor" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 16, folio not stated), and that Nanak asked Angad Dev to move to Khadur, away from Kartarpur, "to avoid the ire of his sons" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 290, folio 264). In his reconstruction Nanak calls Lehna "my angad (of one's body)", has Baba Budha "apply saffron paste on Bhai Lehna's forehead", lays "five copper coins and a coconut at Lehna's feet", and announces: "I have appointed Bhai Lehna, who is from now on to be known as Guru Angad, as my successor" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 301–302, folios 275–276). The Udasi sect, Khalid writes, "is believed to have been founded by the eldest son of Guru Nanak, Sri Chand", who, "Ignored by his father when it was time to appoint the next Guru", founded his own sect (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 36, folio 18). Dhillon gives the death in a parenthesis: "(The date: 22 September 1539.)" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 259, folio 287). Bhandari places it "درعهد سلطنت سلیم شاه افغان درعمر مابین هفتاد و هشتاد سالگی", in the reign of Salim Shah Afghan, at an age between seventy and eighty (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).

## Devotion and Practice

Pilgrims come to the gurdwara on the great anniversaries. None of the books describes a rite performed at this shrine in particular; what they record of worship and festival is said of Guru Nanak's followers or of Nankana Sahib as a whole.

Bhandari, writing in Persian in the fortieth regnal year of Aurangzeb, describes Baba Nanak's followers. "از مریدان و معتقدان بابا نانک اکثرے صاحب حال و مقبول المقال و اهل ریاضات و مستجاب الدعوات می باشند": many of his disciples and devotees, he says, are men of spiritual states and austerities whose prayers are answered (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 519). "خلاصه عبادت اینطایفه مطالعه اشعار مرشد خویش است که بسرود و نغمه میگویند": the sum of their worship is their master's verses, which they sing (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 519). Many have put away worldly ties, and they live "با دوستان یکرنگ و با دشمنان بی جنگ", of one colour with their friends and without war with their enemies; "اعتقادی که این فریق بر مقتداے خویش دارند در طوایف دیگر کم نشان یافته میشود", the faith these people have in their leader is seldom found among others (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 519). Their master's name is always on their tongues; they count service to the traveller, "درخدمت صادر و وارد", as "عبادت عظمی", the greatest worship; and whoever arrives at midnight speaking Baba Nanak's name they treat as a brother and a friend and serve, even a stranger, a thief, a highwayman or an evildoer (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 519). Among the dervishes of Hind he counts the Udasis, "چهارمین صنف اوداسی از معتقدان بابا نانک", who worship in their master's way and whose worship, too, is singing his verses (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 567; p. 566). Near Sambhal, he adds, lies Nanak Mata, a place where Baba Nanak's disciples and devotees throng to pray (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 553).

"It is believed that the tradition of Langar began with Guru Nanak", Khalid writes (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 188, folio not stated). Guru Nanak's birthday is kept at Nankana Sahib every November (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 228, folio 202). Khalid's informant Kartar says "thousands of people come to Nankana Sahib from all over the world" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 182, folio not stated). "According to official estimates, ten to fifteen thousand people come to Nankana Sahib for this festival every year, which is celebrated during the last days of November"; in 2011, "According to media reports, only three thousand people are attending". That year the festival, called Guru Nanak Jayanti or Gurpurab, began on 8 November and ended on the 10th (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated). Police stood at every entrance to the city and no car was let in (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 220, folio not stated); devotees walked barefoot (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 221–222, folio not stated).

Khalid describes the pilgrimage as he saw it. Trains bring pilgrims from Delhi, Gujarat and Amritsar across the border at Wagah, where representatives of the ETPB and the PSGPC receive them and a *langar* is arranged, and they stay three days at Nankana (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 222–223, folio not stated). There are three days of *Akhand Path*; the *Nagar Kirtan* follows the closing *bhog* on the last day; then the pilgrims go on to other cities. Richer pilgrims from Britain, America and Canada come by chartered plane, and travel companies promise "all" the gurdwaras in Pakistan (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 223, folio not stated). "The entire city looks like a mini-India during these few days" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 226, folio not stated). In *Walking with Nanak* Jeeta Singh of Manora holds a small festival of his own "every November when the festival of Guru Nanak is celebrated at Nankana Sahib" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 228, folio 202), and pilgrim tourists "come to Nankana Sahib and Hassan Abdal" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 79, folio 61).

## In Verse

None of the books quotes a verse about the gurdwara or the cobra. Khalid quotes the Guru's own verse throughout *Walking with Nanak*, in published English translations, and the lines below are the poetry of the Guru the shrine remembers. The poem on the sacred thread, which the boy Nanak refused at Talwandi:

*From the cotton of compassion, / Spin the thread of contentment* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 61–62, folios 43–44)

The *aarti*, which Iqbal Qaiser reads as placing God "in nature" and which leads him to call Nanak "a naturalist", begins in one translation:

*The sky the salver, the sun and moon the lamps …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 55, folio 37; p. 56, folio 38)

and in another:

*In the salver of the firmament, / The sun and the moon shine as lamps* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 243–244, folios 217–218)

On the oneness of God:

*My Master is One / He is One, brother, the only One* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 60, folio 42)

*God is One, / The Path is One* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 144, folio 126)

On washing:

*As hands or feet besmirched with slime, / Water washes white* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 192, folio 166)

The song Nanak sings, in Khalid's reconstruction, to the innkeepers who meant to rob him and Mardana:

*The herons in their white feathers dwell in the sacred shrines of pilgrimage* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 123–126, folios 105–108)

Other poems Khalid quotes open: *O my merchant friend* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 56–58, folio 40); *Launch ye the boat of austere contemplation …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 148–149, folios 130–131); *As a beggar goes a-begging …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 158, folio 140); and *His goodness cannot be priced or traded …* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 280, folio 254). From the *Babur Bani*, "the first poem of Nanak that I read", Khalid quotes the line that dates the invaders' coming:

*Coming in seventy-eight (1521 C.E)* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 113–114, folios 95–96)

The line goes on to their departure, in a year whose printed gloss is damaged. Through Iqbal Qaiser's recitation of that poem Khalid "truly discovered Guru Nanak": in it Nanak "lays the blame of Babur's wrath at God's feet" and "challenges the divinity of the Divine" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 15–16, folio not stated).

Bhandari writes that the Guru composed "اشعار هندی", verses in the Hindi tongue, on the truth of God, setting out divine unity "بعبارات واضحه و استعارات لایحه", in clear words and shining metaphors (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520). Khalid writes that the Guru Granth Sahib "contains the spiritual poetry of the various Sikh Gurus", Guru Nanak among them, and of other spiritual teachers such as Bhagat Kabir, Baba Farid and Bhai Mardana (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 82, folio 64), and that later Gurus' poems are recorded "not in their own names but in that of Guru Nanak" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 98, folio 80). Dhillon records that Nanak's *Bani* "was compiled as part of the Adi Granth by Guru Arjan Dev, the fifth Guru, in 1604" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 9, folio 9), and that the Guru's "sermon was his Bani" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 228–229, folios 252–253). Shemeem Burney Abbas's glossary defines the *banī* by him: "Devotional verses such as those of Guru Nanak; verses of supplication, entreaty, humility, and reverence" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 179, folio not stated). She writes that "Poets such as Kabir and Nanak used it to write their mystical poetry", the form being the *dohā*, a couplet whose two verses rhyme (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 141, folio not stated). The verse is sung beyond the gurdwaras: the singer Abida Parvin tells Abbas, "I sing Shah Hussain, Bulle Shah, Mian Muhammad Bakhsh, Hazrat Khawaja Ghulam Farid, Sacchal Sarmast, Baba Farid, and Guru Nanak" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 54, folio not stated). In Sindh, Michel Boivin records, "Sain Vasan Shah became famous for reciting the banis of Shri Guru Nanak and the kalams of the Sufis" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 68, folio 55).

## Setting: Nankana Sahib

What follows is about the town, not the gurdwara. Rizvi places "the village of Talwandi later known as Nankana Sahib, about forty miles south-west of Lahore, now part of Pakistan" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 407, folio 383). "Originally called Rai-Bhoi-Di-Talwandi, this city was rechristened Nankana Sahib in honour of Guru Nanak", Khalid writes in *A White Trail*; because Nanak spent his early years here there are "several Gurdwaras in the city", and pilgrims visit all of them (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated). In *Walking with Nanak* he writes that "The name of Rai Bhoi di Talwindi has been changed to Nankana Sahib in honour of the first Sikh Guru who was born there" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 31, folio 13), and calls Rai Bhoi di Talwandi the place "where there are several gurdwaras marking events from his childhood" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 43, folio 25). Dhillon identifies the place once, as "Nanakana Sahib (earlier called Talwandi)", 180 km from Kartarpur by way of Lahore (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 175).

Dhillon's retelling gives the town a history. "Thirteen times was the village of Talwandi destroyed and thirteen times - like the fabled phoenix - it rose again from its ashes", for it lay between the Ravi and the Chenab, in the path of the armies that had set their eyes on Delhi; "Its last rebirth took place under the supervision of Rai Bhoe", a Bhatti Rajput "who had converted to Islam" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 15–16, folios 17–18). Iqbal Qaiser told Khalid that the mound behind Gurdwara Janam Asthan "is an archaeological mound. The settlement there was destroyed after which Rai Bhoi di Talwindi was constructed within a boundary wall"; Nanak, he said, was born in the new town (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 91, folio 73). The mound "now rests" behind the birthplace shrine, away from the hub of the new settlement (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 91, folio 73). Khalid climbed it: a steep climb "with no proper path", "at least twenty metres from the ground", covered with shrubs of *karir*, with shards of broken pottery underfoot (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 92, folio 74). In the same passage on the mound Khalid places Malji Sahib and Kiari Sahib furthest from the Janam Asthan (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 91, folio 73).

The books count the town's gurdwaras differently. "There are a total of six gurdwaras associated with Guru Nanak at Nankana Sahib", Khalid writes in *Walking with Nanak* (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 90, folio 72). In *A White Trail* he writes: "There are seven Gurdwaras in Nankana Sahib, most of which are associated with incidents from the life of Baba Guru Nanak" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 192, folio not stated). Kar Seva's name is on yellow dustbins and boards placed "in different Gurdwaras of the city" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 223, folio not stated). At "the shrine of Guru Nanak at Nankana Sahib", which Khalid does not name, "There is a picture of Baba Farid accompanying the picture of Guru Nanak" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).

Nankana is "a city considered to be one of the holiest cities in the world for the Sikhs, as the founder of their religion, Nanak, was born here" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 183, folio not stated). It is a city "with a population of over a million" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 176, folio not stated). "There is one main road that runs through the entire city"; "The entire city needs a major uplift. The buildings are old and the road has pot holes"; "Most of the buildings are from the late British or the early Pakistan era" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 186, folio not stated). From a roof Khalid saw the remains of a few Hindu temples, "cone like structures rising from houses abandoned at the time of partition" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 225, folio not stated). "During the Musharraf era (2001-2008), newly constructed roads and other government facilities made their way into the city. In 2005, Nankana Sahib was separated from Sheikhupura and made into an independent district, with this city becoming the district headquarters"; Sikhs abroad have asked for an international hotel and for visa-free "holy city" status, with no progress yet (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 226, folio not stated).

"Guru Nanak is celebrated all over Nankana Sahib. There is a Guru Nanak High School, a Guru Nanak grocery store and a Guru Nanak cloth shop, all run by Sikhs. The Muslims generally refrain from eulogizing Guru Nanak in this particular way" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 172, folio not stated). Iqbal Qaiser explains that the majority of the Muslims in Nankana Sahib are those who migrated from across the border after Partition, their attitudes shaped by that experience (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 76, folio 58). Some shopkeepers keep separate utensils for Muslims and non-Muslims (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 186, folio not stated), and one restaurant owner refuses Sikh customers and complains "We become prisoners within our own city", though not all traders feel so, since the pilgrims bring business (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 226, folio not stated).

## The Sikhs of Nankana Sahib

After Partition, Khalid's informant Kartar says, "There was no Sikh left here after partition. Our Gurdwaras became barren and were in a bad state." Giani Pratap came with a pilgrimage party from India "sometime in the sixties", stayed "to look after the Gurdwara", housed Kartar's family briefly inside it, and made Kartar's father a *sewaadar*, a worker, at the gurdwara (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 184, folio not stated). Most of the present community, Khalid writes, are "not indigenous people from the city, but recent migrants from Khyber-Pakhtunkhwa (KPK) where there is a considerable Sikh population" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 235, folio not stated). The books give different counts. "There were only about thirty to forty Sikh houses in Nankana ten years ago", Khalid writes, and the population has grown almost sevenfold, families arriving after the wars of 1965 and 1971 and the demolition of the Babri Mosque in 1992 (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 174–175, folio not stated); "In about ten years, the population in Nankana Sahib increased to about two hundred houses" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 175, folio not stated); Kartar remembers "only about fifty Sikh houses" a few years earlier and "almost two hundred to two hundred and fifty Sikh homes" now (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 193, folio not stated). *Walking with Nanak* gives the community as "about two hundred-odd households", with Jatha Singh as its leader for several years (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 87, folio 69).

"Most of the newcomers were incorporated within the Gurdwaras, in rooms which were generally reserved for pilgrims", and the influx gave "a confidence boost to the Sikhs who were until now dominated by the Muslim majority" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 176, folio not stated). Many Sikh families practise *hikmat*, the traditional medicine (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 202, folio not stated), and young men have moved to Lahore, Faisalabad and Gujranwala for trade (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 174, folio not stated). Guru Nanak High School is "the only school in the country where the Granth Sahib is taught to Sikh children" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 203, folio not stated). The caretakers of Gurdwara Rori Sahib at Eminabad, Pathan Sikhs, "have moved here from Nankana Sahib" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 117, folio 99). Several people Khalid interviewed at Nankana Sahib felt that the PSGPC "was not representative of the Sikh community in Pakistan" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 36, folio 18).

The town's festivals have grown with the community. Lohri and Guru Gobind Singh's birthday, Kartar says, "have only begun in the last couple of years", made possible by the migration from the tribal areas; "Earlier, there was only a prayer at the Gurdwara." (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 193, folio not stated). On Guru Gobind Singh's birthday in January 2011 the community held a cricket tournament on the ground of Guru Nanak Government College, with teams called Khalsa XI, Nankana XI and Singh XI (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 172–173, folio not stated). For the Sikh New Year, on 14 March 2011, qawwals were invited from Faisalabad to sing first to Baba Farid: "This is the first time since partition that Muslim Qawwalis have been invited to perform at Nankana Sahib" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 218, folio not stated; pp. 217–218, folio not stated).

The town's gurdwaras have also seen violence. "It was the November of 1985, Guru Nanak's birthday, a year after Operation Blue Star"; "Thousands of Sikh pilgrims had come to Nankana from all over the world" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 215, folio not stated). At that time the SGPC in Amritsar still managed the Pakistani gurdwaras through the Indian High Commission (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 215–216, folio not stated). During a speech for a separate Sikh state "the gate of the Gurdwara was closed", and Counsellor B. Jain and First Secretary K. K. Khanna were attacked and barely escaped alive (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 216, folio not stated). "The religious festivals at Nankana and other cities of Pakistan have gone back to being religious festivals" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 216, folio not stated).

## Guru Nanak in the Wider Record

What the books say of Guru Nanak beyond Nankana is said of the Guru, not of this site; it is gathered here because the shrine exists for his sake.

The miracles told of him sit uneasily, for several writers, with what they take to be his own view. Dhillon writes of "Guru Nanak's clear and emphatic disavowal of the miraculous" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 11, folio 11). Khalid writes that Nanak "abhorred the concept of associating miracles with religious personalities", yet "his biography is nothing but a story of his miracles" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 16, folio not stated), and, of the legends of his contests with Sufis, "His genius lies in the poetic use of words and not in miracles" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 199, folio 173). "The only evidence we have is found within the Sikh religion and they are full of stories of miracles", Khalid writes; Iqbal Qaiser's advice is to "read what Nanak wrote at these places and try to get into his mind" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 286, folio 260). Khalid says plainly that "my depiction of Nanak in this book is not an accurate version of Nanak" but what he wants Nanak "to be like" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 229–230, folio not stated / folio 204). Other wonders are told: at Mecca, "According to legend", the Kaaba "miraculously turned towards his feet", a story whose point, for Khalid, is "Nanak's criticism of the concept of a 'sacred space'" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 82, folio 64); and a trader who told Nanak his mules carried "Just some mud" found his sugar and *gur* turned to mud until he repented (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 71, folio 53).

Dhillon calls Nanak "no unworldly mystic" but "a householder in every sense of the word" who "brought a message of peace and harmony to believers of the mutually hostile religions of Islam and Hinduism" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 12, folio not stated). He taught, in Dhillon's summary, that "all men and women were equal, irrespective of caste, creed, wealth, social standing and gender", and that a good life rested on honest labour, compassion, selfless service and prayer (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 12–13, folio not stated / 13). "Nanak himself had been born into a Hindu household and was then educated in a Muslim milieu; however, he was neither a practising Muslim nor a Hindu", Khalid writes; he "offended both Hindus and Muslims by stating that there was no Hindu or Muslim", and his advice to Bhai Bala and Bhai Mardana was that "if one is a Muslim then one should be a good Muslim and if one is a Hindu then one should be a good Hindu" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 95, folio 77). He associated with "people of lowercastes, terming them people of God", and criticised "high-caste landlords and government officials as exploiters" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 82, folio 64). "Guru Nanak was a fakir or a Jogi who spent his entire lifetime in a few clothes" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 99, folio 81). "Nanak incorporated several words which were not part of the Punjabi of the time … which is why Nanak's contribution as a poet to Punjabi is immense" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 278–279, folios 252–253). "Some scholars have argued that the beauty of Nanak's message is that through his own example, he glorified peasantry" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 303, folio 277).

Muslim writers, saints and singers have claimed him too. Khalid writes that "many Muslims, acquainted with the teachings of Guru Nanak, are devoted to him too and visit his shrine" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 220, folio not stated), and his informant Nadeem, a Muslim painter, says "Guru Nanak is like a Muslim Sufi Saint" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 219, folio not stated). A Muslim storyteller at Gurdwara Sachkand called him "a true devotee of God. He was a rightly guided soul" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 75, folio 57). Khalid tells Daleep that "I believe that Nanak was influenced immensely by Islam"; Daleep answers that "seventy-five per cent of Nanak's teachings are similar to Islam", but will not call it influence (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 37, folio 19). Baba Farid "became an inspiration for Guru Nanak when he visited the shrine during his travels" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated); "Guru Nanak was a spiritual follower of Baba Fareed", Khalid writes in *A White Trail*, and travelled to Pak Pattan to collect the saint's poetry from his descendants (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 188, folio not stated). Abbas writes that Farid's "texts are revered among the Sikhs because he influenced the founder of their own religion, Baba Guru Nanak" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 87, folio not stated), and that reformers and mystics like Kabir and Guru Nanak "were certainly influenced by Sufism" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 48, folio not stated). Robert Rozehnal reports that a twentieth-century Chishti Sabiri shaykh, in certain lectures, describes "non-Muslim spiritual luminaries—Krishna, Rama, Sita, Lachman, and Guru Nanak—as believers in the one true God and religious leaders with 'Divine connection' (nisbat)" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 63, folio 53).

Rizvi, rejecting W. H. McLeod's view that "the Panjabi sufism of Guru Nanak's period had evidently departed radically from the classical pattern of Arab and Persian Sufism" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 419–420, folios 395–396), argues that "all literate Panjabis, both Hindus and Muslims, at the time of Guru Nanak were conversant with" ʿAttar, Rumi and Saʿdi (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 420, folio 396). In Rizvi's history Nanak returned from eastern India about 1520 "only to find the Panjab plunged into a number of significant political crises" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 409, folio 385).

Beyond the Punjab his followers are many. Boivin names him as the father of Shri Chand, "the eldest son of Guru Nanak who was not recognized as guru by the mainstream Sikh community", whose tradition the Udasis follow (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 53, folio 40), and notes "a growing similarity between the iconography of Guru Nanak and Jhulelal" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 54, folio 41). Boivin reports the view that "it is a seductive hypothesis that the Bhakti could have permeated Sindh through the teaching of Guru Nanak" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 90, folio 77). The Nanakpanth among Sindh's Hindus, Boivin writes, may have been brought from Punjab by the Aroras in the eighteenth century (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 43, folio 30). Khalid writes of Nanak Panthi Hindus in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Punjab and Sindh: "In their temples, along with pictures of Hindu deities, one also finds a picture of Guru Nanak" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 98, folio 80). Jürgen Schaflechner writes that "Many Lohanas in Sindh follow the teachings of Nanak" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 80, folio not stated), and at the shrine of the goddess Hinglaj he records "the engraved footsteps of Guru Nanak" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 256, folio not stated): "The hollowed-out footsteps of Guru Nanak connect Hinglaj to the Sikh and Nanak paṃth (Hin. sect) traditions" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 257, folio not stated). Omar Kasmani, citing Boivin, notes that the trope of the flower in milk is told also of "Guru Nanak, the spiritual leader and founder of Sikhism" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 195, folio 178, ch. 5 n. 17).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **How the miracle happened.** The site's account has Rai Bular, riding through his fields, see a cobra spread its hood over the sleeping boy's face and withdraw as the men approached. The 1962 register has Rai Bular first notice that the tree's shadow stayed fixed over the sleeping Guru instead of moving with the sun, and only afterwards see the cobra. Khalid's *Walking with Nanak* has a cobra emerge when the sun fell on the boy's face and names no witness (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 91, folio 73); *A White Trail* says only that he "encountered a snake" there (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated).
- **Cattle or buffaloes.** The site's account and the 1962 register have the boy herding cattle, and Dhillon has him herd cattle at sixteen (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 23, folio 27). Khalid has his buffaloes grazing nearby (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 91, folio 73; Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 191, folio not stated).
- **Where it stands.** The site's account places it near the railway station and close to Kiara Sahib. *A White Trail* puts it "about five hundred metres" from Gurdwara Janam Asthan, near the railway station (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 191, folio not stated); *Walking with Nanak* puts it, with Kiari Sahib, "the furthest from Gurdwara Janamsthan" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 91, folio 73).
- **Who honoured the boy.** The site's account says Rai Bular became a devoted follower of Guru Nanak and granted him half of his estate. Dhillon has Rai Bhoe, whom he makes Rai Bular's father, look upon Nanak "as his Guru" on his return in 1506, and Rai Bular dying on a later return (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 17, folio 19; p. 123, folio 137; p. 172, folio 188). Khalid calls Rai Bular "the ruler of Talwindi" and Mehta Kalu's employer and friend (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 22, folio 4; p. 64, folio 46). None of the books mentions the gift of land.
- **The Guru's birth date.** Khalid gives "October 20, 1469" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 314, folio 288). Dhillon gives "14 April 1469" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 17–18, folios 19–20). Bhandari gives the Bikrami year 1526, corresponding to 880 Hijri, in the reign of Bahlul Lodi (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).
- **His death.** Khalid gives 1539 (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 314, folio 288), and Dhillon 22 September 1539 (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 259, folio 287). Bhandari places it in the reign of Salim Shah Afghan, at an age between seventy and eighty (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).
- **Where he was born.** Khalid reports that Meherban "claims that Guru Nanak was born in Dera Chahal and not Rai Bhoi di Talwindi", while other Janamsakhi writers and Sikh scholars are unanimous for Talwandi (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 43, folio 25). Bhandari gives Talwandi Rai Bhoi (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).
- **How his birth came about.** Khalid, citing Dhillon's *The First Sikh Spiritual Master*, has Mata Tripta pray for a child at an ancient Hindu temple on a mound near Talwandi (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 30–31, folio 13). An Auqaf booklet says he was born after Baba Naulakha Hazari prayed for his mother (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 31, folio 13), and Iqbal Qaiser gives this as the Muslim tradition, which "The Sikhs don't accept" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 15, ch. 9, folio not stated).
- **The age of his first wonders.** Bhandari's text says the signs of wonders appeared "درخورد سالگی", in childhood; the editor's footnote gives the variant "ده سالگی", at ten (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).
- **The Sufi of his youth.** Dhillon names "the dervish Sayyad Hussain" (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 24, folio 28). Khalid's reconstruction names "Sayyed Hassan, a Muslim Sufi, who lived near his house" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 62, folio 44).
- **His wife and his marriage.** Dhillon has him marry Sulakhini of Batala on 24 September 1487, before the journeys (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 44, folio 49). Khalid's reconstruction has Sulakhni come from the village of Pakhoke near Gurdaspur (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 67, folio 49). Bhandari has him marry at Batala after travelling through most of the world (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).
- **His younger son's name.** Dhillon writes Lakhmi Das (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 44, folio 49); Khalid writes Lachman Das (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 68, folio 50) and, quoting Iqbal Qaiser, "Sri Lachman" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 44, folio 26); Bhandari writes "لکهمیداس" (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 520).
- **How many journeys.** Dhillon reports five *udasis* in the Puratan Janamsakhi and four in more modern accounts, and holds to four himself (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, pp. 54–55, folios 60–61; p. 56, folio 62). Khalid gives the travels as about twenty-four years (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 54, folio 36); Dhillon's modern scheme spans twenty-three (Dhillon, *Janamsakhis*, p. 55, folio 61).
- **How many gurdwaras in the town.** *Walking with Nanak* counts six associated with Guru Nanak (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 90, folio 72); *A White Trail* counts seven, most associated with his life (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 192, folio not stated).
- **The shrines after Partition.** The site's account says the shrine has been maintained since Partition as part of Pakistan's Sikh heritage. Khalid writes that the shrines of Nankana Sahib were dilapidated until Giani Pratap, the first after Partition to take care of them, stayed to look after them (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 90, folio 72); his informant Kartar says the gurdwaras "became barren and were in a bad state" and that Giani Pratap came "sometime in the sixties" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 184, folio not stated).
- **Ranjit Singh's time.** The site's account dates the renovation only to the time of Maharaja Ranjit Singh. Khalid writes in one place that he became sovereign of Punjab "at the end of the eighteenth century" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 34, folio 16), in another that he ruled "in the nineteenth century" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 87, folio 69), and in *A White Trail* that he was crowned in 1801 (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 207, folio not stated).
- **How many Sikhs live in the town.** *Walking with Nanak* gives "about two hundred-odd households" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 87, folio 69). *A White Trail* gives thirty to forty houses ten years earlier, grown almost sevenfold (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 174–175, folio not stated), about two hundred houses (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 175, folio not stated), and, in Kartar's words, fifty houses a few years earlier and two hundred to two hundred and fifty now (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 193, folio not stated).
- **How many pilgrims come.** Kartar says thousands (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 182, folio not stated). Official estimates give ten to fifteen thousand a year; media reports gave three thousand in 2011 (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 222, folio not stated).

## Legacy

Gurdwara Mal Ji Sahib keeps the memory of a single moment in the boyhood of the founder of the Sikh faith: a child asleep in the fields of Talwandi, shaded by a cobra. It stands, with Kiara Sahib, among the shrines that turn the town of Guru Nanak's birth into a map of his early life. Khalid writes of Pakistan as a whole that "there are several sites all over the country where Nanak stayed or performed miracles. Gurdwaras that were built there later by the devotees still stand tall" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, pp. 168–169, folio not stated).

The Guru's succession ran on through men the books still call by his name. Bhandari calls Guru Har Rai "سجاده نشین بابا نانک", the holder of Baba Nanak's seat (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 76), and writes that the emperor heard from Guru Arjan, the reading of whose name is uncertain in the printed text, "اشعار هندی تصنیف بابا نانک که در معرفت حق گفته", Hindi verses Baba Nanak composed on the knowledge of God (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 164). Khalid describes his own book as "not merely a story about gurdwaras" but "a re-telling of the story of Nanak the son, the poet, the wanderer, the father, the friend", tracing how "Nanak the poet became Guru Nanak the saint" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 2, folio not stated). The gurdwara at Nankana Sahib keeps one of those stories, of Nanak the son, herding in his father's fields.
