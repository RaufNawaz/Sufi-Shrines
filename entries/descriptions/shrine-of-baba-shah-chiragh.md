## Overview

Set beside the Lahore High Court on The Mall, in the shadow of the colonial-era Aiwan-e-Auqaf building that now bears its name, the shrine of Baba Shah Chiragh commemorates Syed Abdul Razzaq, a Sufi of the Qadiri lineage who traced his descent to Sheikh Abdul Qadir Gilani of Baghdad and whose family originated from Uch in what is now Bahawalpur. Given the honorific "Chiragh," meaning lamp, by his grandfather Hazrat Abdul Qadir Sani, he flourished during the reign of the Mughal emperor Shah Jahan and died in 1657 CE. His mausoleum was constructed by imperial order of Aurangzeb, and an adjoining mosque was added later, in 1716, under the patronage of Lahore's Mughal viceroy Zakariya Khan during the reign of Muhammad Shah Rangeela.

The mausoleum, built of limestone on a square plan in restrained Mughal style, contains eight graves in total, including those of Shah Chiragh's father and grandfather, making it a multi-generational family shrine rather than a single tomb. Its façades feature cusped-arch niches and panelled cartouches, while the interior retains traces of floral fresco decoration. In the late nineteenth century the surrounding precinct was absorbed into the grand Indo-Saracenic Aiwan-e-Auqaf complex, built by the British administration between 1875 and 1880, which today houses the offices and library of the Auqaf Department, the same body that has administered the shrine since 1973.

Three nineteenth-century Lahore books describe the tomb. Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori's *Hadiqat al-Awliya* gives the saint his own notice, as Sayyid Abd al-Razzaq, known as Shah Chiragh Qadiri Lahori, and its appendix places his grave in Lahore, adjoining the High Court (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 287, folio 275). Kanhaiya Lal's *Tarikh-e Lahore* names him Sayyid Abdul Razzaq bin Sayyid Abdul Wahhab Gilani, with Shah Chiragh as his title, and describes the tomb, its eight graves and its mosque (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 281, folio 284). Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti's *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* describes the tomb as its author saw it on a visit of his own (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 110).

The same books also name other Lahore men called Chiragh or Abd al-Razzaq, and none of them settles how these men relate to one another. Kanhaiya Lal himself gives two accounts: in one he treats Shah Chiragh and Abd al-Razzaq as a single man; in another he keeps a Sayyid Chiragh Shah Gilani, whose quarter stood where the Chief Court building was going up, apart from a Sayyid Abd al-Razzaq who was a khalifa of Mauj Darya Bukhari (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 79–80, folios 82–83). What follows gives each book's account as that book's own, and sets the other men of the name in a section of their own.

## The Life of the Saint

**Name and descent.** Lahori opens his notice, no. 28 in his book, with Sayyid Abd al-Razzaq, known as Shah Chiragh Qadiri Lahori, and calls him one of the shaikhs of the Qadiriyya, a man of worship and discipline, of abstinence and piety, who united the outward sciences and, as the sentence goes on, more besides (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 66, folio 54). His father, Lahori writes, was Sayyid Abdul Wahab, son of Sayyid Abdul Qadir Thalith, son of Muhammad Ghaus Bala Pir, son of Zain al-Abidin, son of Sayyid Abdul Qadir Sani, son of Sayyid Muhammad Ghaus Uchi Halabi Gilani. That makes him the fifth generation of the Uch Gilani family whose earlier members fill the notices before his own (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 68, folio 56). Chishti gives the chain as Abd al-Razzaq, known as Shah Chiragh, son of Sayyid Abd al-Wahhab, son of Sayyid Abd al-Qadir Thalith (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 108). On the next page he names Sayyid Abd al-Qadir Sani, the name uncertain in the printed text, and makes Abd al-Wahhab a son of Janab Muhammad Ghaus Bala Pir, again an uncertain reading, with Sayyid Abd al-Razzaq, known as Shah Chiragh, as Abd al-Wahhab's son (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 109). Chishti learned his account, he says, from Hazrat Pir Nizam al-Din Shah Gilani, the words before the name being illegible (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 108).

Elsewhere Chishti places the saint in the same family line: Hazrat Shah Chiragh and Abd al-Qadir Thalith, whose tombs around Lahore are places of pilgrimage for the people, are of that family's offspring. He adds that both are descended from Abd al-Qadir Sani, a son of Hazrat Muhammad Ghaus, and that their lives are told at length elsewhere in his book (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 527).

Kanhaiya Lal writes that the real name of this holy man was Sayyid Abdul Razzaq bin Sayyid Abdul Wahhab Gilani, and that Shah Chiragh was his title. Because the family's correct genealogy joins that of Piran-e-Pir Muhi-ud-Din, he adds, people hold them in great respect (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 281, folio 284).

**The lamp of the house.** Lahori tells how the name came about. On the day the child was born, his father said that a lamp had been born into their house, by which the whole family would be lit, and so he was named Shah Chiragh:

Lahori tells how he got his name. On the day he was born, his father said that this son had been born a lamp of the house, by which the whole family would be lit; and so his name was fixed as Shah Chiragh (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 68, folio 56).

Another of Lahori's notices calls him Hazrat Shah Chiragh Gilani Lahori, pairing the family nisba Gilani with Lahori (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 267, folio 255). Whether that notice means this man is discussed below.

**From Uch to Lahore.** Kanhaiya Lal makes the move from Uch in two stages. The saint's elders came from qasba Uch, in the territory of Bahawalpur State, to qasba Satghara [OCR?] in the Montgomery district, and from there came to live in Lahore (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 281, folio 284). Chishti says that Muhammad Ghaus Bala Pir settled in a mohalla whose printed name, perhaps Rasulabad, cannot be read with certainty (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 109).

**Travel and pilgrimage.** Lahori says he loved travel and journeyed through many countries by way of experience, and that he also went to the Haramain Sharifain, the two holy cities (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 68, folio 56).

**A marriage refused.** Lahori records that Shah Jahan was wholly devoted to him and tried more than once to give one of his daughters in marriage to him or to his son, Sayyid Mustafa. The offer was not accepted:

Lahori adds that the emperor Shah Jahan was wholly devoted to him, and tried hard to betroth one of his daughters to him or to his son Sayyid Mustafa; but he did not accept (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 68, folio 56).

The book's index lists the son as Mustafa, son of Shah Chiragh Lahori, against the figure 56 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 330, folio 318). Chishti also tells of a promised marriage that he declined, saying that he did not accept it, the words uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 108).

**A pir with a disciple.** Lahori's notice of Shah Dargahi Qadri calls him a murid of Hazrat Shah Chiragh Gilani Lahori, a man given to visions and miracles, sincerity and purity, abstinence and discipline (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 267, folio 255). Lahori places that disciple's grave beside Shah Ismail Muhaddith's, with a well whose cure became a standing Lahore ritual (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 267, folio 255). The book does not say in that notice when the pir died or where he is buried. Kanhaiya Lal also makes Dargahi Shah the murid of a Sayyid Shah Chiragh, already mentioned earlier in his book (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 299, folio 302). Neither book settles that this pir is the Shah Chiragh of the Mall.

**Death.** Lahori gives the date in words: the twenty-second of Zi Qada, 1068 AH, the year that corresponds to the 1657 of the account above. He adds that his tomb is in Lahore (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 68, folio 56). Kanhaiya Lal gives the same day and month, 22 Dhu al-Qa'da, but the year cannot be read in the printed text (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 281, folio 284). Chishti gives two dates. Beside a sentence that dates his death to the year one thousand and something, the rest illegible and the words uncertain, the margin carries 1060, itself an uncertain reading (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 108). On the next page he gives the day of death as the seventeenth (17) of Rabi al-Awwal and the year, in the margin, as 1092; both readings are uncertain (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 109).

**A chronogram.** With that second date Chishti copies a chronogram on the saint's death. He attributes it to Mufti Ghulam Sarwar, the author of a tazkira. Its opening words are partly illegible, and it ends with this hemistich (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 109):

گفت سرور میر شمس العارفین

*Chishti gives no translation; the closing hemistich names the poet, Sarwar, and calls the saint Mir Shams al-Arifin.* (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 109)

Chishti gives its year again in the margin as 1092 (a reading uncertain in the printed text) (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 109). Most of the verse cannot be read in the printed text, and the book gives no translation of it.

## The Shrine

**Where it stands.** Lahori's appendix lists the tomb under Sayyid Abd al-Razzaq, known as Shah Chiragh Qadiri Lahori: Lahore, adjoining the High Court. The appendix gives no date for him (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 287, folio 275). Kanhaiya Lal explains why the tomb stands where it does. The spot where the tomb now stands was the quarter of Langar Khan Baloch, who was a murid of the family, and that is why the tomb was built there (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 281, folio 284). Chishti names the area Langar Jan, a reading uncertain, on one page (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 108) and Mohalla Langar Khan on the next (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 109). In a passage partly illegible, he sets the tomb by a road and by the kothi of a McLeod Sahib Bahadur, the name uncertain, with the Accounts office, also an uncertain reading, to the west (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 108).

Kanhaiya Lal's contents list the tomb at printed page 284 among the Islamic buildings outside the city (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 4, folio 3), and list a Mohalla Shah Chiragh at printed page 82 in the book's second part (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 2).

**The founding.** Kanhaiya Lal writes that the rauza was built by order of Alamgir Aurangzeb (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 281, folio 284). Chishti dates the building to the time of Alamgir Badshah, and in the same passage says it was built by order of Alamgir the Second, a reading uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 109).

**The fabric.** In Kanhaiya Lal's description the tomb is a square, pakka building of lime, its door facing south, with a roof of brick and a grand dome above (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 281, folio 284). Chishti notes a wooden door with a lattice (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 108). He also saw an inscription of the profession of faith with the year 1268, a reading uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 110).

**The graves.** Kanhaiya Lal counts eight graves inside, one of Shah Chiragh and seven of his offspring (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 281, folio 284). Chishti names two graves, Shah Chiragh's and that of the sahibzada Zain al-Abidin (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 109). He also describes a walled enclosure, a chardiwari, holding thirty-four graves (34, the figure uncertain in the printed text), with a well, trees and a broken floor, and adds of the ground that it must once have been a small garden (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 109).

**The mosque and a woman's bequest.** Kanhaiya Lal writes that the mosque was built by order of Khan Bahadur Zakariya Khan, because his mother was a murida of this family. She had willed that her grave should be here and that a mosque should be built from her jewellery (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 281, folio 284). Chishti describes a mosque with five domes and four cells, built by a Nawab Khan Bahadur, the rest of the name unreadable. He saw the grave of the Nawab's mother under a tree (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 109). Neither book gives the year of the mosque.

**Arsenal, kothi and office.** Both books record what became of the mosque after Mughal rule. Kanhaiya Lal writes that before English rule the Sikhs had put something into it, the word illegible. When the English took over it was made a kothi. A Major [OCR?], a Dwyderburn [OCR?] Sahib, a Summ [OCR?] Sahib Bahadur and Deputy Commissioners lived in it, and then the Accountant's office was set up there (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 282, folio 285). On the page before, he says it now houses the government office of the Accountant and of the Civil Auditor, the latter title uncertain in the printed text (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 281, folio 284). Chishti writes that a mosque has now come into the Accounts office, the word uncertain (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 109), and has been so for many years, again an uncertain reading (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 108).

**Two enclosures.** By Kanhaiya Lal's day the kothi and mosque had their own enclosure, separate from the tomb's, and the tomb was in the possession of the Sadat Gilani; the word kothi is uncertain in the printed text (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 282, folio 285). He reads the complex as one split in two by colonial occupation, the mosque taken into government use as an office and the tomb left in the hands of the saint's own sayyid lineage (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 281–282, folios 284–285).

**Keepers and lands.** Chishti names a living sajjada nashin, Hazrat Sana Shah, the name uncertain in the printed text. A faqir, Musi Shah, also an uncertain reading, had long held the *ʿurs*, taken the income and served as khadim (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 110). There was a broken enclosure where a faqir lived. Before British rule, in the time of the Sikhs, the mosque and the rest had land, the passage partly illegible. Little of that land, Chishti says, was left in his day (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 109).

**Another reading of the site.** In his account of Lahore's quarters, Kanhaiya Lal tells of a Sayyid Chiragh Shah Gilani whose quarter lay east of Mauj Darya Bukhari's. Gilani sayyids lived there. It was settled in the time of Shah Jahan, a reading uncertain in the printed text, and, in his words, was settled as a *jagir*; it was later laid waste by plunderers (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 79, folio 82). He places that quarter where the Chief Court building had begun to go up. Of its buildings he writes that Sayyid Chiragh Shah's tomb and pakka mosque stand to this day. The mosque is in government possession, and the tomb is held by the tomb-holder's descendants (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 79, folio 82). The place answers to the shrine's own location near the High Court, but in this account Kanhaiya Lal does not call the man Abd al-Razzaq, and he gives no death date (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 79–80, folios 82–83).

## Devotion and Practice

**Royal offerings.** Kanhaiya Lal writes that under Muslim rule the family stood very high and the sultans of the time offered lakhs of rupees at their shrine, the word for 'offered' uncertain in the printed text (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 281, folio 284).

**The yearly fair.** Kanhaiya Lal records a mela held here every year on the seventeenth of Rabi-us-Sani, when great crowds gather (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 282, folio 285). Chishti dates the *ʿurs* to Rabi al-Thani, the month's name an uncertain reading, but the day cannot be read (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 108).

**Thursdays.** Chishti writes that every Thursday something sounds out well at the tomb of Hazrat Shah Chiragh. The word naming what was played is illegible in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 109).

**An author's visit.** Chishti, calling himself the humblest of men, went to the tomb and presented himself there in person (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 110).

## Other Men of the Name

Lahore's books know several men called Chiragh, and several called Abd al-Razzaq. Some lived in the same part of the city as the Shah Chiragh of the Mall, and the books do not cross-refer them. They are set out here as the books give them. None of them is identified with this shrine's saint unless a book says so.

**Kanhaiya Lal's second Abd al-Razzaq.** In the same account of the quarters, Kanhaiya Lal describes a Sayyid Abd al-Razzaq of Mohalla Dola Wari. He was a khalifa of Mauj Darya Bukhari and was the first to settle there, after which the quarter flourished (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 80, folio 83). The quarter kept its standing to the end of the Chughtai sultanate, because some holy man always lived in its khanqah, from whom grace reached all around, a word of that sentence being uncertain in the printed text. His pakka rauza stands, called the Tibela [OCR?] Gumbad, in a lane of a bazaar whose printed name, perhaps Urar, cannot be read with certainty, with a pakka mosque inside its enclosure (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 80, folio 83). Under Ranjit Singh both tomb and mosque were kept full of gunpowder, a reading uncertain in the printed text, and iron shot was made in the mosque courtyard. The English emptied both. When the cantonment left Anarkali, the meat contractor Munshi Najm al-Din took the mosque and tomb from the government. He repaired the mosque and opened it again, and a grave was made in the tomb (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 80, folio 83). Kanhaiya Lal gives this man no silsila and no death date, and in this account he never calls him Chiragh (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 80, folio 83).

**Chishti's Abd al-Razzaq near the Kothi Shah Chiragh.** Chishti gives a chapter of its own, "On Hazrat Abd al-Razzaq's Tomb" (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 154). Its subject is a man named Abd al-Razzaq, a sayyid and, by an uncertain reading, a Suhrawardi, a word between being illegible; and though his name was Sayyid Abd al-Razzaq, he was known by another, printed uncertainly as Sayyid Lagi. He first gave his allegiance to Hazrat Mauj Darya, a word of that phrase being uncertain. After Mauj Darya's death he worshipped by night at his tomb and rested by day where his own tomb now stands, and he was buried in that hujra as he had willed. He died on a Thursday, in the year one thousand and eighty-four (1084) AH, a reading uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 157). His tomb stood by the Kothi Shah Chiragh, the name uncertain, its walls new (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 154). In Chishti's telling the tomb was built through a subscription, with a large mosque beside it from what was left over (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 155). Later an order said the building would be seized unless it was put in order within two months, and merchants whose printed name, perhaps the merchants of Billi, cannot be read with certainty raised subscription money for it. He also tells of a dream in which Abd al-Razzaq appeared to the manager of the building, but the text breaks off (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 157–158). Chishti also places the enclosure of Haji Muhammad Saeed to the west of Shah Chiragh's tomb, the word for tomb uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 158).

**Lahori's Shah Abd al-Razzaq Makki.** Lahori has a notice of a Shah Abd al-Razzaq Makki, a murid of Mauj Darya, whose tomb is in Anarkali bazar and who died in one thousand and eighty-four (1084). He bears the saint's personal name and lived in the saint's quarter of Lahore, but he had a different pir and died in a different year (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 269, folio 257). Lahori's index carries Abd al-Razzaq Shah Makki Lahori, against the figures 20 and 257, among four separate Sayyid Abd al-Razzaqs (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 322, folio 310). Kanhaiya Lal's contents list a separate tomb of Abd al-Razzaq Makki, the name uncertain in the printed text, at printed page 289 (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 4, folio 3).

**Other Chiraghs.** Lahori's notice no. 238 is of a mazar of Chiragh Shah outside the Roshnai Gate, the successor of a Sayyid Ali Gilani who died in one thousand two hundred and twenty-seven (1227) (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 275, folio 263). His index files Shah Chiragh Lahori (Abd al-Razzaq), against 55, and Chiragh Shah Lahori, against 262, as separate lines (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 316, folio 304). It also names a grandfather of a Shah Chiragh of Delhi, Abd al-Latif, against 85 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 322, folio 310). Chishti tells of a Hazrat Shah Chiragh Sahib Qadiri Sultanpuri who looked on Nur Husain with the utmost kindness (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 151). Kanhaiya Lal names a living Sayyid Chiragh Shah Hakim of the tenth enclosure in his account, a man with murids in the Chishtiya and the Qadiriya (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 296, folio 299). He also names a Shaikh Abd al-Razzaq Makki of the Nila Gumbad, who came from Mecca [OCR?] in Humayun's time and died in 1085 [OCR?] AH (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 286, folio 289).

**A son of the Ghaus.** Chishti's chain of Mian Mir names a Sayyid Abd al-Razzaq, the true son of Hazrat Ghaus al-Azam, a murid of his father, with a Shaikh Qarshi [OCR?] as his own murid (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 299). The passage concerns the chain and does not speak of a tomb on the Mall.

## Setting

The books speak of the family's origins, of the patron of the mosque and of the colonial city around the tomb. Their words concern those people and places, not the shrine itself.

**The Gilanis of Uch.** Lahori writes that the first of the Qadiri line to come to the Punjab was Hazrat Sayyid Muhammad Ghaus Uchi Jili Gilani. His ancestors had left Baghdad in the time of Hulagu Khan. He came by way of Khurasan to Multan, and settled at Uch. A modern footnote by the editor dates his arrival at Uch, citing Ali Asghar Gilani: by his account, he entered Uch in 887 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 40, folio 28). Shah Chiragh's descent, in Lahori's notice, runs back to this man.

**Zakariya Khan.** Kanhaiya Lal names the governors Nawab Zakariya Khan Bahadur, Shahnawaz Khan and other rulers of the Punjab as living in Mughalpura (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 90, folio 93). He also copied a tile inscription on the north mihrab of another mosque Zakariya Khan built, west of Madho Lal Hussain's mazar. The inscription dates the building to the reign of Muhammad Shah, and its verse names the builder:

زبدۂ بارگاہ او نواب
زکریا خان صوبہ پنجاب

*The verse names its builder, Nawab Zakariya Khan, governor of the Punjab* (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 262–263, folios 265–266).

**The Chief Court and the Lieutenant-Governors.** Kanhaiya Lal describes the Chief Court of his day as the court that gave the final decision in civil and criminal cases. Appeals from the commissionerships of the Punjab came before it, and capital sentences needed its sanction (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 60, folio 63). He names a McLeod Sahib Bahadur, former Lieutenant-Governor of the Punjab, the name uncertain in the printed text (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 213, folio 216). This is the same name Chishti gives to the kothi beside the tomb.

**The Mall today.** Haroon Khalid, writing of a Sunday drive in Lahore, calls Mall Road "one of the busiest boulevards in the city" and writes that "The Lahore High Court, combining European and Eastern architectural techniques, stands close to the Panorama Market" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 100, folio not stated). Khalid does not mention the shrine.

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The year of his death.** The current account gives 1657 CE. Lahori gives 22 Zi Qada 1068 AH, the year that corresponds to 1657 (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 68, folio 56). Kanhaiya Lal gives 22 Dhu al-Qa'da with the year illegible (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 281, folio 284). Chishti carries 1060 [OCR?] in the margin beside a sentence whose year is illegible (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 108), and gives 1092 [OCR?] on the next page, with the chronogram (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 109).
- **The day of his death.** Lahori and Kanhaiya Lal give the twenty-second of Dhu al-Qa'da (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 68, folio 56; Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 281, folio 284). Chishti gives the seventeenth of Rabi al-Awwal (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 109).
- **Who called him Chiragh.** The current account says the honorific was given by his grandfather, Hazrat Abdul Qadir Sani. Lahori says his father gave him the name on the day he was born (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 68, folio 56). Kanhaiya Lal calls Shah Chiragh his *khitab*, his title, and does not say who gave it (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 281, folio 284).
- **His grandfather.** The current account makes Hazrat Abdul Qadir Sani his grandfather. Lahori makes his grandfather Sayyid Abdul Qadir Thalith and places Abdul Qadir Sani four generations above his father (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 68, folio 56). Chishti also names Abd al-Qadir Thalith as his grandfather (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 108). On his next page, though, Abd al-Wahhab is a son of Muhammad Ghaus Bala Pir (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 109).
- **Who lies in the tomb.** The current account counts eight graves, including those of his father and grandfather. Kanhaiya Lal counts eight: his own and seven of his offspring (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 281, folio 284). Chishti names two, his own and that of a sahibzada, Zain al-Abidin (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 109).
- **Which Alamgir built it.** The current account and Kanhaiya Lal credit Aurangzeb (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 281, folio 284). Chishti dates it to Alamgir Badshah and writes that it was built by order of Alamgir the Second, a reading uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 109).
- **One man or two.** Kanhaiya Lal's account of the tomb makes Sayyid Abdul Razzaq and Shah Chiragh one man (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 281, folio 284). His account of the quarters keeps a Sayyid Chiragh Shah Gilani, at the Chief Court site, apart from a Sayyid Abd al-Razzaq of Mohalla Dola Wari, a khalifa of Mauj Darya Bukhari (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, pp. 79–80, folios 82–83). Lahori and Chishti both call the saint Abd al-Razzaq known as Shah Chiragh (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 66, folio 54; Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 108).
- **Who held the tomb.** Kanhaiya Lal's account of the tomb gives possession to the Sadat Gilani (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 282, folio 285). His account of the quarters gives it to the tomb-holder's own descendants (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 79, folio 82). Chishti names a sajjada nashin, Sana [OCR?] Shah, and a faqir, Musi [OCR?] Shah, who held the *ʿurs* and took the income (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 110). The current account says the Auqaf Department has administered it since 1973.
- **What became of a mosque.** Kanhaiya Lal tells two histories of a mosque attached to the name. In one, the Sikhs used it and the English made it a kothi for officers and then the Accountant's office (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 282, folio 285). In the other, belonging to the Abd al-Razzaq of Dola Wari, it was a gunpowder store under Ranjit Singh and was later taken from the government by a meat contractor (Kanhaiya Lal, *Tarikh-e Lahore*, p. 80, folio 83).

## Legacy

Though it lacks the international renown of some of Lahore's other Sufi sites, the shrine is formally catalogued as a protected heritage monument by the Punjab Directorate General of Archaeology. Its distinctive Mughal fabric, set incongruously amid the bureaucratic buildings of modern Lahore, makes it a quietly significant survival from the city's seventeenth-century religious landscape.

An independent English-language academic study of Sufis and pre-colonial Muslim rulers in India, consulted separately from Alam Faqri's tazkira, states in passing that "Aurangzeb gave importance to some other Shari minded Qadri sufi, like Shah Chiragh whose tomb was constructed by Aurangzeb". This corroborates, from an independent scholarly source, the claim that Aurangzeb ordered the mausoleum built. The study's own full citation details have not been recovered, and they should be verified against the original before the citation is treated as fully resolved. A general compendium of Pakistani Sufi saints notes, in its entry on the nearby shrine of Mauj Darya Bukhari, that Mauj Darya Bukhari's own grave lies inside Shah Chiragh's mausoleum complex. Chishti, in his own account of Mauj Darya, also refers to the inside of Hazrat Shah Chiragh's tomb, in a sentence too damaged to read in full (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 104).
