## Overview

At Daraza, near Ranipur in the Khairpur district of Sindh, lies the shrine of Sachal Sarmast (1739–1827) — “the Truthful, the Intoxicated One” — one of the boldest and most beloved of the Sufi poets of Sindh. Where Shah Abdul Latif is the serene ocean of Sindhi mysticism, Sachal is its ecstatic fire: a poet of dazzling daring who sang the unity of being in seven languages and who was called, for the reckless intensity of his verse, the *Second Mansur* — after Mansur al-Hallaj, martyred for crying “I am the Truth.” His shrine at Daraza is a major centre of Sindhi devotion and music.

The books place the shrine in the same country. Michel Boivin writes that "Sachal Sarmast's dargah is located in the village of Daraza, not far from Khairpur, in Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 150, folio 137), and his glossary describes Daraza as "the name of a town in Northern Sindh where Sachal's dargah is" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 218, folio not stated). He lists "the dargah of Sachal Sarmast" among the remarkable sanctuaries of the country around Rohri, Sukkur and Shikarpur (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 31, folio 18), and counts Daraza among the "three main Sufi traditions of Sindh: Lal Shahbaz Qalandar in Sehwan Sharif, Daraza and Tando Jahaniyya in Hyderabad" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 60, folio 47).

The scholars who write of the poet rank him high. H. T. Sorley, in his study of Shah Abdul Latif, writes that "Sachal Sarmast (The Intoxicated) is the best of these poets" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 299, folio 290). Annemarie Schimmel calls him a poet whose "melodies and words are almost as popular today as they were 150 years ago" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 414, folio 383), and places him as a Sufi poet second only to Shah Abdul Latif (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 424, folio 393). They say little of the building itself; what they record is the poet, his verse, and the devotion that his verse has carried beyond Sindh.

## The Life of the Saint

Sachal Sarmast was born in 1739 at Daraza into a Sayyid family of the Farooqi line; his given name was Abdul Wahab, but he became known as *Sachal* — “the truthful” — and *Sarmast* — “the intoxicated,” “the ecstatic.” Orphaned of his father young, he was raised and initiated by his uncle and spiritual guide, Khwaja Abdul Haq, and spent almost his entire life in his native Daraza, rarely travelling, absorbed in devotion and song. It is told that as a boy he was present when the great Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai passed through, and that the elder poet foretold his coming greatness — a meeting cherished as the passing of a torch from one master of Sindhi verse to the next. He lived to a great age and died in 1827.

Boivin gives his name at birth as 'Khwaja Salah al-Din Ḥafiẓ Abd al-Wahhab' (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 93, folio 80). Schimmel reads the two halves of the name he came to bear. Sachal, she writes, is a diminutive of *sach*, 'truth', and seems an allusion to the word *Haqq* as found in Hallaj's sentence *anā'l-ḥaqq*; and "the name Sarmast indicates that he was an" 'intoxicated' Sufi — "and intoxicated he was indeed!" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 424, folio 393). Sorley glosses the second name the same way, as 'Sarmast (The Intoxicated)' (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 299, folio 290).

Of his manner of life Schimmel writes that "Sachal Sarmast was a versatile mystic who led a solitary life" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 424, folio 393). Schimmel and Rafat date him 1739–1826 (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 30, folio 26; Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 40, folio 20); Sorley says only that "he appears to have been born some time" between 1739 and a second year that cannot be read in the printed text (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 299, folio 290). Schimmel's *As Through a Veil* dates him in the body of the book only by a reckoning from an earlier Sindhi poet, Qadi Qadan (d. 1551): "And 250 years later his compatriot Sachal Sarmast writes that:" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 158, folio 140).

Schimmel writes that "Latif's tradition was taken up and developed by Sachal Sarmast (1739-1826), who met him when still a boy" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 30, folio 26), and calls him Shah Abdul Latif's "younger contemporary" and "younger compatriot" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 184, folio 184; p. 211, folio 211). Taufiq Rafat's selection of Bulleh Shah, the Punjabi poet of Kasur, calls Sachal "an admiring contemporary" of Bulleh Shah and "avowedly a follower of Bulleh Shah" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 40, folio 20; p. 42, folio 22). After him, Schimmel names his successor in verse: "Sachal's spiritual heir, Bedil of Rohri" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 202, folio 202).

## The Poetry

Sachal Sarmast poured out poetry in a remarkable range of tongues — Sindhi and Seraiki above all, but also Urdu, Persian, Punjabi, Balochi, and Arabic — earning him the title *Shair-e-Haft-Zaban*, “the poet of seven languages.” His theme, sung with unrestrained ardour, was the doctrine of *wahdat al-wujud*, the unity of all being in God, and the annihilation of the self in the divine reality; his verse breaks fearlessly through the forms of orthodoxy toward a naked declaration of union with the Beloved. It was this that won him the name of the “Second Mansur,” for like al-Hallaj he sang the identity of the lover and the Beloved without disguise. His poems are sung by the faqirs of his shrine and by folk-singers across Sindh, and their ecstatic boldness has made him a patron of all who prize love over law and truth over caution.

Schimmel names four of his languages. In *Pain and Grace* she writes that "he wrote in four languages: Sindhi, Siraiki, Urdu, and Persian" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 30, folio 26), and in *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* that "he wrote in Sindhi, Siraiki (the northern dialect of Sindhi), Urdu, and Persian, uttering whatever came into his heart with no thought for the consequences" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 424, folio 393). She weighs them unequally: "Like his predecessors, he relied upon folk tales and made Sassul and Sohni his heroines; he did not, however, elaborate the subjects as much as" Shah Abdul Latif did. "His Persian and Urdu poetry is traditional and conventional in style and expression, but in his mother tongue his feeling overpowers him." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 424, folio 393). The editions she lists keep his languages apart: a *Risalo* in Sindhi and a *Siraiki Kalam* (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 296, folio 296), and, in Persian, a *Diwan-i Ashikar* edited by Makhdum Amir Ahmad (Lahore, 1957) (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 491, folio 460); her index refers 'Ashikar' to Sachal Sarmast (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 513, folio 482).

What sets him apart, in Schimmel's reading, is that he says openly what Shah Abdul Latif only suggests: "His ecstatic poetry surpassed all limits of expression. In Shah's poetry, allusions and subtle images lead the understanding listener into the secrets of love, longing, and union; in Sachal's poems … the mysteries of all embracing Unity are put openly before the listener" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 30, folio 26). His verse, she adds, "reminds the reader often of the enthusiastic folksongs by Turkish medieval mystics, like those of Yunus Emre (d. 1321)" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 30, folio 26), and in *As Through a Veil* she goes further: "one can almost translate a Sindhi poem by Sachal Sarmast into Turkish and take it for an original verse by Yunus" Emre (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 169, folio 151). She counts him, with Bulleh Shah, among the poets who "identify themselves with everything created": "the Panjabi Bullhe Shah and the Sindhi Sachal Sarmast, with their highly ecstatic songs, are the most typical exponents of this tendency" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, pp. 176–177, folios 158–159).

The Second Mansur of the site's account is the poet of Hallaj in the books. Schimmel writes that, "contrary to his younger contemporary, the enthusiastic hymnodist Sachal Sarmast, and later Sindhi folk bards Shah only covertly alludes to the famous saying" of Hallaj, *ana 'l-haqq* (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 184, folio 184). Christopher Shackle, in his translation of Shah Abdul Latif's *Risalo*, likewise names Sachal Sarmast, with Bulleh Shah, among the Sufi poets who invoked *anā 'l-ḥaqq*, 'I am God', directly where Shah Latif preferred indirection (Shackle, *Risalo of Shah Abdul Latif*, p. 326). Schimmel speaks of "his ardent love for Hallaj, whose name occurs hundreds of times in his verses" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 427, folio 396), and sets him in a line that runs through Sind: "the Hallajian theories lived under the surface and now and then appeared in ecstatic expressions like that of Sarmad, of our Shah Latif, or his younger compatriot, Sachal Sarmast, until they were once more brought into a fascinating system by Muhammad Iqbal" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 211, folio 211).

He was called, too, the ʿAttar of Sind: "Sachal has been called" by that name, Schimmel writes, "and in fact the influence of" ʿAttar's imagery "is clearly visible in his poetry" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 426, folio 395). He drew on the sayings of the Prophet. "The hadith occurs very frequently in Sachal Sarmast's mystical poetry" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 236, folio not stated, footnote 2); "particularly Sachal Sarmast found here the proof for his ecstatic experience of union with God and of God's all-embracing Unity" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 250, folio 250); and "Shah's younger compatriot Sachal Sarmast dealt with the Prophetic traditions which he inserted in various forms and with very outspoken predilections into his poetry" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, pp. 250–251, folios 250–251). Schimmel also counts him among the heirs of the image of the Beloved who dwells within the heart, "Sachal Sarmast in 18th century Sind" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 206, folio 206).

Boivin's study of the Hindu Sufis of Sindh shows the other face of the same verse. "The next step of the initiation ritual shows the importance of Krishna among the Sindhi Sufis, including Muslim Sufis such as Sachal and Qutub Ali Shah" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 136, folio 123); "A number of Sufis from nineteenth-century Sindh, such as Sachal, Qutub Ali Shah and Bedil, themselves include Krishna in their Sufi poetry" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 198, folio 185). He reports that Jotwani called Shah Latif and Sachal "the late participaters in the Bhakti movement which had a major impact in Sindh through the spread of Guru Nanak's teaching" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 91, folio 78).

## In Verse

Schimmel gives a line that, she says, he repeats 'more than once', on the wine of 'Mansur-dom' and the handbooks *Kanz* and *Quduri*:

*Intoxication came from the wine of this Mansur-dom, Kanz and Quduri became annihilated!* (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 202, folio 202)

In *As Through a Veil* she quotes him again on the same books. Those who have tasted love, he says, cast the madrasa handbooks — the *Kanz*, the *Quduri* and the *Kafiya* — into the waves:

*Those who have tasted a sip from the syrup of the ocean of love* … *They say goodbye to syntax, and don't look any more at grammar!* (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 158, folio 140)

His spiritual heir took up the theme, and Bedil of Rohri, in Schimmel's rendering, "sings therefore":

*The ordinary men read Kanz Quduri, the lovers wisdom from God* (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 202, folio 202)

The Beloved within the heart is put as a question: "Why should one roam about in marketplaces to search for the friend? thus asks Sachal Sarmast: the friend is in the heart; He whom heaven and earth can not contain is the guest in the narrow hut of man's purified heart" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 230, folio 230).

Where Shah Abdul Latif is "more careful and less outspoken in his allusions", Schimmel writes, Sachal names the martyrs of love one by one (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 185, folio 185); Shah Latif does not "enumerate the names of those killed by their overwhelming divine love, as for instance Sachal Sarmast loved to do" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 246, folio 246). "A telling example is" a Sindhi poem of his that begins, in her translation:

*Welcome, welcome You are-- / to which place will You bring me? / You will again cut off a head! / Giving a kick to Sarmad You have killed him,* (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 174, folio 156)

The poem goes on that the Beloved "brought Mansur on the gallows" and cut off the head of Shaikh ʿAttar, and closes: "Now You are asking the way here!" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 175, folio 157). "Sachal adds to these generally known Sufis the names of martyred Indian religious heroes, such as Sarmad, Dara Shikoh's friend" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 175, folio 157), and he also speaks of Shah ʿInayat of Jhok (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 175, folio 157). In *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* Schimmel quotes his litany of the afflicted lovers, which counts among the slain Bulleh Shah, Jaʿfar, Sarmad, Mansur, Shaikh ʿAttar, Zakariya, Joseph, Shams, Sanʿan, Karmal and Bilawal, and Shah ʿInayat, whom her footnote calls the martyr-mystic of Jhok; the same footnote says that "Karmal and Bilawal have not yet been identified." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, pp. 425–426, folios 394–395; p. 426, folio 395). She quotes too a riddle on the self that opens 'Perhaps I am…' (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 425, folio 394).

Rafat's selection sets against Bulleh Shah's verse "a political poem of Sachal Sarmast" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 40, folio 20). In the English it prints, the speaker has seen a boat in midstream handled by expert oarsmen who "consider themselves the rulers of the ocean and think nothing of" Sindh and India:

*They are a proud people and consider themselves rulers but they are nothing but plunderers. Whenever they get the chance they take the goods of others by force. Yet the local people put a lot of trust in them...* (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 40, folio 20)

Schimmel points to further poems for the reader of the Sindhi *Risalo*: "One of the loveliest expressions of such a 'spiritual correspondence'" is, she writes, in Sachal's 'Sur Malkos', IV, vv. 1–10, with his 'Marui', III, vv. 2–4 (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 284, folio 266).

## The Shrine

Sachal Sarmast’s shrine at Daraza, ornamented in the glazed blue tilework of Sindh, is a busy centre of pilgrimage and, above all, of music: the singing of his poetry, often to the beat that stirs the *dhamal*, is central to its devotion. The shrine keeps his memory as the ecstatic voice of Sindhi Sufism.

The one feature of the fabric that the books record is an inscription. Boivin writes that "the inscription written on the main gate of Sachal's dargah is haqq mawjud" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 101–102, folios 88–89). The sources consulted give no date of building, no builder, and no account of the tomb chamber.

At Daraza hangs his picture. Boivin describes "the 'official' picture of Sachal, which hangs on a wall at Daraza", and reports the tradition of its making: "It is said that this portrait was drawn by a Sikh follower of Sachal. Sachal is represented in majesty in a pose that could be reminiscent of Guru Nanak’s iconography." In it "he sits on a gaddi with a club in his right hand, and a musical instrument" in his left (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 151, folio 138).

Daraza also holds the remains of Hindu Sufis. Of the Hindu Sufi Nimano Faqir, Boivin writes: "Nimano Faqir was cremated, but his ashes were deposited in a tomb in the dargah compound in Daraza, Pakistan, near that of Sachal Sarmast." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 131, folio 118). Of a woman whose life he follows he writes: "After her death in 1963, her ashes were buried in Daraza close to Sachal Sarmast." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 60, folio 47); and, again, "Her cremation took place on the spot and her ashes were carried to Daraza, where a grave sheltered them." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 74, folio 61).

Daraza has its line of masters, its *sajjada nashins*. Boivin writes that 'sakhi' "is a laqab of the Masters of the Daraza silsila, in Sindh. It is a kind of honorific title they have been given for generations" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 78, folio 65), and that it is "the generic name given to the Sufi masters of Daraza, but designating especially Sachal Sarmast" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 72, folio 59). He names "the new sajjada nashin of Daraza, Pir Khwaja Abd al-Haqq II (1915–78)", who in 1956 visited the shrine to consecrate the spiritual authority of Nimano Faqir (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 74, folio 61), and, in the present day, "Sakhi Qabul Muhammad Faruqi, the sajjada nashin of Daraza" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 96–97, folios 83–84). Nimano Faqir's own master was a Sakhi Qabul Muhammad of Daraza, whom Boivin calls a murshid "of Daraza, Sachal's dargah" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 150, folio 137), and who is nicknamed 'Sakhi Darazi' (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 135, folio 122). Boivin's glossary adds that 'Sakhi' "also refers to several Sufi masters of Daraza" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 220, folio 207). Boivin, following Thapan, writes that the spiritual masters of the Indian Hindu Sufi lineages "are still in Sindh, Pakistan": "They are the sajjada nashin of Daraza, of Tando Jahaniya and of Jhok Sharif. At each of them, a great Sufi poet is attached: Sachal Sarmast, Qutub Ali Shah and Nasir Faqir" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 27, folio 14).

## Devotion and Practice

The annual urs draws great crowds from across Sindh for days of poetry, music, and celebration, in the exuberant Sindhi manner, alongside the rites of prayer and remembrance.

Boivin gives its day: "Throughout the year, one of the most significant events is the urs of Sachal Sarmast. Since it is in the Islamic calendar, the 14th of Ramazan, the date changes every year, be it in the Christian calendar or in the Hindu one named Vikram Samvat (or Sambat)." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 143, folio 130). Omar Kasmani notes that the Sindhi publications of the state, which "hardly mention the saint or the shrine of Sehwan until the late 1960s", featured the other saints of Sindh: "other saints of Sindh (especially Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai, Sachal Sarmast, and Shah Inayat) were regularly featured during this time, and their annual fairs were marked by special editions" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 70, folio 53).

Abida Parvin sings his verse. Shemeem Burney Abbas, who spells him 'Sacchal Sarmast', names him among the poets Abida Parvin sings (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 54, folio not stated), and writes that "she is able to sing the poetry of Sufi poets like Shah Abdul Latif and Sacchal Sarmast in Sindhi, which her male qawwāl counterparts cannot" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 105, folio not stated). She also records what the shrine does not have. She quotes Naseer Mirza on the *loṭevālī* tradition: "there is no such tradition at Sacchal Sarmast… I have not seen such a tradition at Bhit Shah—not at Sacchal Sarmast—not at Rikhail Shah—or any of the Sufi dargāhs [shrines] in the Sind" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 61, folio not stated).

His following reached beyond Muslims. Rafat writes of "such distinct adherents as Sachal Sarmast in Sindh, avowedly a follower of Bulleh Shah, who continued to send his disciples to Amritsar for homage." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 42, folio 22). Boivin writes that "the two main Sufi traditions that were very popular among the Hindus were Daraza and Jhok" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 60, folio 47), and that Sachal "had a large number of followers among the Hindu trade castes of" Northern Sindh, "and some of them transferred their love for singing Sachal's poetry to India when they migrated in the wake of partition" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 150, folio 137).

## Daraza Across the Border

Boivin's study follows those followers into India, where they kept the name of Daraza. "The Daraza school of Sufism still attracts many Hindu Sindhis of India. Already, we mentioned they organized ‘Darazi satsangs’ in Mumbai and Ulhasnagar. They gather to sing Sachal Sarmast's kalam." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 152, folio 139). In this he finds the model case of a Sindhi Sufi carried by a Hindu path without any formal tie: "The first category is made up of Sindhi Sufis without any relation with the involved path. The exemplary example in this context is Sachal." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 109, folio 96). "There are also other Sufi poets claiming to be 'Darazi'." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 152, folio 139), among them a Hindu faqir who took the name into his own, Faqir Radhakrishin Lal Sahib Darazi, whose *Kalam* was printed at Ulhasnagar in 1986 (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 230, folio 217).

"The two darbars devoted to Sachal Sarmast are located in the same area of Ulhasnagar, camp 5." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 151, folio 138). At one of them, the Maqbul Manzil, "only a weekly ritual event is organized", for "In this space devoted to Sachal Sarmast, a Darazi satsang is organized every Thursday under the supervision of Soni Kataria. The satsang starts at 7.00 pm and lasts until 9.00." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 142, folio 129). It opens with an *arti*: "Sachal's devotees started the satsang with an arti: nothing distinguishes it from any other arti performed in a Hindu temple." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 204, folio 191). Then "After the arti, the devotees sing Sachal's kalam. Two devotees are musicians: one plays harmonium and the other the tablas. Soni is the leading singer, and she knows the verses by heart… About twenty people attend the satsangs." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 143, folio 130). The darbar passed down a family: "after his death, it was run by Hari Ram, who took the charge to sing Sachal’s poetry. After him, it was his daughter Soni who performed the satsang every Thursday evening" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 151, folio 138).

The tie to Daraza is kept by the master in Sindh. "The sajjada nashin of Daraza has paid three visits to the darbar", and "he calls on them to give the date of Sachal's versi" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 151, folio 138); "Soni has to call, or is called by phone by the sajjada nashin of Daraza, for him to inform her of the timing." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 143, folio 130). In India "two versis are celebrated over the course of the year: Sachal's versi on the 14th of Ramazan, and Hari Ram's versi in the beginning of November" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 151, folio 138), and at the Sachal Sain darbar "Two versis are celebrated in the darbar: Sachal’s and Pratap’s." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 151, folio 138).

In the Rohani Rah he is worshipped in an image. "The core of the worship in the Rohani Rah is a statue (murti) of Sachal Sarmast. This murti was obviously carved according to the 'official' picture of Sachal" that hangs at Daraza; "The only difference between the two is that, in the statue, Sachal has a golden tilak on his forehead." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 151, folio 138). The shrine itself stands above it: "Above Sachal's murti, there is Krishna as a flute player, without Radha, and still above this, the darbar of Sachal Sarmast in Daraza." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 152, folio 139). In the Radha Krishin Lal darbar it is the other way: at the core of the altar is a photograph of a master of Daraza, Sakhi Qabul Muhammad, "instead of a representation of Sachal. But the whole picture set-up is dominated by an inscription in Latin alphabet: ‘Sai Sachal’"; asked why it was Sakhi and not Sachal, Soni "answered that Sakhi is the one who looks most like Sachal." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 152, folio 139).

His verse is the core of these gatherings' song. "The repertoire consists mainly of works by Sachal and Nimano." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 142, folio 129); "kalams written by Sindhi Sufis like Sachal Sarmast are still sung every day" at Nimano Faqir's shrine (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 74, folio 61); "his poetry is sung in all the Sufi darbars of India" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 109, folio 96). At Dadi Dhan's *versi* in 2012, "The most requested author was Sachal Sarmast, then Rochaldas" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 149, folio 136); Daulatram's was kept "with the chanting of Sufi songs composed by Sachal Sarmast, Shah Abd al-Latif and Qutub Ali Shah" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 150, folio 137); and Mira Rajani "used to sing Sachal's poetry during the satsang" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 151, folio 138). Hindu teachers drew on him: "Mulchand also quotes Sachal Sarmast's poetry in his explanation" of *om* (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 168, folio 155), and "Rochaldas also quotes verses from Kabir, Bedil, Sachal and Ajiz" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 166, folio 153).

In some places his presence has waned. Sachal "was at the origin of the creation of the Sakhi Kuthiyya founded by Nimano Faqir in Baroda, and later on Vadodara" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 132, folio 119), a name that "would mean something like the abode of the generous" and "referred to the original dargah in Sindh, and furthermore to the spiritual head" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 78, folio 65); yet "in Vadodara, Sachal has declined, and now Nimano is at the forefront." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 201, folio 188). "We saw that, in the Nimanal Sangam, the reference to Sachal has even been suppressed." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 204, folio 191). Of the 2011 Vadodara edition of Nimano Faqir's verse Boivin writes: "Interestingly, one cannot find Sachal's poetry, while in original Nimano's kalam, he was given a large part of the poetry. Guru Nanak's poetry has been kept in this new edition." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 172, folio 159). Even so, "Nimano Faqir's verses still embodied the very spirit of Sindhi Sufism, and especially that of the Darazi silsila." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, pp. 204–205, folios 191–192).

## Legends and Miracles

The meeting with Shah Abdul Latif is the best-remembered story of his childhood. Boivin tells it as legend: "There is a legend according to which Shah Latif met Sachal Sarmast. When he was about seven, it is said that Shah Laṭif came to pay homage to the local murshid. After seeing Sachal, he is said to have exclaimed: 'This God-gifted child will one day uncover those divine secrets which I have still kept concealed.'" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 93, folio 80). Sorley writes that he "is alleged as a boy to have met Shah Abdul Latif" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 299, folio 290), and Schimmel, in *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, tells the meeting story and in the same paragraph offers an ordinary origin for it (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 424, folio 393).

A second legend tells of an attempt on his life. "The legend states that the swords passed through his body as if it was water." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 93, folio 80).

## Setting

Boivin's account of the Khairpur country speaks of the place, not of the shrine. After 1857 "many Kayasht migrated to work in the Princely States, including the Khairpur emirate in Sindh" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 26, folio 13), and "the amirs of Khairpur were themselves staunch Shia Muslims, and they built beautiful imambaras in the nineteenth century, but it did not prevent them from being generous patrons for all of the religious buildings" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 31, folio 18). Of the region's languages he writes that Siraiki is "the Sindhi dialect mostly spoken in Northern Sindh, and especially among the Baluchi population", and that "many Sindhi Sufi poets, especially from Northern Sindh, composed Sufi poetry in several languages" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 171, folio 158); Schimmel, likewise, that "Many later Sindhi Sufi poets, mainly those of Baloch origin, used also the northern dialect of Sindhi, Siraiki" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 154, folio 154). The Sufis to whom the Hindus of Sindh look, Boivin adds, "mostly belong to Upper Sindh, with Siraiki as a language used for Sufi poetry besides Sindhi" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 108, folio 95). And of the figure at the centre of Sachal's verse: "In Sindh, Hallaj is a very popular figure, and many Sufi poets mention his name in their verses" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 102, folio 89).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The year of his death.** The site's account gives 1827. Schimmel gives 1826 in three books: "(1739-1826)" and "(d. 1826)" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 30, folio 26; Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 202, folio 202), "(d. 1826)" in the index of *As Through a Veil* (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 361, folio 343), and 1826 in the text and the index of *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 414, folio 383; Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 424, folio 393; Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 524, folio 493). Rafat's selection gives "(1739-1826)" (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 40, folio 20). Sorley gives no year of death (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 299, folio 290).
- **The year of his birth.** The site's account, Schimmel and Rafat give 1739 (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 30, folio 26; Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 40, folio 20). Sorley gives a span: he "appears to have been born some time" between 1739 and a second year that is damaged in the printed text (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 299, folio 290).
- **His dating in *As Through a Veil*.** The index gives "(d. 1826)" (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 361, folio 343); the text has him write "250 years later" than Qadi Qadan, who died in 1551 (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 158, folio 140).
- **His languages.** The site's account gives seven — Sindhi, Seraiki, Urdu, Persian, Punjabi, Balochi and Arabic — and the title *Shair-e-Haft-Zaban*. Schimmel names four, Sindhi, Siraiki, Urdu and Persian (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 30, folio 26; Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 424, folio 393).
- **His name.** The site's account gives Abdul Wahab; Boivin gives his birth name as 'Khwaja Salah al-Din Ḥafiẓ Abd al-Wahhab' (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 93, folio 80). Abbas spells the poet 'Sacchal Sarmast' (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 61, folio not stated).
- **The meeting with Shah Abdul Latif.** The site's account has the boy present when Shah Abdul Latif passed through, and the elder poet foretell his greatness. Boivin's legend has Shah Latif come "to pay homage to the local murshid" when the boy was about seven, and exclaim that the child would "one day uncover those divine secrets" he had kept concealed (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 93, folio 80). Sorley calls the meeting alleged (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 299, folio 290); Schimmel's *Pain and Grace* states it plainly, "who met him when still a boy" (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 30, folio 26); and her *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* offers an ordinary origin for the story (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 424, folio 393).
- **Daraza.** The site's account places the shrine at Daraza, near Ranipur, in the Khairpur district. Boivin calls Daraza a village "not far from Khairpur" in one place (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 150, folio 137) and "a town in Northern Sindh" in his glossary, which names no district (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 218, folio not stated).
- **The day of the urs.** The site's account gives an annual urs and no day. Boivin gives the 14th of Ramazan for the urs (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 143, folio 130) and for the *versi* kept in India, while on the same page he writes that "Sachal's birthday is on Ramazan in the Islamic calendar" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 151, folio 138).
- **The masters of Daraza.** Boivin numbers Nimano Faqir's master, Sakhi Qabul Muhammad of Daraza, in more than one way on different pages, and does not say whether these forms name one man (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 150, folio 137; Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 152, folio 139).
- **Dalpat Sufi.** Boivin once writes "As stated by Dalpat Sufi, or Sachal Sarmast, hereafter Sachal, the true Sufi is beyond religion" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 20, folio 7), as if the two names were one man; his own table of annexes lists Dalpat Sufi as a separate poet, and does not list Sachal Sarmast (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 13, folio not stated).
- **The editor of the Sindhi *Risalo*.** Schimmel's bibliography gives the 1958 Karachi edition of the *Risalo* as edited by Othman Ali Ansari (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 491, folio 460); Boivin's gives "Ashman Ali Ansari" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 231, folio 218).

## Legacy

With Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai, Sachal Sarmast is one of the two great poet-saints of Sindh — the intoxicated counterpart to Bhittai’s sublime serenity — and among the most fearless voices of *wahdat al-wujud* in all of South Asian Sufism. His multilingual verse, his ecstatic sincerity, and his refusal of every veil between the soul and God have kept him beloved for two centuries, and his shrine at Daraza remains a living hearth of the Sindhi Sufi song he raised to such heights.

Schimmel closes her account of him: "But none of the later mystical poets can match Sachal's intense glow; he is a vigorous poet, and the rhythms and energy, the intense yearning and love, give his verses an almost magical quality." (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 427, folio 396). Boivin reports him named among the "'three great Sufi teachers'", "who are Shah Inayat, Shah Abd al-Latif and Sachal Sarmast" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 49, folio 36), and as the second of the 'trinity of the Sindhi poets' (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 91, folio 78). In anthologies of Sindhi Sufi verse he stands at or near the head: in one, "First comes Sachal Sarmast, with forty-six abstracts from his Sindhi poetry." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 158, folio 145); in Vaswani's of 1955, "The first Muslim Sufi is, unsurprisingly, Shah Abd al-Latif, followed by Rohal, Murad, Sachal and Bedil" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 156, folio 143); another author's book "focuses on Shah Latif and Sachal" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 155, folio 142). Boivin also notes where he is absent: in one list "no Sufi from Sindh, including those who are much-acclaimed poets in Sindhi, as for instance Sachal Sarmast, is enumerated." (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 213, folio 200, Ch. 4 n. 4).

A body of scholarship has grown around him. Jethmal Parsram wrote a book on him in 1922 (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 48, folio 35). Boivin records of one woman that "In 1955, she had edited the Sindhi kalam of Sachal Sarmast, and was herself the author of a Sindhi kalam with the title of Haqq mawjud" — the same words that stand over the shrine's gate (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 60, folio 47). Abd al-Karim Sindilo published a glossary of his poetry, *Lughat-e Sachal*, at Khairpur in 1984, from the Sachal Chair of Shah Abdul Latif University (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 94, folio 81; Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 232, folio 219); Ram Panjwani "also published a book on Sachal Sarmast's poetry, with the title" *Sachal jyun kafiyun* (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 215, folio 202, Ch. 6 n. 2); and the Culture and Tourism Department of the Government of Sindh published, in 2002, a *Study of Mysticism in Darazi School of Sufi Thought* by Dr. Sakhi Qabool Muhammad Faruqi (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 234, folio 221). Sorley's study already notes "a big edition of the poetry of Sachal (Sarmast)" by an editor whose name, partly damaged in the printed text, appears to read Agha Ghulam Nabi Khan Sufi (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 212, folio 203, footnote 1), and lists a work titled 'Sachal Sarmast', of 1933, under the name of an Agha Sufi (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 192, folio 183).

His verse has been printed in Sindhi, Siraiki and Persian. Schimmel lists a Sindhi *Risalo* published by the Sindhi Adabi Board at Karachi in 1958 and a Siraiki *Kalam* edited by Maulwi Hakim Muhammad Sadiq Ranipuri, published by the same board at Karachi in 1959 (Schimmel, *As Through a Veil*, p. 335, folio 317), with a Persian *Diwan-i Ashikar* published at Lahore in 1957 (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 491, folio 460). Boivin lists the 1958 *Risalo Sachal Sarmast* as published at Karachi and Hyderabad, a *Sachal jo risalo. Siraiki kalam* edited by Mowlana Muhammad Sadiq Ranipuri and published at Kandiaro in 1997, and a *Sachal Sarmast Jo Kalam* from the Sindhi Akademy at Delhi in 1997 (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 231, folio 218).
