## Overview

Deep in the mountains of the Khuzdar district of Balochistan, in the remote valley of Lahut Lamakan, stands the shrine of Shah Noorani (Syed Bilawal Shah Noorani), one of the most venerated and most inaccessible Sufi shrines in Pakistan. Set amid a landscape long held sacred and reached by a hard journey through the hills, the darbar draws pilgrims from across Sindh and Balochistan, who come in great numbers for its *dhamal* and its blessing, undeterred by its remoteness.

Jürgen Schaflechner, in his study of the goddess Hinglaj and her pilgrimage, describes the valley as "the abode of the saint Shah Noorani in the Khuzdar district in Balochistan", a saint "who is said to have lived in this valley" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 108, folio not stated). He writes the place as Lahoot la Makan, Lahoot or Lāhūt (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 108, folio not stated). Omar Kasmani, writing of the fakirs of Sehwan, calls it Lahut, "a revered site in Balochistan" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 114, folio 97). Neither book gives the saint the names Syed Bilawal, and neither records his dates, his lineage, his spiritual order or an annual *ʿurs*; Kasmani names no saint, tomb or custodian at Lahut at all (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 114, folio 97).

## The Saint and the Sacred Valley

Shah Noorani is remembered as a Sufi saint of the medieval centuries who settled in the wild valley of Lahut Lamakan, a place whose very name (*la-hut la-makan*, “the placeless place of the divine”) marks it as a landscape of ancient sanctity, associated in legend with the mystic quest and revered across religious traditions. His life is known more through devotion and legend than through documented history, but his sanctity is held in the highest regard, and his shrine has for centuries been a goal of arduous pilgrimage. The setting, with its mountains, caves and springs held holy, gives the shrine an aura unlike any other.

Schaflechner's account bears out the valley's landscape. Comparing it with the valley of the goddess Hinglaj, he writes: "There are fascinating similarities between the two valleys. Both are small oases with natural springs (Urd. chaśmah) and caves in confined valleys." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 108, folio not stated) Of the saint himself he says only that he "is said to have lived in this valley" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 108, folio not stated).

The valley's sanctity, in the books, is older than the saint and wider than Islam. Schaflechner reports that "Abro suggests that Lahoot was once an important place for Hindu worship (Abro 1991), which is consistent with the history of other Hindu sites in the area that were converted to Sufi centers over time (Boivin 2007)." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 108, folio not stated) The valley's name also appears in his book in the titles of two works in the region's own languages: Badar Abro's *Hinglāj ai Lāhūt* (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 94, folio not stated) and an Urdu book by Nazamani, *Lāhūṭ lā Makān* (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 108, folio not stated).

Two other saints pass through the valley in these books. Schaflechner lists "Lahoot (Khuzdar)" among the places of Shah Abdul Latif's wandering (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 95, folio not stated). Kasmani, in a note on the journey of the saint he calls Lal, lists Lahut-lamakan among the places where he stopped, with Makran, Panjgur and Pasni in Balochistan and Naing, Lake Manchhar and Sehwan in Sindh (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 188, folio 171, ch. 1 n. 14). What these passages say is said of the valley and of those saints, not of Shah Noorani or his shrine.

## The Shrine

The heart of the site, in Schaflechner's description, is a cave: "The main attraction in Lahoot is the cave of the saint Shah Noorani, who is said to have lived in this valley." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 108, folio not stated) He visited it himself in 2010 (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 108, folio not stated).

Inside the cave is a stone. "Inside the cave, one finds a curiously shaped stone that is allegedly the image of Ali's camel. Some authors claim that this image used to be worshipped as a śiva liṅga before the place became associated with the Sufi saint (Khatri 1973), and indeed, the shape of the stone would support this idea, as I could see during a visit in 2010." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 108, folio not stated)

The sources consulted describe no tomb and record no date of building, no builder or patron, no custodian and no endowment for the shrine.

## Devotion and Pilgrimage

Despite its remoteness, Shah Noorani's shrine is a place of intense devotion, famous above all for the *dhamal*, the ecstatic drum-driven trance-dance performed by crowds of pilgrims, and for the *langar* and qawwali that mark its gatherings. Pilgrims undertake the difficult journey to seek healing, fulfil vows, and share in the charged atmosphere of the sacred valley.

Both books place the valley on a pilgrimage route that runs from Sehwan. Schaflechner writes that "Lal Shahbaz is also the starting point for the pilgrimage to Lahoot la Makan" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 108, folio not stated). Kasmani's fakirs "proceed to Lahut, a revered site in Balochistan, as do many fakirs upon the culmination of the three-day annual fair of" Sehwan (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 114, folio 97). In one of Kasmani's narratives, Lahut is a possible end of a woman's journey, and her husband, searching for her, wonders: "And if she was indeed in Lahut, would she trace her journey back to Sehwan or would she rather follow other fakirs and proceed to shrines in Karachi?" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 114, folio 97)

The route once ran further, to the goddess of Hinglaj. "This pilgrimage route was once connected to the Makran Coast and the abode of 'Nani Hinglaj'… Today, however, this path seems to have been widely forgotten." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 108, folio not stated) Schaflechner finds a trace of it in Nazamani's Urdu book: "A vestige of this now obsolete pilgrimage route can be found at the end of the Urdu book Lāhūṭ lā Makān by Nazamani, which provides a description of the way from Lahoot to Hinglaj (Nazamani 2009, 26)." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 108, folio not stated) In 2010 he met at Lahoot la Makan a Punjabi pilgrim who had come from Sehwan Sharif and who "confirmed that this side of the pilgrimage route is still frequented; however, he mentioned that the longer path to Hinglaj is no longer used due to the dangerous political situation in the area." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 147, folio not stated)

Schaflechner also records a recent change in the journey itself: "Interestingly, and similar to Hinglaj, Lahoot is currently experiencing a new wave of interest mainly due to a newly constructed road leading to the shrine thus making the journey through the mountainous area easier." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 108, folio not stated)

The sources consulted give no date for an *ʿurs* at the shrine.

## The Attack of 2016

The shrine's fame and its throngs also made it a target. In November 2016 a suicide bombing during the evening *dhamal* killed and injured many worshippers, one of the deadliest attacks on a Pakistani shrine; yet, as at other darbars struck by such violence, the devotion of the pilgrims endured and the shrine continued to draw them.

Schaflechner, setting the attack against the new road and the new interest it had brought, writes: "However, a terrible bomb blast in November 2016, killing more than fifty people, has curbed the growing influx of visitors and has created a volatile situation for Balochistan's shrines." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 108, folio not stated) He names no attacker.

## Legends

The one legend the books record at the site belongs to the stone in the cave, which, Schaflechner reports, is "allegedly the image of Ali's camel" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 108, folio not stated). Beside it he sets the claim of other authors that the same stone was once worshipped as a *śiva liṅga* (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 108, folio not stated).

A possible further mention of the saint stands in the same book, and its reference is uncertain. Raj Kumar, a pilgrim whose words Schaflechner reports, says: "Again we are climbing the mountains for Noorani" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 160, folio not stated). The book does not say which Noorani is meant, and its index carries "Noorani" and "Noorani, Shah" as two separate headings (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 354, folio not stated). Another saint of Balochistan, Pir Chhatal Shah Noorani of Jhal Magsi District, bears the same name, so the words may not refer to Shah Noorani of Lahut Lamakan at all.

## Setting

The books say a good deal about Balochistan in general, and some of it frames the shrine's world. None of what follows is said of Shah Noorani or his valley.

Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi dates the first Muslim advance through the region: "In 710 Muhammad bin Qasim al-Saqafi began his advance through Makran and Baluchistan with the conquest of Sind between 711 and 712" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 127, folio 109). After that conquest, writes Shemeem Burney Abbas, "a long line of Sufi mystics came to the region through Baluchistan, Mekran, Multan, and Sind itself" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 50, folio not stated). Annemarie Schimmel finds the popularity of Gilani in the region in its names: "Proper names like Ghauth Bakhsh, 'gift of the help,' in Sind and Balochistan point to his [Gilani's] popularity" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 279, folio 248).

Abbas writes of a shared devotional world: "The mystical lore of the region that includes Sind, Baluchistan, and the coastal belt of Iran shares myths, melodies, and linguistic codes" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, pp. 47–48, folios not stated), and the "Mast Qalandar" *dhun* is "reported to be sung all along the Rajasthan, Sind, Baluchistan, and Iran continuum" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 57, folio not stated). Of the *dhamal* she writes: "Many times, families will bring women who are emotionally disturbed to participate in the dhammāl, which devotees claim has healing powers. In Baluchistan, such healing ceremonies are called guāṭi" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 66, folio not stated). Her glossary defines the same word as "A ritual for making vows at Sufi shrines in Baluchistan" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 182, folio not stated). She describes the *mugarmān* drum as "held sacred by the Sidi communities in Sind, Baluchistan, Rajasthan, and Gujrat who use them in their shrines and for rituals" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 185, folio not stated).

The region's poetry and legend are held in common with Sindh. Abbas writes that the Sassi myth is "told in the lands of Sind and Baluchistan" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 118, folio not stated), and Schimmel counts "the hill slopes of Balochistan" within the geography of Sind's folk tales (Schimmel, *Pain and Grace*, p. 161, folio 161). H. T. Sorley, in his study of Shah Abdul Latif, writes that the poetry of Sind and that of Baluchistan have certain elements in common (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 256, folio 247); quoting Longworth Dames, he describes the Baluchis' poetry as "wholly oral", "sung by professional minstrels called Doms or Dombs who sing the songs in the gatherings of the clans" (Sorley, *Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit*, p. 255, folio 246).

For pilgrims from India, Schaflechner writes, the province is hard to reach: they "must obtain an additional visa allowing travel to Balochistan, a province that has been in a state of a latent insurgency since the establishment of Pakistan (Axmann 2012)" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 169, folio not stated).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **The name of the valley.** The site's account writes Lahut Lamakan, glossing it as “the placeless place of the divine”; the location given for the shrine is Lahoot Lamakan. Schaflechner writes Lahoot la Makan, Lahoot and Lāhūt, and gives the Urdu title *Lāhūṭ lā Makān* (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 108, folio not stated). Kasmani writes Lahut and Lahut-lamakan (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 114, folio 97; p. 188, folio 171, ch. 1 n. 14).
- **The attack of November 2016.** The site's account calls it a suicide bombing during the evening *dhamal* that killed and injured many worshippers. Schaflechner calls it "a terrible bomb blast in November 2016, killing more than fifty people", and does not say when in the day it struck or who carried it out (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 108, folio not stated).
- **The pilgrims after 2016.** The site's account says that the devotion of the pilgrims endured and the shrine continued to draw them. Schaflechner writes that the blast "has curbed the growing influx of visitors" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 108, folio not stated).
- **The journey.** The site's account calls the shrine one of the most inaccessible in Pakistan, reached by a hard journey through the hills. Schaflechner reports "a newly constructed road leading to the shrine thus making the journey through the mountainous area easier" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 108, folio not stated).
- **What guāṭi means.** Abbas, in her text, calls the healing ceremonies of the *dhamal* in Baluchistan guāṭi (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 66, folio not stated); her glossary defines the word as "A ritual for making vows at Sufi shrines in Baluchistan" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 182, folio not stated). She says this of Baluchistan, not of this shrine.

## Legacy

Shah Noorani stands among the great shrines of Balochistan and lower Sindh: a saint of the sacred mountain valley of Lahut Lamakan whose darbar, for all its remoteness, remains a powerful centre of ecstatic devotion. Its *dhamal*, its legends and its holy landscape make it one of the most singular pilgrimage places in Pakistan, and its endurance after the tragedy of 2016 a testament to the tenacity of popular faith.

In the books, the valley's place is that of a station on a long road of devotion: a goal for the fakirs who leave Sehwan when its fair is over (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 114, folio 97), once a stage on the way to the goddess of Hinglaj (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 108, folio not stated), and a valley whose cave and stone carry the memory of more than one faith (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 108, folio not stated).
