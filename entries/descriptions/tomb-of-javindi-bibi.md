## Overview

The Tomb of Bibi Jawindi at Uch Sharif is among the most beautiful — and most poignant — monuments of Islamic architecture in Pakistan: a jewel of blue-and-white glazed tilework, half-ruined by the flooding of the river, that has become the very emblem of Uch Sharif’s medieval glory. Bibi Jawindi was a saintly woman of the great Bukhari sayyid family of Uch, a descendant of Makhdoom Jahaniyan Jahangasht, and her ornate mausoleum, raised in the late fifteenth century, stands within the famous cluster of tombs in the Bukhari quarter of the town.

Two of the books consulted for this article name the tomb, and both saw it. Haroon Khalid, travelling with Iqbal Qaiser, came to "the shrine of Bibi Jiwindi" on a mound outside the town and calls it "arguably the most important" of the three buildings that stand there (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 281, folio 255; Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 285, folio not stated). Robert Rozehnal passed "near the crumbling tomb of Bibi Jawindi" on a day's visit to Uch Sharif in April 1996, and there met the stranger whose story opens his book (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 21, folio 10). The two books spell her name differently, Jiwindi and Jawindi, and they do not agree with each other, or with the site's account, on whose kinswoman she was, on when the tomb was built, or on when the flood that ruined it came. Each account is set out below as its author gives it, and the differences are gathered under 'Where the Sources Disagree'.

The other histories and studies consulted speak of Uch Sharif, its saints and its Suhrawardi past, but none of them names Bibi Jawindi. Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi's account of the Sufis of Uch, the longest treatment of the town among them, names no woman and no builder or craftsman (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 299–304, folios 277–282). What those books say of the town is gathered under 'Setting', as context for the tomb and not as a statement about it.

## Bibi Jawindi and her Tomb

Bibi Jawindi is remembered as a pious woman of the Jalali Bukhari line — by tradition a great-granddaughter of the celebrated saint Makhdoom Jahaniyan Jahangasht — venerated for her holiness. Her tomb was built around 1493, tradition holds, by an Iranian prince, Dilshad, in her honour. It is an octagonal, tiered structure crowned by a dome, its surfaces sheathed in the exquisite cobalt-blue, turquoise, and white *kashi* tilework for which Uch and Multan are famed, and adorned with corner turrets — a masterpiece of the regional style that flourished around the tombs of the Suhrawardi saints. Over the centuries the changing course of the river undermined the structure, and a great part of it collapsed (by tradition in a flood in the nineteenth century), leaving the ravishing half-tomb that visitors see today — a fragment whose surviving beauty only sharpens the sense of what has been lost.

Khalid, too, makes her a great-granddaughter, but of another man: the tomb, he writes, "belonged to the great granddaughter of Syed Jalaludin Bokhari" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 285, folio not stated). Rozehnal makes her a wife, not a descendant: she is "the wife of the renowned fourteenth-century Suhrawardi shaykh, Sayyid Jalal ad-Din Bukhari (1303–1383)" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 21, folio 10). That relation is all Rozehnal gives of her. His book records no date of death for her, no date of building, no custodian, no *ʿurs* and no measurements, and it calls her by no name other than Bibi Jawindi (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 21, folio 10).

Of her death Khalid's book preserves only the beginning of a sentence. Iqbal Qaiser, standing at the tomb, says "that Bibi Jiwindi died during the …", and the rest of the line cannot be read in the printed text (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 285, folio not stated). No other book consulted gives the date or the manner of her death.

Khalid describes the tomb as he found it. The shrines on the mound needed a car to reach; they lay "on the outskirts, situated on top of a mound, which was being used as a graveyard" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 281, folio 255). "Half the structures of the shrine had been razed and were protected by iron rods", and what remained was "made out of glazed Multani tiles, coloured in blue and white", with "a white dome on top" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 281, folio 255). Of the building's date he reports what was posted at the site: "The last shrine on this mound was that of Bibi Jiwindi and it is arguably the most important of these three buildings. According to the board here, this tomb was built in 1494" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 285, folio not stated). Of its ruin Iqbal Qaiser gives a different century from the site's account: "There was a huge flood here in the sixteenth century and these buildings were destroyed at that time" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 281, folio 255).

On the same mound, Khalid writes, there were "several graves … which were covered arbitrarily with the blue and white Multani tiles that had been recovered from these historical shrines" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 285, folio not stated). He calls the whole group "thirteenth and fourteenth century shrines", reached through "the market", and Iqbal Qaiser, who had been there before, remarked: "This place has changed since the last time I came here" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 281, folio 255). Looking at the ruins, Khalid recalls the river that once ran beside them: "River Sutlej, the lost river of Pakistan, used to flow from here before it was lost forever after the Indus Water Treaty was signed between India and Pakistan in 1960", and he imagines that "Nanak must have seen these buildings intact with a mighty river flowing next to it" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, pp. 281–282, folios 255–256).

Khalid's pages on the mound do not themselves name the town. They are read as describing Uch Sharif from the shrines they name and from his later mention of "the shrine of Jalaludin Bokhari at Uch Sharif" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 288, folio 262). Rozehnal's single word for the tomb is "crumbling", and his only description of its surroundings is of the town as a whole (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 21, folio 10).

## The Monuments of Uch Sharif

Bibi Jawindi’s tomb does not stand alone: it is the finest of a group of contiguous mausolea in the Bukhari quarter of Uch — including those associated with Baha al-Halim (a teacher of the line) and the tomb’s own builder — that together form one of the most important surviving ensembles of pre-Mughal funerary architecture in the subcontinent. So exceptional are they that the monuments of Uch Sharif have been placed on UNESCO’s Tentative List of World Heritage sites, and they have been the subject of international conservation efforts. The tombs make Uch Sharif a destination for lovers of architecture and history as much as for pilgrims.

Khalid counts three buildings on the mound and ranks hers the most important of them (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 285, folio not stated). He found the surviving halves of the shrines "protected by iron rods" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 281, folio 255). Rozehnal, who names no tomb at Uch but hers, writes of "the magnificent blue and white tiled mosques and desolate tombs of this ancient center of learning, trade, and pilgrimage built on a hill overlooking the confluence of the Sutlej and Chenab rivers" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 21, folio 10).

## An Encounter at the Tomb

Rozehnal's book begins at this tomb. In April 1996, on a day's journey from the *ʿurs* at Allahabad, "the rural village of Allahabad—sixty miles southwest of the city of Bahawalpur—in southern Punjab", he spent a day wandering through Uch Sharif, and he writes that "near the crumbling tomb of Bibi Jawindi—the wife of the renowned fourteenth-century Suhrawardi shaykh, Sayyid Jalal ad-Din Bukhari (1303–1383)—we were approached by an elderly gentleman" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 21, folio 10). He photographed the stranger. Disciples in Lahore later identified the man in the photograph as "the deceased spiritual master Shaykh Wahid Bakhsh Sial Rabbani", and Rozehnal records that "the shaykh had been deceased for more than a year when the photograph was taken" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, pp. 21–22, folios 10–11). The story is the author's own, and his book presents it as its founding anecdote; it makes no claim for the tomb itself beyond its being the place where the meeting happened.

## Setting

The books that do not name the tomb speak of Uch Sharif, the town around it. What follows is about the town and its saints, not about Bibi Jawindi or her tomb.

Rozehnal places Uch "seventy miles southwest of Multan in Pakistan" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 210, folio 202). Annemarie Schimmel places 'Ucch' northeast of Multan, and writes that "Ucch was, for a while, the center of the Suhrawardiyya, thanks chiefly to the active and pious Jalaluddin Makhdum-i Jahaniyan" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354). Michel Boivin speaks of "one of the masters of the second Sohrawardiyya period, then based in Utch near Bahawalpur in Punjab" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 56, folio 43). Omar Kasmani's only words on the town name its first Bukhari saint, "Jalaluddin Surkhposh Bukhari (d. 1291) of Uchh", and add: "Their shrines are spread across southern Punjab in Pakistan" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 188, folio 171, ch. 1 n. 17). Shemeem Burney Abbas calls Uch Gilanian "the city of saints in Bahawalpur" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 107, folio not stated).

Rizvi records a foundation legend for the town, in a passage on Shaikh Safiu'd-Din Kaziruni. A shaikh is ordered "to mount a camel and travel in whatever direction the animal led him; he was then to remain where the camel finally halted"; the camel halted "in the middle of a desert", and "the Shaikh founded a town later called Uch" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 129, folio 111). Rizvi's own verdict on the tale is: "Although this story would appear mythical, it is typical of medieval tales relating to sufis" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 129, folio 111). He tells, too, of a flying contest with a yogi at Uch (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 129–130, folios 111–112), and writes that "all the stories dealing with three generations of these 'sufis in Uch recount conflicts with Hindu Yogis" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 304, folio 282). He writes that "The Baba found a lonely mosque in Uch" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 158, folio 140), and of one Sufi that "he went to Multan and Uch. At Uch it is possible that he was formally initiated into the Suhrawardiyya order as is suggested by local legends" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 337, folio 315). Carl Ernst and Bruce Lawrence place at Uch, in the well of its Hajj mosque, Farid ad-Din's 'inverted 40-day fast': "For 40 days they would suspend him from a branch of the tree that overhung the well every night. At daybreak they would pull him out" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 168, folio 156).

Uch was also the gate by which, in the books' accounts, the Qadiri order entered the subcontinent. Schimmel writes: "It was in Ucch that the first missionaries of the Qadiriyya settled in the late fifteenth century; from there this order spread into the Subcontinent" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354). Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori's *Hadiqat al-Awliya* writes that of this line Hazrat Sayyid Muhammad Ghaus Uchi Jili Gilani was the first to come to the Punjab, coming by way of Khurasan to Multan and settling at Uch; the date given for his arrival, 887 AH, is the editor's, citing Ali Asghar Gilani, and not the author's (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 40, folio 28). Rozehnal calls Shaykh Muhammad al-Husseini al-Jilani "the founder of the first Qadiriyya khanaqah at Uch", a native of Turkey who travelled to Khurasan and Multan "before settling with his family in Uch", and says of his son 'Abd al-Qadir Jilani Thani, "the famous Qadiri saint of Uch Sharif", that "there seems to be no extant archive of the saint, no textual record to document his life and legacy" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 259, folio 252; Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 210, folio 202). Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti's *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* writes that the four sons of Hazrat Muhammad Ghaus lie at rest at Uch Sharif, beside the tomb of Shah Muhammad Ghaus (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 527), and that the Sayyids of Uch are famous, their word being final on any disputed descent (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 526–527).

Khalid's own approach to the town gives its present. The road ran "through Bahawalpur, heading towards our destination at Uch Sharif", where "the cultures of Punjab and Sindh merge" and the language is Siraiki (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 276, folio 250). The older city stands on a mound, with "massive havelis" of "the current caretakers of the shrine, the Gilanis and the Makhdooms" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 276, folio 250). He writes that "The southern region of Punjab, where Uch Sharif lies, has more traces of religious and cultural syncretism compared to the northern and central region" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 277, folio 251). Khalid's book follows Guru Nanak, and in his reconstruction of Nanak's visit, "Uch Sharif was one of the most famous Muslim shrines in Southern Punjab and Nanak had arrived here only a day ago, travelling from Kartarpur" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 288, folio 262). Of Nanak's traces Iqbal Qaiser showed him a well hidden in a field, "abandoned and juice tetrapaks and wrappers were floating in the black water", and beside it a grassy mound: "This was the Gurdwara of Nanak … Now nothing remained" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 286, folio 260).

Abbas records a hereditary *qawwal* family that left Uch with a saint's household. "We came with Bulle Shah's family from Uch Sharif, and we have learned his poetry from our ancestors, who have always sung here", in the words of Muhammad Bakhsh at Kasur (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 112, folio not stated). She quotes the line "Thou art an inhabitant of Uch city", glossing the name, "[Uch literally means high/great]", and says in prose that Bulle Shah's mentor Inayat Shah "belonged to Uch city" (Abbas, *The Female Voice in Sufi Ritual*, p. 104, folio not stated).

Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, composed in the fortieth regnal year of Aurangzeb, calls the place the township of Uch (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 527), and in describing the suba of Thatta takes the bounds of Multan and Uch as one of its limits (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 529). In its pages armies pass through it again and again: Shihab al-Din took Uch on his first campaign and returned by Multan and Uch on his second (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 405); Mirza Pir Muhammad brought the fortresses of Uch and Multan into his possession (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 333); and Khawas Khan's pursuit went as far as Multan and Uch (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 285). It names no saint and no tomb at Uch in these passages.

## Devotion and Legacy

As the resting place of a holy woman of the revered Bukhari line, Bibi Jawindi’s tomb is honoured by pilgrims who come to Uch to visit the shrines of the sayyids, and it forms part of the sacred fabric of the town. But its fame today is above all as a monument — a breathtaking, wounded masterpiece that stands for the vanished splendour of medieval Uch and for the artistry of the tile-workers who adorned the shrines of the Punjab’s saints. It is, at once, a place of devotion, a heritage treasure, and an elegy in brick and glaze.

Neither book that names the tomb describes devotion at it. Khalid came to it as a visitor to the monuments and found its fallen half secured with iron and the tiles of the ruined shrines reused on the graves around it (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 281, folio 255; Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 285, folio not stated). Rozehnal records no *ʿurs* or custodian there (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 21, folio 10). For him the tomb is remembered as the place where his book began.

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **Whose kinswoman she was.** The site's account makes her a woman of the Jalali Bukhari line and, by tradition, a great-granddaughter of Makhdoom Jahaniyan Jahangasht. Khalid makes her "the great granddaughter of Syed Jalaludin Bokhari" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 285, folio not stated). Rozehnal makes her the wife of "Sayyid Jalal ad-Din Bukhari (1303–1383)" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 21, folio 10). Schimmel gives Jalaluddin Makhdum-i Jahaniyan's death as 1383 (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354), and Rizvi the birth of Saiyid Jalalu'd-Din Bukhari, Makhdum-i Jahaniyan, as 9 February 1308 (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 299, folio 277); Kasmani gives the death of Jalaluddin Surkhposh Bukhari as 1291 (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 188, folio 171, ch. 1 n. 17).
- **When the tomb was built.** The site's account gives around 1493, by tradition. The board at the site, as Khalid reports it, gives 1494 (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 285, folio not stated). Khalid calls the group of buildings on the mound "thirteenth and fourteenth century shrines" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 281, folio 255).
- **When the flood came.** The site's account places the collapse, by tradition, in a flood in the nineteenth century. Iqbal Qaiser, in Khalid's book, says: "There was a huge flood here in the sixteenth century and these buildings were destroyed at that time" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 281, folio 255).
- **Where the tomb stands.** The site's account sets it in the Bukhari quarter of the town. Khalid's shrines lie "on the outskirts, situated on top of a mound, which was being used as a graveyard" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 281, folio 255).
- **The tilework.** The site's account describes cobalt-blue, turquoise and white *kashi* tilework. Khalid describes "glazed Multani tiles, coloured in blue and white" and "a white dome on top" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 281, folio 255).
- **Her name.** The site's account writes Bibi Jawindi, as does Rozehnal (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 21, folio 10); Khalid writes Bibi Jiwindi (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 281, folio 255).
- **Where Uch lies.** Rozehnal sets the town on a hill overlooking the confluence of the Sutlej and Chenab (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 21, folio 10) and "seventy miles southwest of Multan" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 210, folio 202); Schimmel puts it 'northeast of Multan' (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354).
- **When the Qadiriyya came.** Schimmel has the first Qadiri missionaries settle in Ucch "in the late fifteenth century" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 385, folio 354), and elsewhere has the order introduced into the subcontinent "in the late fourteenth century" (Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, p. 278, folio 247). The editor of Lahori's book, citing Ali Asghar Gilani, has Sayyid Muhammad Ghaus enter Uch in 887 AH (Lahori, *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, p. 40, folio 28).
