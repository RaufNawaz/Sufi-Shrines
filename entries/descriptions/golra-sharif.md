## Overview

At Golra Sharif, at the foot of the Margalla Hills on the edge of Islamabad, stands the shrine of Hazrat Pir Sayyid Meher Ali Shah (1859–1937) — one of the most influential Chishti saints and scholars of modern South Asia. A master of both the inward path and the religious sciences, Meher Ali Shah is remembered as a great defender of orthodox Sunni belief, a renowned orator and author, and the founder of a living Sufi centre whose successors remain prominent to this day. His darbar is among the most visited shrines of the Pothohar region and the Islamabad-Rawalpindi area.

The books that name the place say little about its buildings, and much about the man and the institution he left. Carl W. Ernst and Bruce B. Lawrence, in their history of the Chishti order, write that "Another comparable shrine is at Golra Sharif, on the outskirts of present-day Islamabad, where Mihr 'Ali Shah established his prominence in the early twentieth century" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 125, folio 112). Robert Rozehnal refers his readers to a study of "Sayyid Mehr Ali Shah of Golra" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 249, folio 242). A German study of 1988, listed in the anthology *Embodying Charisma*, counts Golra Sharif among three Muslim pilgrimage places of northern Pakistan (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 35, folio 24; p. 169, folio 158). None of these books describes the tomb, gives a date for its building, names its custodians or gives the date of its *ʿurs*.

## The Life of the Saint

Meher Ali Shah was born in 1859 at Golra, into a Sayyid family of learning and sanctity descended from the Prophet. He received a thorough classical education in the Islamic sciences at the seminaries of the age and became a scholar of the first rank, while in the Sufi path he was initiated in the Chishti order, taking spiritual allegiance in the line of Khwaja Shamsuddin Sialvi of Sial Sharif. Combining deep mystical experience with formidable learning, he became a widely sought guide and teacher. He is especially remembered for his role in the religious controversies of his time — above all his prominent stand against the claims of Mirza Ghulam Ahmad of Qadian, which he opposed in debate and in writing, most famously in his work *Saif-e-Chishtiyai*. He died at Golra in 1937 and was buried there.

The books spell his name in several ways: Ernst and Lawrence write "Mihr 'Ali Shah" throughout, and "Pir Mehr Ali Shah" where they cite the title of a study of him (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 214, folio 203); Rozehnal writes "Mehr Ali Shah" and "Mehr 'Ali Shah" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 103, folio 94; p. 229, folio 221); and the anthology *Embodying Charisma* has "Pir Mehr ‘Ali Shah" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 159, folio 148).

Ernst and Lawrence confirm his teacher and add a second affiliation: "although Mihr 'Ali Shah's direct pir was Shams ad-Din Siyal (d. 1882), he was also affiliated with Muhammad 'Ali, one of whose disciples was Habib 'Ali Shah, who in turn was the master of Sufi Saheb (d. 1916)" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 124, folio 111). It is through this second line that they trace a branch of the order that reached South Africa. They give his death as 1937 (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 124, folio 111), and in describing a conflict over the succession at Taunsa they name him as the head of one of its two branches: the second branch of succession from Shaykh Sulayman went, they write, "the other to Mihr 'Ali Shah (d. 1937)" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 124, folio 111).

Rozehnal places him among the reformers of his century: "Other notable twentieth-century South Asian Sufi masters have pursued similar reformist agendas—the Chishti Nizami masters Sayyid Mehr Ali Shah (1856–1937) and Khwaja Hasan Nizami (1878–1955), for example, as well as the Naqshbandi activist, Pir Jama'at 'Ali Shah (1841–1951)" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 103, folio 94). In a note he names the same three men together, and for Meher Ali Shah writes: "On Sayyid Mehr Ali Shah of Golra, see especially Gilmartin, Empire and Islam, 58–59" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 249, folio 242). The three appear there as forerunners of the Chishti Sabiri masters' use of print.

## Politics and Disciples

Ernst and Lawrence find the fullest measure of the man in his public life. Of the controversy with Qadian they write that both he and Haydar 'Ali Shah opposed "the sectarian and messianic" Ahmadi movement, "though Mihr 'Ali Shah was said to be one of those most influential in marginalizing and stigmatizing its latter-day prophet, Ghulam Ahmad of Qadian" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 125, folio 112). They add: "At the same time, he was attracted to the Khilafat movement, which coalesced around the largely symbolic figure of the Ottoman caliph, and he permitted his disciples to support this movement" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 126, folio 113). Comparing Golra with Jalalpur Sharif, they conclude that "the two masters had different roles, with Mihr 'Ali Shah taking on the historical persona of a Sufi pir who was thoroughly engaged by both the regional and international politics of his time" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 125, folio 112).

Among his disciples was Khwaja Hasan Nizami. Ernst and Lawrence record that Meher Ali Shah "initiated Khwaja Hasan Nizami and made him a khalifa", and of Hasan Nizami they write: "At the age of 24, of his own will and believing he had a spiritual sign from Baba Farid, he became a murid of Mihr 'Ali Shah and eventually was appointed his successor (khalifa)" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 127, folio 114).

They also cite a modern study devoted to him, Munis Faruqui's unpublished term paper "The Chishti Revival and Its Response to the Impact of British Rule in Western Punjab: The Peculiar Case of Pir Mehr Ali Shah" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 214, folio 203).

## Learning, Devotion, and Poetry

Pir Meher Ali Shah united the roles of *shaykh*, scholar, and poet. He wrote in defence of Sunni doctrine and on the love of the Prophet, and he was himself a composer of devotional verse — his Punjabi and Persian *naat* in praise of the Prophet, cherished by his followers, remain among the loved poetry of the tradition. His teaching held firmly to the union of the Sharia and the Sufi path, insisting that mystical devotion must rest on sound belief and the example of the Prophet. He drew to himself a vast following, and the guidance he gave — sober, learned, and centred on the love of God and His Messenger — shaped the religious life of the region.

His Punjabi verse is sung by *qawwals* alongside that of the great Persian poets. Rozehnal writes: "Qawwals move fluidly between linguistic registers: from the Persian verses of Jami, Hafiz, and Rumi, to the Punjabi poetry of Mehr 'Ali Shah and Ghulam Farid, and the classical Hindi of Amir Khusrau, among others" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 229, folio 221). None of the books consulted prints a line of his poetry.

His memory is kept in homes as well as at the shrine. The anthology *Embodying Charisma* describes the wall of Mama Ji's living-room in Rawalpindi: "There are, for example, popular prints of the saints ‘Abdul Qadir Jilani (Bagdad), Pir Mehr ‘Ali Shah (Golra/Islamabad), and Baba Lal Shah (Sorasi/Murree)" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 159, folio 148).

## The Shrine and its Living Line

The shrine of Meher Ali Shah at Golra is a large and busy complex, kept by his descendants, who have continued as its sajjada-nashins and as noted religious figures — his successors, among them Pir Ghulam Mohyuddin (Babuji) and later keepers, maintaining the darbar and its teaching mission. The shrine holds the five daily prayers, gatherings of *naat* and qawwali, and the free kitchen, and its annual urs draws great crowds from across the Pothohar and beyond. As a centre of both pilgrimage and learning, Golra Sharif carries the saint’s legacy of devotion joined to scholarship.

The shrine and its kitchen have their own study. Ernst and Lawrence's bibliography lists Hafeezur Rehman Chaudhry's essay on 'The Shrine and Lunger of Golra Sharif', in *Pakistan: The Social Sciences' Perspective*, edited by Akbar S. Ahmed (Karachi: Oxford University Press, 1990), pp. 190–206 (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 227, folio 216). 'Lunger' is the book's own spelling of *langar* in that title. It is the only work in their European-language bibliography devoted to a single named Pakistani shrine other than Pakpattan.

Golra Sharif also holds manuscripts. In Ernst and Lawrence's bibliography of Persian sources, two works are recorded as unique single copies there: a biography of Hujwiri, entered as "Munzawi, XI, 956, no. 187 (1 MS Gulra)" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 236, folio 226), and, under Muhammad Chishti, the *Albab-i thulatha*, entered as "Monzavi, III, no. 2283 (1 MS Gulra Sharif)" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 243, folio 233). They are the only two holdings in that bibliography placed at a living Pakistani shrine rather than at a university, a state library or a private collection. The book does not say whether the copies belong to the shrine's own library, or who holds them. It spells the place "Gulra" and "Gulra Sharif" in its bibliography and index (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, pp. 236, 243, 247, folios 226, 233, not stated).

The anthology *Embodying Charisma* lists, twice, a German study by Harald Einzmann, 'Ziarat und Pir-e-Muridi: Golra Sharif, Nurpur Shahan und Pir Baba', published in his *Drei muslimische Wallfahrtsstätten in Nordpakistan* (Stuttgart: Franz Steiner Verlag, 1988) (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 35, folio 24; p. 169, folio 158). The volume's title, "three Muslim pilgrimage places in Northern Pakistan", sets Golra Sharif beside Nurpur Shahan and Pir Baba. The first of the two listings prints a comma between "Golra" and "Sharif", which read literally would make four places of the volume's three; the second has none. The study is listed in the anthology's bibliographies but not discussed in its text.

A further pointer stands apart from these. Richard M. Eaton, writing on Pakpattan, records a "Golra Sharif in Rawalpindi District" in the sajjada-nishin's list of shrines subordinate to Pakpattan (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 242, n. 12). He says nothing else about it and names no saint there, so that this is the same Golra rests on the place name alone.

## Setting

The books' remarks on Islamabad and its neighbourhood concern the city, not the shrine. Haroon Khalid writes that "Rawalpindi happens to be the twin city of Islamabad, the federal capital of the country" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 265, folio 239). *Embodying Charisma* speaks of "Pakistan's capital city, Islamabad", on whose outskirts stand the village of Nurpur Shahan and Barri Imam's shrine (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 151, folio 140), the Nurpur Shahan that Einzmann's study sets beside Golra. Rozehnal records Sufi life of another order in the city: "Since 1989, The Association of Spiritual Training—founded by Wahid Bakhsh and administered by a number of his senior disciples—has published an English language journal titled The Sufi Path. Based in Islamabad, it has produced fourteen issues to date" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 130, folio 121); and "Another female murid used to host a similar weekly halqa in Islamabad, but this practice has recently been discontinued" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 201, folio 193).

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **His year of birth.** The site's account gives 1859. Rozehnal gives "(1856–1937)" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 103, folio 94). Ernst and Lawrence give no year of birth. The site's account, Rozehnal and Ernst and Lawrence agree on 1937 for his death (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 124, folio 111).
- **Where Golra lies.** The site's account places it at the foot of the Margalla Hills on the edge of Islamabad. Ernst and Lawrence put it "on the outskirts of present-day Islamabad" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 125, folio 112), and *Embodying Charisma* writes "Golra/Islamabad" (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 159, folio 148). Eaton has "Golra Sharif in Rawalpindi District" (Eaton, *Essays on Islam and Indian History*, p. 242, n. 12).
- **How his order is named.** The site's account calls him a Chishti in the line of Khwaja Shamsuddin Sialvi of Sial Sharif, and Ernst and Lawrence name his direct pir as Shams ad-Din Siyal (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 124, folio 111); Rozehnal calls him one of "the Chishti Nizami masters" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 103, folio 94).
- **Whom he left after him.** The site's account names among his successors at the shrine Pir Ghulam Mohyuddin (Babuji) and later keepers drawn from his descendants. Ernst and Lawrence record that Khwaja Hasan Nizami "eventually was appointed his successor (khalifa)" (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 127, folio 114).

## Legacy

Pir Meher Ali Shah stands among the towering figures of modern Sunni Sufism in South Asia — a saint whose combination of mystical stature, scholarship, and public defence of orthodox belief gave him an authority felt far beyond his own region. Through his writings, his poetry in praise of the Prophet, and the living line of his successors at Golra, his influence endures, and his shrine at the edge of the capital remains one of the great centres of Chishti devotion in Pakistan.

The books bear out how far that authority reached: to the controversy with Qadian and the politics of the Khilafat movement (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 125, folio 112; p. 126, folio 113), to his disciple and *khalifa* Khwaja Hasan Nizami (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 127, folio 114), and, through a second line of affiliation, to a branch of the order traced as far as South Africa (Ernst and Lawrence, *Sufi Martyrs of Love*, p. 124, folio 111). His Punjabi verse lives on in the repertoire of the *qawwals* (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 229, folio 221), and his picture on the walls of devotees' homes (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 159, folio 148).
