## Overview

The Ram Mandir at Saidpur — also known as the Ram Kund Mandir — stands in the historic village of Saidpur, set in a fold of the Margalla Hills on the northern edge of Islamabad. A Hindu temple dedicated to *Lord Rama*, it is traditionally dated to the sixteenth century and attributed by some accounts to Raja Man Singh I, the Rajput general of the Mughal emperor Akbar.

None of the books consulted for this article describes the temple, and none mentions this Saidpur or its Ram Kund. What they offer concerns Rama himself. Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti's *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* traces the line of Raja Ramchandar and gives a list of its kings (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 795–796); translations of Bulleh Shah and of Waris Shah gloss the story of the *Ramayana* (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 200, folio 175; Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 4, folio 3, note 13); Jürgen Schaflechner follows Rama along the pilgrimage route to the goddess Hinglaj (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 119, folio not stated); Haroon Khalid writes of his festivals (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated); and the verse of Bulleh Shah and Madho Lal Hussein, and a song sung at Sehwan, carry his name into Sufi poetry (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 116, folio 91; Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 181, folio 181; Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 57, folio 40). That material is set out below as context about Rama and the epic, not as the history of this temple; the books' few passing words on Islamabad and the Margalla Hills are gathered under Setting.

## The Temple

Before Partition, Saidpur was a place of notable interfaith coexistence, home to Muslims, Hindus, and Sikhs together, its skyline holding a mosque, this Hindu temple, and — from the early twentieth century — a Sikh *gurdwara*. The books consulted add nothing to this account: none names the temple's builder, gives the year of its building, or records a custodian or an endowment.

After 1947 the temple ceased to function as a place of worship; it was used for a time as a girls' school, and in the 2000s the Capital Development Authority restored Saidpur as a heritage and arts village, conserving the temple's fabric while its idols were removed and its images painted over.

*Dawn*'s 2016 reporting adds that in 2006 the Capital Development Authority had the by-then-vacated building formally cleared and preserved as a tourist attraction, and that visitors have since documented restaurants operating within the complex serving meat dishes, a practice criticised by Hindu commentators as an affront to the site's sanctity.

The building survives as a protected monument and a popular destination for visitors to Islamabad, though the local Hindu community has not been permitted to resume regular rituals there — a source of continuing appeal by Hindu representatives. A 2020 report independently corroborated the temple's sixteenth-century dating and confirmed that regular Hindu worship remains barred there.

The books speak of Rama's images and temples elsewhere, never of this one. Haroon Khalid's *Walking with Nanak* describes tiles of 'Ram and Sita' at a temple at Manora, their faces wiped clean (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 229, folio not stated). His *A White Trail* finds "depictions of ... Ram and Sita" among the faded frescoes of the Ranjit Singh samadhi (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 209, folio not stated), and reports that the rationale of those who demolished the Babri Mosque "was that the mosque had been built ... on the site of a Hindu Temple, birth site of the mythological Hindu King, Ram" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 21, folio not stated).

## Worship and Festivals

According to *Dawn*'s 2016 reporting, local memory also ties the site to the adjoining Ram Kund pond, where — per the 1893-94 Rawalpindi Gazetteer as cited by that report — an annual fair once drew Hindus from across the district.

The books consulted describe no worship at Saidpur and give no date for the fair. What they record of the festivals of Rama is said of those festivals in general, and is given here as context.

The annotator of Charles Frederick Usborne's translation of Waris Shah writes that the victory over Ravana "is celebrated every year at the Hindu festival of the Dushera when Ravana's effigy is ceremonially burnt" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 4, folio 3, note 13). In Amog Lila's account of Navratri, as Haroon Khalid's *A White Trail* gives it, Sita prayed for nine nights while "Ravan and Rama (Sita's husband)" fought; "On the tenth day, the goddess heard her plea and Ram was able to kill Ravana and thus won the war. The burning of Dushera (on the tenth day) is a remembrance of that victory" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 40, folio not stated). Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti, too, speaks of Dussehra as a commemoration, in a passage whose reading is uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 794).

In *In Search of Shiva*, Khalid quotes William Henry Sleeman's *Rambles and Recollections of an Indian Official*: "When Ram set out with his army for Ceylon, he is supposed to have worshipped the little tree called 'cheonkul', which stood near his capital of Ajodhya". Sleeman says he saw "a procession of more than seventy thousands persons" attend their prince to worship the tree "on the festival of the Dashara" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 9, ch. 3, folio not stated).

The same book argues that Eid Milad-Un-Nabi "was originally inspired by a Hindu festival called Ram Navami. The festival celebrates the birth of Ram, a popular deity"; Khalid writes that "Streets are lit and the entire community takes part", and that "Hindus have been celebrating Ram Navami for centuries" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated).

## Rama and the Legend of the Site

The site's sanctity is bound to the belief that Rama, with Sita and Lakshmana, passed through this country during the years of exile recounted in the *Ramayana*: the natural springs of the village, fed by the hill streams, were revered as sacred pools, and four of them are traditionally named for Rama, Sita, Lakshmana, and Hanuman.

None of the books consulted tells this legend, and none sets Rama, Sita, Lakshmana or Hanuman at Saidpur. What they say of the four is said of the epic at large.

Christopher Shackle, glossing the names in his translation of Bulleh Shah, defines "Ram (Skt. Rāma) Hindu name of God, the divine hero of the Ramayana who killed Ravan." (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 200, folio 175).

Sita he glosses separately: "sita (Sītā) the wife of Ram." (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 200, folio 175). Chishti makes her the daughter of Raja Janak (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 796–797) and tells of her banishment, her *banwas*, which the book explains as exile from the land (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 794). In Khalid's *A White Trail*, Sita bore "the twin sons of Ram, Luv and Kush" at Valmiki's ashram "after she had been banished by Ram", and she is "the perfect woman, the wife of Ram" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 71, folio not stated).

Lakshmana appears in the books as Lachhman in Shackle and, in its Urdu spelling, in Chishti, as Lachman in Robert Rozehnal and as Laxman in Michel Boivin (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 155, folio 130; Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 792; Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 63, folio 53; Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 107, folio 94). Shackle has Ravan's capital of Lanka "under attack by Ram and Lachhman" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 155, folio 130).

Hanuman, in *A White Trail*, "was a follower of Ram and played a central role in the battle between Ram and Ravana" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 64, folio not stated). Taufiq Rafat's selection of Bulleh Shah speaks of "the Rama-Sita story of the 'Ramayana' in which Ravana and Hanuman figure as two poles of mystical knowledge." (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 40, folio 20).

Of Ravana, Shackle writes "ravan (Skt. Rāvaṇa) the ten-headed demon king of Lanka who abducted Ram's wife, Sita." (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 200, folio 175), and of his realm "Lanka the kingdom of Ravan." (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 199, folio 174). The annotator of Usborne's Waris Shah explains that "Ravana is the legendary king of Ceylon, who figures as the chief adversary of Rama in the Ramayana epic"; Ravana carried off Sita, Rama's wife, "whose rescue is the main theme of the poem", and was killed in battle by Rama (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 4, folio 3, note 13).

## The Line of Raja Ramchandar

Maulvi Nur Ahmad Chishti's *Tahqiqat-e Chishti* writes of Rama as Raja Ramchandar, and itself identifies Raja Ramchandar with the Rama of the Ramayana (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 797). Like the other books, it says nothing of the Saidpur temple.

The line, in Chishti's account, runs from Ichhwak of Ajudhiyapuri, the reading of that name uncertain in the printed text, to Dasrath, whose four sons were Ramchandar, Lachhman, Bharat and Satrughan, the last name uncertain in the printed text; the history, he says, is in the book of the Ramayan (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 792–794). Lachhman was Raja Ramchandar's own full brother, and of the Suraj Bansi (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 792). Of Chandrat the book says that it was the particular seat of Lachhman Ji, brother of Raja Ramchandar, the last name uncertain in the printed text (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 791).

By the belief of the Hindus, Chishti writes, Ramchandar was an *avatar* in the Treta age, which the book spells Tarpata (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 794). Raja Ramchandar is to him the eye and lamp of the Suraj Bansi family, and his account of the epic runs through the *ban bas*, the exile in the forest, Ravan, whom he calls Raja of Sangaldip, and the bridge from the Deccan (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 796–797).

A list of kings follows (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 795–796). Ramchandar's two sons, it says, were Kushu and Lohu (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 795), and the rule remained among Ramchandar's descendants for a full five hundred years (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 796). From Ichhwak to Ramchandar it counts fifty-seven holders of the *gaddi*, the reading of the number uncertain in the printed text, and it names the Treta age, the book's *Tarpata jug* (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 796). At an earlier place the book says that Raja Ram Chand had three sons, the reading uncertain at three words (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 661).

On the dating, Chishti reports what English historians say. Ichhwak came, by their reckoning, two thousand or two thousand two hundred years before the Christian era (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 796). Of Ramchandar he writes that he lived twelve hundred years before the Christian era, because a Mr Bentley, if the reading is sound, studied the horoscope by a source whose printed name, perhaps Dal Mika (Valmiki), cannot be read with certainty, and placed it some nine hundred and sixty-one years before, the figure uncertain at both ends in the printed text; the two figures stand side by side as printed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 796–797).

The English historians, Chishti reports, hold that the saying that Rama was a devta and fought the rakhshas, is poetic: brave rajas were called devtas, and their enemies rakhshas (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 797).

Sujan Rai Bhandari's Persian *Khulasat al-Tawarikh* is briefer still. It names a Raja Ram Chand, whom it does not identify further, only as the father of Lav, to whom the founding of Lahore is attributed (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525). Among the books its author read it lists a translation of the Ramayan, which contains the life of Sri Ram Chand and is counted among the famous books, rendered from Sanskrit into Persian by the group already named, whose names the text does not repeat there (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 583). Neither passage names a site.

## Rama on the Road to Hinglaj

Jürgen Schaflechner's study of the Hindu temple of Hinglaj Devi never connects Rama to Saidpur, to Islamabad or to any temple of his own, but Rama runs through its account of the pilgrimage to the goddess: "no figure was ever as present in the representations of the traditional pilgrimage to Hinglaj as Rama, the seventh incarnation of the God Vishnu." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 119, folio not stated). What follows is told of that pilgrimage, not of Saidpur.

The legend, as Schaflechner records it, tells of Rama's *brahm hatyā* and of the sentence of pilgrimage (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 120, folio not stated). He is defeated by the Goddess's army at the Ungakhera Bherum Luk pass and fights a twelve-year battle (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 120–121, folios not stated); a condition is set that he come as a simple pilgrim from Ram Bagh (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 121, folio not stated); the Devi makes a promise to his excluded men (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 121, folio not stated); Hanuman, Lakshmana and Sita work miracles of water (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 121, folio not stated); and Rama carves a sun and a moon on the mountain opposite the temple (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 121, 132, 142, 257, folios not stated).

His ninety-six Brahmins, Schaflechner writes, accepted Hinglaj as their clan Goddess (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 76, folio not stated). The Saraswat Brahmins have a *ṭīkā* which, Schaflechner writes, citing Burton (1851), "according to legend—they had gotten from Ramacandra. Rama was in need of a priest and since he could not find any in Balochistan he simply applied a ṭīkā on a mlecch's (Hin. barbarian) forehead." (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 100, folio not stated). Lalu Jasraj is "the aguā of Ramacandra's party and the first Kapri" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 181, folio not stated).

The *pūjā* places named for him on the route include "the resting place of Rama (Sin. rām jharoku)" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 256, folio not stated), and "Khari Nadi, where people take a bath in the ocean and worship Rama", with "the Rama pūjā" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, pp. 203, 204, folios not stated). Schaflechner writes, too, of "the mytho-historic journey of Rama and his entourage", now largely destroyed, he says, by Karachi's growth (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 322, folio not stated).

## Rama in Verse

None of the verse in the books consulted speaks of Saidpur. What they carry is verse in which Rama's name or story appears, quoted here in the books' own English.

Bulleh Shah's lyrics, in Christopher Shackle's translation, appeal to Ram:

*When I realized this, I appealed to Ram: you and I are one, there is no one else in this house in the city of love* (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 116, folio 91)

Elsewhere in the lyrics:

*Sometimes you utter appeals to Ram, sometimes you put the tilak on your forehead.* (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 59, folio 34)

The epic enters the lyrics too. One line says to its addressee, 'you had Lanka plundered by Ram' (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 141, folio 116), and a passage recalls Kumbhakaran, Ravan and Lachhman:

*When Kumbhakaran goes back the way he came, the mystery of Lanka is created. Now that Ravan the ten-headed is destroyed and Lachhman remains, the unstruck music sounds.* (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 31, folio 6)

Shackle explains one such reference: "This reference to the story of the Rāmāyaṇa describes a reversal of the arrival of Kumbhakaran to assist his brother Ravan, when his capital of Lanka was under attack by Ram and Lachhman." (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 155, folio 130). Another of his notes reads "The capital of Ravan's kingdom, attacked by Ram." (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 168, folio 143).

Waris Shah's romance of Hir and Ranjha, in Charles Frederick Usborne's English, invokes the epic in a single question:

*Did not a woman kill Ravana?* (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 3, folio 2)

Its annotator glosses the line with the story of Ravana and Rama (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 4, folio 3, note 13). Later the epic returns as a courtroom analogy, "This swindler took her away as Ravan ran away with Sita." (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 53, folio 52), which the annotator reads as a tactic: "This reference to the Ravan-Sita episode, which is the subject of the 'Ramayana, is intended to curry favour with the Raja, who is a Hindu." (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 54, folio 53).

In *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, Naveed Alam's translation of Madho Lal Hussein, Ram is one of the two or three names the poet uses in place of Allah, and he uses it often: "If not Sain, then it is Rab, Maula, Ram. Actually, he uses Ram quite often (see kafis 68, 80, 81, 89)" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 181, folio 181); "Mostly Sain, Ram, Rab and Maula have been used" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, pp. 185–186, folios 185–186). In the verse it is the name to be recited and sung:

*Time to invoke, girl, let's rise and praise Ram* (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 28, folio 28, kafi 7)

*What's the joy of life / without the constant evocation of Ram?* (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 160, folio 160)

Other lines run "Praise Ram, I pranced all the way to the groom" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 90, folio 90), "Sometimes rise and recite Ram in the morning" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 103, folio 103), "You lose if you don't sing of Ram" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 104, folio 104) and "Come, girl, let's praise Ram" (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 112, folio 112). One kafi addresses him directly, and so does its Variation:

*The whole world begs your favours, O Ram* — and, in the Variation, *You are the great giver, O Rama* (Madho Lal Hussein, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, p. 172, folio 172)

Taufiq Rafat's selection of Bulleh Shah sets the name Ram beside the name Allah in a two-line poem headed NAMES, an argument about divine labels. The reading of the printed text is uncertain at two points, and the readings proposed there stand in square brackets:

*Having understood, why heed the storm over such. [OCR?] [such] names as Allah.and [OCR?] [Allah and] Ram.* (Rafat, *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, p. 147, folio 127)

Mai Dhadli's Urdu song at a fakir dwelling in Sehwan, in the English of Omar Kasmani's *Queer Companions*, runs:

*Muhammad in one place, Ali in another, and yet elsewhere, Qalandar is Ram.* (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, p. 57, folio 40)

Kasmani adds that the saint's memory is said to span "Muhammad, Ali, or Ram" (Kasmani, *Queer Companions*, pp. 57–58, folios 40–41).

## Rama Among the Sufis

Several of the books trace Rama's name through Sufi teaching and Hindu devotion at large; none of them speaks of this temple.

Saiyid Athar Abbas Rizvi writes of the deity "adored in the form of two of his ten incarnations, Rama and Krishna" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 376, folio 354), and of "devotion to the incarnation of Vishnu in the form of Rama, and of his consort Sita" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 379, folio 357). He notes that "A Hindi scholar identified Pitambar Pir with the Hindu god, Rama", and sets Kabir's 'Nirguna Rama' against 'Ram Chandar' (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 398, 401; folios 374, 377). He records the view that "Ram and Rahim were the same Being" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 412, folio 388), and counts Rama among the "traditional terms used by Hindus and Muslims to invoke God, such as Allah, Khuda, Sahib, Hari and Rama"; in Guru Nanak's usage, as Rizvi presents it, Hari and Rama are names for a single formless God, not the deities of cult sites (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 415, folio 391).

Michel Boivin writes that "Sachal mentions different Hindu gods, such as Ram, Hanuman, Sita and especially Krishna", and that "Sachal also refers to this episode of the Ramayana", in a passage on Diwali and Ram's return to Ayodhya (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 106, folio 93). "Qutub Ali Shah gives the names of Laxman and Sita, Ram and Krishna" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 107, folio 94), and, citing Paniker, Boivin reports that "Murad stated that the jogis are fascinated by Rama" (Boivin, *Hindu Sufis of South Asia*, p. 108, folio 95).

Robert Rozehnal, reporting the lectures of the Sufi shaykh Zauqi Shah, writes: "In certain lectures the shaykh describes non-Muslim spiritual luminaries—Krishna, Rama, Sita, Lachman, and Guru Nanak—as believers in the one true God and religious leaders with 'Divine connection' (nisbat)" (Rozehnal, *Islamic Sufism Unbound*, p. 63, folio 53).

## Setting

The books speak of Islamabad and of the Margalla Hills only in passing, and never of Saidpur. Werbner and Basu's *Embodying Charisma* names "Pakistan's capital city, Islamabad", on whose outskirts stand the village of Nurpur Shahan and Barri Imam's shrine (Werbner and Basu, *Embodying Charisma*, p. 151, folio 140). Haroon Khalid writes that "Rawalpindi happens to be the twin city of Islamabad, the federal capital of the country" (Khalid, *Walking with Nanak*, p. 265, folio 239).

In *In Search of Shiva*, the travel writer Salman Rashid tells Khalid, "I have heard of a bodhi tree in Islamabad and one near Jhelum" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 9, ch. 3, folio not stated). Khalid places Taxila's Dharmarajika "in the heart of Margalla hills, where the federal capital of Pakistan is", and describes it as "a stupa around which are the remains of a monastery and giant sculptures of Lord Buddha and Bodhisattva, dating back to the third century BCE", "believed to have been constructed by the great Mauryan emperor, Ashoka" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 11, ch. 5, folio not stated). What he says there is said of Taxila, not of Saidpur.

## Where the Sources Disagree

- **Who Rama was.** The site's account calls him *Lord Rama*. Shackle has "Hindu name of God, the divine hero of the Ramayana who killed Ravan" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 200, folio 175); Schaflechner, "the seventh incarnation of the God Vishnu" (Schaflechner, *Hinglaj Devi*, p. 119, folio not stated); Rizvi, "the incarnation of Vishnu in the form of Rama" (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, p. 379, folio 357), while he sets Kabir's 'Nirguna Rama' against 'Ram Chandar' (Rizvi, *A History of Sufism in India*, pp. 398, 401; folios 374, 377); Khalid, "a popular deity" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 13, ch. 7, folio not stated). Chishti calls him Raja Ramchandar, an *avatar* by the belief of the Hindus (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 794), and reports the English historians' view that to call Rama a devta who fought the rakhshas is poetic, brave rajas having been called devtas (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 797).
- **When Rama lived.** Chishti gives twelve hundred years before the Christian era, and in the same passage the figure of nine hundred and sixty-one years before, uncertain at both ends, from the reading of the horoscope he reports; the two stand as printed (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 796–797). For Ichhwak, at the head of the line, the English historians he cites give two thousand or two thousand two hundred years before the Christian era (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 796).
- **Rama's sons.** Chishti says in one place that Raja Ram Chand had three sons, the reading uncertain (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 661), and in another names two sons, Kushu and Lohu (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, p. 795). Khalid speaks of "the twin sons of Ram, Luv and Kush" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 71, folio not stated). Bhandari names a Raja Ram Chand, whom he does not identify further, as the father of Lav (Bhandari, *Khulasat al-Tawarikh*, p. 525).
- **Ravana's kingdom.** Shackle makes Ravan "the ten-headed demon king of Lanka" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 200, folio 175). Usborne's annotator calls Ravana "the legendary king of Ceylon" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 4, folio 3, note 13), and Sleeman, as Khalid quotes him, has Ram set out "with his army for Ceylon" (Khalid, *In Search of Shiva*, p. 9, ch. 3, folio not stated). Chishti calls Ravan the Raja of Sangaldip (Chishti, *Tahqiqat-e Chishti*, pp. 796–797).
- **Who killed Ravana.** Waris Shah's poem, in Usborne's English, asks "Did not a woman kill Ravana?" (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 3, folio 2); its annotator writes that Ravana was killed in battle by Rama (Waris Shah, *Hir and Ranjha*, p. 4, folio 3, note 13). Shackle's Ram is the hero "who killed Ravan" (Shackle, *Sufi Lyrics*, p. 200, folio 175). In Amog Lila's account, as Khalid gives it, "the goddess heard her plea and Ram was able to kill Ravana" (Khalid, *A White Trail*, p. 40, folio not stated).
