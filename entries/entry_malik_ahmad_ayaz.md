# Darbar Malik Ahmad Ayaz

## Site and setting

Darbar Malik Ahmad Ayaz is an active Muslim shrine in the name of Malik Ahmad Ayaz, whom the survey identifies as "Slave of Mehmood Gaznavi - Governor Lahore"; the account given to the surveyor states that his tomb was built at this place after his death. The survey records the site's religion as Islam and its affiliation as "Ahl e Sunnat - Ghaznavi silsila" (*silsila*, a chain of spiritual transmission), reproduced as recorded.

The shrine stands in Shah Alam Market, which the survey describes as the largest market in Asia and reports as extremely crowded; the account given to the surveyor places the *mazār* (tomb, mausoleum) near Darbar Ali Hajveri Ganj Bakhsh. The site is small, and the surrounding congestion is reported to prevent many people from reaching it, gradually reducing both the figure's fame and the enthusiasm of visitors. Residents of this densely populated neighbourhood are largely unaware of the life of the figure buried there — a figure about whom, the survey states, Allama Iqbal, Kaniya Lal and other writers have written books.

## The account of Malik Ahmad Ayaz

The life narrated to the surveyor is long, and is given here in the account's own terms.

The survey relates that Malik Ayaz Ahmad was educated in Georgia in 993 AD, and that when "the infidels attacked your country" he was enslaved and taken for sale to the Baghdad slave market — the account notes that slave dealing was then the norm. Too weak to attract buyers, he was passed over until Mahmud Ghaznavi came through and asked his price. The minister questioned the purchase; the king answered that what he saw in the boy the minister could not see. The price asked was ten thousand dinars, greed having been raised in the seller's heart by the king's words; Mahmud paid it and enrolled him among his soldiers.

Tradition holds that the relation of master and slave turned into love, and that Ayaz spent his whole life in devotion to Mahmud. The account preserves the story of the melon: the king, feeding him a melon cut by his own hand, tasted it himself, found it bitter, and asked why Ayaz had said nothing; Ayaz replied that having had sweetness from his master's hand all his life, he had no cause to complain of one bitter piece.

The account states that Mahmud fought "almost 17 battles with Hindus", in which Ayaz "played the role of the mastermind", and that his faith was so firm that "he did not fall for the infidels". In one such story the king, attacking at the "Onkay temple", found the idol suspended in mid-air; Ayaz reasoned that a magnet in the temple held the clay figure up, and that demolishing the walls would bring it down of itself — which, the account says, is what happened, to the king's satisfaction.

The king's family and ministers, the survey relates, mocked the closeness between the two; when the minister tested them by summoning everyone at midnight to eat, Ayaz alone appeared fully prepared, the others arriving late or intoxicated and asleep. Mahmud made him his minister, and he continued always to regard himself as a slave, each night setting aside his costly clothes and taking out the old garments in which he had been brought for sale, so as to remember his past, sit alone and please his God. The last ten years of his life, tradition holds, were largely given to the remembrance of God (*dhikr*).

In Ayaz's last days, the account continues, Mahmud wished to hand his own place to him; Ayaz declined, asking that it go instead to one of his two sons, Saad, and the king relinquished his power. When Ayaz governed Lahore the city had been ruined by war, its systems of livelihood, food, trade and wages broken, and by his intelligence he gave it shape again and set it on its own feet. He fell suddenly ill and died; it is said he was poisoned out of resentment at the love shown him, and in some places it is said he died a natural death. The account gives his death as 8 August 1041, and states that his mausoleum was then built in Shah Alam Market.

## Memory, example and spiritual significance

Asked what should be remembered, the survey names the bond between Mahmud Ghaznavi and Malik Ahmad Ayaz — in its own words, "the love and romance" of king and slave — a slave sacrificing his life for his king, and loyalty so complete that he could answer for it before God on the Day of Judgement; poets and travellers of that era and of the present, it states, have written of these qualities. It adds that he is remembered as the person who gave Lahore a new shape, and known still because he "made a new map of Lahore".

On his teachings the survey is brief: he was generous, intelligent, loyal, hasty, daring, brave, kind and honest, a soldier and also a good and devoted person, who set the example of these qualities in his own life, and people were motivated by seeing it. The place is held spiritually important, the survey states, because he fought alongside the king, served the people, protected their lives and property and instilled the spirit of Islam in them; because after Mahmud Ghaznavi's death he left his residence; and because it was his habit to spend nights in the remembrance of God.

## Formation and later history of the site

The account given to the surveyor states that the tomb was built at this very place after the death of Malik Ayaz, that the Ghaznavi Sultanate built the Darbar, and that it was later maintained in good condition by "Raja Maharaja". The account then relates a period of loss: the water of the Darbar was polluted, and — in wording the transcriber flags as unclear — "the area of crop was stolen for the special place of your Darbar which was spoiled by Sikh Raja Ranjit Singh", after which only the present small plot remained. The survey elsewhere states that some of the site's space was lost during the Sikh era and some to the surrounding market.

## Observances, attendance and custodianship

A mosque named after the figure stands in the vicinity of the Darbar, where the five daily prayers are offered. Visitors come from distant places; the survey records that non-Muslims also visit and that some have made documentaries about the figure, his stories appearing in books. *Diyas* (oil lamps) and *prasād* (consecrated food) are offered daily, in the survey's terms.

The *ʿurs* (death anniversary observance) is held annually on 12, 13 and 14 Zil Hajj, its activities including the lighting of lamps, the changing of sheets, bathing, and the eating of food at the site. Attendance is given as 700 to 800 people daily and 40,000 to 50,000 at the annual *ʿurs*.

The shrine is under the supervision of the Department of Auqaf (Government Mehkama Auqaf), given consistently in the survey as both the present custodian and the body responsible for the site's current condition.

### Reasoning on category and figure_type

**category = Muslim Shrine.** The subject is not presented as a Sufi shaykh: the survey describes a slave, soldier, minister and governor, gives no *pīr*–*murīd* lineage, no initiation, no discipleship, and no teaching corpus (the "teachings" answer lists personal virtues, not doctrine). That would point toward Secular / Memorial. But the survey documents unambiguous devotional practice at the site: an annual three-day *ʿurs* drawing 40,000–50,000 people, with lamps, the changing of sheets, bathing and communal eating; 700–800 daily visitors described as coming "for spiritual purposes"; daily offerings of *diyas* and *prasād*; a mosque named for the figure with the five daily prayers; and an explicit statement that people hold the place "special and divine". Auqaf custodianship also places it in the shrine-administration system rather than among secular monuments. Function decides: the site operates as a shrine, so Muslim Shrine is correct.

**figure_type = Historical person.** The survey's own characterisation is military and administrative — slave, soldier, "mastermind" of battles, minister, governor of Lahore who rebuilt the city. Ascetic and devotional traits are reported (nights in remembrance of God, the last ten years given to *dhikr*, the retained slave's garments), but the account never calls him a saint or places him in a Sufi lineage of teachers, and the "Ghaznavi silsila" given in Q5 names a dynasty rather than an order of transmission. "Historical person" records what the survey supports; "Sufi saint" would import a claim the survey does not make.

## qa_note

Items below are for editorial decision and are deliberately excluded from the public prose.

1. **"8 August 1041" — what it refers to.** The founding-year answer (Q6) is identical to the death date in the life answer (Q9). The survey supplies no construction date of its own: the history answer (Q8) says only that the tomb was built "after the death of Malik Ayaz" by the Ghaznavi Sultanate. The founding field is therefore a death date, not a foundation date, and `year_built` should not be treated as an attested building date.

2. **"8 August 1041" — Hijri or Gregorian? Not determinable from the survey.** The survey attaches no era marker to 1041. Two internal cues pull toward a Gregorian reading — the other absolute date in the same answer is marked "993 AD", and the day-month form "8 August" is Gregorian, whereas the survey uses a Hijri month name where it intends Hijri ("12th, 13th and 14th Zil Hajj"). But no statement in the survey establishes it, and 1041 AH and 1041 CE are roughly six centuries apart. **The archive should not choose on the survey's authority.** Flagged for a documentary decision; also note that a Gregorian reading places the death 48 years after the "993 AD" education, which is internally coherent, while a Hijri reading is not reconcilable with "993 AD" at all. This is recorded as an internal tension, not a correction.

3. **Location: "this village" versus Shah Alam Market.** Q8 states that "only this small place remained in your share in this village", while Q9 and Q16 place the shrine in Shah Alam Market, described as the largest market in Asia with "overwhelming rush". A village and a dense metropolitan bazaar are not compatible descriptions of one setting. Unresolved; both are reported.

4. **No city or province is stated for the shrine.** Lahore appears in the survey only as the city the figure governed and rebuilt. The archive should not silently supply a city, district or province for the site.

5. **"Raja Maharaja" versus Ranjit Singh.** Q8 says the Darbar "was later maintained in good condition by Raja Maharaja" and then that the site was "spoiled by Sikh Raja Ranjit Singh". Whether "Raja Maharaja" is a separate unnamed ruler, a generic term, or the same figure later blamed for the damage is not determinable. If it is the same figure, the answer credits and blames one party in consecutive clauses.

6. **Garbled stretch at the zero-width-space location.** The transcriber records two zero-width-space characters between "the area of" and "crop" in Q8. The surrounding clause reads, as written: "the area of crop was stolen for the special place of your Darbar which was spoiled by Sikh Raja Ranjit Singh". The intended meaning — land seizure, crop revenue, endowment loss, or something else — cannot be recovered and has not been guessed at.

7. **Other unclear or garbled passages, quoted as-is and not interpreted.** Q11: "it was the brave birthday of Allah who along with the king fought a war with many infidels" (the word rendered "birthday" is not recoverable). Q9: "Mgr Malik Ayaz became very surprised"; "the people of your IRD guard told Ghaznavi to accept him"; "I demonstrated the cruelty of the king and the slave by staring at the love, slavery and rule of the ruler and set a prime example that his secrets are cruel"; "After his death, he considered everything of his to be his king Mehmood Ghaznavi" (referent of "his death" ambiguous). Q10: "a king wanting to fill his slave with the people of his family"; "all these sayings and stories of his and many other races will be remembered in the table of Islam". Q9 also shifts into first person in the midnight-test passage ("I said that you people did not consider Malik Ahmad Ayaz good"), apparently voicing the king; the speaker is not marked. The temple in Q9 is given as the "Onkay temple", a spelling not otherwise identifiable from the survey.

8. **Order of deaths is contradictory.** Q9 places the transfer of power at the end of *Ayaz's* life ("In his last days, Mahmood Ghaznavi wanted to give his place to Malik Ayaz"), after which Ayaz governs Lahore and dies — implying Mahmud outlived him or was still reigning. Q11 states that "Malik Ahmad Ayaz left his residence after the death of Mahmood Ghaznavi", implying the reverse sequence. Not reconcilable as recorded.

9. **Name form varies.** "Malik Ahmad Ayaz" (Q1, Q7, Q10), "Malik Ayaz Ahmad" (opening of Q9), "Malik Ayaz" (Q8 and later in Q9). Headword taken from Q1.

10. **Cause of death given two ways within one answer.** Q9 reports both that "Malik Ayaz was poisoned" out of resentment, and that "in some places it is said that he died a natural death". The survey itself presents these as alternatives; retained as such.

11. **Cross-tradition vocabulary at a Muslim shrine.** Q12 records that "diyas and prasad are also given on a daily basis" — both terms belonging to Hindu ritual vocabulary — and Q13 describes activities including "eating food at the temple", using "temple" for the site. Whether the enumerator is describing genuinely shared or syncretic practice, or is using loose vocabulary for lamp-lighting and food distribution (*langar*), is not determinable. The archive should decide whether to gloss, retain, or query these terms; they have been retained in the prose in the survey's own words. Note also that "bathing" in Q13 is unspecified — visitors' ablution, ritual washing of the grave, or something else.

12. **"Ghaznavi silsila" (Q5).** Given in the *silsila* field, but Ghaznavi is a dynastic rather than an initiatic designation, and the account describes political and military service, not spiritual affiliation. Recorded verbatim; needs an editorial ruling on whether it belongs in the `silsila` field at all.

13. **Sensitive content — reported in the account's own terms, flagged for editorial decision.**
    - *Conquest and religious warfare.* The account states that Mahmud Ghaznavi "fought almost 17 battles with Hindus" with Malik Ayaz as "the mastermind", and repeatedly uses "infidels" for non-Muslims, including for those said to have attacked the figure's homeland and enslaved him.
    - *Temple destruction.* The "Onkay temple" story has the figure advising the demolition of the temple walls to bring down the idol, presented in the account as an act of intelligence rewarded by the king.
    - *Religious change.* Q11 credits the figure with having "instilled the spirit of Islam in the people".
    - *Named community and named ruler in a property claim.* Q8 and Q16 attribute the loss of shrine land to "the Sikh era" and specifically to "Sikh Raja Ranjit Singh". Q16 attributes further loss to the surrounding market.
    - *Slavery.* The narrative centres on enslavement and sale, with the account observing that "at that time, selling and buying slaves was the norm".
    - *Framing of the central relationship.* Q10 characterises the bond between Mahmud Ghaznavi and Malik Ahmad Ayaz as "love and romance". Reported as given; presentation needs an editorial decision.
    - *Property/custody.* Present custodianship by the Department of Auqaf, and the reported reduction of the site's ground, are recorded as institutional facts; any land-status claim beyond the survey's wording should not be inferred.

14. **Unanswered questions.** Q20 (books or pamphlets) and Q21 (what a first-time visitor should understand) are blank, as are the trailing export columns `Status` and `Column 1`. Q16 names Allama Iqbal and "Kaniya Lal" as authors who wrote about the figure, but no titles are given and no work has been cited; the bibliography accordingly lists the field survey only.
