# Darbar Hazrat Shah Gohar Peer

## Overview

Darbar Hazrat Shah Gohar Peer is a Muslim shrine in Lahore, built over the grave of the
figure the survey names as Hazrat Sufi Peer Syed Gohar Ali Shah, known to devotees as Shah
Gohar Peer. The survey's answer on sect or *silsila* (Sufi order or lineage) reads "Ahl e
Sunnat - Ismaili Sadaat Uch Shareef", with a *shajra e nasab* (genealogy) traced to Hazrat
Imam Hussain. A mosque named after the saint stands directly in front of it, and a
residential area and graveyard surround the site. The surveyor records that its dome is
the largest of any shrine in Lahore, and that he is the only member of his family whose
shrine is in Lahore, the rest lying at Uch Sharif.

## The Saint

According to the account given to the surveyor, Shah Gohar Peer — given name Syed Ali
Gohar — was born on 11 Rabīʿ al-Sānī 729 AH at Uch Sharif. His father, Syed Ul Hassan
Kabeer, is described as a pious Sufi and a scholar of *dīn* (religion) whose disciples
included Shia, Sunni and people of other sects. He took his education and all his
spiritual knowledge from his father, and the oath of allegiance at his hand; seeing his
rising spirituality, his father bestowed *khilāfat* (the mantle of spiritual succession)
upon him and sent him out to bring non-Muslims into Islam. The family occupation, the
account says, was inviting people to Islam, and he was known by the title "Kuffar Shikan"
— the destroyer of disbelief.

He is said to have preached first in Kashmir. Travelling towards Uch Sharif he arranged a
stay at a place in Lahore the survey says is today known as Mahmood Booti, but within days
changed his mind and camped permanently at the site of the present shrine. The account
presents him as a solitude-loving worshipper, absorbed in *dhikr* (remembrance of God) and
possessed of *bāṭin* (esoteric) knowledge, outwardly a beautiful dervish, who did not
display his spiritual standing and moved constantly between locations; other saints
accompanied his mission from the beginning. He died, the survey states, on 21 Ramzan 825
AH, and a very large gathering assembled, including people from Uch Sharif. The account
holds that a grand shrine was then built for him, attributed in one place to the King and
in another to Sultan Mahmud Ghaznavi, and known ever since for its grandeur and antiquity
(see the note below on that attribution).

## Miracles and Legends

Devotees relate that he was seen riding a lion and a snake, and it is by the lion and the
snake that he is still popularly known. Tradition holds that a canal at his camp site
dried up, or closed of its own accord, once he arrived. It is said that he would be seen
in one place and at the same moment far away. In another story he went to Delhi, had a
vision of Hazrat Ali Murtaza and carried the sacred footprints back on a snake; one is
said to have remained there and one to have been brought to Uch Sharif, where it is still
visited. Pressed with questions about such wonders, the account says, he adopted a
*Qalandarī* bearing so that people would take him for one lost in ecstasy. Emperors and
maharajas, the survey says, came to him for blessings, and Mughal emperors praised his
miracles.

Tradition also holds that he planted a tree with his own hands — named in one place as a
Sukh Chain (Pongamia) and in another as a banyan (*bor*) — on which writing formed by its
own branches is still visible, and that it yielded sugar long after his passing, as the
tree planted by his father at Uch Sharif is said to have given milk. Strikingly, the
surveyor records that according to books compiled about the saint, the graves immediately
adjacent to his own are those of the lion and the snake he rode. Devotees say that a
visitor need not state a request, and that prayers offered through his intercession
(*wasīla*) are still answered.

## The Shrine and its Devotions

Visitors come daily, but Thursday draws the larger crowd, when *mehfils* (devotional
gatherings) are held, *langar* (free communal food) is distributed, *naʿt* is recited,
*qawwālī* is performed and lamps are lit. The annual *ʿurs* (death anniversary observance)
falls on 19, 20 and 21 Ramzan, when a fair is held and very large numbers gather. The
surveyor estimates 800 to 1,200 visitors daily, 2,000 to 2,500 on Thursdays and 100,000 to
150,000 pilgrims at the *ʿurs*. The mosque facing the shrine, built in 1983 and named for
the saint, hosts the five daily prayers and Friday congregational prayer; *niyāz* (food
offerings) is distributed in the surrounding streets.

## Condition and Custodianship

The Auqaf Department assumed responsibility for the site in 1972 and is recorded as its
current custodian. The surveyor reports plainly that the shrine is gradually deteriorating
for lack of proper care, that the Auqaf Department is not carrying out its maintenance
properly, and that such arrangements as are made are made by local people and pilgrims who
love the saint. The survey also notes that Shah Gohar Peer wrote no book himself, there
being no such custom in his time, though later saints and people who had seen him are said
to have written about him; none is named, and none was available.

## qa_note

1. **Builder and death date are chronologically irreconcilable.** The survey states that
   after the saint's death his shrine was built by Sultan Mahmud Ghaznavi, and elsewhere
   gives his death as 21 Ramzan 825 AH (c. 1422 CE). Mahmud of Ghazni died in 1030 CE,
   roughly four centuries earlier, so he cannot have built a shrine for a saint who died in
   825 AH. The attribution is recorded here as tradition, not repeated as fact, and no
   resolution is proposed. Note only that the same account also mentions a Lahore locality
   called Mahmood Booti as a place where the saint briefly stayed; both names appear in the
   survey, and nothing in the survey connects them or explains one by the other. The
   founding-year field ("825 Hijri") has been treated as the death year, not a construction
   date, and `year_built` left unknown.
2. **The sect/lineage answer combines two distinct traditions.** The field reads "Ahl e
   Sunnat - Ismaili Sadaat Uch Shareef - Shajra e nasab Hazrat Imam Hussain (R.A)", and the
   life account likewise says his family lineage was from the Ismaili Sadaat of Uch Sharif.
   Ahl-e-Sunnat (Sunni) and Ismaili are different traditions, and the survey does not
   reconcile them. The lineage is recorded exactly as given; no choice has been made between
   the two, and no silsila has been inferred.
3. **Minor internal variance (not resolved).** The hand-planted tree is described as a Sukh
   Chain (Pongamia) in the answer on the saint's life and as a banyan (*bor*) in the
   additional-information answer. Both descriptions are reported; the survey gives no basis
   for preferring either, and it is unclear whether one tree or two is meant.
4. No other source was consulted. The survey reports that later saints wrote books about
   the saint, but names none, and the export column records "No book available", so no work
   is cited. Nine photographs and three audio/video files were uploaded with the survey and
   are held as Google Drive links in the source record.
