## Judgement calls for V1

1. "Accounts of Imam Ali-ul-Haq differ widely, and on when he lived they differ by some three or four centuries. The account more commonly repeated locally places him ..." The clause rests on "differing by some three or four centuries"; it signals the tenth century account without giving it.
2. "Accounts of his lineage and his companions differ, and some bring him from Arabia, but what they share is the memory of a holy man who came from afar, who is credited with bringing Islam to Sialkot, and who died a martyr on the spot now marked by his tomb." "What they share" rests on the team version's "What is consistent across the accounts"; Arabia on Bhandari and Ganesh Das, kept outside the local telling.
3. "most tell that he fell in battle against the local Hindu ruler, and companions are remembered as having fallen with him." "Most" counts three battle tellings of four; "against the local Hindu ruler" is the local account's phrase (Ganesh Das has a local raja; Bhandari's band fought "the Hindus"). If V1 judges "the local Hindu ruler" more than the majority gives, the fix is "most tell that he fell in battle".
4. "His name is also written Ali al-Haq, and he is known too as Ali al-Haq Sialkoti, Ali al-Haq of Sialkot." From Bhandari and the modern edition's index and list of sources; no time anchor.
5. Overview: "devotees come to it throughout the year to pray and to seek his blessing", from "Devotees come throughout the year to offer prayers, tie votive threads and seek blessings" and the *shahid* whose intercession and *baraka* are sought.
6. "already in the reign of the Mughal emperor Aurangzeb his tomb was a place of visitation" and "In the reign of Aurangzeb his tomb, held to be a place where divine lights are made manifest, was visited by small and great alike." Bhandari's "now his tomb ... is visited", anchored by his bibliography item; "held to be" keeps his phrase from becoming the narrator's claim.
7. Setting: "It was a bustling city in the foothills of the Himalayas, famous for its silk, and an important post on the trade route between West Asia and Delhi." Dhillon's description, in the past tense and undated. "It is located right on the border": Khalid's words; the border is not named because he does not name it.
8. "Legends of its founding differ; the founder most often named is Raja Salbahan, also called Salivahan". Basis (b) on the legible names; the identification of Salbahan with Salivahan follows the team version's "The name Salbahan ... recurs ... (Bhandari's Raja Salbahan, Khalid's Raja Salivahan)". "In legend the raja's son was Puran Bhagat" then rests on Khalid's "Puran Bhagat was the son of the King of Sialkot, Raja Salivahan".
9. The well of Puran: Khalid's "The well of Puran Bhagat was at a little distance from the city" and his temple are joined with Chishti's "the well into which Puran was thrown, his hands and feet cut off, is still to be seen there" (anchored "in the nineteenth century") as one well. The legend is framed "Tradition tells".
10. "In former times Sialkot was the seat of government of the Punjab ... In early times, when Lahore lay in ruins, it became the seat of government of the country, until ..." Two Bhandari statements kept as two sentences; "the country" for his "this country".
11. "Ghori also came to Sialkot after failing at Lahore in the year 580 AH ... and Khusraw Malik with the Khokhars besieged the fort for a time without success." Bhandari's two passages on the fort joined, with "Shihab al-Din of Ghur" written as the Ghori of the previous sentence, as the team version identifies them.
12. "Many Sufi missionaries settled in the region, making it predominantly Muslim and giving it a rich Sufi tradition." Kept in the Setting as said of the region, not linked to the Imam.
13. "The Mughal emperor Babur described Jat pastoralist groups in the Sialkot region." From Eaton's "the Sind Sagar doab and Sialkot regions"; "Mughal emperor" as the team version's Disagree section styles Babur.
14. "Stories are also told of the *faqir*, or wandering mystic, Hamza Ghaus, whose curse was pronounced in the centre of the market square, and of the fear of him that gripped Sialkot." For Khalid's "of whom Sialkot 'was gripped by fear'".
15. "In the early twenty first century Sialkot had become one of the most important cities in Pakistan ... many mounds, probably archaeological ruins, converted into graveyards." Khalid (2016), anchored; "probably" is his own hedge on what the mounds are, not a reading marked uncertain; "many" for "a lot of".
16. Legacy: "This may indeed be characteristic of the earliest layer of Punjabi Muslim sacred history ..." The site's own evaluation, kept with its "may"; "patron saint" and "defining landmarks of old Sialkot" are the team version's own words.
17. Legacy: "An account of his martyrdom, the *Waqa'i Sialkot* of Muhammad Muqim, is dated 1071 AH; it was edited by Dr Abdullah Chaghatai and published at Lahore in 1977." The work is named as part of the saint's legacy (a text about him), not as a source; 1071 AH is not converted.
