## Overview

Gurdwara Tambu Sahib, also written Tambo Sahib, stands in Nankana Sahib, a short distance east of Janam Asthan, the central shrine that marks the birthplace of Guru Nanak Dev Ji, founder of the Sikh faith. Its name comes from the Punjabi *tambu*, a tent, for the gurdwara grew up around a great *van* tree whose spreading, low hanging branches formed a natural canopy, a living tent, beneath which the young Nanak is remembered to have taken shelter. It is one of the cluster of shrines in and around the holy town that preserve, site by site, the episodes of the Guru's boyhood, and pilgrims tie pieces of cloth to the tree's branches as a supplication.

## The True Bargain

The tradition of Tambu Sahib is bound up with one of the most beloved of the *sakhis*, the stories of Guru Nanak's life: the *sacha sauda*, or "true bargain". Legend tells that when Nanak turned eighteen, his father, Mehta Kalu, anxious that his contemplative son should learn a trade, gave him twenty rupees and sent him to the market town of Chuharkana to buy goods and turn a profit, and that Nanak set out with Bhai Mardana. On the way, it is told, he met a company of hungry holy men, and judging that no bargain could be truer than the feeding of the poor and the servants of God, he spent the whole sum on food for them and turned for home empty handed but content.

Fearing his father's anger, the boy is said to have stopped short of the village and sat down beneath the great *van* tree, whose branches enclosed him like a tent, and there, the story goes, he stayed until his family found him and brought him home. The road back from Chuharkana to Nankana Sahib, then still known as Rai Bhoi di Talwandi, ran, it is told, across a vast jungle that lay at that time between the two. The tree stood outside the town. It is an ancient tree, spread out like a tent, and it still stands, held sacred by Sikh devotees.

The gurdwara raised on the spot preserves the memory of that refuge, and it is one of two gurdwaras that commemorate the episode. Another legend of Nankana Sahib, often confused with this one, tells of a tree's shadow that stood still while all others moved; it belongs to the nearby Gurdwara Mal Ji Sahib, not to this site.

## The Gurdwara

A shrine was established beside the tree during the period of Sikh rule in the Punjab, about a furlong east of Janam Asthan, and its architecture echoes that of the other gurdwaras of Nankana Sahib: a domed sanctum set within a square hall, decorated in the idiom of Sikh sacred building, though plainer in some of its detailing than its neighbours. It is a small building, yellow and of a single storey, with a white dome on top. It lies between Janam Asthan and the gurdwaras of Kiara Sahib and Mal Ji Sahib, close to Mal Ji Sahib and to the gurdwara of the fifth and sixth Gurus, so that the pilgrim moving among them traces a compact circuit of the Guru's early life.

The tree is protected by a boundary fence with a single gate, and shoes come off before entering. Behind the tree stands the gurdwara, with a board bearing its name, and the saffron *Nishan Sahib*, the Sikh flag on its tall pole, rises in the courtyard. Within the sanctum the *Guru Granth Sahib*, the Sikh scripture, is installed. Inside stands the *Thara Sahib*, a wooden pavilion that holds the scripture, and two swords lie there in sheaths of blue velvet.

The grounds are large. The area belonging to Tambu Sahib is the second largest in the town, big enough to host an international cricket match, making the gurdwara the town's second most popular destination after Janam Asthan. A tall wall with barbed wire ran round it on all sides, with one gate from the main market and a board by the road in English and in the Punjabi scripts Shahmukhi and Gurmukhi; a second, blue gate was kept permanently locked for security reasons. Vast land lay vacant on both sides of the shrine and was used for farming, with a tractor and a single storey building used by farmers.

Behind the gurdwara stand large hostels, used by Sikh pilgrims at the time of the largest Sikh festival in Pakistan, the birthday of Guru Nanak, celebrated every November. By the entrance stood a newly built residential hall of three storeys, with two larger residential buildings raised from devotees' donations and a Langar Hall of a single storey, which houses the *langar*, the free communal kitchen. Hostel blocks were built here with donations from pilgrims coming from Britain, Canada and America, and a large amount was given by Kar Seva, a non-profit organisation based in England; a yellow board on the Langar Hall read: "This newly constructed building has been made by Kar Seva". Next to the Langar Hall stood Shaheed Hall, a building of five storeys, recently completed and also built from Kar Seva's donations, whose roof was closed to pilgrims. The Government of Pakistan planned to build a Guru Nanak University near the complex, a persistent demand of the Sikh community abroad.

Like the other shrines of the town, the site is maintained by the authorities responsible for Sikh heritage in Pakistan. The gurdwara's vast property was allotted to it in the nineteenth century by Maharaja Ranjit Singh, the Sikh ruler who became sovereign of the Punjab at the end of the eighteenth century; across the province he spent large sums on the renovation and expansion of many gurdwaras and attached vast tracts of land to them, so that the revenues from the land could support their running.

That property was in the charge of Jatha Singh, who had appointed himself the gurdwara's caretaker. He received millions of rupees in donations from Sikhs living in the countries of the first world, to be spent on the betterment of the gurdwara and of the Sikh community of Nankana Sahib, which he had led for several years. He kept a room in one of the hostels, and on one visit he was found arbitrating between two feuding Muslim families, while his guests were served *langar* of vegetables and lentils.

One historical event began within the gurdwara's grounds. On 20 February 1921 unarmed Sikhs from other districts gathered at Tambu Sahib, accused the *mahant*, and after a short prayer resolved to walk to Janam Asthan. Behind the gathering lay the state of the Punjab's gurdwaras: as a result of sectarian divisions among the Sikh communities and the British takeover of the Punjab, a large number of important Sikh gurdwaras were being administered by Hindu *mahants*, heads of religious establishments.

## Devotion and Festivals

The tree is the heart of the devotion, and pilgrims tie pieces of cloth onto its branches as a supplication. In January 2011 the town's Sikhs kept Lohri at the gurdwara. Lohri, like Vaisakhi or Basant, is an ancient festival of the Punjab that marks the change of season, celebrated on the 13th of January. Kartar Singh Kartar tore a saffron cloth from inside the gurdwara, and each person tied a piece of it to the tree; his Muslim friends tied cloth too. Tying a cloth on the branches of this sacred tree, Kartar said, makes wishes come true, and at Guru Nanak's birthday people tie their cloths over one another's. Candles were lit at the base of the *Nishan Sahib*.

The prayer that followed was for Sikhs only, with the other three doors closed. Kartar prostrated himself before the *Thara Sahib* and offered a ten rupee note; the highest note among the offerings was one of fifty, and the donations were to be used for the renovation of the gurdwara. The room filled to about fifty people, as many men as women. A *Granthi*, a reader of the scripture, in his mid sixties read for half an hour; then came a community prayer with *Waheguru*, the Sikh name for God, and *Prasad*, the consecrated offering, of naan and a sweet made in *desi ghee*, clarified butter. Candles were held up in a circle around the *arti*, which carries lit incense, and people placed a hand on top of the fire and rubbed it on their faces. From there the procession went on to Janam Asthan.

Such celebrations, Kartar explained, were new: Lohri and Guru Gobind Singh's birthday had begun only in the last couple of years, made possible by the increase in the Sikh population through migration from the tribal areas, and earlier there had been only a prayer at the gurdwara. As one writer observed, it was ironical that a Punjabi tradition shunned by the Punjab was being revived by a group of Pathan men living in the Punjab.

At the birthday of Guru Nanak, known as Guru Nanak Jayanti or *Gurpurab*, the gurdwara fills. Official estimates put the people who come to Nankana Sahib for the festival at ten to fifteen thousand every year; in 2011, by media reports, only three thousand came, and that year it ran from the 8th to the 10th of November. Most of the town's gurdwaras were kept locked all year round, to be opened only at the time of the festival, and security guards were employed at the entrance to all of them. Tambu Sahib housed most of the pilgrims from the developed countries, and Shaheed Hall was their first choice, though as their numbers fell it came to house many Indian pilgrims. Security was lax compared with Janam Asthan: there were metal detectors and a few guards, but it was fairly easy to walk in unnoticed. Food was served around the clock.

In the parking area stood a book stall of the Dyal Singh Memorial Trust, a trust from before Partition based in Lahore, where Iqbal Qaiser, then fifty eight, sold his *Historical Sikh Shrines in Pakistan*; this was the only time in the year his book sold, he said, somewhere between twenty and thirty copies. Police stood on the roofs of the Langar Hall and the residential buildings. Arshad Malik, a constable of thirty five with eighteen years in the police, on a duty of twelve hours since eight in the morning, said that the entire police force of the town had been deployed at the gurdwaras for the three days. He would not eat from the *langar*: "We are Muslims. We don't eat with the Sikhs or Hindus."

## Nankana Sahib

Nankana Sahib was once the village of Talwandi, about forty miles south west of Lahore. Originally called Rai Bhoi di Talwandi, it was renamed Nankana Sahib in honour of Guru Nanak, the first Sikh Guru, who was born there, and his early years there account for several of the town's gurdwaras. It is considered one of the holiest cities in the world for the Sikhs.

Tradition tells that Talwandi lay between the rivers Ravi and Chenab, directly in the path of the armies that had set their eyes on Delhi, and that thirteen times the village was destroyed and thirteen times, like the fabled phoenix, it rose again from its ashes; its last rebirth, the story goes, took place under Rai Bhoi, who had converted to Islam. It lay too, the same tradition tells, in the path of numerous mendicants, *sadhus*, or Hindu ascetics, and holy men, who camped in a grove of trees on the outskirts of the village.

Behind Janam Asthan rises an old mound. The settlement there, it is said, was destroyed, after which Rai Bhoi di Talwandi was built within a boundary wall. It is believed that in the six or seven years between the *sacha sauda* and his move to Sultanpur, Nanak began to frequent this place, talking with the yogis, *sadhus* and dervishes who passed through.

After Partition the town's gurdwaras stood empty. As Kartar remembered it: "There was no Sikh left here after partition. Our Gurdwaras became barren and were in a bad state." Giani Pratap, seeing the dilapidated condition of the shrines, decided to stay and look after these holy buildings. In the early twenty first century the town's Sikh houses grew in about ten years from only about thirty or forty to about two hundred. Most of these Sikhs were not natives of the city but recent migrants from Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, where there is a considerable Sikh population, and most of the newcomers were housed within the gurdwaras, in rooms generally reserved for pilgrims.

## The Guru

The gurdwara exists for the sake of Guru Nanak, the founder of the Sikh religion. His story is kept in the *janamsakhis*, the cycles of tales told of the life of the first Guru of the Sikhs. Accounts of his birth differ in their details, but he is generally held to have been born at Talwandi in 1469. His father was Kalyan Chand Das Bedi, or Mehta Kalu as he was affectionately called, who in the janamsakhi telling kept the accounts of the Bhatti family under Rai Bular. In his childhood, it is said, signs of spiritual unveiling, of miracles and of wonders beyond the ordinary became manifest in him.

In the janamsakhi telling, at thirteen Nanak refused the sacred thread, the *janeu*. At sixteen, the telling goes on, he herded cattle, and he became well acquainted with the dervish Sayyad Hussain and gained an intimate knowledge of the Sufi line of thought. After the *sacha sauda*, it is told, he moved to Sultanpur, around 60 kilometres from Talwandi, as keeper of Nawab Daulat Khan's stores. Guru Nanak died on 22 September 1539, though accounts of the date differ.

He brought a message of peace and harmony to the followers of Islam and of Hinduism, religions hostile to each other, and taught that all men and women were equal, irrespective of caste, creed, wealth, social standing and gender, and that a good life rested on honest labour, compassion, selfless service and prayer. In Aurangzeb's reign his followers counted the service of those who come and go as the greatest worship, and whoever arrived at midnight speaking Baba Nanak's name was treated as a brother and a friend and served, even a stranger, a thief or a highwayman. It is believed that the tradition of the *langar* began with Guru Nanak. He is held to have disavowed the miraculous and to have abhorred the association of miracles with religious figures; yet, as one writer has put it, "his biography is nothing but a story of his miracles".

He is tied to the Sufis too, and devotion to him reaches beyond the Sikhs. Many Muslims acquainted with his teachings are devoted to him and visit his shrine. Guru Nanak is held to have been a spiritual follower of Baba Farid, and Farid's texts are revered among the Sikhs because, it is said, he influenced the founder of their religion. At one of the shrines of Guru Nanak at Nankana Sahib, a picture of Baba Farid accompanies the picture of the Guru. A Chishti Sabiri shaykh of the twentieth century described Guru Nanak, with Krishna, Rama, Sita and Lachman, as a believer in the one true God and a religious leader with a "Divine connection", *nisbat*. At the shrine of the goddess Hinglaj there are engraved footsteps held to be Guru Nanak's.

## The Guru's Verse

The Guru's *bani*, his devotional verses of supplication, entreaty, humility and reverence, was compiled as part of the Adi Granth by Guru Arjan Dev, the fifth Guru, in 1604. He composed verses in Hindi on the truth of God and on divine unity, and a minstrel named Mardana sang the Guru's verses and drew people into the snare of devotion. In Aurangzeb's reign the worship of his followers centred on reading their master's verses, which they recited in song and melody. Among his poems, fitting for a gurdwara that remembers a bargain, is one that opens "O my merchant friend".

Lines from his hymns, in English translation, give a sense of the poetry. His hymn of praise, the *aarti*, begins:

ਗਗਨ ਮੈ ਥਾਲੁ
ਰਵਿ ਚੰਦੁ ਦੀਪਕ ਬਨੇ …

*In the salver of the firmament, / The sun and the moon shine as lamps*

On the oneness of God he sang:

ਸਾਹਿਬੁ ਮੇਰਾ ਏਕੋ ਹੈ ॥
ਏਕੋ ਹੈ ਭਾਈ ਏਕੋ ਹੈ ॥

*My Master is One / He is One, brother, the only One*

And again:

*God is One, / The Path is One*

His poem on the sacred thread runs:

ਦਇਆ ਕਪਾਹ
ਸੰਤੋਖੁ ਸੂਤੁ …

*From the cotton of compassion, / Spin the thread of contentment*

Of washing he sang:

ਭਰੀਐ ਹਥੁ ਪੈਰੁ ਤਨੁ ਦੇਹ ॥
ਪਾਣੀ ਧੋਤੈ ਉਤਰਸੁ ਖੇਹ ॥

*As hands or feet besmirched with slime, / Water washes white*

His verse is sung beyond the gurdwaras too. The singer Abida Parvin has said: "I sing Shah Hussain, Bulle Shah, Mian Muhammad Bakhsh, Hazrat Khawaja Ghulam Farid, Sacchal Sarmast, Baba Farid, and Guru Nanak." In Sindh, Sain Vasan Shah became famous for reciting the *banis* of Shri Guru Nanak and the *kalams*, the poems, of the Sufis.

## Legacy

Since Partition in 1947 Tambu Sahib, with the other shrines of the town, has been visited chiefly by pilgrim *jathas*, bands of pilgrims, who travel from India and the diaspora for the great anniversaries, above all the *Gurpurab* of Guru Nanak's birth, when the town fills with the faithful. The tent tree of Tambu Sahib carries a lesson dear to the Sikh conscience, that the truest use of wealth is the service of others, the principle of *sacha sauda*; and the site endures as a gentle monument to the boy who thought the feeding of the hungry the best of all bargains.

Tambu Sahib is thus two things at once: a sacred tree that pilgrims and townspeople, Sikh and Muslim, hang with cloth, and, with its grounds the second largest in the town, the second most popular destination after Janam Asthan, where the pilgrims of the Sikh diaspora sleep and the halls their donations built now stand.
