## Overview

The shrine of Peer Makki stands in the Bhati Gate quarter of Lahore's Walled City, close to the great shrine of Data Ganj Bakhsh. It honours Hazrat Syed Azizuddin, a scholar and mystic born in Baghdad, who is said to have spent twelve years in seclusion at Mecca, the years that gave him his title, Makki, before coming to Lahore in 575 AH, around 1178 CE, where he taught for some thirty six years. Devotional custom in Lahore holds that pilgrims should pay their respects at his tomb before going on to Data Darbar, and his yearly *ʿurs*, the commemoration of the saint's death, fills the quarter with devotees.

Like Data Darbar itself, the shrine is administered by the Punjab Auqaf and Religious Affairs Department, the Punjab government's department of religious endowments, and its tradition is Syed and Ahl-e-Sunnat, the Sunni way. Its complex includes an adjoining mosque, the Hazrat Pir Makki Masjid. Despite its nearness to one of Pakistan's most visited shrines, Peer Makki's own tomb receives markedly fewer pilgrims, which gives it a more intimate, contemplative atmosphere.

## The Saint

Hazrat Syed Azizuddin al-Hassani wal-Hussaini was born in Baghdad, the son of Hazrat Peer Syed Abdullah. He was a Syed, a descendant of the Prophet Muhammad, and came of a Hasani and Hussaini family of religious scholars that counted Junaid Baghdadi, Imam Ahmad bin Hanbal and Sheikh Abdul Qadir Jilani among its kin. His father was himself a saint and a scholar who taught in Baghdad, and the son had his first education from him, memorising the Qur'an and then learning the knowledge of the soul and of the sect, seated in the same row as everyone else in his father's circle.

Having taken the outward sciences as far as they went, he judged that he must go in search of the hidden knowledge. In 562 AH, at about the age of thirty, he left Baghdad for Mecca, and he spent twelve years there in ascetic seclusion; from those years came his popular title, Makki, "of Mecca". He heard the pleas of those who came to him and helped them, and kept his nights in the Haram, the sacred mosque, in the remembrance of God. It is said that he received the blessing of the Prophet many times, and that he came to know what was to come but did not show it.

Tradition holds that his coming was by divine instruction. While he was worshipping in the Haram, it is said, he received the Prophet's command to go to Hind, where the people had fallen into associating partners with God, and to bring them back to the Sunna, the Prophet's way, and to the oneness of God. He did not wish to leave the House of God, but the command was the command. He made his last Hajj, the pilgrimage to Mecca, took some companions with him, and turned first to Baghdad, to tell his family and to say the Fatiha, the opening chapter of the Qur'an, at his father's grave.

Two roads led to India, one through the dry desert and one by sea. The journey was long and dangerous, with little to eat or drink, but he preached along the way, and people were moved enough to join his caravan. After nearly a year he reached India and gave thanks.

He came to Lahore in 575 AH, around 1178 CE, and settled in the mosque that today carries his name beside his shrine. There he led the prayers, taught the Qur'an and called people away from polytheism. For some thirty six years, it is said, he taught both the outward sciences of Islamic law and the inward path of the Junaidi Sufi lineage, which traces back to the classical Baghdad master Junayd.

Accounts of who he was and of the year of his death have not always agreed. The shrine remembers him as Hazrat Syed Azizuddin of Baghdad, and the year it keeps for his death, 612 AH or 1215 CE, is the one most accounts give.

## The Shrine

Kings, it is said, maintained the shrine after him and added to its beauty, and in 1958 the Auqaf Department took charge of it. The adjoining Hazrat Pir Makki Masjid is the mosque in which the saint is said to have settled on reaching Lahore.

Accounts differ on exactly where the tomb stands. In the nineteenth century it lay at the southeastern corner of a garden called the Committee Bagh. A brick wall higher than a man enclosed it, and within it the saint's grave, built of brick and mortar, stood on a platform. There were two masonry wells, a platform marking a mosque, and a *ghusl-khana*, a bathing room.

In that century the keeper of the tomb was a woman *faqir*, one who has taken the Sufi path of poverty, and she had two sons, Fateh Din and Karam Din. The shrine also held cultivated land, free of revenue for as long as the *khanqah*, the Sufi lodge, should stand.

## Devotion and Festivals

The custom of paying respects at Peer Makki's tomb before going on to Data Darbar honours him as a spiritual forerunner in the same sacred place. On an ordinary day some 3,500 to 4,000 pilgrims come to his tomb, and the gatherings on Thursday nights draw larger crowds. The narrow street and the Peer Makki Bazaar around it can make the shrine hard to reach.

His annual *ʿurs* is held from the 9th to the 11th of Rabi-ul-Awal. It fills the quarter with gatherings and *dhikr*, the remembrance of God, with drums and *dhamal*, ecstatic devotional dancing to the beat of the drum, and with *qawwali*, the devotional music of the Sufis. The shrine is ritually bathed and offerings are given out, and the festival draws on the order of 400,000 to 500,000 devotees.

In the nineteenth century, too, an *ʿurs* was held at the tomb, and *halwa*, a sweet, and *kheer*, a rice pudding, were served, though fewer people came then than in earlier days.

## Legends

A well loved story tells how the saint foretold the end of Khusrau Malik's rule in Lahore. Khusrau Malik, said to be the last of the line of Mahmud of Ghazni and the son of King Khusrau, is remembered as given to pleasure and unjust, and Muhammad Shahab-ud-Din Ghori, who had already taken Multan and Sialkot, was advancing on the city. When Khusrau Malik came to ask how long his rule would last, the saint told him plainly that it would soon end, that what God has written must come, and that he should turn to God and deal justly with his people.

When Ghori laid siege to Lahore in 1178, the king and the people alike ran to the saint for his prayers. He told them to be patient and to repent: the danger would pass, he said, and return in six years. Ghori withdrew, and the saint's name went round the city. But the people soon returned to their old ways, and exactly six years later Ghori came again. Once more the saint promised a respite, and said it would be the last, and once more the army turned back; this time people called him a liar, saying that the old man only ever said such things and that Ghori had left out of fear.

When Ghori at last took Lahore, Khusrau Malik's men were killed or went over to him, and the king himself was taken prisoner and later killed. Ghori, it is said, came to the saint's door and told him that he had known he was there, and that everything he had said had been true.

Devotion binds Peer Makki to his great neighbour, and traditions of the bond between the two saints differ. The one told at the shrine itself holds that when Ali Hujwiri, Data Ganj Bakhsh, was ordered to migrate to Lahore, he objected that "Peer Makki is present there, who is enough for Lahore", only to arrive and find Peer Makki's funeral already under way. The story fits loosely with the dates, for Hujwiri's death is conventionally placed around 1072, a century before Peer Makki's own arrival, and it should be understood as devotional tradition rather than strict chronology. But it carries the shrine's essential teaching: that Lahore has never been without a saint of Allah.

## The Setting

Bhati Gate is one of the twelve gates of Lahore's walled city. Lahore's Makki Gate is named for another man. Properly the Zaki Gate, it takes its name from Pir Zaki, a gate guard of Mughal times who was martyred at the gate, and the form Makki is said to be a corruption of Zaki; Pir Zaki is a different man from Peer Makki.

In Hujwiri's day Lahore was the Indian capital of the Ghaznavids, the house of Mahmud of Ghazni. Hujwiri reached the city around 1035, while Sultan Mas'ud I, who reigned from 1031 to 1041, ruled in Ghazna. Hujwiri died in Lahore, and his shrine is still a popular place of pilgrimage there. When the Ghurids drove Khusrau Shah, son of Bahram Shah, from Ghazni, he took Lahore, made it his capital and governed the Punjab until his death in 555 AH. His son, Sultan Khusrau Malik, succeeded him, and his rule in Lahore lasted twenty eight years. The Ghurids seized Multan in 1175, when Mu'izzu'd-Din Muhammad bin Sam established firm control and Sunni rule over the southern part of the Punjab.

The Ghurid sultan Shahab-ud-Din Ghori campaigned against Lahore more than once. In his third campaign the fort was nearly taken after siege and battle, and Khusrau Malik sent his son with an elephant as tribute and made peace. In the fifth the sultan besieged Lahore again; Khusrau Malik shut himself in the fort, and the city could not be mastered. In the sixth Khusrau Malik submitted and was taken to Ghazni, and the sultan set his own deputy over Lahore. With that surrender the rule of the Ghaznavids came to an end and that of the Ghurid sultans in Hind began; the Ghurids, it is said, took the land by a thousand tricks and deceits. Accounts of the years of these campaigns differ.

## Legacy

Many writers, among them, it is said at the shrine, authors from the Hindu and Christian communities, have written books about the saint that are still read with interest today. A detailed article on him, under the heading "Pir Makki", the name by which Lahore knows him, has been written for the *Danishnama-e Shibh-e Qarra*, an encyclopaedia of the Subcontinent published in Tehran. The shrine has kept its name and its place beside Data Darbar, and the quarter around it, with Peer Makki Bazaar and Pir Makki Street, carries the title Makki that his years in Mecca are said to have given him.
