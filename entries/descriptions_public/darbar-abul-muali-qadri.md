## Overview

Darbar Abul Muali Qadri is an active Muslim shrine in Lahore, outside Mochi Gate of the old walled city. It is built around the grave of Hazrat Syed Muhammad Khair ul Deen, widely known as Shah Abul Muali Qadri, a saint of the Qadiri order of the Sufis, the mystics of Islam, who came to Lahore at the command of his master and uncle and taught there. The complex holds his tomb, a mosque and a graveyard in which members of the saint's family lie alongside ordinary and notable dead. It is cared for by the Government Auqaf Department, the department of religious endowments, together with the descendants of the shrine's hereditary custodians, and between 2,000 and 2,500 people come to it every day.

Visitors come to pay their respects, to pray, to seek healing and to share in the free food that is given out without pause. By the 1880s it was known as a famous and blessed tomb, and it is held to be a fountainhead of blessings. Its yearly *ʿurs*, the commemoration of the saint's death, is reported to draw 200,000 to 250,000 people, and construction work at the site is ongoing.

## The Saint

Accounts of the form of his name differ; the name the shrine keeps is Hazrat Syed Muhammad Khair ul Deen. Abul Muali, the name by which he is known, was his *kunyat*, an honorific name of the form "father of", and it is also reported that his title was Asadullah, the Lion of God. He was also called *zubdat al-arifin*, the choicest of those who know God. Other holy men bore the name Abul Muali, among them a Shah Abul Muali of the Chishti order and a Shah Abul Muali of a Gilani line, and the saint of this shrine is not to be confused with them.

Tradition holds that he was born on 10 Zil Hajj, the day of Eid al-Adha, in 960 of the Hijri, or Islamic, calendar, in Kirman in Iran; accounts of the year and place of his birth differ. He came of the Kirmani Sadaat, the *sayyids* of Kirman, descendants of the Prophet Muhammad, and he is reported to have been Hassani Hussaini and of the Ahl al-Bayt, the household of the Prophet. His father, Syed Rehmatullah Kirmani, was the son of Sayyid Fath Allah Kirmani. His father is described as a scholar and a poet in Urdu and Persian, who migrated from Iran and settled at Depalpur, in Okara. Two of his father's brothers are named: Sheikh Dawood Bandgi Kirmani, whose shrine is reported to be at Shergarh, and Syed Jalal ud Deen, whose shrine is reportedly in Sindh.

After his father's death, it is reported, he was taken into the care of his uncle Dawood Bandgi, who was also his father-in-law, and he studied with him until the age of thirty; accounts of the length of his training differ. Dawood Bandgi was his *pir* and *murshid*, his spiritual guide. The saint was the son of Dawood Bandgi's own brother, and he became his disciple, his son-in-law and his *khalifa*, the deputy who carries on a master's teaching, receiving from him the *khirqa*, the cloak, of *khilafat*, the authority to guide others.

Accounts of when he came to Lahore differ; the year the shrine keeps is 980 AH, when, at his uncle's command, he migrated from Depalpur to Lahore. There, near Mochi Darwaza, as Mochi Gate is also called, he established a mosque and a seminary, where he led the congregational prayers and taught. He made continuous and tireless efforts for the spread of the Qadiri *silsila*, the spiritual lineage of the order founded by Abd al-Qadir Jilani, and at Lahore he found great acceptance.

He is credited with having wells, mosques and *madrasas*, religious schools, built along his route, with writing books in Urdu and Persian, and with composing poetry, though his focus is reported to have been preaching. It is reported that he taught the Quran and had memorised it, that he possessed both the outward and the inward knowledge, and that he was a dervish and a perfect scholar. He is remembered as the learned man of his time, a *faqir*, a mystic who has embraced poverty, given to *zuhd*, the renunciation of the world, and to *riyazat*, spiritual discipline. He is counted, too, a man of *kashf*, inner unveiling, and of *karamat*, the miraculous gifts of a saint, with a passionate love for his own pir, and he is held to have stood at the degree of *fana fi al-shaikh*, the annihilation of the self in one's master.

Devotees relate that kings came to him with requests, that he never distinguished between rich and poor, and that he kept many *chillas*, retreats of forty days in seclusion, and nights of *dhikr*, the remembrance of God. Accounts of the number of his followers differ; devotees relate that millions pledged allegiance to him, and that because of him millions of people entered the fold of Islam.

He is said to have loved Sheikh Abdul Qadir Jilani and Mian Mir deeply. With Sheikh Abdul Qadir Jilani, honoured as Ghaus al-Azam, the greatest helper, he had a special *nisbat*, a spiritual bond, and a devotion to him beyond bounds. Among his teachings, he did not agree that the bond of love is useless after death: in his view love is lawful and proper in every state. On opposing the self, he held that the self's opposition is a good omen, as the call of a crow on the roof is good news that a guest is coming.

He married twice, it is reported. His first wife was a daughter of Dawood Bandgi Kirmani, who died some time after the marriage, and his second a daughter of the Emperor Shah Jahangir; the couple are said to have given her considerable wealth and lands entirely to charitable purposes. He is reported to have had eight sons, one of whom is said to have been martyred fighting in a war against the Sikhs. His eldest son was Shah Muhammad Baqir.

He died on 16 Rabi ul Awal, the third month of the Islamic year, in 1024 AH, at the age, it is reported, of sixty five. Accounts differ over the reign in which he died; the shrine places his death in the reign of Shah Jahan.

## His Books and His Verse

His most famous book is the *Tuhfat al-Qadiriya*, on the wonders and *karamat* of Ghaus al-Azam. Besides it he wrote the *Naghmat-e Daudi*, on the conditions of his own *murshid*, the *Munis-e Jan*, the *Zafran Zar*, the *Guldasta-e Bagh-e Iram* and a *Sharh-e Yazdah Bai'at* of Shaikh Abdul Qadir Jilani. By the 1880s many books of his composition still survived, and it is reported that many books have been written on his life and his *karamat*. It was at his insistence, it is said, that the commentaries on the *Futuh al-Ghaib* of Abd al-Qadir Jilani and on the *Mishkat* came to be written.

He was a poet too, and wrote under the *takhallus*, or pen name, Gharibi. By the 1870s a *diwan*, a collection of his poems, was in the possession of his descendants. In one couplet he acknowledges the favours of his pir, Shaikh Dawood, and names him:

بہ تخت فقر بنشینم چو حاصل گشت مقصودم
سلیمانی کنم کزجان غلام شیخ داؤدم

*A couplet acknowledging the favours of his pir, which names Shaikh Dawood.*

His death, too, was set in verse, in chronograms, verses whose letters, read as numbers, give a year. One line of them names him Maali Khair-e Din:

رحلتنش گفتا معالی خیر دین

*A line of a chronogram on the saint's death, which names him Maali Khair-e Din.*

## The Shrine

It is reported that when Shah Abul Muali came to Lahore he settled at this spot, and that during his lifetime the place became a centre where people received his spiritual blessings. Devotees relate that he was buried here after his passing, and that his tomb was raised on the site of his own seminary, where he had earlier built a mosque and taught.

The quarter around it, the *mohalla* of the Pirs, was settled in the time of the emperor Jahangir by the saint himself, who lived there. When the sultanate fell into ruin and the people scattered, his descendants moved inside the city wall and the quarter was left desolate; yet in the 1880s, among its old buildings, the *rauza*, that is, the tomb, of the saint still stood, built *pakka*, of solid and lasting construction. It had a very large graveyard and countless buildings belonging to it.

Accounts of who built the tomb differ, but most hold that the saint laid its foundation in his lifetime and that it was not yet complete when he died. His son Muhammad Baqir brought the building to completion. Under Sikh rule it was rebuilt by the people of the *sajjada*, the line of the shrine's hereditary custodians.

In the 1880s the tomb stood on a high platform built *pakka*. It was an octagonal building, its door for coming and going on the south, all of lime, plastered inside and out, with a roof of the kind called *qalbuti* under a grand dome. From outside it rose in two storeys, though inside it was of one, and its staircase lay on the west. In the nineteenth century its octagonal dome was white and decorated, a verse was set over the south door, the door had a green niche and carved latticework, and the tomb bore Arabic words saying that the friends of God do not die. Some of its stones had by then come to need repair, and its western staircase was unfinished.

In the 1880s four graves lay on the platform inside the tomb: those of the saint, of his eldest son Shah Muhammad Baqir, of Shah Muhammad Raza and of Haji Muhammad Fazl. Around the graves, on all four sides, stood a wooden lattice the height of a man.

Custodianship is reported to have passed from the saint to his sons and then to the hereditary custodians, reportedly termed *gaddi nashin*, those who sit on the saint's seat, the first of whom was Hazrat Haider Ali Shah, who is also buried in the shrine. It is said that "the kings of every era took care of this place", and in the present era the responsibility has passed to the Auqaf Department. In the nineteenth century the saint's descendants included Shah Sultan and Shah Muhammad Ali, and in the 1880s Imam Shah, Fazil Shah and Firoz Shah were among them, counted *safed-posh*, men of respectable standing. In the 1880s his grandsons and great-grandsons were countless; all of them lived in Lahore, and their livelihood rested on *piri-muridi*, the guiding of disciples, and on the income of the offerings made at the tomb.

The mosque was built by the saint in his lifetime and rebuilt under the Sikhs by a general of the artillery, and in the 1880s it still stood. In the nineteenth century the mosque faced east, its well had been built by the saint himself, and the well had a platform of red stone that had by then stood for twenty years.

In the nineteenth century the precinct held the graves of grandsons of the saint, of a *murid*, or disciple, named Muhammad Sufi, and of Muhammad Shah and Shah Hasan, with Hazrat Hasan Shah. It had *dalans*, open halls, among them the old *dalan* of Sayyid Imam Shah, and a room built by Sayyid Mehr Shah Sahib and Sayyid Alam Shah, with their *faqir*, besides many houses of the descendants and a graveyard. The room for the ritual washing stood west of the tomb. Many houses and *dalans* had been built for the saint's descendants, each of whom lived in his own house.

The graveyard was once a burial ground for the city. In the nineteenth century burials in the graveyard of the tomb, at Tila Shahid Ganj, were banned by order of the government.

## Devotion and Festivals

There is a press of visitors every day. The descendants of the custodians live at the shrine, and people travel from far away to receive *dum* from them, spiritual healing by breath and recitation. The mosque inside the shrine holds the five daily prayers. A gathering is held every day at which *naʿt*, devotional poetry in praise of the Prophet, is recited, and *langar*, free food from the communal kitchen, is given out without pause. Every Thursday drums are beaten, gatherings are held, lamps are lit and *niyaz*, consecrated offerings of food, is distributed. Devotees also recite the *Fateha*, the opening chapter of the Quran, at the graves.

Visitors come to pay their respects and to pray to Allah through the saint's intercession, and they are reported to say that their prayers are answered there. Whatever the worry or sorrow, they say, they find peace only here, because he had knowledge of the unseen; for them he was a *Wali Allah*, a friend of God, from whose door Allah never lets anyone go empty handed.

The *ʿurs* is held on 15, 16 and 17 Rabi ul Awal. Its programme includes the Chadar Poshi, the laying of the shroud; the Mela Chiraghan, the festival of lights; a *mehfil*, or gathering, of *naʿt* shareef; dhol drumming and *dhamal*, the ecstatic dance of the Sufis; *qawwali*, the devotional music of the Sufis; the Ghusal, the ritual washing; and *langar*. Attendance is reported as 200,000 to 250,000 people.

In the 1880s the fair of both Eids was held at the tomb, and the people of the city went to it in complete faith and gave *nazrana*, offerings. The annual *ʿurs* was held as well, falling each year on the date of the saint's death. On its first night there was *chiraghan*, the lighting of lamps, and courtesans danced, and on the second day there was *qawwali*, to which the *fuqara*, the dervishes, listened. With the two Eid fairs and the *ʿurs*, there were, as it were, three fairs at the tomb in a year.

## Legends

Several incidents are told of the saint, and they are said to be widely known among devotees.

**The *majzub* at the roadside.** It is reported that, while he was still searching for a spiritual master, the saint set out for the house of a companion, Sheikh Hamid Karishmi, in Delhi. On the way he spoke with a *majzub*, one spiritually intoxicated and absorbed in divine love, who was sitting by the road. The *majzub* told him that the one he was wandering in search of was in his own home, and named his uncle, Dawood Bandgi Kirmani. Astonished, he returned to his uncle, who answered him with a verse to the effect of: "Wander and see the whole world, blister your feet, if you find anyone like us, then say." His request for allegiance was accepted, and after some days his uncle sent him back to Lahore with the instruction to spend his life in the path of Allah.

**The promised audience.** It is told that Shaikh Dawood learnt by *kashf* of his disciple's longing, and told him that today or tomorrow he would bring him into the presence of the holy and exalted one, and that he should wait for that hour. One night, between sleep and waking, his pir took him by the hand into the assembly of Ghaus al-Azam, where Abu al-Maali Iraqi sat on the right and his pir on the left. The moment the thought crossed his heart that the man on the right might outrank his pir, Ghaus al-Azam looked at him and said: "O Abu al-Maali, your shaikh is my heart, and the heart is on the left side".

**Dreams of Sheikh Abdul Qadir Jilani.** It is said that whoever pledged allegiance at his hands would see Sheikh Abdul Qadir Jilani in a dream that very night, and that this was due to his *karamat*.

**The rosary.** Tellings differ over who received the rosary, but the shrine tells the story of Dara Shikoh. It is reported that Dara Shikoh wrote that he once accompanied Shah Abul Muali to the court of his teacher, Sheikh Imam Niyamat Ali, where someone presented the saint with a rosary. Dara Shikoh silently wished that it might be given to him. On their return to the seminary the saint handed him that same rosary, telling him to recite *Darood*, the blessings on the Prophet, on it every day, so that the giver, the recipient and the saint would all share in the reward. Dara Shikoh is said to have been amazed that his unspoken thought was known.

**The white turban.** Here too tellings differ over who received the gift, and the shrine tells it of Dara Shikoh, who had never disclosed his love for Sheikh Abdul Qadir Jilani. One night, it is reported, the Sheikh came to him in a dream and gave him a white turban. In the morning, when he presented himself before Shah Abul Muali, the saint gave him a white turban, saying that Sheikh Abdul Qadir Jilani had asked that it be given to him.

**The rain at the Eidgah.** It is reported that during a drought at Lahore, when Hakim Afzal Agha was ruling, the Hakim gathered a very large crowd at the Eidgah outside the city, the ground for the Eid prayers, for congregational prayer for rain, and came to invite the saint. Shah Abul Muali excused himself, saying that he could not walk. The Hakim pressed him, saying that if the saint would not come, he too would remain sitting there. At last the saint told him to go, and said that it would certainly rain after two days. No rain fell for two days; on the third it rained heavily. When the Hakim returned to ask how he had known, the saint is said to have answered that had it rained on the day of the gathering, the arrogant among the worshippers might have taken it for their own wisdom, and it would later have become a cause of sin, and so Allah delayed it.

**Blessed halting places.** It is told that on his way towards Lahore every place where he halted became blessed and settled by the blessing of his steps, and that he stayed awake all night. Such, it is said, was his reverence for his pir, and for it he was raised to high rank. His miracles are said to be famous by the hundreds.

## The Setting

The shrine lies outside Mochi Gate, one of the twelve gates of the walled city of Lahore. In the nineteenth century the tomb served as a landmark for its quarter: a road ran to the saint's tomb, the tomb of a Shah Kamaita stood at its northwest corner, and the Khanqah of Abdi Shah, a Sufi lodge, lay west of its graveyard. The street on which it stands bears the saint's name, Shah Abul Muali Street, in Gowalmandi.

It is further reported that the land for Dyal Singh College in Lahore, close to the shrine, was given by the saint's descendants so that people could acquire education.
