## Overview

The shrine of Hazrat Sultan Bahu, who lived from 1630 to 1691, stands at Garh Maharaja, in the Jhang region of the Punjab. He was one of the greatest of the Punjabi Sufi poet saints and the founder of the Sarwari Qadiri order, his own branch of the Qadiri order of Sufis, or Muslim mystics. His shrine, twice moved and rebuilt as the river Chenab shifted its course, is a major centre of pilgrimage in central Punjab.

Sultan Bahu wrote prolifically in Persian, but he is loved above all for his Punjabi *abyat*, quatrains that end each line with the long, sighing exhalation of the divine name, *Hoo*. They are sung across the Punjab in a soaring traditional style.

## The Saint

Sultan Bahu was born in 1630 in the Shorkot region of Jhang, into a family of the Awan tribe, and his line of descent reaches Hazrat Ali through twenty nine intermediaries. Accounts of the year of his birth differ.

His father, Bayazid Muhammad, was an official of the Mughal administration, who held the rank of *mansabdar*, an officer of the Mughal imperial service, in Kohistan on behalf of the Emperor Shahjahan; accounts of the father's name differ. He was a *hafiz*, one who has memorised the entire Quran, a scholar of his time and strict in the *shariat*, the Islamic law. From Kohistan he came to Multan and settled there, and when orders were issued to send him back to Kohistan he refused. From Multan he came to Shorkot, made his home and received a *jagir*, a grant of land, there; and there he died.

His mother, Bibi Rasti, was a woman of great piety, famous for her sanctity and her abstinence, and she is herself remembered as a saint. Tradition credits her with his very name: Bahu, "with *Hoo*", the one who is with God. In his own words: "God's mercy be on my mother, that she named me 'Bahu'." Accounts of how he came by the name differ.

It is told that while he was still at the breast he would not drink milk by day in Ramadan, the month of fasting, as though he were keeping the fast, and he is said to have been a saint from the womb. His first teaching and training were under his mother's supervision.

Drawn from his youth to the inward life, he received initiation in the Qadiri order at the hand of Sayyid Abdul Rahman of Delhi, and, tradition holds, attained his spiritual completion with extraordinary swiftness. Tradition tells the way to that initiation in stages, and the first is set at the court of the Prophet. It is told that Hazrat Ali, the Imam of the saints, presented him there, and that the Prophet honoured him with *bai'at*, the pledge by which a disciple is initiated into a Sufi order, and then gave him into the charge of Abd al-Qadir Jilani, the Ghaus al-Azam, the greatest helper, founder of the Qadiri order. There, the story goes, he obtained stations beyond telling.

When he was then urged to take *bai'at* with a perfect *shaykh*, a Sufi master, he objected, the story goes on, that after *bai'at* at the Prophet's hand one should not take it from another. His mother answered that this world is a world of means, and that in this world too *bai'at* at the hand of a perfect *shaykh* is needful. He replied that then she was *murshid* enough for him, a *murshid* being a spiritual guide; but she told him that women are not permitted to take anyone's *bai'at*. When he asked where a *murshid* was to be sought, she pointed to the east.

The first *shaykh* he came to was Hazrat Shah Habibullah, who laid down a condition: the seeker of truth must first be free of goods and possessions, and then set foot upon this path. He went home and divested himself of his goods and property. Shah Habibullah then sent him on, telling him that the blessing he deserved was not with him, but that he would show him the sign of his destination: he was to go into the service of Shah Habibullah's own *shaykh*, Abdul Rahman Qadiri, and hold fast to the hem of his robe.

As Shah Habibullah had directed, he came to Delhi. There, it is said, Sayyid Abdul Rahman Qadiri learned of his coming by *kashf*, inner unveiling, and sent a man to bring him. The master took him into seclusion and, it is told, brought him through the stages of the path in a moment, and he was honoured with *bai'at* and with the *khirqa*, the patched cloak of the Sufi, as the mark of his *khilafat*, his authority to guide others.

In Delhi, it is said, he would walk the bazaars, and by his glance whoever he looked upon attained God in a moment. His *pir*, his spiritual master, summoned him, the story continues, and told him that he had been given a special blessing and had made that special blessing common. He answered that he had wished to put the special blessing he had received to the test, to learn how precious it was and what its nature was, and the *pir*, the story ends, enriched him with further blessing.

A chain of masters runs from him back through ten links to Shaykh Abd al-Qadir Jilani. It begins with a Sayyid Abd al-Rahman, from whom he received instruction, *talqin*, and each link received it in turn from the next: Sayyid Abd al-Jalil, Sayyid Abd al-Baqa, Sayyid Abd al-Sattar, Sayyid Abd al-Fattah, Sayyid Najm al-Din Burhanpuri, Muhammad Sadiq, Sayyid Abd al-Jabbar, Sayyid Abd al-Razzaq and Shaykh Abd al-Qadir Jilani.

He founded his own branch of the Qadiri order, the Sarwari Qadiri, which spread widely after him; accounts of the name of his branch differ. It spread through his *khalifas*, his deputies, and one of the well known among them was Hazrat Momin Shah Gilani of Sindh.

From Delhi he returned to Shorkot and gave himself to teaching, instruction and guidance. In one of his own books, the *Kalid al-Tawhid*, he names the fort of Shorkot as the place where he lived. He had no outward livelihood: twice he cultivated the land of the *jagir* and did not reap the crop, and he lived on *tawakkul*, complete trust in God.

He had four wives, and from the four eight sons were born. The youngest, Sultan Hayat Muhammad, died in childhood; among the others were Sultan Nur Muhammad, Sultan Wali Muhammad, Sultan Latif Muhammad, Sultan Salih Muhammad, Sultan Ishaq Muhammad, Sultan Fath Muhammad and Sultan Sharif Ahmad.

He died in 1691 and was buried in his homeland; accounts of the year of his death differ.

## Poetry

Sultan Bahu's Punjabi poetry takes the form of the *siharfi*, the Golden Alphabet, a poem whose stanzas follow the letters of the alphabet in turn, and, most famously, the *abyat*: quatrains in which every line closes on the ecstatic syllable *Hoo*, the sound of the breath and of the divine name, so that the poems are literally sung on the rhythm of *dhikr*, the remembrance of God in repeated recitation. Their theme is the burning of divine love, *ishq*, the primacy of the inward reality over outward form, and the indispensable role of the spiritual guide, the *murshid*.

The most famous of all begins:

اَلف اللہ چنبے دی بُوٹی، مُرشد مَن وِچ لائی ھُو

*Alif, Allah, is a jasmine plant that my master has planted within my heart, Hoo.*

In another English version, the whole of this first stanza runs:

*Alif: Allah is like the jasmine plant which the preceptor planted in my heart, o Hu! / By water and the gardener of negation and positive statement it remained near the jugular vein and everywhere, o Hu! / It spread fragrance inside when it approached the time of blossoming, o Hu! / May the efficient preceptor live long, says Bahu, who planted this plant, o Hu!*

The stanza is a lesson in remembrance. Its lines point to the recollection of the divine name, planted in the mystic's heart by his *pir*. The water of negation and affirmation is the *lā* and the *illā* of the profession of faith, its negative and its positive assertion, as they are used in *dhikr* in the Qadiri order to which Sultan Bahu belonged, and it is this water that makes the plant, God, grow in the heart. The jugular vein alludes to the Quran, Sura 50:16.

In another of his verses he sings that a single lesson of love outweighs all the learning of the schools, and that the heart in which the Beloved dwells needs no other pilgrimage. He is counted among the Punjabi Sufi poets who looked askance at scholars:

*You read and read, became a learned scholar, a seeker of gold / One hundred thousand books you read, but the cruel self didn't die / Without dispossession no one killed the thief within* (translated by Naveed Alam)

In one performance a singer sang a text of his in Siraiki, drawing out its vowels in the singing; among its lines are these:

*Ilm paṛhe-e aśrāf na-a thīnḍā / Pītal dā soṇā-ā mū-ū-l nā theve-e / Shūm t sakha kadhā-a na theve-e / Alī binā-ā imān nahĩ Bāhū-ū-ū / Toṛe dafan vic Medīnā hū-ū*

*One does not become noble from reading books / Thou cannot buy gold for the price of copper / The miser will never become generous / Without Ali there is no faith-O Bahu / For i-t is buried far in Medina hu*

In a second text the singer drew her words from Sultan Bahu to speak of the caretakers of the faith, comparing them with the genuine believer:

*Paṛh paṛh ilm hazār kitāb- ālim hoe sāre hū / Ik harf iśq dā na paṛh jāṇan / Bhulan phiran bicāre hū*

*They read books and think they are scholars / They can't read a word of love / The poor souls are lost*

*Lakh nigāh je ālim vekhe kise kandhī na caṛhī hū / Hik nigāh je aśiq vekhe lakh hazār tare hū*

*Thousands of scholars have we seen but none ever carried them on their shoulders / But when we see even a single lover, he appears like a myriad stars*

Two of his sayings turn on the first letter of the alphabet, the *alif*:

*Who has studied the alif, needs not read the chapter b*

And in his Golden Alphabet he teaches:

*True salvation belongs to those who put a mim to the alif!*

Bahu's verse is at once fiercely devotional and gloriously musical, and the singing of his *abyat*, with the long drawn *Hoo*, is one of the characteristic sounds of Punjabi Sufi devotion.

## His Books and Teaching

A prolific author, he is credited with a great many works on the mystical path, and he composed the Punjabi verses that made him immortal. He wrote many books on mysticism and on *tawhid*, the oneness of God, all of them in Arabic and Persian except his *abyat* and his *siharfi*, which are in Punjabi. Accounts of his learning differ, but by the 1870s he was remembered as a great and deeply learned scholar, who united the outward and the inward sciences.

Twenty four works are ascribed to him: *Ain al-Faqr*, *Ganj al-Asrar*, *Kalid al-Tawhid*, *Nur al-Huda*, *Muhabbat al-Asrar*, *Shams al-Arifin*, *Aurang Shahi*, *Asrar-e Qadiri*, *Taufiq-e Hidayat*, *Mujalasat al-Nabi*, *Tegh-e Barahna*, *Risala-e Ruhi*, *Qurb-e Didar*, *Kalid-e Jannat*, *Mahakk al-Faqr Kabir*, *Mahakk al-Faqr Saghir*, *Miftah al-Ashiqin*, *Kashf al-Asrar*, *Amir al-Kaunain*, *Jami al-Asrar*, *Ain al-Asrar*, *Qutb al-Aqtab*, *Muhkam al-Fuqara* and *Hujjat al-Asrar*. He wrote poetry in Persian as well, and left a *diwan*, a collection of poems, in that language. The *Diwan-e Bahu* and the *Abyat-e Bahu* are his memorials.

His books speak of the world, the kinds of *murshid*, the *faqir*, or Sufi mystic, listening to music, perpetual remembrance, poverty, following the Prophet, sayings and litanies. In his *Ganj al-Asrar* he describes kinds of *murshid*, and the third of them attains perfection in rank and office, and by that advancement reaches the rank of Pharaoh. He is as severe on the *pir* who lacks inward power and on the *pir* who leads by inheritance. The *pir* who has no inward power, who does not watch over his disciple at every moment, who cannot hold him back from sin, and who does not pray and plead for him before God and the Prophet in his death agony, should not be called a *pir*. And one who leads on the strength of being a *shaykh*'s son, without following the Prophet, he writes in the *Kashf al-Asrar*, will go astray himself and lead others astray. To be a *pir* and *murshid*, he adds, is no ordinary work, and the end of greed and envy is disgrace.

In the *Ganj al-Asrar* he speaks against listening to music: whoever listens to *rag*, melody, his heart is dead and his self alive, and the people of pleasure are dead of heart and alive of self. Perpetual remembrance he defines as the hidden *dhikr*, and he likens it to salt: through the name of Allah it runs through the whole being as salt pervades what is eaten and drunk.

On generosity he writes that by giving, the due of God's creatures is paid, and it is shown how far the man of wealth loves his wealth against God; and that the world, silver and gold are the slaves of the *faqir* who knows God. Among the litanies he teaches is the *Dua-e Saifi*, a prayer, given with the conditions under which it takes effect.

## The Shrine and Devotion

Sultan Bahu's shrine has a history bound to the wandering of the Chenab: the river's floods forced the tomb to be moved and rebuilt more than once. Accounts of where his grave has lain differ, but the present shrine stands at Garh Maharaja, a large and much visited complex.

The shrine keeps the round of daily prayer, of *qawwali*, the devotional music of the Sufis, and of the singing of his *abyat*, and it keeps the *langar*, the free communal kitchen. It draws its greatest crowds at the annual *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of the saint's death, held in the spring, when devotees gather in vast numbers from across the Punjab to sing his verses and seek his blessing. The Sarwari Qadiri order he founded maintains his memory and his teaching.

His poetry lives in performance beyond the shrine too. His spirit was among those invoked at the outset of performances, and at a concert in Islamabad two items on the programme bore his name: one under his name alone, in Punjabi and Siraiki, and one under his name with those of Shah Latif and Bulleh Shah, in Siraiki, Sindhi, Punjabi and Urdu. A Punjabi *siharfi* has been composed in his honour.

## Legends

Other wonders are told of him besides those of his infancy and of his path to his master. It is told that he turned earth to gold. A man who had come to ask something of him, the story goes, found him at the plough, and was set to plough in his place while the saint went aside to relieve himself. Returning, it is said, the saint struck upon the ground the clods with which he had cleansed himself, and the earth turned to gold. He told the man to take as much gold as he wished, and the man, the story ends, went home with much gold, his errand fulfilled.

It is told, too, that seventy years after his death, when the Chenab was in flood, his coffin was taken out of the grave, and all who saw it were astonished that his whole body was sound.

By the 1870s it was said that the grace of his path had flowed so freely that thousands of people had benefited from it, and that all the birds of that wild country, too, made the *dhikr* of *Ya Hu*, "O He".

## The Setting

In Aurangzeb's reign Shorkot marked the northern boundary of the *suba*, the province, of Multan, and the Chenab joined the Bihat near the town of Jhang Siyalan, the home of Hir, who is famed for her love of Ranjha.

In Waris Shah's romance of Hir, she is the beautiful and witty daughter of Cucak Siyal, the powerful Jat chief of Jhang Siyal, a vast agricultural territory to the southwest of Lahore. The lovers Hir and Ranjha are said to be buried in a single grave at Jhang, and their shrine has become a place of pilgrimage. The story was first written by a poet from Jhang, Damodar Das Arora, in the reign of the Mughal Emperor Akbar, and in the folk culture of the Punjab the Chenab is known as the river of love.

## Legacy

Sultan Bahu ranks with Shah Hussain, Bulleh Shah and Waris Shah among the immortals of Punjabi poetry, and his *abyat*, with their unmistakable refrain of *Hoo*, are among the most widely sung of all Sufi verses in the language. With Baba Farid and Shah Hussain he is counted among the Punjabi Sufi poets who came before Bulleh Shah.

By the 1870s his sayings and verses in the Punjabi language were very famous. He was counted then among the saints of the Punjab as a man of ecstasy and intoxication, of love and devotion, of sincerity and purity; his fame in the Punjab was complete, and there was no one who did not acknowledge his sanctity.

His verse has been read in other traditions too. In the introduction to a Hindu Sufi's breviary, a book of daily prayers, parallels are drawn with Sultan Bahu's poetry, with Abd al-Qadir Jilani and with the Guru Granth Sahib, the Sikh scripture. His poems have reached English readers under the title *Death Before Dying*, a common refrain in Sufi poetry.

As poet, prolific author and founder of an enduring order, he shaped the spiritual life of central Punjab, and his shrine at Garh Maharaja remains a great centre of the devotion he did so much to inspire.
