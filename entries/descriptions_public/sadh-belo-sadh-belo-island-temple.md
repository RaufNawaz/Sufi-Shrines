## Overview

Sadh Belo is a temple complex on a wooded island in the River Indus, between the twin cities of Sukkur and Rohri in Sindh. One of the most important living centres of Hindu worship in the province, it belongs to the *Udasi* tradition, the ascetic order that traces its lineage to Sri Chand, elder son of Guru Nanak, the founder of the Sikh faith, and so it stands where Hindu and Sikh devotion meet. Pilgrims reach it by boat across the river, and its annual festival gathers Hindus from across Sindh and beyond.

The complex spreads across two connected islets, with temples, shrines, *samadhis*, the memorial tombs of holy men, gardens, a communal kitchen and living quarters. It was founded in the early nineteenth century amid the palms and greenery that give the island its name.

## Baba Bankhandi and the Island

The founding of Sadh Belo is attributed to the *Udasi* saint Baba Bankhandi Maharaj, born Balchand Sharma, whose origins are variously traced to Kero Khetar near Delhi or to Nepal. As a young spiritual seeker, still in his teens, he is said to have arrived in 1823 in Sukkur, then a flourishing trading hub of the subcontinent. Drawn to the tranquil, forested island in the Indus, he settled there and made it a place of meditation and worship, and it came to be known as *Sadhu Belo*, "the woods of the sage". Under Baba Bankhandi and the line of *gaddi nashin*, the spiritual successors who followed him, the island grew into a substantial religious establishment.

Baba Bankhandi is credited with establishing places of worship dedicated to Hindu deities including Annapurna, Hanuman, Ganesh and Shiv Shankar, while also providing for the *Guru Granth Sahib*, the scripture of the Sikhs, and the *Bhagavad Gita*, a Hindu scripture, to be venerated side by side, an expression of the inclusive *Udasi* spirit.

Much of the architecture seen today, the principal temple among it, was raised somewhat later. The main shrine is generally dated to the closing years of the nineteenth century, when it was built under the eighth successor in the line, Sant Harnam Das, and it was further embellished in the decades that followed.

The temple is also counted among the works of princely patrons. The amirs, or rulers, of Khairpur were themselves staunch Shia Muslims, and in the nineteenth century they built beautiful *imambaras*, halls for the Shia rites of mourning. That did not prevent them from being generous patrons of religious buildings of every kind, and the famous *Udasi* temple at Sadh Belo was created thanks to a gift of land.

The principal island, Sadh Belo itself, holds the temples, verandahs and a kitchen in the manner of a *langar*, the free communal kitchen, which fed pilgrims and residents. The adjoining island, sometimes called Deen Belo, holds *samadhis*, a park and the Rishi Nol *mandir*, or temple. Reached by boat across the Indus, the complex offered pilgrims a sacred world complete in itself in the midst of the river, its white temples and shaded courtyards standing out against the surrounding waters.

## The Udasis and the Path of Nanak

The character of Sadh Belo flows from its roots in the *Udasipanth*, the *Udasi* path. This religious and ascetic movement grew from the teachings of Sri Chand, the elder son of Guru Nanak, and blended devotional streams in a way that resists neat sectarian classification, honouring Sikh scripture and Hindu deities alike and cultivating a tradition of renunciation, pilgrimage and hospitality. Sadh Belo became one of the foremost seats of *Udasi* religious life in Sindh.

The temple belongs to a wider world of devotion in the province. In Sindh many Nanakpanthis, followers of the Nanakpanth or "the path of Nanak", belonged to a subgroup known as the *Udasis*, and in the nineteenth century the Nanakpanth was probably the dominant religious path among Hindu Sindhis. Among the Lohanas, a trading community, and related mercantile groups, two main cults were followed, the Nanakpanth and the Daryapanth, the path of the river.

In the colonial period the religious life of the province was recast, and several important Nanakpanthi *darbars*, or shrines, were created and developed throughout Sindh. Some had been created in the late eighteenth century, but the second half of the nineteenth century saw a renaissance of the Nanakpanthi *darbars*. The word *darbar* itself was used by Sikhs, Muslims and Hindus alike.

Many Lohanas in Sindh follow the teachings of Nanak, which somewhat obscured their religious affiliation in the eyes of colonial officers: in the census of 1881 Lohanas from Shikarpur and Hyderabad were counted as Sikhs, while ten years later, in the census of 1891, they were classified as Hindus. In the wake of the Singh Sabha, a movement of Sikh reform, the Sindhi Nanakpanthis were not included in the Sikh community, and they identified themselves as a Hindu religious sect, although the *Udasis* of Sindh have also been described as a heterodox Sikh tradition.

Today many middle and upper class Hindus in Sindh follow the path of Nanak, and it is common to cover one's head before walking into a *tikana*, as Sindhi calls the temple containing the *Guru Granth Sahib*. The Nanakpanthi temples of Sindh and southern Punjab hold portraits of Guru Nanak alongside Hindu deities, and these communities are found in very large numbers in Sindh. The communities who refused the Khalsa, the order of initiated Sikhs, and called themselves followers of Guru Nanak still exist in Sindh and in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, and baptised Sikhs look down upon them.

## Worship and Festivals

The island remains the focus of an annual festival that gathers Hindus from across Sindh and beyond. Boats ferry the pilgrims to the island for days of worship, music and communal meals, and its kitchen has long fed pilgrims and residents alike.

The river crossing was the pilgrims' way to the island in the colonial period too. At the time of the riots at Sukkur, the local Hindus opposed the rehabilitation of a site in the harbour on the Indus, because it was from that harbour that pilgrims reached the sacred site of Sadh Belo.

The temple's hospitality also reached a pilgrim bound for the goddess Hinglaj. On his way, the pilgrim, Shastri, went to Sadh Belo, which he was told was a *tirtha*, a Hindu place of pilgrimage, on an island in the Indus in the city of Sukkur. There the *mahant*, the superior of the monastery, its abbot, grew very fond of Shastri and gave him supplies and advice for his pilgrimage. The advice was practical. The abbot told him to carry plenty of onions in case drinking water ran short, for the juice of an onion would help stave off nausea and dizziness as well as provide liquid.

## Partition and After

The Partition of 1947 dispersed much of Sindh's Hindu population. By early November 1947, 246,000 Hindus had already left Sindh by sea, air or rail, and by 1 January 1948 nearly 500,000 people were estimated to have sought refuge in India; many Hindu Sindhis began to leave especially after the Karachi riots of January 1948.

Partition put the future of Sindh's many shrines in doubt, yet Sadh Belo endured as one of the most significant functioning Hindu sites in Pakistan. It has continued to be maintained by its custodians and to draw devotees, and its survival has made it a cherished anchor for the Hindu community of the region. Accounts differ over what Partition meant for Sadh Belo, but on its island it is counted a rare instance of continuity across that rupture.

Across Sindh, the *darbars* left behind were most of the time preserved under different arrangements for their management, often with *gaddi nashins* or followers in India paying keepers, and despite the fluctuating diplomatic relations between Pakistan and India it was always possible to travel across the border. Sindh is home to the majority of Pakistan's Hindus, and in the early twenty first century government funding for the places of minority communities could be seen in a wave of restorations of temples across the province.

## The Setting

Sukkur, Rohri and Shikarpur form a triangle of northern Sindh rich in sanctuaries. On both sides of the Indus there are remarkable ones such as Khwaja Khizr, on an island, Sadh Belo, on another island, and the *dargah*, the Sufi shrine complex, of Sachal Sarmast.

Between December 1939 and February 1940 there were riots at Sukkur, and they also occurred beyond the city, in Rohri and in other parts of Sindh such as Lakki. In Sehwan, unlike other cities of northern Sindh such as Sukkur after the Manzilgah riots, no communal violence had ever been registered.

Rohri was the birthplace of the Sufi Rochaldas, born there in 1879 to a family remembered as modest, and of the poet Bedil, also a Sufi, who died in 1872, a prolific writer in five languages. Janullah of Rohri, a mystic of the Suhrawardi order of Sufis, was one of the outstanding Persian poets of Sindh in the second half of the eighteenth century. The singer Alan Faqir is said to have sung on the buses and trains between Rohri and Sehwan Sharif in his youth.

In the *Janamsakhis*, the traditional stories of Guru Nanak's life, the Guru comes from Multan to Sukkur, and from there turns southwards along the Indus. Sukkur also appears in Mughal history. In the pursuit of Dara Shikoh, news reached the pursuing armies that Dara, having passed Bhakkar, had halted at Sukkur on the 25th of Muharram, the first month of the Islamic year. The Mughal commanders who crossed towards Sukkur lost many beasts to the thickness of the jungle, the press of thorny trees and the hard stages of the road; the baggage did not come up, and on the 6th of Safar, the second month of the Islamic year, they reached Sukkur. Shaikh Mir stayed a day at Sukkur, where the *zamindars*, the landholders, told him that a road branches off towards Qandahar twenty five *kos*, an old measure of distance, from Sukkur, and a *faujdar*, a military governor, was appointed for Sukkur.

## Legacy

Sadh Belo stands as a testament to Sindh's plural religious heritage and to the enduring vitality of the *Udasi* tradition on the banks of the Indus. A place of active pilgrimage and a monument to the syncretic devotion of Baba Bankhandi and his successors, the island temple remains one of the most beloved sacred sites of Hindu Sindh, its shrines rising from the river much as they have for two centuries.

Its name now stands in two places. Among the Sindhi *darbars* of India is a Sadh Belo in Mumbai, in Maharashtra, which was transferred from Sukkur, though in the Mumbai Sadh Belo it is difficult to see any architectural continuity with the Sadh Belo on its island in the Indus.
