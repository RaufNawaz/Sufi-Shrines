## Overview

At Uch Sharif, the ancient town near the confluence of the Chenab and the Sutlej in southern Punjab, lies the shrine of Sayyid Jalaluddin Surkh-Posh Bukhari, the saint who made Uch one of the great centres of Sufism in the subcontinent. Known as Surkh-Posh, the Red-Robed, he came from Bukhara to settle at Uch, where he propagated the Suhrawardi order of Sufis, or Muslim mystics, and founded the celebrated family of the Bukhari sayyids, descendants of the Prophet Muhammad. Through his descendants, among them his grandson Makhdoom Jahaniyan Jahangasht, his line shaped the religious history of the region for centuries. His shrine anchors the remarkable cluster of tombs that has made Uch Sharif a place of pilgrimage and a treasury of Islamic architecture.

## The Saint

Sayyid Jalaluddin was born at Bukhara, in Central Asia, into a family of sayyids who traced their descent from the Prophet; accounts of the year of his birth differ, and the year the shrine keeps is 1192. His genealogy runs in ten links back to Imam Muhammad Taqi. Sayyid Ali Asghar, his seventh ancestor, had two sons, Sayyid Abd Allah and Sayyid Ismail, and from the descendants of Abd Allah came the family of the Bukhari sayyids, and from those of Ismail another family of sayyids. In Hindustan his standing rested on that lineage: all the sayyids, shaikhs, *amirs*, or nobles, and sultans treated him with deference because of the soundness of his descent.

He migrated southward in the age of the Mongol upheavals. In that age Mongol pressure drove waves of Turkish settlers into the subcontinent, many of whom settled for good in the cities of the Punjab, such as Lahore, Dipalpur and Multan, and Balban's garrisoning of those cities drew in thousands of refugees fleeing before the advancing Mongols, artisans, merchants, petty officials and the like. The Mongol invasions of the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries devastated parts of the Punjab.

Accounts of his road from Bukhara differ, but he is remembered as coming first to Bhakkar in Sindh, where local jealousy forced him to move on, and as settling at last at Uch, which he made the base of his mission. There he remained until his death, preaching and guiding, and the family of the Bukhari sayyids that he founded there became one of the most influential religious lineages of the Punjab and Sindh.

He was a disciple of Shaikh Bahauddin Zakariya of Multan and one of his *khalifas*, the spiritual deputies a master authorises to guide others. He is remembered as a propagator of the Suhrawardi way, the Sufi order founded by Shihab al-Din Suhrawardi, connected to the great Multan line of his master. Bahauddin Zakariya died in 1263, and his tomb is in Multan city. The Qalandari and Jawaliqi dervishes, wandering ascetics who passed through Multan, often came into conflict with Bahauddin Zakariya in his lifetime.

He is remembered as Surkh-Posh, the Red-Robed, for the red garment he habitually wore, and he is also called simply Surkh, the Red. He is also known by the fuller name Sayyid Jalal al-Din Sher Shah, titled Mir Surkh Bukhari Uchi, *Uchi* meaning "of Uch".

Accounts of his sons differ, but he is most often said to have had three: Sayyid Ahmad Kabir, Sayyid Baha al-Din, and Sayyid Sadr al-Din, known as Muhammad Ghaus.

He died at Uch, full of years, on the nineteenth day of Jumada al-Awwal, the fifth month of the Islamic year, in 690 of the Hijri calendar, the year that corresponds to 1291; accounts of the year of his death differ. Most accounts place his grave at Uch, where he was buried and where his shrine stands.

## The Bukhari Line

Through Jalaluddin Surkh-Posh and his descendants, Uch Sharif became a spiritual capital of the southern Punjab, rivalling Multan as a seat of the Suhrawardi order and producing a succession of saints of the first rank. Chief among his descendants was his grandson and namesake, Sayyid Jalaluddin, known as Makhdoom Jahaniyan Jahangasht, one of the most revered Suhrawardi saints of the fourteenth century, his title Makhdoom Jahaniyan meaning one "whom all the inhabitants of the world serve". The grandson was born on 15 Sha'ban 707 AH, a date that fell on 9 February 1308, Sha'ban being the eighth month of the Islamic year, and he took his *bay'a*, the pledge of initiation into a Sufi order, in the Suhrawardi order from a son of the saint.

In the grandson's time Uch was, for a while, the centre of the Suhrawardi order, chiefly thanks to him. In his day the order spread from Uch to Gujarat, the Punjab and Kashmir, even extending its influence to the Delhi of Sultan Firuz Shah Tughluq. Makhdoom Jahaniyan is said to have written in almost every religious field. Uch, near Bahawalpur, was the base of the second period of the Suhrawardi order.

The line continued through generations of *sajjada-nashins*, the hereditary custodians of the saint's seat, who carried great religious and worldly authority. Among the saint's descendants were Sayyid Haji Abd al-Wahhab Bukhari Dehlvi, "of Delhi", and Mauj Darya Bukhari, who was the *sajjada-nashin* of his forefathers' *khanqah*, a *khanqah* being a Sufi lodge where disciples gather. Bibi Jawindi, whose tomb is among the tiled tombs of Uch, is said to have been the saint's great granddaughter, though accounts of who she was differ.

## The Shrine

The shrine of Jalaluddin Surkh-Posh Bukhari is a working *darbar*, or saint's shrine, and the ancestral seat of the Bukhari sayyids. Standing amid the celebrated monuments of Uch Sharif, it is at once a place of living devotion and a link to the deep medieval history of Sufism in the subcontinent.

On one visit the group of shrines of the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, which stood on a mound, was reached through the market, and the saint's shrine was also situated on that mound. It was protected by a thick wall with barbed wire on top, built shortly before to protect it from terrorist attacks. Almost all the visitors there, who numbered in the hundreds, were heading towards this shrine. An old banyan tree stood in the centre, and a banner depicted the saint with Bahauddin Zakariya, Baba Farid and Lal Shahbaz Qalandar; Guru Nanak, the founder of the Sikh faith, was not among them.

Next to the shrine, within the same compound, stood a mosque, itself a historical building. A *madrassa*, a school of religious learning, used to run at the shrine, and a board of the Archaeology Department recorded that Baha-ul-Halim taught there. In the market at the Gilani shrine a poster of the saint was on sale.

## Devotion and Festivals

The shrine is visited throughout the year by pilgrims who come to honour the founder of the line and to seek his blessing. It keeps the round of prayer, *qawwali*, the devotional music of the Sufis, and *langar*, the free communal kitchen, and its annual *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of the saint's death, draws devotees from across the region.

On one visit women, it seemed, were not allowed in, and looked on from the boundary. A man showed a visitor three rooms where, he said, Baba Farid, Bahauddin Zakariya and Lal Shahbaz Qalandar had meditated, put a green shawl with Quranic verses on it round the visitor's neck, and offered water; the visitor, expecting to be asked for a donation, walked off. People stood by the grave for blessings, another man waited with a green box, and devotees offered their donations to the saint.

## Guru Nanak and Uch

Sikh tradition holds that Guru Nanak was invited to Uch Sharif by the saint himself, when the two met at Kandahar on the way back from the Hajj, the pilgrimage to Mecca. Nanak is said to have made the visit on his way to Sindh, after he had settled down. The story is doubted, since the saint had lived in the thirteenth century.

Of Nanak's traces in the town, little was left. On one visit the well of Nanak lay hidden within a field, shielded by a grove of trees, abandoned, with juice tetrapaks and wrappers floating in its black water. Beside it was a grassy mound, which had been the Gurdwara of Nanak, a Sikh place of worship; nothing of it remained.

## The Setting

Uch Sharif is an ancient town built on a hill overlooking the confluence of the Sutlej and Chenab rivers, some seventy miles from Multan and about seventy kilometres from Bahawalpur. Its name, Uch, means high or great, and it has also been called Uch Miran. The town the saint and his descendants made holy is today a dense and evocative landscape of shrines and tombs, among them the exquisite tiled tombs of Bibi Jawindi and others, and it is counted among the most important heritage sites of Pakistan. In April 1996 a visitor spent a day wandering through the magnificent blue and white tiled mosques and desolate tombs of this ancient centre of learning, trade and pilgrimage.

Uch was famous for its Gilani and Bukhari saints, who came originally from Gilan and from Bukhara in Central Asia. Uch Gilanian, the city of saints in Bahawalpur, was where the family of the poet Bulleh Shah, who were sayyids, had settled, and where he was born, and Bulleh Shah's mentor, Inayat Shah, also belonged to Uch. Muhammad Bakhsh, of a hereditary family of *qawwals*, the singers of *qawwali*, at Kasur, once said, "We came with Bulle Shah's family from Uch Sharif, and we have learned his poetry from our ancestors, who have always sung here". In the early 2000s the landowning sayyids of Bahawalpur and Multan supported the shrines of their ancestors.

A legend of the town's founding tells of Shaikh Safiu'd-Din Kaziruni, who was ordered to mount a camel, travel in whatever direction the animal led him and remain where it finally halted. In the legend, the camel halted in the middle of a desert, and there the Shaikh founded a town later called Uch. Another legend of Uch tells of a contest in flight with a yogi, a Hindu ascetic, and all the stories of three generations of the Sufis of Uch tell of conflicts with Hindu yogis.

Uch was also a seat of the Gilani Qadiris, Sufis of the Qadiri order, which was founded by Abd al-Qadir Gilani. The first missionaries of the Qadiri order settled at Uch in the late fifteenth century, and from there the order spread into the subcontinent, where it soon gained a firm footing, and was carried as far as Indonesia and Malaysia. Shaikh Muhammad al-Husseini al-Jilani, a native of Turkey who travelled to Khurasan and then Multan before settling with his family in Uch, founded the first Qadiri *khanqah* there, and his son, Abd al-Qadir Jilani Thani, who died in 1533, is the famous Qadiri saint of Uch Sharif. In the nineteenth century some of Abd al-Qadir Sani's descendants held the *sajjada*, the seat of a saint's successor, at Uch, and the four sons of Hazrat Muhammad Ghaus lay at Uch Sharif beside the tomb of Shah Muhammad Ghaus. A Gilani forebear of Hazrat Pir Muhammad Shah came from Uch Sharif in the time of Akbar Shah, and the elders of the Faqir family lived at Uch Sharif Sayyidan and served the sayyids of Uch.

Uch lay on the roads of armies. The first campaign of the conqueror Shihab al-Din took it and left a deputy there, and his second came again to Multan and Uch and went on to Gujarat; Mirza Pir Muhammad crossed the Sind and took Uch and Multan; and on one march toward Jodhpur, Uch was a dependency of Multan. In Aurangzeb's reign the province of Thatta ran from the borders of Multan and Uch to Thatta and Kach Makran.

On one journey to Uch Sharif, the boundary between Sindh and Punjab became a blur on the road through Bahawalpur, where the cultures of the two provinces merge: some roadside hotels were called Punjab Hotel and others Sindh Hotel. The language there, Siraiki, is an amalgamation of the two languages, and the Siraiki spoken there was more like Sindhi, while in Multan it was closer to Punjabi. Siraiki is mainly spoken in the southern Punjab, with Multan as its capital, and in the Middle Ages this area was a part of Sindh.

The older city stood on a mound, with the massive *havelis*, or mansions, of the Gilanis and the Makhdooms. On the mound were graves covered arbitrarily with the blue and white Multani tiles that had been recovered from the historical shrines there. The river Sutlej, the lost river of Pakistan, used to flow here, before it was lost after the Indus Water Treaty was signed between India and Pakistan in 1960.

## Legacy

Jalaluddin Surkh-Posh Bukhari holds a foundational place in the spiritual history of the Punjab and Sindh: the Bukhara-born saint who rooted the Suhrawardi way at Uch and fathered a dynasty of saints whose influence endured for centuries. Through his descendants, above all Makhdoom Jahaniyan Jahangasht, his line shaped the religion and society of the region, and through the shrines of Uch Sharif his memory is bound to one of the great sacred landscapes of Pakistan.

He has been called "the forefather of a long line of devout mystics and theologians". He is also counted as the third of three figures said to have met Lal at Chothanbi, and their shrines are spread across southern Punjab. In the nineteenth century the sayyids of Uch were famous for their authority in questions of descent: where a dispute over a man's descent arose and they accepted him as of sound descent, the matter was closed, and no one then dared say a word.
