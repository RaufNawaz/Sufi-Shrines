## Overview

At Shergarh, in the Okara district of the Punjab, stands the shrine of Hazrat Daud Bandagi Kirmani, a revered saint of the Qadiri order who lived from 1513 to 1575 and did much to spread that order in the Punjab. A contemporary of the Mughal emperors Humayun and Akbar and of the great saints of Lahore in his time, he made Shergarh a centre of devotion. His *darbar*, as a saint's shrine is called in the Punjab, remains a place of pilgrimage in central Punjab, and its annual *urs*, the yearly commemoration of the saint's death, gathers the faithful of the region.

## The Saint

Daud was born in 1513 into a family of Sayyids, descendants of the Prophet. He is honoured with the title *Bandagi*, "servitude", which marks his devotion, and with the name Kirmani, from his family's origins; he is also known more simply as Shaikh Daud Kirmani, or Shaikh Daud of Shergarh. His father was Sayyid Fath Allah Kirmani and his grandfather Sayyid Mubarak, and among his forebears were Safi al-Din Adam Kirmani and Sayyid Taqi al-Din Ahmad. Family trees differ over the upper links of the line; the one held to be correct runs through Sayyid Muhammad Aaraji, son of Sayyid Musa Mubarqa, son of Imam Muhammad Taqi, son of Imam Ali Musa Raza.

Accounts also differ over how the family came to the Punjab. It is said that his father came from Arabia to India, settled at Haibatpur Patti, and then moved to the *qasba*, or small town, of Chunian. His father died four months before Daud was born. He took a full share of the outward sciences from Maulana Ismail Lahori, and then turned to the inner sciences.

He gave himself to the Sufi path and became a *shaykh*, a master, of the Qadiri order, the line of Shaikh Abd al-Qadir Jilani of Baghdad, at a time when that order was taking firm root in the subcontinent. He was famous among the Qadiri elders of the Punjab as a master of both *hal* and *qal*, the inner spiritual state and the spoken word, and of both *shariat* and *tariqat*, the religious law and the Sufi path. He was at worship from evening until morning and kept awake the whole night, passing it now standing, now prostrate, now bowing, now seated. It is told that once, while he was in *i'tikaf*, a retreat of secluded worship, Hazrat Baha al-Din Hajrawi came to meet him, and because of the retreat the meeting could not take place.

From the abundance of his austerities a special bond, or *nisbat*, arose in him with Ghaus al-Azam, the title by which Shaikh Abd al-Qadir Jilani is honoured. With Ghaus al-Azam's inner permission, it is said, he presented himself to Sayyid Hamid Ganj Bakhsh, reached completion on the path, and received the *khirqa*, the Sufi robe, of *khilafat*, the rank of a spiritual deputy. He was one of the two perfect *khalifas*, or deputies, of Sayyid Hamid Ganj Bakhsh, the other being Sayyid Sher Shah Multani, and through Sayyid Hamid his chain of masters runs back to Ghaus al-Azam.

From his base at Shergarh he taught, guided disciples and spread the Qadiri way across the Punjab, living through the turbulent early decades of Mughal rule. He was among the notable saints of the generation of Shah Hussain of Lahore, part of the flowering of Punjabi Sufism in the sixteenth century. He died in 1575 and was buried at Shergarh.

Two chronograms, phrases whose letters give a year, mark the year of his death, 982 AH. The first is a phrase from which the date of his death appears:

پیر حق پرست

*A chronogram on the saint's death.*

The second gives the same year:

مشتاق منان

*A second chronogram on the same death.*

## Disciples and Successors

The disciple through whom he is best remembered is Shah Abul Muali of Lahore. Daud was Abul Muali's uncle, his father in law and his *pir*, his spiritual guide, and Abul Muali was his *murid*, a disciple pledged to him, and his distinguished *khalifa*. Abul Muali's father had already settled the family at Shergarh, and Abul Muali was born there. He came from Delhi to Shergarh to enter his master's service, and spent thirty two years with him. Abul Muali wrote of his master in a work called the *Naghmat-e Daudi*.

The condition of his training was that he should live always with his *pir*, who told him that in this way he would gain what he could not gain elsewhere in a whole lifetime. The master is said to have read his disciple's state by *kashf*, the inner unveiling of what is hidden, and to have consoled him with words spoken in Persian: today or tomorrow he would bring Abul Muali into the holy and exalted presence, and Abul Muali should wait for the time. It is also said that when the disciple set out from his master at Shergarh, at his halts on the road he would face towards Shergarh. After his master's death Abul Muali came to Lahore.

A second line of succession ran through Shah Abu Ishaq, who was Daud's *murid* and *khalifa* in the Qadiri *silsila*, the chain of spiritual descent. From Shah Abu Ishaq the line passed to Shaikh Shams-ud-Din and on to Shah Bilawal, whose chain of masters runs back through Daud Bandagi and Sayyid Hamid to Ghaus al-Azam.

## The Shrine and Its Devotion

The shrine of Daud Bandagi Kirmani at Shergarh is a centre of pilgrimage kept by his successors, where devotees come to recite the *Fatiha*, the opening chapter of the Qur'an, and to seek the saint's blessing. The place takes its sacred identity from the *darbar* of the saint who made it a seat of the Qadiri order. By the 1870s his *mazar*, the tomb of a saint, was a place of pilgrimage for God's creatures, and every year a great crowd gathered there.

Beside the grave, the footprints of the saint are engraved on a small marble platform. The shrine keeps the customary devotions: prayer, *qawwali*, the devotional music of the Sufis, and *langar*, the free communal kitchen.

At the annual festival early in the twenty first century, a *malang*, a wandering ascetic dervish, was seen halting at the threshold of the shrine to say a silent prayer; he touched the base of the entrance and went in. His head was shaved and his face bare of hair, his eyes were smeared with kohl, and he wore a vertical mark on his forehead, a glass earring on his right ear, thin black clothes and a thick bangle on his right ankle. Circling the grave of the saint, he stopped at the marble platform and kissed the footprints.

Iqbal Qaiser, a historian who guided a visit to the shrine, explained that *malangs* drape a shawl only when they are in public, and that among other *malangs* they wear nothing but a small *lungi*, a cloth wrapped round the waist. In the footprints he saw a likeness to the footmarks of prominent priests that become objects of veneration in Jain temples. "This is exactly like that", he said, and he thought there must be some inspiration from that particular Jain sect as well. The *malang* himself was likened to "a Digamabar Jain monk", of the Jain sect whose monks renounce clothing.

## The Setting

Shergarh, in the Okara district, lies about an hour from Lahore, on the road towards Tibba Haji Deen. On the road out of the town, a small group of ascetics was once seen walking barefoot. The eldest wore a black shawl and a green turban, with a belt of bells around his waist.

Of the Qadiri line, the first to come to the Punjab is held to have been Sayyid Muhammad Ghaus of Uch, who came by way of Khurasan to Multan and settled at Uch, which he entered in 887 AH.

## Legacy

Daud Bandagi Kirmani is remembered as one of the saints who firmly planted the Qadiri order in the Punjab of the sixteenth century, a devout *shaykh* of the age of Akbar whose shrine at Shergarh has kept his memory for four and a half centuries. He belongs to the great generation of Punjabi Sufism, and his *darbar* remains a living centre of the devotion he inspired.

His memory was kept in writing early. Besides his disciple's *Naghmat-e Daudi*, Abdul Baqi, son of Jan Muhammad, wrote the *Maqamat-e Daudi*, a work on the saint dated 1056 AH, which survives in a manuscript copied in 1201 AH. He also has a place in Abdul Qadir Badayuni's *Muntakhab al-Tawarikh*, Nizam al-Din Ahmad's *Tabaqat-e Akbari*, Abdul Haq's *Akhbar al-Akhyar*, Ghausi's *Azkar-e Abrar*, also called the *Gulzar-e Abrar*, and Dara Shikoh's *Safinat al-Auliya*.

Through his disciples his name runs on in the chains of later masters: through Shah Abul Muali, who spent thirty two years at his side, and through Shah Abu Ishaq, Shaikh Shams-ud-Din and Shah Bilawal.
