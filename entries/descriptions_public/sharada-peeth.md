## Overview

Sharada Peeth is the hallowed ruin of what was once among the most illustrious temples and centres of learning in the Indian subcontinent. It stands beside the Neelum River in the village of Sharda, in the Neelum Valley of Azad Kashmir, and was dedicated to the goddess Sharada, the Kashmiri form of *Saraswati*, deity of learning. At once a temple, a fabled university and one of the great *Shakti Peethas*, the seats of the Goddess, it holds a singular place in the cultural and religious memory of Kashmir.

The site lies at nearly 2,000 metres in the shadow of Mount Harmukh, which Kashmiri Pandits, the Hindu Brahmin community of Kashmir, revere as an abode of Shiva. Sharada Peeth gave the goddess's name both to the *Sharada* script and to Kashmir itself, long known as *Sharada Desh*, the "Land of Sharada".

## The Goddess

Sharada Peeth is counted among the *Shakti Peethas*, and by Kashmiri tradition among the eighteen *Maha Shakti Peethas*, the great seats of the Goddess. According to the legend common to all these seats, it marks a place where a part of the body of the goddess Sati fell to earth after Vishnu dismembered her corpse with his discus, to end the dance of Shiva, who was stricken with grief. At Sharada, tradition holds, it was the goddess's right hand that fell, and here she is worshipped in her form as Sharada.

The site's bond with Saraswati joined devotion and scholarship in a way rare among these seats, so that here the goddess was venerated as the very source of knowledge, letters and wisdom. Such was its renown that the great teacher Adi Shankara, in his hymn listing the eighteen seats of the Goddess, named Sharada in Kashmir alongside Kamakshi at Kanchipuram and Vishalakshi at Varanasi.

## The Temple University

Between roughly the sixth and twelfth centuries CE, Sharada Peeth flourished as one of the foremost temple universities of the subcontinent, a rival in reputation to the great monastic seats of learning elsewhere in India. Tradition and later accounts describe a renowned library that drew scholars from great distances, and the site is associated in memory with such luminaries as the philosopher Adi Shankara, the historian Kalhana, whose *Rajatarangini* is a chronicle of the kings of Kashmir, and the scholar Vairotsana. It was above all a centre of Sanskrit scholarship of the first rank, where many important manuscripts were composed and copied.

The most enduring legacy of this learning is the *Sharada* script itself, which developed and spread from this centre and was named in the shrine's honour. For centuries it was the principal script for writing Sanskrit and Kashmiri in the region, and it remains sacred to Kashmiri Pandits, used in ritual and in the writing of horoscopes. So closely was the goddess bound to the intellectual life of Kashmir that admission to the shrine's precincts came to symbolise scholarly attainment.

## Decline, Partition and the Present

The decline of Sharada Peeth unfolded over many centuries, its foremost place in learning fading after the twelfth century amid the political upheavals that swept Kashmir. Earthquakes, neglect and the passage of time reduced what had once been a great complex to the massive stone shell that survives today: walls of large ashlar blocks, squared and dressed stone, standing roofless within a walled enclosure.

The Partition of 1947 and the division of Kashmir that followed placed the shrine in territory administered by Pakistan, close to the Line of Control, which divides the parts of Kashmir held by Pakistan and by India. In effect it was cut off from the Kashmiri Pandit community, for whom it remained a spiritual lodestar.

In recent years the site has become a focus of renewed attention and of appeals for its conservation. Kashmiri Pandit organisations have campaigned for access and preservation, and there have been proposals for a pilgrimage corridor like the one opened at Kartarpur. On the Indian side of the Line of Control, a new Sharada temple was consecrated in Kupwara in 2023, seeking to rekindle the devotional link with the ancient seat.

## Legacy

Sharada Peeth endures as one of the most poignant and evocative sacred sites of the subcontinent, a ruin that once embodied the highest achievements of Kashmiri learning and piety. Sharing its name with a script, a goddess and a land, and revered across generations as a *Shakti Peetha*, it remains a powerful emblem of Kashmir's ancient identity as the "Land of Sharada", even as its weathered stones await the care their history so richly merits.
