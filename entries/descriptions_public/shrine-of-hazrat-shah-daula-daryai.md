## Overview

The shrine of Hazrat Shah Daula Daryai stands in the city of Gujrat, in the fertile plain between the Chenab and Jhelum rivers in the Punjab. It honours a Sufi saint of the Mughal period, remembered above all as a builder of bridges, wells and *sarais*, or rest houses, and as a benefactor of the poor, the traveller and the afflicted. His epithet *Daryai*, from *darya*, river, is traditionally explained by the works he built across the region's torrential streams. For nearly four centuries the *darbar*, as the shrine is called, has been a focus of popular devotion in central Punjab.

It is also the place around which one of South Asia's most persistent and troubling folk traditions grew up: that of the *chuhas*, or "Shah Daula's mice", children with microcephaly who were historically associated with the shrine.

## The Saint

Shah Daula lived in the Mughal period. His birth name is given as Qasim, and tradition places his birth around 1581. His ancestors are said to have been Afghans, though he is also claimed as a Sayyid, a descendant of the Prophet Muhammad.

Accounts of his early years differ, and so do the names given to his master. He is said to have travelled in his youth to the neighbourhood of Sialkot, where he became the disciple of a Suhrawardi *faqir*, a mystic of the Sufi order founded by Shihab al-Din Suhrawardi; his master is remembered as Shah Saidan Sarmast. He served at his master's *khanqah*, the lodge where a Sufi's disciples gather, with great devotion, spending, it is said, twelve years at his feet, and in time he was entrusted with its care.

Tradition tells that his master, as he lay dying, cast a glance full of grace on his disciple. Shah Daula's state was transformed: the chamber of his inner self was lit with the lights of gnosis, the knowledge of God, and the treasures of the unseen were revealed to him. He is said to have settled in Gujrat in the early seventeenth century, and he established his own *khanqah* there, at the place formerly known as Garhi Shah Daula.

There is no settled view of his order. His master is remembered as a Suhrawardi, but Shah Daula is also said to have been initiated into the Qadiri order, founded by Abd al-Qadir Gilani. His spiritual chain, or *silsila*, is traced back through a long line of masters, by way of Shaikh Sadr al-Din Rajan Qattal of Uch, to Shaikh Baha al-Din Multani, and he is said to have received the full measure of *faiz*, spiritual grace, from the Chishtis as well. By the 1870s he was counted among the famous saints of the Punjab: a man of lofty spiritual stations and of marvels, of *jazb*, ecstatic rapture, of *ishq*, love, of *zuhd*, renunciation of the world, and of *riyazat*, spiritual discipline, who united in himself both outward and inward gifts.

In his lifetime his household was like the court of a king, with thousands of servants, horses and palanquins waiting at his door, and a crowd of people in need was there at every hour. Multitudes came from every quarter to visit him and made abundant offerings in cash and in kind, and he gave his visitors and petitioners more than they had brought. His generosity, it was said, was such that a whole lifetime of the generosity of Hatim, the proverbial giver, would not come to a tenth of a tenth of it. He was popular with Hindus as well as Muslims, became famous for his miracles and received large gifts in kind, but he refused to accept grants of land.

He is also credited with *karamat*, the miraculous gifts of a saint, by which, it is said, he was able to check the killing of infant girls, then widespread in Jammu State.

He died at a great age in Gujrat, in 1675, though accounts of the year differ. His death is also marked by a chronogram, a phrase whose letters are read as numbers to give a date: *Khuda Dost*, Friend of God.

## The Builder of Bridges

What is most consistently remembered of Shah Daula is not doctrine but works of public benefit. He is credited with bridges over seasonal watercourses, among them, by tradition, a bridge over the Nala Dek, and with wells, mosques and rest houses along the routes of travel, at a time when such works eased both trade and pilgrimage across the Punjab. He is remembered too for his *langar*, the free communal kitchen, for his charity to the destitute, and for his care for animals and for people living with disabilities. His was a practical sanctity, honoured for the tangible relief of hardship.

When he settled in the *qasba*, the small town, of Gujrat, he built tanks, wells and mosques, and he bridged the Chenab, which came down from the hills and used to damage the town. He raised buildings and bridges in many places, above all a bridge on the highway over the river Deg, five *kos*, an old measure of distance, from Eminabad in the direction of Lahore. It was said to be so solidly built that no man of wealth could raise a building as strong, and it became a landmark: the Deg ran below Shah Daula's bridge, which stood on the highway.

Gujrat, it is said, had never been much of a place until Shah Daula settled there, and his coming made the town grow in people and prosperity. His works of public benefit and his religious buildings are said to have earned him the name *Daula*, wealth, and his bridges above all the second title, *Daryai*, one affiliated to water.

## The Shrine

His tomb, crowned by a dome and set beside a spacious mosque, dates in its essentials from the seventeenth century. It was substantially rebuilt in 1898 and has been repaired and extended since. In the reign of Aurangzeb the tomb was already a place of visitation for people of every class, and by the 1870s it was a place of pilgrimage for the people.

No land was attached to the shrine, so it had no regular income, and its holy men depended entirely on the alms and offerings of the followers. Since the middle of the twentieth century the shrine has been administered by the Punjab Auqaf, or Religious Affairs, Department, the government department of religious endowments. The takeover, it has been observed, could not wholly change the perceptions rooted deep in the minds of the rural people, or the authority of the shrine's administrator on the spot, the *mujawir*.

## Devotion and Festivals

The Auqaf Department now organises the shrine's annual *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of the saint's death, which lasts three days, with arrangements for *langar*, sanitation and security, and with gatherings devoted to the saint's life and teaching.

The shrine has a particular reputation for fertility, justice and punishment. The *dargah*, the shrine complex, is a female domain, at least in numbers, in contrast to the mosques, which are frequented by men; yet the power in shrines is usually held by men.

It was once the custom for parents who had sought a child through his intercession to leave the child, as soon as it was weaned, at Shah Daula's *khanqah* as an offering to him. After his death, devotees would vow either to present the child when it was born or to make an offering to the shrine. The exchange at the tomb has been likened to a bargain between the parents and the *mujawir*, much like the *mahr*, the bridewealth: the shrine's administrator declared the parents *jannati*, dwellers of paradise, and in return they left the child, the gift or *ni'mat*, as a trust, an *amanat*, at the shrine.

## The Chuhas of Shah Daula

*Chuhas*, a Punjabi word meaning mice or rats, is the name long given to children with microcephaly who were associated with the *darbar*. The tradition concerns vulnerable and disabled human beings, and it has often been sensationalised, so it must be approached with care.

Microcephaly is a medical condition in which the head and brain are abnormally small, typically accompanied by intellectual disability. It has recognised genetic and environmental causes and occurs in populations everywhere. At Gujrat, people with this condition came to be linked with the shrine through a folk belief, first described by an observer in January 1839 in the earliest printed notice of the *chuhas*: that childless women who prayed for offspring at the *darbar* would be granted a child, and that the first such child, by the legend, must be dedicated to the shrine and would be born with the characteristic small head. Such dedicated children were called *chuhas*, and they were often kept at the shrine or associated with it. Accounts differ over when the tradition took shape.

In the legend as colonial writers told it, Shah Daula, like other saints, could bring about the birth of a child for a couple who longed for one, but the first child born through his intercession would be a *chuha*. After his death, the legend went on, the miracle continued in a changed form: where a promised gift was withheld, the spirit of the offended saint worked on the parents so that the next child was born a *chuha*, and every child after it, until the original vow was fulfilled. These are the words of a legend, not a description of any person.

From the middle of the nineteenth century the children attracted European medical and ethnographic attention, and with it came the sensational speculation that their small heads were produced deliberately, by binding the skulls of infants or by fitting clamps or vessels to them. The claim has never been substantiated: no such instrument has ever been found, no such procedure has ever been witnessed, and medical authorities regard the deliberate production of microcephaly in this way as implausible without fatal injury.

The modern consensus is that the *chuhas* were, and are, people with naturally occurring microcephaly, whose condition was interpreted through local religious belief and who were drawn into the economy of the shrine. In some periods they were sent out as wandering beggars: after some initial training they were passed on to a *faqir* and made to beg in the name of Shah Daula. A band of faqirs made its living from these children and from the existence of an important shrine; it held no land, had no recognised right of succession to its leadership, and depended entirely on its earnings. Colonial writers also suspected the shrine's followers of trafficking in women. The folk belief that linked the condition to the saint's blessing or curse has no basis in fact.

Reform came in the twentieth century. When the Auqaf Department took control of the shrine, it forbade both the abandonment of such children to the *darbar* and the alleged deforming practices, and the number of *chuhas* associated with the site steadily declined. By the early twenty first century observers reported that only one or two remained. The episode endures chiefly as a cautionary study for historians of medicine, disability and popular religion, in which a genuine medical condition, human vulnerability, exploitation and folk piety became tragically entangled.

## The Shrine in Fiction

The shrine is also the setting of a work of Urdu fiction, Ahmad Nadim Qasimi's story 'Bain', or 'Dirge', which deals with the activities in and around the *dargah* of Shah Daula Daryai in Gujrat. The story does not refer directly to the cult of the *chuhas*, and the shrine it portrays is the story's own.

In the story the shrine has become an institution of justice, symbolised by the holy hand of Shah Daula, which rises from the grave to judge. The upper part of the holy grave opens and the *pir*, the spiritual master, stretches out his holy hand; the culprit, drawn to the shrine from wherever he is, lays his neck in it and dies on the spot. So nobody dares to neglect the orders of Sain Daule Shah, as the story calls the saint.

In the story, the shrine's *mujawir* rules through a dream. He demands that the girl Rano recite at the *dargah* for three days, which, he says, Shah Daula has ordered in a dream; otherwise, the villagers are warned, the saint will put them all to fire and ash.

The story's crisis comes at the *ʿurs*. Rano's coming of age and a proposal of marriage coincide with the anniversary of the saint's death, which marks his union with God, and during the *ʿurs* she falls victim to a corrupted society. The shrine's kitchen and its bath house become the scene of defiance: Rano flings the skirt her parents bring into the burning coals of the *langar*, and her father later throws the *mujawir*'s shroud into the coals of the fire that heats the water for the great ablution, the *ghusl*.

One scholar reads the story as showing that the shrine cult has become a falsehood void of any mysticism, an expression of corrupt political power, in which fear is the prime force that keeps the villagers and devotees loyal to the moral authority of the shrine.

## The Setting

Gujrat lies in the Chaj *doab*, the land between the Chenab and the Bihat. The town was settled under the emperor Akbar, when villages were taken out of the *pargana*, the revenue district, of Sialkot to make it a pargana of its own. Its founding is told this way: all this land had been part of Sialkot, under the sway of the Jats, and as the emperor was travelling from Lahore to Kashmir, a body of Gujars came to him after the crossing of the Chenab to complain of oppression by the Jats. The villages of their *zamindari*, their landholding, were separated off as a pargana, and a small fort and a town were founded. It is said that Gujrat became an important city under Akbar, having been a small village before, and that one of Akbar's wives is buried there.

In the reign of Aurangzeb the town was a market of the province, a resort of every kind of people and a gathering place for the goods of every land and the rare things of the age. Rare swords and *jamdhars*, a kind of dagger, were made there, and more *chikan* work, fine embroidery, was done there than in Sialkot; the district bred Iraqi horses, some worth up to ten thousand rupees.

The town also has its place in Mughal history. When the prince Khusrau rebelled and was turned back at the Chenab, he was seen at the ford at dawn, and Mir Abu al-Qasim Khan and the eunuch Hilal Khan, who were in the neighbourhood of Gujrat, seized him and carried him to the town.

Gujrat is one of several Punjab towns, with Gujranwala and Gujar Khan, named after the Gujars, a famous tribe of the old Punjab who keep animals and sell their milk, and who also farm on a large scale. The town has other saints too: a crow saint seems to have quite a following in this historic city, and when a blast struck in Sindh the city's major shrines were put on high alert.

The Chenab country around the town is the land of Punjabi romance. The tale of Sohni begins with a potter who lived in the Gujrat district of the Punjab, and Takht Hazara, the home of Ranjha's clan, lies in what is now the Gujrat District of Pakistan.

## Legacy

Shah Daula Daryai is remembered in the Punjab as a saint of works and mercy, whose bridges and wells served the living and whose shrine remains a busy centre of devotion in Gujrat. The disquieting tradition of the *chuhas* that attached itself to his name in later centuries belongs to the social history that grew up around the shrine rather than to the saint himself, and it is best understood today through medical fact and compassion for those who were affected. The *darbar* still draws pilgrims, feeds the poor at its *langar* and holds its annual *ʿurs* under the Auqaf Department.

In the reign of Aurangzeb he was remembered as the man who made Gujrat grow in people and prosperity, the builder of a famous bridge on the Lahore highway, and the saint whose tomb drew people of every class. By the 1870s he was counted a saint of the Punjab whose tomb was a place of pilgrimage and whose death was remembered in a chronogram. By the end of the twentieth century his shrine had a particular reputation for fertility, justice and punishment, and it had become the setting of one of Urdu fiction's critiques of shrine power.

A small literature has grown up around him: a *Karamat Nama*, or book of marvels, on his life by Mushtaq Ram Gujrati, dated 1132 AH in the Islamic calendar; a *Tazkira*, a biographical account, of Shah Daula Gujrati by M. S. Nasim, published in Lahore in 1970; and Sharif Kunjahi's life and teachings of the saint, *Hadrat Shâh Daulah Daryâ'î Gujrâtî: Hayât o ta'lîmât*, published in Lahore in 1985 by the Punjab Auqaf Department's imprint.

In 1875 a book on the saints of the Punjab gave him a notice of his own, under the name Shah Daula Daryai Gujrati. That book closes with a verse chronogram by Mufti Ghulam Safdar Lahori, which gives the year 1292 AH:

جو خدا کے دوست تھے پنجاب میں
حال ان کا اس سے ہوتا ہے عیان

*A chronogram on the book's own subject, the saints of the Punjab.*
