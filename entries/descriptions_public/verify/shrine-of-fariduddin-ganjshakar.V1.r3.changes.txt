OLD: More than a hundred *shlokas* bear his name there, more than any other of the poets selected for it, and some of his best Punjabi poetry is preserved to this day in the Gurmukhi script in the sacred texts of the Sikhs, though there is no consensus over whether they were composed by Farid himself or by descendants who wrote under his name. *O crow, eat all my flesh, pick and eat it piece by piece, but do not eat these two eyes, for I still long to see my Beloved.*

The same voice turns the seeker's eye inward, away from the faults of others:

فریدا جے تُوں عقل لطیف ہیں، کالے لِکھ نہ لیکھ
آپنڑے گریوان مہ سِر نیواں کر ویکھ

*Farid, if you are of subtle wit, write not against another’s account; bow your head into your own collar, and look there instead.*

Two of his *dohas*, or couplets, are preserved with a Persian rendering, and both attack false initiation and outward asceticism.

OLD: The stanza that names him is certain to bring offerings, for it appeals to Nizamuddin in the very name of his own spiritual guide, Baba Farid; and the song, words and music alike, is part of the special heritage of the Qawwal Bachche, and is widely sung throughout the Chishti order.

A *qawwali* performed in the United Kingdom opens by invoking him, with the line "Sir devĩ te vafā na mangĩ-ehī pīr Farīd dā dāse":

*Give thy head, expect no loyalty in return, / O, this is Farid, the Saint's wisdom* (translated by Shemeem Burney Abbas)

Another line of the same performance, "Yār Farīd ābād thī vaṇ o jhok-rab phir vīrān basāe", runs:

*O lover Farid, alive were those habitations once: may God bring the wilderness to life again!* (translated by Shemeem Burney Abbas)

But for the sacred frame of its opening, when the ensemble invoked Baba Farid, the performance might have been taken for a secular love song in which a lover seeks a beloved and serenades her.
