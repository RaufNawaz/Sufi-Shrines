## Overview

The shrine of Makhdoom Abdul Rahim Girhori stands at Girhor Sharif, a village some eight kilometres west of Pithoro in Umarkot district, in the semidesert borderlands of eastern Sindh. It honours a leading Naqshbandi mystic and scholar of eighteenth century Sindh, who lived from 1739 to 1778. He belonged to the Mangrio tribe and came to be known as *Girhori* after he settled at Girhor and established there his *khanqah*, the lodge where a Sufi's disciples gather. He is venerated as a *shaheed*, a martyr, and today his shrine is a place of pilgrimage frequented by both Muslims and Hindus, with an annual *urs*, the yearly commemoration of the saint's death, held in his memory.

He is remembered as a scholar and an ascetic, a disciple of the celebrated Naqshbandi master Muhammad Zaman, a commentator on the Qur'an in Sindhi verse, and a saint who died attacking a Hindu shrine.

## The Saint

Abdul Rahim was among the foremost *khalifas*, or deputies, of Khwaja Muhammad Zaman of Luari Sharif, the celebrated *Sultan al-Aulia*, the king of the saints, of the Sindhi Naqshbandis, under whom he was trained. Muhammad Zaman had himself been a disciple of masters who included Mian Muhammad Thattawi. Accounts of Abdul Rahim's order are not unanimous, but he is most often counted a Naqshbandi, a follower of the Sufi order founded by Baha-ud-Din Naqshband, and he is remembered as an orthodox and militant one.

He was a younger contemporary of Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit, and like Mir Dard he was a Naqshbandi mystic. A verse by him expresses his admiration for Shah Abdul Latif, whom he may have met when still a child.

A learned man, Abdul Rahim Girhori is remembered for his religious scholarship and his ascetic life. Unlike the Naqshbandis of Thatta, he was little interested in legal problems. He was an energetic man: once, while attacking the "dry trees" of the school of Makhdum Muhammad Hashim, he even threw an inkstand at a scholar's head. He relied completely upon the Qur'an and tradition, and led a perfectly ascetic life, with long periods of fasting, made harder still because he used to take a purgative every third day.

He died in 1778. Accounts of his death differ in detail. Tradition holds that he fell in battle against a rival Hindu ascetic: he killed the ascetic and destroyed his *marhi*, or shrine, but was himself mortally wounded in the same encounter. That episode is remembered as the origin of his status as a *shaheed*.

## Writings and Teaching

He is said to have composed works in Sindhi, Arabic and Persian, and his principal compositions include *Sharh Abayat Sindhi*, *Fath-al-Fazal*, *Risala Gul Numa* and *Kalam Girhori*. He was a good writer, who commented in Arabic upon his master's Sindhi sayings and composed poetical commentaries on several suras, or chapters, of the Qur'an. Most of his books, however, were destroyed during the Afghan raids in Upper Sindh in 1781.

Perhaps the finest of them is his rhymed commentary on Surat al-Kauthar, the favourite theme of mystical veneration of the Prophet. It is an example of highly complicated *naʿtiyya* poetry, verse in praise of the Prophet, in the Sindhi language. Besides Ad-Duha and An-Najm, Surat al-Kauthar was always a favourite of poets who praised the Prophet, and Girhori's commentary takes up many earlier elements. In it God speaks, saying among other things:

*So much gift did I not give to anyone but you* (translated by Annemarie Schimmel)

The divine address runs on through Jesus, the Torah and Joseph, and closes with words to Ahmad, a name of the Prophet:

*A small quantity only of your love, O Ahmad, has reached Zulaikha … Hell is at your door supplicating like a beggar* (translated by Annemarie Schimmel)

His mystical teaching on the Prophet is in some ways reminiscent of Mir Dard's deep mystical love of the Prophet. Apparently following the statements of his master Muhammad Zaman, he understood the *haqiqa muhammadiyya*, the archetype of Muhammad, as a kind of mediator between the Divine and the human sphere. In this view of the Prophet, Girhori and Mir Dard stand together, and Shah Abdul Latif stands apart from them. His theoretical works describe Muhammad in exalted terms as the Perfect Man, who is like the dawn between the divine light and the darkness of human existence.

Other sayings and readings of his are remembered too. In one saying he called the perfect mystics Kapari, that is Yogis, a special group of Sanyasin, or Hindu renunciants, as Shah Abdul Latif often did. He quoted the Arabic saying *ad-dunya sihr*, "The world is magic". And he applied the words of the Qur'an at Sura 57:4, "And He is with you wherever you are", to the journey of Sassui, the heroine of a Sindhi folk tale, through the desert, something Shah Abdul Latif did not do.

## The Shrine and Its Setting

The shrine stands at Girhor Sharif, where he had settled and established his *khanqah*, and his tomb is honoured as that of a martyred saint. Today the shrine complex draws pilgrims of both faiths, and an annual *urs* commemorates him. The shrine has kept its local importance into recent times: in 2015 an armed attack on its custodian, Syed Asif Ali Shah, stirred sectarian unrest in the area.

Pithoro, the nearby town, is the seat of another saint's tomb. The tomb of Pithoro Pir lies in the hamlet of Pithoro, between Mirpur Khas and Umarkot, on the railway line which formerly connected Hyderabad, in Sindh, to Barmer and Jodhpur in Rajasthan; as at many other sanctuaries, the place bears the saint's name. The area of Umarkot was once a place of rough combat between two groups of Rajputs, the Somras and the Sodhas, and it stood at the centre of conflicts of local, regional and central interests. The chief of the Sodhas, Parmar Sodha, is said to have been established as king, or *rano*, at Umarkot, and in 1907 the Sodhas were described as worshippers of Shiva. Another important group, the Menghwar, counted among the untouchables, mostly settled around Umarkot.

The town has its place in other stories too. One group of pilgrims from a village near Umarkot walked around 600 kilometres to the goddess's temple at Hinglaj. In the legend of Mumal, Umarkot is the seat of the vizier Rano. And in Mughal history, under the name Amarkot, it is the fortress where the emperor's party halted after crossing the desert; its ruler received them with the duties of service, and Prince Muhammad Akbar was born there.

## His Times

The Naqshbandi order came to Sindh from the sixteenth century onwards, with the Tarkhans and the Arghuns, two Central Asian dynasties that ruled Sindh, and it has several important centres in the province. The Naqshbandis of Sindh followed the reforming enterprise led by Shah Waliullah in Delhi, and the majority of them are strongly opposed to music and to the singing of devotional songs. Apart from them, Sufism in Sindh is dominated by poetry expressing *wahdat-e wujud*, the unity of being. At the beginning of the eighteenth century a strong Naqshbandi influence changed the spiritual climate of southern Sindh to a certain extent: Makhdum Muhammad Hashim of Thatta and his relatives attacked the Sufis who defended the emotional side of religion, and this group helped to promote a more sober mystical piety. It was this Muhammad Hashim's school whose "dry trees" Girhori attacked.

His books were lost in the troubles that closed the Kalhoro period. The Kalhoros were overthrown by the former disciples of their saintly ancestor, Adam Shah Kalhoro, and the Talpurs, who thus came to power in 1781, ruled the country until it was conquered by the British in 1843. The Afghan raids in Upper Sindh in which most of Girhori's books were destroyed came in that same year, 1781.

## Legacy

His memory forms part of the wider Naqshbandi devotional network that the disciples of Khwaja Muhammad Zaman spread across Sindh in the eighteenth century. His verse was published in 1956 as *Kalam-i Girhori*, in an edition by Umar Muhammad Daudpota.

For all his militant zeal, it would be wrong to regard Girhori as a typical exponent of Sindhi Sufism. On the whole there was, in one writer's words, "a common basis of understanding, by Muslim and Hindu alike, of the mystical message of Islam", and a number of Hindus became devoted disciples of Muslim saints.
