## Overview

In Baghbanpura, close by the Shalimar Gardens on the eastern edge of Lahore, stands the shrine of Hazrat Madho Lal Hussain, whose name joins two men and is the sign of one of the most extraordinary bonds in the spiritual history of the Punjab. Within it rest Shah Hussain, the great Malamati Sufi, or Muslim mystic, and father of the Punjabi *kafi*, the short sung mystical lyric, and beside him Madho Lal, the Hindu youth he loved and whose name he joined for ever to his own. Around the darbar, the saint's shrine, has grown one of the largest festivals in the region, the Mela Chiraghan, or Festival of Lamps.

From the shrine has flowed a body of Punjabi mystical verse that ordinary people still sing four centuries on. It is at once a place of ecstatic devotion, a monument of the Punjabi language and its poetry, and a beloved emblem of a love that overleaps the boundaries of caste and creed. To understand it, one must begin with the strange and luminous life of Shah Hussain.

## The Saint

Shah Hussain was born in Lahore in 1538; accounts of the year differ, and 1538 is the year the shrine keeps. His was a family of weavers that had once been Hindu. Accounts differ over the family's descent and over which of his forebears first accepted Islam, but the conversion is most often placed in the time of Sultan Firoz Shah Tughlaq. His father, Shaykh Usman, was a religious man who earned a lawful living by the work of weavers, and tradition remembers him too as a craftsman who stitched uniforms for the army of the emperor Humayun. In the Punjab, new Muslims from the weaver castes joined the Islamic community slightly later than in Hindustan, where the mystic poet Kabir, a century earlier, shows the same blend of Sufi and *bhakti*, Hindu devotional, ideals.

The poems name the trade too. In one, the weaver is addressed by name:

*O Hussein, the weaver! He earned no profit* (translated by Naveed Alam)

The poems also hold their one run of biographical negatives:

*Never got engaged, never got married / Never fixed a date, never held a wedding / Neither a householder nor a traveller / Neither a man of faith nor an infidel / He is what he is* (translated by Naveed Alam)

In another translation, the lines run:

*O Friend, Hussayn is a weaver, no profit accrues to him / Neither was he betrothed nor married / Nor was any matrimony ceremony held for him*

He was a child of unusual gifts and piety. He is said to have committed the Quran to memory by the age of seven, and at fifteen to have begun teaching its recitation, *tajweed*, and preaching, leading the congregational prayers and the long *Taraweeh* prayers of Ramadan, the month of fasting. As a boy he went to the local mosque, which also served as a *madrasa*, a school of religious learning, and where learning the Quran by heart was the main method of study; his teacher there was named Abu Bakr. His studies took in the memorising of the Quran, Arabic, and the interpretation of scripture and law, and he read part of the *Tafsir-e Madarik*, a commentary on the Quran, with Shaykh Saad Allah.

It was in that mosque, it is said, that the Sufi master Shaykh Bahlol happened to pass through while the boy was reading the Quran with Abu Bakr, and his glance so fell on Hussain that he became a perfect *wali*, a friend of God, while still a child. How the boy came to finish the Quran is told as a wonder. Bahlol announced that the boy would recite the Quran that Ramadan, and the boy objected that this could not be, for he knew only six *paras*, or parts, of it by heart. Sent to the river, it is told, he met an elder who had him pour water over his hands and, pleased, splashed water on his face, and by the blessing of it he recited the whole Quran that Ramadan. After that recitation Bahlol made him *imam*, the leader of the prayer.

A line of verse dates Bahlol's coming to the year nine hundred and fifty five, without naming the era:

سال پنجاہ و پنج و نہ صد بود

*The year was nine hundred and fifty five*

Shaykh Bahlol Daryai became his master. A dervish, a Sufi who has embraced the ascetic path, who had travelled across the wide expanse of the Islamic world of the fifteenth century, through Arabia, Mesopotamia, Persia and Afghanistan, he was famous as a man of *hal*, the inner state, of *jazb*, the pull of divine attraction, of longing and taste, and of *wajd*, ecstasy, and *sama*, spiritual listening to music, and he was counted the *qutb*, the axis or pole of the saints, of his age. Bahlol took him under his wing, made him his *murid*, a disciple pledged to a Sufi guide, and honoured him with the *khirqa*, the cloak, of *khilafat*, the authority to guide others in his name, before he took his leave; Hussain became his *khalifa*, his deputy. Bahlol's own shrine stands at Tiba Shah Behlol. Hussain showed Shaykh Musa Khokhar the utmost respect, because the two were brothers under the same *pir*, or spiritual guide.

Before leaving, Bahlol instructed him to present himself regularly at the luminous tomb of Data Ganj Bakhsh, Ali Hujwiri, Lahore's patron saint. For some twelve years Hussain gave attendance there with great regularity, at dawn and at *ishraq*, the prayer after sunrise, withdrawing into a long spiritual retreat, or *chilla*, by the darbar and absorbed in the discipline of divine love, *ishq-e-haqiqi*, until in the end, it is said, the reward came to him. Those who came to sit with him there took to calling him, simply, Baba.

For some twenty years he lived the life of an exact and disciplined scholar of the outward law, his devotion so evident that learned men sought his company and came away loving him. The discipline of those years is remembered hour by hour. He is said to have completed a whole reading of the Quran every day. His nights went in recitation, and his hours of stillness were kept at the riverbank and at the tomb of Data Ganj Bakhsh; after the dawn prayer he went to the *maktab*, the school, for the lesson in *tafsir*, the commentary on the Quran, until the afternoon prayer; after it came *zikr*, the remembrance of God, and between the sunset and night prayers *nafl*, voluntary prayers; and he kept to this without variation even in illness. He is remembered as one who fasted always and stood in prayer all night, and it is told that he used to stand up to his waist in the river Ravi, his lips blue from cold, reciting the Quran.

Then everything changed. Accounts of his age at the time differ; it is most often given as thirty six. He was reading the *Tafsir-e Madarik* with his teacher, Shaykh Saad Allah Lahori, who died in 1590, the year 999 AH of the Islamic calendar, when the teacher explained the verse of the Quran that says the life of this world is nothing but sport and play. Hussain understood the world to be mere play. He told his teacher that what he needed was *hal*, the lived state, not *qal*, mere talk; he broke into a spontaneous dance, left the madrasa dancing, and threw his books into a well. He resolved to throw off the constraints of ascetic piety, legal rectitude and rational seriousness, and to lead his life instead as a child at play. Saad Allah argued with him not to take the verse literally, insisting that its tone was sarcastic. Saad Allah himself often voiced the Malamati school, and he was said to walk the Sufi path in the Malamati manner.

The scrupulous scholar became a *malang*, a wandering, dishevelled ecstatic. He set aside the robes and the reputation of a preacher and took up instead the crimson dress by which he is remembered as Lal Hussain, Hussain the Red, dancing the *dhamal*, the ecstatic dance of the Sufis. Accounts differ over the flask he carried in his hand, but most say that it held wine and that he drank it in public. What had happened, in the language of the Sufis, was that the outward path of the *Shariat*, the religious law, had opened for Hussain onto the burning inward path of love, and he chose to walk it in the most confronting way he could.

Word reached Bahlol at Chiniot, it is told, that his disciple drank, stayed intoxicated and had strayed from the *shariat*. He rushed to Lahore, ready to join Saad Allah in urging him back to the straight path, but when he saw Hussain in ripped red clothes, dancing and drinking wine, he realised that this was his disciple's own path to religious sincerity. He blessed him and went home satisfied that Hussain had attained to God.

Hussain belonged in spirit to the *Malamatiyya*, a *silsila*, or Sufi lineage, of free spirited Sufis, the Sufis of the path of blame, who deliberately drew the censure of respectable society upon themselves in order to kill the ego's hunger for esteem, hiding their inner sanctity beneath an outward show of impropriety. To dance and sing and carry a flask through the streets of Mughal Lahore was, for such a mystic, not a fall from grace but a discipline of effacing the self, a refusal of the world's praise. For a Malamati, the disgrace ensures the grace: he is blameworthy, an outcast, a rebel, a transgressor of the false conventions of love, the one who breaks free, and in Hussain's case his humility cuts like an axe. There is no settled view of which order to count him in, but he is most often called a Malamati, a wandering Malamati Sufi of Lahore and a spectacular Punjabi poet. His red dress gave him his name: because he wore red clothing, he became famous as Lal Hussain, a nickname of his own that alludes to his love of wearing only red. Red is the colour of passion, and it runs through his poetry as *lal*.

His manner was also called *qalandarana*, that of the Qalandars, the recklessly bold Sufis who break with religious law and social convention. They renounce productive work, refuse to raise a family, and resist the labour it takes to conform to religious custom and law, and those who oppose them see their lives as waste. Hussain's red clothes and his face shaved clean marked him as a Qalandar.

In the streets his life of blame took public forms of abandon: singing, drumming, dancing, wine drinking, shaving off all facial hair, crossing the divisions of social class, and voluntary poverty, all displayed in public to draw out the hypocrisy of others. With his band of devoted friends and followers he sang and played musical instruments while dancing through the streets of sixteenth century Lahore, a rebel *faqir*, a mystic who has embraced poverty, dressed all in red, with a wine flask in one hand and an earthen bowl in the other, and his reputation as a *laund*, a libertine, and a *rind*, a drunkard, was widely known. A portrait of him from the lives of the saints runs: "He wears red clothes, drinks wine openly, sings and plays music, and is surrounded by smooth-faced young men as beautiful as the moon. He dances with them in the street in front of his home." He had a great fondness for dance and song, and he kept himself hidden in the dress of *faqr*, poverty. His mornings went in recitation, his nights in laughter and weeping, and he used intoxicating things as well.

His sayings on *faqr* were kept letter by letter. Its *f*, he taught, stands for *faqr*, *faqa* and *fana*, poverty, hunger and annihilation; its *q* for *qanaʿat*, contentment, and *qurb-e haqq*, nearness to God; and its *r* for *riyazat*, discipline, and *riza*, acceptance. In the nineteenth century the shrine's *sajjada nashin*, the hereditary custodian who sits on the saint's prayer carpet, held a notebook in the saint's own hand on the five letters of *darvesh*, the dervish, in which, for instance, *d* stands for *dard-e dil*, the pain of the heart, and *sh* for *shukr-e haqq*, thanks to God, and for *sharm*, modesty.

Among the learned and the holy of the city he had many companions and interlocutors. Hazrat Hassu Teli, a Teli, of the caste of oil pressers, and a native of Lahore, was of one age with him, and it is said that Hassu had utter love for Lal Hussain and used to say: Hassu is Husain and Husain is Hassu; no duality remains. Shah Hussain was a contemporary, too, of Damodar.

Tradition also holds that in his last years Shah Hussain repented of every act contrary to the *shariah*, became regular in fasting and prayer, and died in that state. Shaykh Muhammad Tahir Lahori, a *khalifa* of the Mujaddid Alf-e Sani, the Renewer of the Second Millennium, who held extremely strictly to the *shariah* and who died in 1040 AH, said: "If I did not fear the taunts of the exoteric ulama, I would often go to Shaikh Husain's mazar and seek help". The *ulama* are the scholars of religion, and a *mazar* is the tomb of a saint.

## Before the Authorities

Inevitably his way drew fatwas, rulings of religious law, against him, and denunciations, and his actions brought him into conflict with the authorities of Lahore: the police chief, the chief judge, and finally the emperor himself. They were conflicts in which, in the end, he prevailed.

The story of those trials opens in a large public square before the high walls of the Red Fort in Lahore, where crowds have come to see the execution of rebels against the Mughal emperor, highway robbers and tax evaders. Suddenly a very different sort of man is brought forward in chains. He wears ragged scarlet clothes; his head is shaved, and his beard is shaved clean; and he walks into the public square laughing without fear. As the police drag him into the square, the chains keep falling off his wrists and ankles.

The police chief of Lahore, the *kotwal*, was Malik Ali, and he had been asked to keep a watch on Hussain's misdeeds. It is told that he was administering a public punishment in the market when Hussain, at the side of the road, was caught making flirtatious eye contact with a handsome young man who proved to be the police chief's own son. The chief, the story goes, arrested him and had him chained up, charging him with being a heretical innovator in religious matters, and threatened him with impaling. In the verse that tells the story, the threat runs:

*yours will be a disgraceful death, driven through with a spike / A spike that will go into you from behind / so that your soul will leave you from behind*

Hussain, laughing like a woman, answered in verse:

*For what reason do you treat me like this / what sin have I committed, you stupid ass?*

*I may be a sinner in respect to the religious law / but how have I injured you or the king who sent you? / If my actions are a sin, then I sin against God / but I commit no crime against anyone, you or your king!*

His poetry sounded as laughter in the face of distress, and with it he confronted the police chief and his other critics. Tradition tells that he met the police chief with a miracle: the chains kept falling from his ankles, no matter how the police chief struggled to secure them. In the end, the story goes, the police chief himself was impaled to death on the command of the emperor, for having failed him in some unrelated matter.

The second trial was a judge's. Accounts differ over who the judge was; it is told that his case came before the Shaykh al-Islam, the chief religious scholar and jurist of the realm, Abd al-Nabi Sultanpuri, who approached him as a heretic rather than for sexual improprieties and questioned him about the pillars of Islam. Of the five pillars, the saint answered, the first, belief in one God, both of them kept; the second and third, prayer and fasting, he ignored openly; and the fourth and fifth, pilgrimage and alms, the judge ignored in secret. Behind the reply lay two charges against the judge: he had ruled that the *hajj*, the pilgrimage to Mecca, was no longer obligatory for all Muslims, and he was alleged to avoid paying the *zakat*, the obligatory alms, due on the wealth he had accumulated.

At last complaints reached the imperial court, and some demanded that this man who seemed to mock the outward forms of religion be put to death. Accounts differ over which emperor summoned him, but most name Akbar. The most famous story of his life belongs to this moment. Questioned over whether he drank wine in public, Hussain is said to have turned the wine before the court into milk, water and other drinks, and the emperor, the story goes, was convinced and acknowledged him as a great saint. The saint went his way, and the tale has been told ever since as a sign that what the world condemns in the friends of God, it does not understand.

Mughal nobles held him in high esteem. Prince Salim, who would become the emperor Jahangir, was among his devotees, and so was Khwaja Daulat Khan, and the saint himself brought no petition to anyone. One of Akbar's chief nobles, Abd al-Rahim, the Khan-i Khanan, or Khan of Khans, travelling with an army towards Sindh, stopped in Lahore to receive the blessing of Shah Hussain; the Mughal armies successfully conquered Thatta in Sindh, and Hussain's reputation spread. Nobles began to patronise him, seeking blessings for their various endeavours. The admiration of the Mughal nobles protected him from local censure, and in turn he became a saint who protected Mughal rule. Dara Shikoh named him in his book on the spiritual boasts of the saints, the *Shathiyat*, and in his memorial to the saints, the *Hasanat al-Arifin*. Yet there is no account of Hussain in the annals of Akbar, though the Mughals were known for their meticulous record keeping, and no mention of him even in Jahangir's memoirs, the *Tuzk-i-Jehangiri*.

Shah Hussain lived through the high noon of the Mughal Empire, a *salik*, a traveller on the Sufi path, living in Lahore in the reign of Akbar, when the city was a great imperial capital adorned with gardens, mosques and mansions; Akbar had moved his capital there for a time, and the city was home to the Mughal court in its seasonal rotation. His was an age of ferment, of Akbar's experiments in the reconciliation of religions and of popular unrest against the imperial order. Punjabi tradition links the ecstatic *faqir* with the rebel folk hero Dulla Bhatti, who rose against the emperor's revenue demands, and so casts Hussain among the friends of the poor and the defiant of the mighty. Abdullah Bhatti, commonly known as Dulla Bhatti, led a rebellion in the Punjab and was captured and hanged in public on Akbar's orders.

## Madho Lal

The turning point of Hussain's later life, and the reason his name is joined to another's, was his love for Madho Lal, as he is most often called, a young man of a Hindu Brahmin family, of the priestly caste. Madho's father and mother, Brahmins by caste, lived in the small town of Shahdara, where the tomb of Jahangir stands. Madho was very beautiful and, it is said, eccentrically proud, a Brahmin's son, a playful teaser and cold of heart. Age, class and religion separated the two, and Madho looked askance at Hussain as a social inferior, a source of caste impurity and a general threat.

Passing through Shahdara, the saint's eye fell on Madho, and he was overcome, it is said, at the sight of the boy's beauty. He fell in love with that beauty with heart and soul, had the utmost love for him, and drew him by the attraction of love. Accounts differ over the nature of that love, but tradition remembers it in the saint's own answer: when people objected that a Muslim *faqir* should be so taken with a Hindu youth, Hussain answered that his love was not carnal but a kind of wonder. He did not love him, he said; he envied his Creator, for how beautiful He had made him. The saint is said to have circled Madho's house at night while Madho paid him no attention, and his pursuit made him notorious before Madho turned to him.

The courtship had its own wonders. The saint is said to have shown his mystical power by hearing what Madho said within the walls of his house and repeating the words exactly. A spy, it is said, first reported a scene that ended with the pair kissing each other like milk and sugar and the two friends becoming one; told to go back and verify the truth, he returned to find two terrifying lions sitting face to face.

The festival of spring brought them together. On Basant Panchami, the festival that welcomes the spring, which falls near Holi, the Hindu festival of colours, Madho threw colour and *gulal*, coloured powder, over the saint. An ecstatic state came over Hussain and he began to dance, and for the rest of his life he kept Basant every year. Their separation was eased in the spring celebration of Basant, which gave the two a chance to meet in person: on that holiday of frolic and play all conventions could be broken, all hierarchies overturned and all restraints flouted. A poem on his life sings of that day:

*Basant arrived, the day the world is flooded with joy / Hindus play Holi colors: music and wine, flirting and teasing / Everyone gathers together that day without a care / the honorable mingle freely with the shameless / From that joyous day onward Hussayn devoted / himself to play as if each day were Basant* (translated by Scott Kugle)

Verse on the saint's life describes Madho too:

*Madho was wondrous in his beauty and his grace / A young man refined, noble Brahmin by descent* (translated by Scott Kugle)

Slowly the two became inseparable, though Madho's family and the people of his caste looked on the friendship with disapproval. His Brahmin parents and his extended family, it is said, did not accept this dangerous intimacy; they tried to hinder their meetings and even to assassinate Hussain. Madho began to come and go in the saint's service and to share his pure wine, and his household found it intolerable. Their open passion, it is said, ruined Hussain's reputation throughout Lahore, and the two came to live together.

Devotional tradition preserves a famous miracle of their bond. Madho was bound by custom to make the pilgrimage of the Ganga Snan, the ritual bathing in the Ganges, and he feared his parents' anger. Hussain, it is said, bade him take his hands, set his feet upon the saint's own and close his eyes, and in that instant, the story goes, Madho found himself at the distant bathing place, to the astonishment of all. From then on the love was mutual and complete, and the two could not bear to be parted.

Accounts differ over whether Madho became a Muslim. Most say that he did: he came to the saint with sincere devotion, was honoured with Islam, became his *murid*, and through his training became a perfect *wali*, and he is said to have been given the name Mian Mahbub al-Haq. He is said to have been eighteen when he was honoured with Islam, after which he reached *fana*, annihilation in God. It is told, too, that the saint gave him wine, by which he removed the dross of unbelief from Madho's heart; that while drinking wine he embraced Madho and made him arrive at the truth, *wasl bi-haqq*; and that so he made Madho a complete and perfected saint without any rigours or strenuous labours. Yet Madho has remained known by his given, Hindu name, which is inscribed upon his tombstone today. He was one of the saint's *khalifas*, his illumined *pir*'s beloved and desired one.

So closely were their names woven together that, when Madho worried that the world knew only Lal Hussain, the saint is said to have told him that from that day people would speak Madho's name before his own. And so it has been: the extremity of love joined the two names into one, and instead of Shaykh Hussain he came to be called Madho Lal Hussain, his lover's name added to his own. The fusing of the names is a testament to the enduring legacy of love, and illustrates an essential tenet of Sufism, the merging of the lover and the beloved.

There is no settled view of whether the poems speak of Madho. At most he is addressed directly in one *kafi*, and even there it is open to interpretation, since the word *lal* can also mean dear or precious one:

*Dear Lal! How can this life be trusted?* (translated by Naveed Alam)

Two more lines may speak of Madho Lal, of the colour red, or simply of the beloved:

*Forest and wilderness she searches / Haven't found Lal yet* (translated by Naveed Alam)

*Lal, I am inside out, that's my business, / that's my occupation* (translated by Naveed Alam)

In a land later torn by the divisions of religion, the shrine of a Muslim saint and his Hindu beloved, honoured under one name, has become one of the Punjab's most cherished symbols of a love that knows nothing of the frontiers of caste and creed, and, for the Sufi, a living parable of the soul's love for God, in which the lover and the beloved at last become one.

## The Poetry

Shah Hussain is honoured as the first great master of the Punjabi *kafi*, the short, sung mystical lyric that would become the very heart of Punjabi Sufi poetry, taken up after him by Bulleh Shah, Sultan Bahu and Khwaja Farid. His poems, which rolled off his lips in Punjabi, give the clearest view of his religious vision, and they draw intensely on local Indian folklore and Hindu myth. His compositions are *kafis* par excellence, and he tied himself to a metre shaped by the *raga*, the melodic mode of Indian music.

He is said not to have written his verses down himself. Accounts of his scribe differ, but most say that Prince Salim, who would become the emperor Jahangir, ordered one of his courtiers, Bahar Khan, to attend the saint daily and write down his teachings and doings to preserve them for posterity. The work, the *Baharia*, was written in the saint's own lifetime, but it is now lost, even in manuscript form.

The *kafis* are brief and burning, cast in the voice of a woman, a bride, a spinner at her wheel, a girl longing for her absent love, for whom the Beloved is God and separation from Him the only real grief. Almost all of them close with the poet naming himself in the third person, in a signature that has become famous in its own right: *Kahe Hussain, faqir Sain da*, "Says Hussain, the humble faqir of the Lord". In almost every concluding line he calls himself *fakir nimana*, the lowly fakir, or *fakir Sain da*, the Lord's fakir. In Punjabi a fakir is the mendicant, the dervish, the beggar, the pauper, and a *fakir nimana* is a fakir without *ma'an*, or pride, worthless, wretched, of the lowest order. In English the signature has taken many forms: 'the beggar-wanderer', 'the miserable mendicant', 'the wretched fakir', 'the wandering fakir', 'God's fakir', 'the helpless fakir', and 'the lowly fakir', the phrase from which one translation of his verse, *Verses of a Lowly Fakir*, takes its title. Another rendering gives it as *So says Hussain, / The mendicant of the Lord.* (translated by Ghulam Yaqoob Anwar, adapted by Scott Kugle). One text has a quite different signature verse, starting *kahe Husain faqīr*, 'Husain the fakir says'.

In his best loved *kafi* the soul cries out in the voice of a daughter to her mother:

مائے نی میں کینوں آکھاں، دردِ وچھوڑے دا حال نی

*O mother, to whom shall I tell the anguish of this separation?*

Word for word, its first two lines run:

*O Mother, to whom can I tell / the state of separation's ache?* (translated by Naveed Alam)

A gloss of the same two lines runs:

*O my mother, to whom shall I tell? / To whom narrate the agony of my separation?*

Its opening words, *Maiy Ni Mein Kino Akhan*, gave their title to an Urdu edition of his poems. Pathana Khan sings it in Siraiki, the language of the southern Punjab, and at the shrine of Mai Heer in Jhang a lone musician was heard singing it on his harmonium: *O Mother, to whom should I now narrate these tales of my pains?* The poem also carries a reference to the love legend of Hir and Ranjha:

*I roamed all around searching for my Ranjhan / Little that I knew my Ranjhan was always with me*

In another, the whole of creation dissolves into the presence of the one Beloved:

رَبّا میرے حال دا محرم توں
اندر توں ہیں، باہر توں ہیں، رومَ رومَ وِچ توں

*O Lord, You alone are the confidant of my condition: You are within me, You are without me, You are in my every pore.*

And in another the ache of longing lengthens the very hours of the night:

سجّݨ بِن راتاں ہوئیاں وڈّیاں

*Without the beloved, the nights have grown long.*

And in a line that has passed into proverb, he sings of the truth that will not be silenced in the heart of the lover:

منہ آئی بات نہ رہندی اے

*The word that has risen to the lips cannot be held back.*

These songs, deceptively simple and endlessly deep, are still sung by *qawwals*, the singers of Sufi devotional music, and by folk singers across the Punjab, and it is largely through them that Shah Hussain lives: the man who abandoned the scholar's certainties for the ecstatic's, and made of the Punjabi language a vessel for the love of God.

Again and again the poems speak in the poet's own name, and against himself. He refuses the title of master:

*One called Shah Hussein is a fakir, / don't call him a master / I'm not fond of falsehood* (translated by Naveed Alam)

He mocks his own pride:

*Why such self-importance, Hussein? / Who do you think you are? You are / a weed among the garden's fancy flowers* (translated by Naveed Alam)

And his age:

*Shah Hussein, you dotard, / gaps widen between your teeth* (translated by Naveed Alam)

He waits at the Lord's door:

*Shah Hussein lies at your doorstep, Sain, / yearning for your name* (translated by Naveed Alam)

Elsewhere he is 'Shah Hussein stumbling at the very end', and his self humiliation reaches its height in the line *I'm the bitch at your doorstep*. The one property the verse gives him is a field:

*Blackbuck, you grazed / the borders of Shah Hussein's fields* (translated by Naveed Alam)

The speaking voice is a woman's, and in places a working weaver girl's: "This miserable one is caught in cotton", "I'm the lowly cleaner at the esteemed house", "Shah Hussein, I'm the dowry-less".

*A weaver woman tied to the loom / I worked my spindles despite the detractors* (translated by Naveed Alam)

He often spoke in the voice of a young woman just reaching the age of betrothal. His poems turn on her choice, to spin and weave dutifully in the hope of finding a husband or to abandon the bounded courtyard of respectability, and on the dower, for she needs to earn the marriage, since her family will have to pay the dower to a groom. His red rags become a bride's dress:

*To hell with your white garb/ better is the rag of the faqir / only she is wedded who stands up/ to dance in his courtyard with abandon.* (translated by Ghulam Yaqoob Anwar, adapted by Scott Kugle)

The heroine he most often becomes is Hir. Though he never directly names Radha, the beloved of Krishna, he does use the name of the Punjabi heroine Hir, and he often turned to the love epic of the Punjabi folk heroes Hir and her lover Ranjha, placing himself in the persona of the woman. In one poem, which begins "Crying for my beloved, I myself became Ranjha", his identity with Hir is more explicit, and it ends:

*I have finally found the beloved I sought for so long / It was after meeting the sadhus, says Hussayn/ that the uncertainty has vanished.* (translated by Ghulam Yaqoob Anwar, adapted by Scott Kugle)

The image of becoming Ranjha runs in Punjabi:

*Mahi mahi kook di mein ape Ranjhan hoyi / Ranjhan Ranjhan sab koi akho, Heer na akhon koi*

*Calling the name of my beloved I myself have become Ranjhan / Call me Ranjhan only now as I am no longer Heer*

Allusions to the story of Hir are found as early as the sixteenth century in his Punjabi verse. He alluded to popular motifs such as the stories of Hir and of Sohni Mehanwal, and he is the first known representative of a tradition typical of the religious poetry of the Punjab and Sind: the insertion of folk tales into the context of mystical thought. He compared the love of Hir for Ranjha to that of a believer for his God, a theme taken up after him by Bulleh Shah and Waris Shah, and it was Bulleh Shah who properly exploited the rich potential for mystical allegory in the relationship between Hir and her beloved. Through his poetry Hussain brought into the story the idea of *wahdat al-wujud*, the unity of being, which remains an essential part of Hindu philosophy and Islamic spirituality. In both provinces poets favour the motif of spinning and weaving, natural in a land that grows cotton, and the tragic heroines of their folk tales, Hir, Sassui, Sohni and many others, represent the human soul in search of the beloved. His poems carry the traditional mystical vocabulary of longing and of hope for annihilation in God. With the Turkish poet Kaygusuz Abdal, of the Bektashi order of Sufis, he shares a poetic topic, the singing of his insatiable appetite, which ridicules or reverses the classical image of the mystic's insatiable thirst for more and more love.

The poems end, too, on doctrine. The last *kafi* of one collection carries the saying of the hidden treasure, with Arabic phrases naming the Quranic attributes of God, and then names a teacher, Shah Jalal:

*Each thing encompassed, he said, I was the hidden treasure* (translated by Naveed Alam)

*Says Hussein, this the incantation taught by Shah Jalal* (translated by Naveed Alam)

He gives God many names. He rarely says Allah; if not Sain, he says Rab, Maula or Ram, and Ram quite often, and he even calls on Har, Shiva. Not all his *kafis* are in Punjabi: one is not in Punjabi but in Persianised Hindi, similar to the *brij bhasha* of Amir Khusrau, *brij bhasha* being a literary dialect of Hindi, and another is in *brij bhasha* itself. One is a riddle, with ten doors, a niche, a box, a bridal stage inside the box, and finally a dwarf elephant. Another is one of the most elusive, since it does not conform to the typical stanza form, and its red wedding dress, its places across the subcontinent, Kashmir, Gujarat and Multan, and its image of Krishna with his flute all add to its mystery. Another rhymes beautifully throughout, on the end words *mooli*, *gandhooli*, *phooli* and *samjholi*. One *kafi* has become one of the signature songs of the famous Pakistani singer of mystical poetry Abida Parveen, with the refrain *Changi aan kay mandi aan/ mein sahib teri bandi aan*. It is even said that Shah Hussain travelled to Amritsar to have his poetry included in the Granth Sahib, the Sikh scripture then being compiled by Guru Arjun Dev, who lived from 1563 to 1605, and that he offered one of his *kafis* for it.

Kafis, stanzaic poems built on the themes and techniques of folklore, were easy to understand and to memorise, and so served as the ideal means of spreading mystical teaching among people who could not read and depended on the spoken word. Even today most Punjabis and Sindhis are more likely to have heard a *kafi* than read one. His poems were sung before they were written down, and their text has a history of its own. Dr Mohan Singh Diwana, a Sikh scholar of Punjabi in the early twentieth century, is acknowledged as the saviour of the *kafis* in written form: he collected them from the bards and published them in the 1940s, and without him they would have been lost. He found 138 *kafis* in a collection of Hindu, Muslim and Sikh mystical poetry by an unknown Sindhi editor, in the Gurmukhi script, the script in which the Sikhs write Punjabi, published in Lahore around 1900, and twenty five more in a handwritten Gurmukhi manuscript in the Punjab University library, dated 1804, the oldest textual record of his work; together they made up the first edition of his poetry in the Urdu script, published in 1942. As more editions followed, new *kafis* were found, their number rose, and differences between the versions, from minor to major, began to emerge. Some of his *kafis* were mixed with those of Bulleh Shah in an 1882 Lahore volume of that poet's work, and some lyrics are attributed to both poets, whose early textual history is very uncertain. The version of one of them given to Shah Hussain has the refrain *mitrāñ dī mijmānī kāran, dil dā lahū chhānīdā*, 'To entertain my beloved, my heart's blood is filtered'.

Besides the *kafis* in Punjabi, one other work in his name has come to light, the *Risala-e Tahniyat*, in Persian prose, which presents the problems of *tasawwuf*, Sufism, in an easy manner. Its full text, edited and introduced by Muhammad Iqbal Mujaddidi, was printed in *Majalla Maarif* of Azamgarh in August 1970 and again in *Sahifa* of Lahore in July 1972.

## Death and Burial

Shah Hussain died in 1599, in the reign of Akbar, at about the age of sixty. The year of his death is the most disputed fact of his life; the year the shrine keeps, and most accounts give, is 1599, the year 1008 of the Hijri calendar.

Foreseeing his end, tradition holds, he had sent Madho away, into the army, it is said, knowing that his beloved could not bear to watch him die, and he left a strange instruction: that he be buried first on the very bank of the river Ravi, where the flood would in time carry the grave away. When the waters shifted the grave, he is said to have told them, they should dig at the place where it came to rest, and if a rosary, or *tasbih*, a water pot, or *lota*, and a prayer mat, or *jaanamaz*, were found in the earth, they would know they had found the true spot. He was buried by the Ravi at a place he had chosen himself, on a Friday, it is said, the last day of Jumada al-Thani, the sixth month of the Islamic year, and the mourning for him raised a clamour in the Punjab like the mourning for Karbala, where the Prophet's grandson Husain was martyred. A chronogram for his death, a verse whose letters, read as numbers, give a year, has this line:

گفت ہاتف کہ مستِ عشقِ ازل

In it the voice from the unseen speaks of one drunk with the love of *azal*, the eternity that has no beginning. Another chronogram, a quatrain, ends with the line:

سال ترحیل شمع عشق حسین

The line speaks of the year of the departure of Husain, the candle of love.

Madho, told of his death only afterwards, abandoned everything and returned, never again to leave the grave; he is said to have wept night and day, clasping it. Some years later, it is told, the river flooded, as the saint had foretold, and the grave was carried to Baghbanpura, where a *jogi*, a Hindu ascetic, dwelt. There, the story goes, when the ground was opened, the rosary, the pot and the prayer mat were found exactly as Hussain had promised, and on that spot Madho reburied him.

There Madho kept his long vigil. He stayed on at the tomb as a *mujawir*, an attendant of the shrine, and became the saint's *sajjada nashin* and the spiritual successor of his saintly authority. He recited his master's *kafis* in the streets and at the tomb, and in time, it is said, he came to look like Hussain himself. A promise of his gave a gate its name: in the nineteenth century the eastern gate of the enclosure was called the Bihishti Darwaza, the Gate of Paradise, because Shaykh Madho, it was said, had decreed that whoever entered it would be of paradise.

Among those close to Madho was Shaykh Mahmud, commonly known as Pir Muhammad, whose father had been a companion of Shah Hussain, while he himself was a close attendant of Madho. He wrote a Persian poem on the saint's life, the *Haqiqat al-Fuqara*, or Truth of the Saints, in which he set down the wonders he had witnessed; it set the pattern for the lives of him written ever since, and it survives in manuscript at Punjab University in Lahore.

Madho was born in 983 AH and died on 22 Dhu al-Hijja, the twelfth month of the Islamic year, in 1056 AH, or 1646, at the age of seventy three. He was laid to rest beside the friend whose name he bore, within his master's enclosure, as had been Hussain's last wish.

## The Shrine

The darbar stands in Baghbanpura, in the shadow of the Mughal Shalimar Gardens, and spreads today over some twenty *kanals*, a local measure of land. At its heart, in the inner sanctum, lie the two graves, Shah Hussain's and, right next to it, Madho Lal's, the object of a constant stream of pilgrims who come to recite the *Fatiha*, the opening chapter of the Quran, to make vows, and to sit in the presence of the saint of love. The side by side graves are always heaped with fresh rose and marigold garlands, offered by the daily throngs of devotees. A mosque adjoins the shrine, where the five daily prayers are held, and around the complex has grown a large graveyard, for to be buried near Madho Lal Hussain is counted a great blessing, and space within it is hard to come by.

The shrine took shape over the centuries after the saint's death. Its custodians recall a first structure raised in the decades after 1600 and substantial construction achieved by the nineteenth century, and its most recent reconstruction was carried out in 2011 by the Punjab Auqaf Department, the department of religious endowments, which administers it in the Sunni (Ahl-e-Sunnat) tradition. Every Thursday evening the tomb is lit with lamps and fills with visitors, and on ordinary days, too, some eight to ten thousand people are said to pass through.

In the nineteenth century the shrine was a *khanqah*, a Sufi lodge, adjoining the village of Baghbanpura on the north. It had an enclosure of solid masonry, *pakka*. Its great gate on the west was used for traffic, a south gate led towards Baghbanpura, there was a large north gate, and on the east stood the Bihishti Darwaza. In the middle of the enclosure stood a large platform plastered with lime, with solid lattice work on its parapets and four minarets, each as high as a man's waist, at its corners. On it a second platform bore Lal Hussain's grave, its *taʿwiz*, the raised marker over the grave, solidly built and always covered by a *ghilaf*, a cloth cover, and on a further platform to its east lay Madho's grave, also solidly built. But for want of attention and repair many buildings had collapsed, and the enclosure wall had fallen in many places.

In the same century the precinct held, south of a large building on the west, a *deorhi*, or entrance hall, *dalans*, open verandas, and a white *hujra*, a cell, plastered with lime; a place marked as the seat of the *gaddi*, the seat of spiritual authority, where the *sajjada nashin* sits; a place, five by three *gaz*, where Madho had lived, the *gaz* being a traditional measure of length; a women's quarter with three *dalans*; and a well with a working *charkhi*, a wheel for drawing water. An eight sided enclosure, with sloping *kanguras*, or crenellations, had four corner seats, five arched doors on three sides and a door shaped as a half circle on the north. Within the enclosure, the platform called the Maqam-e Takht bore the blessed *taʿwiz* of Hussain, always covered with a *ghilaf*, and to the east of his grave lay the luminous grave of Madho. Madho's grave then had no *ghilaf* of its own, though one was laid on it on the day of the *ʿurs* and of the fair; an upper level behind a marble door was always kept locked; and to the north stood an octagonal white *chiragdan*, a lamp stand, four *gaz* high, in which a lamp was lit every day.

In the nineteenth century a mosque stood to the west of the tomb, built by Nawab Zakariya Khan, known as Khan Bahadur, the *suba*, or governor, of Lahore. A large well with a wheel and two bathhouses lay to the east of the mosque; its courtyard floor was solidly paved but old, with a wall of a man's height round it, and it had three *mihrabs*, the niches that mark the direction of prayer. The central one bore the Bismillah, the formula "In the name of God", and the Kalima, the profession of faith, in *thuluth*, a monumental Arabic script. The north *mihrab* names its era, the reign of the emperor Muhammad Shah, and its builder, the choicest of the emperor's court:

زبدۂ بارگاہ او نواب
زکریا خان صوبہ پنجاب

*The choicest of his court, Nawab Zakariya Khan, Suba of Punjab*

The south *mihrab* places the mosque beside the saint's *dargah*, the shrine of a Sufi, in so many words:

نزد درگاہ صاحب عرفان
واقف سر حضرت رحمان
آنکہ معروف شدہ بہ لال حسین
خاک نعلین اوست سرمہ عین

*Near the dargah of the master of gnosis, knower of the secret of the Merciful, he who has become known as Lal Husain, the dust of whose sandals is collyrium for the eye*

It records too that he newly raised a firm mosque and a firm well:

کرد احداث مسجد محکم
نیز خویش دو در چاہ مستحکم

A further line of the inscription on the south *mihrab* speaks of the building of the mosque and the well:

مسجد و چاہ را انکو تعمیر

In the nineteenth century the mosque belonged to the tomb of Madho Lal Hussain and was in the possession of the *sajjada nashin*.

The saint's following, as tradition counts it, numbered nine thousand perfected servants and one hundred and fifty five thousand *murids*, of whom sixteen became famed *khalifas*, in four groups of four, each with their burial places. Of the four Gharibs, one lies at Ratti Mittha next to Wazirabad, one elsewhere in the Wazirabad district, one in the Deccan, and one beside Shah Hussain's own grave. The four Diwans are Shaykh Madho, the first; Diwan Gorakh and Allah Diwan, buried at Lahore; and Diwan Bakhshi, at Bijapur in the Deccan. The four Khakis are Maula Bakhsh Khaki and Khaki Shah at Lahore, Khaki Shah at Wazirabad, and Haidar Khaki in the Deccan; and the four Bilawals are Shah Rang Bilawal, Budhu Bilawal and Shah Mast Bilawal at Lahore, and Shah Bilawal in the Deccan.

The shrine has had other keepers too. In the time of Maharaja Ranjit Singh, the Sikh ruler of the Punjab, the shrine's *sajjada nashin* was Mian Sain Mahbub Shah. One Jhuggi Shah served the tomb and built a house there at his own cost. In the nineteenth century the offering income of both of the shrine's fairs was taken by Hassan Ali, the *mujawir* of the *khanqah*.

The Shalimar Gardens were built by the Mughal emperor Shah Jahan and counted among his memorials in Lahore, with the praise that the eye of the age had seldom seen such a garden, and the royal canal from the Ravi ran to them. In the saint's day Baghbanpura lay on the outskirts of Lahore; the neighbourhood is now within the expanding city, just next to the Shalimar Gardens.

## Devotion and Festivals

The devotion at Madho Lal Hussain draws on everything the saint was: the ecstatic *malang* who danced where others prayed; the poet whose *kafis* put the love of God into the language of the people; and the lover whose bond with a Hindu youth became a byword for a love beyond division. Pilgrims come for the blessing of a saint famous for *ishq-e-haqiqi*, the real love of the Divine, and for the sense, as the local belief has it, that Hussain belonged to another world, that his whole life was unlike that of ordinary men. Those who could not know him in the flesh, it is said, come to love him through his stories and his songs. His verse is sung at the shrine and across the Punjab, his festival is one of the largest in the land, and his joint name stands, in a country marked by communal division, as a quiet insistence that the highest sanctity and the deepest love recognise no barrier of caste or creed. In this the darbar is not only the tomb of a great saint but a living argument about what devotion, and what love, can mean.

To this day Hussain is seen as one of the patron saints of Lahore. He is counted among the *jamali*, or aesthetic, mystics, whose shrines stand against the *jalali*, or majestic and awesome, reputation of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar. In Lahore today the Qalandars, who completely abandon legal norms and social conventions and even the distinctions of gender, are still associated with Shah Hussain, as with Shah Jamal and Mian Mir. His wonders are counted beyond number, too many to write down in a lifetime, and they are very famous among Muslims.

The shrine's crowning event is the Mela Chiraghan, the Festival of Lamps, its annual *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of the saint's death; the *ʿurs* of a saint is celebrated as a wedding, the union of the mortal bride with the divine groom. Accounts of its date differ; the shrine holds it on the 9th to the 11th of Shawwal, the tenth month of the Islamic year, which in recent years has fallen in the spring months of March and April. It is reckoned among the greatest festivals of the Punjab and is celebrated every year with great fanfare. For three days the darbar and its surroundings blaze with countless lamps and candles, and the crowds swell from the daily thousands to between 100,000 and 125,000, drawn from far and wide. Each day carries its own character: one is given to the fair itself, with *dhamal* danced to the beat of the *dhol*, the great drum; one to the lighting of the lamps, the *chiraghan*, from which the festival takes its name; and one to the ceremonial changing of the *chadar*, the cloth spread over the grave. The whole is accompanied by *qawwali*, the devotional music of the Sufis, by *langar*, the free distribution of food from the communal kitchen, and by great gatherings of devotees. It is a riotous celebration, with frenzied drumming, ecstatic dance and blazing fires.

The festival has ancient associations with the neighbouring Shalimar Gardens, and it belongs as much to the shared popular culture of Lahore as to the shrine alone; for generations it has been one of the city's defining occasions, a meeting of the sacred and the festive in which the memory of the poet saint is renewed in light, music and dance. In the past the Mela Chiraghan was among the very largest fairs of the Punjab, at its height, it is said, rivalling even the great spring festival of Basant, and it was long celebrated in and around the Shalimar Gardens themselves, the lamps spilling out from the shrine into the royal garden. In time the fair was drawn back within the shrine and its precincts, but it remains a vast and joyous occasion, binding the memory of the poet saint to the civic life of Lahore and gathering Muslims and people of other faiths, the devout and the merely festive, into the same three nights of light.

In the nineteenth century two great spring fairs were held at the *khanqah* each year. The first, on the day of Basant, was attended by Hindus and Muslims alike. In the time of Ranjit Singh the Maharaja came in person on Basant day: a silver bungalow was set up to the north and west of the enclosure, and there he held a *darbar*, a royal court, taking *nazrs*, offerings, from all the nobles and giving *khilats*, robes of honour, and he made an offering at the *khanqah*. Once the Sikh kingdom was gone, the Basant fair became an ordinary public fair. The second was the fair of lamps, still a large fair. It had formerly been held on the first of Rajab, the seventh month of the Islamic year, but it was no longer bound to the lunar month and fell in spring, with one night at the *khanqah* and the whole day in the Shalimar Gardens. The Deputy Commissioner made it a commercial fair; many merchants brought goods for sale, and the crowd stayed two days in the gardens. By the early 2000s, at the *ʿurs* of Shah Hussain, women sang before large audiences of men and women alike, with no division of the sexes among the listeners.

## Legends

Many wonders are told of him beyond those of his trials and of his love. It is told that his companions once asked for the volumes he had thrown down the well. He went to the well and told it to give them back, and at once, the story goes, the water rose, and they were floating in it, not in the least wet.

Once, it is said, the headman of a village shut him and his companions in a room until it should rain. Very well, the saint said, but feed us bread and chicken, and then we will pray. After the meal he looked to the sky and asked what delay there was now, and no sooner had he said it, the story goes, than clouds gathered and it rained heavily.

A story is told of a contest with Shaykh Hassu, who teased Hussain that he had never seen him in the court of the Prophet. The next day, it is said, when the Prophet Muhammad held his court in the spirit world, with all the prophets and saints in attendance, Shaykh Hassu among them, a child appeared. The child sat in the Prophet's lap, was passed round the assembly, and naughtily plucked some hairs from Shaykh Hassu's beard. When Hassu challenged him again, the story goes, Hussain silently produced before him a hair from his very own beard, and Hassu conceded: "So it was you, was it? Ah well, it was as a child that you got the better of me". For a Muslim man the beard is an outward sign of piety and a way of signifying his reputation.

## Legacy

Shah Hussain, or Madho Lal Hussain, occupies a place all his own in the memory of the Punjab. He is honoured as the father of the Punjabi *kafi* and so as the fountainhead of a tradition of Sufi song that runs through Bulleh Shah and Sultan Bahu down to the present day; as a Malamati saint who dramatised, in his own scandalous freedom, the truth that the love of God is worth more than the good opinion of the world; and as the lover of Madho, whose story has made his shrine a symbol of harmony between Muslim and Hindu, and of a love that death itself could not part.

In choosing to sing in Punjabi rather than the Persian of the court, and in the voice of a woman rather than that of the scholar, Hussain did something momentous: he made the mother tongue of the Punjab a vehicle of the highest spiritual expression, and founded the tradition of the mystical *kafi* that Bulleh Shah, Sultan Bahu, Shah Sharaf and Khwaja Farid would afterwards carry to its heights. The texture of ordinary Punjabi life fills his verse: the girl at her spinning wheel, the *charkha*, the bride adorned and waiting, the traveller on the long road, the lanes of the parental home that a daughter must one day leave. Into these homely images he poured the whole drama of the soul's love for God and its grief at separation, so that the humblest villager and the deepest mystic could each find themselves in his song. His Malamati freedom was itself a kind of statement, a rebuke, danced and sung in the red robe of the *malang*, to a religion of mere respectability and outward form. In his own person he fused the ecstatic and the poet, the lover and the critic, and left the Punjab a way of speaking to God that it has never since forgotten.

He was a theologian turned Sufi and a Sufi turned social rebel, a great poet in Punjabi and one of the most vivid Sufi personalities of the reign of Akbar. He stands out from the background of Persianate Sufism, for he explicitly challenged patriarchal norms: he never married, never raised children, and was never the patriarchal leader of a household. In his insistence that there were more things in common than differences between Islamic and Hindu devotion, he belongs to the tradition of Kabir and Guru Nanak, and like them he found vernacular and folk poetry the best vehicle for this fusion of Sufi and *bhakti* ideals. In his own day the city's opinion of him ran two ways at once: he earned himself disrepute among the Muslim notables of Lahore, who saw him as a dissolute drunk, but he began to earn the love and admiration of the common people as a saintly poet who stood aloof from worldly power, social status and religious reputation. In his time, too, the idea of the oneness of being was spreading in South Asia through *sama*, listening to love poetry in a devotional setting that might erupt into ecstatic gesture or rapturous dance.

More than four hundred years after his death, the weaver, mystic and fakir still commands great reverence in the Punjab as a poet and saint. He is counted the second great Punjabi poet, and with Baba Farid he stands among the predecessors of Bulleh Shah, in whose verse the Malamati overtones recall his great predecessor Shah Hussain. For one writer today he stands with Baba Farid, Guru Nanak, Bulleh Shah and Waris Shah among the figures who have become a part of his own existence; he is also among the Punjabi poets whom the educated elite of the Punjab ends up disowning, and even ridiculing.

His name is carried by institutions and recordings. A Majlis Shah Hussain in Lahore published Ghulam Yaqoob Anwar's translation of the *kafis*, *The Paths Unknown*, in 1966, and Najm Hosain Syed's *Recurrent Patterns in Punjabi Poetry* in 1968, and a college in Lahore bears his name, Shah Husain College. The Institute of Folk Heritage in Islamabad issued an audiocassette, *A Tribute to Great Mystics: Bulleh Shah, Shah Hussain, and Khawaja Farid*. The singer Abida Parveen names him first among the poets she sings, "I sing Shah Hussain, Bulle Shah…", and his verse has stood on the programme of a concert in Islamabad. Amid the crowds and the lamps of the Mela Chiraghan, and in every *kafi* sung in his voice, the poet saint of Lahore lives on.
