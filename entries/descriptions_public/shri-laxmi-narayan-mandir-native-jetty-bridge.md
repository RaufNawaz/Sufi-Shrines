## Overview

The Shri Laxmi Narayan Mandir is a Hindu temple in Karachi, remarkable for where it stands: beneath the Native Jetty Bridge, on the banks of the Chinna Creek, it is the only temple in the city set directly upon the tidal waters that open onto the Arabian Sea. Reputed to be some two hundred years old, it is dedicated to Lakshmi, the goddess of fortune, and Narayan, the preserving god Vishnu, and it is among the oldest continually operating temples of Karachi. It is cherished above all for the seaside rites that its unique location makes possible.

## Devotion by the Sea

The pairing of Lakshmi and Narayan is one of the most beloved in the Hindu tradition, expressing the union of prosperity and divine sustenance. Lakshmi is the spouse of Lord Vishnu, and on the wall of one Valmiki temple she was pictured resting on a lotus flower. In Saidpur, where every shop kept idols, Lakshmi was the most popular of them.

At Karachi the temple's devotion has been shaped by its place at the water's edge. Overlooking the creek where it meets the sea, the shrine is a favoured site for the rituals that require flowing or tidal water, most importantly *asthi visarjan*, the immersion of the ashes of the dead, by which Hindus commit the remains of the departed to sacred waters. For a community far from the great *ghats*, the stepped landings, of India's rivers and from its coastal *tirthas*, or places of pilgrimage, the temple on the Chinna Creek has offered an irreplaceable link to the wider ritual geography of the faith. Within the sanctuary, alongside the principal images, devotees also honour *Hanuman*, the monkey god, and *Sai Baba* of Shirdi.

The temple's setting is at once its glory and its vulnerability. Tucked beneath the approaches of the Native Jetty Bridge, it looks out across the water, and the sea has long lapped close to its precincts. Its festivals mark the turning of the devotional year: *Ganesh Chaturthi*, the birthday of Ganesha, is celebrated here, together with the spring festival of *Holi* and *Raksha Bandhan*, the observance of the bond between brothers and sisters. On these days the modest shrine fills with the Hindu families of Karachi, for whom it is a place of gathering as much as of worship.

## A Painting of Ramdeo Pir

In a Karachi temple of Lakshmi Narayan, taken to be this one, stands a painting of Ramdeo Pir, one of the deified heroes whom the Hindus of Sindh shared with their Muslim neighbours. In Sindh the most popular of these heroes were Rama Pir, also called Ramdeo or Ramdev, Pithoro Pir, Pabuji and Guga Pir. Ramdeo Pir is still known in Sindh as Rama Pir, and in Sindh he is today integrated into the temples dedicated to the divinities worshipped throughout India; the painting in Karachi is an instance of that. The case of Pithoro Pir shows the variety of the negotiations between Sufism, the mystical path of Islam, and Hinduism in Sindh.

Such figures belong to a wider pattern. In Sindh a number of sacred figures have a dual religious identity, Muslim and Hindu: the Muslim identity is always related to Sufism, while the Hindu side usually belongs to the Nathpanth, the path of the Nath yogis. The Hindus of Sindh are related to Sufism in three ways. The most common is a Sufi tomb visited by both Muslims and Hindus; also relatively common is a saint with a double identity, Muslim and Hindu; and there is the Sufi *darbar*, or shrine, managed by Hindus. The large majority of the Hindus used to visit the Sufi shrines. That does not make them Sufis, but it does mean that for them the Sufis are a part of their religious legacy.

The Muslim Sufis of Sindh were mostly attracted by the Vishnuite school of Hinduism, the tradition devoted to Vishnu. Among the Hindus themselves, the worship of Krishna had already developed among the Amils in Sindh in the late nineteenth century, and the Vallabhachari sect was accepted by some members of the Amil community. A number of Sufis of nineteenth century Sindh, among them Sachal, Qutub Ali Shah and Bedil, included Krishna in their Sufi poetry, and although Sindh was Muslim dominated, Krishnaite *bhakti*, loving devotion to Krishna, was influential among the *wujudi* Sufis, those who held to the unity of being. Wall paintings of Krishna in Hindu temples could mean that there was a kind of Krishnaite revival among the Sindhi Hindus in the nineteenth century.

The Hindu share in the Muslim orders is more visible in Sindh than in the Punjab. Hindu writers wrote mystical poems in which they used the imagery of the Muslims, and they composed poems in honour of the Prophet.

## Under Threat

The very features that make the temple precious have placed it in jeopardy. The development of the port and the construction that has crowded the shoreline near the site have threatened both its access to the seawater essential for its rituals and the integrity of the structure itself. In September 2012, after a petition brought by the local Hindu community, the Sindh High Court issued an order restraining the Karachi Port Trust from demolishing the temple. The episode drew wide attention to the fragility of the city's surviving Hindu heritage and to the community's determination to defend it.

In the early twenty first century, government funding for the places of minority communities brought a wave of temple restorations across Sindh, coinciding with changes in the political landscape. Yet Karachi has also been a city of violence against Hindus, and the Hindus of Pakistan, Sindh included, have come more and more under the threat of the country's radical Muslims. After the demolition of the Babri Mosque in December 1992, Hindu communities in Sindh and Quetta were attacked.

## Karachi

Among the old temples of Karachi is one at Ram Bagh, an area now called Aram Bagh, an added letter that, it has been said, neutralises its religious sense. The temple had a painted Krishna with his maidens under its roof and a peacock guarding the entrance. It was much more elaborately decorated, and its community much larger, than in Lahore. An old man there gave its age as a hundred and fifty years; an officer did not know exactly how old it was, but said that it was old and had existed before Partition. Like the temple at Clifton, it is thought to have been a Hindu temple already in the time of Guru Nanak, the founder of the Sikh faith, and the Clifton temple has been identified as the temple that Nanak visited on his tour of Sindh.

A temple within the Port Grand complex had a newly built mosque for tourists right on top of it. There were separate entrances for women and for men, food was being prepared, probably as an offering from a rich Hindu, and rooms were set aside for different deities. A mural showed a male deity, Jhule Lal, sitting on top of a giant fish. Stairs led down into water blackened by industrial waste; there were fishermen's boats, and pigeons were fed with rice.

At the time of Partition, Karachi was a minor port city; migrants from India made it the largest city of Pakistan. Its port was a modest structure, a minor tower with a clock placed within it, a symbol of colonial rule. The city's Hindu fisherfolk lived here before Kolachi became Karachi, and they are now the only connection between the city and the old village of Kolachi. Legend explains the name: an old woman known as Mai Kolachi, whose son was killed by a fish, is said to have torn the fish apart and retrieved him, and so the place came to be known as Kolachi. It is told, too, that on the quay of Karachi port a disciple about to sail to Iraq saw his master's face in the water.

In colonial Karachi, Sir Charles Napier, the conqueror and first governor of Sindh, built a Masonic lodge in 1843, and the Theosophical Society was founded in the city in 1896 by Dhunjishaw, Jamshed Nusserwanji and Dayaram Jethmal. A printing press was brought to Karachi in the 1850s, but for Sufism the main event was the printing in 1866 of the *Shah jo Risalo*.

Karachi has also been a supplier of Hindu worship elsewhere in Pakistan. Boys from Bahawalnagar bought an idol of the goddess Durga there; a vendor's idols were brought from Karachi, where a reasonable number of Hindus still live, or from India through illegal channels; and pilgrims came from as far as Karachi for *Navratri*, the festival of nine nights. The city is the seat of the Nagnath and Jagnath *akhāṛās*, houses of Hindu ascetics, the staging city for pilgrims from India, the home of the Devipujak-Vagri community, and the start of the road to the goddess Hinglaj, a route of about 240 kilometres from Narayanpur. The growth of Karachi swallowed the old pilgrim road: as the areas where the early stops lay were built over, all but a few of the resting places disappeared completely.

In the late twentieth century Karachi, with Hyderabad in India, was one of two important urban centres of Sufism where members of the Qawwal Bachche lineage actively maintained their special heritage of *qawwali*, the devotional music of the Sufis. In the early twenty first century the teaching shaykh of one Sufi order, Siraj 'Ali Muhammad, led weekly *halqa*, communal sessions of *zikr*, the remembrance of God, on Thursday evenings in Karachi. A Sidi congregation is held in April at Manghopir in Karachi, and at Mango Pir, near Karachi, a miracle of alligators is said to have happened.

## Sindh and Its Hindus

Under the emperor Aurangzeb, the greatest river of the Mughal province of Thatta was the Sind, down which the merchants of Multan and Bhakkar shipped their goods to Thatta. The salt sea was the province's southern bound, and its life turned on boats, which carried most of its traffic and were reckoned, small and great, at forty thousand. Its diet rested on rice, curds and fish.

The Lohanas, the trading caste that formed the great body of the Hindus of Sindh, and related mercantile groups followed two main cults, the Nanakpanth, the path of Nanak, and the Daryapanth. The Daryapanth, in all probability the older of the two, is based on the deification of the Indus River, and it was served by a priestly caste, the Thakurs. The Thakurs were the priests of Jhulelal; they were settled in different cities of Sindh, and from 1947 onwards they migrated to India. In the nineteenth century the Nanakpanth was probably the dominant religious path among the Hindu Sindhis. With the growth of new trading centres in Sindh, Karachi above all, the Lohanas invested in land, and by the end of the nineteenth century they had become powerful landowners, known locally as *zamindars*. It is often claimed that Sindh had no caste system, but there were many untouchables in Sindh.

In 1941 the Hindus made up about 21 per cent of the population of Sindh. By early November 1947, 246,000 Hindus had already left Sindh by sea, air or rail, and the Hindu Sindhis started leaving especially after the Karachi riots of January 1948; estimates of how many left in all differ, from around a million to 800,000 or fewer. Accounts differ, too, over whether Sindh was free of communal strife; its communal violence never reached the level of the Punjab's, but it was not absent. In the narrative of the Hindu Sufi Rochaldas, the violence that spread through Sindh was not perpetrated by the Muslim Sindhis but by the Muhajirs, the Muslim refugees from India, who wanted to take over the houses of the Hindus, at least in Karachi. The departure of the Hindu elite and the arrival of the Muhajirs dramatically altered the local demography. After 1947 the *darbars* of Sindh were for the most part preserved, with different arrangements for their management.

Although a large majority of its Hindus migrated to India from 1947 onwards, Sindh is still the province of Pakistan with the most significant Hindu population and home to the majority of Pakistan's Hindus. By the census of 1998 the Hindus were the largest minority in the country. The majority of them are based in Sindh and southern Punjab. Pakistan's Hindus are put at between 6 and 10 million, an estimate that varies widely; over 90 per cent of them belong to underprivileged tribes and subcastes, and in Sindh, where they number several million, the majority belong to the scheduled castes. Many middle and upper class Hindus in Sindh are followers of the Nanak *panth*, the path of Nanak, and in the early twenty first century the formula *Sab ka malik ek hai*, all have one master, was very popular among the Hindus of Sindh.

The singer Alan Faqir spoke of the province, which the poetic tradition calls Sindhri, as a land that had been looted, plundered and assaulted and yet goes on: it has not died, it will not die, it has existed for centuries and it shall exist; Sindhri is beaten, and yet Sindhri goes on. Among the lines he recited was this:

*Punnu is not in the Kec / Sind is not the amir*

Its second half means that Sindh is not in the position of leadership, or amirship. It is one of Alan Faqir's own political readings of the tales of women.

## Legacy

The Shri Laxmi Narayan Mandir endures as a small but singular monument: a living temple that has kept its place on the Karachi waterfront through two centuries of change, and that continues to serve the ritual needs of a community whose presence in the city long predates the creation of Pakistan. Its survival, secured in part through the courts, stands as a testament to the resilience of Karachi's Hindus and to the enduring importance of the sea in their devotional life. It is also taken to be the Karachi temple of Lakshmi Narayan where a painting of Ramdeo Pir, one of the deified heroes of Sindh, bears witness to the shared religious culture of the province.
