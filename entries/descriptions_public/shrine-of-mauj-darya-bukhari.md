## Overview

On Edward Road, near the old Customs House in Anarkali, Lahore, stands the octagonal tomb of Hazrat Meeran Muhammad Shah Bukhari, popularly called Mauj Darya, the "Wave of the River". He was a saint of the Suhrawardi order and came of the Sadaat of Uch Sharif, the *sayyids*, or descendants of the Prophet, of that town, and in Lahore he is remembered above all as a spiritual confidant of the Mughal emperor Akbar. He died in 1604, and he lies in a small graveyard in the company of his family, his spiritual successors and the *gaddi nasheen*, the custodians who sit on the saint's seat.

The shrine is one of scores of Lahore shrines administered by the Punjab Auqaf and Religious Affairs Department, the Mehkama Auqaf, the department of religious endowments. It continues to draw a devoted following, with its gatherings of Thursday night and a yearly *ʿurs*, the commemoration of the saint's death, in the month of Rabi-ul-Awal.

## The Saint

He was born in 1533 in Uch Sharif, the son of Safi-ud-Din Bukhari of the Sadaat of that town. He was a descendant of Jalaluddin Surkh-Posh Bukhari, whose name is given in more than one form, and his descent is traced through Syed Jalal-ud-Din Jahaniyan Jahangasht. His line runs from father to son: Miran Muhammad Shah was the son of Sayyid Safi al-Din, son of Sayyid Nizam al-Din, son of Sayyid Ilm al-Din the Second, son of Jalal al-Din, son of Sayyid Ilm al-Din the First, son of Sayyid Nasir al-Din, son of Sayyid Jalal al-Din Makhdoom Jahaniyan, whose own father was Sayyid Ahmad Kabir al-Din. Sanctity, nobility and the gift of miracles are held to have been his by inheritance.

He was an adherent of the Suhrawardi *silsila*, the spiritual lineage founded by Bahauddin Zakariya of Multan, and in his youth he became a *hafiz-e-Quran*, one who has memorised the whole Quran. At Uch he was famous for his renunciation, scrupulousness, piety and miracles, and there he held the seat of the *khanqah*, the Sufi lodge, of his forefathers. A Persian quatrain names him Hazrat Miran Muhammad Shah and calls him a Suhrawardi *pir*, a spiritual guide, in the line:

سہروردی پیر شیخ با صفا

*A line of the quatrain, which calls him a Suhrawardi pir.*

His name is met in several forms. He is called Miran Muhammad Shah, known as Sayyid Mauj Darya Bukhari Lahori; in full, Hazrat Miran Muhammad Shah Mauj Darya Suhrawardi Lahori; and Sayyid Miran Shah Bukhari, whom people also call Mauj Darya. Accounts of how he came by the name Mauj Darya differ. Popular tradition holds that he earned it by miraculously turning the River Ravi, which was prone to flooding, away from Lahore.

Above all he is remembered as a spiritual confidant of Akbar. The shrine's tradition relates that the emperor was foretold, through the saint's father, of a windstorm and a lit lamp that would mark the saint's presence at the fort of Chittor, and that when both signs appeared and the Rajput army surrendered, Akbar became his disciple. In the same tradition his prayer and intercession are credited with securing the fort's surrender to Akbar. After Akbar's campaign at Chittorgarh the saint came to Lahore at the emperor's request, and Akbar was his great devotee.

Legend credits him with giving away to the poor a substantial reward that Akbar granted him near Batala; accounts of the size and place of the grant differ. It is said that on the saint's arrival in Lahore the emperor gifted him the land on which he settled. He was the first to settle at the place where his tomb stands, coming there in Akbar's reign, and he founded a whole quarter of the city. His *darasgah*, the seat of learning in which he lies buried, is said still to hold land documents bearing Akbar's own signature, though accounts of where the documents were kept differ.

He lived mostly in Lahore, and there he kept alive the work of a *shaikh*, a spiritual master. He also kept a second household, where his second wife lived and where there were *havelis*, large houses, and he lived sometimes there and sometimes in Lahore. In Akbar's reign he was famous as a God-fearing man of sanctity and miracles, and he was counted an elder possessed of miracles, devout and ascetic. He is remembered, too, as a missionary presence who drew many Hindus and Sikhs to Islam.

Among his disciples was Shah Abdul Razzaq Makki, his *murid*, or pledged disciple. Abdul Razzaq Makki came to Lahore, took service with Akbar and then renounced the world, and from Mauj Darya, it is said, he received such grace that he became the *qutb*, the pole of the saints, of his age. Of Shaikh Abdul Razzaq Makki of the Nila Gumbad it is told that he reached such perfection that Mauj Darya himself honoured him. One of the saint's *khalifas*, the deputies who carry on a master's teaching, was a Sayyid Abd al-Razzaq of Dola Wari. A Sayyid Abdul Razzaq worshipped nightly at the saint's tomb, and his own tomb, it is said, was ordered in a dream.

He died in 1604; accounts of the year and day of his death differ. His son Sayyid Safi-ud-Din brought his body to Lahore, and in the nineteenth century people regarded as blessed the mosque where the body was washed.

By the 1870s his death had been set in a Persian chronogram, a verse whose letters, read as numbers, give a year. The year it gives, written twice within it, is 1014 of the Hijri, or Islamic, calendar, one of the differing accounts of the year of his death:

چوں محمد شاہ ملک عاقبت
در مقام قرب حقانی نشست
از حضور ۱۰۱۴ھ آمد عیان تاریخ او
نیز سرور گفت مرد حق پرست ۱۰۱۴ھ

*A chronogram on the death of Muhammad Shah*

## His Family

He married twice. His first wife, Syedna Bibi Fatima Sani, is remembered as "Bibi Killah" and popularly as Mai Fatima; accounts of her name differ. She was a daughter of Sayyid Abd al-Qadir the Third, son of Sayyid Abd al-Wahhab, son of Sayyid Muhammad Bala Pir Gilani. She died in the year 1016 of the Hijri calendar, and she rests in a separate shrine facing his own, near the historic Jain Mandir, though accounts of where she lies differ. She is remembered as a saint in her own right. She objected to his second marriage. A brother of the saint lies in a domed tomb of baked brick, built in the year 1017 of the Hijri calendar, in the corner of an enclosure at Bibi Pak Daman.

He is most often said to have had three sons, though accounts of their number and of their mothers differ. Sayyid Safi al-Din, named for his grandfather, and Sayyid Baha al-Din were born of his first wife, and Shah Shihab al-Din of the second; tradition tells that Shihab al-Din could take the form of a lion. By the 1870s Shihab al-Din's descendants lived at Tibala, where he had settled, and Safi al-Din's in Lahore; accounts of the town where Shihab al-Din's line settled differ.

It is told that Shihab al-Din was put to school at four years, four months and four days, by the custom of the law of Muhammad. He is said to have slapped his teacher and recited the whole Quran, and his father, greatly pleased, offered two *rakʿat*, cycles of prayer, in thanks.

The division in the household outlived the saint. When Shihab al-Din's body was brought for burial, Safi-ud-Din refused it a place in his father's tomb, saying that only those born of the chaste womb of the first wife might be buried there, and Shihab al-Din was buried elsewhere.

His *langar*, the free communal kitchen, has continued, devotees say, under his descendants to this day. In the 1880s his descendants in Lahore, Sayyid Husain Shah among them, traced their line to him through Safi-ud-Din. In the nineteenth century Sayyid Asghar Ali was among the Sayyids of sound descent in Lahore, and the descendants of the servants of his *langar* were still living.

## The Shrine

At the very spot where the tomb stands, his house stood, and a public *langar* ran there continually. Hundreds of the poor and destitute ate at his *langar*; accounts of how many *langars* he kept differ. Accounts of when the tomb was built differ too, but most hold that it was raised in his lifetime by order of Akbar.

Architecturally the tomb is unusual among Lahore's shrines for its octagonal plan, with a low dome, corner towers at each of its eight angles, and a mixture of recessed blind arches and plain panelled facades, a style thought to echo Multani funerary tradition.

In the 1880s the octagonal tomb stood on a platform of baked brick, with its door on the south. Over the door a niche carried, in a bold hand, a Persian inscription saying that the sacred tomb of the choicest of those who have attained, the exemplar of the gnostics, accepted at the court of the Creator, Miran Sayyid Muhammad Shah Mauj Darya Bukhari, may God illumine his resting place, was built in the reign of Akbar Badshah. There were verses on each of its eight sides. By the 1870s his lofty tomb was a place of pilgrimage for the people, and by the 1880s the *khanqah* was counted among the famous and well known places of Lahore. In the nineteenth century the building stood on a high mound, with its entrance on the south, a *dalan*, or open hall, and two small rooms where the trustees sat, four lattices of brick, and verses over the doors.

The saint lies in a small graveyard in the company of his family, his spiritual successors and *gaddi nasheen*, and his cook, Allahu Samad. In the 1880s the tomb itself held, beside the saint's own grave, the graves of his sons, Safi-ud-Din among them, and of others of his offspring. Outside the tomb, on a platform, lies a great-grandson of the saint, a son of Sayyid Abd al-Rahim, son of Safi-ud-Din, of whom too people said that he was a saint and a man of God. The small tomb of the servant Mian Murid was built by order of Akbar.

In the 1880s the enclosure held a mosque of baked brick, dwellings for the servants of the *khanqah*, a well and other buildings, all of baked brick. In the nineteenth century there stood an old mosque with the name of Abdur Rahman, an officer of the army, over its arch, along with a well, trees and *hujras*, small cells.

The shrine gained unwelcome national attention in 2018, when construction of the underground Anarkali station of the Orange Line Metro Train demolished its mosque, *langar khana* and courtyard, with tunnelling reported to pass close to the saint's grave in apparent violation of the rules for a heritage buffer zone. The episode became a flashpoint in Lahore's debates over balancing infrastructure development against the protection of religious heritage. Yet devotees hold that for all the government construction pressing around it, nothing has ever tarnished the shrine's beauty.

It is reported that the shrine's upkeep ran down a long line before the Auqaf Department took it on: from the emperor Akbar through the rulers who followed him and Maharaja Ranjit Singh, and through the saint's own descendants. In Maharaja Ranjit Singh's time forty rupees a month were received for the expenses of the *khanqah*; by the 1880s nothing at all was received. Among those who later held his seat as *sajjada nashin*, the head of the shrine, were Hazrat Shams Shah and Sayyid Hayat Shah.

## Devotion and Festivals

Daily devotion includes the recitation of the *Fatiha*, the opening chapter of the Quran, the lighting of *diyas*, small oil lamps, the making of vows, or *mannat*, and the presentation of *niyaz*, consecrated offerings. The gatherings of Thursday night, Jummay Raat, remain the busiest of the week.

The annual *ʿurs* is held on the 17th to the 19th of Rabi-ul-Awal, with a Mela Chiraagha, a festival of lights, the ceremonial washing, or *gusal*, of the tomb, the changing of its *chadar*, the sheet of cloth laid over the grave, and *langar* throughout. Some 2,200 to 2,500 visitors come on an ordinary day, and between 225,000 and 300,000 across the *ʿurs*. In the 1880s, too, an *ʿurs* was held at the shrine every year, and a great multitude gathered.

## Legends

His cook, Allahu Samad, is himself the subject of one of the shrine's most striking tales, in which the saint, to answer sceptics, had his cook sit unharmed in a burning cauldron.

Other wonders are recounted of him. A cauldron of rice that he blessed during a shortage is said to have fed the people for nearly seven days. He is said to have prayed over brackish irrigation water for local landowners, and it turned sweet; tellings differ over whose prayer it was. A tree planted in his lifetime, "the blessed tree", is said never to have needed cutting.

One story turns on a *ber* tree, the jujube, in the courtyard of his wife's house, while the saint was in the courtyard of the *khanqah*. Miran Muhammad Shah asked where she had got this bounty, and she answered that this wealth was her ancestral inheritance. A tree at her house could be seen from the *khanqah* of Hazrat Mauj Darya Bukhari. A miracle of a *chadar* is told there too, after which, it is said, the Bukhari and Gilani Sayyids no longer married one another.

It is also reported that books about him by Muslim, Hindu and Sikh authors alike are widely read.

## The Setting

The saint came to Lahore in Akbar's reign, when the emperor had turned to the settlement of Lahore from the start of his reign, built a city and a palace there, raised large buildings and stayed for a time. The quarter that the saint founded was known as Mohalla Mauj Darya. It stayed settled to the end of the Chughtai, or Mughal, sultanate, and it was ruined in the time of the Sikh plunder and the attacks of Ahmad Shah; part of its land went into the roads.

Anarkali, the market district in which the shrine stands, is named after the legendary lover of the Mughal emperor Jahangir, and over the years the area became the largest tyre market in the city.
