## Overview

At Kot Mithan, also called Mithankot, in the Rajanpur district on the edge of the Cholistan desert, lies the shrine of Khwaja Ghulam Farid (1845 to 1901). He is honoured as the supreme poet of the Seraiki language and one of the last great masters of the classical Sufi *kafi*, the short mystical lyric. A Chishti saint of the Kot Mithan line, he poured the mysticism of *wahdat al-wujud*, the unity of being, into verses sung in the sweet Seraiki of the southern Punjab and steeped in the landscape of the Rohi, the great desert he loved. His shrine is the spiritual centre of the Seraiki belt, and his *kafis* are sung across it to this day.

He was one of the great Chishti masters of the nineteenth century, and a popular Seraiki poet of its last decades. His verse has passed into the repertoire of singers such as Abida Parvin and of the qawwals, and into the devotions of the Sindhi Hindu Sufis. His discourses survive in Persian, compiled between 1893 and 1901 and translated into Urdu in 1979.

## The Saint

Ghulam Farid, also known as Khwaja Farid, was born at Chachran, near Kot Mithan, in 1845, though accounts of the year differ. He came of a Sayyid Koreja family of the Chishti Sufi tradition. Sayyids are descendants of the Prophet Muhammad, and the Chishti order of Sufis, or Muslim mystics, founded by Moinuddin Chishti, is prominent in South Asia. His father and, after him, his elder brother were the *sajjada nashins*, the spiritual guides, of their line, and on his brother's death Ghulam Farid succeeded to the seat.

Learned in Arabic and Persian and in the religious sciences, he was nonetheless a poet and mystic to the core. He is famous for the years he spent in the wilderness of the Rohi, the Cholistan desert, whose dunes, herders and camel caravans fill his poetry with a local beauty. He lived in the time of the Nawabs, the rulers, of Bahawalpur, and in matters of the spirit he was their critic; he is remembered for counselling the ruler in the interest of the people.

He also took disciples. Among them was Khwaja Hasan Nizami, whose brother made him a *murid*, a pledged disciple, of Ghulam Farid at the age of sixteen.

Ghulam Farid died in 1901 at Chachran and was buried at Kot Mithan. He is not to be confused with a namesake, Maulvi Ghulam Farid Lahori, who died in 1216 AH and is buried in the Miani graveyard at Lahore, a century earlier and in another city.

## The Poetry

Khwaja Ghulam Farid's fame rests on his *Diwan*, the collected volume of his Seraiki *kafis*. These are hundreds of short mystical lyrics, cast, like those of Shah Hussain and Bulleh Shah before him, largely in the longing voice of a woman for her absent beloved, in which the Beloved is God and the desert of Rohi the very landscape of the soul. His verse unites the boldest metaphysics of the unity of being with the sweetest folk idiom of the southern Punjab, and its refrains are known by heart across the Seraiki country. A further theme of his work is the combination of spiritual exercise and interior pilgrimage; pilgrimage, with its sacred geography, is itself a subject of his poetry.

The *kafi* as a form, a stanzaic poem built on folk themes and techniques, was easy to understand and to memorise. It served as the ideal means of spreading mystical teaching among people who could not read and depended on knowledge passed on by word of mouth, and in the early twenty first century most Punjabis and Sindhis were still more likely to have heard a *kafi* than read one.

The woman who speaks in his *kafis* mourns her separation from her beloved, who has gone to a distant land. Her voice is borrowed from the romance of the Punjab: Ghulam Farid also takes up the voice of Hir, a tragic heroine of its folk tales, to speak of *ishq-e haqiqi*, the ultimate spiritual union, through the imagery of *ishq-e majazi*, earthly love. The figure belongs to the mystical poetry of the region at large. In Sindh as in the Punjab, and rarely elsewhere in the Muslim world, the longing soul is represented in the form of a woman, and the tragic heroines of the Sindhi and Punjabi folk tales, Hir, Sassui, Sohni and many others, stand for the human soul in search of the beloved.

Most famous of all is his declaration that the Beloved is everything, the only reality:

میڈا عشق وی توں، میڈا یار وی توں

*You are my love, and You are my beloved; You alone are all.*

His *kafis* are sung to this day by the folk singers and qawwals of the Seraiki belt, and he is honoured as the poet who gave that language its highest literary and spiritual expression.

One of his lyrics takes up the lovers Layla and Majnun. The singer Abida Parvin sang this song of a black Layla at her Open University concert, attributing the poetry to Ghulam Farid. In the song, when people asked Majnun about his black Layla, he answered with an example from the Quran: if the pages of the Quran can be white while the ink is black, what is so wrong in his loving a woman of colour? It is the poet himself who asks the question on Majnun's behalf, and in one of the lyric's lines he names himself: *Ākhe Ghulām Farīd-ā O jeh nāl dil aṛ pave O gorī hove yā kālī*.

## The Discourses

Beside the poetry stands a book of his conversations. His discourses, or *malfuzat*, were compiled in Persian between 1893 and 1901 as *Maqabis al-majalis*, The Lessons of the Spiritual Assemblies, a work also known as *Isharat-i Faridi*. Christopher Shackle rendered them into English as *The Teachings of Khwaja Farid* (Multan, 1978), and in 1979 Captain Wahid Bakhsh Siyal translated them into Urdu.

The translator was a twentieth century shaykh, and his introduction became a work in its own right. In his long Urdu introduction he gave more than a third of 225 pages to explaining and defending *samaʿ*, spiritual listening, the Sufi session of music, and one chapter of the work is called *Zauq-i samaʿ*, The Taste of Samaʿ.

Through their translator the discourses kept a living Chishti readership. The will of Wahid Bakhsh's master, Shahidullah Faridi, says that Wahid Bakhsh had begun a circle of *zikr*, the remembrance of God, and spiritual guidance in Bahawalpur, and that he should spread the *silsila*, the Sufi chain of teaching, in that place only. In effect, it has been said, his master marked out the southern Punjab as Wahid Bakhsh's own spiritual domain.

## The Shrine and Its Devotion

Khwaja Ghulam Farid's shrine at Kot Mithan is the chief centre of pilgrimage of the Seraiki region. It is kept by his descendants and draws devotees throughout the year for prayer, blessing and the singing of his verse. The annual *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of the saint's death, gathers great crowds for days of *kafi* singing, *qawwali*, the devotional music of the Sufis, and *langar*, the free communal kitchen. The town of Kot Mithan is known above all as the resting place of the poet, and his memory is inseparable from the cultural identity of the Seraiki people. A book of his verse, *Kalam-e Khwaja Ghulam Farid*, has been advertised as the work of Khwaja Ghulam Farid of Kot Mithan Sharif, a style that adds to the town's name *Sharif*, noble, the honorific given to holy places.

His verse is sung far beyond the town. Abida Parvin's repertoire includes him, and he is among the spirits invoked at the outset of performances of Sufi poetry. Her improvised performance at Islamabad drew on Shah Abdul Latif's poetry and on Ghulam Farid's texts, and two items on the programme of that concert were his, both in Seraiki. His name also stands in the full title of a recording, *A Tribute to Great Mystics*. Surraiya Multanikar, a Seraiki singer of Sufi poetry, and of his in particular, has said that she is specially blessed by her *pir*, her spiritual guide, a Gilani Syed, and that because of his prayer there is much *sur*, melody, in her voice.

He is also among the poets of the qawwals' repertoire, as is Mehr Ali Shah. His verse has crossed into the devotions of another community too. Among the Sindhi Hindu Sufis, Sufi poetry by poets who were not Sindhis sometimes played a liturgical role in their performances, and Ghulam Farid is quoted there, as are Bulleh Shah and Shah Bahu; Seraiki is a language in which many Sindhis also composed poetry.

## The Setting

Kot Mithan was a place of learning a century before Ghulam Farid. In the *qasba*, the small town, of Kot Mithan, Qazi Muhammad Aqil kept a *madrasa*, a religious school, and there, in his early days, Khwaja Sulaiman Taunsvi studied with him and acquired the outward sciences. The teacher is identified as Khwaja Muhammad Aqil, of the Faruqi family, whose father was Muhammad Sharif; he died on 14 Rajab 1229 AH. Sulaiman Taunsvi, who was born in 1184 AH and died in 1267 AH, was the saint through whom the Chishti Nizami Fakhri line spread most widely in the Punjab. On his mission to Lahore and Bahawalpur of 1832 to 1833, Captain C. M. Wade made a voyage from Lodiana to Mithankot by the river Satlaj.

Seraiki, the language of Ghulam Farid's verse, is spoken mainly in the southern Punjab, with Multan as its capital, and also in northern Sindh; in the Middle Ages the southern Punjab was a part of Sindh. On the road south through Bahawalpur the boundary between Sindh and Punjab becomes a blur and the cultures of the two merge, and Seraiki has been called an amalgamation of the two languages: the Seraiki of Multan is closer to Punjabi, while further south it is more like Sindhi. Seraiki forms the transition to Punjabi, and so the imagery of all three linguistic areas, Sindhi, Seraiki and Punjabi, is very closely related. The legend of Sohni is sung in Sufi poetry in all the regions of Sindh and in the Seraiki speaking area of the Punjab.

## Legacy

For Seraiki, Khwaja Ghulam Farid is what Bhittai is to Sindhi and Bulleh Shah to Punjabi: the poet who raised his mother tongue to the summit of mystical expression and made of its desert landscape an image of the soul's love for God. His *kafis* remain among the most cherished poetry of the southern Punjab, and his shrine at Kot Mithan endures as the spiritual and cultural heart of the Seraiki belt.

His work has been carried into print and into English largely from Multan. Besides *The Teachings of Khwaja Farid*, Christopher Shackle translated *Fifty Poems of Khawaja Farid* (Multan, 1983). Multan editions of his work also include *Kalām-e Farid*, edited by Kefi Jampuri, *Naghmā-e Sehrā*, translated by Kashafi Multani, and M. Abdul Wasi's *Divān-e Farid Ma Dhorājāt*.
