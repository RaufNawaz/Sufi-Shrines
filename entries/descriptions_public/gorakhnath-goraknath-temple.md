## Overview

The Gorakhnath Temple, also written Goraknath, stands within the historic walled precinct of Gor Khatri, in the heart of the old city of Peshawar in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa. It is dedicated to Guru Gorakhnath, the great saint and *yogi*, a master of the ascetic discipline of yoga, who is venerated as the founder of the *Nath* monastic order, and it is one of the very few Hindu temples in the city still standing and in use. The present temple dates from the nineteenth century, but it sits atop one of the most storied and continuously sacred pieces of ground in the subcontinent, whose religious associations stretch back some two thousand years. Closed for decades after Partition, it was reopened for worship in 2011 after a legal campaign by Pakistan's Hindu community, and its revival has made it a symbol of the region's layered spiritual heritage.

## Gor Khatri, a Sacred Ground

The temple cannot be separated from Gor Khatri itself, an elevated mound in Peshawar's old city that has been held sacred, in various traditions, from the Gandhara period to the Mughal era. Its name is popularly taken to mean the grave of the *Kshatriya*, the warrior class of Hindu society, or of warriors, hinting at ancient associations with burial or with heroes. Excavation, notably the vertical digs begun by Professor Farzand Ali Durrani in the early 1990s, has revealed successive layers of occupation descending some fifteen metres and spanning many civilisations, from the Indo-Greeks of the second century BCE through the Hindu Shahi and later periods.

Gor Khatri is identified with Gorakhtari, a place in Peshawar whose name matches its own. The spot has been associated with "the tower of Buddha's bowl", and like other Buddhist sites taken over by yogis, it became a centre of their activity. Gor Khatri was long a celebrated resort of the *Nath* yogis. After his return from the Middle East, Guru Nanak, the founder of the Sikh faith, seems to have stayed in the Punjab, visiting Ajodhan, Multan and Gorakhtari from time to time. Babur, the founder of the Mughal dynasty, came to Gor Khatri during his forays into the region, drawn by curiosity about the *Shaiva* ascetics, the devotees of the god Shiva, who dominated the place, and he mentions the visit himself.

In the reigns of the emperors Akbar and Jahangir the place was an important centre of pilgrimage for the yogis. Near the city, Kor Khatri, the temple of the *jogis*, or yogis, was famous, and it was demolished in the reign of Shah Jahan.

Under the Mughals the site was transformed: in 1641 Jahan Ara Begum, the daughter of Shah Jahan, built a grand caravanserai here, the Sarai Jahanabad, an inn for caravans and travellers, together with a mosque, a bath and wells for their comfort. In the nineteenth century, during Sikh rule, the precinct served as the residence and headquarters of the Italian mercenary general Paolo Avitabile, governor of Peshawar.

## The Temple

The present Gorakhnath Temple was built in the middle of the nineteenth century, around 1851, within the Gor Khatri complex. At the Partition of British India in 1947 much of Peshawar's Hindu population departed, and the temple was left abandoned. It remained closed to worship for some six decades, its future in doubt amid changing custody of the site.

Its restoration came through the persistence of the Hindu community. In 2011, following a petition and litigation before the Peshawar High Court, the court ordered that the temple be reopened for worship and recognised as part of the site's heritage. The reopening, after roughly sixty years of silence, was widely reported as a significant moment for religious freedom and for the preservation of minority heritage in Pakistan, and Hindus could once again worship at the ancient shrine.

## Guru Gorakhnath

Guru Gorakhnath is one of the most influential figures of medieval Indian spirituality. He is traditionally held to have lived somewhere between the ninth and twelfth centuries, and he is revered as a *mahasiddha*, a great perfected master, and as the founder of the *Nath* order of yogis. He is remembered as the disciple of Matsyendranath. He is believed to have been born from the sweat of Shiva's breast, and it is told that he saved his master, in whose service he had earlier lost an eye. He is also believed to be an *avatar*, an incarnation, of Shiva. Much mystery and legend surround him, but he is known to have written treatises in Sanskrit.

The *Nath* tradition he shaped, with its emphasis on *hatha yoga*, the yoga of bodily discipline, on asceticism and on esoteric discipline, spread widely across the subcontinent and left a deep imprint on popular religion. The transmission of the Nath doctrine rests on a trinity, Shiva, Matsyendranath and Gorakhnath, and the Adi Nath, the First Lord of the Naths, is the Shiva of the Hindus. The Nath tradition venerates nine Naths and eighty four Siddhas, or perfected ones, though this does not mean that these figures are historical.

Gorakhnath is credited with founding the order of the *Kanphata*, the Split Ear Yogis, as the Naths are also called, and its framework has been taken to illustrate his remarkable gift for organisation and foresight. The order has its rite of piercing the ears, and its rite of initiation was still current in the late twentieth century. In Persian writing the chief Hindu figure is the *jogi*, a word usually used for the Nathpanthis, a Shaiva school of renunciants who followed Gorakhnath. The Naths lived in forests, wandered in towns and also founded permanent monasteries. Their goal was mastery of the body: in their teaching, the perfect yogi can transform his body at will and is therefore free of all disease and death. Siddhas such as the disciples of Gorakhnath are believed to have achieved this in the foothills of the Himalayas, and they came to be known as *jivan-mukta*, liberated while living.

Of his Sanskrit treatises the *Goraksha-Sataka* has survived and has been edited and translated into English, while the *Hatha-Yoga* has not. His disciples wrote treatises on magic, alchemy, left-handed occultism and *hatha yoga*. Generations of them also wrote verses in Punjabi and Hindi that are attributed to him, using the name Gorakhnath to establish a spiritual link with their master, the same borrowing of a master's name that is found in the verse attributed to Baba Farid. The *Goraksha-Bijay* of the seventeenth century, based on a Sanskrit work said to have been composed by Gorakhnath, was written by Shaikh Faizu'llah.

In his teaching, Ultimate Reality could not be conceived by logical reasoning; it was a direct experience, beyond the senses and the intellect, in the state of *samadhi*, or trance. He held the highest form of God to be Sat-Cit-Ananda-Murti, the One who reveals Himself as Being, Consciousness and Bliss, the self-manifestation of the Formless One, the Nameless One whose holiest names are Brahma, Shiva, Parmatma and Parmeswara. To Gorakhnath and the Siddhas the phenomenal world was not false or illusory. The *adhars*, in his teaching, are the main sources of the vital and psychical functions, which have to be controlled, and the Absolute Truth realised in the highest spiritual experience lies above *bhava*, existence, and *abhava*, its negation.

Peshawar and the wider north west kept strong *Nath* associations, and Gor Khatri, with its long yogic heritage, was a natural centre for devotion to Gorakhnath. The city also preserved other shrines linked to the tradition, among them the *Dargah*, or shrine, of Pir Ratan Nath in Jhanda Bazaar, a sign of the enduring presence of the *Nath* current in Peshawar's religious life.

## Legends of Gorakhnath

The yogis held Brahma, Vishnu and Mahesh to be both angels and disciples of Gorakhnath, and they claimed all the prophets and apostles as his disciples too. To the yogis the Prophet Muhammad was a pupil and disciple of Gorakhnath, a belief they kept hidden for fear of Muslim retribution. Baba Ratan was identified with Gorakhnath, and it was asserted that the Prophet had learnt yoga through him. Gorakh has also been seen as a counterpart of the awaited Mahdi of Muslim belief: a yogi becomes a Siddha only after meeting Gorakh, and both Gorakh and the Siddhas show the distinctive features of the Mahdi.

Gorakhnath has his part in romance and legend too. In the romance of Hir and Ranjha, Ranjha goes to the great yogi Gorakhnath for initiation and returns transformed from a herdsman into a yogi to win back Hir. In one version of the legend of Goga Pir, Gorakhnath sends an invisible army to help Goga Pir against Rai Pithora. In the Puratan Janamsakhis, early traditional lives of Guru Nanak, the account of the anointing of Guru Angad has Gorakhnath in conversation with Guru Nanak, though Gorakhnath lived and preached long before Nanak's time.

His name reaches as far as the cult of the goddess Hinglaj in Balochistan. Tradition has it that Hinglaj was very fond of the Nath community, and especially of Guru Gorakhnath, and that the goddess waited day after day in Thatta for him to come to meet her, with all the food she had prepared for his arrival. It is said that by a boon attributed to Lakhavat the piled *khichri*, a dish of rice and lentils, turned to limestone, and that anyone who returned from Hinglaj wearing a necklace of these limestone pebbles would be freed from the fetters of this world. In the valley of Hinglaj is the *dhuni*, or fireplace, of Guru Gorakhnath, which goes back to the long tradition of the Nath yogis who made the journey there, and two texts, the *Gorakh Caritra* and the *Gorakhpurana*, describe Gorakhnath's own pilgrimage to Hinglaj. Another story says that Gorakhnath himself brought a stone there from somewhere around Anil Kund.

## Gorakhnath and the Sufis

The Naths lived in continuous exchange with the Sufis, the mystics of Islam, and Peshawar had its place in that exchange. From the eleventh century the Nath yogis began to spread throughout northern India, and from their centre at Peshawar they moved to all parts of Central Asia and Iran, influencing both the *qalandars*, the wandering dervishes, and the Sufis.

The strongest sign of that exchange is the *Rushd-Nama* of Shaikh 'Abdu'l-Quddus, in which Gorakhnath's name stands for God. The Shaikh belonged to the Chishti order, the Sufi order founded by Moinuddin Chishti and prominent in South Asia. The work's verses refer six times to 'Gorakhnath', 'Shri Gorakh', 'Nath' or 'O! Nath'. In five places, as in many Nath texts, these words stand for Ultimate Reality and Absolute Truth, and in the sixth the Perfect Siddha, or Perfect Man. All the verses of Shaikh 'Abdu'l-Quddus on the simile of the mirror and the polishing of the heart rest on ideas expressed by Ibn al-'Arabi and Gorakhnath. In the words of Gorakh, the Shaikh also warned the yogis that wearing rings in their ears and rosaries of Eleocarpus ganitrus around their necks, or reciting *sakhi* and *sabad*, the poetry of the Naths, fails to make one a yogi. Some verses ascribed to Gorakhnath in the Hindi *Gorakhbani* challenge the *qazis*, the Muslim judges, for mechanically crying the name "Muhammad".

The Sufi poet Bulleh Shah, in his lyric of the lover turned yogi, names the authority that a *pir*, a Sufi spiritual guide, must satisfy:

*My Pir gains the approval of guru Gorakh.* (translated by Christopher Shackle)

The city's own name appears in the mystical poetry of the Punjab and Sindh as one of the identities the lover leaves behind. In that vision, as one scholar of Sufism put it, "the Sufi is no longer Arab, Hindu, Turk, or Peshawari".

## Peshawar and the Frontier

Peshawar is an ancient city, which older books also write as Parshawar and Farshawar. In Aurangzeb's reign the district known as Peshawar lay on the side of Hindustan. Its grapes, peaches and melons were like those of Turan, the lands of Central Asia, while its heat and cold, its seasons and its rain were like those of Hindustan; its rice was better than that of Bengal, and crops and grain were plentiful.

Three roads then ran from the city to Kabul. The Nikshab road had many hard defiles, and armies passed along it with difficulty. A second road, joining the highway at Jalalabad, had narrow defiles, hard passes, little water and Afghan raids. The third, the road of Ali Masjid and the Khyber, was harder going for two *kos*, an old measure of distance, yet easier than the others, and armies and caravans used it. In the Janamsakhis, Guru Nanak, on his return from Baghdad, visited Kabul before reaching Peshawar.

Since Partition, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa has been home to a large proportion of religious minorities, who have lived there rather peacefully. They are mainly Hindus, who form the largest minority, and Hindu festivals are celebrated in the province with much pomp. At Partition the riots in the province were less intense than in the Punjab, and many Hindus and Sikhs went on living in their ancestral lands after the creation of Pakistan.

Peshawar's Hindus have also kept the festival of Shivratri, the night of Shiva, at Katas. In 2011 Hindu pilgrims from Peshawar arrived there for it in two large buses, about a hundred people, and the musical band had come from Peshawar too; Hindus from Peshawar had come to Katas in 2010 as well. Of the festival of lights it has been said: "If you want to see the lights of Diwali, go to Peshawar."

## Legacy

The Gorakhnath Temple at Gor Khatri is at once a living place of Hindu worship and a window onto the astonishing antiquity of Peshawar, a city where Buddhist, Hindu, *Nath* yogic, Mughal and Sikh histories are stacked one upon another. It is one of the few Hindu temples in the region still in use and a rare survival of the *Nath* tradition in Pakistan. Its reopening restored a thread of continuity to a sacred ground that has drawn worshippers for two millennia, and it remains a cherished site both for devotees and for those who value the shared heritage of the frontier.
