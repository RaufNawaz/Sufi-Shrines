## Overview

In Multan, on the mound of the old fort, rises the shrine of Hazrat Shah Rukn-e-Alam (1251 to 1335), at once the tomb of a great saint of the Suhrawardi order of Sufis, or Muslim mystics, and one of the supreme masterpieces of Islamic architecture in South Asia. Rukn-ud-Din Abu al-Fath, known by the honorific Rukn-e-Alam, "Pillar of the World", was the grandson and spiritual heir of Bahauddin Zakariya, and under him the Suhrawardi order of Multan reached the height of its fame. His towering domed tomb is the emblem of Multan and among the most beautiful buildings raised in the subcontinent before the Mughal age, and pilgrims crowd to it to honour a saint whose blessing is felt to guard the city.

He was a shaikh who moved among sultans. One sultan gave him sums of hundreds of thousands of *tankas*, or coins, on each visit to Delhi, another granted a hundred villages for his *khanqah*, his Sufi lodge, and the petitions of the needy travelled to the throne in his palanquin. Yet he saved nothing of what came to him, and the people called him *Qibla-e Hajat*, "the Qibla of needs", the qibla being the direction a Muslim faces in prayer, because no one who came to him in need went away empty.

## The Saint

Rukn-ud-Din was born in Multan in 1251 into the leading Suhrawardi family of the region; accounts of the year of his birth differ. He is said to have been born on a Friday, the ninth of Ramadan, the month of fasting. His father was Shaikh Sadr al-Din Arif, son of Bahauddin Zakariya. His grandfather gave him the name Rukn al-Din; his *kunya*, the name of honour formed with Abu, "father of", was Abu al-Fath, and his *laqab*, or title, Fazl Allah. He is also called Shaikh Rukn al-Din Abu al-Fath Suhrawardi and Shaikh Rukn al-Din Multani, and in a saying reported by Shaikh Nasir al-Din Chiragh Dihlawi he is named Shaikh al-Islam Rukn al-Haqq wa al-Din. Among the praises given him, he is called the sun of the sky of guidance. A different man of almost the same name, Sayyid Rukn al-Din Abu al-Fath, son of Sayyid Hamid Kabir, belonged to Uch.

His mother, Bibi Rasti, was a saint in her own right, called the Rabia of her time, after Rabia, the woman saint of early Islam. She was a *hafiza*, a woman who has memorised the whole Quran, a worshipper and an ascetic who feared God, and her practice was to complete a reading of the Quran every day. She was a *murid*, a disciple, of Bahauddin Zakariya, and she died in the year 695 of the Hijri calendar.

It is told that his grandfather foresaw his greatness before he was born. When Bibi Rasti, carrying her child, came into Bahauddin Zakariya's presence, he would rise to his feet, against the custom of the elders. Asked why, he said that the respect was not for her but for the one in her womb, who would be a sun at the height of sainthood and a moon of nobility.

He had a brother, Shaikh Imad al-Din Ismail. Once the brother put a question of doctrine to him: what was the wisdom in the Prophet's *hijra*, his emigration from Mecca to Medina? Rukn-ud-Din's answer turned on the *ashab-e suffa*, the Companions of the Bench, and Nizam al-Din Auliya's answer on the poor of Medina, who could not otherwise have reached the Prophet's company. Rukn-ud-Din himself had no children.

He was raised in the great *khanqah* his grandfather had founded at Multan, and his education and upbringing were under his grandfather's supervision. He was schooled in the religious sciences and the discipline of the order, and he was bound to prayer and fasting from the age of seven. He prayed in congregation and kept *tahajjud*, the prayer of the night, and the voluntary prayers of *ishraq* and *chasht*, after sunrise and in the forenoon; besides Ramadan he fasted in the Ashura of Muharram, the first month of the Islamic year; and he practised *zikr*, the remembrance of God, both silent and spoken, with *muraqaba*, meditation, and *muhasaba*, the reckoning of one's own soul. From the age of ten, it is said, he was distinguished in reading hearts and in the folding of earth and tongue, by which, Sufis hold, a saint crosses great distances or says much in a moment; and from the age of twenty five he possessed the outward and inward perfections. At table he ate little, but he ate well.

His succession was marked by a turban. It is told that when he was four he found his grandfather's turban lying on the leg of a bed and, at play, put it on his own head. His father, Sadr al-Din Arif, forbade him, but Bahauddin Zakariya, calling his son Baba Sadr al-Din, told him not to forbid the boy, for he had put the turban on as of right, and it was Bahauddin himself who had given it to him.

He was his father's disciple, though accounts of his master differ. On his father's death he succeeded him as *sajjada nashin*, the hereditary custodian who sits on the saint's prayer carpet, and he sat on his grandfather's *sajjada*, the prayer carpet of the house, for fifty two years. On his accession he put on that same turban, and he wore the *khirqa*, the patched cloak of the Sufi, of Shaikh Shihab al-Din Suhrawardi, which had come to him from his father. He gave himself to teaching, to *talqin*, the instruction of disciples, and to guidance. He was granted outward and inward wealth, and the sultans of his time counted it their pride to serve him. He became the foremost shaikh of the Suhrawardis in India, guiding a vast body of disciples.

## The Saint and the Sultans

He was held in the highest honour by the Delhi Sultanate, and he moved between Multan and the capital as a figure of immense spiritual and moral authority, interceding with rulers. Sultan Ala al-Din Khalji was a great devotee of his. The shaikh visited Delhi twice in Ala al-Din's reign, and when, at the sultan's request, he went to meet him, the sultan himself came out to receive him. On each visit Ala al-Din presented two lakh *tankas*, a lakh being a hundred thousand, on the day he arrived, and five lakh on the day he left. The shaikh distributed the entire amount among the people of Delhi, and his visits were a source of great jubilation to them.

Sultan Qutb al-Din, too, held him in great faith and honoured him greatly. The shaikh came to Delhi three times in Qutb al-Din's reign, on visits the sultan sponsored in the hope that the shaikh's presence might eclipse that of Nizam al-Din Auliya, the Chishti master of Delhi. But the close friendship between the two shaikhs frustrated the sultan's hopes, and the sultan could not arouse the least jealousy between them. Nizam al-Din came out of his *khanqah* to receive him, and they met in the congregational mosque, the Jami Masjid, of that time. When the sultan asked who had been the first to receive him, the shaikh answered that the first to receive him had been the best man of the city, meaning Nizam al-Din.

Although he had a serious problem with his leg, he ordered his men to lift him from his palanquin so that he could show his respect for Nizam al-Din, but Nizam al-Din prevented him. After a meal at Nizam al-Din's, Nizam al-Din's servant Iqbal laid several bolts of silk and a hundred *ashrafis*, gold coins, wrapped in a fine cloth, at his feet; he would not accept them, and they were given instead to his brother, Imad al-Din Ismail. When Nizam al-Din fell ill, he came from Multan to Delhi to visit him, led his funeral prayer, and afterwards returned to Multan.

He was a broker of petitions to the throne. On his palanquin lay a pile of papers, and he explained that they were petitions from the needy, given to him so that he could recommend them to the sultan. Whenever he went to the court of Sultan Qutb al-Din, the people of Delhi would place petitions inside his *takht-i rawan*, a palanquin like a movable throne; the petitions were then brought in, and the sultan read them and wrote sympathetic replies. A Sufi tradition holds that the shaikh would remain at court until every request had been granted, the sultan meanwhile squatting respectfully at his feet.

With the Tughluq sultans his ties were close, and they in particular revered him. His relations with Sultan Muhammad bin Tughluq were most cordial. When the sultan marched towards Multan, the shaikh met him. The sultan granted him a hundred villages for the upkeep of his *khanqah*. In a battle, as a ruse to confuse the enemy, Muhammad bin Tughluq placed the shaikh's brother, Shaikh Imad al-Din, who closely resembled the sultan, under the royal canopy, and Imad al-Din was killed; accounts of the brother's fate differ.

When Multan rebelled against Muhammad bin Tughluq, the saint is remembered as having saved the city. In 1327 or 1328 Bahram Aiba Kishlu Khan, the governor of Multan, rose in revolt; the sultan defeated him, flayed the *qazi*, the judge, alive, and ordered a general massacre of the people. Accounts of what followed differ, but the shaikh went to the sultan soon after he learned of the sultan's intention and asked for clemency, and the sultan relented.

He went to receive Sultan Ghiyath al-Din Tughluq at the village of Afghanpur and lunched with him in a specially built palace. At the suggestion of the sultan's son, Muhammad bin Tughluq, he left to perform the afternoon prayer, and after he had gone the palace collapsed and Sultan Ghiyath al-Din was crushed to death under its roof. Accounts of the episode differ.

## Life at Multan

Following the tradition set by his father and grandfather, his *khanqah* at Multan was a busy meeting place for distinguished visitors from many countries west of the Indus. His fame reached as far as Alexandria, spread by the merchants who came to Multan, and the traveller Ibn Battuta was advised to see him. In 1333 Ibn Battuta reached Multan and stayed with one of the shaikh's disciples, and his conversation with the shaikh reached even to political matters. A *murid* occupied in worship in a cell of the *khanqah* was told by the shaikh that the *hajj*, the pilgrimage to Mecca, was not obligatory on him, because he was a *faqir*, one who has embraced poverty.

What came in went out again. Whatever offering came to him he spent, saving nothing of it, and when Sultan Ala al-Din presented lakhs of *tankas* on his visits to Delhi he spent the whole sum. Whoever came to him in need of this world or the next did not go away empty, and so the people called him the *Qibla-e Hajat*. When he went out in his palanquin, both his hands hung outside it, so that perhaps the hand of some forgiven soul might touch his and he too might be forgiven. He took great care of scholars, learned men and dervishes, honouring and entertaining them.

He defended a shaikh's wealth in so many words. Shaikh Nasir al-Din Chiragh Dihlawi related that when Rukn-ud-Din visited Delhi, *qalandars*, wandering dervishes, asked him for sherbet and other dervishes asked him for money, and that he satisfied them and said: "The leaders of the community should possess three things." The first was property, to satisfy such demands; the second knowledge, to hold scholarly discussions with the *ulama*, the scholars of religion; and the third *hal*, mystical enlightenment, to impress dervishes. To this Nasir al-Din added that money was not necessary, and that only knowledge and *hal* were essential. His grandfather could not tolerate *qalandars*, but Rukn-ud-Din did, for he considered that their persistent demands, and those of other visitors, justified Sufi shaikhs in possessing wealth.

Works holding his teaching, among them his *malfuzat*, the record of his discourses, were known, but accounts differ over whether any of them survives; his teaching is also found in the works of his *khalifas*, his deputies. He urged his disciples to abandon cruelty, oppression, avarice and greed, for he believed that such vices brought human beings down to the level of beasts. Purification of the self, he held, came only through humility and prayer, as in the verse: "I do not exculpate myself. Lo! the (human) soul enjoineth unto evil, save whereon my Lord hath mercy. Lo! My Lord is Forgiving and Merciful." In the final analysis, he taught, that purification depended on divine grace, as another verse says: "Had it not been for the grace of Allah and His mercy unto you, not one of you would ever have grown pure." The mark of divine grace and mercy, in his view, lay in an insight into one's own faults.

Two more of his teachings concern form and attribute, and conformity in deeds. Judgement, he taught, rests on a person's attribute and not on his form, and the truth of this appears only in the hereafter, where each person is gathered in the form that answers to his attribute. Conformity in deeds means that the limbs are to be held back, in word and deed, from whatever the *sharia*, the religious law, forbids or dislikes, and idle gatherings and the company of idlers are to be avoided.

Among his *khalifas* were Maulana Zahir al-Din, Sultan Hamid al-Din Hakim, Shaikh Fakhr al-Din Iraqi, Makhdum Jahaniyan Jahangasht Bukhari and Shaikh Sama al-Din Dehlvi, though accounts of the list differ. Shaikh Usman Sayyah of Sunnam was enrolled as his disciple near Kilukhari, on the banks of the Jumna, and taught the *Awarif al-Ma'arif*, the Sufi manual of Shihab al-Din Suhrawardi, at Multan; when he returned from seven years of travel, he was given the honour of being presented with the shaikh's own garment and turban.

Makhdum Jahaniyan Jahangasht, Sayyid Jalal al-Din Bukhari, was initiated by Rukn-ud-Din into the Suhrawardi order and completed his training under him and under Nasir al-Din Chiragh Dihlawi. He lived at Multan in the *madrasa*, the school, by the shaikh's permission. When there was friction between Makhdum Jahaniyan's father and Shaikh Jamal al-Din Khandan, the shaikh put the young man on his own boat and sent him to Uch, and on receiving the message the father went at once to Jamal al-Din Khandan and embraced him. The shaikh conferred on him in person the *khirqa* of *khilafat*, the cloak that marks a deputy's authority to guide others, and, it is told, conferred another on him in a dream, which Makhdum Jahaniyan found on his head when he woke. When Makhdum Jahaniyan held the office of *Shaikh al-Islam*, the chief religious dignitary, and the seat of the Khanqah-e Muhammadi at Sehwan, he resigned the post at Rukn-ud-Din's command. Once he lay down below the threshold so that the shaikh might step down upon him, and the shaikh took his hand and pressed him to his breast; and once he bathed in the water that ran off from the shaikh's *ghusl*, the ritual bath, and when the shaikh learned of it he honoured him with a special favour.

He prepared for his own death. For three months beforehand he shut himself in his *hujra*, his cell, day and night, coming out only for the five obligatory prayers. On his last day, after the afternoon prayer, he called Maulana Zahir al-Din and ordered him to make ready what was needed for his burial and his shroud; then he led the sunset prayer as *imam*, the leader of the prayer, and died in the prostration of the last *rak'a*, or cycle, of the supererogatory prayers. He died in 1335. Accounts of the day differ; it is most often given as the sixteenth of Rajab, in the year 735 of the Hijri calendar.

After his death his seat passed to his brother's family, though accounts differ over who took it.

## The Shrine

He was buried in Multan, in the tomb that is said to have been built originally for Sultan Ghiyath al-Din Tughluq and then given over to the saint. Raised in the early fourteenth century, it is one of the glories of the age. It is octagonal in plan, built of red brick banded with timber and dressed with carved and glazed tilework in blue and white, and it rises in three tapering tiers to a high white dome, a colossal structure that dominates the skyline of Multan from its perch on the mound of the old fort. It stands next to the shrine of his grandfather, Bahauddin Zakariya.

The tomb is counted among the earliest and finest monuments of the Tughluq style, a model that influenced the architecture of tombs across the region for generations. It has been repaired and restored over the centuries, and in modern times it has received awards for its conservation. For the people of Multan it is not only a shrine but the very symbol of their city.

By the 1870s his tomb was a place of pilgrimage for the people, and in the twentieth century it drew high and low alike. It is a centre of continuous devotion, thronged by pilgrims who come to recite the *Fatiha*, the opening chapter of the Quran, to seek his intercession, and to honour a saint whose *baraka*, or blessing, is felt to guard the city. The daily prayers, *qawwali*, the devotional music of the Sufis, and the *langar*, the free communal kitchen, are kept there, and his *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of the saint's death, draws great crowds from across southern Punjab.

## Legends

It is told that when the Mughals, as the Mongols were then called, came near Multan, the people asked him for prayers. He went into *muraqaba*, meditation, and then said that the Mughals had gone. On reaching the river they had scattered, and when he was asked why, he said that God Most High had sent an army of angels and scattered them.

A devotee used to receive four loaves from his house. The story goes that he went to Mecca and complained that there he received none, and a dervish told him that the shaikh came there every Friday night, and showed him the spot. He found the shaikh there and saluted him, and from then on, the story ends, four loaves and two bowls of curry reached him without fail.

A *murid*, it is told, dreamed that he was told to go on *hajj*, and the shaikh, on seeing him, said at once that the dream came from Satan, who wished to deprive him of his devotions.

Before one battle, it is told, a sultan kissed the shaikh's feet, and he in turn foretold victory; the battle took place near Abuhar. In Sindh his name enters a birth legend, which in some tellings makes him, rather than his grandfather Bahauddin Zakariya, the Sufi master of the story.

## The Setting

Multan was a strategic commercial centre of the Punjab, and by the middle of the eleventh century Sufism had reached the lands around it. The governors of Multan were bound up with the Tughluqs: Ghazi Beg Tughluq, the governor of Multan, raised an army, killed Khusrau Malik and took the throne as Ghiyath al-Din. By the time Guru Nanak, the founder of the Sikh faith, came to Multan, the city was famous as a major centre of Sufi learning and philosophy. It was then one of the biggest cities of the Punjab, known for the shrines of Muslim saints and for its Jain and Hindu temples, a sacred space for people of all religious denominations. In Aurangzeb's reign it was counted among the ancient cities, with a fort of brick; people of every kind lived there, and many saints rested in that blessed city.

In Multan and Bahawalpur, musicians performed in the family shrines of the Syeds, supported by the Syeds' large landholdings, and the Syeds arranged annual rituals such as the Eid and the Muharram ceremonies marking the tragedy of Kerbala. For the Sindhi and Punjabi poets, Multan, like Mecca and Medina, was inside the heart. The city's fame enters the verse of the poet Madho Lal Hussain too, where a dress bought in Multan is one item in a bride's trousseau:

*The dress / someone carried across the snows / all the way from Kashmir. The dress / someone brought from Gujarat… The dress bought in Multan* (translated by Naveed Alam)

## Legacy

As the heir of Bahauddin Zakariya and the most celebrated of the Suhrawardis of Multan, Rukn-e-Alam carried the order to its height, and through his tomb, at once a place of pilgrimage and a wonder of architecture, the sanctity of the "City of Saints" finds its most visible expression. After his death the Suhrawardi order declined in Multan, only to be rejuvenated in Uch.

In 1875 a book on the saints of the Punjab gave him a notice of his own. The book closes with chronograms, verses whose letters, read as numbers, give the year of its completion, and one of them, by the author's son, Mufti Ghulam Safdar, names the book's subject, the friends of God in the Punjab:

جو خدا کے دوست تھے پنجاب میں
حال ان کا اس سے ہوتا ہے عیان

*A chronogram for the completion of the book, on its subject, the saints of the Punjab*
