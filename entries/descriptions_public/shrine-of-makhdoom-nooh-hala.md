## Overview

The Shrine of Makhdoom Nooh stands at Hala, a town some fifty six kilometres north east of Hyderabad in Sindh. It honours Makhdoom Lutufullah, universally known as Makhdoom Nooh and often as Makhdoom Sarwar Nooh, one of the most revered saints and scholars of the province. A Sufi, or Muslim mystic, of the Suhrawardi order, he is remembered as the founder of its *Sarwari* branch in Sindh, and above all as the first religious scholar of the region to render the Holy Quran into Persian. His shrine is the spiritual heart of Hala, drawing devotees throughout the year and at his annual *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of the saint's death.

He is generally dated to the sixteenth century. He was first buried near his native village, close to Halakandi, but the shifting of the Indus twice compelled the removal of his remains, the second time, in 1777, to the site of new Hala, where his mausoleum now stands.

## The Saint

His real name is given as Lutufullah, and he is known by the title Makhdoom Nooh, *Makhdoom* being a title of honour meaning master; he is often called Makhdoom Sarwar Nooh as well, and by his town he is known as Makhdoom Nooh of Hala. Accounts of the century in which he lived differ, but he is generally dated to the sixteenth century. Tradition places his birth around 1500 to 1505 and his death about 1590, and he is said to have died at the age of 87.

He belonged to the Suhrawardi order, the Sufi order founded by Shihab al-Din Suhrawardi, and is honoured also with an Uwaisi spiritual lineage, a bond with a master never met in life. He is remembered as the founder of the *Sarwari* branch of the order in Sindh. Hala was a place where mystical leaders of the Suhrawardi order had settled, and among them his is the best known name.

His lasting fame rests above all on his scholarship: he is remembered as the first religious scholar of the region to render the Holy Quran into Persian. In his work the *dhikr*, the Sufi remembrance of God through repeated recitation, is itself called a high priced pearl.

## The Shrine

Makhdoom Nooh was first buried near his native village, close to Halakandi. The Indus, shifting its course, twice compelled the relocation of his remains: first to Mula Sanwani, about 1601, and again, in 1777, to the site of new Hala. Accounts differ over where his shrine stands, but it is at new Hala that his mausoleum now stands. The shrine is the spiritual heart of the town. It draws devotees throughout the year and hosts an annual *ʿurs* in the saint's memory.

## The Setting

Hala, in the Matiari District, is a town long associated with the *Makhdoom* line of saints, with Sufi devotional music and with the celebrated blue and white *Kashi* pottery of the region. It is also the birthplace of the great Sindhi poet and saint Shah Abdul Latif, who was born there in 1689 into a family of mystics who were Sayyids, descendants of the Prophet Muhammad. The blue and white tiles that cover his tomb at Bhit Shah are typical of Hala.

The town is the heart of a fertile agricultural belt, where Kolhi and Bhil women who are devotees at the shrine of Shah Abdul Latif can make a living working on the large feudal farms. The country about the town was not always safe: between Sehwan and old Hala, a people given to robbery were a danger to traders, so that the country could not be passed "without a strong guard".

An older name for the town appears in an account of the origins of the Hindu goddess Hinglaj, which holds that Hala was called Kohala in former times, and that the goddess was once the queen of the Kohala or Kohana area. It is a story of the town; it makes no link between the goddess and the shrine.

## Legacy

Makhdoom Nooh is remembered in Sindh as one of the most revered saints and scholars of the province: the founder of the *Sarwari* branch of the Suhrawardi order there, and the first religious scholar of the region to render the Holy Quran into Persian. He has been called "the great Suhrawardi leader of Sind". Twice moved by the shifting Indus, the second time to new Hala in 1777, his remains lie in a shrine that is still the spiritual heart of the town, visited throughout the year and at his annual *ʿurs*.
