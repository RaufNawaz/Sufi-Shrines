## Overview

The Dargah of Pir Ratan Nath, in the Jhanda Bazar of the walled city of Peshawar, is among the last living Hindu temples of that ancient frontier city. A shrine of the British colonial period, it is dedicated in practice to the Hindu god Shiva and centred on the veneration of Ratan Nath, a yogi and saint also called Baba Pir Ratan Nath. It belongs to the tradition of the Nath order of ascetic yogis, and it is one of only a small handful of Peshawar's temples still in regular use, alongside the nearby Kali Bari and the historic Gorakhnath temple at Gor Khatri. Its chief festival is *Maha Shivratri*, the great night of Shiva.

## Ratan Nath and the Naths

The temple's saint, Ratan Nath, belongs to the *Nath sampradaya*, the order of the *kanphata* yogis, named for their split ears. The Naths trace their lineage to Gorakhnath and, before him, to Matsyendranath, the founder of the sect, a figure partly of legend. They flourished across northern India, Nepal and the frontier lands of the north west from roughly the early medieval centuries. They practised a rigorous discipline of yoga and the austerity of *hatha*, the yoga of the body, and revered Shiva as the *adi-nath*, the first lord and archetypal yogi.

Peshawar and its region were an old stronghold of the order. The Gorakhnath temple within the Gor Khatri complex, and the ancient association of the nearby hill of *Tilla Jogian*, the hill of the yogis, across the Indus, with Gorakhnath himself, mark this as Nath country of long standing. From their centre at Peshawar the Nath yogis moved to all parts of Central Asia and Iran, at the same time influencing both the *qalandars*, the wandering dervishes, and the Sufis, the mystics of Islam.

Ratan Nath is remembered in tradition as a wandering seeker and teacher who carried the Nath message of peace, truth and compassion across the frontier lands. His veneration reaches beyond Peshawar to shrines in the wider region, including sites in Nepal. As with many figures of the order, the details of his life are wrapped in legend and cannot be fixed with confidence to a particular century: his story is sacred memory rather than documented biography.

## The Temple

In its present form the dargah is a temple of the nineteenth century, raised in the neighbourhood of Karimpura and the Jhanda Bazar, within the old walls of Peshawar, when the city's Hindu merchant community was numerous and prosperous. Its very name reflects the blended religious idiom of the frontier. *Dargah*, the word for a Sufi shrine, is borrowed from the vocabulary of the Muslim shrine, and on the frontier a Hindu yogi's shrine could be spoken of in the language of the Sufi tomb.

After the Partition of 1947, when the greater part of Peshawar's Hindu population departed, most of the city's temples fell silent or were turned to other uses. The Dargah of Pir Ratan Nath is remarkable for having survived as a functioning place of worship, tended by the small Hindu community that remained, and today it is reckoned one of the very few temples in daily use in the city, together with the Kali Bari.

## Devotion and Festivals

The temple's chief festival is *Maha Shivratri*, the great night of Shiva, when devotees gather for worship. Peshawar's Hindus have also kept the festival at Katas. Hindus from Peshawar came to Katas in 2010, and at Shivratri in 2011 Hindu pilgrims from Peshawar arrived there in two large buses, about a hundred people; the musical band had come from Peshawar too. Four marriages solemnised at Katas that Shivratri were, it was said, of girls from Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and FATA, the Federally Administered Tribal Areas, who had suffered in the floods of 2010.

Diwali, the festival of lights, is the city's other great Hindu festival. It has been said: "If you want to see the lights of Diwali, go to Peshawar." Across Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Hindu festivals are celebrated with much pomp.

## A Name with Many Identities

The name Ratan is borne by more than one figure, and it is not settled whether any of them is the saint of this temple. Among the Nath yogis was a Ratannath, one of the names associated with the Nath movement: a contemporary of Gorakhnath, and the subject of many miraculous tales.

A Muslim figure bears a similar name: Shaikh Baba Ratan, or Hajji Baba Ratan. Tradition makes him a man born before the birth of Muhammad who became a companion of the Prophet. Known by the honorific name Abu'l-Riza, he is said to have come from Tabarhinda or Bhatinda in the Punjab; his death is placed in 1300, and he lies buried in the Punjab. Baba Ratan Hajji has also been identified with Gorakhnath. The Hajji and the yogi Ratannath are sometimes joined into one figure, but there is no consensus that they are one, and Ratannath is also counted in his own right a very important figure of the Nath movement.

The name is also attached to a shrine in Delhi, a *dargah mandir* devoted to a Pir Baba Ratannath. The phrase calls one shrine at once a *dargah*, the Muslim word for a saint's tomb, and a *mandir*, the Hindu word for a temple. It has been counted among the 'paradoxical' terms of shrines that are at once Muslim and Hindu in name, and it carries the same meeting of Muslim and Hindu vocabulary as the Peshawar temple's own name. Whether the Delhi shrine honours the same figure as the Peshawar temple is not settled.

## Peshawar and the Frontier

Peshawar is an ancient city, known to earlier writers as Farshawar, Parshawar and Bagram. In Mahmud's first campaign, Raja Jaipal set out to meet the invader and gave battle at Peshawar. Five thousand of the raja's men fell to the sword in that fight, and Raja Jaipal was taken captive with fifteen of his sons, brothers and kinsmen and was put to death.

In Aurangzeb's reign three roads ran from Peshawar to Kabul. The first, the Nikshab road, had many hard defiles over a long distance, and armies passed over it with difficulty. The second joined the highway at Jalalabad, through narrow defiles and hard passes, with little water. The third was the road of Ali Masjid and the Khyber, which ran eighteen *kos*, an old measure of distance, from the spring of Jamshid, known as Jamrud, to a place on the bank of the Nilab. The Khyber was hard going for two *kos*, yet this road was easier than the others, and it was the one used by armies and caravans.

The district around the city, known as Peshawar, lay on the side of Hindustan. Its grapes, peaches and melons were like those of Turan, the lands of Central Asia, while its heat and cold, its seasons and its rain were like those of Hindustan. Its rice was better than that of Bengal, and crops and grain were plentiful. The district was the home of the Mahmand Afghans and others, who kept to the ways of paying revenue and of living as subjects. The city recurs in Mughal affairs: Dara sent robes of honour to the armies of Peshawar and Kabul, whose governorship Mahabat Khan held, and after the fort of Attock had been laid out the court set out for Peshawar, and from that stage a *farman*, a royal order, of counsel was sent to Hakim Mirza.

In the Janamsakhis, the traditional lives of Guru Nanak, the Guru comes to the city on his return from Baghdad, visiting Kabul before reaching Peshawar.

Peshawar has its place in the life of the Sufis too. Zauqi Shah, who moved between Agra, Jaipur, Bombay, Peshawar, Hyderabad, Ajmer and finally Karachi, began his *Sirr-i dilbaran*, a comprehensive encyclopaedia of Sufi terminology, in Peshawar in 1923. Compiled over a number of years, it was first published in 1951 and reached its fifth edition in 1985. In 2000 a woman disciple from Peshawar, speaking in Lahore, contrasted the shrines of her home region with Pakpattan: "In the frontier, we have so many shrines where you can walk into the saint's shrines. Women can go inside, we can touch the graves." At many places, she added, there is a separate side for the women; it is not like this at Pakpattan Sharif.

The boldest mystical verse of the Punjab and Sindh sees a oneness in which every boundary of birth and creed falls away. In that vision, as one scholar of Sufism put it, "the Sufi is no longer Arab, Hindu, Turk, or Peshawari".

Today Peshawar is a city on the western border of Pakistan. The elders of one Sikh man moved there from a village in the Khyber Agency for better economic opportunities, and Sikh refugees from the tribal areas moved to Peshawar, Hassan Abdal and Nankana Sahib and started living at gurdwaras, the Sikh places of worship, just like the migrants before them. After the attacks of 1971, one woman recalled, her family fled to Peshawar, where there was a considerable Sikh population, and stayed for a few months.

Since Partition, the province of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa has been home to a large proportion of religious minorities, who have lived there rather peacefully. Hindus form the largest minority, and thousands of them are part of mainstream society, often visible in its economic and political fabric. At Partition the riots in the province were less intense than in the Punjab, and many Hindus and Sikhs stayed on in their ancestral lands because of the comparatively peaceful political situation. Earlier, during his reign, Maharaja Ranjit Singh had captured the Pathan regions of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and the tribal areas, as a result of which several people converted to Sikhism.

Thousands of Nanak Panthi Hindus, followers of Guru Nanak, live in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Punjab and Sindh, and the independent followers of Guru Nanak who refused the Khalsa, the order of initiated Sikhs, still exist in Sindh and in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa.

## Legacy

The survival of the Dargah of Pir Ratan Nath is significant out of all proportion to its modest size. In a city more often associated in the modern imagination with the Khyber Pass and the turbulence of the frontier, it preserves the memory of a deep and ancient Hindu and yogic presence: the Nath yogis whose austerities once echoed across these hills. As a shrine still in use, it is both a place of worship for Peshawar's remaining Hindus and a fragile monument to the religious plurality of the old walled city. Efforts to document and protect the temple, including its listing among the heritage sites of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, reflect a growing recognition of its value.
