## Overview

Wedged into the narrow space between the western wall of Lahore Fort and the eastern wall of Hazuri Bagh, close to the Alamgiri Gate, stands one of Lahore's most storied yet architecturally modest religious survivals: the Loh, or Lava, Temple. According to Hindu tradition, Lahore takes its name from Lava, also rendered Luv, a twin son of Lord Rama and Sita in the *Ramayana*, the Hindu epic of Rama. Lava is said to have founded the city, known in Sanskrit as Lavapuri, the "City of Lava", while his brother Kusha is traditionally credited with founding nearby Kasur.

Despite this ancient legendary association, the surviving building is not itself ancient: historians date it to the early nineteenth century, likely built during or shortly after Maharaja Ranjit Singh's conquest of Lahore Fort in 1799. It is thus a commemorative shrine of the Sikh period to an ancient legend. Long neglected, the temple was restored between 2024 and 2026 and reopened to the public on 27 January 2026, although it does not currently host regular congregational worship.

## Lava, Son of Rama

The temple's whole meaning rests on a founding legend. In the tradition the temple keeps, Lava, or Luv, is a twin son of Lord Rama and Sita and the founder of Lahore, which bore the Sanskrit name Lavapuri, the "City of Lava"; his twin brother Kusha, or Kush, is credited with founding Kasur. The twins, the story goes, were born to Sita at the *ashram*, or hermitage, of Valmiki, after she had been banished by Rama. Lahore and Kasur are said to be named after them.

The brothers have a second place in Lahore's living Hindu memory. A portico painting at Neela Gumbad, in Lahore, shows Valmiki teaching his two pupils, Luv and Kush, beside the River Parsuni, the old name of the Ravi, which flows next to Lahore.

By the reign of Aurangzeb, the founding of Lahore, an ancient city on the banks of the Ravi, was attributed to Lo, son of Raja Ram Chand. In the nineteenth century the founder of the city was named Lohu, a son of Raja Ramchandar. Not every account of the city's name agrees, and the name has also been derived in other ways; the tradition the temple embodies is that of Lava.

The name of Lava also travels far beyond Lahore. Among Sindhis a story of the origins of a caste is told in which Rama's son, called Lava, gave his name to the Lohana community, the name Lohana ultimately developing from his.

## The Temple

The surviving temple is a modest building. It is a simple hexagonal brick structure of interconnected chambers around an inner sanctum, historically topped by a dome that has since been lost. It contains no surviving Ramayana carvings or frescoes, consistent with its comparatively late construction. The Walled City of Lahore Authority, which now manages the site, calls it "the temple of Raja Loh", a cluster of interconnected chambers open to the sky, housing a memorial shrine.

There is a belief that the original temple here was built by Luv himself. Accounts of the age of the present building differ; historians date it to the early nineteenth century, likely during or shortly after Ranjit Singh's conquest of Lahore Fort in 1799.

The uncertainty is old. Visiting in 2018, a journalist found the temple, not yet restored, littered with an empty water bottle and marked by graffiti. Neither of the two historians she consulted could say who built it, when, what it once housed, or whether it was ever used for worship at all, questions about which, she concluded, "we can only speculate".

The temple is not to be confused with another building of the Fort that bore the name of a *mandir*, or temple. Ranjit Singh renamed the Fort's Moti Masjid, the Pearl Mosque, the Moti Mandir and kept the treasury of the state there, and in the 1880s the British government kept the treasury of the Punjab in the same building.

For much of the twentieth century the temple stood neglected and largely inaccessible within the Fort precincts. Between 2024 and 2026 it underwent careful restoration by the Walled City of Lahore Authority in partnership with the Aga Khan Cultural Service, Pakistan, funded by the United States Ambassadors Fund for Cultural Preservation, alongside related monuments of the Sikh period nearby. It reopened to the public on 27 January 2026. It does not currently host regular congregational worship in the manner of Lahore's active *mandirs*.

## The Setting

Lahore fell into ruin over long ages, so that little trace of it was left, and the seat of government of the region passed to Sialkot. After Sultan Mahmud's conquest, Malik Ayaz rebuilt the city and built a *pakka*, or solidly built, fort. Akbar built a strong fort, a city wall and a palace, and Jahangir raised the lofty building that still stood in Aurangzeb's reign. In the 1870s the Fort held twenty-nine relics, kept in a separate building.

The Fort was also the scene of a small literary moment at court. On Akbar's second visit to Kashmir, in the thirty-seventh year of his reign, which set out from Lahore in the rains, the emperor, at the crossing of the Ravi below the Fort of Lahore, asked about whom this verse had been written:

کلاه خسروی و تاج شاہی
بہر گل کی رسد حاشا و کلا

The Fort was besieged more than once. In Shihab al-Din Ghuri's fifth campaign, Khusraw Malik, unable to hold out, shut himself up in the Fort of Lahore. In Akbar's reign Muhammad Hakim Mirza besieged the Fort for twenty-two days while Raja Bhagwant Das, governor (*subadar*) of Lahore, held it firm. Under Jahangir, the governor Dilawar Khan strengthened the Fort against the rebel prince Khusrau, and however hard the prince tried, the Fort did not fall to him. In the Sikh period the killers of Maharaja Sher Singh met their end after the siege of the Fort of Lahore and a great battle. In the nineteenth century a building facing the throne on the south side of the Fort, in which a grave had been built, was called by common people the Nigar Khana, the picture house.

The temple's likely date ties it to Ranjit Singh's taking of Lahore in 1799. When Lahna Singh, then ruling the city, began destroying the monuments of former kings, the elders of the city invited Ranjit Singh to come to Lahore and take possession of it, and in 1799 he was greeted enthusiastically by a population sick of the lawlessness of that time.

The Alamgiri Gate, beside which the temple stands, takes its name from the imperial title of the Mughal emperor Aurangzeb, and the domes of the Badshahi Masjid face it. Between the Alamgiri Gate and the Royal Mosque is the Roshani Gate, one of the thirteen gateways that lead into the ancient walled city, once reserved for the prince and the princess, who would go from the Fort to the city through it. The gate also carries Sikh memory: Guru Arjan's torture took place outside the Alamgiri Gate. The Ravi, which once flowed next to the Fort, has changed its course, moving a few kilometres away, and the Fort, once an emblem of power and wealth, is now a tourist destination, where visitors can tour even the private chambers of the Emperor Jahangir.

The temple's quiet sits within a harder history of Lahore's Hindu places. After 6 December 1992, Sitla Mandir at Shah Alami, the Jain Mandir at Anarkali and Bhadra Kali Mandir at Thokar Niaz Baig, all in Lahore, were among the prominent temples attacked, and functional temples such as Krishna Mandir on the Ravi Road and Valmiki Mandir at Neela Gumbad were burned down. The Valmiki temple at Neela Gumbad serves a small community of Balmiki Hindus in Lahore, numbering only a few hundred, most of whom are Christians on record.

## Legacy

The Loh Temple's reopening on 27 January 2026 drew renewed attention in both Pakistani and Indian media to a rare, tangible link between Lahore's Hindu mythological heritage and its physical urban fabric. The Walled City of Lahore Authority frames the restoration as part of a wider effort to show the Fort's heritage across cultures, its Sikh and Hindu temples, Mughal mosques and buildings of the British period standing alongside one another.

The legend the temple commemorates is older and wider than the building. Across three centuries of writing on Lahore, in Persian, in Urdu and in English, the city's founder appears again and again as Rama's son, under the names Lo, Lohu, Luv and Lava. The small hexagonal building by the Alamgiri Gate is where that story is given a place in stone.
