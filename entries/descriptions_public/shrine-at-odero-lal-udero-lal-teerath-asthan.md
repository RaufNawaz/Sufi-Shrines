## Overview

The shrine at Odero Lal, also spelt Udero Lal, stands in the village of the same name near Tando Adam in Sindh. It is a single sanctuary revered and used in common by Hindus and Muslims, where a Hindu temple and a Muslim tomb stand together under one roof and are tended by caretakers of both faiths. To the Sindhi Hindus its saint is Jhulelal, an incarnation of Varuna, the ancient deity of the waters; to the Muslims he is a holy man called Sheikh Tahir, or, in a name both communities honour, *Zinda Pir*, "the living saint". The shrine is one of the great living symbols of the shared religious culture of Sindh, and of the reverence, common to both traditions there, for the waters of the Indus.

The sanctuary is known as the darbar of Udero Lal, a *darbar* being a saint's shrine. It is the main shrine devoted to Jhulelal and the chief centre of the Daryapanth, the cult of the river Indus. The sanctuary is vast and dates back to the seventeenth century, and it is the best example in Sindh of a sacred place whose saint has a double identity, Muslim and Hindu.

## The Legend of Jhulelal

The story that gives the shrine its meaning is set in a time of persecution, and it is told in more than one way. In the telling of Sindhi Hindu tradition, a tyrannical ruler remembered as Mirkh Shah of Thatta commanded the Hindus of Sindh to abandon their faith and accept Islam. In their fear the Hindus gathered on the banks of the Indus and prayed to Varuna, the lord of the river, for deliverance, and the god answered that he would be born among them as a child, at Nasarpur, to save them.

The saviour so promised is Jhulelal, whose birth the Sindhi Hindus still celebrate at *Cheti Chand*, the festival that marks the Sindhi new year and is kept as his birthday. In the devotion of Sindh he is the protector of his people and the spirit of the river itself, and Udero Lal is honoured as the centre of his cult. Tradition also gives the story an ending at the shrine: Jhulelal is said to have disappeared into a well within the darbar of Udero Lal.

## Jhulelal and Sheikh Tahir

The saint of the shrine carries many names, and accounts of who he was differ. The Hindus worship Jhulelal as an *avatar*, an incarnation, of the Vedic god Varuna, the god of the Indus. He is probably a modern form of that ancient god, and history mentions him only briefly. The Indus itself was worshipped under the name Udero Lal, pictured as an incarnation of Varuna, and the figure is also known as Amar Lal, Darya Shah and Darya Nath. The name Udero Lal has also been given to a different saint at a different place: Shah Lal Baz, the Qalandar, or wandering dervish, of Sehwan.

The Muslims know him as Sheikh Tahir. Muslim tradition at the shrine holds that the saint buried here was born a Hindu named Udero Lal and embraced Islam, becoming known as Sheikh Tahir. Some identify him with *Khwaja Khizr*, the deathless guide associated in Islamic lore with the waters, and in Sindh it is Udero Lal who is most often merged with Khizr. Muslims venerate him as a Sufi, a Muslim mystic, who may have belonged to the Suhrawardi order, founded by Shihab al-Din Suhrawardi. A Sufi lineage built for him at an unknown date makes him a disciple of Bahauddin Zakariyya.

Sheikh Tahir is also remembered as a mystic. More visibly in Sindh than in the Punjab, Hindus shared in the Muslim Sufi orders, and some saints were claimed by both communities; the most famous case is Sheikh Tahir, who is venerated by members of both. He was one of the most ardent defenders of the unity of being, *wahdat al-wujud*, the metaphysical doctrine of Ibn Arabi. There is no settled date for his life: he is placed in the sixteenth or the seventeenth century.

Strange legends are told of his ecstatic states. It is said that he saw God even in a camel, and started running after it.

## The Sanctuary

The sanctuary lies about 50 kilometres north of Hyderabad. There are several temples dedicated to Udero Lal, but the village that bears his name is the main centre of his cult. Before Partition the temples devoted to Jhulelal in Sindh were built with a dome, as were some Sikh *samadhis*, memorial shrines, and the darbar of Udero Lal has many domes. Within it is the well into which Jhulelal is said to have disappeared.

A legend accounts for the shrine's double character. When Hindus and Muslims disputed over the saint, it is said, a voice came from the heavens commanding that the shrine be made acceptable to both, one part a temple and the other a *dargah*, a Sufi shrine, for the saint belonged to all.

The plan of the sanctuary answers to its two communities. It is a single sanctuary, but each community worships the saint under its own name and in its own chapel: the Hindus venerate Udero Lal in one chapel, and the Muslims worship Sheikh Tahir in another. Hindus perform *puja*, worship with offerings, and *aarti*, the waving of lamps before the deity, while Muslims offer their prayers at the tomb. A perpetually burning lamp and the veneration of the nearby Indus are shared by both.

The care of the shrine is divided between the two communities, with a *gaddi nashin*, the holder of the seat, for the Hindus and a *sajjada nashin*, the custodian who sits on the saint's prayer carpet, for the Muslims. The annual fair is held separately. The two groups interact very little, and disputes arise regularly. The crossing runs one way: Hindus visit Sheikh Tahir's tomb, but Muslims do not go to the space devoted to Jhulelal.

## Devotion and Festivals

As the main centre of the Daryapanth, the darbar belongs to a cult followed among the Lohanas and related mercantile groups. The Daryapanth is based on the deification of the Indus. It was managed by a priestly caste, the Thakurs, and most of its rituals were related to the changes in the level of the Indus through the year. Among these groups two main cults were followed, the Nanakpanth, the path of Guru Nanak, and the Daryapanth, which is probably the older of the two.

The Thakurs were the priests of Jhulelal. In Sindh they were settled in different cities, and from 1947 onwards they migrated to India. In Sehwan they ran Jhulelal's temple in the northwestern part of the town.

In the colonial period the darbar was a meeting place for the Hindu community of Sindh. In the last decade of the nineteenth century several Hindu *panchayats*, community councils, of Sindh held a meeting at Udero Lal, in the main darbar devoted to Jhulelal. This was the time of an attempt, in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, to create a centralised path devoted to Jhulelal, then known as Amar Lal or Udero Lal.

At the annual fair of April 2016 a huge poster in the darbar showed Shah Abd al-Latif and Jhulelal together, with the epigram *Sab ka malik ek hai*, meaning that all have one master. The formula had for some years been very popular among the Hindus of Sindh.

Devotion to Jhulelal has its own words and songs. An inscription reads *Jhulelal bera par*, "May Jhulelal safely guide our nave", and the devotional songs to Jhulelal are called *panjro*. One devotional book opens with Guru Nanak and Jhulelal, here named Darya Shah, with Zinda Pir in the caption of his image, and its last four pages are filled with *panjro*. The images of Guru Nanak and Jhulelal have also grown more and more alike.

## The Setting

The shrine's double devotion belongs to a wider pattern in Sindh, where the Hindus are bound to Sufism through three kinds of sacred place: a Sufi tomb visited by both Muslims and Hindus, as at Bhit Shah; a saint with a double identity, Muslim and Hindu, as at Udero Lal; and a Sufi darbar managed by the Hindus, as at Pithoro Pir. A number of sacred figures in Sindh have such a dual identity. Their Muslim side is always related to Sufism, while their Hindu side usually belongs to the Nathpanth, the path of the Nath yogis. As early as 1851 it was observed that the worship of the intercessors is the common religious idiom of Hindus and Muslims.

The river had its own place in this common religion. In Sindh the cult of Khwaja Khizr was linked with the worship of the Indus and supported by Muslim and Hindu alike, and Khizr was called Zinda Pir. Under the name of Khwaja Khizr this saint has always held great power in Sindh, and in popular belief he became identified with the spirit of the waters and with the deity of the river Indus. The Daryapanthis of Sindh believed in the sanctity of the river god, the god of the waters, a belief that touched Hindu and Muslim alike. Hindus visited Muslim shrines and made votive offerings there, and offerings to the Indus are still made in the wider region of Sindh.

The name of Jhulelal reaches beyond this shrine. Jhule Lal, now a chant directed at a saint, was once a call to the river deity of the Indus worshipped by the Hindu Daryapanthis, and in the figure of Lal the Quranic river saint Khizr meets an Indic fish god. A pilgrim train, the Jhule Lal Express, carried the name. At Port Grand a temple mural shows a male deity seated on top of a giant fish, with water beneath: Jhule Lal, the famous Hindu deity also revered in certain Muslim traditions.

## Legacy

The shrine at Odero Lal has endured as a symbol of tolerance and common humanity, a place of shared reverence for a single saint and a single sacred river. In a subcontinent whose modern history has too often been marked by communal division, Udero Lal stands as testimony to an older and more generous pattern of coexistence. It continues to draw pilgrims of both faiths, Sindhi Hindus above all, for whom it is a spiritual homeland, and Muslims who honour the *Zinda Pir*. Its festivals, and especially the celebration of Cheti Chand, keep alive a devotion that has bound the peoples of Sindh together for centuries.

After Partition the cult of Jhulelal travelled with the Sindhi Hindus to India. There Jhulelal became the *isht devta*, the community god, of the Hindu Sindhis, the Sindhi god of India above all others and the embodiment of Sindhi culture in India. Before Partition, when most Hindus of Sindh were Nanakpanthis, followers of the path of Guru Nanak, he was already worshipped by some groups among them. The turn to his worship has been seen as a kind of soft Hinduisation of the Sindhi legacy in India, and as his exaltation spread, room for the Sufi legacy among the Hindu Sindhis shrank. The tradition of Jhulelal reaches from Udero Lal in Sindh to Ulhasnagar in Maharashtra, where a temple of Jhulelal was recently built by Thakurs from Sehwan.
