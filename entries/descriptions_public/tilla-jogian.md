## Overview

Tilla Jogian, the "Hill of the Ascetics", crowns the highest peak of the eastern Salt Range in the Jhelum District of the Punjab, rising to some 975 metres above sea level. On its summit lie the ruins of an ancient temple and monastic complex that was, before 1947, the foremost centre of Hindu yogis in the whole of the Punjab. Cooler and greener than the parched country below, the hilltop has for many centuries been a retreat for renunciants seeking solitude, and its long association with the *Nath* order of yogis, with Guru Nanak and with the lovers of Punjabi legend has made it one of the most storied heights in the region.

The hill was the headquarters of the order of *Kanphata*, or 'split-eared', yogis, and the most important place of pilgrimage for jogis in the Punjab. In the reign of the Mughal emperor Aurangzeb, great crowds of people and bands of jogis gathered there for the festival of Shivrat. And in Heer and Ranjha, the romance of the poet Waris Shah, Tilla is the hill to which Ranjha comes to take *jog*, the discipline of renunciation, from the ascetic Bal Nath.

## Gorakhnath and the Nath Yogis

Tradition traces the sanctity of Tilla Jogian back some two thousand years and links its foundation to *Gorakhnath*, the revered yogi and organiser of the *Nath* tradition. That chronology is best read as legend rather than settled history, for Gorakhnath himself is usually placed centuries later, in about the eleventh or twelfth century. He was the head of the *Kanphata* yogis, and he is believed to be an avatar, an earthly form, of the god Shiva.

The Naths, or Nathpanthis, a school of renunciants devoted to Shiva, were the followers of Gorakhnath. They lived in forests and wandered in towns, and they also established permanent monasteries. The word *jogi*, the popular form of yogi, usually means one of them: a wandering ascetic devoted to Shiva who meditates on the One.

Gorakhnath's name also reaches well beyond this hill. One tradition tells that he sent an invisible army to help Goga Pir against Rai Pithora. Another holds that the goddess Hinglaj was very fond of the Nath community, and especially of Guru Gorakhnath, so that she waited day after day in Thatta for him to come to meet her. Two texts, the *Gorakh Caritra* and the *Gorakhpurāṇa*, describe his pilgrimage to Hinglaj, and the fireplace, or *dhuni*, of Guru Gorakhnath goes back to the long tradition of Nath yogis who made that journey.

Sikh tradition, too, brings him together with Guru Nanak. The Puratan Janamsakhis, early collections of stories of Guru Nanak's life, set Gorakhnath in conversation with Guru Nanak in their account of the anointment of Guru Angad, though historically Gorakhnath lived and preached long before Nanak's time.

## The Seat of the Jogis

Tilla Jogian became the principal seat of the *Kanphata* yogis, the 'split-eared' ascetics, so called from the great rings inserted through the cartilage of their ears at initiation, an order that looked to Gorakhnath as its master. On the hilltop the *jogis* maintained shrines, cells and reservoirs of water, and at its height the complex is said to have sheltered hundreds of ascetics who came to meditate there. The site formed the easternmost of a string of ancient Hindu sanctuaries along the Salt Range, a chain that also includes the temples of Katas Raj and, at its western end, the Amb temples above the Soon Valley.

In the reign of Aurangzeb the hill was the place of austerity of a jogi. It stood on top of the salt mountain, as the range was called, seven *kos*, an old measure of distance, from the fort of Rohtas. On the appointed days, and above all on the day of Shivrat, the festival of Shiva, great crowds of people and bands of jogis gathered there and put on displays.

## Guru Nanak and the Hill

Such was its renown as a place of Hindu spiritual power that it drew, in the early sixteenth century, *Guru Nanak*, the founder of the Sikh faith, who is remembered in tradition as having spent forty days in meditation upon the hill during his travels. The episode became part of the sacred memory of the site for Sikhs, adding another layer to a place already venerated by yogis and by Hindu pilgrims from across the Punjab.

The hill enters Guru Nanak's story by another road as well. It is told that Tripta came with her husband to Tilla Jogian, the most important place of pilgrimage for jogis in the Punjab, and that there the couple paid for the jogis' food for one day, as a result of which the jogis prayed all night to bless the young couple.

## Ranjha Takes Jog

Tilla Jogian holds a special place in Punjabi folklore through the tragic romance of *Heer and Ranjha*, immortalised in the eighteenth century by the Sufi poet *Waris Shah*, who dated his poem to the year 1180 of the Hijri, or Islamic, calendar. In the tale Ranjha, parted from his beloved Heer, climbs to Tilla Jogian in his grief and there renounces the world. Accounts differ over who initiated him, but the guru most often named is the ascetic *Bal Nath*, master of the hill, who pierces his ears and initiates him into the order of the *jogis*, granting him *jog*. Through this episode the hill entered the imaginative life of the Punjab as the very emblem of the lover turned ascetic, and generations who never climbed its slopes knew its name from the recitation of Waris Shah's verses.

Ranjha sets out for Tilla sure that Bal Nath knows the way of salvation, and after many days' journeying he reaches the hill on which Bal Nath lives. He bows his head, places before the guru a piece of *gur*, raw sugar, well known as a ceremonial offering in the Punjab countryside, and clasps the feet of all the jogis. The guru is the Hindu holy man and teacher, the Hindu counterpart of the Muslim *murshid*, or spiritual guide, and tradition counts Bal Nath as the fourth guru of the hill, which it also calls Tilla Guruan, the mound of the gurus.

The guru's *chelas*, his disciples, keep his house and kitchen. When Ranjha arrives, "They were all engaged in religious contemplation and prayers. They were reading Gayan Gita Bhagvat and Bharat." They have served Bal Nath for eighteen years, giving up all, living by begging and remembering God day and night, and still he has not given them jog. The tale sets caste against the vocation: "You Jats cannot attain Jog", and "What can a Jat do with a beggars bowl or horn, whose heart is set only on ploughing?" Ranjha's own name is that of a well known caste of the Jats, the farming and herding people of the old Punjab.

Bal Nath sets out the conditions of *jog*. Ranjha will have to dress as a jogi, wear dirty clothes, long hair and a cropped skull, and beg his way through life. He will have to give up the pleasures of birth, cease to rejoice when friends come or to grieve when they die, and abstain from casting eyes on women. He will have to become divinely intoxicated with opium and other narcotic drugs, and go on long pilgrimages to Jagannath, Godavari, Ganges and Jamna. The poem defines *jog* in its own terms:

*Jog means to be dead while alive. One has to sing the song of nonentity using ones meagre body as a guitar. One's self has to be entirely absorbed. It is no child's play.* (translated by Charles Frederick Usborne)

*God has made his abode in this body of dust He is in every thing as a thread runs through the beads.* (translated by Charles Frederick Usborne)

A commentator on the poem reads its song of nonentity as the doctrine of annihilation in God, *fana fillah*, as practised by the Sufis, the Muslim mystics, a doctrine with strong spiritual affiliations with the Nirvana of the Hindus and Buddhists. The poem also names a degree of *jog* called Chit Akas, reached after passing through the stages of Bhut Kas and Juda Kas, the three planes of the philosophy of jog.

Ranjha, for his part, admits that the vocation is a means to his love: "Hir has captivated my heart. That is why I have become a Jogi." Then comes the rite of initiation. At their guru's order the chelas bring the earrings and the razor. Bal Nath takes off Ranjha's clothes, rubs him in ashes, embraces him and seats him by his side; then with the razor of separation he shaves him completely, bores his ears and puts the earrings on him. He places in his hands the beggar's bowl, the rosary, the horn and the shell, and last of all he teaches him the word Allah. With the rite comes a moral rule: "An old woman should be treated as your mother and a young woman as your sister."

So Ranjha, a Muslim, becomes the spiritual disciple of a Hindu yogi. As the same commentator observes, "This may seem strange today, but was by no means unusual at the time."

The chelas turn on their guru, leaving the jogi's house and kitchen and piercing him with shameless taunts. When they rebel, Bal Nath rebukes them, his anger blazing from his eyes; so powerful is the enchantment he lays upon them that they obey at once, and all ill feeling vanishes from their minds. Yet Bal Nath himself comes to regret his gift: "Verily I repent and am sorry for having given Jog to this youth. He has got the treasures of jog without spending a single farthing."

At the hill, too, a prayer is answered aloud. Bal Nath prays, pours ashes over his body and plunges his soul into the deep waters of meditation, and a reply comes from the *Darbar*, the court, of God: "Hir has been bestowed on Ranjha and his boat has been taken ashore." A popular belief of the Punjab countryside holds that fakirs, the wandering mystics, and jogis can, by the force of their piety, make the Moving Finger of Destiny cancel what it has written.

One cry of protest names the hill once more: "Had I known that you would try to keep me from my Love, I would never have set foot on your hill of Tilla." At last Ranjha makes haste to leave Tilla, gathering strange herbs and potent roots from the woods. The boring of the ears is the act that makes a jogi, and the tale returns to it again and again after Ranjha leaves the hill, until at the end the jogi's rings are taken out of his ears.

## The Hill in Sufi Poetry

The Sufi poet Bulleh Shah, too, follows the initiate to Tilla step by step in one of his lyrics:

*Casting aside his drum, he goes toward Tilla, he sits and meditates on his name. He has his ears split and the earrings inserted, and is initiated by the guru* (translated by Christopher Shackle)

The lyric then names the authority a *pir*, a Sufi spiritual guide, must satisfy:

*My Pir gains the approval of guru Gorakh. He keeps crying 'Hir, Hir.'* (translated by Christopher Shackle)

Bulleh Shah's verse draws on the yogis' own world. Its yogi has his ochre dress, his deerskin, skull cup, matted hair and ashes, his forehead mark, and his horn, bowl and beads; yogis sit before a bonfire and rub its ashes on their bodies. Its vocabulary is theirs too. The 'unstruck music' is the mystical sound heard at the climax of the yogic process, the 'circle of nectar' is a traditional image for the ecstatic state that is the goal of yoga, and the 'tenth door' is the hidden aperture in the skull that is opened at that climax. To 'make the Ganges flow backward', an idiom for doing the opposite of the usual, here means the drawing up of psychic energy during the yogic process, and the 'fourth state' is the state of liberation reached by transcending the three *gunas*, the qualities that govern worldly existence.

## The Salt Range

The Salt Range, on whose eastern heights the hill stands, is the line of mountains dividing the plains of the Punjab from the slightly elevated plateau of the Potwar. There is no irrigation in the range.

In the reign of Aurangzeb, rock salt was dug at the foot of the hills near Shamsabad, in the Sind Sagar doab, a *doab* being the land between two rivers, and it was famous above all other salts. Each miner went into the tunnels naked, with a lamp in his hand and a pick on his shoulder, cut a lump of about three maunds, an old measure of weight, and carried it out on his back. The miners were paid wages by the overseers of the work and felt no fear, and the tunnels, it was held, were by divine wisdom neither hot in summer nor cold in winter. Two large tunnels near Shamsabad yielded many lakhs, that is hundreds of thousands, of maunds of salt every year, and their revenue, with that of other places, went into the imperial treasury. Craftsmen made trays, dishes, covers and lampstands of salt, and the sweet gypsum found next to it was used for whitewash inside the houses of the great.

Hindus held that a pool in the range, whose depth no one could tell, was an ancient temple, and on great days such as the sun's entry into Aries they crowded there. They believed that the earth has two eyes: the right is a pool near Ajmer, and the left is this pool. The range was also called the mountain of Jud. Jud was the chief of the Janjua tribe, whose descendants still lived and ruled at the foot of the hills.

## Ruin and Legacy

The complex flourished until the Partition of 1947, when the departure of its Hindu and yogi custodians for India left it without a resident community. Though accounts of its later state differ, deprived of care the buildings fell into decay: the reservoirs silted, the shrines crumbled, and in the decades since the site has been steadily overgrown. By the early twenty first century much of it lay beneath a spread of wild olive and scrub, its masonry slipping into ruin. Conservationists and writers have repeatedly warned that this remarkable monument, at once archaeological site, sacred summit and literary landmark, risks being lost altogether without deliberate protection. Its remoteness has spared it the crowds that press upon lower shrines, but that same isolation has hastened its neglect.

Tilla Jogian gathers into one windswept hilltop several of the great strands of Punjabi religious and cultural history: the antiquity of the *Nath* yogis and their Kanphata order, the pilgrim memory of Guru Nanak, and the deathless romance of Heer and Ranjha. As one of the trio of Salt Range sanctuaries alongside Katas Raj and the Amb temples, it testifies to a landscape once dense with Hindu holiness. For the modern traveller it offers a rare and melancholy grandeur, ruined shrines amid olive groves commanding a vast prospect over the Jhelum plains, and stands as a reminder of a plural sacred past that survives now chiefly in stone and in song.

The romance of Heer and Ranjha lives on in song. It is the greatest of the local legends of the Punjab, set in the world of the Jat pastoral tribes of the western Punjab. Elaborated in more than a hundred versions in Punjabi, Urdu, Sindhi and Persian, it was given its final form in the late eighteenth century by Waris Shah, and it is his verse that is sung, time and again, wherever Punjabis meet; a visitor who stays in a Punjabi village will certainly find somebody to sing it in the classical form he gave it. Waris Shah is said to have recited it with great emotion among the people of his own speech, and many renowned folk artists in the Punjab have sung it, among them Reshma, Zahida Parvin, Nazir Begum, Sabiha Khanum, Tufail Niazi and Iqbal Bahu.

Punjabi poets and their eager audiences poured into Heer and Ranjha the same erotic emotion that was invested in Radha and Krishna in regions closer to the Ganges, and the story became a favourite Sufi device for conveying the value of erotic passion in spiritual life. Already in the reign of Aurangzeb, wonderful verses and designs on the love of Heer and Ranjha were composed and sung in the province of Lahore.
