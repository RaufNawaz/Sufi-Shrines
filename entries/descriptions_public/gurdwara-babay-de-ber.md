## Overview

Gurdwara Babay De Ber, the "gurdwara of the elder's jujube tree", also known as Gurdwara Beri Sahib or Babe di Ber, stands in the old city of Sialkot, near the ancient fort. A *gurdwara* is a Sikh house of worship, and this one marks a spot hallowed by the visit of Guru Nanak Dev Ji, the founder of the Sikh faith, during his travels through the north western Punjab. Its name comes from the *ber*, the jujube tree, beneath which the Guru is remembered to have sat, and a venerable tree, held by tradition to be more than five centuries old, still grows within the gurdwara's precincts as the sacred focus of the shrine. It is among the historic Sikh sites of Pakistan that have been restored and reopened for pilgrimage in recent years.

Tradition tells that the Guru sat beneath the tree to plead for the people of Sialkot, who lived in dread of the wrath of a Muslim holy man of the city, Hamza Ghaus. The gurdwara is held to be the place known in Sialkot as Baba Bair, "the shrine of the Berry saint", and the quarter in which it stands is known as the *mohalla*, or locality, of Baba Bair.

## The Guru and Hamza Ghaus

Tradition holds that Guru Nanak reached Sialkot in the course of his great journeys, coming by way of Jammu. He is said to have found the city gripped by fear, for it was believed to lie under threat of destruction on account of a broken promise made to a Muslim holy man of Sialkot named Hamza Ghaus, who had withdrawn in anger to meditate and whose wrath the townsfolk dreaded. The Guru seated himself beneath a *ber* tree close to the place where Hamza Ghaus was absorbed in his devotions, and began to sing aloud the divine word.

The sound is said to have disturbed the meditation of Hamza Ghaus, who came out and demanded to know who this was, and why he should intercede for a city of liars and promise breakers. The Guru is remembered to have replied that it was not just to punish a whole city for the fault of a single man, a teaching of mercy and proportion that, in the telling, moved the holy man and lifted the shadow from Sialkot. The episode belongs to the recurring pattern of the *sakhis*, the stories told of the Guru, in which his travels bring him into dialogue with the Muslim and Hindu holy men of the places he visits, and his words turn anger and division towards compassion.

The story is told in more than one way, and some tellings say more of the quarrel. Hamza Ghaus was a *faqir*, a Sufi mystic, and a Hindu man, it is said, had broken his promise to give him his firstborn son; the faqir told the people of Sialkot that he would pray for forty days and, when his prayers were done, destroy the city. In the *janamsakhis*, the cycles of tales told of the Guru's life, the Guru sets up his base at the foot of an old *beri*, or jujube, tree, the faqir pronounces his curse in the centre of the market square, and when the Guru leaves, almost the entire city is there to see him on his way.

The shrine of Hamza Ghaus lies in an old graveyard not far from the berry tree under which the Guru is said to have sat.

A similar story is told of Pasrur, about thirty kilometres from Sialkot. There the Guru is said to have humbled an arrogant Muslim Sufi named Mian Mitha, who had threatened to "churn" him. Mian Mitha came barefoot, touched the Guru's feet and became his devotee, and their exchange is held to be recorded in the Guru Granth Sahib, the Sikh scripture.

## The Gurdwara

The shrine that grew up around the sacred *ber* tree was built, in its later form, by a man named Natha Singh, and the complex came to include a garden, a *sarovar* or pool, and residential rooms for pilgrims and attendants. The gurdwara was abandoned in 1947, at Partition, the division of the subcontinent. Like many Sikh shrines of Pakistan, it suffered in the disturbances that followed the demolition of the Babri Mosque in 1992, when a structure within the premises is said to have been pulled down by a mob.

In the years that followed, however, the gurdwara's fortunes revived. Access for Indian Sikh pilgrims was restored around 2012, and a board at the entrance recorded that the gurdwara was renovated in 2012 by the Auqaf Department, the department of religious endowments. After renovation the shrine was formally reopened for worship, an event widely welcomed in the Sikh world as part of a wider effort to restore Pakistan's Sikh heritage, though accounts of its condition differ.

Through all these changes the ancient *ber* tree has remained the heart of the site, a living link, in the eyes of the faithful, to the very afternoon on which the Guru sat in its shade. Pilgrims come to pay their respects before it and to make their devotions within the sanctum, where the Guru Granth Sahib is installed.

The gurdwara is reached through the narrow streets of its *mohalla*, though accounts of exactly where in the city it lies differ. The building was an open structure, with a veranda whose pillars held up the roof and a single room at its centre. The tree stood behind the building: a thick tree, bending towards the ground, that looked ancient, if judged by its size. Tiles in the floor carried the names of Sikh devotees who had contributed towards the initial construction of the gurdwara, and its walls bore pictures of Hindu deities.

Beneath the tree lay a long grave, covered with a red sheet bearing Quranic verses, and a red flag stood at the entrance. The grave was new: when the place had been visited earlier, there had been no such grave. A man who showed visitors round called it the grave of Pir Bairi, "the saint of berries". On a wall the invocations "Ya Allah" and "Ya Muhammad", O God and O Muhammad, had been scribbled with a piece of coal where the text in Gurmukhi, the script of the Sikh scriptures, had been wiped clean, and a mosque had newly been built next to the shrine.

## Guru Nanak

Guru Nanak was the founder of the Sikh religion and the first Guru of the Sikhs. Accounts of his dates differ, but they are most often given as 1469 to 1539. Accounts of how many journeys he made also differ, but his *udasis*, the long journeys of his travelling years, are most often counted as four, and they lasted more than twenty years. He travelled mostly on foot, east to Bengal, south to Sri Lanka, north to Kashmir, Nepal and Tibet, and west to Mecca. He preferred to stay on the outskirts of villages, and whenever he visited an area he would visit its religious shrines, Muslim, Hindu and of any other denomination.

Sialkot recurs in the janamsakhis. The story of the faqir falls on the Guru's second journey, and his third began and ended in the city: from Sialkot he trekked north into Jammu and Kashmir, to Sumeru, the legendary Mount Kailash, and to Ladakh, before returning to Sialkot.

He married Sulakhini, daughter of Mul Chand and Chando Rani of the town of Batala, on 24 September 1487, though accounts of the marriage differ, and his sons were Sri Chand, born on 8 September 1494, and Lakhmi Das, born on 12 February 1497. Lakhmi Das, it is said, was a worthy son, but spiritual wealth was not his portion, and the Guru passed him over. On his deathbed he appointed not his son but his most loyal student as his spiritual successor, a Khatri named Lehna, who became Guru Angad.

Nanak himself is held to have abhorred the association of miracles with religious personalities, yet the stories of his life are full of them. In one of them he tells Karori, who calls his cure a miracle, that "there is no such thing as a miracle". He was no unworldly mystic but a householder, and he brought a message of peace and harmony to the believers of Islam and Hinduism. His advice to Bhai Bala and Bhai Mardana was that "if one is a Muslim then one should be a good Muslim and if one is a Hindu then one should be a good Hindu".

Muslims have claimed the Guru too. Many Muslims acquainted with his teachings are devoted to him and visit his shrine, and to one Muslim painter, "Guru Nanak is like a Muslim Sufi Saint". A twentieth century Chishti Sabiri shaykh counted Guru Nanak, with Krishna, Rama, Sita and Lachman, among the spiritual luminaries outside Islam who believed in the one true God and had a "Divine connection", *nisbat*. The texts of the Sufi saint Baba Farid are revered among the Sikhs, for he is held to have influenced the founder of their religion, and in the Hinglaj valley there are the engraved footsteps of Guru Nanak. In his time, it has been argued, all literate Punjabis, Hindus and Muslims alike, were conversant with the Persian poets ʿAttar, Rumi and Saʿdi.

## Devotion and Verse

In Aurangzeb's time the followers of Baba Nanak, as the Guru was also called, lived at one with their friends and without strife towards their enemies, and their master's name was always on their tongues. The heart of their worship was the reading of his verses, which they sang. They counted the service of those who come and go as the highest worship, and whoever arrived at midnight speaking Baba Nanak's name was treated as a brother and a friend and served, even a stranger, a thief, a highwayman or an evildoer. The Udasis, devotees of Baba Nanak who worship the Creator after their master's way, were counted then as a fourth class among the dervishes of Hind, or India. The tradition of *langar*, the free communal kitchen, is believed to have begun with Guru Nanak.

The Guru composed verses in Hindi on the truth of God, setting out the unity of God, and a singer named Mardana sang them and drew people into the snare of faith. His *bani*, devotional verses of supplication, entreaty, humility and reverence, was compiled as part of the Adi Granth, the first compilation of the Sikh scripture, by Guru Arjan Dev, the fifth Guru, in 1604.

The Guru's verse is sung outside the gurdwaras too. The singer Abida Parvin has said: "I sing Shah Hussain, Bulle Shah, Mian Muhammad Bakhsh, Hazrat Khawaja Ghulam Farid, Sacchal Sarmast, Baba Farid, and Guru Nanak." In Sindh, Sain Vasan Shah became famous for reciting the *banis* of Shri Guru Nanak and the *kalams*, the poems, of the Sufis.

Among the Guru's poems is the *Babur Bani*, a long poem that Nanak wrote at the time the Mughal Babur attacked the Punjab, destroying the cities of Eminabad and Lahore on the way. One of its lines reads:

ਆਵਨਿ ਅਠਤਰੈ …

*Coming in seventy-eight (1521 C.E)*

Lines from the Guru's other hymns, in English translation, give a sense of his poetry. His *aarti*, the hymn sung as lamps are waved in worship, has these lines:

ਗਗਨ ਮੈ ਥਾਲੁ
ਰਵਿ ਚੰਦੁ ਦੀਪਕ ਬਨੇ …

*In the salver of the firmament, / The sun and the moon shine as lamps*

On the oneness of God he sang:

ਸਾਹਿਬੁ ਮੇਰਾ ਏਕੋ ਹੈ ॥
ਏਕੋ ਹੈ ਭਾਈ ਏਕੋ ਹੈ ॥

*My Master is One / He is One, brother, the only One*

And again:

*God is One, / The Path is One*

His poem on the sacred thread, the *janeu*, runs:

ਦਇਆ ਕਪਾਹ
ਸੰਤੋਖੁ ਸੂਤੁ …

*From the cotton of compassion, / Spin the thread of contentment*

Of washing he sang:

ਭਰੀਐ ਹਥੁ ਪੈਰੁ ਤਨੁ ਦੇਹ ॥
ਪਾਣੀ ਧੋਤੈ ਉਤਰਸੁ ਖੇਹ ॥

*As hands or feet besmirched with slime, / Water washes white*

And in another song:

ਬਗਾ ਬਗੇ ਕਪੜੇ ਤੀਰਥ ਮੰਝਿ ਵਸੰਨਿੑ ॥

*The herons in their white feathers dwell in the sacred shrines of pilgrimage*

## The City of Sialkot

In the Guru's day Sialkot was a bustling city in the foothills of the Himalayas, inhabited predominantly by Muslims, with a powerful Hindu trading community. Accounts of its founding differ. Legend gives it to Raja Sela, the uncle of the Pandavas, the heroes of the Mahabharata, and its rebuilding to Raja Satya Vachan, when it became part of the kingdom of Kashmir. Its earliest recorded mention is in ancient Greek texts going back to 322 BC, where it is called Sagala, and it was famous for its silk.

The city stands in the Rachna doab, the land between two rivers, and was counted a city of the ancients. In former times it was the seat of government of the Punjab, its habitation stretching two or three *kos*, an old measure of distance. When Lahore lay in ruins in early times, Sialkot became the seat of government of the region, until the conquest of Sultan Mahmud and the rebuilding of Lahore by Malik Ayaz.

Sialkot passed into the Muslim Sultanate under Shahabuddin Muhammad Ghori, who repaired its old fort and left his army there; Khusraw Malik and the Khokhars besieged the fort for a time without success. Many Sufi missionaries settled in the region, making it predominantly Muslim and giving it a rich Sufi tradition, and the city was an important post on the trade route between West Asia and Delhi. Under the Mughal emperor Akbar, Raja Man Singh, military commander of Jammu and holder of the land grant of Sialkot, repaired the fort and improved the city; in Jahangir's reign Safdar Khan rebuilt the fort and its tower, and most later governors made repairs too.

In Aurangzeb's time Sialkot surpassed every other town of the province in population, and it was a city of crafts and learning. Its paper, the Mansinghi, the silk paper and the Khass-e Jahangiri, was white and durable and sent to other regions; its *karchikan* embroidery in silk and *kalabatun*, thread of gold and silver, went all over the world; and its *jamdhar* and *katari* daggers and *barchhi* spears were well made. Gardens surrounded the city, and the buildings of the *qanungos*, the keepers of the land records, and of some others were most pleasing and delightful.

A stream rising in the hills of Jammu flowed below the city. When it flooded in the rains, all the people of Sialkot, great and small, played in the water, naked but for a loincloth and carrying leather water skins, and anyone from the city who was far away remembered it with pleasure.

The city was a seat of learning and a gathering place of scholars, a mine of virtue and a home of the learned, and in the Mughal period it was one of the centres of Islamic scholarship, where Ahmad Sirhindi studied. Under Akbar, Maulana Kamal al-Din Husain left Kashmir, came to Sialkot in 971 AH and established teaching there; under Shah Jahan, Maulvi Abd al-Hakim spread the sciences, wrote glosses and drew students to his *madrasa*, his religious school. The saint Shah Daulah, whose first master lived in Sialkot, is said to have left the city after disputes with its people and, after some years, to have moved to Gujrat.

The Guru's own lifetime found the Punjab plunged into political crises. Babur invaded it a third time and reached as far as Sialkot, though accounts of what befell the city differ.

Today Sialkot has become one of the most important cities in Pakistan. Its business community financed its own airport, which handles international flights, and the highway. The city lies right on the border, and historically it gave the Punjab a connection to Kashmir. The author of the history *Tarikh Guru Khalsa* belonged to Sialkot.

Across the Pakistani Punjab, hundreds of temples and gurdwaras were taken over by refugees after 1947 and turned into houses, and several were taken over and destroyed by migrants. After the demolition of the Babri Mosque on 6 December 1992, well known Hindus living in the cities of the Punjab had to hide for several months.

## Legacy

Gurdwara Babay De Ber is one of several historic gurdwaras associated with Guru Nanak in the Pakistani Punjab that have drawn renewed attention as pilgrimage and heritage sites, and its story carries a particular moral weight. The story of the Guru's encounter with Hamza Ghaus, and of his plea that a whole community not be condemned for the sin of one, speaks directly to the ethic of compassion and shared humanity at the core of Guru Nanak's message. For the city of Sialkot, the shrine and its ancient tree are a reminder of a plural past in which, as tradition tells, the founder of the Sikh faith and a Muslim saint of the city met beneath the same sky; for pilgrims, it is a cherished station on the map of the Guru's travels through the land of the five rivers.

Everyone in Sialkot seemed to know where Baba Bair was, and the name itself is held to go back to Guru Nanak. Across Pakistan there are several sites where Nanak stayed or is said to have performed miracles, and the gurdwaras built there later by his devotees still stand tall.
