## Overview

Gurdwara Babay Nanki, also known as Gurdwara Janamasthan Bebe Nanaki or Gurdwara Bebe Nanaki, stands in Dera Chahal, a village in the Lahore district some twenty kilometres south east of the city, close to the present border with India. It commemorates Bebe Nanaki, also called Bibi Nanaki, the elder sister of Guru Nanak, the founder of the Sikh faith; in Sikh tradition she was the very first of his followers, the first Sikh. Dera Chahal was the ancestral village of Guru Nanak's maternal family, and Nanaki is traditionally held to have been born here. Though far less frequented than the major gurdwaras, the shrine carries a particular resonance for pilgrims who wish to honour the family that nurtured Guru Nanak and the sister whose faith preceded even his ministry.

## Bebe Nanaki

Bebe Nanaki was born, by tradition, around 1464, and was older than her brother by some five years. Their mother, Mata Tripta, belonged to Dera Chahal. As the story is retold, Tripta, five months pregnant, went to her maternal home in that village, at a little distance from Lahore, and there, about six years after her wedding, gave birth to a daughter. The child's grandmother suggested the name Nanaki, since the child had been born in her maternal house, *Nanaka* in Punjabi. Tradition holds that she was born in a house that stood where the gurdwara now stands, and that Nanak was named after his sister.

From his earliest childhood she is said to have seen in Nanak a spiritual greatness that others overlooked, and she became his steadfast supporter and protector. In the stories of his youth she stands between the young Nanak and their father's displeasure. She restrains her father when he berates the boy, and in one telling she brings her father a glass of hot milk mixed with almonds and defends her brother: "He is a very sensible person." Sikh tradition holds her to be the first to recognise him as a teacher of divine truth, and so the first *Gursikh*, a disciple of the Guru. It also credits her with encouraging his devotional use of music, the practice of *kirtan*, the singing of hymns, which remains central to Sikh worship.

Nanaki married Jai Ram, a Khatri of the Palta or Uppal clan and an official in the service of Daulat Khan Lodi, the governor at Sultanpur, and moved to Sultanpur. It is told that it was she who arranged her brother's marriage, having already found a girl for him, Sulakhni. At the request of Nanaki and her husband, their father sent Nanak to Sultanpur, where he lived with them and took employment at Daulat Khan's *modikhana*, the government store. It was in this period, at the river Bein, that he is said to have received the revelation that inaugurated his mission. Nanaki's household at Sultanpur thus became one of the cradles of the nascent faith. Daulat Khan, Jai Ram's patron, later rose to become the governor of Lahore and the lands around it.

## Sister and First Believer

Nanaki holds a decisive place in the story of Nanak's call. When Nanak disappeared in the river, Daulat Khan and she alone believed that he was alive: "My brother will return safe," she said. Of the disciples who gathered round him at Sultanpur, the most ardent were his sister and her husband, Jai Ram.

She rejoiced at her brother's returns from his journeys, once together with Jai Ram. Though saddened by one of his decisions, she did not try to sway him; on another occasion, at her plea, he put off his departure. It is told that on one journey Nanak set out to visit his sister, her husband and his friend Nawab Daulat Khan at Sultanpur, stopping on the way at Dera Chahal, at Jaman and at one other place.

Nanaki also had a place in her brother's household after his marriage. It is told that she was particularly sympathetic towards Sulakhni, and that she decided to look after Sulakhni's eldest son, Sri Chand. Having no child of her own, she found it easier to adopt him for a time, and Sri Chand followed the spiritual pursuits of his father, encouraged by her.

One story of Nanak gives his sister the moment of discovery. In one storyteller's version of the tale of mud turned into sugar, it was his sister who opened the bags on the mules and found that the mud had been changed into sugar and *gur*, unrefined cane sugar.

Nanaki is believed to have died in 1518, at Sultanpur, in what is now Indian Punjab. She came down with a mysterious ailment and breathed her last, and Jai Ram, unable to bear the loss, died three days later. She died before her brother.

## The Gurdwara

Dera Chahal was the village of Guru Nanak's *nanake*, his mother's family, and the Guru is remembered to have visited it on more than one occasion. Later his followers built a gurdwara here to mark the spot. It honours the village as the *janamasthan*, the birthplace, of Bebe Nanaki, and stands within the settlement as a modest but cherished shrine. It is held to stand on the exact spot where the home of Nanak's maternal family used to be. When Maharaja Ranjit Singh became the sovereign of Punjab at the end of the eighteenth century, he spent large amounts of money on the renovation and expansion of many gurdwaras, this one among them, and attached vast tracts of land to them so that the revenues from the land could pay for their running.

The gurdwara was once one big hall, with a small balcony opening towards the centre of the hall from the first floor. Part of its grounds used to be the hall of the *langar*, the free communal kitchen; by the early twenty first century it had become a lawn with a few rooms around it.

Like the other sites of the Lahore region associated with Nanak, the gurdwara was largely abandoned after 1947 and passed into the care of the Evacuee Trust Property Board, the state body that administers the properties of communities displaced at Partition. Accounts differ over how far it stayed open to Sikhs in the years that followed, but it has continued to receive Sikh visitors, especially on pilgrimages that take in the great shrines of Nankana Sahib and Kartarpur.

After his book came out, the writer Iqbal Qaiser recalls, Sikhs living in Canada, England and America put a lot of pressure on the Government of Pakistan to renovate the gurdwara. Malik Meraj Khalid, a son of the village, was born in 1916 to a poor agricultural family of Dera Chahal. A politician of the PPP, the Pakistan Peoples Party, he was twice Speaker of the National Assembly, once Chief Minister of Punjab and, between November 1996 and February 1997, interim Prime Minister. One of his first orders as Prime Minister was the approval of funds to renovate this historical gurdwara in his village, though he was not a Sikh. The gurdwara was in a horrible state when he ordered its renovation, and even then only its external structure was spruced up.

## Devotion

For Sikhs, Bebe Nanaki embodies the ideals of insight, devotion and selfless love, and she holds a place of special affection as the woman who first understood the Guru's message. By the early twenty first century, pilgrims who came to Pakistan for festivals were allowed to visit her gurdwara, and on those occasions prayers were offered here.

A young priest, Daleep Singh, lived at the gurdwara. Inside, the Guru Granth Sahib, the Sikh scripture, was placed in a wooden *Thara Sahib*, a raised platform, and the priest bowed before it. Ghulab Singh, the first Sikh police officer in the revamped traffic police of Lahore, stayed at the gurdwara when he was in Lahore, and his official bike stood in its courtyard.

The gurdwara also has a tradition of its own. Bhai Bannu, sent by Guru Arjan to Lahore to bind the Adi Granth, the scripture of the Sikhs, is said to have come and stayed at this gurdwara, and to have prepared another copy here without the Guru's permission, adding verses that the Guru rejected. It is also said that one of the movements started from this gurdwara. The Kashmiri binders who once lived in Lahore were known for their meticulous binding.

## The Setting

Dera Chahal lies on the Bedian road, the old route to Amritsar. By the early twenty first century it was flanked by Phases Five, Six and Seven of the Defence Housing Authority, and around the old village stood the new houses of former farmers who had sold their land to developers. More generally, the Defence Housing Authority, the neatest and most expensive residential area of Lahore, sprawled across a vast tract of land, eating into the small historic villages of the area; congested and stricken by poverty, such villages were trapped within a wall after selling their land to it.

The white dome of the gurdwara could then be seen from afar. A saffron flag rose from behind the wall, a sign that members of the Sikh community were living here, and behind it lay a pool of water where buffaloes wallowed, once the sacred pool of this shrine. Next to the pool stood a mosque that was being expanded, and on one occasion a sermon from its loudspeaker drowned out a conversation at the gurdwara. Behind the pool lay agricultural land associated with the gurdwara, and from the gurdwara's window a new apartment block could be seen, one of those built recently to cater to the growing demands of urban Lahore.

That land became the subject of a legal fight. Ghulab Singh filed a case against the Evacuee Trust Property Board in the Lahore High Court over the alleged sale of agricultural land associated with the gurdwara. The Supreme Court of Pakistan took cognisance of the case and asked the construction companies to pay 986 million rupees as surety while the hearings proceeded. Daleep Singh alleged that documents proved corruption at the Board, and that its former head, Asif Hashmi, had earned billions in his tenure of five years. In April 2016 Asif Hashmi was arrested in Dubai.

In the early twenty first century it had become rare to meet a Sikh in Lahore, a city once ruled by a Sikh and the seat of Sikh political strength. After 1947 hundreds of temples and gurdwaras all over Pakistani Punjab were taken over by refugees and turned into households. Sikh architecture in Lahore, it has been said, draws inspiration from both Hindu and Muslim architecture, just as the Sikh religion picks up from both Hinduism and Islam, and Sikh builders made the domes of Muslim building smaller.

## Legacy

The veneration of Bebe Nanaki affirms the honoured place of women in the Sikh tradition from its very origin. The gurdwara at Dera Chahal is one of two principal sites associated with her, the other being at Sultanpur Lodhi in India, and it preserves her memory on Pakistani soil. Its continued existence within Lahore district adds to the region's remarkable concentration of sites tied to the life of Guru Nanak and his circle.

That memory has been under pressure and in renewal at once: the sacred pool given over to buffaloes and the gurdwara's former lands in dispute before the courts, but also a building restored on the orders of a Prime Minister born in the village who was not himself a Sikh, a priest in residence, the Guru Granth Sahib installed, and pilgrims admitted on festival days.
