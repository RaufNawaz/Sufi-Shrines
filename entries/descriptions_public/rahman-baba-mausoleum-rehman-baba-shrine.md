## Overview

At Hazarkhwani, on the edge of Peshawar, stands the mausoleum of Rahman Baba, the poet Abdur Rahman Mohmand. He is the most beloved poet of the Pashto language and the great mystic voice of the Pashtuns, and his shrine is the spiritual and literary heart of the Pashto speaking world, a traditional gathering place of poets and singers.

For three centuries his *Diwan*, the collected volume of his verse, has been the book most cherished after the Qur'an in countless Pashtun homes. Its verses of love, humility and the vanity of the world are quoted as proverbs and sung by musicians from Peshawar to Kandahar.

## The Poet and Saint

Abdur Rahman, lovingly called Rahman Baba, was born around 1653 near Peshawar, into the Mohmand tribe of the Pashtuns. He lived in the reign of the Mughal emperor Aurangzeb. Little of his outward life is documented: he was no courtier, and he left no chronicle of himself. He is remembered as a man of humble means and retiring temperament, a dervish who shunned the powerful and lived in poverty and prayer, and legend has it that he led a life of withdrawal and seclusion.

His verse reveals a soul of deep devotion in the way of the Sufis, the mystics of Islam: gentle, unassuming and wholly turned towards God. He belonged to the broad Sufi current of his time and place, and he is counted a mystic of the Chishti order, a Sufi order prominent in South Asia. Another remarkable poet of the same order, Khwaja Muhammad Bangash, also tried to express mystical teachings in Pashto.

By tradition Rahman Baba performed the Hajj, the pilgrimage to Mecca. He died around 1711, though accounts of the year differ, and he was buried at Hazarkhwani, where his shrine arose.

## The Poetry

Rahman Baba's fame rests entirely on his *Diwan*, a body of Pashto verse of unmatched sweetness and moral depth. His great themes are the love of God, the transience of the world, humility, tolerance and the dignity of the poor. Again and again he preaches that one should return kindness for cruelty and sow good rather than harm. One of his most quoted teachings runs, in sense: "Sow flowers, that your garden may bloom about you; the thorns you plant will wound your own feet."

His poetry is at once popular and profound, simple enough for the unlettered to carry in memory and deep enough to nourish the mystic, and it has done more than any other book to shape the moral imagination of the Pashtuns. His verses are sung to the *rabab*, a plucked string instrument, and recited in gatherings, and his name is spoken with an affection given to no other Pashto poet. His poetry also reflects a sadness and an ascetic feeling not often found among the mystical poets of his period. His verse has reached readers abroad in selections, among them Jens Enevoldsen's *Selections from Rahman Baba*, published in 1977.

## The Shrine

Rahman Baba's mausoleum at Hazarkhwani has long been a centre of pilgrimage and of Pashto literary life. Poets and singers gather there, and an annual *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of the saint's death, is held in his honour with music and *mushaira*, a poetic assembly, alongside the rites of devotion.

In March 2009 the shrine was severely damaged by a bombing, an attack on the tolerant, musical devotion it represents, though accounts of the attack differ. It was afterwards rebuilt, and pilgrims and lovers of his poetry returned. It remains the great shrine of the Pashto speaking world.

## The Setting

Peshawar is an old city; in earlier writings its name also appears as Parshawar and Farshawar. In the reign of Aurangzeb, the district of Peshawar had grapes, peaches and melons like those of Turan in Central Asia; heat, cold, seasons and rain like those of Hindustan; rice better than Bengal's; and crops and grain in plenty. The district was then the home of the Mohmand Afghans and others, and it was into the Mohmand tribe that Rahman Baba was born.

Three roads led from Peshawar to Kabul in that age. One went by Nikshab, hard and long. Another joined the highway at Jalalabad, with narrow defiles, little water and Afghan raids. The third went by Ali Masjid and the Khyber Pass; it was easier than the others and was used by armies and caravans.

The city's religious past goes back long before the poet. From the eleventh century the Nath yogis, an order of ascetics devoted to yoga, began to spread throughout northern India, and from their centre at Peshawar they moved to all parts of Central Asia and Iran, at the same time influencing both the Sufis and the *qalandars*, the wandering dervishes.

## Legacy

Rahman Baba stands to Pashto as Bhittai stands to Sindhi and Bulleh Shah to Punjabi: he is the poet who gave his people's language its deepest and most beloved voice, and he is usually regarded as the best mystical poet in Pashto. For three centuries his gospel of love, humility, tolerance and the emptiness of worldly pride has been a moral compass for the Pashtuns, and his *Diwan* a household treasure. His shrine at Peshawar, rebuilt after violence, endures as the hearth of Pashto poetry and the resting place of a saint whose verse still teaches a gentler way.
