## Overview

In the Dharampura area of Lahore, once the garden suburb of Baghbanpura, stands the shrine of Hazrat Mian Mir, one of the greatest Sufi saints, or Muslim mystics, of the Mughal age and among the most beloved holy figures in the history of the city. A master of the Qadiri order, he was revered in his own lifetime by beggars and emperors alike for the fierceness of his asceticism, the depth of his learning and the breadth of a heart that welcomed Muslim, Sikh, Hindu and Christian without distinction. For nearly four centuries his tomb has drawn pilgrims seeking peace, healing and blessing, and because of his celebrated friendship with the Sikh Gurus it endures as one of Lahore's living symbols of interfaith reverence.

The tomb was raised by a Mughal prince for a dervish who, tradition holds, had forbidden any dome over his grave. Today it is a place of daily prayer, of *qawwali*, the devotional music of the Sufis, on Thursday evenings, and of free food for visitors, and each year its *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of the saint's death, fills its courtyards with hundreds of thousands of devotees.

## The Saint

His name was Mir Mohammad, and he bore the titles Mian Mir and Shah Mir; *Mian* is used in the local language as a mark of respect, in the sense of *sahib*. He was also often called Bala Pir, and he is not to be confused with Sayyid Muhammad Bala Pir Gilani, another man known by the same epithet.

Mian Mir was born in the sixteenth century at Sehwan, also called Sivastan, a town in Sindh between the cities of Thatta and Bhakkar. Accounts of the year differ; it is most often given as 1550. He came of a devout and learned family that traced its lineage to Umar al-Farooq, the second Caliph of Islam, and from that descent he is often given the epithet Farooqi. His father, Qazi Sain Ditta, son of Qazi Qalandar Faruqi, was a man of religious standing respected throughout Sindh, and his mother was Bibi Fatima, daughter of Qazi Qadan. He had four brothers, Mian Qazi, Qazi Usman, Qazi Tahir and Qazi Muhammad, and two sisters, Bibi Badi and Bibi Jamal Khatun, the second of whom was herself counted among the saints.

His father died when he was seven. At about the age of twelve, with the consent and blessing of his widowed mother, the boy left home in search of inner enlightenment, and for a time he wandered as a seeker. His mother is remembered among the saintly mothers who, like those of Shaikh Abdul Haqq Muhaddis Dehlavi and Khwaja Baqi Billah, played a part in making their sons great Sufis. He was drawn to the inward path and joined the Qadiri *silsila*, the spiritual lineage of the order that traces its descent to Shaikh Abd al-Qadir Jilani of Baghdad. Accounts of his initiation differ, but his master is remembered at the shrine as Shaikh Siyustani and is most often named Shaikh Khizr Sistani. From him Mian Mir received his training and, in time, the *khilafat*, the authority to guide others. His Qadiri line runs from Shaikh Khizr Sistani back through Sayyid Abdul Razzaq, called Qutb al-Afaq, to Abdul Razzaq's father, Abd al-Qadir Jilani himself.

At about the age of twenty five, with his master's permission, Mian Mir came to Lahore. He settled in the leafy neighbourhood of Baghbanpura, also called Khanpura, on the edge of the city beside the river Ravi, and there he remained for the rest of a very long life, passing his days and nights in devotion, teaching and the disciplined practice of the spiritual arts, until Lahore itself became inseparable from his name. In Lahore he studied the sciences of his day with Mawlana Saad Allah Lahori, who also taught Shah Husain, and with Akhund Nemat Allah. He is counted the founder of the Qadiri order in Lahore, where he represented it together with his sister, Bibi Jamal.

He was remembered as a worshipper and an ascetic, God fearing in the highest degree, devoted to God, a man who had left the world, and his contemporaries remembered him above all for the severity and the sincerity of his renunciation. In his *hujra*, the bare cell in which he lived, he spread nothing more than a piece of old sackcloth on the floor, and on that humble seat even the emperors of Hindustan did not hesitate to sit. He wore only coarse homespun *khaddar*, washed his own clothes with his own hands in the waters of the Ravi, and carried none of the outward marks of a Sufi master: no staff to lean on in his old age, and no patched cloak, or *khirqa*, to advertise his station. Once, when a devotee brought him a staff and he had walked a few steps leaning on it, he cast it away, saying, "He who has Allah for his support has no need of any artificial prop." When the wealthy pressed gifts of money and goods on him for his needs, he turned them away with the words "I stand in no need of these. I am not a beggar; I am rich, for my Provider looks after my needs", and told them to give their gifts to the poor and the needy. A dervish who begs from another beggar, he held, forfeits his trust in God.

He was a renowned adept of *habs-e-dam*, the disciplined regulation of the breath, a practice akin to the yogic *pranayama*, which he would carry through the night from the *Isha* prayer, the prayer of nightfall, until the light of dawn; it was a discipline he passed on to his own successor. He is said never to have slept at night, to have broken his fast only after a week, and at times, absorbed in God, to have gone a month without eating.

Though a man of deep and illumined learning who could expound the intricacies of the Qur'an and the Hadith, the sayings of the Prophet, to the astonishment of the scholars of his day, he neither wrote prose nor composed verse, and he showed no wish to keep letters or to set down his own words. When a dervish scatters his inner state on paper, he held, the secret entrusted to him by God is unveiled to those unworthy to keep it. Alone among the great figures of Punjabi Sufism, he left behind no *diwan*, no collection of poetry. Ordinarily he was not eager even for visitors: he would welcome a guest, raise his hands in prayer and blessing for him, and then gently ask him to return to his own affairs. Shy of courtly favour, he was extremely popular with people of all religions.

His teaching, as his disciples preserved it, was as demanding as his life. A human being, he taught, is made of three things, *nafs*, the ego, *dil*, the heart, and *rooh*, the soul, and the whole labour of the spiritual traveller is to bring these into balance: to submit to the *Sharia*, the religious law, to walk the path of *tariqat*, the Sufi way, in search of *marifat*, the inner knowledge of God, and so to reach *haqiqat*, the Truth. He counselled his followers to fix the heart on God alone and to empty it of the love of the world; to live plainly, eating little, speaking little and sleeping little; to meet honour and insult with the same evenness of soul; and to accept all weal and woe alike as a blessing from God. Above all he treated every human being, of whatever faith, as a creature of the one God. Nor did he flatter power: unmindful of royal displeasure, he would try to dissuade the emperor and his sons from any act he judged unjust or unethical. In an age of magnificence, he made poverty and truthfulness his splendour.

Of all his family, most is told of his sister, Bibi Jamal Khatun. She received her bond with the Qadiri order, her *nisbat*, from her full brother and from their mother, and in her renunciation she was called the Rabia of her time. The Mughal prince Dara Shikoh wrote her life in detail in both his books of the saints, the *Sakīnat al-Auliyā* and the *Safīnat al-Auliyā*, and she was one of the outstanding saints of the Qadiri order during its formative period in the Punjab. She was the mother of Hazrat Muhammad Sharif, the first *sajjada nashin*, the hereditary custodian who sits on the saint's prayer carpet. Accounts differ over the year of her death, which they place between 1000 and 1057 AH, and over where she lies; most place her grave in Lahore. By the 1870s it was counted within the enclosure of her brother's *khanqah*, the Sufi lodge; in 1974 the place lay between the Karachi railway line and the village of Miani Sahib.

By tradition Mian Mir lived some eighty eight years. In his final illness, worn by age and by the gout that had crippled his legs, he is said to have refused treatment, explaining that he did not wish to be dulled into unconsciousness: he wished to meet death fully aware, and to feel it come. He had spent some sixty years in the same small cell, and it was there that he passed away, at the time of the midday prayer on the seventh of Rabi al-Awwal, the third month of the Islamic year. He died in the reign of Shah Jahan, in 1635 (1045 AH), the year the Auqaf Department holds and most accounts give, though a few place his death in 1645.

## Disciples and the Mughal Court

For all his withdrawal from the world, Mian Mir's fame reached the very summit of the Mughal state. Lahore was then a seasonal capital, home of the Mughal court during its perennial rotation, and the emperor Jahangir sought the saint out. The two held a long interview, for which Mian Mir, notwithstanding his great age and weakness, took the trouble to come to the emperor; Jahangir, deeply moved, wrote to him both before and after their meeting, asking to see him again. Such visitors the saint received seated on his sackcloth, offered a prayer for them, and then dismissed with courtesy to their own concerns.

The emperor Shah Jahan, too, came into his presence several times. In one story of Shah Jahan and the saints of Lahore, Mian Mir refused an offering that Shah Bilawal, another of the city's elders, accepted; speaking to the emperor, Mian Mir likened the perfect *wali*, the friend of God, to a river, and Shah Jahan gave thanks for such perfect saints.

His deepest imprint on the age, however, came through his disciples, and the foremost of them was Mullah Shah Badakhshi. It is told that Mullah Shah came from Kashmir to Lahore, where his search did not succeed; on the road to Agra he learned Mian Mir's name from a stranger and regretted that he had not met him in Lahore, and he returned from Agra and presented himself before the saint. Then the master tried him. For three years Mian Mir showed him no regard. At last he asked where he lived, and when told that it was in a mosque, said that living in a mosque was not fitting; he asked what he ate, and when told that it was bread from the bazaar, said that bazaar bread ought not to be eaten. The disciple gave up both and began to starve, and only then did Mian Mir honour him with *bay'a*, the pledge of initiation, and give him the *khirqa*, the cloak, of *khilafat*.

Mian Mir called his disciple by a title of his own, Muhammad Shah. Once, asked for whom he had prayed, he answered that it was for Mullah Shah, through whom his way would shine; and of his disciple's *riyazat*, his spiritual austerities, he said that none of the masters of the past had performed the like. With his master's permission Mullah Shah spent the summers in Kashmir and the winters at Lahore in his service, and he carried on Mian Mir's nightly practice of the breath. He holds a distinguished rank among the elders of the Qadiri order, and he died in 1660.

Dara Shikoh, son of Shah Jahan and the crown prince famed for seeking the common ground between Islam and the mystical traditions of India, became Mian Mir's most ardent admirer. Accounts describe the bond in different ways, but the prince's own guide is most often named as Mullah Shah, who drew him into the Qadiri way and introduced him to the saint, and Mian Mir was his *dada murshid*, his guide's guide. When Dara Shikoh came to write of the Qadiri order, he set aside another lineage and acknowledged only the line that came down to Mian Mir and then to Mullah Shah. He devoted a whole book, the Persian *Sakīnat al-Auliyā*, "The Tranquillity of the Saints", to the saint, his circle and his wonders, of which it records thousands; it is a fine biography of Mian Mir and the Qadiri order in Lahore, and today one of the principal sources for his life.

In Persian verse the prince honoured him as the very pole of sainthood of his age: Mir Mohammad, he wrote, is the pathfinder for all the friends of God of this age, and while the Qadiri line endures it shall lead others, so long as the universe abides. That a prince of the empire should write so of a barefoot dervish in sackcloth is itself a measure of the awe that Mian Mir commanded.

Dara Shikoh gave his life to the search for the unity underlying Islam and the mystical traditions of India. He gathered the lives and sayings of the saints in his *Safīnat al-Auliyā* and *Sakīnat al-Auliyā*; he wrote the *Majma-ul-Bahrain*, "The Mingling of the Two Oceans", to show the deep harmony between Sufi and Indian metaphysics; he was a patron of Hindu mystics, the *sannyasis*; and he caused the Upanishads, the ancient philosophical scriptures of Hinduism, to be rendered into Persian, a labour that would one day carry Indian thought to Europe. His sister, the princess Jahanara, Shah Jahan's eldest and best loved daughter, was herself a disciple of Mullah Shah, who thought her worthy to be his successor, though this did not come to pass. She set down her own spiritual journey in the *Risala-i-Sahibiya*, one of the rare mystical works to come from the hand of a woman of the imperial house. Through these royal disciples the bare Qadiri cell beside the Ravi reached into the very centre of the empire, and for one brief generation the circle of Mian Mir and Mullah Shah became the spiritual heart of the Mughal court.

Yet the same nearness marked the circle for tragedy. When the throne passed, in blood, to the austere and orthodox Aurangzeb rather than to his mystically minded elder brother, Dara Shikoh was condemned as a heretic and put to death in 1659. The liberal and seeking spirit that Mian Mir and Mullah Shah had nourished was driven from the court, to survive thereafter not in palaces but at shrines such as Mian Mir's, where it had first been kindled.

The line itself ran on. Mullah Shah was Mian Mir's *khalifa*, his successor, and in Lahore the Qadiri chain passed from Mullah Shah to Shaikh Muhsin Shah, and from him to Shah Abd al-Rashid Lahori, and further down to Sayyid Bhawan Shah, reaching the year 1213 AH. Pir Burhan, who came from Bukhara in Akbar's time, received the grace of the Qadiri order, its *fayz*, from Mian Mir and Shah Bilawal, elders of Lahore. The chain of Shaikh Abdullah Shah Baloch joined Mian Mir through four intermediaries; the spiritual pedigree, or *shajra*, of one Mehr Shah ran back through Miran Shah Said to Mian Mir; and Shah Enayat was counted a successor to the Qadiri seat, or *gaddi*, of Mian Mir. The saint's disciples were thousands of people, among them a *faqir*, a mystic who has embraced poverty, named Abd al-Haqq.

Not every order honoured the line. A tradition of the Naushahi order tells of one Hafiz Tahir, who attended Mullah Shah, Mian Mir's *khalifa*, for some years in pursuit of inner knowledge but gained nothing there; he lost his faith, turned from Islam itself, put on the *zunnar*, the sacred thread, and went begging among Hindu *faqirs*, until Naushah restored him.

## A Friendship Across Faiths

The most famous tradition attached to Mian Mir binds his memory to the Sikh faith. He was a close friend of the fifth Sikh Guru, Arjan Dev, and of the sixth, Guru Hargobind, and the Qadiri school he founded in Lahore formed a close and sympathetic link with the Sikhs of that time. Sikh tradition has long held that it was Mian Mir whom Guru Arjan Dev invited to lay the foundation stone of the Harmandir Sahib, the Golden Temple at Amritsar, so that the holiest shrine of the Sikhs might be founded by the hand of a Muslim saint. The contemporary Persian chronicles do not mention the episode, and historians differ over it; but Mian Mir's cordial and brotherly relations with the fifth Guru are well attested, and the tradition is cherished across communities as an emblem of the friendship between Muslims and Sikhs in the Punjab. It continues to draw Sikh pilgrims to the saint's memory.

The friendship between Mian Mir and Guru Arjan Dev is also the subject of several folk tales, and the Sikh memory of the Gurus' ordeals tells of him too. When Guru Arjan Dev was tortured, it is said, his Muslim friend came to him, incensed, and asked whether he should destroy the Guru's oppressors; the Guru told him that he should not. It is believed, too, that Guru Hargobind was released after Mian Mir interceded on his behalf.

Not everyone looked on the saint kindly. The Naqshbandi Shaikh Ahmad Sirhindi is said to have disliked him and, for that reason, to have spoken to the king against him. Mian Mir himself received Sikhs, Hindus and Christians with the same courtesy and reverence he showed his fellow Muslims, and treated them all as children of the one God.

## Legends

As with every great saint, a rich body of tales of *karamat*, the miraculous deeds of a saint, gathered around Mian Mir; his wonders were said to be beyond counting. They were told and retold well into the colonial era, when British collectors of Punjabi folklore took them down. One tells of a dove: a hunter's stone struck down a bird that had been sweetly praising God, and at Mian Mir's touch it revived and flew away unharmed; when the hunter aimed once more, the stone turned back and wounded his own hand, until, repenting and vowing never again to harm a winged creature, he was healed. Devotees tell, too, of a black snake that came to the saint as he sat by the Ravi, circled him three times and slipped quietly away; of a mute boy whom he taught to say "Bismillah", in the name of God, after which the child could speak; of a handkerchief he gave that healed the sick who used it in faith; and of food that appeared, in answer to his prayer, when there was nothing in the house to set before a guest.

Many such tales turn on his knowledge of hidden things and his power over sickness and death. A man came seeking his blessing for a journey to Khorasan; Mian Mir advised him not to go, for the way, he said, was closed, and so, unseen, it proved. Another came in anguish over a son who was choking and suffocating; the saint fell into a trance, called for a cup of water, breathed his recitation over it, and by it the child was relieved. To one visitor he said simply, "you may depart", and within a few days the man breathed his last. A disciple, Meerak Mohammad Lahori, told how, having taken up and chewed a discarded betel leaf from the saint's own mouth, he was carried into so deep an ecstasy that his breast seemed near to bursting with felicity. Stories such as these, more than any doctrine, bound ordinary people to the saint, and they still draw the troubled and the hopeful to his shrine.

A wonder is told of the tomb itself. When Shah Muhammad Ghaus came to Lahore in search of the truth, he spent his first night at the tomb of Mian Mir. In a dream the saint appeared to him and, with complete attention, bestowed on him a *shughl*, a spiritual exercise to practise. In the morning Shah Muhammad Ghaus went to Shaikh Hamid Lahori and asked him, too, for an exercise, and Shaikh Hamid laughed and said that the one Mian Mir had given him that night was enough.

Another wonder is told of his sister. There were some two *man* of wheat in the household, a *man* being a traditional measure of weight. Bibi Jamal Khatun put her hand into it, and for a year it fed the *faqirs*, the poor, the dervishes of the *khanqah* and its dependants every day, as though the two *man* had become several hundred. When the news reached Mian Mir, he said that the display of *karamat* is forbidden and that God is the giver of sustenance: what need was there to obtain grain in this way?

A saying of his concerns his attendant, or *khadim*, Mir Inayatullah, whose *murshid*, his spiritual guide, gave him the title Miskin Shah Amri, because no one knew where he got his food. When someone described his state to Mian Mir, the saint said that this Miskin is Amri: his food comes to him by the *amr*, the command, of God, and he has no need to earn it. Miskin Shah Amri's tomb lies on the west side of the Mian Mir cantonment.

A modern story, told in Bahawalpur by a senior *murid*, a pledged disciple, is set at the shrine too. Captain Wahid Bakhsh Sahab and Hazrat Shahidullah Sahab once went to visit the tomb, and a *majzub* there, one spiritually intoxicated and absorbed in divine love, asked Shahidullah where his hometown was. He answered, "My hometown is London"; the *majzub* laughed and said, "No, no. Your hometown is Medina!", and repeated it many times.

## The Shrine

Tradition records that when his disciples spoke of raising a fitting monument over his resting place, Mian Mir forbade any dome or mausoleum, and he is said to have spoken a Persian couplet on the vanity of such display:

صورتِ قبرم ز بعدِ مرگ ویرانہ خوشتر است
نیستی مانندِ من با خاک یکساں خوشتر است

*The face of my grave, after death, is better left in ruins; for one like me, to be as nothing, level with the dust, is better.*

Yet a mausoleum was raised at his resting place in the years that followed, during the lifetime of Dara Shikoh, who could not bear that his master should lie unmarked. When Shah Jahan entrusted the governorship of the Punjab to the prince, he raised many buildings, among the first of them a *chowk*, a public square, and the lofty tomb of Mian Mir, for which he gathered much stone and building material; he built the tomb and garden of his own guide, Mullah Shah, as well. He meant, too, to lay a road of red stone to the saint's tomb, so clean and pure that he could go and come barefoot every day to pay his respects, and on which none but the prince himself would travel. The plan was cut short when Alamgir Aurangzeb overcame his father and imprisoned him, and had his brother Dara Shikoh killed.

The war of succession also stopped the building work, and the building stood idle for some years; only on a petition to Alamgir was it taken up again and finished, though not to the finish Dara Shikoh had intended. The shrine's mosque shows the two reigns: its base, faced with stone, was laid in Dara Shikoh's time, and the rest was built under Alamgir. Tradition holds that Aurangzeb also drew on stone from the complex in raising the great Badshahi Mosque of Lahore.

The tomb grew into a dignified monument in the Mughal manner, its chambers finished in fine stone, and in the nineteenth century it was counted among the famous and splendid buildings of Lahore. Accounts differ over the stone it is built of; the shrine describes a mausoleum of red sandstone. In the nineteenth century a solid enclosure finished in lime plaster surrounded it, with arches in all four walls and its door on the south. The tomb stood on a grand square platform, or *chabutra*, nineteen by nineteen *zar'a*, an old measure of length, with a marble stair on the south and stone paving on top. Its door panel was of marble in a frame of red stone. Inside, a marble floor was inlaid with black stone, and three arches stood in the walls, with the door in the fourth, the southern one; the marble work of the interior was built in Dara Shikoh's time. Glass work in a vine pattern and a large mirror adorned the flanks of the northern arch and the ceiling of the dome. The grave stood on a marble platform a foot high, its *ta'wiz*, the raised marker over the grave, of marble and always covered with costly cloths, and the finial of the dome, the *kalas*, was of marble too. The glass work belonged to a later age: an Englishman in a merchant's service had it made, and had the outside whitewashed as well.

The shrine's offices were built into its fabric. On the west of the enclosure stood a grand hall, or *dalan*, as the seat of the *mujawirs*, the attendants of the tomb, and beside it a further building held the special seat of the *sajjada nashin*. There was no well within the walls, so a man named Nur Muhammad had one made over the course of fifty years, with a bathhouse beside it. Over the door of the tomb was set a chronogram, a verse whose letters, read as numbers, give a year, and from it the year of the saint's death is obtained.

By the 1870s his tomb in Lahore was a place of pilgrimage for God's creatures, and a Persian chronogram of four lines was written in the saint's honour. Its opening line praises Mian Mir with a play on his name, *Mir*:

میر دنیا و دیں میاں میر ست

Around the tomb, in time, gathered the graves of others who had loved him, so that the barefoot dervish who owned nothing and had asked for nothing came to rest at the centre of a small city of tombs, his wish overruled by the devotion of a prince. Dara Shikoh's wife, Nadira Begum, lies buried near the saint's mausoleum, and the shrine holds that the prince himself was laid to rest close to the saint. Mullah Shah's tomb lies outside Mian Mir's, and the grave of Khwaja Bihari adjoins the saint's tomb. Accomplished disciples of Mian Mir lie by the tomb, among them Haji Sulaiman and Shaikh Abu al-Khair, and so do keepers of the shrine, among them Hafiz Allah Yar and Sain Mir Shah. Beside the *khanqah* and tomb of Khwaja Bahar lies a burial ground of the kin of the late Muhammad Sharif, its graves of various dates, the names of those buried there unknown.

The locality itself bears the saint's name, as the *qasba*, the small town, or the *mauza*, the village, of Mian Mir, and his grave lies at Mian Mir near a railway station. Within the enclosure of Mullah Shah's tomb, whose garden Dara Shikoh also built, there came to stand a village bearing Mian Mir's name; in the nineteenth century *zamindars*, local landholders, lived in the garden, and the whole village was called Mian Mir.

The tomb passed through the turbulence of later ages. In Ranjit Singh's time red, black and marble stones were torn down around Lahore, and when the cantonments of his armies were built, the bricks of old tombs and houses were pulled out, so that thousands of old tombs, domes, mosques, gardens and *havelis*, the great mansions that were the memorials of the nobles of the past, were lost. Mian Mir's tomb did not escape: much of its valuable stone was carried off by the builders of Maharaja Ranjit Singh, who prized the material. Yet through every change of rule the shrine endured as a place of devotion.

Under British rule the tomb came under the care of the department of ancient monuments, and in 1985 it was formally declared a protected site by the Punjab Auqaf Department, the department of religious endowments, which administers it to this day together with the shrine's traditional *sajjada nashins*. Late in the twentieth century the shrine had fallen to pieces; it was then restored and, like Data Darbar, extended and renovated as a mosque with Friday congregational prayer. The mausoleum stands in the Dharampura (Hashimpura) area of Lahore, its dome and gateway a familiar landmark, with an attached mosque and open courtyards to receive the crowds who gather there. Successive generations have repaired and adorned it, so that the austere saint who wished only for a ruined and level grave is honoured, instead, by one of the enduring monuments of the city.

## Devotion and Festivals

The shrine of Mian Mir remains a living centre of popular piety. On ordinary days it draws on the order of one to three thousand visitors, who come to recite the *Fatiha*, the opening chapter of the Qur'an, to tie vows, and to seek the saint's intercession for healing, for children and for relief from hardship; many speak of the peace they feel within its walls and of prayers answered there. *Qawwali* is sung on Thursday evenings, the five daily prayers are held in the shrine's mosque, and *langar*, free food from the communal kitchen, is given out to visitors, sometimes twice a day.

The great occasion of the year is the *ʿurs*, held on 7 February, which marks the saint's union with the Divine; the courtyards then fill with hundreds of thousands of devotees for days of *qawwali*, prayer and communal feasting. Across the centuries, under Mughal, Sikh and British rule and in the Pakistan of today, the shrine has served as a shared space of devotion, a place where the boundaries of community grew soft.

In the nineteenth century five fairs, or *melas*, were held at the tomb each year: one yearly gathering, when the crowd stayed two days and two nights, and four in the rains, on the four Wednesdays of the month of *Sawan*, the month of the monsoon rains. A great fair was held there under the Sikhs too, but later the gathering grew more prosperous still, for the people of Lahore itself, of the Mian Mir cantonment and of the villages gathered in great numbers on the day of the fair, and the offerings brought in much income.

Pilgrims and seekers of many kinds have come to the tomb. The city's Qalandars, dervishes who wholly abandon legal norms and social conventions and even the distinctions of gender, are still associated with Mian Mir, as with Shah Husain and Shah Jamal, among the great Qadiri saints of Lahore.

## The Setting

Mian Mir's life wove him into the deeper Sufi history of Lahore. He is said to have kept a *chilla*, a retreat of forty days in seclusion, at the *khanqah* of Data Ganj Bakhsh, Ali Hujwiri, the eleventh century saint whose *Kashf al-Mahjub* is among the earliest Persian treatises on Sufism and whose shrine, Data Darbar, is the spiritual heart of the city. In going to sit in devotion at Data Sahib's threshold, Mian Mir joined his own sanctity to that of Lahore's patron saint, and later devotion has treasured the link as binding together the two greatest Sufi presences of the city. He was a contemporary, too, of Shah Abul Maali.

In the city itself, in Sadr Bazaar, Anarkali, a house known as Mian Mir's *chilla* stood in the nineteenth century beside a merchant's mansion and shops. Its rent went to *faqirs*; it had an arched way, an underground chamber, or *tah khana*, that was an old building, trees and a well. The saint's *sajjada nashin* held that Mian Mir had spent nights there and that the house had once been a garden, and Qazi Muhammad Afzal, called Fazl Gada, is said to have visited the saint at the place in Anarkali where his *chilla* stood.

The name Mian Mir runs through the map of Lahore. A road ran from Anarkali to Mian Mir, and the tomb of Sayyid Jan Muhammad Huzuri adjoins the Mian Mir road on its west. When the cantonment at Anarkali was given up, a new one was set in the plain of Mian Mir for the breadth of its land, with its own Sadr Bazaar of Mian Mir, great barracks, a church and countless *kothis*, or bungalows, costing lakhs, that is, hundreds of thousands. The city of Lahore had once reached as far as Hashimpura.

## Legacy

Mian Mir stands in memory as the very type of the Sufi ideal: a man who owned nothing and was sought by emperors; who wrote not a single verse and yet was praised in verse by a prince; who, rooted absolutely in his own faith, made his threshold a meeting place for people of every religion. His disciples shaped the spiritual life of the Mughal court, and the Qadiri line that grew from him ran on in Lahore through Mullah Shah and his successors. His friendship with the Sikh Gurus became a legend of tolerance that the Punjab still cherishes, and a hostel for pilgrims built at Dera Sahib in 1996 was named after him. The retreat he is said to have kept at Data Darbar bound him to the patron saint of Lahore, and his tomb remains one of the anchors of the city's sacred landscape. In honouring Mian Mir, Lahore remembers not only a saint of extraordinary austerity and learning, but a vision of holiness generous enough to hold an entire, plural world.
