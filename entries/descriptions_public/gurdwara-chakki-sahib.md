## Overview

Gurdwara Chakki Sahib stands within the old town of Eminabad in Gujranwala District, the settlement known in Guru Nanak's day as Saidpur, and marks the place where a great millstone, or *chakki*, associated with the Guru was kept. A *gurdwara* is a Sikh house of worship, and this one commemorates one of the most dramatic episodes in the life of Guru Nanak, the founder of the Sikh faith: his imprisonment, traditionally dated to around 1520 or 1521, when the Mughal emperor Babur invaded the Punjab and Eminabad was sacked and its people carried off. Since Partition it has had no resident congregation, but it is cared for as part of Pakistan's Sikh heritage, and pilgrims visit it on the great anniversaries, often together with the neighbouring Eminabad shrines of Rori Sahib and Bhai Lalo's well.

The present shrine, a simple building with a flat roof within a compound paved with brick, preserves the memory of the millstone and of the Guru's witness against the cruelty of war. The Guru's own lament on Babur's invasion, the *Babar-vani*, is the poem most closely bound to the town.

## The Captivity at Saidpur

Babur had opened negotiations with Ibrahim, without success, to annex the western Punjab to Kabul. After two minor raids in the region between the Indus and the Jhelum, he invaded the Punjab a third time and reached as far as Sialkot. Saidpur was sacked by his armies, and its people were carried off.

According to Sikh tradition the captives, Guru Nanak among them, were set to grinding corn with hand mills to feed the conquering army. It is remembered that the Guru's millstone turned of its own accord while he poured in the grain and sang the praises of the One God, the hymns lamenting the horrors visited upon the land that are preserved in Sikh scripture as the *Babar-vani*, the "utterances concerning Babur". The marvel is said to have astonished his guards and, when it was reported to Babur, to have brought the emperor himself to the Guru. Moved by his words, Babur is remembered to have sought the Guru's blessing and, at his urging, to have freed many prisoners. Accounts of their meeting differ, and it has been doubted that the two ever met. The encounter has also been taken to mark the beginning of the antagonism between the Mughals and the Sikhs, and Babur, the great grandfather of the emperor Jahangir, is remembered as having imprisoned Guru Nanak.

The story of the millstone that turned by itself is told in the *janamsakhis*, the cycles of tales of Nanak's life written by his devotees after his death. They are the primary sources for his life, and they survive in different versions; the earliest known janamsakhi dates from 1658, nearly 120 years after the Guru's death.

Not every telling of the captivity includes the miracle. Nanak himself is held to have abhorred the association of miracles with religious personalities, yet the stories of his life are full of them. In one of those stories, when a man who has been cured calls his cure a miracle, the Guru answers that "there is no such thing as a miracle".

In the janamsakhi telling the Guru came to Saidpur twice. The first time was early in the first of his journeys, when he came from Sultanpur by way of Talwandi. The second was on the way home from the fourth, when he came by Iran, Kabul, Peshawar, Kandahar, Hasan Abdal, Tilla Balnath and Katasraj to Saidpur and went on to Kartarpur; that return, the stories tell, fell at the time of Babur's third incursion into India, when the Guru took a south eastern route to the Punjab.

## The Gurdwara

In the late 1990s fourteen *ghumaon*, a Punjabi measure of land, were still attached to the shrine, and two rooms surviving from Babur's own time still stood within it. A fair was held here at Visakhi, the spring festival, with the *Nishan Sahib*, the Sikh flag, flying in the courtyard. From 28 February 1922 until 1947 the shrine was administered by the Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee, the committee for the management of Sikh gurdwaras.

One visitor, travelling through Pakistan in the footsteps of Guru Nanak with a historian, was taken by him to a small house in the town, an ordinary structure with a small door and a few steps leading to it. From behind its walls rose a pole wrapped in saffron cloth, with a Khalsa flag on top, and the house was locked. The historian told him what the house had been: "This place was used as a prison at the time of Babur. Guru Nanak was imprisoned here and was forced to work on a stone grinder."

In 2017 the gurdwara's wooden door was locked, and the building was identified only by a small plaque reading "Gurudwara Chakki Sahib, Eimanabad."

## Devotion and Pilgrimage

Pilgrims come to the shrine on the great anniversaries, often in the same round as Rori Sahib and Bhai Lalo's well. To Pakistan's Sikh shrines in general, Sikhs living abroad came for festivals such as Visakhi and the birthday of Guru Nanak, which were attended by thousands of Indian pilgrims.

In Aurangzeb's time the sum of the worship of the followers of Baba Nanak, as the Guru was also called, was the recitation of their master's verses, which they sang to melody. Many of them had put away worldly ties; kin and stranger, friend and foe were the same to them, and they lived at one with their friends and without strife with their enemies. They counted service to the traveller, to all who come and go, as the highest worship, and whoever arrived at midnight speaking Baba Nanak's name they treated as a brother and a friend and served, even a stranger, a thief, a highwayman or an evildoer. The Udasis, counted among the devotees of Baba Nanak, likewise made the singing of their master's verses the sum of their worship.

The tradition of *langar*, the free communal kitchen, is believed to have begun with Guru Nanak. Sikh hagiography has a favourite scene of the Guru's own worship, shown in several paintings: a serene Nanak listening to the songs of Bhai Mardana, his singer, who holds a *rubab*, a stringed instrument, while Bhai Bala stands behind them holding a fly whisk.

## Guru Nanak

Guru Nanak's dates are most often given as 1469 to 1539. He spent a major portion of his life in what is today Pakistan: he was born there, and he eventually settled in a small village that is now on the Pakistani side of the border. In Aurangzeb's time his descendants were still living at his abode on the Ravi, twelve *kos*, an old measure of distance, from Batala.

Accounts of how many journeys he made differ, but his *udasis*, the long journeys of his travelling years, are most often counted as four, and they lasted more than twenty years. He travelled mostly on foot, and whenever he visited an area he would visit its religious shrines, Muslim, Hindu and of any other denomination.

He lived as a householder, and he brought a message of peace and harmony to the believers of Islam and Hinduism; he taught that a good life rested on honest labour, compassion, selfless service and prayer. He offended both Hindus and Muslims by stating that there was no Hindu or Muslim. In the janamsakhis he is said to have declared, after vanishing for a week in the stream at Sultanpur, "There is only one God. There is no Hindu, no Mussalman." This teaching is said to have been questioned at Sultanpur, at Rai Bhoi di Talwandi and at the court of Malik Bhago in Eminabad. His advice to his companions was that "if one is a Muslim then one should be a good Muslim and if one is a Hindu then one should be a good Hindu".

He spoke vehemently against religious institutions and rituals. He associated with people of the lower castes, calling them people of God, and he criticised high caste landlords and government officials as exploiters. The movement that began with him has been called a spiritual reformation and a rebellion against institutional religion.

It has been argued that in Guru Nanak's time all literate Punjabis, Hindus and Muslims alike, were conversant with the poetry of 'Attar, Rumi and Sa'di, and that the Sufis of the Punjab in his day included Shaikh 'Abdu'l-Quddus Gangohi of Shahabad, his disciple Shaikh Jalal of Thaneswar, and the Qadiris, who had made the Punjab the main centre of their activities. Baba Farid is said to have become an inspiration for Guru Nanak when the Guru visited his shrine on his travels, and Farid's texts are revered among the Sikhs, for he is held to have influenced the founder of their religion.

## The Guru's Verse

Nanak wrote the *Babar-vani*, a long poem, at the time Babur attacked the Punjab, destroying the cities of Eminabad and Lahore on the way, and in it he lays the blame of Babur's wrath at God's feet. A hymn of Guru Nanak's preserved in the Guru Granth Sahib, the Sikh scripture, is read as evidence that he was himself present at Eminabad when Babur sacked it. One line of the poem, in English translation, gives its own reckoning of the invaders' coming:

ਆਵਨਿ ਅਠਤਰੈ …

*Coming in seventy-eight (1521 C.E)*

Lines from his other hymns, in English translation, give a sense of his poetry. On the oneness of God he sang:

ਸਾਹਿਬੁ ਮੇਰਾ ਏਕੋ ਹੈ ॥
ਏਕੋ ਹੈ ਭਾਈ ਏਕੋ ਹੈ ॥

*My Master is One / He is One, brother, the only One*

And again:

*God is One, / The Path is One*

His *aarti*, the hymn sung as lamps are waved in worship, begins in one translation:

ਗਗਨ ਮੈ ਥਾਲੁ ਰਵਿ ਚੰਦੁ ਦੀਪਕ ਬਨੇ …

*The sky the salver, the sun and moon the lamps …*

and in another:

*In the salver of the firmament, / The sun and the moon shine as lamps*

His poem on the sacred thread, the *janeu*, runs:

ਦਇਆ ਕਪਾਹ
ਸੰਤੋਖੁ ਸੂਤੁ …

*From the cotton of compassion, / Spin the thread of contentment*

Of washing he sang:

ਭਰੀਐ ਹਥੁ ਪੈਰੁ ਤਨੁ ਦੇਹ ॥
ਪਾਣੀ ਧੋਤੈ ਉਤਰਸੁ ਖੇਹ ॥

*As hands or feet besmirched with slime, / Water washes white*

And in another song:

ਬਗਾ ਬਗੇ ਕਪੜੇ ਤੀਰਥ ਮੰਝਿ ਵਸੰਨਿੑ ॥

*The herons in their white feathers dwell in the sacred shrines of pilgrimage*

Other hymns of his open *O my merchant friend*, *Launch ye the boat of austere contemplation …*, *As a beggar goes a-begging …* and *His goodness cannot be priced or traded …*.

The Guru composed verses in Hindi on the truth of God, setting out divine unity, and Mardana sang his verses and drew people into the snare of devotion. His *bani*, devotional verses of supplication, entreaty, humility and reverence, was his sermon, and it was compiled as part of the Adi Granth, the first compilation of the Sikh scripture, by Guru Arjan Dev, the fifth Guru, in 1604. The poems of the later Gurus are recorded not in their own names but in that of Guru Nanak. He brought into his verse several words that were not part of the Punjabi of his time, which is why, it has been said, his contribution as a poet to Punjabi is immense.

He is counted among the poets of devotion: the poetry of Kabir and Nanak in the fifteenth century led to the founding of Sikhism. Both poets used the *doha*, an old popular Prakrit and Hindi metre in which the two lines of a couplet rhyme, for their mystical poetry. The use of the local language for mystical poetry, in Sindh as in the Punjab, is traced back to the fifteenth century, and after 1800 Punjabi was used almost exclusively by the Sikhs as their literary medium, while the Punjabi Muslims turned to Urdu.

His verse is sung outside the gurdwaras too. The singer Abida Parvin has said: "I sing Shah Hussain, Bulle Shah, Mian Muhammad Bakhsh, Hazrat Khawaja Ghulam Farid, Sacchal Sarmast, Baba Farid, and Guru Nanak." In Sindh, Sain Vasan Shah became famous for reciting the *banis* of Shri Guru Nanak and the *kalams*, the poems, of the Sufis, and a new edition of one Sindhi poet's *kalam* has kept Guru Nanak's poetry in it.

## The Setting

When Babur took over the city of Saidpur, he renamed it Eminabad, though accounts of how the town came by its name differ. The cone like spires of ancient Hindu temples rose among its houses, in contrast to the monolithic Muslim architecture that had come to dominate the town, and its dusty roads and worn houses showed that it had lost its economic strength. Once it was an important trading centre on the Grand Trunk Road, which connected with Lahore and then Delhi; the highway has since moved south, and Gujranwala, not far away, has replaced Eminabad as a trading centre.

One temple, abandoned in 1947, still looked sturdy. Temples such as these had been taken over by migrants from across the border after the creation of Pakistan, and after the destruction of the Babri mosque in 1992 no signs of attack on them were to be found. Much of the town's historical architecture had still been preserved.

Gujranwala lies in the heart of the Punjab, 75 kilometres from the provincial capital. Maharaja Ranjit Singh started from a small fief around it. When he became the sovereign of the Punjab at the end of the eighteenth century, he spent large amounts of money on the renovation and expansion of many gurdwaras, and he attached vast tracts of land to them, so that their revenues could support their functioning; he also allotted Baba Sahib Singh Bedi land around the cities of Gujranwala and Lahore. After the British took the Punjab, a large number of important Sikh gurdwaras were administered by Hindu *mahants*, the heads of religious establishments.

## Legacy

Gurdwara Chakki Sahib preserves the memory of the millstone and of the Guru's witness against the cruelty of war, and through the *Babar-vani* it ties Eminabad to the Guru's hymns lamenting the horrors visited upon the land. Across Pakistan there are several sites where Nanak stayed or is said to have performed miracles, and the gurdwaras built there later by his devotees still stand tall, though after the division of the Punjab several other gurdwaras and temples were taken over and destroyed by migrants. At Eminabad, the small house shown as the Guru's prison still stood, locked, under its Khalsa flag.

Muslim writers and singers have claimed the Guru too. Many Muslims acquainted with his teachings are devoted to him and visit his shrine, and to one Muslim painter, "Guru Nanak is like a Muslim Sufi Saint". A Muslim tradition, which the Sikhs do not accept, holds that Guru Nanak was born by the blessing of Baba Naulakh Hazari. A Chishti Sabiri shaykh of the twentieth century counted Guru Nanak, with Krishna, Rama, Sita and Lachman, among the spiritual luminaries outside Islam who believed in the one true God and had a "Divine connection", *nisbat*.

A growing similarity has been seen between the iconography of Guru Nanak and that of Jhulelal, revered by the Hindus of Sindh, and it has been suggested that *Bhakti*, the path of loving devotion, may have reached Sindh through the teaching of Guru Nanak. At the shrine of the goddess Hinglaj, the engraved footsteps of Guru Nanak connect Hinglaj with the traditions of the Sikhs and of the Nanak *panth*, or sect.
