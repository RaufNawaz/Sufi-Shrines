## Overview

Gurdwara Choa Sahib, the "gurdwara of the exalted spring", stands at the northern edge of Rohtas Fort near Jhelum, close to the fort's Talaqi gate; the great fort, built in the sixteenth century, is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. A *gurdwara* is a Sikh house of worship, and this one keeps a tradition of Guru Nanak (1469 to 1539), the founder of the Sikh faith. Sikhs hold that the Guru, travelling with his companion Bhai Mardana, came to this waterless tract in the heat of summer after forty days at nearby Tilla Jogian, and that he brought forth a spring here. Long neglected after the Partition of 1947, the gurdwara was reopened for worship in 2019.

## The Guru at Rohtas

The gurdwara keeps the legend of the Guru's spring. Guru Nanak and Bhai Mardana, it is told, came to Rohtas in the heat of summer, after forty days at Tilla Jogian. When Mardana lamented his thirst, the Guru is said to have struck the ground with his staff and moved a stone, causing a natural spring to well up: the *choa* that gives the shrine its name. In the Guru's day there was as yet no fort at Rohtas, and the place must have been a desolate one; he and Mardana, it is said, sat here for days. In Aurangzeb's time Tilla was reckoned seven *kos*, an old measure of distance, from the fort of Rohtas.

A further Sikh legend tells that Sher Shah Suri, the builder of Rohtas Fort, three times tried to divert the spring uphill to supply his stronghold, and three times his engineers failed.

The gurdwara's tradition sets the visit on the fourth of the Guru's *udasis*, the long journeys of his travelling years. Accounts of how many journeys he made differ, but they are most often counted as four, and together they lasted more than twenty years. In the *janamsakhis*, the cycles of tales told of his life, the fourth journey, to the Arab lands, went by Multan, Sukkur, Thatta and Hinglaj and across the Arabian Sea. On it the Guru went dressed in blue robes, a colour considered sacred by the Muslims, carrying a staff, a prayer mat, the Quran and an ablution pot. After Mecca, Medina and Baghdad he came home by way of Iran, Kabul, Peshawar, Kandahar, Hasan Abdal, Tilla Balnath, Katasraj and Saidpur to Kartarpur.

Bhai Mardana, whose thirst, the legend tells, brought forth the spring, was the Guru's companion on the road; *Bhai*, brother, is a title of respect. He was a singer who sang the Guru's verses and drew people into the snare of devotion. The Guru's advice to his two companions, Bhai Bala and Bhai Mardana, was that "if one is a Muslim then one should be a good Muslim and if one is a Hindu then one should be a good Hindu". Mardana's own poetry stands in the Guru Granth Sahib, the Sikh scripture, which contains the spiritual poetry of the Sikh Gurus together with that of other spiritual teachers such as Bhagat Kabir, Baba Farid and Bhai Mardana.

## The Gurdwara

The first commemorative structure here, with a *sarovar*, a sacred tank, and a place for the recitation of the Guru Granth Sahib, is attributed to Charat Singh. The present building dates from 1834 and was commissioned by Maharaja Ranjit Singh, who endowed the shrine with twenty seven *ghumaon*, a Punjabi measure of land, and 260 rupees.

Maharaja Ranjit Singh (1780 to 1839), crowned ruler of the Punjab in 1801, spent large sums on the renovation and expansion of many gurdwaras all over the Punjab, and attached vast tracts of land to them so that their revenues could support their functioning.

Under British rule, sectarian divisions among the Sikh communities and the British takeover of the Punjab left a large number of important gurdwaras administered by Hindu *mahants*, the priests in charge of religious establishments. After 1947 hundreds of temples and gurdwaras across the Pakistani Punjab were taken over and turned into homes, and several were taken over and destroyed by migrants; where the population was largely indigenous, such places were better looked after, though with exceptions.

Gurdwara Choa Sahib was long neglected after Partition. In those years the historian Iqbal Qaiser made what he counted the most difficult of his journeys, to this gurdwara. One June, with a friend, Ilyas Ghuman, he climbed a wall of the fort and saw the gurdwara just under the shadow of the fort's wall, then walked for hours through thorny shrubs; when the two reached it they fell on its steps and passed out. An old man who was working there brought them a whole pot of water, told them of a simpler route from the road, and warned them of quicksand and wild animals, and of dacoits, armed robbers, who had taken refuge in the forest.

Later visitors found the gurdwara abandoned. It was a building with a dome on top, unmistakably a Sikh gurdwara, and a black corrosive powder had collected on its walls. Close to, it was a beautiful three storey structure on top of a small mound, beside a forest at whose edge stood the fort, and one could climb to the top and walk around the dome. Facing the gurdwara stood a Shia shrine. Two men in black *shalwar kameez*, loose trousers and a long shirt, who had come by motorcycle to tend it, told the visitors to leave, since it was getting dark and the place was not safe.

The gurdwara was reopened to pilgrims around the time of the 550th anniversary of Guru Nanak's birth, in 2019. In January that year a delegation of the Walled City of Lahore Authority, led by its Director General, Kamran Lashari, inspected it together with the nearby site of Janam Asthan Mata Sahib Kaur, and directed the local administration to restore both. That August, seventy two years after Partition, the Evacuee Trust Property Board formally reopened Gurdwara Choa Sahib for worship, its chairman, Dr Aamer Ahmed, presiding over a ceremony open to Sikh pilgrims "whether they come from India or any other part of the world". From 2020 a Sikh organisation of the diaspora undertook its rehabilitation and restoration.

## Guru Nanak

Guru Nanak was the first Guru of the Sikhs and is held to be the founder of the Sikh faith. Accounts of his dates differ, but they are most often given as 1469 to 1539. He spent a major portion of his life in what is today Pakistan: he was born there, and he eventually settled in a small village that is now on the Pakistani side of the border. There are several sites across the country where he is said to have stayed or performed miracles. He preferred to stay on the outskirts of villages, and whenever he visited an area he would visit its religious shrines, Muslim, Hindu and of any other denomination.

It is said that he travelled through most of the world, then came to the town of Batala, married, and settled in a village of Batala on the Ravi, and that people came in their multitudes from every land to become his disciples. Passing over his son Lakhmi Das, who was held to be a worthy son, he made Lahna, a Khatri by caste, his successor, with the title Guru Angad: on his deathbed he appointed not his son but his most loyal student.

He lived as a householder, and he brought a message of peace and harmony to the believers of Islam and Hinduism. He taught that a good life rested on honest labour, compassion, selfless service and prayer. In the janamsakhis, after vanishing for a week in the stream at Sultanpur, he declares: "There is only one God. There is no Hindu, no Mussalman." He offended both Hindus and Muslims by stating that there was no Hindu or Muslim, and he associated with people of the lower castes, calling them people of God.

Nanak himself is held to have disavowed the miraculous, clearly and emphatically, and to have abhorred the association of miracles with religious personalities, yet the stories of his life are full of them. His genius, it has been said, lies in the poetic use of words and not in miracles.

Muslims, too, have claimed the Guru. Many Muslims acquainted with his teachings are devoted to him and visit his shrine, and to one Muslim painter, "Guru Nanak is like a Muslim Sufi Saint". A Muslim tradition holds that Guru Nanak was born by the blessing of Baba Naulakh Hazari, though the Sikhs do not accept it. Baba Farid, whose poetry also stands in the Guru Granth Sahib, became an inspiration for Guru Nanak when the Guru visited his shrine on his travels, and Farid's texts are revered among the Sikhs because he influenced the founder of their religion. In the Guru's time, it has been argued, all literate Punjabis, Hindus and Muslims alike, were conversant with the poets ʿAttar, Rumi and Saʿdi. A twentieth century *shaykh*, a Sufi master, of the Chishti Sabiri line counted Guru Nanak, with Krishna, Rama, Sita and Lachman, among the spiritual luminaries outside Islam who believed in the one true God and had a "Divine connection", *nisbat*. At the shrine of the goddess Hinglaj there are engraved footsteps held to be Guru Nanak's. In Aurangzeb's time the dervishes of Hind, its religious mendicants, were counted in classes, and the fourth were the Udasis, devotees of Baba Nanak, as the Guru was also called, whose worship was to sing their master's verses.

## Devotion and Verse

A fair was held at the gurdwara on the fifteenth of *Kattak Suddi*, the bright half of the month of Kattak in the Indian calendar. In Aurangzeb's time the sum of the worship of Guru Nanak's followers was the reading of their master's verses, which they sang to melody and song. They counted the service of travellers, those who come and go, as the highest worship, and whoever arrived at midnight speaking Baba Nanak's name they treated as a brother and a friend and served, be he a stranger, a thief, a highwayman or an evildoer. They lived at one with their friends and at peace with their enemies. The tradition of *langar*, the free communal kitchen, is believed to have begun with Guru Nanak. In more recent times Sikhs living abroad came to Pakistan for festivals such as Baisakhi, the spring harvest festival, and the birthday of Guru Nanak, which were attended by thousands of Indian pilgrims.

The Guru composed verses in Hindi on the truth of God, in which he set out the unity of God. His *bani*, devotional verses of supplication, entreaty, humility and reverence, is sung outside the gurdwaras too. The singer Abida Parvin has said: "I sing Shah Hussain, Bulle Shah, Mian Muhammad Bakhsh, Hazrat Khawaja Ghulam Farid, Sacchal Sarmast, Baba Farid, and Guru Nanak." In Sindh, Sain Vasan Shah became famous for reciting the *banis* of Shri Guru Nanak and the *kalams*, the poems, of the Sufis. The *doha*, a rhyming couplet, was a form in which poets such as Kabir and Nanak wrote their mystical poetry.

Among the Guru's poems is the *Babur Bani*, a long poem that Nanak wrote at the time the Mughal Babur attacked the Punjab, destroying the cities of Eminabad and Lahore on the way. In it, as it has been read, Nanak lays the blame for Babur's wrath at God's feet and challenges the divinity of the Divine. One of its lines gives its own reckoning of the invaders' coming:

ਆਵਨਿ ਅਠਤਰੈ …

*Coming in seventy-eight (1521 C.E)*

Lines from the Guru's other hymns, in English translation, give a sense of his poetry. His *aarti*, the hymn sung as lamps are waved in worship, has been read as placing God in nature. In one translation it begins:

ਗਗਨ ਮੈ ਥਾਲੁ ਰਵਿ ਚੰਦੁ ਦੀਪਕ ਬਨੇ …

*The sky the salver, the sun and moon the lamps …*

and in another:

*In the salver of the firmament, / The sun and the moon shine as lamps*

On the oneness of God he sang:

ਸਾਹਿਬੁ ਮੇਰਾ ਏਕੋ ਹੈ ॥
ਏਕੋ ਹੈ ਭਾਈ ਏਕੋ ਹੈ ॥

*My Master is One / He is One, brother, the only One*

And again:

*God is One, / The Path is One*

His poem on the sacred thread, the *janeu*, runs:

ਦਇਆ ਕਪਾਹ
ਸੰਤੋਖੁ ਸੂਤੁ …

*From the cotton of compassion, / Spin the thread of contentment*

Of water and washing he sang:

ਭਰੀਐ ਹਥੁ ਪੈਰੁ ਤਨੁ ਦੇਹ ॥
ਪਾਣੀ ਧੋਤੈ ਉਤਰਸੁ ਖੇਹ ॥

*As hands or feet besmirched with slime, / Water washes white*

And in another song:

ਬਗਾ ਬਗੇ ਕਪੜੇ ਤੀਰਥ ਮੰਝਿ ਵਸੰਨਿੑ ॥

*The herons in their white feathers dwell in the sacred shrines of pilgrimage*

Other hymns open with the words *O my merchant friend*, *Launch ye the boat of austere contemplation …*, *As a beggar goes a-begging …* and *His goodness cannot be priced or traded …*.

## Rohtas Fort and Jhelum

The fort was founded by Sher Shah Suri while he was still known as Sher Khan. After the victory at Kanauj and the pursuit that followed as far as Lahore, he went into the country of the Gakhars, came back, founded a fort beside the hills and called it Rohtas, and he left ten thousand horse there to bar the road to the imperial army. Once the affairs of that country were in order he went to Agra, and he struck coin and had the *khutba*, the Friday sermon, read in his own name as Sher Shah. His successor, Islam Shah, brought the fort to completion.

Rohtas lay on the Gakhar border, between the Bihat and the Sindh. Sher Shah and Islam Shah raised the fort there on the frontier so that the Gakhars would be worn down and submit, but they made no headway at all, and in the end they made peace on condition that Sultan Sarang, the ruler of that country, came in.

Rohtas was the western end of Sher Shah's road. Along it, from Bengal to the Rohtas of the Punjab, stood *sarais*, inns for travellers, two *kos* apart, with two horses kept at each, so that news from Bengal reached Rohtas in two or three days. This was the Rohtas of the Punjab; there were other places of the name, among them a fort in Bihar held by Raja Chintaman.

In Aurangzeb's time the river Bihat passed below the town of Jhelum, and there it took the name Jhelum. In the Guru's lifetime the Punjab was plunged into a number of significant political crises. The region between the Indus and the Jhelum had seen two minor raids by Babur before he invaded the Punjab a third time, in 1520, reaching as far as Sialkot.

Jhelum has legends of its own. After his war with Porus, it is told, Alexander the Great was visited at Jhelum by an old Indian man who told him of two talking trees, one male and the other female, a little distance from Jhelum, and Alexander went to see them and indeed found them talking to each other. The tale comes from letters said to be Alexander's, though written after his death. A bodhi tree, the sacred fig of Buddhism, is said to grow near Jhelum, and another in Islamabad.

## Legacy

Gurdwara Choa Sahib keeps at Rohtas the memory of a spring, a thirsty companion and a Guru on the road. It once stood abandoned, a domed building with a black powder on its walls, reached through thorns and summer heat or at dusk beside a forest said to be unsafe. Its reopening in 2019, seventy two years after Partition, returned it to the pilgrims for whom it was built. Across Pakistan, the gurdwaras that devotees later built at the places where Nanak stayed still stand tall, though it has been asked whether Nanak's legacy, in its true essence, is slowly disappearing.
