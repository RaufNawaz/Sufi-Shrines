## Overview

At Sehwan Sharif, in the Jamshoro District of Sindh, stands the great shrine of Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar, a wandering mystic of the thirteenth century who is Pakistan's most beloved figure of devotion. His golden domed *darbar*, the saint's shrine at the heart of the town, draws millions of pilgrims each year, Muslims and Hindus, women and men, rich and poor, who come with their prayers and vows and to join the *dhamal*, the ecstatic dance beaten out every evening on the great drums of his courtyard. Its *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of the saint's death, is one of the defining festivals of the Sindhi year, and the anthem *Dama Dam Mast Qalandar* has carried his name across the world.

He was a *qalandar*, a mystic of the free and rapturous kind who breaks the rules, and he drew Sindh into the world of Sufi devotion: from the day he settled at Sehwan in the thirteenth century, Sindh produced an immense number of mystics, whose shrines are scattered all through the Indus Valley. The town that keeps his tomb was sacred long before him. As one scholar of the town has put it, "Sufi by renown, Shivaite in heritage, and increasingly Shiʿi in following, Sehwan is in many ways Pakistan at its complex best." Lal and Sehwan cannot be told apart: wherever his story is told, the town is told with it.

## The Saint

The saint the world knows as Lal Shahbaz Qalandar is most often named Sayyid Usman Marwandi, though in Sehwan his followers cannot quite agree on his name, any more than on his doctrine or his Sufi affiliations. He was a Sayyid, a descendant of the Prophet Muhammad, of a family that traced its descent through the Imams. His line runs through Ismail, son of Jafar al-Sadiq, which makes him a descendant of Ali, related to the *ahl-e bayt*, the Family of the Prophet, and an important figure of the Ismaili line of the Shiʿi faith.

He was born around 1177. Accounts of his birthplace differ, but it is most often given as Marwand, in the Persian speaking lands to the west. Steeped from youth in learning and devotion, he took to the path of the wandering dervishes and travelled widely through the Muslim world, through Iraq, the Hejaz and Central Asia. He lived in the age of Muinuddin Chishti and Bahauddin Zakariya, who preached divine love and union, but he stood for another side of mystical life. From the thirteenth century onwards learned and religious minded men came into Sindh in great numbers from the centre of the Muslim world, and Lal Shahbaz was one of them: he came with several companions, some of whom made names for themselves elsewhere in India.

Accounts of his road to Sindh differ, but in the end he settled at Sehwan, an ancient town with a deep sacred history older than Islam. Sehwan was then dominated by followers of Shiva and by the Daryapanthis, who held the Indus River to be divine, and it was also under the influence of the Ismailis. Accounts of the year of his arrival differ; the date considered more reliable is 1272. There he lived as a *qalandar*, drawing crowds by his ecstatic sanctity, for the last year or two of his life.

He is called *Lal*, "the Red", for the crimson robe of his order, and *Shahbaz*, "royal falcon", for the soaring freedom of his spirit, though accounts of how he came by these names differ. The title Lal Shahbaz first appears in writing in the late seventeenth century. The name of his famous song, Mast Qalandar, the intoxicated *qalandar*, is used for the saint himself too.

There is no settled view of his order. The Suhrawardi and Qadiri orders both claim him, but he is remembered above all as a *qalandar*, and many of the *faqirs*, the wandering Sufis, of Sehwan hold him to be one of the original *qalandars* of the thirteenth century. In Sindh the presence of the Qalandariyya, the order of the *qalandars*, reflects his radiance and that of the pilgrimage centre of Sehwan. Accounts of his master differ too, but he is often said to have been initiated at Multan by the Suhrawardi master Bahauddin Zakariya. Bahauddin, it is said, disliked *qalandars* intensely and never encouraged their visits, but he made an exception of Lal Shahbaz and adopted him as his disciple. In Sindhi and Punjabi tradition he is counted among the *chaar yaar*, the "four friends" of the *qalandari* way, alongside such saints as Baba Farid and Bahauddin Zakariya, with whom legend links him in companionship. His successor was Sayyid Ali Sarmast, from whom Qutub Ali Shah descended on the maternal side.

Following the *Sharia*, the religious law, held no interest for him. Like the *malamatis*, mystics who hold themselves beyond the bounds of any religious law, he gave himself to ecstatic dancing, and he is remembered as a mystic who radically rejected the religious institutions, norms and practices of Islam. He won a great reputation for sanctity both in his lifetime and after his death. He never married: he was celibate and left no children, and the *faqirs* of Sehwan make him the very type of celibacy, saying that because he did not marry, he had cast his loins in iron. That celibacy came to matter in 1960, when the state took over his shrine.

He composed in Persian, and a *diwan*, a collection of poems in that language, is ascribed to him. He is also credited with a number of works used in a later age to teach the young. The course of study of the *madrasas*, the religious schools, of Sindh began with the *Mizan-i-sarf*, by Lal Shahbaz, the famous scholar and saint of Sehwan; then came his *Ajnas*, or *Munshaib*, written in Persian on the rules for forming the increased derivatives; and then his *Akd*, on the permutation of letters.

He died at Sehwan, and the stories of his burial place it a year after he arrived in the town. Accounts of the year of his death differ, but it is most often given as 1274. Historical references to him are few, a patchwork of brief mentions, and mystery suits him better than history.

He bears at least three genealogies, Sufi, Shiʿi and Shivaite. As a descendant of Ali he especially draws Pakistan's Shiʿa, and he stands in the place of Ali, if he is not an avatar of Ali himself. Hindus revere him in the guise of Shiva incarnate, as a Shivaite ascetic who embodies an ideal of renunciation. At Sehwan there is only one place of worship, but its saint has two names: Muslims call him Lal Shahbaz Qalandar, and the local Hindus worship him as Raja Bharthari, or Raja Vir, a main figure of the Nathpanth, the tradition of the Nath yogis. At such a shrine there is only one annual fair, in which the Hindus play a leading ritual role. He is known, too, as Jhule Lal, a name originally given to the river deity of the Indus, and as Jivelal, the living *Pir*, or spiritual guide.

## The Shrine

Lal Shahbaz's shrine at Sehwan, with its gilded dome and mirrored interior, is among the most important places of pilgrimage in Pakistan. It grew from a plain grave into a centre of public pilgrimage. The grave is first mentioned in the fourteenth century by the North African traveller Ibn Battuta; a first mausoleum is thought to have stood by 1357, and a second to have been built around 1585. The shrine rose to prominence in the Mughal period, and its buildings are likely to have grown with its wealth. Whatever stood on the site before it, the shrine has a rich history of Hindu association and patronage.

Many in Sehwan believe that the shrine of Lal stands where the high temple of Shiva once stood, and the site is thought to replace an ancient sanctuary of Shiva. Archaeology has not confirmed the belief, though historians think that some overlap is likely. A *lingam*, the emblem of Shiva, is said to have stood long ago inside the shrine.

The building that stands today is recent. Plans for a new mausoleum were carried out in the early 1990s, during the premiership of Benazir Bhutto, when a much larger structure replaced the old Sindhi mausoleum, and the Department of Auqaf, the government department of religious endowments, has made several rounds of repairs before and since. The report drawn up for the redevelopment, probably between 1989 and 1992, in Benazir Bhutto's first government, named its models: Khomeini's Square in Isfahan, the Alhambra in Granada, the Taj Mahal in Agra, and the shrines of imams and saints in Iran and Iraq. The result marked a shift from the vernacular to what the report calls the "truly Islamic". The shape of the golden dome and the form of the minarets copy the mausoleums of the Shiʿi imams in Iran and Iraq, although the state that built them is predominantly Sunni; both Zulfiqar Ali Bhutto and his daughter took a personal part in the plans for the shrine and the town.

The mausoleum has an elaborate blue tiled facade, and its courtyard is long, rectangular and of white marble, flanked along its length by shaded arcades, where many visitors, especially women and poorer families, would spend the night. The *durbar* is the octagonal audience hall where hundreds circle the tomb. Two prayer corners, not true mosques but semiprivate sections for ritual prayer, fill the space left over where the octagon meets the square plan, raised high enough to give an uninterrupted view of the tomb, and a separate room for Shiʿi prayer lies in an adjacent part of the shrine. The durbar has three doors, tall and heavily carved in wood, and a metal lock wrapped in red silk. Where the entrance portal gives way to the open courtyard stand a drum and a metal disc; there are large donation boxes, and secondary entrances, with a tiny shop across from one of them.

There is also an outer yard: an old public square, home to the *qadimi ʿalam*, an old structure commemorating the battle of Karbala, has been paved and enclosed with a low wall as part of an ever extending shrine complex, and markets surround the shrine. Such constant reshaping, much of it at the hands of the administration, was already weakening the shrine's old bond with the bazaar and had greatly altered the life of the place by night.

At the centre of the durbar stands the tomb, lined with silver and decked with silks and rose petals, from which the saint, as *shahanshah*, the King of kings, was said to hold court and grant audience. Today hundreds of funerary sheets drape it, so many that, it is widely claimed in Sehwan, no mortal has ever seen the surface of the tomb itself, and for this reason many *faqirs* and leading *sayyids* of the town do not speak of the shrine as a tomb but call it the *takht*, the throne, of Lal. Over the tomb hung the *guluband*, a heart shaped necklace that belonged to the saint.

The shrine has been remodelled again and again, from the 1990s to its latest expansion, still under way in 2018, a sign of its growing importance and of the state's faith in its economic worth. Between 2009 and 2013 its facade and interior were remodelled, and the marble floor of the courtyard became newer and whiter; by 2018 the shrine had grown grander in scale, its facades more ornate than before and its dome more golden than ever. A room where healing took place was demolished to make way for the growing shrine, and a prayer room was added only in later rebuildings.

The rebuilding also took things away. When the tomb hall was redesigned in 2013, there was no place for the saint's customary five lamps, and a local Hindu holy man who had lit them twice a day could no longer do so. It was part of a gradual but systematic fading of the shrine's Hindu character at the hands of the department: during early repairs in the 1970s the doorbells were removed, and so was what some locals describe as an object like a Shiva *lingam*. In 2013 explicitly Sunni inscriptions, the names of the first four caliphs, were introduced in the tomb hall; by 2018 they were gone, possibly removed when parts of the hall were repaired after the attack of 2017.

Inside the durbar, in a corner like a cove, lies a little grave that was taken into the tomb hall when it was redesigned in the 1990s: the tomb of one of the earliest disciples of Lal. One woman regarded herself as the lone guardian of this forgotten grave. From the old fortress the two domes of the shrines of Lal and of Bodlo, his beloved *faqir*, can be seen together. The sultan Muhammad Shah Tughluq, who came to Sindh in 1351 and died shortly afterwards at Sonda on the banks of the Indus, was buried in Sehwan, near the shrine. The followers of the Hindu *faqir* Mulchand, most of whom had migrated to India, arranged a tomb for him while he was still alive, in a compound by the shrine also known as Mulan Faqir jo asthan, and the disciples of the Hindu lamp lighter Lal Das raised a small *dargah*, a shrine, for him not far from that of the saint he served.

The saint's memory reaches beyond the walls: into a graveyard of the town, into the Indus River, and into Lal-bagh, the garden of Lal, the other major site of his memory. It is a wooded grove a few kilometres south of the town, where Baba-Akram once founded a dwelling for *faqirs*, and where, legend has it, the saint himself camped before making his way into the town proper, so earning the grove its name. Revered trees in the grove and natural springs in the area spoke of the saint's miracles, part of a whole constellation of sacred sites in and around Sehwan that pilgrims were sure to visit, among them Chothambi and the springs of Lal-bagh. At Chhoti Nali, one of the springs associated with the saint on the outskirts of the town, every Hindu man and woman of Sehwan used to bathe on the eve of Shivratri, the festival of Shiva.

The shrine's openness has also made it a target. On 16 February 2017 a suicide bomber struck the courtyard during the evening *dhamal*. Accounts of the toll differ, but scores of worshippers were killed. It fitted the tragic pattern of recent years, in which key Sufi shrines in Pakistan have been targeted by hardline militants and radical Islamist networks: more than twenty five had been attacked since 2005. Amma, a *faqir* woman of the shrine, saw the bombing and survived it, and some of the unidentified dead were laid to rest in the graveyard on the town's northern edge. Yet, in a gesture that became famous, the *dhamal* was danced again at the shrine the very next evening, in defiance of the terror.

The shrine changed after it. Its administration and security were entirely revamped, and simply walking in became an ordeal, with numerous security checks, police and security cameras everywhere and metal detectors at all the entrances. Offerings to the saint were thoroughly screened, intelligence officials joined the order of authorities, so that everyday decisions passed through many channels, and a section of the police force was assigned to protect the shrine. By 2018 the entrances had been pushed further out. The tightening had begun earlier: after a terror attack on Lahore's most prominent shrine, security had been strengthened at major Sufi sites across the country, and much at Sehwan was already changing by 2011.

## Keepers, Lodges and the State

The shrine is administered by the Sindh Auqaf department, and it remains the beating heart of Sehwan. Its traditional custodians are two notable *sayyid* lineages of the town, the Lakkiyari and the Sabzwari, families that claim descent from the Prophet. Each tells its own story: the Sabzwaris hold that their ancestors were chosen disciples who came with Lal on his journey from Iran, while the Lakkiyaris, from the neighbouring hills of Lakki, say that they descend from the saint's primary followers, who received him on the outskirts of Sehwan. Many *faqirs* of the town claim, for their part, that the shrine's original heirs were the saint's ascetic followers.

The two families still hold significant influence in the town, but their claims on the shrine and its revenues can be traced back only as far as the colonial period. In the period after the eighteenth century, under British rule, duties and services at the shrine were shared between Hindu and Muslim groups, and the two lineages fought long court disputes over its revenues and above all its custodianship; the rift between them dates back to British times, and the rivalry endures to this day. The Lakkiyaris are usually accepted as the head of the town's system of lodges, but the Sabzwaris have shared its symbolic power since the shrine came under government control. Among the traditional custodians today is Sayyid Mehdi Raza Shah Sabzwari, and Sayyid Hasan Shah Khatdhani is a prominent spiritual figure of Sehwan.

In the 1950s a countrywide survey of religious endowments was under way, and through a federal ordinance of 1959, the West Pakistan Waqf Properties Ordinance, the state began to take into its custody sites considered *waqf* (plural *auqaf*), inalienable endowments. All endowments that were neither private nor solely charitable could now pass legally into the hands of the state, to be run by the Department of Auqaf under the Ministry of Religious Affairs. The takeovers were not without conflict: some brought long legal battles with the families affected, and a few appeals lasted well into the 1980s. The shrine of Sehwan was taken over from its traditional custodians in June 1960. The saint's celibacy was a key line of argument: a shrine without legal heirs could not be deemed a family's inheritance.

The takeover promised the state new financial means and a check on the influence of local powerhouses. Those who lost by it were the *sayyids*, who had controlled the shrine's revenues at least since the colonial period, local Hindu patrons long tied to the shrine's services and ceremonies, and Hindu families who once had a greater role in them. The former custodians' roles at the shrine were reduced to the ceremonial, but they keep considerable leverage: they seek a say in its upkeep by advising the state, sit on shrine committees and help to organise the annual ceremonies, and a large number of the rented shops in the market beside the shrine belong to members of their families. Most *sayyids* in the town trace their descent to the saint's primary disciples, and centuries of attachment to the shrine and its revenues, of social privilege and of official favour have let these lineages prosper both economically and politically.

Above all, they kept the lodges. In Sehwan a lodge where *faqirs* stay, elsewhere called a *khanqah*, is known as a *kafi*; the origin of the word is unknown, though it may be linked to the *kafis*, poetic verses, sung there. Sehwan has a system of fourteen *kafis*, the town's primary and ancient lodges, believed to have been founded by the saint himself, and only one of the fourteen is owned and headed by someone who is not a *sayyid*. The lodges are fraternities whose histories go back to Lal's earliest disciples: many are named after such figures and run by the local elite, most of whom also trace their lineage to the saint's contemporaries, and as patrons and masters of the *faqirs* in them, the *sayyids* keep part of their old standing. How many lesser lodges there are is hard to determine. The lodges are settled all around the shrine, and their holders are bound to one another by complex ties of authority and dependence, in what has been called the Sehwani system. Among them is the Doda Mard Haqani Kafi, devoted to a figure held to be a disciple of Lal Shahbaz, where a portrait of Ali is shown.

Two of the lodges are managed by Hindus: the *kafi* of Lal Das, and Mulchand Kafi, whose head, its *sajjada nashin*, literally the one who sits on the prayer carpet, is Ramchand. Mulchand Kafi, where Mulchand's tomb stands, adjoins the kafi of Doda Mard Haqani. Although few Hindus remain in Sehwan, it works to some extent as a place of community worship: it is open to the public mainly at the *ʿurs*, but certain ceremonies of the cycle of life are always performed there, and one of its buildings stores the objects paraded in the saint's *mehndi* procession. Asan Gul is another Hindu *sajjada nashin* of Sehwan, and at Sehwan a Hindu disciple of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar initiates Muslims into the path, the *tariqa*, of the Qalandariyya Shahbaziyya.

In 1972, among the hundreds of shrines in Sindh, the shrine of Sehwan earned the most revenue, and its annual fair drew bigger crowds than any other. The state collects the donations pilgrims offer and draws revenue from *waqf* properties through work contracts, rents and leases, which it says it uses to maintain and rebuild old shrines and other religious endowments. Offerings of money to the saint are either collected in donation boxes or shared among the shrine workers on duty, and many of the contract workers seen washing the shrine floor would have found their way into the town's most important economic network through an exchange of favours between the Auqaf and the *sayyids*.

The state also made Sehwan national. In Pakistan's early years the shrine had a largely regional appeal, drawing mostly Sindhi and Baloch pilgrims from around the region. A direct and steady investment in saints' fairs, in the upkeep of mausoleums and their facilities, and in roads and railways to places of pilgrimage changed that: special trains such as the Jhule Lal Express were run for the pilgrims' yearly journey between Lahore and Sehwan, and the superhighway of the early 1980s joined the country's major towns. Zulfiqar Ali Bhutto, a follower of the saint, took a personal interest in the town, and the Sehwan Developmental Authority was founded while he was prime minister; the saint's fame has grown especially since Bhutto's rule in the 1970s.

The state's Sindhi publications hardly mention the saint or his shrine until the late 1960s. From the 1960s onward Urdu and Sindhi newspapers, among them the Sindhi newspaper *Daily Ibrat*, published in Hyderabad, reported the annual fair in great detail, and state bodies such as the Department of Culture and the Sindhi Adabi Board routinely publish books and brochures on the saint's life and on Sehwan's history and heritage. The fair became a platform too: in November 1969, at the annual conference held at the saint's fair, the Sindhi nationalist leader G. M. Syed traced features of the shrine's rituals and of the *faqirs'* practices to Shivaite forms.

The state is explicitly Sunni in its disposition, yet its infrastructure has systematically furthered a place of devotion that is historically Shivaite and increasingly Shiʿi. In the 1990s, a decade of sectarian violence and political division, Sehwan became a place of communal identification and resistance politics for Pakistan's Shiʿa. The saint's unresolved history has left his shrine, with its relics, revenues, services and ceremonies, open to rival claims, and it has drawn long disputes of authority among *faqirs*, princely patrons, the *sayyid* elite, the traditional custodians and, more recently, the state of Pakistan.

## Devotion

Thronged all year round, and fullest at the annual *ʿurs*, the shrine has always been a strikingly inclusive place, drawing Muslims and Hindus alike and women in great numbers, and it is famed for a devotion that is joyous, bodily and unrestrained. Its devotees come from every social, religious and ethnic background: if Sehwan draws the poorest of the poor, Lal's followers have also included the country's former prime ministers and its top fashion models and photographers. Hindu and Muslim communities, local and from afar, visit the shrine regularly and both take part in the worship of the saint. In the courtyard *faqir* women found refuge, spirits surrendered to the sound of the drums and Hindu pilgrims brushed shoulders with Shiʿa devotees, while locals simply passed the time, tea sellers and vendors of every kind sought customers, and children played. Women, men and the *khwaja-sara*, the community of the third gender, cross paths in many places and on most occasions, though not in all. Groups of *malangs*, Sufis who live outside the religious law, dressed in black, gathered around his sanctuary.

Pilgrims kissing the tomb, women singing to the saint, men flagellating their bare torsos, the sick and the seemingly possessed screaming, losing their composure and then regaining it: all of these are daily sights in Sehwan, along with the making of offerings, the touching of relics and dealings with spirits. One devotee tells of holding the *jali*, the metal latticework around the tomb, and taking the saint as *murshid*, spiritual guide. His devotees believe that many prayers are answered at the shrine and many wishes granted. He is held to be a *jalali pir*, a mystic of intense supernatural energy, and his shrine a *jalali* shrine, as against the shrines of the *jamali*, or aesthetic, mystics; the Sufi *faqirs* of Sindh belonged to the two great orders of the Jelali and the Jemali dervishes.

Each day begins with the *ghusl*, the ritual bathing of the saint's tomb. On a Friday in November 2011 it began around half past three in the morning, with employees of the Department of Auqaf, contract workers and cleaners, a handful of *faqirs*, visitors and pilgrims present, while a policeman tried to keep order. The junior manager of the shrine arrived with its keys; there were *khadims*, attendants, some employed by the state and others on contract. A *faqir* from a prominent lodge of the Lakkiyaris walked in with a large bundle wrapped in red cloth, and another, representing a secondary shrine of the town, carried a big basket filled with rose petals.

The Lakkiyaris provide the most important item of the tomb's dressing, the *dastar*, the turban. It is prepared with fresh leaves at one of their lodges just outside the shrine, and every morning a representative delivers it and collects the old one, which is taken back to the lodge and placed at a minor shrine there: one example of a wider exchange of favours between the state and the *sayyid* elite of Sehwan. Ornamented jewels, decorative flowers and peacock feathers add the final touch to the dressing of the saint's crown, finished with perfumed oils and sprays, and wealthy pilgrims sometimes give ornaments for the dressing of the tomb. As the cleaning draws to a close, the Auqaf manager walks over to the three doors of the durbar and sprinkles rose water on those watching from outside; then, with a loud wave of chants, people flock in to touch and kiss the freshly dressed tomb, bringing new covers and fresh petals and offering sweets and money to the saint. That the rite is performed by a junior manager of the department, who holds the keys, is an everyday staging of state power: the shrine of Sehwan is no longer a family's inheritance but a public asset.

Only the men assigned to the washing may enter at this time; the bathing of the saint is held to be an intimate event that needs privacy, and women are discouraged from being inside the durbar during it. On Friday the rite is more elaborate, and it is considered auspicious because of the religious significance of the day. It has narrowed over the years: the cleaning and lighting of five lamps, the *chiraghi*, could no longer be seen by 2013, and by 2018 the bathing had become a completely private affair, with visitors no longer allowed into the courtyard to witness it in the early hours.

The lamps had their own servants. Lal Das, a Hindu from Kashmir, came to Sehwan on pilgrimage and devoted nearly forty years of his life to the service of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar as the *chiraghi wala*, the keeper of the lamps, until he died in the odour of sanctity in 1993.

The evening belongs to the drums. Every day at sunset the *dhamal* is performed in the main courtyard: professional drummers play the great drums, the *naqqara*, which lie in the courtyard during the day, and to the sound of reed and drum hundreds of devotees dance to reach ecstasy, above all on Thursday evenings and at the *ʿurs*. *Faqirs* in orange robes whirl in a circle, a passionate dance said to express the immense *jalal*, the spiritual energy, of the saint. Before the blaze of lamps, the *dhamal* of Sehwan is one of the most electrifying expressions of devotion in the Muslim world. Women take part too, in a different manner and in their own section of the courtyard, and the evening performance is carefully segregated. Families often bring women who are emotionally disturbed to join the dance, which devotees say has healing powers, and in the bazaar outside cassettes of healing music for the emotionally disturbed could be bought. Some compare the *dhamal* of the *faqirs* of this shrine with the dance of the whirling dervishes of Maulana Rumi at Konya. Once a month the *faqirs* of Bodlo Bahar, the main follower of Lal Shahbaz, who has a shrine nearby, come and perform a beautiful whirling dance in their red skirts.

With the *dhamal* comes *haziri*, literally "presence". Each evening in the white marble courtyard, women from across the country experience it: responding to the sounds of drum and reed, spirits that dwell with them are believed to become present, and hundreds of women are left in bouts of exhilaration. When the *dhamal* ends, their families help them recover from the exhausting experience, and *faqirs*, for their gift of telling good spirits from bad, are often called to watch over the women's passage through it.

The sung word has its place too. This is one of the shrines where *qawwali*, the devotional music of the Sufis, is performed every evening after the prayers, and *sufiana kalam*, Sufi poetry sung to music, is a feature of the shrines of Sindh, especially those of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar and of Shah Abdul Latif at Bhit.

The day ends a little after midnight, when the high doors of the shrine are ritually closed and people are asked to withdraw from the courtyard. As the doors are drawn, an elaborate prayer is led by the Auqaf manager, and a *faqir*'s regular chants mark both the opening of the shrine's doors and their closing at midnight. The week has its own peak in the frenzy of Thursday night, characteristic of the saints' shrines of Pakistan.

Women serve the pilgrims too. A woman disciple, a *muridiani*, gives water to the devotees, an act counted in Islam as *savab*, a charitable deed, and praised in the oral Sufi traditions, especially in connection with the battle of Karbala. Around the *guluband* that hung over the tomb, women devotees held up glasses of water to seek its blessing, and then shared the water with their kinswomen and men; to orthodox Muslims it may seem a fetish, but devotees draw strength from the rite.

There is a trade in photographs, too. For 50 rupees a pilgrim could have himself set into a collage of saintly figures, sites and symbols, and of political personalities besides. Pilgrims were routinely caught in postures of prayer, as if posing with the saint, facing not his tomb, as was customary, but the camera, and pictures that digitally set pilgrims close to the saint are all too common; in such pictures Lal also appears flying above in his popular falcon form.

The shrine has drawn other Sufis. Rakhiyyal Shah, a Qadiri Sufi of high rank who lived from 1846 to 1940, once came to pay homage to Lal Shahbaz Qalandar, and the Hindu Sufi Sobhraj Faqir, who loved the *dhamal* and often danced it with Mulchand Faqir, wished to end his life at Sehwan, and did so.

## The ʿUrs and the Mehndi

The great event of the year is the *ʿurs*, called in Sehwan simply the *mela*, the fair. In Pakistan the death anniversaries of saints are celebrated as their union with the divine, and *ʿurs* is Arabic for a wedding; one *faqir* used instead the Urdu word *shadi*, which means the same, and told of the invitation he had received to the saint's wedding. Accounts of its month and length differ, but it is held over three days, the 18th, 19th and 20th of Shaʿban, the eighth month of the Islamic year, when hundreds of thousands of devotees converge on Sehwan for days of *dhamal*, *qawwali* and *langar*, free food from the communal kitchen. During the celebrations it is almost impossible to walk in the narrow streets of the town.

Before Partition, Hindus and Muslims assembled together at his tomb on his saint's day to honour his memory: thousands of them gathered during the annual fair, mixing freely and singing the Sufi songs that ever melt the heart. During the fair the grove of Lal-bagh, at other times ideal for the seclusion of *faqirs*, would fill with a sprawling temporary bazaar, and visiting bands of *faqirs* and large groups of pilgrims would camp on its grounds. The state relies on its working relationship with the town's elite to organise the great events of the year, among them the town's ritual processions at the time of the saint's fair and in the holy month of Muharram. When the three days are over, many *faqirs* go on to Lahut in Balochistan, or follow others to the shrines of Karachi.

The heart of the *ʿurs* is the *mehndi*, the henna of a wedding. Henna stands for the marriage between the Sufi and God, and so for death as their union. Each procession follows the same pattern, with singers, musicians and dancers: a chosen person wears the silk *chadar*, the sheet of cloth that is laid on the tomb of the *qalandar*, and places a tray on which the henna lies. The procession reproduces the classic procession of a marriage, winding through the narrow streets while its followers grow more and more numerous, and the roofs of the houses fill with onlookers who do not want to miss the show. Its order reflects the balance reached by the communities who share in the saint's charisma. The protocol is set by the state, and at the *ʿurs* the position of each group, honorary or real, is reaffirmed. No timetable is fixed for the *mehndi*: it proceeds according to the Auqaf manager's document for the procession of the *matamis*, the ritual mourners.

It is said that laying the *chadar* on the grave amounts to putting the fiancée in her wedding clothes, and that the next morning palm and finger prints can be seen in the henna left there the day before, as though in the night the *qalandar* had coated his hands with henna, ready to be united with his divine lover. Local tradition traces the Hindu share in the rite to the saint himself: two brothers, Meru and Nao, used to look after Lal Shahbaz by bringing him food, and in gratitude he decided that they would lead the *mehndi* after his death, when his own *ʿurs* would be celebrated.

The *mehndi* is led on the first day, the 18th of Shaʿban, by the dominant group of *sayyids*, the Lakkiyaris, and on the second and third days by Hindus. Before Partition, Gul Muhammad Lakkiyari led the first procession; on the second day it left the house of the Miranis and Navanis under the direction of Mulchand Faqir; and on the third day it started from the house of the Kanungos. Today Gul Muhammad's eldest son, Murad Shah, the head of the Lakkiyaris, leads the first day's *mehndi* in his father's place. The Hindu share is old: the Navani family, with the Kanungos, had performed the *mehndi* for centuries, and Dalpat Sufi was born at Sehwan into the Mirani family, one of the families whose members performed it.

Mulchand Faqir, of the Navani family, lived from 1883 to 1962. He was not initiated by any local Sufi master: his only connection with Sufism at Sehwan ran directly through Lal Shahbaz Qalandar. He lived as a recluse, probably leaving his dwelling only to perform the saint's *mehndi*, which he did until his death, and in Sehwan he is known as a *mehndibardar*, a bearer of the henna. Through that ritual role his family was fully part of the town's dominant groups.

Accounts of who succeeded Mulchand differ. His first successor was Chandmal Chandrani, a local Hindu of Sehwan, but in 1971 Chandmal decided to migrate to India, and his son Kanya Lal, who had a vegetable shop in the Shahi Bazar of Sehwan, refused to take care of Mulchand Kafi, so Mulchand's kinsman Mohan Lal took care of it and took on the duty of the *mehndi* as well. When Mohan Lal died in 1981, his son Ramchand began to perform the *mehndi*, and he is still in charge. Ramchand, the *sajjada nashin* of Mulchand Kafi, is an Amil, of a community of Sindhi Hindus, but his mother was a Thakur, of the priestly caste of Jhulelal. Every year he leads the procession from Mulchand Kafi to the saint's *darbar*. Unlike other heads of lodges, he does not stay to welcome disciples or give them advice and *tawiz*, amulets, and no *faqir* stays at his kafi.

The second day's procession starts from Mulchand Kafi, on the 19th of Shaʿban, and it is the main event of the kafi. Before it comes the *dastarbandi* of Ramchand, his coronation, when the ceremonial turban, the *pag*, is set on his head. A musical session follows, with musicians and dancers from the Punjab, and the high point of the listening is the singing of the famous song devoted to the saint, *Dama Dam Mast Qalandar*. Then the henna is prepared under a canopy.

The third day belonged to the Kanungos. Later they migrated to India and settled at Ulhasnagar, and the duty fell to Lal Das, the Hindu from Kashmir who lit the saint's lamps, who became the *mehndibardar*. Today it is fulfilled by Lal Gul, who is said to be his son. For Lal Gul's procession there is a musical session in a house, with the head of the Lakkiyaris, Murad Shah, present.

A caste of saffron workers, the Khuhnbatis, whose name comes from the Sindhi word *khuhnbo*, saffron, was given a priestly role in the ceremony of the saint's mystical nuptials. In the middle of the nineteenth century, every year a daughter of the Khuhnbati caste, the saffron flower dyers, was married to the grave, with music, dance and all the solemnities due to her; she was released after a year, but had to remain unmarried for ever. The custom, known as the Lal Kunwar, the fiancée of the red, was part of the fair's ceremonies until it disappeared at Partition in 1947. It is the one exception in a protocol, and a sharing of tasks between the faiths, that has otherwise not changed in its fundamentals since Partition.

In a Pakistan under the pressure of Islamist extremists, it may seem surprising that Hindus still play a ritual role at Sehwan. The wish to preserve a historic heritage explains it in the first place, but the Hindus do not hold these offices as Hindus: they hold them as Hindu Sufis, and their processions cannot be told apart from the Muslim ones, nothing especially Hindu being seen in them.

## Poetry and Song

Among the Persian verses attributed to him is a ringing declaration of who he was, an intoxicated lover of God and a devotee of the Prophet's family:

حیدریم قلندرم مستم
بندۂ مرتضیٰ علی ہستم

*I am a Haidari, a qalandar, and intoxicated; I am the slave of Murtaza Ali.*

In 1953, at a *versi*, a gathering for a death anniversary, *kalams*, devotional poems, of the Sufi poets of Sindh, such as Bedil and Bekas and Lal Shahbaz Qalandar too, were sung all night long. Lal Shahbaz is reported to have said of the *dhamal*:

*For the sake of the love of the friend / I dance over fire / Sometimes I roll in the dust / And sometimes I dance on the thorns*

The lines end:

*I am not afraid of this disrepute / To dance in every bazaar*

But it is not chiefly his own verse that keeps his memory: it is the great devotional song in his honour, *Dama Dam Mast Qalandar*, which calls on Ali too, and whose refrain rings out wherever South Asian Sufi music is sung:

دَمادَم مست قلندر،
علی دَم دَم دے اندر

*At every breath, the intoxicated Qalandar! Ali is in every breath.*

At the shrine it was a folk devotional number, *O Lal meri*, customarily sung by women pilgrims. In the summer of 1969 it was taken from the shrine and arranged for a Punjabi film: it was composed by Ashiq Hussain, and Noor Jahan, Pakistan's most iconic singer, was asked to sing it. Soon the country was abuzz with its chorus. The song featured twice in the film and set off a short but powerful trend in the Punjabi cinema of the time: at least six films between 1969 and 1975 had songs in praise of the saint, and Lal appeared in several films, each time shown in flight, and rescuing the heroine in distress. So one of his minor miracles became a most enduring image, the falcon saint in flight. The Bukhari brothers, who directed and produced the film number, went on to organise pilgrimages from Lahore to Sehwan, reserving entire trains for their yearly journeys.

The melody, the famous *Mast Qalandar* *dhun*, or tune, is said to be sung all along the lands that run from Rajasthan and Sindh through Balochistan to Iran, and almost every folk singer in the region can sing it.

Sehwan is also the great market of the women musicians of the Sufi shrines. The town, a historic city built within a large fortress from the time of Alexander the Great, is flooded with female musicians who come to earn a living, most of them from the Punjab and from the neighbourhoods of Sindh itself, and they perform in every nook and cranny and draw huge audiences. Many come from the Punjab during the *ʿurs* and sing at the shrine with the utmost devotion, to audiences of men and women alike. Performers such as Reshma sing at the shrine in high, full throated voices, without the support of microphones. Some of the women who sang there as roving minstrels, such as Zeena Bai, became trained classical and semiclassical musicians in Hyderabad, and Taj Mastani, who belongs to Sehwan, moved to Hyderabad because it is a large city.

One tradition of women singers survives in Sindh only here. The *lotevalis*, gypsy women who come with a *lota*, a water pot, sing at the shrine of Shahbaz Qalandar, the only place in Sindh where they still come, and make a living on the rupee or two of alms they receive there.

Reshma's career began in the courtyard. She first sang at the shrine of Shahbaz Qalandar, still goes there every year, and when she was young made a *mannat*, a vow, there for her brother. She was singing there in Siraiki during an *ʿurs* of the 1960s when she was discovered by the media. She believes that the saint has blessed her, and she says that he calls for her at least three or four times, and that she goes and sings for him, her master, and he listens to her songs. The melody she sang honours his *jalal* as a bestower of children:

*Lāj merī pat rakio bhalā / Jhule Lālan, / Sindhṛi dā, / Sehwan dā, / Sakhi Shahbaz Qalandar*

*O, keep my prestige / O Lalan, protector of the cradle, / O thou of Sind, / O thou of Sehwan, / Benevolent Shahbaz Qalandar*

In one lyric he is venerated as the bestower of children:

*Thou giver of children to mothers / Thou giver of brothers to sisters*

The women also sing poems that glorify his *karamat*, the miraculous deeds of a saint. In Sindh, where women often address the saints with endearing diminutives, Piral and Ghauthal, there are poems in honour of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar of Sehwan, who is often implored when a woman wants a son.

The devotion crosses languages. Poems for Lal Shahbaz are found in Balochi too, because Baloch tribes migrate into the Indus Valley in winter, and one Baloch poem praises him:

*also Shahbaz, the generous to his friends, a firm embankment erected by the Ruler of the Faithful.*

In Punjabi verse Lal Shahbaz Qalandar of Sehwan is one of the Five Pirs, saints whose collective powers come to the aid of Ranjha, the hero of the romance of Hir and Ranjha.

## Legends

A great many miracles are said to have been performed by him, and even his tomb is known for innumerable miraculous feats. In legend he fights the old order with miraculous conduct, strikes down his foes and lays the foundations of new communities and institutions. The most defining image of the saint is his flight, preserved in written accounts and carried in oral legend, and it is counted among his minor miracles.

The holy men of Sehwan, it is said, did not welcome him. They sent him a bowl of milk filled to the brim, a sign that the town was already full of the people of God and had no room left for him. Lal Shahbaz floated a flower in the bowl and sent it back to them: just as the flower had made its place in the bowl full of milk, so a place would be made for him too. The flower in milk is a key motif of the Sufi stories of the Indus valley and beyond, told also of Pir Shams of the Ismailis and of Guru Nanak.

A harsher story tells that when the saint wished to settle, the owner of the land refused to give up his claim to it, and Lal struck him dead with a single blow of his staff. Questioned about it, the saint declared that he had merely killed a dog; unconvinced, the dead man's family dug up the grave, and found in it a dead dog.

His burial is told as a wonder too. Amma, the *faqir* woman of the shrine, tells, as others in the town do, that when the time came to bury the saint, the mullahs of the town set out to give his body its funeral bath. As they undressed him they found layers upon layers of cloth tied about his groin: each time they removed one, another appeared, and the truth of his body was never revealed, which for many is proof that he was fiercely celibate. Then a mysterious voice asked that the body be veiled on every side, and when the curtains were lifted, a miraculous tomb was already in place.

The fullest of the legends is told of his beloved *faqir* Bodlo, who, the story goes, called out the saint's name every day as he swept the alleys of Sehwan. Irked by the constant nuisance, the prince who lived in the fortress above the town ordered that Bodlo be killed; but each time he was killed, Bodlo returned to the streets the next morning, and after six failed attempts the prince ordered that he be cut to pieces. When Lal set foot in the town he called out for his beloved *faqir*, and in answer every piece of Bodlo's butchered body rose from the skulls of those who had eaten him. So furious was Lal that, to call down divine punishment, he turned his cup over, and the fortress and all around it were turned on their heads. The place is remembered as the inverted city or the fortress of the infidels, the *ulta sheher*, *kafir-qila* or *ulta qila*, and the memory of Lal's wrath is etched in its landscape, where he is believed to have turned what was once an ancient settlement on its head.

A saying of Sehwan gives Bodlo precedence: "Whoever comes to me must first go to Bodlo," Lal is believed to have said. Most pilgrims therefore make their first offerings at Bodlo's shrine, and the otherwise small shrine is a destination of great importance in Sehwan. Local legend tells, too, that Bodlo's she-camel, a gift from Lal, would leave the lodge every evening to collect food for its *faqirs*, and that when the *faqirs* of the other lodges plotted together and killed the camel, Lal ordered that they be marked from then on with a seal, to set them apart from the *faqirs* of Bodlo.

Tradition holds that the saint himself danced an ecstatic, whirling dance in which he saw the supreme form of *zikr*, the remembrance of God, a view that brought him close to Jalal al-Din Rumi, the celebrated founder of the brotherhood of the whirling dervishes. Through the *dhamal*, it is said, Lal Shahbaz Qalandar reached *fana fi Allah*, the final stage of absorption into God.

For many in Sehwan, *faqirs* or not, coming to the town and leaving it always depend on Lal's approval, and *faqirs* believe that saints summon them. One man in Sehwan spoke of marriage with the saint: when Lal contracts a *nikah*, a marriage contract, with someone, that someone becomes his wife. Zaheda held that a *faqir* woman, because she is a woman, can be close to the saint in a way no male *faqir* can. Amma told of the saint's correction: in the durbar the saint told her, "I will bend your foot!", and her foot was sprained there for the first time, then a second and a third time, and then it was burned. She told, too, of a prayer granted.

Two fairies from Koh-Qaf, the fabled mountains of Qaf, are said once to have come to Sehwan in amorous pursuit of Lal. In Shah-Bibi's description, Sehwan is one of three vertebrae that hold the earth in place, Karbala and the Hijaz being the other two. Every being is present there, she says, those made of light and those made of fire, angels, the friends of God, prophets, and every *qutb*, *ghaws*, *wali*, *pir* and *faqir*, ranks of saintly figures: "Let me tell you this one thing, that this is the main tower!", her main tower being the English for a telecommunications mast. Locals also boast that Sehwan is a sacred geography of 125,000 shrines, or that it has forever been a place of pilgrimage.

## The Setting

Sehwan is an ancient pilgrimage town in Pakistan's Sindh Province, hardly 300 kilometres north of Karachi, a drive of four hours. By the provisional count of the 2017 census, greater Sehwan, with its neighbouring villages, had about 269,291 people, of whom 104,239 lived in the town. It lies in central Sindh, where Vicholi was spoken, the Sindhi dialect that the British proclaimed the official Sindhi language.

The town's past is older than Islam. A seventh century account shows it as a stronghold of the Pashupatas, a sect devoted to Shiva, before the Arab conquest at the start of the eighth century, and Arab geographers called it Sadusan and Siwistan. There is no settled view of where its name comes from, though it is held to be a remnant of the older Shiv-vahan, the village of Shiva.

As a fort and a region, Sehwan and Siwistan were fought over more than once. Under the Sammas, Mirza Shah Beg, the governor of Qandahar, took the fort of Bhakkar, and afterwards took the fort of Sehwan as well before returning to Qandahar. In the Khankhanan's campaign against Thatta, the army first conquered the fort of Sehwan and then moved on. When Dara fled, he came near the fort of Siwistan, and had its *faujdar*, the commander of the fort, blocked his way, Dara would probably have been taken. In Aurangzeb's time Siwistan was one of the four *sarkars*, or districts, of the *suba*, the province, of Thatta, lying on the bank of the Indus.

The town had other religious foundations besides the shrine. Sultan Muhammad bin Tughluq appointed Makhdum-i Jahaniyan the Shaikhu'l-Islam, the chief religious dignitary, and made him the head of a *khanqah*, a Sufi lodge, in Siwistan, that is Sehwan, known as the Khanqah-i Muhammadi, to which several villages were attached. In the time of the Talpurs Sindh had six *madrasas*, and one of them was at Sehwan.

Before Partition, Hindus made up about half of the town's population, and the town had two main Hindu temples, one devoted to Shiva in its north eastern part and the other to Jhulelal on its north western side. From 1947 onwards almost all the Hindus migrated to India. All the Thakurs, the priestly caste of Jhulelal, who could have been the most powerful Hindu group in Sehwan, left Sindh to settle in India, especially in Bombay and Ulhasnagar, and in Bombay a Sehwani *panchayat*, a community council, has published several directories of the Sehwanis in India and overseas since Partition. In Sehwan, unlike other cities of northern Sindh such as Sukkur after the Manzilgah riots, no communal violence had ever been recorded, perhaps because of the spirit of toleration that prevailed there; yet the great majority of its Hindus left, though Mulchand Faqir chose to stay.

Sehwan is a very reputed centre of Sufi pilgrimage, with a strong Sufi ambiance. The poet Bedil went to Sehwan in his teens and is said to have been granted spiritual visions there, and Alan Faqir is said to have sung in his youth on the buses and trains between Rohri and Sehwan.

The town has grown again since. Prospects of economic growth and promises of development drew new settlers, especially from the less urban and poorer parts of the country, and they changed the town's urban and social character. The new migrants claimed the southern part of the town below the old highway, which until then had held mainly graveyards and old cemeteries.

## Legacy

Lal Shahbaz Qalandar embodies the ecstatic pole of South Asian Sufism, the one that crosses every boundary: the *qalandar* who dances his way to God and gathers all comers, of every faith and station, into the rhythm of divine love. Through the song that bears his spirit and the *dhamal* of his shrine, his name is known far beyond Sindh, and his darbar at Sehwan remains one of the great living centres of devotion in Pakistan, a place where sanctity is celebrated not in silence but in drums, colour and dance.

Saints are divisive figures in Pakistan, and none is more vexing than he. His memory spans religious divides, reaches far beyond the town of his burial and can be traced all over Pakistan, and the plural ideas of the social and the divine that he and Sehwan bear will always unsettle a Muslim national present that rests on its severance from its polytheistic Hindu past. He is at the forefront of a struggle for dominion over space and imagination: he is more than a figure who rejects the world and works miracles, and his portrayal is deeply bound up with the social and political life of his town.

His image travels. Posters of him are sold in the market of Uch Sharif, his picture hangs on the banner at Jalaludin's shrine, and at Uch he is named as one of three saints said to have meditated in the rooms of a mosque there. In Rawalpindi his portrait hangs among the religious posters in the *langar* of Mama Ji's darbar, and in Gujarat the *dhamal* of the Sidis lasts seven nights, in the manner of the *dhamal* at his shrine. The Muslim Nanghe and Udasi *sadhus*, holy ascetics, centre their devotion in Pakistan on his shrine, though they spend their lives travelling from one shrine to the next, following the *ʿurs* celebrations.

Sehwan is also a station on longer roads. It is the starting point of the pilgrimage to Lahoot la Makan, the abode of the saint Shah Noorani in the Khuzdar district of Balochistan, and in 2010 a Punjabi pilgrim who had just arrived at Lahoot la Makan from Sehwan confirmed that this side of the route was still in use. Raj Kumar, one of the walking pilgrims from Narayanpur, walked every year to the *ʿurs* of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar, for eleven years by his own account, often with members of the Christian and Hindu *panchayat* of his neighbourhood. They set out on foot from Liari, with their luggage, rations and water on a camel. The journey from Liari to Sehwan was a long and gruelling trek through the Pab mountains, a harder path than the route along the highway to Hinglaj, a Hindu temple, and in 2004 Raj Kumar and his fellow travellers changed not only their route but their destination. Raj Kumar recalls the words: "Man, forget Qalandar! Again we are climbing the mountains for Noorani." So, as he put it, the Qalandar was out, and he left for Hinglaj.

When the Hindus of Sindh left for India at Partition, they took the saint with them. For the Sindhi Hindus he was one of the three main Sufi traditions of Sindh, beside Daraza and Tando Jahaniyya in Hyderabad. A Hindu Sufi's Persian breviary names him among Bayazid Bistami, Junaid, Mansur Hallaj, Shams Tabrizi, Attar, Rumi and even Bu Ali Qalandar, and in the recorded sayings of Rochaldas he is the symbol of *ishq haqiqi*, the true love of God, as Majnun is of *ishq majazi*, earthly love. In India a daily *puja*, an act of Hindu worship, ends with the *jai*, the formula of praise, devoted to the gods, among them Guru Nanak, Jhulelal and Mast Qalandar, that is, Lal Shahbaz Qalandar, and in a hagiography of the Dalit community the *pir* of Pithoro names him with Puran Bhagat and Jhulelal.

His *ʿurs* crossed the border by a dream. Shortly before his death, Rochaldas dreamed that Lal Shahbaz Qalandar appeared to him: although the Hindu Sindhis had forgotten him, the saint said, he always watched over them in India, and he asked Rochaldas to celebrate his *ʿurs*, as at Sehwan. The first celebration seems to have taken place in 1959, two years after the death of Rochaldas, who had left orders for it. For a few years two distinct anniversaries were kept, Lal Shahbaz's *ʿurs* on the 18th, 19th and 20th of Shaʿban and Rochaldas's *versi*, until one year the two fell at the same time, and from then on both were celebrated together at the time of the *versi*.

In Rochaldas's darbar at Ulhasnagar an *ʿalam* was added after the dream: a small square courtyard enclosed by four walls, above which floats the red banner of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar. The darbar has no tower or dome, except perhaps the pole of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar, which is tall enough to be seen from far away, and inside it the passage from the first level to the second is made through a representation of the saint's darbar. There the *dhamal* was adapted into a simple beating of drums before the *samadhi*, the memorial shrine, playing the rhythm of the song *Mast Qalandar*: at Ulhasnagar it is reduced to the drumming, while at Sehwan the drums are played for the ecstatic dance of the devotees. The rite is meant to make Rochaldas's darbar an extension of an original sacred territory in Sindh, the holy city of Sehwan.

The Kanungos who had led the third day's *mehndi* at Sehwan settled in Ulhasnagar, where Rochaldas's son Hari asked them to lead the *mehndi* procession in the darbar. Gandaram, the eldest of the Kanungos, who died in 2008, inherited the charge on his father's death in 1948, but in the 1980s he was transferred to Bangalore, far from Ulhasnagar; his brother Murlidhar lived in Baroda, and the youngest, Lachhman, was not allowed to take it up, so from then on Hari's son Damodar led the *mehndi*. In those days the procession went as far as the river, where a *puja* was performed just as at the shrine, ending with Hari Om Jhulelal. For the Sindhi Hindus of India the *mehndi* of Lal Shahbaz keeps a direct link, through ritual, with the lost land of Sindh.

The Indian rites have since thinned. The *mehndi* procession was simplified once the *mehndibardar* left Ulhasnagar, and after the Ayodhya affair the great Sufi rites, especially the *dhamal*, the *ʿalam* and the *mehndi*, retreated or disappeared: the *mehndi* is now performed inside Rochaldas's darbar, whereas in the time of the Kanungos it went from the darbar to the river. In India, Mulchand's own tie with Sehwan and with Lal Shahbaz Qalandar is not even displayed.

In Sindh itself, at Tando Ahmad Khan, a Hindu *dargah* is a replica of the saint's darbar at Sehwan, though set in a different religious world. It holds a tomb of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar, surrounded by Hindu temples and Sufi *astanas*, saints' thresholds, with many inscriptions referring to an array of religious traditions, among them the Nanakpanth, the path of Guru Nanak's followers. There he is worshipped as Jhulelal: the figure of Lal Shahbaz dominates, since the shrine copies the one at Sehwan, but he bears the marks usually given to Jhulelal. Shrines to Shiva, to Raja Vir and to Shah Sadr stand at its entrance, and a visit there can be seen as a spiritual pilgrimage from Shiva to Lal Shahbaz, who is at last merged, through the place itself, into Jhulelal.

He has a literature of his own. Jethmal Parsram devoted the third part of a small book to him; the Institute of Sindhology at Jamshoro published Inam Muhammad's monograph *Hazrat Lal Shahbaz Qalandar of Sehwan*; and in 2011 Martin Weinhart, with the ethnologist Jürgen W. Frembgen, made the film *Der rote Sufi: Rausch und Ekstase in Pakistan* for Westdeutscher Rundfunk.
