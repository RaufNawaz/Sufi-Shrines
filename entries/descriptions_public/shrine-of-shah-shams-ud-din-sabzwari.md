## Overview

In Multan, the ancient "City of Saints", stands the shrine of Shah Shams-ud-Din Sabzwari, known as Shah Shams or Pir Shams, *pir* being the title of a Sufi spiritual guide. He was a saint of the thirteenth century, remembered as one of the most powerful and colourful holy figures of the region. Renowned for spectacular miracles, and associated in tradition with the spread of a form of the faith through the Ismaili *daʿwa*, the mission or call to the faith, Shah Shams gave Multan one of its oldest and most storied shrines, and his memory is bound up with the very sun and heat of the city.

## The Saint

Shah Shams is said to have come to the Indian subcontinent from Sabzwar, in the Persian lands, in the thirteenth century, as a preacher and missionary of extraordinary spiritual power. His life is more legend than chronicle, and it is famous above all for the miracle from which his name, *Shams*, "the sun", is popularly explained. It is told that, having no fire to cook a fish, the saint commanded the sun itself to come down and roast it, and that the sun drew so near that Multan has been scorched by an intense heat ever since. Such tales made him one of the great saints of the popular imagination, a worker of wonders.

He is connected with the Ismaili mission in Sindh and Multan, part of the complex religious currents of the medieval Indus valley, though today he is venerated broadly across communities. He died in Multan in the thirteenth century.

## The Shrine and Its Devotion

The shrine of Shah Shams Sabzwari is a domed tomb by the old bank of the river. It is among the oldest *darbars*, or shrines, of the city and a continuous centre of pilgrimage. Devotees come to seek the blessing of a saint famed for miraculous power, to recite the *Fatiha*, the opening chapter of the Qur'an, and to fulfil vows. The annual *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of the saint's death, draws crowds in the customary manner of Multan's shrines, with *qawwali*, the devotional music of the Sufis, and *langar*, the free communal kitchen. The shrine forms part of the dense sacred landscape that has made Multan the "City of Saints".

## The Shams of Multan

The shrine knows him as Shah Shams-ud-Din Sabzwari, of Sabzwar, and other names linked with a Shams of Multan have no settled connection with him. A saint called Shams was already honoured in Multan in the reign of the Mughal emperor Aurangzeb, when a Persian history composed in his reign counted a Shams al-Din, under the name Tabrizi rather than Sabzwari, among the saints buried in the city. The tomb of each of these saints, a place where divine lights are made manifest, was a place of pilgrimage for the people, and many friends of God, it was said, rested in that city in felicity. The name and the city are the only link between this Shams al-Din and the saint of this shrine.

Multan's history also remembers a tomb of a Shah Shams in the city. Nawab Shahnawaz Khan, killed in a disturbance in Multan, was buried beside it, and in the nineteenth century that tomb still stood.

The name Shams is linked with Multan in another way too. An Indian tradition holds that Shams-i Tabriz, the spiritual beloved of Maulana Rumi, was flayed alive by the mullahs in Multan, where his shrine is still venerated. It is not settled whether the Hazrat Shams Tabrizi honoured at a shrine in Multan is that renowned figure or another local Sufi of the same name, and as one scholar who was taken to that shrine observed, "Multiple shrines to the same person are not unusual." None of these traditions identifies its Shams with Shah Shams Sabzwari.

## The Setting

The history of Multan helps to place the currents with which tradition connects Shah Shams. In 711 the Arabs conquered Sindh and the provinces to the north of it up to Multan. The Carmathians, an Ismaili movement, ruled not only Bahrain but also northern Sindh and Multan, and tradition holds that the mystic Hallaj was in touch with them there. Multan became a dependency of the Fatimids of Egypt, and Sultan Mahmud dealt Ismaili power there a blow. By the middle of the eleventh century Sufism had reached the country around Multan, and in 1175 Muʿizzu'd-Din Muhammad bin Sam seized the city.

The heat that the legend of the sun explains belongs to the city's older history as well. Multan's winter is mild, its summer excessively hot, and its rain scarce. On Sultan Mahmud's second campaign, his soldiers could not bear the hot air of Multan in that season and begged to go back, so that the sultan turned back to Ghazni.

Tradition sets Shah Shams in the thirteenth century. During the twelfth century many Sufi scholars had made Multan their base, and conversion gained momentum in the early thirteenth century. The city was a strategic commercial centre in the Punjab, and by the time Guru Nanak, the founder of the Sikh faith, came to Multan, it was famous as a major centre of Sufi learning and philosophy. It was known for the shrines of Muslim saints and for its Jain and Hindu temples, a sacred space for people of all religious denominations.

Around the shrines of Multan and Sahiwal, performers sing to earn a living. Not everyone approves of the devotion of the region's shrines. One devotee of the Tableeghi Jamaat, a movement of Islamic revival, has complained that at several shrines in the Multan region and in Sindh people "light lamps, present offerings to the shrine and even bow down to the grave", which he condemns as an act of *shirk*, the sin of associating others with God.

A book of the saints of the Punjab carries a chronogram, a poem in which letters, read as numbers, give a year:

جو خدا کے دوست تھے پنجاب میں
حال ان کا اس سے ہوتا ہے عیان

*A verse of the chronogram written for the book by the author's son; its subject is the saints of the Punjab whom the book describes*

## Legacy

Shah Shams Sabzwari endures in memory as one of the great saints of medieval Multan who worked wonders, a figure of legend whose miracles, above all the calling down of the sun, have entered the folklore of the region, and whose shrine remains one of the oldest and most visited in the city. He belongs to the founding age of Sufism in the Indus valley, and his *darbar* is a fixed point in Multan's ancient devotion. In the reign of Aurangzeb a Shams was already counted among the saints whose tombs made Multan a place of pilgrimage, and who the Shams of Multan was remains an open question.
