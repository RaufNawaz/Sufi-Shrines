## Overview

At Daraza, near Ranipur in the Khairpur district of northern Sindh, lies the shrine of Sachal Sarmast (1739 to 1826), "the Truthful, the Intoxicated One", one of the boldest and most beloved of the Sufi poets of Sindh. Where Shah Abdul Latif is the serene ocean of Sindhi mysticism, Sachal is its ecstatic fire: a poet of dazzling daring who sang the unity of being in seven languages, and who was called, for the reckless intensity of his verse, the *Second Mansur*, after Mansur al-Hallaj, martyred for crying "I am the Truth". His shrine at Daraza is a major centre of Sindhi devotion and music, and his poetry is sung across Sindh and among Sindhi Hindus in India.

Daraza lies not far from Khairpur, and its *dargah*, the Sufi shrine complex, is counted among the remarkable sanctuaries of the country around Rohri, Sukkur and Shikarpur. With Lal Shahbaz Qalandar at Sehwan Sharif and Tando Jahaniyya in Hyderabad, Daraza is one of the three main Sufi traditions of Sindh. As a Sufi poet Sachal has been placed second only to Shah Abdul Latif, and in the 1970s his melodies and words were still almost as popular as they had been a century and a half before.

## The Saint

Sachal Sarmast was born in 1739 at Daraza into a Sayyid family of the Farooqi line; Sayyids are descendants of the Prophet Muhammad. His given name was Abdul Wahab, which is also given in a longer form as Khwaja Salah al-Din Ḥafiẓ Abd al-Wahhab. He became known as *Sachal*, "the truthful", and *Sarmast*, "the intoxicated" or "the ecstatic". *Sachal* is a diminutive of *sach*, truth, and seems to allude to the word *Haqq*, the Truth, in Hallaj's saying *anā'l-ḥaqq*, "I am the Truth"; *Sarmast* marks him as an intoxicated Sufi, or Muslim mystic.

His father died when he was young, and he was raised and initiated by his uncle and spiritual guide, Khwaja Abdul Haq. A versatile mystic who led a solitary life, he spent almost all his years in his native Daraza, rarely travelling, absorbed in devotion and song.

The best remembered story of his childhood is his meeting with the great Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai, and it is told as a legend. When the boy was about seven, it is said, Shah Abdul Latif came to pay homage to the local *murshid*, the spiritual guide, and on seeing Sachal exclaimed: "This God-gifted child will one day uncover those divine secrets which I have still kept concealed." The meeting is cherished as the passing of a torch from one master of Sindhi verse to the next. Sachal, Shah Abdul Latif's younger contemporary, took up his tradition and developed it.

He was also an admiring contemporary of the Punjabi poet Bulleh Shah of Kasur, and avowedly his follower, and he continued to send his disciples to Amritsar for homage. His own spiritual heir in verse was Bedil of Rohri.

Another legend tells of an attempt on his life: the swords, it is said, passed through his body as if it were water. He lived to a great age. Accounts of the year of his death differ; it is most often given as 1826.

## The Poetry

Sachal Sarmast poured out poetry in a remarkable range of tongues. Accounts of how many languages he wrote in differ, but he bears the title *Shair-e-Haft-Zaban*, the poet of seven languages: Sindhi and Seraiki above all, and also Urdu, Persian, Punjabi, Balochi and Arabic. He uttered whatever came into his heart with no thought for the consequences. His Persian and Urdu poetry is traditional and conventional in style, but in his mother tongue his feeling overpowers him. Like the poets before him, he relied on folk tales and made their heroines, Sohni among them, his own, though he did not elaborate these subjects as much as Shah Abdul Latif did.

His theme, sung with unrestrained ardour, was *wahdat al-wujud*, the unity of being, and the annihilation of the self in the divine reality. His verse breaks fearlessly through the forms of orthodoxy towards a naked declaration of union with the Beloved, and his ecstatic poetry surpassed all limits of expression. In Shah Abdul Latif's poetry, allusions and subtle images lead the understanding listener into the secrets of love, longing and union; in Sachal's poems the mysteries of the all embracing Unity are put openly before the listener.

It was this that won him the name of the *Second Mansur*, for like al-Hallaj he sang the identity of the lover and the Beloved without disguise. Shah Abdul Latif only covertly alludes to Hallaj's famous saying, *anā'l-ḥaqq*; Sachal, like Bulleh Shah, invoked it directly. His love for Hallaj was ardent, and Hallaj's name occurs hundreds of times in his verses. Hallaj's ideas lived on under the surface and broke out now and then in ecstatic utterance, in Sarmad, in Shah Abdul Latif and in Sachal, until Muhammad Iqbal brought them once more into a fascinating system.

Sachal has also been called the ʿAttar of Sindh, and the influence of ʿAttar's imagery is clearly visible in his poetry. He drew on the *hadith*, the sayings of the Prophet, inserting them into his poetry in various forms and with very outspoken preferences of his own, and in one of these sayings he found proof of his ecstatic experience of union with God and of God's all embracing Unity. He is among the heirs of the image of the Beloved who dwells within the heart. Why should one roam about the marketplaces to search for the friend, he asks: the friend is in the heart, and He whom heaven and earth cannot contain is the guest in the narrow hut of man's purified heart.

His verse often recalls the enthusiastic folk songs of the medieval Turkish mystics, such as Yunus Emre, who died in 1321; one could almost translate a Sindhi poem of his into Turkish and take it for an original verse by Yunus. With the Punjabi Bulleh Shah, he is the most typical voice of those poets who, in their highly ecstatic songs, identify themselves with everything created.

Krishna, the Hindu god, is important to the Sindhi Sufis, Muslim Sufis such as Sachal and Qutub Ali Shah among them, and Sachal, like Qutub Ali Shah and Bedil, brings Krishna into his Sufi poetry. He and Shah Abdul Latif have been called late participants in the Bhakti movement, the Hindu devotional movement that had a major impact in Sindh through the spread of Guru Nanak's teaching.

A line he repeats more than once sets the wine of Mansur against the *Kanz* and the *Quduri*, handbooks studied in the *madrasa*, the religious school:

*Intoxication came from the wine of this Mansur-dom, Kanz and Quduri became annihilated!*

Elsewhere he says the same of those who have tasted love:

*Those who have tasted a sip from the syrup of the ocean of love* … *They say goodbye to syntax, and don't look any more at grammar!*

His spiritual heir, Bedil of Rohri, took up the same theme:

*The ordinary men read Kanz Quduri, the lovers wisdom from God* (translated by Annemarie Schimmel)

Where Shah Abdul Latif is more careful and less outspoken in his allusions, Sachal loved to name, one by one, those killed by their overwhelming divine love. A Sindhi poem of his begins:

*Welcome, welcome You are-- / to which place will You bring me? / You will again cut off a head! / Giving a kick to Sarmad You have killed him,* (translated by Annemarie Schimmel)

The poem goes on that the Beloved brought Mansur to the gallows and cut off the head of Shaikh ʿAttar, and it closes: "Now You are asking the way here!" To these generally known Sufis, Sachal adds the names of martyred Indian religious heroes, such as Sarmad, the friend of Dara Shikoh, and he speaks too of Shah ʿInayat of Jhok. His litany of afflicted lovers counts among the slain Bulleh Shah, Jaʿfar, Sarmad, Mansur, Shaikh ʿAttar, Zakariya, Joseph, Shams, Sanʿan, Karmal and Bilawal, and Shah ʿInayat, the martyred mystic of Jhok. He also left a riddle on the self that opens "Perhaps I am".

He wrote a political poem as well. Its speaker has seen a boat in midstream whose expert oarsmen consider themselves the rulers of the ocean and think nothing of Sindh and India:

*They are a proud people and consider themselves rulers but they are nothing but plunderers. Whenever they get the chance they take the goods of others by force. Yet the local people put a lot of trust in them...*

His poems are sung by the *faqirs*, the mystics, of his shrine and by folk singers across Sindh, and their ecstatic boldness has made him a patron of all who prize love over law and truth over caution.

## The Shrine

Sachal Sarmast's shrine at Daraza is ornamented in the glazed blue tilework of Sindh. It is a busy centre of pilgrimage and, above all, of music: the singing of his poetry, often to the beat that stirs the *dhamal*, the ecstatic devotional dance, is central to its devotion, and the shrine keeps his memory as the ecstatic voice of Sindhi Sufism. On the main gate of the *dargah* is written the inscription *haqq mawjud*, "the Truth is present".

On a wall at Daraza hangs the "official" picture of Sachal. It is said to have been drawn by a Sikh follower of his. It shows him in majesty, in a pose that could recall the iconography of Guru Nanak, seated on a *gaddi*, the cushioned seat of a spiritual master, with a club in his right hand and a musical instrument in his left.

Daraza also holds the remains of Hindu Sufis. Nimano Faqir, a Hindu Sufi, was cremated, but his ashes were laid in a tomb in the *dargah* compound, near the tomb of Sachal Sarmast. A woman who died in 1963 was cremated, and her ashes were carried to Daraza, where they were buried in a grave close to Sachal Sarmast.

Daraza has its own line of masters, its *sajjada nashins*, the successors who sit on the saint's prayer carpet. For generations they have borne the honorific title *Sakhi*, the generous; the name is given to the Sufi masters of Daraza in general, but it designates Sachal Sarmast above all. Pir Khwaja Abd al-Haqq II (1915 to 1978) was the new *sajjada nashin* of Daraza when, in 1956, he made a visit to consecrate the spiritual authority of Nimano Faqir. Nimano Faqir's own master was a Sakhi Qabul Muhammad of Daraza, a *murshid* of Sachal's *dargah*, nicknamed Sakhi Darazi. In the early twenty first century the *sajjada nashin* of Daraza was Sakhi Qabul Muhammad Faruqi.

## Devotion and Festivals

The annual *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of the saint's death, draws great crowds from across Sindh for days of poetry, music and celebration in the exuberant Sindhi manner, alongside the rites of prayer and remembrance. The *ʿurs* falls on the 14th of Ramazan, and since it follows the Islamic calendar, its date changes every year in the Christian calendar and in the Hindu calendar, the Vikram Samvat. In the years before the late 1960s the Sindhi publications of the state regularly featured Sachal Sarmast, with Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai and Shah Inayat, and marked their annual fairs with special editions.

The singer Abida Parvin sings his verse. She is able to sing the poetry of Shah Abdul Latif and Sachal Sarmast in Sindhi, which her male counterparts among the *qawwals*, the singers of devotional Sufi music, cannot.

His following reached beyond Muslims. Daraza and Jhok were the two main Sufi traditions that were very popular among the Hindus, and Sachal had a large number of followers among the Hindu trade castes of northern Sindh. When they migrated in the wake of Partition, some of them carried their love for singing his poetry to India.

## Daraza Across the Border

In India those followers kept the name of Daraza. The Daraza school of Sufism still attracts many Hindu Sindhis there, and in Mumbai and Ulhasnagar they have organised Darazi *satsangs*, gatherings for devotional singing, to sing Sachal Sarmast's *kalam*, his poetry. Sachal is the model case of a Sindhi Sufi carried by a Hindu path without any formal tie to it, and his poetry is sung in all the Sufi *darbars*, the shrines, of India. Other Sufi poets too have claimed to be Darazi, among them a Hindu *faqir* who took the name into his own, Faqir Radhakrishin Lal Sahib Darazi, whose *Kalam* was printed at Ulhasnagar in 1986.

The spiritual masters of the Hindu Sufi lineages of India are still in Sindh. They are the *sajjada nashins* of Daraza, of Tando Jahaniya and of Jhok Sharif, and to each a great Sufi poet is attached: Sachal Sarmast, Qutub Ali Shah and Nasir Faqir.

Two *darbars* devoted to Sachal Sarmast stand in the same area of Ulhasnagar, Camp 5. At one of them, the Maqbul Manzil, only a weekly rite is held: every Thursday, from seven until nine in the evening, a Darazi *satsang* meets under the supervision of Soni Kataria. It opens with an *arti*, the Hindu rite of worship with lamps, which nothing distinguishes from the *arti* of any Hindu temple. Then the devotees sing Sachal's *kalam*, one playing the harmonium and another the *tablas*, the hand drums, with Soni as the leading singer; she knows the verses by heart. About twenty people attend. The *darbar* has passed down a family: Hari Ram took charge of it and of singing Sachal's poetry, and after him his daughter Soni led the *satsang* every Thursday evening.

The tie to Daraza is kept by its master in Sindh. The *sajjada nashin* of Daraza has paid three visits to the *darbar*, and it is he who gives the date of Sachal's *versi*, the yearly death anniversary: Soni calls him, or he calls her, by telephone, so that he can tell her its timing. In India Sachal's *versi* is celebrated on the 14th of Ramazan and Hari Ram's at the beginning of November, while at the Sachal Sain *darbar* the two *versis* celebrated are Sachal's and Pratap's.

In the Rohani Rah, whose name means the spiritual path, the core of worship is a *murti*, a statue, of Sachal Sarmast, carved after the official picture that hangs at Daraza; the only difference is that in the statue Sachal has a golden *tilak*, the sacred mark, on his forehead. Above the *murti* is Krishna as a flute player, without Radha, and higher still the *darbar* of Sachal Sarmast at Daraza. At the Radha Krishin Lal *darbar* it is the other way: at the core of the altar is a photograph of a master of Daraza, Sakhi Qabul Muhammad, instead of a representation of Sachal, though the whole set of pictures is dominated by an inscription in the Latin alphabet, "Sai Sachal". Asked why it was Sakhi and not Sachal, Soni answered that Sakhi is the one who looks most like Sachal.

His verse is the core of these gatherings' song. Their repertoire consists mainly of works by Sachal and Nimano, and *kalams* by Sindhi Sufis such as Sachal are still sung every day at Nimano Faqir's shrine. At the *versi* of Dadi Dhan in 2012, Sachal Sarmast was the most requested poet, followed by Rochaldas; the *versi* of Daulatram was kept with the chanting of Sufi songs by Sachal Sarmast, Shah Abdul Latif and Qutub Ali Shah; and Mira Rajani used to sing Sachal's poetry during the *satsang*. Hindu teachers drew on him as well: Mulchand quotes Sachal's poetry in his explanation of *om*, the sacred syllable, and Rochaldas quotes verses from Kabir, Bedil, Sachal and Ajiz.

In some places his presence has waned. Sachal stood at the origin of the Sakhi Kuthiyya, founded by Nimano Faqir in Baroda, later Vadodara. Its name means something like the abode of the generous, and it referred to the original *dargah* in Sindh and to its spiritual head. Yet in Vadodara Sachal has declined, and Nimano now stands at the forefront. In the Nimanal Sangam the reference to Sachal has even been suppressed, and the 2011 Vadodara edition of Nimano Faqir's verse leaves out Sachal's poetry, which had a large part in Nimano's original *kalam*, while keeping Guru Nanak's. Even so, Nimano Faqir's verses still embodied the very spirit of Sindhi Sufism, and especially that of the Darazi *silsila*, the Sufi lineage of Daraza.

## The Setting

Daraza lies in the Khairpur country of northern Sindh. After 1857 many Kayasht, a Hindu community of scribes, migrated to work in the princely states, among them the Khairpur emirate. The amirs of Khairpur were themselves staunch Shia Muslims, and in the nineteenth century they built beautiful *imambaras*, halls for the Shia rites of mourning, but that did not prevent them from being generous patrons of all religious buildings.

Seraiki, the northern dialect of Sindhi, is mostly spoken in northern Sindh, and especially among its Baluchi population. Many Sindhi Sufi poets, especially from northern Sindh, composed Sufi poetry in several languages, and many of the later ones, mainly those of Baloch origin, also used Seraiki. The Sufis to whom the Hindus of Sindh look mostly belong to Upper Sindh, where Seraiki is used for Sufi poetry besides Sindhi. In Sindh, Hallaj, the figure at the centre of Sachal's verse, is very popular, and many Sufi poets mention his name in their verses.

## Legacy

With Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai, Sachal Sarmast is one of the two great poet saints of Sindh, the intoxicated counterpart to Bhittai's sublime serenity, and among the most fearless voices of *wahdat al-wujud* in all of South Asian Sufism. His multilingual verse, his ecstatic sincerity and his refusal of every veil between the soul and God have kept him beloved for two centuries, and his shrine at Daraza remains a living hearth of the Sindhi Sufi song he raised to such heights. As one scholar of Islamic mysticism put it, "none of the later mystical poets can match Sachal's intense glow; he is a vigorous poet, and the rhythms and energy, the intense yearning and love, give his verses an almost magical quality."

He has been named, with Shah Inayat and Shah Abdul Latif, among the three great Sufi teachers, and counted the second of the trinity of the Sindhi poets. In anthologies of Sindhi Sufi verse he stands at or near the head. One opens with Sachal Sarmast, with forty six extracts from his Sindhi poetry; in Vaswani's anthology of 1955 the Muslim Sufis begin with Shah Abdul Latif, followed by Rohal, Murad, Sachal and Bedil; and another book focuses on Shah Latif and Sachal.

A body of scholarship has grown around him. Jethmal Parsram wrote a book on him in 1922. A work titled *Sachal Sarmast*, under the name of an Agha Sufi, dates from 1933, and by 1940 a large edition of his poetry had appeared. One woman edited his Sindhi *kalam* in 1955, and was herself the author of a Sindhi *kalam* with the title *Haqq mawjud*, the same words that are written on the shrine's main gate. Abd al-Karim Sindilo published a glossary of his poetry, *Lughat-e Sachal*, at Khairpur in 1984, from the Sachal Chair of Shah Abdul Latif University; Ram Panjwani published a book on his poetry, *Sachal jyun kafiyun*; and in 2002 the Culture and Tourism Department of the Government of Sindh published a *Study of Mysticism in Darazi School of Sufi Thought* by Dr. Sakhi Qabool Muhammad Faruqi.

His verse has been printed in Sindhi, Seraiki and Persian. The Sindhi Adabi Board published a Sindhi *Risalo*, a collection of his poems, at Karachi in 1958, edited by Othman Ali Ansari, and a Seraiki *Kalam* edited by Maulwi Hakim Muhammad Sadiq Ranipuri at Karachi in 1959; a Persian *Diwan-i Ashikar*, edited by Makhdum Amir Ahmad, appeared at Lahore in 1957. Later editions include *Sachal jo risalo. Siraiki kalam*, edited by Mowlana Muhammad Sadiq Ranipuri and published at Kandiaro in 1997, and *Sachal Sarmast Jo Kalam*, from the Sindhi Akademy at Delhi in the same year.
