## Overview

The Shrine of Shah Abdul Karim stands at Bulri, in Tando Muhammad Khan district in southern Sindh, a town that bears his name as Bulri Shah Karim. It honours one of the earliest and most influential of the Sindhi Sufi poets, a mystic of simple, laborious life and a pioneer of devotional verse in Sindhi. He is remembered, too, as the forebear of Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai, the poet of Bhit, who is said to have overseen the building of the shrine about 1741. The *dargah*, the shrine complex of a Sufi saint, remains a vibrant place of devotion, and its annual *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of the saint's death, draws thousands of pilgrims.

Shah Abdul Karim was a noted spiritual teacher in his own right, and among the first to bring the folk romances of Sindh into mystical verse. His short verses in Sindhi were gathered by a disciple soon after his death; Shah Abdul Latif kept them always at hand and inserted some of their lines into his own poetry, and several of the later poet's images are already found in them.

## The Saint

Shah Abdul Karim, often called simply Shah Karim and also Sayid Abdul Karim, was born in 1536 into a Sayyid family of Matiari, a family descended from the Prophet Muhammad. He spent most of his life at Bulri, the place from which he takes his epithet. Before he settled and preached there, he was himself a *murid*, a disciple pledged to a Sufi master, of Makhdoom Nooh of Hala. Beyond that discipleship, no Sufi order and no chain of spiritual descent are remembered for him. He is remembered as a mystic of simple, laborious life and as a noted spiritual teacher in his own right. He died in 1623.

In the generations just before the birth of Shah Abdul Latif, the family went back to the neighbourhood of Matiari; how it had come to Bulri in the first place, and why it returned, is not known.

Above all, he is the forebear of Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai. Accounts differ over how many generations separated the two, but by tradition Shah Abdul Latif was his descendant in the fourth generation. Shah Abdul Latif's father, Shah Habib, was himself a descendant of Shah Abdul Karim, one of the first noted poets in the Sindhi language.

One story takes him far from Bulri. An anecdote relates that he was taught 'the one crooked word', OM, the sacred syllable of the Hindus, by an ascetic in Ahmadabad. The anecdote is told in connection with a Hindu reading of the symbolism of the Arabic letters *alif* and *mim* in Shah Abdul Latif's verse, a reading that has been set aside as too far fetched, with no parallels in his descendant's poetry.

## The Shrine

Tradition holds that Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai himself stayed two years at Bulri and supervised the building of his ancestor's shrine, laying the foundation of the tomb and of its mosque of three domes about 1741, the year 1154 of the Islamic calendar.

By 1940 he was spoken of as the famous Sayid Abdul Karim, his name was still held in reverence in Lower Sind, the south of the province, and his *dargah* at Bulri, then counted in the Hyderabad district, was still a great place of yearly pilgrimage. The gathering had its own Sindhi name: the Shah Karim fair, or *mero*, held on the day of the saint's *ʿurs*. It remains so today: the *dargah* is a vibrant place of devotion, and thousands of pilgrims come to its annual *ʿurs*.

Elsewhere in Tando Muhammad Khan district, at Tando Saindad near Tando Muhammad Khan, the *gaddi nashin*, or holder of the seat of spiritual authority, was Mohsin Shah, a Sufi affiliated to the Qalandariyya, the order of the Qalandars, wandering dervishes.

## The Poet

Shah Abdul Karim stands at the head of a Sindhi tradition. It seems that in Sindh, as in other parts of the Muslim world, the Sufis were responsible for the development of the spoken language into a literary one, and he composed some of the pioneering Sufi poems in Sindhi.

His sayings and verses were first gathered by a disciple, Mir Daryai Tharawi, in a lengthy Persian memoir, the *Bayan al-Arifin*, completed in 1630, seven years after his death. This book of his *malfuzat*, the recorded sayings of a Sufi master, preserves 93 verses in Sindhi, and Shah Abdul Latif is known to have treasured them. A printed edition, under a title that names Bulri, was edited by Dr Omar Muhammad Daudpota and published in Bombay by the Qayyimah Press in 1937, the year 1356 of the Islamic calendar. Judgements of the merit of the verse that survives differ, but it is most often counted pioneering, and it has been said to contain all the ingredients of later mystical poetry.

He was among the first to weave the folk romances of the land, *Sasui and Punhun*, *Sohni and Mehar*, *Umar and Marui* and *Leela and Chanesar*, into poetry expressing the soul's longing for the Divine, a method his descendant Shah Abdul Latif later brought to its summit. Early allusions to such tales appear, veiled, in the verse of Qadi Qadan; they become more obvious in Shah Abdul Karim's short verses and reach full bloom in the *Risalo*, the collected poetry of Shah Abdul Latif. His verses allude to Sasui, and one of them names Bhambhore:

*Nobody ever took with himself / two things at once from Bhambhore, / Yearning for the beloved, / and attachment with one's world*

Another of his verses is a picture of village life:

*Like a jar poised on woman-water-carrier, / and a bird on the water, / Our beloved in the same way / has been close to our soul*

Another, on the swan that dives deep for pearls while others play in the shallows, uses an image his descendant also took up:

*A swan feeds on pearls, / he dives deep down, / He who plays in the shallows*

The image of the swan must have been popular in Sindh for a long time, for Shah Abdul Karim used it more than a century before Shah Abdul Latif. A line of his also survives inside a verse of his descendant's: the second line of one of Shah Abdul Latif's verses is a quotation from him, and his own verse continues:

*The beloved is not separated from you, turn only your face inside*

Several of the motifs of Shah Abdul Latif's poetry begin with him. The phrase *tuhnjo Allah hekro*, 'Your God is One', appears first in his verses and then in Shah Abdul Latif's. The figure of the *jogi*, the Hindu ascetic, can be found in his poetry, though it is fully expressed only in his descendant's verses, and the motif of the thief is already present in his poems. It was probably he who invented a type of verse that mocks the curriculum of the *madrasa*, the school of religious learning, a type that became very popular in later times; among the later poets who carried it on were Sachal Sarmast and Bedil of Rohri. His poems, and those of Shah Inat, are closely related to Shah Abdul Latif's poetry.

## Legacy

His verse outlived him in his descendant's hands. Shah Abdul Latif kept three books constantly by him: the Qur'an, Jalaluddin Rumi's long mystical poem the *Masnavi*, and the Sindhi verses of Shah Karim. He also inserted some of his ancestor's lines into his own poetry.

Shah Abdul Karim is one of the grounds for rejecting the picture of Shah Abdul Latif as an unlettered genius. With Shah Inat Rizvi, who died in 1711, he was one of Shah Abdul Latif's forerunners, and they show that there was already an established culture of vernacular Sufi poetry in Sindh, although it tends to be overshadowed by Shah Abdul Latif's unique reputation in the literary histories.

He has also been the subject of modern study. A monograph by Motilal Jotwani, *Shah Abdul Karim*, was published in New Delhi by Kumar Brothers in 1970; a study of him as a mystic poet of Sindh was published in Karachi by Sindi Kitab Ghar and Indus Publications in 1986; and a work by Jotwani of 1996 includes a complete translation of his Sindhi verses.

At Bulri, the town that bears his name, the shrine that tradition says his descendant raised still draws pilgrims to his yearly *ʿurs*, while in the poetry of Shah Abdul Latif his images of the swan, the *jogi* and the thief, and some of his own lines, live on.
