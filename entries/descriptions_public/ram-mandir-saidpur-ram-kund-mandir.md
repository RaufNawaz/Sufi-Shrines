## Overview

The Ram Mandir, also known as the Ram Kund Mandir, stands in the historic village of Saidpur, set in a fold of the Margalla Hills on the northern edge of Islamabad. It is a Hindu temple dedicated to Lord Rama, traditionally dated to the sixteenth century and attributed by some to Raja Man Singh I, the Rajput general of the Mughal emperor Akbar. Its sanctity is bound to the belief that Rama passed through this country during his exile. No longer a place of regular worship, the building is now a protected monument and a popular destination for visitors to Islamabad.

Rama himself, the divine hero of the *Ramayana*, the epic of his life, is honoured far beyond Saidpur: in the festivals of the Hindu year, on the pilgrimage to the goddess Hinglaj, and in the verse of Sufi poets, the mystics of Islam, who took up Ram as a name of God.

## The Temple

Tradition holds that Rama, with Sita and Lakshmana, passed through this country during the years of exile told in the *Ramayana*. The natural springs of the village, fed by the hill streams, were revered as sacred pools, and four of them are traditionally named for Rama, Sita, Lakshmana and Hanuman. Local memory also ties the site to the adjoining Ram Kund pond, where an annual fair once drew Hindus from across the district.

Before Partition, Saidpur was a place of notable interfaith coexistence, home to Muslims, Hindus and Sikhs together. Its skyline held a mosque, this Hindu temple and, from the early twentieth century, a Sikh *gurdwara*, a place of worship of the Sikh faith.

After 1947 the temple ceased to function as a place of worship, and for a time it was used as a girls' school. In the 2000s the Capital Development Authority restored Saidpur as a heritage and arts village, conserving the temple's fabric while its idols were removed and its images painted over. In 2006 it had the temple, by then vacated, formally cleared and preserved as a tourist attraction. Since then, visitors have documented restaurants operating within the complex and serving meat dishes, a practice Hindu commentators have criticised as an affront to the site's sanctity.

The building survives as a protected monument and a popular destination for visitors to Islamabad. The local Hindu community, however, has not been permitted to resume regular rituals there, a matter on which Hindu representatives continue to appeal. In 2020 regular Hindu worship there was still barred.

## Rama and the Ramayana

Rama, to whom the temple is dedicated, is revered by Hindus as the seventh incarnation of the god Vishnu and as the divine hero of the *Ramayana*, who killed the demon king Ravana. Ram is also a Hindu name of God. Vishnu has been adored in the form of two of his ten incarnations, Rama and Krishna, and devotion has been paid to Rama and to his consort Sita.

In the epic, Sita, the wife of Rama, was the daughter of Raja Janak, and she is held to be the perfect woman. Ravana, the demon king of Lanka with ten heads, abducted her, and her rescue is the main theme of the epic. Rama's brother Lakshmana, also called Lachhman, shared his exile, and Lanka came under attack by Rama and Lakshmana. Hanuman, a follower of Rama, played a central role in the battle between Rama and Ravana. Rama banished Sita, sending her into *banwas*, exile from the land, and at the *ashram*, the hermitage, of Valmiki she bore his twin sons, Luv and Kush.

In the nineteenth century Rama's story was also told as the history of a royal line. As Raja Ramchandar he was the son of Dasrath, whose sons also included Bharat and Lachhman, Ramchandar's own full brother. Ramchandar was the eye and lamp of the Suraj Bansi, the solar line of kings, and in the belief of the Hindus he was an *avatar*, an incarnation of the divine, in the Treta age, one of the four ages of the world in Hindu reckoning. His tale ran through the *ban bas*, the exile in the forest, the story of Ravan and the bridge from the Deccan, and after him the rule remained among his descendants for a full five hundred years. By the reckoning of English historians, the first of his line lived two thousand or two thousand two hundred years before the Christian era, and Ramchandar twelve hundred years before it.

In the reign of the Mughal emperor Aurangzeb, a Persian translation of the *Ramayana* from the Sanskrit, containing the life of Sri Ram Chand, was counted among the famous books. In the same period the founding of Lahore was attributed to Lav, the son of a Raja Ram Chand.

Images of Rama are found elsewhere too. At a temple at Manora, tiles of Ram and Sita have had their faces wiped clean, and depictions of Ram and Sita are among the faded frescoes of the *samadhi*, the memorial, of Ranjit Singh. The demolition of the Babri Mosque was justified by the claim that the mosque had been built on the site of a Hindu temple, the birth site of Ram.

## Festivals of Rama

Rama's victory over Ravana is celebrated every year at the Hindu festival of Dussehra, when Ravana's effigy is ceremonially burnt. In one telling of Navratri, the festival of nine nights, Sita prayed for nine nights while Ravana and Rama fought. On the tenth day the goddess heard her plea, and Rama was able to kill Ravana and win the war; the burning at Dussehra, on the tenth day, is a remembrance of that victory.

It is said, too, that when Rama set out with his army for Lanka, he worshipped a little tree called the *cheonkul*, which stood near his capital of Ayodhya. At the festival of Dussehra a procession of more than seventy thousand people was once seen attending their prince to worship the tree.

Ram Navami celebrates the birth of Ram, a popular deity. Streets are lit, the entire community takes part, and Hindus have been celebrating it for centuries. It has been argued that the Muslim festival of Eid Milad-Un-Nabi, the birthday of the Prophet, was originally inspired by it.

## Rama on the Road to Hinglaj

No figure was ever as present in the representations of the traditional pilgrimage to the goddess Hinglaj as Rama. Its legend tells of Rama's *brahm hatyā*, the sin of killing a Brahmin, and of the pilgrimage he was sentenced to make. At the pass of Ungakhera Bherum Luk he was defeated by the goddess's army, and he fought a battle of twelve years. A condition was set that he come as a simple pilgrim from Ram Bagh; the goddess made a promise to those of his men who were excluded; Hanuman, Lakshmana and Sita worked miracles of water; and Rama carved a sun and a moon on the mountain opposite the temple of the goddess Hinglaj.

Rama's ninety six Brahmins, it is said, accepted Hinglaj as their clan goddess. According to legend, the Saraswat Brahmins had their *ṭīkā*, the mark on the forehead, from Rama: in need of a priest, and unable to find one in Balochistan, he simply applied a *ṭīkā* to the forehead of a *mlecch*, a barbarian. Lalu Jasraj is remembered as the *aguā*, the guide who led the way, of Rama's party.

Along the route he has his places of *pūjā*, or worship: the resting place of Rama, *rām jharoku* in Sindhi, and Khari Nadi, where people bathe in the ocean and worship Rama in the Rama *pūjā*. Of the journey of Rama and his entourage, part myth and part history, much has now been destroyed by the growth of Karachi.

## Rama in Sufi Poetry and Thought

Rama's name and story also run through Sufi poetry, where Ram can stand as a name of God. The Sufi poet Bulleh Shah appeals to him:

*When I realized this, I appealed to Ram: you and I are one, there is no one else in this house in the city of love* (translated by Christopher Shackle)

Elsewhere the lyrics speak of the *tilak*, the mark Hindus wear on the forehead:

*Sometimes you utter appeals to Ram, sometimes you put the tilak on your forehead.* (translated by Christopher Shackle)

The epic enters his lyrics too. One line says to its addressee, 'you had Lanka plundered by Ram', and a passage recalls Kumbhakaran, Ravan and Lachhman:

*When Kumbhakaran goes back the way he came, the mystery of Lanka is created. Now that Ravan the ten-headed is destroyed and Lachhman remains, the unstruck music sounds.* (translated by Christopher Shackle)

One such reference in the lyrics describes a reversal of the arrival of Kumbhakaran to assist his brother Ravan, when Ravan's capital of Lanka was under attack by Ram and Lachhman. In a poem of two lines headed NAMES, Bulleh Shah also sets the name Ram beside the name Allah, in an argument about the names given to God.

Waris Shah's romance of Hir and Ranjha invokes the epic in a single question:

*Did not a woman kill Ravana?* (translated by Charles Frederick Usborne)

Later in the poem the epic returns as an analogy in a court of law, "This swindler took her away as Ravan ran away with Sita", a reference meant, it is explained, to curry favour with the Raja, who is a Hindu.

The Sufi poet Madho Lal Hussein uses Ram, with Sain, Rab and Maula, in place of Allah, and he uses it often. In his *kafis*, short mystical lyrics, it is the name to be recited and sung:

*Time to invoke, girl, let's rise and praise Ram* (translated by Naveed Alam)

*What's the joy of life / without the constant evocation of Ram?* (translated by Naveed Alam)

Other lines run "Praise Ram, I pranced all the way to the groom", "Sometimes rise and recite Ram in the morning", "You lose if you don't sing of Ram" and "Come, girl, let's praise Ram". One *kafi* addresses him directly, *The whole world begs your favours, O Ram*, and so does its Variation: *You are the great giver, O Rama*.

Mai Dhadli's Urdu song, sung at a dwelling of *fakirs*, wandering Sufis, in Sehwan, runs:

*Muhammad in one place, Ali in another, and yet elsewhere, Qalandar is Ram.* (translated by Omar Kasmani)

The memory of the saint there is said to span "Muhammad, Ali, or Ram".

Rama has his place in Sufi thought and Hindu devotion at large. The story of Rama and Sita has been read as one in which Ravana and Hanuman figure as two poles of mystical knowledge. Pitambar Pir has been identified with the Hindu god Rama, and Kabir's 'Nirguna Rama', Rama without attributes, has been set against Ram Chandar. Some held that Ram and Rahim, the Merciful, a Muslim name of God, were the same Being, and Rama was among the traditional names by which Hindus and Muslims invoked God, with Allah, Khuda, Sahib and Hari. In the usage of Guru Nanak, the founder of the Sikh faith, Hari and Rama are names for a single formless God, not the deities of cult sites.

Among the Sufis, Sachal mentions Hindu gods such as Ram, Hanuman, Sita and especially Krishna, and he also refers to an episode of the *Ramayana*, Ram's return to Ayodhya. Qutub Ali Shah names Lakshmana and Sita, Ram and Krishna, and the *jogis*, the ascetics, are said to be fascinated by Rama. In certain lectures the Sufi *shaykh*, or master, Zauqi Shah described Krishna, Rama, Sita, Lakshmana and Guru Nanak as believers in the one true God and religious leaders with a Divine connection, or *nisbat*.

## The Setting

Saidpur lies on the northern edge of Islamabad, Pakistan's capital city. Rawalpindi is the twin city of Islamabad, and on Islamabad's outskirts stand the village of Nurpur Shahan and the shrine of Barri Imam.

In the heart of the Margalla Hills, where the federal capital lies, stands the Dharmarajika of Taxila: a *stupa*, a Buddhist relic mound, around which are the remains of a monastery and giant sculptures of Lord Buddha and Bodhisattva, the Buddha to be, dating back to the third century BCE. It is believed to have been built by the great Mauryan emperor Ashoka. A bodhi tree, the tree sacred to Buddhists, is said to grow in Islamabad, and another near Jhelum.
