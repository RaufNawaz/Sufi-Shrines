## Overview

At Ranmal Sharif, near Phalia in the Mandi Bahauddin region of the Punjab, stands the shrine of Hazrat Syed Muhammad Noushah Ganj Bakhsh (1552 to 1654), the founder of the Naushahia branch of the Qadiri Sufi order. A saint of long life and wide influence, he established a spiritual line that spread across the Punjab and beyond and endures to this day. His *darbar*, or shrine, is the mother shrine of that order and remains an important centre of pilgrimage; its yearly *ʿurs*, the commemoration of the saint's death, draws great crowds.

Noushah Ganj Bakhsh is remembered both as a saint of powerful blessing and as the organiser of an enduring order. He lived through a full century of the Mughal age, and in the 1870s the *faqirs*, or Sufi mystics, of his path in the Punjab numbered in the thousands.

## The Saint

Muhammad Noushah was born in 1552. He is honoured as *Ganj Bakhsh*, 'the bestower of treasure', and as *Noushah*, 'the bridegroom', and he is also known as Haji Muhammad. He is remembered as a *wali*, a friend of God, from his birth. It is said that states of *jazb* and *sukr*, the rapture of a soul drawn towards God and spiritual intoxication, of love and longing, dominated him, and that he had no equal in asceticism, in piety and in *karamat*, the miraculous deeds of a saint.

His father, Haji Ala al-Din, was himself a great elder, who performed the Hajj, the pilgrimage to Mecca, seven times and, it is said, was honoured with a vision of the Prophet. His mother, Bibi Jiwani, was also a great worshipper and ascetic. He had a brother, Shaikh Ismail. A story is told, too, of the marriage of his daughter, at which the bride's side demanded a hundred rupees of *lagan*, customary wedding dues, after the custom of the Muslims of the Punjab.

By the age of seventeen he had completed the outward sciences. Then he renounced the world and gave himself to *zikr*, the remembrance of God, in a reed bed of the Sandal Bar or the Gondal Bar, for accounts differ over which. His parents would not leave him there. When he came away, Mulla Karim al-Din brought him to Shah Sulaiman Qadri, at whose hands he took *bai'at*, the oath by which a disciple pledges himself to a *pir*, a spiritual guide, and within a few years he had reached completion on the path. He became Shah Sulaiman's *khalifa*, his spiritual deputy.

He took the Sufi path in the Qadiri order, the order founded by Abd al-Qadir Gilani, and, attaining a high spiritual station, founded his own branch of it, the *Naushahia*, also called the Naushahia Qadiria, which he spread with great energy across the Punjab. Over his long life he guided a vast body of disciples and built a network of successors.

Accounts of the year of his death differ, as do accounts of where he lies. The year his shrine keeps is 1654, when he died full of years, and he rests at Ranmal Sharif, which became the seat of his line. A Persian chronogram, a verse whose letters, counted as numbers, give a year, marks his passing; its last line carries the phrase *pir-e fayyaz*, 'the bountiful pir'.

آنکہ شد مشہور در اہل جہاں اجلال او
کرد چوں آخر عروج از فرش بر عرش بریں
پیر فیاض است ہم اہل سخاوت سال او

## His Disciples

Two of his *khalifas* were renowned: Abdul Rahman, called Pak Rahman, and Pir Muhammad Sachyar. Pak Rahman was his great *khalifa*, and it is said that his master showed him a favour shown to no one else. In the 1870s Pak Rahman's own radiant tomb was a place of pilgrimage.

One chain of succession runs from Noushah Ganj Bakhsh to Pir Muhammad Sachyar, from him to Shah Farid Lahori, and on through Muhammad Siddiq and Rahman Shah to Fazl Shah. In the 1870s most of the disciples of this line were in the city of Lahore, and the line received material support from the Sikh state through Raja Dina Nath. The spiritual pedigree of Shah Kamaita, too, leads back to Noushah Ganj Bakhsh.

Four of his disciples were *majzubs*, men absorbed in divine love, and each of them bore the name Naushahi: Shaikh Mittha, Sayyid Shah Abdullah, Nanu and Hafiz Tahir. Absorption so mastered Shaikh Mittha, it is said, that he wandered the wilderness and spoke with beasts and birds, and whoever he turned his attention upon became a *majzub* too.

Nanu's real name was Namak, but among the people he became known as Nanu. Hearing that on the Day of Judgement all men save the Prophet would be beardless, he bought tweezers and plucked out every hair of his beard and moustache. After his master's death he left for the wilderness and gave up living among people, and there highway robbers killed him. He is buried in a village of the Gujrat district.

## The Shrine and Its Devotion

The shrine at Ranmal Sharif is kept by the saint's descendants and successors and is a busy centre of pilgrimage. It maintains the customary devotions: prayer, *qawwali*, the devotional music of the Sufis, and *naat*, poetry in praise of the Prophet, together with *langar*, the free communal kitchen. Its annual *ʿurs* draws great crowds of the order's followers and of the wider devout. The Naushahia remain active across the Punjab and among the Punjabi diaspora, so that the shrine stands at the head of a living and widespread spiritual family.

In the 1870s it was said that everyone in the Naushahi order was a person of ecstasy and *sama*, spiritual listening, the Sufi session of music, and of longing, relish and intoxication. Most intoxicated of all at *sama* were the *faqirs* of the line of Pak Rahman. They would not cool until a rope was put round their feet and they were hung upside down, and so they danced for an hour or two; failing that, they were dragged along the ground, and the rope was not untied until they came to themselves. In the nineteenth century, too, the Naushahi followers had their *qawwali* and their *hal*, the state of ecstasy.

## Legends

His miracles, it is said, have no end, and the order's chronicle, the *Tazkira-e Naushahi*, sets them out in detail. Wonders are told of his birth. His father, it is said, set out three months into his mother's pregnancy, saying that the child would be a champion of the faith, and came back from the journey to Mecca when the boy was four. Hazrat Shah Sulaiman, the *qutb* of the age, the axis of the saints in his time, foretold the birth and counselled the family.

Tradition also tells of his youth. He grew up strong and fought in many battles. In the wilderness he fasted without a break and was fed each night with milk, and while studying at a village near Nowshera he learned the whole Quran by heart in a single night.

Two more of his wonders are told through his disciples. Sayyid Shah Abdullah, a *mansabdar*, an officer holding rank, at the court of the emperor Alamgir, came to Noushah Ganj Bakhsh when the saint lay ill, with one day left before his death. The saint accepted his *bai'at* and gave him leave for *zikr* and spiritual practice. But Shah Abdullah insisted on becoming a *majzub*. To remain detached amid attachments, the saint answered, is the work of men; becoming a *majzub* is easy. He gave way only when Shah Abdullah invoked God between them. Left with no choice, the saint gave him a shirt he had worn for some days and told him to put it on, and as soon as Shah Abdullah wore it, it is said, the angelic realm was disclosed to him.

Hafiz Tahir had left Islam and joined a party of Hindu *faqirs*, or ascetics. One day the party passed the saint's house, and he ordered wheat to be given to every one of them. All received it except Hafiz Tahir, for the grain had run out. Then the saint called the stranger by name, telling Hafiz Tahir to come to him, for his share was with the saint. He ordered his servant to take off the man's shirt and break the sacred thread at his neck, and Hafiz Tahir became a Muslim anew; with a single look, it is said, the saint brought him to God.

## Writings and Chronicles

The saint was also a poet. His Urdu and Punjabi verse was selected and edited by Sayyid Sharafat Naushahi as *Ganj-e Sharif* and published at Lahore in 1975.

The Naushahia line has its own written history, in works that include the *Risala al-Ijaz* of Mirza Ahmad Beg Lahori (1107 AH), on the lives of the shaikhs of the Naushahia line, which also carries Razi al-Din Qazi Ganjahi's *Risala Tuhfa-e Razwiya*; Sadaqat Muhammad Mah Ganjahi's *Thawaqib al-Manaqib* (1126 AH); the *Tazkira-e Naushahi* (1146 AH); the *Sahaif-e Qudsiya* of Pir Kamal, son of Shaikh Usman Lahori, and Imam Bakhsh Barqandazi Jalandhari's *Mirat al-Ghafuri*, both of 1186 AH; Muhammad Ashraf Manjri's *Kanz al-Rahmat* (1220 AH), printed at Lahore in 1330 AH; Ghulam Muhi al-Din Faqir Lahori's *Tashrif al-Fuqara*; and Sayyid Sharafat Naushahi's own *Sharif al-Tawarikh*, three volumes of seven thousand pages kept in manuscript by its author.

The *Tazkira-e Naushahi* was composed by Hafiz Sayyid Muhammad Hayat Rabbani Naushahi Sahnpalvi, who died in 1173 AH. It began as an annotated copy of the *Risala al-Ijaz* with additions, and it holds only the affairs of the Naushahia elders; four of its manuscripts are in Sayyid Sharafat Naushahi's library and two in the Punjab University Library. Other works of the order include Umar Bakhsh Rasulnagri's *Manaqibat-e Naushahi*, Ghulam Qadir Naushahi's *Bayaz-e Qadri*, Gul Muhammad Naushahi's *Lataif-e Gul Shahi* and the *Kitab al-Fawaid*, the last three among the family's manuscripts. In modern times most of the order's manuscripts have been kept in a private Naushahi library at Sahanpal, in Gujrat.

## The Setting

Phalia, the town near the shrine, has a story of its own. It is said that the town was named after Bucephalus, the legendary horse of Alexander, so that Phalus became Phalia, and that the horse's grave is there. The story has been disputed, on the grounds that the horse was buried near Jalalpur, on the other bank of the river, where his grave was swept away by a flood only a few months later; that coins of the Indo-Greek era found on the *pahari*, the old mound of Phalia, gave birth to the rumour; and that the version became popular in the colonial era, when British officers roamed all over the country collecting oral history.

In the early twenty first century the old mound held a government office and a school. An old shopkeeper reported that the department had sold it for nine *crore* rupees, a *crore* being ten million, and that a shiny new shopping plaza would stand there.

## Legacy

Syed Muhammad Noushah Ganj Bakhsh is remembered as the founder of the Naushahia Qadiri order, one of the enduring Sufi lines of the Punjab, a saint whose long life and organising genius planted a spiritual family that has lasted nearly four centuries. His shrine at Ranmal Sharif remains its living heart and a major centre of Qadiri devotion in the region.

His spiritual line also reached Lahore. In the 1880s the spiritual pedigree of Jawahar Shah, keeper of a tomb outside the city's Mochi Gate, passed through Alahi Shah, Gami Shah and Pak Rahman up to Noushah Ganj Bakhsh. Jawahar Shah rebuilt that tomb and made its mosque *pakka*, of fired brick, and he became its *sajjada nashin*, the custodian who sits on the prayer carpet of the saint buried there; a hall, a small room and a well for the comfort of the *faqirs* stood within its enclosure.
