## Overview

Deep in the mountains of the Khuzdar district of Balochistan, in the remote valley of Lahut Lamakan, stands the shrine of Shah Noorani, also called Syed Bilawal Shah Noorani. It is one of the most venerated and most inaccessible Sufi shrines in Pakistan. Set amid a landscape long held sacred and reached by a hard journey through the hills, it draws pilgrims from across Sindh and Balochistan, who come in great numbers for its *dhamal*, the ecstatic trance dance of its pilgrims, and its blessing, undeterred by its remoteness.

## The Saint and the Sacred Valley

Shah Noorani is remembered as a Sufi saint of the medieval centuries who settled in the wild valley of Lahut Lamakan. The valley's very name, *la-hut la-makan*, "the placeless place of the divine", marks it as a landscape of ancient sanctity, associated in legend with the mystic quest and revered across religious traditions. The saint's life is known more through devotion and legend than through documented history, but his sanctity is held in the highest regard, and his shrine has for centuries been a goal of arduous pilgrimage.

The setting, with its mountains, caves and springs held holy, gives the shrine an aura unlike any other. The valley bears fascinating similarities to the valley of the goddess Hinglaj: both are small oases with natural springs and caves, set in confined valleys.

The valley's holiness is thought to be older than the saint and wider than Islam. It has been suggested that Lahut was once an important place of Hindu worship, which would be in keeping with the history of other Hindu sites in the area that became Sufi centres over time.

Other saints, too, are tied to the valley. Lahut, in the Khuzdar district, is counted among the places where Shah Abdul Latif wandered. A saint called Lal is remembered as having stopped at Lahut Lamakan on a journey that also took him to Makran, Panjgur and Pasni in Balochistan and to Naing, Lake Manchhar and Sehwan in Sindh. These memories belong to the valley and to those saints, not to Shah Noorani or his shrine.

## The Shrine

The heart of the site is a cave, the cave of Shah Noorani, who is said to have lived in this valley. Inside it lies a curiously shaped stone that is said to be the image of Ali's camel. It has also been claimed that, before the place became associated with the Sufi saint, this stone was worshipped as a *śiva liṅga*, an emblem of the god Shiva, and the shape of the stone would support that idea. Cave and stone together carry the memory of more than one faith.

## Devotion and Pilgrimage

Despite its remoteness, Shah Noorani's shrine is a place of intense devotion. It is famous above all for its *dhamal*, driven by the drum and performed by crowds of pilgrims, and for the *langar*, the free communal kitchen of a shrine, and the *qawwali*, Sufi devotional music, that mark its gatherings. Pilgrims undertake the difficult journey to seek healing, to fulfil vows and to share in the charged atmosphere of the sacred valley.

Lahut lies on a pilgrimage route that runs from Sehwan in Sindh, where Lal Shahbaz is the starting point of the pilgrimage to the valley. Many fakirs, wandering Sufis, proceed to Lahut when the annual fair of Sehwan, which lasts three days, comes to its close. One woman's journey from Sehwan may have ended at Lahut, and her husband, searching for her, wondered whether she would trace her way back to Sehwan or follow other fakirs on to shrines in Karachi.

The route once ran further, to the Makran Coast and the abode of the goddess Hinglaj, "Nani Hinglaj", but today this path seems to have been widely forgotten. A trace of it survives at the end of an Urdu book named after the valley, *Lāhūṭ lā Makān*, which describes the way from Lahut to Hinglaj. In 2010 a Punjabi pilgrim at Lahut who had come from Sehwan Sharif confirmed that the stretch from Sehwan was still frequented, but said that the longer path to Hinglaj was no longer used because of the dangerous political situation in the area.

The journey itself has changed. Before the attack of November 2016, Lahut, like Hinglaj, saw a new wave of interest, mainly because a newly constructed road leading to the shrine had made the journey through the mountainous area easier.

## The Attack of 2016

The shrine's fame and its throngs also made it a target. In November 2016, during the evening *dhamal*, a suicide bombing struck the shrine. It killed more than fifty people and injured many worshippers, one of the deadliest attacks on a Pakistani shrine.

The terrible blast curbed the growing influx of visitors and created a volatile situation for the shrines of Balochistan. Yet, as at other shrines struck by such violence, the devotion of the pilgrims endured, and the shrine continued to draw them.

## The Setting

The history and devotional life of Balochistan in general frame the shrine's world. In 710 Muhammad bin Qasim al-Saqafi began his advance through Makran and Balochistan, and he conquered Sindh between 711 and 712. After that conquest a long line of Sufi mystics came to the region through Balochistan, Makran, Multan and Sindh itself. Proper names such as Ghauth Bakhsh, "gift of the help", in Sindh and Balochistan point to the popularity there of the saint Gilani.

The mystical lore of Sindh, Balochistan and the coastal belt of Iran shares myths, melodies and linguistic codes, and the *Mast Qalandar* *dhun*, a devotional melody, is said to be sung all along the Rajasthan, Sindh, Balochistan and Iran continuum. Families often bring women who are emotionally disturbed to take part in the *dhamal*, which devotees hold to have healing powers; in Balochistan such healing ceremonies are called *guāṭi*. The *mugarmān* drum is held sacred by the Sidi communities of Sindh, Balochistan, Rajasthan and Gujarat, who use it in their shrines and for their rituals.

Balochistan shares its poetry and legend with Sindh. The story of Sassi is told in the lands of Sindh and Balochistan, the hill slopes of Balochistan belong to the geography of Sindh's folk tales, and the poetry of the two lands has certain elements in common. The poetry of the Baluchis was wholly oral, sung by professional minstrels called Doms or Dombs at the gatherings of the clans.

The province is hard to reach for pilgrims from India, who must obtain an additional visa to travel to Balochistan, a province that has been in a state of latent insurgency since the establishment of Pakistan.

## Legacy

Shah Noorani stands among the great shrines of Balochistan and lower Sindh: a saint of the sacred mountain valley of Lahut Lamakan whose shrine, for all its remoteness, remains a powerful centre of ecstatic devotion. Its *dhamal*, its legends and its holy landscape make it one of the most singular pilgrimage places in Pakistan, and its endurance after the tragedy of 2016 is a testament to the tenacity of popular faith.

The valley is a station on a long road of devotion: a goal for the fakirs who leave Sehwan when its fair is over, once a stage on the way to the goddess of Hinglaj, and a place whose cave and stone carry the memory of more than one faith.
