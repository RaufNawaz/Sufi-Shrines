## Overview

Gurdwara Chhevin Patshahi stands on Temple Road in the Mozang locality of Lahore. It is one of several shrines in the Lahore area that commemorate a visit by Guru Hargobind, the sixth Sikh Guru, and its name, Chhevin Patshahi, means the "Sixth Sovereignty". In Sikh tradition the site is bound up with the memory of Mata Kaulan, also remembered as Bibi Kaulan, a devoted follower associated with the Guru, whose story is kept in the devotional literature of the Mozang gurdwara tradition. Since Partition it has been maintained as part of Pakistan's protected Sikh heritage.

Guru Hargobind, the son of Guru Arjan, was the first Guru to wear two swords, one for temporal and one for spiritual power, and he began the militarisation of the Sikh community. In the nineteenth century his building east of the village of Mozang was a spacious walled compound, and the Sikhs held it in honour.

## The Gurdwara

Tradition dates the Guru's coming to 1619. Accounts differ over how long he stayed; the gurdwara remembers a visit, one of several halts that Guru Hargobind made in and around Lahore. In the nineteenth century the place was known as the *takia*, the lodge or resting place, of Guru Hargobind Sahib. In the reckoning of the ten *patshahis*, or sovereignties, the sixth is Guru Hargobind's, and from it the gurdwara takes its name.

An original structure is said to have been raised under Maharaja Ranjit Singh in the early nineteenth century, and in Ranjit Singh's time the building was held in great honour. The building seen today was rebuilt in 1926 under the patronage of Sardar Mehar Singh.

In the nineteenth century the compound lay east of the village of Mozang and of the tomb of Hazrat Shah Abu Ishaq Sahib. Its enclosure was of brick, and its entrance was on the south. On the west stood four *samadhs*, memorial shrines; among those commemorated in them were Suba Singh and Sajjan Singh. There was a brick well, and a *musafir-khana*, a travellers' lodge. A brick *chaubara*, an upper room, with a stone floor and marble, was called the Darbar.

In the 1880s it was a very spacious enclosure of brick and lime. Within it was a second enclosure holding a brick well with a Persian wheel and a *sabil* for giving water, and beside it a small garden. There was a house of three storeys for the *mahant*, the head of the establishment; there were two *kothas*, or rooms, and an elaborate *chaubara* plastered with lime, which was the sitting place; there was a marble staircase; and there was a plastered brick platform three feet high.

Above the platform was an arched doorway with a railing, known as the Mandir Baba Sahib. On its walls were pictures of Guru Nanak and of other Gurus, and its vaulted ceiling was set with mirrors and painted. In the eastern *dalan*, or hall, two *Granths*, holy books of the Sikhs, were kept with all due reverence beneath a silken canopy. By then the building's income went to the *pujari*, the officiating priest, of Guru Arjan's *samadh*.

## Worship

Before 1947 the gurdwara sustained daily congregational worship, and every year it observed an *Akhand Path*, the unbroken reading of the Guru Granth Sahib, around Guru Hargobind's birth anniversary, his *Gurpurb*.

In the 1880s a gathering of Sikhs took place here on the fifth day of every moon. *Prashad*, consecrated food, was distributed, and there was *chiraghan*, an illumination with lamps.

## The Sixth Guru

Guru Hargobind was the son of Guru Arjan, his predecessor as Guru, and of Mai Ganga Sahiba. It is told that Guru Arjan's wife visited a holy man with gifts, and that he foretold a noble son and said that the child should be named Hargobind. He was born on the day Guru Arjan went to the village of Dawali. He was Guru from 1606 to 1644, and like his father he spent a lot of time in Lahore.

On 24 July 1606, after Guru Arjan had appointed him, he decided to give *darshan*, an audience at which his followers beheld their Guru. By then the Akal Takht, the Eternal Throne, had been prepared. He came dressed as a royal, in a saffron gown and *churidar* pyjama, close fitting trousers, with an aigrette, a necklace and two swords, and he refused to take up the religious symbols of Guruhood: "My rosary shall be the sword belt and on my turban, I shall wear the emblem of royalty". This was *miri-piri*: one sword stood for temporal power, the other for spiritual prowess. He was the first Guru to wear two swords.

When the news of his father's death reached him at Amritsar, it is told, he mourned and swore the most solemn of oaths to take revenge on Chandu Lal. Chandu Lal had been appointed Diwan of Lahore, a high office of state, by the Mughal authorities, and he had asked for Hargobind's hand in marriage for his daughter. The Guru ordered that no Sikh should speak Chandu Lal's name, and that whenever he was mentioned a Sikh should first say *phitkar*, a curse, three times. At the spot of his father's *samadhi* Guru Hargobind raised a small structure.

Known as the "soldier-saint", he began the militarisation of the Sikh community following the assassination of his father. He reared horses and encouraged his followers to keep arms in their defence. A saffron flag faced the Akal Takht; there was a large drum, and he kept a hawk as a pet. He had fifty two bodyguards and a force of *Sant Sipahi*, saint soldiers, five hundred to begin with, who were not paid a salary but were exempt from paying the required tribute. Through his *masands*, his appointed agents, devotees were told to offer arms and horses; the offering of weapons at the Guru's court was a practice he began, later reinforced by Guru Gobind Singh. To administer the Sikh community he created the concept of the *Sangat*, the congregation of followers, which was then put under a *masand*.

## The Guru and the Emperors

Jahangir imprisoned Guru Hargobind in Gwalior, and accounts differ over why. A story is told of it. Chandu Lal told Jahangir that the Guru was called the True King and had built a throne, and the emperor sent men to bring him; Bhai Gurdas stayed behind at Amritsar as one of his deputies. At Delhi Jahangir asked him which were good, Hindus or Muslims, and the Guru answered with a line of Guru Arjan's on the different names by which people call on God, some saying Ram and some Khuda. Its opening runs:

کوئی بولے رام رام کوئی بولو خدا …

When a Sikh came asking for his True King, Jahangir sent him on to the Guru. Chandu Lal then arranged that the Guru be held in Gwalior fort, and wrote to the *thanadar*, the officer in charge there, to keep him in hardship. Imprisoned rajas were held there too. The king had a dream in which a voice told him to release the Guru, and when Hargobind asked that these rajas be released as well, the king agreed. The Guru then claimed his father's rosary, and claimed *qisas*, retaliation, against Chandu Lal, who was handed over to him in chains; each Sikh was to give him five blows of a shoe.

In conflict with Jahangir at first, the Guru later came to a cordial relationship with the emperor, helping him in a couple of his military expeditions; some claims have him in Jahangir's employment, serving him in the Punjab and helping him to overcome Tara Chand of Nalagarh. Under Shahjahan three battles were fought, at Amritsar, Lahira and Kartarpur, and all of them were won by the Sikhs. Accounts differ over where he settled, whether at Kartarpur or at Kiratpur on the Sutlej.

The Sikh greeting is traced to him. It is told that the Guru ordered his Sikhs always to ask God for victory, and that in place of the greeting *salam* this phrase was fixed: *Wahguru ji ki fateh*, victory to the Wahguru.

His eldest son, Baba Gurditta, died in his lifetime, and he appointed Har Rai, the younger son of Baba Gurditta, who sat in the place of his grandfather. Following his grandfather's tradition, Har Rai too kept horses and a force of twenty two hundred fighters. Hargobind was the *pir*, the spiritual guide, of the seventh Guru and the father of the ninth, his younger son Tegh Bahadur, who followed in his father's footsteps and kept the sword of Guru Hargobind.

Opinion on him is divided. Critics saw his militarism as "a departure from the teachings of the early Gurus", and he was the first Guru not to compose a single hymn. He remains one of the most controversial of the Sikh Gurus, and yet he laid the foundations of the contemporary face of Sikhism.

## Bibi Kaulan

The story of Bibi Kaulan, as it is told, begins in Mozang. She was a maid employed by a Muslim trader who lived in Mozang and had bonded her against her wishes. Hearing that the Guru was encamped nearby, she pleaded to be rescued, and the Guru took her along with him on his horse. They rested for a while at a spot in the village of Amar Sadhu, inside the Defence Housing Authority of Lahore, before they went on to Amritsar. The gurdwara that stands there, built in honour of the memory of Guru Hargobind, is known as Gurdwara Bibi Kaulan.

In Amritsar, the story continues, she insisted that he marry her. The Guru could not marry her, and so he ordered a pool to be built near the Golden Temple and named after her, decreeing that visitors to the Golden Temple must first perform ablution at the pool of Bibi Kaulan.

## Mozang and Sikh Lahore

In the nineteenth century Mozang was a *mauza*, a village, east of which the Guru's building stood. It was a former settlement outside the city of Lahore, and one of the quarters of the city. Its settling is connected with Pir Shirazi Baloch, to whom Baloch people in Mozang came and went, and Miani lay to the west of the village.

Maharaja Ranjit Singh ruled the Punjab for forty years, and held Kashmir and the Derajat as well. When he became the sovereign of Punjab at the end of the eighteenth century, he spent large amounts of money on the renovation and expansion of many gurdwaras, and attached vast tracts of land to them so that the revenues from the lands could support their functioning. Later, with the divisions among the Sikhs and the British takeover of the Punjab, a large number of important Sikh gurdwaras came to be administered by Hindu *mahants*. In the nineteenth century the departure of Maharaja Dalip Singh from the Punjab, at the end of Sikh rule, was lamented in a *qita*, a short poem.

## Legacy

Since Partition the gurdwara has been maintained, like most of Lahore's Sikh shrines, as part of Pakistan's protected Sikh heritage rather than as a site of resident congregational worship. It remains distinguished among the city's several Chhevin Patshahi gurdwaras, including those at Chowmala/Bhati Gate and at Jhalian, each marking a separate halt in Guru Hargobind's travels.

In the early twenty first century, as one writer on Pakistan's religious minorities put it, "it is now rare that one runs into a Sikh in this city, a city which was once ruled by a Sikh and the seat of their political strength". Across Pakistani Punjab hundreds of temples and gurdwaras were taken over by refugees and converted into households, and several gurdwaras and temples were taken over and destroyed by migrants.

In the nineteenth century the place was a walled compound east of Mozang, with its *samadhs*, its well and *sabil*, its garden and lodge, the painted Mandir Baba Sahib, and the two *Granths* under a silken canopy, a place the Sikhs honoured as Guru Hargobind's.
