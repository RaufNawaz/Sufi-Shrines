## Overview

At Sharaqpur Sharif, north-west of Lahore, stands the shrine of Hazrat Mian Pir Sher Muhammad Sharaqpuri (1865 to 1928), a widely revered Sufi master of the Qadiri and Chishti traditions and one of the notable saints of the Punjab in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Known for his piety, his learning and the great number of disciples he guided, Pir Sher Muhammad made Sharaqpur a centre of devotion and religious teaching. His darbar, or shrine, remains an important place of pilgrimage in central Punjab.

## The Saint

Mian Sher Muhammad was born in 1865 at Sharaqpur into a devout family, and from his youth he turned to the religious sciences and the Sufi path. Trained both in the outward learning of the seminary and in the inward discipline of the Sufi orders, he became a spiritual guide of great reputation. He united the lineages of two Sufi orders, the Qadiri, founded by ʿAbd al-Qadir Gilani, and the Chishti, and drew disciples from across the Punjab and beyond.

He was among the teachers whose light shaped the next generation of Punjabi Sufis. The saint Abul Faiz Qalandar Ali Suhrawardi of Lahore, for instance, is remembered as having received knowledge from him. Pir Sher Muhammad is remembered for a life of prayer, austerity and service, and for the reforming, sober devotion he taught. He died in 1928 and was buried at Sharaqpur.

## The Shrine

The shrine of Pir Sher Muhammad at Sharaqpur Sharif is a large complex kept by his successors, and a busy centre of pilgrimage. It maintains the five daily prayers, gatherings of *naat*, poetry in praise of the Prophet, and of *qawwali*, Sufi devotional music, and the free kitchen. Its annual *ʿurs*, the commemoration of the anniversary of the saint's death, draws great crowds of devotees who come to honour the saint and seek his blessing.

The town of Sharaqpur Sharif takes its very identity from the darbar, and the saint's line has continued as its custodians and as respected religious figures.

## The Setting

Sharaqpur is also the burial place of an earlier saint, Shaikh Muhammad Saeed Chishti Sabri Sharaqpuri, a Chishti Sabiri of the same town. He is not the saint of this shrine: he died in 1214 AH, by the Islamic calendar, and his death and Pir Sher Muhammad's fall more than a century apart.

Sharaqpur has its place in the history of books as well. A work called the *Khazina-e Marifat*, by Muhammad Ibrahim Qasuri, was printed there, and a manuscript of Lal Beg Lali's *Thamarat al-Quds*, a work dating from after 1017 AH, has been held at Sharaqpur in the possession of Maulana Nusrat Naushahi of Sharaqpur. Rani Jindan gave Bhai Budh Singh a *jagir*, a grant of land revenue, in the Sharaqpur area to feed travellers at the *samadhi*, the memorial, of Jawahar Singh.

In the Sikh period no printed book came into the Punjab from Hindustan, and scribes copied books by hand for sale. The British conquest of the Punjab in the 1840s laid new patterns of modernity over the literary culture of the Sufi tradition: Persian gave way to Urdu and English, and publishers in Lahore brought the older Sufi poetry into print. The lyrics of the Punjab's Sufi poets were for the most part collected in printed form only in the late nineteenth century. By the 1880s Lahore was the capital of the province of the Punjab, where the Lieutenant-Governor held his court.

In the Punjab after 1800 the literary heritage of Panjabi was rarely continued by Muslims, who turned to Urdu, though in the twentieth century a number of mystical treatises were written and published in Panjabi. British policy, especially in the Punjab and Sind, was to treat the hereditary saints in essentially the same way as landlords and tribal leaders were treated. In the countryside of Punjab and Sindh, in many cases, the local *pir*, the Sufi spiritual guide, represents the local landlord or is at least closely related to him.

In the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries the British connected the five rivers of the Punjab with each other through canals, turning jungle land into agricultural land. Thousands of new villages were founded, and farming families from all over the Punjab were invited to settle. In the early twentieth century Muhammad Ilyas, founder of the Tableeghi Jamaat, worked in undivided rural Punjab, exhorting Muslims to "purify" their religious practices, which he argued were corrupted by the influence of the dominant Hindu culture.

## The Sufi Orders in the Punjab

Both of the orders in which Pir Sher Muhammad stood had spread in the Punjab before his time. The first of the Qadiri line to come to the Punjab was Hazrat Sayyid Muhammad Ghaus Uchi Jili Gilani, Sayyid Muhammad Ghaus of Uch. His ancestors had left Baghdad in the time of Hulagu Khan, and he came by way of Khurasan to Multan and settled at Uch. In the time of Guru Nanak, the founder of the Sikh faith, the Qadiris had made the Punjab the main centre of their activities. Another Sufi way, the Naushahi, began with Hazrat Naushah Ganj Bakhsh, and by the 1870s its *faqirs*, wandering Sufis, numbered in the thousands in the Punjab.

The Chishti order spread in Hind and the Punjab from Khwaja Muin al-Din Hasan Sijzi, and it is usually considered one of the most powerful Sufi orders in Northern India and the Punjab. The Nizami Chishtis came to prominence in the eighteenth century, principally in Delhi, the Deccan and the Punjab, through masters who were related to local political leaders but in tension with them. The order's Nizami Fakhri branch spread in the Punjab chiefly through Khwaja Sulaiman Taunsvi, who consolidated the order by stressing learning through the establishment of institutions: the academy at Taunsa and other religious schools throughout the Punjab. In the nineteenth century the Nizami branch of Chishti Sufism expanded in the upper Punjab through his disciples.

The Muslim conquest of northern India carried the Persianate world to the Punjab, where Persian remained the dominant literary language of the ruling elite through the Mughal period and beyond, and a strong Sufi presence was quickly established there with the coming of figures of the main Sufi orders. As one historian has observed, "Islam took root much more readily in Bengal or the Punjab than in upper India".

## Devotion and Poetry in the Punjab

The qawwali tunes of the Punjab are distinguished from those of Uttar Pradesh by their rhythm, since they tend to be organised according to stress, and the name Qalandar continues to be intoned in the popular qawwali lyrics of the Punjab. *Dhammal* is the vigorous ritual dance performed at Sufi shrines in the Punjab and Sind. The writing of *taʿwidh*, amulets, for the people has not changed since the thirteenth century: the successors of Baba Farid and his assistants, in common with those of other shrines in the Punjab, continue to write *taʿwidh* for the masses. The Punjab and Sind have also produced hymns extolling the great masters of the mystical orders, particularly ʿAbd al-Qadir Gilani.

In 1292 AH Mufti Ghulam Sarwar Lahori completed *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, a book on the saints of the Punjab, and his son, Mufti Ghulam Safdar Lahori, marked the year with a chronogram, a verse written to record a date, on the book's subject:

جو خدا کے دوست تھے پنجاب میں
حال ان کا اس سے ہوتا ہے عیان

The Punjabi poet Waris Shah opens his *Hir and Ranjha* with an invocation that praises Baba Farid of Pakpattan:

*When Shakarganj made his abode at Pakpattan, the Punjab was delivered of all its troubles.* (translated by Charles Frederick Usborne)

In the epilogue, where he dates his poem to 1180 Hijri, he laments the disorder of the country and turns to a single town:

*Of all the wretched Punjab I am most concerned for Kasur.* (translated by Charles Frederick Usborne)

The Sufi poet Bulleh Shah's verse speaks of the Punjab of the eighteenth century in the same troubled terms:

*The door of doom and torment has opened, and Panjab is in a bad state. It has been struck by fear of the pit of hell.* (translated by Christopher Shackle)

Another of his poems runs:

*Now hell has opened its bogs. / Punjab has gone to the dogs. / This hell is the nethermost hell.* (translated by Taufiq Rafat)

## Legacy

Pir Sher Muhammad Sharaqpuri is remembered as one of the influential Sufi masters of modern Punjab, a guide of many thousands whose teaching joined the Qadiri and Chishti ways and whose disciples carried his influence onward. His shrine at Sharaqpur Sharif remains a centre of the devotion he inspired, and his memory endures among the notable saints of the Lahore region.
