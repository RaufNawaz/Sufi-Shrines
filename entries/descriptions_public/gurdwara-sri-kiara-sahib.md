## Overview

Gurdwara Sri Kiara Sahib stands amid the fields on the eastern side of Nankana Sahib, the town in the Punjab that was the birthplace of Guru Nanak Dev Ji, founder of the Sikh faith. Its name comes from the Punjabi *kiara*, a field or plot of cultivated ground, for the gurdwara marks the field where one of the best loved miracles of Guru Nanak's boyhood is said to have taken place. Set a little apart from the great shrines at the centre of the town, Kiara Sahib belongs to the landscape of sites in and around Nankana Sahib that together preserve the memory of the Guru's early years, and pilgrims come to it on the great anniversaries.

## The Miracle of the Field

The tradition of Kiara Sahib belongs to the cycle of *sakhis*, the accounts of Guru Nanak's life, that centre on his childhood at what was then the village of Rai Bhoi di Talwandi, later renamed Nankana Sahib in his honour. As a boy, the stories tell, Nanak was set to tend the family's cattle, and he would let the animals graze while he sat absorbed in meditation, heedless of the world about him. On one such day, it is said, the buffaloes strayed into a neighbour's standing crop, and the aggrieved peasant hurried to Rai Bular Bhatti, the local landlord, to complain that young Nanak's herd had ruined his field.

When the field was inspected, the story goes on, the crop was found whole and unharmed, as though nothing had touched it. The onlookers, it is told, took this for a miracle, and the plot was thereafter revered as *Kiara Sahib*, "the honoured field". Accounts of the episode differ in their details. For the Sikh tradition it is one of a series of signs by which Rai Bular, a Muslim landlord, came to recognise the extraordinary nature of the child in his village, and it is remembered less as a marvel of crops than as an early disclosure of the Guru's sanctity and of the divine favour that rested upon him.

Other signs are told of his childhood. In his childhood, it is said, signs of spiritual unveiling and of wonders beyond the ordinary course of things became manifest in him. The janamsakhis tell that the village astrologer, Pandit Hardyal, foretold the child's greatness, and that at school the boy showed a brilliance never before seen in any child in Talwandi.

In the janamsakhi telling, the landlord of the story belonged to the family that had rebuilt the village: Talwandi's last rebuilding took place under the supervision of Rai Bhoe, a Bhatti Rajput who had converted to Islam, and Rai Bhoe's son was Rai Bular. In the same telling, Shiv Ram, head of a Bedi family, was accountant to Rai Bhoe, and he was succeeded by his son Kalyan Chand Das Bedi, or Mehta Kalu as he was affectionately called, the Guru's father, who kept the accounts of the Bhatti family under Rai Bular.

The same stories return to the family in the Guru's later life. When Nanak came back to Talwandi with Mardana from the first of his journeys, they tell, Nanak spent time every day with Rai Bhoe, who looked upon him as his Guru, and Mehta Kalu was a little in awe of his son. On a later return, after Pak Pattan, they go on, Talwandi was no longer an obscure little village but had become a place of pilgrimage, and Nanak sat by Rai Bular, who was sick and dying.

## The Gurdwara

A shrine was raised in later times to mark the sacred field, and the present building is associated with the great works of gurdwara construction carried out in the decades before Partition. It was reconstructed in the years leading up to 1947 by Sant Gurmukh Singh, known by the epithet *Sevavale*, "the one devoted to service", for his lifelong labour of building and restoring Sikh shrines. The gurdwara is a square, domed sanctum set upon a raised plinth, with a verandah running round it for the circling of the shrine, in the characteristic idiom of Punjabi gurdwara architecture, and it stands amid open ground that still recalls the farming setting of the miracle.

Nankana Sahib has several gurdwaras marking events from the Guru's childhood. Of those associated with Guru Nanak, Kiara Sahib and Malji Sahib are the furthest from Gurdwara Janamsthan, the shrine of his birthplace. Across the Punjab, Maharaja Ranjit Singh, who became sovereign of the province at the end of the eighteenth century, spent large sums on the renovation and expansion of many gurdwaras, and he attached vast tracts of land to them so that the revenues from the land could support their running.

Since the Partition of 1947, when the Sikh population of west Punjab migrated to India, Kiara Sahib, like the other shrines of Nankana Sahib, has stood in a land with few resident worshippers, cared for by the bodies responsible for Sikh heritage in Pakistan, though accounts of the care of the town's gurdwaras in the years after Partition differ. Kartar, who spoke of the town's gurdwaras as his own, remembered those years: "There was no Sikh left here after partition. Our Gurdwaras became barren and were in a bad state." Giani Pratap, Kartar recalled, came with a pilgrimage party from India sometime in the 1960s, and when he saw the dilapidated condition of the shrines he decided to stay and look after these holy buildings. Most of the town's gurdwaras were kept locked all year round and opened only at the time of the festival, and security guards were employed at the entrance to all of them.

## Devotion and Festivals

Kiara Sahib is visited chiefly by pilgrims who cross the border on the great anniversaries. On the *Gurpurab* marking Guru Nanak's birth, and on other occasions when *jathas*, bands of pilgrims, travel to Nankana Sahib, the gurdwara receives the devotions of the faithful who come to walk the ground of the Guru's childhood. Because Nanak spent his early years in the town, it has several gurdwaras, and the pilgrims visit all of them.

The festival of the Guru's birthday, Guru Nanak Jayanti or Gurpurab, is kept at Nankana Sahib every November. Official estimates put the pilgrims who come for it at ten to fifteen thousand every year; in 2011, by media reports, only three thousand came, and that year the festival ran from the 8th to the 10th of November. Police stood at all the entrances to the city, no car was permitted inside, and devotees walked barefoot. For those few days the entire city looked like a little India.

The pilgrims from India came by train from Delhi, Gujarat and Amritsar and crossed at Wagah, where representatives of the Evacuee Trust Property Board and the Pakistan Sikh Gurdwara Prabandhak Committee received them and a *langar*, the free communal kitchen, was laid on. They stayed three days at Nankana. Three days of *Akhand Path*, the unbroken reading of the Sikh scripture, ended in the *bhog*, the rite that closes the reading, and the *Nagar Kirtan*, the procession singing hymns through the town, followed on the final day; then the pilgrims went on to other cities. Richer pilgrims from Britain, America and Canada came by chartered plane and hired buses, and travel companies' posters promised them "all" the gurdwaras in Pakistan.

Pilgrims from abroad kept the festival alive. Guru Nanak's birthday at Nankana Sahib was among the events that foreign pilgrims kept up after Partition, and Sikhs from abroad came to Pakistan for festivals such as Baisakhi and the birthday of Guru Nanak, which were attended by thousands of Indian pilgrims. Pilgrim tourists came to Nankana Sahib and to Hassan Abdal, and at Manora in Karachi, Jeeta Singh held a small festival of his own every November, when the festival of Guru Nanak was celebrated at Nankana Sahib.

The Sikhs who lived in the town were mostly newcomers: not natives of the city but recent migrants from Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, where there is a considerable Sikh population. Most of them were housed within the gurdwaras, in rooms generally reserved for pilgrims. The community numbered about two hundred households, and Jatha Singh had been its leader for several years.

## Nankana Sahib

Tradition tells that Talwandi lay between the rivers Ravi and Chenab, directly in the path of the armies that had set their eyes on Delhi, and that thirteen times the village was destroyed and thirteen times, like the fabled phoenix, it rose again from its ashes. It lay too, the same tradition tells, in the path of numerous mendicants, holy men and *sadhus*, as Hindu ascetics are called, who camped in a grove of trees on the outskirts of the village. Originally called Rai Bhoi di Talwandi, the town was renamed Nankana Sahib in honour of the first Sikh Guru, who was born there. It lies about forty miles south west of Lahore.

An old mound rests behind Gurdwara Janamsthan, away from the hub of the new settlement. It is held to be an archaeological mound: the settlement there was destroyed, after which Rai Bhoi di Talwandi was built within a boundary wall. The climb was steep, with no proper path, to a top at least twenty metres from the ground, covered with shrubs of *karir*, a thorny desert bush, with shards of broken pottery and other mud utensils underfoot. The young Nanak, it is said, used to spend a lot of time there in conversation with the yogis, *sadhus* and dervishes who passed through Talwandi. On his road back from Chuhrkhana, it is told, he crossed a vast jungle that lay then between Nankana and Chuhrkhana.

The modern town had one main road running through it; the buildings were old, most of them from the late British or the early Pakistan era, and the road had potholes. From a roof the minarets of mosques could be seen, with the small white domes of the gurdwaras and the remains of a few Hindu temples, cone like structures rising from houses abandoned at the time of Partition. In 2005 Nankana Sahib was separated from Sheikhupura and made a district of its own, with the city as its headquarters. It is considered one of the holiest cities in the world for the Sikhs, for the founder of their religion was born there.

Guru Nanak was celebrated all over the town, with a Guru Nanak High School, a Guru Nanak grocery store and a Guru Nanak cloth shop, all run by Sikhs. The regard for Nanak found among the Muslims at Gurdwara Sachkand was not found among the Muslims of Nankana Sahib: most of them, it is said, had migrated from the other side of the border after Partition, and their attitudes had been shaped by those experiences. At one of the shrines of Guru Nanak at Nankana Sahib, a picture of Baba Farid accompanied the picture of the Guru.

## The Guru

The gurdwara exists for the sake of Guru Nanak. The *janamsakhis*, the cycles of tales told of the life of the first Guru of the Sikhs, were written by his devotees after his death, and the earliest known of them dates from 1658, nearly 120 years after he died. Accounts of his birth differ in their details, but he is generally held to have been born at Talwandi in 1469, and he lived there in his grandfather's house. Tradition tells that his mother, Mata Tripta, would regularly visit an ancient Hindu temple on top of a mound not far from Rai Bhoi di Talwandi to pray for a child; accounts of how his birth came about differ.

In the janamsakhi telling, at thirteen Nanak refused the sacred thread, the *janeu*, in his father's courtyard, saying that "it is our actions and our actions alone" that give a man his position. At sixteen, the janamsakhis go on, he herded cattle, and he became well acquainted with the dervish Sayyad Hussain and gained an intimate knowledge of the Sufi line of thought. Legend tells that when he turned eighteen his father asked him to take up a trade and gave him twenty rupees, and that Nanak left the town with Bhai Mardana; so begins the story of the *Sacha Sauda*, the true bargain.

From Talwandi, in the janamsakhi telling, Nanak went to Sultanpur, around 60 kilometres away, as keeper of Nawab Daulat Khan's stores, and there, it is told, he vanished into the stream for a week and came out saying, "There is only one God. There is no Hindu, no Mussalman." He married Sulakhini, daughter of Mul Chand and Chando Rani of the town of Batala, on 24 September 1487, though accounts of the marriage differ, and they had two sons, Sri Chand, born on 8 September 1494, and Lakhmi Das, born on 12 February 1497.

Accounts of how many journeys he made differ, but his *udasis*, the Guru's journeys, are most often counted as four. In the tellings of his life they lasted more than twenty years and took him, mostly on foot, east to Bengal, south to Sri Lanka, north to Kashmir, Nepal and Tibet, and west to Mecca. Whenever he visited an area, it is said, he would visit its religious shrines, Muslim, Hindu and of any other denomination. When at last he settled at Kartarpur, the story goes, he sent to Talwandi for his family.

People are said to have come in multitudes from every land to become disciples of Baba Nanak, as the Guru was also known. In Aurangzeb's reign the place of Baba Nanak lay twelve *kos*, an old measure of distance, from Batala, on the bank of the Ravi, and his descendants lived there. Lakhmi Das, it was said, was a worthy son, but on his deathbed the Guru appointed not a son but his most loyal student as his spiritual successor: a Khatri named Lehna, to whom he gave the title of Guru Angad. His eldest son, Sri Chand, was not recognised as Guru by the mainstream Sikh community, and the Udasis follow his tradition. Guru Nanak died on 22 September 1539, though accounts of the date differ.

In Aurangzeb's reign his followers counted service of the traveller, of all who come and go, as the highest worship, and whoever arrived at midnight speaking Baba Nanak's name was treated as a brother and a friend and served, even a stranger, a thief or a highwayman. It is believed that the tradition of the *langar* began with Guru Nanak. He lived as a householder, and he associated with people of the lower castes, calling them people of God. It is said that when his Hindu and Muslim companions asked him which religion to adopt, he told them to be true Muslims or honest Hindus: "That would be Nanak's religion."

He is tied to the Sufis too. Nanak is held to have been a spiritual follower of Baba Farid, and to have travelled to Pak Pattan, where he met the saint's spiritual descendants and collected his poetry. Farid's texts are revered among the Sikhs, for he is held to have influenced the founder of their religion. A Chishti Sabiri shaykh of the twentieth century described Guru Nanak, with Krishna, Rama, Sita and Lachman, as a believer in the one true God and a religious leader with a "Divine connection", *nisbat*.

Devotion to him reaches beyond the Sikhs. Nanak Panthis, Hindus who believe in the teachings of Guru Nanak but not in the Khalsa, the order of initiated Sikhs, keep portraits of Guru Nanak in their temples in Sindh and southern Punjab, along with Krishna, Kali Mata and other Hindu deities, and many Lohanas in Sindh follow his teachings; in Sindh, too, the iconography of Guru Nanak and that of Jhulelal have grown more and more alike. Many Muslims acquainted with his teachings are devoted to him too and visit his shrine. In the valley of the goddess Hinglaj, engraved footsteps held to be Guru Nanak's connect that temple to the Sikh and Nanak *panth*, or sect, traditions.

The field of Kiara Sahib is one miracle among many told of the Guru. Legend tells that Nanak once travelled to Mecca, where he performed the Hajj, and slept with his feet towards the Kaaba; rebuked, he turned his feet, and as he did so the Kaaba miraculously turned towards them. It is told, too, that a trader who told Nanak that his mules carried "Just some mud" found his sugar and *gur*, raw cane sugar, turned to mud, and that when he repented his mud became sugar and *gur* once again. Other legends tell of Nanak humbling a Muslim Sufi at Sialkot and at Pasrur. Yet Nanak himself is held to have abhorred the association of miracles with religious personalities, and in one story he tells Karori, who calls his cure a miracle, that "there is no such thing as a miracle".

## The Guru's Verse

The Guru's *bani*, his devotional verse, was compiled as part of the Adi Granth by Guru Arjan Dev, the fifth Guru, in 1604. He composed verses in Hindi on the truth of God and on divine unity, and a singer named Mardana sang his verses and drew people into the snare of devotion. In Aurangzeb's reign the whole of his followers' worship was the reading of their master's verses, which they recited with song and melody. Like Kabir, Nanak used the *doha*, a rhyming couplet in an old Prakrit and Hindi metre, for his mystical poetry, and he brought into his verse several words that were not part of the Punjabi of his time. In the *Babur Bani* he lays the blame of Babur's wrath at God's feet.

Lines from his hymns, in English translation, give a sense of the poetry. His *aarti*, the hymn sung as lamps are waved in worship, begins:

ਗਗਨ ਮੈ ਥਾਲੁ
ਰਵਿ ਚੰਦੁ ਦੀਪਕ ਬਨੇ …

*In the salver of the firmament, / The sun and the moon shine as lamps*

On the oneness of God he sang:

ਸਾਹਿਬੁ ਮੇਰਾ ਏਕੋ ਹੈ ॥
ਏਕੋ ਹੈ ਭਾਈ ਏਕੋ ਹੈ ॥

*My Master is One / He is One, brother, the only One*

And again:

*God is One, / The Path is One*

His poem on the sacred thread runs:

ਦਇਆ ਕਪਾਹ
ਸੰਤੋਖੁ ਸੂਤੁ …

*From the cotton of compassion, / Spin the thread of contentment*

Of washing he sang:

ਭਰੀਐ ਹਥੁ ਪੈਰੁ ਤਨੁ ਦੇਹ ॥
ਪਾਣੀ ਧੋਤੈ ਉਤਰਸੁ ਖੇਹ ॥

*As hands or feet besmirched with slime, / Water washes white*

In one modern retelling of his life, Nanak sings to innkeepers who meant to rob him:

ਬਗਾ ਬਗੇ ਕਪੜੇ ਤੀਰਥ ਮੰਝਿ ਵਸੰਨਿੑ ॥

*The herons in their white feathers dwell in the sacred shrines of pilgrimage*

His verse is sung beyond the gurdwaras too. The singer Abida Parvin has said: "I sing Shah Hussain, Bulle Shah, Mian Muhammad Bakhsh, Hazrat Khawaja Ghulam Farid, Sacchal Sarmast, Baba Farid, and Guru Nanak." In Sindh, Sain Vasan Shah became famous for reciting the *banis* of Shri Guru Nanak and the *kalams*, the poems, of the Sufis.

## Legacy

Kiara Sahib is one of the cluster of gurdwaras that make Nankana Sahib the holiest town of the Sikh world after Amritsar, each marking a remembered moment of the Guru's early life: his birth, his schooling, his play, and the miracles of his boyhood. The field of the untouched crop speaks to a theme dear to Sikh devotion: that the greatness of Guru Nanak was manifest from his earliest years, and that even a landlord of another faith was moved to acknowledge it. For the pilgrim, to stand at Kiara Sahib is to enter the pastoral world of the *sakhis*, where a boy lost in the remembrance of God let his cattle wander, and the very fields, it is told, bore witness to his sanctity.

Guru Nanak was born in the city now called Nankana Sahib and died in a small village called Kartarpur, both in present day Pakistan, and the gurdwaras built there later by his devotees still stand tall.
