## Overview

Immediately beside Lahore Fort and the Badshahi Mosque, on the spot where, by most accounts, his body was cremated, stands the samadhi, or memorial shrine, of Maharaja Ranjit Singh, one of the grandest funerary monuments of the Sikh Empire. Ranjit Singh, the "Lion of Punjab", founded the Sikh Empire and ruled it from 1801 until his death on 27 June 1839. Though formally a samadhi rather than a constituted gurdwara, a Sikh house of worship, the site has long been a living place of Sikh devotion, and it remains an active place of pilgrimage. The Evacuee Trust Property Board administers it as one of the most visited symbols of Sikh heritage remaining in Pakistan.

## The Maharaja

Ranjit Singh's family is traced back to one Kalu, and it kept shops in salt and tobacco. One of the family became the *chela*, or disciple, of a Sikh and stopped shaving, and Budh Singh took the *pahul*, the Sikh rite of initiation, at Amritsar; he rode a piebald mare and died in 1716. The line then divided: from Naudh Singh began Ranjit Singh's family, and from another branch the family of the Sandhanwalias. Naudh Singh was shot in 1742.

Charat Singh, an ancestor of the Maharaja, built a fort in the village of Gujranwala, became *sardar*, or chief, fought towards Jammu, and died. In those years Khwaja Abid, the Mughal *subadar*, or governor, of Lahore, attacked Charat Singh's fort, and victory fell to the Sikhs. Mahan Singh, Ranjit Singh's father, succeeded as a boy of ten, and he was the first to seize the collection of relics kept at Rasulnagar. Ranjit Singh was born in 1780. As a boy he had smallpox, and the illness cost him one eye.

After Mahan Singh's death, in the year 1211 of the Islamic calendar, when Shah Zaman Durrani came from Kabul, Ranjit Singh in fear sent his best goods and the relics of Rasulnagar from Gujranwala to his mother-in-law, Sada Kaur, at the fort of Makerian. He later pressed for them more than once and was refused, and under stronger pressure she moved them to the fort of Chunda. At last, when he had seized all her property, she gave them to his son Sher Singh, her grandson, who kept them by him until the day he was murdered; from him the collection passed through Hira Singh and Jawahar Singh to Lahore Fort under the English. By Ranjit Singh's order Faqir Nur al-Din was appointed to subdue Chatti, and the Faqir family then bought a second collection for themselves.

Starting from a small fief around Gujranwala, Ranjit Singh won Lahore at the end of the eighteenth century; accounts of how and in which year the city came to him differ. When he took it, Qazi Nizam-ud-Din was the city's *qazi*, or judge. In 1801 he was crowned Ruler of Punjab by Sahib Singh Bedi, a descendant of Guru Nanak.

He fought at Gujrat and elsewhere, took Kangra, and conquered Multan in 1818, when Nawab Muzaffar Khan was killed, and then the fort of Khyber; Jammu he granted to Gulab Singh and Suchet Singh. In 1802 he employed Akali Phula Singh, a Nihang, a member of an armed Sikh order, to capture the city of Amritsar, and Phula Singh brought two to three thousand Nihangs into the army of the state. He captured the Pathan regions of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and the tribal areas, as a result of which several people converted to Sikhism. Besides the Punjab, Kashmir, the Derajat and other lands were in his possession, and his empire reached from the tribal areas to Kashmir, to Multan in the south and to the River Sutlej. His infantry, not less than seventy thousand strong, drew its pay through Bakhshi Bhagat Ram, and he came to terms with the British. He ruled in the Punjab for forty years, the first Punjabi to rule a land that for more than a thousand years had been ruled by Afghans, Mughals and others who were not Punjabis.

Among the great men of his court was Jamadar Khushal Singh, who received *havelis*, or mansions, and gardens in Lahore and Amritsar, and Dina Nath built a large temple to Shiva in Lahore. Faqir Aziz al-Din held the offices of prime minister and foreign minister, and his family went on living in their ancestral house inside the walled city of Lahore. The Faqir family were known in Lahore as the family of the Faqir sahibs and the *hakims*, the physicians. Among his generals were the Muslims Mohammad Sultan, Elahi Baksh and Ghaus Mohammad Khan. Hira Singh was the Maharaja's favourite, and while the Maharaja lived five rupees were put under Hira Singh's head every night.

The courtesan Moran held such favour with him that he never disobeyed her word. Coin was struck in her name, and the Moranshahi rupee was current throughout the Punjab; he built the village of Moranwala and the Moranwali mosque. Moran held her own court of justice and her own *darbar*, or audience, separately, and her word brought about the ruin of Mehr Muhkam and the destruction of Nawan Kot. He married Gul Begum with the *phere*, the rounds of the wedding rite, and the procession went from Lahore to Amritsar. In Lahore he built a mosque in a *mohalla*, or quarter, called Gul Begum, in memory of his Muslim wife, and another Muslim wife of his, Jind Kaur, ordered the construction of a library at Data Darbar.

## The Maharaja and Lahore

Under him Lahore began to be peopled again. Though none of its ruined outer quarters was resettled, the city within the walls was well peopled once more in the years of his rule, his courtiers laid out large gardens on the outer ruins, and until his death the city grew more populous day by day. He kept the city wall in constant, daily repair, had a deep ditch of masonry dug on all four sides, and put at each gate a bridge and a second solid gate, so that no enemy could ever take the city; he also ordered the deserted quarters resettled. One Nura, who fed his wheat on milk, won access to the court and an appointment to the digging of the ditch. Inside the Khizri Gate the Maharaja kept two lions in cages, for which the guards of the gate answered; the cages went under the British, but the name Shiran Wala Darwaza, the Gate of the Lions, stuck.

In the fort he renamed the Moti Masjid, the Pearl Mosque, as the Moti Mandir, the Pearl Temple, and kept the treasury of the state there, and in the 1880s the English government still kept the treasury of the Punjab province in it. He also restored the Shalamar garden.

He was a patron of shrines and holy men in and around Lahore. It was said of the *majzub* Subah Shah, one spiritually intoxicated and absorbed in divine love, that the outward king was the Maharaja; all the pillars of the state, with the Maharaja, held Subah Shah in regard, and Subah Shah prayed for him. The Maharaja and his successors down to Duleep Singh came every Saturday and gave *nazr*, offerings, and the *sajjada nishin*, the head of the shrine, received four or five thousand rupees each Saturday. Data Darbar received a yearly sum and some sweets, and the shrine of Mauj Darya a monthly allowance; he granted a well at the site of Ghore Shah; at the site of Mani Singh a *sadabart*, a standing distribution of food to the poor, of twenty five rupees a day ran under him; he visited Bhai Sahib Singh time and again; and he remained a heartfelt devotee of Syed Ali Shah all his life.

He also set up a *dera*, or camp, of four hundred *granthis*, readers of the Sikh scripture, with five standards, a *nagara*, or great kettledrum, and five hundred men with *qawwals*, singers of *qawwali*, the devotional music of the Sufis. The samadhi at Shahid Ganj was completed in his reign, and he gave a fund and a *jagir*, a grant of land revenue, to the Baoli Sahib. When he became sovereign of the Punjab he spent large amounts of money on the renovation and expansion of many gurdwaras, attaching vast tracts of land to them so that their revenues could fund them. In his time Baba Sahib Singh Bedi was allotted vast tracts of land around Gujranwala and Lahore, on which he founded Bedian. The gurdwara of Panja Sahib was extended under him, and its frescoes were part of the original complex he built; he first erected the present structure of Dera Sahib and raised the present gurdwara at Mandi Mangat; and in his time the village of Maraka was repopulated a few kilometres from the ruins of the old one.

He also took from older monuments. In his time red, black and marble stones were torn down all around Lahore. At the tomb of the Emperor Jahangir the railings of marble, amber and red stone, worth lakhs of rupees, a lakh being a hundred thousand, were taken to the Darbar Sahib at Amritsar and set in the Ram Bagh; he had the lattices of the minarets pulled out, and the *jagirs* of the tomb's *mujawirs*, its attendants, were confiscated, leaving only the garden. The marble slabs and lattices of Mian Mir's tomb went to a *baradari*, a pavilion of twelve openings, at Amritsar, and the marble of the Sawan-Bhadon *baradaris* of the Shalamar garden was set in the Ram Bagh at Amritsar. The tomb and mosque of Shaikh Ashraf were demolished on his orders, and he had the stone pulled out of the tomb of Pir Hadi Rahnuma, on the road from Anarkali to Mian Mir, which had once been a building of stone. He had all the marble, worth lakhs of rupees, taken off the graves of Zakariya Khan; mosques were used as powder magazines; the mosque and tomb of Shah Sharaf were demolished for the glacis of the Bhati Gate, after which the coffin was reburied and, by his order, a small masonry platform was built over it; and Mohalla Saudagran was expropriated for the gurdwara of Guru Ram Das.

Beyond Lahore, in 1810 he seized the extensive holdings of the shrine at Pakpattan and allowed its *diwan*, the head of the shrine, and his retainers only a thousand rupees a year for their maintenance, drawn from the town duties of Pakpattan, with a fourth share of four small villages nearby. In his time the Sikhs took over the mosque at Shaheed Ganj, placed the Guru Granth Sahib, the Sikh scripture, inside it and made it a gurdwara, and the Muslims could do nothing then. It is said that the Badshahi Mosque was used as a stable for horses.

He was a devotee of Lahore's *majzubs*. He had complete faith in the majzub Mastan Shah Lahori and would give him thousands of rupees, which the majzub would not take; once he seated Mastan Shah on his own elephant to bring him to the fort, and the majzub jumped off. He was the devoted follower of Mai Bhagi Lahori, a *majzuba*, a woman absorbed in divine love, and though she abused him he would not leave her. He first offered her five hundred rupees as *nazr*, and she struck him with bricks more than once; the appointment of a Hindu pandit to a *diwani* post, in the civil administration of his court, was credited to her word. She refused Dhian Singh and Suchet Singh but came with Kharak Singh and stayed eleven days, a building at Kot Saeed was put up for ten thousand rupees, and her fame spread because the ruler was her devotee.

A story is told of him at the tomb of Mian Mir. He came to strip the tomb, it is said, but his horse Laili bolted, and he fell and was hurt; now, he said, he had been punished for his disrespect. In the story he gave five hundred rupees as an offering, ordered the tomb whitewashed, and remained a devotee of the saint and respectful of its *mujawirs*.

## His Death and His Heirs

Ranjit Singh died on 27 June 1839. Ill and near his end, it is told, he sent for the majzub Taj Shah Lahori and asked him for his health, and the *faqir*, the poor wandering mystic, answered that death is for everyone, as the Maharaja's father and the faqir's own father had died, and that sandalwood should be brought to burn him. Hearing this, the story goes on, the Maharaja lost hope, and as Taj Shah left the fort he gave up his life. Tradition holds, too, that on that very day the faqir foretold the ruin of the Sikh kingdom: it would last nine more years, and then the Firangis, the Europeans, would be masters of the Punjab. Taj Shah himself died in the year 1261 of the Islamic calendar.

At the Maharaja's death the buildings of the Baoli Sahib were still going up, and they were left unfinished. His son, Maharaja Kharak Singh, sat after him on the throne of the government of the Punjab. After the Maharaja's death the city did suffer slight blows at the hands of the Sikh army itself.

After the deaths of Kharak Singh and Nau Nihal Singh, Rani Chand Kaur became mistress of the kingdom, set on the throne by the Sandhanwalias, close kin of hers and of the Maharaja, and Raja Dhian Singh withdrew in anger to Jammu. Dhian Singh told Sher Singh that, God willing, he would soon be ruler of the Punjab. Sher Singh came to the throne and was murdered, and his killers were destroyed after a siege of Lahore Fort and a great battle. Raja Hira Singh, minister to Duleep Singh, fled on the pretext of a hunt on the day he was killed; the Sikhs pursued and killed him and made Jawahar Singh minister, and the heads of Raja Hira Singh and Pandit Jalla were hung from a gutter spout.

A treaty was made in 1846, with a Resident at Lahore, and in 1849 the British annexed the Punjab. In December 1849 Duleep Singh, the last Maharaja of the Punjab, left Lahore, and a *qitʿa*, a short poem, was written on his departure. When he had been exiled and English rule was complete, the troops left the Badshahi Mosque.

## The Samadhi

Construction of the samadhi began in the month after the Maharaja's death, under his son Kharak Singh, and it was largely completed by 1848 under his youngest son, Duleep Singh; the British funded the final repairs after their annexation of the Punjab in 1849.

The building brings together Sikh, Hindu and Islamic styles. Gilded, fluted domes and cupolas rise above doorways of red sandstone carved with images of Ganesha, of Devi, the Goddess, and of Brahma, though accounts of which deities they show differ. At the heart of the monument, beneath a marble pavilion inlaid with *pietra dura*, a work of cut and fitted coloured stones, a marble urn shaped like a lotus holds the Maharaja's ashes; accounts of how his remains are marked differ. The frescoes that survive inside were painted between 1839 and 1849.

Around the Maharaja's urn, smaller urns commemorate the four queens and seven attendants who died on his funeral pyre by the custom of *sati*, the death of a widow on her husband's funeral pyre, a custom that came from the ancient Rajput tradition; accounts of who they were differ. With them stand the two smallest memorials, those of two pigeons, which, it is told, came flying at the burning of the body, fell into the pyre and were burned with the Maharaja.

As it stood in the nineteenth century, the samadhi was entered from the east. The gateway was of two storeys, with red stone and floral work above, and shopkeepers kept shops in the cells of the first storey of the eastern wall. Five steps of red stone led up to the doorway, which was framed in red stone. Inside the east gate were a room facing south, a stair facing north and an upper door framed in red stone, and the courtyard was floored with lime. South of the gate lay a *dalan*, a hall open on one side, of five arches, its ceiling of painted and coloured lime plaster, with two rooms, in one of which the income of the place was collected.

The samadhi chamber stood on a grand platform about five feet high, faced with marble to the east and north and with brick to the south and west, and hollow beneath like a cellar; a door on the west led to a cool chamber below. Above a course of marble rose the building itself, of brick in white plaster. Each of its four sides had three doors framed in marble, the middle one large and the two beside it small; only the middle door on the east was kept open, with a large slab of black stone before it. Within was a roofed hall, at its centre a grand marble *baradari* with a *ghulam-gardish*, a covered gallery, running round all four sides, and at the centre of the baradari a marble pavilion whose columns were marble but whose dome was of brick. Over the baradari rose two domes, one below and one above; the floor was of marble, and the ceiling of the great dome was set with mirrors and elaborate gilding.

The gallery was divided into eight bays, four at the corners and four on the sides, with floors and walls of brick but glass work on the ceilings, the four corner ceilings gilded and the four side ones white. A stair of red stone in the south west corner rose to the second storey, which had two balconies on each side, each of three doors, with a latticed bay of three openings between them, railings of red stone in the lattices of the balconies and of marble within the openings. A brick stair from the same corner led to a large roofed terrace with small domes along the parapets and four at the corners, and at the centre of each wall a domed, elongated sitting place of eight doors. Above the great dome stood another lofty baradari, all of brick, with three doors to each side, crowned by a round dome bearing a gilded finial, with four small gilded domes at its corners. Four corner towers carried *kalas*, finials in the form of a pot.

Images of Hindu deities had their place in the samadhi. Inside stood a marble image of Devi Mai, the Mother Goddess, and in a southern niche a marble image of Hanuman. In the 1880s a marble image of the Devi stood with great reverence in one of the chambers of the gate range, her throne and canopy all of marble and the Devi mounted on a marble lion. Rani Jindan, mother of Maharaja Duleep Singh, had kept it in the fort, and when she was exiled she sent it to the samadhi; a separate *pujari*, or priest, was appointed for it. In the dalan stood a movable marble baradari, one that could be carried wherever one wished, holding an image of a *devi*, or goddess, a bull and a marble lion.

In the nineteenth century the whole place had a keeper, who managed the samadhi, with a servant attending there on his behalf. Today the Evacuee Trust Property Board administers the site.

The grand marble baradari at the centre first stood on eight marble columns, which cracked after a few years under the weight of the dome, and the building came near to collapse. Seeing its state, the authorities ordered the Executive Engineer of the Buildings Division at Lahore, Kanhaiya Lal, to secure it; he added eight brick columns to the eight, making sixteen, and bound the cracked ones with very strong iron rings.

Adjoining Ranjit Singh's samadhi, smaller ones honour his son Kharak Singh and his grandson Nau Nihal Singh; their joint building stands to its west, on the western edge of the same platform that lies beneath it. The Maharaja's wives have samadhis of their own, as separate monuments, among them the samadhi of Rani Nakain and Rani Chand Kaur.

In 2011 the samadhi was a white building, adorned with detailed frescoes and other artwork, standing on top of another single storey building painted yellow, in a courtyard with rooms for the pilgrims on its sides. Two loudspeakers had been placed on top of the samadhi, facing the ground, opposite the mosque and the city. In the hall the pavilion stood at the centre on a raised platform, its pillars of white marble. On ordinary days a portrait of the Maharaja, dressed in a white robe and with a long white beard, was placed at the pavilion beside that of his youngest son, Duleep Singh, the last Maharaja of the Punjab; on the anniversary that year the portraits had been removed. The dome was decorated with coloured glass, green, orange and red, in geometric patterns; niches held frescoes; and the walls of the balconies showed scenes of the court and religious drawings from Hindu mythology, among them Krishna, Ganesha, Ram and Sita. The paintings had faded, some had disappeared altogether, and visitors had noted down their names and numbers on them, disfiguring them.

The art historian Dr Nadhra Shahbaz Naeem Khan uncovered guardian figures beneath the whitewash on the building's eastern facade, but later coats of whitewash have covered and lost this decoration again.

## Devotion and Pilgrimage

For decades after it was built, the Guru Granth Sahib was installed and read in the samadhi, and today the samadhi remains an active place of pilgrimage, especially around the anniversary of Ranjit Singh's death and in the spring season of Vaisakhi, the harvest festival of the Punjab, when Sikh visitors, delegations from India among them, gather to pay homage.

In June 2011 the anniversary of his death was marked with an *Akhand Path*, an unbroken reading of the whole Guru Granth Sahib, held in honour of Maharaja Ranjit Singh, also known as Sher-e-Punjab, the Lion of Punjab. About a hundred and fifty people, all men, were in the hall for its conclusion. It fell twelve days after the commemoration of Guru Arjan's death anniversary in the same complex; the earlier pilgrims had left, and a new set had arrived from India. A police official sat on top of the samadhi with his gun in front of him. After the ceremony *langar*, the free communal kitchen, was served in two rows, with dal, rice, rotis and water, and tea boiled with sugar and ginger, which is highly popular in East Punjab.

Among the pilgrims was Amanpreet Singh, 35, a trader from Ludhiana, on his first visit to Pakistan: "The Maharaja was a great leader ... There was no crime". Jaswant Singh, 54, and a friend had come for the first time from the city of Qadian in East Punjab.

## The Setting

The samadhi stands outside the Roshnai Gate, against the city wall and wall to wall with the Badshahi Mosque, and its wall lies to the west of the samadhi of Guru Arjan. The Roshnai Gate, one of the thirteen gateways into the ancient walled city of Lahore, lies between the Alamgiri Gate and the Badshahi Mosque. Lamps were lit all along its passageway, hence its name, the gate of lights, and it was once reserved for the prince and the princess, who would go from the fort to the city through it. Behind it lies the Heera Mandi bazaar, which in 2011 was still one of the largest markets for traditional instruments in the city.

In the middle of these monuments lies the Hazuri Bagh, a small open ground, with a single storey pavilion set symmetrically at the centre of the garden and four lanes leading towards it. Ranjit Singh laid out the garden and held his *darbar* on the *shah-nashin*, the raised royal seat, of its baradari. The pavilion is said to have been built from part of an older tomb, though accounts differ over whose.

## Legacy

Beyond its place in Sikh devotion, the monument is listed by the Punjab Directorate General of Archaeology among the province's protected religious monuments of the Sikh period. The ruler it commemorates was at once the man under whom Lahore revived, a patron of Muslim shrines, and the man who carried the marble of Mughal tombs to Amritsar. He is remembered, too, through the faqirs he could not buy: the majzub who refused his thousands, the majzuba who abused him and whom he would not leave, and the faqir who, it is told, bade him send for sandalwood.

In 2011 his memory was kept on his anniversary by pilgrims from across the border, with the *Akhand Path* read in his honour and the *langar* served afterwards. For the city that the Maharaja once ruled, as one visitor wrote, "he is an alien, his memory kept alive not by people of the land he once ruled but by people from the other side of the border"; it had become rare to meet a Sikh in Lahore, a city once ruled by a Sikh and the seat of Sikh political strength.
