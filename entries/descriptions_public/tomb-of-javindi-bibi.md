## Overview

The Tomb of Bibi Jawindi at Uch Sharif, in southern Punjab, is among the most beautiful and most poignant monuments of Islamic architecture in Pakistan. A jewel of blue and white glazed tilework, half ruined by the flooding of the river, it has become the very emblem of Uch Sharif's medieval glory. Bibi Jawindi was a saintly woman of the great Bukhari family of sayyids, descendants of the Prophet Muhammad, and her ornate tomb, raised in the late fifteenth century, stands within the famous cluster of tombs in the Bukhari quarter of the town. Pilgrims honour it as the resting place of a holy woman, and together with the tombs around it, it draws lovers of architecture and history as much as pilgrims.

The tomb is the finest of a group of contiguous mausolea that together form one of the most important surviving ensembles of funerary architecture from before the Mughals in the subcontinent, and the monuments of Uch Sharif have been placed on UNESCO's Tentative List of World Heritage sites. Uch itself is called the city of saints.

## Bibi Jawindi and Her Tomb

Bibi Jawindi is remembered as a pious woman of the Jalali Bukhari line, venerated for her holiness. Accounts of her place in the family differ, but by tradition she was a great-granddaughter of the celebrated saint Makhdoom Jahaniyan Jahangasht.

Tradition holds that her tomb was built around 1493, in her honour, by an Iranian prince named Dilshad. It is an octagonal, tiered building crowned by a dome and adorned with turrets at its corners. Its surfaces are sheathed in exquisite *kashi*, glazed tilework, of cobalt blue, turquoise and white, the work for which Uch and Multan are famed. The tomb is a masterpiece of the regional style that flourished around the tombs of the Suhrawardi saints, followers of one of the orders of Sufis, or Muslim mystics.

Over the centuries the changing course of the river undermined the building, and tradition holds that a great part of it collapsed in a flood. There is no settled view of when that flood came; it is placed as early as the sixteenth century and as late as the nineteenth. What remains is the ravishing half of a tomb that visitors see today, a fragment whose surviving beauty only sharpens the sense of what has been lost.

## The Monuments of Uch Sharif

Bibi Jawindi's tomb does not stand alone. Among its neighbours in the Bukhari quarter of Uch are the tombs associated with Baha al-Halim, a teacher of the line, and with the tomb's own builder. So exceptional are these monuments that they have been the subject of international conservation efforts.

In the early twenty first century the tomb stood on a mound used as a graveyard, reached through the market, and hers was arguably the most important of the three buildings on it. Half the structures had been razed, and iron rods protected them. What remained was made of glazed Multani tiles, the tiles of Multan, coloured in blue and white, with a white dome on top. Several graves on the mound were covered, haphazardly, with blue and white tiles recovered from these historic shrines.

The river Sutlej once flowed past these buildings. It is called the lost river of Pakistan, for it was lost for ever after the Indus Water Treaty was signed between India and Pakistan in 1960.

## The Setting

Uch Sharif lies in the southern region of Punjab, near Bahawalpur, where the cultures of Punjab and Sindh merge and the language is Siraiki. The name Uch means high or great. An ancient centre of learning, trade and pilgrimage, with magnificent blue and white tiled mosques and desolate tombs, the town is built on a hill overlooking the confluence of the Sutlej and Chenab rivers. The older city stands on a mound, with the massive *havelis*, or great mansions, of the Gilanis and the Makhdooms, the families who keep the town's shrines. The south of the Punjab keeps more traces of religious and cultural syncretism, the blending of faiths and customs, than its northern and central regions.

Armies passed through Uch again and again. Shihab al-Din took it on his first campaign and came back by way of Multan and Uch on his second; Mirza Pir Muhammad brought the fortresses of Uch and Multan into his possession; and Khawas Khan's pursuit went as far as Multan and Uch. In the reign of the emperor Aurangzeb the place was known as the township of Uch, and the bounds of Multan and Uch formed one of the limits of the *suba*, or province, of Thatta.

## The Saints of Uch

Uch has its own founding legend, told in connection with Shaikh Safiu'd-Din Kaziruni. A shaikh, a Sufi master, was ordered to mount a camel and travel wherever the animal led him, and to remain where it finally halted. The camel halted in the middle of a desert, and there the shaikh founded the town later called Uch. It is a tale typical of the medieval stories told of Sufis. Other tales tell of a flying contest with a yogi, a Hindu ascetic, at Uch, and the stories of three generations of the Sufis of Uch all recount conflicts with Hindu yogis.

The first Bukhari saint of Uch was Jalaluddin Surkhposh Bukhari, who died in 1291. For a while Uch was the centre of the Suhrawardi order, thanks chiefly to the active and pious Jalaluddin Makhdum-i Jahaniyan, who died in 1383. It was at Uch, too, that Farid ad-Din is said to have kept his inverted fast of forty days, in the well of the town's Hajj mosque: every night for forty days he was suspended from a branch of the tree that overhung the well, and at daybreak he was pulled out.

Uch was also the gate by which the Qadiri order, the Sufi order founded by Abd al-Qadir Gilani, entered the subcontinent. Its first missionaries settled at Uch in the late fifteenth century, and from there the order spread. Hazrat Sayyid Muhammad Ghaus Uchi Jili Gilani is remembered as the first of this line to come to the Punjab; he came by way of Khurasan to Multan and settled at Uch. The four sons of Hazrat Muhammad Ghaus lie at rest at Uch Sharif, beside the tomb of Shah Muhammad Ghaus. The first Qadiri *khanqah*, or Sufi lodge, at Uch was founded by Shaykh Muhammad al-Husseini al-Jilani, a native of Turkey who settled there with his family, and his son, 'Abd al-Qadir Jilani Thani, is the famous Qadiri saint of Uch Sharif. In the nineteenth century the sayyids of Uch were famous, and on any disputed descent their word was final.

Uch has a place, too, in the story of Guru Nanak, the founder of the Sikh faith, who is said to have come to Uch from Kartarpur at a time when Uch Sharif was one of the most famous Muslim shrines in southern Punjab. In the early twenty first century a well hidden in a field, abandoned, with juice cartons and wrappers floating in its black water, was shown as one of his traces, and beside it a grassy mound marked the site of the Gurdwara of Nanak, a Sikh place of worship, of which nothing remained.

Uch is linked, too, with the poet Bulle Shah, whose mentor, Inayat Shah, belonged to Uch. At Kasur, Muhammad Bakhsh, of a hereditary family of *qawwals*, singers of *qawwali*, the devotional music of the Sufis, said of his family: "We came with Bulle Shah's family from Uch Sharif, and we have learned his poetry from our ancestors, who have always sung here."

## Devotion and Legacy

As the resting place of a holy woman of the revered Bukhari line, Bibi Jawindi's tomb is honoured by pilgrims who come to Uch to visit the shrines of the sayyids, and it forms part of the sacred fabric of the town. Its fame today, though, is above all as a monument: a breathtaking, wounded masterpiece that stands for the vanished splendour of medieval Uch and for the artistry of the tile workers who adorned the shrines of the Punjab's saints. It is at once a place of devotion, a heritage treasure and an elegy in brick and glaze.

The tomb is also the place where one modern book on Sufism in Pakistan begins. In April 1996 its author was at the *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of a saint's death, at Allahabad, a rural village sixty miles southwest of Bahawalpur in southern Punjab, and on a day's journey from there he wandered through Uch Sharif with his companions. Near the crumbling tomb of Bibi Jawindi an elderly gentleman approached them, and the author photographed him. Disciples in Lahore later identified the man in the photograph as the deceased spiritual master Shaykh Wahid Bakhsh Sial Rabbani; in the author's telling, the shaykh had been dead for more than a year when the photograph was taken.
