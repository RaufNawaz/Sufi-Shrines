## Overview

The tomb of Hazrat Baba Shah Kamal lies in Muslim Town, near Ichhra in Lahore, within an old graveyard that still bears his name and lends it to the housing scheme around it. Shah Kamal is remembered above all as the elder brother of the celebrated Sufi, or Muslim mystic, Baba Shah Jamal, whose shrine stands nearby. Local devotees gather at his tomb every Thursday evening for drumming and devotional music, and once a year for his *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of the saint's death.

Far less is known of Shah Kamal than of his famous brother. He is remembered as a devout Sayyid, given to spiritual rapture and famed for his wonders, who settled with his brother at Ichhra and was buried in a domed tomb raised by the disciples of his family. That tomb has outlived the fall of its own enclosure wall, and the city that once lay three miles away now surrounds it. It now stands within a complex of mosque and graveyard, catalogued as a monument of the historic period, in a graveyard still used for burials.

## The Saint

Much of what is remembered of Shah Kamal is bound up with his brother. The two were the sons of Syed Ahmad Shah, and the shrine traces the family's descent from Jalaluddin Surkh-Posh Bukhari of Uch Sharif. Accounts of the family's ancestry differ in detail, but Shah Kamal is remembered as a Sayyid, a descendant of the Prophet Muhammad. Their father himself instructed both his sons in Islamic theology, and the two brothers afterwards settled together in the Ichhra area of Lahore. In the nineteenth century the two brothers were known as a pair, the one Shah Jamal and the other Shah Kamal.

By the late nineteenth century Shah Kamal was remembered as a devout man, given to *jazb*, the rapture of a soul drawn towards God. There is no settled view of his order, but he is most often counted a Suhrawardi, a follower of the Sufi order founded by Shihab al-Din Suhrawardi, and he is said to have come of a Suhrawardi family. Local devotion honours him with the double title Qadiri Suhrawardi, which joins to the Suhrawardi name that of the Qadiri order, founded by Abd al-Qadir Gilani, and so suggests an affiliation with the same overlapping Sufi orders associated with his brother's shrine nearby. No master of his own and no chain of spiritual descent are remembered for him.

He is not to be confused with the other saints who shared his name. Shah Kamal of Kaithal, a Qadiri master in the spiritual line of the Mujaddid, the renewer of the faith, is remembered quite separately from the brother of Shah Jamal, and so are Kamal Bukhari, called Pir Jahaniyan, a Sayyid Kamal, and Sayyid Kamal Shah Chishti, outside the Kashmiri Gate. Nor is he the Sayyid Kamil Shah Lahori of the village of Babu Sabu, whose name is Kamil, not Kamal.

Accounts of his dates differ, and no year of his death is settled. His life is generally placed in the age of the Mughal emperors, between the late sixteenth and the seventeenth century, much the same years as his brother's. He was buried here, close to the brother with whom he had settled at Ichhra.

## The Shrine

The tomb stands within the historic Shah Kamal graveyard, which is still in active use as a burial ground. In the nineteenth century it lay outside the city, near the villages of Ichhra and Rawan, three miles south of Lahore, right beside Shah Jamal's *khanqah*, the lodge where a Sufi's disciples gather.

In the nineteenth century a high wall enclosed the tomb, though part of it had fallen. Within it rose an octagonal platform and an octagonal dome. There had once been four doors, but three had been closed, leaving only the southern door open. The grave itself was finished in lime plaster and was broken in places, and a *kikar*, a thorny acacia, grew to the north of it. For a long time a man named Nihal, known as Nihal Shah, had swept the tomb and lit its lamps.

In the 1880s it was a grand tomb of *pakka*, or fired, brick, standing on an octagonal platform of the same solid brickwork, with its octagonal dome at the centre. Inside, a second platform of fired brick carried Shah Kamal's grave, itself solidly built. There were four doors, one on each side, three of them closed and only the southern one open. The tomb had been raised not by a ruler or a state but by the *murids*, the pledged disciples, of the saint's family.

The place had once been much frequented. By the 1880s its enclosure wall, which had been very high and solidly built, had fallen, but the tomb itself still stood, though it was in need of repair. In a city where building after building was pulled down, its survival was put down to its distance from Lahore: had it stood among the city's buildings, it too would have been pulled down.

Today the shrine forms part of a complex of mosque and graveyard at Karnalpura, Muslim Town, Combo Colony, Lahore. It belongs to the Auqaf Department, the Punjab government's department of religious endowments, which lists it in its Lahore Zone as the "Darbar & Jamia Masjid Hazrat Shah Kamal", the shrine and congregational mosque of Hazrat Shah Kamal. The Punjab Department of Archaeology and Museums formally catalogues it as a monument of the historic period. It is counted among the historic monuments of Mughal Lahore, and its condition is rated "Satisfactory".

## Devotion and Festivals

The tomb continues to draw local devotees. Every Thursday evening they gather for drumming on the *dhol*, a large barrel drum, and for *qawwali*, the devotional music of the Sufis, echoing in more modest form the famous *dhamal*, ecstatic devotional dancing to the beat of the drum, for which Shah Jamal's shrine is known.

Once a year an *ʿurs* is held in his honour, drawing pilgrims from the surrounding neighbourhoods. By the late nineteenth century he was famous for his *karamat*, the miraculous deeds and spiritual gifts of a saint, and for his marvels, though little is told of what they were.

## The Setting

Where the tomb once stood among villages three miles south of Lahore, it now lies within the city, in Muslim Town near Ichhra, with his brother's shrine close by and a housing scheme named after his graveyard all around it. In Lahore today the city's Qalandars, wandering dervishes, are still associated with its great Qadiri saints, among them Shah Jamal, Shah Hussayn and Miyan Mir.

A legend of the founding of the towns round about is tied to Rawan, the village beside which the tomb stands. The tale is told in more than one way; in the version tied to this place, it is said that Raja Ram Chand had three sons: one built Lahore, a second settled another town, and the third founded Rawan. The story ends with the pious formula "God knows best". It is a legend of the place rather than of the saint.

## Legacy

Together with his brother's shrine, the tomb of Shah Kamal helps anchor the religious identity of this part of Lahore as a historic centre of Sufi devotion. It reflects a wider pattern in the city, where whole neighbourhoods have taken their names and character from the saints buried within them, and the graveyard that holds his tomb still lends his name to the housing scheme around it. Raised by the disciples of his family rather than by any ruler, it has outlasted the fall of its own enclosure wall. In the nineteenth century only its southern door stood open, and for a long time a single keeper swept it and lit its lamps. Today it stands within a complex of mosque and graveyard in the care of the Auqaf Department, still visited on Thursday evenings and at its yearly *ʿurs*.
