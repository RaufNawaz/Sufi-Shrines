## Overview

At Golra Sharif, at the foot of the Margalla Hills on the edge of Islamabad, stands the shrine of Hazrat Pir Sayyid Meher Ali Shah, who died in 1937. He was one of the most influential Chishti saints and scholars of modern South Asia, a master of both the inward path and the religious sciences. He is remembered as a great defender of orthodox Sunni belief, a renowned orator and author, and the founder of a living Sufi centre whose successors remain prominent to this day. His *darbar*, or shrine, is among the most visited of the Pothohar region and of the area around Islamabad and Rawalpindi.

It was here at Golra, in the early twentieth century, that Meher Ali Shah established his prominence. Today his shrine is a large and busy complex, kept by his descendants.

## The Saint

Meher Ali Shah was born at Golra in 1859, though accounts of the year differ, into a Sayyid family of learning and sanctity, descended from the Prophet Muhammad. He received a thorough classical education in the Islamic sciences at the seminaries of the age and became a scholar of the first rank.

On the Sufi path he was initiated into the Chishti order, the Sufi order prominent in South Asia that was founded by Moinuddin Chishti. He took spiritual allegiance in the line of Khwaja Shamsuddin Sialvi of Sial Sharif, his direct *pir*, or spiritual guide, who died in 1882. He was also affiliated with Muhammad Ali, one of whose disciples was Habib Ali Shah, who in turn was the master of Sufi Saheb, who died in 1916. It is through this second line that a branch of the Chishti order is traced as far as South Africa.

Combining deep mystical experience with formidable learning, he became a widely sought guide and teacher. In a conflict over the succession at Taunsa, the succession from Shaykh Sulayman divided into two branches, and one of them went to Meher Ali Shah. He is counted, with Khwaja Hasan Nizami and the Naqshbandi activist Pir Jama'at Ali Shah, among the notable Sufi masters of twentieth century South Asia who pursued reformist agendas. He died at Golra in 1937 and was buried there.

## Public Life and Disciples

Meher Ali Shah is especially remembered for his part in the religious controversies of his time, above all his prominent stand against the claims of Mirza Ghulam Ahmad of Qadian. He opposed them in debate and in writing, most famously in his work *Saif-e-Chishtiyai*. Haydar Ali Shah, too, opposed the Ahmadi movement, but Meher Ali Shah was said to be one of those most influential in marginalising and stigmatising Ghulam Ahmad of Qadian.

At the same time he was attracted to the Khilafat movement, which gathered around the largely symbolic figure of the Ottoman caliph, and he permitted his disciples to support it. The masters of Golra and of Jalalpur Sharif had different roles: Meher Ali Shah took on the historical persona of a Sufi *pir* thoroughly engaged in both the regional and the international politics of his time.

Among his disciples was Khwaja Hasan Nizami. At the age of 24, of his own will and believing he had a spiritual sign from Baba Farid, Hasan Nizami became a *murid*, a disciple pledged to a Sufi master, of Meher Ali Shah. Meher Ali Shah initiated him and in time made him a *khalifa*, one of his successors.

## Learning and Poetry

Pir Meher Ali Shah united the roles of scholar, poet and *shaykh*, or spiritual master. He wrote in defence of Sunni doctrine and on the love of the Prophet, and he was himself a composer of devotional verse: his Punjabi and Persian *naat*, poems in praise of the Prophet, are cherished by his followers and remain among the loved poetry of the tradition. His teaching held firmly to the union of the Sharia, the religious law of Islam, and the Sufi path, insisting that mystical devotion must rest on sound belief and on the example of the Prophet. He drew to himself a vast following, and the guidance he gave, sober, learned and centred on the love of God and His Messenger, shaped the religious life of the region.

His Punjabi verse is sung by *qawwals*, the singers of *qawwali*, the devotional music of the Sufis. In their performances they move between the Persian verses of Jami, Hafiz and Rumi, the Punjabi poetry of Meher Ali Shah and Ghulam Farid, and the classical Hindi of Amir Khusrau, among others.

His memory is kept in homes as well as at the shrine. In Rawalpindi, the living room wall of Mama Ji carries popular prints of the saints Abdul Qadir Jilani of Baghdad, Pir Meher Ali Shah of Golra and Baba Lal Shah.

## The Shrine

The shrine of Meher Ali Shah at Golra is a large and busy complex. It is kept by his descendants, who have continued as its *sajjada nashins*, the hereditary custodians who sit on the saint's prayer carpet, and as noted religious figures. Among his successors at the shrine was Pir Ghulam Mohyuddin, known as Babuji, and the keepers who came after maintain the *darbar* and its teaching mission.

The shrine holds the five daily prayers, gatherings of *naat* and *qawwali*, and its *langar*, the free communal kitchen. Its annual *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of the saint's death, draws great crowds from across the Pothohar and beyond. As a centre of both pilgrimage and learning, Golra Sharif carries the saint's legacy of devotion joined to scholarship.

Golra Sharif also holds manuscripts. Two Persian works are recorded there as unique single copies: a biography of Hujwiri, and the *Albab-i thulatha* of Muhammad Chishti.

Golra Sharif is counted, with Nurpur Shahan and Pir Baba, among the Muslim pilgrimage places of northern Pakistan. Like Golra, the village of Nurpur Shahan and the shrine of Barri Imam stand on the outskirts of Islamabad, the federal capital of Pakistan, whose twin city is Rawalpindi.

## Legacy

Pir Meher Ali Shah stands among the towering figures of modern Sunni Sufism in South Asia, a saint whose combination of mystical stature, scholarship and public defence of orthodox belief gave him an authority felt far beyond his own region. Through his writings, his poetry in praise of the Prophet and the living line of his successors at Golra, his influence endures, and his shrine at the edge of the capital remains one of the great centres of Chishti devotion in Pakistan.

That authority reached to the controversy with Qadian and to the politics of the Khilafat movement, to his disciple and *khalifa* Khwaja Hasan Nizami, and, through a second line of affiliation, to a branch of the Chishti order traced as far as South Africa. His Punjabi verse lives on in the repertoire of the *qawwals*, and his picture on the walls of devotees' homes.
