## Overview

At Bhit Shah, in the Matiari region of Sindh, near the town of Hala, stands the shrine of Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai, who lived from 1689 to 1752: the greatest poet of the Sindhi language and one of the supreme Sufi voices of the subcontinent. His tomb, built on the sandhill, the *bhit*, from which he takes his name, is the cultural and spiritual heart of Sindh. Every night his verse is sung there in the tradition of the *Shah jo Raag*, and that singing is among the most moving sounds in all of Sufi devotion.

His life's work, the *Shah Jo Risalo*, a vast compendium of mystical poetry set to music, is to Sindh what the *Divan* of Hafiz is to Persia: scripture, songbook and national treasure at once. In Sindh and the rest of Pakistan, and among Sindhis in India and the wider diaspora, it is acknowledged as the greatest classic of Sindhi literature, and all Sindhis acclaim him as the greatest poet of Sindh and of the Sindhi language.

His tomb is a meeting place of communities, a Sufi shrine visited by Muslims and Hindus alike. The pilgrimage he is said to have made to Hinglaj links it with the shrine of the goddess Hinglaj Devi in Balochistan, and many legends tell of a connection between the saint and the goddess. It is known in the region as a *jamali*, or aesthetic, shrine, for the falsetto in which his *faqirs*, the wandering Sufis devoted to him, sing his verse there.

## The Poet Saint

Abdul Latif was born into a Sayyid family of Sindh, descendants of a line of saints. Accounts of his dates differ slightly, but most give the year of his birth as 1689 and of his death as 1752; accounts of his birthplace differ too, but it is most often given as Hala. The title Shah marks his standing as a Sayyid, one claiming direct descent from the Prophet Muhammad. His family was one of the many lineages of hereditary Sufi saints settled in the countryside of Sindh, where such *pirs*, holy men with a special spiritual authority, have always enjoyed great prestige and power. It was a notable Sayyid family descended from Ali, so that he was the scion of the most illustrious religious house of Sindh tracing a connection to Herat, born to a position of dignity and power, one of the privileged class. His lineage is said to go back to the seventh imam, Musa al-Kazim, though traditions about his mother's family are not unanimous.

His ancestor Shah Abdul Karim of Bulri, who died in 1623, was himself a noted mystic poet, one of the first noted poets in the Sindhi tongue. Shah Abdul Karim's shrine was at Bulri, and in 1940 his name was still held in reverence in Lower Sindh. Accounts differ on how many generations separated the two men. The poet's father, Shah Habib, was a descendant of Shah Abdul Karim, and the boy grew up steeped in devotion and verse. His father is buried at Bhit, a few paces from where the poet's own tomb was later raised, and one verse of the *Risalo* is held to be the poet's reply to an appeal from his father.

He spent nearly all his life within a small compass, living in turn in three villages: Hala Haweli, not far from Matiari, where his father was living; Kotri, close by, where his father went to live; and Bhit Shah. By 1940 both Hala Haweli and Kotri lay in ruins.

As a member of the rural elite he was trained in the Islamic sciences, taught through Arabic and Persian, the standard languages of education, and his poems show a knowledge of both far beyond the ordinary accomplishments of his time, set with quotations in Arabic and allusions to Persian mystical poetry. One of his teachers was Nur Muhammad Bhatti. Tradition holds that three books were always in his hands: the Qur'an, from whose verses he so often quotes in Arabic; the *Masnavi*, the great Persian poem of Jalaluddin Rumi; and the Sindhi verses of his ancestor Shah Karim.

He was not the first in the long line of Sindhi mystical poets. Shah Abdul Karim had written Sindhi poems a century before him, and his older contemporary Shah Inat Rizvi of Nasarpur, who died in 1711, another *pir* with whom he is known to have been in contact, left a collection strikingly similar in scope. Sindh already had an established culture of Sufi poetry in its own language.

His growing following brought him into conflict with the powerful. As the fame of the young man and his saintly way of life spread, and drew *murids*, disciples, to him, his independent ways roused the jealousy of the orthodox hierarchy. He found himself ranged against the Sayyids of Matiari, who for a time won the support of Nur Muhammad Kalhoro, then the most powerful man in Sindh. Nur Muhammad later became convinced of the young man's sincerity and worth, and attached himself to him in friendship. It is told, too, that a Kalhoro prince from whom the poet had become estranged won back his favour by presenting him with a fine copy of Rumi's *Masnavi*.

A friend of his younger days, Mirza Moghul Beg, died an untimely death at the hands of robbers in 1713, and the poet eventually married his daughter. He left no heir. Oral tradition remembers the decorum and dignity of his family life, and remembers him as a man of quiet and unassuming disposition, gentle in manner and speech, with a bent of kindness, compassion and generosity. He is said to have hated cruelty and to have been unable to bear to beat an animal or cause physical pain to man or beast. The same tradition pictures him in his prime as a well set up, handsome, bearded man of average height, with fine black eyes, an intelligent face, a noble forehead and the solemn look of a deep thinking and penetrating mind. In popular pictures he is often shown in the yogic posture, sitting with his head supported by his knees.

In his later years he cut himself off from the old ties and founded a village of his own at Bhit, near the town of Hala, in the true ascetic spirit of the religious hermit. The place took its name, which means sandhill, from the sandhills on which it was built, and he helped to build the village with his own hands. It grew into the settlement of Bhit Shah, 'the Shah's dune', as the place is known in his honour, and there he gathered disciples and musicians about him, composed and sang, and spent the rest of his life.

Among those around him were Tamar and Hashim, the amanuenses who recorded his poems as he delivered them. A great friend of the poet was called Bilal, and another, Inayat, was himself a poet, some of whose verses stand in the *Risalo* as it exists today. One of the Surs makes a little joke about the poet's friend and servant, who, though called stinking, a glutton and ugly, is purified in the company of his master, which is like rose water. His last years were spent in a halo of sanctity, in which the reverence of his followers played an important part, and thousands came to listen to his poetry and to enjoy the beauty of its message.

He seems to have had no formal spiritual master and to have belonged to none of the great Sufi orders, and so he is generally classed as an Uvaisi, the term for those whose spiritual initiation comes directly from divine inspiration, without any saintly human intermediary. Yet his verse quotes with approval the Arabic saying 'Who walks on the Path without shaikh?', and in his instructions to Sassui he uses the famous Sufi saying 'Who has no shaikh, his shaikh is Satan'. He did not call his listeners to rebellion against outward powers, or to the forming of a closely knit mystical community. His mysticism is that of the individual, and it has at its centre only one story: the return of the loving soul to her Lord. Throughout his poetry he preached the Oneness of God.

Asked whether he was a Sunni or a Shia, he is said to have replied that he was between the two. At a later stage of his life he meant to make the pilgrimage not to Mecca but to Karbala, but his followers dissuaded him, insisting that he should not desert them. Instead of going to Karbala, it is said, he composed *Sur Kedaro*, which tells the story of Hasan and Husain.

A verse by his younger contemporary Abdur Rahim Girhori expresses admiration for him, and Girhori may have met him when still a child; Sachal Sarmast met him when still a boy. For his part, he quoted a Persian line usually connected with Shah Inayat of Jhok:

*The head is sacrificed at the Friend's foot, / which is in order* (translated by Annemarie Schimmel)

It is told that shortly before his death he withdrew into solitude. Coming out again, it is said, he performed his ablutions, put on a white sheet and ordered songs to be sung, and when the music stopped he fell into a reverie of divine contemplation. He died at Bhit in 1752, at the age of sixty three, without issue, leaving behind him the thirty Sindhi poems that became the *Risalo*.

He stood at the parting of the ways between the rule of the Mughals and that of the Kalhoras. When Aurangzeb died he was a youth of eighteen, and he was fifty when Nadir Shah sacked Delhi and made Sindh tributary to Persia. Yet his verse contains little or no reference to the political events he witnessed. Little of his own life is documented: he left no notes about his life or about the growth of his mystical ideas, and beyond a few plain facts, much of what is told of him is legend.

## Among the Yogis

As a young man he wandered for years with bands of yogis and ascetics through the deserts and mountains of Sindh and beyond, sharing the life of Hindu and Muslim mendicants alike, and these journeys filled his poetry with the landscapes of Sindh and with a profound sympathy across the boundaries of religion. The wandering yogis crossed Sindh on their pilgrimage to the shrine of the goddess at Hinglaj in Balochistan. Accounts differ on how far and for how long he travelled with them, but he is most often said to have gone with them as far as Hinglaj, a place often named in Sindhi mystical poetry as 'the goal'.

He was fascinated by the *jogis*, as the yogis are called in the vernacular, and saw them as the models of renunciation, as the *jogi* usually is in Sindhi Sufi poetry, and in his poetry above all. The technical words he uses for them show that they were Nathpanthis, followers of the tradition of the Nath yogis, and an important religious community in eighteenth century Sindh.

In the telling of one man, Shaikh Ghulam Hussain, the journeys were nights of song. There were at least fifty places, he said, where the poet used to spend the night: audiences would gather at a camp, a fire would be lit, there would be a *mehfil*, a gathering for music, and when he sang his narratives there would be *faqirs* and dervishes with him.

At Hinglaj a Kohli pilgrim named Putu said: "Long ago Shah Abdul Latif also came here with a group of Yogis. He came here to ask the Mother to help him." Since, as he held, the poet too had walked to Hinglaj, Putu saw it as his duty, his *farz*, to make the pilgrimage, and the men of his group said that one of the main reasons for their journey was the praise of Hinglaj in the poetry of Bhittai.

The yogis live on in his verse. The poem he called *Ramkali*, rendered in English as 'The Holy Men', speaks of Hindu jogis, bairagis, adesis and other religious mendicants together, and some of its passages can refer only to a belief in Islam. The poem counts the ascetics' practice day by day over twelve days. On the seventh day:

*They washed their threads upon the seventh day, / The Sayid says, and stood with folded hands / Before God Indescribable.* (translated by H. T. Sorley)

On the eleventh day, the poem says, there was fulfilment:

*… / Of all Bairagis practised. Holy shrines / The Jogis visited and where there shines / The seat of worship.* (translated by H. T. Sorley)

Sur Ramkali holds the first known Sindhi example of a poem that counts the days of a week or a month. The same poem calls the listener to the wanderers' dwellings, and questions the man who would be an ascetic:

*Come, let us look on the abodes of them / Who pass the day and night in wandering* (translated by H. T. Sorley)

*Why dost thou bore thine ears / If cold thou canst not bear ?* (translated by H. T. Sorley)

The Sur is full of mendicants, *faqirs*, who live as ascetics with slit ears and large rings in their ears. The *Risalo* praises the various groups of Hindu yogis who wandered from place to place in Sindh or beyond as ideal practitioners of the spiritual life, and of the yogi's horn a verse says: "There is nothing like it in the north, in Sindh, or in Hind." The poet praises, too, the bewitching power of the yogis' instruments.

His yogis are also brought into Islam: one verse praises the followers of Shiva 'whose leader is Ali':

*For Sanyasis the path's Ali's field. / They are seated with dust on their heads / Where the plain of Lord Ali's revealed* (translated by H. T. Sorley)

And he warns the yogi against letting himself be made an object of worship:

*Do not let yourself be worshiped, oh yogi, but maintain your yoga. Oh master, to make people your servants is a great fault* (translated by Christopher Shackle)

Another verse on the same theme reads: "Do not let yourself be worshiped, oh yogi, curses on people's devotion… keep your mouth from pretense".

A verse of *Sur Asa* measures Muslim and Hindu alike by sincerity: whether Muslim or Hindu, monotheist or idol worshipper, a man has to be sincere and make no claim to a sanctity he cannot realise.

*You are a liar in infidelity, don't call yourself an infidel: / … / The caste-mark is for those, who are true in their paganism*

Like his Punjabi contemporaries and his successors in Sindh, he accepted the Hindus who were striving on the way to perfection as true pilgrims in the eternal Light.

## The Shah Jo Risalo

His poetry, gathered after his death as the *Shah Jo Risalo*, is organised into *surs*, chapters named for the musical modes, the *raags*, to which they are sung, and each name also points to the chapter's contents. In Sindhi *risalo* means 'the message', and it was the technical term for a collection of poems arranged by musical mode; the first collection to be arranged in nineteen Surs was called a Risalo for that reason. The *Risalo* has thirty Surs, and each ends with a *wai*, a longer poem with one main line that is repeated, in singing, after every line. Many of the melodic modes to which some of the Surs are sung are Sindhi variants of Hindustani forms.

His verses were first improvised in speech or song and then written down, unsystematically, by disciples, and the *Risalo* grew gradually from various collections of the verses he had made on many occasions. The poems as they have come down are not identical with the verses that came from his lips as he recited them: others wrote them down, altered them and edited them, and short verses by other poets, such as Inayat, Lakhmir and Junejo, found their way in. The cult his disciples kept at the shrine preserved his poetry, together with that of others, in the historic manuscript known as the *Ganj*, carefully assembled and dated 1207 of the Hijri calendar, 1792 of the Common Era.

The first printing came in 1866, when the German missionary Ernest Trumpp edited the collection at Leipzig from a manuscript of more than 1,200 pages, with great pains, although he thoroughly disliked anything that smacked of mysticism. A printing press had come to Karachi in the 1850s, but for Sufism in Sindh the main event was the printing of the *Risalo*. The work was 'canonised' by the British in the middle of the nineteenth century and afterwards reclaimed by the Sindhi intelligentsia, and under British rule it was pressed into service for the examinations of the new education syllabus. More than two decades after the first printing, Sindhi men of letters thought it necessary to publish his works so that his poetry could be more accessible to the people, and glossaries of his vocabulary followed. Studies followed, and beneath them an almost limitless body of speeches, articles, booklets and devotional literature, mainly in Sindhi. Even after Partition, both the Sindhi Hindus in India and the Sindhi Muslims in Pakistan continued the work, and it resulted in a number of editions and analyses.

The folk romances are the core of it. Drawing on the tales of Sindh and their tragic heroines, Sassui, Marui, Sohni, Momal and others, he made of their longing and suffering an allegory of the soul's yearning for God, so that the desert wanderings of a lovelorn girl become the seeker's journey to the Divine. Since the tales were known to everyone, he usually begins with the most dramatic moment, to describe the moods of the soul on its way to the beloved. Sohni swims every night across the Indus to meet her beloved on an island, until the jar she uses to keep afloat is shattered and she drowns. Sassui, a Brahman girl from Bhambhor, follows her beloved Punhun through burning deserts and over terrifying hills, until at last she is completely transformed into Love. Marui is the ideal soul, and Nuri, though a simple fishermaid, is a historical figure, the consort of Jam Tamachi, a Sindhi ruler of the fifteenth century. Some of the stories have a historical basis reaching back to the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, and these belong to his favourite themes.

At least ten of the thirty Surs are dedicated to the heroines, the *surmis*. The word is the feminine of *surma*, a hero, someone with prowess, and he means his *surmis* to be seen as women with prowess. He drew them across cultures from centuries old myths of the region that are older than Islam, and blended them into the framework of Islamic mysticism. The singer Alan Faqir gave his own reason for the poet's choice: the poet does not speak of Hir and Ranjha, or of Layla and Majnun, because they have no graves, and Shah wrote only of what could be verified.

In one tale music itself works the wonder. In *Sur Sorath* the minstrel Bijal defies Rao Diach, the king of Junagarh, and the power of his lyre, the *chang*, is such that the queens start crying and the forts begin to crumble. The poet gives the minstrel a kind of magic power, and the figure of the mendicant minstrel, the *charan*, is dominant throughout his poetry. In his hands the story becomes an allegory of the absolute power of the mystical guide, for whose sweet songs the initiate gladly sacrifices everything, his life included.

His verse fuses the deepest mysticism of *wahdat al-wujud*, the unity of being, with the very soil, speech and song of Sindh, and it carries a fierce compassion for the poor and the suffering. He did not use that expression himself: his word was *wahdat*, oneness, in Sindhi *hekro*, as in *tuhnjo Allah hekro*, 'Your God is One'. His emphasis on the unity of being widened his reach among different peoples, for it meant that distinctions, above all of religion, could be set aside in the face of that unity. The *Risalo* calls God by at least thirty four names, several of them among the Ninety Nine Names, and its God is a living and loving God: not a philosophical or mystical principle, but the Creator, the Merciful and Majestic, the First and the Last, and always the Lord as revealed through the Qur'an. None of the great Sufi orders is mentioned in it, nor any of the sanctuaries to which the people of Sindh flocked on many occasions.

Of his own poetry he is famously said to have insisted that it was no mere craft of words but a vehicle of the sacred:

جي تُو بيت ڀانئين، سي آيتون آهِن

[“What you take to be [mere] couplets are, in truth, verses/signs [of God].”]

In another translation the verse reads:

*What you consider to be poems are divine verses. They direct the mind toward the beloved* (translated by Christopher Shackle)

In this famous line he claims for his poems, *baita* in Sindhi, a standing comparable to the verses of the Qur'an, *ayatun*.

Rumi runs all through the *Risalo*, so much that his poetry is sometimes called the *Masnavi* of Sindh. One verse calls the reader to admire Iblis, the fallen angel, as the one true lover and to follow his example; another advises the true Sufi to throw his cap into the fire instead of boasting with it; and he compares the Prophet to the cloud that sends down life giving rain to the thirsty fields, quickening what seemed to be dead. With him the word *purab*, the east, always means the mystical East, the home of the soul.

In the rain song, *Sur Sarang*, the cloud of mercy rises over the desert of eastern Sindh, the Thar Parkar, and is seen floating from Africa to China, until the poet, coming closer and closer to his home region, prays:

*My Lord, be kind over dear Sind, / Friend! Sweet darling! You may make the whole world happy!* (translated by Annemarie Schimmel)

The song closes with a prayer that God make Sindh flourish forever and make the whole world prosperous, and this invocation of God's blessings on Sindh is one of the most often quoted verses of the *Risalo*. The rain brings fertility to the parched soil, milk to the cows and bread to the poor, and from it the poet turns to a short song in which the Prophet, once more the cloud of mercy, is praised because he will protect his community at Doomsday. The lightning of the rain crosses the Prophet's own tomb:

*Across the Holy Prophet's tomb / The lightning streaks did swiftly leap. / They smiled in kindness on the scene / And filled the stream with water deep* (translated by H. T. Sorley)

The poems speak often of tombs and doors. One stanza bids the traveller press on to the Prophet's tomb:

*Let Mecca be thy port or no, / Delay not, urge thy vessel on. / Repeat at Lord Muhammad's tomb / The holy words of God and doom* (translated by H. T. Sorley)

Another speaks of a round of doors:

*No door like Thine is. I have seen / Of doors my round completely* (translated by H. T. Sorley)

*Why go ye then and bow yourselves / In front of others, why go ye ?* (translated by H. T. Sorley)

And some passages condemn false practices, and lapses from the rigour of the ascetic life, among them the worship of *pirs*:

*But of the Lord alone true worship is . / There's none of pir or prophet. They do sin / Who worship pirs and worse than these are they / Who worship idols, those poor luckless folk, / From path misled who grasped untruthfulness. / Self worship not, thou faithful, pure of heart.* (translated by H. T. Sorley)

In one verse, burial in the right ground is a kind of life:

*My corse, Lord, convey / To my home that in graveyard of Marus / In fixed peace I may stay. / Perchance in my death I'd be living, / Were my corse in Malir.* (translated by H. T. Sorley)

The journey that matters is the inward one:

*Go with thine heart towards thy love. / Cease, Sasui, wanderings of thy feet. / Ask not the sand how lies the path. / To travel soul-fully is meet.* (translated by H. T. Sorley)

Caste is no bar: one poem asks why anyone should inquire into the caste of those who come, and says that all who came were welcomed. Yet Sassui feels her caste as a burden:

*Mother, my caste is a slur / In the minds of the men of Kech* (translated by H. T. Sorley)

Separation is the master theme, and there is hardly a poem in the *Risalo* that does not preach it. In *Sur Kalyan*:

*The seas of separation roll / And drown each single separate soul.* (translated by H. T. Sorley)

The love of the heroines began before the world itself:

*Time was when God, the One and the Eternal, spake / Unto the souls and thundered : ' Am I not your Lord ? ' / Then, even then, to Suhini had there come a love / And longing for the neatherd.* (translated by H. T. Sorley)

In the cry of the desert heroine, chaste human love is transformed into the summit of divine love:

*O voice in the desert, as if the grouse were crying . . . / cry from the water's depths: it is the sigh of love. / O voice in the desert, like sound of a violin- / It is the song of love: only the people unaware took it / to be a woman's song.* (translated by Annemarie Schimmel)

On the unity behind all things:

*One castle, a hundred thousand doors, / Windows without number- / Wherever I look, there the Beloved confronts me.* (translated by Annemarie Schimmel)

And on the letter *alif*, the cipher for Allah:

*Those who remember the line which begins with alif: / 'There is no goal but Him in both worlds,' [that is what] they think* (translated by Annemarie Schimmel)

One line has become almost proverbial in Sindhi:

*Thou makest the leaf drown in the stream, and makest the stone swimming* (translated by Annemarie Schimmel)

The martyrs of Karbala have a whole Sur. *Sur Kedaro* is devoted entirely to the martyrdom, the *shahadat*, of Husain, the third Shia imam. Shah Abdul Latif is the first Sindhi poet to write a *marsiyyo*, an elegy, in Sindhi, and after him the form was taken up by many other poets. Its four parts run from the coming of Muharram, the month of mourning for the martyrdom of Husain, to the martyrs' union with God after death. It opens the month of mourning:

*Muhurram's holy month is come, / The Princes' day of woe.* (translated by H. T. Sorley)

It asks the dyer to dye the mourner's clothes in sober mourning black, and goes on:

*They wandered forth to cruel doom / When fate with bloodshed came. / Because of their untimely end / I put myself to shame.* (translated by H. T. Sorley)

The *Kedaro* also takes up the motif of a wedding for the death of the martyrs:

*The houris wind rose chains; flower decorations for the martyrs!* (translated by Annemarie Schimmel)

Work songs are in the *Risalo* too, among them the songs of the cotton spinners, the *kapaiti*:

*Not withstanding the spinners / Carded the strands well / And spun out very fine yarn / Accumulated wealth, O mother*

The song of the cotton spinner is set against the festival of Id. In another poem a crew sets out, and makes its vows to the Prophet before sailing:

*They tied their boats with hawsers fast, / So doth Latif the Poet tell.* (translated by H. T. Sorley)

The poet signs his verses inside them. Within a single Sur the signature varies, now 'says Latif', now 'says Shah'; one verse gives his full name:

*Abdul Latif it is / Who makes these harmonies.* (translated by H. T. Sorley)

## The Shrine

Shah Abdul Latif's shrine at Bhit Shah, richly ornamented with the blue and white glazed tilework of Sindh, the *kashi*, is one of the most beautiful and most visited shrines in the province, and the town that has grown around it is a centre of Sindhi culture, music and letters. Pilgrims come throughout the year. The shrine is maintained by the Sindh Auqaf department, the government department of religious endowments.

The magnificent tomb was built for his body by the local ruler Ghulam Shah Kalhoro, at his own expense. Accounts of when it was raised differ, and there is no consensus: it is dated between 1754 and 1765. Its ornament was the work of a well known mason of the time, Idan of Sukkur. Although the poet left no son to take responsibility for the shrine, his disciples made it the centre of his cult, and the mausoleum became a kind of holy shrine that in 1940 still drew its crowds.

By the 1970s the shrine was crowned by a minaret and covered with blue and white tiles of the kind typical of the Hala district, showing flower motifs, and the pillars at its corners seemed to grow out of large tulips. The ceiling of the entrance hall showed the traditional workmanship of the area: small rectangular tiles in brownish, olive and yellowish tones set between the wooden framework, a style found in many saints' tombs in Sindh. A graveyard lay beside the building.

The dome carries verse. One of the Persian chronograms composed at his death, a phrase or verse whose letters, read as numbers, give a year, is inscribed on its northern side. Accounts of the year of his death by the Hijri calendar differ, and this chronogram gives it as 1165:

*When he became intoxicated by union from the goblet of 'Come back' (Sura 89/17). / The hidden inspirer said: 'The year of his journey was Ridwan-i Haqq,' (God's satisfaction) = 1165*

Others lie within its walls: Shah Abdul Latif's female relatives are buried in a compound of the shrine, where a woman was the caretaker of their tombs. His father lies a few paces from the tomb. The shrine also has a local *sajjada nashin*, literally the one who sits on the prayer carpet, the title of the head of a shrine.

In 1940 several relics of the poet were still preserved at Bhit: his long walking stick, his turban, a piece of cloth he wore, and the beggar's bowl of coconut husk from which he ate and drank frugally. By the 1970s a guest house had been built to lodge at least part of the innumerable visitors who flock to Bhit Shah in the days of the *ʿurs*, and a cultural centre was being developed.

## Devotion and Music

The music of the shrine is the poet's own. He was a musician as well as a poet, who even invented a new type of *tambooro*, the drone instrument, and whose songs blend traditional Indian *raga* with Sindhi folk tunes. His disciples' cult kept the elaborate tradition of performing his poetry that he himself had devised, and the *Shah Jo Risalo* is sung nightly at his shrine by hereditary singers, the *raagi faqirs*, in the unbroken tradition of the *Shah jo Raag*, accompanied by the *tambooro*: a music inseparable from the poetry itself. In the 1970s his melodies were well known all over Sindh.

His *faqirs* sing his poetry at the shrine in the melodies he composed, in the falsetto, imitating a woman's voice, a rite performed every evening since the shrine has been there, and they are renowned for singing the *wai* in falsetto. That is why the shrine is known as the *jamali*, or aesthetic, shrine. *Jamali* mysticism is attained through intuition and creativity, as by a poet and musician such as Shah Abdul Latif, while *jalali* mysticism is achieved through rigour and discipline. One man, Sikander Baloch, explained that the poetry is melancholic, and that this is why the *faqirs* at his shrine wear black; it is sung throughout the night on the *tambooro*, and there are *maqams* in it, points at which certain sections are sung at particular times of the night.

Bhit Shah is also one of the shrines where *qawwali*, the devotional music of the Sufis, is performed every evening after the prayers, the performances continuing well past midnight, and singers such as Abida Parvin are invited to sing there. Besides the nightly singing there was also a weekly gathering for his poetry, though accounts differ on its day. In 1882 a visitor, Dayaram Gidumal, kept vigil at one of these gatherings in the wide courtyard, with more than a hundred men, women and children. The deepest silence prevailed, broken now and then by a hearty *Allahu*. The subdued and tranquil look of the people, their strong faith and their erect but humble attitude of mind filled him with a profound veneration for the poet saint who was their recognised spiritual teacher, and whose burning words moved them to tears.

Each year, in the month of Safar, comes the *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of the saint's death. It draws vast crowds to Bhit Shah, and it hosts one of the great cultural gatherings of Sindh, with music, poetry and celebration alongside the rites of devotion. Up to the late 1960s the state's Sindhi publications regularly featured him among the saints of Sindh, and his annual fair was marked by special editions.

Hindus and Muslims both come to him. Hindus of different paths and castes come every day and for the annual *ʿurs*: they go to the tomb, walk around it and touch it, and then attend the *Shah jo Raag*, the singing of his poetry by the local *faqirs*. In the visit known as *ziyarat*, Hindus and Muslims alike bring textiles to lay on the tomb, or simply touch and kiss it to obtain blessings from the saint. Devotees who are Muslim and devotees who are not venerate his shrine because he was a Sayyid, who claimed descent from the Prophet Muhammad through his daughter Fatima and his son in law and cousin, Ali ibn Abi Talib.

The women of the Kolhi and Bhil peasants are ardent devotees at Muslim shrines such as his. They come above all because the shrine lies in the heart of a fertile agricultural belt, where they can make a living working on the large feudal farms, and also because his poetry is diverse in its languages and tolerant of other beliefs. Standing in the shrine courtyard, one visitor saw a train of colourfully dressed women in their *ghagras*, long skirts, and flowered veils, walking toward the shrine exit and hurrying to the pickups parked by the roadside in which their menfolk waited to drive them back.

The book of his verse is itself revered. Among the musician communities of Sindh the *Risalo* is treated with great respect because of its sacred Qur'anic context, and the book is not allowed to lie around on the floor.

Beyond the shrine his verse travelled by work and by song. People could sing it while ploughing or going out to fish, picking cotton or spinning it, and so the central ideas of Islamic mysticism, the love of God and the Prophet and trust in the eternal divine wisdom, became part of their daily lives. Richard Burton found that the learned praised it for its beauty and were fond of hearing it recited to the sound of the guitar, and that even the unlearned generally knew chosen portions by heart and took the trouble to learn their meaning.

The women of the Manganhar family of the singer Alan Faqir would sing the *arifana kalam*, the mystical verse, of Shah Latif, ending with 'and so says Shah Latif, O Latif, I say your *sana*', your praise. Alan Faqir himself claimed to be a *faqir* of the Bhit Shah shrine, and said of the poet: 'Shah Latif, he is my mentor, he is my *murshid*', his spiritual guide. Abida Parvin opened a concert in Islamabad with a mystical invocation from his Sindhi poetry, and in Kacch folk performers sing the poetry of the *Risalo* in Sindhi.

## Legends

Many stories of wonders are told of him. One tells that when he was being taught the alphabet he would not go beyond the letter *alif*, the first letter, which is the symbol of God and of unity. The same story is told of many Muslim saints from Turkey to India, among them the Turkish folk poet Yunus Emre and the Punjabi poet Bulleh Shah.

Another story tells how the *Risalo* nearly perished at its birth. When Tamar and Hashim brought him the finished collection, it is said, Shah Abdul Latif turned over the pages and, remarking of Sassui's frequent laments that the work was simply a safety valve for the peccant humours of the flesh, flung it suddenly into the Kirar lake. His secretaries remonstrated with him, the story goes, and he allowed them to rewrite the whole from memory.

A third story explains why he left no heir. His wife, it is told, while with child, longed one day for a certain kind of fish, and one of his followers was sent to find it. The Shah, the story continues, asked after this follower, and on learning why he was away remarked: 'When the embryo is such a trouble to my fakirs, what will the full blown adult be? May such a blossom be nipped in the bud!' The child, the story ends, was stillborn, to the great grief of the mother, and the Shah left no heir surviving him.

Tradition likes to add that the birth of Ghulam Shah Kalhoro, the ruler who built his tomb, was the result of the poet's blessing.

There is a story, too, that once, when a boy came to his lodge, the mystic recognised at once that the boy would one day 'open the lid from those things' that he had kept secret. In the story the boy was the young Sachal Sarmast, whose pen name in his Persian poetry was *Ashikar*, 'Open'.

At Hinglaj, legend gives him a miracle and a flight. After a long and difficult journey, it is said, he reached the shrine of the goddess and offered the Mother some milk from his palm, and in that moment the image came alive and the Mother drank from the hands of the saint. This roused the envy of the yogis, the legend goes on, and they decided to kill him. He fled, the legend ends, and disappeared into the earth, only to emerge again at Thatta, some 350 kilometres away.

## The Setting

Bhit Shah lies in the Matiari region of Sindh, near the town of Hala, north east of Hyderabad. In the poet's time Bhit itself, built on sandhills, was unattractive as a place to live, but it was set in the midst of striking scenery. It was close to the Kirar lake, which was fed by the Ali Ganj or Pahar canal, and there were several small sheets of water nearby, with trees and a certain richness of greenery not common in the arid land of Sindh except close to the river or its large branches. The Kirar is a depression near the poet's home at Bhit that fills with water in the rainy season.

Bhit appears in the rain song too, among the places the rain reaches: the rains fill the sky and 'have come rejoicing to Bhit', with flashes of lightning on the north wind, and "The greenery is fragrant, and grass is piled in heaps".

The wider country for which he composed was a land of cultivators and herdsmen, keepers of camels, buffaloes, sheep, goats and oxen, a land of tombs and Sayyids, of dust and heat, and of the yearly rise and fall of the waters of the Indus, inefficiently controlled by a crude system of irrigation. For the common folk of such a country Shah Abdul Latif composed his verses. The Kalhora rulers' practice of deferring to the priestly class led to an extraordinary reverence for *pirs*, Sayyids and holy men, which may be called the chief characteristic of eighteenth century Sindh, and in Sindh alone there are said to be more than a hundred thousand Sufi shrines.

During an invasion in the poet's lifetime, it is told, the minister Divan Gidumal offered the invader two bags which, he said, held the most valuable of Sindh's gifts: "the holy dust from the tombs of numerous saints and Pirs of Sind". Accounts differ on which invader it was. In the 1970s the trees near Sindhi shrines were still decorated with many pieces of cloth, preferably red, in the shape of little flags or tiny cradles, tied there for the sake of a vow, as is usual almost everywhere in the Islamic world.

## Legacy

He is the symbol of Sindhi culture for Muslims as well as for Hindus, in Pakistan as well as in India, and his use of the spoken language has helped to keep him loved across religious boundaries and modern national frontiers. In 1940 his poetry held the Hindus of Sindh in a spell almost as complete as the one it held over Muslims, and its message lay deep in the hearts of all classes of Sindh's people, Muslim and Hindu, lettered and unlettered. Most of the musicians of Sindh had been Hindus, and the musical forms in which the poems are sung are part of the Hindu heritage of India, which may be one more reason for that spell. The poems still bound Sindh with a powerful spell of love and admiration, though modern delight in them was of a different kind: their ideas were still believed, but not in quite the same way or for quite the same reasons.

His reputation is contested. Pakistani scholars have seen him as an authentic spokesman of the Sindhi folk tradition or as an advocate of Sindhi nationalism, while Indian scholars tend to view him as one of the many great premodern poet saints who helped to build the national identity of modern India. Many of his Hindu admirers and interpreters, finding that he explains the wondrous life of the Hindu ascetics in the terms of the Qur'an, have regarded his poetry as a perfect blending of the two religious traditions of India. The scholar Rashdi summed up what had been made of him: "Shah was the crown of the Sufis, Shah was a folk poet, Shah was the master of ragas, Shah was a patriot, Shah was a congressman, Shah was a Muslim Leaguer, Shah was Rumi, Shah was Goethe, Shah has produced Hir, Shah has made alive Marui", in short the medicine for every illness and the remedy for every pain, adding: "the same thing that has been done to Ghalib and Iqbal we have done to Shah Sahib".

In the narratives of Sindhi nationalism his poetry stands beside the Sindhi language, the Indus, the peasant or *hari*, and above all Sufism and mysticism, and it was G. M. Syed who bore the main responsibility for giving Bhittai his role as the most important poet of Sindh. Abdul Qadir Mangi said: "Every Sindhi, of whatever faith he may be, believes in the greatness of Bhitai. From a Kohli Bheel to a Syed, every one considers Bhitai his spiritual guide." His journey among the yogis has been read in opposite ways: Sindhi nationalists look on him as the ideal Sindhi, the perfect example of the tolerant history of Sindh, while more orthodox writers seek to explain the journey away as youthful recklessness, to distance the poet from any possible Hindu influence. The singer Alan Faqir read the heroines politically: for him Sassui and Marui also stand for the land pillaged by conquerors and invaders, and he said that when he sang the poetry he sang it with a passion that would convey the exploitation of the land.

The Sindhi Hindus who went to India took him with them. Before Partition they had shared their passion for Sufi poetry with Muslim Sindhis and others, above all through the *Shah Jo Risalo*. Ram Panjwani named him first of the trinity of the Sindhi poets, and pictured the three in an image: Shah Abdul Latif brings the kettle to the boil, Sachal takes the lid off, and Sami passes the drink on to the rank and file of the people, so that they may be brought to an intense awareness of God even in the affairs of everyday life. Panjwani also gave the poet's words on who a Sufi is: "A Sufi belongs to no sect. No one can label him. He fights with his own selves and divulges nothing. He extends help even to those who are hostile to him."

Among the Hindu Sufis of India his poetry is praised by everyone as the quintessence of Sindhi poetry and literature, and it is sung during *satsangs*, devotional gatherings, yet it has no ritual use in their ceremonies and supplies no prayer. On 23 May 1957, at Poona, Sadhu Vaswani asked Rochaldas to deliver a sermon, and Rochaldas gave a commentary on Shah Abdul Latif's verses, completed with quotations from the *Jap Sahib*, a Sikh prayer. One Indian darbar traces its spiritual genealogy back to the Sufis of Sindh, among them Shah Latif, Bedil and Rohal Shah, and at another shrine's annual fair, in April 2016, a huge poster showed Shah Abdul Latif with Jhulelal under the motto *Sab ka malik ek hai*, the Lord of all is one.

Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai is at once the national poet of Sindh and one of the towering mystics of South Asia, the man who raised the Sindhi language to its highest expression and made of its folk tales a scripture of divine love. His *Shah Jo Risalo* is cherished as the soul of Sindh; his music lives in an unbroken tradition at his shrine; and his vision of a love that embraces all beings, across every division of creed and caste, remains a living force. Bhit Shah is not merely his tomb but the cultural heart of a people.
