## Overview

In Chah Miran, an old riverside locality of Lahore named in his honour, stands the tomb of Syed Miran Hussain Zanjani, one of the earliest Muslim missionary figures associated with the city. Unusually for a site of such age, the grave rests on a simple raised platform without a dome, within what was once a garden named for the saint. The shrine is maintained by the Punjab Auqaf and Religious Affairs Department, and local devotees still gather there every year for his *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of the saint's death.

Zanjani is remembered as the elder of the two missionary saints credited with the early spread of Islam in Lahore, the other being the more widely celebrated Data Ganj Bakhsh, Ali Hujwiri. Tradition holds that Data Ganj Bakhsh reached the city to find Zanjani's funeral under way, the saint having died during the night. His modest tomb is one of Lahore's oldest surviving Islamic devotional sites, older by centuries than the grander shrines of the Mughal era for which the city is more widely known, and it remains the spiritual anchor of the neighbourhood that bears his name, on the historic bank of the River Ravi near the old walled city.

## The Saint

Syed Miran Hussain Zanjani, also remembered as Hazrat Shaikh Hussain Zanjani and as Shah Husain Zanjani, was a Hussaini Syed, a descendant of the Prophet Muhammad, and his family's descent is traced from Imam Musa Kazim through the Zanjan line. Tradition holds that he was born in 958 at Zanjan, in what is now Iran. He is counted among the ancient elders, the saints of old: a man of guidance, austerity, piety and nobility. His spiritual chain, or *silsila*, the line of masters through which a Sufi's teaching is handed down, is said to lead back to Junaid of Baghdad.

He came to Lahore, it is said, around 998, on the instruction of his spiritual master, Sheikh Abu al-Fazal Khatil, who ordered his disciple to move to the Punjab and later sent Ali Hujwiri after him. He is also said to have come together with another saint from Zanjan, Sayyid Yaqub Zanjani, known as Sadr Diwan, whose own grave is on Hospital Road in Lahore. Accounts differ over when and how Zanjani came, but he is remembered to have preached in the city for some thirty six years, converting large numbers of its people to Islam well before the Ghaznavid and later Mughal periods reshaped the religious landscape of the region, and to have spent his whole life guiding them.

The year of his death that the shrine keeps is 1042. He was buried alongside his brother, Hazrat Musa Hussain Zanjani, whose own shrine lies nearby. Accounts of his dates differ, and it has been argued that two men of the same name, an earlier and a later, have been run together; but the shrine and most accounts remember a single saint, the one whose funeral Data Ganj Bakhsh is said to have found under way when he reached Lahore.

## Legends

The best known story about Zanjani, and one of Lahore's early Sufi foundation stories, ties him to Ali Hujwiri, Data Ganj Bakhsh. Tradition holds that when Hujwiri's master ordered him to go to Lahore and preach, he objected that his senior brother in the path, Hussain Zanjani, a fellow disciple of the same master, was already there, and asked what need there was of him. His master repeated the order.

When Hujwiri reached Lahore, the story continues, he saw Zanjani's bier going past: the saint had died during the night, and in the morning his coffin was carried out for burial. Data Ganj Bakhsh joined the funeral and saw to the shrouding and the burial at Chah Miran. On seeing the bier, it is said, he understood how far his *murshid*, his spiritual guide, had seen. In the nineteenth century Zanjani's *karamat*, the miraculous deeds of a saint, were said to be famous by the thousand.

The founder of the Chishti order in India, Khwaja Muin al-Din Chishti, is also linked with Zanjani. When the Khwaja came to Lahore, it is said, he kept company with Zanjani, or else stayed near the tombs of Zanjani and Ali Hujwiri; accounts differ over whether he found the saint alive.

## The Shrine and Its Devotion

The tomb has a modest footprint that belies its historical significance: a grave on a simple raised platform, with no dome. Today it lies in Miran Husain Park, in Chah Miran, which by the 1970s was a suburb of Lahore.

In the nineteenth century the place was known as the *khanqah*, the Sufi lodge, of Hazrat Sayyid Husain Zanjani, on the south side of the village of Khoi Miran. The tomb had a brick enclosure taller than a man, with its entrance on the north. There were also a niche with wooden shutters, a second enclosure with a niche for a lamp, a *dalan*, or hall, of three arches, a few graves and a *kotha*, a small room, and a well with a Persian wheel, a wheel hung with a chain of pots that lifts the water.

By the 1880s the shrine was counted among those outside the walled city, a mile from Lahore to the east. The grave was built of brick, houses of *pakka*, or fired, brick had been put up for *faqirs*, wandering Sufis, to live in, and the Persian wheel at the well was in working order. The building was small, but the place was ancient, and the man buried there was counted among the earliest elders of Lahore.

In the nineteenth century the shrine was in the possession of Karam Ali Shah, the *gaddi nashin*, or holder of the seat, of the line of Sadr Diwan Sahib, the saint said to have come to Lahore with Zanjani, and only a few faqirs lived there. Today the shrine is formally recognised and maintained by the Punjab Auqaf and Religious Affairs Department, the provincial government's department of religious endowments.

Under Sikh rule a great *mela*, or fair, was held at the tomb every year. By the 1880s its liveliness had gradually gone, as attendance had also fallen at the shrine of Fateh Shah Mast once the Sikh state came to an end. An *ʿurs* was held at the grave once every year in the nineteenth century, and the yearly *ʿurs* still draws local devotees today.

## The Setting

In the Punjab at large, the annexation of the province by Mahmud of Ghazna and its incorporation into his empire prompted many Sufis to settle there, and in the eleventh century a number of mystics and scholars had settled in Lahore.

The title of the first to call Lahore to Islam has been given to another man, Shaikh Muhammad Ismail Muhaddith Lahori, a Bukhari sayyid who taught *hadith*, the reported sayings of the Prophet, and *tafsir*, the interpretation of the Quran, there, and through whom, it is said, the faith of Islam spread in the Punjab, though that account, and the year it gives for his death, have been disputed.

In the 1880s a second shrine, that of Faqir Sain Qutb Shah Qadri, lay to the east of the village of Khoi Miran. The shrine of Shah Gada Lahori had the same arrangement in those years: quarters of fired brick for faqirs and a working Persian wheel.

## Legacy

As one of the earliest known Sufi graves in the Lahore region, the shrine bears valuable witness to the gradual spread of Islam in the Punjab, carried by missionaries in the centuries before the Delhi Sultanate and the Mughal empire. Already in the nineteenth century it was counted among the oldest in the city, and its saint was honoured as the first of a company of saints who came from abroad to the land of Hind, and as the master of masters.

His name has also travelled with the story of Data Ganj Bakhsh, whose arrival in Lahore is told through Zanjani's funeral, and with that of Khwaja Muin al-Din Chishti, who is said to have kept company with him or to have stayed near his tomb. Around the grave, the locality of Chah Miran is named in his honour.
