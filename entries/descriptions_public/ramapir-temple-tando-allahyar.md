## Overview

The Ramapir Temple at Tando Allahyar, in Sindh, is a Hindu shrine dedicated to *Ramdev Pir*, also called Ramapir or Baba Ramdev, a saint of Rajasthan venerated across western India and Sindh as a worker of miracles and a champion of the poor and of those of low caste. The temple is the focus of the annual *Ramapir Mela*, which is widely reckoned the second largest Hindu pilgrimage in Pakistan after the *yatra*, the pilgrimage journey, to Hinglaj. It is celebrated above all for the way its devotion reaches across the boundaries of community, drawing Muslims as well as Hindus into its festival.

## Ramdev Pir

Tradition remembers Ramdev Pir as a ruler and saint of medieval Rajasthan, held by his devotees to have been an incarnation of Krishna, who used his powers to defend the humble and to dissolve the barriers of caste. Accounts of when he lived differ; tradition places him in the fifteenth century, and he is said to have taken *samadhi*, the final meditation in which a holy man leaves the world, in the year 1459. His principal shrine stands at Ramdevra, also called Runicha, in the Jaisalmer region of Rajasthan, though the temple at Tando Allahyar has also been called his main temple.

His cult spread widely among both Hindus and the Muslim communities of the desert borderlands, so that he is honoured as a *pir*, the title of a Sufi spiritual guide, as readily as a Hindu saint. In Sindh he is still known as Rama Pir, and he is also the object of widespread worship in Gujarat and Rajasthan. Among the untouchable castes of Sindh, who venerated the goddess Devi as well as heroes raised to the rank of gods, he was one of the most popular of these deified heroes, alongside Pithoro Pir, Pabuji and Guga Pir.

He is very present among the untouchable castes, yet he is also venerated by the *Nathpanthis*, the followers of the Nath tradition of yogis, in Gujarat. Pithoro Pir, another of these heroes, is most often associated with figures of salvation, and of these Ramdev Pir is the most venerated. Most of the Meghwars who venerate Pithoro Pir also venerate Ramdev Pir, though the reverse is less sure. The images of the two saints seem to share a common model of holiness, founded on renunciation.

In Sindh today he is also honoured within the temples dedicated to the gods worshipped across India. In Karachi, for example, a painting of Ramdev Pir stands in the temple of Lakhshmi Narayan, and the city's Narayanpur neighbourhood has a Ram Pir temple of its own, a different place from the temple at Tando Allahyar.

## The Temple

The temple's legend tells that a devotee carried an earthen lamp from the original shrine at Ramdevra, some four hundred and sixty kilometres to the north east, across what is now the frontier between India and Pakistan, and enshrined its flame here, founding the temple in his master's honour. The building itself dates from the British colonial period, and is traditionally placed in the middle of the nineteenth century, around 1859.

Beyond the days of its fair, the temple serves the settled Hindu population of Tando Allahyar and the surrounding district, a community with deep roots in the agrarian society of lower Sindh. The cult of Ramdev Pir, with its emphasis on the dignity of the poor and the irrelevance of caste before the divine, has long held a special appeal for communities at the margins, and the temple remains a place where that message is renewed in song and ritual. Many of Pakistan's Hindus, especially in the rural areas, follow either the teachings of local Sufi *pirs* or those of Ramdev Pir.

## The Ramapir Mela

The temple's fame rests chiefly upon its great fair, or *mela*. Held on the first day of the month of *Bhadva*, which falls in August or September, the Ramapir Mela is a pilgrimage of three days that draws thousands of the faithful from across Sindh and beyond. Its rites unfold in a rhythm of flags hoisted at dawn, barefoot processions, the singing of *bhajans*, or devotional songs, offerings of coconuts and lengths of cloth, and the communal *langar*, the free kitchen that feeds all comers without distinction.

What most strikes observers of the mela is its shared character. Hindu ritual predominates: the *aarti*, the waving of lamps before the deity, the offerings and the devotional song. Yet the festival's appeal has never been confined to a single community. Muslim devotees, too, come to the shrine, offering *chadars*, the embroidered sheets laid upon the tombs of Sufi saints, and joining the processions. It is a fusion of Hindu temple worship and the idiom of the Sufi *dargah*, the shrine of a Sufi saint, that is characteristic of the folk religion of Sindh. In a country where the Hindu minority is small and often anxious, the Ramapir Mela stands out as a rare and vivid expression of a devotional culture that both faiths can share.

## The Setting

Sindh is home to the majority of Pakistan's Hindus, whose number is put at between 6 and 10 million, an estimate that varies widely. Despite mass migration, the Hindus of Sindh still number several millions. Over 90 per cent of Pakistan's Hindus belong to underprivileged Hindu tribes or subcastes, such as the Meghwar, the Bhil and the Kohli, who often work as manual labourers for the powerful landowning elite of Sindh. Since Partition the majority of the Hindus of Sindh have belonged to the low castes. Some name them Dalits, the people of the castes once held untouchable, and some even claim they are not Hindu; among the Hindus there is a debate over whether the term "Dalit" is relevant. The Sufi legacy is still alive among the Dalits of Sindh. Most of the country's remaining non-Muslims are Hindus living in southern Punjab, inner Sindh and Baluchistan.

Several of these communities look east, across the present border, towards the Rajasthan of Ramdev Pir's own legend. Contrary to popular belief, there were many untouchables in Sindh, among them the Bhils or Kohlis and the Meghwars, who are mostly settled around Umerkot, and many of them are thought to have migrated from Gujarat or Rajasthan between 1843 and 1872. The Kohlis are the indigenous peasant communities of Sindh, sometimes migratory, and together with the Bhils they are the native inhabitants of the lands that run on from Sindh into Rajasthan. There is evidence of roving minstrels in that region, and the Manganhar are its hereditary professional musicians.

Sindh offers common ground to Hindu and Muslim devotion. As early as 1851 it was observed that the worship of the intercessors, the saints who plead for the faithful, was the common religious idiom of Hindus and Muslims. In Sindh a number of sacred figures have a dual identity, Muslim and Hindu: the Muslim side is always tied to Sufism, while the Hindu side usually belongs to the Nathpanth. Hindus of Sindh have come to the Sufi mausoleums since the nineteenth century, and the large majority used to visit the Sufi shrines; this does not make them Sufis, but for them the Sufis are part of their religious legacy. Sindh's geography, at once isolated and a buffer zone, has made it host to many unorthodox forms of Hinduism and Islam. Hindu participation in the Muslim mystical orders has been more visible in Sindh than in the Punjab, and Hindu writers wrote mystical poems in the imagery of the Muslims. The south of Pakistan, Sindh above all, offers a whole range of places of salvation, visited by different religious communities and joined in a network of pilgrimage routes.

That shared devotion has not meant a history without strain. The Hindus of Pakistan, Sindh included, are said to be more and more under threat from the country's radical Muslims, and after the demolition of the Babri Mosque on 6 December 1992 Hindu communities in Sindh and Quetta were attacked. In the early twenty first century, government funding for minority places of worship brought a wave of restorations, and many temples across Sindh were renovated, coinciding with changes in the political landscape.

## Legacy

The Ramapir Temple at Tando Allahyar is at once a major centre of Hindu pilgrimage in Pakistan and a living emblem of Sindh's syncretic religious temperament. In the flame said to have been carried from Ramdevra, in the pilgrims who walk barefoot to its doors, and in the Muslim chadars laid alongside Hindu offerings, it preserves a vision of shared sanctity that has become increasingly precious. Second only to Hinglaj among the Hindu pilgrimages of the country, it testifies to the endurance of the faith and to the older, more porous boundaries of devotion in the Indus lands.
