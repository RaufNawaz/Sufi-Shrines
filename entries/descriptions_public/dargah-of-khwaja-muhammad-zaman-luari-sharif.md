## Overview

Luari Sharif, some fifteen kilometres from the town of Badin in the far south of Sindh, is the *dargah*, the Sufi shrine complex, of Khwaja Muhammad Zaman (1713 to 1775), one of the most revered Naqshbandi Sufis and Sindhi mystic poets of the eighteenth century. Luari Sharif is counted among the oldest and most important Sufi sites of southern Sindh. His tomb and the vaulted chambers of his successors form the shrine, and every year his *ʿurs*, the commemoration of the saint's death, draws devotees from across the region.

The Naqshbandis are the order of Sufis, or Muslim mystics, founded by Baha-ud-Din Naqshband. The *khanqah*, the Sufi lodge, that Muhammad Zaman established at Luari became the foremost Naqshbandi centre of eighteenth century Sindh after Thatta, and from it his deputies carried the order's teaching across the province.

## The Saint

Muhammad Zaman was born into a family of the Siddiqi lineage, which claimed descent from the caliph Abu Bakr and had settled in Sindh in earlier centuries. His first teacher was his father, Shaikh Abdul Latif, a follower of the Naqshbandi order. He went on to study at Thatta, then the great seat of learning in Sindh, and there he came under the influence of the Sufi master Khwaja Abul Masakin, whose disciple he became. Tradition holds that he received from Abul Masakin the honorific Sultan al-Aulia, "master of the saints". He is also known as Makhdoom Muhammad Zaman Naqshbandi, and by the title Khwaja Kalan.

He settled at Luari, and the *khanqah* he established there became a place of study, from which his *khalifas*, or deputies, carried the order's teaching across Sindh. The *khanqah* is traditionally dated to 1737, though accounts of when Luari became a Naqshbandi centre differ. When the original settlement became uninhabitable, he founded a new town, also named Luari.

He was also a poet of note. A body of mystical verse in Sindhi is attributed to him, of which some eighty five poems are said to survive. He died in January 1775 and was buried at Luari.

## A Master of the Same Name

A Naqshbandi master of eighteenth century Sindh, called Makhdum Muhammad Zaman and Muhammad Zaman Lanwari, may be the saint of Luari, though the identification is not certain. The name fits, Lanwari may be a form of the place's name, and he lived in Sindh in the generation of Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit, as his younger compatriot.

He has been called "the famous saint", and the Sindhi scholar Abdur Rahim Girhori was his *khalifa*. Among his own masters was Mian Muhammad Thattawi. Girhori commented in Arabic on his master's sayings in Sindhi. Most of Girhori's own books were destroyed during the Afghan raids in Upper Sindh in 1781.

Shah Abdul Latif praised the eye-glass in his verse, but Muhammad Zaman Lanwari used the image the other way, explaining outward science as an eye-glass that stands between the eye and the goal of seeing. On a larger question of doctrine, the reality of the Prophet, *haqiqa muhammadiyya*, as mediator, he has been placed with Girhori and Dard, and Shah Abdul Latif outside them.

## The Shrine and Its Devotion

Muhammad Zaman was buried at Luari, where his tomb and the vaulted chambers of his successors form the shrine complex. Access to the complex has at times been restricted, and a police post has been maintained at its entrance since the 1980s. An annual *ʿurs* commemorates Khwaja Muhammad Zaman and draws devotees from across the region.

In the later twentieth century the trees near Sindhi shrines were still decorated with many pieces of cloth, preferably red, in the shape of little flags or tiny cradles, attached there for the sake of a vow, as is usual almost everywhere in the Islamic world.

## Luari Sharif and the Naqshbandis of Sindh

The Naqshbandi order came to Sindh from the sixteenth century onwards, with the Arghuns and the Tarkhans, two Central Asian dynasties that ruled the province. It had several important centres there, notably Luari Sharif, a village close to Badin in southern Sindh.

Naqshbandi scholars from Luari Sharif wrote treatises in Sindh, and the main author among them was Makhdum Muhammad Hashim (1692 to 1762), who was *qazi*, or judge, in Thatta. These scholars showed that the Muslims of Sindh did not know the Quran. They wrote paraphrases of it and then a Sindhi translation of the Arabic Quran, and they condemned some rituals, especially those labelled Hindu or Shia, as not Islamic. This came against a wider background: before the eighteenth century, few people in Sindh identified themselves as Muslims, probably because the name was restricted to the Sayyids, the descendants of the Prophet, and their dependants. Later Naqshbandi treatises on how to follow the rules of Islam led to the conclusion that the Sindhis, even when they were officially Muslim, did not practise "Orthodox Islam". Yet the official book devoted to the Naqshbandis of Luari Sharif was written by a Hindu follower.

In southern Sindh a strong Naqshbandi influence at the beginning of the eighteenth century changed the spiritual climate to a certain extent. Makhdum Muhammad Hashim and his relatives attacked the Sufis who defended the emotional side of religion, and this Naqshbandi group helped to promote a more sober mystical piety. The Naqshbandis of Sindh followed the reforming enterprise led by Shah Waliullah in Delhi, and in Sindh, as in Bengal, they tried to curb the overflowing emotionalism of the folk songs. Even so, many of the pious in Sindh kept "the marketplace of union warm" by reciting Rumi's *Mathnawi*, and stern Naqshbandis quoted Rumi's work as much as members of the more emotional orders did.

The order stands apart in Sindh in other ways too. Perhaps because of the hold of the Suhrawardi order on Sindh, the Chishti order could not spread there as it did in the rest of South Asia, yet music appeared very early in the Sufism of Sindh. The Naqshbandis are the exception: the majority of them in Sindh are strongly opposed to music and to the singing of devotional songs. Outside the Naqshbandis, Sufism in Sindh is dominated by poetry expressing *wahdat al-wujud*, the unity of being. A Hindu branch of the order is hardly found in Sindh, and the Sufis to whom Sindh's Hindu Sufis look all belong to the *wujudi* school, the school of the unity of being, which leaves out the Naqshbandis.

## The Setting

In the reign of Aurangzeb, Badin marked one edge of the Mughal province, or *suba*, of Thatta. The province ran two hundred and fifty *kos*, an old measure of distance, in length, from Bhakkar to Kech Makran, and a hundred *kos* in breadth, from the *qasba*, the small town, of Badin to the port of Lahari. It was bounded by Gujarat Ahmadabad to the east, Kech Makran to the west, Bhakkar to the north and the salt sea to the south, and it held four *sarkars*, or districts: Thatta, Siwistan, Nasirpur and Amarkot.

The greatest of its rivers was the Indus. Merchants from Multan and Bhakkar shipped goods by it to Thatta, and large armies rarely went by land, because of the jungle and the lack of water. The country depended for the most part on its boats, of which, great and small, there were forty thousand. The diet rested on rice, curds and fish; dried fish was shipped to the ports, and fish oil was used on the boats. Brahmanabad had once been a great capital whose fort had fourteen hundred towers, and Adpur became the seat after it.

Mirza Shah Beg Arghun came from Qandahar with an army and took Thatta, and Arghuns and then Tarkhans ruled until Mirza Jani Beg came to court with the Khankhanan, the holder of the title khan of khans, and the country was joined to the empire. Jani Beg was given the *jagir*, the revenue grant, of Thatta, and the port of Lahari, which lies on the side of Thatta, was made crown land. Sindh was part of the Mughal Empire from 1595, and although representatives of the Delhi court came there, its own rulers enjoyed much freedom.

The Sindh of Muhammad Zaman's lifetime was a province in upheaval. After Aurangzeb's death the native Kalhoro dynasty took over the rule, first in Upper and later in Lower Sindh, only to become the vassals of the invading Nadir Shah, who conquered Sindh and the Punjab before he reached Delhi in 1739. The Kalhoros were overthrown by the former disciples of their saintly ancestor, Adam Shah Kalhoro, and the Talpurs, who thus came to power in 1781, ruled the country until the British conquered it in 1843.

The holy men of that country held a place in its order. Before the British conquest most of Sindh's Sayyids were landowners, *zamindars*, but they were also *pirs*, spiritual guides. The *pirs* of Sindh were mediators between the tribes, and between the rulers and the governed, and their power grew in the eighteenth century, when the Kalhoras developed the irrigation system. The *darbar*, the saint's shrine, was the sacred ground on which Sufi culture was at work. The cult of holy men is particularly evident in the countryside, especially in the Punjab and Sindh, where great landlords and large concentrations of land are crucial features, and there the local *pir* represents the local landlord or is closely related to him. British policy, especially in the Punjab and Sindh, was to treat the hereditary saints in essentially the same way as landlords and tribal leaders.

Sindh's pride in its saints lives in a story told of more than one invasion. Diwan Gidumal, the minister of the Kalhoro princes, is said to have given an invader the most precious treasure Sindh had to offer, the holy dust of its saints. Sindh alone is said to have more than a hundred thousand Sufi shrines.

For all its upheaval, the eighteenth century can be called the most important period for the formation of mystical poetry and prose in Sindh. An Arabic saying was very popular there at the time: "Affliction is for saintliness the same as are flames for the gold". Shah Abdul Latif's verse speaks of the province itself. In his Sur Sarang the cloud of mercy rises from the desert of eastern Sindh, the Thar Parkar, and floats over the world, and the poet, coming home, prays:

*My Lord, be kind over dear Sind, / Friend! Sweet darling! You may make the whole world happy!* (translated by Annemarie Schimmel)

In a lament from the Marui cycle, the poet mourns the passing of the heroes and the holy men of Sindh. The lament has lived on in performance, and the singer Alan Faqir recited its line:

*Punnu is not in the Kec / Sind is not the amir*

Its second half means that Sindh is not in the position of leadership, or amirship. It is one of Alan Faqir's own political readings of the tales of women.

## Legacy

Luari Sharif is counted among the oldest and most important Sufi sites of southern Sindh, and Khwaja Muhammad Zaman is remembered as one of the most revered Naqshbandi Sufis and Sindhi mystic poets of the eighteenth century, whose *khalifas* carried the order's teaching across the province.

That legacy has two sides. Luari Sharif stands for the Naqshbandi scholars who paraphrased the Quran and translated it into Sindhi, and who condemned rituals they judged not Islamic; yet a Hindu follower wrote the official book devoted to its Naqshbandis. And the master called Muhammad Zaman Lanwari, if he is the saint of Luari, is remembered through his *khalifa* Girhori, who commented on his Sindhi sayings in Arabic, and through his image of outward science as an eye-glass standing between the eye and the goal of seeing, a counterpoint to Shah Abdul Latif's own verse.
