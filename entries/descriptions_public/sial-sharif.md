## Overview

At Sial Sharif, in the Sargodha district of the Punjab, stands the shrine of Hazrat Khwaja Muhammad Shamsuddin Sialvi, a leading saint of the nineteenth century in the Chishti order, an order of Sufis, or Muslim mystics, that was founded by Moinuddin Chishti and is prominent in South Asia. His *khanqah*, the Sufi lodge where disciples gather, became one of the most influential centres of the order in the region. An heir to the great Chishti revival that flowed from Taunsa Sharif, he guided a wide following and trained saints of the first rank, among them Pir Meher Ali Shah of Golra, and his *darbar*, or shrine, at Sial Sharif remains a major place of pilgrimage in central Punjab.

## The Saint

Khwaja Muhammad Shamsuddin was born in 1799 at Sial Sharif into a devout family, and from his youth he pursued the religious sciences and the Sufi path. He was initiated into the Chishti order and became a spiritual heir within the line of Khwaja Sulaiman Taunsvi, rising to be among the foremost Chishti *shaykhs*, or masters, of his age. From his *khanqah* at Sial Sharif he taught and guided disciples and kept a great *langar*, the free communal kitchen of a shrine, and his spiritual authority drew seekers from across the Punjab.

Among those who took his guidance was Pir Sayyid Meher Ali Shah of Golra Sharif, whose direct *pir*, or spiritual guide, he was. Through Meher Ali Shah the line of Sial Sharif reached into the next generation of Punjabi Sufism. Shamsuddin Sialvi died in 1883, though accounts of the year of his death differ, and he was buried at Sial Sharif.

## The Shrine and Its Devotion

His shrine at Sial Sharif is a large complex kept by his successors, who have continued as prominent religious figures and as its *sajjada-nashins*, custodians whose title means those who sit on the prayer carpet. The shrine maintains the daily prayers and the free kitchen, and gatherings of *naat*, poetry in praise of the Prophet, and of *qawwali*, the devotional music of the Sufis. Its annual *urs*, the commemoration of the saint's death, gathers great crowds of devotees. The town of Sial Sharif takes its identity from the *darbar*, a continuing seat of Chishti teaching and devotion.

More widely, the Punjab is one of the two principal regions of the *qawwali* tradition, the other being Uttar Pradesh; the tunes of the two can be told apart mainly by their rhythmic setting, and Punjabi tunes tend to be organised rhythmically by stress.

## The Chishti Line in the Punjab

The line of Khwaja Sulaiman Taunsvi, to which Shamsuddin Sialvi belonged, is part of the Chishti revival in the Punjab. The Chishti order is usually counted among the most powerful Sufi orders of northern India and the Punjab. Its Nizami branch came to prominence in the eighteenth century, principally in Delhi, the Deccan and the Punjab, through masters who were related to local political leaders but in tension with them.

The wider spread of the Chishti Nizami Fakhri line in the Punjab came through Hazrat Khwaja Sulaiman Taunsvi, who was born in 1184 and died in 1267 of the Hijri, or Islamic, calendar. He consolidated the order by stressing learning through the founding of institutions: not only the academy called Dar al-'Ulum at Taunsa on the upper Indus, but also other religious schools throughout the Punjab. He urged his followers to take part in resistance against Sikh aggression in the Punjab, yet he worked to keep cordial relations with Punjabi Hindus even while he opposed their Sikh neighbours. In the nineteenth century the Nizami branch of the Chishti order spread through the upper Punjab by way of his disciples.

In the colonial period the history of this lineage, the Chishti Nizami Sulaymaniyya, included battles over shrines in the Punjab, Taunsa among them. Struggles for power within the Sulaymaniyya can be traced to British manipulation and to the rivalry of local Sikhs, Hindus and Muslim reformers, but political contest is only part of its story.

## The Setting

Sial Sharif is a settlement in the Sargodha district, distinct from the city of Sargodha. More generally, the cult of the holy man, the *pir*, and the folk piety associated with him are particularly evident in the countryside, where major landlords and large concentrations of land are crucial features, especially in the Punjab and Sindh. In many cases the local *pir* represents the local landlord or is at least closely related to him.

The district also has a place in the Punjab's most famous romance. In the tale of Hir and Ranjha, Ranjha, the son of a landlord of the Sargodha district, falls in love with Hir, the daughter of the ruler of Jhang.

In the city of Sargodha itself, a man from the city has told of a *fakir*, a wandering mystic, who roams Sargodha all day long surrounded by dogs, refuses to talk to any human being, and offers his food to the dogs first. In one Muslim family's ancestral house in the city the arches had three cusps, which marked them as Sikh. In the early twenty first century sectarian conflict at the processions of Eid Milad-Un-Nabi, the celebration of the Prophet's birthday, remained a problem in cities such as Sargodha, Faisalabad and Dera Ismael Khan, where ultra orthodox Sunni organisations had a large base of support, though the incidents had so far been minor.

## Legacy

Khwaja Shamsuddin Sialvi holds an important place in the Chishti revival of the Punjab in the nineteenth century. He was a master whose *khanqah* at Sial Sharif carried the tradition forward and trained saints, above all Meher Ali Shah of Golra, who would shape the religious life of the region into the twentieth century. His shrine remains a great Chishti centre of central Punjab.
