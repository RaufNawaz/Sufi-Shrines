## Overview

The shrine of Hazrat Imam Ali-ul-Haq, known to the people of Sialkot simply and affectionately as Imam Sahib, stands in the heart of the old city of Sialkot in the Punjab, at the spot where the saint is traditionally believed to have died. He is venerated as a warrior saint and a *shahid*, a martyr, one of the missionary holy men whom local tradition credits with the coming of Islam to this corner of the upper Punjab. His *darbar*, or shrine, is among the most important devotional sites of the city, and devotees come to it throughout the year to pray and to seek his blessing.

He is a separate figure from the celebrated Bari Imam, Syed Abdul Latif Shah Kazmi, of Nurpur Shahan near Islamabad, with whom the similar honorific Imam sometimes causes confusion. The saint venerated at Sialkot belongs to the local history of that city, and already in the reign of the Mughal emperor Aurangzeb his tomb was a place of visitation.

## The Saint

Accounts of Imam Ali-ul-Haq differ widely, and on when he lived they differ by some three or four centuries. The account more commonly repeated locally places him in the medieval period, often in the thirteenth or fourteenth century, and sometimes associates him with the reign of the Delhi sultan Firuz Shah Tughlaq. His name is also written Ali al-Haq, and he is known too as Ali al-Haq Sialkoti, Ali al-Haq of Sialkot.

In the local telling he came as a preacher and a holy warrior, and is said to have brought a large part of the surrounding population to Islam, so turning Sialkot into a predominantly Muslim town. Accounts of his lineage and his companions differ, and some bring him from Arabia, but what they share is the memory of a holy man who came from afar, who is credited with bringing Islam to Sialkot, and who died a martyr on the spot now marked by his tomb.

Tradition holds that he was at last martyred by an unbeliever. Accounts of how he died differ; most tell that he fell in battle, and companions are remembered as having fallen with him.

## The Shrine

The *darbar* of Imam Sahib is not a single monumental tomb but a dense and atmospheric complex in the old city. Visitors describe it as a maze of narrow corridors and stairways opening onto several graves: by tradition those of unnamed companions who fell with the saint, together with the resting places of later *pirs*, or Sufi spiritual guides, and other holy men who chose to be buried in his shadow.

The tomb of the saint himself is approached through a mirrored gateway ornamented with Qur'anic inscriptions and geometric tilework, in the idiom of Punjabi shrine decoration, and the passages leading to it are often lined with prostrating and praying pilgrims.

In the reign of Aurangzeb his tomb, held to be a place where divine lights are made manifest, was visited by small and great alike. Today the shrine, like many of the province's major *darbars*, is administered under the auspices of the Punjab Auqaf Department, the government's department of religious endowments.

## Devotion and Festivals

As a martyr's shrine, the site carries the particular charisma that Punjabi popular religion attaches to the *shahid*, the martyr who is believed to be present and living, and whose intercession and *baraka*, the blessing that flows from a holy person, are sought by the faithful. Devotees come throughout the year to offer prayers, tie votive threads and seek blessings.

The shrine is the focus of an *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of the saint's death. On such occasions the surrounding lanes fill with pilgrims, and the customary features of a Punjabi shrine festival accompany the gathering: devotional music, the recitation of poetry, and the distribution of *langar*, the food of the free communal kitchen.

Because Imam Sahib is regarded as the spiritual founder of Muslim Sialkot, his memory is woven into the civic and religious identity of the city itself, and local commemorations periodically recall his role in its history.

## The Setting

Sialkot lies in the Rachna *doab*, a tract of land between two rivers. It was a bustling city in the foothills of the Himalayas, famous for its silk, and an important post on the trade route between West Asia and Delhi. It is located right on the border, and historically it gave the Punjab a connection to Kashmir.

It is an ancient city. Its earliest recorded mention is found in ancient Greek texts going as far back as 322 BC, which call it Sagala, and in the reign of Aurangzeb it was counted a city of the ancients. It was once called Shalkot, a city held to be named in the *Mahabharata*, the Sanskrit epic, and it was also called Salkot. Legends of its founding differ; the founder most often named is Raja Salbahan, also called Salivahan, whose memorial was a fort of fired brick.

In legend the raja's son was Puran Bhagat. Tradition tells that Puran was thrown into a well, his hands and feet cut off; the well lay a little distance from the city and could still be seen in the nineteenth century, and devotees raised a temple around it, the most important temple in the ancient city of Sialkot. It is told, too, that when Puran came to Sialkot a dried up garden revived on his arrival.

In former times Sialkot was the seat of government of the Punjab, its habitation stretching two or three *kos*, an old measure of distance. In early times, when Lahore lay in ruins, it became the seat of government of the country, until Sultan Mahmud's conquest and Malik Ayaz's rebuilding of Lahore. The city became part of the Muslim Sultanate when Shahabuddin Muhammad Ghori captured the Punjab in 1192. Ghori also came to Sialkot after failing at Lahore in the year 580 AH, a year of the Islamic calendar; he repaired the city's old fort, whose foundation was ancient, and left his army there, and Khusraw Malik with the Khokhars besieged the fort for a time without success. Many Sufi missionaries settled in the region, making it predominantly Muslim and giving it a rich Sufi tradition.

The Mughal emperor Babur described Jat pastoralist groups in the Sialkot region. Under Akbar, Raja Man Singh, the *faujdar*, or military commander, of Jammu and the *jagirdar* of Sialkot, the holder of its land grant, repaired the fort and improved the city. In Jahangir's time Safdar Khan rebuilt the fort and its tower, and most later governors made repairs too.

In the reign of Aurangzeb Sialkot surpassed every other town of its *suba*, or province, in population, and it was a city of crafts. Its paper, of the kinds called Mansinghi, silk or *hariri*, and *khass-i Jahangiri*, was white and durable and was sent to other regions; its *karchikan* embroidery in silk and *kalabatun*, thread of gold or silver, went all over the world; and its *jamdhar* and *katari* daggers and its *barchhi* spears were well made. Gardens surrounded the city, among them the garden of Nazar Muhammad, and the buildings of the *qanungos*, the keepers of the land records, and of some others were most pleasing and delightful. A stream rising in the hills of Jammu flowed below the city. When it flooded in the rains, all the people of Sialkot, great and small, played in the water, naked but for a loincloth and carrying leather water skins, and anyone from the city who was far away remembered it with pleasure.

The city was also a seat of learning and a gathering place of scholars. Under Akbar, Maulana Kamal al-Din Husain left Kashmir, came to Sialkot in the year 971 AH and established teaching there. Under Shah Jahan, Maulvi Abd al-Hakim spread the sciences, wrote glosses and drew students to his *madrasa*, his religious school; his son Maulvi Abdullah ran the *madrasa* after him and was called the imam of his time. Sialkot was one of the centres of Islamic scholarship in the Mughal period, and Ahmad Sirhindi studied there.

Other holy men of the city are remembered too. Shah Daula's first master lived in Sialkot, and Shah Daula himself is said to have left the city after disputes with its people, moving after some years to Gujrat. Stories are also told of the *faqir*, or wandering mystic, Hamza Ghaus, whose curse was pronounced in the centre of the market square, and of the fear of him that gripped Sialkot.

In the early twenty first century Sialkot had become one of the most important cities in Pakistan. Its business community financed its own airport, which handles international flights, and the highway, and on the way into the city stood several factories and many mounds, probably archaeological ruins, converted into graveyards.

## Legacy

Imam Ali-ul-Haq occupies a place in Sialkot comparable to that of a patron saint: the figure to whom the city's Muslim character is popularly traced, and whose martyrdom sanctifies its very ground. That so little of his life is settled, not even the century in which he lived, has done nothing to diminish his standing in local devotion. This may indeed be characteristic of the earliest layer of Punjabi Muslim sacred history, in which the memory of the warrior saint who "opened" a region to Islam is preserved more in cult and story than in dated record.

That memory has long been written down. An account of his martyrdom, the *Waqa'i Sialkot* of Muhammad Muqim, is dated 1071 AH; it was edited by Dr Abdullah Chaghatai and published at Lahore in 1977. His shrine remains a living centre of pilgrimage and one of the defining landmarks of old Sialkot.
