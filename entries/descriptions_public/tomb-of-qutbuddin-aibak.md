## Overview

Tucked amid the crowded lanes of Aibak Road in the Anarkali quarter of Lahore, a modest garden tomb marks the burial place of Sultan Qutb ud-Din Aibak, founder of the Mamluk, or Slave, dynasty and the first ruler of the Delhi Sultanate, the realm of the Muslim sultans who ruled from Delhi. A slave who rose through service to the Ghurid ruler Muhammad Ghori, he established independent rule based in Lahore after Ghori's death, laying the foundations of Muslim dynastic government in the subcontinent, and he is remembered too as the patron who commissioned Delhi's Qutb Minar. The tomb is not the shrine of a Sufi, or Muslim mystic: it hosts no *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of a saint's death, no pilgrimage tradition and no ongoing devotional practice. It is maintained as a protected historical monument, valued for marking the thirteenth century founding moment of Muslim dynastic rule in South Asia, centred, briefly, on Lahore itself.

Aibak died in Lahore in 1210 after a fall from his horse while playing *chaugan*, an early form of polo. The tomb's own history has been anything but continuous: for long the site was a simple, informal grave hemmed in by the houses of the Anarkali bazaar, and the structure visitors see today is essentially a reconstruction of the 1970s.

## The Sultan

Aibak was born around 1150 in Turkestan and sold into slavery as a child. He was called Aibak, it is said, because his little finger was broken. Bought by the Ghurid ruler Muhammad Ghori, also known as Sultan Shihab al-Din and as Muʿizz al-Din Muhammad bin Sam, he rose through his master's service, and after the decisive Second Battle of Tarain in 1192 he came to command the Ghurid territories in northern India.

When Ghori went back to Ghazni, he left Aibak, his chosen slave, behind as his deputy to govern Hindustan. Accounts differ over where Aibak was first posted, whether at the head of the forces in Delhi itself or as viceroy at Kuhram, a small town seventy *kos* from Delhi, the *kos* being an old measure of distance. As deputy he took the fort of Meerut in the shortest time and set governors in every place; in the second year he took the fort of Kol, and then Gwalior, Budaun and other forts. His troops, stationed at Indraprastha near Delhi, had to make several invasions into the Doab, the land between two rivers, and the regions around Ajmer to consolidate their power, and over the area he appointed Saiyid Husain Mashhadi as *darogha*, or military governor. The Saiyid served the Khwaja, a title given to Sufi saints, with great devotion.

He marched on Gujarat and took revenge on Rai Bhim Dev of Gujarat for Ghori's earlier defeat; he plundered the country, returned to Delhi, and had the *khutba*, the Friday sermon, read and coins struck in Ghori's name. When Ghori made his tenth campaign, against the Khokhars near Lahore, Aibak came from Delhi to join him, and together they chastised the tribe. His captains carried the conquest east: the beginning of Islam in Bengal is traced to Malik Muhammad Bakhtiyar, one of Aibak's great *amirs*, or nobles. A general who sent his booty from Bihar to his master at Delhi is remembered too, in a story that takes in a rutting elephant, the governorship of Bihar and an order to take Bengal.

Delhi was his seat, and he lived, as did his eventual successor Iltutmish, in the fort of Rai Pithora. He is remembered as the patron who commissioned the famous Qutb Minar there, and in Ajmer he erected a beautiful mosque, not far from which Muʿinuddin died in 1236. He is not to be confused with another Qutbuddin, Qutbuddin Bakhtiyar Kaki of Farghana, who died in 1235 and whose modest compound at Merauli, near the Qutb Minar in Delhi, is usually filled with pious pilgrims.

Iltutmish was Aibak's slave before he was his son-in-law. Aibak, himself a former slave, took a fancy to him during a visit to Ghazni after his expedition to Anhilwara in 1196, and was able to buy him in Delhi. He adopted Iltutmish and married him to his daughter. After the conquest of Gwalior he gave Iltutmish its command, and then a district with its region and, step by step, Budaun, until Iltutmish rose to be *amir al-umara*, the chief of the nobles, and received from the Sultan his letter of manumission, the deed that set him free. His rise to prominence came between 1197 or 1198 and 1206.

## Sultan in His Own Right

Ghori was assassinated in 1206 and died without a son. Aibak then established independent rule based in Lahore, though accounts differ over whether his reign began there or at Delhi. Coming out of Delhi, he reached Lahore, took the throne in his own right, and on the eleventh of Rabiʿ al-Awwal, a month of the Islamic calendar, had coins struck and the *khutba* read in his own name. From Firozkoh, Ghori's nephew, Sultan Ghiyas al-Din, sent him the parasol, an emblem of royalty, and the trappings of kingship and addressed him by the title of Sultan.

His rival for the Ghurid inheritance was Taj al-Din Yildiz, another of Ghori's special slaves, who had been given the title of sultan at Ghazni after his master's death and came on to the Punjab to take the throne of Hind. The two quarrelled over Lahore and fought a pitched battle, and Yildiz was routed. Accounts differ over what followed, but for a time Aibak's power reached as far as Ghazni itself, and after his victory he gave himself to rule in complete independence.

He ruled with great ability. In all he governed Hindustan for twenty years, and he reigned as sultan from 1206 until his death in 1210. Because he gave lakhs, hundreds of thousands, to the needy, he was called in Hind Lakh Bakhsh, the giver of lakhs. His giving was praised at length in Persian: the hand of his generosity, it was said, brushed the dust of poverty from the faces of the poor. That praise closes with a couplet in his honour:

باهمت او حوصله دریا تنگ
بارفعت او مرتبه گردون پست

## Death and Succession

He died in Lahore in November 1210, though accounts of the year differ. He was playing *chaugan* when he fell from his horse; as one chronicler writing in Persian put it, he carried the ball of his life to the field of the hereafter. The game had come to the subcontinent with the Central Asian armies, and a polo match is the scene of a *kafi*, a short mystical lyric, by the poet Madho Lal Hussein, though the poem does not name the Sultan:

*Let me stand and watch for a few days / the polo tournament. Who wins, who loses, / whose brisk horse makes a clever move? … Let's see who hits the ball / across the field. The crowd cheers and jeers* (translated by Naveed Alam)

He was succeeded, eventually, by his son-in-law Shams al-Din Iltutmish. At first the nobles seated his heir, Aram Bakhsh, on the throne in Lahore as Sultan Aram Shah, and for a short while Lahore was a royal seat again; accounts differ over whether Aram Shah was Aibak's own son or an adopted one. Under Aram Shah the affairs of state did not prosper, and after a year Iltutmish deposed him: when Iltutmish came, Aram Shah moved from Lahore towards Delhi, drew up his ranks and fled after little fighting. Iltutmish, himself remembered as a slave king, went on to rule from Delhi, and under him *muftis*, jurists of Islamic law, were appointed for Lahore. In his reign Yildiz rose again; he took Multan, came to Lahore and plundered the Punjab freely.

## The Tomb

Accounts differ over what stood over the grave in the centuries after Aibak's death, and historians dispute whether a substantial mausoleum ever stood there in the medieval period. At first, it is said, there was only the grave. Iltutmish is said to have ordered a simple small room, barely able to enclose it; built at first of mud, it was bricked in during Akbar's reign. Later a marble tomb stood there, until it was pulled down by Ranjit Singh.

In the nineteenth century the grave lay outside the Lahori Gate, on the north side, in the Sadr Bazaar of Anarkali. A house stood on its land, and courtesans occupied the place. Under Sikh rule lofty buildings of stone had stood there, until the Maharaja demolished them all and sent them off to Amritsar, and only a single grave on a raised platform remained. Old people still remembered a dome of marble two storeys high, unmatched anywhere round Lahore. For long the site was a simple, informal grave hemmed in by the houses of the Anarkali bazaar.

The tomb visitors see today is essentially a reconstruction of the 1970s by Pakistan's Department of Archaeology and Museums, though accounts of its date differ. It was built in 1972 at the order of Prime Minister Zulfiqar Ali Bhutto, given after the Bangladesh war, and Bhutto remarked that Aibak "was a great and kind ruler and deserved the respect of the poor"; the design was led by the architect Kamil Khan Mumtaz. Deliberately evoking the style of the early Sultanate period, it stands within a small walled garden and is maintained purely as a protected historical monument.

## The Setting

The tomb stands in Anarkali, whose market is named after the legendary lover of the Mughal emperor Jahangir. By the 1880s Anarkali was a cantonment, or military station, and a bazaar. In the early twenty first century the area had become the largest tyre market in the city, and the Pak Tea House, just a few shops away, once famed for its gatherings of the city's leading intelligentsia, was on its way to becoming yet another tyre shop.

Aibak's Lahore was a city the Ghurids had only lately taken. Khusraw Shah, son of Bahram Shah, driven from Ghazni by the Ghurids, took Lahore and died there, and his son Khusraw Malik held it for twenty eight years while Ghori attacked the city again and again. In the end Khusraw Malik submitted and was taken to Ghazni, where he died, and Ghori set his own deputy in Lahore. With that surrender the rule of the Ghurid sultans in Hind began, and the Ghaznavid government came to its end.

Old Lahore lost many of its monuments in the Sikh period. In Ranjit Singh's time red, black and marble stones in the neighbourhood of Lahore were torn down, and when the cantonments of his armies were built, the bricks of old tombs and houses were pulled out.

## Legacy

The tomb is valued chiefly for its symbolic role, marking the founding moment of Muslim dynastic rule in South Asia, centred, briefly, on Lahore itself. From Aibak's time Delhi was fixed as the capital of the Muslim sultans. In the nineteenth century one historian of the city held Aibak's grave to be older than any other building near Lahore, and wrote that before the Sultan the land of Hindustan had been without a lamp.

Aibak is also said to have wanted Lahore's dead buried together, setting aside land south of the village of Mozang as a graveyard for at least 150,000 dead, the origin of the present Miani Sahib graveyard.

Bhutto's tribute to a great and kind ruler who deserved the respect of the poor stands beside an older memory, that of the Lakh Bakhsh, whose giving was praised in Persian prose and verse.
