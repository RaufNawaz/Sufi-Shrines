## Overview

Gurdwara Janam Asthan Guru Ram Das stands in the Chuna Mandi quarter of the Walled City of Lahore, on the site traditionally revered as the birthplace and childhood home of Guru Ram Das (1534 to 1581), the fourth of the ten Sikh Gurus and the founder of the city of Amritsar. A gurdwara is a Sikh house of worship, and *Janam Asthan* means the place of birth. The shrine lies along the old *Shahi Guzargah*, the royal road that ran from the Delhi Gate to the Lahore Fort, near the Fort itself and the Begum Shahi Mosque, and it joins Lahore's several great gurdwaras in binding the city to the early history of the Sikh faith.

The Guru's childhood home is said to have survived here until the era of the Sikh Empire, and the gurdwara was raised on the spot by Maharaja Ranjit Singh. By the 1880s it was a stately building made on the pattern of the Darbar of Amritsar. Sikhs showed the blessed house the utmost reverence, and Hindus high and low also came to it and held the place extremely pure.

## The Guru

Ram Das was born in the Chuna Mandi bazaar of Lahore in 1534, into a family of modest means, and in his boyhood he was known as Bhai Jetha. His birth is placed in the month of Kartik. His father was Har Das, and the family, residents of Lahore, were Sodhi Khatris, a caste that has been called an ancient caste of kings. The house of his mother and father stood on this spot. Accounts of his boyhood differ, but this is revered as the place where he was both born and brought up. Orphaned young, he was raised by his grandmother.

He came under the influence of Guru Amar Das, the third Guru, who belonged to the Khatri Bhalla caste, and served him with great devotion and humility. Jetha followed him to Khadur, where he looked after the *langar*, the free communal kitchen, at the Guru's *darbar*, his court, and later to Goindwal. He is remembered as having served in the *langar* for twelve years.

Amar Das's wife, it is said, recommended the young Jetha as a husband for their younger daughter, Bibi Bhani, and the Guru was relieved to learn that he was a Sodhi Khatri. The marriage took place at Goindwal. Jetha's own family complained of his servile attitude towards his in-laws.

Amar Das put his two sons-in-law and his two sons through a few tests, and chose his son-in-law Jetha as his successor. All the Sikhs accepted the choice except Amar Das's eldest son. At his death Amar Das seated his son-in-law in his place, although he had children of his own. Ram Das was Guru from 1574 until his death in 1581, and for those seven years he was the object of his disciples' prostration. He is named among the later Gurus whose stewardship Baba Budha witnessed.

With him the Guruship came to the Sodhi family. He was the first Sodhi Guru, and the office remained within the Sodhi family until Guru Gobind Singh, by whose time it was understood that it would pass only within the Sodhi caste. After Ram Das, it has been said, the succession became a family affair, in which the son of the Guru was preferred over others.

## Amritsar and the Succession

As the fourth Guru, Ram Das is remembered above all as the founder of the settlement that grew into Amritsar. He laid the foundation of Ramdaspur, which in time became Amritsar. The land is said to have been a huge tract that the Mughal emperor Akbar granted to Bibi Bhani, the daughter of Amar Das and the wife of Ram Das, when the emperor met Guru Amar Das. There Ram Das first dug the sacred pool, the *Amrit Sarovar*, the pool of nectar from which the city takes its name, and he is known for starting the construction of the Harmandir Sahib.

To pay for the building of the pool he introduced the system of the *masands*, deputies whom he appointed in different regions to spread the Guru's message and collect offerings, and under him the revenue of the Guru's seat began to increase. He also composed the *Lavan*, the four hymns that form the heart of the Sikh marriage rite and are sung at every *Anand Karaj*, the Sikh wedding ceremony, to this day, and his verses hold a cherished place in the *Guru Granth Sahib*, the Sikh scripture.

That the founder of Amritsar was himself born in Lahore gives this shrine its particular resonance, joining the two cities most closely associated with the early Gurus.

His own succession divided his sons. Accounts differ on how many sons Ram Das had, but two of them, Prithi Chand and the younger Arjan, came to quarrel over an immense property, essentially the whole city of Ramdaspur and the area around it. Ram Das made Arjan his heir and successor in place of Prithi Chand, to Prithi Chand's anger, and passing him over began a conflict that was to last for several generations.

Lahore has its part in the story. It is told that Ram Das sent Arjan to Lahore for a wedding, and that Ram Das, on discovering Prithi Chand's deception, called Arjan back and appointed him Guru; Sikh tradition traces the quarrel between the brothers to Arjan's trip to Lahore. Prithi Chand's rival shrine would lie on the route from Lahore to Amritsar; near Lahore, the Bedian road is the old route to Amritsar.

## The Gurdwara

The Guru's childhood home is said to have survived on this spot until the era of the Sikh Empire. Accounts differ over when the gurdwara was built and at whose wish. Tradition relates that Maharaja Ranjit Singh, the ruler of the Sikh state at Lahore, was asked to raise a fitting shrine here by his wife, Maharani Datar Kaur, during the celebrations that attended the birth of their son Kharak Singh in 1801. The Maharaja agreed and acquired the surrounding plots, the houses of Mohalla Saudagran that adjoined the spot. The gurdwara he built still stands, much altered.

Its place within the dense fabric of the old city, close to the Fort and the imperial mosque of the Mughals, speaks to the layered history of Lahore, where the monuments of Mughal, Sikh and later ages stand shoulder to shoulder.

In the 1880s the gurdwara stood in the southern lane of a bazaar. A lofty brick gateway opened on the bazaar, with a stair in the *deorhi*, the entrance hall. Within lay an upper courtyard, with a well in its north west corner and a *bel*, the wood apple tree. A standard, that is a flag, had been raised in the courtyard, and on the east stood a stepped *jharokha*, a projecting balcony window, of ornamented brick.

The building itself was made on the pattern of the Darbar of Amritsar. It had four doors on its four sides, and though there was no dome at its centre, it had four *shah-nashins*, raised alcoves, each of two storeys.

## Devotion and Festivals

In the 1880s an elaborate floor covering lay spread in the gurdwara, and the Granth Sahib was kept there with the greatest reverence. Sikhs came in numbers, and the Granth was read twice daily before large gatherings. There was singing, and not only by Sikhs: *qawwals*, singers of *qawwali*, the devotional music of the Sufis, delighted the people with monotheist *kafis*, short mystical lyrics.

A larger assembly came round every eighth day. In the third watch a great gathering of men and women took place, a selection from the Granths of the Gurus was read, and *shabads*, hymns, imbued with the oneness of God were sung; the gathering was held to be worth seeing. Once, in the days of Holi, the Hindu spring festival, the Singh Sabha held a great assembly here, lit up the whole temple and the bazaar, and sang *shabads* all night.

In the 1880s the gurdwara's *pujari*, its priest, was Jeet Singh, and the trusted agent of the circle was a man named Kanhiya Lal.

## Partition and After

The Partition of 1947 fell heavily upon the Sikh shrines of Lahore. On the eve of Partition, Tara Singh, a prominent Sikh leader of the time, drew his *kirpan*, the sword that Sikhs carry, on the stairs of the Punjab Assembly in Lahore and declared that the time to take revenge for the Gurus had come. Amid the communal violence that swept the city, some eighteen Sikhs are reported to have been killed within the premises of this gurdwara, one of many such tragedies that emptied the western Punjab of its Sikh population almost overnight.

In the decades that followed, the shrine, like the other historic gurdwaras of Pakistan, passed into the care of the Evacuee Trust Property Board. Across Pakistani Punjab, hundreds of temples and gurdwaras were taken over by refugees and turned into households, and some were taken over and destroyed by the migrants. It is now rare to meet a Sikh in Lahore, a city once ruled by a Sikh and the seat of the Sikhs' political strength.

In recent years the gurdwara has been reopened to worship and to pilgrimage, and the birthday of Guru Ram Das draws Sikh visitors to the narrow lanes of Chuna Mandi to honour the Guru at the place of his birth. Its careful upkeep forms part of the wider, if uneven, effort to preserve and restore Pakistan's Sikh heritage.

## The Setting

Lahore drew the Gurus before Ram Das and after him. Tradition tells that Guru Nanak came to the city when Daulat Khan Lodhi governed it, and that Duni Chand, the *amil*, the revenue collector, of Lahore, became his disciple and gave away his seven lakh rupees; Nanak and Mardana also reached Lahore on their way home. Guru Arjan spent a great deal of time in the city; summoned to Lahore by the emperor Jahangir, he was assassinated there and his remains interred. Guru Hargobind, too, spent a great deal of time in Lahore. Later in the line, Guru Har Rai reached Lahore with his forces and met Dara Shikoh there after the prince's defeat by Aurangzeb, and left Dara's camp at Lahore as others had slipped away.

In Aurangzeb's time Lahore was a great city and a mighty metropolis, with mosques beyond counting in every lane and bazaar. The gates of its walled city, the Delhi Gate among them, are most often counted as twelve. The Roshani Gate, one of the gateways that lead into the walled city, is between the Alamgiri Gate and the Royal Mosque.

The poet Bulleh Shah, who died in 1758, lived through a time when the Punjab was being persistently rent by petty chiefs and marauders. The full bitterness of his line 'Punjab has gone to the dogs', it has been said, comes through only against that backdrop:

*Now hell has opened its bogs. / Punjab has gone to the dogs. / This hell is the nethermost hell.* (translated by Taufiq Rafat)

Another of his lyrics says:

*The door of doom and torment has opened, and Panjab is in a bad state.* (translated by Christopher Shackle)

A verse in a third lyric has been taken as an allusion to the Sikhs, whose guerrilla horsemen ultimately destroyed Mughal authority in the Punjab.

Ranjit Singh, who ruled the Punjab for forty years, came to Lahore in 1799 and was greeted with enthusiasm by a population sick of the lawlessness of that time. He was crowned ruler of the Punjab in 1801. Across the Punjab he spent large amounts of money on the renovation and expansion of many gurdwaras, and attached vast tracts of land to them so that their revenues could support their functioning. After his death the throne of the Punjab passed to his son, Maharaja Kharak Singh, whose birth tradition ties to the founding of this gurdwara. The Sikh state ended with Dalip Singh, who left Lahore.

Sikh building in Lahore, it has been observed, draws inspiration from both Hindu and Muslim architecture, as the Sikh religion picks up from both Hinduism and Islam. The Sikhs made smaller the domes that were first part of Muslim buildings, and Sikh shrines carry depictions of the Sikh Gurus and, in older buildings, sometimes of Hindu deities.

## Legacy

For the Sikh community, Gurdwara Janam Asthan Guru Ram Das is doubly precious. It marks the earthly beginning of the Guru who gave Sikhism its holiest city and its wedding liturgy, and it stands as a reminder that Lahore, for so long a great centre of Sikh power and devotion, was also his cradle. Amid the crowded bazaars of the Walled City, the shrine keeps alive the memory of a boy born here in 1534 whose life would reshape the faith and give the world Amritsar.

The Guru's image appears elsewhere too. His name stands among the figures in the frescoes of the dome at Jhaman, and in the nineteenth century he was pictured among the Gurus in the niche of a *samadh*, a memorial shrine.
