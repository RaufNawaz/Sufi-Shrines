## Overview

The shrine of Hazrat Syed Musa Pak Shaheed stands in Multan, the 'City of Saints', where his *darbar*, or shrine, is counted among the city's notable shrines. Syed Musa Pak was a revered saint of the sixteenth century in the Qadiri order, the Sufi order founded by Abd al-Qadir Jilani, and the ancestor of the Gilani (Musa Pak) sayyids, one of Multan's most eminent religious families. The shrine is a continuous place of pilgrimage, where devotees come to recite the Fatiha, the opening chapter of the Quran, to seek intercession and to honour the saint.

He is honoured with the titles *Pak*, "the pure", and *Shaheed*, "the martyr". Musa Pak carried the Qadiri order of Abd al-Qadir Jilani in Multan and founded a spiritual line that remained influential in the city for centuries.

## The Saint

Syed Musa, called Musa Pak Shaheed, was a Sayyid of the Gilani line, the descendants of the great Baghdad saint Shaikh Abd al-Qadir Jilani, and he flourished in Multan in the sixteenth century. He was the beloved son of Sayyid Hamid Ganj Bakhsh Gilani Uchi, a father praised as the exemplar of the noble Sayyids and the choicest of the great shaikhs. When Musa had completed his training before his father, he was given the title Jamal al-Din Abu al-Hasan, and his name at full length is Sayyid Jamal al-Din Abu al-Hasan Musa Pak Shaheed Gilani Multani.

Learned and devout, he was a shaykh of the Qadiri order and a guide of many disciples, and he is remembered for a life of piety and for the sanctity that earned him the epithet *Pak*, "the pure". He is said to have had a special *nisbat*, an inner bond, with the spirituality of Ghaus al-Azam, "the greatest helper", so that its presence was constant with him, and to have been blessed hundreds of times with visions of the Prophet, waking and sleeping. His whole life, it is said, passed in austerity, struggle, worship, teaching and instruction.

Accounts of how he came to his father's seat differ, but he is remembered as having succeeded to the *masnad*, the seat of spiritual authority, after his father's death. Through him and his descendants the presence of the Qadiri order and of the Gilani line was firmly established in Multan, and the Musa Pak Gilani family became one of the leading religious and landed houses of the region.

## Disciples and Writings

Great *ulama*, scholars of religion, became his disciples, and Shaikh Abdul Haq Muhaddith Dehlvi, too, was his sincere disciple. Abdul Haq became attached to him on 6 Shawwal 985 AH (1577 CE). He took the *bai'at*, the oath of allegiance by which a disciple enters a Sufi order, at Musa Pak's hand, and the shaikh gave his disciple special attention. After the saint's death Abdul Haq went on to Shaikh Abd al-Wahhab, the *khalifa*, or deputy, of Shaikh Ali Muttaqi, and received from him *faiz*, spiritual grace, and a *khirqa-e tabarruk*, a robe given as a blessing.

Musa Pak left a work of his own, *Taisir al-Thaqalain*, on litanies and observances (*awrad* and *ma'mulat*), which was printed at Ferozepur in 1309 AH. Another work, *Bahr al-Sarair*, is devoted to his conditions and practices.

## His Martyrdom

Tradition honours Musa Pak as a *shaheed*, a martyr, and he is said to have been martyred at the hands of ill-wishers of the Langah tribe. Accounts of the year of his death differ, and there is no settled view; it is placed between 998 and 1001 AH. His disciple Shaikh Abdul Haq Muhaddith Dehlvi marked the death with a chronogram, a phrase whose letters, read as numbers, give a year, and from it he derived the year 998 AH. The chronogram is a phrase of three words:

شمع جمع سیادت

*The chronogram on the saint's death by his disciple Shaikh Abdul Haq Muhaddith Dehlvi, from which he derived the year 998 AH.*

He was buried in Multan, where his shrine arose.

## The Shrine and Its Devotion

The shrine of Syed Musa Pak in Multan is kept by his Gilani descendants. It maintains the customary devotions of a Multan *darbar*: prayer, *qawwali*, the devotional music of the Sufis, and the free kitchen. Its annual *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of the saint's death, gathers the faithful. In the 1870s his *rauza*, or tomb, in Multan was a place of pilgrimage for God's creatures, and today it forms part of the great constellation of shrines that gives Multan its identity.

Another building, at Uch Sharif rather than at Multan, may bear on this saint. Next to the Gilani shrine at Uch stands a fifteenth century structure, octagonal, built in the typical Multani fashion with blue tiles, and now in a dilapidated condition. A board beside it, put up by the Department of Archaeology, says that the grave of Moosa Pak Shaheed once lay there and that his body was later shifted to Multan. The link with the saint of Multan rests on the name and on the board's statement that the body went to Multan; the structure at Uch is a separate building from the shrine in Multan.

## The Setting

The first of the Qadiri line to arrive in the Punjab was Sayyid Muhammad Ghaus Uchi Jili Gilani, whose ancestors had left Baghdad in the time of Hulagu Khan. He came to Multan by way of Khurasan and settled at Uch, which he entered in 887 AH. This is the story of the order, not of Musa Pak's own household. In the age of Guru Nanak, in one historian's words, the Qadiris had "made the Panjab the main centre of their activities". Sind and the Punjab also produced hymns in praise of the great masters of the mystical orders, with Abd al-Qadir Gilani above them all.

The Langah, the tribe named in the story of Musa Pak's martyrdom, once ruled Multan. In Multan the Langah raised the banner of sovereignty. Their chief seized the fort of Multan from Shaikh Yusuf Qureshi by a ruse and took a royal title, and Langah sultans followed him. Their rule ended with the Arghun conquest.

Of Multan in Guru Nanak's time one writer has said that "Multan was one of the biggest cities of Punjab and was known for the religious shrines of Muslim saints, and Jain and Hindu temples. It was a sacred space for people of all religious denominations". In another writer's words, by the time Nanak came to Multan "the city was famous as a major centre of Sufi learning and philosophy".

Multan had its place in the region's poetry as well. Of the vernacular poets, one scholar of mystical poetry wrote that "Mecca and Medina (and for the Sindhi and Panjabi, also Multan) are inside the heart". In a poem by Madho Lal Hussein the city appears as a market, in a bride's trousseau:

*The dress / someone carried across the snows / all the way from Kashmir. The dress / someone brought from Gujarat… The dress bought in Multan* (translated by Naveed Alam)

## Legacy

Syed Musa Pak Shaheed is remembered as a chief bearer of the Qadiri order in Multan and the founder of the distinguished Musa Pak Gilani line, a saint whose purity and martyrdom won him lasting veneration and whose family carried his influence through the later history of the city. Among his disciples was Shaikh Abdul Haq Muhaddith Dehlvi, and his own *Taisir al-Thaqalain* was printed at Ferozepur in 1309 AH. In the 1870s his tomb in Multan was a place of pilgrimage, and his shrine remains a revered *darbar* of the 'City of Saints'.
