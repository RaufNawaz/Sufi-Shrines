## Overview

The shrine of Baba Shah Chiragh stands beside the Lahore High Court on The Mall, in the shadow of the colonial Aiwan-e-Auqaf building that now bears its name. It is the tomb of Sayyid Abdul Razzaq, a Sufi, or Muslim mystic, of the Qadiri lineage, who was known as Shah Chiragh, from *chiragh*, a lamp, and who lived in the reign of the Mughal emperor Shah Jahan and died in 1657. Raised by order of the emperor Aurangzeb, the tomb holds eight graves of his family, his own among them, and it survives as a piece of Mughal Lahore among the government buildings of the modern city.

His family were Gilani Sayyids, descendants of the Prophet, who traced their line to Sheikh Abdul Qadir Gilani of Baghdad, the founder of the Qadiri order, and who came to Lahore from Uch, in what is now Bahawalpur. A mosque was later added beside the tomb under Zakariya Khan, the Mughal viceroy of Lahore. Under British rule the mosque became a government office and the precinct was absorbed into the Aiwan-e-Auqaf complex, and since 1973 the shrine has been in the care of the Auqaf Department, the government's department of religious endowments.

## The Saint

Shah Chiragh is remembered as one of the shaikhs of the Qadiri order, a man of worship and discipline, of abstinence and piety, learned in the outward sciences. His real name was Sayyid Abdul Razzaq, son of Sayyid Abdul Wahab Gilani, and Shah Chiragh was his title. Abdul Razzaq and Shah Chiragh are most often taken to be one man, though accounts differ, and Lahore has known other men of both names.

His family's line ran back from his father, Sayyid Abdul Wahab, through Sayyid Abdul Qadir Thalith, Muhammad Ghaus Bala Pir, Zain al-Abidin and Sayyid Abdul Qadir Sani to Sayyid Muhammad Ghaus Uchi Halabi Gilani. Accounts of his descent differ in detail, but the grandfather most often named is Sayyid Abdul Qadir Thalith. Muhammad Ghaus Uchi was the first of the Qadiri line to come to the Punjab. His ancestors had left Baghdad in the time of Hulagu Khan, and he came by way of Khurasan to Multan and settled at Uch, which he is said to have entered in 887 AH, a year of the Islamic calendar.

Like Shah Chiragh, his grandfather Abdul Qadir Thalith lies in a tomb around Lahore; both men descended from Abdul Qadir Sani, a son of Muhammad Ghaus Uchi. The family's road to Lahore ran in two stages. Its elders came from Uch, in the territory of Bahawalpur State, first to a *qasba*, a small town, in the Montgomery district, and from there they came to live in Lahore.

His name has its own story. On the day he was born, it is said, his father declared that a lamp had been born into their house, by which the whole family would be lit, and so he was named Shah Chiragh. Accounts differ over who gave him the name.

He loved travel, and journeyed through many lands for the sake of experience; he also went on pilgrimage to the Haramain Sharifain, the two holy cities of Mecca and Medina. The emperor Shah Jahan was wholly devoted to him, and tried hard to give one of his daughters in marriage to him or to his son, Sayyid Mustafa. The offer was not accepted.

Shah Dargahi Qadri, a man given to visions and miracles, to sincerity and purity, to abstinence and discipline, is remembered as the *murid*, the pledged disciple, of a Hazrat Shah Chiragh Gilani Lahori, though it is not settled that this master was the saint of the Mall. Shah Dargahi's own grave lies beside that of Shah Ismail Muhaddith, with a well whose cure became a standing ritual of Lahore.

Accounts of the date of his death differ, but he is most often said to have died on the twenty second of Dhu al-Qa'da, the eleventh month of the Islamic year, in 1068 AH. He was buried in Lahore. A chronogram on his death, a verse whose letters, read as numbers, give a year, is attributed to Mufti Ghulam Sarwar, the author of a *tazkira*, a book of the lives of the saints. It ends with this line:

گفت سرور میر شمس العارفین

*The line names its poet, Sarwar, and calls the saint Mir Shams al-Arifin, the sun of those who know God.*

## The Shrine

The tomb stands in Lahore, adjoining the High Court. The ground was once the quarter of Langar Khan Baloch, a *murid* of the family, and that is why the tomb was built there; in the nineteenth century the area was known as Mohalla Langar Khan. By the 1880s the tomb was counted among the Muslim buildings outside the city.

It was built by order of the emperor Aurangzeb, Alamgir. Of limestone, on a square plan and in a restrained Mughal style, it has its door facing south, a roof of brick and a grand dome above. Its façades carry niches with cusped arches and panelled cartouches, and inside, traces of floral fresco decoration remain. In the nineteenth century the tomb had a wooden door with a lattice, and an inscription of the *kalima*, the profession of faith, could be seen there.

Inside lie eight graves, Shah Chiragh's own and those of his family, so that the tomb is a family shrine across generations rather than the resting place of one man. Accounts differ over which of his kin lie with him; one of the graves is that of a *sahibzada*, a saint's son, named Zain al-Abidin. In the nineteenth century there was also a walled enclosure, a *chardiwari*, holding graves, with a well, trees and a broken floor, and its ground, it seems, had once been a small garden.

The mosque beside the tomb was added in 1716, under the patronage of Zakariya Khan, the Mughal viceroy of Lahore, in the reign of Muhammad Shah Rangeela. Khan Bahadur Zakariya Khan ordered it built because his mother was a *murida*, a woman disciple, of this family. She had willed that her grave should be here and that a mosque should be built from her jewellery. In the nineteenth century the mosque had five domes and four cells, and the grave of the Nawab's mother lay under a tree.

Under British rule the mosque was made a *kothi*, a residence, where Deputy Commissioners among others lived, and then the government's Accountant's office was set up there; it still housed that office in the 1880s. By then the mosque stood in an enclosure of its own, apart from the tomb's. The complex had been split in two by colonial occupation, the mosque taken into government use as an office and the tomb left in the hands of the Gilani Sayyids, the saint's own lineage.

In the 1880s this part of Lahore was also remembered as the quarter of Sayyid Chiragh Shah Gilani, where Gilani Sayyids lived, east of the quarter of Mauj Darya Bukhari. The quarter had been settled as a *jagir*, a grant of land, and was later laid waste by plunderers, and the Chief Court building had begun to go up where it stood. Sayyid Chiragh Shah's tomb and his *pakka* mosque, solidly built of fired brick, still stood, the mosque in government possession and the tomb held by his descendants.

In the nineteenth century the shrine had a *sajjada nashin*, the custodian who sits on the saint's prayer carpet. There was also a broken enclosure where a *faqir*, a Sufi of spiritual poverty, lived. In the time of the Sikhs, before British rule, the mosque and the rest of the shrine had held land, but by then little of it was left.

In the late nineteenth century the precinct around the tomb was absorbed into the grand Indo-Saracenic complex of the Aiwan-e-Auqaf, built by the British administration between 1875 and 1880. That complex today houses the offices and library of the Auqaf Department, the same body that has administered the shrine since 1973.

## Devotion and Festivals

In the nineteenth century the tombs of Shah Chiragh and of his grandfather Abdul Qadir Thalith were places of pilgrimage for the people. Under Muslim rule the family had stood very high, and because its lineage joined that of Piran-e-Pir Muhi-ud-Din, the pir of pirs, people held its members in great respect.

By the 1880s a *mela*, a fair, was held here every year on the seventeenth of Rabi-us-Sani, the fourth month of the Islamic year, and great crowds gathered for it. In the nineteenth century a faqir had for a long time held the *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of the saint's death, taken its income and served as *khadim*, the attendant of the tomb. Every Thursday, too, music sounded at the tomb of Hazrat Shah Chiragh.

## Other Men of the Name

Several other men called Chiragh or Abd al-Razzaq, in Lahore and beyond, are remembered separately from the saint of the Mall. Sayyid Abd al-Razzaq of Mohalla Dola Wari was a *khalifa*, a spiritual deputy, of Mauj Darya Bukhari, and the first to settle in that quarter, which flourished after him. His *pakka* tomb stood in a lane of a bazaar, with a *pakka* mosque inside its enclosure. Under Ranjit Singh iron shot was made in the mosque courtyard, and the British emptied both buildings. When the cantonment left Anarkali, the meat contractor Munshi Najm al-Din took the mosque and tomb from the government, repaired the mosque and opened it again, and a grave was made in the tomb.

Others of the name were also linked with Mauj Darya. One Sayyid Abd al-Razzaq, after Mauj Darya's death, worshipped by night at his tomb and rested by day in a cell where his own tomb later stood, and he was buried in that cell as he had willed. His tomb was built by subscription, with a large mosque beside it from what was left over, and when an order later threatened to seize the building unless it was put in order within two months, merchants raised money for it. Shah Abd al-Razzaq Makki, a *murid* of Mauj Darya, died in 1084 AH and lies in Anarkali bazaar; he bore the saint's personal name and lived in the same part of Lahore, but he had a different master and died in a different year.

Other Chiraghs include a Chiragh Shah whose *mazar*, a saint's tomb, is outside the Roshnai Gate, the successor of a Sayyid Ali Gilani; a Shah Chiragh of Delhi; Hazrat Shah Chiragh Sahib Qadiri Sultanpuri, who looked on Nur Husain with the utmost kindness; and Sayyid Chiragh Shah Hakim, living in the 1880s, who had *murids* in the Chishti and Qadiri orders. Remembered separately, too, are Shaikh Abd al-Razzaq Makki of the Nila Gumbad, and the Sayyid Abd al-Razzaq who was a son and *murid* of Hazrat Ghaus al-Azam and stands in the spiritual chain of Mian Mir.

## The Setting

The patron of the mosque, Nawab Zakariya Khan Bahadur, was among the governors and rulers of the Punjab, with Shahnawaz Khan and others, who lived in Mughalpura. He also built another mosque in Lahore, west of the *mazar* of Madho Lal Hussain. A tile inscription on its north *mihrab*, the niche that marks the direction of prayer, dates that building to the reign of Muhammad Shah and carries this verse:

زبدۂ بارگاہ او نواب
زکریا خان صوبہ پنجاب

*The verse names its builder, Nawab Zakariya Khan, governor of the Punjab.*

In the 1880s the Chief Court, whose building was rising on the old quarter of Sayyid Chiragh Shah Gilani, was the court that gave the final decision in civil and criminal cases. Appeals from the commissionerships of the Punjab came before it, and capital sentences needed its sanction.

Today Mall Road is one of the busiest boulevards in the city, and the Lahore High Court, which combines European and Eastern architectural techniques, stands close to the Panorama Market. The shrine of Mauj Darya Bukhari stands nearby, and his grave is said to lie inside Shah Chiragh's mausoleum complex.

## Legacy

Though it lacks the international renown of some of Lahore's other Sufi sites, the shrine is formally catalogued as a protected heritage monument by the Punjab Directorate General of Archaeology. Its distinctive Mughal fabric, set incongruously amid the bureaucratic buildings of modern Lahore, makes it a quietly significant survival from the city's religious landscape of the seventeenth century.

Aurangzeb, it has been said, gave importance to some Qadiri Sufis who were mindful of the *sharia*, the religious law, and Shah Chiragh, whose tomb the emperor had built, was one of them. Raised by that emperor's order over a saint whom Shah Jahan had revered, the tomb has seen its mosque become a residence and a government office and its precinct absorbed into a complex built by the British administration, and its keeping has passed from the saint's own lineage to the Auqaf Department.
