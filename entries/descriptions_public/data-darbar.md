## Overview

Data Darbar, in the old heart of Lahore, is the resting place of Hazrat Ali ibn Usman al-Hujwiri, the eleventh century saint and scholar known to all simply as *Data Ganj Bakhsh*, the Master who bestows the treasure. He was the author of the *Kashf al-Mahjub*, the earliest treatise written in Persian on Sufism, the mystical path of Islam, and in the devotion of the Punjab he is the patron saint of Lahore, which is often called *Data ki Nagri*, the city of Data. His tomb, the greatest and most beloved shrine in Pakistan, is more than a shrine: it is a vast devotional complex where prayer, learning, music and, above all, the free kitchen, or *langar*, come together in the daily life of hundreds of thousands of people.

It is Lahore's most important shrine, revered by people of different religions, and Muslim devotees come to it from far for *ziyarat*, the visit to a holy tomb. The founder of the Chishti order in India is said to have kept a retreat of forty days at its foot, and other saints kept their own retreats there. It stands at the fountainhead of the whole tradition of Sufism in South Asia. To understand Data Darbar is to begin with the life of the saint whose blessing, or *baraka*, is believed still to fill it.

## The Saint

His full name was Abu'l-Hasan Ali bin Usman bin Ali al-Ghaznawi al-Jullabi al-Hujwiri. He was born about 1009 in Ghazni, in what is now Afghanistan, into a learned and pious family whose forefathers had lived in Ghazni; his father was Usman and his grandfather Ali. The names al-Jullabi and al-Hujwiri recall the quarters of Ghazni from which he came, for Hujwir is not the name of a city but of a quarter of Ghazni, and that is why he is called Ali Hujwiri. He is counted a Hasani sayyid, a descendant of the Prophet Muhammad of the line of Imam Hasan, son of Ali, and his descent is traced through Zaid Shahid, son of Imam Hasan, though accounts of the links in between differ and the genealogy has been questioned.

He grew into a scholar and mystic of the first rank, a Sunni of the Hanafi school, the school of Abu Hanifa of Kufa, whom he praised. Having finished with the outward sciences, he turned to the inward ones, uniting deep intellectual study with inward devotion. In the manner of the seekers of his age he travelled widely across the Muslim world, through Iran, Iraq, Syria and Central Asia. In Khurasan, Transoxiana, Marw, Azerbaijan and other lands he met many dervishes and drew on the spiritual graces of Shaikh Abu al-Qasim Gurgani, Shaikh Abu Sa'id Abu al-Khair and Shaikh Abu al-Qasim Qushairi; he attended Qushairi's meetings, was counted among his colleagues, and visited almost every leading Sufi of his time. He was a contemporary, too, of Abdullah Ansari, whose work, like his own, was written in part in Persian.

Among the masters he names as his teachers are Khwaja Muzaffar and Abu'l-Abbas Ahmad bin Muhammad al-Ashqani, besides Gurgani. A teacher of his, Shaikh Abu al-Qasim, taught that a *murshid*, a spiritual guide, is one who is a diver, and that a seeker should take the pledge of discipleship only if he finds the strength in himself, for otherwise both will be ruined. His principal guide was Abu'l-Fadl Muhammad al-Khuttali of Syria, who linked him to the sober Sufism of the school of Junayd of Baghdad: the tradition which insists that the mystical life be lived in full obedience to the Sharia, the religious law, and to the example of the Prophet, rather than in intoxicated excess.

From Khuttali he took *bai'at*, the oath by which a disciple pledges himself to his master. Khuttali had taken it from Shaikh Husri, and Husri from Shaikh Abu Bakr Shibli. When his training was complete, his *pir*, his spiritual guide, gave him leave to guide the people of Hind. He was given the titles Sultan al-Tariqat, Ganj al-Haqiqat and Burhan al-Shariat, which hail him as a sovereign of the Sufi path, a treasure of the Truth and a proof of the religious law. He is counted the *qutb*, the axis of the saints, of his age: a man given to the remembrance of God and to reflection, to meditation and the reckoning of the self, to worship and to austerity, and clean of every worldly taint.

In time his pir ordered him to go to Lahore. It is told that he objected, because an eminent Sufi, his *pir-bhai* or brother in the path, Husain Zanjani, was already there; but his pir ordered him to obey. He reached Lahore after dark and stayed outside the city for the night. In the morning, entering it, he met a funeral: Husain Zanjani had died during the night. He saw to the shrouding and the burial, and now, it is said, he understood the wisdom of his pir's command.

Accounts differ over when he came and in whose company; by the usual reckoning he arrived around 1039 (431 AH), in the age of the Ghaznavid sultans. Lahore was then a frontier city newly brought within the Muslim world, the capital of the Ghaznavids in India and a resort of scholars and men of learning. He settled on the spot where his *khanqah*, his Sufi lodge, stands, and built his house and a small mosque himself, at his own expense. There he taught, preached and served the poor, living in humility and voluntary poverty although the doors of royal circles were open to him. Even after settling in Lahore he kept in contact with Sufis in Transoxiana, Khurasan and Syria.

He stirred the city with his learning and his spiritual mastery. He taught students by day and instructed seekers by night, and through him, it is said, thousands of the ignorant became learned, thousands of unbelievers became Muslims, thousands of the misguided were rightly guided, thousands of madmen became sane, thousands of the imperfect became perfect and thousands of sinners became righteous. Under his teaching Lahore became a centre of Sufi learning and devotion, and the seed he planted would flower into the whole later history of Islam in the northwest of the subcontinent.

Not everyone welcomed him. His own book suggests that a number of mystics and scholars who had settled in Lahore were strongly hostile to his views. An expert in Qur'anic commentary sharply disagreed with his interpretation of *fana* and *baqa*, the annihilation of the self in God and the subsistence in God that follows it; and when some Sufis of Lahore held that gnosis came from inspiration, he called the view Brahmanical. These disputes so distressed him that he called himself a captive among uncongenial folk in Lahore, though he was not actually imprisoned. He was disappointed, too, with certain Sufis of his time, whom he called Hululis, and he weighed the Brahmanical influence then current on Sufi thought in Islam.

The mosque he built raised a quarrel of its own. Its *mihrab*, the niche that marks the direction of prayer, was somewhat askew towards the south compared with other mosques, and the *ulama*, the religious scholars of Lahore, objected that it did not face the *qibla*, the direction of the Kaaba at Mecca. He answered none of their objections, and the work went on.

He made Lahore his home, and there he died. Accounts of the year of his death differ; the year the tablet on his tomb gives, and the one most accounts give, is 465 AH, which corresponds to 1072 or 1073. He died at the end of that year and was buried in the courtyard of his own *khanqah*, and from the first his grave drew those who had loved him. Persian chronograms, verses whose letters, read as numbers, give a year, seal the date of his death, among them these:

عیاں تاریخ او چوں ماہ گفتم
علی ہجویری عالی جاہ گفتم

*A chronogram on his death, which gives the year 465.*

چو جستم از خرد تاریخ سالش
عیاں شد کاشف دین ارتحالش

*A chronogram on his death, also giving the year 465, built on the phrase Kashif-e Din, revealer of the faith.*

## His Book and His Teaching

Hujwiri's enduring gift to the world is his book, the *Kashf al-Mahjub*, "The Unveiling of the Veiled". He composed it in Lahore towards the end of his life. It is the earliest formal treatise on Sufism written in Persian, the first known manual of the path in that language, and it remains one of the classics of the tradition, studied wherever Sufism is studied. Drawing on the vast material available in Arabic, it sets out the doctrines and disciplines of the mystical path: the nature of the soul and of knowledge, the meaning of sainthood, the practices and states of the Sufis, the sayings and lives of the earlier masters, and the etiquette of the seeker. He explains the doctrines of the various schools, then relates and assesses them, setting out Sufism according to the school of Junayd.

Its teaching is of a piece with the man: a sober mysticism that weds spiritual devotion to ethical conduct, prayer, humility and restraint, and holds that inward transformation matters more than any outward display of piety. At its heart lies the ideal of *faqr*, spiritual poverty, the emptying of the self of ego, pride and worldly craving, together with *dhikr*, the remembrance of God, sincere service to others and the purification of the heart. Hujwiri urges the seeker to keep the company of a realised guide, and warns sharply against the fraud who counterfeits sanctity or chases miracles, fame and rank. Through this book, more even than through his tomb, the saint has gone on teaching for nine centuries.

By writing it in Persian he ushered in a new period in mystical literature. It belongs to the Persian mystical literature that developed first in the eastern lands of the Caliphate, in Herat and northwest India, in the second half of the eleventh century. Jami counted it among the valid and famous books, and in the Delhi of Nizamuddin it became fashionable to buy it. Of his many works only this one has survived.

Reynold A. Nicholson's English translation, first published in 1911 in the Gibb Memorial Series, has been reprinted often, in London and in Lahore among other places, and the Persian text was edited by V. A. Zhukovsky in 1926 and reprinted at Tehran. The book lives on, too, in the translations of Sufi teachers. Wahid Bakhsh Sial Rabbani translated it from Persian into Urdu with a commentary, published at Lahore in 1995, and his English version, *The Kashful Mahjub ('Unveiling the Veiled'): The Earliest Persian Treatise on Sufism*, was published by disciples at Kuala Lumpur in 1997.

The *Kashf al-Mahjub* names other books of his own, all now lost: the *Minhaj al-Din*, a work on Sufi practices with a detailed account of the Ahl-i Suffa, the people of the Bench who gathered around the Prophet's mosque, and a full biography of Husain bin Mansur al-Hallaj; the *Asrar al-Khiraq wa'l-Ma'unat*, on the patched garments worn by Sufis; the *Kitab-i Fana' wa Baqa'*, which he says he wrote in the vanity and rashness of youth; a work without a title explaining the sayings of al-Hallaj; the *Kitab al-Bayan li-Ahl al-'Iyan*, on union with God; the *Bahr al-Qulub*; the *Ri'ayat li-Huquq Allah*, on the unity of God; and a work on faith whose title is not given. In a book written in the middle of the eleventh century and no longer extant, he felt obliged, for all his admiration of Hallaj, to declare him not firmly settled.

He was a poet too, writing under the pen name, or *takhallus*, Ali. By his own account he composed many verses and poems, and a *diwan*, a collection of poems, as well, and a Persian ghazal of seven couplets is attributed to him. His poetry is still sung: a *qawwal*, a singer of Sufi devotional music, at Kasur, Muhammad Bakhsh, counts Data Ganj Bakhsh among the poets whose texts his party uses, with Khwaja Moinuddin Chishti, Baba Ganj Shakar and Amir Khusrau, though most of all they sing Bulleh Shah.

He wrote of the path of blame, the way of those who draw blame onto themselves to rid themselves of conceit on the path of spiritual growth. "In true love there is nothing sweeter than blame, because blame of the Beloved makes no impression on the lover's heart: he heeds not what strangers say, for his heart is ever faithful to the object of his love." He closed the thought with a line of verse:

*'Tis sweet to be reviled for passion's sake.* (translated by Reynold A. Nicholson)

His counsel was severe on worldly power. A *faqir*, one who has embraced poverty, should reckon acquaintance with kings and rulers as acquaintance with venomous snakes and great dragons, for once a faqir gains a king's favour, his livelihood and his provision for the journey are ruined; and putting a Turkish cap on one's head, he warned, gains one no *faqiri*, the poverty of the faqir. Reckon wealth a torment, he counselled, and give it to the starving as *sadaqa*, alms, for in the end worms will eat it in the grave, whereas if you give it away they will remain your friends. For a *wird*, a regular recitation, he prescribed two of the Most Beautiful Names of God, *Ya Habib* and *Ya Latif*, their meanings to be taken into one's very being. He wrote, too, on selfish interest, which lifts the blessing from any work; on the company of the Sufis and the motives of those who seek it; on the struggle against the self, which the Prophet held greater than *jihad*, holy war; on the faqir; on ten things that devour ten; and on eight sayings chosen from Luqman Hakim.

He held up the four Righteous Caliphs as models for the Sufis: Abu Bakr as a paragon of voluntary poverty, who taught them to renounce all their material goods for Islam; Umar, whose patched gown was a symbol of his denial of himself; Usman, whose life was the best of all possible examples of resignation in a crisis; and Ali, their Shaikh, their leader or teacher, in both the theory and the practice of Sufism. He preserved the sayings and prayers of earlier masters, among them Uways al-Qarani, Sari al-Saqati and Sumnun al-Muhibb. From him come the prayer of al-Baqir, "O my God and my Lord, night has come, and the power of monarchs has ceased"; the words of an unnamed master of his own to a disciple, "To traverse distance is child's play: henceforth pay visits by means of thought; it is not worth while to visit any person, and there is no virtue in bodily presence"; and the saying of Ahmad ibn Khazruya, "the way to God is like the blazing sun; do thou seek thyself, for when thou hast found thyself thou art come to thy journey's end, inasmuch as God is too manifest to admit of His being sought". He described in detail each of the Sufi schools named after the early masters, and set out which of them were approved.

He wrote of love: "Love is concord, and the lover has but one duty in the world, namely to keep the commandment of the beloved, and if the object of desire is one, how can discord arise?" Of the lover and the beloved he wrote: "It is a law that when the lover advances, the beloved retires. If the lover is satisfied with love alone, then the beloved draws near. In truth, the lover has honor only while he has no desire of union." Contentment, he held, is a result of love, since the lover is content with what is done by the beloved. He divided lovers into those whom God's favour and beneficence lead to love the Benefactor, and those so enraptured by love that they reckon all favours a veil, a classification that Ghazali adopted. He set apart, too, "one who is burned by His Majesty in the fire of love and one who is illuminated by His Beauty in the light of contemplation".

Poverty, he taught, has destitution and indigence for its form, but fortune and free choice for its essence; dervishhood in all its meanings is a metaphorical poverty, and the dervish is the Way, not the wayfarer: a place over which something is passing, not a wayfarer following his own will. The penitent, in his words, is a lover of God, and the lover of God is in contemplation of God, where it is wrong to remember sin, "for recollection of sin is a veil between God and the contemplator"; he divided repentance into three, a division found nowhere else. On learning he wrote: "Knowledge is immense and life is short: therefore it is not obligatory to learn all the science . . . but only so much as bears upon the religious law." On sainthood: "You must know that the principle and foundation of Sufism and knowledge of God rest on saintship." Of annihilation and subsistence he wrote that in annihilation there is no love or hate, and in subsistence no consciousness of union or separation, and he called absurd the notions that annihilation means the loss of essence and the destruction of personality, or that subsistence means the subsistence of God in man. The spirit, he insisted, is created and under God's command. Whoever regards his own eminence, he taught, loses its reality, and whoever regards his own saintship loses its reality. True sobriety he defined as reaching the goal. He distinguished three kinds of unification: God's unification of God, that is, His knowledge of His Unity; God's unification of His creatures, His decree that a man shall pronounce Him to be one, and the creation of unification in his heart; and man's unification of God, the knowledge men have of the Unity of God. Of the ritual prayer he observed that some mystics hold it a means of obtaining the presence of God, and others a means of obtaining absence.

Some mystics, he wrote, say "I never saw anything without seeing God therein", which points to an advanced stage of contemplation, while others, fully enraptured, say "I never see anything except God". He weighed the meanings of the very word Sufi: some derive it from the woollen garment the Sufi wears, others from his standing in the first rank, others from the people of the Bench, and others from the word for purity. He compared the ostentatious, who act so as to win popularity, with the *malamati*, the follower of the path of blame, who acts so that people reject him: "Both have their thought fixed on mankind and do not pass beyond that sphere". Looking at youths and keeping their company he held forbidden, and he declared anyone who called it allowable an unbeliever.

He described the life of the *khanqah*, dividing its people into residents and travellers. Sufi masters, he wrote, set novices a probation of three years before instructing them in the real secrets of the path: the first year devoted to serving the people, the second to the service of God and the third to guarding the heart. The patched cloak of the Sufis, he held, was worn by Muhammad and his companions, and he read it as an allegory: "Its collar is annihilation of intercourse [with men], its two sleeves are observance and continence, its two gussets are poverty and purity, its belt is persistence in contemplation, its hem is tranquillity in [God's] presence, and its fringe is settlement in the abode of union." Yet he made remarks about those who make their patched frocks almost an object of worship. The *chilla*, the retreat of forty days in seclusion that later saints would keep at his own tomb, he derived from the fast of forty days that Moses kept when he hoped for a vision from God.

His shrine is known for its music, and he himself wrote on *samaʿ*, spiritual listening, the Sufi session of music. He is counted, with Gisu Daraz, Ali Hamadani and Muhammad Nur Allah, among the important Indian theorists of samaʿ. He summarised the conflicting views of earlier and contemporary Sufis, but severely criticised sensual gratification through empathetic ecstasy; when a traditionist of his day told him that he had written a book on its permissibility, he replied: "It is a great calamity to religion that the Imam should have made lawful an amusement which is the root of all immorality." Yet he admitted that he practised it himself, and so far as he affirmed ecstasy he was bound by the *hadith*, a saying of the Prophet, that bids the reciter of the Qur'an weep, "and if you cannot weep, then you should try to weep".

He records a rebuke to himself from a venerable shaykh who had indulged his desire for music and then told him: "A time will come when this music will be no more to you than the croaking of a raven", and "Take care not to accustom yourself to this, lest it grow (to be) part of your nature and keep you back from higher things." He was himself the disciple of a shaykh disinclined to samaʿ, and he closed his book with a prayer asking God's forgiveness for his past sins in samaʿ. Yet he gave some of the claims made on behalf of samaʿ, and for the first time linked it to the grades of ecstasy, a link that made possible the later and distinctive Chishti contribution to its theory. Mas'ud Bakk followed his method, proceeding from a general description of hearing and its role in Islamic revelation to the practice itself.

He defined samaʿ as comprising first and foremost that which is heard, the divine message which stirs the heart to seek God, and he placed the whole practice firmly within the hierarchical structure of spiritual authority. The principles that he and other writers laid down between the eleventh and fourteenth centuries still contribute greatly to the concepts of *qawwali*, the devotional music of the Sufis, in use today.

## The Bestower of Treasure

The name by which Hujwiri is universally known, *Data Ganj Bakhsh*, was, by cherished tradition, bestowed upon him after his death by another of the giants of South Asian Sufism. It is told that Khwaja Moinuddin Chishti of Ajmer, the founder of the Chishti order in India, on his way into the subcontinent kept a retreat of forty days at the foot of Hujwiri's tomb in Lahore, and that on departing he spoke a Persian couplet in the saint's praise that has become inseparable from his memory:

گنج بخشِ فیضِ عالم، مظہرِ نورِ خدا
ناقصاں را پیرِ کامل، کاملاں را رہنما

*Bestower of the treasure, the bounty of the world, the manifestation of the light of God; for the imperfect, a perfect guide, and for the perfect, a guide.*

From that day, it is said, he became famous as Data Ganj Bakhsh. From this verse, by tradition, comes the title *Ganj Bakhsh*, Bestower of Treasure, and the affectionate *Data*, the Giver, by which the Punjab has called him ever since; some call him simply Ganj Bakhsh, and his devotees speak of him as Data Sahib. As the name suggests, his tomb is renowned as a place of intense power, an open and accessible outlet for spiritual grace. Because the Chishti master, himself the fountainhead of an entire order, bowed at his threshold, Hujwiri is remembered as the teacher of teachers, the saint from whom the later Sufis of India drew their light. Nawab Mir Momin Khan, who had perfect devotion to the saint, held his resting place greater than the highest paradise.

His names take many forms. He is Shaikh Ali Makhdoom, known as Data Ganj Bakhsh; Mir Ali Hujwiri, the choicest of the great saints, one who held learning and sainthood in a single embrace; and Pir Ali ibn Usman Data Ganj Bakhsh Hujwiri. Another saint, Haji Muhammad Naushah Ganj Bakhsh, bears the same epithet and is not to be confused with him.

His tomb has always been greatly venerated by Sufis and Muslims alike. The founder of the Chishti order in India was among the early mystics who undertook hard ascetic exercises there, and he probably stayed near the tombs of Husain Zanjani and Ali Hujwiri. He is said to have lodged with Sayyid Yaqub Zanjani at the beginning and the end of his retreat, and to have met the Zanjani elders at the tomb before he left for Ajmer, the abode of good. According to tradition he was granted there the spiritual dominion, or *wilayat*, of all of South Asia, for himself and his successors. The place outside the shrine where this is remembered is known as the station of Mu'in ad-Din, one of the sites of heroic asceticism where the retreats of forty days were kept.

Many elders came to the tomb to keep their *chillas* and receive its grace, among them Khwaja Farid of Pakpattan. Shah Husain, the saint of the shrine of Madho Lal Hussain at Baghbanpura, kept several *chillas* at the "pearl-scattering" tomb and, it is told, reached completion. It is told that Bahlul directed him there, saying that whoever comes to the radiant tomb finds the way, and that he should remain at the tomb, Hujwiri being his patron and guide. Shah Husain kept his prayers at the *khanqah* for twelve years; as a mendicant student he wandered in the empty lands outside the city walls by day and returned at night to stay in the shrine.

The great Qadiri saint of the Mughal age, Mian Mir, is said to have kept a *chilla* of forty days at Hujwiri's *khanqah*, joining his own sanctity to that of the city's patron. Mian Wadda, on the advice of Sayyid Mahmud, kept one *chilla* there in retreat and, it is said, attained complete purity, and Fakhr-ud-Din spent most of his time at the *khanqah*. Tradition holds that no saint could settle in Lahore, or find his own path, without first paying homage at Hujwiri's grave.

## The Shrine

From the saint's grave grew, across nine centuries, the great complex of today. What began as a modest burial place beside his mosque became a place of pilgrimage almost at once. The first formal enclosure is said to have been raised by the Ghaznavid rulers, and through the centuries that followed the shrine was repaired, adorned and enlarged by the powers of the age. The kings of old showed it the utmost reverence, and in the nineteenth century Qur'ans written by the hands of Sultan Ibrahim Ghaznavi, Sultan Shams-ud-Din Altamsh and other kings were kept at the tomb. Under the Delhi Sultanate and the Mughals its fame only grew, and emperors such as Akbar and Shah Jahan honoured the saint and contributed to his darbar.

Under Maharaja Ranjit Singh, the Sikh ruler, the shrine fared in two ways. He greatly respected the tomb, sent thousands of rupees for it, and gave it the first of its great Qur'ans at the conquest of Peshawar. Yet he left not one of its outer buildings standing, having their stones and doors taken out of the ground, so that only the building of the tomb itself remained. Nearby, the tomb and mosque of Shah Ashraf fell under the Sikhs too. By the nineteenth century the shrine was already among the most revered landmarks of Lahore.

There was formerly no dome over the grave. By the 1870s Haji Nur Muhammad Sadhu had built one, and Mian Muhammad Jan, a *ra'is*, a man of rank, of Amritsar, had paid for its repair and whitewashing. In the nineteenth century the dome was gilded, round and very beautiful, with mirrors fitted below and above the lattice, and the domed building was rebuilt anew on its old foundation, with *hujras*, small cells, a decorated wall, a *deorhi*, or gateway, and the arched doorway of the mosque. Chronograms on the building were written by Nur Muhammad Chishti and by Mufti Ghulam Sarwar.

In the nineteenth century the enclosure was entered by a door facing south. Inside, to the west, stood a gate with a stone threshold and a *deorhi* behind it, and beyond lay a square courtyard with houses and *dalans*, open halls, on all four sides; the *mujawirs*, the attendants of the tomb, lived on its southern side. In the middle of the courtyard a marble platform carried the tomb. The floor above the platform was of marble, and the grave itself was made of a single piece of marble, with a marble *taʿwiz*, the raised marker over the grave, and a wooden lattice on all eight sides. The dome kept its southern door. A pigeon house held many pigeons, and in the widened Qur'an *dalan* the *siparas*, the thirty parts of the Qur'an, were kept in a chest for whoever came to read them. Beside the grave of one of the *mujawirs*, a platform under a brick dome was known as the station of Khwaja Moinuddin. Outside the enclosure to the south stood many solidly built tombs, a mosque and many sitting places of the *mujawirs*, and outside to the east and the south were twelve graves.

Among the inscriptions at the shrine in the nineteenth century were the *kalima*, the profession of faith, a Persian verse naming Makhdoom Ali, and the *basmala*, the words "In the name of God"; over the inner gate stood a gilded inscription of three Persian couplets. The shrine was a house of Qur'ans. The royal copies were kept on *rahls*, book rests, in the rooms of the courtyard, and people went on reading them. Large Qur'ans, each a *gaz* in length, the *gaz* being a traditional measure of length, came as gifts: the first from Maharaja Ranjit Singh at the conquest of Peshawar; the second from Shaikh Ghulam al-Din, who held an office in Kashmir and was the father of Nawab Shaikh Imam-ud-Din; one from a Kashmiri merchant living in Lahore; a fourth from Mian Ghulam al-Husain, a calligrapher of Lahore; one written in musk, whose history is not known; a sixth, in the Multani hand, from the Nawab of Multan; and a seventh, an old one. Jind Kaur ordered the construction of a library at the shrine and gave it all the Qur'ans she had in her possession.

The saint's own mosque stood at the southwest corner of the enclosure. In the nineteenth century Gulzar Shah rebuilt it anew on the same foundation. By 1974 the old mosque had been pulled down to enlarge it and a new one built, and a stone marked the line of the old *mihrab*.

In the nineteenth century the very elaborately built enclosure of Nawab Shaikh Imam-ud-Din stood beside the saint's, wall to wall with it on the east and the south, and was reached by a path from the northern *dalan*. His tomb was square and open to the sky over the grave, with *dalans*, *hujras*, a *balakhana*, or upper room, an iron grille and a wooden cage, and it bore a quatrain in the hand of Mirza Imam Wardi, who signed himself a humble mote. The Nawab's grave was of marble, and his son built the tomb after his death. Persian couplets on its southern wall, a lament that the world will go on long after us, began with this line:

دریغا کہ بے ما بسے روزگار

Chronogram verses stood on the stone of his grave:

چونکہ نواب شیخ امام الدین
شد ز دنیا و رو بخلد نہاد…

*The opening of the chronogram verses on the stone of Nawab Shaikh Imam-ud-Din's grave.*

The precinct holds other graves, old and new, among them the solid grave of Shaikh Sulaiman, one of the *mujawirs*. A modern burial belongs to the story of the saint's book. Two English brothers named Lennard converted to Islam in 1936 after reading the *Kashf al-Mahjub*, left their prosperous English family, took the Muslim names Faruq Ahmad and Shahid Allah, and travelled to the Middle East and India in search of a Sufi master. Faruq Ahmad died in Lahore on 13 February 1945, after a prolonged bout of pneumonia. There was not enough money to fulfil his wish to be buried in the compound of the shrine, and another place was hastily arranged; but as the burial was about to begin, it is told, a stranger suddenly arrived. To this day Faruq Ahmad lies buried in a small underground room within the tomb complex of his patron saint.

In the nineteenth century the shrine had more than seventy *mujawirs*, who divided the income from the offerings in equal shares. They held property beyond the enclosure: the house at the grave of Pir Ghaib, whose income they took; and, in the garden of Bakhshi Kanhiya Lal to the south of the tomb, a pomegranate plot and a bed of ground that were their own property, held without rent under a written statement, with a *kotha*, a small building, that they had built and let to a shopkeeper. The family of Bakhshi Kanhiya Lal sat in their garden by the shrine on Fridays and at the *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of the saint's death.

Today, unlike most of the shrines of the Punjab, Data Darbar has no hereditary keepers, or *sajjada nashins*. Since shortly after the creation of Pakistan in 1947 it has been administered directly by the state, through the Punjab Auqaf and Religious Affairs Department, which manages the offerings, or *nazrana*, the mosque, the security and the vast operation of the *langar*. On the ground the daily life of the shrine depends on a small army of *khadims*, or attendants, imams, *qawwals*, cooks and *langar* workers, cleaners and security staff, overseen by government administrators: in 2026 the Auqaf secretary, Dr Ehsan Bhutta, the director general, Shahid Kalyani, and the shrine administrator, Muhammad Ali Khan. This state custodianship, exceptional among the great shrines, reflects both the enormous scale of Data Darbar and its standing as a national, and not merely a family, trust.

By 1974 the Auqaf Department was planning a further extension of the mosque. In the 1980s the shrine was transformed by a great expansion, with wide marble courtyards, prayer halls and facilities for the swelling multitudes of pilgrims. By the early 2000s it had been extended to almost ten times its original size and remodelled with the most expensive Italian marble, part of a trend, as orthodox Islamic governments came to power in Pakistan, to extend and renovate the shrines of Sufis into mosques and places of worship. Renovation and enlargement have continued into the present century, with further works in progress in 2026, and the Data Darbar of today, with its gleaming courts and its towering mosque, is the work of many hands across nine hundred years, all gathered around one grave.

The shrine also carries the wounds of the present age. On the night of 1 July 2010 it was struck by suicide bombers, one of a wave of militant attacks on the Sufi shrines of Pakistan in those years, as Islamic purism spread over the country under the influence of the Taliban. Accounts of the toll differ, from five dead to dozens, with many more injured. It was an assault not merely on a building but on the whole plural, devotional and tolerant world that shrines like Data Darbar embody, and that the attackers regarded as heresy.

Yet the darbar endured. The wounded were carried out, the dead were mourned, and within days the pilgrims returned; the *langar* was cooked, the prayers resumed and the *qawwali* was sung again. That refusal of ordinary devotees to let fear empty the shrine has become part of its meaning, a testimony to the resilience of a faith of love and service against the creed of violence.

## Devotion and Festivals

Data Darbar is a place of ceaseless devotion, and its rhythms of worship run day and night. The five daily prayers and the Friday congregation fill the grand mosque, and huge congregations of devotees perform the Friday prayers in the shrine courtyard. Devotees recite the Qur'an, offer personal and collective supplication, or *dua*, lay cloths, the *chadar*, and flowers upon the tomb, and sit in silent contemplation. Countless pilgrims come to fulfil vows or to seek blessing for health, livelihood, marriage or success in examinations, in the belief that the saint is a mediator of God's grace. The radiant tomb is held to be a fountainhead of graces and blessings, where wishes are fulfilled.

At the heart of the shrine's life is the *langar*, which feeds thousands every day, embodying the Sufi ethic that the love of God must be shown in generosity and service to His creatures; for the poor of Lahore, Data Darbar is not only a sanctuary of the spirit but a place of bread and belonging. On an ordinary day the shrine draws some thirty to fifty thousand visitors, and on Thursdays and holy nights well over a hundred thousand. It is open to men and women, rich and poor, scholar and labourer alike, one of the few places in the city where all classes meet on level ground. Its devotional culture is continuous with the Barelvi strand of the Punjab's piety, in which the veneration of the saints and the seeking of their intercession stand at the centre of religious life. Zindapir made the shrine his proof that saints outrank kings: at the tomb of the emperor Jahangir, he said, you will find no people, but at Data Ganj Bakhsh there are thousands of people all the time.

The week has long had its fixed nights. By the 1870s the devotees of Lahore and Amritsar gathered at the tomb every Thursday and stayed awake all night, from evening to morning, in *durud*, blessings upon the Prophet, and *naat*, poems in his praise; then on every Friday, by day, there was a *milad*, a gathering to celebrate the birth of the Prophet, and high and low alike were blessed in greeting the saint. Besides the Thursday crowd there was, in the nineteenth century, a special gathering every month. Dara Shikoh said that whoever lights a lamp at the tomb for forty Thursdays in succession will obtain from God whatever he asks, and it is said, too, that whoever attends the tomb for forty days or forty Thursdays will obtain from God whatever he wishes. Since the Partition of India in 1947 devotees have come from Lahore and every part of Pakistan, and from Afghanistan, and pilgrims from other countries too.

On Thursday evenings the shrine fills for *qawwali* and *samaʿ*, and some among the devotees are moved to *dhamal*, the ecstatic rhythmic dance of trance and love. Notable musicians, among them Ustad Bare Ghulam Ali, Ustad Chote Ghulam Ali, Niaz Hussain Shami, Fateh Ali Mubarik and Dina Qawwal, performed *hazri*, the singing of mystical texts, at the *ʿurs* of Data Ganj Bakhsh, and women musicians performed hazri at the Sufi shrines of Lahore, especially at the time of the *ʿurs*. Here, as at the shrine of Baba Fariduddin Ganj-e Shakar at Pakpattan, the same ritual of qawwali is followed. At the *ʿurs* it always opens with a *qaul*, a saying of the Prophet Muhammad, in Arabic, *Mun kunto Maulā fā Alī-un Maulā*, and its poetry is focused on Ali, on the saint buried at the shrine and on his spiritual lineage. This ritual qawwali is an exclusively male domain, and women's presence at such performances is rare.

The crowning event of the shrine's year is the *ʿurs* of Data Ganj Bakhsh, held according to the Islamic lunar calendar in the month of Safar, over three days that fall by tradition around the 18th to the 20th. *ʿUrs* means wedding, and in the language of the Sufis it celebrates not the saint's death but his union with the Divine, so it is kept in reverence and joy rather than mourning. During those days the darbar is transformed: the tomb and its courts are illuminated, special prayers, Qur'an recitations and *chadar* ceremonies are held, and continuous *qawwali* and devotional assemblies fill the nights. The *langar* is expanded to feed tens of thousands, and the streets around the shrine take on the character of a great fair, or *mela*, with stalls of sweets, books and devotional wares. Pilgrims pour in not only from Lahore but from across the Punjab and Pakistan, and the authorities commonly declare special arrangements to manage a gathering that can swell, over the three days, to between one and one and a half million souls.

In the nineteenth century, too, the great fair of the *ʿurs* fell in Safar, and a second fair was held on the last Wednesday of Safar, both drawing people from far. At the *ʿurs* the shrine's management segregates the women's sections, and women may be hidden from men's view by a curtain. Other seasons, too, raise the tide of devotion: the last ten nights of Ramadan, the Prophet's birthday, Eid Milad-un-Nabi, and the other holy nights. But none approaches the scale and centrality of the *ʿurs*, the defining festival of Data Darbar.

The Chishti Sabiri order has the rule "Do not go to any mazar which is not in our silsila", yet its disciples make a notable exception for this shrine, and Hujwiri is remembered among them as an honorary Chishti Sabiri. Their shaykh Wahid Bakhsh wrote in a letter: "Although Hazrat Data Sahab, may God have mercy upon him, is not in the line of our silsila directly, he is great enough to know what you need. Moreover, he is very much associated with the masha'ikh of our silsila. He is very kind to us." The saint is one of five figures on whom the disciples meditate daily, for fifteen to thirty minutes, in *muraqaba-i shaykh*, contemplation of the master. At the tombs of shaykhs outside their own *silsila*, their spiritual lineage, they recite the *Fatiha*, the opening chapter of the Qur'an, twice, but at Data Sahib only once, out of respect, because they are in his silsila.

In the early twenty first century disciples of the order who lived in Lahore visited the shrine regularly on Thursday evenings, before attending the communal *halqa-i zikr*, the circle of remembrance, and often repeated the journey on Sundays; it was Shaykh Wahid Bakhsh who established this weekly pilgrimage. They went privately or as a family, but not as a group, and a senior disciple said that they did not go even for the *ʿurs*, only for one day's attendance. For them contemplation at the tomb was an intimate conversation between the Sufi disciple and the patron saint, alive in his tomb. A woman disciple described her visits: she would say in her heart that she was sitting in front of Data Sahab, close her eyes and take long, slow, deep breaths, and after ten to fifteen minutes of meditation there she would receive an answer to all her problems.

Some come seeking help or a guide. A woman named Rabi'a, whose daughter was under the treatment of the Kharian *pir*, came to Data Sahib and told her dream to a *faqir* there, who read its meaning for her. Another seeker, known as Baba, boarded a bus once a fair was over and arrived at Data Darbar, Lahore's most prominent shrine complex, and there, by Baba's own account, first found mentorship under the tutelage of a *faqir*, staying about a year. Ghulam Ali's *dandiya* group, dancers who perform with sticks, has played at the shrine, one of the famous Muslim shrines where it has performed.

## Legends

Around his coming to Lahore cluster the wonders of a patron saint. The best loved tells of a poor milk seller whose meagre yield, at the saint's blessing, miraculously increased, a small wonder that, in the telling, drew the people of the city to his teaching, and that still speaks of the compassion and generosity his shrine is felt to embody.

The quarrel over his mosque, it is told, was settled by a wonder. It is said that when the mosque was finished he summoned the *ulama* and the *mashaikh*, the Sufi masters, and that all prayed there with him leading the prayer. Then, the story goes, he asked them to consider where the Kaaba lay and turned his attention towards it; the veils were lifted, and all saw the Kaaba before them and the mosque's direction true. His other wonders are said to be countless, lakhs of them, that is, hundreds of thousands.

He is said to appear at his own tomb. A saint of a later age, it is told, was ordered by his pir to attend the radiant tomb regularly, and kept the duty very strictly for twelve years, between the dawn prayer and the school, finding calm and singleness of mind in two places, on the bank of the river and at the tomb of Data Ganj Bakhsh. At last, the story goes, a luminous form appeared to him, asked whether he knew who it was, and said: "I am Ali Hujwiri". This, it said, was the reward of the service he had done for twelve years, and it enriched him with inward bounty, granted him *wilayat*, sainthood, and made him drunk with the wine of unity.

Disciples of the Chishti Sabiri order say that he still appears. When you visualise Data Sahab, they say, you see Data Sahab, and many times he will appear and give you messages. They compare the spiritual energy, or *faizan*, that radiates from the tomb during their meditation to an intense electric current surging through the body.

Wonders are told, too, of the burial of the Englishman Faruq Ahmad. Throughout the time of the funeral, it is said, a light rain fell from a cloud of mercy only above that spot in Lahore; and workers who came to move the grave are said to have discovered that his body was still fresh and had not decomposed, so that his tomb was left undisturbed.

It is commonly told that the Mughal princess Zeb-un-Nisa was building in the vicinity of the shrine, and that the wall of her building kept collapsing.

He spoke of wonders himself. God, he wrote, has made the saints the governors of the universe: through the blessing of their coming the rain falls from heaven, through the purity of their lives the plants spring from the earth, and through their spiritual influence the Muslims gain victories over the unbelievers. Among them are four thousand who are concealed and do not know one another, and there are graded ranks: three hundred called the Akhyar, forty the Abdal, seven the Abrar, four the Awtad, three the Nuqaba, and one called the *Qutb* or *Ghaws*, the helper; all these know one another and cannot act save by mutual consent. Of this, he declared, God be praised, he had the evidence of his own eyes. His book tells, too, how Hasan of Basra was corrected by God in a dream: had he said his prayers behind Habib, and not taken offence at his pronunciation, God would have been well pleased with him.

## The City of Data

More than any other figure, Ali Hujwiri made Lahore a holy city, and the whole later sacred landscape of the Punjab is bound, in devotion, to his threshold. He is remembered as the spiritual founder from whom the city takes one of its dearest names, *Data ki Nagri*, the city of Data. At the shrine of Bibi Pak Daman it is told that Hujwiri himself, on first coming to Lahore, would go each Thursday night to sit in reverence at the women's graves; he is remembered as a devotee of the shrines of the Bibi Pak Daman, offering prayers there and spending considerable time in meditation. And the modern poet and philosopher Muhammad Iqbal, who lies buried a little way off, by the Badshahi Mosque, is remembered in a beloved Lahori legend as having been visited one night by unseen guests, among them Data Ganj Bakhsh himself. Pir Makki is said by some to have been his disciple.

In this way Data Darbar is not merely one shrine among many but the source and centre of a whole network of holiness: the point from which, in the imagination of the Punjab, the sanctity of Lahore radiates outward to Bibi Pak Daman and Mian Mir, to Madho Lal Hussain at Baghbanpura, and beyond. To visit Data is, for many, to begin at the beginning, to salute the master before turning to his spiritual children. It is this that makes the darbar the true heart of the city: not only the busiest of its shrines and the largest of its kitchens, but the origin of its identity as a place under a saint's protection, a city that belongs, in the end, to Data.

The grave was the city's landmark, the point from which other sites were found. The shrine stands outside the Bhati Gate, one of the twelve gates of the walled city of Lahore, on ground that was formerly a graveyard; many tombs there were demolished, though in the nineteenth century many graves still remained. On the far side of the saint's tomb, on a high platform, stood the tomb of Pir Ghazi, known as Pir az Ghaib. Qutb Shah settled outside the Bhati Gate, beside the tomb of Data Ganj Bakhsh, in the house of Sain Jali Shah Majzub, and an unbaked brick *kotha* belonged to Data Sahib. The Shia Imambara, too, stood outside the Bhati Gate, south of the radiant tomb.

The city he came to was a Ghaznavid one. The annexation of the Punjab by Mahmud of Ghazni and its incorporation into his empire prompted many Sufis to settle in the area, and Lahore became the first centre in the subcontinent of a Muslim culture of Persian inspiration; the Ghaznavid panegyrist Mas'ud-i Sa'd-i Salman was born at Lahore and, in several passages of his poems, speaks of it as his native place. Lahore is an ancient city on the bank of the Ravi, whose founding is attributed to Lav, and after Sultan Mahmud's conquest Malik Ayaz rebuilt the city and built a solid fort.

In the reign of Aurangzeb Lahore was a great city and a mighty metropolis, where craftsmen of every land lived, with mosques beyond counting in every lane and bazaar. Besides Hujwiri, others close to the Eternal Lord lay at rest in it, and on its outskirts were many delightful gardens and thousands of flower beds that gave joy.

## Legacy

No shrine stands closer to the heart of Lahore than Data Darbar, and few figures loom larger in the spiritual history of the subcontinent than the saint it enshrines. Ali Hujwiri was the pioneer who carried the sober, learned Sufism of Baghdad into the Punjab; the author whose *Kashf al-Mahjub* remains a lamp for seekers everywhere; and the bestower of the treasure at whose tomb the founder of the Chishti order himself sat in devotion. He was among the mystics who had settled in the subcontinent as early as the eleventh century, and from his grave the whole later tradition of South Asian Sufism drew its beginning.

His ordering of the Sufi world outlived him. The *tazkiras* of later ages, the collected lives of the saints, took their cue from him: he classified the Sufi orders into twelve, ten approved and two heretical, linking each to a famous early Sufi master, a list he acknowledged was a theoretical construct based on individual masters and not a current institutional reality. He was the first witness, too, to a long lament, quoting the saying of Abu al-Hasan of Fushanja that Sufism was in the beginning a reality without a name, but had become over time a name without a reality; he offered no commentary on it.

His book is still read in a set order among living Sufis. In the Chishti Sabiri order his writings head a list of five premodern authors, with al-Ghazali, Ibn al-'Arabi, al-Suhrawardi and Rumi, that are especially popular among its disciples, and a senior disciple placed the *Kashf al-Mahjub* third in the order's course of reading, the book with which one goes still deeper, into all the deeper levels of Sufism. Nicholson's translation sparked Shahidullah Faridi's interest in Sufism. A publisher with a department named after the saint, the Shaikh Ali bin Usman Hujwiri Department, has offered the book in three editions: the Persian text edited by Ali Qawim, an Urdu translation by Sayyid Muhammad Faruq al-Qadiri with a foreword by Hakim Muhammad Musa Amritsari, and Nicholson's English with a foreword by Hazrat Shahidullah Faridi.

The older literature on the saint runs from Attar's *Tazkirat al-Awliya*, Amir Hasan Sijzi's *Fawaid al-Fawad* and Ahmad Zanjani's *Tuhfat al-Wasilin*, a book no longer extant, to Jami's *Nafahat al-Uns*, Abu al-Fazl's *Ain-e Akbari* and Dara Shikoh's *Safinat al-Awliya*, and Dara Shikoh and Maulana Jami praised him to the utmost. A Persian life of the saint, Muhammad 'Alim's *Ahwal-i 'Ali Hujwiri*, whose fourth to sixth chapters concern his authority over India and the Punjab and the visit of Moinuddin Chishti, survives in a single manuscript at Gulra.

To his grave, still, the city brings its dead to be prayed over, its poor to be fed, its sick and sorrowful to seek relief, and its lovers of God to sing through the night. Bombed and rebuilt, endowed by emperors and thronged by the poorest, Data Darbar remains what it has been for nine centuries: the living heart of the city of Data, and one of the enduring wonders of the Muslim world.
