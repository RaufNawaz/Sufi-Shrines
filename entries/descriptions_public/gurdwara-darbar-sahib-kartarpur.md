## Overview

Gurdwara Darbar Sahib Kartarpur, also called Kartarpur Sahib, stands on the right bank of the River Ravi in Narowal District, in Pakistan's Punjab province, about two kilometres from the border with India. It marks the place where Guru Nanak, the founder of Sikhism, spent the last years of his life and where, in 1539, he died, which makes it one of the most venerated sites in the Sikh world and, in a real sense, the birthplace of the Sikh community as a settled, worshipping body. Its white domes, long visible across the fields from the Indian side of the border, became in 2019 the end point of the Kartarpur Corridor, a pilgrims' passage that needs no visa and that reunited the shrine with the devotees of a divided Punjab.

Kartarpur lies about 80 kilometres from Lahore, about an hour from the city of Narowal, and 180 kilometres by way of Lahore from Nankana Sahib, where the Guru was born. The shrine stands out, for there is no habitation around it, and the nearby railway station on the line from Lahore to Chak Amru, in Shakkar Garh, bears its name, Darbar Sahib Kartarpur. It is a different place from the town of Kartarpur about 15 kilometres from Jalandhar, which was founded in 1594 by Guru Arjan Dev.

## The Guru

Guru Nanak was born in 1469 at Nankana Sahib, earlier called Talwandi. His father was Kalyan Chand Das Bedi, the accountant known as Mehta Kalu. He married Sulakhini, daughter of Mul Chand and Chando Rani of the town of Batala, on 24 September 1487, though accounts of the marriage differ, and they had two sons, Sri Chand, born on 8 September 1494, and Lakhmi Das, born on 12 February 1497. Sri Chand became an ascetic and never married, and so the Guru's line is carried on through his younger son. The Udasi sect, which follows Sri Chand's tradition, is believed to have been founded by him, and the mainstream Sikh community did not recognise him as Guru.

He spent much of his life in what is today Pakistan, and it was there that he finally settled. Accounts of how many journeys he made differ, but his *udasis*, the long missionary journeys, are most often counted as four; they lasted more than twenty years and took him, mostly on foot, east to Bengal, south to Sri Lanka, north to Kashmir, Nepal and Tibet, and west to Mecca. Coming back from eastern India, he found the Punjab plunged into a number of political crises, as Babur invaded for the third time, in 1520, reaching as far as Sialkot. In the janamsakhis, the cycles of tales told of the first Guru's life, his last journey went by Multan, Sukkur, Thatta and Hinglaj, across the Arabian Sea to Mecca, Medina and Baghdad, and home by Iran, Kabul, Peshawar, Kandahar, Hasan Abdal, Tilla Balnath, Katasraj and Saidpur to Kartarpur.

## The Abode of the Creator

Guru Nanak is said to have founded the settlement of Kartarpur, "the abode of the Creator", around 1504, on land beside the Ravi; accounts of when he founded it differ. The janamsakhis tell that he came to the region between the rivers Ravi and Beas and chose land on the banks of the Ravi for his permanent home. A farmer of Pakhoke village, Ajit Randhawa, with a group of other farmers, pledged the land, and the foundations of the new settlement were laid. People of every faith and religion came, and the place soon took on the look of a pilgrim town, as Talwandi and Sultanpur had before it. His parents came to live there, and so did the parents of Mardana, the singer of his verses.

In the same telling, after a few months at Kartarpur the Guru set out on his journeys again, and it was at Kartarpur that the last of them ended. There he laid aside the pilgrim's clothes and took the garb of an ordinary householder. He settled at Kartarpur for the last years of his life, and by many accounts he spent some eighteen years there. His wife and children joined him. Here the wandering teacher became the founder of a living community. Setting aside the ascetic's road, he took up the life of a householder and farmer and gathered around him a *sangat*, a congregation, of disciples. Devotees moved there too, and from this base he travelled to the regions around. In his last years, the janamsakhis tell, he crossed the Ravi south east to Achal, a village near Batala in Gurdaspur district, for Maha Shivratri, the Hindu festival of Shiva.

It was at Kartarpur that the lasting institutions of Sikhism took shape. The Guru enjoined on his followers the threefold discipline still summed up as *kirat karo*, to earn an honest living, *vand chhako*, to share one's earnings with the needy, and *naam japo*, to remember the divine Name. He instituted the *langar*, the free communal kitchen, where all, whatever their caste, creed or station, sat together in a single row, or *pangat*, to eat, and he set the daily rhythm of shared devotion, the singing of hymns at dawn and dusk. Kartarpur is thus held to be the first Sikh commune and the model on which every later gurdwara has been built.

Three traditions took firm root there: *seva*, selfless service; *kirtan*, the singing of hymns; and the *langar*. The Guru's compositions were sung at the appropriate times of the day to the music of Mardana's *rabaab*, a plucked string instrument. In the kitchen everyone who could drew water, ground wheat, gathered wood, cooked, served and scrubbed, and people brought whatever they could for the evening meal.

By the end of the Guru's journeys the settlement had grown into a full township of people from different social backgrounds, castes and creeds, yet they were by no means a religious or monastic order: each went about an ordinary, everyday occupation. The Guru and his disciples worked the fields, and there were *sevadars*, volunteers in the community's service, with bullock carts. Disciples flocked to the *dera*, the settlement, and made their homes in it, and Duni Chand of Lahore was among its greatest contributors. Its people were of two kinds: the original inhabitants, who had come and settled when the Guru first marked out the site, and new settlers from the communities of his followers along the routes of his journeys.

## The Passing of the Guru

The Guru's successor came to him at Kartarpur. Bhai Lehna, a Khatri, became his disciple after the Guru had settled there; the janamsakhis tell that he was on pilgrimage to Jwalamukhi when he reached Kartarpur, and that he stayed on, working in the fields, on a building site and in the *langar*. Though he came from a rich family, as the Guru's disciple he was made to perform such humiliating tasks as carrying loads of wet grass. When a newly built wing of the *dharamshala*, the house of worship and shelter, nearly collapsed in the monsoon, it is told, Lehna led an overnight repair.

Before his death Guru Nanak set aside his own sons in favour of this devoted disciple, his most loyal student, renaming him Angad, "of my own limb", and installing him as the second Guru of the Sikhs, Guru Angad Dev. With this choice he founded the line of succession by spiritual merit rather than by birth that would run through ten Gurus. He asked Guru Angad to move to Khadur rather than stay at Kartarpur, to avoid the ire of his sons. Guru Nanak died at Kartarpur, by tradition on 22 September 1539; accounts of the date of his death differ.

A legend of the janamsakhi tradition surrounds his passing. His Hindu and Muslim followers, it is said, fell to disputing whether his body should be cremated or buried, but when the sheet covering him was lifted, only a heap of flowers remained. Each community, the legend goes on, took its share, the one to cremate and the other to bury, and each raised a memorial. The shrine is understood to hold both a *samadhi*, a memorial shrine, and a grave, side by side.

## The Gurdwara

Over the centuries the original memorial by the Ravi was threatened again and again by the shifting course of the river, which is traditionally said to have swept away earlier structures. The present gurdwara, with its white domes, dates from the early twentieth century and is said to have been built with the patronage of the Maharaja of Patiala; accounts of its building differ. At one time, because it lay so close to the border, the place was a centre for smugglers, and drug addicts too had settled there. The structure was renovated in 2001, and its keepers prepared their first *langar* there at that time.

Before the corridor opened, the roads of the border area were in a bad condition, and a mud track led towards the Ravi, which still flowed next to the shrine. On an earlier visit, one visitor had had to walk the three kilometres from the main road to the gurdwara through partly flooded rice paddies. Policemen sat at the gate on a *charpoy*, a woven string bed, drinking tea, and an old Muslim man and his wife from a neighbouring village walked in without any trouble.

A newly built wall protected the main shrine. Beside the parking stood what appeared to be a missile, kept in a glass case with a marble plaque, and next to it a well covered with marble, whose plaque said that it was the well Guru Nanak used to water his fields. Sugarcane grew nearby, and a peasant was working the fields. Visitors took off their shoes and covered their heads with skullcaps placed by the entrance. The main structure stood in the middle of a courtyard, with rooms around it for pilgrims. It was built around the *samadhi* of Guru Nanak, which lay behind a glass door, and the grave lay on one side of the building. "There is a grave of Nanak next to his samadhi," the *granthi*, the reader and keeper of the scripture, said. A *langar* hall and a pool were still to come.

At that time the *granthi* was Inderjit. His family belonged to a village in Faisalabad district and were not Sikhs but Nanak Panthi Hindus, Hindus who believe in the teachings of Guru Nanak but not in the Khalsa, the order of initiated Sikhs; he himself wore the garb of a baptised Sikh of the Khalsa. At Partition his grandfather, on his way to India, was stopped near the border by the Muslims of their village, who promised him security, and the family lived there peacefully until the war of 1965, when it moved to Nankana. Inderjit lived in a shed built with the help of the Sikh diaspora, furnished with a double bed, a sofa set and a television, and as a government official he lived under the government's protection.

The building's style was itself disputed. Inderjit said that Sikhs from England and Canada had complained that the architecture of the shrine was not, strictly speaking, Sikh: it had many features of Muslim architecture, and its arches were plain, a feature of Muslim architecture, where Sikh arches usually had three cusps.

The frontier lies to the east, across the river. The Ravi sometimes crossed into India and at other points came back to Pakistan, and otherwise it was parched throughout the year. Had the line drawn in 1947 erred by only a few centimetres, the shrine of Kartarpur Sahib would have ended up in India.

In the years before 2019 the complex was extensively restored and enlarged, its precincts widened to receive great numbers of visitors, and some count it among the largest gurdwaras in the world. Within, the *Guru Granth Sahib*, the Sikh scripture, is installed and read, and *langar* is served to all who come.

## Devotion and the Langar

The devotion at Kartarpur was not only Sikh. The old Muslim couple who had walked in at the gate prayed at the grave, and the woman touched it with her hand and passed the hand over her head, blessing herself. Local Muslims, the *granthi* said, came regularly and offered their *fateha*, the opening of the Quran recited as a prayer, just as they would at any Muslim shrine. Local people still came to the grave, brought offerings and cleaned the shrine as a service, a custom already followed before Partition.

The *langar* depended on the neighbours. Rich Muslim families of the neighbouring villages gave money to cook it, as their offering to the shrine, and the wood for its cooking was provided by the Rangers, the paramilitary force. At first Muslims were hesitant to eat food cooked by Sikhs, but in time more Muslims came to eat there than Sikhs.

In Aurangzeb's reign the Guru's followers counted service of the traveller, of whoever comes and goes, as the highest worship, and whoever arrived at midnight speaking Baba Nanak's name was treated as a brother and a friend and served, even a stranger, a thief or a highwayman.

## Pilgrimage and the Corridor

The Partition of 1947 left Kartarpur in Pakistan and the town of Dera Baba Nanak, only a few kilometres away, in India. For years Indian pilgrims gathered at a raised viewing platform on their side of the border simply to gaze at the domes. Across the border, behind a fence, hundreds of people gathered every day to perform *darshan*, the devotional viewing of a holy place, looking at the shrine of Guru Nanak through binoculars.

The idea of a corridor was raised as early as 1999, but it was for the 550th anniversary of Guru Nanak's birth that it finally came about. Foundation stones were laid on both sides in November 2018, and the corridor was inaugurated on 9 November 2019, when a first *jatha*, a band of pilgrims, several hundred strong, crossed from India. The passage allows Indian Sikhs to visit the shrine without a visa and to return the same day, and it has been widely hailed as a rare gesture of goodwill between two often estranged neighbours.

## Legends

Several stories gather around the site. The plaque beside the parking said that the missile in its glass case "was dropped here by the Indian forces in 1971 but due to the miracle of Guru Nanak, it did not blow up". Among local Muslims there was a belief that the river floods every twenty odd years and comes right up to the shrine, and that this is the river paying tribute to the shrine of Nanak. The *granthi* spoke too of a giant snake, bigger than two men together, and said that snakes found in the fields are placed beside the shrine, and that after paying their respects they return, for all they want is a sight of the shrine.

The janamsakhis also tell stories of the early settlement. One tells of a Brahmin who came to the place where the *dera* was being built, and of his "kitchen" there: a cubit of consecrated ground was dug and bones were found, and the Brahmin relented and asked to share in the *langar*. In another, Moola Keer, one of the earliest settlers in Kartarpur, covered for a guest who had stolen his wife's jewellery, and the thief confessed after the *kirtan*.

## The Guru's Verse

The hymns sung at Kartarpur were the Guru's own. His sermon was his *bani*, devotional verses of supplication, entreaty, humility and reverence, which was compiled as part of the Adi Granth by Guru Arjan Dev, the fifth Guru, in 1604. He composed verses in Hindi on the truth of God, setting out divine unity in clear expressions and shining metaphors, and the singer Mardana sang his verses and drew people into the net of faith. Like Kabir, he used the *doha*, a rhyming couplet in an old Prakrit and Hindi metre, for his mystical poetry, and he brought into his poetry words that were not part of the Punjabi of his time. In Aurangzeb's reign the sum of his followers' worship was the reading of their master's verses, which they sang with song and melody.

Lines from his hymns, in English translation, give a sense of the poetry. His *aarti*, the hymn sung as lamps are waved in worship, begins:

ਗਗਨ ਮੈ ਥਾਲੁ
ਰਵਿ ਚੰਦੁ ਦੀਪਕ ਬਨੇ …

*In the salver of the firmament, / The sun and the moon shine as lamps*

On the oneness of God he sang:

ਸਾਹਿਬੁ ਮੇਰਾ ਏਕੋ ਹੈ ॥
ਏਕੋ ਹੈ ਭਾਈ ਏਕੋ ਹੈ ॥

*My Master is One / He is One, brother, the only One*

And again:

*God is One, / The Path is One*

His poem on the sacred thread, the *janeu*, runs:

ਦਇਆ ਕਪਾਹ
ਸੰਤੋਖੁ ਸੂਤੁ …

*From the cotton of compassion, / Spin the thread of contentment*

Of washing he sang:

ਭਰੀਐ ਹਥੁ ਪੈਰੁ ਤਨੁ ਦੇਹ ॥
ਪਾਣੀ ਧੋਤੈ ਉਤਰਸੁ ਖੇਹ ॥

*As hands or feet besmirched with slime, / Water washes white*

In the *Babur Bani*, a long poem written when Babur attacked the Punjab, destroying the cities of Eminabad and Lahore on the way, Nanak lays the blame of Babur's wrath at God's feet.

The verse is sung beyond the gurdwaras too. The singer Abida Parvin has said: "I sing Shah Hussain, Bulle Shah, Mian Muhammad Bakhsh, Hazrat Khawaja Ghulam Farid, Sacchal Sarmast, Baba Farid, and Guru Nanak." In Sindh, Sain Vasan Shah became famous for reciting the *banis* of Shri Guru Nanak and the *kalams*, the poems, of the Sufis.

## Legacy

For Sikhs, Kartarpur is where their Guru laid down the pattern of a whole way of life, of work, worship and shared bread, and where he closed his earthly journey. The opening of the corridor turned a shrine long visible but out of reach into a place of living pilgrimage once more, drawing thousands across the border and restoring Kartarpur to its central place in the devotional geography of the Panth, the Sikh community.

It was also a place shared across the line drawn in 1947, where Muslims from the neighbouring villages prayed at the grave, paid for the *langar* and ate at it. Many Muslims acquainted with the teachings of Guru Nanak are devoted to him; to one Muslim painter, Guru Nanak is like a Muslim Sufi saint. Baba Farid, it is said, became an inspiration for Guru Nanak when Nanak visited his shrine on his travels, and Farid's texts are revered among the Sikhs, for he is held to have influenced the founder of their religion. A twentieth century shaykh of the Chishti Sabiri Sufi order counted Guru Nanak, with Krishna, Rama, Sita and Lachman, among the spiritual luminaries outside Islam who believed in the one true God and had a "Divine connection", *nisbat*. Far to the south west, at the shrine of the goddess Hinglaj, the engraved footsteps of Guru Nanak are found.

Across Pakistan there are several sites where Nanak stayed or is said to have performed miracles, and the gurdwaras built there later by his devotees still stand tall. Nanak himself is held to have abhorred the association of miracles with religious personalities, yet the stories of his life are full of them. He lived as a householder, and it is the householder's life, of work in the fields and of hymns sung at the appropriate times of the day, that the janamsakhis place at Kartarpur.
