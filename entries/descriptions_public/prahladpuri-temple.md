## Overview

The Prahladpuri Temple stood on a raised platform at the southern tip of the great fort of Multan, immediately beside the shrine of the Sufi saint Bahauddin Zakariya, so that Multan's Hindu and Muslim sacred pasts meet on the same commanding mound. It was dedicated to *Narasimha*, the incarnation of the god Vishnu as half man and half lion, and named for *Prahlada*, the prince who was Vishnu's devotee. It was among the most storied Hindu temples of the Indus plain, and popular tradition remembers it as a place from which the spring festival of *Holi* took its origin. Today the temple survives only as a ruin.

Known also as the temple of Prahlad Bhagat, Prahlad the devotee, it was one of the most important Hindu temples of Multan, a city that tradition makes the very setting of Prahlada's story. Its destruction in 1992 severed a line of worship that legend traced back to the beginnings of the world's ages.

## The Legend of Prahlada

The temple belongs to the cycle of the *Narasimha* legend, one of the best known stories of Vaishnava devotion, the worship of Vishnu, and a myth canonised in the *Bhagavata Purana*, a Hindu scripture devoted to Vishnu. As the story is told, the *asura*, or demon, king Hiranyakashipu had won a boon that made him all but invincible, and he demanded to be worshipped as a god. His own son, Prahlada, remained steadfastly devoted to Vishnu. Enraged, the king sought to destroy the boy, whose faith was at last vindicated when Vishnu burst forth from a pillar in the royal court in the form of Narasimha, half man and half lion. Narasimha slew the tyrant at a threshold that was neither inside nor out, at a twilight that was neither day nor night, and so got round every term of the boon.

Local tradition holds that it was Prahlada himself who raised the temple at Multan in thanksgiving, enshrining an image of Narasimha on the spot, though accounts of its founding differ. Hiranyakashipu, the tyrant of the story, is believed to have ruled the city of Multan thousands of years ago.

Bound to this is the tradition that connects Multan with the origins of *Holi*. Holika, the demoness sister of Hiranyakashipu, sought to burn the child Prahlada, but she herself was consumed while he emerged unharmed. Her bonfire is remembered in the spring festival of colour and fire, and the temple at Multan was long held to be the very stage of that deliverance. Whatever weight one gives these legends as history, they made Prahladpuri a site of unusual resonance in the sacred geography of the subcontinent.

## The Temple

It is held that the mound came to be considered holy because of the temple, and that it then became a popular spiritual site with mystics and saints. Among the Muslim mystics it drew was Bahauddin Zakariya, whose shrine came to stand next to the temple. In the days of Guru Nanak, the founder of the Sikh faith, Multan was one of the biggest cities of the Punjab, known for the shrines of Muslim saints and for its Jain and Hindu temples, a sacred space for people of every religious denomination.

For centuries the temple was a living centre of worship, repaired and rebuilt through the changing fortunes of Multan's turbulent history. During the siege of Multan in 1848, in the campaigns that came before the British annexation of the Punjab, a shell struck a powder magazine inside the fort, and the explosion that followed is said to have carried away the temple's roof. In the aftermath the East India Company held the fort directly for some years, before restoring the shrines to their communities around 1852. The building suffered again in 1881, when it was set alight amid communal violence, and once more Multan's Hindu community renovated it.

## Destruction and After

The temple's end came in December 1992. On the 6th of December that year the Babri Masjid at Ayodhya in India was demolished, and in the wave of reprisals that followed across Pakistan a mob attacked and destroyed the Prahladpuri Temple and its adjoining *dharamshala*, the rest house for pilgrims, reducing the historic structure to rubble. Accounts differ over whether worship there had continued after Partition, and over how much of the building still stands. Hindus who were well known in the cities of the Punjab had to hide for several months after the demolition at Ayodhya.

Parvati Devi, a classically trained professional singer of Multan who is also a social activist, lived through those days. She recalls that a mob which had destroyed the Valmiki temple near her house wanted to burn her alive; afterwards she took up a Muslim name, Shaila Bibi, and stopped appearing on radio and television.

The site of the temple is held by the Evacuee Trust Property Board, the state body that administers the properties of communities displaced at Partition, though accounts differ over which government body has charge of it. Proposals for its restoration have been discussed from time to time, but no full rebuilding has been achieved. The order of the two holy places on the mound has reversed: in the early twenty first century the temple lay in obscurity under the shadow of the massive shrine beside it.

Parvati Devi recalls that in 2006 and 2008 the authorities of Bahauddin Zakariya's shrine extended it into the premises of the historical Prahlad Temple, building first a place for ablutions and then a hall for the *langar*, the free communal kitchen. The matter reached the courts. Usman Chaudary, president of the RAS Society, the Organisation for Religious and Sexual Minorities, filed a petition in the High Court, and the court passed a stay order against any further construction on the premises of the temple. The society has petitioned that the temple be handed back to the Hindu community of the city. The demand of Multan's Hindus, in Parvati Devi's words, is that the temple "be restored and given to the Hindus of the city who are its real owners".

## Holi in Multan

In the early twenty first century the festival that tradition ties to this temple was kept in Multan, though not at the temple itself: the celebrations were held in a Hindu locality and in front of the city's press club. The Hindus who kept it had lived in Multan since before it became a part of Pakistan, and the thirty odd women who burned the Holika were by then the only link that the city had with that story. The locality, next to the railway station, was one of several areas where the city's Hindus lived. The community numbered little more than a thousand and had no centralised celebration; the settlement had about a hundred houses, only about ten to twelve of them belonging to Hindu families. Multan had several historical temples from the days before Partition; by then all of them had become ruins, and the community had turned other spaces into makeshift temples.

The public festival had lapsed and then returned. As recently as 2010, festivals including Holi were not celebrated in the city. That year Usman Chaudary organised a token celebration of Holi in front of the Multan Press Club, and by the early 2010s Holi, though on a small scale, was celebrated every year in Multan and regularly featured in the local media. Usman has been alleged to be an Indian agent, and more than once he and his friends have been physically attacked.

## The Setting

Multan, the city in which the temple stood, lies where three rivers merge before the Indus, and it was a commercial hub for trade at home and abroad, including trade with Persia, Central Asia and the Arab world. In the time of the *Mahabharata*, the Sanskrit epic, it was the capital of the Tuganta kingdom, and legend has Alexander struck by an arrow there. For the next thousand years Multan was ruled by Hindu and Buddhist kings. A Chinese traveller visited it in 641, and it has been called the city of gold; its Suraj temple, the temple of the sun, was claimed to be by far the biggest temple in the subcontinent.

In 711 the Arabs conquered Sind and the provinces to its north up to Multan, and in time Multan became a dependency of the Fatimids of Egypt. In the eleventh century Mahmud of Ghazni damaged the Suraj temple and destroyed its giant idol, and by the middle of that century Sufism had reached the country around Multan. In 1175 Mu‘izzu'd-Din Muhammad bin Sam seized the city. In time the town became studded with mosques as it had once been with Hindu temples. By the time Guru Nanak came to Multan it was famous as a major centre of Sufi learning and philosophy, and in the reign of Aurangzeb it was counted one of the ancient cities, with a fort of brick, and many saints lay at rest in that blessed city.

The wars of the nineteenth century touched the whole city. Hafiz Muhammad Jamal, a successor of Nur Muhammad, is said to have taken part in resistance to Sikh attacks on Multan before his death in 1811. When Ranjit Singh conquered Multan, with Kharak Singh sent against the city, Nawab Muzaffar Khan was killed, and others of his family were captured with their households and came to Lahore. Diwan Mulraj, son of Sawan Mal, governor of Multan, raised a disturbance in the city and had its new governor killed, together with two sahibs, men of rank. The troubles spread from Multan through Hazara and four hard battles, until the whole country passed into English rule.

Multan enters the poetry of the region too, though never this temple. In one *kafi*, a Sufi devotional lyric, by Madho Lal Hussein, the city is named as the place where one item of a bride's trousseau was bought:

*The dress / someone carried across the snows / all the way from Kashmir. The dress / someone brought from Gujarat… The dress bought in Multan* (translated by Naveed Alam)

For the poets of Pakistan's own languages, Mecca and Medina are inside the heart, and for those who wrote in Sindhi and Punjabi, so is Multan.

## Legacy

The ruins of Prahladpuri remain among the most poignant of Pakistan's lost Hindu monuments, a fragment of the deep Hindu antiquity of Multan, a city whose very name has long been bound to a famed sun temple and whose fort mound has borne temples and shrines in almost unbroken succession for millennia. That the remains of the Narasimha temple and the tomb of a great Muslim saint share the same eminence is itself a lesson in the layered religious history of the Indus valley.

For the Hindu communities of Pakistan, and for pilgrims and scholars abroad, Prahladpuri endures as a place of memory: a temple whose legend gave the subcontinent one of its most joyous festivals, and whose fate marks one of the harder chapters of the region's recent past. Its claim on memory runs both ways. Multan goes on unaware of its ancient past and of its tie to the story of Hiranyakashipu and Prahlad Bhagat, while its Hindus ask that the temple be given back to them.
