## Overview

Gurdwara Dera Sahib, known more fully as Gurdwara Dera Sahib *Patshahi Panjvin*, "of the Fifth Guruship", stands in Lahore, just beyond the walls of the old city, and commemorates the place associated with the martyrdom in 1606 of Guru Arjan Dev, the fifth of the Sikh Gurus and the first to lay down his life for the faith. A *gurdwara*, a Sikh house of worship, it stands amid the great Mughal and Sikh monuments of Lahore, close by the Lahore Fort, the Badshahi Mosque, the Hazuri Bagh and the *samadhi*, the memorial tomb, of Maharaja Ranjit Singh. For Sikhs it is a place of profound solemnity, marking an event that transformed the character and history of their community, and pilgrims come to it above all on the anniversary of the Guru's martyrdom.

## Guru Arjan Dev

Guru Arjan was born in 1563, the son of the fourth Guru, Ram Das, and grandson of the third, Amar Das. He led the Sikhs as Guru from 1581 until his death in 1606; accounts of the year of his death are not unanimous, but 1606 is the year generally given. He presided over the Sikh *Panth*, the community of the faith, at a moment of remarkable growth and consolidation, and under his guidance Sikhism flourished as an organised community with its own scripture, its own centre and a widening body of devotees.

His succession was a contest with his elder brother, Prithi Chand. Sikh tradition finds the root of the conflict in a journey Arjan made to Lahore and in the machinations of Prithi Chand, who hid the letters Arjan wrote from Lahore to their father. When Arjan produced two letters that had been withheld, hidden in Prithi Chand's robe, Prithi Chand was deeply ashamed. Guru Ram Das disowned him and appointed his younger son, Arjan, as the next Guru. It is told that Ram Das, on the advice of the one who from the beginning had seated each Guru on the *gaddi*, the seat of the Guru, with his own hand, asked Arjan for a fourth *pauri*, a stanza, so that the name of the four *pauris* should be remembered until the Day of Judgement, and that when Arjan composed it at once, Ram Das seated him on the *gaddi* to stand in his own place. His fame spread within a short time.

بھاگ ہویا گر سنت ملایا …
جن نانک داس تمہارے جی

*The opening and closing words of the pauri that Guru Arjan composed when Guru Ram Das seated him on the gaddi.*

Prithi Chand then approached the Mughal authorities to get his share of the property, and so began the troubled relation between the Mughals and the Sikhs. He bore his brother enmity to the utmost degree and slandered him before the Emperor Jahangir, complaining through the emperor's *diwan*, a minister who was himself an enemy of the Guru, while Nawab Wazir Khan defended Arjan as a man of God. Arjan admonished his brother again and again, and still the harassment went on. Once Prithi Chand came against Amritsar with some Afghans, intending to kill the Guru, who hid in a village a few *kos*, an old measure of distance, from Amritsar; the Afghans died on the road. Prithi Chand petitioned the court with the support of Chandu Shah, and an officer, a *mir*, was sent to arrest the Guru, but near Ambala he was killed by four servants he had dismissed. The rumour spread that this had happened through the Guru's blessing; Chandu was rebuked, and the Guru was held blameless.

## The Scripture and the Community

Guru Arjan completed the Amrit Sar, the tank of nectar that his father had begun at Ramdaspur, and the central shrine at Amritsar, the Harmandir Sahib, later known as the Golden Temple, was raised there. He began the tradition of the *daswandh*, by which Sikhs gave a tenth of their earnings to the cause of the Guru, collected through the Masands, the Guru's deputies. In 1594 he founded Kartarpur, near Jalandhar, on land granted during the reign of the third Mughal emperor, Jalal-ud-din Muhammad Akbar.

The act for which he is most often remembered is the compilation of the scripture. In 1604, at Amritsar, he completed the *Adi Granth*, the first authoritative scripture of the Sikhs, also known as the Granth Sahib or the Guru Granth Sahib. He gathered into it the hymns of the Gurus before him, Guru Nanak's among them, together with those of Hindu and Muslim saints, including the poems of the fourteen *bhaktas*, devotional poets, who came before Guru Nanak. More than a hundred *slokas*, verses, ascribed to Baba Farid Ganj-i Shakar are in it: Guru Nanak had collected Baba Farid's poetry at Pak Pattan, and Guru Arjan later included it in the Granth Sahib. He installed the volume in the Harmandir Sahib with Baba Buddha as its first custodian.

He sent one of his followers, Bhai Bannu, to Lahore to bind the *Adi Granth*. After the compilation, at Prithi Chand's prompting, he was summoned to answer for its alleged content against Muslims and Hindus, and he sent Bhai Gurdas, who cleared up the misunderstanding. The *Adi Granth* played a large part in the growth of a separate Sikh identity, and among the reasons for its making, it is said, was the rumour that Meherban, the son of Prithi Chand, was compiling a scripture of his own. Guru Arjan himself has the greatest number of hymns in the Guru Granth Sahib.

It is said, too, that the Sufi poet Shah Hussein travelled to Amritsar to have his poetry included in the Granth Sahib then being compiled, and offered a *kafi*, a Sufi lyric, for inclusion.

Among Guru Arjan's verses are letters written in verse to Guru Ram Das, a part of his *gurbani*, the sacred verse of the Gurus. One opens with a line of longing for the *darshan*, the sight, of the Guru:

میرا من لوچے گورو درشن تائین

*A line of longing for the sight of the Guru.*

Another carries this line, in praise of the Guru's face:

تیرا مکھ سہاوا جی

*A line in praise of the Guru's face.*

His followers are remembered at shrines of their own. A gurdwara at Mandi Mangat was the house of Bhai Bannu, a devotee of Guru Arjan, and another shrine is that of Sahari Mal, a disciple of Guru Arjan.

## The Martyrdom of 1606

That very flourishing drew the suspicion of the Mughal state. The Emperor Jahangir, the fourth Mughal emperor, records in his memoir, the *Tuzuk-i-Jahangiri*, his displeasure at the Guru's rising influence and at the many who were drawn to him, and complains further that Arjan had given his blessing to the rebellious prince Khusrau, Jahangir's own son, then in flight through the Punjab. The emperor counted the blessing as treason. Accounts of where the prince and the Guru met differ. On these grounds the Guru was summoned to Lahore, where he came with five of his Sikhs, Bhai Jetha among them, and a heavy fine was imposed on him, with punishment if he failed to pay; accounts of the penalty differ.

Sikh tradition holds that the Guru refused to pay or to alter a word of the sacred scripture, and that, at the instigation of the Lahore financier Chandu Shah, he was subjected to days of torture: seated, it is recounted, upon a heated iron plate while burning sand was poured over him, and made to endure scalding water. Accounts of the torments, and of where he was held and for how long, differ; the torture is most often said to have lasted five days. One of his Sikhs, it is told, recited aloud the Guru's own hymns, his *bani*, while he was held. Bearing these torments with serene equanimity, and declining every inducement to convert, Guru Arjan died in 1606. Before his death he appointed his son, Hargobind, as the sixth Guru.

Accounts differ over who bore the chief responsibility for his death, and over Chandu Shah's name and office. Chandu's enmity is traced to a refused marriage: he had wished to marry his daughter to Guru Arjan's son, the match was declined after Sikhs wrote against it, and Arjan married his son to the daughters of two Sikhs. Prithi Chand is remembered as Chandu's ally.

Tradition holds that the Guru's end came in the River Ravi, which then flowed beside the fort, and that his body was never recovered; accounts of the manner of his death differ. It is believed that he lost his life at the place where the gurdwara stands. His *shaheedi*, or martyrdom, is traditionally commemorated in the month of Jeth, in late May or early June. He was the first Sikh Guru to be martyred, and his death is reckoned a watershed in Sikh history.

It is told that Guru Hargobind later told Jahangir that Chandu held Guru Arjan's *mala*, his rosary. Chandu, it is said, was in the end killed by a man of Lahore whom he had used in tormenting the Guru.

## The Gurdwara

Sikh tradition records that the Guru's son and successor, Guru Hargobind, raised a small memorial at the site of the martyrdom in the years that followed, to mark the Guru's *samadhi*; accounts of the first memorial differ. That small structure was still there in 2011. It was under Maharaja Ranjit Singh, in the early nineteenth century, that the present *gurdwara* was first erected, embellished in the manner of Sikh sacred architecture, with a gilded dome that answers, across the Hazuri Bagh, the domes of the Badshahi Mosque, and the Sikh community extended it in the early years of the twentieth century. Its nearness to Ranjit Singh's own *samadhi* binds it into the great ensemble of monuments in this quarter of Lahore. Within, the *Guru Granth Sahib*, the scripture that Guru Arjan himself first compiled, is enshrined and reverently read, and the shrine is a living house of worship.

In the nineteenth century Guru Arjan's *samadh* stood beyond the Roshnai Gate, between the gate and the royal fort. In the 1880s a *samadh* of Guru Arjan stood outside the city opposite the Roshnai Gate, with the wall of the fort to its east and the wall of Maharaja Ranjit Singh's *samadh* to its west. Its position fits Dera Sahib, but a great well inside its enclosure could point instead to Lahore's other shrine of the Guru, Gurdwara Baoli Sahib, and whether it was this shrine has not been settled.

That *samadh* had been a small building at first, and the grand building was made in Ranjit Singh's time. Its enclosure was of fired brick and lime, with a parapet. Two of its doors were kept shut, and all who came and went passed through the great eastern door. To the south, within the four walls, stood the grand domed *samadh*, and to its north a hall of three bays, in the middle bay of which the Granth Sahib was kept with full reverence. In one corner outside the hall was the great well, of fired brick, its stair running from north to south down to the water, where a slab of red stone was set so that a man standing on it could draw water. In another corner stood four *samadhs* of a man's height, one of them that of the mother of Lachhmi Sarkar, wife of Maharaja Ranjit Singh. On the north were rooms built for the Sikhs' rest, and on the south a building plastered with lime, where a stone mortar was sunk in the ground and a thick wooden pestle kept beside it for pounding *bhang*, a preparation of hemp, which the Sikhs ground and drank there.

After the Partition of 1947 the gurdwara came under the control of the Pakistan government through the Evacuee Trust Property Board, the ETPB, which maintains it and regularly carries out renovation work. In 1996 rooms were built in the complex for pilgrims from India and other parts of Pakistan, and the new hostel was named after the Sufi Mian Mir.

In 2011, facing the fort, the gurdwara was a small yellow building with a golden dome, protected by a thick yellow wall, with street vendors at the base of the wall and the Badshahi Mosque beside it. In the garden beside it, where the Sikh ruler of the Punjab, Maharaja Ranjit Singh, used to meet his subjects, stood a Mughal *baradari*, a pavilion with twelve pillars. A giant gateway leading from the garden into the yellow building was permanently locked.

Inside, beyond a last security check, the inner structure was the same yellow as the wall, and a couple of rooms at the entrance had been taken over by the security officials. The *Nishan Sahib*, the Sikh flag, stood at the edge of the courtyard on a circular platform about five feet from the ground. The heart of the shrine was a small room with windows on all sides, protected by iron bars, and at its centre a small bulge covered by a velvet cloth: the *samadhi* of Guru Arjan. A small door from the *samadhi* led to the Langar Hall, the hall of the *langar*, the free communal kitchen, which was divided into two sections, with its mats laid in four lines.

## Devotion and Pilgrimage

The shrine remains a solemn and much visited station for the Sikh pilgrims who come to Lahore, above all on the anniversary of the Guru's martyrdom in the month of Jeth. On 6 June 2011 about three hundred pilgrims from India had come to Lahore for the commemoration, and several hundred more from other parts of Pakistan. The Indians were to stay for three days, after which some would return home while others would go on to the sacred shrines of Nankana Sahib and Hassan Abdal. Most of the pilgrims were Sikhs bearing the five Ks, the five articles of the Sikh faith, with a few clean shaven Nanak Panthi Sikhs, followers of Guru Nanak's path, from South Punjab and Sindh. The lawn became a temporary camp for pilgrims without rooms, mostly Pathan Sikh families from Nankana Sahib, Hassan Abdal, Peshawar and the Tribal Areas, while the Indian pilgrims and other important guests had rooms.

The courtyard was tented against the intense heat of June and carpeted, the congregation facing the *granthi*, the reader of the scripture, and the recitation was carried through a small speaker. Men prayed to the *Khanda*, the Sikh emblem, on the *Nishan Sahib*, touched its base and kissed their hands, or touched their eyes to its platform. In the *samadhi* room, which could hold no more than five people, there were about twenty, and women recited from pocket Granths. At the conclusion of the *Akhand Path*, the unbroken reading of the whole scripture, came the *bhog*, the community prayer, and the whole congregation prostrated itself before the Granth Sahib as the living Guru. Sikh boys served cold milk, juice and freshly cut watermelons, and the *langar* was prepared by volunteers and employees, most of them, it seemed, Nanak Panthis of low caste from southern Punjab.

For all its renown, the approach to the shrine was closed. The place was deserted: the only road to it was cordoned off by the police behind a barricade fortified with barbed wire, and only people carrying special cards made for them by the ETPB could pass. Many local tourists returned disappointed. On ordinary days hundreds of local visitors peeked in, and the security men's answer was: "If you are a Muslim, you are not allowed." Male and female police searched the pilgrims, and bags passed through an X ray machine.

Members of Pakistan's own Sikh community were at the shrine that year. Kartar Singh Kartar was staying at the gurdwara for his education, given free accommodation and food at the shrine, and joked that he was a *langur*, a kind of monkey; he hoped to do a doctorate in the Punjabi language. Dr Manoj Singh, thirty two, the first MBBS doctor, holding the basic medical degree, from the Pakistani Sikh community, was serving at the Ganga Ram Hospital. Premjeet Singh, twenty five, the first Pakistani Sikh to be commissioned into the Pakistan Rangers, had been allotted a room because of his social standing, and shared it with five other members of his family.

## Legends

For devotees the Guru's end was more than a death. Legend has it that he was lifted alive into the sky by God, and in Sikh belief, when he went into the waters of the Ravi and did not come out, he had performed a miracle and passed on to the other world.

The Sufi Mian Mir has his own place in the story. In Sikh tradition Mian Mir asked the Guru's leave to bury his oppressors alive, and the Guru refused. The friendship between Mian Mir and Guru Arjan is the subject of several folk tales.

## The Setting

The Roshnai Gate, one of the thirteen gateways into the ancient walled city of Lahore, lies between the Alamgiri Gate of the fort and the Badshahi Mosque. Lamps were lit all along its passageway, hence its name, the gate of lights. Behind it lies the Heera Mandi bazaar, and the area is famous too for the food street that runs parallel to the wall of the Badshahi Mosque.

The river beside which the Guru died has moved: the Ravi, which flowed next to the fort, has changed its course, moving a few kilometres away. The city's Sikhs had thinned too, and by 2011 it had become rare to meet a Sikh in a city that was once ruled by a Sikh and was the seat of their political strength. Lahore holds more of the Gurus' memory than this shrine. Guru Ram Das was born in the city; Guru Arjan spent a lot of time in Lahore, and so did Guru Hargobind.

## Legacy

The martyrdom of Guru Arjan altered the course of Sikh history. He is remembered as the last of the Gurus who focused solely on spiritual matters, and his death marks the beginning of a long conflict between the Mughal authorities and the Sikh Gurus. His son, Guru Hargobind, on assuming the Guruship, is remembered as having girded himself with two swords, symbolising *miri* and *piri*, temporal and spiritual authority, and as having built the Akal Takht at Amritsar, so arming and organising the community for its own defence. After the death of the fifth Guru the Sikhs dropped their pacifist policy and took on the martial spirit for which they became famous. The martyrdom gave the community a cohesive motivation; it has been called the Karbala of Sikh history, and it is held to have hastened the development of the Khalsa, the order of initiated Sikhs.

For Sikhs, Gurdwara Dera Sahib stands not only as the memorial of a death but as the birthplace of an idea: that faith may be defended, and that the willing acceptance of martyrdom is itself a form of victory.

Guru Arjan is regarded as the first martyr of Sikhism, and a long list of martyrs followed him. His memory reaches far beyond Lahore: Operation Blue Star was launched on a day when thousands of Sikhs from all over the Punjab had gathered to commemorate it, and its victims became the latest entry in that list. Tegh Bahadur was the first Guru after Guru Arjan to compose hymns. Guru Arjan's name is among those painted after Guru Nanak's picture on the dome at Jhaman.
