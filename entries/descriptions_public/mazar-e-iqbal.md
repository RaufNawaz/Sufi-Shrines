## Overview

Mazar-e-Iqbal is the tomb of Allama Muhammad Iqbal (1877 to 1938), the poet, philosopher and jurist honoured across Pakistan as its ideological father, the Mufakkir-e-Pakistan, or Thinker of Pakistan. It stands in the Hazuri Bagh, the walled garden between the great Badshahi Mosque and the Lahore Fort, in the monumental heart of the old city and within sight of the Minar-e-Pakistan. It is not a Sufi shrine in the traditional sense, the tomb of a Muslim mystic, but the resting place of a modern thinker; yet Pakistanis approach it as a place of pilgrimage and national homage, coming to recite the Fatiha, the opening chapter of the Qur'an, and to honour the man whose verse and vision gave the Muslims of South Asia the idea of a homeland of their own.

It is one of the most revered sites in the country, and to understand it is above all to understand the life, the poetry and the thought of the man who lies within it.

## The Poet's Life

Muhammad Iqbal was born on 9 November 1877. Accounts of his birthplace differ, but it is generally given as the city of Sialkot, in the Punjab, and it was in Sialkot that he spent his boyhood. His family descended from Kashmiri Brahmins who had embraced Islam some generations before and settled in the plains, a descent the tomb's records still register, in the old style, as "Sheikh/Brahman". His father, Sheikh Noor Muhammad, was a tailor of modest means but of unusual spiritual depth, a man remembered for his piety and his love of the Qur'an.

Devotional tradition tells that before Iqbal's birth his father dreamed of a radiant white bird descending from the sky, sought after by many hands, which at last settled in his own lap, and that he woke convinced a remarkable child was coming to his house. It was Sheikh Noor Muhammad who first shaped his son's inner life. He urged the boy to recite the holy book slowly and with attention, as though it had been revealed for him to understand, and that counsel left in Iqbal a lifelong intimacy with the Qur'an.

Near the family home the boy was placed in the care of the learned Sayyid Mir Hassan, an imam, or prayer leader, and a teacher of rare gifts, who taught him Arabic, Persian and Urdu and quickened in him a hunger to understand rather than merely to memorise. Recognising his pupil's promise, Mir Hassan guided him on to the Scotch Mission High School in Sialkot, a Christian school, where the young Iqbal is said to have completed two classes in a single year. In these years too, at his elders' wish and against his own inclination, he was married as a boy of about sixteen to Karim Bibi, of a family from Gujrat. The marriage, entered so young, did not endure happily, and through his life Iqbal's deepest energies were drawn away from a settled home towards learning, poetry and the cause of his people.

From Sialkot Iqbal moved to Lahore to study at Government College, then the foremost seat of learning in the province. There he read philosophy and English literature and came under the influence of the Orientalist Sir Thomas Arnold, a teacher whose learning and friendship shaped him profoundly and who later drew him towards Europe. Even as a student his poetry began to circulate, and when some of his verses were shown to the celebrated Delhi master Dagh Dehlvi, a connoisseur of Urdu, Dagh is said to have marvelled that whoever had written them was no ordinary poet. After taking his degree Iqbal joined the Oriental College, Lahore, in 1899 as a reader in Arabic and taught philosophy, already known in the city's *mushairas*, the literary gatherings where poets recite, for verse of unusual power. From this period, too, dates his long association with his devoted household servant Ali Baksh, who served him faithfully to the end and who, after the poet's death, would tell of the private wonders of his master's life.

In 1905 Iqbal sailed for Europe, where he spent three transforming years. He read philosophy at Trinity College, Cambridge, qualified as a barrister at Lincoln's Inn in London, and took a doctorate at the University of Munich with a dissertation on *The Development of Metaphysics in Persia*, published in 1908. In Europe he came to know Western philosophy at first hand, meeting the currents of thought associated with Nietzsche, Bergson and others, and he saw, too, the spiritual emptiness he believed lay beneath the material power of the modern West. He returned to India in 1908 with a settled conviction: that the Muslims of the world had fallen from greatness through the loss of their own inner vitality, and that they must be reawakened, not by imitating Europe, but by recovering the dynamism, faith and selfhood of their own tradition.

Back in Lahore he took up the practice of law and taught for a time, but he was candid that the law was for him a means and not an end: he practised, he said, only so far as it secured his independence and left him free to serve his nation with his true instruments, which were poetry and thought. After the estrangement of his first marriage he married Sardar Begum and Mukhtar Begum, in the years around 1910 to 1913, and became the father of children, among them his son Javid, to whom the great Persian poem the *Javid Nama* is addressed. In 1922 the British Crown knighted him, and he became Sir Muhammad Iqbal, though the title sat lightly on a man who lived simply and counted his books and his verse his truest companions. He could bestow a title as well: it was Iqbal who started to call the writer Hasan Nizami "Khwaja", or master, a title with ancient resonance in the tradition of the Chishtis, the Sufi order prominent in South Asia.

Iqbal did not hold himself aloof from public affairs. He served as a member of the Punjab Legislative Council and took part in the Round Table Conferences convened in London to debate the constitutional future of India, lending to practical politics the moral authority of a thinker whose deepest labour lay elsewhere. His boldness of thought sometimes drew fire: when he argued that the inherited interpretation of Islamic law must be rethought for the modern age, some among the orthodox turned against him and issued condemnations. In his last years his health declined, and in his final illness he is said to have refused heavy treatment, explaining that he did not wish to be dulled into unconsciousness but to meet death fully aware. He died in Lahore in 1938, on 21 April, the date the nation observes, though accounts of the day differ. To his people he is known by titles that between them capture the range of the man: Shair-e-Mashriq, the Poet of the East; Hakeem-ul-Ummat, the Sage of the Community; and Mufakkir-e-Pakistan, the Thinker of Pakistan.

## The Poet of the East

Iqbal is, before all else, one of the supreme poets of the Urdu and Persian languages, and his verse is the true foundation of his fame. His Urdu collections, *Bang-e-Dara* ("The Call of the Marching Bell"), *Bal-e-Jibril* ("Gabriel's Wing") and *Zarb-e-Kalim* ("The Rod of Moses"), stand beside his great Persian works, among them *Asrar-e-Khudi* ("The Secrets of the Self"), *Rumuz-e-Bekhudi* ("The Mysteries of Selflessness"), *Payam-e-Mashriq* ("The Message of the East") and the visionary *Javid Nama*, in which the poet ascends through the heavens in the company of Rumi. Together they are gathered for readers as the *Kulliyat-e-Iqbal*, the collected works, and it is in this collection that his poetry is most widely read today. His verse ranges from devotion to the most demanding philosophy, and its lines are known by heart, quoted in ordinary speech and sung across the subcontinent.

His books of verse followed one another: the *Asrar-e-Khudi* in 1915, the *Rumuz-e-Bekhudi* in 1917, the *Payam-e-Mashriq* in 1923, the *Bang-e-Dara* in 1924, the Persian *Zabūr-i ʿAjam* in 1927, the *Javid Nama* in 1932, *Musafir* in 1934, the *Bal-e-Jibril* in 1935, the *Zarb-e-Kalim* and *Pas che bāyad kard* in 1936, and the *Armaghān-i Ḥijāz* in 1938. The title *Bang-e-Dara* names the part he took for himself: the caravan bell that helps guide the travellers to the central sanctuary in Mecca.

From his early period comes the children's morning prayer that generations have sung at school assembly:

لب پہ آتی ہے دعا بن کے تمنا میری
زندگی شمع کی صورت ہو خدایا میری

*My longing comes to my lips as a prayer: O God, may my life be like a candle, that it may give light to the world.*

From the same early collection comes the anthem that first made his name a household word, *Tarana-e-Hindi*:

سارے جہاں سے اچھا ہندوستاں ہمارا
ہم بلبلیں ہیں اس کی، یہ گلستاں ہمارا

*Better than the entire world is our land; we are its nightingales, and it is our garden.*

And in one famous *ghazal*, a short lyric poem, from *Bang-e-Dara*, the longing that runs through all his work, the yearning of the servant for the vision of God, finds one of its most quoted forms:

کبھی اے حقیقتِ منتظر نظر آ لباسِ مجاز میں
کہ ہزاروں سجدے تڑپ رہے ہیں مری جبینِ نیاز میں

*O awaited Reality, appear but once in the garb of the material world; for a thousand prostrations writhe in longing upon my brow of supplication.*

Over time his vision widened from the love of the homeland to the love of the whole Muslim community, and finally to a summons addressed to all humanity before God. In *Bang-e-Dara* the long and daring poem *Shikwa* ("The Complaint") gives voice to a believer's plaintive reproach to God at the fallen state of the Muslims, and its companion *Jawab-e-Shikwa* ("The Answer to the Complaint") supplies the divine reply: not consolation but a challenge, calling the community to make itself worthy of its inheritance once more. Recited from public platforms, these poems electrified their hearers and remain among the most famous in the language. The same summons rings through *Tulu-e-Islam* ("The Dawn of Islam"), whose lines became a kind of watchword for Muslim revival:

یقیں محکم، عمل پیہم، محبت فاتحِ عالم
جہادِ زندگانی میں ہیں یہ مردوں کی شمشیریں

*Firm certainty, ceaseless action, and a love that conquers the world: these are the swords of true men in the struggle of life.*

In poems such as *Khizr-e-Rah* ("Khizr, the Guide of the Way") he turned the same searching gaze on the poverty and bondage of his age, on labour and capital and empire, insisting that the message of faith was also a demand for justice in the world of men. Old figures served him too. Farhad, the luckless lover, became in his work, as in other modern progressive writing, the representative of the oppressed working class, while Sultan Mahmud, the king transformed into the ideal lover who finally became the slave of his slave, remained a theme of his poetry.

If any single image belongs to Iqbal, it is the *shaheen*, the eagle or royal falcon: his emblem of the free and austere spirit that relies on itself, scorns comfort, keeps no store and makes its home upon the heights. Against every temptation to ease and every limit set upon human aspiration, he summoned the young especially to a life of soaring effort:

نہیں تیرا نشیمن قصرِ سلطانی کے گنبد پر
تو شاہیں ہے، بسیرا کر پہاڑوں کی چٹانوں میں

*Your nest is not on the dome of the royal palace; you are an eagle: make your dwelling in the crags of the mountains.*

Another couplet carries the same summons further still:

ستاروں سے آگے جہاں اور بھی ہیں
ابھی عشق کے امتحاں اور بھی ہیں

*Beyond the stars there are yet other worlds; there are still more trials of love to be met.*

Nowhere is his union of art and thought more complete than in *Masjid-e-Qurtuba* ("The Mosque of Córdoba"), composed after he visited the great mosque in Spain: a sustained meditation on time, love and the deathless work of the man of God, widely held to be among the finest poems in the Urdu language. Another of his emblems is the tulip, *lāla*, the independent, vigorous plant that appears in the middle of the desert, resembling in its shape and colour the flames that Moses saw on Mount Sinai. Set against the traditional rose that lives in carefully trimmed gardens, it becomes a symbol of man's striving against heavy odds to realise himself.

Iqbal praised the Prophet Muhammad most eloquently, although he never wrote a formal *naʿt*, a poem in praise of the Prophet, in the traditional style. Even his early Urdu poems, such as *Jawab-e-Shikwa*, contain verses in praise of the Prophet, and there he sang: *Light the world, too long in darkness, / With Muhammad's radiant name.* A few years later, in the *Asrar-e-Khudi* of 1915, his first revolutionary Persian *mathnawi*, a long poem in rhyming couplets, he devoted some of his finest lines to Muhammad and claimed: *All our glory is from the name of Muhammad.* In the *Rumuz-e-Bekhudi*, the collection that turns his social and political ideals into Persian verse, the Prophet appears as the lamp in the darkness of existence.

His last verses are devoted to the Hijaz, the country he never visited, and among them stands the line *For us Mustafa is enough*, calling the Prophet by his title Mustafa, the Chosen One. In the *Javid Nama* he had gone further still, in the daring line *You can deny God, but you cannot deny the Prophet!* For Iqbal the Prophet was a leader in this world as well as the next, the political leader able to guide people towards their destiny even here, a view reflected in the complaint of Abu Jahl, one of the Prophet's grimmest enemies, who defends narrow patriotism and the distinction of races and castes. In a passage of the *Javid Nama* he defines the Prophet's state as more than human, in a poem he puts in the mouth of the mystic Hallaj, whose hymn in honour of the Prophet forms the beginning of the mystics' teaching on the Prophet, a poem turning on the word *ʿabduhu*, His slave. In Iqbal's thought Muhammad is both the political leader of his community and the scent of the mystic rose.

In 1936, hoping to be cured of an ailment, he wrote a poem in praise of the Prophet, the Arabian friend, as he called him like many Indian Muslim poets before him. He tells in a letter that he had been advised to write it by a dream apparition of Sir Sayyid Ahmad Khan, an episode that recalls the healing quality remembered of al-Būṣīrī's *Burda*, the poem in praise of the Prophet whose copies were still being sold in the shops of Lahore in the 1970s.

The *Javid Nama*, printed in 1932, takes the form of the heavenly journey made in the company of a spiritual preceptor, in Iqbal's case the spirit of Rumi, who leads the poet through the spheres and into the presence of the divine as Virgil led Dante. The poem is built from the old materials of the mystics. Imitating the title of Hallaj's *Tawasin*, Iqbal invented the *tawasin* of the Prophets, the dwelling places of the prophetic spirits in the Sphere of the Moon, and a section of the poem bears the title "Tāsīn of Muhammad". He took over Rumi's story of the Zoroastrian, where it serves to assert the spiritual strength of the Islamic religion and of true Muslims. And in the poem's Heaven of Jupiter he treated Hallaj as a sort of medieval forerunner of himself, holding up his dynamic idea of love and faith as an ideal for every free Muslim.

## The Thinker

At the centre of Iqbal's thought lies the idea of *khudi*, selfhood, or the realisation of the ego, which he set out first in the Persian *Asrar-e-Khudi* and its companion *Rumuz-e-Bekhudi*. By *khudi* he meant not selfishness but the awakening, disciplining and strengthening of the individual self through love, action and nearness to God, until the human being becomes a conscious fellow worker in the unfolding of creation. Against what he saw as the passivity and weariness of a decayed mysticism, a Sufism that counselled the annihilation of the self, and against the soulless materialism he had observed in the modern West, he preached striving, mastery of the self and the forging of the *mard-e-momin*, the complete believer who unites inward faith with outward power. He cast much of his deepest thought in Persian so that it might address the entire Muslim world. His most famous couplet fixes the height to which he believed the self could rise:

خودی کو کر بلند اتنا کہ ہر تقدیر سے پہلے
خدا بندے سے خود پوچھے، بتا تیری رضا کیا ہے

*Raise your selfhood so high that, before issuing each decree, God Himself asks His servant: 'Tell me, what is it that you wish?'*

And of that complete believer, in whom the human will becomes an instrument of the divine, he wrote in *Bal-e-Jibril*:

ہاتھ ہے اللہ کا بندۂ مومن کا ہاتھ
غالب و کار آفریں، کار کشا، کار ساز

*The hand of the true believer is the hand of God: triumphant and creative, resolving all difficulty, accomplishing all things.*

The doctrine draws on the older vocabulary of the mystics. Iqbal never tired of describing the long way of development from a drop to the ocean, from sperm to man, and eventually to the Perfect Man, the crown and apex of creation. Shabistari's *Gulshan-i raz*, in its form and content, inspired his *Gulshan-i raz-i jadid*, "The New Rose Garden of Mystery", published in 1927 and appended to his Persian *Zabūr-i ʿAjam*; in it he follows Shabistari's notions and offers his own answers to the questions of the Perfect Man, one of his central ideas, of God, and of destiny. He held that the trust spoken of in the Qur'an is the possibility of individuation. And he spoke of a higher predestination, *jabr mahmud*, reached not simply by accepting a given situation but by actively conforming with God's will through love and prayer. He claimed that he would call a people to spiritual resurrection, and in this he took on, in a sense, the role of the *qutb*, the axis or pole of the saints, the highest spiritual station. The three angelic figures Sarosh, Israfil and Gabriel occur very frequently in his poetry.

Iqbal set out his ideas not in verse alone but in searching prose. His English lectures, printed at Lahore in 1930 as *Six Lectures on the Reconstruction of Religious Thought in Islam* and many times since as *The Reconstruction of Religious Thought in Islam*, remain among the most ambitious modern attempts to rethink Islamic philosophy in dialogue with modern science, evolutionary biology and Western metaphysics. In them he argued for the reopening of *ijtihad*, independent reasoning in the interpretation of Islamic law, so that the community might meet the demands of a changing world without losing its spiritual core, and he reflected on the nature of the self, of time, of prayer and of religious experience. It was these bold arguments that drew the censure of some traditional scholars in his lifetime, and the book continues to be studied, taught and debated wherever the future of Islamic thought is discussed.

In the fifth chapter of the lectures he follows the saints of medieval Islam in drawing the difference between mystical and prophetic religion, and he found that difference expressed in a nutshell in a remark of ʿAbdul Quddūs Gangohī about the Prophet's ascension: the Prophet returned in order to work in the world, while the true mystic would never like to return. In his modern reading of mystical symbols he understood the Prophet's saying "I have a time with God in which no created being has access, not even Gabriel who is pure spirit" as a reference to the moment at which serial time, "the infidel's girdle", is torn and the mystic establishes direct contact with God in an encounter of person with person.

His thought was forged in a lifelong dialogue between two worlds. His spiritual master across the centuries was Jalaluddin Rumi, the thirteenth century Persian mystic, whom he took as his guide in the fullest sense, and from whom he drew his conviction that love, *ishq*, is the engine of the self and of the cosmos, a force higher than mere intellect. He read Rumi's "I died as mineral" passage as a statement of evolution, a reading that reached him through the Indian scholar Maulana Shibli's Urdu biography of Rumi of 1903, which deeply influenced his ideas, and he gave the idea of constant development a new interpretation in his poetry. He inserted many of Rumi's lines into his own works, chiefly into his *mathnawis*, which he wrote in the same metre as Rumi's *Mathnawi*. Rumi's contrast of Intellect and Love, in which Intellect still looks for a camel to make the pilgrimage when Love has already reached the hill of Safa, he took up in his lyrical poetry, above all in the *Payam-e-Mashriq*. There too stands his poem contrasting Avicenna and Rumi, with the bookworm that dwells in Avicenna's pages and so lacks real experience of life and love. He admitted, too, his indebtedness to Bedil's poetry for the formation of his thought.

Yet Iqbal had also mastered European philosophy at first hand, and much of his originality lies in how he answered it. He admired the dynamism of Western thought, its science, its energy, its worship of will and action, while rejecting what he saw as its spiritual bankruptcy. Nietzsche's vision of the *Übermensch*, the superman who creates his own values in a world without God, fascinated and repelled him. Iqbal's own ideal, the *mard-e-momin*, is a figure of immense will, but one whose power is surrendered to God and disciplined by love, not a lonely idol of the self, and his idea of the Perfect Man, the one who has developed his individual possibilities to the fullest bloom, is not derived from the Nietzschean superman. From Bergson he took a vivid sense of time as living duration and of the universe as an unfinished, creative becoming, which he wove into his dynamic understanding of *khudi*. He turned a fierce moral gaze on the political forms of the modern West, on a narrow territorial nationalism that set brother against brother and on an imperial capitalism that ground down the poor and the colonised. His counsel to the East was neither to imitate Europe nor to shun it, but to take its fire while keeping its own soul, marrying action to faith and knowledge to love, so that the reawakened Muslim might be at once modern and whole.

He quarrelled with the Sufism of his own day. He attacked what he called pirism: the absolute sway of the spiritual master, the *pir*, over his followers, and the exploitation of ignorant peasants and villagers that went with it. He counted it one of the most dangerous aspects of Islam, a wall that closes off large groups of Muslims from a new and vital interpretation of Islamic values, and more than once he attacked the backwardness of those who passed for spiritual leaders in the subcontinent. He reacted sharply, too, to the poetry of *wahdat al-wujud*, the unity of being, with its imagery of indifference to ritual and its sweeping generalisations that seemed to leave no room for truly Islamic values, and, taking over Hali's verdict on mystical poetry, he claimed that mystical poetry after Hafiz might prove utterly dangerous for the morals of a people. Yet he admitted in the *Javid Nama*: *If the purpose of poetry is to form men, / Poetry is the heir of prophecy.*

The attack has its counterweight, for he himself followed the Sufi thought of the classical period quite closely. As a thinker he turned from the pantheism of Hegel and of Ibn ʿArabi to what seemed to him true Islam, and in fact relied heavily upon the heritage of the great masters of early Sufism. Much as he detested the enthralling, otherworldly mysticism he thought was hidden behind Hafiz's beautiful verses, he used the whole fabric of Sufi imagery in his own poetry, though he often reinterpreted it. Like Bedil, he criticised Sufi gluttony, making a fake *murid*, a pledged disciple, explain that God, though closer than the jugular vein, as the Qur'an has it, is not closer to man than his stomach.

Hallaj stayed with him from his youth onward. In his youth Iqbal described the great mystic as a pantheist, as he had seen him through hundreds of Persian, Urdu and Panjabi poems, but he later recognised Hallaj's strong personal religious commitment and saw in him one of the few who had attained to an experience of the divine higher than that of ordinary people: a man who had called the slumbering Muslims to a personal realisation of the truth, and so came into conflict with religious authorities who were afraid of any ardent witness for the living God. He came to know Hallaj's work through Louis Massignon's books. A former critic of Hallaj, he finally understood his cry *ana al-haqq*, "I am the Truth", as the Infinite entering into the loving embrace of the Finite.

The many aspects of Satan that the Sufis developed over the centuries live on in his work, in a picture of Satan of many colours. He sees in him the lover as well as the intellectual, the monotheist, and the spirit of evil who longs to be overcome by the perfect man and to be broken in order to find salvation, so that in the end he will prostrate himself before man, who proves stronger than he. Iqbal also used the more common image of Satan the seducer, the materialist and the destroyer, and his Satan sadly complains that it is much too easy for him to seduce people.

Prayer and longing run through his work. For him true prayer is constant: it cannot be limited to a number of *rak'as*, the cycles of ritual prayer, and portions of the Qur'an, but permeates man's whole being, an idea in which he follows Rumi. He often speaks of the miraculous power of true prayer, which ends in a complete conformity of the human to the divine will. He interpreted the song of the nightingale through his philosophy of unfulfilled union and longing: only longing gives the soul bird the capacity to sing. In his thought, longing is the highest state the soul can reach, for it results in creativity, whereas union brings about silence and annihilation.

For all his reservations about the legacy of institutional Sufism, which he judged medieval and opposed to progress, his poet's heart produced a *ghazal* in praise of the paragon of Chishti spirituality, Shaykh Nizam ad-Din Awliya': "Iltija-ye Musafir", "A Pilgrim's Plea". Two of its lines, in the English of Shantanu Phukan, turn on the circling of a grave: "A step around your grave's a step into the life within", and, of a saint the poem does not name, "Grant that I step, just once, around his tomb."

## The Idea of Pakistan

Iqbal's enduring place in the history of the subcontinent rests, finally, on his political vision. In his presidential address to the annual session of the All-India Muslim League at Allahabad in 1930, he argued that the Muslims of the northwest of India, of the Punjab, the North-West Frontier, Sindh and Baluchistan, formed a distinct nation with its own culture, law and moral order, and that their destiny would be best secured in a consolidated Muslim state, governing itself, within or without the British Empire. The address is remembered as the intellectual seed from which the demand for Pakistan would grow.

In the last years of his life Iqbal corresponded closely with Muhammad Ali Jinnah, the Quaid-e-Azam, or Great Leader, urging him to return from London to lead the Muslims of India and pressing upon him the case for a separate homeland; their letters are treasured as a founding document of the Pakistan Movement. He and Jinnah are said to have become involved in the Shaheed Ganj riots as they tried to calm the Muslims, a case that the Lahore High Court heard in the British era and again around 1957 and 1958. Iqbal's concern was never merely territorial: he insisted that a true Islamic polity must also be a just one, freeing the poor and the labouring from exploitation, and he saw the proposed state as a place where the ethical vision of his faith might be lived out.

He did not live to see it. He died in 1938, nine years before Pakistan came into being in August 1947, and before the Muslim League presented in Lahore, in March 1940, the Lahore Resolution, drafted by Sir Zaffarullah Khan. The new nation enshrined him as its ideological founder, one of the founding fathers of Pakistan, and its highest honours and its very image of itself are bound up with his name.

## The Tomb

Iqbal was laid to rest on a raised platform in the Hazuri Bagh, in the narrow but hallowed ground between the steps of the Badshahi Mosque and the wall of the Lahore Fort, a site of the utmost symbolic dignity at the ceremonial heart of Mughal Lahore. His mausoleum was raised in the years after his death and completed around 1951, its design a dignified fusion of Afghan with Moorish and Mughal styles. Built chiefly of red sandstone, it is grave and elegant rather than grand. The fine marble within and the two headstones of lapis lazuli are traditionally said to have been gifts from Afghanistan, whose rulers claimed a special reverence for the poet who had sung of the Muslim world beyond India's borders, and verses of Iqbal's own composition are inscribed inside.

The tomb is a protected national monument, maintained by the Government of Punjab, and a ceremonial guard furnished by the Pakistan Rangers is posted at its entrance; the changing of the guard is itself a small daily ceremony that visitors gather to watch. Its placement is deliberate and eloquent: the poet of Islamic revival rests for ever between the greatest mosque and the greatest fort of the old Mughal capital, and close by the Minar-e-Pakistan, raised to mark the demand for the country he imagined.

Iqbal has a second memorial. By the 1970s a *maqam* of Muhammad Iqbal, a place bearing his name, had been raised in the garden of Rumi's tomb at Konya, so that the poet who took Rumi as his guide is remembered in the garden of his master's tomb as well as in his own city.

## Homage and Remembrance

Hemmed in by the Badshahi Mosque, the Lahore Fort and the Minar-e-Pakistan, the mausoleum lies within the single most visited heritage precinct in the country. It draws a great and constant stream of Pakistani and foreign visitors, some forty to fifty thousand a day, and on national days and the poet's anniversaries the numbers swell towards a hundred thousand. Many come first to see the mosque, the fort and the gardens and turn aside to recite the Fatiha at Iqbal's grave; others come expressly to pay homage.

On Pakistan's national days and on Iqbal's death anniversary, floral wreaths and a ceremonial *chadar*, the sheet of cloth laid over a grave, are placed upon the grave, as they are on religious and celebratory occasions through the year, and the Army mounts a parade, rendering a formal guard of honour and a ceremonial salute, the *salami*. Because Iqbal was at once a devout Muslim thinker and a poet of vast popular reach, his tomb has gathered a quiet devotional aura not unlike that of the older shrines of the city: visitors recite the Fatiha there as at a *darbar*, a saint's shrine, and folk memory preserves stories of the poet's nearness to the saints.

Pakistan keeps Iqbal's memory as a kind of national trust. His death anniversary on 21 April, and above all Iqbal Day on his birthday, 9 November, are observed across the country with ceremonies, seminars and recitations of his verse. At the mausoleum, heads of state and visiting foreign dignitaries lay wreaths, and the site serves as a place of national remembrance as much as of private prayer. His poetry is set to music and sung, though in Pakistan *qawwali*, the devotional music of the Sufis, became the one musical genre with a clear Muslim identity, overshadowing even the sung poetry of Iqbal. His lines are quoted by leaders and inscribed on public buildings, his works are taught in every school, and his portrait travels with Jinnah's on the country's everyday surfaces, from a Pakistani flag at the Kallar Kahar cable car, with the two men on its green section, to an office wall at a school in Makhdoom Pur Pahuran.

## Legends

Among the folk stories of Iqbal's nearness to the saints, one is told on the word of his faithful servant Ali Baksh. One night, it is said, the poet bade him fetch *lassi*, a yoghurt drink, for guests who were coming, though it was the dead of night and the bazaar was shut. Setting out, the servant found the lane bathed in an unearthly light and an old man with a luminous face selling lassi where no shop had ever stood, who took no payment, saying, "I have an old account with Iqbal." When Ali Baksh later pressed his master to explain, Iqbal is said to have revealed, under a promise of secrecy kept until the poet's death, that the guests had been Khwaja Moinuddin Chishti of Ajmer, and the seller of lassi none other than Data Ganj Bakhsh, Ali Hujwiri, the patron saint of Lahore.

Hujwiri died in Lahore in 1071, and his shrine, called that of Data Ganj Bakhsh, is still a popular place of pilgrimage in the city. Such stories, whatever the historian may make of them, bind the modern thinker to the ancient sacred landscape of Lahore, and they are part of why ordinary people cherish his memory as they do that of a saint.

Another story of the poet among the saints is told by Ghulam Muhammad, a dervish at a crow shrine in Gujrat. Allama Iqbal, he says, once came to see the saint there, and one of the saint's devotees told him that the Allama wished to speak to him. "All of them are Allamas for me," the saint replied, meaning his crows.

## The Setting

The Roshani Gate, one of the thirteen gateways that lead into the ancient walled city of Lahore, stands between the Alamgiri Gate and the Royal Mosque; lamps were lit all along its passageway, hence its name, the gate of lights. Behind it is the Heera-Mandi bazaar, the area once reserved for the city's prostitutes, before they dispersed all over Lahore after a crackdown on them during the Islamist government of General Zia-ul-Haq. The musicians remain: it is still one of the largest markets for traditional instruments in the city, and the area is famous too for the food street that runs parallel to the wall of the Badshahi Masjid.

The River Ravi, which once flowed next to the fort, has changed its course and moved a few kilometres away. The Parsi community buries its dead in a graveyard next to the Minar-e-Pakistan, facing the ancient walled city. The Lahore Fort was a fort where only royalty stayed, and at the end of Sikh rule, with the British annexation of the Punjab, the troops left the Badshahi Mosque.

Lahore itself became the first centre in the subcontinent of a Muslim culture inspired by Persia, and it was in this city that Hujwiri composed his famous Persian treatise on Sufism; his tomb still provides a place of pilgrimage for the people of the Punjab.

## Legacy

More than a grave, Mazar-e-Iqbal is a meeting place of literature, faith and national identity. It honours a man who reawakened a people through poetry, who dared to rethink his religious tradition for the modern age, and who imagined into being a country he would never see. His verse is recited from schoolrooms to state ceremonies; his philosophy of *khudi* remains a living argument about the self, freedom and faith; his image of the *shaheen* still summons the young to a life of striving. Whole institutions and an entire field of scholarship, *Iqbaliyat*, the study of Iqbal, are devoted to his thought.

His poetry has been translated into many languages, carrying the Poet of the East to readers far beyond the subcontinent. Reynold A. Nicholson translated the *Asrar-e-Khudi* as *The Secrets of the Self*. The *Javid Nama* was translated into English by Shaikh Mahmud Ahmad (Lahore, 1961) and by A. J. Arberry (London, 1966), into Italian by Alessandro Bausani in 1955 and into French by Eva Meyerovitch in 1962, and Arberry also translated part of the *Zabūr-i ʿAjam* as *Persian Psalms* (Lahore, 1948). Annemarie Schimmel rendered the *Javid Nama* into German verse in 1957 and into Turkish prose in 1958.

Studies of his thought followed in several languages, among them Schimmel's *Gabriel's Wing: A Study into the Religious Ideas of Sir Muhammad Iqbal* (Leiden, 1963), Hafeez Malik's edited volume *Muhammad Iqbal: Poet-Philosopher of Pakistan* (New York, 1971), Rais Ahmad Jaʿfri's Urdu study of his love for the Prophet, *Iqbāl aur ʿishq-i rasūl* (Lahore, 1956), Bausani's articles on Satan in his work and on the *Gulshan-i raz-i jadid*, and Bürgel's "The Pious Rogue" (1979). His reach extended into the study of Sufism itself: Annemarie Schimmel's own interest in the eighteenth century mystic Khwaja Mir Dard (1721 to 1785) developed during her studies on Iqbal's work.

Because his verse gave the Pakistan Movement much of its emotional and moral vocabulary, his tomb is not a relic of the past but a living focus of the nation's understanding of itself, where the memory of the man and the idea of the country are renewed together. In the shadow of the Badshahi Mosque and within sight of the Minar-e-Pakistan, it endures as one of the places where Pakistan goes to remember who it is, and who it once hoped to become.
