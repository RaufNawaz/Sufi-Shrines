## Overview

Gurdwara Sacha Sauda stands at Farooqabad, the town formerly called Chuharkana, in the Sheikhupura district of the Punjab. A *gurdwara* is a Sikh house of worship, and this one commemorates one of the best loved stories of Guru Nanak's youth, the *sacha sauda* or "true bargain", in which the young Nanak spent the money his father had given him for trade on feeding hungry holy men. Sikhs tell the story to children and elders alike as the seed of their faith's social teaching, and the shrine that marks its setting is a cherished stop on the pilgrim road through Pakistani Punjab.

The gurdwara stands on an ancient mound, in a walled courtyard with a few trees under which, it is said, Nanak discoursed with the leader of the yogis. After Partition its congregation departed; it has since been renovated with the support of the Sikh congregation of the United Kingdom and opened to organised parties of Sikh pilgrims.

## The True Bargain

The story is told in more than one way, but its heart is the same. Tradition tells that when Nanak was, by the usual reckoning, about eighteen years old, his father, Mehta Kalu, entrusted him with a sum of money, remembered as twenty rupees, and sent him to the nearby market town of Chuharkana to strike a profitable deal, so that he might learn the trade of a merchant and settle to a worldly living. Nanak set out from the town that is now Nankana Sahib with his lifelong companion, Bhai Mardana.

On the road, outside Chuharkana, he came upon a company of holy men who had gone without food. Moved by their need, he spent the whole of his father's money on food for them, holding that no trade could be more honest or more profitable than the relief of the hungry. When he returned empty handed, the story goes, his father reproached him for squandering the money, and Nanak answered that he had made the truest bargain of all, a *sacha sauda*, for he had spent it in the service of God's creatures. The episode is counted among the most important events of Nanak's early life.

In the *janamsakhis*, the cycles of tales told of the Guru's life, the story is told at length. There the boy buys food in Chuharkana from the biggest wholesale grocer, enough to feed fifty starving holy men, with vegetables, fruit and firewood; he hires two cooks and two porters and feeds the holy men at a grove of trees. "Any dilution of my bargain will be a dilution of the truth", he says, and feeding them is "a truly selfless service". At home his father calls him a failure, but Rai Bular, under whom Mehta Kalu served as accountant, takes the boy's part: he has used the money to feed a whole band of famished holy men, a bargain "true in the eyes of God".

In the devotion of the Panth, the Sikh community, the episode is read as the first flowering of the principle that would become central to Sikhism: *langar*, the free communal kitchen where all are fed without distinction of caste or creed, and *seva*, selfless service as an act of worship. It is believed that the tradition of *langar* began with Guru Nanak, when he fed the poor holy men at Chuharkana.

## The Gurdwara

Long afterwards, Sikhs raised a gurdwara at the place associated with the story, built to commemorate the event, and it grew into a popular place of pilgrimage, drawing devotees from across the Punjab and beyond. It is one of the gurdwaras that commemorate the story, and it is held to stand at the spot where the Guru fed the holy men. With the Partition of 1947 the shrine passed into Pakistan, and its congregation departed.

When the historian Iqbal Qaiser first saw the gurdwara, in 1993, while researching his book, buffaloes were tied in the courtyard, and drug addicts and gamblers had occupied the rest of the space. A local man, whom Qaiser remembered only as Shah, tried to keep the place sacred and was beaten by the addicts and gamblers. Qaiser and his companion, Ilyas Ghuman, took him to hospital and filed a police report, and the matter reached a few national newspapers. The Department of Auqaf, the government department of religious endowments, then came and appointed Shah the official guard of the place.

The gurdwara was then renovated with the support of the Sikh *sangat*, or congregation, of the United Kingdom. The renovation is dated to 1993 and 1994, though accounts of its timing differ, and Qaiser himself has said that he played a major part in it. The renovation of this and a number of other gurdwaras was fiercely debated, and once the work on the gurdwara was done, a new road had to be made to connect it with Nankana Sahib. Built for Sikh pilgrims, the road takes only about twenty minutes; before it, pilgrims had to travel to Sheikhupura and then head on towards Chuharkana.

The gurdwara stands a little distance from the main road, within a protective wall topped with barbed wire. Through a portal lies a marbled courtyard with a white gurdwara at its centre. Beside it, within a small boundary, grow a few *waan* trees, the last remnants of the jungle that once grew here, and gurdwara, courtyard and trees stand on an ancient mound, the highest point in and around the town. It is said that it was under these trees that Nanak discoursed with the leader of the yogis.

A Muslim storyteller at Gurdwara Sachkand, another gurdwara of the town, said that the yogis Nanak fed had been meditating "on top of a mound. The mound is where the big gurdwara stands". He called Nanak "a true devotee of God", a rightly guided soul, and said that this had come down from his elders, who used to live with the Sikhs before Pakistan was made.

On one visit, made in the monsoon season during the month of Ramzan, the gurdwara was a splendid structure, recently renovated, but entry was not easy. Two security men sat inside the gate, with a policeman beside them, his gun resting on his shoulder; they were new, Qaiser said, and did not know him, and a boy at the gate would not let the visitors in until the guard had telephoned the caretaker, Boota Singh. Qaiser had always come before with a *jatha*, a group of Sikhs, and Boota Singh showed the visitors round. He was about forty years old, dressed in a green shalwar kameez with a green turban, and extremely shy; he came with his youngest son, a boy of about ten, and he had worked at the gurdwara for twelve years.

Inside was a wooden palanquin in which the Guru Granth Sahib, the Sikh scripture, was placed, with a couple of swords before it. Behind the gurdwara was a new *sukhasan*, the chamber where the scripture is laid to rest at night, carpeted and panelled in wood, with an air conditioner, switched off for want of electricity, and a carved wooden bed. Asked whether the bed had been made in Amritsar, Boota Singh answered: "It was made in Nankana Sahib."

## Devotion and Pilgrimage

The gurdwara has been opened, under arrangement with the Government of Pakistan and the Evacuee Trust Property Board, to organised parties of Sikh pilgrims, especially on the great anniversaries of Guru Nanak's life. It is one of the major shrines visited by the pilgrims who come to Nankana Sahib and Hassan Abdal.

On that visit in Ramzan, before the Guru Granth Sahib, Qaiser placed a ten rupee note, walked round the palanquin and fanned the scripture with a whisk, and his fellow visitor did the same. Leaving the *sukhasan*, Boota Singh walked out backwards, so that his back never faced the Guru Granth Sahib, and the visitors did likewise.

In Aurangzeb's time the followers of Baba Nanak, as the Guru was also called, counted the service of all who come and go as the greatest worship, and whoever arrived at midnight speaking Baba Nanak's name they treated as a brother and a friend and served, even a stranger, a thief or a highwayman.

## Farooqabad

Farooqabad is still a *mandi*, a market town. Its old name was Chuharkana, and it lies about 45 kilometres from Nankana Sahib in a straight line. The new name, it is said, comes from the 1980s. In the Zia era, when elections in the eighties were held without parties, a resident of the town, Farooq Dogar, was elected to the National Assembly. He supported the military dictator and was killed over a personal feud, and the town was renamed Farooqabad in his honour. The first part of the old name, *chuhr*, a Sanskrit word meaning to lead, is explained as the two bangles worn at the start and the end of a set of regular bangles.

Across the Punjab, Maharaja Ranjit Singh, who became its sovereign at the end of the eighteenth century, spent large sums on the renovation and expansion of many gurdwaras, and attached vast tracts of land to them so that their revenues could support their functioning.

## Guru Nanak

The dates of Guru Nanak, the founder of the Sikh religion, are most often given as 1469 to 1539. In the janamsakhi telling, his father was Kalyan Chand Das Bedi, or Mehta Kalu as he was affectionately called, accountant to the Bhatti family under Rai Bular. At thirteen the boy refused the sacred thread, the *janeu*, the beginning of a lifelong crusade against the performing of empty rites and rituals; at sixteen he herded cattle, and he became well acquainted with the dervish Sayyad Hussain and gained an intimate knowledge of the Sufi line of thought. After the true bargain he moved to Sultanpur as keeper of the stores of Nawab Daulat Khan. It is also said that under pressure from his family he became a trader and farmer.

He brought a message of peace and harmony to the believers of Islam and Hinduism, and taught that all men and women were equal, irrespective of caste, creed, wealth, social standing and gender, and that a good life rested on honest labour, compassion, selfless service and prayer. He associated with people of the lower castes, calling them people of God.

He is held to have disavowed the miraculous and to have abhorred the association of miracles with religious personalities; yet, as one writer has put it, "his biography is nothing but a story of his miracles". In Aurangzeb's time it was held that signs of unveiling, of miracles and of wonders beyond the ordinary had appeared from him in his childhood.

Accounts of how many journeys he made differ, but his *udasis*, the long journeys of his travelling years, are most often counted as four, and they lasted more than twenty years. He travelled mostly on foot, and whenever he visited an area he would visit its religious shrines, Muslim, Hindu and of any other denomination.

Trade runs through another of his legends too: a trader, it is said, told Nanak that his mules carried "Just some mud"; his sugar and *gur*, or jaggery, had turned into mud, and when he repented, the mud became sugar and gur once again.

## The Guru's Verse

His *bani*, devotional verses of supplication, entreaty, humility and reverence, was compiled as part of the Adi Granth, the first compilation of the Sikh scripture, by Guru Arjan Dev, the fifth Guru, in 1604. He composed verses in Hindi on the truth of God, setting out divine unity, and in Aurangzeb's time the worship of his followers centred on reading their master's verses, which they recited in song and melody. A minstrel named Mardana sang the Guru's verses and drew people into the snare of devotion.

Lines from his hymns, in English translation, give a sense of his poetry. On the oneness of God he sang:

ਸਾਹਿਬੁ ਮੇਰਾ ਏਕੋ ਹੈ ॥
ਏਕੋ ਹੈ ਭਾਈ ਏਕੋ ਹੈ ॥

*My Master is One / He is One, brother, the only One*

And again:

*God is One, / The Path is One*

His *aarti*, the hymn sung as lamps are waved in worship, begins in one translation:

ਗਗਨ ਮੈ ਥਾਲੁ ਰਵਿ ਚੰਦੁ ਦੀਪਕ ਬਨੇ …

*The sky the salver, the sun and moon the lamps …*

and in another:

*In the salver of the firmament, / The sun and the moon shine as lamps*

His poem on the sacred thread runs:

ਦਇਆ ਕਪਾਹ
ਸੰਤੋਖੁ ਸੂਤੁ …

*From the cotton of compassion, / Spin the thread of contentment*

Of washing he sang:

ਭਰੀਐ ਹਥੁ ਪੈਰੁ ਤਨੁ ਦੇਹ ॥
ਪਾਣੀ ਧੋਤੈ ਉਤਰਸੁ ਖੇਹ ॥

*As hands or feet besmirched with slime, / Water washes white*

Other hymns of his open *O my merchant friend*, *As a beggar goes a-begging …*, *Launch ye the boat of austere contemplation …* and *His goodness cannot be priced or traded …*.

His verse is sung outside the gurdwaras too. The singer Abida Parvin has said: "I sing Shah Hussain, Bulle Shah, Mian Muhammad Bakhsh, Hazrat Khawaja Ghulam Farid, Sacchal Sarmast, Baba Farid, and Guru Nanak." In Sindh, Sain Vasan Shah became famous for reciting the *banis* of Shri Guru Nanak and the *kalams*, the poems, of the Sufis, and Guru Nanak has been called "the greatest poet of the Bhakti", the path of loving devotion.

## Legacy

Gurdwara Sacha Sauda enshrines a story that is, in miniature, the whole ethic of Sikhism: that true profit lies in generosity, and that the service of the needy is the service of the divine. From the boy's true bargain the tradition traces the institution of *langar*, which to this day feeds millions in gurdwaras around the world without charge or condition. For Sikh pilgrims travelling in Pakistan, the shrine is a living link to the compassion of their founder; for the wider tradition, it is the place where a lesson in kindness became the cornerstone of a faith.

The gurdwaras built later by devotees at the places where Nanak stayed or is said to have performed miracles still stand tall, though after Partition several gurdwaras and temples were taken over and destroyed by migrants.

Muslims, too, have claimed the Guru. Many Muslims acquainted with his teachings are devoted to him and visit his shrine. He is held to have been a spiritual follower of Baba Farid: he travelled to Pakpattan and collected the saint's poetry, and Baba Farid became an inspiration for him when he visited Farid's shrine on his travels. Farid's texts are revered among the Sikhs because, it is said, he influenced the founder of their religion. A Chishti Sabiri shaykh of the twentieth century counted Guru Nanak, with Krishna, Rama, Sita and Lachman, among the spiritual luminaries outside Islam who believed in the one true God and had a "Divine connection", *nisbat*. At the shrine of the goddess Hinglaj, engraved footsteps are held to be Guru Nanak's.
