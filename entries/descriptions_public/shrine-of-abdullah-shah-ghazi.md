## Overview

On a low hill in Clifton, overlooking the Arabian Sea in the heart of Karachi, stands the shrine of Hazrat Abdullah Shah Ghazi, the patron saint of the city. Tradition places him in the eighth century and holds him to be a descendant of the Prophet through the line of Imam Hasan. For the people of Karachi he is the guardian of the city, believed to shield it from storm and calamity, and his *darbar*, or shrine, crowned by a green dome, is among the most visited and most beloved sacred places in Pakistan's largest metropolis, a ceaseless resort of pilgrims of every kind.

## The Saint

The historical record of Abdullah Shah Ghazi is thin and wrapped in legend, but the tradition is strong and old. No year of his birth or death has come down, and no teacher and no order of the Sufis, the Muslim mystics, are remembered for him. He is held to be a Sayyid, one of the family of the Prophet Muhammad, by the usual account a descendant of Imam Hasan, who came to Sindh in the early Islamic centuries. Tradition sets his coming in the eighth century, the age when Islam was first spreading in the region through Muhammad bin Qasim and after.

Tradition holds that he came as a merchant, as a preacher and as a warrior, a *ghazi*. He is said to have settled by the sea near what became Karachi and to have died there, and some traditions hold that he was martyred.

## The Shrine

His grave on the Clifton hill became a place of blessing, and over the centuries it grew into the great shrine that now bears his name. As one writer on the folk religion of Pakistan put it, this is "the most famous shrine in the city".

Like other great shrines, it has been a target of violence, and it is counted among the Sufi shrines of Pakistan that have come under attack. In 2010 it was struck by a suicide bombing; the attack came on 6 October and killed eight people. The devotion of Karachi has never faltered, and the shrine, since rebuilt and expanded with its landmark green dome, remains thronged.

## Devotion and Festivals

Abdullah Shah Ghazi is woven into the very identity of Karachi. It is widely believed that his presence protects the coastal city from cyclones and disaster, and that the sea has never brought ruin upon Karachi because the saint keeps watch over it from his hill.

His shrine draws people from right across the city in great and constant numbers, rich and poor, of every sect and background. They come to recite the Fatiha, the opening chapter of the Quran, to tie vows, to distribute food, and to share in the *qawwali*, the devotional music of the Sufis, and the *dhamal*, the vigorous ritual dance performed at Sufi shrines in Punjab and Sindh. *Qawwali* and *dhamal* fill its evenings, above all on Thursdays and at the *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of the saint's death.

On one occasion a news anchor who styled himself a vigilante entered the shrine and secretly filmed people consuming hashish, then began harassing the caretaker for allowing such a practice. Under pressure, in front of the camera, the caretaker gave an apologetic response and promised to monitor the situation better in the future.

## The Setting

Muhammad bin Qasim, in whose age tradition sets the saint's coming, began his advance through Makran and Baluchistan in 710, and conquered Sindh between 711 and 712. The early Arab conquerors settled their families in large numbers in the various towns of Sindh, though in that early period Sindh is not known to have seen the spread of Sufism found further north and west. The Sufi mystical traditions came to the subcontinent through Sindh, and after the conquest a long line of Sufi mystics came to the region through Baluchistan, Makran, Multan and Sindh itself.

A large part of Clifton is built on reclaimed land. Of the city's name, it is said that the area was once known as the area of Mai Kolachi, after an old woman known by that name whose son was killed by a fish; she tore the fish apart and retrieved him, and the city came to be known as Kolachi. At the time of Partition Karachi was a minor port city, and migrants from India made it the largest city of Pakistan.

Karachi is one of two important urban centres of Sufism, the other being Hyderabad in India; in both, members of the same lineage of qawwali musicians, the Qawwal Bachche, actively maintain their special qawwali heritage. The city's shrines are also a stage in the round of the *fakirs*, wandering Sufis: after the annual fair of Sehwan, which lasts three days, fakirs follow other fakirs and go on to shrines in Karachi.

## Legacy

Abdullah Shah Ghazi is the spiritual guardian of Karachi: a saint whom tradition places in the earliest Islamic centuries, whose shrine on the Clifton hill has become the sacred heart of a modern megacity of many millions. In him a coastal metropolis of migrants and strangers finds a common patron and a place of refuge, and his *darbar* remains one of the most vibrant centres of popular devotion in Pakistan.
