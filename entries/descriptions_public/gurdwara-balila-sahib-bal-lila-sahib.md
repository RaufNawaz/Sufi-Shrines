## Overview

Gurdwara Bal Lila Sahib, also called Gurdwara Balila, stands at Nankana Sahib in the Punjab, some three hundred metres to the south east of Gurdwara Janam Asthan, the birthplace of Guru Nanak, the founder of the Sikh faith. Its name, *Bal Lila*, "the play of childhood", marks the open field where, according to Sikh tradition, the child Nanak gathered his companions and passed the days of his boyhood. It is among the cluster of shrines that make Nankana Sahib, the Guru's birth town, the foremost place of Sikh pilgrimage in Pakistan, and it keeps the memory not of a miracle or a martyrdom but of the childhood of the founder of the faith.

The gurdwara stands across the road from Gurdwara Patti Sahib. Tradition holds that the ground was once an open field where Guru Nanak used to play as a child, and the gurdwara was built to mark the spot. Beside it lies a *sarovar*, a holy tank, called Nanaksar, whose waters pilgrims hold sacred.

## The Guru

In the Guru's day Nankana Sahib was the village of Rai Bhoi di Talwandi, held by Rai Bular Bhatti, the local landlord. Nanak was born there in 1469, though accounts of the date differ, and he spent his early years there. Accounts of his birthplace have not always agreed, but it is generally held to be Talwandi. The village was renamed Nankana Sahib in honour of the Guru, and because he spent his early years there it has several gurdwaras marking events from his childhood, all of which the pilgrims visit.

His father was Kalyan Chand Das Bedi, or Mehta Kalu as he was affectionately called, the accountant of the Bhatti family under Rai Bular, and his mother was Mata Tripta. Nanak belonged to the Bedi caste. Tradition tells that Mata Tripta had no child for a long time after her marriage, and that she would regularly visit an ancient Hindu temple on top of a mound not far from Talwandi to pray for a child; accounts of how his birth came about differ.

The *janamsakhis*, the traditional accounts of the Guru's life written by his devotees after his death, dwell fondly on his boyhood. They portray a child unlike his fellows: drawn to contemplation, indifferent to games for their own sake, and given to gathering the other children around him not merely to play but to sit in stillness and turn their minds towards the divine Name. Tradition holds that the field of Bal Lila was the scene of these gatherings, and there the young Nanak is remembered as a leader among his playmates and as a soul already absorbed in God. These stories fixed this patch of ground in the sacred geography of the *Panth*, the Sikh community, and a shrine rose to honour it.

In his early years, it is said, signs of spiritual unveiling and of wonders beyond the ordinary course of things appeared in him. The janamsakhis tell that the village astrologer foretold greatness for the child, and that at school he showed a brilliance never before seen in any child in Talwandi. At thirteen, in the same stories, he refused the *janeu*, the sacred thread, in his father's courtyard, saying that "it is our actions and our actions alone" that give a man his position among men. At sixteen, the stories go on, he herded cattle. They tell, too, that he became well acquainted with the dervish Sayyad Hussain and gained an intimate knowledge of the Sufi line of thought; accounts of the dervish's name differ.

Talwandi lay in the path of numerous mendicants, holy men and *sadhus*, as Hindu ascetics are called, who camped in a grove of trees on the outskirts of the village. It is told that when Nanak turned eighteen his father asked him to take up a trade and gave him twenty rupees, and that Nanak left the town with Bhai Mardana. The tale is remembered as the *Sacha Sauda*, the "true bargain".

From Talwandi he went to Sultanpur, around sixty kilometres away, as keeper of the stores of Nawab Daulat Khan. There, the janamsakhis tell, he vanished in the stream for a week and came out saying, "There is only one God. There is no Hindu, no Mussalman." He married Sulakhini, daughter of Mul Chand and Chando Rani of the town of Batala, on 24 September 1487, though accounts of the marriage differ.

He set out on long journeys, the *udasis*. Accounts differ on how many journeys he made, but together they lasted more than twenty years, and he travelled mostly on foot. Wherever he went he visited the religious shrines of the place, Muslim, Hindu or of any other denomination, and people came from every land in great numbers to become his disciples.

He came home between his journeys. The janamsakhis tell that when Nanak and Mardana returned to Talwandi in 1506, Mata Tripta felt great joy, and Mehta Kalu was a little in awe of his son. In the same stories, after Pak Pattan, he travelled back to Talwandi again; it was no longer an obscure little village but had become a place of pilgrimage, and he sat by Rai Bular, who was sick and failing.

Born into a Hindu household and educated in a Muslim milieu, he was neither a practising Muslim nor a Hindu, and his advice to his companions was that "if one is a Muslim then one should be a good Muslim and if one is a Hindu then one should be a good Hindu". He lived as a householder. Nanak himself is held to have abhorred the association of miracles with religious personalities, though the stories told of his life are full of them.

He spent a major portion of his life in what is today Pakistan: he was born there, and he eventually settled in a small village on the Pakistani side of the border. On his deathbed he appointed not his son but his most loyal student as his spiritual successor. His son Lakhmi Das, it was said, was worthy, but the Guru passed him over, and at the hour of his death he made Lehna his successor, with the title Guru Angad. Guru Nanak died on 22 September 1539, though accounts of his death differ.

## The Gurdwara

Tradition ascribes the original excavation of the tank, together with an early shrine, to Rai Bular, the Guru's own patron, who is remembered in Sikh devotion for the reverence he showed the boy Nanak. The present building is traditionally associated with the middle of the eighteenth century and the munificence of Diwan Kaura Mal, the influential minister of that era. In the early nineteenth century Maharaja Ranjit Singh is said to have had the shrine renovated, the tank enlarged and properly lined with masonry, and generous grants of land settled upon it for its upkeep.

As sovereign of the Punjab, Ranjit Singh spent large amounts of money on the renovation and expansion of many gurdwaras all over the province, and he attached vast tracts of land to them so that the revenues from the lands could support their running.

The shrines of Nankana Sahib were caught up in the great struggle of the early twentieth century to reform the management of the gurdwaras, which had passed into the hands of hereditary custodians known as *mahants*. Across the Punjab, as a result of sectarian divisions among the Sikh communities and the British takeover of the province, a large number of important Sikh gurdwaras were being administered by Hindu *mahants*. In February 1921 a peaceful body of reformers was massacred at Gurdwara Janam Asthan, in one of the defining episodes of the Gurdwara Reform Movement. In the aftermath, tradition records, the *mahants* of Bal Lila Sahib chose to hand over the shrine voluntarily to the Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee, the body that thereafter managed the historic gurdwaras, in exchange for a maintenance allowance for their families.

Since the Partition of 1947 the gurdwara, like its neighbours at Nankana Sahib, has stood in a land where few Sikhs remain, cared for by the Evacuee Trust Property Board and thrown open to the pilgrims who cross the border in their thousands each year. Kartar, whose family was one of the first to move to Nankana Sahib, remembered that no Sikh was left there after Partition and that the town's gurdwaras became barren and fell into a bad state. In the 1960s Giani Pratap came with a party of pilgrims from India and, seeing the dilapidated condition of the shrines, decided to stay and look after these holy buildings.

A Sikh community has returned to the town. Its people are not natives of the city but recent migrants from Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, where there is a considerable Sikh population. Families came following the alienation after the wars of 1965 and 1971 and the demolition of the Babri Mosque in 1992, and within about ten years the community grew to about two hundred households, though accounts of its size differ. Most of the newcomers were housed within the gurdwaras, in rooms generally reserved for pilgrims. Surjeet and Udam, whose families had moved to Nankana a few years before, spoke Urdu with a Pashto accent. Surjeet spoke of a cricket tournament arranged for the first time for the birthday of Guru Gobind Singh, and at one time he explained that the gurdwara was under renovation.

Several people at Nankana Sahib felt that the PSGPC, the Pakistani committee, was not representative of the Sikh community in Pakistan. Most of the town's Muslims had migrated from the other side of the border after Partition, and their attitudes are said to have been shaped by those experiences.

The town's gurdwaras have also known politics. Before the PSGPC existed, the staff of the Indian High Commission in Islamabad received the pilgrims at the border and arranged their stay inside the gurdwaras. At the Guru's birthday in November 1985, a year after Operation Blue Star, an American Sikh spoke for a separate Sikh state at one of the town's gurdwaras. During the speech the gate was closed, and two officials, Counsellor B. Jain and First Secretary K. K. Khanna, were attacked. The religious festivals at Nankana and in other cities of Pakistan went back to being religious festivals.

## Devotion and Festivals

The gurdwara is remembered for children's games. At Lohri, sports competitions are arranged for children within its premises, though accounts differ on how long and how regularly the custom has been kept.

Lohri is an ancient festival of the Punjab that commemorates the change of season. It is celebrated on the 13th of January, generally the last night of the month of Poh, with a village bonfire, dry fruit and folk songs; the children collect the wood, and *kheer*, a rice pudding, is left to cool overnight. After the creation of Pakistan this indigenous festival of the Punjab died down, and at Nankana it was being revived by a group of Pathan men living in the Punjab.

Most of the town's gurdwaras remained locked all year round, to be opened only at the time of the festival, and security guards were employed at the entrance to all of them. The great festival is the Guru's birthday, called Guru Nanak Jayanti or *Gurpurab*, and thousands of people come to Nankana Sahib for it from all over the world. Official estimates put their number at ten to fifteen thousand every year; in 2011, when the festival ran from 8 to 10 November, media reports counted only three thousand.

That year police stood in force at all the entrances to the city, no car was permitted inside, and devotees walked barefoot. Pilgrims from Delhi, Gujarat and Amritsar came by train and crossed the border at Wagah, where representatives of the Evacuee Trust Property Board and the PSGPC received them and a *langar*, the free communal kitchen, was arranged, and they stayed three days at Nankana. Three days of *Akhand Path*, the unbroken reading of the scripture, ended in the *bhog*, the closing rite, followed by the *Nagar Kirtan*, the procession with hymns. Richer pilgrims from Britain, America and Canada came by chartered plane and hired buses, and the posters of travel companies promised them "all" the gurdwaras in Pakistan. For those few days the whole city looked like a little India.

In Aurangzeb's reign the sum of the worship of Baba Nanak's followers, as the Guru was also called, was the reading of their master's verses, which they sang to melody. They counted service to those who come and go, the traveller and the guest, as the highest worship, and whoever arrived at midnight speaking Baba Nanak's name they treated as a brother and a friend and served, even a stranger, a thief, a highwayman or an evildoer. The tradition of *langar* is believed to have begun with Guru Nanak.

The Guru's festival is kept far from Nankana too: at Manora, Jeeta Singh held a small festival every November, when the festival of Guru Nanak is celebrated at Nankana Sahib. At one of the shrines of Guru Nanak at Nankana Sahib, a picture of Baba Farid accompanied the picture of the Guru.

## The Guru's Verse

Guru Nanak's devotional verses are known as *bani*, verses of supplication, entreaty, humility and reverence. He composed verses in Hindi on the truth of God, setting out divine unity in clear words and shining metaphors, and a singer named Mardana sang his verses and drew people into the snare of devotion.

His poem on the sacred thread, the *janeu* that the stories say he refused as a boy, runs:

ਦਇਆ ਕਪਾਹ
ਸੰਤੋਖੁ ਸੂਤੁ …

*From the cotton of compassion, / Spin the thread of contentment*

On the oneness of God he sang:

ਸਾਹਿਬੁ ਮੇਰਾ ਏਕੋ ਹੈ ॥
ਏਕੋ ਹੈ ਭਾਈ ਏਕੋ ਹੈ ॥

*My Master is One / He is One, brother, the only One*

And again:

*God is One, / The Path is One*

The *aarti*, the hymn sung as lamps are waved in worship, begins:

ਗਗਨ ਮੈ ਥਾਲੁ
ਰਵਿ ਚੰਦੁ ਦੀਪਕ ਬਨੇ …

*In the salver of the firmament, / The sun and the moon shine as lamps*

Of washing he sang:

ਭਰੀਐ ਹਥੁ ਪੈਰੁ ਤਨੁ ਦੇਹ ॥
ਪਾਣੀ ਧੋਤੈ ਉਤਰਸੁ ਖੇਹ ॥

*As hands or feet besmirched with slime, / Water washes white*

And in another song:

ਬਗਾ ਬਗੇ ਕਪੜੇ ਤੀਰਥ ਮੰਝਿ ਵਸੰਨਿੑ ॥

*The herons in their white feathers dwell in the sacred shrines of pilgrimage*

A line of the *Babur Bani*, his verses on the invasion of Babur, dates the invaders' coming:

ਆਵਨਿ ਅਠਤਰੈ …

*Coming in seventy-eight (1521 C.E)*

The verse is sung beyond the gurdwaras. The singer Abida Parvin has said: "I sing Shah Hussain, Bulle Shah, Mian Muhammad Bakhsh, Hazrat Khawaja Ghulam Farid, Sacchal Sarmast, Baba Farid, and Guru Nanak." In Sindh, Sain Vasan Shah became famous for reciting the *banis* of Shri Guru Nanak and the *kalams*, the poems, of the Sufis.

## The Setting

Talwandi lay between the rivers Ravi and Chenab, directly in the path of the armies that had set their eyes on Delhi. It is told that the village was destroyed thirteen times and thirteen times rose again from its ashes, like the fabled phoenix, and that its last rebirth came under Rai Bhoe, a Bhatti Rajput who had converted to Islam. In the same telling, Rai Bhoe's son was Rai Bular, and Shiv Ram, head of a Bedi family, was accountant to Rai Bhoe and was succeeded by his son, Kalyan Chand Das Bedi. In the time of Sultan Bahlul Lodi, when Tatar Khan was governor of Lahore, Rai Ram Deo Bhatti took the whole of the Punjab in farm from him, that is, the right to its revenue for a sum of money.

Behind Gurdwara Janam Asthan, away from the hub of the new settlement, rests an archaeological mound. A settlement once stood there; it was destroyed, after which Rai Bhoi di Talwandi was built within a boundary wall, so that Nanak was born in the new town. The young Nanak is said to have spent a lot of time on the mound in conversation with the yogis, *sadhus* and dervishes who passed through Talwandi, and it is believed that he began to frequent it in the six or seven years between the *Sacha Sauda* and his move to Sultanpur. The climb was steep, with no proper path, to a top at least twenty metres from the ground, covered with shrubs of *karir*, a thorny desert bush, with shards of broken pottery and other mud utensils underfoot.

Nankana Sahib lies about forty miles south west of Lahore, and it is counted among the holiest cities in the world for the Sikhs, because the founder of their religion was born there. From a rooftop one could see the minarets of mosques, the small white domes of the gurdwaras, and the remains of a few Hindu temples, cone like structures rising from houses abandoned at the time of Partition.

One main road ran through the entire city; the buildings were old, most of them from the late British or the early Pakistan era, and the road had potholes. In 2005 Nankana Sahib was separated from Sheikhupura and made an independent district, with the city as its headquarters. The city had a population of over a million. Guru Nanak was celebrated all over Nankana Sahib: there were a Guru Nanak High School, a Guru Nanak grocery store and a Guru Nanak cloth shop, all run by Sikhs, while the town's Muslims generally refrained from honouring Guru Nanak in this way.

## Legacy

Where other shrines mark the Guru's teachings, his travels or the sacrifices of the martyrs, Bal Lila Sahib, with its field and its tank, commemorates that the founder of the faith was once a child at play on this ground, and that even then, in the telling of the Panth, the light within him was already shining. For the pilgrim who has bowed at the Janam Asthan, a visit to Bal Lila Sahib completes the geography of Guru Nanak's beginnings. Across Pakistan there are several sites where Nanak is said to have stayed or performed miracles, and the gurdwaras built there by his devotees still stand. In Aurangzeb's reign Baba Nanak was called the caravan leader on the paths of truth and the torch bearer of the mystic way.

Devotion to the Guru reaches beyond the Sikhs. Many Muslims acquainted with his teachings are devoted to him too and visit his shrine, and to Nadeem, a Muslim painter, Guru Nanak was like a Muslim Sufi saint. Baba Farid, it is said, became an inspiration for Guru Nanak when Nanak visited his shrine on his travels, and Farid's texts are revered among the Sikhs, for he is held to have influenced the founder of their religion. A twentieth century shaykh of the Chishti Sabiri Sufi order described Guru Nanak, with Krishna, Rama, Sita and Lachman, among the spiritual luminaries outside Islam, as a believer in the one true God and a religious leader with a "Divine connection", *nisbat*. There is a growing similarity between the iconography of Guru Nanak and that of Jhulelal, and at the shrine of the goddess Hinglaj there are engraved footsteps of Guru Nanak.
