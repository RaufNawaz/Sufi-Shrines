## Overview

Darbar Malik Ahmad Ayaz is the shrine of Malik Ahmad Ayaz, the slave and beloved of Sultan Mahmud Ghaznavi and governor of Lahore, in Lahore's crowded Shah Alam Market, near Darbar Ali Hajveri Ganj Bakhsh. Ayaz is remembered as the man who rebuilt Lahore after Mahmud's conquest, and as a servant so devoted that, in mystical poetry, his king became "the slave of his slave". The shrine is an active Muslim shrine, visited by people from distant places, non-Muslims among them, and its *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of his death, draws tens of thousands.

He is also known simply as Malik Ayaz. In the nineteenth century he was honoured among the saints as Shaikh Ayaz Lahori, the slave and beloved of Shah Mahmud Ghaznavi, and it was said of him that God had given him both outward and inward wealth. The shrine follows the Ahl e Sunnat, the Sunni tradition, and names its *silsila*, its chain of spiritual transmission, as Ghaznavi.

## The Life of Malik Ayaz

Accounts of where Ayaz came from differ. Tradition at the shrine holds that he was educated in Georgia in 993 AD, and that when his country was attacked he was enslaved and taken for sale to the slave market of Baghdad, slave dealing being then the norm. Too weak to attract buyers, he was passed over until Mahmud Ghaznavi came by and asked his price. When the king's minister questioned the purchase, Mahmud answered that what he saw in the boy the minister could not see. His words raised greed in the seller's heart, and the price asked was ten thousand dinars; Mahmud paid it and enrolled Ayaz among his soldiers.

Though the Sultan had many thousands of slaves, Ayaz was the foremost of them. Mahmud made him the intimate of his private assembly and the companion of his select gatherings, and preferred him above all his servants and retinue. Tradition holds that the relation of master and slave turned into love, and that Ayaz spent his whole life in devotion to Mahmud, while the Sultan in turn was caught in the noose of his love.

In the story of the melon, the king cut a melon with his own hand and fed it to Ayaz, then tasted it himself and found it bitter, and asked why Ayaz had said nothing. Ayaz replied that, having had sweetness from his master's hand all his life, he had no cause to complain of one bitter piece.

The king's family and ministers are said to have mocked the closeness between the two. When the minister tested them by summoning everyone at midnight to eat, Ayaz alone appeared fully prepared, while the others arrived late, or intoxicated and asleep. Mahmud made him his minister, yet Ayaz always regarded himself as a slave. Each night he set aside his costly clothes and took out the old garments in which he had been brought for sale, so as to remember his past, sit alone and please his God. Mahmud is also said to have wished to hand his own place to Ayaz; Ayaz declined, asking that it go instead to one of his two sons, Saad, and the king relinquished his power.

Ayaz outlived his king. After Mahmud's death in 1030, Masud took the throne, and in his reign his son Majdud, then a young boy, was made governor of the Punjab; Ayaz was appointed his *atalik*, or tutor, and came with him. When Masud died and his son Maudud took the throne, Majdud rebelled and set up a separate sultanate in Hindustan. Maudud marched against him, and while Lahore was under siege, on the day of Eid, Majdud suddenly died, and Maudud established his hold over the Punjab.

Maudud tried hard to take Ayaz with him to Ghazni, but Ayaz would not go. He renounced the world and took to the company of *faqirs*, wandering mystics; he received complete *faiz*, spiritual grace, from the elders of Lahore, and gave away his boundless wealth to the *faqirs* and the poor for God's sake. The last ten years of his life, tradition holds, were largely given to *dhikr*, the remembrance of God.

He died in 450 AH, though accounts of the year differ, and was buried inside the city of Lahore. At the shrine it is told that he fell suddenly ill and died; it is said that he was poisoned out of resentment at the love shown him, though in some places it is said that he died a natural death.

## The Restorer of Lahore

When Ayaz came to govern Lahore, the city had been ruined by war, and its systems of livelihood, food, trade and wages were broken. By his intelligence, it is said, he gave it shape again and set it on its own feet. Tradition goes further and counts him the founder of Lahore. When Sultan Mahmud Ghaznavi attacked the city, Raja Anangpal, son of Raja Jaipal, was overthrown and fled to Hind, and Mahmud's army plundered Lahore; all its people left, so that not a breathing soul remained in it. Then Ayaz, taking the Sultan's permission, settled the city again, summoning people from far and wide to live in it.

After Mahmud's sack, the city stayed unpeopled for some years. When Malik Ayaz became *hakim*, or governor, of the Punjab by the king's order, he turned to peopling it again and obtained from the Sultan a *farman*, a royal decree, of permission. He went about the work with such zeal that within a few years the wilderness became a mine of *ulama*, religious scholars, and men of learning. Malik Ayaz, the favourite of the Sultan's eye and without equal in goodness and discernment, also raised a fort of baked brick, and the city was rebuilt. The tradition that he refounded Lahore is not universally accepted, and has been dismissed as "later writers' conjecture".

Three of Lahore's gates are tied to him. The settlement began from the *mohalla*, or quarter, called Lahori Mandi, and the gate there was built first of all and named the Lahori Darwaza. The Mori Darwaza is his monument of victory: Mahmud had breached the walls at that spot, and when Ayaz settled the city again he set up a gate there as a memorial of the victory. The Bhati Darwaza dates from his settlement too, for the Bhat community settled inside it after Ayaz had settled the city. Even after his settlement, Lahore remained now in a state of progress, now of decline. It was into this resettled city that Ali Hajveri, the saint of Data Darbar, arrived.

## The Shrine

Tradition holds that the tomb was built at this very place after the death of Malik Ayaz, that the Ghaznavi Sultanate built the Darbar, as the shrine is called, and that it was later kept in good condition by rajas and maharajas. It has since known loss. The water of the Darbar was polluted, and part of its ground was lost in the Sikh era and part to the surrounding market, so that the site is small today.

Beside the Darbar stands a mosque named after Malik Ayaz, where the five daily prayers are offered. The shrine is under the supervision of the Auqaf Department, the government's department of religious endowments, which is also the body responsible for its present condition.

The shrine stands in Shah Alam Market, said to be the largest market in Asia, and extremely crowded. Its *mazār*, or tomb, lies near Darbar Ali Hajveri Ganj Bakhsh, the shrine of Data Ganj Bakhsh, still a popular place of pilgrimage in Lahore and for the people of the Punjab. The congestion around the small site is said to keep many people from reaching it, so that both the fame of Malik Ayaz and the enthusiasm of his visitors have gradually waned. Residents of this densely populated neighbourhood are largely unaware of the life of the man buried there, though Allama Iqbal, Kaniya Lal and other writers are said to have written books about him.

## Devotion and Festivals

Visitors come from distant places. Non-Muslims visit too, some have made documentaries about Malik Ayaz, and his stories appear in books. Every day *diyas*, oil lamps, and *prasād*, consecrated food, are offered at the shrine, and some 700 to 800 people come each day.

The *ʿurs* is held on the 12th, 13th and 14th of Zil Hajj. Its observances include the lighting of lamps, the changing of the sheets, bathing, and the eating of food at the site, and it draws 40,000 to 50,000 people.

The place is held spiritually important because Malik Ayaz fought alongside the king, served the people, protected their lives and property and instilled the spirit of Islam in them; because after Mahmud Ghaznavi's death he left his residence; and because it was his habit to spend his nights in the remembrance of God. He is remembered as generous, intelligent, loyal, hasty, daring, brave, kind and honest, a soldier who was also a good and devoted man, and people were moved by the example he set of these qualities in his own life.

## Legends

Tradition at the shrine holds that Mahmud fought almost seventeen battles with Hindus, in which Ayaz played the role of the mastermind, and that his faith was so firm that the unbelievers could never sway him. In one such story the king, attacking the "Onkay temple", found its idol suspended in mid-air. Ayaz reasoned that a magnet in the temple held the clay figure up, and that if the walls were demolished it would come down of itself; and so, it is said, it did, to the king's satisfaction.

Another story tells of Ayaz's counsel at Somnath. There the amirs, or nobles, advised Mahmud to take the rajas' crores, sums of tens of millions. When Ayaz was asked, he held that breaking the idol was better, for the renown it would bring among Muslim kings, and that God would give more; and the Sultan broke it with his own hand.

The old clothes of Ayaz are the subject of an allegory by the poet Rūmī, counted among "the finest allegories ever written". In it Ayaz, Mahmud's favourite slave, would look at the worn-out shoes and shabby clothes he had worn before Mahmud chose him for himself, for he needed to remember his former lowly state so that he could appreciate the bounty his lord had bestowed upon him. The lesson Rūmī draws is that "man should likewise remember how weak he was created, and then he will recognize his nothingness before God".

Another story tells of the Huma bird, whose shade, in Persian tradition, conveys kingdom. One day the Huma passed over Mahmud's army, and everybody rushed to be touched by its shade; only Ayaz went into Mahmud's shade, for there was his true kingdom. In this, as one scholar of Sufism put it, he is "like the faithful, who does not seek glory and power from anything created but only from his eternally rich, beloved Lord".

One anecdote turns on obedience. Mahmud was a pious and God-fearing man, and "he wrestled much with his love for Ayaz so that he should not diverge" from the Path of the Law. At a carousal, with the wine affecting him, he looked at the curls of Ayaz and drew him to himself; but the 'watchman' of the Qur'anic verse warned him not to mingle sin with love. Fearing that he could not withstand the hosts of Ayaz's locks, he gave Ayaz a knife and told him to cut off his curls in the middle. Ayaz "doubled back his locks to get the measurement, executed the king's command, and laid the two tresses before Mahmud". This ready obedience became a fresh cause of love, and Mahmud gave him gold and jewels beyond his usual wont before falling into a drunken sleep.

Next morning Mahmud saw the clipped tresses and was seized by remorse. No courtier dared ask him why, until his Chief Chamberlain, the Hajib ʿAli ibn Qarib, sent the poet ʿUnsuri in to restore his temper. ʿUnsuri extemporised a quatrain, which in English reads in part:

*Why deem it shame a fair one's curls to shear, … When clipped the Cypress doth most trim appear* (translated by Edward G. Browne)

Mahmud had the poet's mouth filled twice with precious stones, and drank wine to those verses until nightfall, "whereby his melancholy was dissipated". The tale is told among stories of poets, and its moral is that "improvisation is the chief pillar of the Poetic Art".

## Ayaz in Poetry

In poetry the king and his servant became a model of love. The great warrior king of Ghazna, conqueror of northwest India, was transformed in literary tradition into the model of the ideal lover, praised for his close relationship with his officer Ayaz, who appears as the exemplar of the servant; but the king finally became the slave of his slave. In Persian the theme is known as *Shah i gada*, King and Beggar, and the word *niyaz*, humble supplication, is often used in the story of Mahmud and Ayaz for the sake of its fitting rhyme. Several epics have been devoted to the theme, and lyrics abound in allusions to Ayaz. As the same scholar of Sufism put it, "The story of Mahmud and Ayaz is a typical expression of this kind of love and shows, at the same time, how the poets of Iran used historical facts to point out eternal truth", though the romance itself has also been regarded as a literary growth of post-Seljuq times.

The tale of the Sultan's love and *niyaz* and of Ayaz's beauty and *naz*, graceful pride, is set down in most books, above all by Mawlana Zulali, who also gave it a dream version. Verses on this love are found in the *Mahmud-nama* and other treatises, and the Sultan himself is said to have composed them, bringing his inner burning and his hidden secret into view. At the court of love, it was said, king and dervish are equal, and in its presence master and slave are peers. Some poets wrote that Ayaz had no great outward beauty but was matchless in praiseworthy character, so that the Sultan's devotion was to his good disposition and not to his looks.

## Legacy

At the shrine it is said that what should be remembered is the bond between Mahmud Ghaznavi and Malik Ahmad Ayaz, "the love and romance" of king and slave: a slave who would sacrifice his life for his king, and a loyalty so complete that he could answer for it before God on the Day of Judgement. Poets and travellers of that era and of the present are said to have written of these qualities. He is remembered too as the man who gave Lahore a new shape, and he is known still because he "made a new map of Lahore". His settlement of Lahore has also been taken as the turn from which the city's later history runs.

In the words of the same scholar, "The complete surrender of the Turkish officer Ayaz, for whom only Mahmud exists, results in perfect love of the king, who becomes, in a wonderful transformation, the slave of his slave. Ayaz is the symbol of the loving soul".

His name also became a figure of speech. In the nineteenth century it was said of Raja Hira Singh that he was looked upon as an Ayaz, and called son.
