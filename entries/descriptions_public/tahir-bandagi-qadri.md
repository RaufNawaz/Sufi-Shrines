## Overview

The shrine of Hazrat Tahir Bandagi Qadri stands in the Miani Sahib Graveyard of Lahore, said to be the largest graveyard in Asia, and his tomb is the oldest seat within it. A scholar and teacher of the Qur'an, he built a mosque, a *madrasa*, or religious school, and a library on this ground in his own lifetime, and he was buried beside his mosque when he died in 1630. It is said that the graveyard grew up around his tomb over the following centuries, as generation after generation asked to be laid to rest near him. Visitors come every day to pray and to make their petitions, and in Muharram, the first month of the Islamic year, hundreds of thousands gather for his *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of his death.

The graveyard is reported to cover some 475 *kanals*, a local measure of land. Hazrat Tahir Bandagi is remembered as a Sufi, or Muslim mystic, of the Qadiri order, as a disciple of Hazrat Mujaddid Alf Sani, honoured as the renewer of the faith for Islam's second millennium, and as a man who lived by his pen and kept away from the rich.

## The Saint

He was born in Lahore in 1576, though accounts of where he came from differ. His father, Fazlullah, gave him his early education, and he went on to memorise the Qur'an. He is also known as Shaikh Muhammad Tahir Lahori.

For advanced learning he became the *murid*, the pledged disciple, of Hazrat Shah Sikandar, son of Pir Kamal Kaithali, under whom he first completed his training, and he became Shah Sikandar's *khalifa*, an authorised deputy. Accounts of his master differ, but Shah Sikandar is the master most often named, and he is credited with giving him the name Tahir Bandagi. Shah Sikandar is said to have told him, "you are meant for the spiritual reformation of people; simply remain devoted to the service of humanity at all times."

There was also, it is reported, a real trial in his life. For a period he became enamoured of a Hindu woman and stepped back from his religious work. Those who wished him well are said to have appealed on his behalf to Hazrat Mujaddid Alf Sani, Shaikh Ahmad Sirhindi. He then returned to his path, formally became the Mujaddid's disciple as well, and later taught the Mujaddid's own children, among them Shaikh Muhammad Masum. The episode is told plainly, as part of his story rather than something to smooth over. It is told in more than one way, and one telling makes it a prophecy fulfilled. The Mujaddid, it is said, had foretold that one of those present would become a *kafir*, an unbeliever. Then, at Sirhind, Shaikh Tahir drew the *qashqa*, the forehead mark, and went and sat in a temple, having become a Hindu, until the Mujaddid prayed, his prayer was answered and Shaikh Tahir repented.

He attended the Mujaddid, who is also called the Imam-e Rabbani, gained great benefits from him and is counted among his *khalifas*, a man extremely strict in keeping the *shariah*, the religious law. The Mujaddid is said to have conferred on him the *qutbiyat* of Lahore, the rank of its *qutb*, the axis or pole of the saints and the highest spiritual station. It was at the Mujaddid's direction that he set about guiding the people of Lahore, and thousands reached high spiritual stations through him.

He is counted a Qadiri, a follower of the Sufi order founded by Abd al-Qadir Gilani, and he is remembered as the *qutb* of his age in the Qadiri *silsila*, the chain of spiritual descent from master to disciple. Most often he is counted a Naqshbandi as well, of the order founded by Baha-ud-Din Naqshband, in its Mujaddidi line, named after the Mujaddid, and the shrine's full name, Darbar Hazrat Tahir Bandagi Qadri Naqshbandi Mujaddidi, joins the two. He is also said to have initiated others into the Qadiri, Naqshbandi and Suhrawardi orders, receiving their *bai'at*, the oath of initiation into a Sufi order.

He was a worshipper of God, a man of *kashf*, insight into hidden things, and of *karamat*, miraculous deeds and spiritual gifts, and he was accomplished in the outward sciences too. It was said of him that no seeker, whether of faith or of the world, who came to his door went away empty. He lived by his pen: he copied out books of hadith, the sayings of the Prophet, and of *tafsir*, commentary on the Qur'an, and subsisted by presenting them. All his life he went to no rich man, nor admitted the rich to his own *darbar*, or court.

His nights passed in instructing his attendants and in worship. It is said that he spent them absorbed in *zikr*, the remembrance of God, rather than in the comforts that came with his following, that from time to time he fasted and denied himself for stretches of fifteen to twenty days, and that he never allowed the crowds who gathered around him to make him proud. In his later years he married twice, to Mah-e-Khanam and to Ismatullah, the daughter of an Afghan king. In the nineteenth century none of his descendants remained.

He died in the reign of the emperor Shah Jahan, on a Thursday, at the forenoon hour, on 8 Muharram 1040 AH in the Islamic calendar, which is 8 August 1630, and was buried beside his own mosque. Accounts of his age at death differ; the age the shrine keeps is 54, in keeping with his birth in 1576.

## His Disciples

Among his *khalifas* were Abu Muhammad Qadiri, Sayyid Sufi, Shaikh Adam Banuri and Shah Ghulan Sarmast, who has his own tomb in Lahore, outside the Mori Gate, inside the government garden. Shaikh Adam Banuri came to him on foot, walking from Banur to Lahore. In his own account, written in Persian, Adam Banuri told how he received from Shaikh Tahir the *nisbat-e jazba*, the spiritual bond of rapture, which had reached Shaikh Tahir through Shah Sikandar.

Within Shaikh Tahir's enclosure lies the grave of Hazrat Abu Muhammad Qadiri Lahori, his *murid* in the Qadiri *silsila*, whose family were *khadims*, or servants, of Shaikh Tahir. One line of Shaikh Tahir's spiritual descendants runs through three generations: Shaikh Muhammad Fazil Qadri Batalvi was the *murid* and *khalifa* of Shaikh Muhammad Afzal Kalanauri, who was the *murid* of Shaikh Abu Muhammad Lahori, who was the *murid* of Shaikh Muhammad Tahir Lahori.

Shaikh Tahir is said to have wished to visit the tomb of Shah Husain to seek his help, *istimdad*. A book, the *Tazkira-e Mujaddidiya*, whose author is otherwise unknown, has been ascribed to him by Muhammad Din Fauq.

## The Shrine

The land that is now the Miani Sahib Graveyard was given to him in his lifetime so that he could teach, and he settled there in the time of the emperor Jahangir. On it he built a mosque, a *madrasa* and a library, a *kutub khana*, and he taught the Qur'an there himself. He taught *fiqh*, Islamic law, hadith and *tafsir* to students free of charge, made many *murids* and gave instruction in *suluk*, the Sufi path. Because he was learned, accomplished and a perfect *faqir*, a mystic who has embraced spiritual poverty, thousands became his *murids* and pupils within a few years, and a magnificent settlement grew up around him.

The shrine's tradition tells of a raid in his lifetime, though accounts differ over when it happened. Sikh raiders, believing that gold was hidden inside boxes on the premises, broke in and found only books; on discovering this, they threw them back into the locality and left.

When he died, his resting place beside the mosque became his shrine, and the rulers and disciples of the period are said to have kept it under careful administrative care from the start. After his death the quarter stayed settled until the end of Muslim rule. By the 1880s the quarter that had grown up around him lay in ruins, but his tomb still stood, counted among the buildings outside the city, and some of the old fabric of his *madrasa* remained.

His enclosure was then the first and largest in the graveyard: a spacious wall of *pakka*, or fired, brick, inside which were thousands of graves, old and new. Within this great wall stood another large platform of fired brick, and on it his tomb, built of fired brick and plastered with lime.

The tomb has been rebuilt. The present tomb was raised by a Gilani pir, and it is said that when the old tomb was excavated for the new construction, it was found to have been built exactly in the style of the tomb of Hazrat Mujaddid Alf Sani.

His walled enclosure, the *chahar-diwari*, became a burial ground for others too. Sayyid Munawwar Ali Shah Naqshbandi Suhrawardi Lahori, who died in 1264 AH, lies inside it, and Shaikh Haji Ramzan Lahori Chishti lies near the tomb. In the nineteenth century other graves were located by his wall: one row adjoined it, another grave lay in its eastern corner and another to its north, and a neighbouring enclosure stood beside it.

## Keepers of the Shrine

The shrine is overseen today by its *Gaddi Nasheen*, its present custodians, and several people are formally employed as its staff; they are said to consider caring for the shrine all the year round a privilege rather than a job.

In the nineteenth century the office of *sajjada nashin*, the custodian who sits on the saint's prayer carpet, was disputed. Its holders in that century included Sayyid Mansur, *sajjada nashin* of Shaikh Muhammad Tahir's *khanqah*, the lodge where a Sufi's disciples gather, and Sayyid Shahsawar, *sajjada nashin* of his tomb. Sayyid Shahsawar came of a Gilani family whose line is traced to Sayyid Abd al-Qadir Lahori, son of Sayyid Jalal al-Din Baghdadi, who settled at Lahore in the reign of Akbar and married a daughter of Nawab Mir Kifayat Khan; one of the family, Bahadur, son of Chiragh Shah, was an engraver of seals. Accounts of the family's line differ. Later the office was held by Pir Sher Husain Tahiri, whose name Tahiri is taken from the saint himself.

## Devotion and Festivals

A mosque stands beside the shrine, where the five daily prayers are held, and visitors come both to pray and to recite the *Fatiha*, the opening chapter of the Qur'an, and to make their own supplications. Some families have come for generations, following their parents and grandparents before them. Daily attendance is put at 1,200 to 1,600 visitors. Every week, on Thursday nights and Fridays, the shrine holds *Mehfil-e-Naat*, gatherings for the recitation of *naat*, poems in praise of the Prophet, and *langar*, the free communal kitchen.

It is said that the shrine owes its continuing importance to answered prayer across generations: families who saw their petitions answered here return, and bring their children to do the same. Visitors describe Hazrat Tahir Bandagi as a *wali-e-kamil*, a "perfect saint", who was granted visions of the Prophet and of other venerable elders more than once, and devotees offer the presence of jinn, unseen spirits, and of other noble burials nearby as further evidence, in their eyes, of his spiritual standing.

His *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of the saint's death, falls every year on the 7th, 8th and 9th of Muharram, and an estimated 250,000 to 300,000 pilgrims come across the three days. The present custodians say that Hazrat Tahir Bandagi did not care for the *dhol*, a large barrel drum, or for *dhamal*, ecstatic devotional dancing to the beat of the drum, and the *ʿurs* is kept restrained accordingly, centred on *Mehfil-e-Naat* and *langar* rather than on music or dancing. In the nineteenth century his *ʿurs* was kept by the *sajjada nashin* and the graveyard's gravediggers in the first ten days of Muharram.

## Legends

Several traditions are attached to the shrine, and not all of them are agreed on even by today's visitors.

One holds that while he was leading the *Taraweeh*, the night prayers of Ramadan, the month of fasting, he was granted a vision of the Prophet Muhammad, who told him that whoever looked upon him, learned from him or was buried near him would enter Paradise; this is offered as the reason so many notable figures chose to be buried around his tomb. He himself, it is said, used to say that he had asked of God that whoever was buried within the enclosure of his tomb after his death should be of Paradise.

A second, more contested tradition concerns jinn. Graves near the shrine are popularly said to belong to jinn who watch over the site, and some visitors say they have seen figures in human form vanish before their eyes. Others do not accept this. They say that although the graves exist, they have never themselves seen any jinn or wild animal there, and they regard the stories of jinn as ignorance, pointing out, from their own study of his life, that Hazrat Tahir Bandagi never taught or endorsed such beliefs. Both views are held among visitors today.

## The Miani Graveyard

In the 1880s the Miani graveyard lay two miles south of Lahore. It had formerly been a village of the Miani people, that is, of *ulama* and *fuzala*, scholars and men of learning, and it was called Panj Pir Dhera, because five graves stood there before the village. The name Miani is said to come from the learned men who lived there, for in Punjabi a *maulvi*, a learned and accomplished man, is called *miana*. The place is also called Miani Sharif.

In the reign of Akbar, it is said, Miani was a village where only learned men lived. Its chief was Hafiz Jan Muhammad, father of Hazrat Abu Muhammad Qadiri, and after Hafiz Jan Muhammad's death Abu Muhammad was made its head. A dispute over a point of law later emptied the village, until a man appointed by the king settled there and it was peopled again. Shah Muhammad Muqim, travelling to Lahore, halted at the Miani graveyard, near the tomb of Shaikh Muhammad Tahir.

By the 1880s thousands of graves had been made there and many enclosures of fired brick built, and the unfinished enclosure of Rani Gul Begum, a woman of the courtesan caste, stood there too. For the ten or twelve years before, general burial had been stopped, and only those who had such enclosures still buried their dead there. The gravediggers, the *gorkans*, served the graveyard, and in the nineteenth century, whenever a body was brought, the family of the dead paid them their due and gave the *sajjada nashin* something besides, as they could.

## Legacy

Accounts of how the graveyard began differ, but Hazrat Tahir Bandagi Qadri's tomb is the oldest seat of the Miani Sahib Graveyard, and a Chishti and a Naqshbandi Suhrawardi saint lie beside him. By the 1880s all Muslims were said to acknowledge his greatness; the great family of his *silsila* was then at the town of Batala, with thousands of *murids* across the Punjab, and countless *karamat* of his were written in the lives of the saints.

His memory was kept in verse as well. A chronogram, a verse whose letters, read as numbers, give a year, was written on him as a quatrain, and it opens by naming him:

شیخ طاہر پاک باز و پاک دین
اوج عرفان پر تھے مثل آفتاب

Separately, the year of his death is drawn from a single word, *gham*, grief.
