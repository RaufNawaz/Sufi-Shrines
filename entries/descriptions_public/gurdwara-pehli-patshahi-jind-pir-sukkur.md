## Overview

Gurdwara Pehli Patshahi, popularly called Jind Pir, stands on the Sukkur side of the great bridge that carries the Lahore to Karachi railway across the Indus between Sukkur and Rohri. By tradition it marks a spot graced by Guru Nanak, the first Guru, or *Pehli Patshahi*, during his third *udasi*, or journey, through Sindh, and it was from here, the tradition holds, that he crossed to the river islet later famous as Sadh Belo. The gurdwara is a characteristic monument of Sindh's syncretic Nanakpanthi tradition, the tradition of the followers of the path of Nanak, and it is distinct both from the island temple of Sadh Belo and from the other Sikh sites of Sukkur and Rohri. Much of its detailed history survives only in oral and community tradition.

## The Gurdwara

Its first custodian is said to have been an Udasi *sadhu*, a Hindu ascetic, of the Jhulelal devotion. The shrine passed into the keeping of the Nanakpanthi community of Sindh, Hindus and Sikhs together, who honour Guru Nanak without a firm confessional boundary; accounts differ on whether Sindh's followers of Nanak are to be counted as Sikhs.

Within, the *Guru Granth Sahib*, the Sikh scripture, is installed and its *prakash*, the ceremonial opening of the scripture, is performed, while images are also venerated. A lamp said to have been kindled by the Guru himself is kept burning day and night, and pilgrims bow before it.

The name Zinda Pir, or Jinda Pir, also belongs to an older shrine by the river at Sukkur, where Hindus and Muslims worshipped together until the late nineteenth century. At that time the Hindu congregation abandoned their claim and set up a shrine of their own to Jinda Pir on the Sukkur bank. Whether that shrine and this gurdwara are the same structure, two structures sharing one name, or otherwise related remains an open question.

## The Guru

Guru Nanak, the first Guru, is believed to be the founder of Sikhism. He was born in 1469 in a city now called Nankana Sahib, in Pakistan. His father was Kalyan Chand Das Bedi, affectionately called Mehta Kalu, accountant to the Bhatti family under Rai Bular. He was born into a Hindu household and educated in a Muslim milieu, yet he is held to have been neither a practising Muslim nor a Hindu. He died on 22 September 1539; accounts of the dates of his life differ.

In the janamsakhis, the cycles of tales told of the first Guru's life, at the age of thirteen he refused the sacred thread, holding that it is a man's actions alone that give him his position. In the same tellings he became well acquainted with the dervish Sayyad Hussain and gained an intimate knowledge of the Sufi line of thought.

The janamsakhis tell, too, that Nanak moved to Sultanpur, around 60 kilometres from Talwandi, as keeper of Nawab Daulat Khan's store. There he bathed in the Bein rivulet and in the evenings gathered people to hear his hymns. He vanished into the stream for a week, the story goes, and came out with the words: "There is only one God. There is no Hindu, no Mussalman." It is widely believed that it was in Sultanpur that the religious life of Nanak began. When he set out, the janamsakhis say, he was dressed in a strange garb, an approximate combination of what the holy men of Hinduism and of Islam would wear.

He married Sulakhini, daughter of Mul Chand and Chando Rani of the town of Batala, on 24 September 1487, though accounts of the marriage differ, and they had two sons, Sri Chand, born on 8 September 1494, and Lakhmi Das, born on 12 February 1497. In Aurangzeb's reign his descendants lived at the place of Baba Nanak, as the Guru was also called, twelve *kos*, an old measure of distance, from Batala on the bank of the Ravi.

Accounts of how many journeys he made differ, but they lasted more than twenty years. Mostly on foot, he went east all the way to Bengal, south to the island of Sri Lanka, north to Kashmir, Nepal and Tibet, and west to Mecca, and he returned to settle at Kartarpur. He preferred to stay on the outskirts of villages, and whenever he visited an area he would visit its religious shrines, Muslim, Hindu and of any other denomination. People came in their multitudes from every land and became his disciples, and a minstrel named Mardana sang his verses and drew people into the snare of devotion.

The gurdwara's tradition sets his passage through Sindh on his third *udasi*; accounts of which journey brought him to Sukkur differ. Sikh tradition also records that he came to the village of Kolachi and visited the temple of a sea god, for which he had to take a boat to an island neighbouring Kolachi. The temple at Clifton has been identified as the temple that Nanak visited on his tour of Sindh.

On his deathbed he appointed not his son but his most loyal student as his spiritual successor. This was Lahna, a Khatri, whom he gave the title Guru Angad and who is remembered as Guru Angad Dev.

The gurdwara's first custodian is said to have been an Udasi, and the Udasis follow the tradition of Sri Chand, the eldest son of Guru Nanak, who was not recognised as Guru by the mainstream Sikh community. The Udasi sect is believed to have been founded by him, and the sect called Nanghe Sadhu also derives its origin from him. In Aurangzeb's reign the Udasis were counted as a fourth class among the dervishes of Hind: devotees of Baba Nanak who worshipped the Creator in the way of their master.

## Legends

At Mecca, tradition tells, he slept with his feet towards the Kaaba; accounts of what followed differ. At Haridwar, the janamsakhis tell, he faced west and threw water to reach "my newly ploughed fields there", mocking a ritual.

At the Kumbh Mela, in the same tellings, he taught that "it is our souls, the real sinners, that we must wash and not our bodies". In Baghdad, the janamsakhis go on, he sang the *azaan*, the Muslim call to prayer, until his hearers knelt to pray.

Nanak himself is held to have abhorred the association of miracles with religious personalities, yet the stories of his life are full of them. In one, when Karori called his cure a miracle, the Guru answered that "there is no such thing as a miracle".

A Muslim tradition, which Sikhs do not accept, holds that Guru Nanak was born by the blessing of Baba Naulakha Hazari.

## The Guru's Verse

Baba Nanak composed verses in Hindi on the truth of God, and in them he set out the unity of God. The word *bani* itself means devotional verses such as those of Guru Nanak: verses of supplication, entreaty, humility and reverence. Like Kabir, he used the *doha*, an old popular Prakrit and Hindi metre in which a couplet is put together where the two verses rhyme, for his mystical poetry.

Lines from his hymns, in English translation, give a sense of the poetry. His *aarti*, the hymn of the ritual of lamps, has these lines:

ਗਗਨ ਮੈ ਥਾਲੁ
ਰਵਿ ਚੰਦੁ ਦੀਪਕ ਬਨੇ …

*In the salver of the firmament, / The sun and the moon shine as lamps*

On the oneness of God he sang:

ਸਾਹਿਬੁ ਮੇਰਾ ਏਕੋ ਹੈ ॥
ਏਕੋ ਹੈ ਭਾਈ ਏਕੋ ਹੈ ॥

*My Master is One / He is One, brother, the only One*

And again:

*God is One, / The Path is One*

On the sacred thread he refused:

ਦਇਆ ਕਪਾਹ
ਸੰਤੋਖੁ ਸੂਤੁ …

*From the cotton of compassion, / Spin the thread of contentment*

On cleansing:

ਭਰੀਐ ਹਥੁ ਪੈਰੁ ਤਨੁ ਦੇਹ ॥
ਪਾਣੀ ਧੋਤੈ ਉਤਰਸੁ ਖੇਹ ॥

*As hands or feet besmirched with slime, / Water washes white*

And in another hymn:

ਬਗਾ ਬਗੇ ਕਪੜੇ ਤੀਰਥ ਮੰਝਿ ਵਸੰਨਿੑ ॥

*The herons in their white feathers dwell in the sacred shrines of pilgrimage*

The singer Abida Parvin has said: "I sing Shah Hussain, Bulle Shah, Mian Muhammad Bakhsh, Hazrat Khawaja Ghulam Farid, Sacchal Sarmast, Baba Farid, and Guru Nanak." Among the Hindu Sufis of Sindh, Sain Vasan Shah became famous for reciting the *banis* of Shri Guru Nanak and the *kalams*, the poems, of the Sufis.

## The Followers of Nanak in Sindh

In Aurangzeb's reign the sum of the worship of Baba Nanak's followers was the reading of their master's verses, which they recited in song and melody. Their master's name was always on their tongue, and they counted the service of travellers, of those who come and go, as the greatest worship. Whoever arrived at midnight speaking Baba Nanak's name was treated as a brother and a friend and served, even a stranger, a thief, a highwayman or an evildoer. The tradition of *langar*, the communal free kitchen, is believed to have begun with Guru Nanak.

In nineteenth century Sindh the Nanakpanth, the path of Nanak, was probably the dominant religious path among the Hindu Sindhis. In Sindh many Nanakpanthis belonged to a subgroup known as the Udasis. Some of their *darbars*, as shrines are called in Sindh by Sikhs, Muslims and Hindus alike, had been created in the late eighteenth century, and in the second half of the nineteenth century there was a renaissance of the Nanakpanthi *darbars*, with many new ones built all over Sindh. Because the Nanakpanth was so widespread, the Hindu Sufis of Sindh were very familiar with Sikh terminology.

Among the Lohanas and related mercantile groups two main cults were followed, the Nanakpanth and the Daryapanth. The Daryapanth, in all probability the older of the two, is based on the deification of the Indus River and was run by a priestly caste, the Thakurs. The Thakurs were the priests of Jhulelal; they were settled in different cities of Sindh, and from 1947 onwards they migrated to India. Jhulelal was already worshipped by some of the Hindus of Sindh, but the Hindus of Sindh were for the most part Nanakpanthis. Offerings to the Indus are still made in the wider region of Sindh.

In colonial Sindh an attempt was made to create a centralised path devoted to Jhulelal, then known as Amar Lal or Udero Lal, with an attempt to impose a specific script, the Khudawadi, and a meeting organised by several Hindu *panchayats*, or councils, of Sindh at Udero Lal. There was a growing similarity between the iconography of Guru Nanak and that of Jhulelal. Before Partition, temples devoted to Jhulelal in Sindh were built with a dome, as were some Sikh *samadhis*, or memorial shrines.

Many upper caste Hindus in Sindh, especially from the Lohana community, refer to Nanak and his teachings, and many Hindus of the middle and upper classes in Sindh are followers of the Nanak *panth*, or sect. It is common to cover one's head before walking into a *tikana*, the temple containing the Guru Granth Sahib. Inside the temples of the Nanak Panthis in Sindh and Southern Punjab hang portraits of Guru Nanak, along with Krishna, Kali Mata and other Hindu deities. Guru Nanak is mostly depicted in saffron coloured clothes.

The Guru's image also hung in the *darbars* of the Hindu Sufis of Sindh. On the wall of Rochaldas, one of them, hung pictures of Guru Nanak, Qutub Ali Shah, Sai Baba, Ramachandra and Krishna. Among the Hindu Sufis of Sindh, a *jai*, a formula of praise, named among the gods Guru Nanak, Jhulelal and Mast Qalandar.

The Nanak Panthis belong to South Punjab and Sindh, and they are found in very large numbers in the province of Sindh. The communities who refused the Khalsa, the order of initiated Sikhs, and called themselves followers of Guru Nanak still live in Sindh and in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa; baptised Sikhs look down upon them, and at Manora they were seen merging into the larger groups of Sikhs.

After Partition the *darbars* that the Hindus left behind in Sindh were most of the time preserved, with different arrangements for their management, often with *gaddi nashins*, the heads of the shrines, or followers in India paying the keepers; and despite the shifting diplomatic relations between Pakistan and India, it was always possible to travel across the border.

## The Setting

Sukkur stands at one corner of the triangle formed by Rohri, Sukkur and Shikarpur. In the Indus at Sukkur lies the island of Sadh Belo, a *tirtha*, or Hindu place of pilgrimage, which the gurdwara's tradition links to the Guru's crossing. Between December 1939 and February 1940 the city was the scene of the Sukkur riots, whose first victim was Bhagat Kanwar Ram.

Sukkur lay on the road of the armies that pursued Dara Shikoh. News reached them that Dara, having passed Bhakkar, had halted at Sukkur on the twenty fifth of Muharram, the first month of the Islamic year. Shaikh Mir, Diler Khan and Qubad Khan crossed towards Sukkur; because of the thick jungle, the crowding of thorny trees and the hard stages of the road, many beasts perished and the baggage did not arrive, and on the sixth of Safar, the second month, they reached Sukkur. Shaikh Mir stayed there a day, and the *zamindars*, the landholders, told him that twenty five *kos* from Sukkur a road branches off towards Kandahar.

Across the river, Rohri was the home of Hindu and Muslim mystics. Bedil, a Sufi born in Rohri who died in 1872, was a writer in five languages. Rochaldas, the Hindu Sufi, was born in Rohri in 1879, seven years after Bedil's death, and it is told that he once found himself wandering high in the sky above the port on the Indus at Rohri. Janullah of Rohri, a Suhrawardi mystic, was a Persian poet of Sind in the second half of the eighteenth century. Sain Vasan Shah, the singer of Guru Nanak's *banis*, has a *darbar* in Ulhasnagar and another in Rohri, on the bank of the Indus. The singer Alan Faqir is reported to have sung in his youth on the buses and trains between Rohri and Sehwan Sharif, and he also sang at the *dargahs*, the shrines, of Sind.

## Legacy

Guru Nanak is remembered as a teacher who crossed the line between Hindu and Muslim, and a shrine kept by Hindus and Sikhs together, where the Granth and images are honoured side by side, belongs to that memory. It is said that, asked which religion to adopt, he told his Hindu and Muslim companions to be true Muslims or honest Hindus: "That would be Nanak's religion."

Devotion to him reaches beyond the Sikhs. Many Muslims acquainted with the teachings of Guru Nanak are devoted to him too and visit his shrine, and to one Muslim painter, "Guru Nanak is like a Muslim Sufi Saint". Baba Farid, the Muslim Sufi saint who wrote devotional poetry in Punjabi, is said to have become an inspiration for Guru Nanak when Nanak visited his shrine on his travels, and Farid's texts are revered among the Sikhs, for he is held to have influenced the founder of their religion.

A twentieth century shaykh of the Chishti Sabiri order described, in certain lectures, Krishna, Rama, Sita, Lachman and Guru Nanak as believers in the one true God and religious leaders with a "Divine connection", *nisbat*. For the Hindu Sufis of Sindh, the message of the Sufis can also be found through Guru Nanak, Mirabai and Kabir, as well as in the poetry of other *sants*, the poet saints. At the temple of the goddess Hinglaj, the engraved footsteps of Guru Nanak are shown, and they connect Hinglaj to the Sikh and Nanak *panth* traditions.
