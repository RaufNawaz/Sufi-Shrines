## Consensus choices

- **Who initiated Ranjha:** "Accounts differ over who initiated him, but the guru most often named is the ascetic *Bal Nath*, master of the hill". Basis (a) the site's account, and (b) it plus Waris Shah (Usborne and his annotator) against one source (Shackle's glossary, Gorakhnath); Bulleh Shah's "the guru" names no one. Gorakhnath as initiator is not named.
- **Gorakhnath's date:** tradition's two thousand years kept as legend, with the site's own correction ("usually placed centuries later, in about the eleventh or twelfth century"), basis (a); Dhillon's "eleventh century" agrees and is not given separately. The Puratan Janamsakhis' conversation between Gorakhnath and Guru Nanak is kept as Sikh tradition, followed by Dhillon's "historically Gorakhnath lived and preached long before Nanak's time", so no variant date is given.
- **Gorakhnath's place in the order:** "organiser of the *Nath* tradition" (a, the site) and "the head of the *Kanphata* yogis" (Shackle), which do not conflict. Boivin's "founded" is treated as a minor variant of wording and dropped. His being "believed to be an avatar ... of Shiva" is kept as a belief.
- **Name and location of the hill:** the site's, basis (a): Tilla Jogian, highest peak of the eastern Salt Range, Jhelum District. The annotator's 'Tilla Guruan', "the mound of the gurus", is kept as another name tradition gives the hill; his "on the banks of the river Jhelum" is dropped as a minor variant (both place it in Jhelum). Bhandari's name for the place is an uncertain reading (rule 5) and is not given; his "on top of the salt mountain, seven kos from the fort of Rohtas" is kept.
- **The ear ring:** "great rings" (site, basis a); Shackle's "wooden ring" is a minor variant of one word, dropped.
- **The hill since 1947:** "Though accounts of its later state differ", followed by the site's account of decay, basis (a). The annotator's present tense note ("still preserved", the Jog Mat "still practised there") is undated and is the only account against the site's; it is not listed.
- **Spellings:** Heer (the site's) in prose, Hir kept inside quotations and verse; Bal Nath (the site's) for Usborne's Balnath; *Kanphata* (the site's) for Shackle's Kanphat; Waris Shah for Schimmel's Warith Shah; Punjab for Panjab.
- **Time anchors:** Bhandari's material is anchored "in the reign of (the Mughal emperor) Aurangzeb", from the bibliography item ("composed in regnal year 40 of Aurangzeb"); no CE year is computed from it. Waris Shah's 1180 Hijri is not converted. The site's "early sixteenth century" (Nanak), "eighteenth century" (Waris Shah) and "early twenty first century" (overgrowth) stand as given.

## Glosses

From `data/glossary.csv`: *murshid* (spiritual guide), *pir* (a Sufi spiritual guide), *fana* (annihilation in God, as *fana fillah*), fakirs (wandering mystics, the glossary's "wandering Sufi").

Team version's own: *jog* (the discipline of renunciation, the site's); *Kanphata* ('split-eared', the site's and Shackle's); *jogi* (a wandering ascetic devoted to Shiva who meditates on the One, Boivin's glossary, with "the popular form of yogi" as the dictionary sense); guru (the Hindu holy man and teacher, the Hindu counterpart of the Muslim *murshid*, the annotator's); *gur* (raw sugar, a ceremonial offering, the annotator's); Tilla Guruan (the mound of the gurus); Chit Akas, Bhut Kas and Juda Kas (the three planes of the philosophy of jog, the annotator's); the 'unstruck music', the 'circle of nectar', the 'tenth door', 'make the Ganges flow backward' and the 'fourth state' (Shackle's).

Plain dictionary senses: avatar (an earthly form of a god); Shivaite (devoted to Shiva); Shivrat (the festival of Shiva); *kos* (an old measure of distance); maund (an old measure of weight); lakh (a hundred thousand); *doab* (the land between two rivers); *Darbar* (the court, in "the Darbar of God"); the Puratan Janamsakhis (early collections of stories of Guru Nanak's life); Hijri (the Islamic calendar); Sufis (the Muslim mystics); *chelas* (disciples); *gunas* (the qualities that govern worldly existence, from Shackle's own phrase); *dhuni* (fireplace, Schaflechner's own equation of the two); Jats (the farming and herding people of the old Punjab, from the poem's "ploughing" and Shackle's "Jat pastoral tribes").

## Judgement calls for V1

1. **Bhandari's passage is given as about this hill** ("In the reign of Aurangzeb the hill was the place of austerity of a jogi. It stood on top of the salt mountain ... seven *kos* ... from the fort of Rohtas"), as the team version gives it throughout, though the jogi's name and the place's name in it are uncertain readings and are dropped. If V1 holds that the identification itself rests on those readings, the paragraph and the Overview's Shivrat sentence go.
2. **"In the reign of (the Mughal emperor) Aurangzeb"** as the time anchor for Bhandari (Shivrat, the salt, the pool, Jud, the Heer and Ranjha songs), from the bibliography's "composed in regnal year 40 of Aurangzeb"; the team notes say the Source line hedges that date "as the notes report it". The fallback is "in Mughal times".
3. **"Accounts differ over who initiated him, but the guru most often named is ... Bal Nath"**: "most often" rests on the site and Waris Shah against Shackle's glossary alone.
4. **"Though accounts of its later state differ"** for the annotator's undated present tense against the site's account of decay.
5. **The Tripta story** is framed "It is told that" (the team version calls it Khalid's "reconstruction") and introduced as entering "Guru Nanak's story by another road", the team version's own phrase; Tripta is not identified.
6. **The Janamsakhi conversation** is kept as Sikh tradition with the historical correction, and set in its own account (the anointment of Guru Angad), not tied to the hill.
7. **"He was the head of the *Kanphata* yogis"** joins Shackle's "the head of the Kanphat yogis" to the site's "organiser"; "founded" dropped.
8. **"The Naths, or Nathpanthis"**: Rizvi's "Naths" and Boivin's "Nathpanthis" are treated as one group.
9. **The Salt Range sentence** ("the line of mountains dividing the plains of the Punjab from the slightly elevated plateau of the Potwar, a land where there is no irrigation") generalises Werth's setting of a village in the range, and his "the salt range where there is no irrigation", to the range.
10. **"Two texts ... describe his pilgrimage to Hinglaj, and the fireplace, or *dhuni*, of Guru Gorakhnath goes back to the long tradition of Nath yogis who made that journey"**: the fireplace is not placed, since the team version does not say where it is.
11. **The rebellion of the chelas and Bal Nath's rebuke** are told in sequence ("When they rebel, Bal Nath rebukes them"), joining the two passages the team version gives under "Ranjha Takes Jog" and "Legends and Miracles" (both p. 32 of the poem).
12. **The initiation rite**: "At their guru's order the chelas bring the earrings and the razor" for "They obeyed the orders of their Guru"; "last of all he teaches him the word Allah", which the team notes give as a paraphrase of "learn the word `Allah.1`" (a fused note marker, not an uncertain word).
13. **The popular belief** is given without "Pirs" (see Left out), as "fakirs, the wandering mystics, and jogis".
14. **The commentator** (Usborne's later unnamed annotator) is kept unnamed twice: his reading of the song of nonentity as *fana fillah*, and his quoted remark "This may seem strange today, but was by no means unusual at the time."
15. **"Ranjha's own name is that of a well known caste of the Jats, the farming and herding people of the old Punjab"**: the annotator's present tense "still" is dropped; the gloss of Jats is ours.
16. **"It is the greatest of the local legends of the Punjab"** for Shackle's "The greatest of these local legends"; Kugle's sentence on Radha and Krishna is given in the narrator's voice.
17. **"Already in the reign of Aurangzeb, wonderful verses and designs on the love of Heer and Ranjha were composed and sung in the province of Lahore"**: "Already" is the team version's; the reading that this precedes Waris Shah is left to the reader.
18. **"Waris Shah is said to have recited it with great emotion among the people of his own speech"** for "among his speech communities in the Panjab".
