## Overview

In the town of Sakhi Sarwar, also called Nigaha, at the foot of the Sulaiman range in the Dera Ghazi Khan district, stands the shrine of Sultan Sakhi Sarwar, a saint of the twelfth century honoured across an extraordinary breadth of communities. He is known by the loving title *Lakhdata*, "the giver of hundreds of thousands", for his boundless generosity. For centuries his cult was one of the most widespread in the western Punjab, drawing not only Muslims but Hindus and Sikhs, and his shrine at Nigaha remains a major place of pilgrimage in the southwest of the province.

Tradition holds that the saint pitched his tent at the foot of the mountain, where Nigaha now stands, and was martyred there; his tomb stands on the same spot. It has drawn pilgrims for centuries. In the reign of the emperor Aurangzeb the road to it from Multan was crowded with them, and the devotion has continued even after the shrine was bombed in 2011.

## The Saint

His name was Sayyid Ahmad Sultan, and he is called Sakhi Sarwar, "the Generous Chief". A Sayyid is a descendant of the Prophet Muhammad. He lived in the twelfth century, and tradition places his origins in the region of Multan and Shahkot; his life is wrapped in legend more than in documented history. He is counted among the ancient *mashaikh*, the Sufi masters, and the great *awliya*, the friends of God, of the Multan region, and he is remembered as a saint of miraculous power and open-handed charity.

His father was Sayyid Zain al-Abidin, and a genealogy traces the family back to Ali: Sayyid Ahmad was the son of Sayyid Zain al-Abidin, son of Sayyid Umar, son of Sayyid Abd al-Latif, son of Sayyid Baha al-Din, son of Sayyid Ghiyas al-Din, son of Sayyid Baha al-Din, son of Sayyid Salah al-Din, son of Sayyid Zain al-Abidin, son of Sayyid Isa, son of Sayyid Salih, son of Sayyid Abd al-Ghani, son of Sayyid Jalil, son of Sayyid Khair al-Din, son of Sayyid Zia al-Din, son of Sayyid Daud, son of Sayyid Abd al-Jalil Rumi, son of Sayyid Ismail, son of Imam Jafar Sadiq, son of Imam Muhammad Baqir, son of Imam Zain al-Abidin, son of Imam Husain, son of Ali.

Zain al-Abidin came from Arabia to Hind, as India was called, because of some dissension, and settled at a village in the Multan area. He was devout and ascetic and kept vigil through the night, and the headman of the village, Pira, gave him his daughter, Bibi Ayesha, in marriage. The headman is also said to have become his *murid*, his pledged disciple, and to have built a mosque. From Bibi Ayesha the saint was born, a *wali*, a friend of God, it is said, from his birth. There were two wives in his father's household, one of them the saint's mother, and the saint had a brother, Sayyid Abd al-Ghani. Zain al-Abidin lies with Sayyid Mahmud and Sayyid Sohrab in the burial place of his family, a place of pilgrimage for the people.

In his early youth, it is said, he mastered the *nafs-e ammara*, the commanding self, by hard austerities and by devotions beyond what can be borne, and the lamp of mystic knowledge lit the chamber of his inner being. After his father's death his fame spread, and people came to him in droves. His maternal cousins, with whom he owned half the village, grew jealous and tormented him, and he left.

Accounts of his masters differ. He is said to have gone to Baghdad and there to have received inward grace in the service of the Ghaus al-Azam, a title meaning the greatest helper; of Shihab al-Din Suhrawardi, called the Shaikh al-Shuyukh, the master of masters; and of Shaikh Maudud Chishti. He returned to Hind perfected. He is also said to have received the blessing of *wilayat*, sainthood, from Bahauddin Zakariya of Multan, and his family is counted as belonging to both the Chishti and the Suhrawardi Sufi orders. Back in the Punjab he lived for a while at Dhaunkal, and then returned to his village.

He married, though accounts of his marriage differ, and his wife is most often named Bibi Bai. He had a young son, but accounts differ over the boy's name, Siraj al-Din or Sayyid Raj.

## The Martyrdom

Warned of a plot against him, the saint left the village in secret with his brother Abd al-Ghani, his wife and his young son. At the foot of the mountain, in the very midst of a waterless wilderness, he pitched his tent, at the place where Nigaha now stands and where his tomb is. Tradition holds that by the blessing of his step God made a spring flow there out of the desert, and in the 1870s the spring was still there.

There he was martyred, in 577 AH by the Islamic calendar. Accounts differ over who killed him: some tell of his jealous kinsmen, who learned that he had gone, gathered a great company and rode after him; others of a fight with the Khokhars that broke out by chance, in which he and his brother were martyred. His wife and young son died too, with him or after him, and accounts of their deaths differ. The saint and his family were buried at the foot of the hills, and the tomb became known as the tomb of the martyr.

His memory was also set in verse. A Persian chronogram, a verse whose letters, read as numbers, yield a date, names him Sayyid, Sarwar and Sakhi Ahmad:

سید و سرور و سخی احمد
بود در ملک سروری والی
رفت چوں از جہاں بخلد بریں
شد زمین از وجود او خالی
سال تاریخ وصل آں سرور
گفت سرور کہ سرور عالی

The words that carry its date are its last two, *sarwar-e ali*. A second chronogram, written for his death, joins the names Sarwar and Sakhi Ahmad with the title *Sultan-e Alam*.

## The Shrine

The shrine complex, set in the hills, comprises the saint's tomb together with a mosque and associated buildings. The *darbar*, as the shrine is called, remains the defining landmark of its town. The tomb lies forty *kos*, the old measure of road distance, west of Multan, at the foot of the hills of Baluchistan.

In the reign of Aurangzeb the tomb was kept by the descendants of three men, one blind, one a leper and one impotent, who, it is said, had taken up attendance at the tomb and been cured. Devotees also tell of Shaikh Garha, who held the saint's horse while he prayed, and who brought people from the village of Kotli to sweep the *khanqah*, the lodge where a Sufi's disciples gather.

The hills of Baluchistan, at whose foot the tomb stands, were the Baloch borderland of the old *suba*, or province, of Multan. In the time of the Langahs, Sultan Husain Langah granted the country from Karorkot to Mankot in *jagir*, a grant of land and its revenue, to Malik Sohrab and other Baloch who had come to him from Kech Makran. In the reign of the emperor Akbar, Raja Todar Mal, the chief *diwan*, or minister of revenue, confirmed that country to the Baloch and set it as a strong outpost and barrier between Khurasan and Hindustan.

## Devotion and Pilgrimage

His devotees, the *Sultanias* or *Sarwarias*, carried his banners in seasonal pilgrimages, and until modern times his following crossed the boundaries of religion, with Hindus and Sikhs of the Punjab honouring him alongside Muslims; the Sikh tradition itself preserves references to the *Sakhi Sarwar* pilgrims. This shared devotion made him one of the great examples of the composite religious culture of the old Punjab. Devotees come to seek blessing, to fulfil vows and to honour the *Lakhdata*, and the annual pilgrimage and the *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of the saint's death, are great occasions of music, offering and celebration in the regional manner.

In the reign of Aurangzeb multitudes came from every region and corner of the world, gave *nazrs*, votive offerings, and asked for their wishes, which, it was said, were granted. The crowds were greatest at the beginning of winter, and the whole road from Multan to the tomb was full of people, a crowd beyond what writing could set down. The tomb is said to have become a place of pilgrimage after the healing of a Qandahar merchant's camel. In the same reign a place twelve *kos* from Sialkot, associated with Sultan Sarwar, was always a place of pilgrimage too: in summer people came to it from every region and offered *nazrs*, and for two months there was a great throng.

In the nineteenth century a caravan of the black blanket, its people in black caps, stopped in Lahore and went on towards a place some *kos* from Multan. When his pilgrims set out, they halted for a night at the sarai west of Jahangir's tomb in Lahore, and there were ten days of great festivity. It was said that in every village a house of the saint had been built.

Lahore also had a *chauki* of Sakhi Sarwar, a building in his name. In the nineteenth century it stood south of the sarai of Muhammad Shafi in the Anarkali Sadr Bazaar: a building shaped like a *kotha*, or house, with niches for lamps and a wooden door that had once been white and had turned black. It was not his tomb, nor any true place of his retreat. It had been built in his name, it was said, out of worldly desire, and it belonged to six men.

In the early twenty first century his shrine was one of a few Muslim places in Pakistan where Baisakhi, the spring harvest festival of the Punjab, was still celebrated, despite its being considered a non-Muslim celebration. Like other major shrines, it has been touched by the violence of recent years. On 3 April 2011 a suicide bombing at the shrine struck a procession of its pilgrims, and around fifty people were killed. Yet the devotion has continued.

## Legends

Devotees tell a long cycle of his miracles, set in his village and in Multan. In the village, it is said, goats were brought back to life by divine power, and a goat was given as an offering of thanks, while the villagers said that these were sorcerers. When the land was divided he answered that it did not matter, for it was their right, and the wheat he held in trust as a *faqir*, a mystic vowed to spiritual poverty, thrived. He killed a lion, and gave the heap away in the way of God to the *lagis*, the village servants who received a customary share of the harvest.

At Multan, the story goes on, the ruler saw an army and found a man with a shade over him. Sealed vessels that had been left empty were filled by the grace of God, and there was food of paradise. The saint's brothers were jailed, and he was dismissed with a splendid robe of honour. He said that all the prisoners in the jail were his brothers, so all were freed and the *nazr* was shared. *Faqirs* came asking for *iftar*, the meal that breaks a fast. The robe was cut into rags, and the horse was eaten, and by divine power the horse rose up alive and whole. His father, Zain al-Abidin, refused such a king, and the whole city was in uproar. The ruler offered a princess in marriage. Then all the *awliya* and *aqtab*, the saints and the poles of sainthood, came to the gate of Multan; in a contest of archery a khan missed the mark and a holy man hit it, and the *milni*, the meeting of the two families at a wedding, followed.

Devotees tell, too, of his martyrdom. A rider on the plumed mare killed a number of the enemy and was martyred, and the saint, at prayer while Shaikh Garha held his horse, destroyed their army. Then he turned to an old man, told him that he was his killer, and bade him come and martyr him, and he ordered Shaikh Garha and the *mukabbir*, who repeats aloud at prayer the call of God's greatness, to bury him. His widow, Bibi Bai, refused to marry again, and the grave opened and took her; her son Sayyid Raj, six months old, was swallowed after her.

## Legacy

Sultan Sakhi Sarwar is remembered as one of the most widely venerated saints of the old western Punjab, the open-handed *Lakhdata* whose cult, crossing the lines of Muslim, Hindu and Sikh, embodied the shared sacred world of the region before the divisions of modern times. After his death his shrine became the centre of a devotional cult of remarkable reach and endurance. In the reign of Aurangzeb crowds filled the road from Multan to his tomb; in the nineteenth century his pilgrims halted in Lahore and a house of the saint stood in every village; and in the early twenty first century Baisakhi was still kept at his shrine. His shrine at Nigaha endures as a major centre of pilgrimage and as a monument to that older, composite devotion.
