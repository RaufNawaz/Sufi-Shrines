## Overview

At Jhok Sharif, in the Sujawal region of lower Sindh, lies the shrine of Sufi Shah Inayat Shaheed, a Sufi saint who died in 1718 and is remembered not only for his mysticism but for a remarkable social experiment and a martyr's death. He gathered around him a community that farmed the land in common, under the principle that the one who sows should eat, and this challenge to the entrenched order of landlords and rulers led, after a long siege, to his execution. He is honoured today as a saint, a martyr and, in modern eyes, a pioneer of social justice, and his darbar, or shrine, at Jhok is a place of pilgrimage and remembrance.

Shah Inayat of Jhok is famous as a Sindhi Sufi martyr. He wrote verse in Persian, and lines of it are quoted in the *Risalo* of Shah Abdul Latif, the great classic of Sindhi poetry. His *dargah*, the shrine complex at Jhok, is a living centre of initiation, custodianship and song, and most of the Hindu Sufis were in touch with Jhok, directly or indirectly.

## The Saint

Shah Inayat, a Sufi or Muslim mystic, belonged to the tradition of *wahdat al-wujud*, the mysticism of the unity of being, and was schooled in the Sufi currents of his age. He travelled in search of knowledge before returning to his native Jhok in lower Sindh, and there, in the early eighteenth century, he established a *khanqah*, a Sufi lodge where disciples gather. He is known as Shah Inayat of Jhok, or of Jhok Sharif, and by the epithet that the shrine's name also carries, *Shaheed* or Shahid, the martyr.

His own master was Sayyid Shah Mehmud Shah al-Qadiri, a Sufi master from Jhok Sharif who was buried at Bijapur, in Karnataka. The lodge at Jhok belongs to the Qadiri order, the Sufi order founded by Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani, and is tied to it through the holders of its seat. Among his own disciples was Rohal Faqir. Shah Inayat is not to be confused with an earlier poet of a similar name, Shah Inat, a Sindhi Sufi poet of the seventeenth century, also called Sayyid Inayat Allah Rizvi, who lived from 1623 to 1712.

## The Commune and the Martyrdom

Shah Inayat's community at Jhok is remembered as one that held and cultivated its land collectively and shared the produce, a way of life summed up in the Sindhi maxim *jeko kheré, so khaé*, "whoever sows, eats." Accounts of the commune differ, and how far he really aimed at a land reform in the modern sense of the word cannot be known; much that is told in his favour is strongly mixed with legend.

The poor and the landless gathered around him. His way of life, his spirituality and his justice attracted not only Sufi adepts, many of them from the mystical centres of the country, but also labourers, slaves and husbandmen from the neighbouring big farms and villages. The Sufi sheikhs and the feudal lords of the country lost large numbers of disciples and workers, while the dargah at Jhok flourished. After an unjustified attack on Jhok led by a group of Sayyids, descendants of the Prophet Muhammad, a considerable amount of land was distributed among his followers.

Such a gathering was an implicit challenge to the great landholders, the *zamindars*, and to the authorities the Mughals had appointed in the region, and it provoked their hostility. In 1717 he was persecuted under the pretext that he was trying to overthrow the established rule, and this was the first obvious sign of the struggle for power in the lower Indus valley. His adversaries denounced him at court and accused him of conspiracy against the Mughal throne. It was the easiest method of blackmail, for the throne had been in constant danger after Aurangzeb's death, and during the confusion in Delhi between the reigns of Farrukh Siyar and Muhammad Shah it was easy to sow mistrust.

Large armies, gathered from various places in Sindh and in Hind, or India, were sent against Jhok, and the Kalhora clan from upper Sindh, who soon afterwards became independent in Sindh, were particularly active in the struggle against him. Shah Inayat was besieged in his *khanqah*, and Jhok held out for four months. Accounts of how the siege ended differ; he is remembered as having been taken by treachery after that long resistance. He was executed by the Mughal governor in January 1718, and he has been remembered ever after as *Shaheed*, the martyr of Jhok. He was not the first mystical leader in Sindh, or elsewhere in the Muslim world, to engage in politics or social reform.

## The Shrine

The shrine of Sufi Shah Inayat at Jhok Sharif is a revered darbar of lower Sindh, drawing pilgrims who honour the saint and martyr. Jhok Sharif is a village in the far south of Sindh, the home of an important Sufi centre where the saint's *dargah* stands, though accounts describe its exact location in different terms.

The dargah is in the keeping of its *sajjada nashins*, the holders of the seat, and several of the Sufi masters at Jhok have borne the name Abdul Sattar. One of them, Sufi Abd al-Sattar Shah, initiated into the Qadiri path a disciple who spent many years at Jhok Sharif. He set the disciple to cleaning the stable of his master's mare, and when the disciple had reached the highest spiritual degree, that of salvation, he allowed him to give *nam*, the initiatory name, to disciples of his own. Sufi Hazrat Abdul Sattar Shah of Jhok Sharif spoke of *jam wahdat jo*, "the cup of unity". The seat has also been held by Sufi Ataullah Sattari, and Jhok Sharif has an official website in his name.

Three seats in Sindh, those of Daraza, Tando Jahaniya and Jhok Sharif, have *sajjada nashins* who still keep the lineages of Hindu Sufis now in India. A great Sufi poet is attached to each of the three: Sachal Sarmast, Qutub Ali Shah and Nasir Faqir. Nasir Muhammad Faqir, a Qadiri, was the disciple of the *murshid*, the spiritual master, of Jhok Sharif.

## Devotion and Song

The shrine's annual *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of the saint's death, gathers devotees for prayer, Sufi music and remembrance. The voluminous Sindhi verse of one Sufi poet, his *kafis*, short mystical lyrics, is still part of the main poetry sung at the *ʿurs* of Jhok Sharif. The *kalam*, or poetry, of Dalpat Sufi is sung in several dargahs of Sindh run by Muslim *sajjada nashins*, Jhok Sharif among them.

A performance tradition carries the saint's name. In the singing of Sindhi poetry, a style performed on the *capṛi*, the castanets, is known as the tradition of Shah Inayat of Jhok; it is not found in the poetry of Shah Abdul Latif, whose moods are more mellow.

Jhok was a place of initiation for many. At about the age of forty Rakhiyyal Shah adopted the Sufi path and went to the dargah of Jhok Sharif, where he stayed under the guidance of his master. The guru Asardas was a follower of the Sufi masters of Jhok Sharif, Dalpat Sufi was himself a follower of Jhok Sharif, and Bedil also visited Jhok Sharif and Daraza. The poetry of one disciple's own path tells that from his early childhood he had visions of Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani, the founder of the Qadiri order, and that in one of them al-Jilani told him it was time for him to reach Jhok Sharif and take *nam* from the Sufi master there.

The Sufi centre of Jhok Sharif came to play a pivotal role: most of the Hindu Sufis were in touch with Jhok, directly or indirectly, often through their own Sufi master, and Daraza and Jhok were the two main Sufi traditions that were very popular among the Hindus. One Hindu Sufi path counts Shah Inayat of Jhok Sharif among the four masters whose death anniversaries, or *versi*, it keeps.

The dargah's reach crosses the border. After Partition, Molchand was worried that it had become difficult for Hindu Sindhi Sufis to visit the Sufi places in Sindh, now in Pakistan, and a search was made for the grave of Shah Inayat's master at Bijapur. Jhok's official website claims a dargah at Bijapur, that of Hazrat Soofi Abdul Malik, as affiliated with Jhok, and a yearly *melo*, or fair, is kept at Bijapur from the 17th to the 19th of Muharram, as it is at Jhok Sharif. This attempt at creating a Muslim Sufi pilgrimage from Sindh in India is unique, though it does not appear to have been very successful.

## Poetry

Shah Inayat wrote in Persian, and his verse is quoted in the *Risalo* of Shah Abdul Latif, a work with very few Persian quotations. The words *bar khez ba-dih sāqī*, half of a verse, come from one of his *ghazals*, or lyric poems, and a second Persian verse in the *Risalo* is also his. Shah Abdul Latif may have alluded to Shah Inayat's fate in some of his own verses, and he quoted a Persian line usually connected with Shah Inayat which, in English, reads:

*The head is sacrificed at the Friend's foot, / which is in order* (translated by Annemarie Schimmel)

Another Persian verse was tied to his martyrdom by a Sindhi writer, Qani. In his *Maqalat ash-shuʿara* he quoted, in connection with Shah Inayat, a quatrain on the 'kitchen of love' attributed to Sarmad, one of the Sufis in the succession of Hallaj, though the verse itself is much older.

Verse about him followed his death. His fate inspired many Sufi poets, Hindus and Muslims alike, to write touching poetry, and admirers of both faiths composed songs extolling him or describing his martyrdom. Among them was Janullah of Rohri, a mystic of the Suhrawardi order, founded by Shihab al-Din Suhrawardi, and one of the outstanding Persian poets of Sindh in the second half of the eighteenth century. For his death at the hands of the establishment, and for his overwhelming mystical state, Shah Inayat became 'the Hallaj of Sind'. The Sindhi poet Sachal Sarmast speaks of him too, and Sachal's litany of the afflicted tells of ʿInayat killed 'in the fighting arena'.

## The Setting

The epithet 'the Hallaj of Sind' draws on a long memory in the province. Hallaj traversed Sindh shortly after 900, and he is a very popular figure there; many Sufi poets mention his name in their verses, and illiterate folk musicians in remote corners of Sindh repeat it in ecstatic songs, claiming that he was the true lover who paid with his life for his love. Hindu writers of Sindh, too, composed poems in the imagery of the Muslims and devoted ballads to the fate of famous Sufi martyrs.

The Sindh in which Shah Inayat preached was a Mughal province in a time of change. Akbar had added it to the Mughal Empire in the 1590s, and although representatives of the Delhi court came there, the indigenous rulers enjoyed much freedom. The Kalhora ruled as governors from 1701.

The system of land tenure in Sindh, so far as is known, has always been feudal. The deference of the Kalhora to the priestly class led to an extraordinary reverence for *pirs*, the spiritual guides, for Sayyids and for holy men, which marked eighteenth century Sindh above all, and the social religion of the province was built round the reverence of the *murid*, the disciple, for the *murshid*. At the beginning of the eighteenth century a strong influence of the Naqshbandi order, founded by Baha-ud-Din Naqshband, changed the spiritual climate of southern Sindh to a certain extent.

## Legacy

Sufi Shah Inayat Shaheed holds a singular place among the saints of Sindh: a mystic of the unity of being whose commune of shared land and labour, and whose martyrdom in its defence, made him a saint of the poor and a byword for social justice. In the modern age writers, poets and social reformers have celebrated him as an early martyr for the rights of the peasant and the poor against feudal power, so that his shrine is honoured both as a place of mystical devotion and as a symbol of the struggle for justice. He has been called the first social reformer in Sindhi Islam, though progressive writers tend to read him, as they read Hallaj and Bishr al-Hafi, in the light of modern social ideals.

His darbar at Jhok Sharif keeps alive the memory of a holy man who joined the inward path to the cause of the landless, and who paid for it with his life. Jhok stands at the heart of a network that outlived Partition, for the invocation of Jhok Sharif contributes to the construction of a network of sacred territories which are all located in Sindh.
