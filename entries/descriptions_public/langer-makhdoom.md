## Overview

Langar Makhdoom is a village of Chiniot District in the Punjab that takes its name from the Sufi saint to whom its shrine is dedicated, Makhdoom Burhan-ud-din, remembered locally as Hazrat Langar Makhdoom. His shrine is a modest but venerable one of the medieval centuries, traditionally dated to the thirteenth century, and it belongs to the great movement of Sufi settlement that carried Islam into the Punjab countryside. The village's very name keeps the memory of the *langar*, the free communal kitchen that has always stood at the heart of the Sufi hospice, and the shrine remains a focus of local devotion, with gatherings at the tomb on Fridays and the saint's yearly festival in spring.

The village, on the river Chenab, is also remembered as a seat of Sufi teaching: the house of another master, Makhdum Abdul Karim, whose name it once carried as well.

## The Saint

Tradition holds that Makhdoom Burhan-ud-din lived and taught in this corner of the Chiniot region, and that he died in 1245. In his humility, it is said, he asked to be buried in the simple mud dwelling that had been his home, and his grave is said to lie in a burial ground near the village of Changranwala, in the Lalian area of the district. He is not the Burhan al-Din Gharib who lived a century later and far from the Punjab, moving to Daulatabad in 1329, dying in 1337 and lying buried at Khuldabad in the northern Deccan.

In the devotion of the region he belongs to the first generation of Muslim holy men who made their homes among the villages of the central Punjab in the thirteenth century, and his traditional dates place him firmly within that formative era. It was the age of the great Chishti master Farid al-Din Ganj-i-Shakar of nearby Pakpattan, Baba Farid, whose ancestors had come from Kabul and settled in the Punjab in the middle of the twelfth century. In that age, it is held, the teaching and example of the Sufis, rather than the sword of conquerors, drew much of the rural population of the Punjab towards Islam, though there is no settled view of how far Baba Farid himself converted anyone, or of how quickly, and how consciously, the change came. A number of Punjabi tribes still claim to have been converted by Baba Farid, and most of the Jat and Rajput groups that became Muslim in the medieval period credit either him or his contemporary, Baha' al-Haqq Zakariya.

In the invocation that opens his *Hir and Ranjha*, the poet Waris Shah praised Farid, whom he calls Shakarganj, for making his home at Pakpattan:

*"When Shakarganj made his abode at Pakpattan, the Punjab was delivered of all its troubles."* (translated by Charles Frederick Usborne)

## The Shrine and Its Devotion

A mosque bearing the saint's name, the Masjid Burhan-ud-din, stands in his memory. By tradition his devotees gather at his tomb every Friday, the day of congregational prayer, to offer the *fatiha*, the opening chapter of the Qur'an, and to seek the saint's intercession.

His *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of a saint's death, kept in Sufi practice as a joyful "wedding" of the soul with the divine, is observed each spring, traditionally from the sixteenth to the eighteenth of March, and it draws the surrounding countryside to the village.

The name that the saint and his village share is itself telling. Langar Makhdoom means the *langar* of the Makhdoom, a title of honour for a Sufi master. The *langar*, the kitchen where food is cooked and served freely to all who come, whatever their rank or creed, is one of the most characteristic institutions of the Sufi lodge in South Asia, an enactment in daily bread of the ideals of hospitality, charity and the equality of all before God. That the saint should be remembered by this name suggests the reputation for open-handed generosity that clung to his hospice, and it links his memory to the tradition of feeding the poor that Sufi shrines across the Punjab and Sindh keep alive to this day.

## A House of Teaching

The village has another Makhdum in its history. It was the home of Hazrat Makhdum Abdul Karim, remembered as a perfect gnostic, one who knows God, and its name was once written with his, as Langar Makhdum Abdul Karim Sahib.

Shaikh Muhammad Ismail became Abdul Karim's pupil at the age of five, and it was from this master, his enlightened *pir*, or spiritual guide, that he received the *khirqa* of *khilafat*, the cloak by which a master makes a disciple his deputy, in the *Suhrawardiyya*, the Sufi order founded by Shihab al-Din Suhrawardi. In the fourteenth century that order spread from Uch to Gujarat, the Punjab and Kashmir.

Mian Wadda's mother moved with him to Langar Makhdoom, and there he studied with Makhdum Abdul Karim, his *murshid*, or spiritual master. He was first given a task; it is told that a miracle brought that service to an end, and afterwards he was set to study and to cooking for the household. Later, it is said, he was seen carrying the milk vessels and told that he had become a perfect *wali*, a friend of God. It is not settled whether Mian Wadda and Shaikh Muhammad Ismail are one man.

In Mian Wadda's chain of masters, two generations above Abdul Karim, stands a Hazrat Makhdum Burhan al-Din, who bears the saint's name. Nor is it settled whether he is the saint of the shrine.

## The Setting

The village stands on the river Chenab, which in the folk culture of the Punjab is known as the river of love, a river that calls to mind Sohni and Mahiwal and the Takht Hazara of Ranjha.

The town of Chiniot appears in the wars of the Langah sultans of Multan. Sultan Husain, son of Qutb al-Din, took Chiniot from Malik Manjhi Khokhar, the agent of Sayyid Khan. When the army of Barbak Shah was beaten, it fled as far as the town of Chiniot, and Sultan Husain's agent at Chiniot was seized after the battle and sent to the house of nothingness, that is, put to death.

Chiniot is known for its woodwork, and a fine example of it was the *haveli*, or great mansion, of Omar Hayat, completed in 1935. To those who lived in it, it was a cursed house, which claimed the life first of the father, then of the son, and then of his mother; the mother and the son are buried in its main hall. After Partition the haveli fell into disrepair and its top two floors crashed down. More recently the government of Pakistan took it over and renovated it to serve as a museum and a library.

## Legacy

Langar Makhdoom is not among the grand shrines of the Punjab. Yet in this it is representative of a whole class of country shrines whose collective importance is very great: the resting places of the humble Sufi teachers who, village by village, wove Islam into the fabric of Punjabi rural life. Islam took root much more readily in the Punjab, as in Bengal, than in upper India, and in the Punjab and Sind the shrines of great Sufis drew pastoral clans that were not yet farmers, and not Muslim, into their ritual, social, economic and political orbits. The successors of Baba Farid, like those of other shrines in the Punjab, still write *taʿwidh*, written amulets, for ordinary people, a practice that has not changed from the thirteenth century to the present.

The shrine's endurance across some eight centuries of tradition, its Friday gatherings and its spring *ʿurs*, testify to the tenacity of local devotion and to the way a single holy man's memory can give a village its name and its identity. The village is remembered, too, as a seat of teaching: the house of Makhdum Abdul Karim, where Mian Wadda served and studied, and from whom Shaikh Muhammad Ismail received his *khilafat* in the Suhrawardiyya.
