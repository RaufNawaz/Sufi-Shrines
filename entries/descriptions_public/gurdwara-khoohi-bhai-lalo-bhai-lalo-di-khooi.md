## Overview

Gurdwara Khoohi Bhai Lalo, also called Bhai Lalo di Khooi, stands in the old town of Eminabad and marks the home and the narrow well, the *khooi*, of Bhai Lalo, the honest carpenter with whom, tradition holds, Guru Nanak, the founder of the Sikh faith, lodged when he came to the town in the early sixteenth century. The site is inseparable from one of the most famous of all the *sakhis*, the stories of the Guru's life: the story of Malik Bhago's feast and of the milk and the blood. It is part of Pakistan's Sikh heritage and is visited by pilgrims, usually in the same round as the nearby Chakki Sahib and Rori Sahib shrines.

Bhai Lalo is traditionally said to have been born in 1452 into a family of carpenters at Saidpur, the old town held to be Eminabad. He became one of the most beloved of the Guru's early companions, and his house grew into a *dharamsala*, a gathering place for the local followers of Guru Nanak. The story of the milk and the blood, in which, as tradition tells it, the Guru preferred the labouring carpenter's plain bread to a rich official's feast, has ever since stood at the heart of the Sikh ethic of *kirat karni*, of earning one's bread by honest labour and sharing it with others.

## The Guru

Guru Nanak's dates are most often given as 1469 to 1539. He is counted the first Guru of the Sikhs and is believed to be the founder of Sikhism. His father was Kalyan Chand Das Bedi, called Mehta Kalu. He spent a major portion of his life in what is today Pakistan: he was born there, and he eventually settled in a small village that is now on the Pakistani side of the border. In Aurangzeb's reign his descendants were still living at his house on the bank of the Ravi, twelve *kos*, an old measure of distance, from Batala.

Before his travels the Guru worked at Sultanpur. In the *janamsakhis*, the cycles of tales told of the first Guru's life, he moved there, some 60 kilometres from Talwandi, as keeper of Nawab Daulat Khan's store, bathing in the Bein and gathering people in the evenings to hear his hymns. Vanishing for a week in the stream, the janamsakhis tell, he came back to say: "There is only one God. There is no Hindu, no Mussalman." It is widely believed that it was at Sultanpur that his religious life began. In the same telling he set out on his travels dressed in a strange garb, an approximate combination of what the holy men of Hinduism and of Islam would wear.

Accounts of how many journeys he made differ, but his *udasis*, the long journeys of his travelling years, are most often counted as four, and they lasted more than twenty years. He travelled mostly on foot, and with his companion Mardana he would cover thousands of kilometres. He preferred to stay on the outskirts of villages, and whenever he visited an area he would visit its religious shrines, Muslim, Hindu and of any other denomination.

He offended both Hindus and Muslims by stating that there was no Hindu or Muslim, and his advice to his two companions was that "if one is a Muslim then one should be a good Muslim and if one is a Hindu then one should be a good Hindu". He associated with people of the lower castes, calling them people of God, and he criticised high caste landlords and government officials as exploiters. He lived as a householder, and he taught that all men and women were equal, irrespective of caste, creed, wealth, social standing and gender, and that a good life rested on honest labour, compassion, selfless service and prayer. Nanak himself is held to have abhorred the association of miracles with religious personalities, yet the stories of his life are full of them. In one of those stories he tells Karori, who calls his cure a miracle, that "there is no such thing as a miracle".

On his deathbed the Guru appointed not his son but his most loyal student as his spiritual successor: Lehna, to whom at the hour of death he gave the title of Guru Angad, or Guru Angad Dev. His son Lakhmi Das, it was said, was a worthy son, but spiritual fortune was not his.

## Bhai Lalo

Bhai Lalo, the shrine's second figure, is the one who gives it its name. He was a carpenter of the *Getaura* caste, though accounts of his caste differ. In the janamsakhi telling he tells Nanak that people call him Lalo and that he is a carpenter. In the same telling he works on a daily wage for Ramdev, the master carpenter, and cannot afford to miss a single day's work. The stories draw his poverty in a few objects: a single oil lamp, a loosely strung string bed or *charpoy*, a single thick *bajre ki roti* of millet, and a single small onion.

In the janamsakhi telling the Guru came to Saidpur twice. The first time was early in the first of his journeys, when he came from Sultanpur by way of Talwandi. The second was at the end of the fourth, when he came home by Iran, Kabul, Peshawar, Kandahar, Hasan Abdal, Tilla Balnath and Katasraj to Saidpur, and went on to Kartarpur; on that journey, the stories tell, he took a south eastern route to the Punjab about the time of Babur's third incursion into India.

The janamsakhis tell that on the first visit Lalo warned Nanak that by staying with him he risked being cast out of his own *biradari*, his kinship group. Nanak answered, it is told, that "in God's eyes a man's worth is never measured in terms of his caste or social status or his wealth". In the same telling Lalo sings a *bhajan*, a devotional song, of Kabir as he sweeps, and Nanak asks him to lead "one of Bhagat Kabir's hymns". At Lalo's house, the story goes on, Nanak himself sings and the villagers gather, and the hymn he sings opens with the words of the creed, *Ik Onkar Satnam*, there is only one God. Lalo, it is told, marvels at the prescience the Guru displays.

## Malik Bhago's Feast

Malik Bhago, tradition holds, was a wealthy official of Eminabad who held a great feast, and he was affronted when the Guru preferred the plain fare of the labouring carpenter to his own rich table. Summoned to explain himself, Guru Nanak is remembered to have taken Bhai Lalo's coarse bread in one hand and Malik Bhago's fine bread in the other and pressed them. From the honest man's bread, the story goes, there flowed milk, and from the oppressor's, blood. It was a sign, the Guru is said to have explained, that Lalo's food was earned by the sweat of honest toil, while Bhago's was wrung from the exploitation of the poor.

In the janamsakhis Malik Bhago, humbled, came to realise the Guru's spiritual depth. The Guru's teaching that there was no Hindu and no Muslim, it is told, was questioned at three courts, and one of them was the court of Malik Bhago in Eminabad.

## The Gurdwara

Bhai Lalo's house, where tradition holds that Guru Nanak lodged, was made into a gurdwara, a Sikh house of worship, remembered as Bhai Lalu Di Khoi, and the well from which the Guru is said to have drunk is still preserved within it.

After the Partition of 1947 a refugee family of Muslim migrants occupied the building and lived inside it until 1989; accounts of its care after Partition differ. In 1989 Sardar Surjit Singh Panesar, a Sikh, bought the property with funds raised by the *Sangat*, the Sikh congregation, abroad, and restored it, and it was opened for worship. At the restoration the well was covered over with a platform.

One visitor came to the gurdwara through the town. Old men in a market guided him and his companion into an alley, where there was a lock at the entrance of the gurdwara, the site of the house of Bhai Lalo. Far from being a worn down place, it was in good shape, counted among the most popular Sikh shrines in Pakistan and visited by hundreds of devotees of Nanak. The lock was not a chance of the day. The gurdwara was kept locked at all times, and the keys were probably with members of the Sikh community at the nearby Gurdwara Rori Sahib.

## Devotion and Pilgrimage

The gurdwara is a place of pilgrimage. At pilgrimage time several Sikhs came to pay tribute to the memory of Guru Nanak and Bhai Lalo, and pilgrims usually visit it in the same round as the nearby Chakki Sahib and Rori Sahib shrines. Sikhs from abroad came to Pakistan for festivals such as Baisakhi and the birthday of Guru Nanak, which were attended by thousands of Indian pilgrims.

In Aurangzeb's reign the followers of Baba Nanak, as the Guru was also called, counted service to the traveller, to whoever comes and goes, as the highest worship. Whoever arrived at midnight speaking Baba Nanak's name they treated as a brother and a friend and served, even a stranger, a thief, a highwayman or an evildoer. The sum of their worship was the reading of their master's verses, which they sang with song and melody. The tradition of *langar*, the free communal kitchen, is believed to have begun with Guru Nanak.

## The Guru's Verse

The poem most closely bound to Bhai Lalo is the Guru's *Babur Bani*, a long poem that Nanak wrote at the time the Mughal Babur attacked the Punjab, destroying the cities of Eminabad and Lahore on the way. In it Nanak lays the blame of Babur's wrath at God's feet, and again and again the poem addresses Lalo by name:

ਵੇ ਲਾਲੋ

*O Lalo*

Accounts of the year of Babur's invasion differ; one of its lines, in English translation, gives the poem's own reckoning:

ਆਵਨਿ ਅਠਤਰੈ …

*Coming in seventy-eight (1521 C.E)*

The Guru composed verses in Hindi on the truth of God, setting out divine unity. His *bani*, devotional verses of supplication, entreaty, humility and reverence, was sung by Mardana, who drew people into the net of faith. Lines from his hymns, in English translation, give a sense of the poetry. His *aarti*, the hymn sung as lamps are waved in worship, begins:

ਗਗਨ ਮੈ ਥਾਲੁ
ਰਵਿ ਚੰਦੁ ਦੀਪਕ ਬਨੇ …

*In the salver of the firmament, / The sun and the moon shine as lamps*

On the oneness of God he sang:

ਸਾਹਿਬੁ ਮੇਰਾ ਏਕੋ ਹੈ ॥
ਏਕੋ ਹੈ ਭਾਈ ਏਕੋ ਹੈ ॥

*My Master is One / He is One, brother, the only One*

And again:

*God is One, / The Path is One*

His poem on the sacred thread, the *janeu*, runs:

ਦਇਆ ਕਪਾਹ
ਸੰਤੋਖੁ ਸੂਤੁ …

*From the cotton of compassion, / Spin the thread of contentment*

Of washing he sang:

ਭਰੀਐ ਹਥੁ ਪੈਰੁ ਤਨੁ ਦੇਹ ॥
ਪਾਣੀ ਧੋਤੈ ਉਤਰਸੁ ਖੇਹ ॥

*As hands or feet besmirched with slime, / Water washes white*

The verse is sung beyond the gurdwaras too. The singer Abida Parvin has said: "I sing Shah Hussain, Bulle Shah, Mian Muhammad Bakhsh, Hazrat Khawaja Ghulam Farid, Sacchal Sarmast, Baba Farid, and Guru Nanak." In Sindh, Sain Vasan Shah became famous for reciting the *banis* of Shri Guru Nanak and the *kalams*, the poems, of the Sufis.

## Eminabad and Saidpur

Eminabad is held to be the old Saidpur, renamed by Babur when he took over the city, though accounts of how the town came by its name differ. The town suffered when Babur came, for he destroyed the cities of Eminabad and Lahore. Coming back from eastern India, the Guru found the Punjab plunged into a number of political crises: Babur, after two minor raids, invaded the Punjab a third time, reaching as far as Sialkot.

The janamsakhis record a meeting between Nanak and Babur at Saidpur, in which Babur asks for a blessing to conquer India and Nanak rebukes him. The encounter was also taken to mark the beginning of the antagonism between the Mughals and the Sikhs, and Babur, the great grandfather of the emperor Jahangir, is remembered as having imprisoned Guru Nanak.

The cone like spires of ancient Hindu temples rose among the houses of Eminabad, in contrast to the Muslim architecture that had come to dominate the town. It was once an important trading centre on the Grand Trunk Road, which connected with Lahore and then Delhi; the highway has since moved south, and Gujranwala, not far away, has replaced Eminabad as a trading centre. One temple, abandoned in 1947, still looked sturdy, though its paint had worn off and a corrosive layer had formed on it. After the creation of Pakistan such temples were taken over by migrants from across the border. The same visitor looked for signs of attack on them after the destruction of the Babri mosque in 1992 and found none, and much of the town's historical architecture had been preserved.

Gujranwala lies in the heart of the Punjab, 75 kilometres from the provincial capital. Maharaja Ranjit Singh started from a small fief around it, and he allotted Baba Sahib Singh Bedi land around the cities of Gujranwala and Lahore.

## Legacy

Gurdwara Khoohi Bhai Lalo keeps the memory of a carpenter's house in which, as the stories tell it, the founder of the Sikh faith chose honest bread over a rich man's table. Lalo's name lives on, too, in the Guru's own lament for Babur's invasion, which turns again and again to "O Lalo".

Without the family that kept it, Ram Thamman, another shrine in the province, would also have been taken over by refugees and converted into a household, like the hundreds of temples and gurdwaras all over Pakistani Punjab, and across the country several gurdwaras and temples were taken over and destroyed by migrants. Yet at several sites all over the country where Nanak stayed or is said to have performed miracles, the gurdwaras built there later by his devotees still stand tall.

Muslims have claimed the Guru too. Many Muslims acquainted with the teachings of Guru Nanak are devoted to him and visit his shrine; to one Muslim painter, Guru Nanak is like a Muslim Sufi saint, and a Muslim storyteller at Gurdwara Sachkand called him "a true devotee of God. He was a rightly guided soul." A Muslim tradition holds that Guru Nanak was born by the blessing of Baba Naulakh Hazari; the Sikhs do not accept it. Baba Farid, it is said, became an inspiration for Guru Nanak when Nanak visited his shrine on his travels, and Farid's texts are revered among the Sikhs, for he is held to have influenced the founder of their religion. A twentieth century Chishti Sabiri shaykh counted Guru Nanak, with Krishna, Rama, Sita and Lachman, among the spiritual luminaries outside Islam who believed in the one true God and had a "Divine connection", *nisbat*. At the shrine of the goddess Hinglaj, the engraved footsteps of Guru Nanak are found.
