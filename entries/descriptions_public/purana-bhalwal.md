## Overview

At Purana Bhalwal, the old settlement that gives its name to the modern town of Bhalwal in Sargodha District, stands the shrine of Hazrat Shah Sulaiman Nuri, a Sufi of the sixteenth century who belonged to the *Qadiriyya*, the Sufi order founded by ʿAbd al-Qadir Gilani. Locally he is remembered with the affectionate title *Sakhi Badshah*, 'the generous king'. His *dargah*, the shrine complex of a Sufi saint, is the spiritual heart of an otherwise unassuming corner of central Punjab, drawing devotees from the surrounding villages of the Chaj Doab, the fertile tract between the Jhelum and the Chenab.

Shah Sulaiman is remembered for a life of devotion, teaching and open-handed hospitality, and for his place in the spiritual line from which the Naushahi order of the Punjab began. Devotees gather at his tomb, above all for his *urs*, the yearly commemoration of his death.

## The Saint

His devotees remember Shah Sulaiman as a scholar and an ascetic trained in the *Qadiri* path. His father is remembered as Shaikh Abdullah, known as Mian Mangu, or simply Mangu, and is said to have lived in the village of Bhalwal. Accounts of the family's caste differ. Besides Nuri, the saint is also known as Shah Sulaiman Qadri.

At the shrine his spiritual guide, or *pir*, is remembered as Shah Maroof Khushabi of the nearby town of Khushab. It is related that this teacher, recognising his disciple's attainment, directed him to settle at Purana Bhalwal and to make it the centre of his teaching and service. There Shah Sulaiman is said to have spent the rest of his life in devotion, in instruction and in feeding travellers and the poor, and it is from this open-handed hospitality that his title *Sakhi Badshah* is popularly derived.

It is said that he listened to *sama*, the spiritual listening of the Sufi music session, and that in *wajd*, the ecstasy of the mystic, he would fall so senseless that he reached the condition of death. Thousands are said to have entered his *bai'at*, the pledge by which a disciple is initiated by his *pir*, and to have reached high degrees, and his whole life was spent in guiding students.

Tradition gives him a remarkably long life, from the early sixteenth century until 1604. Accounts of the year of his death differ; the year the shrine keeps is 1604, which falls in the Hijri year 1012. He is said to have died at a great age on the night of the 27th of Ramadan, one of the most sacred nights of the Muslim calendar, a detail that has only deepened the reverence in which he is held.

## Master and Disciple

Of the Qadiri order in the Punjab, the first master to arrive is held to have been Hazrat Sayyid Muhammad Ghaus Uchchi Jili Gilani, whose ancestors had left Baghdad in the time of Hulagu Khan. He came by way of Khurasan to Multan and settled at Uch, which he entered in 887 AH.

Shah Sulaiman stands at a turning in the history of the Sufi orders of the Punjab. The shrine remembers Shah Maroof Khushabi as his *pir*; his spiritual line is most often traced from Shah Maruf Chishti, a master who held authority to initiate disciples in both the Qadiri and the Chishti orders, and Shah Sulaiman is counted his *khalifa*, a spiritual deputy of his master. Shah Maruf is said to have marked Sulaiman out while he was still a child.

From Shah Sulaiman the line passed to Haji Muhammad Naushah Ganj Bakhsh, also known as Shaikh Haji Muhammad Qadri, who was brought to him by Mulla Karim al-Din. With Naushah Ganj Bakhsh the Naushahi way began, and in the 1870s its *faqirs*, or mystics, in the Punjab were counted in the thousands. The same line, from Maruf Chishti through Shah Sulaiman Nuri to Naushah Ganj Bakhsh, appears in the *shajra*, or spiritual pedigree, of one Bhage Shah.

## The Shrine and Its Devotion

The shrine at Purana Bhalwal follows the familiar pattern of the Punjab's smaller Sufi *dargahs*. A whitewashed tomb chamber shelters the grave, within a courtyard where devotees gather to offer the *Fatiha*, the opening chapter of the Qur'an, to tie threads of intention and to distribute food.

The saint's *urs* marks the anniversary of his death, understood in Sufi thought as the soul's union with the Divine, and it is the principal event of the devotional year. Then *qawwals*, the singers of *qawwali*, the devotional music of the Sufis, perform, and *langar*, the free communal meal, is served to all who come.

The gathering draws on the devotional traditions of the Punjab. *Qawwali* has two principal regions, Uttar Pradesh and the Punjab, and Punjabi tunes tend to be organised rhythmically by stress, with the drum stressing the beats of the metre more strongly than in Uttar Pradesh. The Punjab, like Sind, has also produced hymns in praise of the great masters of the mystical orders, especially ʿAbd al-Qadir Gilani, in imagery that anyone in the village could easily understand.

For the cultivators of the district the saint remains a figure of intercession and blessing, invoked at times of illness, harvest and family need, in the manner characteristic of the living shrine culture of rural Punjab. The shrine remains in active use today, cared for by the descendants and devotees of the saint and, like many such sites, under the general oversight of the provincial Auqaf administration, the government department of religious endowments.

## The Setting

Bhalwal grew as an agricultural market town under the canal colonies of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. As the newer town expanded around the railway, the shrine anchored the older village of Purana, or 'old', Bhalwal.

Across the province, the British linked the five rivers of the Punjab with one another by canals, turning jungle into farmland. Thousands of new villages, called *chaks*, were founded, and farming families from all over the Punjab were invited to settle in them. The land's former inhabitants had been mainly nomads who could not prove that they owned it, and it was taken from them and allotted to the newcomers.

Before the canals, under the Mughals, the *suba*, or province, of Lahore was divided into five *doabs*, the tracts between its rivers. Its wells were each worked by a wheel of 360 pieces of wood carrying more than a hundred small pots and turned by a pair of oxen, while the *kharif* and *rabi* harvests, of autumn and of spring, and the cheapness of grain depended on the rain.

## Legacy

Shah Sulaiman Nuri belongs to the dense network of Qadiri and Chishti saints whose shrines dot the Chaj and Rechna Doabs, forming a sacred geography that shaped the religious life of the Punjab long before modern boundaries were drawn. He never won the fame of a Data Ganj Bakhsh or a Baba Farid beyond his own region, but his *dargah* shows how Sufi devotion took root at the local level, giving small communities their own centre of blessing and continuity. In the history of the orders he is remembered as the link between Shah Maruf Chishti and Naushah Ganj Bakhsh, with whom the Naushahi way began.

In 1875 a book on the saints of the Punjab gave Shah Sulaiman a notice of his own. The book closes with chronograms for its own completion, verses whose letters give a year. One of them, by Mufti Ghulam Safdar Lahori, whom the book names as the author's son, sets out its scope, the saints of the Punjab:

جو خدا کے دوست تھے پنجاب میں
حال ان کا اس سے ہوتا ہے عیان

*A chronogram couplet on the book's scope, the saints of Punjab; the closing chronograms read 1292 AH.*
