## Overview

The shrine of Hazrat Bulleh Shah stands in the heart of Kasur, south of Lahore, over the grave of Sayyid Abdullah Shah Qadiri, the towering Punjabi Sufi poet whose *kafis*, short sung lyrics, are among the most loved verses ever composed in the language. Heir to the tradition of Shah Hussain and forerunner of Waris Shah, Bulleh Shah wrote verse that scorned the hypocrisy of the learned, dissolved the boundaries of caste and creed, and sang of a love for God that overturns the self. Pilgrims and lovers of his poetry come to his tomb alike, and his songs are sung there by the shrine's own *qawwals*, the singers of Sufi devotional music, as they are by folk singers and pop musicians across South Asia.

By the 1870s the tomb was a place of pilgrimage for the people, and his Punjabi verses and *kafis* on *tauhid*, the oneness of God, were sung by *qawwals* in the gatherings of the *faqirs*, the wandering Sufis. Still more than his tomb, his poetry is a living presence, and Kasur itself is known above all as the place where he lies.

## The Saint

Bulleh Shah was born around 1680 into a respected family of Sayyids, descendants of the Prophet Muhammad. His given name was Abdullah Shah: Bullha is the familiar form of Abdullah, and Bulleh Shah the honorific form proper to a Sayyid, the name by which he is known. He is generally said to have been born at Uch Gilanian, the city of saints in Bahawalpur, though neither the year nor the place of his birth is known for certain, and the family is said to descend from Abdul Qadir Gilani, the founder of the Qadiri order of Sufis.

His father, Sayyid Darvesh Sakhi Muhammad, was a learned and pious man, an imam and a teacher. Accounts of the family's moves differ, but most say that it settled in the Kasur region of the Punjab when the boy was six, and there he received a strictly orthodox religious training. His first teacher was his father, and he became well educated in Arabic and Persian and in the religious sciences. At Kasur he is most often said to have studied under Hafiz Ghulam Murtaza, a scholar for whose learning the town was famed, and tradition holds that this teacher trained two rebellious pupils, Bulleh Shah and the poet Waris Shah.

He might have become a scholar of the orthodox kind, but he was drawn instead to the burning inward path. Having finished with the outward sciences, he longed for the inward ones, which cannot be had without a perfect guide, and he gave himself to the search for one. Accounts differ over how he found his master. It is told that, asleep under a tree, he saw Sayyid Abd al-Hakim, his ancestor five generations back, seated on a throne that flew through the air. The throne came down and the elder asked who he was, and he answered that he was a Sayyid. When they met, the elder asked for water and was given milk; he drank a little of the bowl and returned the rest, and when the young man drank it he was carried out of himself and his breast was filled with light. On parting, the elder said that he had held something in trust for him and had that day handed it over, assigned him ten rupees a day, and ordered him to seek a *murshid*, a perfect spiritual guide.

His father gladly gave him leave to go to Shah Inayat Qadiri, and gave him some rupees and a *dastar*, a turban, to present to the master. Shah Inayat was a Sufi master of Lahore of the Qadiri and Shattari line, and an Arain, of a caste of gardeners and cultivators, rather than a Sayyid of high birth. Bulleh Shah was initiated at his hand in Lahore, taking *bai'at*, the oath by which a disciple pledges himself to his *pir*, his spiritual guide, and was afterwards given the robe of a *khalifa*, a deputy who carries on the master's line. Accounts of his own order differ; he is most often counted a Qadiri, a follower of the order of his master.

That a proud Sayyid should bow to a master of humbler caste scandalised his relations, and the tension became one of the great themes of his verse and of the stories told of his life. In one poem his sisters and his brothers' wives reproach him: "stop clinging to the Arain's skirt. Why have you brought disgrace on the family of the Prophet and the descendants of Ali?" He answers them:

*May anyone who calls us a Sayyid suffer the punishments of hell. / Swings are set up in paradise for anyone who calls us an Arain.* (translated by Christopher Shackle)

In another translation of the same scene, the women of the household tell him that "it's a sin to condemn the prophet's kin", and he replies:

*Does sanctity deserve such scorn? / It's God's carelessness where one is born. … / My caste? I've none. I'm just a man.* (translated by Taufiq Rafat)

Elsewhere he asks: "What ecstatic being is here who is so drenched in oneness for caste he has no care?" Love, not lineage, he defiantly proclaimed, was the only nobility. From Shah Inayat he received the word, the *kalma*, of his Sufi teaching. After a time in his master's service he settled on the bank of the Chenab, given to worship and spiritual exertion, and then made his home at Kasur once more, teaching, instructing and preaching. He had a great love of *sama*, the Sufi practice of listening to music, knew the art of music well and was a poet too. Aware of the Qadiri custom of *sama* and devotional dance, he took up the *sarangi*, the stringed instrument associated with the wandering dervish, and began to sing and dance. He loved his master with a rapturous love, and "love" was the word he used for what he felt towards his teacher. By the 1870s he was remembered as Shah Inayat's *khalifa*, living in the small town of Kasur, with *sama* and *wajd*, ecstasy, his practice day and night, and marvels often coming from him unbidden. Bulleh Shah remained unmarried and left no direct descendants.

His times were violent. He lived through the turbulent decades of the Mughal decline, a time of great social upheaval in the Punjab, when the authority of the declining Mughal empire was challenged by armed Sikh rebellion and by local notables, and they were hard times for the Qadiri order. When the last great Mughal ruler, Aurangzeb, died in 1707, Bulleh Shah was 27 years old. Sikh bands under Banda Bairagi, who lived from 1670 to 1716, laid waste the whole province except Lahore and Kasur, and he lived to see the second and third invasions of the Punjab by Ahmad Shah Abdali, in 1748 and 1752. When plunder became routine in the Punjab, he had no choice of factions, and he died, it is said, a disenchanted man. Like Shah Abdul Latif in Sind and Shah Waliullah, Mir Dard and Mazhar Janjanan in Delhi, he was a witness of an epoch of great political disasters, and like these mystics he found peace in the inner world of love and surrender, singing his mystical songs to console himself and his friends.

These unsettled conditions are only occasionally reflected in his poetry, whose main focus is his spiritual guide. He never directly criticised the rulers, though he appears to have been deeply bruised by the upheavals in the Punjab. One *kafi* speaks of the century itself, the twelfth of the Hijri era, which corresponds to the eighteenth century of the Common Era:

*The twelfth century has come with gaping mouth. / The door of doom and torment has opened, and Panjab is in a bad state. / It has been struck by fear of the pit of hell.* (translated by Christopher Shackle)

He died at Kasur. Accounts of the year of his death differ; the year his tomb keeps is 1171 of the Hijri calendar, which corresponds to 1757 or 1758. The tomb gives it in a Persian chronogram, a phrase or verse whose letters, read as numbers, yield the date. Tradition holds that the orthodox at first refused him burial in the common graveyard, so fierce had been his attacks on their hypocrisy, and that his body lay out in the sun while the mullahs disputed his right to be buried there.

## The Poetry

Bulleh Shah's fame rests on his *kafis*, short sung Punjabi lyrics of astonishing directness and power. The *kafi* is the primary genre of Sufi poetry in Punjabi. It is designed for singing in *qawwali*, the devotional music of the Sufis, and also lends itself to solo singing by devotees and professional performers. Its final verse is regularly marked by the poet's signature, his own name. In his *kafis* he mocks the empty learning of the mullah and the pandit, tramples the divisions of Hindu and Muslim, high and low, and insists that the seeker must lose the self entirely in the Beloved.

His most famous *kafi* asks the question at the heart of all mysticism, who am I, and answers by refusing every label of religion and identity:

بُلھیا! کی جاݨاں میں کوݨ
نہ میں مومن وِچ مسیتاں، نہ میں وِچ کُفر دِیاں ریتاں

*Bulleh! What do I know of who I am? / I am not the believer in the mosque, nor am I in the ways of unbelief.*

The poem has attained the status of a proverb. Its speaker is "neither an Arab nor from Lahore, nor an Indian from the city of Nagaur". The earliest heterodox tradition he took in was that of the Persian mystic poet Rumi, who lived from 1207 to 1273, and this *kafi* is often said to be modelled on Rumi's great Persian ghazal that begins: "What can I do, oh Muslims, for I do not know myself. I am not a Christian nor a Jew, not a Zoroastrian nor a Muslim."

The refusal of labels comes again and again:

*I am not a Hindu, nor a Muslim. I have forsaken pride and become unsullied. / I am not a Sunni, nor a Shia. I have adopted the path of peace toward all.* (translated by Christopher Shackle)

"Bullha is not a Shia or a Sunni, neither a learned scholar nor a magician or philosopher." He sets aside, too, the ranks of caste and the rules of purity:

*I am not twice-born, nor am I thrice-born. / I do not recognize pure and impure.* (translated by Christopher Shackle)

Elsewhere he turns on dry scholarship with a cry that has become proverbial, insisting that a single letter of love outweighs a library of knowledge:

عِلموں بس کریں او یار

*Enough of learning, my friend / Only one letter 'A' is all you need* (translated by Naveed Alam)

The same quarrel with the schoolroom runs through his portraits of himself. In one poem he is the pupil who cannot get past *alif*, the first letter of the alphabet:

*The mullah taught me the lesson, but I could not get beyond alif. / I suffered a beating with his slippers* (translated by Christopher Shackle)

It is said of him, as of the Turkish folk poet Yunus Emre and of Shah Abdul Latif, that he was illiterate and knew only the *alif*. He calls himself a *faqir*: "Bullhe Shah is a fakir of the lord; he disturbs those who are awake", and "Bullhe Shah is a poor fakir; he has departed, proclaiming to the sound of the drum". He takes up the image of the mystic as a dog before God: "Dogs are awake at night, dogs are superior to you".

His verse fuses the imagery of Punjabi village life, the spinning wheel, the beloved's lane and the tale of Heer and Ranjha, with the boldest mystical claims, and this union of the homely and the sublime has kept it alive on the tongues of the Punjab for nearly three centuries. He wrote in traditional forms, among them the *siharfi*, or Golden Alphabet, a poem whose verses run through the letters of the alphabet, and the *baramah*, which follows the lovers' feelings through the twelve months. He used the whole mystical vocabulary and imagery inherited from classical times, but joined it to the rural imagery of his homeland, so that it could be understood by every villager and ploughman and sung by the large crowds who would flock to the shrines of the saints. Like all the folk poets, he defended the unity of Being, the doctrine of Ibn Arabi.

The spinning wheel, the *charkha*, is the basic metaphor of the poems. The life of a rural woman was permanently linked to it, since whatever she spun would become part of her dowry, and the monologue he built on it is "unsurpassed in Indian poetry in its sustained anatomy of a physical object". The spinning theme in the Sindhi poetry of Shah Abdul Latif has been compared with his. The romance of Heer and Ranjha, which Shah Hussain had already read as a spiritual tale, served him, as it served his own younger contemporary Hashim Shah of Amritsar and Waris Shah, for mystical meditation.

Much of the poetry is spoken in a woman's voice: "I, Bullha, am the slave girl of the lord"; "Ranjha is a yogi and I am his yogini"; "I'm just a sweepress". His *kafis* on Hindu themes are written in Hindi, the characteristic language of the yogis, and one *kafi* is a ghazal in fairly standard Urdu. With the Sindhi poet Sachal Sarmast, he is one of the poets who so frequently invoked the ecstatic cry *anā 'l-ḥaqq*, "I am the Truth". Besides being the leading poet of Punjabi Sufism, he also composed a number of Persian prose treatises.

He is the poet most often quoted in Punjabi conversation, the call of his own name, Bullha, fitting easily into common speech; so easily that people often pass off lines of their own as his by adding his name. The line "Bullhya, hor wi neenva ho", for one, is a popular fabrication, and there have been attempts to put verses of the Bektashis, a Turkish Sufi order, into Punjabi and declare them to be his. "Teray Ishq nachaya" is among the most popular of his *kafis* today. One *kafi* is known above all by the second verse of its refrain, ending in the syllables *thaia thaia*, which teachers of dancing use to mark the rhythm of the steps.

There is no settled count of the poems that are truly his. His lyrics, like those of the other Sufi poets of the Punjab, were never kept by an organised community of the faithful, and most were written down and gathered into printed collections only in the late nineteenth century, among them one printed at Lahore in 1889 and another at Kasur in 1896. Faqir Muhammad Faqir's edition of his poems, published at Lahore in 1960, holds 156 *kafis*, besides *siharfis*, *baramahs* and other verse.

## The Shrine

Bulleh Shah's *mazar*, the tomb of a saint, stands in the heart of Kasur. Once, by tradition, a grave the orthodox had shunned, it is today a revered shrine, and in an age that has rediscovered him it has become a place of pilgrimage not only for the pious but for musicians, students and admirers of Punjabi letters. The way to it ran through extremely narrow streets, where large carriages drawn by horses, the *tangas*, passed. The tomb bears a Persian chronogram that gives the year of his death.

By the early 1980s the tomb stood in a concrete courtyard, where the afternoon sun beat down on the barefoot votaries, as if exacting penance from them for a distant injustice to the poet. The cemetery where, by tradition, he had been refused burial was mostly in ruins, encroached upon by a fast growing town. The tomb and the area around it were the only place there free of refuse, and the privileged of the city paid handsomely to be buried near the man they had once rejected; their graves carried stone canopies adorned with mosaic paintings of the sort seen on the back of trucks. The poet lay inside the dilapidated building of his tomb, its stone filigree work broken. Inside, the Auqaf Department, the department of religious endowments, had allowed banners of Urdu verse by the local poets of Kasur to festoon the walls, and a local grandee had given a framed couplet in which his name appeared more prominently than the verse; Bulleh Shah's own poetry was conspicuously absent. The poet lay under layers of tinsel and flowers, and cats slept leaning against his grave on the cool floor.

By the early 2000s, like most shrines, the tomb stood at the centre of a brick courtyard covered with a concrete canopy. The crypt was covered with green and red sheets decorated with gold tinsel, which devotees bring as offerings. Several small tombs surrounded the main tomb within the same compound, where female and male relatives of the poet are buried, and devotees lit candles at them and offered the *fatiha*, the opening chapter of the Quran recited as a prayer. The street outside was colourfully lined with shops selling marigolds, rose petals, candles and *divas*, small earthenware lamps filled with mustard oil and fitted with a cotton wick, which are lit at the shrine. The shops also sold incense sticks, prayer beads and bangles plated with silver and inscribed with verses of the Quran, which devotees take to the shrine or home as souvenirs, and sweets that devotees buy as *tabarruk*, *nazar* or *niaz*, food for distribution in the shrine. Today the shrine has a radically modernised appearance.

## Music and Devotion

The shrine keeps the familiar devotional life of a Punjabi *darbar*, a saint's shrine: the recitation of the *fatiha*, *qawwali*, and the offering of vows. It draws its greatest crowds at the annual *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of the saint's death, when his *kafis* are sung through the night. It is also one of the shrines where *qawwali* is sung every night, and it is said to be a shrine of the *jamali*, or aesthetic, mystics, Sufis who were poets and wrote narratives that could be sung to music, some of them musicians themselves.

The shrine has its own *qawwals*. They have included Muhammad Bakhsh and his paternal uncle Karam Bakhsh, with a young man said to be a nephew and two other musicians, playing a harmonium, a *dholak*, a drum shaped like a barrel and played on both sides, and a pair of tambourines. In their own words: "We sing here twice a day. We sing in the morning from eight to ten, and then throughout the evening." They say they inherited the songs orally from their ancestors, who had been *qawwals* at the shrine for the last three centuries. "The saint will not let us leave this shrine," Muhammad Bakhsh has said, "and even if we do go away to other shrines to sing for the urs, we always return here." They say too that they came with Bulleh Shah's family from Uch Sharif, that they sing his poetry to his own melodies, and that they are the guardians of his oral poetry, some of which has not yet found its way into the written texts.

The women of their family do not sing at the shrine. "Our women have never sung at the shrine," they say; perhaps a century ago their women would go to the Sayyid households on special invitation and sing Baba Bulleh Shah's poetry for the Sayyid women, singing only among women. The *qawwals* sing the poet's Punjabi verse in the Punjabi *ang*, or style, in which they create the female myths of his lyrics.

On one spring evening, two days before the start of the fasting month of Ramazan, they sang the entire evening, until after the evening prayers. All together, including the musicians, there were fifty women and men in the shrine. They sang six compositions on the female voice, all in Punjabi: one was about the Prophet Muhammad, and the other five, which the musicians said were Bulleh Shah's own, were narratives of the stages of his spiritual growth as a mystic and of his relationship with his master, Shah Inayat. Outside the *ʿurs*, a performance of *qawwali* need not follow a strict ritual pattern, and the routines may differ from shrine to shrine. Women who had been praying or lighting candles and incense sticks at the tomb joined the gathering to listen. Men made a small circle in front of the musicians and began to dance in rhythm with the music, and later some *hijras*, eunuchs, joined them, with *ghungru*, small bells, tied to their ankles. The *hijras* are probably linked to the shrine by tradition, through the story of his years among the dancing girls.

By the early 1980s, too, a hoarse group of *qawwals* was constantly singing his rebuking lines, "Tamba chook-chook mandi javain", while the votaries, their feet burning, ran toward the shade of the graves of the city's rich, whose gaudy presence lent the lines an edge.

His songs travel far beyond Kasur. Sufi poetry by poets from outside Sindh, his among them, has sometimes played a liturgical role in Sindhi devotional performances: at one *versi*, a gathering for a death anniversary, in 2012, most of the songs were in Sindhi, but there was also a composition of Bulleh Shah and another of Mirabai. In an anthology compiled in 1959 he is one of the very few Sufis included who are not Sindhi, and he is quoted in a Siraiki anthology too. Ghulam Ali's *dandiya* group, dancers who perform with sticks, has played at Bulleh Shah's shrine as at Data Darbar and Baba Farid Shakar Ganj's, all famous Muslim shrines.

The singer Abida Parvin counts him among the poets she sings, with Shah Hussain, Mian Muhammad Bakhsh, Hazrat Khawaja Ghulam Farid, Sachal Sarmast, Baba Farid and Guru Nanak, and at a concert in Islamabad she blended his Punjabi into a text of Sultan Bahu, closing on a line that means "O gentle Bulle, God is found only by those who are true and noble of heart". Pop musicians and ensembles playing electronic instruments sing his poetry; young musicians draw on it for their lyrics; and his mystical poetry, like Khusrau's, is sung at weddings in Pakistan. His verse also reaches listeners today through well known Sikh and Muslim singers and through the popular selections issued by Indian and Pakistani publishers.

## Legends

Wonders gather around his life. It is said that when he came to Shah Inayat, the master set a price on his initiation, a condition of four items of five hundred rupees each. Another story tells of a young man in despair who sat on the bank of the Ravi, resolved to drown himself. A masked horseman rode up and asked him to hold his horse and guard his things while he bathed, and handed over four things, as many as the items of that price: a purse of five hundred rupees, a pair of gold bangles, his clothes and the horse. Then he went into the river and never came out. When the young man went into the city, people cried out that the clothes and the horse were Shah Inayat's, and Shah Inayat smiled on seeing him.

It is also told that, when Bulleh Shah was a boy, the owner of a field found him asleep with a cobra shading him with its hood. The owner reported the boy dead; his father came with some men, the snake vanished and the boy sat up, and the field proved greener than before. Jiwan Khan then made over the whole field to the boy's father as an offering.

The most popular tale of all tells how he once fell out of favour with his master, allegedly for being too outspoken in his criticisms of formal Islam, and was dismissed from his company. Accounts of the quarrel and of how long it lasted differ, but most tell that Bulleh Shah went away to learn music and dance and came back to win his master over. Knowing Shah Inayat's fondness for music and dancing, he is said to have taken lessons with a dancing girl, then reappeared in Lahore one day dressed as a dancing girl himself, and danced and sang in front of Shah Inayat, who was won over by his performance and took him back into his favour. At the shrine the *qawwals* sing of his reconciliation with his master: in their song Bulleh Shah portrays himself as a dancing girl who tells how she won back her beloved through dance, so that it is the disciple who is the dancing girl. It was the first of the narratives they sang on that spring evening before Ramazan.

His poems lament a separation from his master:

*With the departure of Inayat Qadiri, / my story has again become difficult* (translated by Christopher Shackle)

And they sing the return through dance:

*Bullha, let us go and sit at the gate of Shah Inayat, who made me dress in green and red. / When I started dancing, I found my way to him* (translated by Christopher Shackle)

"Bullha, the lord has made me dance, and the sound is very loud," he sings; "I have stripped off my blanket and dance, making him hurl astonished looks at me"; and "Bullha, it is indeed a strange thing for a woman to start dancing with her veil on." A reproach is heard too: "Satan makes her dance in the courtyard. Talk to her and make her stop." The image of Bulleh Shah dancing is a popular one in modern pictures of him. The *qawwals* of the shrine also keep several legends of how he defied the ways of his family and community to remain Shah Inayat's disciple, though he was a Sayyid himself.

Another story tells of a flight. His father worked as imam of the mosque in the village of Pandoke, and after his father's death the *chaudhris*, the headmen of the village, asked Bulleh Shah to take his place, but he refused. Threatened with a beating, he fled the village, heading towards Kasur, and took refuge in a *gurdwara*, a Sikh place of worship, in the village of Daftu, saying: "I am in the house of God. Let's see who can take me from here." The Sikhs refused to put him out, and in the end, after long discussions, he was allowed to go on to Kasur.

## Kasur

Kasur lies south of Lahore and has been called its twin city. Tradition links the two towns from their beginnings: Lahore and Kasur are said to be named after Luv and Kush. One of the sons of Ram Chand is said to have founded Kasur, and it is also told that Kushu founded Kashur, the town now known as Kasur, before he took the throne, the *gaddi*, at Ajodhya. The father of Baba Farid, too, moved to Kasur. The town has known war: at one time Sikhs gathered at Kasur, many were killed, and the town was badly damaged. Kasur also holds the graves of another family altogether, in a different burial ground from Bulleh Shah's: a Kasur lineage of Naqshbandi Mujaddidi scholars from Sindh, who died in the Hijri years 1202, 1270 and 1295.

In Bulleh Shah's verse, Takht Hazara, Multan and Prem Nagar are idylls, but Kasur, the city where he lived and sang, is a kind of inferno. Two of his *dohras*, or couplets, refer directly to the city after the rise of the Pathans, to the chaos of moral and political values in it and to the suffering this visited upon the common man. It is also the town of an absent beloved:

*You are gone, friend, far away beyond my reach. / Many a day Kasur has missed your smile.* (translated by Taufiq Rafat)

In his verse the whole province, too, is lost, at a time when the Punjab was being persistently rent by petty chiefs and marauders:

*Now hell has opened its bogs. / Punjab has gone to the dogs. / This hell is the nethermost hell.* (translated by Taufiq Rafat)

Waris Shah, whom tradition makes his fellow pupil, turned to the same town in the epilogue to his own *Hir*, the romance of Heer and Ranjha, a poem he dates to the year 1180 of the Hijri calendar:

*Of all the wretched Punjab I am most concerned for Kasur.* (translated by Charles Frederick Usborne)

## Legacy

Bulleh Shah is, with Shah Hussain, Sultan Bahu and Waris Shah, one of the pillars of classical Punjabi poetry, and perhaps the most beloved of them all. He is one of the most celebrated Sufis of the Punjab, and his personality looms large in its poetry of the eighteenth century. He is considered the greatest of the Punjabi mystical poets and the greatest master of the mystical Sufi lyric in Punjabi, and his poetry was so highly esteemed that he was called the Rumi of the Punjab. With him Punjabi mystical folk poetry reached its peak, in exactly the same period as Shah Abdul Latif, and his highly ecstatic songs have been set beside those of the Sindhi poet Sachal Sarmast. His use of the spoken language of the people has helped to ensure his continuing popularity across religious boundaries and modern national frontiers, and his more familiar, outspoken style stands in contrast to the more elusive manner of Shah Abdul Latif.

His insistence that love is higher than law, that the divisions of religion and caste are veils over a single truth, and that the self must be annihilated in God has made him a voice of tolerance and of the ordinary human heart. He is counted with Baba Farid and Waris Shah among those who spread the message of peace and brought several religious communities together instead of shedding blood. His social critique, though vividly expressed, is only a small part of his poetry.

His reputation has been made and remade. In the twentieth century he was read as an exponent of universal spiritual truths owing as much to Vedanta as to Sufism, as an authentic spokesman of the Punjabi folk tradition, as a social revolutionary, or simply as a Romantic poet before his time. Yet he is also among the Punjabi poets whom the educated elite of the Punjab has disowned, and even ridiculed. His *kafis* are sung today from village shrines to concert stages, and his shrine at Kasur remains a fixed point of Punjabi devotion, the resting place of the rebel saint who taught the Punjab to sing its way to God.
