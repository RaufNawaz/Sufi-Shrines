## Overview

Gurdwara Rori Sahib stands at Eminabad, the old town near Gujranwala in the Punjab that was once known as Saidpur. A *gurdwara* is a Sikh house of worship, and this one marks the earthly travels of Guru Nanak, the founder of the Sikh faith, remembering two figures: the Guru and his companion Bhai Mardana, the Muslim minstrel who travelled with him. Its name comes from the Punjabi word *rori*, the gravel, pebbles and coarse grit of the ground, for tradition holds that it was here, on a bed of pebbles, that the Guru rested during his sojourn in the town; accounts of how the place came by its name differ.

The gurdwara belongs to a wider constellation of sacred sites clustered around Eminabad, each preserving the memory of an episode from Guru Nanak's life, and together they make the town one of the most important centres of Sikh pilgrimage in present day Pakistan. It is a different place from another Gurdwara Rori Sahib, at Jhaman near the border of Lahore and Kasur.

## The Guru

Guru Nanak's dates are most often given as 1469 to 1539. He spent a major portion of his life in what is today Pakistan: he was born there, and he eventually settled in a small village that is now on the Pakistani side of the border. Accounts of how many journeys he made differ, but his *udasis*, the long journeys of his travelling years, are most often counted as four, and they lasted more than twenty years. He travelled mostly on foot. He preferred to stay on the outskirts of villages, and whenever he visited an area he would visit its religious shrines, Muslim, Hindu and of any other denomination.

He offended both Hindus and Muslims by stating that there was no Hindu or Muslim. In the *janamsakhis*, the cycles of tales told of his life, he is said to have declared, after vanishing for a week in the stream at Sultanpur, "There is only one God. There is no Hindu, no Mussalman." His advice to his two companions was that "if one is a Muslim then one should be a good Muslim and if one is a Hindu then one should be a good Hindu".

He associated with people of the lower castes, calling them people of God, and he criticised high caste landlords and government officials as exploiters. He lived as a householder, and he taught that a good life rested on honest labour, compassion, selfless service and prayer. Nanak himself is held to have abhorred the association of miracles with religious personalities, yet the stories of his life are full of them.

## The Guru at Eminabad

Eminabad holds a cherished place in the janamsakhis. In their telling the Guru came to Saidpur early, on the first of his journeys, from Sultanpur by way of Talwandi, and he passed through it again on the way home from the fourth, coming by Hasan Abdal, Tilla Balnath and Katasraj to Saidpur and going on to Kartarpur.

Popular memory often links this shrine with the story of the Guru lodging with Bhai Lalo, a low caste carpenter of honest livelihood, in preference to the lavish hospitality of the wealthy official Malik Bhago, though local tradition ties the episode most specifically to the nearby Gurdwara Khuhi Bhai Lalo. In the best loved telling, the Guru is said to have squeezed the simple bread of Bhai Lalo's honest labour in one hand and the rich food of Malik Bhago in the other, so that milk flowed from the first and blood from the second. In the devotion of the Panth, the Sikh community, it is a parable of the difference between righteous earnings and wealth wrung from others. Whether recounted as history or as a story that teaches, the episode fixed Eminabad in Sikh memory as a place where Guru Nanak honoured the dignity of honest work.

In the janamsakhis, Mardana has his own part in the story. At first, it is told, he refused Lalo's millet *roti*, the flat bread, and ate it only when Nanak called it *prasad*, food made holy by being offered; later it was Mardana who carried Lalo's *roti* to Bhago's house. The Guru's teaching that there was no Hindu and no Muslim is said to have been questioned at the court of Nawab Daulat Khan Lodhi at Sultanpur, at the court of Malik Bhago in Eminabad, and at Rai Bhoi di Talwandi.

The town is also associated with the turbulence of Babur's invasion. Babur, after two minor raids, invaded the Punjab a third time, reaching as far as Sialkot. Tradition holds that Guru Nanak witnessed the sack of Saidpur by the Mughal armies around 1520 or 1521, an experience that many hold inspired the anguished hymns known as the *Babur Bani*, in which the Guru lamented the suffering visited upon the innocent. It is told that the Guru and Mardana were taken captive at Saidpur.

The janamsakhis record a meeting between Nanak and Babur at Saidpur, in which Babur asks for a blessing to conquer India and Nanak rebukes him. The encounter was also taken to mark the beginning of the antagonism between the Mughals and the Sikhs.

## The Gurdwara

To Rori Sahib itself attaches the memory of the Guru seated in meditation upon the rough pebbled ground, and it is this humble scene of devotion, the Guru at prayer, indifferent to comfort, that the shrine was raised to preserve.

The gurdwara that survives today owes its grandeur to the era of the Sikh Empire: it was built during the reign of Maharaja Ranjit Singh (1801 to 1839), whose patronage adorned so many of the sites connected with the Gurus, though accounts of its date and its form differ. The central building is an imposing structure of finely cut brickwork rising through several storeys, crowned by a large central dome, the *gumbad*, flanked by smaller domed pavilions, the *chhatris*, in the manner characteristic of Sikh sacred architecture. A room at the rear, roofed with an especially graceful ribbed white lotus dome and ringed by a verandah for walking round the shrine, is said to enclose the very patch of pebbled earth on which the Guru rested.

The gateway was the work of Bhai Ram Singh, Principal of the Mayo School of Arts in Lahore, who designed the Indian gallery in Buckingham Palace. Ram Singh came from a family of carpenters, and he created the effect of woodcutting from the use of bricks. Over the doorway a verse from the Guru Granth Sahib, the Sikh scripture, was carved with an artistic effect of bricks.

The path to the gateway ran between empty grounds, and a few police officials lazed on a *charpoy*, a woven string bed. Visitors took off their shoes at the portal. Beside the gateway was a small courtyard with several rooms, where a few Pathan Sikh children were playing. The gurdwara's caretakers were Pathan Sikhs who had moved there from Nankana Sahib. In the ground beside the gurdwara stood a further structure, also part of the gurdwara, with buffaloes wallowing in the pools around it.

Like most Sikh shrines in the western Punjab, Rori Sahib was left on the Pakistani side of the border when the subcontinent was divided in 1947, and its congregation departed in the great and grievous migration of that year. The building is reported to have been damaged by fire in the disorder that followed Partition, and for decades it stood largely neglected; accounts of its state in those years differ. A bazaar for the sale of buffaloes used to be set up on the gurdwara's premises.

In more recent years, with the easing of pilgrimage across the border and the growing attention paid to Sikh heritage in Pakistan, the shrine has again received visitors, Sikh *jathas*, or pilgrim parties, travelling from India and from the worldwide diaspora, and efforts have been made towards its care and conservation. Security officials came to be posted there at all times, and Sikh pilgrims came every year.

## Bhai Mardana

The gurdwara's second figure is the Guru's companion. *Bhai*, brother, is a title given to Mardana for the exalted status he holds in the Sikh religion, and Mardana was his real name. He was a minstrel and a favourite friend of the Guru's, and he joined Nanak at Sultanpur, where the Guru worked in the commissariat of Daulat Khan; soon afterwards Nanak left Sultanpur, with Mardana beside him. Bhai Bala and Bhai Mardana, a Hindu and a Muslim, were both companions of Nanak.

Mardana was counted among the intimates of Baba Nanak, as the Guru was also called, and he sang the Guru's verses in song and melody and so drew people into the net of devotion. His own poetry is in the Guru Granth Sahib, which contains the poetry of several saints who were not Sikhs, among them Baba Farid, Bhai Mardana and Bhagat Kabir.

In the janamsakhis, Mardana, who played the *rubab*, a traditional stringed instrument, set out with Nanak on his travels. He went with him east all the way to Assam, to Haridwar and Prayag, to Lahore, Sialkot and Multan, to Mecca, Medina and Baghdad, and to Tilla Balnath and Katasraj. The stories give him a perpetual hunger, feet perpetually suffering from sores, and a homesickness that sometimes overcame him, so that he would often sob into his pillow. In one of them, turned away from house to house in an inhospitable village, he asks why two villages received opposite blessings. In the same stories, his parents settled at Kartarpur, and he and Nanak spent a lifetime on the road.

Stories of Mardana are told at other shrines too. At Ghavindi it is told that Mardana, told not to beg, came back hungry from a family celebrating the birth of a son; the child died, by an act of God, and the family sought Nanak's forgiveness. The shrine there had two plinths meant to represent the seats of Guru Nanak and Bhai Mardana, Nanak's higher than Mardana's. In the legend of Panja Sahib, Mardana climbs three times to Wali Qandhari for water. At Kanganpur, old shop paintings bore text that confirmed they were of Guru Nanak and Bhai Mardana.

Mardana died before the Guru. Accounts of where he died differ, and no grave marks the spot where he was buried.

## Devotion and Verse

The tradition of *kirtan*, the devotional singing of hymns, comes from Bhai Mardana. Ever since his time, his descendants as well as other Muslim *kirtan* performers performed at Sikh gurdwaras, a tradition that was disrupted at Partition. In the janamsakhis Mardana would take up his *rubab* and provide an accompaniment for the divinely inspired verses that Nanak sang, and the Guru's call to him was "Mardana, Bani aaye hai", "I have inspiration for a Bani". At a tomb in Baghdad, the stories tell, Mardana tuned his *rubab* and struck up the accompaniment to his master's singing. Sikh hagiography has a favourite scene, shown in several paintings: a serene Nanak listening to the songs of Bhai Mardana, who holds a *rubab*, while Bhai Bala stands behind them holding a fly whisk.

In Aurangzeb's time the sum of the worship of Baba Nanak's followers was the recitation of their master's verses, which they sang in song and melody. They counted service to the traveller, to those who come and go, as the highest worship, and whoever arrived at midnight speaking Baba Nanak's name they treated as a brother and a friend and served, even a stranger, a thief, a highwayman or an evildoer. The tradition of *langar*, the communal free kitchen, is believed to have begun with Guru Nanak.

The Guru composed verses in Hindi on the truth of God, setting out divine unity. His *bani*, devotional verses of supplication, entreaty, humility and reverence, is sung outside the gurdwaras too. The singer Abida Parvin has said: "I sing Shah Hussain, Bulle Shah, Mian Muhammad Bakhsh, Hazrat Khawaja Ghulam Farid, Sacchal Sarmast, Baba Farid, and Guru Nanak." In Sindh, Sain Vasan Shah became famous for reciting the *banis* of Shri Guru Nanak and the *kalams*, the poems, of the Sufis.

The poem most closely tied to Eminabad is the *Babur Bani*, a long poem that Nanak wrote at the time the Mughal Babur attacked the Punjab, destroying the cities of Eminabad and Lahore on the way. In it Nanak lays the blame of Babur's wrath at God's feet. One of its lines dates the invaders' coming:

ਆਵਨਿ ਅਠਤਰੈ …

*Coming in seventy-eight (1521 C.E)*

Lines from the Guru's other hymns, in English translation, give a sense of the poetry that, in the tradition, was sung to Mardana's *rubab*. His *aarti*, the hymn sung as lamps are waved in worship, begins:

ਗਗਨ ਮੈ ਥਾਲੁ
ਰਵਿ ਚੰਦੁ ਦੀਪਕ ਬਨੇ …

*In the salver of the firmament, / The sun and the moon shine as lamps*

On the oneness of God he sang:

ਸਾਹਿਬੁ ਮੇਰਾ ਏਕੋ ਹੈ ॥
ਏਕੋ ਹੈ ਭਾਈ ਏਕੋ ਹੈ ॥

*My Master is One / He is One, brother, the only One*

And again:

*God is One, / The Path is One*

His poem on the sacred thread, the *janeu*, runs:

ਦਇਆ ਕਪਾਹ
ਸੰਤੋਖੁ ਸੂਤੁ …

*From the cotton of compassion, / Spin the thread of contentment*

Of washing he sang:

ਭਰੀਐ ਹਥੁ ਪੈਰੁ ਤਨੁ ਦੇਹ ॥
ਪਾਣੀ ਧੋਤੈ ਉਤਰਸੁ ਖੇਹ ॥

*As hands or feet besmirched with slime, / Water washes white*

And in another song:

ਬਗਾ ਬਗੇ ਕਪੜੇ ਤੀਰਥ ਮੰਝਿ ਵਸੰਨਿੑ ॥

*The herons in their white feathers dwell in the sacred shrines of pilgrimage*

## The Setting

When Babur took the city of Saidpur, he renamed it Eminabad, though accounts of the renaming differ. The cone like spires of ancient Hindu temples rose among its houses, in contrast to the monolithic Muslim architecture that dominated the town, and its dusty roads and worn houses showed that it had lost its economic strength. Once it was an important trading centre on the Grand Trunk Road, which connected with Lahore and then Delhi; the highway has since moved south, and Gujranwala, not far away, has replaced Eminabad as a trading centre.

One temple, abandoned in 1947, still looked sturdy. Temples such as these had been taken over by migrants from across the border after the creation of Pakistan, and after the destruction of the Babri mosque in 1992 no signs of attack on them were to be found. Much of the town's historical architecture had still been preserved.

## Legacy

Gurdwara Rori Sahib endures as a quiet monument to the earliest chapter of the Sikh story, when Guru Nanak walked the roads of the Punjab teaching the oneness of God and the equality of all people. Its very name, a shrine of pebbles, carries the lesson of the tradition it commemorates: that holiness is found not in comfort or wealth but in humility and honest devotion. For the Sikhs of the world it remains a treasured link to their founder, and for the town of Eminabad it is the enduring sign of a visitor whose memory has outlasted empires.

It is also a shrine of the friendship between the Guru and his Muslim minstrel. Across Pakistan there are several sites where Nanak stayed or is said to have performed miracles, and the gurdwaras built there later by his devotees still stand tall. After the division of the Punjab, several other gurdwaras and temples were taken over and destroyed by migrants.

Devotion to the Guru reaches beyond the Sikhs. Many Muslims acquainted with his teachings are devoted to him too and visit his shrine, and to one Muslim painter, "Guru Nanak is like a Muslim Sufi Saint". A Muslim tradition, which the Sikhs do not accept, holds that Guru Nanak was born by the blessing of Baba Naulakh Hazari. Baba Farid is said to have become an inspiration for Guru Nanak when the Guru visited his shrine on his travels, and Farid's texts are revered among the Sikhs, for he is held to have influenced the founder of their religion. A Chishti Sabiri shaykh of the twentieth century counted Guru Nanak, with Krishna, Rama, Sita and Lachman, among the spiritual luminaries outside Islam who believed in the one true God and had a "Divine connection", *nisbat*. At the shrine of the goddess Hinglaj, the engraved footsteps of Guru Nanak are found.
