## Overview

Gurdwara Panjvin and Chhevin Patshahi, the gurdwara "of the Fifth and Sixth Kingships", stands in Nankana Sahib, the town of Guru Nanak's birth, near Gurdwara Tambu Sahib on the road leading to the central shrine of Janam Asthan. Unlike most of the town's shrines, which commemorate the boyhood of Guru Nanak Dev Ji, the founder of the Sikh faith, this gurdwara honours two of his successors: the fifth Guru, Arjan Dev, and the sixth Guru, Hargobind, whose visits to the birthplace of the founder are remembered here. It is a reminder that Nankana Sahib drew not only the devotion of later pilgrims but the presence of the living Gurus themselves.

Guru Arjan, who gathered the Sikh scripture, was the first martyr of the faith; his son, Guru Hargobind, was the first Guru to wear two swords, one for temporal and one for spiritual power.

## The Gurdwara

The gurdwara marks the tradition that both Guru Arjan Dev and Guru Hargobind came to the town of Guru Nanak's birth. Guru Hargobind, revered as the master of *miri* and *piri*, the union of temporal and spiritual authority, is remembered to have visited Nankana Sahib in the month of *Har* in the year 1670 of the Samvat reckoning, corresponding to 1613, while returning from a journey to Kashmir. In memory of his stay his followers are said to have established a *Gur Mela*, a fair, at the very spot where he had rested, and in time the site came to honour the fifth Guru as well. Accounts of the Gurus' visits differ in their details.

Within the enclosure there are two shrines, standing together within a common boundary wall: that of Guru Arjan Dev, built plainly and without a dome, and that of Guru Hargobind, surmounted by a dome. Their modest architecture gives visible form to the succession of the Gurus. As in the other shrines of Nankana Sahib, the *Guru Granth Sahib*, the Sikh scripture, is installed within; it rests on a *Thara Sahib*, a raised platform, with a fly whisk. The gurdwara has a *Nishan Sahib*, the Sikh flag on its tall pole, and a single storey block of rooms set aside for pilgrims.

The shrines form one of the quieter corners of Nankana Sahib, less frequented than the great shrine of Janam Asthan but valued by pilgrims who make the full round of the town's sacred sites. Their place beside Tambu Sahib sets them within the compact eastern cluster of the town's gurdwaras, and the site is cared for by the bodies charged with Sikh heritage in Pakistan.

The gurdwara also keeps a relic. A small room above the shrine is itself regarded as sacred, and in it stands a glass showcase holding a piece of a Waan tree. It is told that Guru Arjan and his son Guru Hargobind, coming on pilgrimage to Nankana Sahib, left their horses outside the city, tied to that tree at this spot, and walked about seven hundred metres on foot to Janam Asthan as a mark of respect. In this telling the gurdwara stands where the Gurus dismounted, at the edge of the town, so that they might enter the birthplace of the founder on foot.

## Devotion and Festivals

Pilgrims who come here prostrate themselves, go round clockwise and fan the scripture, and devotees touch the glass of the showcase that holds the piece of the Waan tree, for blessings. On one visit an old Sikh wrote his name on the platform of the *Nishan Sahib*.

Most of the gurdwaras of Nankana Sahib were kept locked all year round and opened only at the time of the festival, with security guards at the entrance to all of them. The festival is the birthday of Guru Nanak, Guru Nanak Jayanti or *Gurpurab*, celebrated in the last days of November. Official estimates put the people who come to Nankana Sahib for it at ten to fifteen thousand every year, though in 2011 only three thousand were reported to be attending.

Pilgrims from India came by train across the border at Wagah and waited at Nankana for three days. There were three days of *Akhand Path*, the unbroken reading of the scripture; on the final day the *bhog*, the rite that closes the reading, was followed by the *Nagar Kirtan*, the procession singing hymns through the town, and then the pilgrims went on to other cities. For those few days the entire city looked like a little India.

## The Fifth Guru

Guru Arjan was the fifth Sikh Guru and the son of the fourth, Guru Ram Das, who appointed him as the next Guru. He was born in 1563 and was Guru from 1581 until his death in 1606, a span of twenty five years.

His succession was contested. Guru Ram Das chose his younger son, and Arjan's elder brother, Prithi Chand, is said to have hidden the letters Arjan sent from Lahore to their father. Ram Das then disowned Prithi Chand and, a few days later, appointed Arjan as the next Guru. Sikh tradition finds the root of the conflict in Arjan's journey to Lahore and the machinations of Prithi Chand, who went on to approach the Mughal authorities for his share of the property; and so, it has been said, began the troubled relation between the Mughals and the Sikhs.

Guru Arjan built and gathered. He is credited with completing the Amrit Sar, the tank of nectar that his father had begun at Ramdaspur, after which the Harmandir Sahib was built there. He introduced the *daswandh*, the giving of a tenth of one's earnings to the cause of the Guru, collected through the *Masands*, the Guru's appointed collectors, a tradition that he began. In 1594 he founded Kartarpur, near Jalandhar in India, on land granted in the reign of Akbar, the third Mughal emperor.

He was the gatherer of the scripture. Guru Arjan began the compilation of the Guru Granth Sahib, and in 1604 he compiled the *bani*, the sacred compositions, of Guru Nanak as part of the Adi Granth. Into the scripture he gathered the writings of all the earlier Sikh Gurus and of other saints who were not Sikhs. Among them was the poetry of the Muslim saint Baba Farid, which Guru Nanak had collected at Pak Pattan. He himself has the greatest number of hymns in the Guru Granth Sahib. He sent one of his followers, Bhai Bannu, to Lahore to bind the Adi Granth. When it was complete the Guru was summoned over its alleged anti-Muslim and anti-Hindu content, and he sent Bhai Gurdas, who cleared up the misunderstanding. The Adi Granth played a significant part in the growth of a separate Sikh identity.

A meeting with an emperor is also remembered. The emperor crossed the Beas and came to where the Guru, the successor of Baba Nanak, had halted. He was pleased by the meeting, and by hearing from the Guru's own tongue the Hindi verses that Baba Nanak had composed on the knowledge of God, and the Guru, counting the visit an honour, presented a fitting offering. The Guru then pleaded for the peasants: while the imperial armies were quartered in the Punjab grain had been dear and the assessment had been raised, and now the peasants could not meet the demand. At his petition the high revenue officers were ordered to lighten the assessment on the peasants, and the revenue collectors to deal with them accordingly.

His followers are remembered elsewhere in the Punjab. A gurdwara at Mandi Mangat is held to have been the house of Bhai Bannu, and another shrine is that of Sahari Mal, also a disciple of Guru Arjan. The friendship between Mian Mir and Guru Arjan is the subject of several folk tales.

## The Martyrdom of Guru Arjan

Guru Arjan was the first martyr of the Sikh faith. He was summoned to Lahore by Jahangir, the fourth Mughal emperor, over his alleged support for the rebellion of Prince Khusrau, the emperor's eldest son; he appointed Hargobind as the sixth Guru, and in 1606 he was put to death. Khusrau, it is told, had stopped at the Guru's *langar*, the free communal kitchen, and sought his blessing for victory, and the mark of blessing was counted treason by the emperor. In his memoirs Jahangir wrote of "a Hindu named Arjan" at Goindwal on the river Beas, where the rebel prince "happened to halt", and of how Arjan made "a finger-mark in saffron which the Indians call Qashga". He called the Guru "this insignificant fellow", handed his property to Murtaza Khan, and ordered that he be dealt with under the penal laws of the Yasa, the Mongol code of law, and put to death. Accounts of why he died differ, but his death is most often tied to Khusrau's revolt.

For five days the Guru was tortured. In the walled city of Lahore, near the Rang Mahal, there is a place with a well, and it is believed that Arjan was kept in a room next to it, in what was the *haveli*, the mansion, of the Mughal Diwan of Lahore, and that there he was immersed in a cauldron. Sikh belief tells the end as a miracle: on the fifth day the Guru took a dip in the waters of the Ravi and did not emerge, and so passed into the other world. Mian Mir, the tradition continues, asked the Guru's leave to bury his oppressors alive, and the Guru refused. Accounts of his end differ. Guru Arjan's son raised a small structure at the spot of his *samadhi*, a memorial shrine. Both Guru Arjan and his son Guru Hargobind spent a lot of time in Lahore, the city where Guru Arjan died.

His martyrdom marks a turning point in the story of the Sikhs. It has been called "the Karbala of Sikh history" and a low point in Sikh history; Arjan, it has been said, was crushed in the fight between the Mughal "elephants". His death is held to have given the Sikh community a cohesive motivation and to have precipitated the development of the Khalsa, the order of initiated Sikhs. He was the last of the Gurus who focused solely on spiritual matters. Operation Blue Star was launched on a day when thousands of Sikhs from all over the Punjab had gathered to commemorate his death anniversary.

## The Sixth Guru

Guru Hargobind, Guru Arjan's son and successor, was Guru from 1606 to 1644. Known as the "soldier-saint", he is most often held to have begun the militarisation of the Sikh community after his father's death, though accounts differ over when it began. He was the first Guru to wear two swords, one representing temporal power and the other spiritual power.

On 24 July 1606 he gave *darshan*, an audience at which his followers beheld their Guru. He came dressed as a royal, in a saffron gown and the close fitting trousers called *churidar* pyjama, with an aigrette, a necklace and two swords, and he refused to take up the religious symbols of Guruhood. His words are remembered as: "My rosary shall be the sword belt and on my turban, I shall wear the emblem of royalty". This is *miri* and *piri*: one sword for temporal power, the other for spiritual prowess.

He kept a court. A saffron flag faced the Akal Takht, the Eternal Throne; there was a large drum, and he kept a hawk as a pet. He had fifty two bodyguards and a force of *Sant Sipahi*, saint soldiers, five hundred to begin with, who were not paid a salary but were exempt from paying the required tribute. Through his *Masands* devotees were told to offer arms and horses, and the offering of weapons at the Guru's court, a practice he began, was later reinforced by Guru Gobind Singh. To administer the community he created the *Sangat*, the congregation of followers, which was then put under a *Masand*. He reared horses and encouraged his followers to keep arms in their defence.

His relations with the Mughals turned more than once. Jahangir imprisoned him in Gwalior, and later the relationship grew cordial. Under Shahjahan three battles were fought, at Amritsar, Lahira and Kartarpur, and all of them were won by the Sikhs, after which he decided to settle at Kartarpur. Kiratpur, on the Sutlej, was also a place where he and Guru Har Rai lived.

He chose his grandson to follow him. His eldest son, Baba Gurditta, died in his lifetime, and he appointed Har Rai, Baba Gurditta's younger son, who thus sat in his grandfather's place. Following his grandfather's tradition, Har Rai too kept horses and a force of twenty two hundred fighters. The sword passed to Hargobind's younger son, Guru Tegh Bahadur, and then to Guru Gobind Singh.

Opinion on him is divided. Critics call his militarism a departure from the teachings of the early Gurus, and he was the first Guru not to compose a single hymn; Tegh Bahadur was the first Guru since Guru Arjan to compose hymns. Hargobind remains one of the most controversial of the Sikh Gurus, and he laid the foundations of the contemporary face of Sikhism.

Another story of the sixth Guru, told of a different shrine, also turns on a horse. In the village of Amar Sadhu, now inside the DHA in Lahore, a gurdwara built in honour of the memory of Guru Hargobind is known as Gurdwara Bibi Kaulan. Bibi Kaulan, it is told, was a maid bonded to a Muslim trader of Mozang; hearing that the Guru was encamped nearby, she begged him to rescue her, and he took her with him on his horse. They rested at that spot for a while before they went on to Amritsar.

## Nankana Sahib

Nankana Sahib was once the village of Talwandi, about forty miles south west of Lahore. Originally called Rai Bhoi di Talwandi, it was renamed Nankana Sahib in honour of Guru Nanak, the first Sikh Guru, who was born there in 1469. His early years there account for the several gurdwaras of the city, which is considered one of the holiest cities in the world for the Sikhs.

Tradition tells that Talwandi lay between the rivers Ravi and Chenab, directly in the path of the armies that had set their eyes on Delhi, and that thirteen times the village was destroyed and thirteen times, like the fabled phoenix, it rose again from its ashes. Its last rebirth, the story goes, took place under Rai Bhoi, who had converted to Islam, and by the end of Guru Nanak's life Talwandi was no longer an obscure little village but had become a place of pilgrimage. Behind Gurdwara Janam Asthan rises an old mound; the settlement there, it is said, was destroyed, after which Rai Bhoi di Talwandi was built within a boundary wall.

The modern town has one main road running through it, and most of its buildings are from the late British or the early Pakistan era. From a rooftop can be seen the minarets of mosques, the small white domes of the gurdwaras, the remains of a few Hindu temples, and the railway station. In 2005 Nankana Sahib was separated from Sheikhupura and made a district of its own, with the city as its headquarters.

After Partition the town's gurdwaras stood empty. As Kartar remembered it: "There was no Sikh left here after partition. Our Gurdwaras became barren and were in a bad state." Giani Pratap, seeing the dilapidated condition of the shrines, decided to stay and look after these holy buildings. In the early twenty first century most of the town's Sikhs were not natives of the city but recent migrants from Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, where there is a considerable Sikh population, and most of the newcomers were housed within the gurdwaras, in rooms generally reserved for pilgrims. The community numbered about two hundred households.

The town's pilgrimages have also known violence. In November 1985, on Guru Nanak's birthday and a year after Operation Blue Star, thousands of Sikh pilgrims had come to Nankana from all over the world. During a speech for a separate Sikh state at one of the town's gurdwaras, its gate was closed, and Counsellor B. Jain and First Secretary K. K. Khanna were attacked and barely escaped alive. The religious festivals at Nankana and other cities of Pakistan have since gone back to being religious festivals.

## Legacy

Since 1947 the gurdwara, like its neighbours, has come to life chiefly when pilgrim *jathas*, bands of pilgrims, arrive for the great anniversaries. Its dedication to the fifth and sixth Gurus lends the sacred geography of Nankana Sahib a fuller shape: the town is not only the cradle of Guru Nanak but a place touched by the later Gurus who carried his message forward, Arjan Dev, the compiler of the scripture and the first martyr of the faith, and Hargobind, who first girded the sword of temporal power. In honouring their visits, Gurdwara Panjvin and Chhevin Patshahi joins the beginning of the Sikh story to its unfolding.

The gurdwara keeps that succession in a single object: a piece of the tree to which, it is told, father and son tied their horses before they walked, on foot and as a mark of respect, into the birthplace of the first Guru. It is a shrine in Nanak's town to the Guru who gathered the scripture and the Guru who first bore the sword.
