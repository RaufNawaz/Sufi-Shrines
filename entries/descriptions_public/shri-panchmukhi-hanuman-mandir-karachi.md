## Overview

The Shri Panchmukhi Hanuman Mandir, in the Soldier Bazaar quarter of Karachi, is one of the most ancient Hindu temples in Pakistan. It is famed for its *panchmukhi*, or five faced, image of the monkey god Hanuman, which devotees hold to be *svayambhu*: manifested of itself, not made by human hands. Believed by tradition to be many centuries old, the temple was declared a protected site of national heritage under the Sindh Cultural Heritage (Preservation) Act of 1994, and it remains a busy centre of worship for Karachi's Hindu community.

## The Five Faced Image

Hanuman, one of the most loved deities of popular Hinduism, is the devoted servant of Rama, the hero of the Sanskrit epic, the *Ramayana*. Here he is venerated in his rare *Panchamukha* form, with five faces. In classical iconography the five faces represent Hanuman himself together with *Narasimha*, half man and half lion, *Garuda*, the eagle, *Varaha*, the boar, and *Hayagriva*, the form with the head of a horse; each face is turned to a direction, and each embodies a divine power.

What sets the Karachi image apart, in the belief of its devotees, is that it is a "natural" statue, carved by no sculptor but revealed from the earth, and the faithful hold that this makes it unique among shrines of Hanuman. Pilgrims come to offer worship before it, and the temple is especially thronged at *Hanuman Jayanti*, the deity's birth anniversary, as well as at *Holi*, the spring festival of colours, and *Diwali*, the festival of lamps.

## Tradition and Renewal

The temple's origins are wreathed in legend. Devotional tradition holds that Rama himself passed this way during his years of exile, and that long afterwards the image of Hanuman was uncovered at the spot, whereupon a shrine was built to house it. The site is popularly said to be at least fifteen hundred years old, and some accounts reach back further still. Such datings belong to tradition, but they express the deep sense of sanctity and age that the temple carries for its worshippers.

In modern times the temple has passed through periods of difficulty, including the loss of part of its land, but it has also seen renewal. A programme of renovation was undertaken in 2012, supported by donations from local Hindus. In 2019, during construction and restoration work, a number of further images and artefacts were unearthed, reported to include several statues of Hanuman together with other sacred figures. The discovery drew the interest of archaeologists and museum authorities and renewed public attention to the temple's long history. These finds, and the temple's protected status, have strengthened its standing as a heritage site of national importance.

## Hanuman in Legend and Verse

In two legends set beyond Karachi, Hanuman makes water flow. On the pilgrim road that starts from Karachi and leads to the goddess Hinglaj Devi, the Bahur River is said to have been created by Hanuman's attempt to get water for Sita, Rama's wife. Further along the same road, in the Hinglaj valley, pilgrims have even put a small statue of Hanuman at a *parav*, a halting place whose dedication is contested. Another legend holds that the Ram Thamman pool was first dug by Hanuman, who created the ancient pool digging with a shovel only two and a half times.

His image appears elsewhere too. In the nineteenth century a marble image of Hanuman stood in the southern niche of the *samadh*, the memorial shrine, of Ranjit Singh, the Sikh ruler of the Punjab, and at Chandrat he was among the pictures on the walls, beside Ganesh, the elephant headed god. In the early twenty first century, on the wall of a Valmiki temple, he was painted carrying an entire mountain on his hand while flying back to Lanka, the kingdom of Ravana.

Hanuman's name also lives in the poetry of the Sufis, the Muslim mystics. The Sufi poet Bulleh Shah recalls Hanuman's part in the epic of Rama:

*When Ravan the ten-headed abducted poor Sita, Hanuman burned Lanka. Ravan was completely humiliated, and finally you killed him* (translated by Christopher Shackle)

In one modern reading of Bulleh Shah's poems, Ravana and Hanuman figure in the story of Rama and Sita as two poles of mystical knowledge. In the folk verse of one Muslim poet, in which all categories collapse, paired opposites become one and the same: Hallaj and his judge, Shams-i Tabriz and the molla, and Abu Hanifa and Hanuman; there is no longer Turk, Hindu or Peshawari. Sachal and Qutub Ali Shah, counted among the Sufis of nineteenth century Sindh, brought Krishna into their poetry and named Hanuman's circle as well: Sachal mentions Hindu gods such as Rama, Hanuman, Sita and especially Krishna, and Qutub Ali Shah gives the names of Laxman, Rama's brother, and Sita, Rama and Krishna.

## Karachi

Karachi, the temple's city, was once the village of Kolachi. A legend explains the name: an old woman known as Mai Kolachi, whose son was killed by a fish, tore the fish apart and retrieved him, and the place came to be known as Kolachi. The city's Hindu fisherfolk lived here before Kolachi became Karachi, and in the early twenty first century they were the only connection between the city and the old village.

In the late nineteenth century Karachi grew as a new emporium of Sindh. Sir Charles Napier, the conqueror and first governor of Sindh, had built a Masonic lodge there in 1843; a printing press was brought to the city in the 1850s, and the Theosophical Society was founded there in 1896. At the time of Partition Karachi was a minor port city, which migrants from India made the largest city of Pakistan.

The city has other Hindu temples. The area of Ram Bagh is now called Aram Bagh, an added letter that, it has been said, neutralises the name's religious sense. In the early twenty first century its temple had a painted Krishna with his maidens under its roof and a peacock guarding the entrance, and most of the quarters behind it held Hindu households. It was much more elaborately decorated, and its community much larger, than in Lahore, and an officer there said that it existed before Partition. It has been suggested that, along with the temple at Clifton, it was a historical Hindu temple in the time of Guru Nanak, the founder of the Sikh faith, and the Clifton temple has been identified as the temple which Nanak visited on his tour of Sindh. At Port Grand a newly constructed mosque for tourists had been built on top of another temple, whose rooms were reserved for different deities and whose mural showed Jhule Lal, a male deity sitting on top of a giant fish.

Karachi is the start of the pilgrim road to the goddess Hinglaj Devi, whose temple at Hinglaj lies about two hundred and fifty kilometres from the city. Karachi is the staging city for pilgrims from India, the seat of the Nagnath and Jagnath *akharas*, Hindu religious establishments, and the home of the Devipujak-Vagri community. The ongoing growth of Karachi and the urbanisation of the areas where the early stops lay caused all but a few of the resting places on the road to disappear. In the early twenty first century the city, where a reasonable number of Hindus still lived, also supplied images of the gods to Hindus elsewhere in Pakistan: boys from Bahawalnagar bought an idol of Durga, the warrior goddess, from Karachi, and a vendor's idols had been brought from Karachi or, through illegal channels, from India. The city has also known violence against Hindus.

Karachi is a city of Sufis too. It has been counted, with Hyderabad in India, as one of two important urban centres of Sufism, where the Qawwal Bachche, a lineage of singers of *qawwali*, Sufi devotional music, keep their heritage. In the early twenty first century a teaching shaykh of the Chishti Sufi order led weekly *halqa*, communal sessions of *zikr*, the remembrance of God, on Thursday evenings in the city, and after the annual fair at Sehwan, *fakirs*, wandering Sufis, followed other fakirs and proceeded to shrines in Karachi. A congregation of Sidis, a community of African descent, gathered at Manghopir, near Karachi.

## Sindh and Its Hindus

In 711 Muslim navies conquered and occupied the densely populated Hindu and Buddhist society of Sindh. In the centuries after the mass conversions to Islam, Hindus visited Muslim shrines and made votive offerings, and in 1940 it was observed that most of the musicians in Sindh had been Hindus and not Muslims. The stories of the Sufi poet Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit have been held to be all Hindu in origin. The Hindu traders of Sindh undertook long sea voyages to places such as Aden, Gujarat and Lanka; their wives made offerings to the water deity for their husbands' safe return, and the Hindu festival of lamps, celebrated in the autumn, marked the beginning of the trading season.

The *Risalo*, Shah Abdul Latif's collected poetry, carries a prayer for the province itself. Its rain song, the Sarang, catalogues the places the rains reach, among them Umarkot, of which the verse says "Some have passed over Umarkot and have made the ground green". It closes by asking God to make Sindh flourish forever, and that final invocation is one of the most often quoted verses of the *Risalo*:

*Dear friend and sweet beloved, make the whole world prosperous* (translated by Christopher Shackle)

In 1941 Hindus made up about 21 per cent of the population of Sindh. By early November 1947, 246,000 Hindus had already left Sindh by sea, air or rail, and the Hindu Sindhis started leaving especially after the Karachi riots in January 1948. Accounts of how many left in all differ, but the number is most often put at around a million. The narrative of Rochaldas alludes to violence spread through Sindh that was not perpetrated by the Muslim Sindhis but by the Mohajirs, Muslim migrants from India, who clearly wanted to take over the houses of the Hindus, at least in Karachi. The departure of the Hindu elite and the influx of Muslim refugees from India dramatically altered the province's demography. Accounts differ over how peaceful Sindh was between the faiths, but communal violence did occur there, even if it never reached the level of violence in the Punjab.

Sindh is the province of Pakistan with the most significant Hindu population, home to the majority of Pakistan's Hindus, and by the census of 1998 the Hindus were the largest minority in the country. Their number in Pakistan is put at between 6 and 10 million, though estimates vary widely, and most of them belong to underprivileged tribes and castes. Ramdeo Pir, a saint known in Sindh as Rama Pir, is "today integrated into the temples dedicated to the pan-Indian divinities". After the demolition of the Babri Mosque in December 1992, Hindu communities in Sindh and Quetta were attacked. In the early twenty first century, government funding for the places of minority communities was seen in a wave of restorations of temples across Sindh, renovations that coincided with changes in the political landscape.

## Legacy

The Shri Panchmukhi Hanuman Mandir holds a singular place among the Hindu shrines of Pakistan: a temple whose central image is held to be born of itself, whose traditions reach back to the age of the *Ramayana*, and whose soil has yielded up sacred figures buried for generations. For the Hindus of Karachi it is a cherished house of worship and a point of continuity with a distant past; for the wider heritage of the city it is a monument that binds legend, devotion and archaeology into a single enduring landmark.
