## Overview

The shrine of Bibi Pak Daman, "the pure, chaste ladies", stands in the heart of old Lahore, a short way from Empress Road and the old Qila Gujjar Singh. It is one of the oldest and most venerated sacred sites in the city, and among the busiest, drawing many thousands of pilgrims every day and immense crowds through Muharram, the month in which the martyrs of Karbala are mourned. Within its enclosures lie the graves of six holy women, remembered for their piety and for the miracle by which they are said to have chosen death over dishonour, together with the grave of their maidservant. For centuries the *darbar*, or shrine, has been a place of prayer, vows and refuge, cherished above all by women, and Sunni and Shia Muslims alike revere it.

Most pilgrims honour the Bibis as women of the Prophet Muhammad's own household who came to Lahore after the tragedy of Karbala, and tradition holds that when they were threatened the earth itself opened to receive them. Their shrine is one of the rare great shrines of the subcontinent whose sanctity is that of women.

## The Bibis

Accounts differ over who the six women were, and over when and from what country they came to Lahore. The question has been argued over by scholars for generations, and the debate is itself part of the shrine's history. The older and by far the more popular tradition, held by most pilgrims and reflected in the shrine's traditional founding date of about 63 AH, in the first century of Islam, makes them women of the household of the Prophet, the Ahl al-Bayt, the People of the House. In the nineteenth century it was the common tradition on people's tongues. A second tradition, advanced by the shrine's own custodial family and by a line of scholars, makes them the daughters of a saint of Lahore, Sayyid Ahmad Tokhta Tirmidhi, and the shrine holds meaning in both.

In the popular tradition the chief of the six was Sayyida Ruqayya bint Ali, a daughter of Hazrat Ali, the Prophet's son in law, sister of Hazrat Abbas the standard bearer, and wife of Muslim ibn Aqeel. With her were kinswomen and companions of the Prophet's house, among them women remembered as Bibi Hoor and Bibi Noor of the line of Abi Talib, six in all, together with a faithful camel driver named Basheer. Because of this the shrine is a memorial to the family of the Prophet, a place of especially intense devotion for Shia Muslims and for all who love the Ahl al-Bayt, and its whole atmosphere turns towards the remembrance of Karbala.

Both traditions know the six by the same names, each with the title Bibi, lady: Bibi Haj, Bibi Taj, Bibi Noor, Bibi Hoor, Bibi Gauhar and Bibi Shahbaz. Both share, too, the heart of the story: six women of extraordinary holiness, their choice of death over dishonour, and the miracle by which the earth is said to have received them. Divided over history, the traditions are united in reverence. As the shrine's own keepers say, asking forgiveness for any error in what they tell, no book survives from the holy women themselves, and all that is told of them has come down through later scholars and saints.

The Bibis are counted among the women who brought Islam to the subcontinent, and among the female mystics of Islam beside Rabia al-Adawiyya, the great woman mystic of its early centuries. Tradition remembers them as learned in reading and interpreting the Quran and versed in the *hadith*, the sayings of the Prophet, and holds that once they reached Lahore they set about spreading the faith. They are said to have founded assemblies of *zikr*, the remembrance of God, for the recitation of the Quran, to have guided the lives of ordinary people, and to have held mystical assemblies with male Sufi scholars, drawing those men into spreading Islam among the ordinary people of the Punjab who were not Muslims. They spoke to the scholars and to their audiences from behind the veil. Their teaching, in which mysticism had its place, awakened their hearers' interest in the discussion of Islam, and many people are said to have come to Islam through the blessing of their footsteps.

Devotional tradition also tells of an astrologer who boasted that he could read the hidden and the unseen. The elder Bibi had food prepared for the poor and for travellers, and sent him a dish in which fine food lay concealed beneath a plain covering of dry *khichri*, rice cooked with lentils. The astrologer scorned the humble rice, and when he learned what lay beneath it he was humbled, for a man who claimed knowledge of the unseen had not seen the feast under the *khichri*. He became a disciple. Such stories express what the shrine has always meant to ordinary people: a place of hospitality, of humility and of quiet wonder.

## The Journey from Karbala

In the popular telling, the story begins in the aftermath of Karbala, where in 61 AH Imam Hussain, the grandson of the Prophet, was martyred with his companions. The women's ordeal had begun even before that day. Sayyida Ruqayya's husband, Muslim ibn Aqeel, was the cousin and emissary whom Imam Hussain had sent ahead to the city of Kufa, and he was betrayed and martyred there together with his young sons. After the martyrdom of Hussain himself and his companions on the plain of Karbala, the surviving members of the Prophet's household were taken captive and for a year suffered the cruelty of the court of the caliph Yazid, a captivity so harsh, tradition holds, that the very sanctities of Islam were trampled. Hazrat Zain al-Abideen, Hussain's son and the surviving Imam, is said to have charged Sayyida Ruqayya to depart with a few of the women, so that the faith and the lineage of the Prophet's house might be carried to safety and continue.

She set out with her companions and, it is told, with the camel driver Basheer. He had served in Yazid's own palace and had once spoken harshly to the holy women, but he was bound to them by a hidden love, and he was trusted precisely because their destination was to be kept from strangers. They journeyed east across the length of the Muslim world until they reached the land of Hind, as the subcontinent was then known. At Lahore a Hindu raja, named in local tradition as Mahabrand Bhatti, held sway, and scarcely a Muslim was to be found. It is said that the women halted on a mound with no settlement around it, and that the raja's people were thrown into disorder, saying that descendants of the Prophet had come from Arabia. Tradition also preserves the women's lament in their own voice: first the parting from their brothers and the calamity of Karbala, then a strange land where no one understood even their speech, where they were poor travellers, wronged and homeless, beyond all help.

As the women arrived, wonders are said to have followed: the idols of the temples toppled and their lamps went dark. The raja, disturbed, sent his son, remembered as Raja Kumar Bikrama Sahay, with soldiers to discover who these strangers were. When the prince came before the women he was overwhelmed by a radiant light and fell senseless. On recovering he heard from their own lips the tale of Karbala, and he was so moved that he embraced Islam, took a new name, remembered as Abdullah Shah Khaki or Shaikh Jamal, and stayed among them as their servant. Tradition also remembers four *hafiz*, men who had memorised the whole Quran, who stayed behind with the women: Abu al-Fath, Abu al-Fazl, Abu al-Makarim and Sad Allah.

When the enraged raja came with an army to seize the women, they lifted their hands in prayer, begging God to spare them exposure to strangers. The earth opened and drew them into itself, closing again and leaving only the edges of their veils above the ground, and over these their graves were later raised. Their maidservant, Bibi Halima, is said to have been taken into the earth with her mistresses. From this guarding of their honour to the last comes the name by which the women are known, Pak Daman, "the pure of hem".

The tradition is steeped in the devotion of Karbala. On the frontispiece of one of its major histories stands the old Persian line of the lovers of the martyrs, a verse of devotion to the martyrs of the House of the Prophet:

خوشا آنان کہ در خاک و خون غلطیدند

*Blessed are those who rolled in dust and blood.*

## The Shrine

The graves lie within enclosed courtyards. Tradition places the six Bibis in two adjoining walled enclosures, Bibi Haj, Bibi Taj and Bibi Noor in the first and Bibi Hoor, Bibi Gauhar and Bibi Shahbaz in the second, their tombs built up in lime and plaster. Counted among them is the grave of their maidservant, Bibi Halima, remembered affectionately as Bibi Tanoori. The shrine has been rebuilt many times over the centuries, and its most recent major renovation was carried out in 2024.

In the nineteenth century the oldest parts of the fabric were attributed to Sultan Mahmud of Ghazni, who brought Lahore under Muslim rule in the early eleventh century. For four hundred years after the Bibis, it was said, Hindu rajas had ruled; then Mahmud built a wall of fired brick and *dalans*, halls open along one side, and a *dalan* with five doors, repaired from time to time, was attributed to the Ghaznavid sultans of his line. In the reign of the Mughal emperor Akbar many buildings were raised and the graveyard was laid out. Successive rulers attached endowments of land, or *waqf*, to the shrine, and its old land was continued under every ruler.

In that century the grave of one of the Bibis lay on a raised platform of white marble within a *pinjra*, a lattice screen, always covered with a *ghilaf*, a cloth cover for a grave, and its white plaster was attributed to the Ghaznavid sultans. To the south a lower, broken platform held two more graves, and to its west lay two maidservants whose names are not known. A *van* tree spread over the Bibis' tomb. The shrine's mosque, first built by Baba Khaki, had been rebuilt some years earlier, and a house with a *dalan* of three arches had been built for one of the *mujawirs*, the shrine's hereditary attendants.

In the 1880s these tombs, known together as the Mazars of Bibi Pak Daman, were famous among the city's Muslim shrines as the most ancient and blessed. The site, which was also called a *khanqah*, a Sufi lodge, was then counted among the Muslim sites outside the city, two miles from Lahore. A visitor entered from the west, followed a passage like a lane to a second gate, and turned north to the graves. They lay in three enclosures: an outer one holding the graves of the Bibis' maidservants and a domed mosque of *pakka*, or fired, brick; a large second enclosure with the graves of three of the Bibis; and a third holding the graves of Bibi Hoor, Bibi Gauhar and Bibi Shahbaz. Every enclosure was finely built in lime plaster. Many buildings had belonged to this graveyard, and many had fallen for want of care.

Holy men and others of later centuries sought burial in the women's company. Within the complex, in a corner of one of the enclosures, stands a domed masonry tomb of the eleventh century of the Hijra, about 1600 CE, in which lies Sayyid Jalaluddin Haider Bukhari, son of Safi al-Din Bukhari and brother of Shah Mauj Darya Bukhari, of the celebrated Bukhari sayyids of Uch. He died in 1016 AH and was buried by the wall of the Bibis' sanctum, and his tomb was built in 1017 AH. In the nineteenth century its door opened inside the shrine's *khanqah*, and people called it the tomb of the Bibis' teacher.

Others lie in the graveyard of the Bibis too: Khwaja Ayyub Qureshi Lahori, and Mufti Ghulam Muhammad, who died in 1274 AH, the son of Mufti Rahimullah Qureshi Lahori and the father of Mufti Ghulam Sarwar, a nineteenth century historian of Lahore's saints; a chronogram, *Khurshid-e Din-e Muhammad*, the sun of Muhammad's faith, honours him. Syed Mahmud II was buried beside the tomb of the Bibis, and in the nineteenth century the goldsmiths' graveyard lay next to the graveyard of the Bibis. In the 1880s the tomb of Shah Rahmatullah Qureshi stood to the north of the Bibis' *khanqah*.

The shrine stands within a wider sacred neighbourhood, and in 1974 it lay in the settlement of the Bibis Pakdamanan at Muhammad Nagar. Other holy figures are associated with the place, among them Hazrat Ali Rangrez and Hazrat Alif Shah, who is said to have come with the Bibis themselves at the time of their arrival.

The nearby quarter of Chilla Bibiyan holds the tomb of Sayyid Ahmad Tokhta Tirmidhi, the saint whom the second tradition makes the father of the six. He died in 602 AH, 1205 CE, near the beginning of the reign of Sultan Qutb al-Din Aibak, and his title *Murshid-e Punjab*, the guide of the Punjab, is a chronogram of that year, a phrase whose letters, counted as numbers, give the date. A large tomb once stood over his grave, but it collapsed after its marble was taken down under Maharaja Ranjit Singh, the Sikh ruler of the Punjab, and the graveyard around it was cleared for a *haveli*, a great mansion. By the 1880s the grave, solidly built, stood inside a stable, that of Hazrat Ghulam Muhi al-Din, where people came to visit it, and it is now enclosed within the property of a later custodial family. A Persian chronogram, or *tarikh*, preserved in his memory hails him in verse:

سیّد احمد، شہِ برنا و پیر
پیرِ ہادی، میرِ عالی جاہ

*Sayyid Ahmad, king of the young and the old; a guiding master, a lord of lofty station.*

## Keepers of the Shrine

The shrine's hereditary caretakers, the *mujawirs*, are traditionally held to descend from the convert prince, Abdullah Shah Khaki or Shaikh Jamal, who is said to have built the first shrine over the women's graves and to have served it for the rest of his life. He is said to have refused to return to his old faith and to have sat as the attendant of the women's tomb. It is told that at the tomb it was revealed to him that his marriage to a Hindu's daughter was lawful, and the land to the east and south of the *khanqah* was given to his descendants.

In the modern era the shrine has been taken into the charge of the Punjab Auqaf Department, the provincial government's department of religious endowments, which administers it as a shrine under Sunni management. Given the scale of the crowds, government security and the Punjab Police are kept on site at all times.

The question of who the Bibis were carries a sectarian dimension that persists to this day. Because the popular tradition makes the shrine a memorial of the Prophet's martyred family, it is a focus of intense Shia devotion, and the shrine gives its own devotional identity simply as Syed and Shia, while its formal administration and much of its custodial lineage are Sunni. Old disputes have gathered around this difference, over whether the graves are those of Sayyida Ruqayya and the Ahl al-Bayt or of a Punjabi saint's daughters, and over who may claim the shrine. Yet in practice Bibi Pak Daman is revered by Sunni and Shia alike, and the two devotions meet, without merging, in the same courtyards.

Its visitors are not only Muslims. In the early twenty first century Prem Gupta, a Hindu activist from Lahore, then forty years old, and head of the Hindu Sudhar Sabha, whose family lived near the shrine, visited it often, a habit one writer saw as part of "the religious syncretism that was once part of this land but is now vanishing".

## Devotion and Festivals

Bibi Pak Daman is a living centre of devotion, alive around the clock. Pilgrims come to recite the Fatiha, the opening chapter of the Quran, to tie vows, or *mannat*, and to seek the Bibis' intercession, or *waseela*, for healing, for children and for relief from every kind of hardship. Many recount cures received here after physicians had given up hope, and tell of the impossible made possible through the women's grace.

Women in particular find in the *darbar* a sanctuary, a place of comfort and of dignity, and in the affection of the city it is above all a women's shrine. For generations the mothers of Lahore have brought the Bibis their deepest hopes and fears, praying for children, for good marriages, for the safety of daughters, for healing and for protection from harm, and it is chiefly women who fill its courtyards, tie their threads of vow at its screens, and find in it a refuge that the world outside does not always offer them. In Pakistan, where the major mosques keep only a small space for women to pray on Fridays and at festivals such as Eid, and the neighbourhood mosques none, one scholar of women's devotion has concluded that "the important spheres of religious and spiritual participation for women are the Sufi shrines".

Every Thursday night the shrine fills for its largest weekly gathering, with *langar*, the free communal kitchen, *qawwali*, the devotional music of the Sufi shrines, *dhamal*, ecstatic devotional dancing to the beat of the drum, and prayer. A mosque attached to the shrine holds the five daily prayers.

The shrine's greatest season is Muharram, when the sorrow and devotion of Karbala are remembered; it was said in the nineteenth century that at Hussain's death the lament of Karbala rose in the Punjab too. Enormous crowds converge on the *darbar*, and many are said to walk barefoot in groups from their homes to reach it. On an ordinary day the shrine receives on the order of 8,000 to 12,000 visitors, some 250,000 in a month, and at the height of Muharram the numbers rise towards 1.2 million.

Music has a particular place here. Reshma, a renowned singer of Sufi poetry, said that she sang at the shrine whenever the Bibi summoned her, at any time of night or day, and both she and Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan counted themselves devotees of the shrine.

Lahore's own saints came here in devotion, and one tradition in particular is cited by devotees as proof of the shrine's great antiquity. It is said that when Data Ganj Bakhsh, the eleventh century saint Ali ibn Usman Hujwiri, author of the *Kashf al-Mahjub* and patron saint of Lahore, first came to the city, he would go every Thursday night to the graves of the Bibis and sit in attentive reverence at a respectful distance, out of *adab*, courtesy before their sanctity. He is remembered as a devotee of their shrines, where he offered prayers and spent considerable time in meditation.

That so great a saint should honour the shrine in this way is taken to show that the graves were already ancient and holy in his day, for the shrines are said to have stood long before he reached Lahore. He arrived in about 1035: Khattali, who had earlier ordered his disciple Shaikh Husain Zinjani to move to the Punjab, sent Hujwiri after him. The tradition binds the shrine to Data Darbar, the shrine of Data Ganj Bakhsh, the two standing together at the head of Lahore's sacred geography. Mian Mir, another of Lahore's saints, often went to the Bibis in devotion too.

## Legacy

However the question of who they were is answered, the meaning of the shrine is the same: it stands for purity, for courage, and for a refusal to bow before injustice at the cost of one's honour. In the nineteenth century even those who could not accept that the women had come from Arabia at the time of Karbala did not doubt that the place was most blessed. In a sacred landscape crowded with the tombs of male saints, Data Ganj Bakhsh, Mian Mir, Madho Lal Hussain and the rest, Bibi Pak Daman is something rare: a great shrine whose sanctity is that of women. The six Bibis are honoured not for conquest or office or learning alone, but for a purity and courage so absolute that they are said to have chosen to be swallowed by the earth rather than be seen by strangers. In this the *darbar* gives the women of the city a mirror of their own dignity, a place where female holiness stands at the very centre, and where the qualities most often asked of women, and most often unhonoured, are raised to the rank of the sacred.

That a shrine of such scale and antiquity should be dedicated to women is, in the religious geography of the subcontinent, almost without parallel. Across sectarian lines it stands as one of the clearest expressions in South Asian Islam of the veneration of female sanctity. By the 1880s all the people of Islam held the place blessed and served it with heart and soul, and amid the debates of scholars and the changing life of the city the *darbar* of Bibi Pak Daman endures as a place of refuge and of blessing, one of the deepest spiritual anchors of the old city.
