## Overview

Mangho Pir, on the northern outskirts of Karachi, is one of the most unusual and atmospheric shrines in Pakistan. It is famous for three things that meet nowhere else: a medieval Sufi saint, a pool of sacred crocodiles, and hot sulphur springs believed to heal. The *darbar*, or shrine, of Pir Mangho, Hazrat Haji Syed Sakhi Sultan, is a centre of pilgrimage of great antiquity. It is bound up with the culture of Karachi's Sheedi community, Afro-Pakistanis of East African descent, whose annual festival at the shrine is one of the city's most distinctive traditions.

## The Saint

The saint of Mangho Pir is remembered as a holy man of the medieval centuries who settled at this spot in the hills above Karachi. A *pir* is a Sufi spiritual guide, a master of the mystical path of Islam. Accounts differ over whom the shrine honours; it is known as the shrine of Pir Mangho, Hazrat Haji Syed Sakhi Sultan. The titles in that name carry meanings: *Hazrat* is a respectful title for a holy person, a *Haji* is one who has made the pilgrimage to Mecca, a *Syed* is a descendant of the Prophet Muhammad, and *Sakhi* means the generous one.

To one man, speaking of the big Sheedi gathering at the shrine, he was "the pir of the crocodiles".

## The Shrine

Below the tomb lies a pond inhabited by crocodiles. They are held sacred and cared for by the shrine's attendants, and the eldest and largest of them is honoured with the title *Mor Sahib*. Pilgrims make offerings of meat, for the crocodiles are believed to be under the saint's protection, and their favour is taken as a sign of blessing.

Nearby, hot sulphur springs rise from the earth. Bathing in their waters is believed to cure skin ailments and other diseases, so that the shrine is a place of healing as much as of prayer. The springs are a natural phenomenon, one of several thermal sites along the same anticline as Chandrakup, an anticline being an upward fold in the layers of rock.

The shrine stands in the hills above Karachi, in what is now the northern part of the city. Mangho Pir is also the name of the locality, which was home to a leper asylum.

## Devotion and Festivals

Mangho Pir holds a special place for the Sheedi community of Karachi, the descendants of Africans brought to the subcontinent over the centuries. The Sheedis regard the saint as their own and the shrine as a spiritual home. Their annual *Sheedi Mela*, the Sheedi fair, with its drumming, dance and offerings to the sacred crocodiles, is a vivid survival of African rooted culture in Pakistan and one of the most striking festivals of Karachi, binding the shrine to a living community and its heritage.

The festival falls in April. Sheedis come to it from all over, it is a large gathering, and it draws wide coverage in the press.

The shrine also keeps an annual *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of the saint's death.

## Legends

Tradition weaves the crocodiles and the springs into the miracles of the saint himself.

The springs have a place in Hindu tradition too. In the narratives of the Kapri community, Lalu Jasraj is the founder of the Kapris, and the guide and staff bearer of Rama, the hero of the Ramayana, and of his entourage. It is said that Lalu Jasraj stayed at a hermitage near the hot springs, and that story has led to the suggestion that the shrine was once associated with him, an association that would tie the shrine to the Hindu pilgrimage to Hinglaj. This is a possibility, not an established fact, and how widely the Lalu Jasraj stories are told outside the Kapri community of Gujarat is open to question.

## The Setting

The Sheedis, also known as Sidi or Syedi in Sindh and Gujarat, were first brought from Africa as slaves to work in the subcontinent. They came to Sindh continuously through the ages, and in large numbers because of the slave trade, and in Sindh too they were set free when slavery was abolished. Though free, some of them are attached to the feudal households of Sindh, and their native language is Sindhi. The Sheedis of Sindh and Kacch claim descent from Hazrat Bilal, a companion of the Prophet Muhammad.

The drumming of the Sheedi festival has its context in the community's music and ritual. The Sheedi drum, the *mugarmān*, is held sacred by Sheedi communities in Sindh, Baluchistan, Rajasthan and Gujarat, who use it in their shrines and for their rituals. In Sindh the Sheedis and the Manganhar musicians perform the ceremonial singing of devotional narratives, and Sheedi women perform ritual music in the households of the province.

Karachi itself is one of two important urban centres of Sufism, with Hyderabad in India, where members of the Qawwal Bachche lineage keep up their own heritage of *qawwali*, the devotional music of the Sufis. After the annual fair of three days at Sehwan, *fakirs*, wandering Sufi mystics, follow one another on to the shrines of Karachi. A legend tells how the city got its name: the area was known after an old woman, Mai Kolachi, whose son was killed by a fish; she tore the fish apart and retrieved him, and the place came to be known as Kolachi. At the time of Partition, the division of British India, Karachi was a minor port city, and migrants from India made it the largest city of Pakistan.

Karachi is also the starting point of the road to Hinglaj, the Hindu pilgrimage route of about 240 kilometres from Narayanpur. The growth of the city and the urbanisation of the areas where the early stops lay have caused all but a few of the pilgrims' resting places along it to disappear.

## Legacy

Pir Mangho is unlike any other shrine in Pakistan: a place where Sufi devotion, a pool of sacred crocodiles, healing sulphur springs and the African descended culture of the Sheedis come together. Ancient, strange and beloved, the *darbar* remains a centre of pilgrimage, healing and festival on the edge of the great city, and a monument to the many strands that weave through the popular religion of Sindh, among them, it has been suggested, a link to the Hindu pilgrimage to Hinglaj.
