## Overview

At Taunsa Sharif, in the Dera Ghazi Khan region of southern Punjab, stands the darbar, or shrine, of Hazrat Khwaja Muhammad Sulaiman Taunsvi (c. 1770 to 1850), the great Chishti saint honoured as *Pir Pathan*. He is remembered as a chief reviver of the Chishti order in the Punjab, and his *khanqah*, the Sufi lodge where disciples gather, became one of the most important centres of Chishti learning and devotion in the nineteenth century. His darbar remains a major place of pilgrimage in the south west of the province.

He came to Taunsa at his master's command and began by living there in a hut, and gradually the town came to prosper. There he taught, founded a school, kept a great free kitchen and guided a vast following, and his disciples spread the order across the region. After his death the seat passed to his grandson.

## The Saint

Khwaja Muhammad Sulaiman, also called Shah Sulaiman and, after Taunsa, Taunsvi, came of a Pathan family, whence his title *Pir Pathan*. His great grandfather Umar Khan and his grandfather Abd al-Wahhab were Afghans of the Ja'fariya tribe, and his father was Zakariya Khan. He was born around 1770, in 1184 AH, in the village in the hills that was his original home, three *kuroh*, an old measure of distance, from Taunsa.

His father died when he was four, and he was brought up and taught under his mother's care. From his youth he gave himself to religious learning and the Sufi path. He read fifteen *paras*, or parts, of the Quran with Mulla Yusuf Jafar and the remaining fifteen with Mian Hasan Ali, completing the Quran, and from the same teachers he learnt Attar's *Pand-nama* and Sa'di's *Gulistan* and *Bustan*, among other books. He went on to read further Persian literature with Maulvi Wali Muhammad, and then went to Kot Mithan, where he entered the *madrasa*, the religious school, of Qazi Muhammad Aqil. There he finished the Arabic textbooks in a short time and completed his study of the outward sciences.

While he was living at Kot Mithan, Khwaja Nur Muhammad Maharvi came to Uch, and he went there to attend on him. At sixteen he was honoured with *bai'at*, the oath of allegiance by which a disciple is initiated into a Sufi order, at Nur Muhammad's hands. Afterwards he kept going to and from his master at Mahar, sometimes setting out on foot. His *pir*, his spiritual guide, trained him in austerities and spiritual struggle, in *zikr-e jahr*, the vocal remembrance of God, and in *pas-e anfas*, the watching of the breath, and taught him books of *tasawwuf*, the Sufi path, among them the *Adab al-Talibin*, the *Ashara-e Kamila* and the *Fusus al-Hikam*.

At twenty two he received from his master the *khirqa* of *khilafat*, the robe of succession, given, it is said, with the permission of the Prophet himself. After the death of Hazrat Maulana Fakhr al-Din Fakhr-e Jahan he went to Delhi and kept a retreat of forty days at that master's tomb, returning to Mahar after the *fatiha*, the prayers for the dead, said on the fortieth day.

He became the major successor of Nur Muhammad Maharvi and one of his renowned *khalifas*, the deputies a master authorises to carry on his teaching, and he rose to be among the foremost Chishti shaykhs of his age. Nur Muhammad, who died in 1790, was himself the fourth in the line of succession from Shah Kalim Allah Jahanabadi, and the chain of masters ran from Kalim Allah Jahanabadi through Nizam al-Din Awrangabadi, Fakhr al-Din Dihlawi and Nur Muhammad Maharvi to Sulaiman himself.

It was not his own choice to settle at Taunsa. His pir commanded him to live there, and in obedience to his master's word he settled in the small town, at first in a hut, and began the work of guidance and instruction.

He loved his pir with a passion and lived in a renunciation and detachment without equal. He owned nothing but a *lungi*, a length of cloth wrapped around the waist, and in his *hujra*, his small cell, a mat was spread on which he sat to worship. He was a scholar as well as a mystic, with mastery of the Quran, of *hadith*, the reported sayings of the Prophet, and of *fiqh*, Islamic jurisprudence. He loved *sama'*, spiritual listening to music, and was a man of longing and spiritual taste; at times, in dance and ecstasy, he wept.

Both his sons, Khwaja Gul Muhammad and Khwaja Darvesh Muhammad, died in his lifetime. Accounts of the year of his death differ; it is generally given as 1850, or 1267 AH, and he died in the month of Safar. He was buried at Taunsa Sharif.

## His Order and Disciples

He belonged to the Chishti order, the Sufi order founded by Moinuddin Chishti and prominent in South Asia, and to its Nizami branch, which descends from Shaykh Nizam al-Din Awliya, who died in 1325. In the Punjab the wider spread of the Chishtiya Nizamiya Fakhriya order came about through him. A further branch of the Nizami Chishtis, the Sulaymaniyya, is named after him, so that a master of that line is properly called a Sulaymani-Nizami-Chishti shaykh. He is credited with reinvigorating the Chishti tradition in the Punjab at a time of political upheaval, and the lines that led to Sial Sharif and Golra Sharif owe much to his *khanqah*.

His *khalifas* were many. Three were especially close to him, beloved and sharers of his secrets: Shaikh Muhammad Yar, Hafiz Muhammad Ali and Maulvi Muhammad Ali Thani. Mian Abdullah Shah was also among the most famous. Hafiz Muhammad Ali, known as Muharram Ali Khairabadi, was among his earliest *khalifas* and became one of his major successors, and his *khanqah* at Khairabad was a centre of learning. Through him, and through other disciples of the saint, the Nizami branch of the Chishti order expanded into the upper Punjab.

Muhammad Fadil Shah Mirpuri, author of *Za'faran* and *Nafi' al-rasikhin*, was another of his *khalifas*, and so was Muhammad Allah Bakhsh Tawnsawi, whose *Ghaza' al-muhibbin wa samm al-mu'anidin* was compiled by Nur Muhammad Mak'hadi in 1314 AH (1896 to 1897). Another disciple, Ahmad Ali Khayrabadi, wrote the *Qasr-i 'arifan*, a general history of the Sufi orders and of the Indian dynasties, which Muhammad Baqir translated into Urdu (Lahore, 1965).

Two of his *khalifas* are counted among the saints of Lahore, one of them Shaikh Haji Ramzan Lahori. His lineage travelled far. Sufi Saheb, who died in 1916, brought the Chishti Nizami Sulaymaniyya to South Africa.

## The Shrine

Taunsa lies on the west bank of the upper Indus, 40 *kos*, an old measure of distance, from Dera Ghazi Khan, and the shrine is remote and difficult of access. Today it is a large complex, richly built and kept by the saint's successors, and a busy centre of pilgrimage in southern Punjab. The town of Taunsa Sharif takes its name and its identity from the darbar, which has remained, through those successors, a seat of Chishti teaching and devotion. After the saint settled there, given to worship, austerity and spiritual struggle, Taunsa gradually came to prosper. His *mazar*, or tomb, at Taunsa is held to be a fountainhead of spiritual effusion and blessing.

He founded a *madrasa* at Taunsa, a *dar al-ulum* or house of learning, because many people were ignorant of the sciences of the *shari'a*, the religious law, and its aim was to teach them. He himself taught books of the mystical path to select students there, and little by little it grew into a considerable centre of Islamic learning. He consolidated the order by stressing learning through such institutions: besides the academy at Taunsa he established other religious schools throughout the Punjab, linking each to instruction in the mystical insights of the Nizami branch of the Chishti order.

A library was built up and kept at his principal lodge in Taunsa, a collection from which future scholarship stands to benefit. The shrine holds a manuscript calendar of the death anniversaries of the saints, compiled at the end of the eighteenth century and arranged by the months of the Muslim lunar calendar. It does not keep strictly to the direct chain of Chishti masters: it includes figures from outside the Chishti line, such as the first four caliphs, and some little known, undated figures related by family to the main Chishti shaykhs of the line. Books were copied at Taunsa too; a manuscript of the *Adab al-muridin* of Muhammad Chishti was copied there around 1790.

Because both his sons had died before him, the seat passed over a generation. After his death his grandson, Khwaja Allah Bakhsh Taunsvi, became his *sajjada nashin*, the hereditary custodian who sits on the saint's prayer carpet, and he still held the seat in the 1870s. Born in 1231 AH, he died in 1901 (1319 AH). Like his grandfather he was active in spiritual reform and training, and the splendour of the Chishti order in the nineteenth century has been credited to him.

Hereditary custodianship, under the title *gaddi nishin*, the holder of the seat, can be traced in variant forms from the principal Chishti shrine at Ajmer to branch shrines such as Taunsa. Here the succession was later contested. Two branches of succession from the saint came into conflict at the shrine, one linked to his grandson Allah Bakhsh, the other to Mihr Ali Shah, who died in 1937. The power struggles within the Sulaymaniyya have been traced to British manipulation and to the rivalry of local Sikhs, Hindus and Muslim reformers; but political contest is only part of the story.

## Devotion and Teaching

The shrine keeps up the daily prayers, *qawwali*, the devotional music of the Sufis, and *naat*, poetry in praise of the Prophet, and the free kitchen, the *langar*. Its annual *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of the saint's death, draws great crowds.

In the saint's own lifetime thousands of seekers of God were honoured in his service, and thousands of travellers, destitute and poor people ate two meals a day from his *langar*. He is said to have won such acceptance as no one else had in that age: more than a *lakh* of people, that is, over a hundred thousand, became his disciples, and hundreds of unbelievers and sinners repented.

He held strictly to a daily discipline and never let it change in any circumstance. After *tahajjud*, the voluntary prayer of the late night, came vocal *zikr*; after the dawn prayer, a short rest in his cell; then he went out to guide others. After the midday meal he always rested; after the noon prayer he recited the Quran; then came the afternoon prayer. After the sunset prayer he spent a watch of the night in vocal *zikr*, then took the night meal, said the *'isha*, the last prayer of the day, with the congregation, and rested in his cell.

His life passed in worship, austerity, guidance and instruction. He insisted that the purpose of human life is the worship of God, often reciting the Quranic verse that God created jinn and humankind only to worship Him. He held that following the Prophet is indispensable for advancement in this world and in religion. He urged his hearers to shun lying, backbiting, dishonesty, greed, envy, vanity and pride; he condemned the love of the world and taught that the company of worldly people should be avoided as far as possible; and he commended humility, that one should count oneself the lowest and worst of all. Among his sayings are that none can be accepted at God's court without following the *shari'a* outwardly and inwardly, that Muslims should keep away from what the *shari'a* does not allow, and that following it is altogether necessary for purity of heart and spiritual progress.

What marks him out above all is the combination of spiritual standing and political activism. He urged his followers to take part in what has been called "resistance against Sikh aggression in the Punjab", and so, apparently for the first time in Chishti history, spiritual biographies were drawn into partisanship for one political group, the Muslims, against another, those who were not Muslim. Yet he also worked to keep cordial relations with Punjabi Hindus even while he opposed their Sikh neighbours.

## Legends

A story is told about where the wealth of the *langar* came from. A merchant devoted to the saint used to travel to Hindustan, sell his goods and go back, and on his way back he would call on the saint, present an offering, ask for a prayer and leave. Once, seeing a great deal of offerings coming in, he thought that all this *langar* and giving ran on the rupees of worldly people, and that if the offerings stopped the whole work would come to an end.

The saint, it is said, knew the merchant's misgiving by *kashf*, inner unveiling, and answered it with a precedent. Sultan Ghiyas al-Din had once held the same suspicion about the *langar* and the largesse of Hazrat Nizam al-Din Awliya, and forbade anyone at his court to attend on Nizam al-Din or to bring him any offering. When Nizam al-Din learnt of it, he ordered the spending doubled and told his servant to take what was needed from the niche of his *hujra*.

Then the saint's prayer mat was fetched from his cell. The merchant lifted it and was amazed to see beneath it a river flowing in three currents, one of *ashrafis*, the old gold coins, one of rupees and one of jewels. The mat fell from his hand, and he came out and begged forgiveness. The story is counted among his *karamat*, the miraculous deeds and spiritual gifts of a saint.

Another story tells of an old woman who came screaming and weeping, saying that her only son was dead. A servant suggested that the boy might only be in a swoon. The saint found him dead, with no pulse anywhere, but at the servant's request he looked at him closely. Through the attention of his heart the pulse stirred; at a second look it was beating well, and the boy sat up. He lived for a long time afterwards, stayed on in the saint's service, and was given the duty of drawing water.

## Legacy

Khwaja Muhammad Sulaiman Taunsvi is remembered as one of the principal architects of the Chishti revival in the nineteenth century Punjab, though accounts differ over whether his work is best called a revival. Many count him a founding figure of the Chishti recovery, and his *khanqah* at Taunsa nourished a generation of saints. His shrine remains one of the great Chishti centres of southern Punjab.

His life was told in verse several times, and his death was marked in chronograms, verses whose letters give the year of an event. 'Abd al-Rahman Khayrpuri wrote a verse biography, the *Hilya-i mubarak*; Muhammad Husayn Pishawari told his life in seventy one verses in the *Raz-i Hazrat-i Khwaja Muhammad Sulayman*; Khuda Bakhsh Tawnsawi wrote a *Manaqib-i Sulaymani* in verse; and Musa Buzdar, whose master Hafiz Ghulam Shah Sadid al-Din Chishti died in 1960 (1379 AH), wrote the *Fayd-i Muhtawi*, also called the *Muraqqa'-i Sulaymani*, a *mathnawi*, a long poem in rhyming couplets. Even his robe of succession had its poem: Ishaq Afghan, of about 1875, wrote the *Qamis-i sharif* in verse on the pedigree of his *khirqa*, as well as a *Manaqib-i Sulaymani*.

He became the subject of numerous biographies, an example of how the Persian literature of the Chishtis went on multiplying late, right up to the end of the nineteenth century. They include Ghulam Muhammad Chhachari's *Manaqib-i Sulaymani*, compiled in 1256 AH (1840), whose manuscript at Bahawalnagar has an appendix of 1287 AH (1870) telling of the author's later visits to Taunsa and of the saint's death; Yar Muhammad's *Intikhab-i manaqib-i Sulaymani*; Nizam al-Din Ahmad Bada'oni's *Rahat al-'ashiqin*; Najm al-Din Nagawri's *Manaqib-i mahbubayn*, lives of the saint and of Nur Muhammad Maharvi, completed in 1278 AH (1862) and printed at Lahore; Khuda Bakhsh Tawnsawi's *Muntakhab al-asrar*, composed around 1870 to 1872; and a *Manaqib-i Sulaymani* by Nabi Bakhsh. Other works that tell of him include the *Khatam-e Sulaimani*, Muhammad al-Din Sufi's *Zikr-e Habib*, the *Manaqib-e Hafiziya*, Amir Bakhsh's *Anwar-e Shamsiya*, Din Muhammad's *Ahtiram al-Asfiya*, also called *Nishat-e Hafiziya*, and Khaliq Ahmad Nizami's *Tarikh-e Mashaikh-e Chisht*.

His sayings, his *malfuzat*, were gathered in several collections, among them a *Malfuzat-i sharif* compiled by Ghulam Haydar; the *Nafi' al-salikin*, compiled by Imam al-Din Ghulam Shah in 1267 AH (1851 to 1852); and the *Muntakhab al-manaqib*, compiled by Yar Muhammad Dhawqi ibn Taj Muhammad Pakpattani. In 1979 a modern study, Muhammad Husayn Lillahi's *Hazrat Khwaja Muhammad Sulayman Tawnsawi awr un ke khulafa*, a Karachi University doctoral dissertation, was published at Lahore by the Islamic Book Foundation.
