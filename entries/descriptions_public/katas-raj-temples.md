## Overview

The Katas Raj Temples, also known as *Qila Katas* or simply Katas, form one of the most venerable Hindu sacred complexes in Pakistan. They stand at roughly 2,000 feet in the arid folds of the Salt Range on the Potohar Plateau, in Chakwal District, Punjab, near the town of Choa Saidanshah and a few kilometres from Kallar Kahar on the Motorway. Ancient shrines cluster around an emerald pond fed by a spring, the *Katas kund*, whose waters Hindus revere as holy, for tradition holds that they were filled by the tears of the god Shiva. Katas is a *tirtha*, a place of pilgrimage, and its great festival is Shivratri, the night of Shiva.

A short distance to the south west stands a second cluster, the *Satghara*, or "seven temples", the architectural and ceremonial heart of the complex. Occupied across many centuries and by many faiths, a holy site for thousands of years, Katas Raj preserves layers of Buddhist, Hindu Shahi, Sikh and later occupation. It is at once a place of pilgrimage, an archaeological palimpsest and an emblem of the subcontinent's shared heritage.

## Legends of the Pond and the Epic

The sanctity of Katas rests above all on the pond and the legend attached to it. In the tradition of the Puranas, the old Sanskrit books of myth and legend, the goddess Sati immolated herself, and her consort Shiva was plunged into such inconsolable grief that he wandered the earth weeping. His tears, it is said, fell to form two sacred pools, one at Pushkar in Rajasthan and the other here at Katas. The name Katas is popularly derived from the Sanskrit *kataksha*, "tearful eyes" or "raining eyes", an echo of the god's sorrow. The pond is thus held to be filled with the grief of Shiva himself, and bathing in its waters is believed to cleanse the pilgrim of sin.

Pilgrims and guides still tell the story. The pool is built in the shape of an eye, and its water, they say, can wash away the gravest of sins. In 2011 one of the guides, Shakeel, a young graduate from Kallar Kahar, warned visitors that no one knows how deep the pond is and that poisonous lizards and snakes live in it, and he told them a story of Ganesh, the son of Shiva. Beside the myth he kept a truth of his own: that the whole place was once under the sea, and that the pond was formed when the land emerged. As evidence he pointed to the fossil of a leaf, and to the fossil of an animal in a cave with stalactites across the road from the complex. Like Hinglaj, Katas is an ancient Hindu temple and place of pilgrimage around which such myths have developed.

Shiva, whose tears fill the pond in the legend, is one of the most important Hindu deities. In the literature after the Vedas he is known as the god of procreation, and he is also worshipped as the god of cultivation; in Hinduism, the worship of Shiva and the worship of the snake go hand in hand. An archaeologist has suggested that seals from Mohenjo-daro showing a figure seated in a yogic posture could be a prototype of Shiva.

Katas is also woven into the great epic tradition of the *Mahabharata*, the ancient Sanskrit epic. Legend makes the region the *Dvaitavana*, a forest of the epic, and tradition identifies the Satghara as the place where the five Pandava brothers made their home during part of their exile of twelve years, after the Kauravas had taken the throne. It is here, tradition holds, that the celebrated dialogue of the *Yaksha Prashna* took place, when a *yaksha*, a nature spirit guarding the waters, put a series of riddles to the Pandavas and tested the wisdom of Yudhishthira.

It is also believed that the Pandavas built the temples themselves, and that the *Mahabharata* mentions them as sacred spaces. In 2011 Shakeel dated the temples to the time of the epic, and a blue board put up by the authorities gave the date of the *Mahabharata* as around 1500 BCE. Such associations gave the site enduring prestige among pilgrims across the subcontinent.

The brothers' story touches the wider region again at its close. After Yudhishthira gave up his throne, at the coming of the *Kalijug*, in Hindu reckoning the last of the four ages of the world, the five brothers and Draupadi went through Bengal, the Deccan, Gujarat and Dwarka, toured the lands of Multan and the Punjab, and then went on to the mountain of Badrinath.

## The Temples

The Satghara temples represent a distinctive regional building tradition, generally attributed to the Hindu Shahi, or Odi Shahi, rulers who dominated the north western subcontinent from roughly the seventh to the tenth centuries CE. Accounts of their age and builders differ, but they are most often dated between the seventh and the tenth centuries. The principal shrine, sometimes called "Temple A" by surveyors, is dated to around the tenth century. It is built in the *Nagara* manner, the temple style of northern India, here influenced by Gandhara, with squat proportions, recesses under trefoil arches and the fluted ornament characteristic of the Salt Range group. The temples are built of the local *kanjur* stone and were once richly plastered, and some still keep traces of the frescoes that once enlivened their interiors.

The site's religious history reaches deeper still. In the seventh century the Chinese pilgrim Xuanzang noted a substantial Buddhist *stupa*, a domed shrine, in the vicinity, and in 1872 the British archaeologist Alexander Cunningham, surveying the complex as Director-General of the Archaeological Survey of India, documented Buddhist remains alongside the Hindu shrines, evidence of a long sequence of sacred occupation. A *baradari*, a pillared pavilion, and other later buildings cluster near the pond, and small living quarters show that the site remained a living centre of pilgrimage well into the modern era.

The complex holds a natural pond, fossils millions of years old, ancient caves, a Buddhist stupa, Hindu temples said to be thousands of years old, and the site of an ancient university. Several small temples stand around the pond, dedicated to different Hindu deities, and three tall cone shaped temples rise in the complex. An iron fence protects it, with only one entrance, though a visitor can also get in by climbing the mound. The pond is round and turquoise. A rock divides off a small section of clear water only three feet deep, and ancient buildings stand at the edge of the deeper end. The pond is clearly the lifeline of the complex, and numerous myths have developed around it.

Next to the pool stands the shrine of Shiva, a small light brown building with a small dome at the top, a wooden door covered with floral decoration and a marble floor. At its centre is the Shivling, the *lingam*, the phallic form in which Shiva is depicted, which represents the potent force of the god. In Hindu temples the *lingam* is placed inside a *yoni*, the female emblem, and together they represent the divine power of procreation. The shrine is a small room that can hold only about ten people.

The tallest building in the complex belongs to the Sikh period: the *haveli*, or mansion, of Hari Singh Nalwa, a general in the army of Maharaja Ranjit Singh (1780 to 1839). It stands like a cube with a small dome at the top, with the Shiva temples behind it. By 2011 its paintings had recently been renovated, with Hindu deities on the walls, among them Ganesh, and Krishna playing the flute as he flirts with the maidens.

Next to the haveli is a Buddhist stupa that has been partially excavated, with more than half of the structure still underground, and beside it stands a temple dedicated to Ram. There is no settled view of the stupa's age, which has been put anywhere between the third century BC, the time of Taxila, and at least four thousand years ago.

Across the road, on a mound, an ancient staircase leads up to a single storey rectangular building, put up a few years before 2011, which serves as the office of the Archaeology Department; a small hotel also stands across the road from the pond. The building stands on what is held to be the site of a world famous ancient Hindu university, which drew scholars from other parts of the world.

The university carries one of the site's most repeated stories. It is said that Al-Beruni, the Muslim scholar of the eleventh century, studied the Hindu religion here and calculated the radius of the world. He came to India in the first half of that century, after the Muslim invader Mahmud of Ghazni attacked the Punjab, and arrived at this temple, which, the story holds, was known as a seat of learning at that time. He is said to have spent several years here.

## Guru Nanak and the Sikh Period

Katas holds significance for Sikhs as well as Hindus. Tradition holds that Guru Nanak (1469 to 1539), the founder of Sikhism, visited the site, which in his time was a popular destination for ascetics. In the *Janamsakhis*, the traditional stories of his life, Nanak, on his return to the Punjab, visits the shrines of Tilla Balnath and Katas Raj together with Mardana, who travelled with him. A gurdwara, a Sikh place of worship, associated with his memory was later raised at Katas.

A modern reconstruction of the visit imagines it at the annual festival of Shivratri, on a cold February night, when Katas Raj, on the slope of the mountain, was the holiest of holy temples in the Punjab, a cluster of temples around the sacred pond. In this scene the Shiva temple at the edge of the pool is the most important there, and there are temples dedicated to the goddesses Kali and Durga. Pilgrims from hundreds of kilometres away have come to purify their lives. Some devotees bathe the Shiva shrine with milk while others sing his praises, a few yogis, devotees of Shiva, sit in meditation in a small cave, and pilgrims climb to temples higher up. Nanak sits inside the temple among the devotees, while Mardana waits on the neighbouring mountain, because as a Muslim he would not have been allowed inside such a sacred Hindu space. A reconstruction of the lives of Nanak's parents likewise has his mother Tripta and her husband stop at Katas Raj on their way home from Tilla Jogian, and both of them take a dip in the holy pond.

The gurdwara itself has gone unrecognised at the site. Guides at Katas have said that there is no gurdwara there at all, though the writer Iqbal Qaiser had seen and photographed the place for his book. He placed it next to the fort, and the only fort at Katas is the one built by Hari Singh Nalwa. The building stands at a little distance from the complex. It was not renovated with the rest, and by the middle of the 2010s the path to it had been neglected and its roof was gone: what remained was a big building with several pillars that once supported the roof, and a staircase leading nowhere. The guides did not know it: "We never knew what this building was," one of them said.

Under the Sikh Empire of Maharaja Ranjit Singh in the early nineteenth century, the complex received renewed patronage, with repairs and plastering to the principal structures attributed to the noble Sardar Gulab Singh. The Sikh period also left the haveli of Hari Singh Nalwa, Ranjit Singh's general, the tallest building in the complex, and the fort he built near the ruin of Nanak's gurdwara.

Ranjit Singh, remembered as the first Punjabi to rule the Punjab, became its sovereign at the end of the eighteenth century. Across the province he spent large sums on the renovation and expansion of many gurdwaras, and attached vast tracts of land to them so that the revenues from the land could support their running.

## Partition, Neglect and Restoration

Before Partition Katas was a popular religious site for the Hindus, and thousands of pilgrims came from faraway places for Shivratri. The Partition of 1947 severed the temples from their community of worshippers, and the site was abandoned and fell into prolonged neglect. Its buildings fell into disrepair, the pond was polluted and from time to time depleted, and a few cement factories opened in the surrounding area and began drawing water out of it. No one guarded the place. Names and telephone numbers were cut into its walls, and could still be seen in 2011, and the few Hindus who came did so pretending to be tourists. Prem Gupta, who first came in 2003, remembered boys jumping into the sacred pond from the windows and alleys of the temples and leaving juice boxes and crisp packets in the water.

Things changed in 2005, when the veteran Indian politician L. K. Advani came to Katas and expressed his displeasure. That year the Government of Pakistan began a programme of restoration, and major renovation work followed: the pond was cleaned, the buildings were painted and restored, and blue boards were put up. When the site was renovated, the Archaeology Department appointed a few guides, among them Shakeel.

The water level of the sacred pond also fell alarmingly, a drop attributed to the cement factories around the site drawing out the groundwater, an issue that reached the country's Supreme Court. After writers and journalists took up the matter, the government acted, and by the middle of the 2010s the water level had been restored. The pond had recently been renovated and was brimming with water, free of the garbage of earlier years, and Muslim boys dived into it from the rooftops. The cement factories that dot the area had also lobbied hard for the widening of the single lane road to Katas, which the National Highway Authority then had in hand.

The care did not hold everywhere. At Shivratri in 2011 there was no electricity. Haroon Sarab Diyal, who led the pilgrims from Peshawar, said that the authorities had not paid the temple's bill for the past three months, and added: "Ever since the Indians have stopped coming, they have become negligent again. The temple is falling into disrepair." The only washroom the pilgrims could use closed at eleven at night. Yet something had changed. The small building that was the only restaurant in the area served the pilgrims, though just a few years earlier it would have refused them, so that untouchability, at least here, had receded.

## Shivratri and Pilgrimage

The great festival of Katas is Shivratri, a Hindu festival dedicated to the worship of Lord Shiva. Its name means the night of Shiva, and its fuller form, *Maha Shivratri*, the great night of Shiva. The celebration includes the worship of Shiva throughout the night and a symbolic marriage between him and his counterpart Parvati.

Pilgrimages by Hindu devotees from India across the border have occasionally been permitted. After the restoration of 2005 the festival drew pilgrims from India: in the following year the government invited Indian pilgrims for Shivratri, and about three hundred came, which became national news. They stayed for three days, with security, shelter and free food, lodged in the building on the site of the ancient university, in an area cordoned off for them. This continued until 2008. After the Mumbai attacks of 26 to 29 November 2008, pilgrims did not come to Katas for Shivratri in 2009. In 2010 Hindu organisations wrote to the Archaeology Department, the Evacuee Trust Property Board (ETPB), the police and the Ministry of Minorities, and received no response. Even so, about two thousand Hindus came to Katas unannounced from cities across Pakistan, among them Lahore, Rahim Yar Khan, Peshawar and Nowshera.

Shivratri in 2011 fell on 2 March. That afternoon pilgrims were preparing for the night, for which about two thousand were expected from different parts of the country, and Hindu children from Gujranwala played on the steps of the pond. Prem Gupta's banner read "Welcome Yatrees (pilgrims)" and bore a picture of Lord Shiva: a snake is coiled around his neck, on his forehead is the third eye with which he can destroy the world, and the moon is in his hair. A canopy had been raised inside the complex.

Only five police officials stood at the gate. A professor and students from the National College of Arts (NCA) were turned back, a police official telling them, "Muslims are not allowed inside today", while Hindu boys went in wearing a "Maha Shivratri" tag. About a hundred pilgrims came from Peshawar in two large buses, led by Haroon Sarab Diyal, and more from Nowshera, Rahim Yar Khan and Bahawalpur; once all the buses had come, there were about a thousand people. A Deputy Superintendent of Police (DSP) at first refused to open the Archaeology Department's hall, citing the security conditions of the country, and then opened one hall for the women, the children and the old.

The next morning the department's officials found their offices invaded and the temples full of sleeping pilgrims, and junior officials threw the pilgrims' luggage, their utensils for the *langar*, the free communal kitchen, and their rice onto the road. It was too cold to take a bath, so the pilgrims poured the water of the pond over their heads, in place of the ritual ablution, or *ashnan*.

## The Setting

Pilgrims come to Katas from far away, because most of Pakistan's Hindus live far from the Salt Range, in what have been called the "faraway" and "peripheral" regions of the southern Punjab, inner Sindh and Baluchistan; the majority of the community is in Sindh and the southern Punjab. The conditions under which Hindus live in central Punjab have been called the harshest of any region, and many Hindus in the Punjab pass as Muslims or Christians by taking names that are not Hindu, a survival technique in a hostile environment.

Across Pakistani Punjab, hundreds of temples and gurdwaras were taken over after 1947 by refugees and turned into houses, and several were taken over and destroyed by the migrants. It has also been argued that folk religious practices at shrines in Pakistan, even though Islamic in their garb, have roots in the different spiritual traditions that developed around the cult of Shiva.

## Legacy

Today the Katas Raj Temples stand as one of the most significant surviving monuments of Hindu civilisation in Pakistan, a place where myth, epic and archaeology converge. The complex embodies the deep antiquity of Hindu worship in the Salt Range and remains a treasured place of pilgrimage and a heritage site, at once fragile and enduring, its weathered shrines still mirrored in the tears of Shiva.

For the Hindus of Pakistan it is still the place they come to at Shivratri, from Peshawar and Nowshera, Lahore, Bahawalpur and Rahim Yar Khan, even in years when no one answered their letters. It keeps the memory of Guru Nanak's visit, in a ruined building that the site's own guides could not name. And for its guides it remains a place of many stories at once, Shiva's tears beside an ancient sea, the Pandavas beside Al-Beruni, gathered around the pond that is clearly the lifeline of the complex.
