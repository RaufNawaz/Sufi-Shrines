## Overview

Gurdwara Shaheed Ganj Singh Singhnian, in the Naulakha Bazaar quarter of Lahore, outside Delhi Gate, is among the most solemn of Sikh memorials in Pakistan. Its name, from *Shahid Ganj*, "treasury of martyrs", and *Singhnian*, "of the Sikh women", commemorates the many Sikhs, above all the women and children, who were tortured and killed here during the great persecutions of the mid eighteenth century. It stands within the cluster of martyrdom sites at Naulakha, close to the shrine of Bhai Taru Singh, that together mark one of the darkest chapters of Sikh history, and Sikh pilgrims to Lahore come here to pay homage.

The gurdwara, a Sikh place of worship, is also known simply as Gurdwara Shaheed Ganj, and its history is contested as well as sacred. Its building was claimed as a mosque, and the question was fought out in a court battle lasting generations, from British rule into Pakistan's, before the present gurdwara was completed in 2004.

## The Martyrs

After the struggles between the Mughals and the Afghans in the 1740s, Muin-ul-Mulk, the governor of Lahore from 1748, known to the Sikhs as Mir Mannu, set himself to the extermination of the Sikh community. In his time a general order went out to seize and kill the Sikhs; it was given, it is said, because their numbers had grown. Mobile columns hunted the Sikhs who had taken refuge in the forests and wastes, and captives were brought in numbers to Lahore. The men, refusing conversion, were put to death. The women and children were consigned to a prison where they were set to grinding heavy quotas of grain by hand, starved and brutalised.

Sikh tradition preserves the memory of mothers made to watch the killing of their children, and of their steadfast refusal to abandon their faith in exchange for their lives. Traditional accounts speak of enormous numbers of dead. Such figures cannot be verified and are best understood as expressions of collective memory, but the historical reality of a sustained and savage persecution under Mir Mannu is well attested.

It is the constancy of the women, the *Singhnis*, that gives this gurdwara its name and its particular sanctity. In the retelling that every Sikh child learns, the women endured imprisonment, forced labour and the murder of their infants without renouncing the *Khalsa*, the community of initiated Sikhs, and their courage transformed a place of suffering into a place of honour.

The historian Iqbal Qaiser, who was asked to supervise the rebuilding of the gurdwara, tells of men, women and children slaughtered here and thrown into a well on the governor's orders, and of a room built to hold prisoners before their execution. When the gurdwara was being rebuilt, skeletons of children and adults would emerge from the ground, and he identified them as the Sikhs killed on Mir Mannu's orders.

## The Gurdwara

The site came to be venerated as a *shahid ganj*, a treasury of martyrs. Accounts of how the gurdwara began differ. In its own tradition, a gurdwara was raised here after the consolidation of Sikh power in the later eighteenth century, to enshrine the memory of the martyrs. In the nineteenth century a large building stood here, a *samadh* of the Sikhs, a memorial raised because Sikhs had been put to death on that spot, and the *samadh* was given the name Shahid Ganj.

The gurdwara lies outside Delhi Gate, in the area known as Naulakha Bazaar, where Prince Dara Shikoh is said to have had his Naulakha palace. In the nineteenth century the Shahid Ganj quarter also held the tomb of Mir Mannu himself. In the 1870s a tomb in Mohalla Sadhwan, the mass grave of Muslim martyrs of the Ghaznavid period, also bore the name Shahid Ganj; it may or may not be the same place as the Sikh Shahid Ganj at Naulakha.

Today the gurdwara is maintained under the Evacuee Trust Property Board, the state body that administers the properties of communities displaced at Partition, and pilgrims visit it alongside the neighbouring shrine of Bhai Taru Singh. It is austere, less an architectural showpiece than a site of remembrance.

## Devotion

At the gurdwara the recitation of the *Guru Granth Sahib*, the Sikh scripture, and the *ardas*, the formal prayer of the Sikhs, keep alive the names and the example of those who died. In the nineteenth century, too, the scripture was read at Shahid Ganj, and formerly, in the Maharaja's time, the place had been a source of open bounty to all.

More widely, when Maharaja Ranjit Singh became the sovereign of the Punjab at the end of the eighteenth century, he spent large amounts of money on the renovation and expansion of many gurdwaras, and attached vast tracts of land to them, so that the revenues from the lands could support their functioning.

## The Dispute

In the colonial period and after it, the Naulakha martyrdom sites became focal points of Sikh commemoration and, at times, of communal contest over ownership. Here, in the telling of Iqbal Qaiser, the quarrel goes back to a small mosque that stood on a vacant plot, from which the *qazi*, the judge under Islamic law, would announce punishment to criminals. "The mosque was small and was never used as a place of worship", he says. Under Maharaja Ranjit Singh, he goes on, the Sikhs "placed the Guru Granth Sahib inside it, converting it into a gurdwara. The Muslims could not do anything then". In the nineteenth century, too, the Sikhs used one of two mosques that stood near a *sarai*, a travellers' inn, outside Delhi Gate.

Under British rule, Qaiser relates, the Muslims applied to the courts for the building's return. The Lahore High Court asked for a living witness who had seen it used as a mosque, found none, and decided in favour of the Sikhs, who then razed the building. Rioting between Muslims and Sikhs followed, which Qaiser lays at the door of the British policy of divide and rule. Muhammad Ali Jinnah and Allama Iqbal, he says, became involved, and he has seen a photograph of Jinnah addressing a crowd at the Badshahi Mosque at the time of the riot. In the end, in his words, "the gurdwara remained a gurdwara".

After Partition, with the creation of the Auqaf Department, the government department of religious endowments, the question came back to the Lahore High Court, which ruled around 1957 or 1958 that the building would remain a gurdwara. In the late 1980s petitioners sought a review once again. The judge said he would convert the site if they could produce even one person who had prayed at the mosque, and none was produced.

In about 1994 or 1995 the Kar Sewa Committee of England sought permission to renovate the gurdwara, and the government agreed. Avtar Singh Panghedha asked Qaiser to supervise the construction, and, Qaiser says, "all the money, the contracts ... went through me". When the newspapers reported the work, in his words, "the Muslims tried once again to create an issue". The opposition was turned, as he tells it, by an ironsmith named Rana, who persuaded the local traders that the gurdwara would attract foreign pilgrims. The traders of Naulakha Bazaar became stakeholders in the project, and the gurdwara was at last completed in 2004.

## The Setting

Ahmad Shah Abdali, the Afghan, made his first assault on Lahore in 1748. Jani Khan, with his brother Mir Mannu, defeated Ahmad Shah, who went back to Kabul, and after his going Mir Mannu ran the Punjab in his own way. In one modern reading of the governor's war, Mir Mannu was constantly pestered by the Sikh insurgent groups called *misls*, which had sprung up all over the Punjab; they did not control much land, but because of their politics against the status quo the lower rungs of society supported and protected them. In this reading he cast the battle between the state and the insurgents as a war between Muslims and Sikhs, even though there were several Muslims in the Sikh groups and many soldiers who were not Muslims in his own army.

Ahmad Shah had agreed terms under which the governor was to pay him an annual tax for the right of governing the Punjab. Before Ahmad Shah could reach Lahore, "the prize possession of Punjab", Mir Mannu met him at a place near the city of Gujrat. On a later raid, in 1751, the Afghan bypassed the governor and laid siege to the city: "For months the citizens were trapped within the walls of their city". After Mir Mannu's death, wherever a Sikh found the chance, he set himself up as a ruler. The upheaval left its mark on the Muslim scholars of the province too: Ghulam Murtaza Qasuri, worn down by the Sikh disorders in the Punjab, migrated to Peshawar.

One modern writer, looking at these years from the side of the Punjab's peasantry, wrote that "Marauding Sikh bands formed themselves into robber baronies and imposed their own system of extortion on the Punjab farmer", and that Abdali's treatment of the Punjab weakened the government in Lahore and delivered the various *doabs*, the lands between its rivers, into the hands of the Sikh confederacy. In the same writer's words, "As Bulleh Shah lay dying in 1758 at the age of 78, the rape of the Punjab was proceeding apace."

The poet himself left verses on the state of the province that his translators read as a witness to the times. One *kafi*, a Sufi devotional lyric, speaks of 'The twelfth century' that 'has come with gaping mouth', the twelfth century of the Hijri era, the Islamic calendar, which answers to the eighteenth century of the Common Era. It goes on:

*"The door of doom and torment has opened, and Panjab is in a bad state. It has been struck by fear of the pit of hell."* (translated by Christopher Shackle)

In another translation:

*"Now hell has opened its bogs. / Punjab has gone to the dogs. / This hell is the nethermost hell." … "This century cramps our style."* (translated by Taufiq Rafat)

As that translator explained, "The full bitterness of Bulleh Shah's 'Punjab has gone to the dogs' will only come through against this backdrop", a Punjab "being persistently rent by petty chiefs and marauders". Another of the poet's verses is taken to be an allusion to the Sikhs, "whose guerilla horsemen ultimately destroyed the Mughal authority in Panjab".

## Legacy

In Sikh historical consciousness the martyrdoms at Lahore under Mir Mannu occupy a place beside the sacrifices of the Gurus themselves, the founding teachers of the Sikh faith, and they are invoked daily in the *ardas*, which recalls "the Singhs and Singhnis who gave their heads but did not surrender their faith". Gurdwara Shaheed Ganj Singh Singhnian is the physical anchor of that memory in the city where it happened. For the study of the Punjab in the eighteenth century it testifies to the ferocity of the state's campaign against the early *Khalsa*; for the Sikh community it remains sacred ground.

The rebuilding had consequences beyond Naulakha. Afterwards "the Government of Pakistan woke up to the potential of Sikh pilgrimage tourism" and invited the Sikh community abroad "to invest in the renovation of any Sikh gurdwara in the country". The long quarrel also became a byword: at Sachkand, "a situation similar to Gurdwara Shaheed Ganj" was named as the outcome to be feared.
