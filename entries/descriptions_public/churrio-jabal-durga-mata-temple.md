## Overview

The Churrio Jabal Durga Mata Temple crowns a rocky hill in the Nagarparkar region of Tharparkar District, in the far south east of Sindh, amid the ancient granite of the Karoonjhar mountains. Dedicated to the goddess Durga, it is one of the most important Hindu pilgrimage sites in Pakistan, a place where the goddess is venerated as the protectress against evil and where the faithful come to immerse the ashes of their dead in waters held to be sacred. Set in a desert landscape of great beauty and antiquity, the temple is a living centre of devotion for the substantial Hindu population that still inhabits Tharparkar, one of the few districts of Pakistan where Hindus remain a large share of the people.

## Churrio Hill

The temple stands upon Churrio Hill, one of the outcrops of the Karoonjhar range that rises abruptly from the Thar desert near the town of Nagarparkar. The name is a Sindhi word: *churrio* derives from the term for a bangle, and the surrounding villages are said to have long been associated with the craft of making the glass and lac bangles worn by the women of the region. The hill and its temple thus carry, in their very name, the memory of that local trade. The rugged Karoonjhar mountains in which the temple sits are themselves objects of reverence and pride, celebrated in Sindhi poetry and regarded as a natural and cultural treasure of the province.

## Pilgrimage and Devotion

Churrio Jabal is above all a place of pilgrimage. Its greatest festival is *Maha Shivratri*, the great night of Shiva, kept in the spring, when the temple draws enormous crowds, by some estimates as many as two hundred thousand pilgrims. They come from across Sindh and from Balochistan, Punjab and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa for vigils that last the whole night, the singing of *bhajans*, or devotional songs, and the performance of rites at the shrine.

A central act of devotion here is the immersion of the cremated ashes of the dead in the sacred waters associated with the site. This rite makes Churrio Jabal a place of last farewells as well as of festival joy, and it binds families across the region to the goddess of the hill. Through the year, pilgrims climb the stone paths to the summit temple to offer worship to Durga and to seek her protection.

## History and Conservation

The precise age of the temple is not securely documented. It is best described as ancient in the broad sense, its foundations lost in the long Hindu history of the Thar. What is certain is that, unlike so many temples elsewhere in Pakistan, Churrio Jabal survived the upheavals of Partition as a functioning place of worship, sustained by the large Hindu community of Tharparkar, where Hindus have continued to form a substantial portion of the population, by many accounts approaching half.

In recent years the site has become a focus of conservation concern, above all because of the threat that stone quarrying and mining posed to the Karoonjhar range. In October 2023 the Sindh High Court declared the whole of the Karoonjhar mountains a protected monument under provincial law, prohibiting mining and directing that the cultural and ecological heritage of the area, its temples included, be safeguarded.

## The Goddess

Durga, the goddess of the hill, is worshipped far beyond Tharparkar and under many names. She is Durga Mata, Mata Durga and Sheranwali, the One with the Tiger, because she rides a tiger, and her Hindu pilgrims call her *Mata*, mother. Other goddesses are counted as her forms: Kali Mata is an incarnation of Durga, and the temple at Hinglaj honours an incarnation of Durga.

*Navratri* is a festival dedicated to Durga, for which devotees stay up for nine consecutive nights praying to the goddess. It is a celebration associated with the worship of nine different incarnations of the goddess. As one priest explains, one of her incarnations is an adolescent girl, and the *Kunwari pooja*, the worship of the maiden, is the worship of that incarnation. Hindu pilgrims travel to a shrine of Durga during Navratri, wearing uniform shirts, and devotees believe that by travelling to the shrine of Durga Mata all their wishes will come true.

Hindu devotees offer the goddess a *chunri*, a cloth worn over the head, and at a shrine of Durga these are bought in and around the shrine itself. It is told of Lehna that, before he became a devotee of Guru Nanak, he had been a worshipper of the Goddess Durga, and that each year during Navratri he led pilgrims to Jwalamukhi, now in Himachal Pradesh.

Her name is also raised aloud in celebration. At Holi in Multan, at the breaking of the *matka*, an earthen pot, the boys shout 'Bolo bolo bolo Durga Mata ki' and the crowd answers 'Jai', victory; then 'Bolo Sheranwali ki', and again 'Jai'.

## The Setting

Most of Pakistan's remaining non-Muslims are Hindus living in southern Punjab, inner Sindh and Baluchistan. In the census of 1998 the Hindus were the largest minority in the country, with Christians in second place. The majority of the Hindu community is based in Sindh and southern Punjab, where both the lower and the upper castes are represented in proportion, and there Hindus are a part of the social fabric of the area. After the demolition of the Babri Mosque on 6 December 1992, Hindu communities in Sindh and Quetta were attacked.

Sindh is also home to a community that stands between Hindu and Sikh. Nanak Panthi Sikhs belong to the areas of southern Punjab and Sindh, and they are found in very large numbers in the province of Sindh; their temples there hold portraits of Guru Nanak alongside Hindu deities. Followers of Guru Nanak who refused the *Khalsa*, the order of initiated Sikhs, still exist in Sindh and in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, and baptised Sikhs look down upon them.

There are said to be several shrines in the Multan region and in Sindh where people still practise Hinduism and give it the name of Islam. It has been argued that Tableeghi, or missionary, preachers focus on southern Punjab and rural Sindh, where syncretistic religion held sway, and that the focus of the Deoband has been on southern Punjab and Sindh, where there is a ubiquitous saint and shrine culture.

In Aurangzeb's reign the Mughal province, or *suba*, of Thatta was reckoned at two hundred and fifty *kos*, an old measure of distance, in length, from Bhakkar to Kech Makran, and a hundred *kos* in breadth, from the town of Badin to the port of Lahri; on the east it bordered Gujarat Ahmadabad. Its four districts, or *sarkars*, were Thatta, Siwistan, Nasirpur and Amarkot, comprising fifty seven *mahals*, or revenue divisions, and five ports. South of the line from Uch to Gujarat lay sand hills, the country of the Bhatti, whose chief's seat was Jaisalmer, and of other Rajput peoples, and the Jareja were among the peoples living from Bhakkar to Nasirpur and Amarkot. The greatest river was the Sind, and large armies rarely went by land, because of the jungle and the lack of water.

Brahmanabad had been a great city and a seat of rule, its fort with fourteen hundred towers, each a rope's length, or *tanab*, from the next; after it came Dewal, and in Aurangzeb's reign Thatta and Debal were the seat of government. The province had mines of salt and iron in abundance, a yellow stone quarried for building and forty thousand boats great and small, and the diet there rested on rice, curds and fish. Kutch was a separate country, with Sind to its west.

## Legacy

The Churrio Jabal Durga Mata Temple is a powerful emblem of the enduring Hindu life of Sindh, a faith not merely remembered in ruins but still practised, in festival and in mourning, on a desert hilltop at the edge of the country. Together with the nearby Jain and Hindu monuments of Nagarparkar, it makes the Karoonjhar region one of the richest landscapes of religious heritage in Pakistan, and its continued vitality, and the recent moves to protect its mountains, offer a hopeful counterpoint to the losses suffered by sacred sites elsewhere.
