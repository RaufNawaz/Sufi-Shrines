## Overview

Gurdwara Baoli Sahib stands in the crowded Dabbi Bazaar at the very heart of the walled city of Lahore, where its precinct once adjoined the Loha, Kesera and Guru bazaars. It commemorates a visit to Lahore in 1599 by Guru Arjan Dev, the fifth Sikh Guru, and takes its name from the *baoli*, a roofed stepwell, that the Guru is remembered as having had sunk at this spot to provide water for the people of the quarter. Guru Arjan compiled the Adi Granth, the Sikh scripture, and was the first martyr of the Sikh faith; his principal memorial in Lahore is Gurdwara Dera Sahib by the Fort, and as a shrine tied to him, Baoli Sahib is a significant station on the map of Sikh Lahore. Today the site is also known as Baoli Bagh, its surroundings laid out as a garden.

The well is said to have been filled in during the turbulence of the eighteenth century. It was found again and restored in the reign of Maharaja Ranjit Singh, and by the 1880s the house of Baoli Sahib was the largest in extent of the famous Sikh places of worship in Lahore.

## The Guru

Guru Arjan was the fifth Sikh Guru and the son of the fourth, Guru Ram Das, who appointed him as the next Guru. He was born in 1563 and became Guru in 1581; accounts of the year of his death differ, but it is most often given as 1606. He was the younger brother of Prithi Chand, and it was the younger son whom their father chose.

Lahore stands at the root of that succession. It is told that Prithi Chand hid the letters Arjan sent from Lahore to Ram Das, and that Ram Das then disowned Prithi Chand and, a few days later, appointed Arjan as the next Guru. Sikh tradition finds the root of the conflict in Arjan's journey to Lahore and the machinations of Prithi Chand, who went on to approach the Mughal authorities for his share of the property; with this, it has been said, began the troubled relation between the Mughals and the Sikhs. Lahore was the birthplace of Guru Ram Das; Guru Arjan spent a lot of time in the city, and so did Guru Hargobind.

His great work was the scripture. In 1604 he compiled the Adi Granth, gathering into it the *bani*, the sacred compositions, of Guru Nanak and the writings of all the earlier Sikh Gurus and of other saints who were not Sikhs. Among them was the poetry of Baba Farid, which Guru Nanak had collected at Pakpattan. He himself has more hymns in the Guru Granth Sahib than anyone else.

The binding of the book was Lahore work. Guru Arjan sent one of his followers, Bhai Bannu, to Lahore to bind the Adi Granth, and the Kashmiri binders who used to live in the city were known for their meticulous binding. After the compilation the Guru was summoned to answer a charge that the book held anti-Muslim and anti-Hindu content; he sent Bhai Gurdas, who cleared up the misunderstanding. The Adi Granth played a significant part in the growth of a separate Sikh identity, and among the reasons given are rumours that Meherban, the son of Prithi Chand, was compiling a scripture of his own.

Other institutions are credited to him. He introduced the *daswandh*, the giving of a tenth of one's earnings to the cause of the Guru, and its collection through the *masands*, the Guru's appointed collectors, was a practice that began with him. He is credited with completing the Amrit Sar, the tank of nectar that his father had begun at Ramdaspur, after which the Harmandir Sahib was built there. In 1594 he founded Kartarpur, near Jalandhar, on land granted in the reign of Akbar, the third Mughal emperor.

A meeting between Guru Arjan and an emperor is also remembered. As the imperial camp left Lahore for the Deccan, the emperor crossed the Beas and came to the place where the Guru, the successor of Baba Nanak, had halted. The emperor was pleased with the meeting, and with hearing from the Guru's own mouth the Hindi verses that Baba Nanak had composed on the knowledge of God, and the Guru, counting it an honour, presented a fitting *peshkash*, a gift offered to a ruler. Then the Guru made a petition: while the imperial armies were in the Punjab grain had been dear and the assessment had been raised, but now grain had become cheap and the peasants could not meet it. At his request the officers of revenue were ordered to grant the peasants a reduction.

The Guru's name runs also through the Sufi tradition of the Punjab. It is said that the Sufi poet Shah Hussain travelled to Amritsar to have his poetry included in the Granth Sahib that Guru Arjan was compiling, and offered one of his *kafis*, short mystical lyrics, for inclusion. The friendship between Guru Arjan and Mian Mir is the subject of several folk tales. In Lahore the Qadiri order, the Sufi order founded by Abd al-Qadir Gilani, was represented by Mian Mir, who had come there from Sind, together with his sister Bibi Jamal, herself given to mysticism.

## The Guru's Death in Lahore

Guru Arjan died in Lahore. He was summoned to the city by the emperor Jahangir over his role during the rebellion of Prince Khusrau, the emperor's son; he appointed Hargobind as the sixth Guru, and was then martyred. Khusrau, it is told, had stopped at the Guru's *langar*, the free communal kitchen, in Amritsar and sought his blessing for victory, and the *tilak*, the mark on the forehead, that the Guru gave him was counted treason by the emperor. In his memoirs Jahangir wrote of "a Hindu named Arjan" in Goindwal on the river Beas, of how Khusrau "happened to halt at a place where he was", and of how Arjan made "a finger-mark in saffron which the Indians call Qashga". He called the Guru "this insignificant fellow", ordered his property handed to Murtaza Khan, and ordered that he be dealt with under the penal laws of the Yasa, the Mongol code of law, and put to death. Accounts of why he died differ, but his death is most often tied to Khusrau's revolt.

The revolt reached Lahore itself. Khusrau and Hasan Beg Badakhshi plundered along the road, burning *sarais*, the inns for travellers, and seizing horses, until they reached the city. Dilawar Khan, the governor of Lahore, would not meet the prince and made the fort fast, and however the prince tried, the fort did not fall to him. News of Khusrau's capture reached the emperor in the garden of Kamran Mirza at Lahore. From that garden to the gate of the palace the prince's companions were impaled in two rows, crowds came out of the city to watch, and Khusrau was led on an elephant between the stakes and imprisoned in the fort.

For five days, it is said, the Guru was tortured. In the walled city, near the Rang Mahal, there is a place with a well, and it is believed that Arjan was kept in a room next to this well, in what was the *haveli*, the mansion, of the Mughal Diwan of Lahore, and that he was immersed in a cauldron. Sikh belief holds that on the fifth day the Guru took a dip in the waters of the Ravi and did not emerge, having worked a miracle and passed into the other world, and that Mian Mir asked leave to bury his oppressors alive, but the Guru refused. Near Lahore Fort a small yellow structure is now called his *samadhi*, a memorial shrine, and it is believed that the Guru lost his life there. Accounts of his end differ.

His death has been called "the Karbala of Sikh history", and it is held to have given the Sikh community a cohesive motivation and to have precipitated the development of the Khalsa. He is counted the last of the Gurus who focused solely on spiritual matters.

## The Baoli

Accounts of how and when the well came to be made differ. In the tradition kept by the community, a devotee named Bhai Chhajju Bhagat, together with a Pathan merchant, laid a purse of gold *mohars*, gold coins, at the Guru's feet and asked that it be spent for religious purposes. The purse, it is said, held 282 *mohars* and had been in dispute, and the dispute was settled before the Guru. In that tradition, the Guru had a *baoli* sunk at this spot to provide water for the people of the quarter.

It is told, too, that the Guru set five Sikhs to guard his well, and that he himself sometimes stayed there. Immediately to the south, in a building of the bazaar that was as it were part of the house of the baoli itself, Guru Arjan's *langar* used to be held.

Accounts of when and how the well was lost also differ. It is said that the well suffered in the turbulence of the eighteenth century, when it was filled in and built over during the governorship of Nawab Yahya Khan, at the instigation of Diwan Jaspat Rai.

It was found again and restored in the reign of Maharaja Ranjit Singh. Accounts of how it came to light differ; it is most often told that the Maharaja, when he was ill, was advised by his astrologers to bathe in the baoli's water, and that the restoration followed his recovery.

In 1834 Ranjit Singh had a new building raised and a *sarovar*, a sacred tank, dug. The cost was borne by the state: the order came that one month's salary of the Khalsa, that is of the state, be deducted to raise this lofty building. The houses that had been built over the well were levelled, and shops, sitting places and *kothras*, rooms, were built. The work went on slowly and was still unfinished at the Maharaja's death. In 1927 the gurdwara passed under the management of the Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee.

## The Gurdwara

By the 1880s the house of Baoli Sahib was counted among the famous places of worship of the Sikhs of Lahore, and the largest in extent of them all. It stood among the bazaars, the Lohati bazaar one of them, with the bazaar of the iron sellers to the east and the Kasera bazaar of the sellers of vessels to the south. Its great door was on the west.

An arched gateway with two *chaukis*, raised platforms, opened on *dalans*, halls open on one side, to the north and south of the *deorhi*, the entrance hall, with a *shah-nashin*, a raised alcove, above it, lit by three small windows. Beyond lay a very wide open court with a well worked by a wheel, sets of three *kothris*, small rooms, twelve upper *kothris* on the north, two *bel* trees and a *pipal*, the sacred fig. On the south stood the building of the Baoli itself, as it were three very wide *dalans* in one, with *shah-nashins* above.

In the third *dalan* stood a *takhtposh*, a wooden platform, with a small *palang*, a bedstead, on which the Granth Sahib was kept with great honour inside costly coverings. The stair of the baoli lay behind a door that was usually locked and opened when needed, and a second great door on the bazaar was always shut.

The descent to the water was long. One long flight of steps led down to an intermediate courtyard, and a second led on to the water, where an arched door of red stone was set in the wall of the well. Above, on the west of the upper courtyard, a small masonry tank with seven steps had long lain dry, and the wheel that once filled it from the well no longer turned.

The building passed through three hands. Maharaja Ranjit Singh gave it to Bhai Nihal Singh, with an annual *jagir*, an assignment of land revenue, of eight thousand rupees in return for its service. It then passed into the charge of Bhai Jawahar Singh Kartarpuria, who took the monthly rents of the shops. In the 1880s the building's serving officer was a Sikh with a white beard, Bhai Khazan Singh, appointed to its service and care.

The endowment was commercial. Shops belonged to the building, and the enclosure itself served the bazaar as a warehouse: the people of the bazaar had rented all the *kothris* inside it for their goods, cattle were kept within, and many kept their goods there under lock and paid rent. The building of the Roghan Mandi, the oil market, among its dependencies, also paid rent.

When Jawahar Singh's debts sank the income, the state provided a substitute. The government released a monthly sum for the servants' salaries, lamp wicks and *karah prashad*, the sweet offering blessed and shared at a gurdwara, paid each month from the Mahkama Khafifa, and all expenses, flooring included, were to be met from it. A further monthly sum went to the drummers who came every day at dawn to make the offering.

The offerings had dwindled. Those made to the Granth Sahib had once never been less than two rupees a day, and by the 1880s they brought no cash at all. What a raja or a *sardar*, a chief, offered was taken by Bhai Jawahar Singh, and the servants got nothing of it; but the sugar drops, sweets and coppers offered to the Granth Sahib were the right of the *pujaris*, the priests who served there.

## Devotion and Practice

In the 1880s the house of Baoli Sahib served as a place of assembly: whenever a gathering of any kind took place in the Hindu religion, this was the building chosen. A large *naqara*, a kettledrum, in the eastern *kothri* of the third *dalan* was played at two times of the day. The Granth Sahib lay in its coverings in the same *dalan*, *karah prashad* was paid for from the state's monthly grant, and the drummers came each dawn to make the offering.

The gurdwara is tied also to the scripture itself. It was here, at Gurdwara Baoli Sahib and not elsewhere in Dabbi Bazaar, that the original manuscript of the Adi Granth, the Kartarpur *pothi*, was kept in the custody of the Sodhi family. Under Ranjit Singh's patronage it was carried in grand procession to the Lahore Fort, and after the British annexation of 1849 it was restored to the Sodhi family.

The principal commemoration of the Guru's martyrdom in Lahore is held at the other shrine of his in the city, Gurdwara Dera Sahib.

## The Setting

In Aurangzeb's time Lahore was an ancient city on the bank of the Ravi. Akbar had built its fort, city wall and palace, Jahangir had raised buildings that still stood, princes and amirs had built mansions, and the city had grown further under Shah Jahan. It was a great and mighty city: craftsmen of every land lived there, and in every street and bazaar there were very many mosques. Jahangir's palace in the city, the imperial *daulatkhana*, was built of solid masonry.

In the province of Lahore the wells were worked by a wheel of three hundred and sixty pieces of wood and more than a hundred small pots, turned by a pair of oxen. Jahangir ordered along the highway from Agra to Lahore a tall *minar*, a pillar, at every *kos*, the old measure of distance on the road, wells of baked brick at intervals, and two rows of trees.

A Persian couplet is tied to a scene at the fort of Lahore. As an emperor, setting out on his second journey to Kashmir, crossed the Ravi below the fort, he asked about whom this verse had been written:

کلاه خسروی و تاج شاہی
بہر گل کی رسد حاشا و کلا

The well is said to have been lost in the turbulence of the eighteenth century, and the poets of that century in the Punjab wrote of the ruin of their land. Bulleh Shah wrote:

*The twelfth century has come with gaping mouth. The door of doom and torment has opened, and Panjab is in a bad state. It has been struck by fear of the pit of hell.* (translated by Christopher Shackle)

The twelfth century of the Hijri era corresponds to the eighteenth century of the common era, and the poem's allusive verses appear to refer to a separation of Bulleh Shah from his master "due to the troubled conditions then prevailing in Panjab". Another of his poems says:

*Now hell has opened its bogs. / Punjab has gone to the dogs. / This hell is the nethermost hell.* (translated by Taufiq Rafat)

The full bitterness of that line, it has been said, comes through only against the backdrop of a province "being persistently rent by petty chiefs and marauders". Waris Shah, who dates his romance of Hir and Ranjha in the year 1180 of the Hijra, closes it with a lament for the land:

*Of all the wretched Punjab I am most concerned for Kasur.* (translated by Charles Frederick Usborne)

The Sikh Maharaja's Lahore is the setting of the rebuilding. When Maharaja Ranjit Singh became the sovereign of the Punjab at the end of the eighteenth century, he spent large sums on the renovation and expansion of many gurdwaras, and attached vast tracts of land to them so that the revenues from the lands could pay for their running. In 1799 he was greeted with enthusiasm by a population sick of the lawlessness of that time. The whole region of the Punjab came under his rule. He also built a mosque in Lahore, at a *mohalla*, a quarter of the city, called Gul Begum.

Of the city's Sikh buildings one historian has observed that "like the Sikh religion, which picks up from both Hinduism and Islam, Sikh architecture draws inspiration from both Hindu and Muslim architecture". In his view the Sikhs made smaller the domes that had first belonged to Muslim buildings, and Sikh shrines carry graphic depictions of the Sikh Gurus and also, in older buildings, sometimes of Hindu deities.

It is now rare to run into a Sikh in Lahore, a city once ruled by a Sikh and the seat of Sikh political strength. Across Pakistani Punjab hundreds of temples and gurdwaras have been taken over by refugees and converted into households, and in the country at large several gurdwaras and temples have been taken over and destroyed by migrants.

## Legacy

The well's later history is one of decline. Already in the 1880s its courtyard tank lay dry, though the well itself still held water at the foot of its two long flights of steps, behind its door of red stone. By 2015 the baoli had been reduced to "a mere small opening in an open space," with only a few traditional healers still drawing water from it as the water table fell, and by 2018 the demolition of the gurdwara structure itself stood as a grievance that had not been addressed.

What remains is the memory of a well sunk by the fifth Guru in the city where he would die, of a *langar* held beside it, of its loss and of a Maharaja who brought it back. As a shrine tied to Guru Arjan, the compiler of the Adi Granth and the first martyr of the Sikh faith, Baoli Sahib keeps its place on the map of Sikh Lahore, beside Gurdwara Dera Sahib by the Fort.
