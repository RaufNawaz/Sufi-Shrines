## Overview

At Jandiala Sher Khan, in the Sheikhupura district of the Punjab, stands the mausoleum of Waris Shah, who lived from about 1722 to about 1798: the Sufi poet who gave the Punjab its national epic. His *Heer*, the great verse romance of Heer and Ranjha, completed in 1766, is at once the supreme achievement of Punjabi poetry and a profound Sufi allegory of the soul's love for God, and it has been sung, wept over and quoted across the Punjab for two and a half centuries. His tomb is honoured both as the shrine of a saint and as the resting place of the poet whom Punjabis revere as the "Shakespeare of the Punjabi language", and it draws the devout and the lovers of Punjabi letters alike.

The romance he told has been elaborated in more than a hundred versions, in Punjabi, Urdu, Sindhi and Persian, and it was Waris Shah who gave it its final form. By the early 2000s, whenever the legend of Heer and Ranjha was mentioned, it was mainly his text that was meant.

## The Poet Saint

Waris Shah was born around 1722 into a Sayyid family, descendants of the Prophet Muhammad, at Jandiala Sher Khan; accounts of his dates differ. He received a thorough religious and literary education, studying at Kasur, and like other Sufi poets he was well read in Persian and Arabic literature. He belonged in spirit to the Chishti Sufi tradition, the order prominent in South Asia, and traced his discipleship to the line of the saint Makhdoom of Kasur, though accounts of his allegiance and of his teacher differ. Bulleh Shah was his older contemporary, who also took up the tale of Heer and Ranjha in Punjabi, and the two are said to have studied religion together under the same teacher.

Little of his life is recorded with certainty, and much is woven from legend. Tradition holds that the poem grew out of, and transcended, an unfulfilled love of his own. One line of the poem calls on a woman directly:

*Bhag Bhari, help me to meet my Ranjha.* (translated by Charles Frederick Usborne)

The line has been read as addressed to the woman with whom the poet fell in love, though Bhag Bhari need not have been her real name.

He lived through the anarchic eighteenth century, between the collapse of Mughal power and the rise of the Sikhs, and his poem bears witness to the disorder of his age. Its Epilogue describes the troubles of the latter half of that century, which marked the decline of the Mughal Empire in India and was a period of great confusion and disturbance. The Punjab had often been the battleground of invading forces from the North, and the invader rides through the poem's similes: "as if armies from Kandahar had swept over the Punjab", and "today the Punjab has fallen into the hands of Kandaharis". The second line is probably a reference to the invasion of Ahmad Shah Abdali, whose capital was Kandahar and who, in 1748, began a series of invasions of India through the Punjab.

In the Epilogue he turns to Kasur, the town where he had studied:

*Of all the wretched Punjab I am most concerned for Kasur. I am concerned for my faith and conscience even as Moses was frightened on Holy Sinai.* (translated by Charles Frederick Usborne)

When the poem was done, he writes, "Having finished the story I presented it to my master for his acceptance." In his own voice he claims the help of Shakarganj, Baba Farid of Pakpattan: "With the aid of Shakarganj", he writes, "I have conferred this benefit on the world". He died around 1798 and was buried at his native Jandiala Sher Khan.

## Heer

Waris Shah's fame rests on a single work of genius. Taking up the old Punjabi love legend of Heer, daughter of the Sials, and Ranjha, the herdsman who becomes a *jogi*, a wandering ascetic, for her sake, he retold it in some hundreds of stanzas of matchless Punjabi verse. He completed it in 1766, a year he gives in the poem as 1180 of the Hijri calendar; accounts of the date differ. There is no settled view of where he wrote it, though tradition holds that he composed it while living at a village called Malka Hans, in a small chamber beside a mosque. In his own words, "When love came to me I felt a desire to write this story in verse."

On its surface it is a tragic romance; beneath, it is a Sufi allegory in which the human lover's longing is the soul's longing for God, and the pain of separation the very engine of the spiritual path. Its opening lines set love at the foundation of the world:

اوّل حمد خُدا دا ورد کیجے،
عشق کیتا سُو جگ دا مُول میاں

*First, let us utter the praise of God, who made love the very origin of the world.*

The poet tells how the poem came to be written. Near its opening he records a request:

*My friends came to me and said, 'Write for us afresh the forgotten story of the Love of Hir.' So we have written it right cunningly and plucked a new rose in the Garden of Poetry.* (translated by Charles Frederick Usborne)

At the end he reports the task done: "By the Grace of God I have fulfilled the request of my dear friends." The poem's devotional frame is an invocation of Baba Farid of Pakpattan, and one line of it runs:

*When Shakarganj made his abode at Pakpattan, the Punjab was delivered of all its troubles.* (translated by Charles Frederick Usborne)

In the Invocation he also speaks of Mohiuddin Abdul Qadir Gilani, founder of the Qadiri order of Sufis, as one who can score out even the writing of the Pen of Destiny. In the Punjab countryside it was a popular belief that Pirs, Fakirs and Jogis could by the force of their piety "make the Moving Finger of Destiny cancel what it has written".

The story was old when Waris Shah took it up. The legend of Heer and Ranjha existed for a long time before him and was probably sung in the folk traditions for several centuries. It is set in the world of the Jat pastoral tribes of western Punjab, and his is the version that tells the whole story of how Ranjha leaves his family home in search of Heer. When he rewrote it in the eighteenth century the legend became his, and today it is also called Waris Shah's Heer.

Heer belonged to the family of the powerful Sial Jats of Jhang, landowners of the Punjab. The Jats of the Punjab were *zamindars*, landowners, known for practising widow marriage, and the Sial tribe was one of the few tribes in the Punjab that allowed women to inherit property under customary law. The Kheras, a powerful clan of landowners, have their place in the tale too. In his telling the Five Pirs, five saints, intervene miraculously on Ranjha's behalf.

He wrote in the *bait*, the stanza of four lines adopted in Punjabi Sufi poetry, the form Sultan Bahu also used in his *Abiat*. In the talk of his women he uses *nī*, an intimate term of the feminine gender with which women in Punjabi address one another.

Along the way the poem becomes an encyclopaedia of Punjabi life in the eighteenth century, its trades and customs, its proverbs, its landscape and its faith, so that to know *Heer* is to know the Punjab. Its world is the village of the old Punjab, where Ranjha was a well known caste of the Jats, and the poem's dawn at the mosque was how a morning began in a Punjab village. Spinning parties, *tarinjan* or *bhora*, were a famous institution of the village, and a spinning wheel, the *charkha*, used to be the inseparable companion of every woman, old or young, in the rural Punjab. Old houses in the countryside had a big courtyard with living rooms and bedrooms round it, and brothers living with their parents as a joint family was quite common. Sawan and Bhadon were the two rainy months of the year; the Gujars, a famous tribe, kept animals and sold their milk; and the *turrah*, the tuft of the turban, was beloved of the rural aristocracy.

Judging by their names, some of Heer's girl friends are Hindus, and in the village of the old Punjab the community as a whole was regarded as one social unit irrespective of caste or creed. It was by no means unusual for a Muslim family to depend on Hindu Brahmans for fixing a marriage. The arrival of Heer's *baarat*, the bridegroom's wedding procession, is told in a line of the poem that runs "Jevein log nigahein te raatan thaman dhol marde te rang lawande ne". After the *mangni*, or betrothal, a girl did not see her fiancé until marriage, and a traditional Punjab marriage kept the symbolism of marriage by capture. Brides wore coloured garments, while white clothes, by tradition, were worn by widows, particularly among the Hindus; the *arsi* was one of a bride's favourite ornaments, and *dandasa* was the equivalent of lipstick in the rural Punjab. *Gur*, raw sugar, was well known as a ceremonial offering in the countryside, and jugglers with their bears were a familiar sight.

A vow of thanksgiving in the poem reads:

*Oh poet Waris Shah! If I hear that my lover is returning, I will offer sweet cakes and light my lamps with rich butter for oil.* (translated by Charles Frederick Usborne)

Lighting lamps in a mosque, even with ordinary oil, was a well known way of expressing gratitude to God for His favours, and Punjabi mothers promised to light lamps in mosques when they prayed for their children.

## The Poet in His Poem

The poet writes himself into his story. Like most Persian and Urdu poets who wrote verse romances, he makes a personal appearance whenever it pleases him, and even enters into conversation with his characters. He breaks in as a named commentator: "Quoth the poet: 'This is not a boat but a marriage procession.'" He comments, "See what a net of deceit Hir, the Jat girl, has spread". Other asides follow: "Ask Waris the poet, he knows this well."; "Waris, if you trust in God, he will give you your portion of the good things of this world."; and, of the *qazis*, the judges of Islamic law, "Qazis have no fear of God and eat the bread of iniquity". Two more of his asides:

*(Poet Waris: 'None can withstand when eyes fight eyes in the tourney of love.')* (translated by Charles Frederick Usborne)

*Waris, I shall be called the Hir of Ranjha in Heaven, in the assembly of Fatima, the daughter of the Holy Prophet.* (translated by Charles Frederick Usborne)

His name even enters a curse: "May the curse of the Poet Waris Shah fall upon you, Hir, for you have robbed us of our dear brother-in-law." And Luddan the ferryman speaks of him: "Even the son of a Pir like Waris", he declares, "we will not take into our boat for nothing".

The poet makes large claims for his work, and repeats them:

*Poets, you should determine the worth of any poem. I have let my horse loose in the arena. Other poets have wasted their efforts in writing on petty themes. I have composed a grand poem.* (translated by Charles Frederick Usborne)

Only the poet's poetry, he writes, remains in everlasting remembrance, "for no one has written such a beautiful Hir". Of himself and the Kheras he says: "There are none so fortunate as the Kheras, no one so full of sin as the Poet Waris and there is no spell like that of his poetry." He tells himself, "Waris Shah, your name will be famous, if God be kind.", and he adds at the close that "Waris Shah has shed the light of his genius over all the faithful in the world." Beside the boast stands the penitent:

*I have no capital of good works. Of what can I be proud? I have no hope without Thy grace. I am only a poor sinner. Without the favour of the Prophet I am helpless.* (translated by Charles Frederick Usborne)

He closes asking pardon, "God, overlook the fault of poor Waris Shah", and "If I have let fall a word in ignorance may God forgive me."

It is the poet himself who declares that the poem has an inner sense:

*Oh wise man, you should note that there is a secret under the guise of my words. I have written this Hir with care and meditation… I have planted a flower to give a sweet savour.* (translated by Charles Frederick Usborne)

At its close he says, "I have written it as a parable.", and of its readers: "The story of true lovers is like the scent of a rose in a garden. He who reads it with love in his heart will be able to separate the true from the false." Of himself he writes, "Waris Shah is anxious to see God's face even as Hir longed for her lover."

He used the story as a vehicle for mystical thought and interspersed it with mystical explanations. Since the tale was known to everybody, he has his heroes recite short verses, which are commonly interpreted in a mystical sense. The spiritual reading of the tale is traced to the poet Shah Hussain, whose theme was subsequently taken up by Bulleh Shah and Waris Shah.

An allegorical key to the romance, twenty two lines long, carries the mystical interpretation given by Waris Shah. In it, "Our soul is the tragic heroine" Heer, and the key goes on:

*Our body is the lover Ranjha, / Our spiritual preceptor is the Yogi Balmath,*

Further down the key reads:

*The secret chamber is the grave, / Saida Khera is the angel of death.*

*Reason is the flute / that to us the music of Divine wisdom conveys.*

His signature line runs:

*Warith Shah says: Those alone cross over / who have silently prayed to the Lord, / and ever devotedly recited His glorious Name.*

One of the characteristics of Sindhi and Punjabi poetry is that the longing soul is always depicted as a woman, and the tragic heroines of the folk tales of both provinces, Heer, Sassui, Sohni and many others, represent the human soul in search of the beloved.

## The Shrine and the Song

Waris Shah's mausoleum at Jandiala Sher Khan is a place of pilgrimage for the devout and of homage for the lovers of Punjabi letters, and it has been developed over the years into a dignified complex befitting the poet's stature. Devotees recite the *Fatiha*, the opening chapter of the Quran recited as a prayer, and seek blessing; singers come to perform *Heer* and *qawwali*, the devotional music of the Sufis; and the *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of the saint's death, gathers crowds who honour the poet saint with music and remembrance. The shrine stands as a monument not only to a holy man but to the Punjabi language itself.

The poem was made to be heard. Waris Shah composed it as an epic poem to be sung to music in public, and he is said to have recited it with great emotion among the people who shared his speech in the Punjab. He names three kinds of people who will carry it on, those who copy it, those who recite it and those who read it: "May he who reads or copies my poor efforts derive pleasure", he writes, and:

*May God pardon him who copies these words and give his bounty to those who recite it. May its readers enjoy the book.* (translated by Charles Frederick Usborne)

The tale was sung in the Punjab before him. In Aurangzeb's reign the people of the Punjab composed wonderful verses and designs on the love of Heer and Ranjha and sang them.

A historian of Lahore called the tale the story that was on the tongue of good and bad, small and great, rich and poor in the land of the Punjab. By the 1970s a visitor who stayed in a Punjabi village would certainly find somebody to sing the old romance of Heer and Ranjha in the classical form given it by Waris Shah, and a Punjabi would be deeply touched when the story was sung. By the early 1980s his verse was sung, time and again, wherever Punjabis met. For centuries the tale has been sung and dramatised.

Generations of Punjabis have known long passages of *Heer* by heart, and its verses are sung by professional singers of *Heer* in a distinctive traditional style. The poem is invoked in joy and in grief alike, most famously by the modern poet Amrita Pritam, who at the Partition cried out to Waris Shah himself to rise and witness the suffering of the Punjab.

By the early 2000s there was substantial recorded material of his *Heer*, on audiocassettes and videos, sung in the folk traditions, and many renowned folk artists of the Punjab had sung it, among them Reshma, Zahida Parvin, Nazir Begum, Sabiha Khanum, Tufail Niazi and Iqbal Bahu. Heer's lament as she is carried off in her palanquin is traditionally sung at almost every Punjabi wedding at the time of the bride's departure to her husband's home.

The song is not welcome everywhere. In many villages, it has been said, the recitation of Waris Shah's *Heer* is not allowed, for people believe that if the sounds of the verses fall on the ears of young girls, they too will elope like Heer. At another shrine, that of Sahari Mal, a young man put on Waris Shah's *Heer* on his mobile phone while a group of men was playing cards in a corner.

## The Setting

Rani Jindan was first sent to the fort of Sheikhupura, and then to the fort of Chunar Garh. The Ghazi Pir is buried at the foot of the hillock near Shah Kot in the Sheikhupura district, and the people of five districts, Sheikhupura among them, congregated annually at his shrine. Before the new road, Sikh pilgrims had to travel to Sheikhupura and then head towards Chuhrkhana.

## Legacy

Waris Shah occupies a place in Punjabi culture that few poets hold in any language: his *Heer* is the canonical classic of the tongue, memorised, sung and quoted by millions, and revered as scripture is revered. Through it he wove together the Sufi vision of divine love, the folk romance of the Punjab and the living speech of its people, and he made a single poem into a mirror of a whole civilisation.

The closing section of one of Bulleh Shah's lyrics is hardly intelligible without close familiarity with the story as *Heer* tells it. Punjabi poets and their eager audiences poured into Heer and Ranjha the same erotic emotion that was invested in Radha and Krishna in regions closer to the Ganges, and the story of Heer and Ranjha became a favourite Sufi device for conveying the value of erotic passion in spiritual life.

He is counted with Baba Farid and Bulleh Shah among those who spread the message of peace and brought several religious communities together instead of shedding blood. Yet the poetry of Baba Farid, Guru Nanak, Shah Hussain, Waris Shah and Bulleh Shah is also what the educated elite of the Punjab ends up disowning, and even ridiculing. His tomb at Jandiala Sher Khan keeps the memory of the saint who was also the supreme poet of the Punjab.
