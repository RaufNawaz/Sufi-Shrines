## Overview

On a plot known as DB-80 in the Sirey Ghat quarter of Hyderabad, Sindh, stands the Guru Gurpat Mandir, also called the Darbar of Baba Gurpat Sahib. It is a *darbar*, or shrine, of the Nanakpanthis, who follow the Nanakpanth, the path of Guru Nanak, and it is registered with Pakistan's Evacuee Trust Property Board as a functional *mandir*, or temple. It is built around a Sant, a holy man, revered as a descendant of Guru Nanak in the twelfth generation, in the Jagiasi lineage. Unlike many of the small shrines of Sants in upper and central Sindh, of which little is known, the darbar keeps an unusually detailed memory of its founder's life and of its own founding, preserved by the trust that cares for it today.

## The Sant

The darbar's tradition holds that Gurpat Sahib was born in the Punjab into the Jagiasi lineage of Nanakpanthi Sants, the spiritual descendants of Guru Nanak through the Udasi line of Baba Sri Chand, and that he travelled to Sindh in the early nineteenth century. He is said to have settled first at Khairpur, in the reign of the Talpur ruler Mir Murad Ali Khan, carrying letters of introduction and support from the Sikh court of Maharaja Ranjit Singh, and to have moved on to Hyderabad around 1830.

He is said to have brought relics with him and enshrined them, including hair said to be from Baba Sri Chand, a rupee of the Nanak Shahi era, and a ring attributed to Guru Gobind Singh, the tenth Guru of the Sikhs. These objects are still spoken of at the darbar today. In the course of his ministry he is credited with founding several Jagiasi *tikanas*, or seats, beyond Hyderabad, among them at Khairpur, Halani and Kandyaro. He died in 1877, at the age of about seventy.

## The Darbar

By the community's reckoning, the present building at DB-80, Sirey Ghat dates from the year 1988 of the Vikram Samvat, a Hindu calendar era, which is 1931 in the Gregorian calendar. It stands on a plot of some 595 square yards. Today it is kept as a registered charitable trust, and its trusteeship in former times is remembered under Bao Santokdas.

Besides its hall of worship, the darbar today runs a modest vegetarian eatery and has space where pilgrims can stay, and the names of its donors are inscribed on tiles. In this it carries on a hospitality akin to the *langar*, the free communal kitchen, that has long been associated with the shrines of Nanakpanthi and Sikh Sants across Sindh.

The Guru Gurpat Mandir belongs to a wider constellation of small Jagiasi and Nanakpanthi shrines scattered across the belt of Sindh between Dadu and Hyderabad. The same milieu produced the darbars of Sant Baba Bhagat Ram, Bhai Sant Thawan Das and Gurdas Ram, all of them likewise registered with the Evacuee Trust Property Board. With a named founder, a dated building and a preserved lineage, it is one of the better documented sites in this cluster, though little of its history can be traced beyond the memory of its own community.

## The Path of Nanak in Sindh

In nineteenth century Sindh the Nanakpanth was probably the dominant religious path among the Hindu Sindhis. The Lohanas and related mercantile groups followed two main cults, the Nanakpanth and the Daryapanth, which is based on the deification of the Indus River, and in Sindh many Nanakpanthis belonged to a subgroup known as the Udasis. Guru Nanak himself, tradition holds, visited Uch Sharif on his way to Sindh, towards the end of his travels.

There is no settled view of whether the followers of Nanak in Sindh are Hindus or Sikhs. Many Lohanas in Sindh follow the teachings of Nanak, which somewhat obscured their religious affiliation for the colonial officers: in the census of 1881 the Lohanas of Shikarpur and Hyderabad were counted as Sikhs, and ten years later, in that of 1891, as Hindus. As an outcome of the Singh Sabha, a movement of Sikh reform, the Sindhi Nanakpanthis were not included in the Sikh community, and they identified themselves as a Hindu religious sect. The communities who refused the Khalsa, the order of initiated Sikhs, and called themselves followers of Guru Nanak still exist in Sindh and in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa. Baptised Sikhs look down upon them, and at Manora they were seen merging into the larger groups of Sikhs.

The Nanak Panthis, as they are also called, belong to the south of the Punjab and to Sindh, where their temples hold portraits of Guru Nanak alongside Hindu deities, and they are found in very large numbers in Sindh. Today many middle and upper class Hindus in Sindh are followers of the Nanak *panth*, and it is common to cover one's head before walking into a *tikana*, the temple that holds the Guru Granth Sahib, the holy book.

With the spread of the Nanakpanth in Sindh, the Hindu Sufis, Hindus who followed the Sufi path, were very familiar with Sikh terminology, and in Sindh neither the Sufis nor the Nanakpanthis had a central organisation ruling an encompassing body of followers. The Sufi poets Shah Abdul Latif and Sachal have been described as late participants in the Bhakti movement, the movement of loving devotion to God, which had a major impact in Sindh through the spread of Guru Nanak's teaching.

## The Darbars of Sindh

The word by which this site is also known has a history of its own in the province. In Sindh the Persian word mostly used was *darbar*, a term used by Sikhs, Muslims and Hindus alike, and the *darbar* was the sacred territory in which the Sufi culture was at work. British colonisation, it has been argued, brought a renewal of religious life, tied to the spread of a composite middle class and marked by the emergence of the *darbars*, which spread a *darbar* culture.

The Nanakpanthi *darbars* belong to that story. Some had been founded in the late eighteenth century, but the second half of the nineteenth saw a renaissance of the Nanakpanthi *darbars*, and the religious recomposition that Sindh went through in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries showed itself in the building of many new Nanakpanthi *darbars* all over the province.

After Partition the *darbars* of Sindh were for the most part preserved, with different arrangements for their management, often with Indian *gaddi nashins*, the holders of the seat, or followers paying the keepers; and despite the changing diplomatic relations between Pakistan and India, it was always possible to travel across the border. The *darbars* that the migrants founded in India are not centred on tombs, unlike those of Sindh. In the early twenty first century government funding was seen in a wave of restorations of temples across Sindh.

## Hyderabad

Hyderabad was the Talpur capital, the capital of independent Sindh before the British conquest. One Hindu holy man of the city, Dalpat Sufi, founded an *astano*, a place of residence for a renunciant, on the Sipahimalani Lane. He is said to have attracted many followers, Muslims and Hindus, and the Talpur mirs, the rulers of Sindh, are said to have looked after it. After his death it became a centre of pilgrimage under his successor, Keshavram Sipahimalani, and was known as Guru Sehwani's place. Dalpat Sufi seems to have stayed in a place in Hyderabad that can be called a *darbar*. The Sipahimalanis were a well known family of Amils, the Hindu scribal class, of Hyderabad.

Several Hindu Sufis came from the city. Hiranand Advani was born in Hyderabad in a family of Khudabadi Amils, and Molchand Kripalani was born there in 1887 in a Krishnaite family, devoted to Krishna. Mulchand studied up to class 6 at the Hiranand Academy in Hyderabad, and in 1901, aged 22, Rochaldas married and settled in the city to enrol as a student at its medical college. The Amils of Hyderabad played a part in the spread of the Sufi culture, although the devotional centres of Sindh may have shifted north after the British conquest. Annie Besant, known in Sindh as Basanti Devi, delivered a number of lectures in Hyderabad, where her speech was about the Hindu religion, and a printing house founded in Hyderabad was named Blavatsky Printing.

The city has its Sufi shrines. Tando Jahaniyya in Hyderabad was one of three Sufi traditions among the Hindu Sufis. Qutub Ali Shah was a famous Sufi from Hyderabad, and his *dargah*, his shrine, is in Jahaniyya, a neighbourhood of the city; its *sajjada nashin*, the hereditary custodian, came from Pakistan twice, in 1957 and in 1980, to perform the *dastarbandi*, the ceremony of tying the turban, for two Hindu Sufis. One community of musicians takes as its *pir*, its spiritual guide, Qutb Din Darya, whose shrine is called Tando Jahana and stands in the Civil Lines at Hyderabad. Shah Abdul Latif was born in Hala, near present day Hyderabad, and Jhok is not far from the city.

Hyderabad is a centre of Sindhi devotional music on the air. The singer Taj Mastani left Sehwan for the city because it is a large urban centre, and there she could earn a living from the radio. In places such as Hyderabad and Faisalabad local recording companies have helped women musicians earn a living by recording their Sufi lyrics with music, and at Radio Pakistan Hyderabad its director, Sikander Baloch, and its music director, Naseer Mirza, said that they no longer had to go to the *melas*, the fairs, at the Sufi shrines to hunt for talent. The city has two *gharanas*, schools of classical music, the Indore *gharana* and the Gawalior *gharana*, and the singer Zeena Bai is buried in the Hyderabad graveyard where all the great musicians of the city are buried. The Institute of Sindhology in Hyderabad is a source for the documentation of Sindhi Sufi music.

The city is also a place of Sindhi publishing. The Shah Waliullah Academy in Hyderabad, under the presidency of Maulana Ghulam Mustafa Qasimi, has published a number of Waliullah's works and studies of him. The Sindhi Adabi Board of Hyderabad published five volumes between 1960 and 1962, and Lilaram Watanmal's biography of Shah Abdul Latif was published in Hyderabad in 1889. From the 1960s the Sindhi newspaper *Daily Ibrat*, published in Hyderabad, reported the *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of a saint's death, at Sehwan in great detail.

Pilgrims from Hyderabad travel to the temple of the goddess Hinglaj at *mīlād-al-nabī*, the Prophet's birthday, and *ʿāśūrah*, the tenth of Muharram, and at *ʿīd*, the Muslim festival, and in *muḥarram*. On 28 March 2014 a temple in Hyderabad was attacked. A railway line once connected Hyderabad to Barmer and Jodhpur.

## Sindh

In 1843, with the Talpurs in power, the Baluchis stood at the top of the society of Sindh. Muslims were divided between Sunnis and Shiites; the Sunnis were in the majority, but the sovereigns, the Talpurs, were themselves Shiites. Among the Hindus, the Lohanas made up half of the total Hindu population and were divided into Amils and Bhaibands, and the *panchayat*, the community council, prevailed among the Hindus of Sindh. Under Talpur rule, from 1783 to 1843, the Amils almost monopolised the office of *kardar*, the local revenue officer. The *hurr*, the disciples of the Pir Pagaro, gathered first to support Ahmad Brelwi's struggle against the Sikhs, from 1829 to 1831, and later actively joined in the struggle against the British. After 1857 many Kayasht, a caste of Hindu scribes, migrated to work in the princely states, the Khairpur emirate in Sindh among them.

The Sindhi language continued to flourish after the British occupation in 1843. Sindh had always been proud of its mystical heritage: it is told that when Nadir Shah invaded in 1739, the minister Diwan Gidumal gave him the most precious treasure Sindh had to offer, some of the dust of its saints. British policy, especially in the Punjab and Sindh, was to treat the hereditary saints in essentially the same way as landlords and tribal leaders were treated. The integration of Sufism into the religious life of the Hindu Sindhis has been dated to the nineteenth century, its main actors being the Amils, the literary class. In the religious life of Sindh, the devotional relationship of a follower with a spiritual master prevailed over affiliation to an institutional religion.

By early November 1947, 246,000 Hindus had already left Sindh by sea, air or rail, and by 1 January 1948 nearly 500,000 people were estimated to have sought refuge in India; estimates of how many left in all differ, from around a million to 800,000 or fewer. The departure of the Hindu elite and the influx of Muslim refugees from India, the Muhajirs, dramatically altered the local demography. K. R. Malkani, a Sindhi in exile, was a Hindu who left Hyderabad after Partition and settled in New Delhi. After Partition, too, Molchand worried that it was difficult for the Hindu Sindhi Sufis to visit the Sufi places of Sindh, now in Pakistan. The common notion that the religions of Sindh lived in harmony has been called a nationalist utopian ideal: communal violence in Sindh never reached the level of the Punjab's, but it was not absent, though the Sufi culture, it has been argued, still pervades Sindhi society.

Although a large majority of its Hindus have migrated to India since 1947, Sindh is the province of Pakistan with the most significant Hindu population, home to the majority of Pakistan's Hindus. The majority of the Hindu community lives in Sindh and the south of the Punjab, with a proportionate representation of the lower and the upper castes. Pakistan's Hindus are put at between 6 and 10 million, an estimate that varies widely; in Sindh they number several millions, most of them belonging to the scheduled castes, and by the census of 1998 the Hindus were the country's largest minority. It is often claimed that there was no caste system in Sindh. Sindh may have had a social fabric of its own, which colonial rule renewed, but the claim is contested: it is bound up with Sindhi nationalist discourse, which Dalit organisations, speaking for the castes once held untouchable, oppose. In the early twenty first century the formula *Sab ka malik ek hai*, all have one master, was very popular among the Hindus of Sindh.

Under the emperor Aurangzeb, the Mughal province of Thatta ran in length from Bhakkar to Kach Makran. Its greatest river was the Indus, by which merchants from Multan and Bhakkar shipped their goods to Thatta, and most of its traffic went by boat.

Alan Faqir recited a line of song that he read as a statement about Sindh:

*Punnu is not in the Kec / Sind is not the amir*

Its second half means that Sindh is not in the position of leadership, or amirship. Of his land he said that it has been looted, plundered and assaulted, and yet it goes on: it has not died, it will not die, it has existed for centuries and it shall exist; Sindhri, the name the poetic traditions give to Sindh, is beaten, and yet Sindhri goes on.
