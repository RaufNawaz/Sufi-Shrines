## Overview

At Nurpur Shahan, in the Margalla Hills on the edge of Islamabad, stands the shrine of Hazrat Bari Imam, Sayyid Abdul Latif Kazmi (1617 to 1705), the patron saint of the region of Islamabad and Rawalpindi and one of the most venerated holy figures of the Pothohar. His darbar, the shrine where he lies, is the site of one of the largest annual festivals in the country, his *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of the saint's death. For centuries pilgrims have climbed to Nurpur Shahan to seek the blessing of the saint whom the people of the capital regard as their guardian.

He was an ascetic of the seventeenth century who settled in the hills above the plain where Islamabad would one day rise, and there he gathered a wide following. Today his shrine draws pilgrims from the capital region and beyond, and his memory lives on in the houses of other saints too.

## The Saint

Sayyid Abdul Latif Shah, called Bari Imam and Bari Sarkar, was born in 1617 into a Kazmi Sayyid family, the Sayyids being descendants of the Prophet Muhammad; tradition traces his descent to the Prophet through the Imams. From his youth he was drawn to devotion, and after study and long spiritual wandering he took the Sufi path in the Qadiri tradition, the order founded by Abd al-Qadir Gilani. He settled at last at the village now called Nurpur Shahan. There he lived as an ascetic and a guide, tending the poor, and gathered a wide following; he is remembered for a life of prayer, generosity and miracle. He never married. He is said to have been a contemporary of the Mughal emperor Aurangzeb, who is remembered as honouring him.

By 1875 he was counted among the Qadiri saints under the name Shah Latif Bari Qadiri, and his holy tomb was famous. That Shah Latif is identified with the saint of Nurpur Shahan through a later life of him, *Hayat-e Bari Imam*, and through a meeting with the saint in the Pothohar.

He was held in high praise: famous among the elders of the Punjab as a great elder, a great worshipper, an ascetic and a recluse, *mast* and *majzub*, one lost in ecstasy and absorbed in divine love. Thousands of *khawariq* and *karamat*, wonders and miraculous deeds, were famous of him, and thousands of his *murids*, his pledged disciples, reached the stages of spiritual completion.

There is no settled view of his masters. The master from whom he is said to have received his inner blessing is Hayat al-Mir Zinda Pir, held to be one of the grandsons of Ghaus al-Azam and to be ever living; he is also said to have received *faiz*, spiritual grace, from Shaikh Nasir al-Din Qurashi Multani.

A father and his son are remembered as having met him in the Pothohar. Sayyid Hasan Peshawari related that when he reached the country of Pothohar he saw Sayyid Shah Latif Majzub, who bore the marks of great insight and met him with complete courtesy; a day or two passed, and he took his leave. Hasan Peshawari's son, Shah Muhammad Ghaus Lahori, met the saint in 1116 AH, a year of the Islamic calendar, and wrote of the meeting himself.

Accounts of the year of his death differ; established tradition gives 1705. He was buried at Nurpur Shahan.

## The Shrine and the ʿUrs

The shrine of Bari Imam at Nurpur Shahan is a large complex and a ceaseless resort of pilgrims from the capital region and beyond. It keeps the daily prayers and the *langar*, the free communal kitchen, and it is bound up with the identity of Islamabad as the capital's patron darbar. Like other great shrines it suffered a grievous suicide bombing, in 2005, during the *ʿurs*; yet the devotion endured, and the shrine remains thronged.

The *ʿurs*, held in the spring, is among the greatest religious festivals of Pakistan: a gathering of hundreds of thousands over five days, with *qawwali*, the devotional music of the shrines, *dhamal*, ecstatic devotional dancing to the beat of the drum, the *langar* and a vast fair. It draws devotees, dervishes and *malangs*, wandering ascetics devoted to the saints, from across the country.

Before the festival comes a traditional procession of devotees from Peshawar to Nurpur, lasting a week. A legend is told of the saint Chan Agha Badshah: he is said to have once insulted Bari Imam, not recognising his saintliness, and on realising his mistake to have made a vow to journey every year on foot to the saint's *dargah*, his shrine. The procession is organised by the descendants of the repentant Chan Agha and the later pir Agha Sayyid Mir Jani Shah, who died in 1883.

## Devotion beyond the Shrine

The saint's memory lives on beyond Nurpur Shahan, in the darbar of a disciple. The *majzub* Mama Ji Sarkar, who belongs like him to the Qadiri order, took Bari Imam as his *murshid*, his spiritual guide, and like him remained unmarried. At around fourteen to sixteen years of age Mama Ji joined the annual procession, and afterwards he remained near Bari Imam's shrine for about ten or twelve years, living for longer periods in the wilderness surrounding the village.

Mama Ji's own darbar keeps his master present. In a corner stands a model of Bari Imam's shrine, which is taken to Nurpur at the time of the *ʿurs*. In the lamp house of Mama Ji's *langar*, five oil lamps burn in honour of Bari Imam, and devotees take their oil as a remedy against any physical pain. Bari Imam's shrine is pictured twice in the darbar: a fresco by Sayyid Ishrat shows in its background a shrine resembling his, and it appears among the motifs of mirror mosaic dating from 1990. During Bari Imam's *ʿurs* in 1994, in the first week of May, Mama Ji's shrine and his former *baithak*, the room where he received visitors, were richly decorated and drew crowds of visitors, and the occasion was used to make known the name and sacred power of Mama Ji Sarkar.

## The Setting

Nurpur Shahan lies on the outskirts of Pakistan's capital city, Islamabad, whose twin city is Rawalpindi. The capital is also a centre of patronage for Sufi music. The singer Abida Parvin lives in Islamabad rather than in her home town of Hyderabad, because Islamabad is the capital and she can negotiate business from there, and Lok Virsa, the Institute of Folk Heritage in Islamabad, contacted her through Radio Pakistan in Hyderabad. At a concert at the Open University in Islamabad, a woman performer once sang in a fiery mood and, through the frames of Sufi poetry, blasted the orthodox government of the time in the presence of its officials.

Islamabad has its place in the life of the Sufi orders and in religious publishing. Since 1989 The Association of Spiritual Training, founded by Wahid Bakhsh and administered by a number of his senior disciples, has published an English language journal, *The Sufi Path*, based in Islamabad, and a woman disciple once hosted a weekly *halqa*, a circle of disciples gathered for devotion, in the city, a practice since discontinued. The city has a Persian research centre, the Markaz-i Tahqiqat-i Farsi-i Iran u Pakistan, with its own publications and manuscripts, and in 1989 Lok Wirsa Isha'at Ghar issued a study of Shah Hussayn from Islamabad.

Other currents run through the city as well. Al-Huda, one of the most prominent Pakistani religious revivalist organisations, is based in Islamabad and aims to educate women about the Quran and the Sunnah. A bodhi tree, the tree sacred to Buddhists, is said to grow in Islamabad, and another near Jhelum. Lahore and Islamabad are two of the important cities for the representation of the Baha'is.

## Legacy

Bari Imam is the guardian saint of Pakistan's capital region, a Sayyid ascetic of the seventeenth century whose shrine at Nurpur Shahan has become, in the shadow of modern Islamabad, one of the country's greatest centres of popular devotion. His *ʿurs* is a defining festival of the Pothohar, and his memory endures as that of a saint of generosity and blessing watching over the capital from the Margalla Hills.

His memory lives in other saints' houses too, as in Mama Ji Sarkar's darbar, which carries his shrine's image on its walls, keeps lamps for him and sends a model of his shrine to Nurpur at the *ʿurs*. The written tradition about him continues as well. It includes the Persian treatise of Shah Muhammad Ghaus Lahori, *Risala dar Kasb-e Suluk wa Bayan-e Marifat*; Mir Ghulam Kashmiri's *Khawariq al-Adat*; Muqim Muhkam al-Din Hajrawi's *Durr al-Ajaib*; Gul Hasan Shah's *Tazkira-e Ghausia*; a life of Bari Imam published in Rawalpindi, Muhammad Qasim Rajori's *Hayat-e Bari Imam*; Manzur al-Haq Siddiqi's *Shah Latif Bari*, published in Lahore in 1970; and an article, "Bari Shah Latif", in the *Danishnama-e Shibh-e Qarra*, an encyclopaedia published in Tehran.
