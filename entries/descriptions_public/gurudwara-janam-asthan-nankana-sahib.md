## Overview

Gurudwara Janam Asthan, the *janam asthan* or "birthplace" shrine, stands in the town of Nankana Sahib, in Pakistan's Punjab province some eighty kilometres south west of Lahore, at the spot where Guru Nanak, the founder of the Sikh faith and the first of its ten Gurus, is traditionally held to have been born. For Sikhs the world over it is among the holiest of all *gurdwaras*, the Sikh houses of worship, a place of origin comparable in devotional weight to Bethlehem for Christians or the precinct of the Kaaba at Mecca for Muslims. The town itself takes its name from the Guru, and the great shrine at its heart, crowned with white domes and set beside a sacred pool, is at once a monument to a birth, a working house of worship, and one of the principal destinations of Sikh pilgrimage in the land where the faith began. It is also the site of one of the defining modern martyrdoms of the Sikhs, the massacre of 1921.

It is the main gurdwara of Nankana Sahib and the most famous place in it, standing where the one main road of the city begins or ends. Of the town's gurdwaras associated with Guru Nanak it is the grandest, a splendid structure spread over a vast area and built over the place that was once the house of his father, Mehta Kalu. The city is counted among the holiest in the world for the Sikhs, because the founder of their religion was born here.

## Guru Nanak at Talwandi

Guru Nanak was born, by tradition, in 1469, at Rai Bhoi di Talwandi, the settlement that in time came to be called Nankana Sahib in his honour. Accounts of the exact day differ; Sikhs celebrate his birth, the *Prakash Utsav* or *Gurpurab*, on the full moon day of the month of Kartik, in October or November. Accounts of his birthplace have not always agreed either, but it is generally held to be Talwandi. Most of his life fell in the reign of the Lodi sultans.

His father, Mehta Kalu, also called Kalyan Das, was a village accountant, and his mother was Mata Tripta. His parents came from the Khattri community, which was closely connected with the Afghan rulers in commerce and administration, and Nanak belonged to the Bedi caste. The settlement lay within the estates of a local Muslim landholding family. Shiv Ram, head of a Bedi family, was accountant to Rai Bhoe, a Bhatti Rajput who had become a Muslim, and he was succeeded by his son, Mehta Kalu, who kept the accounts of the Bhatti family under Rai Bhoe's son, Rai Bular. As was customary in his profession, Mehta Kalu supplemented his income through agriculture.

Sikh tradition warmly remembers the chieftain Rai Bular Bhatti as among the first, with the Guru's elder sister, Bebe Nanaki, to perceive in the child an extraordinary spiritual destiny. It is told, too, that the village astrologer, Pandit Hardyal, foretold the child's greatness.

When the boy was seven his father took him to receive instruction in Hinduism, and two years later attempts were made to teach him Persian. He seems to have learnt both Sanskrit and Persian. In those days Sa'di's *Karima* and *Gulistan*, as well as Sufi verse in general, were taught to Hindu and Muslim youths alike. In time, under pressure from his family, he became a trader and a farmer, yet he spent most of his time with the yogis who lived in the surrounding jungles.

The Janamsakhis, the traditional stories of his life, tell that at school the boy showed a brilliance never before seen in any child in Talwandi. They tell that at thirteen he refused the sacred thread, the *janeu*, in his father's courtyard, saying that it is our actions, and our actions alone, that give a man his position. At sixteen, in the same stories, he herded cattle, and he became well acquainted with the dervish Sayyad Hussain and gained an intimate knowledge of Sufi thought. In these stories Talwandi lay in the path of numerous mendicants, holy men and *sadhus*, the Hindu ascetics, who camped in a grove of trees on the outskirts of the village.

It is told that when Nanak turned eighteen his father asked him to take up a trade and gave him twenty rupees, and that Nanak left the town with Bhai Mardana, who would later travel with him. The tale is remembered as the *Sacha Sauda*, the "true bargain".

Behind the gurdwara, away from the hub of the town, rises an archaeological mound. A settlement once stood there; it was destroyed, after which Rai Bhoi di Talwandi was built within a boundary wall, so that Nanak was born in the new town. As a young man he used to spend a great deal of time on the mound in conversation with the yogis, *sadhus* and dervishes who passed through Talwandi, and he is believed to have frequented it in the six or seven years between the *Sacha Sauda* and his move to Sultanpur. It is a steep climb with no proper path, at least twenty metres from the ground and covered with shrubs of *karir*, a thorny desert bush, with shards of broken pottery and other mud utensils underfoot.

## The Journeys and the Succession

From Talwandi Nanak went to Sultanpur, some sixty kilometres away, as keeper of the stores of Nawab Daulat Khan. He married Sulakhini, daughter of Mul Chand and Chando Rani of the town of Batala, though accounts of the marriage differ, and they had two sons, Sri Chand, born on 8 September 1494, and Lakhmi Das, born on 12 February 1497. At Sultanpur, the Janamsakhis tell, he vanished in the stream for a week and came out saying, "There is only one God. There is no Hindu, no Mussalman."

He carried that message across the divisions of caste and creed on the long journeys known as his *udasis*, teaching the worship of one formless God through honest living, remembrance of the divine Name, and service to others. Accounts of how many journeys he made differ, but they lasted more than twenty years and took him, mostly on foot, east to Bengal, south to Sri Lanka, north to Kashmir, Nepal and Tibet, and west to Mecca. Wherever he went he visited the religious shrines of the place, Muslim, Hindu or of any other denomination.

His journeys brought him back to Talwandi. The Janamsakhis tell that when Nanak and Mardana came back there on the first journey, Mata Tripta felt great joy, Mehta Kalu was a little in awe of his son, and Nanak spent time every day with Rai Bhoe, who looked upon him as his Guru. In the same stories, on the second journey, after Pak Pattan, he travelled back to Talwandi again; it was no longer an obscure little village but had become a place of pilgrimage, and he sat by Rai Bular, who was sick and dying. Later Kartarpur, a hundred and eighty kilometres from Talwandi by way of Lahore, became a pilgrim town like Talwandi and Sultanpur.

People came from every land to become his disciples. He died on 22 September 1539 at Kartarpur, a small village also in present day Pakistan; accounts of when he died differ. In Aurangzeb's reign his descendants still lived at the place of Baba Nanak, twelve *kos*, an old measure of distance, from Batala on the bank of the Ravi.

On his deathbed he appointed not his son but his most loyal student as his spiritual successor. His son Lakhmi Das was worthy, but spiritual fortune was not his lot, and at the hour of his death Nanak gave a Khattri named Lehna the title of Guru Angad and made him his successor. It came to be believed that the mingling of the light of the Guru with that of Guru Angad was in accordance with God's *Riza*, the divine will, and posterity recognised Nanak as the personification of the light of God. His eldest son, Sri Chand, was not recognised as Guru by the mainstream Sikh community; the Udasi sect, which follows his tradition, is believed to have been founded by him.

In Aurangzeb's time his followers counted the service of travellers, of those who come and go, as the highest worship: whoever arrived at midnight speaking Baba Nanak's name was treated as a brother and a friend and served, even a stranger, a thief or a highwayman. The tradition of *langar*, the free communal kitchen, is believed to have begun with Guru Nanak. It is said that when his Hindu and Muslim companions asked him which religion they should adopt, he told them to be true Muslims or honest Hindus: "That would be Nanak's religion."

## The Gurdwara

A shrine is believed to have marked the birthplace from an early period, associated by tradition with the Guru's descendants. The present complex owes its grandeur chiefly to the patronage of Maharaja Ranjit Singh, ruler of the Sikh Empire, who is held to have had the *gurdwara* rebuilt and richly endowed around 1819 to 1820. Ranjit Singh, who became sovereign of the Punjab at the end of the eighteenth century, spent large amounts of money on the renovation and expansion of many gurdwaras across the province, and attached vast tracts of land to them so that the revenues from the lands could support their work.

Built in the characteristic idiom of Sikh sacred architecture, the shrine presents gleaming white façades, cusped arches, fluted domes and gilded finials. Within lies the inner sanctum enshrining the birthplace itself, where the *Guru Granth Sahib*, the scripture that Sikhs revere as their eternal living Guru, is installed and ceremonially read. A large *sarovar*, or sacred tank, adjoins the prayer halls, and pilgrims gather for *langar*, a hallmark of every gurdwara. The complex is today listed as a protected heritage monument by the Government of Punjab, Pakistan.

The heart of the shrine is small: a room in the middle of a spacious courtyard floored with marble. At its centre is the Thara Sahib, the raised platform where the Guru Granth Sahib is recited, and according to tradition it was in this room that Guru Nanak was born. Beside the shrine is a well behind an iron railing, and the board next to it reads: "This well was part of Guru Nanak's house." The gurdwara stands two steps above the ground, and its gilded door bears depictions of Guru Nanak and scenes from a *langar*. Rooms in the courtyard are allotted to Sikh pilgrims at the major festivals. A room behind the shrine belongs to the Granth: it has a bed, an air conditioner turned on in the summer for the Granth and a blanket for the winter, and no other activity can take place in it.

The approach to the gurdwara was through the small market of Nankana, where most of the shops were run by Muslims and a few by Sikhs. In 2011 the railway station stood opposite the gurdwara and Gurdwara Tambu Sahib a few hundred metres away, and near the Janam Asthan, next to the office of the *Nazim*, the former elected head of the district, stood the Government College, recently renamed Guru Nanak Government College. Right in front of the gurdwara a tall minaret with a spiral stair, the tallest in the city, rose from an unpainted grey mosque. "It is a show of strength," said Kartar, one of the Sikhs of the town. "They want to tell us who the real inhabitants of Nankana Sahib are."

On the night of Lohri in January 2011 the main gate was closed and only a small door was open, where two police officials sitting inside let visitors who were not Sikhs pass on Kartar's word. Inside, on the right, stood buildings where visitors stay when they come for the larger events, and a small garden with two walkways and street lights. Shoes were handed to a volunteer child in a cage of bars and matched by numbered coins, and there was a water niche for washing the feet, with two sinks. In the passage hung two framed pictures, Baba Farid Ganjshakar in a green shawl and Baba Guru Nanak in saffron, and a railing divided the way in from the way out, useful when the crowds are huge. A *Nishan Sahib*, the Sikh flag, stood with candles.

At Guru Nanak's birthday that year the complex held several blocks of hostels, built in various eras, and, to the left of the main shrine, a separate building of communal washrooms, with stalls for laundry, public telephones, juices and food. Its pool was the only one among the gurdwaras of the city filled with water; it grew deeper towards the centre, and police stood at its entrance. A web of lights hung over the façade, and a banner at the main entrance showed Guru Nanak on one side, Guru Gobind Singh on the other and the other Gurus between them. The gurdwara's administration had its offices in the complex. The Langar Hall, newly built, was the last building before the boundary wall, beyond which lay the Guru Nanak Government College; the wall carried barbed wire, and a large brown gate, a recent addition, was open only to officials. A locked room beside the washing area stored raw material for the *langar*, and the courtyard of the main shrine had two entrances, one at the front and one at the right side, facing the Langar Hall.

The building's image travels. On a poster of all the functioning gurdwaras of Pakistan, seen at Panja Sahib, a large picture of the Janam Asthan took the middle.

## The Massacre of 1921

Nankana Sahib is remembered not only for a birth but for a bloodletting. In the early twentieth century the movement to wrest control of the historic gurdwaras from their hereditary custodians gathered force as the Gurdwara Reform Movement. Many of the custodians were *mahants*, heads of religious establishments, of the Udasi order, accused of corruption and misappropriation, though accounts of who the *mahants* were differ. At the Janam Asthan the custodian was Mahant Narayan Das, who was alleged to have sold parts of the vast property connected with the gurdwara.

On 20 February 1921 a peaceful *jatha*, or band, of Akali reformers gathered at Gurdwara Tambu Sahib to protest against the control of the *mahants* over the great gurdwaras, and entered the Janam Asthan unarmed. It was led by Bhai Lachhman Singh Dharowali, though accounts of its leadership differ. Mahant Narayan Das had armed retainers waiting, though accounts of who they were differ, and the reformers were fired upon and cut down. Bhai Lachhman Singh, it is recounted, was tied to a tree and burned, and Bhai Daleep Singh was thrown into a brick kiln that once stood within the premises of the gurdwaras. Scores of Sikhs were killed; accounts of the toll differ, but tradition speaks of at least eighty six martyrs.

The British government acted against Narayan Das and his accomplices: some were sentenced to death and others were sent for life to Kala Pani, the Andaman Islands. The *Saka*, the massacre, at Nankana became a galvanising episode in the reform struggle. It formed the basis of a larger movement to take the major gurdwaras from the *mahants*, and it hastened the passage of the Sikh Gurdwaras Act of 1925 and the establishment of elected management of the shrines.

In 2011 the tree stood next to the portico, with a plaque reading "Sikh Martyrdom" and "20th February 1921 at Gurdwara Janamasthan, Nankana Sahib." A second portico, with a glass room, marks the spot where the martyrs of that day were cremated in one big pile; its golden palanquin was given to the gurdwara by Captain Amarinder Singh, the former Chief Minister of the Indian Punjab. Two paintings hung in the glass room: Baba Nanak with Bhai Mardana and Bhai Bala, Mardana playing the *rubab*, a plucked string instrument, and Bala holding the fly whisk; and the boy Nanak shaded by a cobra.

## The Keepers and the Community

After the Partition of 1947, when the newly drawn border left Nankana Sahib within Pakistan and the great mass of Sikhs on the Indian side, the shrine passed into the care of the state, though accounts of its care in the decades that followed differ. Today it is administered through the Evacuee Trust Property Board and the Pakistan Sikh Gurdwara Prabandhak Committee, the PSGPC, which oversee its upkeep and the reception of pilgrims.

Kartar, whose father settled inside the Janam Asthan, remembered an empty town: no Sikh was left after Partition, and the gurdwaras became barren and fell into a bad state. In the 1960s Giani Pratap came with a party of pilgrims from India and, seeing the dilapidated condition of the shrines, decided to stay and look after these holy buildings. Kartar's father moved from the Tirat Valley, in the tribal areas, after the war of 1971 between India and Pakistan. Giani Pratap housed the family inside the gurdwara for a time and made Kartar's father a *sewadar*, a worker, at the gurdwara.

In 1978 Rajbir moved to Nankana with his cousin, within two months of a passionate speech by Dr. Jagjit Singh Chauhan, who had invited Pakistani Sikhs to move to Nankana Sahib and take care of its gurdwaras. "There were hardly any Sikhs here at that time," he recalled. By 2011, at seventy, he was one of the oldest employees of the Janam Asthan. That year the head *granthi*, the reader and keeper of the Guru Granth Sahib, was Lalit Singh, a young man in his late thirties whose family had lived there since the late 1970s; he was considered the most learned man in religion among the town's Sikhs, and he led the community prayers most of the time.

The town's Sikhs were not indigenous to it but recent migrants from Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, where there is a considerable Sikh population. Most of the newcomers were housed within the gurdwaras, in rooms generally reserved for pilgrims, and their arrival gave a boost of confidence to Sikhs who had until then been dominated by the Muslim majority. Within about ten years their numbers multiplied, to about two hundred households, and for several years the community was led by Jatha Singh. Some of the young men spoke Urdu with a Pashto accent, and not all the Sikhs there could speak Urdu or Punjabi. Several people in the town felt that the PSGPC was not representative of the Sikh community in Pakistan.

## Devotion and Festivals

On the night of Guru Gobind Singh's birthday in January 2011, people walked to the gurdwara through a fog near freezing, and the guard at the entrance was too cold to check for anyone suspicious trying to enter. Lalit Singh played the *tabla*, the pair of hand drums, on the stage next to the shrine, with two women singing, one of them at the harmonium, and teenage girls sang a song to Guru Gobind Singh. The evening was dedicated to the children, who prostrated themselves before the Granth Sahib and recited the lines they had learnt. They wore white and, for the day, the five Ks, the five articles of the Sikh faith, though most of them had not yet been baptised, and their turbans were orange, blue or black by group.

Surjeet ran the children's programme: recitation from the Guru Granth Sahib, songs to the bravery of Guru Gobind Singh, and a poem by a girl of fourteen lamenting that the young were cutting their hair and their turbans were being replaced with caps. Udham called out "Jo bole so nihal!", and the audience answered "Sat Sri Akal!", the Sikh call and its response. It went on until eleven at night. The festival had opened with a cricket tournament on the ground of Guru Nanak Government College, reserved for the Sikhs that day, and it closed the next morning with a prize giving and a communal prayer, the trophies brought from the main shrine.

Lohri followed. The procession went from Tambu Sahib to the Janam Asthan, the main gurdwara, while Muslims in the shops and on motorbikes watched with burning curiosity. About a hundred people heard *kirtan*, the singing of hymns, in a small room with a portico. After an hour of *kirtan*, Lalit Singh led the communal prayer, and the Granth was carried on an elderly man's head around the shrine to its bedroom and covered with a blanket, which marks the end of the prayer and the beginning of the Lohri festival. The community then gathered at a flame in front of the main building, in the outer courtyard, an integral part of the celebration of Lohri.

Lohri, like Vaisakhi or Basant, is an ancient festival of the Punjab that commemorates the change of season, kept on the 13th of January, generally the last night of the month of Poh. After the creation of Pakistan this indigenous festival of the Punjab died down. At Nankana, Lohri and Guru Gobind Singh's birthday had begun to be celebrated only in the couple of years before 2011, made possible by the growth of the Sikh population through migration from the tribal areas; before that, Kartar said, there had been only a prayer at the gurdwara. The celebration of 2011 was an attempt to revive this cultural tradition of the Punjab, and it was being revived by a group of Pathan men living in the Punjab.

On the Sikh New Year, 14 March 2011, Rajbir Singh stood with a fly whisk before the palanquin that holds the Granth Sahib, concluding the *bhog*, the communal prayer, while hundreds of devotees stood outside following his lead. The community then moved to another pavilion, with a golden palanquin, where in a glass chamber a young boy was reading the last pages of the Granth as the final reader of the *Akhand Path*, the unbroken reading of the whole scripture.

The congregation was mixed, Pathan Sikhs of Nankana Sahib and clean shaven Sindhi devotees, about three hundred people, half from each community. "We are Sikhs but not Singhs," said Ajay Kumar, forty two, from Sukkur in Sindh; his party of more than a hundred people had come to hold prayers in memory of Baba Thara Singh, and had arranged the concluding *Akhand Path*, the *langar*, and the sweets and biscuits given as *prasad*, the consecrated food. The festival was not publicised, since it was not only a religious event but also a political one, with no precedent in Pakistan. Pakistani Sikhs keep the original Nanakshahi calendar, which is why the New Year was celebrated there on 14 March, while Indian Sikhs continue to celebrate it on 13 April.

That night six Muslim *qawwals*, singers of *qawwali*, the Sufi devotional music, performed at the Janam Asthan in brown *shalwar kameez*, the loose trousers and long shirt of Pakistan, and Muslim caps, and they sang first to the Muslim saint Baba Farid. "This is the first time since partition that Muslim Qawwalis have been invited to perform at Nankana Sahib," said Kartar. "We have called them from Faisalabad."

The *Gurpurab* is the shrine's great day. Official estimates put the number who come to Nankana Sahib for it at ten to fifteen thousand every year; in 2011, when it ran from 8 to 10 November, media reports counted only three thousand. Pilgrims from India cross the border at Wagah, where representatives of the Evacuee Trust Property Board and the PSGPC receive them and a *langar* is laid on, and they stay three days at Nankana. Three days of *Akhand Path* end in the *bhog*, followed by the *Nagar Kirtan*, the procession with hymns. Richer pilgrims from Britain, America and Canada come by chartered plane and hire buses. Devotees walk barefoot; Mahtab Singh, seventy two, became a celebrity when his interview was broadcast on national television at the festival of 2010, vowing to go without shoes through his pilgrimage.

In 2011 the main focus of the pilgrims was the Janam Asthan, with more police inside it than at the entrance, and everyone, even the representatives of the media, had been allotted a pass. A Geo television van and media cars broadcast live by satellite. A blue bus decorated with yellow flowers waited for the *Nagar Kirtan* round the green belt at the centre of the complex, and traders from other parts of the country set up in tents. Meena Kumari, fifty four, sold food beneath Block 1, a residential hostel, stock she had bought in bulk from Mardan, and stalls sold shirts printed with "Never Forget 1984", "Singh is King" and "Raj Karega Khalsa".

The *langar* served food around the clock, as at Gurdwara Tambu Sahib; the floor was wet with water, food, tea and mud, most of the volunteers were local Pathan Sikhs, and police patrolled the hall. Volunteers performing *sewa*, voluntary service, washed the utensils in two large tubs. The courtyard was covered with tents and carpets where pilgrims slept, most of them locals from Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and Sindh, with a few Indian families as well.

From a stage next to the shrine three men in white *shalwar kameez*, who appeared to be Muslims, sang *kirtan*, their heads covered with a loose turban in a style popular in the villages of West Punjab. A screen showed the proceedings, which were broadcast live through the internet to be viewed by millions of people the world over, the first time this had been done from there. For *darshan*, the sight of the holy scripture, devotees queued behind an orange robe, prostrated themselves before the Guru Granth Sahib, went round clockwise, entered by a small door on the left, fanned the fly whisk over the Book, left by a door on the right, and received *sujji ka halwa*, a sweet of semolina, as *prasad*.

The procession had been drawn in. The round of the city had not taken place for two years because of a security threat, a shopkeeper explained, and a small procession was taken out within the precincts of the gurdwara, where it was safe. After the *Akhand Path* a decorated pickup with a *dhol* player, a drummer, handed out packets of juice. Ten Sikh men in two lines of five led the way, the front row in white *cholas*, long robes, and saffron turbans, carrying the *Nishan Sahib*, the row behind in saffron *cholas* and blue turbans, with *kirpans*, the ceremonial daggers of the Sikhs. The bus carried the Guru Granth Sahib in a golden palanquin, devotees on the main gate threw red rose petals, and its halt at the starting point concluded the *Nagar Kirtan* and with it the religious rituals of the festival.

Security shaped the day. Police stood in force at all the entrances to the city, and no car was permitted inside. Nadeem, a Muslim painter returning after seven years, found the main gate, once made of iron bars through which one could watch the activities inside, turned into one big closed structure barring any view; the two smaller gates beside it were shut, the wall had been raised and protected by barbed wire on top, and police with guns stood at the gate, on top of the walls and on all the other higher structures of the complex. A new entrance had been opened at the side, some distance from the main gate. Pilgrims stood in two queues, the women searched by female police in a small enclosure, the men checked in the open before passing two further security checks.

## Muslims and the Birthplace

At the same festival a police official checked Nadeem's identity card and told him: "No Muslims allowed." Nadeem protested that he had come before, and received no reply. Barefoot before the main gate, he raised his hand in the direction of the shrine: "I will just say hello to the Baba from here then. Good bye, Baba Ji." For Nadeem, Guru Nanak was like a Muslim Sufi saint, and many Muslims acquainted with the Guru's teachings are devoted to him too and visit his shrine.

A Muslim tradition, which Sikhs do not accept, holds that Guru Nanak was born by the blessing of the saint Baba Naulakha Hazari: Nanak's parents, it is believed, came to the saint's shrine, and the saint blessed them, after which Nanak was born.

The regard for Nanak found among Muslims at Gurdwara Sachkand was not found among the Muslims of Nankana Sahib. Most of the town's Muslims had migrated from the other side of the border after Partition, and their attitudes are said to have been shaped by those experiences. Some shopkeepers kept one set of glasses for Muslim customers and another for the rest. A restaurant owner, Aslam Chaudary, refused Sikh customers, "We do not have your utensils", and complained, "We become prisoners within our own city", though not all traders resented the pilgrims who brought business to the town. At Guru Nanak Government College only a few Sikh boys studied, and the majority of the students were Muslim. A group of newly appointed government officials once came to sit with the head priest, and one of them told him that Muslims had always liked the Sikhs, who were closer to their religion, and that the Hindus were the ones they could never be friends with. Within the gurdwara, one visitor who sat with his back towards the main shrine was told by a Sikh man not to sit that way.

## Legends

Many legends gather around the Guru himself. One tells that Nanak once travelled to Mecca, where he performed the *Hajj*, the pilgrimage. He slept with his feet towards the Kaaba and was rebuked; he turned his feet, and as he did so, the Kaaba miraculously turned towards his feet.

In the Janamsakhis he meets saints long dead, and at Panipat, it is told, he held discussions with the spirit of Shaikh Sharafu'd-Din Abu 'Ali Qalandar. He is said, too, to have debated with the Naths and Siddhas, yogis and ascetic adepts, on the legendary Mount Sumeru.

When Babur invaded, it is told, the Guru was taken prisoner, and he was freed because the conqueror was so impressed with his hymns. The Janamsakhis also record a meeting between Nanak and Babur at Saidpur, where Babur asked for a blessing to conquer India and Nanak rebuked him.

Other legends tell of Nanak humbling Muslim Sufis, Hamza Ghous at Sialkot and Mian Mitha at Pasrur. Yet Nanak himself is remembered as one who abhorred the association of miracles with religious personalities, and in one story he tells Karori, who calls his cure a miracle, that "there is no such thing as a miracle".

## The Guru's Verse

The shrine's worship is sung. Nanak's *bani*, his devotional verses of supplication, entreaty, humility and reverence, was compiled as part of the Adi Granth by Guru Arjan Dev, the fifth Guru, in 1604, and the Guru Granth Sahib carries also the poetry of other spiritual teachers, among them Bhagat Kabir, Baba Farid and Bhai Mardana. The Guru composed Hindi verses on the truth of God, setting out divine unity in clear words and shining metaphors, and a singer named Mardana sang his verses and drew people into the snare of devotion. Like Kabir, he used the *doha*, a rhyming couplet in an old Prakrit and Hindi metre, for his mystical poetry, and he brought into Punjabi several words that were not part of the Punjabi of his time. In Aurangzeb's time the worship of his followers was the reading of their master's verses, which they sang to melody.

Guru Nanak is held to have been a spiritual follower of Baba Farid, the Sufi saint whose writings the Granth Sahib also contains. He travelled to Pak Pattan, where he met the spiritual descendants of Baba Farid and collected the saint's poetry, and Baba Farid's texts are revered among the Sikhs because he influenced the founder of their religion.

In one hymn the Guru tells of the failure of the miracle workers against Babur's army:

ਕੋਟੀ ਹੂ ਪੀਰ ਵਰਜਿ ਰਹਾਏ ਜਾ ਮੀਰੁ ਸੁਣਿਆ ਧਾਇਆ ॥
ਥਾਨ ਮੁਕਾਮ ਜਲੇ ਬਿਜ ਮੰਦਰ ਮੁਛਿ ਮੁਛਿ ਕੁਇਰ ਰੁਲਾਇਆ ॥
ਕੋਈ ਮੁਗਲੁ ਨ ਹੋਆ ਅੰਧਾ ਕਿਨੈ ਨ ਪਰਚਾ ਲਾਇਆ ॥

*Millions of priests tried by their miraculous power to restrain the Emperor when they heard of his approach. He burned houses, mansions, and palaces; he cut princes to pieces, and had them rolled in the dust. No Mughal hath become blind; no priest hath wrought a miracle*

In the *Babur Bani*, his hymns on Babur, Nanak lays the blame of Babur's wrath at God's feet.

The *aarti*, the hymn sung as lamps are waved in worship, begins:

ਗਗਨ ਮੈ ਥਾਲੁ
ਰਵਿ ਚੰਦੁ ਦੀਪਕ ਬਨੇ …

*In the salver of the firmament, / The sun and the moon shine as lamps*

On the oneness of God he sang:

ਸਾਹਿਬੁ ਮੇਰਾ ਏਕੋ ਹੈ ॥
ਏਕੋ ਹੈ ਭਾਈ ਏਕੋ ਹੈ ॥

*My Master is One / He is One, brother, the only One*

And again:

*God is One, / The Path is One*

His poem on the sacred thread runs:

ਦਇਆ ਕਪਾਹ
ਸੰਤੋਖੁ ਸੂਤੁ …

*From the cotton of compassion, / Spin the thread of contentment*

Of washing he sang:

ਭਰੀਐ ਹਥੁ ਪੈਰੁ ਤਨੁ ਦੇਹ ॥
ਪਾਣੀ ਧੋਤੈ ਉਤਰਸੁ ਖੇਹ ॥

*As hands or feet besmirched with slime, / Water washes white*

And in another song:

ਬਗਾ ਬਗੇ ਕਪੜੇ ਤੀਰਥ ਮੰਝਿ ਵਸੰਨਿੑ ॥

*The herons in their white feathers dwell in the sacred shrines of pilgrimage*

The Guru's verse is sung outside the gurdwaras too. The singer Abida Parvin has said: "I sing Shah Hussain, Bulle Shah, Mian Muhammad Bakhsh, Hazrat Khawaja Ghulam Farid, Sacchal Sarmast, Baba Farid, and Guru Nanak." In Sindh, Sain Vasan Shah became famous for reciting the *banis* of Shri Guru Nanak and the *kalams*, the poems, of the Sufis.

## The Setting

Talwandi lay between the rivers Ravi and Chenab, directly in the path of the armies that had set their eyes on Delhi. It is told that the village was destroyed thirteen times and thirteen times rose again from its ashes, like the fabled phoenix, and that its last rebuilding was overseen by Rai Bhoe, the father of Rai Bular. In the fifteenth century the Punjab enjoyed a peaceful period under Afghan rule, as the Afghans resettled the land, founded many new towns and promoted trade and commerce.

Originally called Rai Bhoi di Talwandi, the city was renamed Nankana Sahib in honour of Guru Nanak, and because he spent his early years here it has several gurdwaras, most of them associated with incidents from his life, all of which the pilgrims visit. In 2011 most of them remained locked all year round, to be opened only at the time of the festival, with security guards employed at the entrance to each.

The town had one main road running through it; its buildings were old, most of them from the late British or the early Pakistan era, and the road had potholes. From a roof one could see the minarets of mosques, the small white domes of the gurdwaras, and the remains of a few Hindu temples, cone like structures rising from houses abandoned at the time of Partition. During the Musharraf era, from 2001 to 2008, new roads and other government facilities made their way into the city, and in 2005 Nankana Sahib was separated from Sheikhupura and made an independent district, with the city as its headquarters. Sikhs abroad had asked for an international hotel and for the status of a holy city that pilgrims could visit without a visa, but by 2011 without result.

Guru Nanak was celebrated all over Nankana Sahib: there were a Guru Nanak High School, a Guru Nanak grocery store and a Guru Nanak cloth shop, all run by Sikhs. Madan Singh, a former head of the PSGPC and a businessman of the town, ran Guru Nanak High School, the only school in the country where the Granth Sahib was taught to Sikh children. Many Sikh families practised *hikmat*, traditional medicine, and there was a growing migration of Sikh boys and men from Nankana Sahib to bigger cities such as Lahore, Faisalabad and Gujranwala.

The town's gurdwaras have known politics. In November 1985, on Guru Nanak's birthday, a year after Operation Blue Star, thousands of Sikh pilgrims came to Nankana from all over the world. The PSGPC had not yet been formed, and the staff of the Indian High Commission in Islamabad received the pilgrims at the border and arranged their stay inside the gurdwaras. After the *Akhand Path* and the *bhog*, an American Sikh, Sardar Ganga Singh Dhillion, spoke for a separate Sikh state at one of the town's gurdwaras. During the speech the gate of the gurdwara was closed, and Counsellor B. Jain and First Secretary K. K. Khanna were attacked and barely escaped alive. Madan Singh, only nineteen at the time, was among the attackers; he was arrested with twenty five others and held in Sheikhupura Jail for ten days. By 2011 the festivals at Nankana and in other cities of Pakistan had gone back to being religious festivals.

## Legacy

As the cradle of the faith and the site of one of its defining modern martyrdoms, Gurudwara Janam Asthan holds a singular place in the Sikh imagination, a home to which the community continually returns. Each year, and above all at the *Gurpurab* marking the Guru's birth, large numbers of Sikhs travel from India and across the diaspora to Nankana Sahib, where the town swells with congregations and the sound of *kirtan*.

Guru Nanak's birthday at Nankana Sahib was among the few religious events kept alive by foreign pilgrims after Partition. Sikhs from abroad came for festivals such as Baisakhi and the birthday of Guru Nanak, which were attended by thousands of Indian pilgrims, and pilgrim tourists come to Nankana Sahib and Hassan Abdal. During the festival the entire city looked like a little India for a few days. In the city of his birth, as at Kartarpur, where he passed away, the gurdwaras built later by his devotees still stand tall.

Devotion to the Guru reaches beyond the Sikhs. The Nanak Panthis, Hindus who believe in the teachings of Guru Nanak but not in the Khalsa, the order of initiated Sikhs, keep portraits of Guru Nanak in their temples in Sindh and southern Punjab, along with Krishna, Kali Mata and other Hindu deities. Many Lohanas in Sindh follow the teachings of Nanak, and in Sindh there is a growing similarity between the iconography of Guru Nanak and that of Jhulelal. In the valley of the goddess Hinglaj the engraved footsteps of Guru Nanak connect that shrine to the Sikh and Nanak *panth*, or sect, traditions. A Chishti Sabiri Sufi master of the twentieth century described Guru Nanak, with Krishna, Rama, Sita and Lachman, among the spiritual luminaries outside Islam, as a believer in the one true God and a religious leader with a divine connection, *nisbat*.

The birthplace is remembered far from it. At Manora in Karachi, Jeeta Singh held a small festival every November, when the festival of Guru Nanak is celebrated at Nankana Sahib, and insisted that Nanak never came to Manora: "He was from Punjab, Nankana Sahib."
