## Consensus choices

- **Her place in the family:** "Accounts of her place in the family differ, but by tradition she was a great-granddaughter of the celebrated saint Makhdoom Jahaniyan Jahangasht." Basis (a), the site account, supported in part by (b): two of the three accounts make her a great-granddaughter (the site account and Khalid, whose ancestor is "Syed Jalaludin Bokhari", a name that could be either Jalaluddin of Uch). Rozehnal's "wife of Sayyid Jalal ad-Din Bukhari (1303 to 1383)" is not listed. Her line is given as the Jalali Bukhari line and the great Bukhari family of sayyids, which all three accounts allow.
- **When the tomb was built:** "Tradition holds that her tomb was built around 1493, in her honour, by an Iranian prince named Dilshad"; the Overview says "raised in the late fifteenth century". Basis (a), the site account. The board at the site, as Khalid reports it, gives 1494: a year's difference within "around", dropped silently as minor. Khalid's "thirteenth and fourteenth century shrines" is a loose label for all three buildings on the mound, while his own report for her tomb is the board's 1494; dropped silently as minor.
- **When the flood came:** "There is no settled view of when that flood came; it is placed between the sixteenth and the nineteenth century." Basis (d). The site account places the collapse, by tradition, in a flood in the nineteenth century; Iqbal Qaiser, in Khalid's book, says a huge flood in the sixteenth century destroyed these buildings. Basis (a) would favour the site account, but the only other account is against it; (b) is a tie, one each; (c) does not settle it, since the site account's source is undated. That the river undermined the building and that a flood brought much of it down is common ground and is told, the flood framed as tradition.
- **Where the tomb stands:** in the Bukhari quarter of the town, basis (a), the site account. Khalid's "on the outskirts" is dropped as minor; his mound used as a graveyard, reached through the market, is kept as a description of the ground in the early twenty first century, since a mound graveyard does not contradict the quarter. V1 should confirm this is not a merger of two accounts.
- **The tilework:** the site account's "cobalt blue, turquoise and white *kashi* tilework" for the building, basis (a); Khalid's glazed Multani tiles in blue and white and his white dome are kept as what remained in the early twenty first century. No side by side variants; not a real conflict.
- **Her name:** Bibi Jawindi (the site account and Rozehnal); Khalid's Jiwindi dropped silently as a spelling variant.
- **Where Uch lies:** Rozehnal's "seventy miles southwest of Multan" and Schimmel's "northeast of Multan" cannot be settled: no shrine position, one source each, and neither passage is devoted to this site. Both directions are left out, and the town is placed by what no account contests: southern Punjab, near Bahawalpur, on a hill overlooking the confluence of the Sutlej and Chenab.
- **When the Qadiri order came:** its first missionaries settled at Uch "in the late fifteenth century", basis (b): Schimmel's Uch passage, and the edition's 887 AH for Sayyid Muhammad Ghaus's arrival also falls in the late fifteenth century (887 AH begins in 1482 CE; the conversion is ours, so no Common Era year is printed, and 887 AH itself is left out as the editor's date, not the author's). Schimmel's other date, "the late fourteenth century", concerns the order's arrival in the subcontinent at large, not Uch; dropped silently as minor.
- **The first Qadiri masters at Uch:** Lahori's Hazrat Sayyid Muhammad Ghaus Uchi Jili Gilani (the first of the line to come to the Punjab) and Rozehnal's Shaykh Muhammad al-Husseini al-Jilani (founder of the first Qadiri *khanqah* at Uch) are not identified with each other in the team version. They are told in separate sentences, neither merged nor contrasted, and al-Jilani's route by Khurasan and Multan is left out so that the two do not read as one journey told twice. Chishti's four sons of Hazrat Muhammad Ghaus beside the tomb of Shah Muhammad Ghaus is told in its own sentence, without identifying the names.
- **Time anchors:** Khalid (2016, firm) gives "In the early twenty first century" for the mound and for Nanak's well, since his visit precedes the book but its year is not given. Rozehnal's April 1996 is in the team version's text. Chishti (1867, "the date unconfirmed") gives only "In the nineteenth century" for the fame of the sayyids of Uch. Bhandari (composed in regnal year 40 of Aurangzeb) gives "In the reign of the emperor Aurangzeb" for the township of Uch and the bounds of Thatta.

- **Rival accounts (rule 4):** Rozehnal's "wife of Sayyid Jalal ad-Din Bukhari"; Khalid's ancestor "Syed Jalaludin Bokhari" as a named alternative; the board's 1494; "thirteenth and fourteenth century shrines"; the two directions from Multan; Schimmel's "late fourteenth century"; the edition's 887 AH; the birth years of Makhdum-i Jahaniyan (1303 in Rozehnal's bracket, 9 February 1308 in Rizvi), which are not needed once the wife account goes.
- **Speculation:** Khalid's surmise that Nanak "must have seen these buildings intact with a mighty river flowing next to it", an author's imagining, and at odds with the building date.
- **Passages whose subject is unclear:** Rizvi's "The Baba found a lonely mosque in Uch" (which Baba is not said); the unnamed Sufi who was possibly initiated into the Suhrawardiyya at Uch "as is suggested by local legends" (unnamed, and a possibility only); Boivin's "one of the masters of the second Sohrawardiyya period, then based in Utch" (the master is unnamed and it is unclear what was based there); Kasmani's "Their shrines are spread across southern Punjab" (whose shrines is unclear); Khalid's "the shrine of Jalaludin Bokhari at Uch Sharif" (which Jalaluddin is not said); the line "Thou art an inhabitant of Uch city" (the person addressed is not given), keeping only the meaning of the name.
- **Minor detail:** the car needed to reach the mound; Iqbal Qaiser's remark that the place had changed since his last visit; Rozehnal's remark that there seems to be no record of the life of 'Abd al-Qadir Jilani Thani (a statement about records).
- **Reworded:** Rizvi's verdict "Although this story would appear mythical, it is typical of medieval tales relating to sufis" is kept as "It is a tale typical of the medieval stories told of Sufis", since the tale is already framed as a legend. Abbas's "Uch Gilanian" is given simply as Uch, "known as the city of saints".

## Glosses

From `data/glossary.csv`: sayyids (descendants of the Prophet Muhammad); *ʿurs* (the yearly commemoration of a saint's death); *khanqah* (Sufi lodge); *qawwali* (the devotional music of the Sufis); the Qadiri order (the Sufi order founded by Abd al-Qadir Gilani); shaikh (a Sufi master, from the glossary's master or teacher).

Plain dictionary senses, for terms the glossary lacks: Sufis (Muslim mystics); *kashi* (glazed tilework); Multani tiles (the tiles of Multan); *havelis* (great mansions, as in shah-jamal); syncretism (the blending of faiths and customs); *suba* (province); yogi (a Hindu ascetic); *qawwals* (singers of *qawwali*); gurdwara (a Sikh place of worship); Guru Nanak (the founder of the Sikh faith).

The Suhrawardi order is glossed only as "one of the orders of Sufis, or Muslim mystics", without the glossary's founder, Shihab al-Din Suhrawardi, because the Setting names another Shihab al-Din (the one who took Uch on his first campaign) and the two would be confused.
