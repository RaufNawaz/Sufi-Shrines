## Overview

At Mozang Chungi on Queens Road in Lahore, not far from Fatima Jinnah Medical University, lies the shrine of Shah Inayat Qadiri. He is one of the most historically consequential Sufi figures of Mughal Punjab, less for his own writings, considerable though they were, than as the *murshid*, the spiritual guide, of Bulleh Shah, arguably the most celebrated of all Punjabi Sufi poets. His shrine and its adjoining mosque remain an active place of pilgrimage, and his yearly *ʿurs*, the commemoration of the saint's death, draws thousands of devotees.

He came to Lahore from Kasur, served as imam of a mosque near Bhati Gate, founded a school of his own and wrote Persian works of mystical philosophy. His acceptance of the young Syed Bulleh Shah as a disciple, across the lines of caste, became one of the most retold episodes in Punjabi Sufi lore, and Bulleh Shah's songs name him again and again, in joy, in longing and in the pain of separation.

## The Saint

Shah Inayat was born around 1643 at Kasur, into a farming family of the Arain community, a caste of gardeners and small cultivators who worked their own land. Accounts of his origins differ, but most tie him to Kasur, and he is described as "of Kasur, then of Lahore". His father was Pir Muhammad. His full name, as it stands in his own works *Zail al-Aghlat* and *Ghayat al-Hawashi*, was Hafiz Abu al-Maarif Muhammad Inayat Allah Hanafi Qadiri Shattari; he is also called Shah Inayat Allah Qadiri and Inayat Shah. *Hafiz* is the title of one who has memorised the whole Qur'an, and he is said to have known it by heart by the age of five. He also bears the titles Sheikh-ul-Mashaikh, master of masters, Shah-e-Lahore, king of Lahore, and Mufakkir-e-Pakistan, thinker of Pakistan.

He studied the outward sciences with Maulana Sayyid Abu al-Nasr, called Sayyid Ilyas, and with Maulvi Abdul Hadi Lahori, and took inner guidance from Shah Ali Raza Faruqi, a great-grandson of the Mujaddid Alf-e Sani, the renewer of the second millennium, and from Shaikh Muhammad Sultan Bukhari. Accounts of his master differ; the one most often named is Shah Raza Qadri Shattari of Lahore, to whom he attached himself after 1111 AH and who died in 1118 AH (1707 CE). Shah Inayat became one of his *khalifas*, his deputies, and was counted a man of great rank, versed in both the outward and the inward sciences.

He combined the Qadiri and Shattari orders in his own practice, and his full name carries both, though he is often counted simply a Qadiri, a follower of the order of Sheikh Abdul Qadir of Baghdad. Within the Shattari order the doctrine of *fanā fī 'l-shaiḳh*, the obliteration of the self in the master, was given particular importance. His learning in Arabic and Persian was overlaid with an acceptance of the mystical traditions of the people of the subcontinent, an openness that one writer has called ironical, since the creed of the Qadiri order's Hanbali founder was originally opposed to innovative adjustments of that kind. His life spanned, it has been said, the Mughal rule from Aurangzeb to Mohammad Shah.

At Kasur he fell out with Husain Khan, the ruler of Kasur, and left to settle in Lahore. Local tradition attributes part of the friction to the ruler's ill intent towards Shah Inayat's wife, who is described as unusually beautiful and of noble family. In Lahore he served as imam, the leader of the prayers, of the Oonchi Masjid near Bhati Gate and founded his own *madrasa*, a religious school, where he taught students without regard to caste or class. In one of Bulleh Shah's lyrics Lahore, the seat of royal authority in the Punjab and hence called *taḳhat lahor*, the throne of Lahore, is celebrated as Shah Inayat's home.

He wrote several Persian works of mystical philosophy, among them the *Dastur-ul-Amal* and the *Lata'if-e-Ghaibiya*. Sixteen of his works have been traced, of which *Ghayat al-Hawashi* is the largest. In the *Dastur-ul-Amal*, it has been argued, he pointed to a Hindu origin for the mystical doctrines and to their reappearance in Greek thought centuries later, and he is said to have believed in the Hindu origin of Islamic mysticism, a knowledge carried, in his view, by the Greek soldiers of Alexander the Great and borrowed from Greece by the mystics of Islam.

## Bulleh Shah, the Disciple

His acceptance of Bulleh Shah as a disciple became one of the most retold episodes in Punjabi Sufi lore, because it crossed rigid social boundaries. Bulleh Shah came from a Syed family, descendants of the Prophet, of higher perceived status, yet he sought out and remained devoted to a teacher of humbler, land tilling background, an Arain of lower rank. It is said that he faced religious decrees, *fatwas*, from his own family over the choice, and that he answered only "what I have seen in him, you cannot perceive." One of his lyrics has been read as casting the master as Ranjha of the romance of Hir: just as Ranjha was despised by Hir's family, so Shah Inayat was disparaged for being an Arain. The relationship, immortalised in Bulleh Shah's own verses, is still cited in Pakistan as an example of Sufism's disregard for caste hierarchy.

Tradition holds that Bulleh Shah was sent to him by another master. Abd al-Hakim Qadiri declined to take the young man as his disciple and told him that his share lay with Shah Inayat Qadiri, and that he should go to him and take it. With his father's leave Bulleh Shah went to Lahore to take *bai'at*, the oath of initiation, and entered Shah Inayat's service.

The master, it is said, set a price on the initiation: he would give *bai'at* only if the young man first brought five hundred rupees in cash, a horse worth five hundred rupees, a pair of gold bangles worth five hundred rupees and a suit of clothes worth five hundred rupees. When all four had been delivered exactly, he honoured Bulleh Shah with *bai'at*, and afterwards with the *khirqa* of succession, the robe that marks a master's successor. In the town, the story goes, people would say that these clothes and this horse were Shah Inayat's. From him Bulleh Shah received the word, or *kalmā*, of his Sufi teaching.

After several years, master and disciple parted. Accounts of the cause differ, but most tell that Shah Inayat disapproved of his disciple's verse. He himself, as one writer on Bulleh Shah put it, worked under "the careful camouflage of sophisticated Persian metaphor", and he was dismayed by the rough vernacular of a disciple whose poetry was too outspoken against the priestly class, openly critical on matters of gender, class and caste, and mocking of institutional religion. He advised Bulleh Shah to stop railing against the orthodox postures that the political might of the Mughal court upheld, and Bulleh Shah did not listen. These were, it has been argued, hard times for the Qadiri order: in Aurangzeb's time the Qadiri mystics of Lahore were on the defensive, careful not to offend an orthodoxy inclined to take severe punitive measures against heretics. Afraid of the political forces of the time, Shah Inayat shut his rebellious disciple out of his spiritual company. It is said that he withdrew his disciple's *wilayat*, his sainthood, and later restored it, as though it were a grant that could be revoked.

The separation lives on in Bulleh Shah's songs. His *kafi*, or short mystical lyric, *Teray Ishq nachaya* has been read as an expression of his desolation and helplessness before an unavoidable passion, and of his estrangement from his master, and a line of one famous *kafi* as symbolising the gloom created by the master's departure. Bulleh Shah knew that Shah Inayat was fond of music. It is said that, turned out of his master's house, he took up the *sarangi*, the stringed instrument associated with the wandering dervish, and, aware of the Qadiri custom of *samaʿ*, spiritual listening, and devotional dance, began to sing and dance. In time, the story continues, seeing that his disciple's heterodoxy had a divine source, the teacher took him back, and the reunion was dramatic. In the most popular tale, Bulleh Shah reappeared in Lahore one day dressed as a dancing girl, and danced and sang before Shah Inayat.

Tradition also tells of a dramatic encounter between the two in Lahore, each outdoing the other in a display of miraculous powers. Waris Shah is said to have been a second prominent disciple of this period.

## In Bulleh Shah's Verse

Bulleh Shah names his master again and again, and most often he calls on him at the end of a whole train of suffering caused by separation. A true parallel to that closing invocation, it has been said, is found only in Rumi. Where Shah Husain makes no reference to his beloved Madho Lal in his *kafis*, as has been observed, Bulleh Shah's invocation of Shah Inayat is constant. The poet as a saint's disciple is a common figure in Sufi poetry, and one with no counterpart in Western literature. In the lyrics the master is where the search ends:

*I have found Shah Inayat.* (translated by Christopher Shackle)

*I got caught in its whirlpools, but Shah Inayat delivered me to the other side.* (translated by Christopher Shackle)

*With every breath I have remembered Inayat.* (translated by Christopher Shackle)

He holds the secret, and he is the stone that transmutes:

*If Shah Inayat reveals the secret, then all mysteries are solved.* (translated by Christopher Shackle)

*Bullha, Shah Inayat is a master mystic, he is the lord of my heart. I am iron and he is the philosopher's stone.* (translated by Christopher Shackle)

One poem places him in the royal city:

*Baghdad is the place of the Pir of Pirs; our guide is in royal Lahore* (translated by Christopher Shackle)

Within a few lines the same poem has:

*Shah Inayat has set out his hooks, and without being seen he tugs the line* (translated by Christopher Shackle)

He is the poet's faith and his very being:

*Shah Inayat is my religion, the one whom we accept in this world and the next* (translated by Christopher Shackle)

*My whole being became Inayat; then it got the name Bullha* (translated by Christopher Shackle)

*Leaving my parents, I have clung to your skirt, oh my lord Shah Inayat.* (translated by Christopher Shackle)

Another poem closes on a profession of the mystical identity between the disciple and the master standing directly before him.

In absence and in longing the poems call him by his order's name:

*With the departure of Inayat Qadiri, my story has again become difficult* (translated by Christopher Shackle)

*Come, Inayat Qadiri, my heart desires you* (translated by Christopher Shackle)

And the poems go to his gate:

*Bullha, let us go and sit at the gate of Shah Inayat* (translated by Christopher Shackle)

Elsewhere the disciple burns with love, waits at the door, and holds that only the master can lead the way:

*The flames engulfing my house, Bulleh, only love can douse. Inayat calls up the aisle* (translated by Taufiq Rafat)

*I'm waiting, pass this way once more, Inayat Shah, my eyes are sore with looking for you at the door.* (translated by Taufiq Rafat)

*Who will lead me to the place of my master, so by his grace he may my heart and body restore.* (translated by Taufiq Rafat)

*not Bulleh, but Inayat must steer.* (translated by Taufiq Rafat)

*Only the proper guide can lead you to His side; and unless you choose Inayat, you will lose.* (translated by Taufiq Rafat)

Elsewhere the guide goes unnamed: "then help there will be none till you call on Bulleh's guide." In the end the master is the lord who shows himself:

*As Shah Inayat, you show yourself to Bullha all the time.* (translated by Christopher Shackle)

*See how my lord Shah Inayat flirts with me.* (translated by Christopher Shackle)

*Bullha, he lets himself be found. Shah Inayat is discovered.* (translated by Christopher Shackle)

One poem also plays on his name: *Inayat* means grace or favour in Arabic. He is the one addressed in a lyric as "Oh you with a great family", *vaḍ parvāriā*, and another line is read as telling how the divine light entered Shah Inayat.

## The Shrine

Accounts of the year of his death differ; the year the shrine keeps is around 1728. Shah Inayat is buried at Mozang alongside two of his sons, Zamanaullah, a Sufi poet who left no descendants, and Zahidullah, through whom the family line continued. A daughter is buried alongside him as well. In the 1870s his *mazar*, the tomb of a saint, was spoken of as radiant, and his descendants were still living in Lahore.

The administration of the shrine and its adjoining mosque has passed from the family's own descendants to the Auqaf Department, the government department of religious endowments.

## Devotion and Festivals

The *ʿurs* is observed from the 25th to the 27th of Jamadi al-Thani and draws thousands of devotees for nights of *zikr*, the remembrance of God, and *qawwali*, the devotional music of the Sufis; some 40,000 to 60,000 people come during the festival. The five daily prayers are said at the attached mosque, and a corner of the shrine is set aside for lighting oil lamps. Every Thursday there is a Mehfil-e-Milad, a gathering in celebration of the Prophet's birth, with *langar*, the free communal kitchen. Some 300 to 500 visitors come each day, rising to 1,500 to 3,000 on Thursday nights.

His memory is also sung far from his own tomb. Of six compositions that one party of qawwals, the singers of *qawwali*, sang at the shrine of Bulleh Shah, five were narratives of the stages of Bulleh Shah's spiritual growth as a mystic and of his relationship with Shah Inayat, singing of the disciple, the *murid*, and the mentor, the *murshid*, through female and male representations. A song that Bulleh Shah composed for his master, *Merā pīyā ghar āyā o lālnī*, "Celebrate thou, for my beloved has come home", is also sung at weddings.

## Legacy

Shah Inayat is the key to his disciple's poetry. As one translator of Bulleh Shah has put it, he is the most important focus of the poet's love, "now as the present source of his joy, now as the absent cause of his pain, now as the master who is mysteriously both different from and identical to his adoring disciple." The signature verse of a *kafi*, the verse that carries the poet's name, often proclaims Bulleh Shah's powerful identification with his master, who is frequently alluded to as "the lord", and the poems tell of the love between the human and the divine as lived out between the poet and his lord. Behind that bond stands the Sufi pattern in which spiritual disciplines passed within the circle of disciples formed around a master, while poetry carried the Sufi message to a wider audience.

His own life has its literature in Urdu. A life of Shah Inayat was being completed in 1974, and Muhammad Iqbal Mujaddidi prepared a *Hayat-e Shah Inayat Qadri*. His titles, and the thousands who come for his *ʿurs*, show a saint remembered in his own right as well as through the verse of his disciple.
