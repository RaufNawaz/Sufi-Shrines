## Overview

The shrine of Hafiz Muhammad Jamal Multani, near the Aam Khas Bagh outside the historic Daulat Gate, is among the most historically important Sufi tombs of Multan, the "city of saints". Hafiz Jamal, a Sufi, or Muslim mystic, and a renowned teacher and poet, is traditionally credited with establishing the Chishti order, a Sufi order prominent in South Asia, firmly in Multan. His tomb, a fine work of late Mughal architecture, still preserves original wall paintings of the Mughal era, and it remains an active centre of devotion, with an annual *ʿurs*, the yearly commemoration of the saint's death.

Born at Multan in the eighteenth century, Hafiz Jamal was a disciple of the Chishti master Khwaja Noor Muhammad Maharvi, and wrote in Arabic, Persian and Siraiki. His tomb belongs to the dense sacred geography of Multan, alongside the great shrines of Bahauddin Zakariya, Shah Rukn-e-Alam and Shah Shams Sabzwari.

## The Saint

Hafiz Muhammad Jamal Multani was born at Multan around 1747, the year 1160 in the Hijri calendar, the Islamic calendar reckoned from the Prophet's migration to Medina. He became a disciple of Khwaja Noor Muhammad Maharvi, a leading Chishti master of the region, and the third of the master's four chief *khalifas*, the deputies a Sufi master appoints to carry on his line, who were called *sahib-e qurb*, those of nearness. With his contemporary Khwaja Sulaiman Taunsvi, he is counted among Noor Muhammad's successors.

Hafiz Jamal became a renowned teacher and a poet in his own right. His sayings, his *malfuzat*, the collected sayings of a Sufi master, were gathered in three collections: the *Gulzar-e Jamaliya* by Allama Abd al-Aziz Parharvi, the *Anwar-e Jamaliya* by Ghulam Hasan, and the *Asrar al-Kamaliya* by Zahid Shah.

Hafiz Jamal is said to have taken part in the resistance to Sikh attacks on Multan, and his contemporary Sulaiman Taunsvi urged his own followers to take part in the resistance against Sikh aggression in the Punjab. Hafiz Jamal died in 1811, the Hijri year 1226, and his shrine is in his native city.

## The Chishti Order in Multan

Hafiz Jamal is remembered above all as the master who established the Chishti order firmly in Multan. In the city, it is held, the Chishti *silsila*, the spiritual lineage handed down from master to disciple, flourished thanks to him. The wider spread of its Nizamiya Fakhriya line through the Punjab is credited to his contemporary Khwaja Sulaiman Taunsvi.

The generation of Noor Muhammad's successors belongs to a longer story. The Nizami Chishtis came to prominence in the eighteenth century, principally in Delhi, the Deccan and the Punjab, through masters who were related to, but in tension with, local political leaders. The spread of the order in Hind, that is India, and in the Punjab is traced to Khwaja Mu'in al-Din Hasan Sijzi.

The order had touched Multan long before. When the Chishti master Qutb ad-Din visited Multan, a strategic commercial centre of the Punjab, Farid ad-Din pledged allegiance to him there. It was also in Multan that Qutbuddin Bakhtiyar Kaki, coming from Baghdad, met Shaikh Bahauddin Zakariya.

## The Shrine and Its Devotion

The tomb stands near the Aam Khas Bagh, outside the historic Daulat Gate of Multan. It is a fine piece of late Mughal funerary architecture: a square chamber rising to a drum of twelve sides and a hemispherical dome. It is especially valued because it preserves original wall paintings of the Mughal era, a rarity, since the murals of most such monuments have been lost to whitewash and renovation.

The shrine remains an active centre of devotion. Visitors come to it continually in search of blessing, and every year it keeps the saint's *ʿurs*.

Some performers of Sufi music sing around shrines in Multan, as in Sahiwal, to earn a living, and in an earlier age a *qawwal*, a singer of *qawwali*, the devotional music of the Sufis, named Abu Bakr came from Multan to visit the teacher of Nizam ad-Din.

## Poetry

Hafiz Jamal composed in Arabic, Persian and Siraiki, the tongue of the Siraiki belt around Multan, Bahawalpur, Mianwali and northern Sind. He is remembered above all for his *Siharfi*, an acrostic poem of the Punjabi and Siraiki tradition, whose verses open with the letters of the alphabet in turn.

His own age falls within what is counted the golden age of Muslim Punjabi poetry, the late seventeenth and the eighteenth century, when the province was invaded one time after another by the Mahrattas, by the Sikhs and, in between, by Muslim armies. Popular mystical poetry in the Punjab may be older still, and may have emerged as early as the days of Fariduddin Shakarganj.

For the mystical poets who wrote in the vernacular languages, the holy cities lie within; as one scholar of mystical poetry put it, "Mecca and Medina (and for the Sindhi and Panjabi, also Multan) are inside the heart". A poem of Sachal speaks of Shams-i Tabriz, the spiritual beloved of Maulana Rumi, who, Indian tradition holds, was flayed alive by the mullahs in Multan, where his shrine is still venerated. The Persian poet Fakhruddin 'Iraqi attached himself in Multan to Bahauddin Zakariya, and the tender and intoxicating love poems he composed are still sung by Pakistani musicians at the door of his master's tomb in the city.

Multan appears once in the Punjabi *kafis*, the short sung poems, of Madho Lal Hussein, as the source of a bride's finery:

*The dress / someone carried across the snows / all the way from Kashmir. The dress / someone brought from Gujarat… The dress bought in Multan* (translated by Naveed Alam)

The saints of the Punjab are the subject of the *Hadiqat al-Awliya*, a Persian book completed in 1875, whose modern edition names Hafiz Jamal among Noor Muhammad's *khalifas*. It closes with a chronogram by the author's son, Mufti Ghulam Safdar, a verse whose letters encode the year of the book's completion, 1292 AH:

جو خدا کے دوست تھے پنجاب میں
حال ان کا اس سے ہوتا ہے عیان

*A chronogram on the saints of the Punjab, whose lives the book sets down.*

## The Setting

By the middle of the eleventh century, Sufism had reached the lands around Multan. The city is the resting place of Bahauddin Zakariya, the great saint of the Suhrawardi order, the Sufi order founded by Shihab al-Din Suhrawardi, and many Muslim clans of the Punjab claim to have been converted either by him or by his contemporary Baba Farid. In the days of Guru Nanak, Multan was one of the biggest cities of the Punjab, known for the shrines of Muslim saints and for its Jain and Hindu temples, a sacred space for people of all religious denominations; by the time Nanak came there, the city was famous as a major centre of Sufi learning and philosophy. In the reign of the emperor Aurangzeb, many saints lay at rest in that fortunate city. A sultan who came to Multan once made the round of the tombs of its shaikhs, its spiritual masters.

The Sikh attacks on Multan, in the resistance to which Hafiz Jamal is said to have taken part, belong to a longer conflict over the city. In the Sikh period, uprisings led by Muslim leaders began in Kasur and Multan, and the rebellion was given a religious colour, against the "infidel" Ranjit Singh. Kharak Singh was sent with an army to conquer Multan, and the city was taken. When Ranjit Singh conquered Multan, which had been ruled by Nawabs, Muslim princes, Nawab Muzaffar Khan was killed, and members of his house were taken with their families to Lahore, where they afterwards lived. The notables of the Punjab suffered under the Sikhs, and the tombs of its shaikhs fell into ruin.

## Legacy

Hafiz Muhammad Jamal Multani is remembered as the master through whom the Chishti order took firm root in Multan, and, with Sulaiman Taunsvi, as one of the successors of Khwaja Noor Muhammad Maharvi. His sayings are kept in the *Gulzar-e Jamaliya*, the *Anwar-e Jamaliya* and the *Asrar al-Kamaliya*. His tomb, with its rare surviving wall paintings of the Mughal era, remains an active centre of devotion and part of the dense sacred geography of Multan, alongside the great shrines of Bahauddin Zakariya, Shah Rukn-e-Alam and Shah Shams Sabzwari.
