## Overview

Gurdwara Dash Mesh Pita is one of the small number of Sikh institutions that survive in Sukkur, the city on the Indus in northern Sindh. Pakistan's Evacuee Trust Property Board lists it as a functioning gurdwara, a Sikh place of worship, on a plot of some 300 square yards. Its name honours Guru Gobind Singh, the tenth Sikh Guru: Dashmesh Pita is a title of reverence for him, meaning roughly "father of the Tenth [House]".

It is a separate place from Sukkur's two much better documented gurdwaras, Sadhu Bela, on its island in the Indus, and Gurdwara Pehli Patshahi, or Jind Pir, near the bridge between Sukkur and Rohri, both with detailed histories tied to the travels of Guru Nanak, the first Guru. Of Dash Mesh Pita's own past little is known, but much is told of the Guru whose name it bears, and of Sukkur and the followers of Nanak in Sindh.

## The Gurdwara

When the gurdwara was built, who established it and what event or visit it commemorates are not known. Its name suggests some historical or devotional tie to the tenth Guru, but nothing more specific can be said. As with several of the smallest gurdwaras of Sindh, what is certain is what the Evacuee Trust Property Board's register gives: its name, its place in Sukkur and that it is in use. Its history, and the size and character of its congregation today, await a local or community account.

It should not be confused with the Dashmesh Darbar Singh Sabha, a separate gurdwara at Jacobabad, roughly 100 kilometres away in another district, though the likeness of the two names may have caused confusion in the past.

## The Guru

The life of Guru Gobind Singh is the history behind the gurdwara's name, not the history of the gurdwara itself. The tenth, the number in the gurdwara's name, runs through the names he is given: the tenth Sikh Guru, the tenth Guru Gobind Singh Sahib, and the last of the Gurus, who stands at the end of the line as the ninth incarnation of Guru Nanak. He was referred to as Sacha Padshah, the True King, and his court as Sacha Darbar, the True Court, and a book of his bears the name Daswan Padshah ka Granth, the book of the Tenth King.

He was born at Patna in 1666, the son of Guru Tegh Bahadur. Before heading to Delhi, his father appointed him his heir, and it was the young Gobind Singh who urged his father to aid the Pandits, the learned Brahmins. Tegh Bahadur was killed by the emperor Aurangzeb, and in 1675, at the age of nine, Gobind Singh became Guru. When Jaita recovered Tegh Bahadur's body, he brought it to Guru Gobind Singh at Anandpur, a city his father had founded, and the Guru cremated it.

After his father's death the gravest threat came from his uncle Ram Rai, the disgruntled son of Guru Har Rai. Ram Rai approached the emperor Aurangzeb, and the chiefs of the Punjab surrounded the Guru's abode at Anandpur.

The Guru kept a royal court. He lived in a regal tent and kept a war drum, and he commanded a standing army whose weapons came from the offerings of devotees or from raids upon neighbouring villages. The offering of weapons at the Guru's court had been started by Guru Hargobind and was reinforced by Gobind Singh, and Hargobind's sword passed to him; he can be seen as the true descendant of Guru Hargobind. He loved war epics, hunted regularly and took an active part in military exercises, and he was adept in Persian, Arabic and Sanskrit. Much more inclined to arms than his father, he is known as a warrior and a poet.

Bhai Mani Singh and Gulab Singh were in attendance on him. Bhai Mani Singh, a Sikh scholar and martyr of the eighteenth century to whom one set of the janamsakhis, the tales of Guru Nanak's life, is supposedly attributed, had been his childhood companion.

## The Khalsa and the Granth

On the first of Vaisakh, 14 April 1699, the occasion of the spring festival of Baisakhi, thousands of Sikhs gathered at Anandpur Sahib, in East Punjab, India. The Guru asked for volunteers ready to give their lives, and five offered their heads. He led each of them away and came back each time with a blood soaked sword, but in reality all were safe, and the blood was a goat's. The five became the Panj Pyare, the five beloved ones, the first of the Khalsa, the Pure Ones.

The Khalsa gave the Sikh community its distinct attire and identity. The Guru made compulsory the five Ks, the five articles of faith whose names begin with the letter K: *kesh*, uncut hair; *kanga*, a comb; *kara*, a bangle; *kachera*, short breeches; and the *kirpan*, a sword, which he ordered his followers to keep with them at all times. He gave the men the surname Singh, lion, and the women Kaur, lioness, to dilute caste identities. The Khalsa order had a code of conduct, and only the official institution embodied by the Guru was held able to provide salvation, through following that code. Under him the Sikhs became a militant body, and they have a religious prohibition against shaving and smoking.

With the Khalsa came the end of the *masands*, the Gurus' agents among the congregations. The Guru abolished the Masand system when he established the Khalsa, because of the corruption that had seeped into it, and devout Sikhs believe that he thereby purged Sikhism of corruption. The *daswandh*, the offering of a tenth of one's earnings, on the other hand, was carried into the Khalsa.

He is credited with completing the compilation of the Guru Granth Sahib, the Sikh scripture, and he bowed before the book. Sikh tradition holds that he ended the line of the Gurus by declaring the Guru Granth Sahib the eternal Guru: no person would succeed him, he instructed that the Granth Sahib be revered as the Guru, and after him the book became the living Guru for eternity. Accounts differ on when he made the declaration; it is placed between the founding of the Khalsa in 1699 and 1708. After compiling the final version, he arranged the first *Akhand Path*, the unbroken reading of the whole scripture, in which five readers read it to him while he stood in one place throughout.

In the nineteenth century a *granth*, a sacred book, of the tenth Padshah lay on a *takhtposh*, a wooden platform, at the Shahid Ganj *darbar*, or shrine, and another *granth* was kept there too. The Guru is also the author of the book known as the Dasant Granth, which the ascetics called the Nanghe Sadhus came to worship in place of the Guru Granth Sahib, refusing to acknowledge its supremacy as the eternal Guru.

The warrior was a poet too. In his *Vichitr Natak*, a portion of the Daswan Padshah ka Granth, he told how the Sodhis replaced the Bedis. The tale names Sodhi Rai, the Bedis at Kashi and the king of Madar Des, and in it the Bedis vowed to return the compliment in Kaliyuga, the last of the four ages of the world, when their descendant Nanak would raise the Sodhis to a prime position in the world.

## War and Loss

Guru Gobind Singh was a warrior who challenged the mighty Mughal Empire and set up a Sikh army. On Lohri, the Punjabi winter festival, the Sikhs under his command defeated a large Mughal army in a battle fought at Muktsar, in India, and he founded the Nihang order as a fighting body of the Khalsa.

He had four sons, among them Jujhar Singh and Fateh Singh, and he saw all of them die at the hands of the Mughals in his own lifetime, in 1705; accounts of their deaths differ. A warning of Amar Das is said to refer to the martyrdom of these grandsons of Tegh Bahadur.

Guru Gobind Singh died in 1708. Accounts of his death differ; it is said that he was assassinated by a Pathan, or Pashtun, horse trader. In one religious and historical reading, he and his father are cast together as victims of Aurangzeb.

## How the Guru Is Remembered

On his birthday his devotees pay tribute to him by enacting scenes of war exercises. Sports have always been an essential feature of the day, traditionally war games, with horse riding, swordsmanship, archery, spearing and the use of the stick, because the Guru was a warrior himself. When the birthday was celebrated at Nankana Sahib in January 2011, the songs and recitations were dedicated to his bravery, with an emphasis on the valour of the Singh, and all the recitations of that night to the Guru and the significance of the Khalsa. The birthday falls only a few days before Lohri. As one writer on Pakistan's religious minorities has observed, the Guru's birthday and Lohri "had no historical significance to particular shrines on this side of the border".

His image recurs elsewhere. On the banner at Janamasthan he is a young, good looking man in a silk gown and several pearl necklaces, with a turban like a tiara adorned with jewels, an eagle resting on his right hand and a quiver full of arrows on his shoulder. The dome at Jhaman shows him in battle, and again with his sons Jujhar Singh and Fateh Singh. Pictures of him hang on the wall of Rajesh's gurdwara at Manora, and two in the *kirtan* hall, the hall of devotional singing, at Panja Sahib, one of them showing him collecting money for *langar*, the free communal kitchen.

## Sukkur and Sindh

The history of Sukkur and of Sindh is the setting of the gurdwara rather than its own story. Sukkur stands at one corner of the triangle formed by Rohri, Sukkur and Shikarpur, and within the city, on an island in the Indus, lies Sadhu Bela, a *tirtha*, or Hindu place of pilgrimage. In the janamsakhis a journey runs from Multan to Sukkur, and from there south along the Indus.

Sukkur lay on the road of the armies that pursued Dara. News reached them that Dara, having passed Bhakkar, had halted at Sukkur on the twenty fifth of Muharram, the first month of the Islamic year. Shaikh Mir, Diler Khan and Qubad Khan crossed towards Sukkur; because of the thick jungle, the crowding of thorny trees and the hard stages of the road, many beasts perished and the baggage did not arrive, and on the sixth of Safar, the second month, they reached Sukkur. Shaikh Mir stayed there a day, and the *zamindars*, the landholders, told him that twenty five *kos*, an old measure of distance, from Sukkur a road branches off towards Kandahar. Saf Shikan Khan made an appointment to the *faujdari*, the military command, of Sukkur.

At the time of Nadir Shah's invasion of Hindustan and his sack of Delhi, Upper Sindh formed part of the *subah*, or province, of Multan, and its "Moghulee", or Mughal, portion, Sukkur, Bukkar and Shikarpur, was then annexed to the Durani kingdom. Towards the second half of the eighteenth century Sukkur rose, with Karachi, Hyderabad and Shikarpur, to be one of the four large towns of modern Sindh, displacing older towns, among them Tatta, Nasarpur, Rohri, Sehwan and Kandiaro. Unlike Sehwan, where no communal violence had ever been registered, Sukkur saw such violence following the Manzilgah riots. Between December 1939 and February 1940 the city was the scene of the Sukkur riots, whose first victim was Bhagat Kanwar Ram.

Guru Nanak himself came to Sindh. He visited Uch Sharif on his way there, towards the end of his travels, after he had already visited the East, South, North and West, and the temple at Clifton has been identified as the temple he visited on his tour of Sindh. In one summary of the many religions that had flourished in Sindh, such as Buddhism, Jainism and Hinduism, two others were said to have become dominant more recently: Sikhism and Sufism. Colonial rule brought a renewed religiosity among the Sindhis, whether Muslim, Sikh or Hindu, performed in the *darbar*, a word used by Sikhs, Muslims and Hindus alike. Sindh's figures are missing from the first census, of 1872; in one count of the province, Sindh had 1,718,688 Muslims, 393,092 Hindus and 5,034 Christians, while 86,663 persons were put in a category titled "Other castes such as Sikhs, Parsis, Jews, etc."

Among the Lohanas and related mercantile groups two main cults were followed, the Nanakpanth, the path of Nanak, and the Daryapanth, the path of the river. In the nineteenth century the Nanakpanth was probably the dominant religious path among the Hindus of Sindh. In Sindh many Nanakpanthis belonged to a subgroup known as the Udasis, an order of ascetics. As the religious life of Sindh took new shape, several important Nanakpanthi *darbars* were created and developed throughout the province. Some had been created in the late eighteenth century, and in the second half of the nineteenth century there was a renaissance of them, with many new *darbars* built all over Sindh. Because the Nanakpanth was so widespread, the Hindu Sufis of Sindh, Hindus who followed the Sufi path, were very familiar with Sikh terminology. Before Partition the temples devoted to Jhulelal in Sindh were built with a dome, as were some Sikh *samadhis*, or memorial shrines.

There is no settled view of whether Sindh's followers of Nanak are to be counted as Sikhs. Many Lohanas in Sindh follow the teachings of Nanak, which somewhat obscured their religious affiliation for the colonial officers, and in the census of 1881 the Lohanas of Shikarpur and Hyderabad were counted as Sikhs, while ten years later, in that of 1891, they were classified as Hindus. Many middle and upper class Hindus in Sindh follow the Nanak *panth*, or sect, and it is common to cover one's head before walking into a *tikana*, the temple containing the Guru Granth Sahib. The Nanak Panthis belong to South Punjab and Sindh, and they are found in very large numbers in the province of Sindh; in their temples portraits of Guru Nanak hang alongside Hindu deities. They do not believe in the Khalsa. The communities who refused the Khalsa and called themselves followers of Guru Nanak still exist in Sindh and in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa; baptised Sikhs look down upon them, and at Manora they were seen merging into the larger groups of Sikhs.

## Legacy

Guru Gobind Singh is seen as having completed Nanak's mission. He codified Nanak's teachings and founded the Khalsa, completing the legacy that Guru Hargobind had begun, and by the time of the tenth Guru the movement had taken an institutional form, codified by the formation of the Khalsa. He concluded that both Hinduism and Islam were flawed religious traditions, and that any follower of Nanak who refused to obey the commandments of the later Gurus was not a Sikh.

The Khalsa he founded came to define Sikh identity in law: the Gurdwara Act of 1925 defined that identity, identifying it with the Khalsa of Guru Gobind Singh. He founded the Damdami Taksal, a Sikh religious organisation, to impart religious education. In 1746, twelve years before the death of Bulleh Shah, the son of Zakaria Khan carried out the general massacre of the Sikhs at Shaheed Ganj in Lahore and perpetuated the resistance begun under the Guru. There is a view that Ranjit was a true follower of Guru Gobind Singh, and Bhindranwale passionately claimed that the new generation of Sikhs did not respect the Sikh way of life as taught by the Guru.
