## Consensus choices

- **Where Jhok lies.** Overview: "in the Sujawal region of lower Sindh", basis (a), the site's account (treated as basis (a) as on the mithankot row); The Shrine: "a village in the far south of Sindh" (Boivin's "extreme South", which agrees with the site's "lower Sindh"), with one clause, "though accounts describe its exact location in different terms". Taluqa Mirpur Bathoro and "not far from Hyderabad" are not given: set beside Sujawal they would be side by side variants, and writing "Taluqa Mirpur Bathoro, in the Sujawal region" would join two descriptions the team version does not join.
- **Who came to him.** Not a choice between accounts: the site's "the poor and the landless gathered around him" is kept, and Schimmel's two lists (adepts from the mystical centres and labourers from the big farms; Sufi adepts and slaves and husbandmen from the villages) are given in one sentence. The differences are complementary and treated as minor (rule 4, third bullet); Schimmel's "according to the sources" hedge on the slaves and husbandmen is dropped as source talk. V1 to rule (judgement call 2).
- **The land.** Basis (a): the commune, framed "is remembered as one that held and cultivated its land collectively", with one clause that keeps Schimmel's caution without her name: "Accounts of the commune differ, and how far he really aimed at a land reform in the modern sense of the word cannot be known; much that is told in his favour is strongly mixed with legend." The land distributed among his followers after the attack is kept as an event in the narrative, not as a rival account of the commune (judgement call 3). Schimmel's remark on progressive writers reading him in the light of modern ideals is in Legacy.
- **How the end came.** The common ground is told plainly: denunciation at court, the charge of conspiracy against the Mughal throne, large armies from Sind and Hind, the Kalhora particularly active, the siege of the *khanqah*, four months, execution by the Mughal governor in January 1718. The manner of the end: basis (a), "taken by treachery after that long resistance", with one clause, "Accounts of how the siege ended differ". The surrender ("had to surrender to the overwhelming army"; "surrendered after four months' siege") is the variant and is not given; "Jhok held out for four months" keeps the length of the siege without it (judgement call 4).
- **The date.** January 1718, basis (b) (two of Schimmel's books; the third has "early 1718", Shackle "d. 1718", the site 1718). No "accounts differ" clause, since the year agrees throughout. The persecution's start in 1717 is kept as a fact of the story, not a rival date.
- **The holder of the seat.** Minor. Both men are named as holders of the seat (Sufi Abd al-Sattar Shah, who initiated a disciple; Sufi Ataullah Sattari), with "several of the Sufi masters at Jhok have borne the name Abdul Sattar"; no order, no dates. Sufi Hazrat Abdul Sattar Shah's "cup of unity" stands under his own name and is not identified with Abd al-Sattar Shah.
- **The Bijapur dargah.** The website's claim is given as a claim ("Jhok's official website claims a dargah at Bijapur, that of Hazrat Soofi Abdul Malik, as affiliated with Jhok"). The absence of that affiliation in Molchand's biography, and Boivin's "I do not know what the relations were between Molchand and the sajjada nashin of Jhok", are dropped as statements of absence (minor).
- **Boivin's article on Jhok.** Minor: only the subject and the year 2012 are given (both citations agree on them); the place (New York or London) and the pages (94 to 109 or 95 to 110) are dropped.
- **When Sind became Mughal.** 1592 (Sorley, under Akbar) against 1595 (Schimmel). Basis (d), given as a range, "Akbar had added it to the Mughal Empire in the 1590s"; Akbar is named because Schimmel's account names no emperor and does not contradict him. Alternative if V1 prefers the brief's literal form: "between 1592 and 1595" (judgement call 8).

## Left out

- **Source apparatus:** every citation, page and folio; the Overview's second paragraph as narration (its content is kept: the famous Sindhi Sufi martyr, Persian verse in the *Risalo*, the dargah as a centre of initiation, custodianship and song, most Hindu Sufis in touch with Jhok); the paragraph on how each book writes his name (kept: Shah Inayat of Jhok, of Jhok Sharif, Shahid; dropped: "Sufi Shah Inayat", Abbas's "Shah Inayat of Jhokevale" and the team writer's own rendering of it); every "Boivin writes", "Schimmel records", "in her telling"; the description of Schimmel's article and Boivin's chapter as cited works (kept in Legacy as scholarship on him, judgement call 13).
- **Statements of absence:** "Of his own spiritual descent the books say little"; no birth or death date and no chain for him; "none of them describes the tomb, its building or its date"; no dates for the holders of the seat; no date, month or length for the *ʿurs*; whether the 17th to 19th of Muharram at Jhok are the *ʿurs*; the unnamed governor; who the Sayyids were; the master of Rakhiyyal Shah and of Nasir Muhammad Faqir unnamed; the "he" of "he decided to look for his grave" (the public text uses the passive, "a search was made", so it names no one); that "neither book refers to the other" over the English line.
- **The four sacred days of one Hindu Sufi path** (the 11th, 15th, 17th and 27th): not matched to the four masters in the team version; given without the caution, a reader would pair one with Shah Inayat. The path's keeping of his *versi* is kept.
- **Flagged readings (rule 5):** "the Sayyids of Bulrri" ("printed so"; the team notes keep it apart from Bulri): the attack is "led by a group of Sayyids", without the place. The second Persian line, *sar dar qadam-e yār fdā shud chi ba-jā shud* ("printed so, with *fdā* for *fidā*"): its words are left out and only "a second Persian verse in the *Risalo* is also his" is kept. Schimmel's locator "Sohn. II".
- **Hedged identifications:** Boivin's "'three great Sufi teachers'", "Shah Inayat, Shah Abd al-Latif and Sachal Sarmast" ("The name stands there without a place, and the company it keeps is the only sign that this Shah Inayat is meant"), and Kasmani's bare "Shah Inayat" among the saints of Sindh featured in the state's Sindhi publications until the late 1960s, whose annual fairs were marked by special editions ("only the phrase 'saints of Sindh' and his companions in the list point to the saint of Jhok"). Left out, as the mithankot row left out its uncertain identification; giving them plainly would claim more certainty than the team version allows (judgement call 9).
- **Minor:** "Jhok, contrary to Daraza, is located in Southern Sindh"; the 1972 work of Nasir Faqir in which Abdul Sattar Shah's words appear (a citation); the *Liber Amicorum* name and the article's pages; Schimmel's "Shah Inayat of Jhok (exec. 1718)" and Shackle's "the martyred" (folded into the narrative); the Persian qualifier "much older" kept, "Hallajian succession" given as "the succession of Hallaj".
- **Reworded only:** no dash or hyphen of our own: the site's dashes become a colon or a comma; "1623–1712" becomes "1623 to 1712"; "Mughal-appointed authorities" becomes "the authorities the Mughals had appointed in the region"; "half-verse" becomes "half of a verse"; "martyr-mystic" is not used; "eighteenth century Sindh" unhyphenated. "Sind" is written Sindh in prose; it stays Sind inside the quoted epithet 'the Hallaj of Sind'. "Mogul", "Moghul" are written Mughal; "Moharram" Muharram. Transliterations that carry a hyphen (*wahdat al-wujud*, al-Qadiri, al-Jilani, Abd al-Sattar, *Maqalat ash-shuʿara*, *ba-dih*, Shihab al-Din, Baha-ud-Din, Bishr al-Hafi) keep it.

## Glosses

From `data/glossary.csv`: *wahdat al-wujud* (the mysticism of the unity of being, the team version's own wording); *khanqah* (a Sufi lodge where disciples gather); *dargah* (the shrine complex); *ʿurs* (the yearly commemoration of the saint's death); the Qadiri order (founded by Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani; the glossary's Gilani, spelled as the team version spells him, which also calls him the founder of the Qadiriyya); the Suhrawardi order (founded by Shihab al-Din Suhrawardi); the Naqshbandi order (founded by Baha-ud-Din Naqshband); Sayyids (descendants of the Prophet Muhammad); *pirs* (the spiritual guides); *murid* (the disciple); *murshid* (the spiritual master).

Team version's own: *zamindars* (the great landholders); *sajjada nashins* (the holders of the seat); *nam* (the initiatory name); *versi* (death anniversaries); *capṛi* (castanets); *Shaheed* (the martyr); *jeko kheré, so khaé* ("whoever sows, eats"); *jam wahdat jo* ("the cup of unity").

Plain dictionary senses: Sufi (Muslim mystic); darbar (shrine); *kafis* (short mystical lyrics, the mithankot gloss; the team version's "verses (kafis)"); *kalam* (poetry); *melo* (fair); *ghazals* (lyric poems); Hind (India).

## Judgement calls for V1

1. **Location:** "in the Sujawal region of lower Sindh" (Overview) and "a village in the far south of Sindh ... though accounts describe its exact location in different terms" (The Shrine). Taluqa Mirpur Bathoro and "not far from Hyderabad" dropped as the variants.
2. **Followers in one sentence:** "His way of life, his spirituality and his justice attracted not only Sufi adepts, many of them from the mystical centres of the country, but also labourers, slaves and husbandmen from the neighbouring big farms and villages." Joins Schimmel's two lists; preceded by the site's "The poor and the landless gathered around him." Is this a merge of two accounts?
3. **Land distribution beside the commune:** "After an unjustified attack on Jhok led by a group of Sayyids, descendants of the Prophet Muhammad, a considerable amount of land was distributed among his followers." Kept as an event, with the place name Bulrri left out. If V1 holds it is the side by side variant of "The land", it should be removed.
4. **The end of the siege:** "Jhok held out for four months. Accounts of how the siege ended differ; he is remembered as having been taken by treachery after that long resistance." "held out for four months" for "surrendered after four months' siege".
5. **"He was executed by the Mughal governor in January 1718"** joins "executed in January 1718" (two books) and "executed by the Moghul governor in early 1718" (a third).
6. **"His adversaries denounced him at court and accused him of conspiracy against the Mughal throne. It was the easiest method of blackmail ..."** joins the two Schimmel tellings (denunciation at court; accusation of conspiracy). "In 1717 he was persecuted under the pretext that he was trying to overthrow the established rule, and this was the first obvious sign of the struggle for power in the lower Indus valley" restates her "The first obvious sign ... becomes visible in 1717, when a mystical rebel was persecuted".
7. **"Large armies, gathered from various places in Sindh and in Hind, or India, were sent against Jhok"** joins "Large armies were sent to Jhok" with "the overwhelming army that came from various places in Sind and Hind"; "or India" is a dictionary gloss.
8. **"Akbar had added it to the Mughal Empire in the 1590s"** (consensus choices).
9. **The two hedged pointers left out** (Boivin's three great teachers; Kasmani's annual fairs). If V1 counts either as an important omission, the hedge has to come with it, and the team version's own hedge is source talk.
10. **Castanets:** "In the singing of Sindhi poetry, a style performed on the *capṛi*, the castanets, is known as the tradition of Shah Inayat of Jhok; it is not found in the poetry of Shah Abdul Latif, whose moods are more mellow." For "The sūng—the manānā—is done on the capṛi [castanets]—this is the tradition of Shah Inayat of Jhokevale—but we do not find this in Shah Latif's poetry"; the two terms are not explained in the team version and are left out; the speaker is not named.
11. **The English line** follows the sentence on Shah Inayat's two Persian lines in the *Risalo* without saying whether it renders either of them, and "Shah Abdul Latif ... quoted a Persian line usually connected with Shah Inayat" keeps Schimmel's wording. The reader is not told it is the same line; the team version says that is not stated.
12. **"a second Persian verse in the *Risalo* is also his"** for Shackle's "a verse from a poem by the famous Sindhi Sufi martyr Shah Inayat of Jhok"; not "another of his poems", since the team version does not say it is a different poem from the *ghazal*. "a work with very few Persian quotations" rests on "one of the very few Persian quotations in the Risālo".
13. **Legacy names two scholars:** "Annemarie Schimmel devoted to him an article whose title calls him 'A Sindhi Mystic of the early 18th century', published at Leiden in 1969 in a volume in honour of C. J. Bleeker, and a study of the Sufi centre of Jhok Sharif by Michel Boivin was published in 2012." Kept as the scholarship that forms part of his legacy (the mithankot row named Christopher Shackle as translator), not as attribution. The title phrase is given as what the title calls him, since the team version quotes only that part of it. V1 round 1 did not accept this (issue 1: source apparatus); the two sentences on Schimmel and Boivin were deleted, and both works stay in the Bibliography.
14. **One website:** "Jhok Sharif has an official website in his name" (Ataullah Sattari) and "Jhok's official website claims a dargah at Bijapur", treated as the same site, as the team version's Bijapur disagreement item treats them.
15. **"One of them, Sufi Abd al-Sattar Shah"** places him among the masters named Abdul Sattar; "He set the disciple to cleaning the stable of his master's mare" keeps the team version's ambiguous "his Master's".
16. **"The lodge at Jhok belongs to the Qadiri order ... and is tied to it through the holders of its seat"** for "ties the lodge at Jhok to the Qadiri order through its sajjada nashins" and the chart's "Jhok Sharif (Qadiriyya)".
17. **The three seats and their poets:** the two lists are given as the team version gives them ("A great Sufi poet is attached to each of the three: Sachal Sarmast, Qutub Ali Shah and Nasir Faqir"), not paired, followed by "Nasir Muhammad Faqir, a Qadiri, was the disciple of the *murshid* ... of Jhok Sharif".
18. **"a search was made for the grave of Shah Inayat's master at Bijapur"**: passive, because the team version does not name who decided to look for it.
19. **"most of the Hindu Sufis"** and "very popular among the Hindus", without "of Sindh", as the Sachal Sarmast row kept it.
20. **Overview:** "Shah Inayat of Jhok is the famous Sindhi Sufi martyr" (Shackle's "the famous Sindhi Sufi martyr"); "the great classic of Sindhi poetry" for "that great Sindhi classic"; "lines of it are quoted" for the two lines traced to him.
21. **"The poetry of one disciple's own path tells that ..."** frames the vision as the path's own telling ("as the published kalam of his own path relates"); "al-Jilani told him" names the speaker so "the saint" is not read as Shah Inayat.

24. **"Jhok stands at the heart of a network that outlived Partition, for the invocation of Jhok Sharif contributes to the construction of a network of sacred territories which are all located in Sindh."** The team version's own sentence joined to Boivin's words, unquoted.
