## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)
- Abu 'Ali ibn Sina (Avicenna): Qabus marries the love-sick young prince to his cousin on Abu 'Ali's diagnosis. Abu 'Ali "went to Ray, and finally became minister to the Shahinshah 'Ala'u'd-Dawla" (p. 108, folio 90). He was prime minister to 'Ala'u'd-Dawla Muhammad ibn Dushmanziyar: "since Alexander the Great, whose minister was Aristotle, no King had had such a minister as Abu 'Ali" (Abu Kalanjar's account, as Nizami relates it) (p. 110, folio 92). He cures the prince who thought he was a cow (pp. 110–111, folios 92–93).
- Avicenna's disciples are named as "Kiya Ra'is Bahmanyar", "Abu Mansur ibn £ila [text-layer?: Zila]", "'Abdu'l-Wahid JuzjanP [text-layer?: Juzjani]", "Sulayman of Damascus" and Abu Kalanjar (p. 110, folio 92). Browne's footnotes give these dates: Bahmanyar "died about A.H. 458 (A.D. loo^ [text-layer?])". Ibn Zila "died in A.H. 440 (A.D. 1048-49)". Juzjani attached himself to Avicenna "in Jurja"<-m [text-layer?: Jurjan] A.H. 403 (A.D. 1012-1013)" for "uY^/ut [text-layer?: about] 25 years" (p. 110, folio 92).
- 'Ali ibnu'l-'Abbas al-Majusi, author of the "Kdmilus-Sind'at [text-layer?: Kamilu's-Sina'at]": physician to 'Adudu'd-Dawla in Pars, in Shiraz. He cures a porter's headache by having him beaten and dragged until his nose bleeds (pp. 108–109, folios 90–91). Browne's footnote: "he died in A.H. 384 (A.D. 994)". 'Adudu'd-Dawla "reigned A.H. 338-372 (A.D. 949-982)" (p. 108, folio 90).
- Abu'l-Hasan ibn Yahya, author of the "Hippocratic Therapeutics" ("Mu'dlaja-i-Buqrdtz [text-layer?]"). Browne's footnote calls him "court physician to the Buyid prince Ruknu'd-Dawla from A.H. 332 (A. D." 943-4 [text-layer?]) onwards" (p. 109, folio 91).
- Adib Isma'il ("Adi'b Isma'fi [text-layer?]") of Herat, "in the reign of Malikshah and during part of the reign of Sanjar". He was a philosopher who lived by his receipts as a physician. He revives a butcher believed dead, having foreseen the butcher's apoplexy (pp. 111–112, folios 93–94).
- Shaykhu'l-Islam 'Abdu'llah Ansari, "this celebrated mystic" in Browne's footnote. Nizami says he "conceived a fanatical hatred" of Khwaja Isma'il and "burned his books" (p. 112, folio 94). The footnote gives these details: "born in A.H. 396 (A.D. 1006) and died in A.H. ^I^ [text-layer?] (A.D., 1089)". He calls himself "Pfr-i-Ansdr, fir-i-Hfri [text-layer?: Pir-i-Ansar, Pir-i-Hiri]". "Though a mystic, he was a fanatical Hanbali." See Note XXXI (p. 112, folio 94).
- Galen cures a notable of Alexandria of pain in the finger-tips by treating the shoulder (Anecdote "Xkl" [text-layer?: XLI]) (p. 113, folio 95).
- Fadl ibn Yahya al-Barmaki and "Paul the Catholicos in Shiraz": Paul traces Fadl's leprosy to his father Yahya's displeasure (pp. 113–114, folios 95–96). Browne's footnote: the anecdote "occurs only in C." (p. 113, folio 95).
- Battle "at the gates of Awba" in "A.H. 547 (=A.D. 1 1 52-3 [text-layer?])" between Sanjar ibn Malikshah and 'Ala'u'd-Dunya wa'd-Din al-Husayn ibnu'l-Husayn. Nizami "wandered about Herat in the guise of a fugitive, because I was connected with the House of Ghur" (p. 114, folio 96).
- House of Ghur / Shansab (Note I). The kings claimed descent from Dahhak. Shansab "is said to have been contemporary with" 'Ali ibn Abu Talib and to have accepted Islam at his hands. The family refused the Umayyad order for "the public cursing in the pulpits of the House of 'Ali". Their independent sway lasted "about 67 years (A.D. 1148-1 2 is [text-layer?])". There were two branches, with capitals at "Firtiz-ktih [text-layer?: Firuz-kuh]" and Bamiyan (p. 119, folio 101).
- 'Ala'u'd-Din Husayn "Jahan-suz" occupied Ghazna and "looted, massacred and burned it for seven days". He "reigned from VH. [text-layer?: A.H.] 545 to 556 (A.D. 1150-1161)" (p. 119, folio 101).
- The author's patrons (Note I and Browne's footnote to the Conclusion): Fakhru'd-Din Mas'ud was "first of the Kings of Bamiyan" (p. 116, folio 98) and "outlived the year 558/1163" (p. 120, folio 102). Shamsu'd-Din Muhammad survived "until the year 586/1190" (p. 120, folio 102). Husamu'd-Din Abu'l-Hasan 'Ali, "our author's patron to whom the Chahar Maqala is dedicated", "must therefore have been living in 5*51-2 [text-layer?]/1156—7"; "further particulars of his life are lacking" (p. 120, folio 102).

## Practices, institutions, economy
- Courtly medicine: diagnosis is by pulse and urine (pp. 108, 112, 113; folios 90, 94, 95). Treatments include:
  - letting a nosebleed run, "three hundred dirhams weight of blood" (p. 109, folio 91)
  - Avicenna's mock-butcher cure (p. 111, folio 93)
  - Ansari's urine sent "under the name of another" (p. 112, folio 94)
  - prescriptions sized in "istar" units; Browne's footnote: "A weight consisting of 4^ [text-layer?] mithqals" (p. 112, folio 94)
- Avicenna's daily routine as minister: before dawn he wrote "a couple of pages of the Shifa", then taught disciples and led prayers. "A thousand mounted men" met him, and that number had reached "two thousand" by the time he arrived at the Government Offices. He then attended the King; "there was no third person between him and the King" (p. 110, folio 92).
- Physicians, patronage and rewards: Qabus "maintained Abu 'Ali… in the best manner possible" (p. 108, folio 90). Fadl "enriched the Catholicos and sent him back to Pars" (p. 114, folio 96). Adib Isma'il "derived his income and livelihood from his receipts as a physician" (p. 111, folio 93).
- Religious hostility to medicine: according to Nizami, Ansari's dislike "arose from religious motives, for the people of Herat believed that he could restore the dead to life, and this belief was injurious to the common people" (p. 112, folio 94).
- The author's own practice (Nizami): he cured a nobleman's daughter by opening "the basilic vein in both her arms", taking "a thousand dirhams' weight of blood". He reports that "most of the ten indications were present" (p. 115, folio 97). "I called her my daughter, and she called me her father" (p. 116, folio 98).
- Dedication and royal titulature: the Conclusion praises Husamu'd-Dawla wa'd-Dunya wa'd-Din Abu'l-Hasan 'Ali ibn Mas'ud, "exterminator of heathens and infidels, subduer of heretics and innovators", and also Fakhru'd-Dawla wa'd-Din, "King of the kings of the Mountains" (p. 116, folio 98).
- Book trade: the Maqamat-i-Hamidi was "lithographed at Kanpiir [text-layer?] (Cawnpore) in 1 268/18^1-2 [text-layer?], and at Tihran in 1290/1873-4" (p. 121, folio 103). Bal'ami's Persian Tabari was "lithographed at Lucknow in 1291/1874 (800 pages)" (p. 122, folio 104).
- Maymandi "caused all official documents to be written in Arabic, not, as had previously been the case, in Persian" (p. 122, folio 104).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)
- Khwaja Isma'il raises a butcher reported dead. Nizami frames it as foresight, not miracle: "men were astonished, because that great man had foreseen that the man would be stricken by apoplexy" (p. 112, folio 94).
- Note I legend: the Ghurid ancestor Shansab "is said" to have received from 'Ali "a standard and recognition of his rule" (p. 119, folio 101).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)
- Nizami (author): "one cannot heal by such methods of treatment save by virtue of extreme excellence, perfect science, and unerring acumen" (p. 111, folio 93).
- Nizami's statement of purpose: "not to flaunt my merits or recall my services, but rather to guide the beginner, and also to glorify my master" (p. 116, folio 98).
- Notes (Mirza Muhammad, as abridged by Browne), Note III: the Maqamat mention fixes the date of composition "between these two limits (A.H. 551-552; A.D. 1156-1157)", since Sultan Sanjar is still living in the text and "died in 552/1157-8" (p. 121, folio 103).
- Notes, Note IV: Anecdote II shows "glaring anachronisms and historical inaccuracies which too often deface the otherwise admirable work of our author". Iskafi could not have served Nuh II. Alptagin conquered "Ghazna (not Herat, as the author erroneously asserts)" (p. 124, folio 106).
- Notes, Note V: in Anecdote III, Makan's rebellion fell under Nasr II, not Nuh II. The general who defeated Makan "was not Tash… but the Amir Abu 'Ali Ahmad ibn Muhtaj" (p. 125, folio 107).
- Note VI: Isma'il ibn 'Abbad was "of Talaqan", not "of Ray". Browne reports his own MSS of al-Mafarrukhi's history of Isfahan and his abridged translation in "t\\ey.fi.A.S.t [text-layer?: the J.R.A.S.] for 1901" (p. 125, folio 107).
- Browne, translator (p. 117, folio 99): Mirza Muhammad's Persian notes are not translated in extenso; the shorter ones become footnotes and the longer ones are redistributed "with considerable rearrangement and condensation". The astrological notes (XXIV and XXXII) are by "Mr Ralph Shirley and Mr W. Gornold".
