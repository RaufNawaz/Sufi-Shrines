Abu Rayhan correctly foretold that a fifth door would be dug in the eastern wall. He was thrown from the palace, landed safely on a net, and showed that he had predicted this in his Almanac. He was then "confined in the citadel of Ghazna, where he remained forrsix [text-layer?] months" (pp. 83–84, folios 65–66). ## Practices, institutions, economy
- Court patronage: the King granted the author the revenue of the Warsad lead-mine for a festival period. Browne's footnote gives this as "from the end of Ramadan until the roth [text-layer?] of Dhu'l-Hijja, a period of two months and ten days" (p. 79, folio 61).
- Browne's footnote reports that Mirza Muhammad "suggests that our author, Nizami, was a Sayyid, or descendant of the Prophet, and that the khums, or fifth part of the profits ... was in this case made over wholly to him". An alternative reading is a net 12,000 maunds after deducting the khums (p. 79, folio 61).
- Royal rewards for astrologers: "a horse caparisoned with gold, a royal robe, a satin turban, a thousand dinars, a boy slave and a handmaiden" for Abu Rayhan (p. 84, folio 66).
- Nizamu'l-Mulk prepared for death. He "confirmed all his pious endowments, gave effect to his bequests, wrote his last testament, set free such of his slaves as had earned his approval, discharged the debts" (p. 88, folio 70; the sentence is column-garbled in the text layer).
- Grave visitation: the author visited 'Umar-i-Khayyam's grave "on the eve of a Friday (seeing that he had the claim of a master on me), taking with me one to point out to me his tomb". The tomb was "at the foot of a garden-wall, over which pear-trees and peach-trees thrust their heads" (p. 89, folio 71).
- A woman astrologer: the author's woman-servant, born "on the 28th of Safar, A.H. 51 12 [text-layer?] (July ist [text-layer?], A.D. 1117)", was taught astrology by the author from age fifteen. "Ladies used to come to her and question her" (p. 85, folio 67). Browne's footnote: the MSS read "512" or "510". Browne also substituted "a woman-servant" for the text's "an old woman" (p. 85, folio 67).
- A Ghazna soothsayer in Isfahan's "Street of the Dome": "women used to visit him, and he used to write them love-charms" (p. 91, folio 73).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)
- 'Umar-i-Khayyam foretold his grave would be "in a spot where the trees will shed their blossoms on me twice a year". The author found the grave covered with fallen flower-leaves "so that his dust was hidden beneath the flowers", and wept (p. 89, folio 71; the quotation is column-garbled in the text layer).
- Al-Kindi read the hidden words "The Rod of Moses" by astrology. He also saw that Abu Ma'shar had "come from the East to slay me" (pp. 82–83, folios 64–65).
- Abu Rayhan foretold the fifth door, and his own fall from a height with a safe landing (pp. 83–84, folios 65–66).
- The woman-servant's prediction that a lost son had returned. She attributed it to "a fly came and settled on the number of the degree of the Ascendant" (p. 86, folio 68).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)
- The author: the astrologer needs "acute mind, approved character, and great natural intelligence, though apparently [some degree of] folly, madness and a gift for soothsaying are amongst the conditions" (p. 81, folio 63). He tells the Da'udi story so "that the King may know that as regards astrological predictions folly and insanity are amongst the conditions of this craft" (p. 87, folio 69).
- The author attributes the woman-servant's success to "the Part of the Unseen" on "the degree of the Ascendant" (p. 86, folio 68).
- The author on 'Umar: "I did not observe that he had any great belief in astrological predictions; nor have I seen or heard of any of the great [scientists] who had such belief" (p. 90, folio 72). Also: "prognostication by the stars, though a recognized art, is no^£ [text-layer?] to be relied on ... whatever the astrologer predicts he must leave to Fate" (p. 90, folio 72).
- Mahmud, as reported by the author: "kings are like little children; in order to receive rewards from them, one should speak in accordance with their views" (p. 84, folio 66). The author: "one of the conditions of the king's service" is "that one must be with him in right or wrong, and speak according to his wish" (p. 85, folio 67).
- The author on religion and service: the King should keep near him one who "is a believer in the Holy Law"; "Whoever does not believe in the religion of God ... in him can no man trust" (pp. 90–91, folios 72–73).
- Browne's corrections of the author: al-Kindi was not a Jew (p. 82, folio 64); Nizamu'l-Mulk was killed at Nihawand, not Baghdad (p. 88, folio 70); Sadaqa never attacked Baghdad (p. 91, folio 73). Browne also confesses that the astrological terms in Anecdote XXVII "are beyond me" (p. 89, folio 71).
