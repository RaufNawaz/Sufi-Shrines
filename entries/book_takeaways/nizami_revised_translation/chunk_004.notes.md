## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)
- Farrukhi (author's anecdote, continued from the previous chunk): recites an elegy and then a rhapsody "on the branding-ground" before the Amir, who offers him as many colts "bred in Khatlan" as he can catch; drunk, he chases them to a ruined rest-house and falls asleep; the colts counted "forty-two in number" and given to him (p. 62, folio 44). He is also given "a horse and equipments", "two tents, three mules, five slaves", then waits on "Sultan Yaminu'd-Dawla" Mahmud, and "twenty servants girt with silver girdles rode behind him" (p. 62, folio 44; p. 63, folio 45). Browne's footnote: Khatlan/Khuttal is "a place in Transoxiana celebrated for its fine horses" (p. 62, folio 44).
- Mu'izzi (Anecdote "XW" [text-layer?] [= XVI?], author's account): in "A.M. [text-layer?] [= A.H.?] 519 (A-.D. 1116-11*7 [text-layer?])" Sanjar son of Malikshah was encamped "in the plain of Turuq" near Tus; the author came from Herat, composed a qasida and went to "Mu'izzi the Poet-laureate" (p. 63, folio 45). Mu'izzi's own story, told to the author: his father "Burhanf" [text-layer?] [= Burhani] died "in the town of Qazwin in the early part of the reign of Malikshah"; Mu'izzi became Malikshah's court-poet but for a year received no salary, ran "a thousand dinars" into debt, because "the Nizamu'1-Mulk ... had no opinion of poetry ... nor did he pay any attention to any one except religious leaders and mystics" (p. 63, folio 45; p. 64, folio 46). Via "Amir 'All ibn Far'amarz 'Ala'u'd-Dawla" he received a hundred dinars, then, for a verse on the new moon of Ramadan, a horse "worth three hundred dinars of Ni'shapur", then a thousand dinars; the King named him "Amir Mu'izzi" after his title "Mu'izzn'd-Dunyd wrfd-Din" [text-layer?]; next day "a thousand dinars as a gift, twelve hundred more a* [text-layer?] allowances" and "a thousand! maunds of corn" (pp. 64–66, folios 46–48).
- Tughanshah ibn Alp Arslan and Azraqi (Anecdote XVII): Tughanshah's companions were poets (Abu Bakr Azraqi, Abu Mansur son of Abu Yusuf, Shuja'i of Nasa, Ahmad Badihi, and others) (p. 66, folio 48). At backgammon he threw two ones instead of two sixes and grew murderous; Azraqi's quatrain calmed him and he placed "five hundred dinars" in Azraqi's mouth; the author heard this from Abu Mansur "at Herat in the year A.H. 509 (A.D. 1115-1116)" (p. 67, folio 49). Browne's footnote: Tughanshah "governed Khurasan from Herat" during Alp Arslan's reign (p. 66, folio 48).
- Mas'ud-i-Sa'd-i-Salman (Anecdote XVIII): in "A.H. 472 (A.D.lO/g-ioSo [text-layer?])" Sultan Ibrahim, suspecting his son Amir Mahmud Sayfu'd-Dawla of going to Malikshah, imprisoned him and his intimates, among them Mas'ud, sent "to Waji'ristanS' [text-layer?] [= Wajiristan] to the Castle of Nay" (p. 67, folio 49; p. 68, folio 50). The author: he "remained imprisoned for twelve years in the days of Sultan Ibrahim" and "for eight years more in the reign of Sultan Mas'ud ibn Ibrahim", released by "Thiqatu'1-Mulk Tahir ibn 'All ibn Mushkan" (p. 69, folio 51). Browne's footnote after Mirza Muhammad: two imprisonments, "the first for ten years, of which seven were spent in Sii and Dahak (between Zaranj and Bust in Sistan), the second for seven or eight years in Maranj in India"; Mas'ud's own verses give "ten (not twelvte [text-layer?]) years" (p. 69, folio 51).
- Wajiristan: Browne's footnote — "Mirza Muhammad (Persian notes ...) at first failed to identify Wajiristan, but now believes it to be identical with the modern Waziristan"; Nay is otherwise known only from the Nuzhatu'l-Qulub "section dealing with Marw-i-Shahjan" (p. 68, folio 50). No archive row; see Doubts.
- Khidr Khan and Rashidi (Anecdote XIX): "Sultan Khidr ibn Ibrahirfc [text-layer?]" ruled "Transoxiana and Turkistan"; "seven hundred maces of gold and silver" carried before him; poets in his service incl. Amir 'Am'aq (Poet-Laureate) and Rashidi (p. 70, folio 52). 'Am'aq said Rashidi's verse "wants a little spice"; Rashidi's retort won him "four trays of red gold, each containing two hundred and fifty dinars" (p. 71, folio 53).
- Firdawsi (Anecdote XX): "one of the Dihqans (land-owners) of Tus, from a village called Bazh in the district of Tabardn [text-layer?]"; one child, a daughter, for whose dowry he versified the Shahnama; "engaged for twenty-five years on this work" (p. 72, folio 54). Transcribed by 'Ali Daylam "in seven volumes", recited by Abu Dulaf; Huyayy son of Qutayba, revenue-collector of Tus, abated his taxes (p. 73, folio 55). Presented at Ghazna through "the great Minister Ahmad ibn Hasan"; enemies called him "a Rafidi and a Mu'tazilite"; "only twenty thousand dirhams were paid"; he divided it between the bath-man and the sherbet-seller (p. 73, folio 55; p. 74, folio 56). Fled to Herat, hid "six months" at the shop of "Azraqf's [text-layer?] father, Isma'il the bookseller", then went to the Sipahbad Shahriyar in Tabaristan, wrote "a satire of a hurydred [text-layer?] couplets"; Shahriyar bought it at "100,000 dirhams" ("a thousand dirhams" a couplet) and had it expunged; "only these six verses of it remained" (p. 75, folio 57). Mahmud later sent "sixty thousand dinars' worth of indigo" to Tus; it arrived as Firdawsi's corpse was carried out "from the Gate of Razan" (p. 76, folio 58; p. 77, folio 59).
- Mahmud (Sultan): a "zealot" who accepted the imputations against Firdawsi (p. 74, folio 56); story via Mu'izzi from Amir 'Abdu'r-Razzaq: "Mahmud was once in India, and was returning thence tov/ards [text-layer?] Ghazna", met a rebellious chief's fortress, was moved by a Firdawsi verse recited by the Minister (p. 76, folio 58).

## Practices, institutions, economy
- Court patronage of poets: salary and allowances (Browne's footnote after the Editor: "jdmagl [text-layer?] ... means wages in cash, while ijrd ... means allowances, especially in kind"); a draft "on Ispahan" for the salary (p. 64, folio 46; p. 66, folio 48).
- Transoxianan custom: gold and silver placed in trays in audience-chambers, "which they call sim-tdqd [text-layer?] or juft", dispensed "by the handful" (p. 71, folio 53).
- Author on reciprocity of patron and poet: each achieves renown through the other, "these two things being interdependent" (p. 71, folio 53).
- Land-tax: Huyayy son of Qutayba, "revenue-collector of Tus", abated Firdawsi's taxes (p. 73, folio 55).
- Burial dispute: a preacher in Tabaran refused Firdawsi's burial "in the Musulman Cemetery, because he was a Rafidf (Shi'a)"; he was buried in his own garden within the Gate, "and there he lies to this day" (p. 77, folio 59).
- Tomb visit: "in the year A.H.5io(A.D. II 16-1 1 17) [text-layer?] I visited his tomb" — the author at Firdawsi's tomb (p. 77, folio 59).
- Endowment of a public work: the refused royal gift was given "to the Imam Abu Bakr ibn Ish^q-i-Krramf [text-layer?]" for "the repair of the rest-house of Chaha ... between Merv and Nfshapur [text-layer?]", which was carried out; the preacher was expelled from Tabaran (p. 77, folio 59). Browne's footnote: he was head of "the Kira"mi [text-layer?] sect at Nishapur", a sect that "inclined to anthropomorphism" (p. 77, folio 59).
- Backgammon board: Browne's footnote names the six "houses" (p. 67, folio 49).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)
- Author (Nizami) on Mas'ud's imprisonment: "it was not a laudable deed", and "the ill repute of this deed remained on this noble House" (p. 69, folio 51); "that misery of Mas'ud passed, while this ill repute will endure till the Resurrection" (p. 70, folio 52).
- Author on Firdawsi's verse: "In eloquence I know of^no [text-layer?] poetry in Persian which equals this, and but little even in Arabic" (p. 73, folio 55).
- Browne (footnote) disputes the attribution of Burhani's deathbed verse to the Nizamu'l-Mulk, holding the Chahar Maqala's authority, derived "directly from Mu^izzi [text-layer?]", superior (p. 63, folio 45).
- Browne (footnote) on the MSS. date "572" for Anecdote XVIII: "an evident error", reasoning from reign dates as printed (p. 67, folio 49).
- Browne (footnote, after Mirza Muhammad): "an extra-ordinary confusion of dates and persons in this story" (Anecdote XVIII/XIX) (p. 70, folio 52).
- Browne (footnote): Firdawsi's remarks on 'Ali Daylam and Abu Dulaf "seem rather ungrateful" (p. 73, folio 55); the claim that only six verses of the satire survived would imply "the well-known satire, as we have it, is spurious" (p. 75, folio 57); unsure where the quotation ends, "but the last sentence of this paragraph is certainly Nizami's" (p. 77, folio 59).
