## Shrines and figures in the archive
- **darbar-malik-ahmad-ayaz** (the book's "Ayaz" / "Aydz" [text-layer?] [Ayaz], Anecdote XIV, "SULTAN MAHMUD AND, AYAZ" — the author Nizami-i-'Arudi's narrative):
  - "The love borne by Sultan Yamtnttd-Dawla [text-layer?] [Yaminu'd-Dawla] Mahmud to … the Turk is well-known and famous" — the line is interleaved in the text layer and Ayaz's name before "the Turk" is not legible (p. 55, folio 37).
  - Ayaz described (interleaved lines, reading reconstructed from the fragments [text-layer?]): "not remarkably handsome, but was of sweet expression and olive complexion, symmetrically formed, graceful in his movements, sensible and deliberate in action, and mightily endowed with all the arts of pleasing" (p. 55, folio 37).
  - Mahmud "was a pious and God-fearing man, and he wrestled much with his love for Ayaz so that he should not diverge … from the Path of the Law" (p. 55, folio 37).
  - At a carousal, the wine affecting him, Mahmud looked at "the curls of Ayaz" and "lover-like he drew him to himself"; the "watchman" of the Qur'anic verse warned "O Mahmud, mingle not sin with love"; fearing he could not withstand "the hosts of Ayaz's locks", he gave Ayaz a knife and bade him cut off his curls "in the middle" (p. 55, folio 37).
  - Ayaz "doubled back his locks to get the measurement, executed the king's command, and laid the two tresses before Mahmud"; "this ready obedience became a fresh cause of love", and Mahmud gave Ayaz gold and jewels "beyond his usual wont" before falling "into a drunken sleep" (p. 55, folio 37).
  - Browne's footnote: here and in the next sentence he preferred "the alternative reading of the MSS." to the printed text, which places "We believe and we affirm" differently (p. 55, folio 37).
  - Next morning Mahmud "saw the clipped tresses", was seized by remorse; no courtier dared ask, until "Hajib 'All [ibn] Qarib" [text-layer?] ['Ali], "his Chief Chamberlain", sent "'Unsurf" [text-layer?] ['Unsuri] in to restore his temper (p. 56, folio 38).
  - 'Unsuri "extemporized" the quatrain "Why deem it shame a fair one's curls to shear, … When clipped the Cypress doth most trim appear"; Mahmud had "precious stones" twice fill the poet's mouth and drank wine till nightfall to those verses, "whereby his melancholy was dissipated" (p. 56, folio 38).
  - The author's moral: "improvisation is the chief pillar of the Poetic Art" (p. 56, folio 38).
  - Ayaz's position, burial, governorship, death date, Lahore: (not stated).

## Practices, institutions, economy
- Royal councils "held and adjourned" until unanimous on ambassadors' enigmas; the Ministry of Posts as an obsolete royal custom under the Saljuqs (p. 44, folio 26). Embassy costs: "disbursements from the treasury for the expenses of the ambassadors … and the maintenance of the Imams" (pp. 44–45, folios 26–27).
- Poetic training (Excursus): memorize "20,000 couplets of the poetry of the Ancients", keep "10,000 verses" of the Moderns as models, study prosody incl. works of "Abu'l-Hasan Bahrami of Sarakhs" (p. 50, folio 32).
- Patronage: the King should patronize a qualified poet; "no money should be wasted" on a poor one, "especially if he be old"; a talented young poet should be patronized "according to the Code of Nobility" (p. 50, folio 32). Improvisation brings rewards: "thus can money be extracted from the treasury" (p. 56, folio 38).
- Farrukhi's allowance from a dihqan of Sistan: "two hundred measures of corn, each containing five maunds, and a hundred dirhams"; he asked "three hundred measures" and "one hundred and fifty dirhams" and was refused further increase (p. 57, folio 39).
- Abu'l-Muzaffar "had 18,000 breeding mares", branding the colts yearly at a branding-ground with tents, wine and a great fire; the Amir "drinks wine and gives away horses" (p. 58, folio 40).
- Browne's footnote on "Khing-i-nawbatl" [text-layer?]: a saddled "sentry-horse" kept always at the king's palace gate (p. 53, folio 35).
- Herat's produce: "one hundred and twenty different varieties of the grape", Parniyan and Kalanjari; a Kalanjari cluster "sometimes attains a weight of five maunds" (pp. 51–52, folios 33–34).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)
- The author: "an intelligent and accomplished secretary is the greatest ornament to a king's magnificence" (interleaved lines [text-layer?]) (p. 45, folio 27).
- Definition of poetry: the art whereby the poet "can make a little thing appear great and a great thing small" and moves men's temperaments (p. 45, folio 27).
- A king's name endures "by reason of the poet's verse" when army and treasure vanish (p. 47, folio 29).
- Rudagi's couplet contains "seven admirable touches of art"; Browne reports Mirza Muhammad's objection that the first and second are identical and that "style" is not a rhetorical artifice (p. 54, folio 36).
