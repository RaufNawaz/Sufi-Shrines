- **Jundi-Sabur** ("Jurtdir Sabur" [text-layer?], "Gunde-Shapur", "Junday-Sabiir" [text-layer?]): the family's native town, with its "great hospital (JBimdristdn) [text-layer?]" and medical school; quoting Rawlinson, a Nestorian see under Shapur II "(A.D. 309-379)", its School "renowned, during the reign of Anrisharwan [text-layer?] (A.D. 531-578)"; Edessa's Nestorian scholars took refuge there after "A.D. 488-9"; it surrendered on terms during the Arab invasion "(A.H. 15-^7 [text-layer?]; A.D. 636-8)"; the Caliph al-Mansur "(A.H. 136-158; A.D. 754-775)" summoned Jurjis I to Baghdad, where he stayed "until A.H. 152 (A.D. 769)" (p. 164, folio 146). Per al-Muqaddasi, in the mid-tenth century few Christians, not many Jews and Zoroastrians; of Muslims many Mu'tazilites, Shi'a and Hanbalites (p. 165, folio 147).

[text-layer?] [A.H.] 260 (A.D. 873)"; son Ishaq "died in A.D. 910 or 911" (p. 165, folio 147).
- **Thabit ibn Qurra of Harran**: chief of the "so-called Sabeans" of Harran, "known as Hellenopolis"; "born at Harran in A.H. 221 (A.D. 836)"; "Thdbit I (d. Feb. 19, 901 A.D.)"; favour of al-Mu'tadid "(A.D. 892-902)" (p. 166, folio 148).
- **Abu Bakr Muhammad ibn Zakariyya ar-Razi** ("Rhazes"): spent his first thirty years in Ray; pupil at Baghdad of "'Ali ibn Rabban at-Tabari", who composed the Firdawsu'l-Hikmat "in A.I}. [text-layer?] [A.D.] 850"; director of the hospital at Ray then Baghdad; wrote "12 books" on Alchemy (p. 167, folio 149). Death "generally placed either in the year A.H. 31 it [text-layer?] (A.D. 923-4), or in A.H. 320 (A.D. 932)", one Qifti MS "as late as A.H. 364 (A.D. 974-5)" (p. 168, folio 150).

## Practices, institutions, economy
- Court medicine and patronage: from Jurjis I's time "until the middle of the eleventh century some member of the family was always one of the chief physicians of the Court at Baghdad" (p. 164, folio 146); Jurjis I stayed at court "in spite of his refusal to forsake the Christian for the Muhammadan faith" (p. 164, folio 146).
- Razi was "struck on the head by his disappointed patron Manstir [text-layer?], governor of Ray" over failed alchemy, and became blind (p. 167, folio 149).
- Hospitals: Bimaristan at Jundi-Sabur (p. 164, folio 146); Razi directed hospitals at Ray and Baghdad (p. 167, folio 149).
- Diagnosis (Note XXVI): ten genera of pulse, "37 primary varieties of pulse" (pp. 158–159, folios 140–141); urine inspection, the vessel called "Tafsira" (p. 160, folio 142); "sarsdnc [text-layer?] [sarsam]" ("delirium") = Phrenitis, which "would therefore appear to be equivalent to meningitis" (p. 160, folio 142); fever defined and classified from the Dhakhira-i-Khwarazmshahi, Book V, incl. the hot-bath analogy (hectic, humoristic, quotidian) and "Thirteen kinds of fever" (pp. 160–162, folios 142–144).
- Language at Jundi-Sabur: an Arab physician complains (al-Jahiz) that "his language is Arabic" and it "should have been the language of Jund*-Sabur" (p. 165, folio 147).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)
- "The almost miraculous cure of the butcher related in Anecdote XXXIX of this book" is ascribed by Qifti and Ibn Abi Usaybi'a to Thabit ibn Qurra (p. 166, folio 148).
- Hunayn was once seen "in the guise of a long-haired wandering bard reciting Homer in the streets" (p. 165, folio 147).
- Razi refused an eye operation on learning the surgeon "was ignorant of the anatomy of the eye", saying "he had looked on the world until he was tired of it" (p. 167, folio 149).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)
- Mirza Muhammad (via Browne): in the Three Friends story al-Bakharzi "has simply been replaced by the poet 'Umar-i-Khayyam", and 'Amidu'l-Mulk by Nizamu'l-Mulk, "the Imam Muwaffaq remaining in both versions" (p. 156, folio 138).
- Browne: 'Umar is far more celebrated in the West than in Persia, "where no one would think of ranking 'Umar as a poet in the same category as Firdawsi, Sa'di or Hafiz"; reasons: FitzGerald, substance over form, and mood congenial to the age (pp. 157–158, folios 139–140).
- Note XXVI: "our author chiefly follows Avicenna"; the remarks on the systole "ascribed by our author to Avicenna appear to be really quoted by him from Galen" (pp. 158–159, folios 140–141).
- Browne: Razi "was probably the greatest practitioner of the so-called Arabian Medicine who ever lived", and as clinician "far surpassed" Avicenna (p. 166, folio 148); the Hawi "stands on an altogether different plane from the Qdnun [text-layer?]" (p. 167, folio 149).
- Mirza Muhammad (Persian notes pp. 231-3 and 240-1, cited by Browne): the author of the Chahar Maqala errs in identifying Razi's patron with "the sixth Samanid ruler Mansur ibn NiiH [text-layer?] [Nuh] ibn Nasr", who reigned "from A.H. 350 to 366 (A.D. 961-976-7)", "long after the death of Razi" (p. 168, folio 150).
- Only four of Razi's works are named in the Chahar Maqala; the "Tuhfatu 'l-Muluk" "cannot be identified"; the "Mttrshid [text-layer?] [Murshid]" is properly "al-Fustil fit-Tibb" (p. 168, folio 150).
- It is uncertain whether the Bukht-Yishu' "mentioned in the text (Anecdote .XX^CII I [text-layer?])" is Jibra'il's father or son (p. 164, folio 146).
