# tahqiqat_chishti — chunk 074 — pp. 806–816

This chunk is not about any shrine. It is a running narrative of dynastic history. It opens (p. 806) with the tail of a Persian elegy and chronogram for Sultan Mahmud, then follows his sons Muhammad and Mas'ud (pp. 806–807). A short section titled "پرستش" covers the spread of Shiva worship in the Deccan and the killing of the raja of Qanauj (p. 807). Next comes "بقیہ احوال اولاد سلطان محمود", the later Ghaznavids down to Khusrau Shah and Khusrau Malik at Lahore (pp. 807–808). The narrative then runs without new headings through the Ghurids and Qutb-ud-Din (p. 809), Shams-ud-Din/Iltutmish, Raziya and Balban (pp. 810–811), Kaiqubad and the Khaljis, including Ala-ud-Din, Padmini and Malik Kafur (pp. 812–814), the Tughluqs (pp. 814–815), Timur's invasion and sack of Delhi (pp. 815–816), and Khizr Khan and the Sayyids. It breaks off mid-sentence at the end of p. 816. Lahore and Multan appear only as places in this political history. No archive shrine is described.

## Practices, institutions, economy

- Temples:
  - Mas'ud demolished the temples ("مندرون") at Hansi (p. 806).
  - After Mahmud's time the temples he had damaged were rebuilt, and "راجاؤن کی کثرت نذور سے بہت سی دولت جمع" and worship resumed (p. 807).
  - Iltutmish broke the idols from Ujjain at the door of Delhi's Jama Masjid (p. 810).
- Building: after Padmini, Ala-ud-Din had "خوب خوب عمارتین مساجد وغیرہ" built (p. 814).
- Revenue and coinage: Muhammad Tughluq paid off the Mughals and laid such "سخت محصول" that zamindars abandoned their land (p. 814). He issued a copper token coin "دخل [OCR?]" (p. 815).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- The author draws inferences of his own:

  - He doubts the size of Ram Dev's tribute: "یہہ معاملہ قرین قیاس نہین مگر شاید کہ اوس وقت کا من اس وقت کے من سے کم ہوگا" (p. 812). He concludes from the campaign that Muslims saw the Hindu rajas as "بالکل بزدل اور ڈرپوک" (p. 812).
  - On the Mughals sparing India: "شاید باعث یہہ تہا کہ اونکو ملک ایران مین بہت کار رہتے تہے" (p. 810).
  - On Ala-ud-Din's coin title "سکندر ثانی": "یہہ مقولہ [OCR?] بیشک درست تہا" (p. 814).
  - On Timur leaving after sixteen days: "اس سے صاف معلوم ہوتا ہی کہ اوس کا ارادہ بادشاہی کا نہ تہا", and that his aim was to make his name through plunder (p. 816).

## Doubts

- No archive shrine is described in this chunk. All mappings are place-level (Lahore, Multan), person-level (Qutb-ud-Din, Shiva) or province-level (Punjab), and come from a general political history.
- Lahore bullet: place-level, not site-specific. "یہہ ملک" on p. 809 is read as continuing the Lahore narrative of p. 808. That reading is a judgement.
- Multan bullet: place-level, not site-specific. "اکثر ملتان [OCR?]" (p. 811) carries a flag.
- tomb-of-qutbuddin-aibak: the book never prints "ایبک". The identification rests on the Delhi ruler "قطب الدین" who served Muhammad Ghori, died "سن بارہ سو دس [OCR?]" and had Shams-ud-Din as slave and son-in-law (p. 809). The consolidator should confirm it. The chunk also names two other men called Qutb-ud-Din, who must not be merged with him: "قطب الدین محمد غوری", who married Bahram's daughter and was killed, and Saif-ud-Din's brother Qutb-ud-Din (both p. 808). No tomb is mentioned.
- Shiva bullet: person-level, not site-specific (many-row rule). The passage is about the Deccan, outside Pakistan, and names no site of any row.
- "جگن ناتہہ" (p. 809) is where raja Lachhman fled to, used as a destination. It is the same name as the principal figure of jagannath-temple-sialkot, but nothing ties the two. I did NOT map it and put it under Other. This is flagged for the consolidator and the coordinator as a possible deviation from the person rule.
- Sultan Mahmud is named (pp. 806–807) but Ayaz is not. Per the brief, darbar-malik-ahmad-ayaz is reached only through Ayaz, so it is not mapped.
- "کشمیر" (p. 806) is not mapped to sharada-peeth or to the Azad Kashmir province. The book does not say which part of Kashmir it means.
- "پنجاب [OCR?]" (p. 814) carries a flag. Province-level, weigh accordingly. The Punjab bullet's period follows the book's own regnal narrative. The book draws no boundaries.
- Dates as printed contradict each other or are implausible:
