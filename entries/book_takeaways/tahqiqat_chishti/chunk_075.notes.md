# tahqiqat_chishti — chunk 075 — pp. 817–828

This chunk is not about shrines. It is a run of general dynastic history of the kings of Delhi and Hindustan. It opens mid-narrative (p. 817) at the end of the Sayyid kings: Sayyid Ala-ud-Din's retreat to Badaun, Bahlul Lodi's accession, Sikandar Lodi and Ibrahim Lodi, ending with Babur's victory at Panipat. Two tables follow: "فہرست شاہان قوم سادات جو دہلی کی ۳۶ [OCR?] سال تک بادشاہ رہی" and "نقشہ خاندان سلطنت لودیہ شاہان دہلی" (p. 818). The titled sections then run in this order: "ذکر سلطنت مغلیہ وتخت نشینی بابر بادشاہ" (pp. 818–819) with "تاریخ وفات بابر بادشاہ" (p. 819); "ذکر سلطنت ہمایون بادشاہ" (pp. 819–822); "خاندان افغانان سوری کا حال" (pp. 822–823), which returns to Humayun's restoration and death, with "تاریخ وفات ہمایون بادشاہ" (p. 823); "ذکر محمد جلال الدین اکبر ابن ہمایون بادشاہ" (pp. 823–826), with a "قطعہ تاریخ" (p. 825); and "حال شاہزادہ سلیم ابو المظفر نور الدین عرف جہانگیر بادشاہ" (pp. 826–828), which is mostly the story of Nur Jahan. The chunk ends at p. 828 on Nur Jahan's coin verse, and most of that page is illegible. No shrine, saint or takia is described anywhere in the chunk. The only archive mappings are place-level ones (Lahore, Multan, Amarkot).

## Practices, institutions, economy

- Sher Shah's works: a road "گنگا سی لیکر دریای سندہ تک جو تین ہزار میل انگریزی کا فاصلہ ہے", fruit trees on both sides, a well every two miles, and a sarai for travellers. "ہر ایک مسافر کو بادشاہ کی طرف سی کہانا ملتا تہا" (p. 822).
- Sikandar Lodi's destruction of temples and ban on temple-building at Mathura (p. 817).
- Akbar's administration: rules for war, revenue and taxes, and "بہت سی مفید قانون" (p. 825). He gathered "لوگ تحقیق مذہب عیسوی کے واسطی", heard the Injil, and had them debate "علما محمدی" (p. 826).
- Coinage in Nur Jahan's name, with "مقام اکبرآباد" named in a damaged line (p. 828).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- The infant Nur Jahan, left in the wilderness by her parents, was found with "ایک کالا سانپ اوس لڑکی کو لپٹا ہوا". When the father cried out, the snake went up a tree (pp. 826–827).
- Babur's repentance: his army refused to accept him as imam because he drank. He sold his gold and silver wine vessels and gave the money to the poor, after which the army obeyed (p. 819).
- In a skirmish, a chance arrow struck the enemy commander's forehead and saved Humayun. The book frames this with "سبحان اللہ تیری قدرت کا کوئی شریک نہین" (p. 821).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- On Akbar's religion, the author reports two views: "بعضی کہتے ہین کہ وہ شماس [OCR?] تہا" and others that he had great faith in the Hindu religion. The author's own judgement is "اصل حال یہہ کہ وہ کسی اور مذہب کا معتقد نہ تہا", only that he loved hearing every religion's books (pp. 825–826). He praises "صلح کل" as the reason Akbar's rule lasted (p. 826).
- The author reports that "تاریخ جہانگیر" counts Jahangir's love for a noble's wife, and his marrying her after killing her husband, as "سب سے [OCR?] بڑا ماجرا اور گناہ" (p. 826).
- The author praises the Babur chronogram as a feat of craft: each hemistich yields "سنہ ۹۳۷ [OCR?] ہجری", and "ایک قطعہ سی آٹہہ مادی تاریخ برآمد کرنی صنعت سی خالی نہین" (p. 819).

## Doubts

- The Lahore bullet (35 ids) is place-level, not site-specific. Every Lahore mention in this chunk is a passing reference in Mughal and Afghan political history (a governor, a muster, a caravan's arrival, a mint name), and none concerns any shrine. Weigh it accordingly.
- The Multan bullet (8 ids) is likewise place-level. The only fact is that Multan's governor invited Babur (p. 817).
- Amarkot bullet:
  - It is place-level.
  - The book's "امرکوٹ" is matched through the index's own row name "Umarkot (Amarkot) Shiv Mandir" and through the location "Umarkot" of the Girhori row. The toponym map lists these under separate spellings ("umerkot" and "umarkot").
  - "سندہ" here could be the river (the book says "دریای سندہ" on pp. 819 and 822) or the region. It is treated as part of a single-town statement (ruling (c)(4)) and not as a province bullet.
  - The Akbar birth date "۱۵۴۲ء" is flagged [OCR?].
- "خواجہ ایاز [OCR?]", Nur Jahan's father (pp. 826–827), is NOT mapped to **darbar-malik-ahmad-ayaz**. The book calls him a Tatar who came to Hindustan and served Akbar, and nothing ties him to Malik Ayaz of the archive row. The match is a name coincidence only.
- No "Punjab" province statement appears in this chunk, so there is no province bullet.
- Numerals:
  - All AD and hijri years in the tables and text are flagged [OCR?] as printed. None is converted here.
  - The Sayyid table gives Khizr Khan's reign as "۰ [OCR?] سال" beside "۱۴۱۴" to "۱۴۲۱", which is internally inconsistent as printed. The reigns "۱ [OCR?] سال" for Muhammad Shah (۱۴۳۵ to ۱۴۴۵) and "۳۶ [OCR?] سال" in the heading are likewise unreliable.
  - Humayun's accession is "۱۵۳۰" and his death "۱۵۴۹ع [OCR?]" (p. 823). Jahangir's accession is "سنہ ۱۵۹۹ [OCR?]" (p. 826), against Akbar's chronogram year "۱۰۱۵ [OCR?]" (p. 825). These are printed as is and not reconciled.
- The book prints "بیٹی" (daughter) where the context refers to Akbar: "اوسکی بیٹی [OCR?] کو لکڑیون مین باندہ" (p. 821), "اپنی بیٹی اکبر کو چہاتی سے لگایا" and "اپنی بیٹی جلال الدین اکبر" (p. 822). These are probably بیٹے, but the printed form is kept.
