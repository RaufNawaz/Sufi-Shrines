# tahqiqat_chishti — chunk 073 — pp. 795–805

The chunk opens mid-section (p. 795) with the last lines of an account of a jogi's establishment whose heading and name are in the previous chunk (chunk 072): its gaddi succession, income and langar. It then runs through these titled sections in order. First, "ذکر اولاد راجہ رامچند" (pp. 795–796), which says Raja Ramchandar's two sons founded Lahore and Kasur, followed by a long list of the kings descended from Kush in Ajodhya. Second, "احوال خاندان راجہ ہای ہندوستان" (pp. 796–797), on the Suraj Bansi and Chandar Bansi dynasties, the dating of Ramchandar and the Ramayana story. Third, "حال خاندان چندر بنسیان" (pp. 797–802), a general history of India: Brahmins and Kshatriyas, Darius, the Buddha, Alexander and Porus, Seleucus and Chandragupta, Bikramajit and Shalivahan. Fourth, "تذکرہ شاہان اہل اسلام" (pp. 802–805), which runs from the Prophet and the early Arab campaigns in Sindh through the Samanids and Sabuktigin to Sultan Mahmud (Jaipal at Lahore, Somnath, Mahmud's death). It ends with the first line of a chronogram for Mahmud's death (p. 805). Very little of this chunk concerns shrines. Folio markers are printed (none above p. 803) but are not cited here (brief rule).

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- Darius ("دارا") took the throne of Kaikhusrau "سن عیسوی سے پانسو اٹہارہ سال پہلی". His admiral "سائلیکس [OCR?]" came down the Indus. India paid him tribute in gold (pp. 797–798).
- Gautam / "ساکیا [OCR?] بدہ": born in south Bihar, often stayed at Gaya; his time is given as "پانسو چالیس [OCR?] برس پہلی سن عیسوی". The book describes the enmity of Buddhists and Brahmins, which ended with the Brahmins winning (p. 798). It says the Buddhists later went to Sarandip / Lanka, built a great building there, and cut temples into a mountain, the largest "چالیس فٹ لمبا نوی [OCR?] فٹ چوڑا اور پنجتالیس [OCR?] فٹ اونچا", with a thirty-foot Buddha image (p. 801).
- Alexander ("سکندر"): he was twenty when he reached Attock, crossed it with "ایک لاکہہ بیس ہزار فوج", and was hosted by "ٹیکسائیلز [OCR?]" at Taxila. He fought Porus on the Jhelum: he crossed at a ford ten kos away with eleven thousand horse, and Porus's son was killed. Porus surrendered and asked to be treated as a king, and Alexander restored his kingdom. Alexander founded two cities on the Jhelum, crossed the Ravi[OCR?], and turned back at the Sutlej when his army refused to go on against Magadh. He died "بعمر تیس [OCR?] سال" (pp. 799–800).
- Seleucus ("سلوکس") invaded again and defeated Chandragupt, who agreed to give fifty elephants a year; Seleucus married his daughter to Chandragupt. Chandragupt died "دوسو بانوین [OCR?] برس پہلی" the Christian era (pp. 800–801).
- Bikramajit ("بکرماجیت"): there were eight rajas of the name. Citing "تاریخ فرشتہ", the book makes the famous one a Parmar of Ujjain who took the throne "سن عیسوی چہپن [OCR?] برس پہلی". He slept on a mat, kept fourteen hundred[OCR?] learned men including "کالیداس", worshipped one God, and founded his own samvat. Muhammad bin Qasim then attacked and won battles against "واہر [OCR?]" (p. 803). "راجہ توار [OCR?]" was on the throne at Delhi (p. 803).
- Samanids: "اسماعیل سرکمانی [OCR?]", governor of Mawara al-Nahr and Khurasan, declared himself king in "سن دوسو ترسیٹہہ [OCR?] ہجری" with his capital at Bukhara, founding the "خاندان سامانیا [OCR?]". The succession dispute is told with Mansur, "النگین [OCR?]" / "التگین [OCR?]", Ishaq and Sabuktigin (p. 803).
- Sabuktigin: the army raised him to the throne of Ghazni. He claimed descent from the Iranian kings and called "یزدجرد [OCR?]" his grandfather. He died "سن نوسو ستانوین [OCR?] عیسوی" after "بائیس [OCR?] برس" of rule, and his elder son Ismail ruled for a few months (pp. 803–804).
- Sultan Mahmud: took the throne "بعمر تیس [OCR?] سالہ" and spent "چار [OCR?]" years settling his kingdom. Then "سن ایک ہزار ایک [OCR?] عیسوی" he invaded India, coming "بارہ [OCR?] دفعہ" (pp. 804–805). Jaipal "جیتی جی [OCR?] آگ مین جل مرا", and his son Anand Pal took the throne (p. 805). Mahmud was of middling height and "چیچک رو"; he died "بعمر ترسیٹہہ [OCR?] سال" and is said to have wept over his jewels at death. His sons were Muhammad, "رحم دل", and Mas'ud, harsh like his father; Mahmud divided the kingdom between them in his lifetime (p. 805). A chronogram by "مظہر الحق سعید اکبر آبادی" begins "آنکہ محمود غزنوی بودہ" (p. 805).
- Somnath ("سومنات"): an idol "سولہ فٹ اونچی اور چہہ فٹ زمین مین مدفون". Mahmud broke it and sent pieces to Ghazni and Mecca, and jewels worth twice the rajas' offer came out of it (p. 805).

## Practices, institutions, economy

- Jogi gaddi (opening fragment): income is "زر معافی", and on "چندرات" pilgrims give some cash and goods; "اوس روز پچاس ساٹہہ آدمیونکو کہانا دیتا ہے" (that day he feeds fifty or sixty people) (p. 795). Succession is by chela, and the chela becomes "مالک مکان" (p. 795).
- Somnath as described: "بوقت گرہن کے تین لاکہہ یاتری یہان جمع ہوتے تہی". Two thousand villages were "معاف" for its expenses. It employed two thousand Brahmins, "پانسو کنچنیان" and three hundred kalawants, and three hundred barbers came to shave the pilgrims (p. 805).
- Customs of India that Alexander's companions recorded, as the book reports them: girls were married at seven, people wore coloured shoes, and only great men carried umbrellas (p. 800, partly [illegible]).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- Bikramajit: Hindu poets say that without his permission the magnet would not lift iron, nor amber lift grass (p. 801). The book attributes this to the poets, as exaggeration.
- Somnath: "قدرت الہی سے" so many jewels came out of the broken idol that the rajas' offered sum was half their value (p. 805).
- Vyasa's birth: the book says Hindu historians tell a strange story about it that is "قرین قیاس نہین", then defends it with the verse "کار پاکان را قیاس از خود مگیر" (p. 797).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- The author: "تواریخ ہندوستان کے بہت مبہم ہے اور مضامین شاعرانہ اوسمین بکثرت مندرج ہین البتہ تواریخ اہل اسلام صاف معلوم ہوتی ہے" (Indian history is vague and full of poetic matter, while Muslim history is clear), so he offers only a short digest (p. 797).
- He states why he includes this material: "واسطی یادگار" he writes a little on the Hindu raja clans, because India "قدیم سے اہل ہنود [OCR?] کے قبضہ مین رہا ہے" (p. 796).
- He sets Hindu belief (the treta yug, lakhs of years ago) against "مورخان انگریزی" (2000–2200 years before the Christian era), notes that historians differ, and gives Mr Bentley[OCR?]'s horoscope dating (p. 796).
- He reports the English historians' reading of the Ramayana's gods and rakhshas as poetic language for brave rajas and their enemies (p. 797).
- Sources he names: "ہندوں کی تواریخ" (p. 796); "ہیرودوٹس [OCR?]" (p. 798); "مورخان سکندر" (p. 799); "تاریخ فرشتہ" (pp. 801, 804).

## Doubts

- Damage inventory: this chunk has about 150 `[OCR?]` and 40 `[illegible]` flags, far below the brief's measured range. Almost every name in the king list (pp. 795–796) is flagged. Nearly every numeral carries a flag (the Ramchandar dating, Buddha's date, Alexander's age, the dates for the Prophet's birth, Walid, the Samanids and Sabuktigin, and Mahmud's ages and invasions). Sabuktigin's first invasion date is printed "سن [illegible] سو ستہتر [OCR?] ہجری", with its hundreds digit illegible. Every flag is carried inline above.
- Lahore bullet (35 ids): the attachment is place-level, not site-specific, under ruling (a). It is pre-Islamic and Ghaznavid history of the city.
- Multan bullet (8 ids), Peshawar bullet (10 ids), Kasur (mazar-of-bulleh-shah), Aror (kalka-cave-temple-asthan-of-kalka-devi): each attachment is place-level, not site-specific. Kasur's founding form is "کشور [OCR?]". The Aror name is itself flagged "ارور [OCR?]".
- ram-mandir-saidpur-ram-kund-mandir: the attachment is person-level (Rama is the principal figure), not about the Saidpur temple. The book itself ties "راجہ رامچندر" to the Ramayana's "راما" (p. 797). The row's principal_figure reads "Rama". Weigh accordingly.
- loh-temple-lava-temple: the book's form is "لوہو", the son of Ramchandar who founded Lahore. The equation with the row's "Lava (Luv)" rests on the name and the father, and the chunk does not say "Lava". It is a bare name, so weigh accordingly. The row is also in the Lahore bullet.
- darbar-malik-ahmad-ayaz: the evidence is the bare name "ایاز", advising Sultan Mahmud at Somnath (p. 805). The chunk gives no other identification. It is mapped by the person rule and is not a Lahore toponym id.
- "پنجاب" (p. 799): this is ancient (Alexander-era) usage, and the book does not bound it. It is province-level, so weigh accordingly. The book does not say which of Porus or Abisares lived in Punjab.
- "سندہ": the book uses the same word for the river Indus ("دریائی سندہ", pp. 798–799) and for the country ("ملک سندہ", p. 802). Only clear country uses went into the province bullet. River uses and "غرب رویہ سندہ" (p. 805) were left out as ambiguous. Province-level, so weigh accordingly.
- "گجرات" (pp. 802, 803: "گجرات اور سندہ مین تجارت"; "واہر [OCR?] کے حاکم گجرات"): the spelling matches the location of shrine-of-hazrat-shah-daula-daryai (Gujrat, Punjab). The chunk does not say which Gujrat/Gujarat it means, and the context is the early Arab campaigns. I have NOT mapped it, so this is recorded as a possible deviation from ruling (a) for the consolidator to decide.
- "موضع اچہرا" (p. 795): Ichhra is in the toponym map (shah-jamal, shrine-of-baba-shah-kamal). Here it names only the jogi's night lodging, as a location, so it is not mapped under the brief's mohalla/location rule. The consolidator should weigh whether it belongs.
- Jhelum (pp. 799–800) appears only as a river ("دریای جہلم"), not the town of the rows gurdwara-choa-sahib and tilla-jogian, so it is not mapped. Taxila, Attock, Ajmer, Delhi, Kanauj, Kalinjar, Ujjain, Ghazni, Bukhara and Somnath have no rows.
- "مہاشیو [OCR?]" and "شہادیو [OCR?]" (p. 795) are names in the king list, not the deity Shiva, so they are not mapped to Shiva rows.
- Opening fragment: the jogi establishment's name, place and heading are in chunk 072. "یہان" (here) cannot be placed from this chunk.
- p. 796: it is unclear whether "پانسو برس" refers to Ramchandar's descendants' rule or to something else. Quoted as printed.
- p. 803 has no folio marker (the folio sequence jumps from 800 to 802). This is noted only for the record, since no folios are cited.
