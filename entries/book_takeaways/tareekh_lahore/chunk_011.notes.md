# tareekh_lahore — chunk 011 — pp. 148–161

**A mosque-by-mosque section of the city survey, with two mazars embedded in it** (pp. 148–161,
folios 151–164). The chunk opens mid-passage in a dispute over a mosque next to a Sikh baoli and its
confiscated shops, then gives two short mazar notices (Pir Siraj-ud-Din / Pir Shirazi; Pir Bhola),
then six mosques in sequence — Masjid Bazar Bi[OCR?], Masjid Pari Mahal, the "low mosque", Masjid
Muhammad Salih Kambo, Masjid Chinian Wali[OCR?], and the mosque of Nawab Wazir Khan with its
founder's long life-story — and closes on the mazar of Sayyid Ishaq Gazaruni[OCR?] known as Miran
Badshah inside the Wazir Khan mosque courtyard. **The chunk's heaviest value for this project is not
hagiographic**: it is endowment and revenue administration (confiscated shop rents, a nazul
holding, a notables' committee, a named mutawalli, waqf used to defeat a confiscation order),
building and repair dates from inscriptions, and one fully reported Thursday mela with an annual urs
and majlis-e sama.

**Folio markers as the chunk prints them**: every page in this chunk carries a `[folio N]` line, and
each reads three above the PDF index — p. 148 → folio 151 through p. 161 → folio 164, each marker
reading exactly that. Only the printed folio is used below.

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **gurdwara-baoli-sahib-guru-arjan-dev-ji-lahore** (the book calls it only "باولی صاحب", Baoli
  Sahib):
  - **It appears as the reason a neighbouring mosque's call to prayer was objectionable.** The
    faqirs' argument put before the Maharaja was that "تمام پنجاب کی مسجدون کے ملا کہبین[OCR?] بانگ
    بلند آواز سے نہین کہتے چہ جانیکہ باولی صاحب کے پاس جہان گرنتہہ صاحب رکہا ہو مسجد کا ملا اذان
    دیوے" — the mullas of all the mosques of the Punjab do not give the *bang* in a loud voice, let
    alone that the mulla of a mosque **beside Baoli Sahib, where the Granth Sahib is kept**, should
    give the azan (p. 148, folio 151). The book's phrase for the Sikh scripture's presence is "جہان
    گرنتہہ صاحب رکہا ہو".
  - The site is named **only** as "باولی صاحب"; the chunk gives it no quarter, no bazaar, no founder
    and no date **(not stated)**. See Doubts — the identification rests on the name plus the Granth
    Sahib alone.
  - The dispute is reported entirely from the mosque's side; nothing is said here about the baoli's
    own custodians, income or practices **(not stated)**.

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- **مزار پیر سراج الدین المعروف پیر شیرازی** — Mazar of Pir Siraj-ud-Din, known as Pir Shirazi
  (pp. 149–150, folios 152–153). Inside Lahore, in "محلہ جوطے[OCR?] موری", Mohalla Jhote[OCR?] Mori,
  and "زیارت گاہ خلق ہے", a place of pilgrimage for the people. "بعض لوگ[OCR?] ان[OCR?] بزرگ کو پیر
  شیرازی کے نام سے مشہور کرتے ہین" — some people make this elder famous by the name Pir Shirazi. He
  came "بخارا کی طرف سے", from the direction of **Bukhara**, "سلطان محمد تغلق کے عہد مین", in the
  era of **Sultan Muhammad Tughlaq**, and settled in Lahore; being "عارف کامل وفاضل اکمل[OCR?]",
  hundreds attended him for "تدریس[OCR?] وتلقین[OCR?]". Death year **(not stated)**. "بعد وفات کے
  اپنے مسکن کے اندر ودفنایا گیا" — after death he was buried inside his own dwelling; the mazar is
  "پختہ بنا ہوا" and its enclosure stands inside the surrounding houses. His refusal of the qaziship
  is under Practices.
- **مزار پیر بہولا** — Mazar of Pir Bhola (p. 150, folio 153). Built pukhta "پچیلہ[OCR?] کے حمام کے
  محلے مین", in the mohalla of the hammam of Pachila[OCR?]. The occupant was "ایک بے قید[OCR?]
  مجذوب[OCR?] فقیر عالمگیری عہد مین", an unrestrained majzub[OCR?] faqir of the **Alamgiri era**;
  no name beyond Pir Bhola, no dates **(not stated)**. "لڑکون سے اُسکو کمال محبت تہی" — he had utter
  love for boys, roamed the city all day with troops of them, and distributed among them "نقد
  وجنس", cash and goods, that people gave him. His cult of children is under Practices and his
  sweets under Legends. "اس بزرگ کا نام ابتک لاہور مین روشن ہے" — his name is bright in Lahore to
  this day.
- **مزار سید اسحاق گاذرونی المشہور میران بادشاہ** — Mazar of Sayyid Ishaq Gazaruni[OCR?], famous as
  **Miran Badshah** (pp. 156, 161; folios 159, 164). Inside the Wazir Khan mosque, in the courtyard
  attached to the mosque at the edge of the special courtyard, "مائل سمت جنوب", inclining southward;
  the grave itself lies "مسجد کی کرسی کے اندر تہ خانہ[OCR?] کے بیچ مین", within the mosque's plinth
  in the middle of an underground chamber, reached by descending a stair, with a four-walled
  enclosure and the form of a grave built above it as well. No death date, no silsila, no genealogy
  **(not stated)**. **"یہ قبر مسجد کی تعمیر سے پہلے اس موقع پر بنی ہوئی تہی"** — this grave was
  built on this spot **before** the mosque's construction, and Wazir Khan "اسکو بدستور قائم رکہا
  بلکہ اس مزار کو اپنی مسجد کی رونق و زینت تصور کیا", kept it as it was and considered the mazar the
  splendour and ornament of his mosque. Its mujawir, mela and urs are under Practices. **See Doubts
  — this name must not be mapped to any archive row.**
- **نواب بکہاری خان** — Nawab Bukhari Khan, founder of the mosque the chunk opens on; "سنہ
  ۱۱۶۵[OCR?] ہجری مین مر گیا", died in **1165 AH**[OCR?] (p. 148, folio 151). After the death of
  "میر معین الملک صوبہ لاہور", Mir Muin-ul-Mulk, subah of Lahore, when the government of Lahore came
  into the grip of "مرا وبیگم اُسکی زوجہ", his wife — name garbled in the transcription — she kept
  Bukhari Khan as "مختار ومدارالمہام سلطنت". She wished him to "مناکحت[OCR?] کرے[OCR?]" with her; he
  refused; "محل مین بلوا کر اُسکو قتل کروا دیا یہانتک کہ اسکی نعش کا بہی نشان نہ ملا" — she had him
  summoned to the palace and killed, so that not even a trace of his corpse was found (p. 149,
  folio 152). The author's verdict is under Arguments.
- **نواب وزیر خان مرحوم** — the late Nawab Wazir Khan, founder of both Masjid Pari Mahal and the
  great mosque, "شاہجہانی عہد مین لاہور کا حاکم یعنی صوبہ", ruler i.e. subah of Lahore in the
  Shahjahani era (p. 158, folio 161). His original name was **"شیخ علم الدین انصاری"**, Shaikh
  Ilm-ud-Din Ansari; "قصبہ چنیوٹ مین اسکی سکونت تہی", his residence was in the town of **Chiniot**;
  he studied "طبابت", medicine, and practised it in Chiniot, then moved to **Lahore**, then
  **Delhi**, then **Akbarabad**, where he cured Shah Jahan's mother, then still a prince's mother, of
  a years-long illness (p. 159, folio 162). The footprint stratagem by which he lanced **Nur Jahan
  Begum**'s "دنبل", boil, without her feeling the lancet, is told at length (pp. 159–160, folios
  162–163); its rewards are under Practices. He was made the imprisoned Prince Khurram's guard and
  fled with him to the Deccan at the urging of "وزیر آصف جاہ", Wazir Asaf Jah, Nur Jahan's brother;
  Jahangir "کشمیر کے رستے مین بہ بیماری ضیق النفس فوت ہو گیا", died on the Kashmir road of asthma,
  and Asaf Jah put Nur Jahan under house arrest at Lahore (p. 160, folio 163). "نواب وزیر خان خطاب
  پایا" — he received the title Nawab Wazir Khan and the governorship of Lahore, and being fond of
  building left the mosque as a memorial "کہ قیامت تک اسکا نام روشن رہیگا" (p. 161, folio 164).
- **نواب سرفراز خان المعروف افراز خان[OCR?]** — Nawab Sarfaraz Khan, known as Afraz Khan[OCR?],
  founder of Masjid Chinian Wali[OCR?] (p. 154, folio 157). Faujdar of the province of Lahore in the
  Shahjahani and Alamgiri periods, "پانچہزاری منصب تہا", holding a mansab of five thousand;
  denounced to Aurangzeb as "دست پرورہ و ملازم دارا شکوہ ولی عہد کا", the protégé and servant of the
  heir-apparent Dara Shikoh[OCR?]. His waqf manoeuvre is under Practices.
- **منشی محمد صالح دیوان صوبہ پنجاب** — Munshi Muhammad Salih, diwan of the province of Punjab,
  founder of the mosque inside Mochi Darwaza; "بڑا عالم و فاضل و شاعر وحید[OCR?] منشی شاہجہانی
  دربار کا۔ ذات کا کمبو تہا", a great scholar, accomplished and a poet, munshi of the Shahjahani
  court, a Kambo by caste; **"منشی عنایت اللہ مصنف کتاب بہار دانش اسکا ماموں اور خسر تہا"** — Munshi
  Inayatullah, author of *Bahar-e Danish*, was his maternal uncle and father-in-law (p. 152,
  folio 155).
- **ذوالفقار خان** — Zulfiqar Khan, founder of the "پست مسجد یعنی نیچی مسجد", the low mosque;
  "ایک امیر لودیہ سلطنت کا ایک اہلکار تہا", a noble and official of the **Lodhi** kingdom, holding
  the post of "سر دفتر" in the court of "ہیبت خان[OCR?] صوبہ لاہور", Haibat Khan[OCR?], subah of
  Lahore (p. 151, folio 154). Date **(not stated)**; the building is called only "بہت پرانی صدہا سال
  کی", very old, of hundreds of years.
- **غلام مہندی خان** — Ghulam Mahindi Khan, named in the inscription as founder of Masjid Bazar
  Bi[OCR?]; "بانی اسکا کوئی امیر یا تو غیر[OCR?] تہا جسکا نام غلام مہندی خان کتبہ مین لکہا ہے مگر
  سنہ پڑہا نہین جاتا" — its founder was some noble, whose name is written in the inscription, **but
  the year cannot be read** (p. 150, folio 153).
- **British and Indian officials named over the mosque-shops case** (p. 148, folio 151): "جناب اجرٹن

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **A mosque restored but its shop-rents kept confiscated: the chunk's central endowment case**
  (p. 148, folio 151). The Maharaja's settlement was that "مسجد بدستور ملا کے حوالے کر دی جاوے اور
  اُس سے مچلکا لے لیا جاوے کہ بانگ ندیوے مسجد کی ودوکانون کا کرایہ بہی ضبط رہے" — the mosque be
  handed back to the mulla as before, a **mochalka** (bond) be taken from him that he give no call to
  prayer, **and the rent of the mosque's shops remain confiscated**. "مسلمانون نے اتنی بات کو ہی
  غنیمتا جانا اور مسجد پر دوبارہ قبضہ پایا مگر دوکانین ہاتہہ سے جاتی رہین" — the Muslims counted
  even this a gain and regained possession of the mosque, but the shops were lost from their hands.
  A shrine-adjacent dispute settled by **separating the building from its income**.
- **The confiscation survived the change of regime as nazul** (p. 148, folio 151): "بعد انقراض سلطنت
  سکہی انگریزی عملداری مین بہی وہ دوکانین بصیغہ نزول ضبط رہین" — after the extinction of the Sikh
  kingdom, under English administration too those shops remained confiscated **under the head of
  nazul**. "اہل اسلام نے بہت دفعہ عرضیان کین اور مقدمات کئے مگر حکام نے بدین خیال کہ یہ محال
  مہاراجہ کیوقت سے ضبط ہے اب واگزار نہ کیا[OCR?] جاوے" — many petitions and lawsuits, refused by the
  officials on the reasoning that the holding had been confiscated since the Maharaja's time and
  should not now be released. **The colonial state is shown inheriting and defending a Sikh-era
  confiscation of religious property.**
- **The release, its value, and a committee of notables** (p. 148, folio 151). "بتیس[OCR?] ۳۲ برس کے
  بعد" — after **thirty-two[OCR?] years** — the case was resolved under Egerton: "مسجد کی ودوکانین
  جنکے کرایہ کی آمدنی پانسو روپیے سالانہ ہے سرکار سے واگزار ہوگئین اور رؤسائے اہل اسلام کی کمیٹی
  قرار پائی کہ آمدنی کا روپیہ وہی وصول کرین اور وہی بطرز عمدہ ومناسب صرف کرین" — the mosque's shops,
  **whose rent income is five hundred rupees annually**, were released by the government, and a
  **committee of Muslim notables** was constituted to collect and to spend the money in a fitting
  manner. An itemised income figure and a named administrative form, both rare.
- **What the released income pays for** (p. 148, folio 151): "اب یہ مسجد نہایت آباد ہے امام وموذن
  تنخواہ پاتا ہی[OCR?] مرمت ضروری ہوتی ہے چنانچہ سفیدی[OCR?] کی بہی از سرِ نو کرائی گئی ہے" — the
  mosque is now much frequented, **imam and muezzin draw a salary**, necessary repairs are done, and
  whitewashing[OCR?] has been redone from new. And the endowment has grown: "زمین کی زیادتی کے سبب
  دوکانین بہی زیادہ ہوگئی ہین", because of an increase of land the shops too have increased.
- **The Wazir Khan mosque's waqf, its erosion, and the author's conditional** (p. 158, folio 161).
  "جسقدر جائداد نواب وزیر خان نے مسجد سے لیکر دہلی دروازے تک دو طرفہ بازار کے دوکانین و سرائے و حمام
  تعمیر کرکے وقف کی تہین ان سب کی آمدنی مسجد کے متعلق تہی" — all the property Nawab Wazir Khan built
  and endowed **from the mosque as far as Delhi Gate, on both sides of the bazaar — shops, a sarai
  and a hammam** — its whole income was attached to the mosque. "مگر اب سرائے سرکاری جائداد ہے اور
  حمام بہی سرکار کے متعلق ہے" — but **now the sarai is government property and the hammam too belongs
  to the government**; of the remaining land "بہت سی جائداد منتقل بنام اور لوگوں کے ہوگئی", much was
  transferred into other people's names. What survives is held in the names of the mosque's
  servants: "خادمان[OCR?] مسجد امام و موذن و بہاڑو کش[OCR?] و شمع افروز وغیرہ" — **imam, muezzin,
  sweeper and lamp-lighter** — and even "مسجد کا حوض بہرنے والا بہی دوکانوں کا کرایہ کہا[OCR?] جاتا
  ہے", the man who fills the mosque's tank is said to draw shop-rent. Then the author's judgement:
  **"اگر جائداد کی آمدنی اسکے متعلق نہوتی تو آبادی کی صورت مفقود ہو جاتی"** — had the property's
  income not been attached to it, the very form of its habitation would have been lost.
- **A named mutawalli carrying out repairs** (p. 155, folio 158): "مرزا نور علی متولی و مہتم مسجد نے
  چونے سے کرائی ہے" — Mirza Nur Ali, **mutawalli and mohtamim** of the mosque, had the needed repair
  done with lime. The chunk also records that under the Sikhs no attention was given to repair at
  all, beyond adding two-tier[OCR?] work above the four minarets[OCR?] for fear of earthquake.
- **Waqf used to defeat a confiscation order, and compensated by the emperor** (p. 154, folio 157).
  Warned that Aurangzeb had ordered his dismissal and "اسکی جائداد کی ضبطی", the confiscation of his
  property, Sarfaraz Khan "فی الفور اپنی کل جائداد و غیر منقولہ وقف کردی اور حویلی مسکونہ جو اپنی
  زمین پر تہی گرانی شروع کردی اور مشہور کر دیا کہ مین اسجگہ مسجد بنانا[OCR?] ہون" — at once made
  **waqf of all his property and immovables**, began demolishing his own residential haveli, and gave
  it out that he was building a mosque there. The subah reported this; the emperor "یہ حال سنکر بہت
  خوش ہوا", was much pleased, ruled that "ایسے دیندار شخص کو معزول کرنا [illegible] چاہئے", such a
  religious man should not be dismissed, ordered **replacement property in exchange for what he had
  endowed** ("محالات منزدول[OCR?] ہے جائداد اسکو دی جائے") and restored him to his office. A waqf
  functioning as legal shelter, and the state paying for it.
- **A mosque in sectarian possession, repaired at the holders' expense** (pp. 154–155, folios
  157–158): "اب بہی آباد ہے مگر قبضہ مسلمانان سلمہم وہابیہ کا ہے جو سنی و شیعہ دونو فرقے کے مخالف
  ہین۔ وہابیون نے اب بہت سا روپیہ خرچ کرکے مسجد کی مرمت کی ہے" — it is in use still, but the
  **possession is of the Wahhabis, who are opposed to both the Sunni and the Shia sect**, and they
  have now spent much money on repairing it: a grand new door in place of the old one, and a
  well-proportioned[OCR?] tank "جو نلکہ کے پانی سے پر آب رہتا ہے", kept full from the **tap**.
- **A mosque revived by petition and paid for by a meat contractor** (p. 150, folio 153). Masjid
  Bazar Bi[OCR?] lay "چند سال یہ غیر آباد وپڑی رہی" in the early English administration; "مولوی غلام
  قادر سنے سرکار مین درخواست کرکے اس مسجد کے آباد کرنے کا حکم لیا" — Maulvi Ghulam Qadir petitioned
  the government and obtained an order to bring it back into use; and "امام بخش قصاب سنے جسکے پاس
  ٹہیکہ گوشت رسانی گورون کا تہا" — Imam Bakhsh the butcher, who held the **contract for supplying
  meat to the گورون** (the word as printed; the referent is not stated), restored it and "بہت سا
  روپیہ صرف کرکے اسکی مرمت وسفیدی کرائی", spent much money on its repair and whitewashing.
- **Imamate as heritable office** (p. 150, folio 153): after Ghulam Qadir came "مولوی نظام الدین اس
  مسجد کا امام رہا اور اُسکے مرنے کے بعد اُسکے ودو بیٹے مولوی عزیزالدین وعلی الدین احمد[OCR?] اس
  مسجد کے امام ابتک موجود ہین" — Maulvi Nizam-ud-Din was imam, and after his death **his two sons**
  are the mosque's imams to this day and perform the imamate.
- **A mosque used as a powder magazine** (p. 150, folio 153): "سکہون کیوقت مہاراجہ کی بارود[OCR?]
  اسمین بہری رہتی تہی" — in the Sikhs' time the Maharaja's gunpowder[OCR?] was kept stored in it.
- **A mulla's subsistence from the neighbourhood, with no endowment named** (p. 151, folio 154): at
  the low mosque, "اہل محلہ کی خبر گیری سے مسجد کی رونق اور ملا کا گزارہ ہوتا ہے" — by the care of
  the people of the mohalla the mosque's liveliness and the **mulla's گزارہ** (subsistence) are
  provided. No waqf, no shops **(not stated)**.
- **Mosque land sold by its own imam, and the door stripped** (p. 153, folio 156). At Masjid Chinian
  Wali[OCR?], the eastern gateway's slabs[OCR?] and its stone and marble lintels[OCR?] were taken out
  by Gauhar Singh[OCR?] and the door "منہدم ہو گیا", was demolished; then "زمین کسی طامع امام مسجد
  نے فروخت کر دی جہان نائیوں کی دوکان اور حمام بنا ہوا ہے" — the land was **sold by some greedy imam
  of the mosque**, and a barbers' shop and a hammam now stand on it; entry was afterwards made by
  breaking the northern wall for a small door.
- **A residential teaching establishment attached to the Wazir Khan mosque** (pp. 156–157, folios
  159–160): "اس مسجد کی تین طرف علما و فضلا و طالب علمون کے رہنے کے لئے حجرے بنے ہوئے ہین" — cells
  built on three sides for **ulama, fuzala and students** to live in, the northern cells each with a
  window onto the bazaar; and besides these, "سولہ بڑے پتیسے[OCR?] حجرے مسجد کی دیوار کے چار سو مین
  بنے ہین", **sixteen** large cells built around the four sides of the mosque wall.
- **The Miran Badshah mazar: a mujawir, a Thursday mela, an annual urs with sama** (p. 161,
  folio 164). "زینہ کے آگے ایک چوکہنڈی سی بنی ہوئی ہے جہان مجاور بیٹہتا ہے" — in front of the stair
  a small chaukhandi is built **where the mujawir sits**. "سکہون کے وقت ہر جمعرات کے روز اس مزار پر
  میلہ ہوتا تہا اور طواف لوگ مسجد کے گنبد مین [illegible] چاہتے تہے [illegible] لوگ ہزارون جمع ہوتے
  تہے" — in the Sikhs' time there was a **mela at this mazar every Thursday**, people sought to
  circumambulate in the mosque's dome, and **thousands** gathered. "سالینہ عرس و مجلس سماع بڑی دہوم
  دہام سے ہوتی تہی" — the **annual urs and a majlis-e sama** were held with great pomp. And in the
  present: "اب بہی جمعرات کے روز ہندو مسلمان حاضر ہونے ہیں۔ سالینہ عرس بہی ہوتا ہے" — even now
  **Hindus and Muslims** attend on Thursdays, and the annual urs also takes place. Note that the
  book reports **sama in the past tense** and the urs in the present.
- **A five-hundred-rupee nazr by a ruler, with a vow** (p. 161, folio 164): after his illness the
  Maharaja "مزار پر آیا اور پانسو[OCR?] روپیہ نذر چڑہا کر جبین سائی کی اور توبہ کی کہ پہر ایسی حرکت
  وقوع[OCR?] مین نہ آئیگی" — came to the mazar, **offered five hundred[OCR?] rupees as nazr**, rubbed
  his forehead on it, and repented, vowing the act would not recur. Compare the identical figure,
  five hundred rupees, for the released shop-rents at p. 148 (folio 151) — coincidence of numeral,
  not of case.
- **A children's replica-shrine practice** (p. 150, folio 153). At Pir Bhola's grave: "اب بہی اس
  بزرگ کے مزار پر دن بہر لڑکے کہیلتے ہین قبر کو گہوڑا[OCR?] تصور کرکے اوپر چڑہ بیٹہتے ہین" — boys
  play at the mazar all day and, **imagining the grave a horse**[OCR?], climb up and sit on it. And:
  "ہر ایک جمعہ کے روز لڑکے [illegible] جمع کرکے گلی گلی کوچہ کوچہ پیر بہولا کا مزار بنا لیتے اور
  اُسپر بہول چڑہاتے اور چراغ جلاتے ہین" — **every Friday** the boys gather and **make a mazar of Pir
  Bhola in lane after lane**, offer flowers on it and light lamps. A dispersed, unendowed, entirely
  juvenile cult, recorded as current.
- **The chowk as commercial and religious complex** (p. 158, folio 161). Chowk Wazir Khan has shops
  of solid arched construction and three great arched gates, one facing the mosque, one by the
  mosque's northern stair, one adjoining Raja Dina Nath's haveli; inside it two domes — "ایک خانقاہ
  مسجد معروف کا بنیہ میانا محمد ملطان[OCR?] تہیکہ دار", a **khanqah** of the mosque built by a
  contractor, "اور دو سرا بالا سے چاہ بنیہ راجہ دینا ناتہہ کا", and a **well built by Raja Dina
  Nath**. Under the Chughtai kings the chowk stood open; "سکہون کے عہد مین اس چوک کے اندر دوکانہا و
  نشستگاہ مین بن گئی تہین", under the Sikhs shops and sitting-places were built in it and its form
  changed; "مگر انگریزی عہد مین وہ سب مکانات گرائے گئے اور چوک کی زمین بدستور سابق نکالی گئی" — but
  **in the English period all those buildings were pulled down and the chowk's ground cleared as
  before**, as it stands to this day. A three-regime sequence for one public square.
- **Building and repair dates from inscriptions, as the chunk prints them**:
  - Masjid Pari Mahal, built by Nawab Wazir Khan opposite Shah Alami Gate, small, "صرف اپنی نماز
    پڑہنے کے لئے", only for his own prayers; "یہ مسجد ابتک آباد ہے کبہی ویران نہین ہوئی" — in use to
    this day, never desolate. Date **(not stated)** (p. 151, folio 154).
  - Masjid Bazar Bi[OCR?]: "شاہجہانی وقت کی بنی ہوئی ہے", built in Shahjahani times; exact year
    unreadable (p. 150, folio 153).
  - Masjid Muhammad Salih Kambo: "اس مسجد کی عمارت سنہ ۱۰۵۸[OCR?] مین باہتمام پہنجی[OCR?] … نے اسکو
    تعمیر کیا" — built in **1058 AH**[OCR?]; the third of three niches over the gate carries "سنہ
    ۱۰۵۸[OCR?]", and the other two read "بانی این مسجد زیبا نگار" and "بنائے آل محمد صالح است"
    (p. 152, folio 155).
  - Masjid Chinian Wali[OCR?]: the demolished gateway's couplet "طرفہ معمار خرد تاریخ سال / گفت زیبا
    مسجد از افراز خان[OCR?]" yields "سنہ ۱۰۸۲[OCR?] ہجری", **1082 AH**[OCR?]; "مسجد کے اندر کی محراب
    پر تاریخ تعمیر سنہ ۱۰۸۰[OCR?] تحریر ہے", the inner mihrab carries **1080 AH**[OCR?] (p. 153,
    folio 156).
  - Masjid Wazir Khan: the second inscription reads "در عہد بو المظفر صاحبقران ثانی شاہجہان بادشاہ
    غازی اتمام یافت" — completed in the era of Shah Jahan; the fifth chronogram "سال تاریخ بنائے
    مسجد عالی مقام / از خرد جستم بگفتا سجدہ گاہ اہل فضل" gives "سنہ ۱۰۴۳[OCR?] ہجری", **1043
    AH**[OCR?]; the sixth, "تاریخ این بنائے چو پرسیدم از خرد / گفتا بگو کہ بانی مسجد وزیر خان", gives
    "سنہ ۱۰۴۴[OCR?] ہجری بحساب ابجد", **1044 AH**[OCR?] by abjad; the third names the patron, "بانی
    بیت اللہ ثانی فدوی با خلاص مرید خاص الخاص قدیم الخدمت وزیر خان" (p. 157, folio 160).
- **Two elapsed-time figures given for the age of buildings** (see Doubts for the inconsistency):
  Masjid Muhammad Salih Kambo, "عرصہ دو سو چہبیس ۲۲۶[OCR?] سال" — a lapse of **226[OCR?] years**
  (p. 152, folio 155); Masjid Wazir Khan, "باوجود گذرنے عرصہ دو سو اکیاون سال" — despite the passage
  of **251 years** (p. 155, folio 158).
- **Urban stratigraphy eating mosque endowments.** At Masjid Muhammad Salih Kambo the plinth was high
  with shops beneath, "مگر … اب بازار کی زمین اونچی ہوگئی ہے اور دوکانین آدہی آدہی زمین مین غرق
  ہوگئی ہین" — the bazaar's ground has risen and the shops have sunk half into it; only five steps of
  the plinth remain (p. 152, folio 155). At Masjid Chinian Wali[OCR?] the founder must have kept the
  plinth high with shops beneath the northern wall, "مگر اب کوچہ کی زمین … اسقدر بلند ہو گئی ہے کہ
  مسجد کی کرسی جاتی رہی ہے", but the lane has risen so far that the plinth is gone and the northern
  window that stood a storey up now clears about **two feet** (p. 153, folio 156). Rising street
  levels are recorded as a direct loss of rentable shop frontage.
- **Water and sanitation as endowed infrastructure**: the low mosque's water "باہر کہین نہین جاتا
  اور غرقیان[OCR?] اسی جگہ بنی ہوئی ہین", never leaves the site, with soak-pits[OCR?] on the spot
  (p. 151, folio 154); the Wazir Khan mosque's attached courtyard holds "حوض ایک سو گز مربع", a tank
  of **one hundred square yards** (p. 156, folio 159); Masjid Chinian Wali[OCR?] once had a tank now
  vanished but "قدیمی چاہ موجود و ہے", the ancient well survives (p. 154, folio 157).
- **A saint's refusal of state office, and a death sentence for it** (p. 149, folio 152). The king,
  visiting Lahore from Multan, "اسکو چاہا کہ شہر کا قاضی مقرر کرے مگر اس سنے وہ عہدہ منظور نکیا" —
  wished to appoint Pir Siraj-ud-Din **qazi of the city**, and he refused the office; for this
  "عدول حکمی" the king ordered him beheaded in the crossroads bazaar, and the ulama who had brought
  him to court obtained his pardon by much pleading. "اُسروز سے اس نے تعلیم وتلقین[OCR?] کا دروازہ
  بند کر دیا اور تارک الدنیا ہوکر گوشہ مین ہو بیٹہا" — from that day he **closed the door of teaching
  and instruction** and sat in a corner as a renunciant. A refusal of state patronage paid for with a
  teaching career.
- **Physician's fees at court, itemised** (p. 160, folio 163). On the day of Nur Jahan's "غسل صحت",
  bath of recovery: "بادشاہ نے ایک لاکہ روپیہ کا خلعت حکیم کو دیا اور سات لاکہ روپیہ نقد مرحمت کیا" —
  a robe of honour worth **one lakh rupees** and **seven lakh rupees** in cash; the queen gave him all
  the jewellery she was wearing, and eleven[OCR?] of her attendant slave-girls gave theirs in
  "تصدق" for her, which also went to the hakim; "غرض اس روز بایس[OCR?] لاکہ روپیے کی جمع حکیم کے پاس
  ہو گئی" — in sum **twenty-two[OCR?] lakh rupees** accumulated with him in one day. He had already
  received "شاہی شفا خانون کی افسری", **superintendence of the royal hospitals**, from the day of the
  operation.
- **Visitors and copyists as a form of shrine traffic** (p. 155, folio 158): of the Wazir Khan
  mosque's tilework, "بڑے بڑے نقاش نقاشی کا سبق اسسے لیتے ہین اور دور دور کے مسافر لاہور مین آ کر
  اون طاقچون کی نقلین اتار کر لیجاتے ہین" — great painters take their lessons in painting from it,
  and **travellers from far off come to Lahore and carry away copies of those niches**.

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **Miran Badshah's wrath brings down a ruler, and the book credits it with saving the mosque**
  (p. 161, folio 164). "ایک مرتبہ مہاراجہ رنجیت سنگہ موران [illegible] کو ساتہہ[OCR?] لیکر اس مسجد
  کے مینار پر آیا اور درون بہر اور پردہ کر کے عیش و نشاط مین سرگرم رہا۔ اتفاقاً اسی شب کو بیمار ہو
  گیا" — Maharaja Ranjit Singh came onto the mosque's minaret with Moran, screened himself, and gave
  himself to pleasure and revelry; that very night he fell ill. **"لوگون نے کہا کہ یہ آثار غضب اس
  بزرگ کے ہین جسکی مسجد وزیر خان مین قبر ہے"** — people said these were the marks of the wrath of the
  elder whose grave is in the Wazir Khan mosque. The nazr and vow follow (see Practices). The book
  attributes the diagnosis to "لوگون" and the protection to its own judgement: "فی الحقیقت اس بزرگ کے
  تصدق سے سکہی عہد مین بہی یہ مسجد سکہون کے دست برد سے حفاظت مین رہی" — in truth, by this elder's
  blessing the mosque remained safe from Sikh depredation even in the Sikh era.
- **Sweets from nowhere** (p. 150, folio 153). Of Pir Bhola: "اکثر اوقات وہ اپنی بغل سے شیرینی نکال
  کر لڑکون کو بانٹتا اور معلوم نہوتا کہ یہ شیرینی کہان رکہی ہو" — he would often draw sweets from
  under his arm and share them among the boys, and it was not known where the sweets had been kept.
  The book gives this as the ground of his reputation, attributed to the public: "اس بات سے اُسکو لوگ
  صاحب کمال تصور کرتے تہے" — from this, people considered him a man of perfection.
- **No karamat is told of Pir Siraj-ud-Din, of Nawab Bukhari Khan, or of any mosque founder** in this
  chunk; the reprieve from beheading is narrated as intercession by the ulama, not as a marvel
  (p. 149, folio 152).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The author's own valuation of the Wazir Khan mosque, argued from foreign esteem** (p. 155,
  folio 158): "جسکی شہرت ولایتیون مین ہے", its fame is among the Europeans; "ایسی خشتی کانسی[OCR?]
  کا عمدہ عمارت دوسری جگہ کم دیکہی جاوگی بلکہ اس مسجد کو لاہور کا فخر و اقتدار کہا جائے تو سجا[OCR?]
  ہے" — **if this mosque be called the pride and dignity of Lahore it would be fitting**; and "بڑے
  بڑے خوشنویس و لایتیون کے اسکو دیکہ کر دنگ ہو جاتے ہین", great calligraphers and Europeans are
  astounded on seeing it. The engineer-author's aesthetic judgement is repeatedly warranted by
  European opinion.
- **He reconciles two conflicting inscription dates rather than choosing** (p. 153, folio 156): the
  outer gateway's chronogram gives 1082[OCR?] and the inner mihrab 1080[OCR?], "جس سے پایا جاتا ہے کہ
  اندر کی عمارت اور دروازہ بیرونی کی تعمیر مین دو سال کا عرصہ گزرا تہا" — from which it is found that
  **two years passed** between the building of the interior and of the outer gateway. A
  contradiction turned into a construction sequence.
- **He marks the limits of his own evidence.** Of Masjid Bazar Bi[OCR?]'s founder: the name is in the
  inscription "مگر سنہ پڑہا نہین جاتا", but the year **cannot be read** (p. 150, folio 153). Of the
  Chinian Wali[OCR?] gateway's lost couplet, his source is oral: "معمر و بزرگون کی زبان سے یہ سنا
  جاتا ہے" — it is heard from the tongues of the aged and the elders (p. 153, folio 156). Of Pir
  Shirazi's second name, he reports it as others' usage, not his own: "بعض لوگ[OCR?] … پیر شیرازی کے
  نام سے مشہور کرتے ہین" (p. 149, folio 152).
- **He reconstructs vanished features in the presumptive mood** (p. 153, folio 156): "بوقت تعمیر اس
  مسجد کے بانی نے مسجد کی کرسی بہت اونچی رکہی ہوگی اور شمالی دیوار کے نیچے بنے دوکانین بہی ہونگی" —
  at the time of building the founder **must have** kept the plinth very high and there **must have**
  been shops below the northern wall. An explicit inference, flagged as such by the grammar.
- **A murdered official is declared a martyr** (p. 149, folio 152): "وہ نیکو کار جوان اس ستمگار عورت
  کے ہاتہہ سے بدرجہ[OCR?] شہادت پہنچا یعنی ناحق مارا گیا" — that virtuous youth **attained the degree
  of martyrdom** at the hand of that tyrannous woman, that is, he was killed unjustly. The author's
  own gloss ("یعنی") converts the theological term into a legal one.
- **Endowment income is treated as the cause of a mosque's survival** (p. 158, folio 161): the
  counterfactual quoted under Practices is the chunk's clearest statement of the author's economic
  reading of religious architecture — habitation follows revenue.
- **Sikh rule is characterised as the period of loss and English rule as the period of restitution**,
  across four separate cases in this chunk: the confiscated shops (p. 148, folio 151), the powder
  magazine (p. 150, folio 153), the stripped gateway (p. 153, folio 156) and the encroached chowk
  (p. 158, folio 161). The pattern is the author's, not any one source's, and should be cited as his
  framing rather than as an independent finding.

## Doubts

- **The mosque the chunk opens on is unnamed here.** Page 148 (folio 151) begins mid-sentence; the
  mosque's section heading and its identification fall on a page before this chunk. Everything under
  the Baoli Sahib bullet and the first three Practices bullets therefore belongs to **a mosque this
  chunk does not name**, identified only by its founder Nawab Bukhari Khan and by its adjacency to
  "باولی صاحب". **A human must read the preceding chunk before this material is attached to any
  site.**
- **The Baoli Sahib identification rests on the name and the Granth Sahib alone.** The chunk writes
  only "باولی صاحب کے پاس جہان گرنتہہ صاحب رکہا ہو" (p. 148, folio 151) — no quarter, no bazaar, no
  founder, no date. The archive row **gurdwara-baoli-sahib-guru-arjan-dev-ji-lahore** is placed at
  Dabbi Bazaar in the Walled City; the chunk supplies nothing that confirms or excludes that. Carry
  the mapping as provisional.
- **"میران بادشاہ" must not be mapped to any archive row.** The chunk's figure is "سید اسحاق گاذرونی
  المشہور میران بادشاہ" — Sayyid Ishaq Gazaruni[OCR?], known as Miran Badshah — buried in a tahkhana
  inside the Wazir Khan mosque (pp. 156, 161; folios 159, 164). The archive carries two Lahore rows
  whose names share the element *Miran*: **shrine-of-miran-hussain-zanjani-zanjani-sahib** (Syed
  Miran Hussain Zanjani, Chah Miran) and **shrine-of-mauj-darya-bukhari** (Hazrat Meeran Muhammad
  Shah Bukhari). The chunk's name, nisba and location match neither, and it gives no death date to
  test against either row. **Leave unmapped and treat as a candidate for a new row**; note that the
  shared element plus the shared city makes this a likely future mis-match.
- **The Maharaja of p. 148 is not named.** The mosque-shops settlement is attributed only to
  "مہاراجہ" (p. 148, folio 151); **Maharaja Ranjit Singh** is named for the first and only time at
  p. 161 (folio 164). Do not merge the two passages into one attribution, and do not attach either to
  **samadhi-of-maharaja-ranjit-singh** — the chunk never mentions the samadhi.
- **The two elapsed-age figures are mutually inconsistent.** The chunk dates Masjid Muhammad Salih
  Kambo to 1058 AH[OCR?] and calls it 226[OCR?] years old (p. 152, folio 155); it dates Masjid Wazir
  Khan to 1043–1044 AH[OCR?] and calls it 251 years old (p. 155, folio 158). The inscription dates are
  about fourteen or fifteen years apart while the elapsed figures are twenty-five years apart, so the
