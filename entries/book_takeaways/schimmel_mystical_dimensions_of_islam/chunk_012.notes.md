## Practices, institutions, economy

- Obedience to the sheikh is owed regardless of his character, on the Iblīs analogy: `the same applies
  to the disciple who has to honor and obey his sheikh, even if the sheikh is a veritable satan`
  (p. 224, folio 193).
- A hierarchy of living saints is described as an office-holding structure: `The highest spiritual
  authority is the, qutb, "axis, pole," or ghauth, "help." He is surrounded by three nuqabff`
  [text-layer?: nuqabāʾ]`, "substitutes," four autad, "pillars," seven abrdr` [text-layer?: abrār]`,
  "pious," forty abddl` [text-layer?: abdāl]`, "substitutes," three hundred akhydr` [text-layer?:
  akhyār]`, "good," and four thousand hidden saints.` (p. 231, folio 200).
- A competing count is recorded beside it, not reconciled: `Some authorities, e.g., Ibn cArabi, claim
  that there are seven abddl, one for each of the seven climates` (p. 231, folio 200), footnoted to
  `Muhyiuddm Ibn cArab!, Al-futuhdt al-makkiyya, 4 vols. (Cairo, 1329 h./ign)` [text-layer?: 1911]`,
  chap. 198, fasl 31.` (p. 231, folio 200).
- `Not a few mystics claimed to be the qutb of their time, and quite a number of them assumed the
  role of the Mahdi, the manifestation of the hidden imam at the end of time.` (p. 231, folio 200).
- Place-guardianship by a group of saints, and group veneration: `The Seven are mentioned in some
  towns as the spiritual guardians of a sacred place… Often, seven saintly persons or virgins are
  treated as a single unit in veneration (that can be the case with the Forty as well).` (p. 233,
  folio 202).
- The *abdāl* title is explained as a succession practice: `Later, the word was used to designate a
  saint who after his death would be "substituted" for (badal) by another person.` (p. 233,
  folio 202).
- A named rank above the *quṭb* is claimed dynastically: Sirhindi `claimed for himself and three of
  his descendants the rank of the qayyum` (p. 234, folio 203).
- Saint veneration as an object of modernist attack: `It is because of these darker sides of Muslim
  saint worship—which are scarcely compatible with the basic teaching of Islam—that Sufism has come
  into disrepute among many Muslim modernists, who see only the negative aspects as they are
  manifested at the lower levels of popular piety.` (p. 236, folio 205).
- **Fertility practice at shrines, stated as worldwide and explicitly not only Islamic**: `the hopes
  of barren women are expressed all over the world—not only in Islamic lands—by the same magical
  means: by binding little cradles at the gate of the saint's abode, by taking vows, by "selling" the
  child to the saint (in Turkey many boys are called Satilniish` [text-layer?: the Turkish word for
  "sold"; the printed form is damaged and is not reconstructed here]`, "sold," or girls` — the
  sentence is cut off by the end of the chunk (p. 237, folio 206).
- `tawajjuh, the concentration of two partners on` (p. 236, folio 205) `each other, is necessary to
  produce such an effect; it is taken for granted that the sheikh and his disciple are, so to speak,
  on the same wave length.` (p. 237, folio 206).
- A named manuscript provenance: `British Museum, Or. 1362` (p. 232, folio not stated).



## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- The technical distinction, given as the theologians': `the saint's miracles are called karamat,
  "charismata," whereas the prophet's miracles are classified as rmfjizat` [text-layer?: muʿjizāt]`,
  "what renders others incapable to do the same," and the two types must never be confused` (cited
  by Schimmel as `K 59-61`) (p. 237, folio 206).
- `The general term for anything extraordinary is khariq ul-^dda` [text-layer?: khāriq al-ʿāda]`,
  "what tears the custom" (of God)` (p. 237, folio 206).
- `Subki distinguishes twenty-five main types` of miracle, and `whole collections have been composed
  to show the various kinds of miracles performed by Muslim saints.` (p. 237, folio 206).
- Attributed by Schimmel to Jāmī's hagiography: a saint is one of whom it is said `"he was heard when
  he prayed," or "such as he said would definitely come true," or "when he became angry God Almighty
  would quickly take revenge for his sake"` (cited as `N 506`) (p. 236, folio 205).
- *Firāsa*: `"Beware of the firdsa [discernment] of the faithful," it was said, "for he sees by God's
  light"` (cited as `B 588`) (p. 236, folio 205); `Innumerable stories are told about a sheikh's
  insight into his disciple's heart` (p. 236, folio 205); `There were even some who claimed to be
  able to discern whether a person was destined for Heaven or Hell` (cited as `N 439`) (p. 236,
  folio 205).
- `The saint was able to disappear from sight, to become completely invisible, and to practice buruz,
  exteriorization, i.e., he could be present at different places at the same time.` (p. 236,
  folio 205).
- Attributed by Schimmel to legend: `According to legend, Rumi attended seventeen parties at one time
  and wrote a poem at each one!` (p. 236, folio 205), footnoted to `Johann Karl Teufel, Eine
  Lebensbeschreibung des Scheichs ^Ali-i fiamadani` [text-layer?: the name's initial letter is
  damaged; not reconstructed] `(Leiden, 1962), p. 108.` (p. 236, folio 205).
- *Tayy al-makān*: `In cases of danger the sheikh might suddenly appear in the midst of a band of
  robbers to drive them away or assume the shape of the ruler in order to protect a disciple who
  called for help` (p. 236, folio 205).
- Reported by Schimmel from her own fieldwork: `or—as I was told several times in Turkey—the master
  might appear, in spiritualized form, at a sick person's bed in order to cure him or at least
  relieve him temporarily from his pain.` (p. 236, folio 205).
- `"To enter under the burden of the sick"` (cited as `N 397`) `was a miracle often performed by
  Sufis.` (p. 236, folio 205).
- Vicarious suffering: `when the disciple is hurt, the master's body shows the traces. Or, as in the
  story of Bayezid Bistami, when his disciples tried to kill him, the wounds appeared on their own
  bodies.` (p. 237, folio 206).
- Attributed to one of Hujwīrī's unnamed masters: `"To traverse distance is child's play: henceforth
  pay visits by means of thought; it is not worth while to visit any person, and there is no virtue
  in bodily presence"` (cited as `H 235`) (p. 237, folio 206).
- The saints' nightly patrol of the world, quoted from Hujwīrī's book: `Every night the autad must go
  round the whole universe… they must then inform the Qutb, in order that he may fix his attention on
  the weak spot and that by his blessing the imperfection may be removed.` (cited as `H 228`)
  (p. 234, folio 203).
- Agricultural and military benefits of saints: `It is through the blessings of the saints that rain
  falls from heaven; on the places touched by their sacred feet plants spring up, and thanks to their
  help the Muslims will gain victory over the unbelievers.` (p. 234, folio 203).
- Saints' concealment: `"Out of jealousy, God puts a veil upon them and keeps them concealed from the
  public"` (cited as `N 442`) and `The hadith qudsi, "verily my saints are under my domes, and only I
  know them," is often cited to support this idea.` (p. 234, folio 203).
- Secret assemblies: `they recognize fellow saints without ever having met them, and many stories are
  told about secret meetings of saints on certain mountains in which, sometimes, an adept is allowed
  to partake.` (p. 233, folio 202).
- A folklore motif Schimmel names as such: `such as the idea that a saint can grant a hundred-year-
  old couple three sons only to call them back when the parents do not behave decently.` (p. 237,
  folio 206).
- `Fertility miracles are one of the most popular aspects of saint worship` (p. 237, folio 206).
- Baqlī's 360: `three hundred persons whose hearts are like Adam's heart, forty whose hearts are like
  Moses's heart, seven whose hearts are fashioned after Abraham's heart, five whose hearts correspond
