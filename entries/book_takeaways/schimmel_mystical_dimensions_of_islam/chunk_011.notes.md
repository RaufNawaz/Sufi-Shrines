## Practices, institutions, economy


**Initiation and the transmission of the dhikr.**

- `the talqln adh-dhikr, the teaching of the formula of recollection, was one of the most important
  aspects of mystical initiation and was later developed into a complicated art.` (p. 200, folio 169).
- `Only a dhikr properly inspired by the spiritual director, and constantly controlled by him, was
  effective` (p. 200, folio 169).
- `The dhikr could be learned, if not from a living master, then from Khidr, the mysterious guide of
  the wayfarers; but it had to be a proper initiation. The formula has to be transmitted through the
  chain of spiritual leaders that goes back to the Prophet himself or to an angelic inspirer of
  Muhammad.` (p. 200, folio 169).
- The techniques are sometimes traced to the first caliphs: `the Prophet allegedly taught Abu Bakr the
  silent dhikr when he was with him in the cave during his emigration to Medina, whereas CAH`
  [text-layer?: ʿAlī] `was granted the loud dhikr.` (p. 200, folio 169).
- `The mystical leader had to decide, first, which kind of dhikr was fitting for the spiritual stage of
  his disciple.` (p. 201, folio 170).

**Equipment, posture, cell.**

- `Since the ninth century the rosary (tasblh, subha) has been used to count the repetitions of the
  formulae, yet a true mystic would regard this medium as a toy "to attract heedlessness" (N 79)`
  (p. 200, folio 169).
- `The rosary, with its thirty-three or ninety-nine beads, is used for counting the names.` (p. 208,
  folio 177).
- Posture, per `Simnani (d. 1336)`: `the mystic should sit cross-legged, the right hand on the left,
  and the left hand keeping the right leg in position on top of the left leg (CL i is)` — and `Even if
  the sitting style might vary according to the orders, the correct position was considered of great
  importance for the success of the dhikr, and the manuals carefully explain the relevant rules.`
  (p. 201, folio 170).
- The cell and the master's image: `The adept must not only purify himself inwardly and outwardly
  before entering the cell to perform his dhikr, but should keep the image of his sheikh before his
  eyes for spiritual help during the recollection. This practice is, apparently, not of very early
  origin, but was elaborated by the orders.` (p. 201, folio 170).
- A later handbook `orders the Sufi to drink water after performing the dhikr to cool down the internal
  heat that he has produced` (p. 201, folio 170).

**Visitation of tombs — the one directly shrine-focused practice in this chunk.**

- `The Naqshbandi masters considered it useful to visit the tombs of deceased saints and to concentrate
  upon the spirit of particular departed Sufis in order to increase their spiritual strength. By
  directing one's thought to the spirit of this or that mystic—instead of concentrating upon one's
  sheikh exclusively—a mystic might achieve a greater sublimation of the spirit, according to the power
  of the saint in question.` (p. 206, folio 175). No shrine, region or order-house is named, no ʿurs,
  no offering, no custodian, no endowment — all `(not stated)`.

**Loud versus silent dhikr, and its institutional consequences.**

- `dhikr is generally divided into two branches: recollection with the tongue (dhikr jail, jahrl,
  calanlya, lisani)` [text-layer?: jalī, jahrī, ʿalāniya, lisānī] `and recollection in the heart (dhikr
  khafi, qalbT)` [text-layer?: khafī, qalbī] (p. 202, folio 171).
- `The problem of whether the true dhikr should be hidden or spoken aloud caused a major split in the
  Naqshbandi order in Central Asia in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries; it had grave political
  consequences resulting in religious wars in Sinkiang.` (p. 206, folio 175).
- `although silent dhikr was preferred by most of the Naqshbandi leaders, one of the fourteenth-century
  masters had answered the question whether loud dhikr was licit by quoting the example of the talqin,
  the loud recitation of the profession of faith in a dying person's ear, "and for the dervish, every
  breath is his last breath" (N 391)` (p. 206, folio 175).
- Against the loud dhikr: `They also held that the loud dhikr pronounced by a small group could disturb
  the people in the mosque who were absorbed in silent meditation.` (pp. 206–207, folios 175–176).
- The halqa: `Often the Sufis would join in a halqa, a circle formed around a qutb, "pivot"; they would
  put right hands on the left hands of their neighbors and with closed eyes repeat the shahada until it
  consisted merely of the last h.` (p. 207, folio 176; the footnote marker after `h.` is printed `7S`
  [text-layer?: 78]).
- `the dhikr-i arra, the "sawing dhikr," practiced primarily among Central Asian and Turkish orders.
  The heavy sounds that come from the larynx are indeed reminiscent of a crosscut saw.` (p. 207,
  folio 176).
- `Unfortunately, in some Muslim countries dhikr sessions of this type have been used lately as tourist
  attractions.` (p. 207, folio 176).

**Breath control and bodily technique.**

- `Dhikr in its developed forms is usually connected with some sort of breath control.` (p. 204,
  folio 173).
- Jāmī's record of the early Naqshbandi practice, from `Khwaja cAttar`: `"The purpose in dhikr is not
  to speak much; one says in one breath three times la Hah ilia Allah` [text-layer?: lā ilāh illā
  Allāh — `ilāh` is printed `Hah`]`, beginning from the right side, and brings it down to the heart,
  and brings forth Muhammad rasul Allah from the left side. A ninefold or eighteenfold repetition in
  one breath is also possible" (N 390-91)` (p. 205, folio 174).
- `In the later Middle Ages, especially in Afghanistan and India, the habs-i dam, holding the breath
  for a very long time, was practiced—a disputed technique that may show influence from Indian
  asceticism.` (p. 205, folio 174).
- `The Naqshbandi tradition has taught the dhikr of the five latffif` [text-layer?: laṭāʾif]`, five
  subtle points in the body upon which the mystic has to concentrate his recollection until his whole
  being is transformed.` (p. 205, folio 174). Mir Dard's five points and the terminal `dhikr sultani,
  the royal recollection` are set out at pp. 205–206, folios 174–175.
- Indian ascetic borrowings: `Some ascetics among the Indian Sufis added to the difficulty of their
  task by sitting in front of a fire on a summer day so that the heat was, in fact, intolerable for
  anybody—a custom inherited from Hindu ascetics.` (p. 201, folio 170).

**Devotional formulae, reward and the divine names.**

- `Manuals of piety, like Ghazzali's Ihyff 'uliim ad-din` [text-layer?: *Iḥyāʾ ʿulūm ad-dīn*]`, teach a
  number of dhikr formulae, along with exact prescriptions as to how often each of these formulae
  should be repeated in order to bear fruit in Paradise (G 1:265, 302 ff).` (p. 207, folio 176).
- `Reciting the profession of faith seven thousand times can save the mystic himself` (p. 207,
  folio 176) `or any other person from hellfire (N 531). These ideas were, and still are, widely
  accepted by the masses.` (p. 208, folio 177).
- `Many a pious old woman will never get up from her ritual prayer without adding to it a number of
  dhikr formulae` (p. 208, folio 177).
- `The usual collection of these names of glorification comprises ninety-nine names. The Greatest Name
  is hidden, but many a mystic has claimed that he possesses it and that it enables him to perform
  every kind of miracle.` (p. 208, folio 177).
- Named rules for individual divine names, `a few examples given by Ibn ^ta3 Allah`: `al-Hadl, "the
  Guiding One," must work upon the adept when he turns to God in his dhikr (N 410)`; `al-Latlf` in
  seclusion; `Al-Hdfiz` to preserve a state; `Al-Wadud` to be loved by all creatures; and `The name
  al-Fffiq` [text-layer?: al-Fāʾiq]`, "The Overpowering," should never be used by the beginner, but only
  by the gnostic in a high station.` (pp. 208–209, folios 177–178). `A wrong application of a divine
  name was considered to produce grave consequences for the person who used it or for those close to
  him.` (p. 209, folio 178).
- `the master could also give his disciples, or those who called upon him, rather long formulae to be
  recited after the ritual prayer or in case of special need (wird, aurdd, or hizb). Many of these
  prayers are credited with supernatural powers, and the most famous one is probably the hizb al-bahr,
  attributed to ash-Shadhill and used mainly during travel as a protective charm.` (p. 209, folio 178).

**Samāʿ as an institution.**

- `This mystical dance was noted by the first European visitors to the convents of the Mevlevis, the
  Whirling Dervishes. For the Mevlevi tarlqa is the only order in which this whirling movement has been
  institutionalized, though it has been practiced throughout the world of Islam from early times.`
  (p. 210, folio 179).
- `Orthodoxy would ban every musical and rhythmical movement, and some of the sober orders, like the
  Naqshbandiyya, followed their example. Others saw in samcf an outlet for the religious feeling of the
  pious—and it was this musical side of some of the fraternities that attracted large masses.`
  (p. 211, folio 180).
- `Samcfkhanas` [text-layer?: samāʿkhānas]`, houses in which the Sufis could listen to music and let
  themselves be drawn into ecstatic states, were founded in Baghdad as early as the second half of the
  ninth century. The orthodox were scandalized by the sight of what took place there.` (p. 212,
  folio 181).
- `They very much objected, among other things, to the rending of the garments that frequently happened
  in samaf.` (p. 212, folio 181), and to the participation of `charming young boys` in the later period
  (p. 212, folio 181).
- Restriction on novices: `It is more desirable that beginners should not be allowed to attend musical
  concerts lest their natures become depraved. (H 416, 430)` (p. 212, folio 181) — Schimmel adds that
  `This last sentence would be subscribed to by all spiritual directors in the moderate orders`
  (p. 213, folio 182).
- Rūzbihān's three requisites for perfect spiritual enjoyment: `fine scents, a beautiful face to look
  at, and a lovely voice (N 256)—the beauty of the singer is a prerequisite for spiritual happiness.`
  (p. 213, folio 182).
- `In India, samcf was advocated by the Chishtl order.` (p. 216, folio 185).
- Modern Turkish state practice: `The activities of the Mevlevi dervishes in Turkey, along with those
  of the other orders, were banned by Atatiirk in 1925; since 1954 they have been allowed to perform
  the samff on the occasion of the anniversary of Rumi's death on December 17 in Konya, though not in
  the central sanctuary (they have even performed outside the country).` (p. 216, folio 185). `1925`,
  `1954` and `December 17` are printed cleanly.

## Legends and miracles (karamat)


- Sahl at-Tustarī's disciple, trained to unceasing dhikr, is struck on the head by a falling piece of
  wood: `The drops of blood which trickled to the ground bore the legend 'Allah! Allah! Allah!"` —
  cited by Schimmel as `(H 195)` (p. 200, folio 169). Schimmel introduces it as a story `we may well
  believe`.
- Sufis `completely drowned in sweat when uttering the shahada (N 166)` and `able to melt the snow
  around them because of the heat of their meditation (N 138)`; Schimmel states `There is no reason to
  doubt stories according to which` these things happened (p. 201, folio 170).
- Qushayrī's *Tartīb as-sulūk* describes limbs that `first they move, and then their movements grow
  stronger until they become voices and sounds; these voices, uttering words of dhikr, can be heard
  coming from the mystic's whole body, except from his tongue.` (p. 204, folio 173).
- `the grotesque stories told about several Sufis of India. Their limbs became separated from them
  during the dhikr and recollected God each in its own Way.` (p. 204, folio 173), sourced in footnote
  72 to Dārā Shikōh, Aʿzam Tattawī and Mīr Dard.
- `al-Haddad`, absorbed in a blind man's Koran recitation in the bazaar, `put his hand into the fire,
  and without using the pincers, drew out a piece of molten iron from the furnace` — cited as `(H 124)`
  (p. 211, folio 180).
- In samāʿ: `Some have died in ecstasy (N 195); others are said to have just disappeared in the air in
  a flight produced by their spinning movement (N 146); or, as the legends tell, a negro would turn
  white as long as his ecstatic state continued (N 102, 164, 37).` (p. 212, folio 181).
- Junayd's reply to Nūrī while sitting still during the whirling dance: `"You see the mountains—you
  think them firm, yet they move like clouds"`, cited as `(Sura 27:90; N 188)` (p. 212, folio 181).
- Rūzbihān's story of the thieves: `"A certain mystic saw a group of thieves who drank wine and played
  tambour. He went and sat with them and listened to that music as if it were a samcf. The thieves
  enjoyed his attitude so much that all were converted"` — cited as `(B 597)` (p. 213, folio 182).
- Rūmī on the doors of Paradise: `"A certain man objected to Rumi's statement and said that he disliked
  the sound of scratching doors. Whereupon the master replied: 'You hear the doors when they close—I
  hear them when they are opened' "` — cited as `(N 462)` (p. 214, folio 183).
- `True samac is also "a dance in blood"—an allusion to the legend that Hallaj danced in his fetters on
  the way to the place of execution` — cited as `(M 3:96)` (p. 215, folio 184). Schimmel marks it `the
  legend`.



## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)


- Schimmel adopts Nwyia's terminology for wajd: `Nwyia has proposed (W 254) calling this state
  "instasy" instead of "ecstasy" since the mystic is not carried out of himself but rather into the
  depths of himself` (p. 209, folio 178).
- On Indian and Shamanist influence, three separate judgements, each hedged: the fire-sitting of Indian
  Sufis is `a custom inherited from Hindu ascetics` and `an exaggeration of dhikr practices` (p. 201,
  folio 170); the limb-separation experience `is known from Shamanism, but was apparently not rare
  among later Sufis, mainly in the Subcontinent` (p. 204, folio 173); `habs-i dam` is `a disputed
  technique that may show influence from Indian asceticism` (p. 205, folio 174). She also parallels the
  dhikr heat with `the tapas, the ascetic "heat" of the Hindu ascetics, and about Christian saints`
  (p. 201, folio 170).
- Comparative framing of dhikr technique: `everyone who has practiced dhikr in one religious tradition
  or the other—be it the Jesus prayer of the Greek monks,` (p. 204, folio 173) `or the Namu Amida Butsu
  of Amida Buddhism—knows that concentration without control of breathing is scarcely possible.`
  (p. 205, folio 174). She states that `One cannot determine the exact dates when different methods of
  breath control were adopted into Sufism` (p. 204, folio 173).
- The `"sawing dhikr"` is offered, on Fletcher's authority, as `"the Yesewiya answer to the ecstatic
  Shamanist dances."` (p. 207, folio 176, footnote 79).
- On the antiquity of dance as a religious act: `dancing and whirling belong to the oldest religious
  acts of all. Dance is the "absolute play"` ... `The encircling of a sacred object—or a person, as
  sometimes in the sama'—means to partake of its magical power or to endow it with power.` (p. 210,
  folio 179). This is the chunk's only statement bearing on circumambulation of a person or object.
- Christian comparison: `"Where there is dance, there is Satan," said St. Chrysostom, and this could as
  well have been the verdict of an orthodox Muslim theologian.` (p. 211, folio 180).
- She dates the Junayd–Nūrī episode by inference: `This episode must have occurred about the year 900.`
  (p. 212, folio 181).
- On the practice of holding the sheikh's image: `This practice is, apparently, not of very early
  origin, but was elaborated by the orders.` (p. 201, folio 170).
