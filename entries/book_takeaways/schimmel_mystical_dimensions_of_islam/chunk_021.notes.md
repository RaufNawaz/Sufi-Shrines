**What this chunk is.** Seventeen continuous pages inside chapter 8, `SUFISM IN INDO-PAKISTAN`
(folio 355–371). It carries: the closing paragraph of the Sehwan / Lal Shahbaz passage (p. 386);
the growth of the orders in India and the Shattariyya (p. 386); Indo-Muslim Sufi literary
production — `malfuzdt`, order histories, letter collections (pp. 387–388); the reception of
Ibn ʿArabī in India (p. 388); the question of Hindu–Muslim mutual influence (pp. 388–389); the
Moguls from Babur to Aurangzeb, with Akbar (pp. 389–391) and Dara Shikoh (pp. 391–394) at the
centre; then, from a mid-page heading on p. 394, the section **`THE "NAQSHBANDI REACTION"`**
(pp. 394–402), covering Naqshbandi origins in Central Asia, Ahmad Sirhindi, the `qayyum`
doctrine, and the beginnings of Urdu poetry. Footnotes **14 through 39** are printed at the foot
of these pages, and several carry content, not just references.

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The Sehwan fair.** `The "fair" of Sehwan—celebrated between Shawwal 18 and 20` [text-layer?:
  clean] `—has become notorious for its rather illicit events.` (p. 386, folio 355). The book uses
  `"fair"` in quotation marks and never the word *urs*; no year, no attendance, no organiser.
- **Malangs at Sehwan.** `strange groups of bl-shar^` [text-layer?: bī-sharʿ] `, "lawless," Sufis
  (malang) gathered, dressed in black` around the sanctuary (p. 386, folio 355) — an institution
  described by dress and by legal status.
- **A lingam beside a Muslim tomb.** `A lingam close to the actual tomb proves the Shivait
  background of some of the rites.` (p. 386, folio 355).
- **`malfuzdt` as an institution.** `In India, a special literary genre became very popular—the
  so-called malfuzdt` [text-layer?: malfūẓāt] `, collections of sayings of the spiritual
  preceptors.` `The Indian Sufis, however, carefully collected the dicta of their masters from day
  to day` (p. 387, folio 356). `In later times, collections of malfuzdt were fabricated with more
  or less pious intent.` (p. 387, folio 356).
- **Order histories.** `Some other mystics set out to write histories of their orders` — Mir
  Khurd's `Siyar al-auliyff` is `still the most trustworthy account of the Chishti silsila and of
  medieval Chishti khdnqdh` [text-layer?: khānqāh] `life` (p. 387, folio 356).
- **Letters as an institution and as politics.** `The collections of the letters of Muslim Indian
  Sufis from the thirteenth century on are an extremely important source` and, `as in the case of
  Ahmad Sirhindl, have played a remarkable role even in politics.` (p. 387, folio 356). Sirhindi's
  `fame rests chiefly upon his 534 letters, of which 70 are addressed to Mogul officials. They
  were, like many letters by mystical leaders, intended for circulation, with only a few of them
  meant for his closest friends.` `The letters have been translated into Arabic, Turkish, and Urdu
  from the original Persian` (p. 398, folio 367).
- **Mogul patronage and building.** Akbar `built a magnificent tomb` for Muhammad Ghauth Gwaliori
  (p. 386, folio 355). `In gratitude for the blessings of this saint, Akbar erected a wonderful
  Sufi dargah in his new capital Fathpur Sikri.` `When Jihangir grew up, he adorned Ajmer,
  Mucinuddm Chishti's city, with beautiful buildings of white marble.` `The close association of
  the Chishtiyya with the Moguls, so alien to their previous antigovernment attitude, is reflected
  in many stories about Akbar and his successors.` (p. 391, folio 360).
- **Ascetic practice and its provenance.** `the strict vegetarianism of some of the Indian Sufis,
  like Hamiduddin Nagori and others` (p. 389, folio 358); `the breath control advocated by early
  Sufis in their dhikr`, which `existed, like the "inverted chillah," among the Sufis of eastern
  Iran before Sufism spread to India` (p. 389, folio 358); `a few outstanding Sanskrit works in
  this field were translated into Persian during the Mogul period` (p. 389, folio 358).
- **Naqshbandi ritual practice.** `The center of Naqshbandl education is the silent dhikr, as
  opposed to the loud dhikr, with musical accompaniment, that attracted the masses to the other
  orders.` `The second noteworthy characteristic is suhbat, the intimate conversation between
  master and disciple conducted on a very high spiritual level (cf. N 387).` `The close relation
  between master and disciple reveals itself in tawajjuh, the concentration of the two partners
  upon each other that results in experiences of spiritual unity, faith healing, and many other
  phenomena (cf. N 403).` (p. 397, folio 366). `The Naqshbandiyya is a sober order, eschewing
  artistic performance—mainly music and samcf` [text-layer?: samāʿ] (p. 396, folio 365).
- **The eight Naqshbandi principles, as printed** (p. 395, folio 364) — the list numerals are
  damaged and are given as printed: `i)` [text-layer?: 1] `hush dar dam, "awareness in breathing,"`
  · `a)` [text-layer?: 2] `nazar bar qadam, "watching over one's steps,"` · `3) safar dar watan,
  "internal mystical journey,"` · `4) khalwat dar anjuman, "solitude in the crowd,"` · `5) ydd
  hard, "recollection,"` · `6) bdz gard, "restraining one's thoughts,"` · `7) nigdh dasht, "to
  watch one's thought,"` and · `8) ydd ddsht, "concentration upon God."`
- **Order economy and political power.** `His order established connections with the trade guilds
  and merchants, and his wealth grew parallel to the growth of his spiritual influence, so that he
  and his followers and friends controlled the Timurid court and carefully watched over religious
  practices there. At almost the same time the order became highly politicized.` (p. 396,
  folio 365). `It was the Khwaja's conviction that "to serve the world it is necessary to exercise
  political power" and to bring the rulers under control so that the divine law can be implemented

  as Ishan, "they."` (p. 396, folio 365).
- **A living devotional usage recorded as contemporary.** `Even today, the sellers of wild rue in
  Afghanistan may offer their merchandise, which is to be burned against the evil eye, with an

- **The `qayyum` as an office.** `It is through the qayyum that the world is kept in order; he is
  even higher than the mystical leader, the qutb, and keeps in motion everything created.` `The
  qayyum has been elected by God, who bestowed upon him special grace` (p. 400, folio 369).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **On `malfuzdt` as evidence.** `as Khaliq Ahmad Nizami has rightly pointed out, these "diaries"
  constitute a valuable source for our knowledge of life outside the court circles. They are a
  necessary corrective of the official historiography; they allow us interesting glimpses into
  social and cultural problems that the official authors wittingly or unwittingly overlooked.`
  (p. 387, folio 356) — Schimmel endorsing Nizami. Against this she sets a decline: `As time
  passed, the mystics became ever more preoccupied with collecting biographical notes and making
  compilations of second- and often third-hand information.` (p. 386, folio 355).
- **On Hindu–Muslim influence, she declines to settle the question and argues against the easy
  answer.** `The question of the probability and extent of mutual influences of Muslim and Hindu
  mysticism has been discussed often by Oriental and Western scholars.` (p. 388, folio 357). On
  vegetarianism she supplies a counter-example: the Rābiʿa story `makes it clear` the trait is
  older, and it is `found in the Maghreb as well, where no Hindu or Jain influence can possibly
  have been present` (p. 389, folio 358). On breath control: `To what extent the breath control
  advocated by early Sufis in their dhikr developed under the influence of Indian practices cannot
  be judged properly.` (p. 389, folio 358). Her limited concession: `For the later period, however,
  we may assume that a deepened interest in the ascetic practices of their Hindu neighbors might
  have colored some aspects of Indian Sufi life.` (p. 389, folio 358).
- **On the durability of the boundary.** `On the whole, the boundaries between Muslim and Hindu
  saints remained quite well defined during the first five centuries of Muslim rule over large
  parts of India, though there were syncretistic movements, like that of Kabir in the fifteenth
  century.` (p. 389, folio 358).
- **On Akbar.** A possible Mahdawi influence is offered and then held open: `thus an influence
  cannot be completely excluded.` (p. 390, folio 359). Her verdict: `Akbar's religious tolerance
  was, in its practical consequences, scarcely compatible with Muslim law` (p. 390, folio 359), and
  `Akbar's attempts could not bring forth a perfect solution of the problems confronting a large,
  multireligious country; on the contrary, they evoked much resistance in all segments of the
  population. Yet they are reflected in the poetry and the fine arts of his time.` (p. 391,
  folio 360).
- **On Mogul Persian poetry.** `most of the leading poets between 1570 and 1650` [text-layer?:
  clean] `, in fact, came from Iran`; `the vocabulary was enriched by allegorical stories from
  Indian sources and allusions to the newly discovered European world`; `The nostalgia and
  melancholy so often found in classical Persian poetry was more pronounced in India, hidden under
  a dazzling exterior` (p. 391, folio 360).
- **On Dara Shikoh's writing, a mixed verdict.** The `Risdla-yi haqnuma` `lacks the stature of
  these treatises, just as his mystical poetry, though it shows great skill in the use of words, is
  somewhat dry.` (p. 392, folio 361). But `Dara's great work was the translation of the Upanishads
  into Persian—a work that he completed with the help of some Indian scholars, but that
  unmistakably bears the stamp of his personality.` (p. 392, folio 361).
- **On the European reception of that translation.** `Neither Dara Shikoh nor his translator could
  have foreseen to what extent this first great book on Hindu mysticism would influence the thought
  of Europe.` `Its mystical outlook has, to a large extent, helped to form in the West an image of
  India that has little in common with the realities.` (pp. 392–393, folios 361–362).
- **On Sirhindi's modern image — the chunk's sharpest historiographical argument.** `Yohanan
  Friedman has tried to show that this image developed only after 1919` [text-layer?: clean] `, in
  Abu0! Kalam Azad's` [text-layer?: Abūʾl Kalām Āzād] `work, and that it has been eagerly
  elaborated by many Indo-Muslim, particularly Pakistani, scholars.` Schimmel's own position:
  `It may be true that Sirhindl's direct influence upon Mogul religious life was not as strong as
  his modern adherents claim, but the fact remains that the Naqshbandiyya, though a mystical order,
  has always been interested in politics, regarding the education of the ruling classes as
  absolutely incumbent upon them.` `We may safely accept the notion that Ahmad's successor in the
  order had a hand in the political development that followed his death and that this situation
  continued until about 1740` [text-layer?: clean] `.` (pp. 398–399, folios 367–368).
- **On `wahdat ash-shuhud` versus `wahdat al-wujud`.** `Ahmad Sirhindi has been praised primarily
  as the restorer of the classical theology of wahdat ash-shuhud, "unity of vision," or
  "testimonial monism," as opposed to the "degenerate"—as the orthodox would call it—system of
  wahdat al-wujud. However, the problem can scarcely be seen in such oversimplified terms.`
  (p. 399, folio 368). She then adopts Molé's reading `(MM 108-10)` [a siglum: Molé, not a page of
  this book] and adds her own comparison: `(the Zen Buddhist experiences satori exactly at the same
  point: at the return from the unitive experience, he realizes the multiplicity in a changed
  light)` (p. 399, folio 368).
- **On the sociology behind Sirhindi's reclassification.** `Such a reclassification of theories
  elaborated by the Sufis of old was perhaps necessary in a society in which the mystically
  oriented poets never ceased singing of the unity of all religions and boasted of their alleged
  infidelity.` (p. 400, folio 369).
- **On the `Raudat al-qayyumiyya` as a source.** `The Raudat al-qayyumiyya is certainly not a solid
  historical work. Written at the time of the breakdown of the Mogul Empire (after 1739`
  [text-layer?: clean] `), it reflects the admiration of a certain Naqshbandl faction for the
  master and his family; yet it must certainly rely upon some true statements of Sirhindl and shed
  light, if not on Naqshbandl history, at least on mystical psychology.` (p. 401, folio 370).
- **On the Naqshbandiyya's contradiction.** `Thus the order had among its early members two
  outstanding poets who deeply influenced Persian and Turkish literatures and who, like Nawa/%`
  [text-layer?: Nawāʾī] `, actively participated in political life. More than two centuries later
  members of the "anti-artistic" Naqshbandiyya played a similar role in the Indian Subcontinent.`
  (p. 397, folio 366). And on Urdu: `Once more, the Naqshbandiyya played a decisive role in the
  development of a field in which the founders of the order were not at all interested.` (p. 401,
  folio 370).
- **On the vernaculars and the fall of the Moguls.** `The breakdown of the Mogul Empire was
  accompanied by a breakdown of poetical language. Not only did Urdu grow in the capital, but
  Sindhi, Panjabi, and Pashto developed literatures of their own during the last decades of
  Aurangzeb's reign and continued to flourish during the eighteenth century.` (p. 402, folio 371) —
  the chunk's most directly relevant claim for Pakistani vernacular shrine traditions, and its last
  complete argument before the chunk breaks off.
- **Research desiderata Schimmel states outright:** Muhammad Ghauth's `Al-jawahir al-khamsa`
  `deserves a detailed study both in its original Persian and in its widely read Arabic
  translation` (p. 386, folio 355); Gangohi's `Rushdname` `still deserves careful study`
  (footnote 17, p. 388, folio 357); `The theories about the qayyumiyya have only begun to be
  explored by the scholars of Sufism. The role of the four qayyums in Mogul politics has still to
  be studied in detail.` (p. 401, folio 370).
