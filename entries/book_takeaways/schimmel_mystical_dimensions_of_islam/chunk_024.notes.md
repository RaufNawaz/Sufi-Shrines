## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Bektashi convents and practice.** `A number of pictures, mostly used in the Bektashi convents,
  show a tendency toward representing the names of the imams or similar combinations of sacred names
  in the shape of a human face (see BO, Supplement 2)` (p. 444, folio 413). `The Bektashis practiced
  a special mlm duasi, a prayer of the letter m, which is said to point to the manifestation of the
  light of Muhammad (BO 268)` (p. 451, folio `42O` [text-layer?: 420]). Calligraphic lions built from
  sentences about ʿAlī are `a figure often found in Bektashi convents and all over the Shia world`
  (p. 456, folio 425). The Bektashi letter-reading of `tarlqat` [text-layer?: ṭarīqat] is set out in
  five lines `(BO 100 ft.)` [text-layer?: ff.] (p. 453, folio 422).
- **The Mevlevi order and calligraphy.** `In Turkey, the calligraphers—who were often members of the
  Mevlevi order—liked to shape the basmala as stork, a bird praised in Turkish, Persian, and Arabic
  folklore for its piety in performing the pilgrimage to Mecca.` (p. 456, folio 425).
- **Letters as an instrument of Sufi instruction.** `The Sufis themselves, as much as they boasted of
  their disinterest in worldly affairs, were usually very fond of letter-writing. The letters of the
  great masters of old, collected by their disciples, form a valuable source for our knowledge of
  mystical thought and practice. Even today mystical instruction through letters is practiced by some
  Sufi leaders.` (p. 454, folio 423).
- **The Golden Alphabet as a teaching form.** `a poetical form known in Ancient Eastern literature
  and throughout the Muslim world, mainly in later times`, `in which each verse begins with the
  following letter of the Arabic alphabet`; `There is no rule for the verse form`; `It was a form
  that could easily be memorized by anybody who tried to learn the alphabet itself, and that is why
  Turkish, Sindhi, Panjabi, and Pashto poets loved to explain their mystical doctrines and the
  mysteries of faith in the form of Golden Alphabets` (p. 454, folio 423). `alliteration was a
  frequently used poetical device, particularly in Sind` (p. 454, folio 423). Sindhi Golden Alphabets
  are said to have been `collected by Dr. Nabi Bakhsh Baloch in Tih akaryun` (p. 454, folio 423,
  footnote 24).
- **The forty days of seclusion.** The letter mīm, `with the numerical value of forty`, is understood
  as `an expression of the forty grades of divine emanation from universal reason to man and back and
  of the forty days spent in seclusion, which are connected with the experience of the forty degrees`
  (p. 450, folio 419).
- **Executions.** `Fadlullah Astarabadi, who was executed for his heretical ideas in 1398`; and
  `Neslmi`, who `like his master, was cruelly put to death in 1417` (p. 443, folio 412). Both years
  print as clean four-digit numerals.
- **Architecture and material culture.** `the artists created very intricate patterns of floriated
  and lacelike plaited Run script` [text-layer?: unreadable — see Doubts] `on domes, minarets, and
  objects of minor art` (p. 455, folio 424); mirror script `reflecting one central truth of the Koran
  twice or fourfold`, and animals made from `the words of the basmala or the Throne verse (Sura
  2:256)` (p. 456, folio 425).
- **Pigeon post.** `in a civilization in which pigeon post was widely used, allusions to birds as
  letter-carriers are often found: the pigeon that is supposed to bring the letter from the beloved
  is often connected with the pigeons that live in the central sanctuary in Mecca` (p. 455, folio
  424).
- **Literacy.** `Even in an environment in which illiteracy prevailed, particularly among women, the
  motif of letter-writing and waiting for letters is used with amazing frequency. In the folk poetry
  of Sind the heroines always complain that no letter from the beloved has reached them` (p. 455,
  folio 424).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **Letter symbolism is a precondition for reading the poetry, not an ornament.** `It is scarcely
  possible to fully understand and enjoy the poetry of the Muslim world, mainly of Iran and the
  neighboring countries, without a thorough knowledge of the meaning given to the letters.` (p. 443,
  folio 412).
- **Pre-Islamic continuity.** `Even in pre-Islamic times, the poets of Arabia had compared different
  parts of the body, or of their dwellings, with letters—comparisons that were inherited and
  elaborated upon by Islamic poets throughout the world.` (p. 443, folio 412).
- **A comparison Schimmel draws herself.** Computing names, places, dates and events from the
  numerical values of Koranic words works `in the same way as some Christian Cabalists detected
  historical events in the words and numbers of the Bible, mainly in Revelation` (p. 443, folio 412).
  She also notes that Daylamī's alif argument makes `Christian ideas of trinity... much closer to
  Sufism than dualist ideas as defended by the Iranians—a somewhat surprising result, which can,
  however, be understood, since Sufi terminology is very fond of threefold groupings` (p. 449, folio
  418).
- **Fate and writing.** `It is from the idea that fate is written down forever on the Well-preserved
  Tablet that the Islamic expressions for "fate" are derived—maktub, "written," in Arabic;
  sarniwisht or aim yazisi, "what is written on the forehead," in Persian and Turkish respectively.`
  (p. 445, folio 414).
- **Repentance as washing the record.** `Oriental ink is soluble in water, and a page could easily be
  washed off, if necessary. The water of tears... can, similarly, wash off all the ink of black
  sins` (pp. 445–446, folios 414–415); `To indulge in writing and reading nonreligious books, or even
  in the subtleties of intricate law cases or hairsplitting grammar, was, for the Sufis, to add to
  the blackness of the book of deeds; it was to sin.` (p. 446, folio 415).
- **The letter as the permitted image of God.** `Such a vision of the divine in the form of a letter
  is characteristic of a religion that prohibits representation, particularly representation of the
  divine. The letter is, in fact, the highest possible manifestation of the divine in Islamic
  thought.` (pp. 451–452, folios `42O` [text-layer?: 420] and 421). Likewise `Arabic calligraphy is
  the typical expression of a culture that outlawed the representation of living beings` (p. 455,
  folio 424).
- **A change of consciousness in late Indo-Persian poetry**, argued from Bedil's substitution of `"a
  manuscript of nothingness"` for the older image of man as copy of the heavenly Koran (p. 444, folio
  413).
- **Schimmel's own reservations.** `Among these puns are some that may be almost repulsive to modern
  taste` (p. 444, folio 413); `These plays, which may seem strange to a modern Westerner, constitute
  an important aspect of Sufi poetry` (p. 450, folio 419); `it was unimportant to the simple Turkish
  dervishes if they confused Arabic and Turkish words and spellings` (p. 453, folio 422); `though
  sometimes appearing too playful, still conveys a feeling of very serious concern with the mystical
  and magical qualities of the Arabic letters` (p. 454, folio 423).
- **A research programme she proposes.** `A thorough study of the relations between calligraphy and
  Sufi thought would doubtless yield interesting results and contribute to a better understanding of
  some of the basic Sufi ideas as well as of Islamic art.` (p. 456, folio 425).
- **First-person testimony.** `the simple way in which Turkish friends explained to me the secret of
  the tulip and the crescent` — `tulip, Idle` [text-layer?: lāle]`, and crescent moon, hildl`
  [text-layer?: hilāl]`, are formed from the same letters as the word Allah (one alif, two lam, and
  one h) and have, like the divine name, the numerical value of 66.` (p. 453, folio 422).
- **Appendix 2's opening thesis on women.** `The attitude of Sufism toward the fair sex was
  ambivalent, and it can even be said that Sufism was more favorable to the development of feminine
  activities than were other branches of Islam.` The reason she gives is `The sympathy of the Prophet
  for women, his numerous marriages and his four daughters`, which `excluded that feeling of
  dejection so often found in medieval Christian monasticism.` She sets this against `The ideal of
  the Sufi was always the "man" (Persian mard, Turkish er) or the "virtuous young man" (fata or
  jawdnmard` [text-layer?: jawānmard]`)`, and against Sanāʾī's two verses (p. 457, folio 426).
- **The praise of pious women is double-edged, and she says so.** The ideal pious woman `can be
  praised in the most glowing terms (just because of her difference from the ordinary representatives
  of her sex!)` — the exclamation mark is the book's own (p. 457, folio 426).
