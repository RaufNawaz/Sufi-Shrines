### Schimmel's own FOREWORD — her scope, method, stance and stated limits (pp. 26–27)

- **The task is declared impossible at the outset.** `To write about Sufism, or Islamic mysticism,
  is an almost impossible task. At the first step, a wide mountain range appears before the eye—and
  the longer the seeker pursues the path, the more difficult it seems to reach any goal at all.`
  (p. 26, folio xxin [text-layer?: xxiii]). The image runs on through `the rose gardens of Persian
  mystical poetry`, `the icy peaks of theosophic speculations`, **`the lowlands of popular saint
  worship`** and `the endless deserts of theoretical discourses`, and ends `only the elect few will
  reach the farthest mountain on which the mythical bird, Simurgh, lives—to understand that they
  have reached only what was already in themselves.`
- **Her stated method is historical and phenomenological, and she says in advance it will not
  satisfy.** `Thus, to set out and delineate some main features of Sufism, both historically and
  phenomenologically, will yield no result that satisfies everybody: it is easy to overlook certain
  aspects and give too much weight to others.` (p. 26, folio xxiii).
- **Her stated reliability limit is the sheer size of the literature.** `The amount of oriental and
  occidental literature existent in print and in manuscript is beyond counting, so that even from
  this viewpoint a full account is not to be achieved.` (p. 26, folio xxiii).
- **The book's origin and evidence base, in her own words — the single most important line in this
  chunk for the archive.** `Yet, my students at Harvard have urged me to put together the notes that
  formed the basis of several courses on Sufism—notes that consist both of literary evidence and of
  personal experiences with numerous friends in the Islamic East, mainly in Turkey and in Pakistan.`

  acquaintance concentrated in **Turkey and Pakistan**.

### Chapter 1, `WHAT IS SUFISM?` — Schimmel's definitions and her history of the field (pp. 34–40)

- **The book opens by refusing a complete definition.** `In recent years many books have been
  published on Sufism and the spiritual life in Islam. Each of them has touched upon a different
  facet, for the phenomenon usually called Sufism is so broad and its appearance so protean that
  nobody can venture to describe it fully.` (p. 34, folio 3), illustrated with `the blind men in
  Rumi's famous story` and the elephant, cited as `M 3:12 59-68` (p. 34, folio 3). Footnote 1 to
  that page adds `Fritz Meier, "Zur Geschichte der Legende von den Blinden und dem Elefanten," …
  Eranos-Jahrbuch 14 (1946): 174`, `"The Blind Men and the Elephant," a Hindu fable by John Godfrey
  Saxe`, and the `Shah Wallullah` parallel above (p. 34, folio 3).
- `Such is the case with Sufism, the generally accepted name for Islamic mysticism.` (p. 34,
  folio 3).
- `Mysticism has been called "the great spiritual current which goes through all religions." In its
  widest sense it may be denned as the consciousness of the One Reality—be it called Wisdom, Light,
  Love, or Nothing.` [text-layer?: defined] (p. 35, folio 4), footnoted to `Evelyn Underbill,
  Mysticism: A Study in the Nature and Development of Man's Spiritual Consciousness (1911; paperback
  ed., New York, 1956)` as `The best introduction to mysticism` [text-layer?: Underhill] (p. 35,
  folio 4).
- `Mysticism can be defined as love of the Absolute—for the power that separates true mysticism from
  mere asceticism is love.` (p. 35, folio 4).
- **Three symbol-groups common to mystics of all religions**: the `"Path"` on which the `"wayfarer"`
  proceeds; the transformation of the soul `expressed in the imagery of alchemy`; and `symbols taken
  from human love` (pp. 35–36, folios 4–5).
- **Her first typology: `Mysticism of Infinity` and `Mysticism of Personality`** (p. 36, folio 5).
  The former `has found its highest and purest expression in the system of Plotinus and in the
  Upanishads, particularly as elaborated in Shankara's advaita philosophy. Sufism comes close to it
  in some of the forms developed by the Ibn cArabi school.` [text-layer?: Ibn ʿArabī] It `was often
  attacked by prophets and reformers, because it seemed to deny the value of the human personality
  and to result in pantheism or monism`. In the latter, `the relation between man and God is
  perceived as that of creature and Creator, of a slave in the presence of his Lord, or of a lover
  yearning for his Beloved. This type is more commonly found in earlier Sufism.` She immediately
  qualifies: `These two types of mystical experience, however, are rarely met with in their purest
  forms.` (pp. 36–37, folios 5–6).
- **Her second typology: `"voluntaristic"` and `"gnostic"`** (p. 37, folio 6). The voluntaristic
  mystic `wants to "qualify himself with the qualities of God," as the Prophetic tradition says`;
  the gnostic `strives for a deeper knowledge of God … although no mystic could ever dare to "know"
  His Essence.` `In Islamic mysticism, both aspects are equally strong, and in later periods they
  are intermingled.`
- **Her third typology, taken from Jāmī and aligned with the prophetic/mystic distinction** (pp.
  37–38, folios 6–7): one kind of advanced Sufi is returned `on the shore of separation … so that
  they might lead the people towards salvation`; the other is `completely submerged in the ocean of
  Unity … and the sanctity of perfecting others is not entrusted to them` (`N 8-9`). She glosses:
  `those who practice complete reclusion (Weltabkehr) and are solely concerned with their own
  salvation … and those who return from their mystical experience in a higher, sanctified state of
  mind and are able to lead other people on the right path.`
- **On what can and cannot be studied** (p. 38, folio 7): `To analyze the mystical experience itself
  is next to impossible since words can never plumb the depths of this experience. Even the finest
  psychological analysis is limited; words remain on the shore, as the Sufis would say.` She names
  as workable instead: analysis of structures (`the French scholar Henry Corbin, in his book on Ibn
  'Arabl`), analysis of `the "mystical lexicon" (Louis Massignon and, more recently, Paul Nwyia)`,
  the study of symbols and images, and the study of initiation and the orders (Fritz Meier).
- **Her history of European scholarship** (pp. 38–40, folios 7–9), in her order: Ramon Lull;
  Rābiʿa's legend via Joinville; travellers' reports of dervish rites; Fabricius of Rostock, 1638;
  `Sacdl's Gulistan has been one of the favorite books of European intellectuals since Adam Olearius
  produced its first complete translation into German in 1651` [text-layer?: Saʿdī's]; `A century

- **Her diagnosis of why nineteenth-century scholarship went wrong.** `Yet most of the sources
  available were of rather late origin and rarely contained reliable information about the earliest
