## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Melā Chirāghān, Lahore.** `Madho Lai's anniversary, still celebrated in Lahore near the Shalimar
  Garden on the last Saturday in March, is famous as Meld Chiraghdn, the "Fair of Lamps."` (p. 415,
  folio 384). A movable date (last Saturday in March), a place-by-landmark, and a name. No
  attendance, no custodian, no origin date — `(not stated)`.
- **Samāʿ as a scheduled institution.** `The samcf meetings that he arranged twice a month were
  famous in Delhi, and even the ruler, Shah cAlam II, with the pen name Aftab, sometimes attended
  them.` (p. 411, folio 380) — twice monthly, with the reigning emperor as an occasional attender.
- **Samāʿ contested inside an order.** Dard `had written a book on music (Hurmat-i ghina)` in his own

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- ʿAndalīb's founding vision, attributed by Schimmel to Dard's own tradition: `Muhammad Nasir had
  been blessed about 1734 by a vision of the Prophet's grandson Hasan ibn CAH, who had introduced
  him to the secret of the true Muhammadan Path (at-tariqa al-Muhammadiyya).` (p. 405, folio 374).
- Dard's foreknowledge of his death: `since his father had died at that age and God had promised him
  that he would resemble his father in every respect, he was sure that he would die very soon. In
  fact, he died shortly afterward, on 11 January 1785.` (p. 406, folio 375).
- The father's knife, quoted by Schimmel from Dard's own Arabic: `his father put the knife to his
  throat in one of the states of being drawn near to God in the beginning of his way, with the
  intention of slaughtering him for God` (p. 408, folio 377), and the father's saying: `"Whoever has
  not seen a dead person wandering around on earth may look at this son of mine who lives through me
  and who moves through me!"` (pp. 408–409, folios 377–378).
- Ninety-nine names: `ninety-nine names have been bestowed upon him, corresponding to the ninety-nine
  most beautiful names of God.` Schimmel immediately withholds judgement — `We cannot judge whether
  these names point to a veiling of his real personality or whether they are an expression of the
  search for identity` (p. 410, folio 379).
- The hagiographical hyperbole about Dard's patience, measured against Farīd Ganj-i Shakar, who
  `would have … bite[n] his fingers from amazement like sugarcane` (p. 411, folio 380) — attributed
  by Schimmel to `One of his biographers`.
- Makli Hill: `the last resting place for 125,000 saints, according to the tradition` (p. 415,
  folio 384) — Schimmel attributes the figure to tradition, not to herself.
- Sheikh Tahir / Lāl Uḍerō `saw God "even in a camel."` (p. 418, folio 387).
- The two endings of Hir Ranjha: `According to one version, they are poisoned by Hir's family; in
  another version they die from thirst in the desert during their pilgrimage to Mecca and are
  eventually united in death (or, by drinking the water of life, in eternal life).` (p. 419,
  folio 388).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **Waliullah as the bridge between the two tauḥīds.** `It was Waliullah who tried to bridge the gap
  between the tauhid-i shuhudi and the tauhid-i wujudi, just as he tried to explain the differences
  between the law schools as historical facts with no basic difference of importance.` (p. 403,
  folio 372). Schimmel also notes `That he was attacked by a disciple of Mazhar shows the manifold
  currents inside eighteenth-century Naqshbandi theology.` (p. 403, folio 372).
- **A scholarly gap asserted twice.** Waliullah's `works still await serious study in the West`
  (p. 403, folio 372); of Dard, `Scarcely anyone has studied his numerous Persian prose works`
  (p. 404, folio 373).
- **Dard's sobriety is not original to him.** `This spiritual way is not peculiar to Mir Dard; it
  reminds us of the traditional Naqshbandi theories.` (p. 404, folio 373).
- **The father-sheikh coincidence as the key to Dard's system.** `Traditional Sufi theories regard
  the sheikh as the father of the disciple; for Dard the two functions actually coincided in
  Muhammad Nasir. This led him to interpret the theory of ascent and descent in a peculiar way.`
  (p. 406, folio 375), and `This experience was born out of Dard's relationship with his own
  father-sheikh and could not be shared by anybody else.` (p. 407, folio 376).
- **Dard against waḥdat al-wujūd.** He `frequently attacks those "imperfect Sufis" who claim "in
  their immature minds" to be confessors of unity, but are entangled in a sort of heresy, namely,
  believing in wahdat al-wujud`; he `was well aware that the "pantheistic" formulation so dear to
  the poets—"everything is He"—was dangerous and incorrect` and held instead `"everything is from
  Him"` (p. 407, folio 376).
- **Comparative placement of Dard's prophet-list** against Ibn ʿArabī's twenty-eight prophets, against
  ʿAndalīb's own seven-attribute list, and against Simnānī's seven `latcfif` [text-layer?: laṭāʾif]
  with their colours (p. 410, folio 379, with `(CL 182)` and `(CL 193)` as the source for the
  Simnānī scheme). Schimmel's conclusion: `Dard did not propound a color mysticism, but his claims
  are revealing.` (p. 410, folio 379).
- **A blunt negative judgement on Dard as a commentator.** `the way that he explained the simplest and
  most charming verses in the framework of mystical philosophy, with a terminology borrowed from Ibn
  cArabi, is surprising and, quite frankly, not very uplifting.` (p. 412, folio 381).
- **Poetry as the measure of his fame.** `His Persian poetry followed the traditional patterns and the
  inherited imagery, and is good and readable, but his fame rests primarily on the small Urdu Dlwan
  of some 1200 verses, most of which are of unsurpassable beauty.` (p. 412, folio 381).
- **Dard read as a barometer of Delhi's ruin.** `In Dard's verses, the feeling of the dreamlike state
  is filled with hopelessness—what could an inhabitant of eighteenth-century Delhi expect from
  life?` (p. 414, folio 383); his verses `reflect the situation in Delhi very well` (p. 414,
  folio 383).
- **Punjab and Sind as one literary region.** `The Punjab and the Indus valley have a common tradition
  of mystical folk literature.` (p. 414, folio 383); `The Punjab and Sind show close similarities in
  the types of mystical literature that nourished there.` (p. 416, folio 385).
- **A dating claim about vernacular mystical poetry.** `Reliable tradition traces the use of the native
  language for mystical poetry both in Sind and in the Punjab back to the fifteenth century.`
  (p. 415, folio 384) — offered as tradition, with Farīduddīn `credited with` Old Panjabi songs
  earlier still (p. 415, folio 384).
- **Vernacular media suit feeling, not theory.** `The more rustic, very idiomatic Panjabi and the
  complicated, musical Sindhi—both strong, expressive languages—were excellent media to express
  mystical feelings, though not mystical theories.` (p. 416, folio 385).
- **The anti-mullā motif is inherited, not local.** `The old accusations against the dry-as-dust
  theologians, the molld` [text-layer?: mullā]`, which form a standard motif of Persian and Turkish
  mystical and quasi-mystical poetry, were taken up again by the mystics in the northwest part of
  the Subcontinent.` (p. 416, folio 385), and such verses `appealed particularly to the peasants and
  the illiterate` (p. 416, folio 385).
- **Schimmel's central polemic in this chunk: against the advaita reading.** Certain audacious verses
  `have led a number of authors, particularly the Hindus who studied this aspect of Indo-Muslim
  religious life, to believe that here Indian advaita mysticism gained a complete victory over
  Islamic monotheism. The love for the sheikh and the attempt at becoming annihilated in him … has
  also been explained as the typical Hindu love for the guru.` (pp. 417–418, folios 386–387). Her
  rebuttal: `There is no doubt that the Indian Sufis were inclined to take over images and forms
  from their Hindu neighbors; however, none of the Panjabi or Sindhi mystics neglected the love of
  the Prophet` (p. 418, folio 387). She repeats the charge against Hindu scholarship: they `usually
  lacked a true understanding of the Koran and the foundations of Muslim faith and tried to explain
  everything in the light of Hindu philosophy` (pp. 418–419, folios 387–388). In the footnotes she
  endorses the same line: of Ramakrishna's *Panjabi Sufi Poets*, Johann Fuck `rightly criticized the
  overall Hindu interpretation` (p. 415, folio 384, footnote 60 continued).
- **Bibliographic judgement.** `Lajwanti Ramakrishna, Panjabi Sufi Poets (London and Calcutta, 1938),
  is the only introduction to the subject` (p. 414, folio 383, footnote 60) — a 1975-vintage
  statement of the field, useful as a dating of the scholarship, not of the shrines.
- **Prophet-veneration eclipses the historical Prophet.** `The popular veneration of the Prophet was
  grounded theoretically in such a way that the historical person of the Prophet was almost totally
  eclipsed behind the veil of light that the Sindhi Sufis wove about him.` (p. 418, folio 387).
