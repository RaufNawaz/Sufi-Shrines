## Practices, institutions, economy


- **Children vowed or "sold" to a saint's threshold.** The chunk opens in the middle of this
  sentence, so its subject is cut off: `Sati, because they were granted by a living or dead saint
  to whose threshold they were "sold" by the parents).` (p. 238, folio 207). What the naming
  practice is called and who bears it is in the preceding chunk, not this one — `(not stated)`
  here. A living **or dead** saint grants the child; the transaction is with the **threshold**.
- **Healing by formula, and its standing against biomedicine.** `That illnesses are cured by means
  of religious formulae is well known—the story of the deaf girl who was cured by the saint's
  whispering the call to prayer into her ear is only one example from a long list of miracles in
  which the Sufis used prayer and dhikr formulae for healing purposes.` (p. 239, folio 208), and
  `Even today the recitation of a Fdtiha` [text-layer?: Fātiḥa] `or similar prayer, together with
  "breathing upon" (damidan, uflemek) the sick, is common in the Muslim world (cf. N 352)—and the
  faith in a religious formula pronounced by an alleged saint is often, in rural areas, much
  greater than the trust in a Western-trained physician.` (p. 239, folio 208). Note the book's own
  hedge, `an alleged saint`.
- **Khanqah hospitality, counted and catered.** In the fifteenth-century Indian cat story: `When
  travellers were about to come to the khanqah, it used to indicate their number by mewing; from
  this the servitors of the khanqah would know for how many guests to lay the cloth for the meal.
  At the time when food was served, the cat also was given a portion equal to that of others who
  were present` (p. 241, folio 210). The khanqah has `servitors`; a party of `dervishes` arrives
  and is fed by portion count (pp. 241–242, folios 210–211).
- **A tomb built for an animal.** The cat leaps into the boiling milk-rice to save the dervishes
  from a snake, and `The Sufi remarked that the cat had sacrificed his own life for the sake of the
  dervishes, and a tomb should be built for him.` (p. 242, folio 211).
- **Saintly contests as an institution.** `In connection with the manifestation of spiritual power,
  contests (munaqara) were held to prove which saint was more powerful and could work more exciting
  miracles. These contests—which go back at least to Shibli—are usually ascribed to the popular
  saints, who could easily turn their enemies into stones or wither trees with their curses.`
  (p. 242, folio 211). Schimmel calls the field `otherwise rather unpleasant and, basically,
  un-Sufi` (p. 242, folio 211).
- **Visitation for blessing.** Abū Saʿīd's answer names visitation itself as the miracle: `Is it not
  the greatest miracle that a butcher, who was also a butcher's son, should be attracted to the
  mystical Path, talk to Khidr, and be visited by innumerable people who want to obtain his
  blessings (N 288)?` (p. 243, folio 212).
- **"Shopkeeper sheikhs."** Miracle-mongering `constituted part of the activities of the
  "shopkeeper sheikhs" and pseudo-mystics` (p. 243, folio 212) — the book's own term for the
  commercial end of the trade in karāmāt.
- **Convent cats as guardians.** `Cats, especially, are often associated with miracles, and some
  convents had cats as guardians.` (p. 240, folio 209); the Heddawa `explicitly venerates cats`
  and `even the novices are called quetat, "little tomcats."` (p. 241, folio 210).
- **The maulid: its date and its spread.** `As early as the late eleventh century, and generally
  from the twelfth century on, the veneration of the Prophet assumed a visible form in the
  celebration of the maulid, his birthday, on 12 Rab? ul-awwal` [text-layer?: Rabīʿ ul-awwal]`,
  the third month of the Muslim lunar year. This day is still celebrated in the Muslim world.`
  (p. 247, folio 216), and `From the eastern end of the Muslim world to the west the maulid is a
  wonderful occasion for the pious to show their warm love of the Prophet in songs, poems, and
  prayers.` (p. 247, folio 216).
- **Death anniversaries, the fortieth day, and vows.** Of Süleyman Çelebi's *Mevlud*: `This poem is
  still recited on special occasions in many Turkish families: at the anniversaries, or at the
  fortieth day, of a bereavement or in fulfilment of a vow.` (p. 247, folio 216).
- **Intercession at Doomsday as the popular core.** `In the hearts of the masses, Muhammad is
  primarily the intercessor at Doomsday; as the only prophet, he will intercede for his community,
  as the Koran has promised. It is this trust in his help at the terrible Day of Judgment that has
  largely colored the popular veneration of Muhammad.` (p. 248, folio 217).
- **Objects of devotion sold in mosque courtyards.** `Since Muslim tradition forbids figural
  representation, one finds, instead, accounts of Muhammad's lofty qualities (hilya) written in
  exquisite calligraphy hanging on the walls of the houses; such hilyas are still sold in the
  courtyards of some Turkish mosques.` (p. 245, folio 214).
- **Baraka in a name; naming practice.** `The problem of the baraka, the blessing power of the
  Prophet's name, has often been discussed, and the Turkish usage of calling boys by his name, but
  pronouncing the consonant skeleton with a different vocalization—namely, Mehmed instead of
  Muhammad—is part of the respect due to the blessed name of the Prophet.` (pp. 248–249,
  folios 217–218).
- **The khirqa and descent from the Prophet.** `the Sufi khirqa was sometimes interpreted as
  inspired by the mantle in which the Prophet had wrapped his family members, and the Sufis thus
  became "members of his family."` (p. 253, folio 222); and `The veneration of the Prophet's
  family, common in Shia Islam, can be found in Sufi circles as well, and the reverence shown to
  the sayyids, his descendants, is part of this veneration.` (p. 254, folio 223).
- **Imitation of the Prophet in daily life.** `His behavior, his acts, and his words served as
  models for the pious, who tried to imitate him as closely as possible even in the smallest
  details of outward life—be it the form of dress, the style of the beard, the performance of the
  tiniest particulars of ablution, and even his preference for certain kinds of food.` (p. 244,
  folio 213).
- **Fire- and blade-handling by dervishes.** `the Aissawiyya dervishes in North Africa dance with
  burning coals in their hands without feeling any pain.` (p. 240, folio 209); `One may think, on a
  lower level, of the Rifaci dervishes and many pretenders to Sufism who injure themselves by
  gashing themselves with a sword or by eating glass.` (p. 240, folio 209).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)


- **Miracle stories are functional, and Schimmel reads them by the audience's needs.** The food
  miracle is explained by the demands of hospitality and desert travel (p. 238, folio 207); the
  animal miracles by the doctrine that `every creature praises God in its own voice and he who has
  purified his soul understands their praise and can join them` (p. 239, folio 208); the fire
  miracle by `the Ashcarite doctrine that there is no real causality, but that God gives or
  withholds the capacities of the elements to which mankind is accustomed: He creates these
  qualities and capacities anew every moment.` (p. 240, folio 209).
- **Comparative framing.** `The later books about Muslim saints, particularly YafiTs Raudat
  ar-riyahln` [text-layer?: Yāfiʿī's Rawdat ar-riyāḥīn]`, abound with stories—similar to the
  legends of medieval Christian saints, and the numerous Buddhist tales—that show the warm,
  pleasant relationship between the man of God and the animals.` (p. 238, folio 207). Also `Similar
  events are often attested to in the history of religions, and particularly in Sufism.` (p. 240,
  folio 209).
- **Conversion is the point of the type.** `In many miracle stories the issue at stake is that of
  conversion. These stories were frequently told in the classical period of Islam, when
  "materialists" or those who denied the basic tenets of Islam were convinced by the Sufis.`
  (pp. 239–240, folios 208–209), and `the purpose was the conversion of an infidel to Islam: that
  is why these stories have been told and retold.` (p. 240, folio 209).
- **The sober masters' case against miracles.** `Junayd's view is typical of the sober,
  high-ranking mystics throughout history; they regard interest in miracles as one of the three
  "veils" that can cover the hearts of the elect (the two others being too much attention to works
  of obedience and hope for heavenly reward).` (p. 242, folio 211); and `The great masters
  considered miracles snares on the way toward God. The adept who has reached a certain stage on
  the Path will certainly gain the power of working some extraordinary feats, and these will
  probably keep him from further progress on the Path.` (p. 243, folio 212).
- **The "menstruation" ḥadīth is placed geographically.** `This saying, which seems to belong to
  the Indian, or at least eastern, part of the Muslim world, indicates that miracles come between
  man and God.` (p. 243, folio 212), with the second explanation via `tdlib ad-dunyd`, `tdlib
  al-dkhira` and `tdlib al-maula` [text-layer?: ṭālib ad-dunyā / ṭālib al-ākhira / ṭālib al-maulā],
  `also current among the Indian Muslims since the thirteenth century` (p. 243, folio 212).
  Schimmel's conclusion: `The performance of miracles is a sign that a person's intention is still
  directed toward worldy approval, not exclusively toward God.` (p. 243, folio 212 — `worldy` is
  printed thus).
- **But she does not dismiss karāmāt.** `There is no doubt that many of the miracles wrought by
  Sufi saints were true and real. These mystics had the power to bring down into the world of
  phenomena events that were still in the calam al-mithal` [text-layer?: ʿālam al-mithāl] `and
  could, by virtue of their spiritual purity, help their disciples on the difficult path.`
  (p. 244, folio 213). This is her own voice and it closes the section.
- **Why the Prophet is doctrinally necessary.** `the Muslims felt that the figure of the Prophet
  was necessary for the maintenance of the Muslim faith in its "legal" aspect`; `The Prophet, as
  Rumi says (M 3:801), is a divine test for man`; `Muhammad constitutes a limit in the definition
  of Islam and sets it off from other forms of faith. Mystics who used the first half of the
  profession of faith exclusively, without acknowledging the special rank of Muhammad, were prone
  to fall into a sweeping pantheistic interpretation of Islam.` (p. 245, folio 214).
- **Early resistance to Prophet-veneration is recorded, not smoothed.** `Some of the early mystics
  might have claimed that their love of God did not leave any room for a specialized love of the
  Prophet.` with the dream dialogue `(T 2:41)`, and `Others might have been reluctant to mention
  his name together with God's name in the call to prayer (Q 17).` (p. 245, folio 214). Against
  which: `But the beginning of a genuine Muhammad mysticism dates back to the early eighth
  century.` (p. 245, folio 214).
- **Chronology of the Muhammadan light.** Muqātil interprets Sura 24:35 of Muhammad `(W 93-94)`
  (p. 245, folio 214); `The idea of the "Muhammadan light" seems to have been fully developed about
  900—Sahl at-Tustari speaks of the three lights of God` (p. 246, folio 215); systematisation `by
  Ibn 'Arab!` [text-layer?: Ibn ʿArabī] `and his followers`, following `a masterful study` by Tor
  Andrae on `how this veneration of the Prophet grew during the first six centuries of Islamic
  religious history` (p. 247, folio 216).
- **The three-stage annihilation.** `first he has to experience annihilation in the spiritual
  guide, who functions as the representative of the Prophet, then the fand ffr-rasul`
  [text-layer?: fanā fi'r-rasūl]`, "annihilation in the Prophet," before he can hope to reach, if
  he ever does, fand fi Allah.` (p. 247, folio 216) — the doctrinal ground for pīr-veneration.
- **Ḥadīth scepticism.** `it cannot be denied that many of the traditions that are favorites with
  the later Sufis have no basis in the "canonical" collections of hadlth completed in the last
  third of the ninth century. As time passed, mystical thinkers and poets made use of certain
  traditions that cannot be verified before the tenth or even the eleventh century.` (p. 252,
  folio 221).
- **The muṭāʿ.** Ghazzālī's `mutcf` [text-layer?: muṭāʿ]`, the "one who is obeyed," whom he defines
  as Allah's khalifa or vicegerent, the supreme controller of the whole universe` — Schimmel:
  `one may assume that the great mystic had in mind the equation mutcf— Muhammad, though this
  cannot be absolutely proved.` (p. 254, folio 223). She then quotes Nicholson: `God indeed remains
  the creator of the world, but He is no longer in any direct sense its ruler.` (p. 254,
  folio 223). The chunk ends mid-quotation.
