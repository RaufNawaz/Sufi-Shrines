## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **Schimmel's framing of Ibn ʿArabī's standing.** `For most of the Sufis after the thirteenth
  century, his writings constitute the apex of mystical theories, and the orthodox have never ceased
  attacking him.` (p. 294, folio 263). `A correct interpretation of Ibn cArabi's thought is
  difficult. The traditional Western view is that he is the representative of Islamic pantheism, or
  monism, and that because of such monist theories, he destroyed the Islamic idea of God as a living
  and active force and was largely responsible for the decay of true Islamic religious life.`
  (p. 294, folio 263). Against this she sets Nasr, who `see[s] in Ibn cArabi's work a full
  explanation of what was already understood by earlier Sufis` (p. 294, folio 263), and her own
  judgement that `he was more a genius of systematization than an enraptured mystic` (p. 294,
  folio 263).
- **The Naqshbandī reaction, in the book's own words.** Sirhindī is quoted conceding the debt: `As
  even Ahmad Sir-` `hindi—usually considered Ibn cArabi's antagonist—had to admit: "The Sufis who
  preceded him—if they spoke about these matters at all—only hinted at them and did not elaborate.
  Most of those who came after him chose to follow in his footsteps and used his terms. We
  latecomers have also benefited from the blessings of that great man and learned a great deal from
  his mystical insights. May God give him for this the best reward."` (p. 294, folio 263). Against
  that: `later Naqshbandls would be more reserved in their judgment, if they did not condemn
  outright the theories contained in these books.` (p. 296, folio 265). The two sentences stand
  side by side and are not reconciled by Schimmel.
- **Waḥdat al-wujūd, and why the translation matters.** `Ibn cArabi's entire system is generally
  designated by the term wahdat al-wujud, "unity of being." The correct translation of this
  expression provides the key to most of his other theories.` (p. 298, folio 267). Schimmel follows
  Molé: `Marijan Mole has put his finger on the difficulty of translating wujud correctly (MM
  59—62): Ara-` `bic, like other Semitic languages, has no verb to express "to be."` (p. 298,
  folio 267); `The term wujud... means, basically, "finding," "to be found," and is, thus, more
  dynamic than mere "existence."` (p. 298, folio 267).
- **The eastward reception — the sentence this archive needs.** `Thus, wahdat al-wujud is not simply
  "unity of being," but also the unity of existentialization and the perception of this act; it
  sometimes becomes quasi-synonymous with shuhud, "contemplation," "witnessing," so that the terms
  wahdat al-wujud and wahdat ash-shuhud, which were so intensely discussed by later mystics,
  especially in India, are sometimes even interchangeable (MM 61; cf. N 408).` (p. 298, folio 267).
  Schimmel does **not** here name Sirhindī as the author of waḥdat ash-shuhūd, does not name any
  Indian order, and gives no date for the controversy — all **(not stated)** in this chunk.
- **Schimmel's own revision of the pantheism charge.** `That would imply that terms like pantheism,
  panentheism, and even Louis Massignon's term "existential monism" would have to be revised, since
  the concept of wahdat al-wujud does not involve a substantial continuity between God and
  creation.` (p. 298, folio 267); `in their actual existence the creatures are not identical with
  God, but only reflections of His attributes.` (p. 299, folio 268). Set against Affifi, who
  `holds that he is a pure pantheist` (p. 297, folio 266) — a contradiction between authorities that
  Schimmel presents as a live dispute, not as settled.
- **The charge that the doctrine caused decline, and its Indian form.** `Has the easy formulation of
  "unity of being" contributed to the` `decline of Islam, especially in its simplified and dangerous
  condensation as hama ust, "everything is He," which left no room for the subtle distinctions
  maintained by Ibn cArabI himself? Was the transformation of classical Sufism with its stress on
  Carnal, "religious work," "activity," not completely superseded by ^irfan, "gnosis," theosophical
  knowledge as provided by an ingenious system?` (pp. 304–305, folios 273–274). `Carnal`
  [text-layer?: ʿamal — the printed word is an ordinary English word and the reading rests on the
  book's own gloss `"religious work," "activity,"`; damage class C4, flagged not silently repaired].
  Schimmel puts both as questions and answers neither; her closing position is that `the discussion
  about his role—positive or negative—will probably never end as long as there are two different
  approaches to the mystical goal—by activity and conformity of will, or by contemplation and
  gnosis.` (p. 305, folio 274).
- **Fazlur Rahman and Ibn Taymiyya against the system.** `This system is the very reverse of the
  teachings of orthodox Islam, as a modern Muslim thinker like Fazlur Rahman holds, following the
  leading medieval Hanbalite theologian, Ibn Taymiy-` `ya (d. igaS): "A thoroughly monistic system,
  no matter how pious and conscientious it may claim to be, can not, by its very nature, take
  seriously the objective validity of moral standards."` (p. 304, folio 273).
- **The tolerance verses, and Schimmel's refusal of the usual reading.** After quoting
  `My heart is capable of every form, / a cloister of the monk, a temple for idols,` etc. (pp.
  302–303, folios 271–272): `But this seemingly tolerant statement contains, rather, a statement
  about the author's own lofty spiritual rank... It is highest self-praise, acknowledgment of an
  il-` `lumination that is far beyond the "illumination of the names," but not tolerance preached to
  the rank and file.` (p. 303, folio 272). This directly contradicts the common use of Ibn ʿArabī in
  shrine-tolerance argument and is recorded as Schimmel's own judgement.
- **The female element**, twice cross-referenced to the book's own Appendix 2: Ibn ʿArabī `could
  speak of the "woman creator," the secret of the compassionate God through which God is revealed
  [see Appendix 2]` (p. 303, folio 272); his education `by two women saints, one of them Fatima of
  Cordova (see Appendix 2)` (p. 295, folio 264). These are the book's own internal cross-references
  and are quoted verbatim, not converted (correction C10).
- **Indian afterlives of particular images**, all of them Schimmel's own attributions: `khamyaza,
  literally "yawning," i.e., "infinite longing"... which plays such an important role in later
  Indo-Muslim poetry, may have its roots in these concepts of the mutual yearning of Creator and
  creatures.` (p. 299, folio 268); `Indo-Persian Sufi poets carried this idea to the conclusion that
  "the mind becomes blank like the stone upon which the name of God will be engraved"` (p. 302,
  folio 271); `in eighteenth-century India the same image was used, though in more sophisticated
  language, to describe the apparent contradictions of life in this created world` (p. 309,
  folio 278), the example being Mīr Dard's `"Dard-i dil," no. 267, in Chahar risala` (p. 309,
  folio 278). Each is marked by Schimmel as a possibility (`may have its roots`) or as her own
  reading, not as a documented line of transmission.
- **Schimmel on why the system spread.** `It is as if the tremendous political changes of the
  thirteenth century—when the Mongols overrode most of Asia and penetrated deep into Europe... —
  produced, in marked contrast to this destruction, an upsurge of mystical activity, ideas, feeling,
  and poetry` (p. 310, folio 279); `It may be that the activity of Ibn cArabi and his followers was
  needed to give this widespread mystical upsurge a framework into which its main ideas could be
  put: a fixed vocabulary and a comparatively simple theology. From the fourteenth century on even
  those mystics who did not accept Ibn cArabi's theories remained spellbound by his terminology and
  used it very freely.` (p. 311, folio 280).
- **On Ibn al-Fāriḍ's poetry as evidence.** `His poetry has often been understood as a witness to
  the pantheistic interpretation of Islam, but, as with many other mystical poets, one must not
  forget that poetry is not to be equated with theoretical discussions about theological problems.`
  (p. 310, folio 279).
