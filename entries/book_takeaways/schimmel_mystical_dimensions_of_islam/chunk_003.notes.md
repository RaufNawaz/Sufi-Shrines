## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The ahl aṣ-ṣuffa as an institution of place.** `the so-called ahl as-suffa, "the People of the
  Bench," poor and pious members "of the community who lived in the mosque of Medina` (p. 59,
  folio 28) — the stray opening quotation mark before `of the community` is as printed.
- **Sufi patronage of artisan trades.** Salmān `was later regarded as the patron saint of small
  artisans, just as some of the ninth- and tenth-century Sufis were to become patrons of the
  artisan groups whose professions they shared; Salman came to stand for the impact of Sufism on
  the large masses` (p. 59, folio 28; `MM 5`).
- **The threefold `islam, imdn [text-layer?: īmān], and ihsan`** is described as `a definition that
  was adopted by the Sufis`, emerging `Out of this nucleus of pious people around Muhammad`;
  `With the addition of this third element the complete interiorization of Islam begins` (p. 60,
  folio 29).
- **The first ascetic settlement named in the chunk**: one of Ḥasan al-Baṣrī's disciples `founded a
  settlement of ascetically inclined people in Abbadan on the Persian Gulf` (p. 62, folio 31).
- **Law, consensus and the closing of ijtihād.** The four schools `were all founded upon the Koran
  and the sunna` and `made the ijmcf [text-layer?: ijmāʿ], the consensus of the "doctors of law" on
  a certain point, an instrument for introducing innovative legal decisions`; `As early as the late
  tenth century, the possibility of free investigation into the sources of law (ijtihdd
  [text-layer?: ijtihād]) in matters that previously had been decided was no longer permitted; this
  led to a fossilization of jurisprudence` (pp. 62–63, folios 31–32).
- **The woolen garment and the name.** `It is even possible that the first Sufis adopted from the
  Christian ascetics the woolen garment from which their name derives.` (p. 66, folio 35).
- **Marriage, celibacy and family among the early ascetics.** `Among the early ascetics, a
  preference for celibacy was common in spite of the Prophet's example of married life and his
  advice to raise a family.`; `the sorrows of family life might be regarded as "punishment for the
  execution of legally permitted lusts" (N 185)`; against this, `many of the great Sufis and
  founders of mystical fraternities were married and had large families` (pp. 67–68, folios 36–37).
- **Material poverty as practice.** `they might use only a brick for a pillow (N 49) and a worn-out
  mat of straw for a bed (if they did not prefer to sleep in a seated position or refrain from
  sleep at all)`; `although they strictly observed the ritual purity required for prayer, Ibn Adham
  was proud of the huge number of lice living in his coat`; `as late as 900, a maidservant of a Sufi
  from Baghdad exclaimed: "O God, how dirty are Thy friends—not a single one among them is clean!"
  (N 621)` (p. 68, folio 37).
- **Nightly prayer.** `The nightly prayer, one of the pivots of early ascetic life, becomes, with
  her, a sweet and loving conversation between lover and beloved` (p. 71, folio 40).
- **The Basran weepers.** `Therefore they were known as "those who constantly weep" (al-bakkffun
  [text-layer?: al-bakkāʾūn])` (p. 62, folio 31).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- Muhammad is said to have known of Uways al-Qaranī's piety without meeting him and to have said
  `"The breath of the Merciful ... comes to me from Yemen."`; tradition `relates that he spent all
  his nights in prayer` (p. 59, folio 28; `T 1:21`). The chunk attributes this to `Mystical
  tradition` and `It is said`.
- Fuḍayl ibn ʿIyāḍ's conversion: `on the way to his beloved, he happened to hear a verse from the
  Koran and immediately gave up banditry` — given as `One of the most famous conversion stories in
  early Sufism` (p. 66, folio 35).
- Bishr al-Ḥāfī `was converted by a miracle: on the road he found a piece of paper, which he took
  with him because the name of God was written on it — this pious act of the otherwise worldly man
  was soon recompensed when, by divine grace, he was transformed into a Sufi` (p. 69, folio 38).
- Ibrāhīm ibn Adham's renunciation at Balkh, `According to the legend`, with `echoes of the Buddha
  legend`; ʿAṭṭār's pun `Although his residence was Balkh, / Balkh became corrected, namely talkh
  ["bitter"]`, `by changing the diacritical dots of the first letter of Balkh` (p. 68, folio 37;
  `U 264`).
- Fuḍayl's smile at his son's death, read by him as `a sign of divine grace` (p. 67, folio 36).
- Rābiʿa with `a torch in one hand and a ewer in the other`: `"I want to throw fire into Paradise
  and pour water into Hell so that these two veils disappear"` — `The most famous story illustrative
  of the singlemindedness of her devotion` (pp. 69–70, folios 38–39).
- Rābiʿa `would shut the windows in spring without looking at the flowers and become lost in the
  contemplation of Him who created flowers and springtime. This story has often been retold by the
  mystical poets of Iran.` (p. 70, folio 39).
- Dhuʾn-Nūn: `Many miracles are ascribed to him, and in strange legends he figures as a kind of
  magician whom men and jinn obeyed`; he read the thoughts of the boy Ibrāhīm al-Qaṣṣār `by means
  of his inner power (N 166)`; `when he died, legend asserts, "it was written on his forehead: 'This
  is the friend of God, he died in love of God, slain by God' " (H 100)`; and the caliph Mutawakkil,
  `deeply impressed by one of his sermons, set him free` (p. 73, folio 42).
- A recurring **fictional type** rather than a single legend: `A meeting with a Christian ascetic or
  with a wise monk is a fictional element in Sufi legends of early times` — the monk explains
  mystical truths, or `a heavenly voice` tells the disciple `that all his asceticism will not gain
  him salvation since he has no faith in Muhammad` (p. 65, folio 34).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Schimmel on government and the pious.** `The negative attitude toward the government engendered
  during these decades has significantly shaped the feeling of the pious throughout the history of
  Islam; the Sufis would often equate "government" with "evil."` (pp. 60–61, folios 29–30).
- **Massignon's two tendencies, reported by Schimmel.** `Louis Massignon has seen in Hasan and in
  the ascetics who followed him a "realistic critical tendency" as opposed to the more "idealistic
  tradition" that prevailed in Kufa` (p. 62, folio 31). Schimmel adds her own judgement: `It is true
  that the first ascetic tendencies in Basra and its environment were almost exclusively devotional
  and lacked any interest in speculative thought.`
- **Nwyia on mystical language.** `Paul Nwyia, noting the "adventures of the mystics" in language,
  has highlighted the fact that, "thanks to the mystics, in the Arabic language, an authentic
  language, that of experience, was born" (W 4)` (p. 64, folio 33).
- **Schimmel's claim about the vernaculars — the chunk's chief Indo-Pakistan argument.** `Likewise
  the Indo-Muslim languages (Sindhi, Panjabi, and to a great extent Urdu and Pashto) are in large
  part the result of the speech and song of mystical leaders who could not address their simple
  disciples in high-flown theological Arabic or poetical Persian` (p. 64, folio 33). No individual
  poet, order or place is named in support here — `(not stated)`.
- **On non-Muslim contacts.** `The presence of Zoroastrian influences must certainly be accepted
  from the beginning of the Abbasid period, when the capital was shifted from Damascus to Baghdad.`
  Buddhist ascetic practices `were of some interest`; `Their contacts with Hinduism as a religion
  were negligible in this period, although India contributed extensively to the development of
  Islamic mathematics and astronomy.` `But India in general was considered the country of the sage,
  and the home of magical practices performed by blackish, ugly people.` (pp. 64–65, folios 33–34).
  The last clause is Schimmel reporting a medieval Muslim view, not asserting it.
- **Schimmel doubts the attribution of Ibrāhīm ibn Adham's threefold zuhd.** `Because of its
  tripartition, which was common after the ninth century, it looks like a word from some later
  source` (p. 68, folio 37).
- **Nwyia's redating of early Sufi definitions.** `The discoveries about the earliest Sufis show
  that some of the definitions attributed to mystics of the ninth century can probably be dated much
  earlier. They also show how Shia and Sufi ideas were, at that early stage, interdependent. But
  many problems still await solution.` (p. 73, folio 42).
- **Schimmel against Browne on Dhuʾn-Nūn.** `However, we would scarcely agree with Edward G. Browne,
  who considers him "the first to give to the earlier asceticism the definitely pantheistic bent and
  quasi-erotic expression which we recognize as the chief characteristics of Sufism."` She is more
  cautious on Nicholson: `Nicholson was inclined to accept Neoplatonic influences upon Dhu'n-Nun`,
  and `he may well have been acquainted with some Neoplatonic ideas`, `but we do not find a
  "philosophical" approach in these words of his` (p. 74, folio 43).
- **Schimmel's assessments of the secondary literature, in her footnotes.** Schaeder `dealt for the
  first time with Hasan but never completed his study`; Ritter `gave an excellent analysis`
  (p. 61, folio 30). Klappstein's and Hallauer's books are `both rather superficial` (p. 66, folio
  35). Tor Andrae's `In the Garden of Myrtles` is `an excellent introduction to the pre-Hallajian
  development of Sufism` (p. 65, folio 34). Margaret Smith's Rābiʿa book `is a fundamental study
  that also deals with the role of women in Sufism in general` (p. 69, folio 38).
- **On the equality of men and women in union.** `since in the unity of God the lovers no longer
  have a separate existence, no distinction can be made between man and woman (T 1:59)` (p. 72,
  folio 41).
