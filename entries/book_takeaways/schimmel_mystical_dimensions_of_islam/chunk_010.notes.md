## Practices, institutions, economy


**Ritual prayer (ṣalāt) — obligation, times, and the mystics' reading of it**
- `One of the five pillars of Islam is the ritual prayer (saldt; in Persian and in Turkish, namaz) to
  be performed five times a day at prescribed hours between the moment before sunrise and the
  beginning of complete darkness.` (p. 179, folio 148). `saldt` [text-layer?: ṣalāt].
- `In the Koran, night prayer was recommended but not made obligatory for believers.` (p. 179,
  folio 148).
- `Early Muslim ascetics and mystics regarded ritual prayer, in accordance with the Prophet's saying,
  as a kind of ascension to Heaven, as a mfraj` [text-layer?: miʿrāj] `that brought them into the
  immediate presence of God.` (p. 179, folio 148).
- `"Ritual prayer is the key to Paradise," says a tradition` (p. 179, folio 148).
- `Some of them connected the word saldt with the root wasala, "to arrive, be united"; thus, prayer
  became the time of connection, the moment of proximity to God` (cited as `L 150`) (p. 179,
  folio 148).
- `Did not the Koran repeatedly state that all of creation was brought into being for the purpose of
  worshiping God?` (p. 179, folio 148).
- `As for the details of ritual prayer, the early Sufis followed the prescriptions of the law
  exactly. They tried to imitate the model of the Prophet even in the smallest details.` (p. 180,
  folio 149).
- The *niya*: `At the moment the mystic utters the niya (the intention to perform his prayer) with
  the correct number of rak^as` [text-layer?: rakʿas] `(units of prostration, genuflection, etc.), he
  expresses the intention to turn away from everything created. During prayer he feels as if he were
  waiting before God at Doomsday.` (p. 180, folio 149).

**Ritual purity and ablution**
- `One of the prerequisites of ritual prayer is that ritual purity (tahdra)` [text-layer?: ṭahāra]
  `be performed according to the strict rules laid down in the Prophetic tradition.` (p. 179,
  folio 148).
- `Sufis who indulged in ritual purification to the extent that they would perform the great ablution
  (ghusl) before every prayer or before visiting their spiritual director` (cited as `N 292`)`, which
  was, for them, a religious duty comparable to prayer.` (p. 180, folio 149). **The great ablution
  before visiting the spiritual director is the one institutional detail of shrine-and-pīr practice
  in this chunk.**
- `Some would purify themselves in a river even in the middle of the Central Asian winter; others
  would become enraptured at the very moment the water for ablution was poured over their hands.`
  (p. 180, folio 149).
- `And a number of Sufis boasted of being able to perform the morning prayer while still in ritual
  purity from evening prayer, meaning that they had neither slept nor been polluted by any bodily
  function. Some of them even reached a state of remaining in ritual purity for several days.`
  (p. 180, folio 149).

**Bodily performance and its dispensations**
- The prayer `of tiptoe`, imitated from the Prophet and then forbidden in a dream vision (cited as
  `X 24`) (p. 180, folio 149) — see `## Legends and miracles (karamat)`.
- `If confined to bed, they would repeat each prostration and movement twice, since, according to the
  tradition, "the prayer of the sitting man is worth only half that of the standing person"` (cited
  as `X 39; cf. S 144`) (p. 180, folio 149).
- `Some would pray hundreds of rakcas a day in order to remain in constant communion with God; others
  had to ask somebody else to count their rak'as during the act of prayer because they themselves
  were not capable of doing it` (p. 183, folio 152).
- `The Sufi who experienced mystical union through prayer would certainly prolong his salat by adding
  long litanies of supererogative rak'as to the basic, prescribed form, whereas the mystic whose
  rapture or contemplation was more or less interrupted by the prescribed ritual would certainly not
  perform more than the minimal requirements.` (p. 183, folio 152).
- **Exemption:** `When a Sufi was completely lost in ecstasy for a period of days, or even weeks, he
  was exempt from praying.` (p. 183, folio 152).
- `Some mystics regarded salat as an ascetic discipline that was absolutely necessary to preserve and
  that the disciples had to follow carefully; others saw and experienced it as an act of divine
  grace.` (p. 183, folio 152).

**Esoteric interpretation of the prayer movements**
- `the prostration reminds him of the vegetable state, the rukff,` [text-layer?: rukūʿ]
  `"genuflection," of the animal state, and the upright position is the prerogative of human beings.`
  (p. 184, folio 153).
- `The angels spend eternities worshiping the Lord in a single attitude, according to their rank;
  only man can represent, in his different prayer movements, the fullness of adoration that is the
  duty of everything created.` (p. 184, folio 153).
- A Bektashi poem connecting the postures to the letters of `Adam`: `When you stand up, an alif is
  formed, | In bending behold: a ddl` [text-layer?: dāl] `is made; j When you have prostrated, a mlm`
  [text-layer?: mīm] `takes shape: • That is, I tell you, to perceive man—Adam. » ^ (` (cited as
  `BO 207`) (p. 184, folio 153). The trailing `|`, `j`, `•` and `» ^ (` are stray glyph debris; see
  `## Doubts`.
- `Other interpreters have seen in the Arabic letters of the name of Muhammad (ju~)` [text-layer?:
  the Arabic word printed here is not recoverable from the text layer] `the figure of man, prostrate
  before God.` (p. 184, folio 153).
- `"The prostration of the body is the proximity of the soul"` (cited as `M 4:11`)`, for God Himself
  said in the Koran: "Prostrate and draw near" (usjud wa'qtarib; Sura 96:19).` (p. 184, folio 153).

**The fātiḥa and the prayer formulae**
- `The formulae used during the ritual prayer were also interpreted according to their esoteric
  meaning—especially the fdtiha,` [text-layer?: fātiḥa] `the first Sura of the Koran, which is
  recited in Islam as often as, or perhaps even more than, the Lord's Prayer in Christianity`
  (p. 184, folio 153), with the seven lines quoted in full (p. 185, folio 154).
- `A hadtth qudsl` [text-layer?: ḥadīth qudsī] `makes God declare that He has divided this prayer
  between Himself and His servant.` (p. 185, folio 154).

**Free prayer (duʿā) and its rules**
- `"To be deprived of prayer (duca)` [text-layer?: duʿā] `would be a greater loss for me than to be
  deprived of being heard and granted"` (cited as `T 1:56`) (p. 186, folio 155).
- `Prayer can be classified as an intimate conversation, mun&jat,` [text-layer?: munājāt] `between
  man and God, as an exchange of words of love that console the afflicted heart even if they are not
  immediately answered. It is "the language of yearning for the beloved"` (cited as `Q 121`)
  (p. 186, folio 155).
- `the necessity of istighfar, "to ask forgiveness," is, as a rule, mentioned among the preparatory
  stages of true prayer.` (p. 187, folio 156).
- `Many mystical masters gave concrete prescriptions even for the so-called "free" prayer` — Ghazzālī's
  rules `are so detailed that they amount almost to a new branch of strictly organized worship and
  seem to lack any free movement of the heart.` (p. 188, folio 157).
- `The correct timing of prayer and the correct attitude during the prayer were regarded as highly
  important, as was the outward form: rhymed prose, for example, should not be used in free prayer.
  Yet the great Sufis often composed their finest prayers in rhymed prose.` (p. 188, folio 157).
- **Repetition counts:** `Three, seven, fifteen, fifty, seventy, and one thousand are the most common
  numbers for the repetitions. These particular numbers are in accordance with the belief in magical
  numbers all over the world, but in Islam these rules are regarded as inherited from the Prophet and
  his companions.` (p. 188, folio 157). All six numerals print as words, not digits.
- **Approved and effective prayers, and popular piety:** `The concept of the du^d ma^thur,`
  [text-layer?: duʿā maʾthūr] `the prayer that is effective because it was first uttered by some
  pious person or inspired by the Prophet through a dream, has greatly influenced Muslim popular
  piety, as Constance Padwick has shown in her fine book, Muslim Devotions. Through this channel of
  "approved prayers" and "effective prayers," mystical piety was infused into the large masses and
  did more to form their religious consciousness than the formulae used in the prescribed ritual.`
  (p. 188, folio 157).

**Night prayer**
- `From this viewpoint, the importance of night prayer in mystical circles can be properly
  understood.` (p. 186, folio 155).
- `Night prayer, though perhaps worthless in the eyes of the common people, is "like a radiant
  candle" in God's eyes` (cited as `M 3:2375`) (p. 186, folio 155).

**Prayer for others, and the epic colophon**
- `A prayer that will always be granted is the prayer for others. The formula of blessing and prayer
  for the Prophet was part of the prescribed ritual and has often been elaborated as a prayer to be
  recited a number of times on every occasion.` (p. 191, folio 160).
- `One has to pray for one's parents, for those whom one loves, and for those who have aided,
  wittingly or unwittingly, in one's progress—even the highway robber who may have been a cause for
  one's repentance should be included in the mystic's prayer` (cited as `M 4:81-99`) (p. 191,
  folio 160).
- `Thus, many Persian, Turkish, and Urdu epic poems and numerous prose works end with an invitation
  to the reader to recite a prayer for the author and for the scribe.` (p. 191, folio 160).

**Saints whose prayers are heard, and prayer as power**
- `we read and hear many legends about Sufi masters who were mustajab ad-difa,` [text-layer?:
  mustajāb ad-duʿā] `"whose prayers were heard" so that "if he would ask for something God would
  certainly grant it."` (pp. 191–192, folios 160–161).
- `In illness and danger, in times of dearth and need, they would utter an effectual prayer for
  themselves or their fellow townsmen, and they were capable of punishing those who disagreed with
  them.` (p. 192, folio 161).
- `Similar words can be heard even today when some village "saint" feels advanced enough to threaten
  God in order to gain his desired end—an act often approximating magic.` (p. 190, folio 159).
- Trance at a shrine, as the plate records it: `Woman in trance at a saint's tomb in Pakistan.`
  (p. 182, folio not stated).

**Prayer that shades into incantation**
- `Some of the prayers practiced by certain Sufis—mainly in later periods—are in fact very close to
  magical incantation: the master played with the letters of the Arabic alphabet, he composed prayers
  by deriving every possible word from a particular Arabic root—for example, the root underlying a
  specific divine name—or wrote prayers in alphabetical order, filling them with certain words and
  letters that are considered powerful and impressive.` (p. 192, folio 161).
- `the number of repetitions required to make these names efficient is dependent upon the numerical
  value of the name (thus, rahman, "the Merciful," has the numerical value of 299 and should be
  repeated that many times).` (p. 192, folio 161). `299` prints with clean digits.
- `Illness could be cured by applying the appropriate divine name; and to strengthen the power of
  prayers, strange-sounding words of non-Arabic origin and intricate names of angelic beings were
  added.` (p. 192, folio 161).

**Poetical prayer as a form of worship**
- `The monorhymed forms of Arabic, and consequently Persian, Turkish, and Urdu poetry, are ideal
  vehicles for mystical experience, whether it be in the long qaslda` [text-layer?: qaṣīda]
  `(mainly a praise poem) or in the short ghazal (love poem).` (p. 193, folio 162).
- `Outside the Arabic-speaking world, the rhyme was often extended by the addition of a radlf,`
  [text-layer?: radīf] `an additional rhyme word or even a whole sentence that is repeated.`
  (p. 193, folio 162).
- `A special genre, developed primarily in Iran and the countries under its cultural influence, is
  the qasida in honor of the divine creator, which became in the course of time a sort of vehicle for
  the author's display of eloquence and erudition` (p. 194, folio 163).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)


- **Prayer intensity, not prayer form, divides the Sufis.** `Notwithstanding their eagerness to
  perform the salat punctually, some Sufis reached such a state of absolute absorption or rapture
  that ritual prayer seemed to constitute for them an interruption of their mystical state.`
  (p. 183, folio 152). Schimmel builds the whole ṣalāt discussion on this polarity, which she takes
  from Hujwīrī's own typology (`H 301`, p. 183, folio 152).
- **The interpretations are too many to exhaust:** `The different interpretations the Sufis give of
  the ritual prayer and its details would fill a large volume. They range from the simple act of
  obedience to the feeling that God himself performs the prayer` (p. 185, folio 154).
- **On quietist objections to prayer:** `Only the stern, quietistic mystics—in Islam as well as in
  other religions—doubted whether it was lawful to utter a prayer at all` (p. 186, folio 155, running
  to p. 187, folio 156); and her verdict, `However, this attitude of perfect resignation cannot be
  regarded as typical for the Sufis in general. Most of them firmly believed in the necessity and
  importance of prayer` (p. 187, folio 156).
- **On the transmission of mystical piety to the masses** — her strongest institutional claim in the
  chunk: through approved and effective prayers, `mystical piety was infused into the large masses
  and did more to form their religious consciousness than the formulae used in the prescribed
  ritual.` (p. 188, folio 157).
- **A comparative frame runs through the section:** `if the classical rule lex orandi lex credendi
  can be applied anywhere, it certainly applies to the mystical circles in Islam` (p. 186,
  folio 155); the imagery of burnt liver is `strongly reminding us of the Old Testament imagery of
  the heart and liver as a burnt offering` (p. 189, folio 158); `The idea of prayer as a gift of
  divine grace, found in early Sufism just as in Christian theology` (p. 196, folio 165).
- **She judges her material openly.** Of Rūmī's cage-and-beggar image: `This is not a very lofty
  theological thought, but it is not unusual in Sufi poetry.` (p. 189, folio 158). Of the village
  saint: `an act often approximating magic.` (p. 190, folio 159).
- **Against Tholuck:** `Although this story has been criticized by Tholuck as leading to immanentism
  and self-contemplation, it seems that; such a danger is out of the question if one compares the
  numerous verses of the Mathnawi that reveal the same idea, i.e., that prayer springs from God's
  presence in the heart and is answered even before it has been uttered.` (p. 197, folio 166).
- **The *oratio infusa* schema** — her own three-way analysis, `according to the conception of
  tauhld` [text-layer?: tauḥīd] `the Sufi practices`: `1) God the creator has preordained every word
  of prayer; 2) God, dwelling in the heart of man, addresses him and inspires him to answer; or
  3) God, the unique existent, is both the object of prayer and recollection and the praying and
  recollecting subject.` (p. 195, folio 164).
- **On the modern reception of these ideas**, she names only Iqbal (pp. 186 and 191) — see the
  `mazar-e-iqbal` bullet above.
- **On the reading of *dhikr* in modern terms:** `In modern terminology one may say that concentrated
  recollection sets free spiritual energies that provide help in the progress on the Path.` (p. 198,
  folio 167).
