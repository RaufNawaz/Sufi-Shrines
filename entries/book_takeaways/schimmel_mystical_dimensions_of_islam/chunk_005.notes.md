Nūrī and of Sumnūn the Lover (pp. 91–93) — and then the whole of the section headed

  - The itinerary: `From Gujerat he wandered through Sind, the lower Indus valley, which had been a

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **A state trial of the Baghdad Sufis, dated.** `Ghulam Khalil brought charges against the Sufis of
  Baghdad in 885. Accused of heresy, and likely to be sentenced to capital punishment, Nuri offered
  his life to save his companions; the caliph, touched by such magnanimity, investigated the case,
  found the Sufis to be good Muslims, and set them free.` (p. 91, folio 60).
- **The politics behind Ḥallāj's trial.** `Aside from the subtle problems of mystical love, political
  and social problems were at stake.` He was `a friend of the chamberlain Nasr al-Qushuri, who
  favored better administration and juster taxation, dangerous ideas in a time when the caliph was
  almost powerless and the viziers, though all-powerful for a short period, changed frequently.` Both
  `The Shia groups who supported the vizier Ibn al-Furat` and `the Sunni orthodox wing surrounding
  the "pious vizier" CAH ibn clsa` [text-layer?: ʿAlī ibn ʿĪsā] `considered Hallaj as dangerous`
  (p. 99, folio 68).
- **Protection in prison.** `Both the caliph's mother and the chamberlain Nasr—who called him "a
  righteous man"—tried to make his imprisonment as comfortable as possible` (p. 99, folio 68).
- **A juridical process with a named bottleneck.** The vizier had to `force the highest judge of Iraq
  to sign Hallaj's death sentence`, and `years passed` before he could (p. 99, folio 68).
- **Substitution of religious duties (`isqat al-jarffid`).** `it even led him to the doctrine of the
  isqat al-jarffid` [text-layer?: the second word is damaged past confident reading]`, i.e., that
  certain religious duties can be exchanged for other acts that are more useful at the moment.
  Instead of performing the pilgrimage, he advised people to invite orphans and to feed and dress
  them and make them happy for the day of the Great Feast. Such ideas, of course, were not acceptable
  to the legalists.` (p. 102, folio 71).
- **An order's initiation place named for Ḥallāj.** `In the Bektashi order, his name is connected
  with the central place of initiation, which is called ddr-e Mansiir` [text-layer?: dār-e Manṣūr]`,
  "the gallows of Mansur"; Bektashi poets from the fourteenth century onward have often imitated the
  "unitive cry," anffl-Haqq` [text-layer?: anā'l-Ḥaqq] (p. 105, folio 74).
- **Liturgical use by fraternities.** `it has been used by some Sufi fraternities in their
  celebrations, and a Tunisian order has an entire litany in honor of the martyr-mystic` (p. 108,
  folio 77). The order is not named — `(not stated)`.
- **Taʿziya and passion play.** Among `the ta'ziyas, the plays written in commemoration of Husayn ibn
  cAlf` [text-layer?: ibn ʿAlī's] `s martyrdom at Kerbela on the tenth of Muharram 680, Enrico
  Cerulli has discovered one piece that deals with the fate of Hallaj` (p. 106, folio 75); footnote
  44 adds `Abdulghafur Rawan Farhadi, "Le majlis de al-Hallaj, de Shams-i Tabrezi et du Molla de
  Roum," Revue des etudes islamiques, 1954, a Persian passion play, ta^ziya` (p. 104, folio 73).
- **A datable act of literary politics.** The Turkish play `Mansur-e Halldj`: `The date of the play is
  especially significant: it was written in 1940, at a time when Islamic religious instruction was
  banned from Turkish schools and laicism had reached its apex.` (pp. 105–106, folios 74–75).
- **A proverb and a poetic commonplace.** `the saying that "the rose, thrown by the friend, hurts
  more than any stone" has become a Turkish proverb` (p. 100, folio 69); in Iran poets liken `the
  tresses of their beloved to the rope of Mansur's gallows` and `see the red rose on its bough as a
  symbol of Hallaj on the gallows tree` (p. 106, folio 75).


## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- ʿAṭṭār's frame-story of the three days, told `(T 2:142)`: hands and feet cut off, gallows, ashes to
  the wind (pp. 93, 95, folios 62, 64).
- `the lamps in the mosque shattered when Sumnun began preaching about disinterested and selfless
  love, and birds killed themselves while listening to his heart-rending sermons` — ʿAṭṭār's stories
  (p. 93, folio 62).
- `Later, Jam!` [text-layer?: Jāmī] `related that men and women gave up their spirits during his
  preaching.` — of Sumnūn (p. 93, folio 62).
- Nūrī `was afraid of lions and therefore stayed in the lion-infested forests along the Tigris to
  conquer his fear` — Schimmel's footnote 37 attributes the story to `Ibn al-Jauzi, Talbls iblls`
  [text-layer?: Talbīs Iblīs] `(Cairo, 1921-22), p. 381`, which `dwells intensely upon this
  frequently told story` (p. 92, folio 61).
- Nūrī's death: `enraptured by the recitation of a verse, he ran into a nearby reedbed where the
  reeds had just been cut; the razor-sharp edges of the stumps hurt his feet without his being aware
  of the pain; soon afterward he died from the wounds.` (p. 92, folio 61).
- `Junayd foretold—according to the legends—an evil end for his former disciple` (p. 97, folio 66),
  and the `ana?l-Haqq` answer at his door is given as what `the tradition contains` (p. 97,
  folio 66).
- `At the same time he claimed miraculous powers; in Mecca he produced sweetmeat from Yemen, and he
  sent down heavenly food in the middle of the desert.` (p. 98, folio 67).
- `The story goes that Hallaj went dancing in his fetters to the place of execution, reciting a
  quatrain about mystical intoxication; then he asked his friend Shibli to lend him his prayer mat`
  (p. 99, folio 68).
- The rose: `When people began to throw stones at him, Shibli—so the legend has it—threw a rose, and
  Hallaj sighed. Asked the reason for his sigh, he answered: "They do not know what they do, but he
  should have known it."` (p. 99, folio 68).
- The expected return: people `still stood on the banks of the Tigris awaiting Hallaj's return`
  around the year 1000 (p. 104, folio 73).
- The divine revelation adjudicating the two `I`s: `"Pharaoh saw only himself and lost Me, and Husayn
  saw only Me and lost himself" (N 444)` (p. 97, folio 66).


## Arguments and interpretations

- **Schimmel's thesis about the name.** Ḥallāj's name `became, in the course of time, a symbol for
  both suffering love and unitive experience, but also for a lover's greatest sin: to divulge the
  secret of his love.` (p. 95, folio 64).
- **A history of European misreading, and a charge of textual error.** After `Edward Pocock
  (d. 1691)`, Tholuck called him `"the Sufi most famous (inclytissimus) by fame and fate"` who
  `"removed the veil of pantheism publicly with incredible audacity."` Schimmel's own judgement
  follows: `The quotation that Tholuck then gives is both wrongly spelled and wrongly interpreted so
  that Hallaj's image was seriously distorted in subsequent times.` (p. 95, folio 64). She then lists
  the successor readings — pantheist (Tholuck, and `the nineteenth-century scholars`), blasphemer,
  `a secret Christian` (August Miiller [text-layer?: Müller]), `a neuropath or … a pure monist`,
  Indian-derived (Alfred von Kremer), `aham brahmasmi of the Upanishads` (Max Horten),
  `full-fledged pantheist` (Max Schreiner, Duncan Black Macdonald), against which
  `Reynold A. Nicholson stressed the strict monotheism and the very personal relation between man and
  God`, and `Adam Mez dwelt upon possible connections between the great Sufi and Christian theology`
  (pp. 95–96, folios 64–65).
- **Massignon as the turning point.** `Now, thanks to the lifelong work of Louis Massignon, the
  environment and influences on Hallaj have been explored so that his life and teachings have become
  better known and better understood in the West.` (pp. 95–96, folios 64–65). Schimmel notes the
  coincidence of dates: the biography `first appeared in 1922—just one thousand years after Hallaj's
  execution` (p. 96, folio 65), which agrees with the `26 March 922` she gives on p. 99, folio 68,
  and with footnote 39's `execute a Bagdad le 26 Mars 922`.
- **Schaeder's verdict, endorsed.** `Hallaj is, as Hans Heinrich Schaeder says in his review of
  Massignon's book, the martyr of Islam par excellence because he exemplified the deepest
  possibilities of personal piety to be found in Islam` (p. 96, folio 65).
- **Ibn an-Nadīm set up as the conventional opposite.** After quoting the `crafty man and a conjurer`
  passage, Schimmel writes: `This—together with the paragraphs that follow in Ibn an-Nadim's
  book—articulates the conventional reading of Hallaj's personality. Sober historical facts, however,
  though sometimes not too clear, reveal something close to the following picture of his life:`
  (p. 96, folio 65). The biography that follows is presented as `sober historical facts`, with
  `according to the legends` and `The story goes` marking what is not.
- **Against reduction to influence.** On the Christian terms `lahut` and `nasut` and the incarnation
  hypothesis: `But his theories are too complicated to be reducible to this or that influence.
  Indeed, they so intimately reflect the uniqueness of Hallaj's thought that it is useless to trace
  each one back to its source.` (p. 103, folio 72).
- **Against the pantheist reading, on Ḥallāj's own terms.** `Hallaj's theory, however, maintains the
  absolute transcendence of God beyond the dimensions of created things, his qidam, the preeternity
  that separates Him forever from the hadath, "what is created in time."` Only `in rare moments of
  ecstasy` may the uncreated and created spirits unite, and only then may the mystic `declare
  ancfl-Haqq` [text-layer?: anā'l-Ḥaqq] (p. 103, folio 72).
- **Suffering as positive, against the Junaydī school.** `this suffering is not a means of
  dehumanizing man and bringing him back into his first state, "as he was before he was," as was held
  by the followers of Junayd. Hallaj did not advocate destruction for the sake of destruction; he saw
  in suffering a positive value` (p. 103, folio 72). And on the vocabulary: `The word cishq`
  [text-layer?: ʿishq]`, with its connotation of "passionate, overflowing love," came to mean, for
  him, dynamic divine love; but this term was considered dangerous, if not illicit, even by moderate
  Sufis.` (p. 103, folio 72).
- **A sociology of the threat.** `The idea of converting the hearts of all Muslims and teaching them
  the secret of personal sanctification and not just of blind acceptance would certainly have been
  dangerous for a society whose religious and political leaders lived in a state of stagnation with
  neither the strength nor the intention to revitalize the Muslim community.` (p. 99, folio 68).
- **Martyrdom increased his reach.** `Since he was put to death by the government—or the
  establishment—his influence became even stronger after his execution than it had been during his
  lifetime.` (p. 104, folio 73).
- **The sober mystics' counter-image.** `for them he resembled the kettle that sings as long as the
  water is not yet boiling; when the water boils and evaporates, the kettle becomes silent`
  (p. 104, folio 73), and some accused him of `hulul, "incarnation."` (p. 104, folio 73).
- **A transmission claim about the subcontinent.** `The poets in Iran and Afghanistan were outdone in
  their devotion to the Hallajian tradition by the Indian poets writing in Persian who made use of the
  figure of Hallaj from the eleventh century on`, whose `images and forms … are almost identical with
  those found in classical Persian poetry`; but `An even stronger predilection for Mansur is to be
  found in the mystical folk songs composed in the vernaculars of Muslim India and adjacent areas.`
  (p. 106, folio 75). This is the chunk's central claim for the archive's region.
- **The author's own prior work on exactly this.** Footnote 44 self-cites `Schimmel, "The
  Martyr-Mystic Hallaj in Sindhi Folk-Poetry," Numen 9 (1962): 3` and `Annemarie Schimmel, ed. and
  trans., Al-Halladsch, Martyrer der Gottesliebe (Cologne, 1969), an anthology compiled from Hallaj's
  writings and from poetry and prose by Muslim authors from different countries` (p. 104, folio 73) —
  a pointer a consolidator may want.
- **The moth and the candle, traced to Europe.** Ḥallāj's moth chapter is `one of their favorite
  allegories` for Persian poets `(SD 311; … cAttar's Mantiq at-tayr … [MT 258])`, and `Through the
  medium of Persian poetry the same symbol reached Europe. Goethe's famous poem "Selige Sehnsucht,"
  in his West-Ostlicher Divan, reflects this very mystery` (p. 101, folio 70), with footnote 42
  citing Schaeder's essay `"Die persische Vorlage von Goethes Seliger Sehnsucht"`.
