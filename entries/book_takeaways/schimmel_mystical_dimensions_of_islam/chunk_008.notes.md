# schimmel_mystical_dimensions_of_islam — chunk 008 — pp. 143–160

**What this chunk is.** Eighteen consecutive pages of continuous exposition inside chapter 3, whose
running header throughout is `THE PATH` (printed variously `THE PATH`, `THE P A T H`, and once split
over two lines). No chapter opening, no section heading, no plate, no blank page: the chunk begins
mid-sentence (`still worships outward things`, p. 143) and ends mid-sentence (`which came to
designate the "present moment," the`, p. 160). The argument runs through the *maqāmāt* in order:
the struggle against the *nafs*, fasting and sleeplessness, *tawakkul* (trust in God), **faqr
(poverty) and the dervish ideal**, *ṣabr* (patience), *shukr* (gratitude), *riḍā* (contentment),
fear and hope, and finally *basṭ* and *qabḍ*.

**Archive yield is four ids, three of them on named persons and one thin.** `bhit-bhit-shah`
(Schimmel names `Shah c Abdu D l-Latif in Sind`, p. 144), `mazar-e-iqbal` (`Muhammad Iqbal (d.
1938)`, p. 148) and `data-darbar` (Hujwīrī named and quoted on pp. 153, 154, 157 and 160) are
straightforward. `shrine-of-makhdoom-abdul-rahim-girhori` rests on a book title in a footnote and is
flagged hard under Doubts. **No toponym in this chunk fires ruling (a).** The one toponym-key hit,
`hyderabad`, is the imprint `(Hyderabad, Sind, 1956)` and is **declined** per brief §6(ii); the
declined id is listed under Doubts so a reversal is one step. `Sind`, `Baghdad`, `Konya`, `Malaysia`,
`Khurasan`, `Iraq`, `Mecca` and `China` all occur and none of them is a key in `toponym_map.txt`.

## Practices, institutions, economy

**The dervish and the faqīr — the core of this chunk.**
- `The central attitude in Sufi life is that of faqr, "poverty." The Koran (Sura 35:16) has
  contrasted man in need of God with God, the ever Rich, the Self-sufficient, and here lies one of
  the roots of the Sufi concept of poverty.` (p. 151, folio 120).
- The two words by which the institution is known: `the main names under which the mystics have been
  known in the West—though often in distorted images—are faqlr, "poor," and dervish, "poor,
  mendicant."` (pp. 151–152, folios 120–121). `faqlr` [text-layer?: faqīr].
- Prophetic warrant: `Poverty was an attribute of the Prophet, who claimed, according to the
  tradition, faqrl fakhrl, "poverty is my pride." There are numerous legends about the destitute
  state and the poverty of his household and the members of his family.` (p. 152, folio 121).
  `faqrl fakhrl` [text-layer?: faqrī fakhrī].
- Outward poverty as an institution, not only an inward state: `The Sufis considered outward poverty
  a necessary station at the beginning of the Path, and they tried to preserve it as long as
  possible, often throughout their lives.` (p. 152, folio 121).
- The dervish's one possession: `The reed mat on which the mystic slept, and which often constituted
  his only worldly possession, became in later Persian poetry a symbol of spiritual wealth, since it
  gives its owner a rank higher than that of Solomon on his air-borne throne` (p. 152, folio 121),
  with a verse cited to `Abu Talib Kalim, Dlwan, ed. Partaw Baida?! (Tehran, 1336 sh./iggy), ghazal
  no. 316.` (note 15; the Gregorian year is damaged — see Doubts).
- The begging rule: `One of the aspects of true faqr is that the mystic must not ask anything of
  anyone` (p. 152, folio 121), because `to receive would burden the soul with gratitude toward the
  giver, a burden that was considered most embarrassing and heavy; both in poetry and in everyday
  speech this feeling of minnat, "gratitude," has a negative value for the faithful.` (p. 152,
  folio 121).
- `"the true faqlr should not possess anything and thus not be possessed by anything" (L 108). He
  needs God, nothing else.` (p. 152, folio 121).
- A rich dervish is possible: `The dervish, the Sufi, may be rich if he has the right attitude, which
  means that his outward wealth and power are of no interest to him and that he would be willing to
  give them up at any moment.` (p. 153, folio 122).
- The institutional debate and its settlement: `About the year 900, there was discussion in Baghdad
  and elsewhere about the superiority of the poor or the rich. Most of the Sufis agreed that faqr
  was superior and preferable to wealth, provided that it was combined with contentment (N 417)—and
  this is the general solution found in later medieval Sufism, as in Abu Najib as-Suhrawardi's Addb
  al-murldln.` (p. 153, folio 122).
- Rank order inside the institution: Jāmī, following Abū Ḥafṣ ʿUmar as-Suhrawardī, `regarded the
  faqir, in the technical sense, as inferior to the real Sufi, for whom faqr is nothing but a station
  on the Path. If he makes poverty a goal in itself, the faqlr is veiled from God by his very "will
  to be poor" (N 11).` (p. 153, folio 122).
- The dress: a colleague thought Ibrāhīm ibn al-Khawāṣṣ's wandering `too lax, since "his Sufi dress
  begged for him"; he made him wear luxurious attire and then sent him to the desert to practice
  real trust in God (X 105)` (p. 150, folio 119).

**Orders, buildings and objects.**
- `the Sufi orders—Qadiriyya and Naqshbandiyya—made it known as far as Malaysia.` — of the phrase
  `al-faqr idhd tamma huwa Allah` (p. 154, folio 123).
- A dated physical object in the author's own hands: `An inscription from a Qadiriyya tekke in
  Konya, dated 1819, in my possession, contains a Persian quatrain ending with these words.`
  (note 16, p. 154, folio 123). `1819` is printed cleanly.
- Architecture read as doctrine: `The equation of faqr with annihilation, and the emphasis on the
  negative, nonexistent aspect of things is expressed, in Islamic art, by the large empty hall of
  the mosque, which inspires the visitor with numinous grandeur. It is also reflected in the negative
  space in the arabesques or in calligraphy.` (p. 154, folio 123).
- Later moderate practice: `Many later orders taught that the middle way, between excessive hunger
  and excessive eating, was safest for the disciple's progress (N 395).` (p. 148, folio 117); and
  `According to the later Naqshbandiyya and other moderate orders, the people who truly fast are
  those who keep their minds free from the food of satanic suggestions` — `"Such people's sleep is
  worship. Their walking and going and resting are glorification of God, and their breathing praise
  of God."` (p. 148, folio 117).

**Diet, fasting and the daily regime.**
- `The chief means for taming and training the nafs were, and still are, fasting and sleeplessness.`
  (p. 145, folio 114).
- `qa'im al-lail wa sffim ad-dahr, "spending their nights upright in prayer and maintaining a
  perpetual fast by day."` (p. 145, folio 114). `sffim` [text-layer?: ṣāʾim].
- The threefold rule: `qillat at-ta^dm, qillat al-mandm wa qillat al-kaldm, "little food, little
  sleep, little talk" (to which often "loneliness, keeping away from men," was added) is still as
  valid as it was a thousand years ago.` (p. 145, folio 114).
- The alternating fast: `they invented the so-called sawn dcfudi, which meant that they would eat one
  day and fast one day, so that their bodies would not become accustomed to either of the two
  states.` (p. 146, folio 115). `sawn dcfudi` [text-layer?: ṣawm dāwūdī].
- `Others not only fasted and restricted their diet to the absolute minimum but, in addition to that,
  took a purgative on every third day.` (p. 147, folio 116).
- Sweets: `as early as the tenth century, the fondness of the Sufis for halvah, sweetmeat, is
  mentioned; and in the eleventh and later centuries poets often speak in derogatory terms of the
  Sufi with milk-white hair who has made the recollection (dhikr) of sugar, rice, and milk his
  special litany (SD 149).` (p. 148, folio 117).

**Money, work and the economy.**
- `To store money or goods was regarded as a major sin—did the pious know whether he would still be
  alive within an hour, or by the next morning?` (p. 150, folio 119).
- `Even mystics who cannot be regarded as typical representatives of strict tawakkul often
  distributed all their money in the evening or gave away everything they had on Friday. They also
  refused any medical treatment.` (p. 151, folio 120).
- Work refused on ritual grounds: `The ascetic regarded everything worldly as contaminated; nothing
  was ritually clean enough for him to occupy himself with. He would rather spend his days and nights
  in worship than pollute himself by "practical" work.` (p. 150, folio 119).
- Junayd's counter-rule on earning: `"The proper method of earning . . . is to engage in works which
  bring one nearer to God, and to occupy oneself with them in the same spirit as with works of
  supererogation commended to one, not with the idea that they are a means of sustenance or
  advantage" (K 73).` (p. 151, folio 120).
- The macro-economic judgement: `If everybody had lived according to the ideals promoted by some of
  the early ascetics, the whole economic and social fabric of the Muslim Empire would have collapsed.`
  (p. 151, folio 120).
- The book's one publishing-scale datum: Makkī's `Qut al-qulub, contains sixty pages (big folio with
  very small print) about this topic, more than about any other aspect of Sufism.` (p. 151,
  folio 120).



## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- The *nafs* seen outside the body — `Sometimes it took the form of a black dog that wanted food but
  had to be trained and sent away; other mystics saw their nafs coming out of their throats in the
  form of a young fox or a mouse (H 206).` (p. 143, folio 112).
- The Prophet's Satan converted — `"When asked how his shaytdn behaved, he answered: 'Aslama
  shayta.nl; my shaytdn has become a Muslim and does whatever I order him,' "` (p. 144, folio 113).
- The snake blinded by an emerald — `according to folk belief, the snake is blinded by the sight of
  an emerald (the connection of the pir's spiritual power with the green color of the emerald is
  significant). Thus, his influence renders the na/i-snake harmless (M 3:2548).` (p. 144, folio 113).
  `na/i-snake` [text-layer?: nafs-snake].
- Shāh Kirmānī's forty sleepless years, told as `The most beautiful story pertaining to this
  attitude`: `" 'O Lord, I was seeking Thee in nightly vigils, but I have found Thee in sleep.' God
  answered: 'O Shah, you have found Me by means of those nightly vigils: if you had not sought Me
  there, you would not have found Me here' " (H 138)` (p. 146, folio 115).
- The seventy-day fast, from Rūzbihān Baqlī — `"In this state food comes to him from the word 'I stay
  with my Lord who feeds me and gives me to drink' " (Sura 26:79). "This is no longer the station of
  struggle, but that of contemplation" (B 364).` (p. 147, folio 116).
- `There are known cases of Sufis who starved to death (L 417).` (p. 147, folio 116).
- The dying man and the wet cotton — `The wish to die in the state of fasting may induce the Sufi
  even to throw away the wet piece of cotton that his friends put in his mouth to relieve him in his
  state of agony (N 245).` (pp. 146–147, folios 115–116).
- The dervish in the Tigris — `asked whether he wanted to be saved, he said "no," and asked whether
  he would rather die, he again said "no"—"for what have I to do with willing?"` (p. 150, folio 119).
- Abū Turāb an-Nakhshabī `was devoured by lions in the desert (Q 17)`, which Schimmel marks as an
  irony (p. 150, folio 119).
- Refusing Khiḍr — Ibrāhīm ibn al-Khawāṣṣ `would refuse the company of Khidr, the patron of pious
  travelers, because his graceful company seemed to negate his perfect trust in God alone` (p. 150,
  folio 119).
- Abraham in the fire — `had not Abraham, after all, refused help even from Gabriel when Nimrod cast
  him onto the blazing pyre? And he was rewarded for this act of tawakkul by God's changing the fire
  into a cool rose garden.` (p. 150, folio 119).
- The Prophet and the bedouin — `"First tie your camel's knee, and then trust in God"?` (p. 151,
  folio 120), cited against exaggerated *tawakkul*.
- Jesus's needle — `even Jesus, the ideal of poverty for the early ascetics, was regarded as
  imperfect in his poverty; he used to carry a needle with him, which proved that his relation with
  the world was not yet completely severed, and that is why he was assigned a place only in the
  fourth heaven, not in the proximity of the Lord.` (p. 153, folio 122).
- The Prophet's speechless gratitude — `"who in the highest bliss of being drowned in the ocean of
  the bounty of gratitude wanted to express his thanks in the tongue without tongues and found
  himself incapable and said Id uhsl thancfan ^alaika, 'I cannot count the praise due to Thee' "
  (B 514).` (p. 157, folio 126).
- Ḥuṣrī's prayer answered — `'O God, art Thou content with me that I am content with Thee?' And the
  answer came: 'O liar— if you were content with Me, you would not ask whether I am content with
  you' " (T a 1290)` (pp. 157–158, folios 126–127).
- **Miracles themselves distrusted**: `Miracles, for instance, could be interpreted as divine ruses,
  for they are still connected with the world` (p. 159, folio 128).



## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- Schimmel's research agenda on ascetic diet: `It would be worth investigating to what extent this
  restriction to extremely small quantities of food contributed to the longevity of the Sufis. It is
  astonishing how many of them lived to be very old.` (p. 147, folio 116).
- Her judgement on the sources' reliability, twice: `The accounts of the extended periods of fasting
  of Muslim mystics are astounding, and we have little reason to doubt their authenticity.` (p. 146,
  folio 115); and `There is no reason to doubt the validity of the stories in which the utter
  destitution of some of the great mystics is dramatically described.` (p. 152, folio 121).
- The Christian comparison, used repeatedly: `Like the early Christian monks who lived exclusively on
  the host, the Muslim saints considered hunger the best way to reach spirituality.` (p. 147,
  folio 116); `The overwhelming feeling of God's all-encompassing wisdom, power, and loving-kindness
  is reflected in the Muslim tradition as fully as in some of the Psalms and in the Christian
  tradition.` (p. 149, folio 118); and Job's `"The Lord gave, and the Lord took away"` (p. 157,
  folio 126).
- Against reading *tawakkul* as fatalism: `This attitude has been a source of strength for millions
  of Muslims, but it is not to be confused with the stoic acceptance of a blind fate, as it is
  usually understood in terms of predestinarian ideas.` (p. 149, folio 118).
- Her assessment of a secondary work: `The Swiss scholar Benedikt Reinert has devoted to this subject
  a book that must be regarded as a model for future research into the concepts of early Sufism.`
  (p. 148, folio 117), the book being `Benedikt Reinert, Die Lehre vom tawakkul in der alteren Sufik
  (Berlin, 1968), an excellent study o£` [text-layer?: of] `the development o£` [text-layer?: of]
  `"trust in God" and its ramifications.` (note 14).
- Her source-critical note on the famous *faqr* dictum: `Its origin is not known. Sometimes it is
  called a hadith. It has been attributed to Uways al-Qarani, but that is impossible; Jam! (N 267)
  ascribes it to a disciple of Kharaqani, which would be historically possible.` (p. 154, folio 123).
  `Jam!` [text-layer?: Jāmī].
- Her verdict on Sanāʾī's reading of the `"blackness of face"` tradition: the interpretation is that
  `just as the Negro—proverbially cheerful in classical Arabic literature—is happy and smiling in
  spite of his black face, so the true poor person is filled with spiritual happiness and permanent
  joy in his state of poverty (S 88)`, on which Schimmel comments `This sounds farfetched but is a
  typical example of the art of interpretation as practiced by the Sufis.` (p. 155, folio 124).
  Quoted exactly as printed, wording included.

## Doubts

**Archive mapping.**

- **`shrine-of-makhdoom-abdul-rahim-girhori` is the one weak mapping in this chunk and a human must
  rule on it.** The book's only occurrence of the name is inside a title in footnote 12: `Umar
  Muhammad Daudpota, Kaldm-i Girhorl (Hyderabad, Sind, 1956), p. 23.` (p. 147, folio 116). Schimmel
  does not say that `Girhorl` is a person, a saint, a Sindhi, or the archive row's `Makhdoom Abdul
  Rahim Girhori`; the identification rests on the nisba alone and on the 18 September ruling that a
  literary reference counts. It is mapped here rather than dropped because dropping it loses the item
  entirely, but **nothing whatever about the figure is asserted** — no dates, no shrine, no order.
  One step to reverse.
- **`bhit-bhit-shah`, `mazar-e-iqbal` and `data-darbar` are person mappings on named persons and do
  not need the 18 September ruling to stand**, though all three are author/literary-reference
  mentions rather than shrine material. None of the three passages says anything about a tomb, an
  ʿurs, a custodian or a location of a shrine.
- **The `(H …)` citations are Hujwīrī's book, not always Hujwīrī's voice.** Four are explicitly his
  (`H 19`, the `(Hag)` block, `H 180`, `H 230`) and are carried as `data-darbar` material. `H 186`
  on p. 160 is explicitly `one of his mystical masters`, so it is reported *by* him and sits under
  `## Other saints, sites and events`, exactly as chunk 006 handled `H 67`. Five more (`H 206`,
  `H 202-204`, `H 138`, `H 321`, `H 303`) name **no** speaker at all; they are recorded under
  `data-darbar` as material drawn from his book, with the speaker `(not stated)`. A consolidator may
  wish to demote those five.
- **`hyderabad` (1 row) was declined**, per brief §6(ii). It occurs exactly once, as a publisher's
  imprint in footnote 12: `(Hyderabad, Sind, 1956)` (p. 147, folio 116). It is **Hyderabad, Sind**,
  not Hyderabad Deccan, so the false-friend refusal does not apply — it is the imprint case. The
  declined id, listed so the reversal is one step: `guru-gurpat-mandir-db-80-sirey-ghat`.
- **No other toponym fires.** `Sind` (p. 144), `Baghdad` (p. 153), `Konya` (p. 154), `Malaysia`
  (p. 154), `Khurasan`, `Iraq`, `Mecca`, `China`, `Tigris`, `Red Sea` are not keys in
  `toponym_map.txt`. In particular **`Sind` is not a key**, so Shāh ʿAbduʾl-Laṭīf's location cannot
  fire ruling (a); only the person mapping carries him.
- **False friends refused, all of them, as the brief directs.** `muslim` fires about fifteen times on
  the ordinary adjective (`every Muslim`, `Muslim mystics`, `the Muslim Empire`, `my shaytdn has
  become a Muslim`, `a true Muslim`) and is never a place here. `mall` fires three times inside
  `small` (`extremely small quantities`, p. 147; `very small print`, p. 151; `small joys of daily
  life`, p. 159). `dina` was measured at one hit but **I could not locate the token it fires on**
  anywhere in pp. 143–160; `Medina` does not occur in this chunk. It is refused regardless.
- **`ʿAṭṭār` is not Bābā Farīd.** `cAttar` appears twice (p. 144, p. 154) and is the author of the
  *Manṭiq aṭ-ṭayr* (`MT`) and the *Muṣībatnāme* (`U`), exactly as the sigla key states. No mapping to
  `shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar`.
- **`Shah Kirmani` (p. 146) is not the archive's `Daud Bandagi Kirmani`** (`shergarh`) and not the
  `Syed Dawood Bandgi Kirmani` named in `darbar-abul-muali-qadri`'s silsila field. The book gives him
