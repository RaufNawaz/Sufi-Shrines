## Practices, institutions, economy

- **A mystical handbook used as an order's teaching manual, with segregated instruction.** Of
  Shabistarī's `Gulshan-i rat` [text-layer?: Gulshan-i rāz] with Lāhījī's commentary, among the
  Niʿmatullāhī dervishes of Iran: it `"served (and perhaps still serves) as a traditional manual for
  all those men or women (instructed in separate groups) who came under their direction in a kind of
  Third Order."` (p. 312, folio 281; Schimmel's footnote 46 credits `Rice, Persian Sufis, p. 28`).
- **A prohibited devotional practice, named and condemned.** `nazar Ha? l-ahddth` [text-layer?:
  naẓar ilā ʾl-aḥdāth], `the "looking toward the young men"`, which Hujwīrī calls a forbidden
  practice and its defender `an unbeliever` (`H 549`) (p. 321, folio 290). Schimmel sets it in its
  social setting: the Sufis lived `in a society in which the feminine element was largely excluded
  from communal life`, and `they directed their admiration to young boys, disciples, or foreigners,
  and the books from the Abbasid period abound with love stories of this kind` (p. 320, folio 289).
  `That many Sufis could not keep their feeling in check is understood from the complaints about such
  tendencies in Sufi circles.` (pp. 320–321, folios 289–290).
- **Sheikh-disciple correction of excess.** A disciple who touched a snake `because "ther` [text-
  layer?: there] `is nothing but God"` was told by `the sheikh wisely` not `to do such things any
  more, for the snake shows God under the veil of wrath, and the disciple is still too weak to draw
  close to this divine manifestation` (`N 261`) (p. 314, folio 283).
- **Samāʿ, concert and dance as remembered practice.** Ahmad Ghazzālī `composed a number of mystical
  treatises, among them one about the problem of sarna^` [text-layer?: samāʿ] (p. 326, folio 295);
  Baqlī's lament asks after `Ruwaym's mystical concert`, `the handclapping of Abu cAmr Zujjaji` and
  `the dance of Abu3l-Husayn Slrwani` (p. 328, folio 297).
- **Preaching as a livelihood, and the charge attached to it.** One of Ahmad Ghazzālī's own
  colleagues called him `"one of the marvels of God in lying, gaining access to worldly possession
  through his preaching."` (p. 326, folio 295; footnote 9 cites `Merlin S. Swartz, Ibn al-Jauzi's
  "kitdb al-qussds wa-'l-mudhakkirln" (Beirut, 1971), #221`).
- **Lineage and institutional continuity.** `Ahmad Ghazzali was a successful preacher and director of
  souls with a number of disciples, who carried on his teachings; some members of his mystical
  lineage became important representatives of Sufi orders.` (p. 325, folio 294). A `hadlth qudsl`
  `was transmitted` `In the spiritual chain of Ahmad Ghazzali` (`N 370`) (p. 326, folio 295).
- **Imprisonment and execution.** `The talented young man was, however, imprisoned in Baghdad for
  some alleged heresy and executed in 1132 in the bloom of youth.` (p. 327, folio 296). Schimmel adds
  her own suspicion: `We may suspect, therefore, that a matter of personal envy or revenge
  precipitated his premature death.` (p. 327, folio 296).
- **Text transmission into India.** The `Tamhldat` `was widely read in the Muslim world; it was one
  of the favorite books of the Delhi Chishtl mystics in the late thirteenth century`, commented on by
  Gīsūdarāz `about 1400` and translated into Dakhni Urdu by a Bijapur saint `(d. 1669)` (p. 327,
  folio 296).
- **Early Western reception.** The `Gulshan-i rat` was `one of the first books on Sufism that was
  translated into Western languages: into German by Hammer-Purgstall in 1838 (almost as
  unsuccessfully as his translations from Ibn al-Farid), and into English by E. H. Whinfield in
  i88o.` [text-layer?: 1880] (p. 312, folio 281). Footnote 47 prints the imprint year cleanly as
  `(London, 1880)`.

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

**The imagery vocabulary and where Schimmel says it comes from.** This is the material the archive's
Sindhi and Punjabi shrine-poetry entries stand on; it is recorded here page by page, with Schimmel's
attributions kept as she makes them.

- **Rose and nightingale.** The chapter is titled `THE ROSE and the NIGHTINGALE: Persian and Turkish
  Mystical Poetry` and its first section `IMMORTAL ROSE` (p. 318, folio 287). Poets `tried to sing of
  it in the imagery of roses and nightingales` (p. 320, folio 289). Baqlī's prose is `the language
  refined by the poets of Iran during the eleventh and twelfth centuries, filled with roses and
  nightingales, pliable and colorful` (p. 329, folio 298).
- **Schimmel's attribution of the rose's religious sanction to Rūzbihān Baqlī**, the single most
  load-bearing claim in the chunk: `It was Ruzbihan Baqli who highlighted the prophetic tradition
  according to which Muhammad declared the red rose to be the manifestation of God's glory (B 265).
  He thus gave the rose—loved by poets throughout the world—the sanction of religious experience`
  (p. 330, folio 299). And the consequence she draws: `Since this flower reveals divine beauty and
  glory most perfectly, the nightingale, symbol of the longing soul, is once and forever bound to
  love it—and the numberless roses and nightingales in Persian and Turkish poetry take on, wittingly
  or unwittingly, this metaphysical connotation of soul-bird and divine rose.` (p. 330, folio 299).
- **Baqlī's own rose-and-nightingale passage, quoted by Schimmel and cited `B 396`:** `"Look well,
  for the heart is the marketplace of His love, and there the rose of Adam on the branch of Love is
  from the color of manifestation [tajalli] of His Rose. When the nightingale 'spirit' becomes
  intoxicated by this rose, he will hear with the ear of the soul the song of the bird of Alast ['Am
  I not your Lord?'] in the fountainplace of preeternity"` (p. 329, folio 298). The bracketed
  `[tajalli]` and `['Am I not your Lord?']` are Schimmel's own insertions, printed in the book.
- **Nightingales burning their wings** — the image closest to the moth-and-candle family that this
  chunk contains: Baqlī invites his beloved `to enter his heart to see the manifestation of pure love
  in the rose petals of his soul, where thousands of nightingales have burned the wings of their high
  ambition in the fire of love` (`BA 47`) (p. 329, folio 298). **There is no moth and no candle
  anywhere in pp. 312–330** — see `## Doubts`.
- **The rose as a screen between lover and beloved**, attributed to Ahmad Ghazzālī: `we recognize him
  as the master of chaste love, who puts between himself and the beautiful beloved a rose,
  contemplating now the rose, now the beloved.` (p. 326, folio 295).
- **Wine and intoxication.** The hedonist reading of Ḥāfiẓ finds `only sensual love, earthly
  intoxication by "the daughter of the grape," and sheer hedonism` (p. 319, folio 288). Jāmī:
  `Sometimes we call Thee wine, sometimes goblet, / Sometimes we call Thee corn, and sometimes snare`
  (p. 314, folio 283). Intoxication is also a technical state: `kufr-i tanqat` [text-layer?: kufr-i
  ṭarīqat]`, a state of intoxication in which the mystic is no longer aware of anything except God`,
  which `is not the last stage he can reach`, since `The classical distinction of "intoxication" as
  preceding the "second sobriety" remains valid, although most of the later Sufis remained in the
  "lower" state.` (pp. 313–314, folios 282–283).
- **Ocean, drop, pearl.** `Niffari seems to have been the first to use the symbolism of the divine
  ocean. Ibn cArabi had visualized the divine essence as a large green ocean out of which the
  fleeting forms emerge like waves, to fall again and disappear in the fathomless depths.` `Rumi
  emulated him in many of his poems` (p. 315, folio 284). Schimmel qualifies her own attribution:
  `But the image is found much earlier`. For poets who rejected `wahdat al-wujud`, `the drop does not
  vanish in the sea but becomes a precious pearl (according to oriental folklore, pearls are
  raindrops, preferably drops of April rain, that rest in the oyster to mature)` (p. 315, folio 284).
  The ocean of love has `intellectual knowledge` as `only the dry shore`; it is `filled with
  dangerous beasts, alligators and whales`, or `made of consuming fire` (`MT 222`) (p. 315,
  folio 284).
- **Glass, chess and backgammon.** `glass pieces that are from the same substance and yet show
  different shapes and colors` (`V 137`); `chess figures that are carved from the same piece of wood
  (as the Taoists would compare the Tao to a block of uncarved wood)`, from `a sixteenth-century
  Malayan Sufi`; `the image of God as the great chess player, who moves the figures in inexplicable
  ways so that the pawn may become a king, was widely used in Iran`; and the `nard play (a kind of
  backgammon in which the most hopeless position is called shashdar, "six doors," and could thus be
  used by the Sufis to allude to those who are imprisoned in the "six walls" of the created world)`
  (p. 316, folio 285).
- **Idol, Brahmin, Kaaba and temple.** `the images that juxtapose the sheikh and the brahmin, the
  Kaaba and the idol temple as contrasting manifestations of the one true reality soon became
  fossilized` (p. 317, folio 286). `The beloved is usually called an "idol" in Persian poetry, worthy
  of worship, a sensual image of the divine, which is hidden by its very brightness.` (p. 322,
  folio 291), preceded by the couplet of `a late Indo-Persian poet`: `Wherever there is an idol, / a
  Brahmin was created.` (`AP 258`) (p. 322, folio 291).
- **`kaj-kulāh`, the cap awry.** `the expression kaj-kulah, "with his cap awry," is found in poetic
  description of, or addresses to, the beloved as early as cAttar's lyrics (AD 26) and is
  commonplace with later poets of Iran and India.` (p. 321, folio 290). Schimmel traces it to the
  suspect ḥadīth quoted above.
- **`shāhid`, `naẓar`, `husn u ʿishq`, `ʿishq-i majāzī`.** `The term shahid, "witness," is used for
  the beautiful beloved, for he is the true witness of divine beauty` (`R 549`) (p. 322, folio 291).
  `Nazar, "looking," becomes, then, one of the central topics of mystical love experience.` (p. 321,
  folio 290). `many Persian, Turkish, and Urdu romances deal with the eternal story of husn u ^ishq,
  "beauty and love."` (p. 322, folio 291). `human love is generally called, in the Persian tradition,
  'ishq-i majdzi, "metaphorical love."` (p. 323, folio 292), on the `classical Arabic saying that the
  metaphor is the bridge toward reality`, which Schimmel ties to Baqlī's position `that the love of a
  human being is the ladder toward the love of the Merciful (BA 183; B 571)`.
- **`niyāz` and `nāz`.** `the lover's actions consist completely of niyaz, "asking and petitioning,"
  whereas the beloved is made of perfect ndz, "coquetry"; beyond this contrast lies the unity in
  love.` (p. 322, folio 291).
- **Love as cosmic force.** `Avicenna has expressed ideas of this kind in his Risdla ffl-'ishq`, and
  Niẓāmī: `If the magnet were not loving, how could it attract the iron with such longing? / And if
  love were not there, the straw would not seek the amber.` — Niẓāmī `claims that "the heaven has no
  mihrab save love."` `This cosmological role of love was emphasized by RumI` (`M 5:3854-58`;
  `D 2674`; `D 1158`) (p. 324, folio 293).
- **Fire.** `the mystics have seen love as "a flame that burns everything save the Beloved"`
  (`N 408`); `The fire symbolism that abounds in the work of a nineteenth-century Urdu poet like
  Ghalib has its roots in this concept of absolute love.` (p. 325, folio 294). `Love is fire, it burns
  everything. First of all it burns the station of patience: "My patience died the night that love
  was born"` (`M 6:4161`) (p. 325, folio 294).
- **Schimmel's methodological warnings, which the archive should carry with the imagery.** `One has
  to distinguish carefully between poetic language and metaphysical systems.` and `one should not
  draw far-reaching consequences from their constant use in poetry` (p. 317, folio 286). `One cannot
  derive a mystical system out of Persian or Turkish poetry or see in it an expression of experiences
  to be taken at face value.` and `The opalescence of Persian poetry has caused much misunderstanding
  in the West, for no translation can reflect the closely interwoven, glittering symbolism that lies
  behind each word of a hemistich or a verse.` (p. 319, folio 288). On the mystical-versus-erotic
  question: `Yet both claims are equally wide of the mark.` and `their ambiguity is intended, the
  oscillation between the two levels of being is consciously maintained` (p. 319, folio 288).
- **Her simile for the religious background of profane verse:** `it is like the pools in the
  courtyards of the mosques, in which the grandeur of the huge building is mirrored, its beauty
  enhanced by the strange effects of tiny waves or of verdure springing forth from the shallow water.`
  (p. 319, folio 288).
- **On `wahdat al-wujūd` and unity.** `tauhld, is eventually understood as "the extinction of the
  ignorance of our essential and unmovable identity with the only Real."` — `It is no longer a unity
  of will, but a lifting of the veils of ignorance: a decisive difference in the whole approach`
  (p. 313, folio 282). `The phrase hama ust, "everything is He," can be found as early as cAttar's
  poetry, even before Ibn cArabi (cf. U 223).` (p. 314, folio 283). Jāmī's qualification: `We and
  Thou are not separate from each other, / but we need Thee, whereas Thou doest not need us.` and
  `"that which is fettered in created forms is in need of the Absolute, but the Absolute is
  independent of the relative."` (p. 316, folio 285).
- **On the Perfect Man.** `The longing for the Perfect Man, theoretically explained in Ibn cArabi's
  and Jill's work, is much older than these theories—the veneration offered to the Prophet shows
  this.` Rūmī `twice took up in his poetry the story of Diogenes, who searched with a lantern for
  "man"` (`M 5: 2887; D 441`), but `Rumi probably did not think of th` [text-layer?: the]
  `implications of a cosmic power embodied in this ideal man` (p. 313, folio 282). Schimmel notes the
  Sufis `frequently regarded the common people as something "like animals" (kcfl-an'am}` [text-layer?:
  kaʾl-anʿām], `relying upon Koranic statements like that in Sura 7:1*78` [text-layer?: unresolved]
  (p. 313, folio 282).
- **On religious boundaries.** `as the Sufis gained more knowledge of God (or thought they had),
  their religious prejudices diminished, and they understood that "the ways to God are as many as the
  breaths of men."` Yet `they would formally cling to their own religion as the last and most
  comprehensive revelation of divine truth, and even though some of them might allegorize the rites
  of Islam, they did not abolish them.` (p. 317, folio 286).
- **On the Sawāniḥ's untranslatability**, a judgement Schimmel repeats for Baqlī: `the language has
  reached such a degree of refinement, the words and the sentences are so fragile, that any attempt
  to render them into a Western language and keep their flavor intact would be futile.` (p. 326,
  folio 295); Baqlī's style `is at times as hard to translate as that of Ahmad Ghazzall and possesses
  a stronger and deeper instrumentation` (p. 329, folio 298).
- **On Baqlī's `Sharḥ-i shaṭḥiyyāt` as a key to Ḥallāj.** `his Sharh-i shathiydt gives us a key to
  the understanding of the theopathic utterances of early mystics` and `Baqli's main concern is the
  writings and sayings of Hallaj, which makes the Shark` [text-layer?: Sharḥ] `indispensable for an
  understanding of Hallaj's work. In this area Ruzbihan's interpretations are unquestionably correct,
  for the Shirazi Sufis had generally been favorable to the martyr-mystic of Baghdad` (p. 328,
  folio 297).
- **On `iltibās`.** `The iltibas, the concealment of preeternal beauty in created forms, is the theme
  of his meditations` (p. 330, folio 299). Corbin's reading of the transformation is cited as
  `(CV 135)` — a siglum **not in the book's key**; see `## Doubts`.
