## Practices, institutions, economy

- Women attending preaching sessions: `Apparently women were allowed to attend the meetings of Sufi
  preachers.` (p. 458, folio 427).
- Some mystics `would not touch food cooked by a woman (N 576)` (p. 459, folio 428).
- Marriage prescribed as a discipline: `even now some spiritual directors will tell their disciples
  to get married, for "the afflictions that wait for you will certainly lead you on the way toward
  God"` (p. 459, folio 428).
- Women's convents: `In the later Middle Ages, the chronicles tell about convents where women could
  gather in pursuit of the mystical path or of religious life in general. In Mamluk Egypt, these
  convents had a shaykha who led the congregation in service and prayer. One of those convents was a
  refuge for divorced women, who could stay there until they found an opportunity for remarrying.`
  (p. 463, folio 432).
- Women as lay members of orders: `A number of Sufi orders had women attached to them as lay
  members. Although some of them do not allow women inside the sanctuaries, the love and enthusiasm
  of the female adherents is, as a rule, very ardent.` (p. 463, folio 432).
- `The order that granted the greatest opportunities to women was the Bektashi in Ottoman Turkey.
  Here, they were absolutely equal with men; they had to undergo the same ceremony of initiation and
  joined in the common festive meals and gatherings—a custom that has lent itself to accusations of
  immorality against the Bektashis.` (p. 463, folio 432).
- **Endowment and patronage**: `From the earliest times on, wealthy women are mentioned as
  benefactors of Sufi masters or institutions or of whole groups of dervishes. They would endow the
  khdnqdhs` [text-layer?: khānqāhs] `with food and money` (p. 464, folio 433). Two named instances,
  both on the same page: `Bibi Fatima supported Abu Sacid ibn Abi'l-Khayr and his disciples in the
  early eleventh century`, and `one of Aurangzeb's daughters offered a whole complex of buildings to
  Mir Dard in eighteenth-century Delhi` (p. 464, folio 433).
- The same page generalises the economy: women `could do much good in social service, in founding
  khdnqdhs, or in contributing to the growth of extant facilities for the dervish community` and, `As
  a reward for their help, they might find consolation and spiritual uplift in the gatherings of the
  mystics.` These activities `can still be witnessed in some parts of the Muslim world.` (p. 464,
  folio 433).
- **Ziyārat at women's tombs, Anatolia and Iran**: the small shrines of women `are visited by women
  to express special wishes connected with conjugal life, children, and similar problems. The same
  is true in Iran.` (p. 464, folio 433).
- **Gender-restricted access, South Asia**: `In all the provinces of Muslim India and Pakistan one
  may see shrines of women saints to which men are not admitted` (p. 464, folio 433).
- **Custodianship and offerings, Multan**: `the cheerful women in charge of the tomb were no less
  greedy than the guardians in many other sacred places` (p. 464, folio 433). This is the chunk's
  only remark on shrine income, and it is a complaint rather than a figure — no sums, no endowment,
  no Auqaf — (not stated).
- **Sind**: `The province of Sind, generally famous for its saint worship, is particularly rich in
  legends connected with women saints` (p. 464, folio 433), and women there `occasionally rise to
  the high rank of a murshid` (p. 464, folio 433).
- Modern teaching: `some of the most genuine representatives of mystical tradition, directors of
  souls, in Istanbul and Delhi (and probably in other places as well) are women, who exert a
  remarkable influence upon smaller or larger groups of seekers` (p. 466, folio 435).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **The celibacy debate.** Stories of saints married to `perfectly dreadful women` are `more or less
  explicit illustrations of the much-discussed problem of whether or not a Sufi should lead a
  celibate life, and the authorities in favor of celibacy indulge in describing the horrors and
  dangers of married life` (pp. 458–459, folios 427 and 428). Married life `could be considered, by
  some, as a substitute for the hellfire the saint may escape when patiently enduring the
  afflictions` (p. 459, folio 428).
- **The "world" as woman.** Schimmel traces the trope to `the sermons of the austere preacher Hasan
  al-Basri`, `frequently used by later authors like Ghazzall, cAttar (R 46), and even Rumi`
  (p. 459, folio 428), and connects it to `a tendency found in both classical Sufism and medieval
  Christianity` (p. 459, folio 428).
- **The nafs.** `The fact that the word nafs is feminine in Arabic makes this comparison both easy
  and apt.` `the nafs can be regarded as the mother, the intellect as the father, of man (M
  6:1436)` (p. 460, folio 429).
- **Against the Christian comparison.** `the Muslims scarcely reached the apogee of hatred displayed
  by medieval Christian writers in their condemnation of the feminine element. Eve was never made
  responsible for the fall of Adam, and the often repeated Christian accusation that "woman has no
  soul according to Islam" has no basis in the Koran or in the classical tradition.` (p. 460,
  folio 429).
- **Zulaykhā and Maryam** as the two Koranic figures who carry the positive valuation: Zulaykhā `has
  become, in Sufi poetry, the symbol of the soul, purified by ceaseless longing in the path of
  poverty and love`, `given its classical poetic form by JamI` and, in Turkish, by `Hamdi (d. 1503)`
  (p. 460, folio 429); Maryam `is often taken as the symbol of the spirit that receives divine
  inspiration` (p. 460, folio 429).
- **Two research programmes Schimmel explicitly proposes.** `It would be worthwhile to study the
  role of the mothers in the biographies of the Sufis.` (p. 461, folio 430). `The role of women
  sponsoring Sufi activities deserves special attention, and its importance should be underlined`
  (p. 464, folio 433).
- **Ibn ʿArabī.** `Woman reveals, for Ibn cArabi, the secret of the compassionate God.` The
  argument rests on `The grammatical fact that the word dhdt, "essence," is feminine`, on the
  ḥadīth of `perfumes and women and joy in prayer`, and on Nicholson's condensation quoted at length
  from `(MC 1:155-56)` (p. 462, folio `43!` [text-layer?: 431]). Ibn ʿArabī `did not hesitate to
  admit the possibility that woman may be found among the abddl, the Forty (or Seven) in the
  hierarchy of saints (N 615)` (p. 462, folio `43!` [text-layer?: 431]).
- **Sufism versus orthodoxy on women's participation.** `Sufism, more than stern orthodoxy, offered
  women a certain amount of possibilities to participate actively in the religious and social life.`
  (p. 463, folio 432).
- **Where women saints are thickest.** `Names of women saints are found throughout the world of
  Islam, though only few of them have been entered into the official annals. But the popular
  imagination often invented tender legends that eventually resulted in the emergence of a new woman
  saint.` … `the area in which women saints flourished most is probably Muslim India.` (p. 464,
  folio 433).
- **THE SOUTH ASIAN ARGUMENT — the archive-relevant core of this chunk** (p. 465, folio 434):
  - `In the province of Sind, as well as in Punjab, a special aspect of mystical appreciation of
    women can be observed that is rarely encountered in other parts of the Muslim world, i.e., the
    representation of the longing soul in the form of a woman.`
  - Its source is Hindu, not Persian: `In the western part of Muslim India, however—from Sind to
    Kashmir—the poets followed the Hindu tradition, which describes the soul as the longing girl, a
    faithful wife, or a loving bride.` The classical Hindu example given is `the legend of Krishna
    and the gopis, the cowherd-girls`, with `the whole complex of Shakti mysticism in Indian
    tradition` set aside in parentheses.
  - Its spread across communities: `Sikh mysticism, too, has adopted this imagery. The ginan—sacred
    songs of the Ismaili community of Indo-Pakistan—also depict the soul as a loving female.`
  - Its literary vehicles: `the earliest Urdu-writing Sufi poets—like Sayyid Muhammad Jiw Jan in the
    seventeenth century—knew this bridal symbolism; but it is used mostly in the mystically
    interpreted folk tales of Sind and the Punjab.`
  - Its heroines and what they mean: `The tragic heroines of the Sindhi and Panjabi folk tales—like
    Hir, Sassui, Sohni, and many others—represent the human soul in search of the beloved, a beloved
    to whom she can be united only by endless suffering and eventually through death on the Path.`
  - Two further genres named: `the popular poetical genre of the baramasa, poems dealing with the
    peculiarities of the twelve months and the feeling of the lover in each of these months`, and
    `the "Golden Alphabets" composed by Sindhi and Panjabi folk poets`; in both, `the soul is the
    bride and God—or the Prophet Muhammad—the longed-for bridegroom`.
  - Her claim about its reception: a Western reader `used to the imagery of the "Song of Songs" and
    to medieval Christian bridal mysticism—can more easily appreciate and enjoy these expressions of
    Sufi experience than he can the usual symbolism of Persian and Turkish mystical poetry`
    (pp. 465–466, folios 434 and 435).
- **Nāṣir ʿAndalīb as an Indo-Persian case of the same symbolism** (p. 466, folio 435): he `thought
  very highly of pious women, some of whom "have surpassed many men in learning and mystical
  knowledge and love and charity."` and `is sure that they, too, will be blessed with the vision of
  God in the otherworld`; yet he `held that the wife should regard her husband as the representative
  of the Lord`, quoting `the alleged Prophetic tradition: "If it were permissible to prostrate
  oneself before anyone but God, I would say that the wives should prostrate themselves before their
  husbands"`. Schimmel calls this `reminiscent of the Hindu ideals of marriage` and concludes that
  in his consummation allegory `the Indian tradition of the bridal soul and the Muslim tradition of
