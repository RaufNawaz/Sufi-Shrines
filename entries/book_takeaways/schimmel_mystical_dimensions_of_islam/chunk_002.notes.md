# schimmel_mystical_dimensions_of_islam — chunk 002 — pp. 41–58

**What this chunk is.** The closing thirteen pages of chapter 1, headed `WHAT IS SUFISM?` (pp. 41–53,
folios 10–22), and the opening five pages of chapter 2, headed `2. HISTORICAL OUTLINES of CLASSICAL
SUFISM` with the section head `THE FORMATIVE PERIOD` (pp. 54–58, folios 23–27). Chapter 1's remainder
is (i) a survey of Western scholarship on the *origins* of Sufism, (ii) a florilegium of Sufi
definitions of `tasawwuf`, and (iii) a polemic on the decline of Sufism, false Sufis and pirism.
Chapter 2 opens on `tauhid`, the primordial covenant, the Koran, and the Prophet as first link of the
chain. The matter is definitional and early-Islamic throughout; the places named are Basra-era Iraq
and Iran, Turkestan, India, the Near East and European printing towns. **Archive yield is
accordingly low: three archive rows, all reached by the person rule, all on author-only or
source-only mentions, and ZERO toponym mappings** — see `## Doubts` for the checked explicit
negative.

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Shrine pilgrimage and grave-kissing, as satirised.** In al-Badr al-Ḥijāzī's satire, translated by
  Arberry and quoted by Schimmel: `When he dies, they make him the object of pilgrimage, and hasten
  to his shrine, Arabs and foreigners alike; / Some kiss his grave, and some the threshold of his
  door, and the d u s t . . . .` (p. 53, folio 22; the satire's first three lines are on p. 52,
  folio 21). This is the only shrine practice described in the chunk.
- **Pirism.** `the absolute sway of the leader over his followers and the attendant exploitation of
  ignorant peasants and villagers` (p. 53, folio 22) — Iqbal's term as Schimmel reports it. The
  master is named `the sheikh or plr` [text-layer?: pīr] and the chunk refers the reader to
  `chapter 5` (p. 53, folio 22).
- **Wandering dervishes and mendicancy.** `the degeneration of the wandering dervishes or faqlrs`
  [text-layer?: faqīrs]`, the "poor," who performed miracles and were beyond the law (bl shar*`
  [text-layer?: bī sharʿ]`), has done much to bring Sufism into discredit. It was such people whom
  European travelers in the East met first, so that one of the honorific names given to the genuine
  mystic, faqlr` [text-layer?: faqīr]`, "poor," has become, in German, the designation of a mere
  trickster.` (p. 51, folio 20). And: `it was certainly easier to beg food at the doors of the rich
  and give a blessing in exchange than to pursue a normal profession` (p. 52, folio 21).
- **The patched frock and the food-bowl** as the Sufi's marks, used pejoratively: `"This patched
  frock must have been sewn in pre-eternity"` (`U 70-71`) (p. 51, folio 20); `"I looked into Hell,
  and I saw that most of its inhabitants were those donning a patched frock and carrying a
  food-bowl"` (`B 309`), attributed to `a saint of the eleventh century` (p. 52, folio 21).
- **The woollen garment** as the distinguishing mark of the first generation: `the coarse woolen
  garment of the first generation of Muslim ascetics was their distinguishing mark` (p. 45,
  folio 14); and, in Hujwīrī's list, `jama-i suf`, `saff-i awwal` and `ashab-i Suffa (the people of
  the Bench who gathered around the Prophet's mosque)` (p. 45, folio 14).
- **The mystical concert.** `The mystical concerts in which the Sufis might become enraptured and
  begin to spin around their axis were taken, by many, for the essence,of Sufism.` (p. 51, folio 20;
  the comma-for-space is as printed).
- **The forty-day seclusion.** Adam was forty days `"in seclusion" (like the novice at the beginning
  of the Path)` (p. 47, folio 16) — the parenthesis is Schimmel's gloss on novice practice.
- **The majdhub.** `The type of the majdhub, the "enraptured one" who, under the shock of a mystical
  vision o` [text-layer?: text lost at line end] `any psychological experience, is bereft of his
  senses and walks around in a fashion prohibited by the religious law (i.e., stark naked) belongs to
  the darker side of the Sufi world.` (p. 50, folio 19).
- **Koran recitation as practice.** `the recitation of the Koran was an important means of leading
  the spirit into a meditative state, or even of producing a mystical rapture. Recited in beautiful
  tones, the rhythmic and musical wording of the holy book carried the minds of the devout into
  higher spheres` (p. 57, folio 26).
- **Calligraphy.** `the "letters of the Koran" have been elaborated according to the artistic taste
  of Persians, Turks, Indians, and Africans, creating the most exquisite calligraphy—the typical art
  of the mystics.` (p. 57, folio 26).
- **Book-destruction as an institution of the Path.** `"To break the ink-pots and to tear the books"
  was considered by some mystics the first step in Sufism` (p. 48, folio 17); `other Sufis were urged
  by dreams to cast their precious collections of books into a river (N 432)` (p. 49, folio 18).
- **The minimum learning a Sufi needs**, as Schimmel glosses Hujwīrī: `enough astronomy to find the
  direction of Mecca as required for the correct performance of prayer, enough mathematics to figure
  out the legal amount of alms one has to pay` (p. 48, folio 17).
- **Titles encoding dates.** `other authors may give, by the numerical value of the title, the date
  of its composition.` (p. 45, folio 14) — the chunk gives no example.
- No urs, no mela, no langar, no offerings, no custodianship, no sajjada nashin, no Auqaf, no
  endowment, no land, no revenue, no visitor numbers, no architecture and no building date appears
  anywhere in pp. 41–58 — (not stated).

## Arguments and interpretations

**On the origins of Sufism (pp. 41–43, folios 10–12) — Schimmel surveys and mostly deflates.**

- Iranian: `Sufism has often been considered a typically Iranian development inside Islam. There is
  no doubt that certain important Iranian elements have survived through the ages beneath its
  surface, as both Henri Corbin and Seyyed H. Nasr have recently emphasized.` (p. 41, folio 10).
- Neoplatonic: `Nobody would deny that Neoplatonism had deeply permeated the Near East—the so-called
  "Theology of Aristotle" (which is, in fact, Porphyry's commentary on Plotinus's Enneads) was
  translated into Arabic as early as 840` [text-layer?: undamaged]. Nicholson called it `"in the
  air"` in his 1898 [text-layer?: undamaged] introduction, `the first book in the long list of his
  still unrivaled publications in the field of Sufism`; but Nicholson held that `the original form of
  Sufism is "a native product of Islam itself."` (p. 41, folio 10).
- Christian: pursued by `Adalbert Merx, Arend Jan Wensinck, Margaret Smith`, `who mainly tried to
  explore the relations of Muslims with the Syrian monks`; `The best studies in this field have been
  written by the Swedish Bishop Tor Andrae, to whom we also owe the classical discussion of the
  veneration of the Prophet Muhammad in mystical Islam.` (p. 41, folio 10).
- Indian: `beginning with Alfred von Kremer (1868` [text-layer?: undamaged]`) and Reinhart P. Dozy
  (1869` [text-layer?: undamaged]`). But even Max Morten's numerous articles in this field could not
  bring any stringent proof of such influences in the early period; for later times, the situation is
  slightly different.` (p. 42, folio 11). Schimmel's footnote 14 judges Horten's book `should be used
  with caution`, and footnote 15 says Zaehner's `Hindu and Muslim Mysticism` `overstresses the Indian
  elements` (p. 42, folio 11).
- Turkestani: `For the earliest period, influences from Turkestan are much more important, as Richard
  Hartmann has shown`; Goldziher's Buddhist parallels `can be easily traced back to the common
  sources, e.g., the Indian fables of the Hitopadesa and Panchatantra` (p. 42, folio 11). Schimmel
  warns against a Turkish nationalist reading: `some Turkish mystics who show a tendency of speaking
  of a typically "Turkish" type of mysticism… But such generalizations are dangerous.` (p. 42,
  folio 11).
- Chinese/Taoist: `even the rather far-fetched possibility` — raised `first by Omar Farrukh`, with
  Izutsu's later parallels to `Ibn cArabf s` [text-layer?: Ibn ʿArabī's] `mystical system` (p. 42,
  folio 11).

**On method and on this book's own limits (p. 43, folio 12).** `the main problem is the fact that
previously unknown manuscripts frequently come to light`; `Even now there is so much material
available in the different languages of Islam that any generalization seems impossible. That is why
this book can give only a glimpse of a few aspects of Sufism; even this will, probably, be tinged by
a personal predilection for mystical poetry derived from the large area of Iranian cultural
influence.` — the author's own declared bias.

**On reading mystical texts (p. 44, folio 13).** `many sayings to which we give a deep theological or
philosophical meaning may have been intended to be suggestive wordplay`; some definitions were
uttered `as a sort of ko^an` [text-layer?: kōan]`, a paradox meant to shock the hearer`. Schimmel
endorses Gairdner's question `"Do we not take their language too seriously? It parades as scientific;
it is really poetico-rhetorical."` She adds the structural argument that Arabic's `triliteral roots`
invite this play, likens it to `an arabesque`, and notes that it `is lost in translation` (p. 44,
folio 13). She also flags `the fondness of many Sufi authors for inventing classifications, usually
tripartite`, which `press the meaning of a word rather than explain it` (p. 44, folio 13).

**On the etymology of `tasawwuf` (p. 45, folio 14).** `the derivation from Greek sophos, "wise," is
philologically impossible. The derivation from suf, "wool," is now generally accepted`. Schimmel
insists the definitions `go back to the earliest period and thus defy the tendency of some modern
Western writers to apply this name only to the later "theosophical" aspect of Islamic mysticism.`

**On the structure of the Path (p. 47, folio 16).** `The Sufis have spoken of the threefold meaning
of tasawwuf according to the sharfa` [text-layer?: sharīʿa]`, the Muslim law, the tarlqa`
[text-layer?: ṭarīqa]`, the mystical path, and the haqlqa` [text-layer?: ḥaqīqa]`, the Truth`
(`L 27-28`).

**On Sufi anti-intellectualism (pp. 48–50, folios 17–19).** Sufism `meant, in the formative period,
mainly an interiorization of Islam`; `The Sufis always remained inside the fold of Islam`, and were
not limited `by their adherence to any of the legal or theological schools` (p. 48, folio 17).
Schimmel notes the irony: `it is a fact that they themselves were among the most productive writers
in Islamic history. And many of their theoretical works are no more readable or enjoyable than the
dogmatic treatises that they attacked in their poems.` (p. 49, folio 18). `The main target of Sufi
criticism was philosophy, influenced by Greek thought` (p. 49, folio 18). She marks the social cost:
`Such an anti-intellectualism, as it was sensed by the orthodox, could lead to dangers for the
communal life.` (p. 50, folio 19).

**On decline and false Sufis (pp. 51–53, folios 20–22).** `the complaint about the decline of Sufism
almost coincides with its beginning` (p. 51, folio 20). The distinction is `between the true Sufi,
the mutasawwif who aspires at reaching a higher spiritual level, and the mustawif, the man who
pretends to be a mystic but is a useless, even dangerous, intruder` (p. 51, folio 20), and the Path
is `"hard to travel except for those who were created for that purpose"` (`H 4`). The eleventh-century
formula: `"Today Sufism is a name without reality, but formerly it was a reality without name. . . .
The pretence is known and the practice unknown"` (`H 44`) (p. 51, folio 20). `As time passed the
complaints about the degeneration of Sufism became more eloquent.` (p. 52, folio 21). `The word Sufi
became a pejorative expression` (p. 52, folio 21). Schimmel closes the chapter by announcing the
next: `to reach this point, we have first to travel the long road through the outward history of
Sufism. We shall see how this movement has assumed various shapes appropriate to the times and the
personalities of its leaders, though its substance has remained the same.` (p. 53, folio 22).

**Chapter 2's thesis (pp. 54–58, folios 23–27).**

- `"Islamic mysticism is the attempt to reach individual salvation through attaining the true tauhld"`
  [text-layer?: tauḥīd] `says one of the leading Western orientalists` — footnoted to Hans Heinrich
  Schaeder (p. 54, folio 23). Schimmel endorses it: `the quintessence of the long history of Sunsm`
  [text-layer?: Sufism] `is to express anew, in different formulations, the overwhelming truth that
  "there is no deity but Allah"` (p. 54, folio 23).
- `The history of Sufism is a chart showing some of the stations on this path of interpretation`; its
  `external history is a history of the spiritual, theological, and literary movements inside Islam`
  (pp. 54–55, folios 23–24).
- The primordial covenant as the organising idea: Corbin's `"the religious conscience of Islam is
  centered upon a fact of meta-history"` (`W 46`), `namely, upon the transhistorical fact of the
  primordial covenant as understood from the Koranic word in Sura 7:171`; `"Am I not your Lord?"
  (alastu bi-rabbikum)` and `"Yes, we witness it" (bald shahidna)` [text-layer?: balā shahidnā]. `The
  idea of this primordial covenant (mlthaq` [text-layer?: mīthāq]`) between God and humanity has
  impressed the religious conscience of the Muslims, and especially the Muslim mystics, more than any
  other idea.` `The goal of the mystic is to return to the experience of the "Day of Alastu"`
  (p. 55, folio 24).
- The Koran as source: `The Koran was accepted relatively early by the faithful as uncreated and
  coeternal with God`; Massignon's `"unique lexicon," the "essential textbook of his sciences, the
  key for his Weltanschauung"` (`P 465`) (pp. 55–56, folios 24–25). `The mystics have played a
  decisive role in the development of the Koranic sciences; their hermeneutical methods range from a
  simple verbal interpretation to symbolical and allegorical exegesis, without, however, denying the
  value of the exterior meaning of the Koranic words.` (p. 56, folio 25). `some of whom could find up
  to seven thousand meanings in a single Koranic verse` (p. 56, folio 25).
- The divine names: `the chain of the ninety-nine most beautiful names—names that were to play an
  important role in later mystical theories and in the life of prayer and were sometimes used in
  almost magical connections. The hope of discovering the Greatest Name of God has inspired many a
  Sufi` (p. 56, folio 25).
- Against system-building: `Just as the scholastic theologians defined God by forty-one
  attributes—twenty attributes of necessity, twenty of impossibility, and one of possibility—many of
  the later Sufis invented complicated systems… Pious mystics have often objected to these
  pseudophilosophical definitions containing names and words not found in the Koran` (p. 57,
  folio 26). `al-haqq` is `a word that was used by most of the later mystics to designate God`
  (p. 57, folio 26).
- `One can certainly speak of a "koranization" of the memory` (p. 57, folio 26).
- The Prophet's illiteracy argued by parallel: `just as in Christianity, where God reveals Himself
  through Christ—the word made flesh—the virginity of Mary is required in order to produce an
  immaculate vessel for the divine word, so in Islam, where God reveals Himself through the word of
  the Koran, the Prophet had to be a vessel that was unpolluted by "intellectual" knowledge of word
  and script` (pp. 57–58, folios 26–27).
- On hadith: `Every tendency within Islam, and so within Sufism, found material to support its claims
  from Prophetic traditions. In later times a considerable number of hadlth` [text-layer?: ḥadīth]
  `that are not found in the official collections as they were compiled in the second half of the
  ninth century were used by the Sufis.` (p. 58, folio 27).
- On Western scholarship's picture of the Prophet: `The Western student of Islam, used to the
  traditional picture of Muhammad as it emerged during hundreds of years of hatred and enmity in the
  Christian world, will be surprised to see the strong "mystical" qualities attributed to this man`
  (p. 58, folio 27). And the author's own agnosticism about the sources: `We do not know how many of
  the later tales of Muhammad's ascetic piety are true and how many simply reflect the ideals of
  later mystical devotion.` (p. 58, folio 27).
