## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The Mevlevi order and the Ottoman state.** `The Mevlevi order, which had been institutionalized
  by Sultan Walad and whose ritual was later elaborated, spread Rumi's word and music through the
  just-emerging Ottoman Empire; and in later times the head of the Mevlevi order was so closely
  connected with the Ottoman court that he had the privilege of girding the Sultan with the sword.`
  (p. 355, folio 324).
- **Seat, titles and branches.** `The center of the order was always in Konya; the leader was called
  by the honorific titles Molla Hunkar and Qelebi` [text-layer?: Çelebi]. `Many smaller tekkes of
  the Mevlevis were found all over the Ottoman Empire, as far away as Egypt and Syria, although the
  order never crossed the Ottoman borders.` (p. 355, folio 324).
- **The samāʿ: timing and dress.** `At the occasion of the ceremonial samac` [text-layer?: samāʿ]`,
  usually held on Friday at noon after the congregational prayer, the dervishes used to wear their
  typical dress: a tennure, a white sleeveless frock, the destegiil` [text-layer?: destegül]`, a
  jacket with long sleeves, a belt, and a black khirqa, used as an overcoat but cast aside before
  the ritual dance started.` The head was covered by `a high felt cap around which a turban cloth
  might be wound`; `The cap, sikke, became the typical sign of the Mevlevis, and many inscriptions
  composed of words of prayers or blessings have been written in the shape of the dervish hat.`
  (p. 356, folio 325).
- **The samāʿ: choreography and discipline.** `The samcf` [text-layer?: samāʿ] `is regulated by very
  strict rules. The sheikh stands in the most honored corner of the dancing place, and the dervishes
  pass by him three times, each time exchanging greetings, until the circling movement starts. This
  is to be performed on the right foot, with accelerating speed. If a dervish should become too
  enraptured, another Sufi, who is in charge of the orderly performance, will gently touch his frock
  in order to curb his movement.` (p. 356, folio 325).
- **The music of the ritual** begins `with the great hymn in honor of the Prophet (na't-i sharif,
  written by Jalaluddm himself) and ending with short, enthusiastic songs, sometimes sung in
  Turkish` (p. 356, folio 325).
- **The order as patron of the arts.** `the best pieces of Turkish classical music, such as those by
  cltri (seventeenth century), were composed by artists who were either members of, or at least
  loosely connected with, the order. The same is true of the calligraphers and miniaturists, many of
  whom belonged to the Mevlevis.` (p. 356, folio 325).
- **Pilgrimage to Rūmī's shrine and the Turkish republican suppression.** `the thousands of visitors
  who come to pay their respect at his shrine in Konya and the large crowds that throng there for
  the festivities in commemoration of his death in December would prove how deeply this love is
  rooted even forty-five years after Atatiirk` [text-layer?: Atatürk] `closed the dervish lodges and
  strictly prohibited any activity of mystical fraternities.` (p. 357, folio 326). **The chunk gives
  no year for the closing and no visitor figure** — `(not stated)`; `forty-five years` is counted
  from an unstated base.
- **Tekke song as a genre.** `The genre of ildhi` [text-layer?: ilāhī]`, a mystical poem in popular
  meter meant to be sung in the meetings of the dervishes, is the typical expression of this
  tradition` — of `poems sung in the tekkes, the centers of Sufi life` (p. 361, folio 330).
- **Dhikr formulae from poetry.** `As shown with great insight by John R. Walsh, a number of Yunus's
  poems, repeating al-hamdulillah, "praise be to God," or other pious sentences at the end of each
  verse, were probably meant to be recited as dhikr formulae in the dervish communities.` (p. 363,
  folio 332). One such poem `is still memorized in Turkish schools` (same page); Yunus's poems more
  generally `are still known today by school children` (p. 360, folio 329).
- **Loose affiliation before the orders.** `Those called Emre probably formed a subgroup of mystics
  in Anatolia, where the system of loosely affiliating oneself with a spiritual guide was apparently
  still in use; the major orders had not yet developed a "missionary" activity.` (p. 361, folio 330).
- **The `abdalan-i Rum`.** `Kaygusuz may have been one of the abdalan-i Rum, dervishes who wandered
  through the Ottoman Empire covered with animal skins and wearing a silver earring.` `The abdalan-i
  Rum were also notorious for their use of opium.` (p. 366, folio 335).
- **A devotional book-list from Sind.** `It is said that some mystics in Sind—and it may be true for
  other provinces as well-kept only three books for spiritual nourishment: the Koran, the Mathnawi,
  and the Diwdn of Hafiz.` (p. 358, folio 327; the book's note 42 attributes this to `Ghulam Mustafa
  Qasimi, "Hashimlya Library," in Mihrdn ja Mofi` [text-layer?: reading not offered]).
- **Translation practice in the Subcontinent.** Nahifi's verse translation `seems to be the first
  complete translation into a foreign language in which concern for the meter was paramount; in
  later times we find similar attempts in the Subcontinent, where Urdu, Panjabi, and Sindhi
  translations have been produced in the same style.` (p. 357, folio 326).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **Plato at Konya**, given as local popular tradition: `Popular tradition in the province of Konya
  has it that Plato lived for many years in this region and that he was a great sorcerer; close to
  the lake of Beyshehir south of Konya, a Hittite monument at a fountainhead is called Eftdtun
  Pinan, "Plato's Spring," for it was here that the great magician transformed the country into its
  present shape.` (p. 349, folio 318).
- **How Konya got its name**, given by Schimmel as `A folk tale`: `"Two saints came from Khurasan,
  gently flying and spreading their wings until they saw the summits of the two hills that bound the
  Konya plain. They liked the view, and the one asked the other: Konahm mi? 'Shall we dwell there?'
  And the other replied: Kon ya! 'Certainly, alight and dwell!' "` Schimmel treats the pun as
  evidence that `the Anatolian people were well aware that the strongest influence upon the
  formation of mystical thought came from Khurasan` (p. 359, folio 328).
- **Barak Baba's conduct** is reported `according to the sources`: he `went around shouting like a
  bear and dancing like a monkey` (p. 366, folio 335).
- **Kaygusuz Abdal's tomb**, `according to tradition`, `is on the Muqattam Hill, where many saints
  of Cairo are buried` (p. 366, folio 335).
- **The chronicler's boast about the Mathnawī in East Bengal**: `In East Bengal, in the fifteenth
  century, its impression was so great that a chronicler said: "The holy Brahmin will recite the
  Mathnawi."` (p. 355, folio 324; the footnote marker after the quotation prints `ss` for 35 — see
  `## Doubts`).
- **The disputed grave of Yunus Emre**: `numerous places in Turkey claim to house his real tomb`
  (p. 361, folio 330).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **Against a Neoplatonic genealogy for Rūmī.** `Attempts have been made to trace much of Rumi's
  theology back to Neoplatonic influences, but it is almost as impossible to disentangle the
  colorful strands of the fabric of his feeling (and, rarely, his thought) as it is to analyze one of
  the colorful brocades of which he sometimes speaks in his poetry.` She concedes `That there are
  Neoplatonic themes in the Mathnawl cannot be doubted`, but concludes: `On the whole, however, his
  work can be explained without difficulty from the Koran and the Prophetic tradition.` (p. 349,
  folio 318).
- **Christian and Hellenistic Konya as context.** `Greek and Christian traditions were very much
  alive in Konya in the thirteenth century`; the Cappadocian centres and `the large monastic
  settlement in the caves near Goreme were only a few days' journey from the capital`, which
  Schimmel offers as why `images alluding to Jesus and Mary occur more frequently in Jalaluddm's
  poetry than in any other comparable poetical work` (p. 349, folio 318).
- **Against the Darwinian reading of `I died as mineral`.** Schimmel sets out the modernist readings
  (Khalīfa ʿAbdul Ḥakīm on medieval anticipation of evolution, Afzal Iqbal on the free development
  of the ego, Muhammad Iqbal on individualization, Golpınarlı on the struggle for survival), then
  rules against them: `But the quotation of Hallaj's verses at the turning point of the chickpea
  story … lends itself to a purely mystical interpretation and seems to exclude a pseudoscientific
  understanding of such verses.` (p. 353, folio 322).
- **Where Rūmī's influence was greatest.** `Rumi's work had its largest influence in the
  Indo-Pakistan Subcontinent, where his poetry has been popular since the early fourteenth century.`
  By contrast his poetry never `become as popular with the Arabic-speaking Sufis as with the
  Persian-speaking mystics; the imagery used was too different` (pp. 355 and 358, folios 324 and
  327). In Iran, `Rumi's work was widely read … up to the Safawid period, when the interest in Sufi
  literature was curbed.` (p. 357, folio 326).
- **How the Subcontinent read him.** `Most of the poets of the Subcontinent—as in Turkey—saw in Rumi
  a representative of the pantheistic trend that had colored most Sufi expressions after Ibn
  cArabi's time`, and `only toward the beginning of the twentieth century were new approaches made
  toward a better understanding` (p. 359, folio 328).
- **The Yunus Emre text problem.** `Just as his tomb will probably never be certainly identified, it
  is almost impossible to select those poems that are really his.` Golpınarlı's successive editions
  `show the learned author's increasing skepticism as to the authenticity of Yunus's poems. At
  present it seems impossible to establish a reliable text—a fact that holds true for many other
  popular Turkish poets.` Her working position: `It is a comfortable working hypothesis to accept as
  Yunus Emre's the poems collected by Golpmarli in his 1943 edition, though many of them are now
  considered spurious.` (p. 361, folio 330).
- **Against reading Yunus as heterodox Turkish nationalism.** `when modern Turkish interpreters of
  his poetry tend to regard him as a representative of unorthodox "Turkish national piety," they
  forget how deeply rooted in the Muslim tradition is his mystical approach` (p. 363, folio 332).
  Likewise on the Turks' claim to him: `"their" Mevlana (for they will never admit that he was not a
  Turk)` (p. 357, folio 326).
- **A structural link to vernacular Sufi poetry of the Subcontinent.** Of Yunus's `I am the First
  and the Last` identity poems: `These poems are in form and content almost identical with later
  Sufi poetry written in the several languages of the Indian Subcontinent.` (p. 365, folio 334).
- **On the traditional commentators.** After quoting Niyazi Misri's allegorical reading of the plum,
  grape and nut as `sharfa` [text-layer?: sharīʿa]`, tariqa` [text-layer?: ṭarīqa] `and haqlqa`
  [text-layer?: ḥaqīqa]: `This interpretation may or may not be correct, but it shows how
  commentators in later centuries discovered the tenets of mystical truth even in a tekerleme.`
  (p. 366, folio 335).
- **On Rūmī's technique.** `There is not a single poetical or rhetorical form that he does not use
  skillfully` (p. 349, folio 318); `there are few poets, especially among the mystics, whose
  repertoire in vocabulary and imagery is as rich as his` (p. 350, folio 319); and `Rumi does not
  refrain from using extremely coarse expressions to shock, perhaps to awaken, his listeners`
  (p. 350, folio 319).
