## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Tomb visitation and rain prayer at Maʿrūf al-Karkhī's grave in Baghdad.** `his prayers were
  heard, and after his death people from Baghdad would cross the Tigris to the section of Karkh and
  pray for rain at his tomb.` (p. 84, folio 53).
- **Sites of blessing dedicated to Bayezid far outside Iran, with a described practice at one of
  them.** `There are still sacred places dedicated to the memory of the lonely mystic of Bistam in
  the remotest corners of the Islamic world: in Zousfana in the Maghreb and in Chittagong in Bangla
  Desh, where huge, whitish turtles inhabit a tank to which people come to obtain blessings for
  themselves and for their children.` (pp. 81–82, folios 50–51). No dates, custodians, numbers or
  endowments — (not stated).
- **A named cemetery.** `one of the cemeteries along the Byzantine wall of Istanbul still bears his
  name` — Merkez Efendi's (p. 77, folio 46).
- **Succession chosen by a test of the disciples**, in `the Khalvati order in Istanbul`: the sheikh
  `sent his disciples forth to get flowers to adorn the convent` (p. 77, folio 46). See
  `## Legends`.
- **The rise of a strict discipleship system.** Abū ʿUthmān al-Ḥīrī `is regarded as one of the first
  to introduce a strict system of education for his disciples, a trend that developed finally into
  the perinde ac cadaver obedience that distinguishes later Sufi education.` (p. 84, folio 53).
- **Initiation chains.** Of Junayd: `The representatives of divergent mystical schools and modes of
  thought could refer to him as their master, so that the initiation chains of later Sufi orders
  almost invariably go back to him.` (p. 88, folio 57).
- **A named order and a named school.** `the founder of the Naqshbandl` [text-layer?: Naqshbandī]
  `order in the fourteenth century attributed to him a particular power when the disciple undergoes
  the process of directing his concentration toward the spirit of one of the great masters (N 119)`
  (p. 88, folio 57) — **the founder is not named** (not stated). And: `their school was known as the
  Salimiyya` (p. 87, folio 56).
- **The saintly hierarchy as an institution of world-government.** `The leader of the Sufi hierarchy
  is the qutb, "pole" or "pivot," or ghauth, "help." The saints govern the universe, certain groups
  of three, seven, forty, or three hundred saints being entrusted with various duties in maintaining
  the world order (see Chapter 4).` (p. 88, folio 57).
- **Work and livelihood against trust in God.** Sahl `tried to combine tawakkul and work; for, as he
  thought, it would be an offense to the Prophetic tradition to avoid or condemn work as a means of
  gaining one's livelihood, but an offense against the faith to neglect "trust in God."` (p. 87,
  folio 56). Ruwaym, by contrast, `explained to his surprised visitor that he liked to care for his
  family and would not leave everything to tawakkul (X 85)` (p. 90, folio 59).
- **Relation to political authority.** Sahl `tried to remain aloof from the theological discussions
  that had shaken the Muslim community in the preceding decades; he advocated the duty of obeying
  the political ruler.` (p. 87, folio 56). He `was eventually exiled to Basra` (p. 87, folio 56).
- **Public preaching as a distinguishable office.** `although a number of Sufis are related to hav`
  [text-layer?: have] `preached in public, he is the only one to be distinguished by the title
  al-wdciz, "the preacher."` (p. 82, folio 51).
- **Orthodox suspicion of the Sufis as a constraint on speech.** `particularly since orthodox circles
  viewed the activities of the Sufis with growing suspicion` (p. 90, folio 59).


## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- Dhū'n-Nūn and the Greatest Name, attributed by Schimmel to Shaʿrānī's `Lawaqih`: `"One said to
  Dhu D n-Nun: 'Show me the Greatest Name of God.' He said: 'Show me the smallest one!'—and scolded
  him"` (p. 75, folio 44). Schimmel adds that the reply `reveals the pious Muslim rather than a man
  who relies upon magical practice` (p. 75, folio 44).
- Dhū'n-Nūn and the woman at the sea shore, attributed to `T i: 123`: `'What is the end of love?'
  And she answered: 'O simpleton, love has no end.'` (p. 76, folio 45). Schimmel notes the stranger
  `in other cases it may be a slave girl, a negro, or an old anchorite` (p. 76, folio 45).
- Shaʿrānī hearing the creatures, quoted from `Lawaqih`: `I heard the voices of the creatures in the
  praise of God, with elevated voices so that I feared for my mind. I heard the fishes who said:
  Praised be the King, the Most Holy, the Lord.` (p. 77, folio 46).
- Merkez Efendi and the withered flower — Schimmel's own attribution: `a charming story was told to
  me by Turkish friends` (p. 77, folio 46); the disciple `found all the flowers busy recollecting the
  Lord`, brought back `one flower` that `had finished its recollection`, and became successor
  (p. 77, folio 46).
- Bayezid's flight beyond space, given by Schimmel as a question rather than a report: `his
  description of his flight beyond space three times thirty thousand years—until eventually he found
  nothing but Bayezid in the divine throne and behind the veil that hides God` (p. 79, folio 48).
- Bayezid faints at the call to prayer, attributed to Ritter's collection (footnote 27): `Bayezid
  once uttered the call to prayer and fainted.` (p. 79, folio 48).
- Bayezid as a mirror, as Rūmī tells it: his disciples rebelled at `"Under my garment there is
  nothing but God!"`, `But when they tried to kill him, the strokes of their knives wounded
  themselves (M 4:2102-40), for the perfect saint is a pure mirror who reflects the attributes of
  others to them.` (p. 81, folio 50).
- The Zoroastrian too weak to embrace Bayezid's religion, told by Rūmī and reused by Iqbal (p. 81,
  folio 50) — see the `mazar-e-iqbal` bullet above.
- Maʿrūf al-Karkhī: `Legends speak of his strong mystical power; his prayers were heard` (p. 84,
  folio 53).
- Sarī as-Saqaṭī's scrupulosity: `he was afraid that his nose might turn black because of his sins`
  (p. 84, folio 53).
- Muḥāsibī's warning nerve: `Muhasibi claimed to have a nerve in his finger that would warn him
  whenever food was not perfectly clean legally (N 572)` (p. 85, folio 54).
- Sarī in illness, from the `Nafahdt al-uns`: `he, eventually exhausted, taught them to pray: 'O God,
  teach us how to behave when visiting the sick' " (N 54; A 10:122)` (pp. 84–85, folios 53–54).
- Bayezid's twelve-year self-forging: `for twelve years he was the blacksmith of his self until he
  made of himself a pure mirror (T 1:139)` (p. 80, folio 49).


## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **Dhū'n-Nūn's place is left open, and Schimmel says so.** `A detailed study of his life and work
  would, in all probability, reveal many previously unknown aspects of his teachings and show
  whether he was, indeed, the first "theosophist" among the Sufis or rather the hymnodist who
  rediscovered the divine glory as ,praised by the creatures.` (p. 78, folio 47).
- **A shift in the Sufi valuation of the created world is dated to Dhū'n-Nūn.** The world's religious
  meaning is `a meaning that the early ascetics had lost sight of because they con sidered it to be
  a detestable veil that distracted them from God. But for Dhu3n-Nun and the generations of Sufis
  following him.'the worth or worthlessness of the world is determined not by itself, but by man's
  relation to it.` (p. 76, folio 45).
- **Otto's tremendum/fascinans is treated as a late restatement of Sufi knowledge.** `he has, thus,
  expressed in scientific language a truth that had been known to every Sufi in the world of Islam
  for centuries` (p. 75, folio 44).
- **Schimmel rejects, with reasons, the strong Indian-influence thesis for Bayezid.** She names
  `R. C. Zaehner` as having `stressed the possibility of Indian influences`, calls the as-Sindī story
  `still doubtful`, and argues: `even if this man had been from the lower Indus valley and not from a
  village called Sind close to Bistam, it seems scarcely possible to draw far-reaching consequences
  from this geographical fact: not every man from Sind could be expected to know all the intricacies
  of Hindu monistic philosophy.` She concedes `some of the equations brought forth by Zaehner seem
  very plausible`, yet concludes `it seems more likely that the mystic of Bistam should have reached
  his goal by means of the Islamic experience of fana` (p. 78, folio 47), because `Bayezid hoped for
  a complete extinction of the traces of self, not for an extension of anything created (cf. MM 56)`
  (p. 79, folio 48).
- **Ritter's study is named the best European treatment**: `The finest one is the short and
  penetrating study by Hellmut Ritter.` (p. 78, folio 47).
- **A dated turning point in the systematisation of Sufism.** `Kharraz was writing a treatise on
  saintship at almost the same time that Sahl at-Tustari (d. 896) was discussing the problem of
  saintship and Tirmidhi was working on his book Khatm al-auliya> … This coincidence indicates that
  during the last two or three decades of the ninth century the necessity for a systematization of
  mystical thought was being felt, and that the problem of sanctity and saintship was one of the
  central ones at the time.` (p. 86, folio 55).
- **Kharrāz as the source of Ḥallāj's `anā'l-Ḥaqq`, on Nwyia's evidence.** `Kharraz goes even further
  by showing that this divine "I" is ontologically connected with the divine name al-Haqq, "the
  Reality"—this seems to be the nucleus of Hallaj's famous phrase anffl-Haqq` [text-layer?:
  anā'l-Ḥaqq] `(W 249)` (p. 86, folio 55). Also `Recently discovered documents reveal that he can be
  regarded as one of Junayd's masters` (p. 86, folio 55), and on Junayd's formation: `the influence
  of Kharraz on his formation is apparently greater than has been proved at the moment` (p. 89,
  folio 58).
- **Sahl as a probable source for Ḥallāj's doctrine of lordliness.** `only the saints are
  predestined to attain the mystery of lordliness, sirr ar-rububiya—here lies, probably, one source
  of Hallaj's similar theories, for Hallaj had lived with Sahl for a while.` (p. 87, folio 56).
- **The preexistent light of Muhammad dated to this milieu.** `it was the time in both Sufi and Shia
  circles that theories about the preexistent light of Muhammad were being developed` (p. 87,
  folio 56).
- **Sobriety against intoxication as the dividing line between Bayezid and Junayd.** `Bayezid Bistami
  preferred mystical intoxication`; `Junayd and his followers, however, considered the "second
  sobriety" the highest and preferable state`; `Fand, "annihilation," is not the ultimate goal, but
  baqd, "remaining," a new life in God.` (pp. 89–90, folios 58–59).
- **Tirmidhī's hierarchy is one of gnosis, not of love.** `The degrees of sainthood as sketched by
  Tirmidhi are related to the degree of illumination and gnosis reached by the person in question—it
  is not a "hierarchy of love."` (p. 88, folio 57).
- **Junayd's deliberate obscurity is explained, not just described.** `It was for this reason that he
  had rejected Hallaj`; `Junayd, therefore, refined the art of speaking in ishardt, subtle allusion
  to the truth—a trend, attributed first to Kharraz, that became characteristic of later Sufi
  writings.` (p. 90, folio 59).
- **Schimmel's footnote verdicts on the secondary literature.** Of van Ess's book: `is an excellent
  analysis of Muhasibi's teachings` (p. 85, folio 54, footnote 31). Of Cihad Tunç's dissertation:
  `Tune is a Turkish theologian; his discussion of Sahl is not fully satisfactory.` (p. 87, folio 56,
  footnote 34).
