# schimmel_mystical_dimensions_of_islam — chunk 006 — pp. 109–124

**What this chunk is.** Sixteen consecutive pages of continuous exposition inside chapter 2, whose
running header throughout is `H I S T O R I C A L O U T L I N E S OF C L A S S I C A L S U F I S M`
(normalised from letter-spaced capitals). No chapter opening, no plate, no blank page, no section
break with a dropped heading: the chunk begins mid-sentence (`sion to the mystical life`, p. 109) and
ends mid-sentence (`I began by reading their books`, p. 124). The narrative runs from Shiblī's death
through Niffarī, the tenth-century handbook writers (Sarrāj, Kalābādhī, Makkī), the *ṭabaqāt*
compilers (Sulamī, Abū Nuʿaym), the Malāmatiyya, Qushayrī, **Hujwīrī**, Anṣārī of Herat, and into
Ghazzālī.

**Archive yield is high for this book and it is concentrated on one page.** `data-darbar` is reached
directly — Schimmel names Hujwīrī, his coming to Lahore, his death there, and his shrine by its
popular name, all on p. 119. Two toponyms fire ruling (a): **Lahore** (35 rows) on p. 119 and
**Multan** (8 rows) on p. 113. **42 distinct archive ids** are carried. One further toponym,
**Karachi**, occurs only as a publisher's imprint city in a footnote and is **declined** — see
`## Doubts`, where it is one step to reverse.

**Folio regime — all sixteen pages print a header folio, and parity passes 16/16.** Every page of
pp. 109–124 carries the folio inside the running-header line; not one page of this chunk is a
chapter- or section-opening page, so the foot-of-page case does not arise anywhere here and no page
takes `folio not stated`. Leading (verso) folios 78, 80, 82, 84, 86, 88, 90, 92 are all **even**;
trailing (recto) folios 79, 81, 83, 85, 87, 89, 91, 93 are all **odd**. `folio = PDF − 31` holds on
all sixteen without exception. Three of the sixteen are printed with the digits-as-letters damage and

p. 112 prints `8l` [text-layer?: 81], p. 122 prints `Ql` [text-layer?: 91]. The headers are apparatus

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Sufi trades, as read off their surnames** — `Many Sufis followed normal professions to earn their
  livelihoods. The surnames of some of them point to these professions: saqati, "huckster"; halldj`
  [text-layer?: ḥallāj]`, "cotton carder"; nassaj, "weaver"; warrdq` [text-layer?: warrāq]`,
  "bookseller" or "copyist"; qawdrln` [text-layer?: qawārīrī — printed `qawdrln`, reading uncertain,
  see `## Doubts`]`, "glassmaker"; hadddd` [text-layer?: ḥaddād]`, "blacksmith" were among them.`
  (p. 115, folio 84).
- **Redistribution of earnings** — `Some would work regularly and use a trifle of their gain for
  themselves, distributing the main part to the Sufis, so that it could be said, for example, that a
  person "veiled his saintliness under the modest shape of a cupper"` (cited as `N 572`) (p. 115,
  folio 84). `Some left their original professions after they had gained fame as mystical leaders and
  attracted a few disciples. Others were trained as theologians, traditionalists, or jurists in one
  of the four schools of law.` (p. 115, folio 84).
- **Dhikr** — `The genuine mystical practices, like the performance of the dhikr, the constant
  recollection of God, occupied only part of their time.` (p. 115, folio 84).
- **Law and the Sufis** — `They did not reject the religious law but rather added to it—additions
  that made more exacting demands on their personal lives.` (p. 115, folio 84).
- **Pilgrimage to a shrine** — the one pilgrimage statement in the chunk is Hujwīrī's shrine at
  Lahore, recorded above (p. 119, folio 88).
- **Sayyid status and baraka, stated as a living fact of the author's own day** — `Widespread, too,
  was the veneration of the sayyids, Muhammad's descendants through CA1I` [text-layer?: ʿAlī] `and
  Fatima. Even in our day some of the sayyid families in countries like Muslim India or Pakistan
  consider themselves exalted above the common Muslim, surrounded by a sanctity or transmitting a
  baraka (spiritual power, blessing) that gives them a peculiar status. This veneration shown to the
  ahl al-bayt, the Prophet's family, constitutes in later times a very important aspect of popular
  Sufism.` (p. 113, folio 82). The only explicit mention of Pakistan in the chunk.
- **The monastic rule** — `the first to draw up a simple monastic rule for his Sufi community`
  (Abū Saʿīd ibn Abīʾl-Khair) (p. 119, folio 88).
- **Madrasas as state institutions** — `Nizamulmulk, the vizier, appointed Ghazzali professor at the
  Nizamiyya madrasa in Baghdad. With the zeal of an ardent adherent of Ashcarite theology, the vizier
  had founded colleges (madrasa) all over the Seljuk territories.` (p. 122, folio 91) `They served as
  training institutions for theologians and proved to be models for later colleges in the Muslim
  world. The school at Baghdad, the seat of the caliphate, was without doubt the most important among
  the institutions founded by Nizamulmulk.` (p. 123, folio 92).
- **Handbook production as a deliberate programme** — `it was considered important to prove to the
  world the perfect orthodoxy of Sufi tenets, and therefore a number of books were composed almost
  simultaneously in the last quarter of the tenth century.` (p. 115, folio 84).
- **The Malāmatī discipline and its decay** — `the Malamatis deliberately tried to draw the contempt
  of the world upon themselves by committing unseemly, even unlawful, actions, but they preserved
  perfect purity of heart` (p. 117, folio 86); Anṣārī's complaint that later claimants brought forth
  `licentiousness and treating lightly of the Divine law and heresy and lack of etiquette and

  wandering dervish who performs only the absolute minimum in religious duties` (p. 118, folio 87).


## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- Shiblī's *himma*: when he told his disciples `to go away and to know that wherever they were they
  were under his protection and he would be with them`, this `points to the power of himma, that
  "high spiritual ambition," or "power," that is strong enough to keep safe those who believe in the
  master` (cited as `6^322` — a damaged siglum, see `## Doubts`); `The claim of the sheikh to possess
  this strong himma is reflected in many legends about saints of later times.` (p. 110, folio 79).
  Attributed by Schimmel to Baqlī's preservation of Shiblī's paradoxes.
- The *munāqara* or "quarrel" of saints: `He threw one of his fellow mystics into the Tigris, saying,
  "If he is sincere, he will be saved, like Moses; if not, he will be drowned, like Pharaoh." A few
  days later he was challenged by that very person to take live charcoals from an oven without being
  hurt` (cited as `B 494`) (p. 110, folio 79). Schimmel adds that `this kind of contest was not
  uncommon among the early Sufis; later sheikhs used to settle questions of priority in a similar way.`
- Shiblī on hellfire: `"fire of Hell will not touch me, and I can easily extinguish it"`; and `In
  another saying, Shibli claimed that hellfire could not burn even a single hair on his body.`
  (p. 110, folio 79). Baqlī explains this by the fire God ordered to be `'cool and pleasant' [Sura
  21:69], as He did for Abraham` (cited as `B 460`), and by *iltibās* (p. 110, folio 79).
- The ḥadīth in which `Hell addresses the true believer with the words: "Thy light has extinguished
  my flames"` (cited as `B 452`) — a tradition `of which the Sufis were particularly fond` (p. 111,
  folio 80).
- The Malāmatī story told by Jāmī: `"One of them was hailed by a large crowd when he entered a town;
  they tried to accompany the great saint; but on the road he publicly started urinating in an
  unlawful way so that all of them left him and no longer believed in his high spiritual rank"`
  (cited as `N 264`) (p. 117, folio 86).
- Sarrāj in ecstasy: `"and the sheikh got into a 'state' and put his face on the fireplace and
  prostrated himself before God in the midst of the fire without being hurt"` (cited as `N 283`),
  given as `according to one story` (p. 115, folio 84).
- Kharaqānī and Bāyezīd Bisṭāmī: `it is said that the scent of Kharaqani reached Bayezid long before
  his spiritual disciple was born` (cited as `M 4:1802-50`) (p. 120, folio 89).
- Kharaqānī's *ṭayy al-arḍ*: `Kharaqani used to pray every evening in Kharaqan, then mysteriously be
  transported to Bistam—a typical miracle of "rolling up space"—pray there, and then perform the
  morning prayer back in his own village` (cited as `T 2:201`) (pp. 120–121, folios 89–90).
- Kharaqānī's oath: `he swore that he would not give his soul to the angel of death, for he had
  received it from God, and only to Him would he return it` (cited as `T 2:212`); and God's words to
  him in a dream, `"for We have loved thee already in the pre-eternity of eternities"` (cited as
  `T 2:253`) (p. 121, folio 90).
- Ash-Shādhilī's dream vision, told by Jāmī: `"The North African Sufi leader Abu^l-Hasan ash-Shadhili
  (d. 1258) saw in a dream vision that the Prophet of Islam was extolling himself with Ghazzali
  before Moses and Jesus . . . and he had ordered the punishment of some who had denied him, and the
  marks of the whip remained visible on their bodies until they died"` (cited as `N 373`) (p. 122,
  folio 91).
- Shiblī's rebuke at a funeral: `"Shibli saw somebody weeping because his beloved had died and blamed
  him: 'O fool, why do you love someone who can die?'"` (cited as `T 2:172`) (p. 109, folio 78).


## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **Periodisation.** `In certain aspects the late tenth century was the period of organiza-tion and
  consolidation for Sufism.` (p. 113, folio 82) — the chapter's own thesis stated in the chunk, and
  the sentence the chapter title rests on.
- **Shia–Sufi relations are declared unsettled, not settled.** `The relationship between Shia thought
  as it crystallized in the ninth and tenth centuries and the theories of Sufism that emerged at
  about the same time has not yet been completely elucidated` (p. 113, folio 82); but `some of the
  Sufi teachings about the primordial light of Muhammad and the theories of saintship seem to
  correspond very closely in their hierarchical structure to Shiite theories about the imamate`
  (p. 114, folio 83). And afterwards: `from that time onward Sufism and the Shia creed were rarely
  combined, and only a few Shia orders exist today.` (p. 114, folio 83).
- **Sentimental allegiance without doctrine.** `It is a well-known fact that many of the Sufis—and
  many of the pious Sunnites in general—felt a kind of sentimental allegiance to the family of the
  Prophet without believing in Shia doctrines.` (p. 113, folio 82).
- **Against the word "reconciliation."** `It would, however, be wrong to speak of a real
  "reconciliation" between Sufism and orthodoxy. For the Sufis were, on the whole, as good Muslims as
  the rest of the community in Baghdad, Nishapur, or Egypt.` (pp. 114–115, folios 83–84).
- **Against the received view of Hanbalism.** `The traditional idea that Hanbalite rigorism and
  mystical emotion are mutually exclusive can no longer be maintained` — evidenced by Anṣārī and by
  ʿAbduʾl-Qādir Gīlānī (p. 120, folio 89).
- **Source criticism, stated plainly.** On Abū Nuʿaym: `his book is a storehouse of information,
  which, however, must be used with caution. Yet even in its present edition, which is not free from
  mistakes, the book is indispensable for the study of the biographies of early Sufis.` (p. 116,
  folio 85). On Kalābādhī: `His book is, on the whole, the somewhat dry exposition of a Hanafi jurist
  and not as enjoyable as Sarraj's study.` (p. 116, folio 85). On Sarrāj: `The matters at stake are
  lucidly laid before the reader`, `though his book apparently has not been as widely read as other
  handbooks.` (p. 115, folio 84).
- **A comparative proposal Schimmel does not carry out.** `a comparison between Niffari's experiences
  as set forth in his books with those of the eighteenth-century Indo-Muslim mystic Mir Dard
  (contained in his ^Ilm ul-kitab) would yield significant similarities.` (p. 112, folio 81).
- **Cross-religious comparison, twice.** Niffarī on prayer as a divine gift is set beside Pascal:
  `"You would not seek Me if you had not found Me."` (p. 112, folio 81); and beside `Francis
  Thompson's Hound of Heaven` (p. 113, folio 82). Molé is cited for the Malāmatī parallel: `Marijan
  Mole (MM 72-74) has shown that, particularly among the early Syrian Christians, there was a similar
  trend to hide one's virtuous actions` (p. 117, folio 86).
- **Vernacularisation as an epoch marker.** Applied to Hujwīrī (Persian *Kashf*, p. 119, folio 88) and
  to Anṣārī, whose work `was written in part in his Persian mother tongue` and who `translated
  Sulami's Tabaqat into the Persian vernacular of his region` (pp. 120–121, folios 89–90).
- **Judgements passed inside footnotes**, which the brief allows as citable content: Obermann's book
  is `a book the main thesis of which can no longer be accepted`; van Leeuwen `sees Ghazzali,
  correctly, as apologist` (fn 66, p. 123, folio 92); Singh's translation of Anṣārī `lacks the
  rhyming patterns which are essential, and the poetic flavor` (fn 65, p. 122, folio 91); Hartmann's
  article is `a fine analysis of Sulaml's treatise` (fn 59, p. 117, folio 86); Sulamī's Koran
  commentary is `a work that still awaits a critical edition` (fn 58, p. 117, folio 86).
- **The chunk identifies several of the book's own sigla by naming their works in the running text**,
  which is useful for the consolidator: `L` = Sarrāj's *Kitāb al-lumaʿ* (p. 115, folio 84); `K` =
  Kalābādhī (p. 116, folio 85); `A` = Abū Nuʿaym's *Ḥilyat al-auliyāʾ* (p. 116, folio 85); `N` =
  Jāmī's *Nafaḥāt al-uns* (p. 117, folio 86); `Q` = Qushayrī's *Risāla* (p. 119, folio 88); `H` =
  Hujwīrī's *Kashf al-maḥjūb* (p. 119, folio 88); `T` = ʿAṭṭār's *Tadhkirat al-auliyā* (p. 120,
  folio 89); `B` = Baqlī (p. 110, folio 79); `W` = Nwyia (pp. 111–112, folios 80–81); `MM` = Molé
  (p. 117, folio 86). All agree with the brief's key.
