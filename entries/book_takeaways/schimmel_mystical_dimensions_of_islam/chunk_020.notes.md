# schimmel_mystical_dimensions_of_islam — chunk 020 — pp. 367–385

**What this chunk is.** The closing nine pages of chapter 7 (`THE ROSE AND THE NIGHTINGALE`,
pp. 367–374, folios 336–343), on Anatolian Turkish mystical poetry, Bektashi religion and the
abolition of the Turkish orders; then the **opening of chapter 8**, printed
`8` / `SUFISM` / `IN INDO-PAKISTAN` (letter-spacing normalised — see `## Doubts`), which begins at
p. 375, folio 344, under the section heading `THE CLASSICAL PERIOD`, and runs to the end of the
chunk at p. 385, folio 354. One full-page plate falls inside the chapter at p. 380. Archive yield is
high and is concentrated in pp. 375–385: **50 distinct archive ids**, reached by ruling (a) on four
toponyms (Lahore 35, Multan 8, Uch Sharif 5, Pakpattan 1) and by the person rule on five named
figures. The chapter-7 pages yield nothing for the archive — see `## Doubts` for that explicit
negative.

**Boundaries.** The chunk opens mid-sentence at p. 367 (`wants to kiss his peachlike face and his
honey lips`) and closes mid-sentence at p. 385: the last words are `its representative is Lai
Shahbaz,` [text-layer?: Lal Shahbaz] (p. 385, folio 354). Everything the book goes on to say about
that figure is outside this chunk.

**Folio regime, checked on every page.** `folio = PDF − 31` holds on all 18 pages of this chunk that
print a number, with no outlier. The running header carries it: leading (verso) `336 / THE ROSE AND
THE NIGHTINGALE` (p. 367), trailing (recto) `THE ROSE AND THE NIGHTINGALE / 337` (p. 368), and so on
unbroken. **The parity self-check passed on all 17 running-header folios**: leading 336, 338, 340,
342, 346, 348, 350, 352, 354 are all even; trailing 337, 339, 341, 343, 345, 347, 351, 353 are all
odd. No digit was discarded or smoothed. Two header folios are digit-damaged and are read by the

prints `35O` [text-layer?: 350] — both still satisfy the leading-is-even check. Two pages need
their own note, below and under `## Doubts`: **p. 375**, the chapter-opening page, prints `344` on
its own line at the foot rather than in a running header, and **p. 380**, the plate page, prints

**Sigla in this chunk — none of them is a page of this book.** Three occur: `(T i: 164)`

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Chishti khanqah economy, given explicitly as the type case.** `The maintenance of Farlduddln's
  khdnqdh—and he is mentioned here as an outstanding example of the early Chishti way of life—was
  difficult, since the sheikh relied exclusively upon gifts (futuh), and the khdnqdh did not own or
  cultivate land from which the dervishes might draw their living.` (p. 378, folio 347).
- **Refusal of endowment.** `No jtigirs` [text-layer?: jāgīrs] `(endowments of land) or grants from
  the rulers were accepted by the Chishtis, for they refused to deal with the worldly government.`
  (p. 378, folio 347), reinforced by the couplet quoted at `## Citable passages` and by `these
  Chishti saints, who considered everything in the hands of the rulers to be unlawful` (p. 378,
  folio 347).
- **Life inside the khanqah.** `The dervishes in the khdnqdh regarded themselves as "guests of God,"
  living and working in one large room; visitors were always welcome, and the table always spread
  for unexpected guests—if there was any food available. The residents had to serve the sheikh and
  the community, and their main occupations were prayer, worship, and the study of books of devotion
  and the biographies of saints.` (p. 378, folio 347).
- **No caste bar.** `The fact that the Chishti khanqdhs avoided any discrimination between the
  disciples and practiced a classless society attracted many people into their fold.` (p. 376,
  folio 345).
- **Suhrawardi counter-model**, all at p. 383, folio 352: hereditary succession; fixed visiting
  hours instead of the open table; the sheikh as a `well-to-do landlord` keeping grain in the house;
  willingness to `mix freely with members of the ruling classes`; and the observation that `This
  contrasting attitude toward the world, and toward its most dangerous representative, the
  government, has survived through the centuries.`
- **Territorial allocation of spiritual authority.** `the sphere of influence of a khalifa was
  designated by the master, who determined the areas where the baraka of this or that disciple
  should become active—a territorial distribution of spiritual power frequently found in Indian
  Sufism.` (p. 377, folio 346).
- **Samaʿ.** `Austere as they were in their preparatory stage of life, the Chishtiyya allowed the
  samcf` [text-layer?: samāʿ]`, the spiritual concert and dance; this predilection is reflected in
  many of their sayings and in their poetry.` (p. 381, folio 350).
- **Pilgrimage and shrine practice, stated in the present tense** (all archive-adjacent): Hujwiri's
  tomb `still provides a place of pilgrimage for the Panjabis` (p. 376, folio 345); Bakhtiyar Kaki's
  compound in Merauli `is usually filled with pious pilgrims, who recite and sing their devotional
  poetry` (p. 377, folio 346); Muʿinuddin's marble sanctuary at Ajmer, `erected by the Mogul Emperor
  Jihangir`, `is also still a center of religious inspiration for thousands of faithful Muslims`
  (p. 377, folio 346); Nizamuddin's tomb is `still the most frequently visited sanctuary in Delhi`
  (p. 381, folio 350); Iraqi's poems `are still being sung by Pakistani musicians at the door of the
  master's tomb in Multan` (p. 384, folio 353).
- **Devotional book market of Delhi**, attributed to Barani: `It became almost fashionable, if we
  are to believe Barani, to purchase copies of the following books: Makki's Qut al-qulub … Ghazzali's
  Ihyd" ^ulum ad-din; Suhrawardi's cAwdrif al-macdnf and Hujwiri's Kashf al-mahjub; Kalabadhi's
  Kitdb at-ta'arruf and its commentary; and the Risdla of Qushayri` (p. 379, folio 348), with
  `Najmuddin Daya's Mirsad ul-cibad`, `the letters of ^ynu^l-Qudat Hamadhani` and `Hamiduddin
  Nagori's Lawdmf`. `No wine, gambling, or usury was to be found in Delhi, and people even refrained
  from telling lies.` (p. 379, folio 348).
- **Suhrawardi handbook as the standard text.** `Suhrawardi's 'Awarif al-macdrif, the guidebook of
  almost all the Indo-Muslim mystics in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries` (p. 379, folio 348).
- **Bektashi institutional material** (Anatolian, recorded compactly): the four gateways `sharfa` /
  `tarlqa` / `mcfrifa` / `haqlqa` with their adherents `cdbid`, `zdhid`, `carz'/`, `muhibb`, each
  `founded upon ten obligations` so that `the sacred number of forty is attained once more`
  (p. 371, folio 340); the hierarchy `dede, "grandfather," in the village of Hacci Bektas`, the
  `Celebi`, the `baba` `in charge of teaching and of the pastoral work`, the dervish `who, like the
  baba, may be married or lead a celibate life`, and the initiated `muhibb` (p. 371, folio 340);
  initiation on the `ddr-i Mansur`, lay `cdshiq` who `have not taken their "part" (nasib]` (p. 371,
  folio 340); the prayer formulae `gulbdng` and `tercemdn`; Muharram and New Year `celebrated in
  good Shia and Persian form`; and the ʿAshura dish `^d^ure`, still cooked `on the tenth of
  Muharram` by `many Turkish families—most of them non-Bektashis` (pp. 371–372, folios 340–341).
- **Women in the Bektashi order.** `One of the outstanding features of the Bektashi order—unique in
  Islam—is that women are treated as equals; they participate in all ceremonies and freely converse
  with the men.` (p. 372, folio 341).
- **Order and army.** `From the fifteenth century on, Bektashi leaders (bdbd) lived close to the
  Janissary barracks to give spiritual guidance to the soldiers`, and they were `involved in the
  fall of the Janissaries in 1826` [text-layer?: clean] (p. 370, folio 339).
- **State suppression.** `the closing of the dervish orders in 1925` [text-layer?: clean] (p. 368,
  folio 337), repeated with its rationale at p. 374, folio 343.


## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- Hajji Bektash's dates are derived by letter-numerology, and the book says so: `The date of his
  birth is given—typical of Bektashi thought—by the numerical value of the word murawwat, "virtue,"
  which is 648/1247` [text-layer?: clean]`, and his death is dated, by a similar device, in 738/1338`
  [text-layer?: clean]`.` (p. 370, folio 339).
- `the vilayetnames, books on saintliness that favor miraculous evidence, speak of some of the
  miracles he performed` — of Hajji Bektash; the miracles themselves are not described (p. 370,
  folio 339).
- Pir Sultan Abdal `alludes to the rose that made Hallaj sigh when his friend Shibli flung it at
  him, while the common people were throwing stones at him` (p. 369, folio 338) — attributed to the
  poem, not asserted.
- Fariduddin's `constant fasting was miraculously rewarded—even pebbles turned into sugar, hence his
  surname Ganj-i shakar, "sugar treasure."` (pp. 377–378, folios 346–347). Schimmel immediately
  hedges: `Whether this charming legend is true or not` (p. 378, folio 347).
- The dead-child tradition, marked twice as legend by the book itself: `When he was informed that a
  child had died—so legend has it—he said: "If fate has so decreed and he dies, tie a rope around
  his feet and throw him out and come back!"` (p. 378, folio 347).
- The Iraqi cell story at Multan, introduced by the book as `The story goes:` and quoted at length
  from Arberry's translation (footnote 11, `c lraqt, Song of the Lovers, no. 15; cf. N 602`): Iraqi
  sits ten days admitting nobody, sings the `sdqi's languorous eye` verse on the eleventh day, the
  brothers complain, the saint answers `that this was prohibited to them, but not to him`, and on
  hearing the poem sung in the bazaar and the taverns declares `"His affair is complete,"` and
  invests him with `the mystic robe` (pp. 383–384, folios 352–353).
- Schimmel's own first-person report of an inner-heat phenomenon, Turkish and not archive-related,
  recorded because it is unusually direct testimony: `the inner heat of the singing mystic warmed up
  the car to such an extent that the windows became fogged and those of us traveling with him felt
  no discomfort from the cold throughout the last hour of our journey.` (p. 368, folio 337), with
  the date `December 1958` [text-layer?: clean].
- Automatic writing in Turkey: recipients `proved capable of jotting down verses in languages not
  even known to them (like Persian)` (p. 368, folio 337).


## Arguments and interpretations

- **European derivation theories rejected.** `earlier European theories that tried to explain Sufism
  as an Islamized form of Vedanta philosophy or of Yoga have now been discarded.` (p. 376,
  folio 345).
- **Conversion by preaching, not conquest.** `Although the type of the soldier-Sufi, the fighter for
  the true religion, is sometimes found in the frontier provinces of India, as it is in other border
  areas between Islam and the land of the infidels, the Islamization of the country was achieved
  largely by the preaching of the dervishes, not by the sword.` (pp. 376–377, folios 345–346), and
  `conversions in India during that period were due mainly to the untiring activity of the Chishti
  saints, whose simple and unsophisticated preaching and practice of love of God and one's neighbor
  impressed many Hindus, particularly those from the lower castes, and even members of the scheduled
  castes.` (p. 376, folio 345).
- **Asceticism had a social cost.** `as a consequence of this inhuman attitude, most sons of early
  Chishti saints turned out to be worldly people, some even drunkards, who had no disposition at all
  for the religious, let alone mystical, life.` (p. 378, folio 347).
- **Sufism as a mass movement.** `During Nizamuddln's time, Sufism became a mass movement in
  northwestern India, and the moral principles laid down by the early Chishti saints did much to
  shape the ideals of the Muslim society in that part of the Subcontinent.` (p. 381, folio 350).
- **The Chishtiyya's ceiling.** `The Chishtiyya remained for centuries the most influential order in
  the Subcontinent, but it never went beyond its borders.` (p. 382, folio 351).
- **Gisudaraz as transmitter.** `Gisudaraz was probably the first author in the Subcontinent who
  tried to introduce the classical works of Sufism on a broad scale` (p. 382, folio 351).
- **Iraqi's importance.** `Iraqi's influence on later Persian and Indo-Persian poetry can scarcely
  be overrated` (p. 385, folio 354); and `In the history of the Suhrawardiyya in India the intensity
  of Iraqi's experience, the splendor of his radiating love, has rarely been repeated.` (p. 385,
  folio 354).
- **A gap the book names in its own apparatus.** `A scholarly study of Muclnuddin is still needed.`
  (footnote 3, p. 376, folio 345).
- **The author's characterisation of two standard surveys**, useful for weighing later citations:
  `Ishtiaq Husain Qureshi, The Muslim Community of the Indo-Pakistan Subcontinent (The Hague, 1962`
  [text-layer?: clean]`), from a strict Pakistani viewpoint; Mohammed Mujeeb, The Indian Muslims
  (Montreal and London, 1969` [text-layer?: clean]`), from an Indian standpoint` (footnote 1,
  p. 375, folio 344).
- **On the Bektashis** (Anatolian): `In their theoretical approach, the Bektashis have added nothing
  to the teachings of the Ibn cArabi school.` (p. 372, folio 341); Christian parallels `should not
  press these similarities too far`, since `The communal meal is much older than the Christian
  sacrament` (p. 371, folio 340); and the `"shamanist" aspect` is explicitly attributed to `modern,
  nationalistic Turkish research` rather than endorsed (p. 371, folio 340).
- **On the abolition of the orders.** `Instead of fulfilling their centuries-old function as centers
  of spiritual education, they became headquarters of obscurantism and backwardness. That is why
  Atatiirk abolished the orders in 1925` [text-layer?: clean]`—a step that some of the leading
  personalities in the mystical hierarchy even approved of.` (p. 374, folio 343).
