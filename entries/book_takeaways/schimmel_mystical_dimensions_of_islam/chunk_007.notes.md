## Practices, institutions, economy


- **Admission and probation.** `The newcomer was sometimes made to wait for days at the sheikh's
  door, and sometimes as a first test was treated very rudely. Usually three years of service were
  required before the adept could be formally accepted in a master's group—one year in the service of
  the people, one in the service of God, and one year in watching over his own heart.` (p. 132,
  folio 101).
- **The three bonds of discipleship.** `The relation of the novice to the master is threefold: by the
  khirqa, by being instructed in the formula of dhikr (recollection), and by company (suhbat),
  service, and education (N 560).` (p. 133, folio 102).
- **The khirqa and baraka.** `by donning a garment that has been worn, or even touched, by the
  blessed hands of a master, the disciple acquires some of the baraka, the mystico-magical power of
  the sheikh.` Two kinds are distinguished: `the khirqa-yi irdda` [text-layer?: khirqa-yi irāda]`,
  which the aspirant gets from the master to whom he has sworn allegiance, and the khirqa-yi
  tabarruk, the "frock of blessing," which he may obtain from different masters with whom he has
  lived or whom he has visited during his journeys` (p. 133, folio 102).
- **Colour of the frock.** `The khirqa was usually dark blue. It was practical for travel, since dirt
  was not easily visible on it, and at the same time it was the color of mourning and distress`;
  later, `in certain circles mystics would choose for their frocks a color that corresponded to their
  mystical station (CL 166), and sabzpush, "he who wears green," has always been an epithet for those
  who live on the highest possible spiritual level` (`cf. V 52`) (p. 133, folio 102).
- **Sewing disputes.** `Some of the Sufis invented complicated rules for the sewing and stitching of
  their patched frocks, disputing whether to use thin or coarse thread in putting the patches
  together` (p. 133, folio 102), to the point that `others might accuse them of worshiping the khirqa
  like an idol` (p. 134, folio 103).
- **The forty-day retreat as an institution.** `arbcfin, chilld` became `very early, a regular
  institution in the Sufi path` (p. 134, folio 103); the sheikh `might exempt a disciple for a time
  from the forty-day seclusion, for instance, because he was spiritually too weak, or because his
  spiritual ecstasy might overwhelm him` (p. 135, folio 104); mystics `were praised if, at the time
  of their death, they had completed thirty or forty chilla` (p. 136, folio 105).
- **The chillakhāna as a building.** `Sometimes they would construct a special chillakhdna`
  [text-layer?: chillakhāna]`, a room for seclusion, which might even be subterranean (N 325), close
  to the center of the fraternity or in a blessed place nearby.` (p. 136, folio 105). This is the
  chunk's only architectural statement; no site, date or patron is given — `(not stated)`.
- **Visiting the master.** `Visiting his master is a religious duty of the disciple (N 115), for he
  will find from him what he will not find elsewhere.` and `Even to have met a leading sheikh at once
  endow` [text-layer?: endows — word truncated at the right margin] `a man with a higher rank
  (N 115).` (p. 135, folio 104).
- **Initiation without a living master.** Three routes are named: the *uwaysī* line, initiation `by a
  nonvisible master or by a saint who had died long ago`, and investiture by Khiḍr, `which was
  accepted as valid in the tradition of Sufi initiation` (pp. 136–137, folios 105–106).
- **The Naqshbandiyya named as an order.** `Later Sufis—particularly the Naqshbandiyya—practiced a
  concentration upon the "spiritual reality" of the great masters in order to be strengthened in
  their mystical pursuits.` (p. 136, folio 105).
- **Koran recitation as a duty.** `"A disciple who does not know the Koran by heart is like a lemon
  without scent" (N 131)` (p. 138, folio 107).
- **Sufis and the state — the chunk's only economic material.** `Food or anything belonging to or
  coming from the ruling classes was regarded as suspect. It is well known that in the old days the
  most pious scholars refused to accept government offices; even in later periods relations between
  the Sufis and the rulers were often cold, if not strained, since the mystics were not interested in
  any contact that might pollute their pure intentions.` (p. 142, folio 111). And the extreme of
  scruple: `Pious women would not spin in the light of a neighbor's candle; a mystic whose only sheep
  had grazed on someone else's lawn by mistake would no longer drink its milk, declaring it to be
  illicit for him after such a transgression.` (p. 142, folio 111).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)


- **Schimmel declines to identify Ghazzālī's mystical master, and says the gap does not matter.**
  `And there it does not matter very much that we do not know who his mystical guide was nor to whom
  his chain of initiation goes back.` (p. 125, folio 94). She endorses Gairdner's formulation: `"What
  saved God for him from his obliterating agnosticism was the experience of the mystic leap, his own
  personal mfraj"` [text-layer?: miʿrāj] (p. 125, folio 94), calling it `W. H. Temple Gairdner's fine
  remark`.
- **The architecture of the *Iḥyāʾ* is read as a statement of doctrine.** `It seems to me typical of
  Ghazzali's way of thought that the center of the book, the twentieth chapter, is devoted to the
  central figure in Islam, the Prophet Muhammad.` (p. 125, folio 94). The forty chapters are read the
  same way: `forty being the number of patience and trial, the number of days of seclusion that the
  adept undergoes at the beginning of the Path` (p. 125, folio 94).
- **The whole book is read as an *ars moriendi*.** `The whole Ihyff may be called a preparation for
  death` (p. 126, folio 95).
- **Ghazzālī's historical effect, stated twice and in tension with itself.** Positively: `This
  teaching—a marriage between mysticism and law—has made Ghazzali the most influential theologian of
  medieval Islam.` and `Ghazzalf s method of combining the life of the heart in strict accord with the
  law and with a theologically sound attitude made even orthodox theologians take the Sufi movement
  seriously. The moderate Sufi outlook began to color the life of most average Muslims.` (pp. 126–127,
  folios 95–96). Negatively, in Anawati's words which she puts as an open question: `Or will Pere
  Anawati's criticism prove right: "Though so brilliant, his contribution did not succeed in
  preventing the anchylosis that two or three centuries later was to congeal Muslim religious
  thought"?` — and then in her own: `Or was his greatness itself the reason for the congealing of
  moderate Islam?` (p. 128, folio 97). **She leaves the question open.**
- **The *Mishkāt al-anwār* is flagged as an unsolved scholarly problem.** `One of the most puzzling
  questions among the many unsolved problems for the scholar is where to place Ghazzall's Mishkdt
  al-anwar, "The Niche for Lights," in the whole body of his writings.` (p. 127, folio 96). Its
  content: `he does not hesitate to put most of the pious orthodox behind the "veils mixed of
  darkness and light," whereas even certain philosophers are "veiled by pure light."` and `The
  Mishkdt al-anwar shows a highly developed light metaphysics—God is the Light—and many later mystics
  have relied upon this book rather than upon his Ihyff ^ulum ad-din.` (p. 127, folio 96).
- **Ghazzālī as a brake on Hellenism that failed.** `Ghazzall's struggle against Hellenistic
  influences and a more or less Neoplatonic philosophy was probably instrumental in repudiating these
  currents for a while. During that time, however, they crystallized anew and came back as Sufi
  theosophy, a new development destined to take shape in the century after Ghazzall's death.`
  (p. 127, folio 96).
- **The state of the field.** `Many aspects of his thought have been studied in recent years, but much
  remains to be done.` (p. 128, folio 97).
- **The Path is derived from, not opposed to, the law.** `This derivation shows that the Sufis
  considered the path of mystical education a branch of that highway that consists of the God-given
  law` and `no mystical experience can be realized if the binding injunctions of the sharfa are not
  followed faithfully first.` (p. 129, folio 98). Compared explicitly to Christian practice: `The
  Christian tripartite division of the via purgativa, the via contemplativa, and the via illuminativa
  is, to some extent, the same as the Islamic definition otsharfa` [text-layer?: of sharīʿa — the
  space is lost]`, tarlqa, and haqlqa.` (p. 129, folio 98).
- **Station versus state.** `The maqam is a lasting stage, which man reaches, to a certain extent, by
  his own striving. It belongs to the category of acts, whereas the states are gifts of grace.`
  (pp. 130–131, folios 99–100). Schimmel stresses that the schemes do not agree: `Even the sequence of
  the stations is not always clear; it varies according to the capacity of the adept, and God's
  activity can change stations or grant the wayfarer a state without apparent reason.` (p. 131,
  folio 100), and she prints three incompatible early lists side by side rather than harmonising them
  (`A 9:374`, `A 10:64`, `G 4:43`) (p. 131, folio 100).
- **The *sheikh at-tarbiya* is dated as a later development.** `though the image of the sheikh
  at-tarblya, who acutely supervised every breath of the mund` [text-layer?: murīd]`, has developed
  only in the course of time.` (p. 132, folio 101).
- **Absolute obedience, and its later cost.** Ghazzālī `also maintained that complete and absolute
  obedience is necessary, even if the sheikh should be wrong` (p. 134, folio 103); `This attitude in
  later times lent itself to dangerous consequences; indeed, it is one of the reasons for modernist
  Muslims' aversion to Sufism. But the original intent was genuine: the master should act like a
  physician, diagnosing and healing the illnesses and defects of the human soul.` (p. 135, folio 104).
- **Seclusion can harm.** `some of the Sufi theoreticians were aware—as every good sheikh in fact
  was—that the seclusion might constitute, for weaker adepts, a source of danger rather than of
  elevation.` (p. 135, folio 104).
- **Correction of a European misreading.** `westerners hav` [text-layer?: have — word truncated at
  the right margin] `often regarded the Sufis as representatives of a movement that has freed itself
  from the legal prescriptions of Islam, no longer caring for religion and infidelity. Sufi became
  almost an equivalent for "free thinker" with many Europeans. That is, however, not correct.`
  (p. 137, folio 106). Her counter: `They did not abolish the rites but rather interiorized them`
  (p. 137, folio 106).
- **A Zoroastrian source is asserted for a Persian mystical idea.** Of the notion that thoughts take
  visible form at the Day of Judgment and that `death will meet man like a mirror`: `The Zoroastrian
  idea of the witness in Heaven who meets the deceased soul has clearly inspired these ideas.`
  (p. 138, folio 107). Attributed to `Ruml, following Sana'i's example` (`M 5:1791-1803`), with
  Sanāʾī going `even further` (`SD 618`).
- **Outward asceticism as a cover for greed.** `This quality was found, as history shows, not only in
  the worldly leaders but also in many of those who claimed to have attained the highest spiritual
