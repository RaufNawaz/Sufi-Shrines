## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The founding of the Mevlevi order is dated and attributed, and not to Rūmī.** `When Rum! died,
  on 17 December 1273, Husamuddin succeeded to the leadership of his disciples. But the formation of
  the order of the Whirling Dervishes, the organization of the dancing ritual and the establishment
  of a true hierarchy, was left to Sultan Walad (d. 1312), Rumi's son, who took over his office
  after Husamuddm's death in 1284.` (p. 346, folio 315).
- **The order's name and the master's titles.** `Jalaluddln Rumi, called by his followers Maulana,
  "our master" (Turkish pronounciation Mevlana), or Maulawl. The order inspired by him, the
  Mevlevis, known in the West as the Whirling Dervishes, early attracted the interest of European
  visitors to the Ottoman Empire` (p. 340, folio 309).
- **Architecture and building dates at Konya, given precisely.** `Baha'uddin Walad was called to
  Konya in 1228. There Sultan 'Ala^uddin Kaykobad had just built the magnificent mosque in the
  center of the town. Numerous smaller mosques and madrasas surrounded the castle; two of them,
  built during Rumi's lifetime, are still standing: the Ince Minareli (1258) and the Karatay Medrese
  (1251).` (p. 343, folio 312). All four dates are printed cleanly.
- **The Karatay dome, described in detail** (p. 343, folio 312): `Out of the four times five
  "Turkish triangles"—which bear in squared Kufi the names of the Prophet and the four righteous
  caliphs, as well as those of other prophets—rises the tambour, decorated with Koranic verses in
  sophisticated plaited Kufi script. The dome itself is covered with intricately interlaced stars in
  turquoise, blue, black, and white tiles, each of them separate and yet all interwoven in an almost
  inexplicable way. The top of the dome is open, so that the sky, and at night the stars, are
  reflected in the little pool in the center of the madrasa.`
- **A tomb rediscovered by excavation, with the finder named.** Of Shams's grave: `This very tomb
  was discovered a few years ago by the then director of the Mevlana Miizesi` [text-layer?: Mevlânâ
  Müzesi]`, Mehmet Onder; it is located beneath the memorial erected for Shams some time after the
  event.` (p. 344, folio 313). `a few years ago` is relative to the book and is not dated —
  `(not stated)`.
- **Konya as a refuge economy during the Mongol invasions.** `In Rumi's time scholars, artists, and
  mystics from all over the eastern Islamic world sought shelter in Konya, one of the few safe
  places during this period when the Mongol onslaught devastated large parts of the Muslim world.`
  (p. 343, folio 312). Its population `spoke partly Greek—there being a strong Christian substratum
  in the former city of Iconium—and partly Turkish. RumI used both languages in some of his verses.`
  (p. 343, folio 312).
- **Succession by chair, not by shrine.** `Baha'uddln Walad died after a few years of teaching, and
  his son Jalaluddm succeeded him in his chair.` (p. 343, folio 312).
- **Composition practice.** `Husamuddin was charged with writing down the verses that would flow
  from Rumi's lips, whether he was walking on the street or even sitting in the bath.` (p. 346,
  folio 315).
- **Samāʿ triggered by trade noise.** `It is said that the hammering of the goldsmiths in the bazaar
  of Konya inspired him to dance and to recite verses, as did the sound of the watermills in the
  gardens of Meram.` (p. 347, folio 316).
- **Output figures.** `more than 30,000 verses of lyric poetry, more than 26,000 verses in the
  Mathnawl` (p. 347, folio 316), both printed cleanly.



## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The blessing of the child Rūmī by ʿAṭṭār — Schimmel marks it as legend.** `It is probably legend
  that young Jalaluddln—who was born in 1207 in the city of Balkh—was blessed by Fariduddin c Attar
  about 1219, when he and his family were wandering through Iran after his father had left Balkh
  before the advancing hordes of Genghis Khan. Yet the story underlines the spiritual affinity
  between the two great masters of mystical poetry` (p. 342, folio 311).
- **ʿAṭṭār as a martyr of love — tradition, and Schimmel says so.** `Later mystical tradition
  includes him in the list of the martyrs of love, slain by the orthodox or, in his case, by the
  infidels` … `Whether he was indeed killed by the invading Mongols is uncertain, but the story
  indicates the rank he was given` (p. 334, folio 303).
- **The murder of Shams**, related as narrative with the chronicles cited: `some of Rurm's disciples,
  with the cooperation of Rumi's son cAla°uddIn, "the pride of professors," decided to send Shams to
  the place from which there is no return. One night they called him out of Jalaluddm's house, which
  was opposite his son's house, and, after stabbing him, they threw him into the nearby well.`
  `the chronicles, however, relate that during his father's sleep, he quickly buried Shams's body,
  which he had taken out of the well, covering the tomb with a hurriedly prepared plaster.` (p. 344,
  folio 313).
- **The reunion of Rūmī and Shams**: `they embraced each other and fell at each other's feet, "so
  that one did not know who was lover and who was beloved."` (p. 344, folio 313). Attributed to `a
  description`, source `(not stated)`.
- **Shams rebukes Auḥaduddīn Kirmānī**, cited by Schimmel as `(N 59)`: `" 'I see the moon in a vessel
  filled with water.' Shams rebuked him with the words: 'If you have no boil on your neck, why don't
  you look at it in the sky?' "` (p. 344, folio 313).
- **The gathering of the masters after Rūmī's death**, which Schimmel expressly does not vouch for:
  `even if we do not take literally the story that the great masters of mystical thought and
  poetry … gathered after his death`; Ṣadruddīn's reply, cited as `(N 464)`: `"If Bayezid and Junayd
  had lived in this time, they would have seized his mantle and would have felt indebted to him; he
  is the majordomo of Muhammadan poverty, and we taste it thanks to him"` (p. 346, folio 315).
- **The dog of the Seven Sleepers**: `even the unclean dog can become a model for the Sufi since,
  keeping faithful company with the Seven Sleepers, he became purified and sanctified` (p. 339,
  folio 308).
- **The elephant who dreams of India**, ascribed to `Khaqani, Nizami, and cAttar in the late twelfth
  century, and then often by Rumi and his imitators` (p. 339, folio 308).
- **The reed and the king's secret**, `taken over from Sana^I (S 484)` and traced further back:
  `This was, originally, the story of "Kin Midas with the donkey's ears"` [text-layer?: King Midas —
  right-margin truncation, C3] `(and Midas's old residence, Gordion, is not far from Konya). A
  similar story is also told about CAH, Muhammad's cousin and son-in-law, who revealed the divine
  secrets that the Prophet had entrusted to him to the lake.` (p. 348, folio 317).



## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **The origin of Persian mystical didactic poetry is placed in eastern Iran, with a named
  genealogy.** `Persian mystical poetry, especially mystical didactic poetry, seems to have
  originated in the eastern part of Iran. One of its early centers was Herat` … `Y. E. Bertels has
  traced the course of Sufi didactic poetry, of which some of Ansari's books constitute a first
  stage, but which found its first master, half a century later, in Ansari's compatriot Abul-Majd
  Majdud Sanal` (p. 332, folio 301). The name is printed `Bertels` in the text and `Y. E. Berthels`
  in footnote 17 on the same page — see `## Doubts`.
- **A negative verdict on the *Ḥadīqa*, reported and partly endorsed.** `Western scholars usually
  consider the Hadlqa a rather boring book; in fact, it lacks the flowing delicacy of many later
  mystical poems.` (p. 333, folio 302). But: `The Hadlqa has become a model for all the later
  mathnawls written for mystical didactic purposes.` (p. 333, folio 302).
- **Schimmel dissents from the allegorising school.** Of the mystical word-charts: `Other poets have
  used even more abstract methods of allegorizing, in which, eventually, the charm of a quatrain is
  lost in the heavy chains of a terminology stemming from Ibn cArabi. It is doubtful whether the
  great masters of mystical love, like Ruzbihan Baqli, would have appreciated this exaggerated
  "mystical" explanation—they knew that the word in itself is like a fan, veiling and unveiling at
  the same time.` (pp. 331–332, folios 300–301).
- **Nicholson's derivation of the *Manṭiq uṭ-ṭayr*, reported not asserted.** `Reynold A. Nicholson
  and others have considered this mathnawl the model for cAttar's Mantiq ut-tayr; Nicholson has even
  spoken of it as prefiguring the theme of the Divine Comedy.` (p. 334, folio 303).
- **A source-reliability warning on the *Tadhkirat al-auliyāʾ*.** `Hagiography was, for him, another
  medium for telling stories about his venerated masters` … `For factual details, this book should be
  supplemented with other, more sober sources.` (p. 335, folio 304). This bears directly on how any
  entry uses ʿAṭṭār's hagiography.
- **An attribution formerly accepted is withdrawn.** `The once widely held idea that "Attar became a
  pious Shiite in the third period of his life can no longer be maintained; the works that show a
  strong Shia inclination and were once thought to be his can now be ascribed to another poet by the
  same name.` (p. 336, folio 305). Ritter's three-stage scheme is the frame (p. 335, folio 304).
- **The reliable sources for Rūmī's life are named and ranked.** Sulṭān Walad's *Maʿārif* and three
  *Mathnawī*s `are considered the only authentic commentaries on Rumi's work and contain most of the
  trustworthy information about his life and teaching—more than the numerous books composed by pious
  devotees.` (p. 346, folio 315). Footnote 27 adds Sipahsālār and `Shamsaddin Ahmad al-Aflaki,
  Manaqib a` / `'•drifin` [text-layer?: Manāqib al-ʿārifīn — split across the line and carrying a
  `•`, a character not in the measured table], and judges Huart's French translation `though not
  fully satisfactorily` (p. 346, folio 315).
- **A gap in the field, stated flatly.** `a truly comprehensive study of Rumi in a Western language
  is still lacking. It will be difficult to do full justice to his work without making an extensive
  study of the Persian originals and the large number of Persian and Turkish works written about
  him, as well as the enormous amount of material composed in the Indo-Pakistan Subcontinent.`
  (p. 342, folio 311).
- **On the *Mathnawī*'s resistance to systematisation.** `it is, however, next to impossible to
  build up a mystical system from its tales and parables; every interpreter has found whatever he
  sought, from pantheism to personal mysticism, from enraptured love to law-bound orthodoxy.`
  (p. 347, folio 316).
- **A comparative judgement between the two poets.** `Like cAttar, RumI liked storytelling, but he
  lacked the sustained narration that distinguishes 'Attar's mystical epics.` (p. 347, folio 316).
- **A reception history of Rūmī in Europe**, with dates: Hammer-Purgstall `(1774-1856)` and his
  `Geschichte der schonen Redekunste Persiens (1818)`; Rückert `(1788—1866)`, whose book `(1819)`
  produced `the first attempt to adapt the ghazal form to German poetry`; Rosenzweig-Schwannau
  `(1838)`; Hegel, who met Rūmī through Rückert; Constantin Brunner; William Hastie's English
  *ghazals* of 1903, made `as an antidote to the cult of Omar Khayyam, as the translator says`;
  Whinfield, Redhouse, and Nicholson's edition `(1925—1940)` (pp. 340–341, folios 309–310).

## Doubts


**The standing false-friend refusals.**

- **`shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar` was refused 37 times.** Farīduddīn ʿAṭṭār of Nishapur is the
  subject of the second half of this chunk's first section and is named, by my count of the page,
  **37 times**: 36 of them carry the element `ʿAṭṭār` in one of its damaged forms (`cAttar`,
  `c Attar`, `'Attar`, `"Attar`, and the section heading `CATTAR`), and **one is the bare first
  element `Fariduddin`**, on p. 334 — `Fariduddin was born in Nishapur and died there, most likely
  in 1220 at an old age.` (p. 334, folio 303). That bare mention is the dangerous one: it is the
  only place in the chunk where the string a matcher would fire on stands alone. Every one of the 37
  is **refused**. The archive row `shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar` is Bābā Farīduddīn Masʿūd
  Ganjshakar of Pakpattan, a different person, and **nothing in this chunk says anything whatever
  about him**. A 38th and 39th trap sit alongside: Schimmel's `Fariduddin c Attar` on p. 342
  (folio 311) is counted among the 37, and footnote 27's `Faridun Ahmad Sipahsalar` (p. 346,
  folio 315) is a *third* unrelated Farīd- name, also refused. Five of this chunk's sigla — `AD`,
  `MT`, `T`, `U`, `V` per the brief's key, of which `AD`, `MT` and `U` actually occur here — are
  ʿAṭṭār's works, and none of them is Bābā Farīd's either.
- **`shrine-of-shah-shams-ud-din-sabzwari` was refused.** `Shamsuddm Tabrlzl` / `Shams` / the
  `"Sun of Truth"` / `Tabriz's sun` (pp. 344–345, folios 313–314) is Rūmī's companion, murdered at
  Konya, not Shāh Shams of Multan.
- **`data-darbar` was refused.** `A similar story is also told about CAH, Muhammad's cousin and
  son-in-law` (p. 348, folio 317). `CAH` [text-layer?: ʿAlī — but `H` for a final `ī` is **not** in
