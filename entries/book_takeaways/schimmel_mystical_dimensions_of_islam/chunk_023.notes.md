## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **bhit-bhit-shah** (the book's `Shah cAbduDl-Latif of Bhit` [text-layer?: Shāh ʿAbduʾl-Laṭīf],
  also printed `Shah ^bdu^l-Latif`, `Shah 'vAbdu^l-Latlf`, `Shah "vAbdu^l-Latif`,
  `Shah 'Abdu'l-Latif`, `Shah "Abdu'l-Latif`, `Shah c Abdu D l-Latif` and `Shah Latif` — six
  renderings of one name in five pages, see `## Doubts`): this is the chunk's densest row, pp. 420–424.
  - Schimmel's ranking of him: `The most outstanding master of popular Sufi poetry in Pakistan is,
    no doubt, Shah cAbduDl-Latif of Bhit. He is not the first in the long line of Sindhi mystical
    poets.` (p. 420, folio 389).
  - Birth: `Shah ^bdu^l-Latif was born into a family of mystics in 1689 in Hala, near present-day
    Hyderabad-Sind,` (p. 421, folio 390). The year prints cleanly as `1689`.
  - Wandering and the Hinglaj journey: `In his early years Latif wandered through the country in the
    company of a group of yogis and perhaps reached the sacred mountain Hinglaj in Balochistan, which
    is often mentioned as "the goal" in Sindhi mystical poetry.` (p. 421, folio 390). Schimmel's own
    hedge `perhaps` is hers and is kept.
  - Settlement: `He then settled down in Bhit, not far from his hometown, and gathered a number of
    disciples around him.` (p. 421, folio 390).
  - **The shrine, described architecturally**: `His tomb in Bhit Shah is one of the loveliest
    sanctuaries in the Muslim world. The building, covered with blue and white tiles (typical of
    Hala), with exquisite ornaments, exudes serenity and peace. The pillar bases are almost
    suggestive of large flowers just opening, and delicate tilework decorates the ceiling.`
    (p. 421, folio 390). **No building date, no patron, no dimensions, no custodian — `(not stated)`.**
  - **Living practice at the tomb**: `Some dervishes who live at the threshold of his tomb perform
    this music in their gatherings on Thursday nights, and the melodies are well known all over
    Sind.` (p. 421, folio 390). The chunk gives **no** urs date and **no** visitor numbers —
    `(not stated)`.
  - Biographical reticence, stated by the author: `We do not know much about Shah Latif's external
    life; his personality is reflected in the poems collected under the title Shdha jo risdlo
    [text-layer?: Shāh jo Risālō], "The Book of Shah,"` (p. 421, folio 390).
  - First printing of the Risālō: `published for the first time by the German missionary Ernest
    Trumpp in 1866` (p. 421, folio 390). Elsewhere in the chunk the same man is spelled `Ernst Trumpp`
    (footnote 76, p. 423) — see `## Doubts`.
  - Form of the Risālō: `The Risdlo contains thirty chapters (sur), named according to their musical
    modes; in addition to traditional Indian ragas like Kalydn and Kabddh, Shah 'vAbdu^l-Latlf invented
    a number of new modes.` (p. 421, folio 390). The chunk does not say how many modes he invented —
    `(not stated)`.
  - Named *surs* and their subjects: `Some of the surs are related to folk ballads of the Indus valley,
    like those dealing with Sassui Punhun, Sohni Mehanwal, cUmar Marul, and Lila Chanesar.` (p. 422,
    folio 391); `The last chapter (Sur Ramakali) is one of the most interesting parts of the Risdlo in
    terms of mystical syncretism, but it should be read together with the praise of the Prophet in Sur
    Sarang, or with the bitter laments for Husayn's death in Kerbela in Sur Kedaro.` (p. 422, folio 391).
  - Koranic reading of the yogis: `the poet describes a group of yogis to whom he applies Koranic
    verses: their knees are like Mount Sinai, places of revelation; their faces shine in the divine
    light.` (p. 422, folio 391).
  - Borrowing from Rūmī, quoted as a siglum citation and **not** a page of this book: Sassui
    `eventually discovers—with a verse translated from Rumi's Mathnawl (M 1:1741)—that` / `Not only the
    thirsty seek the water, / but the water seeks the thirsty as well.` (p. 423, folio 392).
  - Style, in Schimmel's judgement: `He usually speaks in allusions, in difficult and opaque words, in
    oblique expressions. This tendency makes his style as complicated as it is attractive` (p. 424,
    folio 393); and `One may best characterize these verses as sighs, as heartbeats of a longing soul.`
    (p. 424, folio 393).
  - His own acknowledged debt: `Shah "Abdu'l-Latif did use the inherited language of the Sufis; he
    himself acknowledged his indebtedness to Rumi` (p. 424, folio 393).
  - Ḥallāj in his verse: `Shah ^bdu^l-Latif, too, had alluded to Hallaj's fate, seeing in every tree
    and plant the vision of the suffering mystic, proclaiming anffl-haqq [text-layer?: anāʾl-ḥaqq] and
    therefore doomed to death.` (p. 427, folio 306 as printed — rule gives 396).
  - **The chunk gives no death date for him and no silsila** — `(not stated)` for both.

- **shrine-of-sachal-sarmast** (the book's `Sachal Sarmast`): pp. 424–427, second only to Laṭīf in this
  chunk.
  - The meeting-story: `There is a story that once when a boy came to his convent, the mystic
    immediately recognized that he would one day "open the lid from those things" that he had kept

    a Sufi poet second only to Shah c Abdu D l-Latif.` (p. 424, folio 393). Schimmel labels it

    folio 393).
  - The name explained: `His name Sachal, a diminutive of sack [text-layer?: sach], "truth," seems to
    be an allusion to the word Haqq, "truth," as found in Hallaj's sentence anffl-Haqq [text-layer?:

    was indeed!` (p. 424, folio 393).
  - Life and languages: `Sachal Sarmast was a versatile mystic who led a solitary life; he wrote in

  - His material and its unevenness: `Like his predecessors, he relied upon folk tales and made Sassul

  - His epithet: `Sachal has been called "the cAttar of Sind," and in fact the influence of "Attar's

  - Schimmel's final estimate: `But none of the later mystical poets can match Sachal's intense glow;

  - Two of his poems are quoted at length, the `Perhaps I am…` self-riddle (p. 425, folio 394) and the

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- **Qadi Qadan** — `A prominent master was Qadi Qadan (d. 1546), of whose work only a few verses have
  been preserved; they distinctly show, however, all the peculiarities of later poetry.` (p. 420,
  folio 389). Named as an early Sindhi mystical poet; Schimmel dates the Sindhi tradition to `at least
  the sixteenth century` (p. 420, folio 389).

  related to Latif's poetry` (p. 421, folio 390); footnote 73 gives `Miyen Shah ^Inat jo kalam, ed. Dr.
  Nabi Bakhsh Baloch`. **Kept separate from Shāh ʿInāyat Shahīd of Jhok** — see `## Doubts`.

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Music at Bhit Shah, weekly and resident.** `Some dervishes who live at the threshold of his tomb perform
  this music in their gatherings on Thursday nights, and the melodies are well known all over Sind.`
  (p. 421, folio 390). Three separate institutional facts: dervishes **resident at the threshold**, a
  **Thursday-night** cycle, and a repertoire **transmitted province-wide**.
- **The Risālō as a performed, not merely read, text.** `Each group of poems, which rhyme according to
  particular rules of popular Indian prosody, usually contains an alternation between the recitation of the
  main verses by a solo voice and the repetition of a so-called kafl [text-layer?: kāfī] or way by a chorus;
  the way usually sums up the contents of the whole group of verses.` (p. 421, folio 390). This is a
  description of Sindhi performance practice generally, not of one shrine.
- **Shrine architecture.** Bhit Shah: `covered with blue and white tiles (typical of Hala), with exquisite
  ornaments`; `The pillar bases are almost suggestive of large flowers just opening, and delicate tilework
  decorates the ceiling.` (p. 421, folio 390). No date, no builder — `(not stated)`.
- **An annual observance in April.** `His anniversary at the end of April is usually celebrated in a solemn
  manner.` (p. 430, folio 399) — of Raḥmān Bābā. The book does not use the word *urs* anywhere in this chunk.
- **Dargāh economy and its social consequence.** `The Sufi sheikhs and the feudal lords in the country lost
  large numbers of disciples and workers, whereas the dargah in Jhok flourished.` (p. 428, folio 397), and
  `A considerable amount of land was distributed among Shah clnayat's followers after an unjustified attack
  on Jhok` (p. 428, folio 397). A siege of `four months'` and an execution `in January 1718` (p. 428,
  folio 397).
- **Shrine wealth as a target of criticism.** `The exaggerated veneration of a saint's tomb has been made the
  target of some of the best modern Sindhi short stories.` and `The discrepancy between the wealth of the
  shrines and their guardians and the poverty of the masses indeed provides an impressive theme for social and
  religious criticism. That holds true not only for Sind, but for other parts of the Muslim world as well.`
  (p. 429, folio 398). Footnote 87 — **whose number is missing from the transcription, see `## Doubts`** —
  identifies the story as `Jamal Abro, Munhun kdro`, translated by Schimmel as `"Geschwarzten Gesichtes"`.
- **Saint veneration as the majority practice, against reform.** `With or without the Naqshbandi reform,
  however, the majority of the masses continued, and partly still continue, in the veneration of their saints,
  which is echoed in hundreds of simple folk songs and popular verses.` (p. 429, folio 398).
- **Naqshbandī suppression of emotional practice, twice.** In Sind: the Thatta group `undertook to attack all
  those Sufis who defended the emotional side of religion as exemplified in the dancing practices on Makli
  Hill. Through their literary activities, this Naqshbandi group contributed to promoting a more sober mystical
  piety.` (p. 428, folio 397). In Bengal: `In Bengal as in Sind, the Naqshbandiyya and some more militant
  mystically inspired groups tried to curb the overflowing emotionalism expressed in the lovely folk songs. As
  a defense against syncretism, the later Naqshbandiyya has played a remarkable role in all parts of Muslim
  India.` (pp. 432–433, folios 401–402).
- **Naqshbandīs and Ḥallāj.** `The generations of mystics who followed Sachal took over this practice, except
  for the members of the Naqshbandiyya, who have little sympathy for the martyr-mystic.` (p. 427, folio 306 as
  printed — rule gives 396).
- **Language and print in Sind.** `Sindhi continued to flourish after the British occupation in 1843. With the
  introduction of a new Arabic alphabet in 1852, a considerable literature grew up that included mystical
  poetry and prose.` (p. 427, folio 306 as printed — rule gives 396).
- **Panjabi's transfer to the Sikhs.** `In the Punjab, the literary heritage of Panjabi was rarely continued by
  Muslims after 1800… The language was used almost exclusively by the Sikhs as their literary medium, whereas

- **Laṭīf recognises the boy Sachal.** `There is a story that once when a boy came to his convent, the mystic
  immediately recognized that he would one day "open the lid from those things" that he had kept secret.`
  (p. 424, folio 393) — attributed by Schimmel as `a story`, and immediately given a non-miraculous origin:
  `The anecdote may have been derived from the pen name Sachal used in his Persian poetry` (p. 424, folio 393).
- **The dust of Sind's saints as tribute.** `Did not Diwan Gidumal, the minister, give the invading Nadir Shah
  (1739) the most precious treasure Sind had to offer, some of the dust of its saints?` (p. 427, folio 306 as
  printed — rule gives 396) — put by Schimmel as a rhetorical question, not a verified event.
- **Raḥmān Bābā's seclusion.** `As legend has it, he led a life of withdrawal and seclusion` (p. 430,
  folio 399) — marked as legend by the author.
- **Zakariyā sawn in the tree.** `According to Islamic legend, which is based on Christian apocrypha, Zakariya
  (Zacharias) was persecuted by his enemies, and when he sought shelter in a hollow tree, the tree was sawed and
  he as well.` (footnote 79, p. 426, folio 395) — glossing Sachal's line `Thou hast split Zakariya with a saw`.

  suffering mystic, proclaiming anffl-haqq and therefore doomed to death` (p. 427, folio 306 as printed — rule
  gives 396).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **The feminine soul is an inheritance from Hinduism.** `one of the characteristics of Sindhi and Panjabi
  poetry is that the longing soul is always depicted as a woman; this typical aspect of Indian Sufi poetry was
  inherited from Hinduism (see Appendix 2).` (p. 420, folio 389). A causal claim about origin, with the evidence
  deferred to an appendix outside this chunk.
- **Sindhi mystical poetry has a continuous pre-Laṭīf line.** `Attempts at expressing mystical experiences in
  the Sindhi tongue go back at least to the sixteenth century.` (p. 420, folio 389), with Qāḍī Qādan's surviving
  verses as the evidence: `they distinctly show, however, all the peculiarities of later poetry.` (p. 420,
  folio 389).
- **Sindhi compactness is a grammatical fact, not a stylistic choice.** `Their language is extremely compact,
  since the Sindhi grammar allows very intricate combinations of words.` (p. 421, folio 390).
- **Laṭīf's technique of beginning in the middle.** `Everybody knew the contents of these tales, so that the poet
  did not need to dwell on the preliminaries; he could begin his poems with the most dramatic moment and then
  elaborate the feeling of the heroine and the different shades of the soul's movements—hope, longing, fear,
  annihilation.` (p. 422, folio 391).
- **A direct rebuke to the existing scholarship, including a named British scholar.** `Shah "Abdu'l-Latif did use
  the inherited language of the Sufis; he himself acknowledged his indebtedness to Rumi, and his work cannot be
  fully appreciated without a thorough knowledge of the different currents of Sufism in Iran and India. This is
  the point at which most of the Hindu interpreters, and even an excellent British scholar like Herbert Tower
  Sorley, have not done full justice to him.` (p. 424, folio 393). Footnote 74 praises the same book while
  qualifying it: `H. T. Sorley, Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit: His Poetry, Life, and Times (1940; reprint ed., Oxford,
  1966), is an excellent study, although it underscores the political and social background slightly too much.`
  (p. 421, folio 390). **Two judgements on Sorley, three pages apart, and they do not agree in tone** — kept side
  by side.
- **Trumpp's hostility is treated as a distorting factor.** `Some critics have considered them rather unpolished in
  terms of classical (i.e., "Persianized") metrics and vocabulary, or thought they were too full of jingling rhymes
  and puns (thus Trumpp, who thoroughly disliked Sufism).` (pp. 423–424, folios 392–393).
- **Sachal's litany is read as evidence about provincial knowledge, and partly discounted.** `This poem gives a good
  idea of the historical information on which a mystic in a distant province of the Muslim world could rely, though
  some of the charges cannot be verified.` (p. 426, folio 395). Schimmel then places the litany in a genre:
  Rūzbihān Baqlī `had devoted a whole paragraph in his Sharh-i shathiydt to the enumeration of the afflictions of
  the Sufis, beginning with the words: "Did you not see what the people of the exterior meaning did to Harith
  al-Muhasibi and Macruf and Sari?" (B 37)` — the `(B 37)` is a paragraph in Baqlī, **not** a page of this book —
  and `At about the same time, cAttar enumerated long lists of prophets as models for the suffering of those who
  love God most (MT 12; V 6).` (p. 426, folio 395). Function of the genre: `the poets established their spiritual
  genealogy with all the lovers who had been afflicted or tried by God throughout the ages.` (p. 426, folio 395).
- **The sources on Shāh ʿInāyat are unusable in both directions.** `cannot be detected from the sources, which are
  mostly unfriendly to him; positive evaluations, on the other hand, are strongly mixed with legends.` (p. 428,
  folio 397) — a methodological statement, not a verdict.
- **Political engagement by Sufi leaders is presented as recurrent, not exceptional.** `This was not the first time
  that mystical leaders in Sind, or in other parts of the Muslim world, were engaged in politics or in social
  reforms.` (p. 428, folio 397); and, of the *hurr*, `by acts that cannot be called mystical or even religious.`
  (p. 427, folio 306 as printed — rule gives 396) — Schimmel's own disqualification.
- **Pashto mysticism is sober where Sindhi and Panjabi are exuberant.** `A deeper study of Pashto mystical poetry
  would be worthwhile, and it would probably strengthen the impression that the inhabitants of the Afghan hills
  rarely indulged in the exuberant, fanciful love songs and poetical sighs that the Sindhi or Panjabi mystics did,
  but followed, on the whole, a more sober line in Sufism that was fully consistent with the tradition developed
  earlier in Ghazna.` (p. 432, folio 401) — flagged by Schimmel herself as an impression needing study.
- **Why later Sufism is understudied.** `In the course of this study we have seen how much criticism was directed
  against Sufism from the early tenth century on. The complaint about the decay of mysticism becomes stronger from
  century to century. This may be one reason why the later forms of Sufism have attracted comparatively little
  interest on the part of the scholars. Another reason is certainly the indebtedness of the majority of later Sufis
  to the system of Ibn cArabi, which apparently left very little room for independent theological thought.`
  (p. 434, folio 403).
- **The nineteenth-century turn to the Prophet.** `It is symptomatic of the development of Sunsm [text-layer?:
  Sufism] in the nineteenth century that the emphasis was placed once more upon the person of Muhammad.` (p. 434,
  folio 403).
- **The general modern verdict on the orders, reported not endorsed.** `The general view is that most of the
  orders, in their present forms, appeal to the lower instincts of the people and can be classified as either
  un-Islamic or antimodern.` (p. 435, folio 404). The counter-evidence Schimmel gives is the success of `a
  subbranch of the Shadhiliyya, which teaches the punctual fulfillment of man's duties rather than a way to ecstasy`
  (p. 435, folio 404).
- **No great Islamic poet can be read without Sufism.** `That statement could describe almost every great poet of
  the Islamic world, especially those under the influence of Iran. Even the work of those who are often considered
  to be frivolous, or pagan, or unreligious, cannot be properly interpreted without a thorough knowledge of the Sufi
  heritage.` (p. 437, folio 406).
- **The book's closing thesis.** `The mysticism of love and suffering—which teaches man to live and to die for a
  goal outside himself—is perhaps the most important message of Sufism today.` (p. 438, folio 407), followed by
  Schimmel's refusal to resolve the modernist question: `One may agree with this idea or reject it in favor of a
  modernized world view without the whole firmament of saints, which, like the stars at dawn, is naughted when the
  sun of reality appears.` (p. 438, folio 407).
- **Letters as veil** (Appendix 1): `the letters constitute something different from God; they are a veil of
  otherness that the mystic must penetrate. As long as he remains bound to the letters—as Niffari put it—the mystic
  is in some sense fettered by idols` (p. 442, folio 411), cited `(W 363-65)` — a locator in Nwyia, **not** a page
  of this book.
