## Practices, institutions, economy


**This is where the chunk's value lies.** Section "Community Life" is Schimmel's account of the Sufi
convent as an institution, and it describes the form every shrine in the archive took.

**The institutions and what they are called (pp. 262–263, folios 231–232).**
- `At the time that the fraternities came into existence, the center of mystical activity was no
  longer the private house or shop of the master. A more institutional structure proved to be
  necessary to cope with the growing number of disciples and adepts.` (p. 262, folio 231).
- `These new centers were usually called khanqah in the eastern Islamic world; the same term was
  used in medieval Egypt, where the Sufi khdnqahs formed cultural and theological centers and were
  su-` / `sidized by the government or endowed by influential benefactors.` (pp. 262–263,
  folios 231–232). `su-`/`sidized` [text-layer?: subsidized — the word is broken at the right margin
  and the `b` is absent; **not** reconstructed as a printed form].
- `The word zawiya, literally "corner," was used for smaller units, like the solitary dwelling place
  of a sheikh. The Turks would call the Sufi convent tekke.` (p. 262, folio 231).
- `The term ribdt, essentially connected with the frontier castles of the soldiers who defend Islam
  and expand it, could also be used to refer to the center of a fraternity. Often the expression
  dargdh "door, court," is used.` (pp. 262–263, folios 231–232). `ribdt` [text-layer?: ribāṭ];
  `dargdh` [text-layer?: dargāh].

**Architecture, and how a shrine forms around a grave (p. 263, folio 232).**
- `The history of Islamic art can contribute more to the history of the construction of such Sufi
  buildings, which were sometimes isolated but more frequently were connected with a mosque, a large
  kitchen for both the disciples and the guests, and sometimes a school.`
- `The tomb of the founder was usually located in the same compound—unless the compound was built
  later around the sacred place where the first saint of the order or subgroup was buried.`
- `In some khanqdhs, the dervishes lived in small cells—the Mevlana Miizesi in Konya is a fine
  example of this type; other convents had only one large room in which all the dervishes lived,
  studied, and worked.`
- Sanāʾī's couplet on the institution, `The khanqdh is the nest for the bird "purity," / it is the

  (p. 263, folio 232); footnote 5 cites `Abu 3 l-Majd Majdud Sana3!, Sana/'Pabad, line 289.`
  [text-layer?: Sanāʾīʾābād].

**Endowment, hospitality and langar (p. 263, folio 232).**
- `The organization of the khanqdh was not everywhere alike. Some khanqdhs lived on futuh,
  unsolicited gifts or donations, whereas others enjoyed regular stipends.`
- `Orders like the Chishtis in India were extremely hospitable, and foreign visitors were always
  admitted; in others, strict rules were enforced about visiting times and the types of visitors who
  were allowed to see the master. Arrangements for occasional and for long-term visitors were found
  in almost every khanqdh.`
- `The sheikh himself would live, with his family, in one quarter of the compound and see his
  disciples at fixed hours to supervise their spiritual progress; he would generally lead the five

- The communal-kitchen principle is stated twice more: the compound holds `a large kitchen for both

**Training, silsila, initiation and the khirqa (p. 265, folio 234).**
- `The formerly more individual regulations became stricter, the larger the number of adepts was,
  but the main object remained the same for most of the orders—to break and train the lower soul.
  Only a few orders laid more emphasis upon the purification of the heart than upon ascetic
  preliminaries.`
- `The Mevlevis, to mention a prominent example, trained the novice in different kitchen functions;
  at the same time he had to study Rumi's Mathnawl and its correct recitation and interpretation, as
  well as the technique of the whirling dance. This training continued for 1001 days.` `1001` is
  printed cleanly.
- `Each adept, of course, had to learn the silsila, the spiritual lineage that leads from his master
  back through past generations to the Prophet; the pivotal figure in most silsilas is Junayd. The
  proper knowledge of the affiliations, difficult as it may be for an uninitiated person, is
  absolutely necessary for an understanding of the mystical tradition.`
- `During the initiation ceremony—a festive day in the dervish community—the adept had to pronounce
  the bay'a, the oath of allegiance, and was invested with the khirqa, the Sufi frock. An essential
  part of the ceremony consists of the novice's putting his hand into the sheikh's hand so that the
  baraka is properly transmitted.`
- `Another important act is the bestowing of the taj, the dervish cap. Headdresses differed from
  order to order both in shape and in color, and the number of their parts—twelve, according to the
  twelve imams, or nine, or seven—has symbolic meaning.`
- The warning against fossilisation, from Yunus Emre `(Y 176)`, `(Y 520)` and from

**Other points a human should check.**

- **p. 264 yields no folio and almost no content.** The plate caption `Rembrandt's copy of a Mogul
  miniature, showing the founders of the four great mystical orders.` (p. 264, folio not stated)
  names **none** of the four founders and gives **no** date for the miniature or for Rembrandt's
  copy; only `British Museum` is given as the holding institution. Anything more would be invented.
- **`Kazarum` appears twice and ends in `-um` both times** (main text p. 260, footnote 4 p. 260),
  where the order's name is printed `Kazeruniyya`. The missing final `-ī` may be right-margin
  truncation (class 6) or may be how the text layer renders the name; it is flagged and **not**
  reconstructed, since the two occurrences cannot decide it.
- **The second Birge quotation on p. 269 has no citation.** `(BO 202)` attaches to the first
  quotation only; `"even if the sheikh is well meaning but ignorant, his influence is certain to
  count for evil."` follows in quotation marks with no locator — source `(not stated)`. Do not
  silently extend `BO 202` to cover it.
- **`Islamic Studies \, no. 4 (1962)` (p. 258, footnote 46) has lost its volume number.** The `\` is
  not in the measured substitution table, the volume is not recoverable, and it is not guessed. The
  parallel citation in footnote 6 prints `Islamic Studies 4, no. 4 (1965)` cleanly, which is a
  *different* article and must not be used to repair this one.
- **`Abu Sacid` is spelled three ways in two pages** — `Abu Sacld` (p. 266, p. 272), `Abu Sacid`
