# rafat_bulleh_shah_selection — chunk 003 — pp. 92–221

**What this range is.** The parallel-text body of the book. **Odd (recto) pages carry Taufiq
Rafat's English verse translation**, headed by the printed folio number and then the poem's title
in capitals (e.g. p. 151 opens `131` then `LASS, LOOK TO YOUR SPINNING`). **Even (verso) pages
carry the Punjabi original in Urdu/Shahmukhi script**, which the tesseract route rendered as Latin
gibberish — see Doubts; nothing on a verso is read, quoted or transliterated below. **I count 51
titled English poems** across the 65 rectos, because several poems run over two or three rectos
(`THE FOLLOWER` pp. 99–101; `WHO'S KEEPING THE GOSSIPS BUSY?` pp. 111–113; `LOVE COMES ON BRIGHT
NEW WINGS` pp. 131–133; `ENOUGH OF LEARNING, FRIEND` pp. 135–139; `STOP THE SPINNING-WHEEL, GIRL`
pp. 141–143; `LASS, LOOK TO YOUR SPINNING` pp. 151–155; `WHO CHOOSE COLD-HEARTED DEARS`
pp. 161–163; `MY LOVE HAS COME TO CALL` pp. 165–167; `TRUTH WILL OUT` pp. 179–181; `STRANGE ARE THE
WAYS OF MY LOVE` pp. 193–195; `THE STORY OF CREATION` pp. 203–205; `LOVE'S ARRIVAL` pp. 207–209),
and because at least two versos are effectively blank (p. 124, p. 214).

**Folio, as the pages actually print it.** Every recto in this range prints its folio as the first
or last line except **pp. 99, 117 and 119, which print no legible number**. Where printed, the
number agrees with `folio = PDF − 20` at every single one of the 62 readable rectos — p. 93 = 73,
p. 125 = 105, p. 151 = 131, p. 185 = 165, p. 221 = 201. **No page in this chunk contradicts the
rule.** Folios are set at the head on most rectos but at the foot on pp. 95, 107, 109, 111, 113,
179, 185, 209, 213, 219 and 221. Versos print no legible number and are cited `(p. N, folio not
stated)`.

**Negative finding, stated once because it governs everything below.** In all 51 poems **not one
shrine, tomb, mazar, dargah, ʿurs, mela, langar, sajjada nashin, custodian, endowment, offering,
Thursday observance or exorcism is named** — (not stated). The only built religious places named
are the generic "mosque", "temple", "monastery" and "precincts"; the only burial word in the range
is "grave" (p. 115, folio 95). The religious economy in this chunk is entirely satirical and
generic: mullahs, priests, scholars, merchants, tax-collecting servants.

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`** (the book's spelling is **"Bulleh Shah"**, usually shortened to
  **"Bulleh"** in the signature line): **the poet's takhallus closes or turns roughly a third of the
  poems in this range, and the chunk gives it in two forms.** The full form twice — the title
  `BRIEFING BULLEH` and its line "Every female from near and afar turned up to brief Bulleh Shah."
  (p. 93, folio 73), and "Bulleh Shah, take a cue, whom you seek is seeking you" (p. 113, folio 93).
  The short form at least fifteen times: "Dance, Bulleh, dance, your love has come to you."
  (p. 105, folio 85); "To win his love, Bulleh exercises his wits" (p. 115, folio 95); "Bulleh,
  you'd better mend your ways" (p. 145, folio 125); "Bulleh, only love can douse" (p. 149, folio
  129); "Bulleh, love devours all, takes all to task" (p. 157, folio 137); "Bulleh, what is my
  life unless love twists its knife?" (p. 167, folio 147); "Love, Bulleh, is part of us" (p. 181,
  folio 161); "Bulleh, every beat of your heart cries love" (p. 185, folio 165); "not Bulleh, but
  Inayat must steer" (p. 189, folio 169); "Bulleh, love will not settle for less, your most is
  little" (p. 191, folio 171); "Bulleh, what can you hope to find when love is totally blind?"
  (p. 195, folio 175); "Bulleh. [OCR?] [Bulleh] tries hard to shirk the embrace of Hindu and Turk."
  (p. 197, folio 177); "Bulleh, do what you're told" (p. 209, folio 189); "A stove is better than
  Bulleh, it cooks something at least." and "Love is a pinprick, Bulleh, to a slaughterhouse beast."
  (p. 217, folio 197).
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **the only statement in the chunk about his caste or descent, and it
  is a refusal.** `BRIEFING BULLEH` has the women of the household tell him "it's a sin to condemn
  the prophet's kin. Those who acknowledge them, you tell, will get the one-way ticket to hell."
  and he answers "Does sanctity deserve such scorn? It's God's carelessness where one is born. …
  My caste? I've none. I'm just a man." (p. 93, folio 73). **The book gives him no Sayyid claim, no
  order, no silsila and no lineage anywhere in this range** — (not stated); the archive row's
  "Abdullah Shah Qadri" is nowhere supported here.
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **a spiritual guide is claimed for him twice, unnamed on one occasion
  and named "Inayat" on the others — but the naming is held unmapped, see Doubts.** Unnamed: "then
  help there will be none till you call on Bulleh's guide." (p. 155, folio 135). Named alongside
  the signature: "not Bulleh, but Inayat must steer." (p. 189, folio 169) and "The flames engulfing
  my house, Bulleh, only love can douse. Inayat calls up the aisle" (p. 149, folio 129).
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **the poems' persona is female throughout the spinning and
  waiting poems, and the signature sits inside that persona.** "See, | [OCR?] [I] am dressed to the
  nines for that cold man." (p. 185, folio 165); "| [OCR?] [I] was all dolled up for him when he
  went off on a whim." (p. 117, folio not stated); "Love has brought me to the door of inayat
  [OCR?] [Inayat], dressed like a whore." (p. 101, folio 81); "I'm just a sweepress." (p. 187,
  folio 167). Recorded because the archive row's saint is male and an entry quoting these lines
  needs to know the voice is not.
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **the one place in the chunk where his own town is named is a bare
  toponym and is therefore held under "Other" — see that bullet and Doubts.** "Many a day Kasur has
  missed your smile." (p. 149, folio 129).

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- **Inayat / Inayat Shah — the murshid of the refrains. HELD UNMAPPED; this is the largest single
  judgement in the chunk and the Doubts paragraph below is the operative one.** The name occurs
  **five times** in this range and never with a location, a date, an order or a tomb — all four are
  (not stated). In order: (i) "Love has brought me to the door of inayat [OCR?] [Inayat], dressed
  like a whore. — I click my heels, and leap in glee." (`THE FOLLOWER`, p. 101, folio 81); (ii)
  "Inayat calls up the aisle" (`COME SEE ME ONCE IN A WHILE`, p. 149, folio 129); (iii) "I'm
  waiting, pass this way once more, Inayat Shah, my eyes are sore with looking for you at the
  door." — the only occurrence of the fuller form **"Inayat Shah"** — followed in the same poem by
  "Who will lead me to the place of my master, so by his grace he may my heart and body restore."
  (`I'M WAITING`, p. 185, folio 165); (iv) "not Bulleh, but Inayat must steer." (`THE LOAD`, p. 189,
  folio 169); (v) "Only the proper guide can lead you to His side; and unless you choose Inayat,
  you will lose." (`THE STORY OF CREATION`, p. 205, folio 185). **What the chunk establishes is only
  this: Inayat is "my master" and "the proper guide", he has a "door" and a "place", and choosing
  him is necessary. Nothing in the chunk says where he lived, when he died, what order he held, or
  where he is buried.**
- **Ranjha and Heer** — `THE TRANSFORMATION` is built on the identification: "'Ranjha, Ranhja
  [OCR?] [Ranjha]' | [OCR?] [I] cried till only Ranjha is there? [OCR?] [here.]  I'm trarnsformed
  [OCR?] [transformed], now Heer has disappeared." and closes "'Ranjha, Ranjha' | [OCR?] [I] cried
  till only Ranjha is here." (p. 121, folio 101). Also "Ranjha is one with his bride. She looked
  for him far and wide, but he had never left her side." (p. 133, folio 113). **Waris Shah is not
  named anywhere in this chunk** — the Hir story appears without its author.
- **Hazara and Sial** — the only two toponyms from the Hir cycle, both in one line: "Hazara calls
  me, in Sial no friends are near." (p. 121, folio 101). No shrine, tomb or saint attached.
- **Shams** — named once, in a list of martyrs, with no gloss: "Sanaan made to feed swine, Shams
  was skinned alive, Mansur crucified." (p. 207, folio 187). **Two archive rows could take this
  bare name — see Doubts. Do not resolve it from this chunk.**
- **Mansur, Sanaan, Abraham, Zachariah, Joseph** — the rest of the same martyr-list, and the whole
  of it: "Abraham set alight, Zachariah sawn in half, Joseph was auctioned off. And what will be my
  lot?" (p. 207, folio 187). No archive row names any of them.
- **Allah and Ram** — the two-line poem `NAMES` in full: "Having understood, why heed the storm
  over such. [OCR?] [such] names as Allah.and [OCR?] [Allah and] Ram." (p. 147, folio 127). **Ram
  is a bare divine name in a couplet about religious labels; held here rather than mapped — see
  Doubts.**
- **Kasur** — "You are gone, friend, far away beyond my reach. Many a day Kasur has missed your
  smile." (`COME SEE ME ONCE IN A WHILE`, p. 149, folio 129). **A bare toponym: no tomb, shrine,
  saint, ʿurs or custodian is attached to it here**, so it is held under the interim convention —
  see Doubts for the row it would take if the ruling reverses.
- **Punjab** — in the same poem, as a place gone to ruin: "Now hell has opened its bogs. Punjab has
  gone to the dogs. This hell is the nethermost hell." followed by "This century cramps our style."
  (p. 149, folio 129). **This is the chunk's only datable-sounding phrase and it gives no date** —
  (not stated).
- **Lahore** — twice, both times as a seat of power or of menace, never with a shrine attached:
  "The rulers of Lahore under threatening skies put their hands to their ears and shut their eyes."
  (`I'M HARBOURING A THIEF`, p. 175, folio 155); "| [OCR?] [I] should have kept it in check, but
  Lahore's thug covered his bluff." (p. 193, folio 173). Bare toponyms — see Doubts.
- **Tirath and Mecca** — "God's not in Tirath or Mecca, but in hearts where true love sings."
  (p. 131, folio 111). No row; the chunk gives no gloss on "Tirath".
- **The sadhu** — named once as a positive figure, against the mullahs and priests elsewhere: "Who
  knows what the sadhu knows will surely find repose. On the tideless river of peace he is afloat."
  (p. 179, folio 159).

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Ritual named and dismissed, as a set — the chunk's most archive-usable passage.** The poem
  `RITUALS` in the round: "Fasting, pilgrimage, and the call to prayer, love has drowned them all.
  — Can any artifice survive when messages of love arrive? … The moment Love came toward me,
  rituals went by the board." (p. 125, folio 105). The same list recurs elsewhere: "Now prayer and
  fast are done. [OCR?] [done,] for the cup is raised in skoal." (p. 165, folio 145); "And God was
  temple and mosque and all you could ask, but cold to fast and prayer-call." (p. 205, folio 185).
- **The objects of observance, listed to be destroyed.** "Scatter the rosary, spurn ablutions and
  the prayer-mat burn, for lovers they sigh and yearn to taste prohibited things." (p. 131, folio
  111). And the bodily cost of prayer: "Genuflexions night and day have worn the knee-caps away."
  (p. 131, folio 111); "You stay up all night to pray, but dogs are sleepless too; they're better
  than you." (p. 145, folio 125).
- **Mosque, temple, monastery and "precincts" — the buildings, always in pairs and always
  relativised.** "He shuttles between temple and mosque." (p. 157, folio 137); "Merchants in mosque
  and monastery , [OCR?] [monastery,] thugs in the landlord's pay." (p. 123, folio 103); "I read
  love's book. Ever since, mosques have made me wince. Now I revel in precincts echoing to a
  thousand strings." (p. 131, folio 111). **"Precincts echoing to a thousand strings" is the nearest
  thing to samaʿ in the chunk; no shrine, qawwal or assembly is named** — (not stated).
- **The muezzin and the sermon.** "The muezzin shatters the air while you heap prayer on prayer and
  sermons drip from your hair, but to what end?" (p. 137, folio 117).
- **The religious professions as a predatory economy — the densest run in the chunk.** "Mullahs mug
  their way to power, and now are judge and juror; and avarice grows by the hour, and straight to
  hell you wend." and "You dream up problems in a wink, schism is your meat and drink, beneath
  saintly garb you stink, saying what you don't intend" (p. 139, folio 119). Earlier in the same
  poem: "Has this knowledge brought relief or added grief to grief? You nab the just, and the thief
  you fail to apprehend." and "Such learning fools elevates and some fresh bother creates, is used
  to con illiterates, make vows with false intent" (p. 137, folio 117). Also "Mullah and
  torch-bearer come of one stock, giving light to others, themselves in the dark." (p. 173, folio
  153) and "Talk only of God, the rest is merely chatter, though scholar and priest have tried to
  confuse the matter." (p. 199, folio 179).
- **Oath-taking on scripture, and the hypocrisy of it.** "whose heart is absent when the hand is
  placed on the Holy Book in solemn promise. O ruthless ones, the trap you set for others, is set
  for you." (p. 159, folio 139).
- **The fair and the bazar as the figure for the world.** "The world's a fun-fair. … Stroll through
  the bazar, join the revelry there, then on to the next. The world's a fun-fair." and "The world
  and my love made me their slave. But you'll go empty-handed to the grave." (p. 115, folio 95).
- **Merchants, price and purchase.** "A hundred merchants are here who tell of rubies they have
  brought to sell. … What was the price they asked? I-cried [OCR?] [I cried] at pin-pricks, and
  they wanted my head. … The lowly should not reach for the sky. A glass-bead is all they can buy."
  (`THE BUYER`, p. 129, folio 109). And accounting turned eschatological: "What use are these
  assets, alas, my balance-sheet is up there." (p. 213, folio 193).
- **Taxation by a lord's servants.** "| [OCR?] [I] must deal with one cold to the bone, his
  servants tax each stick | [OCR?] [I] own which is quite a bundle. As soon as it is tied, the
  merchants will be here." (`THE LOAD`, p. 189, folio 169).
- **Caste, untouchability and wage labour — the sweepress poem, the chunk's one sustained social
  description.** "I'm just a sweepress. Hair uncombed, barefoot, | [OCR?] [I] receive word of his
  coming … The broom my meditation: with it I've swept into my basket all that the world has kept.
  The judge knows much, the king rules with fear, but I'm happy to be allowed in here. … Being
  untouchable, none comes near me, but I'm on the way, I do not care. What's my pay after a hard
  day's grind? A hard pillow, and what you leave behind. This is my life: cold and sickness and
  scorn; an empty stomach, clothes that are torn. The straws of my broom are all that | [OCR?] [I]
  own." (p. 187, folio 167).
- **Caste refused, three times over.** "My caste? I've none. I'm just a man." (p. 93, folio 73);
  "What ecstatic being is here who is so drenched in oneness for caste he has no care?" (p. 111,
  folio 91); "Having no caste, sect, or creed, | [OCR?] [I] am different indeed." (p. 197, folio
  177).
- **Spinning, dowry and marriage — three whole poems and part of a fourth, the chunk's largest
  single theme.** `THE BROKEN SPINNING-WHEEL`: "The spinning-wheel is broken, | [OCR?] [I] cannot
  spin. Call the smith, for the axle 'wobbles [OCR?] [wobbles] on its pin. The thread keeps
  breaking, smith, Let your job be neat; it sways so that not a spool comes off complete. … its
  grease is all used up, it creaks and groans." and "He is gone with the cows and my mind's feet
  follow him to the pasture. The girls say come and spin, but my heart is heavy, and hands numb."
  (p. 119, folio not stated). `STOP THE SPINNING-WHEEL, GIRL`: "Your wedding day is just a week
  away … If dowryless you leave how can you hope to please? Your spouse will grumble and grieve."
  (p. 141, folio 121) and "Playmates who are wise are busy with needle 'and [OCR?] [and] dyes, but
  you are set for a house where you'll not get a square meal, girl." (p. 143, folio 123).
  `LASS, LOOK TO YOUR SPINNING`: "The new cotton crop is in. Take it for scouring, and then sit
  down to spin and spin, or spinning time will be gone." (p. 153, folio 133) and "The sun is high.
  Attend to your chores, and send the dowry to the dyer, then you'll have earned each one's
  respect." and "A tough mother-in-law waits." (pp. 153–155, folios 133–135). And in
  `GATHER ROUND ME, FRIENDS`: "What was my dowry? A stove and a shawl. When | [OCR?] [I] think of
  it now the tear-drops fall." (p. 177, folio 157).
- **Cloth, metal and the trades, used to level social rank.** "Warp and woof, spindle and reel,
  back and front, head and heel, will end up in the one cloth — that will not last the night." and
  "Khaddar and muslin, coarse or fine, dress for ploughman, groom, divine, issue from the self-same
  loom no matter what the guise." and "The bangles worn by girls, and rings and bracelets and such
  adornings are from the same silver beaten, though hands are dark or light." (`ALL COTTON-BOLLS
  ARE WHITE`, p. 127, folio 107). Also the potter: "first fashions a pot, then shatters it to bits"
  (p. 115, folio 95), and "Each in his turn youngster and old man fade, innkeeper and servingman,
  mistress and maid." (p. 115, folio 95).
- **Divination, astrology, spells and cures — the chunk's only "ritual practice" outside formal
  worship.** "| [OCR?] [I] ask each passing magus, why is it everything | [OCR?] [I] do turns out
  badly? Astrologer, be kind, tell the truth for once. Am | [OCR?] [I] cursed? Was | [OCR?] [I]
  born ill-fated?" and "Someone has cast a spell on me so that 'my [OCR?] [my] eyes cannot even
  shed tears." (`ACCURSED`, p. 97, folio 77). Also "To entice him | [OCR?] [I] did use all sorts of
  spells and brews." (p. 167, folio 147) and the apothecary: "since within me it opened its shop
  and | [OCR?] [I] drank from its poisoned cup. Come to my help, apothecary." (p. 99, folio not
  stated).
- **Dance and music as devotion.** "Dance, Bulleh, dance, your love has come to you." (p. 105,
  folio 85); "Love has brought me to the door of inayat [OCR?] [Inayat], dressed like a whore. — I
  click my heels, and leap in glee." (p. 101, folio 81); "Sweet music adds its boon to a sweet
  singer in tune." (p. 165, folio 145).
- **Pilgrimage and renunciation as instructions given to others.** "Truth wrecks the dogmatist's
  pride, it gives the lover a bride and a gay new world beside, and says to the pilgrim, go." and
  "Renounce, renounce, take the vow." (`BE SILENT NOW`, p. 107, folio 87). Ascetic dress: "Don the
  sackcloth, throw the white dress away, for they are covered with stains who whiteness wear."
  (p. 121, folio 101).
- **Alms and the shared meal of beggars.** "When beggars get together of morsels they make a
  feast." (p. 217, folio 197). And charity's limits: "Hats off to those who a lost coin reimburse,
  but have no such scruple about a fat purse." (`HONESTY`, p. 211, folio 191 [OCR? printed `19]`]).
- **Letter-carrying and the messenger.** "Wanderer, ho! Bring back a word of love, or so … Don't be
  impatient, take your time in giving my news; with zestures [OCR?] [gestures] show" (p. 95, folio
  75), and "He has not come. In the evening | [OCR?] [I] sit and write letters." (p. 97, folio 77).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **No miracle is claimed for any saint anywhere in this chunk.** The only narrative material is a
  single list of martyrdoms, given as assertions with no source and no gloss: "Abraham set alight,
  Zachariah sawn in half, Joseph was auctioned off." and "Sanaan made to feed swine, Shams was
  skinned alive, Mansur crucified." (`LOVE'S ARRIVAL`, p. 207, folio 187). The poem continues with
  the beloved as arsonist: "Whatever house you tried was burnt to the ground; on the ash-heap you
  were found. Are you now satisfied?" (p. 209, folio 189).
- **A cosmogony, given in the poet's own voice and not attributed to any authority.** "A Word
  existed in space which had no form or face. None mighty or small. No God or prophet at all. …
  The Word had no sire or heir, it was beyond compare. It had no colour or smell. There was no
  heaven or hell. … Then the Word was mated and angels and saints created to choir around His seat,
  and kneel at His feet." (`THE STORY OF CREATION`, p. 203, folio 183).
- **An incarnational claim, flat and unhedged.** "God has come down as man. He is deer and leopard,
  kills and is killed, he is master and slave, sells and is sold." (p. 183, folio 163); and
  "Whatever the time and place God is in every face." (p. 181, folio 161).

## Doubts

- **The Shahmukhi versos in pp. 92–220 are untranscribed and must be treated as absent.** Tesseract
  was run with a Latin model over the Urdu/Shahmukhi original and produced Latin gibberish on every
  even page in this range — `MTEL` (p. 92), `[4 uf &4 E 6` (p. 100), `afer Zit ui Ly! Lf` (p. 100),
  `ly lait` (p. 94), `Wilase` (p. 170), `SMG` (p. 220). **None of it is text**, none of it is read
  or quoted above, and **any Punjabi original needed for an entry must be re-read from the page
  image**. RULE 2 consequence: this chunk can supply no name, date or place in the original script.
- **The verso headers carry a short glyph string that is probably a garbled Urdu-numeral folio, but
  it is not legible and I have not used it.** Examples in the printed order the OCR gives them:
  `oy` (p. 94), `OTA` (p. 102), `AA` (p. 108), `4A` (p. 118), `1*A` (p. 128), `IrA` (p. 148), `ifs`
  (p. 150), `Ir?` (p. 152), `1OA` (p. 178), `my` (p. 182), `14°` (p. 190), `1K¢` (p. 194), `laA`
  (p. 198), `\Ar` (p. 202), `(AY` (p. 206), `{AA` (p. 208), `19°` (p. 210), `iar` (p. 212), `194`
  (p. 216), `(9A` (p. 218). **A human reading the page images could recover the verso folios from
  these; I have not attempted it and every verso above is cited "folio not stated".**
- **THE INAYAT MAPPING — the operative doubt in this chunk, covering all five occurrences at once.**
  The archive holds two rows that the bare name "Inayat" fits: **`shrine-of-shah-inayat-qadiri`**
  (Shah Inayat Qadiri, Mozang Chungi, Lahore, d. c. 1728, Qadri Shattari) and
  **`dargah-roza-sufi-shah-inayat-shaheed`** (Sufi Shah Inayat Shaheed, Jhok Sharif, Sindh,
  d. 1718). **This chunk gives no location, no death year, no order and no tomb for Inayat**, so
  nothing in it discriminates between them, and I have **not** picked one: the material is held in
  full under "Other saints, sites and events" so the consolidator can move it in one operation.
  What the chunk does establish, and it is all it establishes: Inayat is "my master" and "the
  proper guide" of the poems' speaker, with a "door" (p. 101, folio 81) and a "place" (p. 185,
  folio 165), and the fuller form of the name is **"Inayat Shah"** (p. 185, folio 165). The two
  Lahore mentions in this chunk (p. 175, folio 155; p. 193, folio 173) belong to different poems
  and are **not** attached to Inayat — they must not be used to place him. **If the consolidator
  has the book's own Introduction from chunk 001 or 002 and it locates Inayat, five bullets and
  one citable passage move to that row at once.**
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah` is mapped on the signature alone.** Bulleh Shah is the row's principal
  figure and this book is his poems in translation, so the 18 September person-ruling applies
  directly; but **the chunk gives no tomb, no death year, no birth year, no order and no shrine for
  him** — every one is (not stated). Only the name, the takhallus and the caste-refusal at p. 93,
  folio 73 are actually evidenced.
- **Kasur is a bare toponym here and is held under the interim convention (a).** "Many a day Kasur
  has missed your smile." (p. 149, folio 129) attaches no tomb, shrine, saint, ʿurs or custodian to
  the town; the poem's addressee is the absent beloved, not a buried saint. **If the toponym ruling
