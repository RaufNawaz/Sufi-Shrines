# rafat_bulleh_shah_selection — chunk 002 — pp. 33–91

The back half of Taufiq Rafat's **Introduction** (pp. 33–52, folios 13–32), then the Michigan
digitisation leaf (p. 53), then the **first fifteen translated poems** (pp. 55–91, folios 35–71)
printed **recto-only, each facing a Shahmukhi original on the verso** — and every one of those
verso pages (pp. 54, 56, 58, 60, 62, 64, 66, 68, 70, 72, 74, 76, 78, 80, 82, 84, 86, 88, 90) comes
through tesseract as unreadable Latin noise. See Doubts: **the original script is present in the
book and lost in this transcription**, so the protocol's "quote the original script" rule cannot be
met for a single word of it.

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`** (the book spells him **"Bulleh Shah"** in roman throughout, and
  **"Bulleb Shab"** [OCR?] [Bulleh Shah] wherever the name falls inside an italic title):
  **the death year, flat and unqualified.** "Bulleh Shah died in 1758." (p. 48, folio 28).
  **No tomb, no shrine, no burial place, no ʿurs, no custodian and no descendant is named for him
  anywhere in this chunk** — all (not stated), including at Qasur.
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **an age, from which the book's implied birth year can be read but is
  never printed.** Of the Shahidganj carnage of 1746: "That year Bulleh Shah was 66 years old; he
  was to die twelve years later, a disenchanted man, witness to three more Afghan invasions of
  extreme severity under Ahmad Shah Abdali." (p. 43, folio 23). The book states neither a birth
  year nor a birth place — (not stated). See Doubts.
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **his order, stated once and stated plainly.** "Although he clearly
  expressed himself as being in the Qadiriya tradition to which his preceptor Shah Inayat belonged,
  Bulleh Shah cuts across the mystical politics of his times." (p. 39, folio 19). The archive row's
  "Qadiri" is therefore supported by this book, in the form "Qadiriya".
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **Qasur as the place he lived, and the book's governing image for it.**
  "If Takht Hazara, Multan and Prem Nagar are idylls, the city of Qasur where he lived is a kind of
  inferno." (p. 40, folio 20); "Bulleh Shah lived and sang in the inferno of Qasur" (p. 47, folio
  27); "Bulleh Shah's references to political unrest and social injustice are related most
  immediately to the state of affairs in and around Qasur, the city he has condemned explicitly in
  his 'dobvas' [OCR?] [dohras]" (pp. 40–41, folios 20–21). The marginal heading over the passage is
  *Qasur as bell* [OCR?] [Qasur as hell] (p. 40, folio 20). **Qasur itself is held as a bare
  toponym — see Other and Doubts.**
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **the two dohras on the city, and what they are said to say.** "Two

- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **the author's critical judgement of him, stated against his
  reputation.** "Much is made of Bulleh Shah's mysticism. As in Rumi, it tends to limit him as a
  poet and causes him to repeat himself." (p. 43, folio 23); "a large number of purely mystical
  kafis also exist and they are monotonous in their unvarying insistence on mysticism." (p. 43,
  folio 23). And on his politics: "Bulleh Shah cannot be designated a conscientious objector par
  excellence against the political despotism of his times simply because he treated the subject in

- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **the spinning-wheel as the book's named "basic metaphor".** "The basic

  rural woman was permanently linked… whatever she spins would become a part of her dowry." (p. 46,
  folio 26). Rafat calls the resulting monologue "unsurpassed in Indian poetry in its sustained
  anatomy of a physical object" (p. 46, folio 26).
- **`shrine-of-shah-inayat-qadiri`** (the book writes **"Shah Inayat"** once and **"Shah Enayat"**

  explicit about Lahore and about Mian Mir.** "Shah Enayat of Lahore, the beloved preceptor of

  single most discriminating sentence in the chunk for this row** — see Doubts.
- **`shrine-of-shah-inayat-qadiri`**: **his order, named twice.** "the Qadiriya tradition to which his

  'Dastur-ul-Amal'" (p. 44, folio 24). **No death year, no tomb, no shrine and no Mozang** — all

- **`shrine-of-shah-inayat-qadiri`**: **he is credited with a written work.** "Dr. Lajwanti claims
  that Shah Enayat Qadiri in his book 'Dastur-ul-Amal' points to the Hindu origin of the mystical
  doctrines and their recrudescence in Greek thought centuries later." (p. 44, folio 24). Reported
  by Rafat as Lajwanti's claim, not as his own.
- **`shrine-of-shah-inayat-qadiri`**: **the rejection of Bulleh Shah, given a political motive.**
  "It also accounts for Shah Enayat's early rejection of Bulleh Shah as a follower when the latter
  began to sing his rebuking lines against the orthodoxy." (p. 42, folio 22) — the surrounding
  argument being that "During Aurangzeb's period, the Qadiriya mystics in Lahore were on the
  defensive, careful not to offend an orthodoxy inclined to take severe punitive measures against
  heretics." (p. 42, folio 22). **No dancing-girl story appears in this chunk.**
- **`shrine-of-shah-inayat-qadiri`**: **the master as the object of the envoi, which Rafat makes a
  structural argument.** "it is only in Rumi, therefore, that we find a true parallel to Bulleh
  'Shah's invocation of Shah Enayat at the end of the kafi" (p. 45, folio 25); "The most common
  reference to Shah Enayat in Bulleh Shah's kafis comes at the end of a whole train of suffering
  caused by separation." (p. 45, folio 25); "Shah Enayat is made to be the source of mystical
  secrets" (p. 45, folio 25); "the mursbid [OCR?] [murshid] is called upon to provide solace to a

- **`shrine-of-shah-inayat-qadiri`**: **quoted inside a verse line, as a figure of restraint.**
  "Shah Enayat is a figure of political restraint: 'Bajb vaseelay likbiya na jaey/ Shab Enayat bbed

- **`shrine-of-mian-mir`** (the book writes **"Mian Mir"**): **he founded the Qadiriya school of

  school of Lahore founded by Mian Mir had begun a close sympathetic link with the Sikhs when they
  had yet to adopt their anti-Muslim posture. Guru Arjun was a follower of the great Qadiriya mystic
  and had got him to lay the foundation stone of the Hariman-dir temple at Amritsar." (pp. 41–42,
  folios 21–22). **No death year, no tomb, no Dharampura** — (not stated).
- **`shrine-of-mian-mir`**: **named as a target of Naqshbandi hostility at court.** Of Sheikh Ahmad

- **`shrine-of-sachal-sarmast`** (the book writes **"Sachal Sarmast"**): **dated as printed, and
  placed.** "a political poem of Sachal Sarmast (1739-1826), an admiring contemporary." (p. 40,

- **`shrine-of-sachal-sarmast`**: **the quoted political poem, in full as the chunk carries it.**

- **`mausoleum-of-waris-shah`** (the book writes **"Waris Shah"**): **contemporary, fellow student,

- **`mausoleum-of-waris-shah`**: **named twice more, in the survey of criticism.** "A 1979 book of

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- **Qasur** — the city, held as a bare toponym under the interim convention: "the city of Qasur where
  he lived is a kind of inferno" (p. 40, folio 20); "the Pathan rulers of Qasur who came out of their
  defensive underground fastnesses to rule the area after the decline of the Mughal rule from Delhi"
  (p. 41, folio 21); "the Sikhs were an enemy in the satrapy of Lahore and in the Pathan fastness of
  Qasur from 1707 onwards" (p. 41, folio 21); Banda Bairagi's Sikhs "were to lay waste the whole of
  the province excluding Lahore and Qasur" (p. 43, folio 23); "The governor of Lahore, Abdul Samad
  Khan marched on the stronghold of the Qasur Pathans and the ensuing battles further impoverished
  the countryside" (p. 43, folio 23); on Nadir Shah's "second march he did not take the route via
  Qasur as the countryside was denuded of all resources" (p. 43, folio 23). **No tomb, shrine, ʿurs
  or custodian is attached to Qasur anywhere in this chunk — see Doubts.**
- **Lahore** — a bare toponym here, and it resolves to no single row: "the satrapy of Lahore"
  (p. 41, folio 21); "the Qadiriya mystics in Lahore were on the defensive" (p. 42, folio 22); "the
  repressive atmosphere in Lahore" (p. 42, folio 22); "The governor of Lahore, Abdul Samad Khan"
  and "A later governor Zakriya Khan" (p. 43, folio 23); as a publishing centre (pp. 48–49, folios
  28–29); "Lahore's University of the Punjab" and its "M.A. courses in Punjabi" (p. 50, folio 30).
- **Multan, Takht Hazara, Prem Nagar** — the three "idylls" against which Qasur is set: "Takht
  Hazara, the birth place of Ranjha, is a mystical idyll which attains the same status in the eyes
  of Bulleh Shah as the Prem Nagar of bbakti [OCR?] [bhakti] tradition and Multan as the seat of the
  Suhrawardiya school of mysticism." (pp. 38–39, folios 18–19); "If Takht Hazara, Multan and Prem
  Nagar are idylls" (p. 40, folio 20). Bare toponyms — see Doubts for the rows Multan would take.
- **Amritsar and the Harimandir** — "the foundation stone of the Hariman-dir temple at Amritsar"
  (p. 42, folio 22), and Sachal Sarmast "continued to send his disciples to Amritsar for homage"
  (p. 42, folio 22). **Not an archive row.**
- **The carnage of Shahidganj, 1746** — "Aroused by the plunder of the Sikhs, Zakriya Khan enacted
  the carnage of Shahidganj in 1746, putting to the sward [OCR?] [sword] three thousand captives of
  war." (p. 43, folio 23). **No gurdwara, shrine or custodian is named — held here, see Doubts.**
- **Guru Nanak** — "the Sikh saint Guru Nanak taken his slokes from a descendant of the poet two
  centuries later" (p. 47, folio 27); and a long quoted sloke: "'Modesty and religion bave [OCR?]
  [have] disappeared because falsehood reigns supreme, The Muslim Mullab [OCR?] [Mullah] and the
  Hindu Pandit bave [OCR?] [have] resigned their duties, the Devil reads the marriage vows. Praises
  of murder are sung and people swear themselves with blood instead of saffron'" (p. 41, folio 21);
  "Nanak's lines, written during the chaotic Lodhi period, are non-sectarian because in Nanak's
  life-time Sikkism [OCR?] [Sikhism] had not yet come into being." (p. 41, folio 21). **Held here
  rather than mapped: Guru Nanak is the principal figure of roughly nineteen archive rows and
  nothing in this chunk discriminates — see Doubts.**
- **Guru Tegh Bahadur** — "Aurangzeb had Guru Tegh Bahadur beheaded in Delhi in 1675 for having
  befriended Dara Shikoh during the prince's flight to Lahore." (p. 42, folio 22). **No archive row
  names him.**
- **Sheikh Ahmad Sirhindi, Mujaddid Alif Sani** — "Sheikh Ahmad Sirhindi, Mujad-did Alif Sani
  (1546-1624)" (p. 42, folio 22), head of the Naqshbandiya "school … in the ascendant at the court
  of Mughal Emperor Jahangir". **No archive row.**
- **Sarmad** — "their renowned poet-mystic Sarmad (named by Bulleh Shah in a kafi whom Aurangzeb had
  ordered executed in Delhi, was still fresh." (p. 42, folio 22) — the opening parenthesis is never
  closed in the printed text. **No archive row.**
- **Rumi** — the book's single largest comparison: "Like Rumi, who is doubtlessly the biggest
  influence, his erudition in the Quranic and mystical lore is extraordinary." (p. 37, folio 17);
  "The ecstatic example of Rumi and his torrent of extempore verse in three distinct metrical forms
  was obviously the model for Bulleh Shah." (p. 38, folio 18); "Rumi who calls upon the
  preceptor-beloved Shams4-Tabriz [OCR?] [Shams-i-Tabriz] in most of his lyrics in the 'Divan'"
  (p. 45, folio 25); "Rumi's worldview was moulded by the doctrines of Plotinus" (p. 44, folio 24).
- **The figures of Rumi's and Bulleh Shah's shared reference-range**, listed together at p. 37,
  folio 17: "the story of Yusuf and Zuleikha"; "Mansoor Hallaj, the mystic-martyr, executed in 922
  A.D."; "Prophet Moses and Mount Sinai"; "Christ and the cross"; "Bayazid, the famous mystic of
  Khurasan, hero of mystical stories, who died in 874 A.D."; "Junaid the chief of the sufis of

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Succession to a Qadiriya seat is described as a *gaddi*, and this is the chunk's only explicit
  succession mechanism.** "Shah Enayat of Lahore … was a successor to the Qadiriya 'gaddi' of Mian
  Mir" (p. 42, folio 22). No sajjada nashin, no Auqaf, no endowment and no revenue appear anywhere in
  this chunk — all (not stated).
- **Two orders contrasted by their economic and social practice — the most archive-usable sentence in
  the chunk.** "The Suhrawardiya tradition of patronage is diametrically opposed to that of the
  Qadiriya who practise asceticism and are given to assimilation of local cults." (p. 39, folio 19).
- **Multan as an institutional seat.** "Multan as the seat of the Suhrawardiya school of mysticism"
  (pp. 38–39, folios 18–19); "his reverential reference to Multan as a holy city and to Bahauddin
  Zakria, the founder of the Suhrawardiya school is frequent" (p. 39, folio 19).
- **The Naqshbandiya at the Mughal court, and the political consequence the book draws.** "The
  Naqshbandiya school was in the ascendant at the court of Mughal Emperor Jahangir" (p. 42, folio
  22); Sirhindi's letter condemning Guru Arjun; "Guru Arjun was tortured to death in Lahore on orders
  from Delhi." (p. 42, folio 22). Bulleh Shah's own position: "his disapproval of the Naqshabandiya
  verdict against the Shia sect" (p. 39, folio 19); "Bulleh Shah's intense distaste of the
  Naqshbandi polemic against the Shia Muslim sect comes through quite clearly in the kafts [OCR?]
  [kafis]" (p. 42, folio 22).
- **Cross-tradition pilgrimage: a Sufi sending disciples to a Sikh centre.** Sachal Sarmast
  "continued to send his disciples to Amritsar for homage." (p. 42, folio 22).
- **Wartime taxation and plunder as the economic background of the poetry.** "The Sikh marauders
  under Banda Bairagi (1670-1716) were to lay waste the whole of the province excluding Lahore and
  Qasur, imposing bone-crushing taxation .on the common peasant for the raising of armies on both
  sides." (p. 43, folio 23); "A later governor Zakriya Khan paid heavy tribute to Nadir Shah as he
  marched twice on Lahore. So extreme was the depredation caused by Nadir Shah's looting soldiers
  that on his second march he did not take the route via Qasur as the countryside was denuded of all
  resources." (p. 43, folio 23); "when plunder became routine in the Punjab, Bulleh Shah had no
  choice of factions." (p. 43, folio 23).
- **Oral transmission by qawwals, and textual corruption as its cost — the chunk's central
  transmission argument.** "A hundred and thirty years later there was no written evidence of his
  poetry, most of it preserved in the singing of the gawwals [OCR?] [qawwals] and minstrels of the
  countryside" (p. 48, folio 28); "The need felt throughout the hundred years of Bulleh Shah's
  printing to correct the textual corruption of the gawwal [OCR?] [qawwal] finds its culmination in
  Dr. Nazeer's brave effort to restore-prosodic health to the kafis." (p. 49, folio 29); "the kafi,
  'Bulleb noo samjbavan ayan' [OCR?] [Bulleh noo samjhavan ayan] was discovered by her directly from
  the gawwals [OCR?] [qawwals]." (p. 48, folio 28).
- **The printing history of Bulleh Shah, dated edition by edition** (p. 48, folio 28): "The first
  compiler of his works was a Sikh, Munshi Diwan Chand, whose collection was bought by a publisher in
  Gujranwala in 1886 although the first ever book of the kafis published was Kafian Hazrat Bulleb
  Shab [OCR?] [Bulleh Shah], 1882, in Lahore. This latter was a compilation of one Malik Hira which
  contained 47 kafis but was atrociously copied"; "The first considerable collection was that of
  Anwar Ali Rohtaki, Qanoon-e-Ishq containing 116 kafis, published in 1889. In 1896 Prem Singh Qasuri
  published 130 kafis; then an artisan of Lahore gchiq-saaz [OCR?], put together another collection
  in 1932. In 1933 a lady scholar, Dr. Lajwanti Ramakrishna did her doctoral thesis on the sufi poets
  of the Punjab which was published as Punjabi Sufi Poets".
- **The two modern editions the book relies on.** "The most authoritative compilation of Bulleh
  Shah's work was published in 1960 by Dr. Fagqir [OCR?] [Faqir] Mohammad from the Punjabi Adabi
  Academy Lahore… Dr. Faqir Mohammad's edition is today the only definitive volume of Bulleh Shah's
  poetry and luckily preserves a high standard of scholarly treatment and an impeccable kitabat."
  (p. 48, folio 28); "In 1976 Dr. Nazeer Ahmad published his selection Kalam Bulleb Shab [OCR?]
  [Kalam Bulleh Shah] (Packages Ltd., Lahore) based on the choices made by a group of poets and men
  of letters." (p. 49, folio 29).
- **The Granth as a preservation institution for non-Sikh poets.** "Guru Arjun (1563-1606) had
  already compiled the Granth, the holy book of the sect, which was in actual fact a compilation of
  'bbakti' [OCR?] [bhakti] poetry including such classics as Baba Farid, Kabir, Mira, Tulsi and
  Surdas." (p. 41, folio 21); "Farid is a poet of the Granth and has survived with the sacred book of
  the Sikh religion." (p. 47, folio 27).
- **Kafi as a musical and liturgical category, and Rafat's objection to the usage.** "Since the sufi
  poetry is composed for the minstrel it was natural for it to be absorbed by the elaborate system of

  incorrect." (p. 49, folio 29). On the form: "Bulleh Shah changed the structure of what is called
  the kafi beyond recognition… Kafi, therefore, is determined by its mood." (p. 49, folio 29). The

  is an account of the seven days of the week; sibarfi [OCR?] [siharfi] … is an acrostic on the
  alphabet" (p. 50, folio 30).
- **The teaching institution that carries Punjabi literature, named.** "essays written in what is
  purported to be get-through histories of Punjabi literature for the M.A. courses in Punjabi at
  Lahore's University of the Punjab." (p. 50, folio 30). And the commissioning of the standard work:
  "Commissioned by C.A. Woolner, the great scholar of the language who also worked on its
  lexicography, the pioneering essay on Bulleh Shah relied on written, oral and manuscript sources"
  (p. 51, folio 31).
- **Pilgrimage treated economically inside a translated poem.** "From a pilgrimage some have returned,
  / but any merit they may have earned, / despite humility in dress, / in percentage and shady deals
  is burned." (p. 83, folio 63).
- **Prayer and mosque observance attacked inside the poems.** "In prayer why abrade your forehead? /
  Away .with it, tear down the facade / of morality. Causing people pain / is the only sin you should
  dread." (p. 81, folio 61); "Prayer is body's gall. / One point settles it all." (p. 83, folio 63);
  "In a mosque no worshipper, / nor a temple-frequenter, / I'm not pure, nor impure" (p. 89, folio

- **The dowry-and-spinning cycle as a described rural institution.** "When she attains awareness she
  is taught to spin and is constantly rebuked when she neglects the spinning-wheel… After puberty, she
  is constantly reminded that she must spin more than an average output because whatever she spins
  would become a part of her dowry." (p. 46, folio 26).
- **Agrarian conflict inside a poem.** "What parents sowed, the children reap. / Who's the
  benefactor? Who the thief? / When the corn came in, the knives came out; / brother fought brother
  for every sheaf." (p. 79, folio 59).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **No miracle is claimed for any saint anywhere in this chunk.** The nearest things to hagiographic
  narrative are three items, all reported at second hand by the book: Guru Arjun getting Mian Mir "to
  lay the foundation stone of the Hariman-dir temple at Amritsar" (pp. 41–42, folios 21–22); Waris
  Shah and Bulleh Shah "said to have studied religion together … from the same teacher" (p. 38, folio
  18); and Sirhindi's "dislike of the Qadiriya saint Mian Mir … also said to have prompted him to
  speak to the king against him" (p. 42, folio 22).
- **The martyrdoms the book lists as Bulleh Shah's stock of reference**, attributed to Islamic
  mystical lore and not to any saint of the archive: "Mansoor Hallaj, the mystic-martyr, executed in
  922 A.D."; "the martyrdom of Husain as vindication of mystical truth" (p. 37, folio 17); Sarmad
  "whom Aurangzeb had ordered executed in Delhi" (p. 42, folio 22); "Guru Tegh Bahadur beheaded in
  Delhi in 1675" (p. 42, folio 22).
- **A voice from the sky, inside a poem.** "Uproar in Mecca when a voice came from the sky, not you,
  Bulleh, but someone else will die." (p. 63, folio 43).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The book's central polemic: Bulleh Shah is a Muslim Sufi, not a Hindu monist, and Lajwanti
  Ramakrishna is the named opponent.** "the poets, for all their colloquial self-abasement, retained
  the desire to be considered part of the Islamic sufi tradition. This partly demolishes the communal
  gloss so often placed on him by those, like Dr. Lajwanti: Ramakrishna, who claim him as a bbakta
  [OCR?] [bhakta] in the tradition of Hindu monism." (p. 38, folio 18). The evidence offered is
  quantitative and ironic: "in his most 'Hindu' Kafi Sayyo ni bun [OCR?] [hun] mein sajan payoni', he
  employs the largest number of Arabic phrases from the Quran." (p. 38, folio 18).
- **The Kannada vacana poets as Bulleh Shah's structural analogue — the argument the chunk opens
  on.** The Kannada movement broke "with the conventional polytheistic religion conveyed through
  Sanskirt [OCR?] [Sanskrit]", "took one God, Siva, for celebration in a local dialect", and "assumed
  an iconoclastic tone which subverted the orthodoxy… Both these characteristics are observable in
  Bulleh Shah." (p. 33, folio 13). But Rafat marks the difference too: "Kannada poets had gone to the
  local dialect and free verse in revolt against the formalism of Sanskrit and created a complex oral
  poetic. Bulleh Shah, a man of great erudition as we shall see, composed in sophisticated metrical
  form, created complex rhyme schemes as no other practitioner of Punjabi verse has done before and
  since his times." (p. 36, folio 16).
- **The first-line refrain identified as the technique that produces the "oral effect".** "he
  succeeds in getting across a rough oral effect, as if the poems he composed were a spontaneous
  everyday expression. He achieves this effect… mainly through the device of first line refrain. Most
  of his 'kafis' contain refrains which are meticulously non-poetic and are taken from common
  conversation." (p. 36, folio 16); "First he puts down a most non-poetic utterance, like Mullab
  [OCR?] [Mullah] meinoon marda ee', then proceeds to add to it a poem which is an elaborate
  structure technically" (p. 37, folio 17); "His famous kafi 'Ki janan mein koan' has attained the
  status of a proverb." (pp. 36–37, folios 16–17).
- **Sufi doctrine traced to Greek and to Indian sources, with Advaita given priority.** "Islamic sufis
  absorbed their doctrines from Greek sources and these doctrines predated the Greeks in the Indian
  philosophy of Advaita." (pp. 43–44, folios 23–24); "the basic philosophy of Plotinus is detectable:
  the achievement of perfect union with God; there is absolute unity in existence which cannot be
  expressed." (p. 44, folio 24); "The movement of bhakti, which was a revolt against later Sanskrit
  domination, arose from this internal Hindu source" (p. 44, folio 24).
- **The Sikh question handled with deliberate hedging.** "Some scholars connect such references in the
  kafts [OCR?] [kafis] to the maraudin [OCR?] [marauding] activities of the Sikhs although the poet
  does not name either the gurus or their followers." (p. 41, folio 21); "A line, 'bborian walley
  rajay boay' [OCR?] [bhorian walley rajay hoay] is frequently taken to refer to the Sikhs but it may
  merely point to the Pathan rulers of Qasur" (p. 41, folio 21); "It may have been politic for Bulleh
  Shah not to point to the 'bbakti' [OCR?] [bhakti] element in the Sikh movement" (p. 41, folio 21);
  "a reference to 'satt' guru in a kafi is in a positive sense" (p. 41, folio 21).
- **A stated reason for the Qadiriya–Sikh rapprochement, used to explain Bulleh Shah's silence.**
  "Shah Enayat … could not have inclined to an overt opposition to. the spiritual traditions of the
  Sikhs. Indeed the relationship existing between the two bbakti [OCR?] [bhakti] traditions was
  carried on by such distinct adherents as Sachal Sarmast in Sindh" (p. 42, folio 22).
- **The Shia–Sunni question.** "He refers to Husain and Yazid once as being symbolic of the conflict
  between good and evil but considers it in the monistic sense, thus avoiding a clear-cut
  identification of the sectarian conflict within the South Asian Muslim community after the eclectic
  reign of the Mughal Emperor Akbar. His scorn of the Shia-Sunni politics is, however, frequently and
  most forcefully expressed." (p. 39, folio 19).
- **A blunt cultural judgement about the Punjab's Muslims, offered as the explanation for textual
  loss.** "A lack of intellectual tradition among the Muslims of the Punjab accounts for this
  phillistine [OCR?] [philistine] indifference to the classics. Bulleh Shah was treated no
  differently." (p. 47, folio 27); "In terms of written sources, the Punjab and indeed the whole of
  the Indus basin, has always seemed a wasteland to scholars." (pp. 46–47, folios 26–27).
- **The state of Punjabi letters, and the verdict on every critic named.** "Punjabi literature in
  Pakistan is in a state of neglect. The language has become a purely spoken medium and the Punjabis
  are no longer able to decipher it in the written form." (p. 50, folio 30). Najam Husain Syed's work
  "is non-methodical and he gives the impression of a poet taking time out to write prose" (p. 50,
  folio 30); Dr. Tirlochan Singh's is "introductory and rudimentary" (p. 50, folio 30); Professor
  Sarfaraz Husain Qazi's and Hamidullah Shah Hashmi's "are cramming courses for students" (p. 50,
  folio 30); Arif Abdul Mateen "remains superficial … handicapped by a verbosity he has imbibed from
  Urdu" (pp. 50–51, folios 30–31); "The celebrated East Punjab journal Punjab: Past and Present has
  not featured an article on Bulleh Shah over a period of 20 years." (p. 51, folio 31). The one
  exception: "The only living critic of Punjabi literature who is clearly worthy of a definitive
  assessment of Bulleh Shah is Syed Ali Abbas Jalalpuri" (p. 51, folio 31).
- **Ramakrishna defended against her detractors, with one reservation.** "the 1938 Book of Dr.
  Lajwanti Ramakrishnan Punjabi Sufi Poets, remains the standard assessment of Bulleh Shah although
  the frog-chorus of later critics does not tire of referring to her insultingly"; her essay "remains
  the best investigation to-date on the Qadiriya background of the poet. Apart from her claim about
  the progression of Bulleh Shah to an exclusively Hindu monistic inspiration, Dr. Ramakrishnan's
  grasp of the basic characteristics of the poet was sound." (p. 51, folio 31).
- **A world-historical framing of the poet's lifetime.** "Yet the world was not passing through a dark
  age when Bulleh Shah lived and sang in the inferno of Qasur. The year he was born, England was well
  on its way to a strictly parliamentatary [OCR?] [parliamentary] rule, the Habeas Corpus Act had
  been passed and Isaac Newton had led the world by the nose into the modern scientific age. He was
  nine when the English factory at Calcutta was established and was still a teen-ager when Swift and
  Carlyle were writing their works of social criticism" (pp. 47–48, folios 27–28).
