## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`** (the book's spelling throughout is **"Bulleh Shah"**, with the poetic
  signature given as "Bullha"): **a birthplace and a descent claim, both in note 1, and neither
  appears anywhere else in the chunk.** "Bulleh Shah is said to be a descendant of the founder and
  was born in Uch Gilanian." (p. 235, folio not stated). "The founder" is the antecedent of the
  preceding sentence: "the founder saint, Abdul Qadir Gilani" (p. 235, folio not stated). **The book
  hedges it — "is said to be" — and gives no birth year, no source for the claim beyond the
  hagiographic tradition it goes on to name, and no tomb, death date or place of death anywhere in
  this chunk** — all (not stated). Rafat's own gloss on the claim is sceptical in effect: "The
  hagiographer of Bulleh Shah has, thus, an immaculate background against which to present him to
  posterity." (p. 235, folio not stated).
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **the name explained, and a warning about spurious quotation.**
  "'Bulleh Shah' is derived from Abdullah Shah which is non-declinable; from Bulleh it becomes
  Bullah, and Bullbya [OCR?] [Bullhya], representing dative, genitive and vocative cases." (p. 240,
  folio 220). Preceding it: "Bulleh Shah is the most frequendy [OCR?] [frequently] quoted poet in
  Punjabi conversation… his lines are essentially non-poetic in terms of rhythm. A non-thythmical
  [OCR?] [non-rhythmical] exclamatory quality, with a vocative usage of the proper noun 'Bullha',
  makes for precise assimilation in common speech." (pp. 239–240, folios 219–220). **And the
  forgery warning:** "Very often people pretend to quote Bulleh Shah with a vocative of his name but
  the lines are their own, not Bulleh Shah's. For instance, Bullbya, bor wi neenva bo [OCR?]
  [Bullhya, hor wi neenva ho] is purely popular fabrication and is totally non-poetic." (p. 240,
  folio 220).
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **the signature line the book treats as his most famous.** "His
  self-reference in Bullbya ki janan mein kaon [OCR?] [Bullhya ki janan mein kaon] is proverbial in
  usage." (p. 242, folio 222); the same poem is named again at p. 243, folio 223 as "Bulleh Shah's
  famous poem Bullbia ki janan mein kaon [OCR?] [Bullhia ki janan mein kaon]", and its English text
  is printed in full there from Ramakrishna's translation, opening "Bullha, what do I know who I
  am?" and closing "Bullha, who is the true master?" (p. 243, folio 223).
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **the corpus is dated to a hostile moment for his order.** "Sheikh
  Ahmad's revival of the sharia took firm hold in the reign of Aurangzeb and became the yardstick of
  orthodox faith. The times of Shah Enayat and Bulleh Shah were therefore hard times for the
  Qadiriya order." (p. 241, folio 221).
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **a stated gap in the scholarship, based on the author's own count.**
  Note 26: "Based on an inquiry at libraries. The author has a file of the journal from 1965 to 1975
  which does not contain a single article on Bulleh Shah." (p. 244, folio 224). **The journal is not
  named in this chunk** — (not stated).
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **five poems printed in this chunk, each headed by its folio then its
  title in capitals.** `SATURDAY` (p. 223, folio 203), `THURSDAY` (p. 225, folio 205), `SPRING`
  (p. 227, folio 207), `RAIN` (p. 229, folio 209), `KNOTS` (pp. 231 and 233, folios 211 and 213).
  All five are confirmed by the book's own index at pp. 261 and 263 — see Practices below.
- **`shrine-of-shah-inayat-qadiri`** (the book's spelling is **"Shah Enayat"** throughout, never
  "Shah Inayat" and never "Qadiri" as part of his name): **the discriminating facts this chunk
  gives, all three from the notes — see Doubts before using any of them.** (i) **Order:** "The times
  of Shah Enayat and Bulleh Shah were therefore hard times for the Qadiriya order." (p. 241, folio
  221), the sentence closing a note whose whole subject is the Naqshbandi campaign "against the
  Qadiriya order" in Lahore (p. 241, folio 221). (ii) **A written work of his own:** "Dr. Lajwanti
  examines in detail Shah Enayat's publication Dastur-ul-Amal." (p. 241, folio 221). (iii) **A
  doctrine attributed to him:** "The saint believed in the Hindu origin of Islamic mysticism: 'this
  knowledge, he believed, was carried by the Greek soldiers of Alexander the Great of Greece, from
  where it was borrowed by the mystics of Islam.'" (p. 241, folio 221), the inner quotation from
  Ramakrishna, *Punjabi Sufi Poets* (Oxford 1938) P. 45. **No death year, no tomb, no location and
  no place of burial is given for him anywhere in this chunk** — all (not stated).
- **`shrine-of-shah-inayat-qadiri`**: **the relationship, stated as a contrast with Shah Husain.**
  "He also notes that Shah Husain makes no reference to his beloved Madho Lal in his kafis, On the
  other hand, Bulleh Shah's invocation of Shah Enayat is constant." (p. 242, folio 222), reporting
  Nazir Ahmad's introduction to *Kalam Shab Husain* [OCR?] [*Kalam Shah Husain*].
- **`madho-lal-hussain`** (the book spells him **"Shah Husain"**, and names **Madho Lal**

  Poetry (Majlis Shah Husain, Lahore)." (p. 244, folio 224) — **the imprint is "Majlis Shah Husain",
  a body named for him; the chunk says nothing more about it** (not stated).
- **`madho-lal-hussain`**: **a modern edition of his verse, with publisher and year — an exact

- **`shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar`** (the book calls him **"Baba Farid"**): **dates, textual
  transmission and canonical standing, all in note 18.** "Asaf Khan has put together in this book
  the latest research on the provenance of the poetry of Baba Farid (1173-1265). Guru Nanak
  (1469-1538) collected the great first poet's work from his heirs and wrote it down in his

- **`shrine-of-mian-mir`** (the book calls him **"Mian Mir"**): **an origin, a death year, a

  Qadiriya order in Lahore was Mian Mir (d. 1635) who had come from Sehwan Sharif in Sind. Ascetic

  counted among his disciples Prince Dara Shikoh whose wife lies buried near his mausoleum."

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- **Abdul Qadir Gilani / Abd-al-Qadir al-Jilani, founder of the Qadiriya** — named in two spellings
  in adjacent notes. Note 1: "the founder saint, Abdul Qadir Gilani" whose relics are "beads,
  scissors and cap" (p. 235, folio not stated). Note 2, quoting Memon: "The oldest of Sufi orders,
  the Qadiriya, derived its name from the Hanbalite Abd-al-Qadir al-Jilani (D. 561/1166) a native of
  the district of Jilan south of the Caspian. Al-Jilani studied at Baghdad where he later took up
  public preaching in 521/1127." (pp. 235–236, folio not stated / 216). "It seems, strangely enough,
  that al-Jilani himself had no intention of founding an order. The order that arose after him in
  his name was largely the work of his sons." (p. 236, folio 216).
- **Sheikh Ahmad Majaddid Alif Sani, the Naqshbandi chief** — the chunk's most developed
  non-archive figure. "The Naqshbandi order was brought to India from Samarkand in Central Asia. In
  India, its highest exponent was Sheikh Ahmad Majaddid Alif Sani (1564-1624). The order was opposed
  to the sufis and nursed a particular antipathy towards the Shia community. Sheikh Ahmad's treatise
  Rad-e-Rawafiz adjudged them liable to death by execution." (p. 240, folio 220). "Emperor Jahangir,
  whose wife had a Shia background, did not take kindly to Sheikh Ahmad, wrote extremely insultingly
  about him in the Tuzuk, and finally put him in prison." (p. 240, folio 220).
- **Guru Arjun (1553-1606)** — "Guru Arjun (1553-1606) put together the first copy of the Holy
  Granth out of these notebooks" (p. 242, folio 222); and in note 10, Jahangir "was suspicious of
  Guru Arjun who had made a show of support of the King's rebellious son, Khusrau, When Guru Arjun
  died Sheikh Ahmad was jubilant over the death of 'this accursed heathen', as Ikram quotes from a
  letter of his." (p. 240, folio 220). **Held here rather than mapped: three archive rows name Guru

- **Guru Nanak (1469-1538)** — "Guru Nanak (1469-1538) collected the great first poet's work from

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Custodianship and relics at Uch Gilanian — the single most archive-usable sentence in the
  chunk.** "The lineal saint who ascends the gaddi of the Qadiris possesses such relics of the
  founder saint, Abdul Qadir Gilani, as beads, scissors and cap." (p. 235, folio not stated). It is
  immediately generalised: "Almost all Qadiri saints and su/fis [OCR?] [sufis] in India claimed a
  physical or spiritual link with Uch Sharif, be their actual position as far away as Bengal or
  Bihar." (p. 235, folio not stated). **Succession (gaddi), lineal descent and physical relics are
  all named in two sentences; no custodian, family or date is named** — (not stated).
- **Qadiriya and Naqshbandi as rival institutions under state patronage, with a named fiscal
  instrument.** "Naqshbandi order was opposed to the Qadiriya saints and recommended persecution of
  the non-Muslim subjects in the levy of a special punitive tax called jazia." (p. 240, folio 220).
  The mechanism is given as court politics: "It was, in fact, the Turani-Irani struggle taking place
  at the Mughal court." (p. 240, folio 220). "Jahangir, who kept the Sheikh in his camp after
  granting him release from prison, seems to have heeded his advice against the Qadiris. " (p. 241,
  folio 221). And the Naqshbandi chief is given high administrative contacts: "He also indicates his
  high4evel [OCR?] [high-level] contacts with Qalich Beg the governor of Lahore in 1602." (p. 240,
  folio 220).
- **The Qadiriya's character in India, as Rafat characterises it.** "In India, the Qadiriya order
  were late in coming and represent the most freewheeling of the mystical orders in terms of
  ritual." (p. 236, folio 216). And: "By far the most peaceful and moderate, the Qadiriya gradually
  became the most widespread Sufi order and the image of al-Jilani, in glorifying which his
  spiritual disciples had strained all the resources of their minds, came to supplant the image of
  the prophet of Islam." (p. 236, folio 216, quoting Memon).
- **Qawwals as textual agents — a transmission claim with direct bearing on shrine repertoire.**
  "self-reference in Shah Husain was frequently an interpolation of the qawwals which upset the
  prosodic balance of the poem" (p. 242, folio 222). **The qawwals are named here as corrupters of
  the text, not as performers of it.**
- **Exorcism and the figure of the guide, attacked inside a poem.** `THURSDAY`: "Think of the
  simpletons / who go to be exorcised / by devilish priests of demons, / when all they need is a
  guide / to set them free." (p. 225, folio 205). **This is the chunk's only poem-internal statement
  about shrine or ritual specialists.**
- **The murshid as the agent of fulfilment, inside a poem.** `RAIN`: "Friends hurry to my side /
  with 'Lucky, lucky you.' / My mentor, my guide / made my dreams come true." (p. 229, folio 209).
- **Wedding economy: forty knots, the dowry, the market and the merchants.** `KNOTS`: "How many
  knots shall | [OCR?] [I] tie, / dear, tell me true? / Forty days to the wedding, / so forty will
  do." (p. 231, folio 211); "The first knot | [OCR?] [I] opened, and / grumbled and cried, / should
  the dresses be sequinned, / or should they be dyed? / | [OCR?] [I] went off to the market with /
  an empty purse, / and the merchants supplied me / a push and a curse." (p. 231, folio 211). The
  procession and the transfer to the husband's house: "The loud procession is here, / this is the
  day / when the ready and the unready will / be taken away, / to be with the master and / sit by
  his side" (p. 231, folio 211), continuing "remembering nothing at all but. / she's his bride. /
  Now the last rites.are [OCR?] [rites are] ended" (p. 233, folio 213).
- **Holi named as a festival inside a poem.** `SPRING`: "The girls are rapt in Holi / while | [OCR?]
  [I] weep solely" and "Holi! Holi!" (p. 227, folio 207). The glossary independently carries the
  gloss "A Hindu festival" (p. 254, folio 234) — but see Doubts: it cannot be attached to its
  headword.

  234), "Transmigration of souls" (p. 247, folio not stated), "A Path; sect" (p. 248, folio 228),
  "A good believer" (p. 251, folio 231), "Depths of Hell" (p. 254, folio 234); **revenue, trade and
  credit** — "A revenue officer" (p. 253, folio 233), "Principal of a debt; at all; completely"

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The chunk's central controversy: whether Bulleh Shah's thought is Hindu (advaita) or Islamic.**
  Rafat sets out Ramakrishna's position at length and calls it "The bone of contention": "'The third
  and last phase of Bullha's mystic life was unique. Here he resembles no sufi or vaisnava of the
  Punjab or the rest of India. During this time he is a firm believer in advaita and sees that
  all-pervading spirit, God, in all and independently of all religions.'" (pp. 242–243, folios
  222–223). And her stronger claim: "'We have stated that the pantheism of Bulleh Shah was Hindu in
  its entirety and therefore differed a good deal from the pantheism of the sufis. His advaita,
  which was Indian in its essence, had so overpowered him, nay had transformed him in such a way
  that any sort of conversion was beyond his understanding.'" (p. 243, folio 223).
- **Rafat's counter-move is evidential, not assertive: he prints the Rumi lyric beside it.** "At
  this juncture it would be in order to present the lyric of Rumi on which Bulleh Shah had based his
  kafi." (p. 243, folio 223) — then sets Ramakrishna's translation of "Bullha, what do I know who I
  am?" directly against Nicholson's Rumi, "What is to be done, O Muslims? for I do not recognise
  myself. I am neither Christian nor Jew nor Parsi nor Muslim." (p. 243, folio 223). **The argument
  is made by juxtaposition; Rafat does not state the conclusion in this chunk.**
- **He also names an ally.** "Dr. Tirlochan Singh in his article Bulleb Shab da Tassavuf [OCR?]
  [Bulleh Shah da Tassavuf] in the said compilation concentrates on the Islamic derivation of Bulleh
  Shah's mysticism in a conscious antithesis to Dr. Ramakrishna's opinion." (p. 244, folio 224).
- **A sustained attack on Nazir Ahmad's scholarship, quoting him against himself.** "Dr. Nazir,
  otherwise of moderate opinion, is unfortunately guilty of the most unscholarly comment on Dr.
  Lajwanti Ramakrishnan when he says: 'This home-broken dame must be serving up pilao of peas,
  tikkas of cheese and balls of saag but knows little about poetry.'" (p. 244, folio 224). Rafat's
  two specific charges: Nazir "accuses him of positing an immoral proposition, showing no
  discernment of the device of transferrence of poetic persona", over the line "If I state an
  untruth something remains (is saved); by telling the truth the fire spreads" from the kafi "Mub ai
  baat na rabindi bai [OCR?] [Muh ai baat na rahindi hai]"; and "his dismissal, as a mystical
  commonplace, of the metaphor of the spinning-wheel, central to Bulleh Shah's poetry, is tantamount
  to tossing the baby away with the bath-water." (p. 244, folio 224).
- **A source-critical judgement on Ikram.** "Ikram's treatment is defensive because of the rise in
  Sheikh Ahmad's status in the wake of Hindu-Muslim differences in India." (p. 240, folio 220).
- **The Dravidian-origin thesis for Punjabi, reported and weighed.** Faridkoti "is now considered the
  leader of critics opposing the British orientalist view that Punjabi is a Sanskrit off-shoot. He
  is at pains to show that Punjabi is a Dravidian language." (p. 239, folio 219). Rafat's verdict:
  "A more in-depth study is, however, needed to overthrow the orientalist conclusions; yet, the
  Dravidian view is gaining ground among Punjabi scholars also as a kind of local chauvinistic
  reaction to the ancient external trespass of the Aryans. For Muslim scholars the view is
  attractive because Aryan civilisation was subsumed by Hinduism and now represents its caste-ridden
  social system." (p. 239, folio 219).
- **On the standard dialect, and on Grierson and Bailey.** Asaf Khan "accepts the Manjhi dialect of
  the Central Punjab as the standard Punjabi language" (p. 236, folio 216). Of Grierson and Bailey:
  "Both these works remain basic, internationally recognised, sources… Punjabi writers disagree with
  these conclusions but they neither have international authority nor any sizeable research on the
  subject to effectively counter them." (pp. 238–239, folios 218–219).
- **Bulleh Shah's indifference to sin and virtue, illustrated by Rafat's own translation.** Note 15:
  "For instance, these lines which demonstrate the mystic's indifference to the idea of sin'and
  [OCR?] [sin and] virtue: The Lord is everywhere/and always perfect/wbat [OCR?] [what] does He
  care/for man's sin or/the righteousness of man?" (p. 242, folio 222).
