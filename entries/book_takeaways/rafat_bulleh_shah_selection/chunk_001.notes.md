## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`** (the book spells him **"Bulleh Shah"** throughout, and gives his given
  name as **"Abdullah Shah"**): **the tomb and its town, named directly.** "The tomb of Bulleh Shah
  in Qasur and the area around it is today the only place free of collective refuse and the
  privileged of the city pay handsomely to be buried in the proximity of the man they had once
  rejected." (p. 21, folio not stated). The town is spelled **"Qasur"** by Khaled Ahmad (pp. 21, 22,
  27) and **"Kasur"** by Rafat (p. 16, folio xvi) — see Doubts.
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **the burial refusal, which is the Introduction's opening image.** The
  afternoon sun "exacted penance from the barefoot votaries for a distant deed of injustice to the
  poet whose dead body had lain out in the sun in 1758 as the mullahs disputed his right to be
  buried in a communal grave-yard. Today the same cemetery, where orthodoxy had refused him
  burial-ground, is mostly in ruins, encroached upon by a fast-growing town whose hard-pressed
  denizens defecate on its collapsed graves." (p. 21, folio not stated). **No custodian, family or
  sajjada nashin is named anywhere in the chunk** — (not stated).
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **the fabric of the shrine as the author saw it.** A "concrete
  courtyard of the mausoleum" (p. 21, folio not stated); "Bulleh Shah lies serenely inside the
  dilapidated building of his tomb, its broken stone filigree-vork [OCR?] [filigree-work]
  proclaiming his indifference to the well-heeled buried around him." (p. 21, folio not stated);
  and, of the rich men's graves around it, "stone canopies adorned with mosaic paintings of low
  taste, of the sort seen on the back of trucks" (p. 21, folio not stated). **No building date,
  patron, dimension or plan is given** — (not stated).
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **the Auqaf Department, named as the authority inside the tomb.**
  "Inside the tomb, the Auqaf Department has allowed mediocre Urdu verse composed by the poetasters
  of Qasur to festoon the walls in colourful banners. A local grandee has contributed a framed
  couplet of execrable taste in which his name appears more prominently than the verse he has
  concocted. Bulleh Shah's own poetry is conspicuously absent in this ambience of bourgeois
  profanity." (p. 22, folio not stated). This is the chunk's **only statement of shrine
  administration**, and it is a criticism, not a description of office-holders.
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **qawwali at the grave, with the line they sing.** "The qawwals
  constantly sing his rebuking lines: 'Tamba chook-chook mandi javain', Their feet burning, the
  votaries run toward the shade of the graves of the city's rich" (p. 21, folio not stated); the
  rich men's "gaudy presence lends an edge to the lines shouted by a hoarse group of qawwals"
  (p. 21, folio not stated). **No ʿurs, mela, date or visitor number is given** — (not stated).
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **the interior, in the author's closing image.** "Lying under layers
  of tinsel and flower, the poet communes with the cats that sleep leaning against his grave on the
  cool floor." (p. 22, folio not stated).
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **birth, dates and age, all from the Introduction.** The section
  heading prints "Bulleh Shah 1680-1758" (p. 21, folio not stated). "Abdullah Shah was born in 1680
  A.D. in Uch Gilanian,'the city of Saints in Bahawalpur, in the family of a pious Syed." (p. 22,
  folio not stated). "When the last great Mughal ruler Aurangzeb died in 1707 Bulleh Shah was 27
  years old" (p. 26, folio not stated). "As Bulleh Shah lay dying in 1758 at the age of 78, the rape
  of the Punjab was proceeding apace." (p. 28, folio not stated). And 1746 is "twelve years before
  Bulleh Shah's death" (p. 27, folio not stated). **The four figures are internally consistent on
  1680–1758; the archive row carries 1757 — see Doubts.**
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **childhood move to Qasur, and the teacher.** "His father moved to a
  village of Qasur, a district of Lahore division today, where the six-year old Abdullah received a
  strictly orthodox religious training. No one knows how the boy made his inner journey from
  received knowledge to an intensely felt heterodox devotional religion but hagiographic 'tazkéras'
  [OCR?] [tazkiras] narrate that his teacher Khawaja Hafiz Ghulam Murtaza Qasuri trained two
  rebellious personalities; the other was Waris Shah" (p. 22, folio not stated).
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **no order (silsila) is claimed for him.** The only order named in the
  chunk is "the Qadiriya School of Muslim mysticism in India", and it is attached to **Shah Enayat**,
  not to Bulleh Shah (p. 22, folio not stated). What the book says of Bulleh Shah is only that "His
  allegiance to Shah Enayat was his only concession to form which also provided him a mooring in the
  Islamic tradition. In nothing else does he recognise discipline" (p. 24, folio not stated). **Do
  not read the archive row's "Qadri" out of this chunk** — see Doubts.
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **the poetic sources the book assigns him.** "There is no doubt that
  the earliest heterodox tradition he imbibed was that of the Persian mystic poet Rumi (1207-1273
  A.D.). His most rebellious kafi, and the most popular, 'Ki janan mein kaun', is almost a literal
  translation of a ghazal from Diwan-i-Shams Tabriz." (p. 22, folio not stated). Also "'Teray Ishq
  nachaya' perhaps the most popular of his Kafis today" (p. 24, folio not stated).
- **`shrine-of-shah-inayat-qadiri`** (the Introduction calls him **"Shah Enayat"**; Rafat calls him
  **"Inayat Shah"** — see Doubts before using any of this): **the master, with the two facts that
  discriminate this row from the Sindh row.** "Shah Enayat, whose life spanned the Mughal rule from
  Aurangzeb to Mohammad Shah, belonged to the Qadiriya School of Muslim mysticism in India. His
  scholarship of Arabic and Persian was overlaid with an acceptance of the mystical traditions of
  the people of the subcontinent. This openness to local devotional cults was ironical since the
  creed of the Hanbali teacher Sheik Abdul Qadir of Baghdad," the founder of the School, was
  originally opposed to innovative adjustments of this sort." (p. 22, folio not stated). The second
  discriminator is place: "Hagiographers tell of a dramatic encounter between the two in Lahore"
  (p. 22, folio not stated). **No tomb, burial place, death year or Mozang is given for him
  anywhere in the chunk** — (not stated).
- **`shrine-of-shah-inayat-qadiri`**: **the master's practice and his objection to his disciple.**
  "The saint Shah Enayat, who practised bagigat [OCR?] [haqiqat] (direct knowledge) under the
  careful camouflage of sophisticated Persian metaphor, was dismayed by the rough vernacular
  expression of his disciple and advised him to desist from railing against orthodox postures upheld
  by the political might of the Mughal court. Bulleh Shah did not listen" (p. 23, folio not stated).
  After the quoted kafi: "The last couplet, not translated, emphasised his allegiance to the baqiqi
  [OCR?] [haqiqi] aspects of Shah Enayat, while the rest of the poem obstinately resisted the advice
  that he should pay homage to the Syed paramountcy in religion." (p. 23, folio not stated).
- **`shrine-of-shah-inayat-qadiri`**: **the expulsion and the samaʿ that followed it.** "It is said
  that Shah Enayat turned him out of his house after this. Aware of the Qadiriya custom of Sama and
  devotional dance, Bulleh Shah took to the sarangi, the stringed instrument associated with the
  wandering dervish, and began to sing and dance." (p. 24, folio not stated). "'Teray Ishq nachaya'

- **`shrine-of-shah-inayat-qadiri`**: **the reunion, with the pun the book turns it on.** "Seeing

  Bullab? [OCR?] [Bullah]' and got the answer: 'Not Bullab [OCR?] [Bullah] but Bbulla [OCR?]
  [Bhulla]'. Bhulla meant lost." (p. 24, folio not stated).
- **`shrine-of-shah-inayat-qadiri`**: **Rafat's independent naming of the same man, under a
  reversed name.** "Bulleh Shah's frequent references to his mentor and guide Inayat Shah can,

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Burial-for-payment around the saint's grave — the chunk's sharpest economic statement.** "the
  privileged of the city pay handsomely to be buried in the proximity of the man they had once
  rejected" (p. 21, folio not stated). No sum, agency or period is given — (not stated).
- **The Auqaf Department as the permitting authority inside the tomb**, allowing banners of "mediocre
  Urdu verse composed by the poetasters of Qasur" and a grandee's framed couplet (p. 22, folio not
  stated). **This is the only named institution at the shrine in the chunk.**
- **Qawwals as resident performers at the grave**, singing "'Tamba chook-chook mandi javain'" and
  shouting lines at the rich men's canopies (p. 21, folio not stated). Elsewhere the qawwal is
  treated as a textual agent: "What is today often put down as corruption wrought by the qawwal may
  be the unfolding of an oral poetic in the kafis." (p. 32, folio 12).
- **Barefoot votaries and the physical conditions of ziyarat.** "The afternoon sun beat down on the
  concrete courtyard of the mausoleum. It exacted penance from the barefoot votaries" (p. 21, folio
  not stated); "Their feet burning, the votaries run toward the shade of the graves of the city's
  rich" (p. 21, folio not stated).
- **The graveyard's condition and urban encroachment.** "the same cemetery… is mostly in ruins,
  encroached upon by a fast-growing town whose hard-pressed denizens defecate on its collapsed
  graves" (p. 21, folio not stated).
- **Samaʿ named as an order's custom, with its instrument.** "Aware of the Qadiriya custom of Sama
  and devotional dance, Bulleh Shah took to the sarangi, the stringed instrument associated with the
  wandering dervish, and began to sing and dance." (p. 24, folio not stated).
- **The itinerant singer-saint as teacher.** "it must be remembered that the travelling singer saint
  was also revered as a teacher. He was constantly exhorting his listeners to avoid certain pitfalls,
  and practice certain virtues." (p. 17, folio "es" [OCR?] [xvii]).
- **Orthodox schooling as the shrine-figure's formation.** "the six-year old Abdullah received a
  strictly orthodox religious training" under Khawaja Hafiz Ghulam Murtaza Qasuri (p. 22, folio not
  stated).
- **Taxation and extortion in the Punjab of Bulleh Shah's lifetime.** The Qasur rebellion "brought
  great hardship on the people who were repeatedly required to cough up taxes to finance the
  internecine armies" (p. 27, folio not stated); Nadir Shah in 1738 "spared Lahore but exacted a
  heavy ransom" (p. 27, folio not stated); "Marauding Sikh bands formed themselves into robber
  baronies and imposed their own system of extortion on the Punjab farmer." (p. 27, folio not
  stated). Abdali's conquest "delivered the various doabs into the hands of the Sikh confederacy
  called the misis [OCR?] [misls]" (p. 27, folio not stated).
- **The knot-string calendar, a living village custom Rafat describes at length.** "It refers to a
  custom still prevalent in some remote villages where the illiterate folk having no calendars or
  diaries tie knots on a string or a piece of cloth to remind them of a forth-coming event. Suppose a
  wedding has been fixed for thirty days later; thirty knots are tied on a string; each morning one
  knot is untied so that the simple people know how many days remain." (p. 15, folio xv). He names
  the surrounding complex as "a whole tradition of weddings, fairs, funerals, circumcisions, rituals
  and superstitions" (p. 15, folio xv).
- **The ferry crossing as ordinary experience.** "In an era when very few bridges existed what
  peasant had not waited hours for the pattan or ferry?" (p. 16, folio xvi).
- **The dowry and the spinning-wheel read as preparation for death.** "the meticulous advice to a
  girl to prepare her dowry is in fact advice to prepare for death" (p. 15, folio xv); "The symbol of
  the charkba [OCR?] [charkha] or spinning-wheel is one his audience could immediately identify…
  for the message in each case was the same—make ready for the Bridegroom Death, the day of
  departure is at hand." (p. 17, folio "es" [OCR?] [xvii]). Also the objects Rafat says resist
  translation: "the spinning-wheel, the begging-bowl, and the blanket" (p. 15, folio xv).
- **The sufi reading key Rafat supplies for the whole book.** "Love in sufi poetry refers to the love
  of or for God, or the mentor, or desire for absorption in Nature, it never means love for a woman;
  and the woman or Beloved in the poems is simply the object of spiritual adoration… The Bridegroom
  in the poems is Death." (p. 15, folio xv).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Khaled Ahmad against the "progressive" reading of Bulleh Shah.** "Some authors begin their
  discussion of his poetry with what they consider Bulleh Shah's protest against contemporary
  political and social injustice, an idiosynerasy [OCR?] [idiosyncrasy] which allows a progressive
  label to be pasted on to him. The truth of the matter is that Bulleh Shah never directly criticised
  the rulers and whatever reference to the contemporary situation occurs in his poetry is random and
  oblique." (p. 26, folio not stated). What he concedes: "he appears to be deeply bruised by the
  upheavals taking place in the Punjab and was depressed by the sufferings of the common man; indeed,
  it is certain that the situation contributed greatly to his gloomy world-view." (p. 26, folio not
  stated).
- **The political chronology is deployed as causal argument.** "The period of Muhammad Shah coincided
  with the coming to maturity of Bulleh Shah and both together coincided with the beginning of the
  misfortunes of the Punjab. The decay of Mughal administration gave rise to small local tyrannies
  which lasted into the 19th century and formed the character of the inhabitants of the region. The
  crisis of character in the Punjabi of today is traceable to this period of duress in the history of
  his land." (pp. 26–27, folios not stated). One poem is tied to one event: "Bulleh Shah makes a
  forlorn reference to this state of affairs in his 'Hun a mil yar ptyariya' [OCR?] [piyariya]"
  (p. 27, folio not stated).
- **Against Lajwanti's Advaita thesis, and against its Muslim mirror-image.** "Dr. Lajwanti began
  this most self-defeating and colourless exercise by categorising the various phases in his poetry
  as Islamic sufism, Bhakti Vasnaivism [OCR?] [Vaisnavism] and triumphantly discovered the apex of
  his poetic genius in the achievement of monistic advaita… She claimed that in 'Ki janan mein kon'
  Bulleh Shah finally broke with his Islamic background and merged with the more immanent Hindu
  philosophy of Advaita, unaware that the kafis was literally inspired by Rumi's ghazal… Muslim
  authors, their hackles up, just as assiduously pushed him back into the Islamic fold by pasting on
  him the exclusive label of Ibn Arabi's 'Wabdat-ul-Wujud' [OCR?] [Wahdat-ul-Wujud]. But beyond and
  above this communal humbug, Bulleh Shah seems to incorporate and transcend all the contemporary
  influences till only a voice remains" (p. 28, folio not stated).
- **Bhakti defended against a Pakistani orthodoxy, and redefined as Dravidian.** "The tradition of
  bhakti in Indian poetry has been condemned by orthodox Pakistani writers as a Hindu inroad into the
  pure Islamic sufi tradition. This criticism is more political than scientific. Bhakti was in fact a
  Dravidian revolt against the Aryan sacerdotalism and philosophy." (p. 30, folio 10).
- **The Dravidian thesis about Punjabi itself.** "British scholars like Grierson and Bailey had
  categorised Punjabi as a Prakrit off-shoot of Sanskrit… After a century of acceptance, Punjabi
  researchers led by Ain-ul-Haq Faridkoti, have begun to claim that Punjabi is a Dravidian language;
  and Prakrit, instead of being a spinoff from Sanskrit at the mass level, was a Dravidian idiom
  which in fact transformed Sanskrit" (p. 28, folio not stated). The concession: "the Punjabi scholar
  is humbled by the fact that he cannot trace literary development in his language parallel to the
  developments in other Dravidian languages." (p. 29, folio not stated).
- **The vacana comparison, which is the Introduction's positive placing of Bulleh Shah.** "In this
  quality of voice rather than verse he is very close to the vacana tradition of Kannada Bhakti poets
  from 1100 A.D. to 1200 A.D." (p. 31, folio 11); "The vacana is a form of free verse which relies
  for its effect on the spontaneous patterns of the mother tongue, an attraction that remains
  undiminished in the poetry of Bulleh Shah, It has an oral poetic and a rhetoric which introduces
  inner restraint into free verse." (p. 32, folio 12). Chowdayya's winnowing poem is set beside
  Rafat's "Lass, look to your spinning" as "his parallel poem with a more dexterous employment of
  localised metaphor" (p. 32, folio 12).
- **Form as evidence of temperament.** "His kafi is a kafi only in terms of its mystical content;
  compared to him, his predecessors seem to be too hide-bound in the forms they practised." (pp.
  24–25, folios not stated) — Baba Farid's slokes, Shah Hussain's raga-metre and Sultan Bahu's bait
  are the three foils (p. 25, folio not stated).
- **Rafat's translation ethic, argued from Fitzgerald.** "I suppose I have broken every accepted
  canon of translation including Eliot's warning that the translator should at least not add material
  which is his own… My own feeling is that anything goes to achieve the desired 'translucency'."
  (p. 13, folio xiii). The precedent: "No source has yet been found for his fifth ru bai… If a
  translator could insert an entire apocryphal rubai, I was certainly justified in including a phrase
  or two of my own making." (p. 13, folio xiii). And "This bolsters my contention that the end
  justifies the means." (p. 13, folio xiii).
- **Rafat's stated audience, and his stated failure conditions.** "my renderings are not for the
  academic, They will find plenty here to make them rub their hands in glee. Yet I feel they should
  suspend judgment till an acceptable translation of Bulleh Shah can be cited." (pp. 13–14, folios
  xiii–xiv). "What I have tried to catch is the gusto of Bulleh Shah, for he is a very virile poet.
  If sacrifices have had to be made to retain this vigour, I have not hesitated to make them."
  (p. 14, folio xiv). On versification: "Wherever possible I have tried to imitate the form and
  rhyme-scheme, but unfortunately I have had to resort to departures in the majority of the poems."
  (p. 18, folio xviii), with 'Accursed' and 'Briefing Bulleh' named as the extreme cases and
  'Briefing Bulleh' the one poem with "an extra line" (p. 18, folio xviii).
- **The word "rendering", explained as a defensive manoeuvre.** "The English versions are close
  enough to the original to merit the word 'translation', but there are always some hide-bound
  readers who cavil at the least deviation. I have, therefore, called the poems 'renderings' to fox
  them." (p. 18, folio xviii).
- **Rafat on what cannot cross: the si harfi, the dotted letters, and the alif.** "How is one to
  tackle si barfi [OCR?] [si harfi]? This means beginning a poem or a stanza with a letter of the
  alphabet, following it up with a word beginning with that letter, for instance, A Allab [OCR?]
  [Allah]." (p. 14, folio xiv). On the dot: "Certain letters of the Arabic alphabet (in which
  Pakistani Punjabi is written) are differentiated only by a dot… The nearest equivalents I could
  find were O and Q. Confer the poem 'The Difference'. But this solution is less than satisfactory,
  because the two letters are not contiguous, and worse, the all-important dot is missing." (p. 14,
  folio xiv). On alif: "the letter alepb [OCR?] [aleph] which is symbolised thus [illegible] has many
  mystical connotations. It stands for the beginning, for Allah or God, for uprightness, and so many
  things that it is impossible to endow A with that richness." (p. 15, folio xv).
- **Rafat on Bulleh Shah's anti-clericalism and its Western reception.** "Response to Bulleh Shah's
  unremitting attacks on priesthood and the established church will meet more general approval. These
  institutions are too recent in Western memory. Some isolated countries like Ireland are still
  priest-ridden." (p. 16, folio xvi). And the corrective on learning: "A superficial reading of Bulleh
  Shah is likely to give the impression that he was against all learning… but the type of learning he
  is railing against is that of the sedentary scholar, the pedant." (p. 16, folio xvi).
- **Rafat's rule for reading the corpus whole.** "Bulleh Shah's philosophy of life is not summed up in
  any one poem. Poem is balanced against poem, and the work must be judged as a whole." (p. 16, folio
  xvi). His example of an apparent contradiction: "'lass, attend to your spinning,' he says in one
  place; in almost the same breath he sings 'stop the spinning-wheel, girl'. These are not
  contradictions of belief, but distinctions in mood." (pp. 16–17, folios xvi and "es" [OCR?] [xvii]).
