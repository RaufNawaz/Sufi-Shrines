*Source: Taufiq Rafat (tr.), *Bulleh Shah: A Selection*, with an introduction by Khaled Ahmad
(Lahore: Vanguard Publications Ltd., 8-Davis Road, 1982; title page p. 3, imprint p. 4). 263 pages,
transcribed `tesseract` (transcription date not stated in the chunk notes); provenance in

## What this book is

A verse translation of seventy-one kafis and shorter poems of Bulleh Shah into English, printed as a
parallel text — Shahmukhi original on the verso, Rafat's English on the recto — and framed by **two
distinct authorial voices that must never be merged**. Khaled Ahmad's **Introduction** (pp. 21–52,
folios 1–32) is the biographical and historical apparatus: it alone describes the poet's tomb at
Qasur, his teacher, his master Shah Enayat, and the Mughal collapse it reads his poetry against.
Taufiq Rafat's **"TRANSLATOR'S NOTE"** (pp. 11–18, folios xi–xviii) is a statement of translation
method, and the **back matter** — `NOTES ON THE INTRODUCTION`, notes 1–30 (pp. 235–244), `GLOSSARY`
(pp. 247–254) and `INDEX OF FIRST LINES` (pp. 257–263) — carries the book's scholarly substance,
including nearly all of what it says about the Qadiriya order, Mian Mir and Uch Sharif. The stance of
the apparatus is polemical: against Dr. Lajwanti Ramakrishna's reading of Bulleh Shah as a Hindu
advaitin, and against the "progressive" reading of him as a political protest poet.

The poems themselves are the book's bulk and are, for this archive, almost empty: across all
seventy-one **not one shrine, tomb, mazar, dargah, ʿurs, mela, langar, sajjada nashin, custodian,
endowment or offering is named** (p. 92–221 stated once as a governing negative finding; confirmed
for pp. 55–91 and pp. 222–233). The only built religious places in the verse are the generic
"mosque", "temple", "monastery" and "precincts", and the only burial word is "grave" (p. 115,
folio 95).

### mazar-of-bulleh-shah — Mazar of Bulleh Shah

The book's subject. He is named in all four chunks and is the only archive figure whose **shrine the

Introduction (pp. 21–22). The book's spelling is **"Bulleh Shah"** in roman throughout and
**"Bulleb Shab"** [OCR?] [Bulleh Shah] wherever the name falls inside an italic title; the poetic
signature is given as **"Bullha"** (p. 243, folio 223), and in the translations the takhallus closes

    last great Mughal ruler Aurangzeb died in 1707 Bulleh Shah was 27 years old" (p. 26, folio not

### shrine-of-shah-inayat-qadiri — Shrine of Shah Inayat Qadiri

The book's second figure, and the archive row that gains most from consolidation: the poems (chunk
003) carry five invocations of a murshid named only "Inayat" with no place, date or order, and the
Introduction and notes supply all three. **The book names him five ways** — "Shah Enayat" throughout
Khaled Ahmad's Introduction and the notes (pp. 22–24, 42, 44–46, 241–242), as the marginal headings
*Shab Enayat* [OCR?] [Shah Enayat] (p. 22) and *Reference to Shab Enayat* [OCR?] [Shah Enayat]
(p. 44, folio 24), "Shah Inayat" once (p. 39, folio 19), "Shah Enayat Qadiri" once (p. 44, folio 24),
"Inayat Shah" in Rafat's note (p. 14, folio xiv) and in the verse (p. 185, folio 165), and "inayat"
[OCR?] [Inayat] with the line-initial capital lost (p. 101, folio 81).

- **Saint**
  - **The identifying sentence, which gives Lahore, the Qadiriya and Mian Mir's own seat in one
    line.** "Shah Enayat of Lahore, the beloved preceptor of Bulleh Shah, was a successor to the
    Qadiriya 'gaddi' of Mian Mir and could not have inclined to an overt opposition to. the spiritual
    traditions of the Sikhs." (p. 42, folio 22).
  - **The order, named four more times.** "Shah Enayat, whose life spanned the Mughal rule from
    Aurangzeb to Mohammad Shah, belonged to the Qadiriya School of Muslim mysticism in India. His
    scholarship of Arabic and Persian was overlaid with an acceptance of the mystical traditions of
    the people of the subcontinent. This openness to local devotional cults was ironical since the
    creed of the Hanbali teacher Sheik Abdul Qadir of Baghdad," the founder of the School, was
    originally opposed to innovative adjustments of this sort." (p. 22, folio not stated); "the

## Cross-cutting material

### Orders, succession and custodianship

- **Succession is a *gaddi*, held by a lineal saint who inherits physical relics — the book's only
  succession mechanism, and it is stated twice.** "The lineal saint who ascends the gaddi of the
  Qadiris possesses such relics of the founder saint, Abdul Qadir Gilani, as beads, scissors and
  cap." (p. 235, folio not stated); "Shah Enayat of Lahore … was a successor to the Qadiriya 'gaddi'
  of Mian Mir" (p. 42, folio 22). **No sajjada nashin, no descendant by name, no endowment, no land
  and no revenue figure appears anywhere in the book** — all (not stated). The one named institution
  at any shrine is the **Auqaf Department** at Bulleh Shah's tomb (p. 22, folio not stated).
- **Two orders contrasted by their economy and social practice.** "The Suhrawardiya tradition of
  patronage is diametrically opposed to that of the Qadiriya who practise asceticism and are given to
  assimilation of local cults." (p. 39, folio 19).
- **The Qadiriya's character in India.** "In India, the Qadiriya order were late in coming and
  represent the most freewheeling of the mystical orders in terms of ritual." (p. 236, folio 216);
  and, quoting Memon, "By far the most peaceful and moderate, the Qadiriya gradually became the most
  widespread Sufi order and the image of al-Jilani, in glorifying which his spiritual disciples had
  strained all the resources of their minds, came to supplant the image of the prophet of Islam."
  (p. 236, folio 216).
- **The order's foundation, and the book's claim that its founder did not intend one.** "The oldest
  of Sufi orders, the Qadiriya, derived its name from the Hanbalite Abd-al-Qadir al-Jilani (D.
  561/1166) a native of the district of Jilan south of the Caspian. Al-Jilani studied at Baghdad
  where he later took up public preaching in 521/1127." (pp. 235–236, folio not stated / 216); "It
  seems, strangely enough, that al-Jilani himself had no intention of founding an order. The order
  that arose after him in his name was largely the work of his sons." (p. 236, folio 216). Khaled
  Ahmad names the same man as "the Hanbali teacher Sheik Abdul Qadir of Baghdad," the founder of the
  School" (p. 22, folio not stated).
- **The Naqshbandiya as the Qadiriya's institutional enemy, with a named fiscal instrument.** "The
  Naqshbandiya school was in the ascendant at the court of Mughal Emperor Jahangir" (p. 42, folio
  22); "Naqshbandi order was opposed to the Qadiriya saints and recommended persecution of the
  non-Muslim subjects in the levy of a special punitive tax called jazia." (p. 240, folio 220); "It
  was, in fact, the Turani-Irani struggle taking place at the Mughal court." (p. 240, folio 220);
  "Jahangir, who kept the Sheikh in his camp after granting him release from prison, seems to have
  heeded his advice against the Qadiris. " (p. 241, folio 221); and the order's reach, "The
  Naqshbandi order was brought to India from Samarkand in Central Asia." (p. 240, folio 220).
  Bulleh Shah's position on it: "his disapproval of the Naqshabandiya verdict against the Shia sect"
  (p. 39, folio 19); "Bulleh Shah's intense distaste of the Naqshbandi polemic against the Shia
  Muslim sect comes through quite clearly in the kafts [OCR?] [kafis]" (p. 42, folio 22).
- **Cross-tradition pilgrimage.** Sachal Sarmast "continued to send his disciples to Amritsar for
  homage." (p. 42, folio 22).

  shrine*: "The qawwals constantly sing his rebuking lines" at Bulleh Shah's tomb (p. 21, folio not
  stated). *As the sole preservers of a corpus*: "A hundred and thirty years later there was no
  written evidence of his poetry, most of it preserved in the singing of the gawwals [OCR?]
  [qawwals] and minstrels of the countryside" (p. 48, folio 28); "the kafi, 'Bulleb noo samjbavan
  ayan' [OCR?] [Bulleh noo samjhavan ayan] was discovered by her directly from the gawwals [OCR?]
  [qawwals]." (p. 48, folio 28). *As corrupters of a text*: "What is today often put down as
  corruption wrought by the qawwal may be the unfolding of an oral poetic in the kafis." (p. 32,

- **Samaʿ, named as an order's custom and given an instrument.** "Aware of the Qadiriya custom of
  Sama and devotional dance, Bulleh Shah took to the sarangi, the stringed instrument associated with
  the wandering dervish, and began to sing and dance." (p. 24, folio not stated). Inside the verse,

  qawwal or assembly is named in any poem** (not stated).
- **The itinerant singer-saint as a teaching office.** "it must be remembered that the travelling

  certain pitfalls, and practice certain virtues." (p. 17, folio "es" [OCR?] [xvii]).
- **Kafi as a musical category, and Rafat's objection to the usage.** "Since the sufi poetry is
  composed for the minstrel it was natural for it to be absorbed by the elaborate system of the
  Indian classical music. Kafi is, therefore, commonly referred to as a raga which is technically
  incorrect." (p. 49, folio 29); "Bulleh Shah changed the structure of what is called the kafi beyond
  recognition… Kafi, therefore, is determined by its mood." (p. 49, folio 29). The minor forms:

- **The printing history of Bulleh Shah, dated edition by edition** (p. 48, folio 28; p. 49,

### Ritual attacked from inside the poems

- **Ritual named as a set and dismissed — the most quotable passage of the translation.** `RITUALS`:
  "Fasting, pilgrimage, and the call to prayer, love has drowned them all. — Can any artifice survive
  when messages of love arrive? … The moment Love came toward me, rituals went by the board."
  (p. 125, folio 105). The same list recurs: "Now prayer and fast are done. [OCR?] [done,] for the
  cup is raised in skoal." (p. 165, folio 145); "And God was temple and mosque and all you could ask,
  but cold to fast and prayer-call." (p. 205, folio 185).
- **The objects of observance, listed to be destroyed.** "Scatter the rosary, spurn ablutions and the
  prayer-mat burn, for lovers they sigh and yearn to taste prohibited things." (p. 131, folio 111);
  "Genuflexions night and day have worn the knee-caps away." (p. 131, folio 111); "In prayer why
  abrade your forehead? / Away .with it, tear down the facade / of morality." (p. 81, folio 61);
  "You stay up all night to pray, but dogs are sleepless too; they're better than you." (p. 145,
  folio 125); "The muezzin shatters the air while you heap prayer on prayer and sermons drip from
  your hair, but to what end?" (p. 137, folio 117).
- **Pilgrimage treated economically.** "From a pilgrimage some have returned, / but any merit they may
  have earned, / despite humility in dress, / in percentage and shady deals is burned." (p. 83, folio
  63); "God's not in Tirath or Mecca, but in hearts where true love sings." (p. 131, folio 111).
- **Exorcism and the ritual specialist, attacked by the name of the function.** `THURSDAY`: "Think of
  the simpletons / who go to be exorcised / by devilish priests of demons, / when all they need is a
  guide / to set them free." (p. 225, folio 205) — **the book's only poem-internal statement about
  shrine or ritual specialists.** Against it, the guide who works: `RAIN`: "Friends hurry to my side
  / with 'Lucky, lucky you.' / My mentor, my guide / made my dreams come true." (p. 229, folio 209).
- **The religious professions as a predatory economy.** "Mullahs mug their way to power, and now are
  judge and juror; and avarice grows by the hour, and straight to hell you wend."; "You dream up
  problems in a wink, schism is your meat and drink, beneath saintly garb you stink, saying what you
  don't intend" (p. 139, folio 119); "Such learning fools elevates and some fresh bother creates, is
  used to con illiterates, make vows with false intent" (p. 137, folio 117); "Merchants in mosque and
  monastery , [OCR?] [monastery,] thugs in the landlord's pay." (p. 123, folio 103); "Mullah and
  torch-bearer come of one stock, giving light to others, themselves in the dark." (p. 173, folio
  153); "Talk only of God, the rest is merely chatter, though scholar and priest have tried to
  confuse the matter." (p. 199, folio 179). Against them, one positive religious figure: "Who knows
  what the sadhu knows will surely find repose." (p. 179, folio 159).
- **Divination, astrology and spells — the verse's only ritual practice outside formal worship.**
  "| [OCR?] [I] ask each passing magus, why is it everything | [OCR?] [I] do turns out badly?
  Astrologer, be kind, tell the truth for once. Am | [OCR?] [I] cursed? Was | [OCR?] [I] born

### Rural economy, marriage and caste

- **The dowry-and-spinning cycle as a described rural institution, with the reading key Rafat
  supplies for it.** "When she attains awareness she is taught to spin and is constantly rebuked when
  she neglects the spinning-wheel… After puberty, she is constantly reminded that she must spin more
  than an average output because whatever she spins would become a part of her dowry." (p. 46, folio
  26); "the meticulous advice to a girl to prepare her dowry is in fact advice to prepare for death"
  (p. 15, folio xv); "The symbol of the charkba [OCR?] [charkha] or spinning-wheel is one his
  audience could immediately identify… for the message in each case was the same—make ready for the
  Bridegroom Death, the day of departure is at hand." (p. 17, folio "es" [OCR?] [xvii]). Rafat names
  the objects that resist translation: "the spinning-wheel, the begging-bowl, and the blanket"
  (p. 15, folio xv).
- **The wedding as an economy, in `KNOTS` and the spinning poems.** "How many knots shall | [OCR?]
  [I] tie, / dear, tell me true? / Forty days to the wedding, / so forty will do."; "| [OCR?] [I]
  went off to the market with / an empty purse, / and the merchants supplied me / a push and a
  curse."; "The loud procession is here, / this is the day / when the ready and the unready will / be
  taken away, / to be with the master and / sit by his side" (p. 231, folio 211), closing "Now the
  last rites.are [OCR?] [rites are] ended" (p. 233, folio 213). "Your wedding day is just a week away
  … If dowryless you leave how can you hope to please? Your spouse will grumble and grieve." (p. 141,
  folio 121); "A tough mother-in-law waits." (pp. 153–155, folios 133–135); "What was my dowry? A
  stove and a shawl. When | [OCR?] [I] think of it now the tear-drops fall." (p. 177, folio 157).
- **The knot-string calendar, a living village custom Rafat describes at length.** "It refers to a
  custom still prevalent in some remote villages where the illiterate folk having no calendars or
  diaries tie knots on a string or a piece of cloth to remind them of a forth-coming event. Suppose a
  wedding has been fixed for thirty days later; thirty knots are tied on a string; each morning one
  knot is untied so that the simple people know how many days remain." (p. 15, folio xv). He names
  the surrounding complex as "a whole tradition of weddings, fairs, funerals, circumcisions, rituals
  and superstitions" (p. 15, folio xv).
- **Caste, untouchability and wage labour — the one sustained social description in the verse.**
  "I'm just a sweepress. Hair uncombed, barefoot, | [OCR?] [I] receive word of his coming … The broom
  my meditation: with it I've swept into my basket all that the world has kept. The judge knows much,
  the king rules with fear, but I'm happy to be allowed in here. … Being untouchable, none comes near
  me, but I'm on the way, I do not care. What's my pay after a hard day's grind? A hard pillow, and
  what you leave behind. This is my life: cold and sickness and scorn; an empty stomach, clothes that
  are torn. The straws of my broom are all that | [OCR?] [I] own." (p. 187, folio 167). Set against
  it, the levelling of rank by trade: "Khaddar and muslin, coarse or fine, dress for ploughman,
  groom, divine, issue from the self-same loom no matter what the guise." (p. 127, folio 107).
- **Agrarian conflict, merchants and alms.** "What parents sowed, the children reap. / Who's the
  benefactor? Who the thief? / When the corn came in, the knives came out; / brother fought brother
  for every sheaf." (p. 79, folio 59); "A hundred merchants are here who tell of rubies they have
  brought to sell. … What was the price they asked? I-cried [OCR?] [I cried] at pin-pricks, and they

- **Taxation, tribute and plunder as the economic background of the poetry.** The Qasur rebellion

### The book's own arguments

- **Bulleh Shah is a Muslim Sufi, not a Hindu monist — the central polemic, run by both voices, with
  Lajwanti Ramakrishna as the named opponent.** Khaled Ahmad: "Dr. Lajwanti began this most
  self-defeating and colourless exercise by categorising the various phases in his poetry as Islamic
  sufism, Bhakti Vasnaivism [OCR?] [Vaisnavism] and triumphantly discovered the apex of his poetic
  genius in the achievement of monistic advaita… She claimed that in 'Ki janan mein kon' Bulleh Shah
  finally broke with his Islamic background and merged with the more immanent Hindu philosophy of
  Advaita, unaware that the kafis was literally inspired by Rumi's ghazal… Muslim authors, their
  hackles up, just as assiduously pushed him back into the Islamic fold by pasting on him the
  exclusive label of Ibn Arabi's 'Wabdat-ul-Wujud' [OCR?] [Wahdat-ul-Wujud]. But beyond and above
  this communal humbug, Bulleh Shah seems to incorporate and transcend all the contemporary
  influences till only a voice remains" (p. 28, folio not stated). Rafat: "the poets, for all their
  colloquial self-abasement, retained the desire to be considered part of the Islamic sufi tradition.
  This partly demolishes the communal gloss so often placed on him by those, like Dr. Lajwanti:
  Ramakrishna, who claim him as a bbakta [OCR?] [bhakta] in the tradition of Hindu monism." (p. 38,
  folio 18), with evidence both quantitative and ironic: "in his most 'Hindu' Kafi Sayyo ni bun
  [OCR?] [hun] mein sajan payoni', he employs the largest number of Arabic phrases from the Quran."
  (p. 38, folio 18).
- **The contested passage quoted in full, and Rafat's counter-move by juxtaposition.** Ramakrishna's
  position, which Rafat calls "The bone of contention": "'The third and last phase of Bullha's mystic
  life was unique. Here he resembles no sufi or vaisnava of the Punjab or the rest of India. During
  this time he is a firm believer in advaita and sees that all-pervading spirit, God, in all and
  independently of all religions.'" (pp. 242–243, folios 222–223); and her stronger claim: "'We have
  stated that the pantheism of Bulleh Shah was Hindu in its entirety and therefore differed a good
  deal from the pantheism of the sufis. His advaita, which was Indian in its essence, had so
  overpowered him, nay had transformed him in such a way that any sort of conversion was beyond his
  understanding.'" (p. 243, folio 223). Rafat answers evidentially: "At this juncture it would be in
  order to present the lyric of Rumi on which Bulleh Shah had based his kafi." (p. 243, folio 223),
  setting Ramakrishna's "Bullha, what do I know who I am?" against Nicholson's Rumi, "What is to be
  done, O Muslims? for I do not recognise myself. I am neither Christian nor Jew nor Parsi nor
  Muslim." (p. 243, folio 223). **He does not state the conclusion.** He names an ally: "Dr.
  Tirlochan Singh in his article Bulleb Shab da Tassavuf [OCR?] [Bulleh Shah da Tassavuf] in the said
  compilation concentrates on the Islamic derivation of Bulleh Shah's mysticism in a conscious
  antithesis to Dr. Ramakrishna's opinion." (p. 244, folio 224).
- **Ramakrishna nevertheless defended against her detractors.** "the 1938 Book of Dr. Lajwanti
  Ramakrishnan Punjabi Sufi Poets, remains the standard assessment of Bulleh Shah although the
  frog-chorus of later critics does not tire of referring to her insultingly"; her essay "remains the
  best investigation to-date on the Qadiriya background of the poet." (p. 51, folio 31). And Nazir
  Ahmad quoted against himself: "'This home-broken dame must be serving up pilao of peas, tikkas of
  cheese and balls of saag but knows little about poetry.'" (p. 244, folio 224).
- **Bhakti defended against a Pakistani orthodoxy, and redefined as Dravidian.** "The tradition of
  bhakti in Indian poetry has been condemned by orthodox Pakistani writers as a Hindu inroad into the
  pure Islamic sufi tradition. This criticism is more political than scientific. Bhakti was in fact a
  Dravidian revolt against the Aryan sacerdotalism and philosophy." (p. 30, folio 10). Sufi doctrine
  traced to Greek and Indian sources: "Islamic sufis absorbed their doctrines from Greek sources and
  these doctrines predated the Greeks in the Indian philosophy of Advaita." (pp. 43–44, folios
  23–24).
- **The Dravidian-origin thesis for Punjabi, argued in both halves of the book.** "British scholars
  like Grierson and Bailey had categorised Punjabi as a Prakrit off-shoot of Sanskrit… After a
  century of acceptance, Punjabi researchers led by Ain-ul-Haq Faridkoti, have begun to claim that
  Punjabi is a Dravidian language" (p. 28, folio not stated), with the concession that "the Punjabi
  scholar is humbled by the fact that he cannot trace literary development in his language parallel
  to the developments in other Dravidian languages." (p. 29, folio not stated). Rafat's verdict in
  note 6: "A more in-depth study is, however, needed to overthrow the orientalist conclusions; yet,
  the Dravidian view is gaining ground among Punjabi scholars also as a kind of local chauvinistic
  reaction to the ancient external trespass of the Aryans. For Muslim scholars the view is attractive
  because Aryan civilisation was subsumed by Hinduism and now represents its caste-ridden social
  system." (p. 239, folio 219).
- **The state of Punjabi letters, and a cultural explanation for textual loss.** "A lack of
  intellectual tradition among the Muslims of the Punjab accounts for this phillistine [OCR?]
  [philistine] indifference to the classics. Bulleh Shah was treated no differently." (p. 47, folio
  27); "In terms of written sources, the Punjab and indeed the whole of the Indus basin, has always
  seemed a wasteland to scholars." (pp. 46–47, folios 26–27); "Punjabi literature in Pakistan is in a
  state of neglect. The language has become a purely spoken medium and the Punjabis are no longer
  able to decipher it in the written form." (p. 50, folio 30). And the policy fact from note 4: "All
  regional languages in Pakistan are taught in schools except Punjabi. All regional languages in
  Pakistan possess dictionaries except Punjabi." (p. 238, folio 218).
- **Rafat's translation ethic, and the sufi reading key he supplies for the whole book.** "Love in
  sufi poetry refers to the love of or for God, or the mentor, or desire for absorption in Nature, it
  never means love for a woman; and the woman or Beloved in the poems is simply the object of
  spiritual adoration… The Bridegroom in the poems is Death." (p. 15, folio xv); "References to Heer
  and Ranjha are not to the legendary lovers, but to the devotee and the sought." (p. 15, folio xv).
  On method: "I suppose I have broken every accepted canon of translation including Eliot's warning
  that the translator should at least not add material which is his own… My own feeling is that
  anything goes to achieve the desired 'translucency'." (p. 13, folio xiii); "This bolsters my
  contention that the end justifies the means." (p. 13, folio xiii); "The English versions are close
  enough to the original to merit the word 'translation', but there are always some hide-bound
  readers who cavil at the least deviation. I have, therefore, called the poems 'renderings' to fox
  them." (p. 18, folio xviii). **Every quotation from a poem in this book must be read against that
  statement.**
- **Rafat's rule for reading the corpus whole.** "Bulleh Shah's philosophy of life is not summed up in
  any one poem. Poem is balanced against poem, and the work must be judged as a whole." (p. 16, folio
  xvi); "'lass, attend to your spinning,' he says in one place; in almost the same breath he sings
  'stop the spinning-wheel, girl'. These are not contradictions of belief, but distinctions in mood."
  (pp. 16–17, folios xvi and "es" [OCR?] [xvii]).
- **What cannot cross into English, in Rafat's own list.** On the acrostic: "How is one to tackle si
  barfi [OCR?] [si harfi]? This means beginning a poem or a stanza with a letter of the alphabet,
  following it up with a word beginning with that letter, for instance, A Allab [OCR?] [Allah]."
  (p. 14, folio xiv). On the dot: "Certain letters of the Arabic alphabet (in which Pakistani Punjabi
  is written) are differentiated only by a dot… The nearest equivalents I could find were O and Q.
  Confer the poem 'The Difference'. But this solution is less than satisfactory, because the two
  letters are not contiguous, and worse, the all-important dot is missing." (p. 14, folio xiv). On
  alif: "the letter alepb [OCR?] [aleph] which is symbolised thus [illegible] has many mystical
  connotations. It stands for the beginning, for Allah or God, for uprightness, and so many things
  that it is impossible to endow A with that richness." (p. 15, folio xv).
