## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Proximity rather than pilgrimage.** Mama Ji's devotees "habitually just went around the corner to
  experience communitas in the presence of their saint. Such 'normative' communitas was part of their
  ordinary lives" and "did not need to be embedded in the liminal situation of a temporary pilgrimage
  setting" (p. 163, folio 152).
- **Women and access.** "In the folk veneration of saints it is usually women who are more involved
  than men", but at Mama Ji's new site "women are conspicuously absent in Nurpur, partly since, during
  this provisional stage of transition, no suitable arrangements have yet been made for their stay and
  proper social contacts" (p. 163, folio 152).
- The older house is physically restrictive: "Mama Ji's astan in ‘Pindi is already very confined and,
  in my observation, women do not find it easy to go there" (p. 163, folio 152).
- **Exclusion in the saint's lifetime.** "In his lifetime he sometimes sent them away from his bed—even
  if they were not menstruating, as stated by informants—and only allowed access to men" (p. 163,
  folio 152). Frembgen's counterfactual: "A higher participation of women in the cult would most
  probably have lent it another character" (p. 163, folio 152).
- **Building programme at Nurpur, dated year by year.** "During my subsequent visits, in 1992 and
  1993, the devotees were increasingly discontented with the initial hasty planning of the new shrine
  and decided they wanted a more generous layout" (p. 164, folio 153).
- The projected building exists as painted images in both houses: "two large paintings by Sayyid
  Ishrat of the future shrine building are displayed, showing a prestigious, cube-shaped, domed
  structure with a veranda surrounding it" (p. 164, folio 153).
- "In September 1992, a forged iron door was erected at the entrance to the shrine, to protect it from
  goats" (p. 164, folio 153).
- "Heavy monsoon rains in summer 1994 threatened to wash away the tomb. In response, a solidly
  cemented basement, accessible by four stairs, was built and the tomb itself was brought down"
  (p. 164, folio 153).
- **The langar and its property, dated and counted.** "By April 1994, the langar had already
  mushroomed, with several makeshift huts, small gardens, and fences for the langar's property—now
  consisting of five goats and one sheep!" (p. 164, folio 153).
- **Its character as an institution.** "It is still a casual, unstructured hang-out which, in its
  informality and availability of hashish, attracts free spirits and easy-goers, as well as seekers of
  mystical blessings" (p. 164, folio 153).
- **Land tenure.** "The plot belongs to the villager Sain Ikram" (p. 169, folio 158) — the shrine
  ground is not said to be owned by the cult, and no waqf, endowment, Auqaf department or state body
  is mentioned anywhere in the chunk (pp. 163–179, folios 152–168).
- **An anjuman as the organising body.** "the anjuman was able to attract crowds of visitors to Mama
  Ji's shrine and to his former baithak" (p. 164, folio 153). Its membership, founding date and
  officers are **(not stated)** (p. 164, folio 153).
- **Piggybacking on a bigger ‘urs.** The 1994 visitor surge was produced by scheduling: the crowds came
  "During Barri Imam's ‘urs in 1994, in the first week of May", and "the event was successfully used to
  publicise the name and sacred power of Mama Ji Sarkar" (p. 164, folio 153).
- **Visitor numbers, with the author's caveat attached.** "His own ‘urs, the fourth one, attracted
  hundreds of pilgrims—if not thousands as proclaimed by his followers" (p. 164, folio 153).
- **A subsidiary devotional site.** The saint's "former baithak, a holy place with a cheragh-khana in
  honour of the majzub, maintained by Sain ‘Abdul Majid near the roadside" (p. 164, folio 153); during
  the ‘urs "both places of devotion were richly decorated" (p. 164, folio 153).
- **Care of the living saint's body by his attendants.** "the mujawirs were concerned with his health
  and with the vigour of his body: they fed him, applied `[text-layer?]` [a word appears to be missing
  after 'applied'; not recoverable], and massaged him" (p. 166, folio 155).
- **Shrine and mosque as adjacent institutions.** "Mama Ji's shrine in ‘Pindi… is adjacent to the Jama’
  Masjid", so that the opposition between mystical and "official Islam represented by the 'religion of
  the mosque'" is "aptly reflected in the geographical setting" (p. 167, folio 156).
- **What the ‘Pindi shrine contains and does.** "Pictorial representations of Mama Ji and other, related
  saints"; "In the intimacy of the shrine and langar they help to produce a stimulating atmosphere
  where the saint, whose lifestory is told, becomes familiar and close"; "Surrounded by Mama Ji's
  portraits, his murids extol the virtues of their murshid, tell their dreams… and make his saintly
  qualities a topic of everyday discourse" (p. 167, folio 156).
- **Music as shrine practice.** "devotees are further sensitised by the flow of qawwali-music. This is
  a style of mystical music associated with divine love in its most intense form" (p. 167, folio 156).
- **Fieldwork frame for all of the above.** "During a seven-year period from March 1988 to September
  1995 (in the course of twelve trips to Pakistan) I visited these two shrines frequently. Following
  the death of the majzub in 1991, the visits were also to the Nurpur mazar" (p. 169, folio 158).
- **Hereditary custodianship at the unnamed Lahore-area family shrine.** "Ashiq had been designated the
  future successor of his paternal uncle, who is the current caretaker (gaddi nishin) of the family
  shrine" (p. 174, folio 163).
- **Its annual festival.** Rabi‘a's children "subsequently took us to the village to participate in and
  observe the annual ‘urs celebration at the shrine" (p. 174, folio 163). No date, month or attendance
  figure is given — **(not stated)** (p. 174, folio 163).
- **Succession reassigned after a death.** "All say that after Sa’in Sahib, this boy should sit on the
  gaddi" (the honored seat; i.e. he will be the caretaker of the shrine)… "At first the nazar [gaze]
  was on Ashiq, but now, after his death, people's nazar is on Achi" (p. 179, folio 168). The
  qualification for the seat is withdrawal from ordinary work: "he doesn't go to school, doesn't work,
  always sits near Sa’in Sahib. His parents sent him to work, but he also gave that up. He returned to
  the village" (p. 179, folio 168).
- **Healing economy around pirs.** A ta‘wiz (amulet) bought for Rabi‘a's dream affliction (p. 176,
  folio 165); a pir's prescription, "Take an almond that has two pieces inside it and bring it to me. I
  will read something over it" (p. 177, folio 166); repeated journeys to "a pir in Kharian" (p. 177,
  folio 166); and a faqir consulted at Data Sahib (p. 178, folio 167).
- **Yunani medicine alongside shrine remedies.** "kol, tabashir [chalk, which she said was commonly
  used as a Yunani medicine to reduce sores on the tongue or inside body]… and choti ilachi
  [cardamom]", given for five days (p. 176, folio 165); Ewing calls it "a Yunani (Greek) medicine"
  that "corrected the problem of excessive heat in his body" (p. 178, folio 167).
- **Disbelief inside the family.** Of the faqir's diagnosis, "Her husband [Rabi‘a's daughter's husband]
  doesn't believe this. He says, 'We will see'" (p. 178, folio 167); Ewing reads this as the point at
  which "whether black magic can cause disease and whether pirs can cure it" becomes explicit (p. 178,
  folio 167).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The majzub's body needs no washing.** "as his body was intrinsically pure, without emitting any
  odour, it did not need more care (washing, etc.)" (p. 166, folio 155). Attributed by Frembgen to his
  own observation and to his informants' practice (p. 166, folio 155).
- **Immobility as a sign**, of Baba Dhannaka: he "has not moved from his sitting position for the past
  twenty-nine years" (p. 166, folio 155).
- **The saint's substance as baraka.** Devotees "thought of his bones, flesh, bodily fluids and
  'residues' (hair) as consisting of baraka, or having sacred qualities (hence the importance of
  relics…)" (p. 166, folio 155).
- **Bodies stolen and saints killed for their bodies**, reported by Frembgen as a known pattern rather
  than a specific case: "besides the theft of the saint's body, even the killing of the saint so as to
  get his body is known in the history of Sufism" (p. 166, folio 155). No shrine, saint or date is
  attached to this — **(not stated)** (p. 166, folio 155).
- **Healing by contact.** "Through touching the saint's body and paying loving attention to it, they
  transmitted this mystical power to themselves"; "Even though he remained a passive healer, through
  his body energy flowed to his disciples, conveying wellbeing and consolation to them" (p. 166,
  folio 155).
- **Dream appearances after death.** Murids "tell their dreams in which he appears as a saviour"
  (p. 167, folio 156).
- **Touching the mad for blessing.** "Many people will go up to such unfortunates and touch them to
  receive God's blessing through them" (p. 171, folio 160).
- **The burned mind.** Ewing's escorts explained "that the saint had gotten so close to God that the
  encounter had 'burned' his mind: he had become majzub" (p. 171, folio 160).
- **Speech restored on the fifth day.** After five years of silence, "I gave it to him for five days.
  After five days, Ashiq said 'Mama.' Then he began to talk" (p. 176, folio 165).
- **Ashiq's truthful speech, given by his mother as evidence of sainthood.** "That which he spoke to me
  came true. Usually he spoke little, but whatever he said was true" (p. 179, folio 168).
- **Hair-cutting as black magic.** "On the way, a strange woman cut a bit of my little boy's hair and
  some threads of my piranda. After this, he became sick" (p. 179, folio 168); Ewing glosses the
  earlier version as alluding "to a common technique of black magic" (p. 177, folio 166).
- **A dream read as a body.** The faqir at Data Sahib interpreted a pipe filled with dust as "Your
  daughter's birth canal is closed…" (p. 178, folio 167).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Frembgen on communitas.** The devotees' communitas is "normative" and domestic rather than liminal,
  which he sets against Turner; note 8 points to "a critical review of Turner's concept, see Bilu
  (1988:302–330)" (p. 163, folio 152; p. 169, folio 158).
- **Frembgen frames the chapter's theoretical problem as Weberian.** "Fundamental is the problem of
  charisma: in one of his key theories, Max Weber argues that with the death of the charismatic, his
  mystical power either is converted into a 'charisma of office' and thereby routinised, or becomes
  'tradition' in the form of a hereditary charisma" (p. 164, folio 153), with note 9 citing "Weber
  (1947:297, compare 54); Weber (1972:143–145); compare Turner (1974: Ch. 2); Lindholm (1990:23–27)"
  (p. 169, folio 158).
- **The majzub breaks both Weberian routes, and this is Frembgen's central finding.** "As it is in the
  nature of a majzub not to have an heir, the saint's pristine source of authority has obviously been
  lost. Consequently one would expect a decline in the 'personalised' character of his charisma and a
  growing institutionalisation… Yet the majzub's disciples did not become real officials, and the
  rational organisation remains modest and unstructured" (p. 164, folio 153).
- **Death as a break in the devotees' lives, not only in the saint's.** "the saint's transition to the
  'real life' with God means a break in his followers' ordinary lives, a kind of set-back into
  normalcy" (pp. 164–165, folios 153–154); "Many of them often think: 'When Mama Ji was alive, all
  things went better!'" (p. 165, folio 154).
- **The two houses compared.** "In theory, there seems little to choose between… the 'pure', albeit
  short-lived and unstable, 'living' charisma of the saint in his first house and the manifestation of
  his baraka in the second house's tomb: in both, the saint embodies the vitalising force of his
  disciples' lives and remains himself, as the subject/object of devotion, relatively passive"
  (pp. 165–166, folios 154–155).
- **Durkheim invoked for the community effect.** "In accordance with the ideas of Emile Durkheim
  (1915), we observe that the saint's disciples form a close community sharing enthusiasm and common
  memories focused on their murshid" (p. 166, folio 155).
- **Frembgen's objection to a purely constructivist charisma.** "this conceptualisation of charisma as
  'imagined' and 'constructed' must contend with an ontological difference: the live saint was
  experienced not only as powerfully sacred but also as human, as vulnerable, as requiring care and
  loving attention" (p. 166, folio 155).
- **The saint as both protector and protected.** "Mama Ji was 'mother' in two senses (hence the name
  ma-ma)—as protector and giver but also as an object of protection" (p. 166, folio 155).
- **A contradiction he names between doctrine and practice.** Islamic mysticism holds "the saint's
  power being considerably enhanced after his death", yet "my observations of living Sufism show that
  the presence of the majzub amidst his followers is of crucial importance to them"; disciples "attach
  value to the living, physical body of their saint, whereas in the ideology of mysticism the body is
  seen as an obstacle" (p. 166, folio 155).
- **Comparative body theory.** "The body has been a project for mystics not only in Islam but also in
  other world religions (Shilling 1993; Feher 1989)" (p. 166, folio 155), and Devisch is quoted: "the
  human body is the central device or principal key that opens up and stimulates the whole system of
  healing" (1993:264) (p. 166, folio 155).
- **Darshan and psychosomatic unity.** Devotees "experienced the saint through a darshan-like
  contemplation as well as through his body as a medium of interaction; in this way they perceived
  Mama Ji as a psychosomatic unity, as soul and body together" — an orientation of "a high degree of
  concreteness differing in quality from the cult of a dead saint whose body is enshrined in a tomb"
  (p. 167, folio 156).
- **Charisma survives personalised because of love.** "Charisma and love are intertwined like the warp
  and weft of a woven fabric. In our case this link seems to be strong enough to personalise the saint
  even after death" (p. 167, folio 156), sustained by "an 'original charismatic community', to use
  Weber's words (Weber 1947:297)" with a strong "'We-relation'", note 11 citing "Schütz (1964:24–33)"
  (p. 167, folio 156; p. 169, folio 158).
- **Mysticism as anti-structure.** "The peculiar, essentially irrational charisma of a religious
  virtuoso stands as anti-structure to the rational (anti-mystical), rule-oriented, official Islam
  represented by the 'religion of the mosque'"; the saint's silence and "charismatic utterances, which
  are mystical revelations per viam negationis (on the road of negation)" stand "in sharp contrast to
  the stereotyped prayers recited at the mosque" (p. 167, folio 156).
- **Aesthetics as the chapter's key term.** Kapferer is quoted twice: "The aesthetic object frames
  individual experience, sets it in motion, and establishes the context of its experiencing"
  (1983:191), and of qawwali, "It demands the living of the reality it creates" (ibid.: 187); "Religious
  folk-art here serves as a formula of experience and emotion" (p. 167, folio 156).
- **Frembgen's closing position.** Quoting Lindholm (1990:7) that "charisma has an implicit structural
  form as a process that takes place over time and under certain conditions", he leaves "the further
  development of Mama Ji's (male?) cult" open and concludes that devotees "celebrate a form of Islam
  which offers a lot to the emotional needs of its adherents" (p. 168, folio 157).
- **Frembgen's etic hypothesis, explicitly hedged.** In note 6 he proposes Tourette's syndrome —
  "investigated by the French neurologist Gilles de la Tourette at the end of the last century"
  (Shapiro et al. 1988) — as "a clue to an understanding of some majzubs", adding "Not being a
  physician, I can obviously only suggest the possibility"; he says "a lot of majzubs—including Mama Ji
  Sarkar—seem to answer to several traits of Tourette's syndrome" (pp. 168–169, folios 157–158).
- **Acknowledgements name the volume's editor as a reader.** "I am especially grateful to Pnina Werbner
  for reading different versions of the present chapter"; Hugh van Skyhawk read an earlier draft
  (p. 168, folio 157).
- **Ewing opens by stating a received generalisation and then withdrawing it.** "in popular Muslim
  belief madness and feeblemindedness are regarded not as disease, but as a sign that the affected
  person lives in a state closer to God" (p. 171, folio 160) — then: "But a general statement such as
  this is, of course, wrong" (p. 172, folio 161).
- **Her definition of the term.** "In the Urdu language, the majzub is 'one whom God draws to Himself'
  (Platts 1983:1002). The majzub is one whose speech and actions appear to lack sense because his or
  her mind has been 'burned' by the closeness to God" (p. 171, folio 160).
- **Her target is the constructed gulf.** Such a statement "highlights (or, rather, creates) a gulf that
  separates a modern Western discourse that constitutes 'mental illness' as an observable, clinical,
  medical entity (see Foucault 1973) from a Muslim discourse that apparently does not" and "obscures
  the nuances and contextual specificity with which Pakistani Muslims assess and react to those they
  label pagal" (p. 172, folio 161).
- **Modernity/tradition talk as a dominant discourse.** Such statements "are distinctions organized in
  terms of a dominant discourse that posits progress and locates signs of this progression in Western
  practices (see, for example, Said 1979; Mudimbe 1988)" (p. 172, folio 161).
- **Cultural relativism criticised too.** "the cultural relativist's stance also draws an artificial
  boundary between 'us' and 'them' that is insupportable when the day-to-day management of
  relationships involving those considered pagal or majzub is examined"; "Anthropology has frequently
  been guilty of exoticizing the other" (p. 172, folio 161).
- **The same person can be saint or madman by context.** "Though the majzub is explicitly associated
  with sainthood, the same individual may in another setting be called pagal (mad) and be rendered
  powerless" (p. 172, folio 161).
- **The chapter's thesis.** The mother's "stories about two saints—her own son and her brother-in-law—
  express an ambivalence that reflects not so much a tension or discontinuity between tradition and
  modernity as her own conflictual positionings vis-à-vis discourses about family dynamics, assessments
  of disease, and attributions of sainthood"; "Modernity and tradition do not stand dichotomized as
  alternative explanatory models" (p. 173, folio 162).
- **Against rational-choice models in medical anthropology.** Such models "do not do justice to the
  experience of individuals", make the other "appear 'like us'… in ways that he or she actually is
  not", and are "reminiscent of that of Frazer's and Tylor's theories of primitive religious practices
  and of Malinowski's (1948) description of magic as stepping in where science has not yet been
  sufficiently developed" (pp. 173–174, folios 162–163).
- **Her alternative.** "I propose looking at the person who behaves oddly as a nexus of conflicting
  interpretive processes that serve as a vehicle for articulating self-experience and identity"; the
  process "is especially complex for family members" and reveals "an array of responses that manifest
  our common humanity" (p. 174, folio 163).
- **Sainthood is not simply inherited.** "Though his social positioning as a descendant of saints had
  made it possible for Ashiq to assume the identity of 'saint,' this positioning alone would not have
  been sufficient to account for the reverence with which he seemed to be viewed. Ironically, it was
  precisely the qualities that had given Rabi‘a so much worry… that led to his acquisition of this
  identity" (p. 175, folio 164).
- **A possible biomedical reading, offered and then set aside.** "He often had 'fits' at night, for
  instance, suggesting that he had suffered minor brain damage at some point that had produced epilepsy
  and some retardation… But this objectification does not in any way capture who Ashiq was" (p. 176,
  folio 165).
- **Person-theory borrowed from South Asianist ethnography.** Rabi‘a's sense that "she had somehow
  passed her tension on to Ashiq" is read "through a process consistent with a model of the person
  prevalent in South Asia in which interaction with another can shape the 'substance' and wellbeing of
  a person (Marriott 1976)" (p. 176, folio 165); the sequence of ailments is read as a discourse of
  "'complaints' (Wilce 1994)" (p. 177, folio 166).
- **Explanations offered interchangeably, not ranked.** Heatstroke and hair-cutting magic "seem to have
  been presented interchangeably" (p. 177, folio 166).
- **The ethnographer's presence as a variable in the data.** The modern/superstition issue "did not seem
  particularly salient for Rabi‘a, except to the extent that my presence as an American made it so. She
  seemed hesitant to commit herself to a position that she suspected I might think was foolish"
  (p. 178, folio 167).
- **The two tellings compared, which is the chapter's method.** In the first, the explanations "seemed
  to be presented as an array of alternative possible 'diagnoses' proffered by the many people she
  consulted"; in the second, "the inconsistent elements are apparently being presented as a sequence of
  facts… not given as diagnoses but as facts that serve as explanations for why he was different from
  others" (p. 179, folio 168). In the first telling she "merely stated that some people had said that
  he was a sa’in"; in the second she asserted "My own son was sa’in" (p. 179, folio 168).

## Doubts

- **The three thin archive mappings, set out so the consolidator can weigh them.** `bari-imam` rests on
  two sentences that name only an ‘urs and its week, never the shrine, the saint's name, his tomb or
  his place (p. 164, folio 153). `golra-sharif` and `shrine-of-pir-baba-syed-ali-tirmizi` rest on a
  **single German book title in the reference list** and on nothing else in the chunk (p. 169,
  folio 158). All three are mapped under the ruling of 18 Sep 2026; none of them supports a fact beyond
  the fact of being named (p. 164, folio 153; p. 169, folio 158).
- **The book spells it "Barri Imam" and the archive row spells it "Bari Imam".** The doubled r is in
  the printed text, in both occurrences, and is **not** text-layer damage — the surrounding letters are
  clean (p. 164, folio 153). Recorded so that a consolidator does not silently normalise it or read it
  as a different saint (p. 164, folio 153).
- **"Pir Baba" is a bare name inside a bibliography, and the archive has one row only.** The book gives
  no place, no given name, no date and no silsila (p. 169, folio 158). The archive row
  `shrine-of-pir-baba-syed-ali-tirmizi` is the only candidate, but the evidence for it is a two-word
  string inside a German title, and a human may prefer to drop the mapping entirely (p. 169,
  folio 158).
- **Interim toponym convention (a) fires several times here; each is recorded reversibly with the row
  named.** (1) **Nurpur / Nurpur Shahan** (p. 163, folio 152; p. 165, folio 154; p. 169, folio 158) —
  the site of Mama Ji's tomb and langar, and also the second of Einzmann's three pilgrimage places
  (p. 169, folio 158). **If the toponym ruling reverses, the row it would take is `bari-imam`**, whose
  location field reads "Bari Imam, Islamabad, Islamabad Capital Territory"; the circumstantial support
  is that Barri Imam's ‘urs is the occasion that brings crowds to Nurpur (p. 164, folio 153). **But the
  book nowhere says that Barri Imam's shrine is at Nurpur**, and within this chunk the shrine at Nurpur
  is Mama Ji's, not his (p. 164, folio 153). No id is assigned now. (2) **Rawalpindi / ‘Pindi**
  (p. 163, folio 152; p. 166, folio 155) — "among the shrines of Rawalpindi", plural and unnamed
  (p. 166, folio 155); **if the ruling reverses, no single row follows**, because the archive carries
  five Rawalpindi rows (`darbar-sakhi-shah-chan-charagh`, `eidgah-sharif`, `krishna-mandir-kabari-bazar`,
  `lal-kurti-temple-balmiki-mandir-rawalpindi`, `valmiki-swamiji-mandir-gracy-lines-rawalpindi`) and
  nothing points to any one of them (p. 166, folio 155). (3) **Lahore** (p. 171, folio 160; p. 174,
  folio 163; p. 177, folio 166) — likewise, roughly twenty Lahore rows and no discriminating detail,
  except where the text names Data Sahib, which is mapped (p. 178, folio 167). (4) **Islamabad**
  (p. 169, folio 158) — four candidate rows (`bari-imam`, `golra-sharif`,
  `gurdwara-sahib-saidpur-guru-nanak-dev-ji`, `ram-mandir-saidpur-ram-kund-mandir`) and nothing to
  choose between them (p. 169, folio 158). (5) **Mansehra / Hazara District** (p. 166, folio 155) —
  two rows lie near Mansehra (`shiv-mandir-chiti-ghati`, `gurdwara-chhevin-patshahi-chitti-gatti`);
