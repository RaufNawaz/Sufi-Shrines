## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Darbars as necessary infrastructure.** "pirs generally need to be associated with a space. Almost
  all pirs of substance have a seat of power, or a place on the map where they can be located" (p. 60,
  folio 49); "Darbars are part of the necessary compromise between accessibility and remoteness"
  (pp. 60–61, folios 49–50), which "enables pirs not only to stay in business but also to effect the
  transformations their customers desire of them" (p. 61, folio 50).
- **Building as fundraising.** "The plans for expansion in Atroshi are a means of eliciting donations
  of labour and wealth" (p. 61, folio 50); other Bangladeshi pirs' darbars are "a prelude to greater
  things, if their devotees can only keep the faith and keep the offerings rolling in" (p. 61,
  folio 50).
- **Architecture at Atroshi.** "Certain kinds of construction activities, such as building mosques and
  madrasahs, are regarded as meritorious acts"; the mosque "presently under construction at his lodge
  is his final work, as well as being the greatest task he has undertaken"; Atroshi "always seems more
  like a building site than a sacred place", with "Makeshift elements ... juxtaposed with concrete
  formations" (p. 61, folio 50). **No building dates are given: (not stated).**
- **Gendered access at tombs.** "there are very often gendered regimens in dealing with entombed and
  living pirs. Women, thought to be impure, are often not permitted to approach them directly"
  (p. 64, folio 53).
- **Terminology at Nagore.** Sufi saints' shrines "were recorded in the earlier gazetteers as pallis
  (mosques), but the term, in fact, denotes a dargah (court, shrine) of a Sufi saint. Even today, the
  Tamils refer to the dargah of a Sufi as a palli" (Hemingway 1906:809; Shariff 1921:121) (p. 67,
  folio 56).
- **Shared ritual repertoire.** "The practice of offering flowers, sweetmeats and food, the conduct of
  worship, the playing of musical instruments, although disapproved of by Islam reformists, are all
  practices shared by Muslims with Hindus in local traditions" (p. 67, folio 56). Some shrines have
  sacred plants or sacred groves, and many "are purified with water in a way reminiscent of the
  pouring of libation water on idols by Hindus" (p. 66, folio 55).
- **Land endowment at Nagore.** "According to legend, the saint cured the king's illness and in return
  the monarch endowed him with land as an offering of gratitude" (p. 69, folio 58). **No acreage, no
  date, no later history of the grant: (not stated).**
- **The town's economy.** "In the early nineteenth century, the port of Nagore played a prominent role
  in the import-export business (ibid.), far exceeding that of its neighbouring town, Nagapatnam. In
  due course, however, Nagore lost its economic significance as a port, but it continued to flourish
  as the abode of the holy saint" (p. 69, folio 58).
- **The tomb and the start of pilgrimage.** "A mausoleum was constructed over the grave and people
  began venerating the site of burial. Thus, the pilgrimage to Nagore Andavar's tomb was first
  initiated by his son, Yusef Saheb" (p. 71, folio 60), and has since been kept up "throughout the
  Indian subcontinent and beyond it, in Singapore and Sri Lanka" (p. 71, folio 60).
- **Substitute-hajj merit.** Among Tamil-speaking Muslims: "Those who cannot afford to pay their visit
  to Mecca, seven visits to the shrine of Nagore-e-Sharif conveys the same merit as the hajj" (Rahim
  1971:21) (p. 71, folio 60). More (1993:91) dissents, holding that people visit Nagore and Muthupet
  "to wash away their sins" (p. 72, folio 61).
- **The ʿurs, and its local name.** "The pilgrims to the tomb of Nagore Andavar come to attend the
  annual 'urs of the saint. The 'urs is celebrated in commemoration of the anniversary of the saint's
  death" (p. 72, folio 61). In south India and Sri Lanka "the annual festivals are known as 'Kanduri'
  by the Tamil-speaking Muslims" (p. 72, folio 61); the word is "derived from the Persian word for
  tablecloth", denotes a feast or commemorative meal in Indonesia (Cyril 1978:540), and is defined as
  "a feast or ceremony observed in honour of holy personages" (Moini 1932:336) (p. 72, folio 61).
- **Duration and calendar.** "The Kanduri festival of the Nagore Andavar lasts fourteen days, starting
  from the first day of Jamathul Akhir, the sixth month of the Muslim calendar year" (p. 73,
  folio 62); the flags "remain flying on the minarets for fourteen days until the completion of the
  Kanduri festival" (p. 78, folio 67).
- **The five minaret flags and who supplies them.** "Four flags (kodi in Tamil) are received from the
  Nagapatnam Muslim jama'at. They are traditionally sent from Singapore by devotees of the Nagore
  Andavar, while the fifth flag is donated by the descendants of the then Maratha kings of Tanjore."
  After the fatiha ritual "the five flags are carried on the back of the dargah elephant to
  Nagore-e-Sharif. Later, the flags are displayed in the Mera Palli (mosque) at Nagapatnam" (p. 73,
  folio 62).
- **Flag colours and their purposes.** "The flags used during the festivals of Qadir Wali of Nagore
  and the saint Sayyid Abdul Qadir Jilani of Baghdad are lal (saffron) and hara (green)
  respectively" (p. 72, folio 61); and "the devotees offer green flags for success in any adventurous
  acts and for prosperity, the saffron flags as an offering to the saint" (p. 72, folio 61). See
  Doubts.
- **Flags outside the ʿurs.** The flag rituals are observed "not only during the occasion of the
  anniversary of the saint's death but also at the commencement of any auspicious occasion, such as
  khatna (circumcision) for boys and the marriage ceremony of either boys or girls"; flags are taken
  in procession from the devotee's house and "hoisted on a tree known as Jande ka Jahad" (p. 72,
  folio 61). "It is believed that the flag rituals give the merit of ziarat (visitation) to the dargah
  of Nagore" (p. 72, folio 61).
- **Remote participation, cash offerings and a bank account.** "The devotees who are unable to attend
  the flag festival at Nagore can send offerings in either cash or kind through the pilgrims who do
  attend it. Nowadays, the dargah management has adopted a new method: they invite the devotees to
  offer donations to the dargah trust bank account and, in return, they send tabarruk (sanctified
  food) to the devotees after the completion of the flag festival of the saint" (p. 73, folio 62).
  Saheb calls this "one instance of sacred exchange" (p. 73, folio 62).
- **A fixed fee, stated as a figure.** "Devotees who desire to hoist the flag on the dargah Minaret
  have to pay a fee (kanikai) of Rs 1000 as a gift to the dargah office. Such devotee flags are
  hoisted daily on the big minaret, at 6 p.m." (p. 74, folio 63). **No year is attached to the Rs 1000
  figure: (not stated).**
- **What is embroidered and printed on the flags.** "the Zulfikar emblem ... (two swords and one
  shield)" (p. 73, folio 62); the words "'Ya Nagore! Murade Hassil' ('Oh! Lord Nagore! My vows are
  fulfilled')"; and "sandalwood paste palm prints of the devotees' hands" (p. 74, folio 63).
- **The faqir jama'as.** "It is said that the saint has 404 disciples as his followers in his
  'spiritual' team. All these are divided into four groups, each of which consists of one hundred
  members along with a headman. The four groups are the Banwa, Malang, Mondal and Jalali" (p. 74,
  folio 63). "In due course, the local mendicant priest association, the Rafai jama'a, was added to
  the earlier four mendicant groups of Yusef Saheb. Thus, at present, five mendicant groups attend the
  saint's Kanduri festival" (p. 75, folio 64).
- **Arrival times and marks of each group.** Banwa jama'a arrive at 8 a.m., "honoured with garlands,
  the cannon (thop) is fired and the musical pipes are played", then "get their heads tonsured", hence
  "mutte faqir (tonsured mendicant priests)"; "They claim higher social status than the other four
  mendicant groups." Malang jama'a arrive at 12 p.m. and "grow their hair long", hence "jhata faqir"
  (p. 75, folio 64). Mondal faqir "enter the dargah precincts at 3 p.m." and play the "danka (drum)
  while going out to preach Islam", hence "Danka Faqir jama'a"; Jalali Faqir "carry along with them a
  kombu (horn)", hence "Kombu Faqir jama'a"; Rafai jama'a "play with the guruj (mace) during the
  festival in order to entertain the public", hence "Guruj Faqir jama'a" (p. 76, folio 65).
- **Chilla and ziarat.** The five jama'as "perform chilla (forty days' meditation) at Nagore-e-Sharif.
  The main duty of the five jama'as is to conduct the rituals at the saint's anniversary fair and to
  perform ziarat in the evening, during their stay on the dargah premises" (p. 76, folio 65). Since
  "the tomb of the saint Nagore Andavar was first erected, these saintly followers have visited the
  fair annually, to pay their homage to their spiritual master" (p. 75, folio 64).
- **The procession, and who pays for each float.** It "starts at midday at Nagapatnam town, from the
  Meerapalli mosque", after the fatiha ritual (p. 76, folio 65). Five floats, "conceived of as
  offerings: a big chariot (peria ratham), a small chariot (chinna ratham), a model of Mohammad
  Ghouse's ship (testa), a model of Mohammad Gwalior's ship and the model palanquin (pallaq) offered
  by the merchant caste" (p. 76, folio 65). The big chariot's expenses "are borne by the Nagapatnam
  Muslim jama'at"; the model palanquin "is paid for by the Hindu merchant group and this explains its
  name, the Chettiar palanquin"; Mohammad Ghouse's ship "is funded by individual contributions made by
  the devotees of the locality" (p. 76, folio 65).
- **The five named minarets and their flags.** First flag on "the Talamattu Manora minaret, located
  towards the head of the saint's shrine complex"; second on "the Sahib minaret"; "The Muthubaq
  minaret, which is the third one located on the north side of the dargah"; "The fourth, Ottu minaret
  receives the flag carried by devotees residing in Singapore. The fifth minaret flag is carried by
  the model of Mohammad Gwalior's ship" (p. 76, folio 65). The minarets are five in number and were
  "built by the Maratha ruler Pratap Simha, Tanjavur, Tamil Nadu" (p. 75, folio 64).
- **Devotees' own models, and what happens to them afterwards.** "small ships (cuppal), boats
  (padagai), ship engines, minarets (manora), flower palanquins (poopallaq) and model fish (minu), all
  of which belong to the local area ... driven by the devotees, to represent the fulfilment of their
  vows to the Nagore Andavar. After the procession the models are kept at home, imbued by the power of
  their sacred contact with the shrine, and they are used again only at the saint's next annual
  festival" (p. 76, folio 65). The tradition "is perpetuated from generation to generation by the
  descendants of the devotees" (p. 77, folio 66).
- **Sound and smoke in the procession.** An "aloe-wood cart (ud-vandi) which releases the smoke of the
  aloe wood"; at the back, "Western musical bands (sanai) and double-reed pipes (nadaswaram) are
  played, representing the 'Hindu' part of the festival" (p. 77, folio 66). See Doubts on "Western".
- **The route, street by street.** "from the Meera Palli mosque and passes through Abhiram Ammal
  Sannadi Street, Neela Dathchayani Ammam West Street, East Street, North Street, Nethaji Road and
  Hospital Road, traversing the town of Nagapatnam. From there the procession proceeds to the town of
  Nagore through streets such as Kunjali Mericar Street, Miya Street, Pandha Saliar Street, Theru
  Palli Street and Sayyadu Palli Street, until it finally reaches the illuminated west gate of the
  dargah, Alankara Vasil" (p. 77, folio 66).
- **The flag-hoisting itself.** "The five flags are received with great honour by the dargah management
  and hoisted on the five minarets at 8.30 p.m., after the performance of the fatiha prayer. This
  ritual is known as thuvaja rohanam (flag hoisting)" (p. 78, folio 67).
- **Visitor numbers, as the book states them.** The hoisting "is witnessed by tens of thousands of
  pilgrims from different parts of the region and beyond it" (p. 78, folio 67). **That is the only
  attendance figure in the chunk; no count, no year: (not stated).**
- **Cannon.** The flag-hoisting "is honoured by the firing of the dargah cannon" (p. 78, folio 67);
  the cannon (thop) is also fired to greet the Banwa jama'a (p. 75, folio 64).
- **Fireworks and the days that follow.** The fireworks display "takes place a week after the
  flag-hoisting ritual, on the eighth day of the month of Jamathul Akhir, starting at 8.30 p.m. and
  lasting for two hours" (p. 78, folio 67). "The following day, on the 9th Jamathul Akhir, the
  Nagapatnam jama'at assembles at the shed for the 'sandalwood paste anointment', carried by the
  koodu" (p. 78, folio 67).
- **Installation of the ritual pir.** "That evening the ceremonial installation of the ritual saint
  takes place. At 10 p.m., a disciple from the Malang Faqir jama'a is selected to be 'saint' (pir) in
  a ritual which is referred to as 'the installation of the pir'. It is performed at the place where
  the funerary prayers over the saint's body were originally offered (janaze ke namaz)" (p. 78,
  folio 67). A plate caption adds that "The devotees are blessed by the 'ritual saint' (pir) through
  theertam, sandalwood paste, lemon, rose petals and amulets" (p. 78, folio 67).
- **Dargah management as an institution** appears throughout as the acting body: it welcomes the

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- Landell Mills reports, as a disciple's claim and not as fact, that "Atroshi was a wilderness when he
  arrived there with little to his name", calling this claim "erroneously but compellingly" made and
  "a narrative associated with many other pirs in South Asia (Werbner 1995, 1996)" (p. 60, folio 49).
- The son of a Motijheel 'friend' told Landell Mills, "somewhat jokingly": "My father was very angry
  because he was always an atheist, but he escaped from a plane crash, and as he was running from the
  wreck he kept seeing that man before him" (p. 60, folio 49).
- Saheb reports from "The hagiological literature" that Sahul Hameed "came into the 'material' world
  through 'the divine message of Allah to his parents'" and "performed miracles (karamat) even while
  still a foetus in his mother's womb" (p. 67, folio 56).
- He "was said to have been born on a Friday and to have been named Sahul Hameed by God's messenger,
  Khidr" (p. 67, folio 56).
- Blessing of the childless couple: "endowed with divine power, blessed them with a son of great
  spiritual excellence who would become his disciple" — Yusef Saheb (p. 69, folio 58).
- The cure of the king: "According to legend, the saint cured the king's illness" (p. 69, folio 58).
- The saint "predicted his death by conveying to his son, Yusef Saheb, that he was about to leave this
  mundane world of appearances", revealing the funeral rites and "the place of his desired burial"
  (pp. 69 and 71, folios 58 and 60).
- The reply from the grave: the saint told his son that "if [he] were to get a reply from the saint's
  tomb, [he] should continue his stay at Nagore; otherwise [he] could leave the place"; Yusef Saheb
  "visited the grave on the third day, conveyed his greetings and got a reply from his father's grave"
  (p. 71, folio 60).
- Posthumous power: "Even after the death of the saint Nagore Andavar, his miraculous powers did not
  disappear. As soon as his tomb was consecrated, prayers there in the name of God and the saint
  brought devotees relief from pain and cure from personal afflictions" (p. 71, folio 60).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Landell Mills: material growth and spiritual reputation grow together.** "their imagined inner
  being, their iconic surface and their devotees' miraculous experience of them all grow in tandem",
  and "The acquisition of worldly impedimenta is inevitably taken as a sign of hitherto undisclosed
  spiritual pre-eminence" (p. 60, folio 49). He puts the case counterfactually: "It seems unlikely
  that the pir of Atroshi would be as easily attributed with the spiritual powers he is purported to
  possess without the symbolic and material edifices that are taking shape around him" (p. 60,
  folio 49).
- **Landell Mills: the hidden pir is parasitic on the visible one.** An orthodox 'alim told him, of
  those who publicise themselves, "Those who are friends of God keep quiet about it [jara oli'allah
  tara chupchap thake]" (p. 60, folio 49); but "While people may imagine 'hidden masters' ... these
  are only comprehensible as the unseen counterparts of a more visible phenomenon" (p. 60, folio 49).
- **Landell Mills: the pir's career is the exploration of a contradiction** "between the need to be not
  only active and accessible, but also quietist and aloof"; pirs, "Often described as 'living
  scriptures' (kitab)", are "products and prisoners of the imperative to make palpable the
  simultaneous presence and invisibility of divine blessing" (p. 60, folio 49).
- **Landell Mills: embodiment and its metonymic extension.** "Pirs embody a divine order, poised at the
  brink of the world"; "the attributes of pirs are treated as interactive extensions of their authors'
  powerful personalities"; "The identity of a pir is a product of interaction. The pir's embodiment
  represents the social relations which create him as a holy man" (p. 61, folio 50). The extension
  runs to "spoken words, rituals, small gifts, buildings or even territory" (p. 61, folio 50).
- **Landell Mills: living pir, dead pir.** "The unfolding career of a living pir ideally constitutes
  the creation of the saintly identity of a dead pir. It gradually confers a power which is infinite
  and inexhaustible, an unlimited good" (pp. 61–62, folios 50–51), "achieved through the effective
  suffusion of inanimate objects with anthropomorphic qualities" (p. 62, folio 51). In a note he adds
  that "'saint' could only be used as a translation of 'pir' when the pir concerned is a dead person
  whose sanctity is less contested than that of the often controversial living pirs" (p. 63,
  folio 52).
- **Landell Mills against Dumont.** Pirs' "uniquely individual identities are assets which are
  indispensable to their aspirations; they are not merely the by-product of challenging a
  conservative, holistic social order, as Dumont has suggested for the Hindu 'world renouncer' (Dumont
  1957)" (p. 62, folio 51).
- **Landell Mills endorsing Waghorne (1994).** He takes up her claim that the "'vital surfaces' of
  objects (and of holy persons like South Asian divine kings or pirs) have been neglected by religious
  studies and by anthropology as a result of the Victorian bowdlerisation of sanctity and the
  concomitant devaluation of magic", quoting her at 1994:258 (p. 62, folio 51).
- **Landell Mills: scripturalist and orientalist devaluations are the same move.** "The logocentric
  bias of the latter regards Sufism as degenerate, a view endorsed by some orientalist scholars
  seeking the 'essence' of the religion in its scriptural past, just as clerics find a perfect Islam
  in the Qur'an and Hadiths. Both types of scholar devalue local practice in favour of the 'great
  tradition'" (p. 62, folio 51). Against this: "Sufi traditions represent a diverse set of practices"
  whose Islamic identity has been interrogated because the notions of spiritual knowledge as power
  they purvey "seem to be less exclusively Muslim" (p. 62, folio 51).
- **Landell Mills on the Prophet as the anchor and the limit.** "It is upon his unsurpassable
  uniqueness that the religion hinges" (p. 62, folio 51); Sufis, "by combining in themselves human and
  divine inspiration ... flirt with and thereby re-emphasise the boundaries that hedge the Prophet as
  a singular figure" (p. 62, folio 51), leaving "an ambiguity and transgressive potential which Sufism
  can both contain and exploit" (p. 63, folio 52).
- **Landell Mills's closing formulation.** "Sufi Islam requires the corporeal (and by extension the
  anthropomorphic 'hardware') to describe divinely inspired spiritual immanence. At the same time, it
  evokes the transcendence of the divine by attempting the impossible" (p. 63, folio 52).
- **Landell Mills's notes as argument.** Note 1 restricts his use of 'anthropomorphism' to "the
  attribution of a non-human object with human intentionality", citing Boyer (1996), and declines to
  treat it as "a psychological process in its own right" (p. 63, folio 52). Note 2 calls Eaton (1993)
  "The most authoritative recent account of the Islamisation of Bengal", who "demystifies some of the
  mythical associations between pirs and territory by explaining the conversion of Bengali Muslims as
  connected to the eastward spread of rice cultivation on the 'Bengal frontier'" (p. 63, folio 52).
  Note 5 cites Ewing (1980) on "the importance of dream communication between Pakistani pirs and their
  disciples" and on the pir's visage as a focus of meditation (p. 63, folio 52). Note 6 says "The
  Atroshi pir's practice of darshan seems also to borrow from that of Mughal emperors, as described by
  Cohn (1987), and by extension of the Nawabs" (p. 64, folio 53).
- **Landell Mills on Islamism and on intra-Muslim polemic.** By 'Islamist' he means Jama'at-e Islami,
  seeking "a top-down assertion of Islamic rule", and Tabliq Jama'at, seeking "a bottom-up
  reconversion of the society"; "Both organisations derive in certain respects from Sufism but are
  antagonistic towards pirs" (p. 64, folio 53). He reports that in Chittagong district those holding
  that the Prophet is constituted of light (nur) "characterise themselves as 'Sunni' ... against those
  they call 'Wahabi'", whose belief that the Prophet was made of clay (mathi toyri) "is seen as
  impudent (be-adab)" (p. 64, folio 53).
- **Landell Mills on rulers and class in Bengal.** "Whether Mughal or local, rulers were always
  upper-class, Urdu-speaking (ashraf) Muslims rather than indigenous (ajlaf/bangalee) Muslims"
  (p. 64, folio 53).
- **Saheb's framing thesis: shrines as inter-religious institutions.** "The liberal attitude of Sufism
  and its tolerance of other religions have made its shrines centres of inter-religious cooperation.
  The pir-murid (master-disciple) relationship is an open one, and hence Sufi lodges in South Asia are
  places of worship for communities of both Muslims and Hindus" (p. 66, folio 55). A single saint can
  therefore be "a common saint for Hindus as well as Muslims, and his lodge may become a shared
  religious centre which maintains local communal amity" (p. 66, folio 55).
- **Saheb declines to call the Hindu elements borrowings.** "It might seem that Hindu devotees'
  involvement and participation in a shrine's activities legitimise 'Hindu' practices in the dargah's
  sacred performances. Yet the origin and current provenance of various customs is not so clearly
  identifiable" (p. 67, folio 56). Of the flags specifically he writes: "Historically, the flag
  festival may have been borrowed from local Hindu customary celebrations, although the use of flags
  is common in Islam too" (p. 73, folio 62).
- **Saheb: the festival makes locality sacred.** The cultural fusions "are modes of incorporating and
  sacralising an inter-religious community and its spaces, joined in celebrating the anniversary of
  the saint's death" (p. 67, folio 56); the procession brings "the residential areas of the town into
  contact with the sacred emblems of the dargah" (p. 76, folio 65), and the aloe-wood smoke "may also
  be conceived of as a way of sanctifying the residential areas of the locality" (p. 77, folio 66).
- **Saheb: distance is overcome by flag rituals performed elsewhere.** "devotees who are unable to
  attend the flag festival at Nagore can perform the same festival at their respective native places
  by way of offering fatiha in a manner similar to that at the Nagore dargah", a tradition observed
  "throughout south India and outside the country, in Singapore and Sri Lanka" (pp. 72–73, folios
  61–62); "flag rituals provide different spaces, which are, in turn, linked to the Nagore sacred
  shrine complex. Thus, the shrine complex revitalises the wider social world in time and space
  through the flag festival of the saint" (p. 73, folio 62).
- **Saheb on the two origin stories of the flags, reported and not adjudicated.** Version one, from
  "the Tanjore District Records (Hemingway 1906)", makes the flags a royal Maratha sign "of victory
  and prosperity", so that a richly decorated flagpost "would mean a rich and prosperous dargah
  commanding royal patronage", "a sign of pomp and pride" (p. 73, folio 62). Version two, "told by the
  dargah management and descendants of the saint", makes the flags "the symbolic representation of
  Ali", confirming "that the descendants are the true preachers of the message of God" (p. 73,
  folio 62). Saheb's own reading is that "These multivocal associations explain why one can observe a
  mixture of faiths under the combined shadow of the flags" (p. 73, folio 62).
- **Saheb on the fusion in the procession itself.** The hoisting and carrying of flags is "an
  unselfconscious fusion of Hindu as well as Islamic rituals": the Islamic rites "include recitation
  of Qur'anic verses and observance of the fatiha ritual, whereas the procession with its models and
  floats is reminiscent of other Hindu processions in Tamil Nadu" (p. 73, folio 62).
- **Saheb on the merit economy of shrine pilgrimage.** "These sayings highlight the fact that Sufi
  saints possess the power to bring about not only material blessings but also the same religious
  merit endowed by the pilgrimage to Mecca" (p. 71, folio 60), and "Such beliefs endow Sufi cults with
  continued vitality among the local populace of India wherever such shrines exist" (p. 72, folio 61).
