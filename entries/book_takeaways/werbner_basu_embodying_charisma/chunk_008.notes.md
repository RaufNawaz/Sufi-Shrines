## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Legal status and state control**: "Legally, the shrine falls under the laws of the religious endowment act, which places it under state control vested in the charity commissioner" (p. 131, folio 120).
- **Trust**: the shrine is "Formally managed by a trust consisting of three male members—two Bohras, one Sidi" (p. 131, folio 120).
- **The dispute and its date**: "Conflicts over traditional rights at the shrine originated in the seventies, when the charity commissioner's officers charged the customarily installed mujavirs (officiating faqirs) with misuse of religious donations and subsequently dismissed them from their offices" (p. 131, folio 120).
- **The system before that**: "the shrine was managed on behalf of the jama'at as a whole. Shrine services were carried out by three faqir families taking weekly turns"; "Authority over shrine rituals was held by the elders, several of whom happened to be women" (p. 131, folio 120).
- **Redistribution**: "At the occasions of large shrine festivals, gifts of money and natural products given to the saints were redistributed among the other members of the Sidi jama'at" (p. 131, folio 120).
- **Outcome of state intervention**: "the centralisation of authority in the family of the Sidi trustee and an increasing dependence or marginalisation of other Sidi" (p. 131, folio 120).
- **Visitor numbers and days**: formerly Thursdays, "the day reserved for the memory of the dead", drew the largest crowds "(c. 150–200)"; "nowadays Sundays tend to be even busier" (p. 131, folio 120).
- **Growth of the cult**: formerly "mainly a centre for Sidi faqirs, their families and peasants from the area", but "during the last thirty years the cult following has expanded considerably", adding 'Tribals' (Bhil), Hindus and "an ever increasing number of Muslims" (p. 130, folio 119).
- **Urban pilgrimage**: "In the large industrial centres such as Surat, Bharuch, Baroda or Ahmadabad the dargah has, since the mid-1980s, been discovered as a major destination of weekly pilgrimages"; the followers are "from an urban, lower middle-class milieu, many from the Sunni Bohra caste", including "shopkeepers, small traders, manufacturers, artisans and workers" (pp. 130–131, folios 119–120).
- **Alms outside the trust**: on Thursdays and Sundays "other Sidi women sit at one of the many tombs scattered over the landscape—which do not fall under the direct management of the trust—ready to receive alms (jakat)" (p. 132, folio 121).
- **Who staffs the ritual routine**: the women who "organise rituals and other services mostly belong to the households of men employed by the trust as mujavir" (p. 132, folio 121).
- **Population**: "The Sidi in Gujarat number around 6,000–7,000, and some live in town working as domestics" (p. 130, folio 119).
- **Network of Sidi shrines**: "The Sidi maintain many small shrines all over Gujarat. In each of their settlements there is at least one shrine housing ancestor saints" (p. 130, folio 119).
- **Becoming a faqir**: the role is "explained in two ways: as a spontaneous charismatic calling based on a hukm (order) received from one of the ancestor saints, or as privileged access to shrine service inherited from a kinsman or kinswoman"; the term "includes women as well, unlike the Malang described by Ewing (1984b)" (p. 130, folio 119).
- **Terminology of rank**: "The Sidi address only Sayyid saints with the title pir, whereas the terms murshid or faqir denote a holy person from their own jama'at (caste) or any other non-Sayyid social category (for a variation, see Mayer 1967)" (p. 130, folio 119).
- **Division of ritual labour**: devotees come to "'work' (kam)" with the saints; "Such services (seva) are carried out not by the gaddivaras or by mastans but by the shrine servants called mujavir" (p. 138, folio 127).
- **'Urs defined**: "the 'urs (commemoration of a saint's death), performed on behalf of the black saints", in which the gaddivaras and mastans "assume prominent roles" (p. 138, folio 127).
- **The gaddivaras**: "the ritual head of the cult and representative of the senior saint Bava Gor", who "also emblematically respresents the Sidi caste"; his hotness "is induced by the saint, whose 'throne' (gaddi, pillow) is extremely hot and affects successors instantly" (p. 138, folio 127).
- **The bedi ordeal**: "Called bedi, the term literally denotes the iron rings put around the ankles of a criminal suspect"; "Whereas only male faqirs may perform the bedi divination ritual, accused subjects are frequently women, especially when the accusation concerns witchcraft or adultery" (p. 139, folio 128).
- **Caseload**: "At the time of my fieldwork, every week between ten and thirty bedi cases were brought before the saint. The majority came from towns and big industrial centres" (p. 139, folio 128).
- **Who accuses**: "kharkhana (factory) and shop owners who suspected their employees of stealing money from their businesses, middle-class women suspicious of their maidservants, and several husbands, accompanied by their mothers, who suspected their wives of sexual infidelity" (p. 139, folio 128).
- **Procedure**: "The necessary acts are usually carried out by the Sidi trustee himself"; a purificatory bath behind the shrine renders the suspect pak (pure); clad in a wet loincloth or wet sari the accused has the iron rings put round the ankles, is purified "with burning loban (incense)", swears an oath, and while "a large drum is beaten inside the shrine" runs behind the mujavir towards the tomb — "If the iron rings spring open during the run, the innocence of the suspect is established" (p. 139, folio 128).
- **Limit of the shrine's jurisdiction**: "The punishment of a culprit, however, is beyond the jurisdiction of the shrine. It is left to the accusers to mete out appropriate retribution" (p. 139, folio 128).
- **Vows and offerings for children**: rituals confirm "a vow (mannat), made either by a woman or by a man, in order to secure the birth of a child"; the desire for offspring is "visibly manifested by numerous small, wooden, brightly painted cradles that are decoratively placed upon the frames protecting the tombs of the three saints" (p. 140, folio 129).
- **Mai Mishra ni khichadi**: sponsored by the barren woman or a close relative (usually her mother); "seven Sidi women, who represent the seven sisters of the female saint, consume a dish made from rice and lentils mixed with sugar and ghee"; one woman, "usually a wife of one of the mujavir", organises it and "receives a lump sum from the sponsor which covers the food expenses and a small money gift for each of the participating Sidi women" (p. 140, folio 129).
- **Commensality and niaz**: the seven women eat "'in parda'" and "share the food from one common dish. Their leftovers are afterwards distributed as niaz (blessed food), first to the afflicted woman herself, then to other women devotees. Mai Mishra's khichadi is not shared with men" (p. 140, folio 129).
- **Women's goma**: the women "gather next to the shrine of the female saint and sing songs (jikkar) dedicated to Mai Mishra and her saintly sisters"; one or two dance "while spectators wave rupee notes over the heads of the musicians as a sign of auspiciousness", the afflicted woman presiding (p. 141, folio 130).
- **Bava Habash ni dudh**: at least seven Sidi men "gather in front of Bava Habash's shrine and consume a hot liquid mixture made from milk, ginger and spices"; "the leftover is given as niaz, first to the sponsor and then to other men present", followed by "a short drumming and dancing session… performed by Sidi men" (p. 141, folio 130).
- **Exorcism caseload**: "At the time of fieldwork, between thirty and fifty people suffering from symptoms of spirit possession came to the shrine on Thursdays. They are called hajrivale", the majority women (p. 142, folio 131).
- **Local healers and referral**: faqirs performing healing "are usually based at a small Sidi shrine in an urban neighbourhood"; a healer diagnosing demonic illness "usually prescribes at least five visits to the shrine of Bava Gor", the place counting for more than the healer (p. 142, folio 131).
- **Banishment of the spirit**: "departing spirits are banished to a large tree overshadowing the dargah of Mai Mishra, the female saint. Strands of the patient's hair are nailed to its trunk by a mujavir while he again confirms the final identity of the bhut" (p. 142, folio 131).
- **'Urs and the collapse of hierarchy**: "at the large annual shrine rituals ('urs) hierarchy is temporarily collapsed", signified by "a different style of goma stressing experiences of trance" (p. 143, folio 132).
- **Who travels, and why**: Sidi "travel from all over Gujarat to the shrine of Bava Gor—far in the south by local standards—in order to celebrate the death anniversary of their kulpir (clan saints)", the dancing itself "a major attraction promising a lot of maja"; the good trance is "(hal)", opposed to possession trance "(hajri)" (p. 143, folio 132).
- **Trance conduct and dress**: "men are possessed exclusively by male saints, women by Mai Mishra or another female saint"; a woman so seized wears "the veil put over her face", men "tie a green cloth around the lower parts of their bodies"; carers arrange clothes modestly, give water, "and they keep a lemon over the head of a person entranced too violently" (p. 144, folio 133).
- **Regional shrine hierarchy**: shrines "are connected by spiritual master-disciple bonds (pir-khalifa, pir-murid). Higher and lower saints are linked through a spiritual genealogy (silsila)", with khalifas intermediate between pir and murid (p. 144, folio 133).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The Sidi myth of Bava Gor and the demoness**, "a myth that is often told by the Sidi": the saint comes to Gujarat on the Prophet's command, refuses to fight a woman, restrains his enraged younger brother and sends him "to another hill", and the sister finally subdues Makhan Devi (pp. 134–135, folios 123–124).
- The fire by Bava Gor's tomb marks "a site where Bava Gor is reputed to have meditated for twelve years"; "Bava Gor is cold because he burned his own desire as the fire consumes wood and turns it into cold ash" (p. 134, folio 123).
- "Mai Mishra overwhelmed the demoness by pushing her into the interior of the earth" (p. 135, folio 124).
- "the struggle of Mai Mishra against evil forces is `continously [text-layer?: continuously]` recreated through ritual exorcism" (p. 135, folio 124).
- Karamat is glossed by the chapter as "capacity to perform miracles", part of the inherited charisma claimed by the Sayyid (p. 130, folio 119).
- "the sacred area represented by the tombs is imbued with those saintly powers (karamat) that ultimately provide the most effective means of exorcising spirits" (p. 142, folio 131).
- "The iron rings are seen as signs through which the saint directly communicates the truth to the people. If they do not open, there is no way to deny the accusation" (p. 139, folio 128).
- The saint's "'throne' (gaddi, pillow) is extremely hot and affects successors instantly" (p. 138, folio 127).
- Bava Habash "is said to pursue his enemies in his anger and bring misfortune on their homes and families", and "to react especially fiercely against those who take a favour but neglect to fulfil their promises" (p. 136, folio 125).
- At the 'urs the saints "unite with their living descendants. They step down to earth for a few moments. Such moments are extremely hot" (p. 144, folio 133).

## Arguments and interpretations

- Basu grounds the regional hierarchy in descent: the Sayyid claim inheritable charisma manifested "in shrines managed by their families", while the Sidi, "while acknowledging the ritual superiority of the Sayyid… claim a similar though lower-order charisma transmitted by birth from their African saintly ancestors" (p. 130, folio 119).
- She notes both the persistence of shrine worship and its male control: "Despite systematic attacks by Muslim reformers, ritual worship at saints' shrines remains for many Muslims an expression of true faith. In Gujarat, the hierarchical network connecting shrines is controlled by men" (p. 130, folio 119).
- She reads the state's intervention as a reorganisation of the cult, not merely a legal event: "Whereas the traditional system focused upon the collectivity as a whole, the intervention by state agencies into the formal organisation of the cult resulted in the centralisation of authority in the family of the Sidi trustee" (p. 131, folio 120).
- She marks the cult's distinctiveness: "The Sidi cult differs importantly from other saint cults practised in the area in the emphasis it places upon complementary male and female ritual domains" (p. 131, folio 120).
- Cosmology: "a cosmology that places God (Allah) over and above a hierarchy of saints, humans and (Satanic) demons", the order "structured by Sufi principles of closeness and distance to God that define high (uncha) and low (niche) status" (p. 132, folio 121).
- She borrows Valerio Valeri's (1985:54) 'type'/'token' relation, the deity being "a paragon for empirical actions and subjects…and endows their actions with significance", to make saints and demons "opposed types of positively and negatively evaluated exemplary moral person" (p. 132, folio 121).
- Hot and cold are not fixed values: they "refer metonymically to both physical and mental processes, to fertility, death, gender, power, ecstasy, possession, emotion, purity and impurity… manifestations of a scale of evaluation that depends on relationships and transformation" (p. 132, folio 121).
- "The Sidi hierarchy appears itself as a reversal: the assembly of Sidi saints is divided by gender according to a male-female duality" (p. 132, folio 121).
- She reports Sudhir Kakar's psychoanalytic reading — spirits "are the reification of certain unconscious fantasies of men and women which provoke strong anxiety in the Indian cultural setting" (1982:29) — and then qualifies it with Kapferer (1983), concluding that "demons and malign spirits are reifications of social disorder" (p. 133, folio 122).
- "the healing of possession is seen more as a means of countering a cosmic threat to the hierarchical order than as a cure centred upon the individual patient" (p. 133, folio 122).
- "saints (pirs) are said to be cold because they have overcome the hot nafs (desiring soul), that is, the instinctual, 'animal' nature of humans (Kurin 1984)", while baraka has "positive, cooling effects" (p. 133, folio 122).
- She invokes "fundamental assumptions of femininity in South Asia, shared by both Hindus and Muslims", under which menstruation, pregnancy and parturition are hot and impure, heat being "a necessary precondition of female fertility" that must be transformed into "a cold state of nurturing motherhood" (p. 134, folio 123).
- The myth inverts the shrine's spatial order: "Whereas the spatial order of the shrine manifests the superiority of male over female, the myth establishes an inversion of female and male positions"; on a cosmic level "male actions of power and war are attributed to women" (p. 135, folio 124).
- Magaj is offered as the key emotional term: "Magaj is a key term used to refer to experiences of the self embedded in a mind-body-emotion ensemble", resembling "English notions of temperament" (p. 136, folio 125).
- The ritual economy is emotional: "The ritual routine of the shrine is designed to transform dukh into maja", where dukh encompasses "hunger, unemployment, illnesses, marital problems (divorce), imprisonment of family members, and material and physical injuries inflicted by communal riots" (p. 136, folio 125).
- Goma is claimed as a caste monopoly with an African etymology: "possibly derived from Swahili ngoma, dance, and also known as dammal in Hindi, from dam, healing breath"; it "refers to rhythms and movements not practised otherwise by Gujaratis" and "is restricted to Sidi as agents of joy" (p. 137, folio 126).
- Basu reads the Sidi's self-description as an inversion of value: they devalue "'aql (reason)", call themselves "'people without reason' ('aql vagar loko)" and "'mad people' (ganda loko)", and thereby "claim a superior status as dilvale or 'people of the heart'", also "'Badshah loko' or kingly people" — "Sidi are 'kings of the heart'" (p. 137, folio 126).
- Mast is linked to majzub "(intoxicated or mad with divine love), as Frembgen and Ewing show in this volume" — an explicit cross-reference to two other chapters of the same volume (p. 137, folio 126).
- The ordeal is read as class power: "Bava Gor supports the more powerful against the less powerful; the dominant—patrons, employers—against the dominated—labourers, clients, and women. Seen in this context, the dargah is a place where class-based social control is publicly exercised" (p. 140, folio 129).
- The fertility rites are placed in a wider comparative field: such "thermodynamic 'tempering' and 'framing' of heat in coolness for the sake of fertility are widely found throughout South Asia (see Beck 1969; Werbner 1986, 1990a; Fuller and Logan 1985; O'Flaherty 1980) and even beyond it (R.Werbner 1989)" (p. 141, folio 130).
- Seva rituals build hierarchy: "worldly and cosmic hierarchies of superior Sidi and inferior cult followers are constructed", and "The lowest position at the shrine is accorded to Hindu and Tribal women afflicted by spirit possession" (p. 143, folio 132).
- The 'urs suspends one hierarchy while enforcing another: "while the hierarchy of saints and humans is collapsed in trance, the gender hierarchy is maintained and emphasised" (pp. 143–144, folios 132–133).
- Three styles of goma correspond to three relationships — small-version sacrifice, mass trance, and a third performed "in the context of the wider shrine regional network hierarchy" — and "each type of goma involves an inversion of hierarchy at a different level" (p. 144, folio 133).
- The chapter closes by nesting the cult: the shrine "constitutes hierarchical space in which Sidi faqirs are constructed as superior to cult followers. At the same time, it is encompassed by a wider hierarchical system of regional shrines" (p. 144, folio 133).

## Doubts

- **This is a zero-mapping chunk and that is the finding, not a gap in the work.** All 169 rows of `shrine_index.tsv` were checked against every saint, shrine, caste, settlement and cited author on pp. 130–144 (folios 119–133); the chapter's entire subject matter is Gujarat, India, and no row corresponds to any of it.
- **The one genuine mapping question a human must rule on is Shiva.** "the Hindu god Shiva" is named at p. 134 (folio 123) purely as a cultural stereotype of the renouncer.
