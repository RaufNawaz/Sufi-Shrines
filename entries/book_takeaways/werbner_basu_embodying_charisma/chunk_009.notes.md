## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The categories of gift in Basu's account**: nazrana, "that is, tributary gifts such as those
  given by a client to a patron", "formally the possession of the saint…usually redistributed at the
  shrine and used for the benefit of all"; "jakat (alms)", "sadaqa" and
  "`qurbáni [text-layer?: qurbani]` (animal sacrifices)" (p. 145, folio 134).
- **Langar**: "blessed food handed out to all pilgrims and supplicants at the shrine"; "the
  distribution of langar to devotees is a common practice at higher-status shrines, and especially
  so during the festive days of the 'urs celebration" (p. 145, folio 134).
- **An explicit negative at the shrine of Bava Gor**: "no langar is provided at the shrine of Bava
  Gor. Instead, niaz is given by individual cult followers in fulfilment of a vow"; niaz "refers
  generally to food consecrated by sacrifice", and although shared by whoever is present "it is
  explicitly prepared for and directed to the Sidi as recipients" (p. 145, folio 134).
- **Eid**: "at the Islamic festival of eid celebrated at the end of Ramadan, the month of fasting…
  other Muslims visit Sidi hamlets and distribute parts of a sacrificial ram" (p. 145, folio 134).
- **'Urs service as a marker of rank**: the asymmetry between Rifa'i Sayyid pirs and Sidi faqirs "is
  revealed particularly at 'urs celebrations at Rifa'i shrines when Sidi men and women perform
  ritual services for the pir" (p. 145, folio 134).
- **Succession among the Rifa'i**, quoted from van der Veer: "in each generation one member of the
  family which inherits the sajjada nishin (the spiritual succession of Sayyid Ahmad Kabir Rifa'i)
  becomes a mastan", and "a mastan is clearly beyond the Law but…he can never be head of the family,
  sajjada nishin" (van der Veer 1992:560) (p. 146, folio 135).
- **Money thrown at goma performances**: at Sayyid shrines the comic goma is danced "in a courtyard
  of Sayyid shrines by relatively large groups of male Sidi dancers", and "the audience interacts
  with the performers by throwing money (coins and notes) into the circle" (p. 147, folio 136).
- **Mama Ji's langar in his lifetime**: he "gave comfort and advice to supplicants seeking help,
  prayed for them, and kept a langar (centre for communal distribution of food) to feed the poor"
  (p. 155, folio 144).
- **Tenure of the Rawalpindi darbar**: "The living-room with anteroom at the ganda nala in which the
  saint lived is part of the waqf (religious endowment) property of the adjacent Jama' Masjid. Mama
  Ji's followers collect the monthly rent for this property among themselves" (p. 157, folio 146).
- **Its acquisition**: "In 1978/1979 his followers rented one of the rooms behind the Jama' Masjid on
  his behalf, situated in a very narrow lane which falls steeply away to a dirty channel (ganda
  nala), a place now called Mama Ji Sarkar Mohalla" (p. 153, folio 142).
- **Change of name after death**: "The house served as a darbar and baithak for the pir and his
  devotees during the saint's lifetime; after his physical death it is now called an astan."
  Opposite it, "just across the narrow lane, there is the half-open langar, built with wooden planks
  over the dirty nala" (p. 158, folio 147).
- **The chillagah**: a "very narrow and low partitioned area, lockable with a metal door", decorated
  with a Sayyid Ishrat fresco of a Punjabi landscape, "was the saint's chillagah (place of
  seclusion) and is now used as a place either for sleeping or for smoking hashish"; pigeons,
  "traditionally associated with saint's abodes in South and West Asia", are sometimes kept above it
  (p. 158, folio 147).
- **Spatial hierarchy inside the darbar**: the saint's bed in the right-hand corner "with a space
  reserved for women between the foot of the bed and the door"; "the place of the mutawalli/mujawir
  on duty is between the bedhead and the fireplace"; "privileged seats for high-ranking persons are
  near the end wall, close to the mutawalli", and "the lowest-ranking seats are, of course, near the
  door" (pp. 158–159, folios 147–148).
- **Devotional objects donated by murids**, "from colourful kitsch knick-knacks to a kashkol
  (begging bowl), shanka (conch), four photographs of the pir adorned with flower garlands, and
  various pictures" (p. 159, folio 148).
- **A shrine-side image economy**: "Up to 1989, Ishrat rented a part of a shop adjacent to the astan
  where he sold hand-painted portraits of the saint…as well as individual portraits based upon
  passport photographs"; the portraits are placed in homes and shops and "have a popularising, if
  not advertising, effect", one bus driver displaying his pir's photograph "with a plastic board
  giving his name, honorary titles and address in the front window of his vehicle"
  (p. 159, folio 148).
- **A dated building campaign**: "the mujawirs decided to order a change of the living-room into a
  shish mahal (lit. 'palace of mirrors')…So in February/March 1990, the local ustad (master), Shabir
  Ahmad, and his shagird (apprentice), Mohammad Amin, carried out extensive mosaic work, using
  motifs like the Kaaba, the tomb of the Holy Prophet in Medina, the shrine of Barri Imam,
  calligraphies with different blessing formulas, and the depiction of flower gardens in the Mughal
  style"; mirror mosaic is said to have become popular "in the time of the emperor Shah Jahan
  (1628–1658)" (p. 159, folio 148).
- **The cheragh-khana**: the langar entrance is "marked by a glass case on a base built with
  mortar…used as a cheragh-khana (oil-lamp house)", containing "five oil lamps symbolising the Holy
  Family" (p. 159, folio 148).
- **Langar personnel and provision**: "the mujawirs, cheragh-walas (attendants looking after the oil
  lamps) and kismatgars (servants) offer food and tea as well as the huqqa (water-pipe) to the
  visitors; hashish is also consumed extensively"; chickens or quails are kept in a hutch and
  "before a mela (fair) one or two goats are fattened in advance of being butchered"
  (p. 159, folio 148).
- **Visitor numbers**: "From morning until evening on an average day ten to fifteen persons—mostly
  men but usually also a few women—were constantly present in the darbar. The total number of
  visitors per day was around fifty to a hundred" (p. 160, folio 149).
- **Social composition and patronage**: visitors were "mostly members of the urban unprivileged
  classes, including labourers, craftworkers, small employees, drug addicts, unemployed and the poor
  generally", while "acts of patronage mainly came from a few persons belonging to the middle
  classes"; because Mama Ji "was not a Sayyid…non-adherents see him in a lower position within the
  hierarchy of saints" (p. 161, folio 150).
- **Offerings**: "As nazr (offerings) they donated money and brought food to him, especially eggs,
  because he liked eggs very much", which the mujawirs read as "bahut garm asar ('very' hot' food as
  classified in the local folk medicine, derived from the classical Galenic version)" and as a sign
  of his "'hot state' of intoxication with God" (p. 161, folio 150).
- **Care of the saint's body**: the mujawirs did "everything for him, pushing strands of hair back
  from his forehead, fanning him, wiping his nose, putting a blanket around his shoulders, massaging
  him, feeding him, removing his faeces"; "the old man was confined to his bed for the last six
  years of his life" (p. 161, folio 150).
- **Weekly assembly and music**: "each Thursday evening, and often on other weekdays as well, a
  mehfil (assembly) with qawwali was held in front of a congregation of twenty, thirty or even fifty
  people. If musicians were not present then cassettes by the Sabri Brothers were played", and
  "frequently, also, drummers came to perform naubat (ritual drumming) at the saint's baithak"
  (pp. 161–162, folios 150–151).
- **Ritual policing by the attendants**: "in both sanctuaries, in 'Pindi and Nurpur, expressions of
  high ecstasy and excitement by the saint's adherents were disapproved of and seldom seemed to
  occur. The group of mujawirs successfully promoted a serious framework of rituals in order to
  bring them into line with other orthodox Qadiri and Chishti tomb-cults" (p. 162, folio 151).
- **Building the new shrine**: "In the same month, the mujawirs and prominent murids collected
  enough money to start building the frame of a shrine, which was structurally already complete by
  July 1991" (p. 162, folio 151).
- **A financial dispute that stopped construction**: "A period of stagnation followed in the
  construction of the mazar because of a deep disagreement and rift with Mahbub Ilahi, a 42-year-old
  man from Fatehjang, living in 'Pindi, who had been considered as the fuqra-khadim (leader of the
  followers) of the saint. He embezzled money donated for the shrine and was compelled eventually to
  leave 'Pindi" (p. 162, folio 151).
- **Formal organisation**: "In Ramadan 1992, the followers of Mama Ji formed an anjuman ('society'),
  called muridan muntazim kamiti Mama Ji Sarkar (Committee of the Associated Followers of Mama Ji
  Sarkar) or sometimes also intizamia bara-ye ta'mir darbar Mama Ji Sarkar (Organisation for
  Building the Shrine of `Mami Ji Sarkar [text-layer?: Mama Ji Sarkar]`), for the maintenance and
  organisation of the Nurpur-mazar and of the astan in 'Pindi"; its presidents were "first Babu
  Mohammad Iqbal and then, since 1994, Shaikh Khalid Mahmud", and "as its degree of
  institutionalisation is still limited, arrangements are not yet bureaucratised"
  (p. 162, folio 151).
- **The first 'urs, dated and costed**: "The anjuman organised the first 'urs on 1 April 1992: with a
  kitchen langar consisting only of two huts and one tent, it was still a modest event, with about
  100–130 visitors coming in the evening. After the 'isha' prayer, the guests were served with rice,
  meat, and tea, under a shamiana tent; a generator provided light for the langar. The tomb under
  construction was illuminated with neon garlands. Later on, a mirasi drummer and a young
  professional dhamal dancer were invited to perform. The visitors at that event were exclusively
  male and the majority belonged to the urban lower classes" (p. 162, folio 151).
- **The old darbar after the death**: the 'Pindi astan is "now the akhir maqam (last place) of the
  saint, where a framed painting of the majzub is placed on his bed. The latter symbolises the
  saint's 'empty throne'" (p. 162, folio 151).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **Basu, reporting "more elaborate versions of the myth"**: Bava Gor met Sayyid Ahmad Kabir Rifa'i
  in Baghdad, became his murid, was renamed by him and made his khalifa for the area where he
  settled (p. 145, folio 134).
- **Frembgen, reporting "pious tradition"**: the saint-to-be's mother prayed at the shrine of the
  panj pir for a son, and a gaddi-nishin "gave her a little sugar and advised her to name her future
  son Gul" (p. 153, folio 142).
- **Attributed to the mujawir Rahim Gul Motilala**: Chan Agha Badshah's insult to Barri Imam, his
  instant regret, and his vow of an annual barefoot journey to the dargah (p. 153, folio 142).
- Mama Ji "used to run up and down the streets in a state of divine ecstasy (jazba-e jalal) shouting
  'I am the Qalandar of God', or 'Ah, Gul Makay'" (p. 154, folio 143).
- "Once the enraptured one seized a man by the throat, lifted him up easily, and threw him from one
  side of the road to the other. The man remained completely unhurt" (p. 154, folio 143).
- **A dated police karamat**: "at Bunni Chowk in the 1960s, during Ayub Khan's rule", the police
  arrested him as "only a beggar and hashish addict"; the van's motor refused to start, "two of the
  van's tyres burst without any visible reason", and the motor started only "as soon as the saint
  descended from the vehicle" (p. 154, folio 143).
- A 'Pindi taxi-driver's stalled car started when the saint said "Try it again now!"
  (p. 154, folio 143).
- A young man who "fell down in the ganda nala near Mama Ji's darbar and badly injured one of his
  feet with cuts from broken glass" was cured and "today, he is Mama Ji's youngest mujawir"
  (p. 154, folio 143).
- **A dated vision**: the murid Liaqat 'Ali Bhatti, dismissed from his job in 1987 and about to sail
  from Karachi port to Iraq, "perceived the face of his murshid" in the water; a security guard
  "confirmed that a long-haired, bearded saint's face was reflected in the water", and within days of
  returning to Rawalpindi he "was reinstated in his former job" (p. 154, folio 143).
- Clairvoyance and prediction: "when a person entered his room the saint directly knew about the
  problems and difficulties facing the visitor"; he addressed high officials "with their proper names
  without ever having met them before"; he "foretold future events (like the coming of rain, the
  imminent arrival of particular persons, and so forth)" (p. 155, folio 144).
- **Nazarbazi**: "'His eyes are so powerful that one cannot return his look'", a trait of majzubs
  "called nazarbazi—literally 'playing with looks', but in fact influencing, teaching, and healing
  persons" (p. 155, folio 144).
- **The Tank dervish's corpse**: at the post mortem, "the moment the sweeper…put the knife to the
  body, the new operating table broke down—a fact widely interpreted as proof of the magical power
  of the majzub" (p. 156, folio 145).
- **Incorruptible body**: the murids explain "that Mama Ji scarcely had excretions in his last years
  and that they did not smell at all…as a further proof of saintliness, because the physical body of
  a saint is intrinsically pure and free of decay" (p. 157, folio 146).
- **A good death**: "shortly before he died, he is said to have uttered the basmala (invocation of
  God) and the kalima (the Muslim creed)" (p. 162, folio 151).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Basu**: shrine hierarchy is not static but transactional — "the hierarchy of shrines is
  continuously recreated through asymmetrical exchanges of gifts to God", and she builds on Pnina
  Werbner's formulation that gifts to God "are directed either 'downwards', to the poor, in the form
  of sadaqa, or 'upwards' as religious tribute to saints and holy men, whether alive or dead"
  (Werbner 1990b:271; and "see also Chapter 5 in this volume") (p. 145, folio 134).
- **Basu**: the Sidi occupy the inverse position — "at the shrine of Bava Gor, by contrast, Sidi
  faqirs receive gifts to God in a downwards direction. They embody the category of the poor whose
  gifting increases the religious merits of the donor" — yet "although low and marginal, are not
  beyond the fold of a regional Muslim hierarchical system represented by shrines"
  (p. 145, folio 134).
- **Basu**, against Parry: gifts carry the donor's substance (Mauss 1966), and where Benares funeral
  priests are "negatively charged with the sins of devotees", gifts to the Sidi are "loaded with
  negatively evaluated emotions of hot dukh"; but "unlike Hindu funeral priests in
  `Benaras [text-layer?: Benares]`, the Sidi do not absorb dukh but transform it into positive
  emotions of maja…through a situational inversion of hierarchy" (p. 146, folio 135).
- **Basu**: the faqir is both opposed to and encompassed by the pir — "opposed to the superior pir,
  the category of inferior faqir is at the same time encompassed by the former" — a structure she
  finds replicated inside the Rifa'i family in the figure of the mastan (p. 146, folio 135).
- **Basu's conclusion**: "Sidi constructions of hierarchy are built upon a productive ambivalence";
  coolness marks "the unequivocal superiority embodied by the Sayyid" while "the subordinate and
  encompassed quality of the 'hot' is ambiguously defined—powerfully good or dangerously bad", and
  "the positively hot mastan overcomes negatively hot spirits" (p. 146, folio 135).
- **Basu on the comic goma**: "the whole `carnevalesque [text-layer?: carnivalesque?]` performance
  seems designed to bring down the high to a low bodily realm (cf. Bakhtin 1968; Basu 1996)", and
  "the act of giving that usually bestows superiority upon the giver and inferiority upon the
  recipient is exposed as ridiculous" (p. 147, folio 136).
- **Basu's final claim**: the Sidi cult "recognises, and is partly resonant with, the hegemonic
  Muslim status hierarchy which places Sayyids at its apex", but "a counter-discourse simultaneously
  subverts the basic premises of this hierarchy", redefining "people normally judged as extremely low
  (black, begging, servile) as morally and cosmically superior" (p. 147, folio 136).
- **Frembgen's stated aim**: to trace "a cult in transition", "from a cult centred on devotion to a
  living majzub residing in a darbar (court, Sufi lodge) to the establishment, after his death, of a
  dargah/mazar (shrine complex) and the reconstruction of the cult around this new focal place of
  devotion", and "to understand the 'holy houses' of such majzubs, alive or dead, as lived-in,
  concrete, everyday realities" (p. 151, folio 140).
- **Frembgen against the category 'folk' Islam**: by "interrogating the subjective realities of
  followers we may gain new insight into what is normally referred to, somewhat dismissively, as
  'folk' Islam", citing Wilfred Cantwell Smith that "'the study of religions…the study of persons'"
  (1959:35) (p. 151, folio 140).
- **Frembgen on the saint's private language**: he relates it to "the phenomenon of speaking in
  tongues" and explicitly refuses the classical comparison — "there is as yet no indication that
  Mama Ji's utterances have anything to do with the ecstatic pronouncements and theopathic locutions
  (shathiyat) of famous Sufis" (p. 155, folio 144).
- **Frembgen against biomedical diagnosis**: "Advocates of Western orthodox biomedicine might suggest
  as a diagnosis that many majzubs are actually schizophrenics, hysterics, epileptics or autistics",
  but such a diagnosis "misses…the emic perspectives on charismatic 'possession'"; in Islamic terms
  "a majzub means a person being in a permanent state of ecstasy and divine emotion, in most cases
  enraptured since birth", the term deriving from "the verb yazaba—jazaba in the vernacular"
  (p. 156, folio 145).
- **Frembgen on charisma against inheritance**: "Being a real majzub gifted with a miraculous
  charisma makes him superior to any sajjada-nishin or gaddi-nishin, descendants of former respected
  saints, who are nothing more than 'heirs of prayer carpets'; he is therefore considered as having
  far more personal baraka (blessings) than those embodying a mystical hierarchical order"
  (p. 157, folio 146).
- **Frembgen on the living saint versus the tomb**: murids "experienced a direct transmission of the
  saint's blessings", "comparable to the flow of energy received in a normal mazar, but much stronger
  and of a superior quality", making it "something like a darshan—obtaining audience—in the Hindu
  context"; the atmosphere was "less ritualised than in the realm of an institutionalised tomb-cult"
  (p. 161, folio 150).
- **Frembgen on the 'urs**: it "can be interpreted as a liminal situation for the pilgrims, who
  developed among themselves a communitas-type relationship characterised by a sense of equality,
  fraternity and solidarity", citing Turner 1973:207 (p. 162, folio 151).

## Doubts

- **`bari-imam`: the book's name and dates do not match the archive row's, and both must be kept.**
  The book prints "Barri Imam" (double r) throughout and gives "(1617–1705/1706)"; the archive row
  gives "Sayyid Abdul Latif Kazmi (Bari Imam)", died 1705. The book never prints "Sayyid Abdul Latif
  Kazmi" and never resolves 1705 against 1706. Nothing from the archive row's fields may be
  attributed to this book (p. 152, folio 141).
- **`golra-sharif` rests on a bare name inside a parenthesis.** "Pir Mehr 'Ali Shah
  (Golra/Islamabad)" appears once, as the subject of a popular print; the book says nothing about
  the shrine at Golra, and the spelling differs from the archive's "Pir Sayyid Meher Ali Shah". The
  mapping is made under the author-only/bare-name ruling of 18 Sep 2026; weigh it as thin
  (p. 159, folio 148).
- **`lal-shahbaz-qalandar` likewise rests on a bare name.** "Lal Shahbaz Qalandar (Sehwan)" appears
  once, as a poster in a langar; no date, silsila, shrine or practice is attached
  (p. 160, folio 149).
- **Near-miss refused: "Chan Agha Badshah" is not `darbar-sakhi-shah-chan-charagh`.** That row's
  principal figure is "Syed Sakhi Shah Chan Charagh (Shah Zaman)" of Rawalpindi District; the book's
  figure is "Pir Chan Agha Badshah", explicitly "from Peshawar" and dated "seventeenth century".
  Different name, different place. **No id assigned**, recorded so the shared "Chan" is not later
  mistaken for a match (p. 153, folio 142).
- **Near-miss refused: "Sain Kanwan Wali (Gujrat)" is not `shrine-of-hazrat-shah-daula-daryai`.**
  That row is located at Gujrat, Punjab, and its principal figure is "Hazrat Shah Daula Daryai" — a
  different name from the one the book prints. The book gives Gujrat only as a parenthetical
  locator. **No id assigned**; if a human rules that the parenthesis alone is enough, the row it
  would take is `shrine-of-hazrat-shah-daula-daryai` (p. 160, folio 149).
- **Near-miss refused: "Baba Lal Shah (Sorasi/Murree)" is not `mohra-sharif-khanqah`.** That row's
  location is "Mohra Sharif village, Murree Tehsil" and its principal figure is "Khwaja Muhammad
  Qasim Sadiq" — again a different name. **No id assigned** (p. 159, folio 148).
- **Near-miss refused: "the shrine of the panj pir (the five pirs)" is not `panj-tirath`.** That row
  is a Hindu temple in Peshawar whose principal figure is "King Pandu (Mahabharata)"; the book's
  panj pir shrine is a Muslim shrine of five pirs with gaddi-nishins. The names rhyme and the city
  may be the same, but nothing else matches. **No id assigned** (p. 153, folio 142).
- **Near-miss refused: "Sa'id Sarmad (d. 1661)" and the couplet's "Mama Ji Sarkar sarmast" are not
  `shrine-of-sachal-sarmast`.** That row's principal figure is "Sachal Sarmast (Abdul Wahab
  Faruqi)", Daraza, Khairpur, d. 1827. The book's Sarmad is a seventeenth-century naked majzub-poet,
  and "sarmast" in the couplet is an epithet of Mama Ji. **No id assigned**
  (pp. 156 and 160, folios 145 and 149).
