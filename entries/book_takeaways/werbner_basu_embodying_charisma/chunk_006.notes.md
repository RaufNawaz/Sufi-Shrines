## Practices, institutions, economy

**Trees, groves and shrine land (Werth)**
- "The grove outside Rajpur is left growing only because it belongs to the shrine which is inside it,
  and the people usually emphasize the fact that no wood should be taken from there" (p. 95,
  folio 84).
- "In the majority of the shrines there is at least one big, old tree growing in the compound of a
  shrine" (p. 95, folio 84).
- Compound dimensions as measured by Werth: pir Geru's, "about fifteen by thirty metres long" (p. 95,
  folio 84); the child's, "approximately four by four metres" (p. 95, folio 84).
- Shrines outside village borders are associated with springs or wells (p. 95, folio 84).
- Graves "are nearly always decorated with beautifully adorned shawls" (p. 96, folio 85).
- Healing repertoire: "These shrines all have the power of healing, often of physical pain, and of
  bringing fertility to barren women" (p. 96, folio 85).
- Visiting pattern: "they are regularly visited by local people" (p. 96, folio 85); villagers "go
  there to state their fears, whether personal or communal—like the danger of drought. Women—and
  men—supplicate for the wellbeing of relatives or for children" (p. 97, folio 86).

**Custodianship, caste and building (Werth)**
- pir Kara's shrine "is managed by his living relatives" (p. 96, folio 85) — the only custodianship
  arrangement Werth specifies in this chunk.
- "The Sayyid caste, which is held to be the caste of pirs, has served a traditional role within the
  population of the area for generations" (p. 98, folio 87).
- In a neighbouring village "Sayyids (of another subcaste) constitute the dominant caste", and in
  another, "inhabited mainly by Awan (a landowning caste) —a tomb was built for a father's brother's
  son of the pir of Rajpur" (p. 98, folio 87).
- Deference, one generation back: the Bahadur of Rajpur, "who call themselves ‘Rajput’, being
  addressed by other castes with the honorary title ‘Raja’", said "that one generation ago they would
  sit on the floor in the presence of a Sayyid, letting him alone sit on a charpai (a South Asian
  bed)" (p. 98, folio 87).
- **Building date, relative not absolute:** "The green tomb inside Rajpur… is younger than the shrine
  in the grove; it was erected within living memory" (p. 98, folio 87). **No year is given: (not
  stated).**
- The pir of the green tomb "never asked for any payment for his services. Everything was given to him
  with the people's free will. He did not care for material possessions" (p. 99, folio 88).
- Contrast of scales: at "the famous, large shrines of Pakistan", "the life of whose devotees,
  particularly the most devoted murids, may be considerably influenced by the authority of their pir",
  whereas "Nothing of this holds true for the small shrines" (p. 99, folio 88).

**The ʿurs and langar at Ghamkol Sharif (Werbner)**
- **The 1990 sacrifice, with counts as printed:** "In 1990 ninety-five goats, thirteen sheep, and
  seventeen cattle and buffalo were slaughtered over a three-day period, the equivalent of over
  3,000 kg of meat, distributed to some 20,000 or more pilgrims and supplicants" (p. 110, folio 99).
- The festival is "the major three-day festival" (p. 110, folio 99).
- **Women's preparatory labour, and its class composition.** "local high-class Sayyid women of wealthy
  families who claim descent from the Prophet, along with a large number of women from other respected
  families throughout Pakistan, clean and scrape thousands of clay dishes, hundreds of flat bread
  baskets, about twenty or thirty giant clay pots and an equivalent number of iron chappati stoves
  (loh)" (p. 110, folio 99).
- The stoves "accumulate a layer of rust over the year and need to be scraped and polished in readiness
  for the thousands of chappatis that are to be baked on them" (p. 110, folio 99); the women then
  "use heavy iron picks to dig up the dry earth in order to install the ovens" and "smear the surface
  around the ovens with mud" (p. 110, folio 99).
- **Labour is unpaid and religiously framed:** "The labour of the women is all voluntary, an act of
  khidmat, of public service" (pp. 110–111, folios 99–100); "The women explain that they work for
  sawab, merit, and for God's forgiveness, Allah mu‘af kare" (p. 111, folio 100).
- Gendered division: "While the women wash, scrape and polish, the men invest their voluntary labour in
  piling up vast quantities of wood" (p. 111, folio 100).
- **Offerings.** Disciples "bear with them tributary gifts for the saint known as nazrana and offerings
  for the langar, the sacred food provided freely at the lodge for all supplicants, disciples and
  pilgrims who visit it" (p. 111, folio 100).
- Werbner's gloss of the term: langar is "the communal distribution of food at a religious lodge or
  celebration" (p. 107, folio 96).
- Purity of the food: "The food is pure, I was told, because it is cooked by men of pure heart who
  chant zikr (the incantation of God's name) as they cook" (p. 111, folio 100).
- **Langar is continuous, not annual.** "The ‘urs is the high point of a continuous flow of food
  provided at the lodge by the saint all year round… a perpetual sacrifice, one that provides an
  apparently endless supply of sacrificial meat every day throughout the year" (p. 111, folio 100).
- **Nothing is bought.** "All the animals slaughtered for the langar are freely given by pilgrims,
  disciples and supplicants—none is purchased" (p. 113, folio 102).
- **What the convoys carry:** "convoys of trucks and buses coming from the different villages, towns,
  factories, army barracks or workplaces throughout Pakistan and beyond it, including from Britain…
  bear with them not only animals but sacks of flour, bags of rice, large containers of clarified
  butter (ghee) and money donated for the purchase of food for the langar" (p. 114, folio 103).
- Slaughtering and cooking are themselves service: the men "do so as a meritorious, freely given act,
  an act of selfless service (khidmat). All the labour in the lodge is freely given in the name of
  God" (p. 114, folio 103).

**Sacrifice as a category (Werbner)**
- "nominally in Islam every animal slaughtered is a sacrifice (see contributions in Brisbarre and
  Gokalp 1993)" (p. 111, folio 100).
- "Ritual sacrifices are, however, set apart from routine Islamic slaughter by additional customs
  related to particular festive occasions—the eid, ‘aqiqa (on the birth of a son) or sadaqa
  (sacrifices of exorcism or expiation) (see Werbner 1990)" (p. 111, folio 100).
- **Sacralisation of a bought animal:** "a purchased animal must be transformed through rites of
  sacralisation before it can be sacrificed or defined as an offering. Among Pakistanis this
  sacralisation is usually achieved through a communal reading of the whole Qur'an in a cooperative
  gathering, or through a special specified prayer of dedication—such as the eid morning prayer" or
  through "an offering of vegetarian food known as tabarruk" (p. 113, folio 102).
- **The direction of giving.** "Gifts to God, including animal sacrifices and offerings of food and
  money, are always unilateral—given without expectation of return" (p. 114, folio 103); they go
  "either ‘downwards’, to the poor, in the form of sadaqa (alms), or ‘upwards’, as religious tribute
  to saints and holy men, whether alive or dead. Alternatively, offerings are made to communal causes
  such as the building of a mosque or the langar" (p. 114, folio 103).

**Hajj and umra practice, as reported to Werbner by returning pilgrims**
- Method: "My research on the hajj was conducted in Manchester through discussions with returning
  hajjis" (p. 108, folio 97).
- Sequence: pilgrims start "from Mina on the eighth day of the month of hajj", move "on the ninth day
  at the valley of Arafat", and the eid sacrifice "is followed on the tenth day by the encounter with
  the devils" (p. 108, folio 97).
- Dress: "The pilgrim starts this symbolic journey dressed in two white sheets, likened to the shrouds
  of the dead" (p. 108, folio 97); afterwards "his or her head is shaved or clipped" (p. 108,
  folio 97).
- **Pebble counts as printed:** at Muzdaliffah "they collect forty-nine tiny pebbles"; at Mina they
  "cast one lot of seven pebbles onto a single pillar, that of Aqaba" (p. 108, folio 97); in the
  post-Meccan stonings "the pilgrims cast forty-two pebbles, fourteen at each of the pillars (seven

- After the sacrificial meal "they can put on their normal clothes and all taboos are lifted, except
  the prohibition on sexual intercourse" (p. 109, folio 98).
- The three stages of umra: tawaf, "the circumbulation of the house of God"; the zamzam, where "the

  Saf and Marwa" (p. 109, folio 98).
- **Tokens carried home:** "pilgrims return from hajj bearing with them sacred tokens—bottles of water

  people back home, spreading the blessings they received" (p. 110, folio 99).
- "One of the key features of the eid sacrifice on the hajj is that both the sacrificial slaughter and
  the prayers accompanying it are multiplied a thousand times" (p. 110, folio 99).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **pir Geru and the cut branches** (told by villagers to Werth): a man cut the branches of the faqir's
  trees; a servant taunted the pir, who answered "‘Just wait a little.’ That night the man who had cut
  the branches had an arrow shot into his heart, and he died" (p. 95, folio 84).
- **The musalli taken by jinns** (a Rajpur story): a drummer returning from a marriage "was taken by
  jinns by the village spring where people draw water for their own marriage, and where he used to
  play his drum, and was only later released" (p. 95, folio 84). Werth glosses musalli as "low service
  caste with ceremonial duties at marriage" (p. 95, folio 84).
- **Baba Farid and the bitter lake** (reputed, near Kallar Kahar): he met girls carrying water and
  asked for a drink; "they teased him and said: ‘The water is bitter.’ Three times he repeated his
  request, with the same answer, at which point he turned the lake's sweet waters bitter (its waters
  are salty)" (p. 95, folio 84). "Then he disappeared into the mountain above, to a lonely place whence
  nowadays his spring emerges, flowing with sweet water containing healing powers" (pp. 95–96,
  folios 84–85); "He is said to have vanished into a cave near the top of the mountain" (p. 96,
  folio 85).
- **pir Kara and the staff** : he "is thought to have miraculously produced the spring by beating on the
  ground with his staff when he arrived in the area. This spring also flows with sweet water, whereas
  the streamlets nearby are all salty" (p. 96, folio 85).
- **The moving light** (reported by people in Rajpur): a light "to have seen on Thursdays moving
  between different shrines in the area" (p. 95, folio 84), also given as "a light is seen moving on
  Thursday evenings from shrine to shrine" (p. 98, folio 87).
- **The young man of Faizlabad** (told to Werth by a young man from Faizlabad): for three years what he
  ate "appeared to other people to be white worms, but for him it was rice"; when seven men who had
  kidnapped a girl passed by, "He just waved his hand, and the seven fell unconscious"; the girl's
  brothers "prostrated themselves in front of him, and touched his feet. He disappeared on the spot"
  (p. 96, folio 85).
- **The baithak pir who disappeared alive**: "he did not have a grave because he disappeared alive
  after sitting at his place for a long time without motion, talking to jinns" (p. 97, folio 86).
- **pir ghaib's appearances**: "apart from appearing as a lion or even as a living man, he appears to
  people in dreams to tell them his wishes, or, for example, that his grave is not well cared for"
  (p. 97, folio 86).
- **The pir of the green tomb and the circle**: "he twice sat for two months at the place where his
  shrine now is, and read the Qur'an and namaz (the daily prayers) day and night. It was said that he
  had drawn a circle around himself, and outside the jinns and bhuts (evil spirits or demons) were
  sitting and speaking, but he gave them no answer" (p. 99, folio 88).
- **The plague cure**, attributed but marginal: "his power of healing the bubonic plague, a tale which
  was told to me only by the blind hafiz" (p. 99, folio 88). Werth qualifies the identification in a

- **Zindapir never sleeps**: he "is said never to sleep but to spend all his time meditating and
  praying" (pp. 113–114, folios 102–103).
- **Qur'anic narratives as pilgrims retold them to Werbner**: the zamzam "which Hajara (Hagar)

## Arguments and interpretations

**Lukas Werth (pp. 95–102, folios 84–91)**
- **Shrines index wilderness.** "there is an association between trees and shrines, and most of all
  shrines which lie outside the borders of villages, and which do not have a mausoleum with a roof.
  These shrines seem to have a clear association with the wilderness" (p. 95, folio 84).
- **The connection is not local to Rajpur.** "Many of South Asia's shrines, large or small, seem to
  rely at least in one of their dimensions on the same symbolism (see for example Werbner 1996). They
  are, or were originally, built outside settlements, in areas conceived as jangal" (p. 96, folio 85).
  Werth glosses jangal as referring "not only to forest, but also to wilderness" in Urdu and Punjabi
  (p. 101, folio 90), and in the main text as "uncultivated places in general" `in both Punjabi as and
  Urdu [text-layer?: a stray or transposed "as"]` (p. 96, folio 85).
- **Small shrines differ from large ones in kind, not just scale.** "Large shrines are connected with
  the names of their saints, and with their individual biographies. In the case of the village shrines
  described here, by contrast, the stories of their pirs carry no individuating features" (p. 96,
  folio 85).
- **The saint's marginality is what makes him a saint.** "Unknown saints typically come from outside
  the village and are mendicants, and/or otherwise people outside the normal order" (p. 96, folio 85);
  of the Faizlabad figure, "It is that externality which enables him to perform a miracle, and his
  final disappearance qualifies him to become a saint" (p. 97, folio 86).
- **A shift from person to type.** "The centre of gravity has shifted from the unique character of
  persons to what is shared by them, symbolized by their graves" (p. 97, folio 86).
- **Liminality, after Turner but against him.** "shrines belong to a liminal space and are thus imbued
  with a sacred liminality" (p. 97, folio 86); communitas "is surely not absent from the village
  shrines, but the dominant feature of boundary shrines fits more precisely the term ‘liminality’:
  they mark the threshold with the wilderness beyond the village" (p. 97, folio 86).
- **Wilderness is ambivalent, not merely "nature".** "‘Wilderness’ refers not simply to ‘nature’ in the
  Western sense; it is the realm of danger, where humans may be caught by jinns or churels, but it is
  also the realm of saints who sustain, nurture and protect the villages" (p. 97, folio 86).
- **A graded cosmology reported from a devotee.** The Rawalpindi informant's picture was "of a
  multitude of pirs graded at different spiritual levels, with the Prophet at the top, but each being
  relevant in his own context" — a picture Werth "found quite common among followers (murids) of pirs
  in Pakistan" (p. 98, folio 87).
- **Method claim: these shrines resist historical analysis.** "The small shrines at the village of
  Rajpur cannot be understood if analyzed historically. They are stripped of almost all specific,
  differentiating attributes apart from their spatial location and putative Sayyid connections"
  (p. 99, folio 88). Werth sets this against the alternative approach, "a history of people and
  ideas" (p. 99, folio 88), and concedes "These two approaches need not be contradictory" (p. 99,
  folio 88).
- **Werth allows that a shrine origin story may be invented.** "It may well be that the reason given
  for the pir's move to the village… is an invented myth which emerged later, but it marks a
  long-established, realistic and pervasive desire in the area for a guardian saint" (p. 98,
  folio 87).
- **Large and small shrines do opposite social work.** Large shrines, "By constituting centres of
  pilgrimage and drawing their following from different areas and social contexts… break up the
  confinements, separations and divisions of Pakistani society, whereas the small shrines… also define
  relations between them" (p. 100, folio 89).
- **Saints and malevolent beings may be construed alike.** "Perhaps it is not too far-fetched to
  suggest that these ‘abstract’ saints and the malevolent beings afflicting human beings are construed
  along parallel lines" (p. 100, folio 89) — supported in a note by Dinzelbacher (1995) on medieval
  Christian saints and witches (p. 101, folio 90), and Werth notes the parallel is "surely not shared
  by the people of Pakistan" (p. 101, folio 90).

**Pnina Werbner (pp. 106–114, folios 95–103)**
- **Pilgrimage as sacred exchange, not just experience.** "the symbolic transformations pilgrims
  undergo effect a sacred exchange between two symbolic worlds, and mediate the contradictions between
  those worlds (see R.Werbner 1989:261–262, 296)" (p. 106, folio 95).
- **Against Turner.** Turner's theory "powerfully captures an important dimension of the pilgrimage
  experience, while glossing over the fact that pilgrimage is a highly structured process of metonymic
  (and not just metaphoric) transformation"; Werbner's view is that "pilgrimage is both
  ‘anti-structure’ and ‘counter-structure’" (p. 106, folio 95).
- **Against the market reading, which she names and attributes.** Eade and Sallnow describe such
  exchanges as "‘self-interested exchanges between human beings and the divine’ (1991:24)" and as
  "‘sacred commerce’" (p. 107, folio 96). Werbner replies that "reduction of processes of sacred
  exchange in pilgrimage to mere interested reciprocity, however disguised, obscures the highly
  structured and complex set of symbolic operations which bring about the desired transformation"
  (p. 107, folio 96).
- **The chapter's thesis.** "the langar… at a Sufi saint's lodge may be regarded as a form of perpetual
  sacrifice which is a key symbolic moment of metonymic exchange during pilgrimage to the lodge. As
  such, it structures both the routine organisation of the lodge and the wider organisation of the
  Sufi regional cult focused upon it" (p. 107, folio 96) — and "it also structures gender relations
  and makes women integral to the process whereby God's blessing is objectified at the lodge" (p. 107,
  folio 96).
- **Her inversion of the usual sociological move.** "My argument thus reverses a common sociological
  tendency to seek material interests beneath the surface of apparent altruism" (p. 108, folio 97);
  the aim is "to unmask a self-interested discourse in order to reveal the central experience of
  altruism and humanism which energises Sufism" (p. 108, folio 97).
- **Why the market reading is seductive.** "In Sufism, a discourse of market relations and patronage is
  used by supplicants to ‘explain’ their relation to the saint, alive or dead… this Sufi allegory of
  interested exchange may easily be accepted at face value" (p. 108, folio 97); and "the occasional
  `un-masking` of individual saints as exploitative charlatans or sexually promiscuous seducers of
  innocent female supplicants, is seen as proof of the manipulative nature of Sufism" (p. 108,
  folio 97).
- **Hajj as a reversal of narrative time.** "This reversal of the original time sequence of the sacred
  Qur'anic (and Biblical) narratives is not accidental, in my view, but part of the process through
  which the pilgrim gradually sheds her or his sins and becomes as pure and innocent (masum) as a
  newborn infant" (p. 108, folio 97); "The movement is thus backwards from death towards purity like
  that of a baby" (p. 109, folio 98).
- **The two key identifications, one male and one female.** "with Ibraham
  `[text-layer?: Ibrahim — spelled "Ibrahim" elsewhere on the same page]` and the ordeal he faced in
  having to sacrifice his son, and with Hajara, his wife" (p. 109, folio 98); "Just as all the
  pilgrims, both male and female, identify with Ibrahim, a male, so too they all identify with Hajara,
  a female" (p. 110, folio 99). Werbner calls the identification with a woman "significant" (p. 110,
  folio 99).
- **Mediation is collective, not individual.** "What is stressed here is mediation with God not by a
  single person but by the community of believers, united in their intentionality"; and for Sufis "this
  mediation is itself mediated by the ability of these saints to mobilise the multitude in a shared
  ordeal" (p. 111, folio 100).
- **The bridge from hajj to lodge.** "In saints' lodges and shrines in South Asia, this moment is
  expanded and magnified to become the central trope of the lodge, binding together the moral ideas of
  mystical Sufism with the organisational agendas of the lodges as centres of far-flung regional
  cults" (p. 110, folio 99).
- **What makes a sacrifice a sacrifice: an act of conversion.** "The conversion is from the cash
  economy to the moral economy, the good faith economy, the gift economy. Unlike ordinary halal
  slaughter, a ritual sacrifice is the slaughter of an animal freely given, removed from the cash nexus
  of commercial buying and selling" (p. 113, folio 102).
- **Hierarchical gift economies.** "Islamic sacrifice in South Asia, as I have argued elsewhere
  (Werbner 1990: chs 5, 8), is framed by a semiotic of inequality characterising what I call
  hierarchical gift economies (see Figure 5.1)" (p. 114, folio 103).
- **The chunk ends on the unfinished claim** "Saints, regarded as descendants of the Prophet or his
  close companions, are" (p. 114, folio 103) — the predicate runs into chunk 007.
