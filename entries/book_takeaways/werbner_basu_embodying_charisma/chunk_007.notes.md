## Practices, institutions, economy

- **A three-way classification of religious gifts.** *Sadaqa* is "gifts to the poor"; *nazrana* is "a
  religious tributory [text-layer?: tributary] gift of money, valuable objects or food given as a
  mark of respect or in gratitude for a blessing bestowed"; *tabarruk* is "blessing, thanksgiving",
  the remains of communal meals "distributed… among the people to be taken home" (p. 115, folio 104).
  "Members of these castes, I was told, will not accept sadaqa… but only nazrana", and "Sayyids are
  not offered and do not usually accept tabarruk" (p. 115, folio 104).
- **A three-way classification of animal sacrifice.** *Sadaqa*, "an expiatory gift at the time of
  extreme danger of a life-threatening kind, in which the victim is given to the poor in its
  entirety"; *qurbáni* (printed with an acute accent), "the eid sacrifice, a portion is given to the
  poor and the rest shared among kin and friends conceived of as equals"; and *zabah*, "an animal…
  given as a tribute at a saint's lodge and… usually shared out as langar" (p. 115, folio 104).
- **The ʿurs crowd figure.** "The 'urs is said, somewhat hyperbolically, to draw 300,000 pilgrims to
  the lodge, all of whom are fed by the saint" (p. 116, folio 105). **Werbner marks the figure as
  hyperbolical in the same sentence** (p. 116, folio 105).
- **A cash figure for hired religious labour.** "Only yesterday I paid a maulvi [Islamic scholar] 400
  rupees [about £15] for giving a sermon at the mosque" (p. 116, folio 105) — contrasted with the
  saint, who "asks for nothing except from Allah" (p. 116, folio 105).
- **The langar's opening hours.** "The langar at Ghamkol Sharif is open twenty-four hours a day all
  year to all supplicants and pilgrims, from the very poor to the most elevated and powerful in the
  land" (p. 117, folio 106).
- **There are two langars at the lodge.** "One is an open, general langar, and the other is run by
  his son's wife. Many of the most respected guests are fed from this more exclusive langar, although
  chappati bread usually comes from the central langar" (p. 117, folio 106). The Sayyid women Werbner
  stayed with during the ʿurs "had brought their own food with them for the festival, and ate almost
  nothing from the central langar", denying any significance to the avoidance "beyond a matter of
  taste preferences" (p. 117, folio 106).
- **The lodge's productive endowment.** "Ghamkol Sharif has its herds of cattle, goats and other
  livestock, its orange groves and fruit orchards, its vegetable gardens, as well as storage rooms
  for grain, rice and clarified butter, brought by supplicants or purchased with their donations"
  (p. 118, folio 107). "Gifts of camels and horses, and of strange and beautiful wild birds and
  animals, as well as of goats, sheep and buffalo are not unusual. Many are dedicated to the langar
  and cannot be used for any other purpose" (p. 118, folio 107).
- **Slaughter, Pakistan and Britain compared.** "At Ghamkol Sharif… animals are donated on the hoof
  and one of the disciples slaughters them, assisted by several companions. The same disciple acts as
  chief slaughterer for the lodge on all major festivals" (p. 117, folio 106). In Britain slaughter
  "is entrusted to the Pakistani butcher, who slaughters it at the abattoir", and "the choice of the
  animal, like the slaughter itself, is entrusted to the butcher" (pp. 116–117, folios 105–106).
- **The British festival calendar of the langar**: the ʿurs; "eid milad-un nabi (the commemoration of
  the Prophet's birth/death)"; the monthly *gyarvin sharif*, "the eleventh of the month", "held in
  most of Zindapir's satellite lodges and mosques throughout his regional cult"; and daily
  distribution "during the month of Ramadan… at the mosque branches of Zindapir's Sufi order in
  Manchester and throughout Britain" (p. 116, folio 105). "In all these instances the food is donated
  and its cooking is voluntary" (p. 116, folio 105).
- **The hajj langar's financing and logistics.** "This langar is organised and funded by his
  vicegerent in Britain—Sufi Abdullah… The money for the hajj langar is donated by British Pakistanis,
  while the pots and utensils are kept by disciples of Zindapir living in Mecca who are labour
  migrants working in Saudi Arabia" (p. 116, folio 105).
- **Volunteer labour and building works before the ʿurs.** "For three weeks before the 'urs both men
  and women begin arriving at the lodge to contribute their voluntary labour… Much of the building of
  the lodge takes place during this period, including the extension of water pipes, sewerage and
  electricity. New hostels are constructed for pilgrims" (p. 122, folio 111).
- **Who the women volunteers are.** Mostly "from the high-ranking Sayyid family, which is the
  largest, wealthiest and most important in Jungel Khel"; its members are "scattered throughout the
  world, occupying professional positions as doctors, engineers or pilots", hold "many of the top
  administrative positions in the town", "own large houses" and employ servants — yet take on "the
  most menial, dirty, unpleasant tasks" and sleep "on mats on the floor" (p. 122, folio 111). During
  the ʿurs "they also help supervise the visits of women supplicants to the saint" (p. 122, folio 111).
- **The daily supplicant flow and the audience protocol.** "a constant daily flow of supplicants
  seeking healing for their ailments or divine blessings in their jobs, careers and marital fortunes"
  (p. 122, folio 111). The saint "takes in about ten at a time", instructs supplicants to perform
  their daily prayers, throws "a rolled-up, inscribed paper amulet in their general direction" or
  tells them to collect amulets "outside", then raises his hands in duʿa (p. 123, folio 112). "On
  their visits to his room women supplicants sit behind a low wooden barrier to prevent them from
  reaching too close to him" (p. 123, folio 112). Both sexes "expose their faces, the women drawing
  their veils above their heads" (p. 123, folio 112).
- **What the saint gives back.** "white chiffon headscarves, embroidered praying caps and perfumes",
  drawn "out of the treasure house of objects he accumulates in his room, buried in the silk and
  brocade cushions on which he reclines"; "salt and amulets to the supplicants… along with du'a and
  dam"; and money "to the poor and needy who come to him with requests for help" (pp. 121–122,
  folios 110–111). Elsewhere the list is "white cotton scarves and white embroidered hats for his
  disciples, gowns for his khalifa, amulets and salts for supplicants" (p. 125, folio 114).
- **What Werbner herself was given**, recorded as ethnographic data: "several clothing outfits,
  jewellery, perfume and a handbag from the shaikh's son's family; four large bottles of honey
  collected from the lodge's beehives; a lovely white, finely woven cotton headscarf of a type
  usually reserved for disciples; a beautiful length of silk cloth; and a box of sweets (mithai)"
  (p. 122, folio 111).
- **The lodge's site and its building history.** "The lodge was built outside any established
  settlement, very gradually, over many years, in what was previously an uncultivated valley"
  (p. 125, folio 114). **No founding date and no building dates are given anywhere in this chunk:
  (not stated)** (pp. 115–126, folios 104–115).
- **Tribute flows from the branches to the centre**: "the branches in both countries send tribute to
  the shaikh himself" (p. 124, folio 113); British langar is provided at "Sufi dar ul ulooms (places
  of learning)" and "differs rather little in organisation and ideology from the langar in Pakistan,
  except for the fact that, as yet, it is not a perpetual sacrifice" (p. 124, folio 113).
- **Motives for giving to the langar**, as Werbner reports them: "in fulfilment of a vow (niyat) or
  to seek merit (sawab), but also as an act of identification with an unbounded Muslim community, the
  umma" (p. 118, folio 107).
- **A dated public event used as context**: "the celebrations of fifty years of Pakistani
  independence, which took place in August 1997" (p. 119, folio 108).
- **Fieldwork dates and funding** (Werbner's own acknowledgements): "research in Pakistan at the
  lodge of Zindapir during several weeks in 1989 and 1991, and… research in Manchester among
  returning pilgrims from hajj during 1988–1989", "supported by the ESRC, UK, and the Leverhulme
  Trust"; an early version of the chapter was given "at Nanterre University in Paris in 1991 to a
  seminar series on Muslim sacrifice organised by Anne-Marie Brisbarre and Altan Gokalp, of the
  Laboratoire d'Ethnologie et de Sociologie Comparative" (p. 126, folio 115).
- **Basu on the economy of a Gujarati shrine hierarchy.** "Shrine cults revolve around exchanges of
  ritual services and gifts between unequals, and the rank of different regional cults, co-existing
  within a single area, may be inferred from the direction of the gifts between cults" (p. 128,
  folio 117). "The most important cult centres are managed by Sayyid pirs who deny taking gifts
  altogether, but assert their supreme status by displaying generosity and giving" (p. 128,
  folio 117). The Sidi faqirs' self-image, by contrast, "reconciles ideas of moral and ritual
  superiority with the acceptance of alms" (p. 128, folio 117).
- **Basu's demographic figure for Gujarat**: "In Gujarat, Muslims represent a heterogeneous social
  ensemble comprising about 10 per cent of the population", "organised in different castes and sects
  (Sunni and Shi'a)", with the higher categories called *ashraf*, "the 'noble ones'" (p. 129,
  folio 118).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **Command over the wild**, attributed by Werbner to "the legends about the Zindapir": "He can cure
  poisonous snake bites, honey bees are said not to sting, and wild animals do not invade the lodge
  or attack its inhabitants" (p. 118, folio 107).
- **Feeding the ants.** "Every morning he feeds the ants with the remains of his sweet morning tea.
  Killing ants and any other creatures, however minute, is prohibited within the lodge area. This is
  also true of his conduct during the hajj, I was told" (p. 118, folio 107).
- **The water trough.** "A special water trough has been constructed by the saint's son for the wild
  animals that descend from the hills at night to drink from" (p. 118, folio 107).
- **The founding myth of the lodge**, told by Zindapir: on the first journey to establish it he saw a
  companion carrying a sack of flour and told him, "What will you eat when the flour is finished?
  Throw it away and trust in Allah" (p. 120, folio 109). Werbner calls this "the paradigmatic tale
  which illuminates the place of the lodge as the sole source of God's boundless nurture" (p. 120,
  folio 109).
- **The refused three days of langar, told in two versions.** In one, a great landowner from Sahiwal
  district offered all the food for the langar for three days in exchange for permission to heal by
  *dam*, and Zindapir answered: "If I let you provide the langar for three days I will make you a
  partner with God which would be shirk [polytheism, blasphemy]" — refusing even to make him a murid
  (p. 120, folio 109). In the other, the offer came from the Minister of Finance's uncle after
  Zindapir cured him of an incurable disease — "Pir Sahab cast dam (blew a prayer) on him and said:
  'Let him eat from the langar's food and he will be cured'" — and the refusal ran: "You will provide
  for the langar for three days but what will happen after that? I cannot make you a partner (sharik)
  with God" (p. 120, folio 109). **Werbner presents these explicitly as two versions of one tale and
  does not choose between them** (p. 120, folio 109).
- **The refusal of the Saudi politician's hospitality.** Invited to stay at a Saudi politician's home
  in Jedda, Zindapir replied: "When I visit the house of God, I am the guest only of Allah" (p. 120,
  folio 109).
- **The Viceroy of India at Khwaja Ajmeri's shrine**, as Zindapir tells it: "India is ruled by two
  governments—the British government and the government of Khwaja [lord, gentleman] Ajmeri, and the
  second one is the greater power because it rules people's hearts" (p. 121, folio 110).
- **Healing by insight into hidden causes.** "The healing powers of the saint are grounded in a
  belief in his ability to see below the surface, to the occult and social causes of illnesses, the
  thoughts, feelings and accidental transgressions which have brought about pain, chronic illness,
  infertility, depression, business failures and so forth" (pp. 122–123, folios 111–112).
- **The annually repeated journey narrative.** "In his final supplication for the sake of the
  community, the saint recalls his own first arduous journey to the lodge. Indeed, he repeats the
  story of this legendary journey almost verbatim every year" (p. 125, folio 114).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Werbner's central claim**: "The langar, conceived of as perpetual sacrifice, is the key organising
  feature of Sufi lodges in South Asia. Such lodges are centres for the collection and redistribution
  of food on a vast scale" (p. 117, folio 106), and "the langar objectifies this perpetual source of
  saintly divine spiritual power" (p. 117, folio 106).
- **Giving is hierarchical, and the hierarchy is the argument.** Mediation with God runs "either by
  giving to the poor or, indirectly, via a tribute to a saint who, in turn, is expected to use the
  tribute, if it is a sacrificial animal, for the langar" (p. 116, folio 105). The saint "is the
  infinite giver through whom flows the bounty of Allah to his followers below him", while the ʿulama
  "are mere receivers, dependent on human generosity, employees of low status and honour" (p. 116,
  folio 105).
- **Werbner explains the saint's vegetarianism structurally, not devotionally**: "As a perpetual giver
  he cannot take. The unilateral nature of gifting would be compromised if he took of what was given
  him" (p. 118, folio 107). The saint is "an exemplar of the perfect man, insan kamil" [text-layer?:
  the sentence runs on without punctuation after "kamil"] (p. 118, folio 107).
- **Nazrana is not sacrifice.** "the giving of nazrana… is much more simply an expression of love and
  respect for the saint by his disciplies [text-layer?: disciples]… it is not an act of sacrifice,
  unless we recognise the identification of the saint, as the exemplary person, within the community.
  Sacrifice is necessarily mediated either by the poor or by the community" (p. 118, folio 107).
- **The poor are not scapegoats.** "since they need the meat for their sustenance and survival, it is
  assumed it will do them no harm" (p. 118, folio 107).
- **The good faith / bad faith political economies.** People give because of "the contrast that they
  and the pir draw between the greed and corruption of the 'world' (dunya) and the purity of the
  lodge as a place of true religion (din)" (p. 119, folio 108); pilgrims see postcolonial Pakistan as
  "one of mendacious politicians, of greed, selfishness and violence" (p. 119, folio 108). Hajji
  Bashir on intercession: "if they stood alone before God they would definitely not go to heaven",
  "Because this is not an Islamic country and it is very hard to be a good Muslim here" (p. 119,
  folio 108).
- **Werbner names the paradox she will not resolve**: "just as he denies the importance of the visits
  and honours granted him by state officials and politicians, so too, paradoxically, do the visits
  prove that Zindapir is indeed a great and spiritually powerful friend (wali) of Allah" (p. 121,
  folio 110); and "despite the constructed ideological opposition between the lodge and the world,
  the growth and success of the lodge have profited from official goodwill" (p. 121, folio 110).
- **Against Eade and Sallnow's 'sacred commerce'.** "We see the limitations of the suggestion put
  foward [text-layer?: forward] by Eade and Sallnow (1991) according to which much of the activity at
  saints' lodges is a matter of 'sacred commerce'. Such a conception denies, moreover, the
  experiential dimensions of voluntary labour and sacrifice" (p. 122, folio 111). She grants that on
  the surface exchange looks like "a calculus of exchange" and that the volunteers themselves call it
  "a calculating act of service before God for the purpose of accumulating merit", but holds that
  "these ideological statements tell only a very partial truth" (p. 125, folio 114).
- **The saint's healing power derives from gendered ambiguity.** "His persona combines male and
  female qualities—the gentleness, love and tenderness of a woman with the power, authority and honour
  of a man (see also Kurin 1984)" (p. 123, folio 112); "he is a maternal father or a paternal mother,
  protective yet authoritative" (p. 123, folio 112). Werbner marks this as speculation: "One can only
  speculate" (p. 123, folio 112).
- **Two registers of relationship, distance and intimacy.** "To most women supplicants the saint is a
  distant, charismatic figure, fearful and aweinspiring [text-layer?: awe-inspiring]" (p. 123,
  folio 112), whereas the women disciples who prepare the ʿurs "treat him with the freedom of
  companions", entertain him with gossip "including the ridiculous behaviour of the anthropologist",
  and are "an invaluable source of information to him about what goes on beyond the confines of the
  room in which he meditates" (p. 123, folio 112).
- **The throat-operation episode is used as evidence for "moral amity".** "I tell this story to
  underline the clear connection between sacrifice and moral amity… The langar objectifies the moral
  community embodied by the saint himself as a figure of infinite generosity" (p. 124, folio 113).
- **Transnationalism and labour migration.** "the spread of Zindapir's cult has been associated with
  contemporary global movements of migrant labour from Pakistan to the West" (p. 124, folio 113); the
  hajj langar is "the ultimate objectification of this diasporic transnationalism" and produces "a
  perpetual sacrifice at the global centre of Islam" through "this ritual international division of
  labour" (p. 124, folio 113). The global regional cult "mediates between the universalism of Islam
  and the particularism of migrants' networks of associations (see Werbner 1989)" (p. 124, folio 113).
- **Sacred peripherality and pilgrimage as text.** "a key feature of Ghamkol Sharif is its sacred
  peripherality (Turner 1974)" (p. 125, folio 114); returning workers "move back in time by
  journeying once again to the lodge in order to be renewed", and "The women's gossip during the long
  working hours is in itself a work of making history, reliving the myth of the lodge's
  establishment" (p. 125, folio 114). Werbner reads the ʿurs against Eliade's eternal return
  (Ricoeur 1981:197–221; Eliade 1954) and Turner's communitas, then goes "Beyond calculus or
  communitas" to argue that the pilgrimage "is a text which is both personal and performative"
  (p. 126, folio 115).
- **The chain of pretexts.** Each personal text "allegorises the shared pretext of the saint's first
  journey to the lodge, itself an allegory of the texts of the Prophet's migration and return to
  Mecca, for which Ibrahim's test of faith by sacrifice, and Hajara's ordeal in the desert, searching
  for water for her infant son, are the ultimate pretexts" (p. 126, folio 115).
- **Basu's opening argument (chapter 6).** Sufism "is often presented as a realm of Islamic emotional
  discourse opposed to the cold and 'technical' constructions put forward by theologians and judicial
  scholars (Schimmel 1975; Rahman 1979)" (p. 128, folio 117). Against a purely textual reading, Basu
  holds that emotional terms are "constituted situationally in social discourses by eliciting and
  evaluating social relationships" and are not confined to "inner experiences of the self
  characteristic of Western individualism" (p. 128, folio 117).
- **Basu's stated subject**: "this connection between constructions of emotions and the creation of a
  counter-hegemonic world view, one that seeks to give asymmetrical ritual exchanges a different
  meaning, which is the subject of the present chapter" (p. 128, folio 117).
- **Basu on hierarchy, after Dumont**: hierarchy as "a logic of encompassment by higher of lower
  levels and their situational reversal of qualities such as purity and pollution (1980, 1986)", so
  that "in the realm of Muslim shrines… the pure saint encompasses the impure temporal order"
  (p. 129, folio 118). "Against this logic, the Sidi operate through a complementary and
  encompasssing [text-layer?: encompassing] logic of heating and cooling… their use by the Sidi
  inverts the hierarchy of purity and pollution by posing an alternative moral order which is also
  connected to fertility and social reproduction" (p. 129, folio 118).
- **Basu's methodological point**: "shrine hierarchies are not merely imposed givens but are
  constantly created and recreated through asymmetrical social interactions", and cosmologically they
  are "articulated by the Sidi through the complex of relations said to exist between saints, demons
  and humans" (p. 129, folio 118).
- **Basu cites Werbner's own chapter.** Sayyid pirs "act as mediators through whom the grace of Allah
  flows downwards (cf. Werbner 1990b and this volume)" (p. 129, folio 118) — an explicit internal
  cross-reference from chapter 6 to chapter 5.
- **The poor as the proper recipients of saintly love.** "the poor are elevated as the subjects of
  specific saintly love because of the purity of their faith", and, after Pinto (1989: 124), divine
  love "is a mystery that should be received in the same way a poor person reciprocates, with
  'gratefulness, faith and a pure heart'" (p. 129, folio 118).
