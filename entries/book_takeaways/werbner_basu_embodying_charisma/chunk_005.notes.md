## Practices, institutions, economy

**Nagore: the Kanduri festival, its officers and its economy**

- Personnel: "all dargah trustees, the khalifa (spiritual successor) of the dargah, members of the
  five Faqir jama’as and the rest of the pilgrims assemble near the ritual saint, pir mandapam"
  (p. 79, folio 68). Four of the five jama'as are named in the figure key — "Mandal jama’a",
  "Julali Jama’a", "Malang Jama’a", "Rafai Jama’a" (p. 86, folio 75); **the fifth is not identified**
  — **(not stated)**. The Malang jama'a has a "headman (sarguru)" (p. 80, folio 69).
- The pir installation: "the pir’s toes are tied with a fine thread. It is believed that the thread
  should remain in this position for three days"; during it he "observes meditation (zikr),
  constantly reciting the names of God" and "throws lemons to the crowds" (p. 79, folio 68).
- The chadar: "In earlier days, the chadar of Nagore Andavar’s rowla sharif used to be presented by
  the then Maratha kings of Tanjore. Later, Palaniandi Pillai, a devotee of Nagore Andavar, requested
  the Maratha king to allow him to present the shawl to the saint. Since then the tradition has been
  upheld by the descendants of the Palaniandi Pillai" (p. 79, folio 68). No date is given for either
  the Maratha custom or the transfer: **(not stated)**.
- The shawl's collection: "On the morning of the day following the installation of the ritual saint,
  at 7 p.m., the dargah trustees and the descendant of the saint’s son, Yusef Saheb, visit the family
  of Palaniandi Pillai’s descendants, Karthikeyan, to receive the shawl" (p. 79, folio 68) — **the
  "morning… at 7 p.m." is an internal contradiction, see Doubts**. "the shawl is carried on the back
  of the dargah elephant" and then "spread on the saint’s tomb after being anointed with sandalwood
  paste" (p. 79, folio 68).
- The sandalwood procession: "That evening, at 8.30 p.m., the chariot containing sandanam is pulled
  by the pilgrims, preceded by the aloe-wood cart, from Nagapatnam town to Nagore" (p. 79, folio 68);
  it includes "drums (tappu), a troupe of horns, the double-reed nadaswarams played in Hindu temples
  and a three-piece Western band, making it much like a fair (mela)" (p. 79, folio 68). "No models of
  the devotees are carried during this procession" (p. 79, folio 68). Householders "offer fatiha to
  it by inviting the Muslims accompanying it into their houses", give "tea, coffee and snacks",
  "honour them with towels (thundu), and distribute prasadam (sweetmeats)" (p. 79, folio 68). It
  reaches the dargah's south gate "Finally, at 4.30 a.m." (pp. 79–80, folios 68–69).
- `Plate 3.5`: "Carrying the sandalwood paste with which the saint’s lineal descendant will anoint
  the tomb of the saint. The illuminated chariot of the procession is seen in the background" (p. 80,
  folio 69).
- The anointing: the two pots of sandanam are taken "inside the sanctum sanctorum" by the saint's
  descendants; the act "is performed by the spiritual successor (khalifa) of the dargah, who first
  utters the fatiha and recites the spiritual genealogy of the saint"; only "After the anointment
  ritual, the dargah gates are opened to the pilgrims and public" (p. 80, folio 69).
- The release: "Two days later, on the 12th of Jamathul Akhir at 5 p.m., the dargah khalifa, along
  with the trustees and five Faqir jama’as, assemble around the pir mandapam. After saying the
  fatiha, the knots on the ritual saint’s toes are untied" (p. 80, folio 69); the released pir goes
  to the seashore "to break his fast (roza)" (p. 80, folio 69).
- The close: "On the evening of the 14th of Jamathul Akhir, two days after the procession to the sea,
  the flags from the five minarets are lowered… This ritual is known as thuvaja avarohanam (the
  flag-lowering ritual). That night the Qur’an is read along with the silsila of the saint" (p. 81,
  folio 70). "This marks the completion of the saint’s annual Kanduri festival, attended by vast
  numbers of disciples and local people, which has lasted in total fourteen days" (p. 81, folio 70).
  **No visitor number is given** — **(not stated)**.
- Reuse: "the flags, the models and the chariots are carefully preserved to become once again, the
  following year, the ritual bearers of the shrine’s sanctity" (pp. 81 and 83, folios 70 and 72).
- Daily and weekly regime: "daily and weekly rituals are also held at the dargah. The daily ones,
  such as cleaning the tomb and adorning it with flowers, lighting the lamps and offering fatiha, are
  performed by the religious custodians of the dargah. Thursday (Jumerat) has special significance,
  as it does in most Sufi lodges" (p. 83, folio 72). **Opening hours**: "The dargah is kept open
  daily for one hour, after the morning and evening prayers; on Thursdays it remains open from
  afternoon to evening" (p. 83, folio 72).
- Offerings and their redistribution: flags are the local form — "At the dargah of Sahul Hameed
  Nagore Andavar, however, the devotees, as we have seen, offer flags in the name of the saint. This
  special form of offering is a feature of worship at saints’ shrines throughout south India, and
  particularly in Tamil Nadu" (p. 83, folio 72); "The flag offerings are made during the annual death
  ceremony of the saint" (pp. 83–84, folios 72–73). Food offerings "consist of puffed-up dried sugar
  mixed with wheat (malida), along with an offering of flowers of a special variety of chemeli. These
  offerings are given to the descendants of the saint, the mujawar (the ‘protectors’ of the dargah)"
  (p. 84, folio 73). "a little quantity of the offering is taken by a mujawar, and the rest is
  returned to the devotees for distribution among their friends and relatives. This sanctified
  offering is known as tabarruk" (p. 84, folio 73). Elsewhere, "Some shrines tie threads to the
  strings of the dargah gate. After the fulfilment of the vow these threads are offered before the
  saint along with nazarana (a tributary gift)" (p. 83, folio 72). The figure key names a "Hundi =
  gift collection box" (p. 86, folio 75).
- Relics taken away by devotees: "The thick layers of soot (masi) available in the sacred niches of
  the sanctum sanctorum are also taken by the devotees as a proof of the living memory of the saint.
  The ghee (sanctified butter) available in the sacred niche lamps is touched and smeared on the face
  and hands as a token of blessings from the saint" (p. 84, folio 73).
- Healing as an economy: supplicants come for "physical illness, mental disturbances and a range of
  personal problems, from a sense of disgust with life to the suffering and hardships of poverty",
  and seek "cures for barrenness and infertility, success in examinations or job promotions, and
  victory in civil or criminal court cases" (p. 83, folio 72); "the poor, who cannot afford more
  costly biomedical treatments, depend upon the supernatural treatment of the saint, available free
  to almost any supplicant" (p. 84, folio 73). Sufferers "may stay there for months at a time"
  (p. 84, folio 73).
- Inter-communal composition: "Among the regular supplicants are included not only Muslims, but
  Hindus and Christians as well" (p. 83, folio 72); "the fusion of a single Hindu and Muslim
  brotherhood at the Kanduri festival" (p. 83, folio 72); "the veneration by offering flags and
  lighting ghee lamps at his tomb by local people (particularly Hindus), and the acceptance of all
  these customs by Muslims" (p. 84, folio 73).
- Architecture, read symbolically: the sanctum is "a constellation of the five elements", with "the
  foundation of the sacred complex of the dargah represent[ing] earth (jameen), the walls of the
  rowla sharif symbolis[ing] water (pani) and the minarets within the dargah complex represent[ing]
  fire (aug), while the kalasa (dome) of the minarets stands for air (hava)" (p. 81, folio 70).
  **No building date and no architect are given anywhere in this chunk** — **(not stated)**.
- Vocabulary of tombs, from note 4: "The place of ziarat is called ziaratgarh or dargah, which also
  refers to the Sufi saint’s residence or burial place. The burial place of an ordinary person is
  referred to as qabar (grave); for high dignitaries such as ‘ulama (Islamic scholars) the word mazar
  (grave) is used. Thus at the regional level there is a distinct style of usage of words for the
  graves of Muslims in south India" (p. 85, folio 74). Note 5 defines fatiha as "a single recitation
  of the Sura fatiha (the first verse of the holy text) and three times of the Sura Iklas" (p. 85,
  folio 74). Note 6 defines pir as "a spiritual mentor, one who initiates the novices into the
  spiritual line of Sufism. He is called shaikh or murshid", but adds that "in the present study the
  word pir denotes the disciple of the saint" who acts as the festival's "ritual saint" (pp. 85–86,
  folios 74–75). Note 3 describes the flags, "`fande` [text-layer?: an initial letter is lost, as in
  note 2; the intended word is not recoverable from this chunk] are the dargah flags, carrying the
  Zulfikar emblem (see Plate 3.1, p. 57), which are ceremonially hoisted on a tall and imposing
  wooden pillar called thuvaja-stambha (flagstaff )" (p. 85, folio 74).

**Rajpur: small shrines without an economy**

- The village: "In the mountains dividing the plains of the Punjab from the slightly elevated plateau
  of the Potwar, the salt range, lies the village Rajpur, inhabited mainly by the `Bahadar.1`
  [text-layer?: Bahadar, with a flattened endnote marker 1 glued to it] Rajputs who live there regard
  it as the place of their origin" (p. 88, folio 77).
- Shrine 1, the centre: "just beside the mosque, stands a small, green-painted tomb"; "The tomb is
  their shrine (ziarat, khanqah), and their relatives still visit the village from time to time.
  Local people have just completed a small lodge beside the shrine for their stay when visiting the
  village" (p. 88, folio 77). **The only endowment recorded in the chapter**: "Once the people of
  Rajpur wanted to have a saint (pir) in their village, and they called a man from a family of
  Sayyids living in the area. They gave food, money, and fields to him, all of their own free will;
  the pir never asked for anything" (p. 92, folio 81). Custodianship has drifted sideways on
  sectarian lines: the pir's family "are, however, Shi‘a, whereas the majority of the population of
  Rajpur are Sunni Muslims. Only the biradari (local kin group) of one service caste (kammi), the
  shoemaker (mochi), are also Shi‘a, and they have now somewhat taken over the care of the shrine,
  sponsoring a celebration there at Moharram to which they invite other people" (p. 92, folio 81).
  The known descendants "live some thirty miles away, and although when they visit they are treated
  with considerable respect, they are themselves poor people" (p. 89, folio 78).
- Shrine 2, the faqirni's: "hardly more than a heap of rough stones, integrated into a wall
  separating a plot from the public street (gali)", with "some old bottles and tin oil lamps; signs
  that the shrine is worshipped by women on Thursdays" and "A series of smooth stones… people rub
  them over their limbs when they feel physical pain" (p. 88, folio 77). "The small shrine of the
  mendicant woman is not regularly used as a place of worship at all, but sometimes, on a Thursday, a
  woman will come, pray, and light an oil lamp there" (p. 92, folio 81).
- Shrine 3, the grove: "a rectangular compound about six metres by six, surrounded by a wall about a
  metre high; the small gate leading into the compound is never locked. There are two graves inside
  it" (p. 88, folio 77), with "One shrub growing inside the compound… adorned with pieces of cloth,
  testimonies to the vows of the worshipping women" (p. 89, folio 78). "This shrine probably plays
  the most central role in the social life of the village" and is "the most frequented one" (p. 89,
  folio 78; p. 93, folio 82). **A standing economic interdiction**: "No wood, not even dead branches
  or twigs, is ever taken from the dense grove, except when preparing a sacrificial meal for the pir
  right there on the spot, although firewood in that mountainous area has to be gathered from far
  away, an arduous task, and the thick trunks of the trees growing around the shrine could be
  profitably sold" (p. 89, folio 78). Upkeep: "The two graves of the shrine in the grove are always
  adorned by a beautifully decorated shawl (dupatta), oil lamps are lit by women on Thursdays"
  (p. 90, folio 79).
- Vows and cash at shrine 3: women "knot pieces of cloth and hang them on the shrub by the grave as
  tokens of a vow, and when their requests are fulfilled, they hang a new piece of cloth on the same
  shrub. If they are very grateful, they bring a whole shawl and spread it on the grave. If that gets
  old, it is hung on the shrub along with the other pieces of cloth. People also leave money at the
  shrine" (p. 93, folio 82).
- **A collective rain ritual, witnessed**: when "the winter rains were late, and in the salt range
  where there is no irrigation", "the people of Rajpur decided to appeal to the pir ghaib. Some men
  gathered money from every family, and right in front of the shrine they prepared one big pot of
  spiced rice, and one of sweet rice. To light the fire for this, they were permitted to use the wood
  lying around the shrine"; "They took care that the whole Qur’an was recited aloud. The women came in
  separate groups, and also prayed at the shrine"; the participants "included not only peasants from
  the village, but also men working in cities or serving in the army, who were spending some time in
  their natal home" (p. 93, folio 82).
- Marriage use: "People also go to supplicate at this shrine before marriages, to pray that nothing
  will disrupt the celebrations; and after them, if all went well"; the bridegroom and friends break
  off his horseback round to pray there "as they do at the shrine in the centre of the village"; and
  at "doli uthana" the bride's "dupatta… is tied to the bridegroom’s turban in front of the grave.
  This is done only there, not at the village shrine" (p. 93, folio 82).
- **The explicit absence of an economy**: "There are no business interests of any importance involved
  in the maintenance of this shrine, and none at all in the other shrines. Nevertheless, people take
  care of the places" (pp. 89–90, folios 78–79); "the small shrines at Rajpur and elsewhere are
  visited only by local people, not by pilgrims, and normally no big annual celebrations take place
  there" (p. 89, folio 78); "Here, there are no economic or political interests involved, and no
  elaborate interactions take place" (p. 90, folio 79).
- **The contrast case, the big shrines**, described generically and never named: they are "often
  powerful centres of regional influence and political factors of considerable importance, managed
  under the guardianship of descendants of the deceased saint (pir) who once lived there. These
  descendants are pirs in their own right; they participate in the charisma of their ancestors and
  may acquire new fame, and, once they die, they too will be worshipped. If these pirs handle the
  authority which has been passed down to them skillfully and astutely, they may enhance their power,
  even gaining considerable influence in national politics" (p. 89, folio 78). "The visible expression
  of this power is a magnificently decorated mausoleum and the flock of people who come there daily
  but especially at the time of the ‘urs, the annual celebration of the death of the pir, which is
  conceived of as the moment of his union with God. Like other villagers, the people of Rajpur too
  visit these great regional shrines" (p. 89, folio 78). **No such shrine is named** — **(not stated)**.

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- Note 1 defines the category: "Karamat refers to the powers possessed by the Sufi saint (wali)
  through which human needs are satisfied… The miracles performed by the prophets are known as
  mujizat" (p. 85, folio 74).
- The lemons: "a lemon thrown by the pir has miraculous powers; the pilgrims believe that these
  lemons are auspicious, and will bring good luck to anyone who catches them"; "The womenfolk
  especially believe that if a barren woman catches a lemon, it means that she will be blessed with
  offspring"; people wait "day and night… burning in the scorching heat of the sun or being soaked by
  the rain" (p. 79, folio 68). Attributed by Saheb to popular belief.
- The sweet water: at the chilladi dargah "the water in a shallow pit is sipped ritually. People
  believe that the water in this excavated pit at this moment is sweet, whereas at other times it is
  salty. This transformation reflects the miraculous powers of the saint and his power over nature"
  (p. 80, folio 69).
- The iron chain: "In front of the saint’s tomb there is an iron chain hanging from the roof. It is
  believed that this iron chain has miraculous powers. The tradition relates that it was given by the
  very first human couple, Adam and Eve, to the saint, when he visited Adam’s peak during his
  preaching in Sri Lanka. The inscriptions at Adam’s peak witness this fact (Qadhiri 1980:17)"
  (p. 84, folio 73) — the last clause is Saheb reporting his source's claim as fact.
- The tank water: devotees "touch the iron chain with this sacred water, before carrying the water
  home. This water is consumed during any illness and its divine powers, it is believed, will cure
  any disease. The water is thought to have the same effect as that of the zamzam (sacred spring)
  water in Mecca" (p. 84, folio 73).
- Transmission by dream: "All these discourses and miracles are known to the devotees of the saint
  through dreams (basharat)" (p. 84, folio 73).
- Exorcism: "The shrine of Nagore Andavar is known for its efficacy in exorcising jinns" (p. 84,
  folio 73). Possession is displayed at the tomb — "The supplicant fulfilling a vow at the shrine
  often experiences an epileptic fit… This ecstasy is understood as a tribute either in fulfilment of
  a vow or in submission of requests to the saint" (p. 83, folio 72).
- The saint's intercession can harm as well as heal: his prayers "can bring success in love and,
  indeed, can damage the property and life of rivals in love; they can resolve marital conflict and
  bring about the return home of a lost child, or the reform of a wayward husband or son" (p. 83,
  folio 72).
- **Rajpur, the plague miracle** (told to Werth in the village): "once a great disease broke out in
  Rajpur. First the rats fell dead from the roofs, and then the people developed lumps under their
  armpits, and `died.8` [text-layer?: died, with a flattened endnote marker 8 glued to it] But the
  pir and his wife saved the village by praying for whole nights one sura (ya‘sin) from the Qur’an"
  (p. 92, folio 81); "In a similar way they once also saved the cattle of the village from an
  epidemic" (p. 92, folio 81).
- **The disappearance story, in two versions, both as told to Werth.** Version 1: "once a
  bald-headed bridegroom was marrying a one-eyed bride. When the marriage procession (Urdu barat,
  Punjabi janj) was standing at the place of the shrine, people ridiculed them, at which point the
  whole marriage party, including the bridal couple, disappeared" (p. 92, folio 81); "The graves
  around the shrine are those of the marriage party. Therefore, I was told, the man must have been a
  pir" (p. 93, folio 82). Version 2, "In another, diluted version of the story, the pir had come to
  Rajpur to find a wife, but the people disrespected him (besti dena), and he suddenly vanished when
  walking over the place of the shrine" (p. 93, folio 82). **Werth reports both and asserts neither.**
- The wood interdiction enforced: "when a woman once tried to defy this interdiction, she was
  harassed by him during the night, and had to return all the wood the next morning" (p. 89, folio 78).
- The money interdiction enforced: "it is said that once, when a mochan (woman of the shoemaker
  caste) had taken away money from there, she was afflicted with many troubles until she brought the
  money back" (p. 93, folio 82).
- Appearances: "he sometimes appears to people in their dreams, and he is seen occasionally in the
  shape of a lion near his shrine" (p. 93, folio 82); "On Thursday nights, people say, a light may be
  seen coming to Rajpur from another shrine at a neighbouring village… and it moves on from there to
  a third shrine at a nearby hamlet in the wilderness" (p. 93, folio 82).
- The refused roof: "when the people once tried to build a proper tomb with a roof over the grave, it
  broke down, and he appeared before somebody in a dream and said he did not want a ‘closed’ shrine
  for himself" (p. 94, folio 83).
- The healing licence: the pir's wife "taught `theQur’an` [text-layer?: the Qur'an] to the blind-born
  child… and gave him the permission (ijazat) to heal people by breathing holy verses upon them (dam
  dena). Pirs are able to heal people in this manner, and they may grant this power to other people
  if they consider them worthy of it" (p. 92, folio 81).
- Afflicting beings: "A shadow (saya) might have fallen on a woman, or a churel or bala (witch-like

  marker 7 glued to it]" (p. 92, folio 81). "it is often said that a woman who urinates under a tree,

  (p. 94, folio 83). The bala/churel "are thought to be ugly and naked, with long loose hair covering

## Arguments and interpretations

**S.A.A. Saheb (chapter 3)**

- The processions perform a **spatial argument**: "What is effected by these acts is a fusing of
  public and domestic spaces through sacred exchange, in which offerings and food mediate between the
  spaces of houses, the public spaces of the city and the shrine" (p. 79, folio 68).
- The shawl is a carrier of the towns it has passed through: it is "imbued symbolically and
  metonymically with the spaces of the towns through which the sandalwood paste chariot has moved"
  (p. 80, folio 69).
- The seaward movement is a movement out of culture and back: "a series of movements between the town
  and the shrine culminate in a movement beyond ‘culture’, to a site which symbolises the rejuvenating
  power of ‘nature’ and the control of the saint over the powers of life and fertility" (p. 81, folio
  70); ritual substances are juxtaposed hot against cool — "the hot fire of the fireworks is
  counteracted by the coolness of the lemons, of the sandalwood paste and finally, of the sweet water
  and the sea" (p. 81, folio 70).
- **The five-element reading of the shrine, explicitly borrowed from a Hindu temple theory.** Saheb
  quotes Rao (1979:84): "‘the foundation of the temple is made to represent Earth (prithvi), the walls
  of the sanctum [are] water (ap) and the tower over it fire (tejas); the finial (kalasa) of the tower
  stands for air (vayu) and above it is the formless ether (akasa)’" and then maps the dargah onto it
  term by term (p. 81, folio 70). Because "fire being the active element that fuses the others, the
  minarets which carry the flags become an important limb in the dargah" (p. 81, folio 70).
- The transaction model of worship: "matter (flags) moves up and the spirit (blessings of the saint)
  flows down. The devotee who stands in front of the tomb is expected to partake in this transaction.
  The emanations that proceed from the tomb of the saint must be picked up by the faith in the
  devotee’s heart. Devotion is the transformer" (p. 81, folio 70); the rituals work "along the
  horizontal axis of tomb, saint and devotee. The devotee represents active matter and the tomb of
  the saint, passive spirit" (p. 81, folio 70).
- Locality is made by the festival and not merely expressed by it: "Through the festival, localism is
  inscribed in the dargah, while the sanctity of the dargah is carried to the towns in its immediate
  vicinity, affording them spiritual blessing and protection" (p. 81, folio 70).
- A general claim about shrine objects: "Although saint shrines differ from locality to locality and
  from one region to another, in all, objects embodying the sanctity of the saint emerge over time"
  (p. 83, folio 72) — the flag being the Tamil instance.
- A class claim, asserted without evidence in this chunk: "Sufi saints are believed to have immense
  power. Undoubtedly, support for Sufi cults comes from the lower classes and castes of Muslim
  society. Yet, despite the wholesale rejection by Muslim reformists of the rites at the saints’
  tombs, many pious Muslims respect the asceticism of saints and their simple way of life, devoted to
  the propagation of Islam" (p. 84, folio 73).
- The closing thesis: the shrine is "a great Muslim shrine and yet at the same time… a universal one"
  (p. 84, folio 73), and "Though the people who come to the dargah may belong to different castes and
  different religious faiths, their destination is one: the saint, with his power to intercede
  between the living and God. They derive faith from their concrete relation to the dargah and its
  guardians rather than from a direct relation to a distant and metaphysical God" (p. 85, folio 74).
- Saheb cites Werbner 1989 for the comparative frame: "(For a case study of sacred exchange, ritual
  organisation and the path to local eminence, see Werbner 1989.)" (p. 84, folio 73) — the reference
  list identifies this as Richard Werbner, *Ritual Passage, Sacred Journey* (p. 87, folio 76), not the
  volume's co-editor Pnina Werbner, whose 1996 article is listed separately (p. 87, folio 76).

**Lukas Werth (chapter 4)**

- Werth's programme, stated as a question: "How, then, are all these small shrines used, and why are
  they there? My hope is to find some tentative answers to the question: ‘What, if anything, is a
  shrine in `Pakistan?’4` [text-layer?: Pakistan?', with a flattened endnote marker 4 glued to it]"
  (p. 90, folio 79). He announces a two-part method: first "interrogating the identity associated
  with the notion of the saint, or pir, and the people’s attitude towards their saints"; second
  exploring "what role the shrines themselves play in the social life of villagers… and their
  location in the eyes of the worshippers" (p. 90, folio 79).
- His object is deliberately the least prestigious case: "In this chapter I shall concentrate on these
  small shrines of seemingly only local importance—or not even that—like the ones at Rajpur" (p. 90,
  folio 79).
- The framing claim about Pakistani Islam: "Islam in Pakistan, as elsewhere in the Muslim world,
  cannot be explained only by its Five Pillars, but rests also essentially on local traditions.
  Shrines are a part of those traditions in which the universal principles of Islam find the
  application they seem to need" (p. 90, folio 79).
- Comparative frame: shrines express "a tradition which is shared with Christianity and some forms of
  Judaism: the veneration of holy persons, known as saints. Islamic shrines function, of course, in
  their own way" (p. 89, folio 78), the differentia being "Without an institutionalized church, but in
  a cultural environment which places much emphasis on the principle of descent" (p. 89, folio 78).
  He cites Eliade 1986:116ff and Peters 1982:194ff for the "religions of the book" (p. 90, folio 79).
- The theology of the grave: "Death frees the potentialities of saints: in a religion where people’s
  real destination is conceived to be reached only after death, to be where God is situated likens
  the saints to God in their transcendence of death" (p. 90, folio 79) — with a qualification for
  Islam, "where more emphasis is placed on living saints" (pp. 90–91, folios 79–80).
- **The localization argument, which is the chapter's core so far**: "the link to the world beyond
  the grave is in the locality itself: in the relics of Christian saints or the graves of Muslim
  pirs. These define a particular point in space associated with a saint, and hence sacralized… But
  since God is everywhere, it is the plurality of saints that makes this ubiquitous God concrete. It
  turns the macrocosm into an extension of the microcosm by attaching it to a certain locality"
  (p. 91, folio 80). Localization also determines what is asked for: "as the invisible becomes a
  graspable entity in a particular locality, so too the purpose of praying also becomes specifically
  defined. People often pray to the pirs at their shrines because of concrete problems: disease,
  childlessness and other afflictions" (p. 91, folio 80).
- Sainthood can be conferred against the saint's own will — Ibn Taimiya is the example (p. 90,
  folio 79).
- **Caste as a category of holiness.** "In Pakistan the pirs normally, but by no means always, belong
  to the caste of the Sayyids, the descendants of Muhammad"; "The expression pira-di qaumzat, ‘the
  caste of the pir’, is often used as an alternative to sayyida-di qaum, ‘the caste of the Sayyid’.
  The meaning remains the same in both cases, with no discernible shift" (p. 91, folio 80). The
  brahmin parallel is offered and then limited: "Such parallels should, however, not be carried too
  far… the high status of the Sayyids is due not to their exclusive right to conduct religious
  services (they are not priests), but to their participation in the sacredness of Muhammad, and
  therefore of God" (p. 91, folio 80). The conclusion: "the ideology of the caste system is refracted
  in Muslim ideas: a category of Islamic holiness is represented as a caste (qaum, zat), which means
  a distinct quality or `species.5` [text-layer?: species., with a flattened endnote marker 5 glued
  to it]" (p. 91, folio 80).
- **The payoff, which explains the chapter's anonymous saints**: "This is one reason why it is not
  necessary in Pakistan to know the name or even the life of the pir of a small shrine: the fact that
  he is supposed to belong to a holy category is sufficient to qualify him to be prayed to, even
  though the personality of the saint has been almost effaced and is of very limited importance"
  (p. 91, folio 80). Werth immediately limits it: "But still the fact remains that it is human graves
  which are the point of veneration, and by no means the grave of every Sayyid becomes a shrine"
  (p. 91, folio 80). The same logic explains the faqirni's shrine, "She is, however, regarded as a
  member of the Sayyid caste, which is the common reason given why a shrine was made for her"
  (p. 88, folio 77).
- **The wilderness argument.** The grove shrine's traits are read as a set: "His shrine is clearly
  outside the borders of the village, and he came once upon a time from beyond the village and is
  often thought to have been a faqir (beggar or ascetic). He sometimes appears as a lion, and he also
  takes care that nobody carries away the wood which grows around his shrine. All this points to the
  shrine’s association with the area outside the settlement, the wilderness" (p. 94, folio 83). And
  the wilderness is dangerous: "Trees, therefore, are associated with the opposite of human culture,
  with the wilderness, and hence with danger" (p. 94, folio 83). The spirits' traits are read as
  inversions: "their characteristics are clearly anti-cultural: the nakedness, the long, loose hair,
  the transposition and reversal of body parts, their fear of iron" (p. 94, folio 83). **Werth notes
  in passing that the green of the tomb and the epithet share a colour**: "The colour green is
  regarded as holy in Islam, but the association with the jangal is made plain here" (p. 94, folio 83).
- An implicit paradox Werth records but does not yet resolve: the grove shrine "is the only one of the
  three shrines which lies outside the village boundaries, but it is the most frequented one. If the
  people of the village have troubles, they are most likely to go there" (p. 93, folio 82) — i.e. the
  most dangerous location is the most used.
