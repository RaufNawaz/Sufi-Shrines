## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Langar as the conversion of the market**: at a saint's lodge "the capitalist, commodity economy is converted… into a good-faith, moral economy through altruistic giving to the communal langar", argued here by Werbner (p. 26, folio 15); the lodges are "set apart as spaces of expressive amity and emotional good will" (p. 26, folio 15).
- **Lodge architecture as imperial mimicry**: "Sufi lodges, we show (Landell Mills, Saheb), are often shaped architecturally like Mughal courts, the lodge (dargah = court) replicating imperial courts, just as the latter in the past replicated charismatic saints' modes of eliciting allegiance (Cohn 1983)" (p. 26, folio 15).
- **The Nagore-e-Sharif processions (Saheb's chapter, summarised by the editors)**: they "stamp the saint's charisma on the neighbourhoods and mohallas (wards) in the vicinity of the shrine through an elaborate ritual of sacred exchange"; "the processors move back and forth to the lodge over an extended period of several weeks"; "They carry with them sacred icons and flags to be revitalised at the lodge, coming from as far away as Singapore and Sri Lanka"; the processing "includes both Hindus and Muslims, and many different castes and organised associations" (p. 27, folio 16).
- "The various communities participate as equals in the processions to the shrine centre, but Hindus construct the saint as a `diety [text-layer?: deity]`, while for Muslims he was/is an extraordinary man" (p. 32, folio 21).
- **Sufi membership figures for Egypt, on Baldick's authority**: "in 1982 there were six million members of Sufi brotherhoods in Egypt, representing a third of the male population, united through a Supreme Council recognised by the state, and having powerful legislative, judicial and executive functions (Baldick 1989:159)" (p. 28, folio 17).
- **Shrines as healing institutions, on van der Veer's account**: "whereas for Muslim followers a spiritual relationship to the pir is of central importance, Hindus see the dargah mainly as a healing domain specialising in the cure of demonic illnesses" (p. 31, folio 20); consequently "Muslims and Hindus are clearly divided by different degrees of participation and non-participation in rituals defined as Islamic, such as prayers in the mosque (van der Veer 1994a:207; 1994b)" (p. 31, folio 20).
- **Possession as the dominant supplicant category at two shrines**: "The majority of individual Hindu supplicants both at Nagore-e-Sharif and at the shrine of Bava Gor in Gujarat are, Saheb and Basu indicate here, women suffering afflictions of spirit possession" (p. 31, folio 20).
- **The volume's own institutional origin**: "This book is the result of a workshop on 'Muslim Shrines in South Asia' convened by the editors at the Thirteenth European Conference of Modern South Asian Studies at Toulouse in August 1994" (pp. 32–33, folios 21–22). "Sylvia Vatuk and Claudia Liebeskind also helped make the workshop a success. Jamal Malik advised on Urdu meanings and transliterations" (p. 33, folio 22).
- **Custodianship and succession among living Bangladeshi pirs (Landell Mills)**: he is "primarily concerned here with living pirs who invite public recognition and create or inherit sacred sites by spiritual or filial succession" (p. 45, folio 34). "Living pirs are not regulated or recognised by any higher authority, and depend for social recognition on their own ability to recruit a body of disciples, devotees and supplicants" (p. 45, folio 34). "As pirs are not part of an ecclesiastic structure, the process of being and becoming a pir is fluctuating and ambiguous" (p. 45, folio 34).
- **A pir's standing as entrepreneurship**: "a pir's standing depends on individual initiative, entrepreneurial flair and organisational virtuosity, quite apart from spiritual attainment itself" (p. 45, folio 34).
- **Scale of the Bangladeshi tomb landscape**: living pirs invoke "a whole tradition built upon veneration of dead saints, many of whom are enshrined in thousands of tombs scattered all over Bangladesh" (p. 45, folio 34). **No number, district list or date is given beyond "thousands": (not stated).**
- **Territory and rank**: "Pirs in South Asia have always been associated with territory claimed for Islam, which they were depicted as controlling in hierarchical delegation from God (Gaborieau 1989; Haq 1975; Roy 1983). The greater the stature of the saint the greater the area controlled" (p. 45, folio 34).
- **Types of pir who fall outside his sample**: "Some teach in Islamic schools (madrasah) and only take disciples within that realm, some are tantric faqirs who do not have a wide following" (p. 45, folio 34).
- **Spiritual authority versus spiritual power, as an access rule**: "the spiritual authority of a pir is for those who are allowed to become disciples, his spiritual power is more freely available" (p. 44, folio 33).
- **Varieties of Sufi in medieval Bijapur, on Eaton's authority**: "warrior Sufis, literati, reformers and rebels (called majzub) who accepted, challenged and disputed each other's religious positions (Eaton 1978:243ff)" (p. 29, folio 18); they "mediated Islamic concepts of power, value and knowledge" while "Variously interacting with both Muslim powerholders and the local population" (p. 29, folio 18).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **Muʿin al-din Chishti of Ajmer** "is said to have conquered a 'Dev' (god) at whose former temple the shrine is now situated (Currie 1989:66ff)" — reported by the editors on Currie's authority (p. 31, folio 20).
- **At Bava Gor, Gujarat**: "the shrine narrative represents the Muslim saints as victorious destroyers of local Hindu deities represented as demons" (p. 31, folio 20); there "it is Hindus who are seen to instantiate the demonic by embodying evil spirits when seeking treatment" (p. 31, folio 20).
- **Muslim spirits in Hindu possession**: "Kakar too notes that the majority of possessed Hindus he encountered were afflicted by Muslim spirits" (p. 31, folio 20).
- **The sixteenth-century Bengali epic Nabi-Bamsa ('Family of the Prophet')**, on Eaton's reading: "Adam is said to have been created by a Bengali goddess, and the four vedas are seen as preceding revelations made by God to different 'great persons' of whom Muhammad was the last (ibid.: 289)" (p. 30, folio 19); its structure "resembles that of an eighth-century Arabian text (ibid.: 285)", both dividing into "the first on the predecessors of the Prophet, the second on his own life" (pp. 29–30, folios 18–19).
- **Iblis in the same epic**: "the Hindu social order of pre-Islamic times is depicted as the realm and creation of the fallen angel Iblis or Satan (ibid.: 290)" (p. 30, folio 19); Brahmans are "portrayed as the unwitting teachers of a body of texts deliberately corrupted by Iblis", and "It was only from Adam and Eve's son, Shish, that a 'rightly guided' community, the Muslim umma, would descend" (p. 30, folio 19).
- **The miraculous repertoire of Bangladeshi pirs (Landell Mills)**: "Pirs may visit devotees in dreams, rescue people in distress, appear everywhere, read people's minds and bodies, grant disciples miraculous experiences" (p. 44, folio 33).
- **Blessedness as contagion**: "Devotees believe that the spiritual mastery achieved by the pir irradiates his body with blessedness, and that this spreads to the persons and objects with which he comes in contact" (p. 43, folio 32).
- **Post-mortem presence**: "on leaving the world, the institutions they bequeath express their post-mortem absence as a kind of active 'presence'" (p. 44, folio 33).
- **The Bengali term the chapter gives for a pir's power**: objects and places are consecrated "by dint of his 'spiritual power' (adhartik shakti)" (pp. 44–45, folios 33–34).
- **Charisma named in the volume's own vocabulary**: "charisma (baraka, karamat) is revealed as an embodied quality of exemplary persons" (p. 32, folio 21).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Werbner and Basu, against siting charisma at the centre.** Building on Shils, they note that "institutionalised charisma 'permeates' all walks of life", its source being "'contact through inspiration, embodiment or perception, with the vital force which underlies man's existence'", a force "located at the centre of society (ibid.: 201)" (p. 26, folio 15); its defining features are "intensity, embodied centrality (of values or institutions) and the capacity for ordering" (p. 26, folio 15). They then break with it: "There is, however, something intrinsically problematic in this siting of charisma at the centre and equating of it with state power and its routinised extensions" (p. 26, folio 15). What needs theorising instead is "the nature of charismatic (saintly) dissent and opposition to the bureaucratic domination of the state" (p. 26, folio 15).
- **Their model of the state as anti-charisma.** "The state and its politicians, by contrast, are seen as menacing, corrupt, greedy and unfeeling. They are not truly 'rational' in the Weberian sense since they bend the rules to their selfish interests" (p. 26, folio 15); "Theirs is a charisma of unbridled power" (p. 26, folio 15), against which the saint's charisma "is the product of his perceived (and projected) self-denial and self-mastery, of love and generosity" (pp. 26–27, folios 15–16).
- **The taming-the-wilderness thesis**, credited by the editors to Werbner (1996b) "and as Werth and Landell Mills highlight in the present volume": the "capacity for ordering and cultivating, for taming the wilderness, is also the hallmark of Sufi saints in South Asia" (p. 26, folio 15).
- **Geertz on sovereign centrality**, as the editors summarise him: through "royal progresses and processions" sovereigns mark out their territories "'as almost physical parts of them' (Geertz 1983:125)", shaping dominion "according to some cosmic, 'exemplary and mimetic' plan (ibid.: 134)"; "Even in the context of modernity… political authority retains its charismatic aura" (p. 26, folio 15).
- **Charles Lindholm's chapter, as the editors report it.** He "asks himself why, whereas Sufism has been peripheralised in the Middle East, in South Asia it has remained a vital institution" (p. 27, folio 16); one suggested reason is that "South Asian Muslims share with Hindus notions of hierarchical order which support the belief in transcendentally inspired individuals", whereas "the individual egalitarianism and progressive rationalisation of Middle Eastern societies make charismatic saints an anachronism" (p. 27, folio 16). The editors note this "tends to support one kind of interpretation of Weber's theory" holding that "'in the modern world, with its cumulative rationality and machine technology', personal charisma has no place (Wilson 1975:ix)" (p. 27, folio 16).
- **The editors qualify Lindholm against himself**: he "argues here and elsewhere that the alienation, fragmentation, materialism and solipsism of modernity 'push individuals toward immersion in a charismatic group' (Lindholm 1990:82)" (p. 27, folio 16), and that in Egypt "the lure of charisma has resurfaced in the form of Islamist movements" which "'have also been driven to idealisation of charismatic leaders and Sufic organisation', while condemning 'all formal authority as immoral imposition' (ibid.: 206, 207)" (pp. 27–28, folios 16–17).
- **A methodological suspicion about the Middle East literature**: "The impression that Sufism in North Africa is no longer viable may stem largely from a dearth of detailed contemporary research on living saints"; alternatively "political repression by successive colonial and postcolonial regimes has turned charismatic leaders towards more violent agendas" (p. 28, folio 17).
- **The book stated as a challenge to a neighbouring field**: "in highlighting the continued vitality of Sufism in South Asia, this book also constitutes a challenge to Middle East scholars to probe more deeply into the embodiments of Sufi charismatic authority and organisation in the Middle East and North Africa today" (p. 28, folio 17).
- **The orientalist and historian positions the editors reject.** From orientalists, "contemporary shrine cults predominantly appear as degradations of classical Sufism into 'decadence' characterised by superstition and magical practices (see Lindholm, this volume; Ahmad 1969)" (p. 28, folio 17); historians "have for long emphasised the syncretism of saint worship or popular Islam, depicting it as part of a process of 'indigenisation' of Islam and rendering saint worship as basically a Hindu institution (Misra 1964; Moini 1989; Mujeeb 1967; Schwerin 1981; Sen 1985)" (p. 28, folio 17).
- **Imtiaz Ahmad's three levels**, as the editors set them out: "(1) 'the beliefs and practices that are traditionally described as belonging to formal or scriptural Islam' (1981:12); (2) customs glossed as being Islamic (ibid.: 13); and (3) 'practical religion'" containing "supernatural theories of disease causation, propitiation of Muslim saints and, occasionally at least, deities of the Hindu pantheon, or other crude phenomena such as spirit possession, evil eye, etc." — levels which "co-exist… and are integrated at the local level" (pp. 28–29, folios 17–18).
- **Francis Robinson against pluralism**: he "proposes that the exemplary life of the Prophet constitutes a unifying template of practice and belief, progressively adopted by Muslims in India and worldwide (Robinson 1983)" (p. 29, folio 18).
- **The editors' objection to both Ahmad and Robinson**: for both, "'Islamisation' refers exclusively to Islamic orthodoxy at the cost of plurality, thus overlooking the diversity of views represented by Islamic scholars, theologians, Sufis and holy men as well as the historically contingent and shifting nature of internal Muslim debates over questions of correct practice" (p. 29, folio 18). On Eaton's showing, "there never was a uniform agreement upon the definition of 'orthodoxy'" (p. 29, folio 18).
- **Eaton against the syncretism frame**, quoted at length by the editors: present constructions of syncretic folk-religion rest on "the projection of contemporary religious categories, which gained prominence in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, onto a premodern Indian past", postulating "'the more or less timeless existence of two separate and self-contained communities in Bengal'" (p. 29, folio 18). He instead proposes "'a single undifferentiated mass of Bengali villagers' unsystematically picking and choosing 'from an array of reputed instruments—a holy man here, a holy river there—in order to tap superhuman power' (1993:281)" (p. 29, folio 18).
- **The editors' own central inversion, and their reason for quoting Eaton at length**: "We have quoted Richard Eaton extensively in order to clarify the proposition that Islamisation processes occur in the form of embodied ideas not reducible to the effects of Muslim reformists' missionary zeal. In contrast to Ahmad's theory of the 'indigenisation of Islam' we have to recognise the reverse process of an 'Islamisation of the indigenous'" (p. 30, folio 19). Islamisation as "'purification of local cosmologies'" is "not confined to the encounter with modernity but represents a concrete manifestation of the very rise of Islam under different geographical and cultural conditions (Eaton 1993:284)" (p. 30, folio 19).
- **Peter van der Veer on the politicisation of religion**, as the editors report: since Partition religion "has become more intensely politicised than ever, shaping theories of both syncretism and anti-syncretism (van der Veer 1994a)" (p. 30, folio 19); "the politics of syncretism in South Asia defines religious 'purity' and 'hybridity' in political terms (Stewart and Shaw 1994)" (p. 30, folio 19). He criticises Nandy's view (1990), "aligned with that of Ahmad, that while orthodox fundamentalist ideas promote communalism, syncretic 'folk-religion' promotes communal harmony" (p. 30, folio 19).
- **The editors take van der Veer's side only partly.** "Yet against this view is a counter-reality, described by Basu and Saheb in the present volume, in which Muslim shrines do often create islands of Hindu and Muslim communal harmony" (p. 31, folio 20). They accept his caution that "The mere presence of Hindus at Muslim shrines… cannot be taken as a sign of a common, syncretic practice of folk-religion" (p. 31, folio 20), but conclude "Against van der Veer it may be argued that Hindus and Muslims do, in fact, still join together at shrines in amity" (p. 31, folio 20).
- **The mirror-reversal argument.** At Bava Gor, "Rather than Hinduism encompassing an impure Islam, it is Islam which is constructed as infinitely superior and more powerful, subjugating immoral, alien forces" (p. 31, folio 20). The general conclusion: "the charismatic power embodied by a Sufi shrine is crucially dependent on the cosmological ideas actors bring to bear on the saint's image" (p. 32, folio 21).
- **Against the marginality of shrine Islam**: "To argue that these practices are marginal to the 'true' Islam represented by the mosque and the 'ulama (religious scholars or officials) is to misrecognise the centrality of eschatological ideas about redemption and salvation to Islam in general (Werbner 1996a)" (p. 32, folio 21).
- **Jamal Malik's chapter, as the editors summarise it**: in "his subtle interpretation of a modern Urdu short story, written as a critique of the practices at saints' shrines", reformist ideas are "'still bound up with norms and symbols which are rooted in a long tradition'"; "although the progressive writer Ahmad Nadim is highly critical of the traditional order, the narrative structure he deploys in his texts follows a pattern similar to that underlying mystical Sufi discourses. According to Malik, modernist reformist values are influenced by a common symbolic framework" (p. 32, folio 21).
- **The volume's stated programme**: the contributors "interrogate the fabrications of charisma as they extend to concepts of the body, emotion, morality and sacred topography", across "Pakistan, India and Bangladesh" (p. 32, folio 21), helping explain Sufism's vitality "both as a mystical philosophy and as a utopian imaginary of an alternative social order" (p. 32, folio 21).
- **Samuel Landell Mills's thesis (chapter 2).** "The sanctity of pirs derives not from some imagined essence of Sufism or Islam, nor from the mere appropriation of a holy name, but from the structured use by pirs and devotees of a whole range of paraphernalia, associated with the saintly body" (p. 42, folio 31). Objects "extend the physical presence of the pir, and provide solid matter for the construction of his sanctity" (p. 43, folio 32), and the devotional imagining of that capacity, "often downplayed as a peripheral ramification of his spiritual power, is the crux of his 'saintly' performance" (p. 43, folio 32).
- **His move against Durkheim.** Durkheim's vision of the sacred attributed its "'contagious' quality to the fictive, abstract nature of religious entities as collective 'ideas awakened by the experience of society… [and] projected outwards'", hence without "a 'fixed material point of reference' (1966:21)" (p. 43, folio 32). Landell Mills counters: "the empowerment of sacred objects which from a Durkheimian perspective is seen to stem from the experience of collective psychology may in reality be derived from a more intimate experience, that of dyadic intercommunication with its moments of mutual incorporation" (p. 43, folio 32).
- **His anthropomorphism claim**: "the notion of a transcendent power itself rests upon an `anthropomorphism.1 [text-layer?: anthropomorphism + endnote 1]`"; the thinkability of "a limitless source of blessing" is "based on an extrapolation from the experience of a relative difference in the subtlety of interpersonal and person-object relations" (p. 43, folio 32).
- **The paradox of the parochial exemplar**: "because of this equation between person and object, the more personal and parochial the exemplar is, the more his universality is demonstrated" (p. 44, folio 33). Hence "Sufism can therefore be both accessible and appealing (an ideal instrument of conversion) and elitist and esoteric" (p. 44, folio 33).
- **The disciple as extension**: "The disciple who submits to a pir, in approximating the ideal of complete malleability, becomes another tentacle through which he acts… The chain of deference leads through the pir to the Prophet and to God" (p. 44, folio 33).
