## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Succession is charismatic, not hereditary and not codified**: "Since the authority of a pir was
  charismatic, it could not be rationally codified, and it was not automatically handed on within the
  saint's family. Instead, the mantle was inherited by the most spiritually qualified initiate—who
  could only be recognized by his own ineffable charisma" (p. 232, folio 221).
- **Miracle contests as an institution of succession**: the ambiguity of succession "led candidates
  to make more and more extravagant claims for their sanctity, and to demonstrate their holiness in
  competitions with rival 'friends of God' where each contestant displayed his spiritual strength by
  eating live coals, walking on water, and curing the sick" (p. 232, folio 221).
- These contests "could escalate into supernatural battles"; the weapons were "intangible" but
  "believed to be just as deadly as spears and swords, and casualties among the muridin could be
  high" (p. 232, folio 221).
- Disciples' own stake in inflation: "The muridin also elevated their leader to ever more empyrean
  heights because of the great spiritual status they themselves gained through their personal
  association with a great saint" (p. 232, folio 221). A rival's shaikh "might be presented as the
  present qutb, or at least as one of the most elevated members of the hidden holy administration"
  (p. 232, folio 221).
- Posthumous elevation: overstatement occurred "even when spiritual leaders made absolutely no
  grandiose claims for themselves in life, but were elevated to sainthood after their deaths by
  devotees who had their own reasons for deifying their dead pir" (p. 232, folio 221).
- **The anti-Sufi reform front in South Asia**, as Lindholm names it: "Puritan reformers influenced by
  Saudi Arabian Wahhabism, preachers instructed in the textualism of the Deobandi school (Metcalf
  1982), and zealots inspired by the messages of self-taught scholars such as Maulana Maududi"
  (p. 235, folio 224).
- **Hajj as a vector of anti-Sufi opinion**: the attacks "resonated with the ideals of the expanding
  Muslim middle class, especially as many South Asian Muslims have ventured to Mecca and discovered
  the low estate of Sufism in the Islamic heartland" (p. 236, folio 225). Gardner is quoted: such
  Muslims separate themselves "from the cults of charisma and miracles which are increasingly left to
  the poor and powerless" (Gardner 1995:147) (p. 236, folio 225).
- **Pirs' scholarly defence**: "pirs have been staunchly supported by a number of friendly 'ulama, who
  marshal scriptural arguments to support Sufi practices", and "many South Asian shaikhs are often
  also religious scholars, and have proven to be well able to defend themselves by citing holy texts"
  (p. 236, folio 225).
- **Sufism in the South Asian diaspora**: "Sufi brotherhoods may actually have gained converts among
  South Asian immigrants to Europe, who join tariqas in order to find moral and social support in
  their new, foreign environments (see Werbner 1996b)" (p. 236, folio 225).
- **The economic base of South Asian lodges under colonial rule**: "These brotherhoods held
  substantial local power as a result of their early connections with the Mughal state, their large
  entitled land holdings, and their armies of loyal supporters" (p. 237, folio 226).
- **Indirect rule as the mechanism**: the British "were more or less content to leave local matters
  alone, so long as peace was maintained and taxes paid"; Sufis "were usually equally content to
  maintain the peace as long as they were allowed to continue their religious routines and keep their
  estates and authority" (p. 237, folio 226). The contrast drawn is with "more interventionist Italian
  and French administrations" in the Middle East (p. 237, folio 226).
- **Post-independence vote-broking**: "Secular aspirants to political power in South Asia were
  therefore obliged to make coalitions with and concessions to local power brokers, including the
  independent and powerful Sufi brotherhoods, who could offer voting blocs in return for state
  favouritism. The brotherhoods therefore had concrete advantages to distribute to followers, and
  could keep their loyalty" (p. 237, folio 226).
- The contrasting Middle Eastern case: "brotherhoods there lost all political or economic
  independence, and had little to offer their disciples" (p. 237, folio 226).
- **The two Hur rebellions** "against the British in the Sind by the followers of pir Pagara" are
  named as exceptions to Sufi quietism under colonial rule, alongside "the numerous religiously led
  uprisings in the NWFP" (pp. 241–242, folios 230–231).
- **Class and the interpretation of karamat**: Gilsenan "notes that the more educated, respectable
  and wealthy members of brotherhoods see karamat (miracles) as connected to learning and character,
  while lower-status members cite dreams and voices as evidence of their master's powers" (p. 241,
  folio 230). Gardner is cited for "a parallel phenomenon in Bangladesh among newly wealthy Muslims,
  who now seek to 'sanctify' their ancestors by claiming them to have been extraordinarily pious and
  orthodox" (p. 241, folio 230).
- **Gender of the Sufi pathway**: "Sufis were overwhelmingly male, though some women have followed
  the pathway, and the imagery of women in esoteric Sufism is often very positive" (p. 241,
  folio 230); in the body text, "all men (although not women…), regardless of standing, could and did
  meet and interact without deference" (p. 233, folio 222).
- **Etymology of "Sufi" as the book gives it**: "Some believe the uniform of the early saintly
  preacher-ascetics was a rough robe of wool (suf), hence the term 'Sufi'" (p. 241, folio 230).
- **Greeting and deference as institutions**: "The handshake and the embrace, which are signs of
  equality and intimacy, are Middle Eastern institutions" (p. 233, folio 222); "the legitimacy of the
  king was traditionally marked by a simple handshake given to him by his advisors and the 'ulama"

  folio 228).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- A bird's fatal discourtesy: "It is said that a bird that flew over Jilani's head without making
  obeisance was immediately blasted out of the air, while goats eating pasture belonging to a saint
  were believed to die automatically" — attributed by Lindholm to Hodgson 1974, Vol. II:227 and
  Serjeant 1981 (p. 234, folio 223).
- The stopped train: "train conductors who demanded a fare from a shaikh might find their trains
  suddenly immobilized, their watches stopped—but all came right again when abject apologies were
  made" (p. 234, folio 223).
- Shams's deafening and killing: "Rumi's mentor Shams magically deafened a pious murid, and forced
  him to recite in public that 'there is no God but God, and Shams-i Tabrizi is his Prophet.' When the
  student was attacked for this blasphemy, Shams roared with such might that he killed one of the
  attackers, proving his miraculous powers" — cited to Hodgson 1974, Vol. II:245 (p. 234, folio 223).
- Lindholm's framing of these, which he does not endorse as fact: "Other recorded miracles were
  rarely the kindly acts Westerners might expect"; "In folklore the pir maintains respect much as a
  sultan might: through the use of brute force" (p. 234, folio 223).
- **The popular image of the saint**, reported as belief, not as the author's judgement: saints "were
  thought to be hungry for power, extremely jealous, and suffused with a potent but polymorphous
  sexuality that allowed them to seduce those who went to them for spiritual help. Saints also used
  magic to punish anyone who failed to pay proper respect to them" (p. 234, folio 223).
- The Moroccan proverb of ambivalence: "God's mercy comes from visiting a saint and going away soon"
  (quoted in Westermarck 1926:228) (p. 235, folio 224).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Lindholm's core claim in this chunk**: extravagant claims for saintly authority "are in direct
  contradiction to the values of egalitarianism and individualism that are central to Islam and that
  are at the core of Middle Eastern society as well" (p. 232, folio 221), a position he says he
  argued at length in Lindholm 1996 (p. 232, folio 221).
- The egalitarian evidence he assembles is ethnographic and is quoted, not summarised: Rosenfeld on
  Bedouin nomads (Rosenfeld 1965:174), Antoun on Jordanian villagers (Antoun 1967:295), Black on the
  sedentarized Lur of western Persia (Black 1972:616), Rabinow on urban Morocco (Rabinow 1977:116),
  and Lewis and Hodgson on the absence of hereditary titles and the ordinariness of kings (Lewis
  1988:64, 23; Hodgson 1974, Vol. I:283, 344, 253) (pp. 233–234, folios 222–223).
- **He explicitly rejects the false-consciousness reading**: "Black's view that the ideology of
  equality is merely a form of false consciousness is highly debatable (Lindholm 1996)" (p. 233,
  folio 222).
- **A contrast with Europe he draws in a single line**: the egalitarian attitude to poverty "is in
  marked contrast to the European Mediterranean, where the poor are thought to have less honor than
  their superiors (Stewart 1994:132)" (p. 233, folio 222).
- **Ambivalence, not simple veneration, is his account of popular feeling**: the pir's qualities
  "also aroused qualms in the general public, who regarded the 'friends of God' with a mixture of awe
  and fear that sometimes verged on repulsion" (p. 234, folio 223); the saint was "followed when he
  could offer spiritual influence, ecstatic experience, and a variety of worldly connections and
  services, but abandoned when other sources of leverage and power were available that were less
  perilous, ambivalent, and demanding" (p. 235, folio 224).
- **He brackets the truth question**: "The truth or falsehood of the beliefs held by the common
  people in the Middle East about the 'friends of God' is irrelevant for the argument made here;
  certainly some saints were of a peaceful and kindly character" (p. 234, folio 223).
- **Periodisation**: "the zenith of Sufism in the central Muslim lands was during the Seljuk period,
  when the caliphate had fallen and a purely secular authority had begun to suborn and encapsulate
  the 'ulama", so that the lodges "offered Muslim masses the experience of moral unity under
  sanctified leadership, as well as an imaginary universal order overseeing the disrupted universe"
  (p. 235, folio 224).
- **Why Middle Eastern Sufism declined, in his own summary**: the delegitimization "was accelerated
  by modern conditions and especially by the dominant power of the central state, but was also rooted
  in a deep and pervasive conflict between Sufism… and the characteristic Middle Eastern and Islamic
  values of equality and autonomy" (p. 235, folio 224). Its residue: Sufis "now serve only as
  mediators in marginal tribal areas, or provide ecstatic performances in impoverished urban
  communities, or serve as guides for cultured elites" (p. 235, folio 224).
- **He names and rejects the diffusionist explanation** of South Asian Sufi survival — that South
  Asia is merely "on the periphery of inexorable theological changes emanating from the central
  cultural core of Islamdom" and that purist Islam "will necessarily triumph everywhere" (Robinson
  1983): the position "has a degree of plausibility, but it also assumes that a standard reading of
  the sacred texts is both possible and likely—dubious assumptions at best" (p. 236, folio 225).
- **He also states the difficulty of his own comparative method**: cross-cultural comparison is
  "exceedingly problematic" and "the South Asian cultural universe is so incredibly complex and
  internally differentiated" that it is "very hard indeed to make any statements that hold true both
  for the Gangetic heartland of India and for the Indus Delta" (p. 236, folio 225).
- **Three reasons he does advance, in order.** (1) *Political*: indirect rule plus democratic
  post-colonial politics left brotherhoods economically and socially independent and able "to take the
  moral high ground" (p. 237, folio 226) — but "these cannot be the sole sources of Sufi authority in
  South Asia", since "colonial or state repression of a religious sect may lead not to the
  disintegration of the sect but to increased zeal… as was demonstrated in the Iranian revolution"
  (p. 237, folio 226), and Middle Eastern lodges that led liberation struggles were broken anyway
  (p. 238, folio 227). (2) *Insecurity*: "South Asian Muslims have also been plagued by a deep and
  pervasive sense of insecurity, engendered this time by the regional dominance of Hinduism"
  (p. 238, folio 227) — but this too "cannot be invoked as the primary reason", since Middle Eastern
  Muslims have suffered "the humiliation of domination by Christian and Zionist Westerners" and
  "found solace in reformism and Islamist radicalism" instead (p. 238, folio 227). (3) *Cultural
  hierarchy*: South Asian acceptance of saintly claims "is a reflection of a prevailing world view
  that valorizes precisely what the Middle Eastern world wishes to deny: rank and distinction within a
  unified community" (p. 239, folio 228).
- **The caste argument, stated with its own qualification.** South Asian Islam "totally disavows the
  Hindu caste hierarchy" and Islam's "affirmation of human dignity and equality… has made it so
  appealing to many low-caste members of Indian society" (p. 239, folio 228); yet South Asian Muslims
  "still accept, albeit without convincing religious or ideological foundations (Ahmad 1978:11),
  essential distinctions between persons of differing castes" (p. 239, folio 228). Madan is quoted:
  Muslims in India have "two sets of representations, one stemming from ideological considerations and
  the other from the compulsions of living" (Madan 1981:58) (p. 239, folio 228). Gaborieau is quoted
  **in French, untranslated**, on Nepali Muslim bangle-makers (p. 239, folio 228).
- **The substitution thesis, his strongest formulation**: "Sufi saints in South Asia therefore were
  and are the Muslim equivalents of sanctified Hindu gurus, and the acceptance of pirs in South Asia
  was and remains in large part a direct substitution of the former for the latter" (p. 240,
  folio 229).
- **The Weberian frame he names explicitly**: the Middle Eastern conflict "is the conflict between
  austere emissary religion and charismatic exemplary prophecy" (p. 240, folio 229); the Conclusion
  restates it as "the tensions implicit in Muhammad's charismatic enunciation of an emissary religion
  of unparalleled purity" (p. 240, folio 229), with Weber (1946) cited in note 1 (p. 241, folio 230).
- **Turkey is conceded as an exception** to the whole Middle Eastern argument: the 'ulama "were
  completely absorbed into the Ottoman state", so that after the Ottoman fall "the only source of
  religious legitimacy were the Sufi sects, which remained strong despite Ataturk's attempts to
  suppress them"; he adds "a deep tradition of saint worship in rank-conscious Turkish society,
  especially in the military, where spiritual hierarchies date back to Central Asian shamanistic
  practices" (p. 241, folio 230).
- **The NWFP is excluded from "South Asia" by fiat**, a methodological move worth flagging for any
  entry that uses this chapter on Khyber Pakhtunkhwa shrines: "from my perspective the NWFP has more
  in common with the Middle East than with South Asia" (p. 242, folio 231).
- **He qualifies the caste picture he has just used**: "much modern scholarship shows considerable
  social movement and entrepreneurship, even among spiritual virtuosos", including renunciants who
  claim holiness "by engaging in cannibalism and self-degradation or indulging in intoxication and
  excess" (p. 242, folio 231).
- **He cross-references the volume itself**: "As the material in this volume shows, despite potent
  attacks by various reformist schools, Sufism nonetheless remains an important force in South Asia
  among all social groups" (p. 236, folio 225); and directs the reader to Werbner 1996a and Werbner
  1995a/1996b for the 'ulama-support argument and for "the history of one such pragmatic cult in
  Pakistan and the United Kingdom" (pp. 236 and 241, folios 225 and 230).

## Doubts

- **THIS CHUNK REACHES BOTH INDEXES, AND EVERY LOCATOR IN THEM IS A PRINTED PAGE NUMBER (FOLIO), NOT
  A PDF PAGE.** The Name Index runs pp. 245–248 (folios 234–237) and the Subject Index opens on
  p. 249 (folio 238). **No index locator may enter a shrine entry as a PDF page reference.** Every
  locator I have recorded above has been converted explicitly with the run's rule (PDF = folio + 11)
  and labelled as a printed page; the page reference attached to each such fact is the page the index
  itself sits on, never the locator. A consolidator must preserve that distinction (pp. 245–249,
  folios 234–238).
- **All four archive mappings in this chunk rest on bare index headwords, and none on Lindholm's
  prose.** `data-darbar` ("Hujwiri, A. 172", p. 246, folio 235), `mazar-e-iqbal` ("Iqbal, Muhammad

  `bari-imam` / "Bari Imam" (p. 249, folio 238), and **"Shâh Daulah"** with circumflexes and a final
