## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The lodge and its vocabulary.** "Inspired Sufi saints (shaikhs, murshids or pirs) founded tariqas
  (lodges—literally pathways) where they were surrounded by pupils (murids—ones who have made it
  their5 [text-layer?: their, with endnote 5 flattened onto it] will to enter the path, also known as
  saliks, wayfarers)", dated "By the twelfth century" (p. 224, folio 213).
- The alternative term and its regional variant: disciples "gathered together in a khanaqa (a Sufi
  lodge, zawiya in North Africa)" to "learn from their master, practice the discipline of the group,
  undertake collective rituals, and participate in the local community" (p. 225, folio 214).
- **Common property inside the brotherhood**: the novice "shared out all his worldly goods with his
  fellows, living with them in a communist community where distinctions of wealth, knowledge and
  strength ceased and the word 'my' was anathema" (pp. 224–225, folios 213–214).
- **The tomb as the engine of cult formation**: "After the founder's death, the tomb of a revered
  saint became the centre for the proliferation of new devotional cults around Sufi lodges
  (Trimingham 1971)" (p. 225, folio 214).
- **Silsila defined by Lindholm**: "Each new cult validated its authenticity by tracing a silsila, or
  spiritual genealogy, to the sacred founder (often Abdul-Qadir al-Jilani), who himself was linked
  back through to Ali or the first caliph, Abu Bakr, and if possible connected back to Muhammad
  himself" (p. 225, folio 214).
- **Dhikr as the revenue-bearing ritual**: "the ecstatic practice of dhikr (remembrance) soon became
  the central and defining 'liturgical' ritual (Gilsenan 1973) of successful Sufi orders, as dramatic
  group performances of remembrance drew crowds of onlookers, who often became lay devotees of the
  brotherhoods, giving money and patronage to them" (p. 225, folio 214).
- **The economy of a thirteenth-century brotherhood, itemised**: "By the thirteenth century,
  successful brotherhoods relied primarily upon emotional public preaching, redistribution of wealth
  in charity, spectacular presentations of dhikr, and the performance of miracles to gain the approval
  of the populace and endowments from lay brothers" (p. 231, folio 220).
- **Saints were not monastics.** "Sufi saints have never been able to withdraw completely into
  monastic isolation, but were often married men who raised children, worked as craftsmen, merchants,
  soldiers, bureaucrats, and so on, while simultaneously serving as the fountains of God's grace"
  (p. 228, folio 217). Many "advocated hard work, asceticism, and obedience to authority as
  disciplines leading to enlightenment, instilling in their devotees an almost Calvinist devotion to
  their daily duties in the workplace" (p. 228, folio 217).
- **Charismatic leadership and commercial success**: "The huge financial success of the South Asian
  Khojas, who are devoted to their spiritual leader, the Aga Khan, indicates the potential economic
  virtues of charismatic leadership—when the leader has a high degree of business acumen" (p. 228,
  folio 217).
- **Anti-shrine policy of the Wahhabi revival**: "the eighteenth-century Wahhabi revival in Saudi
  Arabia, an ethically rigorous austerity movement which promulgated anti-Sufi doctrines such as the
  prohibition of all worship at tombs and the cessation of Sufi practices such as dhikr" (p. 228,
  folio 217).
- **The saint's self-presentation as a costume**: "most saints dressed in special clothing, had long
  beards, moved with solemn dignity, showed no overt interest in power, appeared chaste and demure,
  carried no weapons, and spoke with deliberation. Others, taking the role of dervish, had begging
  bowls and wore patched robes; they wandered without a home, transformed in drug-induced trance"
  (p. 231, folio 220).
- **Hierarchy of deputies.** The present-day qutb is surrounded by "an elaborate status order of his
  khalifa (deputies)"; "the resident pir was the khalifa of a greater pir, who was supposedly under
  the authority of a still greater hidden master, and so on" (p. 226, folio 215).
- **Shrine-studies bibliography in Malik's list**, useful as institutional context: Troll, Christian

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Lindholm states the chapter's two questions up front**: why "charismatic Sufism first appeared in
  Islamic society, despite the fact that the message of Islam is overtly opposed to all forms of
  spiritual elitism", and why "Sufism has more or less vanished as an active force in the Middle East,
  yet has remained influential in South Asia" (p. 220, folio 209). He calls the treatment "admittedly
  … preliminary and sketchy" (p. 220, folio 209).
- **His thesis for the contrast**: Middle Eastern decline is "an historically conditioned reflection of
  fundamental tensions within the egalitarian Middle Eastern cultural ethos, the rise of modern
  ideals, and the heightened repressive power of the central state", while "the relative success of
  Sufism in South Asia has its roots in a sociocultural context wherein hierarchical distinctions
  between human beings are taken for granted and where the local autonomy and moral authority of
  powerful Sufi brotherhoods have largely been retained" (p. 220, folio 209).
- **The egalitarian baseline**, on Makdisi's authority: mainstream Islam is "characterized by the basic
  equality of all Muslims in the eyes of the law as God's submissive servants" (Makdisi 1983:85);
  Sunnis have "no authoritative body to interpret doctrine, no ecclesiastical council, no synods or
  pope, as in Christianity, no gaon as in Judaism, no sanctified imam, as in Shi'ism" (p. 220,
  folio 209).
- **The Weberian frame.** Sunni Islam is "the purest case of what Max Weber called emissary religion;1
  [text-layer?: religion, with endnote 1 flattened onto it]", in which "any claim to union with the
  deity is an unforgivable sin" (p. 221, folio 210). Against it Weber sets "exemplary prophecy, wherein
  the prophet is the living receptacle of a static, immanent, and abstract essence"; "Exemplary
  religion is the natural home of charisma" (p. 221, folio 210).
- **Why charisma nonetheless appears**: because "The Qur'an, being finite, does not and cannot
  explicitly cover all circumstances", which "opens the door not only for analogical reasoning but also
  for charismatics who claim a special capacity to intuit God's desires.2 [text-layer?: desires, with
  endnote 2 flattened onto it]" (pp. 221–222, folios 210–211).
- **The Calvinist analogy**, twice deployed: "in Islam, as in Calvinism, only God knows the state of a
  human soul; the blasphemous drunkard may be saved while the pilgrim who piously prays, fasts, and
  gives alms to the poor may be damned" (p. 222, folio 211); and, on Sufi work discipline, "as in
  Calvinism, the prosperity which sometimes followed … was understood by the followers to be a sign of
  divine approval and a guarantee of salvation" (p. 228, folio 217).
- **Muhammad's charisma was not invented after his death**: "Muhammad's charisma was not solely a
  posthumous creation. By all accounts, Muhammad was indeed an individual who drew his followers to
  him by his exceptional character; he was loved first, then obeyed" (p. 222, folio 211). Lindholm's
  conclusion for the section: "Charisma in Islam then is both a response to the anxieties aroused by
  its own austerity and a reflection of the potent and compelling character of the founder … a
  creative tension that has animated Islam ever since" (p. 223, folio 212).
- **Shi'ism as the failed charismatic route**: the search for the true imam "was prone to repeated
  splits, since in every generation there were a number of potential claimants to the imamate"; the
  Fatimid and Safavid regimes "fell prey to the inevitable destroyers of all charismatic political
  movements: rationalization and demystification" (p. 223, folio 212). Modern Iran is "seeking, against
  all odds, to escape a similar fate" (p. 223, folio 212).
- **Origin of Sufism dated on Massignon's authority**: "According to Louis Massignon (1954), by the
  late eighth century a few of these early hadith transmitters had evolved into the pioneers of
  Sufism" (p. 224, folio 213); the ascetics "withdrew into remote regions where, like Muhammad, they
  preached to the rural people, who were drawn to the missionaries because of their austerity,
  dedication, and learning.4 [text-layer?: learning, with endnote 4 flattened onto it]" (p. 224,
  folio 213).
- **Walis defined**: "These walis or 'friends of God' believed they were destined to renew the faith
  and stifle the evil practices of the day", requiring "an actual transformation of the devotee through
  strict discipline in channeling and controlling the passions (nafs)" (p. 224, folio 213).
- **The Sufi's structural problem**: having no "sure signposts for his quest", "how could the murid be
  sure he was not deluding himself, mistaking his own desires for those of God, and allowing enthusiasm
  to lead him into heresy or insanity?" (p. 224, folio 213). The answer is submission to the master in
  "a relationship of absolute love and trust (tawakkul)" (p. 225, folio 214), and the emptying of ego
  "through wajd, or 'finding'", an experience Schimmel (1975) defines "as that of 'instancy' rather
  than ecstacy [text-layer? or printed: ecstasy]" (p. 225, folio 214).
- **The hidden administration of saints** is Lindholm's key institutional image, quoting Macdonald: the
  khalifas form "a saintly board of administration by which the invisible government of the world is
  carried on" (1909:163) (p. 226, folio 215). Its function: it "provided ordinary Muslims with
  intercessors who could state their case to a fardistant [text-layer?: far-distant] and impenetrable
  God" (p. 226, folio 215), and, with Hodgson, "There might no longer be a Caliph with power in the
  ordinary political sense. But there remained a true spiritual Caliph" (Hodgson 1974:228) (p. 226,
  folio 215).
- **The heyday**, dated: "In its heyday in the twelfth century during the reign of the Seljuks, and for
  centuries afterwards, the network of Sufi brotherhoods exerted tremendous influence"; Sufi sects
  "could introduce themselves into the moral margins between the tribes and had an affinity with the
  hierarchial [text-layer? or printed: hierarchical] structure of the guilds and clubs of the urban
  quarters" (pp. 226–227, folios 215–216).
- **The decline stated and localised**: "by the twentieth century Sufism had ceased to be a moving force
  in the Middle East, though it still remained potent in South Asia, as the contributions to this book
  demonstrate" (p. 227, folio 216) — an explicit cross-reference by Lindholm to the rest of the volume
  (p. 227, folio 216).
- **Reformist movements named on both sides.** In North Africa, "salafism, which arose in the 1930s …
  as a response to French anti-Islamic propaganda", whose "Salafa reformers … vehemently repudiated
  Sufism as anti-Muslim, primitive, and collaborationist" (p. 227, folio 216); in South Asia, "the
  puritan 'ulama of the Deobandi school and later by various reformist movements such as the Tablighi
  Jama'at and the Jama'at i-Islami" (p. 227, folio 216).
- **Gellner's thesis, then Lindholm's qualification of it.** Gellner: anti-Sufi movements coincided
  with "the increased domination of the tribal and rural world by the new urban administrative and
  technical class", and reformist Islam is "universalistic, open, rational, this-worldly, egalitarian,
  and impersonal, and therefore has what Weber would call an elective affinity to the social conditions
  of modernity (Gellner 1983)" (p. 227, folio 216). But, Lindholm notes, "as Gellner himself has
  documented (1981:99–113), it is not at all evident that charismatically structured organizations,
  such as Sufi tariqas, are necessarily unable to adjust to modernity" (p. 227, folio 216).
- **Three explanations of the Middle Eastern decline are tested and rejected by Lindholm.** (1)
  Capitalism and modernity: "the practice of charismatic Sufism—or any charismatic religion—is not
  precluded by the advent of capitalism or modern complexity" (p. 228, folio 217). (2) Wahhabism: "if
  Wahhabism succeeded in undermining Sufism in the Middle East, why did it not do so in South Asia,
  where the Wahhabis were also vigorously active?" — and anyway "some major Sufi orders, such as the
  Nasiriyya and the Sanusi", adopted "Wahhabi-like purification of Islam", the "neo-Sufi movements, as
  Fazlur Rahman has called them (Rahman 1982)" (pp. 228–229, folios 217–218). (3) Capitulation to
  colonialism or the state: "The Sanusi of Libya, for example, headed the struggle against Italian
  domination"; "in Turkey Naqshbandi Sufis led a rebellion to free Kurdistan in 1925", and earlier Sufi
  rebellions "led to the rise of the great Safavid dynasty in Iran, and to the ascendance of the
  Almoravids and Almohads in North Africa and Spain" (p. 229, folio 218).
- **What Lindholm accepts as a proximate cause**: "present-day Middle Eastern state regimes,
  notoriously authoritarian and undemocratic, have made it a policy to suppress all dangerous
  oppositional voices", so Sufi preachers "have lost what remained of their popular authority, and have
  been succeeded by Islamist zealots.7 [text-layer?: domination, with endnote 7 flattened onto it]"
  (p. 229, folio 218).
- **His own hypothesis, stated as such**: "I hypothesize that this cause is to be discovered in the
  compulsive tendency of Sufi sects to exaggerate the powers of their founders, which led them to set
  themselves at irreconcilable odds with the ascetic and egalitarian principles that animate Middle
  Eastern society" (p. 230, folio 219).
- **Nicholson's counter-reading of the boasting**, which Lindholm quotes at length: "As the Bedouin
  brags about himself in order to assert the dignity of his tribe, so when the Mohammedan saints boast
  of the unique endowments which God has bestowed upon them, it is not self-glorification, but
  thanksgiving" (Nicholson, ibid.: 173) (p. 230, folio 219).
- **Why the arrogance is sharper in Islam than elsewhere**: "the characteristic arrogance of mystics is
  amplified in Islam, where the theology of an absolute, omnipresent, omnipotent God points toward
  complete merger—and incomparable powers for the one who is merged" (p. 230, folio 219).
- **The "drunken" school's victory** over al-Junayd's sober school is made the mechanism: it
  "emphasized precisely the devotional practices of dhikr and other mystical disciplines that pushed
  adepts to believe themselves merged in 'naughted' unity with an absolute deity—and then to claim their
  natural due as God's earthly vessel" (p. 230, folio 219).
- **Market competition among orders**: "Sufi sects such as the Qalandariyya, lacking a doctrine beyond
  the urgent quest for a transcendent state of consciousness, became ever more successful; in
  competition, the masters of established tariqa also had to appeal to a sensation-seeking public"
  (p. 231, folio 220).
- **The parallel retreat of the intellectuals** into Ibn al-Arabi's system, in which "ordinary people
  were inferior 'link animals' (Massignon 1952)" (p. 231, folio 220).
- **The charlatanism problem, which is where the chunk breaks off**: "because the spiritual status of a
  pir rested primarily on a supposed inner transformation merely symbolized by his outer appearance and
  actions, there was a great potential for charlatanism" (p. 231, folio 220).
