## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The legal distinction that governs shrine property in Bangladesh, set out in full.** "there is a
  distinction between the lodges inhabited by pirs, popularly known as darbar, or 'courts', where a
  living descendant of the founding pir is in control and which are therefore entitled to remain in
  the private sector, and dargah (a legal category), where a tomb exists but the caretakers (khadem)
  who run it are no longer of the same bloodline. At this point the place of burial becomes subject
  to government regulation" (p. 46, folio 35).
- **State encroachment and its three stated motives.** "In recent years the government has been
  attempting to extend its grip upon the mazar (a generic term for saintly tombs) in Bangladesh, in
  order to regulate their activities, appropriate their income and ensure that their influence is
  not subversive" (p. 46, folio 35). No date is attached to "recent years": **(not stated)**.
- **The endowment loophole.** "Although both darbar and dargah can be legitimate recipients of
  endowments (waqf), which legally require government administration and scrutiny, endowments to
  living saints do not become waqf, and thus many lodges can remain free of government interference
  in their administrative and financial affairs" (p. 46, folio 35).
- **Tax.** "One complaint I often heard against pirs was that they paid no income tax on the huge
  income which they derived from pilgrims and supplicants" (p. 46, folio 35). No figure is given:
  **(not stated)**.
- **Succession as conflict.** "Almost always, this involves post-mortem struggles between the
  families of a pir and his unrelated devotees for control over his identity and name, and the
  benefits they bring" (p. 46, folio 35).
- **ʿUrs as the load-bearing economic ritual.** "the death-day celebration is the crucial ritual of
  almost every pir cult I witnessed, not least for economic reasons" (p. 52, folio 41). "Devotional
  activity centres power in the darbar (space-wise) and reinvigorates it at the annual 'urus
  (time-wise)" (p. 50, folio 39); the ʿurs is glossed as the "ritual commemoration of the day of his
  death" (p. 50, folio 39).
- **ʿUrs at Modaleb Shah.** "At the time of 'urus the dargah overflows onto the surrounding streets,
  and a kind of imagined universality is celebrated, even though there are many in the locality who
  disapprove of such revelry" (p. 52, folio 41).
- **ʿUrs at Atroshi.** It "identifies the pir, the discipleship and the place in order to rehearse
  an ideal image of 'Muslim Bangla', uncompromised by economic, political and cultural subordination
  to the West and to India" (p. 52, folio 41). At the ʿurs of the Atroshi pir's own preceptor,
  "thousands of disciples of the pir of Atroshi cry when he takes to the specially constructed
  stage" (p. 56, folio 45).
- **Architecture at Atroshi.** "Architecturally, the place is unremarkable except for the mosque,
  which has been taking shape gradually since the late 1980s" (p. 49, folio 38). Later: the pir has
  "constructed a mosque on the scale of the Taj Mahal which will be his enduring monument" (p. 59,
  folio 48).
- **Architecture at Modaleb Shah.** Through the main door "one reaches a kind of yard. Beyond it is
  a small brick room where food is cooked and some khadem sleep on rush mats. The pir is represented
  by the tomb edifice which exists within its own enclosure, accessible only to male visitors"
  (p. 52, folio 41). The devotee "enters this enclosure individually and prostrates himself before
  the tomb", while "the wider enclosure of the darbar is an area of public ritual and celebration
  where music is made, food is consumed" (p. 52, folio 41).
- **Visitor numbers and patronage at Atroshi.** "The place is famous as a pilgrimage centre for
  hundreds of thousands (claimed as millions) of devotees" (p. 49, folio 38). "As a result of the
  patronage of the president's followers, there was a big increase in visitors and income to the
  darbar, and the pir became famous throughout Bangladesh" (p. 49, folio 38).
- **Audience regime at Atroshi.** "The pir gives darshan (appearance) three times a day, and the
  disciples who have come to petition him are managed in such a way as to prevent their becoming too
  burdensome, clinging or obstructive to others waiting behind them" (p. 49, folio 38). Because of
  "the sheer size of his following, the pir is available to disciples only within a rigid framework
  of etiquette rules" (p. 49, folio 38).
- **Total control of the site.** "Nothing is allowed to happen there without the pir's
  authorisation" (p. 49, folio 38); the site "runs according to a fixed pattern in which everything
  has to be instigated by the pir and can be changed arbitrarily only by him and by nobody else.
  This includes prayer times, eating and all practical work" (p. 50, folio 39).
- **Commercial ventures.** The Atroshi pir, "having nearly completed a 600-bed hospital, has also
  entered the textile business, building a spinning mill and importing Chinese machinery to compete
  with the influx of Indian produce" (p. 59, folio 48).
- **Party politics, dated.** "The growth of the Atroshi pir's stature during Ershad's regime
  (1982–1990), led first to his forming a songarton, or association, in 1986, and then a political
  party (the Zaker Party) in 1989. Despite an expensive campaign in the 1991 election, the party won
  no seats" (p. 59, folio 48). The pir declared the party "was not a political party in the ordinary
  sense", existing "only… to defend Islam from external threats, a coded language for Indian
  (post-secular 'Hindu') domination" (p. 59, folio 48).
- **Silsila of the Atroshi pir.** "The pir of Atroshi affiliates himself to the
  Naqshbandi-Mujaddidiya Tariqa, which has a history of involvement in politics in north India,
  Pakistan and Afghanistan" (p. 59, folio 48).
- **Recruitment as career.** "Disciples who bring in new people to the tariqa are given high status,
  as well as the straightforward respect which is due to those who are tariqater boro, senior within
  the brotherhood. One enhances one's position as a disciple by recruiting new disciples… or by
  contributing one's money and labour" (p. 59, folio 48). "As they acquire wealth, some of it is
  reinvested in development of their resources" (p. 59, folio 48).
- **Bodily protocol at tombs.** "Respect is offered to a pir's tomb as if to a living master"
  (p. 56, folio 45). At Korosani Baba's urus, "I was ushered into the tomb room and was invited to
  touch my forehead to the eastern end of the tomb, where the feet of the pir were pointing
  respectfully away from Mecca" (p. 56, folio 45). At Modaleb Shah's tomb, "On nearing his tomb the
  devotees enter complete prostration, as to God in orthodox prayer" (p. 56, folio 45).
- **Dress inversion at Modaleb Shah.** "All of the dead pir's Hindu disciples were commanded to wear
  lungi (the Muslim lower garment), and the Muslims were instructed to don dhoti (the Hindu lower
  garment), reversing their normal dress codes" (p. 56, folio 45).
- **Thursday-night music at the mazar of Modaleb Shah, timed.** "the music starts at eight and
  continues for three or four hours. All of the songs are murshidi gan, the songs of the spiritual
  guides" (p. 57, folio 46); "At eleven, the music and dancing stop abruptly and people disperse"
  (p. 57, folio 46). "The proceedings in the women's section are already considerably more intense
  than in the male devotees' section" (p. 57, folio 46). The dance is described: men "move as if
  running on the spot… as if scooping blessing into the heart and hurling devotion back out"
  (p. 57, folio 46).
- **Food as blessing.** Objects associated with the pir — "tombs, buildings, land, furniture, food,
  amulets" — "are described by the adjectival noun tabarok (full of blessing)" (p. 46, folio 35);
  "The giving of food to the pir which is subsequently consumed as tabarok seems to echo prasad
  (food offered is a deity in Hinduism) `[text-layer?: food offered to a deity]`" (p. 54, folio 43).
- **Devotional labour as the economy of the court.** The sweep of emotion at Atroshi "is enabled by
  the preceding devotional labour (khidmat), whether physical, clerical, economic or mental, of the
  disciples under the aegis of the pir" (p. 56, folio 45).
- **Vocabulary the chapter glosses in passing**: `jikir`, "the repetition of the divine name"
  (p. 49, folio 38); `doa`, "blessing" (p. 48, folio 37); `antorik` (inner) and `bajjik` (outer)
  (p. 48, folio 37); `choronduli`, "the 'dust of his feet'" (p. 54, folio 43); `darshan`
  (appearance) (p. 49, folio 38); `Sunna`, "body of traditional Islamic law" (p. 58, folio 47);
  `mullah`, "scholar learned in scripture" (p. 56, folio 45); `azzan`, "call to prayer" (p. 55,
  folio 44).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Landell Mills's central thesis, the "hardware of sanctity".** "In practical terms, the spiritual
  power of a dead pir is accessible to devotees through the material objects and structures with
  which he has come to be identified" (p. 46, folio 35); "It is the material qualities of these
  objects that makes their anthropomorphisation the key to the revelation of spiritual power"
  (p. 46, folio 35). "The hardware of sanctity, then, enables pirs and their devotees to elaborate
  and sustain an image, that of a miraculous agency ever poised at the brink of temporal
  intervention" (p. 46, folio 35).
- **A living pir builds his own tomb-effect before death.** "A living pir and his devotees
  progressively sublimate his public persona in the objective structures constructed around him, so
  that they can be taken as extensions of him" (p. 46, folio 35); "In this way, a death-transcending
  personality is constructed during his lifetime" (p. 50, folio 39). At Atroshi the pir's devotional
  labour "will be eternalised, having been detached from his physical person even in his lifetime"
  (p. 50, folio 39), so that death "will represent the consummation of his sanctity" (p. 50, folio
  39).
- **Imitation and institutional inheritance.** "Pirs, by imitation, inherit the institutions of their
  predecessors" (p. 46, folio 35); less successful pirs "may selectively imitate, or may emphasise
  their apprenticeship or service to, a more established pir" (p. 46, folio 35).
- **Growth is structurally required.** "most pirs' establishments are set on a path of expansion"
  (p. 48, folio 37); "When a pir moves from being of local interest to becoming a regional, national
  or international attraction, his putative spiritual power and his accoutrements increase in
  tandem" (p. 48, folio 37); "there is an implicit imperative to expand embedded within the pir
  identity" (p. 59, folio 48).
- **Stillness and action; authority distinct from the ʿulama.** Pirs "can enjoy a kind of authority
  distinct from that of the scripturally learned ('ulama) who form a worldly class of ritual
  specialists, or that of politicians who are seen as primarily pragmatic and self-interested"
  (p. 47, folio 36). "Each pir must blend action and stillness, the visible and the unseen, into an
  image of power itself" (p. 47, folio 36).
- **Devotees complete an unfinished image.** The pir "is offering very limited information about
  himself to the disciples in the anticipation that devotees will be able to fill in the gaps
  themselves" (p. 52, folio 41); "Devotees are offered a set of unfinished scripts which they must
  complete by themselves" (p. 53, folio 42); "ritual is allowed to leave intention obscure" (p. 53,
  folio 42).
- **Tripartite structure of a pir's identity** — "an inner self which forms a sanctum sanctorum into
  which the privileged few may have an insight, an outer appearance which devotees may reverentially
  harness, and a world of effects (miraculous experiences)" — "is reflected in the structure of the
  places saints in Bangladesh occupy before and after death" (p. 51, folio 40).
- **The tomb as the one symbol that unifies a very varied field.** "The Islamic saints of Bangladesh
  vary enormously in their attributes, and sometimes it is only through the use of the tomb symbol
  that some commonality and shared meaning in the pir category is evoked" (p. 50, folio 39). "The
  tradition is thereby susceptible to renegotiation, renewal and change" (p. 50, folio 39).
- **Neo-Sufi counter-current.** The pir of Sayedabad's refusal to make disciples "is likely to
  strengthen the influence of the orthodox conception of pirs in neo-Sufi discourse, which downplays
  their supernatural powers and instead depicts both living and historic pirs as pious men whom God
  may favour, but who have no esoteric power to call their own" (pp. 50–51, folios 39–40).
- **Sufism as millenarism with a concrete focus.** "What gives Sufism its distinct flavour is its
  mortuary symbolism and its permanent dwelling in the charismatic moment" (p. 46, folio 35); pirs
  "linger in a personalised avant-armageddon liminality" (p. 47, folio 36). Landell Mills compares
  "the suspension of time by Hindu Aghori renouncers described by Parry (1982)" (p. 47, folio 36)
  and invokes Bloch 1986 on millenarist "abandonment of ordinary productive activities" (p. 47,
  folio 36). "Sufism, by privileging the tomb, not only brings this sense of anticipation to a pitch
  of intensity, but also gives it a concrete focus and grants it perpetuity" (p. 47, folio 36).
- **Hindu idioms, consciously deployed.** Babb (1986, 1987) "suggests that Hindu holy men enable
  devotees to experience a transcendental wholeness by being 'known'"; Eck (1985) "describes the
  all-seeing eye as being at the centre of Hindu temples, and this can be applied to the structure
  of the darbar/mazar complex" (p. 54, folio 43). But "these idioms are used self-consciously by
  pirs such as Atroshi to convey 'Islamic' messages"; pirs represent "an image of indigenous culture
  and personhood subordinated by the reality of divine power, of Bengal made Islamic" (p. 54, folio
  43).
- **Postcolonial reading of shrine architecture.** Against "the perceived British interruption of
  the temporal dominion of Islamic rulers in Bengal", Maishbandar's Mughal-inspired design implies
  "the ideals of Islam are still sovereign" (pp. 55–56, folios 44–45); "As a self-consciously
  Islamic building, antithetical to colonialism and modernity, it suggests that the Sufi tradition
  offers a more enduring truth" (p. 56, folio 45). "There is a historical depth to Sufism as a
  source of postcolonial identity" (p. 55, folio 44).
- **Pirs contrasted with Islamists.** "Whilst Islamists seek to bring the unrealised order of Islam
  into the world, pirs are impresarios of a hidden reality of divine power in the present" (p. 55,
  folio 44). Unlike the Islamist view of Islam "as transcending history and demanding realisation",
  pirs' "embodiment and the material and ritual structures which pirs bequeath enfold layers of
  history" (p. 55, folio 44).
- **Mimesis of the Prophet, and why it does not usurp him.** At Atroshi "the domination of the pir
  over his darbar evokes the Prophet as the unique individual in whom the political and spiritual
  leadership of the umma were combined" (p. 58, folio 47), citing Combs-Schilling 1989:87. "mimesis
  does not so much usurp the Prophet's position as implicitly acknowledge that there is no
  unmediated experience of God (Katz 1983:4). Without human exemplars the transcendental has neither
  salience nor identity" (p. 58, folio 47).
- **Why the Zaker Party failed — his own explanation, offered as likelihood not fact.** "it seems
  likely that we are concerned with a disjunction: although people might credit the pir with
  spiritual authority, they were less ready to grant political power to his family and their
  henchmen" (p. 59, folio 48). He also suggests the push may have come from below: "many 'disciples'
  of the pir were opportunists seeking to tap into the connections of the darbar to members of the
  governing regime" (p. 59, folio 48).
- **Spiritual authority requires separation from politics.** "in Bangladesh at least, the Prophet's
  singular fusion of spiritual and temporal authority cannot be surpassed by his imitators, because
  their 'spiritual' authority demands a materially enshrined separation from the hurly-burly of
  routine politics" (p. 58, folio 47).
- **Peak moments sustain the "active" tomb.** "there have to be peak moments of presence objectified
  by devotees in order to maintain the notion of the 'active' tomb… these are moments when the
  living not only can revitalise the image of the tomb but are remade in its image" (p. 57, folio
  46).
- **On nomenclature and essentialising**, citing Al-Azmeh 1993:51: "so the construction of saintly
  tombs throughout the Muslim world provides an 'objective correlative' around which concepts of
  saintly power can coalesce" (p. 56, folio 45).

  and `umarkot-amarkot-shiv-mandir` (plus `chandragup-baba-chandragup`, "Shiva (associated)").
  **Because the name could match seven or eight rows and the book names or implies none of them, no

  Naqshbandi-Mujaddidiya Tariqa** (p. 59, folio 48) is a silsila, not a figure; two archive rows

  site nor either principal figure is named or implied. No id. (3) **"Shah Jahlal"** (p. 50, folio
  39) is **not** the "Shah Jamal" of `shah-jamal` (Syed Shah Jamal Uddin Naqvi Bukhari, Lahore,
  d. 1671); the chunk gives him warrior and preaching attributes and no place, and the archive has
  no Shah Jahlal/Jalal row. No id. (4) **"Saddar Uddin Chisti"** (p. 57, folio 46) carries the nisba
