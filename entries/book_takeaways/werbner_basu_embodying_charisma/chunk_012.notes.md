## Practices, institutions, economy

- **What a shrine is, in Malik's summary**: "The shrine is an institution which is simultaneously
  therapeutic, social, economic and political; and in contrast to mosques, it also provides an
  alternative source of communication and identity, especially in the rather monotonous lives of
  peasants generally and women in particular" (p. 200, folio 189).
- Its services: "Here sick persons can be healed, destitutes find shelter and refuge, and, in
  political crises, sanctuaries can be used as catalysts for mobilisation" (p. 200, folio 189).
- **Custodial offices named and ranked**: "The holy man, the enlightened one and his descendants—the
  sajjâdah-nashîn and administrator (mutawallî, mujâwir) —exert absolute authority, and pious
  villagers, often illiterate, can come, with the passage of time, to be regarded by the
  shrineholders as servants, or even, indeed, as slaves" (p. 200, folio 189).
- The structural fault Malik names: "there is a significant contradiction between a postulated
  ritual equality and the practised hierarchy at the bigger shrines and lodges" (p. 200, folio 189).
- **Eighteenth-century reformist critique of shrine economy**: reformists "condemned what they called
  the exploitative and parasitical shrine cult and the monopoly of landed property mostly stemming
  from the ideology of the ‘feudal’ system" and "demanded a purification of Islam from local ideas
  and rituals" (Ansari 1984; Baljon 1989; Puhlitî 1988) (p. 200, folio 189).
- **State custodianship**: the Shâh Daulah tomb "was taken over by the Pakistan Auqaf Department in
  the 1960s" (p. 204, folio 193), and the Auqaf's "nationalisation policy… since the 1960s" left the
  mujâwir's authority intact, "who ideally represents state power" (p. 206, folio 195).
- **Endowment and revenue**: "no land was attached to it", producing "total dependence on the alms
  and offering of the followers" (p. 206, folio 195); the *Indian Antiquary* editor stresses "the
  absence of landed property in possession of the band, or any recognized right to succession to the
  leadership, and the entire dependence on the earnings" (p. 206, folio 195). Shâh Daulah himself
  "received large gifts’ in kind, but refused to accept land grants" (p. 205, folio 194).
- **Offering of children as a vow**: "The custom used to be to leave the child, as soon as it was
  weaned, at Shah Daulah’s khanqah [Sufi lodge], as an offering to him"; after his death devotees
  "would make a vow either to present the child when born or make an offering to the shrine"
  (p. 205, folio 194).
- **Begging as a shrine economy**: the Chûhâs are "passed on to a faqîr (religious mendicant) and
  made to beg in the name of Shâh Daulah" (p. 205, folio 194); "a band or order of faqírs makes a
  living out of a certain class of local microcephalous idiots, and the convenient existence of an
  important shrine" (p. 206, folio 195).
- A charge of deliberate mutilation is reported, not endorsed: "it is also suspected that these
  children are ‘artificially produced after their birth as ordinary infants’ (Ibbetson and Maclagan
  1911–1919:637)" (p. 206, folio 195).
- **Building date**: the shrine "had been reconstructed (1898)" (p. 206, folio 195).
- **ʿurs**: in ‘Bain’ "the girl’s maturity or sexualisation and the marriage proposal coincide with
  the anniversary of the holy man’s death, ‘urs, the unification of the pîr with God" (p. 209,
  folio 198), and "During the ‘urs the girl falls victim to the corrupted society" (p. 211,
  folio 200).
- **Langar**: Rano flings the skirt her parents bring "into the burning coals of the public kitchen
  (langar)" (p. 212, folio 201); her father later throws the mujâwir's shroud (kafan) "into the
  burning coals of the fire heating the water for the great ablution (ghusl)" (p. 212, folio 201).
- **The transaction at the tomb**: "a kind of a bargain between the parents and the mujâwir, like
  the mahr (bridewealth): the parents are declared dwellers of paradise (jannatî—without
  intercession of any authority) by the shrine administrator. In response they leave the child—the
  present (ni‘mat) —as a trust (amânat) at the dargâh" (p. 211, folio 200).
- **Gendered composition of the shrine public**: "the dargâh is a female domain, at least in
  quantitative terms—in contrast to mosques, which are frequented by men" (p. 208, folio 197), with
  the counter-note that "the power in shrines is usually held by men" (p. 214, folio 203).
- **Literary institutions**: *Angâre* (Burning Coals) "was published in 1932 in Lucknow by four young
  Urdu writers who had spent some time in England", was banned "some months later", and "since then
  it has not been republished in Urdu" (p. 202, folio 191); it was later "reprinted in Hindi", with
  controversial passages omitted and "several important Persian and Arabic words… replaced by
  Sanskrit idioms" (pp. 214–215, folios 203–204).
- The Progressive Writers' Association (PWA) "first came together in Aligarh and was soon constituted
  in London", held its "first all-India meeting in 1936 under the chairmanship of Premchand", split
  "after some years", was declared illegal in 1954, and was "formally re-established" only in 1987
  before fading away (pp. 202–203, folios 191–192).
- The **halqah-ye arbâb-ye dhauq (Circle of Possessors of Good Taste)** "was established in 1939",
  and it and the movement of **adab islâmî** "developed in reaction to this new literature" (pp.
  202–203, folios 191–192); in adab islâmî "individuals and their interaction with God are supposed
  to be of prime importance", its critique aimed at "the agnosticism and obscenity propagated by the
  PWA" (p. 215, folio 204).
- The **colonial printing press** "introduced in the nineteenth century had a decisive impact on Urdu
  literature" (p. 201, folio 190).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The Chûhâ legend, quoted by Malik from colonial sources**: "Shah Daulah, like other saints, could
  procure the birth of a child for a couple desiring one, but the first child born in response to his
  intercession would be a Chuha—brainless, small-headed, long-eared and rat-faced" (p. 205,
  folio 194).
- Its post-mortem continuation: "After the saint’s death the miracle continued, but in a modified
  way"; where a promised gift was withheld, "the spirit of the offended saint so worked on the
  parents that the next child was born a Chuha, and all subsequent children as well, until the
  original vow was fulfilled" (Ibbetson and Maclagan 1911–1919:630) (p. 205, folio 194).
- **Shâh Daulah's control of female infanticide**, attributed by Malik to "the colonial sources",
  worked "through his charismatic or miraculous powers (karâmât)" (p. 205, folio 194).
- A physiological explanation of the Chûhâs is reported from Kunjâhî 1985: "the bad conscience of
  parents, particularly mothers, set free certain chemical reactions that caused this peculiarity"
  (p. 206, folio 195).
- **The holy hand, as narrated inside ‘Bain’**: "The upper part [sar bâne kî taraf se] of the holy
  grave opened and the Pîr stretched out his holy hand [dast-e mubârak]. The culprit then was drawn
  to the shrine [mazâr sharîf] from wherever he was, laid his neck into the holy hand of the Sân’înjî
  [the pîr] and died on the spot [dher honâ]" (p. 210, folio 199).
- The sanction that follows from it: "Therefore, nobody dared to neglect Sân’în Daule Shâh’s orders"
  (p. 210, folio 199).
- **A dream as the instrument of the mujâwir's demand** that Rano recite at the dargâh: "this, he
  adds, Shâh Daulah had ordered in a dream" (p. 207, folio 196), the dream being "a popular way of
  conveying messages and legitimising actions (von Grunebaum and Caillois 1966; Azam 1992)" (p. 209,
  folio 198).
- **Rano's own miracles inside the story**: she is "able to act as a holy being by practising
  miracles (karâmât), again illuminated by divine light (nûr)" (p. 209, folio 198); "Rano represents
  baraka and bestows life: ‘Women used to come to [her] with their dishes and utensils filled with
  water’ to be blessed by her" (p. 208, folio 197).
- The journey miracle: birds "dived down to the camel as if they were drinking the nectar [sharbat]
  of the voice of my little daughter", and the father asks "what luminous [nûrânî] birds are these
  flying along with us?… They look like angels [farishte] coming from heaven!" (pp. 210–211, folios
  199–200).
- Possession: at the dargâh "It is said that she is possessed by a wicked ghost" (p. 207, folio 196);
  "In her agony she is ‘possessed’ by ghosts (jinn, bhût), as the dargâh family opines, in a manner
  reminiscent of institutionalised witch-hunting" (p. 212, folio 201).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Malik's framing thesis about shrines**: because a variety of activities including "socalled
  [text-layer?: so-called] ‘un-Islamic activities’" take place around them and because "shrines also
  often represent the last refuge of marginalised social groups, they can be regarded as microcosms
  of local Islam" (p. 200, folio 189).
- **Colonialism inherits and enlarges the reformist critique**: "British colonialism adopted this
  critique and developed it further into a comprehensive attack on the ‘Orient’, with the objective
  of legitimising colonial power and pushing through its notion of modernisation" (p. 200,
  folio 189); it "perceived local Islam as a backward, fanatic and obscurantist religion
  characterised by superstition" (p. 200, folio 189). "The nineteenth-century urban reform movement
  in India and elsewhere in the Muslim world was very much connected with this colonial expansion and
  encroachment on the minds of Muslims (Hourani 1967)" (p. 201, folio 190).
- **Reformism as class project**: eighteenth-century reformism "appealed mostly to restricted urban
  groups", and "was designed mainly to legitimise their new social status as traders, bankers and
  representatives of ‘scribal groups’, and to express an emancipatory world view (Gran 1979;
  Washbrook 1990)" (p. 200, folio 189).
- **The intermediary class and the new literature**: colonialism produced "new social classes that
  ostensibly found themselves caught between the polarities of ‘tradition’ and ‘modernity’", of "some
  form of intermediary societal status (Malik 1996)" (p. 201, folio 190); traditional genres such as
  "dâstân (story, fable, tale), poetry and elegies slowly gave way to new literary production", and
  "The only traditional mode of literary communication that really survived this aesthetic revolution
  was the ghazal (lyrical love poem, ode)" (p. 201, folio 190).
- **Social realism and the short story**: the short story "seems to have developed only in the first
  quarter of the twentieth century", "Influenced by Western and Russian models", and became "one of
  the most popular literary genres in contemporary South Asia, as well as in the Arab and Persian
  world" (p. 201, folio 190); it was "the most suitable approach by which to display a new
  self-statement vis-à-vis the colonialists" (p. 201, folio 190).
- **The reformers' contradiction, which is the chapter's spine**: "their criticism of their own
  culture required them to perceive an inherent oriental backwardness or non-development", while
  their self-definition "as an avant-garde was based primarily on their having studied in Europe and
  on their claim to privileged agency vis-à-vis the masses" (p. 202, folio 191).
- **Malik's stated method for the chapter**: to examine "Qâsimî’s literary expertise and his stand
  vis-à-vis traditional institutions and specifically shrines", by summarising a story "which
  illuminates elements of Islamic mystic culture veiled behind a critique of the shrine cult", so as
  to "make clear how far reformist writers are affiliated with popular Islamic traditions" (p. 203,
  folio 192).
- **What ‘Bain’ does, in Malik's reading**: it "draws a picture of the complex hierarchical world of
  shrines and their milieu as well as of the social stratification and stigmatisation of female
  children and their exploitation and evident rape by male authorities, even in a surrounding which
  is more or less a female domain, the shrine" (p. 207, folio 196).
- **The political allegory**: the story "implies a critique of the political culture based on an
  authoritarian and quasi-military system" (p. 204, folio 193); "the liberal democratic culture
  stands in contrast to the totalitarian military domination" (p. 211, folio 200); note 9 offers the
  key — "The girl’s age of 16–17 may call to mind the duration of the era between 1962 and 1979",
  1962 being the lifting of martial law under Ayyub Khan and 1979 the year "the military finally
  re-established its political authority (Jalal 1992:302 ff)" (p. 215, folio 204).
- **The neglected dimension Malik claims to recover**: the story "also elaborates upon the shrine
  cult in a literary-aesthetic way and reflects the inherent romantic mysticism of the author, who
  himself hailed from a mystical family tradition. It is this dimension of the story which has
  hitherto been neglected" (p. 204, folio 193).
- **The Sufi scheme Malik maps onto the narrative**: taraqqî (the mystical ascent), ishrâq
  (illumination), the graded souls al-nafs al-ammâra, al-nafs al-lawwâma, al-nafs al-mulhama,
  al-nafs al-mutma’inna, al-nafs al-râdiya, al-nafs al-mardîya and al-nafs al-kâmila, and the three
  levels sharî‘a, tarîqa and haqîqa (pp. 208 and 211, folios 197 and 200); the worlds ‘âlam al-nâsût,
  ‘âlam al-malakût, ‘âlam al-jabarût and ‘âlam al-lâhût are read into the camel journey (p. 211,
  folio 200).
- **Initiation without a chain**: Rano "becomes a follower (chelî) or murid of the dargâh", but in "a
  kind of Uwaisî tradition… or through the spirit of the legendary Khidr… rather than in an act of a
  formal bai‘a", and "no silsila (chain of spiritual descent) is referred to which would legitimise
  the spiritual position of the murshid (spiritual guide) and the murîd" (pp. 209–210, folios
  198–199).
- **Fear as the binding mechanism**: "fear is the prime motivational force inducing the villagers and
  adepts to be loyal to the moral authority emanating from the shrine", producing an identification
  such that "the villagers must experience a feeling of destroying both themselves and the mystical
  leader in the event of disobedience" (p. 210, folio 199).
- **Malik's verdict on the cult**: the story "shows that the shrine cult has become a falsehood void
  of any mysticism, or, in other words, an expression of corrupt political power" (p. 208,
  folio 197).
- **Where ‘Bain’ stands doctrinally**: "‘Bain’ stands in the reformist tradition. Mysticism as it was
  propagated by early Sufi masters like Hasan al-Basrî and al-Hallâj is accepted; the veneration of
  holy men, however, is repudiated as unlawful innovation, that is, bida‘" (p. 214, folio 203).
- **The cost of that demand, which Malik presses**: such claims "are, however, at the expense of the
  vast majority of the population, especially women, who find an alternative institutional and
  communicative framework in the shrine cult" (p. 214, folio 203).
- **The closing contradiction**: "even if many contemporary literati publicly argue against this kind
  of mysticism or shrine cult, which displays feudal and undemocratic structures, they still
  use—as has been shown paradigmatically with ‘Bain’ —traditionally known and accepted patterns of
  cultural articulation" (p. 214, folio 203).
- **Malik turns the critique on the writer too**: "In one sense one may also detect Qâsimî abusing
  Rano, for the sake of his supposedly political message" (p. 215, folio 204).
- **A self-correction in the notes**: "The underlying orientalist approach may, however, be
  questioned; it seems problematic to maintain that these new social formations were unilaterally
  inspired by colonialism… they not only referred to a long indigenous tradition, but also inspired
  the colonial power" (p. 214, folio 203).
- **What the story omits, per the Conclusion**: "there is no discussion of using children for
  economic purposes; one does not find direct hints of the female infanticide which was supposedly
  prevalent at this shrine in former times; and the rat-cult is not mentioned" (p. 213, folio 202).

## Doubts

- **"around AH 1676" cannot stand as printed, and I have not corrected it.** The chapter says Shâh
  Daulah "lived in Mughal times and died at an advanced age around AH 1676 in Gujrat" (p. 205,
  folio 194) — an AH year that does not fall in the Mughal period, while the archive row
  `shrine-of-hazrat-shah-daula-daryai` records the death year as 1675. Whether the book's error is
  the era marker, the numeral, or neither is **(not stated)**; a human must check the printed page
  before this date enters an entry (p. 205, folio 194).
- **The silsila is doubled in the book itself and must not be harmonised.** "twelve years at the feet
  of a Suhrawardi Sufi, but it is recorded too that he was initiated into the Qadiri order" (p. 205,
  folio 194); the archive row's silsila field reads Suhrawardi alone. Keep both as the book prints
  them (p. 205, folio 194).
- **`Dargâ‘î` is the one damaged word in this chunk that carries a fact.** The laqab glossed as "‘one
  affiliated to water’" is printed `Dargâ‘î` (p. 205, folio 194), while the shrine and the saint are
  named `Daryâ’î` on the same subject four lines earlier and at p. 204, folio 193. The gloss fits
  Daryâ’î; **the printed letters are not recoverable from the text layer and I have not guessed
  them** (pp. 204–205, folios 193–194).
- **`mazar-e-iqbal` rests on a single clause, not on a shrine.** "Muslim modernists like Muhammad
  Iqbal have referred to these developments in ideological terms as pirism" (p. 200, folio 189) is
  the whole of it; Iqbal is named as a modernist critic, with no tomb, date, place or silsila. The
  mapping is made under the author-only ruling of 18 Sep 2026 and the consolidator should weigh it as
  thin (p. 200, folio 189).
- **The bare-toponym convention (interim decision (a)) fires seven times; here are the rows each
  would take if the ruling reverses.** **Sialkot** (p. 205, folio 194) — **no single row follows**:
  the archive carries five Sialkot rows (`allo-mahar`, `gurdwara-babay-de-ber`,
