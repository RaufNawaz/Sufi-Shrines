## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **Saints alive in the grave.** "alongside living saints are their mythical predecessors, believed
  to be alive from beyond the grave" (p. 14, folio 3); "From a cosmological perspective, the saint
  remains present and alive in the grave and, indeed, as Jürgen Frembgen shows here in his study of
  Mama Ji Sarkar… the death of their beloved saint is conceptualised by disciples precisely in these
  terms" (p. 20, folio 9).
- **Intercession and baraka.** Saints "are thought to be able to intercede for the living in their
  search for personal boons (fertility, worldly success, health) by granting them saintly baraka,
  divine blessing" (p. 15, folio 4).
- **Miracle-working and hagiography, as an outsiders' description.** "Such saints, unlike Sufis, are
  described as miracle makers whose deeds are publicised through sacred hagiographies" (p. 15,
  folio 4). **The editors give this as part of the scholarly construction they are criticising, not
  as their own account.**
- **Bodily purity of a majzub before death.** Of Mama Ji Sarkar: "even the bodily functions that
  render ordinary human beings impure were seen in his case to be particularly pure; he had hardly
  any excretions and they did not smell. He had attained physical purity even before death" (p. 21,
  folio 10). Restated at p. 25 (folio 14): "The charismatic body, as Frembgen shows here, exudes pure
  polluting substances."
- **The Sidi saints' defeat of demonic forces.** The triad of saints "mythologically are said to have
  defeated dangerous ‘Hindu’ demonic forces"; between them they "embody cosmogonic forces of heating
  and cooling, of anger, love and rationality" (p. 21, folio 10). Attributed by the editors to Basu.
- **Saintly powers, listed.** The faqir who has killed the nafs becomes "the conduit of divine giving,
  blessing the world, with the powerful capacity to change and order both nature and human society;
  to overcome evil spirits and demonic possession; to heal and pacify" (p. 17, folio 6).
- **Death as perfection.** "In Ewing's analysis, the death of the majzub marks the point in his
  mother's narrative when he is recognised as a true saint. Death means perfection" (p. 20, folio 9).

## Arguments and interpretations

**All of the following is chapter 1, the editors' joint introduction, unless a contributor is named;
where a contributor is named the claim is Werbner and Basu's summary of that contributor.**

- **The volume's thesis.** "the power of ritual is seen to derive not from belief as a set of
  abstracted ideas but from ritual as a complex set of transformative, embodied, negotiated ethical
  and aesthetic practices and the experiences which their enactment generates" (p. 19, folio 8).
- **The false dichotomies the book attacks.** "spurious separations: between magic and religion (the
  one supposedly instrumental, the other ethical); between ritual and belief (the one aesthetic and
  symbolic, the other cognitive); between folk and official or normative religion; between syncretic
  practice and Islamic orthodoxy" — "imposed upon different facets of what is, we argue here, a
  single, total, symbolic reality" (p. 15, folio 4).
- **The split between "Sufism" and "saints' cults".** On one side Sufism as "an elaborate and coherent
  neo-Platonic theosophy of mystical realities", whose Sufis are "historically renowned mystics and
  ascetics, now long dead, whose writings and poetry today inspire mostly middle-class or elite urban
  circles" (Gilsenan 1982:244–246); on the other "the cults of saints or marabouts whose tombs are
  the focus of magical, superstitious ‘folk’ (North Africa) or (‘Hinduised’) ‘syncretic’ (South Asia)
  practices by the ignorant (eclectic, tolerant) masses" (p. 15, folio 4).
- **A named target in the discipline.** "a third scholarly discursive disjunction… best exemplified in
  anthropology by the work of Clifford Geertz and Ernest Gellner (Geertz 1968; Gellner 1981; for a
  critique see Baldick 1989:155–156), severs Sufism from its organisational underpinnings" (p. 15,
  folio 4). Trimingham (1971) is credited with having "spelled out clearly the generative connections
  between saints, cults and tariqa" (p. 15, folio 4). Rural saintly lineages, the editors complain,
  "are simply ‘there’, at most mediating as peacemakers between warring tribes (Barth 1959; Gellner
  1969)" (p. 15, folio 4).
- **Where the material comes from.** Reformist critique of shrine practice is an indigenous discourse,
  not only an orientalist one: the folk/official opposition "draws on indigenous Islamic reformist
  discourses critical of the practices at saints' tombs, as Jamal Malik shows in his chapter on
  reformist literary texts" (p. 16, folio 5). Those avant-garde writers "despite their modernist
  thrust… continued to draw on allegories of divine, mystical illumination" (p. 16, folio 5).
- **Contested tradition and the reification of belief.** "contemporary Sufism is a contested
  tradition, as indeed are all the different Islamic streams in South Asia (Metcalf 1982; Werbner
  1996a; Ahmad 1991)"; under attack, adherents "have been compelled to create discursive defences of
  their ‘beliefs’, which thus emerge as increasingly reified and remote from the practices that
  underpin them" (p. 16, folio 5).
- **The Sufi anthropology of the body, as the editors state it.** Sufism posits an analogy "between a
  denial of embodied desire (nafs) and the acquisition of true knowledge, gained by the detached,
  eternal, rational ‘soul’ (ruh)"; "the death of the desiring soul, the nafs, brings about the
  eternalisation of the body after death, while the soul retains its living agency" (p. 17, folio 6).
  "But this Sufi theory of charisma and the elaborate cosmology it has generated are not necessarily
  articulated explicitly either by saints or by their devotees. The Sufi theory remains an implicit,
  embodied form of charismatic knowledge" (p. 17, folio 6).
- **Three theoretical lineages the volume draws on**: "the processual, grounded in the work of
  Durkheim and of van Gennep on liminality in rites of passage; the aesthetic, inspired by French
  phenomenologists and poststructuralists; and the substantive, building on Mauss's insights into the
  symbolic embodiment of subjects through objects" (p. 18, folio 7). Victor Turner's Ndembu study is
  called "foundational" (Turner 1967); Richard Werbner (1989) is credited with showing "that
  substantive flows between persons, and between persons and places, are morally incorporative";
  McKim Marriott (1976) and Richard Kurin (1983, 1984) are credited with extending substance-flow
  analysis to South Asia and to Sufi cults (p. 18, folio 7).
- **A disagreement stated outright.** "In this sense one has to disagree with Asad's genealogical
  account of anthropological studies of ritual which, he argues, almost universally have tended to
  divorce symbolic analysis from ‘pragmatics’ (Asad 1993: Ch. 4)" (p. 19, folio 8).
- **A second disagreement, with Csordas and Shilling.** Their proposal "that the study of embodiment
  needs to look beyond nature-culture or social-biological dualisms in order to recover the pre-or
  unsocialised dimensions of emotion and sensuality" fails because "the moment of the emotionally
  expressive is also the moment of dialogical and social communication, that is, the moment of the
  cultural"; "We may speculate upon, but cannot reach, the biologically ‘unspeakable’" (pp. 19–20,
  folios 8–9).
- **Sufism as resistance and counter-narrative.** "Sufism too often occupies, as many of the
  contributors here argue, such a resistive space" (p. 19, folio 8); "Sufism, we show, creates its own
  alternative texts—utopian experiential imaginaries of other, possible world orders" (p. 19,
  folio 8).
- **Emotion beyond the saint-disciple dyad.** Existing studies of emotion in saints' cults "have so
  far been confined to a consideration of saint and disciple… (cf. Nanda and Talib 1989; Pinto 1989,
  1995)"; several authors here "go beyond the stress on dyadic relations to examine constructions and
  experiences of emotion generated amongst followers themselves and the organisations these create
  (Frembgen, Ewing, Basu, Werbner)" (p. 19, folio 8). **Samuel Landell Mills** is reported as "arguing
  against the Durkheimian view of symbols as collective representations", suggesting "that
  saint-disciple relations can indeed be interpreted as a model for all human dialogical interaction"
  (p. 19, folio 8).
- **Majzub and mastan.** "those drunk on divine love, the rebels and madmen of medieval Sufism (Eaton
  1978; Digby 1984)"; "In Islamic discourses, majzub represent the Other of orthoprax Sufism
  categorised as ba-shar, outside and beyond the reach of the Law (cf. Ewing 1984, 1988; Frembgen
  1993; Gaborieau 1986)"; their "madness" is "understood as a positive sign of proximity to God and
  absorption in love" (p. 20, folio 9). **Frembgen** works on "followers' experiences of a living
  majzub", **Ewing** on "the narrative experiences of the mother of a majzub" (p. 20, folio 9).
- **Ewing's argument as the editors report it.** Against "South Asianist ethnocultural interpretations
  of personhood that stress their cultural incommensurability with Western notions", she argues that
  "Beyond cultural differences… there are also shared assumptions, rooted in the present instance in
  maternal concerns for the health and happiness of children" (p. 21, folio 10). "There is sadness
  and a sense of guilt at the death of a son, even as there is pride in his canonisation" (p. 21,
  folio 10).
- **A question the editors say is rarely asked.** "One fascinating question rarely addressed in
  studies of Sufism is the sense of loss Sufi devotees experience when their saint dies" (p. 20,
  folio 9).
- **Basu's argument as the editors report it.** The Sidis' "perceived command of the full emotional
  spectrum of human passions, from love to anger, symbolised as a hot-cold continuum, enables them to
  transcend their lowly caste status andi nvert [text-layer?: and invert] hierarchical definitions of
  purity and pollution"; these place their cult "below those of Sayyid saints who claim descent from
  the Prophet" (pp. 21–22, folios 10–11). The result "places the cult ambiguously within the broader
  regional network of saints' cults in Gujarat" (p. 22, folio 11). At p. 25 (folio 14): "the mastan
  are the rebels in a respectable establishment of Sayyid Sufi shrines. When participating in the
  rituals of these shrines, they enact a carnivaleque anti-structure."
- **Pilgrimage: counter-structure, not anti-structure.** Against Turner (1974: Ch. 5) and in the face
  of critiques (Sallnow 1987; Eade and Sallnow 1991; Fuller 1993:212–213), "it seems more accurate to
  say that sacred pilgrimage creates not ‘anti’-structure but ‘counter’-structure"; Turner's key
  point nonetheless "still seems valid" (p. 23, folio 12). Regional cults "create spatially
  alternative focal organisations to those centred on bounded, territorially based states or
  administrative units", "inclusive rather than exclusive, tending to cross ethnic and national
  boundaries" (Werbner 1989: Ch. 7) (p. 23, folio 12).
- **Werth's argument as the editors report it.** The anonymity of the village shrines "is a key
  feature… of their placement as mediators between the dangerous forces of nature and cultural
  continuity"; the shrines "can be understood as standing for a broader and more inclusive ethical
  vision, beyond the little community: as guarantors of safe passage for stranger-travellers in the
  wilderness, they create connections between villages, across the wild spaces that separate them"
  (p. 23, folio 12).
- **Landell Mills's argument as the editors report it.** He "draws attention to the creation of an
  iconic image of the living pir through contrasting images of stillness and action. The stillness of
  the saint is like the immobility of the tomb, while the projection of his power depends upon a
  blend of action and stillness"; "A living pir thus reverses the image of the dead saint and
  anticipates his own death" (p. 24, folio 13).
- **Saheb's argument as the editors report it.** "As a powerful emperor incorporates the subjects of
  his state, so the pir of Nagore-e-Sharif, Sahul Hameed, extends the ambit of his embodied persona to
  the congregations of his followers or subjects throughout his spiritual (wilayat) and geographical
  domain, which includes Muslim and Hindu occupational groups and devotees from as far afield as
  Singapore and Sri Lanka" (p. 24, folio 13). Royal-court allusion is "a common feature of saint
  worship (Eaton 1982, 1984; Gilmartin 1984; Metcalf 1984)" (p. 24, folio 13).
- **Metonym and metaphor.** "the whole study of ritual embodiment, and of charisma as sacred
  embodiment, necessarily hinges on an understanding of symbolic movement as effecting both a
  metonymic and a metaphoric transformation" (p. 24, folio 13).
- **Inversion as the common mechanism.** "Cults of saints create realms of anti/counter-structure by
  reversals of quotidian logic"; the examples given are Werth's absent saint, Werbner's hajj reversal
  of the Ismail myth, Frembgen's pure polluting body and Basu's Sidi hierarchy (p. 25, folio 14).
- **Charisma against modernity (the section that opens where the chunk ends).** "Perhaps it is this
  anti-structural emphasis that accounts for the continued vigour of saint cults in Muslim South
  Asia" (p. 25, folio 14). Weber via Eisenstadt (1968:xv, xviii–xix): charisma as "antithetical to
  routine and rationalised institutional orders"; disenchantment as "the product of increasing
  rationalisation" (Weber 1948:245 passim); charismatic domination as "highly personalised, intense,
  expressive and irrational" (p. 25, folio 14). Foucault (1992:251, 241, 201) is used for the link
  between "self-mastery, truth and love" and for self-fashioning as "a precondition both of true
  knowledge and of lasting, other-oriented bonds of love and friendship" (p. 25, folio 14).
