## Practices, institutions, economy

- **An annual mela for a dead majzub, at a family shrine.** "His mela takes place every year. So Sa’in
  Sahib repeats all these things. Everything happens just as it was on the first day. They cook big pots
  of food. Qawwal [singers] come. There is qawwali [devotional singing] the whole night." (p. 189,
  folio 178).
- The mela is glossed by Ewing in a bracketed insertion as "the annual celebration at the family shrine"
  (p. 188, folio 177), and the ritual is said to be one "that is repeated every year" whose liminal time
  "is recreated as an annual ritual" (p. 191, folio 180).
- **Rites established after the death.** Rabi‘a "had already listed the several activities that were
  carried out as part of the rituals established by Sa’in Sahib and the family to commemorate Ashiq’s
  death" (p. 181, folio 170); among them, "After his death, Tariq started bhangra [a special kind of dance
  when someone beats the drum], because Ashiq liked bhangra very much" (p. 181, folio 170).
- **Offerings at the death, and their scale.** "First they collected the money in their shirt-tails. Later,
  there was so much money that they made a jholungia [a big cloth held up by four sticks]" (p. 189,
  folio 178); "The drum beaters came. A lot of money was collected…" (p. 189, folio 178).
- **The collection was foretold as part of the death.** Ashiq to Sa’in Sahib: "Seven people will come with
  drums. And many people will come. Much money will come. And tie a turban on me when I die." (p. 188,
  folio 177), and earlier to him alone, "You will collect a lot of money." (p. 188, folio 177).
- **The turban and the sera.** Sa’in Sahib tied the turban on Ashiq before he died, at Ashiq's own
  request, and "cried as he did it" (p. 189, folio 178); "After he died, a sera was tied on him [an
  ornament with gold threads that hang over the face, usually worn by a man at his wedding]. A turban was
  put on." (p. 189, folio 178). Ewing reads the turban as the moment "he adopts the appearance of a saint"
  (p. 190, folio 179).
- **A village-wide suspension of routine.** "The whole village did not cook for sixteen hours" (p. 189,
  folio 178), which Ewing treats as the start of "a liminal period—the suspension of everyday time" and as
  evidence of "his power to suspend the routines of a whole village" (p. 191, folio 180).
- **A circuit of melas.** After the family mela, "After a few days, there was another mela in another
  village", and Ashiq later "went with Sa’in Sahib to another mela in the next village. He got sick
  there." (p. 188, folio 177).
- **The ethnographers attended.** "a few days later, we, too, went to the village to participate in the
  mela that commemorates his death every year. We were also warned of its liminality, told that we could
  not be served like guests while we were there." (p. 191, folio 180).
- **Amulet-writing as the making of a sa’in**: "He sat at the shrine and started to write ta‘wiz. He
  became a sa’in." (p. 184, folio 173).
- **Succession.** During Ashiq's childhood Sa’in Sahib "developed a relationship with the boy and let it
  be known that he intended to designate Ashiq as his successor" (p. 181, folio 170).
- **A pir with murids, and a renouncer's economy.** Sa’in Sahib shops accompanied by "one of his murids
  [disciples]" (p. 181, folio 170); Muhammad Din came "because he says ‘Peace is available from Sa’in
  Sahib. I have no need for land and home.’" and, having taken "charas [hashish] and bhang [an
  intoxicating drink]", "when he came to Sa’in Sahib he gave it all up" (p. 183, folio 172).
- **Celibacy as institution.** "Our Sa’in Sahib is a langotia faqir [i.e. has made a vow of celibacy never
  to remove his loin cloth]" (p. 183, folio 172); he "did not produce children of his own" (p. 182,
  folio 171).
- **Marriage and divorce, with figures as given.** He married "his khala’s [mother’s sister’s] daughter"
  at "About 25 or 26"; "He didn’t return to the house for twelve years after his marriage. Instead, he
  sat in the dere [village meeting place] outside. After twelve years, his khala said, divorce my
  daughter." (pp. 182–183, folios 171–172). He wrote the divorce on paper; asked whether he got it from
  the court, Rabi‘a said "I don’t know where he got it. But he spent a lot of money on it." (p. 184,
  folio 173).
- **Where he ate**: "usually he ate in the dhera [men’s gathering place in the centre of the village]"
  (p. 184, folio 173).
- **Gendered deference in the household**: "Sa’in Sahib told me to put my dupatta [thin shawl, worn as a
  sign of modesty by virtually all Pakistani women either across the shoulders or on the head] on my
  head in front of him" (p. 182, folio 171).
- **The fieldwork's own economy.** "The field research on which this paper is based was conducted in
  1984–1985 and was supported by a grant from the American Institute of Pakistan Studies. I have used
  pseudonyms to protect the privacy of the people who were willing to share their lives with me."
  (p. 192, folio 181).
- **Malik on the shrine economy of South Asia.** "The cult of the holy man (pîr) and the associated folk
  piety is particularly evident in the countryside, where major landlords and large-scale land
  concentrations are crucial features, especially in Punjab and Sindh. In many cases, the local pîr
  represents the local landlord or is at least closely related to him." (p. 199, folio 188).
- **Malik on the pîr's exchange**: the pîr "guarantees the local villagers’ participation in divine
  blessing (baraka), which is tied to strict obedience (itâ‘a) and self-sacrifice by followers of the pîr
  (bai‘a means literally the buying of the soul of the adept or disciple by the saint)" (p. 199,
  folio 188).
- **Malik's gloss on ʿurs**: "the annual feasts (Arabic: ‘urs; literally marriage, i.e. the soul’s union
  with the beloved, God; cf. Turner 1979:97; Gellner 1985:80;" — the sentence breaks off at the chunk's
  end (p. 199, folio 188).
- **Malik on ritual authority**: "Authority is regenerated and stabilised through cyclically repeated
  rituals which have an affirmative character for the local population, are based on a normative
  consensus, and convey a collective identity." (p. 199, folio 188).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **Foretelling his own death.** Ashiq to his family before the last journey: "This is the last time I
  will see you. Now I won’t return."; to his aunts, "I went to them to ask forgiveness because I won’t
  return."; to his eldest sister, told to water his trees herself, "No, I won’t return." (pp. 187–188,
  folios 176–177). Rabi‘a: "I thought that he was talking nonsense [fazul]. But after he died, I realized
  that he had spoken the truth." (p. 188, folio 177).
- **The predictions fulfilled**: "After he died, all his utterances came true. The drum beaters came. A
  lot of money was collected…" (p. 189, folio 178).
- Ewing's framing: "The ability to predict one’s own death is generally recognized as a sign of great
  spirituality. It is an ability attributed to many saints." (p. 187, folio 176) — qualified in her note
  11, that "when describing a deceased parent, it is not uncommon for a person to attribute their parent
  with this ability and provide a detailed, idealized account of the parent’s last day" (p. 193,
  folio 182).
- **The porcupine.** Ashiq of the injured animal: "Ammiji, it will drink water, and then it will die. Let
  him drink the water." — "This very thing happened. After drinking the water, it died." (p. 186,
  folio 175).
- **A faqir's curse, as the village read it.** Sa’in Sahib's divorced wife remarried and "then she became
  blind"; "People began to say that it was because she was divorced from a sa’in", and "People said that
  she received a faqir’s bad-du‘a [bad prayer, curse; i.e. that Sa’in Sahib had cursed her]" (p. 184,
  folio 173). Attributed by Rabi‘a to "people", not asserted.
- **Prayer for children that works only for others**: "He prayed for other people for children, but no
  children were born in his house. All is Allah’s work. Sa’in Sahib says, ‘I pray, but God’s willingness
  is necessary.’" (p. 184, folio 173); Ewing's gloss, "He produces cures and children for others, but he
  could not do so even for himself, much less for her." (p. 185, folio 174).
- **Everyday deference to a majzub**: "people allow him to wander off with their goats; they give him
  gifts; shopkeepers call him to their shops for his blessing. This is the power of the majzub." (p. 187,
  folio 176).
- **A daughter's warning dream**: "Mother, you should go to the village. I saw a horrible dream
  [khofnak]." (p. 188, folio 177).
- **Black magic as a rival explanation.** Rabi‘a's account of Ashiq's condition moved "immediately" from
  calling him a sa’in to describing "the act of black magic (kala ‘ilm) (see Ewing 1982)" (p. 180,
  folio 169).
- **Westermarck's Moroccan cases**, reported by Ewing in note 1: the naked dead saint of Fez, and a man
  eating openly during Ramadan of whom it was said, "The poor fellow does not know what he is doing, his
  mind is with God" (Westermarck 1926:I, 49) (p. 192, folio 181).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Ewing: two explanatory frames held simultaneously, not resolved.** "These two explanatory models—that
  he was a sa’in and that he was ill—are tied to different historical moments in the evolution of her
  relationship with her son. She has not chosen one over the other as ‘reality,’ but rather experiences
  both as true." (p. 180, folio 169).
- **Ewing: the mother's ambivalence is the chapter's engine.** "He was a source of worry and
  embarrassment, a sign of her failure as a mother." (p. 180, folio 169); "Rabi‘a’s concerns as a mother
  were encompassed by a narrative of sainthood. Yet they were not erased. Both voices, both narratives,
  emerged simultaneously (Bakhtin 1981)." (p. 181, folio 170).
- **Ewing: interpellation into a discourse of sainthood.** "Rabi‘a did not describe the precise point in
  time when Ashiq was first described as a sa’in, but the process of Ashiq’s interpellation vis-à-vis a
  discourse of sainthood can be discerned from her stories." (p. 181, folio 170).
- **Ewing: two sources of authority in tension.** Sa’in Sahib's power "stemmed from two sources: he was
  Rabi‘a’s husband’s elder brother … and he had a special connection to God that was reflected in his
  status as sa’in" (p. 181, folio 170); they "reinforce each other but nevertheless co-exist in uneasy
  tension", because his fraternal authority "might well have been questioned had it not been for his
  status as sa’in" (p. 182, folio 171). She "expresses this ambivalence in stories that position him both
  as a social oddity and as a saint" (p. 182, folio 171).
- **Ewing: sainthood as operating at the edge of respectability.** He "was accused of being corrupt, of
  indulging in debauchery with women and drugs behind the cover of his spiritual claims" (p. 183,
  folio 172); "his identity in many respects operates at the very edge of everyday respectability and
  even challenges the foundations of everyday life" (p. 183, folio 172).
- **Ewing: transgression of gender as the sharper violation.** Anwar's aside — "He doesn’t look like a
  man—he wears gold earrings, pink clothes." (p. 184, folio 173) — leads to the claim that "he is guilty
  of an even more basic violation of social order, dressing like a woman", and that this disruption is
  "precisely the quality that makes sainthood so powerful: it forces people to confront the arbitrariness
  and limited nature of their everyday lives" (p. 184, folio 173).
- **Ewing: a Lacanian oedipal reading.** "This is a classic oedipal situation. To use Lacanian
  terminology, the father of these narratives is the representative of the Law, the Symbolic Order"
  (p. 186, folio 175), qualified in note 10 as "the father as signifier", not an actual father (p. 193,
  folio 182). "A saint may be simply an alternative representative of the Symbolic Order, as the pir often
  is … But the true Sufi, as Sa’in Sahib suggests, undercuts this Symbolic Order" (p. 186, folio 175).
- **Ewing: the majzub as escape, via abjection.** Ashiq "identifies with the abject, that which has been
  dismissed as impure, dangerous, and disgusting (see Kristeva 1982), and evades the discipline of
  everyday life" (p. 187, folio 176).
- **Ewing: death as the transformative event.** "The death reshapes reality and transforms the ambiguities
  of Ashiq’s strange actions and peculiar statements into the utterances of a majzub" (p. 189, folio 178);
  "The moment of death is the transformative event." (p. 189, folio 178); "From that moment, all of his
  ‘foolish’ statements and actions are retroactively transformed into the acts of a majzub." (p. 190,
  folio 179).
- **Ewing: the mother as mirror, and as the marginalised witness.** "She becomes his mirror … He has
  forced even his mother to recognize him as a saint. This recognition comes only through the mediation
  of Sa’in Sahib, who once again has inserted himself between mother and son." (p. 190, folio 179); "We
  hear many of the events by listening through a crack in the door, or sitting quietly facing the back of
  the saint’s head." (pp. 189–190, folios 178–179).
- **Ewing: the story is multivocal and should not be reduced.** "I have interpreted this story as the
  recognition of a saint and the creation of a ritual time and space in connection with that recognition.
  But the story is multivocal." (p. 191, folio 180).
- **Ewing's conclusion on the majzub.** "So what does it mean to be majzub, to have one’s mind ‘burned by
  God?’ For me, for now, it means to be able to step outside of the confines of a restrictive social
  order" (p. 191, folio 180), through "the acknowledgement of that which in other circumstances is
  abjected, dismissed as disgusting, polluted, childish, dangerous. Ashiq is one who escaped." (p. 192,
  folio 181).
- **Ewing aligns the majzub with Deleuze and Guattari against psychiatry.** They "have suggested that the
  conceptual apparatus and practices of psychiatry and psychoanalysis are a repressive structure that
  reproduces the constraints of a capitalist order"; "Similarly, those who revere a majzub refuse to
  reduce such a person to the identity of one who is mentally defective or psychotic." (p. 192,
  folio 181).
- **Malik: literature as an unused source.** "The use of contemporary literature as a source for the
  reconstruction of religious and societal conditions and discourse has been neglected by orientalists
  thus far." (p. 198, folio 187); he credits "an early and, it would appear, little noted contribution",
  Rotraud Wielandt's use of "modern Arabic literary texts for a valuation and assessment of Islamic folk
  piety (Wielandt 1984)" (p. 198, folio 187).
- **Malik: reformism may still be traditional underneath.** "one may pose the question of whether these
  forces are not still bound up with norms and symbols which are rooted in a long tradition. Esoteric
  ideas and mystical organising principles are, for that matter, also observable in the religious
  political community of the Muslim Brethren and other similar organisations." (p. 198, folio 187); he
  asks "if this rather unilateral typology" of 'reform literature' "should not be abandoned" (p. 198,
  folio 187).
- **Malik's stated plan for the chapter**: "to show how mystical tradition and veneration of holy
  men—and in this context especially gender problematics—are perceived in contemporary literature and how
  old and handed-down ideal patterns and symbols lie hidden behind reformist-aesthetic perceptions", in
  modern Urdu literature, "which is most popular in India and Pakistan, but also enjoys considerable
  popularity in `Great Britian`[text-layer?] [Great Britain] and North America" (pp. 198–199,
  folios 187–188).
- **Malik's literature review of shrine cults**, given as a bare citation string: "Much has been written
  about Sufi shrine cults, widespread in the Muslim world (Crapanzano 1973; Gellner 1979; Gilsenan 1973;
  von Schwerin 1981; Pfleiderer 1985; Troll 1989)" (p. 199, folio 188).
- **Malik quotes two authorities on the shrine's social work.** Eickelman: people "seek and find a source
  of satisfaction in an organised associational life and ‘fraternal certitude and security’ in mystical
  institutions (Eickelman 1981:228)" (p. 199, folio 188). Eaton: "‘For it was through its rituals that a
  shrine made Islam accessible to nonlettered masses, providing them with vivid and concrete
  manifestations of the divine order, and integrating them into its ritualized drama both as participants
  and as sponsors’ (Eaton 1984:334)" (p. 199, folio 188).

## Doubts

- **The single archive mapping in this chunk, `data-darbar`, rests on an author-only citation and should

  Hujwiri 1976:62–63)" (p. 183, folio 172), and as a reference-list entry (p. 193, folio 182). The book
  attaches to him no tomb, no shrine, no Lahore location of his own, no death date and no silsila. The
  mapping is made under the author-only ruling of 18 Sep 2026; nothing from the archive row's fields
  (Data Ganj Bakhsh, 1072) may be attributed to this book on the strength of these two pages (pp. 183 and
  193, folios 172 and 182).
- **A name in the Hujwiri reference entry may have lost a letter, and the text layer cannot settle it.**
  The translator is extracted as `Reynold A.Nicholas` (p. 193, folio 182). The missing space after the
  initial is the book's known initials-damage class; whether the surname itself is the book's own printing
  or has dropped a letter **is not recoverable from the text layer** and must not be guessed. A human
  should look at the rendered page before this entry is quoted (p. 193, folio 182).
- **Every personal name in chapter 8 is a pseudonym, by the author's own statement**, so no name in
  pp. 180–194 (folios 169–183) can ever be matched to an archive row: "I have used pseudonyms to protect
  the privacy of the people who were willing to share their lives with me" (p. 192, folio 181). This

  p. 188, folio 177; p. 193, folio 182) — named as the city Rabi‘a returns to, as the frame for the
