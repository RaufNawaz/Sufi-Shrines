# rizvi_history_of_sufism_india_1 — chunk 016 — pp. 265–280

**The folio regime in this chunk is clean and needs no adjudication.** `folio_map.tsv` gives pp. 265–280 → folios 245–260, every one sourced `header`, and every one of the sixteen pages does in fact print its number in the OCR ("…Centres 245" on p. 265; "260 A History of Sufism in India" on p. 280). **No page in this range is `unknown`, so no bullet below carries "(folio not stated)".** The recto running header is `The Chishtiyya, Suhrawardiyya and Kubrawiyya Centres` on every odd page and the verso is `A History of Sufism in India`, so **the brief's identification of this range as Chapter Four is confirmed by the book's own headers.** Three section headings fall inside the chunk: `Challenge to Wahdat al-Wujud` (p. 268, folio 248), `Gisu Daraz` (p. 270, folio 250) and `Bengal` (p. 276, folio 256).

**Where the book contradicts the brief, it contradicts it hard, and this is the chunk's main finding.** Chapter Four was expected to be the densest chapter in the book for archive rows because it is about the centres. **For pp. 265–280 the opposite is true: this is a one-row chunk.** The centres actually treated here are **Delhi** (pp. 267, 276; folios 247, 256), **Ilkhanid Iran — Simnan, Baghdad, Sultaniyya, Tabriz, Bukhara** (pp. 268–270, folios 248–250), **the Deccan — Daulatabad, Ahasanabad Gulbarga, Bidar** (pp. 271–273, folios 251–253) and **Bengal — Lakhnauti, Pandua, Sunargaon, Devagaon** (pp. 276–280, folios 256–260). **None of these is an archive location. Multan, Uch, Ajodhan, Sehwan, Sindh, Peshawar and Karachi do not appear in this chunk at all, and the only trace of Lahore is a nisba on a person's name** (p. 277, folio 257). Exactly one archive row is engaged, by one sentence: **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar** (p. 276, folio 256).

**What the chunk is rich in is everything except identification.** It carries the institutional and economic material the earlier chunks went looking for and did not find: a khanqah financed from purchased land and its revenue, a Shaikh buried inside his own khanqah, a khanqah built by a prince, a shrine site created by an act of banishment whose tomb "still stands", liberal grants to a dead saint's descendants and khanqah, futuh drying up, two tombs becoming "a significant centre of pilgrimage" with "large endowments from affluent devotees", a hereditary line of spiritual succession, a khilafat-nama explicitly withheld, and a forty-day chilla in a named saint's cell.

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar** (the book writes only "Baba Farid", with no other name, title or date): **the single archive mapping in this chunk, and it is one sentence about a cell in Delhi, not about Ajodhan.** Gisu Daraz, having been told by the young Shaikh Piyara that he had once loved a Hindu girl, "was delighted with Piyara's bold enterprise… Then Gisu Daraz ordered him to perform forty days continuous meditation and penance in the Delhi cell of Baba Farid. The cell was later incorporated into the tomb of Shaikh Qutbu'd-Din" (p. 276, folio 256). **So the book records three things about him here and nothing else: that he had a cell in Delhi; that the cell was still a place of forty-day retreat for a disciple of a disciple of Gisu Daraz; and that the cell ended up inside another man's tomb.** No first name, no "Ganj-i Shakar", no death date, no Ajodhan, no silsila and no tomb of his own — all **(not stated)** in pp. 265–280. The passage is footnoted "AA, pp. 172-73; Ma'ariju'l-Wilayat, f. 309a" (p. 276, folio 256), with "AA" unexpanded in this range. See Doubts on the bare form of the name and on the second Qutbu'd-Din.

**No other archive row is touched anywhere in pp. 265–280 (folios 245–260), and the negative is worth stating in detail because of how much shrine material the chunk contains.** **Hinglaj is not mentioned**, so the index's empty-id defect does not arise here. **No Hindu deity appears as the principal figure of anything** — the only figures of that order are "Shakyamuni or the Buddha", described as "a prophet endowed with a life span of 84,000 years" (p. 268, folio 248), and the unnamed temple of an unnamed Hindu girl in the Shaikh Piyara story (p. 276, folio 256); **so the unruled deity question does not bite in this chunk either.** **Multan is absent**, so nothing here advances the `shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya` or `shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam` questions left open by earlier chunks. **Every tomb named in the chunk is in the Deccan or Bengal** (pp. 272, 279, 280; folios 252, 259, 260). Four ids I considered and declined are listed under Doubts.

## Practices, institutions, economy

**The positive first, because this is the chunk's real yield.** pp. 265–280 (folios 245–260) contain **land purchase and revenue as khanqah finance, burial inside a khanqah, a princely khanqah endowment, the founding moment of a tomb site, hereditary succession, grants to a saint's descendants, the drying up of futuh, tombs as a pilgrimage centre with endowments from devotees, a forty-day chilla, and a khilafat-nama explicitly withheld.** What is **absent**: no ʿurs, no mela, no langar under that name, no sajjada nashin under that name, no Auqaf, no visitor numbers, no building measurements and no architectural date for any structure — all **(not stated)**.

- **Land and revenue as the finance of a khanqah, stated flatly and joined to a doctrinal rejection of poverty. This is the single most citable economics sentence in the chunk.** "Sufis, said Simnani, were incorrect in believing they should live in poverty, as this meant a rejection of God's bounty towards His creatures. He himself bought large tracts of land and revenue from these was used to finance his khanqah" (p. 269, folio 249). **A saint as landowner, and the doctrine that licenses it, in consecutive sentences.**
- **A Shaikh buried inside his own khanqah, named.** "On 22 Rajab 736/6 March 1336, Simnani died and was buried in his khangah [OCR?] [khanqah], Sufiyabad-i Simnan" (p. 270, folio 250). **The burial-in-khanqah statement the chunk 006 worker recorded as missing from pp. 100–117 is here.**
- **The khanqah itself dated and sited.** "In 1290 he founded a khangah [khanqah] at Sufiyabad, near Simnan, dedicating the rest of his life to mystical exercises, teaching and the writing of books" (p. 269, folio 249).
- **A prince builds a khanqah for a living saint and then attends his gatherings.** Of the Bahmani Sultan's brother Ahmad: "He had a khangah [khanqah] built for him and then attended his conversational and sama' gatherings" (p. 272, folio 252). **Royal patronage and samaʿ attendance in one sentence.**
- **The founding of a shrine site by an act of banishment — and the book says the tomb is still standing.** "Finding the Saiyid's impertinence intolerable, the Sultan banished him from his khangah [khanqah] near the fort. With his family the Saiyid moved outside the town to the site where his tomb, which still stands, was later built" (p. 272, folio 252). **Two khanqah locations for one saint, a quarrel as the cause of the move, and the present-tense "still stands".**
- **Grants to the dead saint's descendants and to his khanqah, immediately on the accession of the ruler he had blessed.** "Gisu Daraz's descendants and his former khanqah were given liberal grants by the new Sultan" (p. 272, folio 252).
- **The endowments outlast the town's importance — the clearest statement in the chunk of a shrine economy detaching from political geography.** "Gulbarga sank into obscurity but the lavish endowments to Gisu Daraz's disciples continued, as did the devotion of the people of the Deccan to the memory of the Saiyid" (p. 273, folio 253). **Set against "The Sultan's personal devotion to Saiyid Muhammad Gisu Daraz's successors was fleeting"** (p. 273, folio 253) — **popular devotion and endowment continue while royal devotion lapses.**
- **A robe and cap obtained from a distant Shaikh and received as if it were a farman — relic transmission staged as statecraft.** "Sultan Ahmad sent emissaries to Saiyid Ni'matu'llah Husaini of Kirman and obtained for himself the Shaikh's robe and cap. These gifts were received with great ceremony at a specially constructed platform and the occasion was reminiscent of royal ceremonies at which farmans from paramount powers were received" (pp. 272–273, folios 252–253). **A "specially constructed platform" is the chunk's only built structure besides khanqahs and tombs.**
- **A khirqa carried to its recipient by a third party, at a distance.** "Isfara'ini's khirga [OCR?] [khirqa] was brought to him by the local sufi Sa'du'llah, under whom he had earlier been initiated into the practice of ascetic exercises" (p. 269, folio 249). **Investiture without the pir present.**
- **Succession without a document: the khilafat-nama named and explicitly withheld.** "It may be recalled that Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din Chiragh-i Dihli did not bequeath the relics of his pirs to any of his own disciples. - **A regional inventory of tombs, in a letter — the densest single sentence of shrine geography in the chunk.** Saiyid Muhammad Ashraf Jahangir Simnani "mentions the tombs of seventy important khalifas of Shaikh Shihabu'd-Din Suhrawardi in Devagaon, and refers to other Suhrawardi tombs in Mahisun (or Mahasthan), in the Bogra district and those of the Jalaliyya order in Devatalla. - **Sufi disciples in government service, with a resignation rule.** "If a master ordered a disciple to perform duties which were forbidden by religion, he should resign his post… However, if the disciple had no other means of livelihood, he could reluctantly serve his ruler, using the first opportunity to resign. Accountants were singled out as being members of the lowliest of all professions, for they were constantly involved in the counting of money" (p. 275, folio 255).

- **Conversion motives, enumerated by a sufi and dismissed by him.** "conversion occurred in the following five circumstances: 1. fear of death; 2. fear of the enslavement uf [OCR?] [of] one's family; 3. the preaching of Muslims; 4. the desire for stipends and booty; and 5. the bigotry of ancestral religions. Such conversions, therefore, were superficial" (p. 266, folio 246).
- **Public debate as a conversion mechanism, with stakes.** "Like Simnani he indulged in debates with Brahmans on the condition that whoever was beaten should embrace the religion of the victor. ## Legends and miracles (karamat)

Attributions are the book's own, including its hedges ("It was believed", "It is believed", "appears to be a later legend", "Equally fanciful", "is said to have").

- **A birth attributed to a pir's blessing.** Of Shaikh Sadru'd-Din Hakim: "It was believed that the child was born because of the blessings of his father's pir and the great sufi was to become his spiritual guardian" (p. 267, folio 247).
- **The long-hair legend and the prophecy couplet, then the author's demolition of it.** "It is believed that once he was walking beside the palanquin of his pir when his long hair was caught in its wooden leg. Considering disentanglement would disturb the Shaikh, the Saiyid travelled a long distance with his hair caught in the palanquin", after which the Shaikh prophesied: "'Whoever is admitted to the discipleship of Gisu Daraz / By God! it is true that he is engrossed in (divine) love.'" **Rizvi then says: "This story, however, appears to be a later legend as an earlier authority says that 'Gisu Daraz' was not an Indian title, and that in countries outside India, Saiyids were known as Daraz Gisu (Long Locked Ones)"** (p. 271, folio 251).
- **A refused blessing and a fulfilled succession prophecy — the legend that underwrites the Gulbarga shrine's royal patronage.** The Saiyid refused to bless the heir-apparent, "saying that Ahmad, the Sultan's brother, was destined for the throne and that there should be no interference in the divine decree" (p. 272, folio 252); later "Ahmad managed to escape this fate and took refuge in Gisu Daraz's house; the Saiyid blessed him and prophesied he would succeed to the throne" (p. 272, folio 252), which came to pass on "5 Shawwal 825/22 September 1422" (p. 272, folio 252).
- **A devotee ranks his pir above the Prophet — reported by Firishta, given without comment.** "'Although Muhammad was the prophet of God; God be praised, Saiyid Muhammad Gisu Daraz was something else'" (p. 273, folio 253).
- **Death by ecstasy at a wedding, and two bodies in one palanquin.** "In keeping with the Indian Muslim custom, Saiyid Yadu'llah first saw his bride's face reflected in a mirror at his wedding; the sight so overwhelmed him he was seized with ecstasy, and the experience so intense he died. The bride and Yadu'llah's body were placed in a palanquin and carried away. When it was opened two bodies were revealed instead of one. Both bodies were buried side by side." **Rizvi calls it "a romanticized account"** (p. 276, folio 256).
- **The Shaikh Piyara story, which Rizvi introduces as "Equally fanciful"** — he "revealed that at one stage so deeply had he loved a Hindu girl that to meet her he had worn a Brahmanical thread and had begun living in the temple where she worshipped. Gisu Daraz was delighted with Piyara's bold enterprise" (p. 276, folio 256). **A Muslim disciple living in a Hindu temple, told approvingly.**
- **Light produced by a bodily zikr technique, "believed to be mystical"** (p. 269, folio 249).
- **A vision as the trigger of a career.** "In 1284 a vision aroused in him a desire for further mystical experiences" (p. 268, folio 248).
- **Rain compelled by a king's self-abasement.** "if a country was seized with drought the king should don tattered clothes, start digging with a spade, then sow some seeds and pray for rain. Thus a monarch demonstrated he was more helpless than the lowliest beggar and such an admission would cause rain to fall" (p. 275, folio 255).
- **Martyr-sainthood as a genre, flagged by the author as unreliable.** "Many early sufis were honoured as martyr saints and their exploits became legendary, at the expense of historical authenticity. These will be discussed at greater length in the following chapter" (p. 280, folio 260). **A forward pointer to Chapter Five.**
- **A saint's son tortured to death over a hidden treasury.** "Raja Ganesa is said to have banished him to Sunargaon and then had tortured him to death in order to discover where his ancestral treasury was hidden" (p. 280, folio 260). **Note the assumption, unexplained, that a saintly family had "an ancestral treasury".**

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Rizvi's central explanatory move in this chunk: a doctrine explained as a political by-product.** "Simnani's philosophy emerged as a by-product of the political conditions in Iran and these warrant a brief explanation" (p. 268, folio 248); and then the two reasons given for his opposition to Ibn al-'Arabi — "Firstly the IIkhanid [OCR?] [Ilkhanid] programme for the annihilation of the Buddhists which he himself supported, was likely to be undermined because the Wujudi ideas could be accommodated with the particular brand of Buddhism in Iran. Secondly, the broad compatibility of Ibn al-'Arabi's philosophy with Shi'i beliefs was a great challenge to Sunnism" (p. 270, folio 250).
- **The doctrinal content of the quarrel, as Rizvi sets it out.** Simnani held "Being was an attribute of God, and thus, from His essence", and that "the final stage of sufi ascent was 'ubudiyya or servantship"; "So a logical extension of the Unity of Being theory was that the servant was the Lord, and the Lord was the servant"; his own theory "came to be later known as Wahdat al-Shuhud or Wahdatus'h-Shuhud (Unity of Appearance)" (p. 269, folio 249). **The sentence distinguishing it from the Hallaj school prints the same term on both sides — see Doubts.**
- **The verdict on Simnani's campaign: it failed.** "Regardless of Simnani's opposition, Ibn al-'Arabi's ideas by this time were so deeply rooted in Iranian sufism that his own success, and that of his followers, was marginal"; and "some of Simnani's leading disciples adopted a more conciliatory attitude and in fact accepted the Wahdat al-Wujud, and wrote commentaries on the Fusus al-Hikam. What really distinguished his disciples from other sufis was their missionary zeal" (p. 270, folio 250).
- **The same verdict on Gisu Daraz.** "Gisu Daraz's many works criticizing the teachings of Ibn al-'Arabi appear to have had little impact on the Indian sufis" (p. 273, folio 253).
- **The 'ulama' and the Iranian missionaries as allies.** "The 'ulama,' who already considered such ideas heretical, readily joined hands with the Irani missionaries" (p. 270, folio 250).
- **Rizvi's own dating of Gisu Daraz's migration, against two named authorities.** "According to Shaikh 'Abdu'l-Haqq, Gisu Daraz left Delhi for the Deccan after the death of Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din. Abu'l-Fazl relates that Gisu Daraz moved south at the insistence of his pir. It would seem that the news of Timur's invasion prompted Gisu Daraz to migrate to a safer place" (p. 271, folio 251). **"It would seem" is Rizvi's, and it is a third explanation.**
- **A source-quality judgement used to prefer the Deccani historians.** "Firishta and Taba-Taba, who had access to sources in the Deccan unavailable to north Indian scholars, say that it was about 815/1412-13…" (p. 272, folio 252).
- **A source-quality judgement used against Minhaj Siraj.** "By that time the Khalji invasion was already a legend, and Minhaj's account of the conquest of Nodia (Nadia)… reads like a historical novel rather than a detached history"; and Rizvi's own reconstruction: "The fact remains, however, that a full-scale invasion of the Sena kingdom was beyond Bakhtiyar's resources and it was his surprise lightning raid which decided Bengal's fate" (p. 276, folio 256).
- **An attribution rejected on grounds of age.** "He had studied Sanskrit, but itisdoubtfulifhe wrote the Mi'rajal-'Ashigin [it is doubtful if he wrote the Mi'raj al-'Ashiqin] and certain other treatises in the Deccani dialect whose authorship is attributed to him. By that time he was too old to embark on such a new course" (p. 273, folio 253).
- **The hagiologists caught in a gap, and Rizvi's conjecture to fill it.** "Hagiologists fail to mention the name of the Sultan to whom they refer in this anecdote. The conflict may have occurred during the reign of any of three Sultans: Ilyas Shah, Sikandar Shah (1356-89) and Ghiyasu'd-Din A'zam Shah (1389-1409)"; "If the story concerning Shaikh 'Ala'u'l-Haqq is correct, however, it may have taken place after 1359, when Sikandar Shah began a policy of gradual replacement of dignitaries who had established friendly relations with Sultan Firuz Shah Tughluq" (p. 278, folio 258). **Rizvi also notes the story's type — "The story of the Shaikh's charity in Sunargaon reminds one of similar stories in which great expense incurred by other sufis preceded clashes with Sultans, the most notable being the quarrel between Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya' and Mubarak Shah Khalji"** (p. 278, folio 258).
- **Gisu Daraz's theology of sainthood, aligned with Simnani.** "although the wilaya implied nearness to God, gnosis and a knowledge of the secrets of unification, the prophets were God's messengers and therefore their superiority to the saints was unquestionable" (p. 273, folio 253), with *wilaya* footnoted "Saintship; see Chapter Once [OCR?] [Chapter One], pp. 39-41, supra". **A shrine-relevant doctrinal ceiling on saints, from a saint who became one.**
- **Resurrection read literally against Ibn al-'Arabi's allegory.** "to Gisu Daraz there were no secrets contained in the Day of Resurrection and the reward and punishments mentioned in the Qur'an were not allegorical" (p. 274, folio 254).
- **Hindu ascetic practice dismissed as merely physical.** "Gisu Daraz refused to recognize the importance of Hindu ascetic exercises calling them merely physical exercises, which failed to lead to an understanding of the truth (literally, 'heart')" — and, notably, "the arguments of the Brahmans who discussed Hindu mysticism with Gisu Daraz were based on the theory of the Unity of Being" (p. 274, folio 254).
- **A Chishti breaks Chishti custom.** "By contrast with prevailing Chishti custom, Gisu Daraz learnt Sanskrit and acquired a knowledge of the Hindu epics in an attempt to refute the religious beliefs in Hinduism" (p. 274, folio 254). **The learning is real; the purpose the author gives it is refutation, not syncretism.**
- **Rizvi's verdict on Gisu Daraz's politics.** "the paternalistic and patronising attitude towards the state evolved by Gisu Daraz failed to provide a climate for a rapprochement" (p. 276, folio 256).
- **A regional culture explained by fusion.** "From the time of the fifteenth century, Bengali sufism was strongly fused with indigenous mystic elements, mainly from Naths. This gave to Bengal a unique culture which crystallized, especially under the Husain Shahi Sultans, from 1494 to 1538" (p. 280, folio 260); earlier, "Changes in the government provided a stimulus for the growth of a composite Bengali culture" (p. 277, folio 257). **No evidence is offered for the Nath claim in this chunk.**
- **Why Bengal lost its sufis to Jaunpur.** "with the establishment of Sharqi rule in Jaunpur, outstanding sufis from other parts of India and even abroad, preferred to settle there, rather than in Bengal. Even important disciples of Shaikh 'Ala'u'l-Haqq settled in Jaunpur" (p. 280, folio 260).
- **A political calculation explained without reference to piety.** Ibrahim Shah Sharqi did not invade again because "an invasion would have been unpopular with the pro-Ganesa faction of Muslims, and Sultan Ibrahim Shah realistically saw his prospects of permanently ruling Bengal as bleak" (p. 279, folio 259).

- **`shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar` is mapped on the two words "Baba Farid" and nothing else, and the known two-Farids trap applies.** The brief records that "Shaikh Farid" in this book is two men — Faridu'd-Din Mahmud of Nagaur and Baba Farid Ganj-i Shakar — and that the book does not disambiguate them. **Here the book writes the epithet form "Baba Farid", not "Shaikh Farid", and attaches to it a cell in Delhi** (p. 276, folio 256) — **not Ajodhan, which the book elsewhere uses for Ganj-i Shakar's seat and which does not appear in this chunk at all.** I have mapped it because only one Farid row exists in the index and the settled ruling of 18 September 2026 gives a row's principal figure his id on a bare naming; **but the evidence here is a bare epithet plus a Delhi location, and a human should confirm against the book's usage elsewhere before this reaches an entry.** If it is the wrong Farid, this chunk maps zero rows.
- **`tomb-of-qutbuddin-aibak` — considered and declined, and the chunk is a fresh instance of the four-Qutbs trap.** The sentence "The cell was later incorporated into the tomb of Shaikh Qutbu'd-Din" (p. 276, folio 256) gives **"Shaikh" Qutbu'd-Din — a sufi title, not a sultan's — with a tomb in Delhi**, whereas the archive row is a secular memorial in Anarkali, Lahore. **The chunk also contains three further "Qutb" names that are none of these men**: "Shaikh Nur Qutb-i 'Alam" and "Shaikh Qutb-i 'Alam" of Pandua (pp. 278, 279, 280; folios 258, 259, 260) and the monument "Minar-i Shamsi (Qutb Minar)" (p. 267, folio 247). **No Qutb mapping is made and none should be inferred.**
- **`shrine-of-jalaluddin-surkh-posh-bukhari-jalaluddin-bukhari` and `shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht` — both considered, both declined, following the previous workers.** "Shaikh Jalalu'd-Din Tabrizi" appears twice (pp. 277, 280; folios 257, 260). **This chunk supplies no date for him, and the only place it gives him is Bengal — Pandua, Sunargaon and the Jalaliyya tombs at Devatalla — which is neither Uch nor Bukhara.** So the chunk pushes away from both rows rather than settling between them, and I map neither. **A fourth Jalalu'd-Din, "Sultan Jalalu'd-Din Muhammad Shah" of Bengal (p. 280, folio 260), is in the chunk as well and is certainly none of them.**
- **`tilla-jogian` and `gorakhnath-goraknath-temple` — considered and declined on the single word "Naths".** "Bengali sufism was strongly fused with indigenous mystic elements, mainly from Naths" (p. 280, folio 260) names a movement, not a site, and **does not name Gorakhnath, any temple or any place in the Salt Range or Peshawar.** No mapping.
- **`shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya` and `shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam` — explicitly not advanced here.** **Multan does not occur in pp. 265–280, and "Shaikh Shihabu'd-Din Suhrawardi" is named once, as the master of "seventy important khalifas" whose tombs are at Devagaon in Bengal (p. 280, folio 260) — no Indian Suhrawardi descendant of his is named.** The earlier workers' weak Ruknu'd-Din mapping gains nothing from this chunk; **no "Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din" appears at all.**
- **The `Shaktipeeth Shri Hinglaj Mata Mandir` empty-id defect does not arise: Hinglaj is not mentioned in pp. 265–280.** No id has been invented.
- **The unruled deity question does not arise either.** The only figures of that order are "Shakyamuni or the Buddha", called "a prophet" (p. 268, folio 248), and an unnamed Hindu girl's unnamed temple (p. 276, folio 256). **No Krishna, Ram or Siva.**
- **The unruled toponym convention (a) is doing no work in this chunk, despite the brief's warning that Chapter Four is organised by place.** Every place named here (full list under "Other saints, sites and events") is in Delhi, Awadh, Iran, Iraq, Central Asia, the Deccan, Gujarat, Bihar or Bengal, **and not one matches an archive row's location.** Reversing the ruling would add zero ids to this chunk. **The single word in the chunk that touches an archive location is the nisba "Lahori" in "Shaikh 'Ala'u'l-Haqq bin As'ad Lahori" (p. 277, folio 257) — a person's origin, not a site, and he is not any row's principal figure.**
- **The brief's expectation about Chapter Four is contradicted by these sixteen pages and the contradiction should be passed on.** The headers confirm the chapter (p. 265, folio 245), but its "Centres" in pp. 265–280 are Delhi, Simnan, Gulbarga and Pandua. **If Chapter Four does carry the book's densest archive material, it must lie in pp. 261–264 or pp. 281–322, outside this chunk.**

**Internal contradictions and unresolved statements in the chunk, kept side by side rather than reconciled:**

- **A term is said to be distinct from itself.** "This theory came to be later known as Wahdat al-Shuhud or Wahdatus'h-Shuhud (Unity of Appearance) and was distinct from the Wahdat al-Shuhud, the (Oneness of Witness), of the Hallaj school" (p. 269, folio 249). **As printed, both sides of "distinct from" read "Wahdat al-Shuhud". Either the OCR has flattened one term or the print itself is wrong; I cannot tell which, and the sentence cannot be quoted until someone checks the page.**
- **Nur Qutb-i 'Alam has two incompatible death dates, and Rizvi prefers neither.** "According to the Akhbaru'l-Akhyar, Nur Qutb-i 'Alam died in 813/1410-11, but the Mir'atu'l-Asrar states that the Shaikh died on 10 Zu'lqada 818/11 January 1416" (p. 279, folio 259). **A five-year spread on the death of the saint whose grave, with his father's, is said to have become the pilgrimage centre.**
- **Ghiyasu'd-Din A'zam Shah's reign and his death do not agree.** His reign is printed as "(1389-1409)" (p. 278, folio 258), but the next page says "After the death of Ghiyasu'd-Din A'zam Shah, between 1410 and 1415, the Raja acted as king-maker" (p. 279, folio 259).
- **Ilyas Shah's death year is given two ways in one paragraph.** "in 1354 a peace was concluded. Three years later Ilyas died" implies 1357, while the same sentence dates his successor's reign "Sikandar Shah (1356-89)" (p. 277, folio 257).
- **Simnani's own death year is internally consistent but his succession of khanqah names is not fully so** — the khanqah is "at Sufiyabad, near Simnan" when founded (p. 269, folio 249) and "Sufiyabad-i Simnan" at his burial (p. 270, folio 250). **Probably one place, but the book gives two forms.**
- **Simnani opposes poverty and buys land; his own missionaries preach against the wealthy's company; Gisu Daraz keeps the Chishti rule of "avoiding the company of the wealthy" while living on royal grants** (pp. 269, 272, 275; folios 249, 252, 255). **The book records all three without noticing the tension — the same poverty-versus-endowment problem chunk 006 found in the earlier chapter.**
- **The Gisu Daraz succession is contested inside one paragraph.** "Later authorities assert that Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din chose Gisu Daraz as his successor, but this view was not supported by any contemporary writer" (p. 271, folio 251), against the same paragraph's "to all intents and purposes l@ [he] was regarded as the Shaikh's khalifa". **The same page also demolishes the "Gisu Daraz" epithet-legend, so two of the row-adjacent traditions about this saint are marked unreliable by the author on one page.**
- **The book's transliteration conventions are not OCR damage and must not be "corrected"** — it writes "Hadis" and "Ahadis" for *hadith*, "Saiyid" for *Sayyid*, "si/sila"/"silsila", "zikr" not *dhikr*, "sama'" not *samāʿ*, "Shari'a", "Figh"/"Fiqh", "khalifa"/"khilafat-nama", "Wahdat al-Wujud"/"Wujudi", "'ulama'", and "Qur'an" with varying apostrophes.
