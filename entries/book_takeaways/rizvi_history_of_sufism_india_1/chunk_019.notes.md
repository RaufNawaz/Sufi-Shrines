## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **lal-shahbaz-qalandar** (the book calls him "Shaikh Lal Shahbaz Qalandar" and "Lal Shahbaz"): **the chunk gives his personal name, his native place, his initiation and the origin of both parts of his by-name, all in one passage.** "Another exception was Shaikh Lal Shahbaz Qalandar… Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din disliked qalandars intensely and certainly never encouraged their unwelcome visits. Lal Shahbaz was an exception and was adopted as his disciple." (p. 328, folio 306). **His name: "Lal Shahbaz's real name was Mir Saiyid 'Usman and he was a native of Sehwan in the Sind" (p. 328, folio 306).** The book writes **"Mir Saiyid ʿUsman", not "Usman Marwandi"** — see Doubts.
- **lal-shahbaz-qalandar**: **his practice is described as antinomian and ecstatic.** "Following the Shari'a held no interest for Lal Shahbaz who like the malamatis, indulged in ecstatic dancing." (p. 328, folio 306). He is introduced inside a run of qalandars "Among the Suhrawardiyyas" (p. 328, folio 306).
- **lal-shahbaz-qalandar**: **the two halves of the name are each given an origin, one of them a title conferred by a named Shaikh.** "Often he wore a red dress, hence his name lal (red). Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya gave him the title 'Shahbaz,' the noblest species of falcon." (p. 328, folio 306).
- **lal-shahbaz-qalandar**: **the tomb is named as a working miracle-site, in the present tense.** "A large number of miracles are said to have been performed by him and even his tomb in Sind is known for innumerable miraculous feats." (p. 328, folio 306), sourced in the footnote only as "Ma'ariju'l-Wilayat, f. 542b". **The book locates the tomb as "in Sind" and does not name Sehwan as the tomb's place — Sehwan is given only as his birthplace. No death date, no ʿurs, no shrine building, no custodian and no silsila name beyond the Suhrawardi initiation are stated** — all (not stated).
- **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya** (the book calls him "Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya" and "Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din", at Multan): **he appears as the standing antagonist of the qalandars entering India, which is the frame for the whole Lal Shahbaz passage.** "Mongol domination of Central Asia and Iran facilitated the movement of the Qalandariyyas and the Jawaliqi groups from Turkey and Egypt to India. Passing through Multan, they often came into conflict with Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya" (p. 325, folio 303).
- **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya**: **the dislike is stated as a settled personal disposition, and then broken twice.** "As also mentioned previously, Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din disliked qalandars intensely and certainly never encouraged their unwelcome visits." (p. 328, folio 306) — yet he initiated Shaikh Fakhru'd-Din ʿIraqi, "Among the Suhrawardiyyas, Shaikh Fakhru'd-Din 'Iraqi was a qalandar and as described earlier, even after his initiation by Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya he continued to live as a wandering dervish" (p. 328, folio 306), and adopted Lal Shahbaz as his disciple and named him (p. 328, folio 306).
- **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya**: **Multan appears here as his seat and as the qalandars' route into India** (p. 325, folio 303). **No date, no tomb, no shrine, no ʿurs and no death notice for him anywhere in this chunk** — (not stated).
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar** (the book calls him "Baba Farid" and "the Baba"): **he is set against Zakariyya as the qalandars' patron, in the same sentence.** "Passing through Multan, they often came into conflict with Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya, but such experiences were counteracted by the generosity of Baba Farid." (p. 325, folio 303). **"Generosity" is the book's own word and is the whole of what it says about the act** — no place, no date, no amount (not stated).
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **he gives political advice to a sufi who ignores it, and the book prints his death year while doing so.** Sidi Maula "As an emigrant en route to Delhi he visited Baba Farid before the latter's death in 1265. Finding him ambitious, the Baba warned Sidi Maula to avoid the company of Maliks and Amirs for such associations were calamitous for dervishes. The advice fell on deaf ears." (p. 330, folio 308). **The book's date here is "1265", matching the 664/1265 recorded from earlier chunks and disagreeing with the archive row's 1266** — see Doubts.

**No other archive row is mapped in pp. 315–331 (folios 293–309).** In particular, and worth recording as explicit negatives because the chapter title invites the opposite expectation: **the three modern "Qalandar" rows — `shrine-of-qalandar-baba-auliya`, `shamsabad` (Shams Ali Qalandar) and `abul-faiz-qalander-ali-suharwardi` — are not touched anywhere in a chapter devoted to qalandars**; **Hinglaj is not mentioned**, so the index's empty-id defect does not arise in this range; **no Uch figure, no Rukn-e-Alam, no Makhdum-i Jahaniyan, no Jalal Surkh, no Hujwiri and no Lahore shrine appears**; and **the entire Kashmir narrative of pp. 315–322 (folios 293–300) yields nothing** — its saints (Mir Saiyid ʿAli Hamadani, Mir Muhammad, Saiyid Hisari, Shaikh Hamza Makhdum, Shaikh Yaʿqub, Mir Shamsuʾd-Din ʿIraqi, the Bayhaqi Saiyids) are principal figure of no row.

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

Dates and names below are exactly as the book prints them, AH/CE pairs included.

- **Mir Saiyid ʿAli Hamadani** — the Kubrawiyya figure the Kashmir narrative turns on. "The authorship of 170 books have been attributed to Saiyid 'Ali and undoubtedly this is an exaggeration. Generally he wrote short treatises of which about fifty survived; his most famous contribution to sufi literature was the religio-political treatise entitled the Zakhiratu'l-Muluk." (p. 315, folio 293). His advanced treatises "depart seriously from the philosophy expressed by Shaikh 'Ala'u'd-Dawla Simnani in so far as they spiritedly support the Wahdat al-Wujud theory of Ibn al-'Arabi"; he "translated the Fusus al-Hikam into Persian"; in the *Wujudiyya* God "revealed His Essence to Himself in the world" emanating "from His Ahadiyya (Oneness)"; the epitome of manifestation is "the /nsan-i [OCR?] [Insan-i] Kamil (Perfect Man)" (p. 315, folio 293). He "left Kashmir" allowing "only a handful of Irani Saiyids" to return with him to Iran; "He was destined, however, not to return" (p. 318, folio 296).
- **The *Risala-i Futuwwa* and the Akhi organization** — Saiyid ʿAli distinguishes three senses of *Akhi*, the third being "a particular stage in the sufi journey which was known as Futuwwa"; "all stages of the mystic journey depended on Futuwwa as did all human achievements"; a man's meditation after "ascetic exercises for a hundred years" is "useless unless it had been done under a master of the Tariga [OCR?] [Tariqa] and the Futuwwa"; "'Ali bin Abi Talib, the Saiyid said, had been made custodian of the knowledge of the Futuwwa by the Prophet Muhammad" (p. 315, folio 293). Definitions collected from "Hasan Basri", "Haris Muhasibi", "Fuzayl bin Iyaz", "Junaid", "Shaikh Sahl bin Tustari", "Abu Yazid Bastami" (pp. 315–316, folios 293–294); the four pillars of ʿAli bin Abi Talib's Futuwwa, "forgiveness when one could inflict revenge, patience at the time of anger, wishing an enemy well and a preference for the needs of others rather than one's own" (p. 316, folio 294).
- **Sultan Qutbuʾd-Din of Kashmir** — "Sultan Qutbu'd-Din adopted Persian dress and divorced one of his wives whom he had illegally married earlier. Occasionally he attended congregational prayers led by the Saiyid on a platform built at the site of the Kali Mandir, which he himself had helped to destroy." (p. 318, folio 296). **No regnal dates are given for him** — (not stated). **This is not Qutbuʾd-Din Aibak; see Doubts.**
- **Saiyid Hisari** — "in about 1390, another eminent Kubrawiyya sufi, called Saiyid Hisari, whose ancestors came from Balkh, migrated to Kashmir from Hisar. He was a very liberal mystic who became a favourite of Sultan Sikandar (1389-1413) and settled in Srinagar, where he remained for the rest of his life." (p. 318, folio 296); later "The Sultan began to neglect Mir Hisari" and "Before long Hisari's influence returned" (p. 319, folio 297).
- **Mir Muhammad, son of Saiyid ʿAli** — "Born in 774/1372 at Khuttalan, after completing his education in 796/1393 he migrated to Kashmir" (p. 318, folio 296); Sultan Sikandar "became his disciple and built an imposing khangah [OCR?] [khanqah] for the young migrant at the site where his father had first built the prayer platform" (p. 319, folio 297); "he was Hisari's junior and had even accepted from the veteran sufi, a khilafat-nama" (p. 319, folio 297); "after a stay of twelve years, like his father he too decided to leave" (p. 319, folio 297).
- **Suha Bhatta / Saifuʾd-Din** — "A powerful and influential noble in the Sultan's administration, Suha Bhatta, embraced Islam after instruction by Mir Muhammad. He adopted Saifu'd-Din as his Muslim name and married his daughter to his young teacher." (p. 319, folio 297).
- **Jonaraja** — "the Brahman historian of Kashmir", who reports the king "waited on Mir Muhammad daily 'humble as a servant'" and "'like a student... took his lessons from him'" (p. 319, folio 297), and that the Sultan "'fixed with some difficulty a limit to the advance of the great sea of the Yavanas} [OCR?] [Yavanas]' (Muslims), and abolished turushkadanda (jizya)" (p. 319, folio 297).
- **The Bayhaqi Saiyids** — "The descendants of Taju'd-Din, a Saiyid sufi from Bayhaq", footnoted "A district in Khurasan west of Nishapur"; "they established family relations with the ruling house and the ministers of Kashmir"; "Sultan Zainu'l-'Abidin (1420-70) was a great patron of the Bayhagis [OCR?] [Bayhaqis]"; the Sultan "is rightly remembered by the Kashmiris as Bud Shah, the Great King" (p. 320, folio 298).
- **Shaikh Hamza Makhdum** — "a great opponent of the Shi'i Isna 'Ashari movement" (p. 320, folio 298); "He was born in 900/1494 in the Baramula district, obtained a good education and was initiated into the Suhrawardiyya order by Saiyid Jamalu'd-Din Bukhari" (p. 321, folio 299); "a deeply ecstatic mystic who would utter phrases reminiscent of Bayazid and Hallaj"; "exiled from Srinagar by Sultan Ghazi Chak (1561-63)", returned, "dying in Srinagar on 24 Safar 984/23 May 1576" (p. 321, folio 299).
- **Baba Dawud** — disciple of Shaikh Hamza "initiated in the Suhrawardi si/sila [OCR?] [silsila]", "an eminent scholar"; his verses "contained in the Wirdu'l-Muridin, and its commentary, Dasturu's-Salikin, in Persian, is a significant manual on mysticism" which "enthusiastically describes the miracles of Shaikh Hamza" (p. 321, folio 299).
- **Shaikh Yaʿqub Sarfi (Shaikh Yaʿqub)** — "Another outstanding Kashmiri sufi of the seventeenth century was the grammarian, Shaikh Ya'qub" (p. 321, folio 299) — **note the book's own chronological problem here, since it then dates his death to 1595; see Doubts.** "initiated into the Kubrawiyya order at Samarqand by Makhdum Kamalu'd-Din Shaikh Husain of Khwarazam"; friend of "Shaikh Salim Chishti"; "a respected figure at the courts of Humayun and Akbar in Delhi"; used the *Tamhidat* of "'Ainu'l-Quzat Hamadani" to argue "that the divine title, a/-Hadi [OCR?] [al-Hadi] (the guide), personified Muhammad and that a/-Muzill [OCR?] [al-Muzill] (the tempter) personified Iblis or Satan"; his Qurʾan commentary "has not survived"; "A frequent visitor to the Mughal court in Lahore, in 1594 he finally retired to Srinagar, where he died on 8 Zu'lqa'da, 1003/15 July 1595" (pp. 321–322, folios 299–300).
- **Mir Shamsuʾd-Din ʿIraqi** — "Shi'ism was introduced to Kashmir by Mir Shamsu'd-Din 'Iraqi who in 1481 arrived as an envoy of the Timurid Sultan, Husain Mirza (1469-1506). This was during the reign of Hasan Shah (1472-84)." (p. 320, folio 298); disciple of "Shaikh Isma'il Kubrawiyya, a revered sufi in Kashmir"; worked "under the name of a prominent Irani sufi, Nur Bakhsh"; dismissed after "secret reports"; "In 1501, the Mir again entered Kashmir"; "began initiating sufis into the order of Saiyid Muhammad Nur Bakhsh, although in reality he made them Isna 'Ashari Shi'is" (p. 320, folio 298); left for "Skardu in Ladakh, where he succeeded in converting a number of Buddhists to Shi'ism" (p. 321, folio 299). **See Doubts on the "Shamsuʾd-Din" collision.**
- **Baba ʿAli Najar, Musa Raina, Saiyid Muhammad Bayhaqi, Kaji Chak, Mirza Haidar Dughlat** — the Kashmiri political cast: Baba ʿAli Najar "who had succeeded the retired Shaikh Isma'il"; "Musa Raina, an influential government official, became his disciple" and "helped the Mir to build a khanqah at Jadibal, a suburb of Srinagar" (p. 320, folio 298); "Saiyid Muhammad Bayhaqi, the influential vizier of Muhammad Shah, who reigned for the second time between 1493-1505, was to prove an inveterate enemy" (p. 320, folio 298); "In 1505 Fath Shah (1505-14) became Sultan for the second time and appointed Musa Raina his vizier"; "Kaji Chak the vizier of Muhammad Shah, who ruled for the third time between 1516 and 1528"; "the Mughal Mirza Haidar Dughlat (1540-51) seized the throne of Kashmir and violently repressed the Shi'i Chaks"; "a Chak dynasty was established in Kashmir which ruled between 1551 and 1586" (p. 321, folio 299).
- **Baba Tahir ʿUryan, Abu Saʿid bin Abiʾl Khair, Khwaja ʿAbduʾllah Ansari** — the three eleventh-century sufis who "all shared a profound interest in qalandars" (p. 323, folio 301). Baba Tahir's quatrain and Ansari's *Qalandar Nama* are under "Citable passages" and "Legends".
- **The Qalandariyya and the malamatis, as the *ʿAwarifuʾl Maʿarif* distinguishes them** — the Qalandariyya "so seized by the intoxication of 'tranquillity of the heart' (ga/b [OCR?] [qalb])" that "they rejected normal social pleasantries"; "They performed obligatory, but not supererogatory prayers, and fasted"; "The Qalandariyya sought to violate orthodox features in their behaviour. The malamatis, on the other hand, merely tried to conceal their spiritual achievements." (pp. 323–324, folios 301–302). "As Hujwiri explained at great length, malamat (blame) was the distinctive practice of the Qassaris who violated religious laws and behaved in an offensive manner so that people would not bother them." (p. 324, folio 302) — **Hujwiri is named here only as an explainer of malamat; see Doubts, since he is `data-darbar`'s principal figure.**
- **Yusuf, the Spanish Arab** — "According to al-Maqrizi the Qalandariyyas first appeared in Damascus in c. 610/1213-14. One of the early founders was a Spanish Arab from Egypt called Yusuf." (p. 324, folio 302), with al-Maqrizi's account quoted at length.
- **Shaikh Jamaluʾd-Din of Sawa** — "Another founder of the Qalandariyyas, who was well-known in Damascus"; "Between 1210 and 1225 he was a student there, before moving to Damietta on the Nile in Egypt"; the shaving of beard and eyebrows "later adopted as the distinguishing feature of the Qalandariyya"; footnoted "Gibb identifies it with Sawaq or Sawaj, a city between Hamadan and Tehran" (p. 324, folio 302); described by "Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din Chiragh-i Dihli" as "a mu/fti [OCR?] [mufti] and a travelling library for he could give legal opinions extempore, without recourse to references" (pp. 324–325, folios 302–303).
- **Hasan al-Jawaliqi** — "Another founder of Qalandariyya traditions… An Irani, during the reign of al-Malik al-'Adil Ketbogha (1294-96) he founded a monastery of qalandars near Cairo." (p. 325, folio 303).
- **Tughril and Balban** — "In the reign of Balban Tughril, his rebel governor in Bengal became deeply devoted to one particular qalandar. Tughril gave the qalandar and his followers a huge quantity of gold, enabling them to wear gold necklaces, bangles and ear-rings instead of iron. In 1281 Balban crushed Tughril's rebellion and in the ensuing reprisals mercilessly slaughtered the rebels, as well as the qalandars who were hung from gibbets." (p. 326, folio 304).
- **Shah Khizr Rumi** — brought the Qalandariyya-Chishtiyya to India (p. 326, folio 304); "Although considerable legendary elements are ascribed to Shah Khizr Rumi, nevertheless he is a historical personality. According to Shaikh 'Abdu'l-Haqq he arrived in Delhi from his native Anatolia during the lifetime of Shaikh Qutbu'd-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki"; "at his own request he became the disciple of Shaikh Qutbu'd-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki"; "According to the Mir'atu'l-Asrar, Shaikh Qutbu'd-Din allowed him to maintain the dress and customs of a qalandar"; "From Delhi, Shah Khizr Rumi went to eastern U.P. and at Surhurpur, near Jaunpur, initiated Saiyid Najmu'd-Din Ghausu'd-Dahr as his disciple. Later he returned to Anatolia where he died in 750/1349-S0 [OCR?] [1349-50]" (p. 326, folio 304).
- **Shaikh ʿAbduʾl-ʿAziz Makki** — the legendary link in that chain, and **the one figure in this chunk placed at Ajodhan**: "Shaikh 'Abdu'l-'Aziz then went to Ajodhan, and like Imam Mahdi of the Shi'is, disappeared into a grotto (sardabad) on the outskirts of the town. Before his final disappearance he prophesied he would reappear at the same time as the last Imam Mahdi before the Day of Resurrection." (p. 326, folio 304). **See Doubts: "Ajodhan" is the form previously recorded for `shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar`'s seat, but the book attaches it here to a different saint.**
- **Shaikh Sharafuʾd-Din Abu (Bu) ʿAli Qalandar of Panipat** — "the celebrated qalandar", claimed by Qalandariyya-Chishtiyya legend as disciple "either… of Shah Khizr Rumi or that of Saiyid Najmu'd-Din", which "linked him with Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki"; ʿAbduʾl-Haqq calls the claims of discipleship "unauthentic"; "'Abdu'r Rahman Chishti compromised by asserting that Shaikh Abu 'Ali Qalandar had been initiated into the Chishtiyya order by the spirit of Shaikh Qutbu'd-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki"; "As a qalandar he refused to observe the rules of the Shari'a. Nevertheless he approved when Maulana Ziya'u'd-Din Sunnami, an orthodox 'alim, clipped his moustache to the length required by holy law"; his letter on Beauty and the lump-of-sugar image; his father "Salar Fakhru'd-Din, had migrated from Iraq"; "After the Shaikh's death on 13 Ramazan 724/3 September 1324 both the people of Karnal and Panipat claimed his grave to be in their respective towns" (pp. 327, folio 305).
- **Saiyid Najmuʾd-Din Ghausuʾd-Dahr** — initiated by Shah Khizr Rumi at Surhurpur near Jaunpur; "The disciples of Saiyid Najmu'd-Din flourished in Jaunpur and also in many towns of eastern U.P. and around Lucknow." (p. 326, folio 304).
- **Hamid, Masʿud-i Bak, Saiyid Muhammad Jaʿfar Makki** — "Hamid, the celebrated author of the Khairu'l-Majalis was a qalandar. The verses of Mas'ud-i Bak and Saiyid Muhammad Ja'far Makki extolling the mystic ecstasy of the qalandars helped to soften many earlier bitternesses." (pp. 327–328, folios 305–306).
- **Shaikh Fakhruʾd-Din ʿIraqi** — "Among the Suhrawardiyyas, Shaikh Fakhru'd-Din 'Iraqi was a qalandar and… even after his initiation by Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya he continued to live as a wandering dervish." (p. 328, folio 306).
- **Saiyid Raju Qattal** — "the descendants of the disciples of Saiyid Raju Qattal, known as the Qalandariyya-Suhrawardiyya Murtazawi, settled in eastern U.P." (p. 328, folio 306). **A bare name with a branch and a region: no dates, no genealogy, no tomb** — (not stated).
- **Saiyid Shah ʿAbduʾr-Rasul** — "Members of another Qalandariyya-Suhrawardiyya branch in the hills near Alwar were the disciples of Saiyid Shah 'Abdu'r-Rasul and was known as the Rasuli branch." (p. 328, folio 306).
- **Saiyid Shah Niʿmatuʾlla Shah of Kirman** — "the 'King of Dervishes'"; "Although no birth date has been documented we know that the Saiyid died on 22 Rajab 834/5 April 1431 in Mahan near Kirman"; his apocalyptic sayings "concerning the 'Mischief of the Last Day' and the advent of the Mahdi"; the Niʿmati-Qalandariyya "believed that their founder accompanied Sultan Mu'izzu'd-Din Sam on his campaign against Prithviraj and was killed at Hansi. According to these qalandars, Shah Ni'matu'llah's tomb was in Hansi, not in Kirman, as was commonly believed." (p. 328, folio 306).
- **Haidar of Sawa and the Haidaris** — "a Turkic saint called Haidar. He lived in Sawa in Kuhistan (Mountain Land), a province about seventy miles south of Nishapur"; "In 617/1220, the Mongols invaded Sawa"; "The town of Sawa where Haidar lived and was buried gradually became known as the TJurbat-i [OCR?] [Turbat-i] Haidari (the Tomb of Haidar)" (p. 329, folio 307).
- **Shaikh Abu Bakr Tusi Haidari** — "The most prominent Indian Haidari… settled in Delhi in the mid-thirteenth century"; "Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya' was a frequent visitor of Abu Bakr as was Shaikh Jamalu'd-Din of Hansi when he was in Delhi. The latter gave Shaikh Abu Bakr the title Baz-i Safid (White Falcon)" (p. 329, folio 307); "the ambitious Shaikh Abu Bakr Tusi chose to support Arkali Khan, rather than his rival, the Khan-i Khanan"; "Shaikh Abu Bakr's followers declined owing to a lack of leadership. However, the wandering Haidaris survived for many centuries." (p. 331, folio 309).
- **Nuruʾd-Din Malik Yar Parran** — "The Malik was a native of Lar in Fars who had migrated to Delhi during the reign of Balban. He was attached to the silsila of Shaikh Abu Ishaq Kaziruni."; he brought a farman "so quickly that the impressed Shaikh Abu Bakr gave him the title Parran (Flier)" (p. 330, folio 308).
- **Sidi Maula** — the chapter's martyr. "Sidi Maula came from what was known as the Upper Region (presumably the Helmand river region)" (p. 330, folio 308); his lavish spending under Balban and Muʿizzuʾd-Din Kaiqubad; "The Sultan's eldest son, the Khan-i Khanan, whom Sidi called his son, became a devotee"; "An eminent qazi, Jalal Kashani Balbani, was among the regular visitors to the Sidi's khanqah"; "Kotwal Biranjtan and Hathiya Payak, became Sidi's disciples" (p. 330, folio 308); his execution and its aftermath (p. 331, folio 309) — under "Practices" and "Legends".
- **Barani and ʿIsami** — the two named authorities on Sidi Maula; "According to Barani, Sidi's habits were strange" (p. 330, folio 308); "'Isami, an earlier authority, argued that khirga-poshan-i kham [OCR?] [khirqa-poshan-i kham] (sufi impostors) falsely accused Sidi Maula of being an alchemist" (p. 331, folio 309).
- **Ibn Battuta** — the source of the Sawa shaving story (p. 324, folio 302), of the Haidaris' iron rings "through their ears, hands and other parts of their bodies" (p. 329, folio 307), and of the Haidari party "in a village near Amroha in c. 1342 ap [OCR?] [AD]", whose quotation is cut off at the chunk boundary (p. 331, folio 309).
- **Jamali** — the source for the *sikh-i muhr*, for the Parran episode, and for the Sultan's whereabouts, which Rizvi corrects (pp. 329–330, folios 307–308).
- **Bare toponyms, listed under the interim convention and taking no id** — Kashmir, Srinagar, Jadibal, Baramula, Pompur, Vijabror, Martand, Anantnag, Sopur, Skardu, Ladakh, Khuttalan, Balkh, Hisar, Bayhaq, Nishapur, Samarqand, Khwarazam, Herat, Damascus, Damietta, Cairo, Egypt, Sawa/Sawaq/Sawaj, Kuhistan, Hamadan, Tehran, Kirman, Mahan, Anatolia, Iraq, Lar, Fars, Delhi, Jamna, Panipat, Karnal, Hansi, Jaunpur, Surhurpur, Lucknow, Alwar, Amroha, Bengal, Thatta, Siwalik, the Punjab, eastern U.P., Ajodhan. **Only two of these bear on an archive row and both are handled elsewhere: Multan appears with Shaikh Bahaʾuʾd-Din Zakariyya attached and so is not bare (p. 325, folio 303), and Ajodhan appears with a different saint attached (p. 326, folio 304, under Doubts).** **"Lahore" appears twice, once as a place Shaikh Yaʿqub visited — "A frequent visitor to the Mughal court in Lahore" (p. 322, folio 300) — and otherwise only as a publication imprint ("Arnold, T.W.. The preaching of Islam, Lahore, 1956", p. 317, folio 295; "Oriental College Magazine, Lahore, May 1965", p. 328, folio 306). **Even if the toponym ruling reverses, "Lahore" here would take no single row**: the archive holds some twenty Lahore rows and nothing in the passage selects among them.

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **A khilafat-nama, named as such — and it fills a gap the chunk 006 notes recorded as absent.** "Although Mir Muhammad was the son of Mir Saiyid 'Ali Hamadani, he was Hisari's junior and had even accepted from the veteran sufi, a khilafat-nama." (p. 319, folio 297), sourced "Saiyid 'Ali, Turikh-i [OCR?] [Tarikh-i] Kashmir, f. 10b". **The deputisation document the earlier chunk could not find anywhere in the book's pre-Indian chapters is here, and it is used as an argument about seniority.**
- **A Sultan builds a khanqah for a migrant saint, on the site of his father's prayer platform — the chunk's clearest act of royal shrine-patronage.** "Sultan Sikandar also became his disciple and built an imposing khangah [OCR?] [khanqah] for the young migrant at the site where his father had first built the prayer platform." (p. 319, folio 297). **The platform itself stood on a destroyed temple site (p. 318, folio 296), so the sequence is temple → prayer platform → khanqah on one plot.**
- **A rival khanqah built deliberately to compete with an existing one.** "Musa Raina helped the Mir to build a khanqah at Jadibal, a suburb of Srinagar which was intended to rival Mir Saiyid 'Ali's khangah [khanqah]." (p. 320, folio 298).
- **Khanqahs as nodes of craft-guild organisation — the chunk's most directly economic statement.** "The Irani khangahs [khanqahs] also became important links between the Kashmiri and Irani artisan and craft guilds (asnaf)." (p. 319, folio 297); and behind it, "the organizations of Anatolian and Iranian dervishes who themselves were either members of, or associated with, merchant and artisan guilds" (p. 316, folio 294), with the Akhi teaching "enabling them to unite sufism with broadly based social ethics" among "Kashmiri Muslim artisan and merchant groups" (p. 317, folio 295).
- **A jurisdictional dispute over khanqah territory, settled by royal farman.** "In Delhi he planned to build a khangah [khanqah] near Shaikh Abu Bakr's but the latter, considering it an encroachment of his territory, insisted that a farman first be brought from the Sultan." (p. 330, folio 308). **A saint's *wilaya* enforced through a state document — the most concrete instance of shrine territoriality in the chunk.**
- **A temple demolished to make room for a khanqah and a samaʿ assembly.** Of Shaikh Abu Bakr Tusi Haidari in Delhi: "There he demolished a temple on a site on the banks of the Jamna where he built a khangah [khanqah] and organized sama' gathering." (p. 329, folio 307).
- **Fiscal and legal measures against Hindus in Kashmir, and their reversal.** "Ancient temples in Pompur, Vijabror, Martand, Anantnag, Sopur and Baramula were desecrated and demolished; many puritanical and discriminatory laws were introduced and the jizya or poll tax was imposed on Hindus for the first time in Kashmir." (p. 319, folio 297); then the Sultan "abolished turushkadanda (jizya)" (p. 319, folio 297); under Zainuʾl-ʿAbidin the policy "included restoration of devastated temples" (p. 320, folio 298); under Fath Shah "Temples were demolished, land confiscated and non-Islamic Kashmiris were forcibly converted to Shi'ism" (p. 321, folio 299).
- **The twenty clauses of the alleged "covenant of ʿUmar", printed in full as the basis Saiyid ʿAli accepted for Muslim–zimmi relations** (pp. 317–318, folios 295–296). Those bearing on shrines and temples: "1. They (the Hindus) will not build new idol temples."; "2. They will not rebuild any existing temple which may have fallen into disrepair."; "3. Muslim travellers will not be prevented from staying in temples."; "4. Muslim travellers will be provided hospitality by zimmis in their own houses for three days."; and on funerary practice, "18. They will not carry or bury their dead near Muslim graveyards."; "19. They will not mourn their dead loudly." **Clauses 18 and 19 are the chunk's only material on burial custom and mourning, and they are prohibitions on Hindus.**
- **Patronage in gold, converted into dervish insignia, then punished.** Tughril "gave the qalandar and his followers a huge quantity of gold, enabling them to wear gold necklaces, bangles and ear-rings instead of iron"; in 1281 the qalandars "were hung from gibbets" (p. 326, folio 304). **A dated state execution of dervishes.**
- **Qalandar hostility to endowed, settled shrine life, stated as doctrine.** "Both groups are depicted as being strongly hostile to the settled khangah [khanqah] life of the Suhrawardiyya and the Chishtiyya, considering it unholy and binding in its duty to assist homeless dervishes." (p. 325, folio 303); and the qalandars from the Punjab "moved to Delhi creating scenes wherever they saw a khanqah" (p. 325, folio 303).
- **The reverse movement: qalandars settling down.** "In the thirteenth century, however, many qalandars began to adopt the settled life of the khangah [khanqah]. The earliest moves towards a sedentary existence can be seen amongst members of the Qalandariyya-Chishtiyya" (p. 326, folio 304); "gradually many itinerant qalandars became absorbed into the Chishti order" (p. 327, folio 305). A monastery as an institution: al-Jawaliqi "founded a monastery of qalandars near Cairo" (p. 325, folio 303).
- **Dress, tonsure and bodily insignia as the qalandar's institutional markers.** A "namd or garment of coarse cloth" (p. 323, folio 301); the shaved beard and eyebrows, "as did their habit of never being without a razor" (p. 324, folio 302); "he shaved his beard, put on a coarse woollen cloth and facing Mecca sat in a graveyard to meditate" (p. 325, folio 303); "Indian loin cloths round their private parts, pieces of blanket on their shoulders and iron pieces, probably necklaces, bangles or chains" (p. 325, folio 303); ear-rings adopted from the Nath-yogis (p. 325, folio 303); "iron rings on their necks and arms" (p. 331, folio 309); "iron rings through their ears, hands and other parts of their bodies" and "the Haidaris passed round iron rods through their male organs and because both ends were sealed called them sikh-i muhr (rods of the seal)… it indicated their determination to remain celibate. The Haidaris like other qalandars adopted the practice of ear piercing from the Nath-yogis or Kanphata yogis (Split Ear Yogis)." (p. 329, folio 307).
- **Intoxicants shared with the yogis.** "Another custom they shared with the yogis was the consumption of a type of grass, probably Indian hemp, and some other drugs which were popular not only in India but throughout the Middle East." (p. 325, folio 303).
- **A shrine-economy dispute over a grave, with two towns claiming it.** "After the Shaikh's death on 13 Ramazan 724/3 September 1324 both the people of Karnal and Panipat claimed his grave to be in their respective towns." (p. 327, folio 305). **A second rival-tomb claim in the same chapter**: "Shah Ni'matu'llah's tomb was in Hansi, not in Kirman, as was commonly believed" (p. 328, folio 306).
- **A place-name generated by a tomb.** "The town of Sawa where Haidar lived and was buried gradually became known as the TJurbat-i [Turbat-i] Haidari (the Tomb of Haidar)." (p. 329, folio 307).
- **A khanqah kitchen at scale, and an unexplained income — the chunk's fullest account of shrine finance.** Of Sidi Maula: "Although his clothing was the simplest and his food humble, his guests were fed lavishly. Travellers from many countries thronged to his khangah [khanqah] and they, along with the population of Delhi, benefited from his huge feasts. Barani was amongst the beneficiaries of Sidi's fine table. The latter's financial sources remained a mystery; he accepted neither land grants nor any other type of gift." (p. 330, folio 308). **An explicit refusal of land grants and of gifts — the opposite of the endowment model** — with the money instead appearing miraculously, "Whenever he purchased anything or wished to pay someone he would specify a spot and freshly coined money would be found. From this grew the story that he was an alchemist." (pp. 330–331, folios 308–309). Rizvi's own explanation: "the funds were provided by the Khan-i Khanan and by merchants from abroad" (p. 331, folio 309). **Merchants again as the financial base.**
- **The unlawful killing of a dervish as a political event, with a named punishment and a named protest.** "He was brought to the throne inchains [OCR?] [in chains]"; "a Haidari leapt forward mortally slashing his fellow dervish with a razor. Arkali Khan, one of the royal princes, beckoned the elephant drivers who trampled Sidi to death. Such an unlawful execution was deplored by the orthodox" (p. 331, folio 309). The ʿulamaʾ's procedural objection is recorded earlier: "The 'ulama' argued that trial by fire was nowhere sanctioned by the Shari'a." (p. 331, folio 309).
- **Disaster as the fiscal and demographic consequence.** "The immediate repercussions were an unprecedented dust storm which swept through Delhi and a severe drought and famine throughout the empire. Not a drop of rain fell in the Siwalik region and the Hindu cultivators were forced to migrate to the capital. Starving people began to commit suicide in groups of twenties and thirties by drowning themselves in the Jamna. The following year the country was inundated with floods." (p. 331, folio 309).
- **Negative, recorded explicitly:** in pp. 315–331 (folios 293–309) there is **no ʿurs, no mela, no langar named as such, no sajjada nashin, no Auqaf, no waqf deed, no visitor number, no architectural description of any shrine building and no building date for any tomb.** The institutional vocabulary here is khanqah, khilafat-nama, farman, jizya, land grant, asnaf and gibbet.

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

Attributions are the book's own, including its hedges ("According to legend", "is said to have", "alleged", "A curious story").

- **The qalandar who turned Khwaja ʿAbduʾllah Ansari back from wandering.** "One day a leading qalandar visited his school. He wore a namd or garment of coarse cloth but his face looked like that of a fairy… Khwaja 'Abdu'llah was so impressed that he followed him into the mountains. There the qalandar dissuaded him from following the path of a wanderer" (p. 323, folio 301).
- **The shaving of Shaikh Jamaluʾd-Din of Sawa, in Ibn Battuta's version** — the woman of Sawa, the locked house, the lavatory, the shaved beard and eyebrows, her passion "instantly abated", and "In gratitude for his unscathed virtue the Shaikh retained his shaved appearance." (p. 324, folio 302).
- **The poisoned water and the restored beard.** "at his request, poisoned boiling water was administered to him which he drank as if it were cool liquid"; upbraided for his shaven beard, "He bent his head, and as he raised it exposed a chin covered with a long white beard." "An identical story is related by Ibn Battuta, but in his version the wise man is replaced by Qazi Ibn-i 'Imad." (p. 325, folio 303).
- **Wall-smashing.** "dervishes from both orders were reported as performing incredible miracles, one example being wall-smashing with a single blow of the hand." (p. 325, folio 303).
- **The 500-year life of Shaikh ʿAbduʾl-ʿAziz Makki**, told as legend: approved by the Prophet for shaving his head, beard and moustache, "the people in Paradise looked just like him"; the mountain cave; reappearance under Caliph ʿAli; reappearance "in the third century Hijri" as disciple of Bayazid Bastami; "emerging after two hundred years to initiate Khizr Rumi"; then the disappearance into the grotto at Ajodhan and the prophecy of return with the last Imam Mahdi (p. 326, folio 304).
- **Initiation by a dead saint's spirit.** "'Abdu'r Rahman Chishti compromised by asserting that Shaikh Abu 'Ali Qalandar had been initiated into the Chishtiyya order by the spirit of Shaikh Qutbu'd-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki." (p. 327, folio 305).
- **Lal Shahbaz's miracles and his tomb's.** "A large number of miracles are said to have been performed by him and even his tomb in Sind is known for innumerable miraculous feats." (p. 328, folio 306).
- **Shah Niʿmatuʾllah's apocalyptic verses.** "His apocalyptic sayings concerning the 'Mischief of the Last Day' and the advent of the Mahdi also contain an allegorical declaration of many mysteries and revelations and are believed to allude to future events in the world." (p. 328, folio 306).
- **Haidar's fire and snow, and the iron jewellery.** "One legend is that in summer he would walk through fire, and in winter he would stand in the snow. Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya' alleged that when seized with ecstasy he would hold rods of red hot iron in his hands and, as if they were wax, fashion necklaces and bangles, which he could then wear." (p. 329, folio 307).
- **The Mongol dervish who outmatched Haidar — told by Nizamuʾd-Din Auliyaʾ and flagged by Rizvi as superstition.** "'…he replied that a dervish accompanying the troops had placed them under his protection. Haidar added that he had attempted to stop the dervish but had been miraculously thrown to the ground. Such an action, he asserted, indicated that the Mongols would be victorious and therefore all the dervishes should attempt an escape.'" (p. 329, folio 307).
- **Coin from nowhere.** "Whenever he purchased anything or wished to pay someone he would specify a spot and freshly coined money would be found. From this grew the story that he was an alchemist." (pp. 330–331, folios 308–309).
- **Divine retribution for killing a holy man.** "Barani believed that the bloodshed of a holy man called down divine retribution on Sultan Jalalu'd-Din Khalji"; the dust storm, drought, famine and floods; "To Barani the moral was clear—no king could afford to kill a dervish" (p. 331, folio 309).
- **Miracles as a literary genre inside the silsila.** Baba Dawud's *Dasturuʾs-Salikin* "enthusiastically describes the miracles of Shaikh Hamza" (p. 321, folio 299); Shah Khizr Rumi "Even then he performed exciting miracles" (p. 326, folio 304).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Rizvi separates the ʿalim from the sufi in one sentence, and it is his verdict on Saiyid ʿAli's politics.** "In emphasizing such a covenant, Saiyid 'Ali was acting as an 'alim and not as a sufi." (p. 318, folio 296). He has already called the covenant "in reality a convenient justification for the intolerant practices of later ages" and said its clauses "were, to say the least, detested Hindus" (p. 317, folio 295).
- **Rizvi supplies a motive for a temple demolition that his sources do not.** "The demolition of the temple contravened the covenant; probably the Brahmans had not allowed Saiyid 'Ali's followers to stay in the temple and the infringement was used as a pretext and later a precedent set by the Saiyid in Kashmir." (p. 318, folio 296). **"Probably" is his own; no source is cited for it.**
- **An explicit admission that the evidence runs out.** Of Sultan Sikandar's policy reversal: "No existing sources explain such changes." (p. 319, folio 297).
- **A guild hypothesis offered as a presumption.** "Although available records give no evidence of a connection between the Saiyid followers of Mir 'Ali Hamadani and guilds it may not be wrong to presume that before joining Mir Saiyid 'Ali some of them were connected with all the three organizations." (p. 316, folio 294).
- **Sufi followers as temple-looters, in Rizvi's own words.** "In Kashmir they seem to have found new avenues to promote commercial interests and exhibit a Robin Hood-type spirit, ransacking Hindu temples and enriching themselves and their local followers. The shocked Mir Saiyid 'Ali Hamadani found it necessary to remind them of the true mystical aspects of the Futuwwa and Akhi, but the real philosophy had little impact." (pp. 316–317, folios 294–295).
- **A source-criticism warning that should govern every qalandar fact in this chapter.** "Although the Chishtiyya records are our only source of information about the Qalandariyyas and the Jawaliqis, and represent their behaviour as shocking and sacrilegious, the authors admit that among these radicals were genuine spiritualists." (p. 325, folio 303).
- **Sidi Maula made the proof-text of Chishti quietism.** "Sidi Maula's life was to become the great example which justified the Chishti principle of unreserved non-involvement in politics and all power struggles." (p. 330, folio 308).
- **Rizvi against both his sources on the Sidi Maula plot.** "The available evidence tends to indicate that it was the Khan-i Khanan, Arkali Khan's rival who was the real conspirator and Sidi Maula a mere tool. The belief that the latter's finances came from alchemy or a divinely inspired source were myths" (p. 331, folio 309).
- **A miracle story kept for what it reveals, not for what it reports.** "A curious story related by Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya', concerning this invasion is noteworthy, not for its novelty, but for the sufi response to the Mongol invasion of Iran… this one is significant because it reveals the superstitious beliefs associated with the power of dervishes." (p. 329, folio 307).
- **Two claims of Hindu origin for qalandar bodily practice.** The *sikh-i muhr* is "A custom acquired from the Hindu Naga sanyasis"; ear piercing came "from the Nath-yogis or Kanphata yogis (Split Ear Yogis)" (p. 329, folio 307); and earlier, "The contact of qalandars with Nath-yogis, also wandering throughout that part of the world, influenced them to wear ear-rings." (p. 325, folio 303). **No evidence is offered for any of the three.**
- **A burial located by inference rather than evidence, and the book says so.** "However the Shaikh is well known as Sharafu'd-Din Panipati and therefore it is reasonable to assume he was buried in his home town." (p. 327, folio 305). **A grave attributed on the strength of a nisba — directly relevant to how this archive's rows treat contested tombs.**
- **Rizvi correcting a named source on a matter of fact.** "According to Jamali, the Sultan was in Thatta, although it was more likely he was in Bengal." (p. 330, folio 308).
- **Shaikh Yaʿqub's role in a conquest, stated with a disclaimer.** "Although he personally did not initiate Akbar's conquest of Kashmir, Shaikh Ya'qub's knowledge of the local situation eased the annexation of Kashmir by the Mughal empire in 1586." (p. 322, folio 300).
- **The qalandars as the chapter's organising claim.** "HE [The] history of sufism has been made infinitely more colourful by the role of the qalandars." (p. 323, folio 301) — the drop-cap T is lost in the OCR.

**Archive-mapping doubts, in order of how much a human needs to look at them:**

- **`lal-shahbaz-qalandar`: the book's name for the saint is not the archive's, and the book gives no death date.** The row's principal figure is "Lal Shahbaz Qalandar (Usman Marwandi)" with `figure_died` 1274. **The book writes "Lal Shahbaz's real name was Mir Saiyid 'Usman and he was a native of Sehwan in the Sind" (p. 328, folio 306) — "Mir Saiyid ʿUsman", with no nisba "Marwandi" anywhere, and no death date at all.** It also locates the tomb only as "in Sind", not at Sehwan; Sehwan is given as his birthplace. **The identification is nonetheless secure** — Lal Shahbaz Qalandar, Sehwan, Sind, the red dress, the Shahbaz title and a miracle-working tomb are all in the one paragraph — **but an entry must not take "Usman Marwandi" or "1274" from this book, because this book does not print them.**
- **Baba Farid's death year: the book prints "1265" again, against the archive row's 1266.** "he visited Baba Farid before the latter's death in 1265" (p. 330, folio 308). **This is the book's third statement of the 1265 date across the chunks so far and it is consistent with itself; the disagreement with the row is the archive's to resolve, and I have not reconciled it.**
- **The Hindu-deity question is live in this chunk, and the deity is Kali.** "a platform built at the site of the Kali Mandir, which he himself had helped to destroy" (p. 318, folio 296), in Kashmir, under Sultan Qutbuʾd-Din. **I have not mapped it.** If the unruled question is settled in favour of deities as principal figures, the candidate rows are `kalat-kali-temple` (principal figure "Kali", Kalat), `kali-bari-mandir` (principal figure "Kali", Peshawar) and `kalka-cave-temple-asthan-of-kalka-devi` (principal figure "Kalka Devi (Kali)", Aror) — **and note that even on reversal none of them can take this passage, because the Kali Mandir here is in Kashmir and the book gives it no other identification.** So the reversal sweep should record this as a deity-named-but-site-elsewhere instance, not as a mapping. **No other deity is named in pp. 315–331: no Krishna, no Ram, no Siva, no Durga, no Valmiki, no Jhulelal.** The other temples in the chunk (Pompur, Vijabror, Martand, Anantnag, Sopur, Baramula, the Jamna-bank temple in Delhi) are named by place only, with no deity — pp. 319, 329 (folios 297, 307).
- **The Nath-yogi question, which the deity ruling does not cover.** The book names "Nath-yogis" twice and "Kanphata yogis (Split Ear Yogis)" and "Hindu Naga sanyasis" once each (pp. 325, 329; folios 303, 307), always as a collective and never as an individual, and **never names Gorakhnath**. The row `tilla-jogian` gives its principal figure as "Guru Gorakhnath (Nath yogis)" — **the parenthetical makes the collective arguably the row's figure, which is why I flag it rather than ignore it.** `gorakhnath-goraknath-temple` names "Guru Gorakhnath" without the parenthetical and is a weaker candidate. **I have mapped neither**; on a reversal, `tilla-jogian` is the row to sweep, and the evidence would be two bare collective nouns and a borrowed ear-piercing custom.
- **"Qutbuʾd-Din" is two more men in this chunk, and neither is Aibak.** **(i) "Sultan Qutbu'd-Din" of Kashmir** (p. 318, folio 296), who destroyed the Kali Mandir and prayed on the platform raised on its site — a Kashmiri sultan, given no regnal dates, in a narrative running 1372–1413. **(ii) "Shaikh Qutbu'd-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki" / "Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki"**, the Delhi Chishti, named four times (pp. 326–327, folios 304–305). **Neither is `tomb-of-qutbuddin-aibak`, whose principal figure is "Sultan Qutb ud-Din Aibak" (d. 1210, Lahore); the book never prints "Aibak" in this chunk.** The row is considered and not mapped.
- **"Jamaluʾd-Din" is at least four men here, and one of them collides with an archive row.** The book names **(i) "Saiyid Jamalu'd-Din Bukhari", who initiated Shaikh Hamza Makhdum (b. 900/1494) into the Suhrawardiyya in Kashmir** (p. 321, folio 299); (ii) "Shaikh Jamalu'd-Din of Sawa", the Qalandariyya founder (pp. 324–325, folios 302–303); (iii) "Shaikh Jamalu'd-Din of Hansi", who gave Abu Bakr Tusi his title (p. 329, folio 307); and (iv) "Makhdum Kamalu'd-Din Shaikh Husain of Khwarazam" is a separate Kamalu'd-Din, not to be conflated (p. 322, folio 300). **The collision is (i) with `shah-jamal`, whose principal figure is "Syed Shah Jamal Uddin Naqvi Bukhari" of Lahore, Suhrawardi, d. 1671.** The names agree on three points — Jamal(uʾd-Din), Bukhari, Suhrawardi — **and disagree on place (Kashmir, not Lahore) and on any date at all, since the book gives this man none.** **I have not mapped it.** A human should decide; the evidence in the book is one clause.
- **"Shamsuʾd-Din" collides with a Multan row and should not be mapped.** "Mir Shamsu'd-Din 'Iraqi" (pp. 320–321, folios 298–299) is an Irani envoy of 1481 who introduced Shiʿism to Kashmir. `shrine-of-shah-shams-ud-din-sabzwari` names "Shah Shams-ud-Din Sabzwari" of Multan, `figure_died` 1276. **The book supplies no identification linking them and the two centuries do not meet; considered, not mapped.**
- **"Ajodhan" appears, but not attached to Baba Farid.** "Shaikh 'Abdu'l-'Aziz then went to Ajodhan, and like Imam Mahdi of the Shi'is, disappeared into a grotto (sardabad) on the outskirts of the town." (p. 326, folio 304). **"Ajodhan" is the spelling this book uses for the seat of `shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar` (the book never writes "Pakpattan"), but here it carries a legendary figure's disappearance, not Baba Farid's shrine**, and the passage names no shrine, tomb or ʿurs there. **The Farid row is already mapped on other evidence (pp. 325, 330; folios 303, 308), so nothing turns on it — but this passage must not be cited as shrine evidence for that row.**
- **Hujwiri is named once, as an explainer, and I have not added a bullet for him.** "As Hujwiri explained at great length, malamat (blame) was the distinctive practice of the Qassaris" (p. 324, folio 302), footnoted "Nicholson, pp. 183-84, See Chapter One. pp. 41-42, supra." **Under the 18 September ruling this is an author-only mention and would take `data-darbar`.** I have left it out of the mapped section and flagged it here instead **because it is a single subordinate clause carrying no fact about the man, the book or the shrine** — the consolidator should decide whether a clause of this weight earns a row bullet, since chunk 006 already holds five substantial Hujwiri bullets under that id. **If it is wanted, the whole of what the book says is the sentence quoted above.**
- **Hinglaj is not mentioned in pp. 315–331**, so the empty-id defect on the row `Shaktipeeth Shri Hinglaj Mata Mandir` does not arise here. **No id has been invented anywhere in these notes; every bold id is copied from shrine_index.tsv.**
- **`shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya` is internally inconsistent in this chunk, and the book does not notice.** "Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din disliked qalandars intensely and certainly never encouraged their unwelcome visits" (p. 328, folio 306) stands against his initiating the qalandar Shaikh Fakhruʾd-Din ʿIraqi and his adopting and naming Lal Shahbaz, both on the same page, and against the qalandars' "conflict" with him at Multan (p. 325, folio 303). **The two exceptions are flagged by the book as exceptions but the dislike is still stated absolutely.**
- **A chronological slip in the book, not OCR.** Shaikh Yaʿqub is introduced as "Another outstanding Kashmiri sufi of the seventeenth century" (p. 321, folio 299) and then given a death date of "8 Zu'lqa'da, 1003/15 July 1595" and a retirement in 1594 (p. 322, folio 300), which is the sixteenth century. **Reported, not reconciled.**
- **Unexpanded sigla leave several facts unsourceable from this chunk** — "AA", "FF", "JFF", "Jamali, p. 67", "Nicholson, pp. 183-84" and "Nicholson, pp. 62-9" carry no titles (pp. 324–331, folios 302–309). **The Lal Shahbaz tomb-miracle sentence, the chunk's single most citable line for `lal-shahbaz-qalandar`, rests on "Ma'ariju'l-Wilayat, f. 542b" alone** (p. 328, folio 306), a manuscript folio with no edition given.
