## Practices, institutions, economy

**The negative first, because this chapter was the run's best hope for shared-site and temple-economy rows and it does not deliver them: in pp. 402–419 (folios 378–395) there is no ʿurs, no mela, no langar, no sajjada nashin or custodian of any kind, no Auqaf, no waqf deed, no endowment to a named shrine, no visitor number, no shrine dispute, no architecture and no building date for any structure.** What is here instead is taxation, pilgrimage economics, labour ethics and land-revenue officials — and one temple's revenue, in Ceylon.

- **A tax on shrines, in Guru Nanak's own hymn — the single most citable economy line in the chunk.** "'The Primal Being is now called Allah; the turn of the Shaikhs hath come. **There is a tax on the shrines of the gods; such is the practice established.** There are ablution-pots, calls to prayer, five daily prayers, prayer-carpets, and God appeareth dressed in blue. In every house all say Mian (a Muslim title); your language hath been changed'" (p. 411, folio 387). **The book dates the hymn only in a footnote: "These hymns seem to have been written sometime after 1526"** (p. 411, folio 387, footnote 1).
- **A temple funded by a town's whole tax yield.** "The town's entire revenue from taxes was spent on this temple" — the Hindu temple near Adam's footprints in Ceylon, staffed by "three thousand Brahmans and yogis and five hundred Devadasis dancing before an idol", on Ibn Battuta's authority (p. 411, folio 387).
- **ʿushr imposed on Muslim rent-free religious grants, twice.** "Shaikh 'Abdu'l-Quddus' letter protesting the imposition of 'ushr on Muslim rent-free grants" (p. 410, folio 386); and "Babur's extravagance necessitated the imposition of new taxes including 'ushr on the madad-i ma'ash. Although this distressed the Muslim community, Guru Nanak saw it as the divine will" (p. 412, folio 388). **Named instrument: *madad-i maʿash*. This is the chunk's only endowment-revenue material and it is about taxation of grants, not about granting.**
- **Pilgrimage ranked below the Name — the chunk's recurring devaluation of pilgrimage economics.** "The profit which is obtained from pilgrimages, repeating the Smritis, and bestowing alms all day long, Is, O Nanak, obtained in one ghari by remembering the Name which conferreth greatness" (p. 411, folio 387); "'Pilgrimages, Austerities, Mercy, Charity, Bring but honour small and paltry. One must Hear, Believe, Love the Name, And Bathe at the sacred fount within one's frame'" (p. 415, folio 391); and of Kabir, "To Kabir, the prayers, pilgrimages and fasting of the Muslims were equally abominable" (p. 402, folio 378).
- **Two named institutions of religious company, one sufi and one bhakta, equated.** "The suhbat (company of the pious) was as important a spiritual institution to sufis as the satsang (society of holy men) was to the bhaktas" (p. 413, folio 389). **The chunk's clearest institutional parallel between the two traditions.**
- **Living saints' company as a transferable blessing, and the dust of a saint's foot as a relic-substitute.** "Only true saints should be sought as companions and those who even spoke to them received blessings transferred by them" (p. 404, folio 380); "The dust from a saint's foot had more value than a rosary or any other such objects" (p. 404, folio 380).
- **A yogi pilgrimage centre on a Buddhist site, dated by reign.** Gorakhtari, "Like other Buddhist sites occupied by yogis, it became a centre for their activities. In the reign of Akbar and Jahangir it was an important yogi centre of pilgrimage" (p. 413, folio 389). **This is the chunk's one institutional statement about a site now in Pakistan.**
- **Religious professionals living on charity, condemned.** "The great Guru emphasized honest work and deprecated mullas, pirs and yogis who lived on charity" (pp. 417–418, folios 393–394); "'They who eat the fruit of their labour and bestow something, O Nanak, recognize the right way'" (p. 418, folio 394); "'He alone, O Nanak, Knoweth the Way, Who earneth with the sweat of his brow, and then shareth it with the others'" (p. 417, folio 393), on which Rizvi remarks "The teachings of the Chishtis on this subject were identical" (p. 417, folio 393).
- **Kabir on trade, revenue officials and village debt — a rural economy in three sentences.** "Kabir noted with distress how people dealt in bronze, copper, cloves and betel nuts. Thakurs measured the fields and the villagers were never free of debts entered in the Patwari's books. To him the most important accounts were those with God" (p. 406, folio 382), with the book's own glosses "Village leaders subordinate to revenue officials" and "A village revenue official", and the cross-reference "An important case in point is the concern of Farid over the tyranny of village officials. He later became Sher Shah. Tarikh-i Sher Shahi, pp. 16-25" (p. 406, folio 382, footnotes 3–5).
- **"blood-sucking rajas and dog-like mugaddams [OCR?] [muqaddams]"** ascribed to the Kaliyuga, with the book's gloss "Traditional village leaders" (p. 418, folio 394).
- **Wealth, hoarding and rank as religious problems.** "Poverty, patience and humility were the marks of a saint; men of high rank were strangers to religion. They were like animals who stuffed themselves with food, forgetting their human nature and so making their salvation difficult"; "Those who accumulated wealth and property without spending it, were also targets for Kabir's attack" — "'God gave the miser wealth to keep, but the blockhead calleth it his own'" (p. 405, folio 381).
- **Idol worship described as a food economy that feeds Brahmans.** "Idol worshippers offered food to their gods, which in reality was eaten by Brahmans, said Kabir and he expressed shock to see that people killed creatures in order to feed these clay gods" (p. 402, folio 378).
- **Caste, accepted and criticised at once.** "Guru Nanak accepted the caste structure in a spirit of resignation to the divine will but condemned the social prejudices surrounding the concepts of high and low castes believing that only those who considered themselves nich (low) before God attained salvation" (p. 418, folio 394); Kabir "rebuked Brahmans who found defilement in almost everything, reminding them that no impurity was attached to those who had God in their hearts" and held that "A man was courageous who ignored the rituals of his own caste" (p. 405, folio 381).
- **A confession-and-restitution practice named as sufi.** "In keeping with recognized sufi practices, Guru Nanak advised Sajjan to openly confess his sins and make reparation to his victims" (p. 409, folio 385).
- **The formation of a lineage — the succession mechanism that turns a teacher into an order.** "It came to be believed that the mingling of 'the light' of the Guru with that of Guru Angad was in accordance with God's Riza. **The idea of the transmission of light helped to form the disciples of Guru Nanak and his successors into a panth or order**" (p. 419, folio 395). **A named institutional term, *panth*, and a stated mechanism — the closest thing in this chunk to a silsila.**
- **State persecution of religious teachers, twice, and its stated failure.** Kabir "was imprisoned but various supernatural feats saved his life" (p. 406, folio 382); Lodhan "was imprisoned, instructed in Islam and after he refused to convert, was executed" after "the Sultan convened an assembly of the empire's leading 'ulama'" at Sambhal; **"Persecution, however, did not silence the bhaktas and sants and they continued to increase both in number and significance"** (pp. 406–407, folios 382–383).
- **War as the maker of a religious community.** "After the death of the Fifth Guru, Arjan, at the hands of the Mughal Jahangir, the Sikhs dropped their pacifist policy and adopted their famous martial spirit. This led to a series of protracted conflicts between the Sikhs and the Mughals which soon took the form of a religious war" (p. 419, folio 395).
- **Urbanisation and resettlement as the fifteenth-century Panjab's condition.** "In the fifteenth century, the Panjab enjoyed a peaceful period due to Afghan rule. The Mongol invasions between the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries had devastated portions of the Panjab and the Afghans had successfully contributed to its re-settlement and urbanization by founding many new towns and promoting trade and commerce" (p. 408, folio 384).
- **A recitation obligation attached to a scripture.** "The Japji, the opening chapter of the Adi Granth which all Sikhs are required to repeat in the morning" (p. 414, folio 390).
- **Dress as a religious signal, and the malamati mode.** "The Guru was dressed like a malamati" at Delhi (p. 409, folio 385); "a muwahhid dressed as a malamati so as to edify them through his example"; "Qalandars, malamatis and majzubs frequently visited Mecca dressed in a manner which might appear bizarre, fantastic and even ludicrous to some, but from the time of Hallaj to-Jamali, many sufis travelled to Mecca barely clad and their bodies encrusted in dust" (p. 412, folio 388).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

The attributions are the book's own, including its hedges ("According to tradition", "is alleged", "The legend that", "It came to be believed").

- **Kabir saved from prison by miracle.** "Kabir was imprisoned but various supernatural feats saved his life. Although such a story would seem mythical…" (p. 406, folio 382). **Rizvi's own hedge is part of the sentence.**
- **The Kaʿba turns towards Guru Nanak — the chunk's best-known legend, and the book's explanation of it.** "The legend that the Ka'ba or the mihrab of the principal mosque, moved in the direction of the Guru's feet need not be interpreted literally. The story is based on the sufi belief that great saints did not need to circumambulate the Ka'ba, for whichever way they looked it would appear before them. In fact sufis believed that eminent saints and holy men were recipients of the divine light, while the K'aba [OCR?] [Kaʿba] itself was a building of mere stone and rubble" (p. 412, folio 388). **The book adds that the Janam-Sakhi authors "had no real knowledge of the fact that the Ka'ba was a cube-like building, in the middle of the mosque at Mecca. There was no question of a mihrab"** (p. 412, folio 388, footnote 4).
- **Conversation with dead saints, presented by the book as the shared sufi-and-Nath doctrine that underwrites the Baba Farid and Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya meetings.** "the Janam-Sakhis expressed the sufi and Nath belief that great saints did not die but remained accessible to important mystics in later ages through spiritual conversation" (p. 413, folio 389); "when the Janam-Sakhis describe Guru Nanak's conversation with the saints of the past, this should invariably be interpreted as a mystic experience or, in the sufi sense, a chat with the spirits, although presented as if taken place by two living people" (p. 414, folio 390); and the enacted instance, "At Panipat Nanak also had discussions with the spirit of Shaikh Sharafu'd-Din Abu 'Ali Qalandar" (p. 409, folio 385).
- **The parallel adduced for it, from Ibn al-ʿArabi, in Affifi's words.** "'He tells us the dates when and the places where he had the visions, in which he saw prophets and saints and discoursed with them; and others in which a whole book like the Fusus was handed to him by the Prophet Muhammad who bade him "take it and go forth, with it to the people that they may make use thereof."'" (pp. 413–414, folios 389–390).
- **The failure of miracle against Babur's army, in Guru Nanak's own hymn.** "'Millions of priests tried by their miraculous power to restrain the Emperor when they heard of his approach. He burned houses, mansions, and palaces; he cut princes to pieces, and had them rolled in the dust. **No Mughal hath become blind; no priest hath wrought a miracle**'" (p. 410, folio 386). **An anti-karamat karamat passage, and the chunk's strongest.**
- **A prayer answered with a refusal.** Shaikh ʿAbduʾl-Quddus "prayed that people might escape such calamity but was told by Allah that even Prophets and great sages had had to suffer similar hardship and the Shaikh himself was not their equal" (p. 410, folio 386, footnote 1).
- **Kabir's feigned drunkenness with a courtesan, as malamati practice.** "There is one story that he became so disturbed by visitors that, in true ma/amati [OCR?] [malamati] style, he pretended to be drunk and walked round the city with his arm around the neck of a courtesan" (p. 403, folio 379).
- **A saint's non-death.** "To Kabir saints didn't really die they just returned home, while infidels and the unholy remained subject to the endless cycle of transmigration" (p. 404, folio 380).
- **Transmission of light at the succession.** "posterity recognized him as the personification of the light of God. It came to be believed that the mingling of 'the light' of the Guru with that of Guru Angad was in accordance with God's Riza" (p. 419, folio 395).
- **Babur moved by hymns to free a prisoner.** "so impressed with the Guru's hymns was he that Guru Nanak was released along with some other prisoners" (p. 410, folio 386).
- **Debates with Naths and Siddhas on a legendary mountain** — "the legendary Mount Sumeru or Maru where he was alleged to have held debates with Naths and Siddhas" (pp. 411–412, folios 387–388), which Rizvi says "need not be accepted literally" (p. 412, folio 388).
- **A yogi who reached the Kaʿba.** "the Siraju'l-Hidaya mentions a yogi accompanying a Muslim saint to the Ka'ba" (p. 412, folio 388). **Cited by Rizvi as hard evidence, not as legend — and it is the chunk's one attested Hindu presence at a Muslim sanctuary.**

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Rizvi's running thesis in this chapter: the Hindu-Muslim interaction is real, but its literary record is hagiography and must be read as such.** "The Janam-Sakhis, as W.H. McLeod states, are hagiographic accounts from the life of Guru Nanak and like all such writings are intended to fulfil the spiritual cravings of the Guru's followers. They were modelled on the pattern of the Maulud-Namas of the Prophet Muhammad, the Puranas and the legendary sufi hagiologies. Inevitably supernatural and miraculous material predominates, in order to inspire devotion to the Guru" (p. 407, folio 383). **He then turns the same scepticism on the sceptics:** "The disinterring of the 'historical Nanak' is an interesting intellectual exercise, attempted by many scholars, the latest being W.H. McLeod. What is disconcerting is the general dependance, notably by McLeod, on historically unreliable material and an interpretation founded on nineteenth and twentieth century conditions" (p. 407, folio 383).
- **A source-criticism of the Afghan-period chronicles, which matters for any date taken from this book.** "The board of scholars appointed by Akbar to write one thousand years of Islamic history from the time of the death of the Prophet, admitted that they had no written record of the Afghan days and compiled an account of that period from Mughal sources and oral traditions… Afghan historians who wrote during Jahangir's reign based their compilations on the Tabagat-i Akbari with occasional recourse to Wagi'at-i Mushtaqi and anecdotes from members of old Afghan families. **This material cannot be safely relied on to dispute the account written by Bhai Gurdas Bhalla**" (p. 408, folio 384).
- **But the Janam-Sakhi itinerary is defended.** "The authors of the Janam-Sakhis were not aware of the geography, history and customs of the regions in which Guru Nanak had travelled and gaps were filled in on the basis of his hymns. **This does not necessarily imply that the basic outline of Guru Nanak's itinerary is unreliable**" (p. 408, folio 384).
- **Rizvi against McLeod on the Mecca journey, and the argument is about sufi dress.** "McLeod's arguments are founded on an ignorance of contemporary sufi accounts… Moreover to Muslims travelling to Mecca, Guru Nanak would not have been considered a Hindu, but a muwahhid dressed as a malamati" (p. 412, folio 388).
- **Rizvi against Trumpp on Ceylon.** "Trumpp says that the account of the trip was unhistorical and a later invention. His remark that the Sikh authors were generally unaware of the fact that the popular religion in Ceylon was Buddhism proves his ignorance of the Ceylonese religious and racial composition" (p. 411, folio 387).
- **Rizvi against the epigraphers, on both Baghdad and Ceylon — directly relevant to how shrine claims get "proved".** "Some Sikh scholars have sought archaeological confirmation of Guru Nanak's journey to Baghdad and their attempt shows more enthusiasm than judgement. The better type of sufis and sants or bhaktas always shrank from publicity; service to mankind was their aim, not personal glory. **The Arabic inscription at Baghdad which is alleged by some to refer to the Guru in fact does not and is in sixteenth century Turkish**" (p. 413, folio 389), on Menage's authority; and "A similar effort is made by other scholars to authenticate Guru Nanak's visit to Ceylon on the basis of epigraphical evidence but such pursuits have no real relevance to the great Guru's life" (p. 413, folio 389).
- **Rizvi against McLeod on sufi influence — the chapter's central disputed question, with McLeod quoted at length so his position can be read.** McLeod: "'The appearance is, however, misleading. Affinities certainly exist, but we cannot assume that they are necessarily the result of Sufi influence. Other factors suggest that Sufism was at most a marginal influence, encouraging certain developments but in no case providing the actual source of a significant element'"; and "'the Panjabi sufism of Guru Nanak's period had evidently departed radically from the classical pattern of Arab and Persian Sufism… the blend of modified orthodoxy and debased Sufism which was dominant'" — **the quotation is cut off at the chunk boundary** (p. 419, folio 395). Rizvi's framing: "The following remarks, however, make McLeod's understanding of sufism clear" (p. 419, folio 395).
- **Rizvi against McLeod on divine will.** McLeod's claim that "'In Islam the divine will, if not actually capricious, is at least "unpledged," whereas the Hukam of Guru Nanak's usage is definitely pledged and dependable'" "fails to show an understanding of either the theological Riza or the mystical Riza which is equivalent to Hukam" (p. 417, folio 393), with the source-jab "McLeod owes his definition of the divine will to Canon Kenneth Gragg [Cragg?] but fails to acknowledge any reference to any of the latter's works" (p. 417, folio 393, footnote 2).
- **The equivalences Rizvi actually asserts between the traditions — the substance of "interaction" in this chunk.** *maya* "is the counterpart of the sufi nafs-i lawwama, and Kabir uses it in this sense" (p. 406, folio 382); Nanak's "Unity of Being or the Wahdat al-Wujud represented as Dvaitadvaita-vilakshana-vada by the Nath sages" (p. 414, folio 390); "Guru Nanak's perception of man is based on the sufi galb [OCR?] [qalb], as opposed to a physical heart, and is therefore identical to that of the Naths and of Kabir" (p. 417, folio 393); "The haumai (1) [OCR?] [(I)], in the sense used by Abu Yazid Bastami" (p. 417, folio 393); "His Qudrat3 in the technical sense of sufism is beyond comprehension" (p. 414, folio 390); "Guru ka Sabad or Guru's Word… is his Jsm-i A'zam [Ism-i A'zam]" (p. 415, folio 391); "suhbat"/"satsang" (p. 413, folio 389); "The teachings of the Chishtis on this subject were identical" (p. 417, folio 393); "The sufi interpretation of the divine will (Riza) and Guru Nanak's concept of Hukam… Some hymns use Hukam and Riza as interchangeable terms" (p. 416, folio 392). **A difference is also asserted: "There is an element of fear in his bhakti which is absent from the love for God of such great sufis as Rabi'a"** (p. 417, folio 393).
- **Kabir's and Nanak's shared rejection of both scriptures.** Kabir: "Those who kept themselves aloof from the Vedas and the sacred books of Islam were pure" (p. 403, folio 379); "'but for me the books of both religions are useless'" (p. 403, folio 379); "In one of his hymns Kabir tells Brahmans and mullas alike that they should not condemn each other's religious texts as false" (p. 403, folio 379). Nanak: "the literal meaning of the Vedas or the Quran was no help. They should not necessarily be rejected, but the perception of Reality and Being which he called Ek-Onkar or Par-Brahm, should not be confined to narrow religious principles" (p. 407, folio 383); "Learning the Vedas, the Puranas and the Qur'an without following the truth contained in these works was useless" (p. 418, folio 394).
- **The book's judgement on the later Sikh reading of its own origins.** "Gradually the Sikhs came to see themselves as a reformed Hindu sect, tracing the source of their beliefs chiefly from the Vedas, the Upanishads and later Sanskrit classics. The sants are often referred to in passing, however, the superiority of Sikhism as a religion was asserted over other faiths. Even in sober works, Islam and sufism were referred to contemptuously" (p. 419, folio 395).
- **The one historicising move Rizvi makes against Kabir's influence.** Of Lodhan: "In both these regions the impact of Kabir's ideas was not great. Lodhan seems instead to have been influenced by the spiritual milieu of the fifteenth century" (p. 406, folio 382).
- **Rizvi's political reading.** "Although not specifically stated, Kabir's above criticisms were directed against Muslim state officials" (p. 405, folio 381); and of Nanak, "kingship in Guru Nanak's world-view was not evil. Like all sufis, he reminded rulers to be just and not only concerned with the acquisition of power" (p. 418, folio 394), with the eschatological parallel "similarly sufis blamed religious and ethical degeneration to their age's remoteness from the golden era of the Prophet and the Khulafa-i Rashidun, and awaited a Mahdi or Messiah" (p. 418, folio 394).
- **On idols, the argument from the sculptor.** "Kabir noted that sculptors while carving idols stood on them yet were not instantly struck dead" (p. 402, folio 378), and "if God was found worshipping stone, he would worship a mountain" (p. 402, folio 378).

- **Eighteen bold ids rest on one man, and none of them rests on a site.** Guru Nanak is the principal figure of eighteen archive rows, and under the ruling of 18 September 2026 every one of them takes its bold id from this chunk. **Only three of the eighteen have any site-specific support in the book: `gurudwara-janam-asthan-nankana-sahib` (Talwandi/Nankana Sahib as birthplace, p. 407, folio 383), `gurdwara-darbar-sahib-kartarpur` (death at Kartarpur in 1539, p. 418, folio 394), and `gurdwara-rori-sahib` (through Bhai Mardana, its second principal figure, pp. 409–410, folios 385–386).** For the other fifteen the evidence is the person alone. **A consolidator must not let the biography of pp. 407–419 be written into fifteen site entries as though the book described those sites; it describes none of them.** If the coordinator wants a narrower convention for a figure who carries this many rows, this chunk is the case that forces the question.
- **"Saiyidpur (Amanabad)" (p. 410, folio 386) is a two-row collision and I have not resolved it.** The book's own gloss equates "Saiyidpur" with "Amanabad". The archive has `gurdwara-sahib-saidpur-guru-nanak-dev-ji` at "Saidpur Village, Margalla Hills, Islamabad" and three rows at "Eminabad, Gujranwala District" (`gurdwara-chakki-sahib`, `gurdwara-rori-sahib`, `gurdwara-khoohi-bhai-lalo-bhai-lalo-di-khooi`). **The captivity-and-massacre episode, which is the chunk's one datable event in Guru Nanak's life (926/1520, p. 409, folio 385), must not be attached to any of these four rows until someone establishes which town the book means. I have recorded the episode under `gurdwara-rori-sahib` only because Bhai Mardana is named in it, not because I have placed Amanabad.**
- **`gorakhnath-goraknath-temple` is mapped on the site name and the city, and on nothing else.** The book writes "Gorakhtari", "Gorkhattari" and "Gorakhtari" (three forms on one page), places it in Peshawar on McLeod's authority, and describes it as a Buddhist relic-site occupied by yogis and a yogi pilgrimage centre under Akbar and Jahangir (p. 413, folio 389). The row is "Gorakhnath (Goraknath) Temple… Gor Khatri area, Peshawar", principal figure "Guru Gorakhnath". **The book never uses the word "temple", never names a deity there, and never names Gorakhnath anywhere in pp. 402–419.** It also records that "According to Trumpp the existence of such a town has not been substantiated by modern geographers" (p. 413, folio 389) — i.e. the book preserves a dispute about whether the place exists at all. **If a reviewer judges the name-and-city match insufficient, this id should be moved to an unmapped flag; it is the chunk's single riskiest mapping and also its single best shared-ground find.**
- **`tilla-jogian` considered and NOT mapped.** The chunk names "Naths and Siddhas" (p. 412, folio 388), "the Nath sages" (p. 414, folio 390), "yogis who lived in the surrounding jungles" (p. 408, folio 384) and "the Naths and of Kabir" (p. 417, folio 393) — **category, never the figure Gorakhnath and never the site Tilla or the Salt Range.** Per the brief this is not enough.
- **`sevapanthi-darbar-bhai-gurdas-gandava` and `khatwari-darbar-shikarpur` considered and NOT mapped — a three-way name collision.** The book's "Bhai Gurdas Bhalla, who assisted in the compilation of the Adi-Granth", author of "Bhai Gurdas' Var I", "written in the first quarter of the seventeenth century" (p. 408, folio 384) is given a patronym the archive rows do not carry: the rows name "Bhai Gurdas (Sevapanthi)" at Gandava, Balochistan, and "Bhai Gurdas Singh (Kanhiya Lal)" at Shikarpur. **The book supplies no place, no dates and no sect for its Bhai Gurdas beyond the Adi-Granth work, so the name could be any of three men and I have picked none.**
- **`gurdwara-babay-nanki` considered and NOT mapped.** The book names "Guru Nanak's brother-in-law, Jai Ram" (p. 409, folio 385), which implies a sister, but **Bebe Nanaki is never named** — (not stated).
- **No Hindu deity is mapped, and here are the rows a reversal would sweep.** The chunk names deities only as names for the one God or as objects of criticism: **Rama and Hari** in "traditional terms used by Hindus and Muslims to invoke God, such as Allah, Khuda, Sahib, Hari and Rama" (p. 415, folio 391), **Ram** in "Ram and Rahim were the same Being" (p. 412, folio 388), **Hari** in "if Hari (God) was estranged" (p. 404, folio 380), and **"Brahma, Vishnu and the Shiva"** as created beings in a Nanak hymn (p. 416, folio 392), plus "O Primal Word, Maya, Brahma, Hail to Thee" (p. 415, folio 391). **If a Hindu deity is ever ruled to be a row's principal figure, the rows this chunk would give are: for Rama, `ram-mandir-saidpur-ram-kund-mandir`; for Shiva, `amb-temples-amb-sharif`, `bhagnari-mandir`, `katas-raj-temples`, `shahwala-teja-singh-mandir`, `shiv-mandir-chiti-ghati`, `shree-ratneshwar-mahadev-temple-karachi`, `umarkot-amarkot-shiv-mandir` and `chandragup-baba-chandragup`.** **Krishna, Kali, Durga, Valmiki, Hanuman, Jhulelal, Varuna, Lakshmi-Narayan, Narasimha, Jagannath, Sharada, Lava and the Jain Tirthankaras are not named anywhere in pp. 402–419**, so `krishna-mandir-kabari-bazar`, `krishna-mandir-ravi-road`, `kalat-kali-temple`, `kali-bari-mandir`, `kalka-cave-temple-asthan-of-kalka-devi` and the rest are untouched even on reversal. **I judge the deity mentions here to be weak candidates in any case: the book presents "Hari" and "Rama" as names for a single formless God in Nanak's usage, not as the deities of cult sites.**
- **Rows the bare-toponym convention withholds, named so a reversal is a clean sweep.** **Sialkot** (p. 409, folio 385) would give `gurdwara-babay-de-ber`, `shrine-of-imam-ali-ul-haq`, `jagannath-temple-sialkot`, `shahwala-teja-singh-mandir`. **Lahore** (p. 407, folio 383, as a compass bearing only) would give the archive's Lahore rows — `data-darbar`, `shrine-of-mian-mir`, `madho-lal-hussain`, `shah-jamal`, `bibi-pak-daman`, `tomb-of-qutbuddin-aibak`, `mazar-e-iqbal`, `gurdwara-dera-sahib` and the rest. **Kashmir** (p. 411, folio 387) would at most give `sharada-peeth`, and weakly, since the book names the province and not Sharda. **Peshawar** (p. 413, folio 389) is *not* withheld — it is attached there to the named Gorakhtari site and maps through it; the other Peshawar rows (`kali-bari-mandir`, `panj-tirath`, `dargah-pir-ratan-nath-jee`, `shrine-of-akhund-darweza-baba`, `rahman-baba-mausoleum-rehman-baba-shrine`, `gurdwara-bhai-beba-singh`, `gurdwara-bhai-joga-singh`, `wadpagga-sharif`) are untouched. **Multan and Ajodhan are not withheld either** — both are attached to named saints (p. 413, folio 389) and map through `shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya` and `shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar`.
- **The "Farid" trap fires again in this chunk, in a fourth sense.** "the concern of Farid over the tyranny of village officials. He later became Sher Shah" (p. 406, folio 382, footnote 5) is **Sher Shah Suri, not Baba Farid**; the book says so itself in the same sentence. `shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar` is mapped only from "Baba Farid" at Ajodhan (p. 413, folio 389).
- **`shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam`, `tomb-of-qutbuddin-aibak` and the other collision-prone rows do not arise**: no "Ruknu'd-Din" and no "Qutbu'd-Din" of any kind is named in pp. 402–419.

**Internal contradictions in the chunk, kept side by side rather than resolved:**

- **Guru Angad is called "the First Guru" while Arjan is called "the fifth Guru".** "As the First Guru he was known as Guru Angad (1539-52)" (p. 419, folio 395) against "The fifth Guru, Arjan Dev" (p. 407, folio 383) and "the Fifth Guru, Arjan" (p. 419, folio 395), and against Guru Nanak being the founder whom Angad succeeded (p. 418, folio 394). **Either the book errs, or "First" is an OCR substitution for a different ordinal. I have recorded it as printed and flagged it; it must not be entered as a fact.**
- **The chronology of the journeys does not close.** "About 1520 Guru Nanak returned from his visit to eastern India" (p. 409, folio 385) and "Guru Nanak's second and third visits seem to have been completed between 1520 and 1527. By 1528 he was back in the Panjab" (p. 412, folio 388) — **but the Mecca and Baghdad journey is placed after that 1528 return ("Later the Guru went to Mecca and Baghdad", p. 412, folio 388), and then "After his return from the Middle East, Guru Nanak seems to have remained in the Panjab" (p. 413, folio 389), leaving 1528–1539 to hold a Middle Eastern journey the book neither dates nor numbers.**
- **Rizvi relies on McLeod and demolishes him in the same pages.** "The Janam-Sakhis, as W.H. McLeod states, are hagiographic accounts" and "McLeod rightly locates the site in Peshawar" (pp. 407, 413; folios 383, 389) against "What is disconcerting is the general dependance, notably by McLeod, on historically unreliable material", "McLeod, who did not have access to the original sources, is unnecessarily suspicious", "McLeod's arguments are founded on an ignorance of contemporary sufi accounts" and "fails to show an understanding of either the theological Riza or the mystical Riza" (pp. 407, 409, 412, 417; folios 383, 385, 388, 393). **The Gorakhtari identification that carries one of this chunk's bold ids rests on McLeod.**
- **Miracle is denied in the hymn and reported in the narrative.** "No Mughal hath become blind; no priest hath wrought a miracle" (p. 410, folio 386) stands on the page after "Kabir was imprisoned but various supernatural feats saved his life" (p. 406, folio 382) and two pages before the moving Kaʿba (p. 412, folio 388). **The book does not note the tension; it explains the Kaʿba legend but lets the Kabir one stand as "tradition".**
- **Guru Nanak is said to have left no material memorial, in a chapter whose subject's shrines the archive documents.** "certainly nothing of this kind was left by him in his own country" (p. 413, folio 389) against the book's own "Guru Nanak died at Kartarpur in 1539" (p. 418, folio 394). **The sentence is aimed at the Baghdad inscription, but it is the only thing the chunk says about memorials, and an entry quoting it must say so.**
