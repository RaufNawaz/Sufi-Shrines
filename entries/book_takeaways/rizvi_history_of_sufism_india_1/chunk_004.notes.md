## Practices, institutions, economy

**This chunk is the institutional prehistory of the Indian khanqah rather than its description, and the
negative must be stated: there is no ʿurs, no mela, no langar, no sajjada nashin or gadinishin, no
Auqaf, no waqf, no land grant, no revenue assignment, no visitor number, no shrine dispute and no
building date anywhere in pp. 66–81 (folios 50–65).** What the chunk does supply is the emergence of
discipleship, the two named institutions (khanqah and madrasa), an occupational profile of the early
sufis, and alms and begging as economic facts.

- **Alms as a deathbed instruction, and the chunk's only mention of the word.** "When asked for his last
  testament, Maʿruf said that his shirt might be taken from his back and given in alms so that he could
  leave the world naked as he had emerged from his mother's womb" (p. 66, folio 50).
- **Gifts of food accepted as a matter of doctrine, against a named contrary practice.** "'It was reported
  that whenever food was presented to Maʿruf as a gift he always accepted and ate it. Someone said to
  him: "Your brother Bishr bin al-Haris always refused such food," and Maʿruf replied: "Abstaining
  causes my brother's hands to be tied, whilst Gnosis causes my hands to be stretched forth. I am only a
  guest in the house of my Lord…"'" (p. 66, folio 50, footnoted to "Qut al-Qulub, 1V [OCR?] [IV],
  p. 61"). **A stated norm on accepting offerings — the closest thing in the chunk to shrine economy.**
- **Begging prescribed by a master as discipline, in a named city, twice.** Shibli's self-mortification
  "included begging in the streets of Baghdad"; "Returning to Baghdad, Junaid again urged Shibli to beg
  and also to perform menial services for his master's companions. All this was done so that not a
  vestige of the former governor's pomp and pride remained" (p. 75, folio 59). **Begging here is imposed
  by the shaikh on a former state official, and the stated purpose is the destruction of rank.**
- **Restitution as a practice on leaving office.** "Later Shibli returned to Damawand where he went from
  house to house attempting to make amends to those he may have dissatisfied while gover-nor" (p. 75,
  folio 59).
- **The trades and origins of the early sufis, which the chunk gives systematically.** Sari "had been a
  merchant of spices and seasonings" (p. 66, folio 50); "Kharraz was a cobbler by trade" (p. 67,
  folio 51); "Junaid's father was a glass merchant" (p. 70, folio 54); Hallaj's "father was a wool
  carder, who later settled in the textile centre of Wasit" (p. 73, folio 57); Shibli was "The son of a
  high-ranking court official" and "had served as governor of Damawand" (p. 75, folio 59); Ibn al-ʿArabi
  of Basra was "for about thirty years a Hadis teacher in Mecca" (p. 78, folio 62). **Five crafts or
  trades and two offices — a usable social profile.**
- **The organisation of discipleship, dated and localised.** "During the first two centuries of Islam,
  sufi discipleship had become better organized. Followers gathered around al-Hakim in Tirmiz,
  Abu'l-ʿAbbas Sayyar in Marw, Qassar in Nishapur, Bayazid in Bastam, Khafif in Shiraz, Sahl in Tustar,
  and around Kharraz, Muhasibi, Junaid and Nuri in Baghdad. This gave rise to the development of sufi
  sects. Each sect evolved its own framework of mystic practices under the guidance of its director
  (Shaikh, Pir or Murshid). Their forms of recollection (zikr) and meditation differed; their ideologies
  were often irreconcilable but there was no hostility among sufis who adhered to the basic framework of
  the Shariʿa" (p. 77, folio 61). **The three titles for the director — Shaikh, Pir, Murshid — are given
  as equivalents, and difference of zikr form is named as the mark of a sect.**
- **The school as a teaching institution with a named method.** Sari "chose the Socratic method of
  instruction through the posing of thought-provoking questions. Sari's influence converted the Baghdad
  school of sufism into a group known as the Masters of Unification (Arbab al-Tawhid). Their theories
  were based on academic knowledge and their approach to mysticism was intellectual" (p. 66, folio 50).
- **The curriculum order laid down by a master, as a rule of precedence.** Sari to Junaid: "'Yes, go and
  acquire his doctrine (ʿi/m) and his method of self-training (adab), but leave his splitting of words
  in speculation (tashqiq liʾl-kalam) and his refutation of the Muʿtazilites alone.'… 'I heard Sari say,
  May God make you a traditionist who is a sufi and not a sufi who is a traditionist'—that is, that
  knowledge of the traditions and the Sunna should come first, and then by practising asceticism and
  devotion he might advance in knowledge of sufism… but that the reverse process… was dangerous"
  (p. 70, folio 54). **An explicit sequencing rule for training.**
- **Khanqah and madrasa named as the two institutions to be reconciled — the chunk's single most
  important institutional statement for this archive.** "Before his death, Ghazali once more retired to
  .ue [OCR?] [the] life of a sufi in his own khangah [OCR?] [khanqah] at Tus. But the principal mission
  of his later years was to reconcile the life of the madrasa, or seminary, to that of the khanqah or
  monastery" (p. 80, folio 64). **The book glosses khanqah as "monastery" and madrasa as "seminary", and
  attributes a personal khanqah at Tus to Ghazali.**
- **The Nizamiyya seminaries as state-founded, salaried posts, with dates and a founder.** "From 1091 to
  1095 he was a professor at the Nizamiyya seminary in Baghdad, which had originally been founded by the
  Saljuq vizier, Nizamuʾl-Mulk Tusi (b. c. 1018, died 1092)"; "In 1106, he accepted the post of lecturer
  at another Nizamiyya seminary, this time in Nishapur" (p. 80, folio 64). **Vizierial foundation, a
  professorship, a resignation "for political reasons" in 1095, and a return to salaried teaching in
  1106.**
- **Sufi relations with rulers, stated as a general rule with an exception.** "In general, sufis avoided
  the courts of their rulers and the company of the governing classes. However, they did not hesitate to
  remind the élite, whenever the opportunity arose, that the common Muslims had been divinely entrusted
  to their care" (p. 77, folio 61). **Against which stands Hallaj's court following: "the number of
  Hallaj's supporters at the Caliph's court was by no means meagre" (p. 73, folio 57), and his fate
  turning on "the scramble for power between viziers of opposing sects" (p. 73, folio 57).**
- **State and juristic suppression of sufi practice, with the specific charges named.** "The movement's
  esoteric and ascetic practices and its members' indulgence in music and dancing also provided the
  theologians with opportunities to crush sufi activities" (p. 78, folio 62); the doctrine of "Aulul
  [OCR?] [hulul]… was to become the most convenient and dangerous weapon in the ʿulamaʾ armoury with
  which to denounce and suppress sufism" (p. 78, folio 62). Earlier, "the study of Muhasibi's writings
  was banned" (p. 68, folio 52), and Hallaj's "enemies denounced him for claiming mystical union with
  God and for causing moral instability among the people" while "Hallaj's disciples and friends
  explained that mystic inspiration was beyond the jurisdiction of an earthly court" (p. 73, folio 57).
  **Music and dancing named as a ground of prosecution; a jurisdictional defence stated in the sufis'
  own mouths.**
- **Confinement, asylum and execution as the state's instruments.** Hallaj was imprisoned "in 301/913"
  for "nine years" and "hung on a gibbet after various revolting and merciless tortures" on "29
  Zuʾl-Qaʾda 309/1 April 922" (pp. 73–74, folios 57–58); "For some time Shibli was committed to a mental
  asylum because of his ideas, however, no stronger action was taken against him" (p. 75, folio 59);
  Shibli is described as "confined in chains" (p. 75, folio 59).
- **Dress as institutional marker, and its deliberate breach.** Hallaj "wandered preaching through
  Khurasan dressed in a soldier's uniform, instead of the traditional woollen cloak of a sufi", and on
  his last pilgrimage wore "only an Indian loin-cloth round his waist and a piece of patched and motley
  cloth thrown around his shoulders" (p. 73, folio 57); the general rule is stated negatively — "The
  wearing of woollen garments and patched frocks, a knowledge of the mystical allegories, anecdotes and
  technical expressions or hypocritical prayers, and fasting, did not lead to mystic illumination"
  (p. 76, folio 60).
- **A pilgrimage count: Hallaj's hajjs are numbered.** "made a hajj to Mecca"; "After a second
  pilgrimage"; "About 290/903 he again went to Mecca"; "After this, his final pilgrimage, Hallaj remained
  in Baghdad" (p. 73, folio 57). **Three or four journeys, only one dated.**
- **A disciple count.** "By this time Hallaj had gathered about four hundred disciples who accompanied him
  on his travels" (p. 73, folio 57). **The chunk's only quantified following.**
- **A funeral attendance count, and a funeral dispute.** Muhasibi "died in 243/857 only four people
  attended the funeral" (p. 68, folio 52); and of Maʿruf, "After his death, Jews, Christians and Muslims
  all claimed Maʿruf as their own, but only Muslims were able to lift his bier from the ground before his
  burial" (p. 66, folio 50). **A contested corpse settled by a miracle at the graveside — the closest
  thing in the chunk to a shrine-formation story, and the book attaches no tomb or cult to it**
  — **(not stated)**.
- **Zikr and self-examination as the daily disciplines.** Maʿruf's successor tradition aside, Muhasibi's
  title comes from "his practice of frequent self-examination while involved in the recollection of God",
  and "strict abstinence (waraʿ) leading to godliness (taqwaʿ) was possible only through self
  examination" (p. 69, folio 53); Sari "reminded his fellow sufis that the very start of gnosis depended
  on the withdrawal of the soul that it might be alone with God" (p. 67, folio 51); Ghazali's path begins
  with "the sinking of the heart completely in the recollection of God" (p. 81, folio 65).
- **Personal prayer named as a technique with an Arabic term.** "Personal prayer or munajat, according to
  Muhasibi, was the finest means to get near to God" (p. 69, folio 53).
- **Treatment of animals as a social practice with a stated theological edge.** "Their humane treatment of
  animals and birds, especially to dogs, regarded as unclean by orthodox Muslims, was noteworthy and it
  tended to affect their attitude towards all human beings, including non-Muslims and members of the
  so-called heterodox sects" (p. 77, folio 61).
- **A borrowed ascetic rule against display, quoted from Christ.** Muhasibi "quoted a phrase attributed to
  Christ: 'If one of you fasts, let him anoint his head and comb his hair and put collyrium on his
  eyes.'" (p. 69, folio 53).
- **Translation as an institution of transmission, three times over.** al-Kalabazi's work "was immediately
  translated into Persian by another Bukhara scholar, Mustamli" (p. 78, folio 62); Qushairi's Risala was
  translated into Persian by his disciple "before Qushairi's death in 465/1072" (p. 79, folio 63);
  Ansari's lectures on Sulami produced "a new work in Persian… also entitled Tabaqat al-Sufiyya" (pp.
  78–79, folios 62–63); and Ghazali "himself abridged the /hya al-ʿUlum [OCR?] [Ihyaʾ al-ʿUlum] in a
  Persian edition, and added some new material in order to give further impact to his teachings"
  (p. 80, folio 64). **Arabic-to-Persian abridgement for reach is stated as the author's own motive in
  the last case.**

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

The chunk reports these as anecdotes and dreams, with the attributions the book gives.

- **The bier that only Muslims could lift.** "After his death, Jews, Christians and Muslims all claimed
  Maʿruf as their own, but only Muslims were able to lift his bier from the ground before his burial"
  (p. 66, folio 50). **Reported by the book without a named source.**
- **Sari's dream of the winnowing of mankind**, told at length by Junaid — God speaks: "'O Sari, I created
  mankind, and all of them claimed to love Me. Then I created the world, and nine-tenths of them deserted
  Me…'", ending "'These are indeed My servants and My true lovers.'" (p. 67, folio 51; footnoted "An
  early mystic of Baghdad, p. 40"). **Attributed to Junaid's report of Sari's dream.**
- **Shibli stoning his visitors to prove them false friends** — "'Who are you?,' he cried. 'Your friends,'
  they told him. He at once began to throw stones at them… 'Liars!' he shouted. 'Do friends run away from
  their friend because of a few stones?'" (p. 75, folio 59; related by ʿAttar, footnoted "Arberry,
  p. 281").
- **Shibli running to burn the Kaʿba, and to burn Hell and Paradise.** "'I am running to set fire to the
  Kaʿba,' he answered, 'so that men may henceforward care only for the Lord of the Kaʿba'"; and, holding
  "a piece of wood alight at both ends", "'I am going to set Hell on fire with one end and Paradise with
  the other… so that men may concern themselves only with God'" (pp. 75–76, folios 59–60; ʿAttar via
  Arberry). **The book presents these as Shibli's "own rationalization of his behaviour, which others
  interpreted as madness" (p. 75, folio 59).**
- **The bridge of Sirat, glossed from Hadis.** "the bridge of Sirat, which must be crossed" (p. 70,
  folio 54), footnoted: "The bridge across hell, according to the Hadis, is thinner than a hair and
  sharper than a sword's edge" (p. 70, folio 54, footnote 1).
- **The revelation denying the eternity of the spirit, with its occasion.** "When the Quraish idolaters,
  prompted by the Jews, asked the Prophet Muhammad to explain the nature and essence of the spirit, God in
  a revelation denied the eternity of the spirit saying. 'The spirit belongs to that which (that is, the
  creation of which) my Lord command' (Qurʾan, XVII, 87)" (p. 77, folio 61, footnote 2).
- **The pre-existential covenant (misaq) as a Qurʾanic event**, quoted in full from "Qurʿan, VII, 172":
  "'And (remember) when thy Lord brought forth from the Children of Adam, from their reins, their seed,
  and made them testify of themselves, (saying): Am I not your Lord? They said: Yea, verily. We
  testify…'" (p. 71, folio 55).
- **Visions on the mystic path, as Ghazali describes them.** "With this first stage of the 'way' there
  begin the revelations and visions. (They)…behold angels and the spirits of the prophets;… Later a higher
  stage is reached…they come to stages in the 'way' which it is hard to describe in language" (p. 81,
  folio 65).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Rizvi's periodisation: the formative stage of sufism closes with Shibli's death.** "By the time Shibli
  died, sufism had completed its formative stage" (p. 76, folio 60) — Shibli having died "in 334/946"
  (p. 75, folio 59). **A datable terminus for the whole of the preceding narrative.**
- **And the second periodisation, which is the chunk's hinge for the volume's Indian argument.** "However,
  the transformation of sufism into an organised religious movement during the eleventh and twelfth
  centuries was coupled with the appearance of sufi texts in which the major ideas of mysticism were
  argued, codified and substantiated. This helped enormously to give the movement a firmer, more
  legitimate foundation on which to develop" (p. 78, folio 62). **Organisation and textual codification
  are treated as one process, in the two centuries immediately before the silsilas reach India.**
- **The ʿulamaʾ–sufi split stated as a mutual accusation, with the doctrine of the heart as its pivot.**
  "Thus a division grew up between the 'ulama,' who administered the Shariʿa, and the mystics, who they
  denounced as ignorant of the law. In turn, sufis criticized the ʿulamaʾ as externalists and formalists
  who were unaware of the real spirit of the Shariʿa. To the sufis, the study of the esoteric was: 'the
  science of the actions of the interior which depended on an interior organ namely, the heart
  (al-galb [OCR?] [al-qalb]).' To externalists, the ga/b [OCR?] [qalb] was only a physical organ of flesh
  and blood, but to the sufis it was a spiritual organ" (p. 76, folio 60).
- **Hulul as the instrument of suppression — Rizvi's causal claim.** "The orthodox sufis dissociated
  themselves from the doctrine of Aulul [OCR?] [hulul] which was to become the most convenient and
  dangerous weapon in the ʿulamaʾ armoury with which to denounce and suppress sufism" (p. 78, folio 62).
  **And the same doctrine is what the case against Hallaj finally rested on: "The basis of the case against
  Hallaj which finally secured his condemnation was that he rejected the transcendence of God and preached
  infusion theories or incarnation (Aulul [OCR?] [hulul])" (p. 74, folio 58).**
- **The Hululis are given an Indian, Chinese and Tibetan genealogy — the chunk's one claim of eastern
  doctrinal influence on early sufism.** "A sect of sufis which was imbued with Hindu, Chinese and Tibetan
  beliefs of the eternity of the spirit shocked the majority of sufis who believed in the Islamic concept
  of the spirit. The former were known as Hululis" (p. 77, folio 61). **This is a strong claim, made in one
  sentence, with the supporting footnote devoted to the Qurʾanic proof rather than to any evidence of the
  borrowing itself — see `## Doubts`.**
- **Hallaj is said to have studied Buddhism and Manichaeism in India and Turkistan.** "After a second
  pilgrimage, he wandered through India and Turkistan, where he acquainted himself with Buddhism and
  Manichaeism" (p. 73, folio 57). **The chunk gives no source and no itinerary for this** — **(not
  stated)** — and it is the only statement in the range that connects the Baghdad school to India.
- **Rizvi defends Hallaj against the charge on which he was killed.** "Hallaj's concept of Anaʾl-Haqq does
  not imply that human nature (nasut) is identical or interchangeable with the Divine (Lahut); to take a
  less elaborate simile—water does not become wine, when they are mixed" (p. 74, folio 58); of "'We are two
  spirits fused together (hala/na [OCR?] [halalna])in a single body'" he writes, "This, however, also does
  not prove his belief in Aulul [OCR?] [hulul]"; and "Actually Hallaj meant that his 'I' was 'acted upon' by
  divine grace" (p. 74, folio 58). **The author is explicitly overruling the medieval verdict.**
- **The comparison to Christian mysticism is the author's own move.** "Hallaj's concept is identical with
  that of the leading Christian mystic, St. John of the Cross" (p. 74, folio 58). **"identical" is Rizvi's
  word and carries no source.**
- **The sober and intoxicated schools named, paired, and assigned.** "In contrast to this was the controlled
  and disciplined side of sufi life, known as sahw (sobriety). Later the states of sukr and sahw were
  recognized as two different schools of sufism; the former represented by Bayazid and Hallaj and the latter
  by Muhasibi and Junaid" (p. 76, folio 60); and "Together with Muhasibi, Junaid was the founder of the sober
  (sahw) school of sufism" (p. 71, folio 55). **Shatahat are explicitly quarantined: "These expressions were
  not, however, a normal aspect of sufi life; they emanated from what was called sukr" (p. 76, folio 60).**
- **The rain-drop formula given as the common ground of both schools.** "What sufis of both schools tended to
  believe was that, as a rain drop was not annihilated in the ocean, although it ceased to exist
  individually, similarly the sufi soul in the unitive state was indistinguishable from the Universal
  Divine" (p. 76, folio 60).
- **Junaid's Tawhid read through Neo-Platonism — an interpretative claim the author makes explicitly.**
  "Junaid interpreted this verse in the light of the Neo-Platonic doctrine of the pre-existence of the soul"
  (p. 71, folio 55), with Abdel-Kader quoted in support (p. 71, folio 55). **Likewise maʿrifa: "Maʿrifa or
  the gnosis of Hellenistic theosophy was based on a light of divine grace that flashed into the heart"
  (p. 76, folio 60).**
- **Unification is carefully distinguished from identity — the doctrinal guard-rail the chunk states twice.**
  "There is no implication that the worshipper in this state can become identical with God (ittihad); nor
  does it imply that by abandoning his own qualities, a soul can become part of God's attributes.
  Unification means 'the passing away of man's will in God's will'" (p. 72, folio 56).
- **Qushairi's Shariʿa/Haqiqa settlement, quoted as the authoritative definition.** "'The Shariʿa is
  concerned with the observance of the outward manifestations of religion [i.e., rites and acts of devotion
  (ʿibadat) and duties (muʿamalat)]; whilst Hagiga [OCR?] [Haqiqa] (Reality) concerns inward vision of
  divine power (mushahadat ar-Rububiyya). Every rite not informed by the spirit of Reality is valueless,
  and every spirit of Reality not restrained by the Law is incomplete… Know that the Law is the Reality
  because God ordained it, and the Reality is also the Law'" (p. 79, folio 63). **Rizvi's own framing:
  "Extensive studies equipped Qushairi to define sufi terms authoritatively and at the same time to make
  them acceptable to a sizable section of later readers" (p. 79, folio 63).**
- **Ghazali's mysticism as experience over intellect, and the author's summary of his claim.** "Convinced
  that mysticism could not be approached through the mind alone, Ghazali advocated immediate experience
  (zawg [OCR?] [zawq], literally 'tasting') attained through ecstasy and a moral rejuvenation. Ghazali's own
  ascetic exercises opened for him the door to mysticism" (pp. 80–81, folios 64–65); "Through his own
  experiences with the mystic path, Ghazali claimed to have achieved the true and unique nature of
  revelation. This, according to him, was not correctly understood by either scholastic theologians or
  philosophers" (p. 81, folio 65).
- **Ghazali's God is personal, not philosophical.** "He is both transcendent and immanent, but He is not the
  Absolute of philosophers but the personal God of the Muslims" (p. 81, folio 65).
- **The mujaddid claim as Ghazali's self-understanding.** "Now he was both an ʿa/lim [OCR?] [ʿalim] and a
  sufi, with a growing conviction that he was personally destined to lead a revival in Islam of its earlier
  pristine purity (mujaddid)" (p. 80, folio 64). **The term is glossed by the situation, not defined.**
- **Sufi asceticism did not withdraw the sufis from society — stated as a general historical judgement.**
  "The asceticism and renunciation of the mystics did not cut them off from Muslim society anywhere in the
  Islamic world. Their example radiated to all sections of the community" (p. 77, folio 61).
- **Antinomianism disowned, and erotic language conceded.** "All true sufis denounced antinomianism and
  libertinism. Nevertheless, their language describing divine love unavoidably involved the use of erotic
  symbolism which was tinged with sensuality" (p. 76, folio 60). **A concession the author makes in his own
  voice.**
- **A bibliographic argument about survival and loss, made repeatedly.** Of Ibn al-ʿArabi of Basra's Tabaqat
  al-Nussak, "Although this text has not survived, extracts indicate that the author laid a firm foundation
  for the later sufi literary tradition" (p. 78, folio 62); al-Khuldi's Hikayat al-Awliyaʾ — "The work itself
  has not survived" (p. 78, folio 62); Junaid — "Of Junaid's works only his treatises… have survived"
  (pp. 70–71, folios 54–55); Hujwiri — "Of his many works only his greatest, the Kashf al-Mahjub, has
  survived" (p. 80, folio 64); Muhasibi — his works are "most of which have survived" (p. 69, folio 53);
  Kharraz — "his books, some of which have survived" (p. 67, folio 51). **The author is building the source
  base of his own volume in these sentences.**
- **Why the literature was written, in the author's words.** "From the middle of the tenth century onwards,
  many scholars of Figh [OCR?] [Fiqh] and Hadis brought their academic training to bear on the study of
  sufism and wrote texts related to sufi theories and ideas in an attempt to clarify misunderstandings.
  They were essentially scholars who were also trained in sufism" (p. 78, folio 62). **Set against the
  earlier position that "Generally their works were composed for specialists" (p. 78, folio 62).**

**The book's own transliteration conventions must not be mistaken for OCR damage.** It writes *z* for
*dh* throughout — "Hadis" for Hadith, "Misaq" for Mithaq, "Tirmiz", "Zuʾn-Nun", "Zuʾl-Qaʾda",
"al-Kalabazi", "zikr", "az-zalal" — and *s* for *th* in "muhdas" (p. 77, folio 61) and "Haris"
(pp. 68, 70, folios 52, 54). These are consistent across sixteen pages and are editorial, not scanning
errors.

**Internal contradictions and problems in the text itself:**

- **Muhasibi's flight is stated in a way that contradicts the sentence before it.** "Muhasibi migrated to
  Baghdad early in his life. There he managed to acquire a perfect understanding of Hadis… Muhasibi's life
  became so endangered that he fled to Baghdad" (p. 68, folio 52). **He cannot have fled to the city he is
  already in. Either "to" is wrong or a place name has been lost. No fact about Muhasibi's last years should
  be taken from this sentence until the printed page is checked.**
- **Hallaj's imprisonment arithmetic works in CE but not in hijri as printed.** "im-prisonment in 301/913"
  (p. 73, folio 57), "Imprisoned for nine years", execution "29 Zuʾl-Qaʾda 309/1 April 922" (p. 74,
  folio 58). 913 to 922 is nine years; 301 to 309 is eight. **Not an error I can resolve; flagged so that
  neither figure is quoted as the book's arithmetic.**
- **Two different volumes of the same work are cited for the same book.** "Qut al-Qulub, 1V, p. 61" (p. 66,
  folio 50) and "Al-Makki's Qut al-Qulub, I, p. 158" (p. 70, folio 54). The first locator is also
  typographically corrupt ("1V"). **Both are unverified; the second footnote also attributes the work to
  "Al-Makki" while the first gives no author.**
- **The modern scholar's surname is printed two ways.** "A.H. Abdel-Kadar" (p. 66, folio 50) against
  "Abdel-Kader (ʿAbduʾl Qadir)" (p. 71, folio 55), and the book's short title varies between "The life,
  personality and writings of Al-Junayd" (p. 66, folio 50) and "Personality and writings of al-Junayd"
  (pp. 71, 72, 73, folios 55, 56, 57).
- **A French title given an English article.** "translated into French by Louis Massignon and entitled The
  Passion d'al-Hallaj" (p. 74, folio 58). **Recorded as printed; not corrected.**
- **The Hululi genealogy is asserted without evidence in the chunk.** "imbued with Hindu, Chinese and Tibetan
  beliefs" (p. 77, folio 61) carries a footnote (footnote 2) that argues the Islamic doctrine of the spirit
  from the Qurʾan and Nicholson, and **says nothing about any Hindu, Chinese or Tibetan source.** The claim
  should not be repeated from this chunk as though it were documented here.
- **"Abu Saiʿd's disciple" is ambiguous.** At p. 78 (folio 62) al-Makki is called the disciple of "Abu
  Saiʿd", immediately after two candidates have been named — "Abu Saʿid Ahmad ibn Muhammad… ibn al-ʿArabi"
  on the same page, and "Abu Saʿid Ahmad ibn ʿIsa al-Kharraz" at p. 67 (folio 51). **The chunk does not
  disambiguate and neither do I.**
- **A forward reference the chunk does not fulfil.** ʿAbduʾllah al-Ansari al-Harawi "will be discussed
  separately" (p. 79, folio 63); that discussion is not in this range.

**Archive-mapping doubts:**

- **`data-darbar` is mapped on the principal figure alone, and on author-only mentions.** Under the ruling of
  18 September 2026 Hujwiri takes the row's id where the book names him as an author or authority, which is
  all that happens here: he is quoted for a doctrine (p. 68, folio 52), cited for the eternity of the spirit
  and the Hululis (p. 77, folio 61), credited with approving the sufi sects in the Kashf al-Mahjub (p. 77,
  folio 61, footnote 1), and given his full name and a one-line bibliography (p. 80, folio 64). **Nothing in
  pp. 66–81 (folios 50–65) mentions Lahore, a tomb, a shrine, a ʿurs, a custodian, an endowment or a death
  year for him, and the archive's "Data Ganj Bakhsh" and its death date of 1072 appear nowhere in this
  chunk.** A consolidator must not read these bullets as shrine evidence, and must not take the chunk's
  placement of Hujwiri between Qushairi (d. 465/1072) and Ghazali (450/1058—505/1111) as a date for him —
  **that inference would be mine, not the book's.**
- **Every Hujwiri quotation here is at second hand.** Both doctrinal quotations are footnoted to "Nicholson"
  (pp. 68, 77, folios 52, 61), i.e. to a translation of the Kashf al-Mahjub, not to the text. **Any entry
  quoting Hujwiri from this chunk is quoting Nicholson's English as Rizvi reproduces it.**
- **"India" and "Turkistan" (p. 73, folio 57) and every other place name in the chunk are left unmapped, and
  in this chunk the unruled toponym question has no consequence.** The toponyms here are Iraqi, Iranian,
  Central Asian, Arabian and Egyptian — Baghdad, Karkh, Kufa, Basra, Wasit, Ahwaz, Mecca, Khurasan, Tur,
  al-Bayza, Samarra, Damawand, Tehran, Tirmiz, Marw, Nishapur, Bastam, Shiraz, Tustar, Bukhara, Tus, Mashhad,
  Gurgan, Syria, Alexandria — **and not one of them is the location of an archive row, so there is no row that
  would take them if the interim convention reverses.** "India" and "Turkistan" name a subcontinent and a
  region, not a site, and attach to no shrine, tomb, saint, ʿurs or custodian.
- **No Hinglaj mention, so the known index defect does not bite here.** The row `Shaktipeeth Shri Hinglaj Mata
  Mandir` with its empty id cell is not engaged by this chunk. **Recording that only so the consolidator can
  see the check was made.**
- **No candidate for a new archive row arises from this chunk.** The one burial event described — the contest
  over Maʿruf's bier at Baghdad (p. 66, folio 50) — is outside the archive's geography and the book attaches
  no tomb or cult to it.
- **One name pair that a consolidator could confuse, and must not.** "Abu Saʿid Ahmad ibn Muhammad ibn Ziyad
  ibn Bishr ibn al-ʿArabi" of Basra and Mecca, "who died in 341/952-53" (p. 78, folio 62), is **not** the Ibn
  ʿArabi whose Fusus and Futuhat run through later Indian sufism. **The chunk makes no link between them, and
  none should be inferred.** Similarly, "al-Hakim in Tirmiz" (p. 77, folio 61) is given as a bare name with a
  city and nothing more — **(not stated)**.
