## Practices, institutions, economy

**The negative first, because it is large and clean: in pp. 383–401 (folios 361–377) there is no ʿurs, no mela, no langar, no sajjada nashin or any named custodian, no Auqaf, no waqf or land grant to a shrine, no revenue figure, no visitor numbers, no shrine-building date, no architectural description of any shrine, and no custodianship dispute.** The tomb material in the range amounts to two sentences about Kabir. What the chunk does carry is patronage, recitation, initiation, ascetic technique and funerary practice, below.

- **A royal cash grant to a sufi, stated as a grant for living expenses — the chunk's only direct state-to-sufi transfer.** Of Mir 'Abdu'l-Wahid: "At Akbar's invitation, he visited the Emperor's court and received a huge grant from him for his living expenses" (p. 384, folio 362). **The amount, the form and the date are (not stated).**
- **The dedicatory order of a Hindi sufi masnawi, which puts the reigning monarch and the pir into the text itself.** "The works based on the model of Persian masnawis always began with verses of gratitude to Allah, followed by praise for Muhammad and his companions, the reigning monarch and lastly tributes to the particular pir" (p. 386, folio 364); the *Padumavati* duly "begins with an invocation in traditional sufi style to God, Muhammad and his companions, then it mentions Sher Shah (1539-45) as the ruling king, introduces the author's pirs" (p. 389, folio 367).
- **Vernacular poetry recited from the pulpit for ecstatic effect, defended by the preacher on Qurʾanic grounds — the chunk's clearest performance-practice passage.** Bada'uni: "Even Maulana Shaikh Tagqiu'd-Din [OCR?] [Taqiu'd-Din], a godly preacher (wa'iz-i rabbani) used to recite its verses from the pulpit. It had an indescribable ecstatic effect upon the audience. When certain learned men asked the Shaikh why he chose that masnawi for his discourses, he replied, 'the whole of it is divine truth and is not only agreeable to the taste of people who are interested in divine Love, but it is compatible with the interpretation of some verses of the Qur'an. Even now sweet-singers of India captivate the heart by reciting it.'" (p. 386, folio 364). **"Even now" is Bada'uni's present, not Rizvi's.**
- **Ecstatic technique in a named sufi's own practice.** Mir 'Abdu'l-Wahid "indulged in ecstatic exercises and songs to induce trance-like states" (p. 384, folio 362). **The sentence is grammatically broken in the OCR — see Doubts.**
- **Two initiations without a living pir, both named as such.** "The Mir claimed that in a vision the Khwaja had made him his disciple" — Khwaja Hafiz, dead since the fourteenth century (p. 384, folio 362); and "Some authors imply that Kabir had no earthly guru and like a Uwaisi sufi, was mystically initiated by God" (p. 398, folio 374). **The book supplies the technical term "Uwaisi" for the second and none for the first.**
- **Double initiation across two silsilas, and a disciple who chooses which to claim.** "Shaikh Budhan seems to have been initiated into both the Chishtiyya and Suhrawardiyya orders. Qutban preferred to call himself a Suhrawardi" (p. 389, folio 367).
- **A Chishti who becomes a Mahdawi under a Mahdawi pir.** "Like some other members of the Chishti order, Malik Muhammad Ja'isi, also became a Mahdawi under the influence of his Mahdawi pir, Shaikh Burhan of Kalpi" (p. 394, folio 370), footnoted to "Muslim revivalist movements in northern India, pp. 131-33, 273".
- **The yogi's outer kit, named as a list but not itemised by the book.** "In the Padumavati Jai'si gives an interesting list of objects needed to complete the outer appearance of a yogi" (p. 393, folio 369). **The list's contents are (not stated) in this chunk.**
- **Nath body-fort esotericism given in full, as the reading of a poem — the densest practice passage here.** "According to Nath esoteric practices, the human body is a fort in its own right. There the sun (Shakti) and the moon (Shiva) exist separately, but finally their union leads to a state of bliss. The nine cakras are impregnable because of thousands and thousands of evils which surround them. Five calamities, acting as guards, prevent the yogis from obtaining control over the cakras… Each cakra is controlled by a goddess who is guarded by a lion, who refuses to allow the yogis to penetrate the cakras. Elephants representing ignorance are frightened by the lions. The golden staircase is Susumna-nadi (literally vessels, here meaning nerve) whose substance is the three-fold gunas, sattva, rajas and tamas which respectively produce virtue, passion and dullness, and whose form is the moon, sun and fire" (pp. 390, 393; folios 368–369).
- **The sufi and yogic itineraries set against each other as parallel numbered systems.** "Yogic obstacles and stages correspond with those along the sufi path. They are numbered differently; the four stages of the sufi journey are nasut, Malakut, Jabarut and Lahut. According to the sufis of Ibn al-'Arabi's school, nasut (human nature) is like a vessel which contains the Lahut (Divine Nature)" (p. 393, folio 369); the three-station version, "the temporary stations… related to nasut. The permanent stations are Malakut or Jabarut", is in the dictionary at the head of the chunk (p. 384, folio 362).
- **Sati, three times, twice as narrative and once as a sufi exemplar.** Mrigawati and Rukmani "became saris [OCR?] [satis] by throwing themselves on their husband's funeral pyre" (p. 388, folio 366); "the two widows, Padumavati and Nagmati, immolate themselves on their husband's pyre" (p. 390, folio 368); and Amir Khusraw's couplet held up to sufis, "Khusraw, in love rival the Hindu wife, / For the dead's sake she burns herself in life." (p. 385, folio 363).
- **A temple built as a residence for a grieving prince, and used as a wedding venue.** "his father constructed a temple near the lake in which the prince could live"; "The two were married in the temple" (p. 388, folio 366). **Literary, from the *Mrigawati*, not a report of a real site.**
- **A funeral contested between two communities, with the disposal method as the point at issue.** "After his death Kabir's body was claimed by both Muslims and Hindus, the former wishing to bury it and the latter to cremate it. When the door of the room where the dead body was lying was opened it was missing. According to tradition only a bunch of flowers was found under the sheet and these were divided amongst the two groups" (p. 399, folio 375). **This is the chunk's central "shared ground" passage.**
- **Two tombs of one man, in two provinces, on the authority of Abuʾl-Fazl.** "Abu'l-Fazl refers to two different tombs of Kabir; one at Puri in Orissa and the other at Ratanpur in Awadh" (p. 399, folio 375). **Neither is described, dated or attributed to a builder** — (not stated).
- **A deliberate death-place chosen to break a pilgrimage belief.** "Before his death he is said to have migrated from Banaras to Maghar… The decision was deliberately taken by Kabir in order to belie the current Hindu belief that one who died in Maghar would return in a following life as an ass" (pp. 398–399, folios 374–375). "Some authors suggest that Maghar was close to Banaras, others believe it was in the district of Basti, near Gorakhpur in U.P." (p. 399, folio 375).
- **Trade and caste as the social base of the material.** Kabir "was a weaver" and Shaikh Taqi "also a weaver by trade" (p. 398, folio 374); "Bhaktas came from all classes of Hindu society, but their devotionalism was not concerned with any particular God or one of His incarnations" (p. 396, folio 372); the bhaktas "were hostile to all idolatrous practices and caste distinctions and with equal vehemence ridiculed Muslim forms of worship" (p. 396, folio 372).
- **Marriage against renunciation, in a figure the archive's Nanakpanthi rows would recognise.** "Kabir was married and although he was unhappy with his role as husband and father he preached neither renunciation nor celibacy. Throughout his life when he was not travelling he lived the traditional life of a married man" (p. 398, folio 374).
- **Ritual attacked from inside the tradition.** "Fearlessly and cuttingly Kabir criticized ritualism and priest-craft, refusing to spare even monasticism in his scathing attacks. He also denounced hypocrisy, falsehood and deceitfulness in both religious and social ethics. Devotion, penance, austerity, fasting and ablutions were meaningless without knowing the way to love and serve God" (p. 401, folio 377); and "Frequently Kabir came into contact with yogis, but he always remained unimpressed by their matted locks and unkempt appearance" (p. 401, folio 377).
- **Devotional images commissioned by patrons.** "A number of eighteenth century painters made portraits of him according to suggestions from their patrons" (p. 398, folio 374). And, of the *Dastan-i Amir Hamza*, "The work was very popular at Akbar's court and became the theme for many paintings" (p. 396, folio 372).
- **Court patronage of vernacular religious poetry, itemised by office.** Qutban's patron "Sultan Husain Shah Sharqi of Jaunpur whom he considered to be a great and generous king"; "At the time Sultan Husain lived as a dethroned refugee in Kahalgaon in Bengal, and continued to issue coins like a ruler. The loyal Qutban had not lost hope of his master regaining the throne and wrote of him as if he were still a reigning monarch" (p. 389, folio 367); the Arakanese chain of war minister, minister and prime minister (p. 395, folio 371).
- **Transmission by memory, with the book's own warning about what that does to a corpus.** "The most important of his verses were generally memorized by his disciples after they had been uttered, and then written down immediately or soon afterwards. This process gave rise to considerable interpolation and naturally many unauthentic verses are included" (p. 399, folio 375).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

Attributions are the book's own, including its hedges ("is said to have", "Some legends state", "According to tradition", "The Mir claimed").

- **A dead poet initiates a living one in a vision.** "The Mir claimed that in a vision the Khwaja had made him his disciple" (p. 384, folio 362).
- **A beauty whose passage absolves sin.** Of Chanda in the *nakh-shikh*: "As she walks, men prostrate themselves before her only to find their sins washed away. Rishis and gods such as Indra, Brahma, Vishnu, Murari and Gandharva are enchanted by her" (pp. 386–387, folios 364–365).
- **A son granted for generosity.** "Ganpati, the raja of Chandragiri, was finally blessed with a son because of his great generosity" (p. 388, folio 366).
- **Fairies at a lake, and a doe who is a woman.** "One day seven fairies came to bathe in the lake; one of them was called Mrigawati"; she "had disguised herself as a doe to catch his eye" (p. 388, folio 366).
- **A parrot whose praise induces love at a distance, and nine Naths and eighty-four Siddhas as helpers.** "The parrot visits Padumavati and through the power of the king's austerities she falls in love with him. Although nine Naths and eighty-four Siddhas also assist Ratna-Sena in his pursuit, in reality it is the parrot's efforts which enable him to marry Padumavati" (p. 390, folio 368).
- **The marks of a Siddha, given as a list of bodily prodigies.** "The Siddha is one on whose limbs flies do not settle: the Siddha does not close his eyes for an instant. The Siddha is one who is not attended by a shadow: The Siddha is one who feels neither hunger nor confusion of thought. He whom the Lord has made a Siddha in this world, none can recognise him whether he be revealed or disguised." (p. 393, folio 369).
- **Gorakh as the Mahdi's counterpart — a cross-tradition equivalence stated flatly.** "a yogi becomes a Siddha only after meeting Gorakh, a counterpart of the Mahdi. Both Gorakh and Siddhas exhibit the distinctive features of the Mahdi" (p. 393, folio 369).
- **A virgin birth by a saint's blessing, compared by the book to Christ.** "Some legends state Kabir was the illegitimate son of a Brahman widow. One version of the legend is that he was conceived by a widow because of Ramananda's blessings, and that, like Christ, this occurred without a natural father"; the abandonment "is reminiscent of the adoption of Moses by the Pharoah's daughter" (p. 398, folio 374).
- **The vanished corpse and the flowers.** "When the door of the room where the dead body was lying was opened it was missing. According to tradition only a bunch of flowers was found under the sheet and these were divided amongst the two groups" (p. 399, folio 375).
- **A stake on the path of love reserved for a thief or a Hallaj.** "On that path the sharp point of a stake arises: a thief will be impaled thereon, or a Mansur (Hallaj)" (p. 393, folio 369).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Rizvi's central denial about the Hindi sufi masnawis, stated against unnamed opponents.** "The motive of these sufi poets who wrote Hindi masnawis was to arouse indescribable ecstasy both in themselves and in others thus obliterating the distinction between 'Thou' and 'I.' Their writings were not designed to fulfil a missionary aim, as some admirers have suggested" (p. 386, folio 364). **No opponent is named and no evidence is given** — (not stated).
- **His limit on syncretism, which is the sentence any "shared ground" argument in this archive has to answer.** "These spiritual trends and movements which we have discussed drew from each other both in a conscious and unconscious way; in general, however, they remained within a framework of their respective religious traditions. For example Muslims never subscribed to the Hindu belief in transmigration. Their main concern was with self-realization through Yoga or love or both, and this goal was realized differently according to the varying tenets of Islam and Hinduism" (p. 396, folio 372).
- **Why sufi poets took Indian themes — an aesthetic and opportunistic explanation, not a devotional one.** "From the fourteenth century the sufi poets who chose Indian themes and wrote masnawis in Hindi and other regional languages were prompted to do this because such themes offered them wide opportunities to express their thoughts on mysticism. Indian imagery and symbolism were not only new but were also artistic" (p. 385, folio 363).
- **A precedent argument for borrowing, reaching to Magians, Manichaeans, Christians and Buddhists.** "Non-Indian sufis of the early centuries of Islam did not hesitate to learn lessons from the Magian and Manichaean interaction of Being and non-Being… and also from the Christian Trinity typifying the Light of Being, the Mirror of the purified Human soul and the rays of the divine outpouring. They were impressed with the Buddhist nirvana as well as with the enthusiasm for their idols" (p. 385, folio 363), resting on the maxim "a/-majazu guntartu'l Hagiga [OCR?] [al-majazu qantaratu'l Haqiqa] (the phantasmal is the bridge to the Real)".
- **Jaʾisi's Nath material explained by the author's own interest, not by his plot.** "It was not only that Ratna-Sena became a yogi in the Padumavati which prompted Ja'isi to describe Nath beliefs and practices but his own deep interest in Naths. The author describes a number of other situations in a way which can only be meaningfully interpreted in the light of the traditions and customs of Naths and sufis" (p. 390, folio 368).
- **Kabir's metaphysics derived from the Naths, and his reputation explained by that derivation.** "Kabir's concept of Absolute Reality was founded on the dvaitadvaita-vilakshana-vada of the Naths. Its compatibility with the Wahdat al-Wujud was responsible for Kabir's fame as a muwahhid" (p. 399, folio 375); and "Kabir's void referred to sunya, a concept in Mahayana Buddhism and to Hindu esoteric philosophy… he indulged in the ancient Indian practice of describing Reality through negatives: neti, neti (not this, not this)" (p. 401, folio 377).
- **Kabir's parentage legend declared an invention, and a Muslim–Nath origin substituted.** "This story was an obvious invention and was an attempt to associate Kabir's parentage in some way with Hinduism. What is more probable is that Kabir was born into a Muslim family, the members of which weré [OCR?] [were] deeply imbued with Nath beliefs. That his parents' ancestors were yogis is not impossible" (p. 398, folio 374). **"Is not impossible" is the whole of the evidence offered.**
- **Two source-dates rejected, one date accepted, all on the author's judgement alone.** "Nothing can be said about the authenticity of Shaikh Taqi's date of death but that for Kabir is undoubtedly incorrect" (p. 398, folio 374); "Of various dates for his birth 1425 is the most acceptable" (p. 398, folio 374); "Of the many dates given for Kabir's death 1505 is the most reliable" (p. 399, folio 375). **No criterion is stated for any of the three.**
- **A miracle traced to a sufi prototype rather than to the event.** The vanished-corpse story "undoubtedly owes much to the tale mentioned in Chapter One of the bier of Ma'ruf Karkhi which was fought over by Muslims and non-Muslims alike, and may therefore be of sufi origin" (p. 399, folio 375). **A cross-reference to Chapter One, outside this chunk.**
- **A guru identification rejected on chronological grounds.** "Shaikh Taqi of Kara Manikpur… should not be identified as Kabir's guru for he was a disciple of Shaikh Salim Chishti (1479-1572)" (p. 398, folio 374); and on Ramananda, "legend fails to suggest he was formally initiated by the saint" (p. 398, folio 374).
- **A scholarly claim declined for want of evidence.** On Qutban following his patron: "Some scholars suggest that Qutban also retired to Bengal with his master, although the paucity of evidence would make such assertion difficult to sustain" (p. 389, folio 367).
- **Where bhakti came from, on Kabir's own admission.** "In a verse, Kabir admitted that Bhakti had been born in the Deccan and brought to northern India by Ramananda. Although Kabir was not the founder of Nirguna Bhakti as such, he was the movement's earliest known exponent in medieval India" (p. 397, folio 373); with the Nirguna/Saguna distinction set out on the same page.
- **A judgement on a sufi's verse quality.** Of Mir 'Abdu'l-Wahid: "His verses lack the lyrical impact of Hafiz and in some of them, the mysticism is rather laboured" (p. 384, folio 362).
- **A Bengali poet's religious-linguistic polemic reported.** The *Nur-Nama* of Razzaq Nandan 'Abdu'l-Hakim "militantly seeks to assert it is misguided to conceive that Bengali was one of the languages of Hindus" (p. 396, folio 372).

## Doubts

**The source carries no transcriber apparatus — no `[OCR?]`, no `[illegible]`, no `[folio N]` lines — so every inline flag above is mine, and the inventory below is the damage class list for pp. 383–401 (folios 361–377), per interim convention (b), which I have applied in both forms.**

**Archive-mapping doubts, most consequential first:**

1. **"Gorakh" is the biggest call in this chunk and I have not mapped it.** The book names the figure, not merely the category — "a yogi becomes a Siddha only after meeting Gorakh" (p. 393, folio 369) and "Gorakh's real absorption" (p. 401, folio 377) — which is the condition the brief sets for mapping. **But "Gorakh" is the principal figure of two archive rows at once: `tilla-jogian` ("Guru Gorakhnath (Nath yogis)", Salt Range) and `gorakhnath-goraknath-temple` ("Guru Gorakhnath", Gor Khatri, Peshawar).** Under the rule that a name which could be two rows goes under Doubts unmapped, I have withheld both. **Two things a human should weigh: (i) the ambiguity here is not "which man?" but "which of one man's two shrines?", which is not the case the two-row rule was written for, and if the archive lets one figure carry both rows then these two page references belong in both entries; (ii) the book prints "Gorakh", never "Gorakhnath", and gives him no site, no date and no shrine, so even the orthographic identification with "Guru Gorakhnath" is an inference I have not made in the body.** Nothing is lost by the withholding — both passages are quoted in full above — but nothing is gained either until this is ruled.
