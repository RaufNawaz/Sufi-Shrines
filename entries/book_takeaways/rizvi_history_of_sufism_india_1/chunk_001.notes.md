### Note on Dates (p. 12, folio not stated) — the book's date convention, transcribed in full

> "USLIM"[OCR?] [MUSLIM — dropped drop-cap] "dates are given according to the Hijra era or the event
> marking the Prophet Muhammad's emigration from Mecca to Medina. Although he arrived in Medina on
> 24 September 622, seventeen years later the Second Caliph 'Umar (634-44) instituted Muslim dating
> on the basis of the lunar months, beginning with Muharram. Thus the first Muharram was calculated
> to have fallen on 16 July 622. The adoption of the lunar calendar leads to the loss of one year
> every thirty-three years of the Roman calendar. Hence 1392 Hijra (H) or Anno Hegirae (AH) begins in
> 1972 ap"[OCR?] [AD] "and not in 2014. Of the two dates separated by an oblique in this book, the
> first is the Hijra (H) or Anno Hegirae (AH) and the second is AD. Where neither H. nor AD is
> mentioned alongwith dates, AD is invariably implied. All equivalent dates have been taken from
> Wustenfeld-Mahler'sche"[OCR?] [Wüstenfeld-Mahler'sche — lost umlaut] "Vergleichungs-Tabellen."

**This is the most operationally important page in the chunk: every `NNN/NNNN` pair anywhere in this
book is AH/AD in that order, a bare date is AD, and the AH→AD conversions are Wüstenfeld-Mahler,
not the author's own.** The page's heading reads "Note on Dates" while the Contents (p. 7) lists it
as "Notes on Dates" — see `## Doubts`.

## Shrines and figures in the archive

**Only two archive rows are touched in pp. 1–29, and both are touched under the author-mention ruling
of 18 September 2026, not by any statement about a shrine.** Nothing in this chunk describes a tomb,
a darbar, an ʿurs, a sajjada nashin, an endowment or a building date at any archive site. This is
front matter and the opening of an Introduction devoted to source criticism, so the yield is
apparatus and institutional history rather than shrine description.

- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar** (the book calls him "Shaikh Faridu'd-Din Ganj-i Shakar, who was
  known as Baba Farid", and elsewhere "Shaikh Faridu'd-Din" and "Baba Farid"): **he is named as the
  putative author of one spurious malfuzat and the putative subject of two others — the chunk's
  central point about the Chishti textual record.** "The Fawa'idu's-Salikin, the alleged discourses of
  Shaikh Qutbu'd-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki; the authorship attributed to Shaikh Faridu'd-Din"; "The
  Asraru'l-Auliya', the alleged discourses of Shaikh Faridu'd-Din; the authorship attributed to his
  son-in-law, Maulana Badr Ishaq"; "The Rahatu'l-Qulub, the alleged discourses of Shaikh Faridu'd-Din;
  the authorship attributed to Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya'" (p. 18, folio 4). **All three sit in a
  list the author introduces as works "embroidered by anonymous authors who were bereft of either a
  feeling for history or a first-hand knowledge of the lives of their heroes"** (p. 18, folio 4), and
  the relationship named — "his son-in-law, Maulana Badr Ishaq" — is stated by the book.
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **he is the reason Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya' gave for writing
  nothing down.** "Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din answered that he also had tried to write down what he had
  heard from Shaikh Faridu'd-Din (Baba Farid), but that he, too, had always been by what he said that
  he was unable to write a word." (p. 19, folio 5). **The sentence has a dropped word or line between
  "been" and "by" — see `## Doubts`; the fact that Nizamu'd-Din could not write survives the damage.**
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **a named place attached to him, and an eighteen-year term of
  service — the closest thing to a shrine-economy datum in this chunk.** The grandfather of Amir
  Khwurd, "Saiyid Muhammad bin Mahmud Kirmani, a merchant who traded hetween"[OCR?] [between]
  "Kirman in Iran and Lahore. During business trips to Lahore via Ajodhan, he would call on Shaikh
  Faridu'd-Din Ganj-i Shakar, who was known as Baba Farid, and on one occasion became his disciple …
  Although his father-in-law pressed him to remain in Multan, the Saiyid finally settled in Ajodhan
  and for about eighteen years loyally served his pir. After Baba Farid's death, the Shaikh and his
  sons migrated to Delhi and became great companions of Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya'." (p. 23, folio
  9). **"Ajodhan" is the only place the chunk associates with Baba Farid. No death date is given —
  (not stated) — and the book does not equate Ajodhan with Pakpattan.**
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **he heads the second of the three tabaqas by which the
  Akhbaru'l-Akhyar organises Indian sufi biography** — "2. Shaikh Faridu'd-Din Ganj-i Shakar and his
  followers and disciples." (p. 26, folio 12).
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **an entire hagiographic literature is built on him and his
  descendants, and the author rejects it.** "'Ali Asghar bin Shaikh Maudud bin Shaikh Muhammad Chishti
  of Fathpur … used a eulogistic style in his Jawahir-i Faridi to glorify the spiritual achievements
  of Shaikh Faridu'd-Din Ganj-i Shakar and his descendants. They were the ancestors of Shaikh Salim
  Chishti whose prayers were said to have been responsible for the birth of Akbar's son, Prince Salim,
  later Emperor Jahangir. The author drew his material from the many legends surrounding Shaikh
  Faridu'd-Din Ganj-i Shakar and his spiritual descendants. Their myths related to the performance of
  supernatural feats such as flying through the air and to the conversion of huge numbers of Hindus,
  the latter being quite contrary to the Chishti mission … The work was completed in 1033/1623."
  (p. 27, folio 13). **The genealogical claim linking Baba Farid's descendants to Shaikh Salim Chishti
  is reported as the Jawahir-i Faridi's, not asserted as fact.**
- **shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht** (the book calls him "Saiyid Abu 'Abdu'llah Jalalu'd-Din
  Husain bin Ahmad Bukhari, better known as the Makhdum Jahaniyan", and at p. 24 "the Makhdum
  Jahaniyan, Saiyid Jalal Bukhari"): **the chunk's fullest single archive-relevant passage, and it is
  dated to the day.** "Largely modelled on the pattern of the Fawa'idu'l-Fu'ad, though lacking the
  mystical sensitivity of the ideas expressed by Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya', is the collection of
  utterances of the celebrated Suhrawardi saint, Saiyid Abu 'Abdu'llah Jalalu'd-Din Husain bin Ahmad
  Bukhari, better known as the Makhdum Jahaniyan. The account dates from Sunday 8 Rabi' II 781/24 July
  1379 and ends on Tuesday 17 Muharram 782/23 April 1380, during the time of Shaikh's visit to Delhi."
  (p. 22, folio 8). **Two dates with weekdays, and a stated visit to Delhi. He is called "the
  celebrated Suhrawardi saint" — the silsila is the book's own word.**
- **shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht**: **four works of his teachings are named, with their
  compilers, and he is said to have supervised one.** The Delhi discourses were "the work of his
  disciple Abu 'Abdu' llah 'Ala'u'd-Din 'Ali bin Sa'd bin Ashraf bin 'Ali al-Quraishi al-Husaini. The
  Makhdum Jahaniyan encouraged the author in his undertaking and clarified particular points he could
  not understand. The work was called the Khulasatu'l-Alfaz Jami'u'l-'Ulum. Another of the Makhdum
  Jahaniyan's disciples, Makhdumzada 'Abdu'llah, compiled a summary of discourses delivered by the
  great saint on different occasions, but these lack the pithiness of those featured in the
  Khulasatu'l-Alfaz. The work is entitled the Siraju'l-Hidaya. The Khazinat al-Fawa'id al Jalalaliyya,
  composed by Ahmad bin Ya'qub in 752/1351 and the Khazinah-i Jalali by Abu'l Fazl bin Ziya' are
  compendiums of the teachings of the Makhdum Jahaniyan which, however, lack the personal touches
  contained in malfuzat." (p. 22, folio 8).
- **shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht**: **his family line to the fourth generation is the
  subject of a fifth work.** "The Mahbubiyya by Najmu'd-Din Yusuf bin Ruknu'd-Din Muhammad
  Nia'mu'llah Gardezi covers various stories in the life of the Makhdum Jahaniyan, Saiyid Jalal
  Bukhari, and the latter's son, grandson and great-grandson." (p. 24, folio 10). **No names, dates or
  burial places for the son, grandson or great-grandson — (not stated). The alias "Saiyid Jalal
  Bukhari" collides with a second archive row; see `## Doubts`.**
- **shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht**: the first history of his order is dated. "Although a
  Suhrawardi, Hamid bin Fazlu'llah did not hesitate to draw on material from Chishti malfuzat in his
  account of Suhrawardi saints. This appears to be the first available history of the Suhrawardi order
  compiled in this way." (p. 25, folio 11), of a work "written between 937/1530 and 942/1536".

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- **Shaikh Abu Sa'id bin Abu'l Khair of "Mayhana (Mehna or Meana)"** — the subject of "The earliest
  known book in the first category … the Halat wa Sukhanan-i Shaikh Abu Sa'id bin Abu'l Khair", by
  "Jamalu'd-Din Abu Ruh, a great-grandson of Shaikh Abu Sa'id"; a longer work by "Muhammad bin
  Munawwar bin Abi (Abu) Sa'id bin Abi Tahir bin Abi Sa'id" called "Asraru't-Tawhid Fi Magqamat"[OCR?]
  [Maqamat] "-i Shaikh Abi Sa'id", "started in 548/1153-54, but not until around 570/1174~-75 was it
  completed and dedicated by its author to the Ghurid Sultan Ghiyasu'd-Din (1163-1203)" (p. 17, folio
  3). Its internal structure is given with page ranges: first section "(pp. 3-42)"; the middle section
  in three chapters, "(pp. 45-204)" on miracles, "(pp. 207-94)" on anecdotes of other sufis,
  "(pp. 295-344)" on teachings; the third section "(pp. 347-53)" last testament, "(pp. 354-60)" death,
  "(pp. 361-92)" miracles (pp. 17–18, folios 3–4).
- **Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya'** — the chunk's most frequently named figure and the hinge of its
  argument: he denied authorship of any book on "15 Muharram 710/20 September 1301"[OCR?] — **see
  `## Doubts`, this date pair cannot stand as printed** (p. 18, folio 4); he urged Amir Hasan Sijzi to
  complete a malfuzat (p. 19, folio 5); his discourses, begun in diary form "Early in 1308" and prompted
  again "On 8 Shawwal 708/18 March 1309", became the Fawa'idu'l-Fu'ad, covering "the fifteen years up
  to 722/1322-23" (p. 19, folio 5); Amir Hasan produced a draft "of an account of the Shaikh's
  discourses over a period of thirteen months", after which "Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din praised them and
  filled in a few remaining gaps" (p. 19, folio 5).
- **Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din Mahmud Chiragh-i Dihli** — "(d. 1356)"; Shaikh Hamid Qalandar "first came into
  contact with Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din (d. 1356) in 754/1353-54 and was encouraged by him to write an
  account of the latter's discourses", the Khairu'l-Majalis, "consisting of one hundred chapters and an
  appendix" and undated in its entries; an earlier draft "written by the Shaikh's nephew had so
  disappointed the Shaikh that he had rejected the draft" (p. 20, folio 6); he "categorically refuted
  that any of his predecessors had written sufi texts" (p. 18, folio 4); he heads the third tabaqa of
  the Akhbaru'l-Akhyar (p. 26, folio 12).
- **Shaikh Mu'inu'd-Din Sijzi / Khwaja Mu'inu'd-Din Chishti** — named as the alleged author of the
  Anisu'l-Arwah and the alleged subject of the Dalilu'l-'Arifin (p. 18, folio 4); heads the first
  tabaqa, "Khwaja Mu'inu'd-Din Sijzi, his contemporaries and disciples" (p. 26, folio 12); the subject
  of Jahan Ara Begum's Munisu'l-Arwah, "completed in 1049/1640" (p. 27, folio 13).
- **Shaikh Qutbu'd-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki** — alleged author of the Dalilu'l-'Arifin and alleged subject of
  the Fawa'idu's-Salikin (p. 18, folio 4); his malfuzat, with those of 'Usman Harwani, "were not
  available while Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya' was alive" (pp. 18–19, folios 4–5). **Not to be read
  against `tomb-of-qutbuddin-aibak`; see `## Doubts`.**
- **Shaikh 'Usman Harwani** — alleged subject of the Anisu'l-Arwah (p. 18, folio 4).
- **Amir Hasan Sijzi** — "disciple and gifted scholar" of Nizamu'd-Din Auliya', compiler of the
  Fawa'idu'l-Fu'ad; "Amir Hasan's official duties often kept him outside Delhi but during a stay in
  the capital, he would attend the Shaikh's assembly once a week" (p. 20, folio 6).
- **Amir Khwurd (Saiyid Muhammad bin Mubarak bin Muhammad 'Alwi Kirmani)** — author of the
  "Siyaru'l-Auliya' fi Muhabbat al-Haqq jalla wa'-ala'", "the earliest known biographical dictionary of
  sufis written in India"; his family history, his grandfather's death "in 711/1311-12", his father's
  initiation "from Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din Maudud, the son of Khwaja Abu Yusuf of Chisht", his association
  with "Amir Khusraw, Amir Hasan, Fakhru'd-Din Zarradi and Ziya'u'd-Din Barani", and his forced
  migration to Daulatabad "In 1327" (pp. 23–24, folios 9–10).
- **Shaikh Hamidu'd-Din Nagauri and his grandson Faridu'd-Din Mahmud** — the Sururu's Sudur, "written
  by the long-lived grandson of Shaikh Hamidu'd-Din Nagauri", which "throws considerable light on
  Sultan Muhammad bin Tughluq's intense devotion to the descendants of Shaikh Hamidu'd-Din Nagauri"
  (p. 20, folio 6).
- **Shaikh Sharafu'd-Din Ahmad bin Yahya Munyari or Maneri** — the leading Firdausiyya figure of the

## Practices, institutions, economy

**No ʿurs, mela, langar, offering, sajjada nashin, Auqaf, waqf, land grant to a shrine, visitor
number, custodianship dispute or building date appears anywhere in pp. 1–29.** What the chunk does
carry is khanqah and jama'at-khana institutional history, one explicit royal grant, one coinage
series, and the urban economics of Delhi — all of it from the author's source survey.

- **Malfuzat compilation as a normal function of the khanqah** — "Following a precedent established by
  the disciples of Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya' and Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din, the compiling of malfuzat
  became an important feature of khanqah life" (p. 21, folio 7). The literature gives "glimpses into
  the daily routine of a khanqah" and takes the reader "down to the lower echelons of Muslim society"
  (p. 21, folio 7).
- **Royal grants to khanqahs, named and dated to a reign — the chunk's one endowment datum.** "The
  confusion and consternation produced by Sultan Muhammad bin Tughluq's religious policies among a
  section of Delhi's religious élite was replaced by the orthodoxy of Sultan Firuz, but neither the
  reversal of the former policies, nor the new liberal grants to khangahs"[OCR?] [khanqahs] "satisfied
  the aged Nasiru'd-Din Chiragh" (p. 21, folio 7). **A patronage transaction and its refusal by a
  named shaikh, with no sums, lands or deeds given — (not stated).**
- **The jama'at-khana as the institutional setting of discipleship** — "the serenity of Shaikh
  Nizamu'd-Din Auliya's jama'at-khana had filled him with a fervent love for mysticism"; after exile
  Amir Khwurd sought "some form of spiritual comfort at the jama'at-khana, of Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din
  Auliya's successor" (p. 23, folio 9); the Siyaru'l-Auliya' exposes sufi lives "both in the
  jama'at-khanas and their own houses" (p. 24, folio 10).
- **Chishti institutions outside the capital** — the Rafiqu'l 'Arifin "give interesting insights into
  Chishti centres in small towns" (p. 21, folio 7); and the Chishtiyya-i Bahishtiya is informative on
  "sufis between Shikohabad in Mainpuri (U.P.) and Jaunpur" (p. 28, folio 14).
- **Forced relocation of the religious classes by the state, twice stated and once dated.** "the
  transfer of the capital by Muhammad bin Tughlugq"[OCR?] [Tughluq] "from Dehli to Daulatabad in the
  Deccan" (p. 19, folio 5); "In 1327 Sultan Muhammad bin Tughluq, for reasons which will be explained
  at greater length, forced the 'ulama' and sufis to migrate to Daulatabad. Amir Khwurd was a reluctant
  participant in this mass exodus of the élite. After a few years, along with others, he was allowed to
  return" (p. 23, folio 9).
- **The urban economy of Delhi as the Siyaru'l-Auliya' reveals it — the chunk's fullest economic
  passage.** "It shows that the lives of Muslims in that affluent eastern capital, continually
  replenished by the proceeds from booty and plunder, were burdened by serious problems. Accommodation
  was one of the gravest difficulties encountered by the average citizen in Delhi and employment in the
  metropolis was exceedingly hard to find. The Siyaru'l-Auliya', the most authentic record of about
  half a century of life in Delhi, stresses the social and economic tensions caused by many divergent
  influences and through religious conflicts." (p. 24, folio 10). The groups it documents: "members of
  the court, the princes, the governing classes, the 'ulama', merchants, agriculturalists, artisans and
  the common Muslims" (p. 24, folio 10).
- **Royal devotion to a pir's descendants** — the Sururu's Sudur "throws considerable light on Sultan
  Muhammad bin Tughluq's intense devotion to the descendants of Shaikh Hamidu'd-Din Nagauri. What
  emerges from the book is the image of a considerate and compassionate ruler, in contrast to the
  capricious and cruel character portrayed by Barani and Ibn Battuta." (p. 20, folio 6).
- **A coinage series, dated, and otherwise unattested** — "There is also a reference to a Bengali
  Sultan named Ikhtiyaru'd-Din Ghazi, who issued his own coins between 750/1349-50 and 753/1352—53 but
  is otherwise unknown." (p. 22, folio 8).
- **A military campaign and a shaikh's civil-protection advice** — the Malfuz-i Safar "includes an
  illuminating reference to the second expedition of Sultan Firuz to Bengal. Travelling from Jaunpur
  towards Orissa, via Bihar, the Sultan and his army aroused panic and terror among the masses in
  Bihar and the Shaikh advised them to remain in their houses for protection." (p. 22, folio 8).
- **Long-distance trade and a mint office as the occupations of a pir's disciples' family** — the
  Kirman–Lahore merchant who called at Ajodhan, and "Saiyid Ahmad Kirmani, an uncle of Saiyid Muhammad
  bin Mahmud, an officer in the Multan mint" (p. 23, folio 9).
- **Sama' and music as a documented ritual practice** — "The author's knowledge of the music used in
  sufi sama' rituals enabled him to give useful details about different aspects of the development of
  music in northern India" (p. 28, folio 14); and the book's own frontispiece subject, "Sufi Sama'

- **The form of the discourse assembly** — "The discourses delivered by a leading sufi to a select
  gathering of sufi disciples and visitors gave rise to a distinctive genre of Persian literature"

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

The attributions below are the book's; the author reports each of these as another writer's material
and in most cases distances himself from it.

- **The flying camel of Nizamu'd-Din Auliya'** — Amir Khwurd "could not restrain himself from
  recording that each night during the Shaikh's lifetime a flying camel had stopped at the window to
  take Nizamu'd-Din Auliya' to the Ka'ba and bring him back in time for early breakfast" (p. 24, folio
  10). **The chunk's one fully narrated miracle, and the author frames it as a lapse.**
- **A dervish's blessing puts a sultan on the throne** — Minhaj Siraj "emphasized that it was the
  blessings of a dervish which enabled Sultan Shamsu'd-Din Iltutmish to succeed to the throne of
  Delhi", and "'Isami gave a different colour to this particular story" (p. 29, folio 15). **Two
  sources, two versions, and the book keeps both.**
- **Shaikh Salim Chishti's prayers and the birth of Jahangir** — "Shaikh Salim Chishti whose prayers
  were said to have been responsible for the birth of Akbar's son, Prince Salim, later Emperor
  Jahangir" (p. 27, folio 13). **"were said to have been" is the book's hedge.**
- **Flight and mass conversion attributed to Baba Farid's descendants** — "Their myths related to the
  performance of supernatural feats such as flying through the air and to the conversion of huge
  numbers of Hindus" (p. 27, folio 13).
- **Shaikh Abu Sa'id presented as a supernatural being** — "The Asraru't-Tawhid was designed to glorify
  the mystical achievements of Shaikh Abu Sa'id and presents him as a supernatural being. Nevertheless,
  glimpses of the economic and social life of the Shaikh which tend to emerge accidentally are both
  informative and entertaining." (p. 18, folio 4). **The chunk's clearest statement of the author's
  method: hagiography read against the grain for social and economic data.**
- **Chishti miracle literature of the Sabiriyya branch** — the Saiyaru'l-Aqtab[OCR?] is "legendary and
  fantastic in the recounting of Chishti miracles, particularly of the Sabiriyya branch" (p. 27, folio
  13).
- **Miracles as by then inseparable from sufi belief** — of the Mir'atu'l-Asrar: "The Shaikh could not
  resist the temptation to record many miracles and legends, which by that time were an integral part
  of the beliefs associated with sufis" (p. 28, folio 14).
- **Miracles deliberately excluded by one compiler** — Shaikh 'Abdu'l-Haqq "scrupulously excluded all
  references to supernatural and miraculous elements, highlighting instead the mystical and spiritual
  ideas, ethical behaviour and psychological perceptions of the great sufis" (p. 26, folio 12).
- **A revenge plot with elephants** — the dervish 'Ayyub's "attempts to have the Qazi trampled to death
  by elephants seem to have been thwarted by the" — **sentence truncated at the chunk boundary**
  (p. 29, folio 15).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The book's own definition of its subject, in its first sentence** — "HE"[OCR?] [THE — dropped
  drop-cap] "terms, sufi, Wali-Allah (protégé of God), dervish and fagir"[OCR?] [faqir], "are applied
  to Muslim spiritualists who attempt to achieve a development of their intuitive faculties through
  ascetic exercises, contemplation, renunciation and self-denial." (p. 15, folio not stated).
- **He concedes the popular image of shrine Islam before qualifying it — directly relevant to the
  archive's subject matter.** "There is no doubt that sufism or Tasawwuf conjures up images of such
  institutions and customs as saint cults, fascination with the occult, thaumaturgic and orgiastic
  practices, a mysterious world of visions and miracles, erotic poems and lascivious dancing. However,
  a significant section of sufis managed to help their followers to stabilize their emotions and to
  inculcate an understanding among different groups within the Muslim community." (p. 15, folio not
  stated).
- **His periodisation claim** — "By the twelfth century sufism had become a universal aspect of Islamic
  social life whose influence had spread to all Muslims." (p. 15, folio not stated).
- **His stated method and thesis** — "The present work seeks to study sufism as a psycho-historical
  phenomenon, the author seeing it as a potential force to meet social and political challenges
  produced by protracted political upheavals, associated with autocratic oppression and economic
  deprivation." (p. 15, folio not stated).
- **The scope of the two volumes, and what this volume excludes.** Volume I covers "sufi history from
  the establishment of the Delhi Sultanate to the beginning of the Mughal empire", emphasising "the
  Chishtiyya, Suhrawardiyya, Firdausiyya and Kubrawiyya orders", with "qalandars and legendary and
  semi-legendary saints" not neglected (p. 15, folio not stated); "The fifteenth century also saw the
  introduction to India of the Shattari and the Qadiri orders. These orders, along with the Naqshbandis
  and Chishtis, will be discussed in the second volume." (pp. 15–16, folios not stated / 2). **This
  matters for the queue: the Qadiri and Naqshbandi material that most Punjabi and Sindhi archive rows
  would need is in Volume II, not here.**
- **His disclaimer of comprehensiveness** — the volumes "deal with only the most important
  personalities of each order, their basic teachings and their contributions to religious, mystical,
  social, economic and political thought. They are not intended to be a directory of Indian sufis."
  (p. 16, folio 2).
- **His source base and its date** — "based mainly on Arabic and Persian sources, but they have also
  drawn on sufi works in the main regional Indian languages"; material "collected and analyzed over a
  period of twenty years and the present volume committed to paper only during 1973-74" (p. 16, folio
  2).
- **His stated preference for manuscripts over nineteenth- and twentieth-century printings — a
  methodological rule with direct consequences for citation.** "Thus many works published in the
  nineteenth and twentieth centuries and available only to a fortunate few have been by-passed in
  favour of their manuscript copies to be found in libraries available to all. Printed material from
  such libraries as that of the India Office, London, has been rapidly disappearing … the manuscripts
  used as source material for this work are older and more reliable than the nineteenth and early
  twentieth century publications, some of which were neither carefully edited nor accurately printed.
  A large number of these publications even fail to mention manuscripts from which the editions were
  prepared." (p. 16, folio 2).
- **His seven-part classification of the material** — "1. Treatises in prose and verse written by
  leading sufis who are discussed in this book. / Letters written by leading sufis. / Sufi discourses
  known as malfuzat. / Doctrinal works based on the above three categories. / Sufi biographical
  dictionaries. / Histories; and the biographies of nobles and poets. / Medieval geographies and
  accounts written by travellers, sailors and pilgrims." (pp. 16–17, folios 2–3; the numerals 2–7 are
  lost in the OCR and survive only as the garbled line "NAWPwWN" — see `## Doubts`).
- **His two-class test for the authenticity of malfuzat — the chunk's most reusable analytic tool.**
  "There were two classes of compilations of authentic discourses": those "collected by a descendant or
  disciple of the sufi long after his death", whose "flavour … differed little from that of Christian
  hagiological works"; and "The most dependable … the second category which consisted of discourses
  recorded soon after they were delivered by a pir, and which were also dated. At times sufi masters
  revised the drafts themselves and confirmed the accuracy, or otherwise, of these statements."
  (p. 17, folio 3).
- **Pious forgery as a market response, and his refutation of a rival explanation.** Seven spurious
  Chishti malfuzat are listed as "alleged discourses" (p. 18, folio 4); "Nizami has ventured the
  hypothesis that the fabricated works tried to fill a vacuum in the Chishti si/sila"[OCR?] [silsila]
  "caused by the transfer of the capital by Muhammad bin Tughluq from Dehli to Daulatabad in the
  Deccan. The view is untenable for unauthentic sufi literature had already begun to appear by the time
  of Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya', as the reference to his alleged work would tend to indicate; it also
  shows the daring of the authors who ascribed books to saints who were still living." (pp. 18–19,
  folios 4–5). **A named scholar's hypothesis expressly rejected, with the author's evidence given.**
- **Forgery as a general phenomenon, not a sufi one** — "This type of pious forgery was not peculiar to
  sufis, the Qussas or story tellers during the first and second centuries of Islam and the forgers of
  the Hadis of the Prophet Muhammad were many." (p. 19, folio 5).
- **Why the forgeries sold: demand, and communal polemic.** They "catered to the insatiable popular
  taste for details of the miracles of mystics, as well as those of yogis and dalandars"[OCR?]
  [qalandars]; "They also provided for the proselytizing militancy of many Muslims whose concerns were
  not really with sufism, but in the assertion of their own superiority in the field of religion."
  (p. 19, folio 5).
- **His critique of the intellectual effect of the malfuzat genre** — "Never in debate form, the
  discourses failed to include an analysis of opposing views and were believed to bear the stamp of
  infallibility. This tended to produce a rigidity in the thinking of the devotees of the great Shaikh
  and to prevent the emergence of a detached rationality." (p. 20, folio 6).
- **His willingness to say a revered source is wrong** — "For example, the views of Shaikh
  Nizamu'd-Din Auliya' about Nur Turk's leadership in the attempted overthrow of the government of
  Raziyya are not necessarily correct." (p. 20, folio 6).
- **His judgement on the sufis of Delhi in 1327** — "reading between the lines it is not difficult to
  assess that by that time sufis in Delhi had tended to depart from the traditions established by the
  early Chishtis and had become used to the way of life in the capital and were therefore less than
  keen to depart to a more hostile and alien environment in the Deccan." (p. 24, folio 10).
- **His verdict on the two best malfuzat** — "The Fawaidu'l-Fu'ad and the Khairu'l-Majalis are the best
  examples of the art of malfuzat writing." (p. 21, folio 7); and on biographical dictionaries, "The
  most dependable and scholarly collection of biographies of Indian sufis is the Akhbaru'l-Akhyar fi
  Asraru'l-Abrar" and "our best source of information for biographies still remains the
  Akhbaru'l-Akhyar" (pp. 25–26, folios 11–12).
- **His account of the sober compiler's technique, which the archive could adopt.** Shaikh
  'Abdu'l-Haqq's three-tabaqa scheme "helped Shaikh 'Abdu'l-Haqq to place the biographies of sufis
  whose dates were questionable into a reasonable historical sequence, thus avoiding the acceptance of
  dates based on unauthentic traditions." (p. 26, folio 12).
- **His demolition of the most widely read nineteenth-century source — the single most important
  argument in the chunk for anyone using shrine hagiography.** Of the Khazinatu'l-Asfiya': it "refers
  at length to many earlier sources, and its author believed these should all be considered of equal
  importance, even though many confused fact with fiction. Using unreliable sufi family trees, Ghulam
  Sarwar accepted every date given in these texts and composed verses of chronograms. The publication
  of this work synchronized with the impact of British liberalism in India, giving scholars an
  opportunity to glorify the mission of sufis and the impact of their miracles in the Islamization of
  the sub-continent." (p. 29, folio 15). **A dated, motivated and named source of exactly the kind of
  genealogy-and-chronogram material that shrine records rest on, declared unreliable.**
- **His characterisation of political chronicles as sufi sources** — "Political chronicles refer to
  sufis only in connection with their political role in history or in short biographical notes either
  at the end of each reign or in the conclusion of the works themselves." (p. 29, folio 15).
- **An assessment of the social level the sources reach, with a judgement attached** — the malfuzat
  "take us down to the lower echelons of Muslim society, and show that the passivity and inertia of its
  members was matched only by their firm faith in Allah." (p. 21, folio 7).
- **His stated finding on Hindu–Muslim interaction, announced in advance of Chapter Six** — "A detailed
  discussion of the interaction of medieval Hindu mystic traditions and sufism shows a unique polarity
  between the intolerant rigidity of the orthodox and the flexibility of the sufis in India." (p. 15,
  folio not stated).

## Doubts

**This book's transcription carries no `[OCR?]`, `[illegible]` or `[folio N]` markers at all — the
tesseract route leaves no transcriber apparatus. Every `[OCR?]` above is mine, and the damage classes
below are the inventory.** The damage is heavy in transliterated titles and proper names, which is
exactly where this chunk's value lies.

1. **q→g — the most dangerous class here, because it hits title words that are also citation
   sigla.** "Tahagat"/"Tabagat" for *Tabaqat* (pp. 9, 29), "Kalimatu's-Sadiqgin"/"Kalimatu's-Sadigin"/
   "Kalimatu's-Sadigqin" for *Kalimatu's-Sadiqin* (pp. 9, 27, 28) — three different spellings of one
   title in one chunk, "Khulasatu'l-Managib" for *Manaqib* (p. 24), "Zubdatu'l-Mugamat" for *Maqamat*
   (p. 27), "Magqamat" for *Maqamat* (p. 17), "Mir'atu'l Makhlugqat" for *Makhluqat* (p. 28), "fagir"
   for *faqir* (p. 15), "dalandars" for *qalandars* (p. 19), "khangahs" for *khanqahs* (p. 21),
   "Tughlugq"/"Tughlug" for *Tughluq* (pp. 19, 24).
2. **y→v and f→t in transliterated Arabic titles, concentrated in the sigla key** — "Akhbaru'l-Akhvar"
   for *Akhyar*, "Hilyat al-Auliva'" for *Auliya'*, "Sivaru'l-'Arifin" and "Sivaru'l-Auliva'" for
   *Siyaru'l-*, "Tahagat al-Sufivva" for *Sufiyya*, "al-Qushairivyva" for *al-Qushairiyya*, "al-Yate'i"
   for *al-Yafi'i* against the siglum's own "Yafe'i" (all p. 9). **Every one of these is a citation
   siglum expansion; a consolidator must not treat the sigla-key spellings as the book's.**
3. **Apostrophe and ʿ/ʾ loss or substitution** — "Fawai'du'l-Fu'ad" (p. 9) against "Fawa'idu'l-Fu'ad"
   (p. 18) and "Fawaidu'l-Fu'ad" (p. 21) for one title; "'Abdw't-Qadir Jilani" for *'Abdu'l-Qadir
   Jilani* (p. 25); "Ibn al-\*Imad" (p. 9); "Hindu'" (p. 7); "wa'-ala'" (p. 23).
4. **Single-letter substitutions in personal names, where a wrong reading is a wrong person** —
   "Ghulam Mu'inu'd-Rin" for *Mu'inu'd-Din* (p. 28, corrected by "Ghulam Mu'inu'd-Din's work" eleven
   lines later on the same page); "Muhammad Sagiq Kashmiri" for *Muhammad Sadiq Kashmiri* (p. 9,
   corrected at p. 27); "Nauwab" for *Nawwab* (p. 16); "Ris disciple" for *his disciple* (p. 19);
   "hetween" for *between* (p. 23); "$.A.A. Rizvi" for *S.A.A. Rizvi* (p. 20).
5. **Dropped drop-caps at every section opening** — "USLIM" for *MUSLIM* (p. 12), "HE terms" for *THE
   terms* (p. 15), "HE author" for *THE author* (p. 11). **Systematic, cosmetic, and it does not touch
   a fact.**
6. **"AD" rendered as lower-case "ap" throughout**, because the book sets it in small capitals:
   "to 1600 ap" (p. 3), "begins in 1972 ap" (p. 12). **The Note on Dates itself prints "AD" correctly
   twice in the same paragraph, which proves the reading.**
7. **Lost umlaut** — "Wustenfeld-Mahler'sche" for *Wüstenfeld-Mahler'sche* (p. 12), the source of
   every AH→AD conversion in the book. **"Ethe" (p. 9) is presumably *Ethé*, likewise stripped.**
8. **Corrupt numerals and footnote markers — flag every locator below; none may enter an entry
   unchecked.** The volume number of one journal is printed "I" at p. 18 and "1" at p. 19 for the same
   article ("Medieval India quarterly, Aligarh, I, October, 1950, no. 2, pp. 1-42" vs "Medieval India
   quarterly, 1, no. 2, p. 39"); footnote markers are read as letters and symbols — "AFF, pp. 34-5" for
   *4FF* (p. 19), "2\*Chishti mystic records" (p. 19), "'1Details of editions" (p. 17), "Shaikh Abu
   Sa'id bin Abu'l Khair!" where "!" is marker 1 (p. 17); "1163-1203", "1240-42", "1719-48",
   "1551-1602" and "570/1174~-75" carry stray dash forms; the numerals 2–7 of the seven-part list of
   source categories are lost entirely and survive only as the line "NAWPwWN" (p. 17) — **the seven
   items are legible, their numbering is not**; the folio digit in the header of p. 21 is lost as "|".
9. **A dropped word or line inside a sentence about Baba Farid** — "but that he, too, had always been
   by what he said that he was unable to write a word" (p. 19, folio 5). Something is missing between
   "been" and "by". **The fact survives; the wording must not be quoted as printed.**

**Date pairs that cannot stand as printed — the highest-priority check in this chunk:**

- **"On 15 Muharram 710/20 September 1301 a visitor to the Shaikh referred to a book written by him"
  (p. 18, folio 4).** By the book's own Note on Dates (p. 12) the first element is AH and the second
  AD, and 710 AH and 1301 AD cannot correspond — the AH year would have to be in the low 700s for
  September 1301. **I have not corrected it and I do not assert what the right figure is; the printed
  form is recorded above exactly. Either the typesetting or the OCR has damaged one of the two numbers,
  and this locator must be checked against the printed book before any fact resting on it is used.**
- **All other AH/AD pairs in this chunk are recorded exactly as printed and are unverified**, including
  "8 Rabi' II 781/24 July 1379", "17 Muharram 782/23 April 1380" (p. 22, folio 8), "8 Shawwal 708/18
  March 1309" (p. 19, folio 5), "15 Sha'ban 749/8 November 1348", "21 Sha'ban 774/15 February 1373",
  "1 Muharram 775/23 June 1373" (p. 22, folio 8) and "27 Ramazan 1049/21 January 1640" (p. 27, folio
  13). **The weekday attributions ("Sunday", "Tuesday") are likewise as printed and unchecked.**
- **"First published 1978" and "©1975" on the same page (p. 4, folio not stated)**, with the
  Acknowledgements dated "1 January, 1978." (p. 11, folio not stated). Three years for one book. The
  manifest's "first published 1978" matches the imprint line; the 1975 copyright is the book's own and
  is not an OCR artefact as far as I can tell.
