## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar** (the book calls him "Shaikh Faridu'd-Din", "Shaikh Faridu'd-Din (Baba Farid)" and "Baba Farid"): **he is named six times, always as Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din's own pir and as the authority behind Nizamu'd-Din's practice. No death date, no Pakpattan, no tomb, no ʿurs, no shrine and no silsila label is given for him anywhere in pp. 181–197 — all (not stated).**
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **he is the terminus of the chain by which Nizamu'd-Din dates the arrival of mass discipleship**, in Nizamu'd-Din's own reported words — "From the times of Shaikh Abu Sa'id (bin) Abi'l Khair to those of Shaikh Saifu'd-Din Bakharzi and Shaikh Shihabu'd-Din Suhrawardi and Shaikh Faridu'd-Din (Baba Farid), large numbers of people began to throng the doors of sufi teachers. They belonged to all classes—rulers, noblemen, dignitaries and commoners" (p. 192, folio 174). **The parenthesis "(Baba Farid)" stands inside the quotation and appears to be the author's gloss, not the speaker's words** — see Doubts.
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **he is the stated source of Nizamu'd-Din's authority to initiate disciples at all, and this is the chunk's single most consequential sentence about him.** "I accept what people say to me, and use no intermediaries, as a perfect saint (Baba Farid) has authorized me to initiate disciples" (p. 192, folio 174).
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **he is the origin of Nizamu'd-Din's amulet practice, in an anecdote with a physical object.** "the fact that Shaikh Faridu'd-Din once gave me a pot of ink and ordered me to write amulets for the people. Finding me hesitant, he upbraided me saying that many would come to me for assistance and that I should refuse no-one" (p. 193, folio 175).
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **his descendants are named as objects of a reigning Sultan's devotion — the chunk's one statement that a saint's family, not only the saint, drew royal attachment.** Muhammad bin Tughluq "was a most religious man, who was deeply devoted to the descendants of Shaikh Hamidu'd-Din Nagauri and Baba Farid" (p. 194, folio 176). **This is Rizvi's own assertion, sourced in the chunk only to "All independent sources affirm" with no source named** — (not stated).
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **he is the head of a three-generation transmission of one physical book, given with a khilafat-nama.** Nizamu'd-Din gave Shaikh Qutbu'd-Din Munawwar "the copy of the 'Awarifu'l-Ma'arif which his grandfather had given the Shaikh to be passed on to his grandson. Shaikh Jamalu'd-Din had originally obtained it from Baba Farid at the time of receiving his khilafat-nama" (p. 196, folio 178). **A named manuscript moving Baba Farid → Shaikh Jamalu'd-Din of Hansi → Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din → Shaikh Qutbu'd-Din Munawwar, tied at one end to the grant of a khilafat-nama.**
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **he is cited as the precedent for refusing a Sultan's grant of villages.** "Quoting a precedent set by Baba Farid, the Shaikh requested the Qazi to admonish those who acted contrary to the traditions of their pirs" (p. 196, folio 178). **The content of the precedent is not given** — (not stated).
- **data-darbar** (**the person is not named in this chunk at all; only his book is**): **the *Kashfuʾl-Mahjub* is cited by Amir Khusraw as the model of the first of nine literary styles.** "1. The style of sufis and saints, which is of two kinds: (a) Of men of "gravity and stations," like that to be met with in Kashfu'l-Mahjub, Suluku'l-Musafirin, and other similar works" (p. 189, folio 171). **No author, no name "Hujwiri", no Lahore, no tomb, no date** — all (not stated). **Mapped on the ruled author-only convention of 18 September 2026 stretched one step further, to a title-only mention; see Doubts, where the case for unmapping it is set out.**

- **Amir Khusraw** — "India's most famous Persian poet" and "his most beloved disciple" (p. 186, folio 168). Of Turkic extraction, "born into the Lachin tribe which originally inhabited the area near Balkh", the tribe "Uprooted by the Mongol invasion"; father "Amir Saifu'd-Din Mahmud, referred to by the poet Saif-i Shamsi", who rose under "Sultan Shamsu'd-Din Iltutmish" and settled "in Patiali, in the Eta district", marrying the daughter of "'Imadu'l-Mulk"; "Abwl-Hasan [OCR?] [Abu'l-Hasan] Khusraw was born in 651/1253, the second of three male siblings"; began composing at eight, his father dying about the same time (pp. 186–187, folios 168–169). He "learnt Persian, also some Turkic dialects, and Hindawi from the Hindu chiefs who would call on his grandfather. But he failed to develop a taste for Arabic" (p. 187, folio 169). Patrons in sequence: "Malik Chajju 'Ala'u'd-Din Kishli Khan, Balban's nephew" from about 671/1272-73; two years later "Balban's son, Bughra Khan, governor of Samana"; then "Prince Muhammad, and governor of Multan", whom he served five years and who "in Zul'hijja 683/March 1285 … was killed by Mongols"; then Awadh and "Malik Amir 'Ali Sarjandar"; then "Mu'izzu'd-Din Kaiqubad" at Delhi (pp. 187–188, folios 169–170). **In Multan "Khusraw encountered the Mongols and was, for a short time, imprisoned by them"** (p. 187, folio 169). He died "on 18 Shawwal 725/27 September 1325", "a further six months after the Shaikh's death", after reciting a verse at the grave (p. 190, folio 172); **the footnote gives a rival date, "Other dates ure [OCR?] [are] 29 ZuIqa'da [OCR?] [Zu'lqa'da] 725 6 November 1325"** (p. 190, folio 172, footnote 2). **He was "not a khalifa of Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya'", but "important communications of the khalifas to their Shaikh were made through the sufi poet"** (p. 186, folio 168).

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The khilafat-nama as a produced, dated, signed and disputed document — the chunk's single richest institutional passage, and the one most usable in an entry on succession.** "A model khilafat-nama was drafted by Maulana Fakhru'd-Din Zarradi, the copies were produced by Saiyid Husain, the uncle of Amir Khwurd, and signed by both the copyist and Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya'. They are dated 20 Zu'lhijja 724/8 December 1324, but among the Chishtis a controversy developed over their authenticity, and it was alleged that they were not signed by the Shaikh while in a state of full consciousness" (p. 181, folio 163). **A drafter, a copyist, a double signature, a date and a succession dispute, all in four lines.**
- **A khilafat-nama written by the Shaikh's own hand, with its text of instruction.** For Qazi Muhi'u'd-Din Kashani: "Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din wrote Kashani's khilafat-nama with his own hand, adding the following: 'Lead the life of an ascetic; pay no attention to things of this world and its authorities. Do not accept gifts from rulers. When you have visitors and have nothing to offer them, consider this a great blessing and favour from Allah.'" (pp. 194–195, folios 176–177).
- **Langar, with the book's own definition in a footnote, and a deathbed order about its stores.** The Shaikh "summoned his relations as well as his servant, Iqbal, and ordered them to confirm that there was not a morsel of food in the house. They assured him that only a few handfuls of grain remained, and they had been kept for the /angar [OCR?] [langar]. The Shaikh was enraged at Iqbal preserving worthless assets and ordered it to be immediately given to the poor" (p. 181, folio 163), the word footnoted "An alms house for the distribution of free food" (p. 181, folio 163, footnote 3). **And its confessional openness, stated as inference: "Almost certainly Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din's /angar [langar] was open to Hindus and Muslims alike"** (p. 184, folio 166).
- **A Sultan builds a dome over a saint's grave against the saint's own wish, and the dome has not survived.** "Although he had wished to be buried in the open, Muhammad bin Tughluq later constructed an imposing dome over the Shaikh's grave", footnoted "SSA [OCR?] [SA], p. 154. However it has not survived" (p. 181, folio 163). **The building date, the builder's motive and the date of loss are all (not stated).**
- **Tomb veneration by both communities, and dust as relic.** "Both Hindus and Muslims were attracted to the Shaikh's tomb and considered its dust a sacred relic" (p. 181, folio 163).
- **A Sultan as a serial builder of shrines — the most transferable institutional sentence in the chunk.** Of Muhammad bin Tughluq: "He also built a number of tombs at the graves of eminent sufis" (p. 194, folio 176). **No site, no number, no date is given** — (not stated).
- **The jama'at-khana described as a building, with an explicit complaint that the sources do not describe it.** "Sufi sources frequently mention the Shaikh's jama'at-khana, but fail to give a detailed description. Stray references tend to imply that Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din's quarters and the jama'at-khana were two separate units. A banyan tree was the main feature of the complex. The jama'at-khana itself was a pillared hall, with a few rooms on the first floor, and an open space in front. Its court-yard was surrounded by four walls and access was through a vestibule. On both sides of the hall there were small rooms which the Shaikh used for his midday rest. A few other small rooms were built near the jama'at-khana to store grain for distribution to the people, and there was also a kitchen, where food was cooked for the poor and visitors" (p. 181, folio 163). **Named components: pillared hall, upper rooms, forecourt, walled courtyard, vestibule, side rooms, grain stores, kitchen, banyan tree.**
- **Its materials, its discomfort and its unplanned growth.** "The pillars and walls were made of mud and the roofs were covered with logs and earth. The building was uncomfortable for' its occupants in all seasons. During the summer the hot Delhi winds roasted them; in the rainy season, cooling breezes were rare. As the work of the jama'at-khana increased, so its buildings were expanded in a sprawling, unplanned way" (p. 181, folio 163).
- **Sleeping arrangements as a rank system.** "Beds would be offered only to distinguished guests; permanent members occupied places assigned to them on the floor" (pp. 181–182, folios 163–164).
- **The daily round, the mosque, and the two concessions to old age.** "A day in the jama'at-khana was routinized and broken by prayers offered by the Shaikh in the Kilukhari mosque. He always walked to the mosque but towards the end of his life he reluctantly accepted the gift of a mare from a disciple who was a servant of Malik Yar Parran. Old age also appears to have compelled him to use a palanquin. It seemed that a small house was built for the Shaikh near the mosque, and other senior members of the order also appeared to have acquired small houses as personal residences in either Ghiyaspur or Kilukhari" (p. 182, folio 164). **The Shaikh's residence and his senior khalifas' residences are here private houses, distinct from the jama'at-khana.**
- **Indiscriminate initiation stated as deliberate policy, with two stated reasons.** Barani "posed the question why the Shaikh accepted disciples on an indiscriminate basis in contrast to other pirs who chose their followers with great care", and the reply names both reasons: that imposing "difficult conditions" would deprive disciples "of even that level of piety", and "The second reason for my accepting so many disciples is the fact that Shaikh Faridu'd-Din once gave me a pot of ink and ordered me to write amulets for the people" (pp. 192–193, folios 174–175). **Amulet-writing for the public is here a founding instruction, not a later accretion.**
- **Charity doctrine, against hoarding and against indiscriminate giving alike.** "he opposed those who accrued money in excess of their needs … Clothes were necessary to cover the body, but spare ones were not to be hoarded. However, indiscriminate charity was extravagant, only charity to please God had spiritual merit" (p. 185, folio 167). And renunciation defined as distribution: not "wearing a loincloth in a state of asceticism for one should continue to wear clothing and also to eat, but rather as the distribution to the poor of anything surplus" (p. 183, folio 165).
- **Royal grants refused, part-refused and torn up — four separate transactions, with figures.** (i) Kashani, on becoming a disciple, "tore up the farman of idrar or stipends granted him by the Sultans for his scholarship and exchanged them for the poverty of asceticism" (p. 194, folio 176). (ii) 'Ala'u'd-Din Khalji sent Kashani a farman including "the grant of the post of the Qazi of Awadh, which had been held by his ancestors, as well as a number of villages", which he accepted, at the cost of a year's estrangement from his pir (p. 195, folio 177). (iii) Muhammad bin Tughluq "dispatched a farman of two villages to the Shaikh through Sadr-i Jahan Qazi Kamalu'd-Din, to test his asceticism", and Qutbu'd-Din Munawwar refused (p. 196, folio 178). (iv) Firuz "bestowed on him a gift of 100,000 tankas. On being pressed by them to accept the gift, the Shaikh consented only to receive 2,000 tankas which he gave in charity", and later "The Sultan's gift of an expensive robe was refused" (p. 197, folio 179). **The 100,000 / 2,000 tanka figures are the chunk's only cash sums attached to a shrine figure.**
- **The unsolicited gift as a question put to the pir.** Amir Hasan's "impecunious state prompted him to ask Shaikh Nazimu'd-Din [OCR?] [Nizamu'd-Din] if he could accept an unsolicited gift" (p. 191, folio 173). **The Shaikh's answer is not given** — (not stated).
- **Spiritual territory as a bounded jurisdiction between two living Shaikhs — a wilaya boundary named on the ground.** Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din told Sultan Firuz "that his prayers had helped the army to reach Sarsuti, but from there onwards was the spiritual domain of Shaikh Qutbu'd-Din Munawwar and the Sultan should approach him for assistance", and Qutbu'd-Din answered "'As my brother, Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din has entrusted the care of the people to me, I pray God that Delhi may fall into your hands'" (p. 197, folio 179). **The boundary is placed at "Sarsuti, about 300 kilometres from Delhi"** (p. 197, folio 179).
- **Sufis as instruments of, and obstacles to, state policy.** Muhammad bin Tughluq "planned to transfer most of the 'ulama' and sufis of Delhi to Daulatabad, in order both to undermine their influence in Delhi and to populate the Deccan with a Muslim élite", and raised "the slogan of jihad against the Mongols … in order to mobilize the aid of the 'ulama' and the sufis", but "A large section of both groups, however, refused" (p. 193, folio 175). **"The compulsive nature of the migration of the Muslim élite aroused popular feeling against the government"** (p. 193, folio 175).
- **A disciple assigned to a military campaign for spiritual protection, and left there afterwards.** An officer sent "to conquer Chanderi asked the Shaikh to send a disciple to accompany him to battle, to give him spiritual protection. Maulana Yusuf was assigned the task and after the war on the Shaikh's orders he remained in Chanderi" (p. 195, folio 177).
- **Sama' as composed repertoire, and as a disciple's consolation.** "It is believed that Khusraw invented the musical instrument, the sitar, and several melodies for it by a mixture of Persian and Indian tunes. Popular Indian melodies such as Qawi/ [OCR?] [Qawl] are undoubtedly the invention of Amir Khusraw. They were designed to produce novelty in sufi sama' rituals, in which he himself participated" (p. 190, folio 172). Amir Hasan "confided that the performance of other than obligatory prayers was a burden for him, but that he took great comfort in sama'" (p. 191, folio 173).
- **The pir placed inside the object of contemplation, beside God and the Prophet.** Asked "whether a disciple should contemplate God, the Prophet Muhammad and his pir separately, or the three simultaneously … he should think that God was in front of him, the Prophet on his right and his pir on the left" (p. 195, folio 177). **A spatial prescription for devotion to a living master.**
- **The pir as indispensable, stated through a disciple's poem.** Khusraw's praise of the Shaikh in the *Nuh-Sipihr* "tended to emphasize his belief that a pir was indispensable in the pursuit of a sufi path" (p. 188, folio 170).
- **A khalifa's asceticism as withdrawal plus delegated almsgiving.** Maulana Shamsu'd-Din "became so withdrawn from the world that he even refused to see people who called. His servant distributed gifts received by him" (p. 195, folio 177).
- **Slaves, purchase and release, at named prices.** "While in Devagiri, Hasan's servant bought a slave girl for five tankas. When they were to leave with the army for Delhi, the distraught parents begged to re-buy their daughter for twice the amount the servant had originally paid. Amir Hasan himself paid the sum and returned the girl to her family", which the Shaikh approved "because of a precedent set by his teacher, Maulana 'Ala'u'd-Din Usuli", who had freed a slave woman parted from her son "in Katihar" — "Although he realized the 'ulama' disapproved of this policy, it greatly impressed Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din" (p. 184, folio 166).
- **The negative, because the queue is looking for it: pp. 181–197 (folios 163–179) contain no ʿurs, no mela, no death anniversary observance, no sajjada nashin or hereditary custodian, no Auqaf or endowment deed, no revenue figure, no visitor count, no architectural date for any surviving structure and no named shrine building other than the lost dome over Nizamu'd-Din's grave** (p. 181, folio 163). **The chunk's institutions are the jama'at-khana, the langar, the khilafat-nama and the farman, all of them attached to living Shaikhs.**

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

Attributions are the book's own, including its hedges ("It is believed", "It appears", "is recorded as", "involuntarily").

- **A saint reveals angels physically moving the heavens, to silence a philosopher.** Shaikh Shihabu'd-Din Suhrawardi "then proceeded to reveal to the philosopher and the Caliph the supernatural sight of angels moving the heavens" (p. 185, folio 167). **Told by Nizamu'd-Din as an argument against philosophy.**
- **Involuntary prostration before the Shaikh.** Two young scholars who came "Deciding to test the Shaikh's scholarship … called at the jama'at-khana and immediately found themselves involuntarily prostrated on the ground before him" (p. 195, folio 177).
- **Inspired knowledge as the Shaikh's diagnostic gift.** "In mystic language his inspired knowledge ('ilm-i ladunni) is recorded as greatly helping those who called on him" (p. 182, folio 164).
- **The dust of the grave as relic** (p. 181, folio 163) — see under Practices.
- **A verse at the grave read as an unwitting prophecy of the reciter's own death.** Khusraw recited "'The beloved sleeps on her couch with her face covered with her curled lock, Oh Khusraw! Return to your own home for the entire world is now covered by night'", glossed: "The curled lock in sufi symbolism represents the divine mysteries and home is eternity. Khusraw was in fact predicting his own death" (p. 190, folio 172).
- **Grief so total it could not be wept.** "he became so overwhelmed at his loss that he apparently was unable to weep for the Shaikh" (p. 190, folio 172).
- **A child's terror dissolved by a sentence, and a vision of nobles as lambs.** "his father whispered to the child: 'Greatness and power belong to God alone.' His terror immediately abated, and he saw the assembly of great nobles as a collection of mere lambs" (p. 197, folio 179).
- **Prayers credited with moving an army 300 kilometres.** Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din "said to Sultan Firuz that his prayers had helped the army to reach Sarsuti" (p. 197, folio 179), and Qutbu'd-Din's answering prayer, "'I pray God that Delhi may fall into your hands'" (p. 197, folio 179), is followed by Firuz's accession.
- **A handshake that converts a hostile Sultan.** "It appears that the ruler was deeply impressed by the Shaikh's handshake, and mollified somewhat … Admitting he had been misled regarding the Shaikh's character" (p. 197, folio 179).
- **A charitable reputation saves a captive from Chingiz Khan.** Told by the Shaikh: the intercessor pleaded that Khwaja 'Ali's father "had been a constant magnanimous distributor of food to the poor", and to the Khan's question whether the recipients "were those from the sage's own community, the Khwaja answered that he had given to all, regardless of their origin. Chingiz Khan was so impressed that he released the sufi" (pp. 183–184, folios 165–166).
- **Water-carrying rated above prayer, in a majzub's rebuke.** The majzub "chastised him for calling him to prayers, and stated that the only really meritorious act was the filling of water pots for others" (p. 183, folio 165).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Rizvi's central revisionist claim in this chunk: Muhammad bin Tughluq was not anti-sufi, and the standard explanation of his hostility is wrong.** "Modern authorities, such as Mahdi Husain and Nizami, believe that the Sultan's hostility to the mystics was influenced by the teachings of Maulana 'Abdu'l-'Aziz Ardbili, a disciple of the puritanical Hanbali reformer, Ibn Taymiyya (1263-1328). **This theory is without foundation** for the struggle between the sufis and the Sultan started at the beginning of his reign in 1325. The date of Ardbili's arrival at Muhammad bin Tughluq's court is unknown, but he was certainly absent from it during these early years, and probably reached India from Syria sometime after 1330" (p. 194, folio 176). **The refutation is a chronological argument from an admitted unknown date, and Rizvi's own "probably" carries it.**
- **His positive counter-thesis, and the evidence he offers for it.** "All independent sources affirm that the Sultan was a most religious man, who was deeply devoted to the descendants of Shaikh Hamidu'd-Din Nagauri and Baba Farid. He also built a number of tombs at the graves of eminent sufis and was not interested in implementing puritanical reforms of the kind recommended by Ibn Taymiyya. The Sultan was not hostile to the sufi movement per se, as has been represented, but was unprepared to tolerate opposition towards the implementation of his political policies" (p. 194, folio 176). **No independent source is named** — (not stated).
- **A source-criticism of the Chishti tradition itself, unusual in this book.** "Chishti sources and other authorities, impressed with the accounts of both sufis and members of the 'ulama' group, have condemned the Sultan as irreligious and heretical" (p. 194, folio 176), and the instruction to read the khalifas' careers "against such a background, rather than in the light of assumptions made by a number of medieval and modern scholars" (p. 194, folio 176).
- **The philosophers as a political faction, which is Rizvi's own framing of the conflict.** "The philosophers, whom both the 'ulama' and sufis had hitherto tried to suppress, rose to a new dominance in the government councils due to Muhammad bin Tughluq's developing interest in that discipline. Through their newly-found political power the philosophers sought to further alienate the Sultan from the Delhi religious élite" (pp. 193–194, folios 175–176).
- **An inference about the langar flagged as such by its own adverb.** "Almost certainly Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din's /angar [langar] was open to Hindus and Muslims alike. Although the Shaikh's main concern was an amelioration of the conditions of the Muslims, he also extended concern and care for Hindus" (p. 184, folio 166). **Rizvi's conclusion, supported in the chunk only by anecdotes about individual Hindus.**
- **Khusraw's Hindi corpus: a disputed attribution Rizvi partly concedes and partly resists.** "Some collections of Hindi or Hindawi verses are ascribed to Amir Khusraw. Their authenticity is doubted by a number of modern linguists. However, the Hindi words and phrases occurring in his Persian verses tend to indicate that this language came to his mind spontaneously … There may be some interpolations in collections ascribed to him, for example in the Khaliq-Bari, but the sufi subject matter was of his own creation" (pp. 189–190, folios 171–172).
- **An unevidenced attribution of musical invention, asserted at two strengths in two sentences.** "It is believed that Khusraw invented the musical instrument, the sitar" is hedged; "Popular Indian melodies such as Qawi/ [Qawl] are **undoubtedly** the invention of Amir Khusraw" is not (p. 190, folio 172). **No source is given for either** — (not stated). **Directly relevant to qawwali at shrines and should not be quoted without this note.**
- **Khusraw as a reluctant historian whose value is accidental.** "Khusraw was prompted to write historical works in order to comply with requests from his patrons, but in reality he was uninterested in historiography, although his works provided indispensable information to later historians. To him the kaleidoscope of changes in the political world were merely manifestations of the divine will" (p. 190, folio 172), and "His dated documents in the /'jaz-i Khusrawi are few, but they fill very important gaps in our historical knowledge" (p. 189, folio 171).
- **Earthly love as a bridge, which is the doctrinal basis of the poetry the shrines sing.** "Amir Khusraw believed that earthly love, expressed in allegorical and historical stories, was a bridge to spiritual love" (p. 190, folio 172).
- **Rizvi's character judgement on Barani, delivered without a source.** "A bonvivant who lived extravagantly, he was endowed with a naive sense of his own intellectual and religious superiority" (p. 192, folio 174).
- **The Shaikh's own teaching set against philosophy, twice, as Rizvi reads it.** "The conversations of Nizamu'd-Din indicate that he was deeply concerned with the conflict between philosophy and theology. He believed the study of philosophy should be discouraged" (p. 185, folio 167); "Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din's antipathy towards followers of the philosophical discipline is again revealed in this story" (p. 186, folio 168).
- **Conversion by example, not preaching — the chunk's clearest statement of the Shaikh's position on non-Muslims.** "the Shaikh was overcome with sadness, and told his disciple that people remained untouched by preaching as only pious example would result in conversion" (p. 184, folio 166); and "some Hindus had been convinced of the truth of Islam nevertheless they had refused to be converted" (p. 184, folio 166).
- **The nafs/qalb psychology as a social theory of conflict.** "The animal soul was nafs and the human soul was galb [OCR?] [qalb]. Nafs was responsible for all evil, whereas the qalb produced peace, resignation and good-will. If a person was dominated by nafs, and someone else controlled by galb [qalb], the nafs was defeated. However, if both sides were dominated by nafs a confrontation would culminate in evil" (p. 185, folio 167).
- **Renunciation ranked above ritual, and altruism above obligatory prayer.** The kettle-and-meat allegory, in which "meat was renunciation and such things as praying and fasting were supplementary" (p. 183, folio 165); and "altruistic service to others is more meritorious than the performance of obligatory prayers" (p. 183, folio 165).
- **Family life defended against total asceticism.** "Family responsibilities which produced a necessary involvement in worldly affairs were worthy and acceptable to the Shaikh, who fully realized that few could become totally dedicated ascetics … Those who supported their families were not really worldly, although they might appear so" (p. 185, folio 167). **Set beside the Shaikh's own celibacy** (p. 186, folio 168) **and Amir Hasan's** (p. 191, folio 173).
