## Practices, institutions, economy

**The negative first, because it is the finding.** In pp. 366–382 (folios 344–360) there is **no ʿurs, no mela, no langar, no sajjada nashin or named custodian, no Auqaf, no tomb, no shrine building, no architectural date, no visitor number, no shrine dispute and no shared or contested building of any kind.** The chapter's "shared ground" is entirely of persons and repertoire — Brahmans who become Rishis, Muslims who become yogis, Hindu and Muslim Bauls, Vaishnavite lyrics inside sufi samaʿ — and **not one physical site is said to be held, visited or contested by two communities.** What *is* here is revenue and grant policy, one order's turn to landholding, and one anti-pilgrimage poem.

- **A sufi who refuses land, and pays for it.** Of ʿAbduʾl-Quddus at Shahabad: "However, he does not seem to have accepted either financial assistance or land grants. His family then faced a severe economic crisis often starving for days. Butlike [OCR?] [But like] many other outstanding sufis, the Shaikh's meditation was undisturbed by such a situation" (p. 366, folio 344). **The refusal of grants is stated as hedged ("does not seem to have"), and the price of it is stated flatly.**
- **The unit of revenue, defined by the book itself.** "pargana" is footnoted "A grouping of villages for purposes of revenue collection and administration" (p. 366, folio 344, footnote 1). **A definition worth keeping, because the archive's endowment rows turn on it.**
- **The tax fight over holy men's grants — the densest institutional passage in the chunk.** "Apparently Babur had suggested the imposition of ʿushr on the wajah-i maʿash of the ʿulamaʾ and the sufis. While requesting the Emperor to honour, ʿulama,ʾ aimas and the weak, the Shaikh commented, that the imposition of ʿushr upon the wajh-i maʿash of these classes should not be permitted and should be considered a heinous sin; he added it was a particularly unwise act to ask for money from dervishes. The tax should be remitted so that all those people who would have fallen into such a category could live peacefully and pray for the prosperity of the Emperor and the Muslim community" (p. 367, folio 345). **The book glosses its own terms in the footnotes: ʿushr is "One-tenth of the produce"; wajah-i maʿash is "Rent-free grants to holy men and the ʿulama"; aimas is "Holy men"; muhtasibs are "Officers for the enforcement of Islamic law in urban areas"; jamaʿ is "Land revenue"** (p. 367, folio 345, footnotes 3–7). **The bargain the Shaikh offers is explicit: exemption in exchange for prayers for the ruler's prosperity.**
- **Where the enforcement was to sit.** "The muhtasibs should be appointed in towns and bazaars so that the Shariʿa could be enforced" (p. 367, folio 345).
- **The Shaikh's fiscal-communal programme, at length and unsoftened.** Revenue to be realised "according to the traditions of the Khulfa-i Rashidun, the First Four Caliphs"; "Only the pious should be appointed as government officers"; "No kafir should be appointed to any post in the diwan of a Muslim capital or should hold offices such as amirs and ʿamil. They should receive no financial assistance from the government, and should live in a miserable condition. Kafirs should be forced to pay regular revenue and taxes on their agricultural and commercial undertakings, their dress should differ from Muslims, their worship should be in secret and they should not openly indulge in heretical practices. They should not draw salaries from the Baituʾl-mal but confine their activities to their traditional trades and professions. Equal treatment with Muslims was not to be given in the interests of Islam" (p. 368, folio 346). The book glosses "diwan" as "A chancery, concerning civil administration", "amirs" as "Governors", "ʿamil" as "A revenue official", "Baituʾl-mal" as "The treasury of the Muslim state" (p. 368, folio 346, footnotes 1–4). **"their worship should be in secret" is the chunk's one explicit demand about non-Muslim religious practice, and it is the negative image of the archive's shared-ground question.**
- **A jamaʿat-khana destroyed by fire — the only building event in the range, and its material is given.** "He returned to Shahabad again when his house and thatched jamaʿat-khana there were burnt in an accidental fire" (p. 367, folio 345). **"thatched" is the only construction detail for any structure in seventeen pages.** Which place "there" refers to is ambiguous in the sentence as printed — see Doubts.
- **A royal visit to a living saint's hermitage.** "Humayun paid a visit to the Shaikh's hermitage in Gangoh" (p. 368, folio 346), after "Another letter was written by Shaikh ʿAbduʾl-Quddus to Prince Humayun recommending that he accord honourable status to the ʿulamaʾ and holy men".
- **An ascetic order turning into a landholding one — the chunk's key economic sentence.** "A century later many Rishis also began to live in khangahs [OCR?] [khanqahs], accepting land and money from the government and their own devotees. Nevertheless they remained dedicated servants of the people, irrespective of class and religious distinctions" (p. 373, folio 351). **Two income streams are named — the government and the order's own devotees — and the book records the change without dating it beyond "A century later".**
- **What the order did with itself instead of building.** "The Rishis strongly impressed both Abuʾl-Fazl and Emperor Jahangir. Both mention them planting fruit trees for the benefit of the people"; "Rishi authors believed that members of their order had turned Kashmir into a heaven for the people, although they themselves led harshly austere lives" (p. 373, folio 351). **Two imperial sources are invoked and cited as "A ʿin, Il [OCR?] [II], p. 170, HI [OCR?] [III], p. 549; Tuzuk-i Jahangiri, p. 302".**
- **Order discipline: celibacy, diet, and a breath technique named in both traditions at once.** "The contemplative life of the Rishis was founded on the pas-i anfas or pranayama. Generally they remained celibate believing that a family was a great impediment to the pursuit of a saintly life. Shaikh Nuruʾd-Din admitted that although meat eating was permitted by the Shariʿa to him it was cruelty to animals" (p. 373, folio 351). **"pas-i anfas or pranayama" is Rizvi equating a sufi and a yogic term as one practice.**
- **A named, described habit invented for an order.** "Shaikh Zainuʾd-Din invented a distinctive dress for Rishis which consisted of a variegated woollen cloak with a black and white pattern running through it" (p. 373, folio 351). **A dated-to-a-person costume, with its colours — the only vestment description in the chunk.**
- **Muslims taking up yogic livelihoods.** "By the fifteenth century many groups of Muslims also became yogis, though not necessarily Naths or Siddhas. Some became professional beggars and acrobats" (p. 376, folio 354).
- **Where Naths lived.** "The Naths and the Siddhas spread from the Panjab to Bengal lived in forests, wandered in towns and also established permanent monasteries" (p. 376, folio 354). **"permanent monasteries" is the nearest thing to a Nath institution here; none is named or located.**
- **Devotion to the pir ranked above devotion to God — a doctrine the archive's shrine practice rests on, stated by a sufi.** "A disciple worshipping his pir was better than the worshipper of the Lord, argued the Shaikh, for the latter was busy with the contemplation of his own self and therefore neglected God; one who adored his pir, however, worshipped God through the contemplation of His creature" (p. 370, folio 348).
- **Group singing with instruments, as a form of worship.** Chaitanya's "favourite form of worship as a bhakta was in kirtan or samkirtan (group singing) which was accompanied by drums, cymbals or a one-stringed fiddle and during which the words Hari and Krishna were constantly chanted" (p. 379, folio 357).
- **Relic veneration of a founder, recorded as a relapse.** "Like the Alvars, Chaitanya was opposed to class distinctions, but his followers allowed the practice to creep back into the movement. They not only worshipped Vishnu, Krishna and Radha, but also relics associated with their founder" (p. 379, folio 357). **The clearest shrine-forming process in the chunk, and Rizvi reports it without comment.**
- **Vernacular devotional song inside sufi samaʿ, with a compiled reference work and a defence.** "The popularity of Vaishnavite themes used in sufi samaʿ rituals of Hindi speaking regions is a most remarkable development. The sufis regarded them as welcome additions to their devotional poetry to induce ecstasy" (p. 381, folio 359); the *Haqaʾiq-i Hindi*'s three sections cover "Hindi words used in Dhurpad songs" (footnoted "A kind of musical note"), "the words used in Vaishnavite songs in Braj Bhasha, the dialects of the Mathura region", and "the words used in Hindi sufi poetry"; and "The Mir justifies the popularity of the names of kafirs used in sufi samaʿ on the grounds that the Qurʾan itself uses the names of both kafirs and enemies" (p. 381, folio 359). **A named apologetic for singing Hindu divine names in a Muslim ritual.**
- **Technical lexicons as a shared genre.** "Several dictionaries of sufi technical terms were written. The ancient Indian mystics such as the Sahajiyas, Tantrics and the Nath-Yogis also compiled dictionaries of their own mystical and technical terms" (p. 381, folio 359).
- **Pilgrimage rejected by name, in a Baul poem — directly on the archive's subject.** "You have been to Gaya, Benares (Kasi) and Vrndavana,—and have travelled through many rivers and forests and other places of pilgrimage; but say,—have you seen in all these anything of Him of Whom you have heard?" (p. 380, folio 358).
- **Temples and idols rejected by name, in Lalla's verse.** "What are thine idols but lumps of stone? / What but stone the temples that are thine? / Venerable Brahman, who alone / Offerings to these to make Divine?" (p. 372, folio 350). **"Offerings" is the one word in the chunk touching shrine economy on the Hindu side, and it appears inside a refusal.**
- **Idol worship reframed rather than condemned, by a sufi.** "one who had learnt to perceive God saw nothing but Him … he who saw God and not a stone idol was in fact His worshipper, whereas one who never saw God but stone was given to vanity and infidelity", introduced by the quoted verse "Had the idol worshippers been able to know the truth about the idol, they would have not been misled" (p. 371, folio 349).
- **Conversion, reported as a genre.** "The stories of their conversions are like many others concerned with mystic conversions, but all consistently portray Shaikh Nuruʾd-Din as a spiritual beacon to Kashmiri Muslims and Hindus alike" (p. 373, folio 351).
- **Caste and access, in three positions.** Ramanuja "taught that all classes should be given access to the sacred Vedas … permitted idol worship and accepted the Hindu division of society into castes, but he admitted Sudras and outcastes to his order" (p. 377, folio 355); the Alvars were "Rebels against the superior caste claims of the Brahmans" (p. 377, folio 355); Lalla "was suitably married to a member of another Brahman caste" "As was the custom of her caste" (p. 371, folio 349).
- **A manuscript lost to war.** ʿAbduʾl-Quddus's "earliest literary attempt … a Persian poetical translation of the Chandaʾin. The manuscript however was lost during the upheavals in Rudauli caused by the wars between Sultan Bahlul and Husain Shah Sharqi" (p. 370, folio 348).
- **The letters as a corpus, with a number.** The *Maktubat-i Quddusiyya* was collected "by his disciple, Buddhan, the son of Rukn Siddiqi of Jaunpur"; "The work contains 189 letters which deal with almost every significant sufi theme. Also included are several Hindi verses" (p. 370, folio 348). Other works listed: "Nuruʾl-Huda", "The Qurratuʾl-ʿAin … another detailed work on the Wahdat al-Wujud", "the Risala-i Qudsiyya" (p. 370, folio 348).
- **The channel by which yoga reached the sufis, named.** "Bengal was the state in which Hatha-Yogic practices became known to the sufis because of the early translation into Arabic and Persian of the Amrita-Kunda" (p. 373, folio 351); and the counter-fact, "no Bengali work in prose or poetry on sufism written between the thirteenth and fifteenth centuries has yet come to light" (pp. 373–374, folios 351–352).
- **State patronage of a vernacular as the condition for a literature.** "The patronage of the Bengali language by the Husain Shahi Sultans (1494-1538 ab [AD]) was the greatest incentive for the emergence of a body of Bengali literature" (p. 374, folio 352).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Rizvi convicts his own subject of political incoherence — the sharpest evaluative passage in the chunk.** "It is interesting that the Shaikh objected to Hindus holding high administrative posts, at the same time failing to censure the Rajput military classes and showing no concern for Hindus charging high interest rates as long as they were involved in traditional roles. It would not be unfair to suggest that the Shaikh's views were inconsistent and extreme and the result of a sufi theorist indulging in politics in a polarized fashion" (p. 368, folio 346); and "The puritanically severe demands made by the Shaikh to Babur were matched only by those emanating from the most conservative amongst the orthodox" (p. 368, folio 346).
- **A departure from Chishti quietism, dated and explained.** The Shaikh's advice to Sikandar Lodi "was based on the traditional Perso-Islamic political theories defined by Ghazali, but it marks a departure from the traditional Chishti practice of unreserved non-involvement in politics" (p. 366, folio 344), the letter arguing "that an hour spent by rulers in the pursuit of justice was more commendable than sixty hours of prayers by others", that "Communities needed kings just as the body needed the soul", and that Sultans "were distinguished by the title 'Shadow of God on Earth'" (pp. 366–367, folios 344–345).
- **Migration explained by expediency, not piety.** "It would appear, however, that the move was precipitated more by expedient than pious motives" (p. 366, folio 344), set in a context of "Rajput invasions of Rudauli and other predominantly Muslim towns in the Sharqi kingdom" which "were commonplace occurrences" (p. 366, folio 344).
- **Rizvi rejects two named scholars on Lalla — a direct refusal of the influence thesis.** "modern scholars such as R.C. Temple and Muhibbuʾl Hasan have mistakenly sought to prove a sufi influence on Lalla's verses composed while in a state of ecstasy. These, however, so strongly express the teachings of the Kashmiri Shaivites that such a theory seems implausible" (p. 371, folio 349). **The direction of influence is then reversed: "The cross fertilization of sufi beliefs with those expressed by Lalla throughout her verses led to the establishment of the Rishi order of sufis in Kashmir" (p. 371, folio 349), and "It would seem, however, that the Shaikh obtained inspiration from Lalla who by that time was well-known to Kashmiris" (p. 372, folio 350).**
- **Kubrawiyya claims on Nuruʾd-Din rejected on doctrinal grounds.** "Serious differences between the attitude to religion and mysticism of the Kubrawiyya and of Shaikh Nuruʾd-Din would make any relationship unlikely" (p. 372, folio 350), against sources that "anachronistically make the Shaikh a disciple of the Mir himself" (p. 372, folio 350).
- **"Heterodox" refused as a category for sufism anywhere.** "Some Muslim Bengali scholars characterize the mystic themes in Bengali works as heterodox but the term is hardly applicable to the sufi movement in any region. Sufism had had a long tradition of borrowing both ideas and practices from other non-Islamic mystical systems and Bengali sufis did not depart seriously from the path shown by their predecessors"; and "To call Bengali sufism heterodox amounts to the application of a concept on sufism which has no relevance to such a system at all" (pp. 375–376, folios 353–354). **The warrant offered is the respectability of the *Rushd-Nama* and of ʿAbduʾl-Quddus Gangohi (p. 376, folio 354).**
- **Rizvi against Nizami on Chaitanya, at length and in a footnote that turns personal.** "A modern Muslim scholar who appears not to be conversant with the different forms of Bhakti argues that Chaitanya was influenced by Shaikh Nur Qutb-i ʿAlam" (p. 379, folio 357), footnoted to "K.A. Nizami, Tarikh-i Mashaʾikh-i Chisht, Delhi, 1953, p. 202"; the footnote quotes A.K. Majumdar against Nizami ("There was hardly any saint of the bhakti school who had not passed some of his time in a khanqah." … "We would be happy if such a state of affairs existed in that age, but the evidence is nil … Dr. Nizami is also wrong about Caitanya's social position.") and then adds Rizvi's own barb: **"Majumdar's only mistake is in referring to Nizami as 'Dr.'"** (pp. 379–380, folios 357–358).
- **Where the influence did land, in Rizvi's account.** "However, the deep impact made by Chaitanya was felt on a more popular level, for example in the Baul movement" (p. 380, folio 358).
- **Sufi influence asserted from vocabulary alone, in a footnote.** On Namdeva's "Come God, the Qalandar / Wearing the dress of an Abdali": "Abdal, mystical officers of the Divine court; see p. 40. supra. The use of these Sufi terms shows a deep sufi influence on Namdeva" (p. 378, folio 356, footnote 1). **The whole inference rests on two loan-words and Rizvi marks no doubt about it.**
- **Systematic equation of yogic and sufi anatomies, reported without objection.** "According to him the nasut was the abode of ʿIzraʾil and could be identified with the muladhara … The Malakut was identical with the manipura cakra … The Lahut in the heart was identified with the anahata-cakra of the yogis" (pp. 374–375, folios 352–353); and Saiyid Sultan's "Gnana Pradipa gives a detailed analysis of the mystic physiology of Hatha-Yoga in order to make sufi perception of the Pas-i Anfas more meaningful" (p. 375, folio 353).
- **The human body as a compendium of all scriptures, including the Qurʾan.** "As a microcosm, the human body is depicted as possessing all the attributes of the world … It is the abode of the four Yugas, the four Vedas and the four scriptures (the Old and the New Testaments, the Psalms and the Qurʾan)" (p. 374, folio 352).
- **Allegorical reading offered as the standard defence of erotic devotion, twice.** Nabhaji on the *Gita-Govinda*: "the love scenes and rhetorical graces of the poet are not to be understood in the sense that persons of evil minds and dispositions attach to them. Radhika the heroine is heavenly wisdom" (p. 381, folio 359); and the *Haqaʾiq-i Hindi*'s glossary, in which "Krishna: Sometimes Krishna and his other names in Hindawi (Hindi) indicate the Prophet Muhammad and sometimes the (Perfect) Man … Sometimes it represents Iblis. Often it stands for idols, Christians or the sons of fire-worshippers", "Gopi and Gujari (milkmaid): Sometimes these words represent angels", "Uddhava … Sometimes it indicates Gabriel" (p. 382, folio 360).
- **A named limit on allegory.** "If wise men perceive into them a different meaning, the source of difference is the intellect itself. The symbols themselves do not warrant any difference" (p. 382, folio 360).
- **The Vaishnavite philosophical positions as Rizvi sets them out** — Ramanuja's "visisht-advaita, that is, qualified non-dualism, as opposed to the pure monism, advaita (allowing no second), of Shankara" (p. 377, folio 355); Madhava's "dvaita, total dualism" (p. 377, folio 355); Nimbarka's "dvaitadvaita (dualistic non-dualism)" (pp. 377–378, folios 355–356); Chaitanya's "achintya bhedabheda (incomprehensible dualistic monism)", illustrated by "the connection between fire and a simple spark which neither were identical nor different" (p. 379, folio 357); and Shaivite non-dualism in Lalla, "Shakti is an aspect of Shiva who is both He and She, a unity in duality, and a duality in unity" (p. 371, folio 349).
- **A dated genealogy of Bengali literature, from a named scholar.** "M.R. Tarafdar divides these works into the following categories: (a) 'the poems dealing with the snake cult, (b) the versified translation of the Mahabharat, (c) the Vaisnava Padavali, (d) a poem on Yoga philosophy and (e) romantic poems represented by the Vidya-Sundara of Shidhara'" (p. 374, folio 352).

**Archive-mapping doubts, in order of how much a human should care:**

- **The one-to-many fan-out on Guru Nanak is the biggest judgement in this chunk and it rests on eighteen words.** Rizvi names him **once**, in a comparative clause, and gives no site, no date and no doctrine (p. 368, folio 346). Under the 18 September ruling he is the principal figure of **eighteen archive rows**, so eighteen bold ids are carried above from a single sentence. **A consolidator may reasonably want this pruned to the biographical rows rather than replicated into every gurdwara entry; I have not pruned it, because the ruling does not authorise me to choose among rows whose principal figure is the same man.** Note also that **`gurdwara-babay-nanki` is deliberately excluded** — its principal figure is "Bebe Nanaki", who is not named in this chunk — as are `gurdwara-panjvi-chati-patshahi`, `gurdwara-dera-sahib` and the other Arjan/Hargobind/Ram Das/Gobind Singh rows.

- **Name collisions the brief warned about, all considered and all refused.** `shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam` — **this chunk's "Shaikh Ruknuʾd-Din" is the son and successor of ʿAbduʾl-Quddus Gangohi, who "died in AD 1575-76" (p. 368, folio 346) and wrote the *Lataʾif-i Quddusi*; he is not Multan, not Suhrawardi and not a grandson of Bahaʾuʾd-Din Zakariyya, so the row is not taken.** `tomb-of-qutbuddin-aibak` — the chunk's "Nur Qutb-i ʿAlam" is a Bengal sufi of Chaitanya's period (pp. 379–380, folios 357–358), not Sultan Qutb ud-Din Aibak. `shrine-of-jalaluddin-surkh-posh-bukhari-jalaluddin-bukhari` and `shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht` — the chunk's "Shaikh Jalal Thaneswari", d. 1581-82 (p. 368, folio 346), is neither Uch figure and carries no Bukhari nisba. `lal-shahbaz-qalandar`, `abul-faiz-qalander-ali-suharwardi`, `shrine-of-qalandar-baba-auliya` and `shamsabad` — the chunk's "Shaikh Bu ʿAli Qalandar" / "Shaikh Abu ʿAli Qalandar" (pp. 371, 374; folios 349, 352) matches none of these principal figures. `shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar` — "Shaikh Farid" as a name does **not** occur in this chunk at all; only the toponym Ajodhan does, and it is handled above.

**Internal problems in the text itself, kept rather than resolved:**

- **A pronoun makes the fire ambiguous, and with it the only building fact in the chunk.** "amongst the emigrants was the Shaikh who settled at Gangoh in the Saharanpur district of U.P. He returned to Shahabad again when his house and thatched jamaʿat-khana there were burnt in an accidental fire" (p. 367, folio 345). **"there" reads most naturally as Gangoh, in which case a Gangoh fire drove him back to Shahabad; but the sentence can also be read as a Shahabad fire, which would make the return incoherent. The book does not disambiguate, and the thatched jamaʿat-khana therefore cannot be assigned to a place.**
- **Rizvi asserts and then undercuts the same influence claim within two pages.** He calls sufi influence on Lalla "implausible" and reverses the arrow (pp. 371–372, folios 349–350), yet two pages later infers "a deep sufi influence on Namdeva" from two loan-words in a hymn (p. 378, folio 356, footnote 1) **without applying the evidentiary standard he had just used against Temple and Hasan.**
- **Two dates for Namdeva sit four lines apart and are not the same.** "The great Marathi poet, Jnanadeva (1275-96)" is followed by "Born around 1270, Namdeva came from a low caste family" (p. 378, folio 356) — **the parenthetical belongs to Jnanadeva and the "around 1270" to Namdeva; the run-together OCR at "Namdevacame" makes the boundary easy to misread, and a consolidator must not attach 1275-96 to Namdeva.**
- **A forward reference is made and not kept inside this chunk.** "As we shall see, Guru Nanak exhibited more equanimity…" (p. 368, folio 346) and "the Nirguna Bhakti movement, discussed at the end of this chapter" (p. 378, folio 356) both point past p. 382. **Nanak's substantive treatment, and with it any real material for the eighteen gurdwara rows, lies in a later chunk of Chapter Six — that is the single most useful forward pointer this chunk produces.**
- **Ramanuja's dates are given by the book as uncertain and should be quoted that way** — "whose dates are believed to be 1050 and 1137" (p. 377, folio 355) — as is Nuruʾd-Din's birth, "according to some authorities" (p. 372, folio 350), and Shaikh Zahid's book, of which Tarafdar "is not certain of the date of its composition" (p. 374, folio 352).
