# rizvi_history_of_sufism_india_1 — chunk 012 — pp. 198–214

**The brief's section boundary is correct, and the book states it in its own running headers, so there is nothing to correct here.** The recto headers read `The Chishtis 181`, `183`, `185`, `187`, `189` on pp. 199, 201, 203, 205, 207, and the verso headers `180`, `182`, `184`, `186`, `188 A History of Sufism in India` on pp. 198, 200, 202, 204, 206. **p. 208 carries no running header at all: it prints `Chapter Three` and the chapter title `The Suhrawardiyya and the Firdawsiyya Silsilas` (p. 208, folio 190)**, and from p. 209 onward the recto header is `The Suhrawardiyya and the Firdawsiyya Silsilas` with the folio. **So Chapter Two ends on p. 207 and Chapter Three opens on p. 208, exactly as briefed.** All folios above and below are taken from `folio_map.tsv` and none is computed; see Doubts for the one place where the map's `source` column understates its own evidence.

**The chunk is three archive rows deep and extremely wide on practice and institution.** Its first half (pp. 198–207, folios 180–189) is the close of the Chishti chapter — the khalifas of Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya' and the life, assassination attempt, death and tomb of Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din Mahmud Chiragh-i Dihli; its second half (pp. 208–214, folios 190–196) is the opening of the Suhrawardi chapter and is dominated by **Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya of Multan**, who is the principal figure of `shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya` and gets roughly five continuous pages. The rows touched are **`shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya`**, **`shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar`** and — on a single footnote, flagged — **`shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam`**. **The material this stage exists to collect is unusually rich here: tomb construction with dated phases, relic burial, thread-tying at a tomb, langars, sama' around tombs and gardens, royal gifts to khanqahs, a khanqah with granaries, and the Shaikhu'l-Islam office paid in stipends and land.** Four separate name collisions with archive rows are flagged rather than mapped.

## Shrines and figures in the archive

### **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya**
The book calls him **"Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya"** throughout, and once **"Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din"** alone. This is the chunk's densest row by a wide margin.

- **He is named as one of the two real founders of the order in India** — "The real founders of the Suhrawardiyya order in India, however, were Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya and Shaikh Jalalu'd-Din Tabrizi" (p. 208, folio 190). On the second name see Doubts.
- **Ancestry, birth and birthplace, as printed.** "The ancestors of the parents of Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya seem to have come to Sind with the army of Muhammad bin Qasim"; "Baha'u'd-Din was born at Kot Karor, near Multan, about 578/1182-83"; "His father died when he was twelve years old" (p. 208, folio 190). **The ancestry claim is sourced to Ibn Battuta and is explicitly doubted by Rizvi in the same footnote** — see the `shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam` bullet below.
- **Education and travels before Multan.** "He memorized the Qur'an and then went to Khurasan for further studies. He remained there for seven years, and left for Bukhara, where his piety earned him the title 'Angel.' After performing a hajj to Mecca, he went to Medina where for five years he studied Hadis under Maulana Kamalu'd-Din Muhammad, the greatest scholar of that discipline in his days. From there he went to Jerusalem and later to Baghdad, where Shaikh Shihabu'd-Din Suhrawardi initiated him into his order and made him his khalifa" (p. 208, folio 190).
- **His initiation is given a number, and a rebuke to the other disciples.** "The training period of Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din lasted for only seventeen days"; the other disciples "were annoyed at an Indian being elevated to a high status in such a short time", and were told "that when they had first come to him they had been like green wood which would not catch fire, whereas Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din had been like dry wood, which had begun to burn with a single breath" (p. 208, folio 190).
- **His arrival at Multan and the rose-in-the-milk exchange — the founding anecdote of the Multan centre.** "Leaving Baghdad, Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din reached Multan, via Nishapur. His arrival was a source of consternation to the town's holymen and religious dignitaries and they requested him to settle elsewhere"; they sent "a cup full of milk, pointing out that the town was as full of scholars as the cup of milk and had no room for others", and "placing a rose on the milk, returned the cup", implying "that he would occupy the same place among the scholars and holymen of Multan as the rose in the milk" (pp. 208–209, folios 190–191).
- **He did not choose Multan himself.** In the quarrel with the jwalgis he said "he had not personally chosen to make Multan the Suhrawardi centre, but had been sent there by Shaikh Shihabu'd-Din" (p. 211, folio 193). **This is the book's own statement of why the shrine is where it is.**
- **His conflict with the ʿulamaʾ of Multan, and the named opponent.** "soon a conflict arose between him and Maulana Qutbu'd-Din Kashani. Nasiru'd-Din Qubacha, the ruler of Multan, held the Maulana in great respect and built a madrasa where he lectured and performed his daily prayers"; the Maulana "tried to dissuade Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya from going to the madrasa for morning prayers on the pretext that it was too far for him to travel from his khangah [OCR?] [khanqah]"; "The clash finally came over a legal point relating to namaz (obligatory prayers). The Shaikh defended his action on the basis of his inner light (nur-i batin). The Maulana rejected the Shaikh's defence arguing that an inner light which was incompatible with Shari'a was in fact darkness"; "the Shaikh left the debate, vowing never to return to the madrasa" (p. 209, folio 191).
- **His khanqah: its economy, its architecture and its door policy — the single most citable institutional passage on this row.** "It appears that merchants from Iraq and Khurasan were attracted to him in large numbers. The Shaikh erected an extensive khangah [OCR?] [khanqah] containing granaries. However it was not a meeting place for the common people; only eminent religious peopleand [OCR?] [religious people and] perhaps state dignitaries and wealthy merchants were admitted. The Shaikh discussed with them topical theological and spiritual problems and in his own estimation they all benefited from his company" (p. 209, folio 191). **Granaries, a merchant clientele and an excluded laity, all in one sentence.**
- **He ate only in his own khanqah.** "It was the Shaikh's custom to refrain from taking nourishmentexcept [OCR?] [nourishment except] in his khangah [khanqah]" (p. 210, folio 192) — stated in the account of Qubacha trying to trap him at court.
- **He invited an invasion of his own city, in writing.** "The Shaikh openly sided with Sultan Shamsu'd-Din Iltutmish when he wished to add Multan and Sind to the Delhi Sultanate. The invasion of Chingiz had already weakened Qubacha and the Shaikh did not hesitate to write to Sultan Shamsu'd-Din inviting him to conquer Multan. The Qazi of Multan also joined the Shaikh in extending such an invitation to the Sultan. Both letters fell into the hands of Qubacha. He immediately had the Qazi executed and summoned the Shaikh to his palace" (p. 209, folio 191). At the audience "The Shaikh went fearlessly and, as usual, sat at Qubacha's right"; he "affirmed it had been written by him", said "everything he had written was true and had been divinely prompted", and added "that Qubacha could take any action in his power, but in reality, of course, he had no real independent power"; "When the Shaikh had eaten, Qubacha's anger subsided" (pp. 209–210, folios 191–192).
- **He was made Shaikhuʾl-Islam, and the office is described as a paid one.** "After the annexation of Multan and Sind by Iltutmish in 1228, relations between the Sultan of Delhi and the Shaikh became more intimate. Iltutmish invited him to preside over the mahzar organized to judge the allegations against Shaikh Jalalu'd-Din Tabrizi by the Shaikhu'l-Islam, Najmu'd-Din Sughra. After the latter's dismissal, the Sultan made Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya, Shaikhu'l-Islam" (p. 210, folio 192). **"To Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din, it meant additional finance to his khangah [khanqah]"** (p. 210, folio 192). See "Practices, institutions, economy" for the description of the office itself.
- **His political restraint, as Rizvi qualifies it.** "he is not known to have been closely involved in political matters beyond recommending his favourites to the Sultan" (p. 210, folio 192).
- **He negotiated with a Mongol besieger, with a date, a name and an intermediary.** "The repeated Mongol invasions of Multan made the life of townfolk miserable, but the presence of Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya was to them a great blessing. In Zu'l-hijja 644/April-May 1247, the Mongol, Suli Nuyin, besieged the Multan fort, but the Shaikh succeeded in negotiating peace through Malik Shamsu'd-Din, a Muslim dignitary in the Mongol army" (p. 210, folio 192).
- **He was rich, and the book says so flatly and contrasts him with the Chishtis.** "With the Suhrawardi order there are few stories revealing that its members lived in extreme poverty, unlike members of the Chishti si/sila [OCR?] [silsila]. It would appear that Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya, even before he was appointed Shaikhu'l-Islam, was very rich. Large sums of money were paid to his children's teachers" (p. 210, folio 192).
- **The grain-store episode — a saint acting as a public granary for the state.** "On one occasion, the Governor of Multan needed grain and was given a store-house full of grain by the Shaikh. A pot of silver coins, which had been found amongst it, was returned to the Shaikh by the Governor, who said he had asked for grain, not money. The Shaikh replied he had merely wished to give the governor money as well" (p. 210, folio 192).
- **He turned wandering ascetics away at the door, and the book gives the resulting riot.** "Jwalgis [OCR?] [Jwalqis?] and qalandars from Khurasan and Central Asia would first visit Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din's khangah [khanqah] at Multan en route to Delhi. In keeping with the Shaikh's custom, they were not welcomed. Once a group of jwalgis [OCR?] called on the Shaikh and were not given gifts. Emerging from the khangah [khanqah] they became very noisy and started throwing bricks at the building. The Shaikh appeared and argued with them … The Jwalgis were speechless and departed peacefully" (p. 211, folio 193). On "Jwalgis" see Doubts.
- **His relations with the Chishti shaikhs, and the territorial division between them.** "Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din's relations with the Chishtis, Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din Bakhtiyar Kakiand [OCR?] [Kaki and] Baba Farid, weremost [OCR?] [were most] amicable. Amongst themselves they had divided areas of respective spiritual influence and this helped to counter any misunderstandings" (p. 211, folio 193). The boundary anecdote is under `shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar` below.
- **His teaching, in the book's summary.** He "strongly discouraged sufis from seeking guidance from a number of different pirs, urging them to lay their heads on one rather than a number of thresholds"; "He laid great stress on performing namaz and admitted that all his achievements were the result of it. According to him, omission of namaz amounted to death. He assigned a secondary place to supererogatory prayers and zikr and sufi discipline"; the ablution anecdote, in which the disciple who finished washing rather than rushing to greet his pir "was praised by Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din as the most outstanding present, for he had chosen to complete his ablutions first and had therefore shown a greater respect for religious duties"; "Unlike the Chishtis, Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din did not observe incessant fasting and ate normally. Occasionally he indulged in sama'"; and on meditation, "the sine qua non of meditation and contemplation was the expulsion of everything from the heart except that connected with God" (p. 211, folio 193).
- **His death date, as printed.** "Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din died on 7 Safar, 661/21 December, 1262" (p. 212, folio 194), footnoted "AA, p. 28". **This does not agree with the archive index — see Doubts.**
- **His standing after half a century at Multan, and why it grew.** "For about half a century after settling in Multan he had been the most celebrated sufi in that region. His significance increased due to Mongol raids in the area which prompted local governors and officials to continually seek his blessings and prayers. The fame of his piety in Khurasan and Transoxiana facilitated successful negotiations with the Mongol invaders" (p. 212, folio 194).
- **Negative, and an important one for the row: the chunk gives no tomb, no shrine building, no burial place, no ʿurs, no successor and no custodian for him.** Everything above is about the living Shaikh and his khanqah. The narrative stops at his death sentence and moves to the next khalifa (p. 212, folio 194) — all shrine-fabric material is (not stated) in pp. 198–214.

### **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**
The book calls him **"Baba Farid"** and, once, **"the Baba"**. No personal name, no title "Ganj-i Shakar", no dates and no tomb are given for him in this chunk — all (not stated).

- **He is named as one of the two founders of the silsila whose durability Rizvi is defending.** "The view that Sultan Muhammad bin Tughluq broke the central Chishti organization is incompatible with the spirit of the si/sila [OCR?] [silsila] which had been set up by Khwaja Mu'inu'd-Din and Baba Farid" (p. 207, folio 189). **A bare name inside an argument; see Doubts.**
- **He is one party to the Chishti–Suhrawardi concordat at Multan.** Named with "Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki" as the Chishtis whose relations with Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya "weremost [were most] amicable", the three having "divided areas of respective spiritual influence" (p. 211, folio 193).
- **The water-tank boundary anecdote — the chunk's one statement of the geographical limit of his wilaya, and it is told against Ajodhan.** "Once a musician called 'Abdu'llah was intending to go to Multan from Ajodhan. He asked Baba Farid to pray for his safe journey. The Baba, however, replied that the limit of his spiritual influence was at a certain water tank and that beyond it began the area of Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din whose prayers he should attain. The musician acted on the Baba's advice and safely completed his journey" (p. 211, folio 193). **The book does not say where the tank is, and does not say Baba Farid was at Ajodhan — only that the musician set out from there** — (not stated).
- **Tombs at Ajodhan are attested as a place of pilgrimage for a Chishti of the next generation, but their occupants are not named.** Of Maulana Fakhru'd-Din Zarradi after Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din's death: "much of his time was spent travelling to the tombs of his spiritual ancestors in Ajmer and Ajodhan" (p. 200, folio 182). **The book names no-one buried at Ajodhan here**; this bullet is placed under the row because the location is the row's own and a tomb, not a bare toponym, is attached — see Doubts, where it is kept reversible.

### **shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam**
- **One footnote mention, as an informant, and the book gives him no shrine, no title and no dates.** "Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din, a grandson of Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya, himself made this statement about his ancestry to Ibn Battuta, however, it is not necessarily authentic" (p. 208, folio 190, footnote 1). The statement in question is that the ancestors of Baha'u'd-Din's parents "came to Sind with the army of Muhammad bin Qasim" (p. 208, folio 190). **The book prints only "Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din" plus the relationship; it never writes "Rukn-i ʿAlam", never gives a death date and never mentions Multan in connection with him** — all (not stated). **Mapped on the strength of the name plus "a grandson of Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya" alone; this is the weakest mapping in the chunk and is flagged prominently under Doubts.**

### Explicit negative for the rest of the archive

**No other archive row is touched anywhere in pp. 198–214 (folios 180–196).** In particular: **Hinglaj is not mentioned**, so the empty-id defect on `Shaktipeeth Shri Hinglaj Mata Mandir` does not arise in this range and no id has been invented; **no Hindu deity, temple, gurdwara, samadhi or Nanakpanthi/Udasi darbar appears anywhere**, so the unruled deity question does not arise either — the only Hindu presence in the chunk is the hostile abstraction "Hindus", "Brahmans", "mushriks" and "idolaters" in Saiyid Nuru'd-Din's four principles (p. 212, folio 194), with no named deity, site or person; **no Lahore, Uch, Sehwan, Bhit, Pakpattan by that name, Peshawar, Sialkot, Karachi, Rawalpindi, Islamabad, Kasur, Jhang, Sargodha or Balochistan location appears**; and the many Delhi shaikhs who fill pp. 198–207 (Nizamu'd-Din Auliya', Nasiru'd-Din Mahmud Chiragh-i Dihli, Qutbu'd-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki, Fakhru'd-Din Zarradi, Burhanu'd-Din Gharib, Akhi Siraju'd-Din 'Usman, Gisu Daraz) are **none of them the principal figure of any archive row** — the archive holds no Delhi, Ajmer, Daulatabad, Burhanpur or Lakhnauti row. **This is a real finding about the book's geography, not a gap in the reading.**

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

Dates are exactly as printed, AH/CE pairs included.

- **Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya'** (also "the great Shaikh", "the Sultanu'l-Mashai'kh" / "the Sultanu'l-Masha'ikh") — the pir of almost everyone in the Chishti half. He sent Maulana Husamu'd-Din "his prayer carpet, indicating that he should be visited immediately", and advised "that it was worthwhile to make two pilgrimages, one for the hajj and the other to visit the tomb of the Prophet, rather than combining the two purposes" (p. 198, folio 180); he set out "six conditions which produced a concentration on God" (p. 199, folio 181); he expelled Burhanu'd-Din Gharib from the jama'at-khana and later restored him (p. 201, folio 183); he was "reluctant to make [Akhi Siraju'd-Din 'Usman] his khalifa because of his poor literary education" (p. 202, folio 184); he ordered Nasiru'd-Din to "remain in Delhi among the people, and suffer whatever indignities and hardships they might inflict on him" (p. 203, folio 185); his relics passed to Nasiru'd-Din (p. 205, folio 187). **No death date, no tomb and no place of burial for him anywhere in this chunk** — (not stated).
- **Maulana Husamu'd-Din Multani** (printed also "Maulana Husamud-Din Multani" and "Maulana Hasamu'd-Din") — "lived in a thatched hut without servants. He was married and had a family. Considering himself merely a humble mulla, he felt he was unworthy to use the title of Shaikh"; studied "Abu Talib Makki in great detail"; made a pilgrimage to Mecca and "stayed in the Kilukhari mosque"; went on to Medina "the day after the conversation" (p. 198, folio 180); "In 1327, along with other 'ulama' and sufis, Maulana Husamu'd-Din was selected by Sultan Muhammad bin Tughluq for transference to Daulatabad, and from there he left for Gujarat where he died" (p. 199, folio 181).
- **Maulana Fakhru'd-Din Zarradi** — "a native of Samana", migrated to Delhi, student of "Maulana Fakhru'd-Din of Hansi who taught him the Hidaya, a work on Sunni jurisprudence"; initially "spoke disparagingly of the Chishti sage"; converted after a disputation, and "Although engaged to be married, Maulana Fakhru'd-Din Zarradi shaved his head, adopted celibacy and became a follower of the Shaikh" (p. 199, folio 181); "rented a house in front of the jama'at-khana"; "Maulana Zarradi's fame as a scholar spread to Baghdad"; after his pir's death "he retired to the banks of the Jamna, near a place where Firuz Shah was to build a palace", then the "Hauz-i Khass side of the river" (pp. 199–200, folios 181–182); his confrontation with Muhammad bin Tughluq (p. 200, folio 182); moved to Daulatabad "about 1327", consulted "the Sadr-i Jahan Qazi Kamalu'd-Din", went to Mecca "after visiting Pethwan for his nephew's wedding", studied Hadis at Baghdad "becoming the acknowledged authority in that field", and "On the return journey to Daulatabad his ship, overloaded with pilgrims, sank and Maulana Fakhru'd-Din was drowned" (p. 201, folio 183).
- **Shaikh Qutbu'd-Din Dabir** — disciple of Nizamu'd-Din and student of Zarradi; carried his teacher's shoes "under his arms and stood behind him in the manner of a servant", took the Sultan's gifts himself knowing Zarradi would refuse, and told the Sultan "Maulana Zarradi was his teacher and his preceptor's Khalifa, therefore it was in keeping for him to carry the Maulana's shoes on his head"; "The Sultan ordered him to give up such blasphemous beliefs or he would be killed, but Shaikh Qutbu'd-Din did not falter" (p. 200, folio 182); harassed in debate by "Ikhtisan Dabir and men like him" (pp. 200–201, folios 182–183).
- **Maulana Shamsu'd-Din Yahya** — summoned with Zarradi and Nasiru'd-Din by Muhammad bin Tughluq; accepted "a woollen garment and a purse of tankas" (p. 200, folio 182); also named at p. 203 (folio 185).
- **Maulana 'Ala'u'd-Din Nili** — khalifa of Nizamu'd-Din, "came from Awadh. An eloquent speaker and an impressive reciter of the Qur'an, he was allowed to enrol disciples, but found the associated responsibilities too onerous. Towards the end of his life, most of his time was spent reading the Fawai'du'l-Fu'ad [OCR?] [Fawa'idu'l-Fu'ad] which he himself had transcribed" (p. 201, folio 183).
- **Maulana Burhanu'd-Din Gharib** — khalifa of Nizamu'd-Din; "invented a peculiar style of dancing which was performed during the ritual of sama'. This came to be known as the Burhani"; "When aged over ninety, Burhanu'd-Din acquired a habit of sitting on a folded blanket"; denounced to his pir by "'Ali Zambili and Malik Nusrat, favourites of Sultan 'Ala'u'd-Din Khalji and also disciples of Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din" for "sitting on cushions like a great Shaikh"; expelled, then restored by the intercession of other disciples; "also forced to leave Delhi for Daulatabad. There, according to 'Isami, he became extremely well-known" (p. 201, folio 183). **On the name collision with an archive row, see Doubts.**
- **Shaikh Zainu'd-Din**, "a khalifa of Burhanu'd-Din's" — at whose suggestion "Sultan Nasir Khan Faruqi (1399-1437), the ruler of the Faruqi dynasty of Khandesh (1382-1601) built a town which he called Burhanpur"; the Sultan "made Burhanpur his capital and also built another town, Zainabad, named after Shaikh Zainu'd-Din" (pp. 201–202, folios 183–184). **Two towns founded and named for a shaikh and his khalifa — the chunk's largest single act of saint-driven urban patronage.**
- **Shaikh Akhi Siraju'd-Din 'Usman** — the khalifa "who laid a firm foundation of the Chishti order in Bengal"; "Lakhnauti was his home town but he first migrated to Awadh, then to Delhi"; arrived beardless and was educated in the jama'at-khana, "Some sheets of paper and a few elementary books were the only possessions of the young Akhi"; his education repaired by Zarradi, "who wrote him short treatises on Arabic grammar"; "In 1327, Shaikh Akhi left Delhi for Lakhnauti, taking with him some books from the great Shaikh's library. The rulers and Muslim élite of Bengal became his disciples" (p. 202, folio 184); on his relic burial see "Practices, institutions, economy".
- **Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din Mahmud, "the Chiragh or Lamp of Delhi"** — the chunk's second-longest life. "His home town was Awadh and he was born in about 675/1276—77. Shaikh Yahya, his father, was a wool merchant and had lived in great affluence. When his father died he was nine"; "By the time he was twenty-five, Nasiru'd-Din Mahmud had abandoned the world"; "Leaves growing wild in Awadh, known as sanbhalu, served to break his fast and to also kill his sexual desires"; he walked daily "to the local mango grove which also held some tombs", where "A small group of Muslim weavers worked in the grove and with Nasiru'd-Din they would gather in congregation under the trees to pray while he acted as their Imam" (p. 202, folio 184); migrated to Delhi "at the age of forty-three", "He had by then completed eighteen years of strenuous mystical exercises" (pp. 202–203, folios 184–185); requests relayed "through Amir Khusraw"; "his fellow dervishes named him Ganj, the Treasury, implying that he was a source of spiritual bounty" (p. 203, folio 185); the assassination attempt (pp. 204–205, folios 186–187); "his death on 18 Ramazan 757/14 September 1356" (p. 205, folio 187); his tomb (p. 205, folio 187, under Practices). **Not an archive row, but the fullest shrine-formation narrative in the chunk.**
- **Shaikh Zainu'd-Din 'Ali** — nephew of Nasiru'd-Din, "who generally attended him" and was absent at the moment of the stabbing (p. 204, folio 186); the Shaikh's dying message was left "Through Zainu'd-Din" (p. 205, folio 187).
- **Saiyed Muhammad Gisu Daraz** — "who will be mentioned again in Chapter Four, washed his master's body. Then, taking some cords from the Shaikh's matted bed, his disciple wound them around his head, declaring that for himself such cords made the best khirqa" (p. 205, folio 187).
- **Turab, "a galandar [OCR?] [qalandar]"** — the assailant of 1353 (p. 204, folio 186); paid twenty silver tankas by his victim and allowed to leave Delhi (p. 205, folio 187).
- **Shaikh Jalalu'd-Din Tabrizi** — named twice: as co-founder of the Suhrawardiyya in India (p. 208, folio 190) and as the subject of "the allegations … by the Shaikhu'l-Islam, Najmu'd-Din Sughra", judged at a mahzar presided over by Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya (p. 210, folio 192). **No dates, no shrine, no place of death** — (not stated). See Doubts.
- **Saiyid Nuru'd-Din Mubarak Ghaznawi** — "Another important khalifa of Shaikh Sihabu'd-Din [OCR?] [Shihabu'd-Din] Suhrawardi"; blessed in infancy by "an eminent saint, Khwaja Muhammad Ajal Shirazi", and "according to tradition it was to that visit that Nuru'd-Din owed his later prominence as a sufi"; "No other details are known of his earlier life"; "Iltutmish appointed Shaikh Nuru'd-Din Mubarak, Shaikhu'l-Islam, and he was called by the people of the city, Mir-i Dihli (Lord of Delhi)"; his four principles for the protection of Islam, reported by "Ziya'u'd-Din Barani" (pp. 212–213, folios 194–195); "Saiyid Nuru'd-Din died in 632/1234-35 and was buried near Shamsi Hauz" (p. 213, folio 195).
- **Maulana Majdu'd-Din Hajji** — "A disciple of Shaikh Shihabu'd-Din Suhrawardi who did not achieve a great deal of fame"; "He is said to have performed the hajj twelve times"; "In the reign of Sultan Shamsu'd-Din, Maulana Majdu'd-Din reached Delhi and at the former's insistence, accepted the position of sadr which he performed efficiently. After two years, however, he resigned, devoting the rest of his life to spiritual exercises" (p. 213, folio 195).
- **Shaikh Ziya'u'd-Din Rumi** — "Another little known khalifa of Shaikh Shihabu'd-Din Suhrawardi … He seems to have lived to an old age as he had initiated Sultan Qutbu'd-Din Mubarak Shah Khalji as both disciple and khalifa. The Sultan's aim to make Ziya'u'd-Din a rival of Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya' was unsuccessful, due to the former's increased senility. Ziya'u'd-Din died soon afterwards and was buried on the road to the tomb of Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki in Delhi" (pp. 213–214, folios 195–196).
- **Shah Turkman Sahib** — "another of Shaikh Shihabu'd-Din Suhrawardi's disciples and like others before him, had migrated to India from Khurasan. No authentic account, however, of Shah Turkman was available to Shaikh 'Abdu'l-Haqq, who mentions only his name and that his tomb was located on the road to Firuzabad" (p. 214, folio 196). **A tomb with a road and nothing else** — (not stated).
- **Qazi Hamidu'd-Din Nagauri** — "Probably the most learned khalifa of Shaikh Shihabu'd-Din Suhrawardi … He is not to be confused, however, with the Chishti Hamidu'd-Din Suwali"; "Hamidu'd-Din Nagauri's first name was Muhammad, his father was 'Ata'u'llah Mahmud. The family migrated from Bukhara to Delhi sometime before 1200, where 'Ata'u'llah died. Shaikh Hamid was appointed the Qazi of Nagaur and served in that position for three years"; he attended at Nagaur the lecture of "the Bada'un-born Hadis scholar, Maulana Raziu'd-Din Hasan Saghani, who compiled the Mashariqu'l-Anwar", on "a book of Hadis, entitled Misbah-al Duja"; he "left for Baghdad where he became Shaikh Shihabu'd-Din Suhrawardi's disciple. There he met Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki"; then Medina "for just over a year", Mecca "for three years", and Delhi, where he and Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din "became close friends" (p. 214, folio 196); his campaign for sama' is under Practices. **No death date and no tomb** — (not stated).
- **Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki** — named as a Chishti party to the Multan concordat (p. 211, folio 193), as Hamidu'd-Din Nagauri's friend from Baghdad onwards (p. 214, folio 196), and as the occupant of a Delhi tomb on whose access road Ziya'u'd-Din Rumi was buried (p. 214, folio 196). **No archive row.**
- **Khwaja Mu'inu'd-Din** — named once, as co-founder of the silsila with Baba Farid (p. 207, folio 189). **No dates, no Ajmer in that sentence, no tomb** — (not stated); Ajmer appears separately as a place of "tombs of his spiritual ancestors" (p. 200, folio 182), with no occupant named. **The archive holds no Ajmer row.**
- **Rulers and officials named** — "Sultan 'Ala'u'd-Din Khalji", whose chamberlain "Muhammad Katib, a hajib" was a disciple (p. 198, folio 180) and whose tomb was a place of thread-tying (p. 207, folio 189); "Sultan Muhammad bin Tughluq", who died "at Sonda near Thatta in March 1351" (p. 204, folio 186); "Firuz"/"Sultan Firuz", "their forty-six year old favourite" made Sultan by "leaders of the 'ulama', sufis and prominent former officers" (p. 204, folio 186); "Sultan Shamsu'd-Din Iltutmish" (pp. 209, 210, 213, 214; folios 191, 192, 195, 196); "Nasiru'd-Din Qubacha, the ruler of Multan" (p. 209, folio 191); "Sultan Qutbu'd-Din Mubarak Shah Khalji" (p. 213, folio 195); "the Mughal Emperor Muhammad Shah (1719-48)" (p. 205, folio 187); "Sultan Nasir Khan Faruqi (1399-1437)" (p. 201, folio 183); "Taghi", whose rebellion in Gujarat ran "Between 1348 and 1350" and who "managed to escape to Thatta" (p. 204, folio 186); "Suli Nuyin" and "Malik Shamsu'd-Din" (p. 210, folio 192); "Chingiz"/"Chingiz Khan" (pp. 200, 209; folios 182, 191); "Qazi Sa'd and Qazi 'Imad" (p. 214, folio 196); "Balban", cited by Barani (p. 213, folio 195).

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **A tomb with a dated building history and named royal and imperial patrons — the single richest architectural passage in the chunk.** "The tomb of Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din Mahmud became a beacon for succeeding generations and the area came to be known as the Chiragh-i Dihli. A tomb was built by Sultan Firuz on the grave but the existing enclosures and mosque were erected by the Mughal Emperor Muhammad Shah (1719-48) between 1142 and 1143/1729 and 1730. Other additions were made by later Mughal princes. The whole area is associated with the memories and traditions of a large number of sufis and saints, many of whom lie buried there" (p. 205, folio 187). **A grave, a sultanate tomb over it, a Mughal enclosure-and-mosque phase with an AH/CE date pair, later princely additions, a saint-named quarter, and a saturated burial ground.**
- **Relics buried with the saint's body, item by item — a burial inventory.** "Although Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din had a large number of disciples, he considered none worthy of receiving the relics bequeathed to him from Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya'. In accordance with his will, these were buried with his earthly remains. The khirga [OCR?] [khirqa] was placed on his rib bones, the staff was laid beside his body, his rosary was wound around his fore-finger, the wooden bowl was placed under his head and the wooden sandals on his breast" (p. 205, folio 187). **Five named relics with their exact placement on the corpse, and an explicit refusal of succession.**
- **A second relic burial, and a saint asking to be buried beside cloth rather than a person.** Of Akhi Siraju'd-Din 'Usman: "Before he died he buried some of Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din's clothes which he had carried from Delhi, and requested that he be buried next to the clothing" (p. 202, folio 184).
- **The body washed by a named disciple, who improvised a khirqa from the deathbed.** Gisu Daraz "washed his master's body. Then, taking some cords from the Shaikh's matted bed, his disciple wound them around his head, declaring that for himself such cords made the best khirqa" (p. 205, folio 187).
- **Thread-tying at a royal tomb for the granting of wishes — the chunk's clearest description of lay shrine practice, and it is at a sultan's tomb, not a saint's.** "They would visit the tomb of Sultan 'Ala'u'd-Din Khalji, and tie threads and pieces of cloth around it so that their prayers and wishes might be granted" (p. 207, folio 189).
- **Langars and public entertainment in the same sentence, with a cost judgement.** "There were a large number of /angars [OCR?] [langars]; the people organized many public entertainments at little expense" (p. 207, folio 189). **The only langar reference in the chunk, and it is plural, urban and undated beyond "the reign of Sultan 'Ala'u'd-Din".**
- **Samaʿ at its peak, performed around tombs and gardens.** "Rituals involving religious music and dancing were often held around the tombs and gardens of the Shaikhs, and the use of sama' was at its peak" (p. 207, folio 189). **Tomb-side music as a described norm, not a controversy.**
- **Khanqahs as a place of refuge in political crisis, with constant access.** "The people of Delhi were reassured during political upheavals by the presence of the sufi orders and their khanqahs to which they had constant access" (p. 207, folio 189).
- **Royal gifts to khanqahs, and the book's verdict on what money does to them.** "Sultan Firuz did not forget the sufis and showered gifts on their khangahs [OCR?] [khanqahs] which resulted in the usual strains associated with material prosperity. The jama'at-khana of the Sultanu'l-Masha'ikh remained firmly entrenched in its traditions of poverty and austerity" (p. 204, folio 186); and "Large gifts from Sultan Firuz to the Chishti order restored the glory of many khangahs [khanqahs]" (p. 207, folio 189). **The two statements are hard to hold together — see Doubts.**
- **A saint who would not be honoured by a king.** "When Sultan Firuz called to see him he was kept waiting for some time, and no special treatment was given" (p. 204, folio 186).
- **The Shaikhuʾl-Islam office described as an honour paid in stipends and land — the chunk's clearest institutional-economy passage.** "Shaikhu'l-Islam was not a permanent position like Sadru's Sudur (chief controller of religious affairs, particularly charities) or Qazi ul-Quzat. The Sultans of Delhi conferred the title on religious dignitaries as an honour and recipients obtained both stipends and land. Incumbents were not obliged to be in constant attendance at court and offered only occasional advice to their rulers. Some Shaikhu'l-Islams, like Najmu'd-Din Sughra, however, took a very active part in politics and the administration. Some sufi authorities themselves gave the title to outstanding sufis, thus indicating their supreme spiritual status" (p. 210, folio 192). **Note the gloss on *Sadruʾs-Sudur* as "chief controller of religious affairs, particularly charities" — the nearest thing in the chunk to a state charities office.**
- **A khanqah containing granaries, restricted to the elite, funded by merchants** (p. 209, folio 191) — quoted in full under `shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya`.
- **Grain and silver moving from a khanqah to a provincial governor** (p. 210, folio 192) — quoted under the same row.
- **Loans and futuh: the two permitted pretexts, given as a ruling.** On "the efficacy of accepting loans while awaiting futuhs, the Shaikh answered that the only two pretexts on which loans could be accepted were the maintenance of one's family and the entertainment of travellers. He added, however, the loan seeking and renayment [OCR?] [repayment] disturbed the spiritual routine of a dervish and that a true ascetic should be totally unconcerned with financial matters" (p. 198, folio 180).
- **Two condemned modes of begging.** "Begging should not be pursued by a dervish and Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya' listed two ways of seeking alms, both he believed to be unworthy of an ascetic, particularly the second. The first was begging from door to door, the second involved a withdrawal from the world but a continued mental occupation with the attainment of gifts" (p. 198, folio 180).
- **Two named types of false sufi, one of them defined by soliciting the state.** "Firstly there was a mugallid [OCR?] [muqallid], or sufi without a pir, secondly, was the jarrat, a person who donned an excellent khirga [khirqa] and the cap of a sufi and who visiting the sultans and his officials, begged for money. Such a person was a jarrat because he sold religion. Common people were forced to go to a bazaar and sell their goods but a spiritualist should not ask for anything from others. He should bolt his door and pray for his spiritual and material needs which come from God" (p. 206, folio 188).
- **Expulsion from the jama'at-khana as a sanction with material teeth.** Nizamu'd-Din "expelled him from the jama'at-khana which meant that none of the Shaikh's disciples were able to offer him lodging. The Maulana's distressed condition aroused the compassion of other disciples and by their intercession he was restored to the Shaikh's favour" (p. 201, folio 183). **Excommunication, destitution and readmission by intercession, in three sentences.**
- **The khalifa's licence to enrol disciples treated as a burden.** 'Ala'u'd-Din Nili "was allowed to enrol disciples, but found the associated responsibilities too onerous" (p. 201, folio 183); and Amir Khwurd's statement of differentiated commissions: "'The Sultanu'l-Mashai'kh entrusted everyone with duties which he found him fit to perform. To one he ordered to remain silent, and behind doors. To another he ordered to enrol a large number of disciples. To a third he would order that he live amongst the people, accept the suffering they may cause, and remain courteous to them.'" (p. 203, folio 185).
- **A new dance form named after its inventor and attached to samaʿ.** Burhanu'd-Din Gharib "invented a peculiar style of dancing which was performed during the ritual of sama'. This came to be known as the Burhani" (p. 201, folio 183).
- **A fight over samaʿ settled by performing it, at a state tribunal.** "Qazi Sa'd and Qazi 'Imad urged Iltutmish to legislate against the practice and the Sultan arranged a mahzar to discuss the question. However, Shaikh Hamidu'd-Din s'lenced [OCR?] [silenced] the Sultan by reminding him that he himself had succeeded to Delhi's throne because of the prayers of the Baghdad sufis, for whom he had waited an entire night while they indulged in sama'. Sa'd and 'Imad pressed the Qazi to prove his case logically, but the Shaikh ordered his servants to start playing music. This is reported as having moved everyone at the mahzar to ecstasy" (p. 214, folio 196). Earlier: "Shaikh Hamidu'd-Din's friendship with Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din aroused in him a great interest in sama'. He made vigorous efforts to popularize the practice and the 'ulama' were powerless to suppress it" (p. 214, folio 196). **Rizvi then rejects the story as anachronistic — see Arguments.**
- **The mahzar as an instrument used twice** — against Jalalu'd-Din Tabrizi under Iltutmish (p. 210, folio 192) and over sama' under the same Sultan (p. 214, folio 196).
- **Forced relocation of sufis and ʿulamaʾ by the state, with dates.** "In the days when Sultan Muhammad bin Tughluq was sending people from Delhi to Daulatabad (1326-27)"; he "summoned all the sadrs (religious dignitaries) and other distinguished residents of Delhi and its environs to the court. He ordered splendid bargahs (tents) to be erected and to place in them pulpits so that he himself might prompt people to jihad" (p. 200, folio 182); "he had ordained that all leading sufis and 'ulama' remain in Daulatabad" (p. 201, folio 183); "In 1327 … Maulana Husamu'd-Din was selected … for transference to Daulatabad" (p. 199, folio 181).
- **State gifts refused and accepted at the same audience.** "the Sultan offered each a woollen garment and a purse of tankas. Maulana Shamsu'd-Din and Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din took the gifts and departed. Shaikh Qutbu'd-Din Dabir knew Maulana Zarradi would not accept the gifts, so he himself took them" (p. 200, folio 182).
- **Sufis holding government office, described as compulsion and then as doctrine.** "However it would seem that Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din and other eminent Chishti sufis were compelled to accept government posts. Shaikh 'Abdu'l-Haqq depicts the Sultan as forcing Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din to accompany him on his travels, and relates that on another occasion he was appointed his jamadar", the office footnoted as "A servant who presents clothes to his master" (p. 203, folio 185); against which "Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din Mahmud advocated that the government service was not necessarily an obstacle to contemplation and meditation", quoting "'The essence of sufism is not an external garment. / Gird up your loins to serve the Sultan and be a sufi.'" (p. 207, folio 189).
- **Shaikhs as king-makers.** "leaders of the 'ulama', sufis and prominent former officers of Muhammad bin Tughlugq [OCR?] [Tughluq], made their forty-six year old favourite, Firuz, Sultan. Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din, one of the leading king-makers, entreated the new Sultan to rule with justice, and in turn received a promise to that effect" (p. 204, folio 186); and Bada'uni's stronger claim, "According to Mulla 'Abdu'l-Qadir Bada'uni, Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din had installed Firuz on the throne" (p. 204, folio 186).
- **A programme for the state written by a Shaikhuʾl-Islam, in four principles, printed in full** (pp. 212–213, folios 194–195): rulers "should promote Islamic customs, promulgate the commands of the Shari'a … and uproot kufr (infidelity), shirk (polytheism) and idolatry", failing which "they should make every effort to disgrace and humiliate Hindus, mushriks (polytheists) and idolaters", "should not tolerate the sight of Hindus, and in particular they should exterminate the Brahmans"; sins "should not be openly committed in Islamic towns", and prostitutes "should be compelled to practise their trade secretly … for if there are no prostitutes, rogues might be forced to rape Muslim women in harems"; enforcement "entrusted to the pious, and God-fearing officers", "Philosophers should be banished", and "Shi'is, should be mercilessly disgraced and should not receive government posts"; and "Justice should be strictly dispensed, but it is only possible if the dread and fear of the king uproots tyranny and tyrants." **Rizvi adds that compliance alone "guaranteed the salvation of rulers and mere prayers, fastings and great acts of charity would not assist them"** (p. 213, folio 195). **A Suhrawardi Shaikhuʾl-Islam's anti-Hindu and anti-Shiʿi state programme, printed without editorial comment beyond the source-critical note under Arguments.**
- **A dervish household described materially.** Husamu'd-Din Multani "lived in a thatched hut without servants. He was married and had a family" and "felt he was unworthy to use the title of Shaikh" (p. 198, folio 180); Zarradi "rented a house in front of the jama'at-khana where he was close to his pir" (p. 199, folio 181).
- **An open-air congregation of weavers under a saint, in a grove holding tombs.** "Early in the morning he would leave his dwelling in Awadh and walk to the local mango grove which also held some tombs. There he spent the whole day in prayer. A small group of Muslim weavers worked in the grove and with Nasiru'd-Din they would gather in congregation under the trees to pray while he acted as their Imam" (p. 202, folio 184). **A working-class congregation, a tomb-grove and a saint as Imam — the closest thing in the chunk to a shrine before there is a shrine.**
- **Six conditions for concentration on God, prescribed as a regime** (p. 199, folio 181): "One should retire to a lonely cell and leave it for neither company nor change"; "One should always exist in a state of performing wuzu (ablutions). One may sleep when necessary but on rising immediately perform wuzu again"; "Perpetual fasting should be observed"; "With the exception of zikr, constant silence should be practised"; "Zikr should be continually recited at the same time as a recollection in the heart of the presence of one's pir"; "One should expel every thought except that of God."
- **Breath control as the essence of discipline, with a technical term and a yogic comparison.** "The essence of sufi discipline was control of the breath and this should be practised during meditation. Every breath was related to the mystic state … In the beginning breath control was a deliberate action, later it became automatic. A sufi was one whose breaths were counted and the perfect sufi was a sahib-i anfas (the master of articulated breath). The breaths of faultless yogis, known as Siddhas were also measured. A dervish related he had learnt concentration from a cat by watching it sit before a rat hole in such a way that it had full control over its breathing so that not a single whisker moved" (pp. 206–207, folios 188–189).
- **Two fetters, and the family as the first of them.** "There were two kinds of fetters … One related to the Shari'a and the other to the self. The first shackle was the family, and the second, sensuality. Love of God drove away all thoughts of the family" (p. 206, folio 188); and "True comfort was to be found in the house of a dervish, there was only grief in the house of a wealthy man" (p. 206, folio 188).
- **A saint's charity weighed against a sultan's, and found second.** The chamberlain reported "that the Sultan had distributed large sums of money to the poor. The Shaikh questioned his disciples on the superior nature of either the Sultan's charity to his people or the fulfilment of a promise, and they all agreed on the latter" (p. 199, folio 181).
- **Books as inheritable khanqah property.** Akhi Siraj left Delhi "taking with him some books from the great Shaikh's library" (p. 202, folio 184); the young Akhi's only possessions were "Some sheets of paper and a few elementary books" (p. 202, folio 184).
- **A saint's forgiveness overriding the criminal jurisdiction of the state.** "The high government officials and Sultan Firuz were unable to punish the assailant due to the insistence of his victim. Turab was able to leave Delhi unmolested, even by a mob infuriated by the attack on the city's beloved Shaikh" (p. 205, folio 187); and the payment, "The Shaikh paid Turab twenty silver tankas and sought his forgiveness lest the latter had suffered some injury while wielding his knife" (p. 205, folio 187).
- **Negative for this range: there is no ʿurs, no mela, no sajjada nashin, no custodian, no Auqaf, no waqf deed, no visitor number, no offering-box, no shrine dispute and no land grant to any shrine in pp. 198–214 (folios 180–196).** The nearest approaches are the Shaikhuʾl-Islam's "stipends and land" (p. 210, folio 192), Firuz's undated "gifts" to khanqahs (pp. 204, 207; folios 186, 189) and the Mughal enclosure-and-mosque dates at Chiragh-i Dihli (p. 205, folio 187). **All shrine administration is (not stated).**

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

Attributions are the book's own, including its hedges ("according to tradition", "is said to have", "This is reported as having", "Such a story, however, is anachronistic").

- **Miracles refused as a category, by Nizamu'd-Din.** Asked "what course should be pursued when requested to perform miracles, the Shaikh's reply was concise: 'A miracle means that one remains steadfast at God's door. If you remain firm on your path, why do you seek miracles?'" (p. 198, folio 180).
- **A foreseen execution.** Zarradi "did not wish to see the Sultan as he used to say that he could see his head rolling before the court. He was determined not to compromise, and believed that death was imminent" (p. 200, folio 182). **He was not in fact killed at that audience** (pp. 200–201, folios 182–183).
- **An army saved by a saint's prayers, claimed by the saint himself.** "Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din claimed that the imperial army reached Sarsuti safely because of his own prayers" (p. 204, folio 186), the book noting "As mentioned earlier".
- **Silence under eleven knife wounds.** "A galandar [OCR?] [qalandar], named Turab, entered the room and inflicted eleven knife wounds on the Shaikh's body. The bleeding was so profuse that it flowed into a drain in his cell. While being stabbed, the Shaikh did not utter a sound" (pp. 204–205, folios 186–187).
- **Green wood and dry wood — the justification of a seventeen-day initiation** (p. 208, folio 190).
- **The rose on the milk** — the scholars of Multan told by a gesture that there was room for one more (pp. 208–209, folios 190–191).
- **A knife mended miraculously by an ironsmith sufi, told by a sceptic against sufism.** Maulana Qutbu'd-Din Kashani "believed that when a student at Kashghar, he had seen the most outstanding sufi of all. The sufi was an ironsmith who made knives. Although he miraculously mended a broken knife of the Maulana, what seems to have impressed him most was the fact that the sufi was usefully employed. As no other mystic was like the ironsmith, the Maulana advocated that it was unnecessary to believe in the sufi movement itself" (p. 209, folio 191). **A karamat used as an argument against sufism — rare, and worth keeping.**
- **A wilaya with a physical frontier, and a journey made safe across it.** Baba Farid "replied that the limit of his spiritual influence was at a certain water tank and that beyond it began the area of Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din whose prayers he should attain. The musician acted on the Baba's advice and safely completed his journey" (p. 211, folio 193).
- **A career owed to an infant blessing.** Of Saiyid Nuru'd-Din Mubarak Ghaznawi: "After his birth, his father took him to an eminent saint, Khwaja Muhammad Ajal Shirazi, to be blessed; according to tradition it was to that visit that Nuru'd-Din owed his later prominence as a sufi" (p. 212, folio 194).
- **A throne won by the prayers of sufis, and a tribunal moved to ecstasy by music.** Hamidu'd-Din reminded Iltutmish "that he himself had succeeded to Delhi's throne because of the prayers of the Baghdad sufis, for whom he had waited an entire night while they indulged in sama'"; "the Shaikh ordered his servants to start playing music. This is reported as having moved everyone at the mahzar to ecstasy" (p. 214, folio 196). **Rizvi rejects it: "Such a story, however, is anachronistic for it was not possible for both the Qazi and the Sultan to have been together in Baghdad."** (p. 214, folio 196).
- **Wishes granted at a tomb by tying threads** (p. 207, folio 189) — reported as ordinary urban practice, with no miracle language.
- **Twelve hajjs, immediately discounted.** Majdu'd-Din Hajji "is said to have performed the hajj twelve times, although, of course, numbers used in mystic literature are not necessarily accurate" (p. 213, folio 195).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Rizvi defends the Chishti order against the claim that a sultan destroyed it, and he does it by appeal to the founders.** "The view that Sultan Muhammad bin Tughluq broke the central Chishti organization is incompatible with the spirit of the si/sila [silsila] which had been set up by Khwaja Mu'inu'd-Din and Baba Farid" (p. 207, folio 189). **No holder of the rejected view is named** — (not stated).
- **His counter-thesis: provincialisation was a choice, and a good one.** "His successors and some other disciples of Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya' wisely decided to carve Chishti centres in the provinces of the empire, rather than remain in the capital"; "The popularity of Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya' had tended to give the impression that a parallel spiritual empire existed in Delhi beside the temporal one, although this of course was alien to Chishti teaching. In reality the sufi spirit worked more successfully remote from centres of political power" (p. 207, folio 189). **Directly relevant to every provincial shrine in the archive.**
- **A decline verdict in the saint's own mouth.** "But the vitality of Delhi's spiritual life, as it had been during the time of Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din, was fast vanishing. Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din commented that the art of sufi teaching had degenerated into child's play, that is, something not to be taken seriously" (p. 207, folio 189).
- **Rizvi sides with the Siddhas against a named modern scholar, in a footnote.** "Although nafs means lower self, nafas means breath. The Shaikh himself translated nafas as dam but Nizami, reluctant to give any credit to Siddhas, deliberately reads na/s [OCR?] [nafs] in the sense of the lower self. Introduction to the Khairu'l-Majalis, p. 24" (p. 206, folio 188, footnote 8). **An accusation of deliberate mistranslation, and the chunk's one claim of yogic influence on Chishti practice.**
- **A source used and then undercut, twice.** Of Amir Khwurd on Muhammad bin Tughluq's harassment of Nasiru'd-Din: "However, Amir Khwurd fails to give details in support of his accusations" (p. 203, folio 185). Of Ibn Battuta on Baha'u'd-Din's ancestry: "Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din … himself made this statement about his ancestry to Ibn Battuta, however, it is not necessarily authentic" (p. 208, folio 190).
- **Badaʾuni's king-making claim reported and then softened.** "According to Mulla 'Abdu'l-Qadir Bada'uni, Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din had installed Firuz on the throne. The Shaikh, however, was uninterested in politics, but rumours of a rebellion in the capital must have been widespread at the time implicating the Shaikh and Firuz in the alleged uprisings" (p. 204, folio 186). **"Must have been widespread" is Rizvi's own conjecture, offered without a source.**
- **Barani's report of Nuru'd-Din's sermons taken apart in three moves.** "Barani quotes Balban as an authority on Shaikh Nuru'd-Din's sermons. This may be Barani'sown [OCR?] [Barani's own] view. Nevertheless, the sermons were an abridged version devised by Ghazali and Nizamu'l-Mulk of the Perso-Islamic system of polity, which had been evolved at the Saljugqid [OCR?] [Saljuqid] court"; and the conclusion, "The information available is, however, insufficient to ascribe, with much certainty, the above theories to Saiyid Nuru'd-Din. However, he may also have forwarded identical, or similar, theories currently accepted in that period, which had been devised earlier" (p. 213, folio 195). **So the four principles quoted above are, by Rizvi's own judgement, of uncertain attribution — anyone citing them must carry this.**
- **A modern scholar's periodisation rejected.** "A modern scholar's view that philosophy was a problem which had been highlighted during the Tughluq period is historically inaccurate, as the study had concerned both theologians and sufis from the end of the tenth century onwards. Concern by the orthodox and sufis at the popularity of philosophy is reflected even in the Fawa'idu'l-Fu'ad" (p. 213, folio 195). The scholar is cited only as "Some aspects of religion and politics, p. 161" (p. 213, folio 195, footnote 2) — **no author** (not stated).
- **A hagiographic story dated out of existence.** "Such a story, however, is anachronistic for it was not possible for both the Qazi and the Sultan to have been together in Baghdad" (p. 214, folio 196).
- **A caution against mystic numbers as data.** "although, of course, numbers used in mystic literature are not necessarily accurate" (p. 213, folio 195). **A general rule from the author himself about the reliability of the genre these notes are drawn from.**
- **A structural contrast between the two orders on wealth.** "With the Suhrawardi order there are few stories revealing that its members lived in extreme poverty, unlike members of the Chishti si/sila [silsila]" (p. 210, folio 192); and "Unlike the Chishtis, Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din did not observe incessant fasting and ate normally" (p. 211, folio 193).
- **The Khairuʾl-Majalis placed as the summit of Chishti thought.** "The teachings of Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din embodied in the Khairu'l-Majalis represented a peak in Chishti philosophy which had evolved in India during the course of the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries" (p. 205, folio 187).
- **The Suhrawardiyya's Indian foundation attributed to migration.** "HE Indian soil was enriched by the migration from Iran and Transoxiana of a number of disciples of Shaikh Shihabu'd-Din Suhrawardi" (p. 208, folio 190) — the dropped `T` of "The" is the chapter's drop-cap, not damage.
- **Political history offered as the cause of sufi prominence.** "The power of the 'ulama' and the sufi orders which the Sultan resented so strongly paled into insignificance in contrast with the spate of rebellions against the Delhi Sultanate beginning only a year after his accession"; "The popular slogan, 'down with the tyrant' (zalim), became a catch phrase and ambitious adventurers raised rebel standards. Many members of the 'ulama' and sufis, long-time enemies of Muhammad bin Tughluq, became their supporters" (pp. 203–204, folios 185–186).
- **An idealised reign reported as the Shaikh's impression, not as fact.** "Conversations of the Shaikh's tend to give the impression that the reign of Sultan 'Ala'u'd-Din was an ideal one for the people of Delhi. It would appear that prices were cheap and, according to an anecdote related by the Shaikh, this was precipitated by philanthropic reasons" (p. 207, folio 189).
- **The saint's own explanation of his persecution, left unglossed.** Asked "the reasons for the Sultan's persecution. He replied: 'There was something between me and God the most High which was settled in that way.'" (p. 207, folio 189).

**Archive-mapping doubts, in order of how much they need a human:**

9. **`shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam` is the weakest mapping in the chunk and should be checked first.** The book prints only "Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din, a grandson of Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya" in a footnote (p. 208, folio 190, footnote 1). **It never writes "Rukn-i ʿAlam" or "Rukn-e-Alam", never gives a death date, never gives a personal name beyond Ruknu'd-Din, and never connects him to Multan or to a tomb.** The identification with the row's principal figure "Shah Rukn-e-Alam (Rukn-ud-Din Abul Fath)" rests entirely on the name plus the stated grandson relationship to a Multan saint. **If a later chunk names a different Ruknu'd-Din in this family, this mapping must be revisited.** The one thing the bullet actually records — that he was Ibn Battuta's informant for a family ancestry Rizvi calls "not necessarily authentic" — is safe regardless of who he was.
10. **`shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya`: the book's death date does not match the archive index, and the book's should be recorded as the book's.** Rizvi prints **"Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din died on 7 Safar, 661/21 December, 1262"** (p. 212, folio 194), sourced to "AA, p. 28". The index row gives `figure_died` **1267**. **I am not resolving this and have not silently adopted either; the bullet above carries the book's date exactly as printed, and a human should decide which the entry uses.** Note also that the book's own "For about half a century after settling in Multan" (p. 212, folio 194) is consistent with its 1262 only loosely, since it never dates his arrival at Multan.
11. **"Shaikh Jalalu'd-Din Tabrizi" could be read onto two archive rows and I have mapped it onto neither.** The candidates are `shrine-of-jalaluddin-surkh-posh-bukhari-jalaluddin-bukhari` ("Sayyid Jalaluddin Surkh-Posh Bukhari", Uch Sharif, `figure_died` 1291, Suhrawardi) and `shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht` ("Sayyid Jalaluddin (Makhdoom Jahaniyan Jahangasht)", Uch Sharif, `figure_died` 1384, Suhrawardi). **The book's man carries the nisba "Tabrizi", not "Bukhari"; he is a direct khalifa of Shihabu'd-Din Suhrawardi and was tried at a mahzar under Iltutmish** (pp. 208, 210; folios 190, 192), **which fits neither index date.** Two rows, one name, no mapping — flagged per the protocol rather than picked.
12. **"Maulana Burhanu'd-Din Gharib" is not mapped to `langer-makhdoom`, but the collision should be recorded.** That row names "Makhdoom Burhan-ud-din" of Chiniot with `figure_died` 1245. The book's Burhanu'd-Din Gharib is a khalifa of Nizamu'd-Din Auliya', was denounced by courtiers of 'Ala'u'd-Din Khalji, was "aged over ninety", and was forced to Daulatabad (p. 201, folio 183). **Chronologically incompatible with 1245 — but the reasoning uses the index's date, not the book's, because the book gives him no dates at all** (not stated). **Recorded, not dismissed.**
13. **Three further surname collisions I am recording so that no-one maps them by accident.** (a) **"Qutbu'd-Din" appears four times as four different men** — "Shaikh Qutbu'd-Din Dabir" (p. 200, folio 182), "Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki" (pp. 211, 214; folios 193, 196), "Maulana Qutbu'd-Din Kashani" (p. 209, folio 191) and "Sultan Qutbu'd-Din Mubarak Shah Khalji" (p. 213, folio 195) — **and none of them is the row `tomb-of-qutbuddin-aibak` ("Sultan Qutb ud-Din Aibak", d. 1210), who does not appear in this chunk at all.** (b) **"Shamsu'd-Din" appears as Maulana Shamsu'd-Din Yahya, Sultan Shamsu'd-Din Iltutmish and Malik Shamsu'd-Din, and none is "Shah Shams-ud-Din Sabzwari" of `shrine-of-shah-shams-ud-din-sabzwari`** (pp. 200, 209, 210, 213; folios 182, 191, 192, 195). (c) **"Gharib" (Burhanu'd-Din Gharib) and "Firuz" both appear, but neither touches `khawaja-feroz-ud-din-gharib-nawaz`, whose principal figure died in 1981.**
14. **Interim toponym convention (a), applied and kept reversible.** Two place names in this chunk fall in archive territory and are left unmapped as bare toponyms. **"Multan"** occurs many times; where it is attached to Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya it is already carried by his row, but the mentions attached to no saint — Qutbu'd-Din Kashani's madrasa, "the Governor of Multan", "the Qazi of Multan", "the Multan fort", the "townfolk" (pp. 209, 210; folios 191, 192) — **would, if the ruling reverses, attach to the Multan rows `shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya`, `shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam`, `shrine-of-shah-shams-ud-din-sabzwari`, `shrine-of-shah-yusaf-gardez`, `shrine-of-syed-musa-pak`, `shrine-of-hafiz-muhammad-jamal-multani`, `shrine-of-hazrat-shah-ali-akbar-shah-ali-akbar-shamsi` and `prahladpuri-temple`** — in practice only to the first, since nothing in the text distinguishes them. **"Sind"** (pp. 208, 209, 210; folios 190, 191, 192) appears only as a conquered province and **would attach to no row in particular.** No other toponym in the chunk is in archive territory.
15. **The Ajodhan tombs bullet under `shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar` is the one place where I have stretched the interim convention, and it is reversible.** "the tombs of his spiritual ancestors in Ajmer and Ajodhan" (p. 200, folio 182) is **not** a bare toponym — a tomb is attached — but the book **names no occupant**, so the mapping rests on the row's location plus the fact that Baba Farid is named at Ajodhan eleven pages later (p. 211, folio 193). **If a reviewer prefers the strict reading, that bullet moves to "Other saints, sites and events" and the row keeps the other three bullets.** Ajmer, by contrast, has no archive row at all, so the parallel question does not arise for it.
16. **`shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar` is otherwise mapped on the ruled author-or-reference convention, and the evidence is thin in one of the three bullets.** "Khwaja Mu'inu'd-Din and Baba Farid" (p. 207, folio 189) is **a bare name inside Rizvi's argument**, with no date, tomb, place or deed attached. The water-tank anecdote and the concordat (p. 211, folio 193) are substantive. **A consolidator should not read the p. 207 mention as shrine evidence.**
17. **The archive defect and the deity question both come up empty here.** The row `Shaktipeeth Shri Hinglaj Mata Mandir` with its **empty id cell** is untouched, because **Hinglaj is not mentioned in pp. 198–214** — no id has been invented and none was needed. **No Hindu deity is named anywhere in the chunk**, so the unruled question of whether a deity can be an archive row's principal figure does not arise; the only Hindu material is the collective abuse in Nuru'd-Din's first principle (p. 212, folio 194), which names no god, temple or person.

**Internal contradictions and chronological strains, kept side by side rather than resolved:**

18. **Royal money is said both to strain khanqahs and to restore them, three pages apart.** "Sultan Firuz … showered gifts on their khangahs which resulted in the usual strains associated with material prosperity" (p. 204, folio 186) against "Large gifts from Sultan Firuz to the Chishti order restored the glory of many khangahs" (p. 207, folio 189). **The book does not note the tension, and the second sentence is immediately followed by the claim that Delhi's spiritual vitality "was fast vanishing" (p. 207, folio 189).**
19. **Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din is a king-maker and uninterested in politics on the same page.** "Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din, one of the leading king-makers, entreated the new Sultan to rule with justice" against "The Shaikh, however, was uninterested in politics" (p. 204, folio 186); and "compelled to accept government posts" (p. 203, folio 185) against "Gird up your loins to serve the Sultan and be a sufi" (p. 207, folio 189).
20. **Muhammad bin Tughluq's target is given two different geographies.** "he intended to seize Turkistan and Khurasan and liquidate the descendants of Chingiz Khan" (p. 200, folio 182) against "his policy regarding the extermination of Mongols from Ghazni and Khurasan" (p. 203, folio 185), the second explicitly back-referencing the first ("As previously mentioned"). **Turkistan and Ghazni are not the same place.**
21. **A generational strain in the Burhanpur story that the book does not remark on.** Burhanu'd-Din Gharib was a khalifa of Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din and was sent to Daulatabad in the Muhammad bin Tughluq transfers (p. 201, folio 183); **his khalifa Shaikh Zainu'd-Din is then said to have advised "Sultan Nasir Khan Faruqi (1399-1437)"** (pp. 201–202, folios 183–184). **On the book's own printed dates, that is a disciple of a disciple of a shaikh of the 1320s advising a sultan who reigned from 1399. Recorded, not resolved.**
22. **One clean internal confirmation, worth recording as such.** Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din "born in about 675/1276—77"; "By the time he was twenty-five, Nasiru'd-Din Mahmud had abandoned the world" (p. 202, folio 184); migrated to Delhi "at the age of forty-three" having "completed eighteen years of strenuous mystical exercises" (pp. 202–203, folios 184–185) — **25 + 18 = 43, so these printed values agree**; and the stabbing "One day in 1353" (p. 204, folio 186) with "lived a further three years" and death "on 18 Ramazan 757/14 September 1356" (p. 205, folio 187) also agree. **No other date set in the chunk can be cross-checked from within it**, and the pairs 578/1182-83, 661/21 December 1262, 644/April-May 1247, 632/1234-35, 1142 and 1143/1729 and 1730, and the regnal spans (1719-48), (1399-1437), (1382-1601) are recorded above exactly as printed and are **unverified**.
23. **Two attributions the book itself marks as unsafe, and which must travel with any quotation drawn from them.** Saiyid Nuru'd-Din's four principles: "The information available is, however, insufficient to ascribe, with much certainty, the above theories to Saiyid Nuru'd-Din" (p. 213, folio 195). Baha'u'd-Din's Arab ancestry: "it is not necessarily authentic" (p. 208, folio 190).
