# rizvi_history_of_sufism_india_1 — chunk 008 — pp. 134–147

**The folio regime in this chunk is confirmed page-for-page against folio_map.tsv, with no discrepancy anywhere.** All fourteen pages print a running header, and every one of them matches the table: verso `116 A History of Sufism in India` (p. 134), `118 …` (p. 136), `120 …` (p. 138), `122 …` (p. 140), `124 …` (p. 142), `126 …` (p. 144), `128 …` (p. 146); recto `The Chishtis * 117` (p. 135), `The Chishtis 119` (p. 137), `121` (p. 139), `123` (p. 141), `125` (p. 143), `127` (p. 145), `129` (p. 147). **So pp. 134–147 = folios 116–129, exactly as the table gives them, and I have taken every folio below from the table and not from the page.** The only header damage is the stray `*` between title and numeral on p. 135 (folio 117). Chapter title in every recto header: `The Chishtis`.

**The chunk is inside Chapter Two and the brief's section description holds, but I cannot confirm either of the brief's chapter boundaries from inside these pages — see Doubts, where the book's own forward reference gives some evidence on how "pp. 132 … 207" should be read.** The chunk opens mid-sentence ("the middle ages, decided to settle in the east in Ajmer, on the borders of the Ghurid empire", p. 134, folio 116) and closes mid-sentence ("the differences consisted only between various kinds of men, rather than a dis-", p. 147, folio 129). It carries three of the book's own section headings: **`Khwaja Mu‘inu‘d-Din`** (p. 134, folio 116), **`Ajmer`** (p. 143, folio 125) and **`Nagaur`** (p. 145, folio 127).

**Four archive rows are touched, and unlike chunk 006 this is genuine shrine material, not author-only material.** `data-darbar` and `shrine-of-miran-hussain-zanjani-zanjani-sahib` appear together as two **tombs at Lahore** that Khwaja Mu‘inu’d-Din lodged beside (p. 139, folio 121); `shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya` is named with his silsila and his city and given a two-page verbatim dispute about wealth (pp. 146–147, folios 128–129); `tomb-of-qutbuddin-aibak` is named three times as a military and administrative actor (pp. 139–140, folios 121–122). **The chunk's single densest body of material — the Ajmer shrine's construction, endowment, langar, succession dispute, custodianship and shrine music under Akbar, Jahangir, Shah Jahan and Aurangzeb (pp. 143–145, folios 125–127) — maps to no archive row at all, because Ajmer is not in the 169.** That negative is recorded below and is the most important thing a consolidator should know about this chunk.

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **data-darbar** (the book calls him "Shaikh ‘Ali Hujwiri", and in a footnote on the same page simply "Hujwiri"): **his tomb at Lahore is attested as a standing, visitable site — this is the row's first shrine evidence in the book so far, as against the author-only citations of chunk 006.** Khwaja Mu‘inu’d-Din "reached Lahore and stayed near the tombs of Shaikh Husain Zanjani and Shaikh ‘Ali Hujwiri" (p. 139, folio 121).
- **data-darbar**: **the book prints two irreconcilable reports of when he died relative to Zanjani, and then adjudicates between them.** "Jamali says that the same year the Khwaja reached Lahore, Shaikh ‘Ali Hujwiri died but that Shaikh Husain Zanjani was still alive. According to Fawa‘idu'l-Fu’ad, Hujwiri reached Lahore in the year Shaikh Husain Zanjani died" (p. 139, folio 121, footnote 3). Rizvi's own ruling: "Fawaidu'l Fu‘ad's statement appears to be correct and it is likely that the Khwaja stayed near the two tombs" (p. 139, folio 121, footnote 3). **No death year, no personal name beyond "‘Ali Hujwiri", no title "Data Ganj Bakhsh", no *Kashf al-Mahjub* and no silsila are given anywhere in this chunk** — all (not stated).
- **shrine-of-miran-hussain-zanjani-zanjani-sahib** (the book calls him "Shaikh Husain Zanjani", and in Abu’l-Fazl's report "Shaikh Husaini Zanjani"): **named jointly with Hujwiri as the second of the two Lahore tombs the Khwaja lodged beside** — "stayed near the tombs of Shaikh Husain Zanjani and Shaikh ‘Ali Hujwiri" (p. 139, folio 121).
- **shrine-of-miran-hussain-zanjani-zanjani-sahib**: **the book cannot decide whether he was alive or dead when the Khwaja arrived, and prints all three positions.** Jamali has him "still alive"; the *Fawa‘idu'l-Fu’ad* has Hujwiri arriving "in the year Shaikh Husain Zanjani died"; and "According to Abu'l-Fazl, Khwaja Mu'inu'd-Din lived with Shaikh Husaini Zanjani. Ain, II] [OCR?] [III], p. 168" — a statement that would require him to be alive (p. 139, folio 121, footnote 3). Rizvi prefers the tomb reading. **No dates, no location within Lahore, no shrine building and no silsila are given** — all (not stated).
- **tomb-of-qutbuddin-aibak** (the book calls him "Qutbu’d-Din Aibak", and twice simply "Qutbu’d-Din"): **named as Mu‘izzu’d-Din's slave left in command of conquered Delhi** — "Mu‘izzu’d-Din Muhammad bin Sam had conquered Delhi and, departing for Ghazni, left his favourite slave, Qutbu’d-Din Aibak, as head of his forces in the occupied city" (p. 139, folio 121).
- **tomb-of-qutbuddin-aibak**: **he is the appointing authority behind the Ajmer garrison, which is the chunk's link between the Turkic conquest and the Khwaja's protection.** "Qutbu’d-Din had appointed Saiyid Husain Mashhadi as the darogha (military governor) of the area. The Saiyid served the Khwaja with great devotion" (p. 140, folio 122).
- **tomb-of-qutbuddin-aibak**: **his troops' position and their campaigning are given as the political context for the Khwaja's move.** "Occupying troops stationed at Indraprastha near Delhi under Qutbu’d-Din Aibak were forced to make several invasions in the Doab and the regions around Ajmer to consolidate their power" (p. 140, folio 122). **No tomb, no Lahore, no death date, no title beyond "favourite slave" and "head of his forces" is given for him in this chunk** — all (not stated). Mapped on the ruled person-convention of 18 September 2026; see Doubts, where a second and different "Qutbu’d-Din" in the same chunk is flagged.
- **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya** (the book calls him "Shaikh Baha’u'd-Din Zakariyya of the Suhrawardi si/sila [OCR?] [silsila] in Multan", and at the close of the dispute "Shaikh Baha’u’d-Din Zakariyya Suhrawardi"): **he is named with his order and his city, which is as much identification as any figure gets in this chunk** (p. 146, folio 128). This is the row's principal figure, his location and his silsila, all from the book.
- **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya**: **he is the correspondent, then the interlocutor, in a dispute about whether a sufi may hold wealth — the fullest treatment of shrine wealth in the chunk.** "In order to ascertain the attitude of the Suhrawardi order towards wealth, Shaikh Hamidu’d-Din wrote a number of letters to Shaikh Baha’u'd-Din Zakariyya… Although the latter replied, Shaikh Hamidu'd-Din was not completely satisfied with the answers received" (p. 146, folio 128).
- **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya**: **the book states flatly that he was rich, in its own voice, in a footnote.** "This remark was made in reference to Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din, himself extremely rich" (p. 146, folio 128, footnote 6); and in the text, he was "mystically prompted by the teachings of his pir, who also had vast wealth" (p. 147, folio 129). **His pir is not named here** — (not stated).
- **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya**: **his defence of wealth, in his own reported words** — "Although wealth is a serpent, someone who has learnt the incantation to overcome the venom, need not have any fear from the serpent"; and, pressed further, "Your achievement as a dervish is not endowed by such beauty and elegance as to be immune from the evil eye, but our achievements as dervishes are endowed with such great beauty as to gain no harm even if the whole of the world’s blackness were applied to its face" (pp. 146–147, folios 128–129). **The book twice records that he lost the exchange**: "Shaikh Baha’u’d-Din was speechless" and "Shaikh Baha’u’d-Din Zakariyya Suhrawardi was unable to reply" (p. 147, folio 129). Source given as "Sururu's-Sudur, pp. 86-7" (p. 147, folio 129, footnote 1).
- **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya**: **he and Shaikh Hamidu’d-Din met face to face at Delhi at a named institutional occasion.** "Both met in Delhi during the mahzar4 to resolve the charges against Shaikh Jalalu’d-Din Tabrizi" (p. 146, folio 128); *mahzar* glossed as "An assembly of ‘ulama’ and sufis to decide an important dispute; literally the word means either a royal court or any document attested by witnesses" (p. 146, folio 128, footnote 4). **No date is given for the mahzar** — (not stated).
- **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya**: **one of his sons carried the quarrel to Ajmer and tried to have Hamidu’d-Din indicted for non-attendance at Friday prayers.** "Controversy over the acquisition of material wealth by members of the Suhrawardi order annoyed one of Shaikh Baha’u’d-Din’s sons, who visited Ajmer. Discovering that Shaikh Hamidu’d-Din did not attend Friday congregational prayers, he used this as a pretext to attack the Shaikh and gained some support from a group of ‘ulama’ who demanded that the Shaikh fulfil his religious duties" (p. 147, folio 129). **The son is not named** — (not stated).

**No other archive row is touched anywhere in pp. 134–147 (folios 116–129), and the biggest body of material in the chunk falls outside the archive entirely.** In particular: **Ajmer and the shrine of Khwaja Mu‘inu’d-Din are not an archive row**, so the whole construction, endowment, custodianship, langar and shrine-music sequence of pp. 140 and 143–145 (folios 122, 125–127) is recorded below under "Practices, institutions, economy" and not against any id; **Nagaur and Shaikh Hamidu’d-Din are not an archive row** either, although he is the chunk's second protagonist. **Hinglaj is not mentioned**, so the index's empty-id defect on `Shaktipeeth Shri Hinglaj Mata Mandir` does not arise here and no id has been invented. **No Sikh, Hindu, Jain, Udasi or Nanakpanthi site in the index appears**, and no shrine in Sindh, Balochistan or Khyber Pakhtunkhwa is named. The two Pakistan locations that do appear — Lahore and Multan — appear attached to the four figures above and are mapped through them.

## Practices, institutions, economy

**This chunk is the opposite of chunk 006: burial, tomb-building, endowment, langar, custodianship, succession dispute, pilgrimage and shrine music are all present and dated — but every one of them belongs to Ajmer, which is not an archive row.** Recorded here in full because the consolidator will want them as comparative material for the archive's Chishti rows.

- **Burial and the fabric of the grave, in stages.** "His earthly remains were buried at the place where he had spent most of his life. The original grave was of bricks, later a stone cenotaph was built over it, leaving the brick construction intact. Khwaja Husain Nagauri built a tomb over the grave" (p. 143, folio 125). **Three successive states of the grave — brick, stone cenotaph over intact brick, then a tomb — with only the last given a builder and none given a date** (not stated).
- **The khanqah on the tomb site, and its patron.** "A khangah [OCR?] [khanqah] on the tomb site was built by the rulers of Mandu, probably by Mahmud Khalji (1436-69)" (pp. 143–144, folios 125–126). **"probably" is the book's own hedge.**
- **The gateway, with a year.** "A gateway known as Buland Darwaza (High Gateway) seems to have been constructed near the tomb by Mahmud Khaljiin 1455 [OCR?] [Mahmud Khalji in 1455]" (p. 144, folio 126).
- **Mosques accreted to the tomb complex over four reigns.** "The Malwa Sultans also built another mosque in the tomb complex, which was later extended by Jahangir and Aurangzeb" (p. 144, folio 126); "Shah-Jahan constructed a mosque of white marble in the tomb complex and added a gateway" (p. 145, folio 127); and, under Akbar's appointee, "The great mosque of Ajmer was built under his supervision" (p. 144, folio 126).
- **Offerings from non-Muslims, named with the technical term, and a described annual visit.** "Even those who refused to accept Islam would send large offerings and futuh to what Jamali called Hazrat Ishan (His Holiness Court, here meaning tomb). Even today, the kafirs of that area are devoted to the Khwaja’s tomb in the same manner as their ancestors had been. They visit it each year and ‘prostrate themselves on the dust of the tomb of that eminent one and the full moon of the heavens of his sainthood’" (p. 140, folio 122). **An annual visit, a described bodily act, and *futuh* from people explicitly outside the faith. The book does not call it an ʿurs and does not give a date** — (not stated).
- **Shrine servants paid from the offerings — the one custodianship-and-income sentence in the chunk.** "At the same time, large amounts of money are paid to those who tend the shrine" (p. 140, folio 122). **No office is named, no sajjada nashin, no Auqaf** — (not stated).
- **Conversion located at the shrine rather than in the saint's lifetime — Rizvi's own reading of his source.** "it does not refer to conversions made by the Khwaja himself, but to the process of conversions beside the Khwaja's shrine which was visited by the Hindus of the region" (p. 140, folio 122, footnote 1).
- **Imperial pilgrimage, with four dated journeys.** Akbar's devotion began "after hearing the songs of some minstrels at Midhakur, near Agra, glorifying the Khwaja"; "He commenced his journey on 14 January 1562"; "After his Chitor conquest, Akbar made a pilgrimage to Ajmer on 6 March 1568"; "In February 1570, Akbar reached Ajmer by foot from Agra to offer thanks for the birth of Prince Salim on 30 August 1569"; "Early in 1614, Akbar’s successor, Jahangir, stayed in Ajmer" (pp. 144, folios 126). **Barefoot-style pedestrian pilgrimage by an emperor, and a vow of thanksgiving for a birth.**
- **The cauldrons and the feeding of the poor — the chunk's langar, with a number.** "On this occasion he presented the Khwaja’s khanqah with a huge cauldron" (p. 144, folio 126); "Jahangir … presented a smaller cauldron than Akbar’s, to the shrine. Food for the poor was cooked and 5,000 assembled people were fed from the cauldron" (p. 144, folio 126); "The two survive" (p. 144, folio 126).
- **Precious-metal furnishing of the cenotaph, and its later replacement.** "the gold enclosure around the cenotaph, donated by Jahangir, has been replaced by a silver one" (p. 144, folio 126). **By whom and when is not stated.**
- **Offerings generating a family dispute — the economy of the shrine producing its own conflict.** "Wealth from the offerings of Akbar and his entourage resulted in a dispute for it amongst the Khwaja’s descendants" (p. 144, folio 126).
- **A succession and custodianship case decided by the state, with the outcome recorded.** "The dispute over alleged family successors of the Khwaja between Shaikh Husain, who claimed to be a descendant, and his rivals, was placed before the Emperor for judgement. The Shaikh’s opponents alleged that the Khwaja had left no descendants and that the claims of Shaikh Husain were false. The descendants of Shaikh Salim and some qazis supported Shaikh Husain’s rivals. After a laborious investigation, Akbar ruled against the Shaikh, and transferred the administration of the shrine to Shaikh Muhammad Bukhari, a trusted officer and a member of a distinguished family of holy men" (p. 144, folio 126). **A hereditary claim defeated and administration handed to a state appointee — directly comparable to Auqaf takeovers in the archive's rows.** Sourced to "Bada’uni, Muntakhabu't-Tawarikh, Il [OCR?] [II], p. 87. For details of earlier disputes see Chapter Four" (p. 144, folio 126, footnote 3). **The chunk itself records that the Khwaja did leave sons (p. 142, folio 124), which the opponents' allegation denies** — see Doubts.
- **Shrine management and pilgrim welfare as an administrative task.** "It would appear that Shaikh Muhammad greatly improved the shrine’s management while attempting to provide better conditions for pilgrims. Moreover, he looked after the erection of mosques and khangahs [khanqahs] in the territory" (p. 144, folio 126).
- **Gifts to shrine personnel as a standing practice.** "Both Jahangir and Shah Jahan distributed generous gifts to all connected with the Khwaja’s tomb" (p. 145, folio 127).
- **Music at the shrine, a customary payment to the musicians, and an emperor's reversal — the chunk's most quotable practice passage.** "Because the Emperor’s officers had stopped the musicians from playing, they refused to accept the customary gift. On a subsequent visit, the Emperor prevented his officers from interfering with the playing of music performed in memory of the Khwaja. The anecdote relates that the Emperor was deeply moved by it and paid the musicians double the normal gift" (p. 145, folio 127). **A named customary payment, a refusal of it as protest, and the state backing down — all attributed to Aurangzeb and marked by the book as an "anecdote".**
- **Land: the *ihyaʾ* grant and the royal *farman*, with the book's terms defined.** The Khwaja "had an ihya’ village near Ajmer. The muqta‘ of Ajmer urged him to obtain a royal farman for the land" (p. 143, folio 125). *Ihyaʾ*: "In Muslim legal literature Jhya’ [OCR?] [Ihya’] means ‘bringing to life’ and refers to unclaimed land lying fallow in distant and unpopulated areas. /Jhya’ [Ihya’] becomes the property of anyone who cultivates it. According to Hanafi law, the previous authorization from the /mam [Imam] is essential. At the beginning of Turkic rule in India, land was in abundance and the cultivators of waste lands were recognized as de facto owners and no advance authorization was apparently demanded" (p. 143, folio 125, footnote 1). *Muqtaʿ*: "The chief officer of an /gta‘ [Iqta‘] or revenue grant made to military chicfs [chiefs] in order to consolidate the power of the government, collect the revenue and maintain an army" (p. 143, folio 125, footnote 2). *Farman*: "A royal command or authorization, an edict" (p. 143, folio 125, footnote 3). **Rizvi rejects the whole anecdote: "no muqgta‘ [muqta‘] would dare to harass the Khwaja to gain a farman for wasteland."**
- **A saint who refuses land and state money — the counter-case, with his subsistence itemised.** "Although Chishti saints were permitted to receive cash gifts, the Shaikh believed that his own path was different. Hamidu'd-Din had a small ihya’ plot in the village of Suwali, near Nagaur. He lived from the income it gave and drank milk from his one cow, refusing offers from both the mugta‘ [muqta‘] of Nagaur and the Sultan for more land and money. His wife did her own spinning and the yarn sufficed for their meagre clothing" (pp. 145–146, folios 127–128). **"Chishti saints were permitted to receive cash gifts" is a general statement about the order and is the most transferable institutional line in the chunk.**
- **Vegetarianism, and a death-anniversary food custom explicitly refused.** "He also strongly advocated that no harm be done to any form of life, and pleaded with his followers to be totally vegetarian. Before his death he requested his descendants not to distribute meat for his soul’s peace. On being asked where lay the harm of such a traditional custom, if the meat had first been bought from a butcher’s shop, the Shaikh replied that the butcher would have to kill again in order to replace the meat which was sold" (p. 146, folio 128). **The "traditional custom" of distributing meat for a dead saint's soul is attested here precisely by being rejected.**
- **Friday prayer and the village/town distinction as a legal defence.** "The Shaikh replied he was indeed performing his duties, for the law specified that attendance at Friday prayers was not compulsory as Nagaur was a village and not a town" (p. 147, folio 129).
- **The khirqa and the khalifate, with a dating consequence.** "The Shaikh was highly impressed and gave Mu‘inu’d-Din a khirga [OCR?] [khirqa], appointing him his khalifa" (p. 138, folio 120); and "Khwaja Mu‘inu’d-Din obtained his khirga [khirqa] from Shaikh ‘Usman at the age of fifty-two … This would imply that he received his khirga [khirqa] in 588/1192-93 in the Ghazna region, and later left for India after having first obtained permission from Shaikh ‘Usman" (p. 137, folio 119, footnote 2). **Permission from the pir to migrate is a named precondition of the journey.**
- **Deputisation by territory.** "The Khwaja appointed Ziya’u’d-Din his deputy in Balkh and left for Ghazna" (p. 139, folio 121).
- **The nine conditions of the dervish life, dictated and written down — the chunk's one written rule of conduct.** The Khwaja "asked his disciple, Hamidu'd-Din Nagauri, to both explain and write them down so that such rules could be made known to a wider section of Muslims" (p. 141, folio 123). As printed (pp. 141–142, folios 123–124): "One should not earn money"; "One should not borrow money from anyone"; "One should not reveal to anyone nor seek help from anyone if one has eaten nothing, even for seven days"; "4. If one gains plenty of food, money, grain or clothing, one should not keep anything until the following day"; "5. One should not curse anyone; if anyone is very hurt, one should pray to God to guide one’s enemy towards the right path"; "6. If one performs a virtuous deed, one should consider that the source of the virtue is due either to one’s pir’s kindness, to the intercession of the Prophet Muhammad on one’s behalf, or to divine mercy"; "7. If one performs an evil deed one should consider one’s evil self responsible"; "8. Having fulfilled all the above conditions, one should regularly fast during the day and spend the night in prayer"; "9. One should remain quiet, and speak only when it is imperative to do so." **The numerals of items 1–3 are destroyed in the OCR — see Doubts. Items 1–4 are a prohibition on income, credit, begging and accumulation, which is exactly the economic question the Multan dispute reopens.**
- **The Shariʿa/Tariqa relation, stated twice by two different men.** The Khwaja: "according to the Shari‘a one who fully obeyed the commandments of God and abstained from what He had forbidden had, in fact, renounced the world. But the Tariga [OCR?] [Tariqa] prescribed nine conditions" (p. 141, folio 123). Hamidu’d-Din: "Shari‘a and Tariga [Tariqa] were related in the same way as the body and the soul" (p. 147, folio 129).
- **Worship redefined as service, and pilgrimage relativised.** "The distinguishing mark of one who had recognized God was his flight from crowds of people (khalq). The hajjis walked around the Ka‘ba, but the ‘arifs (spiritualists) circumambulated the heart. The most superior kind of worship was to assist the helpless and to feed the hungry" (p. 142, folio 124).
- **Celibacy abandoned late, and marriage justified as imitation of the Prophet.** "After finally settling at Ajmer, Khwaja Mu‘inu’d-Din, who until then had been celibate, took two wives. According to tradition he decided to marry in order to imitate all the Prophet’s practices" (p. 142, folio 124). "The wedding seems to have taken place in 606/1209-10" and the marriage lasted "twenty-seven years" (p. 142, folio 124). **Set against the *Masalikuʾl-ʿArifin* celibacy rule reported in chunk 006 — the book keeps both without comment.**
- **Fame and secrecy as a discipline, including the concealment of miracles.** "Personally hating notoriety of any kind, Shaikh Hamidu’d-Din asserted that sufis who sought fame in their own lifetime were soon forgotten. Those, however, who withdrew from worldly adulation achieved a long-lasting fame in death. One’s spiritual achievements and miraculous powers were also to be preserved from the gaze of the ordinary man" (p. 146, folio 128). **The Khwaja twice leaves a city because of crowds** — Herat ("His fame attracted a large number of people. He disapproved of such popularity and fame", p. 138, folio 120) and Delhi ("Once again crowds of followers and devotees gathered around the Khwaja and he was forced to leave Delhi for Ajmer", p. 140, folio 122).
- **The cash economy as a measure of sanctity, said satirically.** Hamidu’d-Din told a Delhi audience "that the greatest contemporary Shaikh was jital (copper coin), and that he who possessed jital in abundance was marked as a Shaikh. This analogy implied that piety was of little concern to anyone for people were only attracted to worldly riches" (p. 145, folio 127). **A named coin.**
- **Residence in a fort.** "It would appear that the Khwaja lived in Ajmer fort" (p. 141, folio 123).
- **The *zimmi* category, defined at length, with Hindus and Buddhists expressly at issue.** "Ahl al-Zimma, a non-Muslim subject of a Muslim state, such as Jews and Christians who paid the poll-tax and were regarded as protected subjects. Later Zoroastrians were also given the status of zimmis. Idolaters, however, were not regarded as zimmis. On the analogy that the idol worshippers of Arabia were not given the status of zimmis, many jurists also refused to regard Hindus or Buddhists as zimmis. From the time of the earliest conquest of the Arabs by Muhammad bin Qasim in 711-13, Hindus and Buddhists, however, have been recognized as zimmis" (p. 141, folio 123, footnote 1). **Directly relevant to how the archive's Hindu sites stood in Muslim law.**
- **Idleness as unbelief.** "Idleness was impiety (kafiri), and one should never be inactive for even a moment" (p. 147, folio 129); and on knowledge, "one who knew the law and acted contrary to it committed one sin, but a person who did not know the law and acted incorrectly was guilty of two" (p. 147, folio 129).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

The attributions below are the book's own, including its hedges ("apocryphal", "so the story goes", "The anecdote relates", "Legend relates", "According to tradition", "a stock-in-trade sufi story").

- **The prophecy against Prithviraj, and its fulfilment.** "he prophesied: ‘We have seized Pithaura alive and handed him over to the army of Islam.’ About the same time, Sultan Mu‘izzu'd-Din Muhammad's army arrived from Ghazna, attacked the forces of Pithaura and defeated them. Pithaura was taken alive, and thus the Khwaja’s prophesy was fulfilled" (p. 134, folio 116), from "Amir Khwurd, Siyaru’l-Auliya’, Delhi, 1885, pp. 45-7". "The Akhbaru’l-Akhyar also contains the same account" (p. 135, folio 117).
- **The long *Jawahir-i Faridi* cycle, given entire in one footnote and attributed to "‘Ali Asghar Chishti, Jawahir-i Faridi, Lahore, 1884, pp. 155-60"** (p. 135, folio 117): "Twelve years before the Khwaja's arrival at Ajmer, Pithaura’s mother, an expert in astronomy and magic, had prophesied the Khwaja's arrival"; his portrait circulated to the officers so "Every foreigner’s face was compared with that picture"; "The Khwaja stayed in Delhi with his forty disciples only because of his spiritual power"; "seven hundred people, besides Hamidu’d-Din Dihlawi, embraced Islam"; at Samana "the Prophet Muhammad had warned the Khwaja, during meditation, against the treachery of officials"; at Ajmer "the camel keepers ordered him away as the area belonged to the Ra’i"; "His servants killed a cow and cooked kebabs for him"; "There were one thousand temples on the two lakes"; "As soon as the ewer touched the Pansela Lake, all the lakes, tanks and wells around became dry"; the idol "Sawi Deva" made to "recite kalima" and "converted … into a human being, naming it Sa‘di"; "Jaipal proceeded to fight the Khwaja with 700 magical dragons, 1,500 magical discs and 700 disciples", all killed inside a protective circle; "The Khwaja’s prayers restored water to the lakes, tanks and wells"; Jaipal's flight "Sitting on his deer skin" and the Khwaja "ordered his slippers to bring Jaipal back to earth, which they did"; the joint ascent "to the highest heaven" and Jaipal's conversion "in order to gain the full benefit of that spiritual bliss".
- **The sesame seeds and the awakening.** "The majzub took out some sesame seeds, chewed them, and put them in Mu‘inu’d-Din’s mouth. This aroused in him a great spiritual illumination" (p. 137, folio 119).
- **‘Usman Harwani and the Zoroastrian fire temple.** "Shaikh ‘Usman asked if their hands were put into the fire would they be burnt. At the receipt of a negative reply the Shaikh snatched the boy and jumped into the fire with him. After some hours they both emerged unharmed. The head priest embraced Islam and the fire temple was demolished" (p. 137, folio 119, footnote 2). **The book records a variant with the actors changed: "In the KAairu'l-Majalis [Khairu'l-Majalis] the Zoroastrian priests are replaced by Hindus and the conversation is reported in the Hindawi."**
- **The crane at Balkh.** "he killed a crane and asked his servants to make a fire to roast it… As soon as the Maulana had eaten the leg he underwent a deep spiritual experience, after which all philosophical learnings were obliterated from his mind" (p. 139, folio 121), told with the book's hedge "so the story goes".
- **The terror of the Khwaja's presence at Sabzwar.** "Yadgar Muhammad came to the garden and the sight of the Khwaja frightened both him and the people with him. Yadgar placed his head at the Khwaja’s feet and his followers fell prostrate before him" (p. 139, folio 121, footnote 7). Rizvi calls it "a stock-in-trade sufi story".
- **Ram Deo's instant conversion, quoted from a modern author.** "Legend relates that in approaching the Khwaja, Ram Deo was so much impressed by his personality that he became, from that moment, a faithful disciple of the Khwaja" (p. 136, folio 118).
- **Conversion by the blessing of relics, at the tomb.** "Many prominent kafirs of the region accepted Islam because of the barakat (blessings) of the asar (relics, monuments or traditions) of that (embodiment of) sufism, meaning the Khwaja, who contained ‘the essence of the divine secrets’" (p. 140, folio 122). **The book glosses *asar* itself as "relics, monuments or traditions" — the ambiguity is the book's, not mine.**
- **Firishta's and Abu’l-Fazl's milder versions, set against each other.** "Firishta, however, says that a number of kafirs embraced Islam because of the Khwaja's spiritual blessings"; "According to Abu'l-Fazl the Khwaja: ‘lit a large number of (spiritual) lamps and crowds of people benefitted from his intuitive soul.’ Abu'l-Fazl's statement does not necessarily imply conversion. It would seem that the beneficiaries were the Muslims" (p. 140, folio 122, footnote 1).
- **The granting of a wish, and the disciple who asked for nothing.** "Khwaja Mu‘inu'd-Din asked his companions to request anything of God and it would be granted. Some asked for riches, others begged for a glorious eternity, but the Shaikh replied that he desired nothing as he had already surrendered himself to the divine will" — resulting in the title "Su/tanu’t-Tarikin [Sultanu’t-Tarikin] (King of Hermits)" (p. 145, folio 127).
- **Miracles to be hidden, in a deliberately shocking image.** "Miracles were to be compared, said the Shaikh, with what he called the ‘menstrual discharge of men’. As women maintained a veil of secrecy around their own menstrual flow, so should sufis hide from the public view their own supernatural powers" (p. 146, folio 128).
- **A royal birth attributed to a saint's intervention.** "the birth of the crown prince, Salim, which was attributed to the spiritual intervention of the famous Chishti saint, Shaikh Salim of Sikri" (p. 144, folio 126).
- **The Khwaja's own posthumous reputation, summarised by the book as a process.** The early anecdotes "tend to indicate that within about a hundred and fifty years of his death, the Khwaja had become a legend in India" (p. 134, folio 116).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The governing source-critical judgement of the chapter, stated in the first paragraph of the section.** "Both medieval and modern scholars have showered copious praise on Khwaja Mu‘inu’d-Din Chishti, but no reliable information regarding his early life, before he settled in Ajmer, remains. The only information recorded is the name of the area where he was born, the name of his teacher, and the fact that he had travelled widely" (p. 134, folio 116). **An argument from silence follows: "Strangely enough, the voluminous book, Khairu’l-Majalis, does not mention Khwaja Mu‘inu’d-Din in any connection, and the Fawa’idu’'l-Fu'ad refers to him merely in passing" (p. 134, folio 116).** And: "The earliest works which relate anecdotes of the early life of Khwaja Mu‘inu’d-Din Chishti and his encounters with the court of Prithviraj at Ajmer are the apocryphal malfuzats" (p. 134, folio 116).
- **The Prithviraj encounter declared chronologically impossible on the testimony of two Mughal sources.** After quoting Abu’l-Fazl's *Akbar Nama* and *Aʿin-i Akbari*, the footnote concludes: "Both latter accounts make the Khwaja’s encounter with Prithviraj an impossibility" (p. 135, folio 117), reinforced by "Muhammad Sadiq Dihlawi says that in the year in which Sultan Mu‘izzu'd-Din defeated Raja Pithaura and seized Delhi the Khwaja reached Lahore from Ghazni" and "So Firishta’s account that the Khwaja reached Ajmer after its conquest concurs with Abul-Fazl’s" (p. 135, folio 117). **This is the chunk's central historiographical demolition, and it cuts directly at the foundation legend of the Chishti order in India.**
- **A sustained attack on a named living scholar, and on a communal reading of Indian history.** Of K.A. Nizami: "It is obvious that in his story of Hindu mistrust of Islam, Nizami borrows from al-Biruni, who relates only the situation created by the plundering raids of Mahmud of Ghazna"; "Although Nizami admits that authentic details were unavailable, he did not hesitate to claim that Prithviraj and the high caste Hindus disliked the Khwaja nevertheless the common people flocked to him. This only indicates the thinking of modern Muslims who seek the support of a theory that the caste system was responsible for the Hindu defeat in India and that by it Islam rescued the low caste Hindus from degredation"; and the closing question, "How could Hindus amongst whom Muslims lived look ‘‘askance at every foreigner?”" (p. 136, folio 118). **The quoted authors are introduced as "A modern author states rather naively" (p. 135, folio 117) and "A more quaint appraisal of the Khwaja's work by another modern Muslim scholar" (p. 136, folio 118).**
- **Jamali defended as a source despite his legends.** "the anecdotes which his hosts in Irani khangahs [khanqahs] related are not necessarily correct, but they are set in the perspective of contemporary mystical traditions, and Jamali cannot be censured for accepting them" (p. 136, folio 118); and the verdict, "Despite some discrepancies noted in footnotes, and his acceptance of stock-in-trade anecdotes of sufism, Jamali’s research on the Khwaja’s career appears, to a large degree, to be factual" (p. 140, folio 122).
- **The two accounts declared irreconcilable, with a political and a psychological reason for preferring one.** "The inconsistencies between the Chishti legend and Jamali’s study are irreconcilable, but the latter’s account is supported to a greater degree by political events and the Khwaja’s own philosophy of what entailed a saintly life" (p. 140, folio 122). The political argument: Tarain in 1192 "failed to make the Turks masters of the whole of northern India"; "It was only around 1206 that continual Turkic raids finally liquidated Rajput resistance"; "Moreover after Mu‘izzu’d-Din’s death, Lahore had become the capital, and this would naturally have impelled Khwaja Mu‘inu’d-Din to seek another home" (p. 140, folio 122).
- **Ajmer chosen as a periphery, on the model of Chisht — Rizvi's structural claim about where Chishtis settle.** "he left both Lahore and Delhi and ultimately chose Ajmer, on the borders of the Delhi Sultanate, for this purpose. By so doing, he followed the tradition of the founders of the Chishti silsila, who had chosen for their activities remote Chisht, rather than other great centres of political power either in Iran or Central Asia" (pp. 140–141, folios 122–123). **This directly contradicts the Nizami passage Rizvi quotes and rejects, which made Ajmer a deliberately chosen centre of "political and religious significance" (p. 136, folio 118).**
- **Conversion described as forcible, in the author's own voice.** The Khwaja's ascetic life "was an inspiration to both the Turkic ghazis, who swelled the Islamic forces through a lust for plunder, and to the Hindus who were forcibly converted to Islam" (p. 141, folio 123). **Unhedged, unsourced, and in the same sentence as a favourable characterisation of the saint.**
- **Dating arguments made against named sources, one by one.** The Ghuzz invasion "began in 564/1168-69… they were therefore not responsible for the Khwaja’s migration from Sistan", nor were the Ismaʿili incursions after 523/1129: "It would seem that neither of the two raids were the cause of the Khwaja’s migration. He was prompted to devote his life to education and to spiritual perfection through his introspective temperament, and the visit of Khwaja Ibrahim would have been only a minor incentive" (p. 137, folio 119, footnote 2); "Amir Khwurd’s statement that he served as Shaikh ‘Usman's apprentice for twenty years does not correlate with the Khwaja’s visit to Shaikh ‘Abdu'l-Qadir" (p. 137, folio 119, footnote 2); "as he died in 697/1298, the meeting would have been impossible" (of Auhadu’d-Din Kirmani, p. 138, folio 120, footnote 4); "Jamali's statement is confusing" (p. 138, folio 120, footnote 5); "The Sururu’s-Sudur states he was then ninety, but this would appear to be incorrect" (p. 142, folio 124).
- **An anecdote rejected on grounds of administrative plausibility.** "Undoubtedly this anecdote is apocryphal. Firstly, no muqgta‘ [muqta‘] would dare to harass the Khwaja to gain a farman for wasteland. Secondly, the Khwaja could have sent a messenger to Qutbu’d-Din Bakhatiyar and gained a farman without personally going to Delhi" (p. 143, folio 125).
- **A second, political reason offered for the Delhi visits.** "The latter made a further trip there apparently in order to be personally acquainted with the struggle between the ‘ulama’ and the Chishtis and the general crisis brewing in the life of sufis in Delhi" (p. 143, folio 125). **A standing ʿulamaʾ–Chishti conflict is asserted here and deferred to a later part of the book.**
- **Ajmer's later political value to the empire, distinguished from its devotional value.** "Akbar’s earlier visit to Ajmer had been as a devotee of the Khwaja. Later the location of Ajmer in the heart of the Rajput states and on the route to Gujarat, which Akbar was to later conquer, gave Ajmer a new political significance" (p. 144, folio 126). **A two-stage account of imperial shrine patronage: devotion first, strategy after.**
- **The Khwaja's doctrine placed in a named lineage.** "His sufi views were based on the statements of Abu Sa‘id bin Abi’! Khair [Abi’l Khair], Khwaja ‘Abdullah Ansari and ‘Ainu’l-Quzat Hamadani, which involved a belief in the concept of an ecstatic love for God. This, according to the Khwaja, did not allow a differentiation between the lover, the beloved and love itself" (p. 142, folio 124).
- **Hamidu’d-Din's grading of faith, and his refusal to multiply faiths.** "the faith of the ignorant amounted to having fled from sin and performing obedience; the middle path consisted in abandoning the world and finding solace in thoughts of the rewards in an after-life; but the most superior kind of faith was held only by those who closely followed the life of the Prophet… However, these distinctions did not mean that there were various kinds of faiths. Faith was one entity and the differences consisted only between various kinds of men" (p. 147, folio 129). **The sentence is cut off at the chunk boundary.**

## Doubts

**Archive-mapping doubts:**

- **`tomb-of-qutbuddin-aibak` and a second, different Qutbuʾd-Din three pages later.** The book names "Qutbu’d-Din Aibak" (pp. 139, 140; folios 121, 122) and, separately, "Khwaja Qutbu’d-Din Bakhtiyar", a sufi who housed the Khwaja at Delhi and interceded with Iltutmish (p. 143, folio 125). **They are plainly two men in the book — one a slave-commander, one a Shaikh — and I have mapped only the first. A consolidator merging shortened forms must be careful: p. 140 (folio 122) says simply "Qutbu’d-Din had appointed Saiyid Husain Mashhadi", and p. 143 says simply "Qutbu’d-Din had never previously visited him", and those two bare occurrences refer to different people.** Note also that the row `tomb-of-qutbuddin-aibak` is categorised "Secular / Memorial" and located at Lahore, whereas this chunk places Aibak at Delhi and Ghazni and gives him no tomb, no death date and no Lahore — the mapping rests on the person, not on the site.
- **"Shaikh Jalalu’d-Din Tabrizi" could be read against two archive rows and I am not resolving it.** The archive has `shrine-of-jalaluddin-surkh-posh-bukhari-jalaluddin-bukhari` ("Sayyid Jalaluddin Surkh-Posh Bukhari", Uch Sharif, d. 1291, Suhrawardi) and `shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht` ("Sayyid Jalaluddin", Uch Sharif, d. 1384, Suhrawardi). **The book prints the nisba "Tabrizi", which matches neither row, and gives him no dates, no place of burial and no order; it also makes him the disciple-line of "Shaikh Abu Sa‘id Tabrizi" (p. 138, folio 120) and the subject of charges at a Delhi mahzar (p. 146, folio 128). I have therefore made no mapping — but I am flagging rather than dismissing, because the given name matches two rows exactly and the book defers his treatment to "pp. 199-200, injra [infra]", which is outside this chunk.**
- **Three nisba-only near-collisions, none mapped, all flagged because the rule says to flag rather than pick.** (i) "Shaikh Rashid Makki", whose grave marks the Khwaja's Delhi lodging (p. 140, folio 122), against `peer-makki` ("Hazrat Syed Azizuddin al-Hassani wal-Hussaini (Peer Makki)", Lahore, d. 1215) — different given name, different city, **not mapped**. (ii) "Shaikh Muhammad Bukhari", Akbar's shrine administrator at Ajmer (p. 144, folio 126), against the five Bukhari rows in the index, nearest by name being `shrine-of-mauj-darya-bukhari` ("Hazrat Meeran Muhammad Shah Bukhari") and `shrine-of-hazrat-muhammad-ayub-shah-bukhari` — **not mapped**. (iii) "Shaikh Auhadu’d-Din Kirmani" (p. 138, folio 120), against `shergarh` ("Daud Bandagi Kirmani", d. 1575) and the Kirmani name in the silsila field of `darbar-abul-muali-qadri` — the book gives Auhadu’d-Din's death as 697/1298, **not mapped**.
- **"Shaikh Jamal … at Kol (Aligarh)" (p. 139, folio 121) is exactly the collision case the protocol names.** The index has `shah-jamal` ("Syed Shah Jamal Uddin Naqvi Bukhari", Lahore, d. 1671) and `shrine-of-hafiz-muhammad-jamal-multani` ("Hafiz Muhammad Jamal Multani", d. 1811). **The book's Shaikh Jamal is a descendant of Shaikh Nizamu’d-Din Abu’l-Muy’id who died at Aligarh, which fits neither, and the author "Jamali" of the *Siyaruʾl-ʿArifin* is a third distinct name. Not mapped; flagged.**
- **Khwaja Mu‘inu’d-Din Chishti is the chunk's main subject and is NOT an archive row — this must not be "fixed" downstream.** The nearest thing in the index is `khawaja-feroz-ud-din-gharib-nawaz` ("Hazrat Khawaja Feroz-ud-Din Gharib Nawaz Chishti Nizami", Miani Sahib Graveyard, Lahore, died 1981), which carries the epithet *Gharib Nawaz*. **The book never prints "Gharib Nawaz" anywhere in pp. 134–147, and that row's figure died in 1981, so there is no collision in the text — but a consolidator working from epithets rather than from the printed page could easily create one, and the whole Ajmer body of material would then land on the wrong row. Ajmer has no id in this index and none has been invented.**
- **The unruled toponym convention (a) costs this chunk one genuinely substantive link: Sabzwar.** The book devotes most of a page to Sabzwar as a place — "Sabzwar was notorious for Shi‘i sympathies", "a hotbed of schism, its inhabitants being all fanatics", and the Badaʾuni anecdote about the search for a man named Abu Bakr, with two lines of Rumi quoted (pp. 138–139, folios 120–121). **The archive row `shrine-of-shah-shams-ud-din-sabzwari` (Multan) names its principal figure "Shah Shams-ud-Din Sabzwari", whose nisba is this town.** Under the interim convention I have left Sabzwar as a bare toponym under "Other saints, sites and events" and mapped nothing. **If the toponym ruling reverses, this is the row it would take — and it would then be the only place in the chunk where reversing the ruling adds real content, because the book's characterisation of Sabzwar as a Shiʿi centre bears directly on how that row's saint is read.** The book does not connect the two and neither do I.
- **Lahore and Multan under the same convention.** Lahore appears as a bare toponym at pp. 135, 140 and 145 (folios 117, 122, 127) — "the Khwaja reached Delhi from Lahore", "Lahore had become the capital", "His father, Ahmad, migrated from Lahore to Delhi" — and as the site of two tombs at p. 139 (folio 121), where it is mapped through `data-darbar` and `shrine-of-miran-hussain-zanjani-zanjani-sahib`. **Lahore alone cannot select among the index's many Lahore rows, so reversing the ruling would not resolve the bare mentions in any case.** Multan appears only attached to Bahaʾuʾd-Din Zakariyya (p. 146, folio 128) and is mapped through him.
- **The deity question arises twice and I have mapped neither.** (i) "Sawi Deva", the named idol of "the Anasagar Lake temple" at Ajmer, "converted … into a human being, naming it Sa‘di" (p. 135, folio 117) — a named Hindu cult object, in a legend, at a site outside the archive; **not mapped, per the standing instruction not to map a deity.** (ii) **"Ram Deo", "head of the priests of Ajmer" (p. 136, folio 118), is a harder case worth a human's eye.** In the book he is a man, quoted from Yusuf Husain's modern account, not a deity — but the index row `ramapir-temple-tando-allahyar` names its principal figure "Ramdev Pir (Ramapir)", a Rajasthani figure whose name is the same, and this passage is set in Rajasthan. **I have not mapped it and do not think it should be mapped on this evidence, but the coincidence of name and region is close enough that it should be recorded rather than silently dropped.** The book also names "Muhammad bin Qasim" (p. 141, folio 123) and "Mahmud of Ghazna" (p. 136, folio 118); neither is an archive row's principal figure.

**The brief's chapter boundaries, which I cannot confirm and which the book gives indirect evidence about:**

- **The brief says Chapter Two, "The Chishtis", "opens at p. 132 and runs to p. 207", without saying whether those are PDF pages or printed folios, and nothing inside pp. 134–147 settles it.** What the chunk does confirm is that these fourteen pages are inside the chapter (recto header "The Chishtis" on all seven rectos) and that the chapter began before p. 134, since the chunk opens in mid-sentence. **Two pieces of indirect evidence favour reading the brief's numbers as PDF pages, i.e. printed folios 114–189.** First, the book's own cross-references are to printed folios, not PDF pages: "see pp. 86-8, supra" (p. 138, folio 120) points back to the Suhrawardi material that chunk 006 recorded at folios 88–90, which is a printed-folio reference. Second, the forward reference "See pp. 199-200, injra [infra]" for the charges against Shaikh Jalalu’d-Din Tabrizi (p. 146, folio 128) sends the reader to printed pp. 199–200; **if the chapter ended at printed folio 189 (PDF 207), that forward reference lands in the following chapter, which is where a Suhrawardi figure such as Jalalu’d-Din Tabrizi would belong — whereas if the chapter ran to printed folio 207, the reference would fall inside the Chishti chapter itself.** This is an inference from the book, not a reading of a header, and I flag it as such: **a worker on the chunk containing PDF 207 (folio 189) should check the header there and settle it.** I am not contradicting the brief, only recording that it is ambiguous and that the evidence in my pages leans one way.

**Internal contradictions in the chunk, kept side by side rather than resolved:**

- **The Khwaja's apprenticeship is twenty years in one account and two and a half in another, and the book flags the problem only obliquely.** "he then served his master for twenty years without a moment's rest" (p. 134, folio 116, from the *Siyaruʾl-Auliyaʾ*) against "he practised rigorous austerities for about two and a half years" (p. 137, folio 119, from Jamali). Rizvi's footnote addresses the twenty years only as a chronological problem — "Amir Khwurd’s statement that he served as Shaikh ‘Usman's apprentice for twenty years does not correlate with the Khwaja’s visit to Shaikh ‘Abdu'l-Qadir" — and then offers a reconciliation, "It is, however, not unlikely that the Khwaja met his teacher again in Khurasan and Central Asia and stayed with him for a few more years" (p. 137, folio 119). **The two-and-a-half-year figure is not mentioned in that footnote.** Note also that "about two and a half years" is the same span the book gives for ‘Usman Harwani's own stay at the fire temple (p. 137, folio 119, footnote 2).
- **Three death years for the Khwaja appear in the chunk.** "6 Rajab 633 Hijri" (p. 137, folio 119) and "On 6 Rajab 633/16 March 1236" (p. 143, folio 125), against **"After the death of the Khwaja in 1234"** in the Yusuf Husain passage Rizvi quotes at length and criticises for its tone but not for its date (p. 136, folio 118). **The 1234 figure must not enter an entry as the book's own.**
- **Two birth years, both printed without preference resolved.** "he must have been born in 536/1141-42" (Rizvi's own back-calculation) and "Abu’l Fazl says he was born in 537/1142-43" (p. 137, folio 119).
- **The marriage age is left contradictory, though the book rejects one figure.** "The Sururu’s-Sudur states he was then ninety, but this would appear to be incorrect", with the wedding placed at "606/1209-10" and the marriage lasting "twenty-seven years" (p. 142, folio 124). **Against the book's own birth year of 536 and death year of 633, a wedding in 606 makes him seventy, not ninety, and twenty-seven years of marriage reaches 633 exactly — so the book's rejection of "ninety" is consistent with its other figures. That is my arithmetic on the book's printed numbers, offered as a check and not as a fact for an entry.**
- **The succession case turns on a claim the chunk itself refutes two pages earlier.** At Akbar's court "The Shaikh’s opponents alleged that the Khwaja had left no descendants" (p. 144, folio 126), while p. 142 (folio 124) states that both wives "are said to have borne the Khwaja children" and names three sons. **The book does not note the tension, and it also does not say whether the opponents' allegation was accepted — only that "Akbar ruled against the Shaikh".**
- **Whether the Khwaja arrived at a tomb or at a living man is left open for Zanjani.** Jamali has Zanjani alive and Hujwiri newly dead; the *Fawaʾiduʾl-Fuʾad* has Hujwiri arriving in the year Zanjani died; Abu’l-Fazl has the Khwaja living *with* Zanjani; Rizvi concludes "it is likely that the Khwaja stayed near the two tombs"; and Firishta "simply mentions that after leaving Ghazna, the Khwaja stayed at Lahore" (p. 139, folio 121, footnote 3). **Four positions on one page. This is the evidential state of the only Lahore material in the chunk, and both mapped Lahore rows depend on it.**
- **Ajmer is both a deliberate periphery and a deliberate centre, in Rizvi's text and in the text he quotes.** Rizvi: the Khwaja "chose Ajmer, on the borders of the Delhi Sultanate", following founders who chose "remote Chisht, rather than other great centres of political power" (pp. 140–141, folios 122–123). Nizami, quoted: "Ajmer was not merely the seat of Chauhan power; it was religious centre also where thousands of pilgrims assembled from far and near. Shaikh Mu‘inu'd-Din's determination to work out the principles of Islamic mysticism at a place of such political and religious significance shows great self-confidence" (p. 136, folio 118). **Rizvi quotes this in order to attack its communal reasoning, but he does not answer its claim that Ajmer was already a major pilgrimage centre — a claim that matters for how the shrine's later economy is read.**
- **"Hamidu’d-Din" may be one man or two, and the book does not say.** "Hamidu’d-Din Dihlawi", a Delhi convert in the *Jawahir-i Faridi* legend (p. 135, folio 117), and "Shaikh Hamidu’d-Din"/"Hamidu'd-Din Nagauri", the Khwaja's disciple who was born at Delhi (pp. 141, 145; folios 123, 127). **The chunk supplies no statement either way.**
- **Three different "Abu Saʿid"s appear within five pages and must not be merged** — "Shaikh Abu Sa‘id bin Abi’l Khair", whose tomb at Mayhana the Khwaja visited (p. 138, folio 120); "Shaikh Abu Sa‘id Tabrizi", a living teacher at Tabriz (p. 138, folio 120); and "Shaikh Abu Sa‘id", the Khwaja's eldest son (p. 142, folio 124).
