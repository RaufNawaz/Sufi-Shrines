## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The khilafat-nama as a physical, signed, counter-signed and forgeable document — the chunk's richest institutional seam, and the archive's best evidence on how Chishti succession was actually administered.** Copies were prepared by Maulana Badru'd-Din Ishaq, "who prepared copies of the khilafat-nama was required to sign them, and they were later endorsed by the Baba's senior disciple, Shaikh Jamalu'd-Din, who lived at Hansi" (p. 167, folio 149). The reason given is a forgery threat by a rejected disciple (pp. 166–167, folios 148–149). The counter-signature was a real veto: "Once Shaikh Jamal refused to endorse the khilafat-nama which the Baba had granted a disciple, under some pressure. Shaikh Jamal was so angry that he tore the paper containing the khilafat-nama to pieces, while informing the disciple he was unworthy of such an honour. Baba Farid supported Shaikh Jamalu'd-Din's actions telling the disciple he was unable to mend what Jamal had rent apart" (p. 171, folio 153). **A document made of paper, signed by a scribe-disciple, endorsed by a senior khalifa, capable of being torn up, and known to be worth forging.**
- **The content of an actual khilafat-nama, summarised.** "It recorded that Muhammad, son of Ahmad, had studied the Tamhidu'l-Muhtadi of Abu Shakur with great precision under his direction, and was now authorized to teach. Moreover, he was permitted to disseminate the Baba's teachings he had acquired and was advised to lead an ascetic life" (p. 175, folio 157). Its holder was told to show it to two named men in two named towns, "Maulana Jamalu'd-Din at Hansi, and to Qazi Muntajabu'd-Din at Delhi" (p. 175, folio 157). "The copy of the khilafat-nama given to him by the Baba is also preserved" (p. 166, folio 148).
- **Initiation: the valid form and the condemned form, stated as a rule against shrine-side ritual.** "the initiation of a disciple should be performed personally by his preceptor. He was opposed to the initiation customs involving head shaving rituals at the tombs of sufi saints. When one of his own sons performed such a ceremony at the foot of Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din Bakhtiyar's tomb, his father remarked that such an initiation was invalid. True initiations involved the disciple holding the Shaikh's hand, while it was usual for his pir to offer blessings and prayers for the disciple's welfare" (p. 166, folio 148). **This is a direct, dated objection to initiating at a saint's tomb — and the archive documents exactly such tombs.** See Doubts on who "the Shaikh" is here.
- **Head-shaving is nevertheless the form Baba Farid himself used, twice.** "Nizamu'd-Din was immediately initiated and his head shaved" (p. 174, folio 156); and on re-initiation, "The Shaikh renewed his initiation, 'Arif shaved his head and once more firmly accepted the sufi path" (p. 172, folio 154). **The chunk therefore holds an objection to head-shaving at tombs alongside head-shaving before a living pir, without comment.**
- **Futuh as the working income of a jama'at-khana, named four times.** "Gradually, however, the Shaikh obtained some futuh and was able to afford both paper and wages and the book was transcribed" (p. 169, folio 151); at Ghiyaspur, "No regular futuh was received, and only a zambil provided the Shaikh and a few disciples with sustenance" (p. 177, folio 159); "He sent futwh [OCR?] [futuh] and also made an offer of some villages" (p. 177, folio 159); "as with other futuhs, distributed the money amongst both the needy townsfolk and dervishes" (p. 179, folio 161).
- **A land grant offered by a sultan and refused, against the advice of starving disciples — the clearest statement in the chunk of the Chishti position on endowment.** Jalalu'd-Din Khalji "was informed of the deprived circumstances of the Shaikh and his disciples. He sent futwh [futuh] and also made an offer of some villages. Starvation having driven them to the point of death, a number of disciples urged the Shaikh to accept the gift. Consulting the Kirmanis and some of the senior disciples of Baba Farid, they advised Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din to reject the offer. Delighted, the Shaikh replied that he was uncaring if all his disciples deserted him, his concern was only for them" (pp. 177–178, folios 159–160).
- **A very large cash gift accepted from a contested ruler and immediately given away, followed by a demand for its return.** "Khusraw paid large sums of money to the sufis and dervishes of Delhi to pray for his success. Some of them accepted the payment, keeping it safely so that it could be returned to the next ruler if Khusraw lost. Three eminent dervishes refused financial gifts; Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din, however, accepted 500,000 tankas offered him and, as with other futuhs, distributed the money amongst both the needy townsfolk and dervishes" (p. 179, folio 161). Then Ghiyasu'd-Din Tughluq "ordered the recipients of Khushraw's [Khusraw's] money to refund it to the treasury. Some sufis acceded to his wish; Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din replied to the Sultan that the money belonged to the treasury of the Muslims which he had himself distributed among the poor, while expending nothing on himself" (p. 179, folio 161). **A named sum, a named practice of holding the money in escrow against a change of ruler, and a state clawback.**
- **The same ethic in miniature, and a named quantity.** Qalandars "raised five hundred silver tankas among themselves, and presented the money to the Shaikh to be used for his own needs" and "the Shaikh distributed the same day the entire amount among the poor" (p. 170, folio 152), on the maxim "'if you get money, spend it, for there is no dearth of gifts; if it goes away, do not store it for fear of not getting it again,'" (p. 170, folio 152).
- **A mosque-and-house appointment, and its withdrawal over a wedding — patronage as a revocable employment.** "Shaikh Najibu'd-Din accepted the post of Imam of a mosque built by a Turkic officer in Delhi who also gave the Shaikh a house to live in. After the Turk spent 100,000 jira/s [OCR?] [jitals] at his daughter's wedding, the Shaikh suggested that if this money were used on God's work, it would earn a large amount of spiritual reward. Indignant, the Turk dismissed him from the mosque and seized the house" (p. 169, folio 151); afterwards "a Turk called Aitkar… cared for the Shaikh's family" (p. 169, folio 151).
- **A khatib's property abandoned on becoming a disciple.** Shaikh Jamalu'd-Din "had been the khatib of Hansi, owning villages and extensive property. Discipleship with the Baba meant abandoning material prosperity" (p. 171, folio 153) — and when his poverty "became overwhelming", the Baba replied "that it was Jamalu'd-Din's duty to bear the spiritual burden assigned to him" (p. 171, folio 153).
- **The jama'at-khana as a legal sanctuary.** Of Shaikh 'Ala'u'd-Din, "fugitives who took refuge in his jama'at-khana were never extradited to the government" (p. 167, folio 149).
- **Physical infrastructure built for visitors on a pilgrimage road — the chunk's one piece of shrine-adjacent construction for the living.** "From Delhi to Ghiyaspur, platforms with thatched roofs were constructed with wells dug for water for the performance of ablutions before prayers of the Shaikh's visitors. Senior disciples exercised a profound influence over junior ones, and an atmosphere of serenity and spirituality surrounded the area" (p. 178, folio 160).
- **The social composition of a fourteenth-century following, listed.** "A large number of disciples were attracted to Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya'. These included the powerful and the common, the rich, poor, learned and illiterate, townsfolk, villagers, soldiers, warriors, freemen and slaves. Naturally many could not become disciples of the Shaikh, but their contact with him undoubtedly added a new dimension to an understanding of Islamic ideals of morality and religious observances" (p. 178, folio 160).
- **Tombs built by sultans over sufis' graves, three instances in this chunk.** "Sultan Mahmud Khalji of Malwa erected a tomb over his grave" (Shaikh Kamalu'd-Din, p. 167, folio 149); "Muhammad bin Tughluq built a tomb for the Shaikh after his death near his grandfather's mausoleum" (Shaikh 'Ala'u'd-Din, p. 167, folio 149); over Najibu'd-Din's grave, "A structure was erected on it by Sultan Muhammad bin Tughluq" (p. 170, folio 152). **No dates, costs or descriptions for any of the three** — (not stated).
- **Popular tomb devotion recorded as fact for one site.** Sabir's tomb at Kaliyar "was quite famous, and he had many devotees" at the time the Akhbaru'l-Akhyar was written, and there is a "story of the rediscovery of the Shaikh's tomb in Shah-Jahan's reign" (p. 172, folio 154).
- **Touching and kissing a living saint, with a crowd.** "On a day of 'Id, when the Shaikh was returning from prayers, a large crowd followed, kissing his hands and touching his garment" (p. 170, folio 152).
- **Sama' as a formal legal proceeding — a mahzar with a counted bench.** "the Sultan organized a mahzar. Over two hundred and fifty scholars were lined up to oppose the Shaikh who attended with his disciples, including Maulana Fakru'd-Din Zarradi [OCR?] [Fakhru'd-Din Zarradi]. The latter, having written a book on the subject, believed he was well prepared to answer the objections of the orthodox. Qazi Ruknu'd-Din Walwalji, an inveterate enemy of the Shaikh, who enjoyed dominating any situation, led the discussion" (p. 180, folio 162). The procedural objection: "the Qazi objected to his argument on the grounds that the Shaikh was not a mujtahid and therefore not entitled to quote hadis as an authority. Instead he should support his argument from the statement of Imam Abu Hanifa (699-767)" (p. 180, folio 162). The verdict: "Sultan Ghiyasu'd-Din Tughlug [Tughluq] refused to give a judgement, and the Shaikh and his followers were allowed to depart honourably" (p. 180, folio 162), and "later dismissed Qazi Ruknu'd-Din Walwalji" (p. 180, folio 162).
- **The comparative-practice defence of sama', argued from travel.** Maulana 'Alamu'd-Din "had travelled to Mecca, Medina, Egypt and Syria. Everywhere he had been, he argued, sufis indulged in sama'; there was no objection in those areas to its use by those who knew its merits. As the Shaikh and his companions were holy men, endowed with ethical and ascetic qualities of a high calibre, argued Maulana 'Alamu'd-Din, no objection could be raised against their indulgence in sama'" (p. 180, folio 162).
- **Zikr recorded in a household, by slave girls.** In Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya's khanqah "even his slave girls performed constant zikr" (p. 173, folio 155).
- **Qawwals as the carriers of a saint's reputation between cities.** The report that first attached Nizamu'd-Din to Baba Farid came "through a qawwal (a musician) who had been to both Multan and Ajodhan"; afterwards "Nizamu'd-Din was so excited that after each prayer he began to repeat the name of Baba Farid" (p. 173, folio 155).
- **Qalandars as a recognised group with money of their own.** "Some dervishes, probably qalandars, were greatly impressed" (p. 170, folio 152); "The poverty of Shaikh Najibu'd-Din aroused pity even among the qalandars" (p. 170, folio 152).
- **A state attempt to compel congregational attendance, and a refusal.** Mubarak Shah "prohibited his nobles from visiting Ghiyaspur" and "also constructed a mosque, the Masjid-i Miri, where all the sufis and 'ulama' were ordered to perform their prayers. The Shaikh refused to comply with the Sultan's orders, remonstrating that the mosque in his neighbourhood had a greater claim on him" (pp. 178–179, folios 160–161). He also evaded the monthly homage: "The Shaikh further angered the Sultan by sending a servant as his delegate" (p. 179, folio 161).
- **Praying at a mother's tomb — a small but concrete act of grave visitation by a saint.** Threatened with "serious consequences if he personally failed to pay homage", "the Shaikh quietly prayed at his mother's tomb and returned to his jama'at-khana" (p. 179, folio 161).
- **Certification of learning as a parallel document to the khilafat-nama, dated.** "On 22 Rabi' I 679/22 July 1280, the Maulana gave the Shaikh a certificate confirming that he had studied under him the Mashariqu'l-Anwar consisting of the most significant aspects of the Sahih- Bukhari and the Sahih-Muslim, diligently and perceptively. The certificate also referred to Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din as Magqbul al-Masha'ikh al-Kibar [OCR?] [Maqbul al-Masha'ikh al-Kibar] (Approved One of the Great Sufis)" (p. 177, folio 159).
- **A named refusal of a sultan's imamate.** "Sultan Balban desired the Maulana to act as his Imam and lead his prayers. The Maulana, however, refused, informing the Sultan that the only thing left to him were his own prayers which he did not wish to waste by acting as the Sultan's Imam" (p. 177, folio 159). Compare Maulana 'Ala'u'd-Din Usuli, who "refused to accept gifts and starved himself incessantly. He generally ate seeds out of which the oil had previously been extracted" (p. 172, folio 154, footnote 6).
- **A saint's lodging arrangements as a housing history, with a three-storey plan.** "The house was a large three-storeyed one, near the bastion of the Delhi fort. The lower storey was occupied by the Kirmani family, the grandfather and the father of Amir Khwurd. The middle storey was used by the Shaikh and the third by other sufis. Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din was served by the Kirmanis. After about two years, Rawat-i 'Arz's sons returned without warning and evicted the Shaikh. The Kirmanis carried the Shaikh's only possession, a bundle of books, toa [to a] small thatched mosque situated in front of the house of Siraj Baqqal" (p. 176, folio 158), until "they finally settled with Shamsu'd-Din Sharabdar" (p. 176, folio 158).
- **How a settlement grew around a saint — Ghiyaspur in three stages.** "a small village near Kilukhari, then virtually unknown to the citizens of Delhi" (p. 177, folio 159); then, when Kaiqubad "made Kilukhari, about two kilometres from Ghiyaspur, his new capital. Consequently Ghiyaspur became a much busier suburb of Delhi" (p. 177, folio 159); then under 'Ala'u'd-Din Khalji, "Ghiyaspur became a prosperous suburb of Delhi" (p. 178, folio 160).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

The attributions are the book's own, including its hedges ("is said to have", "are legendary", "prophesied", "This myth").

- **The desert prophecy fulfilled by two deaths.** Baba Farid "begged them not to go through the desert but to take another route. Ignoring his advice, they proceeded to travel along the desert route. Shortly afterwards, news was brought that the four had died from heat exhaustion and the fifth, who finally reached a well, drank so much water that he also died" (p. 164, folio 146).
- **The withheld prayer that foretold a death.** "The Shaikh made nineteen visits to Baba Farid at Ajodhan, and on each occasion the Baba prayed that there might be a further visit. On the nineteenth he refrained from his usual prayer, and a few months before the Baba's death Shaikh Najibu'd-Din died" (p. 170, folio 152).
- **The Baba's foreknowledge of his own death, spoken to his khalifa.** "I have handed over all these things to you for at the time of my death you will not be present.'" (p. 175, folio 157).
- **A saint's teaching miracle.** Of Shadi Muqri, "His teaching was considered to be miraculous and enabled his students to master the Qur'an perfectly" (p. 172, folio 154).
- **The turban prophecy of 'Ali Maula, with its two stated reasons.** "So touched was the Shaikh that he prophesied Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din's future prominence as a saint. There were two reasons which prompted this 'Ali Maula stated: firstly, Nizamu'd-Din had prostrated himself before his elders; and secondly, his turban was completely made of cotton, without a single silken thread, thus proving his simplicity and piety" (p. 173, folio 155).
- **The water of blessing — poverty turned into a karama by the guests themselves.** "The Shaikh took a glass of water and stood with it before the dervishes. Being spiritualists also, they understood, and drank the water in order to receive the giver's blessing" (p. 170, folio 152).
- **The melon-and-turban vow that freed a prisoner.** "Removing his turban, the Maulana emphatically stated he would neither eat the melon nor wear his turban unless Sharafu'd-Din was released. The latter returned to the jama'at-khana soon afterwards" (p. 171, folio 153).
- **The prophecy of victory at Warangal, confirmed the same day.** "Reassuring him, Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din prophesied victory, at the same time extending hopes for an even greater success in the future. The same day news of the army's defeat of the enemy was received from Malik Na'ib" (p. 178, folio 160).
- **The month that never came — the Sultan's death read as the answer to a threat.** "But the first day of the next month did not come for the Sultan, he was assassinated on ihe [OCR?] [the] first night of the Jumada II, 720/8 July 1320 by his favourite and protégée, Khusraw Khan Barwar" (p. 179, folio 161).
- **The prophecy of calamity, which Rizvi both reports and corrects in his own footnote.** "he prophesied that some calamity was likely to befall the capital" (p. 180, folio 162), footnoted: "Similar prophesies are aiso [also] ascribed to other saints. The Shaikh himself, however, did not quote a Hadis but an opinion expressed by Ghazali" (p. 180, folio 162, footnote 4).
- **"Delhi is still far away", which Rizvi calls a myth.** "the Shaikh is reported to have remarked, 'Delhi is still far away'. This myth became popular mainly because of Sultan Ghiyasu'd-Din Tughluq's sudden death caused by the collapse of the Afghanpur pavilion hurriedly built for a grand reception" (p. 180, folio 162).
- **The crescent answer, on appointing a child khalifa.** "The servant pointed out that Badru'd-Din was very young but the Shaikh replied that the crescent of Islam was also small" (p. 171, folio 153). **The boy is named Burhanu'd-Din two sentences earlier** — see Doubts.
- **The miracles of Sabir, and the rediscovery of his tomb, both dismissed.** "The story of the rediscovery of the Shaikh's tomb in Shah-Jahan's reign, and the account of the miracles attributed to the Shaikh described by Ilahdiya, are legendary" (p. 172, folio 154).
- **Baba Farid's detection of a hidden theft, without being told.** Of the hundred tankas of which 'Arif kept fifty: "the Baba smiled and said that 'Arif had already divided it on a brotherly basis, rather than giving him the entire amount entrusted to him" (p. 172, folio 154).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Rizvi's placement of Baba Farid's khalifa at the centre of Chishti history.** "The life of Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya' in Delhi was to become the epitome of Baba Farid's teachings, and it marked the crystallization of the ideology of the Chishti Order" (p. 175, folio 157); and "The greatest sufi saint of the fourteenth century in India and the most celebrated disciple of Baba Farid was Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya'" (p. 172, folio 154).
- **A source-critical demolition of a named modern scholar's chronology — the chunk's most substantial piece of argument, and it is in a footnote.** "Relying uncritically on the Sivaru'l-Auliya' [OCR?] [Siyaru'l-Auliya'], Nizami says that the visit took place in Jumada I, 664/February, 1266 and the khi/ajat-nama [khilafat-nama] is dated 13 Ramazan 664/18 June 1266. The life and times of Shaikh Faridu'd Din, p. 77. The main difficulty in following this chronology is that the Baba is believed to have died on 5 Muharram 664/17 October 1265. AA, p. 54. Nizami also accepts this date (see p. 56.) but fails to reconcile the two dates which imply that the Baba would have been visited by the Shaikh five months after his death and the khilafat-nama granted nine months after his death. It would therefor [therefore] seem that 664 must be a printing error. Nizami's conversion of Hijra dates is also wrong" (p. 174, folio 156, footnote 9). **Rizvi's own dates in the text — Jumada I 663/February 1265 and 13 Ramazan 663/29 June 1265 — are the corrected ones** (pp. 174–175, folios 156–157).
- **A rejection of the religious reading of a succession war.** "All medieval, and some modern, historians interpret the battle between Ghazi Malik and Khusraw as a Hindu-Islamic conflict. In fact, the struggle was political, each party raising emotive, religious slogans to rally support" (p. 179, folio 161). **No evidence is offered for the correction in this chunk.**
- **A later-source tradition labelled a myth, with a psychological explanation for its spread.** "A number of later works such as the Tabagqat-i Akbari [Tabaqat-i Akbari], however, assert that the Sultan continued to bear enmity towards the Shaikh and ordered him to leave Delhi… This myth became popular mainly because of Sultan Ghiyasu'd-Din Tughluq's sudden death" (p. 180, folio 162), qualified immediately: "Although there is no indication that the misunderstanding between the Sultan and the Shaikh abated, the Sultan was indeed too shrewd a politician to adopt extreme measures against the ageing Shaikh" (p. 180, folio 162).
- **A silent correction of the book's own narrative in its own footnote.** Having written that the Shaikh "quoted a Hadis from the Prophet" (p. 180, folio 162), Rizvi's footnote says "The Shaikh himself, however, did not quote a Hadis but an opinion expressed by Ghazali" (p. 180, folio 162, footnote 4).
- **A silsila-founder's absence from the early record, stated as a source problem.** "Early sources do not refer to Shaikh 'Ala'u'd-Din 'Ali bin Ahmad Sabir, the founder of the Sabiri branch of the Chishti order"; the branch "rose to prominence in the sufi movement mainly because of the popularity of Shaikh Ahmad 'Abdu'l-Haqq" (p. 172, folio 154). **The founder's fame is thus explained by a later figure, not by himself.**
- **Amir Khwurd's khalifa-list defended against later expansion.** "It seems that later authorities included the khalifas from all three categories, while Amir Khwurd lists khalifas of the first two groups" (p. 168, folio 150).
- **A correction of a named traveller.** "Ibn Battuta confused him with Shaikh Faridu'd-Din" (p. 167, folio 149, footnote 7), citing "Ibn Battuta, Voyages D'Ibn Batoutah, ed. by C. Defremery, Et Le, Dr B.R. Sanguinetti, III, Paris, 1855-59, p. 135".
- **Rizvi's reading of 'Ala'u'd-Din Khalji's reforms — economics first, sufi influence second.** "Sultan 'Ala'u'd-Din's strict market regulations and his suppression of drinking and debauchery were prompted by political and economic reasons, however, a positive moral consciousness was engendered by the Shaikh's influence and by that of the sufi movement in Ghiyaspur" (p. 178, folio 160).
- **The sama' attack explained as envy rather than doctrine.** "The 'ulama' and some sufis were envious of Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din's personal popularity among the élite and the masses, and attempted to further estrange relations between him and the Sultan. They re-opened the age-old question of sama' as a weapon by which to attack him" (p. 179, folio 161).
- **The saint as politically abstinent but not politically safe.** "Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din took no interest in political upheavals but could not escape the brunt of Sultan Mubarak Shah's fury for having made Khizr Khan his disciple" (p. 178, folio 160); and, of the refund demand, "this marked the beginning of the conflict between them which abated only with their deaths" (p. 179, folio 161).

- **`shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar`: the book never prints "Pakpattan" anywhere in pp. 164–180 (folios 146–162).** Its name for the place is "Ajodhan" throughout, and the burial passage says only that the coffin was brought "inside" the "city walls" (p. 168, folio 150). **The identification of Ajodhan with the row's location, Pakpattan, is not made by the book and I have not made it.** A consolidator must source that equation elsewhere before an entry states it. The mapping of the row itself is secure on the person — the book names him in full as "Shaikh Faridu'd-Din Ganj-i Shakar" (p. 174, folio 156), matching the row's principal figure "Baba Fariduddin Mas'ud Ganjshakar".
- **`shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya` rests on a bare name in two sentences, and on a reported report.** The book gives "Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya'" no dates, no place, no tomb and no silsila (pp. 173, 180; folios 155, 162). **The khanqah is described only as what a qawwal told a twelve-year-old, and the book does not say it was at Multan** — the inference is available only because the same sentence says the qawwal "had been to both Multan and Ajodhan" (p. 173, folio 155), which is not a statement of location. Flagged per the author-only/bare-name rule.
- **Interim convention (a), toponyms: three places in this chunk would take an archive id if the ruling reverses, and I name them so the decision is reversible.** (i) **"Siwistan (Sehwan) in Sind"** (p. 172, folio 154) — a bare toponym, a posting for Shaikh 'Arif, with no shrine, tomb, saint, ʿurs or custodian attached. **It could take either `lal-shahbaz-qalandar` (location "Sehwan Sharif") or `shrine-of-lakhi-shah-saddar` (location "Laki (Lakhi), near Sehwan Sharif"), and I am not picking between them.** (ii) **"Multan"** (pp. 169, 172, 173, 176; folios 151, 154, 155, 158) — bare in all four occurrences; **eight archive rows give Multan as their location**, so a reversal would not identify one. (iii) **"Uch"** (p. 172, folio 154), in "a Malik of Uch and Multan" — bare; **five archive rows are at Uch Sharif**. In none of the three cases does the book attach a shrine to the name.
- **A name collision I am not resolving: "Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din Bakhtiyar" (p. 166, folio 148) and "Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din" (p. 170, folio 152) against the archive row `tomb-of-qutbuddin-aibak`, whose principal figure is "Sultan Qutb ud-Din Aibak".** The book's man carries the title "Khwaja", has a tomb used for sufi initiation ceremonies, and is used as a landmark for another sufi's grave; the row's man is a sultan. **They read as different people, but the book supplies no dates, no place and no identification for its Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din at all, so I am flagging rather than dismissing, and I have made no mapping.**
- **A referential ambiguity that changes whose rule the anti-tomb-initiation statement is.** The paragraph runs "The copy of the khilafat-nama given to him by the Baba is also preserved. The latter regarded the relationship… According to the Shaikh, the initiation of a disciple should be performed personally by his preceptor… When one of his own sons performed such a ceremony… his father remarked that such an initiation was invalid" (p. 166, folio 148). **"The Shaikh" here may be Baba Farid (who had five sons, p. 167, folio 149) or Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya', named two sentences earlier; the phrase "his father" points away from whoever "the Shaikh" is, which makes the sentence read against itself.** I have recorded the rule without assigning it to a person. **This matters: it is the chunk's one explicit condemnation of ritual at saints' tombs, and the archive would want to know whose it is.**
- **An internal contradiction in a name, two sentences apart, in the child-khalifa story.** "the maid-servant brought the Shaikh's son, Burhanu'd-Din to Baba Farid. To her great surprise he made Badru'd-Din his khalifa. The servant pointed out that Badru'd-Din was very young" (p. 171, folio 153). **The boy is introduced as Burhanu'd-Din and then twice called Badru'd-Din.** Either the book or the OCR has substituted the name of Maulana Badru'd-Din Ishaq, who is prominent on the facing pages. **Not resolvable from this chunk.**
- **A death recorded twice with two different causes and dates, and the book keeps both.** Of Baba Farid's soldier son: "According to Amir Khwurd, Nizamu'd-Din was killed fighting against a Mongol army which had attacked Ajodhan. Later sources refer to his death during 'Ala'u'd-Din Khalji's invasion of Ranthambhor in 1301" (p. 168, folio 150). Rizvi does not choose.
- **A chronological wobble in Nizamu'd-Din Auliya's own dates, inside two pages.** He is "born in 636/1238" (p. 172, folio 154); the turban graduation is "Around 1253" (p. 173, folio 155); and "At the same time, when Nizamu'd-Din was twelve years old, and studying under Maulana Usuli, he first heard of Baba Farid" (p. 173, folio 155). **1238 + 12 = 1250, not 1253, so "at the same time" spans three years.** Recorded as printed; not adjudicated.
- **The book's death date for Baba Farid is carried only in a footnote and only as a belief, and the footnote's own purpose is to attack someone else's chronology** (p. 174, folio 156, footnote 9). The main text never states when he died. **A shrine entry should cite it as "AA, p. 54, as reported by Rizvi", not as Rizvi's own assertion.**
- **Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya' is not an archive row and I have not forced him into one.** He dominates pp. 172–180 (folios 154–162) and is the single densest source of practice, economy and state-relations material in this chunk; **that material is recorded above under "Practices, institutions, economy" and "Other saints, sites and events", and it is almost all attached to Delhi and Ghiyaspur, which no archive row covers.** If the archive ever adds a row for him, this chunk is the place to come back to.
- **One misdirection in the book's own cross-references, worth noting because the consolidator will follow them.** "See Chapter Three, p. 200, supra" (p. 173, folio 155) uses *supra* for a page that is later in the book; "See Chapter One, pp. 41-2, supra" (p. 168, folio 150) and "infra, p. 182" (p. 180, folio 162) are used correctly. **All three cross-references are in printed folios, not PDF pages.**
- **The chunk ends mid-sentence.** "They both died in 1325, the Sultan in Sha'ban 725/" (p. 180, folio 162) — **the CE conversion, and Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya's own death date, fall in the next chunk.**
