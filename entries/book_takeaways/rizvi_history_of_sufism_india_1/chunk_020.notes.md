## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht** (the book prints the bare title "the Makhdum Jahaniyan"): **he is named as the pir of the man whose shirt cured a cursed Sultan — the chunk's one transmitted-relic cure.** "The Sultan sought the protection of Shaikh Siraj Sukhta, a disciple of the Makhdum Jahaniyan, who gave him his shirt to wear. The holy shirt cured the Sultan" (p. 340, folio 318). The Sultan is "Qadir Shah, (1410-35), the son of Sultan Muhammad and a descendant of Sultan Firuz Shah", ruler of Kalpi (p. 340, folio 318). **No date, no seat, no Uch, no silsila and no tomb for the Makhdum himself** — all (not stated). Mapped on the settled reading of "Makhdum-i Jahaniyan"; see Doubts.
- **shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht** (here printed as the compound "Makhdum Jahaniyan Saiyid Jalal Bukhari"): **a named section of his following is described as a qalandarised, Shiʿi, naked-ascetic order — the single most substantive passage on him in this chunk, and the one most useful to the row.** "The custom and ideas of the Qalandariyya and the Nath yogis also had considerable impact on the beliefs of a section of the followers of Makhdum Jahaniyan Saiyid Jalal Bukhari. Unlike other sufis they adopted Shi'ism and in the same manner as the Madaris went naked and to warm themselves sat before fires. Although their hair was unmatted, they shaved their heads, beards, moustaches and eyebrows and spent their time wandering from place to place" (p. 342, folio 320).
- **shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht**: **the initiation rite of that following is given in full, and it is a total transfer of property.** "When they came into the order they offered all their material goods to their preceptors, and in return were given a cap and the spiritual tree of the order which was worn on their heads or hung round their necks" (p. 342, folio 320). **A named object of investiture — a cap and a "spiritual tree of the order" — and no khirqa, no baiʿa and no khilafat-nama** (not stated).
- **shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht**: **the followers are given a name and a miracle.** "Jalali saints who had achieved a certain standard of perfection like the Haidaris would swallow snakes saying they were 'Ali's fish and scorpions were called 'Ali's prawns" (p. 342, folio 320). Sourced to "Dabistan-i Mazahib, p. 215" (p. 342, folio 320). **The book calls them "Jalali saints" — it does not use the word "silsila" or "order" of them, and it does not say where they were.**
- **data-darbar** (the book writes only "Hujwiri", with no first name, no honorific, no Lahore and no tomb): **he is cited once in this chunk, as the authority on Brahmanical influence and on the Hululis — and the citation is a back-reference, so the substance is not in these pages.** "However Hujwiri's disappointment with certain sufis of his own time, whom he called Hululis, has already been mentioned, as has his assessment of current Brahmanical influence on sufi thought in Islam" (p. 345, folio 323). Footnoted only as "Nicholson, pp. 236, 243, 260-66, 271" (p. 345, folio 323). **This places Hujwiri, in Rizvi's own framing, among the *evidence for Hindu–sufi interaction* — the opening move of Chapter Six.** Mapped under the ruled author-only convention of 18 September 2026; see Doubts.
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar** (the book writes "Baba Farid"): **his jamaʿat-khana is named as a meeting-point between Chishti sufis and Hatha-Yoga Siddhas — a short sentence, but it is one of only two pieces of concrete Indian evidence Rizvi offers for sufi–yogi contact before al-Biruni.** "The topics discussed at the jama 'at-khana gatherings of Baba Farid were of great interest to visiting Siddhas whose beliefs were founded on Hatha Yoga" (p. 345, folio 323). **No date, no place, no Ajodhan, no silsila, no tomb and no ʿurs** — all (not stated). See Doubts: the book gives only the short form here.

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- **Sipah Salar Masʿud Ghazi, "popularly known as Ghazi Miyan"** — the chunk's largest single subject, pp. 333–336 (folios 311–314). Tomb "alleged to be in Bahraich in eastern U.P.", "now, as it has been since the fourteenth century, a centre of popular pilgrimage" (p. 333, folio 311). Barani makes him "an outstanding warrior in the army of Mahmud of Ghazna" (p. 334, folio 312); Abu'l-Fazl "one of the martyrs of the Ghaznawid armies" (p. 334, folio 312); Shaikhu'l-Hidaya, "a sufi from Khairabad near Lucknow", told Mulla Badaʾuni he "was an Afghan who had met a martyr's death" (p. 334, folio 312); Amir Khusraw's *Iʿjaz-i Khusrawi* calls him "a well-known saint" (p. 333, folio 311). Pet names: "Bale Miyan (Revered Boy), Bale Pir (Boy Saint) and Hateli Pir (Obstinate Saint)" (p. 336, folio 314); the *Mirʾat-i Masʿudi* calls him "the Prince of Martyrs" (p. 335, folio 313). **"To Muslims and Hindus alike, Salar Mas'ud was the great local saint of eastern India"** (p. 336, folio 314). The legendary biography, dates exactly as printed: Sultan Mahmud subdued Rum, Turan and Iran "in 401/1011"; Salar Shahu, the Sultan's "brother-in-law", relieved Ajmer and then "conquered Kanauj"; "On 21 Sha'ban 405/14 February 1015, their son Mas'ud was born"; Masʿud served at "his Somnath expedition in 1025-26 and persuaded the Sultan to smash the Somnath idol"; "Seizing Multan and Delhi he crossed the Ganges and camped at Satrikh in the Barabanki district, near Lucknow"; "On 25 Shawwal 423/4 October 1032, Salar Shahu died from natural causes"; "On 14 Rajab 424/15 June 1033, he was struck at Bahraich by an arrow from a Hindu chief and died aged nineteen" (pp. 335, folio 313).
- **Prince Nasiruʾd-Din Mahmud (1246-66)** — "who sought refuge in Bahraich to protect himself against the intrigues of his rivals, is said to have fought several battles against the local Hindu chiefs and from there marched on to Delhi under his mother's leadership, to seize the throne" (p. 333, folio 311). **Rizvi's alternative occupant of the grave: "Probably it was one of Prince Nasiru'd-Din's fallen generals who was the real occupant of the grave which was to become the centre of the Salar Mas'ud's legend"** (p. 333, folio 311).
- **Shaikh ʿAbduʾr-Rahman Chishti** — author of the *Mirʾat-i Masʿudi*, written "during Jahangir's reign", and of the *Mirʾat-i Madari*, "Written in 1064/1654" near Shah Madar's shrine at Makanpur (pp. 334, 340; folios 312, 318). He claimed both works were dictated in part by the saints' spirits (pp. 335, 340; folios 313, 318), and claimed his Masʿud history "had been corroborated by a learned Hindu Brahman of Bahraich from his own Sanskrit sources" (p. 336, folio 314). His alleged source, "the Tarikh-i Mulla Muhammad Ghaznawi", "is not referred to by any Ghaznawid authority and seems to have been written to satisfy popular curiosity about Salar Mas'ud at a later date" (p. 334, folio 312).
- **Shaikh Jalal of Sylhet / "Shah Jalal Mujarrad (Bachelor)"** — pp. 336–337 (folios 314–315). "a Turkistani by birth and a khalifa of Saiyid Ahmad Yasawi of the Silsila-i Khwajgan" per the *Gulzar-i Abrar*; his pir blessed him for "the lesser jihad or warfare against infidelity in a dar al-harb (Land outside the Islamic oecumené)"; "seven hundred (a mythical figure) most eminent disciples"; at "Sirhat (Sylhet)" he had 313 followers and defeated "Raja Gaur Govind, heading one hundred thousand footmen and several thousand horsemen"; he "apportioned the land among his followers, thus enabling them to take wives and settle down" but did not marry himself. Rizvi's reconstruction: "driven by the Mongol invasions to Baghdad and from there he went to Multan and Uch. At Uch it is possible that he was formally initiated into the Suhrawardiyya order as is suggested by local legends"; a Delhi meeting with "Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya'" is "not improbable"; "The Shaikh was reported to have died in 748/1347, therefore he would have been born in 598/1201" (p. 337, folio 315). **Rizvi states flatly that "Ibn Battuta confused Shaikh Jalal with Shaixh [OCR?] [Shaikh] Jalalu'd-Din Tabrizi, and so many later writers following the Moorish traveller did likewise"** (p. 337, folio 315).
- **Shaikh Jalaluʾd-Din Tabrizi** — named once, only as the man Ibn Battuta confused with Shah Jalal of Sylhet (p. 337, folio 315). No dates, no tomb, no location. Per the settled reading he is a third man and maps to no archive row; see Doubts.

[OCR?] [Haqq Go] (Speaker of the Truth) was executed by Sultan Muhammad bin Tughlaq for accusing the latter of cruelty"; initiated "by his father and by Shaikh Jalalu'd-Din Bukhari"; invited to Bihar by "Shaikh Sharfu'd-Din Yahya Munyari" but arrived after his death; "He married into a Bihari Hindu family"; settled at Chittagong, "believed to be haunted by evil spirits"; "The traditional site of his chilla (cell) where he would perform ascetic exercises often for forty days is still a centre of pilgrimage" (p. 338, folio 316). ## Practices, institutions, economy

- **An endowment of whole villages to a shrine, made by a Buddhist and Hindu population to a Muslim saint — the chunk's single most citable economic fact.** "A memorial (astana) was built on the top of a hill in Chittagong and according to Talish was cared for by attendants who spent most of their time in prayer and fasting. **The Buddhist and Hindu Magh people settled some villages as endowment for the shrine and made it a centre of pilgrimage**" (p. 339, folio 317). The footnote glosses *astana*: "Literally a tomb; Bengalis believed that the saint was buried there" (p. 339, folio 317, footnote 2). **A named custodial body ("attendants"), a named endowment class (villages), a named donor community (the Magh) and a stated purpose (pilgrimage). No revenue figure, no deed, no date** — (not stated).
- **Royal giving to a tomb, twice, from two dynasties.** "After his visit Muhammad bin Tughlug gave liberally to Salar Mas'ud's tomb, but the reforming Sultan Firuz Shah Tughluq was reported to have gone to extreme limits in exhibiting his devotion to the tomb. In 776/1374—75 he visited Bahraich and stayed there for some time" (p. 334, folio 312). **No sum, no land grant and no named office** — (not stated).
- **A ruler's shrine visit as the hinge of a religious policy.** Per Shams Siraj ʿAfif, after Salar Masʿud appeared to Firuz Shah in a dream at Bahraich, "Next morning, in the manner of a sufi novice the Sultan shaved his head and a large number of Khans and Maliks followed suit. 'Afif traces the beginning of the orthodoxy of Sultan Firuz Shah and the abolition of non-Islamic taxes to this period" (p. 334, folio 312). **A tomb visit is made the origin of a fiscal measure — the abolition of non-Islamic taxes — by a contemporary historian.**
- **A state ban on a pilgrimage procession, and its failure.** "Although Sikandar Lodi banned the processions of pilgrims carrying spears on their annual visit to the tomb of Salar Mas'ud, the festival was only stopped temporarily" (p. 334, folio 312). Sourced to "Tahagat-i Akbari [OCR?] [Tabaqat-i Akbari], p. 336; Tarikh-i Dawudi, p. 38" (p. 334, folio 312).
- **A puritan emperor visits a shrine anyway, dated to the day.** "On 14 Rabi' II, 1069/9 January 1659 while marching against his rival Prince Shuja', the puritanically orthodox Aurangzeb yet saw fit to visit the tomb" of Shah Madar at Makanpur (p. 341, folio 319).
- **A living ʿurs with a stated gender exclusion.** "Every year on the occasion of the Shah's 'urs large crowds of Hindus and Muslims assemble, **although women are still not permitted near the shrine**" (p. 341, folio 319). **The only custodial or access rule in the chunk stated in the present tense.**
- **Two festival calendars at one tomb, deliberately out of step — a solar-agrarian one over an Islamic one.** "Although his 'urs is celebrated on 12, 13 and 14 Rajab, the flags are taken to the tomb on the first Sunday of the Hindi month Jaith (May-June). **After the completion of the wheat and barley harvest, the leisure of the peasants in June is devoted to celebrations marking the Ghazi Miyan festival**" (p. 336, folio 314). **A harvest-cycle explanation of festival timing, stated by the author as fact.**
- **Offerings, present tense.** "Offerings of all kinds are even now made to the tomb of Salar Mas'ud" (p. 336, folio 314). Earlier, of Shah Jalal: "His fellow dervishes consumed the gifts given to him by devotees, while the Shah lived only on milk from his cow" (p. 337, folio 315). **The devotees' gifts feed the establishment, not the saint — the clearest statement of shrine economy in the chunk.**

- **Joint Hindu–Muslim ritual with a named apparatus.** Pir Badr and Khwaja Khizr "Both were worshipped in a joint ceremony held on a village pond or nearby river in which a little grass raft carrying a lighted lamp was floated" (p. 339, folio 317).
- **Occupational patronage.** "Shah Madar is the patron saint of both Hindu and Muslim jugglers, acrobats and those who earn their livelihood by exhibiting tricks with monkeys and bears" (p. 342, folio 320); Pir Badr is "the patron saint of sailors, both Muslim and Hindu" and "of sailors and mariners" (pp. 338, 339; folios 316, 317).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Rizvi's flat verdict on the whole legendary apparatus, at the close of Chapter Five** (p. 343, folio 321): "However incredible these legends may appear, they formed an integral part of popular sufi beliefs, not only in India but throughout the Islamic world. **They were mainly designed to glorify Islam and to strengthen beliefs in the supernatural and in the performance of miracles**." **He treats the legends as instruments with a purpose, not as errors.**
- **He dismantles the Salar Masʿud tomb's identity while conceding its cult.** "There is no mention of Salar Mas'ud in the history of the I]bari [OCR?] [Ilbari] Turks"; "**Probably it was one of Prince Nasiru'd-Din's fallen generals who was the real occupant of the grave** which was to become the centre of the Salar Mas'ud's legend. By the reign of Sultan Muhammad bin Tughlug the legend had been greatly expanded, although certain accretions were-also [OCR?] [were also] developed in subsequent years" (p. 333, folio 311). **A named archive-relevant methodological move: the author separates the historicity of the occupant from the reality of the cult, and keeps both.**
- **He dates the source-forgery.** Of the *Tarikh-i Mulla Muhammad Ghaznawi*: it "is not referred to by any Ghaznawid authority and seems to have been written to satisfy popular curiosity about Salar Mas'ud at a later date" (p. 334, folio 312).
- **He reads a hagiographer's Brahman corroboration as apologetics aimed at the orthodox.** "Such an exercise by Shaikh 'Abdu'r-Rahman would have gone a long way in satisfying a section of the orthodox who were critical of the many innovations in the commemoration of the great exploits of the holy martyr" (p. 336, folio 314).
- **He states, without qualification, that historical accuracy is irrelevant to the cult's constituencies.** "To the common Muslims who remembered Salar Mas'ud Ghazi with pet names … no historical perspective was needed. **To the Hindus who considered him a saint of miraculous power, the number of their brethren he killed or Islamized was then, as it is now, meaningless**" (p. 336, folio 314). **This is the chunk's most quotable and most contestable interpretive sentence.**
- **He calls an anecdote absurd and explains its function.** Of the Babur–Sikandar Lodi story: "**The story is, of course, absurd, and has been devised around Babur's later image as a ruler who was fiercely proud of his qalandar associations**" (p. 333, folio 311).
- **He explains the Panch Pir by numerical convergence across three religions.** "It may be noted that Hindus and Muslims alike attach some importance in religious usage to the number five; the Panch Pir are the counterparts of the Panch Tathagatas of the Buddhists and the Panch Pandits of the Nath yogis" (p. 340, folio 318).
- **He accounts for uncritical hadis transmission by period.** "Sufis and scholars who flourished between the thirteenth and the sixteenth centuries were unconcerned with scholarly criticism and the Kubrawiyya order incorporated writings attributed to Baba Ratan into its own traditions" (p. 342, folio 320); the endorsements of Simnani and Hamadani are "the saintly seal finally affixed" (p. 342, folio 320).
- **Chapter Six opens by naming the field's problem.** "**HE study of the interaction between medieval Hindu mystic traditions and sufism has been marred by deep prejudices and studded with conflicting hypotheses.** The subject can best be studied by dividing it into two epochs: firstly, the pre-eleventh century and secondly, the eleventh to the sixteenth centuries" (p. 344, folio 322). **The chapter's own periodisation, stated in its first paragraph.**
- **He rejects the Aryan-reaction thesis and keeps the contact thesis.** "The fact that mystic urges are now recognized as inherent in all religious systems tends to dampen such theories as those expressed by early Western orientalists that sufism developed from an Aryan (implying here, Iranian) reaction against the conquering Semitic Arabs. However … the presence of Nestorian, Buddhist, Manichaean and Sanskrit scholars in the remote regions of Khurasan and Central Asia between the eighth and the tenth centuries cannot be denied" (p. 344, folio 322).
- **He leaves the Buddha/Ibrahim bin Adham analogy open.** "Although there is no evidence that Ibrahim bin Adham had the example of Siddhartha Gautama in mind when he renounced his throne, the controversy is interesting" (p. 344, folio 322).
- **He overturns Nicholson on nirvana and fanaʾ, and uses modern Buddhology to do it.** Nicholson is quoted ("while nirvana is purely negative, fana' is accompanied by baqa'", p. 344, folio 322) and then answered: "**This theory which has great appeal to many Muslim scholars is questionable in the light of modern research. Nirvana is not a purely negative concept but a positive one.** The state of arhantship in which nirvana has been achieved, cannot be described as either existence or non-existence and can be likened to the goals not only of Abu Yazid, but even of Muhasibi and Junaid" (pp. 344–345, folios 322–323).
- **He concedes an evidential vacuum on Hallaj and Sind.** "There are no objective data available to determine the impact of Hallaj's ideas of the soul after his visit to Sind but Shaikh 'Ala'u'd-Dawla Simnani had no misgivings about the influence that Hallaj's travels had on his philosophy" (p. 345, folio 323).
- **He names the evidential poverty of the jogi material — directly relevant to how any Nath row can be sourced.** "From the eleventh century onwards in India, the contacts and conflicts between sufis and yogis became more frequent and meaningful. … **Unfortunately existing literature throws little light on yogis, who are constantly referred to as 'jogis'**" (p. 345, folio 323), followed by the two scraps he can offer (Chiragh-i Dihli on the Siddhas; Baba Farid's jamaʿat-khana) before falling back on al-Biruni.
- **He claims yogic influence on the qalandars and the Rifaʿiyya.** "The various branches of qalandars and sufis of the Rifa'iyya order, confined mainly to Turkey, Syria and Egypt, **were significantly influenced by wandering yogis**" (p. 345, folio 323). **No evidence is offered for this in the chunk.**
- **He builds the rest of Chapter Six's opening on al-Biruni as the reliable witness** — "unquestionably a profound authority on comparative religrom@ewho [OCR?] [comparative religion, who] notes sufi parallels in the Yoga Sutra of Patanjali, which he himself translated into Arabic" (p. 345, folio 323) — and then quotes him at length on the soul "like rice in its cover" (p. 345, folio 323), on immanence "in animals, trees and the inanimate world, which they call His universal appearance" (p. 346, folio 324), on Paradise as no special gain (p. 346, folio 324), on liberation and "svasa and prasvasa" (p. 346, folio 324), on the sufi's "two souls" (p. 346, folio 324), on "The bodies are the snares of the souls" (p. 347, folio 325), on monism (p. 347, folio 325) and on "pantheistic union" (pp. 347–348, folios 325–326).
- **He corrects al-Biruni's translator on a technical term.** "The original word is muwahhid, meaning **one who is absorbed in Divine Unity rather than monism**" (p. 347, folio 325, footnote 3), and "]ttihad [OCR?] [Ittihad] in original" (p. 347, folio 325, footnote 4).
- **He notes that a Qurʾanic interpretation is contested.** "The meaning is controversial, some commentators consider it refers to the martyrdom of Jesus Christ" (p. 348, folio 326, footnote 1).
- **He treats conversion as a by-product of charisma, twice.** "Naturally with such claims made for him the Shah's personality became tremendously popular with the local Hindus and Buddhists amongst whom he had settled. **The magic surrounding his name prompted many to embrace Islam**" (p. 337, folio 315); and Pir Badrʾs "stay in this wilderness greatly impressed the predominantly Buddhist and animistic Hindus of Chittagong and Arakan" (p. 338, folio 316).
- **He notes the courts' differing postures to qalandars.** "The relationship of the Haidaris with the court of Muhammad bin Tughlug is unknown, but the Sultan, who was deeply interested in yogis and the different mystic movements, undoubtedly would not have ignored the Haidaris. **It is possible the orthodox may have kept the Haidaris from Firuz's court, as there is no mention of their presence there**" (p. 332, folio 310). **The first half is explicitly an inference from silence, and Rizvi says so.**

**Archive-mapping doubts:**

- **`shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht` is mapped twice on two different printed forms, and the second form collides with another archive row inside a single name.** p. 340 (folio 318) prints the clean title "the Makhdum Jahaniyan", which the settled reading assigns to this row. **But p. 342 (folio 320) prints the compound "Makhdum Jahaniyan Saiyid Jalal Bukhari", which fuses the grandson's title with the name that the settled reading gives as the handle for the grandfather's row `shrine-of-jalaluddin-surkh-posh-bukhari-jalaluddin-bukhari`.** The book supplies no date, no seat, no Uch and no generation in either passage. I have followed the title, because the title is the discriminator the ruling names — **but if the Jalali order is in fact traced to the grandfather, the p. 342 material (Shiʿism, nakedness, fire-sitting, the cap and spiritual tree, the snakes) moves wholesale to the other row.** This is the mapping in this chunk most worth a human's eye.
- **"Shaikh Jalalu'd-Din Bukhari" (p. 338, folio 316) is left unmapped, because it could be either of two rows.** The book says only that Pir Badr-i ʿAlam "was initiated into the secrets of mysticism by his father and by Shaikh Jalalu'd-Din Bukhari", with no epithet, no date, no Uch and no "Surkh"/"Makhdum" title. The candidates are `shrine-of-jalaluddin-surkh-posh-bukhari-jalaluddin-bukhari` and `shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht`. **Pir Badr died 27 Rajab 844/22 December 1440 (p. 339, folio 317), which is the only datum bearing on it, and the book draws no inference from it. Unmapped.**
- **"Shaikh Jalalu'd-Din Tabrizi" (p. 337, folio 315) is confirmed as a third man and maps to neither row** — the book itself separates him from Shaikh Jalal of Sylhet and places the confusion in Ibn Battuta. No mapping; recorded here so a consolidator does not re-open it.
- **`shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar` is mapped on the short form "Baba Farid" alone (p. 345, folio 323).** The book gives no seat (neither "Ajodhan" nor "Pakpattan"), no date, no silsila and no tomb in this sentence. Within this book "Shaikh Farid" denotes at least three men; **"Baba Farid" plus a jamaʿat-khana in a Chishti–Siddha context is the ground for the mapping, and nothing more.** If a consolidator wants a harder test, this bullet is the one to re-read against the print.
- **"Shaikh Farid" in the Panch Pir list (p. 339, folio 317) is deliberately NOT mapped**, even though `shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar` is mapped elsewhere in this chunk. The list runs "Ghazi Miyan (Salar Mas'ud), Zinda Ghazi, Shaikh Farid, and Khwaja Khizr" — **a bare name in a list of legendary Bengali figures, two of whom the book treats as mythical.** The book does not say which Farid this is, and the Panch Pir are an eastern cult. **A consolidator who wants to attach this to the row must do so on evidence outside this chunk.**
- **"Zinda Ghazi" (p. 339, folio 317) is a bare name; `darbar-ghamkol-sharif-zinda-pir` was considered and rejected** — the row's principal figure is "Khwaja Muhammad Qasim (Zinda Pir)", d. 1999, Kohat, and the book's Zinda Ghazi is an undated Bengali Panch Pir. **A shared epithet is not an identification.**
- **"Shaikh Baba Ratan or Hajji Baba Ratan" (p. 342, folio 320) is the chunk's hardest unresolved collision, and I have left it unmapped.** The archive row `dargah-pir-ratan-nath-jee` names "Baba Pir Ratan Nath" at Jhanda Bazar, Peshawar, and is categorised as a Hindu Temple. **The book writes "Baba Ratan", never "Nath"; gives Tabarhinda or Bhatinda in the Panjab as his origin, "Abu'l-Riza" as his kunniya, 700/1300 as his death and the Punjab as his burial place; and presents him as a Muslim companion of the Prophet, not a Nath.** The two figures are conflated in some traditions and the row's category would have to be reconsidered if they are one man — **so this is a row-level question, not just a naming one, and it needs a human. No id assigned; nothing invented.**
- **`tomb-of-qutbuddin-aibak` was considered and rejected.** The chunk's only Qutbuʾd-Din is "Shaikh Qutbu'd-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki" (p. 348, folio 326), a Chishti who died of samaʿ ecstasy — not Sultan Qutb ud-Din Aibak. **Per the standing trap, the bare short form is never mapped, and here even the full form is a different man.**
- **`shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam` was considered and rejected.** The chunk's only Ruknuʾd-Din is "Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din 'Ala'u'd-Dawla Simnani" (p. 342, folio 320), a Kubrawi in the Baba Ratan endorsement chain — **no Multan, no Suhrawardi, no descent from Bahaʾuʾd-Din Zakariyya.** The book does not print "Rukn-e-Alam" anywhere in this range.
- **`gorakhnath-goraknath-temple` and `tilla-jogian` were considered and rejected on the category rule.** "the Panch Pandits of the Nath yogis" (p. 340, folio 318) and "the Qalandariyya and the Nath yogis" (p. 342, folio 320) name a category; "the Siddhas" (p. 345, folio 323, twice) and "'jogis'" (p. 345, folio 323) likewise. **Gorakhnath is not named; the Salt Range and Gor Khatri are not named; no jogi site of any kind is named in pp. 332–348.** Recorded so that a reversal of the category question — if one ever comes — has a clean list.
- **The unruled deity question is only weakly live in this chunk, and I name the rows so a reversal is a clean sweep.** The sole deity named is **Vishnu**, once, in a comparison: "a counterpart of the fish incarnation of Vishnu" (p. 339, folio 317). **If a deity may be a row's principal figure, the rows a reversal would engage from this one sentence are `prahladpuri-temple` (principal figure Narasimha, a Vishnu avatar) and `shri-laxmi-narayan-mandir-native-jetty-bridge` (Lakshmi-Narayan) — and neither is a secure match, since the book names Matsya only descriptively and names no temple.** Separately, **"the Somnath idol" (p. 335, folio 313) names no deity at all**, so the archive's eight Shiva rows are not engaged even under a reversal; and **Krishna, Ram, Siva and Kali are absent from pp. 332–348**, so `krishna-mandir-kabari-bazar`, `krishna-mandir-ravi-road`, `ram-mandir-saidpur-ram-kund-mandir`, `kalat-kali-temple`, `kali-bari-mandir` and `kalka-cave-temple-asthan-of-kalka-devi` gain nothing here either way. **No deity has been mapped.**

**Internal contradictions and unresolved arithmetic, kept side by side rather than reconciled:**

- **Salar Masʿud's identity is given four incompatible ways in two pages.** Barani: "an outstanding warrior in the army of Mahmud of Ghazna" (p. 334, folio 312). Abu'l-Fazl: "one of the martyrs of the Ghaznawid armies" (p. 334, folio 312). Shaikhu'l-Hidaya to Badaʾuni: "an Afghan who had met a martyr's death" (p. 334, folio 312). Rizvi himself: **probably not Salar Masʿud at all, but "one of Prince Nasiru'd-Din's fallen generals"** (p. 333, folio 311). **The book prefers the last and prints the other three without ranking them.**
- **A chronological impossibility the book raises but does not resolve.** ʿAbduʾl-Haqq "contradicts the popular belief that Salar Mas'ud was a disciple of Khwaja Mu'inu'd-Din Chishti" (p. 334, folio 312), yet footnote 7 on the same page reports Shah ʿAlam Bukhari saying Muʿinuʾd-Din "would transfer requests made to him to Salar Mas'ud" — **and the Mirʾat-i Masʿudi places Masʿud's death at 14 Rajab 424/15 June 1033 (p. 335, folio 313), long before Muʿinuʾd-Din.** Rizvi prints all three without comment.
- **Shah Jalal's lifespan is inferred, not stated, and the inference is extraordinary.** "The Shaikh was reported to have died in 748/1347, therefore he would have been born in 598/1201 and it is possible he could have visited the Caliph al-Musta'sim" (p. 337, folio 315). **That is 150 lunar years, and the book neither states the age nor flags it.** The figures it does give — Ibn Battuta's 1345 visit, the book completed "December 1357", the caliph's "(640/1242-656/1258)" — are recorded above exactly as printed.
- **Shah Madar's lifespan is likewise unremarked.** Born "715/1315" at Aleppo (p. 340, folio 318), he is placed at the court of "Qadir Shah, (1410-35)" (p. 340, folio 318) and is credited with "thirty-five years of his life in Syria, forty in Mecca, Medina and Najaf, and fifty in India" (p. 341, folio 319) — **125 years by the book's own arithmetic, with no comment.**
- **Warrior sainthood is at once condemned as bloodshed and recorded as devotion.** Shaikh Jalal's expedition "was not an ascetic or peaceful one. The booty gained from their warring enabled them to live luxuriously" (p. 336, folio 314), set beside the same saint's austerity, "he lived only on milk from his cow" (p. 337, folio 315). **The book records both without mediating them.**
- **Rizvi's own register shifts inside Chapter Five.** The Babur anecdote is "of course, absurd" (p. 333, folio 311) and the *Tarikh-i Mulla Muhammad Ghaznawi* a later fabrication (p. 334, folio 312), yet the Salar Masʿud ʿurs, the Panch Pir, the boatmen's chant and the coal-walking Madaris are all reported in the present tense with no such judgement (pp. 336, 338, 339, 342; folios 314, 316, 317, 320). **The closing sentence of the chapter (p. 343, folio 321) is the only place he generalises the verdict.**
- **A single unverified transcription problem in a quoted liturgy.** The Bengali chant "Amara achchi polapan / Gazi achche nigahman, / Shire Ganga dariya Panch Pir, / Badr, Badr, Badr" (p. 338, folio 316) is printed in Roman transliteration only, with the book's own English gloss beneath it; **there is no Bengali script in the print, so the "original script" requirement cannot be met for it and the words must be checked against the page before any entry quotes them.**
