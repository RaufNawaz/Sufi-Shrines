**The brief's section statement is confirmed by the book.** The recto running header reads "The Suhrawardiyya and the Firdawsiyya Silsilas" on every numbered recto in this chunk (pp. 215, 217, 219, 223, 225, 227, 229, 231; folios 197, 199, 201, 203, 205, 207, 209, 211), so the whole chunk sits inside Chapter Three as the brief says. **One qualification the brief does not make: nothing in pp. 215–232 is about the Firdawsiyya.** The entire range is Suhrawardi material — Qazi Hamidu'd-Din Nagauri, Shaikh Jalalu'd-Din Tabrizi, and then the Multan succession Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya → Sadru'd-Din 'Arif → Ruknu'd-Din Abu'l Fath. The Firdawsiyya must lie past p. 232.

**Four archive rows are touched, and this is the densest archive chunk of the book so far.** `shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya` is central to the whole range; `shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam` is settled here, for the first time in this book, by the book printing the name **"Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din Abu'l Fath"** (p. 230, folio 210); `shrine-of-jalaluddin-surkh-posh-bukhari-jalaluddin-bukhari` is settled by **"Saiyid Jalalu'd-Din Bukhari, also called Jalal Surkh (Red)"** migrating to Uch and dying there (p. 223, folio 203); and `shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar` is touched twice as "Baba Farid". **Two of the queue's standing traps are resolved by these pages and a third is not** — see Doubts.

## Shrines and figures in the archive

### shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya

- **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya** ("Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya"): **the book gives his death as December 1262, against the archive row's 1267, and gives it incidentally, inside a chronological argument about somebody else.** "Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya died in December 1262, ten years earlier" (p. 226, folio 206). **No day, no month-date, no AH equivalent and no place of death is given here** — (not stated). This is the same disagreement earlier chunks recorded; the book's value stands.
- **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya**: **he is the hinge of the order's whole Indian history, stated as an exclusive claim.** The other disciples of Shihabu'd-Din Suhrawardi "were eminent in their own right, but the continuous history of the silsila can be traced only through the khalifas of Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya" (p. 220, folio 202).
- **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya**: **his succession was hereditary and he made it so himself.** "He appointed his own son, Sadru'd-Din, known as 'Arif (Gnostic), his khalifa" (p. 220, folio 202).
- **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya**: **the size of the estate he left, and the number of heirs.** "Shaikh Sadru'd-Din had six brothers, but he alone inherited from his father property worth 700,000 tankas. According to Jamali, he immediately gave the entire amount to the poor, for like his father he believed he was unable to spend it judiciously" (p. 220, folio 202). **A rival account is given on the same page — see Doubts.** Rizvi's own conclusion: "it seems clear that wealth accumulated by Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din did not long remain with his sons" (p. 220, folio 202).
- **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya**: **a named disciple who is himself an archive row.** "Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya had many famous disciples, one was Saiyid Jalalu'd-Din Bukhari, also called Jalal Surkh (Red)" (p. 223, folio 203) — see that row below.
- **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya**: **his illiterate disciple, and the boast attached to him.** "One of Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din's disciples, Hasan Afghan, was illiterate. The Shaikh was proud of Hasan Afghan and used to say that if God asked him what he had brought from the world, he would present Hasan as a gift" (p. 223, folio 203).
- **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya**: **Rizvi names one reason above all others for his standing among scholars, and it is a poet.** "The main reason for Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya's fame in the scholarly world, however, was due to the achievements of his disciple, Shaikh Fakhru'd-Din Ibrahim, popularly known as 'Iraqi" (p. 224, folio 204).
- **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya**: **his khanqah at Multan took in a wandering qalandar party and kept one of them.** The qalandars "reached Multan where they stayed with Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din"; when the party dispersed in a storm "'Iraqi returned to the Shaikh's khangah [OCR?] [khanqah]" and "began living in a cell which the Shaikh had assigned him, as a spiritual retreat" (p. 224, folio 204).
- **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya**: **his stated position on poetry and music, and the exception he made.** "Baha'u'd-Din was opposed to poetry and music, but he tolerated its indulgence by Fakhru'd-Din Ibrahim" (p. 224, folio 204). His other disciples "were shocked to learn that this ghazal was being sung in local taverns to the accompaniment of the harp and zither. They complained to the Shaikh" (p. 224, folio 204) — and he ruled for 'Iraqi.
- **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya**: **the four instruments of his deputisation of 'Iraqi, given in one sentence and in order.** He "declared 'Iraqi's training to be complete. He ordered him to give up retirement and meditation, presented him with his own khirga [OCR?] [khirqa] to wear and later gave him his daughter in marriage. Before he died, Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din appointed him his khalifa" (pp. 224–225, folios 204–205). **Robe, marriage into the family, and the khalifa appointment — no document of any kind is mentioned** — (not stated).
- **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya**: **his death broke the succession he had arranged.** "after his death, the jealousy of other disciples forced 'Iraqi to leave Multan about 1263. It would appear he had been there since about 1239" (p. 225, folio 205). **A twenty-four-year residence at Multan, given by the book as two approximations.**
- **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya**: **a devotional verse naming him as the guide of the world, quoted by the book as evidence of 'Iraqi's devotion.** "'If thou shouldst ask of the world 'Who is the guide of men?' / Thou wilt hear from heaven no other answer than 'Zakariyya'.'" (p. 225, folio 205).
- **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya**: **he is placed at the head of a three-man praise-list in a disciple's masnawi.** In the Kanzu'r-Rumuz "Husaini praised the Shaikhs, Shihabu'd-Din Suhrawardi, Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya and Sadru'd-Din" (p. 226, folio 206).
- **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya**: **two authorities attach Amir Husain to him as a disciple and Rizvi refutes both on chronology.** "Jamali describes him in connection with the disciples of Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya"; "Modern scholars also describe him as Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din's disciple" (p. 226, folio 206). Rizvi: Husain "must have been born about 671/1272-73, while Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya died in December 1262, ten years earlier" (p. 226, folio 206).
- **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya**: **his relations with a rival Shaikh, recorded twice and in both directions.** He travelled with Shaikh Jalalu'd-Din Tabrizi from Baghdad to Nishapur and "would constantly pray and meditate" while the other "would visit local dervishes and spend time sightseeing"; they parted at Nishapur (p. 217, folio 199). At Delhi he was made chairman of the mahzar precisely because of "the rivalry between Shaikh Jalalu'd-Din and Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya" (p. 218, folio 200) — and then destroyed the proceeding by deference: he "ran to receive him and carry his shoes", saying that Jalalu'd-Din "had served his pir for seven years and therefore it was fitting for him to use the dust from Shaikh Jalalu'd-Din's feet as an eye-wash" (p. 218, folio 200).
- **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya**: **his reputation for withholding praise, and one exception to it.** "Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya, not well-known for his praise of other sufis, was a great admirer of Shaikh Ahmad" — the weaver Shaikh Ahmad Naharwani (p. 216, folio 198).
- **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya**: **he is given a prenatal prophecy about his own grandson, and then endorses the child against the child's father.** He "prophesied his prominence while he was still in his mother's womb" (p. 230, folio 210); and "at the age of four, Ruknu'd-Din donned Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din's turban, and although his father objected strongly, Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din approved of the child's action" (p. 231, folio 211).
- **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya**: **his turban becomes succession regalia two generations on.** "After Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din succeeded his father, he wore the same turban and Shaikh Sadru'd-Din's khirga [OCR?] [khirqa]" (p. 231, folio 211).

### shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam

- **shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam** ("Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din Abu'l Fath"): **the book prints the full name form here, and it is the name form the archive row carries.** "The successor of Shaikh Sadru'd-Din was his son, Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din Abu'l Fath" (p. 230, folio 210). **The book never prints "Rukn-e-Alam" anywhere in this chunk, gives him no death date and gives no tomb** — all (not stated). See Doubts for why I treat "Abu'l Fath" as settling and what it does not settle.
- **shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam**: **his descent, three generations of it, in one page.** Son of Shaikh Sadru'd-Din, grandson of Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya; "The latter's mother was a most pious lady" (p. 230, folio 210); at four he donned his grandfather's turban with his grandfather's approval and against his father's objection (pp. 230–231, folios 210–211).
- **shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam**: **the two objects that carry his succession, and they are both garments.** "he wore the same turban and Shaikh Sadru'd-Din's khirga [OCR?] [khirqa]" (p. 231, folio 211).
- **shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam**: **the sums a Sultan paid him per visit, and what he did with them — the single best economy-of-religion passage in the chunk.** "In the reign of Sultan 'Ala'u'd-Din Khalji, Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din visited Delhi twice. The Sultan himself went to welcome him and rode back with him to the city. During each visit he paid 200,000 tankas at the Shaikh's arrival and 500,000 tankas at his departure. The Shaikh always distributed the entire amount among Delhi's population and naturally his visits were a source of great jubilation to the people" (p. 231, folio 211). **700,000 tankas per visit, twice, redistributed in full.**
- **shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam**: **a Sultan tried to use him as a counterweight to a rival Shaikh and failed.** "His three visits to the capital during Sultan Qutbu'd-Din's reign were, however, sponsored by the Sultan who wished that Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din's presence might eclipse that of Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya'. But the close friendship between the two great Shaikhs frustrated the Sultan's hopes and he could not arouse the tiniest jealousy between them" (p. 231, folio 211). Asked by the Sultan who had met him first, "Unhesitatingly the Shaikh replied that the leading figure amongst the élite was the one who had met him first, that is, Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din" (p. 231, folio 211).
- **shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam**: **he is physically infirm and the book says so.** "Although Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din had a serious problem with his leg, he ordered his men to lift him from his palanquin so he could show his respect for Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din, but the latter prevented him from doing so" (p. 231, folio 211).
- **shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam**: **he refuses a gift of cloth and gold and passes it to his brother — a recorded refusal of futuh.** "Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din's servant, Iqbal, presented Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din with several pieces of fine cloth and a purse of gold coins from his master, but the latter would not accept them and asked his brother, Maulana 'IImu'd-Din [OCR?] ['Ilmu'd-Din] to accept them instead" (p. 232, folio 212).
- **shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam**: **he functioned as a petition-broker to the throne, and the book describes the mechanism in detail.** On the palanquin "there was a pile of papers. Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din mentioned that they were petitions from the needy and had been given to him so that he could recommend them to the Sultan" (p. 232, folio 212). And systematically: "whenever Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din went to the court of Sultan Qutbu'd-Din, the people of Delhi would place petitions inside the Shaikh's takht-i rawan (a type of palanquin similar to a moveable throne)… The petitions would then be brought in and, after having read them, the Sultan would write sympathetic replies. According to a sufi tradition, the Shaikh would remain at court until all the requests had been granted" (p. 232, folio 212). **A named vehicle — takht-i rawan — a three-vestibule court protocol, and a Sultan who "would squat respectfully at his feet".**
- **shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam**: **a hajj obtained by visiting a saint instead of travelling.** On "the tenth Zu'lhijja, the day when pilgrimages to Mecca were performed", with Nizamu'd-Din ill, "Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din decided instead to visit the Shaikh in order to gain from him the blessings of a hajj" (p. 232, folio 212).
- **shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam**: **he leaves the collapsing palace at Afghanpur minutes before a Sultan is killed in it.** He "went to receive Sultan Ghiyasu'd-Din Tughluq at the village of Afghanpur, about five miles from the city. He lunched with the Sultan in a specially constructed palace and left to perform afternoon prayers at the suggestion of Muhammad bin Tughlugq [OCR?] [Tughluq], the Sultan's son. After the Shaikh left, the palace collapsed and Sultan Ghiyasu'd-Din was crushed to death under its roof" (p. 232, folio 212). **The book draws no miraculous inference and names no agency** — (not stated).
- **shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam**: **his relations with the next Sultan, contrasted with the Chishtis'.** "Unlike his relations with the Chishti sufis of Delhi, those between Sultan Muhammad bin Tughluq and Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din were most cordial. In 1327-28, Bahram Aiba Kishlu Khan, the Governor of Multan," — **the sentence is cut off at the chunk boundary** (p. 232, folio 212). **This is the only place in the chunk where Ruknu'd-Din and Multan stand in the same sentence, and the sentence does not finish.**
- **shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam**: **his brother is named two incompatible ways** — "Shaikh Maulana 'IImu'd-Din" in the text against "the Siyvaru'l-Auliya' [OCR?] [Siyaru'l-Auliya'] gives the name of Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din's brother as Maulana '*Imadu'l-Millat wa'd-Din Isma'il. SA. p. 137. *Imadu'd-Din seems to be his title" (p. 231, folio 211, footnote 4).
- **shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam**: **many biographers make him, not his father, the pir of Amir Husain.** "As he was about fourteen years old when Shaikh Sadru'd-Din died, many biographers believe he was in fact the disciple of Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din, the son of Shaikh Sadru'd-Din" (p. 226, folio 206).

### shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar

- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar** (the book writes only "Baba Farid" and "the Baba"): **he is named as a teacher of a specific lost text.** Of Qazi Hamidu'd-Din Nagauri's Lawa'ih: "Baba Farid lectured on the work to his chief disciples" (p. 215, folio 197). **The book says the work "no longer exists but 'vas [OCR?] [was] a most important and advanced sufi text book at that time"** (p. 215, folio 197).
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **he is the reason a correspondence survives, and the subject of the chunk's most vivid sama' anecdote.** "The Qazi's letters were not compiled in book form but those written to Baba Farid were preserved by him. Once the Baba wished to partake of sama' although no musician was present. Asking Maulana Badru'd-Din to bring him the Qazi's letters, he began to read them and instantly fellintoanecstaticswoon [OCR?] [fell into an ecstatic swoon]" (p. 216, folio 198). The verse that produced it is quoted in full on the same page.
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **no personal name beyond "Baba Farid" and "the Baba", no dates, no silsila, no Ajodhan, no tomb and no ʿurs are given for him** — all (not stated) (pp. 215–216, folios 197–198). See Doubts: the book names a separate "Shaikh Faridu'd-Din 'Attar" in this same range (pp. 217–218, folios 199–200).

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

Names, dates and titles below are exactly as the book prints them.

- **Qazi Hamidu'd-Din Nagauri** — the subject of pp. 215–217 (folios 197–199). His dispute with the 'ulama' "was a protracted one"; "The Qazi's works were studied enthusiastically by both Chishtiyya and Suhrawardiyya sufis" (p. 215, folio 197). Works: **Lawa'ih (Flashes of Light)**, lost; the **'Jshgiyya [OCR?] ['Ishqiyya]**, the **Tawali' al-Shumus** and the **Risala Min Kalam**, whose "manuscripts… have survived" (p. 215, folio 197). Doctrine of the 'Ishqiyya: "although Lover and Beloved appear as two separate identities, they are in fact identical. Whoever sees them as two is absurd and whoever does not see them at all is insane"; "Love is the source of everything that exists. Fire is the burning quality of love, air is the aspect of restlessness in love; water is the movement of love and earth is its immutable aspect" (p. 215, folio 197). The Tawali' al-Shumus "is a detailed exposition of the names of Allah. The greatest name of God is Huwa (He)" (p. 215, folio 197). **He did not build a following: "Despite his eminence as a scholar and in the sufi movement, Qazi Hamidu'd-Din Nagauri did not generally enrol disciples, and only four were named"** (p. 216, folio 198). His son **Maulana Nasihu'd-Din** "lived in Delhi and also enrolled disciples" (p. 217, folio 199).
- **Qazi Minhaj Siraj** — "the author of the Tabagat-i Nasiri [OCR?] [Tabaqat-i Nasiri]"; "Despite his official position as a Qazi, Minhaj Siraj was a great supporter of sama'" (p. 215, folio 197).
- **Maulana Ruknu'd-Din Samargandi [OCR?] [Samarqandi]** — "an inveterate opponent of sama'", who "accompanied by a group of followers, rushed to the house where it was being held" (p. 215, folio 197).
- **Shaikh Qutbu'd-Din Bakhtiyar** — present at that sama' gathering with "other distinguished sufis" (p. 215, folio 197). **Not identified further, and not the same man as the Sultan Qutbu'd-Din of pp. 231–232** — see Doubts.
- **Qutbu'd-Din Kashani** — "A distinguished scholar named Qutbu'd-Din Kashani believed that everything he and other students had read, as well as what they had not read, were to be found in the treatises of Qazi Hamidu'd-Din" (p. 216, folio 198).
- **Shaikh Ahmad Naharwani** — "Onewasa weaver [OCR?] [One was a weaver]", the Qazi's first named disciple; the thief story and the gift of "about twelve yards of cloth he had woven himself"; "Later he became an eminent sufi with a large number of followers" (p. 216, folio 198).
- **Shaikh 'Ainu'd-Din** — "The Qazi's second disciple… was a butcher" (p. 216, folio 198).
- **Shaikh Shahi Muy-tab (Hair-Rope Maker)** — third disciple, "lived in Bada'un"; the milk-pudding judgement, and his blood-letting expiation (pp. 216–217, folios 198–199); refused at first to pray for Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya', "considering it an impertinence. He excused himself by saying he was merely an artisan" (p. 217, folio 199).
- **Khwaja Mahmud Muyina-Duz (Tailor of Fur)** — "The Akhbaru'l-Akhyar mentions a fourth disciple of Qazi Hamidu'd-Din"; "Like his pir he was a close companion of Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din and was buried near the Khwaja's tomb" (p. 217, folio 199).
- **Shaikh Jalalu'd-Din Tabrizi** — the longest single narrative in the chunk, pp. 217–220 (folios 199–202). "The second most outstanding disciple of Shaikh Shihabu'd-Din Suhrawardi, who became famous in Bengal"; "He studied at several places, including Bukhara"; he and his father were disciples of **Shaikh Abu Sa'id Tabrizi**, and after that Shaikh's death he "went to Baghdad and became the disciple of Shaikh Shihabu'd-Din" (p. 217, folio 199); he built a portable stove to keep his aged pir's food hot and "continued to serve his pir with great devotion for seven years" (p. 217, folio 199); he "did not remain long in Multan and travelled to Delhi, via Ajodhan" (p. 218, folio 200); the haunted-house test, the slave-boy episode and the Gawhar accusation at Delhi (p. 218, folio 200); Bada'un, the Qazi Kamalu'd-Din dream and the conversion of the Katihar curd seller (p. 219, folio 201); Lakhnauti, Devatalla and the demolished temple (p. 219, folio 201); **"The date of Shaikh Jalalu'd-Din Tabrizi's death is unknown, and no reliable information of his Bengali khalifas is available"** (p. 220, folio 202). **See Doubts: he is not mapped, and this chunk shows why.**
- **Shaikh Faridu'd-Din 'Attar** — "the great poet and mystic", visited by Jalalu'd-Din Tabrizi at Nishapur, whose personality so overwhelmed him "that he had been unable to recall the mental image of Shaikh Shihabu'd-Din" (pp. 217–218, folios 199–200). **A third distinct Farid in this book** — see Doubts.
- **Sultan Shamsu'd-Din** — welcomed Jalalu'd-Din Tabrizi to Delhi, ordered him housed near the palace, convened the mahzar and dismissed the Shaikhu'l-Islam (pp. 218–219, folios 200–201). Identified in a damaged footnote as "Sultan Shamsu d-Din [hutmish [OCR?] [Iltutmish] is meant here" (p. 216, folio 198, footnote 4).
- **Najmu'd-Din Sughra, the Shaikhu'l-Islam** — resented Jalalu'd-Din's presence, gave him the haunted house **Baitu'l-Jinn**, hired the dancing girl, and "was dismissed by the Sultan" (pp. 218–219, folios 200–201).
- **Gawhar** — "A dancing girl, called Gawhar, was employed for a sum of 500 dinars"; "the galaxy of 'ulama' and sufis so overwhelmed her that she admitted the charge was false" (pp. 218–219, folios 200–201).
- **Qazi Kamalu'd-Din** — "the local administrator" of Bada'un, who "had written a number of treatises on the ways to perform prayers"; after the dream of God's throne he "apologized and enrolled his son as his disciple" (p. 219, folio 201).
- **'Ali / 'Ali Maula** — a curd seller and robber "from Katihar, a place which abounded with robbers", converted at sight and renamed 'Ali; "He possessed one hundred thousand jitals and these he presented to the Shaikh"; taught only "to perform prayers five times daily", he "became so renowned for his piety that many great sufis, the 'ulama' and others craved for his blessing"; "He was present at the ceremony of the turban-tying of Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya'" (p. 219, folio 201).
- **Lakhnauti, Devatalla (Deva Mahal), Pandua, Tabrizabad** — Jalalu'd-Din's Bengal sites; "He moved to Devatalla (Deva Mahal) near Pandua in northern Bengal"; "Devatalla came to be known as Tabrizabad and attracted a large number of pilgrims" (pp. 219–220, folios 201–202).
- **Shah Jalal of Sylhet** — "In the Rihla of Ibn Battuta, Shah Jalal of Sylhet is confused with Shaikh Jalal al-Tabrizi whom he had visited" (p. 220, folio 202).
- **Halayodha Misra** — "the author of Shek Subhodya, also made the same mistake. The author is said to have been the court poet of Lakshmansena, the last Sena ruler of Bengal, but the work is of a later date" (p. 220, folio 202).
- **Shaikh Sadru'd-Din 'Arif** — son and khalifa of Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya (p. 220, folio 202); the divorce affair with Balban's son (p. 223, folio 203); his malfuzat, the **Kunuzu'l-Fawa'id**, compiled by **Khwaja Ziya'u'd-Din** and "no longer extant" (p. 223, folio 203); his teaching that "No breath should be inhaled or exhaled without zikr, for perpetual zikr was a divine light which removed all darkness" (p. 223, folio 203); **"Shaikh Sadru'd-Din died on 23 Zu'lhijja 684/19 February 1286"** (p. 223, folio 203) — the chunk's most precise death date.
- **Shaikh Hamidu'd-Din Suwali** — cursed the son of Baha'u'd-Din "who had harassed him for not going to congregational prayers" (p. 220, folio 202).
- **Balban's son, Prince Muhammad** — "Sadru'd-Din's relations with Balban's son, Prince Muhammad, were cordial and the khangah [OCR?] [khanqah] received from him many gifts" (p. 220, folio 202); "a drunkard" with "a temperamental personality", the divorce-and-remarriage affair, and his death when "Multan was invaded by the Mongols" (p. 223, folio 203). **Jamali gives him a different name — see Doubts.**
- **Ruknu'd-Din Ibrahim, a son of Iltutmish** — father of Prince Muhammad's wife (p. 223, folio 203). **A second Ruknu'd-Din** — see Doubts.
- **Shaikh 'Abdu'l-Haqq** — the authority for the Kunuzu'l-Fawa'id (p. 223, folio 203).
- **Hasan Afghan** — illiterate disciple of Baha'u'd-Din; the rebuke to the Imam whose mind "travelled from here to Delhi, did some shopping, and then went to Khurasan and Multan, then back to the Mosque"; "Although illiterate, Hasan Afghan could recognize Qur'anic verses from some Persian and Arabic writings" (pp. 223–224, folios 203–204).
- **Shaikh Fakhru'd-Din Ibrahim, "popularly known as 'Iraqi, because this was his takhallus ('nom de plume')"** — pp. 224–226 (folios 204–206). "He came from the neighbourhood of Hamadan, where he had memorized the Qur'an by heart and would recite it in his melodious voice. He built a beautiful madrasa in Hamadan where he lectured"; he followed a qalandar party "in the guise of a qalandar, with shaved beard and eyebrows" (p. 224, folio 204); Multan, the cell, the ghazal, the khirqa and the marriage (pp. 224–225, folios 204–205); then Mecca, Asia Minor, "At Quniya (Iconium) he attended lectures given by Shaikh Sadru'd-Din on the Fusus al-Hikam by Shaikh Muhi'u'd-Din Ibn al-'Arabi", and composed "his famous treatise, the Lama'at (Flashes or Effulgences)" (p. 225, folio 205); Tuqat, Egypt — "the Sultan became Iraqi's disciple" — then Syria and Damascus; **"'Iraqi died on 8 Zu'lqa'da 688/23 November 1289 and was buried near the tomb of Ibn al-'Arabi"** (p. 225, folio 205). His son **Kabiru'd-Din** "who had come from Multan, joined his father" (p. 225, folio 205). His **Diwan**, the masnawi **'Ushshag-Nama [OCR?] ['Ushshaq-Nama] (Book of Lovers)**, and the Lama'at, on which **Jami** wrote the **Ashi'atu'l-Lama'at (Rays of Flashes)** (p. 225, folio 205).
- **Mu'inu'd-Din Parwana** — "An important dignitary of Asia Minor, named Mu'inu'd-Din Parwana, built 'Iraqi a khanqah at Tuqat which became an important centre for sufi musical gatherings" (p. 225, folio 205).
- **Shaikh Sadru'd-Din of Quniya** — the lecturer on the Fusus al-Hikam (p. 225, folio 205). **The book does not distinguish him by any further name from Shaikh Sadru'd-Din 'Arif of Multan** — see Doubts.
- **Amir Husain / Husaini**, "the son of 'Alim bin Abi'l-Hasan al-Husaini" — pp. 226–230 (folios 206–210). Born "about 671/1272-73" by Rizvi's reckoning from the Nuzhatu'l-Arwah, in which "the author says he was then forty and that he had written the work in 711/1311-12" (p. 226, folio 206); "Amir Husain's birth place was at Ghizv, a village in Ghur"; "he went to Multan and became a disciple of Shaikh Sadru'd-Din"; "For a long time he stayed in Multan and wrote verses in praise of Sultan Jalalu'd-Din Firuz Khalji (1290-96). He then migrated to Herat where he became very famous" (pp. 226, folio 206). Works named: **Nuzhatu'l-Arwah (Delight of Souls)**, **Kanzu'r-Rumuz (The Treasure of Mysteries)**, **Tarabu'l-Majalis (Emotion of Mystic Assemblies)**, **Zadu'l-Musafirin**, the **Diwan**, **Sirat al-Mustagim [OCR?] [al-Mustaqim]**, **Ruh al-Arwah**, **Sirr-Nama** — the last three "are quoted in various texts but have not yet been discovered" — and **Hajt Ganj [OCR?] [Haft Ganj] (Seven Treasuries)**, "recently been brought to light" (p. 229, folio 209).
- **Sa'du'd-Din Mahmud Shabistari, "(d. 720/1320)"** — "the celebrated mystic of Tabriz", to whom Amir Husain wrote "Early in 1311… listing the following fifteen questions on sufism" (p. 226, folio 206); his answers run pp. 227–229 (folios 207–209).
- **Mansur al-Hallaj** — named in Shabistari's eighth answer: "Someone like Mansur al-Hallaj, who has discarded his phenomenal self can say 'I am the Truth.'" (p. 228, folio 208).
- **Sa'di of Shiraz** — "Shaikh Abu 'Abdu'llah Musharrafu'd-Din bin Muslih Sa'di of Shiraz, the author of the Gulistan and Bustan was one of the greatest Persian poets"; "(b. between 610—5/1213-19, d. 691/1292)"; "Sa'di's Gulistan mirrors his infinite humanitarianism, religious tolerance, mild scepticism and contempt for wealth" (p. 229, folio 209, text and footnote 1).
- **Sana'i** — named for the **Hadigatu'l-Hagqiqa [OCR?] [Hadiqatu'l-Haqiqa]**, which the Zadu'l-Musafirin "imitates" (p. 229, folio 209).
- **Maulana Husamu'd-Din** — a disciple of Sadru'd-Din who "migrated to Bada'un and lived there until his death. He appears not to have had many disciples, however, the people of Bada'un gave him the title, Maulana of Multan" (p. 230, folio 210).
- **Shaikh Ahmad-i-Ma'shuq** — "a native of Qandahar where his father was a merchant. Ahmad-i-Ma'shuq was an alcoholic"; converted by sherbet; "distributed his property to dervishes and for seven years, having withdrawn totally from the world, remained completely engrossed in meditation"; "In the latter part of his life, Shaikh Ahmad-i Ma'shugq [OCR?] [Ma'shuq] was so often in a state of ecstasy that he even abandoned obligatory prayers" (p. 230, folio 210).
- **Shaikh Salahu'd-Din Dervish** — "another outstanding disciple of Shaikh Sadru'd-Din, was a contemporary of Sultan Muhammad bin Tughluq"; "he migrated to Delhi from Multan, and began living near Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din Chiragh-i Dihli. He strongly opposed the Sultan, and unlike Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din, was not humble and forbearing* towards the political powers of his time" (p. 230, folio 210).
- **Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya', "the Sultanu'l-Masha'ikh (the Sultan of the sufis)"** — pp. 217, 219, 223, 231–232 (folios 199, 201, 203, 211–212). His Kilukhari mosque Friday prayers and his jama'at-khana at Ghiyaspur (p. 231, folio 211); his final illness and his reply "he had seen the Prophet Muhammad in a vision and had been summoned by him" (p. 232, folio 212); his servant **Iqbal** (p. 232, folio 212).
- **Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din** — the authority for the drought-and-sama' story (p. 216, folio 198) and for the Hasan Afghan story (p. 223, folio 203); **Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din Chiragh-i Dihli** at p. 230 (folio 210). **The book does not state whether these are the same man.**
- **The Sultans, in the order the chunk gives them** — Sultan Shamsu'd-Din (Iltutmish) (pp. 216, 218; folios 198, 200); Balban (p. 220, folio 202); Sultan Jalalu'd-Din Firuz Khalji (1290-96) (p. 226, folio 206); Sultan 'Ala'u'd-Din Khalji (p. 231, folio 211); Sultan Qutbu'd-Din (pp. 231–232, folios 211–212); Sultan Ghiyasu'd-Din Tughluq (p. 232, folio 212); Sultan Muhammad bin Tughluq (pp. 230, 232; folios 210, 212); **Bahram Aiba Kishlu Khan, "the Governor of Multan"**, in "1327-28" (p. 232, folio 212).
- **Gisu Daraz** — named as the authority for the Deva Mahal demon story, which Rizvi calls "stock-in-trade": "It was related by such an authority as Gisu Daraz" (p. 219, folio 201, footnote 4).
- **Modern scholars named** — "M.A. Rahman, Social and cultural history of Bengal, 1, Karachi, 1963, p. 99", quoted only to be rejected (p. 220, folio 202); "K.A. Nizami in the Encyclopaedia of Islam, new, II1 [OCR?] [III], p. 635" (p. 226, folio 206).

(p. 222, folio not stated). **The same manuscript and shelfmark supplied the samaʿ plate in chunk 006 (there at f. 119 R); this is f. 79 R.** The rest of p. 222 is unreadable OCR of the image itself.

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **A khanqah founded, endowed with a langar, and given agricultural land bought by the Shaikh himself — the clearest institutional-foundation sentence in the chunk.** "He first lived at Lakhnauti, constructed a khangah [OCR?] [khanqah] and attached a /angar [OCR?] [langar] to it. He also bought some gardens and land to be attached to the monastery" (p. 219, folio 201). **A named income-producing endowment — gardens and land — bought, not granted.**
- **A temple demolished and a khanqah built on the site, with mass conversion as the stated result.** "There a kafir (either a Hindu or a Buddhist) had erected a large temple and a well. The Shaikh demolished the temple and constructed a takiya (khangah) [OCR?] [khanqah] and converted a large number of kafirs" (p. 219, folio 201). **The book gives "takiya" as a synonym for khanqah here. The well is not said to have been destroyed** — (not stated).
- **A site becoming a pilgrimage centre, renamed after the saint, with cross-community veneration.** "the Shaikh's memory was treasured by both Hindus and Muslims alike. Devatalla came to be known as Tabrizabad and attracted a large number of pilgrims" (p. 220, folio 202). **No numbers, no ʿurs date and no custodian are given** — (not stated).
- **Burial near a saint's tomb, recorded twice as a deliberate practice.** Khwaja Mahmud Muyina-Duz "was buried near the Khwaja's tomb" (p. 217, folio 199); 'Iraqi "was buried near the tomb of Ibn al-'Arabi" (p. 225, folio 205).
- **Royal payment on arrival and on departure, itemised, and redistributed in full.** "During each visit he paid 200,000 tankas at the Shaikh's arrival and 500,000 tankas at his departure. The Shaikh always distributed the entire amount among Delhi's population" (p. 231, folio 211).
- **Princely gifts to a khanqah as an ordinary flow.** "Sadru'd-Din's relations with Balban's son, Prince Muhammad, were cordial and the khangah [khanqah] received from him many gifts" (p. 220, folio 202).
- **A Shaikh who refuses gifts, and one who refuses to pay dervishes.** Ruknu'd-Din "would not accept" the cloth and gold and passed them to his brother (p. 232, folio 212); Qazi Hamidu'd-Din "refused to give payments to visiting dervishes, saying that, he too, was running a spiritual shop, and it was therefore most improper of them to ask for anything" (p. 216, folio 198). **The metaphor of the khanqah as a shop is the book's, not mine.**
- **Money given to a saint and held in trust, distributed back through him.** The convert "possessed one hundred thousand jitals and these he presented to the Shaikh. Jalalu'd-Din asked him to keep the money in trust and distribute it through him" (p. 219, folio 201). **A named unit of account — jitals — and a trusteeship arrangement.**
- **A Shaikh's estate as inheritable property, and its size.** "property worth 700,000 tankas", one of seven sons inheriting it, given away at once (p. 220, folio 202); the robbers' ransom version, in which the sons' shares are extracted one after another (p. 220, folio 202).
- **Petition-brokerage between the populace and the throne, described as a routine of the palanquin.** "the people of Delhi would place petitions inside the Shaikh's takht-i rawan"; "the Sultan would write sympathetic replies"; "the Shaikh would remain at court until all the requests had been granted" (p. 232, folio 212); and the loose pile of papers on the palanquin at Ghiyaspur (p. 232, folio 212).
- **Court protocol for receiving a Shaikh, in three stages.** "The court was entered after passing through three vestibules; the Shaikh would travel through the first two on his palanquin, and would then be greeted by the Sultan in the third. He would be taken inside where the Sultan would squat respectfully at his feet" (p. 232, folio 212). And on arrival in the city, "The Sultan himself went to welcome him and rode back with him to the city" (p. 231, folio 211).
- **Blessings of a hajj obtained by visiting a living saint on the day of the hajj.** "It was the tenth Zu'lhijja, the day when pilgrimages to Mecca were performed… Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din decided instead to visit the Shaikh in order to gain from him the blessings of a hajj" (p. 232, folio 212).
- **Turban-tying as a public ceremony.** The convert 'Ali "was present at the ceremony of the turban-tying of Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya'" (p. 219, folio 201).
- **Succession by garment: the turban and the khirqa, both inherited objects.** Ruknu'd-Din at four donned Baha'u'd-Din's turban; on succeeding, "he wore the same turban and Shaikh Sadru'd-Din's khirga [khirqa]" (p. 231, folio 211). And Baha'u'd-Din "presented him with his own khirga [khirqa] to wear" when completing 'Iraqi's training (p. 224, folio 204).
- **Hereditary succession stated flatly, twice in three generations.** "He appointed his own son, Sadru'd-Din… his khalifa" (p. 220, folio 202); "The successor of Shaikh Sadru'd-Din was his son, Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din Abu'l Fath" (p. 230, folio 210).
- **The cell as a formal retreat inside the khanqah.** 'Iraqi "began living in a cell which the Shaikh had assigned him, as a spiritual retreat" (p. 224, folio 204).
- **A patron builds a khanqah as a music venue.** Mu'inu'd-Din Parwana "built 'Iraqi a khanqah at Tuqat which became an important centre for sufi musical gatherings" (p. 225, folio 205).
- **Samaʿ as a contested public event with a legal procedure around it.** Qazi Hamidu'd-Din "arranged a sama' gathering"; the opposing party "were then faced with the dilemma of whether or not to enter the house without the owner being present. However, they decided to withdraw, after which sama' was continued" (p. 215, folio 197). **A property rule, not a doctrinal one, decides the case.**
- **Samaʿ without a musician, performed by reading letters.** "Once the Baba wished to partake of sama' although no musician was present" and was brought the Qazi's letters instead (p. 216, folio 198).
- **Samaʿ commissioned by the state as a rain ritual.** "The Sultan, whose name he fails to mention, sent a message to the dervishes that his business was war and theirs was to pray for the welfare of the people, therefore they should pray for rain. Qazi Hamidu'd-Din advised the Sultan to arrange an entertainment for the dervishes in order that they might perform sama'. The Sultan was pleased, and after the sama' rain fell heavily" (p. 216, folio 198). **An explicit division of labour between throne and khanqah, stated by the throne.**
- **Samaʿ as the property of holy men, and the charlatan's inversion of it.** Amir Husaini "considered [sama'] to be the exclusive practice of holy men"; "Charlatans considered that indulgence in lust was Tariga [OCR?] [Tariqa], to them good food and merrymaking were asceticism, the forsaking of prayers, worship and an opposition to Shari'a was Hagiqa [OCR?] [Haqiqa]" (p. 229, folio 209).
- **The mahzar as an institution: a convened inquiry with a chairman and a stated quorum.** The Sultan "organized a mahzar to investigate the allegation. About two hundred eminent sufis and 'ulama' were invited" (p. 218, folio 200); the chairmanship was proposed by the accuser's ally and accepted by the Sultan; "The Sultan said that such respect by a chairman for the accused had made the mahzar useless" (p. 218, folio 200). Amir Husain is also said to have attended it "in the reign of Iltutmish… with his spiritual director" (p. 226, folio 206) — **which Rizvi's own chronology makes impossible** (see Doubts).
- **Cash sums recorded in three currencies of account across the chunk** — "1,500 dinars" for a Turkic slave boy, "500 dinars" for the dancing girl of which "250 dinars in cash" with the rest "deposited with a Muslim baggal [OCR?] [baqqal] (grain merchant)" (p. 218, folio 200); "one hundred thousand jitals" (p. 219, folio 201); "700,000 tankas", "200,000 tankas" and "500,000 tankas" (pp. 220, 231; folios 202, 211).
- **Khidmat as a measured service with a stated duration.** Jalalu'd-Din Tabrizi "continued to serve his pir with great devotion for seven years" (p. 217, folio 199) — the fact later used to justify a Shaikh carrying his shoes (p. 218, folio 200). Ahmad-i Ma'shuq likewise withdrew "for seven years" (p. 230, folio 210).
- **An ascetic self-punishment recorded as an act of solidarity, with a barber as its instrument.** Shahi Muy-tab, "summoning a barber… asked him to take from his body blood equivalent to the quantity of perspiration lost by his disciples, in order that he too might share their suffering" (p. 217, folio 199).
- **A rule about equal distribution of food, enforced as a fault.** The two sufis drank the boiling milk "rather than have it wasted. The Shaikh replied that it was better to waste the milk than not to have distributed it equally. He refused to accept their apologies" (p. 217, folio 199).
- **Artisan and trading disciples as a class.** Of the Qazi's four named disciples, a weaver, a butcher, a hair-rope maker and a fur tailor (pp. 216–217, folios 198–199); Ahmad-i Ma'shuq's father "was a merchant" (p. 230, folio 210).
- **Ecstasy as a ground for abandoning obligatory prayer, recorded without censure.** Ahmad-i Ma'shuq "was so often in a state of ecstasy that he even abandoned obligatory prayers" (p. 230, folio 210).
- **A jamaʿat-khana and a congregational mosque named as distinct spaces in one saint's week.** The Kilukhari mosque "where the latter always went for Friday prayers" and "Ghiyaspur to see the Shaikh at his jama'at-khana" (p. 231, folio 211).
- **What is absent from pp. 215–232 and the consolidator should not look for here:** there is **no ʿurs, no mela, no sajjada nashin, no Auqaf, no custodian, no revenue grant by a ruler, no visitor number, no architectural date and no description of any tomb building**. The tombs that appear are named only as burial locations for someone else (pp. 217, 225; folios 199, 205). The one endowment is land and gardens **bought** by a Shaikh (p. 219, folio 201); the one royal money-flow is per-visit cash that is given away the same day (p. 231, folio 211).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

Attributions are the book's own, including its hedges ("is recorded as", "according to sufi tradition", "obviously mythical", "reads like other stock-in-trade stories").

- **Rain after samaʿ.** "According to Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din, at one time there was a long drought"; after the Sultan's entertainment and the samaʿ, "rain fell heavily" (p. 216, folio 198).
- **Evil spirits vacating a house before a saint enters it.** "Before Shaikh Jalalu'd-Din entered the house, to the bewilderment of Najmu'd-Din, the evil spirits had departed" (p. 218, folio 200).
- **Clairvoyance, with the author's own naturalistic alternative attached in the same sentence.** "Shaikh Jalalu'd-Din is recorded as knowing that this was happening through his own spiritual powers, although it is possible the slave may have informed him" (p. 218, folio 200).
- **A dream of God's throne that converts a Qazi.** "That night the Qazi saw God's throne in a dream with Shaikh Jalalu'd-Din praying before it. Next day, he revisited the Shaikh, apologized and enrolled his son as his disciple" (p. 219, folio 201).
- **Instantaneous conversion by sight.** The Katihar robber, "as soon as his eyes fell on the Shaikh, he was so impressed that he became a Muslim and was renamed 'Ali" (p. 219, folio 201).
- **A demon killed with a staff and an idol vanquished — a story Rizvi disowns while printing it in full.** "An anecdote relating to Shaikh Jalalu'd-Din's stay in Deva Mahal reads like other stock-in-trade stories and fairytales. It was related by such an authority as Gisu Daraz"; the Shaikh "killed the demon with a single blow from his staff"; "The sight of their vanquished idol prompted them to accept Islam" (p. 219, folio 201, footnote 4).
- **A prince killed by a saint's miracle.** "Multan was invaded by the Mongols and the Prince was killed, according to sufi tradition, due toa [OCR?] [due to a] miracle of Shaikh Sadru'd-Din" (p. 223, folio 203).
- **Enlightenment transmitted through a shared drink.** "the Shaikh drank part of a glass of sherbet, and offered the rest to Ahmad. After drinking the cooling liquid, Ahmad-i Ma'shug [Ma'shuq] underwent an intense spiritual enlightenment" (p. 230, folio 210).
- **A prophecy made before birth.** Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya "prophesied his prominence while he was still in his mother's womb" (p. 230, folio 210).
- **A summons by the Prophet in a vision, as a refusal to be healed.** "the Sultanu'l-Masha'ikh replied he had seen the Prophet Muhammad in a vision and had been summoned by him" (p. 232, folio 212).
- **A collapsing palace a Shaikh has just left.** He "left to perform afternoon prayers at the suggestion of Muhammad bin Tughlugq [Tughluq]… After the Shaikh left, the palace collapsed and Sultan Ghiyasu'd-Din was crushed to death under its roof" (p. 232, folio 212). **The book attaches no miracle and no agency to this** — (not stated).
- **A body divided between intercessors, which Rizvi footnotes as yogic.** Shahi "would pray for Nizamu'd-Din's body from the head to the navel, and the others should pray for the recovery of each side of the Shaikh's lower parts"; footnote: "The division is influenced by the Hath- Yogic ideas" (p. 217, folio 199 and footnote 2).
- **A whole conversion cycle dismissed.** "The stories of Amir Husain's conversion to sufism are obviously mythical" (p. 226, folio 206).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Rizvi refutes a named modern scholar on who converted in Bengal, and does it in one sentence.** "There is no evidence that they were 'down-trodden and persecuted Buddhists and Hindus,' as a modern scholar writes" — footnoted to "M.A. Rahman, Social and cultural history of Bengal, 1, Karachi, 1963, p. 99" (p. 220, folio 202). **This is the chunk's one direct engagement with the caste-and-conversion literature, and it is a flat negative.**
- **He identifies a confusion of two saints that runs from Ibn Battuta to a Bengali text.** "In the Rihla of Ibn Battuta, Shah Jalal of Sylhet is confused with Shaikh Jalal al-Tabrizi whom he had visited. Halayodha Misra, the author of Shek Subhodya, also made the same mistake"; and he dates the text down: "the work is of a later date" (p. 220, folio 202).
- **He states the limits of the evidence for Bengal outright.** "The date of Shaikh Jalalu'd-Din Tabrizi's death is unknown, and no reliable information of his Bengali khalifas is available" (p. 220, folio 202).
- **He makes the Multan line the only continuous one, excluding the Bengal and Delhi Suhrawardis from the silsila's history.** The other disciples "were eminent in their own right, but the continuous history of the silsila can be traced only through the khalifas of Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya" (p. 220, folio 202).
- **He refuses to adjudicate between two sources on the fate of a Shaikh's fortune, and takes the outcome instead of the story.** "Regardless of which story is the correct one, it seems clear that wealth accumulated by Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din did not long remain with his sons" (p. 220, folio 202).
- **He applies the same move to a miracle story.** "Whether the story is true or not, there remains little doubt that the Shaikh was a man of independent views" (p. 223, folio 203).
- **He redates a figure against Jami and against Jamali, using the man's own colophons.** From the Nuzhatu'l-Arwah's statement of age and date of composition he derives the birth "about 671/1272-73"; and "Similarly, the date of his death, 16 Shawwal 718/11 December 1318, given by Jami is incorrect, for another of his works, entitled the Zadu'l- Musafirin… was composed in 720/1320" (p. 226, folio 206). **A dated work used to overturn a dated death — the chunk's one piece of real source criticism.**
- **He assigns an intention to a manuscript gift.** "Before his death 'Iraqi sent a copy of the Lama'at to Shaikh Sadru'd-Din 'Arif, obviously in an attempt to popularize Ibn al-'Arabi in India" (p. 226, folio 206). **The word is "obviously"; no evidence is offered.**
- **He evaluates a sufi author's prose against his own standard and finds it wanting.** "The ethical works of Husaini lack a mystical sensitivity and a passion in the expression of divine love and could even be described as pedagogical" (p. 229, folio 209).
- **He reads a yogic source into a sufi prayer practice, in a footnote and without argument.** "The division is influenced by the Hath- Yogic ideas" (p. 217, folio 199, footnote 2). **This is the same interpretive move chunk 006 recorded for Ghujduwani's eight principles.**
- **The chunk's doctrinal core is Shabistari's fifteen answers to Amir Husain, pp. 227–229 (folios 207–209)**, of which the load-bearing ones for shrine religion are: the Perfect Man who "journeys back down to the phenomenal world where he confirms to outward laws" and "Perfection in the saintly state would be seen in the Mahdi, 'the seal of the Saints'" (p. 227, folio 207); law and sainthood as shell and pearl — "The law can be compared to a shell and the state of sainthood to the pearl within it… it is the disposition of saintliness which sanctifies the efforts of devotees" (p. 228, folio 208); and the closing tolerance of other religions — sufis "are also ready to appreciate the positive and true aspects contained in other religions such as Christianity, Magianism and even in idolatry" (pp. 228–229, folios 208–209).
- **A verse that reads Hindu idol-worship as unwitting zikr, printed without authorial comment.** From the Zadu'l-Masafirin: "'Hindu, who always worships idols, / Every morning makes invocations. / On his tongue there is nothing but Thy zikr, / Brahmanical thread he wears ang [OCR?] [and] the names he takes are only intermediaries. / All these are part of his religion and faith, / In reality he sees nothing in his faith but Thou.'" (p. 230, folio 210). **Set against the temple demolition at Devatalla on p. 219 (folio 201), which the book also prints without comment.**
- **A saint characterised by his posture towards power, in explicit contrast to another.** Salahu'd-Din Dervish "strongly opposed the Sultan, and unlike Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din, was not humble and forbearing* towards the political powers of his time" (p. 230, folio 210); against Ruknu'd-Din, whose relations with Muhammad bin Tughluq were "most cordial", "Unlike his relations with the Chishti sufis of Delhi" (p. 232, folio 212).

**Archive-mapping doubts:**

- **`shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam` is mapped here, and I think this chunk settles it — but I am stating exactly what settles it and what does not, because two earlier workers flagged this as their weakest mapping.** What settles it: **the book prints the full name "Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din Abu'l Fath"** (p. 230, folio 210), and the archive row's principal figure is "Shah Rukn-e-Alam (Rukn-ud-Din Abul Fath)" — the *kunya* is the match, not the bare "Ruknu'd-Din" the earlier workers had. It is corroborated by descent: son of Shaikh Sadru'd-Din 'Arif, grandson of Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya (pp. 230–231, folios 210–211), which is the Multan Suhrawardi line the row belongs to. **What does NOT settle it, and a human should know this:** the book **never prints "Rukn-e-Alam"** in this chunk; it gives him **no death date**; it gives him **no tomb**; and **it never states in a completed sentence that he lived at or was based in Multan** — the only sentence putting him and Multan together is the one cut off at the chunk boundary, "In 1327-28, Bahram Aiba Kishlu Khan, the Governor of Multan," (p. 232, folio 212). The Multan links in this chunk belong to his father (p. 226, folio 206) and his father's disciples (p. 230, folio 210).
- **"Ruknu'd-Din" is at least three different men in this chunk, which is a new instance of the "Qutbu'd-Din" trap and should be added to the queue's list.** (i) **Maulana Ruknu'd-Din Samargandi**, "an inveterate opponent of sama'" (p. 215, folio 197); (ii) **Ruknu'd-Din Ibrahim, a son of Iltutmish**, father-in-law of Balban's son (p. 223, folio 203); (iii) **Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din Abu'l Fath**, the one mapped (p. 230, folio 210). **Only (iii) carries the id. The bare short form must never be mapped.**
- **`tomb-of-qutbuddin-aibak` was considered and is NOT mapped.** The chunk holds four different Qutbu'd-Dins and **none of them is Aibak**: "Shaikh Qutbu'd-Din Bakhtiyar" at the samaʿ gathering (p. 215, folio 197); "Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din", near whose tomb Khwaja Mahmud was buried (p. 217, folio 199); "Qutbu'd-Din Kashani", a scholar (p. 216, folio 198); and "Sultan Qutbu'd-Din", whose reign the book places **between 'Ala'u'd-Din Khalji and Ghiyasu'd-Din Tughluq** (pp. 231–232, folios 211–212) — which rules out Aibak on the book's own sequence. **The book never writes "Aibak" anywhere in pp. 215–232.**
- **`shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar` is mapped on the form "Baba Farid" alone, and there are now three Farids in play.** The book gives him no personal name, no dates, no Ajodhan, no silsila and no tomb (pp. 215–216, folios 197–198). Against him stand (i) the queue's known trap, Faridu'd-Din Mahmud of Nagaur, whom this book does not disambiguate, and (ii) **"Shaikh Faridu'd-Din 'Attar", the poet of Nishapur, named twice in this same chunk** (pp. 217–218, folios 199–200), who is plainly a different man. **My reason for mapping is that the book writes "Baba Farid" and "the Baba", which is the form it uses for Ganj-i Shakar and not for either of the others — but that is a form-of-name argument, not evidence, and a human should confirm it against the chapter's Chishti sections.**
- **`shrine-of-jalaluddin-surkh-posh-bukhari-jalaluddin-bukhari` is mapped, and this chunk resolves a standing queue trap in the process.** The earlier workers were told that "Shaikh Jalalu'd-Din Tabrizi" could be read onto this row or onto `shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht` and mapped neither. **This chunk shows they were right to, because the book itself treats them as two men and gives each a separate career on adjacent pages**: Shaikh Jalalu'd-Din **Tabrizi** goes Baghdad → Multan → Delhi → Bada'un → Bengal and dies on an unknown date (pp. 217–220, folios 199–202), while **Saiyid Jalalu'd-Din Bukhari, "Jalal Surkh (Red)"**, goes Bukhara → Bhakkar → Uch and dies at Uch (p. 223, folio 203). **The epithet "Jalal Surkh (Red)" plus Uch is what maps the second man to the Surkh-Posh row.** I considered **`shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht`** — also Uch, also Suhrawardi, also a "Sayyid Jalaluddin" — and did **not** map it: this man is a direct disciple of Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya (d. December 1262 per the book, p. 226, folio 206), which is the grandfather's generation, and the book gives no *Jahaniyan* or *Jahangasht* epithet anywhere. **Shaikh Jalalu'd-Din Tabrizi himself remains mapped to nothing, as before.**
- **`shrine-of-hafiz-muhammad-jamal-multani` was considered and is NOT mapped.** The name "Jamali" appears as a source siglum on almost every page of this chunk (pp. 216, 217, 220, 223, 224, 226, 230, 231, 232; folios 198, 199, 202, 203, 204, 206, 210, 211, 212). It is a different name from the row's "Hafiz Muhammad Jamal Multani", and it is cited as an authority on thirteenth- and fourteenth-century events. **Flagged only so the consolidator does not treat "Jamali" as a Multan shrine figure.**
- **Interim toponym ruling (a): Multan and Uch are the two names that would change this chunk's result if it reverses.** **Multan** appears without any shrine, tomb or ʿurs attached on pp. 217, 218, 223, 224, 225, 226, 230, 232 (folios 199, 200, 203, 204, 205, 206, 210, 212). - **Hinglaj is absent from pp. 215–232**, so the empty-id defect on `Shaktipeeth Shri Hinglaj Mata Mandir` does not arise and no id has been invented.
- **The unruled Hindu-deity question does not bite in this chunk.** No deity is named: the demolished temple at Devatalla has none (p. 219, folio 201), the Deva Mahal story names only "the demon" and "their god" (p. 219, folio 201, footnote 4), and the Zadu'l-Masafirin verse names "idols" and "Brahmanical thread" but no god (p. 230, folio 210).

**Internal contradictions in the chunk, kept side by side rather than resolved:**

- **Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din's visits to Delhi do not add up.** The book gives "visited Delhi twice" under 'Ala'u'd-Din Khalji (p. 231, folio 211), then "His three visits to the capital during Sultan Qutbu'd-Din's reign" (p. 231, folio 211), then "A visit to Delhi by Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din, **described as his fourth**, seems to have taken place in the reign of Sultan Ghiyasu'd-Din Tughluq" (p. 232, folio 212). **Two plus three is five, not three, and the fifth is called the fourth.** Rizvi's "described as" may be a hedge on his source, but he does not note the arithmetic. **The 700,000-tankas-per-visit figure should not be multiplied out until this is checked.**
- **Amir Husain cannot have attended the mahzar the book says he attended.** "Jamali describes him… adding that in the reign of Iltutmish he visited Delhi with his spiritual director for the mahzar which had been organized to investigate the allegations against Shaikh Jalalu'd-Din Tabrizi" (p. 226, folio 206) — while Rizvi's own reckoning on the same page puts Husain's birth "about 671/1272-73". **Rizvi rebuts the discipleship claim on chronology but prints the mahzar claim without rebutting it.**
- **Two incompatible accounts of Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya's estate, from two named sources, on one page.** Jamali: Sadru'd-Din alone inherited 700,000 tankas and "immediately gave the entire amount to the poor". "The Sururu's-Sudur, however, gives a different story": a cursed son was abducted by robbers who took his share and then extracted Sadru'd-Din's as well (p. 220, folio 202). **Rizvi declines to choose.**
- **The prince of the divorce story is named two ways.** The text calls him Balban's son "Prince Muhammad" (pp. 220, 223; folios 202, 203); the footnote says "According to Jamali the name of the Prince was Qadr Khan" (p. 223, folio 203, footnote 1).
- **Amir Husain's death date: Jami's "16 Shawwal 718/11 December 1318" against a work "composed in 720/1320"** (p. 226, folio 206). Rizvi rules Jami "incorrect" but supplies **no replacement date** — (not stated).
- **One title is glossed two different ways three pages apart.** "Zadu'l- Musafirin (Supplies for Travellers on the Mystic Road)" (p. 226, folio 206) against "Zadu'l-Musafirin (Provisions for Travellers on the Sufi Path)" (p. 229, folio 209) — and the title itself is printed "Zadu'l-Masafirin" in the text of p. 230 (folio 210) against "Zadu'l-Musafirin" in that page's own footnote. **Two glosses is Rizvi; the Masafirin/Musafirin split is OCR.**
- **"Sadru'd-Din" is two men and the book does not separate them.** Shaikh Sadru'd-Din 'Arif of Multan, d. 23 Zu'lhijja 684/19 February 1286 (p. 223, folio 203), and the Shaikh Sadru'd-Din at Quniya who lectured on the Fusus al-Hikam and to whom 'Iraqi sent the Lama'at (pp. 225–226, folios 205–206). **The second is not given a nisba anywhere in this chunk, and p. 226 (folio 206) names the Multan one as the recipient of the Lama'at while p. 225 (folio 205) names the Quniya one as 'Iraqi's teacher — a consolidator must not merge them.**
- **"Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din" appears with and without a nisba** — bare at pp. 216 and 223 (folios 198, 203), as "Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din Chiragh-i Dihli" at p. 230 (folio 210). **The book does not say whether the bare form means the same man** — (not stated).
- **Temple demolition and religious tolerance sit five pages apart without comment.** "The Shaikh demolished the temple and constructed a takiya (khangah) [khanqah]" (p. 219, folio 201) against sufis being "ready to appreciate the positive and true aspects contained in other religions such as Christianity, Magianism and even in idolatry" (p. 229, folio 209) and the verse reading the Hindu's worship as zikr (p. 230, folio 210). **Rizvi notes no tension.**
- **The book's own transliteration conventions are not damage and must not be "fixed"** — "Saiyid" for *Sayyid*, "sama'" not *samāʿ*, "zikr" not *dhikr*, "Hadis", "khalifa", "mahzar", "Nagauri", "Bada'un", "Ajodhan" (**the book writes "Ajodhan", never "Pakpattan"** — confirmed again at p. 218, folio 200).
