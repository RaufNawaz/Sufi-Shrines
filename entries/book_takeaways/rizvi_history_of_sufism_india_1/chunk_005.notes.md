## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- **Ghazali** — the chunk's opening pages close an account of him. His comments on Abu Yazid and Hallaj
  are "a significant reminder to mystics that the words of passionate lovers in the state of ecstasy
  should not be spoken but remain concealed" (p. 82, folio 66). **His "principal contribution to sufism
  was in the great emphasis he laid on the observance of the outward form of religious activities," the
  consummation of sufism being "impossible if associated with a neglect of formal observance"** (p. 82,
  folio 66). He condemned "pride, vanity, self-conceit, self-deception, envy, jealousy, anger, malice,
  love of wealth and status," but "like al-Muhasibi, it was hypocrisy, which he considered to be a form
  of polytheism, that was singled out for his most scathing attack"; repentance "involved an expiation
  for past evil and a simultaneous examination of one's inner life" (p. 82, folio 66). The chunk's first
  sentence is the tail of a quotation on rulers who "do not freely grant a private audience except after
  the offer of gifts and bribery" — **its subject is in the previous chunk and cannot be recovered here**
  — **(not stated)** (p. 82, folio 66, footnote citing "Al-Ghazali, Jhya al-'Ulum al-Din'" [OCR?]
  [*Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din*]). He also appears as political theorist: "A number of orthodox intellectuals,
  including Ghazali, popularized the idea of the interdependence of Muslim kingship and Sunni
  orthodoxy… Monarchs, he wrote, were entrusted with the material well-being of God's servants. Their
  unique position endowed them with a special kind of divine light (farr)" (pp. 83–84, folios 67–68).

## Practices, institutions, economy

**This is what the chunk is for. It contains a caliphal land grant with a stated plot size, the earliest
detailed khanqah plan the author says exists, a written rule of discipline in ten clauses, two resident
head-counts, a named fundraiser, an explicit refusal of endowment income, an intercession doctrine with a
stated radius, and one dated tomb rebuilding.** It contains **no ʿurs, no mela, no sajjada nashin or
gaddi succession, no Auqaf, no waqf named as such, no land-revenue figure, no visitor statistics beyond
the two counts below, and no shrine anywhere in South Asia** — **(not stated)** on all of those.

- **The chunk's structural claim about sufi economic independence, which frames everything that follows.**
  "The political upheavals from the tenth to the thirteenth centuries reinforced sufi beliefs in the
  transitory nature of the world and the necessity to remain both apart and independent from it. By
  contrast, the 'ulama,' dependent on the state economically, became embroiled in political activities.
  Sufis were now in a position to remain independent from the government for merchants and craft guilds
  assumed positions as their patrons. The intimate relationship between guilds and sufi khanqahs can be
  seen in stories connected with the life of Abu Sa'id bin Abi'l-Khair" (p. 84, folio 68). **A stated
  two-sector model: state-funded 'ulama' against merchant-funded sufis.**
- **Ribats, khanqahs and the private house as rival venues.** "Although there were numerous ribats
  (hospices) and khanqahs scattered throughout Khurasan, many sufis in Mayhana, Abu Sa'id's father
  included, preferred to live, and hold sama' rituals, in their own houses" (p. 84, folio 68). The boy
  was "taken to the house of a sufi where a sama' party was being held," where a quatrain was "sung by a
  gawwal [OCR?] [*qawwal*] or musician" (p. 84, folio 68). **A named performer-role and a domestic
  sama' assembly, with no building, endowment or fee attached** — **(not stated)**.
- **The physical fabric of a deserted ribat, described from the inside.** Abu Sa'id "practised ascetic
  exercises in a ribat-i kuhan, an old deserted ribat" (p. 85, folio 69); his father's account names its
  parts — a gate that shuts, a roof one can go up on, "a chapel which was in the ribat" with a door and a
  "chapel window", "a stick lying on the floor" with "a rope fastened to it", and "a pit that was at the
  corner of the chapel"; the penance ends "in the middle of the ribat" with ablution (pp. 85–86, folios
  69–70). **The chunk's only description of a ribat's interior.**
- **The khirqa as the material token of initiation, received twice from two masters.** "Abu Sa'id obtained
  a khirga [OCR?] [*khirqa*] (a cloak which marked sufi initiation) from the celebrated mystic writer,
  al-Sulami. He also received another khirga, this time from an Amul sufi, Abu'l-'Abbas al-Qassab"
  (p. 85, folio 69).
- **A quantified ascetic regime: eighteen self-imposed obligations, quoted by the book in the Shaikh's own
  voice, several of them economic.** "I fasted continually; 1 [OCR?] [*I*] abstained from unlawful food;
  I practised recollection (zikr) uninterruptedly; I kept awake at night; I never reclined on the ground;
  I never slept but in a sitting posture; I sat facing the Ka'ba… **I did not beg**; I was content and
  resigned to God's will; **I always sat in the mosque and did not go into the market, because the
  Prophet said that the market is the filthiest of places and the mosque the cleanest**… Every
  four-and-twenty hours I completed a recitation of the Qur'an… For a whole year I conversed with no one"
  (p. 86, folio 70). **"eighteen things" is the book's own count; the quoted list as printed does not
  obviously run to eighteen discrete items — see `## Doubts`.**
- **Menial service and organised begging as spiritual technique.** "In order to achieve self-abasement,
  he performed various services to the poor such as bring them water, and helping them in heavy
  labouring. For the dervishes, he would clean their cells, lavatories and privies. Feeling that begging
  was the most difficult and humbling task of all, Abu Sa'id would ask for food for his fellow sufis.
  Through this experience he came to believe that the shortest way to God was in extending material
  comfort to his fellow Muslims" (p. 86, folio 70). **Note the contradiction with "I did not beg" on the
  same page; see `## Doubts`.** "cells, lavatories and privies" is also a list of khanqah facilities.
- **A khanqah population count, split by status.** "On one occasion, the number of sufis in his khanqah
  was estimated as eighty travellers and forty permanent residents" (p. 86, folio 70). **Two categories
  and two numbers; the occasion, date and source of the estimate are not given** — **(not stated)**.
- **State regulation of a sufi, initiated by a complaint and conducted under the Shari'a.** The
  theologians "wrote to Mahmud of Ghazna with the following complaint: 'A certain man has come hither
  from Mayhana and pretends to be a sufi. He preaches sermons but does not quote the Traditions of the
  Prophet. **He holds sumptuous feasts and music is played by his orders, whilst the young men dance and
  eat sweetmeats and roasted fowls and all kinds of fruit.** He declares that he is an ascetic, but this
  is neither asceticism nor sufism. Multitudes have joined him and are being led astray. Unless measures
  be taken to repair it, the mischief will soon become universal'" (p. 87, folio 71). "The Sultan ordered
  leading Shafi'i and Hanafi 'ulama' to make a thorough investigation of Abu Sa'id and, if guilty, to
  punish him according to the Shari'a… Feeling trapped, the 'ulama' decided to drop their case against
  Shaikh Abu Sa'id" (p. 87, folio 71). **A named menu, a named offence (ordered music and dancing), a
  named legal standard and a named pair of schools conducting the inquiry.**
- **Offerings, their scale, and their sources.** "The anecdotes of Abu Sa'id's luxurious feasts, in which
  thousands of candles were burnt during the day, give some indication of the amounts both available, and
  offered, to sufis by merchants and other devotees at that time. **Often former traders and other
  wealthy disciples, before becoming ascetics, would give all their possessions to a khanqgah [OCR?]
  [*khanqah*] of their choice**" (p. 88, folio 72). **A stated mechanism of endowment by total
  divestment, with the donor choosing the house.**
- **A named fundraising officer, debts, and an explicit refusal of a permanent income.** "Sometimes Shaikh
  Abu Sa'id would send his servant, Hasan Mu'addib to raise money for his extravagances from his
  disciples, and on occasions, from his enemies. He contracted huge debts which were invariably paid by
  visiting merchant caravans. **However, the Shaikh was opposed to a fixed source of income—nothing was
  accumulated for future use and everything was given away or used the same day it was received.**
  Moreover, the Shaikh took care that his neighbours, and often people from the town, shared the
  pleasures of his entertainment" (p. 88, folio 72). **This is the chunk's sharpest economic datum: a
  deliberately unendowed, same-day-redistribution khanqah financed by caravan traffic — the opposite of
  the waqf model.**
- **A doctrine of charity as superior to ritual, priced against prayer.** "This story was used by the
  Shaikh to illustrate his own attitude towards wealth, that is, that it should be used for philanthropic
  purposes only. When a disciple performed an act of kindness to a dervish, he observed that **it was
  better than a hundred genuflexions during prayers; if he gave him a mouthful of food, it was more
  praiseworthy than an entire night spent praying**" (p. 89, folio 73). "The Shaikh believed God's men
  were not confined to mosques alone, they were in taverns, too" (p. 89, folio 73).
- **The written rule of discipline for khanqahs, in ten clauses.** "In order to regularize behaviour in
  different khanqahs, Shaikh Abu Sa'id outlined the following rules of discipline: '1. Let them (the
  inmates) keep their garments clean and themselves always pure. 2. Let them not sit in the mosque or in
  any holy place for the sake of gossiping. [3.] In the first instance let them perform their prayers in
  common. [4.] Let them pray much at night. [5.] At dawn let them ask forgiveness of God and call unto
  Him. [6.] In the morning let them read as much of the Qur'an as they can, and let them not talk until
  the sun has risen. 7. Between evening prayers and bedtime prayers let them occupy themselves with
  repeating some litany (Wirdi-u-zikri) [OCR?]. 8. Let them welcome the poor and needy and all who join
  their company, and let them bear patiently the trouble of (waiting upon) them. 9. Let them not eat
  anything save in participation with one another. 10. Let them not absent themselves without receiving
  permission from one another'" (p. 90, folio 74). **The numerals 3 to 6 were lost by the OCR and survive
  only as the debris line `DAP w`; I have supplied them in brackets from their position — see
  `## Doubts`. Clauses 8, 9 and 10 are the institutional core: an open door to the poor, obligatory
  commensality, and permission required to leave.**
- **Intercession, and the radius over which it is claimed to operate.** "He also popilarized [OCR?]
  [*popularized*] a high regard for spiritual directors and a belief in the power of their intercession
  on behalf of followers and friends": "'Whoever has seen me and has done good work for my family and
  disciples will be under the shadow of my intercession hereafter. I have prayed God to forgive my
  neighbours on the left, on the right, in front and behind, and He has forgiven them for my sake…**My
  neighbours are Balkh and Marw and Nishapur and Herat.** I am not speaking of those who live here
  (Mayhana)…If any one has mounted an ass and passed by the end of this street, or has passed my house
  or will pass it, or if the light of my candle falls on him, the least thing that God will do with him
  is that He will have mercy upon him'" (p. 90, folio 74). **Blessing is made a function of physical
  proximity — a street, a house, the fall of candlelight, and four named cities as "neighbours". This is
  the chunk's most directly transferable passage for shrine doctrine, and it is attributed to the saint
  himself, in life, not to a tomb.**
- **The destruction of the khanqah's region, dated.** "Large numbers of visitors came to the Shaikh's
  khangah [OCR?] [*khanqah*] until 548/1153 when the invasion of the Ghuzz Turkic tribes completely
  devastated the region. Many of the Shaikh's own family were massacred by their conquerors" (p. 90,
  folio 74). **A terminal date for a pilgrimage traffic, and the killing of the saint's descendants.**
- **A caliphal land grant, with the size of the plot and the method of building.** Dust Dada "applied to
  the Caliph, al-Qa'im, (422/1031-467/1075), **for land to build a khanqah on the banks of the Tigris,
  across the river in an uninhabited region. After receiving permission, a plot of about 200 square yards
  was selected** by Dust Dada and he began collecting in a small bag, building material from dilapidated
  buildings nearby" (p. 91, folio 75 corrected). **A formal application, a formal permission, a stated
  area and scavenged materials. On the arithmetic of "about 200 square yards" against the buildings
  listed below, see `## Doubts`.**
- **The khanqah financed twice over by one merchant caravan, and its plan given building by building.** "a
  caravan of merchants and sufis arrived in Baghdad and at his request, camped on the land. Dust Dada
  would take his zanbil into the town to beg food, and then offered it to the campers. At prayer times he
  would lead. Impressed with his charity and self-sacrifice, members of the caravan returned his
  hospitality by giving him a considerable amount of money. **After their departure, Dust Dada proceeded
  to build a khanqah with a big covered platform, a jama'at-khana (assembly hall), kitchen and lavatory.
  Other buildings in the complex were a large arcaded mosque and a number of cells.**" On the caravan's
  return "the Shaikh begged food for his guests, and they again re-paid him liberally. **This enabled him
  to complete the khanqah by adding a harmmmam [OCR?] [*hammam*] (bath), more rooms and assembly halls.
  Later Dust Dada erected a bazaar of shops and a caravanserai in front of the khanqah's gateway**"
  (p. 91, folio 75 corrected). **The bazaar and the caravanserai are the chunk's only income-producing
  properties attached to a shrine-type institution, and they face the gateway.**
- **The author's claim about the record, and the normal size of a khanqah.** "This is the first detailed
  description of a khanqah complex which is available at the present time, however, all khanqahs were not
  built on such a grand scale; **a few cells and a jama'at-khana, plus a mosque and a lavatory, were
  generally their main components**" (p. 91, folio 75 corrected). **A stated baseline of four components
  for an ordinary khanqah.**
- **The begging bag as an institution, with an origin claim.** "zanbil… Literally a basket made of palm
  leaves, technically a bag hung across the shoulder to collect food obtained by begging locally. **The
  custom is obviously of Buddhist origin**" (p. 91, folio 75 corrected, footnote). **"obviously" carries
  no evidence in the chunk; see `## Arguments and interpretations`.**
- **A second head-count, a caliphal visit, and the shaikh installed as a broker of state favour.** "The
  fame of Dust Dada's khangqah [OCR?] [*khanqah*] attracted many sufis, and most of the people of Baghdad
  became his disciples. **The Caliph, accompanied by his chief officials, also visited the khanqah where
  he was greatly impressed by the sight of more than fifty sufis praying in the jama'at-khana.** It
  appears that after Dust Dada had made the Caliph his disciple, the latter entrusted the welfare of the
  all Baghdadi Muslims to him. This increased Dust Dada's popularity with the local people, and **many
  requested him to act as a go-between when requesting favours from the Caliph, who built himself a
  palace near the khanqah complex**" (p. 91, folio 75 corrected). **A ruler relocating beside a khanqah,
  and petitioning through the shaikh — the mechanism the archive's Punjabi and Sindhi rows repeat five
  centuries later.**
- **A tomb rebuilt by a named ruler on a dated occasion.** "His tomb in Gazirgah, about three miles
  north-east of Herat was first rebuilt by the Timurid ruler, Shah Rukh, (807/1405-850/1447) about
  829/1425 and later was further embellished by other Timurid princes" (p. 93, folio 77). **A distance
  and a direction from the city, a first rebuilding date, a named royal patron and continued princely
  embellishment — the chunk's only shrine-architecture datum, and it concerns a figure with no archive
  row.** **The original construction date, the ʿurs, the custodians and the endowment are all**
  **(not stated)**.
- **Honorific offices named.** "Shaikhu'l-Islam (Leader of the Muslims)" and "the Pir, or spiritual
  director, of Herat" for Ansari (p. 93, folio 77); "the Shaikh al-Shuyukh, or chief sufi, of Baghdad"
  for Dust Dada (p. 91, folio 75 corrected). **Two of the three are territorial titles, attached to a
  city.**
- **Devaluation of pilgrimage, fasting and formal prayer, in the words of a translated Munajat.**
  "'Fasting only means the saving of bread, / Formal prayer is the business / Of old men and women, /
  **Pilgrimage is a pleasure of the world.** / Conquer the heart, / Its subjection is conquest indeed'"
  (p. 94, folio 78). With it the two-Ka'ba and two-temple doctrine: "'Know that the Prophet built an
  external Ka'ba / Of Clay and water, / And an inner Ka'ba in life and heart. / The outer Ka'ba was built
  by Abraham, The Holy; / The inner is sanctified by the glory of God Himself'"; "'On the path of God /
  Two places of worship mark the stages. / The material temple, / And the temple of the heart. / Make
  your best endeavour / To worship at the temple of the heart'" (p. 94, folio 78). **A built place of
  worship ranked below an interior one, twice, by an eleventh-century figure whose own tomb became a
  Timurid monument (p. 93, folio 77) — the chunk sets the two facts three pages apart without remarking
  on the tension.**
- **Court patronage of religious poetry, and its renunciation.** Sana'i "was a court poet, competing with
  a galaxy of other Persian poets who wrote panegyrics to the Ghaznawid sultans. After some time, he
  relinquished his post, retiring to the life of a dervish" (p. 95, folio 79); yet "Sana'i's work, The
  Garden of Truth and Law of the Way, was dedicated to Sultan Bahram Shah of Ghazna (1118-52)" (p. 96,
  folio 80).
- **A dancing order, its instruments, and its persistence under a second name.** "During his lifetime,
  Rumi invented the famous whirling dance through which dervishes could achieve ecstasy. **This was
  accompanied by lamenting reed pipes and beating drums.** The order he founded was known as the Mevlevi
  or the Mawlawiyya, and its outstanding feature was its dancing dervishes. The order became significant
  in Turkey; **in 1332 when Ibn Battuta visited Quniya he found it flourishing under the name,
  Jalaliyya.**" "The devotion of members of the order to music and dancing is responsible for their being
  known in Europe as 'whirling dervishes'" (p. 98, folio 82). **Two named instruments, a dated
  eyewitness visit and a name change.**
- **The silsila as an organisational device, described in institutional terms.** "The twelfth century saw
  the crystallization of the new silsilas, or orders, as chains of lineages through which different sufis
  could be traced. **The establishment of silsilas placed sufism on a firm and organized basis**… It
  connected them with a spiritual hierarchy, thus giving sufis greater respectability and a stronger base
  of defence against the onslaught of the orthodox" (p. 99, folio 83). "The founders of the silsilas in
  the twelfth century linked themselves with Muhammad through either 'Ali or Abu Bakr, but generally
  through the former"; "the main emphasis of the silsilas was on continual meditation of the verses of
  the Qur'an and on different names of Allah. Gradually many sufi sayings were intermingled with the
  traditions of Muhammad" (p. 99, folio 83). "**Each silsila devised its own method or school of guidance
  as well as its own rituals and ceremonies. Later generations were to follow these rules rigidly**"
  (p. 99, folio 83). **No individual order is named anywhere in this chunk — the Chishti, Suhrawardi,
  Qadiri and Naqshbandi lines of the archive rows lie beyond p. 99** — **(not stated)**.

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

The attributions below are the book's own.

- **Telepathy as the Shaikh's defence against a state inquiry, reported by the author without hedging.**
  "Through his telepathic powers, he was aware of the proposed inquiry. A sumptuous feast was duly
  ordered and the Shaikh's indifference to worldly authorities, coupled by his supernatural powers,
  succeeded in stunning his opponents" (p. 87, folio 71); and "The Shaikh's biographers however, mention
  a number of stories in which the two sufis were reconciled through Abu Sa'id's use of telepathy"
  (p. 87, folio 71) — **the second instance attributed to "the Shaikh's biographers", the first not
  attributed at all.**
- **A prophecy of high office.** He "was reported as having prophesied that Nizamu'l-Mulk would become a
  great vizier" (p. 88, folio 72).
- **The dead Sultan's dream, told by the Shaikh against royal power.** "'A high official of Mahmud saw
  the Sultan in a dream and asked after his health. Mahmud answered: "There is no place for any sultan
  here. I am nothing here. The Sultan is God the Most High—. Everything said in the world was wrong."…
  Mahmud replied: "I am a prisoner here and have to account for every minor thing that happened during
  my life. The treasury was enjoyed by someone else, grief and lamentation have befallen me"'" (p. 88,
  folio 72, from "Asraru't-Tawhid, p. 268"). **Introduced as "an interesting story related by Abu Sa'id
  about Mahmud of Ghazna."**
- **Intercession promised for anyone who passes the house or is touched by the candlelight** (p. 90,
  folio 74) — quoted in full under `## Practices, institutions, economy`.
- **A voice from the Ka'ba guaranteeing a school of law to a dynasty.** "Rawandi, a Saljuq historian in
  Anatolia and Turkey, wrote of a supernatural power which spoke from the Meccan Ka'ba to Abu Hanifa,
  the founder of the Hanafi school of Figh [OCR?] [*Fiqh*], promising him that as long as the sword
  remained in the hands of the Turks, the Hanafis would not perish" (p. 84, folio 68).
- **A Hadis of the Ascension, set as a memory exercise for a novice.** "'God said to me on the night of my
  Ascension, O Muhammad! as for those who would draw nigh to Me, their best means. . .is by performance
  of the obligations which I have laid upon them. My servant continually seeks to win My favour by works
  of supererogation until I love him; and when I love him, I am to him an ear and an eye and a hand and a
  helper: through Me he hears, and through Me he sees, and through Me he takes'" (p. 85, folio 69).
- **A Hallaj-like utterance, with its stage business.** "Once Abu Sa'id shocked his audience by declaring,
  like Hallaj,: 'there is none other than God in this robe.' At the same time, according to his
  biographer, Abu Sa'id pushed his forefinger through his cloak" (p. 88, folio 72).
- **Two flat refusals of miracle, by two different figures, in near-identical words — the chunk's most
  striking internal echo.** Abu Sa'id "compared those who claimed to walk on water with frogs and
  waterfowls, and those who claimed to fly through the air with flies and insects, all of whom were
  similarly mobile" (p. 89, folio 73); Ansari's Munajat, five pages later: "'If thou canst walk on water
  / Thou art no better than a straw. / If thou canst fly in air / Thou art no better than a fly. /
  Conquer thy heart / That thou mayest become somebody'" (p. 94, folio 78). **The book does not note the
  parallel.**
- **A Tradition licensing the appearance of madness.** "People called me a lunatic, and I allowed them to
  give me that name, relying on the Tradition that a man's faith is not made perfect until he is supposed
  to be mad" (p. 86, folio 70).
- **God's rebuke to Moses over the shepherd's prayer, from the Masnawi.** "'A revelation came to Moses
  from God— "Thou hast parted My servant from Me."… I have bestowed on every one a (special) way of
  acting; I have given to everyone a (peculiar) form of expression… I look not at the tongue and the
  speech; I look at the inward (spirit) and the state (of feeling)'" (pp. 98–99, folios 82–83).
- **The birds who find they are the Simurgh** — 'Attar's allegory of annihilation, quoted at length in
  Browne's translation: "'They perceived themselves to be naught else but the Simurgh, while the Simurgh
  was naught else than the thirty birds (si-murgh)'" (pp. 96–97, folios 80–81). **The book presents this
  as literary allegory of "the sufi conception of annihilation in God (fana' fi'llah)", not as an event.**

## Arguments and interpretations

- **On what made sufism orthodox: prose, not poetry.** "It was the prose works of tenth and eleventh
  century sufis which had the greatest effect in fashioning sufism into an orthodox mould. However, the
  sensitivity and euphony of transcendental love, as it led to annihilation, found its greatest
  expression through poetry, particularly that written in Persian" (p. 82, folio 66).
- **An attribution judgement on the most famous body of early sufi verse.** "The ruba'is (quatrains)
  ascribed to Shaikh Abu Sa'id bin Abi'l-Khair were, in fact, the great poetical legacy of his
  predecessors. It was remarkable that such a body of sufi poetical works were available to Shaikh Abu
  Sa'id, which he in turn recited in lectures and bequeathed after his death to future generations"
  (p. 82, folio 66). **Rizvi detaches the quatrains from their nominal author.**
- **The economic argument already quoted at p. 84 (folio 68) is the chunk's governing thesis** — sufi
  independence from the state is explained by merchant and guild patronage, and the 'ulama's political
  entanglement by state salaries. **Rizvi states it as fact and offers the Abu Sa'id stories as the
  evidence.**
- **The purpose of the political survey, stated by the author.** "Having dealt with the major political
  changes between the tenth and thirteenth centuries in order to give a background to the sufi movement,
  we shall now return to a description of sufi poetry, khanqahs and the movement's leading figures. To
  illustrate the life of mystics in this period, four sufi poets will be discussed briefly" (p. 84,
  folio 68). **A declared method: political frame first, then poets as illustration.**
- **A judgement on the Ghurids that points forward to the volume's subject.** "The Ghurids' enduring
  achievement was the conquest of northern India" (p. 83, folio 67).
- **The Dust Dada story is explicitly presented as evidence for a system, not as biography.** "The story
  of Dust Dada is significant, however, as it serves to illustrate the interrelation between khanqahs,
  caravans, merchants and sufis" (p. 91, folio 75 corrected); and the plan is offered as the earliest of
  its kind: "This is the first detailed description of a khanqah complex which is available at the
  present time" (p. 91, folio 75 corrected). **Both are claims about the state of the record and should
  be attributed to Rizvi, writing in 1978, when cited.**
- **An unsupported diffusion claim.** The zanbil "custom is obviously of Buddhist origin" (p. 91,
  folio 75 corrected, footnote). **No source, no argument and no intermediary is given; "obviously" is
  doing the work.** It sits beside the broader claim at p. 99 (folio 83) that "the monastic tradition of
  Buddhism and Christianity, and Vedantist and Yogic philosophy were all Islamized by members of silsilas
  in such a way as to make them virtually unidentifiable" — **a thesis of untraceable borrowing that, by
  its own terms, cannot be evidenced.**
- **Reliability judgements on his four poets, stated as such.** Baba Tahir is "among early Persian sufi
  poets whose biographical details and writings appear reasonably authentic" (p. 92, folio 76); Sana'i's
  death is given as "525/1130-31" with the dissent recorded — "Browne, however, has suggested he died
  later, around 1150" (p. 95, folio 79); 'Attar's "dates are the subject of heated disputes amongst
  scholars" and his corpus is contaminated by a second 'Attar of the same town (p. 96, folio 80).
  **Three of the four poets are introduced with an explicit caveat about the evidence.**
- **Comparative literary verdicts.** Ansari was "the sufi poet whose impact was the greatest during the
  eleventh century"; "His Manazil al-Sa'irin is more original than Qushairi's Risala"; his Munajat is
  "the greatest masterpiece written in Persian… unique in mystic literature"; his short tracts
  "represented a peak in the expression of sufi asceticism" (p. 93, folio 77). Of Rumi: "In the Masnawi
  of Jalalu'd-Din Rumi, mystic poetry was to reach its greatest heights" (p. 97, folio 81). Of the
  twelfth and thirteenth centuries: "sufi poetry reached its highest peak in the form of masnawis or
  narrative poems. The three greatest exponents of this style were the sufi poets Sana'i, 'Attar and
  Rumi" (p. 95, folio 79).
- **A geographical explanation of a saint's fame.** Herat, "Strategically located on the trade routes
  between the Mediterranean Sea and India and China… proved a natural source from which the fame of its
  leading sufi was to spread widely" (p. 93, folio 77). **Reputation explained by trade routes — the same
  logic as the caravan-financed khanqah at p. 91 (folio 75 corrected).**
- **Patronage explained away rather than as venality.** "Although Sana'i's work… was dedicated to Sultan
  Bahram Shah of Ghazna (1118-52), the author was not hoping for temporal rewards from his ruler.
  Perso-Islamic political theory had made the role of Sultan an indispensable factor in the preservation
  of social stability and the freedom of Islamic practices. Sana'i was therefore not in a position to
  ignore his earthly ruler" (p. 96, folio 80). **Rizvi's own defence of a poet's dedication, offered
  without evidence from Sana'i.**
- **An invention claim.** "During his lifetime, Rumi invented the famous whirling dance through which
  dervishes could achieve ecstasy" (p. 98, folio 82). **Stated flatly, with no source.**
- **The interpretation of the silsilas as a defensive institution.** They gave "sufis greater
  respectability and a stronger base of defence against the onslaught of the orthodox" (p. 99, folio 83),
  and the orders "transformed mystical exercises, which they used to achieve union with God, into an
  organized framework… all aimed towards making the Tariga [OCR?] [*Tariqa*] or path of sufism, more
  effective to its followers" (p. 99, folio 83). **The sentence beginning "Although the charisma of the
  early founders of the silsilas and an adherence to strict discipline, helped to curb charlatanism and
  anti-" is cut off by the chunk boundary (p. 99, folio 83) — its conclusion is in chunk 006 and must not
  be guessed at.**
- **Abu Sa'id's doctrine as Rizvi summarises it.** "To him the first stage of sufi discipline was self
  mortification and the last, contemplation. When the unveiling was completed, ascetic practices and
  religious forms would not be necessary, for sufis lived in a state of permanent communion with God.
  There was no hell but selfhood, no paradise but selflessness" (p. 89, folio 73); and on governance of
  disciples, "To him sympathy and compassion, rather than punishment, were the most effective means to
  correct errors in disciples" (p. 90, folio 74). **The Shaikh's own definitions include "Even this,
  sufism, is polytheism (shirk)" and "To be a sufi is to cease from taking trouble (takalluf); and there
  is no greater trouble for thee than thine own self (tu 'i-yi tu)" (p. 89, folio 73).**

## Doubts

**This book's text carries no `[OCR?]`, `[illegible]` or `[folio N]` markers — the tesseract route leaves
no transcriber apparatus — so every `[OCR?]` above is mine, and the damage classes below are an inventory
of what I found, not of what was flagged for me.**

1. **q→g, the heaviest class, and it attacks the chunk's key institutional vocabulary.** The word
   *khanqah* itself is printed four different ways: "khanqah" (correct, pp. 84, 87, 88, 91, 99),
   "khanqgah" (p. 88, folio 72), "khangah" (pp. 90, 91, folios 74, 75 corrected) and "khangqah" (p. 91,
   folio 75 corrected). Also "Figh" for *Fiqh* (pp. 84, 85, folios 68, 69), "khirga" twice for *khirqa*
   (p. 85, folio 69), "gawwal" for *qawwal* (p. 84, folio 68), "Khargani" for *Kharqani* (p. 88,
   folio 72), "galandar" in "darwish-i galandar" for *qalandar* (p. 92, folio 76), "'ag/" for *'aql* and
   "'ugba" for *'uqba* (p. 92, folio 76), "Tabagat al-Sufiyya" twice for *Tabaqat* (p. 93, folio 77),
   "Hadigatu'l-Hagiga wa Shari'atu't-Tariga" for *Hadiqatu'l-Haqiqa wa Shari'atu't-Tariqa* (p. 95,
   folio 79), "Tariga" for *Tariqa* (p. 99, folio 83), "Mulagat" for *Mulaqat* (p. 87, folio 71). **A
   consolidator must not treat "khangah", "khanqgah" and "khangqah" as anything but *khanqah*.**
2. **Letter substitutions in names and technical terms — the class that endangers facts.** "hAajj" for
   *hajj* (p. 88, folio 72); "harmmmam" for *hammam* (p. 91, folio 75 corrected); "prcefound" for
   *profound* and "popilarized" for *popularized* (pp. 91, 90, folios 75 corrected, 74); "Jhya" for

   "encvelopacdia" for *encyclopaedia* and "Krames" for *Kramers* (p. 87, folio 71, footnote); "ete." for
   *etc.* and "aD" for *AD* (pp. 87, 93, folios 71, 77); "'ala'l-'arshi 'stawa" for *istawa*, "the word
   Aun" for *kun*, and "kun fa-vakunu" for *kun fa-yakunu* (p. 87, folio 71, footnote); "1 abstained" for

3. **Corrupt Roman numerals in volume numbers — every single citation of Browne and of Nicholson's Rumi

   p. 841" (p. 92, folio 76) — the volume numeral cannot be checked against anything in the chunk. The
   Qur'an citations inside the Karrami footnote print as "(VII, 55; X, 3; XIII, 2; XX. 5; ete.)" (p. 87,
   folio 71), with a full stop where a comma belongs and numerals that cannot be verified here.
   **No locator from this chunk may enter an entry unchecked.**
4. **Footnote markers and list numerals lost outright.** On p. 86 (folio 70) the markers render as
   `'`, `“`, `4`, `4` where the sequence must be 1–4, so "Muhammad bin Munawwar, Asraru't-Tawhid" and the
   Karrami note both carry `4`; on p. 89 (folio 73) the third marker is gone — "2ibid, pp. 49-50. ibid,
   p. 67." runs two notes together. **Most consequentially, the numerals 3, 4, 5 and 6 of Abu Sa'id's ten
   khanqah rules were stripped from the list and survive only as the debris line `DAP w` (p. 90,
   folio 74); I supplied them in brackets from position alone, and the count of ten rests on the surviving
   `1, 2, 7, 8, 9, 10`.**
5. **Two citation forms for one work, and one author under two names.** Rawandi's history is footnoted as
   "Rahat al-Sudur, London, 1921, pp. 13, 17" (p. 84, folio 68) and as "Rawandi, Rahatu's-Sudur, London,
   1921, p. 99" (p. 92, folio 76) — same place and year, two transliterations. And at p. 93 (folio 77) the
   Tabaqat al-Sufiyya is "Ansari's" in one sentence and "Harawi's" two sentences later, with no statement
   that these are the same man; **a consolidator could easily read Harawi as a third author.**
6. **The Persian-mystics citation is unusable as printed.** The Munajat extracts at pp. 94–95 (folios

8. **Numerals in the institutional data, which are the reason this chunk matters, are equally unverified
   and one of them looks wrong.** "eighty travellers and forty permanent residents" (p. 86, folio 70);
   "more than fifty sufis praying in the jama'at-khana" (p. 91, folio 75 corrected); "thousands of
   candles" (p. 88, folio 72); "about 26,660 couplets" (p. 98, folio 82); "about three miles north-east of
   Herat" (p. 93, folio 77). **"a plot of about 200 square yards" (p. 91, folio 75 corrected) is hard to
   reconcile with what the same page says was built on it — a covered platform, a jama'at-khana, a
   kitchen, a lavatory, "a large arcaded mosque", "a number of cells", a hammam, "more rooms and assembly

9. **Two internal contradictions in the same account, which the book does not resolve.** Abu Sa'id's own
   list of eighteen novice obligations includes "I did not beg" (p. 86, folio 70), and the very next
   paragraph says "Feeling that begging was the most difficult and humbling task of all, Abu Sa'id would
   ask for food for his fellow sufis" (p. 86, folio 70) — **begging for oneself against begging for
   others, but the book does not draw the distinction.** And the book states "I bound myself to do
   eighteen things" while the printed list does not obviously resolve into eighteen discrete items
   (p. 86, folio 70). **Separately, the Shaikh's rule 2 forbids sitting "in the mosque or in any holy
   place for the sake of gossiping" (p. 90, folio 74) while his own discipline was "I always sat in the
   mosque" (p. 86, folio 70) — not a contradiction, but the two should not be quoted as one norm.**
10. **The header on p. 91.** The running header prints `Early Sufism 77`, which duplicates p. 93's header
    and falls out of sequence between p. 90's `74` and p. 92's `76`. **I have treated the header as
    OCR-damaged (5→7) rather than as dissenting from the offset ladder, and cited that page as folio 75
    throughout, marked "corrected". The ladder's −16 offset and the neighbouring headers agree on 75, so
    this is the one page in the range where the printed header does not win, and the reason is that the
    header is damaged, not that it disagrees.** A human should confirm 75 on the page image.

**Archive-mapping doubts. Nothing here is mapped, and these are the near-misses a consolidator will
otherwise map by mistake:**

- **`shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar` — the most dangerous collision in this chunk, and it is a
  non-match.** The chunk's "Faridu'd-Din Abu Hamid Muhammad 'Attar" is the poet of Nishapur, "born in
  537/1142-43… died there in 617/1220" (p. 96, folio 80), and is not the archive row's Baba Fariduddin
  Mas'ud Ganjshakar of Pakpattan. **The shared name "Faridu'd-Din" must not produce a mapping.**
- **`shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya` — likewise a non-match.** The chunk's "Baha'u'd-Din Walad" is Rumi's
  father, "a preacher who, in 614/1217 was forced to emigrate from Balkh" and who died at Quniya about
  629/1231 by the book's arithmetic ("three years later", p. 97, folio 81). **Not the Multan figure.**
- **`shrine-of-shah-shams-ud-din-sabzwari` and
  `shrine-of-hazrat-shah-ali-akbar-shah-ali-akbar-shamsi` — non-matches.** The chunk's "Shamsu'd-Din
  Muhammad Tabrizi", also "Shams" and "Shams-i Tabriz" as Rumi's takhallus, is the dervish murdered at
  Quniya "in 645/1247" (p. 98, folio 82). **The chunk makes no link to Multan or to any Shamsi line, and
  none should be inferred.**
- **`shrine-of-jalaluddin-surkh-posh-bukhari-jalaluddin-bukhari` and
  `shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht` — non-matches.** The chunk's two Jalalu'd-Dins are Rumi
  (d. 672/1273, Quniya) and "the last Khwarazm-Shah, Jalalu'd-Din" driven across the Indus in 1221
  (pp. 84, 98, folios 68, 82).
- **`tahir-bandagi-qadri` and `shrine-at-odero-lal` — non-matches on "Tahir".** The chunk has "Baba
  Tahir" of the Hamadan–Luristan region (p. 92, folio 76) and "Tahir bin al-Husain", founder of the
  Tahirid dynasty (p. 82, folio 66). **Neither is the Lahore figure nor "Sheikh Tahir" of Odero Lal.**
- **`darbar-malik-ahmad-ayaz` — not mapped, and the reason should be recorded.** The chunk is full of
  Mahmud of Ghazna, whom that row's field survey names as the master of its principal figure, and it
  gives his dates ("388/998-421/1030"), his conquest of "the Panjab", and the sack of Ghazna by Jahan Suz
  (p. 83, folio 67). **But Malik Ahmad Ayaz is not named anywhere in pp. 82–99, and Mahmud is not himself
  any row's principal figure, so the ruling of 18 September 2026 does not reach him. This material is
  background for that row, not evidence about its figure.**
- **`tomb-of-qutbuddin-aibak` — not mapped for the same reason.** The chunk carries the Ghurid conquest
  of northern India and names "Shihabu'd-Din (later Mu'izzu'd-Din Muhammad 599/1203—602/1206)" (p. 83,
  folio 67), but **Qutb ud-Din Aibak is not named.**
- **`data-darbar` — an explicit negative worth recording.** Hujwiri is not named anywhere in this chunk,
  and the Kashf al-Mahjub is not mentioned; the eleventh-century prose work discussed here is
  **Qushairi's Risala** (pp. 87, 93, folios 71, 77). **No mapping, and none is available.**
- **Hind, Sind and the Panjab — left unmapped under the interim toponym convention (p. 99, folio 83;
  p. 83, folio 67).** Unlike the calibration case, **no single row is the candidate if the ruling
  reverses**: "Sind" and "the Panjab" are regions, and a reversal would implicate every Sindhi and
  Punjabi row in the archive at once on the strength of one line of a Rumi translation and two clauses of
  dynastic history. **I record that as evidence for keeping the convention rather than as a row to
  attach.**
- **Hinglaj is not mentioned in this chunk, so the empty-id defect in the row
  `Shaktipeeth Shri Hinglaj Mata Mandir` does not bite here. The defect stands and still needs an id from
  Rauf; this chunk adds nothing to it.**
- **Candidate rows this chunk would support if the archive ever widens beyond present-day Pakistan:**
  Ansari's tomb at Gazirgah near Herat, with a Timurid rebuilding date (p. 93, folio 77), and Dust Dada's
  khanqah complex on the Tigris at Baghdad (p. 91, folio 75 corrected). **Both are described with more
  architectural detail than many existing rows carry; noted, not mapped.**
