## Practices, institutions, economy

**The single most institutionally valuable page in the chunk is p. 47 (folio 31).** Everything below
is early-Islamic and Near-Eastern; nothing is located in India or present-day Pakistan.

- **A khanqah foundation, dated by a death and credited to a named class of patron — the chunk's one
  building-and-endowment fact.** "Before his death at the end of the eighth century, a khanqah (sufi
  monastry) [OCR?] [monastery] was founded in Ramla, near Jerusalem, by a Christian dignitary"
  (p. 47, folio 31). **The footnote gives the motive, and it is an explicit statement of why such a
  building was needed:** "The story goes that the Christian dignitary encountered two dervishes who
  amicably chatted together, shared their food and then went their separate ways although they had
  never seen each other before. Impressed by the warmth between them the Christian later learned that
  there was no resting place for such dervishes so he built a khanqah for them" (p. 47, folio 31,
  footnote 2). **No name for the patron, no cost, no endowment and no later history are given** —
  all **(not stated)**.
- **Khanqahs, ribats and tombs as a recognised class of institution, and a named guide-book to
  visiting them.** "The Arabic maritime literature, the accounts of other travellers and the works
  describing sufi tombs, khanqahs and ribats (‘hospices’) also manage to shed interesting light on
  the development of sufism in the Islamic world. An important work, for example, is the Jsharat ila
  ma'‘rifat al-ziyarat by al-Harawi who died in 611/1214" (p. 33, folio 17). **A ziyarat literature
  is thus named as a source-genre for this very book**; the title is OCR-damaged in its first word.
- **Khanqahs and organised hospices as places a traveller actually lodged.** Ibn Battuta "stayed with
  merchants and members of the artisan guilds (asnaf), in sufi khangahs [OCR?] [khanqahs] and in the
  hospices run by the Akhi and Futuwwa organizations. He also was a keen observer of the customs and
  lives of qalandars and yogis" (p. 30, folio 16). **The one mention of qalandars and of yogis in the
  chunk, and both are generic** — see `## Doubts`.
- **What Rizvi says the Rihla is worth as an institutional source** — it "contains a comparative
  study of the political, economic, social and religious institutions of the various countries the
  author visited and the penetrating analysis of his impressions" (pp. 30, 33, folios 16–17).
- **The Futuwwa orders, their purpose and the date of their convergence with Sufism.** "‘Ali and
  Hasan Basri fulfilled the same role for members of the Futuwwa orders. These were chivalric groups
  who fought against injustice and its source wherever it was found. From the time of the eighth
  century, both movements, the Futuwwa and the sufi, drew closer together in mutual assistance and
  respect" (p. 45, folio 29).
- **The economy of dress: wool, its cost in status, and the term it gave the movement.** "By Hasan's
  time the wearing of wool (suf) had become fashionable among Muslim ascetics", with Hasan's warning
  that "He who wears wool out of humility towards God increases the illumination of his insight and
  his heart, but he who wears it out of pride and arrogance will be thrust down to Hell with the
  devils" (p. 44, folio 28). "The wearing of wool was, according to sufis, a legacy of the prophets
  and the Christian apostles and ascetics" (p. 44, folio 28). Malik bin Dinar "declared himself unfit
  to wear wool because according to him it was the mark of purity (safa)" (p. 45, folio 29). ‘Umar's
  "patched gown or muraqqa’" is the parallel garment-as-doctrine datum (p. 41, folio 25).
- **A price, and the editor's own gloss on its value — the chunk's only monetary figure.** Rabi‘a was
  "seized by a man in the street and sold for six dirhams", with the footnote "A coin worth less than
  a penny" (p. 46, folio 30).
- **An ascetic's entire property, itemised.** "A broken pitcher, out of which she drank and made
  ritual ablutions, an old reed mat, and a brick which she occasionally used as a pillow, were her
  only belongings" (p. 46, folio 30). She "felt ashamed to ask for anything from the world's Creator,
  let alone human beings" (p. 46, folio 30) — **a refusal of solicitation, i.e. of the offerings
  economy, stated of a named saint.**
- **The cell (and the shared cell) as the unit of residence.** Hasan "passed by the door of his
  cell"; Hasan and Habib "even shared the same cell for a period" (p. 45, folio 29). Rabi‘a's "lonely
  hermitage" and her withdrawals "to the wilderness" (p. 46, folio 30).
- **Obligatory and supererogatory practice, distinguished.** "Islam made prayers, five times a day,
  and fasting for the whole of Ramazan, the ninth month of the Islamic calendar, obligatory. However,
  the AA/ al-Suffa [OCR?] [Ahl al-Suffa] and many other followers of Muhammad, observed incessant
  prayer and fasting, as did the Prophet himself" (p. 37, folio 21). Rabi‘a "attained an exalted
  spiritual status through prayer and continual fasting" (p. 46, folio 30). Against it, Abu Darda's
  measure: "one hour of reflection was better than forty nights of prayer, and that one particle of
  righteousness, combined with godliness and assured faith, was preferable to unlimited ritual
  observance" (p. 38, folio 22).
- **A scriptural prohibition on hoarding, given a date and an occasion.** "The Qur’anic chapter
  entitled A/-Bara’at (Immunity) or A/-Tauba (Repentance) was revealed in the Ninth Hijri or 630 ab.
  [OCR?] [A.D.]" and "drew attention to the duties of Muslims to avoid hoarding wealth" (p. 37,
  folio 21), quoting "They who hoard up gold and silver and spend it not in the way of Allah, unto
  them give tidings (O Muhammad) of a painful doom", introduced by the charge that "many of the
  (Jewish) rabbis and the (Christian) monks devour the wea!th [OCR?] [wealth] of mankind wantonly"
  (p. 37, folio 21).
- **Poverty as a claimed status, with its technical term.** The Prophet "considered his own pursuit
  of fagr [OCR?] [faqr], that is, a life of poverty and resignation to God's will, a source of
  personal pride" (p. 37, folio 21). Against it, the conquests: "They were now not only exposed to
  the evils of material prosperity ... Some amassed immense fortunes" (p. 38, folio 22); and Hasan
  Basri "condemned the possession of riches and of all worldly attachments" (p. 43, folio 27).
- **Initiation, and the recognised category of the uninitiated — a lineage rule with a date.** "From
  the eleventh century onwards, sufis who did not obtain initiation from a particular preceptor called
  themselves Uwaysis" (p. 41, folio 25). Set beside it, the doctrine of spiritual descent: "the sufi
  orders trace their spiritual descent through him [Hasan al-Basri] from Muhammad and ‘Ali" (p. 43,
  folio 27), and "According to the sufi tradition, however, Hasan became ‘Ali's disciple through some
  indescribable spiritual experience" (p. 43, folio 27).
- **Sufis made juridically dependent on the Sunni schools — an institutional subordination, stated as
  a consequence.** "The growth of the four schools of Sunni jurisprudence, and the crystallization of
  the Shi‘i school ... forced sufis to become dependant on the Sunni school of jurisprudence for rules
  regarding external prayers and religious worship. Of course, in reality the sufi system itself
  transcended such legal differences" (pp. 42–43, folios 26–27).
- **The making and unmaking of centres, with foundation dates.** Medina "began to lose its
  importance as the guiding light behind developments in the esoteric doctrines of Islam" after
  Ja‘far's death in 765 (p. 42, folio 26); "Medina and Mecca remained the two holy places of Islam.
  Immigrants from other parts of the Islamic world also increased their importance" (p. 43,
  folio 27). Basra was "founded by the Arabs in 638, on the site of the Iranian settlement known as
  Vahishtabaz Ardasher. In the beginning it was a military camp controlling the route from the
  Persian Gulf, Iraq and Iran, but soon it developed into an important cultural centre" (p. 43,
  folio 27). Kufa "originally was an Arab military cantonment in Iraq which had been founded
  immediately after the conquest of Mesopotamia ... about 17/638" (p. 47, folio 31). "Mecca, Medina,
  Basra and Kufa were undoubtedly the earliest centres of Islam's contemplative and ascetic life"
  (p. 48, folio 32). ‘Ali "transferred his capital from Medina to Kufa"; Mu‘awiyya "founded the
  hereditary Umayyad Caliphate (661-750) with Damascus as its capital, superseding both Medina and
  Kufa" (p. 38, folio 22); "In 736 Khurasan's capital was transferred from Marw to Balkh" (p. 49,
  folio 33).
- **State persecution as a recurring condition of the ascetic's life — three named instances.** Abu
  Zar's "court exile during the reign of ‘Usman (644-56)" (p. 38, folio 22); Hasan Basri's hiding
  "in 705 ... until Hajjaj's death in 714" for "bold criticism of the repressive religio-political
  policies of Hajjaj bin Yusuf, the Umayyad governor of Iraq" (p. 43, folio 27); Sufyan "forced to
  escape to Mecca in 158/774-75 and like Hasan of Basra before him was obliged to hide from the state
  persecution" (pp. 47–48, folios 31–32). **And an inquisition:** al-Ma’mun "instituted an
  inquisitorial tribunal for the trial and conviction of those who denied the Mu‘tazilite dogma of
  ‘the creation (kha/q) [OCR?] [khalq] of the Qur'an’" (p. 39, folio 23).
- **Quietism as the prescribed response to bad government.** "in general Hasan did not approve of
  rebellion against, or the removal of, vicious governors. He pleaded that ‘violent actions of tyrants
  were a punishment sent by God which could not be opposed by the sword but must be endured with
  patience'" (p. 43, folio 27). Against it, the ‘ulama’ who "managed to overthrow the Sultan himself
  in 1242" and Minhaj Siraj's disapproval of "sufis dabbling in the political arena" (p. 30,
  folio 16).
- **Religious buildings destroyed or taken by conquest, dated.** Balkh's "Zoroastrian temples" and
  "the Buddhist cloister, the Nawbahar", "raided by Arabs in 653 and conquered in 663-64", with
  "Firm Muslim control ... established only after the conquests of Qutayba bin Muslim in 715"
  (p. 49, folio 33). "Buddhism had died out in the Khurasan region and only a mere handful of
  Buddhist monuments remained between Khurasan and India" by about 1000 (pp. 48–49, folios 32–33).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

Attributed as the book attributes them — "sufi legends", "Many hagiologies say", "The story goes".

- **The Prophet's forecast of a visit to a man he never met.** "The Prophet had never met him, but
  forecast that ‘Umar and ‘Ali would visit him some time" — which they then did, at Qaran, conveying
  "the Prophet's greetings" (p. 41, folio 25).
- **Hasan of Basra corrected by God in a dream for refusing to pray behind a non-Arabic speaker.**
  "The same night, Hasan dreamed that he saw God and said to Him: ‘O Lord, wherein does Thy good
  pleasure consist?' and that God answered: ‘O Hasan, you found My good pleasure, but did not know
  its value: if ... you had said your prayers after Habib, and if the rightness of his intention had
  restrained you from taking offence at his pronunciation, I should have been well pleased with you'"
  (p. 45, folio 29, from the *Kashf al-Mahjub*).
- **Wild animals gathering around a saint and fleeing from others.** "In the mountains, deer,
  mountain goats, ibexes and asses would surround her, and then flee at the sight of others,
  including sufis" — reported as what "Many hagiologies say" (p. 46, folio 30).
- **A bird's suicide at a grave and a voice from the tomb — the one tomb miracle in the chunk.**
  Sufyan freed a caged bird which "refused to leave him and became his lifelong companion, watching
  while he prayed during the night. After Sufyan's death, the bird suicided by dashing itself on the
  ground near its liberator's grave. A voice from the tomb was heard to say that God the Most High
  had forgiven the sufi because of his compassion for the creatures which were His" (p. 48,
  folio 32).
- **The dog preferred to wife and child.** "If I give bread to the dog ... he keeps watch over me all
  through the night so that I can pray. If I give it to my wife and child, they hold me back from my
  devotions" (p. 48, folio 32).
- **The thrice-repeated voice at the hunt, the third time from the saddle.** Ibrahim bin Adham "went
  out to hunt for pleasure, and a voice called him, saying: ‘Not for this was thou created, and not to
  this was thou commanded.' Twice the voice called him; and on the third occasion the call came from
  the pommel of his saddle" — given as "sufi legends" and quoted from al-Kalabazi (p. 49, folio 33).
  **Rizvi adds that the legend grew in transmission**: "When translated from Arabic to Persian and
  from Persian to Malay and Javanese, the legend of Ibrahim became increasingly fanciful" (p. 49,
  folio 33).
- **The city of the blind and the elephant, as a sufi parable about God.** "The following parable,
  indicating how a man can only form a partial and distorted view of God, is often repeated by sufis"
  (p. 34, folio not stated), quoted at length from Sana’i and ending "Naught of Almighty God can
  creatures learn, / Nor e'en the wise such mysteries discern" (p. 35, folio 19).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Rizvi's opening definition, and his deliberate restriction of the term.** "Sufism [OCR?; printed
  "Qu", the chapter's dropped initial swallowed] represents the inward or esoteric side of Islam; it
  may, for the sake of convenience, be described as the mystical dimension of Islam" (p. 34, folio
  not stated). He adopts Stace's exclusions — mysticism "is not to be understood in the sense of the
  occult or telepathy, and he excludes even visions and voices from the list of mystical phenomena"
  (p. 34, folio not stated) — and quotes Stace's definition, "the apprehension of an ultimate
  non-sensuous unity in all things" (p. 34, folio not stated, citing "W.T. Stace, The teachings of
  the mystics, New York, 1960, pp. 14-5").
- **Mysticism detached from religion, then re-attached inside each creed.** "Mystical experience is
  not necessarily a religious phenomenon ... followers of different religions can operate mystically
  within the laws of their own creed. Thus we have Hindu mysticism, Jewish mysticism, Christian
  mysticism, Islamic mysticism and Buddhist mysticism, although, of course, the Buddha of the Pali
  canon of the Hinayana repudiated the concept of self" (p. 34, folio not stated). **The Rigveda is
  cited as the Indian instance of one Reality behind many names** (p. 34, folio not stated).
- **Sufism as plural rather than systematic.** "Sufism is not, therefore, a rigid system. According
  to one outstanding sufi, the paths by which its followers seek God: ‘are in number as the souls of
  men.' Asceticism, purification, love and gnosis assist sufis in finding the Universal Self. These
  are merely the means to an end, and not the end itself" (p. 36, folio 20). **The "one outstanding
  sufi" is not named** — **(not stated)**.
- **The Prophet as sufi — a modern claim quoted and then extended by the sufis' own view.** "A
  modern scholar says: ‘Muhammad was a sufi when on his way to becoming a prophet.' Sufis believe
  that Muhammad was indeed a sufi throughout his whole life" (p. 36, folio 20, citing "D.B.
  Macdonald, Development of Muslim theology, jurisprudence and constitutional theory, New York,
  1903, p. 227"), evidenced by the night-vigil sura and the patience suras (pp. 36–37, folios 20–21).
- **Rizvi's flat rejection of the Hasan-Basri-initiated-by-‘Ali tradition.** "Anecdotes describing
  Hasan's conversion to sufism present him as reasonably mature at the time, and as a jewel merchant
  trading with Byzantium. Hasan's discipleship with ‘Ali and his conversion by him, would appear to
  be pious myths composed by later writers. Both medieval and modern scholars have challenged their
  authenticity" (p. 43, folio 27). **This is the chunk's clearest instance of the author overruling a
  silsila claim** — and the silsila claim itself is stated on the same page.
- **Rabi‘a's disinterested love as a datable turning point.** "Her conception and expression of this
  idea is regarded as an important milestone in the development of sufism, just as the acceptance of
  an unselfish love of God became a crucial part of the journey of the individual sufi" (p. 47,
  folio 31); she teaches that "Neither the desire for paradise nor the fear of hell should be the
  incentive for prayers or love of God" (p. 46, folio 30).
- **Christian influence asserted without hedging, and named.** "The deep Christian influence in the
  early development of sufism is undeniable; Jesus was a model of self-denial and of the saintly
  life. That sufis tended to see Jesus in the light of their own traditions does not lessen the
  significance of the impact of the Nestorian and Jacobite churches on the movement" (p. 48,
  folio 32).
- **The Buddha–Ibrahim parallel: a named scholar's claim, a named scholar's objection, and Rizvi's
  own verdict against the parallel.** "Goldziher was the first scholar to point out the similarity of
  this story with the Buddha's conversion, however, such an interpretation has been questioned by
  Louis Massignon. The circumstances of Ibrahim's conversion are indeed different to the Buddha's;
  the former renounced the world on the impetus of what he believed to be a ..." — **the sentence is
  cut off by the chunk boundary at p. 49 (folio 33)** and its conclusion is not in this chunk.
- **An India link proposed for Islamic theology, on a cited authority.** "S. Pines believes that the
  Mu‘tazilite theory is very different from all Greek atomistic doctrines and sees in it an
  ‘undeniable similarity between various important points of the Ka/am [OCR?] [Kalam] doctrine and
  the Indian (Nyaya-Vaisheshika and Buddhist) atomistic doctrines'" (p. 39, folio 23, citing "P.M.
  Holt et. al., ed, The Cambridge history of Islam, 11 [OCR?] [II], Cambridge, 1970, p. 793").
- **Greek philosophy as the Falasifa's inheritance, routed through named cities.** "the legacy of
  Near Eastern Hellenism, semi-orientalized by Aramaic and Christian influences, which had survived
  in Alexandria, Antioch and Gondeshapur, was inherited by the Falasifa or Muslim philosophers. The
  intellectual mysticism of Plotinus, and the doctrines of Hermetic origin, also made a deep impact
  upon them" (p. 40, folio 24). "Both the Mu‘tazila and the Ash‘ari depended on Aristotelian logic"
  (p. 40, folio 24).
- **Watt quoted at length for seven differences between al-Ash‘ari and the Mu‘tazila** (pp. 39–40,
  folios 23–24), including the eternal attributes, the uncreated Qur'an, the vision of God, kasb —
  with Watt's own caution that "though he was familiar with the concept, he does not appear to have
  held the doctrine himself" — and the eschatological "Basin, the Bridge, the Balance and
  intercession by Muhammad" (p. 40, folio 24, citing "Encyclopaedia of Islam (new edition). 1 [OCR?]
  [I], p. 694").
- **The word "sufi" declared a grammatical error, and Rizvi endorses the verdict.** "A modern Irani
  scholar rightly points out that the word, sufi, for a wearer of a woollen garment is incorrect from
  the point of view of Arabic grammar. The word was invented by some Irani on the pattern of the
  grammar of his own language and assimilated into Arabic" (p. 44, folio 28, citing "Jalalu'd-Din
  Huma‘i, Mishahu'l-Hidaya [OCR?] [Misbahu'l-Hidaya], Tehran, nd (2nd edition), pp. 81-2"). **The
  adverb "rightly" is Rizvi's.** He also notes the movement's earlier names — Sahaba, "Tabi‘un",
  "Taba‘ Tabi‘un" — and that "This word was not originally used to describe the movement begun by the
  companions of the Prophet Muhammad" (pp. 40–41, folios 24–25).
- **A political quietism attributed to the first sufi generations.** "They deemed the rule of the
  first four Caliphs ‘pious', which merely implied that the political activities of the Umayyads or
  the ‘Abbasids were of no concern to them. They had no use for sectarian controversies between the
  Sunnis and the Shi‘is" (p. 41, folio 25).
- **Converts as the channel of foreign doctrine into Sufism — the chapter's organising thesis.**
  "Early sufi movements often contained converts from other religious communities, such as those of
  Zoroastrianism, Christianity, Judaism and so on. These members often imbued sufism with their
  ancestral philosophies" (p. 49, folio 33); Manichaeism "had deeply penetrated Khurasan and survived
  as far as eastern and northern India" (p. 48, folio 32). **The stated plan of the chapter:** "In
  order to better understand sufi developments from the eighth to the end of the tenth centuries aD
  [OCR?] [A.D.] our study has been divided into four regions: 1. Khurasan and Transoxiana; 2. Other
  parts of Iran; 3. Syria and Egypt; and 4. Baghdad" (p. 49, folio 33), with "The second two
  important regions where sufism blossomed were Iran and Khurasan" (p. 48, folio 32) and the
  explanation that distance from Damascus and Baghdad left those regions "centres for a number of new
  religio-political movements" (p. 48, folio 32).
- **Barani read as psychology rather than chronicle.** "His work is an interesting psychological
  study in the analysis of the background to conflicts between different groups at all levels of
  society. Dealing with both pathological behaviour and the essential needs of human behaviour ..."
  (p. 30, folio 16).
