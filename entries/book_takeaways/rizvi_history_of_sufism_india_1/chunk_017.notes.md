# rizvi_history_of_sufism_india_1 — chunk 017 — pp. 281–298

**The folio regime in this chunk is confirmed page by page by the running headers and matches `folio_map.tsv` exactly.** Every recto prints `The Chishtiyya, Suhrawardiyya and Kubrawiyya Centres <n>` (pp. 281, 283, 285, 287, 289, 291, 293, 295 → folios 261, 263, 265, 267, 269, 271, 273, 275) and every verso prints `<n> A History of Sufism in India` (pp. 282, 284, 286, 288, 290, 292, 294, 296 → folios 262, 264, 266, 268, 270, 272, 274, 276). **No folio was computed; all are taken from `folio_map.tsv`.** The two closing pages carry no folio in the map and none in print: **p. 297 is a bare scan stamp, its whole OCR being "; Original from / Digitized by Google UNIVERSITY OF CALIFORNIA" (p. 297, folio not stated), and p. 298 is a plate leaf whose only content is a caption plus a destroyed line of image noise (p. 298, folio not stated)**. That is exactly what the brief predicted, and it is reported here as scan furniture, not squeezed for body text — except that the plate caption does print six saints' names, which is real content and is handled below.

**The chapter is Chapter Four throughout, and the chunk is entirely within it.** Its section headings in this range are, in order, `Jaunpur` (p. 281, folio 261), `Rudauli` (p. 290, folio 270), `Lucknow` (p. 292, folio 272), `Kalpi and Iraj` (p. 293, folio 273) and `Malwa` (p. 295, folio 275). **Contrary to what the chapter title promises, there is no Suhrawardiyya-headed and no Kubrawiyya-headed section anywhere in pp. 281–296** — the Suhrawardiyya appears only as a second initiation taken by Chishti figures (pp. 292, 293, 295; folios 272, 273, 275) and the Kubrawiyya is not named once. The chapter's Suhrawardi and Kubrawi centres must lie past p. 296.

**The chunk is three archive rows deep, and all three are thin.** Chapter Four was expected to be the densest chapter in the book for archive rows, and for this slice of it that expectation is not met: **the chapter is organised by place, but every place it is organised by — Jaunpur, Manikpur, Rudauli, Lucknow, Kalpi, Iraj, Mandu, Ajmer, Nagaur, Kara, Kalpi, Khairabad, Panipat, Sunam, Pandua, Sunargaon, Munyar, Narnol — is in India, and not one of them is an archive location.** The three rows touched are reached through persons, not places: **`shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya`** (one sentence), **`shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht`** (four mentions, all as an initiator), and **`shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar`** (a bare name in the plate caption). The material the run was looking for — tomb endowment, custodianship, state control of a shrine — **is here in quantity, but it attaches to Ajmer and Nagaur, which have no archive rows**; see "Practices, institutions, economy", which is the richest section of these notes.

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht** (the book calls him "Makhdum Jahaniyan", "Makhdum Jahaniyan Saiyid Jalal Bukhari" and "Saiyid Jalalu'd-Din Makhdum Jahaniyan"): **he is named four times in this chunk, always as the man other sufis go to for initiation, never as a tomb, a shrine or an ʿurs.** The identification rests on the title "Makhdum Jahaniyan" plus Uch, both printed; **no death date, no birth date, no "Jahangasht", no tomb and no custodian are given — all (not stated).**
- **shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht**: **the book makes him the originator in India of double initiation across two orders, which is the chunk's single most consequential institutional claim.** "The practice seems to have been started in India by the Suhrawardi, Makhdum Jahaniyan Saiyid Jalal Bukhari who, as we shall see, also obtained initiation in the Chishtiyya order" (p. 292, folio 272). The forward reference "as we shall see" means the book's substantive treatment of him is outside this chunk.
- **shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht**: **he is placed at Uch and given a silsila.** Shaikh Qiwamu'd-Din, a disciple of Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din Mahmud, "After the death of the latter … went to Uch where he obtained initiation from Saiyid Jalalu'd-Din Makhdum Jahaniyan of the Suhrawardiyya order" (p. 293, folio 273). **This is the sentence that settles the Uch attribution; see Doubts for why it also settles the standing Jalalu'd-Din collision for this chunk and only for this chunk.**
- **shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht**: **he hosts Mir Saiyid Ashraf Jahangir Simnani on the latter's journey into India.** "Then taking the route through Multan and Uch to India, Simnani lived for a period with Makhdum Jahaniyan" (p. 287, folio 267). **No length of stay, no place of residence and no date are given** — (not stated).
- **shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht**: **he initiates, with a second man, into the Suhrawardiyya.** Shaikh Yusuf Budh "was initiated into the Suhrawardiyya order by Saiyid Jalal Bukhari and Shaikh Raju Quttal" (p. 295, folio 275); on the neighbouring page Shaikh Sarang, "on his return from Mecca, became the disciple of both Shaikh Yusuf Budh and of Shaikh Raju Qattal" (p. 293, folio 273). **Here the book prints only "Saiyid Jalal Bukhari", without "Makhdum Jahaniyan" — a bare form that is ambiguous on its own; it is mapped here only because p. 292 (folio 272) equates the two names. See Doubts.**
- **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya** (the book calls him "Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya"): **he is named exactly once, and only as the authority for the rule that double initiation broke.** "Although Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya had insisted during his lifetime that sufis join one order only, from the fourteenth century sufis were often initiated into both the Chishtiyya and the Suhrawardiyya" (p. 292, folio 272). **No Multan, no dates, no tomb, no order named for him, no title — all (not stated).** Mapped under the ruled convention of 18 September 2026; see Doubts, and note that the death-date disagreement recorded by earlier chunks (book: 7 Safar 661/21 December 1262; row: 1267) does not recur here because this chunk prints no date for him at all.
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar** (the book prints "Baba Farid"): **he appears only as one of six names in the caption of the plate leaf, in the right-to-left listing of the figures in the miniature.** "An Imaginary Gathering of Eminent Sufis. XVII century miniature in the Hermitage Museum, Leningrad. Left: Khwaja Mu'inu'd-Din Chishti; Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki; Baba Farid. Rig/it [OCR?] [Right]: Shaikh *'Abdu'l Qadir Jilani; Shaikh Abu "Ali Qalandar; Shaikh Nizamu' d-Din Auliya'." (p. 298, folio not stated). **No dates, no Ajodhan, no Ganj-i Shakar, no tomb and no silsila — all (not stated).** The book's usual seat-name for him, "Ajodhan", does not appear anywhere in this chunk. Mapped under the ruled author-or-reference convention; **this is the weakest of the three mappings and carries a Doubts line of its own.**

**No other archive row is touched anywhere in pp. 281–298 (folios 261–276 and two unnumbered leaves).** In particular: **Hinglaj is not mentioned**, so the index's empty-id defect does not arise here and no id has been invented; **no Hindu deity is named as a principal figure anywhere** (the only Hindu figures are the Raja Ganesa of Bengal and the Ranas of Mewar, both rulers — pp. 288, 295, 296; folios 268, 275, 276), so the unruled deity question does not bite; and **the 166 remaining rows are untouched because the chapter's geography is Indian**. That zero is a real finding about the chapter, not a gap in the reading.

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

Dates below are exactly as the book prints them, AH/CE pairs included.

- **Qazi ʿAbduʾl-Muqtadir** (printed both "Qazi 'Abdu'l-Muqtadir" and "Qazi 'Abdu'l-Mugtadir [OCR?] [Muqtadir]") — "The Delhi sufi and scholar whose disciples and descendants made Jaunpur an important Chishti centre", "himself an outstanding disciple of the famous Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din Muhmud [OCR?] [Mahmud]"; he "advocated that deliberations on disputes of the Shari'a were far superior to long hours spent in prayer designed to promote self admiration and hypocrisy"; "He was also a poet, who wrote in Arabic"; "'Abdu'l-Muqtadir died on 26 Muharram 791/25 January 1389" (p. 281, folio 261).
- **Qazi Shihabuʾd-Din Daulatabadi** — disciple of the above and pupil in mysticism of "Maulana Muhammad Khwajgi"; left Delhi before Timur's invasion; invited to Jaunpur by Sultan Ibrahim Shah Sharqi, "appointed the Qaziu'l-Quzat (Chief Qazi) and given the title Maliku'l-'Ulama' (Prince of the 'Ulama')"; author of the *Bahr-i Mawwaj*, "a Persian commentary on the Qur'an", of "a commentary on the Usul of Bazdawi, which remained incomplete", of the *Usul-i Ibrahim Shahi* dedicated to his patron, and of the *Managibu's-Sadat [OCR?] [Manaqibu's-Sadat]* (Eulogies on the Saiyids), which replaced a destroyed treatise "discussing the superiority of the 'ulama' over Saiyids"; "Shihabu'd-Din Daulatabadi died in 848/1444—45" (pp. 281–282, folios 261–262).
- **Shaikh Abuʾl-Fath** — "The most important contemporary Chishtiyya mystic of Qazi Shihabu'd-Din", "a grandson of Qazi 'Abdu'l-Muatadir [OCR?] [Muqtadir]"; "born on 14 Muharram 772/8 August 1370"; migrated to Jaunpur after Timur's invasion, where he "frequently differed with Qazi Shihabu'd-Din over the interpretation of the controversial aspects of Figh [OCR?] [Fiqh]"; "did not accept a government office and led a life of poverty and near-starvation"; "He died on 13 Rabi' I 858/13 March 1454" (pp. 282–283, folios 262–263). **Not the same man as Shaikh Abuʾl-Fath ʿAlaʾi Qureshi of Kalpi below — see Doubts.**
- **Shaikh Ahmad ʿIsa and Shaikh Muhammad ʿIsa** — father and son who "migrated from Delhi to Jaunpur", the son "then seven years old"; he trained under "Shaikh Fathu'llah", then Qazi Shihabu'd-Din Daulatabadi, then Shaikh Abu'l-Fath; "He was such a natural mystic that for days he would be completely unaware of his own physical surroundings" (pp. 282–283, folios 262–263).
- **Shaikh Bahaʾuʾd-Din of Jaunpur** — the most noteworthy disciple of Shaikh Muhammad ʿIsa; "Like his pir his attitude to material things was that of a devoted ascetic" (p. 283, folio 263). **One of three distinct men named Bahaʾuʾd-Din in this chunk — see Doubts.**
- **Shaikh Adhan Jaunpuri** (printed once as "Shaikh Adhan Jaunpun [OCR?] [Jaunpuri]") — son of the above; "At his death he was over a hundred years old and in his later years he was so weak he was unable to stand without the help of two people"; "Sama' was his great joy in life and he was so enthusiastic that ten men were not strong enough to quell his inspired dancing"; "Adhan Jaunpuri died in 976/1568-69 and was buried in Jaunpur" (p. 283, folio 263).
- **Saiyid ʿAli / Shaikh ʿAli** — a disciple of Shaikh Bahaʾuʾd-Din who "behaved like a majzub and refused to adhere to any particular mode of dress. Sometimes he donned a sufi khirga [OCR?] [khirqa] and other times he wore a soldier's uniform"; "He had four wives and received regular futuh"; "He had no servants and he himself waited on other dervishes"; known personally to Shaikh ʿAbduʾl-Haqq's father; "He greatly approved of sama' and believed that the musicians should recite ghazals or melodies of their own choice"; "Shaikh 'Ali died in 905/1499-1500" (p. 283, folio 263).
- **Maulana Jalaluʾd-Din of Manikpur** — disciple of "Shaikh Muhammad", himself a disciple of Shaikh Nizamuʾd-Din Auliyaʾ; he "led an austere life", rising when "the other khangah [OCR?] [khanqah] inmates retired" and reciting "the Ya Sin chapter of the Qur'an forty-one times"; "The Shaikh survived by transcribing the Qur'an and the completed copies were sold in Delhi" (pp. 283–284, folios 263–264); refused for forty years to eat in a Qazi's house, consenting only at the house of the Qazi's son once told the son did not work for the state judiciary (p. 284, folio 264). **No death date** — (not stated).
- **Maulana Khwaja** — "Maulana Jalalu'd-Din's natural son", "a saintly scholar. Often he would fast for three days and he always refused to eat anything he believed to have been earned from impious means" (p. 284, folio 264).
- **Shaikh Husamuʾd-Din Manikpuri** — son of the above; trained at Pandua under Shaikh Nur Qutb-i ʿAlam, "performed all kinds of service, including the arduous task of carrying from the forest wood for fuel"; "In 804/1401-02, he was appointed a khalifa by Shaikh Nur Qutb-i 'Alam and returned to Manikpur"; his *malfuzat* were "compiled into a book by his disciple Farid bin Salar, which was called the Rafiqu'l-'Arifin"; "The Shaikh died in 853/1449-S0 [OCR?] [1449-50]" (p. 284, folio 264). **The CE half of his death date is OCR-damaged — see Doubts.**
- **Shaikh Kamal alias Shaikh Kalu** — one of three well-known disciples of Husamuʾd-Din; "a most dedicated sufi and died at Kara, about forty-one miles west of Allahabad" (p. 284, folio 264). **Name collision with an archive row — see Doubts.**
- **Shah Saidu / Shaikh Saidu** — "a government servant during his early years. Impelled by the ecstatic urge, he gave away his entire property and became a khalifa of Shaikh Husamu'd-Din"; "Dressed in his khirga [khirqa] he visited the girl he had loved and told her he had decided to become a dervish, so in order to become his wife she also adopted a life of poverty"; "Shaikh Saidu was buried in Fathpur Hanswa, a town between Kanpur and Allahabad" (p. 285, folio 265).
- **Raji Hamid Shah** — co-disciple in the quilted-gown story; his "ancestors were Saiyids from Gardiz", footnoted "A town in modern Afghanistan, east of Ghazni", who "arrived in Delhi during the reign of Sultan Shamsu'd-Din Iltutmish"; "They received the title 'Raji' from the Muslim community because they came from a long line of saintly and learned men"; "In his youth Raji Hamid Shah was a soldier"; "For some time he lived at Jaunpur but later he retired to Manikpur where he died. The date of his death is not authoritatively known" (p. 285, folio 265) — **the book itself writes the date as unknown.**
- **Raji Saiyid Nur** — his son, "also a resident of Manikpur", who "combined a profession in the army with mysticism" (p. 285, folio 265).
- **Shaikh Hasan Tahir** — son of Tahir, who "migrated from Multan"; Hasan "was born in Bihar"; his father "appears to have been greatly opposed to the teachings contained in the Fusus al-Hikam" and forbade him the *Wahdat al-Wujud*; he "became a disciple of Raji Hamid Shah and moved to Jaunpur" (p. 285, folio 265); Barbak Shah, "the brother of Sultan Sikandar and Bahlul's viceroy in Jaunpur", is said to have been his disciple; invited to Agra by Sultan Sikandar, "where the court had been located since 1502"; "After a few months he went to Delhi and began living in the Vijai Mandal house, situated in one of the bastions built by Sultan Muhammad bin Tughlug [OCR?] [Tughluq], where many sufis had also lived. On 24 Rabi' I 909/16 September 1503, the Shaikh died and was buried near Vijai Mandal" (pp. 285–286, folios 265–266).
- **Maulana Ilahdad, known as Sharih (Commentator)** — classmate of Hasan Tahir, disciple of "Mulla 'Abdu'l-Malik 'Adil of Jaunpur, in turn, a disciple of Qazi Shihabu'd-Din"; he "was shocked when his friend Hasan Tahir became a sufi and criticised him for degrading the status of religious learning", then became Raji Hamid Shah's disciple after the mystic read his difficulties about the *Hidaya* unprompted; "Among the Maulana's disciples, Shaikh Ma'ruf was the most prominent" (p. 286, folio 266).
- **Mir Saiyid Ashraf Jahangir Simnani** (also printed "Saiyid Muhammad Ashraf Jahangir Simnani") — **the longest treatment in the chunk, pp. 286–290 (folios 266–270).** "born at the end of the thirteenth century and left Simnan at about the age of twenty-three"; friendly with "Shaikh 'Ala'u'd-Dawla Simnani" but unconvinced by his *Wahdat al-Shuhud*; studied at Kashan under "Shaikh 'Abdu'r-Razzaq Kamalu'd-Din bin 'Abdu'l-Ghana'im al-Kashani", leaving "some time before Shaikh 'Abdu'r-Razzaq Kashani's death in 730/1329" (pp. 286–287, folios 266–267); travelled with "Mir Saiyid 'Ali Hamadani" to "Iran, Iraq, Syria and Turkey", then Mashhad, then Transoxiana where "he spent some time with Khwaja Baha'u'd-Din Naqshband", then Multan and Uch; rebuffed at Delhi by "Saiyid Gisu Daraz"; "reaching Munyar on 2 January 1381", where he "led the pre-burial prayers of Shaikh Sharafu'd-Din Yahya Munyari"; "initiated as a Chishti by Shaikh 'Ala'u'l-Haqq" at Pandua, "his last initiation as he had already become a member of every existing sufi order"; then Sunargaon, "visiting the descendants of Shaikh Sharafu'd-Din Tawwama"; then Jaunpur, which he left at once for "Kichaucha, in the modern Faizabad district of U.P." (p. 287, folio 267). His books for novices: "the Basharatu'l-Ikhwan, the Fawa'idu'l-Ashraj [OCR?] [Ashraf], the Basharatu':-Zakirin [OCR?] [Basharatu'z-Zakirin] and Tanbihu'l-Ikhwan", and for specialists "The Miratu'l-Haqai'qg [OCR?] [Haqa'iq] and the Kanzu'd-Daqai'qg [OCR?] [Daqa'iq]" (p. 288, folio 268). **On his contested death date see "Arguments and interpretations".**
- **Rukn, the Mahdi claimant** — "Before Saiyid Muhammad Ashraf's arrival in India, a holyman named Rukn claimed to be the Mahdi. It was during the reign of Sultan Firuz Shah, who duly had Rukn beheaded" (p. 289, folio 269). **A one-word name that collides with an archive row's short form — see Doubts. This man is not a saint of the archive and is not mapped.**
- **Shaikh Sadruʾd-Din of Quniya** — named in Simnani's Mahdi discussion as a Shaikh who "had become embroiled in 1 [OCR?] [a] great controversy because of his refusal to endorse the impostor's claim" (p. 290, folio 270).
- **Shaikh Ahmad ʿAbduʾl-Haqq of Rudauli** — "The founder of the earliest centre there"; "born in Delhi", his father dead in his childhood, his elder brother "Shaikh Taqiu'd-Din"; disciple at Panipat of "Shaikh Jalal Panipati, who in turn traced his spiritual genealogy from Shaikh Shamsu'd-Din Turk also from Panipat, a khalifa of Shaikh 'Ala'u'd-Din 'Ali Ahmad Sabir. Therefore Shaikh Ahmad 'Abdu'l-Haqq could trace his own spiritual descent through the Sabiri branch of the Chishtis" (p. 290, folio 270); went to Sunam, then Bengal after "Timur's invasion in 1398-99 devastated both the Panjab and Delhi"; stayed at Pandua "with a kotwal (police officer)"; "left Bengal before its domination by Raja Ganesa when he was about fifty. Initially he stayed in Bihar and Awadh, finally settling in Rudauli"; "Twice he visited Jaunpur but Qazi Shihabu'd-Din conspired to prevent him from being granted an audience with Sultan Ibrahim Sharqi" (p. 291, folio 271); "Shaikh Ahmad 'Abdu'l-Haqq died on 15 Jumada II 837/27 January 1434" (p. 292, folio 272).
- **Shaikh Bakhtiyar of Rudauli** — "Formerly the slave of a jewel merchant", the most prominent disciple of Shaikh Ahmad; "Even after his initiation as a mystic, Shaikh Bakhtiyar continued to trade" (p. 292, folio 272). **Not the Khwaja Qutbuʾd-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki of the plate caption — see Doubts.**
- **Shaikh ʿArif and Shaikh Muhammad of Rudauli** — "Shaikh 'Arif, Shaikh Ahmad's son, was his spiritual successor. 'Arif was also a poet and a man of great tolerance to different ideas. His son, Shaikh Muhammad, was pir to the great sixteenth century sufi, Shaikh 'Abdu'l-Quddus Gangohi. His achievements will be discussed in Chapter Six" (p. 292, folio 272). **A hereditary succession of three generations at one centre — the clearest sajjada-style descent in the chunk, though the book does not use that word.**
- **Shaikh Jamal, a Muslim Gujar** — Shaikh Ahmad's contemporary "in Awadh (Ayodhya)", footnoted "A caste-like group among Muslims tending cows and living like gypsies"; "According to Shaikh Ahmad his disciple was the only real Muslim he had found between Pandua and Awadh" (p. 292, folio 272). **Name collision with an archive row — see Doubts.**
- **Shaikh Qiwamuʾd-Din of Lucknow** — disciple of Shaikh Nasiruʾd-Din Mahmud; the man through whom double initiation "seems to have been introduced" in Lucknow (pp. 292–293, folios 272–273).
- **Shaikh Sarang** — his disciple; "became an important dignitary in the administration of Sultan Firuz Shah Tughlugq [OCR?] [Tughluq] and is said to have founded Sarangpur in Malwa, naming it after himself"; resigned government service to become a disciple; made a hajj (p. 293, folio 273). **His tomb is the object of a described devotional practice — see "Practices, institutions, economy".**
- **Shaikh Mina** — "brought up from childhood by his pir as a son and later he became the disciple of Shaikh Sarang. A celibate", who sat "on a fence so that if he dozed he would fall to the ground" and "amidst a pile of thorns"; "He died at Lucknow and is buried right in the centre of the old part of the town" (p. 293, folio 273). **No death date** — (not stated).
- **Saʿduʾd-Din Khairabadi and Allahdiya Khairabadi** — the former "came from Khairabad in the modern district of Sitapur, in Uttar Pradesh", "an accomplished scholar of Arabic, grammar and Figh [Fiqh]" who wrote on "the Futuhat al-Makkiyya of Ibn al-'Arabi" and "After Shaikh Mina's death he completed the Shaikh Mina's malfuzat"; his disciple "Allahdiya Khairabadi who lived to a ripe old age, dying in 993/1585" (p. 293, folio 273).
- **Maulana Khwajgi** (also printed "Maulana Khwaijgi [OCR?] [Khwajgi]") — "the spiritual teacher of Qazi Shihabu'd-Din"; "a pupil of Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din Chiragh-i Dihli" who also studied under "Maulana Mu'inu'd-Din 'Umrani", "an enemy of sufism"; he migrated to Kalpi before Timur's invasion; "Both died at Kalpi where they were buried", with Maulana Ahmad (pp. 293–294, folios 273–274).
- **Maulana Ahmad Thaneswari** — another disciple of Shaikh Nasiruʾd-Din who stayed in Delhi and was arrested with his family during Timur's invasion; presented to Timur's court, where "a grandson of Maulana Burhanu'd-Din Marghniyani was Timur's Shaikhu'l-Islam"; he "alleged that Marghniyani had made several glaring errors in his book, the Hidaya"; "Timur ended the dispute by dismissing the court"; he then migrated to Kalpi and was buried there (p. 294, folio 274).
- **Shaikh Abuʾl-Fath ʿAlaʾi Qureshi** — "Another leading sufi saint of Kalpi", "a disciple of Mir Saiyid Muhammad Gisu Daraz. After making a pilgrimage to Mecca and Medina he finally settled in Kalpi. He was the author of treatises on grammar and sufism. When he died he was buried at Kalpi" (p. 294, folio 274). **No death date** — (not stated).
- **Khwaja Ikhtiyaruʾd-Din ʿUmar of Iraj** — "Both he and his ancestors held very high government posts. Impelled by the mystic urge, the Khwaja resigned from the government and became a disciple of Qazi Muhammad Sawi, who had been a disciple of Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din Mahmud. Ikhtiyaru'd-Din died at Iraj on 14 Muharram 809/1 July 1406" (pp. 294–295, folios 274–275).
- **Shaikh Yusuf Budh** — his disciple, "a poet as well as a writer of prose"; "He translated Ghazali's Minhaju'l 'Abidin into Persian"; his leading disciple was "Muhammad Bihamad Khani, the author of the Tarikh-i Muhammadi"; "In 834/1430-31, Shaikh Yusuf died while engrossed in a performance of sama'" (p. 295, folio 275).
- **Khwaja Muʿinuʾd-Din Khwurd and his line** — "the eldest son of Shaikh Husamu'd-Din Sukhta and the grandson of Shaikh Fakhru'd-Din, who in turn was the son of Khwaja Mu'inu'd-Din Chishti"; "a disciple of Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din Mahmud"; his sons moved to Mandu "after the occupation of Ajmer by the Rana of Mewar"; "One of his sons who had migrated to Mandu was called Shaikh Qutbu'd-Din" (p. 295, folio 275). **On "Shaikh Qutbuʾd-Din" see Doubts.**
- **Shaikh Qutbuʾd-Din, titled Chisht Khan** — offered the administration of Ajmer in 1455 by Sultan Mahmud Khalji, having earlier been given "the title of Chisht Khan, making him a commander of twelve thousand soldiers"; "Chisht Khan, who was brought up at Mandu, accepted the title but not the position at Ajmer" (p. 295, folio 275); he then engineered his cousin's removal to Ajmer out of jealousy (pp. 295–296, folios 275–276).
- **Shaikh Bayazid** — Chisht Khan's cousin, "who had retired to Baghdad returned to Mandu"; married to the daughter of "The Shaikhu'l-Islam of Mandu, Shaikh Mahmud Dihlawi", and also to a daughter of Shaikh Husain Nagauri; "Sultan Mahmud became Shaikh Bayazid's devotee" (pp. 295–296, folios 275–276).
- **Khwaja Husain Nagauri** — a leading sufi of Nagaur patronised by the Sultans of Malwa "even though he had refused to migrate to Mandu, the capital of the Malwa Sultans" (p. 295, folio 275); he "confirmed that the latter was a descendant of Khwaja Mu'inu'd-Din and the Sultan accepted this statement" (p. 296, folio 276); later the pir of Shaikh Ahmad Majid Shaibani (p. 296, folio 276).
- **Shah Miyanji of Malwa** — "noteworthy for his solitude and disregard for the power of the local 'ulama,'"; "A disciple of a khalifa of Saiyid Muhammad Gisu Daraz had been Miyanji's pir. The latter lived for 150 years and Shah Miyanji survived for 120. For periods of six months the Shah would remain without any human contact, at the same time starving himself by only drinking water or syrup"; he refused the Qazi of Mandu's attempts "to force him to conform to such laws of Shari'a as congregational prayers" (p. 296, folio 276).
- **Shaikh Ahmad Majid Shaibani** — "The son of Qazi Majdu'd-Din, whose ancestors were related to scholars who worked with the celebrated Imam Abu Hanifa"; "born at Narnol. By the age of eighteen he was an accomplished scholar"; as a child at Mandu he corrected Shaikh Mahmud Dihlawi's error in congregational prayers and criticised "the humiliating custom of prostration before rulers, which the Sultans of Mandu had introduced"; "Shaikh Ahmad left Mandu at an unknown date to become the disciple of Khwaja Husain Nagauri"; "Most of the Shaikh Ahmad's life was spent at Ajmer but in 922/1516 he left the town because of its occupation by Rana Sanga of Mewar. He migrated to Nagaur where he finally died on 25 Safar 927/4 February 1521" (p. 296, folio 276).
- **The dynastic frame, printed with regnal dates** — Jaunpur "In 1359 Sultan Firuz Shah Tughluq founded Jaunpur as a strategic military outpost to be used for his conquest of Bengal"; the eunuch "Malik Sarwar", titled "Khwaja-i Jahan (Master of the World) and Sultanu'sh Sharq (Sultan of the Eastern Region)" by "Muhammad Shah III (1389-93)", appointed governor in 1394 by "Mahmud Shah", carving a kingdom "extending from Kol (Aligarh) and Etawah to Bihar and Tirhut in the east", dying "in 1399"; "Mubarak Shah Sharqi"; "Ibrahim Shah Sharqi (1401-40)"; "Sultan Bahlul Lodi (1451-89)"; Jaunpur seized "In 1483-84"; Sultan Husain's death "in 1505" (p. 281, folio 261). Kalpi "In 1389-90 … became semi-independent under the nominal hegemony of Delhi", with the fort Iraj "on the Betwa, about forty-two miles north-east of Jhansi" (pp. 293–294, folios 273–274). Malwa: "Dilawar Khan Ghuri, whom the Tughlug [Tughluq] Sultan, Nasiru'd-Din Muhammad bin Firuz Shah, had in 1390-91, appointed governor of the province. In 1401-02, Dilawar Khan Ghuri broke away from the Delhi Sultanate", threatened by "the Ranas of Mewar and the Sultans of Jaunpur, Gujarat and the rulers of the Bahmani kingdom"; "Sultan Mahmud Khalji (1436-69)"; "Sultan Hushang (1406-35) of Malwa" (pp. 288, 295; folios 268, 275).
- **Bare toponyms, listed under the interim convention (a)** — Jaunpur, Delhi, Kol (Aligarh), Etawah, Bihar, Tirhut, Bengal, the Doab, Manikpur, Allahabad, Pratabgarh, Kara, Fathpur Hanswa, Kanpur, Gardiz, Ghazni, Agra, Rudauli, Lucknow, Panipat, Sunam, the Panjab, Pandua, Sunargaon, Munyar, Kichaucha, Faizabad, Awadh (Ayodhya), Khairabad, Sitapur, Kalpi, Iraj, Jhansi, the Betwa, Jalaun, Malwa, Mandu, the Vindhya mountains, Ajmer, Nagaur, Sarangpur, Narnol, Mewar, Gujarat, Simnan, Kashan, Mashhad, Iran, Iraq, Syria, Turkey, Khurasan, Transoxiana, Quniya, Baghdad, Mecca, Medina, Egypt (pp. 281–296, folios 261–276). **None of these is an archive row's location, so the interim convention withholds no id here** — with the two exceptions below, which are the only archive locations named in the whole chunk.
- **Multan, named twice and bare both times** — "Shaikh Hasan's father, Tahir, migrated from Multan" (p. 285, folio 265) and "taking the route through Multan and Uch to India" (p. 287, folio 267). **No Multan shrine, tomb, saint, ʿurs or custodian is attached to either mention**, so under interim convention (a) no id is taken; see Doubts for the rows this would reach if the ruling reverses.

## Practices, institutions, economy

**This is the strongest section of the chunk, and its centre of gravity is Ajmer, which has no archive row.** The passage on the Ajmer tomb complex is the single most transferable piece of shrine-economy evidence in pp. 281–296.

- **Royal patronage of tombs, stated as a general practice of a dynasty — the chunk's key sentence on shrine endowment.** "The Sultans of Malwa showered riches on the Chishti tombs at Ajmer and Nagaur and patronized the leading sufis of Nagaur, such as Khwaja Husain Nagauri even though he had refused to migrate to Mandu, the capital of the Malwa Sultans" (p. 295, folio 275). **Two tomb sites, a named living beneficiary, and a refusal to relocate that did not cost him the patronage. No sums, no grants, no dates** — (not stated).
- **A madrasa attached to a tomb, and a contested right to teach at it — custodianship by descent, disputed.** Chisht Khan "succeeded in having Bayazid sent back to Ajmer at the madrasa attached to the tomb of Khwaja Mu'inu'd-Din. This disturbed the other teachers and they complained to the Sultan that Khwaja Mu'inu'd-Din did not have any offspring and Bayazid was therefore not a fit person to work at the Ajmer seminary" (p. 296, folio 276). **The claim contested is descent from the saint; the remedy sought is exclusion from the shrine's teaching institution.**
- **The dispute is settled twice, in opposite directions, centuries apart — and the second settlement is a state seizure of a khanqah.** First: "Shaikh Husain Nagauri who had also married his daughter to Shaikh Bayazid confirmed that the latter was a descendant of Khwaja Mu'inu'd-Din and the Sultan accepted this statement" (p. 296, folio 276) — **note that the confirming witness is the claimant's father-in-law, which the book does not remark on.** Then: "However, as has been mentioned earlier, the board of enquiry which Akbar, the great Mughal Emperor, had appointed did not accept the argument that the Khwaja had issue, and the Emperor assumed control over his khangah [OCR?] [khanqah]" (p. 296, folio 276), cross-referenced to "see Chapter Two, supra, pp. 125-26". **A named imperial board of enquiry, a finding of no issue, and a takeover — the clearest case of state control of a shrine institution in the chunk.**
- **Repeated refusal of land grants by a Shaikh, twice narrated, with the instruments named.** At Rudauli "the Shaikh led the traditional ascetic existence of a mystic. This took the form of constant refusals of land grants for either his khangah [khanqah] or for his family. Once a son-in-law accepted a farman from the mugta' [OCR?] [muqtaʿ] of Rudauli. So incensed was the Shaikh when he discovered the gift, he tore the paper into shreds and ordered that not a single piece remain in his khangah [khanqah]" (p. 291, folio 271). And: "On another occasion a qazi of Sultan Ibrahim presented another farman of a number of villages from his ruler. Firmly, the Shaikh refused the offer, saying that as God provided food for Sultan Ibrahim, his elephants and horses, he felt he could also be trusted to feed himself and his descendants" (p. 291, folio 271). **A farman, a muqtaʿ, and a grant of villages — the vocabulary of shrine endowment, here appearing only to be refused.**
- **Futuh as a named and divided income, with a household attached.** Saiyid ʿAli "received regular futuh, half of which he needed to support his numerous wives, the rest he gave to the needy. He had no servants and he himself waited on other dervishes" (p. 283, folio 263). **A fifty-fifty split between household maintenance and alms is the only quantified division of offerings in the chunk.**
- **A sufi trading for a living, bounded by his pir's spiritual territory — wilayat as a commercial boundary.** Shaikh Bakhtiyar "continued to trade, but took great care to stay within the boundaries claimed by Shaikh Ahmad as his particular domain of spiritual influence. This story tends to indicate that Shaikh Ahmad was not opposed to a sufi earning an adequate income while simultaneously living as an ascetic" (p. 292, folio 272).
- **A Shaikh living by copying the Qurʾan, and a fast tied to revenue oppression.** "The Shaikh survived by transcribing the Qur'an and the completed copies were sold in Delhi. If revenue officials made depredations on the ra'iyat (ryot) in order to collect revenue, the Maulana would stop eating meat, fearing it might have been obtained from illegally seized animals" (p. 284, folio 264). **An explicit link between land revenue practice and a saint's diet.**
- **Tomb visitation as an ascetic discipline, with a distance and a posture — the chunk's only description of ziyarat to a saint's grave.** "In the winter months Shaikh Mina would pray at the tomb of the adopted father, keeping himself awake by wearing wet clothes. Shaikh Sarang's tomb filled him with such awe that, barefooted, he would walk forty miles in order to reach it" (p. 293, folio 273). **Barefoot, forty miles, in winter, in wet clothes.**
- **Ziyarat to the Delhi saints named as an intention, and overridden by a royal summons.** "Although the Shaikh wished to visit the tombs of the Delhi saints, he left for Agra where the court had been located since 1502" (p. 286, folio 266).
- **Burial in the centre of a town, recorded as a present-tense fact.** Shaikh Mina "died at Lucknow and is buried right in the centre of the old part of the town" (p. 293, folio 273). Other burials recorded: Adhan Jaunpuri "buried in Jaunpur" (p. 283, folio 263); Shaikh Saidu "buried in Fathpur Hanswa" (p. 285, folio 265); Shaikh Hasan "buried near Vijai Mandal" in Delhi (p. 286, folio 266); Maulana Khwajgi and Maulana Ahmad "Both died at Kalpi where they were buried" (p. 294, folio 274); Shaikh Abuʾl-Fath ʿAlaʾi Qureshi "buried at Kalpi" (p. 294, folio 274). **In no case does the book say a tomb was built, endowed, visited by pilgrims or given a custodian** — (not stated).
- **Double initiation into two orders, with a rule broken, a first instance and a stated limit.** "Although Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya had insisted during his lifetime that sufis join one order only, from the fourteenth century sufis were often initiated into both the Chishtiyya and the Suhrawardiyya… Some sufi migrants had already been received into an order but were again initiated into other silsilas after their arrival in India. Later sufis obtained initiation from several orders and they in turn initiated disciples into numerous other orders. In such a situation, however, there remained a dominant interest and allegiance to one main order" (pp. 292–293, folios 272–273). **Simnani is the extreme case: his Chishti initiation at Pandua "was his last initiation as he had already become a member of every existing sufi order" (p. 287, folio 267).**
- **Deputisation recorded with a date — the chunk's one dated khilafat.** "In 804/1401-02, he was appointed a khalifa by Shaikh Nur Qutb-i 'Alam and returned to Manikpur where his learning and ascetic achievements made a considerable impact on the local people" (p. 284, folio 264). Two further khalifa appointments carry no date: Shah Saidu "became a khalifa of Shaikh Husamu'd-Din" (p. 285, folio 265), and Shaikh Shamsuʾd-Din Turk was "a khalifa of Shaikh 'Ala'u'd-Din 'Ali Ahmad Sabir" (p. 290, folio 270). **No khilafat-nama and no khirqa-granting ceremony is described anywhere in the chunk** — (not stated).
- **Malfuzat compilation as a disciple's task, twice.** "His malfuzat (discourses) were compiled into a book by his disciple Farid bin Salar, which was called the Rafiqu'l-'Arifin" (p. 284, folio 264); and "After Shaikh Mina's death he completed the Shaikh Mina's malfuzat" (p. 293, folio 273).
- **Samaʿ in three registers — ecstatic dancing, a rule about repertoire, and death during a performance.** Adhan Jaunpuri "was so enthusiastic that ten men were not strong enough to quell his inspired dancing" (p. 283, folio 263); Shaikh ʿAli "greatly approved of sama' and believed that the musicians should recite ghazals or melodies of their own choice" (p. 283, folio 263); "In 834/1430-31, Shaikh Yusuf died while engrossed in a performance of sama'" (p. 295, folio 275). **A performer's freedom of repertoire is a rare institutional detail; the book's usual word for the singer, qawwal, does not appear here.**
- **A collective vocal practice criticised as pir worship — the chunk's only recorded accusation of shrine-style veneration of a living Shaikh.** "With his disciples in the Khangah [khanqah] he showed great devotion to pas-i anfas (breath control) and the key word before and after prayer was Haqq. Whether they were talking or buying and selling in the bazaar, the Shaikh's disciples would cry 'Haqq.' When the Shaikh visited the mosque his disciples walked in front of him crying *'Haqq'. Many people were critical of such a custom, calling it pir worship, but the Shaikh justified it by quoting from the Futuhat al-Makkiyya of Ibn al-'Arabi" (p. 292, folio 272). **The defence is textual, from Ibn al-ʿArabi.**
- **A state expulsion of qalandars and dervishes from a town, with the book's own guess at the motive.** At Pandua "Ghiyasu'd-Din A'zam Shah was the Sultan of Bengal and had embarked on a scheme to expel all galandars [OCR?] [qalandars] and dervishes from the town. Possibly this was part of an attempt to purge non-Bengalis from the administration" (p. 290, folio 270), the anecdote ending "Next morning the king ordered the Muslim mystics to leave Pandua. All were arrested and escorted to boats which took them to exile. This action led to great unrest in the town" (pp. 290–291, folios 270–271). **Arrest, deportation by boat and urban unrest: the chunk's one episode of the state acting against dervish institutions.**
- **Qalandars and yogis compared on commensality, by a sufi, to the qalandars' disadvantage.** In the same anecdote the disguised king is driven off by the qalandars but fed by the yogis, who "replied that this was in accordance with their custom of sharing all food equally, even with dogs" (p. 290, folio 270). **A langar-like practice of equal food-sharing, attributed to yogis rather than to a Muslim shrine.**
- **Office, title and rank conferred on religious men by rulers.** Qazi Shihabuʾd-Din "was appointed the Qaziu'l-Quzat (Chief Qazi) and given the title Maliku'l-'Ulama' (Prince of the 'Ulama')" (p. 282, folio 262); Shaikh Qutbuʾd-Din was given "the title of Chisht Khan, making him a commander of twelve thousand soldiers" and offered "Ajmer's administration" in 1455 (p. 295, folio 275); "The Shaikhu'l-Islam of Mandu, Shaikh Mahmud Dihlawi" (p. 295, folio 275); "a grandson of Maulana Burhanu'd-Din Marghniyani was Timur's Shaikhu'l-Islam" (p. 294, folio 274); the sadr's duty to "place before rulers applications from the many saiyids, qazis and sufis" (p. 288, folio 268). **Conversely, three men resign or refuse state service: Shaikh Sarang (p. 293, folio 273), Khwaja Ikhtiyaruʾd-Din ʿUmar (p. 294, folio 274) and Shaikh Abuʾl-Fath, who "did not accept a government office and led a life of poverty and near-starvation" (p. 282, folio 262).**
- **Court ceremonial criticised from inside the religious class.** Shaikh Ahmad "was also disparaging about what he believed to be the humiliating custom of prostration before rulers, which the Sultans of Mandu had introduced" (p. 296, folio 276).
- **A khanqah's internal routine, briefly.** Maulana Jalaluʾd-Din "would rise when the other khangah [khanqah] inmates retired. From then until morning he would incessantly pray and recite the Ya Sin chapter of the Qur'an forty-one times. In the morning he would give religious instruction to his disciples" (p. 283, folio 263). **Forty-one recitations of Ya Sin is the only counted devotional quota in the chunk.**
- **Poverty as shared property, with a garment divided in three.** "At one time their entire clothing amounted to one quilted gown. Dividing the garment into three portions. Shaikh Husamu'd-Din Manikpuri gave the upper and lower parts to the disciples and wrapped himself with the cotton threads" (p. 285, folio 265).
- **Refusal of food from state servants as a standing rule.** "The Maulana said that for forty years he had not taken a meal in a Qazi's house", relenting only for the Qazi's son once told the son did not work "for the state judiciary" (p. 284, folio 264); Maulana Khwaja "always refused to eat anything he believed to have been earned from impious means" (p. 284, folio 264).
- **Hereditary spiritual succession at a centre, three generations deep.** Shaikh Ahmad ʿAbduʾl-Haqq → "Shaikh 'Arif, Shaikh Ahmad's son, was his spiritual successor" → "His son, Shaikh Muhammad, was pir to the great sixteenth century sufi, Shaikh 'Abdu'l-Quddus Gangohi" (p. 292, folio 272). **The book does not use the word sajjada nashin, and gives no property, income or shrine attached to the succession** — (not stated).
- **A saint's counsel on government, set out as a programme.** Simnani's letter to Sultan Hushang: "in the event of an infidel invasion jihad was compulsory for all Muslims, but as retaliation for an internal revolt of kafirs, jihad was optional. The administration should be run in consultation with the heads of different power groups" (p. 288, folio 268); and the ruler "should hold public audiences in which complaints of peasants and common Muslims were heard. Justice and the holy law should be enforced by the Sultan… A sadr should have integrity and kindness without distinction, and preferably should also be a mystic. A vizier should be accomplished in all the sciences and the arts, but more significantly, he should be deeply religious" (p. 288, folio 268).
- **A saint intervening in a war of succession — at another saint's request.** "At Shaikh Nur Qutb-i 'Alam's request, the Saiyid persuaded Sultan Ibrahim Sharqi [OCR?] [Sharqi] to liberate Bengal from the domination of Raja Ganesa" (p. 288, folio 268).
- **And a saint refusing to pray for a throne.** "Like other ambitious princes, Barbak Shah asked his spiritual director to pray that he might gain the Delhi throne, the Shaikh, however refused, telling him that God wished to elevate someone else" (p. 285, folio 265) — the refusal itself then earns him a royal invitation (p. 286, folio 266).
- **An access dispute at a court, saint against qazi.** "Twice he visited Jaunpur but Qazi Shihabu'd-Din conspired to prevent him from being granted an audience with Sultan Ibrahim Sharqi" (p. 291, folio 271); Simnani likewise "immediately left for Kichaucha" because "the personality of Qazi Shihabu'd-Din Daulatabadi so dominated the Sultan's court" (p. 287, folio 267).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

Attributions are the book's own, including its hedges ("is said to have", "the story continues", "are legendary").

- **Two supernatural circumnavigations of the world, which the book explicitly separates from the real one.** "Simnani travelled round the world only once; and then performed the same journey on two occasions in a supernatural way; hagiologists including the author of the Lata'if-i Ashrafi, however, have failed to discriminate between the two" (p. 286, folio 266).
- **A miraculous flight, with its technical term footnoted.** The *Lataif-i Ashrafi* "tends to imply that he again travelled around the entire world with Shah Madar, this of course, would imply a miraculous journey while in the state of Tair, a common occurrence attributed to many sufis of that century", footnoted "A sufi term which indicates miraculous flying from one place to another" (p. 287, folio 267).
- **An arrival timed to a death — the chunk's neatest hagiographic coincidence.** Simnani reached Munyar "on 2 January 1381. At the time of his arrival, the body of Shaikh Sharafu'd-Din Yahya Munyari was awaiting burial. The Saiyid led the pre-burial prayers of Shaikh Sharafu'd-Din, and this would indicate he had already achieved considerable prominence" (p. 287, folio 267). **Rizvi reads it as evidence of standing, not as a miracle.**
- **Thought-reading, twice.** The Qazi of Manikpur "first tested the Maulana's power of inspiration, and after being duly impressed, invited the Maulana to his house for a meal" (p. 284, folio 264); and "Without being told, the mystic knew of the difficulties Ilahdad had about the Hidaya and managed to ease his mind. So impressed was the Maulana, he immediately became Shaikh Raji's disciple" (p. 286, folio 266).
- **A cure by forced emesis, converting an enemy of sufism.** Maulana Muʿinuʾd-Din "suffered from severe attacks of asthma and all treatments had failed"; "The Shaikh forced his visitor to eat a mixture of rice and yogurt which precipitated violent vomiting, but which finally cured him. Thestory continues [OCR?] [The story continues] that much to his pupil's satisfaction the Maulana was so impressed he also joined the ranks of devotees of Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din" (p. 294, folio 274).
- **Extreme longevity, given as plain numbers.** "The latter lived for 150 years and Shah Miyanji survived for 120" (p. 296, folio 276); and Adhan Jaunpuri "At his death he was over a hundred years old" (p. 283, folio 263).
- **A teaching miracle by demonstration rather than wonder — pebbles, mud and water.** The Shaikh had a disciple dig a hole, fill it with water, fill it with pebbles and remove them one by one, then poured in mud which could not be retrieved: "He was expected to lose himself in the Divine, the Shaikh told his pupil, like the mud in the water, and also to simultaneously efface himself in order to become a sufi" (p. 291, folio 271).
- **A feast for dogs — a rebuke disguised as an insult.** A bitch owned by Shaikh Ahmad "had puppies and her owner celebrated by giving a large feast for all the dignitaries and common people in Awadh, but excluded Shaikh Jamal… Shaikh Ahmad however replied that as the feast had been given only for dogs, that is the worldly, how could humans have been invited 76 [OCR?] [invited?]" (p. 292, folio 272).
- **A saint's insulated presence at court.** Shaikh Ahmad and "a dervish (majzub) friend went to the palace to test the king's reaction to their presence. They remained unnoticed for quite some time, then left… The Shaikh declared that the king did not expel dervishes and galandars [qalandars], only ignorant mystics" (pp. 290–291, folios 270–271).
- **Leaves as a head-covering, in the poverty story.** "En route to the Jami' mosque, the trio were presented with a parcel of sweetmeats. Taking the leaves in which the food was wrapped, Shaikh Husamu'd-Din placed them on his head so it would not be bare in the mosque" (p. 285, folio 265).
- **Mahdi claimants and their fate.** "a holyman named Rukn claimed to be the Mahdi… Sultan Firuz Shah, who duly had Rukn beheaded" (p. 289, folio 269); "The many signs of the Mahdi had been recorded in the authentic Ahadis of the Prophet, said Simnani, and it was therefore easy to discredit false claimants"; "Simnani met another Mahdi pretender in Turkey and advised him to refrain from such adventurous and dangerous ambitions" (p. 290, folio 270).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Rizvi's source criticism of a saint's biography, naming the unreliable texts — the chunk's methodological centrepiece.** "The story of his life in the biographical accounts, such as the Miratu'l-Asrar, are legendary"; hagiologists "including the author of the Lata'if-i Ashrafi… have failed to discriminate between the two"; "More reliable are the Saiyid's own letters, although they fail to provide biographical evidence" (p. 286, folio 266).
- **A death date rejected on internal evidence, and a rival date preferred — with a named modern authority on each side.** "As indicated by his letters to Sultan Ibrahim Shah Shargi [Sharqi] on Ganesa's uzurpation [OCR?] [usurpation] of power, Saiyid Muhammad Ashraf was still alive in 1415. This would tend to negate the date of his death as given in the Khazinatu'l Asfiya' as 27 Muharram 808/25 July 1405, although it has been accepted uncritically by many modern scholars. Basing his assumption on the Tabagqat-i Shahjahani [OCR?] [Tabaqat-i Shahjahani], Rieu believes the Saiyid died after 840/1436—37. Although this would make the Shaikh's life rather long, this date is more acceptable than the anachronistic date specified in the Khazinatu'l-Asfiya'" (p. 288, folio 268). **Two incompatible death dates are printed side by side and the later one preferred; neither is asserted flatly.**
- **Madrasa and khanqah reconciled in one man, with the reason he was honoured by sufis.** "In true Ghazalian style, Qazi Shihabu'd-Din united the diverse philosophies of the khangah [khanqah] and the madrasa. He himself, however, was more famous as a theologian. It was his rejection of the notion of the 'alim's innate superiority that gave him an honoured place amongst sufis" (p. 282, folio 262).
- **A local intellectual trend tied to the fall of a dynasty.** "The early mystics of Jaunpur took considerable interest in Figh [Fiqh] but this trend did not long survive the demise of Sharqi rule. During that period sufis who wished to remain aloof from the traditions of Qazi 'Abdu'l-Mugtadir [Muqtadir] and Qazi Shihabu'd-Din moved to other towns" (p. 283, folio 263). **Migration of sufis away from a court is explained as doctrinal distancing.**
- **A court culture judged for its effect on an order's independence — the chapter's clearest political verdict in this range.** "It would seem that the atmosphere of the Mandu court did not foster the independence of the Chishti order from the state. The religious tone of the court alternated between the authoritarianism of the 'ulama' and…" (p. 296, folio 276). **The sentence is cut off at the chunk boundary and its second half must be read at the head of chunk 018.**
- **A political motive proposed for an expulsion, and immediately conceded to be unsupported.** "Possibly this was part of an attempt to purge non-Bengalis from the administration. The following anecdote by Shaikh Ahmad 'Abdu'l-Haqq, although it gives an insight into the social ethics of contemporary galandars [qalandars] and yogis, fails to throw light on the political motivation of such a scheme" (p. 290, folio 270).
- **Wahdat al-Wujud made the mainstream position, and its opponents given a non-doctrinal motive.** "Simnani emphasized that the majority of sufis were followers of the Unity of Being theory and the hostility of the 'ulama' and the jurists was prompted by reasons of expediency" (p. 288, folio 268); Simnani wrote for novices because "Indian sufis did not have an adequate knowledge of the terminology contained in the theory of the Wahdat al-Wujud" (p. 288, folio 268); and he "popularized the use of the Persian term Hama Ust (All is He) thus emphasizing the belief that anything other than God did not exist—the Tawhid meant that what existed was He… The Unity of Being did not imply that Being was united with the world, wrote Simnani instead it was a realization by the slave that the one Divine Essence was manifested in creation" (pp. 288–289, folios 268–269).
- **The Rumi verses are read as ascent, not transmigration.** "Simnani saw the death of self in terms of a spiritual ascent towards the Divine and maintained that it demanded complete severance from involvement in earthly existence. The verses did not, however, advocate transmigration, which Simnani added, was a different thing altogether" (p. 289, folio 269).
- **A defence of Ibn al-ʿArabi on Pharaoh's faith, in a letter to a hostile jurist.** "In a letter to Qazi Shihabu'd-Din Daulatabadi, Simnani gave a very spirited defence of Ibn al-'Arabi's theory of the obedience and faith of Pharaoh Thothmis I of Egypt… Ibn al-'Arabi observed that the Pharaoh feared neither hell nor its tortures, however, he did obey the creative will of God (a/-Mashiyya [OCR?] [al-Mashiyya]) and His eternally predetermined command. Thus he was not a free agent and his disobedience was neither purely religious nor irreligious and therefore it was unfair to call him unfaithful" (p. 289, folio 269).
- **The four-term ladder of Shariʿa, Tariqa, Haqiqa — a citable doctrinal summary.** "The Shari'a was described by him as the 'girding up of one's loins' in a determined attempt to obey the commands of Islam. The Tariga [OCR?] [Tariqa] involved liberation from one's self and dissociation from anything not apertaining [OCR?] [appertaining] to God. Hagiqa [OCR?] [Haqiqa] was to be linked with the divine and this union was to be enjoyed. The Shari'a was freedom from want, the Tariga [Tariqa] was Fana' and the Hagiga [Haqiqa] was Baga' [OCR?] [Baqa']… there were neither roads nor stages in the journey towards Allah, Sayr ila'-Allah" (p. 286, folio 266).
- **A parable of obedience that is also a theory of transmitted grace — directly relevant to how shrine lineages justify themselves.** "A truly obedient disciple was like a white patch on a garment of the same colour. When the garment was washed, the patch also became white and this symbolized the fact that whatever divine grace was received by the pir, the disciple also shared. A disciple who did not obey his pir received no blessings, was a disciple in name only and could be likened to a black patch on a white garment" (p. 284, folio 264).
- **Hallaj judged immature — a rare evaluative remark on the archetypal martyr.** "Often Shaikh Ahmad 'Abdu'l-Haqq remarked that Hallaj had been a child to disclose divine secrets and that there were some sufis who were so mature they could drink an ocean of divine secrets and reveal nothing" (p. 291, folio 271).
- **Malamati concealment described as a deliberate method, with the state as its instrument.** Shaikh Muhammad "associated with the Sultan, in true malmati [OCR?] [malamati] fashion in order to make himself unpopular with the people, and thus conceal his true spiritual achievements" (p. 283, folio 263). **Note the inversion: proximity to the state is presented as self-abasement, not as compromise.**

**Archive-mapping doubts, in order of how much a human should care:**

- **`shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar` is my weakest mapping and I am flagging it rather than defending it.** The evidence is **one word, "Baba Farid", inside a plate caption on a leaf the brief itself identifies as scan furniture** (p. 298, folio not stated). The known trap for this book is that "Shaikh Farid" is two men and the book does not disambiguate them; **here the printed form is "Baba Farid", not "Shaikh Farid", and the company is the Chishti chain — "Khwaja Mu'inu'd-Din Chishti; Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki; Baba Farid" — which points to Baba Farid Ganj-i Shakar rather than to Faridu'd-Din Mahmud of Nagaur.** But **the chunk supplies no date, no Ajodhan, no "Ganj-i Shakar" and no tomb**, and Nagaur is separately and repeatedly named in this same chunk (pp. 295, 296; folios 275, 276), which is exactly the confusion the trap warns about. **A consolidator may legitimately drop this mapping; it carries no fact beyond the name.**
- **`shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht` versus `shrine-of-jalaluddin-surkh-posh-bukhari-jalaluddin-bukhari`: I believe this chunk settles it, and I say so rather than leaving it open, but the settlement is title-and-place only.** The book prints the title "Makhdum Jahaniyan" three times (pp. 287, 292, 293; folios 267, 272, 273) and places the man at Uch (p. 293, folio 273); "Makhdum Jahaniyan" is the distinguishing title of the Jahangasht row and not of the Surkh-Posh row, which is why I mapped it where previous workers mapped nothing. **What would have made it airtight and is absent: a death date, the word "Jahangasht", or a generation marker** — all (not stated). **The fourth mention, "Saiyid Jalal Bukhari" bare (p. 295, folio 275), is ambiguous on its own** and is mapped only by transfer from the equation "Makhdum Jahaniyan Saiyid Jalal Bukhari" on p. 292 (folio 272); **if a human disagrees with that transfer, drop the p. 295 bullet, not the other three.** Note also that the nisba "Bukhari" alone is shared by no fewer than five archive rows — `shrine-of-jalaluddin-surkh-posh-bukhari-jalaluddin-bukhari`, `shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht`, `shrine-of-mauj-darya-bukhari`, `shrine-of-hazrat-muhammad-ayub-shah-bukhari` and `wadpagga-sharif` — so it can never carry a mapping by itself.
- **`shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya` rests on one clause and nothing else.** The book names him once, gives him no place, no date and no order, and uses him only as the authority for a rule (p. 292, folio 272). **The mapping is correct under the 18 September ruling but it is a single-sentence row, and nothing in it is shrine evidence.**
- **`tomb-of-qutbuddin-aibak` was considered and deliberately NOT mapped, twice.** The chunk contains two men called Qutbuʾd-Din and neither is Aibak: **"Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki" in the plate caption (p. 298, folio not stated)**, who the standing trap says is a different man, and **"Shaikh Qutbu'd-Din", son of Khwaja Muʿinuʾd-Din Khwurd and later titled Chisht Khan (p. 295, folio 275)**, a fifteenth-century Mandu courtier. A third, "Shaikh Nur Qutb-i 'Alam" of Pandua (pp. 284, 288, 291; folios 264, 268, 271), shares only the element *Qutb*. **No bare short form was mapped.**
- **`shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam` was considered and NOT mapped.** The only "Rukn" in the chunk is "a holyman named Rukn" who "claimed to be the Mahdi" and was beheaded by Sultan Firuz Shah (p. 289, folio 269). **This chunk gives no Shaikh Ruknuʾd-Din, no Multan for him, no title and no date**, so it contributes nothing to settling the weak mapping two earlier workers flagged — and it must not be allowed to look like corroboration.
- **`shah-jamal` was considered and NOT mapped.** "Shaikh Jamal, a Muslim Gujar" is a fifteenth-century figure "in Awadh (Ayodhya)" (p. 292, folio 272); the row's principal figure is "Syed Shah Jamal Uddin Naqvi Bukhari" of Lahore, `figure_died` 1671. **The book gives this Shaikh Jamal no date, no Lahore and no Bukhari nisba**, and the reasoning that separates them uses the index's date, not the book's, so it is flagged rather than dismissed.
- **`shrine-of-baba-shah-kamal` was considered and NOT mapped.** "Shaikh Kamal alias Shaikh Kalu… died at Kara, about forty-one miles west of Allahabad" (p. 284, folio 264); the row is a Lahore Qadiri. **No date is given for either in the chunk**, so the separation rests on place alone.
- **`shrine-of-shah-shams-ud-din-sabzwari` was considered and NOT mapped.** "Shaikh Shamsu'd-Din Turk also from Panipat" (p. 290, folio 270) shares only the element *Shamsuʾd-Din* with the Multan row; the book gives him Panipat and a Sabiri Chishti affiliation, not Multan.
- **`lal-shahbaz-qalandar` and `shamsabad` were considered and NOT mapped.** The word *qalandar* appears only as a generic class, "all galandars [qalandars] and dervishes" (p. 290, folio 270), and once as a title of an unrelated man in the plate caption, "Shaikh Abu "Ali Qalandar" (p. 298, folio not stated). **No Sehwan, no Marwand, no Usman, no Shams Ali.**
- **Multan is the one archive location the interim toponym convention actually withholds anything for in this chunk.** It is named twice and bare both times (pp. 285, 287; folios 265, 267). **If ruling (a) reverses, the rows it would reach are `shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya`, `shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam`, `shrine-of-shah-shams-ud-din-sabzwari`, `shrine-of-shah-yusaf-gardez`, `shrine-of-syed-musa-pak`, `shrine-of-hafiz-muhammad-jamal-multani`, `shrine-of-hazrat-shah-ali-akbar-shah-ali-akbar-shamsi` and `prahladpuri-temple` — eight rows on two passing references, which is precisely the reason the interim convention exists.** Uch is the other archive location named, but neither of its two mentions is bare, so it needed no withholding (pp. 287, 293; folios 267, 273).
- **Hinglaj does not appear**, so the empty-id defect on the row `Shaktipeeth Shri Hinglaj Mata Mandir` does not arise. **No id has been invented anywhere in these notes; every bold id is copied from `shrine_index.tsv`.**

**Name collisions inside the chunk itself — the book uses one name for several men and does not always warn the reader:**

- **"Shaikh Bahaʾuʾd-Din" is three men**: the Jaunpuri disciple of Shaikh Muhammad ʿIsa (p. 283, folio 263); "Khwaja Baha'u'd-Din Naqshband" of Transoxiana (p. 287, folio 267); and "Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya" (p. 292, folio 272). **Only the third is mapped.**
- **"Shaikh Abuʾl-Fath" is two men**: the grandson of Qazi ʿAbduʾl-Muqtadir, d. 858/1454 (pp. 282–283, folios 262–263), and "Shaikh Abu'l-Fath 'Ala'i Qureshi" of Kalpi, a disciple of Gisu Daraz (p. 294, folio 274). **The book does not cross-reference them.**
- **"Shaikh Husamuʾd-Din" is two men**: Husamuʾd-Din Manikpuri (pp. 284–285, folios 264–265) and "Shaikh Husamu'd-Din Sukhta", father of Khwaja Muʿinuʾd-Din Khwurd (p. 295, folio 275).
- **"Shaikh Muhammad" is at least three**: the disciple of Nizamuʾd-Din Auliyaʾ and pir of the Manikpur Qazi's party (pp. 283–284, folios 263–264); Shaikh Muhammad ʿIsa (p. 282, folio 262); and Shaikh Muhammad of Rudauli, pir of ʿAbduʾl-Quddus Gangohi (p. 292, folio 272).
- **"Shaikh Ahmad" is at least three**: Shaikh Ahmad ʿIsa (p. 282, folio 262); Shaikh Ahmad ʿAbduʾl-Haqq of Rudauli (pp. 290–292, folios 270–272); Maulana Ahmad Thaneswari (p. 294, folio 274); and Shaikh Ahmad Majid Shaibani (p. 296, folio 276).
- **"Shaikh Nasiruʾd-Din Mahmud" and "Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din Chiragh-i Dihli" are given as the same man without the book saying so** (pp. 281, 294; folios 261, 274). Both are named as the pir of Maulana Khwajgi.
- **"Simnani" names two men in the same paragraph** — "Mir Saiyid Ashraf Jahangir Simnani" and "Shaikh 'Ala'u'd-Dawla Simnani", whose doctrine the first rejected (pp. 286–287, folios 266–267). **The book also switches between "Mir Saiyid Ashraf Jahangir Simnani", "Saiyid Ashraf", "Saiyid Muhammad Ashraf" and "Simnani" for the first man across five pages (pp. 286–290, folios 266–270); these are one person, not several.**

**Internal contradictions and gaps, kept rather than resolved:**

- **A man is said to have refused all land grants and is simultaneously the founder of a centre that passed to his son and grandson** (pp. 291, 292; folios 271, 272). **The book does not explain what the Rudauli khanqah subsisted on after the refusals**, and names no endowment, no income and no offerings there — (not stated).
- **Simnani's death date is left genuinely open by the book** (p. 288, folio 268): 27 Muharram 808/25 July 1405 from the *Khazinatuʾl-Asfiyaʾ*, rejected; "after 840/1436—37" from Rieu, preferred but conceded to make the life "rather long"; and the fixed point "still alive in 1415". **All three must travel together if any is used.**
- **Raji Hamid Shah's death date is stated to be unknown** — "The date of his death is not authoritatively known" (p. 285, folio 265) — as is Shaikh Mina's (p. 293, folio 273) and Shaikh Abuʾl-Fath ʿAlaʾi Qureshi's (p. 294, folio 274). **These are the book's own blanks, not mine.**
- **The chapter's title promises three orders and this chunk delivers one.** Chapter Four is "The Chishtiyya, Suhrawardiyya and Kubrawiyya Centres" in every recto header (pp. 281–295 odd, folios 261–275 odd), yet **pp. 281–296 contain only Chishti centres; the Suhrawardiyya appears solely as a second initiation (pp. 292, 293, 295; folios 272, 273, 275) and the Kubrawiyya is never named.** This is a book-versus-brief observation, not a disagreement: the brief's page range for the chapter (PDF 261–322) allows the other two orders to fall after p. 296, and I have not read past my chunk to check.
- **The chunk ends mid-argument.** "The religious tone of the court alternated between the authoritarianism of the 'ulama' and" (p. 296, folio 276) is the last body text before the plate leaf; **the sentence completes in chunk 018.**
