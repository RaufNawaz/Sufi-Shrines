# rizvi_history_of_sufism_india_1 — chunk 018 — pp. 299–314

**Folios, taken only from `folio_map.tsv`, which for this whole range gives `source = header` with no `unknown`:** p. 299→277, 300→278, 301→279, 302→280, 303→281, 304→282, 305→283, 306→284, 307→285, 308→286, 309→287, 310→288, 311→289, 312→290, 313→291, 314→292. **The chunk's own printed running headers agree with the table at both ends** — p. 299 prints the recto header `The Chishtiyya, Suhrawardiyya and Kubrawiyya Centres 277` and p. 314 prints the verso header `292 A History of Sufism in India` — so no folio in this chunk is in doubt and none was computed. Every recto in the chunk carries the same chapter header, which confirms the brief: **pp. 299–314 lie wholly inside Chapter Four, "The Chishtiyya, Suhrawardiyya and Kubrawiyya Centres."**

**The brief expected Chapter Four to be the densest chapter for archive rows; this chunk only partly bears that out, and the reason is geographic.** Chapter Four is organised by centre, and the centres in these sixteen pages run **Uch (pp. 299–304, folios 277–282) → Gujarat (pp. 304–306, folios 282–284) → "Other Suhrawardi centres": Kalpi, Kanauj, Rajgir, Delhi (pp. 306–311, folios 284–289) → Kashmir (pp. 311–314, folios 289–292)**. Only the Uch opening is in the archive's territory. **From p. 304 (folio 282) to the end of the chunk not one archive row is touched**, because Gujarat, Awadh, Delhi and the Kashmir valley hold no rows in `shrine_index.tsv`. **All four mappings below come from pp. 299–304 (folios 277–282), and three of the four come from p. 299 (folio 277) alone.** That page is, however, unusually good: it gives the Makhdum-i Jahaniyan row its birth date, its Uch seat and its Suhrawardi affiliation, and gives the Surkh-Posh row a three-generation genealogy.

**This chunk also does the thing the queue asked earlier workers to watch for and could not get: it supplies a printed death date and a printed burial place for a row's principal figure** — "Saiyid Jalalu'd-Din Bukhari died on 10 Zu'lhijja 785/3 February 1384 and was buried in Uch" (p. 303, folio 281) — and that date agrees exactly with `shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht`'s `figure_died` of 1384. It is the first archive date in this book so far that the book confirms rather than contradicts.

## Shrines and figures in the archive

However, Saiyid Jalalu'd-Din did not remain there long" (p. 299, folio 277). **Named institution, named place, and an endowment in villages — the clearest shrine-economy sentence in the chunk.** On Sehwan and `lal-shahbaz-qalandar` see Doubts.
- **shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht**: **the khanqah run by a deputy in his absence** — "It seems likely that for most of the reign of Sultan Muhammad bin Tughlugq [OCR?] [Tughluq], the Saiyid lived abroad and his Ahangah [OCR?] [khanqah] was administered by his brother" (pp. 299–300, folios 277–278).
- **shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht**: **the source of the "Jahangasht" title** — "So well-travelled did he become that Saiyid Jalalu'd-Din was called the Jahangasht (World Traveller)" (p. 300, folio 278). **This is the second half of the archive row's own name, printed and glossed by the book.**

His travels mentioned in the Safar Nama-i Makhdum Jahaniyan (Book of the Travels of Makhdum Jahaniyan) are mostly legendary and all of a later date" (p. 300, folio 278). - **shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht**: **his seat, and his visiting pattern to the capital** — "During the time that Sultan Firuz Shah Tughluq ruled in Delhi the Saiyid remained mainly at Uch, only visiting the capital periodically. - **shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht**: **a book of his sayings, made during that Delhi visit, with its compiler named** — one of the disciples, "Abu 'Abdu'llah 'Ala'ud-Din 'Ali bin Sa'd Bin Ashraf, collected many of the Master's sayings, at the same time noting the background against which they were made. They were arranged in book form and the final product was called the Khulasatu'l-Alfaz Jami'u'l-'Ulum" (p. 300, folio 278). Its doctrine: "beginners on the mystic path should not concern themselves with trying to understand the philosophy of the Wahdat al-Wujud as it was for experts only" (p. 300, folio 278).
- **shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht**: **royal patronage, stated as unlimited influence** — "the Sultan gave Saiyid Jalalu'd-Din Bukhari a royal reception and continued to visit him in his khangah [khanqah] throughout his stay. So great was the Saiyid's hold with the Sultan that all his recommendations were granted! His assistance was also sought by the governors of Sind" (p. 301, folio 279). **The exclamation mark is as printed and may be OCR damage to a footnote marker — see Doubts.**
- **shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht**: **a peace treaty brokered by him at Thatta** — "In 763/1362 accompanied by a large army Sultan Firuz marched on Thatta… The Sammah ruler, Jam Jauna bin Babinya (756/1355—770/1369), sought to arrange peace-terms through Saiyid Jalalu'd-Din. It was finally agreed that Jam's son, Mani, should rule as a tributary of Sultan Firuz of Delhi. The peace was generally hailed as a triumph for Makhdum Jahaniyan" (p. 301, folio 279).
- **shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht**: **a tax remission credited to him** — "The abolition of about twenty-eight taxes by Sultan Firuz Shah largely imposed on towns throughout the empire may be ascribed to the influence of the Saiyid" (p. 301, folio 279). His ruling on royal stipends: "according to the books of Fatwas, it was makruh (disapproved) however it was not invalidated by the Shari'a" (p. 301, folio 279).
- **shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht**: **conversions ascribed to him** — "It is believed that the Sumirahs of the lower Sind who were Isma'ilis, adopted Sunnism under the influence of the Saiyid. A Jat chief of Sind, later named 'Abdu'llah, is also said to have embraced Islam through a supernatural feat performed by Makhdum Jahaniyan" (p. 301, folio 279).
- **shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht**: **a death-bed scene at Uch, with a named Hindu official** — "The Hindu, Nawahun, visited Makhdum Jahaniyan on his death-bed and while praying for his recovery stated that the Saiyid was the seal of the saints, just as the Prophet Muhammad was the seal of the prophets" (p. 301, folio 279); the Saiyid "concluded that Nawahun's statement amounted to a protestation of faith" (p. 302, folio 280). Nawahun is identified as "the darogha of Uch", footnoted "A revenue official entrusted to keep accounts in Persian" (p. 301, folio 279).
- **shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht**: **his ruling against the Shab-i Barat grave customs — the single most directly shrine-relevant passage in the chunk, and it is a saint condemning shrine practice.** "A notorious puritan, Makhdum Jahaniyan strongly opposed the tradition which had developed around the Shab-i Barat festival which was held in the evening of the middle of Sha'ban, the eighth month in the Muslim Calendar, when fire works were exploded, graves were white-washed and lighted lamps were placed on them" (p. 302, folio 280). **Graves, lamps and whitewash, named as the objects of a saint's disapproval.**
- **shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht**: **his other puritan rulings** — Muslims "should pray and give the money, which otherwise would be used on crackers and lamps, to the poor"; "Only in India, he added, was there such a blatant disregard of the holy law, for such innovations were not to be found in Ghazna, Khurasan or Arabia"; "He also sternly disapproved of giving Allah such Hindi names as Thakur (Lord), Dhani (Rich) and Kartar (Creator)"; "He advised the aima [OCR?] [aʾimma] not to accept any payment which came from illegal sources of revenue" (p. 302, folio 280).
- **shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht**: **his rule on sufis and rulers, and the visit direction that matters** — "Makhdum Jahaniyan urged that dervishes, sufis and the 'ulama' visit rulers, government officials and the rich in order to elicit assistance for the deprived and justified his own visits to Sultan Firuz on these grounds. Dervishes, however, should never allow such people to visit them at their Ahangahs [khanqahs]; if this did occur, the time should be beneficially used by preaching on the significance of the Shari'a" (p. 302, folio 280). **This is directly contradicted on the previous page — see Doubts.**
- **shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht**: **he called his disciples "Akhi", and Rizvi derives the practice from Anatolia** — "The Saiyid preferred to call his disciples Akhi (brothers)"; "Saiyid Jalalu'd-Din in fact borrowed the ideal both from the Akhi system, contained in the Anatolian craft guilds, and the Akhi and Futuwwa organizations among dervish brotherhoods in Khurasan and Transoxiana" (p. 303, folio 281).
- **shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht**: **succession, and the line that carried the order on** — "His successor was his brother, Sadru'd-Din, who achieved fame under his nicknames, Raju, and Qattal (Slayer), for his militant evangelism" (p. 303, folio 281); "The Suhrawardi si/sila [OCR?] [silsila] spread further through both the sons and grandsons of Makhdum Jahaniyan and Saiyid Raju Qattal" (pp. 303–304, folios 281–282). His son "Saiyid Mahmud Nasiru'd-Din… too lived in Uch and attracted disciples from Multan and neighbouring areas. He had a very large family and is believed to have died in 847/1443-44" (p. 304, folio 282).
- **shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht**: **his khirqa as an instrument of deputisation, given to a named man at a named place** — "A more famous Suhrawardiyya sufi in the region was Shaikh Rajgiri, to whom Makhdum Jahaniyan gave the title 'Akhi.' A native of Daryabad in Awadh, Akhi was given Makhdum Jahaniyan's khirga [OCR?] [khirqa] when he first settled at Kanauj" (p. 306, folio 284). Also "Kalpi became such a centre due to the prestige of Shaikh Siraj Sukhta, a favourite Imam of Makhdum Jahaniyan" (p. 306, folio 284).
- **shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht**: **his khanqah at Uch is still a place of call a generation later, and the visit is recorded as a snub** — "Mir Saiyid 'Ali on his way to Kashmir passed through Uch and visited the khangah [khanqah] of the Makhdum Jahaniyan. When Saiyid Makhdum Jahaniyan was informed of the visit of the Hamadani Saiyid, without taking much notice he stated merely that Hamadan (All Knowing) was none but the Great Knower of the Invisible World, that is, God the Most High" (p. 313, folio 291). **The one time the book describes the Uch khanqah as a destination rather than a residence.**
- **shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht**: **his reach into Kashmir through disciples** — "the Suhrawardiyya order was revived in Kashmir by Saiyid Muhammad Isfahani, a disciple of Makhdum Jahaniyan", and "Saiyid Jamalu'd-Din, a sufi of the Makhdum Jahaniyan's order, made a spectacular impact on the sufi scene in Kashmir" (p. 312, folio 290).

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

Dates below are exactly as the book prints them, AH/CE pairs and dashes included.

Mahmud Nasiru'd-Din "had been a precocious and gifted child", "lived in Uch", "had a very large family and is believed to have died in 847/1443-44"; Hamid Kabir "was known for his kindly disposition"; "Saiyid Ruknu'd-Din Abu'l-Fath, the son of Saiyid Hamid Kabir, was an equally great sufi" (p. 304, folio 282). **On the last of these see Doubts — his name collides with an archive row's principal figure.**
- **Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din Chiragh-i Dihli** — the Chishti who initiated Makhdum-i Jahaniyan at Delhi; his fame "had apparently been spread by Indian pilgrims" as far as Mecca (p. 299, folio 277). The footnote dates his death: Firishta says the initiation was "in 772 1370-71, but as the Shaikh died in 1356, this must be incorrect" (p. 299, folio 277, note 2).
- **Shaikh ʿAbduʾllah al-Yafeʿi al-Yamani** — "the most respected saint in Islam's holiest city", at Mecca, who "aroused Saiyid Jalalu'd-Din's interest in Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din Chiragh-i Dihli" (p. 299, folio 277).
- **Burhanuʾd-Din, called Qutb-i ʿAlam** — grandson of Makhdum-i Jahaniyan and the founder of the Gujarat line; "Oneof the sonsof Muhammad,a son of Makhdum Jahaniyan [OCR?] [One of the sons of Muhammad, a son of Makhdum Jahaniyan], was Burhanu'd-Din. After he became famous in Gujarat he was known as the Qutb-i 'Alam (the Pole of the Universe). Born either in Uch or Multan in 790/1388—89 he was initiated by his father and, at his suggestion migrated to Gujarat during the reign of Sultan Ahmad (1411-42). He settled near the new capital, Ahmadabad" (p. 304, folio 282); "The Shaikh died on 8 Zu'lhijja 857/10 December 1453 and was buried in Batwa near Ahmdabad [OCR?] [Ahmadabad]" (p. 305, folio 283). **A named tomb site outside the archive's territory.**
- **Saiyid Muhammad, called Shah-i ʿAlam and Shah Manjhan** — son of Qutb-i ʿAlam; "born on 9 Zu'lqada 817/20 January 1415 and was trained by Shaikh Ahmad-i Khattu as well as by his father"; "He died on 20 Jumada II 880/21 October 1475" (p. 305, folio 283). **His weekly access regime is under "Practices".**
- **Malik Dawaruʾl-Mulk** — disciple of Shah-i ʿAlam and "A prominent and pious official of Sultan Mahmud I Begarh's (1459-1511) of Gujarat"; "he fell fighting on 13 Zu'lqa'da 915/22 February 1510. Long remembered by the people of Gujarat and the Deccan after his death, they continued to seek Dawaru'l-Mulk's blessings" (p. 305, folio 283). **A layman whose posthumous blessing is sought — a shrine-type cult without a shrine named.**
- **Shaikh ʿAli Khatib, Shaikh Sirajuʾd-Din, Malik Muhammad, Aminuʾl-Mulk** — the Gujarat circle around Sultan Mahmud Begarh (pp. 305–306, folios 283–284). Malik Muhammad "and his wife moved to Shaikh Siraju'd-Din's hermitage. The Shaikh considered him the Ibrahim bin Adham of his age"; he drew "the Shaikh's water from the River Sabarmati, unflinchingly walking through the bazaar with the pot on his head" (p. 306, folio 284).
- **Saiyid Muhammad of Jaunpur** — "In 1496 Saiyid Muhammad of Jaunpur (1443-1505) reached Gujarat and a year later at Ahmadabad declared himself the Mahdi. A large number of 'ulama' and sufis accepted the Saiyid Mahdi. Although he was banished from Gujarat in 1500, such was the atmosphere generated by Saiyid Muhammad that many people withdrew from the world to a life of asceticism" (p. 306, folio 284).
- **Shaikh Rajgiri, "Akhi"** — "A native of Daryabad in Awadh"; settled at Kanauj, then "Finding his constant stream of visitors irksome, he moved to a lonely village on the Ganges called Rajgir" (p. 306, folio 284). **His Holi trance is under "Legends".**

## Practices, institutions, economy

**The endowment and revenue material in this chunk is the strongest so far in this book, and it is concentrated in three places: the Makhdum-i Jahaniyan tax-and-stipend passages (pp. 301–302, folios 279–280), the Kashmir khanqah-and-langar grants (pp. 312–314, folios 290–292), and Shaikh Gadaʾi's office as controller of religious grants (pp. 310–311, folios 288–289).** By contrast **there is no ʿurs, no mela, no offering at a tomb, no sajjada nashin, no Auqaf and no visitor number anywhere in pp. 299–314**, and the only tomb practice described is one a saint condemns.

- **A khanqah endowed with villages, by a Sultan, to a named saint.** "Sultan Muhammad bin Tughluq appointed him the Shaikhu'l-Islam and made him the head of a khangah [khanqah] in Siwistan (Sehwan) known as the Khanqah-i Muhammadi to which several villages were attached" (p. 299, folio 277). **A named khanqah, a named place, land in villages, and a state office in the same sentence.**
- **A khanqah administered by a deputy during the Shaikh's absence.** "his Ahangah [khanqah] was administered by his brother" (pp. 299–300, folios 277–278). **The chunk's only statement about khanqah administration in a saint's absence, and the deputy is a brother, not a khalifa.**
- **Grave decoration as a festival practice, named and dated in the calendar — the chunk's one piece of tomb ritual.** At Shab-i Barat, "held in the evening of the middle of Sha'ban, the eighth month in the Muslim Calendar", "fire works were exploded, graves were white-washed and lighted lamps were placed on them" (p. 302, folio 280). **Recorded as what a saint opposed, not as what his own followers did.**
- **Revenue terms defined or used: kharaj, jizya, madad-i maʿash, iqtaʿ, darogha, aʾimma.** "A letter from 'Ainu'l Mulk Mahru to Saiyid Jalalu'd-Din complained that the Saiyids were avoiding paying land revenue tax (kKharaj [OCR?] [kharaj]) and asked him to impress on them'their obligations to the state" (p. 301, folio 279). **A named category of religious grantee dodging land tax, and a governor asking a saint to enforce the state's claim.** "the taxes remitted by the Sultan were not the main source of income which came from kharaj and jizya" (p. 301, folio 279). The *aʾimma* are footnoted as "Those who had received madad-i ma'ash or stipends from land as their main source of income" (p. 302, folio 280). The *darogha* is "A revenue official entrusted to keep accounts in Persian" (p. 301, folio 279).
- **Twenty-eight taxes abolished, credited to a saint's influence, and the stated motive is to launder royal income for religious men.** "The abolition of about twenty-eight taxes by Sultan Firuz Shah largely imposed on towns throughout the empire may be ascribed to the influence of the Saiyid. The lifting of these taxes was designed to make the sources of income of the Sultan legally acceptable to the 'ulama'" (p. 301, folio 279), and the Sultan thereby "making his stipends holy for the 'ulama' and sufis" (p. 301, folio 279).
- **The legal status of a royal stipend, ruled on by a saint.** Asked "whether or not it was permissible to accept stipends offered by the Sultans; his answer was that according to the books of Fatwas, it was makruh (disapproved) however it was not invalidated by the Shari'a" (p. 301, folio 279). **The single most citable sentence in the chunk on shrine finance and state money.**
- **A saint's rule on the source of a grantee's income.** "He advised the aima [aʾimma] not to accept any payment which came from illegal sources of revenue" (p. 302, folio 280).
- **The direction of visiting between sufis and rulers, prescribed.** Dervishes, sufis and ʿulamaʾ should "visit rulers, government officials and the rich in order to elicit assistance for the deprived"; but "Dervishes, however, should never allow such people to visit them at their Ahangahs [khanqahs]" (p. 302, folio 280). **Contradicted on the facing page — see Doubts.**
- **Renunciation defined against inheritance.** "the Saiyid asserted it was fraudulent to adopt asceticism after bequeathing property to a son for such a way of life involved the total distribution of all possession to the poor" (p. 300, folio 278). **A rule directly against hereditary sufi property, from a saint whose own line became hereditary in Uch and Gujarat (pp. 303–305, folios 281–283).**
- **Gifts to qalandars, reported from a hagiography the author distrusts.** "The Mahbubiya states that the Saiyid gave huge gifts to qalandars" (p. 300, folio 278). **The word is printed correctly as "qalandars" here, not as the "galandar" corruption the run was warned about.**
- **The Akhi/Futuwwa hospice economy, quoted at length from Ibn Battuta — the fullest description of a sufi institution's finances in the chunk.** "The Akhi builds a hospice and furnishes it with rugs, lamps, and what other equipment it required. His associates work during the day to gain their livelihood, and after the afternoon prayer they bring him their collective earnings; with this they buy fruit, food and the other things needed for consumption in the hospice. If, during that day. a traveller alights at the town, they give him lodging with them; what they have purchased serves for their hospitality to him and he remains with them until his departure. If no newcomer arrives, they assemble themselves to partake of the food, and after eating they sing and dance" (p. 303, folio 281, note 2). "The members are called fityan. and their leader, as we have said, is the Akhi." Rizvi adds that the Akhis "were also warriors of the faith" and that "the Akhi system never really penetrated into India" (p. 303, folio 281).
- **A weekly access regime at a living saint's khanqah, with a named day.** Of Shah-i ʿAlam: "Six days of the week were spent by the Shah in prayer and meditation and during this period he was inaccessible even to the ruler. On Fridays everyone was welcomed including the common people, and part of the day was spent in teaching and giving sermons. After Friday afternoon prayers, Shah-i 'Alam would retire to his cell for the rest of the week" (p. 305, folio 283). **A named building element — his cell — and a named open day.**
- **Samaʿ and qawwals at a khanqah, with the influence named.** "Under Chishti influence he became a lover of sama' and gawwals [OCR?] [qawwals] thronged to his khangah [khanqah]" (p. 305, folio 283). **The chunk's only qawwals; the word is OCR-damaged exactly as the run's trap list predicted.** Separately, Shaikh Gadaʾi's "sama' assemblies were attended by both the Emperor and the Prime Minister" (p. 310, folio 288).
- **A pious administrator's tax policy, and cultivator migration as its measure.** Malik Dawaruʾl-Mulk "would accept only those taxes under his igta' [OCR?] [iqtaʿ] which were permissible under holy law. Cultivators loved to migrate to his igta' [iqtaʿ] and even the most desolate area in his care tended to prosper under his humane administration" (p. 305, folio 283).
- **Personal service to a Shaikh as a form of devotion, two named acts.** Dawaruʾl-Mulk "chose to perform the service of pouring water on the hands of Shah-i 'Alam at the time of his wuzu (ablution)" (p. 305, folio 283); Malik Muhammad "chose the lowly service of drawing the Shaikh's water from the River Sabarmati, unflinchingly walking through the bazaar with the pot on his head. His humility drew crowds which tended to annoy his pir" (p. 306, folio 284). **Crowds gathering around a devotee, and the pir's irritation at them.**
- **The khirqa as the instrument of transmission, twice, once exchanged between equals.** Makhdum Jahaniyan "gave the title 'Akhi'" to Shaikh Rajgiri and "Akhi was given Makhdum Jahaniyan's khirga [khirqa] when he first settled at Kanauj" (p. 306, folio 284); and of Shah-i ʿAlam and Malik Muhammad, "they exchanged khirgas [khirqas]" (p. 306, folio 284). **An exchange, not a grant — the chunk's only reciprocal khirqa.**
- **The founding grant of the first Kashmir khanqah, with a langar, from a convert king.** "Rinchana, who took Sadru'd-Din as his Muslim name, gave grants to Bulbul Shah for the establishment of a khangah [khanqah] and a /angar [OCR?] [langar]" (p. 312, folio 290). **A royal grant naming both institutions — the clearest langar endowment in the chunk.**
- **A second Kashmir khanqah-and-langar, sited and measured.** Saiyid Tajuʾd-Din "was given facilities to build a khangah [khanqah] and a /angar [langar] at Shihabu'd-Din Pura, some nine miles north-west of Srinagar" (p. 313, folio 291). **A distance and a direction from a city — a locatable site.**
- **A khanqah built on a demolished temple.** "Kali Mandir was demolished and subsequently the Saiyid's khangah [khanqah] was built there" (p. 314, folio 292).
- **A count of sufi centres founded across a province.** Saiyid ʿAli Hamadani left "approximately twenty eminent Irani sufis who had accompanied him from Iran in different parts of the valley where they established khanqahs and /angars [langars]. Some of the Saiyid's Kashmiri disciples also established independent sufi centres" (p. 314, folio 292). **A number — about twenty — for a wave of khanqah foundation.**
- **A state office controlling all religious land and stipends, and the revocation of grants as a political act.** Bairam Khan repaid Shaikh Gadaʾi "by elevating him to the post of Sadru's-Sudur, the controller of land and stipends granted ior [OCR?] [for] religious purposes, which was accompanied by unprecedented powers. Apparently the Shaikh revoked the grants previously given to a number of the 'ulama' and to sufis who had supported the Afghans" (p. 310, folio 288). **Grants to sufis cancelled for the previous dynasty's politics — the chunk's one instance of an endowment being taken away.** In disgrace he "returned to Delhi and was content to be supported by a minor madad-i ma'ash" (p. 311, folio 289).
- **Named burials, with their places.** Makhdum-i Jahaniyan "was buried in Uch" (p. 303, folio 281); Raju Qattal died at Uch (p. 303, folio 281); Qutb-i ʿAlam "was buried in Batwa near Ahmdabad [Ahmadabad]" (p. 305, folio 283); Shaikh Samaʿuʾd-Din "was buried on the embankment of the Hauz-i Shamsi in Delhi" (p. 308, folio 286); Saiyid ʿAli Hamadani's "body was taken to Khuttalan, now part of the U.S.S.R., where it was buried" (p. 314, folio 292). **Five burials named; only the first is in the archive's territory, and none is given a tomb building, a builder or a date of construction** — (not stated).
- **A relic market, in a footnote, with prices.** "The Sultan bought four hoofs believed to have belonged to the ass of Christ. For each he paid fifty thousand tankas. Later someone else brought him another onc [OCR?] [one] claiming that it also was a genuine hoof from the ass of Christ. The Sultan bought that too" (p. 299, folio 277, note 1). **A priced relic transaction — fifty thousand tankas each — told as a joke about credulity.**
- **Objects becoming relics through a miracle.** Of Qutb-i ʿAlam's tank object: "Miraculously the object turned into a mixture of each of the three materials, and later became celebrated relics" (pp. 304–305, folios 282–283). **The book does not say where the relics were kept** — (not stated).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

The attributions below are the book's own, including its hedges ("is said to have", "is alleged", "reportedly", "are mostly legendary", "are mythical", "merit no attention").

- **The Mahbubiya's travel miracles, reported and disowned in the same paragraph.** The stories "should not be accepted literally"; the Saiyid "even performed the rare and hazardous journey to China and Mongolia, at the same time converting the local inhabitants en route"; "stock-in-trade anecdotes of Saiyid Jalalu'd-Din Bukhari's encounter with yogis, all whom he ultimately converted to Islam"; "the Saiyid travelled through Ceylon where, according to the author, Islam had failed to penetrate. There again he was reported to have converted a yogi" (p. 300, folio 278).
- **A conversion by supernatural feat.** "A Jat chief of Sind, later named 'Abdu'llah, is also said to have embraced Islam through a supernatural feat performed by Makhdum Jahaniyan" (p. 301, folio 279).
- **A death curse that works instantly.** Saiyid Raju "retorted that the Shaikh was dishonest and would die for it. Instantly Shaikh Muhammad was seized with a violent stomach-ache from which he died. Nawahun was handed over to Saiyid Raju who again refused to embrace Islam and was executed" (p. 302, folio 280). **A killing that follows directly from a saint's word, told without comment.**
- **A dying saint declared the seal of the saints by a Hindu.** Nawahun, "while praying for his recovery stated that the Saiyid was the seal of the saints, just as the Prophet Muhammad was the seal of the prophets" (p. 301, folio 279).
- **The relic miracle in the tank.** "One day while bathing in a tank his feet knocked some unknown object. Not recognizing what it was, Qutb-i 'Alam remarked he didn't know whether the object was stone, iron or wood. Miraculously the object turned into a mixture of each of the three materials, and later became celebrated relics" (pp. 304–305, folios 282–283).
- **A generosity story the book says is told of many saints.** Of Saiyid Hamid Kabir: "The following story ascribed to many sufis has also been told of Saiyid Hamid. Some robbers entered his house which was always unlocked. Voluntarily he gave to them all his possessions, but forgot a bag containing ten thousand dinars. He made amends, however, by chasing the robbers and handing over the rest of the money" (p. 304, folio 282).
- **Three generations of Uch saints in contests with yogis.** "all the stories dealing with three generations of these 'sufis in Uch recount conflicts with Hindu Yogis. Although the ultimate success in the performance of supernatural feats is ascribed to the Suhrawardis, that of the yogis is not to be underestimated. Habitually, the stories end with the Islamization of the vanquished in various types of competitions" (p. 304, folio 282).
- **A Holi trance lasting three days and nights.** "on the day of the Hindu festival of Holi a group of Hindu boys passed his house singing and dancing. Their music so moved him that he fell into a trance, and followed the revellers around the streets of Kanauj for three days and nights. So infectious was the group that many of the townsfolk joined them" (pp. 306–307, folios 284–285). **A Suhrawardi saint in ecstasy at a Hindu festival, and townspeople following.**
- **A coronation blessing obtained by a ruse.** Prince Nizam "visited Shaikh Sama'u'd-Din and requested lessons in Arabic grammar. Beginning with prayers to God and Muhammad, the Shaikh repeated a sentence wishing Nizam success in both worlds. After the Prince asked the Shaikh to repeat the sentence three times he knelt and kissed the ground. Although the story may appear fanciful to modern readers, medieval historians ascribed its possible authenticity to the Sultan's wisdom in attaining a blessing through indirect means" (p. 307, folio 285).
- **A tomb that splits to admit a second body — a literary, not a hagiographic, miracle.** In Jamali's *Mihr wa Mah*: "The young Prince dies of grief and Mihr, distraught by the separation from her lover, shares the same fate. The Prince's tomb, already sealed, breaks in half and the two are buried side by side" (pp. 309–310, folios 287–288).
- **Fairy-tale conversion legends around Bulbul Shah, dismissed.** "later sufi hagiologies relate fairy-tale type legends of Rinchana's conversion" (p. 312, folio 290), with the footnote "The story of his conversion as outlined by Kashmiri historians is a fable and Abu'l-Fazl's account is more historical" (p. 312, folio 290, note 3).
- **The conversion of a temple priest, dismissed by name.** "The miracles alleged to have been performed in order to convert the Brahman priest of Kali Mandir in Srinagar are reminiscent of those attributed to Khwaja Mu'inu'd-Din Chishti and to a large number of other sufis. Yogis at the court of Sultan Muhammad bin Tughlugq [Tughluq] had been seen by Ibn Battuta to fly through the air. Such stock-in-trade legends merit no attention" (p. 314, folio 292).
- **The legend of three visits to Kashmir, and Rizvi's explanation of how it grew.** "The stories that the Saiyid visited Kashmir three times in 774/1372, 781-1399 [OCR?] [781/1379?] and 785/1383, and that Timur expelled him from Hamadan in 1383, mentioned in many medieval hagiologies and modern works, are mythical" (p. 313, folio 291); "From the Saiyid's alleged statement that he had travelled three times the rub 'i-maskun (an inhabited quarter of the world), and associated with 1,400 prominent sufis, grew the legend of his three visits to Kashmir" (pp. 313–314, folios 291–292). **On the corrupt middle date see Doubts.**
- **A number deflated.** "the Saiyid was accompanied by a considerable number of sufis although they would not have numbered 700 as is popularly believed" (p. 314, folio 292).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The chapter's own thesis about where the Suhrawardiyya went, stated at the head of the Uch section.** "We turn now to the Suhrawardiyya silsila to give an account of its development, which occurred mainly in northern India. The order spread from Uch to Gujarat, the Panjab and Kashmir even extending its influence to the Delhi of Sultan Firuz Shah Tughlug [Tughluq]. As discussed in the previous chapter, after the death of Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din the order declined in Multan only to be rejuvenated in Uch" (p. 299, folio 277). **A stated transfer of the order's centre from Multan to Uch, tied to one man's death.**
- **A source corrected on chronology by the author.** "According to Firishta, the Saivid [Saiyid] was initiated by Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din into the Chishtiyya order in 772 1370-71, but as the Shaikh died in 1356, this must be incorrect" (p. 299, folio 277, note 2). **Rizvi dates Nasiruʾd-Din Chiragh-i Dihli's death to 1356 in the course of rejecting Firishta.**
- **A hagiography read selectively — rejected on facts, accepted on doctrine.** The *Mahbubiya* "should not be accepted literally" (p. 300, folio 278), yet "The Mahbubiyya, however, gives a legitimate insight into the teachings of the Saiyid" (p. 300, folio 278). **The method matters for any entry built on this book: Rizvi takes teachings from a text whose narratives he discards.**
- **An Anatolian origin claimed for an Indian saint's practice, and then qualified out of existence.** "The justification was a technicality and Saiyid Jalalu'd-Din in fact borrowed the ideal both from the Akhi system, contained in the Anatolian craft guilds, and the Akhi and Futuwwa organizations among dervish brotherhoods in Khurasan and Transoxiana… However, the Akhi system never really penetrated into India. It is nevertheless quite reasonable to attribute Saiyid Jalalu'd-Din Bukhari's puritanism and religious militancy to inspiration from the Akhi and Futuwwa organizations" (p. 303, folio 281). **No evidence is offered for the borrowing beyond the shared word "Akhi".**
- **Hagiography read as a later generation's self-image — Rizvi's sharpest methodological remark in the chunk.** Of the Uch yogi-contest stories: "They also give some indication of the type of image Suhrawardi saints later sought to present of their spiritual ancestors" (p. 304, folio 282).
- **Conversions to sufism explained by millennial anxiety.** "during the early sixteenth century the conversion of a number of important officials seems to have been prompted by the approaching millennium of Islam which was completed in 1591-92. This aroused considerable uncertainty and religious and social tensions in all parts of the Islamic world" (p. 306, folio 284). **The stated cause precedes its effect by almost a century, and the book does not address that.**
- **Anecdotes about a poet declared apocryphal.** "The anecdotes relating Jamali's witty replies to Jami's questions, given in later biographical dictionaries of Persian poets, are apocryphal and intended to provide anecdotal background to some of Jamali's verses. Bada'uni's remarks that Jamali had sought corrections of his verses from Jami are also not authentic" (p. 309, folio 287) — while the fullest such anecdote is reproduced in the footnote anyway (p. 309, folio 287, note 2).
- **A scholarly consensus rejected on grounds of what nobody checked.** "To nineteenth and twentieth century scholars from H. Blochman and A.L. Sriwastava to 'Aziz Ahmad, Shaikh Gada'i has been depicted as a Shi'i, for it seemed to them natural that Bairam Khan, who was believed to be a Shi'i, would never have elevated a Sunni to such a status. None of these writers have considered the unlikelihood of a son of Shaikh Jamali becoming a Shi'i or, if such was the case, to try to establish when and how the change occurred" (p. 311, folio 289).
- **Why the Suhrawardiyya succeeded in Kashmir, and what held it back.** "Although the Suhrawardis were successful in Delhi and Gujarat as well as in other provincial kingdoms, their impact in Kashmir was striking. The main reason was the support they acquired from the migrant Kubrawiyyas, although their mutual rivalries in turn retarded progress" (p. 311, folio 289). **Cooperation and rivalry between two orders given as the same explanation.**
- **The long prehistory of Muslim Kashmir, given to argue that conversion was not a conquest.** Contacts "commenced there in the eighth century AD"; the 713 invasion aborted; "A further Arab invasion in 757-58 also failed… but to the Muslims Kashmir continued to have the image of a place of refuge"; Mahmud of Ghazna's attempts "Between 1015 and 1021" failed, though "His army plundered the southern part of the valley and forcibly converted some inhabitants to Islam"; "Muslim expatriate soldiers-of-fortune and merchants formed a core of Muslim settlers in Kashmir" (p. 311, folio 289).
- **Conversion explained politically, by a Buddhist king who could not control Brahmans.** "Failing to assert his authority over the Brahmans, at the suggestion of Shah Mir, Rinchana embraced Islam"; "The rapid Islamization of the Mongols in Central Asia and Iran was a further incentive to Rinchana to become a Muslim" (p. 312, folio 290).
- **The chunk's central comparative claim, and it is about temple destruction.** "Unlike the Chishtis, Mir Saiyid 'Ali Hamadani was imbued with the searing missionary zeal of 'Ala'u'd-Dawla Simnani. This enthusiasm and that of his followers took the form of temple demolition and the enforced conversion of many Kashmiris" (p. 314, folio 292). **Rizvi sets two orders against each other on exactly the question the archive's Hindu and Jain rows turn on.**
- **A Muslim ruler's Hindu practice defended against the charge of syncretism.** "Although Sultan Qutbu'd-Din smoothed their way in Kashmir, he himself was not a bigot. He visited Hindu shrines, dressed like a Hindu and celebrated Hindu festivals; on one occasion he even performed a yajna in order to avert a famine. This does not imply that Qutbu'd-Din was Hinduised or that there were no members of the 'ulama' in Kashmir to keep him on the orthodox path. Rather he followed the traditions of his predecessor, Shah Mir, in maintaining friendly relations with Hindu subjects and the Brahman élite who dominated the administration" (p. 314, folio 292).
- **A legend explained by a misreading of a saint's own boast.** "The authenticity of this statement is doubtful as the fact remains that it fails to indicate how the Saiyid visited every region of the world on three occasions; it merely suggests that he made three different journeys to populated areas, not necessarily each time to the same place" (p. 314, folio 292).

**Archive-mapping doubts, in order of what most needs a human:**

- **`shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam` is my weakest mapping, and this chunk both strengthens and endangers it.** The brief said Multan, a date or the title would settle it. **This chunk gives Multan** — "after the death of Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din the order declined in Multan" (p. 299, folio 277) — and gives him as the Suhrawardi pir who initiated a man born in 707/1308 (p. 299, folio 277), which is chronologically compatible with the row's 1335. **But the book still prints no date for him, never writes "Rukn-e-Alam", never writes "Abul Fath" of him, and never calls him Bahaʾuʾd-Din Zakariyya's grandson in this chunk.** Worse, **the same chunk contains a second, different man of almost exactly the row's full name: "Saiyid Ruknu'd-Din Abu'l-Fath, the son of Saiyid Hamid Kabir" (p. 304, folio 282), of Uch, four generations after Makhdum-i Jahaniyan.** The archive row reads "Shah Rukn-e-Alam (Rukn-ud-Din Abul Fath)". **A consolidator reading p. 304 alone would map the wrong man to this row.** I have mapped only the p. 299 (folio 277) figure and I flag the p. 304 (folio 282) namesake explicitly. If a reviewer wants the safe course, demote the `shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam` mapping to Doubts and keep the p. 304 warning.
- **`shrine-of-jalaluddin-surkh-posh-bukhari-jalaluddin-bukhari` versus `shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht` — the two rows collide on the row names themselves, not on the book.** The first row is titled "Shrine of Jalaluddin Surkh-Posh Bukhari (**Jalaluddin Bukhari**)", and **"Saiyid Jalalu'd-Din Bukhari" is precisely what the book calls the second row's man** (pp. 299, 301, 303; folios 277, 279, 281). I separate them on the book's own wording: **"Saiyid Jalalu'd-Din Surkh (Red)", the grandfather and a disciple of Bahaʾuʾd-Din Zakariyya, goes to the Surkh-Posh row; "Saiyid Jalalu'd-Din Bukhari, the Makhdum-i Jahaniyan… the Jahangasht", the grandson, born 707/1308 and buried in Uch in 785/1384, goes to the Jahaniyan row** (p. 299, folio 277; p. 303, folio 281). **A consolidator matching on the string "Jalaluddin Bukhari" alone will get this backwards.** Note that the run's known trap about "Shaikh Jalalu'd-Din Tabrizi" does not arise here: **the word "Tabrizi" does not occur anywhere in pp. 299–314**, and the men here are "Surkh" and "Bukhari", with a genealogy, a place and, for one of them, two dates.
- **`shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya` rests on six words and nothing else.** "a disciple of Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya" (p. 299, folio 277) is the whole of the evidence; the book gives him no Multan, no date, no tomb and no independent sentence in this chunk. Mapped under the ruled principal-figure convention, but **a consolidator must not read this bullet as shrine evidence — it is a genealogical clause.**
- **Sehwan is a bare toponym for archive purposes and I have withheld two ids.** p. 299 (folio 277) names "Siwistan (Sehwan)" **with** a khanqah and an endowment, so it is not bare in the ordinary sense — but the khanqah is Makhdum-i Jahaniyan's Khanqah-i Muhammadi, and **neither Lal Shahbaz Qalandar nor Lakhi Shah Saddar is named anywhere in the chunk**. Under interim convention (a) I therefore withhold `lal-shahbaz-qalandar` and `shrine-of-lakhi-shah-saddar`. **If the toponym ruling reverses, `lal-shahbaz-qalandar` is the row p. 299 (folio 277) would take.**
- **Lahore is a bare toponym and appears only as an imprint.** "published in Lahore in 1909" (p. 300, folio 278) and "Oriental College Magazine, Lahore X/1" (p. 310, folio 288). **These are publication data, not places in the narrative, so even a reversal of the toponym ruling should not reach `data-darbar` or any other Lahore row from this chunk.**
- **The deity question arises here, on `kali-bari-mandir`, `kalat-kali-temple` and `kalka-cave-temple-asthan-of-kalka-devi`, and I have not mapped it.** The book names "Kali Mandir in Srinagar", its Brahman priest, its demolition and the khanqah built on its site (p. 314, folio 292). **All three Kali rows in the archive are elsewhere — Peshawar, Kalat and Aror — and none is at Srinagar**, so even if a deity could be a principal figure the location would be wrong. **Flagged rather than mapped, per the brief's unruled-deity instruction.** The same page names "Hindu shrines" the Sultan visited, unnamed and unlocated (p. 314, folio 292).

The rows name Syed Shah Jamal Uddin Naqvi Bukhari of Lahore (d. 1671) and Hafiz Muhammad Jamal Multani (d. 1811, Chishti). **Three different men, three centuries, three cities. Considered and not mapped.**
- **"Saiyid Baha'u'd-Din", one of Jalalu'd-Din Surkh's three sons (p. 299, folio 277), is not `tomb-of-baha-al-halim-uch-sharif`.** The row names "Hazrat Baha'al-Halim"; the book prints "Baha'u'd-Din". Different names, and the book says nothing else about this son. **Considered and not mapped.**

- **`gorakhnath-goraknath-temple` and `tilla-jogian` get nothing despite four passages about yogis** (pp. 300, 304, 314; folios 278, 282, 292). **No Nath figure is named, no Gorakhnath, no site** — the yogis are anonymous and appear only as the losing side in conversion stories. **Considered and not mapped.**
- **`sharada-peeth` gets nothing despite four pages on Kashmir** (pp. 311–314, folios 289–292). **Sharada, Sharda and the Neelum valley are not named**; the Kashmir sites named are Srinagar, Shihabuʾd-Din Pura and Kali Mandir.
- **Hinglaj does not appear in this chunk**, so the archive defect on `Shaktipeeth Shri Hinglaj Mata Mandir` (empty id cell) does not bite here. **No id has been invented.**
- **`darbar-malik-ahmad-ayaz` is not reached by the mention of "Mahmud of Ghazna" (p. 311, folio 289)**: Ayaz is not named, and Mahmud is not that row's principal figure. **Considered and not mapped.**

**Internal contradictions in the chunk, kept side by side rather than resolved:**

- **The rule about rulers visiting sufis is broken by the saint who states it, one page earlier.** "Dervishes, however, should never allow such people to visit them at their Ahangahs [khanqahs]" (p. 302, folio 280) stands against "the Sultan gave Saiyid Jalalu'd-Din Bukhari a royal reception and **continued to visit him in his khangah [khanqah] throughout his stay**" (p. 301, folio 279). **The book prints both and does not note the tension.** The Saiyid's own qualification — that the time "should be beneficially used by preaching on the significance of the Shari'a" — is the nearest thing to a reconciliation, and it is his, not Rizvi's.
- **Renunciation of inherited property is ruled fraudulent by the founder of a hereditary line.** "it was fraudulent to adopt asceticism after bequeathing property to a son" (p. 300, folio 278) stands against the Uch succession passing brother-to-brother and then through "both the sons and grandsons of Makhdum Jahaniyan and Saiyid Raju Qattal" (pp. 303–304, folios 281–282), and against Qutb-i ʿAlam being "initiated by his father" and Shah-i ʿAlam "trained by… his father" (pp. 304–305, folios 282–283).
- **A saint condemns lamps on graves; the book then records five saints' graves and a relic cult without comment.** p. 302 (folio 280) against pp. 303, 305, 308, 314 (folios 281, 283, 286, 292) and the "celebrated relics" of p. 305 (folio 283).
- **Raju Qattal is described as a recluse and as a militant evangelist in consecutive sentences.** Makhdum Jahaniyan "had chosen for Raju the life of a recluse, and of one perpetually engrossed in prayer and contemplation. Such a judgement of Raju, however, belies his militant evangelism" (p. 303, folio 281). **Rizvi notices this one and says so.**
- **Chronology of the millennial explanation.** Conversions "during the early sixteenth century" are attributed to a millennium "completed in 1591-92" (p. 306, folio 284) — **the cause is dated some ninety years after the effect, and the book does not address it.**
- **A father's name slips mid-paragraph.** "Shaikh Sama'u'd-Din's son, Shaikh 'Abdu'llah, was also a great ascetic. Finding his wife an impediment to meditation, **Shaikh Ahmad** left her" (p. 308, folio 286). **The son is named ʿAbduʾllah in one sentence and Ahmad in the next.** This may be OCR or may be in the print; **it is not resolvable from the chunk and the man is not an archive figure, but the same defect on an archive name would be serious.**
- **One rare internal confirmation, worth recording as such:** Makhdum-i Jahaniyan born "15 Sha'ban 707/9 February 1308" (p. 299, folio 277) and dead "10 Zu'lhijja 785/3 February 1384" (p. 303, folio 281) — **785 − 707 = 78, and the two AH/CE pairs are internally consistent with each other and with the archive row's `figure_died` of 1384. This is the only archive date in the chunk that the book confirms.** All other AH/CE pairs above (847/1443-44, 790/1388—89, 857/1453, 817/1415, 880/1475, 915/1510, 901/1496, 942/1536, 923/1517-18, 959/1551-52, 976/1568-69, 763/1362, 781/1379, 782/1380, 714/1314, 783/1381, 786/1385, 661/1263) are recorded exactly as printed and are **unverified**.
