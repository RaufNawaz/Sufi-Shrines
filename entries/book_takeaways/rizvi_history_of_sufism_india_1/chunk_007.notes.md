## Practices, institutions, economy

**This is the densest institutional stretch of the book so far, and nearly all of it is on two pages, pp. 120–121 (folios 102–103).**

- **Baiʿa, with the physical gesture — the initiation rite chunk 006 recorded as missing.** "Through baiy‘a [OCR?] [baiʿa] or formal initiation, disciples were inextricably spiritually linked to their pirs. In order to solemnize the occasion, the pir would place his hand on the disciple's head, or alternately, the ritual involved the grasping of each other's hands" (p. 120, folio 102). **Two alternative gestures, no words of the oath, no witnesses named** — (not stated).
- **The khirqa investiture as a selective act, with its material description.** "A distinguished disciple, or one particularly favoured by the Shaikh, was invested by him with a khirqa. This was not necessarily made of wool but invariably was patched. At the ceremony the Shaikh imparted to his disciple secret instructions commensurate with the latter's abilities and transmitted to him special blessings"; "Investiture of the khirga [OCR?] [khirqa] increased the religious responsibilities of the disciple and he was expected to prove himself worthy of the honour" (p. 120, folio 102).
- **The khilafat-nama: a signed, sealed and witnessed document — the deputisation instrument chunk 006 could not find.** "Some distinguished disciples who had been endowed with khirgas [OCR?] [khirqas] were also sent by their Shaikhs to act as deputies or khalifas at different places during their lifetimes. They were given a licence or diploma called an ijaza or khilafat-nama, authorizing recipients to disseminate the principles and practices of their respective orders. Each khilafat-nama was formally signed and sealed by the Shaikh and witnessed by one or two of his important disciples" (p. 120, folio 102). **A named document, two names for it, an authorization clause, a seal and one or two witnesses.**
- **The succession bequest and the origin of the title Sajjada-Nashin — the custodianship material the archive most needs.** "Before a Shaikh's death, he would nominate his spiritual heir and bequeath to him his prayer carpet or skin called a sajjada, his subha, his staff and personal khirga [khirqa]. The heir's title was Sajjada-Nashin, in Arabic, Shaikh as-Sajjada. Succession was not necessarily hereditary or a decision of the Shaikh, in some orders after a Shaikh's death a khalifa would be elected" (p. 120, folio 102). **Four named regalia — sajjada, subha, staff, khirqa — and three succession modes: nomination, heredity, election.**
- **Burial inside the khanqah, and the ruler-built tomb — the origin story of the shrine as an institution, in one paragraph.** "Generally, Shaikhs were buried inside their khangahs [OCR?] [khanqahs] or close-by. Eventually rulers or important nobles, devoted to a particular Shaikh, erected imposing tombs on the graves of their patron saints. Even the humblest of these burial sites became institutions of far-reaching significance. By the eleventh century, khanqahs had become centres for the spiritual élite. However, the tombs were also for the people, both Muslim and non-Muslim, many of whom travelled vast distances to reach them" (p. 121, folio 103). **The élite/popular split is stated as a split between the khanqah and the tomb, and the tomb is explicitly cross-communal.**
- **Pilgrimage, intercession and the economy of disappointment.** "Pilgrims sought the intercession of the Shaikh's spirit for the fulfilment of their own ambitions, both religious and mundane. Naturally, the most enthusiastic disseminators of miraculous powers attached to the tombs were those whose wishes were attained, but the empty-handed cursed their own lot, rather than questioning the genuine sanctity of the saints. Such activity at the tombs led to an identification between the common man and the silsila, and this was often associated with a genuine feeling of respect for the Shaikh" (p. 121, folio 103). **A stated mechanism for why a shrine's reputation is never falsified by failure.**
- **The ʿurs, defined, with its etymology and its theology — the single most citable institutional sentence in the chunk.** "Great celebrations were held to commemorate the chief landmarks in the life of a Shaikh. Then, as now, the most popular date marked the anniversary of the death of great Indian sufis. This was known as the ‘urs or wedding, for the death of a mystic denoted the return of his soul to the supernatural source from which it had been separated during its earthly existence. This belief was strengthened by the philosophy of Ibn al-‘Arabi in his most significant concept, the Wahdat al-Wujud or the Tawhid-i Wujudi, that is, the Unity of Being" (p. 121, folio 103). **Note "Then, as now" — Rizvi asserts continuity to the present. No ʿurs of any named shrine, no date, no duration and no ritual programme is given** — (not stated).
- **Grave-cult as degeneration, with a named absurdity.** "Gradually the veneration of tombs degenerated into an excess of superstitious practices. The belief spread that the spirit of the saints resided in their graves and could be invoked for private use. Fictitious graves were also constructed and worshipped; a curious story is related by J.P. Brown in which an ass's grave was believed to radiate blessings to a group of villages in Anatolia" (p. 121, folio 103).
- **Zikr: the two modes, assigned to named orders.** "Zikr was performed both communally and in seclusion. The former enabled senior disciples to supervise the progress of their juniors. The zikr-i khafi, recollection performed either mentally or in a low voice, was recommended by the Naqshbandis; the Qadiriyya and the Chishtiyya generally performed zikr-i jali, which was recited aloud. Both forms of zikr involved control of the breath and over inhalation and exhalation. The formulas for zikr differed from one order to another, but generally they involved the recitation of different syllables of the ka/ima [OCR?] [kalima] or some of the many names of God" (p. 119, folio 101). **The first attribution of a practice to the Chishtiyya in the book.**
- **The rosary, with bead counts.** "Zikr popularized the use of the fasbih [OCR?] [tasbih] or rosary, consisting of 99 or 100 beads. Some orders used rosaries of 301 beads, but ones of 1000 beads were not unknown" (p. 119, folio 101). Footnoted with a transmission claim: "It seems probable that the Muslims borrowed the rosary from the Buddhists, and that the Crusaders copied their Muslim opponents and introduced it into Christendom" (pp. 119–120, folios 101–102, quoting "A Dictionary of Islam, p. 546").
- **Meditation on the image of the pir.** "Zikr was followed by meditation to allow the individual thoughts of sufis to emerge. Generally dervishes meditated on some particular verse of the Qur'an, and at the same time an image of the pir was recalled to mind" (p. 120, folio 102).
- **The seven stages of the soul, numbered and named.** "(al-ammara)", "(lawwama)", "(al-mulhama)", "(rmutma‘inna) [OCR?] [mutmaʾinna]", "a contented soul in which the soul renounced everything except God", "marziyya, the approved one", and "a faultless soul (a/-kamila) [OCR?] [al-kamila] … achieved by only the most perfect of sufis" (pp. 118–119, folios 100–101).
- **Mujahida under a pir.** "Mujahida, or self-mortification performed under the guidance of a pir, strictly adhered to the Shari‘a and was designed to achieve purification of the soul" (p. 118, folio 100); and the rule that mystical experience "could be revealed only to one's pir", with "some exceptions to this rule among sufis who claimed they were disciples of Khazir (or Khizr)" (p. 118, folio 100).
- **The shrine vow-economy of Abu Ishaq Kaziruni, in Ibn Battuta's words — the most detailed shrine finance passage the queue has yet produced, and it is a footnote.** Travellers on the Sea of China, fearing pirates, make vows and "each one of them sets down in writing the obligation he has undertaken in his vow. Then when they come safely to land, the servitors of the hospice go on board the ship, take the inventory, and exact (the amount of) his vow from each person who has pledged himself. There is not a ship that comes from China or from India but has thousands of dinars in it (vowed to the saint), and the agents on behalf of the intendant of the hospice come to take delivery of that sum." Poor brethren "receive a written order for some amount, sealed with the die into red wax", the order "being for a thousand or a hundred dirhams, or some intermediate or smaller sum, according to the standing of the poor brother concerned"; the mendicant "takes from him and writes for him a receipt for the amount on the back of the order"; and "The king of India (probably Muhammad bin Tughluq) once vowed ten thousand dinars to the Shaikh Abu Ishaq" (p. 129, folio 111, footnote 1). **A written vow, an inventory, collecting agents, an intendant, a sealed red-wax draft, an endorsement receipt and a royal donation of a stated size — with "(probably Muhammad bin Tughluq)" marked as Rizvi's or the translator's conjecture.**
- **Royal building at Chisht, and a town hostile to sufis.** "In Chisht itself, Ghiyasu'd-Din Muhammad built a madrasa and a mosque" (p. 133, folio 115), against Firuzkuh where "Many prominent poets and scholars settled … but the town was not favourably disposed towards the sufi movement" (p. 133, folio 115). **A dated reign (1163 to 1203) but no date for the madrasa or the mosque** — (not stated).
- **Training periods named in years.** Khwaja Maudud was "trained for four years in various forms of mysticism" at Balkh and Bukhara (p. 133, folio 115); Hujwiri's two journeys were "each for more than two years" (p. 130, folio 112).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

Attributions are the book's own, including its hedges ("according to tradition", "is said to have", "would appear mythical", "It was little wonder that sufis believed").

- **Khizr's immortality and his rescues.** "Belief in Khizr's immortality made him a supernatural being who was involved in the assisting of sufis of well-known orders. In legends Khizr saved men in desperate situations"; "Some sufis were said to be his constant companions, others were believed to have had a casual acquaintance with him" (p. 118, folio 100).
- **The cloak given to ʿAli.** "Another tradition says that after the Prophet's return from his mi‘raj or ascension, Muhammad invested ‘Ali with a cloak which he had been instructed by God to give to one who answered a particular question" (p. 120, folio 102).
- **The ass's grave in Anatolia**, believed "to radiate blessings to a group of villages", from J.P. Brown (p. 121, folio 103).
- **The camel that chose the site of Uch.** Abu Ishaq "ordered him to mount a camel and travel in whatever direction the animal led him; he was then to remain where the camel finally halted", and the halt was "in the middle of a desert" (p. 129, folio 111). **Rizvi's own verdict: "Although this story would appear mythical, it is typical of medieval tales relating to sufis."**
- **The flying contest with the yogi at Uch — the book's first sufi/yogi miracle contest.** "a yogi visited Shaikh Safiu'd-Din at Uch and challenged him to a competitive performance of miracles. The tale continues that the yogi began an exhibition of supernatural powers by flying to the ceiling and returning safely to the ground. When it was his turn, Shaikh Safiu'd-Din prayed to God, begging Him for some miraculous power. Then, leaving the room, he flew to the west, the north and the south, returning to the room and the awestruck yogi"; the yogi conceded that "although he himself could bodily rise in a perpendicular position as a result of powers achieved through his own spiritual exercises … the Shaikh's performance emanated from divine grace and was therefore miraculous" (pp. 129–130, folios 111–112). **The distinction drawn is technique versus grace, and it is put in the yogi's own mouth.**
- **Ibn al-ʿArabi's childhood vision and his dreamt book.** "At an early age he had a vision which changed the course of his life"; "Ibn al-‘Arabi claimed that Fusus had been revealed to him in a single dream" (pp. 121–122, folios 103–104).
- **A prophesied birth in the Chishti chain.** Abu Ishaq Shami "is said to have prophesied the birth of Khwaja Abu Ahmad Abdal which occurred during the same year" (p. 133, folio 115).
- **The pilot's vow miracle, implied rather than narrated** — ships spared pirates and storms after vows to Abu Ishaq Kaziruni, who was "recognized as the protector of travellers" (p. 129, folio 111, footnote 1).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Rizvi's decline thesis continues from chunk 006, and here it lands directly on shrines.** "Gradually the veneration of tombs degenerated into an excess of superstitious practices" (p. 121, folio 103). **But the same page treats pilgrimage, intercession and the ʿurs as normal and even admirable** ("often associated with a genuine feeling of respect for the Shaikh"), so the register shifts inside one page — see Doubts.
- **A foundation myth of Muslim India rejected in as many words.** "The belief that the conquest of Sind at the time of the Umayyad Caliph al-Walid I (705-15) originated as a punitive expedition against the ruling Raja Dahir, after his pirates had seized some Muslims on a hajj to Mecca, is a myth. In reality, the conquest of Transoxiana and Sind was initiated by Hajjaj, Walid's governor in Iraq, and was one aspect of his expansionist policy" (p. 127, folio 109).
- **Why there are no early Sind sufis to write about — a two-factor explanation of an archival silence.** "Lack of information about sufis in Sind may be ascribed to two factors. Firstly, land communications between Khurasan, Transoxiana and Sind were slow and arduous. Secondly, the blossoming of sufism during the tenth century had synchronized with the rise of the Isma‘ili Fatimid Caliphate"; "Therefore sufism, which subscribed to Sunni Islam, was naturally cut, from close relations with other areas of Central Asia, also within the Sunni fold" (p. 128, folio 110). **A sectarian-geographical explanation for why the Sindhi shrine tradition has no eleventh-century documentation.**
- **Where Islam in the subcontinent began, per Rizvi.** "The Arab sea traders operating between the Malabar coast and Ceylon first introduced Islam to that region of the sub-continent" (p. 127, folio 109).
- **When sufism reached Multan.** "It would seem, however, that by the middle of the eleventh century, sufism had penetrated into the areas surrounding Multan" (p. 128, folio 110). **Hedged ("It would seem").**
- **Why sufis went to the frontier — an economic-demographic explanation, not a missionary one.** "it was the news of Sultan Mahmud's conquest of Multan which prompted sufis to advise their talented and adventurous disciples to settle in that region. A new, reasonably unpopulated area offered tranquillity to a contemplative" (p. 129, folio 111).
- **The twelfth century as the watershed, and Ibn al-ʿArabi's actual contribution narrowed.** "The twelfth century was a watershed in the history of sufism. This was brought about by the introduction and widespread acceptance of the theory of Wahdat al-Wujud. Ibn al-‘Arabi did not, in fact, devise the concept himself but he managed to reconcile varying sufi views on Reality" (p. 121, folio 103). **Compare chunk 006, where the twelfth century is when "khanqah life was at its highest peak."**
- **The pantheism dispute, with four scholars and no verdict from Rizvi.** "The belief that this concept was a form of pantheism has now been discarded. Louis Massignon translated it as ‘existential monism’; Affifi also does not approve of the term pantheism, but does not reject it completely, calling it instead ‘Islamic pantheism.’ T. Burckhardt … unequivocally refutes the use of the word"; and "Henry Corbin takes even stronger exception to a monistic interpretation" (pp. 123, 125; folios 105, 107).
- **Idolatry conditionally tolerated — a doctrinal position with obvious bearing on shared shrines.** "As long as an idolater was aware he was worshipping God, idolatry could be tolerated for this tended to make tanzih and tashbih complimentary. If an idol worshipper imagined that a piece of stone or wood was God, he ignored tanzih" (p. 125, folio 107); and Ibn al-ʿArabi "advocated that the Divine existed and was worshipped in all religions, and that God was worshipped in Love, His highest manifestation" (p. 126, folio 108), with the "religion of love" poem quoted at p. 127 (folio 109).
- **Sainthood as an open, post-prophetic office.** "After Muhammad, prophethood, or the process of law-giving, ceased, but the state of Wali never ceased to exist"; "The saints (Wali) who had died to their own ego were Perfect Men, for Wali was a name of God and indicated that saints were an aspect of the Absolute"; and the "Khatam al-Walaya (the seal of sainthood)", which "meant the end of a cycle of saints; he believed that a cycle embodying the heritage of Muhammad and the Messiah ended with himself" (p. 126, folio 108).
- **The thesis Rizvi ends Chapter One on — conflict as the engine of Indian sufism, evidenced only by Hujwiri's Lahore.** "It would appear that from its very inception, sufism in India developed conflicting trends mainly due to challenges from movements amongst local mystics. Sufi history in India is the story both of various challenges and responses, and also of the cross-fertilization of new ideas" (p. 131, folio 113). **The only evidence offered is the hostility Hujwiri met in Lahore.**
- **The Chishtiyya claimed as Indian, and its foreign branches written off.** "T#e [OCR?] [The] Chishti order of sufis is essentially an Indian one. Other branches emanating from the town of Chisht in modern Afghanistan did not survive for long in the Perso-Islamic world" (p. 132, folio 114).
- **The Chishti spiritual genealogy given and then undercut in its own footnote.** The chain from Hasan Basri to Abu Ishaq Shami is printed in full (p. 132, folio 114), with the footnote "Many scholars dispute the authenticity of this genealogical tree" (p. 132, folio 114); and later, "Some Indian Chishti genealogical trees do not mention Khwaja Ahmad or Ruknu'd-Din" (p. 133, folio 115, footnote 7). **Rizvi prints the silsila and disowns its authenticity in the same breath — directly relevant to any shrine entry that recites a chain.**
- **The end of Chisht as a centre, explained by war.** "The deaths of Khwaja Maudud's disciples marked the end of the great spiritual peak in sufism which had occurred in Chisht. Some of their disciples moved elsewhere or lived as wandering dervishes"; the fortress of Firuzkuh "deprived Chisht of its former peaceful existence" and "The region became a fierce battle ground between rival Turkic tribes" (p. 133, folio 115). **This is the stated push-factor for the Chishtis' move to India.**

**Internal contradictions and chronological strains, kept side by side rather than resolved:**

20. **Succession is said not to be hereditary, and then five hereditary successions are recorded.** "Succession was not necessarily hereditary or a decision of the Shaikh, in some orders after a Shaikh's death a khalifa would be elected" (p. 120, folio 102) stands against the Chisht chain, where Abu Ahmad appoints "his son Abu Muhammad", who nominates "his sister's son, Khwaja Yusuf", who is succeeded by "his son, Khwaja Maudud Chishti", who appoints "his son, Khwaja Ahmad" (p. 133, folio 115). **The book does not note the tension.**
21. **A 115-year gap in the Chishti chain, as printed.** Khwaja Yusuf dies "459/1066-67" and is succeeded by his son Khwaja Maudud, who dies "577/1181-82" (p. 133, folio 115). **Both dates are recorded exactly as printed and neither is corrected; the span is the book's, not mine.**
22. **The Ghurids rise in the twelfth century on one page and in the tenth on the next.** "the Ghurid Sultans of the Shansab dynasty established their rule in the twelfth century" (p. 132, folio 114) against "The rise of the Ghurid Turks in the tenth century" (p. 133, folio 115). **Unresolved in the book.**
23. **Chisht's towns are competing for supremacy "By the end of the ninth century" but the region is only Islamized "Until the tenth century … by the governors of Khurasan"** (p. 132, folio 114) — the sequence is stated in the wrong order and the book does not reconcile it.
24. **The tomb cult is a degeneration and a normal institution on the same page.** "Gradually the veneration of tombs degenerated into an excess of superstitious practices" sits eight lines below "this was often associated with a genuine feeling of respect for the Shaikh" and immediately above the ʿurs passage, which is given without a word of criticism (p. 121, folio 103). **As in chunk 006, Rizvi's evaluative register shifts within a page; an entry quoting either sentence must carry the other.**
25. **Hujwiri arrives "c. 1035" but his teacher dies in 1065 with Hujwiri at his side, and he "returned to Lahore" only "After 1067"** (p. 130, folio 112) — so the "settling in Lahore" of c. 1035 is interrupted by at least one long absence. **Not a contradiction, but the dates must be read together before any entry says he "settled in Lahore in 1035 and remained".**
26. **One date set cross-checks from within the chunk** — Abu Ishaq Shami "died in 329/940" having reached Chisht "sometime before 260/873-74", the year he prophesied Abu Ahmad Abdal's birth, and Abu Ahmad "died in 355/965—66" (p. 133, folio 115): the sequence is internally possible, though it gives Abu Ishaq a career of at least sixty-seven years. **No other date set in the chunk can be checked from within it, and the many AH/CE pairs above are recorded exactly as printed and are unverified.**
