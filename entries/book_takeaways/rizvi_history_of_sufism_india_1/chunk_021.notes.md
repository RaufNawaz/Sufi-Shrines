Rizvi adds that the songs "were not composed for propaganda purposes but were a natural evolution from the deep and personal involvement of these two great mystics with their environment" (p. 349, folio 327).
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **a verse of his in the Multani dialect is quoted by a named hagiography and declared hard to read.** "The Siyaru'l-Auliya' quotes a verse composed by Baba Farid in the Multani dialect whose precise meaning is difficult to decipher" (p. 350, folio 328), footnoted "Sururu's-Sudur, pp. 50-1; SA, p. 367" — on the unexpanded siglum "SA" see Doubts.
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **two of his dohas survive in a dated printed collection with a Persian crib, and the book prints them in English.** "The Saba'-Sanabil of Mir 'Abdu'l-Wahid Bilgarami contains two dohas by Baba Farid with a Persian translation. The Mir himself was a good poet in Hindawi and his version makes the doha more intelligible" (p. 350, folio 328), cited as "Mir 'Abdu'l-Wahid Bilgarami, Saba' Sanabil, Kanpur, 1299/1881-82, p. 58". The two dohas as the book renders them are under "Citable passages"; **both attack false initiation and outward asceticism** — "You give a cap as a mark of initiation, and this indicates that you are presumptuous" and "You shave your head but what you should do is to cut lust from the heart" (p. 350, folio 328).
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **more than a hundred verses under his name stand in the Sikh scripture, and the book dates the compilation.** "Controversy surrounds more than a hundred s/okas [OCR?] [slokas] ascribed to Baba Farid Ganj-i Shakar in the Guru Garanth [OCR?] [Guru Granth], compiled by the fifth Sikh Guru, Arjan, in 1604" (p. 350, folio 328). **This is the only calendar date the chunk attaches to anything in the Farid complex, and it is a date for the scripture, not for the saint — the book gives no birth or death date for Baba Farid anywhere in pp. 349–365** (not stated).
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **Rizvi's own verdict on the authorship of those verses, given against three named alternatives.** "Some scholars assert they were composed by Baba Farid himself but the language indicates they were the work of his successors who may have rewritten some of Baba Farid's original slokas into a more intelligible Multani. Some of these slokas are even ascribed to Kabir" (pp. 350–351, folios 328–329); "Others believe that the s/okas [slokas] in the Guru Garanth [Guru Granth] were composed by Shaikh Ibrahim, a successor of Baba Farid, whom Guru Nanak visited at Ajodhan" (p. 351, folio 329); and Rizvi's conclusion, "A careful analysis of Baba Farid's slokas in the Guru Granth would tend to suggest they were not composed by one individual. Therefore it is wrong to ascribe them either to Shaikh Ibrahim or another of Baba Farid's descendants, known as Farid Sani. They represent the teachings of Baba Farid through the years from his own time to the fifteenth century and were therefore composed by a number of different descendants, all using Farid as their nom de plume" (p. 351, folio 329).
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **the seat is written "Ajodhan", never "Pakpattan", exactly as the queue's trap note predicts** — "Shaikh Ibrahim, a successor of Baba Farid, whom Guru Nanak visited at Ajodhan" (p. 351, folio 329). **This is the only place-name attached to the Farid lineage in the chunk, and it is attached to the successor, not to Baba Farid himself.** No tomb, no shrine building, no ʿurs, no custodian and no sajjada nashin is mentioned (not stated).
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **the pen-name practice is restated later and made parallel to the Nath practice — this is the chapter's structural claim about his corpus.** "A corpus of dohas with Farid as the nom de plume which were compiled by a number of descendants of the Baba show that their authors were also influenced by this trend" (p. 353, folio 331), the "trend" being verses written by later disciples under Gorakhnath's name "in order to establish a spiritual link with their master" (p. 353, folio 331).
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **he is named as one of the two sufis for whom Nath doctrine provided common ground.** The *Siddha Siddhanta Paddhati* "and some authentic works by Gorakhnath's followers formed the basis of the doctrines of the puritanical Naths and offered a common ground for the exchange of ideas with such sufis as Shaikh Hamidu'd-Din Nagauri and Baba Farid. Discussions on the conception of the Ultimate Reality enhanced the mutual respect of the Naths and the sufis" (p. 355, folio 333).
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **he is credited with a first in Indian sufi practice — the inverted prayer.** Of *namaz-i maʿkus*: "Although Chishtis believed this type of namaz to be a legacy from the Prophet, as pointed out earlier, Shaikh Abu Sa'id bin Abi'l Khair was the first known sufi to have practised it; the first Indian sufi to perform it was Baba Farid" (p. 364, folio 342). A footnote points back to the book's own earlier treatment: "See the sections on Shaikh Abu Said bin Abi'l-Khair, pp. 68-69. supra, and Baba Farid, p. 139, supra, who were both famous for this type of namaz" (p. 358, folio 336, footnote 6). **Those are the book's own printed page numbers, i.e. folios, not PDF pages — see Doubts.**

- **gorakhnath-goraknath-temple**: **the order he founded, and its initiation rite, described as still current.** "The framework of the order of the Kanphata (Split Ear Yogis) or the Naths founded by Gorakhnath serves to illustrate the latter's remarkable organizational capacity and foresight" (p. 354, folio 332). The rite is under "Practices, institutions, economy".
- **gorakhnath-goraknath-temple**: **his surviving and lost works.** "Of the Sanskrit treatises by Gorakhnath, unlike the Hatha-Yoga, the Goraksha-Sataka has survived and has been edited and translated into English" (p. 354, folio 332); "Gorakhnath's disciples were the authors of a number of treatises on magic, alchemy and left-handed occultism and Hatha-Yoga" (pp. 353–354, folios 331–332); and the verse corpus, "A number of verses in Panjabi and Hindi also attributed to him were written by successive generations of disciples using the name Gorakhnath in order to establish a spiritual link with their master" (p. 353, folio 331). - **gorakhnath-goraknath-temple**: **he stands at the head of the Nath transmission, in a trinity with a deity.** "The transmission of the Nath doctrine however is founded on the trinity: Shiva, Matsyendranath and Gorakhnath" (p. 354, folio 332); and "The traditions venerate nine Naths and eighty-four Siddhas but this does not imply the historical authenticity of these figures. - **gorakhnath-goraknath-temple**: **his doctrine is the chapter's bridge to sufism, and it is summarised at length** — Ultimate Reality "could not be conceived by logical reasoning; it was a super sensuous, super intellectual, direct experience in the state of samadhi (trance)" (p. 355, folio 333); "Sat-Cit-Ananda-Murti (One who reveals Himself as Being, Consciousness and Bliss), Goraknath [OCR?] [Gorakhnath] believed was the highest form of God, the self-manifestation of the Formless and Manifestationless One—Brahma, Shiva, Parmatma, Parmeswara, the holiest names of the Nameless One" (p. 355, folio 333); "To Gorakhnath and the Siddhas the phenomenal cosmic system was not false or illusory" (p. 355, folio 333); "Adhars, according to Gorakhnath, are the main sources of the vital and psychical functions which have to be controlled" (pp. 356–357, folios 334–335); "According to Gorakhnath the Absolute Truth realized in the highest spiritual experience is above the concept of bhava (existence) and abhava (negation of existence)" (p. 361, folio 339).
- **gorakhnath-goraknath-temple**: **his name is used by a Chishti shaikh as a term for God, six times in one Hindi work — the single most striking piece of shared-ground evidence in the chunk.** "Of the many verses in the Rushd-Nama there are six references to either 'Gorakhnath,' 'Shri Gorakh,' 'Nath' and 'O! Also "In the words of Gorakh, Shaikh 'Abdu'l-Quddus warns yogis that wearing rings in their ears and rosaries of Eleocarpus ganitrus around their necks, or the recitation of sakhi and sabad (Nath poetry), fails to make one a yogi" (p. 360, folio 338), and "All the verses by Shaikh 'Abdu'l-Quddus relating to the simile of the mirror and the polishing of the heart are based on ideas expressed by Ibn al-'Arabi and Gorakhnath" (p. 363, folio 341).

## Practices, institutions, economy

**The negative first, and it is the important one for this chapter: in pp. 349–365 (folios 327–343) there is no shrine, no tomb, no dargah, no ʿurs, no mela, no langar, no sajjada nashin or custodian, no Auqaf, no endowment, no land, no revenue, no offering, no visitor number, no dispute over a site, no building and no building date — for any figure, Hindu or Muslim.** The chapter's "shared ground" in this range is **entirely textual and doctrinal**: shared verses, shared vocabulary, a shared translated manual, a Chishti shaikh using a yogi's name for God. **There is no temple taken over, no tomb and temple at one place, and no contested site anywhere in the chunk** (not stated). **A consolidator should not expect physical shared-site material before p. 366.** The one institutional building named is a *khanqah*, and it is named only as a place of service:

- **A khanqah entered as a lifetime of service, against a forced marriage — the chunk's only institution with a person attached.** "He decided to dedicate his life to the service of Shaikh Ahmad's khangah [OCR?] [khanqah] and to live the life of a celibate. Forced by his parents to marry, the Shaikh continuously neglected his family by spending every possible moment in prayer and meditation" (p. 365, folio 343). **The predicted q→g damage lands on exactly this word.** No location beyond Rudauli, no founder's date, no endowment (not stated).
- **A migration with a stated communal cause, from a named source the book itself calls emotional.** "The reasons for the Shaikh's migration, as given in the Lata'if-i Quddusi, are emotional. The work states that Rudauli had succumbed to the infiltration of kafirs, Islamic practices disappeared and pork was openly sold in the bazaar. So concerned was the Shaikh that he left Rudauli for Sultan Sikandar Lodi's camp at Nakhna" (p. 365, folio 343). **Rizvi flags the source's emotional register and does not endorse it — the nearest thing to communal conflict in the chunk, and it is about a bazaar, not a shrine.**
- **Samaʿ gatherings with vernacular song, and a loss.** "Most of the Hindawi songs recited at sama' gatherings held during this period have been lost" (p. 349, folio 327). **No performer, no qawwal, no venue and no payment is named** (not stated).
- **The Kanphata initiation rite, in full, and the book says it is still performed.** "At the time of initiation, the ear cartilages of a novice were as is still customary today split, and two enormous ear-rings were inserted in the holes. Ear splitting was believed necessary to open a mystical channel in order to assist the development of yogic powers. During the ceremony a knife was driven into the ground and vows recited over it by the initiate; these included a vow to protect the ears which, according to Hindu mysticism, were believed to contain a network of invisible nadis (ganglia) connecting them with the inner organs of perception. The rites also included the symbolic slaying of the neophyte, the washing of his entrails, and the hanging of his body on a tree" (p. 354, folio 332). **The phrase "as is still customary today" is the chunk's only present-tense claim about a living practice.**
- **Caste policy of the Naths, stated twice and as a boundary of the order.** "The Naths initiated members of all castes, including those outside the Hindu caste system, such as Chandalas and sweepers, into their non-hierarchical order. Generally it would seem the Brahman castes were not attracted to the Naths" (p. 354, folio 332); "All Naths however were hostile to Hindu caste distinctions, particularly those practised by Brahmans and respected even the pariah and the untouchable" (p. 355, folio 333). Compare "Manikchandra, a bania by caste, who left his wealth to join the Naths" (p. 354, folio 332) — **the chunk's one recorded act of renouncing wealth**.
- **The ascetic orders distinguished from the Naths by where they eat and what they eat.** "The Shaivite ascetics who haunted cemeteries, ate from skulls and even consumed corpses at the burning ghats, known as Aghoris or Aghora-panthis and the ascetic order of Kapalikas (wearers of skulls) are also known as yogis but are basically different to the Naths" (p. 354, folio 332); and the geographical consequence, "Aghoris and Kapalikas were uninterested in Middle Eastern regions where there were no burning ghats" (p. 355, folio 333). **Cremation ground as an institutional prerequisite — a rare piece of concrete sacred geography in the chunk.**
- **A numbered pantheon.** "The traditions venerate nine Naths and eighty-four Siddhas" (p. 354, folio 332).
- **The Hatha-Yoga body technique the sufis took up, with its named operations.** "The processes of asana (posture), dhauti (washing), bandha (different kinds of motionlessness), mudra (gesture), pranayama (breathing) and other techniques of Hatha-Yoga are prescribed so as to achieve the transformation of the body in order to achieve the full control of the mind. The prerequisite of Nath discipline is the control of the vayu (the vital wind). This entire philosophy is called Hatha-Yoga, the union of the moon (tha) and the sun (ha)" (p. 357, folio 335). The cakra system: "nine cakras or centres of psycho-vital forces", the serpent power "located in the lowest muladhara cakra of every human body", rising "to the highest cakra (sahasrara)", with "The region below the navel … the region of Shakti, while that above it is that of Shiva" (p. 356, folio 334).
- **The counted breath-practice, with the Hindu syllable inside a sufi discipline — the chunk's densest practice passage and its clearest borrowing.** "Onkar is the basis of pranayama. In the initial stages, breath is drawn up through the left nostril, the ira [OCR?] [ida], while the sacred Hindu syllable 'Om' is repeated slowly sixteen times. The breath is then suspended in the upper part of the nose where the breath nostrils meet. The junction of the nostrils is called the sukhmana. Just as the breath has been drawn up by the left nostril, so it is forced down through the right nostril to pingala, while the syllable is again repeated sixteen times. The highest degree of perfection is extremely difficult to achieve, but Shaikh 'Abdu'l-Quddus expects sufis to absorb themselves in Onkar through zikr" (p. 359, folio 337). **Sixteen repetitions each way is the one number in the chunk that could enter an entry.**
- **The inverted prayer, named twice and with a lineage of three practitioners.** Defined in a footnote as "Namaz performed hanging upside down with the legs suspended from a roof or a branch of a tree" (p. 358, folio 336, footnote 6), where it is called *salat-i-maʿkus*; and described in practice, "For years after the evening namaz he would perform the namaz-i ma'kus. This was carried out by hanging, probably head downwards, and was generally continued the whole night. Although Chishtis believed this type of namaz to be a legacy from the Prophet … Shaikh Abu Sa'id bin Abi'l Khair was the first known sufi to have practised it; the first Indian sufi to perform it was Baba Farid. Shaikh 'Abdu'l-Quddus considered it to be the counterpart of the u/ti sadhna [OCR?] [ulti sadhna]" (p. 364, folio 342). **Rizvi marks the Chishti claim of prophetic origin as a belief and supplies a different, sufi-Iranian origin.**
- **A daily prayer regime with a count, and the physical evidence for it.** "Besides obligatory prayers the Shaikh would perform four hundred rak 'ats of namaz during the day and four hundred rak 'ats at night. The clothes covering his knees would be threadbare from kneeling. Winter's excessive cold and frost were no obstacle to his praying. After performing the evening namaz he would begin the zikr-i jahr. Those who joined him would tire, but the Shaikh's absorption in the Wahdat al-Wujud failed to quench his enthusiasm" (p. 364, folio 342). **Eight hundred rakʿats a day, a named vocal zikr (zikr-i jahr), and companions who could not keep up.**
- **A named ecstatic state produced by the practice, and its terminal state.** "Continual performance of namaz-i ma'kus produced in the Shaikh a condition he called sultan-i zikr in which one experienced strange changes in the physical and spiritual condition including a deprivation of the senses and a lack of feeling of consciousness. Repeated appearances of the sultan-i zikr led to the state of fana' al-fana'" (p. 364, folio 342), the state described phenomenologically over pp. 364–365 (folios 342–343): it "would appear just before waking", "the inner contemplation made wakefulness and sleep appear identical", "Initially the contemplative was quite frightened, but gradually he became accustomed to the condition".
- **A translated Hindu manual inside the sufi curriculum, with a transmission history in four stages.** "the most significant impact of Hatha-Yoga was the treatise, the Amrita-Kunda. It is believed that it was translated by Qazi Ruknu'd-Din Samarqandi … who visited Lakhnauti between 1209-10 and 1216-17 and was initiated into Hatha-Yogic principles by a Siddha, called Bhojar Brahman. The work was later translated into Persian. A further Arabic version was again prepared by a Brahman from Kamrup, apparently in collaboration with a Muslim scholar. This version was re-translated into Persian by Shaikh Muhammad Ghaus Shattari (906/1500-01-970/1562-63)" (p. 357, folio 335); ʿAbduʾl-Quddus, who knew "the Arabic and Persian versions … which were widespread before the translation by Shaikh Muhammad Ghaus, imparted its essence to one of his disciples, Shaikh Sulaiman" (p. 357, folio 335). Its Arabic-Persian titles are given as "Hauzu'l-Hayat, an exact equivalent of the Amrita-Kunda. Another title of the book is Bahru'l-Hayat (Ocean of Life or Immortality)" (p. 357, folio 335, footnote 2). Its content: "prescribes methods for the continued suppression of respiration … and fixed the eye on the tip of the nose", with "control of the semen" as "A prerequisite for yogic discipline" (p. 358, folio 336).
- **The Sikh scripture dated and its compiler named** — "the Guru Garanth [Guru Granth], compiled by the fifth Sikh Guru, Arjan, in 1604" (p. 350, folio 328). **A Sikh institutional date, the only one in the chunk.**
- **Discipleship rule invoked against yogis and sufis alike.** "Quoting the sufi belief that those who had no human pir were disciples of the devil, in a Hindi verse the Shaikh said that if a blind man led another blind man, both were bound to fall into a well. A ceaseless effort was needed to find the perfect guru whom the Shaikh likened to a diamond mine—unless it was dug patiently and assiduously, the diamonds would never be found" (pp. 363–364, folios 341–342). Set against ʿAbduʾl-Quddus taking initiation from Shaikh Muhammad "In order to have a living pir as a guide" (p. 365, folio 343) — **and, in the same sentence, claiming inspiration from a dead one.**
- **Outward marks of religion rejected, on both sides.** Against yogis: "wearing rings in their ears and rosaries of Eleocarpus ganitrus around their necks, or the recitation of sakhi and sabad (Nath poetry), fails to make one a yogi. These were only means to achieve worldly ambitions and were not true Yoga" (p. 360, folio 338). Against qazis: verses in the *Gorakhbani* "challenge qazis for mechanically crying the name 'Muhammad' and remind them that it was most improper for them to call themselves Muslims for they recited the Kal/ima [OCR?] [Kalima] without gaining its real meaning" (p. 361, folio 339). And in Baba Farid's own doha, against the cap of initiation and the shaven head (p. 350, folio 328). **Three separate polemics against outward marks, from a sufi, a Nath text and a sufi's Hindawi verse — the chapter's strongest common-ground evidence after the shared vocabulary.**

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

The attributions are the book's own, including its hedges ("According to legend", "believed to have been", "reported to have lived", "the subject of many miraculous tales").

- **Matsyendra overhears the doctrine while in fish form.** "According to legend, while swimming like a fish he overheard the esoteric doctrines of the Nath which the Lord Shiva was imparting to his spouse, Parvati. Matsyendra is therefore known by such names as Minanath or Lui-pa, meaning fish Lord in Tibetan" (p. 353, folio 331).
- **Gorakhnath's birth from sweat, and the eye lost in service.** "Gorakhnath, Matsyendra's disciple, believed to have been born from the sweat of Shiva's breast, saved his master, in whose service he had earlier lost an eye" (p. 353, folio 331). **The rescue is asserted and never narrated — what he saved his master from is (not stated).**
- **A queendom ruled by female succession.** "Lakshinkara, a legendary princess of the mythical kingdom of Indrabhuti which was successively ruled by female issue. Lakshinkara has been compared to Eleanor of Aquitain" (p. 353, folio 331).
- **Ratannath and the tales.** "Ratannath, a contemporary of Gorakhnath, and the subject of many miraculous tales" (p. 354, folio 332); and "Puranbhagat who, with his half-brother Rasula, has inspired hundreds of miraculous tales" (p. 354, folio 332). **Neither tale is told.**
- **Power over serpents.** "Guga (or Guganath) whose power over serpents was phenomenal" (p. 354, folio 332).
- **Bodily immortality as the yogi's attainment.** "The perfect yogi can transform his body according to his will and is therefore free of all diseases and death. Siddhas, such as the disciples of Gorakhnath and others are believed to have achieved the practical aspects of this philosophy while in the foothills of the Himalayas and came to be known as the jivan-mukta (liberated while living)" (p. 357, folio 335); and Kanha's "instantly becomes a magician; he fears not old age and death" (p. 360, folio 338, footnote 1).
- **Posthumous initiation from a dead shaikh's spirit — reported as the man's own claim.** ʿAbduʾl-Quddus "also claimed to have directly obtained inspiration from the spirit of Shaikh Ahmad 'Abdu'l-Haqq" (p. 365, folio 343).
- **An ecstatic state compared to prophetic revelation.** "Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din then compared the condition of sultan-i zikr with that experienced by the Prophet Muhammad when he received wahi" — glossed in a footnote as "Inspiration received from the angel Gabriel by the ears of the Prophet Muhammad: the occasion engendered a supernatural condition in the Prophet" — "In short, he added that at the commencement of sultan-i zikr, the meditator felt as if he were listening to the humming of a bell whose sound then gradually became thunderous" (p. 365, folio 343), which "according to Shaikh 'Abdu'l-Quddus … had special relevance to nad" — footnoted "A mystical voice", printed "SA invsiical voice [OCR?] [A mystical voice]" (p. 364, folio 342, footnote 6) — "and was a privilege of only a few outstanding sufis" (p. 365, folio 343).
- **The hadith of the Hidden Treasure, attributed to a revelation to David.** "All sufis believe in the following explanation of creation, said to have been revealed by God to David: 'I was a Hidden Treasure and I wished to be known, so I created creation that I might be known.'" (pp. 362–363, folios 340–341). **Rizvi then says "This desire is identical with the concept of divine will as held by the Naths."**
- **Rizvi's own scepticism, recorded because it is unusual for him to say it so flatly.** "The traditions venerate nine Naths and eighty-four Siddhas but this does not imply the historical authenticity of these figures" (p. 354, folio 332); and of Gorakhnath, "Much mystery and legend also surround Gorakhnath's personality, but we know he wrote some treatises in Sanskrit" (p. 353, folio 331).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The chapter's governing thesis about vernacular verse and Hindu interest in sufism.** "Hindawi was a more convenient language in which to utter the feelings of a heart filled with divine love … Although euarlier [OCR?] [earlier] sufis had expressed this idea in many different ways in both Persian poetry and prose, the later use of Hindawi in further explanations of this concept was most probably a significant factor in the arousal of Hindu interest in sufism" (p. 349, folio 327). **Note "most probably" — Rizvi hedges his own central claim, and gives no conversion evidence for it in this chunk.**
- **Vernacular verse as spontaneous, not missionary.** "Such songs were not composed for propaganda purposes but were a natural evolution from the deep and personal involvement of these two great mystics with their environment" (p. 349, folio 327). **An explicit rejection of the conversion-by-poetry reading, from the author of the chapter about interaction.**
- **A two-stage literary prehistory for the Hindawi sufi poets.** "Firstly there was the poetry of the Siddhacharyas, followers of the Buddhist Sahajiya cult which began during the eighth century. This literature continued to influence, in both style and spirit, the poetry written in local dialects until the twelfth century. Secondly this type of poetry was succeeded by that written by members of the Nath cult" (p. 351, folio 329).
- **The Nath cult's genealogy, given as syncretism from the start.** "Founded on the psycho-chemical process of yoga in which the old Siddha cult of the yogis specialized, the Shaivite Nath cult developed, assimilating elements from the Buddhist Sahajiya cult. The Adi Nath, the First Lord of the Naths, is the Shiva of the Hindus as is the Buddha, in the form of the Vajra-Sattava [OCR?] [Vajra-Sattva], of the Buddhists" (p. 353, folio 331). **Sahajiya Buddhism itself is placed as "an offshoot of Tantric Buddhism, the Vajrayana or 'Vehicle of the Thunderbolt', which was patronised by the Pala kings of Bengal" (p. 351, folio 329).**
- **The common-ground claim, stated as mutual respect and not as influence in one direction.** "The Siddha Siddhanta Paddhati, and some authentic works by Gorakhnath's followers formed the basis of the doctrines of the puritanical Naths and offered a common ground for the exchange of ideas with such sufis as Shaikh Hamidu'd-Din Nagauri and Baba Farid. Discussions on the conception of the Ultimate Reality enhanced the mutual respect of the Naths and the sufis" (p. 355, folio 333). **Note "puritanical Naths" — Rizvi's common ground is with a sober subset he has just distinguished from another.**
- **The water-and-bubbles analogy as the technical meeting point of Nath monism and Wahdat al-Wujud, with the Sanskrit printed.** "The Siddha Siddhanta Paddhati demonstrated the relationship between advaita (non-dualism) and dvaita (dualism) by using the analogy of water and bubbles familiar to that used by sufis in the Wahdat al-Wujud system" (pp. 355–356, folios 333–334), with "Akulam kulam adhatte kulam cakulam icchati jala-budbuda-bat nyayat ckakarah [OCR?] [ekakarah] Parah Sivah" and its translation, "Akula embraces Kula (the phenomenal self-expression of Reality) and Kula yearns for Akula (the noumenal essence of Reality). The relation is analogous to that between water and water bubbles. In reality Para-Siva (Supreme Spirit) is absolutely one" (p. 356, folio 334); and the sufi restatement, "The simile of the relationship between river water and bubbles applied to the Naths could also be used to explain the Wahdat al-Wujud" (p. 362, folio 340), extended by ʿAbduʾl-Quddus to "steam rising from a river … vapour … a cloud … rain water" (p. 362, folio 340).
- **The three equations Rizvi draws between Nath and sufi doctrine, in his own voice.** "The Nath theory of the Lord existing alone in a void is no different from Jili's theory of a/-'Ama [al-ʿAma] (the dark mist and blindness)" (p. 363, folio 341); "To Shaikh 'Abdu'l-Quddus the Nath theory of creation was a replica of Ibn al-'Arabi's theory" (p. 363, folio 341); "This philosophy of Gorakhnath and the Siddhas called the Dwaita-dwaita-vilakshana-vada or Pakshapata-binirmukta-vada is nearest to the Wahdat al-Wujud" (p. 362, folio 340). **All three are assertions of identity, none is argued from a text placed side by side; only the bubbles analogy is actually demonstrated.**
- **Shared verse as jointly owned property — the single most quotable line of the chapter for this archive.** "In fact some verses with slight variations are included in Nath poetry as well as in that of Kabir and Gorakhnath. Such verses were regarded as the common property of both Muslim and Hindu mystics" (p. 358, folio 336).
- **Equations of technique, not only of doctrine.** "the union of Shakti, the sun, and Shiva, the moon, according to the Shaikh, is the salat-i-ma'kus of the sufis. The yogi equivalent is the ulti sadhna (regressive process) involving the 'complete reversal of human behaviour, from "respiratory behaviour" (replaced by pranayama) to sexual behaviour (annulled by the technique of the return of semen)'" (pp. 358–359, folios 336–337); and "sultan-i zikr was comparable to the Nath Siddha's nad, and that fana' al-fana' was a state experienced by the jivan-mukta" (p. 364, folio 342); and "The sunya, or sahaja of the Shaikh is also identical with the sunya and the sahaja of the Naths" (p. 360, folio 338).
- **Sufi vocabulary re-derived from Hindi terms, in the author's summary of the Rushd-Nama** — *sabad*, footnoted "Sabad or sabda means word, but it may also mean a hymn" (p. 358, folio 336, footnote 4), becomes "the indefinable divine word … perceived by only perfect mystics" for both parties (p. 360, folio 338); *Onkar*, footnoted "In medieval devotional literature Onkar is an equivalent to Om or Omkar" (p. 359, folio 337, footnote 3), is made "the Absolute Oneness, is interchangeable with Niranjana and indicates the state of sunyata (void)" (p. 359, folio 337); *Alakh Niranjan* is used by the Shaikh "in the same sense" as the Nath *Alakh-Nath* (p. 359, folio 337), and "In another verse the Shaikh identifies Niranjan with Khuda and calls Him the creator of the different worlds" (p. 359, folio 337).
- **Rizvi's evaluative paragraph on Nath morals, recorded because the register is his own and unsupported here.** "Many Naths might have indulged in homosexuality, orgiastic practices, necrophilia, scatology, bestiality and other sexual perversions, but there were many groups of sober Naths who disseminated the real spiritual tenets of their founders" (p. 355, folio 333). **"Might have" carries the whole sentence; no source, no case and no text is cited for any of it.**
- **The Sahajiya polemic, reported as Saraha-Pada's.** "the Brahmanical claims of class superiority were unfounded, the naked Jaina Ksapanaka-Yogins were frauds, the Buddhist monks were superstitious, the Tantras and the Mantras led to confusion and only Sahaja helped a mystic to gain a true understanding of the real nature of yogic discipline. Brahmanical sacrifices, pilgrimages and penances were of no avail, what had to be done was to fix the mind to the Niranjana or Stainless One" (p. 352, folio 330). **"Pilgrimages … were of no avail" is the chunk's only statement about pilgrimage of any kind, and it is a rejection of it.**
- **A definitional footnote on Tantricism that bears on the archive's shared-ground question.** "Tantricism is the cult followed by certain left-handed sects of Hindus and Buddhists. Although not hostile to the Vedas, Tantricists do not approve of the conventional schools of Hindu philosophy. Tantric doctrines are opposed to caste and class distinctions" (p. 352, folio 330, footnote 1).
- **Nath influence reaching the qalandars.** "from their centre at Peshawar moved to all parts of Central Asia and Iran, at the same time influencing both qalandars and sufis. … Some of the yogis who thronged the court of the Mongols were Buddhist Tantrics, but they were hardly distinguishable from the yogis" (p. 355, folio 333). **The last clause is self-contradictory as printed — see Doubts.**

**Archive-mapping doubts, most consequential first:**
