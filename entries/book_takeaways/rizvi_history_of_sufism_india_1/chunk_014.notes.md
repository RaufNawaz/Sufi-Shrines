# rizvi_history_of_sufism_india_1 — chunk 014 — pp. 233–248

**The folio regime in this chunk is fully header-attested and agrees with `folio_map.tsv` on every one of the sixteen pages.** Rectos print `The Suhrawardiyya and the Firdawsiyya Silsilas <n>`, versos `<n> A History of Sufism in India`, giving folios 213–228 across PDF pp. 233–248. All folios below are taken from `folio_map.tsv`, never computed. **One correction to that table's `source` column, and not to any of its values:** it marks pp. 235, 243 and 245 as `run`, but all three pages do print a running header — the detector was almost certainly defeated by OCR damage inside the chapter title itself, which reads `Si/silas` on p. 235 (folio 215), `Si/vilas` on p. 243 (folio 223), and carries a stray `;` before the numeral on p. 245 (folio 225). Folios 215, 223 and 225 are therefore header-attested, not interpolated.

**Section, measured from the book's own headers: the chunk lies wholly inside Chapter Three, "The Suhrawardiyya and the Firdawsiyya Silsilas", exactly as the brief states — no contradiction found.** Two of the chapter's internal section headings fall in range: **"Chishti and Suhrawardi ideologies and practices" opens at p. 236 (folio 216)** and **"The Firdawsiyya" opens at p. 246 (folio 226)**. The chunk begins mid-sentence ("headed a revolt against Sultan Muhammad bin Tughlugq [OCR?] [Tughluq]", p. 233, folio 213) and ends mid-narrative on Shaikh Sharafu'd-Din Abu Tawwama (p. 248, folio 228).

**Three archive rows are touched, and one of them is the row two earlier workers flagged as their weakest mapping.** `shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam` is settled here on the book's own evidence — Multan, a death date, and the descent from Bahaʾuʾd-Din Zakariyya — though **the book still never prints "Rukn-e-Alam"** (see Doubts). `shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya` and `shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar` are both dense. **`shrine-of-jalaluddin-surkh-posh-bukhari-jalaluddin-bukhari` and `shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht` are again NOT mapped, and this chunk gives a positive reason rather than mere silence** — see Doubts. This is also the richest ʿurs-and-langar passage the book has yielded so far (p. 247, folio 227).

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam** (the book calls him "Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din", once in the fuller form "Shaikhu'l-Islam Ruknu'l Haqq Wa'd-Din"; **it never prints "Rukn-e-Alam" and never prints "Abul Fath"** — both (not stated)): **Multan, a death date and the descent are all here, which is what the earlier runs lacked.** He is "his grandson", the grandson of Shaikh Bahaʾuʾd-Din Zakariyya (p. 236, folio 216); "Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din died in 735/1334-35" (p. 234, folio 214); and Multan is his seat throughout — the Sultan "Marching towards Multan he was met by Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din" (p. 233, folio 213).
- **shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam**: **he meets Sultan Muhammad bin Tughluq on the march and prophesies victory, and the Sultan performs the obeisance the chunk elsewhere makes a controversy.** "The Sultan kissed the Shaikh's feet and he in turn prophesied victory. The battle took place near Abuhar" (p. 233, folio 213).
- **shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam**: **his brother is killed as the Sultan's double in that battle.** "In a ruse to confuse the enemy the Sultan placed Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din's brother, Shaikh 'Imadu'd-Din, who closely resembled himself, under the royal canopy. 'Imadu'd-Din was killed" (p. 233, folio 213).
- **shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam**: **the single largest endowment recorded anywhere in this book so far, and it is a grant of villages to a khanqah.** "The Sultan awarded Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din one hundred villages for the upkeep of his khangah [OCR?] [khanqah]" (p. 233, folio 213). **No date, no names of villages and no deed are given** — (not stated).
- **shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam**: **his intercession in the massacre of Multan is reported two incompatible ways and the author rejects one of them.** The Sultan "flayed the Qazi alive and ordered a general massacre of the population. According to 'Isami, Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din retreated into meditation for seven days, while the inhabitants were massacred; and emerging from isolation, he then interceded with the Sultan in an effort to save further lives. This story, however, would appear apocryphal. Barani says the Shaikh approached the Sultan soon after he learnt of his intention to massacre the population, and that the Sultan relented on his request for clemency" (p. 233, folio 213).
- **shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam**: **an international reputation carried by merchants, and a dated visit by Ibn Battuta.** "Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din's fame reached as far as Alexandria, being spread by merchants who visited Multan, and Ibn Battuta was recommended to see him. In 1333, after reaching Multan Ibn Battuta stayed with one of the Shaikh's disciples, and it is recorded that he had an interesting conversation with Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din, which exter.d2d [OCR?] [extended] even to political matters" (p. 233, folio 213). **His khanqah as an institution: "Following the tradition set by his father and grandfather, the Shaikh's khangah [OCR?] [khanqah] was a busy rendezvous for distinguished visitors from many countries west of-the Indus"** (p. 233, folio 213). **"his father" is not named in this chunk** — (not stated).
- **shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam**: **his writings are recorded as lost.** "Shaikh 'Abdu'l-Haqq mentions a number of works containing the teachings of Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din, including his Malfuzat; none of these however still exist" (p. 233, folio 213).
- **shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam**: **his teaching, as summarised and as quoted.** He "exhorted his disciples to abandon cruelty, oppression, avarice and greed, as he believed such vices rendered human beings to the level of beasts. Self-purification came only through humility and prayer", citing the Qurʾan — "'I do not exculpate myself. Lo! the (human) soul enjoineth unto evil, save whereon my Lord hath mercy. Lo! My Lord is Forgiving and Merciful.'" (p. 233, folio 213); and "purification of the self in the final analysis depended on divine grace", on the verse "'Had it not been for the grace of Allah and His mercy unto you, not one of you would ever have grown pure.'", with "the mark of divine grace and mercy emanated from an insight into one's faults" (p. 234, folio 214).
- **shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam**: **the doctrine that a Shaikh should be rich, given in his own reported words — the chunk's most quotable defence of shrine wealth.** Shaikh Nasiruʾd-Din Chiragh Dihlawi relates: "When Shaikhu'l-Islam Ruknu'l Haqq Wa'd-Din visited Delhi, qgalandars [OCR?] [qalandars] and jwalgis [OCR?] [reading not resolved] would make demands of him, the former requesting sherbet and the latter, money. The Shaikhu'l-Islam would satisfy them and remark: 'The leaders of the community should possess three things. Firstly, they should have property in order to satisfy their demands… Secondly, the leaders should have knowledge inorder thatthey mighthave scholarly discussions with 'ulama.' Thirdly, they should be endowed with ha/ [OCR?] [hal] (mystical enlightenment) so that they might impress dervishes.'" — and the Chishti rebuttal, "Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din added that money was not necessary—only knowledge and hal were essential" (p. 234, folio 214). **Rizvi frames the anecdote as showing "the ideological differences between Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din and his Chishti counterparts"** (p. 234, folio 214).
- **shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam**: **he tolerated qalandars where his grandfather did not.** "Qalandars were intolerable to Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din but the attitude of his grandson, Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din, was different. He tolerated them as he considered that their persistent demands and those of other visitors justified sufi Shaikhs possessing wealth" (p. 236, folio 216).
- **shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam**: **the succession after him is disputed in the sources and ends in confiscation and an execution.** "According to Jamali, his nephew, Shaikh Isma'il, succeeded him. Ibn Battuta says that Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din nominated his grandson, Shaikh Hud, as his successor, but his nephew challenged the claim. The dispute was laid before Muhammad bin Tughluq who gave his verdict in favour of Shaikh Hud. After some time, the governor of Sind accused Shaikh Hud of misappropriating the income of Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din's khangah [OCR?] [khanqah] for his own personal use. The Sultan ordered the governor to seize the entire property and the Shaikh was reduced to a miserable condition" (p. 234, folio 214); Shaikh Hud was then "executed on a trumped-up charge of attempting to mastermind a Mongol invasion of India, using as a war-cry the ill-treatment of Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya's descendants by the Sultan" (p. 234, folio 214).
- **shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam**: **one of his disciples is followed in detail** — "Shaikh 'Usman Sayyah of Sunnam", enrolled "near Kilukhari when the latter was performing prayers on the banks of the Jumna", taught "the 'Awarifu'l-Ma'arif" at Multan, and "given the honour of being presented with Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din's own garment and turban" on returning from seven years' travel (p. 235, folio 215). **A robe-and-turban investiture, not a khirqa and not a document.**

- **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya** (the book writes "Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya", and once "Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din"): **no birth or death date anywhere in this chunk** — (not stated). **His khanqah is placed at Multan and its end is dated by an event, not a year.** "This marked the end of the prosperity of Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya's khanqah in Multan. The Suhrawardi silsila, however, started flourishing in other areas, while Multan became a mere shadow of its former glory" (p. 235, folio 215) — the referent of "This" is the execution of Shaikh Hud and the seizure of the khanqah property (p. 234, folio 214).
- **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya**: **the economy of his khanqah, stated twice and consistently.** "The prosperity of Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya's khanqah depended mainly on gifts from merchants and trade guilds, for even local governors were often short of funds. As many merchants traded outside India, to a large extent, they were independent of the government and could not be prevented from giving futuh" (p. 242, folio 222); and of the Suhrawardis generally, "They accepted state patronage, received huge gifts from merchants and artisan guilds, and treasured riches as future assets" (p. 236, folio 216).
- **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya**: **his hospitality was rationed, and his charity was fixed by him personally.** "The food in Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya's khangah [OCR?] [khanqah] was abundant, but was offered to only a select few. Often the Shaikh ordered his servants to pay fixed amounts in charity to those he considered deserving and only the sum he specified was paid" (p. 236, folio 216).
- **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya**: **he refused qalandars.** "Qalandars were intolerable to Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din" (p. 236, folio 216).
- **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya**: **he barred prostration before himself and required prayers first — directly relevant to what is done at his shrine.** "Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya did not encourage his disciples to perform sajda. They greeted him with: as-salam alaikum (Peace be with you). He also expected his disciples to finish their obligatory religious duties first and to greet him afterwards" (p. 240, folio 220).
- **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya**: **the negative on faith-cures is explicit, and it is an author's inference.** "Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya was concerned only with the élite and it is unlikely that people visited his khangah [OCR?] [khanqah] for faith cures. By contrast, the Chishti jama'at-khanas were filled with people wanting amulets" (p. 240, folio 220). **Note the hedge "it is unlikely" — Rizvi does not cite a source for this.**
- **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya**: **his food doctrine, quoted from the Qurʾan.** "Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya was a lively host and followed literally the Qur'anic injunction: 'eat of what is pure and act righteously.'" (p. 237, folio 217).
- **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya**: **his descendants are named as a political grievance after his time** — Shaikh Hud's alleged war-cry was "the ill-treatment of Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya's descendants by the Sultan" (p. 234, folio 214).

- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar** (the book writes "Baba Farid", "the Baba", and in one first-person quotation "Shaikh Faridu'd-Din", identified with Baba Farid by the anecdote itself; **it prints no death date, no Ajodhan, no Pakpattan and no silsila title beyond "That famous Chishti saint"** — all (not stated)): **a hair of his beard is used as a working amulet, in Shaikh Nizamuʾd-Din Auliyaʾ's own reported words.** "Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya' related that even a hair from the beard of Baba Farid was used by him as an amulet"; the quotation runs "One day I was sitting with Shaikh Faridu'd-Din. A hair from his beard fell on his lap. I requested the Shaikh to give it to me so that I could preserve it as a ta'wiz (amulet). He ordered me to do so. I… kept the hair in a piece of cloth and brought it to Delhi. That hair embodied a great blessing. Whenever a sick person approached me for an amulet, I gave the hair to him. He would keep it for a few days and be cured" (p. 240, folio 220). **A named relic, a named use and a named failure — see Legends.**
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **he permitted prostration before the pir, and Nizamuʾd-Din could not abolish it because of him.** Nizamuʾd-Din "believed the practice should cease, but found himself unable to oppose the traditions followed by Shaikh Qutbu'd-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki and Shaikh Faridu'd-Din, who had both permitted prostration" (p. 239, folio 219). **This is the chunk's single most load-bearing statement about devotional practice at a Chishti saint's feet.**
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **his rejection of state patronage is made the reason the Chishti order survived, and the reason it took no land.** "However, Baba Farid, through his total rejection of government patronage, averted the crisis threatening the Chishti order and succeeded in convincing his disciples that the wisest course was not to accept any form of land grant. Holiness could only be obtained, said the Baba, by ignoring princes" (p. 244, folio 224).
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **but the rule bound only khalifas, and he accepted that most disciples served the state.** "But non-involvement in government service was not compulsory for ordinary disciples, and applied only to khalifas. According to Baba Farid, association with rulers and noblemen was disastrous for spiritualists but that those who were connected with the government in some way were in the overwhelming majority" (p. 244, folio 224), footnoted "Counsel to Sidi Maula, Tarikh-i Firuz Shahi, p. 209" (p. 244, folio 224).
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **he recited verses of counsel before a prince.** "The verses recited by Baba Farid before Ulugh Beg, reminded him of Firidun's previous rule of justice and advocated a high degree of tolerance. In advising him to appoint a God-fearing vizier, Baba Farid possibly had in mind the Saljuqid vizier, Nizamu'l-Mulk Tusi" (p. 244, folio 224). **"possibly had in mind" is Rizvi's conjecture, not the source's.**
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **his own family served the army, which the book sets against the doctrine without comment.** "Baba Farid's favourite son was a soldier in Balban's army" (p. 243, folio 223); and a governor of Lakhnauti "resigned the service and became Baba Farid's disciple" (p. 243, folio 223).
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **a curse-like anecdote is told of him and then repudiated by the author.** He "had given a gift to a dervish and then ordered him to go away"; the dervish asked for his comb and predicted prosperity for him, and "Baba Farid replied that he had thrown in the river whatever prosperity was wished on him. Going to bathe in a neighbouring shallow river, the dervish was drowned" (p. 248, folio 228). Rizvi: "the story of the cause of the death of the dervishes and the sons of 'Imad is incompatible with more factual evidence which depicts the kindliness of both Baba Farid and Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya'" (p. 248, folio 228).

**No other archive row is touched in pp. 233–248 (folios 213–228).** Explicit negatives, because the chunk names a great many saints: **Hinglaj does not appear**, so the empty-id defect on the row `Shaktipeeth Shri Hinglaj Mata Mandir` does not arise (p. 233 to p. 248, folios 213–228). **No Hindu deity is named anywhere** — Hindus appear only as a collective (pp. 245, 246, folios 225, 226) and as "the Hindu traders of Gujarat" (p. 242, folio 222) — so the unruled deity question does not arise either. **Multan's other archive rows get nothing**: Shah Shams-ud-Din Sabzwari, Shah Yusuf Gardez, Syed Musa Pak, Hafiz Muhammad Jamal Multani and Shah Ali Akbar Shamsi are not named (pp. 233–248, folios 213–228). **Lahore appears twice but with no saint, shrine or tomb attached** (pp. 241–242, folios 221–222) — see Doubts under the interim toponym convention.

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- **Shaikh Nizamuʾd-Din Auliyaʾ** — the chunk's most frequently cited figure and the hinge of most of its argument. He befriended Shaikh ʿUsman (p. 235, folio 215); favoured celibacy from his time onward (p. 238, folio 218); tried and failed to end prostration (p. 239, folio 219); defended sajda in a long first-person story (p. 239, folio 219); told Sultan ʿAlaʾuʾd-Din Khalji "that being a dervish he was unconcerned with government affairs, and instead was occupied in praying for and the welfare of the Sultan and his people" (p. 244, folio 224); accepted "enormous gifts given to him by Khusraw Barwar" and gifts "from princes like Khizr Khan" on the reasoning "that money belonging to the treasury should be spent on the Muslim community" (p. 244, folio 224); doubled his jamaʿat-khana's expenditure when the Sultan tried to starve it (p. 242, folio 222); and "had previously told Sultan 'Ala'u'd-Din that he was unconditionally prepared to leave Delhi if his stay in the capital was unacceptable to the Sultan" and "appointed an outstanding Khalifa to 'Ala'u'd-Din's army which was involved in an invasion of Chanderi and blessed the Deccan campaign" (p. 245, folio 225). **No death date, no tomb and no Delhi shrine** — (not stated).
- **Shaikh Nasiruʾd-Din Chiragh Dihlawi / Chiragh-i Dihli / Shaikh Nasiruʾd-Din Mahmud** — three forms of one name in this chunk (pp. 234, 238, 247; folios 214, 218, 227). He defined "a khangah [OCR?] [khanqah] as a place of worship and prayer" (p. 236, folio 216); chose celibacy and ate "sambhalu leaves and citrous fruits" and "excessive amounts of lemon juice" against desire (p. 238, folio 218); compromised on prostration — "placing the forehead on the ground before created beings was not permissible, but one could kiss the ground with the lips" (p. 240, folio 220); told the cultivator story (p. 241, folio 221); reported ʿAlaʾuʾd-Din Khalji's price control on the authority of "Qazi Hamidu'd-Din Maliku't-Tujjar (head of the merchant guilds)" (p. 242, folio 222); told a civil servant "that his position gave him an opportunity to serve mankind" (p. 243, folio 223); and held "that rulers were appointed to people as they themselves deserved" (p. 244, folio 224). See Doubts on the `shrine-of-baba-shah-chiragh` name collision.
- **Shaikh Jalaluʾd-Din Tabrizi** — "Shaikh Jalalu'd-Din Tabrizi advised a sufi to eat three times a day and to use the strength gained from the food for prayers and abstention from sin" (p. 237, folio 217); "Jalalu'd-Din Tabrizi lived in the company of a handsome slave boy in order to stabilize his ecstasy" (p. 237, folio 217); and, as "Shaikh Jalalu'd-Din", the subject of a conversion legend in Bengal — "The Suhrawardis, as depicted in the legend surrounding Shaikh Jalalu'd-Din's activities in Bengal, were unhesitating in their enforced conversion of Hindus to Islam" (p. 246, folio 226). **Nisba Tabrizi, region Bengal, no dates, no Uch, no tomb** — see Doubts, where this is the reason I map neither Uch row.
- **Khwaja Qutbuʾd-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki** — "Shaikh Qutbu'd-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki", who had "permitted prostration" (p. 239, folio 219); "Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki advised his disciples to write the different names of God or Qur'anic verses on amulets and give them to those who requested them" (p. 240, folio 220); and a chronological anchor — Khwaja Badruʾd-Din Samarqandi "settled in Delhi after Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki had migrated there" (p. 246, folio 226). **Not Aibak, not mapped** — see Doubts.
- **Shaikh ʿUsman Sayyah of Sunnam** — "The son of one Qazi Wajihu'd-Din, in his youth he had been a petty official"; "became a great ascetic, owning nothing but a loin-cloth"; went to Mecca "without carrying even the basic necessities of a pilgrim—a staff and a water pot", stayed "about a year and then continued travelling to other places for a further six years" (p. 235, folio 215); "died there in 738/1337-38" at Delhi (p. 235, folio 215).
- **Khwaja Badruʾd-Din Samarqandi** — "the founder of the si/sila [OCR?] [silsila] in India" of the Firdawsiyya, sent by Bakharzi "from Samaqand [OCR?] [Samarqand]" to "settle permanently in Delhi" (pp. 246, 247; folios 226, 227); "Relations between Khwaja Badru'd-Din and the Delhi Chishtis were amicable"; he "was very fond of sama'"; "The date of Shaikh Badru'd-Din's death is unknown but probably it was during the reign of Sultan 'Ala'u'd-Din Khalji"; "Apparently the Khwaja had migrated to Delhi as a youth and survived to a ripe old age" (pp. 247, 248; folios 227, 228). **Death date explicitly unknown to the book** — (not stated).
- **Shaikh Saifuʾd-Din Bakharzi** — "an eminent disciple of Shaikh Najmu'd-Din Kubra", from whom "The silsila traced its spiritual descent" (p. 246, folio 226); converted from hostility to sufism at a lecture, "started screaming and tearing his clothes and then fell at the feet of the man who was to become his pir"; "Shaikh Naymu'd-Din [OCR?] [Najmu'd-Din] sent him to Bukhara and prophesied his future fame in that city" (pp. 246–247, folios 226–227); "Shaikh Saifu'd-Din Bakharzi died in 658/1260" (p. 247, folio 227). His ʿurs is the chunk's key practice passage — see below. Footnote gloss on the place: "In the tenth century, Bakharz also known as Guwakharz was a famous town south of Jam on the river Hari" (p. 246, folio 226).
- **Shaikh Najmuʾd-Din Kubra** — named as Bakharzi's pir (p. 246, folio 226), and the protagonist of the conversion story (pp. 246–247, folios 226–227).
- **Berke, Mongol Khan of the Golden Horde** — "Berke (1257-67), the Mongol Khan of the Golden Horde, who was a grandson of Chingiz Khan and the ruler of Transoxiana, was converted in Bukhara by Saifu'd-Din Bakharzi" (p. 247, folio 227); "Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din Mahmud refers to him by both his pre-Islamic name, Khar Banda, and his Islamic one, Khuda-banda" (p. 247, folio 227).
- **Khwaja Ruknuʾd-Din and Khwaja Najibuʾd-Din** — "Khwaja Badru'd-Din was succeeded by two khAalifas [OCR?] [khalifas], Khwaja Ruknu'd-Din (who lived near Kilukhari), and Khwaja Najibu'd-Din" (p. 248, folio 228). **This Khwaja Ruknuʾd-Din is a different man from the Multan Shaikh Ruknuʾd-Din mapped above** — see Doubts. "Najibu'd-Din Firdawsi preferred to lead the quiet life of an ascetic, however, his disciples made him famous" (p. 248, folio 228).
- **Shaikh ʿImad and his two sons** — "The two sons of Shaikh 'Imad, Ruknu'd-Din's successor, were so envious of Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya' that they even condemned him publicly"; both drowned near the langar (p. 248, folio 228).
- **Maulana Fariduʾd-Din** — a disciple of Najibuʾd-Din who "in 777/1375—76, compiled a book on Sunni jurisprudence called the Fatawa-i Tatar Khaniyya", footnoted "Tatar Khan was a noble of Sultan Firuz Shah's reign" (p. 248, folio 228). **A third Fariduʾd-Din in this book** — see Doubts.
- **Shaikh Sharafuʾd-Din Ahmad Yahyaʾ Munyari** — "The most outstanding of all the disciples", "whose ancestors had settled in Bihar in the early thirteenth century"; "The Shaikh's own name was Ahmad, his father's Yahya', and he was given the title, Sharafu'd-Din (Glory of the Faith). Born at the end of Sha'ban 661 /early July 1263"; he "regretted what he believed to be the futility of such a non-spiritual education, wishing instead he had devoted the time to memorizing the Qur'an" (p. 248, folio 228). Footnote on his town: "This town is now known as Maner or Muner but the Sharaf Nama-i Ahmad Munyari rhymes only when the last word is read as Munyari or Manyari" (p. 248, folio 228).
- **Shaikh Sharafuʾd-Din Abu Tawwama** (also printed "Abu Tawama" on the same page) — "passed through Munyar en route to Sunargaon in Bengal. It was during the reign of Balban, and the reasons for Abu Tawama's emigration to the distant east are shrouded in mystery. He came from Bukhara, having obtained an excellent religious education" (p. 248, folio 228). **The chunk breaks off here.**
- **Qazi Hamiduʾd-Din Nagauri** — "The works of the Suhrawardi, Qazi Hamidu'd-Din Nagauri, aroused the interest of all Chishti pirs and were read with interest" (p. 236, folio 216). **Distinct from "Qazi Hamidu'd-Din Maliku't-Tujjar (head of the merchant guilds)" of p. 242 (folio 222)** — see Doubts.
- **Khwaja Muʾinuʾd-Din and Shaikh Hamiduʾd-Din** — "Khwaja Mu'inu'd-Din advocated that within the realm of love there must be both trinity and unity, that is, 'Lover, Love and Beloved are all one,' and Shaikh Hamidu'd-Din wrote 'Ishqiyya in this vein" (p. 237, folio 217). **No Ajmer, no dates, no tombs** — (not stated).
- **Nuruʾd-Din Mubarak Ghaznawi** — the Suhrawardi theorist of the state (p. 245, folio 225).
- **Maulana Burhanuʾd-Din Nasafi** — "both a scholar and a sufi", who accepted a disciple on three conditions, the third being that outside the house the disciple "should not kiss his hand or feet but greet him with the words Salam alaik' (peace be with you)" (p. 239, folio 219).

- **Khwajgi Khujandi** — "a friend of Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya' and a merchant from Delhi", who "sold coarse cloth for the use of dervishes, and did not trade in expensive cloth as used by the Turks and the military. Even this had made him very rich"; he "used to give something to the beggars he met and drop sugar and sesame seeds into ant holes" (p. 241, folio 221).
- **Saiyid Muhammad bin Mahmud of Kirman** — "the grandfather of Amir Khwurd", one of the "highly successful merchants" who "exchanged their comfort and wealth for the hard life of a dervish" (p. 242, folio 222).
- **Amir Hasan and Amir Khusraw** — "distinguished disciples of Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya'… notable members of the government" (p. 243, folio 223); Amir Hasan is also "the Shaikhu'l-Masha'ikh's favourite gawwal [OCR?] [qawwal]" who sang for Shaikh ʿUsman (p. 235, folio 215), and the disputant on prostration and on interrupting prayers (pp. 239, 240; folios 219, 220). **The book does not say whether the qawwal and the disciple are the same man** — see Doubts.

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **A grant of one hundred villages to endow a khanqah — the largest concrete endowment the book has given so far.** "The Sultan awarded Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din one hundred villages for the upkeep of his khangah [OCR?] [khanqah]" (p. 233, folio 213).
- **Confiscation of a khanqah's entire property by the state, on a charge of misappropriating its income.** "the governor of Sind accused Shaikh Hud of misappropriating the income of Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din's khangah [khanqah] for his own personal use. The Sultan ordered the governor to seize the entire property" (p. 234, folio 214). **Followed by the execution of the incumbent** (p. 234, folio 214) **and by the collapse of the Multan establishment** (p. 235, folio 215).
- **A contested succession with three claimants and a royal adjudication — a sajjada-nashin dispute in all but name.** Nephew Shaikh Ismaʿil (per Jamali) against grandson Shaikh Hud (per Ibn Battuta), "The dispute was laid before Muhammad bin Tughluq who gave his verdict in favour of Shaikh Hud" (p. 234, folio 214). **The book gives no title for the office and does not use the word sajjada** — (not stated).
- **ʿUrs, langar and samaʿ together, in Delhi, for a saint buried elsewhere — the single most valuable passage in this chunk for the archive.** "The Khwaja was very fond of sama' and each 'urs, or death anniversary of Shaikh Saifu'd-Din Bakharzi was celebrated with great enthusiasm by them both. Food was freely distributed from Chishtiyya and Firdawsiyya /angars [OCR?] [langars] and dervishes assembled in large numbers" (p. 247, folio 227). **Two orders' langars operating at one ʿurs; "them both" = Khwaja Badruʾd-Din and Shaikh Nizamuʾd-Din Auliyaʾ. No place of burial, no shrine building and no numbers are given** — (not stated). A langar is mentioned once more, as a place beside water: one of ʿImad's sons "had gone to bathe near the /angar [langar] and had started to drown" (p. 248, folio 228).
- **Khanqah versus jamaʿat-khana, defined as an architectural and social distinction.** "Jama'at-khanas were an integral part of Chishti khangahs [khanqahs] and were designed to provide hostel accommodation for a large number of dervishes. Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din defined a khangah [khanqah] as a place of worship and prayer but in reality the purpose of jama'at-khanas was identical. The Chishtis, however, preferred to call their hostels jama'at-khanas, as they were generally thatched assembly halls… the jama'at-khanas were connected to the living quarters of the pirs. In Chishti mystic literature both words, khangah [khanqah] and jama'at-khana, are interchangeable. In contrast, the Suhrawardis built impressive khanqahs" (p. 236, folio 216). **"thatched assembly halls" against "impressive khanqahs" is the chunk's only architectural contrast, and neither is dated or located.**
- **The Suhrawardi economy, stated as a policy.** "They accepted state patronage, received huge gifts from merchants and artisan guilds, and treasured riches as future assets" (p. 236, folio 216) — against the Chishti practice on the same page, "The Chishtis offered food to all guests and, if necessary, did not hesitate to sell their few miserable belongings to pay for it. If they had nothing to offer, a glass of water would be presented accompanied by their apologies" (p. 236, folio 216).
- **Access control: who may enter.** "Chishti jama'at-khanas were open to all. Yogis, qalandars, and others were allowed free access" (p. 236, folio 216) — against Bahaʾuʾd-Din Zakariyya's food "offered to only a select few" (p. 236, folio 216) and his intolerance of qalandars (p. 236, folio 216).
- **Futuh as the shared income of both orders, and a state attempt to cut it off.** Merchants "were the sufis' main source of futuh" (p. 242, folio 222); "The same source supplied futuh to the jama'at-khana of Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya'", and when Sultan Qutbuʾd-Din Mubarak Shah Khalji "forbade his officials to visit the jama'at-khana, hoping that the Shaikh's charities would cease and members of the jama'at-khana would starve to death… the Shaikh learned of the Sultan's action he ordered the expenditure of the jama'at-khana to be doubled" (p. 242, folio 222).
- **Refusal of land grants as Chishti policy, and its rationale.** "Chishtis refused to accept land grants either from rulers or their officers as this also tended to compromise them in their attempts to maintain complete independence from all powers other than God" (p. 243, folio 223); and the general policy, government service "made people authoritarian, reckless, greedy and cruel and… involved a great deal of dependence on worldly authorities and was therefore contrary to the sufi trust in God" (p. 243, folio 223).
- **Cash gifts accepted where land was refused.** Nizamuʾd-Din's acceptance of Khusraw Barwar's "enormous gifts" implies "that the Chishtis were unconcerned with the source of the gift provided it was paid in cash", justified on the ground "that money belonging to the treasury should be spent on the Muslim community"; "The gifts were immediately distributed and without them the Chishtis would have not been able to assist the poor" (p. 244, folio 224). **Cash in, cash out, no accumulation — the explicit opposite of the Suhrawardi rule on the same subject** (p. 236, folio 216).
- **Amulets as a service to the public, prescribed by a named Shaikh.** "Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki advised his disciples to write the different names of God or Qur'anic verses on amulets and give them to those who requested them" (p. 240, folio 220); "the Chishti jama'at-khanas were filled with people wanting amulets" (p. 240, folio 220); and the relic-amulet of Baba Farid's beard hair (p. 240, folio 220).
- **Prostration before the pir: the practice, the objection, the defence and the compromise.** "the Chishti practice of kissing the feet of, and prostration before pirs… shocked their orthodox opponents. Sajda or prostration is an act of worship in which a man's forehead touches the ground" (p. 238, folio 218); it "was not invented by the Indian Chishtis. It was even sanctioned by Shaikh Abu Sa'id bin Abi'l Khair who argued that prostration was designed to show humility before the Shaikh and that this invariably raised the spiritual status of the disciple" (p. 238, folio 218); Nizamuʾd-Din's Qurʾanic defence, ending "Although sajda is not obligatory it is not illegal. There can be no question of prohibiting what is legally permitted" (p. 239, folio 219); Rizvi's verdict, "The argument was unconvincing and the Shaikh's disciples continued to prostrate themselves" (p. 239, folio 219); the alternative "placing of the head on the ground and in this instance the word sajda was not used", rejected by the orthodox (p. 240, folio 220); and Nasiruʾd-Din's compromise of kissing the ground with the lips (p. 240, folio 220). **Also the rule on interrupting prayer: Amir Hasan held "the disciple should abandon them to kiss the pir's feet", Nizamuʾd-Din "disagreed and believed that prayers should be finished first"** (p. 240, folio 220).
- **The zikr technique of the Chishtis, with its yogic component named.** "contacts with yogis added a new dimension to zikr and involved the strenuous co-ordination of limb movements and postures associated with alternate exhalation and inhalation. During zikr, there was a concentration on the utterance of the first part of al-kalimat at-tayyiba (the blessed phrase) or the phrase of shahada (testimony), /a Ilaha Illa'llah [OCR?] [la Ilaha Illa'llah]" (p. 237, folio 217); and the preliminary — "to imagine that his Shaikh was personally present before him, directing his contemplation. The practice amounted to a belief that the Shaikh's spirit was divine both in its emanation and power" (p. 238, folio 218).
- **Samaʿ, order by order.** "Sama' was an indispensable part of Chishti rituals. It sharpened mystical sensitivity, and the trance-like state or ecstasy known as hal offered the spirits and hearts of its participant's mystical experiences which could never be gained through salat or zikr. The Suhrawardis discouraged sama' but were unable to reject it totally. Many eminent Suhrawardis indulged in it enthusiastically" (p. 237, folio 217); **but with a regional exception: "it would appear that the use of sama' by the Suhrawardis was generally not permitted in Multan"** (p. 238, folio 218). **The Multan restriction is the one place-specific practice rule in the chunk.**
- **A samaʿ procession under a state ban, with a count of participants and a distance.** Shaikh ʿUsman persuaded Amir Hasan to sing "At the same time as Sultan Ghiyasu'd-Din Tughluq issued strict orders prohibiting musicians from singing at sama' gatherings or elsewhere"; "When the khangah [khanqah] doors were unlocked about 200 gawwals [OCR?] [qawwals], and a large number of sufis, were standing outside. They set off to Tughluqabad about three miles away, singing and dancing" (p. 235, folio 215). **A number (about 200) and a distance (about three miles) that can be cited.**
- **Fasting and diet as order markers.** "The Suhrawardis sought to perfect themselves through sal/at [OCR?] [salat] (Islamic prayers) and zikr. To them fasting in the month of Ramazan was sufficient"; Jalaluʾd-Din Tabrizi's three meals a day; "By contrast the Chishtis supplemented salat with hard ascetic exercises and fasting. In order to avoid making fasts a regular habit, the Chishtis often fasted on alternate days" (p. 237, folio 217).
- **Celibacy as a Chishti preference dated from a named Shaikh.** "From the time of Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya', celibacy became a desirable aspect of Chishti discipline… He believed that marriage was permissible for a sufi, but that celibacy called for a degree of determination and was therefore preferable" (p. 238, folio 218).
- **Trade, crafts and agriculture endorsed, with an ethics attached.** "The Chishtis encouraged people to indulge in trade and commerce for their livelihood. They also approved of agriculture and the pursuit of crafts. However, these occupations were to be followed honestly and were not permitted to interfere with spiritual exercises" (p. 241, folio 221); zakat defined in the text as "alms given according to Muslim law, by way of purifying or securing a blessing to all of one's possession" (p. 241, folio 221); and the pricing rule — a merchant "should never lie about the price paid by him for any article, but should specify the correct price and be satisfied with a small profit which, in the long run, would make him rich" (p. 241, folio 221).
- **State price control approved by a Chishti Shaikh.** "On the authority of Qazi Hamidu'd-Din Maliku't-Tujjar (head of the merchant guilds), Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din told his disciples that the Sultan had introduced price control for the welfare of his subjects" (p. 242, folio 222).
- **Taxation.** "Tax collectors they believed should show consideration in acquiring revenue and in levying the poll tax" (p. 246, folio 226).
- **Spheres of influence as an inter-order territorial settlement.** "There was no apparent rivalry between the Chishti and Suhrawardi saints. An early sufi tradition based on the example of the Apostles in the Apocryphal New Testament, had to wisely divide different regions of the sub-continent into spheres of their respective spiritual influence and to refrain from interfering with those of others" (p. 236, folio 216).
- **One shared textbook for the whole field.** "The early Chishti preceptors were scholars, but they did not commit their ideas to writing and the Suhrawardi manual, the 'Awarifu'l-Ma'arif, was the only text book used by all sufis" (p. 236, folio 216); it was also what ʿUsman was taught at Multan (p. 235, folio 215) and the mould of mystic ideas "Until the mid-fourteenth century" (p. 237, folio 217).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

Attributions are the book's own, including its hedges ("it is recorded", "Apparently", "were recorded as ascribing", "would appear apocryphal").

- **A prophecy of victory sealed by the Sultan kissing the Shaikh's feet** — "The Sultan kissed the Shaikh's feet and he in turn prophesied victory" (p. 233, folio 213).
- **Seven days' meditation during a massacre, then intercession — a story Rizvi rejects.** "According to 'Isami, Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din retreated into meditation for seven days… This story, however, would appear apocryphal" (p. 233, folio 213).
- **A relic that heals and then hides itself.** Baba Farid's beard hair cured the sick, but "Once my friend Taju'd-Din Multani's child became ill. He approached me for the amulet. I had placed the hair in a niche but I could not find it… his son died of the illness. After a few days someone else approached me for the amulet, to my surprise it was in its usual niche" — with Rizvi's gloss, "The moral of this story is that the hair could not be found as the child was destined to die" (pp. 240–241, folios 220–221).
- **A cultivator with power over rain, and Ghazali as his visitor.** "In the days of Hujjatu'l-Islam Ghazali there was a cultivator who could attain ecstatic states and perform miracles. If he wished, it rained, if he did not, it was dry" (p. 241, folio 221).
- **Two merchant miracles.** A sunken bale "was discovered in the middle of weeds, the contents spouting in the water" after Khwajgi said "that he had paid zakat… on the goods so he would not lose anything"; and "a prophecy by Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya' restored Khwajgi Khujandi's purse which had been stolen by his slave" (p. 241, folio 221).
- **A conviction that a Shaikh's money was not of this world.** "Many gullible Muslims, however, continued to believe that the Shaikh's funds came from other than earthly sources" (p. 242, folio 222). **"gullible" is Rizvi's word.**
- **Abu Saʿid's descending kisses, given in a footnote and marked as late.** The disciple kissed knee, foot, hoof and finally the ground, "so that he might gain in spiritual status. Although the source of this story is a late one, it is typical of Shaikh Abu Sa'id's teachings" (p. 238, folio 218).
- **A conversion by dream-vision of a saint.** Berke "embraced Islam after having had a vision in which Shaikh Bakharzi appeared to him and suggested he become a Muslim" (p. 247, folio 227).
- **A snake spared because it came to listen.** "a snake appeared while Shaikh Bakharzi was speaking to an audience. Members of the group rushed to kill it but the Shaikh prevented them from doing so, saying that the reptile also wished to listen to the words of a sage" (p. 247, folio 227), footnoted as also told of Abu Saʿid (p. 247, folio 227).
- **Two drownings read as a saint's retaliation, and the reading repudiated.** "Just as Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din finished the story, news arrived that the sons of 'Imad Firdawsi had been drowned… Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din's disciples were recorded as ascribing the incident to miraculous elements emanating from their pir. However, the story… is incompatible with more factual evidence which depicts the kindliness of both Baba Farid and Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya'" (p. 248, folio 228).
- **A prophecy of a city's destruction by Hindu merchants** — the Gujarat traders "prophesied that a town where such dishonesty was rife, would be soon destroyed", and the *Fawaʾidu'l-Fuʾad* "went to the extent of ascribing the destruction of Lahore, by the Mongols in 1241, to the profiteering of its merchants" (pp. 241–242, folios 221–222).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Rizvi's governing contrast in this chunk: the two orders differ less in doctrine than in politics and ritual.** "Both orders trained their followers to pursue the conventional mystic path, which began with tauba (repentance), under the guidance of a pir (preceptor)… The differences between the two orders lay in their distinct rituals and ceremonies and, more significantly, in their attitudes to society and politics" (p. 237, folio 217).
- **A dating claim about Wahdat al-Wujud in India.** "The Wahdat al-Wujud of Ibn al-'Arabi was introduced to India through the Suhrawardi, 'Iraqi, however, by this time had not yet penetrated deeply into Chishti or Suhrawardi ideology" (p. 237, folio 217); the love-language of Muʿinuʾd-Din and Hamiduʾd-Din "were expressions of ecstasy rather than an advocacy of the Wahdat al-Wujud. Their source was the Tamhidat of 'Ainu'l-Quzat Hamadani" (p. 237, folio 217).
- **Two rival state theories, with the Suhrawardi one condemned.** "The Suhrawardi view of the function of the state, envisaged by Nuru'd-Din Mubarak Ghaznawi, encompassed the prosperity of the Sunni élite alone; the non-orthodox Sunnis, Shi'is and Hindus were permitted to survive, provided they did so in a deprived economic condition. This concept was an unimaginative replica of the ideas of Nizamu'l-Mulk Tusi, and ignored the situation of the Muslims at that time in India" (p. 245, folio 225); against "The Chishtis believed that governments should strive for the prosperity of the whole empire" (p. 245, folio 225) and "to the Chishtis the state was morally bound to care for the well-being and prosperity of all its people" (p. 246, folio 226).
- **Conversion: forced by the Suhrawardis, refused by the Chishtis — Rizvi's sharpest inter-order judgement.** "The Suhrawardis, as depicted in the legend surrounding Shaikh Jalalu'd-Din's activities in Bengal, were unhesitating in their enforced conversion of Hindus to Islam. By contrast, the Chishtis believed that only the company of pious and ascetic Muslims prompted others to accept Islam, and neither the sword nor preaching served any purpose. The Chishtis were interested in various yoga practices, particularly those connected with breathing, and refused to force Hindus to renounce their faith" (p. 246, folio 226), with the Chishti mission "to work for the integration of those Hindus who embraced Islam for political and economic reasons… and save them from emulating the example of the racist and élitist Turkic governing classes" (p. 246, folio 226). **"racist and élitist" is Rizvi's own characterisation, unsourced.**
- **A source rejected by name on the Berke conversion.** "The statement by Minhaj Siraj, the author of the Tabagat-i Nasiri [Tabaqat-i Nasiri] that Berke was brought up from infancy as a Muslim, is hardly feasible. I: appears [OCR?] [It appears] he was prompted to embrace Islam for political considerations" (p. 247, folio 227), the political reading being to "set himself apart from his Shamanist and Buddhist brethren" and to "reorganize the Tatars through a process of Islamization" against "the Mongol Ilkhans of Iran" (p. 247, folio 227).
- **Politics, not cruelty, is made the cause of the Chishti–Tughluq rupture.** "The final conflict was precipitated not by the cruelty of Sultan Muhammad bin Tughlug [Tughluq], for he was extremely generous to many sufis, but by the activities of the Delhi Chishtis who during his reign appeared to be associated with members of the 'ulama', hostile to government policies" (p. 245, folio 225); and the khalifas who refused to migrate to Daulatabad "were not necessarily following the traditions of Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya'" (p. 245, folio 225).
- **Political conditions, not doctrine, are made the cause of Chishti aloofness.** "Political conditions in India during the 13th and 14th centuries contributed a great deal towards reinforcing the Chishti policy of aloofness from rulers and their officials", with Iltutmish supporting the sufis "In order to maintain a balance between the power of the 'ulama' and the Turkic military oligarchy", and the ʿulamaʾ gaining supremacy after his death because "they changed sides according to self interest, and generally managed to support the victor" (pp. 243–244, folios 223–224).
- **Chishti political theory assimilated to Ghazali.** Their attitude "was similar to Ghazali's, who advised Muslims to obey unquestionably whoever seized power as this was indispensable for the preservation of government, law and order" (p. 244, folio 224); "This would indicate that the Chishtis believed the Sultanate was not necessarily evil" (p. 244, folio 224).
- **An inference the author flags as an inference.** "Although the story does not specifically state it, the implication is that the Lahore merchants were Muslims. Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya' was unhesitating in his praise for the honesty of the Hindu merchants of Gujarat, in spite of the fact that merchants were the sufis' main source of futuh" (p. 242, folio 222).
- **A shared sufi goal stated for both orders.** "Both the Suhrawardis and the Chishtis sought to achieve the sufi goal of jana' [OCR?] [fana'] and baga' [OCR?] [baqa'] or fana 'an al-fana' (annihilation beyond annihilation) as defined by masters such as Junaid, Ghazali, Abu Sa'id bin Abi'l Khair and 'Ainu'l-Quzat Hamadani. Like Ghazali they believed there were mysteries beyond the stages of fana' and baga' [baqa'] but members of both orders preferred to refrain from its expression" (p. 236, folio 216).
- **The Firdawsiyya placed on the Suhrawardi tree and given a regional history.** "The Firdawsiyya order was part of the collateral line of the Suhrawardiyya… The Firdawsis, however, were unable to make a mark in Delhi. Late in the fourteenth century they established themselves in Bihar, a move which was to prove most successful" (p. 246, folio 226).

## Doubts

**The chunk carries no transcriber apparatus — no `[OCR?]`, no `[illegible]`, no `[folio N]` lines — so every inline flag above is mine. This is a tesseract route, so the marker is `[OCR?]` throughout and never `[text-layer?]`. The damage classes are inventoried below as well as marked inline, per interim ruling (b).**

**Archive-mapping doubts, most important first:**

1. **`shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam` — I mapped it, and this chunk is the first to supply what the earlier workers said was missing, but the name itself is still absent.** The book prints "Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din" and once "Shaikhu'l-Islam Ruknu'l Haqq Wa'd-Din" (p. 234, folio 214). **It never prints "Rukn-e-Alam" and never prints "Abul Fath"** — the row's principal figure is "Shah Rukn-e-Alam (Rukn-ud-Din Abul Fath)". What the book does supply: **Multan as his seat** (pp. 233, 235; folios 213, 215), **"died in 735/1334-35"** (p. 234, folio 214) against the row's `figure_died` 1335, and **"his grandson", i.e. grandson of Bahaʾuʾd-Din Zakariyya** (p. 236, folio 216), which matches the row's Suhrawardi silsila and Multan location. **On the brief's own test — Multan, a date, or the title — two of the three are met, and I record the mapping as now well-evidenced rather than weak. A human should still confirm that Rizvi's Multani Shaikh Ruknuʾd-Din is the archive's Shah Rukn-e-Alam, since the book itself never makes the equation.** Note the death year disagreement is only apparent: 735 AH is given by the book as 1334-35, the row says 1335; **I record the book's, and do not reconcile.**
2. **"Ruknuʾd-Din" is two different men inside this one chunk, and the second one is easy to mis-map.** The Multan Shaikh Ruknuʾd-Din above (pp. 233–236, folios 213–216) is not "Khwaja Ruknu'd-Din (who lived near Kilukhari)", a Firdawsi khalifa of Khwaja Badruʾd-Din at Delhi, whose successor was Shaikh ʿImad (p. 248, folio 228). **The Kilukhari Khwaja Ruknuʾd-Din takes no id.** Compare the Qutbuʾd-Din trap: in this chunk "Qutbuʾd-Din" is likewise two men, **Khwaja Qutbuʾd-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki** (pp. 239, 240, 246; folios 219, 220, 226) and **Sultan Qutbuʾd-Din Mubarak Shah Khalji** (p. 242, folio 222). **Neither is Aibak, and `tomb-of-qutbuddin-aibak` is NOT mapped** — Aibak is not named in this chunk at all.
3. **`shrine-of-jalaluddin-surkh-posh-bukhari-jalaluddin-bukhari` and `shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht` — still not mapped, but this chunk gives a positive reason where earlier chunks gave only silence.** The book's man is "Shaikh Jalalu'd-Din Tabrizi" (p. 237, folio 217), and his one located activity is **Bengal** — "the legend surrounding Shaikh Jalalu'd-Din's activities in Bengal" (p. 246, folio 226). **Both candidate rows are at Uch Sharif, and neither principal figure carries the nisba Tabrizi.** The book gives him no dates, no Uch, no tomb and no ʿurs (pp. 237, 238, 246; folios 217, 218, 226). **So the evidence here points away from both rows rather than between them, and a consolidator should record that as a finding, not as an unresolved collision.**
4. **`shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar` — mapped, but the book does not disambiguate "Shaikh Farid", and this chunk adds a third claimant to the name.** It prints "Baba Farid" (pp. 240, 243, 244, 248; folios 220, 223, 224, 228) and, inside one quotation, "Shaikh Faridu'd-Din" for the same man in the same anecdote (p. 240, folio 220), plus "Shaikh Faridu'd-Din" paired with Bakhtiyar Kaki on prostration (p. 239, folio 219). **It also names a wholly separate "Maulana Faridu'd-Din", a Firdawsi disciple who compiled the *Fatawa-i Tatar Khaniyya* in 777/1375—76** (p. 248, folio 228) — **that one takes no id.** The chunk supplies **no death date, no Ajodhan and no Pakpattan** for Baba Farid — all (not stated) — so the mapping rests on "That famous Chishti saint" (p. 248, folio 228), the Balban-era chronology (p. 243, folio 223) and the relationship to Nizamuʾd-Din Auliyaʾ (p. 240, folio 220). **The known distinction from Fariduʾd-Din Mahmud of Nagaur is not made by the book and cannot be made from this chunk.**
5. **`shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya` — mapped without a death date, so this chunk does not bear on the book-vs-archive disagreement already recorded** (book 7 Safar 661/21 December 1262 elsewhere; row 1267). **No date of any kind for him appears in pp. 233–248 (folios 213–228)**; the mapping rests on Multan plus the khanqah (p. 235, folio 215) and on his grandson (p. 236, folio 216).
6. **A name collision I considered and did not map: `shrine-of-baba-shah-chiragh`.** The book's "Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din Chiragh Dihlawi" / "Chiragh-i Dihli" (pp. 234, 238; folios 214, 218) shares only the word "Chiragh" with that row's principal figure "Syed Abdul Razzaq (Shah Chiragh)" of Lahore. **The book places its man at Delhi and in the fourteenth century** (pp. 240, 245; folios 220, 225). **No mapping; flagged so that no consolidator makes it on the word alone.**
7. **Toponyms withheld under the unruled interim convention (a).** **Lahore** appears twice with no shrine, saint, tomb, ʿurs or custodian attached — "the destruction of Lahore, by the Mongols in 1241" and "A group of Lahore merchants visited Gujarat" (pp. 241–242, folios 221–222), plus the footnote on "Tayir Mongol" and the town's merchants (p. 242, folio 222). **If ruling (a) reverses, Lahore here would not resolve to a single row** — the archive holds roughly twenty Lahore rows (`data-darbar`, `shah-jamal`, `madho-lal-hussain`, `shrine-of-mian-mir` and others) and this chunk names none of their principal figures, **so reversing the ruling would gain this chunk nothing**. **Multan** is not withheld: it is attached to two mapped saints and their khanqahs (pp. 233, 235, 238; folios 213, 215, 218). **"Taju'd-Din Multani"** (p. 240, folio 220) is a nisba on a private individual, not a Multan shrine figure, and takes no id.
8. **`Shaktipeeth Shri Hinglaj Mata Mandir` (empty id cell) does not arise** — Hinglaj is not mentioned in pp. 233–248 (folios 213–228). **No id invented.** The deity question does not arise either: no Hindu deity is named in this chunk (pp. 233–248, folios 213–228).
9. **Three further internal name collisions worth a human's eye, none of them resolvable from the chunk.** (i) **"Badruʾd-Din"**: "Shaikh Badru'd-Din became a victim of this power struggle as has already been mentioned" (p. 244, folio 224) versus "Khwaja Badru'd-Din Samarqandi" the Firdawsi founder (p. 246, folio 226), whose own death the book dates only as "probably… during the reign of Sultan 'Ala'u'd-Din Khalji" (p. 247, folio 227) — **the book uses "Shaikh Badru'd-Din's death" for the Firdawsi on p. 247 (folio 227) and "Khwaja" elsewhere, so the two titles are not reliable discriminators.** (ii) **"Qazi Hamiduʾd-Din"**: "Qazi Hamidu'd-Din Nagauri", a Suhrawardi author (p. 236, folio 216), versus "Qazi Hamidu'd-Din Maliku't-Tujjar (head of the merchant guilds)" (p. 242, folio 222). (iii) **"Amir Hasan"**: "the Shaikhu'l-Masha'ikh's favourite gawwal [qawwal]" (p. 235, folio 215) versus the government official and disputant on sajda (pp. 239, 240, 243; folios 219, 220, 223) — **the book never says they are the same man.** None of the six takes an archive id.

"gawwal"/"gawwals" for *qawwal* (p. 235, folio 215, three times). "qgalandars" for *qalandars* (p. 234, folio 214) against correct "Qalandars"/"qalandars" on p. 236 (folio 216). "khirga" for *khirqa*, twice (p. 243, folio 223). "baga'" for *baqa'*, twice, and "jana'" for *fana'* once (p. 236, folio 216) against correct "fana'" on the same page. 14. **Footnote markers and locators, corrupt throughout and not usable unchecked.** Roman numerals: "IIl", "Ill", "111" all for *III* in "Voyages D'Ibn Batoutah" (pp. 233, 234; folios 213, 214); "IXXXII, p. 15" for what must be a sura-and-verse reference (p. 238, folio 218); "Qur'an, XII, p. 53" where "p." stands where a verse number belongs (p. 233, folio 213). Markers read as letters or punctuation: "Sibid" for *5ibid* (p. 233, folio 213); "KM, pp. 74-5" with its number 3 lost (p. 234, folio 214); "IFF", "‘FF", "SFF", "\FF", "'KM", "IXXXII" (pp. 238, 239, 240, 243; folios 218, 219, 220, 223); "ONU, Pp. 432" for *6NU, p. 432* and "TJamali" for *7Jamali* (p. 247, folio 227); the marker after "died in 658/1260" printed as "°" (p. 247, folio 227); and on p. 246 (folio 226) the note beginning "NU, pp. 430-31" has lost its number entirely. **The sigla AA, KM, FF, SA, NU and Kalimat are never expanded in this chunk, so none of the anecdotes above can be traced to a title from these pages alone.**
15. **The book's own conventions, which are not damage and must not be "fixed"** — "Hadis" for *hadith*, "Saiyid" for *Sayyid*, "zikr" not *dhikr*, "sama'" not *samāʿ*, "Ramazan", "Qur'an", "'ulama'", "Firdawsiyya"/"Suhrawardiyya", "Munyari" (which the book defends in a footnote against *Maner*/*Muner*, p. 248, folio 228), and the doubled forms "Abu Tawwama"/"Abu Tawama" on one page (p. 248, folio 228), which may be the author's inconsistency rather than OCR.

**Internal contradictions, kept side by side rather than resolved:**

16. **The Chishti refusal of patronage against the Chishti acceptance of cash.** "Chishtis refused to accept land grants either from rulers or their officers" (p. 243, folio 223) and Baba Farid's "total rejection of government patronage" (p. 244, folio 224) stand against Nizamuʾd-Din accepting "enormous gifts given to him by Khusraw Barwar" and gifts "from princes like Khizr Khan" (p. 244, folio 224). **The book notices the tension and resolves it by the cash/land distinction, but does not say Baba Farid held that distinction.**
17. **Aloofness from politics asserted and undercut on the same pages.** "Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din believed in a strictly observed non-involvement in politics and the administration" (p. 243, folio 223) against his appointing "an outstanding Khalifa to 'Ala'u'd-Din's army" and blessing "the Deccan campaign" (p. 245, folio 225), and against Amir Khusraw and Amir Hasan being "notable members of the government" (p. 243, folio 223).
18. **Two incompatible accounts of the same intercession** — ʿIsami's seven days of meditation against Barani's immediate approach (p. 233, folio 213) — **with the author preferring Barani and calling ʿIsami's "apocryphal" without giving a reason.**
19. **Two incompatible accounts of the same succession** — Jamali's nephew Shaikh Ismaʿil against Ibn Battuta's grandson Shaikh Hud (p. 234, folio 214) — **left unresolved by the book.**
20. **No rivalry, and then rivalry.** "There was no apparent rivalry between the Chishti and Suhrawardi saints" (p. 236, folio 216) against the "ideological differences" anecdote (p. 234, folio 214), against the whole comparative section (pp. 236–246, folios 216–226), and against the Firdawsi brothers who "were so envious of Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya' that they even condemned him publicly" (p. 248, folio 228).
