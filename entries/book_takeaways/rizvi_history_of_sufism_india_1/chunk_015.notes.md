## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

Dates are exactly as the book prints them, AH/CE pairs included.

- **Shaikh Sharafu'd-Din Ahmad Yahya' Munyari** — the subject of pp. 249–260 (folios 229–240), called "Ahmad" until p. 249 (folio 229), where the book announces "Shaikh Ahmad, who will now be referred to as Shaikh Sharafu'd-Din". Educated under Shaikh Abu Tawwama, whom he accompanied to Sunargaon and with whose family he settled (p. 249, folio 229); "the Managibu'l-Asfiya' relates that to prevent being distracted he refused to even read family letters"; the sources disagree on his marriage — "According to some versions of this text, Abu Tawwama married his daughter to Ahmad, but others relate that he married in Munyar after his return from Sunargaon. This he did on medical advice" (p. 249, folio 229). "In Sha'ban 690/July 1291 Shaikh Yahya' died and Ahmad left for Munyar to be with his aggrieved mother" (p. 249, folio 229). He went to Delhi to visit Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya', "however the Shaikh did not initiate Ahmad into his discipleship" (p. 249, folio 229); then to Panipat, where he "found Abu 'Ali Qalandar (d. 724/1324) to be so often engrossed in ecstasy that he was unsuitable as ateacher [OCR?] [as a teacher]" (p. 249, folio 229); "Finally he became the disciple of Shaikh Najibu'd-Din Firdawsi and was himself given written authority to enrol disciples" (p. 249, folio 229). **He "died from natural causes on 5 Shawwal 782/2 January 1381"** (p. 252, folio 232).
- **Shaikh Abu Tawwama** — left Delhi "because of Sultan Balban's jealousy at his popularity with the people of that city", per the "Mangib al-Asfiya' [OCR?] [Manaqib al-Asfiya']"; Rizvi prefers envy of "the local 'ulama'" (p. 249, folio 229). His visit to Munyar "opened up new opportunities for the young Ahmad's education", and Sunargaon "became a centre of Islamic learning in Bengal, attracting many scholars" (p. 249, folio 229).
- **Rajgir** — "Ancient Rajagriha, the early home of the Buddha and the capital of ancient Magadha is now near Patna in Bihar. It is surrounded by five hills. The town abandoned by the Muslims was allowed to become a dense jungle" (p. 249, folio 229, footnote 5). Sharafu'd-Din's ascetic exercises were performed there, not "in the forests near Agra" as the *Akhbaruʾl-Akhyar* has it (p. 249, folio 229). **A place with a Buddhist and a Muslim history and no shrine attached** — listed here under the interim toponym convention.
- **Maulana Nizam Madani** — "a disciple of Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya' who was greatly attached to Shaikh Sharafu'd-Din", and the builder of his first khanqah at Munyar (pp. 249–250, folios 229–230).
- **Shaikh Najibu'd-Din Firdawsi** — Sharafu'd-Din's pir, who granted him written authority to enrol disciples and, when Sharafu'd-Din objected that "he was unqualified, after only a few days, to accept such responsibility", answered that "His decision had been based on divine inspiration" and "asked Shaikh Ahmad to leave for Munyar" (p. 249, folio 229).
- **Zainu'd-Din Majdu'l-Mulk** — "the mugta' [OCR?] [muqta'] of Bihar", ordered by Sultan Muhammad bin Tughluq to build the khanqah and assign Rajgir to it (p. 250, folio 230).
- **Shaikh 'Izz Kaku'i and Shaikh Ahmad Bihari** — "the Shaikh's friends and fellow sufis", executed under Sultan Firuz; "The two were outspoken in their ideas on the Wahdat al-Wujud"; "The people of Delhi believed they were crazy"; a *mahzar* was convened, "They were condemned in a fatwaissued [OCR?] [fatwa issued] by the 'ulama' and later executed" (p. 251, folio 231). The name recurs as "Kakui" on p. 256 (folio 236). The *Futuhat-i Firuz Shahi* version: "Shaikh Ahmad and his followers were atheists who misguided people in the name of solitude and renunciation. Some people in Bihar considered Ahmad to be God" (p. 251, folio 231, footnote 3).
- **Khwaja Muhammad bin Sirin** — "an eminent sufi", quoted from the *Adabuʾl-Muridin* that "he would pray for the Sultan if his prayers were answered, for he believed a prayer for the ruler was a prayer for the welfare of the people" (pp. 250–251, folios 230–231).
- **Shaikh Muzaffar** — the most outstanding of Sharafu'd-Din's disciples; ancestors "came from Balkh"; his father was the disciple of "Shaikh Ahmad Charm-Posh (One Who Wears Skins), a cousin of Shaikh Sharafu'd-Din"; studied two years in Delhi "teaching in a seminary founded by Sultan Firuz"; set to menial service in the khanqah until "Constant ascetic exercises in the seclusion of the khangah [OCR?] [khanqah] made his body a mere skeleton of bones with little flesh" (p. 257, folio 237). With his brother Shaikh Mu'izz and Mu'izz's son Shaikh Husain he left for Mecca via Chittagong, reaching Bengal "By 798/1395—96"; the party left Chittagong "About the end of 800/1397-98 or early 801/1398-99"; "Shaikh Mu'izz died there as did Shaikh Muzaffar at Aden in 803/1400-01. Shaikh Husain returned to Bihar" (pp. 258–259, folios 238–239).
- **Sultan Ghiyasu'd-Din A'zam Shah** — "the Ilyas-Shahi Sultan … (1389-1409)", who "had greatly improved trade and commerce, developed the port of Chittagong and established cultural and diplomatic relations with China", whose "relations … with the Hindus were cordial and they occupied high posts in the government", and who "is known to have invited the great Persian poet, Hafiz of Shiraz, to migrate to Bengal" (p. 258, folio 238). Shaikh Muzaffar "addressed Sultan Ghiyasu'd-Din as his 'prosperous son'" (p. 258, folio 238).
- **Qazi Shamsu'd-Din** — "an administrator of Chausa in Bihar", whose official duties kept him from the assemblies, so "At his request, in 747/1346-47, the Shaikh wrote him a hundred letters" (p. 260, folio 240).
- **Shaikh Zain Badr 'Arabi** — "a very close disciple", who "In 769/1367-68 … also compiled another collection of 151 letters of the Shaikh's, his first compilation" (p. 260, folio 240).
- **Sharafu'd-Din's sons** — "Qazi Husamu'd-Din, Qutbu'd-Din, 'Abdu'l Malik and Fakhru'd-Din", among the addressees of Zain Badr's collection (p. 260, folio 240). **On "Qutbu'd-Din" see Doubts.**
- **Dawar Malik** — "a son-in-law of Sultan Muhammad bin Tughluq", addressee of two letters (p. 260, folio 240).
- **Maulana Sadru'd-Din** — a disciple "who had accepted the post of deputy gazi [OCR?] [qazi]" and was "reminded in unminced words that they had been ill-advised to embark on political careers and were in fact wasting their time" (p. 260, folio 240).
- **Mas'ud-i Bak** — "The pioneer of Delhi's Wahdat al-Wujud movement"; "His real name was Sher Khan and his ancestors had migrated from Bukhara to Delhi"; "a relative of Sultan Firuz Tughluq" whose "many years as a high ranking government official had made him fantastically wealthy" before "a mystical experience prompted him to adopt asceticism"; "became a disciple of the Chishti, Shaikh Ruknu'd-Din, son of Shaikh Shihabu'd-Din"; he "believed Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya' to be his real teacher"; "a later authority writes that Sher Khan was beheaded, on a fatwa from the 'ulama.'" (p. 261, folio 241). Works: a *Tamhidat* based on ʿAinuʾl-Quzat's, which "cannot be traced"; the *Mirʾatuʾl-ʿArifin*, "divided into fourteen chapters called Kashfs or Revelations"; and "his Diwan, entitled the Nuru'l-Yagin [OCR?] [Nuru'l-Yaqin]" (p. 262, folio 242).
- **Saiyid Muhammad Husaini bin Ja'far al-Makki** — "A contemporary of Mas'ud-i Bak … an equally important figure in the sufi world"; his father "Amir Ja'far, who had been given the title Amir Kabir (Great Amir), was a mugta' [OCR?] [muqta'] in Cambay and an officer of 1,300 horsemen"; "He was initiated into the Chishtiyya order by Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din Mahmud Chiragh-i Dihli"; he served his pir "even cleaning with his beard the istinja clay of his preceptor" (p. 264, folio 244). ## Practices, institutions, economy

**This is the densest section of the chunk and the part most useful to the archive.** It contains the written deputisation instrument, two acts of khanqah building, a named revenue assignment, the return of that grant, a burial-of-letters practice, a charity rule and a khanqah network. **Across the whole range it contains no ʿurs, no mela, no langar, no sajjada nashin, no Auqaf, no visitor numbers and no architectural description or building date for any structure** — all (not stated) for pp. 249–264 (folios 229–244).

- **Written authority to enrol disciples — the deputisation document that chunk 006 looked for and did not find.** Shaikh Najibu'd-Din Firdawsi made Sharafu'd-Din his disciple "and was himself given written authority to enrol disciples" (p. 249, folio 229). **The book does not use the word khilafat-nama and does not describe the document** — (not stated).
- **A private patron builds a small khanqah, and the recipient compares it to an idol-temple.** Maulana Nizam Madani "built a small khangah [OCR?] [khanqah] for his use while in Munyar. Shaikh Sharafu'd-Din accepted the gift, but referring to the khanqah stressed that his friends had forced him to sit in the temple of an idol" (p. 250, folio 230). **Note the same page prints both "khangah" and the correct "khanqah".**
- **The state builds a khanqah and assigns it a named revenue district — the chunk's central economic fact.** "During the early period of his reign, Sultan Muhammad bin Tughluq ordered Zainu'd-Din Majdu'l-Mulk, the mugqta' [OCR?] [muqta'] of Bihar, to build an imposing kKhangah [OCR?] [khanqah] for the Shaikh and assign the pargana, Rajgir, to pay the expenses" (p. 250, folio 230), the term glossed in the book's own footnote as "A revenue district comprising several villages" (p. 250, folio 230, footnote 1).
- **Acceptance under duress, and the author's reading of the refusal.** "Shaikh Sharafu'd-Din reluctantly accepted the offer for he feared, if he refused, Majdu'l-Mulk might suffer from the Sultan's wrath. The Shaikh's reluctance was not founded on any ideological tradition of his si/sila [OCR?] [silsila], but sprang from his own ascetic temperament. In this respect he was more of a Chishti than a Suhrawardi" (p. 250, folio 230).
- **An inauguration ceremony with a named imperial gift.** "a lavish function was organized by the mugta' [OCR?] [muqta'] to inaugurate the khanqah. The Shaikh took his place on a Bulgarian prayer mat which the Sultan had sent as a gift" (p. 250, folio 230); "Uninterested either in his new khangah [khanqah] or the imported prayer rug, he said to one of the assembled dervishes that he considered himself unworthy even of Islam, but felt he had no choice but to accept such favours" (p. 250, folio 230).
- **The grant was given back — and the book gives two accounts of when, side by side.** "In a letter Husain Mu'izz Balkhi mentions that Shaikh Sharafu'd-Din returned the villages after a period of fifteen years. Saiyid Hasan 'Askari suggests that the farman granting the villages appears to have been returned during Sultan Firuz's visit to the Shaikh in 762/1360 when he called on the Shaikh during his second expedition against Ilyas Shah of Bengal"; Rizvi's own preference follows — "If the farman was returned, the most appropriate time for this to have taken place seems to be the period when the Sultan was alienated from the Shaikh, because of the latter's condemnation of the executions of two saints from Bihar" (p. 250, folio 230, footnote 3). **A ruler's visit to a living Shaikh, dated, is the nearest thing in this chunk to ziyarat.**
- **The khanqah as a place of resort across two reigns.** "Throughout the reigns of Muhammad bin Tughlug [OCR?] [Tughluq] and Firuz Tughlugq [OCR?] [Tughluq], the khangah [khanqah] of Shaikh Sharafu'd-Din Munyari was a rendezvous for the many who sought a spiritual life" (p. 250, folio 230).
- **Visitors managed by a weekly routine — a rare glimpse of crowd pressure on a living saint.** "Gradually, as his existence in Rajgir became known, he received many visitors. To reduce these disturbances he would come to Munyar each Friday for congregational prayers, returning to the forest afterwards" (p. 249, folio 229).
- **Numbers of disciples, as the hagiography gives them.** "The Shaikh's biographers estimated that the number of his disciples exceeded 100,000, of which forty were respected as having achieved union with God. Of those who achieved this supreme state, Shaikh Muzaffar, Malikzada Fazalu'd-Din and Maulana Nizamu'd-Din were regarded as the most outstanding" (p. 257, folio 237).
- **Menial service inside the khanqah as a prescribed cure for scholarly conceit.** "Sharafu'd-Din ordered him to act as a menial domestic at the khangah [khanqah], serving other dervishes so that the conceit which usually overpowered an 'alim (which he had become through study), could be replaced by humility" (p. 257, folio 237).
- **A same-day disposal rule for charity received.** Shaikh Muzaffar "strictly adhered to the sufi rule that whatever was received in charity should be given away the same day it had been obtained" (p. 257, folio 237). **This is the chunk's only rule about offerings, and it forbids accumulation.**
- **Letters buried in a grave by testamentary direction — a practice worth recording in its own right.** "About two hundred of them were said to have been buried in the grave of Shaikh Muzaffar, according to a directive contained in his will. A bag containing twenty-eight letters survived, which were later compiled into a book-form and also published in 1870" (pp. 257–258, folios 237–238).
- **A khanqah network as the transmission mechanism.** "A network of small khangahs [khanqahs] stretching from Bihar to Sunargaon and reaching many areas of India disseminated Shaikh Sharafu'd-Din's teachings, as embodied in his letters" (p. 260, folio 240); a discourse collection, "the Ma'danu'l-Ma'ani, which was compiled by a disciple, also became a spiritual beacon to future generations of sufis" (p. 260, folio 240).
- **Letters as a deliberately guarded medium, written against the risk of prosecution.** The hundred letters to Qazi Shamsu'd-Din "were intended to teach other disciples, spread throughout all of northern India, the spiritual truth and were carefully written in order to avoid reprisals from the 'ulama' and other externalists" (p. 260, folio 240); by contrast the later collection is "franker and more expressive … There is a strong ascetic streak throughout these later letters, and criticism of the 'ulama' is trenchant" (p. 260, folio 240).
- **A text with a measurable circulation inside khanqahs.** Mas'ud-i Bak's "Diwan was a favourite text in Chishti khanqahs between the fourteenth and sixteenth centuries and later sufis, both followers and opponents of Wahdat al-Wujud, drew on it heavily" (p. 262, folio 242).
- **Judicial machinery used against sufis: the mahzar, the fatwa and the farman.** Sultan Firuz "convene[d] a mahzar"; the two were "condemned in a fatwaissued [fatwa issued] by the 'ulama' and later executed" (p. 251, folio 231); against Sharafu'd-Din "A farman was issued authorizing this, but was cancelled" (p. 251, folio 231); Sher Khan "was beheaded, on a fatwa from the 'ulama.'" (p. 261, folio 241). **Rizvi's institutional reading: "it was not unusual for the Sultan to find himself helpless in religious matters against the power of the 'ulama.'" (pp. 261–262, folios 241–242).**
- **A Shaikh writing to Sultans on justice, and a ruler's public grievance proclamation.** "The Shaikh wrote two letters to Sultan Firuz advising him to be impartial in dispensing justice … he reminded Firuz of the Prophet Muhammad's injunction that an hour spent pursuing justice was far superior to sixty years of worship" (p. 251, folio 231); and Firuz "had it proclaimed throughout the city of Delhi that whosoever might have any grievance or claims against Firoz, the slave of God, might come and put forward his charges and claims which he would satisfy" (p. 258, folio 238). Firuz's own reported procedure: "he enquired about the opinion and the 'Fatwas' of the Qazis, and the Muftis had to cite precedents after which he gave his award" (p. 258, folio 238).
- **A sufi intervening in state employment policy on communal grounds.** "To his great disappointment, many high posts were held by the Hindus" (p. 258, folio 238); "In another letter the Shaikh advised the Sultan that high government posts should not be given to Hindus", arguing "An infidel may be entrusted with some work but he should not be made Wali (chief supervisor or Governor) so that he may have control over and impose his authority on Muslims" (p. 259, folio 239). **A named office, Wali, and an explicit exclusion rule.**
- **A seminary founded by a Sultan, used by a sufi as a teaching post** — Shaikh Muzaffar studied in Delhi "at the same time teaching in a seminary founded by Sultan Firuz" (p. 257, folio 237).
- **The pir as the indispensable institution.** "The absence of a pir turned seekers of the mystic path into philosophers, materialists, heretics, mu'tazila, ibahatis and the like. The perfect pir saved his disciple from indulging in such heretical beliefs as Aulul [OCR?] [hulul] … and ittihad" (p. 255, folio 235); and "A sufi disciple should not be attached to any particular sect … but adhere to the faith of his pir, in order to guarantee the attainment of his goal" (p. 263, folio 243).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

Attributions are the book's own, including its hedges ("is mythical", "were said to have been", "claimed to have").

- **A hagiographical number the author debunks.** "The figure of twelve years for the length of his stay in these forests is mythical, as this was the standard period used to describe prolonged mortifications performed in lonely forest areas" (p. 249, folio 229).
- **Forty disciples said to have achieved union with God**, out of "more than 100,000" (p. 257, folio 237).
- **Supernatural powers produced by austerity, ranked against Hindu yogis and Christian monks.** "These endowed those who performed them with supernatural powers like the Hindu yogis who walked on water and flew like birds. The Shaikh believed that these exercises made Christian monks superior even to yogis—they were able to receive spiritual inspiration and to read thoughts. To him, however, these achievements were not the essence of sufism" (p. 253, folio 233).
- **Direct divine answers as the sufi's superiority over the ʿulamaʾ.** "sufis consulted the Lauhu'l Mahfuz (Preserved Tablet), and if the answer was not there they questioned God and received a reply directly from him" (pp. 254–255, folios 234–235).
- **A retributive reading of imperial collapse.** "The author of the Managqibu'l-Asfiya [OCR?] [Manaqibu'l-Asfiya'] ascribed the disintegration of Sultan Firuz's administration, fall of the Tughluq empire and the catastrophe that befell Delhi during Timur's invasion, specifically to the execution of the two sufis" (p. 251, folio 231); and Sharafu'd-Din's own "surprise that a town which instigated and tolerated such killings remained standing and escaped destruction at the hand of God" (p. 251, folio 231).
- **An accusation of incarnation made against a living saint.** "Ahmad's disciples preached that God had appeared in Delhi in the form of Ahmad" (p. 251, folio 231, footnote 3).
- **Two moral anecdotes the book itself calls charming.** The broken fast — "Expiation of a sin for breaking such a fast was possible, added the Shaikh, but there was no atonement for the sin of breaking someone's heart"; and the drunken imam, of whom the Shaikh said "that no doubt he did not drink during the fasting month of Ramazan" (p. 252, folio 232).
- **The idle devil.** "a dervish who found the devil sitting idle. On being asked the reason for doing nothing, the devil replied that the worldly 'ulama' had taken his place and there was no work left for him to do" (p. 255, folio 235).
- **Iblis as gatekeeper of the Divine.** "La (No) was the station of /b/is [OCR?] [Iblis], and Illa (Except), the stage of the Prophet Muhammad. /b/is [Iblis] was the sentry at the door of the Divine and Muhammad was the keeper of the treasury of Wahdut [OCR?] [Wahdat] or Unity. Unless one passed the guard the treasury could not be reached" (p. 262, folio 242).
- **A claim of having met 380 saints.** "Ja'far Husaini claimed to have met at least 380 sufi saints during his travels" (p. 264, folio 244).
- **An extreme act of service.** Muhammad Husaini served his pir "even cleaning with his beard the istinja clay of his preceptor instead of rubbing it on wood or a floor to make it smooth", the term glossed in the book as "Cleaning the private parts after passing urine" (p. 264, folio 244, and footnote 2).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Rizvi routinely substitutes a sociological motive for a hagiographical one.** On Abu Tawwama's exile: "A more probable explanation is that he aroused the envy of the local 'ulama'; he was a newcomer, who no doubt had succeeded in stealing their religious limelight" (p. 249, folio 229).
- **He adjudicates between his two main sources on the basis of family tradition.** "The story of the Managqibu'l-Asfiya' [Manaqibu'l-Asfiya'] seems more authentic as it incorporates family traditions" (p. 249, folio 229, footnote 2) — deciding against the *Akhbaruʾl-Akhyar*, which says Nizamu'd-Din Auliya' was already dead.
- **He corrects a source on geography.** "According to the Akhbaru'l-Akhyar, Shaikh Ahmad … practised arduous ascetic exercises in the forests near Agra. It would appear, however, that these were performed in Rajgir, in Bihar" (p. 249, folio 229).
- **He reads temperament, not doctrine, into a saint's attitude to state patronage** — "not founded on any ideological tradition of his si/sila [silsila], but sprang from his own ascetic temperament. In this respect he was more of a Chishti than a Suhrawardi" (p. 250, folio 230). **This is a direct statement that Suhrawardi practice accepted state endowment and Chishti practice did not.**
- **He asserts a clean record between Shaikh and Sultan.** "No conflict between Sultan Muhammad bin Tughluq and the Shaikh has been reported in any relevant source" (p. 250, folio 230).
- **He dismisses the charges against the executed sufis as a genre rather than as evidence.** "The accusations are typical of the kind of resentment shown by the orthodox against the Wahdat al-Wujud and did not necessarily have to be proved" (p. 251, folio 231, footnote 3).
- **He revises the historiographical verdict on Sultan Firuz — twice, in two chapters.** "the verdict of Muslim historians, both medieval and modern, is in favour of Sultan Firuz who is depicted as both a pious Sunni and a reformer king" (p. 251, folio 231); and, in Chapter Four, "In the name of Islam and at the instigation of the 'ulama' the list of men this pious Sultan had killed was no shorter than that of Muhammad bin Tughluq who had had victims liquidated for political reasons, however earlier religious authorities tended to by-pass such details" (p. 262, folio 242).
- **He accepts a late source on an execution with an argument from plausibility.** Of Sher Khan's beheading: "Contemporary sources fail to reveal this hostility and Shaikh 'Abdu'l-Huqq [OCR?] ['Abdu'l-Haqq] is also silent on the matter; however a later authority writes that Sher Khan was beheaded … There is no reason to doubt the validity of this statement" (p. 261, folio 241). **The mapping-relevant point: the only evidence is a late authority and the author's own sense of what was usual.**
- **He corrects a named modern historian on a technical term.** "Ibaha means permission to perform an act as one wishes to do so, however, it is also a term of abuse for sufis of antinomian leanings; it is not a Hindu sect as I.H. Qureshi believes" (p. 255, folio 235, footnote 3).
- **The chapter's governing thesis about why centres multiplied.** "long before Timur's inyasion [OCR?] [invasion] in 1398-99 which accelerated the establishment of new Chishti centres throughout India, the Wahdat al-Wujud theory had become firmly rooted in the mystic soil of Delhi" (p. 261, folio 241). **Doctrine first, then dispersal under political shock — the frame for the whole of Chapter Four.**
- **Sharafu'd-Din's own doctrinal positions as Rizvi summarises them** — the perfect sufi as "a paragon of mercy and generosity", "fearless … like the sun which shone on both enemies and friends alike" (p. 252, folio 232); self-abnegation, "he should be like the earth, upon which people walked" (p. 252, folio 232); charity outweighing ritual — "hundreds of prayers and days of fasting equalled a single act of kindness or assistance given to a Muslim" (p. 252, folio 232); a definition of a Muslim as "one who harmed another Muslim neither by hand nor by tongue" (p. 252, folio 232); the greater jihad — "It was not jihad … against non-Muslims which was the real war, but that against the nafs-i kafir (the heretical nafs) of one's own lower self" (p. 253, folio 233); *tajrid* and *tafrid* defined, with "fafrid [OCR?] [tafrid]" entailing "a breaking away from oneself" (p. 253, folio 233); the Shariʿa–Tariqa relation worked through four concrete examples, ending "Seking [OCR?] [Seeking] the Tariga [OCR?] [Tariqa] without the Shari'a was like climbing to a roof without steps" (p. 254, folio 234); the cosmology "from the 'world of humanity' (masut [OCR?] [nasut?]) to the celestial and angelic kingdom (Malakut) and then to 'the world of divine omnipotence' (Jabarut)" (p. 254, folio 234); and against the ʿulamaʾ, "the dervish who was dependant on the 'ulama' was not one at all" (p. 254, folio 234).
- **His four-stage scheme of the sufi journey, with Wahdat al-Wujud as the last.** "To Shaikh Sharafu'd-Din, Tawhid or the Wahdat al-Wujud was the fourth and final stage in the sufi journey, related to Lahut (the indescribable world of Unity)" (p. 256, folio 236), followed by the sun-particles and mirror passage (pp. 256–257, folios 236–237).
- **Mas'ud-i Bak's epistemology and his universalism.** The three knowledges — "'ilm al-Yaqin (knowledge through inference) … 'ain al-Yagin [OCR?] [ʿain al-Yaqin] (knowledge through perception) … hagq al-Yagin [OCR?] [haqq al-Yaqin] (knowledge through intuition)"; "Thus Shari'a is learning, the Tariga [Tariqa] is burning and the Hagiga [OCR?] [Haqiqa] is illumination" (p. 262, folio 242); and "that in all religions only One Road was concealed. If the subtle truth of this path, gleaned from different forms, was to be understood, all the different religions would appear identical" (p. 263, folio 243). **Set against his refusal to rank saints above prophets: "Mas'ud-i Bak rejected the growing belief in the superiority of the saints over the prophets … saints themselves not possessing intrinsic spiritual eminence" (p. 263, folio 243) — a doctrinal position directly hostile to shrine cult, from the man the chapter calls Delhi's pioneer.**
- **Ja'far Husaini's three-fold classification of souls** — common Muslims under scrupulous Shariʿa, "although he believed that the true goal could not be obtained from this path"; ascetics on an "inward-looking" path "for the holy and the pious alone"; and "the spiritual élite who had reached the stage of understanding the Lahut" (p. 264, folio 244). "He was deeply concerned with religious chauvinism and the narrow pride exhibited by all religious communities in the world" (p. 264, folio 244).

### Archive-mapping doubts (read these first)

1. **`shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht` vs `shrine-of-jalaluddin-surkh-posh-bukhari-jalaluddin-bukhari` — the collision the traps list warned about, and I believe this chunk settles it, but a human should confirm.** The book prints "Makhdum-i Jahaniyan Saiyid Jalal Bukhari" (p. 251, folio 231) and, four pages later and inside a translated letter, "Syed Jalalu'd-Din (Bukhari)" arriving at Delhi "from Uch, near Multan" (p. 258, folio 238). **The distinguishing evidence is the title:** "Makhdum-i Jahaniyan" is the first half of the row name "Makhdoom Jahaniyan Jahangasht", and the row's location is Uch Sharif, which the book's "Uch" matches. ### Internal contradictions and implausibilities, kept as the book prints them

19. **The book gives two incompatible accounts of Sharafu'd-Din's non-initiation by Nizamu'd-Din Auliya', and says so itself.** The text: he "set off for Delhi to visit Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya', however the Shaikh did not initiate Ahmad into his discipleship"; the footnote: "According to the Akhbaru'l-Akhyar, Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya' had died and Shaikh Ahmad then contacted Shaikh Najibu'd-Din Firdawsi who welcomed Shaikh Ahmad saying that for years he had been awaiting Ahmad's arrival" (p. 249, folio 229 and footnote 2). **Refusal in one version, death in the other.**
20. **The life-span given for Saiyid Muhammad Husaini bin Ja'far al-Makki cannot be right as printed.** "His long life extended from the reigns of Sultan Muhammad bin Tughlugq (1325-51) to that of Sultan Bahlul Lodi (1451-89)" (p. 264, folio 244) — **a span of up to 164 years**, and the same page dates his last letter to 27 Muharram 825/21 January 1422, which is before Bahlul Lodi's reign begins. **I am recording the printed values and not reconciling them.**
21. **A cross-reference in the book points to the wrong printed page.** Chapter Four says the ʿulamaʾ "beheaded two Bihari sufis" and footnotes "See, Chapter Three, p. 230. supra" (p. 261, folio 241), but the executions are narrated on the page `folio_map.tsv` gives as folio 231, i.e. PDF p. 251. **A one-page slip in the book's own apparatus, not an OCR artifact as far as I can tell.**
22. **The chunk holds the acceptance and the rejection of state patronage side by side without comment** — a Sultan-built khanqah with a *pargana* assigned to it and an imperial prayer mat at its inauguration (p. 250, folio 230), against the recipient saying his friends "had forced him to sit in the temple of an idol" (p. 250, folio 230), against the grant later being returned (p. 250, folio 230, footnote 3), against the rule that charity be given away the day it is received (p. 257, folio 237), and against disciples being reproached for taking office (p. 260, folio 240). **Any entry citing Rizvi on shrine endowment must carry both sides.**
23. **The chapter's headline figure is hostile to saint cult.** Mas'ud-i Bak "rejected the growing belief in the superiority of the saints over the prophets … saints themselves not possessing intrinsic spiritual eminence" (p. 263, folio 243). **This sits oddly with the chapter's own subject, the growth of shrine-centred Chishti "Centres", and Rizvi does not remark on it.**
