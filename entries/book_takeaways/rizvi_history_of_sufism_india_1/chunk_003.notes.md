sixteen PDF pages 50–65 carry a folio in the running header, giving folios 34–49, so folio = PDF − 16
on every page — exactly the band the measured ladder predicts for PDF 33–105, and **the headers
confirm the table rather than contradicting it**. Verso (even PDF pages 50, 52, 54, 56, 58, 60, 62, 64)

number, and no plate leaf or "Digitized by Google" leaf falls inside it.** Three headers carry a stray
character between numeral and title — `42 : A History of Sufism in India` (p. 58), `48 ° A History of
Sufism in India` (p. 64) — and one recto prints a full stop after the chapter title, `Early Sufism. 43`

`Early Sufism`, so the whole chunk sits inside that chapter. Every page ends with the digitisation
watermark, printed `Google` or `» Google`, which carries no number and is apparatus only.

**This chunk touches exactly one archive row, and only as an author.** pp. 50–65 (folios 34–49) are
Rizvi's survey of the ninth- and tenth-century Sufis of Khurasan, Transoxiana, the rest of Iran, Egypt,
Syria and Baghdad — Balkh, Marw, Nakhshab, Tirmiz, Nishapur, Bastam, Rayy, Kirman, Shiraz, Tustar,
Basra, Ikhmim, Daraya, Baghdad. **No shrine in Pakistan or India is named, no ʿurs, no sajjada nashin,
no Auqaf and no Indian saint appears anywhere in the range** — the only two references to the
subcontinent are a footnote self-citation, "For the Mahdavi movement in India see S.A.A. Rizvi, *Muslim
revivalist movements in northern India*, pp. 68-134" (p. 57, folio 41, footnote 8), and the phrase "the
province of Sind in modern Pakistan" inside a disputed etymology (p. 61, folio 45). The single archive
mapping is **data-darbar**, on Hujwiri named three times as a source. **The institutional, doctrinal
and economic material below is the substance of this chunk, and it is dense**: ribat foundation, tomb
visitation, the three classes of the poor, pilgrimage money redirected to orphans, marriage-baraka as a
priced good, a saint tried for heresy by the state, and Rizvi's own verdict on the Indian origin of
fanaʾ.

## Practices, institutions, economy

**This is the chunk's richest vein, and it is about the economy of asceticism rather than of shrines.
There is no ʿurs, no mela, no langar, no sajjada nashin or gadinishin, no Auqaf, no waqf, no land
grant, no revenue assignment, no visitor number, no custodial dispute and no building date anywhere in
pp. 50–65 (folios 34–49) — with the two exceptions recorded first below.**

- **A built religious foundation, with a stated dual purpose — the one endowment fact in the chunk.**
  "Al-Hanzali built two hospices (ribats) at Marw, one for traditionists and the other for
  jurisprudents" (p. 54, folio 38). **Rizvi's gloss of *ribat* is "hospices". No founding date, cost,
  endowment, staff or later history is given** — **(not stated)**.
- **Tomb visitation, with a duration and no other detail — the one shrine-practice fact in the chunk.**
  Of al-Sayyari of Marw: "Although he died there in 342/953-54, for centuries his tomb at Marw was
  visited by devotees" (p. 55, folio 39). **No shrine building, no ʿurs, no custodian, no offerings and
  no visitor numbers are stated** — all **(not stated)**. "for centuries" is the only measure given.
- **A three-part annual cycle of pilgrimage, war and trade, with the profits given away.** "In
  alternate years he would perform a hajj, and go to war, and during the third year, he would engage in
  commerce, distributing his profits to the poor" (p. 54, folio 38). **A merchant Sufi's own rotation
  of hajj, jihad and commerce, stated as a rule of life.**
- **Pilgrimage expenditure explicitly redirected to alms — the chunk's sharpest statement on religious
  spending.** Of Bishr al-Hafi: "The highest spiritual merit was earned by doing service to mankind. He
  accordingly advised pilgrims to Mecca to give their money to an orphan or to a poor man and thus earn
  more religious merit and spiritual satisfaction. To him pilgrimage was the jihad of women" (p. 55,
  folio 39). **A named saint telling pilgrims to spend the money on the poor instead.**
- **A three-class taxonomy of the poor, printed as a numbered list.** Bishr "distinguished three types
  of poor: / 1. those who neither begged nor accepted anything, yet received everything they asked for
  from God; / 2. those who did not beg but accepted what they were given; and / 3. those who held out
  for as long as they could, but then begged" (p. 55, folio 39). **Begging is the discriminating
  variable, and the ranking is explicit.** Also his three prohibitions: "asking a boon of anyone,
  speaking ill of anyone and accepting an invitation for food from anyone" (p. 55, folio 39); and
  "Poverty, according to Bishr, was to be borne with patience and charity" (p. 55, folio 39).
- **Professional poverty attacked by name — the chunk's most quotable critique of a religious economy.**
  Shaqiq on the religious men of his time: "When the learned man is covetous..., whom can the ignorant
  man imitate? And when the poor man (fagir [OCR?] [faqir]) is famed for his poverty, and is desirous of
  this world...whom can the covetous man find to imitate, in order to escape from his greed? When the
  shepherd is the wolf, who will care for the sheep?" (p. 51, folio 35).
- **Labour, lawful earning and the ranking of earning above ritual.** Of Ibrahim bin Adham: "Although
  Ibrahim worked as a labourer, proceeds from his toil were given to the poor, while he himself went
  hungry. When travelling he would sell whatever he had with him to provide some comfort for his
  companions. He was sparing in his own consumption of food, but believed that the partaking of
  nourishment, which was lawfully earned, was more meritorious than prayer and fasting" (p. 50,
  folio 34). Also his division of goods: "the world should be enjoyed by worldly people and the rewards
  of the Unseen should be reaped by souls which thirsted for it" (p. 50, folio 34).
- **Redistribution as a personal rule.** "For himself, Shaqiq would distribute anything he received"
  (p. 51, folio 35).
- **Baraka priced as a marriage good — the chunk's clearest instance of sanctity as a transactable
  asset.** Of Ibn Khafif: "ʿAbduʾllah's fame as a mystic made him exceedingly eligible as a marriage
  prospect to the daughters of kings and nobles. Marriage to him was considered a great prize because of
  the blessing accruing from it. ʿAbduʾllah did not choose one wife but many, and is said to have
  contracted four hundred marriages. These were not consummated and were generally later annulled"
  (printed "laterannulled") (p. 61, folio 45). "Two or three of his wives, however, would rotate in
  performing service for their husband, but only one, a vizier's daughter, is recorded as having
  endeared herself to ʿAbduʾllah" (printed "Twoor three") (pp. 61–62, folios 45–46). **"four hundred
  marriages" and "fifteen knots" (p. 62, folio 46) are the only large numerals in the chunk.**
- **Marriage into the governing family, stated without comment.** Ahmad ibn Khazruya was "Married to the
  daughter of the local governor" (p. 53, folio 37).
- **Marriage and celibacy disputed among the saints.** Ibrahim "was married and left a child in Balkh,
  but advocated celibacy for mystics", with the simile that a married dervish "could be compared with
  someone embarking on a sea voyage—when children were born, he drowned" (p. 50, folio 34); against
  Bishr, who "did not himself marry" but "never preached against family life" (p. 55, folio 39).
- **State power addressed directly by a saint, with a fiscal and penal programme.** Shaqiq to Harun
  al-Rashid: God "has appointed you its doorkeeper, and has equipped you with three things—wealth,
  sword and whip. 'With these three things,' He commands, 'keep the people away from Hell. If any man
  comes to you in need, do not grudge him money. If any man opposes God's commandment, school him with
  this whip. If any man slays another, lawfully exact retaliation on him with this sword.' If you do not
  these things, you will be the leader of those that enter Hell!" (p. 51, folio 35). **An explicit
  obligation of relief to the needy, placed alongside punishment and retaliation.** And on
  administration: "You are the fountain, and your agents are the rivulets... If the fountain is bright,
  it is not impaired by the darkness of the rivulets. But if the fountain is dark, what hope is there
  that the rivulets will be bright?" (p. 51, folio 35).
- **Subsistence as a claim against the ruler at the Last Judgement.** Fuzayl "urged him to rule with
  justice and reminded him that on the Resurrection Day God would question him concerning every single
  Muslim under his protection, and would exact justice for each of them. If one night an old woman had
  gone to sleep in her house without provisions of any kind, at the Last Judgement she would pluck the
  Caliph's gown and give evidence against him" (pp. 53–54, folios 37–38). **A named welfare standard —
  one night's provisions for one old woman — enforced eschatologically.**
- **Famine, slavery and the ownership of a village, inside one story.** "when there was a fearful famine
  in Balkh, a young slave appeared at the bazaar, joyful and content. When asked why he was happy when
  so much sadness abounded, he replied that his own welfare did not concern him for his master was
  wealthy"; Shaqiq's response: "O Lord God, this youth rejoices so much in having a master who owns a
  single village, but Thou art the King of kings, and Thou hast promised to give us our daily bread; and
  nevertheless we have filled our hearts with all this sorrow (printed "ail this sorrow") because we are
  engrossed with worldly things" (p. 50, folio 34). **"a master who owns a single village" is the
  chunk's only unit of landholding.**
- **Service of the poor named as a Sufi institution, and named in order to say Bayazid did not practise
  it.** Ritter, quoted: Abu Yazid was "in contrast..with the later sufis Abu Ishaq al-Kaziruni and Abu
  Saʿid bin Abiʾl Khayr, a wholly introvert sufi. He did not exercise, as they did, a social activity
  (khidmat al-fugaraʾ [OCR?] [khidmat al-fuqaraʾ]), yet was ready to save humanity, by vicarious
  suffering, from hell" (p. 60, folio 44). **The technical term for organised service of the poor
  appears here as the thing a great saint omitted.**
- **Karamat and zikr explicitly devalued as obstacles.** For Bayazid, "The 'world' (dunya), 'flight from
  the world' (zuhd), 'worship of God' (ʿibadat), miracles (kKaramat [OCR?] [karamat]), zikr, even the
  mystic stages (magamat [OCR?] [maqamat]) are for him no more than so many barriers holding him from
  God" (p. 60, folio 44). **Miracle and recollection ranked as impediments — the exact opposite of
  Tirmizi's position on miracles at p. 57 (folio 41).**
- **Breath control as a practice, and as a borrowing.** "His advocacy of understanding of the controlled
  use of breath was also Indian" (p. 61, folio 45). **A named technique with an asserted Indian origin.**
- **Ascetic technique, listed.** Bayazid's "continuous vigils and hunger fasts" and his thirty years of
  "wandering from country to country" (p. 59, folio 43); Fuzayl "spending all his time in prayer and
  fasting" (p. 53, folio 37); Ibrahim's hunger (p. 50, folio 34); Bishr's barefoot destitution (p. 55,
  folio 39); Sahl's "self-punishment" as a positive means (p. 62, folio 46); the monk "emaciated by
  self-mortification and bent double by fear of God" (printed "selt-morti-fication") (p. 54, folio 38).
- **Dress as concealment and as status.** Ahmad ibn Khazruya "wore the clothes of a soldier" "To remain
  a stranger to the people of the town" (p. 53, folio 37); Abu Turab's dervish whose "clothing was
  piety" (p. 56, folio 40); "the prayer carpet was infinitely preferable to the coronet" for Ibn Khafif
  (p. 61, folio 45); "the girdle of the magians (zunnar)" as Bayazid's self-image (p. 60, folio 44).
- **State and orthodox coercion of Sufis, four instances.** Tirmizi's "teachings brought him into
  conflict with the authorities of his own town; he retired to Nishapur" (p. 58, folio 42); Sahl —
  "persecution by the orthodox forced Sahl to take refuge in Basra" and "Orthodox divines accused him of
  combining the Law (Shariʿa) and Truth (Hagqiga [OCR?] [Haqiqa])" (p. 62, folio 46); Zuʾn-Nun — "In
  214/829 he was accused of heresy, arrested and sent to Baghdad. After examination the Caliph had him
  released" (p. 63, folio 47); Bayazid — "Only for a short period was he forced to hide because of the
  enmity of orthodox elements in Bastam" (p. 59, folio 43). **A heresy arrest, a caliphal release, a
  forced retirement and a period in hiding — the chunk's whole record of church–state friction.**
- **Sectarian conflict in a Sufi town.** "The struggle between the Shiʿis and the Sunnis, particularly
  the dominant Karrami Sunnis, was an interesting feature of the religious, political and social history
  of Nishapur. But the most colourful aspects of life in the town were those connected with the sufi
  movement" (p. 58, folio 42).
- **Trade and urban economy as the setting of Sufism, stated three times.** "Tirmiz was the greatest
  emporium of goods coming from the north to Khurasan" (p. 56, folio 40); "Nishapur was the leading
  trade centre of Khurasan and featured a daily traffic of caravans. Sacked by the Ghuzz Turks in the
  middle of the twelfth century, and again by the Mongols in the middle of the thirteenth, its economic
  recovery was meteoric" (p. 58, folio 42); "Baghdad, the ʿAbbasid capital in Iraq, situated on the
  Khurasan road, was a junction of caravan routes" (p. 65, folio 49). **Sacks dated only by half-century
  ("middle of the twelfth", "middle of the thirteenth") — no year is printed.**
- **Caravan protection as a purchased service.** "A pious merchant was travelling in Marw region
  accompanied, not in the traditional manner by a hired, protective escort, but by a professional reader
  of the Qurʾan" (p. 53, folio 37). **A hired escort is named as the tradition, and a paid Qurʾan
  reciter as the substitute.**
- **Wealth acquired and lost in trade.** Yahya al-Razi "Having acquired considerable wealth from
  trading, he wished to return to Rayy. After losing everything in a robbery he settled in Nishapur"
  (p. 59, folio 43); al-Hanzali "became a very rich merchant" (p. 54, folio 38).
- **Demand for esoteric teaching can fail — a stated market failure.** "It seems apparent that the
  people of Rayy had little interest in esoteric doctrines and Yusuf's lectures generally failed to
  attract an audience" (p. 61, folio 45).
- **Saints as healers and answered intercessors, in a divine address.** God to the saints: "He gave them
  knowledge of the places where the means of healing is to be found. . .and to them He gave assurance of
  an answer to their prayers, and He said: 'Oh My saints, if there come to you one sick through
  separation from Me, heal him, or a fugitive from Me, seek him out...or afraid of Me, then reassure
  him'" (p. 64, folio 48). **The doctrinal charter for healing and petition at a saint's hands, and the
  most shrine-relevant sentence in the chunk.** Also "He put love towards them into the hearts of His
  creatures" (p. 64, folio 48) — devotion to saints as divinely implanted.
- **A hierarchy of saints with fixed numbers.** Tirmizi's rungs are "al-budala, al-siddigin [OCR?]
  [al-siddiqin] (or al-umana), al-muhaddasun, and Khatm al-Awliyaʾ, in order of increasing precedence"
  (p. 57, folio 41), and the footnotes quantify two of them: the budala "are forty in number and
  surround the divine throne" (footnote 2), the umana "have offered their souls to God, and He has
  rewarded them with His light; they also number forty" (footnote 3), the muhaddasun "are the sadat
  al-awliyvaʾ [OCR?] [sadat al-awliyaʾ], 'masters of the saints'" (footnote 4) (p. 57, folio 41).
- **Prayer formulas and supererogatory works as the route to divine love.** The Hadis Tirmizi cites:
  "The best way for My servant to draw near to Me is through regularity in his religious duties, and
  continual striving for My proximity by works of supererogation until I grant him My love. When I love
  him I will be his hearing, his sight, his tongue, his hand, his leg, and his heart" (p. 57, folio 41).
- **Discipleship and transmission by station.** "Al-Hiri's associations with Yahya perfected in him the
  'station' of hope, those with Shah Shujaʿ inculcated in him jealousy, but his discipleship under Abu
  Hafs perfected him in affection" (p. 59, folio 43); Shaqiq "acknowledged this youth as his preceptor"
  after the famine story (p. 50, folio 34); Bishr became "a disciple of his own maternal uncle" (p. 55,
  folio 39). **Three different modes of affiliation — a named shaikh, a chance layman, and kin.**
- **Hospice-free instruction: teaching by lecture and by book.** Shaqiq "As a lecturer... attracted many
  students" (p. 51, folio 35); Abu Hafs "amazed his fellow sufis with his eloquent Arabic" at Baghdad
  (p. 58, folio 42); and repeatedly, books that have not survived — Shaqiq's (p. 51, folio 35), Shah ibn
  Shujaʿ's (p. 61, folio 45), Yahya's (p. 59, folio 43) — against al-Hanzali's, of which "one on
  asceticism has survived" (p. 54, folio 38).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

Attributed as the book attributes them.

- **A dream that converts, reported without hedge.** Bishr picked up a paper inscribed "In the name of
  God, the Merciful, the Compassionate" and "deposited it reverently in his house. The same night God
  visited him in a dream, extending His approval. This prompted Bishr to turn to asceticism" (p. 55,
  folio 39).
- **A conversion at an ambush, which the author calls possibly false.** Fuzayl's conversion by hearing
  "Is not the time yet come unto those who believe, that their hearts should humbly submit to the
  admonition of God?" — "The story of his conversion depicts it as sudden, dramatic and is perhaps
  apocryphal" (p. 53, folio 37, citing "Qurʾan, LVI, 16" [OCR?] [locator to be checked — see Doubts]).
- **Wild animals fed at a saint's house, "according to tradition".** "Like Rabiʿa, Sahl had a special
  affinity with animals. According to tradition, wild beasts and lions would come from the forests to
  his house where he would feed them" (p. 63, folio 47).
- **Ascetic knots grown in the body, told by an eyewitness wife.** "From his breast to his navel there
  were fifteen knots (ʿagd [OCR?] [ʿaqd]) growing out of his belly... 'They are knots made by the
  tribulation and anguish of my abstinence in renouncing a face like this and viands like these'"
  (p. 62, folio 46).
- **A night mistaken for an evening, ending in self-reproach.** ʿAbduʾllah ibn al-Mubarak stood at the
  wall of his beloved's house all night: "when ʿAbduʾllah heard the call to morning prayers he thought
  it was time for evening prayers; and only when the sun began to shine did he discover that he had
  spent the whole night in rapturous contemplation of his beloved" (p. 54, folio 38).
- **Bayazid's ecstatic utterances (shatahat) and his miʿraj, reported through Ritter.** "Subhani! Ma
  aʿzama shaʾni"—"Glory be to me! How great is My Majesty!"; "Thy obedience to me is greater than my
  obedience to Thee"; "I am the throne and the footstool"; "I am the Well-preserved Tablet"; "I saw the
  Kaʿba walking round me"; "In meditation he made flights into the supersensible world; these earned him
  the censure that he claimed to have experienced a miʿraj in the same way as the Prophet" (p. 60,
  folio 44). **The author's frame: these "so scandalized and shocked his contemporaries" (Ritter's
  words).**
- **Miracles as evidential proof of sainthood — Tirmizi's doctrine, quoted.** "It is possible for saints
  to work miracles. The occurrence of miracles inspires in others the belief in the genuineness of the
  sainthood. When a miracle becomes manifest it is a sign of true sainthood. The miracle is both the
  proof of this genuineness and its result, for it is the saint's genuineness that enables him to work
  miracles" (p. 57, folio 41), with "Saints understand both the future and what is hidden from ordinary
  men, and Tirmizi is inclined to ascribe miraculous powers unreservedly to those who are outstanding"
  (p. 57, folio 41).
- **The Mahdi and the Antichrist, in a long footnote quotation from Ibn Khaldun.** "at the end of time a
  man from the family (of the Prophet) will without fail make his appearance, one who will strengthen
  the religion and make justice triumph... He will be called the Mahdi. Following him, the Antichrist
  will appear... After (the Mahdi), ʿIsa (Jesus) will descend and kill the Antichrist" (pp. 57–58, folios
  41–42, footnote 8).
- **A conversion story the author rejects outright.** Maʿruf al-Karkhi "reportedly embraced Islam
  through the influence of the eighth Shiʿi Imam ʿAli al-Riza. This story must be apocryphal" (p. 65,
  folio 49) — the argument is under Arguments.
- **A count of teachers dismissed as legend.** "It is said that he met one hundred and thirteen
  spiritual teachers during his thirty years of roaming. This would, however, seem to be based on
  legend" (p. 59, folio 43).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Rizvi's verdict on the Indian origin of fanaʾ — the most consequential argument in the chunk, and he
  sets the whole dispute aside.** "Abu Yazid's theory of fanaʾ or the total destruction of the empirical
  self in God is not the only point of similarity between his teachings and those of the Upanishads. His
  advocacy of understanding of the controlled use of breath was also Indian. Some hagiologists suggest
  that Bayazid learnt the doctrine of fanaʾ from his teacher Abu ʿAli Sindi. Among modern scholars
  Nicholson and R.C. Zaehner support this theory, while Arberry rejects it. The latter provides evidence
  to prove that Sind was a village in Khurasan and not the province of Sind in modern Pakistan. The
  controversy is insignificant because ancient Indian thought and ideas on mysticism had continually
  aroused interest in the Khurasanian region and these naturally fused with Bayazid's expression of his
  own mystical experiences" (p. 61, folio 45). **Rizvi neither adopts nor refutes Arberry; he declares
  the question unimportant and substitutes a diffusionist claim of continual Indian influence in
  Khurasan, for which no evidence is cited in this chunk.**
- **Christian and Syriac mysticism as a source of Baghdad Sufism, asserted with a translation history.**
  "From the ninth century onwards translations of Greek, old Persian and Sanskrit literature were
  accompanied by those of Syriac (ancient Syrian or western Aramaic) works by Christian mystics into
  Arabic. Of these the most notable was the translation of Mystic Treatises written by Isaac of Nineveh
  in the seventh century", with four further Syriac mystics "also known to sufis in Baghdad" (p. 65,
  folio 49). **The claim of knowledge among Baghdad Sufis is asserted, not evidenced, in this chunk.**
- **A chronological refutation of a conversion tradition, worked out in full.** "This story must be
  apocryphal for ʿAli al-Riza, who was born in Medina in 148/765, was summoned to Marw by Caliph
  al-Maʾmun (813-33) in 201/816 and was killed at Tus, two years later in 818. Long before the arrival
  of ʿAli al-Riza in Iran, Maʿruf had settled in Baghdad, where he died in 200/815-16. It is unlikely
  that the latter would have gone to Medina in order to" — **the sentence is cut off at the chunk
  boundary** (p. 65, folio 49). **Rizvi's method here is the best-documented instance in the range: a
  hagiographic claim tested against printed dates and rejected.**
- **Tirmizi's theory of sainthood, as Rizvi and al-Geyoushi present it.** Two recurring themes: "a
  detailed elucidation of the ways to sainthood, and the description of the struggle between the desire
  of the carnal soul and the longing of the heart"; and his hierarchy is called "basic for sufi thought"
  (p. 56, folio 40). Sainthood divides into "(a) general, as common to and exhibited by all believers;
  and (b) special, whereby the saints achieve trustworthiness in the eyes of God" (printed "(by
  special"), the special dividing again into "Awliyaʾ Haqq-Allah literally (Saints of Duty towards God)"
  and "Wali-Allah (Saints of God)", the latter reached by "Scrupulous observance of the rules,
  perseverance and patience in obedience" (p. 56, folio 40).
- **Saints ranked against prophets and against the Companions.** "Tirmizi clearly acknowledges the
  superiority of the prophets over the saints: 'the prophets were saints of God before they became
  prophets; hence they possess both qualities, prophethood and sainthood. Nobody is their equal'" —
  but "He sees no reason preventing saints from equalling, and even surpassing the early Caliphs, Abu
  Bakr and ʿUmar" (p. 57, folio 41), with "Who can prevent the mercy of God from prevailing over people
  even in these modern times? ... Do they think that there is no siddig [OCR?] [siddiq], no mugarrab
  [OCR?] [muqarrab], no mujtaba, no mustafa nowadays?" (pp. 57–58, folios 41–42). **A doctrine of
  continuing, contemporary sainthood — the theological ground on which later shrine cults stand, though
  the chunk draws no such conclusion.**
- **The condition on all of it.** "Tirmizi, however, reminds all saints that knowledge of the nature of
  lordship (rububiyya) depends on possessing the proper principles of servantship (ʿubudiyya)" (p. 58,
  folio 42).
- **Law and Truth declared inseparable — Sahl against his accusers.** "Inasmuch as God has joined the
  Law to the Truth, it is impossible that His saints should separate them... Rejection of the Law is
  heresy, and rejection of the Truth is infidelity and polytheism... In short, the Law is a branch of
  the Truth: knowledge of God is Truth, and obedience to His command is Law" (p. 63, folio 47). **The
  answer to the charge that he "combined" Shariʿa and Haqiqa (p. 62, folio 46).**
- **Two "firsts" claimed for Zuʾn-Nun.** "he was the first to attempt a detailed explanation of these
  two ideas" — the mystic states (ahwaʾl [OCR?] [ahwal]) and stations (maqamat) — and "He was the first
  to teach the real nature of gnosis (maʿrifa)" (p. 63, folio 47); with, from the *Encyclopaedia of
  Islam*, "He is one of the first to use the imagery of the wine of love and the cup poured out for the
  lover to drink" (p. 64, folio 48).
- **Love of God must not exclude love of man.** "But the love of God must not exclude love to man, for
  love to mankind is the foundation of righteousness" (p. 64, folio 48).
- **Al-Sayyari's jamaʿ / tafriqa doctrine, credited with lasting influence.** "His explanation of the
  doctrine of jamaʿ (union) and tafriga [OCR?] [tafriqa] (separation) deeply influenced later sufis...
  'Union is that which He unites by His attributes and separation is that which He separates by His
  acts.' This involved a cessation of human effort and an affirmation of the divine will to the
  exclusion of all personal initiative. Jamaʿ did not involve the mingling (imtizaj) of God with created
  beings or God-made objects (ittihad) with His own works or His becoming incarnate (hall [OCR?]
  [hulul]) in things" (p. 55, folio 39). **An anti-incarnationist guard placed on a unitive doctrine.**
- **Malamat defined as the deliberate destruction of one's own welfare.** "He affirmed that malamat was
  the abandonment of all concern for one's welfare. If the worshipper intentionally abandoned the source
  or [OCR?] [of] his own well-being and embarked on a path of permanent misfortune, renouncing all
  pleasure, God's glory might be revealed to him, and the more he became separated from mankind, the
  more he was united with God. Blame had a great effect in making love sincere" (pp. 58–59, folios
  42–43).
- **Hope and fear held in exact balance — al-Darani's doctrine, quoted in full.** "the maintenance of
  belief in Unity consists in right hope, and the maintenance of 'time' in right fear, and both are
  maintained when hope and fear are equal... Hope is connected entirely with contemplation
  (mushahadat), in which is involved a firm conviction (iʿtigad [OCR?] [iʿtiqad]); and fear is connected
  entirely with purgation (mujahadat), in which is involved an anxious uncertainty (iztirab)" (p. 65,
  folio 49); "despair of God is polytheism (shirk)" (p. 65, folio 49).
- **Ghaybat and huzur defined by Ibn Khafif.** "'Presence' is 'presence of the heart,' as a proof of
  intuitive faith (yaqin)... 'Absence' is 'absence of the heart from all things except God'... and the
  sign of this state is withdrawal from all formal authority (hukm-i rusum), as when a prophet is
  divinely preserved from what is unlawful. Accordingly, absence from one's self is presence with God,
  and vice versa" (p. 62, folio 46).
- **Trust in God (tawakkul) as the doctrine that made a reputation.** Shaqiq's "most significant
  contribution to sufi philosophy was on the subject of tawakkul, or resignation to the divine will.
  Through this he attracted many students and Shaqiq appears to have excelled even Ibrahim bin Adham at
  his interpretation of this idea" (pp. 50–51, folios 34–35), with the canine simile: one "should be
  patient, in the same manner as dogs behave towards their masters" (p. 51, folio 35); and Hatim
  al-Asamm's threefold lust and "Real trust in God proceeds from right knowledge, for those who know Him
  aright have confidence that He will give them their daily bread" (p. 52, folio 36).
- **Rizvi's periodising frames, stated in passing.** "The early trends of sufism in Balkh synchronized
  with spectacular developments in Marw" (p. 53, folio 37); "There foundations of sufism were laid amidst
  hectic orthodox, intellectual and sectarian developments" of Baghdad (p. 65, folio 49); "It was an
  Irani, however, who founded the Baghdad school of Islamic mystics" (p. 65, folio 49). **The chunk's
  organising principle is city by city, and Rizvi's claim is that each city's Sufism has a founder and a
  character.**

## Doubts

**This book's transcription carries no `[OCR?]`, `[illegible]` or `[folio N]` markers at all — the
tesseract route leaves no transcriber apparatus — so every `[OCR?]` above is mine, and the damage
inventory below is the only record of what is unmarked in the source.**

1. **q→g, the heaviest class and the one that touches names.** "Shagiq" and "Shagqig" for *Shaqiq*,
   printed on the same pages as the correct "Shaqiq" (pp. 50, 51, 52, folios 34, 36 — the name appears
   both ways within four lines at p. 50); "fagir" for *faqir* (p. 51); "tafriga" for *tafriqa* (p. 55);
   "Figh" for *Fiqh* (p. 56); "magamat" for *maqamat* and "haddad nafui" for what is probably *haddad
   nafsi* (p. 60); "Hagqiga" for *Haqiqa* (p. 62); "ʿagd" for *ʿaqd* (p. 62); "iʿtigad" for *iʿtiqad*
   (p. 65); "khidmat al-fugaraʾ" for *al-fuqaraʾ* (p. 60); "siddig" and "mugarrab" (pp. 57–58);
   "Mugaddimah" for *Muqaddimah* (p. 57, footnote 8); "Tarikh Demashg" for *Tarikh Dimashq* (p. 64,
   footnote 2). **A consolidator must treat "Shagiq"/"Shagqig"/"Shaqiq" as one man and must not carry
   any of these spellings into an entry.**
2. **Letters lost to 1 / l / t / d / k / z, and dropped ʿ and ʾ.** "ail" for *all* (p. 50); "onethics"
   for *on ethics* (p. 52); "selt-morti-fication" for *self-mortification* (p. 54); "ibn al al-Haris"
   with a duplicated article (p. 54); "Abu Yizid" for *Abu Yazid* and "kKaramat" for *karamat* and
   "/a-kayfiyya" for *la-kayfiyya* (p. 60); "laterannulled" and "Twoor three" (p. 61); "ahwaʾl" for
   *ahwal* (p. 63); "aor" for *nor* and "nor any sovereign by Myself" for what is probably *but Myself*
   (pp. 64–65); "abandoned the source or his own well-being" for *of his own well-being* (p. 58);
   "sadat al-awliyvaʾ" (p. 57); "hall" for *hulul* (p. 55) — the same l→d/h class the Schimmel run
   recorded for *hulul*.
3. **Citation locators — the class that endangers facts most, and none of these may enter an entry
   unchecked.** "Massignon, pp. 243-$6" — **a dollar sign standing for a digit** (p. 61, footnote 1);
   "Ibn Asakar, Tarikh Demashg, Damascus, | 329-32;1911-13" — **a pipe character for a digit and lost
   spacing in an AH/CE date pair** (p. 64, footnote 2); "Abu Nuʿaim, pp. 189-212" — **the Roman volume
   numeral is missing where every other Abu Nuʿaim citation in the chunk has one** (p. 63, footnote 2);
   "Encyclopaedia of Islam (new edition), 1, pp. 1244-246" — **arabic 1 for Roman I and a truncated page
   range** (p. 55, footnote 1); "NU, p. 41-3" — singular "p." for a range (p. 50); "al-Yafeʿi, 1, pp.
   415-17" (p. 54) and "al-Yafeʾi, Il, pp. 92-4" — **"Il" for II** (p. 55); "[bn Hajar" for *Ibn Hajar*
   (p. 55); "pp. 93 134" and "pp. 192 94" and "pp. 183 91" — **hyphens lost from page ranges** (p. 61);
   "pp. 439-43. 469-72" — a full stop for a comma (p. 56). Titles: "Tahagqat al-Sufiyya" for *Tabaqat
   al-Sufiyya* (p. 50); "A/-Risalat al-Qushairiyya" and "/slamic Quarterly" / "/sʹamic Quarterly" —
   **slashes for capital I and l** (pp. 50, 56, 57); "al-Khaub" for *al-Khatib* at p. 64, against the
   correct "al-Khatib" at pp. 59 and 61; "Al-Nuʿaim" for *Abu Nuʿaim* at p. 54, footnote 5.
4. **"Qurʾan, LVI, 16" (p. 53, folio 37, footnote 4) is the single most dangerous locator in the chunk**
   — a Roman numeral in a scripture citation, in a class of damage that routinely eats an I. **The verse
   quoted is "Is not the time yet come unto those who believe, that their hearts should humbly submit to
   the admonition of God?" I have not corrected the numeral and it must be verified against the Qurʾan
   before it enters anything.**
5. **Footnote marker digits, corrupted on at least six pages.** ">For notes" for *5* (p. 54); "JNicholson"
   for *3* (p. 55); "4M.I. Al-Geyoushi" where the text's marker is *3* (p. 56); "Sibid, pp. 23-5. Sibid,
   p. 26." for *5ibid* and *6ibid* (p. 57); "4Nicholson, p. 115." where the text's marker is *3*
   (p. 52); "4For notes on his biography" where the sequence gives *3* (p. 58). In the body text the
   markers are read as "!", "?", "}", "©", "*2", "1" and "‘". **Any footnote-to-text pairing taken from
   this chunk must be re-derived from the page, not from the digits.**
6. **A scholar's name given two ways inside one page-pair.** The text names "Ibrahim al-Geyoushi, a
   modern authority on Tirmizi" (p. 56, folio 40); the footnotes name him "M.I. Al-Geyoushi" (p. 56,
   folio 40, footnote 3; p. 57, folio 41, footnote 1). The same article is also cited with two different
   locator forms — "/sʹamic Quarterly, XV; no. I, London, January-March, 1971, p. 18" and "/slamic

**Archive-mapping doubts:**

- **`data-darbar` is mapped on three author-mentions and nothing else.** Hujwiri is named at p. 51
  (folio 35), p. 59 (folio 43) and p. 61 (folio 45), always as a source for someone else's saying. **No
  date, place, tomb, silsila, Lahore reference or biographical fact about Hujwiri appears anywhere in
  pp. 50–65** — all **(not stated)**. The mapping follows the ruling of 18 September 2026; a
  consolidator must not read these bullets as evidence about Data Darbar the shrine.
- **The *Kashf al-Mahjub* bullet under `data-darbar` is the one placement I am least sure of.** The
  chunk quotes the book twice (pp. 53, 62) and **never states its author**. I have placed it under the
  row because Hujwiri is separately named in the same chunk and because Rizvi's Hujwiri citations run
  through "Nicholson" (the *Kashf al-Mahjub* translator, though the chunk does not say so either). **If
  the consolidator will not accept an unattributed title, the bullet should move to "Other saints, sites
  and events" — the evidence for the link is not on these pages.**
- **The siglum `NU`, used twice, is nowhere expanded in this chunk** — "NU, p. 41-3" (p. 50, folio 34,
  footnote 2), "NU, pp. 51-2" (p. 56, folio 40, footnote 1), "NU, p. 60" (p. 59, folio 43, footnote 1).
  The book's abbreviation key is not in this range, so the Hujwiri reference at p. 59 cannot be traced
  from here.
- **"Sind" (p. 61, folio 45) — left unmapped, and the interim toponym convention cannot be applied to
  it.** The word appears only inside Arberry's argument, as "Sind was a village in Khurasan and not the
  province of Sind in modern Pakistan". It is a province name matching dozens of archive rows and no
  single one, so there is no row to name under the reversal clause. **It should not be attached to any
  row, in either direction of the ruling.**
- **`madho-lal-hussain` — a cross-reference, explicitly NOT a mapping.** That archive row carries the
  silsila "Malamati", and this chunk gives the founder and the definition of the *malamat* path — Hamdun
  al-Qassar of Nishapur, d. 271/884-85 (pp. 58–59, folios 42–43). **The book names no Indian or
  Pakistani malāmatī, no Shah Hussain, and no connection of any kind between Hamdun and the
  subcontinent.** Flagged only so the consolidator knows the doctrinal background is available here; any
  bridge from Hamdun to Lahore would have to come from outside this book.
- **No other archive row is touched, and the negatives are worth stating.** Hinglaj does not appear, so
  the known empty-id defect in the `Shaktipeeth Shri Hinglaj Mata Mandir` row is not engaged by this

  Waris Shah, Iqbal, Guru Nanak and every other archive principal figure are **absent from pp. 50–65**;
  no mapping should be inferred for any of them from the chunk's Khurasanian material.

**Internal tensions and chronological problems in the chunk itself:**

- **A teacher shared across a gap of at least a dozen years.** Fuzayl "became a disciple of Sufyan
  al-Sawri" (p. 53, folio 37) and died "in 187/803" (p. 54, folio 38); Sahl al-Tustari "was born in
  c. 200/815 and studied first with Sufyan al-Sawri" (p. 62, folio 46). **On the chunk's own dates,
  Sahl was born thirteen years after the death of a man who was his teacher's-generation contemporary,
  and the chunk gives no dates for Sufyan al-Sawri at all — (not stated).** This must be checked before
  either discipleship is repeated.
- **Yahya al-Razi is said to have settled in two places.** He "finally chose Balkh for his activities as
  a sufi preacher" and, in the next two sentences, "settled in Nishapur" and "died in Nishapur in
  258/871-72" (p. 59, folio 43). **The chunk does not reconcile "finally chose Balkh" with dying at
  Nishapur.**
- **Bayazid's death date is given twice and neither is chosen** — "261/874 or 264/877-78" (p. 59,
  folio 43).
- **Miracles are both the proof of sainthood and a barrier to God, eight pages apart.** Tirmizi: "The
  miracle is both the proof of this genuineness and its result" (p. 57, folio 41). Bayazid, via Ritter:
  "miracles (karamat), zikr, even the mystic stages (maqamat) are for him no more than so many barriers
  holding him from God" (p. 60, folio 44). **Rizvi sets the two positions side by side without
  comment.**
- **"Kirman, in the Fars region" (p. 61, folio 45)** is the book's own geographical statement and is
  recorded as printed; I have not checked or corrected it.
