# rizvi_history_of_sufism_india_1 — chunk 009 — pp. 148–163

**The chunk is pp. 148–163 = folios 130–145, and every folio in the range is confirmed twice over.** The running headers print verso `130 A History of Sufism in India` (p. 148) through recto `The Chishtis 145` (p. 163), and each one agrees with folio_map.tsv, which I have used as the sole authority for every folio cited below. **One header is OCR-damaged: p. 159 prints `The Chishtis 14]`, where the final digit is broken** — folio_map.tsv gives p. 159 → folio 141 with source `run`, and I have taken 141 from the table, not from the damaged header. **The recto running header on every recto in the chunk is `The Chishtis`** (pp. 149, 151, 153, 155, 157, 159, 161, 163; folios 131, 133, 135, 137, 139, 141, 143, 145). **This contradicts the brief and I am recording the book's own evidence — see Doubts, item "Chapter boundary".** The chunk opens mid-sentence ("parity of faith itself", p. 148, folio 130) and closes mid-episode ("Before", p. 163, folio 145); no chapter-opening page and no printed chapter number falls inside it.

**Three archive rows are touched: `shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar` (very deep — roughly half the chunk), `shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya` (one sentence), `tomb-of-qutbuddin-aibak` (one footnote sentence).** The chunk is exceptionally rich in exactly the material chunk 006 reported as absent: **burial of a Shaikh, a tomb built over him by a Sultan, tomb construction paid for by a Sultan, burial beside the master's tomb, a farman of four villages offered and refused, a khanqah built for a Shaikh and condemned for it, relics as succession tokens, a basket-collection of offerings, a steward, amulets, crowd control around a living saint, and merchant gift-withdrawal as an economic weapon.**

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar** (the book's names for him: "Shaikh Faridu'd-Din Ganj-i Shakar, popularly called Baba Farid", p. 156, folio 138; also "Shaikh Faridu'd-Din or Baba Farid", p. 154, folio 136; "Shaikh Farid", p. 162, folio 144): **ancestry and migration.** "Shaikh Faridu'd-Din's ancestors came from Kabul, but migrated to the Panjab in the middle of the twelfth century" (p. 156, folio 138), the migration ascribed to "The orgy of Ghazna's destruction by 'Ala'u'd-Din Husain of Ghur in 1150-51, followed by the Ghuzz invasion six years later" (pp. 156–157, folios 138–139).
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **father, place and office.** "Baba Farid's father, Qazi Shu'aib, went to Lahore and later moved to Qasur, south-east of Lahore. The Ghaznawid Sultan of the Panjab made him the Qazi of Kahtwal, a town in Multan, between Maharan and Ajodhan" (p. 157, folio 139). **"Maharan" and "Kahtwal" are recorded exactly as printed and I cannot adjudicate them** — see Doubts.
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **brothers and birth date, with the book giving two alternatives and preferring neither.** "There Qazi Shu'aib married and had three sons, 'Izzu'd-Din Mahmud, Faridu'd-Din and Najibu'd-Din. Shaikh Farid was born in 569/1173-74 or 571/1175-76" (p. 157, folio 139).
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **his mother is named as the greater formative influence, and she is not named.** "The Shaikh's father was a scholar, but it was his mother, an exceedingly pious woman, who prayed nightly at lengthy vigils, who exerted the greater influence on the future sufi saint" (p. 157, folio 139). **Her personal name is (not stated).**
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **early asceticism and the townsfolk's verdict.** "Unconcerned with food or clothing, he was constantly occupied with meditation in a place behind the mosque at Kahtwal. The townsfolk came to believe he was deranged, and they spoke so to Shaikh Jalalu'd-Din Tabrizi of Baba Farid and what to them were his strange habits" (p. 157, folio 139).
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **initiation — and the book's own dispute about where it happened.** "At the age of eighteen, Baba Farid settled in Multan to undertake further studies and joined a seminary at the mosque of Maulana Minhaju'd-Din Tirmizi. There he met Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki and asked to become his disciple. According to Jamali, Baba Farid was initiated by the Khwaja at Multan and was advised to complete his education there" (p. 157, folio 139); Rizvi dissents — "Jamali adds that the Baba then visited Qandahar to acquire further knowledge, but it is more likely that Baba Farid reached Delhi shortly after the Khwaja's arrival and was initiated into the distinguished group of sufis residing in the Khwaja's jamu'at-khana [OCR?] [jama'at-khana]" (p. 157, folio 139).
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **his cell at Delhi, and Khwaja Mu'inu'd-Din's prophecy over him.** "Baba Farid lived in a small cell near the jama'at-khana and, under guidance, performed severe ascetic exercises. His austerities so impressed Khwaja Mu'inu'd-Din during his visit to Delhi, that he took a special interest in Baba Farid, prophesied his later fame and asked Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din to join him in prayer for his disciple's future greatness" (pp. 157–158, folios 139–140).
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **the chilla-i ma'kus, described as a physical procedure, and performed at Uch.** He asked "if he could perform a chilla which involved spiritual exercises and fasting for forty days. The Khwaja finally permitted him to perform a chilla-i ma'kus (inverted chilla)"; the details, obtained through "Badru'd-Din Ghaznawi", were that "it required a man to tie a rope around his feet and remain suspended in a well, head down, for forty days and nights, while both fasting and praying. The Baba found a lonely mosque in Uch and, taking the mu'azzin into his confidence performed the chilla-i ma'kus" (p. 158, folio 140). The footnote gives the master's reason for hesitating: "Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din Bakhtiyar's reluctance to permit chilla-i ma'kus sprang from the fact that such devotional exercises gave the devotee unnecessary publicity" (p. 158, folio 140, footnote 3).
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **the name Ganj-i Shakar, with the book flagging it as one of several stories.** "The reason why the Shaikh came to be known as Ganj-i Shakar (Store of Sugar) is described in different stories in various hagiological works" (p. 158, folio 140); the pebbles-into-sugar version is under "Legends and miracles" below.
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **he left Delhi for Hansi and missed his master's death by five days.** "After the Baba's fame in Delhi became an obstacle to prayer and meditation, he left for Hansi in the Hisar district. He was therefore absent from Delhi at the time of the Khwaja's death, arriving five days after the event" (p. 158, folio 140).
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **succession by relic, under a will — the clearest deputisation mechanism anywhere in this book so far.** "In accordance with the Khwaja's will, Qazi Hamidu'd-Din Nagauri gave Baba Farid relics from the Khwaja, including his khirga [OCR?] [khirqa], turban, stick and wooden sandals. Although this implied that the Baba was the Khwaja's successor in Delhi, the Baba's asceticism and total withdrawal from the world precluded any conflict with Shaikh Badru'd-Din" (p. 158, folio 140). **No khilafat-nama and no document of any kind is mentioned — the instruments are four physical objects and a will.** See Doubts on the competing succession claim.
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **Ajodhan — arrival, duration, death date and the strategic value of the site.** "First going to Hansi, the Baba finally settled at Ajodhan, where he remained from about 1236 until his death on 5 Muharram 664/17 October 1265. His long stay on the Satlaj, along one of the main routes from Multan to Lahore and Delhi, was a spiritually rewarding experience" (p. 158, folio 140). **The book writes "Ajodhan" throughout and never prints "Pakpattan"** — see Doubts.
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **he was persecuted at Ajodhan, by officials and by the local Qazi, and twice in fear of his life.** "Although he selected a lonely place to reside, Baba Farid was harassed by local officers of the Qazi of Ajodhan and by the rudeness of the people of the town. This was prompted mainly by the Qazi's hostility towards the sufi movement" (p. 158, folio 140); "On one occasion the Qazi requested the 'ulama' of Multan to issue a fatwa (legal decree) against anyone who lived in a mosque, was known as a dervish, listened to music or danced. The 'ulama' refused to comply, after discovering that such a fatwa would be used against the Baba" (pp. 158–159, folios 140–141); "Nevertheless, official oppression continued, and an attempt was made on his life by a qalandar. At a later date, the Shaikh became convinced that a Muslim magician was employing witchcraft to kill him" (p. 159, folio 141).
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **Rizvi's own reading of why he went to Ajodhan, stated against a named modern scholar.** "The Baba did not, therefore, select Ajodhan in order to reform or convert 'backward Hindu tribes', as Nizami seeks to show, for it would appear that there was a great deal of work to be done among the Muslims of Ajodhan. Rather, through an example of sanctity and austerity, Baba Farid sought to reform the Muslims in the area of north-west India" (p. 159, folio 141).
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **his house and his prohibition on burnt brick — a stated architectural rule of the order.** "Shaikh Faridu'd-Din lived near the Jami' mosque, in a small house of mud walls covered with a thatched roof. He had strictly forbidden the use of burnt bricks for the buildings which, according to Chishti tradition, were not simple enough for an ascetic's dwelling. The door remained open until midnight as a welcome to visitors" (p. 159, folio 141).
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **his possessions, itemised.** "Among his few possessions, the Baba had a small rug which he used by night as a blanket, but which hardly covered him. During the day it was used as a sitting rug. Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din's stick rested behind his head as a pillow" (p. 159, folio 141).
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **his diet and the sharing rule built into it.** "His food consisted of wild fruit and millet bread. Abstaining from nourishment during the day, in the evening he broke his fast by taking sherbet. A bowl of it would be brought to the Baba and often mixed with dried grapes. Of this he drank never more than half, dividing the rest among his visitors. After prayers, two pieces of bread smeared with ghee were taken to Baba Farid. One was given away, the other he himself ate, sharing it with his favourite disciples" (p. 159, folio 141).
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **his relations with the Delhi Sultanate, and the land grant he refused.** "Ulugh Khan himself visited Shaikh Farid and offered a farman of four villages to the Shaikh and a cash payment for dervishes. The Shaikh returned the farman but accepted the money for distribution among the dervishes" (p. 162, folio 144); "Ulugh Khan prevented Sultan Nasiru'd-Din from seeing Baba Farid in 650/1252-53 when the Sultan was marching towards Multan" (p. 162, folio 144); "Ulugh Khan was too discreet to request the Shaikh to pray for his succession to the throne" but "paid close attention to the Shaikh's utterances for a propitious omen" (p. 162, folio 144).
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **the Balban marriage story is rejected outright by the author.** "Later Chishti records give an exaggerated account of the intimate relationship between Baba Farid and Sultan Balban (1266-87)"; "According to the Jawahar-i Faridi, after his accession Balban married his daughter to the Shaikh, but this is hardly historical as the Shaikh died before Ulugh Khan became Sultan Balban of the Delhi Sultanate" (p. 162, folio 144).
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **mass devotion in his lifetime, and the physical management of it.** "Baba Farid was tremendously popular at this time. The soldiers of Sultan Nasiru'd-Din's army also visited him. The large crowd of men concerned him, so he left his house. A sleeve from his shirt was hung from the roof of his dwelling facing the street. Crowds of people came to kiss the sleeve and in the process it was torn to shreds. Baba Farid went to the mosque and on his instructions his disciples formed a circle around him, so his devotees could not come too close. A farrash (menial servant) broke the circle of protectors and falling at the Shaikh's feet, kissed them" (pp. 162–163, folios 144–145).
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **an economic boycott by merchants reduced his community to near starvation at the end of his life.** "As a result of Sher Khan's hostility towards Baba Farid, merchants and wealthy supporters appear to have stopped sending gifts to the sufis at the jama'at-khana. This action impoverished Baba Farid, his family and other members of his religious community, during the last years of his life, and they all were reduced to near starvation" (p. 163, folio 145). The verse he is said to have recited against Sher Khan: "'Alas! You have no knowledge about me; / When you would gain knowledge about me, you would repent.'" (p. 163, folio 145).
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **one recorded intercession with a revenue official, and it failed.** An "'amil (revenue officer)" asked him "to recommend the wali (governor) of Ajodhan to refrain from harassing him. Baba Farid sent a message to the wali requesting that he treat the 'amil kindly. However, the Shaikh was unsuccessful and the wali refused to heed his advice" (p. 163, folio 145); Rizvi adds that he "offered assistance only to people who requested spiritual help and rarely made recommendations to worldly authorities" (p. 163, folio 145).
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **his composure before insult.** "The Baba was serenely indifferent to the reproaches of other men. Rude and insolent dervishes, perhaps qalandars, once visited the Shaikh. They claimed that during their long travels they had not come across one true dervish. The Shaikh requested them to sit down with him so they could become acquainted with a real dervish, but they refused" (p. 163, folio 145) — **the sentence is cut off at the chunk boundary at "Before".**
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **he is quoted rebuking another Chishti Shaikh for accepting a purpose-built khanqah.** Shaikh Badru'd-Din "wrote to Baba Farid in Ajodhan asking for his assistance. The latter replied that such misfortunes befell one who violated the traditions of his preceptors and that no other Chishti sufi had lived in a khangah [OCR?] [khanqah] built for his own personal use" (p. 156, folio 138).
- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**: **he is the counsellor on amulets in the Bakhtiyar Kaki section.** "An eminent disciple, Shaikh Faridu'd-Din or Baba Farid, sought his advice regarding the writing of amulets for which people were constantly asking. The Khwaja replied that the fulfilment of desires belonged to no-one; the amulets contained God's name and His words, and could be given to the people" (pp. 154–155, folios 136–137).
- **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya** (the book calls him "Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya"): **one sentence only, and it places him at Multan in friendship with the Chishti leader.** Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki, leaving Baghdad, "travelled through Khurasan to reach Multan. There he established a friendship with Shaikh Baha'u'd-Din Zakariyya" (p. 153, folio 135). **No dates, no silsila, no tomb, no shrine and no further act are given for him in this chunk — all (not stated).**
- **tomb-of-qutbuddin-aibak** (the book calls him "Sultan Qutbu'd-Din Aibak"): **one footnote sentence, inside the slave-biography of Iltutmish.** "Sultan Qutbu'd-Din Aibak, also a former slave, during a visit to Ghazna after his Anhilwara expedition in 1196, took a fancy to Iltutmish and was able to buy him in Delhi, and between 1197 or 1198 to 1206 Iltutmish rose to prominence" (p. 153, folio 135, footnote 2). **No death date, no tomb, no Lahore and no burial are given for Aibak here — all (not stated); the date 1206 in that sentence closes a range describing Iltutmish's rise, not Aibak's life.** Mapped on the ruled convention that a row's principal figure takes its id wherever the book names him; see Doubts on the bare-name evidence and on the three-way "Qutbu'd-Din" collision in this chunk.

**No other archive row is touched anywhere in pp. 148–163 (folios 130–145).** Explicitly: **Hinglaj is not mentioned**, so the empty-id defect on `Shaktipeeth Shri Hinglaj Mata Mandir` does not arise here and no id has been invented; **`data-darbar` is not touched** — neither "Hujwiri" nor the *Kashf al-Mahjub* appears in this chunk, in contrast to chunk 006; **`gorakhnath-goraknath-temple` and `tilla-jogian` are not touched** although Nath yogis appear (see Doubts); **and none of the many Sultans, Qazis, Shaikhs and scholars named here besides the three above is the principal figure of an archive row.** `shrine-of-jalaluddin-surkh-posh-bukhari-jalaluddin-bukhari` and `shrine-of-pir-baba-syed-ali-tirmizi` were both considered and both rejected — see Doubts.

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- **Shaikh Hamidu'd-Din (of Nagaur)** — his teaching occupies the opening page: the three types of insight into phenomenal existence, the Qur'anic proof text "'whichever way you turn there is the Face of God'" (footnoted "the quotation is from the Qur'an, Chapter II, verse 115"), self-effacement as what "opens the doors", "Heaven and hell are nothing but one's own actions", and the contrast of *futuwwa* — printed "JSutuwwa [OCR?] [futuwwa] (the qualities of the chivalrous)", "like a tree growing in a garden of friendship" — with "muruwwa (the counterpart of futuwwa)" (p. 148, folio 130). He "died on 29 Rabi' II 673/1 November 1274 and was buried at Nagaur" (p. 148, folio 130). Also called "Shaikh Hamid" (p. 148, folio 130) and, in one damaged instance, "Shaikh Hamudu'd-Din [OCR?] [Hamidu'd-Din]" (p. 149, folio 131).
- **Shaikh Faridu'd-Din Mahmud (of Nagaur)** — grandson and successor of Shaikh Hamidu'd-Din, "as the Shaikh's own son, 'Azizu'd-Din, had died during his father's lifetime"; Muhammad bin Tughluq "gave Shaikh Faridu'd-Din an honoured place in his court"; "Shaikh Farid's correspondence indicates that the Sultan also gave generous gifts to holy men, Saiyids and members of other Muslim religious groups" (p. 148, folio 130). **The book calls him "Shaikh Farid" — a direct collision with Baba Farid; see Doubts.**
- **Shaikh Fathu'llah bin Shaikh Auhadu'd-Din and Bibi Rasti** — "Sometime after 1327-28 the Sultan married his daughter, Bibi Rasti, to Shaikh Faridu'd-Din's grandson, Shaikh Fathu'llah bin Shaikh Auhadu'd-Din, after he himself had suggested it in a letter to Shaikh Farid. Fathu'llah was unable to adjust to court life" (p. 148, folio 130) and "he and his wife were allowed to settle at Deh, not far from Nagaur" (p. 149, folio 131).
- **Khwaja Husain Nagauri** — "a descendant of Shaikh Hamid, and a disciple of one of Shaikh Farid's sons, Shaikh Kabir"; "Most of the credit for the re-establishment of Nagaur as a strong centre of sufism goes in reality to" him; "lived like the local cultivators, driving a bullock cart and caring for his animals himself. He lived both at Ajmer and Nagaur"; "He wrote a detailed commentary on the Qur'an, entitled the Nuru'n-Nabi and also compiled treatises on sufism. A significant contribution was his biography of Ahmad bin Muhammad al-Ghazali" (p. 149, folio 131). He is also credited with "Other constructions, including a gateway" at Shaikh Hamidu'd-Din's tomb (p. 148, folio 130).
- **Shaikh Khizr, Shaikh Mubarak and Shaikh Abu'l-Fazl** — "The rise of the Rajput powers in Marwar and Mewar in the sixteenth century failed to undermine the importance of Nagaur as a sufi centre. As well as the Chishti silsila, the town attracted other sufis and it was this galaxy of saints and scholars that prompted Shaikh Khizr, the father of Shaikh Mubarak and the grandfather of Shaikh Abu'l-Fazl, to settle there" (p. 149, folio 131). **This Shaikh Khizr is a historical man and must not be confused with "Khwaja Khizr" of the miracle stories (p. 152, folio 134) — see Doubts.**
- **Khwaja Ziya' Nakhshabi (Ziya'u'd-Din Nakhshabi)** — "Among the disciples of Shaikh Farid, the most notable"; "Virtually nothing is known of his ancestors, but it appears they were from Nakhshab in the Sughd province and had migrated to Nagaur during the Mongol invasion of that area"; fame "based on his scholarship and mastery of the didactic and on his sufi poetry which was often interspersed in his prose. A born raconteur, his works in Persian describe many well-known sufi teachings" (p. 149, folio 131); "Nakhshabi died in 751/1350-51" (p. 151, folio 133). Works named: "The Silku's-Suluk (String of Sufism) … describes the basic principles of the movement in 151 short chapters" (p. 149, folio 131); "*Ashra-i Mubasshshara [OCR?] ['Ashra-i Mubashshara] and Kulliyyat-wa Juziyyat are of interest, but he is mainly remembered for his work the Tuti Nama (Stories from a Parrot)" (p. 150, folio 132); and "the Lazzatu'n-Nisa' (Sex Enjoyments)" (p. 151, folio 133).
- **Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki** — "the leader of the Chishti sufis in Delhi"; "born at Ush, in the province of the Jaxartes", an area that in the tenth century "had also developed into a strong centre for sufis of the school of al-Hallaj"; his father "Kamalu'd-Din Ahmad Musa" died when he was eighteen months old; he learnt the Qur'an "under Aba Hafs [OCR?] [Abu Hafs]" and "finally committed it to memory after he had settled in Delhi" (p. 152, folio 134); divorced his wife "finding his wife an obstacle to prayer" and "left for Baghdad. There, in a mosque, he met Khwaja Mu'inu'd-Din Chishti", becoming his disciple "in spite of the fact that other eminent and pious sufis, including Shaikh 'Abdu'l Qadir Jilani and Shaikh Abu'n-Najib Suhrawardi also lived in Baghdad at that time" (p. 152, folio 134); he "preferred to stay in Kilukhari, near the Jumna" before agreeing to live in Delhi, and "Twice weekly he was visited by the eminent people of Delhi" (p. 153, folio 135); he refused the post of Shaikhu'l-Islam (p. 154, folio 136); "Two sons were born" to him, "One survived after his death, but the other died when he was seven years old" (p. 155, folio 137); he "trained nine disciples, only two of which are well-known" (p. 156, folio 138); he died "On the fifth night, 14 Rabi' I 633/27 November 1235 … and was buried in Mahrauli about eleven miles from Delhi, at a place he himself had chosen" (p. 156, folio 138).
- **Khwaja Mu'inu'd-Din Chishti** — met Qutbu'd-Din in a Baghdad mosque (p. 152, folio 134); ordered him to remain in Delhi rather than come to Ajmer, "believing he should continue his work there" (p. 153, folio 135); travelled to Delhi "to investigate the confrontation between the Khwaja and the Shaikh", upbraided Najmu'd-Din Sughra, and then reversed his own decision to remove Qutbu'd-Din when the Delhi crowds followed him (p. 154, folio 136); prophesied Baba Farid's fame (pp. 157–158, folios 139–140). **His tomb is named as a building project: "the tomb of Khwaja Mu'inu'd-Din" (p. 149, folio 131). He has no archive row — see Doubts.**
- **Qazi Hamidu'd-Din Nagauri (Shaikh Muhammad 'Ata)** — "Shaikh Muhammad 'Ata, called Qazi Hamud'd-Din Nagauri [OCR?] [Hamidu'd-Din], who had known the Khwaja at Baghdad, settled in Delhi. The Qazi, although a Suhrawardi was deeply interested in the practice of sama' as a source for inducing a mystical state of ecstasy" (p. 153, folio 135); he is the man who executes Qutbu'd-Din's will and hands the relics to Baba Farid (p. 158, folio 140).
- **Najmu'd-Din Sughra** — received the office of Shaikhu'l-Islam after Qutbu'd-Din refused it; "Gradually, however, the Shaikh became jealous of the Khwaja and used the latter's practice of gaining spiritual ecstasy through sama' as a pretext to incite the 'ulama' against him"; he "refused to call, as was traditional, on Khwaja Mu'inu'd-Din", then "apologized, admitting that he was concerned at the people's devotion to Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din" (p. 154, folio 136).
- **Shaikh Badru'd-Din (of Ghazna)** — "His successor in Delhi was Shaikh Badru'd-Din, who came from Ghazna. From there he received news of the death of his family during the Mongol invasion"; "it was his skill as a poet and orator that made him famous. All the eminent 'ulama' and sufis were attracted to his sermons"; "A great enthusiast for sama' rituals, at a ripe old age Shaikh Badru'd-Din still danced like a boy of ten. He died in about 657/1258-59 and was buried near the tomb of his master, Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki" (p. 156, folio 138). Probably the same man as "Badru'd-Din Ghaznawi" (p. 158, folio 140) — see Doubts.
- **Maulana Badru'd-Din Ishaq** — one of Baba Farid's eminent disciples "who lived in other areas", who "stayed and became a steward of the jama'at-khana"; he wrote out amulets when demand exceeded what the Baba could supply (p. 160, folio 142). **A third Badru'd-Din in the chunk — see Doubts.**
- **Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya'** — the transmitter of the three dated yogi conversations at Baba Farid's jama'at-khana, "Many years later these were recalled by Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya' and retold to his disciples" (p. 161, folio 143); he questions a yogi on "the secret and real essence of his mystic path" (p. 161, folio 143, printed "Shaikh Nizammu'd-Din Auliya' [OCR?] [Nizamu'd-Din]"); he is "disgusted" at a disciple seeking hair-growth medicine (p. 161, folio 143, printed "Shaikh Nazamu'd-Din [OCR?] [Nizamu'd-Din]"); and he memorises and repeats the yogi's list of conception days, to Baba Farid's dismissal (p. 162, folio 144).
- **Nizamu'd-Din Kharitadar (The Treasurer)** — the official who "built a khangah [OCR?] [khanqah] for the Shaikh" Badru'd-Din, then "was charged with embezzlement and lost his position. His loss of power tended to undermine the prestige of the Shaikh" (p. 156, folio 138). **Not the same man as Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya' — see Doubts.**
- **Shaikh Jalalu'd-Din Tabrizi** — the distinguished visitor whom the townsfolk of Kahtwal told about Baba Farid (p. 157, folio 139); in the footnote he visits Baba Farid, who "had only torn clothing to wear", and offers him a broken pomegranate (p. 157, folio 139, footnote 2). **Considered against an Uch archive row and rejected — see Doubts.**
- **Maulana Minhaju'd-Din Tirmizi** — named once, as the man at whose Multan mosque Baba Farid joined a seminary at eighteen (p. 157, folio 139). **A bare name with no dates and no further act — see Doubts.**

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **A Sultan builds a tomb over a dead Shaikh, and the completion date is printed to the day.** "Sultan Muhammad bin Tughlug [OCR?] [Tughluq] erected a tomb over his remains, which was completed on 15 Sah'ban [OCR?] [Sha'ban] 730/3 June 1330. Other constructions, including a gateway, were built by Shaikh Husain Nagauri" (p. 148, folio 130). **A builder, a beneficiary, a completion date and a named later addition — the fullest shrine-construction datum in the chunk. The Hijri month name is damaged; see Doubts.**
- **Royal endowment of a saint's family, and their absorption into the administration.** "Muhammad bin Tughluq liberally bestowed endowments on the Shaikh's family and gave Shaikh Faridu'd-Din an honoured place in his court. Other descendants of Shaikh Hamid obtained important administrative posts, mainly in the Nagaur region" (p. 148, folio 130). **Descent from a saint converted into office — the mechanism behind hereditary custodianship, though the book does not use the word sajjada nashin anywhere in this chunk.**
- **A marriage alliance between the Sultanate and a saint's lineage, initiated by the Sultan in writing.** "Sometime after 1327-28 the Sultan married his daughter, Bibi Rasti, to Shaikh Faridu'd-Din's grandson, Shaikh Fathu'llah bin Shaikh Auhadu'd-Din, after he himself had suggested it in a letter to Shaikh Farid" (p. 148, folio 130).
- **Royal money spent on two tombs, named — the chunk's most directly usable shrine-economy sentence.** Of Khwaja Husain Nagauri: "the money he received from Sultan Ghiyasu'd-Din Khalji (1469-1501) was spent in constructing the tomb of Khwaja Mu'inu'd-Din and the gateway of Shaikh Hamudu'd-Din's [OCR?] [Hamidu'd-Din's] tomb" (p. 149, folio 131). **Royal cash to a living descendant, converted by him into shrine fabric at two separate sites.**
- **A khanqah built for a Shaikh by a state officer, and the Chishti rule it broke.** Shaikh Badru'd-Din "became a close associate of Nizamu'd-Din Kharitadar (The Treasurer) who built a khangah [OCR?] [khanqah] for the Shaikh"; when the Treasurer fell for embezzlement, Baba Farid wrote back that "such misfortunes befell one who violated the traditions of his preceptors and that no other Chishti sufi had lived in a khangah [khanqah] built for his own personal use" (p. 156, folio 138).
- **Burial beside the master — the practice chunk 006 could not find in pp. 100–117.** Shaikh Badru'd-Din "died in about 657/1258-59 and was buried near the tomb of his master, Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki" (p. 156, folio 138); and the master himself "was buried in Mahrauli about eleven miles from Delhi, at a place he himself had chosen" (p. 156, folio 138). **A saint choosing his own burial place in advance, and a disciple buried at his feet.**
- **Succession transmitted by relics under a will.** Qazi Hamidu'd-Din Nagauri "gave Baba Farid relics from the Khwaja, including his khirga [OCR?] [khirqa], turban, stick and wooden sandals" (p. 158, folio 140), and one of those objects stays in use: "Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din's stick rested behind his head as a pillow" (p. 159, folio 141).
- **Futuh as the working income of a household, twice.** Qutbu'd-Din repaid the grocer "as soon as some futuh was received" (p. 155, folio 137); Baba Farid's rule was stricter — "Anything received as futuh if not used immediately was kept no longer than a day and was distributed to the needy. To a sufi a new day ushered in new hope in God, and a concern for future needs was seen as totally opposed to a complete trust in Him" (p. 160, folio 142).
- **A twice-daily collection of offerings in the town, by a named vessel, pooled for all.** "In the early years of his stay at Ajodhan a zanbil (a basket made of palm leaves hung round the neck) was carried by some members of the jama'at-khana twice a day to the town and the offerings placed in it were shared by everyone. The Baba also preferred to eat bread from the zanbil, perhaps to be more fully integrated into the life of all members of his jama'at-khana" (p. 160, folio 142). The footnote cross-refers to "how Baba Sa'id Dust Dada, a disciple of Shaikh Abu Sa'id, collected food in a cumbil, p. 74, supra" (p. 160, folio 142).
- **Borrowing: permitted by his teachers, forbidden by him.** "Other Chishti Shaikhs, including his own teachers, had permitted the borrowing of small amounts of money for household needs, but the Baba strictly forbade this practice" (p. 160, folio 142). **Compare Qutbu'd-Din borrowing "up to thirty dirhams (copper coins) from a neighbouring Muslim grocer for household expenses" (p. 155, folio 137) — see Doubts.**
- **Foraging as institutional supply.** "Fuel and wild berries, which were then boiled, were collected from the forests" (p. 160, folio 142).
- **The physical plant of the jama'at-khana, and its furniture rule.** "The jama'at-khana consisted only of a thatched hall, used for communal living and a separate cell was for the Shaikh's meditation"; "The jama'at-khana contained not a single piece of furniture, every member sat and slept on the floor. On special occasions a bed was provided for a new visitor. The Shaikh, followed the same practices as his disciples. Once when ill and forced to rest on the bed he apologized profusely to those sitting on the floor" (p. 159, folio 141).
- **Who actually came — a stated social cross-section, and two lengths of stay.** "The jama'at-khana, situated on a main route, attracted many visitors. These included scholars, merchants, government servants, artisans, sufis and qalandars, in short, men from all classes and sections of Indian society. Some, disillusioned with a life of affluence and comfort, became permanent members, others stayed for short periods to seek the Baba's blessing and experience a spiritual rejuvenation" (p. 160, folio 142).
- **A named office inside the establishment.** "The establishment was run by Baba's chief disciples" (p. 160, folio 142), and Maulana Badru'd-Din Ishaq "stayed and became a steward of the jama'at-khana" (p. 160, folio 142).
- **Amulets as a mass service, with a stated supply problem.** "A large number requested ta'wiz (amulets) and the demand for amulets became so great that it was physically impossible for the Baba to fulfil all requests, so often they would be written instead by Maulana Ishaq" (p. 160, folio 142); and the doctrinal licence for them from Qutbu'd-Din — "the fulfilment of desires belonged to no-one; the amulets contained God's name and His words, and could be given to the people" (pp. 154–155, folios 136–137).
- **A farman of four villages offered and refused, the cash accepted for redistribution.** "Ulugh Khan himself visited Shaikh Farid and offered a farman of four villages to the Shaikh and a cash payment for dervishes. The Shaikh returned the farman but accepted the money for distribution among the dervishes" (p. 162, folio 144). **A land grant declined and a cash grant taken — the sharpest statement in the chunk of where a Chishti Shaikh drew the line with the state.**
- **Merchant and elite gifts as the community's economic base, demonstrated by their withdrawal.** "merchants and wealthy supporters appear to have stopped sending gifts to the sufis at the jama'at-khana. This action impoverished Baba Farid, his family and other members of his religious community … and they all were reduced to near starvation" (p. 163, folio 145).
- **Crowd management around a living saint, with a garment used as a contact relic.** "A sleeve from his shirt was hung from the roof of his dwelling facing the street. Crowds of people came to kiss the sleeve and in the process it was torn to shreds"; "on his instructions his disciples formed a circle around him, so his devotees could not come too close. A farrash (menial servant) broke the circle of protectors and falling at the Shaikh's feet, kissed them" (pp. 162–163, folios 144–145).
- **Dust from a saint's road collected as a relic.** "Iltutmish and the people of Delhi were so upset at the Khwaja's departure that he was followed, and the dust on the road where he had passed was collected as a relic" (p. 154, folio 136).
- **Sama' as the object of state arbitration.** "The Qazi, although a Suhrawardi was deeply interested in the practice of sama' as a source for inducing a mystical state of ecstasy. Unwavering in their opposition to sama', the 'ulama' were over-ruled by Iltutmish who supported the sufis, using them as a counter to the former" (pp. 153–154, folios 135–136). **A Sultan settling a ritual dispute in the sufis' favour, and using them politically while doing so.**
- **Sama' inside a khanqah, and as the setting of a death.** Qutbu'd-Din "took part in a sama' ritual in the khangah [khanqah] of Shaikh 'Ali Sijzi" (p. 155, folio 137); Shaikh Badru'd-Din, "A great enthusiast for sama' rituals, at a ripe old age … still danced like a boy of ten" (p. 156, folio 138); and the attempted fatwa at Ajodhan was aimed at anyone who "lived in a mosque, was known as a dervish, listened to music or danced" (pp. 158–159, folios 140–141).
- **The office of Shaikhu'l-Islam, refused.** "The Sultan offered the post of Shaikhu'l-Islam to Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din, but after he refused it the office was conferred on Najmu'd-Din Sughra" (p. 154, folio 136).
- **A fixed visiting rhythm for the elite.** "Twice weekly he was visited by the eminent people of Delhi" (p. 153, folio 135).
- **The chilla and the chilla-i ma'kus as specified exercises** — forty days of "spiritual exercises and fasting", and the inverted form requiring suspension in a well "head down, for forty days and nights, while both fasting and praying" (p. 158, folio 140).
- **Head-shaving as a required sufi discipline with a stated reason.** "hair shaving was performed to cleanse one from pride and was therefore an essential part of sufi piety" (p. 161, folio 143).
- **A stated doctrine of institutional transmission by training numbers.** Qutbu'd-Din "trained nine disciples, only two of which are well-known" (p. 156, folio 138), and Baba Farid is called another of his "khalifas" (p. 156, folio 138).
- **A cash reward measured in tankas, and its refusal.** The Sultan "rewarded him with thirty-five thousand tankas. In gratitude, the poet requested the Khwaja to take half for the poor, but the Khwaja refused to accept payment" (p. 154, folio 136).
- **Nine dependants in a saint's household.** "According to him the Khwaja's family totalled nine" (p. 155, folio 137).
- **Open discussion as house practice, with named non-Muslim participants.** "Discussions in the jama'at-khana were liberal and covered all aspects of sufism. Baba Farid also participated but he gave others the opportunity to express their own opinions freely" (p. 161, folio 143); "Amongst the most interesting conversations recorded are probably those between the jogis or Nath yogi visitors to the jama'at-khana" (p. 161, folio 143). **Three of these are dated to the day: "Wednesday, 23 Muharram 712/31 May 1312" (p. 161, folio 143); "Thursday, 5 Ramazan 720/9 October 1320" (p. 161, folio 143); "Sunday, 11 Zu'lhijja 720/12 January 1321" (p. 161, folio 143).**
- **No ʿurs, no mela, no langar, no Auqaf, no sajjada nashin, no visitor numbers and no revenue figure appear anywhere in pp. 148–163 (folios 130–145).** The food-sharing at Ajodhan (p. 159, folio 141) and the pooled zanbil collection (p. 160, folio 142) are the nearest thing to langar, and the book does not call them that; the Nagaur descendants' "important administrative posts" (p. 148, folio 130) are the nearest thing to custodianship, and the book does not call it that either.

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

Attributions are the book's own, including its hedges ("According to tradition", "A traditional story", "There was a story", "is hardly historical", "Both stories are myths").

- **Khwaja Khizr delivers the child Qutbu'd-Din to his Qur'an teacher — two incompatible versions, and the author's flat verdict on the genre.** Version one, from Shaikh Nasiru'd-Din Chiragh-i Dihli: the mother "gave some sweets to her slave girl and asked her to take the child to the Hafiz who lived close-by. On the way Qutbu'd-Din met an old man who took him to the mosque where Aba Hafs [OCR?] [Abu Hafs] taught children. Aba Hafs warmly greeted the old man and kissed his feet … Aba Hafs then told Qutbu'd-Din that the old man was Khwaja Khizr." Version two, Jamali's: "when Qutbu'd-Din was five, his mother asked one of her neighbours to take the boy to a teacher. Khwaja Khizr met the neighbour on the way to school and offered to take the boy himself." Rizvi: "Some type of intervention by Khwaja Khizr in sufi stories is quite common and is included to add a pious flavour" (p. 152, folio 134, footnote 1).
- **The blind arrow that turns back the Mongols, with the author's rationalist counter-explanation in the same sentence.** "Nasiru'd-Din, Qubacha of Multan, requested Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din for help against the enemy. Giving him an arrow, the Khwaja suggested he shoot it blindly into the enemy's camp. Qubacha obeyed, and the following day the Mongols retreated. Although, in reality, the Mongols left the Indus region for political reasons, this further increased Qubacha's devotion to the Khwaja" (p. 153, folio 135).
- **The Prophet appears to a Sultan and a saint to site a public water tank.** "A traditional story which presents the sanctity and supernatural powers of Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din Bakhtiyar involved a tank which was built to overcome Delhi's water shortage. Sultan Iltutmish devised a scheme for it, but was unsure where to choose the site. According to tradition, the Prophet Muhammad appeared to both the Sultan and the Khwaja, indicating a particular spot" (p. 154, folio 136).
- **A poet's success at court obtained by mental invocation of the saint.** "A poet named, Nasiri from Transoxiana, begged the Khwaja to pray for the success of his poetry at the Sultan's court… At court a recitation of the first verse failed to capture the Sultan's attention, but the poet mentally invoked the power of the Khwaja. At that point, the Sultan began to listen with rapt attention" (p. 154, folio 136).
- **The miraculous bread that gives Kaki his name — two versions, differing in mechanism.** Amir Khwurd's: "a piece of bread would miraculously appear under his prayer carpet… The wife revealed the secret of the bread's appearance and it never appeared again" (p. 155, folio 137). Jamali's: "He pointed to a niche in his cell and told his wife to go there and recite B'ismillah and she would get as much bread as she needed. So the Khwaja became known as 'Kaki' as he lived on miraculously received breads alone" (p. 155, folio 137). The name is glossed "the word Kaki or Man of Bread" (p. 155, folio 137).
- **A saint so absorbed he does not know his own son has died.** "The story depicts the complete other-worldliness of the Khwaja as he was totally unaware of his son's illness or death" (p. 155, folio 137).
- **Death by sustained ecstasy over five nights, triggered by a sung verse.** The verse, by Shaikh Ahmad of Jam: "'The martyrs of the dagger of taslim (surrender) / Each moment get a new life from the Unseen World.'" (p. 155, folio 137); "the Khwaja ordered the verse to be repeated each time he regained consciousness, which always occurred at the time of obligatory prayers. He then lapsed back into an ecstatic state. On the fifth night … he died" (p. 156, folio 138).
- **A thief blinded by the sight of Baba Farid's praying mother, and healed by her.** "a thief, entering the house glanced at the praying woman and was instantly blinded. The robber implored the Shaikh's mother to restore his eyesight. She did so, and the blindness was removed. The following day, the thief returned with his family to the Shaikh's house and was converted to Islam" (p. 157, folio 139). **A conversion attributed to a woman's karamat — the only conversion episode in the chunk.**
- **The pomegranate seed that illuminates the heart.** Jalalu'd-Din Tabrizi "had with him a pomegranate, and broke it, offering it to Baba Farid. As the Baba was fasting he refused to eat it, but after his guest had left he picked upa [OCR?] [picked up a] seed and in the evening ate that. Instantly his heart was illuminated and he lamented not having eaten the whole fruit. However in Delhi Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din consoled the Baba saying that in each pomegranate there was only one seed endowed with spiritual blessings and that he had had it" (p. 157, folio 139, footnote 2).
- **Pebbles turning to sugar three times, and the master's ruling that legitimises eating them.** "overpowered by incessant fasting for three days, the Baba placed some pebbles in his mouth. These immediately changed into sugar. Baba Farid, believing that this had been the work of the devil, spat them out… Finally, overpowered by extreme hunger he ate some pebbles which had become sugar so that he could continue praying. Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din approved his action, telling him that whatever was received from the unseen world must necessarily be good" (p. 158, folio 140).
- **A wall felled instead of a disciple.** "Seeing that the qalandar was determined to strike the disciple, the Baba asked him to strike the wall instead. The qalandar complied and the wall fell down" (pp. 160–161, folios 142–143).
- **A dervish's prophecy of kingship, reported and then rejected by the author.** "A dervish there prophesied he would become a king and according to Minhaj Siraj, Iltutmish owed his throne to the blessings of that dervish"; the competing Baghdad versions from the *Fawa'idu'l-Fu'ad* and "'Isami"; and Rizvi's verdict — "Both stories are myths because the better slave market was at Ghazna not Baghdad" (p. 153, folio 135, footnote 2).
- **A verse read as a political omen.** Ulugh Khan "found one in the following verse: 'The glorious Faridun was not an angel, / He was not made of aloe wood or ambergris, / Justice and munificence were the source of his celebrity, / If you are munificent and just, you are Faridun.'" (p. 162, folio 144).
- **A dervish's fatalism against a king's request for intercession.** "There was a story that the king of Nishapur sent a message to a dervish to beg him to avert a Mongol invasion. The dervish replied that it was already too late and only a surrender to the divine will was advisable at that stage" (p. 150, folio 132), footnoted as "generally ascribed to Shaikh Faridu'd-Din 'Attar" (p. 150, folio 132, footnote 1).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Why Delhi became the centre of Indian sufism — a refugee thesis, stated as cause.** "at the beginning of the thirteenth century, due to Mongol invasions in Central Asia and Iran, Delhi became the heart of the sufi movement. Sultan Shamsu'd-Din Iltutmish (1210-35) moved his capital to Delhi, andthe Indian empire of the Delhi Sultans becametheonly peaceful region [OCR?] [and the Indian empire of the Delhi Sultans became the only peaceful region] in the Islamic east" (p. 151, folio 133); "The exodus of scholars and holy men from Central Asia and Iran after the fall of the Muslim powers to the Mongol barbarians, made Delhi the strongest eastern Islamic capital" (p. 152, folio 134). **Note the author's own word "barbarians".**
- **Establishing the Chishtis in Delhi is framed as a contest for respectability.** "For Qutbu'd-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki, the leader of the Chishti sufis in Delhi, it was a Herculean task to firmly establish the order there" (p. 152, folio 134); "The Khwaja's task in Delhi was made extremely arduous as it involved making the Chishti order respectable amongst the most eminent and prestigious Muslim divines of the Islamic world, many of whom had collected there" (p. 153, folio 135).
- **Politics as a cause of Chishti decline in Delhi, and the transfer of the order's centre of gravity.** "Shaikh Badru'd-Din's political associations retarded the growth of the Chishti tradition in Delhi. The image of that order, however, was perfected in distant Ajodhan by another of Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din's khalifas, Shaikh Faridu'd-Din Ganj-i Shakar" (p. 156, folio 138); against the general claim that "in general, Chishti sufis refrained from such activity" (p. 156, folio 138). **See Doubts for the contradiction this sets up.**
- **Rizvi against K.A. Nizami, twice, on two different questions.** On Iltutmish: "Nizami's attempt to prove that Iltutmish's rise to the throne was due to the influence of Baghdad mystics precludes him from re-examining other historical possibilities" (p. 153, folio 135). On Ajodhan: "The Baba did not, therefore, select Ajodhan in order to reform or convert 'backward Hindu tribes', as Nizami seeks to show" (p. 159, folio 141). **The second is the chunk's most consequential claim for the archive: Rizvi denies that a major Punjab Chishti shrine site was chosen as a conversion mission.**

This has led to considerable confusion in later works. According to the Siyaru'l-Auliya', Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din met Khwaja Mu'inu'd-Din in the mosque of Imam Abu'l-Lais Samarqandi, see p. 48. - **Nakhshabi's Tuti Nama as a documented transformation of a Sanskrit text.** "This is the Persian version of the Sanskrit work entitled Suka-Saptati by Chintamani Bhatta"; "The original seventy stories in Nakhshabi's version were reduced to fifty-two and the new work contained a large number of Hindi words. Nakhshabi arranged it in a more elegant style and substituted some new stories for the old"; he "dubbed the characters with Arabic names, adding a Persian background and changed the ending" — where the Sanskrit ends in reconciliation and the parrot's release, "In Nakhshabi's work … The latter kills his wife and frees the parrot. The husband then embraces sufism" (pp. 150–151, folios 132–133). The region from the head to the navel is associated with Shiva, while that below the navel is connected with Shakti" (p. 161, folio 143). **See Doubts on the deity question.**
- **What the yogi conversations are offered as evidence of.** "These three stories indicate the broad range of discussions held at the jama'at-khana" (p. 162, folio 144). **Rizvi draws no conversion or syncretism conclusion from them here.**
- **Nakhshabi's own positions as Rizvi reports them** — only "a knowledge of the Tawhid and the Shari'a excelled a general understanding of sufism"; "To him the intellect and love were diametrically opposed. The 'ulama' were masters of the intellect and fagirs [OCR?] [faqirs] were people of love… Only prophets could dominate both of them, as human beings were the weakest of all creatures" (p. 149, folio 131); the four categories of men — the outwardly pious but inwardly unworthy ("the worldly"), the "inwardly brijliant [OCR?] [brilliant], but externally bad" ("majzubs"), those "as evil as the outward one" ("the common people"), and those "excellent both inwardly and outwardly" ("the sufis") (p. 150, folio 132); the world as a prison, taught by warm bitter water (p. 150, folio 132); and the reciprocity rule — "Without some of the qualities of a dervish an 'alim was like an animal and a dervish without some of the virtues acquired from learning was ineffectual in his own spiritual journey" (p. 150, folio 132).
- **Nakhshabi's Islam of the middle way, in his own verse.** "'Oh Nakhshabi! Adopt the religion of those who follow a middle course. / The Prophet himself has ordained to do so. / The middle of the road policy is praiseworthy. / The commandment of Islam is moderation.'" (p. 151, folio 133).
- **Nagaur's resilience as a sufi centre across a political change.** "The rise of the Rajput powers in Marwar and Mewar in the sixteenth century failed to undermine the importance of Nagaur as a sufi centre" (p. 149, folio 131).

26. **Baba Farid's birth year is given twice, and the book prefers neither** — "569/1173-74 or 571/1175-76" (p. 157, folio 139). **Set against his death "on 5 Muharram 664/17 October 1265" (p. 158, folio 140), the two birth dates give ages at death of 95 and 93 AH years; the book does not state his age and I have not computed one into any bullet above.**
27. **Where Baba Farid was initiated is disputed inside one paragraph** — "According to Jamali, Baba Farid was initiated by the Khwaja at Multan", against Rizvi's "it is more likely that Baba Farid reached Delhi shortly after the Khwaja's arrival and was initiated into … the Khwaja's jamu'at-khana" (p. 157, folio 139). **Multan or Delhi, and the book's own preference is hedged with "more likely".**
28. **Where Qutbu'd-Din was initiated has five answers in the book** — Baghdad (main text, p. 152, folio 134), Ush, the mosque of Imam Abu'l-Lais Samarqandi, "at the age of eighteen" then Baghdad, and Isfahan (all p. 152, folio 134, footnote 2). **Rizvi names the consequence — "This has led to considerable confusion in later works" — and does not adjudicate.**
29. **One date set can be checked from inside the chunk and it holds**: Shaikh Hamidu'd-Din died "29 Rabi' II 673/1 November 1274" and the tomb Muhammad bin Tughluq built over him "was completed on 15 Sah'ban [Sha'ban] 730/3 June 1330" (p. 148, folio 130) — 730 − 673 = 57 AH years between death and completed tomb, which is internally coherent with the Sultan's reign as the chunk describes it. **No other AH/CE pair in the chunk can be cross-checked from within it**, and all of them — 673/1274, 730/1330, 751/1350-51, 633/1235, 657/1258-59, 569/1173-74, 571/1175-76, 664/1265, 650/1252-53, 536/1141, 712/1312, 720/1320, 720/1321 — are recorded above exactly as printed and are **unverified**.
