## Practices, institutions, economy

**This is the strongest section of the chunk, and it is strong because Chapter Seven is a summing-up of exactly this material. None of it is about a Pakistani shrine.**

- **The khanqah count for Delhi, with the book's own hedge.** "In the reign of Muhammad bin Tughlugq [Tughluq], while in Damascus Shihabu'd-Din Ahmad al-'Umari was told that in Delhi there were two thousand large khanqahs. **The number may have been exaggerated, but it is clear that by that time khangahs [khanqahs] exercised a deep social, political, economic and cultural influence in India**" (p. 421, folio not stated).
- **Sufism as an agent of urbanisation, and one of the three examples is in Pakistan.** "In some cases, the deep impact of sufism and its popularity among the masses transformed rural regions, such as **Uch**, Nagaur and Sylhet, into flourishing urban centres" (p. 421, folio not stated). **The chunk's only economic claim about a Pakistani place, and it names no shrine, saint or custodian there.**
- **Who joined orders, and why.** "The reliance of sufis on God, particularly in the material sense, attracted both people from economically deprived classes and members of the oppressed élite into orders. Khangahs [khanqahs] gave to most people a feeling of hope and a vision of a bright future, both in this world and the one to come" (p. 421, folio not stated).
- **The Suhrawardis as a political and financial interest.** "They also amassed large fortunes and tried to pressurize the government into taking a very narrow view of the world. **Through the Suhrawardis petitions from the people were presented to rulers and their periodic visits to Delhi were eagerly awaited**" (p. 422, folio 398). **The clearest statement of shrine-lineage brokerage in the chunk, and it names the silsila of eleven archive rows.**
- **The Chishtis as an emergency service.** "Assistance from the withdrawn and ascetic Chishtis who had turned their backs on the world was also sought to avert such calamities as drought and panic, for example, during times of political crises" (p. 422, folio 398); "the Chishti khangahs [khanqahs] did offer consolation, peace and nourishment to the thousands of Muslims who crowded the towns" (p. 422, folio 398).
- **'Spiritual insurance' — the chunk's single most quotable economic institution.** "Some khangahs [khanqahs] operated a type of **'spiritual insurance' scheme in which financial pledges were made by merchants in return for sufi prayers for protection during a journey, instalments being collected en route.** The chain of khangahs of Shaikh Abu Ishaq from Kazirun to China is one case in point. Naturally the system was one-sided and hardly compares with a modern insurance scheme. **If the prayers of a great sufi saint failed, the unfortunate merchants and travellers were killed, and there was no means by which the money advanced could be restored**" (p. 423, folio 399). Elaborated in Appendix C: "Many sufis of the Kaziruni orders began to travel from India to China to collect money promised by sailors and merchants to the order for the protection believed to have been given to them by Shaikh Abu Ishaq Kaziruni" (p. 433, folio not stated); "money pledged to Shaikh Abu Ishaq Kaziruni was paid to the khangah in Calicut. Its Shaikh was Shihabu'd-Din Kaziruni, and all gifts promised to his order's founder were presented to him" (p. 434, folio 410); and the summary judgement, "khanqahs established by the disciples of Shaikh Abu Ishaq Kaziruni were not only centres for the collection of money and for hostel accommodation to travellers, but also gave the local people in these areas a greater exposure to the ideas of Islam" (p. 434, folio 410).
- **ʿurs as a cultural institution — the chunk's general statement on ʿurs, and it is not attached to any named shrine.** **"The 'urs ceremonies and other anniversaries celebrated in khanqgahs [OCR?] [khanqahs] developed into significant cultural institutions and were eagerly awaited by both the poor and affluent alike"** (p. 423, folio 399).
- **A named ʿurs with an expenditure attached, at Sarkhij in Gujarat.** "At Gujarat Shaikh Ahmad lived like a prince. **Large amounts were spent on such occasions as the 'urs of Baba Ishaq Maghribi, although the money was also spent on charity throughout the year**" (p. 432, folio 408), sourced "ibid, ff. 33b-34a, 59b-60a" (p. 432, folio 408, footnote 2).
- **Futuh at a scale, with a named officer and named sums — the chunk's most concrete shrine-economy passage.** "Gradually large amounts of futuh were given to him. **The Shaikh was forced to employ a steward (baqqal) to handle the large amounts of futuh and its dispersal.** According to the Tuhfatu'l-Majalis, Shaikh Ahmad's malfuzat, **once a year, two hundred thousand tankas were spent by the khanqah in gifts. Some nobles gave such large amounts as 25,000 tankas**" (p. 430, folio 406), sourced "ibid, ff. 19b-20b" (p. 430, folio 406, footnote 5).
- **Futuh as corruption — Rizvi's verdict, and it is the Conclusion's harshest economic line.** **"The continual flow of futuh became a source of degeneration to khangahs [khanqahs] and led to the gradual dependence of their inmates on the state, merchants and the nobility"** (p. 424, folio 400).
- **A mausoleum built by a sultan, with a langar endowed at it — the chunk's only langar.** "The building of a beautiful mausoleum over the earthly remains of the Shaikh was commenced by Muhammad Shah at Sarkhij and completed in the reign of his successor Qutbu'd-Din Ahmad (1451-59). **Even after his death huge amounts of money were spent in the /angar [OCR?] [langar] of the mausoleum for the provision of food for the poor**" (p. 431, folio 407), sourced "AA, p. 157" (p. 431, folio 407, footnote 4). **A building sequence, two named royal patrons and a food endowment, all for a Gujarati shrine.**
- **Futuh refused — the counter-case, in Nizamu'd-Din Auliya's own story.** "The Shaikh asked the man how could he, a lowly-paid labourer, refuse to accept futuh. The man replied that his father had also sold vegetables… When she was dying, she pointed to some money hidden in the thatched roof. **Some of it was to be used for her burial**, she said, **the rest, twenty dirhams, was the son's share.** Like his father he should continue as a vegetable seller and not depend for his living on anything or anyone else" (p. 426, folio 402), the offered gift being "a silver tanka", sourced "KM, pp. 277-78" (p. 426, folio 402, footnote 3). **A burial provision, a named coin and an inheritance, in one paragraph.**
- **Mortuary practice at Ajodhan: prayers and food for the poor for a soul.** "the latter appropriately had prayers said and food distributed to the poor for the repose of his mother's soul" (p. 426, folio 402).
- **Tomb visitation as crisis management, twice, both by Nizamu'd-Din Auliya'.** "To the end of his days, the Shaikh would pray at her tomb during any crisis or period of anxiety" (p. 426, folio 402); "After her death, Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din Auliya' used to go to her tomb to offer prayers and obtain spiritual satisfaction" (p. 426, folio 402).
- **A shrine's life-cycle and its decline, in one sentence.** "Bibi Fatima's tomb was in the old Indraprastha; **it was a rendezvous for holy men until either the fourteenth or fifteenth century, but by the end of the sixteenth century it was deserted**" (p. 427, folio 403). **A pond and a tank are named at the same tomb: "the tomb of Bibi Sam which was near a pond… he dropped them near the tank where he performed ablutions"** (p. 426, folio 402).
- **The structural exclusion of women from the khanqah system — the load-bearing institutional claim of Appendix A.** **"Moreover, female mystics were never incorporated into khanqahs and orders as spiritual succession could not be traced through them. Often they became hermits or lone dervishes and more often than not, lived without the comforts, both spiritual and material, provided by pirs and khangahs [khanqahs]"** (p. 427, folio 403). **This bears on every archive row with a woman as principal figure even though the appendix names none of them.**
- **Conversion, and the refusal of the standard explanation.** "What is noteworthy is that **both Chishtis and Suhrawardis only managed to convert high caste Hindus. The theory that the influence of sufism and of Islamic 'egalitarianism' were significant factors which led members of the Hindu lower classes to embrace Islam is unfounded.** The Muslim conquests did not unleash forces of liberation or change the position of the exploited castes of Hindus and of the untouchables. The social and economic position of the masses of Muslim converts who accepted Islam under a variety of pressures, all which have been analysed by Ja'far-i Makki, was in fact no better than that of Hindus" (p. 422, folio 398).
- **Manual labour, and the comparison with monasteries.** "Most sufi khangahs [khanqahs] urged their disciples to pursue hard manual labour in order to crush the lower self, but **unlike medieval Christian monasteries they did not invent labour saving devices, for example in a field like agriculture**" (p. 423, folio 399).
- **Sama' and Hindi dohas, with qawwals named.** "Shaikh Ahmad was very fond of sama'. While with Tumur's [Timur's] army he succeeded in convincing the 'ulama' of Khurasan that sama' was quite legal. He himself composed Hindi dohas, which gawwals [OCR?] [qawwals] would recite at his sama' gatherings" (p. 432, folio 408).
- **The Hindi turn in sufi poetry, and the book's argument that it was not missionary.** "Sufi poetry written in Hindi added a new dimension to Indian mysticism… **The use of ancient Indian music and language was not chosen with a missionary intent for the recital of the Chanda'in in mosques could in no way serve Islamic proselytization**" (p. 423, folio 399).
- **Sufis as a shelter against the state.** "Sufis in this period also sheltered both the politically and socially persecuted at the risk of their own popularity or reprisals from the government, at the same time helping Muslims to stabilize their emotions" (p. 424, folio 400).
- **A building foundation dispute settled by paying off a yogi's prior claim.** "It is mentioned that when the walls of the town were being built up to about six feet high they collapsed. The Sultan consulted the Shaikh who summoned a yogi named Bank Nath. According to him the site had previously been his property and therefore his name should be mentioned somewhere in the town. **This was promptly done and the walls were completed**" (p. 431, folio 407). **A foundation date is given twice over: "the founding of Ahmadabad, near Asawal, on the banks of the river Sabarmati", "the foundation, in 1411 or 1413, was laid by four different people, all called Ahmad"** (pp. 430–431, folios 406–407).
- **Ascetic practice described as a discipline with rules.** "He used to say that for twelve years he had travelled alone, bare-footed and without a water pot. At nights he would stay in mosques and never suffered nocturnal pollution. While travelling he always fasted" (p. 431, folio 407); and forty days' seclusion "eating only four dates during the whole period" (p. 429, folio 405); and of the second Bibi Fatima, "two cakes of barley each day… along with a glass of water", "For forty days and nights she remained without sleep or food, and the bread was given to dervishes" (p. 427, folio 403).
- **Why merchants carried preachers and mystics — three stated reasons, and they are an account of what a travelling sufi was for.** "Firstly, as experts in religious law and the practical side of theology they acted as Imams in congregational prayers, as peace-makers and as judges in solving disputes involving the Shari'a. Secondly, they offered spiritual comfort to boat passengers undertaking voyages which were often hair-raising and dangerous. Thirdly, they were useful as intermediaries between merchants and local authorities whenever the former were involved in political or economic crises" (p. 433, folio not stated).
- **Trade as the vehicle of Islam on the southern coast.** "Before the advent of Islam to India, the Arabs and Iranians who sailed from Yemen and the Persian Gulf controlled the trade between India and China in the East and Egypt, Syria and Rome in the West" (p. 433, folio not stated); "Consequently many fishermen and their families and the servants of Arab and Iranian merchants lived in Calicut, and at other trading ports and islands off the south coast, embraced the new religion" (p. 433, folio not stated); "Ships from China, Sumatra, Ceylon, the Maldives, Yemen and Fars would stop at Calicut and merchants from all parts of the world thronged to the port" (p. 434, folio 410).
- **Hindus at a khanqah threshold, and the shaikh who cursed them.** "Both Hindus and Muslims, who included government servants, would visit the Shaikh's khangah [khanqah]. **The Kayasthas, who by this time had adopted Muslim dress, were amongst those who would humbly kiss the threshold of the Shaikh's khangah.** However Shaikh Ahmad was unimpressed, and would emerge only to curse the Hindus and bless the Muslims who had gathered to show their respects" (p. 432, folio 408), footnoted with the Persian **"Musalmanan ra nikbakht farmudand wa kafiran ra halak-Allah Gufta, f. 65a"** (p. 432, folio 408, footnote 5).
- **Generosity taught as indifference to the recipient's use of the gift.** "Once his pir asked him to give four copper coins to a dervish who was a drug addict. Shaikh Ahmad pointed out that the dervish would spend the money on hemp leaves, but Baba Ishaq replied that every one would have to answer for his own actions so whatever the dervish did was of no concern to them" (p. 431, folio 407).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

The attributions are the book's own, including its hedges ("is believed", "allegedly", "it is recorded", "probably mythical", "the miracle is legendary").

- **A thread from a mother's garment brings rain to Delhi.** "On one occasion there was a drought in Delhi. Everyone started praying for rain and the Shaikh was asked to do likewise. Taking a thread from a garment worn by his mother he held it in his hand and started praying. **Instantly it began to pour**" (p. 425, folio not stated), sourced "AA, p. 294".
- **Baba Farid's mother blinds a thief and restores his sight, and his family converts** (p. 425, folio not stated). **Rizvi's own gloss makes it evidentially significant: "Indeed this is one of the rare conversions through early Chishti influence, although the circumstances which prompted it, according to the story, were miraculous."**
- **The bag of bones that empties itself.** "Presuming them to be those of his mother who had fallen prey to a lion or some other beast, the Shaikh collected them in a bag and took them to Ajodhan. **When the bag was opened, to the astonishment of the two brothers, it was empty**" (p. 426, folio 402). **The book calls the whole episode "strange": "The background to the death of Baba Farid's mother is also strange"** (p. 425, folio not stated).
- **A woman who fasts to death on the fortieth day.** "One night Bibi Fatima believed she was dying, so she decided to eat nothing and not lose a moment from prayer… On the fortieth day she died" (p. 427, folio 403), the moral being that "a real sufi was one who was an ibnu'l-waqt", glossed "Literally an ibnu'l-waqt means a time server or a sycophant, but the sufi meaning is quite different" (p. 427, folio 403, footnote 2).
- **A dust storm that abducts a child.** "While a child, Ahmad was playing outside his house when suddenly a dust storm blew him to a place far from his home. Loosing [OCR?] [Losing] his way after some time he fell into the hands of Baba Ishaq Maghribi in Khattu who gave him shelter and brought him up" (p. 428, folio not stated); repeated with a variant in the *Ma'ariju'l-Wilayat*, "Meanwhile a strong dust storm carried Shaikh Ahmad from his house and he fell into the hands of a caravan of cotton merchants and was later offered to Baba Ishaq" (p. 429, folio 405).
- **A childless Brahman given five sons, and the promised eldest hidden away.** The bargain, the deception with "the son of a slave, under the guise of his eldest child", and the shaikh's knowledge of the hiding place (pp. 428–429, folio not stated / 405). **The boy so acquired "died at the age of twenty-five, and although the Shaikh was heart-broken it is recorded that he was offered in a divine message an even worthier child"** (p. 429, folio 405).
- **A leafless mulberry tree turns green.** "Although the tree was without leaves, the Shaikh's presence made it turn green, and this miracle was formed the basis [OCR?] [this miracle formed the basis] for his immediate fame" (p. 429, folio 405).
- **Bread from heaven for forty prisoners, and Timur's conversion into a devotee.** "Seized by the Mughal army he was imprisoned with forty people in a house. **Each day the spiritual powers of the Shaikh provided each prisoner with a ration of hot cake allegedly sent from heaven.** When Timur heard of the Shaikh, he released him along with the other prisoners in the house. Timur later became his devotee" (p. 430, folio 406). **Rizvi flags it in his own footnote: "The source of the bread remains unknown and the miracle is legendary"** (p. 430, folio 406, footnote 2).
- **A prophecy of Timur's invasion, fifteen days out.** "Fifteen days before the invasion, he warned his disciples of the calamity which was likely to befall Delhi" (p. 430, folio 406).
- **A yogi's prior claim on the ground of Ahmadabad, and collapsing walls** (p. 431, folio 407).
- **Knowledge at a distance, between Delhi and Bengal.** Shaikh Nur Qutb-i 'Alam knowing of Shaikh Ahmad without having met him: "Shaikh Ahmad replied that Shaikh Nur had never met him and therefore his knowledge must have been miraculous" (p. 431, folio 407).
- **A dream in Medina in which the Prophet offers the world as a girl.** "There an attractive girl was brought to him and the Prophet offered her to the Shaikh. He hesitated to accept such a gift, but his pir indicated he should do so. **The girl was the world personified**" (p. 432, folio 408). **The book presents it as the shaikh's own explanation of his wealth: "The Shaikh ascribed his affluence in Gujarat-to [OCR?] [Gujarat to] the blessings of the Prophet Muhammad."**
- **A motionless yogi between two idols, who reappears in China two centuries on.** "Near Sandapur (Goa) Ibn Battuta stopped at a small island where he met a yogi who was standing in a temple motionless between a pair of idols. He impressed Ibn Battuta with his miraculous powers and **his gestures convinced the Moorish traveller that the yogi was in fact really a Muslim.** Strangely enough at Canton, in China, Ibn Battuta met an old cave dweller who was reported to have been two hundred years old. He neither ate nor drank but told Ibn Battuta he was the same yogi he had met in the island temple" (pp. 434–435, folios 410–411). **Rizvi's own framing: "Local Muslims related strange anecdotes about the yogi. Such legendary personalities seem to have greatly influenced the local islanders who, prior to the impact of Islam, were imbued with animistic beliefs and were greatly attracted to miraculous and supernatural feats"** (p. 435, folio 411). **This is the closing sentence of Appendix C and of the book's main text.**
- **The Prophet Khizr as patron of travellers** — "who also helped travellers on both the sea and on rivers" (p. 434, folio 410).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Rizvi's closing blow against McLeod, and it turns on there being no such thing as classical sufism.** "These remarks introduce a number of ideas which are founded on a total ignorance of both sunni orthodoxy and sufism in India. **Readers of the previous pages need not be reminded that there was no classical pattern of Arab and Persian sufism**" (p. 420, folio 396); "A large number of sufi centres had emerged all over the Islamic world which had established their own particular features and **the developments in India were significant in their own right**" (p. 420, folio 396).
- **The charge that McLeod conflates orthodoxy with sufism.** "McLeod's remark that amongst the Muslims who came into contact with Guru Nanak: 'There would certainly be some strict sunnis' and some who might fitly be described as Sufis' **tends to indicate that in his mind Sunni orthodoxy and sufism were identical**" (p. 420, folio 396); cited to "Guru Nanak and the Sikh religion, pp. 158-59" (p. 420, folio 396, footnote 1).
- **The Qadiris of the Panjab adduced against McLeod's "debased sufism" — the chunk's one silsila claim about Pakistan.** "What McLeod is sure of is the existence of a debased sufism in the Muslim community of the Panjab. He seems to be unaware of Shaikh 'Abdu'l-Quddus Gangohi who lived in Shahabad, his disciple, Shaikh Jalal of Thaneswar, **and the Qadiris who had made the Panjab the main centre of their activities**" (p. 420, folio 396). **No Qadiri shrine, saint or town in the Panjab is named** — (not stated).
- **Convergence, not borrowing, and the book's last word on the whole Hindu-Muslim question.** "The spiritual life of India discussed in this chapter bears out the Rig Vedic assertion: **'The One Reality, the learned speak of in many ways.'**" (p. 420, folio 396).
- **Majumdar's thesis, quoted in full so it can be refuted — the most substantial counter-position in the chunk.** "'… the role of both medieval mysticism and sufism in the history of Indian culture is often exaggerated beyond all proportions… their historical importance is considerably limited by the fact that the number of Indians directly affected by them, even at their heyday which was shortlived, could not be very large… **the two orthodox religions showed no visible signs of being seriously affected by this sudden intrusion of radical elements. They pursued their even tenor, resembling the two banks of a river, separated by the stream that flows between them. Attempts were made to build a bridge connecting the two, but ended in failure. Even if there were any temporary bridge, it collapsed in no time**'" (pp. 421–422, folio not stated / 398).
- **Rizvi's two-sentence reply, which does not defend the bridge.** "Firstly, the influence of sufism was not short-lived; secondly, **members of the orthodox sections of both Hinduism and Islam moved in different spheres, while both sufis and Hindu saints (bhaktas) remained unconcerned with the activities pursued by the orthodox**" (p. 422, folio 398). **He concedes the ground he takes: "This is not the place to discuss the circumstances which may have made, as Majumdar suggests, the so-called temporary bridge collapse."**
- **Hindi sufi poets and bhaktas were not syncretists — the Conclusion's most careful formulation.** "**They were unconcerned with the idea of achieving any form of union between the two religions and instead tended to work within their respective religious communities for an understanding of the spiritual and social values of each other**" (p. 423, folio 399).
- **A blunt list of sufism's costs, in Rizvi's own voice.** "**The Ghazalian tradition in sufism in India did inculcate hostility towards philosophy. The sufi movement tended to promote gullibility and credulity and discourage self-reliance**" (p. 423, folio 399); and "**The most serious threats to the survival of sufism were the presumptuous and preposterous claims of sufi charlatans and impostors.** The latter exploited the influence of sufism, and the popular passion for the occult and thaumaturgy, to their own advantage. Their poetry and music promoted immoral practices, the use of drugs and the practice of homosexuality" (p. 424, folio 400). **Also on poetry: "this form of poetry gradually tended to degenerate as it became conventionalized and developed grotesque language, erotic obsessions and imitative and repetitive thinking"** (p. 423, folio 399).
- **Sufi poetry as an outlet for what could not otherwise be said.** "an avenue for emotions and feelings which would otherwise have never been expressed due to the fury of the orthodox, social inhibitions and political repression" (p. 423, folio 399).
- **Disciples deified their pirs against the pirs' own wishes.** "Sufi disciples tended to deify their pirs, even though the latter were totally disinterested in turning themselves into Godheads" (p. 421, folio not stated).
- **The Appendix A verdict on women in sufism — Rizvi's own summary judgement.** "**Therefore, it would not be unfair to say that, Muslim women who became deeply committed to mysticism and a life of asceticism did so in spite of a lack of encouragement and assistance from their male counterparts and from Islam in general**" (p. 427, folio 403); and the evidentiary caveat before it, "We have mentioned here only the most outstanding female sufis amongst those whose lives have been documented. **The biographies of a large number of saintly women remain unknown**" (p. 427, folio 403).
- **A travel book as the cause of a migration.** "It would seem that the fame of the Rih/a [OCR?] [Rihla] of Ibn Battuta prompted a sufi from Maghrib… to migrate to India" (p. 428, folio not stated). **The founders' origins are stated as a fact the appendix exists to qualify: "The founders of the sufi orders discussed earlier were natives of central Asia, Iran or Iraq"** (p. 428, folio not stated).
- **Rizvi sorting myth from probability inside a hagiography — the method worth citing.** "**The first part of this story is probably mythical but it is quite likely that Shaikh Ahmad was presented to Baba Ishaq by some merchants when he was about four years old**" (p. 429, folio not stated — the sentence stands at the head of p. 429 before the running header's folio 405 applies to the page; cited as p. 429, folio 405). He does the same with a number: "The number may not be correct as it is used for long ascetic exercises" — of the twelve years of asceticism (p. 430, folio 406, footnote 1).
- **A source-conflict left open, on how Gujarat became independent.** "Zafar Khan refused to undertake such a hazardous venture and **is said to have abdicated in favour of his son. Another version of the story is that he was in fact imprisoned by his son**" (p. 430, folio 406), sourced to "Tabagat-i Akbari [OCR?] [Tabaqat-i Akbari], pp. 90-1; Mir'at-i Sikandari, pp. 16-18; Gulshan-i Ibrahimi, p. 181; AA, p. 161" (p. 430, folio 406, footnote 3).
- **A great sufi recorded as an enemy of Hindus, without mitigation.** "**Shaikh Ahmad, however, showed a high degree of antagonism towards Hindus**" (p. 432, folio 408). **The last substantive statement of Appendix B, and it cuts directly against the syncretism thesis of Chapter Six.**
- **No written record of the maritime conversion process.** "Muslim merchants and the preachers and sufis who accompanied them, introduced Islam to islands and countries quite remote from Iraq. **No authentic record of this process exists**" (p. 433, folio not stated). **Rizvi also notes the argument that makes the missionary unnecessary: "in the daily practice of Islam, an official preacher or priest is not needed, every Muslim being able to perform his own prayers either singly or in congregation"** (p. 433, folio not stated).
- **An admission of gaps in Ibn Battuta's coverage.** "There may well have been other minor Kaziruni khanqahs in the islands which Ibn Battuta fails to mention" (p. 434, folio 410).

## Doubts

**The source carries no transcriber apparatus — no `[OCR?]`, no `[illegible]`, no `[folio N]` lines — so every inline flag above is mine, on the tesseract route, and the classes below are the damage inventory for pp. 420–441. No fact above rests on an unflagged damaged word.**

**Placement and mapping**

1. **The eighteen Guru Nanak ids rest on one page of methodological argument and on nothing else.** p. 420 (folio 396) names Guru Nanak repeatedly but names no site, no gurdwara, no town in Pakistan and no date. Under the ruled principal-figure convention of 18 September 2026 the ids stand; **a consolidator must not read any of them as site evidence, and should weigh p. 420 as commentary on Guru Nanak's formation, not as biography.**
2. **`shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht` is mapped from a title alone.** The book writes only "the Makhdum Jahaniyan" / "Makhdum Jahaniyan" and "the famous Suhrawardi" (p. 429, folio 405), with no personal name, no dates and no Uch. **The title "Makhdum Jahaniyan" is also borne in other sources by more than one Bukhari sayyid of Uch; the archive has five Uch rows (`shrine-of-jalaluddin-surkh-posh-bukhari-jalaluddin-bukhari`, `shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht`, `tomb-of-baha-al-halim-uch-sharif`, `tomb-of-javindi-bibi`, `tomb-of-ustad-nuriya`), and the book gives nothing that distinguishes among them except the title itself.** I have mapped to the row whose `principal_figure` column carries that exact title, and flag it here rather than treating it as settled.
3. **Interim convention (a), toponyms withheld.** **Uch** (p. 421, folio not stated) would take the five Uch rows named in Doubt 2 if the ruling reverses — and it is the chunk's only substantive economic statement about a Pakistani place, so this is the withholding that costs most. **Multan** (p. 420, folio 396) would take `shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya`, `shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam`, `shrine-of-shah-shams-ud-din-sabzwari`, `shrine-of-shah-yusaf-gardez`, `shrine-of-syed-musa-pak`, `shrine-of-hafiz-muhammad-jamal-multani`, `shrine-of-hazrat-shah-ali-akbar-shah-ali-akbar-shamsi` and `prahladpuri-temple`. **Ajodhan** at p. 420 (folio 396) would take `shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar` — but that row is mapped anyway from pp. 425–426, where Baba Farid is named at Ajodhan, so nothing is lost there.
4. **"the Qadiris who had made the Panjab the main centre of their activities" (p. 420, folio 396) is deliberately left unmapped.** It names a silsila and a province but no figure and no site, so neither the ruled person convention nor convention (a) reaches it. The archive has fourteen rows whose `silsila` column reads Qadiri or a Qadiri compound; **I have not distributed the sentence among them, and a consolidator should treat it as cross-cutting background, not as row evidence.**
5. **No woman named in Appendix A is mapped to `bibi-pak-daman`, `tomb-of-javindi-bibi`, `gurdwara-babay-nanki` or `gurdwara-shaheed-ganj-singh-singhnian`, and the non-mapping is deliberate.** Bibi Sara (p. 425, folio not stated), Bibi Fatima Sam "of Delhi" (p. 426, folio 402), the second Bibi Fatima (p. 427, folio 403) and Bibi Auliya' (p. 427, folio 403) are all placed at Delhi or given no place at all; Baba Farid's mother and Shaikh Nizamu'd-Din's mother are never named (pp. 425–426, folio not stated / 402); Rabi'a's own place is (not stated) in this chunk (p. 425, folio not stated). **Sharing the honorific "Bibi" is not evidence, and the book supplies nothing else.**
6. **"Qutbu'd-Din Ahmad (1451-59)" (p. 431, folio 407) is a sultan of Gujarat and must not be read as Sultan Qutb ud-Din Aibak (`tomb-of-qutbuddin-aibak`).** Likewise **"Khwaja Qutbu'd-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki" (p. 425, folio not stated) is the Delhi Chishti and takes no archive id.** Flagged because the strings collide.
7. **`shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar` is mapped four times over but the evidence is oblique** — three of the four bullets concern his mother, his brother or a woman he praised, and the fourth is two bibliography lines. **The one direct biographical fact is "When Baba Farid settled at Ajodhan" (p. 425, folio not stated).**

**Internal contradictions and things the book leaves open**

8. **The book gives two versions of how Gujarat became independent and does not choose** — abdication or imprisonment (p. 430, folio 406) — and then narrates as fact that "Zafar Khan established the independent Sultanate of Gujarat, adopting Muzaffar Shah as his title" (p. 430, folio 406), which sits awkwardly with the abdication it has just reported.
9. **Ahmadabad's foundation is dated "in 1411 or 1413" and credited to four men at once** (p. 431, folio 407), while the *Tuhfatu'l-Majalis* "gives most of the credit… to Shaikh Ahmad Khattu" (pp. 430–431, folios 406–407). The book reports the conflict without resolving it.
10. **"both his father and son who knew the Shaikh went to see him to persuade him to remain in Gujarat" (p. 430, folio 406) has no clear antecedent** — whose father and son is not stated, though the surrounding sentences concern Zafar Khan and Tatar Khan. Possibly a lost word; I have not supplied one.
11. **Two Bibi Fatimas, four name-forms, and the book never says whether they are one woman.** "Bibi Fatima Sam of Delhi" (p. 426, folio 402); "the tomb of Bibi Sam" (p. 426, folio 402); "she became known to the local people only as Bibi Sa'ima or Bibi Sham" (p. 427, folio 403); and "a female sufi, Bibi Fatima, who constantly fasted" reported by a different authority (p. 427, folio 403). **A consolidator must not merge them.**
12. **Baba Farid's mother's residence is given as "either Hansi or Delhi"** (p. 425, folio not stated) and the endurance of Bibi Fatima's tomb as "until either the fourteenth or fifteenth century" (p. 427, folio 403). **Both hedges are the book's own and are reproduced, not resolved.**
13. **"'Ali Sher Qani, Mir, Tuhfatu'l-Kiram, Hyderabad, Pakistan, 1971" is followed by "Bahr-i Zakhkhar, Allahabad, 1313/1895" with no run-on dash printed** (p. 439, folio 415). Elsewhere in the Bibliography a second work by the same author carries a long dash; **here it is absent, so I cannot tell whether Bahr-i Zakhkhar is attributed to Qani' or is a separate headless entry.**
14. **"Amir Khwurd, Saiyid Muhammad bin Mubarak, Siyaru'l-Auliya', Delhi, 1302/1885" is printed with the run-on dash that marks it as a further work of Amir Khusraw Dihlawi** (p. 439, folio 415). Amir Khwurd is a separate author; **this looks like the book's own typographic error, not an OCR fault, since the dash is clean.**
15. **The brief's format example `(p. 437, folio 413)` cannot be met** — p. 437 prints the heading `Bibliography` in place of a running header and carries no folio. The same holds for pp. 421, 425, 428 and 433. **Sixteen of the chunk's twenty-two pages print a folio; five print none; p. 436 prints nothing at all.**
