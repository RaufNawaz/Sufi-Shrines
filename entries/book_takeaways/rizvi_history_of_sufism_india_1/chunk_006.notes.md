## Practices, institutions, economy

**The negative first, because the run expected more than is here: in pp. 100–117 (folios 84–99) there is no baiʿa, no khilafat-nama, no deputisation document, no statement that any Shaikh was buried inside or near his khanqah, no tomb, no shrine building, no ʿurs, no mela, no langar, no sajjada nashin or custodian, no Auqaf, no land, no revenue, no visitor numbers, no dispute and no architectural date for any structure except the khanqah built for Shihabuʾd-Din.** The khirqa, initiation, zikr and khanqah-economy material is all here; the burial-and-endowment passage the queue was looking for is not, and must lie past p. 117 — see Doubts.

- **A ruler builds and equips a khanqah for a Shaikh and his family — the chunk's one act of princely endowment, and its one building description.** "Al-Nasiralso builtanextensive khangah [OCR?] [Al-Nasir also built an extensive khanqah] for Shaikh Shihabu'd-Din and his family, which included additions such asa bath-house anda garden [OCR?] [such as a bath-house and a garden]" (p. 104, folio 88). **A caliph is the builder; the beneficiary is the Shaikh "and his family"; the named components are a bath-house and a garden. No date, no cost, no site and no endowment deed** — (not stated).
- **Khanqah income, stated as a closed list by a named authority.** "According to Shaikh Shihabu'd-Din there were two sources of income for a khanqah—endowments and begging" (p. 109, folio 91). **This is the single most citable institutional-economy sentence in the chunk.**
- **Khanqahs as an admitted innovation with a claimed prophetic germ.** "Suhrawardi admitted that the establishment of khanqahs was an innovation, but saw in them the germs of the suffa life at the time of Muhammad" (p. 109, folio 91); and separately, "On etymological grounds he rejects the idea that the word was derived from suffa but accepts the fact that the life of Muhammad's companions at the Medina mosque, 'the People of the Verandah,' resembled those of later sufis" (p. 104, folio 88).
- **The three stated advantages of an established khanqah — a functional justification of the institution.** "Firstly, they offered board and lodging to sufis who were often without financial resources of any kind. Secondly, the corporate life of a khangah [OCR?] [khanqah] provided an opportunity for individual members who shared a close relationship to exchange views and experiences. Finally, the propinquity of life in a khangah [khanqah] provided for a healthy rivalry between sufis in the perfection of their morals and conduct" (p. 109, folio 91).
- **The hospitality code for travellers, with a time of day and an officer.** Travellers, "generally wandering dervishes, should reach the khanqah before afternoon prayers, and if late, should spend the night in a mosque. A warm welcome to the visitor should be extended by the residents and the best food offered to them by the khangah steward [OCR?] [khanqah steward]. The travellers should not be pestered with questions, although resident sufis should themselves answer all queries. If a dervish entered who was ignorant of khangah customs and the traditions of sufi life, he should not be expelled" (p. 109, folio 91). **A named office — the khanqah steward — and a rule against expulsion.**
- **The internal ranking of khanqah residents, in three grades tied to duties and to sleeping arrangements.** "The first group contained members of the novice or servant class who were assigned to such duties as waiting on others, which enabled them to become acquainted with the company of mystics and to be initiated in humility. Service enabled novices to rise to the second group of mystics who learnt the social ethics of sufism. Members of the third category were aged sufis, generally living in seclusion, who were entirely dedicated to prayers and meditation. In a khangah [khanqah], the young should live communally in the jama'at-khana, allowing older sufis to reside privately in cells" (p. 109, folio 91). **Two named spaces inside the building: the jamaʿat-khana and private cells.**
- **The khanqah's officers and its commensality.** "The head of the khangah [khanqah] was the Shaikh, the others were the ikhwan or brethren. Inmates should co-operate and take food communally. Differences between sufis should be overcome and there should be no hypocrisy in relationships" (p. 110, folio 92).
- **Retreat (periodic retirement) as an innovation, graded by how often the recluse eats.** "Shaikh Suhrawardi believed the periodic retirement of sufis to retreats to be a later innovation, but considered the custom of great assistance in self-examination and meditation. He divided recluses into three: the weakest, who broke their fasts every evening; the above average sufi who took nourishment on alternate nights and the strongest, who ate only on the third" (p. 110, folio 92).
- **The khirqa: an admitted accretion, and the mechanism of transmitted sainthood.** "Shaikh Shihabu'd-Din admitted that the wearing of the khirga [OCR?] [khirqa] or sufi robe was also an accretion, but saw it as a symbol of the radical aspect of sufi life. According to him, it identified the wearer as one who was outside the mainstream of life, who did not indulge in fine raiment and rich food. As sufism was founded on a belief in the transmission of wilaya or saintship, the granting of a khirga [khirqa] was significant for it was a sign that the recipient had, according to his pir, reached a satisfactory religious standard" (p. 110, folio 92). **This is the chunk's nearest approach to deputisation, and it is a robe and a standard, not a document** — no khilafat-nama is mentioned.
- **The two classes of khirqa grant, one of them open to non-disciples.** "one awarded in recognition of a disciple's personal spiritual achievement or one given by a Shaikh to a person seeking the blessings which surrounded it. There was no necessity for those in the second group to be sufi disciples, but they should at least observe the Shari'a and keep company with sufis. After the receipt of a khirga [khirqa] the blessings accrued from it might lead to a total acceptance of the mystic path" (p. 110, folio 92). **A blessing-seeking laity is written into the institution — the closest thing in this chunk to a devotee relationship with a living Shaikh.**
- **The khirqa also functions in the martyrdom narrative as a garment with a working shape** — Kubra "put on his khirga [khirqa]. ..girded up his loins, filled the upper part of his khirga [khirqa], which was open in front, with stones on both sides" (p. 112, folio 94). **The only physical description of the robe in the chunk: open in front and able to hold stones.**
- **Samaʿ: supported, but placed under strict rules, with the named risk.** "On the controversial subject of sama', the Shaikh was lengthy. Conflicting views of sama' (literally, 'audition'), from different sufis were detailed by him in the 'Awarifu'l-Ma'arif. Overall, the Shaikh supported the practice, but prescribed its performance under strict rules, to prevent a degeneration into the use of music and dancing to promote licentiousness" (p. 110, folio 92). Ghujduwani's parallel ruling: "Mystics should not necessarily avoid sama' but any over-indulgence should be abandoned" (p. 113, folio 95). Jami's report of Bahaʾuʾd-Din: the Naqshbandis "should not practise spoken zikr, seclusion and sama'" (p. 115, folio 97). **Three positions on samaʿ in one chunk, from permissive-with-rules to prohibitive.**
- **Hujwiri's defence of samaʿ, quoted against the charge of dancing** — "all foot-play (pay-bazi) is bad in law and reason, by whomsoever it is practised", but genuine ecstatic "agitation (iztirab) is neither dancing nor foot-play nor bodily indulgence, but a dissolution of the scul [OCR?] [soul]. Those who call it 'dancing' are utterly wrong" (pp. 110–111, folios 92–93).
- **Samaʿ attested visually** — the plate is captioned "Sufi Sama" and drawn from a manuscript "dated 959/1552" (p. 108, folio not stated).
- **The zikr technique of the Silsila-i Khwajgan, given as a physical procedure — the chunk's densest practice passage.** Under *Yad-kard*: "The sufi controls his breath from below the navel, shuts his lips tightly and fastens his tongue to his palate to prevent suffocation. He then diverts the spiritual heart into a union with the physical heart which is pineal in shape, and zikr is begun. It takes the following form. The uttering of /a [OCR?] [la] (no) involves a process whereby the word is lifted from the navel to the brain; //aha [OCR?] [illaha] (God) is expressed, at the same time as the right shoulder is jerked sharply and i/'Allah [OCR?] [illa Allah] (but Allah) is uttered as if the heart of flesh has been soundly struck. This process produces a spiritual heart which ontologically circulates throughout the body" (p. 114, folio 96). **Breath control, tongue position, shoulder movement and a physiological claim about the heart, all printed as prescription.**
- **The eight principles of Ghujduwani, as the book names and glosses them** (pp. 113–114, folios 95–96): "1. Hosh dar dam (awareness while breathing)"; "2. Nazar bar gadam [OCR?] [Nazar bar qadam] (watching the steps)"; "3. Safar dar watan (journey to one's homeland)" — "a journey from human to angelic attributes"; "4. Khalwat dar anjuman (solitude in an assembly)" — "A sufi may wander into a bazaar but so engrossed in zikr should he be that not a single voice is heard by him"; "5. Yad-kard (remembrance) is related to both oral and mental zikr"; "6. Baz-gasht (restraint)" — after "al-kalimat al-tayyiba" (footnoted "The Islamic confession of faith") the reciter "should add: 'Oh God! Thou art my Goal and I seek Thy satisfaction'"; "7. Nigah-dasht (watchfulness)"; "8. Yad-dasht (recollection)". **The bazaar clause is the one social setting named for zikr in the chunk.**
- **The three Naqshbandi additions, one of them a counted quota.** "1. Wuquf-i Zamani (temporal pause) is the constant self-examination by a sufi on the way his time is spent"; "2. Wuquf-i 'Adadi (numerated pause) is the prevention of thought-anarchy through a process of counting the number of times zikr in the heart is repeated. The mystic completes one round of zikr between three and twenty-one recitations in the one breath; however, if there is no spiritual change after a second round of twenty-one zikrs this implies there has been no real impact"; "3. Wuquf-i Qalbi (heart pause)" — "control of the breath and the counting of zikr were not indispensable but he insisted that the Wuquf-i Qalbi was imperative. According to Khwaja Baha'u'd-Din, this was the essence of sufi discipline" (p. 115, folio 97). **Numbers that can enter an entry: three to twenty-one recitations per breath, two rounds of twenty-one as the test.**
- **Discipleship rules of the Masalikuʾl-ʿArifin, including celibacy.** Disciples "should acquire a precise learning of the Qur'an, Hadis and Figh [OCR?] [Fiqh]. It urged dervishes to dissociate themselves from both ignorant sufis and those who promiscuously indulged in mixed company. Peace in the heart and a control of the eyesight would help in the pursuit of celibacy. Married life involved the sufi in every-day problems and exposed him to the threat of loss of faith" (p. 113, folio 95). **Set against ʿAbduʾl-Qadir Jilani marrying at fifty-one (p. 100, folio 84), Kubra marrying his preceptor's daughter (p. 111, folio 93) and a khanqah built for a Shaikh "and his family" (p. 104, folio 88) — the chunk holds celibacy and married Shaikhs side by side without comment.**
- **Initiation, described as having no standard.** "Disciple initiation was a complex phenomenon. Some pirs carefully examined the talents and potential of those who approached them for initiation; others admitted disciples indiscriminately to their silsilas. Each order followed its own special methods of training and formulas for zikr and contemplation. No uniform pattern was evolved. Progress depended on the capacity of the novice; some completed their initiation into sufi life after a short period, others underwent a lengthy period of training" (p. 117, folio 99). **No baiʿa, no oath, no hand-clasp and no ceremony is described** — (not stated).
- **The three-year probation and its yearly curriculum**, on Hujwiri's authority (p. 117, folio 99) — see the `data-darbar` bullet above.
- **The Path as a supervised itinerary, with its named stations.** "The Tariga [OCR?] [Tariqa] was an arduous journey involving numerous risks and impediments. It could be traversed only under the strict supervision of Shaikhs or pirs who had themselves traversed all the hills and dales of the Path"; the stations "included repentance (tawba), conversion (inabat), renunciation (zuhd) and trust in God (tawakkul)", reached "through self mortification, performing zikr, and contemplation, and these all needed constant supervision by a Shaikh"; and the conditions "such as gabz [OCR?] [qabz] (contraction), bast (dilation) and illumination, descended into the heart from God" (p. 117, folio 99). **The sentence is cut off at the chunk boundary.**
- **The khalifa as the mechanism of continuity, and the charge against it.** "The silsila system guaranteed the transmission of mystical knowledge acquired by founders of the order to further generations of sufis through their successors or kKhalifas [OCR?] [khalifas]. The 'ulama' accused the sufis of deifying their spiritual teachers (Shaikhs, pirs or murshids) but the sufis saw their Shaikhs as being illuminated by the light of Muhammad which had existed even before his birth and was the sole cause of creation" (p. 116, folio 98). **Three interchangeable titles for the office — Shaikh, pir, murshid — and an explicit ʿulamaʾ accusation of deification.**
- **Territoriality: the wilaya as a bounded jurisdiction modelled on secular government.** "No attempt was made to develop a central silsila for the entire Islamic world and all sufi Shaikhs believed their own spiritual influence should be confined to a limited territory. This conviction also sprang from the current practice whereby governors of different territories respected the independence of other administrations"; "In his own spiritual territory or wilaya the pir looked after the material, as well as the spiritual needs, of his disciples and also attempted to assist others who asked for his help" (p. 116, folio 98). **The pir as a provider of material needs, and "The various silsilas did not develop in an atmosphere of rivalry and hostility towards each other."**
- **Merchants as the financial base, and protection given in return.** "As has already been seen, merchants were the leading financial supporters of the sufi movement and in return the khangahs [khanqahs] offered them both hospitality and protection. Gifts from rich men, government officials, princes and rulers were also welcomed, but sufi literature indicates that eminent sufis themselves did not crave offerings. The unsolicited gift (futuh) from devotees was their only source of income and a popular belief in the efficacy of sufi prayers for the fulfilment of both worldly and non-worldly ambitions provided for the continual flow of gifts" (pp. 116–117, folios 98–99). **A named category of income — futuh — and a stated mechanism for its flow: belief in the efficacy of the saint's prayers.**
- **Institutional begging, including at wealthy khanqahs, with two stated purposes.** "Begging was also resorted to in the initial stages in the development of a new khangah [khanqah], but affluent khangahs [khanqahs] also insisted that neophyte sufis go to town begging. Firstly this custom was intended to produce humility, and secondly to strengthen the feeling among sufis that they were the lowliest of all God's creatures" (p. 117, folio 99).
- **Sufis in ordinary trades.** "Many eminent sufis worked as petty traders, artisans or cultivators, to enable them to remain part of the community, rather than becoming inhabitants of ivory towers" (p. 117, folio 99).
- **The stance towards the state and towards war.** "Instances of sufis waging holy wars are not unknown, but the historical records indicate that jihad on these occasions was intended to repel enemy invasions, such as those from the heathen Turks or Mongols. Sufis kept themselves aloof from both the administrative machinery and imperialist wars of the government. However, the mystic tawakkul (trust in God) did not imply a turning away from society" (p. 117, folio 99). **This is contradicted within the chunk — see Doubts.**
- **Why people went to the orders at all, given as a political vacuum.** "The political upheavals and crises of the eleventh and twelfth centuries, precipitated by the Ghuzz and Mongol invasions in Khurasan and Iran, had undermined the authority of the Turkic Sultans and this in turn had led to an increase in the importance of the sufi silsilas. As there was no official avenue through which grievances of the common people could be registered, the only alternative was to seek assistance from members of sufi orders" (p. 116, folio 98).
- **Poverty and wealth as a matter of indifference, against obsessive renunciation.** "the mystic Tariga [Tariqa] was not identical to either fagr [OCR?] [faqr] (poverty) or zuhd (asceticism), although these could lead to fana'. To him an obsessive opposition to wealth was a sign of weakness, amounting to a dependence on causation and an attachment to the anticipation of reward. A true sufi did not differentiate between poverty and wealth and was concerned with neither fear nor the need for recompense" (p. 105, folio 89). **Directly relevant to how endowed shrines justify wealth.**
- **The madrasa and the khanqah held together in one life.** "He himself managed to combine the life of the madrasa with that of the khangah [khanqah]" — of ʿAbduʾl-Qadir Jilani, who "succeeded al-Mukharrimi as head of a seminary in Baghdad" (p. 100, folio 84). Ahmad al-Ghazali likewise "succeeded his brother as a professor at the Nizamiyya seminary in Baghdad" (p. 102, folio 86).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

The attributions below are the book's own, including its hedges ("is alleged", "It is claimed", "is said to have", "his disciples however, were convinced").

- **ʿAbduʾl-Qadir Jilani's miracles, reported as allegation.** "The legendary life of Shaikh 'Abdu'l-Qadir is filled with incomprehensible miracles and supernatural feats. He is alleged to have crushed mountains, dried up oceans and raised the dead to life. It is claimed that a large number of Jews and Christians embraced Islam through the influence of his spiritual prowess" (p. 101, folio 85), and "Whatever his alleged supramundane powers, Shaikh 'Abdu'l-Qadir died an earthly death in 561/1166" (pp. 101–102, folios 85–86).
- **His claimed primacy among saints, in his own reported words.** "His eminence was enshrined in the belief that he was superior to everyone of God's saints" (p. 101, folio 85), footnoted: "He is reported to have said: 'My foot is on the neck of every saint of God.'" (p. 101, folio 85, footnote 3).
- **A living miracle tradition with drums, reported in the present tense.** "Magical and esoteric teachings were also associated with Shaikh 'Abdu'l-Qadir Jilani and such practices form an integral part of the beliefs of the Javanese Qadiris who, even today, perform supernatural feats accompanied by beating drums" (p. 101, folio 85).
- **Kubra's martyrdom, in Jami's narration** (p. 112, folio 94): the prophecy "Arise quickly and depart to your own countries, for a Fire is kindled from the East which consumes nearly to the West"; his refusal to pray it away — "this is a thing irrevocably predetermined which prayer cannot avert"; his refusal to flee — "there shall I die a martyr, for it is not permitted to me to go forth"; and the closing prodigy — "They say that at the moment of his martyrdom he had grasped the pigtail of one of the heathen, which after his death could not be removed from his hand, until at last they were obliged to cut it off."
- **Posthumous, spirit-to-disciple training — a Naqshbandi initiation claim the book marks as report.** "With Saiyid Kulal, Baha'u'd-Din is said to have obtained training from the spirit of Khwaja 'Abdu'l-Khaliq Ghujduwani" (p. 115, folio 97).
- **Khizr as the source of the eight principles, believed by the disciples against the author's own explanation.** "The Shaikh's disciples however, were convinced that he had learnt these practices from Khizr" (p. 113, folio 95).
- **Hujwiri's hierarchy and cosmic function of the saints** (pp. 115–116, folios 97–98): "(God) has made the Saints the governors of the universe"; "Through the blessing of their advent the rain falls from heaven, and through the purity of their lives the plants spring up from the earth, and through their spiritual influence the Muslims gain victories over the unbelievers"; "Among them there are four thousand who are concealed and do not know one another"; and the graded officers — "three hundred, called Akhyar, and forty, called Abdal, and seven, called Abrar, and four, called Awtad, and three, called Nugaba [OCR?] [Nuqaba], and one, called Qutb or Ghaws. All these know one another and cannot act save by mutual consent." **Hujwiri claims direct sight of it: "I myself—God be praised!—have had ocular experience (khabar-i 'iyan) of this matter."**
- **The light of Muhammad predating his birth** — "the sufis saw their Shaikhs as being illuminated by the light of Muhammad which had existed even before his birth and was the sole cause of creation" (p. 116, folio 98).
- **The defence of Iblis as monotheist, twice.** "Like the martyr Hallaj, Shaikh Ahmad, defended the monotheism of Iblis" (p. 102, folio 86); and the *Tamhidat* passage in Arberry's translation — "That mad lover whom you call Iblis in this world—do you not know by what name he is called in the divine world? … This mad one loved God" (p. 103, folio 87).
- **A saying reported of Kubra** — "'The ways of God are as numerous as the number of breaths of his creatures.'" (p. 112, folio 94).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Rizvi's decline thesis, stated at the head of the chunk and directly aimed at shrine religion.** "the sufi movement in all parts of the Islamic world tended to become a legalistic system, obsessed with authoritarianism. Only within conventional forms could personal and individual enlightenment be expressed. Poetry alone offered an outlet for the expression of individualistic mystical experiences" (p. 100, folio 84); and then: "Only in their later degenerate state were some silsilas extravagant and demoralized. The members of the degenerate silsilas instituted saint worship, which precipitated a high degree of gullibility regarding the miraculous powers of different saints. The repetition of zikr became artificial; music and stylized dances, designed to produce ecstasy, were reduced to entertainment. Sufism became a popular cult, its poetic inspiration lost creativity and the mystical concept of love became sexual. However, at times these aspects of sufism were rejuvenated by far-sighted and genuinely spiritual directors. The history of the silsilas is thus marked by the perennial rise and fall of true spirituality" (p. 100, folio 84). **This is the author's governing evaluative frame for everything the archive documents, and it is hostile to saint veneration. It carries no source or evidence in this chunk.**
- **The counter-claim in the same paragraph: the orders as a social good.** "From the twelfth to the end of the fourteenth centuries, the sufi silsilas served in Iran as a bulwark against the pessimism aroused by the onslaught of the Ghuzz and the Mongols. They re-orientated religious life away from strict and often meaningless formalities. However their passivity and serenity did not become inertia" (p. 100, folio 84).
- **Where the orders came from, geographically and intellectually.** "The most popular silsilas in the East were based in Baghdad, Iran, Khurasan and Transoxiana. Many important founders of orders in Baghdad were Iranis who had been greatly influenced by the Baghdadi system of mystic legalism. These men absorbed the main features of the earlier sufi schools of sufism, at the same time relegating them into the background through the development of new frameworks of their own" (p. 100, folio 84).
- **On why the Qadiriyya spread: legend, not organisation.** "The expansion of the Qadiriyya order was very slow; in many parts of the Islamic world, the legends associated with Shaikh 'Abdu'l-Qadir played an important role in its development" (p. 101, folio 85).
- **The ʿAwarifuʾl-Maʿarif as the turning point in sufi–orthodox relations.** "This work marks a watershed in the reconciliation of sufism with orthodoxy. To later generations of sufis it became the most closely studied text on sufism" (p. 104, folio 88).
- **A Buddhist source claimed for a sufi mode of life.** Of the cave-dwelling Shikaftiyyah: "His description implies that that particular mode of life was based on the life-pattern of a section of Buddhist monks" (p. 104, folio 88). **Rizvi's inference from Suhrawardi's description, not Suhrawardi's statement.**
- **A yogic source claimed for the Naqshbandi disciplines — the chunk's most consequential borrowing claim.** "The writings of Shaikh Ghujduwani were founded on the Shari'a but his eight principles of sufi life and the rituals he advocated were largely based on yogic practices, current in the Bukhara region" (p. 113, folio 95). **No evidence, no source and no named yogic text is given for this; the sentence's only support in the chunk is the contrasting disciple tradition of Khizr.**
- **A named scholar rejected outright.** "Mole's conclusion that Shaikh Najmu'd-Din tended to create a type of Sunnite-Shi'ism has no real basis" (p. 113, folio 95), with the general rule Rizvi substitutes: "A large number of sufi poets, particularly Iranians, enthusiastically eulogised about the greatness of 'Ali and his progeny, at the same time remaining Sunnis" and "many Indian mystics whose Sunnism was never doubted, credulously adored the Saiyids and their offspring" (pp. 112–113, folios 94–95). **Note the word "credulously" — the author's own evaluation.**
- **A caution about inferring sect from poetry.** Kubra's "poems on 'Aliand his descendants [OCR?] ['Ali and his descendants] are botheloquent and touching [OCR?] [are both eloquent and touching], however, they fail to prove the author was a Shi'i" (p. 112, folio 94).
- **The Suhrawardiyya's standing in India, asserted without the comparison completed.** The order "became one of the two most significant orders of early sufism in India" (p. 103, folio 87). **The second order is not named in this chunk** — (not stated).
- **Suhrawardi's own doctrinal positions as Rizvi summarises them** — that esoteric knowledge, not legalistic knowledge, is "the basis of sufism", applying *Al Rasikhun fi al-ʿIlm* to "those whose hearts have a total perception of the Truth", knowledge which "cannot be learnt in school but is a legacy from the prophets" (p. 105, folio 89); that gnosis does not absolve from Shariʿa, "The law and Hagiqa [OCR?] [Haqiqa] (Reality) were interdependent"; that he "condemned sufis who, following the analogy of the divine and human aspects of Christ's personality, believed in the doctrine of incarnation (Aulul [OCR?] [hulul])", and that "Sufis who spoke of submerging themselves into the ocean of Divine Unity … were misdirected. Precedence should be given to fulfilling the divine will" (p. 105, folio 89).
- **His psychology and his technical distinctions** — the spirit "neither eternal nor subsistent, but created and an attribute of God", the animal spirit "connected with the digestive organism of the body" and the heavenly spirit belonging "to the world of command" (p. 105, folio 89); the three stages of the *nafs*, "The desire for evil (ammara), repentance (lawwama) and satisfaction (mutma'ina)" (pp. 105–106, folios 89–90); the heart *qalb* of the believer, unbeliever, hypocrite and the "many-faceted heart" (p. 106, folio 90); reason as "an innate human talent" that must be "supported and supplemented by the light of Islamic law" (p. 106, folio 90); and the *hal*/*maqam* distinction — "State involved a changing psychological condition, while stage was relatively permanent", with meditation a *hal*, contemplation the second stage and "observation (mushahada)" the third (p. 106, folio 90); and the two stages of *fanaʾ* and the recovery of agency in *baqaʾ*, "a second birth from the womb of the spirit to the kingdom of a newly-awakened spirit" (pp. 106, 109, folios 90, 91).
- **ʿAbduʾl-Qadir Jilani's four stages, as Rizvi reports them** — "Obedience to religious law was the first stage … The state of reality, equivalent to that of Saintliness (wilaya), was the second stage … The third stage was that of resignation … The fourth and final stage was fana'" (p. 101, folio 85); and his inner jihad — "A jihad fought against self-will was, to Shaikh 'Abdu'l-Qadir, far superior to that waged with the sword. Through this struggle the idolatry of the self and the worship of created things (the hidden shirk) could be vanquished" (p. 101, folio 85).

**Archive-mapping doubts:**

- **`data-darbar` is mapped on the author-only ruling alone, and on a bare surname.** The book writes only "Hujwiri" and identifies him only as the author of the *Kashf al-Mahjub* (p. 109, folio 91). **It gives no personal name, no death date, no Lahore, no tomb, no title "Data Ganj Bakhsh" and no silsila anywhere in pp. 100–117** — all (not stated). The identification with the row's principal figure "Hazrat Ali ibn Usman al-Hujwiri (Data Ganj Bakhsh)" rests on the authorship of the *Kashf al-Mahjub*, which the book states. **A consolidator must not read these five bullets as shrine evidence; they are evidence about a text.**
- **"Tirmizi" (p. 115, folio 97) — a name collision I am not resolving.** The book prints the surname alone, with no first name, dates or place. The archive row `shrine-of-pir-baba-syed-ali-tirmizi` names "Syed Ali Tirmizi (Pir Baba)" with `figure_died` 1583. **The chunk's own chronology makes that row impossible here**: this Tirmizi is a source Hujwiri drew on, and Hujwiri's *Kashf al-Mahjub* is placed in the chunk as the work Suhrawardi (d. 632/1234-35) improved upon (pp. 104, 109; folios 88, 91). **So I make no mapping and none should be inferred — but I am flagging it rather than dismissing it, because the reasoning uses the index's date and not the book's, and the book supplies no identification of its own.**
- **The material this run was sent to find is partly absent, and the absence is a locatable one.** There is **no statement anywhere in pp. 100–117 about Shaikhs being buried inside their khanqahs or close by, and none about rulers or nobles endowing tombs** — the single endowment act in the range is a caliph building a living Shaikh a khanqah with a bath-house and a garden (p. 104, folio 88), and the single income rule is "endowments and begging" (p. 109, folio 91). Likewise **there is no baiʿa and no khilafat-nama**: the chunk's transmission instruments are the khirqa (p. 110, folio 92) and the khalifa (p. 116, folio 98). The chapter section "The organisation and rituals of Silsilas" opens at p. 115 (folio 97) and is **cut off mid-sentence at the chunk boundary** ("descended into the heart from God", p. 117, folio 99), so the burial-and-endowment passage should be looked for at the head of chunk 007.
- **Hinglaj does not appear in this chunk**, so the empty-id defect on the row `Shaktipeeth Shri Hinglaj Mata Mandir` does not bite here. It remains unresolved from the earlier runs and no id has been invented.
- **No toponym in this chunk is withheld by the unruled interim convention in a way that matters.** All the place names in pp. 100–117 are Iranian, Iraqi, Egyptian, Anatolian or Central Asian (list under "Other saints, sites and events"), and **none corresponds to an archive row's location** — so unlike the schimmel run, reversing the toponym ruling would gain this chunk nothing.

**Internal contradictions in the chunk, kept side by side rather than resolved:**

- **Sufi income is described as single-source and as multi-source within two pages.** "The unsolicited gift (futuh) from devotees was their only source of income" (p. 116, folio 98) stands against "there were two sources of income for a khanqah—endowments and begging" (p. 109, folio 91), against "Gifts from rich men, government officials, princes and rulers were also welcomed" and "merchants were the leading financial supporters of the sufi movement" on the same page as the "only source" claim (p. 116, folio 98), and against "Many eminent sufis worked as petty traders, artisans or cultivators" (p. 117, folio 99). **"Only source of income" cannot be quoted without this context.**
- **Aloofness from the state is asserted and contradicted.** "Sufis kept themselves aloof from both the administrative machinery and imperialist wars of the government" (p. 117, folio 99) stands against Shihabuʾd-Din Suhrawardi being "despatched … as his ambassador" by Caliph al-Nasir to three foreign courts and being given an "extensive khangah" by that same Caliph (p. 104, folio 88), and against the Caliphs granting Ibn al-Jawzi "what amounted virtually to inquisitorial powers" (p. 104, folio 88). **The book does not note the tension.**
- **Celibacy is urged and married Shaikhs are recorded without comment** — the *Masalikuʾl-ʿArifin* on celibacy and the dangers of married life (p. 113, folio 95) against ʿAbduʾl-Qadir marrying at fifty-one (p. 100, folio 84), Kubra marrying his preceptor's daughter (p. 111, folio 93), and a khanqah built for a Shaikh "and his family" (p. 104, folio 88).
- **Saint veneration is condemned as degeneracy and then described neutrally as doctrine.** The p. 100 (folio 84) passage makes "saint worship" and "gullibility regarding the miraculous powers of different saints" the marks of "degenerate silsilas"; pp. 115–116 (folios 97–98) set out Hujwiri's hierarchy of saints as "governors of the universe" without any such comment, and p. 113 (folio 95) calls Indian mystics' adoration of the Saiyids "credulous" while defending their Sunnism. **The author's evaluative register shifts inside the chunk.**
- **One rare internal confirmation, worth recording as such:** ʿAinuʾl-Quzat "Born in 492/1098" and "martyred at the age of thirty-three" in "525/6-7 May 1131" (pp. 102–103, folios 86–87) — 525 − 492 = 33, so these three printed values agree. **No other date set in the chunk can be cross-checked from within it**, and the many AH/CE pairs (470/1077—78, 488/1095, 520/1126, 521/1127, 523/1129, 539/1145, 561/1166, 563/1168, 618/1221, 632/1234-35, 535/1140, 718/1318, 791/1389 and the doubled dates of the Khwajgan chain) are recorded above exactly as printed and are **unverified**.
