# hadeeqat_ul_aulia — chunk 019 — pp. 196–204

**The fifth chapter's early Lahore and Kashmir entries, nos. 132 to 140, plus the whole of the
cut-off Data Darbar footnote from chunk 018.** Four archive rows take substantial material:
**data-darbar** (the editor declares its death year wrong and updates its attendance for 1947),
**sakhi-sarwar** (entry no. 132: origin, martyrdom, a dated year, the site at Nigaha and a spring),
**shrine-of-miran-hussain-zanjani-zanjani-sahib** (entry no. 133, with the editor separating two
saints of the same name and defending the author's date from a 1071 AH document), and
**bibi-pak-daman** (entry no. 134 names the six bibis as one man's daughters). Its three most
substantial features: **"آپ کا سال وفات ۴۶۵ھ غلط ہے" — Data Ganj Bakhsh's death year of 465 AH is
wrong**, with 490 AH argued from *Kashf al-Mahjub*'s internal evidence; **Sakhi Sarwar's shrine
explained as the site of his murder by jealous cousins in 577 AH**; and **a Kashmir khanqah endowed
with several villages whose income paid its staff**.

**Folio markers: this chunk settles the offset problem, and the printed markers for pp. 176–197 are
the error.** Here p. 196 → folio 194 and p. 197 → folio 195 (offset 2), but then **p. 198 → folio
186** and so on to p. 204 → folio 192, all offset **12**. A printed page sequence cannot run
…194, 195, 186, 187…, so **the markers cannot all be right**; since folio = PDF − 12 holds for
pp. 168–175 (chunk 016) and again for pp. 198–204 here, **the relation is right and the markers for
the pp. 176–197 stretch are inflated by ten.** On that reading the true folios are p. 196 → 184,
p. 197 → 185, and the notes for chunks 016, 017 and 018 need their folio values reduced by ten from
p. 176 onward. Below, folios are given as printed with the corrected value in brackets where the
marker is suspect.

- **sakhi-sarwar** (سید احمد المشہور بسخی سرور سلطان — entry no. 132, pp. 197–198, folios 195–186 as
  printed [corrected: 185–186]):
  - **Placed by region, not by order.** "قدمائے مشائخ و کبرائے اولیائے **خطہ ملتان** سے ہیں" — he is
    of the ancient mashaikh and the great awliya of the **region of Multan** (p. 197).
  - **Twenty-one-step pedigree to Ali**, on the authority of **the author of *Tashrif
    al-Shurafa***: Sayyid Ahmad bin Sayyid Zain al-Abidin bin Sayyid Umar bin Sayyid Abd al-Latif bin
    Sayyid Baha al-Din bin Sayyid Ghiyas al-Din bin Sayyid Baha al-Din bin Sayyid Salah al-Din bin
    Sayyid Zain al-Abidin bin Sayyid Isa bin Sayyid Salih bin Sayyid Abd al-Ghani bin Sayyid Jalil bin
    Sayyid Khair al-Din bin Sayyid Zia al-Din bin Sayyid Daud bin **Sayyid Abd al-Jalil Rumi** bin
    Sayyid Ismail bin **Imam Jafar Sadiq** bin Imam Muhammad Baqir bin Imam Zain al-Abidin bin Imam
    Husain bin Ali (p. 197).
  - **Family origins, with his mother named.** His father **Zain al-Abidin** came from **Arabia** to
    Hind "بسبب کسی تفرقہ کے", because of some dissension, and settled at **"کہر سی کوٹ [OCR?]" in the
    Multan area**; being devout, ascetic and a night-vigil keeper, "اس گاؤں کے **مقدم مسمی پیرا** نے
    اپنی لڑکی **بی بی عائشہ** ان کے نکاح میں دے دی" — the **muqaddam of that village, named Pira**,
    gave him his daughter **Bibi Ayesha** in marriage; from her the Hazrat was born, **"ولی مادر
    زاد"**, a wali from birth (p. 197).
  - **Training at Baghdad, under three named masters.** After his father's death his fame spread and
    people came in droves; his maternal cousins, **with whom he owned half the village** ("جن کے ساتھ
    یہ نصف گاؤں کے مالک تھے"), grew jealous and tormented him; so he migrated and at **Baghdad**
    received inward faiz in the service of **Ghaus al-Azam**, **Shaikh al-Shuyukh Shihab al-Din
    Suhrawardi** and **Shaikh Maudud Chishti**, returning to Hind perfected (p. 197).
  - **Two Punjab residences before the shrine site.** "پنجاب میں پہنچ کر چندے بمقام **دھونکل** سکونت
    کی پھر **کہر سی کوٹ [OCR?]** میں آئے" — on reaching the Punjab he lived a while at **Dhaunkal**,
    then came back to Kahr Si Kot (p. 197).
  - **A governor's daughter and a son.** **"صوبہ ملتان نے اپنی لڑکی ان کے نکاح میں دی۔ اس سے ایک بیٹا
    سراج الدین نامی پیدا ہوا"** — the **governor of Multan** gave him his daughter in marriage, and
    from her a son named **Siraj al-Din** was born (p. 197).
  - **The shrine site is the site of the murder, and it is named.** Warned of a plot to kill him, he
    left the village secretly "مع بھائی **عبدالغنی** و زوجہ **سراج الدین**، پسر خرد سال" — with his
    brother **Abd al-Ghani**, his wife, and his young son Siraj al-Din — and **"زیر دامان کوہ جہاں اب
    موضع نگاہا آباد ہے اور روضہ بھی حضرت کا اسی مقام پر ہے"**, at the foot of the mountain **where
    the mauza Nigaha is now settled, and where the Hazrat's roza also is**, pitched a tent "عین
    ویرانے بے آب میں", in the very midst of a waterless wilderness (p. 197). Then: "پیچھے ان کے جب
    حاسد بھائیوں نے خبر پائی کہ وہ چلے گئے تو مجمع کثیر کر کے ان کے پیچھے سوار ہوئے اور اس مقام پر
    آکر **حضرت کو مع لواحقین شہید کر دیا**" — the jealous brothers, learning they had gone, gathered
    a great crowd, rode after them and, coming to that place, **martyred the Hazrat together with his
    dependants**. **"یہ واقعہ سنہ پانسو ستتر ہجری میں واقع ہوا"** — this event occurred in **577 AH**
    (p. 198). **The archive row carries no `figure_died`: 577 AH, a martyrdom, and Nigaha as the site
    are all new from this chunk.**
  - **A spring said to still flow.** God, by the blessing of his step, **"وہاں ریگستان میں سے پانی کا
    چشمہ جاری کر دیا جو اب تک ہے"** — made a spring of water flow there out of the desert, **which
    exists to this day** (p. 197). A physical feature of the shrine site, in the author's present.
  - **A Persian chronogram** is printed without a numeral: "سید و سرور و سخی احمد / بود در ملک سروری
    والی // رفت چوں از جہاں بخلد بریں / شد زمین از وجود او خالی // سال تاریخ وصل آں سرور / گفت سرور
    کہ سرور عالی" (p. 198). The chronogrammatic word is "سرور عالی"; **the year is not printed beside
    it**, unlike the Data Ganj Bakhsh and Miran Badshah verses.
  - The chunk gives **no urs, no langar, no custodian and no building date** for this shrine, and the
    editor adds **no footnote at all** to this entry.

  - **The father's shrine and the quarter's name.** Sayyid Ahmad Tokhta Tirmizi died **602 AH**, "اور
    **مرشد پنجاب** ۶۰۲ مادۂ تاریخ ہے" — "Murshid-e Punjab" is the chronogram — "اور مزار گوہر بار
    **اندرون شہر لاہور محلہ چہل بی بیاں** طویلہ حضرت غلام محی الدین کے زیارت گاہ خلق ہے", and his
    mazar **inside the city of Lahore, in Mohalla Chihal Bibiyan**, by the "طویلہ" of Hazrat Ghulam
    Muhyi al-Din, is a place of pilgrimage (p. 200). **"محلہ چہل بی بیاں" — the quarter of the forty
    bibis — is a distinct name from "بی بیاں پاکدامن"**; whether the two are the same site is not
    stated: see Doubts.
  - **The father's own details**: originally of **Tirmidh**; came to Hindustan by an unseen sign; "یہ
    بزرگ **سید حسینی** تھے", a Sayyid Husaini, descended through **Imam Ali Asghar bin Imam Zain
    al-Abidin bin Imam Husain**; "شرافت و ولایت و کرامت ان کی **موروثی** تھی"; and "**توسل خاندان
    جنیدیہ** سے تھا", his affiliation was with the **Junaidi** family (pp. 199–200). **His pedigree
    is corrected twice by the editor: see Arguments.** The origin of his epithet is under Arguments
    too.
  - **Nothing here about the Pak Daman shrine's fabric, urs or custodianship** — this chunk supplies
    a **family**, not a site: a father with a dated Lahore grave, four unmarried ascetic daughters and
    two married ones, and a warning that the literature about them is unreliable.

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- **Sayyid Ahmad Tokhta Tirmizi Lahori** (entry no. 134, pp. 199–200): see under bibi-pak-daman above
  for his family and grave. His epithet: per **Pir Farah Bakhsh Farhat**, his shaikh once summoned
  him; finding the room's door shut he stood outside all night "کہ مبادا شیخ یاد فرمائیں اور میں نہ
  ہوا", lest the shaikh call and he not be there; at dawn the shaikh opened the door, found him
  standing, and said **"سید احمد توختہ!"** — and from that moment *Tokhta* became part of his name, a
  **Turkish** word meaning **"ایستادہ کھڑا"**, standing upright (*Nami, Tarikh-e Jalila* 126–127, on
  the authority of *Azkar-e Qalandari*) (p. 199, حاشیہ 1). The fourth saint's title in these chunks
  given a vernacular or foreign etymology, after *chuhar* (Hindi), *jhula* (Punjabi) and *rajan katal*
  (Syriac).
- **Sayyid Yaqub, addressed as Sadr-e Diwan Zanjani Lahori** (entry no. 135, pp. 200–201, folios
  188–189 as printed): a gatherer of the outward and inward sciences; came from **Zanjan** to Lahore
  "بارادۂ سیر", with the intention of travel, and settled; thousands reached the stations of nearness
  through his bai'at. His father was **Hamid[OCR?] Ali Musavi Husaini Zanjani**, at whose hand his own
  bai'at was taken; "ان کا توسل **سلسلہ سہلویہ [OCR?]** کے ساتھ تھا". **Tughril, subah of Lahore**,
  was his murid — see Practices. "تمام پنجاب میں حضرت کی مشیخت کا آوازہ تھا۔ دور دور سے لوگ حصول
  ارادت و توسل کے لیے خدمت میں حاضر ہوتے تھے." Death **16 Rajab 604 AH**; mazar in Lahore city, no
  quarter. **He is Husain Zanjani's brother** (per the quoted *Waqai-e Sialkot* passage) and the man
  Husain came to Lahore with. **The editor calls his whole Lahore chronology doubtful: see Arguments.**
- **Sayyid Shaikh Aziz al-Din Makki Lahori** (entry no. 136, p. 201, folio 189 as printed): originally
  from **Baghdad**; went thence to **Mecca** and passed **twelve years** in worship at the Bait Allah,
  becoming famous as "شیخ کی [OCR?]"; then turned to Hindustan and **entered Lahore in the year 574
  AH, the year in which Sultan Shihab al-Din Ghauri was laying siege to Lahore**. **Khusrau Malik bin
  Khusrau Shah Ghaznavi** was besieged within the city; hard pressed, he came and sought the Hazrat's
  prayer, and after muraqaba and mukashafa the Hazrat said: **"خداوند حقیقی کی جانب سے چھ برس تجھ کو
  اور امان ہے۔ بعد چھ برس کے خدا کو منظور ہے کہ اس ملک میں غوریوں کا تسلط ہو جائے"** — from the true
  Lord you have **six more years** of safety; after six years it is God's will that the **Ghurids'**
  dominion be established in this country. "پس اس سال میں شہاب الدین لاہور کے فتح کیے بغیر **غزنین**
  کو چلا گیا اور چھ برس کے بعد پھر آیا اور لاہور پر قابض ہوا۔ باقی ماندہ سلطنت غزنوی بادشاہوں کی
  نیست و نابود ہو گئی" — that year Shihab al-Din withdrew to Ghaznin without taking Lahore, returned
  six years later and seized it, and the remaining Ghaznavid sultanate was annihilated. Death **612
  AH**; mazar in Lahore. **A dated prophecy about the fall of a dynasty, made inside a besieged city
  — and the same name appears with a different death year in the author's other book: see Doubts.**
- **Sayyid Mitha Lahori** (entry no. 137, pp. 201–202, folios 189–190 as printed): **"لاہور کے
  بزرگوں میں سے یہ بزرگ آج تک مرجع خاص و عام ہیں"** — to this day a resort of high and low; "حضرت کی
  بزرگی کا حال زبان زد خرد و کلاں ہے", the account of his greatness is on the tongues of small and
  great. His father **Sayyid Jamal al-Din** was of the province of **Khwarazm**; **"جب خوارزم کی
  ولایت پر چنگیز خاں کا دخل ہوا اور شاہان خوارزم کی سلطنت جُڑ سے اُکھڑ گئی اور وہ ملک ویران و برباد
  ہو گیا تو انہوں نے ہندوستان کا راستہ لیا اور لاہور میں آکر سکونت اختیار کی"** — when **Chingiz
  Khan** took the province of Khwarazm and the sultanate of the Khwarazm kings was torn up by the
  root and that country laid waste, they took the road to **Hindustan** and settled in **Lahore**.
  Sayyid Mitha completed under his father and **"باپ کے مرنے بعد یہ جانشین ہوئے"**, became his
  janishin after his death. **His name from a Punjabi word**: "چوں کہ شیریں زبان اور خوش خلق نہایت
  تھے۔ سید مٹھہ مشہور ہو گئے کہ **مٹھا شیریں کو کہتے ہیں**" — because he was sweet-tongued and of
  excellent character he became famous as Sayyid Mitha, for *mittha* means sweet. Nineteen-step
  pedigree "باقوال صحیح", by correct reports, through **Imam Ali Raza, Musa Kazim, Jafar Sadiq,
  Muhammad Baqir, Zain al-Abidin** to **Imam Husain**. Death **661 AH**; mazar inside the city of
  Lahore. **A Mongol-displacement origin for a Lahore shrine family, and a father-to-son succession
  stated as such.**
- **Muayyid al-Din Bulbul Shah Kashmiri** (entry no. 138, pp. 202–203, folios 190–191 as printed):
  per the author of *Tawarikh-e Azami* his **real name was Sharaf al-Din**; he came to **Srinagar**
  and gave Islam currency, and by his attention the **raja of Kashmir became Muslim** with all his
  officers. The conversion story and its aftermath are under Legends and Practices. Death **727 AH**;
  buried in the city of Kashmir; **"مکان مسکن مدفن ان کا اب تک لنگر بلبل شاہ مشہور ہے"** — his house,
  dwelling and burial place is famous to this day as **Langar Bulbul Shah**. The editor adds: he was
  a murid of **Shah Ni'mat Allah Farsi**, "جن کا تعلق **سلسلہ سہروردیہ** سے تھا", of the Suhrawardi
  silsila, and **came to Kashmir from Turkestan with a thousand refugees, in Sahdeva's reign, in fear
  of the Mongol attack** (*Muhibb al-Hasan* 55), and was learned in the sciences and arts (*Azami*
  p. 31) (p. 203, حاشیہ 3).
- **Raja "Nar Sanjan[OCR?] Deo" / Rinchan / Sadr al-Din** (pp. 202–203): the convert. The editor:
  "نرسنجن کا صحیح اور پورا نام **مہاجن بن مہاجن ڈنگوس گرب [OCR?]** تھا جو **۱۲۹۰ سے ۱۳۲۰ء تک لداخ
  کا حکمران** رہا پھر **۱۳۲۰-۱۳۲۳ء کشمیر پر حکمرانی** کی۔ ۱۳۲۰ء میں وہ کشمیر کے تخت پر بیٹھا اور
  تمام شورشوں کو دبا کر ۱۳۲۰ء کے بعد حضرت شرف الدین معروف بہ بلبل شاہؒ کے دست حق پرست پر اسلام قبول
  کیا اور **صدر الدین** نام رکھا گیا۔ اس کا سالار **راون چند** بھی مشرف با اسلام ہوا" — his correct
  and full name was Mahajan bin Mahajan Dangos Garb[OCR?]; he ruled **Ladakh from 1290 to 1320 CE**,
  then **Kashmir from 1320 to 1323 CE**; he took the throne in 1320, suppressed all revolts, and after
  1320 accepted Islam at Bulbul Shah's hand and was named **Sadr al-Din**; his commander **Rawan
  Chand** also embraced Islam (*Muhibb al-Hasan* 55–56) (p. 202, حاشیہ 1).
- **Shaikh Sayyid Abu Ishaq Gazruni, famous as Miran Badshah Lahori** (entry no. 139, p. 203,
  folio 191 as printed): among the ancient elders of **Lahore**, famous as a great one. Originally of
  the city of **Gazrun**; **murid of Shaikh Auhad al-Din Isfahani**; came to Hind by way of travel and

  786**. **And then the shrine's later history in one sentence: "جب علم الدین حکیم المشہور نواب وزیر
  خان نے جامع مسجد اس جگہ تعمیر کی تو ان کے مزار کو مسجد کے صحن کے اندر ایک تہ خانہ میں رکھا جو اب
  تک زیارت گاہ خلق ہے"** — when **Ilm al-Din Hakim, famous as Nawab Wazir Khan**, built the **Jami
  Masjid at that place**, he **placed his mazar in a basement (tahkhana) inside the mosque's
  courtyard**, which is a place of pilgrimage to this day. See Practices; and note the second death
  year for him in the author's other book, under Doubts.
- **Shaikh Nur al-Din Wali Kashmiri** (entry no. 140, opening only, p. 204, folio 192 as printed):
  a gatherer of outward and inward sciences, "زہد میں طاق، ریاضت میں یگانہ آفاق". **A quantified
  twenty-six-year fast**: at thirty the longing for God seized him, and for **twelve years** he lived
  alone in a wilderness jungle without food or sleep, eating "تھوڑی سی کائی کی پتی", a little moss
  leaf, when hunger overcame him; then for **twelve years** his daily food was **one bowl of milk**;
  then he gave that up and for **two and a half years** took the water of a little boiled grain;
  **"غرض چھبیس سال تک حضرت نے روٹی نہ کھائی"** — in short, for **twenty-six years the Hazrat ate no

  Kashmir; and when Mir Muhammad went on Hajj, he attended **Mir Sayyid Husain Samani, Shaikh Baha
  al-Din, Shaikh Sultan Kamli[OCR?] and Baba Haji Adham Kashmiri** and received the title **Qutb
  al-Afaq**. Per *Tawarikh-e Azami* he was a wali from birth: while in his mother's womb the **rijal
  al-ghaib** came and greeted her, and **Bibi Lal Ded (بی بی لل ودی), a gnostic woman** — the entry
  breaks off here and continues in chunk 020. **Bibi Lal Ded is the second named holy woman in this
  chunk after the four unmarried bibis.**

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **A khanqah endowed with villages, whose income paid its staff — the only true waqf in these
  chunks.** Of Bulbul Shah: **"رنچن نے دریائے جہلم کے کنارے اپنے محل کے پاس ہی بابا بلبل شاہؒ کے لیے
  ایک خانقاہ بنوائی اور اس پر کئی گاؤں وقف کیے۔ جن کی آمدنی سے ملازمین کا خرچ چلتا تھا"** — Rinchan
  had a khanqah built for Baba Bulbul Shah **on the bank of the Jhelum, right beside his own palace**,
  and **endowed several villages upon it, from whose income the expenses of the employees were met**
  (*Muhibb al-Hasan* 56) (p. 203, حاشیہ 1). **Four elements the archive can use**: a royal
  foundation, a location relative to the palace, a landed endowment in villages, and **paid staff**.
  Compare Mauj Darya's one-lakh jagir (chunk 017), which funded a langar rather than salaries.
- **A mosque built beside it, for the patron's own use.** "خانقاہ کے پاس رنچن نے ایک مسجد بھی تعمیر
  کی جس میں وہ **پانچ وقت نماز ادا کرتا تھا**" — near the khanqah Rinchan also built a mosque, in
  which he prayed the five daily prayers (*Muhibb al-Hasan* 57) (p. 203, حاشیہ 2). The text's own
  version differs: "راجہ نے ان کی خاطر کشمیر میں ایک مکان خانقاہ تعمیر کیا۔ جس میں حضرت سکونت رکھتے
  تھے۔ **ایک مسجد عالی شان حضرت نے خود تعمیر کی**" — the raja built the khanqah house, and **the
  Hazrat himself built a lofty mosque** (p. 203). See Doubts.
- **A shrine still known by its langar's name.** "مکان مسکن مدفن ان کا اب تک **لنگر بلبل شاہ** مشہور
  ہے" (p. 203) — the house, the dwelling and the grave under one name, and that name is the **langar**.
  The strongest single instance in these chunks of a feeding institution giving a shrine its identity.
- **A public langar funded by a governor's discipleship.** Of Sayyid Yaqub Zanjani: **"مسمی طغرل صوبہ
  لاہور جو بہرام شاہ غزنوی کی طرف سے لاہور کا حاکم تھا۔ ان کا مرید تھا۔ اس سبب سے قبول عظیم ان کو
  حاصل ہوا۔ دولت ظاہری و باطنی کے خزانے بھر گئے اور لنگر عام جاری ہوا"** — one named **Tughril, subah
  of Lahore**, governor on behalf of **Bahram Shah Ghaznavi**, was his murid; for that reason he
  gained great acceptance, the treasuries of outward and inward wealth were filled, and **a public
  langar was set running** (p. 200, folio 188 as printed). The causal chain is stated plainly:
  **official discipleship → wealth → langar.** The editor holds the chronology behind it to be
  doubtful: see Arguments.
- **A grave enclosed in a basement inside a Mughal mosque's courtyard, by a named builder.** Miran
  Badshah's mazar, placed by **Nawab Wazir Khan (Ilm al-Din Hakim)** "مسجد کے صحن کے اندر ایک تہ
  خانہ میں", in a tahkhana inside the courtyard of the Jami Masjid he built there, "جو اب تک زیارت
  گاہ خلق ہے" (p. 203, folio 191 as printed). **The second Lahore shrine in these chunks preserved as
  a tahkhana inside a later monument**, after Shah Jhula Bukhari inside the Badshahi Qila (chunk 016,
  p. 174). The pattern — a saint's grave kept as a basement beneath imperial construction — is worth
  recording as such.
- **A chilla performed at another saint's grave, with lodging arranged.** Khwaja Muin al-Din came from
  **Ajmer** expressly to perform a chilla at Data Ganj Bakhsh's roza and was Sayyid Yaqub Zanjani's
  guest "انہیں کے اول و آخر چلہ سے چند روز", for some days at the beginning and the end of the chilla
  (pp. 200–201). A pilgrimage practice with a host, a purpose and a duration.
- **A spring at a shrine site, attributed to the saint's arrival and said to persist** (p. 197) — the
  only water source in these chunks treated as part of a shrine.
- **A grave inside a named Lahore quarter, located by a third party's property.** Sayyid Ahmad Tokhta
  Tirmizi's mazar in **Mohalla Chihal Bibiyan**, "طویلہ حضرت غلام محی الدین کے" — by the *tawila*
  (stable/enclosure) of Hazrat Ghulam Muhyi al-Din (p. 200). A locating landmark that was current when
  the author wrote and may not survive.
- **Half a village held in co-ownership, and jealousy over it as the cause of a martyrdom.** Sakhi
  Sarwar and his maternal cousins "نصف گاؤں کے مالک تھے", owned half the village between them
  (p. 197) — the property relation that the entry makes the motive for his murder. **The only
  statement in these chunks of a saint's family landholding before sainthood.**
- **A manuscript held by a named living owner in Pakistan**: *Samarat al-Quds* of Lal Beg,
  "قلمی مملوکہ **مولانا نصرت نوشاہی شرقپور**" (pp. 196, 201) — cited twice, once in the Data Ganj
  Bakhsh list and once for Sayyid Mitha. And *Waqai-e Sialkot* (1071 AH) is cited in **Dr Abdullah
  Chughtai's edition, Lahore 1972** (p. 201, حاشیہ 2) — a two-year-old imprint at the time of this
  edition.

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **A king's conversion decided by who is first seen at dawn.** Raja "Nar Sanjan Deo" resolved to
  accept whichever religion proved true after full inquiry; he summoned the ulama of every religion to
  Kashmir and had them debate, "مگر بسبب اس کے کہ ہر ایک صاحب ملت اپنے دین کو اچھا کہتا اور وجوہات
  بیان کرتا تھا راجہ کی کامل تسلی نہ ہوتی" — but since each man of each community praised his own
  religion and gave reasons, the raja was never fully satisfied. So he settled it in his heart that
  **"کل علی الصباح جس دین کا آدمی پہلے سب سے میرے روبرو آئے گا۔ اس کا دین اختیار کروں گا"** — tomorrow
  at dawn, whichever religion's man first comes before me, I shall adopt his religion. At dawn he
  climbed his palace and looked out, and **Bulbul Shah was praying at the edge of the Dal**; already
  inclined to Islam, he was delighted, called him before him **"اور اسی وقت … مشرف با اسلام ہوا"**,
  and at that very moment was honoured with Islam; most of his nobles followed, "اور نور اسلام کشمیر
  کی سرزمین پر روشن ہوا" (pp. 202–203). **A conversion narrative in which the decisive act is an
  ordinary prayer seen from a window** — the book's fullest account of a ruler's conversion, and it is
  dated by the editor to after 1320 CE.
- **A six-year reprieve granted to a besieged king, and the fall of his dynasty foretold** — Aziz
  al-Din Makki to Khusrau Malik in 574 AH, fulfilled to the year (p. 201).
- **A spring opened in a waterless wilderness by the blessing of a saint's step**, at the place that
  became his shrine (p. 197).
- **Greeted in the womb by the rijal al-ghaib** — Shah Nur al-Din Wali, per *Tawarikh-e Azami*
  (p. 204). The passage naming Bibi Lal Ded breaks off mid-sentence.
- **Twenty-six years without bread**, in three measured stages (p. 204) — the most precisely
  quantified austerity in these chunks, and it is recorded as a spiritual achievement rather than a
  miracle.
- **A murshid's far-seeing eye, proved by a funeral.** Data Ganj Bakhsh arrives in Lahore to find
  Husain Zanjani's bier going past, which is why his murshid had insisted he go — told by the editor
  as the Mufti's own report from *Fawaid al-Fawad*, and **used against him** (p. 198).
- **A martyrdom recorded without a miracle.** Sakhi Sarwar and his household are killed and the entry
  simply gives the year (p. 198); the karamat in his entry is the spring, which precedes the murder.

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The book's most consequential single sentence for the archive: "آپ کا سال وفات ۴۶۵ھ غلط ہے."**
  Data Ganj Bakhsh's death year of 465 AH — printed three times in chronograms in the entry — is
  declared **wrong**, on **Abd al-Hai Habibi's** argument from the internal evidence of *Kashf
  al-Mahjub*, which puts its composition at **490 AH [OCR?]** and its author alive until then
  (p. 196, حاشیہ 5.2, with the article cited at 5.13: *Oriental College Magazine*, February
  1940[OCR?]). Note the form: the editor does **not** supply a replacement death year, only a
  terminus post quem. He also gathers three further "نتائج" of modern research — an indeterminate
  birth of 400 AH[OCR?], the separation of the two Husain Zanjanis, and the rejection of *Kashf
  al-Asrar* as a forgery — and offers them as a set, "حضرت علی ہجویری کے بارے میں **جدید تحقیق** کے
  بعض نتائج", some results of modern research. **This is the editor at his most explicitly
  modern-scholarly, and the authorities are a Moscow edition, an Afghan historian and two
  twentieth-century Lahore publications.**
- **An author caught between his own two books, and the contradiction resolved by splitting the
  saint.** The Husain Zanjani footnote (p. 198, حاشیہ 1, continuing p. 199) is the longest sustained
  argument in these chunks: the Mufti's *Khazinat al-Asfiya* 2/232 makes Husain Zanjani Data's
  pir-brother, dead before 400 AH; the same book at 2/250 **and this present book** give him 600 AH
  **"بغیر کسی حوالے کے"**, without any reference; the editor's verdict is that the Mufti is
  **"خاصے الجھے ہوئے"**, quite tangled, and that **two different men bear the name**. He then supports
  the later dating twice: by Muin al-Din Chishti's meeting with him around **585 AH**, on five
  sources; and by **Khizr Khan's** appearance as his grandson in **758 AH** in the *Waqai-e Sialkot*
  of **1071 AH**, concluding **"شیخ حسین زنجانی کا مذکورہ سال وفات ۶۰۰ھ قرین صحت ہے"** — the stated
  death year of 600 AH is close to correct (p. 199). **This is the only place in these chunks where
  the editor's research confirms the author rather than overturning him, and the method is
  prosopographical: date a saint by a dated descendant.**
- **A shrine's arrival narrative called doubtful on chronological grounds.** Of Sayyid Yaqub Zanjani:
  **"بہرام شاہ کا عہد ۵۱۲-۵۵۲/۱۱۱۸-۱۱۵۷ء تھا اور اس کے ساتھ ہی خواجہ معین الدین اجمیری (ورود لاہور
  حدود ۵۸۸) کے شیخ یعقوب زنجانیؒ کے مہمان ہونے کا ذکر اور پھر شیخ یعقوب کا سال وفات ۶۰۴ھ لکھا۔ خاصا
  متضاد نظر آتا ہے اس لیے شیخ یعقوب زنجانیؒ کے ورود لاہور کی روایت بعہد طغرل نائب بہرام شاہ مشکوک
  ہے"** — **Bahram Shah's era was 512–552 AH / 1118–1157 CE**; setting beside that the statement that
  Khwaja Muin al-Din Ajmeri (**arrival at Lahore about 588 AH**) was Shaikh Yaqub's guest, and then
  the death year of **604 AH**, **appears quite contradictory; therefore the tradition of Shaikh
  Yaqub Zanjani's arrival at Lahore in the time of Tughril, Bahram Shah's deputy, is doubtful**
  (p. 200, حاشیہ 2). **The langar, the governor's discipleship and the wealth all hang on that
  tradition** — so the editor has undercut this shrine's whole institutional story without deleting
  it. A model of the edition's method: the report stays, the doubt is printed beside it.
- **Two names in a pedigree replaced and two more restored.** Of Sayyid Ahmad Tokhta Tirmizi's chain:
  "یہاں **سید حسین مدنی کی بجائے حسن ثانی** ہونا چاہیے" — here, instead of Sayyid Husain Madani, it
  should be **Hasan Thani** (*Tarikh-e Jalila* 127); and "**ناصر الدین کے بعد دو نام مفتی صاحب چھوڑ
  گئے ہیں** یعنی سید حسینی **حمیص [OCR?] بن سید حسن**" — **after Nasir al-Din the Mufti has omitted
  two names**, namely Sayyid Husaini Hamis[OCR?] bin Sayyid Hasan (same source, 127) (p. 200, حاشیہ
  3–4). **The father of the Bibis Pak Daman therefore has a pedigree with one substitution and one
  lacuna on this edition's own showing.**
- **A transliteration corrected.** "موجودہ تحقیق **سجزی (س۔ج۔ز۔ی)** ہے" — current research gives
  **Sijzi**, spelt out letter by letter, against the text's "سنجزی" for Khwaja Muin al-Din's nisba
  (p. 200, حاشیہ 3). A small note, but it shows the editor correcting the text's orthography of names
  as well as its dates.
- **An attribution rejected on identity grounds.** "مولانا عبد الحی نے شیخ بہاؤ الدینؒ کو **خواجہ
  بہاؤ الدین نقشبند بخاری** لکھا ہے (نزہۃ الخواطر ۱۸۸/۳) **جو درست نہیں ہے**" — Maulana Abd al-Hai
  wrote Shaikh Baha al-Din [Kashmiri] as Khwaja Baha al-Din Naqshband Bukhari, **which is not
  correct** (p. 204, حاشیہ 3). The same author of *Nuzhat al-Khawatir* whom chunk 018's footnote
  accused of taking *Khazinat al-Asfiya*'s reports "بغیر کسی تحقیق کے", without investigation.
- **A ruler renamed and redated from modern scholarship.** The conversion story's "نرسنجن دیو" is
  given a correct full name, two reigns with CE dates, a post-1320 conversion, a Muslim name (Sadr
  al-Din) and a converting commander — all from **Muhibb al-Hasan, *Kashmir Salatin ke Ahd mein***,
  in **Ali Jawad Abbasi's translation, Dar al-Musannifin, Azamgarh, 1967** (pp. 202–203). **The
  editor's Kashmir apparatus rests almost entirely on this one 1967 translation and on Muhammad
  Azam's *Tarikh-e Kashmir Azami*.**
- **A saint's order supplied where the text gives none.** Bulbul Shah's Suhrawardi affiliation,
  through Shah Ni'mat Allah Farsi, and his arrival "با ایک ہزار پناہ گزینوں کے ساتھ ترکستان سے", from
  Turkestan with a thousand refugees fleeing the Mongols (p. 203, حاشیہ 3) — a refugee movement of
  stated size behind the Islamisation of Kashmir, and it is the editor's addition, not the author's.

## Doubts

- **The folio offset is now resolved against the printed markers, and three earlier notes files need
  amending.** p. 197 → folio 195 followed immediately by p. 198 → folio 186 is a **backward jump of
  nine in a sequential printed book**, which is impossible; therefore the markers for **pp. 176–197**
  are wrong and the relation **folio = PDF − 12** holds throughout. **The folio values recorded in
  chunks 016 (pp. 176–177), 017 (all) and 018 (all) are ten too high**, and chunk 017's paragraph
  asserting a systematic ten-page pagination jump is mistaken. A human should either re-mark the
  transcription for that stretch or record the correction once at book level.
- **data-darbar: the row's death date is the one this edition rejects, and no replacement is
  offered.** 465 AH / 1072 CE is called "غلط"; 490 AH is where the author is last shown alive, and it
  is **OCR-flagged in both places it appears**; the birth of 400 AH is called indeterminate and is
  also flagged. **The honest entry states the traditional 465 AH, the edition's rejection of it, and
  the 490 AH terminus, and marks all three figures as unresolved.** Note too that the editor does not
  say what his own view is, unlike his practice with Shah Daula and Ayaz.
- **data-darbar: the Muin al-Din arrival date is given twice, differently, by the same editor.**
  "قریباً ۵۸۵ھ" at p. 198 (حاشیہ 1, citing *Tarikh al-Salaf* 7, 9) and "ورود لاہور حدود ۵۸۸" at
  p. 200 (حاشیہ 2). **Three pages apart, in two arguments that both depend on the date.** Neither is
  flagged; the difference is three years and does not affect either conclusion, but it should not be
  quoted as a single figure.
- **data-darbar: one footnote is empty.** "حاشیہ: ۲۔ دارا شکوہ:" (p. 196) — the note attached to the
  Dara Shikoh forty-Thursdays quotation of chunk 018 consists of the name and a colon. **Whatever
  reference or comment stood there is lost to the transcription, and it is the note that would have
  sourced the shrine's best-known votive practice.** A human must re-read this page.
- **shrine-of-miran-hussain-zanjani-zanjani-sahib: the row's date and the row's site come from two
  different men.** The archive's 1042 (≈ 434 AH) fits the *Fawaid al-Fawad* Husain Zanjani whose bier
  Data met; the archive's Chah Miran fits the 600 AH Husain Zanjani of this entry. **The edition says
  in terms that these are "مختلف شخصیت", different persons.** The row therefore needs a decision that
  this book cannot make for it, and the decision is Rauf's: whether the archive's Zanjani Sahib is the
  eleventh-century figure or the thirteenth-century one. **Both readings are documented here; do not
  silently adopt either.**
- **shrine-of-miran-hussain-zanjani-zanjani-sahib: whose tomb "گذر سنجا" is, is unclear.** The quoted
  *Waqai-e Sialkot* passage reads "نواسۂ سید حسین برادر … سید یعقوب صدر شاہ زنجانی کہ **مرقد مقدس
  ایشاں** در لاہور گذر سنجا [OCR?] زیارت گاہ خاص و عام است" — "their holy tomb", with "ایشاں" able to
  refer to Yaqub, to Husain, or to both. The place name is OCR-flagged, as is "قصبہ رودل [OCR?]"
  later in the same sentence, and the Persian syntax as transcribed is broken. **The passage's only
  secure content is that Khizr Khan was alive in 758 AH and was Husain's grandson** — which is all
  the editor draws from it. **Do not take a location for either Zanjani shrine from this quotation.**
- **bibi-pak-daman: this chunk gives the family, and warns that the literature is wrong, but the
  book's own entry on the Bibis is elsewhere.** The editor's "ملاحظہ ہو کتاب ہذا تحت بی بیاں پاکدامن"
  (p. 199) points to a later entry not in this chunk. **Nothing should be written into the row from
  here without that entry**, especially given "ان کی نسبت خاصی غلط بیانی کی گئی ہے" — considerable
  misstatement has been made about them. Note also that this account has **six daughters of whom two
  married**, which an entry must not silently reconcile with any other count.
- **bibi-pak-daman: "محلہ چہل بی بیاں" is not "بی بیاں پاکدامن".** The father's grave is placed in
  the **quarter of the forty bibis** (p. 200); the daughters are cross-referenced under **Bibiyan
  Pakdaman**. **The chunk does not say the two are the same place**, and "chihal" (forty) does not
  agree with six. Leave the relation unresolved.
- **sakhi-sarwar: nothing dates the shrine, only the death.** 577 AH is the year of the murder; the
  roza is placed at Nigaha with no building date, no patron, no urs and no custodian, and the editor
  adds **no footnote whatsoever** to this entry — unusual for a figure of this standing, and worth
  noting as an absence. The village name **"کہر سی کوٹ [OCR?]"** is flagged both times it appears, so
  his birthplace is not recoverable from this chunk.
- **sakhi-sarwar: the Baghdad training is chronologically strained on the chunk's own figures.** He is
  said to have received faiz from **Ghaus al-Azam**, **Shihab al-Din Suhrawardi** and **Maudud
  Chishti** together at Baghdad, and to have died in **577 AH** (pp. 197–198). The chunk gives no
  dates for any of the three masters, and **the editor does not comment** — which, given his treatment
  of Sayyid Yaqub Zanjani's chronology on the next page, is a silence worth recording.
- **Entry no. 136's saint appears with two death years in the author's two books.** Here Sayyid Aziz
  al-Din Makki Lahori enters Lahore in **574 AH** and dies in **612 AH** (p. 201). Chunk 018's
  footnote cited, from *Khazinat al-Asfiya* 2/255, "سید عزیز الدین **کی** لاہوری **ف ۷۹۶ھ [OCR?]**"
  — the same unusual "کی" in the name, with a death of **796 AH** — and used him as an example of an
  eighth-century figure. **A gap of 184 years between the author's two books on the same man, and the
  editor does not remark on it here.** The epithet "شیخ کی [OCR?]" is OCR-flagged in this chunk too.
- **Entry no. 139's saint likewise.** Miran Badshah dies **786 AH** here (p. 203); chunk 018's
  footnote cited "سید اسحاق گازرونی لاہوری **ف ۸۹۰ھ [OCR?]**" from *Khazinat al-Asfiya*. **A
  104-year discrepancy between the author's two books**, and again unremarked here. Both this and the
  preceding item are worth surfacing because **chunk 018's editor used exactly these two entries to
  date *Tuhfat al-Wasilin***: his argument depends on the 796 and 890 figures, and this chunk prints
  612 and 786.
- **Bulbul Shah's mosque has two builders.** The text: the Hazrat built the lofty mosque himself; the
  editor: Rinchan built a mosque near the khanqah for his own prayers (p. 203). These may be two
  mosques or one; **the chunk does not say**, and the editor does not mark a correction.
- **The raja's identity is given three ways on two pages** — "نرسنجن [OCR?] دیو" in the text,
  "رنچن" in the editor's first footnote, and "مہاجن بن مہاجن ڈنگوس گرب [OCR?]" as the "correct and
  full name" in another — with a Muslim name of **Sadr al-Din**. Two of the three are OCR-flagged.
  **Use the editor's dates (Ladakh 1290–1320, Kashmir 1320–1323) and leave the name as a set of
  variants.** Note also that a Kashmir reign ending in **1323 CE** and Bulbul Shah's death in **727
  AH** (the chunk gives no CE equivalent) are not reconciled anywhere in the chunk.
- **Nur al-Din Wali's second set of masters is contradicted by the editor's own footnote.** The text:
