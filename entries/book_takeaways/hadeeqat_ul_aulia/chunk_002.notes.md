# hadeeqat_ul_aulia — chunk 002 — pp. 24–36

Continuation of the editor's *muqaddima*: the author's descent and family, his biography, his other
works, an assessment of Hadiqat al-Auliya with its merits and defects, the list of earlier printed
editions and the editorial method — then the author's own opening verses (hamd, naat, manqabat) begin
at p. 36. Almost all of it concerns the book and its author rather than individual shrines.

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya** (شیخ بہار الدین زکریا ملتانیؒ — printed thus, "Bahar al-Din" for
  Bahauddin):
  - The author's own *shajara-e nasab* is traced to him, and the chain is given in full: Mufti Ghulam
    Sarwar b. Mufti Ghulam Muhammad b. Mufti Rahmat Allah b. Mufti Hafiz Muhammad Taqi b. Mufti
    Muhammad Taqi b. Maulana Kamal al-Din Khurd b. Mufti Abdul Sami b. Maulana Atiq Allah b. Maulana
    Burhan al-Din b. Mufti Muhammad Mahmud b. Shaikh al-Islam Abdul Salam b. Shaikh Inayat Allah b.
    Maulana Kamal al-Din b. Shaikh Makhdum known as Mian Kalan b. Shaikh Qutb al-Din b. Shaikh
    Shihab al-Din Anwar b. Shaikh Bahar al-Din Zakariya Multani (p. 24, folio 12; concluded p. 25,
    folio 13). Sixteen generations as printed, with the editor's footnote 1 disputing one link (see
    Doubts).
  - The editor sources the genealogy to the book itself: "غلام سرور مفتی : حدیقۃ الاولیاء ۱۱۸ — ۱۱۹"
    (p. 25, folio 13, حاشیہ 1).
  - The family descended from him left Multan for Lahore: Makhdum Mufti Shaikh Muhammad Qureshi,
    known as Mian Kalan, was "the first elder of this family of Muftis who came from Multan and
    settled in Lahore" (p. 27, folio 15).
- **shrine-of-mauj-darya-bukhari** (شیخ موج دریا بخاری لاہوری): "بی بی فاطمہ زوجہ شیخ موج دریا
  بخاری لاہوری" — Bibi Fatima, wife of Shaikh Mauj Darya Bukhari of Lahore — is named in the
  editor's list of figures whose accounts are found *only* in Hadiqat al-Auliya, no earlier writer
  having recorded them (p. 32, folio 20).

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **A mosque office held as heritable property.** Mufti Inayat Allah appointed his son Abdul Salam
  his successor in 1014 AH / 1604 CE and made over to him "the imamat, khitabat, *tauliyat* and the
  writing of fatwas" of the ancestral Masjid Muftian (p. 26, folio 14) — custodianship (*tauliyat*)
  transmitted by a living predecessor, in a family that held the post across the generations listed
  above.
- **Land grant and the founding of a Lahore muhalla.** Sultan Bahlul Lodhi sent Mian Kalan from
  Multan to Lahore on the office of *ifta* and gave him the area of Haibatpur as *madad-e maash*
  (subsistence grant) — glossed by the editor as "موجودہ پٹی قبل تقسیم ہند ضلع لاہور، حال ضلع
  امرتسر", present-day Patti, in Lahore district before the partition of India and now in Amritsar
  district. He settled in Muhalla Mullawal Khan Lohani [OCR?] — glossed as "حال گذر حویلی میاں خاں
  اندرون موچی دروازہ لاہور", now Guzar Haveli Mian Khan inside Mochi Darwaza, Lahore — built a haveli
  and founded a muhalla that became known as Kotli Muftian; he died 891 AH (p. 27, folio 15).
- **Destruction of shrines and of a family's institutions under Sikh rule.** In Mufti Rahim Allah's
  time, called "سکھہ گردی کی بدترین مثال" (the worst example of Sikh misrule), the family was twice
  the target of Sikh plunder: the havelis of Kotli Muftian were demolished at Sikh hands, the mosque
  was left desolate, and a library gathered over years was destroyed — yet he stayed on there
  (p. 25, folio 13). The editor adds that the author recorded, as an eyewitness, the dishonouring of
  Punjab's notables by the Sikhs and the destruction of the shaykhs' *maqabir* (tombs) (p. 33,
  folio 21).
- Mufti Rahmat Allah's lifetime is described as one in which Pakistan and India were left prey to
  "مرہٹہ گردی، سکھ ظلم و ستم اور احمد شاہ ابدالی کے حملوں" — Maratha depredation, Sikh oppression and
  the invasions of Ahmad Shah Abdali (p. 26, folio 14).
- **Publishing history of the book**, as institution and economy of the text (p. 35, folio 23):
  1. Lahore 1875; 2. Kanpur 1877; 3. Kanpur 1889; 4. Munshi Nawal Kishore, Lucknow, 1899;
  5. Munshi Nawal Kishore, Kanpur, 1906 — and "یہ متن موخر الذکر نسخہ ۱۹۰۶ء پر مبنی ہے", this text is
  based on that last, 1906 copy, with no alteration to the book's text beyond the correction of
  scribal slips.
- Khazinat al-Asfiya's own print history is given: Lahore 1284 AH, then مطبع ثمر ہند [OCR?] Lucknow
  ۱۳۰۴ء [OCR?], Kanpur 1312 AH, then 1902 CE, at about ۱۱۷۶ [OCR?] pages; and al-Maktaba al-Maarif
  Lahore has begun issuing a translation, of which the first part is printed (p. 30, folio 18).

## Arguments and interpretations

- The editor announces a companion work of his own, "پنجاب کی تاریخ تصوف کے مآخذ" (Sources for the
  history of tasawwuf in Punjab) — a list of the tazkiras, malfuzat and maktubat of Punjab's sufis
  from the beginning to the present, compiled and soon to be published in book form, which will help
  those who wish to understand Hadiqat al-Auliya (dated in the same sentence "سنہ ۱۲۹۲ھ / ۱۸۷۵ء") and
  work further on it (p. 24, folio 12).
- He states that no comprehensive tazkira carrying the circumstances and the *places* of Punjab's
  sufis, and the detail of their labours in preaching, has ever come before his eyes (p. 24,
  folio 12).
- **Scope of the book, as the editor states it**: the accounts of ۲۴۴ [OCR?] sufis of Punjab, divided
  into seven *chaman* — Qadiriyya, Chishtiyya, Naqshbandiyya, Suhrawardiyya, shaykhs of miscellaneous
  silsilas, *majanin wa majazib*, and righteous women (pp. 31–32, folios 19–20); covering Punjab's
  sufis "from Sultan Mahmud Ghaznavi down to 1292 AH / 1875 CE" in plain simple Urdu, but admitting
  some well-known sufis who had no connection with Punjab at all (p. 32, folio 20).
- **Merits the editor claims for the book** (pp. 32–33, folios 20–21): (1) the accounts of some
  Punjab shaykhs survive only here, no earlier author having written them, and later tazkira-writers
  merely copied them from Hadiqat al-Auliya; (2) the Mufti is the first tazkira-writer to take
  special care over recording death years; (3) he preserved the accounts of many *contemporary*
  shaykhs, so that for them this book is the unique source; (4) he recorded some important political
  events inside the shaykhs' biographies, including the Sikh-period destruction he had witnessed
  himself, and took other accounts from reliable informants by word of mouth.
- **Defects the editor lists** under "دوسرا رخ" (pp. 33–34, folios 21–22): the rules of
  tazkira-writing are not all observed; the entries were written from whichever names came to mind
  in one sitting, without much labour; most elders' genealogies of descent and of *tariqa* are not
  correctly transmitted, as the editor found by comparison with contemporary sources; the author gave
  little attention to historical circumstance and spent all his force on recording karamat; he
  included hearsay, and wherever he writes "با قوال صحیحہ" the reader should understand the sayings
  are oral only and exist in no written form; and despite using Akhbar al-Akhyar as a basic source he
  ignored the critical method Shaikh Abdul Haq Muhaddith Dehlvi had brought to tazkira-writing.
- The seventh point is at once praise and warning: the author saw the absence of birth and death
  years as the great defect of earlier tazkiras and tried every possible means to remedy it, but "بے
  شمار سنین قیاسی اور غلط ہو کر رہ گئے ہیں" — countless of the years remained conjectural and wrong
  — and those whose death years he could not find he placed in a separate chapter (p. 34, folio 22).
  **This is the editor's own instruction for how to use every date in the book.**
- **The karamat controversy.** A long footnote quotes Professor Muhammad Habib against Khazinat
  al-Asfiya: its great defect was that the author, leaning on belief, entirely set aside the
  principles of *isnad* that the ulama had held for centuries to be the soul of learning, and he gave
  details of karamat so dreadful "that human reason feels ashamed on reading them" (cited to
  تاریخ مشائخ چشت، مقدمہ ص ۲۰ - ۲۱). The editor answers that Habib ignores that karamat were in that
  era an inseparable part of the genre and that the people themselves demanded their manifestation
  and their recording; that the sufis of Pakistan and India had to contend with "یہاں کے ہند و
  جوگیوں اور شعبدہ بازوں", the Hindu jogis and conjurers, in the course of preaching Islam, and if
  they did not display karamat against them, what were they to do; that before the Mufti there is no
  tazkira of Pakistan and India in which karamat are not recorded; and that a Muslim shying so much
  from the very name of karamat "معنی دارد!" (pp. 33–34, folios 21–22, حاشیہ).
- **Editorial method for the footnotes** (p. 35, folio 23): the author's oversights (*tasamuhat*) are
  pointed out; an important aspect of a person's life missing from the text is indicated; and further
  possible sources for the persons are indicated, with contemporary and single-subject (*mufrad*)
  sources preferred and later sources set aside. This is the warrant for treating the حاشیہ material
  as a second, later voice in the book.
- The editor's sources for his account of the author are declared: Storey, *Persian Literature*
  Vol I Part II pp 1044 [OCR?], London 1953; Mahmud Alam, *Zikr-e-Jamil*, Lahore 1968, pp. 111–131
  [OCR?]; and the introductions to Diwan-e-Wisal-e-Sarwar and Kulliyat-e-Sarwar (p. 31, folio 19).
  A separate footnote claims that no other tazkira of the asfiya of Pakistan and India has had the
  fame of Khazinat al-Asfiya, that any work on sufis done anywhere after 1281 AH has it as a source,
  and that Storey (vol. 1 part 2, pp. ۹۲۳ - ۱۳۴۷ [OCR?]) cites it frequently (p. 30, folio 18).
- The author's twenty other works are listed with dates of composition and printing (pp. 30–31,
  folios 18–19). Hadiqat al-Auliya is no. 10, dated 1292 AH; the other tazkira-like items are
  Guldasta-e-Karamat (۱۲۷۷ھ [OCR?]), Khazinat al-Asfiya (1280–1281 AH), Ganjina-e-Sarwari alias
  Ganj-e-Sarwari (1284 AH, a versified list of death years), Tarikh-e-Makhzan-e-Punjab (1285 AH, a
  general history of Punjab in Urdu, Lahore 1285 AH and Lucknow 1877 and 1889), Madinat al-Auliya
  (two volumes, Lucknow, largely the same biographies as Khazinat al-Asfiya), Makhzan-e-Hikmat
  (1288 AH, again 1295 AH) and Aqwal al-Akhirat, in Punjabi verse.
- The author's biography as the editor tells it (pp. 27–29, folios 15–17): third son of Mufti Ghulam
  Muhammad; born ۱۲۴۴ھ [OCR?] / ۱۸۳۷ء [OCR?] in the ancestral Muhalla Kotli Muftian, Lahore; taught
  by his father, including medicine, and **given bai'at by him in the Suhrawardi silsila**; then
  completed tafsir, hadith, fiqh, adab, grammar, rhetoric, logic and history in the teaching circle
  of Maulana Ghulam Allah Lahori; called the unequalled scholar, litterateur, poet, chronogrammatist,
  historian and world-renowned tazkira-writer of his time. He died on Hajj: setting out in June 1890
  with his nephew, leaving for Madina on بیس [OCR?] Zi al-Hijja, cholera [ہیضہ وبائے OCR?] broke out
  among the travellers at the third stage, he fell ill at the fifth, and he died near the seventh
  stage on Thursday 24 Zi al-Hijja 1307 AH / 14 August 1890 CE, buried at the stage
  بیر بالاحسانی [OCR?] in the environs of Badr (p. 28, folio 16).
- **The author's refusal of colonial patronage** is the editor's central characterisation of him: he
  wanted neither title nor jagir, would not write under government influence, refused an honorary
  Fellowship of Punjab University despite Leitner's insistence, and made no literary or political
  alliance with the government to the end of his life [تا دم زیست OCR?] (pp. 28–29, folios 16–17).
  His era was one in which the English had taken the sultanate of Hindustan from the Muslims and
  Muslim civilisation and the deeds of Muslim notables were being dismissed as worn-out tales; in
  that setting his pen tried, as a teacher of morals in the guise of a tazkira-writer, to steady a
  falling nation (p. 29, folio 17).

## Doubts

- **Contradiction on the base edition.** The preface in the previous chunk gives the source text as
  "مطبوعہ لکھنؤ (۱۹۰۶ء)", printed at **Lucknow** 1906 (p. 14, folio 2), while this chunk's list of
  editions gives no. 4 as Munshi Nawal Kishore **Lucknow 1899** and no. 5 as Munshi Nawal Kishore
  **Kanpur 1906**, and says the text is based on that last one (p. 35, folio 23). The place of the
  1906 printing is therefore stated two ways inside the same book's apparatus.
- **Contradiction on the number of entries.** The editor states the book holds ۲۴۴ [OCR?] sufis of
  Punjab (p. 31, folio 19), but the table of contents numbers its biographical entries to 247
  (p. 21, folio 9, in the previous chunk). The figure here also carries an `[OCR?]` flag, so it may
  be a transcription artefact rather than a real discrepancy — but it must be resolved by counting
  before either number is cited.
- **Dates of the two prefatory pieces.** The *muqaddima* is signed 12 December 1974 (p. 35, folio 23)
  and the *pesh lafz* 6 November 1999 (p. 14, folio 2, previous chunk). The introduction is therefore
  the 1974 original carried unchanged into the 2000 edition; a claim made in the introduction (for
  instance that the companion volume of sources will "soon" appear, p. 24, folio 12) is a 1974 claim
  and must not be read as a statement of 1999.
- **Genealogy disputed inside the book.** Footnote 1 records that Mufti Mahmud Alam made Khwaja
  Muhammad Ayub Lahori a son of Mufti Muhammad Taqi and put him in the family tree, "جو درست نہیں"
  (which is not correct), since Ghulam Sarwar himself called him Muhammad Taqi's pupil and son-in-law
  (خزینہ ۲/۳۷۰); and that in Ghulam Sarwar's own account the name of Mufti Muhammad Taqi is missing
  after Mufti Rahim Allah (p. 24, folio 12, حاشیہ 1). Footnote 5 likewise rejects Mahmud Alam's
  naming of Mufti Abdul Salam's father as Qazi Mufti Muhammad Tahir in favour of Shaikh Mufti Inayat
  Allah (p. 26, folio 14). The chain to Bahauddin Zakariya is thus contested in detail even where the
  editor accepts it as a whole.
- **Two successive Muhammad Taqis.** The genealogy prints "مفتی حافظ محمد تقی بن ۱ مفتی محمد تقی"
  (p. 24, folio 12), and the separate paragraph on حافظ محمد تقی says he was the son of
  "حافظ مفتی محمد تقی [OCR?]" (p. 26, folio 14) — i.e. as transcribed, of a man of the same name. One
  of the two is likely an OCR duplication; the death years (1146 AH [OCR?] and 1131 AH) are all that
  separate them.
- `[OCR?]` flags carried over that touch facts above: the author's birth "۱۲۴۴ھ [OCR?] / ۱۸۳۷ء
  [OCR?]"; "بیس [OCR?] ذی الحجہ" and the burial place "بیر بالاحسانی [OCR?]" (p. 28, folio 16);
  "۱۱۴۶ھ [OCR?]" for Hafiz Muhammad Taqi (p. 26, folio 14); the entry count "۲۴۴ [OCR?]" (p. 31,
  folio 19); "فتح گڑھ چونیاں [OCR?]" (p. 27, folio 15); "محلہ ملاول خاں لوہانی [OCR?]" (p. 27,
  folio 15); Guldasta-e-Karamat's date "۱۲۷۷ھ [OCR?]" and its original author "شیخ محمد صادق شہبانی
