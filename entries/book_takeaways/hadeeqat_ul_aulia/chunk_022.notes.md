# hadeeqat_ul_aulia — chunk 022 — pp. 225–235

**Entries 156 (tail) to 170 — the book's last and most recent entries, running from Shah Abd al-Aziz
Dehlvi to men the author knew personally, under Durrani, Sikh and British rule.** Three archive rows
take material: **bibi-pak-daman**, where **the author's own father was buried in 1274 AH**;
**tahir-bandagi-qadri**, inside whose enclosure wall a saint was buried in 1264 AH; and
**shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya**, from whom the author claims descent in seventeen steps. The three
most substantial features: **the author writes his own family into the book by name, generation and
address** ("کوچہ کوٹلی مفتیاں … جس میں اب بھی مؤلف کتاب قیام پذیر ہے"); **a saint cures the author's
own two sons of a two-year eye disease**, which dates the composition against a death in 1290 AH; and
**three successive regimes appear as the settings of shrine business** — Ahmad Shah Durrani's court at
Kabul, Mahan Singh and Jai Singh Kanhiya in the Sikh misls, and "عملداری انگریزوں کی", the rule of the
English, in a Lahore courtroom.

**Folio markers: all eleven run folio = PDF index − 12**, p. 225 → folio 213 through p. 235 →
folio 223. The relation holds throughout chunks 020–022.

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **bibi-pak-daman** (گورستان بی بی پاکدامنان — within entry no. 166, p. 233, folio 221):
  - **The author's own father is buried in the Bibi Pak Daman graveyard, and the year is given.** Of
    **Mufti Ghulam Muhammad bin Mufti Rahim Allah Qureshi Lahori**, "یہ بزرگ احقر غلام سرور مؤلف کتاب
    کے **والد ماجد** تھے" — this elder was the honoured father of the most humble Ghulam Sarwar, the
    author of the book: "وفات حضرت کی سال **ایک ہزار دو سو چہتر** میں واقع ہوئی" — he died in
    **1274 AH** (p. 232, folio 220) — "اور **مدفن گورستان بی بی پاکدامنان** کی مادہ تاریخ ہے" — and
    the burial place is the **graveyard of Bibi Pakdamanan** (p. 233, folio 221).
  - **What the row gains**: a **dated burial (1274 AH / c. 1857–58 CE)** in the Bibi Pak Daman
    graveyard, of a named Lahore mufti and physician, recorded by his own son. **With chunk 017's
    subsidiary rauza of 1016 AH inside the enclosure, this row now has two dated burials in its
    precinct from this book, 258 years apart** — direct evidence that the site functioned as a
    working burial ground across that span.
  - **The wording of the second clause is broken**: "اور مدفن گورستان بی بی پاکدامنان **کی مادہ تاریخ
    ہے**", which reads as though the chronogram and the burial place have been run together — the
    preceding clause gives the chronogram as "خورشید دین محمد". **The burial place is unambiguous;
    the syntax is not.** See Doubts.
  - The chunk says nothing else about the shrine — no urs, no custodian, no fabric, and no reference
    to the Bibis themselves. **The book's own entry on the Bibis, which chunk 019's editor
    cross-referenced and warned about, is not in these chunks.**
- **tahir-bandagi-qadri** (شیخ طاہر لاہوری — within entry no. 163, p. 231, folio 219):
  - **A saint buried inside his walled enclosure, dated.** Of Sayyid Munawwar Ali Shah Naqshbandi
    Suhrawardi Lahori: "وفات حضرت کی **ایک ہزار دو سو چونسٹھ** ہجری میں واقع ہوئی اور **مرقد چار
    دیواری شیخ طاہر لاہوری کے اندر ہے**" — he died in **1264 AH** and **his tomb is inside the
    chahar-diwari (enclosure wall) of Shaikh Tahir Lahori** (pp. 230–231, folios 218–219).
  - **What the row gains**: the shrine had, by the mid-thirteenth Hijri century, **a walled enclosure
    (چار دیواری) used for the burial of other saints** — an architectural and functional fact the
    archive row does not carry — plus a dated occupant of it.
  - **The identification rests on the name and the city.** The book elsewhere calls him "حضرت شیخ
    محمد طاہر بندگی لاہوری (ف ۱۰۴۰ھ)" (chunk 012, p. 129), whose death year matches the archive row
    exactly. Here he is only "شیخ طاہر لاہوری". **Confident but see Doubts.**
- **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya** (شیخ بہاء الدین زکریا ملتانی — within entry no. 166, p. 232,
  folio 220):
  - **The author claims descent from him in seventeen steps, and names the ancestor who moved the
    family to Lahore.** "نسبت آبائی ان کی **حضرت شیخ بہاء الدین زکریا ملتانی** کے ساتھ ملتی ہے۔ اس
    طرح پر کہ مفتی غلام محمد بن مفتی رحیم اللہ بن مفتی رحمت اللہ بن مفتی حافظ محمد تقی بن مفتی محمد
    نقی بن مولانا کمال الدین بن مفتی عبد السمیع بن مولانا عتیق اللہ بن مولانا برہان الدین بن مفتی
    محمد محمود بن شیخ الاسلام عبد السلام بن شیخ عنایت اللہ بن مولانا کمال الدین بن **شیخ مخدوم
    المشہور میاں کلاں** … بن شیخ قطب الدین بن شیخ شہاب الدین بن **شیخ بہاء الدین زکریا ملتانی**"
    (p. 232, folio 220). **This is a `nasab` claim, not a silsila** — chunks 017, 018 and 020 gave
    Baha al-Din Zakariya as the terminus of three *spiritual* chains; here he is claimed as a direct
    ancestor.
  - **The migration that founded the family's Lahore presence**: of Mian Kalan, "**جو شہر ملتان سے
    حسب الطلب بادشاہ وقت کے لاہور میں آئے۔ افتار [OCR?] پر ممتاز ہوئے**" — who came from the city of
    **Multan to Lahore at the summons of the king of the time**, and was distinguished in the giving
    of fatwas (p. 232, folio 220). **A Multan-to-Lahore transfer of a Suhrawardi descent line, by
    royal summons** — and the family's order is stated on the same page: "**ذکر و شغل ان کا طریق
    آبائی سہروردیہ پر تھا**", their dhikr and practice was on the ancestral Suhrawardi way.
  - **What the row gains**: the standing of Baha al-Din Zakariya as the claimed lineal ancestor of a
    nineteenth-century Lahore family of muftis, one of whose members wrote this book. **Nothing about
    Multan or his own shrine.** See Doubts on the Mian Kalan identification.

- **Maulana Abd al-Qadir bin Wali Allah Muhaddith Dehlvi** (entry no. 158, p. 227, folio 215): "کمال
  فصاحت و بلاغت **ترجمہ تفسیر فتح الرحمان ہندی زبان میں** کیا کہ مقبول و مطبوع خاص و عام ہے۔ **کسی
  کو اس پر جائے اعتراض نہیں**" — with utmost eloquence he translated the *Tafsir Fath al-Rahman* into

  Qadri, *Majmua-e Wasaya Arba'a* 26) (p. 227, folio 215, حاشیہ 1). **The second attribution in two
  chunks that the editor takes off a Delhi scholar and puts on the Ahl-e Hadith**, after Shah Wali
  Allah (chunk 021, p. 217).

  چند پشت سے ان کا قیام لاہور میں تھا"** — for several generations his family had lived in Lahore.

  **Kotla** — against the chronogram's 1244[OCR?]: see Doubts. **Five sons**: **Miran Sayyid Ghulam

- **Sayyid Ali Akbar** (within entry no. 159, pp. 227–228, folios 215–216): Ghulam Muhyi al-Din's

  بیٹا **سید علی اصغر** اب تک وہاں موجود ہے" — his son Sayyid Ali Asghar is there **to this day**.
- **Maulvi Ghulam Rasul Fazil Lahori** (entry no. 160, p. 229, folio 217): "پنجاب میں کوئی شخص ان کے
  وقت میں علمائے وقت سے فیض رسانی میں ان کے ہمتا نہ تھا." **His teaching timetable is under

  حاشیہ 1). **The only mention of 1857 in these chunks, and it is the editor's.**

  tense: world-renouncing, a hafiz of the Quran, who "شہر **امرت سر** میں بمقام **باغ رامانند** تمام
  زمانہ سے الگ ایک حجرہ میں **تنہا رہتے ہیں**" — lives **alone in a hujra at Bagh Ramanand in the
  city of Amritsar**, apart from all the world; **"مؤلف کتاب بھی ان کی زیارت سے بہرہ مند ہوا ہے"** —
  the author of the book **has himself had the benefit of visiting him**; "بے شک بہت خوب آدمی خدا
  پرست ہے۔ حلیم و خلیق و بزرگ ہیں **اور ایسے زمانہ میں کہ مردان خدا عنقا ہو گئے ہیں**", and in such
  a time as this, when men of God have become as rare as the phoenix, his person is among the
  treasures of the age. **A Muslim ascetic living in a garden bearing a Hindu saint's name, in
  Amritsar, visited by the author.**
- **Sayyid Ghulam Ghaus** (entry no. 169, pp. 234–235, folios 222–223): the **eldest son** of Sayyid
  Ghulam Muhyi al-Din Gilani. "اعتقاد مند لوگوں کا **ہجوم ہمیشہ حضرت کے دروازے پر** رہتا۔ تمام زمانہ
  با ادب پیش آتا۔ **بڑے بڑے رئیس اور حکام وقت حضرت کے ارشاد کی تعمیل بدل و جان کرتے تھے**" — great
  ra'ises and **the officials of the time** carried out his instruction with heart and soul. Died
  **1279 AH**. **His descendants and the named sajjada nashin are under Practices.**
- **Sain Qutb Shah Lahori** (entry no. 170, p. 235, folio 223): murid of **Shaikh Ghulam Husain**;
  **"حصول قوت حلال کے واسطے جولاہوں کا کام کرتا تھا"** — for lawful sustenance he did **weavers'
  work**; "اگرچہ فقیر تھا مگر ہرگز طمع نہ رکھتا"; "کم بولتا اور کم کھاتا اور لوگوں سے کم ملتا۔ زبان
  سے جو کہتا۔ اکثر اوقات وقوع میں آ جاتا". **The healing of the author's sons is under Legends.** Died
  **1290 AH**; buried at **mauza Khoi Miran**. **This is the latest death year in these chunks and the
  book's last entry in this range.**
- **Rulers, officials and chiefs named**: **Ahmad Shah Durrani** (Kabul), **Raja Ranjit Deo of

  کی") with an unnamed magistrate (pp. 227–234).
- **Places named**: **Lahore** (Kotli Muftiyan, Mohalla Alaul Khan Kohani[OCR?], Shaikh Tahir's

  **Gujranwala**; **Bhairyan[OCR?]**; **Kotla**; **Rasulnagar**; **Mandranwala**; **Har Do

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **A craftsman-saint with a fixed charity rate and interest-free lending.** Of Ladhe Shah:
  **"حصول قوت حلال کے واسطے گھوڑے کے بالوں کی چھلنیاں بناتا تھا۔ اس میں سے جو حاصل ہوتا نصف خدا کے
  نام خیرات کر دیتا"** — for lawful sustenance he made **sieves from horsehair**, and of what he
  earned from it he gave **half in charity in God's name**; and **"اکثر لوگ اس سے روپیہ قرض لے جاتے
  اگر وہ شخص از خود روپیہ لے کر آتا تو بعد انکار لے لیتا۔ ورنہ اس سے کبھی طلب نہ کرتا"** — many
  people would take money from him **as a loan**; if a man brought the money back himself he would
  take it **after first refusing**; **otherwise he never asked him for it** (p. 229, folio 217). **A
  named trade, a stated proportion given away, and a lending practice with no recovery — the most
  exact small-scale shrine economy in these chunks**, and a deliberate contrast with the jagirs and
  grants of the earlier entries. Compare **Sain Qutb Shah's weaving** (p. 235) and **Hassu Teli's
  grain-selling** (chunk 017): three Lahore saints supported by their own trades.
- **A teaching day set out hour by hour.** Of Maulvi Ghulam Rasul: **four gharis before dawn**, after
  finishing the Tahajjud prayer, his **special students** came in, and their lesson ended before the
  Fajr prayer; then **"ایک انبوہ و ہجوم شاگردوں کا جمع ہوتا۔ اس قدر کہ مسجد میں بیٹھنے کو جگہ نہ
  رہتی"**, such a throng of students gathered that **there was no room to sit in the mosque**, and he
  taught each with the utmost affection; this lasted **till noon**; then food and a nap of about an
  hour; meanwhile the students **whose appointed time was after the Zuhr prayer** assembled, and he
  rose, prayed Zuhr and resumed; and so "**پہر رات گئے** ہنگامہ تعلیم و تدریس جاری رہتا", the teaching
  continued until a *pahar* of the night had passed (p. 229, folio 217). **A full timetable for a
  Lahore mosque-madrasa, in three shifts** — the most detailed account of shrine-attached education in
  these chunks.
- **A teaching establishment inherited by a brother, and the author's place in its chain.** "بعد وفات
  مولوی غلام رسول ان کے بھائی کے **علاقہ تدریس ان کے متعلق ہوا**" (p. 231, folio 219); and the heirs:
  **"ان کی اولاد میں خلیفہ نظام الدین بمبئی میں درس پڑھاتے ہیں اور خلیفہ احمد الدین و حمید الدین
  لاہور میں کار تدریس میں مصروف ہیں"** — among his descendants **Khalifa Nizam al-Din teaches in
  Bombay**, and **Khalifa Ahmad al-Din and Hamid al-Din are engaged in teaching in Lahore**; then
  **"احقر المحقر غلام سرور مؤلف کتاب بھی اسی خاندان کے کمترین شاگردوں میں ہے"** — the most humble
  Ghulam Sarwar, author of the book, **is himself among the least of this family's students** (p. 232,
  folio 220). **A Lahore teaching lineage with a branch in Bombay, and the author declaring his own
  pupilage in it.**
- **Weekly Friday preaching, counted by the day.** "**آٹھویں دن جمعہ کے روز** ہنگامہ وعظ و نصیحت گرم
  ہوتا" (p. 231, folio 219) — Maulana Jan Muhammad's public sermon every eighth day, on Friday. Read
  with Data Darbar's Thursday vigil and Friday milad (chunk 018), the book's Lahore practice is
  weekly rather than annual throughout.
- **A named living sajjada nashin, and a family in colonial service.** Of Sayyid Ghulam Ghaus's
  descendants: **"سید محمد علی شاہ سررشتہ دار محکمہ رکھ پنجاب"** — Sayyid Muhammad Ali Shah,
  **sarrishtadar of the Punjab Rakh (preserve/forest) Department**; his brother **Sayyid Ali al-Haq
  Shah**, "جن سے مؤلف کتاب کو بھی نیاز حاصل ہے"; and the second son **"سید گنج بخش سجادہ نشین سید
  غلام"** — **Sayyid Ganj Bakhsh, sajjada nashin of Sayyid Ghulam** — whose three sons are **Sharafat
  Hasan, Sharafat Husain and Aziz al-Hasnain** (p. 235, folio 223). **One branch holds the sajjada,
  another holds a government office: the clearest picture in these chunks of a shrine family under
  colonial administration**, and four generations named.
- **A shrine's cases brought to three successive regimes.** Under the **Durranis**, a scholar-physician
  is taken to Kabul "بہزار التجا" and rewarded with the title **Mirza Bahadur** and wealth (pp. 227–228);
  under the **Sikh misls**, **Mahan Singh** of Gujranwala presses the saint to reside in his seat of
  rule and is refused, and **Sardar Jai Singh Kanhiya** summons him on arrival in Lahore (p. 228);
  under the **English**, a theft case is decided in a Lahore court (p. 231). **For the archive: the
  same family's standing is negotiated with an Afghan king, a Sikh sardar and a British magistrate in
  three consecutive generations, and the book records all three without comment on the change of
  regime.**
- **A marriage arranged by three named local notables, fixing where a saint would live.** "حسب
  التجائے **چودھری سہجی خاں [OCR?] مالک وسوہہ [OCR?]** و **نور دین خاں رئیس میانی** و **لبر بائش
  [OCR?] رئیس غلنیان [OCR?]** شادی حضرت کی **بمقام کوٹلہ خاندان سید معروف سبزواری** میں ہو گئی اور
  مدت العمر وہاں ہی سکونت رکھی" — at the request of these three, his marriage took place at **Kotla,
  into the family of Sayyid Maruf Sabzwari**, and he lived there the rest of his life (p. 228,
  folio 216). **A shrine sited by a marriage brokered by local landholders to keep the saint from
  returning to Lahore** — "وہاں کے اعتقاد مند لوگوں نے ان کو نہ چھوڑا کہ یہ پھر لاہور میں آویں", the
  devout of that place would not let him go back.
- **A cow-slaughter prosecution under Dogra rule, and a courtier's refusal.** A Muslim was arrested
  "بر تہمت **گاؤ کشی**" in the **Kashmir court**; his brother came to Ahmad Shah Kashmiri for prayers;
  **Mian Lal Din, "ایک مصاحب مہاراجہ جموں کا"**, a courtier of the Maharaja of Jammu, was present, and
  the Hazrat told him to work on the case; **"اس نے ہاتھ باندھ کر عرض کی کہ ہندوؤں کی عملداری میں کس
  کی طاقت ہے کہ گاؤ کشی کے بارے میں عرض کرے"** — he folded his hands and said, **under Hindu rule, who
  has the power to plead in a matter of cow-slaughter?** (pp. 233–234, folios 221–222). **The book's
  most direct statement about religious law under a non-Muslim government, put in the mouth of a court
  official** — and the shrine's response is to take the case elsewhere: see Legends.
- **A physician's practice run alongside a teaching one.** The author's father "تمام روز تدریس و
  تعلیم و **معالجہ مریضان** میں مصروف رہتے" (p. 232, folio 220); his great-uncle Sayyid Ali Akbar was
  a "طبیب حاذق" (p. 227). **Two generations of Lahore shrine-families practising medicine**, and see
  Legends for the saint who told the author to stop treating his sons medically.
- **The author's address, and how the family came to hold it.** Mian Kalan, on arriving from Multan,
  **"محلہ علاول خاں کوہانی [OCR?] میں بہت سے مکان زر خرید کیے کہ اب تک وہ کوچہ کوٹلی مفتیاں کہلاتا
  ہے۔ جس میں اب بھی مؤلف کتاب قیام پذیر ہے"** — bought **many houses for cash** in Mohalla Alaul Khan
  Kohani[OCR?], **so that the lane is to this day called Kotli Muftiyan, in which the author of the
  book still resides** (p. 232, folio 220). **With chunk 020's "Masjid Muftiyan", the book's own
  institutional base in Lahore is now named twice: a family mosque and a family lane, both called
  after the muftis.** A purchase, not a grant — worth noting against the jagirs elsewhere.
- **Fifty years of seclusion at a named village as a form of shrine foundation** — Shah Sahib at Har
  Do Thala[OCR?], Hoshiarpur, buried where he lived (p. 234, folio 222); and **a hujra in a garden at
  Amritsar** as a living one (same page).
- **A saint's tomb built inside another saint's enclosure wall** — Munawwar Ali Shah inside Shaikh
  Tahir Lahori's chahar-diwari, 1264 AH (p. 231, folio 219). The fifth instance in these chunks of a
  grave sited deliberately beside or within an earlier saint's precinct.

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **A name written on a forehead wins an acquittal in a British court.** **"لاہور میں جب عملداری
  انگریزوں کی ہوئی تو"** — when the rule of the English came to Lahore — a man named **Ghulam Qadir**
  was arrested on a charge of theft, and some witnesses of his own **بادری [OCR?]** (community?), who
  were his mortal enemies, were bent on getting him jailed. On the day of the hearing he came to
  Maulana Jan Muhammad, who **"اپنی انگلی سے اس کی پیشانی پر اسم ذات لکھ دیا اور فرمایا کہ جاؤ تم کو
  کچھ اندیشہ نہیں"** — wrote the **ism-e zat** on his forehead **with his finger** and said, go, you
  have nothing to fear; "جب وہ عدالت میں گیا **حاکم نے فی الفور جرم سے رہا کیا**", and when he went
  into court the magistrate released him from the charge at once (p. 231, folio 219). **A shrine
  intervening in a colonial criminal trial, with the technique described.**
- **A case taken from a Dogra court to "the court of the Most Just of Judges".** When Mian Lal Din
  pleaded that nothing could be done about a cow-slaughter charge under Hindu rule, "یہ سن کر [OCR?]
  حضرت **متبسم** ہوئے اور فرمایا کہ **ہم احکم الحاکمین کے دربار میں اس کی رہائی کے لیے عرض کریں گے**۔
  چنانچہ وہ ماخوذ **اسی روز** مواخذے سے بری ہو گیا" — hearing this the Hazrat **smiled** and said, we
  shall petition for his release in the **court of the Most Just of Judges**; and the accused was
  acquitted **that same day** (pp. 233–234, folios 221–222). **The karamat is constructed as a
  jurisdictional move — an appeal from a human court to a divine one — and the phrasing mirrors the
  mujawir's answer to the subahdar in chunk 012.**
- **The author's own two sons cured of a two-year eye disease, by his own account.** "**مؤلف کتاب کے
  دو فرزند غلام صفدر و اکبر** آنکھوں کے عارضہ سے سخت بیمار ہو گئے اور بیماری **دو سال** تک درازی
  کھینچ گئی۔ آخر ایک دوست کے کہنے سے **بندہ** ایک آثار [OCR?] **قند سیاہ** اور ایک پاؤ **تیل** لے کر
  حاضر ہوا" — the author's two sons Ghulam Safdar and Akbar fell gravely ill with an eye complaint
  that dragged on **two years**; at last, at a friend's word, **this servant** came with a measure of
  **black sugar** and a quarter-seer of **oil**. Sain Qutb Shah lamented at their state, **"دست
  مبارک ان کے منہ پر پھیر کر کہا کہ اب طبیب کا علاج نہ کرنا"** — passed his blessed hand over their
  faces and said **now do not take a physician's treatment**; the next day and the next night much
  matter ran from the boys' eyes, and on the third morning they woke "بالکل تندرست … گویا کبھی ان کو
  آنکھوں کا عارضہ نہ تھا", completely well, as though they had never had the ailment (p. 235,
  folio 223). **The author's own first-person testimony, naming his sons, the offering he brought and
  the instruction to stop medical treatment — in a family of physicians.** It is also a dating
  anchor: see Arguments.
- **A dead man named as a debating champion.** In the Kabul disputation, when the Shia ulama were at a
  loss "تو یہ کہتے کہ **آرم ملا رفیع را کہ با سید علی اکبر مباحثہ نماید**" — they would say, bring
  Mulla Rafi to debate Sayyid Ali Akbar — **"اور ملا رفیع ایک عالم جید ایرانی تھا جو اسی زمانہ میں مر
  چکا تھا"**, and Mulla Rafi was a great Iranian scholar **who had already died in that same period**
  (p. 228, folio 216). **A mockery rather than a miracle: the opponents' last resort is a man who
  cannot come.**
- **A prayer life recorded as evidence.** The author's father missed **no prayer in his whole life but
  three**, and died with his lips moving in dhikr and his fingers telling a rosary that was not there
  (p. 232, folio 220) — the book's closest approach to a miracle in its own family, and it is offered
  as biography.

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The author signs the book three times in eleven pages, with his family and his address.** "احقر
  المحقر **غلام سرور مؤلف کتاب**" (p. 232, folio 220); "یہ بزرگ **احقر غلام سرور مؤلف کتاب کے والد
  ماجد** تھے" (p. 232); "**یہ گنہگار عاصی شرمسار غلام سرور**" with his five sons and a daughter named
  (p. 233, folio 221); and "**مؤلف کتاب بھی ان کی زیارت سے بہرہ مند ہوا ہے**" of a living saint at
  Amritsar (p. 234, folio 222). **With chunk 020's pedigree and chunk 021's entry on his grandfather,
  the book's authorship, family, order (hereditary Suhrawardi), teachers, lane and house are all
  stated from inside the text.** Any archive citation of *Hadiqat al-Awliya* can now name the author
  and say where he stood: **a Lahore mufti of Kotli Muftiyan, of a Qureshi family claiming descent
  from Baha al-Din Zakariya, trained in the Ghulam Rasul–Ghulam Allah teaching line, writing about
  saints several of whom he had met.**
- **The composition is datable from entry no. 170, and the three dating clues now agree.** Sain Qutb
  Shah died **1290 AH** and the healing happened before that; entry no. 166 records a sister's death
  in **1288 AH** and a brother's in a flagged "ایک ہزار نوے [OCR?]" (almost certainly 1290); chunk 018
  gives "827 years" since a death in 465 AH, i.e. **1292 AH**; and chunk 021's "who has died in this
  year" attaches to an entry ending in 1236 AH. **Taken together, the latest dated deaths in the book
  fall in 1288–1290 AH and the internal arithmetic gives 1292 AH — a consistent composition date in
  the early 1290s AH, i.e. the mid-1870s CE.** The editor's own "سوا نو سو سال" (chunk 019) then
  refers to his own 1974 present. **None of this is stated as a year anywhere; it is arithmetic on
  the book's own figures and should be labelled as such.**
- **An attribution taken off a Delhi scholar and given to a modern school.** Of *Mauzih al-Quran*:
  **"موضح القرآن کے نام سے جو تفسیر آپ کی طرف منسوب کی گئی ہے، وہ علمائے اہل حدیث کا کارنامہ ہے"** —
  the tafsir attributed to Abd al-Qadir Dehlvi under that name is **the achievement of the Ahl-e
  Hadith ulama** (p. 227, folio 215, حاشیہ 1). The editor's second such note after Shah Wali Allah's
  (chunk 021, p. 217: "اہل حدیث علماء نے بھی چند کتابیں آپ کی طرف منسوب کر دی ہیں"). **A consistent
  editorial position: the Waliullahi corpus has had works added to it by a later movement.** Note that
  he keeps the genuine work, *Mauzih-e Quran*, and rejects only the near-identical title — a
  distinction an archive citation must preserve.
- **A shrine family's religious authority tracked into colonial politics.** The editor's note on
  Muhammad Ishaq Dehlvi makes him **Shah Abd al-Aziz's janishin** and successor in "مسلمانان ہند و
  پاکستان کی **مذہبی قیادت**", the religious leadership of the Muslims of India and Pakistan, records
  his **emigration to the Hijaz in 1257 AH / 1841 CE[OCR?]** because "ہندوستان کے حالات موافق نظر نہ
  آئے", and then names **five of his students who took part in the 1857 war of independence as
  ulama** (p. 230, folio 218, حاشیہ 1). **The only place in these chunks where the edition connects a
  succession to an anti-colonial movement**, and it does so by listing named students with their
  offices.
- **A chain and a practice deliberately distinguished.** Of Munawwar Ali Shah: his eight-step bai'at
  chain is **Suhrawardi through Kashmir**, and yet "رغبت حضرت کی **سلسلہ نقشبندیہ** کی طرف زیادہ تھی
  اور مریدوں کو بھی ذکر و شغل **بطریق نقشبندیہ** عالیہ فرماتے" (pp. 230–231). **The book states that
  a saint's inherited silsila and his taught method were different orders** — a distinction the
  archive's single `silsila` column cannot express, and this is the clearest example of it in these
  chunks. The entry's own heading carries both: "نقشبندی سہروردی لاہوری".
- **A family failure recorded without mitigation.** "افسوس ہے کہ ان کی اولاد میں سے کوئی لائق پیدا نہ
  ہوا" (p. 231, folio 219). **A tazkira author writing that a saint's descendants were unworthy is
  rare**, and it sits two entries from his own family's entry. It calibrates the book: the praise
  elsewhere is not indiscriminate.
- **The author's own pedigree, now in its fullest and third version.** Entry no. 166 gives "مفتی حافظ
  محمد تقی بن **مفتی محمد نقی** بن مولانا کمال الدین" (p. 232). **This resolves the discrepancy
  flagged in chunk 021**: chunk 020's editor gave "مفتی محمد تقی ف ۱۱۳۱ھ بن مفتی کمال الدین",
  omitting Naqi; chunk 021's editor gave "رحمت اللہ بن مفتی محمد نقی [OCR?] بن مفتی محمد تقی [OCR?]",
  reversing the two. **The author's own order — Rahmat Allah ← Muhammad Taqi ← Muhammad Naqi ← Kamal
  al-Din — is the fullest and is unflagged here**, and the editor's two versions are both flagged or
  short. **Prefer the author's on this one point, and note that the editor's dates (Taqi d. 1131 AH,
  Rahmat Allah d. 1190 AH[OCR?]) attach to it.**

## Citable passages

  lane is called Kotli Muftiyan, in which the author of the book still resides." (p. 232, folio 220)

## Doubts

- **bibi-pak-daman: the burial sentence is syntactically broken.** "تاریخ وفات حضرت کی مادہ **خورشید
  دین محمد** سے حاصل ہوتی ہے۔ اور **مدفن گورستان بی بی پاکدامنان کی مادہ تاریخ ہے**" (p. 233,
  folio 221). The first clause gives the chronogram; the second runs the burial place and the word
  "chronogram" together in a way that does not parse. **The burial place is not in doubt — "مدفن
  گورستان بی بی پاکدامنان" — but the clause should be re-read from the PDF before it is quoted**, in
  case a phrase has dropped out. The year 1274 AH comes from the previous page (p. 232) and is
  unflagged.
- **tahir-bandagi-qadri: the name in this chunk is shortened.** "شیخ طاہر لاہوری" (p. 231) against the
  book's own "شیخ محمد طاہر بندگی لاہوری" in chunk 012 (p. 129). The identification rests on the
  shared name-element, the city, and the fact that both appear in the same book; **the chunk gives no
  dates for him and no location for the enclosure.** A human should confirm before the enclosure fact
  enters the row. **Note also that the archive places this shrine in the Miani Sahib graveyard, and
  this chunk's chahar-diwari is unlocated** — the two are compatible but not shown to be the same.
- **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya: "میاں کلاں" in the author's pedigree is probably NOT chunk 018's
  Mian Kalan.** Here he is a **Qureshi mufti summoned from Multan to Lahore by a king, distinguished
  in ifta[OCR?], and a direct descendant of Baha al-Din Zakariya** (p. 232). Chunk 018's entry no. 126
  is "شیخ محمد اسماعیل لاہوری المشہور **میاں کلاں**" — also **Mian Wada** — born 995 and died
  1085 AH, son of Fath Allah bin Abdullah bin Sarfaraz, **"قوم کے زمیندار کھوکھر"**, Khokhar
  zamindars, whose link to Baha al-Din Zakariya is a **thirteen-step spiritual chain**, not descent.
  **Different clan, different relation to Zakariya, different profession. Do not merge them**, and
  note that the shared epithet makes this a likely future conflation. The author's Mian Kalan is
  undated.
- **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya: the descent claim is the author's own and carries no source.** No
  footnote, no citation, and the editor — who corrected this same family's pedigree twice in chunks
  020 and 021 — **says nothing here.** Cite it as a family claim.
- **Sayyid Ghulam Muhyi al-Din has two death years on facing pages.** The prose: "اسی مقام پر **ایک
  ہزار دو سو چالیس** ہجری میں فوت ہوئے", died at Kotla in **1240 AH** (p. 228, folio 216). The
  chronogram printed two pages later gives **"۱۲۴۴ھ [OCR?]"** and, beside it, **"۱۲۸۴ م [OCR?]"**
  (p. 229, folio 217) — and the verse's opening word is flagged too ("چُگل [OCR?]"). **Three numerals,
  two of them flagged, and the second ("۱۲۸۴ م") is not even the same era-marker.** Unusable as
  transcribed.
- **At least three men named Ghulam Muhyi al-Din stand in these chunks and must not be merged.**
  (1) **Miran Sayyid Ghulam Muhyi al-Din**, Qadiri, d. 1240 AH, entry no. 159. (2) **Maulana Ghulam
  Muhyi al-Din Qasuri**, whose manuscript collection at the **Ganj Bakhsh Library, Rawalpindi** is
  cited in chunks 018, 019 and 021. (3) **Ghulam Muhyi al-Din**, the author's own cousin, son of Mufti
  Ghulam Rasul, alive when the book was written (p. 233, folio 221). And the **Bukhari sanad plate**
  of chunk 021 names its grantee "مولوي غلام محي الدين صاحب [OCR?]". **Chronologically, (1) could be
  the plate's grantee — Shah Abd al-Aziz died 1239 AH and he died 1240 AH — but the chunk says nothing
  of Delhi or of a hadith sanad. Leave all four separate and flag the possibility.**
- **Entry no. 160 has no death year and no grave.** Maulvi Ghulam Rasul Fazil Lahori's entry ends with
  the teaching timetable (p. 229, folio 217); his death is mentioned only in entry no. 165 as the event
  that passed the madrasa to his brother. **Do not supply a year from elsewhere.** His brother's own
  year is flagged ("ایک ہزار دو سو **ہفتر [OCR?]**", p. 232) and Jan Muhammad's too ("**اڑسٹھ
  [OCR?]**", p. 231).
- **"ایک ہزار نوے [OCR?]" cannot be right as printed.** Hafiz Ghulam Ahmad is the author's own brother,
  raised in the same household, and the surrounding deaths are 1274, 1280, 1288 AH; a death in 1090 AH
  is impossible. **The reading is almost certainly 1290 AH, but that is an inference — record the
  flagged text.** (p. 233, folio 221)
- **موضع کھوئی میراں and چاہ میراں may be the same place, and the chunk cannot settle it.** Sain Qutb
  Shah is buried at **"موضع کھوئی میراں"** (p. 235, folio 223); chunk 019's editor put the 600 AH
  Husain Zanjani "مدفون **چاہ میراں**" (p. 196), which is the archive's location for
  **shrine-of-miran-hussain-zanjani-zanjani-sahib**. *Khoi* and *chah* both mean "well", so the two
  names could denote one Lahore locality in two languages. **This is a philological guess, not a
  finding; flag it for a human, and map nothing on it.**
- **Three OCR-flagged names sit at the centre of entry no. 159's political narrative**: "قصبہ بھیریاں
  [OCR?]", "چودھری سہجی خاں [OCR?] مالک **وسوہہ [OCR?]**", "**لبر بائش [OCR?]** رئیس **غلنیان
  [OCR?]**" (p. 228, folio 216). **Four of the entry's places and patrons cannot be identified as
  transcribed**, though **Kotla**, **Gujranwala**, **Rasulnagar**, **Mandranwala** and **Miani** are
  clear. **Mahan Singh**, **Jai Singh Kanhiya** and **Raja Ranjit Deo** are unflagged.
- **Entry no. 167's two central names are flagged.** The saint's own epithet, "شیخ احمد شاہ کشمیری
  **تازہ بلی [OCR?]**", and his pir's, "آدم شاہ المشہور **اک شاہ [OCR?]**" (p. 233, folio 221). His
  four-order bai'at, the cow-slaughter case and the 1277 AH death are clear. **The entry carries no
  editorial footnote at all**, which is worth noting for an event under Dogra rule that the editor
  could have dated.
- **Entry no. 168's village is flagged**: "موضع **ہر دو تھلہ [OCR?]** ضلع ہوشیار پور" (p. 234) — and
  it is also his **burial place**, since he was buried where he lived. **His shrine cannot be located
  from this chunk.** "باغ رامانند" in Amritsar is unflagged; note that the book records **a Muslim
  ascetic resident there in the author's own time**, which is a fact about a site now across the
