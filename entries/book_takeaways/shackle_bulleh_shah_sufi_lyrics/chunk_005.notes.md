# shackle_bulleh_shah_sufi_lyrics — chunk 005 — pp. 143–172

translated kāfīs**, kāfīs 146(end)–157 (pp. 143–150, folios 118–125); a blank leaf (p. 151), the
**ABBREVIATIONS** page (p. 152, folio not stated), a second blank leaf (p. 153); and the opening of

pages, `Notes to the Translation` on the note pages.

**The back matter is where this book's archive value sits.** The lyric pages carry a poet's signature
and one reference to a shrine gate; the notes name Bullhe Shah's hometown, his master, his caste
standing, his hagiography, his editions and his sources, and they gloss the ritual and institutional
vocabulary the poems assume.

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`** (the book's forms: "Bullhe Shah" in the notes, "Bullha" in the poems'
  signature verse): **his hometown is named, and named as Kasur.** "S suggests that the phrase
  'citadel of knowledge' (koṭ ilam de) refers to Bullhe Shah's hometown of Kasur, which was famed for
  the learning of the local Islamic scholar Maulana Ghulam Murtaza, called 'The Master of the Panjab'
  (maḳhdūm-e panjāb)." (p. 155, folio 130). A second, separate note places another toponym in the
  same district but does not name it: "The name of a place in District Kasur." (p. 165, folio 140).
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **his allegiance, stated as a choice against the local scholar.**
  "Rather than study with this great scholar, Bullhe Shah gave his allegiance to Shah Inayat, from
  whom he received the word (kalmā) of his Sufi teaching." (p. 155, folio 130).
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **his caste standing, set against his master's.** "The discrepancy
  between Bullhe Shah's own high status as a Sayyid and the lower rank of Shah Inayat as an Arain is
  a prominent theme of the poet's hagiography. See also 24, 70, 108, 151." (p. 165, folio 140).
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **the dancing-girl episode, given as hagiography and cross-referenced
  to kāfī 105.** "The reference is to the famous episode in which Bullhe Shah disguised himself as a
  dancing girl to regain the affection of Shah Inayat. See 105." (p. 156, folio 131).
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **a rupture with the master, dated by circumstance not by year.** "The
  allusive verses of this kāfī appear to refer to a separation of Bullhe Shah from Shah Inayat due to
  the troubled conditions then prevailing in Panjab." (p. 165, folio 140). The note on the same kāfī
  supplies the era only: "The twelfth century of the Hijri era corresponds to the eighteenth century
  C.E. (1100 A.H. = 1688 C.E.). Compare 4 for the allusions to the disturbed state of contemporary
  Panjabi society." (pp. 165–166, folios 140–141). **The poet's own birth and death years are
  (not stated) anywhere in this chunk.**
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **the historical setting of the poems, given as Sikh–Mughal conflict.**
  "The social turmoil attendant on the internal uprisings of the Sikhs against the authority of the
  Mughals, which is the unusual subject of this kāfī, is interpreted as a manifestation of the
  mysterious workings of the divine beloved." (pp. 155–156, folios 130–131).
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **the master is addressed in the poems as the divine beloved, by
  name.** "As Shah Inayat, you show yourself to Bullha all the time." (p. 146, folio 121); "See how
  my lord Shah Inayat flirts with me." (p. 148, folio 123); "Bullha, he lets himself be found. Shah
  Inayat is discovered." (p. 150, folio 125); "If Inayat creates thousands of Bullhas, all that they
  will say and all that they will repeat is this: 'My beloved put me behind a veil.'" (p. 145,
  folio 120). A note confirms the identification for a further kāfī: "I.e., Shah Inayat." (p. 170,
  folio 145), and another glosses the epithet "Oh you with a great family" (vaḍ parvāriā) as "i.e.,
  Shah Inayat." (p. 165, folio 140).
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **the signature verse is a fixed formal feature** — the last verse of
  each kāfī opens with his name. "Bullha, let us fall at the lord's feet." (p. 143, folio 118);
  "Bullha, when Satan did not obey the lord's command, you brought about his disgrace." (p. 144,
  folio 119); "Bullha, the beloved has come to my house" (p. 145, folio 120); "Bullha goes to see the
  lord, according to his will." (p. 147, folio 122); "Bullha, if you guess this riddle, remove it
  from your head and throw it on the ground." (p. 147, folio 122); "Bullha, no one sees the lord."
  (p. 148, folio 123); "Bullha, what has the lord done to me?" (p. 149, folio 124); "Bullha, I am
  tormented by love." (p. 149, folio 124).
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **he wrote in more than one language, and the choice is thematic.**
  "Like other kāfīs on Hindu themes, this poem is written in Hindi, the characteristic language of
  the yogis. Compare 15, 20, 66, 113." (p. 155, folio 130); "This poem is composed in the kind of
  Hindi that is associated with its predominantly Hindu theme; see 3." (p. 159, folio 134); and of
  kāfī 35, "The language is fairly standard Urdu, and it lacks the usual refrain of a kāfī. It is
  actually a ghazal in the hazaj meter of four feet." (p. 166, folio 141).
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **the form of the kāfī, as the translator defines it.** "This unrhymed
  half-verse constitutes the refrain of the kāfī." (p. 154, folio not stated); "In the Panjabi text,
  the short verses have a single rhyme throughout and are paired by theme. For other monorhymed kāfīs
  of this same formal type, compare 6, 13, 58, 114." (p. 155, folio 130); "The poem is of the same
  formal type as kāfī 4, with a single rhyme throughout, but since the verses are here clearly
  grouped as pairs they are numbered accordingly." (p. 172, folio 147).
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **recurring themes named as his.** "This long poem, with its memorable
  refrain, is one of Bullhe Shah's most substantial treatments of the memento mori theme." (p. 154,
  folio not stated); the bride's dowry (dāj) "is frequently used by Bullhe Shah to symbolize the need
  to build up a store of rightly guided acts before the inevitable approach of death." (p. 154, folio
  not stated); kun fa-yakūnu "is frequently used by Bullhe Shah to indicate the act of creation"
  (p. 160, folio 135); "Ayub, the biblical Job, is always referred to in Bullhe Shah by the epithet
  'the patient one' (Ar. ṣābir)." (p. 161, folio 136).
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **a named Persian model for one of the famous kāfīs.** "It has often
  been observed this famous kāfī is modeled on Rumi's great Persian ghazal beginning cih tadbīr ai
  musalmānān kih man ḳhud-rā namīdānam, na tarsā nai yahūd-am man na gabr-am nai musalmān-am, 'What
  can I do, oh Muslims, for I do not know myself. I am not a Christian nor a Jew, not a Zoroastrian
  nor a Muslim.' Compare the thematically similar 106." (p. 167, folio 142).
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **explicit negative.** The chunk gives **no** tomb, mazar, ʿurs,
  darbar, sajjada nashin, custodian, endowment, building date, death year or silsila for him. His
  connection to Kasur is stated as birth/home, **not** as burial — "(not stated)".

- **`madho-lal-hussain`** (the book names him only "Shah Husain" and, in the signature line,
  "Husain the fakir"): **he is named as a rival claimant to poems printed here as Bullhe Shah's.**
  "This poem is generally considered to be by Bullhe Shah and is included as such in R, Q, F, and J.
  Like kāfī 63, however, it is excluded from S as the work of Shah Husain." (p. 164, folio 139).
- **`madho-lal-hussain`**: **the competing text is cited in full bibliographic form.** "For the
  notably variant Shah Husain text, see Daudi 1990: 91–92, kāfī 148 (trans. Anwar 1966: 25–26), which
  has the refrain mitrāñ dī mijmānī kāran, dil dā lahū chhānīdā, 'To entertain my beloved, my heart's
  blood is filtered,' followed by six verses only partly overlapping with 29, and with a quite
  different signature verse starting kahe Husain faqīr, 'Husain the fakir says.'" (p. 164,
  folio 139).
- **`madho-lal-hussain`**: **the translator's judgement on the rival attribution, and its ground.**
  "Given the very uncertain early textual history of both poets, this rival attribution can hardly be
  regarded as definitive, and the poem is therefore included here. Besides kāfī 63, other instances
  of poems also attributed to Shah Husain include kāfīs 130 and 145, which are not excluded by S.
  Compare the introductory notes on 63, 130, 145." (p. 164, folio 139).
- **`madho-lal-hussain`**: **explicit negative.** No shrine, no Lahore, no "Madho Lal", no dates, no
  order, no ʿurs — all "(not stated)". The whole of this row's material here is the author-only
  mention and its textual dispute. See Doubts.

- **`ram-mandir-saidpur-ram-kund-mandir`** (the book names him only "Ram", inside glosses on the
  Rāmāyaṇa): **named twice as the attacker of Lanka.** "This reference to the story of the Rāmāyaṇa
  describes a reversal of the arrival of Kumbhakaran to assist his brother Ravan, when his capital of
  Lanka was under attack by Ram and Lachhman." (p. 155, folio 130); "The capital of Ravan's kingdom,
  attacked by Ram." (p. 168, folio 143). **Nothing about a temple, a site or a cult.** This mapping
  is made under the 18 September ruling and is the weakest in these notes — see Doubts.

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The one shrine reference in the lyrics.** "I search for you at the gate of the shrine, but you get
  up and flee." (p. 144, folio 119). The word "shrine" occurs nowhere else in pp. 143–172; no shrine
  is named, located or described.
- **The abbreviations page is the book's citation key, and it is intact.** Printed as two lists under
  the heading `ABBREVIATIONS` (p. 152, folio not stated):
  - *Languages*: `Ar.` Arabic · `P.` Panjabi · `Pers.` Persian · `Skt.` Sanskrit
  - *Textual References*: `F` Faqir 1960 · `J` Jagtar 2008 · `PS` Puri and Shangari 1986 ·
    `Q` Qasuri 1896 · `R` Ruhtaki 1889 · `S` Sabir 1991
  Every siglum used in the notes of this chunk (F, J, PS, Q, R, S) is covered by this list.
- **Custodial and clerical ranks, with their incomes.** "A qazi is a religious judge, who ranks higher
  than the humble mullah attached to a local mosque. Qazis are regularly castigated for their greed
  and willingness to take bribes." (p. 161, folio 136); "Mullahs are paid a small fee to recite in
  Arabic when butchers slaughter an animal in the prescribed halal fashion by cutting its throat."
  (p. 161, folio 136); "The writing of religious formulae to be worn on the body as protective amulets
  (tāvīz) is a recognized skill of Muslim religious specialists." (p. 161, folio 136). In the poem:
  "It is you who are the mullah and you who are the qazi. It is you who impart instruction." (p. 146,
  folio 121).
- **Life-cycle ritual described as a fee-taking trade.** "Here the 'robbers' (ṭhag) are the religious
  specialists who create the rituals surrounding birth and death and live off the fees they earn from
  them." (p. 158, folio 133) — cross-referenced to kāfīs 20, 40.
- **The economy of pilgrimage, stated flatly as an exploitation.** "The prestige gained from their
  performance of the hajj, or pilgrimage to Mecca, is exploited by the hajjis on their return in order
  to increase the offerings of the pious." (pp. 160–161, folios 135–136). And the contrast the poems
  draw from it: "The contrast between the orthodox performance of the hajj to Mecca and the lover as
  pilgrim to the beloved occurs elsewhere. Compare 102, 126, also 105, 128." (p. 166, folio 141);
  "The popular Panjabi pronunciation of Ar. ġhāzī, 'warrior for the faith,' as gājī rhymes with hājī."
  (p. 166, folio 141).
- **Visible marks of piety as social capital.** The mihrab-shaped prayer mark: "The shape of the mark
  on the forehead that is made by frequent pious prostration in prayer is here compared to that of a
  mihrab." (p. 160, folio 135); "A mihrab (Ar. miḥrāb) is the niche in the wall of a mosque that marks
  the Qibla, the direction of the Kaaba toward which those offering prayer must face." (p. 156,
  folio 131); the Hindu counterpart, "The tilak is the mark drawn on the forehead as a sign of Hindu
  religious affiliation, as opposed to the religious practice and duty (sunnat faraz) of Islam."
  (p. 168, folio 143); "The title Hafiz is given to those who have learned the entire Qurʼan by
  heart." (p. 159, folio 134).
- **Dress as communal marker.** "Clothes dyed blue were particularly associated with Muslims in
  premodern India." (p. 160, folio 135); "'Turk' was a standard term in medieval India for 'Muslim,'
  as opposed to Hindu. Compare 20, 40, 78, 106, 131." (p. 158, folio 133); the fakir's alfī,
  "the shroudlike sleeveless garment worn by fakirs, so called because it is straight in shape like
  the letter alif" (p. 166, folio 141); "Stains do not show on the blanket worn by a fakir, which is
  dyed a dark color." (p. 172, folio 147); "Yogis dress in ochre-dyed garments, whatever the cloth
  they are made of." (p. 165, folio 140); "The deerskin is typically associated with yogis, like the
  skull used as a cup for begging, the long matted hair, and the ashes smeared on the body."
  (p. 156, folio 131). In the poems: "Take off your turban and throw it on the ground." (p. 146,
  folio 121); "Wearing the turban at a tilt is the sign of a dandy, like the casual way of wearing
  slippers." (p. 167, folio 142).
- **The madrasa syllabus, named with its textbooks and their authors' death dates.** "These Arabic
  textbooks are classics of the legal syllabus and are frequently cited as a pair to indicate the
  formal syllabus of Islamic law. Their full titles are Kanz al-Daqāʼiq (The treasury of difficult
  points) by Abdullah ibn Muhammad Nasafi (d. 1310), and Muḳhtasar al-Qudūrī (The compendium of
  al-Quduri) by Ahmad ibn Muhammad al-Quduri (d. 1036). Compare 72." (p. 159, folio 134). The poem
  names a third: "When the beloved came to my house, I forgot the Commentary on the Wiqāya." (p. 143,
  folio 118), alongside "I forgot all about logic and syntax" (p. 143, folio 118).
- **Halal slaughter as the type of formal obligation.** "the Muslim dietary requirement to eat only
  halal meat, which has been properly slaughtered with the beast being bled after its throat has been
  cut, while avoiding the flesh of animals that have died a natural death (Pers. murdār)." (p. 159,
  folio 134).
- **The spinning party as a village women's institution.** "The spinning party (attaṇ) is where the
  girls of a village traditionally gathered together to spin. Compare 70." (p. 165, folio 140); in the
  poem, "My girlfriends have got together and have gone to ask: 'Is it time for the spinning party,
  oh?'" (p. 144, folio 119). Spinning is also the governing metaphor of kāfī 37: "The use of spinning
  as a metaphor for the correct conduct of life is here developed with an abundance of terms referring
  to different parts of the spinning wheel." (p. 166, folio 141).
- **The marriage economy, itemised.** The dowry: the bride "needs to prepare the clothes that she will
  take with her as her dowry (dāj) when she leaves for her husband's house" (p. 154, folio not
  stated); the ceremony of departure: "The wedding culminates in the ceremony (muklāvā) of the bride
  being taken away from her parents' home to her husband's house, where she will spend the rest of her
  life." (p. 154, folio not stated); the palanquin: "the Kheras compelled Hir to return with them in a
  bridal palanquin (ḍolī) to her new husband's home. Compare 61." (p. 165, folio 140); the countdown:
  "The countdown to the day of the wedding is calculated by progressively undoing the knots (ganḍhīñ)
  that have been tied for each day of the waiting period." (p. 169, folio 144); the bride's colour:
  "Bright red (sūhā) is the color that symbolizes the joyful state of a bride." (p. 160, folio 135);
  and the farewell to the sahelīāñ (p. 164, folio 139). Kin terms are glossed: "Literally, 'father's
  elder and younger brothers' (kiā cāce kiā tāe), the core kin group of the patrilineal family."
  (p. 156, folio 131).
- **Cloth as a named and graded commodity.** "cauñsī and paiñsī are woven from 400 and 500 threads
  respectively, khaddar and dhotar are types of coarse cloth, and malmal and khāsā are varieties of
  fine muslin." (p. 164, folio 139).
- **Peddling and rural credit.** "The 'traders' (vaṇjāre) are the peddlers who supplied the village
  women with their jewelry, and are here as often elsewhere used to symbolize the saints who bear the
  uniquely valuable message of all-demanding love for the divine. Compare 70, 117." (p. 166,
  folio 141); "Before the introduction of pockets and purses, money was kept tied in the hem of a
  garment." (p. 166, folio 141).
- **A unit of weight given exactly.** "A maund (maṇ) is a large measure of weight, equivalent to 80
  pounds." (p. 166, folio 141).
- **The state as tax-collector, in the poem.** "The constables who pursued us have gone off to pay in
  our taxes." (p. 146, folio 121). The note to that line is not in this chunk.
- **Festival.** Holi: "There is a deliberately shocking juxtaposition of the pious Muslim formula 'In
  the name of God' (Ar. bi’smi’llāh)… with the Hindu spring festival of Holi." (pp. 168–169, folios
  143–144); "The tube (pickārī) used to squirt colored dye at other people during the celebration of
  Holi." (p. 169, folio 144).
- **Divination as a paid service.** "I have called on the wise women and got them all to draw figures.
  The astrologers have given me a blunt answer." (p. 150, folio 125); the letter-numerology behind it:
  "The term abjad is given to the allocation of numerical values to the letters of the Arabic alphabet
  in the historic order of Hebrew and other older Semitic alphabets." (p. 161, folio 136).
- **Ecstatic dance, in the poem.** "You get up and join in the dance with those who are covered in
  mud. I am beautifully dressed, but you run away from me." (p. 145, folio 120).
- **The idol temple and the girdle of unbelief, as institutions the poems invert.** "You wrap the
  girdle of unbelief around your neck. You go into the idol temple and sit there." (p. 146,
  folio 121). Both notes are outside this chunk.
- **Games and gambling.** "A seventeen (satārāñ F S) is a top throw of the dice in the game of caupaṛ,
  versus the usual idiom in pauñ bārāñ jāñ paiñde ne J, 'when getting a twelve.' Compare 97."
  (pp. 166–167, folios 141–142).
- **Intercession at the judgement as doctrine.** "On the day of judgment, the prophet Muhammad will
  intercede for those who have followed the commandments of Islam. Compare 17, 104, 117, 119."
  (p. 167, folio 142).
- **Sufi technical vocabulary glossed as institutional terms.** "'Real love' (Pers. ‘ishq-e haqīqī) is
  the mystical love of God as the true reality, which is exalted in Sufism above the human love called
  'apparent love' (Pers. ‘ishq-e majāzī). Compare 74." (p. 159, folio 134); "The word 'adept' (Ar.
  ‘ārif) is a Sufi term, like 'fakir' (Ar. faqīr)." (p. 158, folio 133); "A technical term of Sufism
  (Ar. fanā fī ’llāh), used to describe the obliteration of the self in the divine." (p. 169,
  folio 144); "By 'inspirational knowledge' (Pers. ‘ilm-e ladunnī) is meant the mystical science of
  Sufism." (p. 161, folio 136); "A Panjabi translation of a popular Sufi saying (Ar. mūtū qabla an
  tamūtū)." (p. 167, folio 142); "The Sufi doctrine of 'peace toward all' (Pers. ṣulḥ-e kull) was also
  adopted in India as a definition of Akbar's policy of religious tolerance toward non-Muslims."
  (p. 167, folio 142); the cupbearer "(Pers. sāqī) who in Persian poetry represents the teacher who
  dispenses the wine of mystical knowledge" (p. 171, folio 146).
- **Yoga as a described practice.** "The phrase 'make the Ganges flow backward' (ulṭī gangā bahāio) is
  an idiom meaning 'to do the opposite of the usual,' which here refers to the drawing up of psychic
  energy during the yogic process." (p. 155, folio 130); "The 'unstruck music' (anhad nād) is the
  mystical sound heard at the climax of the yogic process. Compare 15, 47, 66, 67, 117, 137, 147."
  (p. 155, folio 130); "The 'circle of nectar' (amrit manḍal) is another traditional image for the
  ecstatic state that is the goal of yoga." (p. 155, folio 130). In the poem: "He played the unstruck
  music on his string." (p. 143, folio 118).
- **A cosmology given a number.** "The fourteen zones (Ar. tabaq) that together make up the entire
  universe. Compare 77, 135." (p. 168, folio 143).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

All attributed as the book attributes them — mostly as stories glossed by the translator, several
hedged.

- **Shams resuscitates a prince and is flayed for it** — "supposed to have been uttered by the
  mysterious twelfth-century Sufi saint Shams when he misused his miraculous powers to resuscitate
  the dead son of the king of Ghazna… Shams was sentenced to death by being hanged upside down and
  flayed alive." (p. 157, folio 132). Note the translator's hedge, "supposed to have been uttered".
- **Mansur al-Hallaj executed for anā ’l-ḥaqq** (pp. 161, 163; folios 136, 138); in the poem, "You
  had him arrested and put on the gallows." (p. 144, folio 119).
- **Junaid's robe** — "There is nothing in my robe [except God]" (p. 163, folio 138).
- **Bullhe Shah as a dancing girl** — the hagiographic episode, cross-referenced to kāfī 105 (p. 156,
  folio 131).
- **Abu Jahl and the speaking stones** — "S explains the story: Abu Jahl once hid some stones in his
  clenched fist and came to ask the Prophet what he had in his hand. Muhammad told him they were
  stones that would magnify God. Then from his hand could be heard the proclamation, 'There is no god
  but God, and Muhammad is his Apostle.' Abu Jahl then fled in discomfiture." (p. 171, folio 146).
- **Ayub's worms** — "He was tested by God by having his body filled with worms." (p. 161, folio 136);
  in the poem, "You put the worms in the body of Ayub the patient. You put back any that dropped off
  him." (p. 144, folio 119).
- **Zakariya sawn inside a tree** — "took refuge in a hollow tree from pursuit by the soldiers of the
  Jewish king Herod. He died when the soldiers sawed through the tree while he was still inside it."
  (p. 161, folio 136).
- **Yahya beheaded at prayer** — "she had him beheaded while he was at prayer, then had his head
  served up on a china platter." (p. 162, folio 137).
- **Sulaiman's ring and flying throne** — "a magic ring that gave him power over the jinn and other
  supernatural beings. They supported the magic throne on which he could fly through the air. Once
  Sulaiman lost his ring and was then reduced to poverty and forced to earn his living by stoking a
  furnace." (p. 162, folio 137).
- **Ibrahim saved from the pyre** — "he was sentenced by the evil king Namrud to be burned on a pyre,
  from which he was rescued by God." (p. 167, folio 142).
- **Jonah and the fish** — "The biblical Jonah, who was swallowed by a large fish." (p. 168,
  folio 143).
- **Pharaoh drowned** — "who was eventually drowned in the Nile when he tried to pursue him."
  (p. 171, folio 146).
- **Yusuf in the well and in the slave market** — "put down a well by his jealous brothers, who sold
  him into slavery in Egypt; there Zulaikha… saw him in the slave market and was overwhelmed by love
  for him." (pp. 157–158, folios 132–133); and the dream: "In Jami's Persian poem Yūsuf Zulaiḳhā,
  Yusuf comes to Zulaikha in her dreams long before the pair actually meet." (p. 172, folio 147).
- **Sassi dies following tracks in the desert** (p. 160, folio 135); **Sohni drowns** (p. 154, folio
  not stated; p. 170, folio 145).
- **The philosopher's stone** — "The magic stone (pāras) that alchemically transforms iron into gold.
  Compare 146." (p. 172, folio 147); in the poem, "Pearls and jewels and the philosopher's stone are
  right next to you." (p. 143, folio 118).
- **The end of the world** — the false prophet "whose coming will mark the end of the world and whose
  teachings will destroy true religion and create social chaos." (p. 170, folio 145).

## Arguments and interpretations

The notes are a working textual-critical apparatus, and the translator is repeatedly explicit about
uncertainty. This is the most usable material in the chunk.

- **The recension is built from six named editions, and their disagreements are recorded.** kāfī 11:
  "The opening of this short poem makes it an obvious choice as the first kāfī in the Persian-script
  editions F and S. But it is not attested in the earlier editions R and Q." (p. 157, folio 132).
  kāfī 35: "This kāfī is not included in R. All subsequent editions have taken this poem from Q, but
  some doubts must attach to its authenticity." (p. 166, folio 141). kāfī 51: "This remarkable
  depiction of the strife in contemporary society as a set of disputes between different kinds of
  snacks and sweets is first recorded in Q. It is absent from R, and its authenticity has sometimes
  been questioned. It is not translated in PS." (p. 170, folio 145). kāfī 42 n. 12: "This verse is
  included in S, but not in most other editions." (p. 168, folio 143).
- **An argument for oral transmission from the textual evidence.** "All editions follow R in printing
  kāfīs 49 and 50 as separate poems. Their rhymes –āriā (49) and –oliā (50) may be different, but
  these two short poems are otherwise so similar that they are probably variants of a single original
  that diverged in the oral tradition. Compare the similarly paired 130 and 131." (p. 170, folio 145).
- **Authorship between Bullhe Shah and Shah Husain is declared unsettled, and the ground is the state
  of the record.** "Given the very uncertain early textual history of both poets, this rival
  attribution can hardly be regarded as definitive." (p. 164, folio 139).
- **The translator marks his own guesses.** "This is a tentative interpretation of the Panjabi text
  (bhogaṇ karan adāīñ)." (p. 156, folio 131); "The translation follows S, but the word translated as
  'flood' (jagātar) in the original appears corrupt. Any translation must therefore be guesswork."
  (p. 156, folio 131); "The sense of the rhyme word aṇghātāñ (translated as 'mysterious') is obscure
  both here and in 117." (p. 156, folio 131); "The rhyme word bār (here translated as 'sense') is
  variously interpreted." (p. 154, folio not stated); "The translation 'philosopher' is very doubtful,
  since the rhyme word junnī seems to be invented to fit the awkward rhyme with sunnī; compare 138. S
  suggests that junnī is a distorted form of yūnānī, 'Greek.'" (p. 161, folio 136); "The overall sense
  of the poem is powerfully conveyed, but the precise meaning of some verses is not always entirely
  clear." (p. 170, folio 145); "The sense is unclear." (p. 165, folio 140); "the identity of the
  donkey-herdsman is obscure." (p. 165, folio 140); "The verse could be interpreted either as a
  reference to the condition of man following the fall of Adam or as the poet's statement of
  bewilderment as to how the disgrace incurred by love is to be resolved." (p. 156, folio 131).
- **He adjudicates between his predecessors, and rejects one on grounds of anachronistic doctrine.**
  "PS explains as, 'I have become immortal in Your love and it is only my mind which has been
  eliminated forever,' but this owes more to Radhasoami than to Sufi ideas." (p. 165, folio 140);
  "The phrase 'both heaps' (doveñ thok) is better understood with PS as 'this world and the world to
  come,' rather than with S as the two words of the formula lā ilāha, 'there is no God.'" (p. 167,
  folio 142).
- **He corrects a received scriptural attribution.** Of lā tataḥarraku: "Although it seems to be
  understood as scriptural, it does not actually occur in the text of the Qurʼan. Compare 77."
  (p. 171, folio 146).
- **He identifies filler produced by metre.** "This half-verse virtually repeats the refrain and
  appears to be a filler devised to make up the metrical scheme." (p. 170, folio 145).
- **The Qurʼanic and Arabic substrate is treated as deliberate craft, not ornament.** "This poem is
  remarkable for its deliberate clustering of Qurʼanic and other Arabic phrases, most of which also
  recur in verses of other kāfīs." (p. 162, folio 137). Twenty-odd citations are given with sura and
  verse: Qurʼan 7.143, 6.32, 2.18, 2.117, 2.115, 15.26, 7.172, 15.29, 50.16, 57.4, 51.20–21, 17.70,
  2.152-3, 2.138, 112.2 (pp. 157–163, 169; folios 132–138, 144).
- **Letter-mysticism read as the book's structural key.** alif as "the first letter of the Arabic
  alphabet and the first letter of the word 'Allah'", identical in shape with the sign for 1 (Ar.
  aḥad), "as opposed to the second letter, be… which stands for the world of duality. Compare 22, 26,
  42, 58, 92, 118, 144, 150." (p. 157, folio 132); "In the Arabic script, Ahad 'the One,' i.e., God,
  is virtually identical with Ahmad, i.e., the prophet Muhammad. Only the letter mīm in its very small
  medial form (-m-) marks the difference" (p. 162, folio 137); "the removal of the initial letter ‘ain
  from anā ‘arab… results in anā rabb, 'I am the lord.'" (p. 162, folio 137); "The small dot that
  distinguishes letters in the Perso-Arabic script symbolizes the concentrated essence of mystical
  reality." (p. 160, folio 135).
- **A textual variant produced by the script itself.** "The Persian script does not distinguish
  between se, 'those,' and sai, 'hundreds,' and some editions prefer the latter." (p. 166, folio 141).
- **Social conflict read as theology.** The Sikh–Mughal turmoil "is interpreted as a manifestation of
  the mysterious workings of the divine beloved." (pp. 155–156, folios 130–131).
- **Where the poems level religious difference.** "From the astronomical perspective of Sahadev, the
  geographical locations on earth of Lanka and Mecca are identical, proving the meaninglessness of
  their rival associations with Hindu and Muslim identity." (p. 159, folio 134); "The distinction
  between the mynah and the parrot symbolizes the apparent differences of superficial reality, which
  are destroyed by the perception of the underlying divine unity." (p. 159, folio 134); "The teachings
  of love are diametrically opposed to the commandments of formal religion" (p. 159, folio 134).
- **In the poems' own voice**: "Fasts and pilgrimage and prayer, Mother, are all forgotten when my
  beloved comes." (p. 143, folio 118); "Since I planted thorns in the name of the lord, the garden has
  blossomed." (p. 146, folio 121); "He fashions pots of many kinds, then breaks them into pieces."
  (p. 147, folio 122); "Lovers are destroyed in both worlds." (p. 148, folio 123); "Bullha, no one
  sees the lord… He has no color, form, or mark." (p. 148, folio 123).

## Doubts

- **Three kinds of number are on these pages and they must not be confused.** (i) The **folio** is set
  at the foot of the page and so appears last in the transcription of each page — e.g. `118` at the
  end of p. 143. (ii) The **kāfī number** is centred above each new poem — `147`, `148` (p. 143),
  `149` (p. 144), `150`, `151` (p. 145), `152` (p. 146), `153`, `154` (p. 147), `155` (p. 148), `156`
  (p. 149), `157` (p. 150) — and in the notes section it is the centred heading over each block of
  notes, running `1` to `58` across pp. 154–172. (iii) The **note number** is the left-hand numeral
  within a block. The trap is real: `147` at the head of p. 143 is a kāfī number, while `147` at the
  foot of p. 172 is a folio. Every reference above was taken from position on the page, not from the
  numeral alone.
- **The folio rule PDF − 25 was confirmed for all 26 numbered pages of this chunk**; no page
  contradicts it. p. 151 and **p. 153** are transcribed as `[blank page]`; p. 152 and p. 154 carry
  text but print no number. The run brief listed p. 151 as a blank leaf and pp. 152–154 as printing no
  number — **p. 153 is additionally blank**, which is a small correction to the brief rather than a
  conflict.
- **THE SHAH INAYAT TRAP IS LIVE AND I HAVE NOT RESOLVED IT.** The index holds two rows whose
  principal figure is a Shah Inayat: `shrine-of-shah-inayat-qadiri` (Shah Inayat Qadiri, Mozang,
  Lahore, d. c. 1728, Qadri Shattari) and `dargah-roza-sufi-shah-inayat-shaheed` (Sufi Shah Inayat
  Shaheed, Jhok Sharif, Sindh, d. 1718). **What the chunk states, and only this**: he is Bullhe Shah's
  master and the giver of his kalmā (p. 155, folio 130); he is "an Arain" of "lower rank" than the
  Sayyid poet (p. 165, folio 140); the separation happened "due to the troubled conditions then
  prevailing in Panjab" (p. 165, folio 140); he is addressed by name in the poems as "Shah Inayat"
  (pp. 146, 148, 150; folios 121, 123, 125) and once as "Inayat" (p. 145, folio 120). **The chunk
  gives no town, no district, no province of residence, no tomb, no death year and no silsila for
  him.** A Panjabi caste term and a Panjab setting lean toward the Lahore row, but that is an
  inference, not a statement, and RULE 2 forbids me from making it. **Not mapped. A human must
  decide**, and should look for the introduction (an earlier chunk) where the master is likely
  identified outright.
- **`madho-lal-hussain` is mapped on an author-only mention, per the 18 September ruling.** The
  evidence is the name "Shah Husain" and the signature "kahe Husain faqīr" in a note on textual
  attribution (p. 164, folio 139). The chunk **never** says Lahore, never says "Madho Lal", gives no
  dates, no order and no shrine. If the consolidator judges that this "Shah Husain" might be a
  different poet of the same name, nothing here rules that out.
- **`ram-mandir-saidpur-ram-kund-mandir` is the weakest mapping in these notes and should be reviewed
  first.** "Ram" appears twice, both times as a character inside a gloss on the Rāmāyaṇa (p. 155,
  folio 130; p. 168, folio 143) — a mythological reference in a third party's epic, not a person
  named as an author or for a doctrine, which is what the 18 September ruling's four examples are.
  **Whether the ruling extends to deity principal figures is undecided.** If it does not, this bullet
  moves whole to "Other saints, sites and events".
- **Krishna is two rows, so he is not mapped.** `krishna-mandir-kabari-bazar` (Rawalpindi) and
  `krishna-mandir-ravi-road` (Lahore) both name Krishna as principal figure, and the chunk gives no
  site, no town and no cult — only "Krishna as the divine cowherd" (p. 165, folio 140) and Mathura as
  where "Krishna spent his youth as a herdsman" (p. 168, folio 143). Recorded under "Other saints".
- **"Shams" (p. 157, folio 132) could be the archive's `shrine-of-shah-shams-ud-din-sabzwari`, and
  could equally not be.** The book gives "the mysterious twelfth-century Sufi saint Shams", the
  Ghazna miracle and the flaying — **no Multan, no Sabzwar, no full name, and a century (twelfth) that
  does not match the archive row's death year of 1276.** Not mapped; recorded under "Other saints".
- **"Lahore" (p. 148, folio 123) is a bare toponym inside a poem** — "the robber from Lahore" — with
  no shrine, tomb, saint, ʿurs or custodian attached, so under the interim convention it takes no id.
  **If that convention reverses it could not be resolved anyway**: the index holds well over twenty
  Lahore rows (`data-darbar`, `shrine-of-mian-mir`, `madho-lal-hussain`, `shah-jamal`,
  `bibi-pak-daman`, `shrine-of-shah-inayat-qadiri` and the rest), and the poem names none of them.
