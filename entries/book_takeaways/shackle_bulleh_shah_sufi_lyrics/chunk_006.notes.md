# shackle_bulleh_shah_sufi_lyrics — chunk 006 — pp. 173–199

Editorial back matter, not verse: the close of **Notes to the Translation** (notes to poems 59–157,
pp. 173–196, folios 148–171), a **blank leaf** (p. 197), and the **opening of the Glossary**, headwords
`ABDULLAH` to `qadiri`, i.e. the run A–Q only (pp. 198–199, folios 173–174). Running heads as printed:
`Notes to the Translation` (pp. 173–196), `GLOSSARY` (p. 198), `glossary` (p. 199). **No Bibliography
and no Index falls in this chunk** — the Glossary is cut mid-letter-Q at the chunk boundary.

The printed folio sits alone at the foot of each page and the rule **folio = PDF − 25 holds unbroken
across every page of this chunk**, verified against the printed number on all twenty-six numbered
pages: p. 173 carries `148`, p. 196 carries `171`, p. 198 carries `173`, p. 199 carries `174`. p. 197
is a blank leaf and prints no number; the folio it would carry, 172, is simply skipped in the printed
sequence, which is why the Glossary opens on folio 173. Page references below are PDF page indices
with the folio the page actually prints.

This is the chunk where the translator identifies persons, dates editions and manuscripts, and defines
institutional terms, so its value to the archive is out of proportion to its length. See **Doubts** for
the glossary/index headword test and for the three-way number collision (poem number / note number /
folio) that this chunk is full of.

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Annual shrine festivals with wrestling and sports — the single most useful institutional statement
  in the chunk.** "The image of the contest (chinj) is taken from the practice of putting on shows of
  wrestling and other sports at shrines on the occasion of their annual festivals. It is here developed
  to describe what happens to the soul after death." (p. 195, folio 170, n. 1 on 152). The word ʿurs
  is not used; the book says "annual festivals".
- **Tomb pilgrimage.** "This invitation to visit his tomb in Multan, which is a major center of
  pilgrimage, strikes a strange note" (p. 186, folio 161, n. 5 on 122); "Kaaba (Ar. Ka‘ba) the shrine
  in Mecca that is the goal of the hajj." (p. 199, folio 174).
- **Yogic initiation as a bodily, institutional act.** Ear-cartilage piercing and the wooden ring at
  Tilla Jogian (p. 187, folio 162, n. 10 on 122); "Yogis sit in front of a bonfire (dhūāñ) and rub its
  ashes (bhabūt) on their bodies." (p. 190, folio 165, n. 3 on 137); the yogi's forehead mark — "she
  will put a yogi's mark (tilak) on her forehead and become a yogini" (p. 190, folio 165, n. 1 on 137);
  "The tenth door is the hidden aperture in the skull that is opened at the climax of the yogic
  process." (p. 173, folio 148, n. 2 on 63); "The 'fourth state' (cauthā pad, Skt. turīyāvasthā-) is a
  yogic term that denotes the state of liberation achieved through transcending the three guṇas that
  govern worldly existence." (p. 176, folio 151, n. 2 on 71).
- **Alms.** "Sahti gave Ranjha some millet as alms, but at first he angrily rejected this inferior
  grain." (p. 187, folio 162, n. 13 on 122).
- **The four stages of the Sufi path, given twice with the Panjabi and Persian terms.** "'Reality'
  (sacc, Pers. haqīqat) is the third stage on the Sufi path, reached after the law (sharī‘at) and the
  way (tarīqat)." (pp. 175–176, folios 150–151, n. 1 on 71); "The verse describes the four stages on
  the Sufi path. The law (Ar. sharī‘at, P. sharhā) is followed by the way (tarīqat), then reality
  (haqīqat), and finally gnosis (ma‘rifat)." (p. 178, folio 153, n. 7 on 78).
- **The guide as an office, glossed in the Glossary.** "PIR (P. Pīr) Sufi spiritual guide."
  (p. 199, folio 174); "Thanks to the insight I have been given by my teacher, who is both Guru and
  Pir, all people appear equally worthy of respect." (p. 189, folio 164, n. 5 on 131). Also the
  "Magian elder" — "In the language of the Persian ghazal, the 'Magian elder' (Pers. pīr-e muġhāñ) is
  the master who runs the tavern where the lovers drink the wine of love." (p. 190, folio 165, n. 5 on
  136).
- **The Sufi gathering place against the mosque.** "The 'tavern' (ḳharābāt), literally, the ruins
  where drinkers hang out, is a key term in the language of the Persian ghazal. It denotes the place
  where the followers of Sufi teachings gather, as opposed to the mosque where the orthodox
  congregate." (p. 189, folio 164, n. 2 on 135).
- **Ritual prayer described as a sequence.** "The verse describes the ritual observances of Muslim
  prayer, beginning with the muezzin's call… The preliminary formulation of righteous intent (niyyat)
  is followed by prostration, which is performed facing the mihrab, the niche in the wall of a mosque
  that marks the direction of the Qibla." (p. 187, folio 162, n. 4 on 125); ablution — "the use of the
  whole well rather than the more modest amount of water normally used for ritual ablution (vuzū)
  before prayer" (p. 187, folio 162, n. 3 on 125); "The least elaborate kind of prayer." (p. 185,
  folio 160, n. 5 on 117).
- **Hindu ritual, characterised polemically by the poet.** "This verse and those following describe
  various Hindu religious rituals, including the marriage ceremonies performed for idols of the gods,
  as meaningless distractions from reality." (p. 174, folio 149, n. 2 on 64); "The proliferation of
  kinship terms mocks the whole notion of idols being related to one another." (p. 174, folio 149,
  n. 6 on 64); "The 'idol temple' (Pers. but-ḳhāna) is another standard term in the language of the
  Persian ghazal, here used for a Hindu temple." (p. 194, folio 169, n. 2 on 151); "The 'girdle' (Pers.
  zunnār)… is used here to indicate the Hindu sacred thread." (p. 194, folio 169, n. 1 on 151).
- **Caste and tribe as social facts the poems turn on.** "arain (Arāīñ) the caste of vegetable
  gardeners" (p. 198, folio 173); "As a sweeper girl of the untouchable Chuhra caste, she is reckoned
  to be neither a Hindu nor a Muslim." (p. 189, folio 164, n. 3 on 131); "Here 'twice-born' (do jarme)
  is the familiar designation of Hindus of the higher castes, while 'thrice-born' (trai jarme) may be
  intended to refer to Muslims" (p. 189, folio 164, n. 4 on 131); "The Ranghars were a tribe of Muslim
  Rajputs who achieved prominence in eighteenth-century Panjab, and who were proverbial for their
  arrogance." (p. 179, folio 154, n. 3 on 86); "The Jat tribe to which Hir belonged." (p. 177, folio
  152, n. 3 on 76); "Hot Baloch tribe to which Punnun belonged." (pp. 198–199, folios 173–174);
  "khera (Kheṛā) name of the tribe into which Hir was forcibly married." (p. 199, folio 174).
- **Marriage rites, itemised.** The bridegroom's "diadem (mukṭā) from which a veil is hung over the
  face and the forehead mark (tilak)" (p. 179, folio 154, n. 3 on 81); "the ritual anointing and
  cleansing of the bridegroom before the marriage ceremony" (p. 186, folio 161, n. 8 on 122); "māñjhā…
  the custom of dressing the bride in old clothes and confining her to the house for some days before
  her marriage" (p. 182, folio 157, n. 2 on 104); the qazi performing a forced marriage at the father's
  instance (pp. 186, 190–191; folios 161, 165–166, nn. 4 on 122 and 12 on 137).
- **Music, dance and drumming as paid or menial work.** "As a menial employee of Chuchak, Ranjha had
  to play the drum (ṭamak) during the festivities for Hir's wedding." (p. 187, folio 162, n. 9 on 122);
  "The jhumbar is a round dance performed by women with joined hands." (p. 194, folio 169, n. 3 on
  149); "Literally, 'I perform the giddhā,' a folk dance in which girls clap and sing." (p. 184, folio
  159, n. 3 on 114); the syllables "thaīā thaīā… are used by teachers of dancing to mark the rhythm of
  the steps" (p. 182, folio 157, n. 1 on 105); the flute is "the preferred musical instrument of
  herdsmen" (p. 181, folio 156, n. 1 on 104); "the divine sound of the unstruck music (anhad vājā)
  experienced at the climax of mystical union" (p. 175, folio 150, n. 3 on 67).
- **Women's work and gathering.** "These parties (trinjaṇāñ) are where the girls of the village gather
  to spin and to gossip." (p. 175, folio 150, n. 1 on 70).
- **Time reckoning.** "The passage of time was traditionally marked by the beating of a gong. The
  night and the day were each divided into four watches (pahir) of about three hours, which were in
  turn subdivided into eight units of about twenty-four minutes (ghaṛī)." (p. 175, folio 150, n. 1 on 67).
- **Money and fiscal imagery.** "Cowrie shells were used as the smallest units of currency." (p. 181,
  folio 156, n. 3 on 98); "The relentless emissaries of death are compared to tax-collectors' agents."
  (p. 195, folio 170, n. 2 on 152); "Literally, '100,000,' i.e., a large reward." (p. 191, folio 166,
  n. 1 on 139); "When Yusuf was put up for sale in the Egyptian slave market, an old woman offered a
  hank of yarn for him." (p. 181, folio 156, n. 2 on 98); the distiller — "The distiller (kalāl) is the
  equivalent of the cupbearer, who dispenses the wine that banishes all thoughts of ritual observance."
  (p. 175, folio 150, n. 5 on 67).
- **Magic, spells and divination.** "A spell is activated by blowing out the words with the breath."
  and "The seeds of the wild rue (harmal) are burned to banish evil spirits." (p. 178, folio 153,
  nn. 2–3 on 79); "the display of stars under baskets is a proverbial example of a magic trick"
  (p. 179, folio 154, n. 3 on 83); "I.e., the malign influence of an evil spirit." (p. 179, folio 154,
  n. 1 on 83); "Shapes (auñsīāñ) are drawn on the ground with a rake as a means of foreseeing the
  future." (p. 196, folio 171, n. 3 on 157).
- **Death, burial and the afterlife.** "The 'city of the silent' (shahir ḳhamoshāñ dā) is the land of
  the dead." (p. 179, folio 154, n. 1 on 84); "The expression here refers to the first night after
  death." (p. 188, folio 163, n. 6 on 126); "The narrow bridge that the soul must cross over after
  death in order to reach paradise, while avoiding falling into the torments of hell." (p. 185, folio
  160, n. 9 on 119), recalled as the Sirat bridge (p. 195, folio 170, n. 4 on 152); "This represents
  the actions (Ar. a‘māl) that are the product of life on earth and upon which the soul will be judged
  after death." (p. 194, folio 169, n. 2 on 150).
- **The Sikh panth named as a third category.** "This mention by an eighteenth-century Muslim poet of
  the distinctive 'path' (panth) of the Sikhs as distinct from both Muslims and Hindus is very
  unusual" (p. 189, folio 164, n. 3 on 133); and "Taken to be an allusion to the Sikhs, whose guerilla
  horsemen ultimately destroyed the Mughal authority in Panjab." (p. 193, folio 168, n. 35 on 143).
- **Religious education.** "Literally 'studied a quarter (pā paṛhiāñ),' explained by S as 'those who
  have studied [only] a quarter [of one of the thirty sections (sipārā) of the Qurʼan].'" (p. 189,
  folio 164, n. 1 on 132); "The word 'dunce' (kuṭīcal)… with the sense of 'a child who is regularly
  beaten for poor performance in class.'" (p. 180, folio 155, n. 1 on 92); "HAFIZ (Ar. Ḥāfiẓ) title of
  those who have memorized the Qurʼān by heart." (p. 198, folio 173).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

All attributed as the book attributes them — mostly to "the commentators", to "the most popular
understanding", or to Varis Shah's *Hīr*.

- **Bullhe Shah dances before Shah Inayat to regain his favour, reported as the popular reading, not
  as the translator's own.** "The most popular understanding of the verse is that it refers to an
  actual incident in which Bullhe Shah, having fallen out with Shah Inayat, dressed up as a dancing
  girl and regained his master's favor with his performance. Compare 5, 77, 126, 149." (p. 182, folio
  157, n. 4 on 105); repeated as "the famous story of Bullhe Shah dressing up as a dancing girl to win
  Shah Inayat's favor" (p. 187, folio 162, n. 1 on 126) and "The verse is sometimes taken to be a
  direct reference to the story of Bullhe Shah dancing before Shah Inayat in order to win his favor."
  (p. 194, folio 169, n. 3 on 149).
- **The Five Pirs intervene miraculously for Ranjha.** "Five great saints who are believed
  collectively to possess special powers. Their miraculous interventions on behalf of Ranjha are a
  notable feature of Varis Shah's Hīr" (p. 186, folio 161, n. 2 on 122).
- **Bayazid Bistami's austerities separate body from spirit** (p. 178, folio 153, n. 10 on 78).
- **Bu Ali Qalandar spends twelve years immersed in a river** (p. 192, folio 167, n. 17 on 143).
- **Ibrahim ibn Adham is tested repeatedly by his guide for anger; "The gall bladder is believed to be
  the seat of anger."** (p. 192, folio 167, n. 13 on 143).
- **Moses' white hand** — "when God restored the hand of Moses that had been burned on the orders of
  Pharaoh, it had been made miraculously brilliant. It is thus a symbol of spiritual powers." (p. 177,
  folio 152, n. 4 on 77).
- **The Prophet's ascension** — "On the night of his ascension (Ar. mi‘rāj) to heaven the prophet
  Muhammad was brought into the divine presence, mounted on the mysterious animal called Buraq, who
  had been led to him by the archangel Jibrail. According to Sufi understanding, the Prophet's
  heavenly ascension is an archetypal version of the mystical process." (p. 190, folio 165, n. 8 on
  137).
- **The infant Husain attended by Gabriel** — "is believed to have been attended upon as an infant by
  the archangel Gabriel" (p. 184, folio 159, n. 9 on 110).
- **The primordial light of Muhammad** — "Understood by S as the primordial light of Muhammad that
  preceded the creation of Adam." (p. 178, folio 153, n. 2 on 80); and the divine Tradition "lau lāka
  lamā khalaqtu ʼl-aflāk, 'If it were not for you [I should not have created] the heavens.'" (pp.
  194–195, folios 169–170, n. 3 on 151).
- **God's promise to the souls** — "An allusion to the belief that when God was putting the souls into
  bodies, he promised that he would come with them into the world." (p. 188, folio 163, n. 1 on 129).
- **Majnun sustained for a year in a well** — "Majnun could not bear to emerge from a well after Laila
  had bathed in it one day. He received miraculous sustenance during the year he spent there."
  (p. 192, folio 167, n. 18 on 143).
- **Namrud killed by a mosquito** — "Namrud believed himself to be the equal of God but died after
  being bitten by a mosquito." (p. 192, folio 167, n. 27 on 143).
- **Harnakash killed at the palace gates** — "He believed that he was invulnerable, both in[text-layer?][in-]side
  and outside of his magnificent palace, but met his death at the palace gates." (p. 193, folio 168,
  n. 31 on 143).
- **Sahti's snake-bite ruse** — "Sahti made it appear that Hir had been bitten by a snake so that
  Ranjha could be asked to cure her." (p. 187, folio 162, n. 14 on 122).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The translator runs a running textual-critical apparatus keyed to sigla `R`, `Q`, `S`, `F`, `J`
  and `PS`, and the key to those sigla is not in this chunk.** Examples as printed: "This poem is not
  in R, and is omitted from PS. Most editions derive from Q." (p. 173, folio 148, n. 1 on 60); "This
  poem is included in Q, S, and J, but omitted from F." (p. 179, folio 154, n. 1 on 86); "This
  fragmentary poem is not included in the earliest editions, R and Q." (p. 185, folio 160, n. 1 on
  118); "S omits this verse." (p. 193, folio 168, n. 32 on 143).
- **Authorship is treated as genuinely unsettled between Bullhe Shah and Shah Husain.** "This kāfī,
  with its unusually irregular rhyme scheme, is generally considered to be by Bullhe Shah and is
  included as such in Q, F, and J, although it is absent from R. As with kāfī 29, its exclusion from S
  as the work of Shah Husain cannot be taken as being fully established." (p. 173, folio 148, n. 1 on
  63). The same doubt is raised for 130 (p. 188, folio 163) and for 145 (p. 193, folio 168).
- **Hagiography is diagnosed as a cause of textual growth.** Of kāfī 108: "This unusual kāfī is
  probably overconfidently described in PS as a rare instance 'where a classic poem depicts a true
  incident of life.' Verse 108 is substantially identical to 31. It seems likely that this kāfī is a
  later hagiographic elaboration than 31 of the familiar theme of the disparity in outward status
  between the Sayyid poet and his revered master from the lower-ranking Arain caste. Its genuineness
  is anyway open to question on stylistic grounds" (p. 183, folio 158, n. 1 on 108). Of the kāfī
  "ishk sharhā dā jhagṛā pai giā": "It has not been included in any of the subsequent standard
  editions of Bullhe Shah's poetry, unlike the similarly sourced 108, which fulfills a more satisfying
  hagiographic need." (p. 176, folio 151, n. 1 on 72). **This is the translator's explicit argument
  that the biographical legend shaped the canon**, and it bears directly on how the dancing-girl story
  should be used.
- **Three passages are called possible later interpolations**, each on a different ground: the
  Hir–Ranjha narrative material in 122, "less characteristic of the Sufi lyric than of narrative
  treatments of the legend, like the famous Hīr by Varis Shah, and may be insertions by later
  imitators" (p. 186, folio 161, n. 1 on 122); the invitation to Baha ud Din Zakariya's tomb, which
  "strikes a strange note, and the verse may be a later interpolation" (p. 186, folio 161, n. 5 on
  122); and the Sikh *panth* verse, whose unusualness arouses "the suspicion that the verse may be a
  later interpolation" (p. 189, folio 164, n. 3 on 133).
- **Irregular rhyme is used as evidence of bad transmission.** "The rhyme scheme of this kāfī is very
  irregular, suggesting that the text has been unreliably transmitted." (p. 176, folio 151, n. 1 on
  74); "The text of this verse is very uncertain, and the meaning is doubtful in places." (p. 178,
  folio 153, n. 10 on 78).
- **Doublets are traced to separate oral recordings.** "This kāfī's rhyme –āroñ is shared with 130,
  and the refrains of the two poems are virtually identical. It seems likely that they are variants of
  a single original that have been separately recorded in R from the oral tradition. Compare the
  similar overlap between 49 and 50." (p. 188, folio 163, n. 1 on 131).
- **The translator repeatedly declines to resolve a meaning**, a pattern worth recording as a measure
  of the edition's caution: "the sense of the verse is obscure" (p. 178, folio 153, n. 3 on 78); "The
  significance of this particular tree (jindbaṛ) is uncertain." (p. 176, folio 151, n. 4 on 74); "The
  meaning of the rhyme word tārotār is uncertain." (p. 177, folio 152, n. 5 on 76); "It is unclear who
  these four are intended to represent, although PS offers a Radhasoami interpretation" (p. 188, folio
  163, n. 2 on 127); "It is not clear who exactly is meant here." (p. 189, folio 164, n. 4 on 133);
  "The phrase apṇā bhāṇā kardā… is of uncertain meaning." (p. 195, folio 170, n. 5 on 152); "the whole
  verse is somewhat obscure" (p. 180, folio 155, n. 2 on 96).
- **Editorial practice stated once in the open.** "Since the refrain is here needed to complete the
  sense of this verse, it has exceptionally been printed throughout this poem." (p. 179, folio 154,
  n. 1 on 88).
- **A commentator, `S`, is quoted as an interpretive authority repeatedly and sometimes contradicted.**
  "'Mothers' (māīāñ) is understood by S as 'those deluded by illusion (māiā).'" (p. 174, folio 149,
  n. 9 on 64); "So interpreted by S, but the rhyme word tāre (here translated as 'holes') normally
  means 'strings.'" (p. 182, folio 157, n. 5 on 104).
- **Language choice is read as thematic, not accidental.** "In keeping with its theme, the language of
  the kāfī is Hindi; see 3." (p. 174, folio 149, n. 1 on 66); "The Hindu imagery of this poem is
  typically reinforced by a preponderance of Hindi vocabulary." (p. 184, folio 159, n. 1 on 113).

## Doubts

- **Glossary / index headword test — the result, stated explicitly.** The chunk carries the Glossary's
  **A–Q run only** (pp. 198–199, folios 173–174), ending at the headword `qadiri`; the rest of the
  Glossary, the Bibliography and any Index fall past p. 199 and are **not testable from this chunk**.
  Within the letters actually covered:
  - **ABSENT as headwords**: `Auqaf`, `dargah`, `endowment`, `faqir`/`fakir`, `karamat`, `khanqah`,
    `langar`, `land`, `mazar`, `mela`, `murshid`, `offerings`, `pilgrimage`, `qalandar`, `qawwali`.
    Every one of these begins with a letter the run covers, and none of them is a headword.
  - **PRESENT as headwords, and directly relevant**: `PIR (P. Pīr) Sufi spiritual guide.` (p. 199,
    folio 174) — the only institutional office glossed; `qadiri (Qādirī) Sufi order with which Shah
    Inayat and Bullhe Shah were affiliated.` (p. 199, folio 174) — the only order glossed;
    `Kaaba (Ar. Ka‘ba) the shrine in Mecca that is the goal of the hajj.` (p. 199, folio 174) — the
    words "shrine" and "pilgrimage"/"hajj" occur here **inside a definition, not as headwords**;
    `arain`, `HAFIZ`, `bahaval` (a saint's title) are the other institutionally loaded entries.
  - **NOT TESTABLE from this chunk** (letters R–Z, past the chunk boundary): `revenue`, `sajjada
    nashin`, `shrine`, `tomb`, `ʿurs`, `wakf`, `ziarat`, and also `Sayyid`, `Sohni`, `Sassi`, `Shah
    Inayat` (under S), `Varis Shah`, `ziarat`.
  - **So the finding, on the evidence available here, matches the two earlier books**: this apparatus
    glosses persons, castes, tribes, legends and scripture densely, and glosses exactly two
    institutional terms (`PIR`, `qadiri`) — but **no term for a shrine, its custodianship, its
    festival, its endowment or its economy appears as a headword in the A–Q run**. The remaining
    letters and the Index must be tested from the next chunk before this is reported as a whole-book
    result.
- **The `Shah Inayat` trap, and why I mapped it as I did.** The archive holds two rows — `shrine-of-shah-inayat-qadiri`
  (Shah Inayat Qadiri, Mozang, Lahore, d. c. 1728) and `dargah-roza-sufi-shah-inayat-shaheed` (Sufi
  Shah Inayat Shaheed, Jhok Sharif, Sindh, d. 1718). The book does **not** state a bare name: it states
  Lahore as "the home of Shah Inayat" (p. 185, folio 160), affiliation to the Qadiri order (p. 180,
  folio 155; p. 199, folio 174), and membership of the Arain caste (pp. 183, 195, 198; folios 158,
  170, 173). Those three together fit the Lahore row and exclude the Sindh row, so I mapped to
  `shrine-of-shah-inayat-qadiri`. **But the book never prints the name "Shah Inayat Qadiri", never
  names Mozang, never gives a death year and never mentions a tomb or a shrine for him anywhere in
  this chunk** — a human should confirm the identification against the book's introduction before any
  entry treats it as the book's own claim.
- **`shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht` vs. `shrine-of-jalaluddin-surkh-posh-bukhari`.** The
  book gives "Jalal ud Din Makhdum Jahaniyan of Uch" (p. 186, folio 161). The archive holds two Uch
  Sharif rows whose principal figures are both named Jalaluddin — Sayyid Jalaluddin Surkh-Posh Bukhari
  and Sayyid Jalaluddin (Makhdoom Jahaniyan Jahangasht). I mapped on the distinctive title "Makhdum
  Jahaniyan", but the book gives nothing else — no date, no tomb, no order — so the choice rests on
  the title alone and should be checked.
- **Gorakhnath is the principal figure of two archive rows.** The index names "Guru Gorakhnath (Nath
  yogis)" for `tilla-jogian` and "Guru Gorakhnath" for `gorakhnath-goraknath-temple` (Gor Khatri,
  Peshawar). I put his bullets under `tilla-jogian` because the book names Tilla Jogian in the same
  note run as the Kanphat yogis' headquarters (p. 187, folio 162). **The book says nothing about
  Peshawar or Gor Khatri**; the consolidator may want the same two bullets duplicated onto
  `gorakhnath-goraknath-temple`, but that is a judgement I have not made.
- **Toponym rule, written reversibly.** If the unruled toponym convention reverses, these bare
  toponyms would take rows: **Lahore** (pp. 176, 183; folios 151, 158) — candidate rows include
  `data-darbar`, `madho-lal-hussain`, `shrine-of-mian-mir`, `shah-jamal` and the other Lahore rows,
  with no way to choose between them from this chunk; **Peshawar** (p. 183, folio 158) — candidates

  `panj-tirath`, `kali-bari-mandir`, `wadpagga-sharif`; **Multan** at p. 186 (folio 161) is *not* bare
