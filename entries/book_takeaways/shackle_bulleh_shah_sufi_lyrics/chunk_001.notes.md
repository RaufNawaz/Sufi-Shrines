## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Samāʿ at shrines, and custodianship by lineal descendants — the single most archive-usable
  passage in the chunk.** "Persian ghazals were sung in musical performances (called samā‘) that
  were a central part of ritual practice and usually took place at the shrines constructed around
  the tombs of former saints, which were themselves typically administered by a master's lineal
  descendants. They employed the specialist professional musicians known as qawwals. Their
  strongly rhythmic style of performance (called qawwali) has become a well-recog-nized form of
  world music while continuing to be practiced in its original setting.8" (p. 11, folio x). The
  endnote sends the reader to "Qureshi 1986; for the local context of Sufi music and its
  performers, see Pannke 1999" (p. 24, folio xxiii).
- **The master–disciple circle as the transmitting institution.** "Besides transmission of
  distinctive spiritual disciplines within the circle of disciples formed around a master (called
  Shaikh or Pir), the Sufi message reached a wider audience through poetry." (p. 11, folio x).
  "A strong Sufi presence was rapidly established with the arrival of charismatic fig­ures
  [text-layer?] associated with the main Sufi orders." (p. 11, folio x).
- **The Shattari order named, and a named doctrine.** "the Sufi doctrine of a disciple's
  self-obliteration in the master,18" (p. 18, folio xvii), glossed at note 18 as "(Ar. fanā fī
  'l-shaiḳh) … given particular importance within the Shattari order with which Shah Inayat was
  affiliated" (p. 24, folio xxiii). **This is the only order named in the whole chunk.**
- **The kāfī as a performance form, tied to qawwali and to amateur devotional singing.** "The main
  lyrical form is a strophic poem with a refrain, called kāfī, which is designed for singing in
  qawwali and also lends itself to solo singing by amateur devotees and professional performers."
  (p. 13, folio xii). Performance also shapes the text: "the well-known liberties taken by
  professional sing-ers and the uncertainties of the textual transmission" (p. 14, folio xiii).
- **Present-day circulation of the poems across communal lines.** "whether through performances by
  well-known Sikh or Muslim sing-ers or through the popular selections issued by Indian and
  Pakistani publishers" (p. 9, folio viii).
- **A fair (mela) named inside the poetry, with an explicitly economic metaphor.** "Come, fakirs,
  let us go to the fair, and listen to the music of the adept.1 … Without union the fair is no
  good; both capital and interest are squandered. Fakirhood is difficult, but it is the path of the
  lover" (kāfī 15, p. 38, folio 13).
- **The transmission and print history of the corpus.** The Sufi lyrics "were never systematically
  preserved by an or­ga­nized [text-layer?] faith community, and they were for the most part
  rec­orded [text-layer?] and assembled in printed collections only in the late nineteenth
  century." (p. 10, folio ix). The turn to print is dated to the colonial period: "Only with the
  British conquest of Panjab in the 1840s did the literary culture of the Sufi tradition come to be
  overlaid by new patterns of modernity. Persian was quite rapidly replaced as the language of
  education, administra-tion, and elite literature by Urdu and Eng­lish [text-layer?]" (pp. 11–12,
  folios x–xi).
- **The size of this edition's corpus.** "The present volume includes 157 of these lyrics, although
  the uncertain transmission of the text makes it dif  ­fi­cult [text-layer?] to be sure of the
  exact number of authentic items." (p. 13, folio xii).
- **Epigraphy at the tomb.** The Persian chronogram on Bullhe Shah's tomb giving "A.H. 1171"
  (p. 8, folio vii) is the only inscription named in the chunk; its text is (not stated).
- **Mosque ritual as the poems name it** — muezzin, call to prayer, prostration, mihrab: "Love
  acted as muezzin and gave the call to prayer. They get up and run there, since it is their
  religious duty. They perform their prostrations, then race home, where you have made your
  forehead their mihrab.4" (kāfī 8, p. 34, folio 9). And the objects of observance, listed to be
  destroyed: "Burn your prayer rug and break up your ablutions pot; do not pick up your rosary,
  your staff, or your stick. Lovers loudly proclaim, 'Give up halal food and eat dead meat.'3"
  (kāfī 19, p. 41, folio 16).
- **The religious professions the poems attack** — "the mullah and the qazi show me the road,
  setting me in a circuit of confusion… But these robbers are the world's fowlers, who spread their
  nets everywhere. They preach the duties and actions required by the law, and shackle my feet in
  chains." (kāfī 13, p. 37, folio 12). Shackle calls these "his occasional pungent attacks on the
  dishonesty of contemporary religious special-ists" (p. 15, folio xiv).
- **The madrasa curriculum, named by its textbooks.** "I have studied logic and semantics, the
  Kanz and the Qudūrī,2 but all this learning has destroyed me, my girl." (kāfī 18, p. 40, folio
  15). **The chunk gives no gloss on either title** — the endnote is outside this chunk.
- **Hindu and yogic practice as the poems name it** — the conch, the pandit and the Veda, the
  temple, sacred bathing places: "I went to enter the temple of the lord, where a thousand conches
  are blown" (kāfī 19, p. 40, folio 15); "Behold, God is openly revealed, whatever the pandit may
  then proclaim from the Veda… Oh yogi, when you are united with God, you give the call to prayer
  though you sound the conch." (kāfī 20, p. 41, folio 16). The yogi's kit: "Wearing a deerskin3 and
  carrying a skull, I went to beg for a sight of you. Calling myself a yogini, I wore my hair long
  and rubbed ashes on my body." (kāfī 8, p. 33, folio 8).
- **The girl's dowry and the spinning wheel as the standard image of preparation for death.**
  Shackle: several of the long­est [text-layer?] poems "develop this memento mori theme with
  metaphorical reference to the typical life cycle of a girl who grows up in the security of her
  parental home but must duti-fully spin the thread that will make up the clothes she will take
  with her as dowry after she has been married and goes to live with her husband's family"
  (p. 16, folio xv). In the poem itself: "You have not even started spinning thread on the wheel.
  You have no dowry prepared, so what will you do?" (kāfī 2, p. 28, folio 3).
- **Burial imagery.** "One day you must leave the world and go to be interred in the grave. The
  worms will eat your flesh. Be aware and do not forget death." (kāfī 2, p. 28, folio 3).
- **A social world turned upside down, itemized.** "The true get shoved aside, while the false are
  given seats of honor. Former leaders sit in destitution, while carpets are spread for those who
  were at the back. Those who wore rough blankets have been made kings, while former kings beg for
  alms." (kāfī 4, pp. 31–32, folios 6–7). Also kāfī 10 on grain theft and unequal justice: "Khaira
  profits and Jumma3 gets beaten; the wrong person gets the summons." (p. 35, folio 10).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The dancing-girl story, given by Shackle as "the most popular tale of Bullhe Shah" and
  explicitly hedged.** "The poetry also bears out the gist of the most popular tale of Bullhe Shah,
  of how he once fell out of favor with his guide, **allegedly** for being too outspoken in his
  criticisms of formal Islam, and was dismissed from his company. Since Bullhe Shah knew of Shah
  Inayat's fondness for music and dancing, he **is then said to have** gone away and taken lessons
  with a dancing girl, then reappeared in Lahore one day, himself dressed as a dancing girl. There
  he danced and sang in front of Shah Inayat, who was won over by his performance and readmitted
  him to his favor" (pp. 18–19, folios xvii–xviii). Shackle's warrant is the poetry itself, quoted
  immediately after: "Bullha, let us go and sit at the gate of Shah Inayat, who made me dress in
  green and red." (p. 19, folio xviii). **No miracle is claimed for the saint anywhere in the
  chunk** — this tale is the only hagiographic narrative in it.
- **The modern iconography of the dancing saint.** Endnote 19: "The image of Bullhe Shah dancing is
  a popular one in modern iconography; see Frembgen 2006: 106–107." (p. 24, folio xxiii).
- **Legends the poems invoke rather than tell**, all attributed by the book to Islamic or Indic
  legend and not to any saint of the archive: Mansur executed for saying *anā 'l-haq* (p. 20,
  folio xix); Shams "hanged upside down.There [text-layer?]" (kāfī 12, p. 36, folio 11); Yusuf put
  in the well and sold in the bazaar (kāfī 98 quoted p. 20, folio xix; kāfī 12, p. 36, folio 11);
  Sulaiman's throne "which used to fly high in the air" (kāfī 2, p. 30, folio 5); Moses at Sinai,
  "With the reprimand You shall not see me,4 you uttered your command" (kāfī 12, p. 36, folio 11);
  Ranjha's transformation into a yogi by Gorakhnath's initiation (p. 21, folio xx).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The book's stated corrective purpose.** Twentieth-century readings of Bullhe Shah were
  "increas-ingly in­flu­enced [text-layer?] by the nationalist thinking prevalent among the new
  middle class of the colonial period"; he "became subject to a va­ri­ety [text-layer?] of
  interpreta-tions.11 … Most of these interpretations may be shown to rely upon often highly
  selective readings of his poetry, which are used to support already assumed ideological
  positions. The present volume may help correct the biases that have too often continued to
  distort un­der­stand­ings [text-layer?] of Bullhe Shah." (pp. 12–13, folios xi–xii). The four
  readings he lists: "an exponent of universal spiritual truths owing as much to Vedanta as to
  Sufism; an authen-tic spokesman of the Panjabi folk tradition; a social revolu-tionary; or simply
  a Romantic poet avant la lettre.12" (p. 12, folio xi).
- **Panjabi Sufi poetry and Sikh hymnody are two distinct traditions, separately transmitted.**
  "In the case of Panjabi, these two ­genres [text-layer?] of religious poetry are represented by
  two distinct traditions,3 which have been quite separately preserved and transmitted." (pp. 9–10,
  folios viii–ix). The shared feature is "their common preference for popular poetic styles that
  draw upon folk-songs rather than the learned poetry of the courts.4" (p. 10, folio ix) — but the
  asymmetry is the argument: the Gurus' hymns "may to a considerable extent be legitimately
  understood in their own terms", whereas Panjabi Sufi poetry "is hardly to be understood properly
  without wider reference to the larger religious and literary traditions by which it is so
  intimately informed" (p. 10, folio ix).
- **Sufism is not a syncretic exception but an integral part of Islam — Shackle's flat corrective.**
  "This means un­der­stand­ing [text-layer?] that Sufism in India is no exception to the general
  rule that Sufism is and always has been an integral part of Islam." (p. 10, folio ix). He presses
  it again on the poetry: "The core expression of this vision is again very Islamic, relying upon
  the repeated citation of a number of Qur'anic phrases and other sacred sayings in their original
  Arabic wording" (p. 17, folio xvi).
- **Persian, not Panjabi, is the frame.** "While the use of local languages by Sufi poets has helped
  to ensure their continuing popularity across religious bound-aries today, it should be remembered
  that their activity took place within a literary culture whose principal language was Persian.9"
  (p. 11, folio x).
- **Against the reading of Bullhe Shah as a social revolutionary — an explicit proportion
  argument.** "Bullhe Shah's critique of the social upheavals in early eigh­teenth-century
  [text-layer?] Panjab, which is so interesting to many modern readers, represents only a very
  small, if vividly expressed, part of his poetry. Like his occasional pungent attacks on the
  dishonesty of contemporary religious special-ists, verses expressing social critique are quite
  subsidiary in quantity to the poems of admonition designed to arouse a very Islamic sense of the
  inevitability of death" (pp. 15–16, folios xiv–xv). The didactic poetry is in turn "subsidi-ary to
  the mystical vision that is its principal theme" (pp. 16–17, folios xv–xvi) — a three-tier
  hierarchy Shackle builds deliberately, "begin[ning] at the bottom".
- **Textual authenticity treated as irrecoverable in detail but secure in the whole.** "Absolute
  authenticity in the precise wording of any poem generally agreed to be by Bullhe Shah is hardly
  to be expected… But while its boundaries cannot be de­fined [text-layer?] with absolute
  preci-sion,14 the corpus that has come down to us provides ample evidence of a powerfully
  coherent poetical and mystical imagination." (p. 14, folio xiii). Note 14 names the disputed
  items: "Some attributions are doubtful on grounds of style, e.g., 35; of content, e.g., 108, or of
  authorship in the case of 29, 63, 130, and 145, which are also at­trib­uted [text-layer?] to Shah
  Husain. The in­de­pen­dent [text-layer?] sta­tus of some poems is questionable, as in the case of
  the close pairs 49–50 and 130–131." (p. 24, folio xxiii).
- **The Hindu–Muslim unity poems placed in context rather than celebrated.** "his central perception
  of the meaninglessness of outward diversity inspired those memorable poems on the essential unity
  of 'Turk' and 'Hindu' that have acquired such a special sig­nifi­cance [text-layer?] in modern
  South Asia" (p. 18, folio xvii) — set against his earlier warning that "fuller un­der­stand­ings
  [text-layer?] depend upon proper attention being given to those differences between bhakti and
  Sufi poetry, which, despite sig­nifi­cant [text-layer?] commonalities, are by no means identical
  in their fundamental presuppositions" (p. 9, folio viii).
- **A stated limit on the existing scholarship.** "There is no very satisfactory his­tory
  [text-layer?] of Panjabi literature in Eng­lish [text-layer?], but compare Sekhon 1993–96. Studies
  of Bullhe Shah in Eng­lish [text-layer?], **none of which is at all comprehensive or very
  critical**, include Usborne 1982 [first published c. 1905], Rama Krishna 1938: 40–71, and Kohli
  1987." (p. 24, folio xxiii).
- **The translation policy, stated.** "The present translation tries only to convey the sense of the
  poetry in a consistent style of plain Eng­lish [text-layer?] prose that aims for a middle path
  between off-put­ting [text-layer?] formality and jarring colloquialism… Italics are used to mark
  Bullhe Shah's citation of Qur'anic verses and other Arabic sayings.28" (p. 23, folio xxii), with
  note 28: "The translations of Qur'anic verses are based on Yusuf Ali 1977." (p. 25, folio xxiv).
  **Shackle also warns that the formal unity of the kāfī is lost in this translation**: "the formal
  unity generated in the original language by the rhyme scheme announced in the refrain is hardly
  apparent in the Eng­lish [text-layer?] prose translations presented in this book" (p. 15, folio
  xiv).

## Doubts

- **The folio rule supplied for this run is wrong for PDF 26 and 27, and the pages prove it.** The
  run sheet says "PDF 1–27 = front matter, printing lower-case roman folios, roman value = PDF − 1",
  which would make p. 26 = xxv and p. 27 = xxvi. The chunk shows that **the roman sequence stops at
  xxiv on p. 25** (the last page of the introduction's endnotes), that **p. 26 is the half-title
  `Sufi Lyrics` and p. 27 is blank, neither printing any number**, and that **p. 28 prints 3**.
  Since the arabic rule PDF − 25 gives p. 26 = 1 and p. 27 = 2, the arabic sequence in fact begins
  at PDF 26 as unprinted folios 1 and 2, exactly as the Contents' "SUFI LYRICS 1" requires (p. 6).
  **Correct reading: roman i–xxiv = PDF 2–25 (printed only from vii/p. 8); arabic 1–180 = PDF
  26–205.** Recorded as the third derived-rule failure of 18 September.
- **The Contents' page numbers may not line up with the run sheet's blank/unnumbered leaves.** The
  Contents (p. 6) puts "abbreviations 127" and "notes to the translation 129", which by PDF − 25
  are PDF 152 and PDF 154 — but the run sheet states that **PDF 152–154 print no number**. Either
  those leaves are section openers that suppress their folio (normal in this series) or the offset
  shifts somewhere after p. 41. **A human should check PDF 150–155 directly** before the
  consolidator cites any note by page.
- **The Shah Inayat mapping is the live trap in this book and it is a judgement, not a fact the
  book makes.** The index holds two rows — `shrine-of-shah-inayat-qadiri` (Shah Inayat Qadiri,
  Mozang Chungi, Lahore, d. c. 1728, Qadri Shattari) and `dargah-roza-sufi-shah-inayat-shaheed`
  (Sufi Shah Inayat Shaheed, Jhok Sharif, Sindh, d. 1718). **I mapped to the Lahore row** on three
  things the book itself states and nothing else: the death year "(d. 1728)" (p. 8, folio vii), the
  scene set "in Lahore" (p. 19, folio xviii), and the "Shattari order with which Shah Inayat was
  affiliated" (p. 24, folio xxiii) — all three fit the Lahore row and all three exclude the Sindh
  row (d. 1718, Jhok Sharif). **But the book never writes "Qadiri", never names Mozang, never calls
  him a shrine's saint, and never says where he is buried** — every one of those is (not stated).
  The Lahore of the dancing-girl story is where Bullhe Shah is said to have *performed*, which is
  not the same as where the master lived or is buried. **A human must confirm this mapping before
  any fact above enters `shrine-of-shah-inayat-qadiri`;** if it is reversed, five bullets move.
