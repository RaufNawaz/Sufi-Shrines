# shackle_bulleh_shah_sufi_lyrics — chunk 003 — pp. 75–108 (folios 50–83)

Continuous text of the numbered lyrics, English translation only, with endnote reference markers but
**no note text, no headnote and no translator's prose anywhere in the chunk**. The running head of
every page is `SUFI LYRICS`. Poems **58** (p. 75, folio 50) through **100** (p. 108, folio 83) fall
here; poem 58 begins mid-page, so poem 57 is in an earlier chunk, and poem 100 closes on the last
page of the chunk.

**The folio rule holds on every one of the 34 pages.** The printed number sits alone at the foot of
each page and equals PDF − 25 throughout: p. 75 → `50`, p. 83 → `58`, p. 95 → `70`, p. 108 → `83`,
with no break. Poem numbers are set alone at the *head* of a poem and are always higher than the
page's folio in this chunk (by 8 at p. 75, by 17 at p. 108), so the two are separable everywhere —
with one value collision noted under Doubts.

**No original Panjabi script and no transliteration appear in this chunk** — English only, so the
protocol's "quote in the original script" requirement cannot be met for any name, date or place
below. Dates: **none are printed anywhere in the chunk** — no death year, no ʿurs date, no building
date, no era.

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`** (the book names him "Bullha" and "Bullhe Shah"): **the poet signs
  himself inside the closing line of most poems, in two interchangeable forms.** "Bullha" at
  pp. 75, 76, 77, 78, 80, 81, 82, 86, 87, 88, 90, 92, 93, 94, 95, 96, 98, 99, 101, 102, 103, 104,
  105, 106, 108 (folios 50, 51, 52, 53, 55, 56, 57, 61, 62, 63, 65, 67, 68, 69, 70, 71, 73, 74, 76,

  p. 100 (folio 75) and p. 101 (folio 76). Both are used of the same speaking voice — "Bullhe Shah,
  this is your delusion" (p. 100, folio 75) against "Bullha, no one gets to stay here" (p. 78,
  folio 53).
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **he calls himself a fakir, and the fakir's blanket is his emblem.**
  "Bullhe Shah is a fakir of the lord; he disturbs those who are awake" (p. 79, folio 54). "Get rid
  of your white shawl, girl, and put on the fakirs' blanket. The white shawl will get covered with
  stains, but there is no stain on the blanket.2" (p. 75, folio 50). "This love and this life of a
  fakir are true" (p. 104, folio 79); "The quilt of Sufi poverty has been stitched by the thread of
  love" (p. 104, folio 79).
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **his name is said to be given him by, or merged into, his master.**
  "My whole being became Inayat; then it got the name Bullha" (p. 93, folio 68); "Here he is given
  the name of Bullha, with the screen of earthliness7 placed between us" (p. 105, folio 80).
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **he claims the fourth station of the Sufi path for his own speech.**
  "Bullhe Shah now speaks of reality.1 He examines the truth of the law and the way. He reveals the
  secret of the fourth state.2" (pp. 85–86, folios 60–61).
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **he describes himself as unlettered and beaten, "Dunce" being the
  name he gives himself.** "'Dunce'1 is my name, oh. The mullah taught me the lesson, but I could
  not get beyond alif.2 I suffered a beating with his slippers" (p. 102, folio 77), and in the same
  poem he is thrown out by in-laws, grandparents' people and his father's house (p. 102, folio 77).
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **he sets caste aside and gives the lord to both communities.** "He
  dyes you and me in the ocean of unity, and does not ask what your caste2 is" (p. 84, folio 59);
  "He is both Turk and Hindu,4 there is no one else" (p. 92, folio 67); "It is he who has proclaimed
  the Veda and the Qurʼan [text-layer?]" (p. 81, folio 56).
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **nothing in this chunk touches the site.** Kasur, a tomb, a grave, an
  ʿurs, a sajjada nashin, a date of death and a silsila are all **(not stated)**. Everything above
  comes from the signature lines of the lyrics themselves.
- **`shrine-of-shah-inayat-qadiri`** (the book names him "Shah Inayat", "Inayat" and once "Inayat
  Qadiri"): **he is named as the poet's master and his religion, five times.** "I do not just speak
  of Shah Inayat with my lips, I search for your face in every direction" (p. 84, folio 59); "Shah
  Inayat is my religion, the one whom we accept in this world and the next" (p. 90, folio 65); "My
  lord is Shah Inayat. It is he who delivers me across" (p. 101, folio 76), in the same breath as
  "He found true guidance when he took hold of the true guide" (p. 101, folio 76); "My whole being
  became Inayat; then it got the name Bullha" (p. 93, folio 68).
- **`shrine-of-shah-inayat-qadiri`**: **his departure is named as the cause of the poet's
  difficulty.** "With the departure of Inayat Qadiri,1 my story has again become difficult"
  (p. 104, folio 79). **This single line, printing the nisba "Qadiri", is the only thing in the
  chunk that distinguishes which Shah Inayat is meant — see Doubts, where the competing archive row
  is named. The mapping is made on that word alone and is reversible.** The chunk gives no place,
  no date, no tomb, no order beyond that one word, and no account of the "departure": all **(not
  stated)**.
- **`shrine-of-shah-shams-ud-din-sabzwari`** (the book names him "Shah Shams"): **named once, in a
  list of martyred lovers, as flayed alive.** "You had Shah Shams2 flayed alive. You had Mansur3
  sent to the gallows. You set the saw to Zakariya's4 head" (p. 105, folio 80). The flaying recurs
  unattributed two poems later: "Over there some have their skins flayed,6 some are cut up with
  saws,7 others are seized and put on the gallows.8" (p. 107, folio 82). **The evidence is the bare
  name plus the manner of death; no place, date or order is given, and endnote 2 is not in this
  chunk — see Doubts.**

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The fakirs' blanket as the garment of the path, set against the white shawl.** "Get rid of your
  white shawl, girl, and put on the fakirs' blanket. The white shawl will get covered with stains,
  but there is no stain on the blanket.2" (p. 75, folio 50). The black blanket is also the
  herdsman-disguise's mark: "a black blanket on his shoulders; light dwells in the eyes" (p. 77,
  folio 52).
- **A fakirs' communal meal, described as a gathering.** "The fakirs have come together and held a
  party, where every crumb is eaten" (p. 99, folio 74), in the same poem as "Better than Bullha is
  a hearth2 on which food is cooked" (p. 99, folio 74).
- **Shrine visitation on a named day of the week — the only shrine practice in the chunk.** "On
  Monday I go to the shrine and pray to the prophets and saints. When I gain a vision of the
  beloved, I adorn myself" (p. 90, folio 65). **No shrine is named.** The same poem runs a weekday
  scheme: "Friday brings a special delight… Monday1 gave us pains; on Tuesday we gained no
  understanding. We were sacrificed to love on Saturday. Wednesday2 removed our awareness"
  (p. 90, folio 65).
- **Pilgrimage relocated to the self.** "They are lost in error, lord. Now the place of pilgrimage
  is correctly placed right beside us" (p. 81, folio 56).
- **Brahman ritual economy, itemised — the densest institutional passage in the chunk.** "The
  Brahmans2 have come to scare their patrons. They make them anxious by telling them how their
  ancestors suffer" (p. 79, folio 54); the demand as quoted speech: "'Your ancestors are in pain.
  Make offerings of raw sugar, rice, and cloth. Undo your shirt and put on your sacred thread.'
  Then they are quickly given a handful of Ganges water" (pp. 79–80, folios 54–55); and the verdict:
  "This is not how suffering is removed, with cash, rupees, vessels, and cattle" (p. 80, folio 55).
- **Image worship and a temple wedding procession, described as manufacture.** "First the image of
  the goddess Chandi4 was made… Then the great bridegroom's5 procession went out. The palanquin has
  come with lots of loud noise" (p. 80, folio 55); "People mistakenly think they are divine and bow
  their heads before the idols. They were the ones who made them, yet they feel no shame at all"
  (p. 80, folio 55); "the sacred basil is made the mother. It is married to the ammonite sacred to
  Vishnu. It is put into the bridal palanquin with rejoicing" (p. 80, folio 55).
- **The guru as an institution, given a whole poem (poem 65, pp. 81–82, folios 56–57).** "The guru
  does whatever he wants" (p. 81, folio 56); "When I clung to the guru, I became aware that the
  goods that were lost had been regained" (p. 81, folio 56); "The guru calls himself God. The guru
  appears as Ali4 and the Prophet. The guru dwells in the hearts of everyone" (p. 82, folio 57).
- **The guide (murshid) alongside it.** "Who but the guide directs us?" (p. 85, folio 60); "it is
  the crimson of the guide, my friend" (p. 91, folio 66); "He found true guidance when he took hold
  of the true guide" (p. 101, folio 76).
- **The four stations of the path, named in English throughout.** "The law7 is our nurse, the way is
  our mother. Next came reality, and we gained something real from gnosis" (p. 93, folio 68); "He
  examines the truth of the law and the way. He reveals the secret of the fourth state.2" (p. 86,
  folio 61); "Truth destroys the law" and "It is like the law for the follower of the way" (p. 85,
  folio 60); "Taking me from the law to freedom from the law" (p. 88, folio 63).
- **Prayer, fasting and the two-prostration prayer, repeatedly named and repeatedly set aside.**
  "Praying and fasting and prayers of two prostrations4 are forgotten, when the distiller5 gives me
  a cup of liquor" (p. 83, folio 58); "Are prayer and fasting to be performed by one who has drunk
  wine and become intoxicated?" (p. 86, folio 61); "Bullha forgot the prayer of two prostrations4
  once he heard the music play" (p. 91, folio 66); "I do not study the principles of logic and
  semantics, obligations, duties, the law, and extra rituals" (p. 90, folio 65).
- **Mosque and formal worship named once, as something the divine itself does.** "He gets dressed
  and becomes a creature, and enters the mosque and worships" (p. 81, folio 56).
- **The night watchman's gong as timekeeping.** "Get rid of the gong beater.1 Today the beloved has
  come to my house… He strikes the gong at every quarter.2 He is shortening our night together"
  (pp. 82–83, folios 57–58).
- **Music: the unstruck sound, the instrument-less dance, the flute.** "the door that leads to the
  unstruck,4" (p. 82, folio 57); "The unstruck music3 plays sweetly. The capable musician plays the
  melody and the tune" (p. 83, folio 58); "The body smitten by perfect love dances without a tune,
  without a beat" (p. 86, folio 61); "He does not desire any music or instrument, he just sits and
  plays in ecstasy" (p. 87, folio 62); "This flute that Krishna played inflicted a wound on my
  heart" (p. 97, folio 72); "show him your dancing,15" (p. 92, folio 67); "I have removed the veil
  from my face and danced" (p. 102, folio 77).
- **The distiller and the drinking house as a working trade.** "when the distiller5 gives me a cup
  of liquor" (p. 83, folio 58); "Right beside you is the distillers' house, where the thirsty drunks
  come. They drink full cups and goblets… Do not stand near the drunks, or you will be called one
  too" (p. 107, folio 82).
- **The spinning party (women's work-gathering) as the place where the saint is talked about.** "Let
  us go and see that wild one, of whom there is so much talk in the spinning parties.1 He dyes you
  and me in the ocean of unity, and does not ask what your caste2 is" (p. 84, folio 59). The
  spinning wheel recurs as a device of remembrance: "In this courtyard the spinning wheel looks
  good… I will remember my beloved with every turn of the spinning wheel" (p. 79, folio 54).
- **Caste named directly, once, and only to be dismissed** (p. 84, folio 59, quoted above).
- **The maktab and the mullah's discipline.** "The mullah taught me the lesson, but I could not get
  beyond alif.2 I suffered a beating with his slippers" (p. 102, folio 77); "I recognized alif,3 I
  recognized be, then te began my scriptural recitation. I recognized sīn,4 I recognized shīn"
  (p. 75, folio 50); "For all their learning, the Brahmans and mullahs are defeated" (p. 86,
  folio 61).
- **Spells, charms, magicians, the sacrificial fire and wild rue as named ritual technique.** "I
  performed many spells and charms, and many great magicians came" (p. 83, folio 58); "I will use
  spells and charms to win my dear beloved back. I will recite and blow this spell.2 I will use the
  sun to light the sacrificial fire" (p. 93, folio 68); "With love as my brazier and the stars as
  wild rue,3 I will offer the sun on the fire" (p. 94, folio 69); "Ask if it is magic or a shadow.1"
  (p. 96, folio 71).
- **Astrologer, doctor and apothecary as the recourses a sufferer goes to.** "Oh brother astrologer,
  just tell me the truth. Do not remain silent just because my fortune is bad" (p. 104, folio 79);
  "Is there any doctor or apothecary for me?" (p. 97, folio 72); "The cruel one takes my pulse, but
  then he runs away from me" (p. 104, folio 79).
- **Usury and dishonest trade named as the mark of a false Muslim.** "On whatever you give, you
  charge an extra 25 percent. You play a tricky game. Where did you discover how to be this sort of
  Muslim?" (p. 101, folio 76); "You greedily consume the wealth that belongs to others"
  (p. 101, folio 76); "You oppress people and torment them. You have stolen their food and snatched
  away their livelihood" (p. 98, folio 73).
- **Traders as a recurring figure of the relation with God.** "How will I get to see those robber
  traders?" (p. 76, folio 51); "A sign of the trader's7 house is The hand of God is over their
  hands.8" (p. 85, folio 60); "In my hand there is a basket of flowers; bring a trader here"
  (p. 104, folio 79).
- **The state's reach, named once.** "The constables pursued us there, so we came here. We do not
  get to stay here; where will we move in the future?" (p. 78, folio 53).
- **Crossing, fare and the boatmen — the economics of the ferry.** "I do not have even the smallest
  coin on me. I long to cross over to the other side. I am not on friendly terms with the boatmen"
  (p. 95, folio 70); "I have no pole or oar, and my raft is old. There are whirlpools, and no
  crossing place is marked" (p. 96, folio 71).
- **The graveyard as "the city of the silent", twice, with the angel of death as ferryman.** "You
  must go and settle in the city of the silent,1 into which the whole country is admitted. Full
  loads of passengers are taken across by the cruel ferryman, the angel of death" (p. 98, folio 73);
  repeated almost verbatim at p. 108 (folio 83), "Always keep your eyes on the city of the silent,1
  into which the whole world is admitted. Filling his boat with loads of passengers, the relentless
  ferryman who is the angel of death takes them across." Also "There is no awareness in the grave,
  so why this anguished thrashing about?1" (p. 78, folio 53).
- **The fair, the caravanserai and the departure drum as images of the lifespan.** "It is a fair
  that is over in a flash… You get one night's stay in this caravanserai. When you get here, do not
  spread yourself out. Tomorrow the departure drum sounds for everyone" (p. 99, folio 74).
- **Butchery and the goat, three times, as the figure of the disciple.** "The lover is the goat and
  the beloved is the butcher. Saying 'Me, me,'6 it is slaughtered" (p. 76, folio 51); "I am a goat
  among butchers" (p. 97, folio 72); "the lord is found by those who become a goat for the butcher"
  (p. 99, folio 74).
- **Bridal adornment itemised, then voided.** "There is henna on your hands and feet, and your hair
  is tied in a bridal plait. Perfumed with oil and flower essence, you chew betel, and your teeth
  are decorated with missī.1" (p. 106, folio 81); "You wear garlands and necklaces around your
  neck, bangles clink upon your wrists, and from your ears fine earrings hang clustered, but all
  this display is false" (p. 106, folio 81).
- **Renunciant marks worn by the divine in disguise.** "He has shaved his head5 and has entranced me
  in love. He wears cowrie shells6 in his ears" (p. 82, folio 57); "sometimes he is sent around
  begging with earthen cup and staff. Sometimes he is a master yogi, sometimes a hedonist"
  (p. 94, folio 69); "He is wearing a diadem3 and a forehead mark" (p. 96, folio 71).
- **Women drawing water in turn, as a picture of mortality.** "The women have all filled their
  vessels with water, each in their own turn. Some have got water and come, some have departed,
  while others are standing with outstretched arms" (p. 106, folio 81).
- **Gambling named once**: "You want a winning throw, but you get three ones.2" (p. 106, folio 81).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

The chunk attributes no miracle to any saint or site. What it has are martyrdom and ordeal stories
told as the poems tell them, plus the Hir–Ranjha material treated as fact about lovers.

- **Mansur revealed the secret and was hanged for it**: "It is necessary to study this science but
  unacceptable to reveal it. The one who did reveal it was Mansur,9 who was seized and put on the
  gallows" (p. 93, folio 68).
- **Three martyrdoms named in one breath, and attributed to God**: "You had Shah Shams2 flayed
  alive. You had Mansur3 sent to the gallows. You set the saw to Zakariya's4 head. What account is
  there left to settle?" (p. 105, folio 80).
- **The cry "I am God" reassigned away from Mansur**: "It was you who made him say I am God.3 It was
  not Mansur who cried out, people. Then you became the mullahs4 who stood near" (p. 106, folio 81).
- **Bayazid left his body**: "Bayazid10 was separated from his body and became a perfect adept"
  (p. 93, folio 68).
- **Hir became Ranjha**: "He was Hir and then became Ranjha. Very rare are the people who realize
  this. Once they do so, all disputes are resolved" (p. 105, folio 80).
- **The lord's power told as conjuring**: "You have hidden the stars under baskets.3 You have turned
  fiber ropes into snakes. I am sacrificed to all this magic" (p. 97, folio 72); "What a show the
  juggler has mounted; he has made me dance like a puppet" (p. 94, folio 69); "He is the actor who
  put on the show.5" (p. 82, folio 57).
- **The covenant before creation, told as an event**: "On the day of the covenant he announces Am I
  not? He desires They said, 'Yes, we testify.'3" (p. 81, folio 56); "Am I not your lord?7 was
  revealed. They said, 'Yes' was hung around their necks" (p. 91, folio 66); "I was smitten by love
  from the first. From the first, from the day of pre-eternity.1" (p. 103, folio 78).

## Arguments and interpretations

This chunk carries **no translator's prose at all** — no headnote, no introduction, no note text,
only the poem numbers and endnote reference markers. What follows is therefore the primary source's
own judgements, in its own voice.

- **Ritual and learning are worthless without vision, stated flatly and often.** "What use are minds
  trained in religious law? Nothing matters except seeing him" (p. 90, folio 65); "The Kanz and the
  Qudūrī,3 logic and semantics — all this knowledge is lost" (p. 86, folio 61); "No philosopher can
  tell you what true knowledge is. The intellect is your child.10" (p. 81, folio 56); "I have no
  belief at all in what the intellect says, when love cries out aloud.5" (p. 91, folio 66).
- **Priestcraft of both communities is attacked in the same poem.** The Brahmans "have come to scare
  their patrons" (p. 79, folio 54) and "For all their learning, the Brahmans and mullahs are
  defeated" (p. 86, folio 61).
- **Non-duality asserted as the resolution of religious dispute.** "Get rid of duality, there is no
  confusion. He is both Turk and Hindu,4 there is no one else. Call everyone a holy man, there is no
  thief" (p. 92, folio 67); "There is no separation between the tree and the garden… Duality is
  destroyed" (p. 80, folio 55); "While the heart is affected by duality, who can say 'My ego is
  dead'?" (p. 93, folio 68).
- **Self-accusation as a rhetorical move**: "Bullha, you have gone astray, and have led others
  astray. You have disciplined yourself with lengthy vigils. You have made loud proclamations. But
  in no way are you distinct from me.8" (p. 80, folio 55); "Bullha has long been more sinful than
  all these creatures" (p. 98, folio 73).
- **An internal contradiction on whether the ecstatic claim may be spoken.** "Bullha, do not act
  outside the law, and sleep undisturbed. Do not come out with I am God,9 or you will be singing
  merrily up on the gallows" (p. 107, folio 82) — against, two pages earlier, "It was you who made
  him say I am God" (p. 106, folio 81), and against "Truth destroys the law… Truth is the delight of
  the lover's house" (p. 85, folio 60). The contradiction is the book's; it is not resolved in this
  chunk.
- **Silence and concealment urged as a discipline.** "Get along by keeping silent. When they hear
  the truth, people cannot endure it. If you tell the truth, they fall on you" (p. 85, folio 60);
  "It is necessary to study this science but unacceptable to reveal it" (p. 93, folio 68); "Keep
  silent when I speak. Make sure you do not tell this to anyone" (p. 102, folio 77); "If I tell the
  truth, I face the gallows and the noose. If I lie, I flourish" (p. 89, folio 64).
- **Repentance judged by conduct, not by profession.** "They profess repentance with their lips, not
  with their hearts. They are disgraced in this world and the next" (p. 101, folio 76).
- **Predestination stated without qualification.** "Whatever he does, it is God's will; what can
  anyone do by himself? I am grateful for whatever fate is written on my forehead" (p. 76,
  folio 51); "Whatever happened that day was fated to happen" (p. 88, folio 63).
- **The world as dream, twice.** "I am a dream, the whole world is a dream, and my father's people
  are a dream" (p. 78, folio 53); "The world is a dream and a fancy, my friend" (p. 92, folio 67).
- **Arabic and Persian tags are quoted inside the verse throughout, each carrying an endnote marker
  whose note is outside this chunk** — "We have honored the sons of Adam,3" (p. 75, folio 50); "Let
  it be, and it was2" (p. 81, folio 56); "We are nearer,3" and "And in your own selves.4" and
  "Remember me, and I will remember you.6" and "The hand of God is over their hands.8" (p. 85,
  folio 60); "Your flesh is my flesh,1" (p. 88, folio 63); "Over all things8" and "Die before you
  die,11" and "You do not move at all.12" (p. 91, folio 66); "You will not attain righteousness,
  recognize the mystery of until,13" (p. 92, folio 67); "And he is with you1" (p. 102, folio 77);
  "If it were not for you6" (p. 105, folio 80).
- **A technical point of Arabic orthography used as doctrine, twice**: "How can one know about ‘ain

  "A single form was assigned to ‘ain [text-layer?] and ġhain.11 The only difference between them is
  a single dot. Once the dot is removed from the heart, ġhain is shown to be ‘ain [text-layer?]"

## Doubts

- **The Shah Inayat trap, and exactly what the pick rests on.** The archive holds two candidate
  rows: `shrine-of-shah-inayat-qadiri` (Shah Inayat Qadiri, Mozang Chungi, Lahore, d. c. 1728,
  Qadri Shattari) and `dargah-roza-sufi-shah-inayat-shaheed` (Sufi Shah Inayat Shaheed, Jhok
  Sharif, Sindh, d. 1718). **Four of the five mentions in this chunk are the bare "Shah Inayat" or
  "Inayat"** (pp. 84, 90, 93, 101 / folios 59, 65, 68, 76) **and on their own would fit either
  row.** The pick was made on the fifth, "With the departure of Inayat Qadiri,1" (p. 104, folio 79),
  where the printed nisba matches the Lahore row's principal figure and not the Jhok Sharif row's.
  **That one word is the whole of the evidence.** The chunk gives no place, no date, no tomb, no
  ʿurs and no order beyond it, and endnote 1 on that line is not in this chunk. A human should
  confirm against the volume's introduction or endnotes before any entry cites these pages, and the
  consolidator may reverse the mapping without losing any fact, since every bullet under that id
  states only what the book prints.
- **"Shah Shams" (p. 105, folio 80) is a bare name with an endnote marker whose note is not in this
  chunk.** It was mapped to `shrine-of-shah-shams-ud-din-sabzwari` on the name alone. The index also
  holds `shamsabad` (Shams Ali Qalandar, Okara, d. 1966), whose figure's name also contains "Shams";
  the chunk gives nothing — no place, no date, no order — that excludes either. Flagged rather than
  resolved.
- **"Zakariya" (p. 105, folio 80) was deliberately NOT mapped.** The row it would take is
  `shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya` (Bahauddin Zakariya, Multan, d. 1267, Suhrawardi). The chunk gives
  only the bare name in a list of ordeals ("You set the saw to Zakariya's4 head") alongside Mansur
  and Shah Shams, and carries endnote 4, whose text is not in this chunk. A sawn head is also told
  of a prophet of that name, so the chunk does not establish which Zakariya is meant. **A human
  must read endnote 4 before this is either mapped or discarded.**
- **Krishna (pp. 82, 97 / folios 57, 72) takes no id because two archive rows share him as
  principal figure** — `krishna-mandir-kabari-bazar` (Rawalpindi) and `krishna-mandir-ravi-road`
  (Lahore). Nothing in the chunk points to either place. Recorded under "Other saints, sites and
  events" as the protocol requires for a name that could be two rows.
- **Waris Shah is nowhere named in this chunk**, although Hir, Ranjha, the Sials and the Kheras all
  are (pp. 78, 90, 100, 105 / folios 53, 65, 75, 80). `mausoleum-of-waris-shah` therefore takes
  nothing from these pages. Recording the negative so a later chunk's mention is not double-counted.
- **The open toponym question does not bite in this chunk.** The bare place names here — the Ganges
  (pp. 80, 82), the Chenab (pp. 95–96), Rum (p. 89), Bareilly (p. 97) — correspond to **no archive
  row at all**, so no id is being withheld by the interim convention and nothing needs revisiting if
  the ruling reverses.
- **No Hindu, Sikh, Jain or Nanakpanthi site in the archive is named in this chunk**, Hinglaj
  included; the Hindu material here is generic ritual and deity reference, not a site. So the
  empty-id defect on `Shaktipeeth Shri Hinglaj Mata Mandir` does not arise on these pages.
- **Poem numbers versus folios — one value collision.** Poem numbers run 58–100 and folios run
  50–83, so the two ranges overlap at 58–83. Position separates them everywhere: the poem number is
  set alone at the head of a poem, the folio alone at the foot of the page. The single collision of
  value is **58**, which is a poem number at the head of p. 75 (whose folio is 50) and the folio at
  the foot of p. 83 (whose poems are 67 and 68). No reference above was derived from a number in an
  ambiguous position.
- **The folio rule was confirmed, not assumed.** Every one of the 34 pages prints its number at the
  foot and every one equals PDF − 25, with no gap, no blank leaf and no unnumbered page in this
  range. The chunk therefore contradicts nothing in the measured rule.
- **Text-layer damage inventory, printed form first, reading in brackets.** This chunk is markedly
  **cleaner than the companion Risalo volume: the `fi`/`fl`/`ff` ligature loss that runs through
  that book does not occur here at all** — "first", "fine", "finery", "fire", "flute", "flesh",
  "flourish", "offer", "offerings", "suffer", "difficult", "difference", "effort", "affected",
  "profit", "buffaloes" and "sacrificed" are all printed whole. The faults that do appear are these,
  and only these:
  - `‘ain` [ʿain] — the ayn is rendered as a left single quotation mark, at p. 86 (folio 61) once
    and p. 93 (folio 68) three times. Flagged inline above on each occurrence.
  - `Qurʼan` [Qurʾan] — a modifier apostrophe where a right half-ring is expected, p. 81 (folio 56).
  - `ġhain 2` — the endnote marker is separated from its word by a space at p. 86 (folio 61), where
    elsewhere markers abut ("Majnun5", "Adam,3"). A reader could take the 2 for part of the word.
  - Endnote numerals abut the preceding word throughout ("Inayat Qadiri,1", "Shah Shams2",
    "Zakariya's4", "Mansur3"). **No numeral in this chunk is a date**, but the pattern means any
    apparent figure attached to a name must be checked against the endnote apparatus before use.
  Diacritics are otherwise preserved intact — `sīn`, `shīn`, `mīm`, `Gulistān`, `Bostān`, `Qudūrī`,
  `Yāsīn`, `missī`, `palās`, `ġhain` all carry their marks.
- **No original script and no transliteration anywhere in pp. 75–108.** A Murty volume normally
  prints the original facing the translation; these pages carry English only, with the running head
  `SUFI LYRICS` on every page. Either the Panjabi pages fall outside this chunk's range or the
  transcription dropped them — **worth checking the PDF before assuming the original is absent from
  the book.** Every name, place and phrase above is consequently in English only, and the protocol's
  original-script requirement cannot be satisfied from this chunk.
- **Endnote markers appear on roughly seventy passages across the forty-three poems, and no note
  text falls in this chunk.** Every gloss that would identify Shah Shams, Zakariya, Ali, the
  Kheras, the Ranghar, "the grandfather", "the fourth state", "the screen of earthliness", the
  "letters of exemption", the "hearth", the palās tree, "bamboo from Bareilly", "three ones", "Ahmad
  emerged from Ahad", the scriptural tags, or the identity of Inayat is therefore **(not stated)**
  here and must come from the endnotes chunk.
- **"the great bridegroom's5 procession" (p. 80, folio 55)** is unglossed — the chunk does not say
  which deity or figure is meant, and it should not be paraphrased.
- **Poem 64 runs across three pages (pp. 79–81, folios 54–56) and poem 75 across three (pp. 88–90,
  folios 63–65)**, so a single poem's material is split over several page references above; that is
  the book's layout, not a transcription fault.
