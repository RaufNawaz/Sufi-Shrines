# shackle_bulleh_shah_sufi_lyrics — chunk 004 — pp. 109–142 (folios 84–117)

Continuous text of the numbered lyrics, English translation only: **poems 101–146**, opening mid-run
at poem 101 (p. 109, folio 84) and breaking off inside poem 146 (p. 142, folio 117). Running head
`SUFI LYRICS` on every page of the chunk. No translator's headnote, introduction or note text falls
in this chunk — only endnote reference markers, which run into the preceding word ("Majnun1",
"Mecca.2", "Bahaval Haq.5") and are reproduced as printed in the quotations below.

**Folio check.** The printed number stands alone at the foot of each page and the rule holds without
a single break across all thirty-four pages: p. 109 = 84 … p. 142 = 117, i.e. folio = PDF − 25. I
checked every page individually because this book also numbers its poems; the poem number stands at
the **head** of a poem, the folio at the **foot** of a page, and the two never coincide in value on
the same page in this chunk (the closest is p. 130, which heads poem **130** and foots folio **105**).
See Doubts for the one case worth a second eye.

The poet signs almost every poem in its closing line, as **Bullha**, **Bullhe** or **Bullhe Shah**.

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`** (the chunk names him "Bullha", "Bullhe", "Bullhe Shah"; never "Abdullah
  Shah", never "Qadri", never Kasur): **the signature is the poem's closing device**, in three
  printed forms — "Bullha, the lord has come to my house" (p. 109, folio 84, poem 101); "Bullhe Shah,
  we are not going to die; let someone else fall into the grave" (p. 121, folio 96, poem 120);
  "Bullhe, the lord is the lover of those who have destroyed their selves" (p. 138, folio 113,
  poem 140). Also in the third person inside a poem: "Then they would kill friend Bullha" (p. 120,
  folio 95) and "arrest Bullhe Shah's thief and bring him here" (p. 122, folio 97).
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **his family, and the caste quarrel, stated at length and in their own
  voices** (p. 113, folio 88, poem 108). "Bullha's1 sisters and his brothers' wives have come to give
  him their advice." Their reproach: "Listen to what we say, Bullha, stop clinging to the Arain's
  skirt. Why have you brought disgrace on the family of the Prophet and the descendants of Ali?"2
  His answer: "May anyone who calls us a Sayyid suffer the punishments of hell. Swings are set up in
  paradise for anyone who calls us an Arain." And the close: "If you desire the joys of spring,
  become the servant of the Arains. Why do you ask what Bullhe Shah's caste is? Just be grateful for
  whatever pleases Him." The chunk states that his family is reproached as "the family of the
  Prophet and the descendants of Ali" and that he repudiates the title Sayyid; it does **not** name
  the Arain he is said to cling to — see Doubts.
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **he calls himself a fakir, and says he departed to the sound of a
  drum** — "Bullhe Shah is a poor fakir; he has departed, proclaiming to the sound of the drum: 'Our
  name is glorious in the world; you have created us from light and saved us!'" (p. 141, folio 116,
  poem 143, the closing verse).
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **he denies every communal, regional and confessional placing of
  himself** — "Bullha,1 what do I know about who I am? … I am neither an Arab nor from Lahore, nor an
  Indian from the city of Nagaur.4 I am not a Hindu, nor a Turk5 from Peshawar. Nor do I live in
  Nadaun.6 … Bullha, who is the lord7 standing here?" (p. 112, folio 87, poem 106). Restated at
  p. 133 (folio 108, poem 135): "Why do you ask what Bullha is like? He does not create, nor is he
  created." And at p. 131 (folio 106, poem 131): "I am not a Hindu or a Turk.3 I am not twice-born,
  nor am I thrice-born.4 I do not recognize pure and impure."
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **he speaks in a woman's voice throughout the chunk** — "I, Bullha, am
  the slave girl of the lord" (p. 110, folio 85, poem 103); "I1 am a poor sweeper girl from the
  establishment of the true lord" (p. 130, folio 105, poem 130); "Ranjha is a yogi and I am his
  yogini" (p. 142, folio 117, poem 144).
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah`**: **dancing is named as his practice and as what brought him to his
  master** — "Bullha, let us go and sit at the gate of Shah Inayat, who made me dress in green and
  red.4 When I started dancing, I found my way to him" (p. 111, folio 86, poem 105); "Bullha, the
  lord has made me dance, and the sound is very loud" (p. 115, folio 90, poem 112); "I clap and
  dance3 with my girlfriends" (p. 116, folio 91, poem 114); "You have made me dance to a single
  tune.6" (p. 134, folio 109, poem 136). The reproach against the dancing is quoted too: "Satan
  makes her dance in the courtyard. Talk to her and make her stop."1 (p. 116, folio 91).
- **`shrine-of-shah-inayat-qadiri`** (the chunk's forms: "Shah Inayat", once "Inayat Qadiri", once
  "our guide"): **the master is named in seven places, and the book supplies both the order-name and
  the city, which is why this row and not the Sindh row is entered here** — see the Doubts entry
  before using it. "Bullha, let us go and sit at the gate of Shah Inayat, who made me dress in green
  and red.4" (p. 111, folio 86); "Leaving my parents, I have clung to your skirt, oh my lord Shah
  Inayat. Protect the honor of the one who clings to you" (p. 115, folio 90); "Shah Inayat has set
  out his hooks, and without being seen he tugs the line" (p. 122, folio 97); "Let me see Shah
  Inayat, who has made me meet my lord" (p. 126, folio 101); "Come on in with your swaying movement,
  my Shah Inayat" (p. 126, folio 101); "Come, Inayat Qadiri,5 my heart desires you" (p. 127, folio
  102); "Bullha, I am not worthy of the lord. Shah Inayat has delivered me" (p. 129, folio 104).
- **`shrine-of-shah-inayat-qadiri`**: **the master is placed at Lahore, against Baghdad** — "Baghdad
  is the place of the Pir of Pirs;4 our guide is in royal Lahore" (p. 122, folio 97, poem 121), in
  the same poem and within four lines of "Shah Inayat has set out his hooks". The same poem twice
  says the summons was "heard in royal Lahore" (pp. 121 and 122, folios 96 and 97). The chunk gives
  **no tomb, no date, no death, no shrine and no location beyond "royal Lahore"** — all **(not
  stated)**.
- **`shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya`** (the chunk's form: "the great saint Bahaval Haq"): **named as
  the object of a pilgrimage to Multan, in the mouth of Hir addressing Ranjha** — "Come, Ranjha, let
  us go to Multan, to pay homage to the great saint Bahaval Haq.5 Let us gain our desire immediately.
  My heart longs for the lord" (p. 123, folio 98, poem 122). This is the only ziyarat to a named
  saint's place anywhere in the chunk. No tomb, date, order or building detail: **(not stated)**. The
  identification rests on "Bahaval Haq" plus "Multan" — see Doubts.
- **`tilla-jogian`** (the chunk's forms: "Tilla", "guru Gorakh"): **named as the place of yogic
  initiation and as the authority a Pir must satisfy** — "Casting aside his drum,9 he goes toward
  Tilla,10 he sits and meditates on his name.11 He has his ears split and the earrings inserted, and
  is initiated by the guru" (p. 123, folio 98, poem 122), and in the next verse "My Pir gains the
  approval of guru Gorakh.12 He keeps crying 'Hir, Hir.' The one for whom he had his head shaven has
  a face as pale as the cotton boll" (p. 124, folio 99). No location, no shrine, no date: **(not
  stated)**. See Doubts for the competing row.

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Forced labour, tax and the certificate of exemption — the chunk's densest institutional
  passage**, in two companion poems spoken by a sweeper girl. "I1 am a poor sweeper girl from the
  establishment of the true lord. With the fan of attention and the broom of knowledge I keep
  sweeping up lust and anger… The qazi knows, the governor knows, I have a certificate freeing me
  from forced labor. By day and night all I ask is this: 'Do not send me away from this court.'"
  (p. 130, folio 105, poem 130). And: "If I take hold of my fan and make greed fly away, I am
  exempted from being taxed… I have made a pile of grain6 from my gleanings… Bullha, if I am owned
  by the lord, I am released from forced labor" (p. 131, folio 106, poem 131).
- **Agricultural labour under a revenue establishment**, in the safflower poem: "I have had enough of
  picking safflowers.1 This safflower has rather fine thorns… The master of this safflower is harsh,
  and his accountant is cruel. This safflower has four officers.2 They demand a hefty sum. The other
  girls3 have picked very little. I have filled my basket… The merchants have come to stay. The
  passage is difficult and the way is hard. I have a heavy bundle on my head" (p. 128, folio 103,
  poem 127).
- **Spinning and dowry as women's work**: "Sister, I am exhausted with all this spinning. The wicker
  baskets for the thread are at the back of the house… In front of me is the spinning wheel, behind
  me is the stool… Why should I work on preparing my dowry?… my spindle has fallen off. My spool of
  thread has got tangled" (p. 115, folio 90, poem 112).
- **The marriage ritual, performed under compulsion before a qazi**: "Chuchak called for the qazi and
  sat him down… Forcibly, the marriage ceremony was recited and the ritual was performed. Oil and
  lotion8 were rubbed on his shoulders, and sandal paste was smeared on his forehead… What use to me
  are the bangles on my arm?" (p. 123, folio 98). Elsewhere the bridal palanquin (p. 136, folio 111),
  the bridegroom's procession (p. 123, folio 98), and "Those who were sent to their in-laws are back
  home" (p. 127, folio 102).
- **Yogic initiation, described step by step**: "He has his ears split and the earrings inserted, and
  is initiated by the guru" (p. 123, folio 98); "The yogi came to practice yoga, with his head and
  beard and face clean-shaven. He has put on ochre-colored clothing, and has a yogi's coarse black
  thread around his neck" (p. 124, folio 99); "He goes to the door and sounds his horn"; "His bowl is
  broken and his beads scattered… The grains of millet13 are scattered on the path. Picking up the
  millet grains, he puts them in his pouch" (p. 124, folio 99); "he has rings in his ears and beads
  around his neck" (p. 141, folio 116); "smearing the yogi's ash on my limbs" (p. 136, folio 111);
  "a yogi has come. He has set up his fire3 at our door" (p. 135, folio 110); "I will go with the
  yogi, having put a mark on my forehead.1" (p. 135, folio 110); "having cast the omens thousands of
  times" (p. 135, folio 110).
- **The Five Pirs, invoked twice as protectors and as recipients of propitiation**: "The Five Pirs2
  protect my beloved, they search for him among people" (p. 122, folio 97); "I belong to the yogi,
  after propitiating the Five Pirs.13" (p. 136, folio 111).
- **The fakir and the blanket as marks of a status the poems prefer to rank**: "The poor fakir6 is
  mingled with the dust" (p. 123, folio 98); "Anyone who has discovered the fakir's secret has
  tracked the path within himself" (p. 118, folio 93); "Bullhe Shah is a poor fakir" (p. 141, folio
  116); "The Mughals drank cups of poison; you made men in rough blankets35 into kings. The nobles
  all go about in silence; you have truly disgraced them" (p. 140, folio 115); "With a staff in my
  hand I drive the buffaloes before me, wearing a rough blanket around my shoulders" (p. 142, folio
  117).
- **Hajj set against the master's gate, three times**: "Hajjis go to Mecca.2 You are our pilgrimage,
  my beloved" (p. 109, folio 84); "Hajjis go to Mecca,4 but I gaze on your face" (p. 127, folio 102);
  "Why should I go to the Kaaba?1 My heart longs for Takht Hazara. People prostrate themselves toward
  the Kaaba; my prostration is to my dear beloved" (p. 129, folio 104). Also "My dear friend shows me
  the Kaaba and the Qibla.3" (p. 111, folio 86).
- **Canonical prayer, curtailed**: "Love taught me only the prayer of two prostrations,5 omitting
  those of three and four" (p. 118, folio 93); "Love made the muezzin give the call to prayer.4 …
  Forming my intention, I went quickly to perform the prostration. Oh, I have turned my face toward
  the mihrab" (p. 126, folio 101); "the crown of the ascension2 and made me perform my ablution from
  the well.3" (p. 126, folio 101).
- **Burial, described from inside the grave**: "On the first night of being together,6 my heart is
  full of fear. After the deep grave was dug, the tomb was split open. When the first tie of my
  winding sheet was undone, my face was turned toward the Kaaba" (p. 127, folio 102). Set against
  the two communities' funerary practice: "Muslims fear the burning grounds, Hindus fear the grave.
  Both of them die in this dispute. This is the enmity between the two" (p. 121, folio 96).
- **The maktab and the mullah's discipline**: "The1 mullah beats me. The mullah taught me the lesson.
  I could not understand anything beyond alif, but all he keeps shouting is 'Be, be!'2" (p. 118,
  folio 93, poem 118).
- **Mosque and temple named as institutions the poems set aside**: "I am not a believer in the
  mosques, nor do I follow the rites of unbelief" (p. 112, folio 87); "Here there is the business of
  the mosque. Here you have become a Hindu temple. Here you are an ascetic wearing matted locks"
  (p. 132, folio 107); "Somewhere there is a Shia, somewhere there is a Sunni. Somewhere there is a
  yogi with matted locks, somewhere there is one whose head is shaved" (p. 137, folio 112).
- **Caste and ritual status vocabulary used directly**: "Arain", "Sayyid" (p. 113, folio 88);
  "Sometimes I see a Brahman, sometimes a Shaikh… the pandit describes" (p. 116, folio 91); "I am not
  twice-born, nor am I thrice-born.4 I do not recognize pure and impure" (p. 131, folio 106); "I am
  not among the pure or the polluted" (p. 112, folio 87); "a poor sweeper girl" (pp. 130–131, folios
  105–106).
- **Trade and money as the recurring metaphor**: "Transact your business6 in the name of the word.
  The lord will be your witness" (p. 111, folio 86); "The traders3 behold the face of the lord"
  (p. 118, folio 93); "I am a trader whose business is to see your face" (p. 135, folio 110); "I give
  you one lakh,1 I will give you two lakhs" (p. 137, folio 112); "All the money I have staked falls
  to you. I am settling a long-standing account" (p. 129, folio 104).
- **Music and instruments**: the flute is the chunk's governing instrument — "This instrument has
  five or seven holes.5 All are filled with their own notes" (p. 111, folio 86); "The flute is
  something that everyone can hear or play, but very few grasp its meaning.3" (p. 110, folio 85);
  "The stem of this flute is green" (p. 110, folio 85). The **unstruck music** (anahad) four times:
  pp. 110, 118, 124, 136 (folios 85, 93, 99, 111) — "The unstruck flute plays its high notes"
  (p. 124, folio 99). Also **drums** — "'Boom, boom' roll the drums of love" (p. 134, folio 109);
  "the departure drum" (p. 142, folio 117); "a large drum on my head" (p. 122, folio 97); the drum
  Ranjha casts aside before Tilla (p. 123, folio 98).
- **Dress as a sign of affiliation**: "who made me dress in green and red.4" (p. 111, folio 86); "I
  am wearing a tunic of many colors"; "What is the parting in your hair marked with vermilion? Your
  red tunic looks good on you" (p. 127, folio 102); "I destroyed my blouse and scarf and my hut"
  (p. 117, folio 92); "ochre-colored clothing" (p. 124, folio 99).
- **The Sirat bridge, twice**: "It is you who made the Sirat bridge.9 … It is the excellent word10
  that delivers us safely across" (p. 120, folio 95); "I have gone down the first stair and am
  stationed on the Sirat bridge.3" (p. 127, folio 102).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

All of these are narrated by the poems as received story, and none is attributed by the book to a
saint of the archive or to a site.

- **Mansur laughs on the gallows**: "He puts himself upon the gallows, and standing nearby he
  laughs,3" (p. 113, folio 88).
- **Shams is flayed for a word**: "When Shams16 uttered his famous words, it was you who made him
  say, Arise by my permission. You had his skin flayed from head to foot" (p. 140, folio 115).
- **Shah Sharaf chilled in the river for twelve years** (p. 140, folio 115).
- **Zakariya split with the tree**: "When the tree hid Zakariya,10 you made them take his concealment
  amiss. You had the saw set on his head, so he was split along with the tree" (p. 114, folio 89).
- **Namrud killed by a mosquito; Qarun swallowed by the earth** (p. 141, folio 116).
- **Sahti's snake trick**: "Bullha, Sahti devised a trick.14 She caused Hir of the Sials to fight with
  the snake. The yogi came to cast his spells. Both their desires were fulfilled" (p. 124, folio 99).
- **Ranjha's yogic power over Hir**: "He has played the unstruck music5 on his flute. He has ruined
  Hir the Sial, wreaking his powerful magic" (p. 136, folio 111); "He is not a yogi but some magic or
  shadow" (p. 136, folio 111).
- **The yogi reads fate in Hir's face**: "He sits and looks intently at Hir. He discovers the fate
  that is written there" (p. 124, folio 99).
- **The ascension**: "At the time of his ascension8 he asked for Buraq, whom Jibrail led in welcome.
  The houris sang joyful songs" (p. 136, folio 111); "He had his cradle swung by Jibrail, then had
  his throat cut in his thirst" (of Imam Husain, p. 114, folio 89).

## Arguments and interpretations

This chunk carries no translator's voice at all — no headnote, no note text, only endnote markers.
What follows is the primary source's own judgement, in its own words.

- **Caste is declared irrelevant, and the declaration is put in the poet's own mouth against his own
  family**: "Why do you ask what Bullhe Shah's caste is? Just be grateful for whatever pleases Him"
  (p. 113, folio 88). The same poem inverts the ranking outright: "Swings are set up in paradise for
  anyone who calls us an Arain."
- **The master's gate displaces the Hajj** — stated three times in different poems (pp. 109, 127, 129;
  folios 84, 102, 104) and never qualified.
- **The two communities' quarrel is presented as a single figure in two guises**: "Sometimes he is
  Ram Das, sometimes he is Fateh Muhammad,2 hence all this long-standing uproar. The quarrel between
  them was removed, when someone else emerged" (p. 121, folio 96); "I have found out, I have found
  out, that it is you who have changed your form. Here you are a Muslim and recite the scriptures.
  Here you are a devout Hindu and repeat your muttered prayers" (pp. 131–132, folios 106–107);
  "Neither believer nor unbeliever is apparent to me when I enter unity" (p. 117, folio 92).
- **Law is opposed to love and declared beaten**: "The game between love and law has started… I have
  broken the law and won the game. I go around in disgrace" (p. 116, folio 91).
- **Learning is ranked by depth, not rejected**: "I flee from those who have studied a little bit.1 …
  Learned scholars are my brothers; those who have studied a little bit drive me mad" (p. 131, folio
  106, poem 132).
- **Concealment is prudent and is said to be so**: "If I reveal the mystery, all disputes will be
  forgotten. Then they would kill friend Bullha. Veiled expression5 is suitable here" (p. 120, folio
  95).
- **The lord is charged with the suffering of his own favourites** — poem 143 (pp. 139–141, folios
  114–116) is a sustained indictment running through prophets, saints, lovers and the Mahabharata,
  ending "I have seen your power, oh lord, and my head is sacrificed to you."
- **The poems disclaim the poet's own agency**: "Dear Mansur said I am God.2 But who was the one who
  made him say it?" (p. 138, folio 113); "I cannot help saying what comes onto my tongue… As it
  hesitates, my tongue speaks.1" (p. 118, folio 93).

## Doubts

- **The Shah Inayat trap, and why this chunk was entered under the Lahore row rather than held
  back.** The archive holds two candidate rows: `shrine-of-shah-inayat-qadiri` (Shah Inayat Qadiri,
  Mozang Chungi, Lahore, d. c. 1728) and `dargah-roza-sufi-shah-inayat-shaheed` (Sufi Shah Inayat
  Shaheed, Jhok Sharif, Sindh, d. 1718). The chunk names the bare form "Shah Inayat" at pp. 111, 115,
  122, 126 (twice) and 129, which on its own decides nothing. What tips it is two things the book
  itself prints: the form **"Inayat Qadiri,5"** (p. 127, folio 102) and, in the same poem as "Shah
  Inayat has set out his hooks", the line **"our guide is in royal Lahore"** (p. 122, folio 97).
  Order-name plus city both match the Lahore row and neither matches the Sindh row. **But the book
  never says in so many words that "Inayat Qadiri", "Shah Inayat" and "our guide" are one person, and
  it never says Mozang, never gives a death date and never mentions a tomb.** A human must confirm
  this against the volume's endnotes and introduction before any entry cites these pages, and if the
  identification fails the same bullets belong to `dargah-roza-sufi-shah-inayat-shaheed` unchanged.
- **The Arain is not named.** Poem 108 (p. 113, folio 88) has the family say "stop clinging to the
  Arain's skirt", and poem 111 (p. 115, folio 90) has the speaker say "I have clung to your skirt, oh
  my lord Shah Inayat". The idiom is the same and the inference is obvious, but **the chunk nowhere
  joins them**, and I have not joined them. Do not write "Shah Inayat, an Arain" from these pages.
- **"Bahaval Haq" is not spelled out as Bahauddin Zakariya.** The chunk gives only "the great saint
  Bahaval Haq.5" with "Multan" in the same line (p. 123, folio 98); endnote 5 is not in this chunk.
  The mapping to `shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya` rests on the title plus the city. Confirm against the
  endnote.
- **"guru Gorakh" is the principal figure of two archive rows** — `tilla-jogian` (Salt Range) and
  `gorakhnath-goraknath-temple` (Gor Khatri, Peshawar). I entered `tilla-jogian` because the book
  names **"Tilla"** as the place of initiation in the immediately preceding verse (p. 123, folio 98),
  which is that row's own location, and because "Tilla" here is not a bare toponym — initiation,
  ear-splitting and a guru are attached to it. The alternative row should be reconsidered if endnote
  10 (not in this chunk) glosses "Tilla" as somewhere else.
- **Deities named in the lyrics have been left unmapped, deliberately and reversibly.** The ruling of
  18 September covers a *person* who is a row's principal figure named as an author or literary
  reference; it does not address a deity invoked inside a devotional lyric, and I did not extend it
  without a ruling. If it is extended: **Ram** (p. 116, folio 91; p. 141, folio 116) would take
  `ram-mandir-saidpur-ram-kund-mandir`; **Hanuman** (p. 141, folio 116) would take
  `shri-panchmukhi-hanuman-mandir-karachi`; **Krishna** (pp. 110, 119, 141; folios 85, 94, 116) is in
  any case a two-row name — `krishna-mandir-kabari-bazar` and `krishna-mandir-ravi-road` — and would
  have to go to Doubts even under the extended ruling.
- **Bare toponyms, listed with the row each would take if the open toponym convention reverses.**
  `garh-maharaja-shorkot` for **Jhang** (p. 135, folio 110 — the row's location is "Garh Maharaja
  (Shorkot), Jhang, Punjab"); no single row for **royal Lahore** (pp. 121, 122, 124; folios 96, 97,
  99), which the archive carries for some twenty Lahore rows and which cannot be assigned from a bare
  name; **Peshawar** (p. 112, folio 87) likewise stands over several Peshawar rows. **Takht Hazara**,
  **Nagaur**, **Nadaun**, **Baghdad**, **Mecca**, **Sinai**, **Egypt**, **the Nile** and **Lanka**
  correspond to no archive row at all.
- **"Shams16" (p. 140, folio 115) is unglossed and is a live two-person ambiguity.** The flaying
  story and the words "Arise by my permission" are told of Shams in Panjabi tradition, and the
  archive holds `shrine-of-shah-shams-ud-din-sabzwari` (Shah Shams-ud-Din Sabzwari, Multan). The
  chunk gives no city, no date and no epithet beyond "Shams", so this is **not** mapped. If endnote
  16 identifies him as the Multan saint, this bullet is that row's.
- **"Shah Sarmad" (p. 140, folio 115) must not be read as Sachal Sarmast.** The chunk says only "You
  had Shah Sarmad's15 throat cut". Nothing connects it to `shrine-of-sachal-sarmast` (Sachal Sarmast,
  Daraza, Khairpur), and Sachal Sarmast is not named anywhere in this chunk. Flagged because the
  names are close enough to be conflated downstream.
- **"Ram Das" (p. 121, folio 96) is not stated to be Guru Ram Das.** It is paired with "Fateh
  Muhammad" as the two communities' name-types inside a single line. If anyone later reads it as the
  Sikh Guru it would touch `gurdwara-guru-ram-das-ji`; the chunk gives no ground for that.
- **No `[text-layer?]` damage found in this chunk — and that is a positive finding worth recording.**
  The `fi`/`fl`/`ff` ligature loss that runs through the companion Shackle volume is **absent**
  here: "fulfilled", "flute", "offering", "difficult", "first", "officers", "safflower", "flee",
  "fires" and "suffer" all print whole (pp. 111, 110, 128, 131, 134). Diacritics survive too —
  "mīm,1" (p. 113, folio 88). No word in this chunk carries a flag, and no fact above is qualified by
  one.
- **Italics are lost by the text layer, which blurs quotation boundaries.** The volume prints
  scriptural and Persian tags in italic; the text layer gives them unmarked, so "Then there is the
  face of God 1" (p. 109), "Am I not your lord?  4" (p. 128), "We are nearer 1" (p. 137), "Let it be,
  and it was.3" (p. 122), "I am God" (pp. 113, 138) and "Arise by my permission" (p. 140) have had
  their extent inferred by me from the endnote markers and sense, not read off the page. Anyone
  quoting these should check the printed page for where the italic actually starts and stops.
- **No original Panjabi and no transliteration anywhere in the chunk.** Folios 84–117 run
  consecutively with English on every one, so the facing original a Murty volume normally prints is
  not in this page range. Every name and place above is therefore available in English only, and the
  protocol's "quote the original script" rule cannot be satisfied from this chunk.
- **No endnote text falls in this chunk**, though roughly a hundred markers do. Every gloss that
  would identify Bahaval Haq, Tilla, guru Gorakh, Shams, Shah Sarmad, Shah Sharaf, Inayat Qadiri,
  Takht Hazara, the Five Pirs, "the rough blankets", Sanaan, Roda, Dhido, Sammi and Dhola is
  **(not stated)** here and must come from the endnotes chunk.
- **Poem number versus folio.** The one page a reader could trip on is **p. 130**, which heads poem
  **130** and foots folio **105**; also p. 131 (poem 131, folio 106) and p. 125 (poem 125, folio 100).
  In every case the poem number stands above the poem's first line and the folio stands alone at the
  page foot, and the folio in each case equals PDF − 25, so the two are separable by position and by
  arithmetic. **No page in pp. 109–142 disagrees with the stated rule**, and I found no page where I
  could not tell a folio from a poem number.
- **The chunk begins and ends mid-sequence**: it opens at poem 101 (p. 109, folio 84), so poems 1–100
  are in earlier chunks, and it stops two lines into poem 146 (p. 142, folio 117).
- **An internal contradiction the book does not resolve, reported as it stands**: the yogi of poem
  137 is both "my heart's beloved" and the killer of Hasan and Husain — "Hear about this yogi's
  arenas. He slew the Prophet's darlings, Hasan and Husain,9 in battle, depriving them of water and
  making them thirst" (p. 136, folio 111). The poem attributes every calamity in the catalogue to the
  same beloved; it is not a transcription fault.
