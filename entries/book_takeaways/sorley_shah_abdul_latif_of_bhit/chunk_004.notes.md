# sorley_shah_abdul_latif_of_bhit — chunk 004 — pp. 54–66

Continuous prose and quoted archival extracts from Sorley's chapter on the English in Sind: the
close of the **first East India Company factory** (Moghul period, records of 1646–1662, pp. 54–57)
and the whole opening of the section headed **`IV.    The English in Sind, I7S8-75`[text-layer?]**
(p. 57, folio 48), covering the **second factory, 1758–75** (pp. 57–66). The chunk opens
mid-extract ("other great mens fees will carry away at least 50,000 rupees more", p. 54, folio 45)
and breaks off mid-sentence inside Robert Erskine's report of 14 November 1760, on saltpetre —
"the Honble Company's exclusive right to `�his`[text-layer?] article fully enforced, which it has
never," (p. 66, folio 57).

**A stable printed-page offset holds across the whole chunk: printed page = PDF page − 9.** PDF
p. 54 carries printed **45** and PDF p. 66 carries printed **57**, and every intervening page
agrees. The one exception is PDF p. 65, whose running head prints no number at all (see Doubts);
by the offset it would be printed 56. References below give the PDF page and, where the page
actually prints one, the folio.

**No shrine, tomb, dargah, ʿurs, khanqah, sajjada nashin, endowment, custodian or named saint
appears anywhere in this chunk.** The word `Facquirs` occurs once, in a list of the classes a
Kalhora firman is addressed to (p. 64, folio 55), and that is the whole of the chunk's religious
content. Its value to the archive is entirely economic and institutional: revenue farming, transit
dues, port silting, famine, textile manufacture, coinage and the collapse of the Shikarpur–Tatta
trade in the lifetime of the poet the book is named for. See Doubts before drawing any site fact
from these pages.

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- **Tatta / Tattah / Tuttah** — the capital and the Company's inland station throughout; "Tattah the
  capital was then a place of great opulence and the residence of a number of rich merchants and
  shroffs" (p. 61, folio 52), against its decline "definitely set in with the growing difficulties
  of navigating the Lower Indus" (p. 58, folio 49). Site of a Moghul mint (p. 54, folio 45).
- **Laribunder / Laree Bunder / Bunder (Laribandar)** — the old Indus-mouth port, "becoming
  difficult owing to silting" by 1652 (p. 54, folio 45); later "the silted up Laribunder of the
  first factory's days" (p. 58, folio 49).
- **Aurangabunder or Kukrillo** — "a new port" built by Aurangzeb as Governor of Multan, named in a
  letter of 31 March 1652 as "his new port of Cuckerhallah" (p. 54, folio 45); still a proposed
  factory site in 1758 (p. 63, folio 54).
- **Shahbunder / Shaw bander** — "the port that had displaced the silted up Laribunder" (p. 58,
  folio 49); the firman's preferred factory site (p. 60, folio 51); "the mud building at Shaw
  bander would soon be habitable", 14 March 1759 (p. 65).
- **Karachi / Carachee / Crotchy** — "It is to this desire on the part of the Afghans that Karachi
  owes its first importance as a port and centre of trade" (p. 58, folio 49); the residue of the
  Shikarpur trade "now centers at Crotchy where there `rrout`[text-layer?] to and from is pretty
  clear of the Princes dominions" (p. 62, folio 53).
- **Bucker, Derbella** — named together for "the badd government at Bucker, Derbella and those
  partes" in 1652 (p. 55, folio 46); "Derbella jaggatt" is where 18 rupees in officers' fees were
  paid (p. 55, folio 46).
- **Siccarpore / Sicc.a Poer** (Shikarpur) — the source of an annual merchant traffic to Tatta,
  extinguished by revenue-farming oppression (p. 62, folio 53); "the Patans had slipped by the
  Marattas into `Sicc.a`[text-layer?] Poer near Scindy", 16 December 1759 (p. 66, folio 57).
- **Nasarpur / Nasarpfir / Nasarpoor** — cloth-making centre (pp. 54–55, folios 45–46) and the place
  Scrivener writes from, 5 June 1656 (p. 56, folio 47).
- **Kandiaro** and **Gambat** — the other two named cloth-making places (pp. 54–55, folios 45–46).
- **Saume / Sume (Sann)** — the book's own gloss; a stretch of river needing a guard against the
  Samejas, 1656 (p. 56, folio 47).
- **Cora Bollah (possibly Ghorabari)** — the book's own hedged gloss, the place a second Baloch
  force came from in 1656 (p. 56, folio 47).
- **Ghulam Shah Kalhoro / Golam Shaw / Gulam Shah / `Gulii m Shah`[text-layer?]** — granted the
  parwana of 22 September 1758 (p. 59, folio 50); deposed his elder brother "Mahomed
  `Ya,rr`[text-layer?] Caun", described as "a vile tyrant" (p. 63, folio 54); "reduced the tribute
  to about eleven lacks", "haveing erected forts and collected a little fleet" (p. 61, folio 52);
  won a second action against his brothers, 10 December 1759, and "was returned to Halla chand,
  his future residence" (p. 65). Died — **(not stated)**.
- **Sarfaraz Khan Kalhoro** — his "maladministration" is given as the reason the second factory was
  given up; Sorley calls him "that tyrant" (pp. 57, 61, folios 48, 52).
- **Muhammad Yar Khan / Mahomed Ya,rr Caun** and **Uttar Khan / Utthar Caun / Uttar Caun** — Ghulam
  Shah's brothers; the three divided the land between them for "four months only" (p. 64, folio 55);
  Uttar Caun "had again collected `f.-esh`[text-layer?] troops among the `Daiid`[text-layer?] Poots"
  (p. 66, folio 57).
- **Mir Muhammad Kalhoro** — named by Sorley as "the old deceased prince" who "was fond of brass
  cannon" (p. 63, folio 54).
- **Aurangzeb** — Governor of Multan until 1652 (p. 54, folio 45); the civil war of his accession

- **Nadir Shah / Nadir Shaw** — his "conquest of India in 1740" is dated in Erskine's report of 1760

- **Achmet Shah** (of Candahar) — "The first demand [for woollens] was for the army of Achmet Shah"

  folio 53).
- **Company servants named**: Fremlen, Scrivener, Spiller (p. 59, folio 50); John Spiller, at Tatta

## Practices, institutions, economy

This is where the chunk's whole substance lies.

- **Escheat on a merchant's death, described as a novelty and a grievance.** "This all men exclaime
  againest, being the worst (they say) of all ill customes that ever came upp yett ; for now if a
  merchant of fashion or any other that hath meanes dyes his wealth will all be seized on though he
  hath children and brothers to leave it unto." A governor's fees alone "will carry away at least
  50,000 rupees more" (p. 54, folio 45).
- **The dustak and rahadari.** "The death of the Governor has invalidated the dustack he gave us for
  freeing us from the irksome duty of water rawdeare", the book's footnote glossing it "i.e.
  rahadari"; the pass had to be "reneued" because "some of the officers of the Ghaat began to
  trouble our boatemen" (p. 54, folio 45).
- **Hindu commercial dominance under the Moghuls, as the author reads it.** "even at that time big
  business was in the hands of Hindu traders, some of `whorn`[text-layer?] did succeed in acquiring
  considerable wealth despite the fact that all circumstances were unfavourable to their being
  allowed to retain it" (p. 54, folio 45).
- **Freight-carrying and the bar.** "The byah who is next man to the Shahbunder offered to give
  three rupees more per load for freight to the King and this was accepted… Dare not bring the
  vessel over the bar for fear she wold not be able to pass it again" (p. 54, folio 45).
- **Port silting as the driver of Sind's port history.** Laribunder silting by 1652 forced
  Aurangabunder (p. 54, folio 45); Shahbunder then displaced Laribunder; Karachi then rose against
  Shahbunder; and Tatta declined "with the growing difficulties of navigating the Lower Indus"
  (p. 58, folio 49).
- **The Moghul mint at Tatta, with a mintage rate negotiated.** "He (Spiller) leaves here 65,000
  abbasis on which Mughal Khan has undertaken to levy no more mintage than is paid in Surat or was
  required by the late Shahbunder. The money should therefore be taken to Tatta and coined."
  (p. 54, folio 45)
- **Cloth manufacture measured by the yard, place by place.** An order for "6000 pieces of Sind
  baftas", preferring "the `Nasarpfir`[text-layer?] make because that is two yards longer than the
  Kandiaro cloth"; Kandiaro cloth latterly "fully 15 yards in length as against `14j`[text-layer?]
  at `Nasarpfir`[text-layer?]"; "The Gambat cloth is about `12!`[text-layer?] yards long but two or
  three inches wider than that of Kandiaro" (pp. 54–55, folios 45–46). In 1656 Spiller had 20,000
  pieces of cotton goods at Tatta for despatch to Surat (p. 55, folio 46).
- **The first factory's commodity list**: baftas and calicoes, saltpetre, indigo, raw silk, iron,
  lead, steel, woollens; funds "usually supplied from `Ahmad!vad`[text-layer?]" (p. 55, folio 46;
  pp. 57, 66, folios 48, 57).
- **Transit dues, bribery and the farming of office, in detail.** At Bucker and Derbella "a boat
  whose dueties to the Prince came to be but `!`[text-layer?] of a rupee should pay 6 rupees
  officers fees (or rather bribes) ; which kind of usadge have caused all Multan men to leave the
  `pla,ce`[text-layer?] and alsoe most of the Tahtah men." A broker spent two months among the
  officers over "the chellehearh"; the "Droga of the chelleheark" took "a bribe of 88 rupees to
  himselfe `a,nd`[text-layer?] companions"; a further "18 rupees more att Derbella jaggatt"; and at
  Tatta clearing a boat "at the gahat" required "moree", which the book glosses as "(perhaps a fee
  for a clearing certificate, muhri)" — Spiller, 30 September 1656 (p. 55, folio 46).
- **Armed pastoral/tribal power outside Moghul control, 1656.** "This Bucksee having abused a chiefe
  man of the Curmuttes or Bulchees he getting free hath raised what force `bee`[text-layer?] could
  make of his owne men and turned rebell… They have not left a towne between Bunder (Laribandar) and
  this place `unplunclered`[text-layer?], carrying away cattle, people and all away" (pp. 55–56,
  folios 46–47).
- **River piracy and the choukee as convoy.** The Samejas "doe not faile to pilladge such boats as
  they can muster"; the factor will "send what servants and peons I have to encounter it about
  Saume (Sann)" and "write to Sume and Sewanna for a choukee to accompany them" (p. 56, folio 47).
- **Famine and plague, 1659–60, quantified by its effect on lading.** Goods at Laribunder "would be
  fewer than usual" — "being the famine and plague in Scinda is so great that it hath swept away
  most part of the people and those that are left are few and what they make is bought by the
  country merchant at any price" (p. 56, folio 47). And, 13 April 1660: "in the latter place neaver
  famine raged worse in any place, the living being hardly able to burye the dead." (p. 56, folio 47)
- **The first factory wound up.** Bell ordered to get in all outstanding debts and keep within his
  allowance, "any excess would be charged to himself personally" (p. 57, folio 48); the *Vine* took
  him with the saltpetre and calicoes from Laribunder to Swally Road, arriving 12 November 1662 —
  "an experiment that was not to be repeated for another ninety-six years" (p. 57, folio 48).
- **Afghan tribute as the structuring fact of Kalhora finance.** The Kalhora's chief difficulty was
  "how to shoulder as lightly as possible the Afghan yoke in the shape of the payment of a yearly
  tribute", paid "partly in woollen goods purchased at low rates from the English merchants and then
  transferred at a high valuation to the Pathans in part payment of the tribute" (p. 57, folio 48).
  Ghulam Shah "reduced the tribute to about eleven lacks" (p. 61, folio 52). Erskine dates the
  tributary period: Scindy, Lahor and Multan "continued under their sovereignty and
  `annualiy`[text-layer?] paid tribute till about `1 757·`[text-layer?]" (p. 66, folio 57).
- **The chupper as an institution.** Afghan officers "under the `appealation`[text-layer?] of
  Chuppers who were sent here for provideing all sorts of necessaries for the army and the cloth was
  usually cut up into coats etc. in Tattah" (p. 61, folio 52); everything they bought "`we.s`
  [text-layer?] free of all duties or impositions", which made them "highly disagreeable to the
  Prince" (p. 61, folio 52). By Callendar's day the resident chopper had been "in Tattah near four
  months in a manner confined to his `bouse`[text-layer?]", allowed only "two or three dellals who
  are tutored by the government", his goods forced on him "at an advance of 50 per cent" (pp. 61–62,
  folios 52–53).
- **The two parwanas and their exact fiscal terms.** Parwana of 22 September 1758, a second order
  "on `I I`[text-layer?] December 1758" (p. 59, folio 50): officers to charge "no more than one and
  a half per cent duty above the market price on all goods purchased by the British agent for export
  and to levy on importation only one half of that paid by other merchants"; no duty on goods
  returned undisposed of; ships' supplies duty free; assistance to buy or build "a house or
  warehouse at the bunder or at Tattah"; but "no other Englishman is to have a house or any
  encouragement"; "all unjust duties were to be remitted but those of export were to be paid as
  usual"; duty remission conditional on the Agent residing at Shahbunder (p. 60, folio 51; cited to
  "Postans : op. cit., p. 284"). Parwana of 22 April 1761 to Erskine at the Kalhoro's camp: all
  Europeans but the English excluded from trade with Sind, plus **salvage rights** — officers of the
  Sind Government to assist at shipwreck, and "wrecked and salvaged goods were to be delivered over
  to the Resident subject only to payment for salvage" (p. 60, folio 51).
- **Who a Kalhora firman is addressed to — the one religious term in the chunk.** "Be it known to
  all Commanders, officers, Facquirs, Farmers and Inhabitants of Darat, Laree Bunder, Aurungabunder,
  Carachee, Dharajah, Chaotra, Massoolie, Nakass, Barbundie, Galabajur, Agar Goozar Rajah Gunt,
  `I6hibar`[text-layer?], Sarkar, Chacha gom, Chacar Hallow, Nasarpoor, Holicandy; Sarkar Sovistan,
  `Cooda-bage`, Sarkar Nohorey Abey etc., and places belonging to Sarkar." The firman is dated in
  both calendars: "The phirmaund dated `1 8th`[text-layer?] Moharum or 22nd September `1 75
  8`[text-layer?]" (p. 64, folio 55).
- **Revenue farming and delallage as the mechanism of extraction.** Pathan traders' duties "were
  settled at `2!`[text-layer?] per cent on all purchases ; but what from the pernicious practice of
  farming the right of delallage and a collusion between the government farmers and Delools I am
  well assured that none of them gett off clear in one day or other for less than 15 per cent"
  (p. 62, folio 53).
- **A measured decline, in three indices, 1758 to c. 1775** (Callendar, p. 62, folio 53): Multan
  merchant houses at Tatta from "about thirty reputable houses… and some from Surat" to "none from
  Surat and but one Multan house of any credit"; raw silk imports from "three to four hundred
  freight bales… annually" to "not exceeded 40 bales on a medium"; and the exchange of the
  imaginary current rupee against silver from "about `i20`[text-layer?] current to `lOO`[text-layer?]
  silver" to "`145·`[text-layer?] current to `roo`[text-layer?] silver", with silver so scarce that
  "any sudden demand in Tattah for but twenty thousand rupees would certainly raise it above 150".
  The country "was rich and populous when it unfortunately fell under the government of this family
  about 35 years agoe, but has gradually declined ever since".
- **The Shikarpur caravan trade and its diversion.** Merchants came down annually "bringing with
  them both money and other goods, such as putchuck, ring shawls etc., and carrying back quantities
  of the different kinds of imported merchandize which were from thence distributed in to the
  internal provinces"; after "the last eight or ten years hardly any have ventured down" (p. 62,
  folio 53).
- **The order of withdrawal, 24 October 1775.** The Prince demanded "50 per cent above the usual
  sale price… for his emolument on all woollens sold to the King of Candahar's Chupper"; on refusal
  he "laid a total prohibition on the sale of woollens at our Factory"; the Company agreed to
  withdraw "with Proper Caution and `Secresy`[text-layer?] lest the Prince `shmild`[text-layer?]
  make any Efforts to detain him", under "the 32 paragraph of their commands dated 6th April
  `1 770`[text-layer?]" (p. 62, folio 53).
- **The factory's physical plant and guard.** Requirement of "two good strong houses, one at Tattah
  and one Aurungabunder and a subaltern with 25 or 30 men… for preventing being robbed, and securing
  the boats in going and coming from ships at the bar which is greatly infested by the Gigas
  robbers" (p. 63, folio 54). By 14 March 1759 the Resident "had hired a house at Tattah and… the
  mud building at Shaw bander would soon be habitable" (p. 65). The Prince separately "wanted him to
  build one Factory at Goodjee `whir,h`[text-layer?] he had made the Bunder and where he had given
  him `perwannaS`[text-layer?] to land all goods `CUStom`[text-layer?] free" (p. 65).
- **Sepoys as an export and a contract.** Supply of sepoys from Sind to Bombay was "in the hands of
  a contractor to whom money was advanced"; the arrangement was unsatisfactory, letter of 12 October
  1759 (p. 64, folio 55). Establishment reduced from "twenty companies" to "eight companies of the
  established number", supernumerary officers' pay of "sixteen thousand (16,000) rupees per annum"
  paid `ofi`[text-layer?] (p. 65).
- **Salaries and private trade.** Beaven employed "at the usual allowance of Rs. 30 per month"
  because no Covenanted servant could be spared (p. 64, folio 55); the earlier factory had "ill-paid
  factors largely engaged in private trade" (p. 59, folio 50); the President "has had a house and
  servants there ever since he has presided here" (p. 63, folio 54).
- **Woollens: the volumes.** The vend of woollens for Scindy, Multan and Lahor "never exceed from 5
  to 10,000 rupees in one year, of which the greatest part was purchased by the Multan Merchants
  agents"; the broker Bumba `Riipchand`[text-layer?] "will contract to take off about 100 bales of
  woolens annually", half the price payable within six months of arrival and the rest within the
  year, on one per cent vakeelage (p. 66, folio 57). Woollens were "very little known or sought
  after… till Nadir Shaw's conquest of India in 1740", the peak being the tribute years c. 1751–57
  when "Persian chuppers who came down to receive the tribute" found them cheaper in Scindy than via
  Persia or "from the Caspian Sea by the way of Mesched" (p. 66, folio 57).
- **Arms and metals as princely demand.** Iron, lead and steel were "formerly taken by the present
  prince's father and eldest brother, who were fond of making arms of different kinds and keeping by
  them a large store of these articles as well as keeping their ships and vessels in repair"
  (p. 66, folio 57); lead by 1758 was "in so little demand that… what went there four or five years
  ago is all returned to Bombay" (p. 63, folio 54).
- **Grain and the date of the turn.** Erskine's benchmark: Sind "as plentiful in grain and most
  other necessaries of life as it ever was till about the year `1 75 7`[text-layer?]" (p. 66,
  folio 57).
- **Diplomatic gifts and sanads.** The Maratha agents "finished their treaty and presented the
  Prince with a sannud from the Government of Scind, with horse, `zigar`[text-layer?] etc., for
  which public `rejoieings`[text-layer?] were made", 10 December 1759 (p. 65).
- **Protocol as a trade instrument.** The Resident was forced to court, "our Vakeel" carried thither
  by the Prince's officers, and pilots refused "till too late to carry the Tartar over the bar";
  Bombay backed his refusal to go and held that unless he "is sent for in a creditable manner"
  further attempts at a factory would be "improper and in vain" (p. 65).
- **Coastal piracy on both sides.** The Company's fleet was "more than a match for the pirates which
  in those days infested the Gujarat, Kathiawar and Sind coasts" (p. 58, folio 49); "indeed the
  whole coast are rogues on both `s�des`[text-layer?] to the eastward and westward" (p. 63,
  folio 54).

## Arguments and interpretations

Sorley writes as a 1940 Indian Civil Service author; the following are recorded as **his**
judgements, not as fact.

- **Method stated openly.** The records "are as dramatically and graphically written as
  `as�-ate`[text-layer?] the letters of Fremlen, Scrivener and Spiller and like them may be
  `··-allowed`[text-layer?] to tell their own story for themselves" (p. 59, folio 50); Callendar's
  narrative is "so illuminating and so well expressed that there is every `j ustification`
  [text-layer?] for quoting it extensively" (p. 61, folio 52).
- **The author holds that Hindu traders already dominated big business under the Moghuls**, and that
  the escheat custom explains why their wealth did not survive them (p. 54, folio 45).
- **The author explains English suspicion of the Dutch structurally, not morally** — "the
  Commonwealth and Holland were then not only bitter trade rivals but engaged in war against each
  other in Europe" (p. 55, folio 46).
- **A gendered aside offered as a general finding about Moghul administration**: "even in Moghul
  days officials were liable at times to be under the thumb of their wives", introducing the Tatta
  governor's wife who "beares the greatest sway and commands all" (p. 56, folio 47).
- **Sorley partly exculpates the Kalhora**: "the fault is not to be laid entirely at the door of the
  `Kalh6ra`[text-layer?] who had their own difficulties to contend against", i.e. the Afghan tribute
  (p. 57, folio 48). He nonetheless calls Sarfaraz Khan "that tyrant" (p. 61, folio 52) and the Sind
  regime "an unreasonable and capricious native government" (p. 59, folio 50).
- **The origin of Karachi is argued causally**, and attributed to Afghan tax avoidance rather than
  to geography or British initiative: the Afghans "sought to develop a new route via Karachi and
  Mekran and Kelat, instead of by the Indus valley, since this had the `ine�timable`[text-layer?]
  advantage to them of avoiding the territory of the `Kalh6ra`[text-layer?] and the exactions of a
  host of minor officials" (p. 58, folio 49).
- **The Company's restraint in Sind is admired and explicitly linked to the conquest of Bengal.**
  "it is impossible not to admire the `adroitness`[text-layer?] with which the Sind factors
  maintained a complete political neutrality"; their conduct foreshadows "the administrative wisdom
  which stood the Company in such good stead when Clive by assuming the Diwani of Bengal changed it
  from a trading corporation to a political authority exercising sovereign civil rights. In Sind,
  however, this change never took place. The Company was always very much the timid guest of the
  Sind Government" (p. 58, folio 49). The temptations resisted are named: lending ships "for an
  expedition against Cutch" and recovering Ghulam Shah's alleged property from a Gujarat trader.
- **The two factories contrasted as pre-modern and modern.** The second shows "all the signs of
  modern big business" — properly maintained accounts, an organized fleet — against the first's
  "irregular and uncertain voyages, its ill-paid factors largely engaged in private trade, its
  difficulties of communication" (p. 59, folio 50).
- **A period judgement about the eighteenth century, with England as the standard.** "Judged by

## Doubts

- **This chunk contains no archive material about any shrine.** Across pp. 54–66 there is no shrine,
  tomb, dargah, ʿurs, khanqah, sajjada nashin, endowment, custodian, pilgrimage, building date or
  saint. The only religious word is `Facquirs`, one item in a firman's list of addressees (p. 64,
  folio 55). **Nothing here can support a factual statement about `bhit-bhit-shah` or any other row
  as a site.**
- **The mapping to `bhit-bhit-shah` rests on the running head alone.** The head `SHAH ABDUL LATIF OF
  BHIT` appears on every page of the chunk, in thirteen different states of spacing damage, but the
  body text never names the poet, never mentions Bhit, and gives no date, order or lineage for him.
  This is an identification made from the book's title, which is not itself in this chunk.
- **Three place names could be read onto archive rows, and should not be without a human decision.**
  (a) `Sewanna` (p. 56, folio 47, "shall write to Sume and Sewanna for a choukee") and `Sarkar
  Sovistan` (p. 64, folio 55, in the firman's addressee list) both plausibly denote Sehwan, the
  location of `lal-shahbaz-qalandar` and near that of `shrine-of-lakhi-shah-saddar` — but the book
  glosses `Saume` as "(Sann)" and `Cora Bollah` as "(possibly Ghorabari)" while glossing neither of
  these, so the identification is mine and not the book's, and in any case the references are to a
  police post and a revenue division, not to a shrine. (b) `Halla chand`, Ghulam Shah's "future
  residence" (p. 65), could be Hala, the location of `shrine-of-makhdoom-nooh-hala`, but the book
  does not say so and prints it as two words. (c) `Siccarpore` / `Sicc.a Poer` (pp. 62, 66, folios
  53, 57) is the town of three archive rows (`gurdwara-sach-khand-sahib`,
  `khatwari-darbar-shikarpur`, `sant-bhagat-kanwar-ram-temple-chak`) but is named here only as a
  trading centre. **None of the three is recorded above as a mapping.**

- **PDF p. 65 prints no page number.** Its running head is `S H A H A B D U L LAT I F O F B H I T`

- **Body text is broken by stray layout artefacts in two places on p. 54.** A lone `·` interrupts

- **`E.F.I.`, `P.D.D.` and `S.P.D.D.` are never expanded in this chunk.** They are used throughout

- **`Postans : op. cit., p. 284.` (p. 60, folio 51) is a short-form citation whose full reference is
  not in this chunk**, so the parwana's terms are quoted here at one remove, from Sorley quoting
  Postans quoting the Bombay Government Records.
- **Two brokers named "Bumba-" are not connected by the book.** `Bumbae` "(probably Bambomal)",
  c. 1650 (p. 54, folio 45), and `Bumba Riipchand`[text-layer?], active 1758–60 (p. 66, folio 57).
  A century apart; **do not merge them.**
- **Ghulam Shah's name is spelt five ways** across the chunk — `Ghulam Shah Kalh6ro` / `KalhOro`
  (pp. 57, 59, 63), `Golam Shaw` (p. 63), `Gulam Shah` (p. 61), `Gulii m Shah`[text-layer?] (p. 65)
  — and the dynasty as `Kalh6ra`, `KalhOra`, `Kalhorn`[text-layer?] and `Kalhoro`. `Kalhorn` for
  `Kalhora` (pp. 58, 60, 61, 65) is a text-layer fault, not a variant spelling; the `6` and `O` for
  `ó` are the same fault.
- **Two dates for the same tributary turning point sit unreconciled in the chunk.** Callendar's
  account has the Pathans making Sind tributary after Nadir Shah, with the Company's factory founded
  1758 (pp. 61–62, folios 52–53); Erskine dates Nadir Shah's conquest of India to 1740 and the end
  of tributary payment to "about 1757" (p. 66, folio 57); the Bombay Castle letter of 8 April 1758
  says Sind had been tributary to the Persians "ever since Nadir Shaw's conquest of the Mogul
  Empire" and was then subdued by "the Patans in their way to Delhi" (p. 63, folio 54). These are
  three witnesses in the book's own quoted documents, reported as they stand.
- **The chunk is cut at both ends.** It opens inside an extract already in progress (p. 54, folio
  45) and stops mid-sentence inside Erskine's report of 14 November 1760 on saltpetre (p. 66, folio
  57), so the saltpetre figures that sentence was about are in the next chunk.
