# sorley_shah_abdul_latif_of_bhit — chunk 006 — pp. 80–94

Continuous prose, no verse. The chunk falls inside Sorley's survey of **European travellers'
accounts of Sind**: the close of the Nicholas Withington narrative (pp. 80–81, section number not
in this chunk), then three numbered sections as printed — `(2) Frey Sebastian Manrique (1641)`
(p. 81), `(3) Niccolao Manucci (1659)` (p. 86), `(4) Alexander Hamilton (1699)` (p. 89) — and it
breaks off four lines into a new chapter section, `VI. Trading conditions in Sind in the
seventeenth and eighteenth centuries` / `(a) The conveniences. of trade` (p. 94).

**Printed page numbers are set in the running head of every page and the offset is stable: printed
page = PDF page − 9.** PDF p. 80 carries printed page **71**; PDF p. 94 carries printed page **85**;
the offset of 9 holds unbroken across all fifteen pages. References below give both, as
`(p. 90, folio 81)`. The running head itself is damaged on most pages — see Doubts.

**Shah Abdul Latif is named exactly once in the chunk** (p. 90, folio 81). **No shrine, tomb, ʿurs,
khanqah, darbar, sajjada nashin, endowment, langar or pilgrimage is mentioned anywhere in
pp. 80–94, and no karamat of any kind is narrated** — the `## Legends and miracles (karamat)`
heading is therefore omitted rather than padded. The chunk's value to the archive is almost
entirely economic and institutional: customs, caravans, serais, looms, debt enforcement, siege
prices, river transport.

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **bhit-bhit-shah** (the book calls him "Shah Abdul Latif"; the running head calls the book
  "SHAH ABDUL LATIF OF BHIT"): **the single dated statement about him in this chunk fixes his age
  against a traveller's visit, not against a birth year** — "When Captain Hamilton visited
  Laribunder in 1699 Shah Abdul Latif was a boy of about eight or nine years of age and the
  Kalhora were beginning to assert them­selves in Upper Sind" (p. 90, folio 81). **The chunk gives
  no birth year, no death year, no silsila, no pir, no place of burial and no mention of Bhit
  itself**: all **(not stated)**. Any birth date derived from "eight or nine years of age" would be
  an inference the book does not make here.
- **bhit-bhit-shah**: **Sorley's claim about the evidentiary value of Hamilton** — "He is the only
  European traveller whose narrative is actually contemporary with the life of Shah Abdul Latif"
  (p. 90, folio 81). This is the stated reason the Hamilton section belongs in a book about the
  poet; Withington (1614), Manrique (1641) and Manucci (1659) are, on the book's own logic, all
  pre-contemporary. Recorded as Sorley's judgement, not as fact.
- **bhit-bhit-shah**: **the political frame the book puts around his boyhood** is one clause long —
  "the Kalhora were beginning to assert them­selves in Upper Sind" (p. 90, folio 81), set in 1699.
  The chunk says nothing further about the Kalhora, names no Kalhora ruler and gives no dates for
  them.
- **lal-shahbaz-qalandar** (the book names only the place, **"Sehwan"**, never the saint or the
  shrine): **Sehwan appears three times, purely as territory, revenue post and battlefield.** The
  Indus narrows "in uncultivated country in the territory of Sehwan", which was "a spot notorious
  for pirates, as we know from Withington and from the East India Company letters of the
  seventeenth century factory" (p. 83, folio 74). Manrique's boat reached Sehwan two days after the
  pirate attack, "where the custom house was passed `�i�hout` `trq.uble[text-layer?]`" (p. 84,
  folio 75), and "Below. Sehwan the `_country` was once more `cultivat�d[text-layer?]` and
  inhabited" (p. 84, folio 75). Aurangzeb's divisions nearly cut Dara Shikoh off "in a river fight
  near Sehwan" (p. 87, folio 78). **Nothing here supports a statement about the shrine**; see
  Doubts on whether a bare toponym should be attached to this row at all.

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- **Tatta** — "the metropolis · of Sind" (p. 84, folio 75) and, in Hamilton, "the emporium of the
  province, a very large and rich city. It is about three miles long and one and a half broad and
  is 40 miles distance from Laribunder" (p. 91, folio 82). Sorley's own verdict: "Tatta was by far
  the wealthiest town in Sind in these days and the country round it was the centre of ·a thriving
  agriculture" (p. 94, folio 85).
- **Laribunder / Debal** — "The Augustinians had a. settlement at Debal or Laribunder in Sind. It
  was the.seat of a Vicar" (p. 81, folio 72). Hamilton found it "five or six leagues from the sea
  on a branch of the Indus" and able to take ships of 200 tons; "It is but a village of 100 houses
  built of crooked sticks and mud ; but it has a `largE'.[text-layer?] stone fort with four or five
  great guns mounted upon it" (p. 90, folio 81).
- **Bakhar** — island fortress in the Indus, besieged 1659; "This fortress is in the middle of the
  mighty river Sindi (Indus) , founded upon the live rock, stones from which could be used as
  flints for muskets. In the middle was a cavalier (tower) overlooking both banks of the river"
  (p. 88, folio 79). Also "a town where there is considerable traffic on account of the camel-trains
  that leave every year for Persia, Khorasan and other distant places" (pp. 82–83, folios 73–74).
- **Sukkur and Rohri** — the imperial army learned of Dara's flight on reaching Sukkur (p. 86,
  folio 77); the Bakhar garrison "bombarded the towns of Sukkur and Rohri" (p. 88, folio 79). In a
  footnote Sorley argues Manrique's garden-and-grove description "is truer `o` Rohri than `o`
  Bakhar" and weighs Raverty's surmise that Bakhar may then have been an isthmus to the Sukkur
  bank — "what Manrique calls Bakhar may really have been ' Old Sukkur '" (p. 83, folio 74,
  footnote 1).
- **Parkar** — Withington's refuge after being stripped by his guard: "one of them promised to lead
  us into the. way that goes to Parkar"; "the sixth day at night we came to `Park:ar[text-layer?]`
  miserably weary and hungry" (p. 80, folio 71); the bania "returned to Parkar, where he had
  business for ten days" (p. 81, folio 72). Named as a place only — see Doubts.
- **Lahore** — Manrique's point of departure downriver (p. 82, folio 73) and the site of his
  grenade demonstration "in a garden at Lahore where they created a great sensation" (p. 83,
  folio 74, footnote 4); Dara's cannon foundry (p. 88, folio 79); the place Khalilullah Khan
  "returned in disgust to" and where the eunuch Primavera "was killed shortly afterwards" (p. 89,
  folio 80).
- **Multan** — only as the start of Dara's river flight, "the flight down the river from Multan to
  Bakhar" (p. 87, folio 78), and in a footnote to "Aurangzeb's Governorship of Multan" (p. 86,
  folio 77, footnote 3).
- **Nicholas Withington** — English factor; robbed, witnesses a multiple murder, journeys on foot
  from the mountains to Parkar and back to Ahmedabad, "the first of March 1614" (p. 80, folio 71).
- **Frey Sebastian Manrique** — "a Portuguese from Oporto, who took orders in Goa in 1604",
  attached to "the Bengal Augustinian Mission . of `Hiigli[text-layer?]` by the Father Provincial
  for India, Father Luiz Coutino, about 1629", sent to Arakan, in India 1640–1, "brought him to
  Sind in `164I[text-layer?]`"; later "Difinidor and Procurador General for the Portuguese estates
  in the Curia at Rome" with the title of Maestro; "murdered by his own Portuguese servant in
  London in 1669", the body hidden in a box and thrown into the Thames, a death Sorley links to
  "the English King's (Charles II's) leanings towards the Church of Rome" (p. 81, folio 72).
- **Father Jorge de la Natividad** — "the Superior of the Mission" at Laribunder, with whom
  Manrique "talked over … the re­building of the church" (p. 85, folio 76).
- **Niccolao Manucci** — "the Venetian adventurer whom Irvine has called ' A Pepys of Moghul
  India '", who after "the death of his English master Lord Bellomont, entered the
  `servicez[text-layer?]` of Dara Shikoh as an artilleryman"; "cannot have been more than nineteen
  or twenty years of age" in 1659 (p. 86, folio 77).
- **Captain Alexander Hamilton** — "a Scot and a sort of merchant adventurer and traveller, a sea
  captain", by his own account spending 1688–1723 "trading and travelling by sea and land … between
  the Cape of Good Hope and the Island of Japan" (p. 89, folio 80); his *New Account of the East
  Indies* "published in Edinburgh in 1727 and dedicated to the Duke of Hamilton" (p. 90, folio 81).
- **Dara Shikoh, Aurangzeb, Shah Jahan, Asaf Khan** — Shah Jahan appears as "the Emperor Corrombo
  (i.e. Khurram, the name Shah Jahan had borne as Prince)" (p. 82, folio 73); "Prince `Assufa
  Khaq[text-layer?]` (Asaf Khan)" commissions Manrique's errand (p. 82, folio 73) and reappears as
  "`Assofo[text-layer?]` Khan" (p. 84, folio 75). Dara is pursued from Lahore into Sind, betrayed

- **Shaikh Misr and Saf Shikan** — the two divisional commanders of Aurangzeb's pursuing army, right

- **Khwaja Basant / Primavera** and **Sayid Abdur Razak** — "Dara's eunuch Basant, or Khwaj a
  Basant, also called Primavera by Manucci", joint commanders of the Bakhar defence (p. 86,

- **Khalilullah Khan** and **Bahadur Khan** — the reinforcing commander from Lahore and the enemy
  commander who granted the garrison's terms (pp. 88–89, folios 79–80).
- **Sulaiman Shikoh's wife and young sons** — left in the fort, "' much cherished as being his

- **Thévenot (1667)** — printed throughout as `TMvenot[text-layer?]`; "The French traveller

- **The Jats, the "Baliichis and Mekrans (Mekranis)", the Jams** — the Jats appear as charitable

- **An unnamed Raja** — the murderer of Withington's merchant companions, who nonetheless spared
  him, "' telling them I was of a very far country and would do them no hurt, wanting language '"

- **An unnamed bania of Ahmedabad** — met at Parkar; "offered me what money I would have, not

## Practices, institutions, economy

This is where the chunk's weight lies.

- **Customs as the central fiscal institution, described four times over.** Manrique's imperial
  pass gave "free passage through all the choquidares or customs posts as I had to travel through
  the states of several princes who, though tributary and vassal to the Great Mogul, yet
  `exer�ised[text-layer?]` full jurisdiction as well over the customs as over the punishment of
  transgressors of the law" (p. 82, folio 73). At Bakhar "There was little trouble at the custom
  house" (p. 83, folio 74); at Sehwan "the custom house was passed `�i�hout`
  `trq.uble[text-layer?]`" (p. 84, folio 75); at Tatta the ship "arrived too late for the officials
  at the custom house so that the passengers· had to stay on board · till `nejd[text-layer?]` day",
  and bedding and "' other necessary utensils '" were "not liable `fo[text-layer?]` duty" (p. 84,
  folio 75).
- **The cash value of an exemption, stated.** Manrique was pressed by the merchants to lend his
  firman to their goods: "' my `orcler[text-layer?]` would have secured two thousand rupees, a
  `m9st[text-layer?]` `welcoine[text-layer?]` addition for defraying the `expense·s` of that
  Mission '" — and he refused, on the ground that the Church "does not under the cloak
  `ofp�eserving[text-layer?]` or increasing her `mis�ions[text-layer?]` undertake to trade between
  `di�erent[text-layer?]` countries" (p. 84, folio 75).
- **How an imperial order was recognised and executed.** The Governor of Tatta "placed it most
  submissively three times on `his_head[text-layer?]`, kissing it a similar number of times. After
  reading it, he sent for· the customs officials and before `n;ie[text-layer?]` told them to pass·
  the merchandise through at `o�ce[text-layer?]`; charging only low `rate�[text-layer?]` ·of duty"
  (p. 84, folio 75). Sorley's gloss: the Governor "was able to recognize [it] at once
  `fr�m[text-layer?]` the writing and seal" (p. 84, folio 75).
- **Debt enforcement and the Kazi's court.** The Governor of Tatta "granted Hamilton freedom of
  customs duty and other charges on his goods and also gave him the right, if he did not receive
  proper payment, to imprison the debtors instead of having to send them to the Kazi's court. He
  also said that if the purchasers did not pay he would sell their wives, children or nearest
  relations to make good the debt." Hamilton: "' This privilege … did me singular service when the
  time of payment came.'" (p. 93, folio 84) **This is the only reference to a religious-legal
  institution in the chunk**, and it is a reference to bypassing it.
- **Textile manufacture at Tatta, with a figure.** Manrique noted "' the vast quantities of cotton
  collected there from which on over two thousand looms rich cloths of various kinds are woven and
  exported to `rriany[text-layer?]` parts of Asia as well as to Portugal '"; also "a species of
  silk from which excellent taffetas and ' tafedrias ' are made" (p. 85, folio 76).
- **The hide and leather trade.** "The importance of the export trade in hides is noted, the
  abundance of buffaloes and manufacture of ' the lovely leather which the Portuguese ·style
  " Sinde leather ", ornamented with back stitch work in different coloured silks, in fine designs,
  lined and furnished off with fringes of silk at the ends '." Used "as table covers and as hangings
  in reception rooms and as bed covers, as they are very soft and cool in the hot weather" and "for
  house trappings"; also "' such back stitched quilts and the excellent mattresses called Sind
  Mattresses '", which Sorley says "appear to have been a kind of ' razai '" (p. 85, folio 76).
- **The port and its foreign population.** Manrique "states that the city had a large ' Firangi '
  population and that its port was filled with ships laden with every kind of merchandise" (p. 85,
  folio 76).
- **The caravan (kafila) as an armed commercial institution.** "It was the practice to protect the
  camel caravans (kafilas) with a guard of one hundred to two hundred horses" (p. 91, folio 82).
  Hamilton's own kafila was "' of 1500 beasts and as many more men and women, besides 200 horse for
  our guard '" (p. 92, folio 83). Thévenot gives a different scale: "caravans commonly consisted of
  above . two hundred wagons", each merchant of consequence attended by "' four soldiers or four
  peons '" (p. 91, folio 82).
- **Guards colluding with robbers.** "' But often these protectors suffer the cafillas to be robbed,
  pretending the robbers are too numerous to be restrained by their small force and afterwards come
  in shares with the robbers.'" A 1699 raid took out "the guard of 250 ·horse and 500 of the
  merchants and carriers" with "four or five thousand ' villains '" (p. 91, folio 82).
- **The serai, described twice and as an institution.** Hamilton's stage between Tatta and
  Laribunder was "a ' mud wall fort called Dungham, a very proper English name for such a
  fortification '", built "midway between Tatta and Laribunder to secure the kafila from being set
  upon· at night, ' who all lodge within it, men and beasts promiscuously '"; "' There are about
  twenty little cottages built close to it and the residents breed fowls, goats and sheep to sell
  to passengers and these are all the houses to be seen between Tatta and Laribunder.'" (p. 92,
  folio 83). Manucci's general account: serais "' are like fortified places with their bastions and
  strong gates ; most of them are built of stone or of brick '"; an official closes the gates at
  sunset and calls out that everyone must "' picket his horses by the fore and hind legs '"; at six
  in the morning "' the watchman gives three warnings '" and "' if anyone suspects that any of his
  property is missing the doors are not opened till the lost thing is . found '"; "' These serais
  are only intended for travellers (soldiers do `nof[text-layer?]` go into them) . Each one of them
  might hold more or less from 800 to `rooo[text-layer?]` persons with their horses, camels,
  carriages '" (pp. 92–93, folios 83–84).
- **Saltpetre from inhabited ground — Sorley's aside, and a genuine economic note.** "Indeed it was
  camping grounds and old inhabited sites which were the chief source of supply of saltpetre in
  those days, the mineral being removed by the refinement of the manure­laden soil." (p. 92,
  folio 83)
- **Land transport: no wheels between port and capital.** "All merchandise between Laribunder and
  Tatta was conveyed by pack animals ; there were no carts engaged on this business, the nature of
  the country with its covering of bushes and scrub combined with its marshy character making road
  and wheeled traffic very difficult." (p. 91, folio 82) Against which Thévenot reports "wagons, for
  carrying goods, with solid wheels but no spokes and drawn by eight or ten oxen" and "' the finest
  palanquins that are in all Indostan are made at Tatta '" (p. 91, folio 82).
- **River craft as rented commercial space.** "' Their vessels are called Kisties of several sizes.
  The largest can lade about 200 tons. They are flat bottomed and on each side `_cabins` are built
  from stem to stem that overhang about two feet, and in each cabin is a kitchen and a place of
  exoneration which falls directly in the water '" — Sorley adds "(a characteristic still of the
  Indus river boats)". "' These cabins are hired out to passengers and the hold, being made into
  separate . apartments, are let out to freighters so that every one has a lock on his cabin and
  apartment in the hold and has `his·` goods always ready to dispose on at what places he finds his
  market '" (pp. 93–94, folios 84–85).
- **Riverine fishing technique, described in detail.** Fishermen "' dispense with the encumbrance of
  nets and assistants for they go out fishing on large earthen vessels with the circumference of ten
  to twelve palms in breadth, flattened at the rim and open at the top in a big circular aperture
  just of a size to receive the front of a man's stomach '", the man lying on it and "' directing his
  earthen ship with his legs, `·his` hands being busy with the spear '"; they "' furnished us with
  most excellent shad very cheaply '" (p. 82, folio 73).
- **Food supply and prices as a recurring measure of order.** Manrique found "' an abundance of good
  cheap provisions in every place we anchored at '" (p. 82, folio 73) and at Bakhar "The market was
  well stocked `w�th[text-layer?]` provisions owing to the high `-fertility` and productivity of the
  district" (p. 83, folio 74). Against this, **siege prices at Bakhar in 1659**: "' For two days
  before the evacuation I bought two calves for six hundred rupees and paid `o'ne[text-layer?] rupee
  for every ounce of butter. Without exaggerating I bought one chicken for thirteen rupees. '"
  (p. 89, folio 80). The fort was surrendered on Dara's order "to save the garrison from famine and
  destruction" (p. 89, folio 80).
- **Military pay, in figures.** Manucci was made captain of the Europeans and "' ordered them to
  give me five thousand rupees to divide among my men and doubled my pay. It had been one hundred
  and fifty and he made it three hundred rupees '", with the promise of nobility, "' the present of
  a serpao (serapa) '" and "' a boat-load of Persian and Kabul wine '" (p. 87, folio 78). A robe
  "(sarapa) and some bottles of rose water" were the reward for fidelity under fire (p. 88,
  folio 79). Manrique before leaving Lahore "engage[d] `more·` sepahis or mercenary soldiers",
  insisting "on their having their priming powder refined and their ' bandocos ' repaired" (p. 83,
  folio 74, footnote 4).
- **Logistics of the river flight.** "Dara's train consisted of five hundred and seven boats … and
  eight cannons carrying shot of from 60 .to `rzo[text-layer?]` pounds besides light artillery",
  each boat carrying "more or less a hundred tons of cargo"; the garrison left behind was "two
  thousand selected men … Pathans, Sayids, Moghuls and `Rajpiits[text-layer?]`, and twenty-two
  Europeans of different nationalities, and servants" (p. 87, folio 78).
- **Church property as a state matter.** Manrique's firman granted "permission for the
  reconstruction in the Principality of Sinde of our churches and Residencies which the Emperor
  Corrombo … had himself a few years earlier ordered to be razed to , the ground and destroyed"
  (pp. 81–82, folios 72–73). Fifty-eight years later the building was still derelict: "' The
  Portuguese had formerly a church at the east end of the city. The house is still
  `entii-e[text-layer?]` and in the vestry are some old pictures of saints and some holy vestments
  which they proffered to ·sell, but I was no `m�rchant[text-layer?]` for such bargains.'" (p. 94,
  folio 85)
- **Hindu cremation on the riverbank, performed for a mercenary killed in the pirate attack.** "The
  voyage was in fact interrupted `QY[text-layer?]` the death ceremonies of the dead man whose body
  was cremated with Hindu rites on the bank of the river" (p. 83, folio 74). Manrique's own words
  are given in full contempt — see Arguments.
- **An unnamed order of women renunciants at Tatta, reported hostilely and worth flagging as the
  chunk's only religious institution in Sind.** Manrique describes "' a certain order of hypocritical
  women devotees, or to put it more plainly, prostitutes ', who, while professing to despise the
  world, `e:µgaged[text-layer?]` in the most shameful form of sexual immorality in the very
  streets" (p. 85, folio 76), and adds: "' But what is worse is that these women who 'act in this
  disgraceful way in less frequented streets are held to be the greatest renunciators of the world
  and its ways.'" (p. 86, folio 77). **The book gives the order no name, no location beyond Tatta,
  no affiliation and no date**; the only datum that survives the polemic is that renunciation
  carried public prestige.
- **Violence on the trade roads, itemised.** A Raja's men strangled two merchants and their five
  men "' tying their camels' ropes about their necks and with a short truncheon twisted the ropes
  till they were strangled '" (p. 80, folio 71), and the same Raja "' took from me likewise eighty
  rupees of the Company's monies '" (p. 80, folio 71). Withington's own guard then stripped him,
  leaving "' neither me nor my men anything, save only our breeches '", including "' a quilt of
  escite (chintz) '" (p. 80, folio 71). Hamilton's answer to the same problem was to have his
  seamen shoot the robbers' heralds dead, twice (p. 92, folio 83).
- **Charity of the poor as the counterweight, in both narratives.** "' The poor people made much
  `o[text-layer?]` `f[text-layer?]` us, giving `u s` such victuals `a s` they had '"; "' every
  night we came to a little village and begged for our victuals finding all the inhabitants
  charitable people '" (p. 80, folio 71).
- **The Governor of Tatta's hospitality as a measure of the office.** Encamped six miles out "in
  command of an army of eight or ten thousand men intending to punish the robbers of the caravans",
  he sent Hamilton "' `art[text-layer?] ox, five sheep, as many goats, twenty fowls and fifty
  pigeons with sweetmeats and fruits in abundance '" and an invitation "' to drink a dish of coffee
  '"; Hamilton's return gift was "' a looking glass of about five pounds in value, a gun, a pair of
  pistols well gilded, a sabre blade and dagger blade gilded and a glass knife for his tobacco and
  an embroidered standish (`st3:nd[text-layer?]` dish) '" (p. 93, folio 84).

## Arguments and interpretations

Sorley's own voice is unusually forward in this chunk; his position as a 1940 Indian Civil Service
author is visible in most of it, and the judgements below are recorded as his.

- **The Withington narrative is read as evidence about Mughal state capacity.** "This very human
  narrative shows how insufficient was the Moghul's authority to preserve safety on the highways of
  commerce, and displays the lawlessness which prevailed outside the main towns." (p. 81, folio 72)
- **And the same narrative is read sentimentally.** "More pleasing are the kindness and charity of
  the common people to a luckless foreigner, and the wonderful generosity of the Hindu merchant who
  found his English friend in sore distress." (p. 81, folio 72)
- **Sorley grades his sources explicitly.** Manrique "is generally `unsympathetic�[text-layer?]` in
  particular, towards Islam while regarding the practices of Hinduism as the delusions of a poor
  misguided portion of humanity. He is, however, an acute and accurate observer but is no stylist
  and he does not hesitate to borrow without `acki:iowledgement[text-layer?]` from the works of
  other observers." (p. 81, folio 72) Manucci "is a vivid story-teller and probably something of a
  romancer as well", though nothing in the Sind material seems "very incredible or much overdrawn"
  (p. 86, folio 77). Hamilton "appears to have been uncommonly well educated" for "a seaman of
  those days" (p. 89, folio 80). Where sources conflict, "Hamilton as the . eye witness is entitled
  to greater credence" over Thévenot (p. 91, folio 82).
- **Manrique's religious prejudice is named as a limit on his testimony.** Sorley says his
  judgement of Tatta was "largely coloured by his religious prejudices" (p. 85, folio 76), and that
  he described the cremation "with a certain amount of supercilious contempt" — quoting the phrase
  "' their useless diabolical rites prompted by motives foolish in themselves but most saddening to
  me when I thought of the blind thraldom in which the common enemy of the human race held those
  barbarians '" (p. 83, folio 74).
- **A comparative defence of Mughal administration against European standards of the same century.**
  "While it is the fashion to decry the inefficiency of the Moghul Empire for its inability to
  secure the safety of wayfarers, it must not be forgotten that in England, France and Germany of
  the `sam�[text-layer?]` period travel was still an adventure and that every traveller armed
  himself to resist the attacks of highwaymen." (p. 90, folio 81) Sorley extends this over most of
  a page, with the English stage coach as his measure.
- **But he also holds that Mughal order did not improve across the century.** "The allusion to the
  Jams shows that there had been little improvement in their behaviour since the time of Withington
  over eighty years earlier, and that the later Moghul empire in Sind was no better than the early
  one in preserving law and order." (p. 91, folio 82) Against which he reads Manrique's easy passage

## Doubts

- **No transcriber flags.** This chunk carries **no `[OCR?]` and no `[illegible]` markers** — the
  route is `text_layer`, not vision OCR, and the extractor left no flags. **This is not a claim that
  the text is sound**: the damage inventoried below was found by reading, not by flag, and a
  human should assume more of it than is listed.
- **The mapping of "Sehwan" to `lal-shahbaz-qalandar` is a judgement, and a weak one.** The chunk
  names Sehwan only as a territory on the Indus, a custom house and the site of a river fight
  (pp. 83, 84, 87; folios 74, 75, 78). **It never mentions Lal Shahbaz Qalandar, a shrine, a tomb,
  a dargah or any religious activity at Sehwan.** The row is listed above so the pages are findable,
  not because the chunk says anything about the shrine. The same reasoning was **not** extended to
  the other archive-bearing towns the chunk names in passing — Sukkur, Rohri, Multan, Lahore — and
  a human should decide whether bare toponyms belong in the archive section at all.
- **"Parkar" (pp. 80, 81; folios 71, 72) could correspond to either of two archive rows and is
  matched to neither here.** The Nagarparkar area holds both
  `nagarparkar-jain-temples-nagarparkar-cultural-landscape` and
  `churrio-jabal-durga-mata-temple`, and the chunk gives only the bare form "Parkar" /
  "`Park:ar[text-layer?]`" as a desert town two days' journey from somewhere unnamed, with a bania
  resident and trade links to Ahmedabad and Radhanpur. **No temple, shrine or hill is mentioned.**
  Do not attach these pages to either row without further evidence.
- **The date of Withington's journey wobbles inside the chunk.** The narrative itself is dated
  "the first of March 1614" (p. 80, folio 71); Sorley dates Manrique's 1641 account "twenty-seven
  years" after Withington (p. 82, folio 73), which gives 1614; but he also writes of "the guards
  paid by Withington in 1613 for safe conduct" and of Hamilton's 1699 visit falling "over eighty
  years" after Withington (p. 91, folio 82). **1613 and 1614 are both printed; the chunk does not
  reconcile them**, and the earlier part of the Withington section is not in this chunk.
- **"that year's foundation of the river" (p. 86, folio 77) is very probably a damaged word and is
  left undecided.** The sentence — "much of the country in the vicinity of the Indus must have been
  under water as a result of that year's `foundation[text-layer?]` of the river" — reads as though
  it should be *inundation*, but the text layer prints "foundation" and it is recorded as printed.
  Note that "founded upon the live rock" occurs two pages later (p. 88, folio 79) in a genuine
  sense, so the word is not simply a stray.
