## Practices, institutions, economy

This is the substance of the chunk and is given in full.

### The pir, the murid and the religious hierarchy as a class

- **Sorley's central proposition about Sindhi religion, stated twice.** "The social religion of Sind was
  built up round the reverence of the murid for the murshid, and the sarn;tity[text-layer?] of the Pir."
  (pp. 166–167, folios 157–158) And: "The system goes back for its roots to the beginning of Islam, but
  few Muslims have carried a respect for sanctity so far." (p. 167, folio 158)
- **The religious classes named as a list, and as a power.** "The Sayids, the Pirs, the Pirzadas, the
  Kalandars, the Siifis[text-layer?] and a host of others were the real power in the land. No ruler
  could afford to neglect them and all were afraid of them. The reason lay in the peculiar psychology
  of the Sindhi Muslim peasantry." (p. 166, folio 157) **Five categories are distinguished, including
  "Kalandars" and "Siifis"[text-layer?] as separate classes from pirs and pirzadas.**
- **The religious hierarchy is classed as a privileged order alongside the landholders.** "The
  privileged classes under Moghul and Kalhoro were (omitting the Government officials) the jaghirdars,
  the zamindars, the Sayids, fakirs, pirs and holy mendicants, and the · most powerful Baliich[text-layer?]
  tribes which could not be antagonized." (p. 163, folio 154) Sorley's transition to the subject is
  explicit: "There remained the much more formidable privileged class of the religious ' hierarchy '."
  (p. 166, folio 157)
- **A quantitative estimate of the cost of the religious establishment — the single most citable
  economic figure in the chunk.** "MacMurdo estimated the expense of the ecclesiastical establishments
  at one-third of the gross revenue enjoyed by the state." (p. 167, folio 158) **No date, no source
  page and no method is given for MacMurdo's estimate: (not stated).**
- **A count of shrines, as reported.** Dr Burnes: "' There is no country in Asia or rather on earth that
  is so perfectly priestridden. It is sai4[text-layer?] to possess no fewer than one hundred thousand
  tombs of saints '" (p. 167, folio 158). **The figure is spelled out in words, not numerals, and so is
  one of the few large numbers in this chunk not destroyed by the text layer**; it is nonetheless
  reported by Burnes at second hand ("It is said to possess").
- **Sayid privilege, described as social immunity.** Dr Burnes: "' The meanest wretch who can boast his
  origin from the holy stock enjoys a place in society higher than temporary rank can bestow. Among the
  crowds who came to me for medicine all readily gave place to the Sayids. No person under any provoca­
  tion would dare to abuse or strike one unless at the risk of being torn to pieces by the populace,
  and, in consequence of the privil�ges[text-layer?] and immunities they enjoy, they flock from all the
  neighbouring centres into Sind where they are a constant tax on the poorest of the inhabitants. '"
  (p. 167, folio 158) **Immigration of Sayids into Sind is presented as a response to fiscal privilege.**
- **Mendicancy as organised tribute-taking, not begging.** Sir Alexander Burnes: "' The mendicants in
  Sind are more numerous than in any other country in Asia. They can scarcely be called beggars, for
  they levy tribute in crowds and by threats of great arrogance. One of the most common is to sit all
  day on the house top and repeat the sacred name " Allah " as many thousand times as the tongue can
  utter it.'" (p. 168, folio 159)
- **The demand, with its tariff.** Captain Del Hoste: they "went about wearing sword and shield, and
  riding horses, declaring that they would not move till given sums of money varying from two or three
  to one hundred and fifty rupees. They enforced attention by blowing horns, ringing bells, beating
  drums and shout­ing at th� pitch[text-layer?] of their voices." (p. 168, folio 159) The set piece:
  a Sayid before the British Ambassador's tent — "' O Feringhee Elchi, give me a hundred rupees or I
  will stay here all day ; and, if you do not, I will stay here all day ; and; if you do not, I will
  tell Mir Murad Ali Khan not to comply with anything you purpose, whereas if you make me your friend,
  you shall get whatever you want.'" (p. 168, folio 159; footnote: "Quoted by Burnes : op. cit., p. 76
  from Del Hoste's MS. Journal.") **The clause "I will stay here all day" is printed twice; see Doubts.**
- **Where the demand was pressed hardest.** "The streets of the towns were infested by such persons
  whose importunity and insolence made heavy demands upon the superstitious poor under thr.eat of all
  kinds of penalties." (p. 168, folio 159) — followed immediately by the Sehwan shrine sentence above.
- **Popular devotional practice as observed, in the same pages and in a different key.** Sir Alexander
  Burnes: "' Nothing more arrests the notice of a stranger on entering Sind than the severe attention
  of the people to the forms of religion as enjoined by the prophet of Arabia. I have observed a
  ·boatman quit the laborious duty pf[text-layer?] dragging the vessel against the stream and retire

### Tomb veneration, offerings and the distribution of shrine income

- **The fullest description of shrine practice in this chunk, and the only itemisation of offerings**,
  quoted by Sorley from Lieutenant James on the tomb of Shahul Muhammad Kalhoro: "' The doorway of this
  court and of the mausoleum is hung with native offerings of those who consider that their prayers for
  any particular blessing have been heard through the mediation of the saint. These consist chiefly of
  iron bells and strings of shells. The tomb itself is covered with rich' silk and brocade, the
  offerings of the wealthier visitors. . . . On descending from the edifice a party of miserable
  mendicants, whose duty it is to keep the courts in a state of cleanli­ness, clamorously demand a fee,
  a portion of which is retained by them and the remainder distributed to the poor surviving
  descendants of the family. '" (p. 168, folio 159) **Four distinct institutional facts sit in this
  passage: votive offerings for answered prayer, graded by the giver's wealth; a *chadar* of silk and
  brocade over the tomb; a body of sweepers with a customary fee; and a fixed split of that fee between
  the sweepers and the saint's descendants.** The chunk gives no ʿurs, no mela, no langar, no numbers
  and no dates for this shrine: **(not stated)**.
- **The Sehwan shrine as a site of cross-communal tribute** — "where Hindus as well as Muslims paid
  tribute to the demands" (p. 168, folio 159). See the archive section.
- **Ornamenting tombs as a diagnostic of the Kalhora period.** Crowe, "writing shortly after the
  Kalh6ra were displaced": "' There is no zeal but for the propaga­tion of the faith, no spirit but in
  celebrating the Id, no liberty but in feeding Sayids and no taste but in ornamenting old tombs.'"
  (p. 167, folio 158) **The only ʿid or calendrical observance named anywhere in the chunk is "the Id",
  and it is named only in this quotation.**
- **Darwish dancing before a shrine, as a transmitted custom.** The Tatta passage from the
  Tarikh-i-Tahiri, quoted above (pp. 168–169, folios 159–160) — "transmitted from generation to
  generation", resisted by "wise teachers and just governors", never stopped.
- **The proverb attaching tombs to a city.** "a Persian proverb about Multan that applied -equally to
  Sind that it consisted of heat, dust, beggars and tombs" (p. 166, folio 157).

### Endowment, wakf and religious land

- **Wakf named as a category of tenure the British formally recognised at annexation.** "On the
  annexation of Sind the British recognized three kinds of these alienations, first, jaghirs which had
  been held from antiquity, and were aUowed[text-layer?] to continue hereditarily, second, puttadari
  holdings of old standing, and third, **grants of a religious or charitable character (wakf)**."
  (p. 164, folio 155)
- **Who held them.** "The religious and charitable grants were at the disposal of mosques, Sayids,
  pirs, fakirs and holy men of all descriptions." (p. 164, folio 155)
- **How old the practice is said to be.** Quoting Elliot: "' Much · of the conquered land was, during
  the whole course of the Arab occupation; liberally bestowed upon sacred edifices and institutions as
  wakf or mortmain, of which some remnant dating from the earlier period is found even to this day. '"
  (p. 167, folio 158) And Sorley's own periodisation: "The great granting of privilege to
  reiigious[text-layer?] persons began to be pronounced under the Kalhora, as distinct from the
  ordinary Muslim system of wakf which had existed from the original Arab conquest." (p. 170,
  folio 161)
- **Non-land endowment: a fishing right granted to a mosque.** "At Moghulbhin the fishing rights had to
  be granted to a mosque and there were many other instances of valuable alienations in favour of the
  priestly caste." (p. 169, folio 160) **Note the phrasing "had to be granted"** — Sorley presents the
  endowment as extracted, not donated.
- **A shrine or religious establishment holding the customs farm outright.** Pottinger: "' they have
  great establishments at Sehwan, Halakhandi and other places where they have the absolute right of
  collecting customs and transit dues. '" (p. 169, folio 160)
- **The measured scale of alienated land, though not for religious grants separately.** "The area of
  land held in jaghir and inam was estimated in 1847 by the Commissioner in Sind at one-tenth of the
  total area of the Government land." (p. 164, folio 155; footnote 1: "Bombay Government Records.
  Revenue Arrangement Sindh, Vol. 203.") **The figure covers jaghir and inam together; the wakf share
  is not separated: (not stated).**
- **Garden grants, described as a public benefit.** The Commissioner in Sind: "' These gardens were to
  be found through­out the whole province and consisted chiefly of allotments in the neighbourhood of
  towns. Planted ·by their owners or their fathers, they were in the hands of all classes, from
  infiuential[text-layer?] Baluch jaghirdar or Afghan puttadar down to the poorest faquir or gosain.
  Some assumed the form of " topes " or groves. Al1[text-layer?] were, however, more or less public
  benefi.ts[text-layer?] created by private cost and labour. It was owing to them that the wayside
  traveller did not lack shelter, shade, refresh­ment and repose. '" (p. 165) **"the poorest faquir or
  gosain" puts Muslim and Hindu mendicants side by side as garden-holders**; the traveller's shelter
  function is the nearest thing to a langar or serai institution in this chunk.

### Land tenure, jaghir, inam and the zamindari

- **The framing claim.** "The system of land tenure in Sind, so far as is known, has always been
  feudal. In all feudal systems the chief method by which the overlord assured himself of the homage of
  his vassals was by the grant of fiefs. In Sind these grants took a great variety of . forms."
  (p. 163, folio 154)
- **Jaghir defined.** "the j aghirdar held his land either entirely free or on payment of a small rent
  and was entitled to collect for himself the land assessment. Jaghirs were granted principally to
  chiefs who provided armed retainers, to officials who discharged public duties, to menials who
  performed domestic services and to courtiers who had won the favour of the court." (pp. 163–164,
  folios 154–155)
- **Jaghir distinguished from inam, by the sanad.** "A j aghir, however, differed from a plain gift
  (inam) in that it involved obligations which terminated with the death of the j aghirdar and the
  sanad was called in and had to be regranted to a successor at the will of the ruler. An inam was a
  free irreclaimable gift." (p. 164, folio 155) **The escheat-and-regrant mechanism is stated here as
  the defining difference** — relevant to any shrine or pir family holding land on a sanad.
- **Talpur policy on inherited grants.** Mr Ellis: "' the Talpiirs, though liberal in their grants to
  their own immediate retainers and dependants, were not remarkable for generosity in confirming grants
  · of their predecessors. Certain grants in the old Shikarpiir Collectorate (in Sukkur district) were
  found to be traceable to the time when the Afghans had possession of Upper Sind.'" (p. 164, folio 155;
  footnote 2: "ibid., Selections LXVI, New Series, p . 45 .")
- **Puttadari and its origin.** "The new cultivators wishing to secure a light assessment petitioned
  their sovereign . . . and obtained puttas (leases) for the cultivation of certain lands on payment of
  a stipulated sum or on condition of the remission of the Government demand." "The puttadaris, in
  Upper Sind especially, are held mostly by Afghan settlers dating from the time of Nadir Shah, and
  even earlier .from the days of the Arghiins[text-layer?] and .Tarkhans." (p. 164, folio 155)
- **Sorley's own testimony as a serving official, one of very few first-person claims in the book so
  far.** "But I am myself familiar with at least one case of a claim of puttadari by a Hindu descendant
  of an Amil in the service of the . Government." (p. 164, folio 155)
- **The jaghirdar tribes, listed.** "Talpurs, Nizamanis, Lagharis, Marris, Jamalis, Bhugrais,
  Bhagranis, Rinds, Changs, Chalgaris, Lasharis, Nuhanis, Lunds, Kalvis, Jettors, Nundanis, Jelalanis,
  Kh6sas[text-layer?], Kh6khars[text-layer?], Abras, Katians, Sayids, Pirs and Pathans. The vast
  majority of these are Baluchi tribes." (p. 165) **"Sayids" and "Pirs" are listed as *tribes* holding
  jaghirs.**
- **The zamindar's economic role.** "the zamindars occupied the position · of a honourable middle
  class... He paid the revenue, he made regular advances to the cultivators, and he enjoyed a large
  number of feudal privileges in respect of the husbandmen who tilled his land and took their share of
  the produce for their pains." (p. 165)

  pala6[text-layer?] and gh5sht[text-layer?] for special feasts and a considerable latitude in his

- **Sub-letting and undivided liability.** Postans: "' The zamindar ', says Postans, ' was at
  liperty[text-layer?] to let ·to parties under him any portions of the land he proposed to cultivate,

- **Payment in kind, and the village offices.** "He paid his workpeople of every description in grain,

  affairs. He was called Mukhadam or Arbab, a title that has not yet died out in Sind." (p. 165)
- **Village boundaries and field tenure.** "The boundaries of the village ' deh ' were defined, but the
  fields were not separated from each other. The lands were held on the tenure common throughout many

### Revenue heads, local cesses and tax farming

- **The Mughal central heads, named.** "Under the Moghuls the chief heads of central revenue were land
  revenue, customs, mint, inheritance, presents, monopolies and indemnities." (p. 156, folio 147)
  **"inheritance" here is the escheat head.**
- **The local cesses, named as a class — and *rahadari* named.** "The local cesses consisted of a
  variety of duties levied on artisans, retailers, prooucers[text-layer?] and consumers, transit dues
  of every description, called loosely rahadari and j agat. The total incidence of these was
  exceedingly heavy." (p. 156, folio 147)
- **Continuity across the dynastic change.** "coming of the Kalhora, these local burdens and cesses were
  not abolished. They continued to be levied and under the Talpiirs the collection was even tightened
  up. But as all the revenue then went into one exchequer, the conflict disappeared and with it much of
  the unsettlement that kept the Moghul administration in a continual condition of instability."
  (p. 156, folio 147)
- **The pre-Mughal origin of the system.** "The system of providing funds for local needs was not
  originated by the Moghuls. It was the traditional system which they found prevailing in India."
  "The system, as Fariikiz[text-layer?] shows, was derived from the practice of Hindu dynasties and
  embraced almost every transaction that could be taxed for the benefit of the ruler. It went back to
  the Hindu law of taxation dependent upon the primitive village system of India, according to which
  the raj a took his share of all gains of any description whatsoever." (p. 156, folio 147; footnote 2:
  "Fariiki : op. cit., p. 481.")
- **The contemporary chronicle Sorley leans on.** "The chronicle of Shahabuddin Talish translated by
  Sarkar makes the position absolutely clear. That native annalist states, ' From the first occupation
  of India and its ports to the end of Shah Jahan' s reign it was the rule and practice to exact hasil
  (revenue) from every trader-from the rose-vendor down to the clay vendor, from the weaver of fine
  linen to that of · coarse cloth, to collect taxes from new comers and from travellers, mer­chants and
  stable keepers. '" (p. 156, folio 147; footnote 1: "Quoted in Moreland : ibid., p. 284.")
  **The weaver appears here as a taxed household, as in earlier chunks.**
- **The survival of the system into Sorley's present.** "The system is still the basis of local taxation
  in India, where local bodies depend largely for their income upon octroi, terminal and toll taxes,
  market and stall fees and, occasionally, profession taxes, the direct descen­dants of the old
  obnoxious transit dues" (p. 156, folio 147).
- **The British abolition, dated, and what was left standing.** "When the British annexed Sind in 1843
  they took immediate steps to abolish the more pernicious of these local exactions, which under the
  Kalhora and Talpiirs had attained a remarkable degree of com­prehensiveness." (pp. 156–157, folios
  147–148) The Commissioner in Sind reported: "' Town and transit duties which formerly existed have
  been abolished as also many inconsiderable and partial cesses prevalent under the late Government '
  leaving behind as the chief source of revenue land, fisheries, sea and frontier customs, excise on
  spirits and drugs, judicial fees, fines and miscellaneous items of small importance." (p. 157,
  folio 148)
- **Tax farming named as the central abuse.** "One of the great curses of Moghul, Kalh6ro[text-layer?]
  and Talpiir[text-layer?] rule alike was the farming of the right to collect taxes and imposts of
  various kinds. This was tantamount to permitting the tax farmer to exact as much as he possibly could
  from every class, up to the point that the populace would bear. The system was a bad one as it
  admitted a middle­man who had no interest in the incidence of the taxation he collected." (p. 157,
  folio 148)
- **The collecting establishment.** "The vigilance of the collect­ing staff under Moghul,
  Kalh6ro[text-layer?] an4[text-layer?] Talpiir was remarkable. Few managed to escape for long from its
  unceasing and persecuting demands." "The tax farmers, ij aredars and contractors were multitudinous
  and. formed a close bureaucracy. Theywere[text-layer?] mostly Hindus who knew very well just how far
  they could go with impunity." (p. 157, folio 148)

### The position of Hindus, and the working of the whole system

- **Pottinger's three-class division of the population.** "the first was the privileged class consisting
  of Amirs, Chiefs, Baliichis, Sayids and religious mendicants, the second, of the pastoral agricultural
  and other Mussulman tribes found all over Sind in the rural areas and constituting the majority of
  the population, and the third, of the Hindus who were ' tyrannized over in . the most infamous and
  open manner by all classes of Mussul­mans '" (p. 170, folio 161; footnote 2: "For the maltreatment of

- **Sorley's summary of the transfer.** "The plain facts were that the unprivileged classes had to
  support, in the manner already described, by their toil and industry the privileged so that the

- **What changed from Moghul to Kalhora.** "while under the Moghuls the privileged classes consisted of
  the feudatory chiefs and officials, · many of the latter being foreigners, the rest of the population,

  certain taxes and exactions as non-Muslims, according to the practice of Aurangzeb. Under the Kalhora,
  however, it became no longer necessary to support an alien Moghul bureaucracy. The position of
  privilege formerly occupied by the Moghu1[text-layer?] aristocracy was takeri[text-layer?] by the

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The chunk's controlling thesis: shrine reverence is a political product of Kalhora weakness.** "The
  Kalhorn[text-layer?], weak followers of Moghul models, with less strength behind them and controlled
  by the fanaticism of an ignorant populace, were even more dependent on the existence of privileged
  classes. It was the Kalhora's practice of deferring to the priestly class that led to the
  extraordinary reverence for pirs, Sayids and holy men, which may be said to have been the predominant
  characteristic of eighteenth century Sind." (p. 163, folio 154) **Sorley makes state weakness the
  cause and popular veneration the effect** — the reverse of a devotional account, and the book's most
  quotable claim for an economics-of-religion argument.
- **He then makes the same class self-reinforcing.** "No ruler could afford to neglect them and all were
  afraid of them. The reason lay in the peculiar psychology of the Sindhi Muslim peasantry." (p. 166,
  folio 157) **The causal chain is: peasant psychology → pir power → ruler deference → more pir power.**
- **His verdict on the whole arrangement, stated as fact and as distaste.** "The extent to which holy men
  or reputed holy men were reverenced and pampered in Sind is almost outside the bounds of credibility.
  This was the least satisfactory feature of the Sind social structure." "The facts admit of no dispute
  and are extremely unedifying." (p. 166, folio 157) **The phrase "or reputed holy men" is Sorley's own
  hedge and carries his distinction between genuine and spurious sanctity — the distinction the Shah
  Abdul Latif sentence later turns on.**
- **The defence of the poet against the religion around him.** "The real form and meaning of Islam in
  Sind is not to be found in these ebullitions of popular and incredible fanaticism, but is to be read
  in the beautiful spiritual poems of Shah Abdul Latif. It will be most unfair to judge Sind by the
  former and forget the latter." (p. 169, folio 160) **This is an evaluative separation of the poet from
  the shrine culture the book has just spent four pages condemning, and it is the argumentative purpose
  the chapter serves in a book about him.**
- **The closing structural verdict on Sind.** "the social condition of the country was due to the
  gradual disintegration of the Moghul · system influenced by the psychological attitude of
  com­plaisance shown by the Muslim populace in respect · of autocratic government and its reverence for
  holy men. In such circumstances the trading and commercial elements were crushed as .between · the
  upper and nether millstones. **A quasi-theocratic state, dependent on an unsound division of labour of
  this kind, could have taken rto[text-layer?] other form.**" (p. 171, folio 162) **"A quasi-theocratic
  state" is Sorley's own term for eighteenth-century Sind.**
- **Tax farming judged the decisive institutional flaw.** "The system was a bad one as it admitted a
  middle­man who had no interest in the incidence of the taxation he collected. It opened the door to
  all kinds of corruption and oppression, to which contemporary records refer copiously." (p. 157,
  folio 148)
- **A counterfactual defence of taxation that Sorley declines to make.** "Had the proceeds of this
  taxation been employed generously on public works and on education, there would have been something
  to say in favour of it." (p. 159, folio 150) **Compare the closely parallel passage at p. 94 in chunk
  006 on "that kind of conscience … reserved for governments of a later day".**
- **A deliberate comparative move: Sind's medievalism is Europe's too.** Sorley quotes a description of
  collective responsibility, then reveals it is "not a description of Sind … but of medievalEngland",
  adding that it "might very well apply word for word to the Sind of Moghul and Kalh6ro days" (p. 160,
  folio 151). **The same rhetorical device as the highwaymen comparison at p. 90 in chunk 006.**
- **Continuity of the fiscal system into British India, argued.** The old transit dues are "the direct
  descen­dants" of present-day octroi, terminal and toll taxes (p. 156, folio 147); the system was
  Hindu in origin, adopted rather than invented by the Mughals (p. 156, folio 147). **Sorley's argument
  cuts against a communal reading of Muslim fiscal oppression, while his religious-hierarchy chapter
  cuts the other way.**
- **A rare first-person evidentiary claim.** "But I am myself familiar with at least one case of a claim
  of puttadari by a Hindu descendant of an Amil in the service of the . Government." (p. 164,
  folio 155)
- **An explicit refusal to go on.** "Such instances could be multiplied a hundred fold but it is needless
  to elaborate the matter further." (p. 168, folio 159) **The shrine material is thus presented as a
  sample, not an inventory** — worth noting for anyone tempted to treat the chunk's two shrines as the
  book's full list.
- **A point of self-correction about learned Muslim opinion.** "Many of these fanatical exhibitions by an
  ignorant and unlettered populace met with the disapproval of learned and cultured Muslims, who,
  however, were powerless to interfere and knew better than to try to do so." (p. 168, folio 159), with
  the Tarikh-i-Tahiri cited as a Muslim source hostile to the Tatta darwish dance (pp. 168–169, folios
  159–160). **Sorley is careful here to source the critique internally rather than only to Europeans.**
