# sorley_shah_abdul_latif_of_bhit — chunk 020 — pp. 317–347

**This chunk is NOT continuous prose.** It is the translation section of the book: verse
translations of Shah Abdul Latif's poems arranged into numbered thematic sections, plus three pages
of apparatus — the tail of a concordance table keyed to a named printed edition (p. 317), a page of
five English and Persian epigraphs (p. 318), and two contents pages, one for each Part of the
translation (pp. 320 and 342). Because the concordance and the two contents pages are a source-and-
arrangement record, they are recorded in full under `## Bibliography and apparatus`, placed after
`## Arguments and interpretations` as the convention requires. **There is not one sentence of
Sorley's own prose anywhere in these thirty-one pages** — no commentary, no footnote, no citation in
the body. Every word below except the apparatus is verse.

## Arguments and interpretations

**Sorley advances no argument in words anywhere in this chunk. What he does here he does by
arrangement, and three editorial decisions are visible and should be recorded as his claims about
the poetry.**

- **He abandons the sur as the unit of presentation and substitutes English thematic titles.** The
  translation is divided into two Parts with their own titles — "MERCY AND GRACE" (p. 320,
  folio 311) and "THE DAILY ROUND" (p. 342, folio 333) — and then into twenty-one and six numbered
  sections respectively, every one of them headed by an English phrase of his own ("THE HARD WAY",
  "THE TRAFFICKING", "BLESSED ARE THE MEEK", "THE SONG OF THE RAIN", "THE HOLY MEN"). **No Sindhi
  sur title appears as a heading. The reader of the translation alone would not learn that the
  Risalo has surs at all**, except from the one word "Surs" in the concordance table head (p. 317,
  folio 308).
- **He keeps a concordance so the rearrangement can be reversed.** The table at p. 317 (folio 308)
  maps his own numbered sections back onto "Pokardas' s Edition", section by section and couplet by
  couplet. **This is an explicit editorial claim that the rearrangement is a presentation choice and
  not a claim about the text's own order, and it is the only place in the chunk where Sorley's hand
  is visible as a scholar's.**
- **He frames Sindhi mystical verse with English metaphysical and devotional poetry.** The epigraph
  page (p. 318) sets Quarles, Herbert, Crashaw and Watts — four English poets, three of them
  seventeenth-century devotional writers — alongside one Persian quatrain from Abu Sayid Ibn Abu'l
  Khair, and offers no comment on the pairing. **Chunk 016 recorded Sorley ranking Latif far below
  the Persian masters (pp. 252–253, folios 243–244) while calling him the greatest imaginative
  artist Sind has produced (chunk 012, p. 178, folio 169). This epigraph page is a third placement
  again — beside English devotional verse — and the chunk argues for it nowhere.**
- **The two Part titles are themselves an interpretation.** "MERCY AND GRACE" gathers the poems of
  God, love, martyrdom, the sea and the guide; "THE DAILY ROUND" gathers the rain, the martyrs, the
  king and the minstrel, the wanderers, the holy men and the spinner. **The division is between the
  transcendent and the worked day, and Sorley's epigraph for the second Part — "' The
  d_aily_[text-layer?] ro�nd,[text-layer?] the common task / Will.furnish[text-layer?] all we ought
  to ask'" (p. 342, folio 333) — states the principle.** **The chunk names no author for that
  epigraph** — **(not stated)**.
