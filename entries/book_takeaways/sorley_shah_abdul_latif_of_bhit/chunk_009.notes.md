# sorley_shah_abdul_latif_of_bhit — chunk 009 — pp. 126–140

Continuous prose, no verse, no poetry, no hagiography. The chunk sits deep inside Sorley's economic
chapter on Sind and carries three of its lettered sub-sections as printed: it opens mid-paragraph in
what the preceding chunk began, then `( b) Population and food` (p. 126), `(c) Wages, prices, social
condition` (p. 130) and `(d) Irrigation` (p. 135), and it breaks off in the middle of the account of
canal-clearance obligations (p. 140). **This is the densest run of institutional and economic
material seen in the book so far** — population estimates, farmed customs, wage and price series,
coinage, moneylending, forced labour, *jaghirs*, and a full description of the Sindhi irrigation
regime and who was liable to pay for it.

## Practices, institutions, economy

This is where almost the whole chunk lives.

### Population, and the argument about it

- **Sorley's method is to set four incompatible estimates against each other and refuse to choose.**
  Del Hoste "calculated in 1832 that the population was 850,000, of which about one-fourth were
  Hindus" (p. 126, folio 117). Burnes "in 1828 estimated the population at about one million"
  (p. 126, folio 117). The Commissioner in Sind in 1847 "put the total population of Sind at
  l,274,732[text-layer?]" (p. 127, folio 118). Pottinger's Tatta figures are quoted at length and
  then discarded: "But Pottinger is rightly distrustful · of the accuracy of these figures for the
  KalhOro days" (p. 126, folio 117). Sorley's own verdict: "From this conflicting evidence it is very
  difficult to reach any very satisfactory conclusion" (p. 127, folio 118).
- **Burnes's town-by-town breakdown, as printed** — "25,000 were in Shikarpiir, 20,000 in Hyderabad,
  18,ooo[text-layer?] in Tatta, 15 ,000 in Khairpiir and Karachi, and ro,ooo[text-layer?] in Sehwan
  and Mirpiir" (p. 126, folio 117). Note the numerals `18,ooo` and `ro,ooo` are damaged in the text
  layer and the Khairpur/Karachi figure is given as a single number for two places.
- **Density, and the Commissioner's own doubt about his own figure.** "' taking the area at
  48 ,000[text-layer?] square miles, would give nearly 27 to the square mile. With reference,
  however, to the prevalent opinion as to the deficiency of population and the large portion of the
  surface occupied by desert and morass I should be inclined to think even this moderate propor­tion
  a high estimate. '" (p. 127, folio 118)
- **The conclusion Sorley does draw, and the reason.** "It would thus appear to be a safe conclusion
  that during Moghul and Kalhorn[text-layer?] days . the population cannot have exceeded one million
  altogether, or less than a quarter of what it is at the present day. The difference today is
  accounted for by the vast extension of irrigation since the date of the British annexation."
  (p. 127, folio 118)
- **The one demographic fact he treats as certain.** "But one fact is certain, namely that between
  1740 and 1 800[text-layer?] there was a great shifting of the population of Sind, the chief
  concentration altering from Tatta to Shikarpiir, Hyderabad and Karachi." (p. 127, folio 118)
- **A plague of weavers, dated 1699 and put at 80,000 dead.** "Hamilton in 1699 .had noted that
  80,000 weavers had died of plague, caused by the · failure of rains shortly before his arrival in
  the city" (pp. 126–127, folios 117–118). The city is Tatta. **This is the single largest mortality
  figure in the chunk and the only named epidemic.**
- **The Talpur-depopulation thesis, raised and rejected.** "Most writers have assumed that the
  tyranny of the Talpiirs had by the time of the British annexation reduced the population below what
  it had been a century earlier. There is, how­ever, no reliable evidence to prove any such
  conclusion." (p. 126, folio 117)
- **Why the population is thought to have been static.** "It seems likely that all through
  l\foghul[text-layer?] and Kalhorn[text-layer?] days the population remained , fairly constant for
  the simple reason that irrigation was statiC[text-layer?]. It continued to be dependent on the
  bounty of the river, helped by the rule of thumb methods of water distri­bution, under a system that
  was not financed, as modem[text-layer?] irrigation works are, by the full resources of a settled and
  highly organized system of administration with other ideals of service than collection of revenue
  and waging aggressive wars." (p. 127, folio 118)

### Land tenure, *jaghirs* and feudal obligation

- **The Moghul settlement described as devolved and conditional.** "under the Moghul system the local

  kept faith with and observed their feudal obligations" (p. 135, folio 126).
- ***Jaghirs* named as an obstacle to state control of water.** "Canals could often be seen running
  parallel with each other for long distances and the frequent jaghirs made it very difficult for the
  Government to do much in the way of unifying' control." (p. 140, folio 131) **This is the chunk's
  only use of the word and the only reference to granted land**; the chunk gives no example, no
  grantee and no religious grant: **(not stated)**.
- **The village as a protected settlement under a feudal chief**, carried over from the previous
  chunk's argument: the built core "was usually inhabited by the petty Hindu traders while the
  Musulman cultivators dwelt in their brushwood landhis round about it under the protection of the
  feudal chief. or headman" (p. 126, folio 117).
- **Village self-defence.** "the village had to take its own measures to resist the robbers who

## Arguments and interpretations

Sorley's own voice is again strongly present, and as in chunk 006 his position as a serving Indian
Civil Service officer writing in 1940 shapes most of it. Recorded as his judgements, not as fact.

- **He refuses the depopulation thesis on evidentiary grounds, twice.** Against "Most writers" on
  Talpur tyranny: "There is, how­ever, no reliable evidence to prove any such conclusion" (p. 126,
  folio 117); and against Pottinger's Tatta numbers, which he calls figures Pottinger himself was
  "rightly distrustful · of" (p. 126, folio 117).
- **He nevertheless converts irrigation into the master variable of Sindhi history.** Population was
  static because "irrigation was statiC[text-layer?]" (p. 127, folio 118); the fourfold modern
  population is "accounted for by the vast extension of irrigation since the date of the British
  annexation" (p. 127, folio 118); and "Sind has always been a pioneer of irrigation in India and has
  had a system of canals and watercourses since the beginning of historical time, tended by people who
  knew very well how to use the bounty of nature" (p. 138, folio 129). **The two halves of that
  argument are in tension — an ancient, skilful indigenous system that nevertheless held the
  population flat — and the chunk does not reconcile them.**
- **He claims continuity of the peasant's material condition across three centuries.** "The
  labourers, artisans and peasants were therefore · relatively little worse off in Moghul and Kalhoro
  times than they are today. In fact their general condition was much the same : they lived just as
  little above the subsistence level." (p. 131, folio 122)
- **But he also holds that the modern state is qualitatively different in conscience.** "The margin
  between earnings and subsistertce[text-layer?] was probably more precarious than it is today when
  famines and other calamities of major importance · are provided against generously by subventions
  from state funds." (p. 132, folio 123) And: "The . state . h�d[text-layer?] not then arrived at the
  conception that part of its duty is to give. ever}rone[text-layer?] a fair chance as far as
  circumstances permit." (p. 132, folio 123)
- **He rules anachronistic moral judgement out of court, and names his targets.** "It is fallacious
  therefore to apply to the defects of the system the ethical standards of a much later day, a fault
  much indulged in by Indian historians and economists." (p. 133, folio 124) And: "While admitting
  this melancholy fact we shall waste our labour in deprecating it, because nothing could then have
  altered a system which was thoroughly ingrained in Indian life, habits and social observance. In
  Europe at the same time, as Fariiki[text-layer?] has pointed out very clearly, the condition of the
  poor was deplorable. The Moghuls and the Kalh6ra[text-layer?] were alike creatures of their age, a
  harder and less sentimental age than the present." (p. 135, folio 126) **This is the same
  comparative-defence move he made for Mughal policing in chunk 006.**
- **He corrects a witness he otherwise uses.** Of Carless's claim that the peasantry "' never marry
  '": "But he exaggerated considerably when he continued … The whole economic history of India refutes
  this rash generalization. In such circumstances the family goes to work, the wife and the children
  included, and somehow or other it manages to exist and multiply." (p. 132, folio 123)
- **His theory of wages, stated explicitly.** "the reward of labour, agricultural, industrial or
  casual, has little real relation to the . yield of the harvest or productivity alone, but depends
  upon the suitable balancing of supply and demand and upon the extent to which the labourer is
  permitted by the govern­ment or by social standards and practice to enjoy the profits of his toil."
  (pp. 134–135, folios 125–126)
- **He treats religious and kin-based relief as structurally weak** because both "depended largely on
  private conscience, which works capriciously" (p. 133, folio 124). **For an archive concerned with
  shrine economies this is the chunk's most consequential interpretive claim, and it is made without
  any reference to shrines, endowments or langar.**
- **He reads the village layout as a European analogy.** "The whole system is intensely reminiscent
  of the early Middle Ages in Britain" (p. 126, folio 117).
- **He treats the Sindhi lexicon as historical evidence** for the depth of the pastoral–agricultural
  culture (p. 129, folio 120), and modern travellers' eyes as corroboration of nineteenth-century
  ones: "Present-day travellers in Sind who have seen the jowari standing high enough to hide a
  mail[text-layer?] on horseback will recognize the accuracy qf[text-layer?] this picture" (p. 129,
  folio 120).
- **He is candid that he cannot read his own source base.** Of the Company's rupee transactions: "it
  is not easy to understand them on the evidence available now" (p. 134, folio 125).
- **A methodological transfer he makes openly, and which a user of these figures should notice.**
  Because Sind's own wage evidence is thin, he imports the *Ain*'s statistics and figures from
  Masulipatam, Surat, Agra and Akbar's court, on the stated ground that there is "no reason to believe
  that the statistics of the Ain … are untrue as far as Sind is concern_ed" (p. 130, folio 121).
  **Almost every wage figure quoted above is therefore not a Sindhi observation.**
