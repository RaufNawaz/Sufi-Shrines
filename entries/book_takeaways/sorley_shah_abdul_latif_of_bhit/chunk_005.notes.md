## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Saltpetre as the Company's whole reason for being in Sind, stated flatly.** "The Company's
  chief interest in Sind was to obtain saltpetre as sole buyers and sole exporters : all native
  merchants were supposed to deal through the Company and all transport of the commodity was to be
  carried on in Company's ships" (p. 67).
- **How saltpetre was got, as the Resident describes it — a note on settlement archaeology as much
  as on trade.** It is "extracted from the earth on which towns or villages formerly stood or where
  any number of men and cattle have been encamped for any considerable time (and after plentiful
  rain, a larger quantity is always procurable than after a dry season)" (p. 67).
- **The scale and plant of refining, itemised.** Prospective output "from 5 to ro,ooo[text-layer?]
  pucca maunds crude, which would produce according to its goodness 2 to 4 or 5,000 pucca maunds
  refined salt petre"; to work it would need "at least 20 or 25 large iron furnaces or coppers…
  and from 25 to 40 part refiners part labourers, 4 or 5 writers and 8 or ro[text-layer?] sepoys in
  constant pay at least for 8 months in the year", plus "the expence of firewood and customs, etc.,
  charges bringing down to Shah Bunder" (p. 67).
- **The men who actually made it, and the Company's opinion of them.** "The only people who have
  hitherto traded in this articl�[text-layer?] and gone up counti:y[text-layer?] to make it are
  Moormin[text-layer?] much like the Borahs here and about Surat ; but to contract wit;h[text-layer?]
  or advance the Hon. Company's money to these people for this article seems to me
  improdent[text-layer?] few or none of them being people of credit or substance nor will any other
  substantial merchant be security for them." They brought down "l, 500[text-layer?], some
  l,ooo[text-layer?], 800 or 500 pucca maunds of crude salt petre according to their circumstances,
  which is generally much adulterated" (p. 67).
- **The monopoly was routinely broken, and the state let it be broken.** "The Sind Government was,
  however, never very averse from allowing saltpetre ' interlopers ' to do business if it profited
  the Government to allow this" (p. 67). In the Resident's own words: "Notwithstanding your
  perwanna that the Honble English Company should alone purchase and export saltpetre from your
  country, the Porebunder and other merchants at Shahbunder publickly purchase and export it
  without the least impediment from your officers and even more so since the granting of that
  perwanna than before" (p. 67). A *perwanna* is thus a written grant the ruler issues and does not
  enforce; the plural is printed later as "our perwans[text-layer?] entitle us to" (p. 73, folio 64).
- **Saltpetre volumes actually moved, 1763–64.** New saltpetre houses and warehouses built in 1763
  (p. 73, folio 64); "six copper boilers and sundry stores and stationery amounting altogether to
  Rs. 38,526-2-88" sent from Bombay 16 December 1764 (p. 73, folio 64); the Admiral Pocock returned
  "with 1254 bags of saltpetre and 9 unserviceable copper boilers amounting as per invoice and bill
  of lading enclosed to Rs . 1 5,884-0-52" (p. 73, folio 64); a contract to furnish "3000 bags or
  6000 pucca maunds of refined salt petre" by the end of March (p. 75, folio 66).
- **Customs, presents and the normal cost of doing business.** "The need felt by the Company's
  officers to keep on good terms with the Sind officials led to the giving of presents and
  inducements to the farmers of the customs, the customs officers and the clerks employed" (p. 68,
  folio 59). The sums are given: "It[text-layer?] piece of scarlet perpets, l piece of
  popinj ae[text-layer?] perpets etc. , amounting in all to about 60 rupees, which is I
  :find[text-layer?] a custom long since introduced by Mr. Sumption of making a great number of
  small presents on the import of a considerable quantity of goods t_o[text-layer?] all the officers
  of the customs both at Tatta and Aurangabander for speedily dispatching his business" (p. 68,
  folio 59). The customs were **farmed**, not directly collected — "the farmers of the customs"
  (p. 68, folio 59).
- **Port dues at the bar, a fixed charge in the hands of a local chief.** "When ships arrive at this
  bar they always pay anchorage 25 rupees to the Jham though this is the same for one as for many"
  (p. 68, folio 59). A separate present "to his Duans and Officers of perpets amounting to about 35
  rupees" was made on the late Resident's instruction (p. 68, folio 59).
- **Obstruction by the customs officer as a technique of government.** Gulabroy "having of late
  frequently impeded our business there by delaying to give the usual dispatch to boats with
  putchuck, crude salt petre etc., from the Gaat and put off settling the Customs Accounts since his
  administration under pretence of undue customs and demands on the crude salt petre and the
  workmen employed in extracting the same seemingly with a view to create som�[text-layer?]
  troublesome disputes hereafter", and detaining "a boat with bricks and chunam coming from thence
  for the use of the house at Aurangabander" (p. 73, folio 64).
- **Three currencies in circulation at once.** Sorley states it as a finding — "three kinds of
  rupees were in circulation in Sind" (p. 70, folio 61) — and the record shows the exchange being
  worked: a freight offered at "67 curr. rs. which at the present wattan is about
  52j-[text-layer?] N. Rs." per maund, with a footnote glossing "N. Rs." as "i.e. Nadir Shah rupees"
  (p. 70, folio 61). A third, "34 Ticca rupees a piece", prices the scarlet perpets (p. 71,
  folio 62). Cattle are accounted in "Nadir Shah rupees 88 7[text-layer?] rupees 1 quarter 75 reas"
  (p. 69, folio 60), and the Prince's payment in "26,344 r$.[text-layer?] 3 qrs. ·50 reas" (p. 76,
  folio 67) — so the *rea* is the subdivision in use.
- **The commodities of the trade, named.** Saltpetre, crude and refined (throughout); **woollens**
  — "Crimson draps 1200 yards IO[text-layer?] r. 2 qrs., thick worcesters 1500 yards at 4 rupees,
  Persia fine wine 450 yards at 4 r. 3 qrs. Black superfine 780 yards at 6 rupees in all amounting
  3930 yards or Rs. 25,41 7-2" (p. 74); **perpets**, scarlet and "popinj ae[text-layer?]" (pp. 68,
  71); **patchuck (putchuck)**, "an article that was required for the China market", freighted from
  Carrachee to Bombay and bartered against woollens (p. 70, folio 61); **cattle** — "132 head of
  cattle now shipped on board the Success and Dispatch" (p. 69, folio 60), which Sorley calls "a
  minor item of the Company's trade"; and the Prince's own present-goods, "broad cloth velvet,
  silk, looking glasses etc." (p. 74).
- **The market for woollens was not Sind.** "Sind had no use for the woollen goods, and the Afghans
  had" (p. 76, folio 67). The buyers were "The Patan Merchants", judged "people of small substance
  or credit", held back by "their jealousy of the Prince and uncertainty when their own King's
  authority may be firmly settled here" (p. 68, folio 59). Ghulam Shah later undertook to encourage
  "the Pattans and other northern dealers to come_ fre.ely[text-layer?] to us without interruption"
  (p. 76, folio 67). By 28 January 1763 the Afghan woollen trade was itself being undercut "because
  of imports of woollens from Russia" (p. 72, folio 63).
- **Tribute as the hinge of the whole quarrel.** Sorley states that Company trade "did not readily
  _accord[text-layer?] with the definite policy of the KalhOra to make the payment of tribute .to
  Kanda,.har �r_Kabul[text-layer?] as difficult as possible", and that "the success of direct trade
  with Afghanistan made it more difficult for the Kalhora to pay their tribute" (pp. 76–77).
- **Building, materials and the shortage of craftsmen — a recurring theme with figures.** At Tatta,
  "the paving the floor with bricks which can be done here is very necessary as also plank and
  oyster shells sufficient for IO[text-layer?] or 12 windows with a couple of carpenters to make
  them which I hope your Honour etc. will please to spare from Bombay, the materials not being
  procurable here and the workmen very ignorant" (pp. 67–68). Bombay carpenters sent April 1760 were
  returned 30 January 1762 at "4 rupees per month", to be replaced by "two country ones on the most
  reasonable terms possible" (p. 72, folio 63). A bungalow on the upper terrace of the Tatta house,
  "(to the amount of about 130 rupees)", was raised 9 May 1764 for ventilation, "having severely
  experienced for some years past the dangerous situation we are in at Tattah during the hot
  months for want of air" (p. 75, folio 66), and described again as built because the house was
  "secluded from by lofty houses in i�s[text-layer?] neighbourhood" (p. 76, folio 67).
- **Construction of the Shahbunder house described in detail.** "chiefly composed of mud together
  with the bad workmanship it was but just habitable and as the roof was only rafters and matts
  covered with mud the warehouses were very improper to lodge valuable goods in, especially woolens
  which would infallible receive damp" (p. 70, folio 61). A second, contradicting report the same
  year: "The house at Shahbunder is in pretty good condition except two of the rooms which must be
  new roofed, the beams being rotten… a little expence will repair the damage done to the saltpetre
  houses" (p. 70, folio 61).
- **No local capacity for metal plant.** "It was impossible to get Sindhi craftsmen capable of
  making the kind of article needed" (p. 75, folio 66); the Resident was reduced "to great
  streights" with "only a single serviceable boiler left" and broke up the damaged coppers to
  reform them locally (p. 75, folio 66).
- **Piracy as an ordinary hazard of the coast, and its handling.** "The existence of pirates on the
  Sind coast caused continual anxiety to shippers" (p. 71, folio 62). A prize dingey "between forty
  and fifty candies burthen" was taken with 30 prisoners on 28 December 1762 after an action in
  which she "returned his fire with a small swivel gun 3 or 4 times and sepoys continued flourishing
  their swords and firing their matchlocks" (p. 71, folio 62). The prisoners "freely confessed
  themselves robbers but begged for mercy that they might either be sent to Bombay or Porebunder
  and not be delivered up to the Seroys", footnoted "i.e. Sirais" (p. 71, folio 62). The Resident
  refused to hand them over, reasoning that executing them "might exasperate them so as to revenge
  their deaths by murdereing the crews of any dingeys or other vessels not only belonging to the
  English but also to the Prince's subjects" (p. 72, folio 63). A further pirate sighting is noted
  24 February 1764, "who, however, departed without doing any damage" (p. 74).
- **Wreck right waived by the ruler.** On the Swallow Gallivat's stranding, "The
  Kalhorn[text-layer?] seems to have acted up to his agreement with the Company over the wreck, he
  having consented to waive the rulers' right to wreckage", and "Both the Prince's officers and the
  Ranah's people… very readily assisted us as much as they could" (p. 73, folio 64). Masts, yards,
  sails, rigging, guns and arms were saved; ammunition, provisions and most shot lost with the hull
  (p. 73, folio 64).
- **The durbar at Shahpiir[text-layer?], 28–29 March 1764 — a full protocol of investiture and
  exchange.** The Prince "wished me to withdraw and receive his sirpah etc. consisting of kimcob
  vest, shawl and turband, a knife and sword mounted with gold, whose scabbard and
  sheat[text-layer?] were ornamented with the same metal a ziga or cluster of emeralds and rubys and
  a Persian horse with silver plated bridle and furniture"; in return the Resident "presented the
  Prince with his customary gold rupee and other presents in the Honble Company's name" (p. 74).
  Cloth "musters" (samples) were sent for inspection the next morning and the woollens contract
  followed (p. 74). A footnote records "considerable delay in obtaining the money as the Prince
  said he had to collect it from revenues" (p. 74) — revenue collection as the limit on the
  ruler's liquidity.
- **Holi halts the commercial calendar at Tatta.** "13th March . 1764. This being the Hooly Festival
  of the Gentoos occasions a delay of five or six days, no business of any kind being attended to
  during this time of riot, madness and confusion" (p. 75, folio 66).
- **Id observed inside the factory as a payment to servants.** "The practice of the Company to give
  presents to its servants on Id (probably Ramzan Id) is mentioned in a letter dated 19 February
  1764 : 'I gave the sepoys, hamrnals[text-layer?] etc. , factory servants their usual enams'"
  (p. 74). *Enam* is thus used here for a customary festival gratuity to servants, not a land grant.
- **Health and mortality as a structural cost.** The Shahbunder house "would certainly prove a
  graveyard to such as might be destined to reside there" (p. 70, folio 61); "if they remained there
  till the end of the season, few of them would survive" (p. 70, folio 61); Erskine "for near a
  month past very much indisposed" takes ship 30 January 1764 (p. 73, folio 64); Mr Moet "afflicted
  with fevers… in this unwholesome climate" (p. 74); two country doctors retained, one at
  Shahbunder "for the sepoy and lascar serVice[text-layer?]" and a brahmin at Tatta (p. 76,

- **Transport and the cost of mobility.** The Resident asks "the expences of keeping a horse and

  at the expence of camels to bring such things as I want overland" (p. 69, folio 60).
- **Overland travel in Lower Sind as a paid, negotiated and unreliable transaction (Withington,

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Sorley's central verdict on the Company's Sind venture, given twice.** "It was actually a rather
  hopeless enterprise in which the Company was engaged" (p. 76, folio 67); and "any far-sighted
  observer could have foretold at least ten years before the trading venture in Sind came to an end
  that that end was inevitable and that it was only a question of time and. opportunity for the Sind
  Government to force its will o;n the English traders" (p. 77, folio 68).
- **He holds the Kalhora policy to have been rational, and says so against his own side's
  interest.** "There was considerable force in what Sarfaraz Khan did and from his own point of view
  the policy ·was certainly not mistaken. We cap.[text-layer?] hardly expect, however, that a-
  trading Company would take the same view" (p. 76, folio 67). And: "they were not prepared to
  tolerate it beyond a point where the · a9vantage[text-layer?] passed from Sind to the Afghans"
  (p. 77, folio 68).
- **But he also attributes the crisis to a personal failing of the ruler.** "There is no doubt that
  with a broad�r-minded[text-layer?] man than Sarfaraz Khan on . the throne, the crisis

## Doubts

- **No archive shrine material whatever in pp. 67–79.** The only mapping offered above,
  **bhit-bhit-shah**, rests on the running head reproducing the book's title. Nothing on these pages
  supports a factual statement about Bhit, about Shah Abdul Latif, or about any of the 169 rows.
  The chunk is a documentary chapter on the English factory and on seventeenth-century travellers.
- **Nagar Parkar is a name collision that must not be resolved into a shrine.** Three archive rows
  carry that place —
  `nagarparkar-jain-temples-nagarparkar-cultural-landscape`, `churrio-jabal-durga-mata-temple`
  (Churrio Hill, Nagarparkar) and `gori-temple-gori-jo-mandar` (between Islamkot and Nagarparkar) —
  but the chunk mentions only "Nagar Parkar, a poor town" and the surrounding desert (p. 78,
  folio 69). **No temple, Jain or Hindu, and no hill or shrine is named.** Do not attach these pages
  to any of the three.
- **"Shahpiir[text-layer?]" / "Shahpoer" (pp. 74, 76) is a place in Sind where Ghulam Shah Kalhoro
  held durbar in camp.** It is not the Shahpur of `shah-yousuf` (Shah Yousuf, Shahpur, Punjab). The
  chunk gives no district for it and does not locate it further; do not merge the two.
- **"Porebunder" appears twice with two possible senses.** At p. 67 "the Porebunder and other
  merchants at Shahbunder" reads as merchants *of* Porbandar trading at Shahbunder; at p. 71,
  folio 62 the pirates ask to be sent "to Bombay or Porebunder" as a destination. The chunk does not
  gloss the name. Not placed.
- **The dynasty's name is printed four different ways in thirteen pages and at least one form is
  damaged**: "Kalhorn[text-layer?]" (pp. 71, 73), "KalhOro" (pp. 72, 74, 76, 77), "KalhOra"
