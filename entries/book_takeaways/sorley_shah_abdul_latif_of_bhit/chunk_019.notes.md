## Practices, institutions, economy

- **THE DOCTRINE OF THE SLEEPING SAINT — the chunk's single most important passage, and the nearest
  thing in the transcribed book to a theory of why shrines exist.** "Then finally there prevails
  throughout Islam the belief that **the saints are not dead, but merely sleeping. They are within
  their tombs. Their tombs have become their houses which they can leave and whence make journeys.
  They are accessible to prayer and grant "f,;lvours[text-layer?]. This is the solid foundation of
  the extraordinary devotion paid in Sind to the tombs of the deceased saints, pirs and holy men.
  This devotion is bound up with the system of personal religion that exists in the
  relatfon[text-layer?] of pir to murid. The disciple must obey his spiritual director. From this
  have risen many darwish orders commonly called after the name of the saints who founded them. Thus
  present practice and continued tradition combine to weld the whole into a coherent system, the
  roots of which go deep into the past.**" (p. 302, folio 293) **Seven separable claims: saints
  sleep rather than die; the tomb is a dwelling; the saint travels from it; prayer reaches him; he
  grants favours; this underwrites tomb devotion specifically in Sind; and it is continuous with the
  pir–murid obedience relation, from which the named darwish orders descend. "f,;lvours"[text-layer?]
  is "favours" destroyed in the text layer and sits on the load-bearing word.**
- **THE PRICE OF A PIR — the chunk's one explicit statement of a market in religious authority, and
  the most quotable economic line in the transcribed book.** Quoting Burton: "' Tasuwwuf[text-layer?]
  ', says Burton, ' under the native governments was as formidable a political engine as most of the
  secret confrater­nities[text-layer?] recorded in history . . . Even among the mild Sindhis a
  noted Pir (religious ·s�perior[text-layer?]) formerly might safely order one of his Murids or
  disciples to murder an enemy. **Yet the native princes encouraged it partly from superstition and
  partly because the price of every Pir was well known to them** ' (History of Sindh, pp. 203-4)."
  (pp. 299–300, folios 290–291) Sorley's own gloss follows: "The Pirs held · positions of great
  personal authority." (p. 300, folio 291) **Four institutional facts: tasawwuf as a political
  instrument comparable to a secret society; a pir's power to order a murid to kill; state

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The darwishes of Tatta take no harm from throwing themselves on the rocks of Makali.** "' These
  excitable men often work themselves into such a state of holy ecstasy that they cast
  them­selves[text-layer?] on the rocks of the mountain of Makali : **but by the blessing of their
  learned doctors and teachers no harm befalls them.** '" (p. 298, folio 289, quoting the
  Tarikh-i-Tahiri). **The protective agency is named — "the blessing of their learned doctors and
  teachers" — not the saint. Sorley reports it inside a quotation and immediately calls popular
  Sufism a degeneration; he does not endorse it.**
- **Al-Khadir / Khwajo Khizr drank the waters of immortality and will live to the last day.**
  "Amongst the chief of these is Al-Khadir, who drank the waters of immortality." (p. 301, folio 292)
  "Khwajo Khizr has a place all to himself as the Zinda Pir, the living Pir, who has drunk the waters
  of life and will live till the last day when, after being reduced to dust, he will be raised up
  again with the rest of mankind." (pp. 301–302, folios 292–293) **Sorley attributes the Sindhi form
  of the belief to "popular superstition" (p. 301, folio 292) and the green-clothed river form to
  "Baliich[text-layer?] poetry" (p. 301, folio 292).**
- **The Khwaja remembers a battle on the river.** "' the Khwaja himself will remember that battle.'"
  (p. 301, folio 292, from a Baluchi song, footnote 2 citing "Longworth Dames : Popular Poetry of
  the Baloches, p. 73."). **Attributed to Baluchi oral poetry, not asserted by Sorley.**
- **The saints are asleep in their tombs, leave them and travel, and grant favours.** "the saints are
  not dead, but merely sleeping. They are within their tombs. Their tombs have become their houses
  which they can leave and whence make journeys. They are accessible to prayer and grant
  "f,;lvours[text-layer?]." (p. 302, folio 293) **Reported by Sorley as a belief that "prevails
  throughout Islam", not as his own view; he calls it "the solid foundation" of Sindhi tomb
  devotion.**
- **The Tuhfat-al-Kiram's miracles of saints, dismissed at second hand.** "' very credulous in
  recording the miracles of saints so numerous that there is· scarcely a village in that
  priest­ridden[text-layer?] country which has not its tombs of holy men whose lives and powers are
  here recorded with implicit faith. '" (p. 298, folio 289, Elliot on the Tuhfat-al-Kiram's author).
  **"lives and powers" — the miracles are of the tomb-holders, and the archive should note that the
  only named collection of Sindhi saints' karamat in this book is one Sorley calls "useless".**
- **Pir Murad's saying.** "' it is better to restore one dead heart to eternal life than life to a
  thousand dead bodies '" (p. 300, folio 291). **Attributed with "who is reputed to have said",
  weaker than chunk 016's "to whom is attributed" (p. 257, folio 248).**
- **The trance as evidence of sanctity.** "It was precisely this form of emotional excitement which
  made the strongest appeal to the common man and convinced him of the divine inspiration and
  saintliness of the hqly[text-layer?] mendicant." (p. 301, folio 292) **Sorley's own analysis of
  how a karamat-claim was made credible, and it is psychological, not theological.**

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The chapter's governing distinction, stated in its first sentence.** "THE practice of Sufism by
  the common people in Sind was a very different thing from the refined system . of the poets and
  theologians." (p. 298, folio 289) **The whole of `CHAPTER VII` (pp. 298–303, folios 289–294) is
  built on this two-tier model, and it is restated at the chapter's end: "The real message of
  tasawwuf is with the intellectuals, not with the professional performers of routine." (p. 303,
  folio 294)**
- **Sorley's declared refusal to write the history he has just said cannot be written.** "The changes
  which Sufism underwent in the course of its history have already been indicated. **It is not
  possible to trace in any detail the progress of tasawwuf in Sind.**" (p. 298, folio 289) **He then
  traces it anyway, over five pages, on Burton, Elliot, Jethmal Parsram and the Tarikh-i-Tahiri —
  all four of which he disparages. The archive should keep the disclaimer attached to the
  material.**
- **His defence of tasawwuf against his own evidence.** "Nor would it be fair to judge tasawwuf in
  Sind by the standards of the lowest pretenders to it . **It is not fair to value
  Siifism[text-layer?] by its aberrations. It should be judged by its successes and not by what
  ignorant persons interpret its ideals to be.**" (p. 300, folio 291) And, immediately before it,
  the concession: "Nothing is more certain than that the criticisms of this system passed by Burnes
  and Burton are more than deserved." (p. 300, folio 291) **He concedes the criticism in full and
  then rules it inadmissible — a move the archive should record whole rather than half.**
- **The unbridgeable gulf between the poet and the fakir, and the complaint about the word "Sufi".**
  "But a mighty gulf yawns between the thinking of Shah Abdul Latif and the utterances of the
  itinerant fakir … **Yet popular nomenclature would make both men Siifis[text-layer?].** We might
  as well expect Plato and the village mummer to speak a common language." (p. 303, folio 294) **A
  terminological argument: Sorley says the word "Sufi" in popular Sindhi usage covers two things he
  regards as incommensurable. This bears directly on how the archive reads the word in any Sindhi
  source.**
- **The claim that Sufism in Sind was carried by individuals, not institutions** — quoted under
  `## Practices, institutions, economy` (p. 302, folio 293; p. 303, folio 294). **It stands in
  unresolved tension with his own "coherent system" passage at p. 302 (folio 293); see Doubts.**
- **The unanswerable question about Sufism's ultimate origin, declared not worth answering.**
  "Whether, as Macdonald thinks, the main influence comes from the religion of Muhammad himself, or
  whether it has been wrought to final perfection by the peculiar bent of the Persian mind working on
  that Islamic material matters not. After all the achievement is there for us to make of it what we
  will." (p. 297, folio 288) **Compare chunk 016's two refusals to resolve the Hindu-substrate
  question (pp. 247, 257): this is the third in the same pattern, and the first where Sorley names
  the scholar whose view he is declining to adjudicate.**
- **The "many facets" argument against systematising Sufism at all.** "No one can exhaust b
  y[text-layer?] description the many facets of Siifism[text-layer?]. Like a Persian carpet or the
  sea over which the soft breeze plays it takes its colour from the light that falls upon it. From so
  many angles can the eye behold it that there is no end to the variety of its·[text-layer?]
  appearances. All that can be done is to trace some of its main ideas and show how they are
  expressed with emphasis now on this aspect now on that of the simple emotional longing of man for
  what he regards as the divine." (p. 296, folio 287) **Immediately followed by the rhetorical "Who
  shall summarize the changing forms of such an attitude ?" (p. 297, folio 288). This is the
  methodological preamble to the passage where he states his limits.**
- **The closing claim for Sufism's value, given as fusion rather than doctrine.** "I prefer,
  therefore, to end on the serious note of Sufism, the great harmony it makes with the deepest
  feelings of the true believer in religion, submissive and humble before the wonder of something
  that far surpasses the individual imagining of one finite mind. This is the prevalent note of
  Islamic mysticism." (p. 297, folio 288) "There is a province where poetry, religion and philosophy
  all meet on common ground. Sufism has certainly found the path to that fusion of the deepest
  thoughts of which the introspective mind of man is capable." (p. 297, folio 288)
- **Nicholson's definition of Persian Sufi poetry, adopted as the yardstick for Latif.** "Writing of
  the Persian Sufi poets Nicholson has said, ' The real basis ·of[text-layer?] their poetry is a
  loftily inculcated ethical system which recognizes in purity of heart, self­renunciation[text-layer?]
  and bridling of the passions, the necessary conditions of eternal happiness. Attached to this we
  find a pantheistic theory of the emanation of all things from God and their ultimate union with
  him.'" (pp. 295–296, folios 286–287, footnote 1 citing "Selected Poems from the Diwani-Shamsi
  Tabriz.    Introduction, p. xxvi."). Sorley's application: "All these traits are evident in the
  Sindhi poet." (p. 296, folio 287)
- **The Sufi ethical system as Sorley states it in his own words.** "The Siifi[text-layer?] philosophy
  thus demands a strict self-discipline with renuncia­tion[text-layer?] of selfish feelings and a
  curbing of evil passions. It is based on a deep ethical system and lays stress upon purity of
  heart. The self, during the stage of purification, is thus a danger and an obstacle. It misleads
  the seeker and interposes between him and the Sought a veil that is hard to pierce." (p. 293,
  folio 284)
- **The doctrine of the soul's relation to God, quoted from an unnamed source.** "But the
  significance lies probably in the Siifi[text-layer?] doctrine that the soul of man differs
  infinitely in kind but not at all in degree from the Divine Spirit ' whereof it is a particle and
  wherein it will ultimately be absorbed '." (p. 293, folio 284) **The quoted phrase carries no
  footnote and no attribution** — **(not stated)**.
- **The translator's dilemma, stated as a general principle.** "The traµslator[text-layer?] is always
  faced with a dilemma. Is he to reproduce the words or the spirit of the original ? Is he to be a
  photographer or an artist ? There is no resolving this dilemma. In the end the translator falls
  back on some kind of compromise because rarely is it possible without loss to transmute the genius
  of one language into that of another." (p. 306, folio 297) **Sorley's own account of his
  translation method follows and is recorded under `## Bibliography and apparatus`. For an archive
  that will quote his English versions, this paragraph is a provenance statement and should travel
  with any quotation of his verse.**
