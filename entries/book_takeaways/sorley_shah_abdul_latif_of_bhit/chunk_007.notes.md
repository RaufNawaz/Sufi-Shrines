# sorley_shah_abdul_latif_of_bhit — chunk 007 — pp. 95–109

Continuous prose, no verse. The chunk sits wholly inside Sorley's economic chapter. It opens
mid-argument in the general survey of Mughal-period Indian economy that chunk 006 left four lines
into (`VI. Trading conditions in Sind in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries` /
`(a) The conveniences. of trade`), carries that section to its close (p. 100), and then runs through
a new lettered section and four numbered sub-sections as printed: `(b) The industries and commerce
of Sind` (p. 100), `(1) The cotton industry in Sind` (p. 102), `(2) Saltpetre` (p. 104),
`(3) Indigo` (p. 106), `(4) Leather and leather-work` (p. 107) and `(S) Fish and fish products`
(p. 109, the section number itself damaged — see Doubts). The chunk breaks off mid-sentence in the
fish section.

individually: PDF 95 → `86 .`, 96 → `87`, 97 → `88`, 98 → `89`, 99 → `90`, 100 → `gr[text-layer?]`
(= 91), 101 → `92`, 102 → `93`, 103 → `94`, 104 → `95`, 105 → `96`, 106 → `97`, 107 → `98`,
108 → `99`, 109 → `100`. The number sits at the left of the running head on odd PDF pages (even
printed) and at the right on even PDF pages (odd printed), without exception. **One page number is
itself destroyed**: PDF p. 100 prints `gr` for `91` (g←9, r←1), which is exactly the numeral-damage
class this book is known for, and which is why the offset must be checked letter by letter rather
than assumed. References below give both, as `(p. 98, folio 89)`.

**Shah Abdul Latif is not named once in the body of this chunk.** He appears only in the running
head of all fifteen pages. There is no poetry, no Bhit, no ʿurs, no sajjada nashin, no named shrine
and no karamat anywhere in pp. 95–109 — the `## Legends and miracles (karamat)` heading is
therefore omitted rather than padded.

**But the chunk is not empty for the archive, and in one respect it is the most valuable stretch so
far.** At p. 98 (folio 89) Sorley states, as a general institutional fact about Sind, that fairs
held on saints' days at saints' tombs were an established part of the commercial system and that
the *Gazetteer of Sind* lists the places and their fixed annual dates. That is the shrine-fair
(mela / ʿurs-market) economy named as an economic institution, by an ICS author writing in 1940,
and it is the first time in this book that shrines enter the economic argument at all. Two further
passages at p. 101 (folio 92) give the building economy of tombs. Those three passages are the find
of this chunk; everything else is trade, manufacture and revenue.

## Practices, institutions, economy

This is the whole substance of the chunk, and the three shrine-related passages come first.

- **Fairs on saints' days at saints' tombs, named as an established commercial institution of Sind —
  the single most important sentence in this chunk for the archive.** "The oriental system of
  markets and fairs on saints' days at saints' tombs was well established and the
  `Gazetteer3 of 'Sind[text-layer?]` gives a long list of places where such fairs were held on
  regular days every year: In fact in this respect Sind's commerce was · on the old medieval system
  which had prevailed in England itself up to the time of the rise of the trading towns and the
  growth of the merchant gilds." (p. 98, folio 89) The footnote is the whole citation: "See
  Gazetteer of Sind by Hughes." (p. 98, folio 89, footnote 3) **Note what the book does and does not
  say.** It says the system was "well established"; it says the fairs fell on saints' days and were
  held at saints' tombs; it says they recurred "on regular days every year"; and it defers the list
  of places to Hughes rather than printing any of them. **It names no shrine, no saint, no district,
  no date and no numbers, and it does not use the words *ʿurs*, *mela* or *langar*.** Any attempt to
  attach this passage to a particular archive row would be an inference the book does not make.
- **Glazed tile as tomb-and-mosque craft.** "The making of glazed tiles was, however, highly skilful
  and the products were used to ornament tombs and mosques." (p. 101, folio 92) This stands inside a
  paragraph whose whole burden is that Sind crafts were primitive — flour milling, oil pressing and
  pottery are all described as unchanged since Mughal times — so the tile trade is singled out as
  the exception, and its market is religious architecture.
- **Tombs of holy men as the only rural occasion for permanent building materials.** "Building was
  unimportant as most of the houses and habitations were made of mud, sticks, grass or branches of
  trees. Only in the largest towns like Tatta and for the tombs of holy men was the use of brick and
  stone much · indulged in. The climate was so dry that mud houses lasted for a 'generation, and the
  poorer classes, just as they do today, lived in shacks and huts built of the cheapest material
  available. Sind is deficient in `hu,ilding[text-layer?]` · stone. It is, therefore, useless
  `loo.king[text-layer?]` in it for masterpieces of the mason's art." (p. 101, folio 92) **Read
  against the tile sentence immediately above it, this is a two-part statement of a shrine building
  economy**: brick and stone reserved for saints' tombs and the largest towns, glazed tile as the
  skilled trade that decorated them, in a province that had to import its stone.
- **Mosques at Tatta in 1842, in the same breath as the town's ruin.** Von Orlich: "' There are only
  a few mosques in the place which are built of stone and · painted white, but they have the same
  dirty and ruinous appearance as the town ; and there are no traces of the extensive trade which
  formerly was carried on . . . . Poverty and dirt everywhere prevail.'" (p. 103, folio 94) The
  count is "only a few"; no mosque is named.
- **Removing shoes at a mosque, raised and then set aside.** Arguing that Sind Muslims held no
  prejudice against leather, Sorley footnotes: "The practice of removing leather from the feet on
  entering a mosque cannot be cited as an instance." (p. 108, folio 99, footnote 1) **The chunk's
  only explicit statement of a Muslim ritual practice**, and it is made in order to discount it.
- **Bovine veneration, the stigma of the tanner, and the shoe as insult — attributed by Sorley to
  "typically Hindu parts of India" and expressly contrasted with Sind.** "Most foreign observers
  write of the typically Hindu parts of India, where the bovine animals are held in superstitious
  veneration, trade in hides is regarded as something dishonourable, the tanner is a despised and
  down-trodden creature, and a beating with shoes is held to be an outrageous insult." Against
  which: "In Sind, a Muslim land, this prejudice against leather is not felt by three-quarters of
  the population and we have no reason to think that in Moghul days the habits of the people
  differed in this respect materially from what any `traveller:[text-layer?]` can see for himself in
  Sind today." (p. 108, folio 99) The "three-quarters" figure is given without source.
- **Hindu vegetarianism as a limit on the fish trade.** "Fish has always been esteemed as an article
  of food except by those classes of Hindus which are vegetarian." (p. 109, folio 100)
- **Fishery revenue farmed to the Mohanas at one-third of the daily catch, with a named collector
  post.** Del Hoste, 1832: "' Fisheries are established at different places, and the rivers and
  water courses portioned off to the Mohanas or `:fishermen[text-layer?]`. At each of these places
  there is a Mehta to collect the duties which consist of one-third of what is daily caught.'"
  (p. 109, folio 100, footnote 4, citing "Secret Department, Sinde Mission Memoirs by Lieutenant Del
  Hoste. Bombay Government Records, 1 832, p. 39.") **A precisely stated share-rent on a natural
  resource, with the collecting officer named by title.**
- **The parwana as an instrument of monopoly, and a Kalhora ruler granting one.** "It was also
  anxious to obtain from the Indian rulers parwanas which would enable the Company effectively to
  cut out all rivals. Ghulam Shah `KalhOro[text-layer?]` did grant such a parwana to the second
  factory, but he was always ready to allow native interlopers from the Persian Gulf to trade in
  saltpetre. This attitude led to protests by the Sind factors." (p. 104, folio 95) **This is the
  chunk's clearest picture of how a Sind ruler actually used a written grant**: issued, then not
  enforced against rivals.
- **Taxation described as a comprehensive system, in one sentence.** "·An elaborate system of
  taxation impinged on every part · of the business of manufacture, distribution and sale, with
  profession taxes, transit dues, customs and imposts on retail sales." (p. 95, folio 86) Four named
  heads of tax, no rates.
- **Customs enforcement and the gold-exchange racket at Tatta.** Tavernier: "' The King has conceded
  to the English captains that they shall not be searched when they leave their vessels to come
  on · shore. But one day an English captain `goi!}g[text-layer?]` to Tatta … was arrested by the
  customs guard from whom he could not defend himself `arid[text-layer?]` who searched him in spite
  of anything he could say. They found gold `on'him[text-layer?]`, he having already conveyed some in
  sundry j ourneys between the vessel and the town. He was let off on payment of the ordinary
  duty.'" (pp. 99–100, folios 90–91) Sorley's gloss names the mechanism: "The usual practice was to
  deprive carriers of gold of their coin and pay its alleged equivalent value in local coins, in
  which it may be presumed the exchange was always against the importer." (p. 100, folio 91)
- **Port procedure at Laribunder, quoted whole from the *Tarikh-i-Tahiri*.** "' Whenever a ship
  enters the creek between Laribunder and t.he ocean it intimates its approach by firing a gun which
  is responded to by the Guard house in order by that signal to inform the people at the port of the
  arrival of a strange vessel. These again instantly send word of its arrival to the
  `mer�hants[text-layer?]` of Tatta and then embarking on boats repair to the place where the guard
  is posted. Ere they reach it those on the look-out have already enquired into the nature of the
  ship. Every vessel and every trader must undergo this questioning. All concerned in the business
  now go to their boats (ghrabs) , to the mouth of the creek. If a ship belong to the port it
  `i�[text-layer?]` allowed to move up and anchor under Laribunder ; if it belong to some other port
  it can go no farther ; its cargo is transferred to boats and forwarded to the city .'" (p. 99,
  folio 90, citing "Elliot : I, p. 277.") **A full working description of a pre-colonial port
  clearance procedure, signal to discharge.**
- **The kafila, with its seasons.** "There was a trade route from Bakhar to Afghanistan and Persia
  frequented by large kafilas of laden camels that had regular seasons for travel which avoided the
  worst of the hot weather and inundation in Upper Sind and the intense winter cold of the highlands
  of `Baliichistan[text-layer?]` and Persia. These kafilas consisted Of hundreds of camels
  accompanied by an army of traders and guards." (p. 98, folio 89)
- **Land transport: pack animals only, wheels almost unknown.** "In Sind itself transport was all by
  pack animals, mostly camels, which are well suited to the terrain. Wheeled traffic was practically
  unknown and it is doubtful if bullock carts were used much except in the environs of the various
  emporia." (p. 98, folio 89) Qualified by Del Hoste's 1832 note that around Khairpur the few good
  carts "belonged to the Amirs" (p. 98, folio 89, footnote 2).
- **River traffic, with two counts and a rejection of a third.** Pottinger: "' In our voyage from
  Hyderabad to Raja ka Dera we counted three hundred and forty-one boats in nineteen days and on our
  return we saw in four days a greater number in proportion to the time. I was assured by a Hindu
  merchant that five hundred boats could be assembled for hire at Bakhar in a week.'" (p. 100,
  folio 91, footnote 2, citing "Memoir of Sinde, p. 247"). Against which Sorley brackets the
  *Ain*: "In the Ain-i-Akbari, II, p. 338, it is stated that no less. than 40,000 boats of all kinds
  could be found in the sarkar of Tatta {probably a gross exaggeration)" (p. 97, folio 88, footnote).
- **The brokers, agents and dalals, and the tax on visible wealth.** "The actual handling of goods
  was done by brokers, agents and dalals, who were mostly Hindus and who probably managed to amass ·
  considerable wealth, though it was fatal to display any kind of opulence lest the capricious
  covetousness of the local · officials should devise some method of confiscating it or levying a
  disproportionate penalty upon it." (p. 100, folio 91) Sorley makes this the explanation of
  Ovington's "bannians": their frugality was "sufficient reason" enough without piety (p. 100,
  folio 91). Ovington's own words: "' Sumptuousness and state suit not very well with the life and
  conditions of a Bannian. . . . This keeps our brokers at Surat who are bannians from all costly
  disbursements though they are reckoned by some to be worth 15, by others `3 0[text-layer?]` lakhs

## Arguments and interpretations

Sorley argues at length in this chunk and takes sides against named scholars. All of the following
are recorded as his positions, not as fact.

- **The chunk's central thesis: European trade was not a drain on India, and the "drain" school is
  wrong.** "The foreign traders sought to gain profit for themselves and did so, but their operations
  were in no sense a drain on the wealth of India because they drew their main profits from the sale
  in Europe of Indian cotton goods which, but for them, would never have been produced in such bulk."
  (p. 96, folio 87) He adds the three supporting claims: the trader "paid reasonably adequate prices
  to the weavers in India"; competition among the Europeans "for the sea carrying trade" cut freight
  charges; and "These trading operations were paid for in gold and silver which India began to absorb
  in enormous quantities in return for the labour and skill of Indian artisans and merchants."
  (p. 96, folio 87) **His conclusion is stated polemically**: the trade meant no drain "as so many
  Indian economic writers continue to assert without any . evidence whatever" (p. 96, folio 87).
  **This is the most clearly period-bound and politically loaded argument in the chunk and a human
  should treat it as an ICS officer's 1940 position, not as a finding.**
- **The monopoly and its terminus.** "The cardinal fact is that India had the practical monopoly of
  the supply of cotton goods for nearly a hundred years. The products of Indian looms were eagerly
  sought for in the markets of the West till well on into the eighteenth century." And: "India could
  in no circumstances have expected to maintain after 1760 the position of privilege it occupied
  from 1600 to 1750." (p. 96, folio 87) He is explicit that the reversal preceded the mills: "This
  happened before the time of the industrial revolution in Britain with its power-looms, its
  factories, its vast increase of skill in the technique of production and its multiplicity and
  improvement of patterns." (p. 96, folio 87)
- **Bernier is rejected as a witness; Findlay Shirras is preferred.** Of Bernier's report that peasants'
  "' children … are carried away as `slayes[text-layer?]` '": "Bernier is clearly here generalizing
  unwisely from some act of tyranny by. which his feelings had been revolted. A truer picture is
  given by Mr Findlay Shirras" (p. 95, folio 86). Shirras's substitute picture makes communications,
  not oppression, the constraint: "' In pre-British India communications were so bad that, except
  for the Ganges and the Indus, or the roads from Agra to Lahore and to the west coast, there were
  no channels of communication and hence_ it was impossible for India to be a commercial country …
  `Production must, therefore, have been limitedin[text-layer?]` these land-locked areas.'" (p. 95,
  folio 86)
- **Moreland is rejected twice, on different grounds, and Faruki is endorsed.** On social conditions:
  "I think that `Fariiki[text-layer?]` is undoubtedly correct in his view that the works of Moreland
  paint too gloomy a picture of the social conditions of · India during the reign of the great
  Moghuls from Akbar to Aurangzeb, as his views are coloured by the belief that the Indian population
  was `�tterly[text-layer?]` downtrodden, harried and tyrannized over by the autocracy of the
  government" (p. 96, folio 87). On leather: "in face of all the evidence it is impossible to accept
  as a true account of the situation the observations of Moreland" (p. 107, folio 98) — those
  observations being "that the export of hides on an extensive scale is `a�[text-layer?]` modem
  phenomenon, that few of the foreign observers say anything about the common people wearing shoes,
  and that the saddlery of Moghul times was composed to a great extent of ropes and cloth and did
  not use leather in the European fashion" (pp. 107–108, folios 98–99). **Sorley nevertheless quotes
  Moreland approvingly three times in the saltpetre and cotton sections** (pp. 102, 104, 105;
  folios 93, 95, 96).
- **The poverty of India is treated as constant and pre-colonial.** "Moreland tends to underestimate
  the fact that India has always been a poor country, that the common man has been content with a
  lesser material standard of comfort than the European and that the vast majority of the population
  has never been accustomed to high standards of living." (pp. 96–97, folios 87–88) He then recruits
  Gandhi and Tagore to the same proposition (p. 97, folio 88). **Recorded as his argument; note that
  it does the work of neutralising the poverty evidence he has just quoted.**
- **The *Ain-i-Akbari* statistics are dismissed as unusable, and the dismissal is first-hand.** "It
  is impossible, as Vincent Smith has shown, to make very much of the amazing jumble of statistical
  material in the Ain-i-Akbari. I have studied the statistics for Sind given in·- the Ain and can
  find very little of value in them. They offer no reliable guide to the social condition of the
  time, and they are maddeningly vague just where the historian desires them to be precise."
  (p. 97, folio 88) Vincent Smith is quoted for the reason: "' All subjects are considered solely
  with reference to the sovereign and the court and little or no attempt is made to compare the
  conditions under Akbar with those ·existing under his predecessors.'" (p. 97, folio 88) **Sorley
  then quotes the *Ain* twice anyway** — for the 40,000 boats, with "{probably a gross
  exaggeration)" attached (p. 97, folio 88, footnote), and for the Lower Sind fish staple without
  qualification (p. 109, folio 100). **An internal inconsistency worth flagging.**
- **The defence of the Kalhora and Talpur rulers against Postans and Burton — the chunk's most
  significant judgement for Sind political history.** "These causes, and not the incompetence of the
  `Kalh6ra[text-layer?]` and `Talpiirs[text-layer?]` as rulers, so naively assumed by persons of
  Postans' or Burton's ways of thinking, were responsible for the decay of the once great
  metropolis." (p. 103, folio 94) The causes he means are the four listed immediately before:
  competing towns, silting at Shahbunder, British self-supply and British export. In a footnote:
  "This view is expressed also by Burnes." (p. 103, folio 94, footnote 2) **Sorley is here defending
  the dynasty under which Shah Abdul Latif lived and died against the standard colonial charge of
  misrule.**
- **Mughal government in Sind is judged well-organized, with the difficulties generalised away.**
  "From these and other contemporary records it may be gathered that trade in Sind was on a very
  well-organized basis, both in respect of land and water j ourneys ; the business was soundly
  established and the government supervision very thorough. The difficulties which traders had to
  face were those common to the India of that day, vexatious imposts and delays, dangers from pirates
  and robbers, and poverty of road communication." (p. 100, folio 91) **This continues, rather than
  contradicts, the comparative defence of Mughal administration recorded from p. 90 in chunk 006.**
- **A comparative frame for the fair system: medieval England.** Sind's saints'-day fairs put its
  commerce "on the old medieval system which had prevailed in England itself up to the time of the
  rise of the trading towns and the growth of the merchant gilds" (p. 98, folio 89). **Sorley's
  analogy, not the archive's** — but it is the interpretive claim attached to the chunk's most
  important shrine passage, and it frames shrine fairs as a stage of commercial development rather
  than as religious practice.
- **Postans' lists are accepted with one correction.** "In the main, however, Postans' lists are
  accurate enough for the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries if the cardinal fact of the decline
  in the importance of the cotton industry, explained in the previous section, is borne in mind."
  (p. 100, folio 91) **The book is explicitly back-projecting a nineteenth-century trade list onto
  the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries** — a methodological move a user of these lists must know
  about.
- **Sind is judged industrially minor and the reason for treating it given.** "Apart from cotton
  spinning and weaving, the finishing of leather goods, the production of saltpetre and indigo, and
  the drying of fish, Sind deserves little special notice industrially. These industries, however,
  are sufficiently important to merit special notice because they attracted the attention of the
  English traders and travellers to the country." (p. 102, folio 93) **The selection principle of
  the whole chapter is stated here: what the English noticed.**
- **Sorley cites his own earlier work.** "See The Marine Fisheries of the Bombay Presidency, by the
  present writer." (p. 109, folio 100, footnote 5)
- **The continuity argument, made five separate times.** Oil presses turn "just as they do today"
  (p. 101, folio 92); the potter's craft "can still be seen in Sind exactly as it was in Moghul and
  Kalhoro days" (p. 101, folio 92); the poorer classes live in shacks "just as they do today"
  (p. 101, folio 92); tanning skill "is not yet dead" (p. 107, folio 98); the pallo fishery is
  "carried on to this day in exactly the same way" (p. 109, folio 100). **This is the book's
  characteristic evidentiary move — present-day Sind used as testimony about the seventeenth
  century** — and it should be flagged wherever a fact in this chunk rests on it.

## Doubts

- **No transcriber flags.** This chunk carries **no `[OCR?]` and no `[illegible]` markers** — the
  route is `text_layer` and the extractor left none. **That is not a statement that the text is
  sound.** Every damaged string inventoried below was found by reading, and a human should assume
  more of it than is listed.
- **The offset held, but one printed page number was itself damaged and had to be reconstructed from
  position.** PDF p. 100 prints `gr` where `91` belongs. The identification rests on the sequence
  (folio 90 before it, folio 92 after it) and on the alternation of the number's position in the
  running head, not on the glyphs. **Every other page number in the chunk is legible as printed.**
- **Sehwan (pp. 98, 106; folios 89, 97) is a bare toponym here and is deliberately NOT mapped.**
  Under the interim convention it sits under *Other sites*. **If bare toponyms are ruled mappable it
  would map to `lal-shahbaz-qalandar`.** Nothing in either passage mentions Lal Shahbaz Qalandar, a
  shrine, a tomb, a dargah, a fair or any religious activity; Sehwan appears as a cotton place and
  as the chief indigo-producing territory of Middle Sind and nothing else.
- **The same ruling is applied to every other bare toponym in the chunk, and these are the rows they
  would take.** Sukkur (pp. 100, 102; folios 91, 93) → `gurdwara-dash-mesh-pita`,
  `gurdwara-pehli-patshahi-jind-pir-sukkur`, and arguably `sadh-belo-sadh-belo-island-temple` and
  `kalka-cave-temple-asthan-of-kalka-devi` (Aror, Sukkur District) — **four candidate rows for one
  bare town name, which is itself an argument against mapping bare toponyms.** Rohri (pp. 98, 102;
