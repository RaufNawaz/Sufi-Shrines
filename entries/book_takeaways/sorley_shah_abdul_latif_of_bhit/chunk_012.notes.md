## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- **Khwajo Khizr / Zinda Pir, and his shrine on an island in the Indus at Sukkur** — the chunk's
  second most substantial shrine account, and the reason two archive rows are flagged in Doubts
  rather than one being chosen. "Khizr, or Elias, was supposed to have drunk of the fountain of the
  waters of life and believed not to die till the last trump. Khwajo Khizr was for this reason
  called in Sind Zinda Pir (The Living Pir) . He had a shrine on an island in the Indus at Sukkur.
  He was the patron saint of waters and rivers and hence had come to be regarded as the god of the
  river Indus" (p. 178, folio 169). Sorley lists "the strange cult of Khwajo Khizr" among Crooke's
  categories of popular religion and singles it out: it "attained a peculiar religious signifi­cance
  of its own, linked up with the worship of the river Indus and supported by Muslim and Hindu alike"
  (p. 177, folio 168). Also: "Burton gives more particulars of this strange river cult, in which
  Islam and Hinduism are curiously intermingled. He also quotes at length a hymn in honour of the
  river Indus" (p. 178, folio 169).

## Practices, institutions, economy

**This is the richest institutional chunk in the book so far.** Everything below is the book's
account of how shrine authority worked as a system, not hagiography.

- **The pir as an office, described feature by feature.** "The Sayid who was also a pir exercised as
  a murshid over his murids an almost regal power. The pir was usually connected with some shrine.
  He was invested with the pag (turban) . He sat, as on a throne, upon his holy seat, himself the
  gadinishin before whom on saints' days the multitude came to offer obeisance." (p. 176, folio 167)
  **Four institutional terms are given in the book's own spelling — "pir", "murshid", "murid",
  "gadinishin" — plus the investiture object, "the pag (turban)", and the calendar anchor, "saints'
  days".**
- **The pir's four working functions, as the book lists them.** "The pir received the offerings of
  his murids, settled their disputes often very satis­factorily, and led the prayers on solemn
  occasions." (p. 176, folio 167) **Offerings, dispute settlement and prayer leadership — an income,
  a jurisdiction and a liturgy.**
- **The tour of the murids as a fiscal and ceremonial circuit.** "When he went on tour to visit his
  murids his progress was one of almost regal magnificence. He rode a gaily caparisoned camel
  attended by bands of followers usually dressed in some kind of uniform and armed with an assortment
  of arms. The devout pressed forward for the honour of touching his stirrup leathers." (p. 176,
  folio 167) **Note the armed and uniformed retinue: the pir travels with a force.**
- **Sorley's name for the resulting political geography** — "The countryside became in this way the
  home of an immense number of these petty imperia." (p. 176, folio 167) And his mechanism: "The pir
  had thus a spiritual power which was always tending to take temporal forms, exactly as happened in
  the most influential days of the papacy in medieval Europe." (p. 176, folio 167)
- **Murshid–murid relations as the hinge of Sindhi dynastic politics.** "The Kalh6ra[text-layer?]
  were the murshids of the Talpiirs[text-layer?]. It was because of the hold they thus possessed over
  the Baliich[text-layer?] shepherd tribe that the Kalh6ra[text-layer?] were able to use the
  Talpiirs[text-layer?] for their own political aggrandizement. When the Kalh6ra[text-layer?]
  commanded as murshids, the Talp.iirs[text-layer?] as murids had to obey." (p. 176, folio 167)
  **This is the single most quotable institutional claim in the chunk for the economics of religion.**
- **And the limit on that power, stated as a political fact.** "Actually, it was an outrageous act by
  a Kalhorn[text-layer?] which shocked the public opinion of the day and led to the displacement of
  the Kalh6ra[text-layer?], the murshids, by the Talpiirs[text-layer?], their murids. The abuse of
  veneration could not, therefore, be carried beyond the limits which the public conscience would
  tolerate." (p. 176, folio 167) **The chunk never says what the outrageous act was** — **(not
  stated)**.
- **Endowed land: the Kalhora inam grants to Sayids.** Burton, quoted: "' Many are learned men, much
  respected by the commonalty in spite of discrepancy of belief. Under the Kalhorn[text-layer?]
  dynasty they became possessed of large landed estates granted as inam in perpetuum.'" (p. 173,
  folio 164) **This is the chunk's one explicit statement of a religious class holding endowed land
  in perpetuity, and the term used is "inam in perpetuum".**
- **Recruitment of the saintly class from outside Sind, as a continuous eight-century process.**
  "Throughout the Muslim domination of Sind for eight centuries there was a continual recruitment of
  the saintly families from outside." (p. 173, folio 164) "They entered Sind at various times from
  the Mussulman lands that lie between Arabia and India. They were a heterogeneous mixture of many
  races." (p. 173, folio 164) Sorley makes this one of two causes of their power: "It is possibly
  this fact, taken in conjunction with the superstitious hero-worship of the common people of Sind,
  which explains the extraordinary influence which they wielded." (pp. 173–174, folios 164–165)
- **Sayid endogamy and the inflation of the status.** "They would not allow their daughters to marry
  Muslims of less dignified stock than their own. If either of the parents was a Sayid, the children
  must be called Sayids too. There was in this way a considerable watering-down of the original Arab,
  Persian, Turk and other Asiatic strains in them." (p. 173, folio 164) **A descent rule with a
  stated demographic consequence.**
- **The taxation regime under the Arabs, as quoted.** "the Hindu landlords paying the land tax
  (kharaj) and the capitation tax (jizya)" (p. 174, folio 165). **Two named taxes, and the
  administrative point that collection stayed in Hindu hands.**
- **Conversion as a mass, late and shallow process — the book's economic-historical claim.** "Most of
  the population was converted to Islam in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, though the ruling
  autocracy of Sumra appears to have been converted earlier." (p. 174, folio 165) "Sind was a Muslim
  state which struggled to semi-independence in the mid-eighteenth century. It sought to found its
  basis on the theocracy of Islam, amongst a population composed for the most part of converted
  Jats." (p. 172)
- **Cross-community shrine visiting and votive offering, named as a problem to be explained.** "How
  are we to explain the extraordinary phenomen.on[text-layer?] of Hindus visiting Mussulman shrines
  and making votive offerings at them, and the general easy-going character of Sind orthodoxy ?"
  (p. 175, folio 166) **The chunk gives no shrine name, no place and no figures for this practice** —
  **(not stated)** — but records it as general.
- **The catalogue of popular institutions Sorley takes from Crooke and says all exist in Sind.**
  "There were miracle-working tombs. There was the canonization of reputed holy men. There was the
  personal worship of pirs. There was the institution of caste saints. There was the working of
  miracles by holy men. There- were nine-yard tombs. There was the strange cult of Khwajo Khizr.
  There was the efficacy of holy relics for the cure of disease. Examples of all could be cited from
  Sind." (p. 177, folio 168) **Eight named categories, including "nine-yard tombs" and "the
  institution of caste saints", both otherwise unexplained in the chunk.**
- **Talismans as a mass-market religious good.** "Demonology and magic were rife and there was the
  universal use of talismans, especially by the peasant class, as a protection against the evil eye
  and all kinds of human ills." (p. 177, folio 168) **The class specification — "especially by the
  peasant class" — is the book's, not an inference.**
- **The Khwajo Khizr river cult: a dated, calendared, Muslim-and-Hindu observance on the Indus.**
  "Muslims offered him oblations of lamps and flowers. These were launched upon the river on Thurs­day
  evenings in the fifth month of the solar year. At this time there was held the festival of the Bera
  (raft) when a raft was launched upon the river in honour of him." (p. 178, folio 169) **A weekday
  (Thursday evening), a month of the solar year (the fifth), a named festival ("the Bera (raft)")
  and two offering types (lamps and flowers).**
- **The Bhit mausoleum's weekly liturgy, still running at the time of writing.** "Since the poet's
  death the poems have been recited weekly on Fridays in the mausoleum which has become a kind of
  holy shrine which still draws its crowds." (p. 185, folio 176) The recitation is called a "' wake
  '" and the 1882 account gives the setting — "the wide courtyard" — the sound — "occasionally broken
  by a hearty " Allahu "" — and the congregation — "more than a hundred men, women and children"
  (p. 185, folio 176).
- **Tomb-building as princely patronage, with a named patron and a named craftsman.** "An imposing
  tomb was built for his body by Ghulam Shah Kalh6ro[text-layer?] in 1754" (p. 183, folio 174); "The
  fine mausoleum built for the poet's body by Ghulam Shah Kalhorn[text-layer?] was ornamented by a
  well-known mason of the time, one Idan from Sukkur" (p. 185, folio 176); and, in the footnote,
  Shah Abdul Latif's tomb "was erected at the expense of Ghulam Shah Kalhorn[text-layer?]" (p. 183,
  folio 174, footnote 1). **A ruler pays; a named mason decorates; the family grave precedes the
  saint's by twelve years.**
- **Burial clustering at Bhit.** "His father Habib Khan died in 1 742[text-layer?] and was buried in
  Bhit a few paces from where later in 1 754[text-layer?] Shah Abdul Latif's own tomb was erected"
  (p. 183, folio 174, footnote 1). **The earliest dated burial at Bhit in this chunk is 1742, twelve
  years before the shrine tomb.**
- **Votive deposit at the Shahdadpur tomb, with the object specified.** "There is a tomb over the
  remains of a celebrated pir on poles, round which are seen the heelropes of horses. For whenever an
  animal in the neighbourhood is afflicted with disease the owner prays at the tomb and on the
  recovery of the animal offers the heelropes in gratitude for the saint's assistance." (p. 177,
  folio 168) **A conditional vow, a livestock-health clientele and a physical ex-voto.**
- **Ritual access rules at the same site.** "They are approached with bare feet and the precincts are
  kept in cleanliness and good order." (p. 177, folio 168) **Barefoot approach and maintained
  precincts — the chunk does not say who maintains them** — **(not stated)**.
- **Charitable feeding as the road to sanctity.** Of Dato Khuhawar: "a man renowned for his wealth
  and the canals dug by him, as for his un­bounded liberality. . He was Chandookah' s Hatim of Tayyi.
  It is related of him that no one passed his door unfed" (p. 177, folio 168). **Canal-building and
  open-table feeding produce the sacred millstones; this is the closest the chunk comes to a langar
  economy.**
- **The murids' power over their pir's movements.** The poet "had intended to make· the pilgrimage to
  Kerbela in the last few years of his life, but was dissuaded from doing so by the insistence of his
  followers that he should not desert them" (p. 183, folio 174). **Followers as a constraint on the
  saint, not only as a constituency.**
- **Murid recruitment as a competitive threat to established families.** The poet's "fame … was
  becoming known and was attracting to him a number of murids", and this is exactly what "arouse[d]
  the jealousy of the orthodox ' hierarchy '" and set "the Sayids of Matiari" against him (p. 182,
  folio 173). **A documented instance of one shrine family's growth being resisted by another.**
- **A shrine village founded ex nihilo by its saint.** Bhit is "a village of his own", built on
  sandhills, "in the true ascetic spirit of the religious eremite", and he "helped to build the
  village with his own hands" (p. 182, folio 173). **Water infrastructure is part of the site
  description — "close to the Kirar lake which was fed by the Ali Ganj or Pahar canal.."** (p. 182,
  folio 173).
- **Textual production as a shrine institution.** The Risalo is "collated by scribes and formed into
  a regular collection which exists in several manuscript forms" (pp. 183–184, folios 174–175), the
  named amanuenses being "Tamar and Hashim" (p. 184, folio 175); Sorley's own manuscript access is
  recorded — "I have consulted a very;[text-layer?] inferior manuscript copy of the poems in the
  library of the India Office, London" (p. 184, folio 175, footnote 1).
- **Oral tradition as a dated, exhaustible resource.** "Until the eighties of last century it was
  still possible to trace oral tradition up to its source because old men were still alive who could
  recall what their grandfathers had told them and some at least of these grandfathers must have been
  contemporaries of the poet." "This source is now stopped. There is no likelihood of any further
  accretion of hard facts, though this will not prevent the oral tradition from developing further."
  (p. 179, folio 170) **A methodological statement with a date-window attached — the 1880s — which is
  also the decade of the 1882 wake account.**
- **The mosque at Sukkur as a dated structure.** "an inscribed stone dating from A.D. 952-3" in "the
  mosque of Khwajo Khizr at Sukkurthere[text-layer?]" (p. 178, folio 169).
- **Sectarian composition of Sind as the book states it.** "The majority of the population were
  followers of the Sunnat. But very influential sections held Shia tenets, though none of them
  embraced the thorough-going heterodoxy of the Persians." (p. 172) Against which the leaders "were
  nearly all Shias by persuasion" (p. 173, folio 164) — **the book's own paradox, stated as such:**
  "Though the religious leaders belonged largely to a school of religious thought at variance with
  the ideas of the greater part of the population, they did not thereby lose their position of
  predominance." (p. 173, folio 164)

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

All of these are reported by Sorley as traditions he disbelieves; the attributions below are his.

- **The blessing that produced a Kalhora heir.** "Indeed tradition likes to add that the birth of
  Ghulam Shah Kalh6ro[text-layer?] was the result of the blessing of Shah Abdul Latif." (p. 182,
  folio 173) **Attributed to "tradition"; Sorley marks it with "likes to add".**
- **The letter Alif.** "One typical story of this kind is that when he was being taught the alphabet
  he would not go beyond the letter Alif, which is the symbol of God and unity. The moral drawn from
  this is, of course, that from the days of his childhood he was engrossed by a vision of the divine
  unity." (p. 180, folio 171) Prefaced by "Many stories of the miraculous order are told of him. But
  no serious-minded person will attach any value to them."
- **The Risalo thrown into the Kirar lake and rewritten from memory.** "When Tamar and Hashim, the
  poet's favourite disciples and secretaries, brought him the complete Risa.Io[text-layer?], Shah
  Abdul Latif turned over the pages and remarked of the frequent laments of Sasui that the work was
  simply a safety valve for the peccant humours of the flesh and flung it suddenly into the Kirar
  lake. His secretaries expostulated with him and he allowed them to rewrite the whole from memory."
  (p. 184, folio 175, attributed to "Dayaram GidumaP[text-layer?]" and footnoted to "Dayaram Gidumal
  : Something about Sind by Sigma, p. 41."). Sorley: "it can be no more than a stupid magnification
  of some trivial incident. But it is more likely to be a legend intended to build up the tradition
  of the inspired delivery of the poems."
- **The curse that explains the poet's childlessness.** "' It is said that the Shah's wife while
  enceirite[text-layer?] longed one day for a certain kind of fish and ·her maid sent one of his
  followers to procure it. The Shah happened to enquire about this follower and learning the cause of
  his absence observed : ' When the embryo is such a trouble to my fakirs, what will the full blown
  adult be ? May such a blossom be nipped in the bud ! The child, we are told, in consequence was
  still-born to the great grief of the mother and the Shah left no heir surviving him. '"
  (pp. 184–185, folios 175–176, footnoted to "Dayaram Gidumal : Something about Sind by Sigma,
  p. 42."). Sorley: "Clearly the obj ect of this fairy tale is to account for the poet's
  childlessness." (p. 185, folio 176)
- **Khizr's immortality.** "Khizr, or Elias, was supposed to have drunk of the fountain of the waters
  of life and believed not to die till the last trump. Khwajo Khizr was for this reason called in
  Sind Zinda Pir (The Living Pir)." (p. 178, folio 169)
- **The inexhaustible flour of Dato Khuhawar's millstones.** "For we are told that God was so pleased
  with his piety and liberality that even if a handful of grain was thrown in the supply of flour was
  equal to all demands." (p. 177, folio 168, quoting James)
- **The tree taboo at the Shahdadpur pir's tomb.** "If a person breaks a branch of the tree which
  overshadows the tomb he is supposed to be doomed to meet with some mishap." (p. 177, folio 168)
- **The powers ascribed to saints in Sind, as a list.** "there were causing the birth of children,
  especially in cases of barrenness and old age ; curing complaints and diseases like madness,
  deafness, dumbness and blindness ; appearing in person at a distance to protect friends against
  unknown dangers ; exercising dominion over birds, beasts and fishes ; breaking through chains,
  fetters, doors and walls ; living without food, drink or sleep ; compellin�[text-layer?] inanimate
  objects to behave as if possessed of life and volition ; still ing[text-layer?] storms and changing
  female into male children. There was hardly a power in the long records of hagiology which could
  not be found credited to some sacred shrine or holy man in Sind." (p. 177, folio 168, footnote 1
  citing "Burton, pp. 229-30.") **Eleven distinct powers, none attached to a named saint or shrine.**
- **Khwajo Khizr as god of the Indus.** "He was the patron saint of waters and rivers and hence had
  come to be regarded as the god of the river Indus." (p. 178, folio 169)
- **The astrological founding of Tatta.** "their latest and greatest capital Tatta was founded on a
  lucky day settled by Brahmans and astrologers" (pp. 174–175, folios 165–166, quoting Elliot,
  III, 273).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The book's stated reason for existing, and for its first five chapters.** "No description of the
  Sind of Moghul and Kalhorn[text-layer?] days would be adequate without some account of the life of
  Shah Abdul Latif." (p. 172) And, more strongly: "The whole of the present work up to this point has
  been written solely for the purpose of making clear the kind of people amongst whom he lived and
  the historical background of their age. Without a deep historical understanding of these matters no
  one unfamiliar with Sind as a country and unacquainted with the Sindhi language can hQpe[text-layer?]
  to realize the depth of his genius or the meaning of his verse." (p. 178, folio 169) **This
  retroactively explains the economic and travel chapters of chunks 001–011.**
- **The problem the chapter is built to solve.** "how is it possible to reconcile the poet's lofty
  etherealized religion with the actual practice of the common folk ?" (p. 172) Sorley's framing
  device is Dean Inge: "' A religion as believed and practised cannot be far in advance of the mental
  and moral capacity of its adherents. A religion succeeds not because it is true but because it
  suits its worshippers. '" (p. 172)
- **His verdict on Sindhi Islam, stated flatly.** "In seventeenth and eighteenth century Sind a great
  gulf yawned between Islam's ideal and the practical working of Islamic . belief." (p. 172) And:
  "As regards the accretion of superstitious practice upon the plain austerity of Islam in Sind the
  evidence is overwhelming." (p. 176, folio 167) **The word he uses throughout is "debasement"** —
  "The debasement of Islam found in these and similar practices seems undoubtedly to have been due to
  the popular religion of the indigenous Sindhis, sometimes called Jats." (p. 178, folio 169)
- **The causal thesis: late mass conversion left Hindu substrate intact.** "These facts seem to
  establish the interesting conclusion that, while the earlier leading families of Sind were
  influenced by Shiaideas[text-layer?] and many of the saintly families continued to be so influenced
  for many centuries, when the mass Sunni conversions occurred they did so amongst a population deeply
  wedded to Hindu practices and Hindu superstitions. The result was twofold ; first to break down very
  considerably the clear-cut distinction between Sunni and Shia which elsewhere runs like a knife-edge
  through Muslim society : and second, to make it inevitable that Islam, as far as the common people
  were concerned, would be to a great extent influenced by Hindu pre­dilections." (p. 175, folio 166)
  **This is the chunk's central historical argument and it is explicitly an argument about why Sindhi
  shrine religion looks as it does.**
- **He declines to push the Hindu-substrate thesis onto pir veneration.** "Whether the exaggerated
  respect for the pir is due to the remains of Hindu influences of hero-worship cannot be predicated ;
  but it is possible that some such influence has been at work in Sind." (p. 175, folio 166)
  **Recorded because it is a limit the author sets on his own argument.**
- **Tomb veneration named as a distinct phenomenon.** "Another form that popular belief took was to
  carry to extremes what is little less than worship of the dead." (p. 175, folio 166)
- **The three named results of Sind's religious history.** "an almost incredible veneration for
  Sayids as such, a great accretion of superstitious practice in the belief of the multitude, and a
  common basis of under­standing, by Muslim and Hindu alike, of the mystical message of Islam as
  expounded in the teachings of the Sindhi Muslim mystics." (p. 175, folio 166)
- **Ethnographic generalisation about Sindhi character, offered as explanation.** "The Sindhi is by
  nature an easy-going being, not given to religious subtlety. This toleraht[text-layer?] trait in
  Sindhi character has produced a rare phenome­non." (p. 173, folio 164) **A period-bound claim; record
  as Sorley's.**
- **He states that the history of Islam in Sind has never been written.** "The history of Islam in
  Sind is extra­ordinarily complicated. It has not yet been adequately described in any book."
  (p. 172) And on the origins of saintly influence: "The facts are, however, very obscure." (p. 174,
  folio 165) **Two explicit admissions of an evidentiary gap.**
- **Sind as the seedbed of a Sufi school.** "The extent to which Sind attracted contemplative and
  religious-minded persons during the middle age of Islam is well known and is the foundation of its
  school of Sufi thought." (p. 173, folio 164) **The chunk names no order, no silsila and no
  khanqah** — **(not stated)**.
- **His method on oral tradition: apply the tests of evidence.** "Sayid-worship being however what it
  is, many of the items in the oral tradition must be treated with great caution. Many of them of the
  miraculous order must be ruled out altogether. They are merely part of the halo of a growing
  sainthood. Miracles of this kind are like· fairies. They cease to exist when people no longer
  believe in them." (p. 179, folio 170) On Mirza Kalich Beg's phrasing: "Many of the facts collected

  Shah Abdul Latif's life. Nor will there ever be." (p. 179, folio 170) **A claim the archive should
  quote when weighing any confident biography of the saint.**

  working of popular psychology in the c:r;eation[text-layer?] of an oral tradition." (p. 184,

- **The Risalo's compilation is compared to the Koran's.** "some will find it difficult to resist the
  belief that it was partly influenced by the story of the compilation of the Koran, which very likely
  was gathered together exactly in the manner that has been ascribed by hero worship to the compilation
  of the Risa.Io[text-layer?]." (p. 184, folio 175)
- **Against the "untaught genius" tradition.** "it is a typical conceit of hero worship to pretend
  that all knowledge came to him as a sort of special revelation"; "The mystical form of his poetry
  could never have been achieved without a deep and sympathetic understanding of the mystical
  development of Islam that came to India through the work of the great Persian poets." (p. 183,

- **The literary verdict.** "He is incompar­ably the greatest man whom Sind has yet produced in the
  realm of imaginative art." (p. 178, folio 169) "He has written poetry that deserves a wider public

  depreciated by the extravagances of ignorance and superstition." (p. 179, folio 170)
