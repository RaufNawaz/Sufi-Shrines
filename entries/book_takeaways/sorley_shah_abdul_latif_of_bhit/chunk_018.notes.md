## Practices, institutions, economy

**This chunk contains no shrine economy at all** — no endowment, no land, no revenue, no custom
farmed, no offering, no fair, no ʿurs, no custodian, no sajjada nashin, no visitor number, no
building and no dispute. What it does contain, and all it contains, is an account of **Sufism
becoming an institution**, which is recorded here because it is the framework the book will apply to
Sindhi shrine life in later chapters.

- **The orders, named as a category the book declines to describe.** Sufism "can be clearly
  distinguished from the many kinds of asceticism and other-worldliness which have emerged in the
  course of Islamic history, the latter chiefly in the orders of darwishes, eremites, and fakirs in a
  variety too compFcated[text-layer?] to receive attention here" (p. 284, folio 275). **Three terms —
  "darwishes", "eremites", "fakirs" — and an explicit refusal to enumerate the orders.** **No silsila
  is named anywhere in the chunk** — **(not stated)**.
- **Sufism's institutionalisation, stated as a process with an end state.** "It produced its orders
  of adherents, its darwishes and its saints, and showed all the outward signs of an institution of
  fixed character" (p. 287, folio 278); and, immediately before, "Sufism had in fact become swallowed
  up in the Islamic church, and, within Islam, made itself into a religious system of its own . It
  became both a school of thought and a system of practice and belief" (pp. 286–287, folios 277–278).
  **This is the chunk's single most useful sentence for the economics of religion: a devotional
  movement becoming a fixed institution that produces saints as an output.**
- **The mechanism of that absorption, named.** "This result was of course achieved through a system
  of interpretation of Sufi utterances by which it was shown that, however much the words of the
  Sufis might differ from the words of Muhammad, there was a deep resemblance in their real and
  ultimate meaning" (p. 286, folio 277). **Doctrinal reinterpretation as the price of institutional
  legitimacy.**
- **The trajectory from heterodoxy to orthodoxy, in one sentence.** "In this way the Sufis, who began
  as ascetics and quietists and later became heterodox theosophists at variance with the established
  dogma of their time, ended as orthodox Muslims teaching a deeper and subtler meaning in the words
  of the Koran" (p. 286, folio 277).
- **Asceticism as a practice with a purpose.** "To reach this perfection he will undergo cheerfully
  the rigours of asceticism. … Thus the grim ascetic and the contented saint are but stages in this
  quest for the ideal goodness" (p. 277, folio 268). **"Ascetic" and "saint" are presented as two
  stages of one career, not two kinds of person.**
- **The early Sufis as a protest movement against formalism.** "The early Suiis[text-layer?] were
  ascetics much influenced by the practice of Christian hermits, who in their turn were familiar with
  Neoplatonic ideas. There were many of these ascetics in Arabia before the days of Muhammad. After
  the establishment of Islam they carried their ideas on. The early Sufi�[text-layer?] were all
  ascetic and quietist. Their point of view was a sort of protest against the formalism of the new
  religion which placed great emphasis on external duties and enforced, with strict insistence, a
  rigid belief" (p. 285, folio 276). **"External duties" versus personal expression is the axis of the
  argument.**
- **Non-Muslim religious houses named as transmission channels.** "the abbot of a convent at Edessa"
  (p. 288, folio 279) and "Buddhist monasteries had existed in Balkh" (p. 288, folio 279). **Both
  presented as institutional vectors by which non-Islamic doctrine reached Sufism.**

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Sorley states the scope limit of the chapter at its first sentence.** "IT is no part of the
  object of this book to trace the development of mysticism, an immense subject on which a vast
  amount of erudite scholarship has been expended" (p. 284, folio 275). **He then traces it for eight
  pages** — the chapter is a compressed history of Sufism's foreign sources, leaning almost entirely
  on Nicholson and O'Leary.
- **The chapter's governing claim about the Risalo's parentage.** "The poetry of Shah Abdul Latif is
  an expression of the later Sufism in India, especially as influenced by the Muhammadan domination
  of Upper India from the fifteenth century onwards. Sufism is merely one of the forms in which
  mysticism has altered the simple religion of the Prophet" (p. 284, folio 275). **"Altered" is
  Sorley's word, and it is the same evaluative register as the "debasement" of chunk 012.**
- **Nicholson quoted against monocausal explanation.** "' No single cause will account for a
  phenomenon so widely spread and so diverse in its manifestation. Sufism has always been thoroughly
  eclectic, absorbing and transmuting whatever " broken lights " fell across its path and
  consequently it gained adherents amongst men of the most opposite views-theists and pantheists,
  Mu'tazilites and Scholastics, philosophers and divines. '" (p. 284, folio 275, footnote citing
  "Literary History of the A rabs[text-layer?], p. 390 .").
- **The four foreign sources, as Sorley adopts them from Nicholson.** "In the same work he has come
  to the conclusion that the ' four principal foreign sources of Sufism are undoubtedly Christianity,
  Neoplatonism Gnosticism, and Indian asceticism and religious philosophy '" (p. 284, folio 275).
  **Printed without a comma after "Neoplatonism", which makes the count of four ambiguous on the
  page — recorded as printed; see Doubts.**
- **The paradox the chapter is built on: the Koran is a bad seedbed for mysticism.** "No more
  unpromising field for the growth of mystic ideas could be imagined than the stern monotheism of the
  Koran. But , as Dean Inge has pointed out, mysticism is part of the raw material of every religion"
  (p. 284, folio 275); "But the Koran viewed as a whole is completely non-mystical. We cannot but
  agree with Mr North when he says : ' No one reading the Koran would lay much stress on such passages
  unless he were looking for the like of them.'" (p. 285, folio 276).
- **And the inversion Sorley draws from it — an argument by reaction.** "If indeed the Koran is
  responsible for the subsequent development of mysticism in Islam it is ·largely because its stern
  rejection of the kind of attitude in which mystics indulge made the intrusion of a more personal
  emotionalism sooner or later inevitable" (p. 285, folio 276). **A causal claim of exactly the shape
  he uses in chunk 012 about popular religion filling a gap.**
- **The Damascus-to-Baghdad thesis: Sufism's character changed with the empire's capital.** "The
  vital change occurred when the centre of the Islamic world was shifted from Damascus to Baghdad. By
  this change Arab influences became subordinated to Persian" (pp. 285–286, folios 276–277), evidenced
  by the Bowen quotation on the Abbasid court (p. 286, folio 277).
- **The two-fold consequence, stated as a numbered result.** "The result was in fact two-fold. In the
  first place the ascetic and quietist character of the early Sufis began to give place to a
  pantheistic tendency often expressed in sensuous and imaginative language which was utterly alien to
  the hard austerity of the Koran and th�[text-layer?] Traditions. In the second place the attack of
  heterodoxy on orthodoxy, in a land where the orthodox religion was itself alien, produced inevitably
  the labours of the scholastics culminating in the supreme achievement of Ghazzali" (p. 286,
  folio 277). **"In a land where the orthodox religion was itself alien" is the same structural
  argument Sorley applied to Sind in chunk 012.**
- **Hindu and Buddhist material enters Islam through Persia — the claim that licenses the book's
  later treatment of Sindhi syncretism.** "On the soil of Persia at this time Buddhist and Vedanta
  ideas flourished in some fashion. Thus were passed into Sufi thought some ofthose[text-layer?]
  beliefs and modes of expression which are characteristic of Hindu India" (p. 286, folio 277); and
  "In this strange way also from the religious experience of India there came into Islam elements
  quite alien to it. But this very mixing of opposites enabled mysticism in Islam to retain the appeal
  it has today amongst people brought up in an utterly different religious tradition" (p. 287,
  folio 278). **This is the chunk's explanation of the cross-community appeal chunk 012 recorded at
  Sindhi shrines, now given a doctrinal rather than a demographic cause.**
- **The two mysticisms inside Sufism, and what each connects to.** "It is the intellectual mysticism
  in Sufi thought that allies it with the mysticism of Christianity largely derived from Greek
  philosophy. It is the beauty mysticism, with its emphasis on self-abandonment in the divine and the
  sinking of individuality in the One, which allies it with the doctrines of Buddhist and Vedantic
  ideas so powerful in the religion of the Hindu peoples of India" (p. 287, folio 278). **A clean
  two-source model, and the sentence most likely to be cited from this chunk.**
- **Nicholson on Sufism as "spiritualized Islam" doing lip-service.** "Professor Nicholson believes
  that Sufism in its ascetic, moral and devotional aspects was a spiritualized Islam but it continued
  to do lip-service to the established religion. Thus Allah, the God of Mercy and Wrath, was in a
  certain sense depersonalized and worshipped as the One Absolutely Real (Al-Haqq)" (p. 287,
  folio 278).
- **Nicholson's periodisation, with the one hard date the chapter gives for a turn in Islamic
  thought.** "He distinguishes a period of rationalism and free thought in the Abbasid age from the
  period of orthod9x[text-layer?] reaction which commenced from A.n. 847[text-layer?]" (p. 287,
  folio 278). **"A.n." is the text layer's rendering of "A.D." — recorded, not repaired.**
- **Greek and Syrian, not Indian, as the immediate source — Nicholson quoted against the Indian
  hypothesis.** "' There may be ', says Nicholson, ' Indian elements in Neoplatonism and Gnosticism,
  but . . . . the immediate source of the Siifi[text-layer?] theosophy is to be sought in Greek and
  Syrian speculation. '" (p. 288, folio 279). **This sits in tension with the Buddhist/Vedanta claim
  at p. 286 (folio 277) and Sorley does not reconcile them — see Doubts.**
- **Merx's origin thesis.** "According to Merx the real mystical origin is traceable to Dionysius the-
  pseudo-Areopagite" (p. 288, folio 279), with the transmission route traced through Syriac
  translation to the Muslims (p. 288, folio 279).
- **O'Leary on the debt of the conquerors to the conquered.** "O'Leary has made very clear the debt
  which the Muslims owed to their subj ect[text-layer?] peoples. Most of the learned works of this
  time were written in Arabic. · But they were not the works of Arabs, who had by then lost the
  hegemony that was theirs under the Ummayids[text-layer?]" (p. 288, folio 279); and his conclusion,
  quoted: "' This ' , says O'Leary, ' is an extremely interesting illustration of the fusion of
  oriental and Hellenistic elements in Siifism[text-layer?] and shows that the theoretical doctrines
  of Siifism[text-layer?], whatever they may have borrowed from Persia and India, receive their
  interpretative hypostasis from neo-Platonism.'" (pp. 288–289, folios 279–280, footnote 1 citing
  "O'Leary : op. cit. , pp. 1 84-94[text-layer?].").
- **Underhill on Neoplatonism's appeal.** "Evelyn Underhill holds that Neoplatonism taught the
  illusory nature of all temporal things and in the violence of its idealism outdid its master Plato.
  It spoke of the existence of an Absolute God, the Unconditioned One, accessible in ecstasy and
  contemplation, and thus made a direct appeal to the mystical elements in man" (p. 289, folio 280).
- **The knowable/unknowable puzzle as a diagnosis of Sufi obscurity.** "Something of this puzzlement
  between the knowable and the unknowable of the Divine Nature runs all through the philosophy of the
  Siifis[text-layer?] and is the occasion for some of the most obscure utterances in their writings"
  (p. 289, folio 280). **Useful when the archive has to handle an unintelligible verse: Sorley's own
  position is that the obscurity is doctrinal, not textual.**
- **The eight doctrines of the Sufis, as Sorley summarises al-Kalabadhi (pp. 289–290, folios
  280–281).** Recorded in full because Sorley says of them "All these ideas will be found in the
  poetry of Shah Abdul Latif" (p. 290, folio 281), which makes this list the book's own key to the
  Risalo. **First**, the characteristics of God — "the Siifis[text-layer?] were agreed that God is
  One, Alone, Single, Eternal, Everlasting, Knowing, Powerful, Living, Hearing, Seeing, Strong,
  Mighty, Majestic, Great, Generous, Clement, Proud, Awful, Enduring, First, God, Lord, Ruler, Master,
  Merciful, Compassionate, Desirous, Speaking, Creating and Sustaining". **Second**, the Gnosis of
  God — "the only guide to God was God Himself and that the part to be played by human intelligence
  is that of an intelligent person in need of a guide". **Third**, Spirit — the
  `riih[text-layer?]`/`nafs`/`{rih)[text-layer?]` distinction quoted above. **Fourth**, Union —
  "union implied being inwardly separated from all but God, seeing inwardly-in the sense of
  veneration of none but God". **Fifth**, Love — "the utter difference between human love and the
  love of man for God : the first was a pleasure, the second an annihilation, in which astonishment,
  surrender and bewilderment play an important part"; glossed, "Thus love for the Siifis[text-layer?]
  is illumination, a piercing o f[text-layer?] the veil of illusory and temporary things".
  **Sixth**, Separation — "a deeply ethical and purifying content separating the mystic from carnal
  longings and from . the desire for pleasant and pleasurable things". **Seventh**, Revelation and
  Veiling — Sahl's three states, quoted above. **Eighth**, the Seeker and the Sought — "the
  Siifis[text-layer?] identified the Seeker with the Sought ' for the man who seeks God only seeks Him
  because God first sought him '".
