## Practices, institutions, economy

- **A shrine in active use, named and located.** Of Adam Shah Kalhoro: "His tomb today, perched on
  its rocky hill at Sukkur, is still a place of veneration and respect, and stands out prominently
  as an acropolis above the surrounding alluvial plain." (p. 37, printed 28). The chunk gives no
  building date, no custodian, no ʿurs and no visitor figures: **(not stated)**.
- **A second tomb, with a votive deposit described as still in place.** "Yar Mahomed
  `Kalh6ro`[text-layer?] directed a number of clubs to be suspended in front of his tomb as a
  memorial to his having conquered the country with clubs alone. The clubs remain suspended to this
  day in front of the tomb at Khudabad." (p. 37, printed 28, footnote 1, quoting "Report by Captain
  Preedy. Bombay Government Records, No. XVII, New Series, p. `67r`"[text-layer?]).
- **The economy of a saintly establishment outgrowing its offerings — the chunk's single most
  archive-relevant passage.** Postans, quoted: "Adam Shah's followers multiplied in great numbers
  and after his death his fame and influence were perpetuated through six generations of lineal
  descendants all regularly succeeding to the patriarchal chair. The great accumulation of adherents
  and attendants forced them to increase means of maintenance beyond the contributions of their
  disciples and they forcibly possessed themselves about the latter end of the seventeenth century
  of lands of different Zamindars." (p. 37, printed 28). Three institutions are named in one
  sentence: **hereditary succession to a "patriarchal chair"**, **disciples' contributions as the
  normal revenue**, and **seizure of zamindari land when those contributions fell short**.
- **Religious endowments confiscated and a genealogy manufactured.** The Kalhora "were originally
  Channo Sindhis and therefore converted Hindus. When the family rose to distinction it asserted a
  right to be called Beni Abbas, but their `Shajar6`[text-layer?] or genealogical tree was pronounced
  by the learned to be a complete failure. Upon this they sent a messenger to copy the documents in
  the possession of the holy men of `Sehrah Khatibah`… and then became undoubted descendants from
  Abbas and Murshids." (p. 34, printed 25, footnote 2, quoting Burton, *History of Sindh*, p. 410).
- **Sanctity as a source of political legitimacy in the countryside.** Kalhora popularity "was
  strengthened by the peculiar character of their reputation as holy men,
  `descen�dants`[text-layer?] of a sainted mendicant, a kind of being who has always made a very
  vivid appeal to the Muslim inhabitants of the `Lower. Indus`[text-layer?] valley" (p. 36, printed
  27). Compare "The rise of a family of religious mendicants to a throne of territorial dignity is
  one of those incidents in which oriental history is so rich" (p. 37, printed 28), and Postans'
  phrase for the tribe, "`sturdy saints`" (p. 37, printed 28).
- **Revenue as the organising purpose of the state.** The Moghul system in Sind rested on "the
  obedience of the local chiefs to the imperial governors and the due collection of the
  `imperiai.`[text-layer?] revenue" (p. 33, printed 24); "It was the weakness of the system that it
  provided no real check on illicit exactions by petty chiefs and by governors… The result of the
  weakness was a continual conflict between the revenue demands of local and imperial interests"
  (p. 33, printed 24); the empire was "maintained as a machine for collecting revenue for an
  `e�travagant`[text-layer?] court, and as a weapon for waging aggressive wars" (p. 34, printed 25).
  Moreland is quoted in the footnote: "in levies of all kinds whether imposed on classes or
  individuals, officials had to avoid such a scandal as might provoke interference from above, but…
  short of this limit they had very large opportunities of raising money" (p. 33, printed 24,
  quoting *From Akbar to Aurangzeb*, p. 295).
- **Tribute, arrears and evasion as the Kalhora's chronic condition.** They "were supposed to
  collect the revenue, remit what was necessary to Delhi, and maintain law and order" (p. 32,
  printed 23); after 1737 "They were required to pay an annual tribute and usually did their utmost
  to avoid doing so", and "the tribute was gradually reduced in amount and was usually very much in
  arrears" (p. 36, printed 27). Suzerainty passed from Delhi to "the Persian kingdom" after Nadir
  Shah, then "about 1747" to "the Pathan kingdom at Kabul" (p. 35, printed 26).
- **Administrative geography.** On annexation by Akbar "the country as a whole was made part of the
  `Siibah`[text-layer?] of Multan and sarkars were established in two places, Bakhar in the north
  and Tatta in the Indus deltaic `reiion`[text-layer?]", under separate governors (p. 32, printed
  23); "the fort of Bakhar, the strongest place of defence in Sind, did not come into the possession
  of the `Kalh6ra`[text-layer?] till 1736" (p. 38, printed 29); the Afghan "portion named Moghulee,
  comprising Sukkur, `Bukkar`[text-layer?] and `Shikarpiir`[text-layer?] and its dependencies was
  annexed to the Durani kingdom", extending "on the north-east to Kashmore" (p. 38, printed 29,
  footnote 2).
- **Irrigation named as the one competence of the dynasty.** "The Kalhora themselves showed no
  special genius for government except that they followed a consistent policy of determined
  isolation and recognized the importance of irrigation works." (p. 36, printed 27).
- **A shift in the urban and commercial map, dated to the later eighteenth century.** "the rise,
  towards the second half of the eighteenth century, of the four large towns of modern Sind,
  Karachi, Hyderabad, Sukkur and `Shikarpiir`[text-layer?], which displaced Tatta,
  `Nasarpiir`[text-layer?], Rohri, Sehwan, Kandiaro and a host of small `pfo.ces`[text-layer?] in the
  economic scheme" (p. 30, printed 21). Yar Muhammad "made `Shikarpiir`[text-layer?] his court and
  obtained from Aurangzeb a grant of the tract between the Indus and the Nara and the right to call
  himself Khuda Yar Khan" (p. 38, printed 29).
- **A closed country as deliberate policy.** "The aim of all Sind policy from 1701 onwards was to
  make Sind independent of Moghul, Persian and Pathan, to diminish the payment of tribute to the
  suzerain authority, to preserve the land as a closed terrain into which no foreigners of any sort
  were allowed entrance except with the utmost difficulty." (pp. 35–36, printed 26–27); the result
  was that Sind remained "a terra incognita" to Europeans despite two factories (p. 36, printed 27).
- **The East India Company factories and their records as economic evidence.** A factory "from 1636
  to 1662 and again from 1758 to 1775", the records "invaluable direct evidence of the condition of
  `t-he`[text-layer?] country, at least to the extent to which traders of the type sent out by the
  Company were interested enough and able to describe it", held "partly in the Bombay Record Office
  and partly in the library of the India Office" (p. 28, printed 19). "It was particularly the
  exactions of the local authorities which troubled the English traders who had come to Sind for
  commercial purposes only." (p. 33, printed 24).
- **War as a recurrent condition of the agrarian countryside**, in Lieutenant James's account of the
  `Chandookah`[text-layer?] pargana: "Chandookah in common with the rest of Sind has been the scene
  of many a bloody conflict, its fields of corn trampled under by the invading horde and its plains
  saturated with the blood of hundreds shed in civil strife" (p. 36, printed 27, quoting "Report on
  Pergunnah of Chandookah by Lieut. James, `1 847`", Bombay Government Records, Selections, No.
  XVII, New Series, pp. `70 9-74`[text-layer?]).
- **Three internal wars dated.** "The reign of the Kalhora was interrupted by three acute spasms of
  civil war, the first on the death of `Niir Muhammad Kalh6ro`[text-layer?] in 1754, the second on
  the deposition of `Sarfataz Khan Kalh6ro`[text-layer?] in `l7J5`[text-layer?], and the third and
  fatal internecine conflict that resulted in the supersession of `Kalh6ro`[text-layer?] by
  `Talpiir`[text-layer?]" (p. 36, printed 27); the Talpurs prevailed "with the aid of the
  `Baliichis`[text-layer?], then settled in Sind in considerable numbers" (p. 36, printed 27).
- **Ecclesiastical office inside the Moghul bureaucracy.** Vincent Smith, quoted: "The only
  considerable officials who did not take rank as army officers were charged with purely
  ecclesiastical and civil legal duties, such as the Sadars and the Kazis" (p. 33, printed 24); a
  governor "was not bound by any rules of either substantive law or procedure unless in so far as
  his conscience required him to follow the Koranic precepts" (p. 33, printed 24).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The miraculous club of the Kalhora**, in Lieutenant James's words as the book quotes them: "the
  `miraculous`1 club of the `Kalh6ra`[text-layer?] placed the descendants of mendicants on its
  throne" (p. 37, printed 28). The book's own footnote flattens it into a military anecdote — the
  Panhwar tribe was conquered "with such ease" that clubs were hung at the tomb (p. 37, printed 28).
- **Canonization by execution.** Of Adam Shah Kalhoro: the Governor of Multan "had him put to death.
  The result was to canonize him and make his name more potent than ever." (p. 37, printed 28).
  The book attributes this to no source; it is Sorley's own narration.
- **Miracle-material in the Persian chronicles, reported by the book only to be discounted.** Mir
  Muhammad Masum "has filled his story with reputed miracles of saints and holy men to such an
  extent as greatly to `depr eciate`[text-layer?] the value of his work for scientific historians"
  (p. 25, printed 16); the Tuhfat-al-Kiram's material "is so `j umbled`[text-layer?] up with items
  of uncritical credulity and stories of saints, miracle workers and holy men that its total value
  is small" (p. 25, printed 16). **No karamat is narrated in full anywhere in the chunk.**

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The chunk's governing thesis: the native sources cannot support the book's period.** The sources
  fall "into `five· classes`" — Sind chronicles; general Moghul histories; East India Company
  records "(a) 1635-62, (b) 1758-75"; European travellers' accounts; and the papers of the British
  occupation (p. 24, printed 15). Of the first class: "The native historians dealing especially with
  Sind are of little help for the times of Shah Abdul Latif" (p. 24, printed 15), and "The native
  historians and annalists taken as a whole must `th�refore`[text-layer?] be classed as thoroughly
  inadequate and unsatisfactory" (p. 27, printed 18).
- **Why they are inadequate: a class argument, made through Elliot.** "of domestic history we have
  in our Indian annalists absolutely nothing… By them society is never contemplated, either in its
  `convent.ional`[text-layer?] usages `<!>r`[text-layer?] recognized privileges… A fact, an anecdote,
  a speech, a remark which will illustrate the condition of the common people… is considered too
  insignificant to be suffered to intrude upon a relation which concerns only grandees and
  ministers, thrones and imperial powers." (p. 26, printed 17, citing "Elliot: Original Preface,
  pp. `xix"".xx`"[text-layer?]). Sorley adds in his own voice: "Most of the native annalists wrote as
  Court flatterers… They did not speculate on or describe the economic structure of the feudal
  society in which they lived and which they did not in fact understand." (p. 26, printed 17).
- **The defect is not peculiar to India** — the same neglect of "the details of economic and
  domestic history" is found in eighteenth-century England, argued from the preface to *Johnson's
  England*: "What is known as social history is nowadays generally found more attractive than
  political history, but it is a fallacy to suppose that it is easier." (p. 27, printed 18, citing
  "Johnson's England. Preface, p. vii. Edited by A. S. Turberville").
- **The method offered in place of direct evidence: inference across a sixty-year gap.** "there is
  no insuperable difficulty in filling the blanks which exist between 1699 and 1758" (p. 29, printed
  20); "it is not untrue to say that the nature of conditions prevailing between 1700 and 1758
  `can·`[text-layer?] be readily inferred from the previous and later evidence" (p. 30, printed 21);
  "I trust… that this work will show by the scientific use of the available material, broken and
  fragmentary though it be, how the breaks in the continuity of the record can be reasonably
  repaired" (p. 28, printed 19).
- **The continuity thesis that licenses the method.** "the main administrative structure of the
  government remained largely unaltered from the days of Shah Jahan to the days of Sir Charles
  Napier" (p. 30, printed 21); "the Sind of Sir Charles Napier's day… was not very different in
  essentials from the Sind of Ghulam Shah `Kalh6ro`… and the Sind of Ghulam Shah
  `Kalhorn`[text-layer?] in its turn did not differ, except in the slight improvements of an
  unprogressive age passing on in years, from the Sind of Akbar, Jahangir, Shah Jahan and Aurangzeb"
  (p. 31, printed 22). The social structure "was made static in a manner possible only by the
  isolation of Sind from the rest of India and the clearly intelligible policy of both
  `KalhOra`[text-layer?] and `Talpiirs`[text-layer?] to maintain Sind as an individual Muslim state
  holding little converse with the rest of the world" (p. 30, printed 21).
- **A revisionist defence of the pre-British governments.** "Much of the bitter controversy which
  that event `occasione()'`[text-layer?] has led to an undue disparagement of the character of the
  native governments which preceded the British occupation. It can be readily proved that many of
  the criticisms of Victorian writers are unjust and have their basis in an exaggerated form of the
  ethical, self-righteous superiority which characterized that part of the Victorian epoch" (p. 30,
  printed 21); Napier and Burton "both of whom attached excessive importance to the stupidities,
  cruelties and crudities which the Kalhora and the `Talpiirs`[text-layer?] had, for their own
  reasons, no particular desire to eradicate" (p. 31, printed 22). Against this, Vincent Smith's
  verdict is quoted at length and then qualified: "It is not a pretty nor an attractive picture. It
  will, however, be a mistake to judge its deficiencies by the standards of modern administration"
  (p. 34, printed 25).
- **The Kalhora as a gradual, not a revolutionary, dynasty.** "The `Kalhora`2 as a dynastic force
  rose very gradually to predominance. They did not disappear in a sudden debacle." (p. 35, printed
  26); five stages are then enumerated, beginning 1701 and ending with the Kalhora–Talpur war from
  1778 (pp. 35–36, printed 26–27). Their significance for Sorley is constitutional: "the importance
  of the `Kalh6ra`[text-layer?] is that from the time they became a powerful political force the
  system was changed and the representative of the Emperor was a native of Sind" (p. 32, printed 23).
- **Unification of Sind is denied throughout.** "Upper and Lower Sind were never definitely united
  till a much later day… even `iill`[text-layer?] the end of the `Talpiir`[text-layer?] rule in 1843
  Sind may be held to have been very doubtfully unified" (p. 31, printed 22); repeated at p. 32
  (printed 23). The three-way division of authority among the Amirs "has always impressed historians
  as an unusual and noteworthy achievement" (pp. 31–32, printed 22–23).
- **There is no history of the dynasty.** "There is no adequate history of the `KalhOra`. The best
  account of them was in 1799 written by Nathan Crowe… Postans writing in 1843 took over, almost
  verbatim and without acknowledgement, much of what Crowe had written" (p. 35, printed 26).

## Doubts

- **The book appears to use two titles for one chronicle without saying so.** The list on p. 24
  (printed 15) names "the `Tarikh-i-Masiimi`" as one of the five; the very next sentence discusses

  said to be indebted to "the `Tarikh-i-Sind` and the `T arikh-i-Tahiri`" (p. 25, printed 16).
  **The identification is not made in the chunk**; a human should confirm it before either title is
  cited.
- **"`Sayid Ali Sher Kania` of Tatta" (p. 25, printed 16)** is given as the author of the
  Tuhfat-al-Kiram. The surname is carried as printed. Given fault 4 above it may be damaged; the
  chunk offers no second occurrence to check it against.
- **The fort is spelled two ways.** Sorley's text has "Bakhar" throughout (pp. 24, 32, 38); the
  Bombay Government record he quotes at p. 38 (printed 29) has "`Bukkar`" alongside "Sukkur". Kept

- **Sehwan is named, the shrine is not.** "Sehwan" appears once, in a list of towns displaced
  economically by Karachi, Hyderabad, Sukkur and Shikarpur (p. 30, printed 21). **This supports
  nothing about the archive row `lal-shahbaz-qalandar`** and has deliberately not been mapped to it.
- **"Mahars" is not Allo Mahar.** The chunk's "numerous tribe of Mahars then powerful in Upper Sind"
  (p. 38, printed 29) is a tribe of the Indus valley connected to the ruling house of Bahawalpur. It
  must not be mapped to the archive row `allo-mahar` (Allo Mahar, Sialkot), which the chunk never
  mentions.
- **Adam Shah Kalhoro's tomb at Sukkur has no archive row.** Of the Sukkur-area rows in the index
  (`gurdwara-dash-mesh-pita`, `gurdwara-pehli-patshahi-jind-pir-sukkur`, `sadh-belo`,
  `kalka-cave-temple-asthan-of-kalka-devi`, `sant-baba-asudaram-darbar-panno-aqil`) **none is this
  tomb**, and it is recorded above as a site not in the archive. If the archive ever adds it, pp.
  37–38 (printed 28–29) are the passage.
- **The chunk is incomplete at both ends.** It opens mid-sentence on p. 24 (printed 15) — the
  subject of "which forms the chief evidence" is in chunk 001 — and stops mid-footnote on p. 38
  (printed 29), the quoted Bombay record breaking off at "The Afghan possessions in Sind extended on
  the north-east to Kashmore on the". Footnote 2 of p. 38 is therefore **truncated**, and its
  closing reference is not in this chunk.
- **A stray `2` sits at the foot of p. 26 (printed 17)**, below the footnote rule. It is read here
  as a printer's gathering signature, not a page or note number, but **the chunk does not say so**.
