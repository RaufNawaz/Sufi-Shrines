## Arguments and interpretations

Sorley argues continuously in this chunk and his 1940 Indian Civil Service position is on the surface
of most of it. Everything below is recorded as his claim, not as fact.

- **The chunk's opening thesis: Company profit was not extracted at the producers' expense.** "It is
  utterly `fallacio�s[text-layer?]` to assert that the profits made by the East India Company in its
  commercial dealings with India in the seventeenth and. eighteenth centuries were to the
  disadvantage of the Indian producers. To suggest such a conclusion is to depreciate too much the
  intelligence of the Moghuls or the Kalhora. The government of the day was very much alive to its
  own interest and permitted only what it considered advantageous to itself. Certainly no part of the
  policy was to impoverish the people of the land in order to enrich foreign traders. These foreign
  traders never had things as they wanted them to be, as the history of the two factories in Sind
  proves conclusively." (p. 110, folio 101) **This is the interpretive spine of the section and a
  reviewer should treat it as Sorley's position, argued from the factories' difficulties.**
- **The three agencies that kept Indian industry alive, on Sarkar's authority.** "A judicious and
  impartial Indian historian has in fact definitely asserted, and `substantiate�[text-layer?]` the
  assertion by quotation from contemporary evidence, that throughout the seventeenth and eighteenth
  centuries `l:ridian[text-layer?]` industries were kept alive and developed by three chief agencies,
  the Emperor of Delhi, the nobility of the time, and the export traders." (p. 110, folio 101)
  Sorley adds: "the wealth of the Delhi Court and of the feudal 'aristocracy which ruled the land was
  one of the main incentives to Indian industrial activity" (p. 110, folio 101), and Sarkar's own
  sentence: "' The foreign traders were, however … the chief cause of the expansion of our
  manufactures, especially of articles of ordinary use as distinct from superfine articles of luxury
  and rare art products.'" (p. 111, folio 102)
- **The Company as a hybrid of official and private enterprise.** "in the days when the East India
  Company was purely a commercial and trading company, the official business was much supplemented by
  the private transactions of the factors and that these private transactions were carried out in a
  sort of `co-operation[text-layer?]` with the Indian moneyed traders who shared in the profits."
  (p. 112, folio 103)
- **Sorley declines to pursue the theme.** "It is no object of this book to enlarge on this theme.
  `Carefol[text-layer?]` students of the Moghul regime are fully aware of the facts" (p. 110,
  folio 101); and again, "It is not relevant to the purpose of this book to trace the subsequent fate
  of the weavers of England" (p. 115, folio 106). **Both are explicit limits on the book's evidence.**
- **The decline of Sind's trade is attributed to impersonal forces, and the Sind dynasties are
  exonerated by name.** "By 1770 India's commanding lead in cotton piecegoods had disappeared for
  ever, entirely because of the force of economic circumstances outside the control of anyone. …The
  decline of Sind's trade from 1775 onwards was not due to the incapacity of the `Kalhorn
  [text-layer?]` or the narrow parochialism `·of[text-layer?]` the `Talpiirs[text-layer?]`. Nothing
  the `Kalhorn[text-layer?]` or `Talpiirs[text-layer?]` could have done would have stopped
  effectively a decline due to circumstances beyond their control." (p. 116, folio 107) He credits the
  argument to Burnes and to Heddle (pp. 116–117, folios 107–108).
- **But the same pages make the later Kalhora the agents of Sind's closure.** "there arose the
  circumstances which the later `Kalhorn[text-layer?]` exploited to turn Sind into a self-contained
  Muslim state closed for nearly three-quarters of a century to Englishmen" (p. 116, folio 107).
  **Exploitation of circumstance and helplessness before circumstance sit in the same paragraph
  without being reconciled.**
- **Kalhora naval foresight praised over Mughal blindness.** Ghulam Shah "In his appreciation of the
  strength of the Company at sea … was ahead of the Moghuls who never, till the end, and when it was
  too late, realized the importance of sea power in India." (p. 116, folio 107)
- **Against Lane-Poole: the poor are the proper subject, and economics is why.** "These words are
  only partially true, and to some extent are definitely misleading. Not only are the lives of the
  poor anywhere not lacking in opportunity, but, were they indescribably trite and monotonous, the
  novels of writers like Gissing and Arnold Bennett could never have won popular appeal. The fact is
  that the modern mind is more interested in the fate and doings of the ordinary man than in the
  lives of princes and nobles. **The study of economics has altered the mental outlook of all who
  think about serious things.**" (p. 118, folio 109) **This is the book's methodological statement
  and the clearest explanation of why a book about a poet spends its first two chapters on trade.**
- **Against Harcourt Butler: famine is not the Indian norm, and is not the Sind norm at all.** See
  the passage quoted in full under Practices (p. 118, folio 109). Sorley calls the famine
  generalisation "a half truth grossly misleading".
- **Against Bernier: his verdict on tillage and canals is declared untrue of Sind.** "' The ground is
  seldom tilled except `unde:r[text-layer?]` compulsion and ·no person is willing or able to repair
  the .ditches and canals,' is untrue of `Silld[text-layer?]`, which could not have supported
  `frs[text-layer?]` population unless the ditches and canals ' `h.ad[text-layer?]` been kept
  `iri[text-layer?]` order." (p. 122, folio 113)
- **Against the nineteenth-century travellers as a class: a standards argument.** "how can this
  delightful picture of bounty, luxury and ease be reconciled with the sad accounts of a
  poverty-stricken land given in the nineteenth century by foreign travellers ? The explanation is
  simple. The standards by which comfort and elegance were judged were very different in 1840 from
  what they had been in 1640 and 1740. Most of the European commentators were too ignorant of the
  spirit of the East to distinguish the vital difference between its mode of `civiliza­tion
  [text-layer?]` and that of a Western world pulsating with the reforming ideals stimulated by the
  prickings of a conscience unnerved by the unfortunate results of an uncontrolled
  `Ihdustrial[text-layer?]` Revolution." (p. 120, folio 111)
- **Burton singled out, on religion.** "It was futile to expect a man of Burton's temperament to be
  anything but critical and supercilious of what he saw in Sind. **He despised Sind for what he
  considered its lax Muhammadanism.** He reviled the harshness of a Moghul type of Government which
  had succeeded in persisting into the middle of the nineteenth century." (p. 120, folio 111)
  **This is the only religious characterisation of Sind anywhere in the chunk**, it is reported as
  Burton's view, and Sorley reports it in order to dismiss the temperament behind it — he does not
  say whether he agrees that Sindhi Islam was lax.
- **The reformers named and grouped.** "Pottinger, Del Hoste, . Postans were all of the same school
  of thought. They were eager Western reformers anxious to change anything they `con­sidered
  [text-layer?]` bad by the standards of an England charged with a civilizing mission. It is not fair
  to gauge an Oriental land by such ethical measuring rods. Only a few sagacious fair-minded
  observers like Heddle and Burnes offered a slightly protesting voice against the chorus of
  disapproval." (p. 120, folio 111)
- **The language argument.** "A language is usually a key to a people. The Sindhi language is a
  beautiful instrument of precision admirably adapted to the civilization for which it was
  intended-a peace-loving agricultural and pastoral culture elaborated by an industrious and
  non-martial people." (p. 120, folio 111) **An ethnographic generalisation about Sindhis as
  "non-martial", offered without evidence.**
- **The verdict on British rule, ambiguous and in Latin.** "Gone are the casual autocracy of the
  `Kalhorn[text-layer?]`, and the capricious laissez-faire of the `Talpiir[text-layer?]` rule. In
  their place has arisen a strange new standard of absolute right and even-handed justice enforced by
  strangers in a strange land. Arabia Felix was verily to be absorbed in this new Roman empire of
  Victorian pattern till every cultivator and shopkeeper should shout with `j oy[text-layer?]`
  ' Civis Romanus `Suro[text-layer?]` and learn new `idea:s[text-layer?]` of justice and equality
  before the law." (pp. 120–121, folios 111–112) **The closing clause is damaged and its tone —
  celebratory or ironic — cannot be settled from the text layer; see Doubts.**
- **The scope disclaimer for Chapter III.** "Since this `bo0k[text-layer?]` does not profess to deal
  exhaustively with social life in the seventeenth or eighteenth centuries in Sind I propose to
  describe briefly merely some selected aspects of the economic conditions in which the people
  lived." (p. 121, folio 112)
- **An evidentiary admission that governs the whole of Chapter III.** "**We have no accounts of
  `Kalhorn[text-layer?]` days. The evidence mostly dates from the time of the `Talpiirs
  [text-layer?]`.** As the statements of these nineteenth century travellers do not differ much in
  general effect from the evidence of Moghul days and accord very much with present-day conditions .
  in agricultural and pastoral areas in Sind, there is no reason to disbelieve them." (p. 123,
  folio 114) **Sorley is reasoning about the poet's century from nineteenth-century and present-day
  observation, and says so.** A reviewer should carry this warning onto every eighteenth-century
  social claim in this chapter.
- **Baluch influence blamed for architectural decline.** "It seems likely that the `Baliich

  standard of building" (p. 124, folio 115).
