# sorley_shah_abdul_latif_of_bhit — chunk 023 — pp. 400–426

**This chunk is neither continuous prose nor bibliography, index or glossary. It is the book's
section of English verse translations from the Risalo**, arranged as numbered story-cycles, each
opened by a short prose headnote captioned `The Story` and then set out in roman-numeralled verse
sections. No `## Bibliography and apparatus` heading is therefore used: **the chunk contains no
source list, no footnotes, no citations and no authorities of any kind** — it is the only chunk so
far in which Sorley cites nobody. The structural record that would otherwise go under that heading
is given in the opening lines here instead, and the chunk's one genuinely bibliographic feature —
the printer's signature marks — is recorded under Doubts.

**Four cycles begin inside the chunk and a fifth ends inside it.** The chunk opens mid-way through
the Sasui and Punhun cycle, whose last page is p. 400 (folio 391). Then, as printed:
`II.   SUHINJ[text-layer?] AND MEHAR` (p. 401, folio 392), `III.   MoMuL[text-layer?] AND
Rl\No[text-layer?]` (p. 411, no printed number), `IV.   LiLAN[text-layer?] AND CHANESAR` (p. 416, no
printed number) and `v.   M.ARUI[text-layer?] AND UMAR` (p. 422, no printed number). **The Sasui
cycle is therefore numbered I, though its number falls before this chunk begins** — the sequence
II–V here brackets it and is not itself an inference, since the printed numerals II, III, IV and v
are on the page. The internal verse sections are numbered in roman within each cycle: Suhini runs
`I` to `x` (pp. 401–410), Momul `I` to `IV` (pp. 411–415), Lilan `I` to `VI` (pp. 416–421) and Marui
`I` to `VI` (pp. 422–426), the last breaking off at the chunk's end with section `VI` complete.

**The −9 offset holds, unbroken, across the whole chunk.** Verified page by page on every page that
prints a number: PDF p. 400 → printed `391`; p. 401 → `392`; p. 402 → `393`; p. 403 → `394`; p. 404
→ `395`; p. 405 → `39 6` (396, space-broken); p. 406 → `397`; p. 407 → `398`; p. 408 → `399`;
p. 409 → `400`; p. 410 → `401`; p. 412 → `403`; p. 413 → `4 04` (404); p. 414 → `405`; p. 415 →
`406`; p. 417 → `408`; p. 418 → `40 9` (409); p. 419 → `4ro[text-layer?]` (410); p. 420 →
`4n[text-layer?]` (411); p. 421 → `4 12` (412); p. 423 → `4 r4[text-layer?]` (414); p. 424 → `4 15`
(415); p. 425 → `4 16` (416); p. 426 → `41 7` (417). **Three pages in this chunk print no number —
pp. 411, 416 and 422, all three of them cycle-opening pages — and each is cited by PDF index only;
no folio has been predicted for any of them.**

**A finding about the drop-folio convention that the earlier chunks' rule does not cover.** p. 401
is also a cycle-opening page (`II.   SUHINJ[text-layer?] AND MEHAR`) and it *does* carry its folio,
`392`, at the foot of the page, after the verse. **pp. 411, 416 and 422 are the same kind of page
and carry nothing.** So in this part of the book the drop folio at the foot of a section opening is
present on one such page and absent on three, and the absence cannot be treated as a scanning
artefact of one page alone. Flagged under Doubts for a check against the PDF.

**The running head is `SHAH ABDUL LATIF OF BHIT` and the text layer destroys it on six of the
twenty-seven pages.** `BRIT` (or `B R I T`) for `BHIT` at pp. 403, 406, 413 and 418; and at p. 409 a
form not previously recorded in this book, **`BHTT`**. The head also loses the capital in `LATIF`
on four pages, printing `LAT i F` (pp. 406, 413, 417) and `L A T i :F`[text-layer?] (p. 419). A full
page-by-page head inventory is under Doubts.

**On "sur": the word does not occur anywhere in this chunk.** The cycles are titled by their story
("SUHINJ[text-layer?] AND MEHAR", "MoMuL[text-layer?] AND Rl\No[text-layer?]", "LiLAN[text-layer?]
AND CHANESAR", "M.ARUI[text-layer?] AND UMAR"), never by a sur name, so this book's standing
sur-ambiguity (Risalo section vs. chapter of the Koran) is **not triggered in this chunk at all**.
Chunk 016 gave a sur title for one of these cycles, `Sur Lilan Chanesar` (p. 243, folio 234); **this
chunk does not repeat that title and does not name the sur of any of the four cycles** — **(not
stated)**.

**No translator is named for any verse in this chunk.** There is no translator's note, no
attribution line, no footnote and no acknowledgement on any of the twenty-seven pages. The standing
finding of the book holds and is at its strongest here, in the chunk that consists almost entirely
of translated verse: **the English renderings of Shah Abdul Latif are unattributed** — **(not
stated)**.

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **bhit-bhit-shah** (the book names him in the running head on every page of the chunk as "SHAH
  ABDUL LATIF OF BHIT", and inside the verse as "the Sayid", "Latif" and "Latif the Poet"):
  **the whole of this chunk is his poetry in English translation, and it is the largest continuous
  block of his actual verse anywhere in the transcribed book so far — twenty-seven pages of it
  against the scattered extracts of chunks 012 and 016.** Four story-cycles are given complete
  within the chunk (Suhini and Mehar, Momul and Rano, Lilan and Chanesar, Marui and Umar) and a
  fifth (Sasui and Punhun) is completed at p. 400 (folio 391).
- **bhit-bhit-shah**: **the poet's three self-namings inside his own verse, all in the third
  person.** As `Sayid.`, used as a speaker label at the head of his own stanzas throughout —
  "Sayid." (p. 400, folio 391), and again at pp. 403, 405, 406, 408, 410, 417, 418, 419, 420, 421,
  423 (folios 394, 396, 397, 399, 401, 408, 409, 410, 411, 412, 414). As a third-person tag inside
  the line: "Her lot was not, the Sayid says, / In happy joys to pass her days" (p. 400, folio 391);
  "Love, thus the Sayid says, undid her utterly" (p. 406, folio 397); "Be not, the Sayid says,
  remiss" (p. 414, folio 405); "With turn of cheek, the Sayid says" (p. 414, folio 405); "I shall
  speak (Sayid saith) the true thoughts of mine heart" (p. 424, folio 415). And as "Latif": "The
  Giver gave it, says Latif" (p. 414, folio 405); "Rise, says Latif, search, use them well" (p. 415,
  folio 406); "See (saith Latif) the virtue. that resides in reeds" (p. 408, folio 399); "for he'll
  not (saith Latif) / Make any delay when he comes" (p. 423, folio 414); "When hot wind touched her
  (saith Latif) / Her camphor-scents of gladness fled" (p. 425, folio 416). **The fullest form is at
  p. 405 (folio 396): "Thus hath Latif the Poet spoken in his verse."** **The verse calls him "the
  Sayid"; the chunk nowhere glosses that word, states a descent, or names a lineage** — **(not
  stated)**, and nothing should be inferred from it here.
- **bhit-bhit-shah**: **the primordial covenant — the Koranic "Am I not your Lord?" — appears twice,
  in two different cycles, and is the chunk's central theological motif.** In the Suhini cycle,
  section VI in full: "Time was when God, the One and the Eternal, spake / Unto the souls and
  thundered : ' Am I not your Lord ? ' / Then, even then, to Suhini had there come a love / And
  longing for the neatherd. 'Twas of God's own will / That might of waters broke her earthen pot in
  twain. / What fate God's will had fashioned for her there indeed / She brought to due fulfilment
  in this world below." (p. 406, folio 397). In the Marui cycle, section I: "When there fell on mine
  earsthe[text-layer?] Word, / ' Am I not then your Lord ? ' / And with ' Yes ' my heart gave
  assent, / It was then that my promise I made / With the folk in the hedges pent." (p. 422).
  **The chunk gives no sura number, no Arabic, no citation and no commentary for either occurrence**
  — **(not stated)**. **This is the doctrinal hinge by which the folk romances are made religious:
  the heroine's love for the herdsman is placed at the moment of the covenant, before the world.**
- **bhit-bhit-shah**: **the Suhini and Mehar cycle, prose headnote in full as printed** — the
  chunk's most complete single statement of a Risalo story. "Suhini was the daughter of a well-to-do
  potter who lived by the bank of the river. Izzat Beg, the son of a rich Moghul merchant, chanced
  to come that way and was struck by the beauty of Suhini. He fell in love with her at sight. Every
  day he came to purchase pots just for the hope of meeting Suhini who returned his love. Spending
  all his money buying pots, lzzat[text-layer?] Beg became penniless and asked to be employed by
  Suhini's father. He was engaged as a cattleman to look after the potter's buffaloes. He then
  changed his name from Izzat Beg to Mehar, or The Herdsman." (p. 401, folio 392). Continued: "But
  this did not please Suhim's[text-layer?] parents, who forbade further meetings of the lovers. To
  clinch matters they married Suhini to another potter's son named Dam. They drove Mehar away." And:
  "Every night Suhini crossed the river on a baked earthenware pot. Her parents remonstrated with
  her and in order to dissuade her from furthh[text-layer?] meetings with Mehar they took away the
  baked pot and substituted for it an unbaked one." (p. 401, folio 392). The end: "Mehar who had
  heard her screams when the pot collapsed, rushed into the river to rescue her, but was drowned
  like the girl he loved. Mehar is called fondly " Sahir " (The Helper) by Suhini." (p. 401,
  folio 392). **"Sahir", glossed by the book itself as "(The Helper)", is the epithet on which the
  whole cycle's religious reading turns — see the next bullets.** **No river is named, no date is
  given, no shrine or tomb is attached to Suhini, to Mehar or to any place in the cycle** — **(not
  stated)**.
- **bhit-bhit-shah**: **"Sahir" is used simultaneously as the herdsman's pet name and as a name of
  God, and the chunk never separates the two.** "Upon the river's border women stand and cry, / '
  Oh, Sahir, Sahir.' While the thoughts of some of them / Are on a private grief concentred, others
  say / . ' We take no reek of life ' and plunge within the flood. / Sahir indeed is theirs who
  risked and entered in." (p. 401, folio 392). "My body, Sahir, is too frail to counter them /
  Without thou help me. Come to me within the stream, / 0 thou, who art for me the lord most
  merciful." (p. 402, folio 393). "Sahir, take thou them / Who have no skill of swimming, to that
  farther shore." (p. 403, folio 394). "These women ne'er will perish who take Sahir's word."
  (p. 408, folio 399). "Sabir[text-layer?] bound the knot that binds my life to me; / May not that
  knot of Sahir's that is life be IOosed[text-layer?]. / 0 God who Hearest, make me
  Sabir[text-layer?] meet, that then / The knot of life may in fulfilment loosened be." (p. 406,
  folio 397). **"the lord most merciful", "0 God who Hearest" and "Sahir" are addressed as one
  figure in these lines.** **Note the text layer alternates "Sahir" and "Sabir" for the same word —
  see Doubts.**
- **bhit-bhit-shah**: **the "purity" line, which is the strongest doctrinal statement of the Suhini
  cycle and is phrased in the language of ritual cleanness.** "Away from Sabir[text-layer?] Suhini
  is a thing unclean. / But by the side of him who drives the horned kine / To purity she riseth.
  She doth droop and fade / From Sahir severed. Fever hath set in upon / Her girlish beauty. Pity
  for her wretched state ! / She, maimed and unlovely in her illness now, / Passeth time sighing, by
  affliction held away / From Sabir[text-layer?]. By the neatherd health abides. Near Dam, / Her
  husband, she doth nought but illness know." (p. 410, folio 401). Followed immediately by "A sight
  of Sahir's physic for the beauteous girl. / His face if she but see, she surely will get well."
  (p. 410, folio 401). **Separation from the beloved is stated as uncleanness and as illness, and
  the sight of him as medicine — the chunk's one use of a healing idiom.**
- **bhit-bhit-shah**: **faith as a raft — the cycle's governing image, stated three times over.** "'
  Of Allah's mercy do thou not despair ', thus runs / Jhe[text-layer?] saying. Make of it thy raft on
  which to fl.oat[text-layer?]. / Then, with the j oy[text-layer?] that fills the hearts of those who
  love / The Lord, thou mayst behold the herdsman's countenance." (p. 402, folio 393). "With help of
  Allah then make thou of faith thy raft. / These women ne'er will perish who take Sahir's word. /
  Wise men do clutch at bushes when they're like to drown. / See (saith Latif) the virtue. that
  resides in reeds : / Perchance they bring thee safely to the bank : perchance / They break and
  take thee with them down amidst the flood." (p. 408, folio 399). Against them, the counter-image:
  "Come not across of .thy self-ferrying : forget / The ways of safety, Suhini. Love himself will
  take / Thee o'er the rough and tumble of the troubled waves. / They quickly cross deep waters who
  have love to do / The piloting. Come not across self-ferrying. / Set forth without conveyance ;
  dash the unbaked jar / To earth in fragments. Take love's yearning on the deep. / The herdsman
  seeks for news of them who seek for him. / For them a raft's a burden who have boundless love."
  (p. 402, folio 393). **"' Of Allah's mercy do thou not despair '" is introduced as a quotation
  ("thus runs Jhe[text-layer?] saying") but is given no source, no sura and no attribution** —
  **(not stated)**.
- **bhit-bhit-shah**: **the mystical-knowledge quatrain of the Suhini cycle, in which the folk tale
  is turned into a lesson in the Law.** "Learn well the lesson, Suhini, of the hidden Law, / How by
  the mystic way the Truth of Justice speeds. / True knowledge is in sooth the j oy[text-layer?] to
  them who love." (p. 403, folio 394). **"the hidden Law", "the mystic way", "the Truth of Justice"
  and "True knowledge" in three lines; no Arabic or Persian term is printed behind any of them** —
  **(not stated)**.
- **bhit-bhit-shah**: **the death-as-gain doctrine, stated flatly.** "Death was her mankind's fate.
  But she who drowned in death / Did by her drowning win a twofold recompense." (p. 406, folio 397).
  And: "While she did live she would not wearied take her rest ; / But entered earth's bosom longing
  for her friends. / She sat not silent while life pulsed her limbs, but dead / She drifted on the
  waves towards the herdsman, dead." (p. 410, folio 401). And the closing of the cycle: "It was the
  ocean deep, / No shallow creeklet drowned her, nor the swampy marsh. / Love for the neatherd
  filled her eyes in death indeed." (p. 410, folio 401).
- **bhit-bhit-shah**: **the buffalo-bell poem, given here in full — and it is the poem chunk 016
  described but could not name.** Suhini section IV and its continuation: "0 sisters ! how the
  tinkling bell / Has set my limbs to sprightly dance. / To stranger-folk how may I tell / The love
  that doth my heart entrance ?" (p. 404, folio 395); "At midnight's hour I did uprise / That I might
  my beloved see. / I slept, but shook sleep from mine eyes / When the bell tinklings came to me."
  (p. 404, folio 395); "I slept and heard the sounds of bells / That tinkled on the farther strand. /
  They moved my heart. Delight now dwells / Within me for this gladsome land." (p. 405, folio 396);
  "I ,slept[text-layer?] and heard the sound of bells / Vibrating on the farther shore. , / My heart
  the herdsman's message tells / And sets my nerves a-tingle more." (p. 405, folio 396). **Chunk 016
  (p. 253, folio 244) described, without a title, a poem "describing the feelings of abandonment
  felt by the woman separated from her lover on the distant bank of the river she will never cross,
  and hearing the sounding of the tinkling buffalo bells that comes from the further shore". This is
  that poem, and it belongs to the Suhini and Mehar cycle.** A concrete cross-chunk identification
  for the consolidator, and the first time an untitled poem from the literary chapters has been
  matched to a cycle.
- **bhit-bhit-shah**: **the Momul and Rano cycle, prose headnote as printed.** "Momul was the eldest
  daughter of a rich merchant living in a palace near the lake of Kak. Her fame and wealth had won
  her many suitors. But most of these came to untimely ends. Un­deterred[text-layer?] by this, Rano,

  sisters in clothes like Rana's[text-layer?] and made her sleep with her on the same couch."; "Ran6
  in utter disgust went away at once leaving his stick by the side of the couch so that Momul would
  recognize it." (p. 411). **No shrine, tomb or observance is attached to Momul, to Rano, to Kak or
  to Umarkot anywhere in this cycle** — **(not stated)**.
- **bhit-bhit-shah**: **the caste-indifference couplet — the single line in this chunk with the most
  direct bearing on the archive's cross-community question.** "There came fresh message yester night
  / From Rano. Won is our behest. / The Giver gave it, says Latif. / Of comer's[text-layer?] caste
  why,make[text-layer?] inquest ? / Who came in com!ng[text-layer?] welcomed were." (p. 414,
  folio 405). **"Of comer's[text-layer?] caste why,make[text-layer?] inquest ?" is put in the poet's

- **bhit-bhit-shah**: **the star-clock stanza of the Momul cycle, which times a night of waiting by
  named constellations.** "I stood : the constellations rose, / And all the constellations set. /
  The camel and the Mindhro prince / Did all night long my memory fret. / Adown my cheeks the tears I
  shed / Till branch in branch of sunlight met." (p. 412, folio 403); and "There waned the Pleiades

  folio 403). **"the Pleiades" and "The three stars in Orion's belt" are the only named stars in the

- **bhit-bhit-shah**: **Momul's self-sacrifice catalogue, the chunk's densest run of offering
  language.** "I'd sacrifice mine house and everything, / Nay more, my life's existence, just for
  him. / Unless my Rano in the kingdom is, / I want no wealth. I beg the Sodha's love / From him. If
  he but come within mine house / To be my guest, I'd take whate'er I know / And cast it out upon
  the fire to burn. / I'd take my pride and throw it in the oven. / I'd sacrifice myself, with
  parents too, / And house and all within it for my love." (p. 413, folio 404). **Property,
  knowledge, pride, self and parents are each named as things to be offered; the frame is
  hospitality — "If he but come within mine house / To be my guest".**
- **bhit-bhit-shah**: **the clothing-of-the-naked image, which the Momul cycle makes an act of grace
  and then of marriage.** "Ungirt I was and Dh6li6[text-layer?] covered me. / His little gift of
  silk he made as cloud / Of Kak to hide my nakedness. Now he / Hath raised me up to be anointed
  bride, / And I am happy in my happiness." (p. 413, folio 404). **"anointed bride" is the chunk's

- **bhit-bhit-shah**: **the Lilan and Chanesar cycle, prose headnote as printed — the chunk's one
  story with a named Hindu protagonist.** "Kam:iru[text-layer?] was the daughter of a Hindu king and

  neck was strung : / M1ne[text-layer?] arms a weight of armlets bore : / My hair was done up

  folio 409). And, as the counter-refrain of section V: "Look on all the lucky wives. / All have

  further stanzas "All have collars round their throats" and "All have jewels on the!r[text-layer?]

  protector is / Of wives who show humility" and "Beloved · walks upon the path / Of wives who show
  humility" are the doctrinal lines.**

- **bhit-bhit-shah**: **the poet's verdict on Sasui's fate, in the Sayid's voice.** "0 burnt-up soul,

  compact, / Thou castest knowing on the flame. / The men who with Beloved came / Have done thee,
  Sasui, great harm." (p. 400, folio 391). **"in death's own fee … love contract" is the chunk's one

  woman. **A resolution for the consolidator, and the only one this chunk supplies for an earlier
  chunk's damaged name.**

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The five daily prayers and the call to prayer, used as the clock of the Suhini cycle — the only
  ritual observance named anywhere in this chunk.** "The water-herons rested on the trees : the time
  / For Muslim's middle prayer had passed. Thereafter she / Did take the pot and enter on the flood
  whenas / She heard the cry that calkto[text-layer?] evening prayer, and scanned / To find the place
  where Sahir well-belov'd might bide." (p. 402, folio 393). **"Muslim's middle prayer" and "the cry
  that calkto[text-layer?] evening prayer" are named; neither is given a Sindhi or Arabic name, no
  mosque is mentioned, and no muezzin** — **(not stated)**. **"calkto"[text-layer?] is a merged and
  damaged string for what must be "calls to"; it is carried as printed.**
- **The lunar month as a calendar of darkness.** "The night is black : the pot unbaked. / Month's
  days in final dark are shed. / No beam of moon ; the waters roar." (p. 407, folio 398). **"Month's
  days in final dark" places the crossing at the end of a lunar month; no month is named** — **(not
  stated)** — but see the next bullet.
- **Sanwan, the rainy month, named.** "The rainy Sanwan like dry winter I would count" (p. 404,
  folio 395). **The chunk's one named month or season, and the only word in the chunk that survives
  from a Sindhi calendar. No date, no festival and no observance is attached to it** — **(not
  stated)**.
- **The potter's trade and the pot economy, which is the material basis of the whole Suhini cycle.**
  "the daughter of a well-to-do potter"; "Every day he came to purchase pots just for the hope of
  meeting Suhini"; "Spending all his money buying pots, lzzat[text-layer?] Beg became penniless";
  "they married Suhini to another potter's son named Dam" (all p. 401, folio 392). **A craft
  household, a retail trade in pots, a man ruined by buying them, and endogamous marriage within the
  potter families — four economic facts in one headnote.**
- **Buffalo-herding as paid employment, and the fall from merchant to servant.** "asked to be
  employed by Suhini's father. He was engaged as a cattleman to look after the potter's buffaloes."
  (p. 401, folio 392); "Mehar however continued to herd buffaloes and used to graze them on the other
  side of the river" (p. 401, folio 392); "The herdsman milked the buffalo / And with the sip of milk
  he gave her made her fey" (p. 406, folio 397); "Young buffaloes he guardeth" (p. 405, folio 396);
  "him who drives the horned kine" (p. 410, folio 401); "Nor empty be his cattle stead" (p. 405,
  folio 396). **A named occupation (cattleman), a named livestock (buffaloes), a grazing arrangement
  across a river, a byre ("cattle stead") and a product (milk).**
- **Fired and unfired earthenware as a technology on which a life depends — the chunk's most
  concrete piece of material culture.** "Every night Suhini crossed the river on a baked earthenware
  pot… they took away the baked pot and substituted for it an unbaked one. They thought by this
  device that she would never dare to trust herself to the water on so fragile a vessel." (p. 401,
  folio 392); "she crossed the current on an earthen jar / Whereon she sate with water reaching to
  the calf full high" (p. 403, folio 394); "The swift deep current and the jar unfired in kiln"
  (p. 408, folio 399); "From the kiln of my worthless skin / Love mounteth in blazing power"
  (p. 408, folio 399). **"unfired in kiln" and "the kiln of my worthless skin" make the kiln both a
  literal and a mystical term in the same cycle.**
- **River crossing without a boat: the named alternatives.** "She had no help of sailor nor of boat ;
  no r�pe[text-layer?] / She tied, but crossed the current on an earthen jar" (p. 403, folio 394);
  "Prepare thy goatskin that the herdsman thou mayst meet" (p. 405, folio 396); "Set forth without
  conveyance" (p. 402, folio 393); "Wise men do clutch at bushes when they're like to drown. / See
  (saith Latif) the virtue. that resides in reeds" (p. 408, folio 399). **Boat, sailor, rope,
  goatskin float, earthen jar, bushes and reeds — a full inventory of the river-crossing means of
  the country, given as a religious allegory.**
- **River hazards named as an economy of loss.** "Ships in the abyss / Have been engulfed whole till
  not a trace of wreck:'[text-layer?] / Nor any timber showeth the catastrophe. / The whirling waters
  hold some power of dread : for ships / Depart thence and return not." (p. 403, folio 394). **Named
  losses: ships, timber, and the crocodiles of p. 402 (folio 393). The chunk does not name the
  river** — **(not stated)**.
- **Camel transport, and the caravan as a social fact.** "Now am I such that camel men / Take me not
  with them" and "forgotten by the road / When cameldrivers laid the load / Upon the camels"
  (p. 400, folio 391); "using for his j ourneying[text-layer?] a very speedy camel which covered the
  distance easily" (p. 411); the whole of Momul section IV addresses the camel directly — "Your
  master kept you just for this, / 0 camel, to be driven on" and "Dear camel, with the Sodha's help,
  / You'll feed on Kak's rid�[text-layer?] verdant grass" (p. 414, folio 405); "Not one of the
  camelherds came" and "the camelherd's come !" (p. 423, folio "4 r4"[text-layer?]). **A named
  transport trade (camel men, cameldrivers, camelherds), a named specialised animal ("a very speedy
  camel") and a stated stage distance — "fifty miles" — covered nightly.**
- **The one distance in the chunk, given twice.** "who lived at Ludh6[text-layer?] fifty miles from
  the lake of Kak" and "every night he made the fifty mile j ourney[text-layer?] from Ludh6
  [text-layer?] to near Kak" (p. 411). **"fifty miles" and "fifty mile" — the only measurement in the
  chunk, and its digits survive as words.**
- **The one sum of money in the chunk.** "a magni­ficent[text-layer?] diamond necklace worth nine
  lakhs of rupees" (p. 416). **"nine lakhs of rupees", written out; no date and no other price
  anywhere in the chunk** — **(not stated)**.
- **Jewellery as a named class of goods, and as the pivot of a plot.** "diamond necklace" (p. 416,
  three times); "On arm I did no armlet wear : / My neck of necklaces was bare… The golden rings from
  mine ears hung : / The necklace round my neck was strung : / M1ne[text-layer?] arms a weight of
  armlets bore" (p. 418, folio 409); "the trinket" and "The trinket's tracery" (p. 417, folio 408);
  "the j ewel[text-layer?] she saw and was beguiled" (p. 418, folio 409); "When I touched the
  ornament / I lost the favour that I had… To sheer destruction was I brought / By necklace's
  catastrophe" (p. 420, folio "4n"[text-layer?]); "All have necklets round their necks", "All have
  collars round their throats", "All have jewels on the!r[text-layer?] heads" (p. 421, folio 412).
  **Necklace, armlet, ear-ring, collar, head-jewel, trinket — a named inventory of women's ornament,
  and a valuation for one piece.**
- **Cosmetics and toilet named as a practice, positively and negatively.** "nor put / Upon mine eyes
  antimony" (p. 418, folio 409); "I deck my beauty betimes / Hoping Beloved to see" (p. 409,
  folio 400); "Mine hair I did not comb… My hair was done up beautifully" (p. 418, folio 409);
  "no scent / Do I put on my hair disarrayed" (p. 424, folio "4 15"); "On her head / She puts no
  soap, nor to her hair / Incense applies" (p. 425, folio "4 16"); "On oil-less hair she puts no
  oil" (p. 425, folio "4 16"); "Her camphor-scents of gladness fled" (p. 425, folio "4 16").
  **Antimony, comb, scent, soap, incense, oil and camphor: a complete toilet vocabulary, all of it
  from women's practice, and most of it named in order to be refused.**
- **Marriage, its rites and its idioms.** "they married Suhini to another potter's son" (p. 401,
  folio 392); "Ran6[text-layer?], however, had already a wife of his own" (p. 411); "Hath raised me
  up to be anointed bride" (p. 413, folio 404); "Thou bridegroom art of many girls : / But art to me
  the only spouse" (p. 414, folio 405); "Thou art / My one spouse ! thou hast many wives" (p. 417,
  folio 408); "You've many wives, but as for me / You are my only husband" (p. 421, folio 412); "I
  have no guardian, nor have I a spouse, / No marriage bond, nor kin in marriage linked" (p. 412,
  folio 403); "She was betrothed to a man of her own tribe of Mariis[text-layer?]" (p. 422); "she
  would consent to become Umar's wife" (p. 422); "A wife by force I will not be" (p. 425,
  folio "4 16"); "Where the throngs of the marriages shine" (p. 424, folio "4 15"). **Polygyny,
  betrothal within the tribe, forced marriage refused, a wedding crowd, a marriage bond named as a
  legal thing, and one rite — the anointing of the bride.**
- **Nose-cutting as the idiom of disgrace, stated four times in three lines.** "The nose of my
  disgrace / Without a word, without a knife he cut." and "Within my fate / Disgraces live. Like
  blooms they blossomed and / To bigness grew. Their noses though I cut, / Disgraced they go not.
  What am I to do, / 0 sisters, with these noseless>[text-layer?] hideous things ?" (p. 413,
  folio 404). **A named punishment used as a figure of honour and shame; the chunk gives no social
  context for it** — **(not stated)**.
- **Kinship, and the reckoning of obligation by house.** "' I gained no profit from the house of my
  sire's sire, / Nor from the house of mother's sire. But from my friends / Great were the kindnesses

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **No karamat, no miracle worked by a saint, and no marvel attributed to a holy person appears
  anywhere in this chunk.** The narrative material is four folk romances, given by Sorley in prose
  headnotes captioned `The Story` and then in verse, and he attributes none of them to a saint, a
  tradition-bearer or a written source. **Recording this as a negative finding: a reader coming to
  the chunk for shrine legend will find none.**
- **The drowning of Suhini on an unbaked pot** — the chunk's central legend. "when night came Suhini
  launched herself upon the river on the pot and was drowned when the water caused it to
  disintegrate" (p. 401, folio 392), and the death of Mehar after her (p. 401, folio 392). **The one
  supernatural element the verse adds is the pre-eternal decree: "'Twas of God's own will / That
  might of waters broke her earthen pot in twain" (p. 406, folio 397)** — the accident is made an act
  of God, not a miracle of a saint.
- **The milk that maddened Suhini** — "The herdsman milked the buffalo / And with the sip of milk he
  gave her made her fey." (p. 406, folio 397). **A love-philtre motif in the mildest form; the chunk
  offers no gloss** — **(not stated)**.
- **Momul's suitors who "came to untimely ends"** — "Her fame and wealth had won her many suitors.
  But most of these came to untimely ends." (p. 411). **The chunk gives no cause, no agent and no
  number** — **(not stated)**. **This is the only unexplained fatality motif in the chunk and it is
  left bare.**
- **The stick left by the couch as a recognition token** — "Ran6[text-layer?] in utter disgust went
  away at once leaving his stick by the side of the couch so that Momul would recognize it. In the
  morning Momul saw the stick and realized the truth." (p. 411).
- **The camel that covers fifty miles nightly** — "using for his j ourneying[text-layer?] a very
  speedy camel which covered the distance easily" (p. 411). **Presented as a feat of the animal, not
  as a marvel.**
- **The primordial covenant as the origin of a love** — "Then, even then, to Suhini had there come a
  love / And longing for the neatherd." (p. 406, folio 397); and Marui's "' Am I not then your Lord
  ? ' / And with ' Yes ' my heart gave assent, / It was then that my promise I made / With the folk
  in the hedges pent." (p. 422). **In both cases a human attachment is dated to before the world.
  This is the closest thing in the chunk to a sacred narrative, and it is Koranic rather than
  hagiographic.**
- **The writ of the stone** — "They were shown me by writ of the stone." (p. 422). **A fate-writing
  motif; the chunk does not explain it** — **(not stated)**.

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Sorley's authorial voice is almost absent from this chunk, and where it appears it is confined to
  the four prose `The Story` headnotes.** He makes no literary judgement, offers no comparison, cites
  no authority and advances no thesis on any of these twenty-seven pages. **Set against the running
  argument of chunks 012 and 016 — the poet's rank, his sources, his audience — this chunk argues
  nothing.** That is itself the finding: the translation section is presented without apparatus.
- **His one ethnographic assertion, made flatly and in his own voice.** "The Mindhros are a Sindhi
  tribe living in Lower Sind." (p. 411). **No source, no date, no population** — **(not stated)**.
- **His one hedged identification, and the chunk's only visible act of scholarly judgement.** "Rano
  is called Dh6li6[text-layer?] (darling) by Momul in Shah Abdul Latif's verses and lives in Dhat,
  which appears to be another name for Ludh6[text-layer?]." (p. 411). **"appears to be" is the hedge;
  he gives no evidence for the equation.** See Doubts, where the Marui cycle's separate "Dhat"
  complicates it.
- **His editorial statements of what each cycle is about — four sentences, each in the same form, and
  together the closest thing in the chunk to an interpretation of the Risalo.** "The poems of '
  Suhini and Mehar ' describe the passionate longing of Suhini to seek her lover across the water,
  and her death in the rushing current." (p. 401, folio 392). "The poems of ' Momul and Ran6
  [text-layer?] ' describe her disillusionment, and her failure to obtain reconciliation with her
  lover." (p. 411). "The poems of ' Lilan and Chanesar ' are concerned with the disillusionment of
  Lilan when she finds that her trickery has recoiled on her own head and that she has lost her
  husband's love through her own folly." (p. 416). "The poems of ' Marui and Umar ', describe the
  feelings of Marui locked in her prison rooms and longing to be released by her people, the rustic
  camelherds and goatherds of Malir." (p. 422). **Three of the four are described in terms of the
  heroine's inner state — "longing", "disillusionment", "the feelings of" — which matches the claim
  of chunk 016 (p. 243, folio 234) that in this poet "Narrative is continually subordinated to
  introspection and soliloquy". The chunk does not make that connection itself.**
- **The two headnotes that state a moral, and both state the same one.** "She knew that her stratagem
  had recoiled on herself and that she had lost Ran6[text-layer?] by her own foolishness." (p. 411);
  "her trickery has recoiled on her own head and that she has lost her husband's love through her own
  folly" (p. 416). **"recoiled" in both. The Momul and Lilan cycles are read by Sorley as one story
  told twice.**
- **His one glossary-like intervention inside the text, which is how the chunk handles Sindhi
  vocabulary.** Words are glossed in parentheses at their first use and never again: "Mehar, or The
  Herdsman" and "" Sahir " (The Helper)" (p. 401, folio 392); "Dh6li6[text-layer?] (darling)"
  (p. 411). **There is no glossary, no footnote and no transliteration table in the chunk** — **(not
  stated)**.
