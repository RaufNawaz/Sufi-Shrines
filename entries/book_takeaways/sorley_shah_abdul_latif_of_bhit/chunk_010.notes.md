## Practices, institutions, economy

This is the bulk of the chunk and the part the archive should take whole.

### Irrigation labour, measurement and the corruption of it (pp. 141–143)

- **The measuring unit was locally variable, which is itself the finding.** "Calculations were made
  in the rupee of the district and the measure was the ' guz ', which had different standard lengths
  in almost each pargana." (p. 141, folio 132)
- **The survey method was crude and deliberately so.** "A cotton rope was used to measure . the work
  and the rise and fall of the land were measured by the eye only." (p. 141, folio 132)
- **Sanction was discretionary and turned on a personal judgement of honesty.** "The Amirs sanctioned
  as much as they thought fit and the amount depended more on their idea of the kardar's honesty
  than on the necessities of the work. Thus both authorities took means to protect themselves, for it
  was likely in such circumstances that the kardar would estimate for more work than he actually
  intended to perform and it is certain that there was much corruption in carrying out clearance."
  (p. 141, folio 132)
- **The regulatory density, with a figure.** "'In Hyderabad and Karachi districts there were no fewer
  than fifty-six sets of rules governing the payment of clearance charges." (p. 141, folio 132) The
  stray opening quotation mark is as printed.
- **Half the wage was paid in grain, not cash.** "Usually half of the amount paid for was paid in
  kind and not in money." (p. 141, folio 132) **This is the same in-kind logic the revenue section
  later describes for land revenue** (pp. 154–155).
- **Two grades of earthwork, differently named and differently priced.** "There was generally a
  distinction between earth carried out by the basket and earth thrown out by the hoe. The former,
  which was called ' `dhiili[text-layer?]` ', was more laborious, and must have been necessary in
  all `.the.` older canals with high banks. The latter was called ' uchal ' (throwing) . The
  difference in remuneration between the two methods was calculated as worth six guz (yards) per
  rupee." (p. 141, folio 132)
- **The labour hierarchy, named post by post.** "The kardar superintended the `'Y�rk[text-layer?]`
  of the kamrios (labourers) and was assisted by amins and mohiris. The amin measured the depth of
  the excavations and the mohiri the length and breadth of them." (p. 141, folio 132)
- **A surviving field technique, and the book's claim of continuity to 1940.** "To help them in their
  calculations, small portions of the canal beds, called takis (bench marks of earth) were left in
  their original state for measurement purposes, exactly as is done in canal clearance in Sind
  today." (p. 141, folio 132; sourced in footnote 1 to "Burton : History of Sindh, p. 37")
- **The Kalhora record is lost; the physical evidence is read instead.** "Col. Scott says that the
  canal clearance records of the Kalhora are not recoverable, but, from the `temains[text-layer?]`
  of sluices and very large canals, much more trouble and expense appear to have been incurred in
  days previous to the `Talpiirs[text-layer?]`. Amongst all the accounts Col. Scott was able to find
  only one for a brick sluice." (p. 141, folio 132) **A direct statement about the survival of
  eighteenth-century administrative records in Sind**, useful well beyond irrigation.
- **Hoarding as fiscal behaviour, with a figure.** "The Talpiirs were undoubtedly miserly, and
  exceedingly anxious to amass large sums of money which they converted into jewels or else stored
  as bullion in their treasuries." (p. 141, folio 132) Footnote 2: "When Mir Fateh Ali died in 1801
  he left thirty-five lakhs to divide amongst his brothers." (p. 141, folio 132)
- **Distrust as an administrative fact.** "Distrusting the integrity of their kardars, they were
  always ready to `c�[text-layer?]` down the demands for canal clearance and for the
  `makirig[text-layer?]` of fresh irrigational works." (pp. 141–142, folios 132–133)
- **One named public work under the Talpurs, dated.** "Some works were, however, carried out by them,
  notably the bund or embankment at Ali Bunder across the Fuleli, which is mentioned by Pottinger as
  the work of Fateh Ali Talpiir in 1799." (p. 142, folio 133)
- **The absence of a public works department is given as the structural reason for inaction.**
  "Possessing no public works department and with the help only of venal, unreliable and
  unscrupulous revenue officers, they could hardly have been expected to indulge in costly
  experiments to bring water back into watercourses that the river had deserted and made
  unprofitable." (p. 142, folio 133)

### Public health, famine and the weaving population (pp. 143–144)

- **Famine was rare but recorded, and the 1659 case is quoted from the factory records.** "Sind was
  relatively immune from famines, though there is mention of one in 1659 in the records of the first
  `Si:p.d[text-layer?]` factory, where it is stated that the goods to be embarked at Laribunder
  ' will be fewer than usual because the · famine and plague in Scinde is so great that it has swept
  away most part of the people and those that are left are few and what they make is bought by the
  country merchants at any price that causeth them not to take care it be good ' ." (p. 143,
  folio 134; footnote 2 "English Factories in India, 1659, pp. `209-10[text-layer?]`")
- **A three-season compound failure in Aurangzeb's own jaghir.** "During Aurangzeb's residence in
  Sind as Governor of Multan (1648-52) we learn that his jaghir produced little revenue as the result
  of drought, locusts, plague and floods in three successive seasons." (p. 143, folio 134; footnote 3
  "Sarkar : History of A urangzeb, pp. `I I4 2 5[text-layer?]` .")
- **The single most important demographic figure in this chunk, and it is about weavers.** "Hamilton
  in 1699 mentions that three years previously, because of a severe plague caused by the absence of
  rain in Tatta, 80,000 weavers perished." (p. 143, folio 134) **This dates the mortality to 1696 by
  the book's own arithmetic, which the book does not perform**: the year 1696 is **(not stated)** and
  should not be printed as if Sorley gives it. The figure bears directly on the weaving-household
  material the archive is collecting from this book, and on the decline of Tatta asserted at p. 142.
- **Medical attendance as a lever of the Company's relations with the Kalhora court.** "The Company's
  surgeons were in continual demand both in the seventeenth and the eighteenth century for attendance
  on the local officials and the rulers of Sind. The Kalhora in `partiq1lar[text-layer?]` were . very
  unwilling to release the Company's surgeon once he had been permitted to attend their Court."
  (p. 143, folio 134) **A named instance of a European skill being retained at a Sindhi court.**
- **War-induced scarcity, dated.** "In 1759 there was great scarcity of grain caused by the demands
  of the troops engaged in the Sind civil war." (p. 143, folio 134)
- **A doctor's wage, in figures.** In 1764 the Resident "wrote on April `nth[text-layer?]` : ' As no
  doctor of any reputation can be had at Shahbunder I thought it necessary to get the ablest I could
  procure here and accordingly have this day engaged with Haji Mahmud at the rate of Rs. 15 per
  month, allowing for expense of country medicine to attend to the Gallivat's crew and Bombay sepoys
  and visit our different factories as the state of the sick may require.'" (p. 144, folio 135)
- **The disease register, from Burnes.** "fatal epidemics and frightful pestilence, resembling in
  some points the plague of Egypt, occasionally devastated the land"; "ague, asthma, rheumatism and
  pulmonary consumption, with the long train of diseases attendant on the combination of heat with
  corrupt exhalations from the earth"; "Dropsy and enlargement of the spleen were also common and at
  certain periods a virulent ophthalmia caused by clouds of fine dust with which the atmosphere was
  impregnated"; "The Delta he found subject to heavy dews which were supposed by the natives to be
  extremely deleterious and even to occasion premature old age." (p. 144, folio 135; footnote 2 "See
  Burnes : op. cit . , p. `n 3[text-layer?]`.")
- **Demand for European medicine, witnessed.** Carless, surveying the Indus in "1 836-7", "remarked
  that disease in the villages near the sea coast was so prevalent that whenever he approached a
  village he was besieged for medicines by the villagers upon whom the · ravages of disease were
  ' disgustingly apparent ' ." (p. 144, folio 135)
- **A barter exchange on the river bank, refused.** Villagers came to Carless's boat "asking if he
  had brought · any English. goods for sale, one wanting a pair of scissors for his wife, another
  some cheap cloth and a third a little brandy for a sick relative. . In payment for these they were
  ready to offer fruits, butter and milk, which Lieutenant Carless politely declined to take from
  them in commiseration for their poverty and distress." (p. 144, folio 135)

### Festival, pilgrimage and the calendar of observance (pp. 145–146)

- **The four named festivals of the Sind year.** "the two `fds[text-layer?]`, Ramzan and Bakri" for
  Muslims and "`Hc'>li[text-layer?]` and Diwali" for Hindus (p. 145, folio 136), celebrated with
  "abandon and merriment … when money was spent freely and much display was made with `·new`
  clothes" (p. 145, folio 136).
- **A monthly, not annual, pilgrimage cycle, named in Sindhi.** From the *Tarikh-i-Tahiri*: "' Each
  month … has several ids for them, the first Friday after the new moon they call in the Sindh
  language ' ' `Mah-pahra JU.ma[text-layer?]` ''" — the crowd on that day fills the Makali mountain
  "that there is scarce room to stand" — and "' the similar festival of Mah-pahra
  `Siimar[text-layer?]`, or the first Monday in each month, a great day for making pilgrimages '"
  (pp. 145–146, folios 136–137). **This is the chunk's one description of pilgrimage practice, and
  it is monthly and twofold (Friday and Monday).**
- **What pilgrims carried and what they did there.** "' The pleasure of visiting each other induces
  them to go in large parties taking with them abundance of sweet river water and food such as they
  can afford. The day is spent in amusements and visits to the shrines.'" (p. 146, folio 137)

## Arguments and interpretations

Sorley writes in the first person repeatedly in this chunk and stakes out a revisionist position
against the standard European indictment of pre-British Sind. The judgements below are recorded as
his.

- **The central revisionist thesis about irrigation: blame the river, not the ruler.** "It may,
  therefore, be quite possible that much of the decay attributed to their carelessness and parsimony
  by English observers was due partly at least to changes in the set of the river which resulted in
  some of the old river channels being left high and dry." (p. 142, folio 133) Extended: "if there
  had been deterioration, it was more likely to have been due to changes in the river Indus than to
  the stupidity of man" (p. 142, folio 133).
- **And its corollary, that the system was sound.** "The facts in any case show beyond all reasonable
  doubt that the Sind canal system during Moghul and `Kalhorn[text-layer?]` days must, within its
  limits, have been a very important source of the country's wealth and was by no means a fit object
  of harsh and ill-informed criticism. It was fully capable of supplying the needs of an industrious
  people extremely competent at their own business of growing crops on land subject to regular
  `:flooding[text-layer?]`." (pp. 142–143, folios 133–134)
- **Burton is named and rejected on this point.** "The view of Burton that Sind came into British
  `hanas[text-layer?]` in an almost exhausted state and that the country was gradually
  `becom.ing[text-layer?]` a desert is one that cannot be accepted without considerable
  qualification." (p. 143, folio 134) **Yet Burton is Sorley's chief authority for intoxicants four
  pages later** (p. 147, folio 138) — the same source is refused on one subject and relied on for
  another, without comment.
- **A defence of the Talpurs against their English critics, immediately qualified.** "The
  `Ta.lpiirs[text-layer?]` were no fools. They did nothing deliberately to ruin the productiveness of
  their country, though they may have been short-sighted and suspicious of improvement." (p. 142,
  folio 133) Balanced against Carless in 1837: "' The Government of the Amirs is unpopular with
  nearly all classes of their subjects and the peasantry do not hesitate to express their hatred of
  the ruling family.'" (p. 142, folio 133)
- **A comparative grading of the three regimes' revenue systems.** The Commissioner's 1848 verdict
  that the revenue system was "' too frequently been arbitrary and subject to no restraint but that
  of an indifferently enlightened self-interest '" is accepted and then relativised: "But in this
  respect it did not differ from the government of the Moghuls or the KalhOra and was probably on the
  whole better than either." (p. 142, folio 133) Burnes is enlisted: "' Ignorant and oppressive as
  her rulers are, her annals do not show that she has ever been better governed than in recent times
  and they have at least the merit of having maintained her in a state of tranquil and almost
  uninterrupted repose for the last thirty years. '" (p. 142, folio 133)
- **The whole recreation section exists to rebut a named modern historian.** It opens by naming the
  misreading it attacks — "If any reader perusing these pages has come to the
  `Gonclusion[text-layer?]` that the peasantry of Sind in Moghul and Kalhorn days spent a life of
  unremitting toil and oppression without any lighter moments, he will have framed an erroneous
  conception of the facts" (pp. 144–145, folios 135–136) — and closes by naming Moreland: "The gloomy
  picture of social conditions painted by the works of Moreland must therefore be considerably toned
  down if a true impression is to be gained of the life of the poor in Sind during Moghul and
  `Kalh6ro[text-layer?]` days." (p. 149, folio 140) **The intervening five pages of festival, drink,
  sport and dance are, on the book's own account, evidence in an argument about welfare.**
- **A parallel rebuttal made in a footnote, using a pilgrimage as the evidence.** After the Tavernier
  Tirupati passage: "From this it may be inferred that conditions under the Moghuls were not really
  so bad as a casual reader might infer from the diatribes of foreign observers impressed by the
  universal poverty they saw and the capricious exercise of authority by the autocratic local
  rulers." (p. 146, folio 137, footnote 2) **Sorley reads the ability to make a long pilgrimage as an
  index of material well-being** — a methodological move the archive may want to cite.
- **The poor's amusements are defended, with the moral question explicitly bracketed.** "The idea,
  therefore, that the ·poor of Sind, despite their poverty, had no means of indulging in a form of
  amusement that appealed to them, is thus shown to be entirely unfounded. That this form of
  amusement was highly deleterious is not to the point." (p. 147, folio 138)
- **The chunk's explicit thesis about the origin of religious intolerance in Sind, offered in the
  first person as a personal reading.** "Baliichis as a class are not at all intolerant. They are a
  hospitable, easy-going people with a high notion of chivalry and hospitality within their social
  code. It seems that the intolerance which ultimately `developed�[text-layer?]` was due to the
  influence of the Kalh6ra who, as fanatical Muslims, were able to draw upon the prejudices of an
  ignorant Muhammadan population only too inclined to take a superficial and external view of the
  meaning of their religion. At least this is my reading of the conflicting
  `eyidence[text-layer?]`, to which I have given a great deal of concentrated study." (p. 152,
  folio 143) **This is the most consequential interpretive claim in the chunk and the one an entry is
  most likely to want to cite or contest. Sorley labels it his own reading; it must be attributed as
  such, and the phrase "as fanatical Muslims" is his.**
- **The difficulty he admits the thesis has to overcome.** "It is hard `fo[text-layer?]` reconcile
  the accounts of the happy camaraderie of all classes that prevailed in Tatta during its prosperous
  days with the distressing stories of religious bigotry common in the records of English observers
  in the nineteenth century." (pp. 152–153, folios 143–144)
- **The Talpur role in bigotry: inherited, not created.** "The Talpiirs finding the tendencies towards
  bigotry well established took no trouble to correct them, as they were perfectly satisfied with
  their own position of personal supremacy and with their opportunities for amassing wealth. On
  grounds of prudence they saw no reason to invite the ill-will of the untutored clans from whom they
  derived their power. They followed, therefore, the easy policy of letting sleeping dogs lie."
  (p. 153, folio 144)
- **A three-regime moral ranking, stated flatly.** "The Moghuls were autocratic but in essence they
  tried to be just . The Kalhora - were tyrannical and capricious because they were at one and the
  same time endeavouring to establish a Sind independent of Moghul, Persian and Afghan domination and
  also to shake `qff[text-layer?]` the growing power of the Baliichi - clans. The Talpiirs had no
  reason to fear the Baliichis but were bent upon keeping Sind as a close preserve of Muslim power,
  with the result that under them non-Baliichis and Hindus alike were treated with less consideration
  than they had been under Moghul or Kalh6ro." (p. 152, folio 143)
- **Peace at a price — the formula the chapter turns on.** "Under the `Moghnls[text-layer?]` and the
  KalhOra Sind was never at peace. Under the `Tfilpiirs[text-layer?]` it was. But the price of peace
  was the tyranny of a Baliich minority which the Moghuls had always endeavoured to suppress and the
  Kalh6ra unsuccessfully to conciliate." (pp. 151–152, folios 142–143)
- **Why British observers got the earlier centuries wrong — an explicit methodological warning.** "It
  was this aspect of the `Tfilpiir[text-layer?]` rule which so unfavourably impressed English
  observers in the nineteenth century and led them to draw entirely erroneous conclusions about the
  political conditions that had prevailed in Sind in previous centuries." (p. 151, folio 142) And:
  "British aims were then concentrated on breaking the Baliichi power and all the emphasis of
  criticism was laid upon the sad plight of non-Baliichis. If these general considerations are borne
  in mind much of the apparently contradictory evidence as to the conditions under which Sind
  Muslims, other than Baliichis, and `Hinduslived[text-layer?]` in Sind during the seventeenth,
  eighteenth and the early part of the nineteenth century will be found readily explicable."
  (p. 152, folio 143) **Directly applicable to how the archive should weigh colonial-era shrine
  reporting.**
- **The chapter's summary formula: form unchanged, spirit changed.** "the moral, therefore, is clear
  that while the framework of the Moghul administration in Sind remained little altered in essentials
  from 1600 to 1840, the change in spirit was considerable." (p. 153, folio 144) The retained
  features are named: "its feudal character, its dependence on the good will of the classes possessed
  of military strength, and its efficiency as a revenue collecting machine." (p. 153, folio 144)
- **An unusual concession about the Talpur rulers as persons.** "The Talpiir rulers themselves were by
  no means unworthy `per-sonages[text-layer?]`. Their bearing and good manners surprised all the
  Europeans with whom they came in contact. Burnes and Pottinger were much impressed with their
  natural dignity, courtesy and intelligence." (p. 153, folio 144)
- **A silence in the native sources is noted and made into an argument.** Of the sick villagers of the
  coast: "This aspect of the common lot of the poor receives no mention at all in the pages of any of
  the native annalists. But the facts must not be forgotten in appraising the culture of the Moghul
  and Kalh6ro epochs." (p. 144, folio 135)
- **Sorley repeatedly claims continuity to his own day.** Canal bench marks "exactly as is done in
  canal clearance in Sind today" (p. 141, folio 132); horsemanship "still a feature of present-day
  Sind" (p. 148, folio 139); wrestling "as popular as it is today" and village matches still drawing
  "a huge gathering at a few hours' notice" (p. 149, folio 140); folk dance "of which Sind possessed
  and still possesses a great variety" (p. 149, folio 140); relaxations "which are still typical of
  the rural economy in which they live today" (p. 149, folio 140). **These "today" statements are
  1940 statements and must be dated as such whenever quoted.**

- **`cases called abwab` (p. 155, folio 146) is almost certainly `cesses`, and is left as printed.**

- **Bare toponyms, held back from archive rows under the interim convention.** Each of the following
