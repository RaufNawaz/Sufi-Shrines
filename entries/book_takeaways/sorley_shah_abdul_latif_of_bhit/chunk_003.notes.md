## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The Kalhora as a ruling house of saints — the chunk's central institutional claim, and the one
  most useful to the archive.** The dynasty "drew its original driving force from the appeal it
  made to the religious predilections of the common man" (p. 40, printed 31); the Talpurs who
  displaced it "never made the same appeal to the common man since they did not pride themselves
  on saintliness nor did they claim long descent in the manner of the Kalhora, who succeeded in
  imposinf:;; [text-layer?] on their Sind subjects the belief, however little foundation there may
  have been for it, that the family of Adam Shah could trace its origin from Abbas, the Uncle of
  the Prophet" (p. 40). The author's word for the regime is "the autocracy of saints" (p. 41).
- **Pir–murid ties used as a recruiting instrument of state.** The Baluchi migration into Sind is
  described as a prince summoning his own disciples: "two of his mountaineer Murids, Mirs Aliido
  [text-layer?] and Masiido [text-layer?], to emigrate from their barren hills and settle in the
  low country" (p. 41, quoting Burton). The author generalizes it: "The earlier Kalhorn
  [text-layer?] had encouraged Baliichis [text-layer?] to settle in Sind in order that the Kalhora
  might take advantage of their military superiority" [text-layer?, stray quotation mark as
  printed] in the battlefield" (p. 40).
- **Reception protocol at the Kalhora court**, in Burton's quoted account: ministers and nobles
  sent out "with presents of clothes and horses with gold saddles to receive and escort his
  distinguished guests to the capital"; they met instead "a troop of beggarly shepherds followed
  by their flocks, and women mounted on asses", and Mir Aliido was "the ragged wayfarer with the
  " dheri in JI [text-layer?] his hand and the " kamb6 " ort [text-layer?] his shoulderswas
  [text-layer?] the personage" (p. 41). The two Sindhi/Baluchi objects, `dheri` and `kamb6`, are
  **left unglossed by the book** in this chunk.
- **Tribute, revenue and the fiscal squeeze on Sind.** A tribute of "twenty lakhs of rupees" to
  Nadir Shah, secured by three princely hostages (p. 39); a "yearly tribute to Kabul" under Ahmad
  Shah (p. 39); under the Moghuls, "the extortion made from well-to-do native traders and the
  escheat of property on death to the Emperor" (p. 53); revenue collectors who "came to receive
  their rents the same day we came to Derbellah but durst not (go) noe further than Khandearah,
  the arbaubs and cheife men being all fiedd [text-layer?], standing out as rebbells" (p. 53).
- **The customs system, described as a working institution from three directions.** (i) At
  Laribunder in 1635: "The customhouse is in an open place upon the rivers side… The customes and
  government of the towne are taken and exercised by Asaph Ckaun's substitutes" (p. 49). (ii) The
  petty customs at Tatta: "on all provisions one quarter pice per rupee and one seare of the sort
  invested upon each corwar (kharwar : ass-load) ; on all sorts of cloth brought from other places
  and s9uld [text-layer?] in this citty the buyer paies ! [text-layer?] pies per rupy and the
  seller ! [text-layer?] pice and many the like, which though we denied to pay… yet they to
  maintayne their customes would not remitt it" (pp. 50–51). (iii) The 1647 transport due, "that
  unreasonable dutie of Cheheleaheeck (chahil-yak or one fortieth, 2! [text-layer?] per cent)",
  given in the author's own text as "transport dues of 2t2 [text-layer?] per cent" (p. 53).
- **Customs privilege as the price of a factory.** By Ghulam Shah's *perwannah* of 22 September
  1758 the Company's officers were directed "to charge no more than one and a half per cent duty
  above the market price on all goods purchased by the British agent for exl?ort [text-layer?] and
  to l�vy [text-layer?] on importation only one half of that paid by other merchants" (pp. 46–47,
  quoting Postans, *Personal Observations on Sindh*, pp. 2 83-4). The grant is called "a perwannah
  or order" and the underlying imperial grant "the Phirmaund obtained from the Prince" (p. 46).
- **Dastaks and the death of a governor.** The English objected to "the trouble and inconvenience
  caused by letters of authority to the Company's agents (dastaks) lapsing when a Governor died
  and having to be renewed by his successor, a process that usually meant delay and the payment of
  a bribe" (p. 53). The Kotwal's attempt "to charge duty on the cloth sold by the weavers to the
  English traders" is recorded as "a new demand" (p. 53).
- **The weaving economy, town by town, with household counts.** Nasarpur: "Report saies near upon
  3000 families of weavers inhabit� [text-layer?] there", the cloth "very substantiall, thredd
  even spunne and well woven", its baftaes "called joories… in length 17 coveds Tuttah and in
  breadth ! [text-layer?] coveds" (p. 50). Sehwan: "About rooo [text-layer?] households of weavers
  live there also who make a very good sort of baftaes, shorter then those of Nassurpore 3 Tuttah
  coveds yet larger r i [text-layer?] inches" (p. 50). Tatta: "about 3000 families of weavers ; yet
  the greatest part of them make not other than divers sorts of checkered alejaes vendible in
  Persia and Turkey", plus "diapher clotheing", a few very fine baftaes "most part for the citty
  use", and "a course sort of ginghams which they call seriaes, made for purpose of sailes"
  (p. 50). Darbelo made "narrow joories… of lengths similar to those of Nasarpiir [text-layer?]"
  (p. 51).
- **Indigo as a measured crop.** At Sehwan "The chiefest commodity there made and in the adjacent
  townes is indicoe in forme like to that of Byana yet nothing so good because in the making they
  are accustomed to mingle sand with it-About 2000 maunds are yearely made : the wieght there 36
  pice per seare though in Tuttha at resale it be weighed at 40 pice per seare" (p. 50).
  Adulteration "with sand and earth" was noted again by Fremlen in 1640 (p. 51). Bamford in 1639
  put the indigo of Bubak and Sann reaching Tatta at not above "moo [text-layer?] greate maens"
  (p. 51). "Ophium is also made there in great quantities" at Sehwan, and "Butter and oyle is
  brought thence hether in great abundance" (p. 50).
- **Saltpetre — the whole reason for the 1758 return.** "The lure which drew the Company to Sind in
  the midst of this turmoil was the chance of obtaining a monopoly in saltpetre", with two minor
  objectives, selling woollens "to the armies of Afghanistan, the peoples of Central Asia and
  residents of the northern plains of India", and shipping "some of the excellent cattle of Sind to
  Bombay for sale" (p. 46). The Directors' instruction: "our Hon'ble masters' principal motive for
  settling at Scindy is to secure to themselves the whole produce of saltpetre in that country…
  exert yourself in getting it refined as well as possible" (p. 46, footnote). In 1635 Tatta's
  "saltpetre made here is very good but very dear, viz 6 rupees per maund" (p. 50).
- **River transport and its costs.** Goods came down the Indus "in fiat [text-layer?] bottomed
  boats of a moo [text-layer?] or 2000 maunds"; Bamford "recommended as the cheapest method of
  transport that of buying at Lahore a moo [text-layer?] maund boat and selling it at Tatta at the
  end of the journey for 250 rupees" (p. 51). Nasarpur's advantage was that "comeinge downe with
  the current charges of transportacion must be very little" (p. 50); Sehwan's goods "arrive
  thence commonly in ro [text-layer?] daies" (p. 50). Overland, camels carried "six maundspucka
  [text-layer?] to Tatta at ri [text-layer?] rupe�s [text-layer?] per camell" (p. 49), and the
  Agra–Multan leg went "by carts" at "not above one rupee per maund from thence to Tuttah" (p. 50).
- **Bamford's schedule of charges**, as printed: "4 rupees to the Governor per maen, l [text-layer?]
  rupee to the town, and freight to Tatta i [text-layer?] rupee" (p. 51).
- **Currency friction in Upper Sind.** Purchases at Kandiaro had to be financed in specie carried
  up country, "and that too in new rupees, which many times are not heere to be gott, which,
  allthough so, when come there will not pass untill translated into pice, which last yeare much
  hindered us" (pp. 52–53, letter of "2r [text-layer?] February 1646").
- **Famine and rural impoverishment, twice.** The Company came to Sind in 1635 partly because
  "since the famine of 1630 Gujarat, then the largest source of supply, had been quite unable to
  cope with the demand" for cotton piece-goods (p. 44). In 1631 the Dutch ship's cargo sold well
  "In spite of the miserable state of the district owing to famine" (p. 49). And in 1644 John
  Spiller found Upper Sind's indigo country unplanted: "the people are so exceedingly opprest and
  kept so miserably poor that notwithstanding the soil is fertile and propper and would produce
  large quantities of good indicoes they have nether the will nor means to manure and sow the
  ground" (p. 52).
- **Prices and provisions at Laribunder, December 1635.** "Otherwise the towne is well provided of
  all necessaries ; fish and fruite in abundance and incredibly cheap ; henns at four pice each,
  sheep at a rupee each, rice and butter very cheap and all other victualling. Waxe is scarce to
  be had and therefore extraordinary deare" (p. 49).
- **Building and domestic technology at Laribunder.** "The towne wel inhabited though m
  [text-layer?] built, the houses being most of mud supported by such poore tymbers that it is a
  wonder how they stand ; to their room they have Kita Ventos (Port. catavento, airshaft)"; on
  which the author's own footnote: "These are the wind scoops still in universal use in Lower Sind
  to catch the south-west wind. They are called ' Mangh ' in Sindhi from a fancied resemblance to a
  goose neck" (p. 49). **The only Sindhi word the book glosses in this chunk.**
- **Hospitality as protocol**: "December 7. The Shahbunder sent us for a feast divers goates,
  henns, meale, flower, rice, butter and sugar"; and on arrival at Tatta the party was "welcomed
  even by the poore mechaniques with their accustomed well wishes" (p. 49).
- **Non-Muslim and foreign religious presence, in two lines only.** The Portuguese at the port were
  "possessing a church at the port" (p. 43); and at Laribunder in 1635, "There are three or four
  padrees who have a very meane place to exercise their devotions in" (p. 49). Nothing further is
  said of either.
- **Banditry and insecurity on the trade roads.** The Samejas between Sehwan and old Hala made the
  country impassable "without a strong guard" and were "at that time (1640) apparently in rebellion
  against the Moghul Governor to the disturbance of the peace of central S1.nd [text-layer?]"
  (p. 51); the road to Kandiaro was "so obnoxious to dainger" (pp. 52–53); an early broker was
  "deteined upward of 8 months by reason of warrs and diffirencies betweene the Rajaes through
  whose country hee was to passe" (p. 48).
- **The Company's establishment in Sind as an institution.** Fremlen's proposal was for five:
  "the chief at Tuttah : one at Nassurpore : one at Seahwaun : one penman and cashier and one
  packer and keeper of petty expences" (p. 50). Head office at Tatta with an outpost at Laribunder
  1635–62 (p. 45), head office at Tatta with an outpost at Shahbunder 1758–75 (p. 47). Duration:
  "twenty-seven years in the seventeenth century, and for seventeen years in the eighteenth
  century. Between the two periods there was a complete blank of ninety-six years" (p. 48).
- **Rated prices as a commercial virtue**: at Laribunder "the prizes are knowne and sett downe in a
  ratebooke, not to bee innovated or altered at every covetous or unjust Governor's will" (p. 51).
- **Quality decline in Sind cloth, 1641–43, as the London letters record it.** "The washers too
  should be warned not to tatter the cloth or put so much starch into it" (p. 52, letter of 29
  November l64r [text-layer?]); "they are thin cloths onlie made fayre to the eye by overmuch
  starching, slicking and beating.-They are neither good cloth, nor full size" (p. 52, letter of
  2 7 November 1643); "The make of all sorts of cloth in that place doth much degenerate from
  former times and yearly declines by reason of the ready vend it finds at Bussora which occations
  many buyers in so much that narrow baftaes of all sorts are lately risen 5 and 6 rupees per
  corge and yet made worse than ever" (p. 52).
- **Company finance as background**: dividends "averaged 25 per cent during the years 1657-91" and
  in 1683 "the market price of the £100 share was £500" (p. 46, footnote, citing *The English in
  India*, p. 66); the charters of 1661, 1669, 1677, 1683, 1687 and the 1708 fusion, ending with
  "the acceptance by Clive in 1765 of the Diwani of Bengal" (p. 47).

## Arguments and interpretations

Sorley writes here as a British Indian Civil Service historian of 1940; the judgements below are
recorded as **his**, not as facts.

- **The author holds the Kalhora period to be a moral failure.** "The whole episode of the Kalhora
  supremacy makes very sad reading. It was certainly unfortunate for the masses of the Sind
  population that a dynasty of their own people, which drew its original driving force from the
  appeal it made to the religious predilections of the common man, should have proved so poor a
  substitute for the government of the Moghuls" (p. 40).
- **Sorley treats the Kalhora genealogy as an imposture** — the family "succeeded in imposinf:;;
  [text-layer?] on their Sind subjects the belief, however little foundation there may have been
  for it", that it descended from Abbas (p. 40). The chunk offers **no evidence either way**; the
  scepticism is asserted.
- **Sorley's verdicts on individual rulers** are unhedged: Sarfaraz Khan is "a particularly
  short-sighted, unfeeling and incompetent tyrant" (p. 40) and "the insensate and blood-thirsty
  tyrant" (p. 48); Ghulam Shah is "the most capable of the Kalh6ra [text-layer?]" who nonetheless
  "did a little to restore some kind of order" and whose "actual achievements were not very
  remarkable" (pp. 39–40); the Talpurs "were plain, blunt men" (p. 40).
- **The Talpur assessment is explicitly framed against British opinion, and is the chunk's clearest
  ICS-coloured passage.** Their government "did not meet with the favour of a later Victorian age
  of British statesmen and did not inspire the British public with any feeling except disgust at
  the intolerance and the savagery of many of the practices current till the British annexation";
  against which Sorley enters a defence — "they did succeed in giving a distracted land peace for
  forty years and… within the limits of their administrative ideals their government was neither
  inefficient nor contemptible" (pp. 40–41). The "practices" are **not specified** anywhere in the
  chunk.
- **Isolation as the author's explanatory key.** "the Kalh6ra [text-layer?] and Talpiirs
  [text-layer?] between them succeeded in keeping Sind shut to the influences of the outside world
  for a hundred and fifty years", so that in 1843 Napier "was able to see in being a survival of
  Moghul administration unaffected by the softening and civilizing influences which, working in
  full force in other parts of India for a century, had changed men's ideas of how a government
  should behave" (p. 41). The civilizing-influence framing is the author's own.
- **Sorley reads the Talpur arrival as a class-and-faith reversal.** "Little indeed did the
  Kalh6ro [text-layer?] of that moment realize that in this beggarly procession of hill
  shep�erds [text-layer?] lay the force that would one day drive his descendants from their proud
  throne, and that the autocracy of saints would fall in a welter of blood before the shepherds
  with their spinning whorls and their slings in which they carried lambs across their backs"
  (p. 41).
- **A stated source problem, which governs the whole chapter.** "For the period I690-I760
  [text-layer?] the direct contemporary evidence available has already been described as remarkably
  deficient"; "There is no European record for Sind between the years I662 [text-layer?] and 1758";
  the years 1662–1755 are "an uncharted sea" with Hamilton "one noteworthy exception"; without the
  Company's records "the period 1600-1760 would be almost a blank" (p. 42). **The author also
  blames the indigenous historians**: "the native historians have failed to make up for the lack of
  English record" (p. 42).
- **Sorley dismisses the standard Mughal-era travellers for Sind.** "Not one of the outstanding
  commentators of the Moghul Empire visited Sind and few of them make more than cursory mention of
  it. Tavernier, Bernier, Thevenot are alike of little value for social conditions in Sind", and
  the same of "the host of minor travellers" — Terry, Fryer, Ovington, Mandelslo, Montserrate,
  Herbert, de Laet, Barbosa, della Valle, Linschoten, Mundy, Ives, Bartolemeo, Luiller, Grose,
  Hedges and Stavorinus (p. 43). He rates Withington, Manrique and Manucci highly by contrast:
  "persons of unusual intelligence, and reliable observers of events and places that were within
  their personal knowledge" (p. 43).
- **A methodological choice stated outright**: "It is best to let the records speak for
  themselves. They are certainly arresting and dramatic enough" (p. 48) — which is why pp. 48–53
  are mostly block quotation from the East India Company factory records (`E.F.I.`) rather than
  the author's narrative.
- **Sorley judges the Dutch archive not worth consulting**: "It is in these circumstances highiy
  unl ikely [text-layer?] that the records of the Dutch Company would do m uch [text-layer?] to
  supplement the evidence available in the English Company's records" (p. 44, footnote).
- **An inference the author labels as one.** Of the 1647 letter: "The events here alluded to
  **appear to be** the Emperor's departure on a military expedition against Central Asia, the
  appointment of Aurangzeb as Governor of Multan, and the grant of authority of various kinds to
  Aurangzeb's brothers with a multiplicity of tax and rent collectors" (p. 53). The document itself
  names only "the Kinge", "Seyed Ckawn" and "Black (Balkh)".
- **A sympathy declared in passing**, worth noting for the author's politics: Charles I is "that
  misunderstood and rather ill-treated monarch" (p. 45).

  "Baliichi"/"Baliichis", "Aliido", "Masiido", "Arghiin"/"Arghiins", "Nasarpiir", "Nusarpiir",
  "Hariharpiir", "Raj apiir", "Fariiki", "Punj ab". **None of these has been repaired above.**
- **"Kalhora"/"Kalhoro" appears in at least eight different damaged spellings** across the chunk —
  `Kalh6ro`, `Kalh6ra`, `Kalhora`, `Kalhorn`, `KalhOra`, `KalhOro`, `KalhOrn`, `Kalli6ro` (pp. 39,
  40, 41, 46, 47, 48). This is one mechanical fault (ō → 6 / O / rn), not eight variant forms used
  by the author, but it has been carried through as printed and must not be silently normalized.
- **"Mian Mir Mahomed, the first prince of that (Kalh6ro) dynasty" (p. 41) must NOT be mapped to
  the archive row `shrine-of-mian-mir`**, whose principal figure the index gives as "Mian Mir (Mir
  Mohammad of Sind)". The names as printed are nearly identical and a consolidator could easily
  merge them. This chunk gives its Mian Mir Mahomed **only** as a Kalhora ruler who invited the
  Baluchis in; it says nothing of sainthood, Lahore, a tomb or a date for him. **A human must keep
  these two apart.** Note also that the chunk's phrase "Mian Mir Mahomed" sits inside a Burton
  quotation, so it is Burton's spelling, not necessarily Sorley's.
- **The Sehwan material is town material, not shrine material.** The archive row
  `lal-shahbaz-qalandar` is at Sehwan Sharif, and this chunk names the place eight times across
  four spellings — "Sewistan" (p. 39), "Seahwaun", "Sehwan" (p. 50), "Sewwan", "Sevensteere
  (Sehwan)" (p. 52), "Seuestan" (p. 52) — **always as an indigo, weaving and administrative place,
  never once as a shrine town.** Nothing here supports a statement about the dargah, the saint, the
  ʿurs or the custodianship. The same caution applies to `shrine-of-makhdoom-nooh-hala` for "old
  Hala" (p. 51).
- **Several other archive locations are named as bare toponyms and should not be mapped at all.**
  "the fort of Umarkot" (p. 39) is a fortress in a military narrative, not the `umarkot-amarkot-
  shiv-mandir` row; "Kelat" (p. 39) is a political actor, not the `kalat-kali-temple` row; Multan
  (pp. 50, 53) and Lahore (p. 51) appear only as trade and governorship points, and the archive
  holds many rows in each, so **no Multan or Lahore row can be attached to these pages**.
- **PDF p. 49 carries no printed page number** in the text layer (running head damaged to

- **No original script anywhere.** The chunk prints no Sindhi, Persian or Arabic characters; all

  satisfied beyond that for these pages.
