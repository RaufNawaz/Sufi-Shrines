## Practices, institutions, economy

**There is no shrine practice in this chunk — no ʿurs, no fair, no langar, no offering, no custodian,
no sajjada nashin, no endowment to a shrine, no tomb-building and no visitor figure.** All
**(not stated)**. What the chunk does supply is the quantitative and documentary substrate of the
Sindhi rural economy in which those institutions sat, and it supplies it in unusual detail.

- **Land seizure by the colonial state, named as "resumption", twice in one year.** "Report on the
  District of Sahita- lately resumed from His Highness Ali Murad" (1853) and "Reports on the districts
  lately resumed from Meer Alli Moorad in Sind" (1 853[text-layer?]) (p. 190, folio 181). **The verb
  the records use is "resumed", the party dispossessed is named, and the reports were made by Deputy
  Collectors — i.e. by the revenue arm.** The archive's interest in seized land should start here.
- **Forced labour in Sind, and its abolition, now with a volume and a year.** `E. OFFICIAL
  CORRESPONDENCE ON THE ABOLITION OF STATU'I'.E[text-layer?] OR FORCED LABOUR IN $IND[text-layer?].
  Vol. 34. 1856.` (p. 190, folio 181). **Chunk 012 saw only the section heading on the contents page;
  this is the full entry. "Statute or forced labour" is corvée — begar — and a single 1856 volume of

## Arguments and interpretations

**No legend or miracle appears anywhere in this chunk, so `## Legends and miracles (karamat)` is
omitted rather than padded.** The arguments below are all Sorley's own, and they are of two kinds:
a statement of editorial method (Appendix VI), and the opening of his theory of poetry (pp. 208–211,
folios 199–202), which is the frame he will apply to the Risalo.

- **Sorley explains, in his own words, the spelling inconsistency that these notes have been
  recording as damage for twelve chunks. This is the single most useful paragraph in the chunk for the
  archive.** `APPENDIX VI / NOTE ON THE SPELLING AND TRANSLITERATION OF ORIENTAL WORDS` (p. 203,
  folio 194): "This work contains many transliterations of Arabic, Persian ·and Sindh:[text-layer?]
  words. I have not thought it necessary or desirable in a book which is meant to appeal to the
  general reader to employ the system of transliteration approved by the Royal Asiatic Society."
- **What he does not mark.** "Thus I have not shown by diacritical marks the distinction between the
  various forms of the ' h ', ' s ', ' t ', ' d ' and z sounds which occur in Arabic, Persian and
  Sindhi. The absence of the distin­guishing marks is not likely to trouble scholars of Arabic and
  Persian who are familiar with the correct . spelling. Readers, however, who are ignorant of the
  Arabic, Persian and Sindhi characters find in phonetic meticulousness merely a disturbance and
  annoyance which has no compensating advantages." (p. 203, folio 194) **Five consonant classes go
  unmarked. Note carefully what the paragraph does NOT say: it says nothing about vowel length, and
  the `ii` / `6` / `fi` strings in `Siifi`, `Talpiir`, `Kalh6ra`, `Nasarpiir`, `Niir`, `Timiir`,
  `Daiidpota` are therefore not explained by it — see Doubts.**
- **What he does mark.** "otherwise I have followed a system which remains faithful to the original to
  the extent that aspirated and breathed sounds are indicated by the presence of the letter ' h '."
  (p. 203, folio 194)
- **Where he surrenders to English usage, with his two worked examples.** "Where Oriental words have
  become Anglicized into some generally accepted form I have used these forms. … Thus I have accepted
  words like ' Sind ' and ' Sukkur ' when on a principle of formal consistency I should have written
  ' Sindh ' and Sakhar '." (p. 203, folio 194) **"Sukkur" in this book is an Anglicization of what
  Sorley would otherwise write "Sakhar" — relevant to every Sukkur mention in chunk 012 and to the
  archive rows at Sukkur.**
- **Why Sindhi names in this book lose their endings, stated as a deliberate editorial choice.** "The
  transliteration of Sindhi words is difficult because every word in Sindhi ends in a vowel sound
  either pure or nasalized. Burton endeavoured, in some of his notes to his History of Sindh, to show
  these final short vowels. The result is not satis­factory and I have not followed his example. I
  have, therefore, omitted all the final short vowels where these occur. Another difficulty with
  Sindhi is the frequency of nasalized sounds which cannot be represented conveniently by any form of
  English trans­literation. Scholars of language who are interested in this matter must . go to the
  Sindhi alphabet for instruction." (p. 203, folio 194) **Sorley strips the Sindhi final vowel. Any
  Sindhi name in this book is therefore systematically truncated relative to its Sindhi form, by the
  author's own declaration — this is an editorial rule, not OCR loss, and the archive must not
  "restore" it silently either.**

- **The exoteric / esoteric division, which is the armature of the whole second half of the book.**

  points of view, the exoteric and the esoteric. The former is concerned with form and expression as a

  the state of mind of the person thinking about it." (p. 208, folio 199) **He restates it at the end

- **The three criteria of poetical value, which will be the test he applies to the Risalo.** "There
  is, first, an examination of external form, words, language, rhythm, subject matter and musical
  composition. There is, second, an intellectual judgement as to the meaning and significance of the
  thought expressed in this external form. There is, third, an emotional attitude called up by the
  aesthetic effect of the poetry on the mind. The first is the exoteric point of view. The second and
  third together constitute the esoteric point of view." (p. 209, folio 200) And the conclusion:
  "According, therefore, as a piece of poetry satisfies these standards of good and skilful
  composition, of clearness, dignity and truth in its ideas, and of aesthetic excellence it is to be
  judged as good poetry. These are the three criteria · of poetical value." (p. 209, folio 200)

  where the performed character of the poetry is even gestured at.**
- **He attacks I. A. Richards by name and at length.** He quotes: "' It is evident ', says Dr I. A.
  Richards in his Principles of Literary Criticism, ' that the bulk of poetry consists of statements
  which only the very foolish would think of attempting to verify. They are not the kind of things

  shall decide which of the many forms of emotive language is supreme ? At the best Dr Richards'

  forms of emotive language is Sorley's own example, and it is a pointed one in a book about
  devotional verse.**

- **Against the idea that emotion changes meaning — which is his standing objection to mystical
  reading.** "by the assertion that in poetry reference is subordinated to attitude it seems to be
  implied that the emotional content produced by poetry affects the significance of the idea. Of

  very different thing." (p. 210, folio 201) **"The listener", not "the reader", throughout this
  passage — see the same word at p. 210 (folio 201), "to play upon the emotions of the listener" and
  "create in the listener's mind a recognizable picture of reality". Sorley's implied audience for
  poetry is an audience that hears it.**
- **He denies that poetic meaning is mysterious — in a book whose subject is a mystic.** "There is
  nothing whatever mysterious or difficult about poetic meaning, however hard it may be to explain the

  definitely calculated, by means of tricks allied to the tricks of music, to play upon the emotions of

  technique, and it stands two pages before a chapter list that includes a music bibliography.**
- **Allusive language is not special to poetry.** "All human language is allusive as well as direct.

  association of ideas, which is the source of simile, metaphor, imagery and symbol­ism. But this form

  meanest intelligence working within the narrow range of direct perception." (p. 211, folio 202)
