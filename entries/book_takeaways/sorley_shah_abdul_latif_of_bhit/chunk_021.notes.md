# sorley_shah_abdul_latif_of_bhit — chunk 021 — pp. 348–372

**This chunk is almost entirely translated verse, not authorial prose**, so the analytical headings
below are thin by necessity and the quotation-bearing ones are long. Two pages are apparatus — a
`PART III` title page and a table of contents running over pp. 368–369 — and those are recorded
under the added heading `## Bibliography and apparatus`, as this book's convention requires. Two
further pages are Sorley's own prose: the story headnote to `III. THE KING AND THE MINSTREL`
(p. 352, folio 343) and the story headnote to `I. SASui[text-layer?] AND PUNHUN` (p. 370, no printed
number). Everything else on the twenty-five pages is English verse presented as Shah Abdul Latif's.

**The word "sur" is not printed anywhere in this chunk.** The translations are titled in English
only — `THE MARTYRS`, `THE KING AND THE MINSTREL`, `THE WANDERERS`, `THE HOLY MEN`, `THE SPINNER`,
and then the five love-cycles of Part III. The book's standing "sur" ambiguity (a section of the
Risalo vs. a chapter of the Koran) therefore does **not** arise here and cannot be resolved from
this chunk. **Neither is any chapter of the Koran named, and no Koranic citation appears.**

**No translator is named anywhere in these twenty-five pages.** This is the largest continuous block
of translated Shah Abdul Latif verse transcribed so far and it carries **no translator credit, no
source note, no acknowledgement and — a change from every earlier chunk — not a single footnote on
any page.** The standing finding is reconfirmed at its strongest point, and the consolidator should
say so.

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The chunk contains almost no authorial argument.** Sorley speaks in his own voice on only two
  pages — the two story headnotes (p. 352, folio 343; p. 370) — and both are narrative rather than
  analytical. **There is no chapter argument, no thesis and no comparison with another literature
  anywhere in these twenty-five pages**, which is itself a fact the consolidator needs: the book has
  moved from commentary to primary text and the change happened before p. 348.
- **Sorley's one interpretive claim about the Minstrel poems is that they are about a transaction.**
  "The pbems[text-layer?] ·of '.The · King. and The. 1'4.instrel[text-layer?] ' are
  co:n,cerned[text-layer?] with the imisician's[text-layer?] visit to "the palace of Diach, the
  singing and the playing, t�e'[text-layer?] chaffering_ for -the musician's reward ·and the · grief
  of Sor�th[text-layer?]" (p. 352, folio 343). **Four subjects, and the third is "the chaffering …
  for the musician's reward" — Sorley's own word for the bargaining. Compare chunk 016's reading of
  the same poems (p. 250, folio 241), where the subject is "the mystery that brings the music
  listener into communion with the divine". The two descriptions are not incompatible but they point
  in different directions, and the archive should print both.**
- **Sorley's one interpretive claim about the Sasui poems is that they are narrowly scoped.** "The
  poems of ' Sasui and Punhiin[text-layer?] ' deal only with the events that happened when Sasui
  realized that the camels had gone and that Punhiin[text-layer?] had departed from Bhambh6r[text-layer?]."
  (p. 370) **A restriction on the cycle's scope, stated flatly and without evidence.**
- **Sorley recognises a variant tradition and uses it to explain an allusion.** "The allusions to
  Sasui passing her time with washermen relate to a version of the story that 'Sasui spent some time
  washing clothes and that in Punhiin's[text-layer?] courting of her he also had helped to wash
  clothes by her side amongst the washermen." (p. 370) **"a version of the story" is the same
  variant-awareness chunk 016 recorded as "the topography of the story is most confused" (p. 257,
  folio 248), applied here to incident rather than place.**
- **Sorley supplies a key to the poems' ethnonyms, which is an editorial rather than a historical
  claim.** "People of Kech are called Kechis and as Kech is in Baliichistan[text-layer?] they are
  referred to as Baliichi[text-layer?] or Barach in the poems. As Punhiin[text-layer?] was the son of
  Ari he is said to belong to the Ariyani family or tribe. The poet calls the Ariyanis by their name
  of Arichas also." (p. 370) **This is Sorley telling the reader how to decode the verse, and it
  presupposes the geographical identification of Kech that chunk 016 left open.**
- **The arrangement of the translations is itself an editorial act, and the chunk states no principle
  for it.** The poems are grouped into numbered sections with English titles and no sur names, and
  Part III is given the title `LOVE` (p. 368, folio 359). **Nothing in the chunk explains the
  grouping, the ordering, the choice of English titles or the decision to drop the sur names** —
  **(not stated)**. **Given that chunk 016's commentary chapter cited this same material by sur
  title, the divergence is a finding for the consolidator, not a conclusion to be drawn here.**
- **The headnote's ruler is a woman, stated without comment.** "In due course she became herself the
  ruler of Bham­bhor." (p. 370) **Sorley records it flatly and makes nothing of it.**
