## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- **Rai Rochaldas / Sai Rochaldas Sahib** — the first of the book's three Hindu Sufi figures, from Rohri.
  He "had not planned to leave Sindh before the January riots" and "migrated due to the violence of 6
  January 1948" (p. 70, folio 57); "In the first months after partition, Rochaldas was working as a medical
  doctor in the sanatorium he had created in Karachi. But during the dramatic events of 6 January, his son
  Hari was threatened with death and his house was looted." (p. 70, folio 57) He created "not … a darbar
  there, but rather a dispensary, as well as in Karachi and Hyderabad" (p. 78, folio 65). **His birth and

- **Nimano Faqir** — the second figure, a woman. Migration date "not really known, but it could have been

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The transfer mechanism, stated as a rule.** "In the context of migration of Sindhi religious figures to
  India, the transference of a Sindhi darbar usually follows the migration of its gaddi nashin." (p. 68,
  folio 55, with an endnote 3 glued to "nashin") "Hence, a number of important darbars of Sindh were
  recreated in India by the gaddi nashins who had migrated." (p. 68, folio 55)
- **Remittance custodianship across the border, and the border itself as passable.** "The darbars in Sindh
  were most of the time preserved with different arrangements regarding their management. But, often, the now
  Indian gaddi nashins or their followers would provide money to some keepers who were in charge of
  maintaining the initial darbar. Furthermore, despite the fluctuant diplomatic relations between Pakistan and
  India, it was always possible to travel across the border." (p. 68, folio 55) **No sums, no periodicity, no
  keeper named: (not stated).**
- **Land granted by rulers to a darbar, undated and unquantified.** Of Baba Sukhdev Sahib at Halani: "His
  spiritual level impressed a lot the Talpur mirs who regularly granted the darbar with lands." (p. 68,
  folio 55) **No acreage, no revenue figure, no sanad: (not stated).**
- **A gaddi nashin's migration and a flat bought as a second centre.** "In 1947, the gaddi nashin decided to
  migrate to India and he settled in Ajmer, in Rajasthan. Once, when he had to go to Mumbai for a surgery, he
  bought a flat which became another spiritual centre." (p. 68, folio 55)
- **Legitimation by print and by chart.** Official narratives "are usually published in books written both in
  Devanagari and in Arabic Sindhi scripts, and of course in the Sindhi language. The legitimation also works
  through the genealogical charts exhibited in the main entrance of the darbars. Additionally, the iconography
  provides a sense of continuity, contrary to architecture." (pp. 68–69, folios 55–56)
- **Branch relations, good or dead.** "Sometimes, the Indian darbar is seen as a branch of the initial one
  located in Sindh. The relationship between them can be good, or sometimes non-existent. But despite this
  situation, the sanctuaries claim to belong to the same tradition." (pp. 69–70, folios 56–57)
- **A Sufi lexicon kept alive on posters.** "The permanence of the Sufi reference can be observed through the
  Sindhi vernacular vocabulary that is used on the posters. Some words, such as shahanshah and darvish, are
  very common, and there is also the faqeeri dhamal." (p. 70, folio 57) The gaddi nashin's title is "'Hazra
  Hazoor Darvish Sai Parmanand Sahab'. Except for his name of Parmanand, all of the other terms are borrowed
  from the Sufi lexicon in Sindhi." (p. 70, folio 57)
- **None of the three principals held an institution in Sindh.** "none of the three Sufi figures focused upon
  here did run a darbar, or even an astano, in Sindh. They were themselves the followers of a Sufi Master, who
  was always Muslim." (p. 70, folio 57)
- **piri-muridi as the surviving institution, against the tariqa.** "The close relationship known in the Sufi
  context as piri-muridi, or the relation between Master and disciple, was never broken. Obviously, the
  legitimizing link was formed more through an individual, a Master, rather than through an institution, such
  as a tariqa, the name given to an institutionalized Sufi order." (p. 73, folio 60)
- **The cross-border visit as the shrine's charter event, and baraka as a ranked quantity.** "the most
  spectacular being the visit of the Pakistani murshid to his now Indian Hindu followers. The climax of the
  relation could be for the very foundation of the Indian darbar, or for the dastarbandi, or coronation, of a
  successor after a deceased Master in India." Otherwise the murshid came "only for him to offer a blessing,
  or if a neologism is relevant here, to 'baraka-ize' the new shrine. In the spiritual hierarchy, the Master of
  the original shrine located in Sindh would always be considered as owning more baraka than the head of the
  Indian shrine." (p. 73, folio 60)
- **Succession by turban, dated twice.** "the coronation ceremony is performed when a new gaddi nashin is
  enthroned: it is named with the Sufi lexicon of dastarbandi, the coronation with the turban, the dastar, or
  sometimes pag, which is a symbol of the spiritual power." (p. 73, folio 60) "the sajjada nashin of Jahaniyya,
  Nur Husayn Shah, who is still the Master of the silsilo, came to Ulhasnagar in 1957 to tie a turban of ochre
  cloth around Hari's head (Gajwani 2000: 199)." (p. 73, folio 60) For 1980: "'He (Nur Husayn Shah) first
  applied the tilak on the forehead of Sri Damodar, tied dastar (folded sheet of ochre cloth) around his head
  and then blessed him. … Thus Sri H. M. Damodar was formally consecrated to the spiritual seat after Dada Sai
  (Dr) R. M. Hari in the lineage of Sai Rochaldas Sahib' (Gajwani 2000: 294–5)." (p. 73, folio 60) **The
  ellipsis is the book's own. Gajwani "does not refer to a dastarbandi of Rochaldas" himself (p. 73,
  folio 60).**
- **A Muslim custodian performing a Hindu act, and a Hindu funeral on the twelfth day.** "while the ceremony
  is Muslim Sufi, so to say, the Hindu belonging of the `gadi nashin` [text-layer? gaddi nashin] is exhibited
  through the application of the tilak, the red dot on the forehead. And it was not a Hindu priest who put it
  on the front, but the Muslim sajjada nashin, Nur Husayn Shah. Furthermore, the funeral rituals performed
  before the `dastarband` [text-layer? dastarbandi] were all Hindu, and specifically Sindhi Hindu, done on the
  twelfth day after the death instead of on the thirteenth day as they are performed in most Hindu
  communities." (p. 74, folio 61) "Nonetheless, this very Sufi ceremony is not regularly performed in the two
  other Hindu Sufi paths." (p. 74, folio 61) An annual repetition: "the dastarbandi is performed every year
  when R. M. Hari's versi is celebrated, but not by Nur Husayn Shah." (p. 73, folio 60)
- **A shrine's relocations and franchises, dated.** "In 1999, a new location was chosen where a new building
  was soon established with the name of 'Nimanal Sangam'. Another place was inaugurated in Bombay, and then in
  the 2000s, in Hong Kong and Malaga, Spain." (p. 74, folio 61)
- **A daily liturgy.** "kalams written by Sindhi Sufis like Sachal Sarmast are still sung every day" (p. 74,
  folio 61); "Nimanal Sangam receives devotees daily, while the Dada Sain Kuthir is mostly invested in by
  pilgrims for the anniversary of the death (versi) of Mulchand Faqir and Gehimal." (p. 82, folio 69)
- **A date turned into a calendar and an inscription.** "one of the biggest annual celebrations happens on 24
  March, the day after the anniversary of his death. It is said that, in March of 1963, while sitting in the
  middle of a group of disciples, Nimano asked that a devotional musical session, known as satsang, be
  organized on the 24th of each month. She died on the 23rd following that day, and her funeral was organized
  for the 24th. This figure then became sacred, and an inscription above the entrance of the darbar reads: 24
  ware sai ki jai." (pp. 74–75, folios 61–62) **The book writes "his death" of Nimano here and "she" in the
  next two sentences; recorded as printed, not reconciled.**
- **Initiation as the giving of a name.** "Gehimal finally 'took nam' (nam vathan) from Mulchand." (p. 75,
  folio 62) "Nimano would have designated Nirmal Jani to give the name (nam) for initiation" (p. 74,
  folio 61). "In India, he got authorization from Mulchand, through a letter, to initiate disciples since
  Mulchand did not want him to bring them to Sehwan." (p. 82, folio 69) "his disciple and successor Gehimal
  initiated three ladies: Sonu Jethwani, Devi Naik and Gopi Gurbuxani. The first one was living in Delhi, and
  the two others in suburbs of Bombay." (p. 82, folio 69)
- **A versi begun while its subject was alive.** "In 1953, Gehimal started the first celebration of Mulchand's
  versi, when he was still alive." (p. 82, folio 69) And after: "when the first versi of Mulchand was
  celebrated in 1972 in the Kuthiyya, it was in an open air since no building had been constructed yet."
  (p. 83, folio 70) "It was decided that the versi of Mulchand and that of Gehimal would be celebrated every
  year. Satsangs would be performed every Friday evening." (p. 83, folio 70)
- **Land acquisition with a price, the chunk's only cash figure.** "Gehimal had gotten land in Ulhasnagar from
  one Shri Vasumal. A 'Sufi faqir' had been living there, but after Shri Vasumal's death, his family sold the
  land to Gehimal for 2,500 rupees." (pp. 82–83, folios 69–70) "Gehimal started to build a wall surrounding
  the land, and the building's foundations for the darbar named Mul Kuthiyya." (p. 83, folio 70) **The "gotten
  … from" and the sale for 2,500 rupees sit in consecutive sentences; both recorded.**
- **Congregational funding and a duty to build.** "The building of the darbar was funded by the three female
  followers of Gehimal – namely Sonu, Gopi and Devi – and also Tirthdas, his brother who was living in
  Ulhasnagar. His own followers had the duty to build the darbar." (p. 83, folio 70)
- **Siting a shrine by the catchment of its followers.** "The Mul Kutiyya was located in Ulhasnagar because
  most of the followers were there, in an area known as 'Sehwani Paro', the 'Sehwani area' where a number of
  Sehwani Thakurs were settled." (p. 83, folio 70, with an endnote 6 glued to "settled")
- **A shrine's fabric at its poorest, and a kitchen as the irreducible institution.** "A very small structure
  was built, and it was consequently called kutiyya, a Sindhi word meaning a cottage or a hut. No facilities
  were provided, such as toilets or electricity. A kitchen was nevertheless built since the sharing of food is
  a primary element of religious rituals in the Indian subcontinent." (p. 83, folio 70)
- **Institutional failure, domestication, and sale.** "After Gehimal's death, his brother Tirthdas Motwani
  took care of the Mul Kutiyya, and after his death, it quickly appeared that nobody among the followers was
  able to manage the darbar. But due to family duties, and because they lived in Delhi, far away from
  Ulhasnagar, Sonu and Basant started to celebrate the versis in their home in New Delhi. After some years,
  the three main followers – Sonu, Basant and Devi (Gopi had passed away in the meanwhile) – decided to sell
  the darbar." (pp. 83–84, folios 70–71) Elsewhere the same event is called "the closure of Mul Kutiyya in
  Ulhasnagar" (p. 76, folio 63).
- **A succession split into two branches.** "After Gehimal's demise, it seems that two branches tried to
  manage Mulchand's legacy. The first one is related to what Max Weber coined 'hereditary charisma': Gehimal's
  family." (p. 75, folio 62) "The second branch is that of Basant Jethwani, the head of the followers who are
  not Gehimal's family members." (p. 76, folio 63)
- **A domestic shrine in a basement, dated to the fieldwork.** Tirathdas's son Daulatram "is in the process of
  building a new Mul Kutiyya in the basement of his building. As we saw previously, the shrine was organized
  and ready for the versi for his death anniversary on 20 November 2012. But it is a modest structure, mostly
  arranged through the portraits put on the samadhi. Nonetheless, the new Mul Kutiyya could work as an
  alternative to the distant darbar in Haridwar." (p. 76, folio 63)
- **A new darbar sited on the Ganges because the Indus was lost.** "The location of the new darbar is
  explained by Basant: 'We don't have the Sindhu anymore, so we are now going to the Ganga.' The new darbar
  was inaugurated on 5 April for Mulchand's birth anniversary." (p. 77, folio 64) **No year for the
  inauguration: (not stated).**
- **Naming as the first act of sacralization, with the plate as the only exterior marker.** "all the three
  darbars do not exhibit on the outside that they are religious, or even spiritual buildings. No such markers
  are displayed: no dome, no shikara, nor a gurudwara like facade. The only element to inform passer-by about
  the nature of the building is a plate." (pp. 77–78, folios 64–65) The scripts differ by shrine: "In
  Rochaldas's darbar, the text is written in two alphabets: Arabic-Sindhi and Devanagari. In Nimano's, it is
  in Devanagari and Roman, while in Mulchand's darbar located in the sacred Hindu city of Haridwar, it is only
  in Devanagari." (p. 78, folio 65)
- **'Rohri dham' and the fifth dham.** "The text of Rochaldas's darbar states 'Rohri dham' – the abode of
  Rohri, or seat of Rohri." (p. 78, folio 65) "there is a reference to the Hindu ideology of the char dham,
  the four sacred places a Hindu is supposed to visit… as if Rochaldas had wished to locate his darbar in the
  sacred geography of India, in adding a fifth dham, the 'Rohri dham', as well as an extension of his lost
  native country." (p. 78, folio 65) "While the word darbar was currently used in conversations, Rai Rochaldas
  obviously discarded its use in favour of the official inscription" (p. 78, folio 65).
- **The Haridwar inscription and the reordering of two masters.** "the inscription is given in Devanagari:
  'Om - Dada Sain kutir'. Interestingly, the Sindhi word kuthiyya has been removed in favour of kutir, a Hindi
  word, and the names of the venerated murshids are not displayed." (p. 79, folio 66) "dada, which is in
  Sindhi paternal grandfather (or dado), a nickname which is given to any elder, and sain, which is Master in
  Sindhi." (p. 79, folio 66) "One can wonder why Dada, who is Gehimal, precedes Sain that is Mulchand, when it
  is known that Mulchand was Gehimal's murshid. It was decided by Sonu, Basant's wife, and her explanation was
  that she had never met Mulchand, while Gehimal had been her murshid. She was given nam by him." (p. 79,
  folio 66)
- **The dome as the absent identity marker.** "if a specific feature was to be mentioned for Sufi shrines, it
  would be the dome (gunbaz), although this is not even restricted to the Sufi shrine, but can be used for any
  tomb. The gunbaz is said to represent the heaven and, in South Asia, it is an identity marker that allows
  somebody to immediately identify a building as Muslim, despite a few exceptions." (p. 79, folio 66, with an
  endnote 5 glued to "exceptions")
- **Rochaldas's darbar, described in full.** "set up on a pretty large field in Shantinagar, an area located
  close to the main Kalyan-Badalpur road that divides Ulhasnagar in two parts, north and south… The darbar of
  Rochaldas was built in the 1950s in a sparsely populated neighbourhood of Ulhasnagar in Shantinagar. It is
  surrounded by an enclosure wall about two meters high. The garden is well maintained which offers an
  appreciable shade in warm season." (p. 80, folio 67) "it does not belong to any religious tradition –
  neither Sufi, nor Hindu, nor Sikh – and includes no artefact allowing outsiders to know it is a sacred
  place." (p. 80, folio 67) "The buildings are built of cement and covered with a sloping tiled roof. The most
  impressive building is the multi storey gaddi nashin residence, which is extended by a hall that houses the
  communal kitchen (langar). The gaddi nashin's flat stands in the top floor." (p. 80, folio 67) **The langar
  is named once and never described: no scale, no funding, no schedule (not stated).**
- **The gaddi nashin's flat, itemised without comment.** "Crossing the entrance door, the visitor enters a
  huge room with, in the centre, a built-in pool. Above the pool, there are rocks with, in the middle, a small
  statue of Shiva in the position of the mahayogi. It is a quite modern flat with a bar, offering a saloon
  with comfortable armchairs. There are also paintings of mountain scenes showing the Alps, as well as a
  statue of young girl which looks like an eighteenth-century European sculpture. There is also a vase with
  colourful flowers." (p. 80, folio 67)
- **Pilgrim accommodation, and the two ritual foci.** "Other buildings accommodate pilgrims. On the religious
  level, the two most significant elements are the alam and the samadhi. The alam is the name given to a
  medium-sized space near the main entrance. This term of Arabic origin is used for metonymy and signifies the
  whole space." (p. 80, folio 67) **No bed count, no charge, no visitor number: (not stated).**
- **The samadhi as sanctum, deliberately bare.** "In the darbar, the sanctum sanctorum is made up of the
  samadhi. It is a relatively large room accessed by a staircase. What is striking is the great sobriety of
  the place, since the darbar is devoid of any inscription and any decorative element… the samadhi proper is
  an altar that houses several images of divinities and spiritual masters, both Hindu and Muslim. Epigraphic
  discourse is almost non-existent, apart from inscriptions that make iconography intelligible." (p. 81,
  folio 68) "It is similar to the Nimanal Sangam, its annex of Bombay, and the Dada Sain Kuthir of Haridwar."
  (p. 81, folio 68)
- **The three darbars compared.** "The darbar of Rochaldas is the most spacious. The other two are limited to
  a single building, which consists mainly of two parts: the place where the gaddi nashin and the pilgrims
  stay on the one hand, and the darbar proper on the other… the Nimanal Sangam is more supplied. The altar
  which the devotees face is composed of several elements, including a kind of throne which is intended for
  the gaddi nashin… In the three cases studied, the darbar proper consists of a large room which is relatively
  undecorated." (p. 82, folio 69)
- **A shrine with no photographic record of its first state.** "The two others, Mulchand's one in Haridwar and
  Nimano's one in Vadodara, were rebuilt. Thus, I do not know how the first versions of their shrines appeared
  since I was not able to find any photograph." (pp. 79–80, folios 66–67) Figure 2.4 is captioned "External
  view of two Indian darbars, Haridwar and Vadodara. (a) The Dada Sain Kuthir, Haridwar, 2012 and (b) The
  Nimanal Sangam, Vadodara, 2012." (p. 83, folio 70)
- **Sikh buildings as the deliberate contrast.** "the gurdwaras, in Vadodara, in Pune or in `Ulhasnagari`
  [text-layer? Ulhasnagar], openly exhibit a 'Sikh' identity, with a characteristic facade, towers and
  sculpture. The same observation can be made of the extensions of the non-Sufi darbars, in India as well as
  overseas." (p. 85, folio 72)
- **Towers as claims to dominance.** "we mentioned the bell tower for the Christians, the minar for the
  Muslims, and the shikara for the Hindus… more pragmatically, it is a way for claiming supremacy and thus
  domination. It is not a coincidence that, in some countries, the non-dominant religious buildings cannot be
  adorned with a tower." (p. 84, folio 71) **No country is named: (not stated).**
- **Communal violence and house-seizure as the mechanism of migration.** "the spread of violence throughout
  Sindh was not perpetrated by the Muslim Sindhis, but by the Mohajirs who clearly wanted to take over the
  houses of the Hindus, at least in Karachi. Even before the riots, a number of cases are known where Mohajirs
  took a house by force while its owners had left for work (Zamindar 2007: 67)." (pp. 70–71, folios 57–58) "a
  number of Hindus had sent their wives and children to India already, while they were staying in Sindh."
  (p. 71, folio 58)
- **The Sehwani literary establishment.** "The Nawanis, his family, had also counted among them a number of
  literati who had been at the vanguard of the literary renaissance of nineteenth-century Sindh." (p. 71,
  folio 58)
- **Forward reference to the ritual chapters.** "the next chapter will examine the making of the mystical
  space of the darbar, which is a virtual space built throughout the singing of Sufi poetry. It is thus after
  exposing this capital framing process that the daily rituals and the annual fairs will be examined."
  (p. 85, folio 72) **The daily rituals and annual fairs themselves are not described in this chunk.**

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **A dream instruction from Lal Shahbaz Qalandar that produced a built structure and an annual urs**,
  attributed by the book to Rochaldas without a source: the alam "was added to the darbar after Rochaldas had
  a dream in which Lal Shahbaz Qalandar asked him to celebrate his urs every year, as it was celebrated at
  Sehwan Sharif in Sindh." (p. 81, folio 68) **No date for the dream: (not stated).**
- **Dream-obedience as the generic legitimating story**, given by the book as a category rather than a case:
  "regarding the latter, a frequent matter of discourse is that somebody had obeyed a superior being, most
  often having been met in a dream." (p. 72, folio 59)
- **A body buried sitting in salt so that only the skeleton would remain.** "According to his will, Mulchand
  was neither burnt nor buried in the ground. His body was placed in the tomb in a sitting position, thanks to
  a small door which has been placed. Mulchand was buried in the sitting position, which is said to be the
  meditating position, with lots of salt-balls so that his flesh melted away and the skeleton remained."
  (p. 77, folio 64) The practice is named by an informant: "A leading follower of Mulchand and Gehimal, Basant
  Jethwani, claims that this tradition is called sami." (p. 77, folio 64)
- **Gorakhnath as an avatar of Shiva**, attributed and not asserted: the jogis "are the followers of
  Gorakhnath, who is believed to be an avatar of Shiva." (p. 77, folio 64)
- **Baraka transmitted by a visit**, the book's own coinage flagged as such: the Pakistani murshid might come
  "only for him to offer a blessing, or if a neologism is relevant here, to 'baraka-ize' the new shrine."
  (p. 73, folio 60)

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The chunk's central thesis: the Hindu Sufi darbar is an artefact of Partition, not a transplant.** "none
  of the three Sufi figures focused upon here did run a darbar, or even an astano, in Sindh… Consequently, it
  is obviously the migration to India that played a role in the foundation of a darbar. Thus, we can reasonably
  claim that the creation of a Sufi darbar was an answer given in the context of a traumatic event, that of
  partition followed by migration." (p. 70, folio 57) Boivin at once widens it: "Nevertheless, one can claim
  this scenario was restricted to the Hindu Sufis: the transference of the above-mentioned darbars answered the
  same issue." (p. 70, folio 57)
- **Three cases, three different causes, against a single migration narrative.** "all three figures focused
  upon here belong to three different scenarios: Rochaldas migrated due to the violence of 6 January 1948;
  Nimano Faqir apparently migrated due to her Sufi Master's request; and Mulchand Faqir did not migrate, while
  his main disciple Gehimal Motwani did. While they belonged to well-to-do Hindu families of Sindh as part of
  the emerging middle classes, migration does not seem to have been a crucial issue." (p. 70, folio 57)
- **Boivin adjudicates between two informant narratives, showing his reasoning.** On why Nimano left: "Since
  it is said that she asked permission of her Master, and knowing that none of them had ever asked any other
  followers to perform the same duty, it is reasonable to think the first narrative is the most probable."
  (p. 72, folio 59) And on Baroda: "Here, the answer is consistent: because there was an important group of
  followers of the Daraza silsila" (p. 72, folio 59).
- **The Sufi life as sealed off from professional and domestic life.** "The divergence between Mulchand and
  Gehimal regarding migration provides an interesting information: in fact, the Sufi life did not spill over
  into professional and private life. Also, the geographical closeness between a Master and his disciple was
  not at all necessary." (p. 71, folio 58)
- **Legitimacy carried by a person, not an institution**, and the Master always left behind in Pakistan. "All
  in all, the authority exercised by a migrant Sufi was always legitimized by the Master of the founder of the
  Indian shrine, and in all of the three cases examined here, the Master had stayed in Pakistan. For the three
  Hindu Sufis, the Master was a Muslim, and thus he had no reason to migrate to India." (p. 77, folio 64)
- **An explicit statement of evidentiary poverty.** "Unfortunately, only oral tradition can help us to
  understand and examine the process of authority and legitimacy, and no other trace of the same has remained,
  such as might be seen in documents or private archives related to the construction of the shrines." (p. 77,
  folio 64)
- **Naming as sacralization.** "Consequently, we can state that naming a place is the first step in its
  sacralization." (p. 78, folio 65) And the name records a doctrinal shift: "Consequently, there is a shifting
  from the Muslim Master to the Hindu follower." (pp. 78–79, folios 65–66) "The two names are preceded by om,
  the sacred Hindu syllable, which gives a final Hindu touch to the `kutīr` [text-layer? kutir]." (p. 79,
  folio 66)
- **Against a Sufi architectural style; for a Sufi organization of space.** "In terms of architecture, it is
  hazardous to speak of a Sufi style. The Sufi shrines were built in innumerable architectural styles, which
  reflect local and historical styles from all over the Muslim world. Thus, more than an architectural style,
  what gives a Sufi shrine a specificity is how the space is organized." (p. 79, folio 66) And: "the spatial
  structures of the darbars do not correspond to a single matrix. This depends on the specific use of each
  darbar." (p. 82, folio 69)
- **Architectural invisibility read as a minority strategy, and the reading offered as provisional.** "Of
  course, such a policy is questionable" (p. 84, folio 71); "the first explanation coming to mind is that the
  somewhat 'ambiguous' religious identity of the darbar does not allow for a full exposure in the late
  twentieth century and early twenty-first century." (p. 85, folio 72) "this is the first and more obvious
  difference between the darbar in Sindh and the darbar in India: the lack of any religious and/or spiritual
  affiliation. The existence of a darbar is known only to those who are related to it." (p. 85, folio 72)
  **The Sikh comparison is the counter-evidence Boivin himself supplies (p. 85, folio 72).**
- **The Nathpanthi hypothesis raised and then refused on a single ritual detail.** "The tradition of burying a
  saint in a sitting position is indeed that of the Nathpanthis… Though it is tempting to consider a possible
  connection, the Nathpanthis never put salt in the tomb; they do use sugar. In fact, they renounce any use of
  salt after being initiated into the path (Briggs 1998: 40). Although the role played by the Nathpanthis in
  Sindh comes in many forms, it is difficult to assume that Mulchand was in touch with them." (p. 77,
  folio 64) **A model of the book's method: an attractive identification declined for want of evidence.**
- **The darbar oriented inward.** "Consequently, the darbar in India prioritizes a local, or better an
  internal, identity. This means that the identity as represented by the mass of the followers is privileged.
  The discourse which is expressed does not aim at reaching non-followers in the area where the darbar is
  located. The planned influence of the darbar is thus quite community-based." (pp. 84–85, folios 71–72)
- **Chapter 3's programme: the mystical space as the third space.** "at least two types of spaces are framing
  the shrines being discussed here. The first type is the physical space… The second type of space is the
  symbolic space which is only created and arranged to implement a mystical representation where the sole
  purpose is to facilitate what is the final goal of the followers: the mystical meeting with the saint, and
  through him with God. In Sufi terminology, it is known as the fana fi'llah" (p. 86, folio not stated). "In
  the case of the Sufi Hindu darbars, the new political situation imposed to build structures that looked
  anonymous from the outside." (p. 86, folio not stated)
- **Three converging dynamics, and the claim that they are not specific to Sufism.** "It is the combination of
  these three components – poetry, iconography and rituals – that allows the follower to be placed in the
  mystical space of the darbar. I agree that this process is that of all religious places. The word darbar
  could be replaced by church, mosque or mandir. But the cases under study here, involving the Hindu Sufis,
  strongly highlight that a shared religious culture, here between Islam and Hinduism, has been at work for
  centuries." (p. 87, folio 74)
- **`wahdat-e wujud` as the enabling doctrine.** The Sufi poetry "contributes to the creation of an imaginal
  world of reference, as well as the ideology of the 'unity of being', wahdat-e wujud, the Persian version of
  the Arabic wahdat al-wujud, introduced as the encompassing paradigm which should be the basis of all human
  relations, and also the basis of the relationship between mankind and God." (p. 87, folio 74) It is "a
  principle without which the meeting of Sufism and Hinduism would not have been possible." (p. 87, folio 74)
- **Boivin's definition of ideology, set explicitly against Lorenzen's, and his refusal of "syncretism".** "In
  using the concept of ideology, I differ from David Lorenzen's statement, which gives ideology a broad
  meaning, and a somewhat loose one, especially when he claims that ideology is a description of the structure
  of a society (Lorenzen 1996: 3). Maybe in a more Marxist understanding, I would consider an ideology as an
  intellectual framework, involving a condition of knowledge working as a prerequisite for the implementation
  of a number of processes, including a process of merging different religious traditions not as a syncretism,
  or a hybridization, but as a new agency of its own." (p. 87, folio 74)
- **The corpus to come, and its native division.** "Different Indian Sindhi authors claim that the Sindhi Sufi
  corpus can be divided into two parts: the Bhakti corpus of Sufism and the Vedanta corpus of Sufism." (p. 87,
  folio 74) **The sentence that assesses this claim is cut off mid-clause and runs into chunk 005.**

## Doubts

- **A flat contradiction about Nimano Faqir's death year, printed twice on the same page, and not
  reconciled here.** "After Nimano's death in 1962, another woman she called her spiritual daughter was in

  (p. 74, folio 61) and "She was ten years old when Nimano died in 1963" (p. 74, folio 61), with "in March
  of 1963, while sitting in the middle of a group of disciples, Nimano asked that a devotional musical
  session… be organized on the 24th of each month. She died on the 23rd following that day" (pp. 74–75,
  folios 61–62). **Two of the four statements say 1963 and one says 1962; a consolidator must not silently
  pick one.** Note also that Mulchand's death year is 1962 (p. 71, folio 58), which is a plausible source of
  the slip but **is not offered here as a reading**.
- **The book writes "his death" of Nimano, a woman, once.** "one of the biggest annual celebrations happens
  on 24 March, the day after the anniversary of his death" (p. 74, folio 61), where every other reference on
  the page is "she" and "her". Recorded as printed; **no correction offered**, and it is unclear whether the
  celebration described is hers or a successor's.
- **Two incompatible offices for Gehimal Motwani, four pages apart.** "he was deputy collector and thus
  posted at different places across Sindh, including Sehwan Sharif" (p. 71, folio 58) against "He was posted
  twice in Sehwan as mukhthyarkar (revenue officer)" (p. 75, folio 62). Both kept side by side; the book does
  not reconcile them and **neither is preferred here**.
- **`bhit-bhit-shah` rests on the bare short name "Shah Latif" inside a list.** The chunk prints "his
  spiritual genealogy goes back to the Sufis of Sindh, including Shah Latif, Bedil, Rohal Shah and others"

  Latif Bhittai" is **my inference from the name alone and the surrounding list of Sindhi Sufi poets**; the
  book nowhere expands it in this chunk. A human should confirm before this sentence enters the Bhit entry.
- **`khatwari-darbar-shikarpur` rests on a name the book spells differently, and on the town.** The book
  prints "the Kathwaro Darbar, which was transferred from Shikarpur to Nagpur (Madhya Pradesh)" (p. 68,

  I have no basis for preferring one.** The row's principal figure is not named and no date is given. **The

- **`gorakhnath-goraknath-temple` and `tilla-jogian` are fired by one clause and share one principal

  the refused deity-name class below.
- **Seventeen Guru Nanak rows off eight words, applied as the ruling requires and flagged as a scale
