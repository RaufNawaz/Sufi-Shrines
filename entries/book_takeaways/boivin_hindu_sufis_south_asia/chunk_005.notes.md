# boivin_hindu_sufis_south_asia — chunk 005 — pp. 88–105

Continuous academic prose, an anthropologist-historian's argument about Sufi poetic corpuses and the
vocabulary of `wahdat-e wujud`, with short transliterated verse quotations and their English glosses
set as displayed lines. The chunk sits **wholly inside one chapter**, whose running-head title is
`Sufi Poetry and the Production of the Mystical Space`; it **straddles four section boundaries**. It
opens **mid-sentence** on p. 88 (folio 75) at the word "movement." — the sentence's subject is in
chunk 004 — carries the chapter's untitled discussion of Bhakti and Vedanta to the foot of p. 90
(folio 77), then opens the section `The classical Sufi corpus` (p. 90, folio 77) which runs to p. 95
(folio 82); `The modern Sufi corpus` (p. 95, folio 82) to p. 99 (folio 86); `The vernacular ideology
of the wahdat-e wujud` (p. 99, folio 86) to p. 104 (folio 91); and `Hindu references in the Sufi
poetry` (p. 104, folio 91), which is **broken off** at the end of p. 105 (folio 92) immediately after
a four-line displayed verse closing `(Sachal Sarmast 1997: 75)` — the section continues into chunk
006. The break falls after a complete quotation rather than mid-sentence, but mid-argument.

**Folio relation: every one of the eighteen pages prints a running head, every folio below was read
off its own page, and none is predicted.** The measured relation `folio = PDF page − 13` held on all
eighteen (p. 88 → 75 … p. 105 → 92) with no exception; that is recorded as the cross-check the
briefing asked for, not as a source of any citation. **No page in this chunk is cited `folio not
stated`** — there is no chapter-opening display page and no full-page illustration here. Two wrinkles
for whoever automates the header scan are set out under Doubts: the folio-first/folio-last parity
holds as briefed but pairs with the **opposite** folio parity from the briefing's worked example, and
the two running heads are the **chapter title** (folio-last pages) and the **book title** (folio-first
pages), not a `Chapter N` form.

**This chunk maps six archive rows** — `bhit-bhit-shah` and `shrine-of-sachal-sarmast` densely,
`shrine-of-shah-abdul-karim-bulri` thinly, and `mazar-e-iqbal`, `mazar-of-bulleh-shah` and
`mithankot-kot-mithan` on the author-only ruling of 18 Sep 2026. The check was run against all 169

eighteen pages**: every Sindhi, Persian and Arabic word and every verse is romanised. **It also
prints no era marker anywhere** — no `ce`, no `AD`, no `AH`; life dates are bare years in parentheses.
Both are recorded as positive findings so a later reader does not think the script or the era style

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- **Sami — Chainrai Bachomal Dattaramani (1743–1850)**, the chunk's principal Hindu Sufi poet and the
  third of the "trinity". "a Sindhi Hindu saint poet, who is known by many as a Hindu Sufi" (p. 94,
  folio 81, endnote 7); "He was born in a well-to-do family in Shikarpur. He was himself a cloth
  merchant and travelled a great deal between Shikarpur and Amritsar in Punjab. After getting married
  when he was about 30, he got in touch with Swami Meghraj, who taught him Vedanta in original Sanskrit
  for about ten years." (p. 94, folio 81) "He is the most influential Sindhi author of the Bhakti, with
  a strong devotion for the god Krishna. He was the author of a huge Sindhi poetical work known as Sami
  jo sloka, and he wrote in the Gurmukhi script, which is one of the scripts used by the Sindhi Hindus."
  (p. 94, folio 81) Doctrine: "Although the Bhakti he expressed encapsulated many similarities with the
  wahdat-e- wujud, Sami frequently referred to typical Hindu concepts, such as illusion, maya, or the
  transmigration of the soul (Sami 2002: 3–12). He mentioned quite often the Veds (Vedas) as the final
  religious scripture… He used the technical lexicon of Sufism, devoting a chapter of his sloks to ishq,
  mohabat or the faqirs." (p. 94, folio 81) "Sami was a Hindu whose poetry perfectly interweaves the
  Bhakti legacy and Sufi heritage." (p. 95, folio 82) His slokas are surmised to have been "widespread
  among the Hindus of Northern Sindh" (p. 104, folio 91). **No shrine, samadhi, darbar or death place
  is given for him: (not stated)**, and **no archive row corresponds to him**.
- **Qutub Ali Shah (1810–1910)** — the chunk's most fully described modern Sufi with a named shrine.
  "He was a famous Sufi from Hyderabad who had many followers among the Muslims and the Hindus of Sindh.
  He was a strong advocate of wahdat-e wujud, a wujudi, and his poetry is dotted with references to
  Hindu concepts and characters. For instance, he uses the word darshan for vision as much as didar.
  One of his most important followers was Rai Rochaldas. **His dargah is located in Jahaniyya, a
  neighbourhood in Hyderabad.**" (p. 95, folio 82) "Qutub Ali Shah was fond of Krishna." (p. 96,
  folio 83) He and his son Hadi Bakhsh belonged to "the Jahaniyyan lineage, whose members were the
  murshids of Rochaldas" (p. 98, folio 85). **This dargah is a named shrine with a named principal
  figure and matches no row in `shrine_index.tsv` — a candidate for a future row.** See Doubts on the
  collision between "Jahaniyya(n)" here and the archive's Uch Sharif Jahaniyan row.
- **Bedil — Faqir Qadir Bakhsh (1814–72)** — "also known as Bedil – literally, without a heart – was a
  Sufi born in Rohri, belonging to the Qadiriyya (Bedil 2012)" (p. 97, folio 84, endnote 10). "In his
  teens, he went to Sehwan Sharif, where he is reported to have been vouchsafed spiritual visions. He
  also visited Jhok Sharif and Daraza since he was a staunch follower of Sachal Sarmast. He wrote
  copiously on mystical topics in Persian, Sindhi, Siraiki and Urdu, and is the author of eighteen
  works, mainly in Persian and in Sindhi." (p. 97, folio 84) "He also wrote a commentary on Abd
  al-Qadir al-Jilani's qasidas" (p. 97, folio 84). "According to his verses, Bedil can be regarded as a
  wujudi, as he states, for example, that a dervish is neither a Shia nor a Sunni. The true Sufi is a

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Custodianship at Daraza, three sajjada nashins named.** Sachal's father "the sajjada nashin of
  Daraza, Khwaja Muḥammad Ḥafiẓ, also known as Sahiḅ Dino I"; his brother "the sajjada nashin Khwaja
  Abd al-Ḥaqq" (p. 93, folio 80); and in the present day "Sakhi Qabul Muhammad Faruqi, the sajjada
  nashin of Daraza" (pp. 96–97, folios 83–84), who "quotes, word for word, Hari's translation, after
  giving the Sindhi text (Faruqi 2002: 35–6)" (p. 97, folio 84). **The book's term is `sajjada nashin`;
  no Auqaf department, no state involvement and no appointment procedure appears anywhere in this
  chunk: (not stated).**
- **The economy of a khanaqah, and a saint who owned no land.** "Although he had never owned a piece of
  land, he was in charge of looking after the business affairs of the Sufi lodge, the khanaqah."
  (p. 93, folio 80) **The only land-and-money sentence in the chunk.** No endowment, no revenue, no
  donation figure, no offering: **(not stated).**
- **A shrine gate as a doctrinal statement.** "the inscription written on the main gate of Sachal's
  dargah is haqq mawjud" (p. 102, folio 89). Also "His dargah is located in Jahaniyya, a neighbourhood
  in Hyderabad" for Qutub Ali Shah (p. 95, folio 82). **These two are the chunk's only references to
  shrine fabric; no building date, dimension or architectural description appears: (not stated).**
- **The darbar as the unit of Hindu Sufi religious life.** "the devotees of all of the Sufis' darbars,
  Hindu and Muslim, are fond of their poetry, to the point where it is used as the devotional reservoir
  for their prayers" (p. 91, folio 78); "what is of interest here is not their literary value, but the
  success they meet in the circle of the Hindu Sufi darbars" (p. 95, folio 82); "The combination of the
  two repertoires plays a primary function in the darbars." (p. 98, folio 85)
- **Two repertoires, and an unwritten rule of attribution.** "Every path broadly uses the classical
  Sindhi Sufi poets, as well as the modern Sufi poets, but they also refer to a repertoire which arose
  inside the community. Most of the time, the poetry has been authored by the founder of the path, or
  his Master… Since a Master is supposed to provide inspiration to his murids, even if a murid composes
  Sufi poetry, it will be attributed to the Master. This untold and unwritten rule is prevalent in many
  religious traditions all over the world." (p. 98, folio 85)
- **Community autonomy against the brotherhoods, named.** "To use the community repertoire means that a
  Sufi community is autono[mous], and there is no relation with any kind of centralized institutionalized
  paths. I am thinking here of the Sufi brotherhoods that are dominant in the landscape of Sindh, such
  as the Qadiriyya or the Sohrawardiyya." (p. 98, folio 85) **The book's spellings are `Qadiriyya`,
  `Sohrawardiyya`, `Darazi silsila`, `Nanakpanth`, `sampradaya`.** "Belonging to a shared Sufi legacy of
  Sindh is implemented through the singing of pan-Sindhi kalams. But the main pattern allowing a
  delimited group to make a community is that the members are united through the worship of a Master."
  (p. 98, folio 85)
- **The ritual mechanism, stated as a sequence.** "It draws a spiritual state of mind that is an
  indispensable condition for reaching the goal: the merging of the follower with the Master, and from
  him/her to God. The singing of the Sufi poetry puts the disciple in an emotional state, which can
  hopefully lead to ecstasy, itself understood as being the threshold of the meeting with God." (p. 99,
  folio 86) "The core of the religiosity is the expression of emotion throughout the singing of poetry."
  (p. 90, folio 77)
- **The performance settings named.** "the Sufi poetry performed in the satsangs and other devotional
  events" (p. 104, folio 91); "Sufi poetry authored by non-Sindhis also played a liturgical role during
  the performances" (p. 91, folio 78); "some verses are attributed to both of them and are used as sung
  prayers during religious performances" (p. 98, folio 85). **No ʿurs, no mela, no pilgrimage
  organisation and no visitor number appears anywhere in this chunk: (not stated).**
- **The musical specification of a poem, in detail.** Qutub Ali Shah's `Sakhi sabhajal` "is to be sung in
  the sur asa purbi, sur being the Sindhi word for rag. It is an interesting association of two surs. The
  first one is named asa, probably borrowed from the Sikhs but seldom heard today. The sur asa is related
  to hope, inspiration and courage. The sur purbi, a vernacularized form of purvi, implies the follower
  to be quiet and serious, as well as mystical. This one, too, is not commonly heard today." (p. 96,
  folio 83) Form: "a short poem with a refrain (thal) and four stanzas" (p. 96, folio 83), with "Sindhi
  text with an English translation… given in the annex".
- **A poem's brevity read as an economic accommodation to a trading class.** "Above all, it summarized
  many of the current middle-class expectations, and is simultaneously well adapted to the modern life
  because of its brevity – making it fit especially well the very busy traders' way of life, as they
  cannot spent too much time in praying." (p. 96, folio 83) `spent` is as printed.
- **Initiation as the line between two audiences.** Dalpat Sufi's poem "is not devoted to the common

- **A sect-formation model, summarised from the Bhakti literature.** "a number of devotees gathered
  around a spiritual Master, and the devotees could be renunciant or housekeepers. The spiritual Master
  was himself the heir of the founder, usually an ascetic who had been granted a revelation by a god, in

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Boivin abandons his own initial terminology, and says why.** "In a first step, I decided to follow
  this cleavage in my introduction to the different Sufi corpuses as used by the Hindu Sufis. In this
  respect, I started using this terminology of 'Bhakti' and 'Vedanta', drawing especially on Motilal
  Jotwani's writing (Jotwani 1996)." (p. 88, folio 75) "Therefore, I think it is more relevant to use
  the sole term of Vedanta." (p. 90, folio 77)
- **His charge against his own source.** "But, Jotwani takes these references for granted: he never
  explains what, in his own understanding, are Bhakti and Vedanta. This suggests that the meanings are
  supposed to be already known by the reader. Nevertheless, Jotwani does not refer to the Neo-Vedanta."
  (p. 88, folio 75) And against Ajwani: "while he mentions the name of Tulsidas and Surdas, he does not
  provide any clue for evidence of the influence of these sants on the spiritual life of Sindh." (p. 90,
  folio 77)
- **The historical claim that carries the chapter.** "the historical data gives evidence that the
  late-nineteenth-century Sindhi literati were in touch with the Vedanta, more exactly the Neo- or
  Modern Vedanta, instead of any historical Bhakti movement." (p. 88, folio 75) "From the end of
  nineteenth century on, the Sindhi literati associated the wahdat-e wujud with the monism expressed by
  the Hindu school of advaita vedanta." (p. 102, folio 89)
- **Why the Sindhi literati nonetheless said "Bhakti", explained through Hawley.** "as was recently
  demonstrated by John Hawley, the idea of the Bhakti movement in early-twentieth-century India had been
  reframed by the Hindu literati in the context of the nascent nationalist movement (Hawley 2015). Since
  then, the Bhakti movement has been introduced as a social movement that aimed at unifying the Hindu
  population and instituting a kind of egalitarian society." (p. 88, folio 75)
- **The limit Boivin puts on the influence claim — an explicit refusal to conclude.** "Briefly put, they
  were both used to refer to the meeting between Sufism and Hinduism through a similarity of concepts,
  rather than providing evidence that the Muslim Sufi poets had a thorough knowledge of the Bhakti or the
  Vedanta, and thus could have been influenced by them." (p. 90, folio 77)
- **Vernacularization as the mechanism of the Hindu–Muslim meeting.** "it is thanks to the process of
  vernacularization that this concept was able to meet that of advaita, and, consequently, to facilitate
  the meeting of Hinduism and Sufism." (p. 99, folio 86) Evidence is lexicographic: `wahadata` in 1879
  means "unity, oneness, but there is no word equivalent to existence or wujud built from the Arabic
  root, WJD" (p. 99, folio 86); Lalwani 1890 has `wahadaniyyat` and `wahdat`, both "unity", and no word
  for `wujud` (p. 100, folio 87); Beg 1913 renders `wahdat` as `hekro` plus "Khoda jo Nalo, 'the name of
  God'", and also as `hekra'i` (p. 100, folio 87). "Therefore, from the late nineteenth century onwards,
  the wahdat, as a central dogma of Islam, was already vernacularized by Hindu Sindhi literati in using
  the term hekro, one." (p. 100, folio 87)
- **A negative finding Boivin states plainly.** "We have observed that, in pre-partition Sindhi authors,
  there are but few uses of words or expressions directly based on wahdat-e wujud. A leading reason is
  perhaps that another expression, haqq mawjud, was being used by the Sindhis and is very close to
  wahdat-e wujud." (p. 101, folio 88) Note the hedge "perhaps".
- **`haqq mawjud` analysed word by word, and read as interfaith work.** "in the expression haqq mawjud,
  the word wahdat is replaced by that of haqq, while the form wujud has become mawjud. 'Truth' or 'haqq'
  is one of the ninety-nine names of God… Furthermore, it is an implicit allusion to the sentence ana
  al-haqq." (p. 102, folio 89) "The word mawjud reinforces the immanence of God since, in Arabic, mawjud
  means the very presence of God. The expression hu mawjud has a similar meaning: 'He is here'."
  (p. 102, folio 89) "Nonetheless, using a new expression means a new step has been taken for
  establishing an interfaith dialogue." (p. 101, folio 88)
- **The equivalence table between Neo-Vedanta and Sufi vocabulary, twice, with different values.** Boivin
  in his own voice: "The first part, sat, stresses the divine as the Truth, as it is in haqq mawjud.
  Thus, sat corresponds to haqq. The second part, chit, roughly means consciousness, while ananda means
  bliss and happiness. Akhanda means non-divisible – that is, unity, wahdat." (p. 103, folio 90) And as
  Rochaldas set it out: "the three words making satchidananda mean existence (sat), knowledge (chit) and
  bliss (ananda). Furthermore, they are the exact equivalents of haqq (truth), ishq (love) and husn
  (beauty) (Hari 1995: 195)." (p. 104, folio 91) **`sat` is glossed "the Truth" by Boivin and "existence"
  by Rochaldas; both are recorded, neither is preferred.**
- **A limit on the Neo-Vedanta thesis, conceded.** "It is not easy to trace Neo-Vedanta in Sufi poetry."
  (p. 104, folio 91) And: "the issue of wahdat-e wujud was addressed by many other scholars who were
  specialists of Sindhi poetry, but they have hardly been able to explain it" (p. 101, folio 88).
- **The classical/modern distinction defined, and its basis disclaimed.** "The word 'modern' is
  principally employed in opposition to that of classical. It also refers to another period of Sufi
  poetry, although the chronology of the Sufis' lives can be seen to overlap. The main distinction
  between the classical and modern Sufi poetry is a shifting in the dominant literary forms, and above
  all in the themes addressed by the poets." (p. 95, folio 82) The classical corpus is defined
  functionally: "The classical corpus is simultaneously a pan-Sindhi repertoire… some Sindhi poets are
  much acclaimed and… the devotees of all of the Sufis' darbars, Hindu and Muslim, are fond of their
  poetry" (p. 91, folio 78); and formally, "because the literary forms they used, as well as the motifs
  they dealt with, became the patterns for the following authors" (p. 91, folio 78).
- **Literary merit set aside as irrelevant to the question.** Quoting Ajwani on the modern poets, "'As
  poets, however, they cannot be placed in the front rank' (Ajwani 1970: 154). Obviously, what is of
  interest here is not their literary value, but the success they meet in the circle of the Hindu Sufi
  darbars." (p. 95, folio 82)
- **What the classical corpus shares, stated as three features.** "The classical Sufi corpus is praised by
  the Hindu Sufis since such poetry is made of two meaningful features: the use of Sindhi local figures
  and themes, and the importance given to music. But above all, they all claim the unity of God."
  (p. 94, folio 81)
- **Shackle's typology, reported and left standing.** "In his chapter on the Siraiki mystical poetry of
  Sindh, mostly based on Sachal and Bedil, Christopher Shackle identifies three styles: Islamic, Persian
  and local. Regarding the use of 'Hindu ideas and practices', he claims they are paradoxically a
  variation within the Islamic style, arguing that 'the paradox was, after all, admirably designed to
  fulfil the poets' attempts to express the insights of a pure monotheism' (Shackle 1981: 255–6)."
  (p. 104, folio 91)
- **Boivin's programme for the section the chunk breaks off in.** "the main aim here is to find out if a
  typology of these Sufi poets can be drawn" (p. 104, folio 91); "focus on deciphering whether a special
  category of Sindhi Sufis is praised by the Hindu Sindhis or not… We will try to scrutinize how they
  deal with Hindu figures and/or Hindu concepts and notions." (p. 105, folio 92) "Three categories of
  references can be found in Sindhi Sufi poetry authored by these poets: figures, gods and
  notions/concepts." (p. 105, folio 92)
- **Why Muslim Sufi poets used Hindu references, in Boivin's reading.** "The main purposes of the poets,
  when referring to Hindu elements, are to show there is no difference between Muslims and Hindus, and
  even to provide evidence that there are equivalencies. Such Muslim practice is equivalent to such
  Hindu practice. Nonetheless, the paradigm of Sufism is renunciation, and the jogi is the pattern of the
  renunciant." (p. 105, folio 92)
- **Three explicit disclaimers of competence or scope, worth recording as a pattern.** "Of course, this
