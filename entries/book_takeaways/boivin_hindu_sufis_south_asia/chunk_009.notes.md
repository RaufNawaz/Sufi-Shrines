## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Custodianship at Sehwan, in four named offices.** The head of the Lakkiyyaris, Murad Shah, eldest son
  of Gul Muhammad Lakkiyyari, leading the first mehndi (pp. 181, 183, folios 168, 170); Ramchand, son of
  Mohan Lal, leading the second since 1981 (p. 183, folio 170); Lal Gul, "alleged son" of Lal Das,
  leading the third since the Kanungos' migration in 1981 (pp. 181, 183, folios 168, 170); and, in the
  figure caption alone, "Asan Gul, a Hindu sajjada nashin in Sehwan Sharif" (p. 182, folio 169).
- **State control of the shrine, dated to the 1960s, and the Awqaf named seven pages later.** "the shrine
  itself came under government control in the 1960s" (p. 180, folio 167); "It proceeds according to the
  document of the Awqaf manager in relation to the procession of the matamis." (p. 183, folio 170) **The
  two statements are never joined by the book, and no department, officer, revenue or property is named:
  (not stated).**
- **A ritual protocol imposed by the State, and unchanged by partition but for one custom.** "These
  positions, be they honorary or real, can be watched during the implementation of a protocol imposed by
  the State. This protocol, as well as the distribution of tasks between confessions, has not undergone
  fundamental change since the partition, with one exception." (p. 181, folio 168)
- **The ʿurs dated to three consecutive days of Shaban.** "on the 18th of Shaban, it is the procession
  led by the Lakiyyaris, on the 19th it is the one led by Ramchand and on the 20th is that of Lal Gul."
  (p. 181, folio 168) **The year is (not stated); the month is lunar and no Gregorian equivalent is
  given.**
- **A hereditary ritual duty transferred twice within twenty years, with both transfers dated.** "After
  the death of Mulchand Faqir in 1962, the duty of the mehndi was transmitted to his cousin, Mohan Lal,
  who then passed it on to his son, Ramchand, in 1981." (p. 183, folio 170)
- **A migration that vacated an office, and the office refilled from outside Sindh.** "The Kanungos
  migrated to India in the same year and the burden fell to Lal Das, a Hindu from Kashmir." (p. 183,
  folio 170)
- **A kafi doing three jobs at once: tomb, community worship and cremation ground.** "Although Hindus are
  very few in number in Sehwan today, it should be noted that Mulchand Kafi works to a certain extent as
  a place of community worship… certain ceremonies of the cycle of life are always realized there…
  Additionally, the kafi shelters the cremation ground of the Hindus of Sehwan." (p. 180, folio 167)
- **A tomb described by measurement against an ordinary grave, with nothing written on it.** "In its
  centre is a brick construction that is the qabr (tomb) of Mulchand Faqir. This construction is four or
  five times the size of an ordinary grave. No decoration or inscription appears, but it is covered, as
  is the tradition, with a chador." (p. 180, folio 167)
- **A shared shrine with two custodianships, two fairs and regular disputes.** "The annual fair is
  performed separately, and there is a gaddi nashin for the Hindus and a sajjada nashin for the Muslims.
  Such a scenario should not be idealized since the groups interact very little, and many disputes arise
  regularly." (p. 178, folio 165) This is Udero Lal.
- **A Hindu-managed Sufi darbar, with architecture and inscription carrying the Hindu identity.** "the
  shrine is managed by Hindus claiming to belong to the Rajputs. These would be the descendants of
  Pithoro's brother, since he was himself a renunciant who never married… the darbar exhibits a Hindu
  identity in the architecture and inscriptions. The 'Sufi' shaped tomb of Pithoro Pir is surmounted by
  a huge Om. There is also in the courtyard an altar devoted to Shiva." (pp. 178–179, folios 165–166)
- **A ritual dropped in one place and kept in another, both named.** "the dhamal was stopped once
  Mulchand's shrine was settled in the classic Hindu pilgrimage city of Haridwar. But in the
  Sindhi-dominated environment of Ulhasnagar, it is still performed in Rochaldar's [text-layer?
  Rochaldas's] darbar." (p. 176, folio not stated) **The date of the stopping is (not stated).**
- **The daily and annual rhythm of Hindu attendance at a Muslim tomb.** "The Hindus belonging to
  different paths and castes come every day and for the annual urs. They go to the tomb, circumambulating
  around it, touching it, and then attend the Shah jo rag, the singing of Shah's poetry by the local
  faqirs." (p. 178, folio 165)
- **A pre-partition Hindu ritual office grounded in a caste monopoly on a dye.** "The Khuhnbatis, as
  saffron specialists, were attributed a priestly function in the ceremony of the mystical nuptials of
  Lal Shahbaz Qalandar." (p. 181, folio 168) "In the traditional marriage of the Daryapanthis, one of the
  preparatory ceremonies of marriage is known as the khuhnbo ceremony (Thakur 1959: 176). A few days
  before marriage, members of the caste as well as seven women who are happily married (sohagan, which
  means they are not widows) prepare the saffron colour to dye ceremonial wedding clothes." (pp. 181–182,
  folios 168–169)
- **A single room serving three institutional functions by rotation — the chunk's sharpest fact on
  refugee precariousness.** "The book also provides interesting information about the precariousness in
  which the Sindhi refugees lived after their migration. Rochaldas had a single room in Ulhasnagar, its
  function changing according to the different parts of the day and the night. At some times, it was a
  free dispensary, and then it would be turned into a spiritual place where satsangs were organized, and
  later on it would become a dormitory." (p. 165, folio 152)
- **Initiation named in Sindhi.** "how he was initiated by him (nam vathan) (Motwani 1965: 35–45)."
  (p. 167, folio 154)
- **A dastarbandi performed by a custodian travelling from Pakistan, twice, with both years given.**
  "Gajwani underscores the fact that both had the dastarbandi ceremony performed by the sajjada nashin
  of Qutub Ali Shah's dargah from Hyderabad. The Sufi pir came for it from Pakistan twice, in 1957 and in
  1980." (p. 167, folio 154) **The sajjada nashin is not named and the visa question is not raised here:
  (not stated).**
- **The malfuzat as an institution of transmission, counted.** "In classical Sufism, it is a very common
  literary tradition known as malfuzat. In fact, Hari compiled the notes taken by different devotees. The
  pattern for each of the thirty-nine meetings is the depiction of a day as spent by Rochaldas, usually
  divided into two parts: singing of devotional songs and spiritual discussion, what they call satsang."
  (p. 165, folio 152) Mulchand's equivalent "is divided into twenty-five short chapters that are arranged
  according to question and answer sessions between Gehimal and Mulchand." (p. 168, folio 155)
- **Charity and medical help as the common activity of the smaller Sufi places.** "While the Sufi paths
  of the three figures under study here are well organized, there are other places devoted to other Hindu

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The Lal Kunwar, "the fiancée of the red" — a woman married annually to the saint's grave**, reported
  at second hand from a nineteenth-century traveller: "In the middle of the nineteenth century, Richard
  Burton mentioned the following custom: 'Every year, a daughter of the Khonbati caste (saffron flower
  dyers) was married to the grave, with music, dance and all the solemnities due to her' (Burton 1851:
  211). This custom, known as Lal Kunwar (the fiancée of the red), disappeared during the partition in
  1947." (p. 181, folio 168) "Burton specifies, however, that the bride was released after a year, though
  she had to remain forever unmarried." (p. 181, folio 168)
- **The rival origin story of the same custom, from a caste the book does not reconcile with the first**:
  "according to other sources, the young Lal Kunwar belonged to the caste of Kanungos. The origin of the
  custom comes from the fact that a young woman of the caste had fallen in love with Lal Shahbaz. The
  latter being a renouncer, she threw herself from a balcony at his feet, thus sacrificing her life for
  him. Lal Shahbaz would have been touched by this sacrifice and he would have authorized that, after his
  death, a young virgin of the caste should be married to his grave. The Lal Kunwar lived for a year in a
  room where she was venerated as a devi, a goddess. When she was released, she was forced to accept
  celibacy." (p. 182, folio 169) **The author then refuses it as unevidenced**: "Despite a relatively
  thorough investigation, with Pakistani and Indian informants, both Muslim and Hindu, it was not
  possible to obtain evidence of this chain of events." (p. 182, folio 169)
- **The saint's handprints in the henna overnight**, attributed by the book to report ("It is said"): "It
  is said that the act of depositing the chador on the grave amounts to putting the fiancée in her
  wedding clothes. It is also said that, the next morning, palms and fingerprints are visible in the
  henna that was deposited the day before. This suggests that, during the night, the qalandar coated his
  hands with henna and is therefore ready to be united to his divine lover." (pp. 182–183, folios
  169–170)
- **A lingam inside the dargah of Lal Shahbaz**, attributed to report and undated: "Many years back, it
  is said that there was a lingam inside the dargah." (p. 178, folio 165)
- **The saint identified as a Hindu king**, attributed to report: "In Sehwan Sharif, Lal Shahbaz Qalandar
  is said to be Raja Barthari, or Raja Vir." (p. 178, folio 165)
- **A death described as an extinction "in the smell of sanctity"**, the phrase the book's own and in
  quotation marks: Lal Das served nearly forty years "before extinguishing himself 'in the smell of
  sanctity' in 1993." (p. 181, folio 168)
- **A saint who would not speak and would not be transmitted**, reported as tradition: "it is said that
  Mulchand did not speak, and that he did not want his tradition to be transmitted." (p. 168, folio 155)
  The author calls the existence of Gehimal's books "paradoxical to some extent" for that reason
  (p. 168, folio 155).
- **A renunciant who never married, and a lineage of custodians descended from his brother**, reported as
  the custodians' own claim: "These would be the descendants of Pithoro's brother, since he was himself a
  renunciant who never married." (p. 178, folio 165)

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The chapter-6 thesis: non-poetic literature matters much less than poetry.** "All in all, it is
  difficult to evaluate the impact of the non-poetic publications among the Hindu Sufis of India and
  their followers. Was Hari's work read by Mulchand or Nimano's followers? In fact, what were the
  relations between the Hindu Sufi masters and their respective followers? Unfortunately, I was not able
  to collect answers for this issue. Notwithstanding this ambiguity, the role played by non-poetic
  literature in the transmission of Sufism is much less significant than the poetic legacy." (pp. 168–169,
  folios 155–156) The author repeats the limit at the conclusion: "if his work is remarkable, it is
  difficult to know its impact on the Sindhis of India, beyond the circle of Rochaldas's followers."
  (p. 174, folio 161)
- **The author's own hypothesis about why one Hindu Sufi breviary prefers minor poets.** "The choice of
  these poets instead of the most acclaimed, such as Shah Abd al-Latif, who was much quoted in
  Rochaldas's spiritual dialogues, should mean something. My hypothesis is that the selected poets
  signify those who were closer to the Hindu tradition, as we saw in Chapter 3. They used open and direct
  references to the Hindu gods." (p. 168, folio 155) **The forward and backward references to Chapter 3
  are the book's own.**
- **A disciple's book judged against its own title.** "It is amazing to observe that there is no mention
  of Rochaldas in the title, but only references to Qutub Ali Shah and Sufism. Gajwani nonetheless did
  not produce personal reflection, but rather a kind of summary of Rochaldas and Hari's thoughts."
  (p. 167, folio 154) "All in all, Gajwani's Sufi galaxy questions the importance of Sufism among the
  Hindu Sindhis of India. As a matter of fact, though, his book only concerns Rochaldas's path – whether
  his enterprise reflects a main trend or is an isolated initiative." (p. 167, folio 154)
- **Hinduization named as the trend, and the BJP named as a cause.** "The Sufi legacy is still apparent
  in the three darbars examined here, although we have observed a slow but regular hinduization due to
  different factors, including the global hinduization of the Hindus in India, this being reinforced by
  the policy of the BJP which is ruling the country." (p. 170, folio 157) "Despite the many changes that
  Hindu Sufism in India has been through, which I shall briefly coin as hinduization, it is amazing to
  see how the Hindu Sufis are still active in terms of transmitting the Sufi legacy." (p. 173, folio 160)
- **Why Jhulelal won and Sufism lost as the ground of Sindhiyyat.** "while he was very successful in
  imposing Jhulelal as the Sindhi god of India par excellence, the role of the Sufi legacy in the making
  of Sindhiyyat did not reach such fame. This was due to many factors, first being the need for the
  migrant and unwelcomed Sindhis to exhibit a Hindu identity interwoven with their Sindhi origins. Also,
  the personality and the charisma of Ram Panjwani were obviously instrumental in the success of the
  Jhulelal enterprise. It may be that the Sufi legacy lacked such a leader, but one can hardly think this
  was the main reason for the decline in the process of Sindhiyyat formation Sufism [text-layer?
  formation. Sufism], generally perceived to be an Islamic expression, could hardly have been selected
  for being the foundation of Sindhiyyat in an independent, Hindu-dominated India." (p. 174, folio 161)
- **A ritual shortage produced by hinduization, stated as a general process.** "But despite the diversity
  of the paths, a general process did impact the Hindu Sufis of India. This process imposed a shortage of
  a number of rituals seeming 'too Muslim' inside the growing hinduization dynamics of the country."
  (p. 176, folio not stated) "Consequently, as expected, the Sufi paths have been reshaped in the context
  of the local and regional set-ups." (p. 176, folio not stated)
- **Islamization renamed sunnization, and dated.** "The country has undergone, since the late 1980s,
  different steps of islamization, a process that would better be called sunnization. The goal of leaders
  such as Ziya al-Haqq was to gradually impose the sharia, a legal system in which the religious
  minorities are officially discriminated against." (p. 175, folio 162)
- **The chapter-7 thesis, stated in the author's own first person.** "Despite the islamization, though,
  and despite the spread of Islamist terrorism in Sindh as in other parts of Pakistan, I argue that the
  Sufi culture still pervades Sindhi society. Seventy years after partition, it is fruitful to mirror the
  situation of the Hindu Sufis from Sindh against those in India." (p. 177, folio 164) "it will allow us
  to balance the representation suggesting that the integrative function performed by Sufism is currently
  weakened in Sindh. Here again, the situation is far more complex than the current depiction we can find
  in the media." (p. 177, folio 164)
- **The book's flat refusal of the tolerance thesis — the sharpest argument in the chunk.** "Sufism does
  not organically imply tolerance towards other religions. For example, the most active people working
  for the conversion of young Hindu girls to Islam are well-known Sufi pirs. The ideology of the wahdat-e
  wujud stating that there is only one God, and that rather there is no prerequisite related to the
  religious belonging of a Sufi, is not accepted by all of the Sufi tariqas." (p. 177, folio 164) The same
  point is put as a question at the close of chapter 6: "How do the Hindu Sufis of Pakistan face the
  increasing islamization of the country, not to speak of how they face the increasing violence towards
  them, including such practices as forced conversions mostly implemented by alleged Sufi murshids?"
  (p. 175, folio 162)
- **A three-part typology of Hindu–Sufi encounter, always attached to a place.** "In Sindh, there are
  different ways through which the Hindus are related to Sufism, according to which three categories of
  encountering can be distinguished. Most of the time, the encountering process is attached to a place."
  (p. 177, folio 164) The three are: the shared Sufi tomb (Bhit Shah), the double-identity shrine (Udero
  Lal) and the Hindu-managed Sufi darbar (Pithoro Pir) (pp. 178–179, folios 165–166). The author then
  concedes the typology leaks: "the basic typology presented in the beginning of the chapter has excluded
  some elements that cannot fit into these categories; in other words, there are some exceptions."
  (p. 179, folio 166)
- **A claimed gap in the literature.** "In the field of Sindh studies, however, there is no published
  research related to Sufism and the Hindu Sufis in Sindh. The publications on the issue, as we saw in
  the introduction, always deal with India." (p. 177, folio 164)
- **The Sehwan case weighed as exception or rule, and left open.** "One can claim that the Sehwan system
  is an exception in Pakistan. It may be that it is, indeed, but this does not prevent us from stating
  that it is still at work. Furthermore, even if it reflects a specific situation, we saw that the Hindus
  of Sindh are coming to the Sufi mausoleums since the nineteenth century." (p. 183, folio 170) **The
  sentence that follows is cut off by the chunk boundary.**
- **The explanation of Hindu ritual survival at Sehwan: heritage, and the category "Hindu Sufis".** "This
  resilience can be explained primarily by the desire to preserve a historic heritage. But beyond this
  integrative dynamic of Sufism, it must be understood that the Hindus do not exercise this function as
  Hindus, but as Hindu Sufis." (p. 183, folio 170)
- **The book's stated comparative purpose.** "the final goal is to see if the circulation of the Sindh ji
  Sufiyyani saqafat still permeates the two 'national sides' of Sindhiyyat, in Pakistan and in India."
  (p. 177, folio 164) "The final goal of this project aims to provide a kind of mirrored perspective,
  intermingling the Sindhi Hindu Sufis of India with those of Pakistan." (p. 175, folio 162)
- **Script loss framed as the real diaspora crisis.** "The real issue the Diaspora Sindhis are facing is
  that of the dismissal of the Devanagari script that the young overseas Sindhis will no longer know. The
  main option to combat this trend is the romanization of the Sindhi language. This is a new step in the
  history of the Sufi legacy among the Hindu Sindhis, and already it has been started by California Hindu
  Sindhis." (p. 175, folio 162)
- **A decline in publishing read as a decline in interest.** "Unfortunately, Gajwani's bibliographical
  research stopped in the mid 1990s. Since then, only a few Sufi works have been published, so that one
  can surmise there is a shortage of interest." (p. 171, folio 158) "Since then there has been something
  of a loss of interest in the Sufi legacy in India." (p. 173, folio 160)

  "Ghulam Ali" at p. 168 (folio 155) as one of the Sindhi Kandri poets — a different man, per chunk 006,
  where he is Murad's son. And "Ghulam Farid" is **not** Baba Fariduddin Ganjshakar;
  **`shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar` was considered and refused**, since the book prints only "Ghulam
  Farid" and gives nothing else.
- **`shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya` rests on one clause** (p. 178, folio 165) in which the saint acts only
  as the initiator of Pithoro Pir. No Multan, no death year, no shrine. Taken under the author-only
  ruling; flag as thin.
- **`shrine-of-sachal-sarmast`: the Daraza bullet is the one to check.** "the sajjada nashin of Daraza"
  (p. 179, folio 166) is a custodian attached to a toponym, so under the interim convention it is not a
  bare toponym and is mapped. **But this chunk never states that Daraza is Sachal's seat** — the link
  comes from the archive row's own `location_short` ("Daraza, Khairpur, Sindh"), not from the page.
  A reviewer who holds that a location-cell match is not textual evidence should move that one bullet to
  "Other saints" and leave the four quotation bullets on the row. The three quotation bullets at pp. 166,
  168 and 172 are also short: "Kabir, Bedil, Sachal and Ajiz" is a bare name in a list of four.
- **`bhit-bhit-shah`: the Shah jo Risalo bullet is an inference risk.** p. 170 (folio 157) prints "Sufi
  poetry – Shah jo Risalo – first" without saying whose work it is. I have mapped it to the row because
  the book uses "Shah" for Shah Abd al-Latif throughout this chunk and calls the Bhit Shah singing "Shah
  jo rag" (p. 178, folio 165); **but the attribution is mine from the book's usage, not a statement on
  the page**, and a strict reader should refuse it. The other five bullets on the row are explicit.
- **`shrine-at-odero-lal-udero-lal-teerath-asthan` is a clean map** — the book names the darbar, both
  figures, both custodianships and the fair — with one caveat: the book spells it "Udero Lal" where the
  archive spells it "Odero Lal (Udero Lal…)", and it hedges Shaykh Tahir's order ("could have been a
  Sohrawardi"), which must not harden into a silsila.
- **`lal-shahbaz-qalandar`: two bullets to weigh.** The figure caption "Asan Gul, a Hindu sajjada nashin
  in Sehwan Sharif" (p. 182, folio 169) is **the chunk's single most quotable institutional fact and its
  least supported**: Asan Gul is in no body sentence, no shrine is named for him, and the body text's
  Hindu office-holders are Lal Gul, Ramchand and Murad Shah. **Asan Gul may be Lal Gul under another
  name, a third man, or a caption error; the book does not say.** And "the 18th of Shaban" (p. 181, folio
  168) is the only ʿurs dating in the chunk — no year, no Gregorian equivalent, and the book does not say
  whether the three-day sequence is the whole ʿurs or only the mehndi cycle within it.

**Near-misses considered against all 169 rows and refused, so the next reader does not redo them.**
- **`shrine-of-lakhi-shah-saddar` — refused, and this is the closest call in the chunk.** The row's
  principal figure is "Syed Shah Sadaruddin Lakyari (Lakhi Shah Saddar)" and its location is "Laki
  (Lakhi), near Sehwan Sharif". The book's "Lakkiyaris" / "Lakiyyaris" / "Lakkiyyaris" are a Sayyid
  lineage at the head of the Sehwani system (pp. 180–181, folios 167–168), and "Gul Muhammad Lakkiyyari"
  and "Murad Shah" are its named heads (pp. 181, 183, folios 168, 170). **The book never names Lakhi Shah
  Saddar, never names a shrine at Laki, and never says the Lakkiyaris descend from or serve him.** A
  lineage name is not a principal figure, so no id is taken. If a reviewer rules that this lineage is that
  row's family, the material at pp. 180–181 and 183 (folios 167–168, 170) transfers to
  **`shrine-of-lakhi-shah-saddar`** mechanically.
- **`shrine-of-shah-shams-ud-din-sabzwari` — refused, on the same reasoning.** "the Sabzwaris also share
  the symbolic power" (p. 180, folio 167) names a Sehwan lineage; the row's principal figure is Shah
  Shams-ud-Din Sabzwari and its location is Multan. **The book names no saint for the Sabzwaris and never
  mentions Multan in this chunk.** If the ruling reverses, that clause takes
  **`shrine-of-shah-shams-ud-din-sabzwari`**.
- **`shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht` — refused.** The book's "Jahaniyyan lineage", "founded" by
  Qutub Ali Shah, with successors Roshan Ali Shah and Hadi Bakhsh and a dargah at Hyderabad (pp. 166–167,
  folios 153–154), is a Sindhi lineage; the row's principal figure is "Sayyid Jalaluddin (Makhdoom
  Jahaniyyan Jahangasht)" at Uch Sharif, who is never named here. Chunk 006 recorded the same lineage's
  seat as "Tando Jahaniyyan" (p. 112, folio not stated). **The shared element is only the word
  "Jahaniyyan".** No id taken.
- **`shrine-of-makhdoom-abdul-rahim-girhori` — refused, and worth stating plainly because the archive
  itself invites the error.** That row's `location_short` reads "Girhor Sharif, near Pithoro, Umarkot,
  Sindh" — it names Pithoro. The book's **Pithoro Pir** (pp. 178–179, folios 165–166) is a saint and a
  darbar "located between Mirpur Khas and Umerkot", initiated by Baha al-Din Zakariyya, managed by Rajput
  Hindus, with a tomb under a huge Om. **Makhdoom Abdul Rahim Girhori is never named, and the book's
  Pithoro Pir is plainly a different site from Girhor Sharif.** No id taken; the whole Pithoro material
  sits under "Other saints".
- **`shrine-of-shah-abdul-karim-bulri` — refused, and named here so the refusal is reversible.** The book
  prints "Shah Karim" once (p. 171, folio 158), as the poet in whose kalam seven of Qazi Qazan's baits
  survived. **No dates, no tomb, no toponym and no "Abdul" are given**, so I have not asserted that this
  is the archive's "Shah Abdul Karim Bulri". **If a reviewer rules that "Shah Karim" is that man, the
  bullet under "Other saints" takes `shrine-of-shah-abdul-karim-bulri`** and nothing else changes.
- **Guru Nanak — refused, because the name could take any of eighteen rows.** "Guru Nanak's poetry has
  been kept in this new edition" (p. 172, folio 159) is the whole evidence, with no site, event or date.

  `gurdwara-sacha-sauda`, `gurdwara-sahib-saidpur-guru-nanak-dev-ji`, `gurdwara-sri-kiara-sahib`,
  `gurdwara-sri-tilganji-sahib`, `gurdwara-tambo-sahib` and `gurudwara-janam-asthan-nankana-sahib`.
