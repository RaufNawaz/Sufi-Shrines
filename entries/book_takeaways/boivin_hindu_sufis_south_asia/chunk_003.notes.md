## Practices, institutions, economy

- **A four-part typology of shared worship, the chunk's organising institutional frame** (pp. 50–51,
  folios 37–38). (1) "certain Sufi cults are practised by Muslims and Hindus. Usually, they worship a
  Sufi saint who is buried in a dargah. Known as ziyarat, both Hindus and Muslims bring textiles to
  put on the tomb, or they only touch and kiss the tomb for obtaining blessings from the buried
  saint." (2) one place of worship, two names — Sehwan Sharif. (3) one sanctuary, two names **and two
  chapels** — Udero Lal. (4) "saints of Muslim origin who are massively venerated by the Hindus, and
  only marginally by the Muslims" — Pithoro Pir, whose "guardians… are Hindu Rajputs".
- **Custodianship recorded in three different vocabularies in one chunk**: "The guardians of Pithoro
  Pir's darbar are Hindu Rajputs" (p. 51, folio 38); "the sajadah nashin of Jhok Sharif" (p. 59,
  folio 46); "the local sajjada nashin" at Bhit Shah (p. 61, folio 48); "His son Hari succeeded him on
  the gaddi" (p. 58, folio 45); and Parsram's Amils as "the main supporters and advisers of both the
  devotees of the Sufi pirs and [the] holders of the Gadi" (p. 49, folio 36) — the square brackets and
  the capital `G` in `Gadi` are the book's own, and the same office is spelled `gaddi` nine pages
  later. **No Auqaf department, no state shrine administration and no revenue figure appears anywhere
  in this chunk: (not stated).**
- **The mehndi right as a hereditary Hindu office inside a Muslim ʿurs, with its calendar date**: two
  Hindu families, the Navanis and the Kanungos, "used to perform mehndi since centuries during the urs
  of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar… on the second day of the urs, the nineteenth day of Shaban" (p. 58,
  folio 45), and Mulchand "performed the mehndi ceremony of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar in Sehwan until his
  death" (p. 59, folio 46).
- **The same office at a second, non-archive shrine**: "Rochaldas was the mehndibardar, the one who
  bore the sacred henna, during the urs of Qutub Ali Shah" (p. 57, folio 44). **The place of that ʿurs
  is (not stated).**
- **Initiation described technically, in Sindhi**: "Mulchand became Rakhiyyal Shah's disciple, his
  taleb, and he was initiated by him. In Sindhi, it is said that he took the name 'nam wanhan' from
  him, because during initiation, the Master gives his disciple a word on which he will perform
  meditation." (p. 59, folio 46) The same mechanism appears at p. 49, folio 36 for Sufi practice at
  large: "the ism-e azam, meaning the expression/word on which meditation is performed", glossed as
  "the exact equivalent of the mantra of the Vedantists (Parsram 1924: 119–20)".
- **Ecstatic and mourning practice, named**: "he also regularly attended the dhamal" (p. 59, folio 46);
  "he was moved to tears by the melodies sung by the sharnais during the processions of tazias for
  Moharram" (p. 56, folio 43); "He would take bhang, a narcotic beverage, every day, and he also
  played ektara, a one-string guitar" (p. 59, folio 46).
- **Poetry as the medium of transmission, and its performers**: "This shared religious culture was not
  transmitted by the Ulemas or the pandits. It was mainly conveyed by poetry and music performed in
  the darbar. While some segments of Sindhi society had their own transmitters, this culture was
  primarily passed on to the villages by the caste musicians (manghanar or mirasi)." (p. 53, folio 40)
  And the repertoire is still in use: kafis "are still a part of the main poetry, which is sung in the
  urs of Jhok Sharif" (p. 59, folio 46); "Her verses are still sung in different places of India and
  abroad" (p. 60, folio 47).
- **The pothi as an institution of the episteme**: "The pothi is a kind of codex which functions as a
  vade mecum – bringing together writings of various types, such as prayers, mystical poetry in
  Sindhi, excerpts from sacred texts, folkloric narratives and treatises on divination." (p. 52,
  folio 39) The corpora are "composed of oral traditions, or sometimes handwritten traditions, which
  are generally classified as folkloric" (p. 52, folio 39).
- **Building and rebuilding, the chunk's only architectural facts**: "although some darbars had been
  created in the late eighteenth century, there was a renaissance of the Nanakpanthi darbars in the
  second half of the nineteenth century. For example, the Halani darbar was reconstructed and a dome
  was added to the main shrine at this time." (p. 54, folio 41) And the Sukkur foundation: "A new
  darbar was built on the bank of the Indus River in Sukkur" (p. 54, folio 41). **No cost, no patron
  and no exact year for either: (not stated).**
- **Community-building by script and iconography**: "There was at this time a growing similarity
  between the iconography of Guru Nanak and Jhulelal, as well as an attempt to impose a specific
  script, the Khudawadi, as the Sikhs had their own script, the Gurmukhi." (p. 54, folio 41) The
  sponsors are named as a class: "an alliance between Sindhi literati and wealthy merchants, an
  emerging middle class in sum" (p. 54, folio 41).
- **Khalsa discipline itemised, as the thing the Sindhi Nanakpanthis did not do**: "Rituals framed the
  whole Khalsa Sikh identity, along with other compulsory duties such as, for the men, wearing the
  five attributes (long hair, beard, iron bangle, wooden comb and dagger)." (p. 53, folio 40) "In
  Sindh, many Nanakpanthis belonged to a subgroup known as the Udasis" (p. 53, folio 40); "the Sindhi
  Nanakpanthis who were heterodox Sikhs and as such, they did not follow the requirements of the
  Khalsa" (p. 53, folio 40); "They were organized in separate communities" (p. 53, folio 40).
- **A welfare economy attached to a Sufi vocation**: "Rochaldas decided to set up dispensaries for the
  poor – first in Rohri, Karachi and then in Hyderabad" (p. 56, folio 43); "He specialized in eye
  surgery, and studied Ayurveda and homeopathy; surgical operations were also performed for free"
  (p. 57, folio 44). The refusal of private practice is quoted from his master: "'If I had wished him
  to make money, I would not have asked him to terminate his contract with the government.'" (p. 56,
  folio 43) And Nimano Faqir's foundation is described in the same register: "the Sakhi Kutiyya place
  of worship and human welfare" (p. 60, folio 47).
- **State employment as the Hindu Sufis' social base**: Rochaldas "was the son of a civil servant
  working in colonial administration" (p. 55, folio 42); his father "was a cleric in the department of
  engineering" and his brother "became chief officer of the municipality of Rohri" (p. 55, folio 42);
  Mulchand's father "was a deputy collector in the revenue department" (p. 58, folio 45); "the Hindu
  Sufis belonged to two main social groups: the merchants and the civil servants" (p. 60, folio 47).
  Rochaldas held "a five-year contract with the government" (p. 56, folio 43).
- **The Manzilgah Affair and the Sukkur riots, with a casualty count**: "there was a precedent of
  communal violence in Sindh, this being the 'Sukkur riots', which had been related to the Manzilgah
  Affair. The Muslims of Sukkur had claimed a building supposed to be a mosque, built by the Mughal
  emperor Akbar, should be reopened as such. But the local Hindus opposed this rehabilitation since it
  was located in the harbour on the Indus River, from which the pilgrims reached the sacred site of
  Sadh Belo. Over the course of three months, between December of 1939 and February of 1940, about 120
  people were killed in the resulting riots, most of them Hindu (105)." (p. 61, folio 48) **A shrine
  access route is the stated cause of the dispute.** The riots spread "beyond the city, in other
  cities such as Rohri, and also in other parts of Sindh, such as in Lakki" (p. 61, folio 48).
- **Partition demography, as the book gives it and hedges it**: "In 1941, the Hindus made up about 21
  per cent of the total population of Sindh, including 6 per cent of the scheduled castes; therefore,
  they would have numbered at less than 1 million – exactly 0.9 million. And since the scheduled
  castes mostly stayed in Pakistan, and a number of other Hindus did not migrate, the migrant Hindus
  would have been closer to 0.8 million, if not less. Nonetheless, there is a kind of consensus that
  they numbered around 1 million." (p. 61, folio 48) **The book prints three different totals in four
  sentences and does not choose between them; all three are recorded.**
- **The migration's transport, chartered by both governments**: "Hindus from interior Sindh enjoyed
  special trains chartered by the Sindh government" (p. 62, folio 49); "The Indian government had
  chartered ships from Bombay and, in December, with the addition of a train, 140,000 Hindus crossed
  the border" (p. 63, folio 50). Rates of departure: "By early November of 1947, 246,000 Hindus had
  already left Sindh by sea, air or rail. As of 1 January 1948, it was estimated that nearly 500,000
  people had sought refuge in India." (p. 62, folio 49) "in a week's time, 11,000 Hindus left Karachi
  (Dawn, 13 January 1948)" (p. 62, folio 49).
- **Property, panic and a government directive**: "He had to enact a government directive in order to
  prohibit owners from selling their property. He hoped to stop the panic that had taken hold of the
  Hindus, who were afraid of being dispossessed, and to deter the Mohajirs from threatening the Hindus
  in hopes that they would sell their goods at low prices." (p. 62, folio 49) "600 Hindu families
  demanded evacuation within two days" (p. 62, folio 49); "The chief minister of Sindh, Muhammad Ayyub
  Khuhro, arrested 500 Mohajirs" (p. 62, folio 49); "on 14 January, the government of India appointed
  a director general of evacuation to formally assist non-Muslims who wanted to migrate from Sindh to
  India" (p. 63, folio 50).
- **A temple burned in Karachi, with two death tolls that do not agree**: "Two hundred Sikhs from the
  inner Sindh, who were to have been evacuated by boat, were installed in a temple in the city centre.
  During the night, a crowd of 8,000 Mohajir surrounded the temple compound and set fire to it,
  forcing the Sikhs out. The Mohajir threw themselves on the Sikhs and began to stab them." (p. 62,
  folio 49) "The death toll ended up being between what was reported by the daily, Dawn, at forty-two
  Sikhs killed and eighty-two wounded (8 January 1948), and the figures reported elsewhere as
  numbering fifteen killed and many looted, according to Sindh chief minister Muhammad Ayyub Khuhro
  (Khuhro 1998: 327)." (p. 62, folio 49) **The temple is not named and is not located beyond "the city
  centre": (not stated).** Also "On 1 September 1947, sabotage derailed a train near Nawab Shah, and
  an armed crowd threw themselves on Sikhs, killing fifteen and wounding seventeen (Khuhro, 1998:
  320)." (p. 62, folio 49)
- **The transfer of darbars as an institution in its own right**: "It was not possible to shift the
  physical structure of the darbar, however. In many cases, the original darbar was left in Sindh and
  a new one was built in India. Many darbars were transferred from Sindh to India from 1947 onwards.
  Of course, this means that the followers never thought they would come back to the land of their
  ancestors. More research needs to be undertaken on this neglected issue of transference" (p. 67,
  folio 54). Two of the three were not transfers: "his darbar was to be a creation – Rai Rochaldas had
  not had any darbar in Sindh – and as for the other, Nimano Faqir, she was asked by her Sufi Master
  in Sindh to go to India for the purpose of creating a 'branch' of the dargah of Daraza" (p. 67,
  folio 54). Compare p. 55, folio 42: "Before partition, none of the three figures had created a
  distinct path, let alone a darbar, in Sindh."
- **Legitimation of a new darbar, described as a procedure**: "The process of legitimizing authority
  was quite similar in the three darbars noted here. It was established as a progression of what had
  been previously accepted: the founder of the darbar in India was already the follower of a murshid,
  now based in the country of Pakistan. It was made clear that, in different circumstances as related
  to Rochaldas and Nimano, the Pakistani murshid did visit the Indian darbars as a final way of
  legitimizing them." (p. 65, folio 52) "In this respect, partition did not alter the dynamics of
  authority, without which no Sufi path can develop." (p. 65, folio 52)

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The origin of the Hindu mehndi right at the Sehwan ʿurs**, attributed by the book to local
  tradition: "According to local tradition, Meru and Nao were two brothers who used to take care of
  Lal Shahbaz Qalandar by providing food for him. Very grateful to them, Lal Shahbaz decided they
  would lead the mehndi ritual after his death, when his own urs would be celebrated." (p. 58,
  folio 45) **The brothers' religion is not stated and the link between them and the Navani/Kanungo
  families is not spelled out: (not stated).**
- **Rochaldas's flight out of his body**, in his own reported account: "He explained that the first
  time he had tried to expel his breath from his body, he had found himself wandering high in the sky
  above the port on the Indus at Rohri." (p. 57, folio 44) Attached to a discipline: "He was also a
  Master in pranayama yoga, to which I shall return." (p. 57, folio 44)
- **Occult powers possessed and refused**, attributed to "the tradition" and to report: "On several
  occasions, the tradition affirms that Rochaldas refused to show off his powers and the spiritual
  degree that he had reached: he simply refused to show them in public." "It is said that Rochaldas
  possessed immense occult powers, but he never used them: he considered them to be obstacles on the
  spiritual path." (p. 57, folio 44)
- **The apparition of Vasan Shah at Lucknow and the gift of a new language**: "One day, when he visited
  disciples in Lucknow, Vasan Shah appeared to him and said, 'A divine purpose is hidden behind
  everything, every language, every sound and every letter. Each letter of each language contains the
  knowledge of uniqueness, non-duality. If the jiva discovers the latent divine meaning, he will
  obtain salvation. It is primordial language or childish language.' By the grace of Vasan Shah,
  Rochaldas began to speak a new language. Rochaldas used the acrostics to suggest a spiritual or
  divine term coming from a letter or a sound." (p. 57, folio 44)
- **Ruqi Bhai's summoning dream at Haridwar**, the origin of her Sufi affiliation: "After his death,
  she decided to visit the holy places of Hinduism, but in Haridwar, she dreamt of a saint who was

- **The recognition of a saintly child**: "Rakhiyyal Shah quickly noticed that Mulchand was not playing

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The chapter's stated aim, from its own Conclusion**: "In this chapter, the aim has been to
  introduce the three main Hindu Sufi figures who created paths to be spread throughout independent
  India. A first step towards this goal, before exploring these individuals, was to draw the social
  and religious scene in colonial Sindh, in which all the three were born and grew up." (p. 63,
  folio 50)
- **`Darbar culture` proposed against Bellamy's `dargah culture`**: "In her book published in 2011,
  Carla Bellamy claimed that dargah culture is 'neither Islamic nor Islamicate', and that it is 'a
  distinct (religious) culture and particularly South Asian' (Bellamy 201: 19). Before addressing the
  issue of the possible 'distinctiveness' of the dargah culture, though, it is necessary to examine
  what I name darbar culture, and to try to provide a kind of typology in the context of the
  Sindhicate area." (p. 50, folio 37) The book's term `Sindhicate` is recorded as printed.
- **Circulation, not syncretism, is the claim**: "The complexity of the figures is an indication of the
  intensity of circulation and the borrowing of cultural and religious patterns between groups whose
  main identification was neither Islam nor Hinduism." (p. 51, folio 38) And the term itself is
  criticised: "the use of the term 'Sufism' is reductive because it does not allow for the restoration
  of the fact that Sufism was itself open to other cults, and could therefore incorporate some parts
  of them." (p. 51, folio 38)
- **Devotion over affiliation, until the colonial census logic bites**: "In the religiosity of Sindh,
  the devotional relationship of a follower with a spiritual Master was prevalent, rather than the
  focus being on affiliation to an institutionalized religion. Nonetheless, while it was common to
  have several spiritual masters belonging to different religions, the Sindhis of the late nineteenth
  century were more and more inclined to identify themselves with a religion as it would be
  acknowledged by the colonial power." (p. 51, folio 38) The same point restated at the chapter
  boundary: "Sindh had drawn – for centuries – a kind of specific religiosity based on devotion, and
  which largely considered faith as being located beyond any formal belonging to an institutionalized
  religion… it was located in the belief of the Oneness of God, whatever the name given to him. This
  devotional religiosity was embodied in persons, saints known as a pir or guru." (pp. 66–67, folio
  not stated / folio 54)
- **Intercession as the shared idiom, on a nineteenth-century authority**: "As Richard Burton noted as
  early as 1851, it is indeed the worship of the intercessors that constitutes the common religious
  idiom of Hindus and Muslims (Burton 1851: 325). More than a century later, Motilal Jotwani would
  call the Sindhi Sufis 'the Great Integrators' (Jotwani 1996: 150–8)." (pp. 51–52, folios 38–39)
- **Why a shrine is structurally necessary to this religiosity**: "this religious culture based on
  devotion and emotion needed a place to be embodied, a territory where the Sufi songs could be sung,
  and the rituals performed. Thus, the shrine plays a leading role in their devotional agency, and
  this culture has been called a 'shrine culture' by some scholars, or more precisely, the 'dargah
  culture', – this being a place where specific worship belonging to the saints' cults was performed.
  In the context of South Asia, such places have been introduced as the very places where a shared
  religious culture was forming." (p. 52, folio 39)
- **The episteme read off the pothi, with Foucault named**: "The morphology of the darbar culture of
  Sindh can be partially complemented by the study of the episteme as it appears in the pothis."
  (p. 52, folio 39) "For Michel Foucault, the episteme refers to 'the set of relations that can unite
  discursive practices at a given epistemological time, which give rise to epistemological figures, to
  sciences, and possibly to formalized systems' (Foucault 1969: 251)." (p. 52, folio 39) And a stated
  gap in the field: "In the context of South Asian Islam, a few specialists have worked on these
  materials, but no major study has really attempted to restore the importance of these corpuses."
  (p. 52, folio 39)
- **Vernacular against cosmopolitan, and the coining of `Sindhian culture`**: "These languages had in
  common that they were not cosmopolitan languages, such as Arabic, Sanskrit or Persian. From the
  seventeenth and eighteenth centuries onwards, though, there was an exuberance of poetic composition
  in the vernacular languages, and Sufi literature represents the majority of this production."
  (pp. 52–53, folios 39–40) "Devotional literature in Sindhi thus formed the basis of a culture that
  transcended social, religious and ethnic divisions. This culture would be called Sindhian culture,
  which refers to a set of social, religious and cultural traits that existed in the Sindhi area at
  the time when it encompassed the southern present Punjab, and also the territories of Rajasthan and
  Gujarat." (p. 53, folio 40) **With a limit stated in the same breath: "Muslim religious elites, like
  the Naqshbandis, certainly did not accept all of them."** (p. 53, folio 40)
- **Nanakpanthi darbar-building read as a reflex of Sikh reform elsewhere**: "this process should be
  seen as reflecting the transformation of the Sikh community when, in the second half of the
  nineteenth century, the first attempt to rationalize and uniformize the Sikh tradition was
  implemented. This process of normalizing the Sikh tradition was centred on Punjab, and it did not
  concern the Sindhi Nanakpanthis" (p. 53, folio 40). The author flags the topic as open: "The issue
  of the foundation of Nanakpanthi darbars in colonial Sindh still deserves to be addressed" (p. 53,
  folio 40).
- **The Jhulelal centralization read as community-making from above**: "in the second half of the
  nineteenth century, there was obviously an attempt (rooted in an alliance between Sindhi literati
  and wealthy merchants, an emerging middle class in sum) to create a community based in his worship,
  probably inspired by the Sikhs." (p. 54, folio 41) "This recomposition pervading the religious
  borders also reflected the construction of a middle class that was ongoing throughout the showdown
  between different social groups that were emerging" (p. 54, folio 41).
- **The Amil thesis tested against three biographies and only partly upheld**: "As we saw, Narsain
  closely related the spread of Hindu Sufism to the influence of the Amils, a process which is
  confirmed by Jethmal Parsram. But the three characters who would start a Hindu Sufi path only partly
  corroborated her assertion." — a zamindar family, a business family and a civil servant's son — "it
  is difficult to claim they were Amils, but in fact, the core point is that they had been educated in
  British schools and, furthermore, they became the Sufi masters of the rising middle class."
  (pp. 54–55, folios 41–42)
- **Partition, not doctrine, is what produced the autonomous Hindu Sufi path**: "the three individuals
  started their Sufi quests through being the followers of Muslim Sufi masters, and it is because of
  partition that they would eventually found an 'autonomous' Hindu Sufi path. Nevertheless, throughout
  their lives, each founder always claimed to be the follower of a Muslim Master who was settled in
  Pakistan." (p. 55, folio 42) "partition created a new and unexpected situation that would compel the
  Hindu Sufis to create new darbars in order for their Sufi persuasion to be maintained." (p. 55,
  folio 42)
- **A geographical claim about where Sindh's devotional centre of gravity moved**: "two of them
  originated from Northern Sindh, coming from cities such as Shikarpur and Rohri. This could be a clue
  suggesting that the devotional centres of Sindh had shifted north sometime after the British
  conquest, despite the role played by the Hyderabadi Amils in the spread of the Sufi culture."
  (p. 60, folio 47) Note the hedge "**could be a clue suggesting**".
- **Sindh's partition violence: the received trope, and the book's refusal of it**: "it is a common
  trope to claim that Sindh did not suffer the violence experienced by Punjab." (p. 61, folio 48)
  Kumar and Kothari's displacement of the blame: "the main trauma for the Sindhis did not come from
  the Muslims in Sindh, but from the behaviour of the Indian administration and local Hindus who did
  not welcome them, and who often openly discriminated against the Hindu Sindhis who were accused of
  not being 'true' Hindus (Kumar and Kothari 2016: 776)." (p. 61, folio 48) The book's own verdict:
  "even if it never reached the level of violence in Punjab, it is irrelevant to claim there was no
  communal violence in Sindh." (p. 62, folio 49) Rumour as a cause: "dramatic reports about Punjab
  were reaching the province, leading many people to feel 'uncertain of the future' (Zamindar 2007:
  66)." (p. 61, folio 48) And a stated difficulty of evidence: "The role played by politicians, both
  from the Muslim League and then from Pakistan, the Sindh Congress, and later on the Indian
  government, is not always easy to disentangle from behind the official discourse which was a genuine
  attempt at trying to convince the Hindus to stay." (p. 62, folio 49)
- **The colonial origin of "Sufism" as an object, with a named printing as the hinge**: "the policy
  implemented by the British commissioners, especially Sir Bartle Frere, gave birth to the Sindhi
  intelligentsia. For historical reasons, the Hindus were at the vanguard of the objectification of
  Sufism, which started after Ernst Trump's printing of the Shah jo Risalo. The Sufi terminology made
  up the main lexicon used for expressing ideas of mystical love and tolerance, also forming the core
  of the Hindu traditions related to the sants and the Nanakpanth." (p. 63, folio 50) **The Risalo's
  author is not named in this sentence: (not stated); see Doubts.**
- **The twofold impact of colonisation, stated as a thesis**: "The impact of British colonization in
  Sindh was twofold. First, it contributed to the objectification of Sufism, enthroning it as the
  fundamental of Sindhi culture at large. The second consequence was a renewal of religiosity in
  relation to the spread of a composite middle class. It was marked by the emergence of the darbars,
  spreading a darbar culture. From the 1920s onwards, there was an increase of the riots between
  Hindus and Muslims, a growing tension that culminated with partition." (p. 64, folio 51) And the
  Theosophical hinge: "The reshaping of Sindhi spirituality occurred when the Theosophical Society was
  created. The society provided a universal stage for spreading the ideology of the Sufism of Sindh,
  along with more humanist values." (p. 64, folio 51)
- **Weber invoked, and a question the class explanation cannot answer**: "In sociological terms, these
  figures belonged to the well-known middle classes Max Weber studied. Drawing on Narsain's thesis
  written in the 1930s, it becomes obvious that the Sufi path fitted these individual's middle-class
  way of life, but nevertheless, the point alone does not answer the question of why they finally
  chose a Sufi path rather than a Hindu one, since they were all three Hindu, and they remained Hindu
  throughout their lifetimes." (p. 63, folio 50)
- **Sindhi displacement as structurally different from Punjabi and Bengali displacement**: "the Sindhis
  had no new territory where they could settle, despite their attempts to build such a space in Kutch,
  as was the case to the Punjabis and Bengalis whose provinces were divided between India and
  Pakistan. Therefore, the Sindhis had to settle in a new environment where, on many occasions, they
  were not welcomed." (p. 64, folio 51) "Even if the Hindu Sindhis were not targeted by mass
  massacres, as happened in Punjab, they had to face the trauma of leaving all of their belongings and
  their ancestral home in a very short time" (p. 64, folio 51).
- **Chapter 2's opening questions, stated as questions**: "it is relevant to wonder if there could be a
  relation between the decision regarding migration and the Sufi affiliation or belonging of the three
  figures. Furthermore, is there enough relevance to ask the question? Were the Hindu Sufis' decisions
  resulting from the same argument as other Hindu Sindhis, or were there specific issues? Last, is it
  possible to link the fact that Mulchand decided not to migrate to his own Sufi belonging and
  conception, and to the place where he was living?" (p. 66, folio not stated) And the argument's
  next step: "for the Sindhis who had decided to leave Sindh and to migrate to India, it was
  impossible not to migrate with their religious legacy." (p. 67, folio 54) "The reasons for selecting
  a place and space were varied, but they always involved a process of sacralization which was crucial
  in the settlement of the Hindu Sindhis in India. Furthermore, the sacralization of a territory could
  not work without a process of legitimization." (p. 67, folio 54)
- **The author's own scope disclaimers**: "The aim of this chapter is far from addressing the issue of
  partition at large, or even that of the migration of the Hindu Sindhis" (p. 66, folio not stated);
  "Partition's consequences are still a subject of academic dispute, despite the numerous works which
  have already been devoted to the issue." (p. 66, folio not stated)

## Doubts

- **The archive defect fires in this chunk: Hinglaj is discussed and cannot be mapped.** "Parsram
  claimed that the universal wisdom is kept in Sindh, in Hinglaj to be exact, which is a pilgrimage
  centre located in the Sindhi-speaking area of Baluchistan." (p. 50, folio 37) The row
  `Shaktipeeth Shri Hinglaj Mata Mandir` exists in `shrine_index.tsv` with an **empty id cell**, so
  the material is filed under "Other saints, sites and events" and **no id has been invented**. A
  human must give that row an id before any of this can enter it. Note also that the book calls
  Hinglaj a pilgrimage centre **in Baluchistan** while saying the wisdom is kept **"in Sindh"** in the
  same sentence; the reconciliation is the book's phrase "the Sindhi-speaking area of Baluchistan",
  and it is left as printed.
- **`dargah-roza-sufi-shah-inayat-shaheed` is mapped on a place-name alone, and the row's principal
  figure is never named at that place.** The chunk gives "the dargah of Jhok Sharif", "the sajadah
  nashin of Jhok Sharif", "the urs of Jhok Sharif" and "the Sufi centre of Jhok Sharif" (pp. 59–60,
  folios 46–47) — a dargah with a custodial office and an ʿurs, which is why interim convention (a)
  was **not** applied to it — but **it never says who is buried there: (not stated)**, and Jhok Sharif
  is the only archive row at Jhok. A human should confirm before any Jhok fact enters that entry.
- **"Shah Inayat" (p. 49, folio 36) could be either of two archive rows and is deliberately not
  mapped.** The name appears once, in "'three great Sufi teachers', who are Shah Inayat, Shah Abd
  al-Latif and Sachal Sarmast (Boivin 2016)". It could be `dargah-roza-sufi-shah-inayat-shaheed`
  (Sufi Shah Inayat Shaheed, Jhok Sharif, Sindh) or `shrine-of-shah-inayat-qadiri` (Shah Inayat
  Qadiri, Lahore). **The chunk attaches no place, no date and no order to the name**, and though the
  Sindhi company he is listed in makes the first row the likelier, that is context and not text.
  **No pick is made here.** The Jhok mapping above rests on the dargah's own name and would stand even
  if this name turned out to be the Lahore row.
- **`gurdwara-pehli-patshahi-jind-pir-sukkur` is the chunk's weakest mapping and a human must confirm
  it.** The book describes "A new darbar… built on the bank of the Indus River in Sukkur, this coming
  to be known as Jinda Pir" (p. 54, folio 41), founded in "the last decade of the nineteenth century"
  by Hindus wanting "a distinct Hindu cult of Khwaja Khizr, also known as Zinda Pir". The archive row
  is "Gurdwara Pehli Patshahi (Jind Pir), Sukkur", a Nanakpanthi/Udasi Darbar "Near the Sukkur–Rohri
  (Lansdowne/Ayub) railway bridge", principal figure Guru Nanak. The match is name + city + riverbank;
  against it, **the book spells `Jinda Pir` and the row `Jind Pir`, the book never calls the place a
  gurdwara, and Guru Nanak is never named in connection with it: (not stated).** If the identification
  fails, the whole bullet moves to "Other saints, sites and events" and nothing else changes.

  `[OCR?]` and zero `[illegible]` flags — but this chunk is NOT clean, and that is a finding about the
