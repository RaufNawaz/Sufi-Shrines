## Practices, institutions, economy

- **A three-day ritual programme, tabulated and dated.** "Table 5.1 Daily programme of Rochaldas’s versi
  in 2012" sets three columns, "First day", "Second day", "Third day", against clock times (p. 146,
  folio 133). First-day entries as printed: "4.00–4.30 dhamal"; "4.30–8.30 kirtan"; "9.00 breakfast";
  "10.00 Pooja and flag sahib dhamal"; "10.30–1.00 pm Kalam and satsang in hall"; "1.00–2.30 Bandharo
  sahib"; "3.30 Tea"; "4.00 Shehnai and dhamal near flag sahib"; "4.30–6.45 Kalam and satsang in hall";
  "6.45–7.00 Dhamal in darbar sahib"; "7.30–9.30 Bandharo sahib"; "10.00–4.00 am (Full night) Kalam in
  hall". Second-day entries mostly read "idem", with these exceptions: "9.45: dhamal, shehnai and mehndi
  sahib"; "Shehnai, mehndi sahib and ceremony dastar bandi in darbar sahib" at 4.00; "4.15: dhamal near
  flag sahib"; "4.30 custom of embracing dastar bandi with garland in hall"; "5.00–5.30 kalam in darbar
  sahib"; "6.00–6.45 kalam and satsang in hall". Third-day entries as extracted: "idem" at 4.00–4.30 and
  4.30–8.30; "9.30 idem"; "10.30 idem"; "idem" at 1.00–2.30; "3.00" and "3.30" with no activity;
  "4.00–4.45 satsang in darbar"; "4.45" with no activity. **The blank and activity-less cells are a
  text-layer problem, not a finding; see Doubts.**
- **Two death anniversaries merged into one festival.** "In the late 1950s, they were celebrated
  separately, but now they are celebrated together." (p. 146, folio 133) "For a few years, two distinct
  urs were celebrated: Lal Shahbaz’s urs in the Islamic calendar, 18, 19 and 20 Shaban, and Rochaldas’s
  versi." (p. 146, folio 133) **The merge is dated only as "One year": (not stated).**
- **A hereditary ritual office split by migration.** The Kanungos' third-day mehndi duty at Sehwan
  followed them to Ulhasnagar in 1962 at Hari's invitation, while "In Sehwan Sharif, another Hindu named
  Lal Das then became mehndibardar" (p. 147, folio 134) — **one office becomes two, on two sides of the
  border.** The Indian line then passes out of the family: Murlidhar was in Baroda, Lachhman "was not
  allowed", and "it was the son of Hari, Damodar, who led the mehndi from that point on" (p. 147,
  folio 134).
- **A liturgical text republished, given away free, and transliterated for a new generation.** "The
  darbar regularly republishes the munajat a little before the versi. Each participant may have a free
  copy. Whereas once it was published in the Arabic Sindhi alphabet, the darbar began to publish it in
  Devanagari so that the younger generations may read it." (p. 148, folio 135) **No print run, no cost,
  no date of the switch: (not stated).** The text itself: "The munajat is made of twenty-one verses
  composed in an Arabicized Sindhi" (p. 147, folio 134), quoting "ain wasi Mustafa iana Ali Murtaza, or
  ‘and the heir of Mustafa, namely Ali al-Murtaza’ (Roshan Ali Shah no date: 4)" and the Quranic ayat on
  authority, "The Muslim believers are said to obey God, the Apostle and those vested with authority,
  the ulul al-amr (Quran 4:59)" (p. 147, folio 134). Its refrain "is a request addressed in simple words
  to the One to accept the prayers of all, Muslim as well as Hindu" (p. 148, folio 135).
- **The festival calendar of one darbar, in full.** Besides the merged ʿurs/versi: "Two other versis are
  celebrated: that of Hari, the son and successor of Rochaldas, on 21 March, and that of Gobindram, a
  renunciant who was close to Rochaldas when he was in Sindh, on the first day of Holi. A last
  opportunity is found in the celebration of the new moon (chand). A hundred disciples then gather to
  sing kalams. Other versis are celebrated for the most outstanding followers of Rochaldas, like for
  example Dadi Dhan." (p. 148, folio 135) **"A hundred disciples" is the chunk's only regular attendance
  figure.**
- **A versi held in a private flat, with its space deliberately unmarked.** Dadi Dhan's versi "was not
  celebrated in the darbar of Rochaldas, but in her small apartment of Mahim, in Bombay. In this case,
  the physical space was reduced and the sacralization of the territory was limited to the presence of a
  small altar: no inscription or any specific decorum gives information on the sacred function that this
  place can exercise." (p. 148, folio 135)
- **One versi counted and instrumented.** "At Dadi Dhan’s place, the versi in 2012 was marked by a
  succession of songs sung either by men or by women, mostly a capella, with one exception where a
  singer used an ektaro, a one-string instrument that serves as a drone. About 150 people were involved
  in the versi, including Pritam, who died in 2015… The latter did not attend, but many disciples had
  travelled from Ulhasnagar." (pp. 148–149, folios 135–136) **"The latter" is the present gaddi nashin,
  who is not named on this page: (not stated).**
- **The order of service of a second path, itemised.** "In Mulchand’s Sufi path, the versi was performed
  on three main occasions: 5 April (birthday of Mulchand and Gehimal), 20 November (Mulcand’s
  [text-layer? Mulchand’s] death) and 29 October (Gehimal’s death). The function started with the
  raising of Mulchand’s alam, now known as jhando. Mulchand’s jhando was navy blue. The function was
  carried on with the murti, which was made virajman, a ritual when the murti is sat on the throne. Then
  it was the time of invocation: a spiritual person covers his face and invites the murshid, and his
  tears start flowing in this part known as sufiyya ji ibadat, or the ‘Sufi worship’. After the arti,
  prasad was distributed among the followers and, finally, flowers were put in flowing water, usually a
  river. The function ended with the singing of Sufi kalams, and with the performance of dhamal. The
  dhamal was nevertheless informal. A devotee was playing the drum and other followers were dancing on
  the Mast qalandar rhythm." (p. 149, folio 136)
- **A Brahman opens a Sufi function.** "At Daulatram, the versi started with the havan (fire) ceremony
  performed by a Sindhi Brahmin, and this goes on mainly with the chanting of Sufi songs composed by
  Sachal Sarmast, Shah Abd al-Latif and Qutub Ali Shah." (pp. 149–150, folios 136–137)
- **Relics kept by a lay family and handled by the congregation.** "a kind of relic also focused the
  worship for the Sufi saint. It is said that he used dice in Sindh with other Sufis. The Bhatia family
  has kept the ivory dice, as well as the green crossed clothe [text-layer? cloth] on which Isar Lal
  played. During the versi, the two objects were exhibited so that the participants could touch them and
  then put their hands on their faces as a sort of blessing." (p. 150, folio 137) Instruments there:
  "a dholak (cattle drum) and ektaro (one-string instrument)" (p. 150, folio 137); the gloss "cattle
  drum" is reproduced as printed, see Doubts.
- **The samadhi in the basement, and the refusal of a sign.** "Usually, the followers have arranged a
  samadhi in the basement of the building where they live. From the outside, there is no inscription for
  identifying the place, or there are a variety of names which are given to a single place. For example,
  the Radha Krishin Lal darbar is also named Maqbul Manzil." (p. 150, folio 137)
- **Two Sachal darbars in one neighbourhood, with their weekly rhythms and their founding dates.** Radha
  Krishin Lal darbar / Maqbul Manzil: founded by "Faqir Radha Krishin Lal Sahib Darazi ‘Krishna’ (d.
  1980)", who "built the darbar in the 1970s"; run after him by Hari Ram, then by his daughter Soni, "who
  performed the satsang every Thursday evening"; two versis a year; "The sajjada nashin of Daraza has
  paid three visits to the darbar." (pp. 150–151, folios 137–138) Sachal Sain darbar / Rohani Rah: "The
  entrance door is decorated by haqq mawjud in Arabic Sindhi script. It stood for about thirty to
  thirty-five years in another building, and shifted to the present one twenty years ago. It was built
  by Pratap Rai Rajani, who passed away in 2007. His wife Mira used to sing Sachal’s poetry during the
  satsang, which is held every Sunday evening. Previously, it was organized twice a week… Mira’s son
  Jaichand daily performs the arti in the darbar at about 9.00 am." (p. 151, folio 138) **Both "twenty
  years ago" spans are relative and the year of writing is (not stated).**
- **Two custodians who no longer speak.** "The two in charge are said to have been in touch but since
  twenty years, they have stopped meeting for an unknown reason. Notwithstanding, still they are in
  touch with the sajjada nashin of Daraza since, in both cases, he calls on them to give the date of
  Sachal’s versi." (p. 151, folio 138) **A Pakistani custodian sets the festival date for two Indian
  darbars that will not meet each other.**
- **The altar read as a hierarchy, twice.** At the Rohani Rah, Pratap below Sachal's statue, two sajjada
  nashins of Daraza to either side, Krishna above, and the Daraza darbar above that (p. 152, folio 139).
  At the Radha Krishin Lal darbar, the core is a photo of a Daraza custodian, "dominated by an
  inscription in Latin alphabet: ‘Sai Sachal’", with the two Indian gaddi nashins also pictured, and
  Sachal himself absent (p. 152, folio 139).
- **Rituals suppressed under political pressure, and rituals simplified for practical reasons.** "Regarding
  the rituals, the evolution has been imposed by the political evolution in India under the rule of the
  BJP, as well as the growing animosity spreading between the dominant Hindus and the Muslims. Here, a
  number of typical Sufi rituals performed in Sindh have been suppressed, such as the alam or the
  dhamal. For more practical reasons, other rituals have been simplified – for instance, the mehndi
  procession at Rochaldas’s darbar, since the mehndibardar left Ulhasnagar." (p. 152, folio 139)
- **Singing as the precondition of every rite.** "The continuity of the mystic space is made through the
  singing of Sufi poetry accompanying the performance of the rituals themselves. No ritual is performed
  without the singing of a Sufi kalam." (p. 152, folio 139)
- **Devotees who are not Sufis.** "Although the devotees performing the rituals and attending the versis
  are mostly not Sufis, in the sense that they have a job and are householders, they feel like belonging
  to a path, to a community whose unity is the veneration of a Sufi Master." (p. 154, folio not stated)
- **Publishing as the main institution of transmission, with its dates.** Vaswani's "Darvishan jo dawr",
  "published in 1955 in Ajmer. In a thick book of almost 600 pages" (p. 156, folio 143); Panjwani's 1945
  book on Shah Abd al-Latif "republished in India in 1981" (p. 157, folio 144); "In 1959, he published an
  anthology he named Sik ji soghat with, as a kind of subtitle, Sufi kalam, which would be republished in
  2000 by Thakur Chawla. About 200 poets of Sindh are mentioned, with abstracts of their poetry. No
  biographical sketch is provided, no date, no reference of any kind" (p. 158, folio 145); "Sindhi ain
  Sindhiyaat", "published in 1987, the year of his death… while the title is in Sindhi, the book of about
  100 pages was published in English" (p. 159, folio 146); Paru Chawla's anthology, "Probably in 1992"
  (p. 161, folio 148); Hari's four-volume dialogues, "First published in Sindhi between 1959 and 1965…
  Hari prepared an English translation that was edited in 1998 by his own son and successor, H. M.
  Damodar" (p. 162, folio 149), and "later on translated into English and published in 1995, and
  republished in 1998, with the title Some Moments with the Master. Spiritual Dialogues with the Sufi
  Saint" (p. 164, folio 151); Hari's 1962 Gita commentary, "It would be translated by his son and
  published in English in 1982" (p. 163, folio 150); "In 1992, Damodar also published a translation from
  Sanskrit made by ‘a devotee’ of the Shri Yoga Vaisishtha" (p. 163, folio 150). **No print runs, no
  prices, no sales figures anywhere: (not stated).**
- **Script as the institutional question of the whole chapter.** "In India, the issue is restricted, so to
  say, to the dilemma of two scripts: Arabic and Devanagari. But most of the young Sindhis living in
  other countries can only read the Latin alphabet. As we shall see, some Sindhis have already decided to
  publish Sufi poetry not in the Sindhi language, but in the Latin alphabet. Others are selecting the
  options of translating Sindhi poetry into English." (p. 155, folio 142) And, against that: "It is
  interesting to see that, as late as in the 1990s, Paru Chawla published this book, as well as others,
  in Arabic Sindhi script, though the non-migrant Sindhis in India can hardly read it. Does this choice
  mean she was targeting the migrant Sindhi generation only?" (p. 162, folio 149)
- **A cultural centre founded, and its later stewards.** "In 1969, he had founded the Sita Sindhu Bhavan,
  a culture centre for the promotion of the different artistic sides of the Sindhi culture." (p. 157,
  folio 144) "After his death, the Sita Sindhu Bhagwan [text-layer? Sita Sindhu Bhavan] was run by a
  number of Sindhi ‘cultural activists’." (p. 161, folio 148)
- **A performance genre named and described.** "Ram Panjwani popularized ‘Sufistic chownkies’, to quote
  Popati Hiranandani (Hiranandani 1984: 55). They are choral singings of Sufi kalams with the rhythmic
  beat of the gharo, an earthenware pot." (p. 161, folio 148)
- **Poetry that remains unpublished on one side of the border.** "From partition onwards, much Sufi
  poetry in Sindhi was published in India. Some of the most important kalams are still unpublished in
  Pakistan – for example, Dalpat Sufi’s poetry." (p. 153, folio 140)
- **A written, non-poetic Sufism, and the one region that produced it.** "In Sindh, save the Naqshbandis,
  Sufism is dominated by poetry expressing the concept of wahdat-e wujud." (p. 162, folio 149) "Among
  the Naqshbandis, including the Hindu Naqshbandis, many treatises were composed to deal with theological
  issues." (p. 162, folio 149)
- **An equivalence table, printed as a technical apparatus.** "‘The stage of ecstasy and trance is
  absorption in the “sirgun” form of Atma. There are three ways to attain this stage. (i) Abhyas-Sumran
  (Recitation of mantras-zikur), (ii) Vivek- (Deep thinking-Fikur-Ghour Khouz), (iii) Dhyan
  (Meditation-Tasawur)’ (Hari 1982: 182)." (p. 164, folio 151) And the four stages: "‘Dharma=Religion=
  Sharyat’, then ‘Karma=Action=Tariqat’, ‘Bhakti=Devotion=Haqiqat’, and ‘Gyan=Perfect Knowledge of Atma=
  Marfat’ (Hari 1982: 17–18)." (p. 164, folio 151)
- **The stations of the path as Panjwani gives them.** "The quest will develop into love, which will take
  him to the valley of oneness, wahadaniyyat. Thus, he will reach the knowledge, marifat. The next step
  is that of detachment, or hirarat. The third and last step is fana: he should merge himself into God
  through annihilating his Ego." (p. 160, folio 147) A second, differently worded list on the same page:
  "tariqa, the meeting with the Sufi path, marifa, the knowledge after initiation and finally the fana,
  the merging with God" (p. 160, folio 147). **The two lists do not agree; see Doubts.**

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **A saint appears in a dream and commissions his own ʿurs in a foreign country** — the chunk's central
  legend, told of a Hindu Sufi and a Muslim saint together: "Shortly before his death, Rochaldas had a
  dream in which Lal Shahbaz Qalandar appeared. He pointed out that although the Hindu Sindhis had
  forgotten him, Lal Shahbaz always watched over them in India. He asked him to celebrate his urs, as at
  Sehwan." (p. 146, folio 133) The author's own hedge follows: "It seems that the first celebration took
  place in 1959, two years after the death of Rochaldas, who had left orders." (p. 146, folio 133)
- **A claim of incarnation and a practice of darshan**, attributed by the book to its claimant: "Sai Baba
  claimed to be an incarnation of Shirdi Sai Baba (1838–1918)… He is said to have accomplished many
  miracles and his main activity was to offer darshan to his disciples, through which they were able to
  see God since he was his incarnation." (p. 148, folio 135)
- **A portrait that resembles a saint more than the saint does**, reported as the custodian's own answer:
  "when I asked Soni why it was Sakhi and not Sachal, she answered that Sakhi is the one who looks most
  like Sachal." (p. 152, folio 139)
- **Relics that transmit blessing by touch**, reported of Isarlal's dice and cloth: "the two objects were
  exhibited so that the participants could touch them and then put their hands on their faces as a sort
  of blessing." (p. 150, folio 137) The provenance carries the book's hedge: "It is said that he used
  dice in Sindh with other Sufis." (p. 150, folio 137)
- **An avatar named inside a scripture the book is describing**, not a karamat but recorded here for
  completeness: "a spiritual dialogue between Shri Ramchandra, an incarnation of Vishnu, and Shri
  Vasishtha as rendered by Valmiki, the legendary author of the Ramayana" (p. 163, folio 150).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **Ritual as the surviving link to a lost territory** — the strongest interpretive claim in the chapter-5
  material: "The mehndi of Lal Shahbaz makes it possible to maintain a direct link through the ritual
  with the lost territory of Sindh." (p. 147, folio 134)
- **A Muslim profession of faith adopted as a Hindu congregation's daily prayer.** "The last part of the
  verse has an amazing Shia flavour. Still more amazing is an indirect Quranic reference… In his munajat,
  Roshan quotes exactly the same references, and also he includes Ali between the Prophet and the ulul
  al-amr: it is a kind of Muslim profession of faith, which is amazingly adopted as dua by Rochaldas’s
  followers (Roshan Ali Shah no date: 5)." (p. 147, folio 134)
- **Attendance read as a symptom.** "Previously, it was organized twice a week, and it may be that the
  reduction to once per week could signify there is a growing lack of interest." (p. 151, folio 138) The
  modal hedging ("it may be… could signify") is the author's own.
- **The chapter-5 thesis: a mystical space that hinduizes across partition.** "the darbar mystical space
  shifted from a Sufi rooted framing to a more hinduized framing throughout the time of partition.
  Nonetheless, this evolution followed what scholars have coined the ‘solidification’ or ‘rigidification’
  of religious identities in South Asia. Another factor could be the local environment." (p. 153,
  folio 140)
- **A gap in the literature, stated as the book's warrant.** "The Sindhis are a very small minority in
  India, and since partition, they have been very active in finding efficient means to preserve and even
  develop the Sindhi language… While this topic has been studied by scholars such as Kothari and Thapan,
  the role given to the Sufi legacy in this process has not been given the attention it deserves."
  (p. 153, folio 140)
- **The chapter-6 programme**, set out in the author's own first person: "Until now, the issue of the
  Sufi legacy among the Hindu Sufis of India has been addressed through two primary perspectives: the
  physical space or darbar, and the mystical Sufi poetry I have depicted as framing a ‘mystical space’.
  Between both, bridging them, are the rituals… In this chapter, I wish to enlarge the focus of this
  study in scrutinizing the issue of transmission beyond the initiation between a Sufi Master and his
  disciple." (p. 154, folio not stated)
- **Objectification and canonization as the mechanism by which poetry became identity.** "following the
  objectification of Sufism after the canonization of the Shah jo Risalo authored by Shah Abd al-Latif
  (d. 1752), the Sufi poetry was accepted as an epitome of Sindhi culture, and thus of Sindhi identity."
  (p. 155, folio 142)
- **Theosophy as the frame through which Sindhi literati read their own Sufism** — advanced twice, the
  second time as the section's conclusion: "The first one is the probable influence of the Theosophical
  Society. As it was for the majority of the Sindhi literati, be they Hindu or Muslim, the ideology of
  the society provided the framework for a reinterpretation of the Sufi legacy of Sindh." (p. 157,
  folio 144) "Finally, Ram Panjwani’s understanding of Sufism is deeply reminiscent of the Theosophist
  representation. It is a state of mind, and it is the spirit of Sufism, more than any other belief
  and/or practice which give to Sindhiyyat a unique flavour." (p. 161, folio 148)
- **An anthology read as a political act.** "Throughout this construction, Panjwani astutely locates
  Jhulelal in the wake of the Sufi legacy, knowing this heritage was already the core of the
  cross-religious Sindhi culture. Even if this was not a totally planned formatting, one may conclude
  that Panjwani was already thinking of promoting a Sindhi God, Jhulelal." (p. 158, folio 145) The three
  advantages are itemised: "first to allow the Sindhis to be finally distinguished from the Sikhs, but
  also to enlarge the Sufi tradition, and finally to give evidence to the existence of a separate Hindu
  Sindhi identity that could be agglomerated to mainstream Hinduism as a new branch of the already
  innumerable sects." (p. 158, folio 145)
- **Sufism demoted to one component among several.** "although we do not have to undermine it in the
  agency of Sindhi identity, Sufism is a component among others, and from the 1960s onwards, it comes
  after Jhulelal’s worship, which can be seen as a kind of soft hinduization of the Sindhi legacy in
  India." (p. 159, folio 146) A contradiction is charged to Panjwani directly: "Panjwani does not escape
  some contradiction. On the one hand, he claims that the Sindhi language is the core of Sindhi identity,
  while it explains Sufism in quoting Persian-speaking poets, including Farid al-Din Attar (1145–1220)."
  (p. 160, folio 147)
- **A genre distinction used to separate two Hindu Sufisms.** "Already, we saw that the main difference
  between the Sindhi Hindu Sufis and the Hindu Naqshbandis studied by Thomas Dahnhardt is the genre of
  their writings, and thus their approach to Sufism." (p. 162, folio 149)
- **A first, and its limits.** "Hari’s endeavour is meritorious, because it is not only, according to my
  knowledge, the first comprehensive attempt achieved by a Hindu Sufi with a Sindhi background to openly
  link Sufism with Vedanta but also a testimony to the two paradoxical trends in which the Hindu Sindhi
  Sufism of India was torn apart in the 1980s and the 1990s. On the one hand, it shows off a strong will
  to keep the Sufi legacy alive, and on the other hand, the spirituality is deeply rooted in the Hindu
  scriptures. In this construction, Sufism only plays the role of a non-essential repertoire and
  reservoir for the spirituality." (p. 164, folio 151) The method is faulted precisely: "The author never
  provided any contextualization regarding the Sufi technical lexicon: it is quoted only as an equivalent
  of a Vedantic term and concept. Second, he stays at the level of the notions, without any practical
  indications." (p. 164, folio 151)
- **A distinction drawn between two of Hari's books.** "The latter did not aim to find bridges between
  Sufism and Vedanta, as can be observed throughout the technical lexicon. It is totally devoted to the
  philosophy of the Vedanta, and one can only very rarely find an Islamic word or expression given as the
  equivalent of the Sanskrit one." (p. 163, folio 150) And of the Gita commentary: "This book is a
  primary attempt by a Sindhi Hindu Sufi to provide a comprehensive reflection on the core topic of the
  encounter between Sufism and Hinduism." (p. 163, folio 150) Damodar's summary of his father's purpose:
  "‘to establish there is no distinction between Vedanta and Sufism’ (Hari 1982: 427)" (p. 163,
  folio 150).
- **The author's own stated limits** — three of them, all worth keeping: "I was unable to collect any
  information about the author" (p. 156, folio 143); "I was not able to pay a visit to these places"
  (p. 152, folio 139); and "The two in charge are said to have been in touch but since twenty years, they
  have stopped meeting for an unknown reason" (p. 151, folio 138).

**Internal contradictions and unresolved tensions in the book's own text**

- **The regnal numeral of the Daraza custodian.** p. 150 (folio 137) has "the sixth murshid of Daraza,
  Sachal’s dargah, Sakhi Qabul Muhammad II (1842–1925)"; p. 152 (folio 139) has "Sakhi Qabul Muhammad VI",
  "Sakhi Qabul VI" and "Sakhi Qabul VIII", and p. 152 also says Nimano "was also initiated to the Sufi
  path by Sakhi Qabul Muhammad VI". **I have deliberately NOT marked "II" as a text-layer error**, and
  have not harmonised it, because "the second custodian of that name" and "the sixth murshid in sequence"
  are compatible statements and the numeral may be exactly right. But note that **only the "II" form
  carries dates (1842–1925)**, and if "II" and "VI" are the same man those dates attach to the VI form
  too. A human must settle this before either numeral or those dates enter an entry for
  `shrine-of-sachal-sarmast`.
- **Two authors for "the dua".** p. 147 (folio 134): "The dua is the devotional supplication (munajat)
  composed by the son of Qutub Ali Shah, Roshan Ali Shah (1852–1932)." p. 149 (folio 136): "the final dua
  whose author was Nasir Faqir." The two may be different duas at different occasions — the first at
  Rochaldas's versi, the second at Dadi Dhan's versi in Mahim — but the book does not say so, and both are
  called "the dua" of a Rochaldas-tradition function. **Not resolved here.** (Chunk 006's notes give

**Mapping: the four rows taken, and why each could be challenged**

- `lal-shahbaz-qalandar` — the strongest mapping in the chunk: named, located at Sehwan/Sehwan Sharif,
  with ʿurs dates, a mehndi office and named holders. **No challenge.** One sub-item is weak: the songs
  "Dam mast qalandar" and "Mast qalandar rhythm" (p. 149, folio 136) are recorded under that row **only
  as titles**, because the book nowhere states whom the song names. A consolidator should not treat them
  as evidence about the saint.
- `shrine-of-sachal-sarmast` — named repeatedly, with his dargah located at Daraza and its custodians
  discussed. **No challenge.** But note that **no date of any kind is given for Sachal himself** in these
  pages.
- `bhit-bhit-shah` — named eight times; **the "(d. 1752)" at p. 155 (folio 142) is the only death year
  in the chunk for any mapped figure**, and it is attached to the Risalo's canonization rather than to a
  shrine. No shrine, no Bhit, no silsila: the row is informed about the poet, not the site.
- `mazar-of-bulleh-shah` — **weakest mapping, flagged as the ruling requires.** The evidence is two bare
  mentions under two spellings, "a composition of Bullhe Shah" (p. 149, folio 136) and "the famous
  Punjabi poet, Bulhe Shah" (p. 158, folio 145). The protocol's ruling of 18 Sep 2026 names this exact
  case ("Bulleh Shah named as a contemporary poet"), so it is mapped, but the consolidator should weigh
  it as two clauses and nothing more. **Nothing was enriched: no Kasur, no date, no Qadiri, none of it is
  in the chunk.**

- **"Ghulam Farid" — refused, written reversibly.** p. 161 (folio 148) prints only "others from a
  non-Sindhi author, Ghulam Farid", with no honorific, no dates and no place. The archive row
  `mithankot-kot-mithan` names "Khwaja Ghulam Farid" as principal figure. **I have not mapped it**,
  because the identification would require a title, a date or a place the chunk does not print, and
  because "Ghulam Ali" (a different man in chunk 006's pages) shows the book's lists do carry
  near-identical names. **If the consolidator judges the identification safe, the id is
  `mithankot-kot-mithan`.**
- **"Sakhi" as an honorific — refused.** The chunk's "Sakhi Qabul Muhammad" and the song "Sakhi
  sabajhal" share a word with four archive principal figures: `sakhi-sarwar` ("Sultan Sakhi Sarwar
  (Lakhdata)"), `darbar-sakhi-shah-chan-charagh`, `purana-bhalwal` ("Sakhi Badshah") and
  `sant-baba-asudaram-darbar-panno-aqil` ("Sakhi Baba"). **None of them is the book's man**; the
  coincidence is the honorific only.
- **"Rohri" — refused, and the one row that tempts it named.** p. 150 (folio 137) does attach a shrine to
  the toponym — "Isarlal, who had a shrine in Rohri" — so this is **not** a bare toponym and convention
  (a) does not apply. It is refused for a different reason: **Isarlal is not the principal figure of any
  archive row**, and the only row whose location string contains "Rohri" is
  `gurdwara-pehli-patshahi-jind-pir-sukkur` (Guru Nanak, at the Sukkur–Rohri bridge), which is a
  different site and a different figure. Isarlal's Rohri shrine is therefore recorded under "Other saints
  and sites" as a candidate for a future archive row.
- **Bare toponyms refused under interim convention (a), with the id each would take on a reversal.**
  "Sehwan Sharif" at p. 161 (folio 148), Paru Chawla's birthplace, with no shrine or saint in the clause

- **"Nanakpanthis" — refused.** p. 158 (folio 145) says the Hindus who worshipped Jhulelal in Sindh "were

  not a mapping. Worth knowing that this chunk gives a one-clause statement about that whole category's
  pre-partition constituency.
