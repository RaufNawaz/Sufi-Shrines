## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The key institutional term of the whole book, defined: `darbar`.** "In South Asia, the Sufi
  sanctuary was centred at the tomb of the Sufi saint, and it was mostly known as the dargah from a
  Persian word, with the meaning of threshold. In Sindh, another Persian word was mostly used, that of
  darbar, or court. Of course, one can find many other words for a sacred place, but the term darbar was
  used by different religions, including the Sikhs.1 In South Asia, it referred first to the court of the
  king, and through a symbolic interpretation, it was the court of God. In Sindh, the darbar was the
  sacred territory in which the Sufi culture was at work. It was therefore a public culture, shared with
  the public, and an open space." (p. 37, folio 24). Note that **`dargah` and `darbar` are given as two
  Persian words with different senses (threshold vs court), not as synonyms.**
- **A nineteenth-century building and renovation boom in darbars, with a stated cause in middle-class
  time-use.** "Many new darbars were founded and the oldest ones were renovated; as such, they shared a
  basic set-up in which the personal relation between the spiritual Master and his disciple was at the
  core of the religiosity – and it was a perfect fit for the busy schedule of the emerging middle
  classes." (p. 38, folio 25). **No darbar is named and no number is given: (not stated).**
- **Three competing kinds of darbar, named as a market.** "The main question involved here is why
  different Sindhis select a Sufi darbar, a Nanakpanthi darbar or a Daryapanthi darbar." (p. 38,
  folio 25) — with the chapter title itself, "The religious market in Sindh on the eve of partition"
  (p. 36, folio not stated), this is the chunk's economic framing.
- **The 1872 census of Sindh, with its own disclaimer attached.** "Sindh's population statistics are not
  available in the first census of 1872. The population was 2,333,527 million. Sindh had 1,718,688
  Muslims, 393,092 Hindus, 5,034 Christians and 86,663 persons put in a category titled 'Other castes
  such as Sikhs, Parsis, Jews, etc.' (Hughes 1876: 878)." (p. 38, folio 25). **The disclaimer and the
  figures sit in consecutive sentences and the book does not reconcile them; the components also do not
  sum to the total, and "2,333,527 million" is printed as such** — all three points are inventoried under
  Doubts and **none is emended here.**
- **The census's categories read as an argument.** "there are three explicitly named religions: Islam,
  Hinduism and Christianity. Their nominal designation implies that they are regarded as major religious
  institutions. The last section also contains religions considered to be minor. Moreover, they are
  called castes." (p. 38, folio 25).
- **Sayyids as the custodial class of Sindh's shrines, in the book's own two-part formula.** "In Sindh,
  the Sayyids had two characteristics. The majority of them were landowners (zamindar), but they were
  also pir. They were responsible for managing the sanctuaries where the Muslim saints were buried. We
  are talking about material management as well as charismatic management." (p. 39, folio 26). **No
  shrine and no lineage is named: (not stated).**
- **The pir as mediator, and the irrigation origin of his power.** "The pirs of Sindh play the role of
  mediators between the tribes, and between the rulers and the governed, as well. The power of the pir
  was reinforced in the eighteenth century when the Kalhora developed the irrigation system. The pir had
  created a new system of social relations that, although inspired by the tribal system, was based more
  on association than on blood ties (Ansari 1992: 28–9). There was in fact a close intertwining between
  tribal power, feudal power and charismatic power" (p. 39, folio 26).
- **Land grants as the currency of Baluchi rule.** "Given the political domination of the Talpurs, the
  Baluchis were at the top of local society. Since the eighteenth century, they provided the bulk of the
  troops of the Kalhoras, who had ruled Sindh before them. Once in power, they reinforced their rule by
  distributing jagir to clan chiefs. Their domination was consequently doubled in power, with their
  being both warriors and landowners." (p. 38, folio 25). And the Jat case: "the malik exercised a feudal
  power over those of the Jats who resided on his jagir" (p. 39, folio 26).
- **A tribal chief who is a spiritual leader without being a pir.** "The malik of the Jats was not a Jat,
  but a Baluch. It was, however, he who exercised the power of chief over the tribe. But although the
  Jats did not regard him as a pir, he was for many of his dependents more than a tribal chief: he was
  also a spiritual leader (Westphal-Hellbusch and Westphal 1964: 22)." (p. 39, folio 26).
- **The panchayat as a temple-financing and ritual-taxing body — the chunk's hardest economic material.**
  "The functions of the panchayat were social and religious. For example, the panchayat fixed the taxes
  relative to marriages and other rites of passage. On the other hand, it had to provide for the
  maintenance of the temple, pay the wages of the Brahmans and give alms to religious orders. It also had
  the power to punish the offenders for his injunctions until they were excommunicated (Thakur 1959:
  74–5)." (p. 41, folio 28). **No sums, no temple named: (not stated).** Jurisdiction: "In rural Sindh,
  the panchayat of the Lohanas could have authority over other castes which, because of their small
  number, had none" (p. 41, folio 28), with the Sonaros (or the Brahmans) as the example, and the
  Brahmans, Jajiks and Bhats "governed by a council known as mastan (Thakur 1959: 73)" (pp. 40–41,
  folios 27–28).
- **Animal sacrifice at a Hindu festival and at a Muslim pir's dargah, on two named occasions.** "the
  Lohana practised animal sacrifices on several occasions. One of them was the celebration of Navratri,
  the festival of Bhavani, a figure of the goddess; a kid or a he-goat was then sacrificed. The second
  occasion was the festival of Khetrapal, a disciple of Shiva: a kid or a he-goat was sacrificed to the
  dargah of a pir in this case (Thakur 1959: 158 and 175)." (p. 43, folio 30). **This is the chunk's only
  recorded offering made at a shrine, and the shrine is unnamed: (not stated).**
- **Ritual exclusion enforced at a mosque.** "The Shikaris, for example, although Muslims, were not
  allowed to enter a mosque without submitting to a purification ritual (Burton 1851: 308)." (p. 39,
  folio 26). And among Hindus: untouchables "lived outside of the villages and their members were never
  permitted to enter the houses of the village (Thakur 1959: 70)" (p. 40, folio 27).
- **Madrasa provision, quantified.** "Arabic was taught in the madrasats, which were to form different
  categories of professional religious agents such as qazi, mufti and faqih. Teaching was divided into
  religious sciences (manqullat) and rational sciences (maqallat). At the end of the seventeenth century,
  there were 400 madrasats in just the city of Thattah, which was comparable to the major cities of Iraq
  (Burton 1851: 341)." (p. 41, folio 28). The book's spellings **`madrasats`, `manqullat`, `maqallat`**
  are reproduced as printed — see Doubts.
- **The manuscript economy of Sindhi religious literature**, from the India Office Library, "the majority
  of which do not go back beyond the eighteenth century" (p. 41, folio 28): Arabic treatises translated
  or commented on in Sindhi; Quranic verses "written in Arabic and then commented on in Sindhi";
  manuscripts on "the prophet, his miracles and his birth or his marriage"; martyrdom texts on Hasan,
  Husayn and Ali Akbar; and the versified Sindhi *Rauzat al-Shahid* (pp. 41–42, folios 28–29).
- **Shiite devotional poetry as a shared, cross-communal genre with Hindu authors.** "The second category
  of the manuscripts of the India Office Library provides a particular track: devotional literature of
  Shiite origin (marsiya). It is noteworthy that, in Sindh, this devotional poetry is cherished by both
  Muslims and Hindus, the latter being performers as well as authors." (p. 42, folio 29).
- **Magic and divination named as a component of religious knowledge.** "The other important element of
  religious knowledge includes a set of practices and beliefs related to magic and divination." (p. 42,
  folio 29). **Nothing further: (not stated).**
- **Print as the engine of Sufism's objectification, with three dated phases.** "The beginning of the
  process is undoubtedly related to the advent of the printing press (Boivin 2014). In Sindh, a printing
  press was brought to Karachi in the 1850s, but the main event in relation to Sufism came with the
  printing in 1866 of the Shah jo Risalo… The second phase was that of the rationalization of the poetry
  through the implementation of an analysis of the technical lexicon, initiated by Hindu literati in the
  1890s, and the third phase came when the Sindhi literati took interest in the Sufi poet's life rather
  than the poetry alone." (p. 48, folio 35). **The third phase is not dated: (not stated).**
- **A named Sindhi publishing infrastructure.** The Sindh Sahitiyya Society, established 1914; Blavatsky
  Printing, founded in Hyderabad (p. 48, folio 35); the Sindh Times, "the official journal of the Sindh
  Sabha", edited by Hiranand Shahani (p. 46, folio 33).
- **The Theosophical Society as the "ideological infrastructure" of the new Sufism, with its Karachi
  branch itemised.** Founded "in New York City in 1875, with the motto 'There is no Religion higher than
  Truth.' The main objective of the society was to build a universal brotherhood rooted in a universal
  wisdom." (p. 46, folio 33). "In Karachi, the Theosophical Society was founded in 1896 after Annie
  Besant visited this city as well as Hyderabad… In the first years of its presence, the Parsis were the
  most active members… According to Aitken, who published his Gazetteer in 1907, the Karachi branch had
  forty members (Aitken 1907: 167). They started by meeting in the new Dayaram Jethmal Science College.
  Later on, an Englishman donated his residence to the Society. Jamshed Nusserwanji then decided to build
  a new building with an auditorium, a library and a lecture room.7 A Montessori school, one of the
  earliest in the city of Karachi, was opened on the building's third floor." (pp. 46–47, folios 33–34).
  **The Englishman is not named: (not stated).** The Society's role for the book: it "provided the
  ideological infrastructure" for "a new reading of the Sufi legacy" (p. 30, folio 17).
- **Freemasonry as the first European spiritual import, with a welfare arm.** "the conqueror and first
  governor of Sindh, Sir Charles Napier, built a Masonic lodge in 1843 in Karachi. Three other lodges
  were founded in the following years, along with, in 1873, an association for the relief of distressed
  widows and orphans (Hughes 1872: 385). Social issues were thus incorporated into the area's practice of
  Freemasonry." (p. 46, folio 33).
- **A missionary literary society, with membership counted.** "In August of 1885, he started the 'Literary
  Society' for 'English-speaking educated Indians'. The main objective of the society was the
  'intellectual and moral improvement of its members', and there were 74 Europeans and 207 Indian members
  (Gidumal 1932: 290). Among them, six Indians, five Hindus and one Muslim graduated from a university.
  The majority of the graduated Indians were Hindu, and then Parsi and Muslim. A number of lectures were
  proposed on topics such as the prevention of child marriage and the remarriage of widows… In Sindh, the
  lectures led to a revival of religious and theological studies." (p. 46, folio 33). **The two sentences
  on graduates conflict** — see Doubts.
- **Language policy as religious infrastructure.** "When Persian as official language was given up in the
  1850s, Sindhi and English were the two languages used in the administration. The Amils kept the same
  administrative role and they were at the vanguard in the huge campaign of translation launched by Sir
  Bartle Frere (1815–84), the commissioner in Sindh." (p. 44, folio 31). "During the Raj, the Amils were
  largely employed by the British for the translation of English texts into Sindhi, to be used in newly
  created schools." (p. 45, folio 32).
- **The kardar office and Amil monopoly, with a regnal span printed.** "Originating from the mainstream
  caste of the Lohanas, they specialized in administration service, probably from the eighteenth century
  onwards. During the Talpur rule (1783–1843), they reinforced their influence to the point where they
  almost monopolized the function of kardar. In a given district, the kardar exercised almost
  royalty-level powers since he was collecting taxes and dispensing justice. Only the army was not under
  his control." (p. 44, folio 31). The parenthesis **`(1783–1843)`** is the only regnal span in the chunk.
- **Commercial capital converted into land.** "After the growth of new emporium in Sindh, especially
  Karachi, the Lohanas took benefit of the extension of the commercial networks throughout the British
  colonial empire all over the world. These benefits allowed them to invest in the agricultural lands,
  mainly cotton, and they became powerful landowners, locally known as zamindars, at the end of the
  nineteenth century." (p. 44, folio 31).
- **The Hindu social map of Sindh, as the British drew it.** "The most numerous Brahmans belonged to the
  caste of Saraswat Brahmans. They accepted food from the Lohanas, for whom they were the spiritual
  guides. The Saraswat ate meat and drank alcohol (Aitken 1907: 182–3). The Lohanas, who represented half
  of the total Hindu population, were specialized in wholesale and retail trade. The British sources
  traditionally divided them into two groups: the Amils, who finally created a new caste… and the
  Bhaibands." (p. 40, folio 27). Traders: "Although trade was dominated by the Hindus, three Muslim groups
  carried out this activity: the Memon, the Khojah and the Bohrah." (p. 39, folio 26). Baniyas of
  Shikarpur "tended to belong to non-literate groups, and their religious inclination was mostly towards
  the Nanakpanth" (pp. 30–31, folios 17–18).
- **Untouchable groups, their migration and their trades.** Bhils, Kohlis and "the Menghwar, mostly
  settled around Umerkot. Part of their devotional literature was and still is in Sindhi." "The first
  census reports that many would have migrated from Gujarat or Rajasthan between 1843 and 1872… the
  majority of these untouchables worked in agriculture. There is hardly any trace of untouchables in
  records of crafts. Rumour has it that the Hindu untouchable artisans all converted to Islam, and it is
  true that crafts were largely in the hands of Muslims." (p. 40, folio 27). The word "Rumour" is the
  book's own hedge.
- **Script loss as the mechanism by which a devotional corpus dies.** "the new generations, especially the
  third one after the migrants,10 have lost their link with the Sufi devotional literature in Sindhi. As
  of today, I have never yet met a third-generation individual who can read Arabic-Sindhi script, an
  alphabet in which the near total of Sufi devotional literature is printed." (p. 33, folio 20), with the
  diaspora workaround: "an American Sindhi started to use the Latin alphabet for printing this poetry"
  (pp. 33–34, folios 20–21). **The American Sindhi is not named: (not stated).**
- **Sufi darbars founded in India after migration, announced as chapter 2's subject.** "These dramatic
  changes in the lives of the Hindu Sindhis will be scrutinized to analyse how Sufi darbars were created
  in India… the main aim here will be to decipher the impact of partition and migration on the traditional
  structures of Sufism, such as authority, legitimacy and succession." (p. 31, folio 18) — **succession is
  named as a problem but no line of succession is given in this chunk: (not stated).** "While the Hindu
  Sufis were in touch with their Muslim masters, now settled in Pakistan, they had also to deal with a
  brand new environment, dominated by non-Sindhi Hindus. In this process, the key event would be the
  construction of a physical space, a darbar, along with its naming." (p. 31, folio 18); "to build a
  physical structure was a necessary process for not only enacting the Sufi cults but also appropriating
  the new soil." (p. 31, folio 18).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The book's central thesis, the "organic theory", stated against both "or" and "and".** "I argue that
  the whole religious system of the Hindu Sindhis was mostly constructed – up to recent years, and is
  still in some cases, including the Hindu Sindhi Sufis – against this 'or', as well as against any 'and'.
  Because, for them, it was a system, so that they did not take the issue of their religion, their beliefs
  as well as their practices, as being this or that, or this and that. This understanding is what I call
  the organic theory, since the Sufism as practised by the Hindu Sindhis is a religious system organized
  of its own, as a separate and distinct entity." (p. 29, folio 16).
- **Against Ramey, and against syncretism vocabulary.** "Furthermore, Ramey does not address the issue in
  the more relevant way when he puts in the title: Hindu, Sikh or Sufi." (p. 29, folio 16); "Ramey tries
  to argue in his conclusion that the Sindhi religious construction adds a new layer to the issue of
  syncretism, since it allows for not conceiving their system as a homogenized religion (Ramey 2008: 190).
  Going further on, I would argue that, once again, scholars are facing the paucity of technical
  terminology, since regarding the religious field, the common vocabulary has been mainly borrowed, or
  coined, from a Christian environment." (p. 29, folio 16).
- **The study's stated final aim.** "Studying the encounters between Islam and Hinduism… is to observe very
  complex processes involving not only religious matters but also primarily social factors, as well as
  political and economic considerations… It is the final aim of the present study: to uncover the various
  and numerous factors that allow for and prevent the formation of an organic tradition." (p. 29,
  folio 16).
- **Sindh's geography as the cause of its heterodoxy.** "It is a fact that Sindh, because of its
  geographical location both as an isolated and a buffer zone, has hosted many unorthodox versions of
  normative religions, especially Hinduism and Islam. This plays out as a breeding ground for new
  religious elaborations that flourished especially in the nineteenth century." (pp. 29–30, folios 16–17).
- **Colonial knowledge production as the origin of modern Sufism in Sindh.** "one of the main consequences
  of the reframing of knowledge as implemented by the British was a new reading of the Sufi legacy, for
  which the Theosophical Society provided the ideological infrastructure. New social agents acted as the
  main agents in this renewal of the Sufi legacy, and they belonged to an intelligentsia and a middle
  class that emerged following the socio-economic impact of colonization, as well as the spread of British
  education." (p. 30, folio 17). And: "The main result was the spread of intelligentsia from 1850 onwards,
  with an emerging middle class made up of civil servants working in the colonial administration, and also
  lawyers and doctors. Combined with the growth of the Theosophical Society, the intelligentsia produced a
  new reading of Sufism, where cultural patterns were dominant, and embodied in the Sufi poetry." (p. 37,
  folio 24).
- **"Darbar culture" as the book's coinage for that outcome.** "The main result was the emergence of the
  darbar culture, a trans-religious denomination of a new religiosity where the Sindhis – whether Muslim,
  Hindu or Sikh – could perform their religious duties by rooting them on an individual conviction,
  allowing them to match up with modernity as defined by the colonial power." (p. 30, folio 17). And:
  "All through this study, I argue that Sindh hosted a specific shared religious culture resulting from a
  process of vernacularization, which is not contradictory of the fact that some of its features can also
  be found in neighbouring provinces. This 'darbar culture' operated through different dynamics,
  implementing different kinds of encounters between Hinduism and Sufism." (p. 37, folio 24).
- **Locality, not only region, as the explanatory unit.** "I shall underscore the regional factor as a key
  factor, referring to the role played by the locality – not only the part of Sindh where they were born
  but also the village, the town or city, which had its own 'personality' framed throughout the
  centuries." (p. 30, folio 17).
- **The chapter's target: a shared Sufi *culture*, not Sufism.** "the main purpose here is to decipher the
  process through which a shared Sufi culture, and not Sufism, was built mostly during the second half of
  the nineteenth century, thus developing exactly at that time when the three Sufi figures noted above
  were born." (p. 36, folio not stated).
- **The Amil thesis stated and then held at arm's length.** "It is usually claimed that a group of Hindu
  scribes of Sindh, the Amils, played a primary role in the spread of Sufism among the Hindus of Sindh.
  While I shall examine this theory, I am more interested in understanding why some Hindus became disciples
  of Sufi masters rather than followers of Hindu gurus, especially since many Hindu darbars were created
  during that period of colonial Sindh." (p. 36, folio not stated).
- **The "no caste in Sindh" claim examined and not conceded.** "Frequently, the claim arises that there was
  no caste system in Sindh, or at least not so rigid a caste system as seen in other parts of India,
  because of the very small number of Brahmans. Indeed, it is possible that Sindh hosted a specific social
  fabric, but even this point is not to underrate another main actor: the colonial power" (p. 36, folio not
  stated). And later, flatly: "Contrary to popular belief, there were many untouchables in Sindh." (p. 40,
  folio 27). **A Doubts-worthy pair of positions, kept side by side here as the book keeps them.**
- **"Caste" as a colonial classificatory reflex.** "Rather than seeing confusion in labelling a religion a
  caste, we must understand that 'caste' as a term had already established itself as an inescapable
  paradigm for categorizing any human group in the Indian context." (p. 38, folio 25).
- **Objectification defined.** "A surge of new representation of Sufism resulted from the birth and growth
  of a new elite, the intelligentsia, which operated a process of objectification of the Sufi legacy in
  Sindh. Throughout this process, Sufism was gradually transformed into an autonomous system of values,
  which can be described, interpreted and distinguished from the other systems by the believers."
  (pp. 43–44, folios 30–31).
- **Reform on both sides as a single rationalizing movement.** "Due to colonization, both the Muslims and
  the Hindus re-evaluated their religious legacies and, for different and sometimes opposite reasons, they
  were convinced their religions were to be reformed. Although this usually meant 'purified' from alien
  accretions, the suppression of superstitious or magic-like beliefs and practices implemented a
  rationalizing process of the religious traditions. The 'dis-enchanting' result was not the disappearance
  of religion, but rather a new discourse on what was religion." (p. 45, folio 32).
- **Naqshbandi reform read against Mughal decline.** "The Naqshbandis of Sindh followed the reforming
  enterprise led by Shah Waliullah in Delhi… This Islamic renewal is to be placed in the more general
  context of the decline of the Mughal Empire. As a matter of fact, while the Muslim power was weakened,
  the Naqshbandis thought one of the main factors was that the Indian Muslims, including the Mughals, did
  not practise the authentic Islam." (p. 42, folio 29).
- **"Sufi math" — the claim that Sufism is a religion in itself.** Of *Sufi sagora* (1921): "This work is
  introduced as a history of what the authors call Sufi math, or namely, the Sufi religion. It can be seen
  as an attempt to state that Sufism is a distinct religion in itself." (p. 48, folio 35).
- **Foucault's "conditions of possibility" as the book's method.** "Throughout this study, great attention
  is given to what Michel Foucault calls the conditions of possibility, which can explain why and how a
  tradition has developed in a given way and not in others. In this study, the Hindu Sufi traditions are
  understood to have been greatly affected by a main historical event: partition." (p. 32, folio 19).
- **Partition as a category crisis, not only a migration.** "While through the late census in Sindh, they
  identified more and more as Hindu, they quickly realized that what they call Hinduism was not considered
  as such by some peoples in the new regions they were to settle, mostly Gujarat and Maharashtra. Obviously,
  this new situation forced them to renegotiate their relation to Sufism, and to construct a new bridge
  between their Sufi legacy and the religion that was now a kind of normative Hinduism." (p. 33, folio 20).
- **Renegotiation conducted through space.** "often, the tool of renegotiation was transacted throughout
  space and territory. This means that not only settlement could be renegotiated but also that space itself
  was devoted to the sanctuaries. The organization of the space was nonetheless constructed in relation to
  the specific components of a given tradition." (p. 33, folio 20).
- **"Hinduization" accepted with a caveat, in the book's own two-sided formulation.** "Seventy years after
  partition, a period when processes of 'solidification' – and sometimes radicalization – of religious
  identities were at work, both Hindu and Islamic, it could sound reductive to speak of a hinduization
  process since, once again, a single term overshadows the many micro-processes involved. But on the other
  hand, it is relevant to speak of hinduization since this term implies a reduction of Sufi referents, both
  in terms of rituals and devotional poetry." (p. 33, folio 20).
- **The political present named.** "Beyond the Islamic origin of Sufism, which is very negatively
  appreciated in a BJP-ruled India, the transmission of Hindu Sufism meets other expectations – first, that
  of the transmission of Sindhi language." (p. 33, folio 20).
- **The author's own stance on decline, hedged against nostalgia and then stated anyway.** "I am not
  inclined to think things were better 'before'. Here, 'before' would mean before partition. Because we all
  know that the human mind has a natural tendency to forget the bad in the past… Nonetheless, beyond the
  rational argumentation, it is difficult not to think that the Sufi legacy of the Hindu Sindhis is
  vanishing." (p. 34, folio 21). And the closing hope: "Notwithstanding, I have some reason to think this
  study will not be an epitaph-like endeavour… my hope is carved in the third generation – namely the
  grandchildren of the migrant Sindhis." (p. 34, folio 21). Note the dating device: "I am writing these
  lines seventy years after partition and migration" (p. 34, folio 21) — **the year of writing is (not
  stated).**

## Doubts

- **The chapter count contradicts itself and I have not reconciled it.** The Introduction announces: "The
  book is divided into seven chapters, out of which six are devoted to India, and to diaspora to a lesser
  extent, and one, the last one, to Pakistan." (p. 29, folio 16). But the same walkthrough then describes
  only six — first (p. 29, folio 16), second (p. 31, folio 18), third (p. 31, folio 18), fourth (p. 32,
  folio 19), fifth (p. 32, folio 19) — and closes with "**The sixth and last chapter** will move the reader
  from India to Pakistan" (p. 32, folio 19). **Seven vs. "sixth and last": both sides recorded, neither
  preferred.** A human should check the printed table of contents before any claim about the book's shape
  enters an entry.
- **The 1872 census paragraph carries three separate problems and I have emended none of them.** (i) The
  disclaimer and the data contradict each other in consecutive sentences: "Sindh's population statistics
  are not available in the first census of 1872. The population was 2,333,527 million." (p. 38, folio 25).
  (ii) **"2,333,527 million"** is printed as such — a figure and a scale word that cannot both be intended;
  flagged as `2,333,527 million` `[text-layer? — the figure alone, "2,333,527", is the reading the
  component numbers support]`, **written reversibly, not substituted above.** (iii) The named components —
  1,718,688 Muslims, 393,092 Hindus, 5,034 Christians, 86,663 "Other castes" — sum to 2,203,477, which is
  130,050 short of the stated total. **All four component figures and the total are quoted exactly as
  printed above; the arithmetic is reported, not resolved.** A human must check the printed page and
  Hughes 1876: 878 before any Sindh population figure is cited.
- **A probable dropped negative, flagged and not emended.** p. 40, folio 27 prints: "On the other hand, it
  is certain whether these populations were established in the east of the province, which is none other
  than the ultimate extension of the great Thar Desert." — flagged `it is certain whether` `[text-layer? —
  "it is not certain whether"]`. The following sentence's "On the other hand, what is **certainly true** is
  that…" implies the first clause was meant to express doubt. **This may equally be a printing error in the
  book rather than an extraction fault, which is exactly why it is written reversibly.**
- **"treaty" where "treatise" appears to be meant, flagged and not emended.** "A special mention must be
  made of the **treaty** composed by Husayn Va’iz Kashefi (d. 1505)" (p. 42, folio 29) — flagged `treaty`
  `[text-layer? — "treatise"]`; the next sentence but one calls the same object "This **treatise**"
  (p. 42, folio 29). Both words are printed, two sentences apart, about the same text.
- **The graduate counts at the Literary Society conflict within two sentences.** "Among them, six Indians,
  five Hindus and one Muslim graduated from a university. The majority of the graduated Indians were Hindu,
  and then Parsi and Muslim." (p. 46, folio 33). Five Hindus plus one Muslim exhausts the six, leaving no
  Parsi. **Both sentences quoted above as printed; no reading offered.**
- **These are the only three `[text-layer?]` flags in the chunk, and the route is otherwise clean.** There
  are **zero `[OCR?]` and zero `[illegible]` flags in the source**, as expected on the text_layer route,
  and I found no character-level corruption: no letter-for-digit substitutions, no broken transliterations,
  no lost diacritics. Curly apostrophes render correctly throughout (`Fara’iz`, `Abu’l Hasan`, `Va’iz`,
  `Sufis’ culture`), as do en dashes in ranges (`1783–1843`, `182–3`, `28–9`) and in parenthetical asides
  (` – `). **This is a positive finding about the route, recorded so it is not mistaken for an incomplete
  check.**
- **Typesetting artefacts recorded once as a class, not flagged per instance.** (i) A **space before a
  possessive apostrophe** at a line break: `Shah Abd al-Latif ’s work` (p. 48, folio 35) — the printed word
  is **al-Latif's**. (ii) A **missing space after an em/en dash**: `the masters –the ones in India`
  (p. 32, folio 19). (iii) **Endnote reference numerals are glued to the preceding character with no space
  and no superscript marking.** The dangerous instances, where the numeral follows a numeral or a comma and
  can be read as part of a date or a page number: **`the migrants,10`** (p. 33, folio 20) — endnote 10;
  **`another book on Sachal Sarmast in 1922,8`** (p. 48, folio 35) — **the year is 1922**, `8` is
  endnote 8; **`(Blumhardt, 1905: 35).4`** (p. 41, folio 28) — the page is 35, `4` is endnote 4. The
  harmless instances, after words: `including the Sikhs.1` (p. 37, folio 24), `the Muslim populations.2`
  and `the Ashrafs and the Ajlafs.3` (p. 38, folio 25), `the intelligentsia.5` (p. 44, folio 31), `ways of
  life'.6` (p. 45, folio 32), `a lecture room.7` (p. 47, folio 34). **Class recorded once; the three
  numeric ones are listed individually because a fact depends on each.**
- **p. 35 prints the bare numeral `22` and nothing else, and my reading of it as a folio is an inference.**
  The page carries no running-head text and no body text. It is read as **folio 22** because (a) the folio
  sits at the top of the page on every other page in this chunk and (b) `35 − 13 = 22` matches the
  relation measured on the nineteen other pages. **The alternative reading — that it is a part-title or
  section numeral, as the `1` on p. 36 is a chapter numeral — is recorded here and refused**, since a part
  numbered 22 makes no sense in a seven-chapter book. **No fact above is cited to p. 35**, so nothing
  depends on the reading.
- **p. 36 is the only page in this chunk cited as `folio not stated`**, and it carries substantive material
  (the three masters, the chapter's aim, the Amil thesis, the caste question). On the −13 relation it would
  be folio 23; that is a cross-check only and is **not** used as a citation anywhere above.
- **Decision (a), written reversibly — Sehwan Sharif.** Named twice as a birthplace and a place of origin
  (p. 30, folio 17; p. 45, folio 32), with **no shrine, tomb, saint, ʿurs or custodian attached** and with
  **Lal Shahbaz Qalandar not named at all**. Under the interim convention it takes no id. **If the toponym
  ruling reverses, the rows it would take are `lal-shahbaz-qalandar` (Sehwan Sharif, Jamshoro District) and
  arguably `shrine-of-lakhi-shah-saddar` (Laki/Lakhi, near Sehwan Sharif).** Note that a reversal would fire
  the archive's most consequential Sindh row off two sentences about a dialect and a translator.
- **Decision (a), written reversibly — Hyderabad, Shikarpur, Rohri, Sukkur, Karachi, Umerkot.** All appear
  as bare toponyms with no sacred site attached (pp. 30, 31, 40, 44, 46, 47, 48; folios 17, 18, 27, 31, 33,
  34, 35). On a reversal: **Hyderabad → `guru-gurpat-mandir-db-80-sirey-ghat`; Shikarpur →
  `gurdwara-sach-khand-sahib`, `khatwari-darbar-shikarpur`, `sant-bhagat-kanwar-ram-temple-chak`;
  Rohri/Sukkur → `gurdwara-pehli-patshahi-jind-pir-sukkur`, `gurdwara-dash-mesh-pita`,
  `kalka-cave-temple-asthan-of-kalka-devi` (and `sadh-belo-sadh-belo-island-temple`, already mapped on its
  own name); Umerkot → `umarkot-amarkot-shiv-mandir`; Karachi → the index's eight Karachi rows.** That is
  well over a dozen ids off passages about dialects, commerce, printing presses and Masonic lodges, which is
  itself an argument for the interim convention.
- **`sadh-belo-sadh-belo-island-temple`: the book's spelling is `Sadh Bela`, the archive's is `Sadh Belo`.**
  Both are recorded; **no normalisation has been applied.** The identification rests on three converging
  details in one sentence — an island site, the Rohri–Sukkur stretch of the Indus, and the label "Udasi
  temple" matching the row's `Nanakpanthi / Udasi Darbar` category (p. 31, folio 18) — but the book gives no
  district, no founder and no date, and **Baba Bankhandi Maharaj is never named: (not stated)**. A human
  should confirm before the gift-of-land fact enters the entry.
- **`ramapir-temple-tando-allahyar` rests on a bare name in a list.** "Rama Pir (Ramdeo or Ramdev)" is
  named once, among four deified heroes, with no site, date, custodian or ʿurs (p. 43, folio 30). Per the
  18 Sep 2026 ruling the id is taken, but the consolidator should weigh it as thin. **Note also that the
  book classifies him as a Hindu "deified hero" venerated by untouchables, where the archive row is a Hindu
  Temple — the two are compatible but the book says nothing about a temple.**
- **`shrine-at-odero-lal-udero-lal-teerath-asthan` versus the two Karachi Jhulelal rows — a name that could
  match three rows.** The person "Udero Lal" is the principal figure of three archive rows:
  `shrine-at-odero-lal-udero-lal-teerath-asthan` ("Sheikh Tahir / Udero Lal (Jhulelal)"),
  `jhollay-lal-mandir` ("Jhulelal (Uderolal)", Karachi) and
  `darya-lal-mandir-darya-lal-sankat-mochan-mandir` ("Jhulelal / Daryalal", Karachi). **Everything the chunk
  says about a site — "the main centre of the Daryapanth is in Udero Lal, the name given to the village"
  (p. 42, folio 29) — points to the first row only**, so the site-level facts are mapped there. **The
  person-level mention, and the unnamed "several temples dedicated to Udero Lal" (p. 42, folio 29), could be
  claimed for the two Karachi rows under the 18 Sep 2026 ruling; I have not claimed them**, because the book
  names neither Karachi nor "Jhulelal" nor "Daryalal" in connection with him. **Flagged rather than decided.**
- **"Ali Akbar" at p. 41, folio 28 is a member of Husayn's family, not the archive's Shah Ali Akbar Shamsi.**
  The sentence is "several manuscripts are devoted to the martyrdom of Hasan and Husayn or to the members of
  Husayn's family, as for example Ali Akbar" (p. 41, folio 28). **`shrine-of-hazrat-shah-ali-akbar-shah-ali-
  akbar-shamsi` (Suraj Miani, Multan) considered and refused.** Recorded so the shared name is not later
  mistaken for a match.
- **"Makhdum Muhammad Hashim" is not any of the archive's three Makhdum rows.** Considered and refused:
  `shrine-of-makhdoom-nooh-hala` (Makhdoom Nooh), `shrine-of-makhdoom-abdul-rahim-girhori` (Makhdoom Abdul
  Rahim Girhori), `shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht` (Sayyid Jalaluddin). **The chunk's Makhdum bears
  none of those names** (p. 41, folio 28). No id assigned.
- **The Nanakpanth is named repeatedly but Guru Nanak is not, and no Sikh row is claimed.** "The Nanakpanth
  is a form of Sikhism that does not recognize the authority of the Khalsa. … It is possible that the Aroras
  imported this cult from Punjab during their migrations in Sindh in the eighteenth century." (p. 43,
  folio 30); also pp. 31, 38, 42 (folios 18, 25, 29). **The person Guru Nanak — principal figure of some
  fifteen archive rows — is never named in this chunk: (not stated)**, so the 18 Sep 2026 person-ruling does
  not fire. A panth's name is not its founder's name; recorded so nobody reads the ruling as covering it.
- **Deity names: Shiva, Devi, Krishna and Manu are named, and no id is taken.** "They were, as elsewhere,

  untouchables venerated Devi" (p. 43, folio 30); the satguru list "Manu, Zarathustra, Buddha, Krishna,

  `shree-ratneshwar-mahadev-temple-karachi`, `umarkot-amarkot-shiv-mandir`, `shiv-mandir-chiti-ghati`, and

- **Era style and script, recorded as a measured negative.** The book prints **no era markers at all** in
  this chunk — no `ce`, no `AD`, no `AH`, no `BCE`. Every year is bare (`1872`, `1866`, `1843`, `1752`,
