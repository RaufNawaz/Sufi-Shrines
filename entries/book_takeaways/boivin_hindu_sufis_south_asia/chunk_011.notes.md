# boivin_hindu_sufis_south_asia — chunk 011 — pp. 204–227

This chunk is **almost entirely back matter**, and it should be read as a reference apparatus rather
than as prose. Four distinct kinds of text follow one another: (i) the **tail of the `General
Conclusion`**, running prose, pp. 204–206 (folios 191–193), with **p. 207 (folio 194) carrying the
bare folio numeral and nothing else**; (ii) the **`Notes`**, the book's endnotes, opening as a
display page on **p. 208 (folio not stated)** and running to p. 217 (folio 204), keyed by chapter
and note number; (iii) the **`Glossary`**, opening as a display page on **p. 218 (folio not stated)**
and running to p. 220 (folio 207), term-by-term in the author's own words; (iv) the **`Annexes`**,
opening as a display page on **p. 221 (folio not stated)** and running to the end of the chunk, of
which `Annex 1` is **Sindhi verse with facing English translation** for six named poets and `Annex 2`
is a **chart of "Hindu Sufis' silsilas in Sindh"** with a page of the author's commentary. The chunk
**opens mid-section** — p. 204 begins "As stated before, the Sufi legacy among the Hindu Sindhis is
partly shared with other South Asian cultures" — and **breaks off mid-sentence** at the foot of
p. 227 (folio 214) at "Maybe because of the Sohrawardiyya's hold on Sindh, the Chishtiyya could not
spread in Sindh as it", which runs into chunk 012.

**Running heads and folios, measured page by page.** Every folio cited below was read off the page
it is attached to; none is computed. **All folios in this chunk sit in the running head at the TOP
of the page. No page in this chunk carries its folio in a footer.** That was checked explicitly,
because the briefing warned that back matter sometimes foots its folios: the feet of pp. 222, 223,
224, 225 and 227 do carry material, but it is the **Annexes' own numbered footnotes** (1–8), not
folios, and the feet of pp. 208, 218 and 221 are empty. The parity rule is uniform across all four
sections: **even PDF pages set the section title first and the folio last** (`General Conclusion
191`, `Notes 197`, `Glossary 207`, `Annexes 209`); **odd PDF pages set the folio first and the title
last** (`196 Notes`, `206 Glossary`, `210 Annexes`). One wrinkle: in the `General Conclusion` the
odd-page head carries the **book's** title (`192 The Hindu Sufis of South Asia`, p. 205), whereas in
the Notes, Glossary and Annexes the odd-page head carries the **section** name. **Three pages print
no folio at all — pp. 208, 218 and 221, the three display openers** — and each is cited throughout
as `(p. 208, folio not stated)` and so on. On the −13 relation those would be folios 195, 205 and
208; that is recorded here as a cross-check only and is **never** used as a citation, so **folios
195, 205 and 208 are never printed anywhere in this chunk**.

**The −13 relation held on every page of this chunk that prints a folio — 21 of 24.** 204→191,
205→192, 206→193, 207→194, 209→196, 210→197, 211→198, 212→199, 213→200, 214→201, 215→202, 216→203,
217→204, 219→206, 220→207, 222→209, 223→210, 224→211, 225→212, 226→213, 227→214. **No contradiction
of the rule was found. It was still not used to supply a folio anywhere.**

**The Notes' running head was checked explicitly against the printed chapter headings, and this book
does NOT reproduce the defect described in the briefing.** The running head on every Notes page
(pp. 209–217) is the bare word **`Notes`** — it names **no chapter at all**, on recto or verso, so it
cannot mis-file one. Every chapter attribution comes from a heading **printed inside the notes**, and

whatsoever**, not a wrong one. See Doubts.

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **`silsila` defined, and equated with `tariqa` — the definition the archive's vocabulary needs, and
  it is in the Notes, not the Glossary.** "The word silsila is an Arabic word very often used in
  Sufism. While it can be employed as an equivalent of **tariqa, or path, and brotherhood**, the
  first meaning is **chain**. Thus, it refers to the idea of transmission and legacy." (p. 210,
  folio 197, Ch. 2 n. 2)
- **`gaddi nashin` and `sajjada nashin` distinguished by community and by furniture.** "The gaddi
  nashin is the one who sits on the throne, the gaddi. The gaddi symbolizes the darbar, and the gaddi
  nashin is thus the one who rules on it. **The expression is used by both Muslims and Hindus.**
  Nonetheless, in the Sufi context, the Muslims also use the word **sajjada nashin**, this meaning
  the one who sits on the carpet." (p. 211, folio 198, Ch. 2 n. 3)
- **The `urs` explained as a wedding, which is what the mehndi is for.** "The death of a Sufi saint is
  commemorated as **his wedding with God**. The Sufi is represented as the bride and God as the
  bridegroom. In South Asia, the bride is decorated with **henna** for her wedding." (p. 210,
  folio 197, Ch. 1 n. 16) **This is the note that explains the mehndi ceremony recorded in chunk 006
  at pp. 118 and 125.**
- **Moharram, and who takes part.** "During the first ten days of the Muslim month of Moharram, the
  Shia Muslims commemorate the martyr [text-layer? martyrdom] of their third imam, Husayn… Different
  events are organized in commemoration, especially including processions of **réplicas**
  [text-layer? replicas] of his tomb, known as **tazia**. **Many non-Shias participate, such as Sunni
  Muslims and Hindus.**" (pp. 209–210, folios 196–197, Ch. 1 n. 14)
- **Domes as a shared architectural form across communities.** "the pre-partition temples devoted to
  Jhulelal in Sindh were built with a dome, **as were some Sikh samadhis**. The main site known as
  the darbar of Udero Lal hosts many domes (Boivin and Rajpal 2018)." (p. 210, folio 197,
  Ch. 2 n. 5) And the glossary's pair: "Gunbaz: the dome of a Sufi shrine; in Sindhi, the tower of
  any temple, **Hindu or Sikh**, thus the equivalent of shikara." (p. 219, folio 206); "Shikara: the
  tower of the Hindu temple. In Sindhi, it is called the gunbaz." (p. 220, folio 207)
- **A concentration of shrines counted, and a population share.** "Ulhasnagar was mostly populated by
  Sindhi refugees but throughout the years, other populations from India and beyond migrated to the
  city and today, **the Sindhis would be about 35–40 per cent of the total population**. Nonetheless,
  **Ulhasnagar probably hosts the most numbers of Hindu Sindhi shrines compared to rest of India.**"
  (p. 210, folio 197, Ch. 1 n. 17) **The only quantity of this kind in the chunk; no count of shrines
  is given: (not stated).**
- **The Amils, dated as an administrative caste.** "The Amils were a **subcaste of the Lohanas**.
  Since the **eighteenth century onwards**, they specialized in becoming the **official scribes in
  the administration of the Kalhoras and the Talpurs**." (p. 214, folio 201, Ch. 4 n. 10) **This is
  the note behind "Ramchand is an Amil" in chunk 006 at p. 125, folio 112.**
- **The Ashraf/Ajlaf distinction among Indian Muslims.** "The Ashrafs (sg Sharif) constituted the
  nobility among Indian Muslims. They were descendants of Arab, Turkish, Persian and Afghan
  immigrants. The Indian converts form the group of Ajlafs whose status could be diverse, from
  honourable to impure. It depends usually on their professional activities." (p. 209, folio 196,
  Ch. 1 n. 3)
- **Amils against Bhaibands, as the social basis of Sufi practice.** "it is still relevant to
  distinguish two main groups among the Hindu Sindhis: **the shopkeepers and businessmen** on one
  side, **the ex-Bhaibands, the civil servants working in Indian administration, including education,
  and the ex-Amils** on the other side. Although it is difficult to conduct a systematically
  sociological approach, it is obvious that **the Amils are more involved in Sufism than the
  Bhaibands**. If we want to be even more precise, it is relevant to claim that the '**real**' Sufis,
  to be distinguished from **the devotees who attend the rituals**, belong to this category in their
  majority." (p. 205, folio 192) **The sentence's own grouping is internally odd — the ex-Bhaibands
  are placed with the ex-Amils and against the shopkeepers; see Doubts.**
- **Renunciation as a pre-partition practice that stopped.** "While in pre-partition Sindh, there are
  many stories where businessmen decided to give up all of their worldly goods and activities to

  independent India.**" (p. 205, folio 192)
- **Property seizure after partition, as the Mohajirs' claim.** "The Mohajirs were the Urdu-speaking
  Muslims who had migrated from India in the wake of partition. They were mostly from the Delhi area
  and also from Hyderabad Deccan. **They had left all of their belongings in India, and many thought
  they were legitimate to take over the goods left by the Sindhi Hindus, or even they had the right
  to seize their properties.**" (p. 210, folio 197, Ch. 1 n. 21)

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **Poems dictated in a dream by a dead master**, attributed by the book to the poet herself: "Dadi
  Ganga believed that it was **Dr Rochaldas who visited her in sleep and made her write these
  poems**; hence, his name." (p. 224, folio 211, Annex footnote 6) The author immediately adds the
  limit: "In Gaibi Awaz, some verses are Dadi Dhan's. **It is not possible to know who the real
  composer is.**"
- **A birth claimed by an official tradition**, attributed to the tradition rather than to a witness:
  Shirdi Sai Baba's "origins and early life are unknown, but he settled in Shirdi… **although his
  official tradition claims he was born a Brahman**." (p. 215, folio 202, Ch. 5 n. 10)
- **A visit to Sindh by a ninth-century martyr**, given in the glossary as flat fact: Mansur Hallaj
  "was beheaded in Baghdad in the year 922. **He visited Sindh in 905.**" (p. 220, folio 207)
- **An emperor's invented religion**, hedged: Akbar "**would have** 'invented' a new religion, known
  as din-e illahi, or the Divine Religion, mostly inspired by Sufism." (p. 208, folio not stated,
  Introduction n. 8)
- **The saint as a swan that separates milk from water**, given as the note to a Dalpat verse: "In
  Eastern philosophy, a true saint is a swan – who can separate the milk from the water, the truth
  from the illusion." (p. 222, folio 209, Annex footnote 1)
- **Majnun and Layla as the type of absolute love**: "The story of **Manjnun and Layla** originated in
  seventh-century Arabia, and it spread all over the Muslim world in many different versions. It
  symbolizes the absolute love, at a point where **Majnun** became crazy since Layla's father did not

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The "Sindhi touch", defined by a ritual role rather than by attendance.** "the Sufi culture is a
  part of the darbar culture, but with a main distinction which can perhaps be called the **Sindhi
  touch**: sometimes, it can be a place where not only the Hindus, or non-Muslims, come for the
  pilgrimage (**ziyarat**) but also a place where **the Hindus still play a ritual role**." (p. 204,
  folio 191)
- **A named gap in the literature, and the author's filling of it.** "In her book on ambiguous sacred
  places in South Asia, **Bellamy did not really examine the 'conditions of possibility'** – that is,
  what makes this encounter possible. In Sindh, it was possible thanks to an ideological framework
  provided by the **wujudi ideology, reformulated in the Theosophical perspective through its
  congruence with advaita vedanta**. Consequently, this construction was expressed in the **late
  nineteenth century**." (p. 204, folio 191)
- **Two determinants of the darbar culture's spread.** "Two factors determined the spread of the
  darbar culture: **locality and social agents**. The darbar culture provided answers to local
  challenges, either urban or rural, as related to **competition for domination involving different
  social classes**." (p. 204, folio 191)
- **Thapan's definition tested against 2017 and replaced with a different question.** Thapan "stated
  that a Hindu who calls himself a Sufi was normally one who had taken initiation from a Sufi pir.
  But, this said, a number of clues we have been through in this study would lead us to wonder **if
  this statement is still relevant in 2017, seventy years after partition**… Consequently, I shall
  address the issue with other words: **is it still relevant to speak of a Hindu Sufism in early
  third millennium India? And, if this is the case, what does it mean now to be a Hindu Sufi?**"
  (p. 204, folio 191) **This dates the author's present to 2017.**
- **Poetry as the residue after hinduization — the General Conclusion's central claim.** "More than
  ever, **the core of Sufism is the singing of poetry: I would argue it is the essence of Sufism**,
  especially since, most of the time, **other rituals have been in their majority hinduized**."
  (p. 204, folio 191)
- **What the singing is for, beyond the link to a fatherland.** "Singing Sindhi Sufi poetry is not
  only a matter of keeping a link, through language and music, with **Sindh as a fatherland**. It is
  also the opportunity to praise a vision of society and religion… in which **love plays a
  fundamental role**, and where it is said that the final message of all of the religions, here
  mostly Islam and Hinduism, is, **if not the same, convergent** to the principle of the unity of
  God: **wahdat-e wujud and advaita**." (p. 205, folio 192) With a qualification immediately after:
  "This belief is a compulsory prerequisite for the one who seeks out divine vision, **didar or
  darsan**, and finally divinization, **although this exalted goal is not really the one shared by
  the Sindhi Hindu middle classes**." (p. 205, folio 192)
- **The internet held open as an unanswered question.** "although it would be fascinating to enquire
  into this virtual Sufism, **it will take some more years to know the real impact of this new
  communicative world**. In fact, the main question now is to know if it will help the young
  generations of Hindu Sindhis, all over the world, to keep in touch with the Sindhi Sufi paths."
  (p. 205, folio 192)
- **Three tariqas dominant and two absent — the argument of Annex 2, and the chunk's single most
  citable structural claim.** "The chart above clearly shows that **three main Sufi tariqas were
  dominant among the Hindu Sufis of Sindh** (Boivin 2015). **The Qadiriyya is the most influential,
  as is the case in the whole Indian subcontinent.** The Qalandariyya is not so widespread over it,
  but in the case of Sindh, it reflects the radiation of Lal Shahbaz Qalandar and the pilgrimage
  centre of Sehwan Sharif (Boivin 2012). The third tariqa is the **Sohrawardiyya** (Al-Huda 2003),
  **the oldest Sufi order that settled in Multan and was predominant in the Indus Valley from the
  thirteenth century onwards**. In this chart, there are **two significant absences: the Chishtiyya
  and the Naqshbandiyya.** The Chishtiyya is usually considered to be one of the most powerful Sufi
  orders in Northern India and Punjab. Most of the scholars accept that the order was instrumental in
  using the **sama**, the musical devotional session, to spread their Sufi ideology, as well as Islam
  in the Middle Ages (Bruce and Lawrence 2002). **Maybe because of the Sohrawardiyya's hold on Sindh,
  the Chishtiyya could not spread in Sindh as it**" (p. 227, folio 214) — **the sentence breaks off
  at the chunk boundary.**
- **A gap in the scholarship on the Qadiriyya, stated as a complaint.** "Amazingly, while all of the
  specialists are aware of the situation, **there is a lack of academic studies devoted to the
  Qadiriyya in South Asia.** Yet, there are monographs on the Sohrawardiyya, the Naqshbandiyya and
  the Chishtiyyas, the three other important Sufi tariqas. As of today, **the best academic reference
  is the synthesis written by Arthur Buehler (Buehler 1999)**." (p. 227, folio 214, Annex footnote 8)
- **The Chishtiyya's absence from Sindh, argued twice.** Besides the Annex 2 commentary, the General
  Conclusion's own note: "it is amazing to observe that **the Chishtiyya was not widespread in Sindh,
  while it is dominant in Punjab and North India**." (p. 217, folio 204, General conclusion n. 1)
- **A refusal to identify two figures, on evidentiary grounds.** "It is tempting to think of a
  connection, or even of a fusion, between Lal Shahbaz Qalandar and Jhulelal, **but none of the
  sources allow for us to go that far**. That said, both are today called Jhulelal." (p. 216,
  folio 203, Ch. 7 n. 5)
- **A defence of the term "Islamicate", quoting Hodgson at length.** "Hodgson provides a long
  explanation for why he was compelled to use a new word such as 'Islamicate': 'There has been,
  however, a culture, centred on a lettered tradition, which has been historically distinctive of
  Islamdom the society, and which has been naturally shared in by both Muslims and non-Muslims who
  participate at all fully in the society of Islamdom' (Hodgson 1974: 58)." (p. 208, folio not
  stated, Introduction n. 3)
- **The two functions of "classical", set out as a definition.** "The use of the term 'classical'
  means that two main functions have been attributed to a body of poetry. The first function is that
  this poetry is represented as being **the apex of the relevant aesthetics, an unsurpassable
  expression of the core of the Sindhi identity**. The second function is related to the fact that it
  is represented as **the pattern for the field**, in terms of both poetry and literature, as well as
  culture at large." (p. 208, folio not stated, Introduction n. 6) Against which a competing label is
  recorded: "**Mohanlal Matlani labelled them as asasi Sindhi shairi, or 'Classical Sindhi poetry'**
  (Matlani 2001)." (p. 211, folio 198, Ch. 3 n. 4)
- **A disagreement with another scholar, recorded rather than resolved.** "Regarding the issue of
  diaspora, **Rita Kothari does not agree with my view on this issue and claims that the Sindhis of
  India belong to the larger Sindhi diaspora.** See Kumar and Kothari 2016." (p. 208, folio not
  stated, Introduction n. 2)
- **A correction of a predecessor.** "**Schimmel wrongly claimed** they were all Rohal Faqir's sons
  (Schimmel 1974: 21)." (p. 213, folio 200, Ch. 3 n. 20)
- **A borrowed label, traced.** "Motwani could have borrowed the expression '**great integrators**'
  from a book where it is used for the sants of the Bhakti (Raghavan 1966)." (p. 211, folio 198,
  Ch. 3 n. 3)
- **"Radical" defined, and equated with hindutva.** "Here, 'radical' designates the different Hindu
  groups who claim that the Hindus are the true and authentic – if not the sole – inhabitants of the
  Republic of India. In fact, it could be understood as an equivalent of the **hindutva**." (p. 214,
  folio 201, Ch. 5 n. 1)
- **A conjecture about the first portrait of Ali, and the author's refusal to endorse it.** "On Shia
  iconography, see the comprehensive study by **Ingvild Flaskerud, who thinks the first portrait of
  Ali was first drawn in Sindh and then brought as a gift to Fath Ali Shah (d. 1824)**, the shah of
  Persia who had friendly relations with the Talpurs, the emirs of Sindh, who were themselves Shia
  (Flaskerud : 25)." (p. 213, folio 200, Ch. 4 n. 5) Set against the author's own admission two pages
  later: "**I have been unable to identify the provenance of this representation of Ali.**" (p. 214,
  folio 201, Ch. 5 n. 2) **The two notes are in tension; see Doubts.**
