## What this book is

A primary source in English translation: Christopher Shackle's rendering of the *Risālo*, the
accreted collection of Sindhi lyrical verse attributed to the eighteenth-century Sufi poet Shah
Abdul Latif, issued in 2025 in the Murty Classical Library of India — a series whose general editor
Sheldon Pollock the Acknowledgments page thanks, along with Leena Mitford and Marina Chellini "for
helping me with access to materials from the British Library", Dr. Fahmida Hussain "for her help in
getting books from the Sindhi Language Authority", "the late Ghulam Rabbani Agro, without whose past
kindness over many years I should never have got to the point of embarking on this translation", and
Adriana Cloud of Harvard University Press (p. 318). Across 351 pages, transcribed by the
`text_layer` route, the book runs Shackle's introduction and its endnotes (pp. 8–27), then the thirty
numbered surs as translated verse with nothing beside it but superscript note-markers (pp. 28–316),
then the translator's NOTES apparatus — an unnumbered headnote to each sur, giving its musical mode,
its story and its secondary literature, followed by numbered endnotes keyed to verse (pp. 320–344) —
then a one-page glossary of names running "ahmad" to "yazid" (p. 346) and a bibliography divided
into "Editions and Translations" and "Other Sources" (pp. 348–351). The verse is not printed facing
an original, and the front matter says why: "This is an English only edition of Risalo by Shah Abdul
Latif, published by Havard [text-layer?] University Press in 2018. The Sindhi text has been taken
out and **the introduction has been slightly edited**" (p. 5) — so this is a reprint of Murty volume
18 of 2018 with its Sindhi removed, and no Sindhi script or transliteration appears anywhere in the
ten chunks, which is why no name or date in this file can be given in the original script. Shackle's
stance is declared and sceptical — "The largely hagiographic accounts of his life are of the usual
limited use in constructing a fully detailed biography" (p. 9) — he reads each romance allegorically
and says so each time, and his notes record where editors disagree and where he is himself unsure;
against that, the PDF's text layer is mechanically damaged throughout, dropping the fi/fl ligatures
and the doubled f ("Suf" for *Sufi*, "flled" for *filled*, "sufer" for *suffer*, "of" for *off*), and
because the `text_layer` route produced no transcriber marks at all, **no `[OCR?]` and no
`[illegible]` flag exists anywhere in the ten chunks** to warn a reader where a word has been eaten —
the damage must be assumed present on every quotation below, whether or not it carries a mark.

## Shrines in the archive this book informs

**Read this section knowing what kind of evidence it is.** Every mapping in this book is
biographical rather than topographical: the book names persons, quotes their verse and dates some of
their deaths, and it **never describes a shrine**. Of the five ids mapped across the ten chunks, only
`bhit-bhit-shah` is given a place-name at all, and the notes apparatus calls Bhit a "residence", not
a shrine (pp. 338, 343). `mazar-of-bulleh-shah` and `shrine-of-sachal-sarmast` each rest on a single
comparative aside, and the chunk 009 notes say a reviewer may prefer to demote both to figures not in
the archive. A passing mention is not a body of material, and nothing below should be read as one.

### bhit-bhit-shah — Bhit (Bhit Shah)

The book's names: for the saint, "Shah Abdul Latif", "Shah Latif", and once "Shah Abdul Lati[f]
Bhittai" (p. 5, where the final f is dropped); for the place, "Bhit", "Bhitshah", "the Bhitshah
shrine" (pp. 8–10). Chunk 010 warns that "Bhit" and "Bhitshah" are used as separate spellings and
that the chunk nowhere states they are the same place — "Shah Latif's residence at Bhit" (p. 343)
against the imprint "Bhitshah" (pp. 348, 350) and the title "Shāh Abdul Latīf of Bhit" (p. 351).

**Where the material is, and where it is not.** All of this row's substance sits in Shackle's
introduction (pp. 8–27) and in four short glosses in the notes (pp. 326, 335, 338, 343), plus
imprint names in the bibliography (pp. 348, 350). Chunk 007 adds Bhit twice as a place the rains
reach (p. 261). **Chunks 002, 003, 004 and 005 contribute only the poet's signature inside the
verse**, and each of those four chunks flags its own mapping as inferential: the chunks supply
"says Latif" / "says Shah" / "says Abdul Latif" and the running head "risalo", and never say that
the speaker is Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai or that he is buried at Bhit — the identification rests on
the title page, which is not in those chunks. **Chunks 006 and 008 map no archive id at all**, and
chunk 006 records that "Bhit, Bhit Shah and the shrine of Shah Abdul Latif are not mentioned
anywhere in it".

**Saint.**
- **Dates, given two ways within five pages, and both kept.** The introduction prints them flat:
  the *Risālo* is "a large collection of Sindhi lyrical poetry by the eighteenth-century Suf
  [text-layer?] poet Shah Abdul Latif (1689–1752) of Bhit, near Hyderabad in modern-day Pakistan"
  (p. 8). The book's own Library of Congress record hedges both: "'Abd al-Laṭīf, Shah, approximately
  1689-approximately 1752, author" (p. 5). Chunk 001's instruction on this stands: **do not present
  1689 as documented on this book's authority** — the introduction gives no birth evidence at all.
- **The notes apparatus gives no year for him whatever.** Chunk 009 records that no birth or death
  year for Shah Latif appears anywhere in the NOTES range (pp. 320–340), and chunk 010 adds none
  (pp. 341–351); every chronology in those two chunks is "(not stated)". The years above are the
  introduction's alone.
- **Death date as recorded at the site**: "where the date of his death is recorded as 14 Safar
  a.h. 1166 (1752 CE)" (p. 10). The Hijri form is printed in Latin transliteration only; no
  Arabic-script or Sindhi-script date appears anywhere in the ten chunks.
- **Sayyid status**: "The honorifc [text-layer?] title Shah indicates his status as a Sayyid claiming
  direct descent from the prophet Muhammad" (pp. 8–9). The damaged word sits on "honorific" inside
  this fact.
- **A hereditary pīr lineage of rural Sindh**: he "belonged to one of the many lineages of hereditary
  Suf [text-layer?] saints established in the countryside of Sindh, where they have always enjoyed
  great prestige and power as pīrs or holy men with a special spiritual authority" (p. 8).
- **Silsila: the book says there was none, and hedges that too.** "unlike many of the pīrs of Sindh
  or leading Sufs [text-layer?] in other parts of South Asia, he was not formally afliated
  [text-layer?] in a chain of spiritual descent to any of the great Suf [text-layer?] orders. He is
  therefore generally classed as an Uvaisi Suf [text-layer?], the term given to those whose spiritual
  initiation comes directly from divine inspiration without any saintly human intermediary" (p. 9).
  Shackle prefaces the whole of it with "It appears that" — the hedge is his. Four damaged words sit
  inside this one fact, including "afliated" for *affiliated*. Spelling to carry as printed:
  "Uvaisi".
- **Formation**: "a member of the rural elite who were trained in the Islamic sciences as transmitted
  through Arabic and Persian, the standard languages of education" (p. 9).
- **Kin and named contacts**: his great-great-grandfather Shah Abdul Karim (d. 1623) of Bulri
  (p. 9, and see `shrine-of-shah-abdul-karim-bulri` below); his older contemporary Shah Inat Rizvi
  (d. 1711) of Nasarpur, another pīr he "is also known to have been in contact with" (p. 9).
- **A father, unnamed**: a verse of sur 12 "is supposed to be a response by Shah Latif to his
  father's appeal in 12.75" (p. 331). The father is not named and no date is given (not stated).
- **Travel, presented as inference from the poetry rather than as itinerary**: "The wide-ranging
  references in Risālo to many diferent [text-layer?] locations in and around Sindh **support the
  claim** that Shah Latif traveled extensively as a young man" (p. 10).
- **His three constant books**: "All the sources agree that he kept three books with him as his
  primary sources of continual inspiration: the Qur'an, from whose verses he so frequently quotes in
  Arabic; Rumi's great Persian Masnavī; and the Sindhi verses of his ancestor Shah Karim" (p. 23).
- **Iconography**: "In popular iconography, Shah Latif is often depicted in the yogic posture of
  sitting with his head supported by his knees" (p. 335) — from the note glossing Mount Sinai in
  sur 18, and the only description of how he is pictured anywhere in the ten chunks.
- **Colonial-era notice of him**: "Richard Burton's notice of Shah Latif in his classic frsthand
  [text-layer?] account of premodern Sindh (Burton 1851: 81–84)" (p. 26, n. 10).
- **The signature inside the verse, in four forms, across every verse chunk** — "says Latif",
  "says Shah", "says Abdul Latif", "Abdul Latif says" (pp. 30, 33, 35, 36, 39; 45, 48, 49, 56, 58,
  68, 74, 76, 77, 80; 81, 82, 85, 93, 97, 99, 104–107, 112, 113; 119, 120, 124, 133, 142, 154; 162,
  163, 173, 174, 177, 183, 186, 187, 189, 194–196; 197, 200, 204, 208, 210, 213, 214, 219, 222,
  225–228, 231; 234, 236, 239, 240, 242, 244, 250, 251, 253, 258, 260, 263, 266, 272; 276, 279–284,
  287, 290, 292, 296, 301–303, 307, 308). The forms are interchangeable within a single sur — "Through
  divine favor she crossed the waves, says Latif" against "Love destroys the force of the current,
  says Shah", both on p. 99 — and no chunk offers any comment on the variation. **One outlier**: "In
  our difculties, Ali says, the beloved answers our call" (p. 271) stands outside the pattern, and
  chunk 007 cannot determine whether it is the poet under another name, a quotation of another
  speaker, or an error.
- **His claim for his own verse, in the poem and in the note**: "What you consider to be poems are
  divine verses. They direct the mind toward the beloved" (p. 109); and "In this famous line, Shah
  Latif claims for his poems (S. baita) a status comparable to the verses of the Qur'an (S. āyatūṉ)"
  (p. 326). Shackle offers the further reading twice hedged: the contrast "**might be seen as** an
  indirect claim for the status of the Risālo as a 'Qur'an in the Sindhi language'… Equally, **it
  might well be said** the Risālo is one of those very rare instances in the literary history of
  South Asia of a genuinely integral Indo-Islamic creation" (p. 24).
- **A tradition about his last composition, reported and not endorsed**: of the long vāī in sur 7,
  "There is a tradition that it was Shah Latif's fnal composition, and this is refected in its
  placement at the end of the entire Risālo in Kazi 1961; compare Baloch 2012" (p. 326). Both damaged
  words, "fnal" and "refected", sit inside the sentence.

**Shrine / Darbar.**
- **The settlement and the name**: "he later settled in the desert near Hyderabad in lower Sindh at
  Bhit, a place now known in his honor as Bhitshah ('Shah's Dune')" (p. 10).
- **The tomb and its patron**: Bhitshah "is the site of the magnifcent [text-layer?] tomb constructed
  in his honor by the local ruler Ghulam Shah Kalhoro, where the date of his death is recorded as 14
  Safar a.h. 1166 (1752 CE)" (p. 10). **No construction date and no dates for the ruler are given**
  (not stated), and the damaged "magnifcent" is the whole of the book's architectural description.
  Spelling to carry as printed: "Ghulam Shah Kalhoro".
- **Succession, with no custodial institution named**: "Although he left no son to assume
  responsibility for the Bhitshah shrine, his disciples ensured that it became the center of his
  cult" (p. 10). **No sajjada nashin, no custodial family, no Auqaf and no modern administration
  appears anywhere in the ten chunks** (not stated). This disciple-not-family arrangement is the only
  custodianship the book describes.
- **The only reference to the shrine's physical appearance is on the copyright page**: the cover is
  an "Illustration based on the Sufi Shrine of Shah Abdul Lati [text-layer?] Bhittai in Pakistan"
  (p. 5) — also the only use of the epithet "Bhittai" in chunk 001.
- **The notes call Bhit a residence, not a shrine.** "Kirar is the name of a depression near Shah
  Latif 's place of residence at Bhit. It flls with water in the rainy season" (p. 338); "A place
  near Shah Latif's residence at Bhit." (p. 343). **No shrine, tomb, grave, urs, custodian or
  building is mentioned at Bhit anywhere in the notes apparatus**, and the word used is "place of
  residence" only. Chunk 010 asks whether an eleven-word note glossing a verse it cannot read is
  worth citing at all.
- **Bhit inside the poem, twice, as a place the rains reach**: "It has rained over Bhit and flled the
  pools of Kirar" (p. 261); and at the next verse, "The rains have flled the sky and have come
  rejoicing to Bhit. The north wind brings fowerlike fashes of lightning. The greenery is fragrant,
  and grass is piled in heaps" (p. 261); the pools stand alone at the third verse of the run, "It has
  flled the pools of Kirar and rained upon the Variyasu desert" (p. 261). Chunk 007 records that
  Bhit here is connected to no shrine, grave, residence, building or practice, and that the mapping
  "rests on a place-name in a rain catalogue and nothing else"; what Kirar is, and how it stands to
  Bhit, is not stated in that chunk and is supplied only by the note at p. 338 above. Five damaged
  words sit in these three verses ("flled", "fowerlike", "fashes").
- **An institution bearing the site's name, from the imprints only**: "Hyderabad: Shah Abdul Latif
  Bhitshah Saqafati Markaz Kameti" (p. 348), "Bhitshah Cultural Centre Committee" (pp. 348, 350),
  "Shah Abdul Latif Bhitshah Cultural Centre Committee" (p. 348), and "Bhitshah, Hyderabad" as a
  place of publication (p. 350). Chunk 010 says nothing about this body's relation to the shrine.

**Practices & Events.**
- **The shrine's musical tradition is attributed to the poet himself**: the cult included "the
  elaborate tradition of musical performance of his poetry **that he himself had devised**" (p. 10).
  This is the only recurring practice the book records at the site.
- **The Ganj as a shrine-made manuscript**: the cult also preserved "the collection of poetry by Shah
  Latif and others that was carefully assembled in the historic manuscript known as the Ganj, dated
  a.h. 1207 (1792 CE)" (p. 10). Where the Ganj is held is not stated. An edition of it appears in the
  bibliography under the same institution's name: "Mirza, Mumtaz. 1994. The Ganj of Shah Abdul Latif
  Bhitai. Hyderabad: Bhitshah Cultural Centre Committee" (p. 348).
- **Oral composition and disciple transcription**: "Shah Latif's verses were frst [text-layer?]
  extemporized orally in speech or song, then unsystematically recorded in writing by disciples"
  (p. 10).
- **Urs: not stated, and the negative is comprehensive.** No urs date or month, no mela, no langar,
  no offerings, no endowment, no land, no revenue, no visitor numbers, no disputes and no state
  involvement appears at Bhit anywhere in the ten chunks. Chunk 001 records the absence for the
  introduction; chunks 004, 005 and 006 each record it again for their forty-page ranges of verse.

**Legends & Miracles.**
- **Uvaisi initiation** — spiritual initiation coming "directly from divine inspiration without any
  saintly human intermediary" (p. 9), reported by Shackle as the standard classification and
  introduced by "It appears that", not as verified fact.
- **Time among the yogis** — "As a young man, Shah Latif **is believed** to have spent time with"
  the wandering yogis who crossed Sindh on pilgrimage to Hinglaj (p. 20). The hedge is the book's.
- **The Sunni/Shia anecdote** — "When Shah Latif was asked if he was Sunni or Shia, he frst
  [text-layer?] replied that he was in between the two, and when told there was nothing in between,
  he gave the perfect Suf [text-layer?] response by stating, 'I am that nothing'" (pp. 22–23).
  Shackle calls it "an interesting anecdote" and sources it to Advani 1970: 32 (p. 26, n. 29).
- **No karamat at the tomb, and none anywhere.** No healing, no intercession and no miracle at the
  site appears in the introduction (chunk 001), and chunks 002, 003, 004 and 005 each record that no
  karamat of any saint and no miracle at any site occurs in their ranges. Every marvel in this book
  is narrated of a poem's persona.

**Significance.**
- **Shackle's own caution governs every life-fact above**: "The largely hagiographic accounts of his
  life are of the usual limited use in constructing a fully detailed biography" (p. 9).
- **Against the "unlettered genius" reading**: "It would however be misleading to see his poetry as
  the entirely original product of an unlettered genius" (p. 9), on three grounds — the rural elite's
  training, Shah Abdul Karim's pioneering Sindhi poems a century earlier, and Shah Inat Rizvi's
  near-contemporary collection "strikingly similar in scope" — concluding: "There was, therefore,
  already an established culture in Sindh of vernacular Suf [text-layer?] poetry, although this tends
  to be overshadowed by Shah Latif's unique reputation in the literary histories" (p. 9).
- **The shrine cult, not the poet, produced the text.** "The Risālo emerged gradually from various
  collections of the verses Shah Latif had produced on many diferent [text-layer?] occasions"
  (p. 10), after oral extemporization and unsystematic transcription; what fixed it was the Ganj of
  a.h. 1207 (1792 CE), some forty years after the recorded death date of a.h. 1166 (p. 10).
- **Indirection as his distinguishing manner**, stated by contrast with two other mapped figures:
  "The oblique reference to this notorious expression, so frequently invoked by other Suf poets like
  Bullhe Shah and Sachal Sarmast, is a telling illustration of Shah Latif's preference for the
  indirect expression of a profoundly Suf understanding of the world" (p. 326).
- **Popularity across boundaries**: use of the vernacular by Shah Latif and Bullhe Shah "has
  certainly helped to ensure their continuing popularity across religious boundaries and modern
  national frontiers today" (p. 12).
- **Institutional afterlife under colonial rule**: "Risālo was pressed into service for the
  examinations prescribed by the new education syllabus" (p. 13).
- **How his reputation is contested, in Shackle's words**: Pakistani scholarship's interpretations
  are "tied to local preoccupations, as when Shah Latif is too narrowly seen as an authentic
  spokesman of the Sindhi folk tradition or as an advocate of Sindhi nationalism" (p. 13, citing
  Syed 1996 at p. 26, n. 13), while Indian scholars "naturally tend to view Shah Latif as one of the
  many great premodern poet saints who helped to construct the national identity of modern India"
  (p. 13); Shackle's own corrective is "the common emphasis upon the Islamic character of Shah
  Latif's poetry" (p. 14), credited above all to Schimmel 1976 (p. 26, n. 14).
- **The most quoted line, per the notes**: "The fnal invocation of God's blessings on Sindh is one of
  the most often quoted verses of the Risālo" (p. 338) — the verse itself reads "Oh my God, make
  Sindh fourish forever. Dear friend and sweet beloved, make the whole world prosperous" (p. 265).
  Both quotations carry damage ("fnal", "fourish").
- **A judgement against the shrine relation itself, in the poet's voice** — worth recording in a
  shrine archive: "Do not let yourself be worshiped, oh yogi, curses on people's devotion… keep your
  mouth from pretense" (p. 237); "Do not let yourself be worshiped, oh yogi, but maintain your yoga.
  Oh master, to make people your servants is a great fault" (p. 237).

**Saint.**

  Jhok. For another quotation from this poet, see 7.5" (p. 321).

**Significance.**

### mazar-of-bulleh-shah — Mazar of Bulleh Shah

**A comparative figure, mapped on the name alone.** Chunk 009 records that this mapping rests on "a
single comparative aside" and that a reviewer "may prefer to demote" it to figures not in the
archive, "since the chunk's interest in them is purely as parallel poets"; chunk 001 gives a little
more, but all of it is literary comparison. The spelling printed throughout is **"Bullhe Shah"**, not
the archive's "Bulleh Shah"; the archive names the figure "Bulleh Shah (Abdullah Shah Qadri)" and the
book gives no second name, so the identification rests on the name alone. Kasur, his grave and any
shrine are **not stated** anywhere in the ten chunks.

**Saint.**
- "his Panjabi contemporary Bullhe Shah (d. 1758)" (p. 12). **Contradiction kept**: chunk 001
  records that the archive index carries 1757 for this row. One year apart; the book's 1758 and the
  archive's 1757 stand side by side, unreconciled.

**Significance.**
- **Vernacular reach**: use of the vernacular by Shah Latif and Bullhe Shah "has certainly helped to
  ensure their continuing popularity across religious boundaries and modern national frontiers
  today" (p. 12).
- **The contrast that defines Shah Latif's manner**: Shah Latif is harder to pin down "especially in
  comparison with the more familiar outspoken style of other well-known Suf [text-layer?] poets of
  the region, like his Panjabi contemporary Bullhe Shah" (p. 23). The same contrast is made again in
  the notes on *anā 'l-ḥaqq*, where he and Sachal Sarmast are the poets who "so frequently invoked"
  the phrase Shah Latif preferred to approach obliquely (p. 326).
- **Genre**: the kāfī is "the primary genre of Suf [text-layer?] poetry in Panjabi" (p. 14), with a
  Bullhe Shah kāfī transliterated in Shackle 2015: xvi–xvii (p. 25, n. 16).
- **The spinning theme** is referred to him with a bibliographic pointer — "like Bullhe Shah (compare
  Shackle 2015: xx–xxi, 3–7, 293–319)" (p. 339).

No shrine, darbar, practice, event or legend material attaches to this row anywhere in the ten
chunks.

### shrine-of-shah-abdul-karim-bulri — Bulri Shah Karim

**Mapped in chunk 001 only, and to the person rather than the site.** Chunk 001 mapped it on a match
of name, death year and place-name against the archive row (Bulri Shah Karim, Tando Muhammad Khan
District), and then said plainly what the book contributes: "the book contributes biography and
literary history here, not shrine evidence. A reviewer who wants a stricter standard should demote
these bullets." **The chunk says nothing about a shrine, tomb, urs or custodianship at Bulri** (not
stated), and no later chunk mentions Shah Abdul Karim or Bulri at all.

**Saint.**
- "His great-great-grandfather Shah Abdul Karim (d. 1623) of Bulri in southern Sindh, who had been a
  noted spiritual teacher in his own right, also composed pioneering Suf [text-layer?] poems in
  Sindhi" (p. 9). The relationship stated is to Shah Abdul Latif; the damaged word sits on "Sufi"
  inside the description of his poems.
- **Transmission of his verse**: "These were recorded soon after Karim's death in a lengthy Persian
  memoir composed by a disciple and are known to have been treasured by Shah Latif" (p. 9). The
  memoir is neither named nor dated (not stated).

**Significance.**
- His Sindhi verses were one of the three books Shah Latif kept constantly by him, beside the Qur'an
  and Rumi's *Masnavī* (p. 23).
- He is one of Shackle's three grounds for rejecting the "unlettered genius" reading of Shah Latif,
  and so for the claim that "There was, therefore, already an established culture in Sindh of
  vernacular Suf [text-layer?] poetry" (p. 9).
- "Jotwani 1996 includes a complete translation of Shah Karim's Sindhi verses" (p. 25, n. 2).

### shrine-of-sachal-sarmast — Shrine of Sachal Sarmast

**The thinnest mapping in the book: one comparative aside.** He is named once, as the second of the
"other Suf poets like Bullhe Shah and Sachal Sarmast" who invoked *anā 'l-ḥaqq* — "Ar. anā 'l-ḥaqq
'I am God'", attributed in the same note to Mansur, "the great Suf martyr" — directly, where Shah
Latif preferred indirection (p. 326). **No shrine, no place and no date** (not stated). Chunk 009
records that a human may prefer to demote this row to figures not in the archive.

Two bibliography entries name him, and **chunk 010 expressly declined to map either to this row**,
on the ground that "a title is evidence a book exists, not evidence about a shrine": "Advani, Kalyan
B. 1971. Sachal Sarmast. New Delhi: Sahitya Akademi" (p. 349) and "Allana, G., trans. 1996. Four
Classical Poets of Sind: Shah Inat, Shah Abdul Latif, Sachal Sarmast, Sami" (p. 349). They are
recorded here so that a reviewer can see what the mapping does and does not rest on.
## Cross-cutting material

### The yogi and *jogi* ascetic complex, and its pilgrimage geography

**The pilgrimage is presented as a real route through Sindh, not a poetic invention.** The
introduction describes "the wandering yogis who traversed Sindh during their pilgrimage to the
shrine of the goddess at Hinglaj in Balochistan", and makes it the subject of three surs —
"Ramakali", "Khahori", "Purab" (p. 20). As a young man Shah Latif "**is believed** to have spent
time with these yogis" (p. 20); the hedge is the book's. The endnotes add the circuit's
end-points: Hinglaj is "A remote site in Balochistan, sacred to the goddess (S. nānī), and an
important center of pilgrimage for the yogis" (p. 335), reached from "The hill in District
Hyderabad in southern Sindh that marks the starting point of the pilgrimage to Hinglaj" (p. 336),
whose name is (not stated) there; the glossary names that hill only as "ganjo Hill in southern
Sindh marking the start of the pilgrimage to Hinglaj" and gives "hinglaj (Hinglāj) Pilgrimage
site in Balochistan" (p. 346). The yogis' homeland "is regularly said to lie east, i.e., in the
Gangetic valley where so many of the sacred sites of Hinduism are located" (p. 334), "Hind
denotes the Gangetic region, as opposed to Sindh and the Indus valley" (p. 336), and sur 20's
name "(S. pūrabu 'the east') relates to the Gangetic region that is the home of the yogis"
(p. 337). Further destinations glossed: Dwarka, "A city in Gujarat, closely associated with
Krishna. But Shiv is the god to whom most yogis are particularly devoted" (p. 335); "Another
remote destination that the yogis make pilgrimage to" (p. 336), unnamed; and *Lahut*, either "a
member of a particular sect of yogis or coming from Lahut, the name of a small place in Sindh on
the yogis' pilgrimage route" (p. 335), the verb form glossed again as "became Lahutis" (S.
lāhūtī thiā)" (p. 337).

**In the verse the same geography is named directly.** "The naked ones have gone to Hinglaj⁸ to
behold the goddess. The devotees of Shiv rejoice at the sight of Dwarka.⁹ Their guide is Ali¹⁰"
(p. 225); "With luck you will meet again with the yogis who have gone to Hinglaj" (p. 238); "The
yogis told me to travel to Hinglaj" (p. 254); "Abandoning the comfort of their own country, they
have eagerly gone to the Ganga" (p. 238); "Some painful thought drives them toward Kabul.¹⁴ The
yogis were talking yesterday about a lodge there" (p. 229), and again "This is the way that the
yogis come to Kabul.²³" (p. 236); "Leaving the settlement on Mount Ganjo,³¹ they eagerly set out"
(p. 240), with four further Ganjo verses in sur 19 (pp. 247–248); "Groups of Khahoris went to
the Pab range" (p. 245). The east is both destination and category: "They ofered the blind the
way to the east" (p. 239), "the naked ones have departed, and none of the easterners remains"
(p. 239), "The yogis from the east shut down their camp and left in the middle of the night"
(p. 254), "The ascetics took me to the land of the east" (p. 254). And the pilgrimage is made
its own object: "That is the goal of my pilgrimage, and my resting place; that is my journey"
(p. 254).

**Becoming a jogi, with its two visible marks.** "Today, mother, I will wash my clothes and color
them with ochre. I will become a yogi, mother, do not try to stop me. For the sake of my Hôt
Baloch, I will wear large rings in my ears.¹⁰" (p. 122) — ochre-dyed cloth and large ear-rings
are the whole of what the verse specifies. The notes gloss both: "Yogis wear ochre-colored
clothes and have their ears split to accommodate their large earrings" (p. 328), and "The
'split-ear' order of yogis whose initiation involves splitting the cartilage of the ear in order
to insert the large wooden rings by which they are distinguished" (p. 335). The verse calls them
"The Kapat yogis¹³", who "have their ears pierced and slit for earrings. As lovers, they sit
forever facing the north wind. They fast and mortify their bodies" (p. 228); also "seal your
mouth with rings. The ears you split countless times have made no diference to you" (p. 230),
"Why get your ears split if you cannot endure the cold?" (pp. 230–231), "Yogi, you have got your
ears split, do not become a woman" (p. 231).

**Appearance and dress.** "Yesterday we met a yogi,¹ a wandering beggar. The master had a ring of
cloth on his head and a fne [text-layer?] necklace around his neck" (p. 184); "a yogi whose body
was covered with clay. Wearing a green shawl around his shoulders, the master had a necklace of
gold" (p. 184); "dear yogi who wears a loincloth" (p. 185); "the yogis who charm snakes" (p. 185)
and "you have made great enemies of the snake-charming yogis" (p. 257); "He slipped of
[text-layer?] leaving his staf [text-layer?] behind,⁶" (p. 189). The chunk notes record that
**yogi**, **fakir**, **ascetic**, **master**, **sannyasi** and **renouncer** are used of the same
figure (pp. 184–193, 234–244); the printed spelling varies between "faqirs" (p. 240) and "fakirs"
(p. 236) within one section.

**The quilt as the marked garment, and the guru's quilt as an institution.** "Wearing quilts, the
yogis spend time together with bells tied to their sides" (p. 235); "those masters whose quilts
contain roses" (p. 235); "Standing to one side of the bazaar, the yogis in their quilts remember
the guru" (p. 235). Then renounced — "Horns, strings, and quilts—these three they have abandoned,
along with their sacred threads" (p. 242); "Curses on your quilts and coverings, set your
blankets on fre" (p. 242) — and then upheld: "The quilt the guru gave me is my pride. Oh
disciple, sit cross-legged and wear it with reverence" (p. 242); "It would be disgraceful to
remove the quilt given by the guru" (p. 243). The endnote glosses the object socially: "Cotton
quilts (S. ruliyūṉ) are associated with the poor" (p. 336).

**The ten- and twelve-day discipline, itemised.** Matted braids and topknots, worn hair-strings,
dust-coated clothes (p. 226); kindling fire in the lodges from "sweepings and straw" (p. 226);
leaning on crutches (p. 226); rubbing ashes from the fire on their bodies and "Begging for a
little grain" (p. 227); washing their clothes on the seventh day and standing "With folded arms…
before God the unseen" (p. 227); going "from place to place" on the eighth (p. 227); completing
pilgrimages and entering "the sacred enclosure" on the eleventh, "They have permanently kept
silent" (p. 228); "The yogis all long for this pilgrimage" on the twelfth (p. 228). Elsewhere:
"They have made their bodies thin by binding them with leather bands" (p. 236); "The yogis, says
Latif, start up at midnight. Seeing the world asleep, they set of on their travels" (p. 236);
"years sitting with their foreheads dirty with dust" (p. 234); "ashes are the perfume they bathe
in" (p. 242); "All the fakirs beg for is the medicine that induces silence" (p. 236); "They keep
no possessions other than their loincloth" (p. 237); "Those who wear the loincloth around them do
not perform ablutions.²⁴ They have heard the call to prayer that preceded Islam.²⁵" (p. 237),
against "wash their loincloths every day" and "They keep their bodies free from impurity"
(p. 235). The two notes behind that couplet are "the ritual ablution (S. vuzū) performed before
the Muslim prayers" (p. 336) and "Instead of the Arabic call to prayer (S. bāngu) they hear the
pre-Islamic sound of the sacred Hindu syllable (Skt. oṃ)" (pp. 335–336).

**The lodge as an institution, and its emptying.** "When I see the lodge where they stayed I am
overcome by grief" (p. 224); "'Alas, alas!' I cry in their lodge" (p. 225); "The lodge where they
stay is nonexistence" (p. 225); "Master, your shelter stands before me like a thorn.¹⁵ Oh naked
one, why did you build it if you were going to leave it?" (p. 229). A five-verse anaphora carries
the emptying: "In the lodge today²⁷ there is no gathering of the yogis" (p. 238); "In the lodge
today the seekers do not talk. The yogis have got up and left; their lodges are the death of me"
(p. 238); "In the lodge today there is no assembly of the yogis" (p. 238); "In the lodge today
those who wept aloud are not there… They have closed their lodge, which now lies silent, and no
instruments play there" (pp. 238–239); "Today there is no smoke or fame²⁹ in the lodge" (p. 239).
Earlier, "They flled the cup of love and drank deeply from it. After that they closed their
lodges and left" (p. 234); and the counsel to stay, "Remain in your cell for as long as your
beloved lives. Leave the lodge only when he departs" (p. 241). The bonfire is the institutional
sign: "there a bonfre³ burns. Who else would light it besides a company of Khahoris?" (p. 248);
glossed "From the bonfres that yogis light wherever they stay" (p. 336) and "A burning bonfre is
the sign of a yogi's camping place" (p. 337).

**Mendicancy, policed from inside and mostly refused.** "They do not carry a bowl to beg with, nor
do they ask for anything from people's houses" (p. 235); "If those who carry a bowl and beg only
realized, they would receive alms in the desert without begging for a single moment" (p. 236);
"They starve and are dying of hunger, but they do not beg from anyone" (p. 236); "See, they have
thrown their begging bowls on the ground and put aside their stafs" (p. 243); "They have thrown
their begging bowl to the ground and smashed it to pieces" (p. 242). Against these, "Hunger is
the charity for which they beg" (p. 242) and "Go and beg from the band of yogis who understand
but say that they do not" (p. 229), set against "Yogis who search for food are false. Accursed
are the wretches who look after their bellies" (p. 231).

**The Khahoris as a foraging community.** "The Khahoris left at dawn with their water pouches"
(p. 245); "They carry dried-up water pouches under their arms and wear rope sandals on their
feet" (p. 247); "The plants the foragers gather do not grow less" (p. 246); "There the Khahoris
wander, picking their food from the trees" (p. 248); "The foragers come for the wild grain that
grows in the wilderness" (p. 250); "The mark of the Khahoris is that the clothes they wear are
all torn" (p. 247); "They do not take strong animals to ride as they go on their distant way"
(p. 247); courtyards at pp. 246 and 250; and the renunciation of livelihood and learning — they
"left their fourishing crops in their search for God.²", "closed up all their books in their
desire for God" (p. 247); "Today the tents of the Khahoris are no longer here" (p. 250). Glossed
twice: "The yogis (S. khāhoṛī) who live on whatever they can fnd in the wilderness" (p. 329) and
"the yogis who roamed the wilderness gathering the vegetation that was their sole diet" (p. 336),
with the foraged plants "Gathered with great difculty" and symbolising "spiritual knowledge that
is to be gleaned only with a comparable efort" (p. 336).

**The guru as the authority the yogi answers to**, in six places: "They have found the path of the
guru, says Latif" (p. 227); "They who fnd [text-layer?] union with the guru are exalted" (p. 228);
"Then, master, you will not be faulted before the guru" (p. 230); "the yogis in their quilts
remember the guru" (p. 235); "They have fnished of their bodies on the path directed by the guru"
(p. 240); "You have not come close to your guru but proclaim that you have been rewarded with his
favor" (pp. 253–254). Also "The mantras that the yogis utter" (p. 336), and the rosary — "With
your heart smoking with love, turn the beads of the rosary in your mind" (p. 229), "If only I
could fnd their rosary beads, their form, and their wisdom, all of which I have forgotten"
(p. 239).

**Islamic ritual vocabulary transferred onto the ascetic body.** "Their knees are a mihrab,²⁰ and
their bodies are a mosque… Proclaiming the divine reality, they have renounced the body" (p. 232);
"The beloved's face is their mihrab,²¹ the entire world is their mosque. They have given up the
Qur'an, and the tablet telling right from wrong" (p. 232); "The knees of the sannyasis are like
Mount Sinai.¹⁹" (p. 231); "Their hearts point the direction to Mecca, their bodies circumambulate
the Kaaba" (p. 232). The mihrab is glossed as "The niche in the wall of a mosque marking the
direction of Mecca toward which worshipers ofer prayer" (p. 335), and the Mount Sinai note is
where Shackle records that "In popular iconography, Shah Latif is often depicted in the yogic
posture of sitting with his head supported by his knees" (p. 335).

**Ascetic indifference stated as a test, and the test's inversions.** "Kak did not detain the
ascetics, its palaces did not beguile them. They were not trapped by the snares of the ladies or
their maidservants. The yogis paid no attention to the lovely women" (p. 187), and the near-repeat
"Kak did not detain the ascetics, its riches did not beguile them. Being in a lofty spiritual
state, they caused sufering [text-layer?] to the lovely women" (pp. 187–188); conversely "To see
them, says Latif, they have become ascetics. The yogis come to Kak, dyed red by love" (p. 187).
The criterion is total severance, stated four times in one formula: "If you think of becoming a
yogi,¹⁶ then break all ties" (p. 229, twice), "then control your mind and destroy it within"
(p. 229), "then drink the cup of nonbeing" (p. 230), "then seal your mouth with rings" (p. 230),
with "How can those who maintain the least connection with the world be called true ascetics?"
(p. 228). Renunciation is tested by hunger rather than observance: "God is still far away from the
sannyasi who is concerned about clothing and food" (p. 240); "The yogis have flled their bags with
hunger and rejoice" (p. 239); "For the yogis, hunger and thirst are like the celebrations of
ordinary people. The intoxicated faqirs observe the fast, the ascetics stay away from Eid"
(p. 240).

**Yoga placed outside law, worship and lineage.** "They do not bow down to the lord, nor does the
lord make them bow down. They do not make yoga an object of pilgrimage or worship" (pp. 234–235);
"Why should they ask about the religious law when the court is within them?²²" (p. 236); "They
have no ancestry or lineage, no mother or father" (p. 237); "How can you ask them about their
caste?" (p. 229). Against that, "It was not the naked ones who bowed to their lord, but their love
that made them bow before him" (p. 234). And eligibility is denied to the poem's speaker: "Yoga is
proper for yogis, and it is yogis whom yoga suits… Ah, alas for me that I did not learn yoga"
(p. 230); "You are not worthy of yoga, why do you talk about it?" (p. 230).

**Being worshipped named as the yogi's characteristic fault — a judgement against the shrine
relation itself, in the poet's own voice**, twice in succession: "Do not let yourself be worshiped,
oh yogi, curses on people's devotion… keep your mouth from pretense" (p. 237); "Do not let
yourself be worshiped, oh yogi, but maintain your yoga. Oh master, to make people your servants is
a great fault" (p. 237).

**The yogis' confessional placement is asserted both ways and reconciled nowhere.** "For what
purpose do the yogis follow this path? Their hearts are not set on hell, nor do they desire
paradise. They have nothing to do with unbelievers, and they do not have Islam in their minds"
(pp. 232–233) — beside "Their guide is Ali" on the Hinglaj–Dwarka pilgrimage (p. 225), "Their souls
are entwined in secret with Ram.¹²" and "Ram always dwells in their hearts" (p. 227), "Ram dwells
in their soul, they speak of nothing else" (p. 234), "They worship Shiv all the time, and wash
their loincloths every day" (p. 235), "Worshipers of Shiv, they stand far from people's homes"
(pp. 235–236), "Day and night their gaze is fxed [text-layer?] on the goddess.¹⁸" (p. 231), and
"While traveling, they met Ram and were spared the rest of the journey" (p. 240). Shackle's own
comment: "The multiple religious references in this verse are remarkable for their blurring of
conventional boundaries" (p. 335); "As elsewhere, Shah Latif's description of the yogis' freedom
from narrow religious constraints moves naturally from a Hindu to an Islamic frame of reference"
(p. 336); and Ram is "used as the common Hindu name for God, although yogis are characteristically
associated with Shiv" (p. 336).

**Marvels attributed to the ascetics.** "Consuming their ego, they have gone, and their feet did
not touch the ground" (p. 225); "The masters have been turned into gold" (p. 226); "They have
brought signs of some great realm" (p. 227); "The yogi's face was flled [text-layer?] with a light
like that of the sun at dawn. A fragrance like that of attar came from his forehead" (p. 184); "The
master shed a light like that of the full moon. Darkness was removed by the yogi's light" (p. 193);
"The whole land was made fragrant by the scent of musk. It was from there that the perfect master
came" (p. 193); "The rain of divine favor fell on the huts of the whole community of yogis"
(p. 234); "The restorers of my soul to life, those spiritual beings, have departed" (p. 238);
"Being with the yogis has brought us to life and removed our grief" (p. 239); "The yogis
constructed their refuge place on the eddying waters of nonexistence. The silent ones used their
divine knowledge to churn the whirlpool" (p. 233). The notes add one legend outright: "a legend in
which the snakes that infested the jungle surrounding Junagarh were destroyed by yogis with their
magical powers" (p. 337), which the verse reaches as "This is the abode of those who set fre to
Junagarh.⁵" (p. 257).

### Castes and occupations named in the verse and the notes

**Fishers.** Nuri is "a Gandiri [text-layer?] fshergirl [text-layer?] who is full of faults" and "a
fshergirl [text-layer?] of the Me caste", also "this Mangar girl" (p. 215). The note ranks the
group: "The various castes of fshermen, named in the sur as Gandiri, Mangar, Me, and Muhano, were
traditionally ranked as the lowest of the low in Sindhi society, but Tamachi's favor promotes Nuri
above his royal queens" (p. 333); the promotion is glossed literally as "made the Me girl a full
human being" (S. māṛihū kayo me)" (p. 334), and its social limit as "the ladies of the court have
no social interaction with the humble circles in which Nuri moves" (p. 334). In the verse: "They
are dark, ugly, base, and in no way attractive" (p. 215); the prior separation — "They did not go
together to marriage pavilions or to funerals, nor did they participate in weddings.²" (p. 218);
and the crossing — "He favored the fshergirl [text-layer?] and took her with him in his carriage"
(p. 216), "He took the fshergirl [text-layer?] into his carriage and raised her status.¹" (p. 218).
Sur 17's family are "expert fshermen (S. ghātū)" (p. 334).

**The fishing economy, itemised.** Baskets and trays of fish, scales for weighing, the cutting and
selling of fish (pp. 215–216); "landing places" and boats (pp. 215, 217); "fishing spears"
(p. 220), nets, hooks and rafts (pp. 220–222); "Use large strong sea nets made with colored twine"
(pp. 221–222); a fish stall and quayside (p. 219). Its collapse is described twice — "There are
heaps of sand where the fshermen [text-layer?] looked for fsh [text-layer?]. Thousands of
fshmongers [text-layer?] have been ruined" (p. 221), glossed as "the collapse of the local fsh
market, including the fshmongers and the tax collectors, following the loss of the fshermen"
(p. 334) — and credit is named: "I have had to borrow fsh [text-layer?].³ God bring back the
fshermen [text-layer?]… do not let me be ashamed before the traders" (p. 221), glossed "In order
to carry on dealing with the fshmongers in the market" (p. 334). In sur 28 the apparatus is turned
on the fish: "Now you must sufer the attacks of the fshermen" (p. 294); "You will fall, today or
tomorrow, into the fshermen's nets" (p. 294); "The fshermen have now blocked your passage with
stakes" (p. 294); "When my beloved planted his hook in my gills… I felt the painful tug of the
fshing line" (p. 294).

**Washermen.** "Friends, I sufered [text-layer?] when I lived in the quarter of the washermen"
(p. 149); "We are washermen, the servants of Prince Punhun. The Hôt is used to musk; I smell of
soap" (p. 149); "Wretched as I am, Punhun became a washerman for my sake" (p. 149); and the rank
inversion made explicit — "Together with the washermen, Punhun holds clothes in his hand to wash
them. Then there came a messenger from Ari Jam, saying: 'Oh perfect one, it is not your task to
pound the clothes.'" (p. 150). Sasui is called "the washergirl" (pp. 123, 134, 149, 154, 174) and
"the Brahman girl" (pp. 118, 134, 135, 165, 168); the note holds both together — she "belonged to
the washerman caste (S. parīṭi ) but was born a Brahman" (p. 328), having been "abandoned and
brought up by a washerman in the city of Bhambhor" (p. 327).

**Potters.** "Suhini was happy when she saw the designs drawn by the potter. The water washed away
the pattern and the glaze could not withstand the impact… In the Indus she came to know that it was
unbaked" (pp. 111–112); "Take with you a fred [text-layer?] pot with fne [text-layer?] designs.
Return the unfred [text-layer?] pot straight to the potter" (p. 112). Sur 23 works the kiln into a
doctrine of concealment: "Why do you not cover your love like a kiln flled with pots? If the fame
escapes, how will the pots be fred? Pay close attention to what the potters do" (p. 270), repeated
next verse with "Act in the same way as the potters" (p. 270); "Learn love from the kiln. It burns
all day long but gives out no steam" (p. 270); "The wretches would die if they knew the least bit
about what the potters kneaded with the clay" (p. 270). Also "whole kilns of noses on their
faces,⁸" (p. 193).

**Blacksmiths and steel** (sur 2, vv. 35–42): "They heat ore and produce steel. Only expert
blacksmiths know about them" (p. 45); the stoker heaping coals, and "the unsmelted steel breaks
into pieces" (p. 45); anvil and blows (pp. 45–46); the whetstone whetted with water to sharpen
swords, and removing rust to "reveal the steel" (p. 46). Elsewhere the trade marks decline — an
earlier generation "used to pierce diamonds and rubies", and where they worked "blacksmiths now
hammer base metals" (pp. 83–84); the quench, "For once put out this fre [text-layer?] with a
shower of water, like the blacksmith does.⁸" (p. 172); "Just as the blacksmith fxes links within
links to form a chain" (p. 274); and "They have been nailed in my heart without a blacksmith"
(pp. 200–201).

**Wine sellers and distillers, named as a low-status trade** (sur 2, vv. 47–61): a wine seller with
a shop, jars and a courtyard; a cupbearer; a tavern; stills and a distiller. "The wine seller is no
gentleman, but comes from a low caste. He destroys the drinkers by pouring cups of wine down their
throats" (p. 47). Travellers are the clientele — "Give the travelers who walk past plenty to drink.
Oh wine seller, that will make your shop popular" (p. 47); "see how they will give lakhs for every
drop you give them" (p. 48); "Sprinkle the dew of the tavern on the travelers… when the morning
drinkers have come into your courtyard" (p. 47). Burial at the workplace: "Those who were overcome
by what he said lie buried beside his stills" (p. 48). Payment in the head: "Give your head and
somehow make a deal with the distiller… a cup costs more than that" (p. 48).

**Doctors and medicine as a trade** (sur 2, vv. 12–25): prescribed diet and its breach (p. 42);
cataracts (p. 42); bandages, plasters and self-dressing of wounds (p. 41); cutting and branding —
"Bad doctors cut me and did not put me together. The ignorant branded me, and gave my body pain"
(p. 43); pulse-taking — "He checks my pulse and gives me the medicine of his mercy" (p. 43);
doctors driving the wounded from their house (p. 42); patients refusing treatment (p. 42). Branding
returns in sur 24: "Doctor, why do you brand my arm, when it is my heart that feels the pain?"
(p. 272).

**Jewellers and the gem trade.** Jewellers "put a loupe in their eye and assess all of them. They
determine the value of a pearl's quality from its circumference" (p. 84); the dealer's "expert eye"
is what secures the value (p. 84); "Glass is bought and jewels are rejected" (p. 84); a misvalued
gold is put "next to a brass pot" (p. 83).

**Minstrels and bards as a trade with equipment, discipline and a hereditary claim.** "To keep his
harp hanging on a hook is not the behavior of a bard" (p. 281); "You sleep the whole night through,
using your instrument as a pillow" (p. 281); "With their instruments on their shoulders, they look
for a path across the wilderness" (p. 281); "Fix your instrument and replace its strings with
silver ones" (p. 283); "The bard tied tassels and bells on his instrument" (p. 285). Bijal calls
himself "a hereditary minstrel" and is answered "Let me hear something from your heritage"
(p. 287) — set against "Gifts are not bestowed according to caste; it is performance that is
rewarded" (p. 282). Begging is a regulated relation to one door: "Prostrate yourself every day at
the threshold of the giver. Oh minstrel, never remove your lips from it for an instant" (p. 283);
"Oh minstrel, beg from the one who gives every day. The doors of the world are false, minstrel"
(p. 283); "'Oh minstrel, why did you leave my door and beg from others? That is why you have
sufered days of hardship'" (pp. 282–283); "Be happy with whatever is placed in your bowl"
(p. 290); "They receive alms to match their bowls" (p. 302). The poet–performer relation is glossed
once: "A reference to the practice of a poet sharing the refreshments served to him with the singer
who has performed his poetry" (p. 321), which answers to the verse "Oh poet, you have acquired this
desire from the singer.¹" (p. 46).

**Spinners and the cotton trade (sur 25 Kapaiti), the chunk notes' fullest single occupation.** The
sur is glossed "(S. kāpāitī 'spinner')… deals with the popular theme of girls engaged in spinning",
with the reward when "the thread they have spun is fnally assessed at its true price by the divine
dealer in cotton" (p. 339), and the workplace as an institution: "The spinning place (S. ātaṇu) is
where the young women gather to spin and enjoy each other's company" (p. 333). In the verse: the
wheel and its parts — "Your spinning wheel's support posts have become detached, and its driving
band has become slack" (p. 277), "Turn the broken wheel until the new one is fxed" (p. 278), "The
wheels are dismantled, and where have the spinners gone?" (p. 279), "The bands on their spinning
wheels are slack, and their huts are closed" (p. 279); carding — "The yarn of those who card it in
secret is valuable" (p. 279), "They carded a quarter of what had been carefully cleaned" (p. 280),
"The cotton balls of even the best girls lie around on the ground" (p. 279). The dealer inspects
and prices: "So long as you are spinning, do not turn your wheel by yourself. Otherwise, the dealer
may spot a defect in this thread of yours" (p. 277); "Every spinner is approved according to the
thread that they have spun" (p. 277); "Then you may smile when you are called by the dealer, and
get a better price" (p. 278); "the all-powerful dealer does not reject the coarse thread that
others spin" (p. 279); "People ofer them jewels for their thread, but they demand an even higher
price" (p. 279). Weighing, and exemption from it, is the mark of favour: "Those who have spun
coarse thread with love in their hearts sell it to the cotton dealers without its being weighed"
(p. 278); "Their thread gets sold without being put on the scales and weighed" (p. 279); against
"As soon as they weighed the warp, many defects emerged. Summoning the spinner, they questioned her
in secret" (p. 280). And the market as place: "To make a proft, they come early in the morning to
the spinning place" (pp. 278–279); "Yesterday they spun and spun, but they have not come into the
spinning area today" (p. 279); "Those cotton plants have gone, and so have the spinners. The
bazaars seem desolate without them, and my heart is grieved" (p. 280). Outside the sur: the
spinning party as a women's gathering with a reputation to fear — "May none of the girls in the
spinning party expose my husband" (p. 149), "Their lovely appearance stops anything being said in
the spinning party" (p. 186); carding refused in grief — "Do not card a cotton ball for me, mother.
Kick the spinning wheel and throw the rolls of yarn in the water" (p. 164); and the trade's
geography, "Some wind yarn in Arabia, others spin in Kabul" (p. 279). Shackle links the theme to
Panjabi: "like Bullhe Shah (compare Shackle 2015: xx–xxi, 3–7, 293–319)" (p. 339).

**Weaving, shearing and dyeing.** No weaver caste is named; the work is the Marus' own. "they shear
the soft wool from the sheep's tails… free from sorrows they spin the wool at their husbands' side.
In the desert even the young sucklings give wool from their backs. Precious shawls of high value
are woven. As they starch the shawls, the women say: 'Marui is needed in Malir'" (p. 204);
blankets "dyed with lac" held "more beautiful than shawls" and wool "better than silk and brocade"
(p. 197); patching and darning as a discipline of honour (pp. 197, 203). Dyeing and its mordant:
"First he plunges me into alum,⁴ then he colors me with dye" (p. 159), "Dyed without alum, they
delight in the company of the beloved" (p. 60) — the note glosses a substance "Used as a fxative
in dyeing cloth" (p. 330); cloth printing — "The girls learned to print on cloth, making Punhun
their pattern" (p. 166); "Millions are dyed as red as betel juice with love for Mumal" (p. 186);
the dyer as a trade one commissions — "Oh brother dyer, prepare black clothes. I am consumed with
love for the royal travelers who have departed" (p. 309); "The natural dye used by the nomads to
color their clothes" (p. 333) and a dye note "For the Eid festival" (p. 333). Suffering is figured
as dyeing across two surs: "Through being dyed in the suferings [text-layer?] of her journey, she
has become fully colored, says Latif" (p. 128), "Dyed in the way, you will become a precious color,
says Latif" (p. 133).

**Camel men and the camel-herding tribes.** "the whole tribe of camel men" (p. 164); "The camel men
did not do what Ari Jam had said" (p. 174); "leading a train of camels" (p. 176); "his camels have
become highwaymen" (p. 169). Glossed as "The tribe of camel drivers" (p. 330), with their speech —
"S. pārīsī, i.e., Balochi, the semi-Persian argot of the camel-herding tribes (S. jata)" (p. 329) —
answering to the verse's "since I became connected by marriage to the Jats,¹⁵" (p. 153) and the
naming of "Balochi" as the kinsmen's speech (p. 146). Sur 4 itemises the gear and feeds: sandal,
sandalwood and sandal juice, cardamoms, "bended branches of **henna**", the **lāṇī** bush, the
**ak**² plant, the **khaṭaṇahāru**⁴ plant whose white flowers the camel refuses, the **sirkhanḍu**⁵
(pp. 72–75); hobbles on "both forefeet and on both rear legs and chains of steel around its chest"
(p. 74), hobble rope and twisted ropes (pp. 73–74), nosebag (p. 73), "a silken saddlecloth" and
neck ornaments of rubies and pearls (p. 72), "a leading rein of gold" (p. 73), blinkers (p. 74);
and prices as printed — "Two branches cost millions, and leaves cost half a million a quarter. That
is what my camel eats every day" (p. 75), "Give millions to drive my precious camel" (p. 75). Camel
age is a herder's measure of a route: "She looks and goes along paths where a fve-year-old
[text-layer?] camel is scared and cannot go, and passes where four-year-olds cannot proceed…
difcult [text-layer?] routes that full-grown beasts fnd [text-layer?] hard" (p. 146). Camels also
drive the oil press — "You have blinkers on your eyes, and your feet are chafed by the oil press.³
Have you forgotten your herd that was bound and used to turn the press?" (p. 74), glossed "Camels
are harnessed to turn the circular presses used to crush oil seeds" (p. 323).

**Herdsmen and shepherds.** Mehar is "the chief of the herdsmen" (p. 97); "May Mehar never die, and
may his byre never be bare. May the hair of the heifers' herdsman never be twisted" (p. 104);
"Safe and happy, the bufaloes [text-layer?] will get across" (p. 104); "The herds grazed the
pasture, then crossed the river and came to an island.¹⁰" (p. 104) — the island glossed as
"Temporary alluvial islands (S. beṭu) are regularly formed in the middle of the river" (p. 325),
and the whole world of the sur as "the pastoral tribes who grazed their bufaloes in the pastures
watered by the Indus" (p. 324). Mehar's name is glossed literally as "'bufalo herdsman'" (pp.
324–325). Shepherds are a source of route information — "I ask you, shepherd,³ about my beloved"
(p. 159), "the shepherd would be shattered" (p. 160), "Umar, may your hands grant freedom to the
shepherds.⁶" (p. 195); nomads and Bedouin, "Without the Bedouin I have to sufer [text-layer?]
troubles here", "I pine for the nomads" (p. 196).

**Caste as a social fact the poems turn on, and then cross.** Sasui's caste is the obstacle: "It
was in Bhambhor that he came to know about my caste.³" (p. 166); "Mother, my caste is a disgrace to
the people of Kech" (p. 166); "My neighbors did not hide my disgrace. They revealed my caste to
gain favor with Punhun" (p. 168); "Let news of my caste not reach Kech" (p. 175); "I am base-born
and unworthy of the Baloch tribe" (p. 174); and in sur 19's closing vāī, "I am of low caste.
Recognize yourself, beloved… If they graciously call me their slave girl, then I am fortunate"
(p. 250). Against these: "Why ask about caste or tribe? Anyone who has come is acceptable"
(p. 194); "Gifts are not bestowed according to caste; it is performance that is rewarded" (p. 282);
"How can you ask them about their caste?" (p. 229). Servitude and naming: "Whatever I am, I am the
Baloch's slave girl… I am less even than the slippers he wears" (p. 158); "I was greeted in the
inner apartments by maids, slaves, and attendants" (p. 181); and the note "Daulat, literally
'Prosperity,' the kind of name given to a slave girl" (p. 329).

**Other trades, offices and devices named in passing.** Scribes and the law court — "Why did you
study letters, you wretch, and become a qazi?" (p. 51), "Oh scribe, just as you write lām joined to
alif,¹³ so does the beloved remain joined to our soul" (p. 52), "Oh scribe, why do you pile pages
on pages?" (p. 52), "Keep a legal adviser¹⁹ within you, so that you will not be helpless before the
judge" (pp. 57–58); mullahs and qazis as the offices that disapprove of music (p. 12); thieves,
chains and the gallows as a model of secrecy (p. 42); field owners and watchmen (p. 73); an
irrigation waterwheel, "they have fooded [text-layer?] so much that the waterwheel is submerged"
(p. 169), and the Persian wheel — "Like the water from a Persian wheel that comes out mixed with
sand" (p. 269); the stone flail, "the stone fail that beats the corn" (p. 274); hunters, dogs and
traps (pp. 249, 255, 299); armor polishers — "The armor polishers have come and burnished the axes
and swords" (p. 310); tax collectors (pp. 221, 293, 334); gravediggers' equipment — "The spade and
the measuring stick⁵ are waiting for everyone" (p. 274), glossed as "Needed to measure out the
grave that is to be dug" (p. 339).

### Textiles, boats, trade and the river

**Hindu long-distance sea trade out of Sindh, given as the real practice behind two surs.** "the
long-distance sea voyages annually undertaken by the Hindu traders of Sindh to locations like Aden,
Gujarat, or Lanka", and the wives "left behind while their husbands are absent on business" (p. 16)
— the surs are "Sirirag" and "Samundi". The notes confirm the frame: sur 5 "uses the imagery of the
sea voyages undertaken by the traders of Sindh as a symbol for human life" (p. 323), and sur 6 "(S.
sāmūṉḍī 'sailor') is based on the theme of traders sailing away on voyages for business" (p. 324).
Destinations glossed without being named: "A port in Gujarat, here symbolizing the destination of
life's journey", "The port in Yemen that was a regular destination for traders from Sindh" (p. 323),
and one "Famous for its wealth, which attracted sailors from distant Sindh" (p. 324).

**Ship, gear and port work.** Sail washed and whitened "with soap" (p. 76), mast, rigging, rudder
blade, timbers, planks that leak, holes stopped and timbers oiled daily before launch (p. 80), an
old boat with loose rigging kept going "so long as the steersman is strong" (p. 80), a telescope
(p. 76). Ashore: "Oil the timbers of your boat and make it sound" (p. 82); the carpenter's pegs rot
(p. 81); traders "cast anchor weights into the waters" (p. 81); sails are "mended… on the harbor
quays. When these were patched and fxed [text-layer?], they erected the masts. At sea they few
[text-layer?] their fags [text-layer?]" (p. 92); crews "sought news from the master mariners"
before sailing (p. 93); a sailor "who keeps watch delivers reports to the captain" (p. 82); "The
fags [text-layer?] of the rich merchants look dazzling in every direction" (p. 91).

**Pilots, hazards and pirates.** "stay close to the pilot" (p. 76); "They had a perfect pilot as
their helper mid-ocean" (p. 78); and the limit of their knowledge — "Pilots gather and bring reports
on waters close to shore. But they provide no information on places where the current rages"
(p. 80); pilots deliver "fearful reports" (p. 81). Hazards named: sandbank, "Turbid water, waves,
white water, foods [text-layer?]" (p. 77), "pitch-dark whirlpools" (pp. 78–79), "dark shoals"
(p. 79). And a displacement of the pilots themselves: "The pilots are not in their former place;
Frankish pirates³ have taken over" (p. 81), glossed in the notes as "Portuguese pirates had an evil
reputation in the seas around Sindh" (p. 323).

**Cargo, customs and passage money.** "They put cloves, cardamoms, fne [text-layer?] clothes, and
lustrous pearls aboard. They placed valuable stores in its bottom. In its rigging… they tied foral
[text-layer?] decorations" (p. 85); musk and ambergris (p. 106); "What they desire are the pearls
of the Indus" (p. 94), preferred to gold by "Major traders" (p. 94); "When they hear of Lanka's
gold, the sailors have no peace" (p. 94). Customs are levied and can be escaped: "By the grace of
the Prophet, they were not stopped by the customs ofcers [text-layer?]. The sailors who voyaged
afar have entered their own country" (p. 86). Passage is paid for and the poor are left ashore: "I
have no money on me to pay for my passage. Boatman, tell me how I can get to my beloved" (p. 93);
"The travelers had nothing on them, nor did the boatmen take them on board for nothing. They
remained on the shore all day long until the sun set" (p. 93).

**Pearl and gem diving.** "Divers know the way to explore the ocean. They have entered the depths
and gathered gems. They have brought up handfuls of diamonds and rubies and given them away"
(p. 78); "Those who went into the sea with protective glass over their faces¹ entered the depths and
sought out shells" (p. 79), glossed "I.e., wearing diving masks" (p. 323). Compare the wild goose
that "is familiar with the pearls that lie in the depths" (p. 255) and "examine the jewels in the
deep and choose them" (p. 256); and the oyster, "The oyster is born in the ocean, its trust is in
the cloud… It produces a pearl because it sufers [text-layer?] in the deep.²⁰" (p. 211), glossed as
popular belief — "the oyster does not drink water from the ocean or river where it lives. It waits
instead for clouds to appear in the sky, when it opens to drink the raindrops that it lives on";
"Pearls are thought to be produced by the hunger and thirst the oyster sufers in the sea" (p. 333).

**Commodity grades and the ethics of stock.** "I dealt in glass, I did not deal in pearls. I did
business in lead, says Shah" (p. 76); "I traded in worthless glass and broke my contract with God"
(p. 76); "Trade in the merchandise that does not become old by being stored, and that does not incur
the slightest loss when sold abroad" (p. 80); and the lover's currency, "He carries no money, but
uses his head to trade with" (p. 55).

**A sailing calendar keyed to wind, festival and month.** "They left when the north wind was
blowing, and they return on the spring breeze" (p. 92); "As soon as the north wind blows, my beloved
talks about going" (p. 95); "Trusting in the north wind, yesterday they unfurled their sails from
the mast. … The traders have loaded their craft with merchandise" (p. 80); "Seeing that it is
Diwali,⁷ the sailors prepare their sails" (p. 95), glossed as "The Hindu festival of lamps
celebrated in the autumn, which marked the beginning of the trading season" (p. 324); a voyage of a
full year — "He has returned in the twelfth month since he started" (p. 95); and a departure the day
after arrival, "after spending only twenty-four hours with me" (p. 95). Savan is the flood season:
"Everyone enters the water in Savan; she is happy in winter" (p. 102), "The days of Savan will not
be here forever. By tomorrow your foods [text-layer?] will subside" (p. 102), "the rains of Savan"
(p. 116), glossed "The frst month of the rainy season in the Indian calendar, corresponding to
July–August" (p. 338) and "savan (Sāvaṇ) The month of the rainy season corresponding to July–August"
(p. 346). Homecoming is itself a festival: "Mother, the sailors have returned, making a fne
[text-layer?] display" (p. 91); "For those whose courtyards they enter, today is a happy Eid"
(p. 92).

**The river and its shifting course as an economic fact.** The introduction records that the Indus's
shifting course has made an area "now made desolate" that was formerly prosperous (p. 22). Sur 28
works this out: "Thorn tree, tell me stories of the lords of the old riverbed" (p. 293); "In reality
the old riverbed has dried up, and only ak bushes grow on its banks. The brave traders have lost
their strength, the water has dried up, and the tax collectors have gone" (p. 293); "The water has
not returned in its former stream to the Patihal" (p. 293); "The boatmen frst realized that the old
riverbed would not remain in its former state. Seeing how the water behaved, they turned their boats
away" (p. 293); "The dried-up old riverbed has become a trickle, and reeds grow on its bank… Only in
a few spots do people gather" (p. 293). The note names the thing: **ḍhoru**, "the dried-up course of
a former branch of the Indus, once the site of bustling trade but now reclaimed by the desert where
only thorn trees grow, following one of the many shifts in the historic route of the river"; "The
Jasodhos were the former lords of this territory" (p. 341). Elsewhere the lake dries the same way:
"The lake has dried up, and the tax collectors have left" (p. 221).

**Caravan economy.** "A splendid caravan came in fair array from Kech. The camels wore bells and had
beautiful aigrettes on their necks" (p. 147); "On their saddlecloths were decorations and thousands
of diamonds" (p. 147); "Their camels were decorated with bells and tassels and ornaments" (p. 133);
"The guests brought their riding camels and made them sit in the campground" (p. 147); "Having
loaded up their goods, they leave for their own land" (p. 144). Distance is reckoned in leagues —
"Everyone travels hundreds of leagues" (p. 130), "On my journey to the Harho I must travel many
leagues" (p. 136) — and routes are plural: "So many wretched women have died near Kech. **Twenty
paths lead there**; how do I know the one they took?" (p. 147). Robbers price the road: "Those who
go alone fall prey to bandits" (pp. 175–176); "There are hundreds and thousands of robbers in the
wilderness. Oh you who travel on foot, take a guide with you in the desert" (p. 176); "Do not become
separated from the caravan, for the company is going to climb the mountain passes" (p. 171).

**Agrarian economy keyed to the rains, with prices and hoarding named.** "Heavy drops of rain are
falling; drive your livestock outside. Leave the lowlands for higher ground, taking your things with
you" (p. 258); "The peasants prepare their plows, the herdsmen are happy" (p. 258); "There are rich
stores in the village granaries" (p. 259); "At dawn the sound of the churning sticks is heard on the
plains. The prosperous farmers' wives rejoice, and their hands are full of butter. They milk the
happy bufaloes" (p. 260); "There are Arab horses in the courtyard, outside there are bufaloes with
twisted horns" (p. 260); and grain prices as a subject of prayer — "Make the waters on the level
ground abundant, and make the price of grain cheap" (pp. 260–261). The hoarders are named and
quantified: "Those who hoarded to make food dear are now wringing their hands. As they turned the
pages of their books, fve would become ffteen.¹⁷ May all hoarders and all oppressors perish"
(p. 266), the note reading "Meaning that the hoarders would make a 300 percent proft" (p. 338) —
**the only figure of any kind in the apparatus**.

**Tax, tribute and exemption, in five places.** "In their country there is no check or hindrance,
nor any revenue tax" (p. 205); "let my kinsfolk be exempted from tax" (p. 215); Nuri "has been
excused from the fshing [text-layer?] gear, the landing places, and the boats" (p. 217); "The Sumiro
women who entered Abro's protection would not pay tribute" (p. 303); "No tribute is paid by the
destitute women who have taken refuge with Abro" (pp. 305–306).

**The Marus' pastoral and desert economy.** Camel-herding — "Umar, give the order for me to become a
herder and to graze the camels on leaves" (p. 197); seasonal migration keyed to the rains — "the
southwest wind has returned… his cattle tracks and pens. The beautiful huts of my husband will be
there beside the dunes" (p. 204), "The Marus go out in the rainy season, turning toward the
borderlands between the river and the desert" (p. 206), "They set up their huts where the rain
falls" (p. 207), "they drive their focks [text-layer?] back to the thickets. They drive the kids to
the lower pastures, grazing them on the plains" (p. 208), "It is their mark that they travel with
their huts and baggage" (p. 209); foraging and diet — "Every day, oh Sumiro, they gather and cook
food from the jungle. They pile up heaps of dried grass… From the lanb [text-layer?] grass, says
Latif, they extract grains of rice to cook. Oh Umar, they do not consider pulao to be as good as
their **ārāṛī**.¹²" (p. 204), glossed as "A simple dish made from desert plants, as opposed to the
richness of pulao, made from meat and rice" (p. 333); "They bring branches of berries they have
picked and put them on to cook. The foragers who frequent the thickets are not thin or weak"
(p. 204); "People are feeding on the wild fruits of the desert" (p. 209).

### Marriage, household and the women's economy

**Wells and water-drawing as women's work, timed and measured.** "They dig wells in the desert and
water their goats. At dawn the women draw water from wells **sixty fathoms deep**. Pouring the water
into buckets, they cry out with joy" (p. 207); "The women get up at midnight because the water lies
deep in the ground. The heedless ones get no opportunity during the day.¹⁶ It was my bad luck that
they snatched me from the well.¹⁷" (p. 207) — the note behind it reading "When the well is
monopolized by men and their animals" (p. 333); "On both sides of the well the ropes dangle idly"
(p. 207); "How wonderful those brackish wells of my native land were, from which I used to draw
water" (p. 206); churning pots at dawn (p. 206). Water-carrying is service elsewhere too: "Be his
humble slave, fll [text-layer?] his water bags" (p. 140), "At dawn my companions fll [text-layer?]
the water bags in their hands" (p. 146), "I will labor for my beloved and bring him water from the
well" (p. 154); with grinding and cooking — "I will grind grain and cook it if you take me with
you" (p. 128).

**Marriage rites.** "I saw that the groom's feet were crooked at the time of the covering.²"
(p. 179), glossed as "a wedding ritual, in which it was believed to be unlucky if the groom's feet
were not placed straight upon the bride's when he entered her parental home" (p. 331); Tamachi
"tied the wedding band on her arm" and "waves the royal peacock fan over her" (p. 217); and the
social precondition that had barred the match — "They did not go together to marriage pavilions or
to funerals, nor did they participate in weddings.²" (p. 218). Marriage is also the standing image
of reward: "Enter the beloved's brightness and become his bridegroom" (p. 44); "They who please the
beloved are the ones who will enjoy their marriage beds… they are chosen and are adorned as
bridegrooms" (p. 67); the Sindhi **ghoṭa** is glossed "bridegrooms" (p. 344).

**Betrothal goods, ornament and allegiance — no dowry is named anywhere in the notes, but betrothal
gifts are.** "The Maru's betrothal threads are like gold to me… Blessed is the thread of the blanket
that my kinsfolk gave to me" (p. 198); "I use the Maru's betrothal oil on my hair, I have no use for
yours" (p. 198); and the refusal of gold — "The Maru women wear glass bangles on their wrists,
taking pride in what most people are ashamed of. We wear black threads around our wrists; for us,
gold is a cause of mourning" (p. 198). Refusal to oil or braid the hair is repeatedly the sign of
her state (pp. 197, 199, 200). Against the Maru austerity, Kak's households are itemised: "The green
shawls they wore were like betel leaves. They had made their bodies fresh with attar and ambergris.
Their braided hair was impregnated with musk and sandal. The lovely woman's ears were decorated with
silver and gold" (p. 186); "The golden-colored Sodho women play with silver. Incense sticks burn in
their reception rooms; their divans are scented with musk. Ewers of perfumed water are poured out in
their bathrooms" (p. 187); "braided hair is always perfumed with jasmine oil" (p. 186); swings,
"When I sat on swings³ I had no awareness" (p. 181). Cosmetics are social marking: "The blackness of
mascara is suitable for women. As a man, do not apply blackness with a stick" (p. 65), red and white
mascara and "the splendor of a scarlet wedding outft [text-layer?]" (p. 65); "With vermilion in the
parting of her hair and kohl on her eyes, she lost the chance to be with him" (p. 135) — against
which renunciation is praised: "blessed are those who are bare of ornaments", "set out unadorned,
all of you" (p. 135).

**Kin obligation and its shame.** "I did not get up before them and prepare fne [text-layer?] food.
**Nor did I assemble my companions to sing songs for them. I could not perform the customs of our
realm.**" (p. 122) — song for guests is a duty of the household, not entertainment. "If I was their
kinswoman, I should have complained about my brothers-in-law" (p. 166); "Wrap the hem of your garment
humbly around your neck and embrace poverty" (p. 182), repeated as "the hem of the shawl that is
humbly wrapped around my neck" (p. 183). Lila's bargain is the negative case: she "trades a night of
her royal husband Chanesar with her rival Kaunru for a necklace" (p. 19); "You exchanged for the
jewels the lord who is the delight of your eyes" (p. 178); "When Lila saw the treasure, she slipped
into selfsh [text-layer?] thoughts" (p. 180); "She was robbed like Lila, who exchanged her lover for
gems.⁴" (p. 135).

**Households without support.** "May the huts the widows built without their husbands not get soaked.
If the north wind blows them down, to whom can they cry out?" (p. 262); "Women left on their own have
lost their worries, says Latif" (p. 260); "What is the state of those whose huts have no support?²"
(p. 269); "How will those whose huts have no support cope with the cold?" (p. 270); and the war
economy of widowhood — "The widows of the slain will cause a rise in the price of indigo" (p. 315).
A woman's want of a mount is stated twice: "I cannot get there on foot, and my father will not give
me a camel to mount early in the morning" (p. 71); "I have no camel or horse to take me to my
beloved by night" (p. 72). The speaking voice is repeatedly a woman addressing "Mother" (pp. 41, 72,
73, 74) and "Sisters" (p. 68).

**Domestic economy in passing.** "Love is not created in a grain jar, meat is not cooked in husks"
(p. 66); "Let me roast on burning sticks of thorn and acacia" (p. 44); "There is something about
these old huts we see. Although no one notices those who live there, they are accepted at his door"
(p. 67), continuing "They are considered strangers, but they eat with the beloved" (p. 67).

**Burial and funeral as household obligation.** Burial in the home ground is asked for five times:
"If I die in this place, take my body to Malir" (p. 202); "Pour the cool earth of the desert over her
dead body. Once my life is over, take my corpse to Malir" (p. 202); "May I be buried in the desert
with the Marus who live there" (p. 203); "make my grave with the desert nomads. Let me smell the
fragrance of the creepers of my ancestral land" (p. 203); "I reckon the dust of my native land to be
the equal of musk" (p. 212) — and resurrection is made conditional on it: "I will be restored to life
after death if my body gets to Malir" (p. 203), repeated next verse with "corpse" (p. 203). Sasui's
grave is dug by a shepherd who "digs the grave in which Punhun will also be buried when he
eventually comes in search of her" (p. 328), and "When Punhun died after fnding Sasui's corpse, the
lovers were buried together" (p. 331). The grave's terror (p. 88), a funeral carried by birds — "The
herons are her pallbearers and the current is her bier. The cranes that live on the eyots ofer
[text-layer?] their shoulders to carry her" (p. 109) — and mourning as practice and as market: "Their
wives throw handfuls of dust over themselves and shriek. They beat their breasts and lament"
(p. 311); "The women whose heads are held high now beat themselves and utter laments" (p. 312);
"Three groups wept for Hasan and Lord Husain: people in their homes, wild beasts in the jungle, and
angels in the heavens. Birds beat their breasts in mourning" (p. 316).

### Shrine practice, Sufi institutions and religious specialists

**Shrine musicians as the medium of Sufi transmission — the book's clearest institutional claim.**
Besides teaching "within the circle of disciples formed around a pīr, the Suf [text-layer?] message
was also transmitted to a wider audience through poetry sung by musicians attached to the Suf
[text-layer?] shrines constructed around the tombs of saints" (p. 12). Clerical opposition is named
by office: "Despite the disapproval of music in the clerical Islam upheld by the mullahs and qazis,
the singing of mystical lyrics gained popularity with the increasing use of local languages for
poetry that coincided with the decline of Mughal authority during the eighteenth century" (p. 12).

**Hereditary pīr lineages as a standing institution of rural Sindh** — "they have always enjoyed
great prestige and power as pīrs or holy men with a special spiritual authority" (p. 8); their
distribution is referred to Ansari 1992: 9–35 (p. 25, n. 8). In the verse the pīr is what a
destitute man attaches himself to: "Here he has obtained clothes, bread, and a fne place with the
pīr" (p. 306); "He sits and says, 'I will not go far from my pīr'" (p. 306); "Look at Vagand,
sitting at the giver's door" (p. 307) — the notes setting "Vagand's hopeless condition when he
relies on his own eforts" against "the bounty he receives from his pīr" (p. 343). Religious
specialists are twice named as a class and both times subordinated: "She has made many learned
pandits and pīrs tremble" (p. 185); "The learned, pundits and pīrs, even lords and Hamirs⁹ will die"
(p. 194).

**Offerings at shrines, dated but undated.** Note 11 to sur 28 reads in full "The day on which
oferings are traditionally presented at Suf shrines." (p. 342) — **which day is not stated**, and
the verse it glosses is outside chunk 010. The verse the notes are keyed to is sur 28's Thursday
sequence: "Mother, who can compete with the wealthy herdsmen who leave such generous Thursday
oferings?" (p. 299, with both "wealthy herdsmen" and "oferings" footnoted); "May those wealthy
herdsmen enjoy a long life. I live under their protection. May they take care of us in difcult
times" (p. 299); "If you want to go to green pastures, depart with the wealthy herdsmen" (p. 299);
"Travelers are welcome in the camps of the wealthy herdsmen who give life to the destitute"
(pp. 299–300). The Sindhi term is glossed at **sanghāra**, "wealthy herdsmen" (p. 342). This is the
densest shrine-economy material anywhere in the ten chunks.

**Women's votive practice at the waterside — performed by traders' wives, not at a shrine.** "The
traders' wives stand at the landing place and make oferings [text-layer?]. They bring musk and ofer
[text-layer?] it to the ocean.³" (p. 93); "She lights lamps on water and dry land, she ties fags
[text-layer?] on trees and plants,⁴ saying: 'Oh God, my hope is that my husband may come.'" (p. 93);
and the obligation stated as a rule — "She who performs pilgrimage to the water and does not ofer
[text-layer?] lamps should not desire her husband's return" (p. 93). Vows are counted and redeemed:
"I have made thousands of vows for your return, my husband" (p. 91); "I give to others the precious
gems that I vowed to my beloved" (p. 92). The notes make it custom: "It was the custom for the wives
of the Hindu traders of Sindh to make oferings to the water deity for their husbands' safe return"
(p. 324); "The making of a vow is marked by lighting lamps and by tying ribbons on trees" (p. 324).

**Zikr, in two forms, and the four stations.** The formula *lā ilāha illā'llāh* "is recited with
inhalation and exhalation of the breath in the Suf meditative exercise called zikr" (p. 321); and
"the silent zikr, or internal repetition of the name of God, as opposed to the 'spoken formula' of
the next line" (p. 321); one further practice is glossed only as "A demanding exercise of Suf
spiritual discipline" (p. 322). In the verse: a "spoken formula" recited all day (p. 50); a "closing
formula.³" the Sufi will not discuss (p. 49); "They recite the holy name of God in their hearts"
(p. 51); "Those who have learned the formula of pain, recite the lesson of sufering [text-layer?].
Holding the slate of contemplation, they study in silence" (p. 51); rosary and bead as bodily
metaphor (p. 67). The four stations are glossed in Arabic — "the four ascending stages on the Suf
path, in which the Law (Ar. sharī‘at) is followed by the mystical Way (Ar. tarīqat), leading in turn
to the higher levels of Reality (Ar. haqīqat) and Gnosis (Ar. ma‘rifat). Compare 3.51, 7.32"
(p. 320), cross-referenced twice (pp. 323, 325) — and appear in English in the verse: "Make the Way
your support, recognize the Law. Get your heart used to Reality and know the place of Gnosis.¹¹"
(p. 66); "First learn the lesson of the Law, Suhini. The truth of Reality far surpasses the Way. It
is Gnosis⁷ that is the real task of lovers" (p. 102).

**The guide as an institution the poems assume, stated most strongly as a hadith-backed
requirement.** "**He who has no guide, has Satan for a guide;⁵** without him there is only gloom.
She who travels alone is led astray by pride. He who travels on the mystic path without a guide [is
like one who sails on the sea without a boat],⁶ this is not a good saying. Without him, millions
have gone astray" (pp. 145–146; the square brackets are the book's). The two sayings behind it are
printed untranslated: "Ar. man lā shaikhun lahu fa-shaikhuhu 'l-shaiṭānu (Suf saying)" and "Ar. bilā
shaikhin yamshī fī 'l-ṭarīqi [ka-man yamshī fī 'l-baḥri bilā safīnatin] (Suf saying)" (p. 329).
Consistent with it: "Mother, with humbly folded hands I will go to my guide" (p. 95); "Oh my guide,
give me your hand, and rescue me from the deep" (p. 99); "On your way toward the other side, humbly
cling to the guide's stirrup" (p. 116); "She traveled without a guide and did not reach her goal"
(p. 129); "Be our guide and let those who travel on foot advance" (p. 137); "The power of helping us
see is vested in our spiritual masters" (p. 63); "In the future do no leave this beggar, oh guide"
(p. 194, printed "do no"); "There the guide will take Abdul Latif by the hand" (p. 308).

**Practice measured against interior sight, and the forty-day retreat twice rejected.** "So long as
you can see yourself, your prayer is of no use… then say 'God is great'" (p. 59), repeated for
prostration (p. 59); "There is no standing or sitting in prayer for them, nor do they perform
prostrations" (p. 60); "Take out your eyes before you break your fast… Seeing the beloved's face is
equal to eating seventy dishes" (p. 60); "Entering his presence is as if one's being, body, and soul
have performed the Hajj" (p. 60); "Faith does not come about by claiming to recite the profession of
faith, when the heart is flled [text-layer?] with deceit, polytheism, and the devil" (p. 64). "A
forty-day vigil¹⁴ is not equal to a sight of the beloved" (p. 52); "The body is a mosque and the mind
is a cell; do not keep a forty-day vigil. Why do you not worship God the unseen twenty-four hours a
day?" (p. 52). Sufi dress is tested the same way: "It is unftting [text-layer?] for a Suf
[text-layer?] to call himself one and yet to be full of desire. Cut up your tall Suf [text-layer?]
cap and throw it into the fre [text-layer?]" (p. 50); "If you wear a Suf [text-layer?] cap, then be
a proper Suf [text-layer?]" (p. 50).

**Attendance at a door as the lover's discipline** — the nearest thing in the verse to shrine-going,
though the poems never call it that: "Every day they go to the beloved's door and weep" (p. 55); "Oh
lover, keep sitting in the beloved's street. Do not lose heart and quit his door" (p. 55); "keep
sitting in the beloved's shop" (p. 56); "keep sitting in the beloved's passageway" (p. 56); against
"It is foolishness to go the beloved's door all the time. Do not hurry to his neighborhood" (p. 56);
"Oh God, although you chase me away, I will not leave your door. … I have seen many other doors, but
you are the only one for me" (p. 75); "At the beloved's door there are many lovers like me" (p. 58);
and "Realize that special dust trodden by his feet is perfect kohl for the eyes" (p. 58).

**Veneration of the remains of the devout — the one passage in the verse chunks that touches shrine
practice directly.** "The ashes of those who spend the whole night awake in remembrance of God fnd
[text-layer?] honor, says Abdul Latif. Thousands come before them and ofer [text-layer?] their
respects" (p. 77). Compare "Go and see the foreigners' graves on the banks by Kak" (p. 185), and
Sasui's burial at the beloved's feet — "The lord of Kech acted as the pallbearer of Sasui's corpse…
The son of Ari brought her from the mountain passes, says Latif. The wretched girl was granted a
place at his feet." (p. 174).

**Muharram and Ashura as observed practice.** "The sight of the new moon of Muharram has made us
concerned for the princes" (p. 309); "Muharram has returned, but the Imams have not come. Oh lord,
let me see the princes of Medina" (p. 309); the party setting out "from Medina at the beginning of
Muharram, the frst month of the Hijri year" (p. 343); "The annual Ashura festival of Muharram 10,
which celebrates the martyrdom of Imam Husain" (p. 344); and the mode Husaini, "associated with
laments for the martyrdom of Imam Husain at Karbala" (p. 330), named inside its own poem — "Mother, I
have been slain by the sound of Husaini.⁵" (p. 168), "It was the lady¹² who frst [text-layer?]
mourned in Husaini for Husain. Afterward the world came to learn about it" (p. 176), with the lady's
identity outside the chunk. Eid divides ruler from ruled: "Oh Umar Sumiro, what is Eid for you is a
time of mourning for us. The sad creatures have forgotten joy and the pleasures of buying things.¹⁰"
(p. 199).

**Alms and the duty to the poor, stated negatively.** "In your house you gathered riches, which came
up to your knees. It is called carrion¹⁰ in the Traditions… You did not share it with the poor or
fear the wrath of God" (p. 88). And largesse as a royal-religious performance: Tamachi "gave
treasures to the humble folk, like a frst [text-layer?] fruits ofering [text-layer?]. He took out
pearls and scattered them like cowries at the fsh [text-layer?] stall… He gave gold away as alms and
made a sport of silver… He made oferings [text-layer?] of turquoises to the fakirs" (p. 219), and
"The blind and the lame came, when the generous one invited them" (p. 219).

**A shrine cult sustained without a biological heir, and manuscript-making as a cult activity.**
"Although he left no son to assume responsibility for the Bhitshah shrine, his disciples ensured
that it became the center of his cult" (p. 10) — the only custodianship arrangement described
anywhere in the ten chunks; the cult included "the elaborate tradition of musical performance of his
poetry **that he himself had devised**" (p. 10) and "the collection of poetry by Shah Latif and
others that was carefully assembled in the historic manuscript known as the Ganj, dated a.h. 1207
(1792 CE)" (p. 10). The bibliography records an institution at the site under several English names:
"Hyderabad: Shah Abdul Latif Bhitshah Saqafati Markaz Kameti" (p. 348), "Bhitshah Cultural Centre
Committee" (pp. 348, 350), "Shah Abdul Latif Bhitshah Cultural Centre Committee" (p. 348), and
"Bhitshah, Hyderabad" as a place of publication (p. 350) — imprint evidence of a body bearing the
site's name, with nothing said about its relation to the shrine.

**Colonial and post-1947 reception as institutional history.** "Only with the British conquest of
Sindh in 1843 did the literary culture of the Suf [text-layer?] tradition come to be overlaid by the
new patterns of modernity"; Persian was "quite rapidly replaced as the language of education,
administration, and elite literature by English and by Sindhi, whose development was actively
encouraged by the colonial authorities in Bombay" (p. 13), and because Sindh was administered from
Bombay rather than Calcutta, "Urdu was not installed as the immediate successor of Persian, as
happened in Panjab" (p. 25, n. 9). "Risālo was pressed into service for the examinations prescribed
by the new education syllabus" (p. 13). Hindu scholars produced "much of the new secondary literature
in English or Sindhi prose on Shah Latif's life and poetry", which "continued until after
independence in 1947, when the mass emigration of the Hindu population from Pakistan to India took
place" (p. 13).

### Kingship, chieftaincy and patronage

**The legends' political setting, as the introduction gives it.** The romances "are loosely set in
the pre-Mughal period when Sindh was ruled by the Muslim Rajput dynasties of Sumiros and Samos"
(p. 17, spellings as printed). Thatta is named as "the Mughal capital of Thatta and other urban
centers in Sindh", where the educated elite wrote the "now forgotten Persian poetry" (p. 25, n. 7).
The tomb at Bhitshah was built by "the local ruler Ghulam Shah Kalhoro", with no construction date
and no dates for the ruler (p. 10).

**Chieftaincy as protection, and as predation.** Chanesar: "The king is a mighty ruler and very
jealous. The lands that lie in every direction are in awe of Prince Chanesar" (p. 178); "Chanesar is
a skilled appraiser who discovers secret thoughts and examines hearts" (p. 180); the note reads his
jealousy theologically — "Through the tacit equation of Chanesar with God, the theme of the divine
jealousy of rival objects of worship is also developed" (p. 331). Umar/Sumiro/Hamir holds Marui with
an apparatus: "Around my neck are collars of iron, I wear fetters and chains. There are shackles on
my feet, and the room is nailed on the inside. There are lookouts in the courtyard, and the ministers
are on guard. I am unhappy in my cell" (p. 203); he is glossed as "a local Rajput chieftain… one day
abducted her and imprisoned her in his fortress of Umarkot" (p. 332); and he is credited with
restraint — "From the very beginning, Umar, chastity was prescribed in your destiny. So you did not
violate the honor of Marui, who was reared by the Marus" (p. 214). Against that, the ruler who turns
predator has no forum of appeal, stated twice: "When the ruler we rely on becomes a robber, how can
the Marus live in the desert, either now or in the future?"; "When he who is our support becomes a
robber, at whose door can the poor Marus complain?" (p. 212).

**Protection and tribute as the chief's function.** Abro: "To save the honor of the Sumiro ladies,
Abro mounted his camel. He was a brave leader, who was killed for the women's sake" (p. 303); "Among
the chiefs, Abro is the greatest protector" (p. 303); "Abro is most generous and kind, the best of
all the Samos" (p. 304); with the two tribute-exemption verses (pp. 303, 305–306). Jakhiro:
"Jakhiro the chief is the support of those who look to him" (p. 303); "God himself created Jakhiro
and gave him form" (p. 304); "the lord of Kachchh does not turn his back on them" (p. 304); "I see
no hero like Jakhiro in the land. He is the leader of all the prophets sent to the world" (p. 305).
The note names him "a Rajput ruler of medieval Sindh famous for his bravery and generosity",
addressed by "the epithet Rahu (the 'upholder of the realm'); his alternative name, Abro; his title,
Jadam; and his tribal afiation as 'the Samo'" (p. 342) — and records the invasion he resisted, by
"the Muslim emperor of Delhi, Sultan Alauddin (d. 1316)", when he "took under his protection the
women of his fellow Rajputs, the Sumiros" (p. 342); in the verse, "Alauddin came with a host of
plumed warriors. No one dared to face him; who could endure his arrows?" (p. 303).

**Raiding as the counter-institution.** "Wake up, Samos of the Jarejo tribe, do not lie there
sleeping. See, Lakho is approaching you and preparing to attack" (p. 300); "These are the signs of
bandits like Lakho. Tightening their horses' straps, they will create chaos in Kachchh" (p. 300);
"Oh Rebari, persuade Lakho with your pleas" (p. 300); "Thousands of Lakhos are talked about, but none
is the equal of Lakho Phulani. Princes and rajas tremble in their forts because of him" (p. 300).
The note: "a legendary bandit from the heroic age of Sindh, who is remembered for his fearless
attacks on the ruling Jarejo Samos. Here he represents the irresistible challenge of death" (p. 342).

**Patronage of musicians as a named royal function.** Sapar: "If you beg at the door of Sapar, you
will receive a fne reward" (p. 281); "the call has come from Sapar. You may not have learned anything
of singing, yet the king is pleased" (p. 282); "The faults of the inexpert are tolerated by Prince
Sapar" (p. 282); "Last night Sapar prepared fne horses for you. The lord of Las Bela is kind even to
one who does not know how to sing" (p. 282); "Sapar is a jealous lord, he examines the hearts of the
minstrels" (p. 283). The note: "A ruler of Las Bela, to the southwest of Sindh proper, who was
legendary for his lavish patronage of musicians. As throughout the following verses, the name of
Sapar is invoked as a symbol of the infnite generosity of God" (p. 340), with "The gift to a
musician of a fne Arab horse is one of the most famous instances of Sapar's generosity" (p. 340).
Largesse and its protocol elsewhere: "The descendant of Ronjho opened his caskets in the night and
took out pearls. The minstrels gathered to collect them and fll their pots" (p. 282); "He gave great
gifts and took the goods of the base. The lord bestowed presents of gold on fools" (p. 284); "Get in
the palanquin, minstrel, and come up. The handsome king asks for you in his apartments" (p. 287);
"Bards do not enter palaces without some good end in view… That is why rulers honor bards" (p. 290);
and the refusal — "He did not take bags of coins, and he rejected jewels" (p. 286). Rai Diyach, "the
ruler of Junagarh in Gujarat" (p. 340), pays in his head: "Rai Diyach gave this head as a sacrifce
to the lord" (p. 292), the minstrel Bijal having "successfully demanded the latter's head as a reward
for his performance" (p. 340). Instruments of a royal reception: "Drums and tabors and pipes would
welcome me" (p. 180); "I was seated in the midst of the company with drums and tabors and pipes"
(p. 181).

**Poverty ranked above kingship, as explicit comparison.** "Their cloth woven from wool is better
than silk and brocade" (p. 197); "For someone who has rejected silver, crores of rupees are like
straw" (p. 198); "It is not the way of the Marus to exchange their kinsmen for gold" (p. 198); "I
will not change the huts that I love for a palace" (p. 199); and the needle verse, "I do not think
kingship can be compared with the needle that covers the naked but itself is bare. Be born again if
you would know the worth of the needle" (p. 206). Also "There are harbors in every land, but precious
gems are not found on sandy shores. Priceless jewels are given by those who are dressed like fakirs"
(p. 93). And the poet's own political and economic judgements: "May all hoarders and all oppressors
perish. The farmers have again spoken of plentiful rain. Everyone is supported by your favor, says
Shah" (p. 266); "Oh my God, make Sindh fourish forever. Dear friend and sweet beloved, make the whole
world prosperous" (p. 265), of which the note says "The fnal invocation of God's blessings on Sindh
is one of the most often quoted verses of the Risālo" (p. 338).

**And the translator's reading of the praise poetry, which unsettles all of the above as history.**
The Jakhiro verses are "to be understood as forming an extended panegyric to the prophet Muhammad",
in keeping with "the conventions of Indian praise poetry" (p. 342); "As often, the prince loved by
Sasui stands for the divine beloved" (p. 341); Rai Diyach "here represents the Suf seeker who is
required to give up everything, even his life" (p. 340).

### Music, the *surando* and the modes

**The Risalo is organised by musical mode, and the notes give each mode's character and hour.**
Kalyan is "a musical mode associated with the late evening or early morning, which is used to express
devotional themes" (p. 320); "Yaman Kalyan is a musical variant of Kalyan" (p. 321); "Asa is a
musical mode that is performed at dawn" (p. 322); "Sirirag (Skt. Śrīrāga-) is the name of a
well-known Indian musical mode" (p. 323); "Khanbat is the name both of a musical mode and of a city
in Gujarat (compare 22.34), which was formerly known in English as Cambay" (p. 323); Desi is "the
name of a well-known musical mode" (p. 329); Husaini "is associated with laments for the martyrdom of
Imam Husain at Karbala" (p. 330); "Kamod is a musical mode associated with feelings of joy" (p. 333);
"Sarang (S. sārangu) is both the name of a musical mode associated with the rainy season and a word
meaning 'the rains'" (p. 337); "The familiar Indian mode Prabhati (S. piribhātī, Hindi prabhātī) is
sung before dawn (S. piribhāti)" (p. 339); one mode is "performed in the early morning (S. vihāgu;
compare Hindi bihāgṛā)" (p. 340); "Dahar is a Sindhi musical mode" (p. 341); "Bilaval is a well-known
Indian musical mode" (p. 342); "Kedaro (S. keḏāro, connected with Skt. kedāra- 'feld') is the name of
a musical mode associated with martial themes" (p. 343). In the verse chunks the titles stand
unglossed, and the chunk notes record them as printed and refuse to infer the category — "The chunk
nowhere states what these titles denote" (pp. 66, 92, 117); verse numbering restarts within each sur
(pp. 58–80).

**Instruments named across the whole book.** The **lute** (pp. 67, 217); the **rebab** — "The
instrument plays in my being, the rebab dwells in my spirit" (p. 97), "There is nothing in piety,
nothing in the rebab.¹⁹" (p. 116), "my veins sound like the strings of a rebab" (p. 268) — which the
notes make a marker of the worldly: the rebab "stands for the joys of music and pleasure, as opposed
to the austerities of the pious life" (p. 326). **Bells** are the guiding sound of Sur Suhini: "the
bells stir my whole body. How can I disclose to all and sundry the love that their clappers have
aroused?" (p. 103), "She is guided in midstream by the tinkling of the bells" (p. 103), "The sound of
the bells in the river thickets arouses me" (p. 103), "I heard the sound of the bells on the far bank
as I slept" (p. 103). Also drums, tabors and pipes (pp. 180–181); the **fddle** (p. 142) and "the
sound of fddles, lutes, and other instruments" filling the desert at night (p. 262); the cut reed —
"The cut reed utters sad sounds,¹⁰ the slain Sasui loudly laments" (p. 141), "Just as the reed lets
out melodious cries when it is being cut,¹" (p. 272), which the notes twice refer to the opening
reed-flute verse of Rumi's *Masnavī* (pp. 329, 339); bird cries used as instruments — "like the cry
of the koil.¹²", "like the cry of the crane" (p. 142); the **harp** on its hook (p. 281); "Pipes and
shawms are sounded on both sides" (p. 311); and "A musical gathering is assembled" with the rains
(p. 263).

**The yogis' horn and the *surando*.** The horn: "Their horns³ are all made of gold, but regard their
detachment and do not speak of their wealth" (p. 223); "The masters use their horns to consume the
self" (p. 224); "The masters carry their horns on their shoulders" (p. 229); "There are still yogis
who are worthy of their horns. They are sitting and playing, if you would hear them, oh sannyasi"
(p. 241); "Realize that that the horn you hold disgraces you.³⁷" (p. 242, the doubled word as
printed). Glossed as "The small animal horn (S. siṅī), which is the characteristic instrument of the
yogis. Compare 18.118V" (p. 334), with the caution "Here the sense seems to be that merely holding a
horn is not necessarily a sign of the true yogi" (p. 336). The **surando**: "I have understood the
secrets of their community from the sound of their surando.⁶" (p. 224); "I am slain by their surando"
(p. 224); "The surandos of the ascetics are no longer heard at dawn" (p. 224). The notes gloss a
bowed instrument "played with a bow, somewhat similar to the sārangī" (p. 335) and separately "the
double shawm (S. murilī), a wind instrument constructed with a gourd" (p. 336), hedged at "Apparently
referring to the double shawm" (p. 336) — the chunk notes do not state which gloss belongs to the
*surando*.

**The horn measured against every other instrument, in sur 18's closing vāī.** "It is not like the
pipe the camel men play. It wipes out the fute, and the gourd⁴⁰ is not its equal. It is not like the
bufalo bells that brought Suhini across.⁴¹" (p. 243); "It is sharper than the strings that severed
Diyach's head.⁴²… It far surpasses thousands of other instruments. Large bells charm wild beasts, but
it enchants men" (p. 244); "Sisters, says Abdul Latif, it brings the dead to life" (p. 244); and the
horn is set against the three regions, "in the north, in Sindh, or in Hind" (p. 244). Shackle's note
on the same vāī: it is a "fne vāī in praise of the yogi's horn" that "contrasts it with the inferior
qualities of the various instruments mentioned in other surs of the Risālo" (p. 336).

**Music as the vehicle of the divine, and as a force.** "The string does not play music but vibrates
with the sound of the divine mystery. Everyone says it is the instrument that sounds, but it is the
hands of the player that make the music" (p. 290); "The minstrel entered the palace with his magical
instrument. When he touched the strings, fortresses fell down" (p. 288); "The music its strings
played caused a tumult throughout the city. The maidservants were confused, the ladies cried out"
(p. 286); "For those whose body is a rosary, whose mind is a bead, and whose heart is the lute, the
strings of seeking resound with the mystery of unity. *He is one and he has no partner* is the tune
their veins play" (p. 67). Against these, sound is also to be refused: "Do not make a sound like an
instrument; listen and let duality slip away" (p. 131). And the secondary literature on Sindhi music
is named in the bibliography: Pannke 2014, *Saints and Singers: Suf Music in the Indus Valley*
(p. 350), and Yusuf, ed. 1988, *Rhythms of the Lower Indus: Perspectives on the Music of Sindh*
(p. 351).

### The Qur'anic, Arabic and Persian citations the translator identifies

**The poet's three constant books.** "All the sources agree that he kept three books with him as his
primary sources of continual inspiration: the Qur'an, from whose verses he so frequently quotes in
Arabic; Rumi's great Persian Masnavī; and the Sindhi verses of his ancestor Shah Karim" (p. 23). The
translation marks them typographically: italics mark Qur'anic and other Arabic and Persian
quotations, with Qur'anic translations "based on Yusuf Ali 1977" (p. 27, n. 33).

**Tags carried in the verse without glosses in the verse chunks** — every one marked by a note whose

### The translator on the Risalo's transmission, its editions and its genres

**The Risalo has no authorial text: it accreted.** "The Risālo emerged gradually from various
collections of the verses Shah Latif had produced on many diferent [text-layer?] occasions" (p. 10),
after oral extemporization and unsystematic transcription — "Shah Latif's verses were frst
[text-layer?] extemporized orally in speech or song, then unsystematically recorded in writing by
disciples" (p. 10). The shrine cult is what fixed it, in the Ganj of a.h. 1207 (1792 CE), forty-odd
years after the recorded death date of a.h. 1166 (p. 10). On the title: Risālo derives "from the
Arabic risāla 'treatise' (typically one written on an Islamic topic, often in prose), which is itself
cognate with the word rasūl 'apostle' used as a title of the prophet Muhammad. The common loose
translation of Risālo as 'the Message' thus conveys an appropriate sense of the poetry's uniquely
inspired character" (p. 8).

**The biography is hagiographic and thin, and Shackle says so at the outset.** "The largely
hagiographic accounts of his life are of the usual limited use in constructing a fully detailed
biography" (p. 9) — the sentence every life-fact in the introduction should be read against. Against
the "unlettered genius" reading: "It would however be misleading to see his poetry as the entirely
original product of an unlettered genius" (p. 9), on threefold evidence — the rural elite's training
in the Islamic sciences through Arabic and Persian, Shah Abdul Karim's pioneering Sindhi poems a
century earlier, and Shah Inat Rizvi's near-contemporary collection "strikingly similar in scope" —
concluding: "There was, therefore, already an established culture in Sindh of vernacular Suf
[text-layer?] poetry, although this tends to be overshadowed by Shah Latif's unique reputation in
the literary histories" (p. 9). And Sufism is presented as integral to Islam, as a corrective:
appreciating the Risālo "frst [text-layer?] means understanding that Sufsm [text-layer?] in India is
no exception to the general rule that Sufsm [text-layer?] is and always has been an integral part of
Islam" (p. 11).

**The genres, described precisely** (p. 14). The **bait**, "commonly known by the Arabic word bait
('verse,' plural abyāt)", in earlier Sindhi Sufi poetry "generally identical with the dohā"; Shah
Latif's bait "more usually contains three or more lines, in which the order of one or more of the
half-lines may be reversed, with the rhyme coming in the middle as in the Hindi soraṭhā"; "The meter
is fairly free, with a strict syllabic count not always being maintained, but the poetic structure of
the half-lines is tightened by Shah Abdul Latif's systematic use of strongly marked alliteration in
each half-line"; "The poet's signature is typically tied into the verse with alliteration." The
**vāī**, "a close relative of the kāfī", consists of "a varying number of monorhymed single verses,
preceded by a refrain repeated after each verse"; the **kāfī** is "the primary genre of Suf
[text-layer?] poetry in Panjabi" (p. 14). The notes add the larger units: the **sur** subdivided into
sections — "The three sections (dāstān) of this sur are defned with unusual clarity by their subject
matter" (p. 320) — and the vāī's function, "The exceptional inclusion here of several vāīs is
intended as a demonstration of the way vāīs are used to punctuate sequences of abyāt throughout the
Risālo" (p. 320). The poet's own word for his verse is set against the Qur'an's: "In this famous
line, Shah Latif claims for his poems (S. baita) a status comparable to the verses of the Qur'an (S.
āyatūṉ)" (p. 326). **The "V" suffix that marks the closing lyric of every sur is never explained
anywhere in the book as the ten chunks have it** — recorded at pp. 58, 67, 75, 87, 95, 116, 133, 142,
154, 162, 176, 183, 194, 214, 219, 222, 243, 250, 254, 257, 266, 270, 276, 280, 284, 292, 300–301,
307–308 and 316, each the last verse of its sur and each set in longer lines; thirty-plus instances
make it a systematic feature of the edition rather than an accident, and no chunk offers a key.

**The "Qur'an in the Sindhi language" reading, offered twice hedged.** The contrast in verse 7.77
between "baita, the ordinary Sindhi word for poems, and āyatūṉ, the Arabic word for verses of the
Qur'an, **might be seen as** an indirect claim for the status of the Risālo as a 'Qur'an in the
Sindhi language' comparable to the classic defnition [text-layer?] of the Masnavī as a 'Qur'an in
the Persian language'. Equally, **it might well be said** the Risālo is one of those very rare
instances in the literary history of South Asia of a genuinely integral Indo-Islamic creation"
(p. 24), compared to the Avadhi premākhyān at p. 27, n. 32.

**Authenticity and the editors, worked out twice for two surs.** On "Kedaro": its "clear alignment…
with the world of Shia mythology has raised questions about its authenticity, which is however
generally maintained with some qualifcation [text-layer?]" (p. 22), with the evidence in the endnote
— "It is omitted without comment in Kazi 1961. In Baloch 2012: 417–425 it is placed separately,
immediately following the main body of surs now generally agreed to be fully authentic, and preceding
a variety of extra surs that were present in earlier editions. These notably include a certainly
inauthentic Hir and a Dhol Marui that extend the geographical coverage of the Risālo to Panjab and to
Rajasthan" (p. 26, n. 28) — i.e. geographical over-reach beyond Sindh is what counts as spurious. The
back-matter headnote restates it: "The exceptional character of the sur has nevertheless caused some
editors to have reservations about its authenticity. It is accordingly omitted without explanation
from Kazi 1961 and is placed immediately before the set of apocryphal surs included at the end of
Baloch 2012" (p. 343), with Shackle's own explanation being confessional breadth — "While the Husain
cult is particularly associated with Shia Islam (Hyder 2006), the fgure of the martyred imam has
always had a far wider appeal. It therefore is not too surprising that this overtly Shia material
should be included in the Suf context of the Risālo (Schimmel 1979)" (p. 343).

**Sequence and placement as editorial acts, his own included.** "'Sorath' hardly deals with the
princess after whom the sur is named, and is accordingly placed separately in our numerical
sequence" (p. 26, n. 21), restated in the notes — "Sorath is not the heroine of this sur, which
instead focuses on the demand for supreme self-sacrifce on the part of Rai Diyach" (p. 340). On sur
3: "Asa is a musical mode that is performed at dawn, and in most editions this sur is placed toward
the end of the Risālo. But its contents are similar in character to those of the two opening surs"
(p. 322). And a tradition about sur 7's long vāī: "There is a tradition that it was Shah Latif's fnal
composition, and this is refected in its placement at the end of the entire Risālo in Kazi 1961;
compare Baloch 2012" (p. 326) — reported without endorsement.

**He records editors' disagreement and his own uncertainty, repeatedly.** "This is a riddling verse
variously explained by diferent editors" (p. 324); "The compressed expression is not entirely clear"
(p. 322); "This seems to be the meaning of this highly condensed half-line" (p. 334); "This is
variously interpreted to indicate the mystery of humility, or the special character of man, formed
from clay" (p. 326); "Apparently referring to the double shawm" (p. 336); "Here the sense seems to be
that merely holding a horn is not necessarily a sign of the true yogi" (p. 336).

**Translation method, declared** (pp. 24–25): "a consistent style of plain English prose that aims to
steer a middle path between of-putting [text-layer?] formality and jarring colloquialism"; no
imitation of rhyme, alliteration only "where this occurred naturally"; "the underlying syntax of the
verses has been maintained, with a full stop marking the end of an individual line and a comma being
used where appropriate to indicate the half-line caesura". He recommends reading each sur's first
endnote before the chapter (p. 25). What the translation loses is itemised in the notes: "The
original alliteration is between S. sikaṇu 'desire' and S. sūrī 'the gallows'" (p. 322); "There is
wordplay between Ar. fī'l-ḥaqīqat 'in reality' and fīl 'elephant'" (p. 323); "the close verbal
similarity in Sindhi between mīṉhuṉ 'rain' and nīṉhuṉ 'love'" (p. 338); and register — "Literally,
those respond with the polite jīu 'yes,' rather than the rude baudu 'yeah?'" (p. 322). **Speaker
attribution is supplied by the translator where the verse leaves it open**, which matters for anyone
quoting the poem: "the quotation marks indicate the words of the woman who has been left behind"
(p. 324); "This and the following verses are to be understood as laments by one of the dead
fshermen's wives" (p. 334); "The speaker here is the poet himself" (p. 333); "The last line is
addressed to God" (p. 328); "The mountain passes reply to Sasui's question" (p. 331).

**Allegory as a standing method, declared case by case.** Suhini — "Suhini's courage is upheld as an
example to all true seekers of the divine, as opposed to the insincere who rely on mechanical aids to
cross the river… the vast river… symbolizes the ocean of existence that every soul must traverse"
(p. 325); Sasui — the story serves "as a powerful image for the devoted pursuit of the divine
beloved" (p. 328), and "As often, Bhambhor symbolizes this world" (p. 328); Lila Chanesar (p. 331);
Ghatu — "The story naturally lends itself to interpretation as an allegory of the struggle against
the power of the lower self" (p. 334); Sorath (p. 340); and the camel of sur 4, which "personifes the
lower self (Ar. nafs) with its stubborn resistance to the spiritual life" (p. 323). The Jakhiro
panegyric and Sasui's prince are read the same way (pp. 341–342).

**Indirection as his central claim about the poet.** Of *anā 'l-ḥaqq*: "The oblique reference to this
notorious expression, so frequently invoked by other Suf poets like Bullhe Shah and Sachal Sarmast,
is a telling illustration of Shah Latif's preference for the indirect expression of a profoundly Suf
understanding of the world" (p. 326). The introduction puts the same point through the Sunni/Shia
anecdote: the "ambiguity may be the fundamental reason why — in contrast to the relative ease with
which the formal structures of Shah Latif's poetry, even the capacious matter of its content, may be
defned [text-layer?] — it is so much harder to pin down the distinctive manner of its expression";
"Shah Latif proclaims the same truth as other more outspoken Sufs [text-layer?] while disclaiming the
need always to speak of it so openly" (p. 23).

**A three-cornered critique of the existing scholarship** (pp. 13–14). (i) Indian scholarship
"naturally tend[s] to view Shah Latif as one of the many great premodern poet saints who helped to
construct the national identity of modern India, and to detect the particular inspiration of the
Vedanta in his exposition of universal spiritual truths". (ii) Pakistani scholarship is "very large",
"mostly written in Sindhi or in Urdu and so has had rather less impact on international
understanding", with interpretations "tied to local preoccupations, as when Shah Latif is too
narrowly seen as an authentic spokesman of the Sindhi folk tradition or as an advocate of Sindhi
nationalism" (p. 13, citing Syed 1996 at p. 26, n. 13). (iii) His own corrective is "the common
emphasis upon the Islamic character of Shah Latif's poetry" (p. 14), credited in English above all to
Schimmel 1976 (p. 26, n. 14). A nativist reading is separately flagged as available rather than
asserted: "The sur also allows for a nativist interpretation that exalts the authenticity of the
values and landscape of Sindh" (p. 332). And the Sindhi tradition is distinguished from the Panjabi
one: "The Sindhi version, which is rather diferent from the Panjabi story cited in most modern
descriptions, is set among the pastoral tribes who grazed their bufaloes in the pastures watered by
the Indus" (p. 324).

### Contradictions the chunk notes record and do not resolve

- **"Anirai" in two incompatible senses, both footnoted.** At p. 289 he is someone to whom a promise
  must be kept — "depart quickly, lest you fail in your promise to Anirai"; at p. 304 he is the type
  of the worthless ruler — "all other rulers are like Anirai". The back-matter note adds a third
  datum, "the ruler who was the rival of Diyach" (p. 343), referring the reader to sur 27's
  introductory note. Do not assert that one figure is meant.
- **Verses 104 and 105 give opposite verdicts on the Hinglaj pilgrimage, in consecutive verses.**
  Verse 104: "The yogis did not look at the place where the abode of the lord is. The yogis with
  misguided faith traveled on a long journey. He is here, but they went in vain to Hinglaj" (p. 241).
  Verse 105: "The yogis did look³⁴ at the place where the abode of the lord is. The yogis with rightly
  guided faith traveled on a long journey. He is right here, and they found him in Hinglaj" (p. 242).
  The distinction is stated as "misguided" against "rightly guided" faith, nothing more, and footnote
  34 sits on the pivot verb — the single most important missing note in the book's verse chunks.
- **The *jalālī*/*jamālī* pairing is printed against the usual sense**: "devotees of divine beauty
  (Ar. jalālī) and those of divine majesty (Ar. jamālī)" (p. 334). Whether that is the book's intent, a
  printing slip or an extraction transposition cannot be settled from the text layer. Do not quote the
  note as a definition without checking the page.
- **Aids to crossing are both forbidden and required, inside one sur.** "For all who feel the force of
  love, a raft is only a handicap" (p. 98); "Do not set your hand on the beloved's raft as you drown"
  (p. 110); "Even if you are drowning, do not touch the beloved's raft… Step in the direction where
  there is no safety hut" (pp. 110–111); "If you drown in preparation for dry land, then go ahead and
  drown. Do not gather straw and thorn and sticks to make a raft" (p. 111) — against "You must depart
  to the other side; construct your raft for the deep water" (p. 111), and Sur Sirirag's sustained
  praise of the prudent sailor who takes "strong equipment" and "the advice of the experts" (pp. 81,
  85).
- **Suhini is destroyed and crosses safely.** "The alligator grasps her leg, the cayman has her head…

- **The journey itself is both the discipline and the error.** The Sasui cycle is a sustained praise of
  setting out on foot — "for God's sake be content with those who travel on foot" (p. 126), "as I make
  my way toward my beloved, I will not wear any kind of shoe" (p. 137), "**Shoes are worn by those who

  to look for Punhun" (p. 125), "why do you look for a messenger to tell you about him?" (p. 125).
- **Weeping is both required and rejected**, in consecutive verses of sur 2: verses 9 and 10 both end

- **Sleep likewise**: verse 54 rebukes the one who "just sleep[s], demanding a comfortable bed"

  are the model for "keeping awake and taking no rest all night long" (p. 42).
- **Reward is detached from birth, from skill, and from neither.** "Gifts are not bestowed according to

  singing, yet the king is pleased" (p. 282) and "Become ignorant and beg, forget all your knowledge"

- **Being lost is preferred to being guided**, against the guide's necessity above: "Learning from the

### Identifications the chunk notes refuse, carried forward as refusals

- **"Lakho", "Lakho Phulani" and the mare "Lakhi" (p. 300) are not
  `shrine-of-lakhi-shah-saddar`.** The chunk's Lakho is a mounted raider of Kachchh with no shrine,
  grave, silsila or sanctity attached, glossed as "a legendary bandit from the heroic age of Sindh"
  (p. 342). The similarity of the names is the whole of the resemblance.
- **"the lord of Las Bela" (p. 282) is not `chandragup-baba-chandragup`.** The chunk names a patron
  king called Sapar and no site, temple or shrine in Las Bela at all; the same refusal was recorded
  for Las Bela as a point on the camels' route (p. 167), and again where the notes repeatedly name the
  district (pp. 327, 328, 330, 337, 340) without ever mentioning Chandragup.
- **"Lahut… the name of a small place in Sindh on the yogis' pilgrimage route" (p. 335) is not
  `shah-noorani-shrine-syed-bilawal-shah-noorani`.** The note places Lahut in **Sindh**, the archive
  row places Lahoot Lamakan in **Khuzdar District, Balochistan**, and the note also offers the
  alternative that *lāhūtī* denotes a sect and not a place at all. Two readings, two provinces: flag,
  do not pick.
- **"Gorakhnath" (p. 237) matches the principal figure of two archive rows at once** —
  `gorakhnath-goraknath-temple` (Gorakhnath Temple, Gor Khatri, Peshawar) and `tilla-jogian` (Tilla
  Jogian, Salt Range) — and the chunk names the figure only, with no place, so nothing distinguishes
  them. Do not pick one. The related note "The master revered by most orders of yogis" (p. 336)
  **gives no name, and none should be supplied**.
- **Hinglaj cannot be filed to an archive row at all**, because the index row named *Shaktipeeth Shri
  Hinglaj Mata Mandir* (Hingol Balochistan National Park) **has an empty id column** — the only such
  row in the index. This is the book's clearest Balochistan material (pp. 20, 225, 238, 241–242, 254,
  335, 336, 346), and the Hinglaj verses 104/105 are its explicit argument about pilgrimage, so the
  gap is consequential. A human must decide whether that row gets an id.
- **"Umarkot" is not `umarkot-amarkot-shiv-mandir`.** The chunks name only a fort, a palace, mansions
  and a cell there (pp. 199, 202), "his fortress of Umarkot" and "the city of Umarkot" (pp. 332, 338),
  and one name in a list of places rained on (p. 265). No temple, and no religious site of any kind.
- **"Kalat" (p. 251) is not `kalat-kali-temple`.** The chunk names Kalat only as somewhere the beloved
  might have gone; no temple, deity or building.
- **"Jhok" (p. 264) was flagged and not mapped** on the verse chunk's evidence, the verse reading only
  "the beloved thunders near Jhok.¹⁴" with its footnote outside the chunk. (The endnotes chunk does
  name "Shah Inayat of Jhok" at pp. 321 and 325 — that is Part A's business, not this section's.)
- **"Shiv" and "Ram" name deities and no site** (pp. 225, 227, 231, 234–236, 240). No temple, place or
  practice-at-a-site is attached to either name in any chunk, so no row is informed. Do not map a
  deity-name to a row.
- **The unnamed hill "in District Hyderabad in southern Sindh" that marks the start of the Hinglaj
  pilgrimage (p. 336)** is exactly the kind of site the archive collects, but the note gives no name;
  the glossary calls it only "ganjo Hill in southern Sindh" (p. 346). Unplaceable from the chunks.

## Doubts and review points

Page references throughout are PDF page indices, as the chunk notes recorded them. Items that block
work come first.

**1. Blocker: the Hinglaj material has no archive row to go to.** `shrine_index.tsv` carries a row
named *Shaktipeeth Shri Hinglaj Mata Mandir* (Hingol Balochistan National Park, Road، Asha Pura,
Pakistan) **whose `id` column is empty** — chunks 006, 009 and 010 each state it is the only such
row in the index. With no id in column 1 there is no valid mapping, so every Hinglaj citation in
this book sits under "Other saints, sites and events" instead, and none of it can be filed against
a row. Four separate chunks hit this independently (001, 006, 007, 009, 010). What is stranded:

- the yogi pilgrimage as a real route through Sindh — "the wandering yogis who traversed Sindh
  during their pilgrimage to the shrine of the goddess at Hinglaj in Balochistan", the subject of
  three surs ("Ramakali", "Khahori", "Purab") (p. 20), with Shah Latif said to have spent time
  among them (p. 20);
- "The naked ones have gone to Hinglaj⁸ to behold the goddess… Their guide is Ali¹⁰" (p. 225);
- **verses 104 and 105, pp. 241–242, which give opposite verdicts on the pilgrimage** — v. 104: "The
  yogis with misguided faith traveled on a long journey. He is here, but they went in vain to
  Hinglaj" (p. 241); v. 105: "The yogis did look³⁴ at the place where the abode of the lord is. The
  yogis with rightly guided faith traveled on a long journey. He is right here, and they found him
  in Hinglaj" (p. 242). Chunk 007 calls this pair "the most consequential thing in the chunk for any
  argument about pilgrimage";
- further mentions at pp. 238 and 254; the translator's gloss "A remote site in Balochistan, sacred
  to the goddess (S. nānī), and an important center of pilgrimage for the yogis" (p. 335) — which
  does not itself print the word Hinglaj, the name appearing only at p. 336; and the glossary entry
  "hinglaj (Hinglāj) Pilgrimage site in Balochistan" (p. 346).

**A human must decide whether that index row gets an id before any of this can enter an entry.**
Note also that `chandragup-baba-chandragup` is located in the same national park; Chandragup is
never mentioned by the book and no mapping to it is implied (chunks 001, 006, 009).

**12. Dates: what this book will and will not support.**

- **The poet's dates are given two ways within five pages.** The introduction prints "(1689–1752)"
  flat (p. 8); the book's own Library of Congress record prints "‘Abd al-Laṭīf, Shah, approximately
  1689-approximately 1752, author" (p. 5). The introduction gives the *recorded* death date as "14
  Safar a.h. 1166 (1752 CE)" (p. 10) and gives no birth evidence at all. Chunk 001: "**Do not present
  1689 as documented on this book's authority.**"
- **No birth or death year for Shah Latif appears anywhere in the notes apparatus** (chunk 009,
  pp. 320–340: "not stated") or in chunks 002–008 and 010.
- The only Hijri date in the book is printed in Latin transliteration only; **no Arabic-script or
  Sindhi-script date appears anywhere**. The only Arabic script that survives the extraction at all
  is in note 27.4 — "the word Ahad ("احد the One," i.e., God, is distinguished from the name Ahmad
  (احمد), i.e., the" (p. 340) — "printed with the parenthesis and quotation marks displaced around
  the script", and the note breaks off mid-sentence at the chunk boundary.
- The Ganj is dated "a.h. 1207 (1792 CE)" (p. 10); where it is held and who holds it now are **not
  stated** (chunk 001). The bibliography records "Mirza, Mumtaz. 1994. The Ganj of Shah Abdul Latif
  Bhitai. Hyderabad: Bhitshah Cultural Centre Committee" (p. 348), which is imprint evidence only.
- Other dates the book does give, each as printed: Shah Abdul Karim "(d. 1623)" and Shah Inat Rizvi
  "(d. 1711)" (p. 9); Bullhe Shah "(d. 1758)" (p. 12) — against the archive row's 1757; Shah Inayat
  of Jhok "(d. 1718)" (p. 325); "Sultan Alauddin (d. 1316)" (p. 342); Karbala "in 680" (pp. 22, 343).

**13. Provenance of the text being quoted.** The front matter states: "This is an English only
edition of Risalo by Shah Abdul Latif, published by Havard [text-layer?] University Press in 2018.
The Sindhi text has been taken out and **the introduction has been slightly edited**" (p. 5); "First
published in Murty Classical Library of India, Volume 18, Harvard University Press, 2018"; copyright
"© 2018 by the President and Fellows of Harvard College" (p. 5); title page "2025" (p. 4). Two
consequences. **The introduction summarised in these notes is an edited version of the 2018 one**,
so any quotation from pp. 8–27 is from the edited text. And the repeated doubt raised independently
in chunks 002, 003, 004, 005 and 006 — that no facing Sindhi original appears anywhere in pp. 41–233
and that a human should check the PDF before recording that this edition prints none — is answered
only by that sentence at p. 5; the chunks themselves could not settle it, and chunk 005 warns that
"Whether the facing original pages are absent from the PDF or merely absent from its text layer
cannot be determined from the chunk". The consequence for the archive's standard, in chunk 002's
words: "**the instruction to quote every name and date in the original script could not be met
here**, because nothing in the chunk is printed in an original script." The only reference anywhere
to the shrine's physical appearance is the cover credit — an "Illustration based on the Sufi Shrine
of Shah Abdul Lati [text-layer?] Bhittai in Pakistan" (p. 5).

**14. Authenticity reservations the book itself records, which attach to anything cited from the
affected surs.** Sur 30 "Kedaro": its "clear alignment… with the world of Shia mythology has raised
questions about its authenticity, which is however generally maintained with some qualifcation
[text-layer?]" (p. 22); the evidence is that "It is omitted without comment in Kazi 1961. In Baloch
2012: 417–425 it is placed separately, immediately following the main body of surs now generally
agreed to be fully authentic" (p. 26, n. 28), restated at p. 343 — "It is accordingly omitted
without explanation from Kazi 1961 and is placed immediately before the set of apocryphal surs
included at the end of Baloch 2012". Sur 3 "Asa" is placed differently here from most editions
(p. 322). A tradition, reported and not endorsed, holds that sur 7's long vāī "was Shah Latif's fnal
composition" (p. 326). And the Risalo has no authorial text at all: it "emerged gradually from
various collections of the verses Shah Latif had produced on many diferent [text-layer?] occasions"
(p. 10), after oral extemporization and unsystematic transcription by disciples.

**15. The footnote apparatus and the verse text are in different chunks, and pairing them is where
an error will enter.** Chunks 002–008 carry dense superscript markers with **no note text at all**
(chunk 003 counts "at least thirty passages"; chunk 004 "roughly 24 markers in Sur 8, 12 in Sur 9,
15 in Sur 10 and 1 in Sur 11"; chunk 007 lists sections 18–24 markers; chunk 008 lists sections
26–30 markers 1–8, 1–10, 1–14, 1–13, 1–11). Chunks 009 and 010 carry the notes but **not the verses
they gloss** — "Every numbered note points at a sur-and-verse reference (1.47, 7.63, 18.118V, and so
on) whose text is in other chunks… Pairing a gloss with its verse is a Pass 2 job" (chunk 009);
"The endnotes are unreadable as evidence without their verses" (chunk 010).

- **The single most important missing note is 34 (p. 242)**, which "sits on the verb that reverses
  verse 104 into verse 105" — i.e. on the pivot of the Hinglaj pair (chunk 007).
- Footnote 9 at p. 148 is attached "to precisely the contradictory line" in the Sasui story and is
  not in that range (chunk 004).
- "Thursday oferings" (p. 299) is footnoted and the note is absent; note 11 to sur 28 says only "The
  day on which oferings are traditionally presented at Suf shrines" (p. 342) and **does not name the
  day**. The verse names Thursday; the note does not confirm it.
- Notes that gloss nothing nameable, quoted so that no one builds on them: "A legendary Arab
  chieftain who is proverbial for his generosity throughout Islamic literature." (p. 343, no name
  given); "I.e., the world." (p. 341); "In ritual preparation for eating." (p. 342); "A common plant
  growing wild in the Indus valley." (p. 341); and in chunk 009 "The master revered by most orders of
  yogis" (p. 336), "One of the names of Satan" (p. 321), "A place in Sindh" (p. 338), "A place in
  Balochistan, to the west of Sindh" (p. 337), "Epithet of Sapar" and "Another epithet of Sapar"
  (p. 340). "Do not build a fact on a note alone."
- A large number of notes give only "Ar. …" or "Pers. …" with a Qur'an, Hadith or "(Suf saying)"
  citation and **no English gloss** — e.g. the two shaikh sayings (p. 329), "Ar. fānī fī 'llāhi"
  (p. 322), "Ar. inna auliyāī taḥta qabāī (Hadith)" (p. 323), "Ar. ashhadu" (p. 323). "Anything that
  depends on those phrases cannot be read from this chunk."
- Six place-names in sur 22 could not be placed from the verse chunk at all: **Kirar, Variyasu,
  Makani, Hadakut, Garang, Padham** (pp. 261–262). Kirar — "the name the chunk twice pairs with
  Bhit" — is glossed at p. 338; the other five remain unplaced. Several terms flagged unglossed in
  chunks 003–005 ("the Harho", "the Vankar", "the Arichos", Sahar/Mehar, Sasui's caste) are glossed
  in the apparatus at pp. 324–328 and in the glossary at p. 346; each such pairing is a human
  judgement across two chunks.

**16. The "V" suffix on the closing verse number of every sur is nowhere explained in these notes.**
Recorded at 123V, 58V, 40V (pp. 58, 67, 75); 67V, 46V, 118V (pp. 87, 95, 116); 99V, 56V, 61V
(pp. 133, 142, 154); 42V, 97V, 30V, 64V (pp. 162, 176, 183, 194); 108V, 27V, 13V (pp. 214, 219, 222);
118V, 32V, 20V, 20V, 43V, 29V (pp. 243, 250, 254, 257, 266, 270); 25V, 21V, 20V, 39V, 56V, 35V, 44V
(pp. 276, 280, 284, 292, 300–301, 307–308, 316). Each is the last verse of its sur and each is set as
a run of long or short lines rather than as a numbered block. Six chunks say the same thing: "do not
gloss it", "Do not expand it". The closest the apparatus comes is the vāī as a named form — "The
exceptional inclusion here of several vāīs is intended as a demonstration of the way vāīs are used
to punctuate sequences of abyāt throughout the Risālo" (p. 320), with a cross-reference to
"18.118V" (p. 334) — but **no chunk states the equivalence, and it must not be asserted from them.**
Note also that 44V (p. 316) is the one closing verse in chunk 008 that does not name the poet.

**17. Internal contradictions the book prints side by side. Keep both; do not reconcile.**

- Hinglaj, verses 104 and 105 (pp. 241–242) — see item 1.
- Suhini is "severed limb from limb" (p. 97) and "crosses safely, unafected [text-layer?] by the
  whirlpools" (p. 101).
- The raft must be refused — "Even if you are drowning, do not touch the beloved's raft" (pp.
  110–111) — and must be built — "You must depart to the other side; construct your raft for the
  deep water" (p. 111); against which sur Sirirag praises throughout the prudent sailor with "strong
  equipment" and "the advice of the experts" (pp. 81, 85). "The contradiction is the chunk's, not a
  transcription problem."
- Sasui reached the beloved only by dying — "Woman, it was after you died that you got to be with the
  beloved" (p. 134), with her death and mourning narrated at pp. 140–141 — against "At last her
  husband came back to her, and her journey turned out well.9" (p. 148) and the closing verses
  announcing his arrival (pp. 133, 154).
- The journey itself is the error — "Do not travel to the hills, the Vankar is inside you" (p. 120),
  "Sasui, search all the corners of your house" (p. 120), "No one goes to a shop to look for Punhun"
  (p. 125) — set against a whole cycle in praise of setting out on foot (pp. 126, 137).
- Weeping: sur 2 vv. 9–10 have the perfect lover "spend the whole night weeping" and v. 11,
  immediately after, "Those who truly think of the beloved do not weep or say anything" (all p. 41).
- Sleep: sur 3 v. 54 rebukes the sleeper and vv. 56–57 make sleep "their worship" (p. 67).
- The yogis are given "Ali" as guide to Hinglaj and Dwarka (p. 225) and hearts in which "Ram always
  dwells" (p. 227), and also "They have nothing to do with unbelievers, and they do not have Islam in
  their minds" (pp. 232–233). "The chunk states both and reconciles neither."
- Reward is by performance — "Gifts are not bestowed according to caste; it is performance that is
  rewarded" (p. 282) — beside "You may not have learned anything of singing, yet the king is pleased"
  and "Become ignorant and beg, forget all your knowledge" (p. 282). "The chunk asserts all three and
  reconciles none."
- "Anirai" is used in two incompatible senses, both footnoted: someone to whom a promise must be kept
  (p. 289) and the type of the worthless ruler, "all other rulers are like Anirai" (p. 304). "Do not
  assert that one figure is meant." Chunk 010 adds "the ruler who was the rival of Diyach" (p. 343).
- **A judgement against the shrine relation itself, in the poet's voice**, which anyone quoting this
  book in support of shrine practice must set beside their quotation: "Do not let yourself be
  worshiped, oh yogi, curses on people's devotion" and "Do not let yourself be worshiped, oh yogi,
  but maintain your yoga. Oh master, to make people your servants is a great fault" (both p. 237);
  "They do not make yoga an object of pilgrimage or worship" (pp. 234–235); "The body is a mosque and
  the mind is a cell; do not keep a forty-day vigil" (p. 52).

**18. Identities the book leaves open.**

- **Umar, Sumiro and Hamir** may or may not be one person: all three are used in direct address in
  the same sur, "Oh Umar Sumiro" (p. 199) joins two of them, and "oh Hamir" appears at pp. 197 and
  202 "with no introduction". "Do not assert an identification from this chunk."
- **Sahar and Mehar** are used interchangeably of Suhini's beloved (pp. 104, 113, 116) but p. 99 says
  "Sahar is the same as Suhini, and it is Sahar who is the sea", "which cuts across the
  identification". The apparatus glosses Sahar as "also called Mehar, literally 'bufalo herdsman'"
  (pp. 324–325). Chunk 003: "Do not resolve this; the chunk does not."
- **"Todi"** (p. 101, verses 29–31) is unexplained, carries endnote marker 5 and occurs nowhere else:
  "It could be a variant name, a second figure, or a musical mode; **the chunk does not say**."
- **"Peacock"**, capitalised as a proper name with a footnote marker — "We are like the fea
  [text-layer?] that clings to Peacock's2 ear" (p. 134). "The note is absent; the referent cannot be
  recovered here."
- **"Sorath"** is both the sur title and a queen's name (pp. 285, 286, 291, 292), and "Sorath is
  dead," at p. 292 carries footnote 10. "Which is intended at that line cannot be settled from this
  chunk." Compare p. 340: "Sorath is not the heroine of this sur".
- **"He is the leader of all the prophets sent to the world" (p. 305) is said of Jakhiro** in a
  section that elsewhere treats him as a Samo chief and lord of Kachchh (p. 304); chunk 008 requires
  a human to read footnotes 5–10 of section 29 first. Chunk 010 supplies the reading: the Jakhiro
  verses are "to be understood as forming an extended panegyric to the prophet Muhammad" (p. 342).
  **Do not cite p. 305 as a claim about a Rajput ruler.**
- **Hasan's absence from Karbala** is asserted flatly and footnoted — "Hasan was not with Husain, who
  had no helper or support" (p. 313) — with no explanation in that chunk; chunk 010 gives "had died
  shortly before the battle" (p. 344). Report as the book prints it; add nothing.
- Unglossed quantities and figures quoted as printed and not interpreted: "eighty-four moons may
  rise" (p. 70); "eating seventy dishes" (p. 60); "give lakhs for every drop" (p. 47); "Two branches
  cost millions, and leaves cost half a million a quarter" (p. 75); "I am the one whom eighty eyes
  cannot fnd [text-layer?] when I am hidden" (p. 84) — "should not be paraphrased"; "Later, when
  broken, its value was more than a billion.5" (p. 84) — "either a deliberate paradox or a
  translation/printing problem", flagged not resolved; "wells sixty fathoms deep" (p. 207); "Meaning
  that the hoarders would make a 300 percent proft" (p. 338), the only figure of any kind in the
  notes apparatus.
- **Verse 2.71 is quoted twice, differently, and endnoted the same way both times** — at p. 16 "…this
  is what Rumi said. If you remove the veil from your heart, you will behold him within" (n. 19,
  "Verse 2.71", p. 25), and at p. 23 "…this is what Rumi **believed**. Those who have seen this place
  do not speak of it" (n. 30, "Verse 2.71", p. 26). "This may be two portions of one long bait or an
  inconsistency; the chunk does not settle it."
