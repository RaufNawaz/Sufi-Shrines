# eaton_essays_islam_indian_history — chunk 005 — pp. 70–82

Chapter Two continued: the three Naga case studies — Ao (pp. 72–78), Sema (pp. 78–82), Angami
(pp. 82–). **No archive row and no South Asian shrine material.** What the chunk contributes is
Eaton's conversion mechanism worked out in detail on a case where the evidence is unusually
good, and three of its findings bear on how the Punjab essays should be read:

1. **Translation of the divine name decides the outcome.** Where the missionaries used an
   indigenous term (Ao *tsungrem*, Sema *Alhou*) conversion was fast; the Angami case, where they
   did not, was slow. Religious change travelled through existing vocabulary.
2. **Mobility disposes a community toward a high god; sedentary agriculture does not.** The
   migratory Semas had the most elaborated high god and converted fastest without missionaries;
   the terrace-farming Angamis had female agrarian deities, "a close fit between their land and
   their fertility cult", and resisted.
3. Point 2 sits **in apparent tension with the Punjab thesis** that Islamization accompanied
   peasantization. See Doubts — it is the single most useful cross-reference in this chunk for a
   thesis that cites both.

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- **Lungkijingba**, Ao high god, "believed to live high in the sky and to be concerned with the
  ultimate destiny of all humans", but so remote that "his effective contact was limited to other
  deities and did not extend to humans"; "Ao villagers did not even make offerings to this high
  god" (p. 72). The leaf-lottery account, quoted from William Smith 1925 summarising E. W. Clark's
  *Ao-Naga Dictionary* (1911), 360–2: he "pulls to pieces certain leaves, pronouncing on each
  piece a fate or destiny… **the great god does not know what is to be the lot of each man; it
  resolves itself into a mere blind game of chance**" (p. 72).
- **Lizaba**, Ao creator of the earth, controller of rain "and consequently of the food supply for
  the dry rice-growing Aos", commander of "sickness and disease"; "most village offerings and
  sacrifices were made to him" (p. 72). Clark, 1911: "the Aos never travelled much beyond their own
  country and their horizon was supposed to embrace all there was of the world, so though Lizaba
  has the credit of being world maker, all he did (by tradition) was to level the surface of the
  plain of the Assam Valley" (p. 73).
- **Ao spirit classes**, all *tsungrem*: sky spirits (*anung tsungrem*); **house-site spirits**
  (*kimung tsungrem*), "fixed beings that haunted the same house-site no matter who occupied it";
  house-spirits (*ki-tsung tsungrem*), "attached to individuals and moved with them wherever they
  went"; and jungle ghosts (*arem tsungrem*), "malevolent dwarf creatures" (p. 73).
- **Ao origin and afterlife topography**: the ancestors "were thought to have come out of the
  earth at **six stones** located at a particular spur on the right bank of the **Dikhu River**,
  while the path to paradise was identified with a particular long ridge that sloped west to east"
  (p. 73). A locality-specific sacred geography of exactly the kind a shrine archive records.
- **Alhou**, Sema high god. Hutton, 1921: "'Omniscience and omnipotence are vaguely ascribed to
  Him… and though He is remote and inaccessible, He seems to be all-good as well as almighty and
  all-knowing… **Alhou is the supreme dispenser of good and evil, and it is He who makes men rich
  or poor**'"; the Semas located him not in a quarter of the cosmos "but rather in all space
  between heaven and earth", and Hutton "heard a Sema attribute to Him the quality of
  omnipresence… though the Sema in question was not educated or even semi-christianized"
  (p. 81).
- **Ukepenopfu**, Angami supreme being, and **Maweno**, "the deity of fruitfulness" — "both
  female, as is typical among the more sedentary, agrarian societies of South Asia" (p. 82).
- Dates: Ao country annexed by the British **1889** (p. 76); the Sema country annexed "in 1906 and
  1921" (p. 80, n. 68); the Kohima mission planted **1878** "in the very heart of Angami country"
  (p. 82); Ao Matthew printed **1883**, complete Ao New Testament **1929**; first Angami New
  Testament **1927**; Mark into Sema **1928**, complete Sema New Testament **1950**; first Lotha
  Gospels in the 1930s (pp. 74, 77, 81).

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Two grades of religious specialist, and how they were chosen and paid.** Ao village priests,
  **putirs**, "were chosen 'by public opinion' from among the eldest males of each clan in an Ao
  village. **They were compensated for their services on the occasion of village feasts.**" A
  second class, **arasentsuxs**, "communicated with the spirit world on behalf of individuals,
  especially for the purpose of exorcising malevolent spirits" (p. 72, citing J. P. Mills, *The Ao
  Nagas*, 1926, 244). The village/individual division of religious labour, with fee at the feast,
  is a close structural parallel to a shrine's mujawir and its individual-petition practice.
- **A chief's dues from his dependants, itemised.** Sema villages "were generally ruled by a
  single autocratic chief who belonged to a ruling lineage that extended throughout the Sema
  country"; the chiefs "directed the villages in war, decided all matters of relations with other
  villages, and determined what lands the village would cultivate", and "In return for land,
  wives, and food when necessary, a chief's subject-dependents—whom Hutton called 'serfs'—gave
  him **obeisance, regular labour in his personal fields, the leg of any sacrificed animal, and
  help in war**" (p. 79).
- **A succession rule that forced constant fission — and the mechanism by which it collapsed.**
  "although they followed a general principle of primogeniture, sons and brothers of a former
  chief also had claims on the latter's lands and labour. To prevent such men from pressing their
  claims, a Sema chief had constantly to transfer retainers to his kin, who would then move away
  and colonize new villages or conquer old ones, themselves becoming chiefs in the process."
  Hutton: "'**The authority of a Sema chief is quickly sapped when he can no longer shed off his
  brothers and sons to found new villages** with retainers of their own. In such circumstances he
  ceases in a generation to be socially distinct from others of his family, and a decline in his
  political authority follows as a matter of course'" (p. 79). The British ban on warfare stopped
  the outlet (pp. 79–80). **A succession-and-endowment dynamic worth comparing with the fission of
  sajjada-nashin lineages.**
- **A printing press hauled up by a village.** In 1883 E. W. Clark "managed to procure a
  second-hand American printing press which, he noted, took 'all the men of the village about a
  week' to haul up to his mission village of Molungyimsen", where he began printing a catechism, a
  biography of Joseph, and Matthew in Ao in Roman script (p. 77).
- **Material and epidemiological change under colonial rule, itemised**: "kerosene lanterns
  replaced reed torches, buttons replaced shells, safety matches replaced friction devices, steel
  needles replaced bamboo needles, and firearms replaced spears"; "metal currency in the form of
  the silver rupee replaced unhulled rice as the medium of commercial exchange"; "new scourges
  such as tuberculosis and venereal disease were introduced from the plains" (p. 71). **Some 4,000
  Nagas** were formed into a Labour Corps in World War I and sent to France (p. 70).
- **Authority moved from persons to writing.** "the face-to-face, fluid nature of pre-colonial
  village authority—whether vested in chiefs as in the more autocratic Sema villages, or in
  village councils as in the more representative Angami villages—was gradually undermined and
  replaced by **a fixed, written authority in the form of codified laws**. Now, should aggrieved
  villagers be dissatisfied with the decisions of their local chiefs or councils, they could seek
  redress with a higher authority, the District Magistrate" (p. 71). The same displacement of local
  religious authority into a colonial court that produces the 1938 Pakpattan litigation.
- **A leaderless congregation growing without clergy.** In the Sema country, "In the mid-1920s…
  a missionary from Impur, J. R. Bailey, toured the region and found **many Christian groups with
  their own meeting houses, holding regular services without visible leadership**. This appeared so
  strange to him that he refrained from baptizing the converts or organizing them in a more regular
  way for fear that he might interfere with this seemingly spontaneous movement." Tanquist in 1936
  reported "villages where around sixty households were Christian, who had been worshiping for
  three or four years without leadership and without ever having seen communion conducted"; and
  "**By 1938 every Sema village to which such teachers had been sent was entirely Christian**"
  (pp. 78–79).
- **Persecution by chiefs, and then chiefs converting.** "we do hear of village chiefs in some
  cases driving Christians out of their villages and into the forest where they formed small groups
  living on roots and leaves"; but "many Sema chiefs, unable to shed their kin from the village,
  and becoming increasingly isolated politically, opted to convert to Christianity and even take
  the lead in conversion movements among their clients" (p. 80). Footnote 71 tells the case of a
  300-house village in 1931 whose hostile old chief, whose baptised younger brother had become
  "the leader of a large section of the village's retainer population", ended the interview by
  "requesting a Christian teacher for his village"; by 1939 Anderson recalled that Sema chiefs
  "'have now become converted and most of them are very loyal and a good many of them spiritual
  leaders as well'" (p. 80, n. 71).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **A god who left when the British arrived.** Before 1889, "Lizaba was believed to manifest
  himself several times a year, visiting certain villages where he would bring portents of coming
  events such as smallpox, good harvests, war, etc. But since the advent of British rule, it was
  noted in 1925, '**Lizaba has not appeared. They say he has gone on a journey to the ends of the
  world**'" (pp. 76–77, citing Smith, *Ao Naga Tribe*, 79). The single most striking datum in the
  chunk: a withdrawal of a deity's appearances dated to a political event by the community itself.
- Mills on the *tsungrem*' ubiquity: "'They are everywhere—in the village, in the field, in the
  jungle, by streams, in trees, and, most favourite haunt of all, **in the huge boulders** which
  were so numerous in the Ao country'" (p. 73, n. 44).
- Mills on spirits taking local character, with a remedy in trade goods: a sick man told "that it
  is a *tsungrem* of the Pham country… which is holding his soul to ransom… will offer a little
  thread of the kind which the Pham buy keenly from the Aos" (p. 73, n. 47).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Three rival explanations of Naga conversion tested and rejected, each with its evidence.**
  (a) Missionary presence: "considering the **negative correlation** between the incidence of
  conversion and the presence of missionaries in either time or space… the distribution of foreign
  missionaries can hardly tell the whole story" (p. 70). (b) Material inducement: "Nor can one make
  a convincing case that Nagas were '**rice Christians**'—that is, that they converted in order to
  acquire some non-religious benefit"; footnote 33 disposes of the specific version of the claim —
  a British official credited the 1905 tax exemption for converts, but "potential converts would
  have had to weigh the benefits of nonpayment of several baskets of rice against the severe
  disapprobation of the other villagers on whom they depended for economic, political, and social
  support. Moreover, **the truly large conversion movement did not begin until four decades after
  that exemption was put in place**" (p. 70 and n. 33). (c) Relative deprivation: "one finds no
  correlation between conversion and the sort of severe decline in economic standards that might
  have placed the hill people in a state of 'relative deprivation'" (p. 70). And the general
  refusal: "it would be wrong to see Naga conversions simply as a function of social change or of
  'colonialism', for while all Nagas experienced the sort of disruptions mentioned above, **their
  responses to Christianity varied considerably from group to group**" (p. 71).
- **What the missionaries conceded and what they refused — an asymmetry Eaton makes central.**
  "For all their condemnation of Naga ritual and social life, **the missionaries were
  extraordinarily accommodating toward Naga doctrine and cosmology**, in which they and their Naga
  converts systematically sought points of entry for Christian terms and ideas… the terminological
  strategies adopted by foreign missionaries and their native assistants in the very earliest
  translations of scripture proved crucial for the success or failure of accommodating Christianity
  to indigenous religions" (pp. 73–74). Clark in 1881: "'The old religion of these people furnishes
  a splendid basis for Christianity… The fundamental ideas are there, perverted it is true, but
  there. And most of the needful terms are there'" (p. 74, n. 48).
- **The Ao mechanism: a generic word for "spirit" was universalised, and the lower tier was
  abolished.** Clark rendered both *kyrios* and *theos* by *tsungrem*, using neither Lungkijingba,
  nor Lizaba, nor Jehovah. Mills thought this risky, since "'tsungrem means a spirit attached to a
  specific place, of a character which at best is neutral and is always liable to be hostile'"
  (pp. 74–75). Eaton's rebuttal and reading: "a *tsungrem* was attached to a place only when a word
  designating that place was attached to the word; otherwise, it was a generic term… By using this
  term for God, then, the missionaries **effectively pulled together what the entire Ao pantheon
  shared in common—its 'spiritness,' or 'tsungrem-ness'—and endowed that notion with the power and
  universality of the Biblical supreme deity**", with orthographic reinforcement: "God was always
  capitalized as *Tsungrem* in Roman script, while the generic term god (as in Acts 14:11) was left
  in the lower case" (p. 75). Then: "the missionaries and their native assistants effectively
  replaced Lungkijingba, the high god who was only vaguely perceived, with a far more sharply
  defined universalization of a generic term. **And they simply liquidated the entire lower tier of
  lesser spirits.** These developments correspond precisely to **Max Weber's notion of religious
  rationalization**" (p. 75, quoting Weber's *Sociology of Religion* in n. 52: "who is deemed to
  exert the stronger influence on the individual in his everyday life, the theoretically supreme
  god or the lower spirits and demons?"). Eaton likens the move to Paul at Athens — "'the God whom
  I proclaim is in fact the one whom you already worship without knowing it'" (Acts 17:22–23,
  p. 75, n. 53).
- **The Nagas' own agency in translation, against the missionary-as-author picture.** "It was these
  native assistants who worked to assimilate Christian ideas, names, and terms into the Nagas' own
  linguistic, and hence religious, universe", and one missionary recalled that the Nagas "'rather
  resent the idea of introducing an outside word, as they are proud of their own language and think
  it is deep enough and that they can find the expressions necessary'" (p. 74). The later reversal
  failed: in **1945** Tanquist supervised a revision replacing *Tsungrem* with *Jihova*, but "'the
  people were not enthusiastic about the change, to say the least,' and they continued to use the
  Ao term *Tsungrem*" (p. 75, n. 54; his own hymnal had *Tsungrem* crossed out in pencil).
  Eaton's verdict: "**Tsungrem was not an alien deity imposed upon the Aos from the outside; it was
  their own generic term made universal by the Bible's message, and made permanent by the medium of
  print technology**" (p. 78).
- **Why the high tier grew: the experienced world grew.** "Clark and his assistants were
  elaborating the nature and power of an indigenous conception of a high god **at the very time**…
  when Ao Nagas had begun paying more attention to the upper tier of their religious cosmology…
  one of the intellectual consequences of the dramatic integration of Ao society with the outside
  world" (p. 76). The evidence he uses is a missionary anecdote of 1907 about a map from Boston,
  where an Ao deacon muttered "'Ish! Ish! how great we have thought ourselves, as though we were
  the big part of all creation'" — "**what yesterday had comprised the entire universe, bounded by
  a distant mountain range, was now but a speck on a vast globe**" (p. 76). Correspondingly the
  lesser spirits, "whose authority had been limited to the much smaller domain of everyday village
  life, now became otiose, or simply irrelevant" (p. 76).
- **Literacy as the source of a god's permanence — the argument that bears directly on shrine
  Islam.** "Lizaba may disappear beyond the mountains; forest spirits may rise or fall in their
  capacity to do harm depending on a people's ad hoc experiences with them; potent tsungrems of one
  generation may be ignored by the next. But Tsungrem, the one deity preached by the Christians,
  was seen as possessing a fixed and unalterable status in the cosmos. **His commands and promises,
  frozen in time by the power and the technology of the written word, could not easily be ignored
  or forgotten**" (pp. 77–78). He quotes J. D. Y. Peel on the Yoruba: in non-literate societies
  "'there is no sense of impersonal or universal orthodoxy of doctrine; legitimate belief is as a
  particular priest or elder expounds it. But where the essence of religion is the Word of God…
  religion acquires a rigid basis'" (p. 77). And Clark's own inference, from 1876: "'aboriginal
  tribes like the Nagas who are not Hindu, Mussalman or Buddhists constitute by far the most
  promising field of missionary labor in India'" — i.e. "**the corresponding imperviousness of
  Hindus, Muslims, and others already exposed to scriptural authority**" (p. 77 and n. 58). Note
  the pairing with ch. 10's question about how a religion "of the Book" reached illiterate
  villagers.
- **The literacy-religion fusion, socially located.** "Literate, young men who in an earlier
  generation would have been head-hunting warriors, were now becoming primary school teachers in
  villages distant from their own, where they would read to other villagers the unchangeable Word
  of God and display the life of Christ with color slides illuminated by lanterns"; one translator
  reported "'the few who can read will recite all the words to the many who are illiterate'"
  (p. 78). Who converted was generational and gendered: it "was these same 'younger warriors' who
  were responding most readily to Christian teachings as presented to them in village schools,
  **leaving village women as the ones most devoted to the local crop deities**" (p. 71) — compare
  ch. 8's claim about rural women as carriers of Islamic culture, which runs the other way.
- **The Sema mechanism: no transfer at all, only elaboration.** "the Christian God was not, as in
  the Ao case, translated by the generic word for 'spirit', but simply identified with Alhou. This
  translation strategy seems greatly to have facilitated the cognitive transfer from the old to the
  new; **or perhaps more accurately, it involved no real transfer at all, but only a refinement and
  elaboration of a thoroughly indigenous conception**… In this way Christianity, by identifying
  its God with Alhou, **rode the coattails of an indigenous movement** that, well before the advent
  of colonial rule, had acquired its own logic and its own momentum" (pp. 81–82).
- **Mobility as the variable behind the high god.** The Semas' clearly elaborated Alhou "conforms
  to Horton's intellectualist theory, which predicts that a community's wider social
  experience—which in the Semas' case was a function of a more migratory life—would dispose that
  community to paying greater attention to their high god"; and "Because of their migratory habits,
  the Semas had for long incorporated into their own religious cosmology the deities of those they
  conquered" (pp. 80–81). Politics is admitted alongside: colonial officials in the 1930s thought
  the movement "at bottom political, since dependent clients seeking to break away from allegiance
  to their weakened chiefs did so by breaking with their chiefs' religious ideology", and Eaton's
  own conclusion is dual — "the Semas' political system—stressed by the advent of British
  rule—drove **both chiefs and their villager-dependents to conversion, though for different
  reasons**" (p. 80).
- **The Angami counter-case: terracing, female deities, and resistance.** "the Angamis, alone among
  the Nagas, did not practice shifting (slash-and-burn) agriculture, but rather built elaborate
  systems of terracing and irrigation by which steep hills were transformed into flooded
  rice-fields. Tied thus to the land, the Angamis were the least migratory of all Nagas", whence
  two religious consequences: the chief agrarian deities were female, and "Angami religion, more
  than the religious systems of less sedentary Nagas, was characterized by **a close fit between
  their land and their fertility cult**. Since agrarian societies with such religious systems are
  notoriously slow in assimilating neighboring deities, the Angamis' ecological orientation may
  well have hindered their reception, or assimilation, of a Christian identity" (p. 82). Footnote 78
  supplies the etymological argument: "the word *pagan*… originally meant 'agrarian society' or
  'country-dweller,' pointing to the tendency of sedentary, agrarian societies to **resist
  religious innovation, at least relative to more mobile communities or classes**" (p. 82).
  Footnote 77 adds the colonial preference that made terracing attractive to administrators: it
  "endeared them to British administrators who, arriving with their experience in the Indian
  plains, had come to admire what they saw as the virtues of a fixed—and hence docile and
  taxable—peasantry" (p. 82).
- **A definitional gap between the converter's and the convert's account, with a measurable
  effect.** "missionaries and Nagas held rather different notions of what constituted conversion—a
  difference that is reflected in discrepancies in the data on the size of the Christian
  population. Owing to their strict criteria of what constituted a convert, **the missionaries'
  baptismal tabulations always lagged behind the much larger Christian 'community'**, which
  comprised those who, regardless of what the missionaries thought, considered themselves Christian
  and told the Census officials so" (p. 70). A caution for any archive that counts adherents from
  an institution's own register.

## Doubts

- **No archive row is touched by this chunk.**
- **A tension worth putting to a human before either claim is cited alongside the other.** Here
  Eaton argues that **sedentary agrarian** societies with land-bound fertility cults resist
  religious change (the Angami case, p. 82 and n. 78), while in chunk 003 he argues that in Punjab
  and Bengal the **adoption of sedentary agriculture** correlated with Islamization (p. 48). The
  two are reconcilable — the Punjab claim is about communities *in transition* to settled farming,
  not about long-settled ones — but the reconciliation is mine, not the book's, and the book does
  not cross-reference the two passages. **Record both; do not present the resolution as Eaton's.**
- Eaton himself hedges the Angami explanation twice in one paragraph ("may well have hindered",
  "seems to have retarded", p. 82) — weaker than his Ao and Sema claims, and should be quoted with
  the hedges intact.
- **Naga terms carry diacritics and spellings that the text layer may have mangled.** *arasentsuxs*
  (p. 72), *stsungrems* for *tsungrems* (p. 73), *Ai-tsung tsungrem* where the pattern suggests
  *ki-tsung* (p. 73, and "kimung" is legible in the same list), *assisimilation* (p. 82),
  "Otziitajung Ken" for the Ao hymnal title and "Siimi Bible Kaku Akitheu" for the Sema New
  Testament (pp. 75, 81) — the doubled vowels are almost certainly lost umlauts or macrons. **Do
  not quote a Naga word from this transcription without checking the PDF.**
- Footnote 32's citation is unreadable — "William C. Smith in Standaret, 31 December 1913, cited in
  Tanquist Papers, Msn 19 135-75" (p. 70): the periodical name and the manuscript year are both
  corrupt. The 1913 date and the quotation itself ("These people are very interesting, but that is
  not the main purpose of our being here") are legible.
- Minor text-layer damage: "watriors" (p. 71), "feacalecrop deities" for "the local crop deities"
  (p. 71 — the reading "local" is inferred from context and should be checked), "forgoteen" (p. 77),
  "adept" for "adopt" (p. 82, n. 78), "thie Angamis" (p. 82), "arrsid" for a word in "a larger
  [political?] and economic universe" (p. 78 — **unrecoverable**), "ae Sema Nagas" for "(Hutton,
  Sema Nagas" (p. 80, n. 68), "ayear" (p. 76), "1967); 95:" (p. 71, n. 37).
