## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- **Sind, conquered and occupied by Muslim navies in 711** — "the densely-populated
  Hindu–Buddhist society of Sind. Thus began the long and eventful encounter between Islamic and
  Indic civilizations" (p. 30). The same conquest reappears as the hinge of Watson's agricultural
  argument (p. 39).
- **The Ka'ba at Mecca**, described as a shrine and a pilgrimage centre: Mecca and Medina
  "remained the spiritual hubs of Islamic civilization, reinforced by the annual pilgrimage to
  the Ka'ba shrine" (p. 34). Before Muhammad's preaching, Allah "was already identified as the
  'Lord of the Ka'ba' (Qur'an 106:3), and hence the chief god of the pagan divinities whose idols
  were housed there" (p. 29, n. 6).
- **The Dome of the Rock, Jerusalem, built 691** — "Islam's earliest surviving monument", with
  Qur'an 4:170–1 inscribed around it, addressed, Eaton argues, to the Jews and Christians of
  Jerusalem and Palestine (p. 38).
- **The Iwan Kisra**, the Persian royal palace on the Tigris, "conveniently vacated by the last
  Sasanian emperor, Yazdegird III" (p. 35).
- **Baghdad, established 756**, grew to "about half a million, or ten times the size of nearby
  Ctesiphon, the former Sasanian capital" (p. 36).
- **Eleventh-century Toledo** "could absorb a community of 10,000 Jews without experiencing the
  sort of anti-Semitic hostility typical of Christian cities of late medieval Europe" (p. 36).
- **The battle of the Talas River, 751** — the Arab victory over T'ang forces, "one of the most
  important battles in the history of Central Asia", which Eaton says "probably determined the
  subsequent cultural evolution of the Turkish peoples of that region, who thereafter adopted
  Muslim and not Chinese civilization" (p. 30).
- Datable conquest sequence given: Iraq, Syria, Palestine, Egypt and western Iran 632–642;
  Cyprus 649, Carthage 698, Tunis 700, Gibraltar 711, Spain 711–16, raids into southern France
  720; Balkh and Bukhara among the Central Asian oasis towns taken by the 650s–8th c. (p. 30).
  Umayyads at Damascus 661–750; Abbasids at Baghdad until 1258 (p. 34). Caliph Uthman assassinated
  656 (p. 34). Muhammad died 632 (p. 33).

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The institutional inheritance Muslim rulers took over from Persia and Rome, itemised** —
  "notions of absolute kingship, courtly rituals and styles, an efficient bureaucratic
  administration, a functioning mint and coinage system, a standing army, a postal service, and
  **the kind of land revenue system on which the political economies of all great empires of the
  Fertile Crescent had rested**" (pp. 34–35). The land-revenue point is the one that recurs in the
  Punjab essays.
- **State patronage of religion as a continuity, not an innovation**: "The caliphs also carried
  over the Sasanian practice of patronizing a state religion, substituting Islam for
  Zoroastrianism. They appointed *qadis*, or Muslim judges, and **promoted the construction of
  mosques**, just as the Persian Shahs had appointed Zoroastrian priests and built fire temples"
  (p. 35).
- **The protection-for-loyalty-and-taxes arrangement**, described as inherited from Sasanian
  minority policy: cities divided "functionally autonomous communities into separate quarters",
  and "the Islamic ruler extended to the communities recognition, tolerance, and protection in
  return for political loyalty and taxes" (p. 36).
- **The Kharijite revolt as an economic event**: it "was spearheaded in conquered Iraq by men of
  piety whose military stipends had just been reduced", and its leaders justified the killing of
  Uthman in 656 "by emphasizing the radical egalitarianism, including social equality for women,
  that had been preached by the Prophet" (pp. 33–34).
- **Land tenure and taxation as instruments of agrarian expansion**: irrigation innovations
  "in addition to systems of land tenure and taxation that encouraged land reclamation and a more
  intensified use of older fields, contributed to a significant increase in food production in
  the eighth to eleventh centuries, making possible the population increases and urbanization so
  characteristic of Muslim societies in this period" (p. 40).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Conversion is eased by prior affinity, not compelled by conquest — the argument Eaton will
  carry into India.** Summarising Michael Morony on Iraq: "non-Muslims found it easier to accept
  Islam when ideas, attitudes, or institutions already present in their own cultures shared
  affinities with those imported from Arabia", with a list of shared practices — animal sacrifice
  with pagans and Zoroastrians, ritual slaughter with Jews, circumcision with Jews and Christians,
  institutionalised charity, head-covering, a month-long fast followed by a festival, ritual
  ablutions, a ritual prayer resembling the Nestorians' (pp. 36–37). His conclusion: such studies
  "show a distinct shift away from earlier and cruder models of religious conversion, which, in
  the tradition of William Muir, tended to conflate the conquests and the conversion of
  non-Muslims into a single process, **thereby reducing Islam to a 'religion of the sword'**"
  (p. 37).
- **Conversion made measurable.** Richard Bulliet's *Conversion to Islam in the Medieval Period:
  An Essay in Quantitative History* is praised as marking "the entry of new social science
  techniques into a field that had formerly been the exclusive preserve of classical, textual
  scholarship"; his method was "to plot the pace and direction of conversion by tabulating the
  patterns of change in **personal names recorded in biographical dictionaries**" (p. 36). A named
  method for dating conversion from onomastic evidence.
- **Islam became a world civilization by a deliberate choice, datable to the eighth century.**
  Muslims faced "a critical choice: Either they could constitute themselves as one more autonomous
  community modeled on those they ruled—thereby preserving Allah as an Arab deity, Islam as an
  Arab cult, and Arabic as the language of the ruling class—or they could try to bring all these
  diverse communities and traditions together into a new cultural synthesis." They took the first
  course at first, then by the eighth century the second, "a move that may have been decided as
  much on practical as on religious grounds", the caliphs being "convinced of the political
  imprudence of a tiny ethnic minority ruling indefinitely over an enormous non-Muslim majority"
  and so "openly encourag[ing] their non-Arab subjects to convert" (p. 37). "**Because Muslims
  chose this second option, Islam became a world civilization, and not just one more parochial,
  ethnic cult**" (pp. 37–38).
- **The conquests re-explained as planned policy, and the older explanations dismissed one by
  one.** Eaton rejects: the traditionalist Muslim account of divine favour, which "tells us more
  of Islamic theology than of Islamic history" (p. 31); Sir William Muir's 1898 account, quoted
  at length and likened to "the screenplay for a Cecil B. De Mille film", which "leaves us with
  little of substance, apart from simply asserting the Arabs' fondness for the 'scent of war'"
  and reflected "colonial fears that Europe's own Muslim subjects might… rise up in revolt"
  (pp. 31–32; Muir was "himself a senior British official in India as well as an aggressive
  activist for the Christian mission"); the **desiccation thesis** and its poverty/overpopulation
  variants, "lacking firm evidence" (pp. 31–32); and the "exhausted empires" thesis, which
  "likewise lacked empirical evidence, and, above all, failed to account for the Arabs' continued
  expansion into lands far beyond the domain of either empire" (p. 32). In their place: "recent
  research has revealed **methodically planned and well-executed military manoeuvres, directed by
  a central command in Medina** and undertaken for quite rational purposes" — an economic need
  for material support and "the political need to contain and channel the tremendous energies
  released by the Prophet's socio-religious revolution", the whole compared to "the French or
  Russian revolutions" (pp. 32–33).
- **Religion restored as a cause rather than a decoration.** "Western historians of the
  nineteenth and early twentieth centuries displayed a chronic inability to accept the possibility
  that the religion itself could have played a fundamental, as opposed to supportive, role in the
  movement" (p. 32). His own account gives the community's "social fragility" and the succession
  crisis of 632 causal weight: Abu Bakr forbade any tribe to leave the umma and declared Muhammad
  the last prophet, which "amounted, in effect, to a declaration of war against those tribes who
  had abandoned the umma", so that "**the initial burst of Muslim expansion after the Prophet's
  death was directed not against non-Muslims, but against just such Arab tribes within the
  peninsula**" (p. 33). And: the distribution of conquered riches, "which looked to the rest of the
  world like senseless plunder, served to actualize the ideal, preached by the Prophet, of
  attaining socio-economic equality among all believers" (p. 33).
- **Continuity over rupture.** Earlier historians, "writing under the spell of Arabic narratives
  that dwelt on the swiftness and thoroughness of the conquests, emphasized the sense of
  discontinuity"; recent work "tended to see more continuity", and "recent research suggests that
  the Arabs' rapid transition from a life of desert nomadism to one of imperial rule resulted
  largely **from the expectations of their non-Muslim subjects**" — Egypt's first Arab governor
  ratified Church patriarchs' appointments as Byzantine governors had; in Iraq the Arabs
  adjudicated Nestorian disputes "at the insistence of the Nestorians themselves"; and for fifty
  years they minted Sasanian-style coins with the Shah's portrait (p. 35).
- **Non-Arabic sources as the field's methodological revolution**, with the controversy that
  followed. Scholars using Greek, Hebrew, Syriac, Coptic and Armenian materials "have reached
  extremely controversial conclusions—for example, that the earliest Muslims considered themselves
  descendants of Abraham through Hagar and Ishmael, that the movement originated in northern
  Arabia and not Mecca, or that Palestine and not Medina was the movement's principal focus"
  (p. 28). The Arabic sources, Crone and Cook object, "did not begin to appear until several
  centuries after the death of Muhammad", are "far from contemporary", and "on crucial issues…
  are ambiguous or even self-contradictory" (p. 28). Eaton's summary of the division: "the
  important division among Western historians is between those whose work is confined to the
  traditional Arabic sources, and those who have begun tapping into the contemporary non-Muslim
  sources" (p. 29). His simile for the change: like WWII historians who had used only German
  sources "suddenly discover[ing] the mountains of wartime sources written in English, Russian,
  Japanese, and French" (p. 28).
- **Paper as a cause of Indo-Muslim state formation.** Papermaking was learned from Chinese
  prisoners after the mid-eighth-century victory; Baghdad had a paper mill by the end of the
  eighth century, Egypt by 900, Morocco and Spain by the twelfth; "Turks introduced it in north
  India in the thirteenth century", replacing the palm leaf. "since paper is the bureaucrat's
  stock-in-trade, papermaking technology greatly contributed to the expansion and consolidation
  of **Indo-Muslim bureaucratic states from the fifteenth century onward**", and, because the
  Qur'an is held to be the actual Word of God, its diffusion "contributed to the growth of the
  religion as well" (pp. 38–39).
- **Andrew Watson's agrarian thesis, with the Sind conquest as its hinge.** Watson "has recently
  laid to rest the myths that the Arabs, because of their desert, pastoral background, were
  somehow disinclined to agrarian life and that early Islamic times had witnessed a decline in
  agriculture", showing instead that between the eighth and thirteenth centuries Muslims "actively
  promoted such innovations and vastly expanded agricultural production everywhere they went"
  (p. 39). "A key event in Watson's analysis was the Arab conquest of Sind in 711, which
  established a direct and regular contact between India and the Fertile Crescent… effectively
  incorporating all these regions for the first time into a single agricultural universe" (p. 39).
  The crops carried west from India between the eighth and tenth centuries are listed: hard wheat,
  rice, sugar-cane, new sorghums; banana, sour orange, lemon, lime, mango, watermelon, coconut
  palm; spinach, artichoke, eggplant; and cotton, "whose diffusion in Africa directly paralleled
  the spread of Islam itself" (pp. 39–40). Because the Indian crops were **summer** crops in a
  region of winter crops, they added "in effect, another growing season for each calendar year"
  (p. 40).
- **The volume's largest historical claim**: "the most significant theme of early Islam is the
  evolution of a relatively parochial Arab cult into a world civilization, indeed history's first
  truly global civilization", the Arab conquests inaugurating "a thousand-year era, lasting from
  the seventh to the seventeenth century, when all the major civilizations of the Old World…
  were for the first time brought into contact by, and within, a single overarching civilization"
  (p. 34).
