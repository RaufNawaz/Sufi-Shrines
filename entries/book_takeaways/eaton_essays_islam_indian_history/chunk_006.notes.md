# eaton_essays_islam_indian_history — chunk 006 — pp. 83–97

The Angami case and the conclusion of Chapter Two (pp. 83–87), then most of Chapter Three,
"Multiple Lenses: Differing Perspectives of Fifteenth-Century Calicut" (pp. 88–97). **No archive
row appears.** Two things here are worth carrying forward. First, the Angami conclusion completes
Eaton's argument that **the naming of the divine decides whether a religion is assimilable** —
the Angami translators went from a foreign name to a fertility goddess and back to the foreign
name, and the movement stalled. Second, the Calicut essay is a **method essay about observer
bias**, built from four eyewitness accounts of one city, and its lesson is directly applicable to
a shrine archive built from travellers', officials' and devotees' testimony: each account is
shaped by the observer's own categories, and the place "virtually dissolves". Along the way it
records mosque counts, a qadi and a holy man at Calicut, pilgrim-merchants, and a Hindu king's
daily temple purification seen through Chinese Buddhist eyes.

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- **Calicut**, Malabar coast: "in the fifteenth century India's busiest port", "even larger than
  Lisbon" on contemporary Portuguese report, "an independent city-state under the control of a
  Hindu king known as the **Samudri Raja**" (pp. 90–91). Its pepper "was judged the finest in the
  world"; "In Vasco da Gama's day a hundredweight of pepper sold in Calicut for only three ducats
  but cost eighty in Venice, a staggering markup of more than 2,500 per cent", and Hangchow was
  consuming Calicut-area pepper "at a rate of nearly a thousand pounds a day in the thirteenth
  century" (p. 91). It was also the transshipment point between the ocean's western and eastern
  halves, since "East Asia and West Asia were too distant for ships to transport goods from one
  end of the ocean to the other in a single monsoon season" (p. 91). Nikitin listed its exports as
  "pepper, nutmeg, cloves, cinnamon, and ginger" (p. 97).
- **Calicut's mosques and Muslim officers.** Ma Huan "observed that most of Calicut's population
  was Muslim and that the city had **between twenty and thirty mosques**" (p. 93); Abdul Razzaq
  "noted the presence of **congregational mosques, a Muslim judge, and a Muslim holy man**", and
  remarked on the "'considerable number'" of permanently resident Muslims (p. 95). The raja
  "arranged that the Muslims would have large mosques in which to worship, and that **holidays be
  celebrated according to proper Islamic law**" (p. 92).
- **Cheng Ho's voyages**: between **December 1406 and April 1407** his fleet of "**317 ships and
  27,870 men**" lay in Calicut harbour while the chief officer went ashore "to proclaim the
  emperor's will, to bestow 'honours' on the Samudri Raja and his chiefs, and… to receive the
  latter's formal submission"; two years later he returned "to officiate at the installation of
  Calicut's new raja" and **erected a commemorative stone plaque** whose text is quoted — "'Though
  the journey from this country to the Central Country is more than a hundred thousand li, yet the
  people are very similar, happy and prosperous, with identical customs. We have here engraved a
  stone, a perpetual declaration for ten thousand ages'" (pp. 92–93).
- **Ma Huan**, "a Chinese Muslim employed as an interpreter on the expeditions of **1413, 1421,
  and 1431**", author of the *Ying-yai Sheng-lan* (1433) (pp. 93, and n. 7 at p. 92).
- **Abdul Razzaq**, "a Muslim Turk from Herat", ambassador of the khan of Herat to Vijayanagara,
  who reached Calicut from the Persian Gulf in **1442** (p. 94).
- **Afanasy Nikitin**, "a petty merchant from Tver", who left Russia in **1466** and reached
  Calicut "probably in 1470" (pp. 96–97).
- **Vasco da Gama** reached India by sea in **1498**; Columbus's voyage 1492; *India Recognita*
  published 1492 from the travels of Nicolo Conti; More's *Utopia* 1517, in which Calicut is "the
  only real non-European city mentioned" and the port from which Raphael Nonsenso embarks
  (pp. 88–90).
- **Ukepenopfu**, Angami supreme goddess, "and ancestress of the human race" — in Angami folklore
  "Ukepenopfu's husband had wandered off to the Indian plains, which explains why a 'father-figure'
  was absent from the Angami cosmology" (p. 83). Hutton noticed in 1921 that she was "'apparently
  at present undergoing a process of change from female to male'" (p. 83). **Terhoma**, the Angami
  generic term for spirit, was "demoted to stand for 'Satan'", the missionaries teaching "'the
  Angami convert to regard all terhoma as evil'" (p. 84).

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **A Hindu king's daily temple service, described by a Chinese Muslim who took it for Buddhism.**
  Ma Huan on the Samudri Raja: "'Every day at dawn the king goes to (the well), draws water, and
  washes (the image of) Buddha; after worshiping, he orders men to collect the pure dung of yellow
  oxen; this is stirred with water in a brass basin (until it is) like paste; (then) it is smeared
  all over the surface of the ground and walls inside the temple'" — which Eaton calls "**a nearly
  perfect description of the purification ceremony performed for a state deity by a traditional
  Hindu king**" (p. 93). A rare outsider's eyewitness account of fifteenth-century temple practice,
  and a case study in how a misapplied category preserves accurate detail.
- **Pilgrim-merchants: the hajj and trade in one career.** The foreign enclaves at Calicut were
  "Gujaratis, Bengalis, Malays, Chinese, Persians, and Arabs, though most tended to share a common
  religious identity as Muslims. **With Mecca located only a few weeks' voyage across the western
  Indian Ocean, many were pilgrims even while they were merchants, peddling goods around the ocean
  en route to Islam's holiest shrine**" (p. 91). Razzaq notes ships arriving "'from the shores of
  the House of God [Mecca] and other parts of the Hedjaz'" (p. 94).
- **The revenue base of a port city**: "The city derived fabulous profits from the sale of locally
  produced pepper, as well as from **warehouse and docking fees charged to international
  shippers**"; and because merchants waited months for the monsoon to turn, they "tended to settle
  in semipermanent communities" (p. 91).
- **A mercantile code enforced by handclasp, and a guarantee of safe harbour.** Ma Huan on the
  broker's formula: "'whether (the price) be dear or cheap, we will never repudiate it or change
  it'… 'there is not the slightest deviation'" (p. 94). Razzaq: "'every ship, whatever place it
  may come from, or wherever it may be bound, when it puts into this port is treated like other
  vessels, and has no trouble of any kind to put up with'", against other ports where "merchant
  vessels might be seized and plundered by the local inhabitants" (p. 95).
- **The one religious law with state force at Calicut**: Razzaq "knew that the Samudri Raja was a
  tolerant prince, and he noted that with only one exception Hindu law was not given the weight of
  state sanction. **The only things absolutely forbidden in Calicut… were cow slaughter and beef
  eating, both of which was punishable by death**" (p. 95).
- **Matrilineal royal succession at Calicut**, remarked on by both Ma Huan and Razzaq: "This
  apparently struck both men as odd, for in the Islamic world, as in China, royal descent was
  invariably patrilineal" (p. 96).
- **A darbar described by a dissatisfied ambassador.** "The king's audience hall was filled with
  two or three thousand Hindus, bare from the waist up and armed with daggers and shields, and
  **around the raja stood the city's most important Muslim leaders**"; Razzaq thought the raja "'a
  man with his body naked, like the rest of the Hindus'", and had been given short shrift because
  his gifts — "a horse, a fur-lined cloak, a gilded garment, and a cap for the celebration of the
  Persian New Year" — "were insufficient, or inappropriate, or both" (pp. 95–96).
- **Angami conversion counts**: "As late as 1906, after twenty-six years of proselytization by
  several American missionaries, **only 35 baptized Angami converts appeared in Mission records**"
  (p. 83), Rivenburg having left after "five years in Kohima without having made a single Angami
  convert" (p. 83).
- **Table 2.4, "Correlates of Conversion among the Nagas"** (p. 85) — seven variables scored for
  Ao, Sema and Angami: conquest by an outside power patronizing a new cult; integration into a
  broader economic or political order; extent of pre-contact literacy; **degree of sedentarism**;
  **degree of attention paid to the pre-contact high god**; degree of perceived break with the
  pre-contact cosmology; degree of proselytization. The plus/minus values are largely corrupt in
  the text layer — see Doubts — but the prose gives the readings that matter.

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The Angami failure completes the translation argument.** "Whereas *Tsungrem* represented an
  inflation of the Aos' generic term for 'spirit' into an all-embracing supreme god, and whereas
  *Alhou* represented an existing supreme god whose power became enhanced through identification
  with the Biblical deity, the translation of scriptural names into Angami proved far less
  successful" — Jehovah in 1890 for both the supreme deity and the generic term; Ukepenopfu from
  1918, until Tanquist realised "'pfu' was a grammatically feminine ending"; then Jehovah again.
  "**the strategy of identifying the Christian high god first with a foreign term, then with a
  fertility goddess, and finally with a foreign term again, appears to have worked against the easy
  assimilation of Christian ideas into the Angami religious system**" (pp. 83–84).
- **But the indigenous category won anyway, and that is Eaton's point.** "Nonetheless, Angami
  religious categories, like Ao and Sema categories, ultimately prevailed. In **1983** Christian
  Angamis, still a minority among the Angami population, persisted in identifying the Christian
  high god with their own, traditional supreme goddess" (p. 84), an Angami Christian being quoted:
  "'Once thought to be far away, Ukepenuopfu is brought nearer through the gospel'" (p. 84, n. 86).
- **The conclusion, stated as a finding about variables.** Three uniformities across the groups
  (outside conquest patronising the new religion; integration into a wider order; pre-contact
  non-literacy), then the differences explained by Horton. "So long as the social experience of the
  Aos and the Angamis—but not the Semas—remained confined to their immediate locale, the lesser
  beings of their cosmology, identified as they were with that locale, **retained their capacity to
  explain, predict and control** the Nagas' experience of reality", so that "the early missionaries
  had been elaborating precisely that part of the Ao and Angami religious cosmologies, the upper
  tier, **with which villagers had been least concerned**. This would explain the Christian
  movement's slow start among those groups" (pp. 84–85). The timing is dated: the opening-up "did
  not really get under way until World War I, accelerating with and beyond World War II" (p. 85).
- **Proselytization eliminated as a variable, in as many words.** "there is **no positive
  correlation between the degree of proselytization and the rate of conversion** among the various
  Naga groups… Since the Semas converted to Christianity with little or no direct missionary
  activity, we can virtually eliminate the degree of exposure to missionary preaching as a
  significant variable in the conversion process. **What mattered, in the last analysis, was the
  nature of Naga cosmologies and the things that were happening within those cosmologies at the time
  of contact with outsiders, together with the Nagas' experience of a wider world.** If we are to
  explain religious change in colonial encounters, this, I believe, is where we must focus our
  attention, and not on some notion of a monolithic 'colonial discourse'" (p. 86).
- **Religion as political resistance is admitted but refused as sufficient.** The Nagas' Christian
  identity "has been of great use in their ongoing resistance to political assimilation with the
  republic of India. This highly-charged political context has doubtless helped consolidate a
  Christian identity among the Nagas since 1947. **But it would be wrong to see that identity as
  simply a foil for political resistance**" (p. 86). The essay then closes on an anonymous Naga's
  statement, which Eaton reads as historical consciousness rather than mere assertion: "'Europeans
  do not have a monopoly on Christianity. When Europeans became Christians they made it a European
  indigenous religion. Now I, like many Nagas, am a Christian, but I am not a European. Now my God
  can speak to me through my dreams, just as happened to my Angami ancestors… What I am talking
  about is Naga Christianity—an indigenous Naga Christianity'" (p. 86). "He is aware that
  Christianity was at one time as alien to Europeans as it was to his own ancestors and
  that—through a process that we may call '**creative adaptation**'—he and his recent ancestors had
  self-assertively made it an indigenous Naga religion" (p. 87). **The strongest available
  statement of Eaton's position that an indigenised religion is not a diluted one** — directly
  applicable to arguments about "syncretism" at South Asian shrines.
- **The Calicut essay's thesis: the place dissolves into its observers.** "Like one of Italo
  Calvino's 'invisible cities', the form and texture of fifteenth-century Calicut resolved into
  ever-changing images depending upon who was doing the observing, and **through what cultural
  lenses they did so**. The Chinese saw the city through Chinese lenses, Arabs through Arab lenses,
  and so forth. It is the classic Indian fable of the 'Elephant and the Five Blind Men'" (pp. 90–91).
- **Worked examples of the thesis, each with its mechanism named.** (a) Cheng Ho's plaque claims
  "identical customs" because "since the people of Calicut were tributary vassals of the Ming
  empire, it seems to have been **ideologically necessary** that they be understood as happy and
  prosperous, and 'civilized', which to the Chinese meant that they must in some measure be
  Chinese" (p. 93). (b) Ma Huan reads a Hindu king as a Buddhist prince: "Never having seen Hindus
  or Hindu society before, Ma Huan **had no intellectual category for them**… as the idea of a
  Buddhist prince would have been familiar to the Chinese, that is what they saw in Calicut"
  (p. 93). (c) Razzaq's "hostile shore" inhabited by "Infidels" "reflect[s] the **binary ideology
  of classical Islam** (and, for that matter, of medieval Christianity), which divided the world
  sharply between believers and unbelievers", and his vantage point "was clearly that of the
  northwestern Arabian Sea; he viewed Calicut as though he were standing in Mecca or Hormuz"
  (p. 95); his disdain for the raja's undress is explained by his own Herat expectations, and by
  the climate (p. 96). (d) Nikitin sees the marginal city because he was marginal in it: "Nikitin
  was a vagabond in Calicut, living on a hand-to-mouth basis… he does not mention a meeting with
  the Samudri Raja or even an awareness of his existence. Rather, **he moved in Calicut's
  marginalized circles, socially far removed from the king**" (p. 97).
- **Practice contradicting ideology, in one sentence.** "although the Chinese came to India
  imagining themselves to be the rulers of client subjects, **in practice they behaved like
  Calicut's other resident communities, demonstrating an unerring respect for the city's customs
  and local business practices**"; and the contrast with Razzaq — the Ming envoys' "behaviour was
  marked by a decorum and deference that indicated otherwise, betraying their status as newcomers
  to the Indian Ocean", while Razzaq's Muslim confidence shows in "his irritation with having to
  live among infidels in a city he called 'this disagreeable place'" (pp. 94, 96).
- **A religious identity coming apart under isolation — the inverse of conversion.** Nikitin, "**a
  nearly pathological sense of anomie**": "'I have nothing with me; no books whatever; those that I
  had taken from Russia were lost when I was robbed. **And I forgot the Christian faith and the
  Christian festivals, and know not Easter nor Christmas, nor can I tell Wednesday from Friday, and
  I am between the two faiths**'" (p. 97). Read against the literacy argument of chunk 005: a
  religion of the book loses its grip when the books are gone, and Eaton makes the loss of the
  books explicit in the quotation he chose.
- **Europe as the underdeveloped party, stated flatly.** "Europe possessed nothing that India
  wanted in exchange for her textiles and spices—nothing, that is, except gold and silver, for
  which Europeans were more than ready to plunder western Africa and the 'New World'. In fact,
  Europe, with its smaller population base, its less sophisticated administrative structure, and
  its more primitive manufacturing and agricultural technology, **would have to be considered an
  underdeveloped region compared to China, India, and the Islamic world**" (p. 89). India "had for
  centuries been the world's 'honey jar'" (p. 88), and its textile trade survives in "pajama,
  shawl, bandana, dungarees, chintz, calico, seersucker, khaki" (p. 89).
- **Da Gama's arrival was a non-event in India, and the silence is the evidence.** "for most
  Indians… the arrival of the Europeans was hardly a significant event. Indeed, India's larger
  states hardly noticed the coming of the mariners from the west. Both the empire of Vijayanagara
  and the Delhi sultanate, the largest states in those days, were based on land not sea power…
  **Not surprisingly, there are no contemporary accounts answering the question of how Indians
  responded to the arrival of Vasco da Gama**" (p. 90).
- The Watson agrarian-diffusion argument of chunk 002 is **repeated almost verbatim** here
  (pp. 88–89), this time with the reference: Andrew Watson, *Agricultural Innovation in the Early
  Islamic World: The Diffusion of Crops and Farming Techniques, 700–1100* (Cambridge, 1983)
  (p. 89, n. 2). Useful for citing the claim.

## Doubts

- **No archive row is touched by this chunk.**
- **Table 2.4's scores are largely unreadable** (p. 85). The Ao row prints "+ : 2" across three
  columns that should be Ao/Sema/Angami; row 2 begins "." rather than a sign; rows 3–6 give tildes
  ("~") where a plus or minus is meant; the row labels themselves are intact. **Do not quote any
  cell of this table.** The prose at pp. 85–86 states the readings that matter (Semas most
  migratory and most attentive to the high god; Aos and Angamis heavily proselytised and Semas
  not; all three pre-literate) and should be used instead.
- **The goddess's name is spelt two ways within three pages** — *Ukepenopfu* in the text (p. 83)
  and *Ukepenuopfu* in the Angami Christian's quotation at p. 84, n. 86 (where the text layer
  further gives "Ukepenuopfii"). Hutton's form is not separately legible. Check the PDF before
  printing the name.

  Rivenburg" at p. 83 against "Sidney Rivenburg" and "S. W. Rivenburg" in chunk 004 (p. 67) and in
  n. 79 here (p. 83). **One of the two given names is wrong in the book or in the transcription.**

  bole | shore" (p. 94). The reading "situated on a hostile shore" is secure from the following
