# eaton_essays_islam_indian_history — chunk 008 — pp. 111–124

Chapter Four's core: "Sufism and State-Building" (pp. 112–116) and "Temple Desecration and State
Building" (pp. 116–124). **The richest chunk so far for the archive**, on two counts.

First, **the Chishti order's partnership with Indo-Muslim states is set out with dates, deaths
and named shrines** — Mu'in al-Din Chishti (d. 1236) at Ajmer, Nizam al-Din Auliya (d. 1325) at
Delhi, Bakhtiyar Kaki (d. 1235), Nasir al-Din Mahmud (d. 1356), Burhan al-Din Gharib (d. 1337),
Zain al-Din Shirazi (d. 1369), 'Ala al-Haq (d. 1398) and Nur Qutb-i 'Alam in Bengal, Salim
Chishti at Fatehpur Sikri. This is the order Baba Farid's shrine belongs to, and Eaton's
explanation of *why* rulers endowed Chishti shrines is the same argument he will apply to
Pakpattan. Footnote 25 also gives **a detailed description of the Ajmer urs protocol**, with the
diwan, khadims, wakil, mutawalli and hereditarily employed *fatiha khwans* — usable comparative
material on shrine offices.

Second, **the desecration count**: relying only on contemporary or near-contemporary epigraphic
and literary evidence across 1192–1729, Eaton identifies **eighty instances whose historicity
"appears reasonably certain"**, against the **60,000** claimed by some Hindu nationalists. And he
shows that image-seizure and royal-temple destruction were **Indian political practice from about
the sixth century**, with a dozen pre-Turkish examples named.

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- **Shaikh Mu'in al-Din Chishti (d. 1236)**, "the founder of the Chishti order of Sufis in India",
  tomb at **Ajmer**, which Eaton calls "the '**mother-shrine**' of the Chishti order in India" and
  "the foundational Chishti shrine" (pp. 112, 114, 115). Ajmer was "the former capital of the
  defeated Cahamana Rajputs—also, significantly, the wellspring of Chishti piety" (p. 120).
  Jahan Ara, Shah Jahan's daughter, "even wrote a biography of Mu'in al-Din Chishti" (p. 115).
- **Shaikh Nizam al-Din Auliya (d. 1325)**, "Delhi's most renowned Sufi shaikh" (pp. 113–114). His
  tomb was rebuilt by Babur's brother-in-law; Akbar built Humayun's tomb near it in 1571; 'Alamgir
  II "repaired and made additions" to it (p. 115).
- **Bakhtiyar Kaki (d. 1235)**, "India's second great Chishti shaikh", at whose shrine Babur
  prayed on entering Delhi in 1526 and beside which **Bahadur Shah II (deposed 1858)** built his
  own mansion — "Bringing the pattern to a full circle… the very site where Babur had prayed more
  than three centuries earlier" (p. 115).
- **Shaikh Nasir al-Din Mahmud (d. 1356)**, "Nizam al-Din Auliya's most eminent disciple to have
  remained in Delhi", over whose grave **Sultan Firuz Shah Tughluq (r. 1351–88) raised a
  magnificent tomb** (p. 114).
- **Burhan al-Din Gharib (d. 1337)**, one of Nizam al-Din's "leading spiritual successors", whose
  arrival in the Deccan 'Isami "considered… was the cause of that region's flourishing state at
  mid-century" (p. 113). His leading disciple **Zain al-Din Shirazi (d. 1369)** received the
  Prophet's mantle (*khirqa*) in 'Isami's narrative (pp. 113–114).
- **Shaikh 'Ala al-Haq (d. 1398)** of Bengal, to whom "The earliest-known monument built by the
  founder of Bengal's Ilyas Shahi dynasty (1342–1486) was a mosque dedicated in **1342**"; his
  master was, like Zain al-Din's, a disciple of Nizam al-Din Auliya. **"Down to the year 1532,
  fully fourteen successive sultans of Bengal enlisted themselves as disciples of the descendants
  of this shaikh, while the tomb-shrine of 'Ala al-Haq's own son and successor, Nur Qutb-i 'Alam,
  became in effect a state shrine to which subsequent sultans made annual pilgrimages"** (p. 114,
  citing Eaton, *The Rise of Islam and the Bengal Frontier, 1206–1760*, 1993, pp. 86, 91). **A
  fully documented case of a shrine lineage as the state's hereditary pir — the closest structural
  analogue in the book to the Pakpattan sajjada nashin's relation to power.**
- **Salim Chishti**, at whose "hospice-site" Akbar began building Fatehpur Sikri in 1571, "the
  shaikh who had predicted the birth of the emperor's son"; and who "Towards the end of his life…
  tied his turban on the head of that son, the future Jahangir, and pronounced him his **spiritual
  successor**" (p. 115). A succession act by turban-tying, imperial rather than familial.
- **Chishti tomb-sites at Gulbarga and Khuldabad** in the Deccan, which Aurangzeb "visited and
  made sizeable contributions to… in addition to sites in Ajmer and Delhi" (p. 115).
- **Sabuktigin (r. 977–97) and Mahmud of Ghazni (r. 998–1030)**: their raids "appear to have been
  undertaken for material reasons" (p. 111). Figures given: Mahmud's sack of **Ray in 1029**
  brought "500,000 dinars' worth of jewels, 260,000 dinars in coined money, and over 30,000 dinars'
  worth of gold and silver vessels", while "**Somnath alone brought in twenty million dinars' worth
  of spoil**" (p. 111, n. 11, citing Bosworth). **The first Turkish attack on Benares, 1033**, by
  the Ghaznavid **governor of Lahore**, is quoted from Baihaqi: "'He marched out with his warriors
  and the army of Lahore, and exacted ample tribute from the Thakurs… The markets of the drapers,
  perfumers, and jewellers, were plundered, but it was impossible to do more'" (p. 111, n. 12).
- **The Delhi Sultanate (1206–1526)**, which "signalled the first attempt to build an indigenous
  Muslim state and society in north India" (p. 112); the **Bahmani Revolution of 1347** and Sultan
  Hasan Bahman Shah (r. 1347–58) (pp. 113–114); Muzaffar Khan of Gujarat declaring independence in
  **1396** immediately after visiting Ajmer; Dilawar Khan of Malwa in **1404** styling himself
  "'the disciple of the head of the holy order of Nasir Din Mahmud'" (p. 114).
- **Pre-Turkish desecrations and image seizures, named and dated** (pp. 118–119, drawn from Richard
  H. Davis, *Lives of Indian Images*, 1997, 51–83): **AD 642**, the Pallava Narasimhavarman I
  looted a Ganesha image from the Chalukyan capital of Vatapi; fifty years later the Chalukyas
  brought back apparent Ganga and Yamuna images from north India; in the eighth century Bengali
  troops destroyed "what they thought was the image of Vishnu Vaikuntha, the state deity of
  Lalitaditya's kingdom in Kashmir"; early ninth century, the Rashtrakuta Govinda III occupied
  Kanchipuram and received Sinhala state images, installing them "in a Saiva temple in his
  capital"; the Pandyan Srimara Srivallabha took "a golden Buddha image—'a synecdoche for the
  integrity of the Sinhalese polity itself'—that had been installed in the kingdom's Jewel Palace";
  early tenth century, the Pratihara Herambapala seized "a solid gold image of Vishnu Vaikuntha
  when he defeated the **Sahi king of Kangra**", which the Candella Yasovarman then seized and
  installed "in the Lakshmana temple of **Khajuraho**"; early eleventh century, the Chola Rajendra I
  furnished his capital with Durga and Ganesha from the Chalukyas, "Bhairava, Bhairavi, and Kali
  images from the Kalingas of Orissa", a Nandi from the Eastern Chalukyas and "a bronze Siva image
  from the Palas of Bengal"; mid-eleventh century, Rajadhiraja plundered Kalyani and took "a large
  black stone door guardian to his capital in Thanjavur, where it was displayed to his subjects as
  a trophy of war". Also the Rashtrakuta monarch (Indra III) who "not only destroyed the temple of
  **Kalapriya** (at Kalpa near the Jamuna River), patronized by the Rashtrakutas' deadly enemies
  the Pratiharas, but… took special delight in recording the fact" (p. 119). And post-conquest
  Hindu cases: **Kapilendra** of the Suryavamshi Gajapatis sacking Saiva and Vaishnava temples in
  the Kaveri delta in the 1460s; **Krishna Deva Raya** looting a Bala Krishna image from Udayagiri
  in **1514** and the Vittala image from **Pandharpur** six years later, "with the apparent purpose
  of ritually incorporating this area into his kingdom" (p. 119, n. 31); the Kashmiri king **Harsha**
  in the late eleventh century, who "raised the plundering of temples to an institutionalized
  activity"; and Paramara kings who "attacked and plundered Jain temples in Gujarat" in the late
  twelfth and early thirteenth century (p. 119, n. 32).
- **Buddhist monastic establishments at Odantapuri, Vikramasila and Nalanda**, targeted in Bihar
  after 1192: "Detached from a Buddhist laity, these establishments had by this time become
  dependent on the patronage of local royal authorities, with whom they were identified" (p. 120).
- **The temples at Khajuraho**, left unharmed because "abandoned by their Candella royal patrons
  before Turkish armies reached the area in the early thirteenth century" (pp. 116–117).
- **Mughal-era instances**: **1661**, Mir Jumla, governor of Bengal, "sacked the temples of the
  neighbouring raja of Kuch Bihar"; the next year he "pushed far up the Brahmaputra valley and
  desecrated temples of the Ahom rajas, **replacing the principal one at Garhgaon with a mosque**"
  (p. 124). **February 1299**, Ulugh Khan sacked Gujarat's famous [temple — sentence continues in
  chunk 009] (p. 124).

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The Ajmer urs protocol, in full, as the clearest description of shrine office-holding in the
  book so far** (p. 116, n. 25, quoting Syed Liyaqat Hussain Moini, "Rituals and Customary Practices
  at the Dargah of Ajmer", in Troll, ed., *Muslim Shrines in India*, 1989, pp. 72, 74). The
  protocols observed "during the annual **'urs** ceremonies at Ajmer, which commemorate the
  deathdate of Mu'in al-Din Chishti, 'betray the impact of Mughal court etiquette. **The diwan,
  dressed Mughal fashion, represents in fact the Mughal king rather than a religious dignitary**,
  and comes escorted by the torch-bearers and mace-bearers wearing Mughal costumes. He takes his
  seat on the cushion (*gadela*) under a special tent (*dalbadal*) erected for the occasion… On
  his arrival in the shrine the diwan kisses the tomb and offers flowers, and then **one of the
  khadims, who happens to be his wakil**, like the other pilgrims, ties a *dastar* (turban) over
  his head, spreads the cloth sheet over his bowed head, prays for him, and then gives him
  *tabarruk*, consisting of flowers, sandal and sweets… Then he sits down and **the fatiha khwans,
  who are permanently and hereditarily employed, recite the fatiha, as well as prayers for the
  sovereign (*badshah-i Islam*), the diwan, the *mutawalli* and other officials, and for the
  general public**.'" Named offices: diwan, khadim, wakil, mutawalli, fatiha khwan; named objects:
  gadela, dalbadal, dastar, tabarruk.
- **Why tomb-shrines attract state money — the doctrine, stated plainly.** "as the spiritual power
  of a charismatic Sufi was believed to **adhere after his lifetime to his tomb-site**, shrines at
  such tombs were patronized by Indo-Muslim rulers just as they were frequented by Muslim devotees"
  (p. 113).
- **Akbar's pilgrimages, counted and dated.** "**Two of Akbar's fourteen pilgrimages to the shrine
  of Mu'in al-Din Chishti at Ajmer, those of 1568 and 1574, were made immediately after conquering
  Chitor and Bengal respectively**" (p. 115, citing P. M. Currie, *The Shrine and Cult of Mu'in
  al-Din Chishti of Ajmer*, 1992, p. 100), "several of them made by foot" (p. 116). Akbar to
  Badauni: "'All this (success) has been brought through the Pir [Mu'in al-Din]'" (p. 115, citing
  Badauni, *Muntakhab al-tawarikh*, trans. Lowe, 2:243).
- **Mughal building at Chishti shrines, itemised**: Babur's brother-in-law rebuilt Nizam al-Din's
  tomb (1526); Akbar built Humayun's tomb near it and began Fatehpur Sikri at Salim Chishti's
  hospice, both in **1571**; **Jahangir built gates and other buildings at or near Ajmer**, "as did
  Shah Jahan as part of his victory celebrations after defeating the raja of Mewar"; Aurangzeb made
  "sizeable contributions" at Gulbarga, Khuldabad, Ajmer and Delhi; 'Alamgir II repaired Nizam
  al-Din's tomb; Bahadur Shah II built beside Bakhtiyar Kaki's shrine (p. 115).
- **A war economy financed by shrine-city loot.** The Ghaznavids "raided and looted Indian cities,
  including their **richly endowed temples loaded with movable wealth**, with a view to financing
  their larger political objectives far to the west, in Khurasan", and "The predatory nature of
  these raids was also structurally integral to the Ghaznavid political economy: their army was a
  permanent, professional one built around an elite corps of mounted archers who, as slaves, were
  **purchased, equipped, and paid with cash derived from regular infusions of war booty**" (p. 111).
- **A temple as a political institution, and the icon as shared sovereignty.** "royal temple
  complexes of the early medieval period were thoroughly and pre-eminently political institutions.
  It was here that, after the sixth century, human kingship was established, contested, and
  revitalized… **the central icon housed in a royal temple's 'womb-chamber' and inhabited by the
  state-deity of the temple's royal patron, expressed the shared sovereignty of king and deity**"
  (p. 117). The deity also had "'a very special relationship, indeed a sovereign relationship, with
  the particular geographical site in which its temple complex was located'", and "even the
  physical removal of an image from its original site could not break the link between deity and
  geography" (p. 117, drawing on Davis and, via Davis, David Shulman: "'A divine power is felt to be
  present naturally on the spot'").
- **A sixth-century text read as evidence of the king–temple bond.** The *Brhatsamhita*: "'**If a
  Siva linga, image, or temple breaks apart, moves, sweats, cries, speaks, or otherwise acts with no
  apparent cause, this warns of the destruction of the king and his territory**'" (p. 118). Eaton's
  inference: "from about the sixth century on, images and temples associated with dynastic authority
  were considered politically vulnerable" (p. 118).
- **A temple converted into a mosque, recorded on the mosque, 1406.** After Firuz Shah Bahmani
  defeated Vijayanagara in the Raichur doab, an inscription records that "'**a mosque has been
  converted out of a temple as a sign of religion**'", and then that the sultan "'conquered this
  fort by the firm determination of his mind in a single attack (lit. on horseback). After the
  victory of the emperor, the chief of chiefs, Safdar… received (the charge of) the fort'"
  (p. 124, n. 38, citing *Epigraphia Indica, Arabic and Persian Supplement*, 1962, 57–8).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The Prophet's mantle as the charter of a sultanate.** "At the core of 'Isami's narrative of the
  Bahmani Revolution… is a narrative of the passing of the Prophet Muhammad's own mantle
  (*khirqa*) from Abu Bakr, the first caliph, down to Burhan al-Din Gharib's leading disciple, Zain
  al-Din Shirazi (d. 1369). It was from that very mantle—'**by whose scent one could master both
  worlds**'—that the founder of the Bahmani Sultanate, Sultan Hasan Bahman Shah (r. 1347–58), was
  said to have received his own power and inspiration" (pp. 113–114).
- **A shaikh's death as the cause of an empire's decline.** 'Isami held that "the decline of Delhi,
  and of the Tughluq empire generally, had resulted in a large part from the demise in 1325 of
  Shaikh Nizam al-Din Auliya" — in his own words, "'As soon as that holy man of virtue departed
  from Dehli to the other world, the country, in general, and the city, in particular, fell into a
  turmoil and were subjected to ruin and destruction'" (pp. 113 and n. 15).
- **A shrine pilgrimage credited with saving Delhi from the Mongols.** "what had saved the Delhi
  Sultanate from Mongol conquest was the respect showed by Sultan Muhammad bin Tughluq (r. 1325–51)
  for the memory of… Mu'in al-Din Chishti, **to whose tomb in Ajmer the sultan had made a
  pilgrimage just after engaging with a Mongol army**" (p. 112).
- **Salim Chishti's prediction of Jahangir's birth**, and the turban tied on his head (p. 115).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The two halves of Indo-Muslim religious policy, stated as a thesis.** With the Ghurids and the
  Delhi Sultanate, "**we can identify two principal components to this project: (a) state patronage
  of an India-based Sufi order, and (b) a policy of selective temple desecration that aimed not, as
  earlier, to finance distant military operations on the Iranian Plateau, but to delegitimize and
  extirpate defeated Indian ruling houses**" (p. 112). The patronage "contributed positively to the
  process of Indo-Muslim state-building" by "effectively injecting a legitimizing 'substance' into
  a new body politic at the moment of its birth"; "Equally important to this process was its
  negative counterpart: the sweeping away of all prior political authority in newly-conquered and
  annexed territories" (p. 116).
- **Why the Chishtis and not another order — a geographical argument.** "since the tomb-shrines of
  the greatest shaikhs of this order were located **within South Asia**, and not in distant Central
  Asia or West Asia as was the case with those of other orders, **a ruling dynasty's patronage of
  Chishti shrines could bolster its claims to being both legitimately Islamic and authentically
  Indian**" (p. 113). The order was also made famous by its publicists: Amir Hasan, Amir Khusrau
  and Zia al-Din Barani "were all disciples of Delhi's principal Chishti shaikh… As writers whose
  works were widely read, these men were in effect publicists for Nizam al-Din and his order. And
  since the three were also patronized by the Tughluq court, **the public and the ruling classes
  alike gradually came to associate dynastic fortune with that of the Chishti order**" (pp. 112–113).
- **A five-year pattern treated as decisive evidence.** "**within the space of just five years,
  between 1342 and 1347, founders of independent Indo-Muslim dynasties in both Bengal and the
  Deccan patronized local Chishti shaikhs whose own spiritual masters had migrated from Delhi where
  they had studied with the imperial capital's preeminent Sufi shaikh, Nizam al-Din Auliya**"
  (p. 114) — then repeated for Gujarat (1396) and Malwa (1404), and traced forward through every
  Mughal to 1858. "In sum, **the entire Mughal dynasty, believing that the blessings of Chishti
  shaikhs underpinned their worldly success, vigorously patronized the order**" (p. 115). And the
  effect ran both ways: "the Mughal–Chishti partnership even survived the collapse of the Mughal
  state. In a sense it persists to this day. **The ceremonies, the terminology, and the protocol
  still found at Chishti shrines generally, and at the Ajmer shrine particularly, all reflect the
  extraordinary intrusion of the Mughals' courtly culture into that of the Chishti order**"
  (p. 116). Directly usable for reading court idiom in any Chishti shrine's present-day practice.
- **'Isami's theory of religion and politics, quoted as the period's own.** "'The world is bound up
  closely with that of the men of faith… In every country, there is a man of piety who keeps it
  going and well. **Although there might be a monarch in every country, yet it is actually under the
  protection of a fakir [Sufi shaikh]**'" (p. 112, with the Persian given in n. 13; 'Isami,
  *Futuh us-salatin*, 1350).
- **Which temples were destroyed, and which were not — the selection rule.** "**When such authority
  was vested in a ruler whose own legitimacy was associated with a royal temple—typically one that
  housed an image of a ruling dynasty's state-deity, or *rastra-devata* (usually Vishnu or
  Siva)—that temple was normally looted, redefined, or destroyed**, any of which would have had the
  effect of detaching a defeated raja from the most prominent manifestation of his former
  legitimacy. **Temples that were not so identified, or temples formerly so identified but abandoned
  by their royal patrons and thereby rendered politically irrelevant, were normally left unharmed**"
  (p. 116), Khajuraho being the example.
- **Iconoclasm as Indian political practice, not Islamic theology.** "It would be wrong to explain
  this phenomenon by appealing to an essentialized '**theology of iconoclasm**' felt to be intrinsic
  to the Islamic religion. For, while it is true that contemporary Persian sources routinely
  condemned idolatry (*but-parast*) on religious grounds, it is also true that **attacks on images
  patronized by enemy kings had been, from about the sixth century AD on, thoroughly integrated into
  Indian political behavior**" (p. 117). Hence "it is clear that temples had been **the natural sites
  for the contestation of kingly authority well before the coming of Muslim Turks to India**. Not
  surprisingly, Turkish invaders, when attempting to plant their own rule in early medieval India,
  followed and continued established patterns" (p. 119).
- **Eighty instances, against sixty thousand — with the evidentiary standard stated.** "by relying
  strictly on evidence found in contemporary or near-contemporary epigraphic and literary evidence
  spanning a period of more than five centuries (**1192–1729**), one may identify **eighty instances
  of temple desecration whose historicity appears reasonably certain**. Although this figure falls
  well short of the **60,000 claimed by some Hindu nationalists**, a review of these data suggests
  several broad patterns" (p. 120). The 60,000 is sourced to a "'Hindu Timeline'" entry in
  *Hinduism Today*, December 1994, via Cynthia Talbot (p. 120, n. 35).
- **He states both directions of error in the record, which is what makes the count usable.** "Undoubtedly some temples were desecrated but the facts in the matter were never recorded, or the
  facts were recorded but the records themselves no longer survive. Conversely, **later Indo-Muslim
  chroniclers, seeking to glorify the religious zeal of earlier Muslim rulers, sometimes attributed
  acts of temple desecration to those rulers even when no contemporary evidence supports the
  claims**" (p. 119). Two worked cases in the footnotes: the *Riyaz al-salatin* of **1788** claiming
  Muhammad Bakhtiyar demolished temples after conquering Bengal in 1204, "though no contemporary
  evidence suggests that he did so"; and — the sharper one — "**Even contemporary sources could make
  false claims**": a mosque inscription at Bidar **dated 1670** claims the Mughal governor Mukhtar
  Khan destroyed a temple and built the mosque on its site, but "'as a matter of fact,' noted the
  epigraphist who published the inscription, '**the mosque is a new construction, and the rock does
  not seem to have been disturbed, for it still survives**'" (pp. 119–120, n. 34).
- **Two findings from the eighty cases.** "**First, acts of temple desecration were nearly invariably
  carried out by military officers or ruling authorities**" — the clause that follows is destroyed in
  the text layer (see Doubts). "**Second, the chronology and geography of the data indicate that
  acts of temple desecration typically occurred on the cutting edge of a moving military
  frontier**" (p. 120). The geography is then traced case by case: Ajmer and the Gangetic Plain
  after 1192 (nos. 1–9), Bihar's Buddhist monasteries, Iltutmish into Malwa in the 1230s
  (nos. 10–11), the Khalajis through eastern Rajasthan into Gujarat (nos. 12–14, 16–17), the
  peninsular raids of **1295** onward (nos. 15, 18–19), Muhammad bin Tughluq in western Andhra after
  **1323** (nos. 20–22), Firuz Tughluq in Orissa (no. 23), the successor states from the late
  fourteenth century (nos. 25–26, 31–32, 34–35, 38–39, 42; 33, 41), Sultan Sikandar in Kashmir
  (nos. 27–30), Malwa against Rajput power in the mid-fifteenth century (nos. 36–37), the Lodis in
  the early sixteenth (nos. 43–45), and Bijapur and Golconda against Orissa, Vijayanagara and its
  successors in the late sixteenth and early seventeenth (nos. 47, 50–51, 53–55, 59–61)
  (pp. 120–121).
- **Delhi's peninsular raids re-explained as fiscal, not annexationist.** They "appear to have been
  driven not by a goal of annexation but by **the Sultanate's need for wealth with which to defend
  north India from Mongol attacks**. For a short time, then, peninsular India stood in the same
  relation to the north—namely, as a source of plunder for financing distant military
  operations—as north India had stood in relation to Afghanistan three centuries earlier, in the
  days of Mahmud of Ghazni" (pp. 120–121); footnote 36 notes that "In 1247 Balban, the future sultan
  of Delhi, had recommended raiding Indian states for precisely this purpose" (p. 120).
- **A negative case used as a test: the early Mughals in north India.** Between **1526 and 1605** the
  Mughals "grew mainly at the expense of defeated Afghans. **As non-Hindus, the latter had never
  shared sovereignty with deities patronized in royal temples, which probably explains the absence
  of firm evidence of temple desecration by any of the early Mughals, in Ayodhya or elsewhere**"
  (p. 121). Footnote 37 disposes of the Baburi Masjid claim on evidentiary grounds: the notion that
  Mir Baqi destroyed a Rama-birthplace temple "was elaborated in 1936 by S. K. Banerji. However,
  **the author offered no evidence that there had ever been a temple at this site, much less that it
  had been destroyed by Mir Baqi**. The mosque's inscription records only that Babur had ordered the
  construction of the mosque, which was built by Mir Baqi and was described as 'the place of descent
  of celestial beings' (*mahbit-i qudsiyan*). **This commonplace rhetorical flourish in Persian can
  hardly be construed as referring to Rama, especially since it is the mosque itself that is so
  described, and not the site or any earlier structure on the site**" (pp. 121–122, n. 37). Where the
  Mughals did cross into Hindu-ruled domains — Kuch Bihar 1661, Assam 1662 — "we again find
  instances of temple desecration" (p. 124).
- **The purpose of desecration, stated in its final form.** "Contemporary chroniclers and
  inscriptions left by the victors leave no doubt that field commanders, governors, or sultans
  viewed the desecration of royal temples as **a normal means of decoupling a former Hindu king's
  legitimate authority from his former kingdom, and more specifically, of decoupling that former
  king from the image of the state-deity that was publicly understood as protecting the king and
  his kingdom**" (p. 124). Three methods are distinguished: destruction of temples "considered
  essential to the constitution of enemy authority"; conversion into mosques, "which more visibly
  conflated the disestablishment of former sovereignty with the establishment of a new one"; and —
  "a pre-Turkish practice" — "**the seizure of the image of a defeated state-deity and its abduction
  to the victor's capital as a trophy of war**" (p. 124).
- **'Utbi's 986 notice identified as the origin of a rhetorical trope.** "Linking religious
  conversion with conquest—with conquest serving to facilitate conversion, and conversion serving
  to legitimize conquest—'Utbi's brief notice **established a rhetorical trope that many subsequent
  Indo-Muslim chroniclers would repeat**, as for example in the case of the 1455 inscription at
  Dhar" (p. 111). The trope, not the event, is what travelled — a caution for every conquest-and-
  conversion narrative in a shrine's own literature.
- **Michael Willis's inversion, reported approvingly**: the imperial Pratiharas may not have built
  great temple complexes "precisely [because of] their determination to avoid the localization of
  sovereign power that temples necessarily projected", so that "the most active patrons of temple
  construction in this period were **subordinate** kings who did not have such vast imperial
  pretensions" (p. 117, n. 28).

## Doubts

- **No archive row is named, but the temptation to map is real and should be resisted here.** The
  Chishti chain traced in this chunk (Mu'in al-Din → Bakhtiyar Kaki → … → Nizam al-Din Auliya) is
  the chain to which the archive's Chishti rows belong, and Bakhtiyar Kaki is Baba Farid's master
  in the standard succession — **but Eaton does not say so in this chunk and does not mention Baba
  Farid at all here**. Any link must come from the ch. 9 chunks, not from this one.
- **One of the chunk's two headline findings has its second clause destroyed.** "acts of temple
  desecration were nearly invariably carried out by military officers or ruling authorities; that
  is, such acts that we— [then] Setar Sea TE oe en" (p. 120). The point being made — presumably
  that such acts were not popular or mob actions — **cannot be read from the transcription**. A
  human must re-read p. 120 from the PDF before this finding is quoted.
- **The table and three maps that carry the eighty cases are not legible here.** Maps 2a (p. 122)
  and 2b (p. 123) survive only as the words "Arabian", "Sea", "Indian Ocean" and their captions;
  the table itself is "at the end of the chapter" and has not yet appeared. **The case numbers
  cited in the prose (nos. 1–66) cannot be resolved to places from this chunk.**
- **The Rashtrakuta king who destroyed the Kalapriya temple is named only in part**: "the
  Rashtrakuta monarch Tides + : III" (p. 119) — the name is destroyed, and the "III" plus the early
  tenth-century date points to Indra III, but **that is inference, not the book's text**. Note also
  the number disagreement in the same sentence ("not only destroyed… but **they** took special
  delight"), which is the printed text's own or a transcription slip.
- **'Isami's dates and titles need care.** He is called "the Bahmani court-poet" writing in 1350
  (p. 112), yet he is quoted as explaining the Bahmani Revolution of 1347 and the Deccan's
  flourishing "at mid-century" — consistent, but the *Futuh us-salatin* is cited from two different
  editions (Usha, Madras 1948; Agha Mahdi Husain, London 1967) with different paginations, and the
  footnotes run "Ibid." across both.
- **A textual disagreement inside the footnotes**: n. 12 cites Baihaqi's *Tarikh-i Baihaqi* via
  Elliot and Dowson 2:123–4, then gives the text as "'Ali Akbar Fayyaz, ed., **Tarikh-i Yamini**
  (Mashshad, 1971)" — but *Tarikh-i Yamini* is 'Utbi's work, not Baihaqi's, and Fayyaz's Mashhad
  edition is of the *Tarikh-i Baihaqi*. **The title in that footnote is probably wrong in the book
  or the transcription**; the page reference reads "ST?!" and is lost (p. 111).
- Printed page 95 appears as "3" in the running head at p. 107 (noted in chunk 007) and printed 99
  appears correctly here; the offset PDF → printed is **+12** throughout this chunk.
