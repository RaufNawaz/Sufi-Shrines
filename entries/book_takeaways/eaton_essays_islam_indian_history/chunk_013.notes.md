# eaton_essays_islam_indian_history — chunk 013 — pp. 184–197

The closing pages of the essay "The Articulation of Islamic Space in the Medieval Deccan" — the
Vijayanagara goddess-shrine narrative and the "Comparisons and Contrasts" section (pp. 184–187) —
then the opening of Chapter Seven, "Historical Introduction to Firuzabad, Palace City of the
Deccan", reprinted from Michell and Eaton, *Firuzabad: Palace City of the Deccan* (Oxford, 1992)
(pp. 188–197).

**Nothing in this chunk is in Pakistan and no Pakpattan material appears.** Its value for the
archive is entirely mechanistic: this is where Eaton states, in the plainest form anywhere in my
chunks, the *two different ways a state can buy legitimacy from sacred ground* — patronizing a
**mobile** Sufi shaikh versus appropriating a **fixed** shrine — and then works one case (Sultan
Firuz Bahmani, Khalifat al-Rahman, and a document assigning a whole city's revenues to a shrine)
in enough detail to see the transaction. Two name collisions with archive rows ('Ala al-Din Mauj
Darya, Shah Kamal Mujjarad) are flagged under Doubts and must not be merged into those rows.

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **shrine-of-mauj-darya-bukhari** (as "**'Ala al-Din Mauj Darya**"): named as "a holy man in the
  Punjab" whom the future Sultan Ghiyath al-Din Tughluq, then governor, was "attracted to" for
  "the spiritual power and piety" he possessed, and visited frequently (p. 195, n. 15). He gave
  turbans to Ghiyath al-Din, his son and his nephew — the future Muhammad bin Tughluq and Firuz
  Tughluq — "and told that each was destined to rule India. The length of each turban, moreover,
  exactly corresponded to the number of years each would reign" (p. 195, n. 15). Eaton's source
  is Shams-i Siraj 'Afif, *Tarikh-i Firuz Shahi*, ed. Vilayat Husain (Calcutta: Asiatic Society of
  Bengal, 1891), 27–8 (p. 195, n. 15). No shrine, tomb, town or death date is given for him
  (not stated). **This is almost certainly a different figure from the archive's Lahore Mauj
  Darya — see Doubts before letting a word of it near that entry.**

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- **Pampa**, river goddess of "Pampa's crossing" on the Tungabhadra: earlier "merely protected by
  Mahakala Deva to whom she was in no way subordinate", later "reduced to a subordinate status as
  Siva's 'consort'", with South Indian texts describing her marriage to **Virupaksa** on the model
  of Siva and Parvati (p. 184).
- **Vijayanagara / the Kampila dynasty**: from the late thirteenth century the Kampilas were "the
  shrine's principal patrons"; in 1327 Muhammad bin Tughluq "overpowered the Kampilas' stronghold
  at **Kummata**, where both the raja and his son died in the struggle"; the **Sangamas** then
  emerged as the site's "protectors" and the place "would ultimately be called Vijayanagara, or
  City of Victory" (p. 184).
- **Tirupati**: the sixteenth-century rulers of Vijayanagara "patronized the great Vaishnava temple
  at Tirupati with its pan-South Indian connections" (p. 184).
- **Firuzabad**, on the north bank of the Bhima River: "one of the most historically important yet
  unknown architectural legacies of medieval India", royal palace and second capital of the Bahmani
  kingdom (1347–1537), built for **Sultan Firuz Shah Bahmani (r. 1397–1422)**, "Constructed between
  1399 and 1406", in sporadic use "through the end of the fifteenth century, after which it
  disappeared from recorded memory" (p. 188). It lies "seventeen miles (twenty-eight km.) due south
  of Gulbarga" (p. 194).
- **Shrine and tomb of Khalifat al-Rahman**, "a Muslim holy man, locally known as Khalifat
  al-Rahman", "Some 1.3 miles (2 km.) north of [Firuzabad's] walls" (p. 194). "we possess no
  authentic contemporary records concerning the life of this saint" (pp. 194–195, n. 14).
- **Saiyid Muhammad Husaini Gisudaraz (d. 1422)**, "perhaps the most famous Sufi of the Deccan"
  (p. 196): Firuz "judged Gisudaraz deficient in learning and so withdrew royal favour from him",
  and had earlier "ordered the shaikh to leave the city, on the pretext that his meetings with
  fellow dervishes were creating a public nuisance"; Firuz's brother Ahmad "lavishly patronized the
  shaikh by building a residence for him and by attending his meetings devoted to ecstatic
  practices" (p. 196).
- **Baba Kamal Mujarrad** (also printed "Shah Kamal Mujjarad"), taken by Firuz as "his spiritual
  advisor", tomb "supposed to have faced the sultan's own tomb in Gulbarga" (p. 196 and n. 18).
  **See Doubts for the collision with the archive's Baba Shah Kamal of Lahore.**
- **Sagar, renamed Nusratabad**, "'City of Victory,' perhaps mocking the name Vijayanagara, which
  also means 'City of Victory'" (p. 193).
- **Gulbarga and Bidar**: Ahmad "shifted the Bahmani capital from Gulbarga, which had been the
  capital since the founding of the dynasty in 1347, to Bidar, a new site some sixty miles (96 km.)
  to the northeast" (p. 197).
- **Hasan Khan**, Firuz's son: passed over in the will in favour of Firuz's brother Ahmad; Firuz on
  his deathbed in September 1422 said "Go to Firuzabad and see to it that the rest of my family not
  deviate from this will of mine" (p. 194); Ahmad's counsellors advised him "to confine, blind, or
  murder Hasan" (p. 197).
- **Mir Fazl Allah Anju**, "The kingdom's chief minister", who counselled Firuz that *mut'a* was
  lawful in the Shi'i tradition though polygamy was forbidden in Sunni tradition (p. 190, n. 3).
- **The Raichur Doab / plain**, "formed by the Krishna and Tungabhadra rivers", lying "directly
  between the capital cities of Gulbarga and Vijayanagara" (p. 193), "one of the wealthiest strips
  of land in the entire peninsula" (p. 186).
- **Daulatabad**: Jami' mosque of 1318, 260' by 260', 67,600 sq. ft (p. 191, n. 7).

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **A shrine's growth measured in households.** "Throughout this long period of religious
  evolution, the site became an ever more important pilgrimage centre, as we know from inscriptions
  that enumerate the many hundreds of households then located there" (p. 184). No figure is given
  (not stated).
- **Patronage escalates with political ambition.** "from the thirteenth century politically
  ambitious or already-dominant rulers in the area began cultivating closer ties with the shrine and
  its deities, in contrast to the earlier phase, when the shrine was patronized by none but passing
  chieftains" (p. 184).
- **Patronage and sanctity feed each other.** "Royal patrons gravitated toward a shrine to a river
  goddess that was already endowed with sanctity, **and whose sanctity increased with increasing
  royal patronage**" (p. 185) — the compounding mechanism, stated in one clause.
- **Architecture subordinated to the shrine.** "the major royal and religious monuments even of
  Vijayanagara's later days were architecturally aligned to this goddess shrine" (p. 185, citing
  Michell, *Where Kings and Gods Meet*, 153).
- **Two instruments of Bahmani legitimation, named.** "One way they did this was to patronize Sufi
  shaikhs whose territorial sovereignty was theoretically unlimited; another was to receive, quite
  literally, the mantle of the Prophet of Islam" (p. 185).
- **Temples as the soft target of interstate war, and who bore the loss.** "In Vijayanagara, the
  Brahmans who controlled fixed wealth were frequent losers in these conflicts since temples were
  often targeted for attack and looting by northern armies" (p. 186).
- **Firuzabad's congregational mosque, measured.** "Completed probably in 1406", the Jami' "measures
  343' by 202' (104.5 by 61.5 meters) and covers an area of 69,286 square feet (6,427 square
  meters)", "Nearly double the size of the royal mosque in the official capital of Gulbarga",
  "then the largest in the Deccan" (pp. 190–191). Comparative list: Daulatabad 1318, 67,600 sq. ft;
  Gulbarga 1367, 216' by 176', 38,016 sq. ft; Firuzabad 1406, 69,286 sq. ft (p. 191, n. 7, from
  Z. A. Desai, 'Architecture: The Bahmanis').
- **The dating inscription and its displacement.** The 1406 date derives from an inscription "dated
  30 Rajab 808 A.H., corresponding to 21 January, 1406", naming "Abu'l-Muzaffar Taju'd-Dunya
  wa'd-Din, Firuz Shah the Sultan" and even "the mosque's architect, **Ahmad bin Husain
  al-Hisnkayfi**"; but "The inscription, however, is not at present located on the Jami' Mosque, but
  on a small mosque in the shrine of Khalifat al-Rahman, outside the city walls to the north", and
  Akbaru'd-Din Siddiqi argued it "was probably shifted at some time from the ruined Jami' Mosque to
  its present place in order to ensure its preservation" (p. 190, n. 6; p. 191, n. 6 continuation).
- **A whole city's revenues assigned to a shrine — the single most citable economic item here.**
  "Muhammad Suleman Siddiqi reports finding a document, belonging to Firuz's reign, that records the
  donation of the whole of Firuzabad's revenues to Khalifat al-Rahman. The document also gives an
  account of a miracle that the Sufi purportedly performed in the sultan's presence, **which leads
  Suleman Siddiqi to question the document's authenticity**" (p. 195, n. 14, citing Siddiqi, *The
  Bahmani Sufis*, Delhi, 1989, 76). Note the source's own doubt travels with the fact.
- **Shrine-building as royal habit.** "Struck by the awesome spiritual powers commonly attributed to
  nearby Sufis, and believing in many cases that their own lease of power had been given them by
  such persons, Indo-Muslim monarchs typically sought their favour and **patronized the construction
  of great edifices for them**" (p. 195).
- **Dating the Khalifat al-Rahman shrine by style and a gravestone.** It "appears to have been built
  by Sultan Firuz. Stylistically, the monument is contemporary with the other buildings of
  Firuzabad, and a nearby gravestone bears the date 1421" (p. 195, gravestone from Siddiqi, 'Two
  Inscriptions', 44).
- **A tomb complex with a reservoir, and what survived to 1611.** Of Baba Kamal's tomb, Shirazi
  "wrote that his tomb was an elaborate construction, that it faced the sultan's own tomb in
  Gulbarga, that it had beneath it a reservoir that the sultan had built during the saint's
  lifetime, and that in Shirazi's own day, i.e., 1611, the tomb's dome and the reservoir were still
  in existence"; the candidate complex "is accompanied by a mosque, a saray, and an unidentified
  structure" (p. 196, n. 18).
- **A city as a personal estate, and as a prison.** Ahmad "made his nephew a noble (*amir*) with the
  humble rank of 500 horses and assigned Firuzabad to him as a personal estate. But the unfortunate
  Hasan Khan was in effect placed under house arrest and made a prisoner in his father's palace
  city" (p. 197).
- **Army logistics as the reason for religious scale.** "In these circumstances the city would have
  had to accommodate great numbers of soldiers, **which would explain the large size of Firuzabad's
  congregational mosque**" (p. 194) — mosque size read as a demographic instrument.
- **Firuzabad's built form per Firishta**: "Its markets and shops achieved the acme of cleanliness
  and excellence, while its streets were both wide and straight. The city's citadel, constructed of
  plaster and stone, opened out onto the river on one side and canals carried water from the river
  into its interior", plus "Separate villas as lovely as the moon" for the harem (p. 189, quoting
  *Tarikh-i Firishta*, 1:308–9, Eaton's own translation).
- **Campaign frequency**: "Firishta records that Firuz led twenty-four campaigns against
  non-Muslims, meaning in particular Vijayanagara, which amounts to an average of exactly one
  campaign for each year of his reign" (p. 191; see Doubts).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The turban prophecy of 'Ala al-Din Mauj Darya**: three future sultans given turbans whose
  lengths "exactly corresponded to the number of years each would reign" — attributed by Eaton to
  Shams-i Siraj 'Afif's *Tarikh-i Firuz Shahi* (p. 195, n. 15).
- **Gisudaraz's divided turban**: he "took a turban and divided it in two parts, placing one part on
  the head of the sultan's brother Ahmad, and another part on the head of Ahmad's own son,
  symbolically predicting the future sovereignty of both" — attributed to Firishta (p. 196).
- **Prediction as appointment**: "This is why we occasionally come across Sufis predicting who would
  attain political office, and for how long they would hold it. **Many understood such predictions
  as virtual appointments**" (p. 195); and "such 'predictions' were also understood as effective
  appointments" (p. 196).
- **A miracle inside the revenue document**: the Firuzabad-revenues document "gives an account of a
  miracle that the Sufi purportedly performed in the sultan's presence" — reported by Muhammad
  Suleman Siddiqi, who treats it as a reason to doubt the document (p. 195, n. 14).
- **Pampa's marriage to Virupaksa**, described in South Indian texts "in terms that paralleled the
  all-Indian myth of Siva's marriage to Parvati" (p. 184).
- **Firishta's rumour of fratricide**: "a rumor, current in the historian's day, that Ahmad had
  risen to power by having had his brother strangled to death"; Shirazi "stating without
  reservations that Ahmad had arranged for his brother's death at the hand of an Abyssinian
  assassin" — against Tabataba's account of reconciliation and natural death (p. 197).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Sanskritization as a documented process, not a label.** "between the seventh and twelfth
  century, this regional shrine had gradually become 'Sanskritized,' as a local river goddess was
  pulled up into, and in the process transformed by, the big world of pan-Indic Saivism" (p. 184).
- **A state founded on a shrine, as a precedent.** The Kampilas' "attempt to build a state on the
  site of a goddess-cum-Siva shrine, **thereby to reap the ritual and political benefits of an
  expanding religious cult**, established an important precedent for the kings who subsequently
  ruled from the site" (p. 184). And even at imperial scale Vijayanagara "never shed its rootedness
  to a fixed and ancient shrine dedicated to a river goddess" (pp. 184–185).
- **The two kingdoms were alike in everything but sacred space.** "the so-called 'Islamic' Bahmani
  kingdom and the 'Hindu' Vijayanagara kingdom were a good deal alike. Sharing similar historical
  origins, and comparable in terms of military organization, political economy, moral economy,
  aesthetic tastes, royal dress, titles, and public comportment, both states were firmly integrated
  into the medieval Islamicate 'world-system'." "The two kingdoms differed most fundamentally,
  however, in their contrasting conceptions of sacred space, as reflected in the different ways they
  constructed themselves ideologically" (p. 185).
- **Why a rebel state needs a universal saint: the post-1258 legitimacy problem.** The Bahmanis
  "made universalist claims to legitimacy in a post-1258 world that no longer possessed a
  centralized caliphate"; since the Delhi Sultanate had become "the refuge of Islam" for Turks and
  Persians, "a revolution against that sultanate could be justified only by making extravagant
  appeals to much higher authority than anything found within India"; "like French or American
  revolutionaries, who justified their inherently illegal acts by appealing to territorially
  unlimited claims like the Universal Rights of Man or the Pursuit of Liberty, Bahmani
  revolutionaries made universalist claims in order to justify their seditious acts" (p. 185).
- **Mobility is the whole point of the shaikh.** "Sufi shaikhs, like robes or mantles, were highly
  mobile, which is another reason they played such critical roles in launching new states in the
  Deccan, as they did elsewhere in medieval India. Both charismatic and peripatetic, these luminous
  figures exemplified an Islamicate world very much 'on the move' in the thirteenth and fourteenth
  centuries" — a world, per André Wink, "of long-distance trade, of mobile wealth, of extensive
  monetization, and of easily recruitable and purchasable military slaves" (p. 185, citing Wink,
  *al-Hind*, II, ch. 6, 'A World on the Move').
- **The mechanism stated twice, and this is the citable core of the chunk.** Because "Turko-Persian
  states could emerge virtually anywhere, since military leadership depended upon outside recruits
  and not upon territorially-rooted warrior lineages like the Rajputs", the founder's problem "always
  came to this: how to sink down permanent roots and rule with security and credibility in a land
  that only a generation earlier had been plundered with impunity? **This was where immigrant Sufi
  shaikhs, as portable vessels of spiritual sovereignty, played their crucial role.**" Against that:
  "State-building at Vijayanagara, then, was not a matter of recruiting and patronizing mobile
  holymen brought in from some sacred centre elsewhere, but of appropriating and then exploiting the
  sacred power of a fixed religious site through public acts of patronage" (pp. 185–186).
- **Fixed versus mobile does not reduce to Hindu versus Muslim.** "There is no evidence, however,
  that these contrasting strategies of state legitimization led to polarized notions of 'Islamic'
  and 'Hindu' civilizations in the medieval Deccan" (p. 186).
- **Polarization is explained by land, not religion.** It "appears rather to have resulted from
  fierce interstate competition over control of one of the wealthiest strips of land in the entire
  peninsula, the Raichur Doab"; "The numerous wars fought over this fertile tract between the late
  fourteenth and mid-sixteenth centuries in time generated a rhetoric of mutual demonization by
  states on either side of the Doab" (p. 186).
- **The rhetoric is traced to the material position of its authors.** Brahman-patronized inscriptions
  and chronicles "tended therefore to stigmatize state enemies as *adharmic*, or opposed to 'order'
  in the sense of both social stability and cosmic harmony" — offered as a consequence of Brahmans
  controlling fixed, lootable wealth (p. 186). Symmetrically, the Persian chroniclers of the
  Bahmanis — "Saiyid 'Ali Tabataba, Rafi' al-Din Shirazi, or Muhammad Qasim Firishta — were all
  high-born Iranian immigrants who tended to adopt a colonialist view towards non-Muslim Indian
  society", "routinely stigmatized the people of Vijayanagara as 'infidels'", and "were hired to
  chronicle their patrons' grand deeds" (pp. 186–187).
- **Historiography as inherited war rhetoric.** "the rhetoric of warfare generated by literate
  ideologues on both sides of the Krishna ultimately took on a life of its own and hardened into the
  Maginot Line that today continues to divide Deccani historiography into a 'Hindu' south and a
  'Muslim' north" (p. 187).
- **Why a Hindu court adopted Islamicate culture — and what it was not.** "Vijayanagara's rulers did
  not patronize Islamicate culture as a deliberate strategy for controlling their Muslim subjects, in
  the way that, for example, French colonial rulers did in North Africa. Rather, as in the case of
  Sicilian and Byzantine courts of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, rulers of fourteenth and
  fifteenth century Vijayanagara chose to participate in a territorially decentred Islamicate
  world-system **that was compelling precisely because it was not identified with political
  domination from any hegemonic centre**" (p. 187).
- **The limit on saintly sovereignty, in practice.** "Although the territorial reach of a Sufi's
  *wilayat* was theoretically unlimited, in practice it was always identified with well-defined,
  specific localities, just as the river goddess Pampa was unalterably identified with the
  Tungabhadra River" (p. 187) — i.e. the mobile and the fixed converge once the shaikh settles.
- **Baraka as a lease on office — the theory behind shrine patronage.** "according to some traditions
  prevalent in India of Firuz's day, Indo-Muslim sultans were never endowed with the same degree of
  religious, or even political, legitimacy as were some Sufi shaikhs. In this view, temporal rulers
  had only been entrusted with a sort of temporary lease of power through the grace of Muslim shaikhs
  considered to have been especially well-endowed with *baraka*, or the grace of God" (p. 195).
- **Source criticism of Firishta, worked out loud.** Firishta makes the sultan build the city "primarily
  for expanding his linguistic skills, and secondarily for indulging his appetite for female
  companionship"; Eaton: "It would be a mistake, however, to understand from these remarks that the
  city served merely as a royal language lab or a multi-ethnic playpen. References in other historical
  literature, the city's plan, and the nature of its monuments all belie such an interpretation"
  (pp. 189–190). Three counter-arguments: Firishta's own word *takhtgah*, "which means 'throne-place,'
  or court"; the "massiveness of the city's walls"; the "enormous size of its Jami'" (p. 190). On the
  800 women: "We know that Firishta was often given to exaggeration, and this is probably one such
  instance. Indeed, Shirazi wrote that the sultan had but one wife" (p. 190, n. 3). And the earlier
  scholarship is faulted for the same credulity: Klaus Fischer's 1955 essay, the only interpretive
  study to date, called the city "'more a pleasure resort than a residence'" — "Excessive reliance on
  Firishta" (p. 190, n. 5).
- **Why Tabataba is preferred, and how the reading is tested.** Tabataba "discusses the city in the
  context of the sultan's continual wars with his chief adversary... and of his concerns with the
  military defense of his kingdom", quoting him: "'Anytime the king had the intention of waging war on
  the infidels of Vijayanagara, on the occasion of departure or returning he would stay several days
  in that glorious place'" (p. 194). Eaton: "Tabataba thus understood the city's essential character
  primarily in military terms... **This interpretation is confirmed by the city's location**" — 17
  miles south of Gulbarga, on the near side of the Bhima, on the normal southern campaign route
  (p. 194). Conclusion: "Firuzabad served both as a military camp and as a personal retreat"
  (p. 194).
- **On the reliability of chronicles a century late.** "Although their histories were not recorded
  until a century after the collapse of the Bahmani dynasty and the abandonment of Firuzabad, the
  three men were all eminent scholars who based their work on oral traditions of their own day and on
  earlier histories, some of which are now lost" (p. 188); Firishta relied on three named lost
  chronicles (p. 189, n. 1).
- **Patronage as substitution when a blessing is withheld — Eaton's inference, clearly marked as
  such.** After 1415 "Firuz ruled his kingdom without the blessings of the most eminent shaikh in the
  realm. Worse still, those blessings had been given to his own brother and potential rival for the
  throne, Ahmad. **It therefore seems likely that at some time after 1415, after having fallen out
  with Saiyid Gisudaraz, Firuz sought and found other Sufis on whom to lavish his patronage**"
  (p. 196) — hence Baba Kamal Mujarrad, and "it would appear that Firuz had adopted still another
  spiritual advisor, Khalifat al-Rahman, whose impressive shrine he had built just outside the city"
  (p. 197). Note the hedges are his: "This is probably what happened" (p. 195), "it seems very likely"
  (p. 196).
- **Moving a capital to escape a shrine-backed faction.** Ahmad's transfer from Gulbarga to Bidar
  "symbolized his efforts to leave behind him the dangerous political world associated with the
  ancestral capital city" — a political world in which "Ahmad enjoyed the support of the respected
  shaikh, Saiyid Gisudaraz, and no doubt of the latter's numerous followers, while many nobles still
  supported the cause of Firuz's son, Hasan Khan" (p. 197).

## Doubts

- **The Mauj Darya identification is a name collision and I have not resolved it.** The chunk's figure
  is "'Ala al-Din Mauj Darya", "a holy man in the Punjab" active in "the early fourteenth century",
  visited by the future Ghiyath al-Din Tughluq (dynasty dated "1321–98" here) (p. 195, n. 15). The
  archive's **shrine-of-mauj-darya-bukhari** row gives Hazrat Meeran Muhammad Shah Bukhari (Mauj
  Darya) of Edward Road, Anarkali, Lahore, **died 1604** — roughly three centuries later and with a
  different personal name. Eaton gives no location, tomb or death date for his figure (not stated), so
  the chunk cannot settle whether the two are the same lineage, the same title borne twice, or
  unrelated men. **Do not merge this material into that entry without an external identification;**
  the honest use is a note that a fourteenth-century Punjabi "Mauj Darya" appears in Eaton's footnote.
- **A second collision: Baba Kamal Mujarrad / Shah Kamal Mujjarad (p. 196, n. 18) is not obviously the
  archive's shrine-of-baba-shah-kamal (Lahore).** Eaton places this saint's tomb in Gulbarga, facing
  Firuz's own tomb, so on the chunk's own evidence it is a Deccan figure; but the archive row gives no
  death year for its Baba Shah Kamal, so I cannot rule the pairing out from here either. Recorded as a
  non-match with the name flagged, not as a match.
- **Eaton's own two names for the Deccan saint differ within one footnote**: "Baba Kamal Mujarrad" in
  the text (p. 196) and "Shah Kamal Mujjarad" as a candidate identification in the note, with a second
  candidate — "the as yet unidentified tomb lying to the immediate south of Sultan Firoz's tomb" — left
  open (p. 196, n. 18). "Extant hagiographical literature does not record the name of this saint"
  (p. 196, n. 18). So the tomb attribution is unsettled in the source itself.
- **The revenue-donation document is reported at second hand and doubted by its own finder.** Eaton
  does not endorse it; Muhammad Suleman Siddiqi "question[s] the document's authenticity" (p. 195,
  n. 14). Any use in the archive must carry that doubt.
- **Internal arithmetic slip**: Firuz's reign is called "twenty-five year" (p. 191) while 24 campaigns
  are said to give "an average of exactly one campaign for each year of his reign" (p. 191). Trivial,
  but the phrase "exactly one" should not be quoted as a computed rate.
- **Gisudaraz chronology is given out of order and should be checked**: the 1415 refusal to endorse
  Hasan Khan is narrated first, then "Some eight years earlier, Firuz had ordered the shaikh to leave
  the city" (p. 196), i.e. c. 1407, while the withdrawal of royal favour is undated (not stated).
- **The manner of Firuz's death is contradictory in the sources and Eaton says so**: strangling
  (Firishta, as rumour), an Abyssinian assassin (Shirazi, "without reservations"), versus
  reconciliation and natural death (Tabataba, "the earlier and probably more reliable account")
  (p. 197).
- **Text-layer damage affecting a quotation.** At p. 185 the sentence about the Delhi Sultanate reads
  "the Delhi Sultanate had become a majorvreftige of Islam, and for Turks and Persians in the eastern
  Islamic world, the refuge of Islam" — the phrase is garbled and apparently duplicated; **the clause
  cannot be quoted from this chunk** and must be re-read from the PDF. At p. 191 a clause loses a word
  entirely: "the city's _ appears almost frivolous". Both are quoted above only in the reduced form the
  transcription supports.
- **Transliteration and title mangling throughout, in the text layer rather than the book**: Shirazi's
