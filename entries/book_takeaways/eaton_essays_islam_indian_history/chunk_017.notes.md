hagiography and chronicles, this chunk works from sworn testimony by named and aged villagers,

Graded Hierarchies" (p. 247), "Rituals and Symbols at the Shrine" (p. 250).

For the economics thesis the load-bearing pages are **248–249** (the two-tier lineage system:
Chishti rentier clan over Jat tenant murids, with a colonial settlement report as the evidence),

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar** (as "the shrine of Baba Farid", "Baba Farid", "Baba Farid
  Shakar Ganj", "the gaddi", the shrine at Pakpattan):
  - Death: "Baba Farid, who died at the age of ninety in A.D. 1265" (p. 241).
  - **Saint and shrine were not distinguished by devotees.** "many murids referred to the saint and
    his shrine in the same terms, sometimes defining themselves as murids of Baba Farid, and
    sometimes referring to themselves as murids of the gaddi, or shrine… The shrine was Baba Farid"
    (p. 241). This is Eaton's reading of the depositions, and it is the premise of his conversion
    argument below.
  - **Rank among shrines.** Devotees saw it as "occupying one in a network of sacred wilayats, or
    territories under the spiritual authority of one or another pir (saint), which dot all of North
    India", and these shrines as "related to each other in terms of a ranked hierarchy, with the
    shrine of Baba Farid being the supreme shrine of the Punjab, to which others were subordinate"
    (pp. 241–242). Eaton also calls it "the most popular Islamic shrine in the western Punjab"
    (p. 246) and "the preeminent shrine of the Punjab" (p. 252).
  - **The subordinate-shrine list, as given by one witness.** "The sajjada-nishin of one of these
    shrines listed the following as among the shrines subordinate to that of Baba Farid in
    Pakpattan: that of Shaikh Jamal al-Din of Hansi, Hissar District, of Muhammad Shah in Basi Nau,
    Hoshiarpur District, of Nizam al-Din Auliya' in Delhi, Piran Kaler in Saharanpur District, Golra
    Sharif in Rawalpindi District, Taunsa Sharif in Dera Ghazi Khan District, Mukhan Sharif and Basal
    Sharif in Campbellpore District, Uch Sharif in Bahawalpur State, and the shrines of Kasur and
    Lakhneke in Lahore District, Kastiwala in Gurdaspur District, and Panipat in Karnal District"
    (pp. 241–242, n. 12, citing Lahore High Court, 1:180). **This is one sajjada-nishin's claim, not
    Eaton's own finding**, and it is a list of *places*, mostly without a saint's name — see Doubts
    for the archive rows it touches and the ones it cannot be pinned to.
  - **How the "daughter" shrines came to be subordinate — two distinct mechanisms.** "Although one
    shrine located in the nearby town of Dipalpur had been given land by an earlier Diwan of
    Pakpattan and for this reason saw itself as subordinate to Baba Farid's shrine, most of the
    'daughter' shrines of Baba Farid in the Punjab were founded by family descendants of Baba Farid,
    and hence they looked upon the shrine at Pakpattan as their spiritual superior" (p. 242,
    n. 13, citing 2:51). So: a land grant in one case, descent in the rest. The Pakpattan shrine here
    is a *grantor* of land, not only a grantee.
  - **The hierarchy was performed, not just asserted**: "this ranked hierarchy was ritually acted out
    according to an unwritten etiquette governing the attendance of shrine rituals by the
    sajjada-nishins of other shrines" (p. 242).
  - **Baraka distributed through living pirs as well as shrines.** "the saint's baraka was seen as
    continuing to flow not only through the medium of such shrines, but also through living pirs who
    likewise traced their spiritual, if not familial, descent from Baba Farid" (p. 242). A
    fifty-four-year-old cultivator-and-pir of Dipalpur: "I am a ghulam ['slave'] ef [of] Baba Farid's
    sélsila ['spiritual order']. I am Watuzai Pathan and my ancestor of the 16th degree higher up was
    a disciple of Baba Farid. I am personally a pir of my tribe" (p. 242, n. 14, citing 2:13). Eaton:
    such a pir's authority "stemmed from his descent not from Baba Farid's family, but from one of the
    saint's disciples", by which the following "spread out over many ethnically distinct groups, yet
    was sustained within such groups by living pirs" serving only their own clan (p. 242).
  - **Those village pirs took payment too.** "Like the main shrine in Pakpattan, such pi[r]s also
    received cash payments from their murids, who readily identified the pirs residing in or near
    their villages as their links with the shrine of Baba Farid in Pakpattan" (p. 242, nn. 15–16,
    citing 2:164 and 1:193). No amounts are given for the village pirs (not stated).
  - **Catchment.** "It is in Pakpattan, a town that lay within easy reach of only several days' travel
    in the Punjab, that God's grace was made concrete and accessible, that Heaven and Earth met"
    (p. 247).
  - **The succession dispute, dated.** The former Diwan **Sa'id Muhammad** died in 1934 (p. 250,
    n. 42); his son **Qutb al-Din**, aged eleven, was turbaned on "1 February 1935" (p. 252, n. 50,
    citing 3:215; 2:163); the trial ran 1935–8 (p. 249); Judge **Guru Datta** delivered final judgment
    on "2 December 1938" (p. 243, n. 17, citing 3:290). The plaintiff lost (p. 249).
  - **What the case was actually about.** "the essential purpose of the trial of 1935–8 was to judge
    the plaintiff's claim that the prerogative to select a new Diwan belonged to the Chishti clan and
    not to the previous Diwan" (p. 249).
  - **The dream as a point of law.** "one of the major points of contention[] that emerged was whether
    or not the former Diwan, Sa'id Muhammad, had received a dream in which Baba Farid revealed to him
    that his son Qutb al-Din should succeed him as Diwan" (p. 243). The judge's ruling, quoted: "I
    decide that the defendant has sufficiently succeeded in proving that Diwan Said Muhammad had
    received revelation and that the defendant's nomination was made on the strength of that"
    (p. 243, n. 17).
  - **The Diwans' medieval and eighteenth-century political role, as testified.** "Formerly, in the
    history of medieval Punjab, the Diwans of Pakpattan had frequently played important political
    roles, enjoying close relations with the sultans and Mughal emperors of Delhi. And during the
    decline of Mughal authority, the Diwans' political roles even outweighed their religious roles"
    (p. 243, Eaton). The evidence is a shrine functionary's deposition: from the early 1700s the
    Diwans "were inclined to make conquests, to maintain armies, and to build forts. They themselves
    stopped giving spiritual education and employed Maulvis, Imams, Khatibs and Mubilighs for the
    purpose" (pp. 243–244, n. 18, citing 2:278).
  - **The shrine's own social structure, as Eaton reconstructs it.** "At the bottom of the shrine's
    social hierarchy, then, were the peasant murids, 'we smaller people,' belonging for the most part
    to Jat agricultural clans in southwestern Punjab. At the top, of course, was the Diwan, the supreme
    patron of all ritual functions at the shrine and the chief dispenser of Baba Farid's blessings. In
    between these was an endogamous clan of family descendants of Baba Farid, the Chishti clan (named
    after the Sufi brotherhood to which the saint had belonged), which possessed both economic
    privileges and ritual status" (p. 248).
  - **Murids were excluded from the succession decision.** "Neither side in this case argued that the
    murids should have had any say in the matter, and in fact the evidence shows that the murids were
    clearly outside the decision-making process" (p. 249); a villager: "the [Chishti] biradri was at
    first consulted about the appointment of the plaintiff. The murids were never consulted, they only
    said Amin to the biradri's election" (p. 249, n. 40, citing 2, 18–19). A fifty-five-year-old Jat
    peasant asked why he had not signed the installation petition: "Prominent Pemres [text-layer
    garble] were made to sign, and we smaller people were not asked to sign" (p. 248, n. 34,
    citing 1:148).
  - **Clans claiming conversion by Baba Farid, from the depositions**: "the Wattu, the Siyal, the
    Dogar, the Kathia, the Bhatti, and the Tiwana" (p. 241, n. 9, citing 1:124, 138, 188, 225;
    2:80, 87) — "many of the major endogamous clans that are spread throughout the southwestern
    Punjab, mainly as peasant agriculturalists" (p. 241). Two quoted statements: a fifty-four-year-old
    cultivator of Lyallpur District, "Our Tiwana tribe was converted by Baba Farid. Since then our
    relation of discipleship has been continued with the gadd[i]" (p. 241, n. 10, citing 2:87); and
    another Lyallpur farmer, "Baba Farid converted us Bhattis into Islam. Since that time we are the
    followers of the gaddi and respect the gaddinashin [i.e., sajjada-nishin, or Diwan] in office"
    (p. 241, n. 11, citing 2:80). A Wattu villager: "Khewa, one of my ancestors, was converted into
    Islam by Baba Farid, and since that time we have been following the gaddi in dispute" (p. 248,
    n. 35, citing 2:64).
  - **Universalizing claim by one devotee**: "All the M[uh]ammadans are the Murids of Baba Farid… and
    so is my family" (p. 248, n. 37, citing 1:205).
  - **The Diwan as Baba Farid's living representative.** A forty-year-old landholder of Ferozepore
    District: "I believe in the sanctity of the sayings, commands, or revelations of Baba Farid…
    Baba Farid's command can never be incorrect. As the Sajjada Nashin in office is a representative
    of Baba Farid, the same sanctity and reverence is shown to the Gaddi Nashin as is shown to Baba
    Farid" (p. 247, n. 32, citing 1:220). One witness called the first four successors to the Prophet
    "gaddi-nishins" rather than caliphs (p. 247, n. 33, citing 1:150).

- **golra-sharif** (as "Golra Sharif in Rawalpindi District"): named in the sajjada-nishin's list of
  shrines subordinate to Pakpattan (p. 242, n. 12). Nothing else is said about it. The chunk gives no

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The two-tier lineage system — the chunk's central economic mechanism.** The Chishti clan
  "possessed both economic privileges and ritual status vis-à-vis the local [text lost here]" (p. 248).
  Eaton's evidence is a colonial settlement document: the Assessment Report for the Pakpattan region
  describes the Chishtis as "a 'semi-religious Mussalman tribe'" having "'considerable local
  influence'" and who "'are not working agriculturalists, but depend for cultivation entirely on
  tenants'" (p. 248, n. 38, citing P. J. Fagan, *Assessment Report for Pakpattan Tahsil, Montgomery
  District, 1897* (Lahore, 1898), 50). His conclusion: "As these tenants were drawn from among Jat
  clans whose members were murids of the shrine, what we see here is a system embracing two tiers of
  lineages: an upper tier comprising the Chishti clan, and a lower tier comprising a number of
  subordinate lineages linked religiously to the shrine as murids, and economically to the Chishti
  clan as tenant farmers" (p. 248). **Eaton limits the claim geographically himself:** "Away from the
  immediate area around Pakpattan, this connection was probably religious only, and not economic,
  since the dominant Chishti clan, many of whose members were landholders, were traditionally
  clustered around the town of Pakpattan" (pp. 248–249, n. 39). The word "probably" is his.
- **Nazrana at initiation.** A murid's account of his own *bai'at*: "I was made [a] disciple some 18
  years back. I was made to go through the following ceremonies:—my hand was placed in the hand of the
  Gaddi Nashin. I offered Nazrana, and I was given spiritual instructions to lead a truthful and noble
  life. I was also given a 'Wazifa' for my recitation" (p. 251, n. 47, citing 1:109). No amount is
  given for an ordinary murid's nazrana (not stated).
- **Nazrana at the dastar-bandi, with figures — and from Hindus.** "Hindu civic leaders and wealthy
  merchants also attended, and even participated." Ganpat Rai testified that "on the dastarbandi of
  the defendant nazranas were paid [by] us on behalf of the Mandi [market]" (p. 253, n. 51, citing
  2:174). Devi Bakhsh, a Khatri, "attended the ceremony and offered the new Diwan a turban and
  twenty-one rupees cash" (p. 253, n. 52, citing 2:224). Ram Rakha Mal "attended the ceremony, offering
  a turban and eleven rupees" (p. 253, n. 53, citing 2:228). A Hindu witness described the attendance:
  "there was a large number of persons including Zamindars [landholders], the gentry, and the
  sajjadanashins present" (p. 253, n. 54). The amounts are printed as words, not digits.
- **Cash payments to village pirs**, replicating the shrine's income stream at the village level
  (p. 242, n. 15).
- **Land granted *by* the shrine** to a Dipalpur shrine by an earlier Diwan, producing acknowledged
  subordination (p. 242, n. 13).
- **Salaried religious employees.** From the early 1700s the Diwans "employed Maulvis, Imams, Khatibs
  and Mubilighs" to give the spiritual education they had stopped giving themselves (pp. 243–244,
  n. 18) — religious labour bought in while the custodians pursued conquest, armies and forts.
- **Custodianship and the succession rule, contested in open court.** The defendant's side: succession
  by "de facto primogeniture… accorded with the shrine's traditional custom", and since "the shari'a is
  not generally interpreted as sanctioning primogeniture", his lawyers argued "'this Gaddi follows
  custom and not Mohammadan Law'" (p. 245, n. 22, citing 1:12); they also produced "a wealth of
  historical evidence showing that previous Diwans had succeeded to the shrine's leadership as minors,
  though according to most interpretations the shari'a would not have sanctioned such succession"
  (p. 245). The plaintiff's side: he "claimed to have been 'elected' Diwan by the Chishti [biradari],
  which is the brotherhood of family descendants of Baba Farid", arguing "'The gaddi in dispute is an
  Islamic gaddi… It is governed by Mohammadan Law and not by custom'", that the fittest man is selected
  by assembly "'as was in vogue during the time of Khulafa-i Rashedin'", and "'We, the descendants of
  Baba Farid, claim to be the descendants directly from Hazr[a]t Umar, the second Caliph'" (p. 245,
  n. 23, citing 2:95).
- **The Court's resolution.** It "saw the [shrine] basically as a local institution governing its
  affairs by its own traditional custom, but that, conveniently, this traditional custom happened to
  coincide with the shari'a since 'Muhammadan Law itself reserves [customary] usage as the primary rule
  for governing succession to a religious institution'" (pp. 245–246, n. 27, citing 3:254), and it cited
  jurisprudence "to show that the shari'a even allows a shrine's leader to name his own successor"
  (p. 246, n. 28).
- **Devotees agreed with the compromise.** A fifty-two-year-old villager: "The gaddi in dispute is [an]
  Islamic gaddi, but is governed by custom. The gaddinashin [Diwan] must be of his father's caste, the
  mother's caste is immaterial. A Dewan can appoint whether he receives a revelation or not. But a
  revelation is generally received" (p. 246, n. 29, citing 2:138).
- **Endogamy as a control instrument, with a dated document.** A faction of the Chishti clan issued on
  "16 February 1921" a notice that the descendants of "Hazrat Shah Ala-ud-Din Mauj Darya … grandson of
  Hazrat Baba Sahib" are "the most honourable and distinguished of all the descendants of Farid", have
  intermarried among themselves, and "though they took girls of other families in marriage, yet they did
  not give away their daughters in marriage in other families for the last five or six centuries…
  Whoever of the descendants of Mauj Darya gives his daughter in marriage in [another] family, should be
  excommunicated from the brotherhood, and it is also improper to maintain connections of any sort with
  him" (p. 249, n. 41, citing 4:27). The document justified itself from "'traditions from the holy
  Prophet (peace be on him) in regard to prohibition from abandonment of one's own family in matters of
  matrimonial relations'" (pp. 249–250, n. 42). The judge observed that the former Diwan's having "given
  his own daughter in marriage outside the lineage constituted one of the ultimate causes of the entire
  dispute, since that act had touched off the profound discord within the Chishti baradari that erupted
  only after the former Diwan died in 1934" (p. 250, n. 42, citing 3:298).
- **Bai'at (initiation).** "Belonging to a kin group that at some distant point in time had been
  converted to Islam by Baba Farid did not automatically make one a murid of the shrine. One still had
  to participate in a formal initiation ceremony during which one made a solemn oath, known as bai'at,
  swearing spiritual allegiance to Baba Farid and his spiritual descendants, including of course the
  incumbent Diwan" (p. 251). Once in a lifetime, per one devotee (p. 251, n. 45, citing 2:39). Recorded
  ages at initiation: "one was age twenty-eight, one eighteen, one fourteen or fifteen, one ten or
  twelve, and one said simply that he was a young child" (p. 251, n. 46, citing 1:109, 199, 160, 222,
  186). Elements: hand placed in the Diwan's hand, nazrana, spiritual instruction, a *wazifa* — "a
  prescribed liturgical formula, often a verse from the Qur'an" (p. 251). Three murids "reported having
  their forelocks ([j]at) clipped by the Diwan as part of their bai'at ceremony" (p. 251, n. 48, citing
  1:160, 162).
- **Dastar-bandi (installation).** Held on "the fortieth day after the death of the previous Diwan",
  coinciding with the *chehlum*, "thereby balancing the grief over the loss of a departed Diwan with the
  joy over the installation of a new one" (p. 252). Purpose, per the court record: Q. "What is the aim
  and object of the Dastar bandi performed on the fortieth day?" A. "So that all may come to know that
  such and such person has succeeded" (p. 250, n. 43, citing 3:110). The candidate's "whole head was
  shaved" (p. 252, n. 49, citing 1:170). On stage: "the family of the Diwan, the most respected members
  of the Chishti clan, resident mystics living at the shrine, and sajjada-nishins of all of Baba Farid's
  'daughter' shrines in the Punjab" (p. 252). **The rank rule:** "Only somebody of equal or higher
  spiritual rank than the new sajjada-nishin could perform this ceremony… since there was no one within
  the shrine's own hierarchy possessing a higher rank than that of the Diwan-elect, this ceremony was
  normally performed by the head of an outside shrine having at least equal if not m[o]re spiritual
  authority" — so heads of subordinate shrines attended as guests and only a superior shrine's head
  performed it (p. 252). In 1935 that was the sajjada-nishin of Ajmer (p. 252). Murids massed as an
  "off-stage audience… in the shrine's spacious courtyard in front of Baba Farid's tomb" (pp. 252–253).
- **'Urs / Bihishti mela.** "the anniversary of Baba Farid's death was celebrated in a vast pageant
  known in 1938 as the Bihishti mela" (p. 253). Eaton glosses 'urs as "'marriage with God'", so that it
  "commemorates not the saint's death so much as his everlasting union with God, and hence his spiritual
  survival for purpose of passing on his grace, or baraka, to his devotees" (p. 253). **"Called the
  bihish[ti] darw[a]za, or 'Gate of heaven,' the south door of Baba Farid's tomb remained closed on all
  days of the year until the date of Baba Farid's 'urs, the fifth of Muharram"** (p. 253) — the sentence
  breaks off there at the end of the chunk. No attendance figure or revenue figure for the mela
  (not stated).
- **The spiritual chain as an institution.** "establishing and maintaining a graded, spiritual hierarchy
  descending from God through the Prophet Muhammad and Baba Farid to the Diwan and from him to his
  khalifas (spiritual adepts) and murids (spiritual affiliates)", with "a perceived flow of grace, or
  baraka, from God to the murid in which process the shrine is critical since it represents the visible,
  tangible point at which this grace is localized" (p. 247).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The succession dream.** The former Diwan Sa'id Muhammad was held, by his son's party and ultimately
  by the Court, to have "received a dream in which Baba Farid revealed to him that his son Qutb al-Din
  should succeed him as Diwan" (p. 243); the judge found the revelation proved (p. 243, n. 17). Reported
  by Eaton from the court record, not endorsed by him.
- **Revelation as normal practice**, per a villager witness: "A Dewan can appoint whether he receives a
  revelation or not. But a revelation is generally received" (p. 246, n. 29).
- **The Prophet's four principles**, as a fifty-seven-year-old villager attributed them to Muhammad:
  "Shariat is my word, Tar[i]qat is my actions, Haq[i]qat is my conditions, and Ma'rifat is my mystery"
  (p. 247, n. 31, citing 2:43). Eaton notes the irony that here "Muhammad the Prophet was… cast generally
  in a Sufi light, [while] Baba Farid the historical Sufi was cast as an infall[i]ble revealer of God's
  essence and a commander of God's Law" (p. 247).
- **Conversion of whole clans by Baba Farid** — carried here as the depositions' claim (p. 241,
  nn. 9–11; p. 248, n. 35), and reinterpreted rather than endorsed by Eaton (see Arguments).
- **Ritually entering heaven at the 'urs** — implicit in the *bihishti darwaza*, "Gate of heaven"
  (p. 253); the chunk breaks off before describing the passage itself.

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The conversion claim is resolved, not dismissed — and this reverses the emphasis of chunk 015.**
  "Now we know that most of those Jat or Rajput clans claiming conversion at the hand of Baba Farid were
  not in fact anywhere near the Pakpattan region in the thirteenth century when Baba Farid himself was
  living, meaning that in any literal sense such an event could not have occurred. But since, as is
  clear from these depositions, saint and shrine are conceptually fused in terms of religious function,
  no real contradiction exists. As the tribes were converted at some point in medieval history by the
  agency of the shrine, so also were they converted by Baba Farid" (p. 241). **The shrine, not the man,
  is the agent of conversion** — Eaton's own conclusion, and the positive explanation that chunk 015's
  argument broke off before reaching. The mechanism is not spelled out on this page (not stated here).
- **A shrine's authority is measured by the shrines it outranks.** Eaton takes the witness's list and the
  etiquette of ritual attendance as evidence that "The spiritual potency, or baraka, of Baba Farid's
  shrine thus flowed out over the Punjab, infusing a series of subordinate shrines with sanctity"
  (p. 242), and that the hierarchy was reproduced through descent-founded daughter shrines and, in one
  case, a land grant (p. 242).
- **Litigation in a colonial court de-politicized the shrine.** "the resolution of the shrine's internal
  conflicts in an Anglo-Indian court of law seemed to promote the popular perception of the shrine as a
  religious institution only, and to diminish perception of it as a political institution" (p. 243).
  Evidence from both directions: older murids "tended to see the Diwan's traditional function as both
  religious and political", some calling the incumbent a "pishwa, or leader, 'who leads us on the
  material and spiritual lines'" (p. 244, n. 19, citing 2:240–1); while "other murids sensed a sharp
  separation between the Diwan's religious and political authority, and saw the Diwan and the shrine as
  greatly reduced in their political authority" (p. 244). A Jat murid of Bahawalpur: "The Khulafa-i
  Rashedin, who were four in number, were both political and spiritual and religious heads… On the
  political side, there is now no Khalifa in British India, only the British Government governs the
  country. The spiritual system was taken up by Baba Farid Shakar Ganj" (p. 244, n. 20, citing 2:288).
  Eaton's inference: "The fact that 'only the British Government governs the country' had as its natural
  corollary, then, the enhancement of the shrine's religious status in the eyes of its devotees" (p. 244).
- **The contradiction of imperial jurisprudence, stated as such.** "Here was an Anglo-Indian civil court
  deciding, according to British legal procedures, a dispute between two Muslims over which one of them,
  according to Islamic Law as well as local custom, should legitimately succeed to the leadership of a
  Muslim shrine" (p. 243). Being drawn in, "the Anglo-Indian courts were forced to make decisions that
  were strictly religious in nature: for example, that the former Diwan did in fact have a revelation
  from Baba Farid. The case would seem, therefore, to have posed theoretical difficulties for a ruling
  British government that professed to steer clear from interference with India's religious matters"
  (p. 243).
- **The Court's ruling read as political expediency, not jurisprudence.** "The Court, in other words, had
  it both ways. It saw the shrine at once as a local institution that ruled by its own custom, and as a
  Muslim institution whose traditions conformed with Islamic Law. In retrospect, this formula would seem
  to have provided the Court with a practical means of settling the dispute: the shrine could continue
  using its own customary practices, which the ruling authorities would simply declare as conforming with
  Islamic Law. In 1938, with the forces of Islamic reform and Muslim separatism rising on all sides in
  British India, the government could hardly have taken the politically dangerous step of declaring that
  the most popular Islamic shrine in the western Punjab was not, after all, Islamic" (p. 246). The
  "would seem" is Eaton's own hedge.
- **Why the compromise satisfied devotees.** "This view would seem to have allowed devotees to follow
  familiar religious practices without facing the disturbing thought that they were in any way violating
  the dictates of the shari'a. On the other hand many of the urban 'ulama', for whom the whole culture of
  shrines and saints smacked of idolatry, would have flatly rejected this position. But this was 1938,
  the very twilight of British India. And though the demand for Pakistan was becoming increasingly
  strident, the 'ulama' were not yet in positions of effective power" (p. 246).
- **The shrine produced a social hierarchy as well as a spiritual one.** "it is evident from the documents
  of the Court's proceedings that the shrine had the objective function of establishing and maintaining
  not only a spiritual hierarchy, but also a social hierarchy as between the Diwan and his innermost
  family, the Chishti clan, and the many agricultural clans who as murids constituted the shrine's lay
  affiliates and traditional supporters. But these hierarchies were maintained not just by the fact of
  birth-ascribed kin groups or the marital policies of elite groups at the shrine. A series of elaborate
  initiatory and commemorative rituals had the effect of reinforcing existing hierarchal arrangements,
  while at the same time providing expressive meaning and cognitive order in the religious world of the
  shrine's devotees" (p. 250). **Ritual is thus assigned a distributive and stratifying function, not
  only an expressive one** — the load-bearing claim for a welfare-provision argument.
- **Hindu social forms inside a Muslim institution, argued from the marriage rule.** "It is well known
  that a rigid control over marital contracts, especially with respect to bride-giving, is a key mechanism
  by which the Hindu social order maintains its system of endogamous castes ranked in a graded hierarchy.
  The influence of Hindu social concepts on the functioning of the Chishti clan's social relations is
  therefore obvious" (p. 249). Eaton finds it "even more remarkable" that the 1921 statement "appealed to
  Islamic Law for its justification" (p. 249). The same move is made for hair: forelock-clipping "follows
  a basic Indian paradigm of religious initiation in which such clipping symbolizes for the initiate an
  avowal of an ascetic way of life", and since murids took no ascetic vows it "must either be a remnant
  from earlier times… or, as is more likely the case, it was borrowed from Hindu-Buddhist initiatory
  practices" (p. 251). Both are inferences by Eaton; no source asserts the borrowing.
- **The theatre metaphor returns, now via Geertz.** The dastar-bandi answer "hints at what is perhaps the
  most important on-going business of the shrine, namely, the projection of a dramatized image of the
  sacred order into this world. In Clifford Geertz's terms, the shrine provided its client clans of murids
  with a tiny 'theatre-state' of its own" (p. 250, n. 44, citing Geertz, *Islam Observed* (Chicago:
  University of Chicago Press, 1971), 38, and *Negara: The Theater State in Nineteenth-Century Bali*
  (Princeton University Press, 1980), "especially chapter four"). "Through the ceremonies and celebrations
  that marked its liturgical calendar, the shrine displayed the pageantry, order and beauty of the Court of
  God, albeit on a microcosmic scale" (p. 250) — the phrase the essay is titled for. Note "**client
  clans**": the relation is named as clientage.
- **Hindu participation read as integration, with a caveat.** "As Hindus, these gentlemen were probably not
  offering nazrana out of expectations consonant with the Muslim-oriented conceptual structure of the
  shrine, but out of respect for a local institution of great repute, and perhaps also out of a sense of
  civic responsibility… Nonetheless, the presence of Hindus participating in this event suggests how, in
  the past, generations of people of various castes and creeds had become integrated into the public
  aspects of the shrine's activities" (p. 253). Eaton calls this "one of its oldest and historically most
  significant roles" and refers to his own Baba Farid essay "in the present volume" (p. 253, n. 55). Both
  "probably" and "suggests" are his.
- **Collective rather than individual self-description.** "the murids almost without exception identified
  themselves not as individuals but in terms of a collective group, be that their kin group (baradari) or
  the collective mass of murids" (p. 248), with the two varieties illustrated by the Wattu deposition and
  "we Jat people" (p. 248, nn. 35–36) against "All the M[uh]ammadans are the Murids of Baba Farid"
  (p. 248, n. 37).
- **Pointers to other literature**, given by Eaton rather than argued: David Gilmartin, "Shrines,
  Succession, and Sources of Moral Authority", in Barbara Metcalf, *Moral Conduct and Authority: The Place
  of Adab in South Asian Islam* (Berkeley and Los Angeles: University of California Press, 1984), 221–40,
  on "the intrusion of British power in Punjabi shrines and the implications this had for the modes of
  shrine leadership" (p. 244, n. 21); and Kathy [Katherine] Ewing, "The Politics of Sufism: Redefining the
  Saints of Pakistan", *Journal of Asian Studies* 42, no. 2 (Feb. 1983), 251–68, on "how the Government of
  Pakistan has dealt with the culture of shrines and saints since 1947" (p. 246, n. 30).

## Citable passages

- "what we see here is a system embracing two tiers of lineages: an upper tier comprising the Chishti clan,
  and a lower tier comprising a number of subordinate lineages linked religiously to the shrine as murids,
  and economically to the Chishti clan as tenant farmers." (p. 248)
- The Chishtis "are not working agriculturalists, but depend for cultivation entirely on tenants" — Fagan's
  1897 Assessment Report for Pakpattan Tahsil, quoted at p. 248.
- The Diwans from the early 1700s "were inclined to make conquests, to maintain armies, and to build forts.
  They themselves stopped giving spiritual education and employed Maulvis, Imams, Khatibs and Mubilighs for
  the purpose." (pp. 243–244)
- "the government could hardly have taken the politically dangerous step of declaring that the most popular
  Islamic shrine in the western Punjab was not, after all, Islamic." (p. 246)
- "the shrine provided its client clans of murids with a tiny 'theatre-state' of its own." (p. 250)

## Doubts

- **'Ala' ad-Din Mauj Darya appears again and is again NOT the archive's Lahore row.** The 1921 endogamy
  document names "Hazrat Shah Ala-ud-Din Mauj Darya … grandson of Hazrat Baba Sahib", i.e. Baba Farid's
  grandson and second Diwan of Pakpattan, whose descendants form the Chishti clan's senior faction (p. 249).
  The archive's **shrine-of-mauj-darya-bukhari** is Hazrat Meeran Muhammad Shah Bukhari of Edward Road,
  Anarkali, Lahore, **died 1604** — a different man, city, century and tomb. **Recorded here as a flagged
  collision, not a match;** this material belongs to shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar. This is the same
  collision chunk 015 flagged, and it is now attested in a second essay, which strengthens the case that
  chunk 013's bullet filed under the Lahore id should be re-filed at consolidation.
- **"Uch Sharif in Bahawalpur State" (p. 242) cannot be assigned to one archive row.** The archive holds
  five Uch Sharif rows — **shrine-of-jalaluddin-surkh-posh-bukhari-jalaluddin-bukhari**,
  **shrine-of-makhdoom-jahaniyan-jahangasht**, **tomb-of-javindi-bibi**, **tomb-of-baha-al-halim-uch-sharif**
  and **tomb-of-ustad-nuriya** — and the witness names only the town, with no saint (not stated). Do not
  attach the subordination claim to any one of them.
- **"the shrines of Kasur… in Lahore District" (p. 242) is likewise unassignable.** The archive's
  **mazar-of-bulleh-shah** is at Kasur, but the chunk names no saint and uses the plural "shrines"
  (p. 242). No link should be inferred.
- **The two matched rows carry district discrepancies against the archive.** The witness places "Golra
  Sharif in Rawalpindi District" and "Taunsa Sharif in Dera Ghazi Khan District" (p. 242), where the archive
  gives Islamabad Capital Territory for **golra-sharif** and Punjab/Taunsa for
  **darbar-hazrat-khawaja-shah-muhammad-sulaiman-taunsvi-r-a**. These are 1930s administrative
  descriptions, and the chunk gives no saint's name for either, so the identification rests on place name
  alone. A human should confirm before either entry cites the subordination claim.
- **"Basal Sharif", "Mukhan Sharif", "Piran Kaler", "Lakhneke", "Kastiwala"** — none is in the archive and
  none is given a saint (p. 242). Some are in present-day Pakistan (Campbellpore = Attock) and may be
  future rows, but the chunk supports nothing beyond the name.
