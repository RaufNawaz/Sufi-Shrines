## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar** (Eaton: "Farid al-Din Ganj-i Shakar", "popularly known as
  'Baba Farid'"; the shrine at Pakpattan, Punjab):
  - **Death date given as 1265**: "that of Farid al-Din Ganj-i Shakar (d. 1265)" (p. 16). The
    archive row carries **1266**. One year apart; see Doubts.
  - Eaton calls it "perhaps the most famous such tomb-shrine in the Punjab" (p. 16).
  - **Two whole essays in this book are about this one shrine** — Section 3, Punjab: ch. 9 "The
    Political and Religious Authority of the Shrine of Baba Farid" (printed pp. 203ff.) and
    ch. 10 "Court of Man, Court of God: Local Perceptions of the Shrine of Baba Farid,
    Pakpattan, Punjab" (printed pp. 225ff.) (p. 9).
  - **Chapter 9's question, in his own framing**: it "seeks connections between the processes of
    Islamization and peasantization in the western Punjab. Specifically, I ask why and how it
    was that Jat tribesmen, as they abandoned pastoral nomadism for settled agriculture between
    the thirteenth and nineteenth centuries, gradually took on an Islamic identity" (p. 16).
  - **Chapter 10's evidence base, datable and archival**: the essay is "based on legal
    depositions given in the context of a **1938 court dispute over the rightful control of the
    same shrine**" (p. 16). This is a named class of record for the shrine's custodianship
    history; the dispute year is 1938.
  - **Chapter 10's question**: how "Islam, a religion par excellence 'of the Book,' [has] been
    mediated among illiterate villagers, and how has the shrine served to promote a conceptual
    conflation of Heaven and Earth among Muslims of rural Punjab?" (pp. 16–17).
  - **A map of the shrine's region is printed**: "Punjab circa 1605", at printed p. 214 (p. 10),
    i.e. inside the ch. 9 essay.
  - Original publication of ch. 9: "'The Political and Religious Authority of the Shrine of Baba
    Farid in Pakpattan, Punjab', in Barbara Metcalf, ed., *Moral Conduct and Authority: The
    Place of 'Adab' in South Asian Islam* (Berkeley: University of California Press, 1982),
    333–56" (p. 11). Of ch. 10: "in Richard C. Martin, ed., *Islam in Local Contexts*, vol. 17

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The source classes the post-1950s historiography opened up — a list an archive of shrine
  provenance can use directly.** The new generation of historians "dredged up an impressive
  variety of source materials: commercial documents, **tax registers, official land grants**,
  administrative seals, census records, coins, **gravestones**, magical incantations written on
  bowls, **memoirs of pilgrims**, archaeological and architectural data, biographical
  dictionaries, inscriptional evidence, and most recently, **oral history**" (p. 25). Eaton's own
  Punjab essays draw on land grants and court depositions, so this is his stated method as well
  as his history of the field.
- **Sufi tomb-shrine cults as a distinct object of study.** Ch. 9 "shifts attention from the
  Deccan to the Punjab, and from Sufi literature to **the cults that coalesced around Sufi
  tomb-shrines**" (p. 16). The distinction between the literature and the shrine cult is his own
  organising division between chs. 8 and 9.
- **Qawwali music and "Islamic tomb-cults"** are named among the things Eaton recognised in South
  Asia as "refracted through the prism of Iran" (p. 13).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The book's core negative thesis: no religion is a fixed essence, so "spread" is the wrong
  verb.** "The essays also challenge the image of Islam or of any religion as a fixed or
  monolithic essence… religious traditions have been continuously redefined, reinterpreted, and
  contested, as competing social groups have risen or fallen in prominence and influence"
  (p. 14). The consequence he draws: "if religions are understood in terms of fixed or monolithic
  essences, then a phenomenon like religious conversion… can be seen only in terms of a
  tradition's one-way movement from a 'heartland' to a 'periphery'. Such thinking has given rise
  to **the flawed notion of the 'spread' or 'expansion' of Islam** and other religions in India,
  as though South Asia were an inert soil that only passively received the active seed of a
  religion imported from elsewhere" (p. 14). [The chunk prints "de seen" for "be seen" — a text-
  layer slip, not a variant.]
- **"Orthodox", "syncretic" and "fundamentalist" are declared useless as analytical categories.**
  "it becomes unproductive, or flatly wrong, to speak of one group as 'orthodox' and another as
  'unorthodox', or of one religious variant as 'fundamentalist' and another as, for example,
  'syncretic'. As rhetorical clubs, these labels might help identify competing social classes or
  factions in a given historical situation. But as analytical tools, they are quite useless"
  (p. 14). Directly relevant to how an archive labels shrine practice.
- **The demographic puzzle that drives the Punjab and Bengal essays.** "a glance at a map of South
  Asia's religious demography shows that **Islam took root much more readily in Bengal or the
  Punjab than in upper India, the area with the deepest traditions of Indo-Muslim rule and
  patronage**" (p. 14). His case studies are deliberately chosen from regions "peripheral to
  upper India's imperial heartland, that is, the Delhi Doab", so that the volume's implicit
  concern is "the dynamics of metropolis-frontier relations" (pp. 13–14). Also put as: "how did
  it happen that, worldwide, more Muslims came to live east of Karachi, than west of that city?"
  (p. 13).
- **His stated objection to the Sufi-agency literature: it names an agent but never a
  mechanism.** "The remaining essays address Islamization in South Asia, a process in which the
  secondary literature identifies Sufis as principal agents. **Few scholars, however, have
  specified the precise mechanisms** by which a small number of mystics or holymen might have
  effected such dramatic social transformations as the wholesale conversion of entire
  populations. Nor have scholars considered how an elite body of literate mystics could have
  influenced, or even cared about, the non-literate masses they are said to have converted"
  (p. 16). This is the gap chs. 8, 9 and 11 are written to fill.
- **The romanticised account of Bengali conversion is rejected for want of evidence.** The delta
  "has long posed a challenge to explaining Islamization in terms that might go beyond the
  simplistic notion, found repeatedly in the secondary literature, of **pious Sufis preaching love
  and equality among an underclass oppressed by Brahmanical Hinduism. No contemporary evidence
  supports this facile and somewhat romanticized view of India's past**" (p. 17).
- **Islamization is to be explained as a by-product of peasantization, in both Punjab and
  Bengal.** Ch. 11 "explores linkages between peasantization and Islamization in the Bengal delta
  during the sixteenth to eighteenth centuries", contrasting "the style of Islamic piety imported
  by the imperial Mughals with that of a new Muslim peasantry, a class then forming in the
  eastern delta from amongst tribal groups that had been at best only partially integrated into
  the values of caste Hinduism" (p. 17); the theme is "sketched out in Chapter Nine in the context
  of the Punjab" (p. 17).
- **A gendered claim about who carried Islamic culture into the countryside.** Ch. 8 "advocates a
  gendered understanding of India's pre-modern social history, **highlighting the role of rural
  women in the diffusion of Islamic culture** in the region" (p. 16), and does so by "attempting
  to bridge the gap between the 'mystical' and 'folk' literature produced by Sufis of the
  medieval Deccan" (p. 16).
- **The end point he claims for the whole process**: "by the eighteenth century Islam in this part
  of the world had in many respects become **as thoroughly Indian as any other religion of South
  Asia**" (p. 17).
- **Historiographical argument of ch. 1, as evidence about the evidence.** Since the eleventh
  century "it was the fate of Islamic civilization to serve in the European imagination as a
  wholly alien 'other,' a historic and cultural foil in opposition to which Europeans came to
  define their own collective identity" (p. 22); the nineteenth-century philologists, "in their
  attempt to give definition to Muslim civilization… tended to present Islam as a 'tradition'
  that was static, timeless, and uniform, and by implication, impervious to the dynamics of
  change" (p. 23), and their concentration on the "formative eighth-to-eleventh centuries"
  "encouraged the belief that this particular period represented some sort of 'golden age', after
  which Islamic civilization was doomed to a slow and painful decline" — a notion which "suggested,
  in turn, that Europe's relatively easy conquest of Muslim societies… had been not only
  inevitable but justified" (p. 23).
- **The methodological turn he endorses, in one sentence.** "Whereas classical Islamicists had
  asked the question 'What can the text tell us of the civilization?' a new generation of
  historians began asking, 'What can the data tell us of the societies?'" (p. 23), under the
  influence of Marc Bloch and the *Annales* school and of anthropology (p. 24). The shift moved
  attention "from the literate elite classes from whose milieu the authors of the classical texts
  usually came, to those many other communities whom Eric Wolf has called the 'people without
  history'" (p. 24).
- **Why he distrusts chronicles — a warning applicable to shrine tazkiras.** Such texts "were
  frequently formal works written by Muslim chroniclers—many of them **in the pay of political
  leaders**—who were self-consciously writing about their own present or recent past with a view
  to posterity. Hence the texts such authors produced were deliberate constructions or
  reconstructions of people or events, carrying with them the same risks of bias, judgment,
  perspective, or interest" (p. 24).
- **Patricia Crone's evidence used to collapse an entire explanatory chain.** Against Watt and
  Shaban's socio-economic account of Islam's rise, "Patricia Crone has recently published
  considerable evidence showing that far from occupying the hub of a vast and expanding commercial
  network, **Mecca at the time of Muhammad was quite peripheral to world trade**… If Mecca was not
  the thriving commercial centre that most social historians had alleged it to be, then the entire
  sequence of sociological arguments that rest on that assumption… collapses" (p. 27). A model of
  the move Eaton makes repeatedly: attack the premise, not the conclusion.
