# eaton_essays_islam_indian_history — chunk 015 — pp. 212–227

The opening of **Section 3, "Punjab"** (p. 213) and the first thirteen pages of the essay
"**The Political and Religious Authority of the Shrine of Baba Farid**" (p. 215), "Reprinted from
Barbara Metcalf ed. *Moral Conduct and Authority: The Place of Adab in South Asian Islam*
(Berkeley: University of California Press, 1982) 333–56" (p. 215). This is the essay the manifest
flagged. The chunk runs from the essay's framing of shrines as "theatre" against the Book (p. 216),
through the ta'widh–futuh economy and the institution of the *diwan*-ship (pp. 217–219), Tughluq
patronage including the assignment of a whole city's revenues (pp. 220–221), the spatial *wilayat*
(p. 222), and then the long Jat pastoralism-to-agriculture argument that sets up the conversion
question (pp. 223–227). It ends mid-argument on p. 227, just after Table 9.1.

For the economics thesis the load-bearing pages are **218** (redistribution mechanism), **219**
(langar khana and 'urs as instituted practice), **220–221** (revenue alienation, *muafi*, villages
and lands confirmed), **222** (devotee-built memorial shrines; wilayat as territory) and **223**
(pastoralists dependent on riverine peoples for ritual) — the mechanism is stated in each case, not
just the conclusion.

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar** (as "the shrine of Baba Farid", "Shaikh Farid ad-Din Ganj-i
  Shakar (d. A. D. 1265)", the shrine of Ajudhan / Pakpattan):
  - Location: "lies on the right bank of the Punjab's most southeasterly river, the Sutlej, roughly
    halfway between Ferozepore and Bahawalpur" (pp. 216–217). "The town in which the shrine is
    located, known since the sixteenth century as Pakpattan, is the ancient city of Ajudhan"
    (p. 217).
  - The town's economic function before the saint: "As the principal ferry point on the Sutlej
    river, this town served from ancient times as a major nexus for east-west trade between the
    Delhi region and Multan" (p. 217).
  - The site was already Islamically equipped when he arrived "sometime in the early thirteenth
    century": "a Jami mosque had already been established there, a resident [q]adi was there
    administering justice according to Islamic law, and the city was politically subordinate to a
    Muslim governor in neighbouring Dipalpur" (p. 217, citing *Siyar al-arifin*, 33–4). The mosque
    is "now part of the shrine complex" and its "large size… attests to the presence of a
    substantial Muslim population at that time" (p. 223, citing Chaghatai, 134–5).
  - Two distinct constituencies in the saint's own lifetime: full-time residents of the convent
    (*jama'at khana*, printed "jamd'a.khana") "initiated into the Chishti order, who lived a
    communal life of a strongly ascetic nature"; and the "more popular-oriented" tradition of the
    masses who received amulets (p. 217).
  - **The amulet-and-gift economy.** Baba Farid "handed out ta'widh or amulets to the common
    masses, who saw in these ta'widh a protection against evil, a boon for good fortune, or an
    agent for the cure of an illness" (p. 217). "Huge crowds gathered daily at Baba Farid's convent
    to receive ta'widh", normally "a scrap of paper on which were written the names of God or some
    Qur'anic verses"; the work's "tediousness… compelled the saint to delegate much of the work to
    assistants" (p. 218). In return the devotee "would frequently offer to the shrine some kind of
    gift, called futah [*futuh*]… typically… in the form of sweets, but could be almost anything,
    and even in Baba Farid's own day the gift swelled to enormous proportions" (p. 218, citing
    *Fawa'id al-fu'ad* via Nizami).
  - **Continuity to the present**: "even now the successors of Baba Farid and his assistants, in
    common with those of other shrines in the Punjab, continue to write ta'widh for the masses"
    (p. 218). Written 1982 (see the reprint line, p. 215); no date of observation is given
    (not stated).
  - Death and the founding of the complex: "In 1265 Baba Farid died, and with his death began the
    career of the vast shrine complex based on his tomb, in time encompassing a mosque, a welfare
    kitchen (dangar khana [*langar khana*]), and a number of related buildings" (p. 218).
  - **Hereditary succession established immediately.** "the position of prime successor to Baba
    Farid, later called the 'diwan,' fell to his son, Badr ad-Din Sulaiman (1265–81)"; his
    accession "immediately set a pattern of hereditary religious leadership at the shrine"
    (p. 219). Footnote: "although every diwan of the shrine down to the present has been the eldest
    son of his predecessor, Baba Farid's own first and second sons were passed over in favour of
    Badr ad-Din, who was the third son", and he alone did not receive the office from his
    predecessor but "directly by the Chishti elders in Chisht, western Afghanistan" (p. 219, n. 9,
    citing *Siyar al-auliya'*, 188).
  - **All the shrine's major rituals are dated to the first successor** by the *Jawahir-i Faridi*
    (compiled 1623): the *dastar bandi* or "tying on of the turban… indicating formal inheritance
    of Baba Farid's spiritual authority"; "the regularizing of ecstatic singing (qawwa[li]) at the
    shrine"; "the establishing of a public kitchen (langar khana) from which the diwan himself
    would, on formal occasions, direct the distribution of food and sweets"; and the diwan "opening
    the southern door (shisha darwaza) of Baba Farid's tomb on the occasion of the shrine's annual
    'urs celebration, allowing the masses of common devotees to pass by the shrine's sanctum
    sanctorum, ritually entering heaven" (p. 219, n. 11, citing *Jawahir-i Faridi*, 298–300).
  - **The 1315 'urs, described by an eyewitness-era source.** Amir Khusrau "mentioned the annual
    'urs or death-date celebration on the occasion of the fiftieth anniversary of the shaikh's
    death and described in detail the 'urs celebration for the year 1315: the pilgrimage of pious
    persons to Ajudhan, the recitation of the saint's wonderful deeds, and the entertainments
    performed by an ensemble of darweshes" (p. 219, n. 10, citing *Raha al-muhibbin*, Urdu ed.
    Lahore 1957, 63–4, via Chaghatai, *Iqbal Review* 9 (1968)). No attendance figure (not stated).
  - **Baba Farid himself refused the court.** He "assiduously avoided contact with the mundane world
    of the court and its ministers" (p. 219); Barani has him warning a Sufi leaving Ajudhan for
    Delhi: "Have nothing to do with maliks and amirs, and beware of their intimacy as dangerous; no
    darwesh ever kept up such intimacy, but in the end found it disastrous" (p. 219, n. 12, via
    Elliot and Dowson, 3:144).
  - **Court patronage arrives with the Tughluqs.** The Khalaji [Khalji] sultans (1288–1321) "do not
    seem to have been interested in the shrine"; "the whole picture changed with the advent of the
    Tughluq period (1321–98)" (p. 220).
  - **A whole city's revenues assigned to the shrine.** Ibn Battuta "wrote that Sultan Muhammad ibn
    Tughluq had bestowed the city of Ajudhan [up]on the shrine. This seems to be the earliest
    reference to the court's alienation of local revenues in favour of the shrine's support"
    (p. 220, n. 16, citing *Rehla*, trans. Mahdi Husain, Baroda 1953, 20).
  - **An endowment specifically for the kitchen.** In Diwan Mu'izz ad-Din's "brief successorship
    (1335–38), Muhammad ibn Tughluq granted an endowment or muafi [printed "muafin"] support of the
    shrine's public kitchen" (p. 221, n. 22, citing *Jawahir-i Faridi*, 308).
  - **Repairs plus confirmation of landholding.** "Barani recorded that Sultan Fir[u]z Tughluq
    (1351–88) not only repaired the tomb of Baba Farid, but 'granted robes of honour to his
    descendants and confirmed them in possession of their villages and lands.'" (p. 221, n. 23,
    citing Barani, *Tarikh-i F[i]roz Shahi* (Calcutta, 1862), 543, via Rose, *Glossary*, 1:495)
  - **The shrine's custodians absorbed into the state.** Mu'izz ad-Din "was even called to Delhi by
    Muhammad ibn Tughluq, placed in government service, and sent to Gujarat as deputy governor",
    and "The diwan's brother, meanwhile, was appointed to the office of Shaikh al-Islam of India"
    (p. 221, nn. 20–21). Eaton: "Never were the affairs of the shrine more firmly welded to court
    interests. The shrine's leaders were now under Delhi's control, and the shrine became even more
    dependent economically upon court patronage" (p. 221).
  - **The grandson's tomb dominates the complex.** "soon after 'Ala' ad-Din's death in 1335, Sultan
    Muhammad commissioned two engineers to construct what proved to be one of the finest examples
    of Tughluq architecture in the subcontinent. It is also the most imposing structure in the
    entire shrine complex, dwarfing by far Baba Farid's own tomb" (p. 221). Its date "comes from an
    inscription on the tomb itself—Safar, 737, which corresponds to Sept./Oct., A.D. 1336"
    (p. 221, n. 19; architectural discussion attributed to Ahmad Nabi Khan, 'The Mausoleum of
    Shaikh 'Ala al-Din at Pakpattan (Punjab)', *East and West* 24, nos 3–4 (1974): 324–5, cited
    p. 220, n. 13).
  - **The shrine's fame reached Egypt.** Ibn Battuta's Alexandria prediction and his 1334 visit
    show "that Baba Farid, the saint, was now clearly identified with the physical shrine complex,
    and that his (its) fame had spread as far as Egypt" (pp. 221–222).
  - **Satellite shrines built by the poor, not the state.** "small memorial shrines to Baba Farid
    began appearing, scattered throughout the countryside of central Punjab"; "what is significant
    is that they were built not by the Tughluq sultans as were the main structures of the Ajudhan
    complex, but by the common people themselves" (p. 222). Amir Khurd, a contemporary of 'Ala'
    ad-Din: "in the countryside around Ajudhan, Dipalpur, and in the hills toward Kashmir, the
    people out of love and belief have built structures and tombs in the name of his shrine…and
    they go to these villages for alms and devotions" (p. 222, n. 25, citing *Siyar al-auliya'*,
    193).
  - **Conversion claimed by clans, doubted by Eaton.** "of those Jat and Rajput groups that became
    Muslim in the medieval period, the vast majority of them claim to have been converted either by
    Baba Farid or by his contemporary, Baha' al-Haqq Zakariya (d. 1263)" (p. 227); Table 9.1 lists
    the clans and their sources (p. 227, transcribed below). Eaton's own position is against the
    claim — see Arguments.

- **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya** (as "Shaikh Baha' ad-Din Zakariya", p. 222, and "Baha' al-Haqq
  Zakariya (d. 1263), whose tomb is in Multan city", p. 227):
  - His *wilayat* bordered Baba Farid's, and the border was described physically: Baba Farid told a
    traveller "from here [Ajudhan] to such-and-such a village is in my charge, and from
    such-and-such a reservoir is the frontier of Shaikh Baha' ad-Din Zakariya [beyond which] is in
    his charge" (p. 222, n. 26, citing *Siyar al-arifin*, 115, described as "an
    early-sixteenth-century hagiography").
  - He is named, with Baba Farid, as one of the two saints to whom "the vast majority" of medieval
    Jat and Rajput Muslim converts credit their conversion; his death year is given here as 1263
    and his tomb placed "in Multan city" (p. 227). **The archive row gives 1267 — see Doubts.**

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- **Shaikh 'Ala' ad-Din Mauj Darya**, second diwan, Baba Farid's grandson, "Badr ad-Din's eldest
  son", dated "(1281–1334)" at p. 219 but with death "in 1335" at p. 221: he "firmly established a
  tradition of hereditary religious leadership associated with Baba Farid's shrine and witnessed

  to the door of the Jami mosque, and if kings would come, he would not budge from his place"
  (p. 220, n. 15, *Siyar al-auliya'*, 194) — against which Eaton sets Ibn Battuta's revenue report.
  **This is the figure who collides with the archive's Lahore "Mauj Darya" row; see Doubts, and note
  that this chunk resolves the identity of the footnoted figure in chunk 013.**
- **Diwan Badr ad-Din Sulaiman (1265–81)**, first diwan, third son (p. 219 and n. 9).
- **Diwan Mu'izz ad-Din (1335–38)**, third diwan (p. 221).
- **Ghiyath ad-Din Tughluq**, while governor of Dipalpur, "became one of the many local notables
  attracted to the spiritual power and piety of Diwan 'Ala' ad-Din Mauj Darya" and "made frequent

- **Shaikh Nizam ad-Din Auliya'**, appearing as a *petitioner* at Ajudhan: he came to fetch an amulet

- **Dipalpur** — seat of the Muslim governor to whom Ajudhan was subordinate (p. 217), Tughluq-era

- **Maulana Thanawi's *Bihish[ti] zewar*** — named as the expression of nineteenth-century reformist
  opposition to "the entire culture of saints and shrines" (p. 216).
- **'Abd Allah Rumi** (printed "'Abd Allah Rami") — traveller protected from highway robbers between
  Ajudhan and Multan by Baba Farid's spiritual power (p. 222).
- **Shaikh Burhan-ud-din al-'Araj of Alexandria** — foretold Ibn Battuta's meeting with the Shaikh of
  Ajudhan (p. 221, citing *Rehla*, 20).
- **Balban** — "in order to build a defensive bulwark against Mongol incursions, pursued the policy

- **Jat, Khokhar, Bhatti, Mina, Mandahar** tribes — named by Barani as targets of Balban's governor
  of Lahore and Dipalpur in resisting the Mongols (p. 224, n. 36).
- **Janjua Rajputs and Ghakkars** — dominant in the Sind Sagar doab, "Only in [this] extreme western
  Punjab… were the Jats conspicuously absent as zamindars" (p. 225).

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The shrine as redistributive institution — the core economic claim of the chunk.** "it was Baba
  Farid's practice to distribute among his khalifas and the common devotees the presents that other
  devotees had brought. The shaikh's convent, and later his shrine, thus served as a nexus for the
  circulation and redistribution of a great deal of material wealth in the region" (p. 218, citing
  Mir Khurd Kirmani, *Siyar al-auliya'* (Delhi, 1885), 131).
- **Why the redistribution required organization.** "In a social and institutional sense, moreover,
  the ta'widh-futah system required the shrine to adopt a certain degree of rational organization
  with respect to the distribution of material wealth" (p. 218) — i.e. the institution is derived
  from the gift flow, not from doctrine.
- **The same system stated in its religious register.** "In a religious sense the ta'widh-futah
  system defined and sustained Baba Farid's intermediary status between the devotee and God, as a
  conduit through whose intercession with God one's wishes may be fulfilled" (p. 218). Eaton treats
  the two registers as one mechanism: "Baba Farid's giving of ta'widh, and the devotees' giving of
  fut[u]h or gifts, provided the structural framework upon which the subsequent devotionalism of the
  shrine rested" (p. 218).
- **What devotees came for.** The burial place "frequently evolved into a great centre of pilgrimage
  for persons seeking divine aid in their personal, matrimonial, or business affairs" (p. 216).
- **Langar khana**: instituted under the first diwan, with distribution of food and sweets directed
  by the diwan in person on formal occasions (p. 219); endowed with a *muafi* by Muhammad ibn
  Tughluq in 1335–38 (p. 221, n. 22). The complex's kitchen is also called a "welfare kitchen"
  (p. 218).
- **'Urs**: annual, on the death date; recorded from the fiftieth anniversary; 1315 celebration
  described with pilgrimage, recitation of the saint's deeds and dervish entertainments (p. 219);
  the *shisha darwaza* opening for the masses is an 'urs ritual (p. 219).
- **Qawwali**: "the regularizing of ecstatic singing (qawwa[li]) at the shrine" dated to Badr ad-Din's
  day (p. 219).
- **Custodianship**: office of *diwan*, hereditary and by primogeniture "down to the present", with
  the two documented exceptions in n. 9 (p. 219).
- **Dastar bandi**: turban-tying as the formal transfer of the saint's spiritual authority (p. 219),
  and — through the 'Afif turban story — assimilated to royal succession (p. 220).
- **Revenue and land, itemized**: the city of Ajudhan bestowed on the shrine (p. 220); a *muafi*
  endowment for the kitchen (p. 221); tomb repairs, robes of honour, and descendants "confirmed…in
  possession of their villages and lands" under Firuz Tughluq (p. 221).
- **State capture of the custodians**: government service for the diwan, Shaikh al-Islam for his
  brother (p. 221).
- **Architecture and building dates**: Jami' mosque predating Baba Farid's arrival, later absorbed
  into the complex (pp. 217, 223); the tomb of 'Ala' ad-Din, inscription Safar 737 / Sept.–Oct. 1336
  (p. 221, n. 19); Baba Farid's own tomb, repaired by Firuz Tughluq (p. 221); mosque, welfare kitchen
  and "a number of related buildings" in the complex (p. 218).
- **Shrine networks over territory**: devotee-built "structures and tombs in the name of his shrine"
  in the countryside around Ajudhan, Dipalpur and "the hills toward Kashmir", visited "for alms and
  devotions" (p. 222). No count is given (not stated).
- **The pastoral economy the shrine served.** The *barr* country between the five rivers: "though
  possessing excellent natural soils for agriculture, was but little cultivated owing to the very
  scanty rainfall"; it supported "a type of pastoral nomadism based primarily on the herding of goats
  and camels" (p. 223, n. 29 citing "chapter 1 of Emily Hodges' dissertation, in progress
  (University of California, Berkeley…)"). Its movement was short-range: the pastoralists "moved only
  down to the riverain…in the hot dry months and returned to the barr and [t]ha/ after rains, never
  leaving the Punjab plains and covering at the most a distance of less than one hundred miles"
  (p. 223, n. 30).
- **Ritual supply as one leg of a symbiosis — the hinge of the conversion argument.** Pastoralists
  "needed access to the rivers for their herds, which placed them in a position of potential conflict
  with the riverine peoples, but they were nevertheless dependent upon the agrarian-based urban
  centres for trade. Moreover, and this is an important theme to which I shall return shortly, they
  were also dependent upon the riverine peoples for providing the rituals and belief structures that
  made up their religious system" (p. 223).
- **Measured agrarian dominance of the Jats, c. 1595.** From Abu al-Fadl's Ain-i Akbari figures for
  the Lahore and Multan subas, with the dominant *zamindar* caste and assessed revenue listed per
  pargana, and Jat-equivalent castes added from Ibbetson and Rose: "of the total 186 Punjabi parganas
  whose dominant zamind[a]rs are known, fully 82, or nearly half, were controlled by Jat groups"
  (p. 225, nn. 38–39, citing *Ain-i Akbari*, trans. Jarrett, 2nd ed., 2:320–5 passim).
- **The technology behind the change.** "The economic explanation for this change, argues Irfan Habib,
  lay in the introduction of the Persian wheel in the Punjab and its extensive use by Babur's time
  both in the riverine lands and in the tracts between the riverine area and the grazing zone,
  thereby making much of the arid western Punjab's naturally rich soil capable of supporting the
  cultivation of food crops" (p. 225, n. 41). Babur "specifically mentioned the Bet Jullundur and
  Bari Doabs ('Lah[o]re, Dipalpur, Sirhind, and the neighbouring districts'), where it was prevalent";
  in 1832 Captain Wade "noted the remains of an extensive irrigation system based on the Persian
  wheel" down the Sutlej valley (pp. 225–226, n. 41).
- **Clans claiming conversion by Baba Farid (Table 9.1, p. 227), as printed with the book's own
  sources:** Bhatti (*Jawahir-i Faridi* 323); Chhina (Rose, *Glossary*, 1, 168); Dhudhi (*Jawahir-i
  Faridi*, 323); Dogar (*Rauaij-i 'Amm*, 1860s); Gondal (Rose, *Glossary*, 1, 302); Gondal [printed
  twice] (Ibid.); Hans (*Jawahir-i Faridi*, 323); Jo'iya (Rose, *Glossary* 1, 412); Khokhar
  (*Jawahir-i Faridi*, 323); Siyal (Rose, *Glossary*, 2, 417); Tiwana (Sir 'Umar Hayat Khan); Watt
  (Rose, *Glossary*, 2, 491); Kharral (oral tradition); Ara'in (oral tradition). The table's caption
  is "Some Clans Claiming to Have Been Converted by Baba Farid" — a claim list, not Eaton's
  attribution (p. 227).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The turban prophecy at Ajudhan**: on one of Ghiyath ad-Din's visits "the governor brought along
  his son and nephew, the future sultans Muhammad bin Tughlu[q] and Fir[u]z Tughluq, and all three
  were given a turban by Diwan 'Ala' ad-Din and told by him that each was destined to rule
  Hindustan" — attributed by Eaton to "the fourteenth-century chronicler Shams-i Siraj 'Afif",
  *Tarikh-i F[i]roz Shahi*, ed. Vilayat Husain (Calcutta, 1891), 27–8 (p. 320 in text / p. 220,
  n. 14). The turban-length detail found in chunk 013 is **not** in this version (not stated here).
- **The amulet touched by the saint**: Nizam ad-Din's written names of God became effective when
  Baba Farid "touched it, read it, and gave it back" — reported from Sijzi's *Fawa'id al-fu'ad*
  (pp. 217–218).
- **Protection within the wilayat**: Baba Farid's "spiritual power protected a certain 'Abd Allah
  R[u]mi from highway robbers as he travelled southwest from Ajudhan to Multan", the saint having
  named the exact limits of his charge — from "an early-sixteenth-century hagiography", *Siyar
  al-arifin*, 115 (p. 222).
- **Passing into heaven at the 'urs**: devotees filing past the sanctum through the opened *shisha
  darwaza* were "ritually entering heaven" — as recorded in the *Jawahir-i Faridi* (p. 219).
- **A prediction from Alexandria**: Shaikh Burhan-ud-din al-'Araj foretold that Ibn Battuta would
  meet the saint of Ajudhan (p. 221, quoting the *Rehla*).
- **Mass conversion by the saint** — recorded by Eaton as a *claim* made by the clans themselves and
  carried in the *Jawahir-i Faridi*, Rose's *Glossary* and oral tradition (p. 227), and disputed by
  him (see Arguments).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Moral authority in Islam is textual, and the shrine is the workaround.** "In Islam the ultimate
  source of moral authority is unambiguous… for in Islam God revealed Himself not in any historical
  personage as in Christianity, but in a Book" (p. 215); the moral ideal is "at once objectively
  knowable, universally applicable to all peoples and times, and derived from a source external to
  humanity", in contrast to "Hindu-Buddhist ethical doctrines according to which reward and
  retribution operate on the self-fulfilling and self-regulating principle of karma" (p. 215). The
  problem the essay sets itself: "To Muslims literate in Arabic, the Qur'anic source of moral
  authority presents no problems… But what could nonliterate, non-Arabic-speaking villagers or
  pastoralists make of such a religion?" (pp. 215–216).
- **Gellner's answer, quoted and not adopted wholesale.** For the Berbers of the Atlas the lineages
  of holy men "are not just interpreters of Islam for the tribesmen, nor mere representatives of a
  world religion. 'Koranic propriety emanates from their essence, as it were,' writes Gellner. 'Islam
  is what they do. They are Islam.'" (p. 215, citing Gellner, *Saints of the Atlas* (Chicago, 1969),
  149, with "The emphasis is Gellner's"). Eaton offers the shrine as a *second* way, alongside this
  one: "Another way in which the Book was conveyed to such peoples was through the vast shrines built
  over the tombs of saints" (p. 216).
- **Theatre versus Scripture — the essay's governing image.** "In India these shrines displayed,
  theatre-style and in microcosm, the moral order of the Islamic macrocosm" (p. 216). And of the
  reformists: "supporters of these reformist movements sought to replace the shrine as the source of
  Islamic moral authority with a reassertion of the Book as the only such legitimate source. Theatre,
  in a word, was to be replaced by Scripture" (p. 216). The essay is justified as "an investigation
  of the formation and nature of this theatre-oriented Islam as represented by one such shrine"
  (pp. 216–217).
- **Eaton insists the shrine is religious first, economic second — worth quoting exactly, because it
  qualifies every economic fact he then supplies.** "Although such shrines possessed important
  economic, political, and social ties with the masses of villagers who frequented them, their
  fundamental raison d'être was religious. For it was through its rituals that a shrine made Islam
  accessible to nonlettered masses, providing them with vivid and concrete manifestations of the
  divine order, and integrating them into its ritualized drama both as participants and as sponsors"
  (p. 216). Note "**as sponsors**": the financing is built into his description of the ritual, not
  separated from it.
- **The theology that makes a tomb an institution.** "it was believed that the saint enjoyed a closer
  relationship with God than the common devotee could ever have, and that the saint's spiritual power
  (baraka) to intercede with God on the devotee's behalf outlasted the saint's mortal lifetime and
  adhered to his burial place" (p. 216); and "By the thirteenth century, it had become commonly
  believed in the Muslim world that a great shaikh's baraka adhered, after his death, to his familial
  descendants as well as to the place of his burial" (p. 218) — which is how Eaton derives hereditary
  custodianship from doctrine rather than from mere family interest.
- **A colonial source used as evidence for the medieval period, and flagged as such.** Eaton: "The
  remarks of a British officer concerning the mediating role of Punjabi saints in 1911 are equally
  true of medieval shrines", quoting Major Aubrey O'Brien: "The general idea of our riverain folk
  seems to be that the Deity is a busy person, and that his hall of audience is of limited capacity…
  Thus, all human beings require an intervener between them and God" (p. 216, n., citing *Journal of
  the Royal Anthropological Institute* 41 (1911):511). The 1911-to-medieval extension is Eaton's
  assertion, not the officer's.
- **The saint's settlement as one moment in a migration process.** "Baba Farid's decision to establish
  himself in Ajudhan was but one part of a larger process; it was the religious dimension of a very
  slow transformation of the Punjab's cities from a Hindu to a Turkish-Islamic orientation" (p. 217)
  — with the Jami mosque, qadi and Muslim governor already in place as the evidence (p. 217).
- **Two dimensions of the shrine's institutionalization, named.** "We find two dimensions of this
  growing cult: patronage by the Delhi court and the extension of mass devotionalism into the
  countryside" (p. 219); the second is "a second and even more significant dimension of the shrine's
  institutionalization process" (p. 221).
- **The turban story read as an ideological merger.** "Apart from rationalizing the subsequent
  patronage of Baba Farid's shrine by the leading Tughluq sultans, the story also weaves together the
  shrine's dastar bandi ceremony with succession to the royal throne in Delhi, effectively merging
  the symbols of the shrine and of the royal court" (p. 220) — Eaton's reading of the source, not the
  source's claim.
- **How he adjudicates hagiography against a traveller.** Against traditions of 'Ala' ad-Din's
  "refusal to consort with royalty", he sets Ibn Battuta, "certainly an impartial authority in this
  matter", who reports the grant of Ajudhan's revenues (p. 220). The method is explicit: the
  disinterested outsider outweighs the shrine's own literature on a question where the shrine has an
  interest.
- **Patronage bought pliability.** "Once begun, the Tughluq court's patronage of the shrine continued
  to grow as 'Ala' ad-Din's successors proved more pliable to the court's will" (p. 221), with the
  Delhi appointments and the deepening economic dependence as evidence (p. 221).
- **Wilayat as territory: spiritual sovereignty mapped onto political sovereignty.** The memorial
  shrines show "that the baraka and authority of Baba Farid became physically established over the
  land in much the same way that political/administrative authority was" and "that a certain tract of
  the Punjab had become identified with Baba Farid's wi[la]yat, or spiritual kingdom, which to his
  devotees was perceived as having specific geographic boundaries that bordered the wilayats of other
  saints" (p. 222). On the Zakariya frontier passage: "This passage clearly demonstrates how closely
  the notion of spiritual sovereignty could parallel, in spatial terms, that of political sovereignty,
  and represents one of several ways in which the shrine of Baba Farid fused religious and political
  categories of authority" (p. 222).
- **Who built what matters to the argument**: the state built the Ajudhan monuments, the people built
  the village shrines (p. 222) — the shrine's territorial reach is popular in origin, its monumental
  core courtly.
- **The Jat sequence, argued from dated sources**: Jats moving up from Sind into the Multan area
  between the seventh and eleventh centuries (p. 224); Hsuan Tsang on Sindi cattle-keepers who "'have
  no masters'" (p. 224, n. 31); the eighth-century *Chach-nama* noting "an absence among them of
  social hierarchy" (p. 224, n. 32) — from which Eaton concludes: "This evidence all points to the
  conclusion that these Jat pastoralists, before their entry into the Punjab, had not yet been
  integrated into Hindu society" (p. 224). He adds the dog-association humiliation continued by the
  Arab rulers of Sind (p. 224, n. 33), al-Biruni's "'cattle-owners, low Sudra people'" (p. 224,
  n. 34), the 1260s first mention of Jats in the Punjab proper (p. 224), Babur's 1519 and 1525
  descriptions (p. 225), and Akbar's parganas (p. 225). Conclusion: "The northward movement of Jat
  clans and their settlement in the grazing tracts of the Punjab is thus well supported by
  contemporary evidence" (p. 225), and "from being the pastoralist 'low Sudras' described by
  al-B[i]r[u]ni in the eleventh century, the Jats had clearly risen in social position, having become
  the dominant agrarian caste in nearly half of the Punjab" (p. 225).
- **Conversion is the fourth term in that series — and the claim is the clans', not the sources'.**
  "All of this discussion of the Jats…would be irrelevant were it not that yet another important
  change accompanied those already mentioned: many of them also converted to Islam" (p. 227). Against
  the clans' own attribution he sets two arguments: "as Mohammad Habib notes, converting non-Muslims
  was not a function of the early shaikhs of the Chishti order, Baba Farid included; and the earliest
  primary sources on Baba Farid make no mention of his having converted anyone" (p. 227, n. 42, citing
  *Islamic Culture* (April 1946): 140); and a chronological/geographical objection: "it is probable
  that many if not most of these clans, in the course of their northward migrations up the rivers of
  the Punjab, had not yet reached the Ajudhan area during Baba Farid's lifetime" (p. 227). The
  positive explanation is not yet given in this chunk — the argument breaks off at p. 227, with the
  promised return to pastoralists' dependence "upon the riverine peoples for providing the rituals and
  belief structures" (p. 223) still outstanding.

## Doubts

- **"Mauj Darya" is a name collision with the archive and I have not resolved it.** This chunk's
  figure is Shaikh **'Ala' ad-Din Mauj Darya**, Baba Farid's grandson, second *diwan* of the Pakpattan
  shrine, dated 1281–1334/1335, whose tomb stands *inside the Pakpattan complex* (pp. 219–221). The
  archive's **shrine-of-mauj-darya-bukhari** row is Hazrat Meeran Muhammad Shah Bukhari (Mauj Darya)
  of Edward Road, Anarkali, Lahore, **died 1604** — three centuries later, a different personal name,
  a different city, and a different tomb. **On the chunk's own evidence these are two different men
  and the material here must not be written into that entry;** it belongs to
  shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar. Recorded as a flagged non-match, not a match.
  - **This also bears on chunk 013.** That chunk's notes carry "'Ala al-Din Mauj Darya", "a holy man
    in the Punjab", under the Lahore archive id with a warning, citing 'Afif's *Tarikh-i Firuz Shahi*,
    27–8 (p. 195, n. 15). This chunk tells the same turban story from the same pages of the same
    source and names the figure as the Pakpattan diwan (p. 220, n. 14). The footnote in chunk 013 is
    therefore about this Pakpattan saint, and **chunk 013's bullet under shrine-of-mauj-darya-bukhari
    should be re-filed at consolidation.** Flagged here rather than acted on, since I have not
    re-read that chunk's page images.
- **Bahauddin Zakariya's death year disagrees with the archive row.** The chunk gives "Baha' al-Haqq
  Zakariya (d. 1263)" (p. 227); the archive's **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya** gives 1267. The chunk
  also uses two forms of his name, "Shaikh Baha' ad-Din Zakariya" (p. 222) and "Baha' al-Haqq
  Zakariya" (p. 227), without noting them as variants. The 1263 date must not be imported over the
  archive's without a human check.
- **Table 9.1's "Siyal" is a clan, not the archive's Sial Sharif.** The table lists Punjabi clans
  claiming conversion by Baba Farid (p. 227); the archive's **sial-sharif** row is a nineteenth-century
  Chishti shrine of Khwaja Muhammad Shamsuddin Sialvi. No link is made in the chunk (not stated) and
  none should be inferred. Likewise "Khokhar" and "Bhatti" here are clan names only.
- **'Ala' ad-Din's dates are internally contradictory.** "(1281–1334)" (p. 219) against "'Ala' ad-Din's
  death in 1335" (p. 221) and Mu'izz ad-Din's successorship "(1335–38)" (p. 221); the "fifty-four-year
  term as diwan" said to be ending in 1334 when Ibn Battuta visited (p. 221) computes to 1335 from
  1281. **Do not quote a death year for him from this chunk without checking the printed page.** The
  tomb inscription date, Safar 737 / Sept.–Oct. 1336 (p. 221, n. 19), is the one firm date.
- **"dangar khana" (p. 218) versus "langar khana" (p. 219)** for the same institution — the first is
  almost certainly a text-layer error for the second, but it is what the page carries, so any quotation
  of p. 218 must be checked.
- **"muafin support" (p. 221)** fuses the term and the following words; read as "a *muafi* in support
