# eaton_essays_islam_indian_history — chunk 018 — pp. 254–268

Two essays and a section break. The chunk opens in the **last five pages of the Punjab essay whose
running head is "Court of Man, Court of God"** (pp. 255, 257) and whose concluding section is
headed "COURT OF GOD, COURT OF MAN" (p. 257) — an essay built on the depositions in a
succession suit over the *diwan*-ship of Baba Farid's shrine at Pakpattan, heard "In the years 1935
to 1938" in "the court of the senior subjudge of the Montgomery District, British Punjab" (p. 257).
Its own essay number is not printed in this chunk (not stated); the source it cites throughout is
"Lahore High Court" by volume and page (pp. 254, 258), whose full citation falls in an earlier chunk.
Then **"SECTION 4 / BENGAL"** (p. 259), a lost plate (p. 260), and the first eight pages of essay
**11, "Who are the Bengal Muslims: Conversion and Islamization in Bengal"**, "From Rafiuddin
Ahmed, ed., *Understanding Bengali Muslims: Interpretive Essays* (New Delhi: Oxford University
Press, 2000)" (p. 261).

For the economics thesis the load-bearing pages are **254** (the Diwan's monopoly on access, the
50–60,000 crowd estimate, and the ranked queue with "pass-holders"), **255–256** (turbans and
*nazrana* moving in both directions; an itemized inter-shrine gift), **257–258** (a colonial court
ratifying shrine custom as Islamic law), **265** ("considerable tax-free land" as the standing
Chishti settlement with Delhi) and **266–267** (state patronage of Bengali shrines, including a
cash figure). Each states a mechanism, not only a conclusion.

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar** (as "the shrine of Baba Farid" in Pakpattan, p. 257; "Farid
  al-Din Shakarganj (d. 1265) in Pakpattan, Punjab", p. 265):
  - **The Bihishti Darwaza ceremony, and the Diwan's monopoly of it.** "that day the Diwan
    personally unlocked and opened the gate, passed through it, and was then followed by throngs of
    murids estimated to number between fifty and sixty thousand" (p. 254). "All through the night
    and in states of intense religious ecstasy, the devotees filed through this 'Gate of Heaven' and
    past Baba Farid's flower-bedecked tomb, thereby both commemorating and ritually reenacting the
    ascension of their patron saint to heaven" (p. 254). The crowd figure is footnoted to *Punjab
    District Gazetteers. Montgomery District* ([La]hore: 1884), 185 (p. 254, n. 56) — **the year the
    figure describes is not stated; see Doubts.**
  - **Eaton's mechanism claim about that gate.** "because only the Diwan opened the gate, it was
    only through his agency that devotees gained access to Baba Farid, and only through the saint's
    agency that they gained access to Heaven" (p. 254). A two-stage intercession with the custodian
    holding the first stage.
  - **The 1833 European account of the same ritual**, quoted at length as illustrating "the
    salvation aspect of the ritual": "numbers of pilgrims, both Hindus and Mussalmans, come to visit
    the shrine, and all who pass through this doorway are considered saved from the fires of
    perdition. The door-way is about two feet wide, and cannot be passed without stopping, and the
    apartment itself is not capable of containing thirty people crowded together…. A superlative
    heaven is allotted to those who are first to enter the tomb on the day mentioned. The rush for
    precedence may, therefore, be better imagined than described. The crowd of pilgrims is said to be
    immense, and as they egress from the sacred door-way, after having rubbed their foreheads on the
    foot of the saint's grave, the air resounds with their shouts of Farid! Farid!" (p. 254, n. 57,
    citing Lieut. F. Mackeson, 'Journal of Captain C. M. Wade's Voyage from Lodiana to Mithankot by
    the River Satlaj on His Mission to Lahor and Bahawalpur in 1832-33,' *Journal of the Asiatic
    Society of Bengal* 6 (1837): 192). **Note the cross-tradition attendance in 1833** — "both
    Hindus and Mussalmans".
  - **A ranked queue, with a pass-holding class.** From "a certain Khan Muhammad in his deposition
    of August, 1938": "Dewan Sahib first opens the Bahishti Gate… he enters the gate and thereafter
    his companions pass through that gate. The companions number about 100-125. After that, the
    pass-holders pass through it, they are about 400-500; after that, the darveshes of the Hujra
    [i.e., resident mystics], who number 1,000—1,200. After them the plaintiff, Ghulam Rasul and his
    party, pass through the Bahishti Gate, after that the sajjadanashins of other institutions pass,
    and so on." (p. 254, n. 58, citing Lahore High Court, 2:249). Eaton: "The great masses of common
    devotees, included among the 'and so on' in the above quote, were allowed to enter through the
    gate only after the other dignataries had done so" (p. 254, "dignataries" as printed). The
    existence of "pass-holders" is stated but the pass is not described (not stated).
  - **The Diwan's visit to a subordinate Pakpattan shrine, itemized.** When the Diwan visited "the
    shrine of Baba Farid's son-in-law in Pakpattan, Badr al-Din Ishaq, on the occasion of that
    shrine's 'urs ceremony, the Diwan was carried on a palanquin (pa[l]ki)", a conveyance "from
    ancient times traditionally reserved for persons of privileged status" (p. 255). One villager:
    "it is necessary for the sajjadanashin to go there in a palki" (p. 255, n. 59). "Once there,
    moreover, gifts presented by the Diwan to Badr al-Din's descendant further suggested the style
    and magnanimity associated with [words lost] nobles— 100 rupees in cash, a horse, and a silver
    saddle" (p. 255, n. 60; see Doubts on the gap).
  - **Dastar bandi as the contested instrument of succession.** "the depositions are full of evidence
    indicating that a proper successor to Baba Farid's shrine had to be 'crowned' with a turban
    representing the saint's own turban, i.e., the dastar-bandi ceremony. Both disputants in this
    case accordingly went through such 'turban-tying' ceremonies by way of laying claim to legitimate
    succession as Diwan" (p. 255).
  - **The two rival theories of succession, as the parties stated them.** The defendant "claimed
    that only a former Diwan could choose his successor", and his father "Diwan Sa'id Muhammad, had
    placed a turban on the boy's head during the course of the Bihishti mela of 1933"; "on the
    occasion of the fortieth day after his father's death, the boy had a second dastar-bandi ceremony
    during which the sajjada-nishin of the Ajmer shrine placed the turban on the boy's head"
    (p. 255, n. 61, citing ibid., 2:181, 193). The plaintiff "claimed that a new Diwan must be
    elected by the Chishti clan", and "a special dastar-bandi ceremony was held at which
    representatives of the whole clan placed a turban on his head" (p. 255, n. 62, citing Ibid., 1:47).
  - **The turban is charged by contact with the tomb — and access to the tomb was itself leverage.**
    "the headgear used in the dastar-bandi ceremony, a special saffron-coloured turban reserved only
    for the Diwan, was physically touched to the saint's tomb before being placed on the candidate's
    head" (p. 256, n. 63, citing Ibid., 2:55, 169, 181). The footnote: "Since the plaintiff was not
    given access to the shrine for his dastar-bandi ceremony, this aspect of the rite had to be
    omitted in his case" (p. 256, n. 63).
  - **Turbans distributed at the 'urs by the custodian alone.** A sixty-year-old villager: "pechas
    [turbans] are distributed at the annual urs only by [the] sajjadanashin and nobody else. At that
    time, [the] sajjadanashin has hundreds of pechas with him. He must distribute those pechas
    personally." (p. 256, n. 67, citing Ibid., 2:104)
  - **And offered upward as *nazrana*.** "Yet turbans were passed in the opposite direction, too. We
    have seen that oftentimes nazrana given to the Diwan by devotees, or simply by well-wishers,
    consisted of turbans in addition to cash" (p. 256). ("We have seen" refers back beyond this
    chunk; no amounts are given here — not stated.)
  - **Two words, two social effects.** The *pecha* "got passed to and from persons otherwise
    occupying different positions in the social hierarchy" and so "promoted egalitarian feelings of a
    shared, common tradition", while "the word dastar referred to the turban that served to separate
    Baba Farid's legitimate successor from the common crowd, thereby having the opposite effect of
    maintaining hierarchy" (p. 257).
  - **A devotee's orthodoxy about the tomb.** "one old cultivator who declared, 'doing sajda
    [prostration] to the tomb of Baba Farid under Mohammadan Law is not permissible'" (p. 257,
    n. 68, citing Ibid., 2:39).
  - **Indifference to the litigation.** "'I am a follower of the Gaddi Nashin whosoever may be
    occupying it,' stated one of Baba Farid's murids" (p. 258, n. 70, citing Lahore High Court,
    1:138).
  - **Distance to the court.** Montgomery lay "some 25 miles down a dusty road from the hallowed
    marble domes of Pakpattan" (p. 257).
  - **The shrine inside the Chishti patronage settlement.** Baba Farid's tomb-shrine at Pakpattan is
    named as one of the three "enormous cults" on which the order's "wealth and power centred",
    beside Ajmer and Delhi (p. 265), and "Since the Tughluq period, this order enjoyed a very special
    status among Delhi's rulers, who lavishly patronized the descendants of the great Chishti shaikhs
    with magnificent tombs and considerable tax-free land" (p. 265).
  - **The cult travelled with Punjabi migrants.** Mirza Nathan, a Mughal officer in Bengal, "was
    himself a 'faithful disciple' (mu[ri]d-i bandag[i]) of Farid al-Din Shakarganj, probably because
    the writer's ancestors had come from the Punjab where Baba Farid's cult was especially prominent"
    (p. 265, n. 10) — the "probably" is Eaton's inference, not the source's statement.
  - Death year given as 1265 (p. 265). **The archive row gives 1266 — see Doubts.**

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Bihishti Darwaza / "Gate of Heaven"** at Pakpattan: opened by the Diwan in person, once, at the
  Bihishti mela; devotees file past the tomb all night; the rite "reenact[s] the ascension of their
  patron saint to heaven" (p. 254). In 1833 the doorway was "about two feet wide", the chamber "not
  capable of containing thirty people crowded together", passage single-file (pp. 254–255).
- **Crowd sizes**: "between fifty and sixty thousand" murids following the Diwan through the gate
  (p. 254, n. 56, from the 1884 Montgomery Gazetteer); "The crowd of pilgrims is said to be immense"
  (1833, p. 254). No gate receipts or offering totals are given (not stated).
- **A four-tier access hierarchy at the gate**, with counts: Diwan → companions 100–125 →
  pass-holders 400–500 → *darveshes of the Hujra* 1,000–1,200 → plaintiff's party → *sajjadanashins*
  of other institutions → the masses (p. 254). A resident dervish body of over a thousand is itself
  an institution the shrine supported (p. 254).
- **'Urs at a satellite shrine within the same town**: Badr al-Din Ishaq's 'urs, attended by the
  Diwan in a *palki*, with gifts of "100 rupees in cash, a horse, and a silver saddle" to the
  descendant-custodian (p. 255) — a documented transfer between two custodianships.
- **Bihishti mela of 1933** used as the occasion for a dastar-bandi (p. 255).
- **Dastar bandi**: saffron turban reserved to the Diwan, touched to the tomb, then tied (p. 256);
  performed variously by a predecessor, by an outside *sajjada-nishin* (Ajmer), or by clan
  representatives (p. 255); "the fortieth day after his father's death" as a succession occasion
  (p. 255).
- **Succession rules in dispute**: nomination by the incumbent versus election by "the Chishti clan"
  (p. 255); a witness's *bradari* deciding by ordeal (p. 256). Primogeniture is not asserted in this
  chunk (not stated here).
- **Pecha distribution at the annual 'urs**: "hundreds of pechas", distributed personally and only by
  the *sajjadanashin* (p. 256).
- **Nazrana**: turbans "in addition to cash" given to the Diwan by devotees and well-wishers
  (p. 256).
- **Physical control of the shrine as a resource in litigation**: the plaintiff "was not given access
  to the shrine for his dastar-bandi ceremony" (p. 256, n. 63).
- **Colonial legal machinery**: senior subjudge of Montgomery District, 1935–1938 (p. 257); the
  District Court "merely ratified what it deemed to be the customary practice with regard to
  succession at the shrine, and then declared its judgment to be in conformity with Islamic Law"
  (p. 258); "In British India, the effect of such laws was to render state sanction to whatever local
  customs the courts judged were already being practiced by a majority of the people" (p. 257).
  Superhuman evidence "such as a revelation from Baba Farid was inadmissible" (p. 257).
- **Chishti endowments under Delhi**: "magnificent tombs and considerable tax-free land" to the
  descendants of the great shaikhs, "Since the Tughluq period" (p. 265). No acreage or revenue figure
  (not stated).
- **Bengal shrine patronage, with a figure**: the Pandua tombs were the shrines "most lavishly
  patronized by the Mughals" (p. 266); the Nur Qutb-i 'Alam shrine had been under state patronage
  since Sultan Ahmad (r. 1432-33) became the shaikh's disciple and was "the focus of annual
  pilgrimages performed by Sultan 'Ala al-Din Husain Shah (r. 1493-1532)" by the end of the fifteenth
  century (p. 266, n. 14); "while in Bengal in 1624, the future emperor Shah Jahan distributed
  Rs 4,000 at Nur Qutb-i 'Alam's shrine, his largest cash contribution in all of Bengal" (p. 267,
  n. 17).
- **Vow-driven pilgrimage**: Mirza Nathan "made a three-day pilgrimage to the shrine, having vowed to
  do so should his father recover from an illness" (pp. 266–267, n. 15), and made another on the
  occasion of his marriage (p. 267, n. 16).
- **Relic economy**: the Qadam Rasul footprint "had been purchased and brought from Arabia by one of
  the sultans 'so that the people of Bengal and everybody else, who were destined to come there,
  might attain eternal blessing by kissing the holy footprint'" (p. 266, n. 13).
- **Functional specialization of cities**: Dhaka "primarily devoted to revenue collection,
  administration, politics, and military reviews", also trade and "money-making" — "In short, Dhaka
  was a secular city" (pp. 265–266); Gaur and Pandua, "denied any political significance under the
  Mughals, emerged under their rule as Islamic sacred centres" (p. 266).
- **Fiscal-religious intervention and its cost**: the jizya, "never previously been imposed or
  collected in Bengal", was collected from Dhaka's Hindus "ever so strictly" from early 1681; at
  Qasimbazar officials "forcibly demanded the jizya from Hindu silk workers, which disrupted the
  local textile production business and drove the city's 'little people' into the interior"; Bengal's
  *diwan* demanded it even of resident Dutch East India Company officials (p. 268, n. 20).
- **The "ideology of 'salt'"**: ritual eating binding "people of unequal sociopolitical rank to
  mutual obligations: the higher-ranked person swore to protect the lower, in return for which the
  latter swore loyalty to the higher", across religious and ethnic communities and "over several
  generations" (p. 267, n. 18).
- **Mixed-confession oath practice**: a Qur'an and a *salagram* geode brought to a mixed group of
  officers, each swearing on his own object (p. 267, n. 19).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The gate as salvation** — those passing the Bihishti Darwaza "are considered saved from the fires
  of perdition", with "a superlative heaven… allotted to those who are first to enter the tomb":
  reported by the European visitor of 1833 (p. 254).
- **The child and the turban** — one villager testified that 'Ala' al-Din Mauj Darya "became Diwan by
  placing Baba Farid's very turban on his head when he was a child" (p. 256, n. 64); another gave the
  fuller story, that the child "had one day playfully put Baba Farid's turban on his head", and when
  a servant objected Baba Farid, "then an old man, pardoned the child's behavior saying that the boy
  would one day be his successor anyway" (p. 256, n. 65). Both are 1930s depositions as recorded in
  the court volumes, not Eaton's assertions.
- **The saliva transfer** — the same witness "added that Baba Farid also placed saliva from his own
  mouth into that of the boy 'in order to impart spiritual light from himself to the boy'" (p. 256,
  n. 65).
- **The turban ordeal** — a third witness: Nizam al-Din Auliya came to Pakpattan to arbitrate, and at
  his instance "the turbans of all the rival claimants were emersed [sic] in water. The turban of
  Dewan Alau-ud-Din Mauj-i Darya came out coloured, consequently, the Bradari accepted Dewan
  Alau-ud-Din Mauj-i Darya as the proper Gaddi Nashin to the exclusion of all other claimants."
  (p. 256, n. 66 — citation unreadable, see Doubts)
- **A revelation from Baba Farid** was pressed as evidence in the 1930s case and ruled inadmissible
  (p. 257); Eaton notes in a footnote that "on the very next page, this same judge made a ruling on
  the fact of Baba Farid's revelation" (p. 258, n. 69).
- **Witchcraft, astrology and the houris** — Mirza Nathan's memoir records Muslim practitioners
  causing "blood spontaneously to appear from the throat and stomach of victims", astrologers
  counselling against moving armies northward on Tuesdays or Wednesdays, and houris waiting "with the
  cups of heavenly drink in their hands" for the bravest of the fallen (p. 264, n. 9).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Access is monopolized, and that is the point.** "It is worth noting that because only the Diwan
  opened the gate, it was only through his agency that devotees gained access to Baba Farid, and only
  through the saint's agency that they gained access to Heaven" (p. 254). This is Eaton's own
  inference from the ritual's form — he goes on to argue that even the scramble was ordered: "it seems
  that in the minds of the murids, a fixed order of precedence existed with respect to entering the
  'Gate of Heaven,' despite the rush mentioned by the European visitor in 1833" (p. 254), his evidence
  being the 1938 deposition rather than any observation of the rite.
- **Ritual as the enactment of hierarchy.** "All this suggests that the Bihishti ceremony served to
  act out and confirm the sense of spiritual hierarchy that pervades the whole ethos of the shrine"
  (p. 255) — and the physical constraint is folded into the argument: "The Gate of Heaven, after all,
  is a narrow one, and can be entered only one by one, in single file" (pp. 254–255).
- **Royal symbolism marks an administrative office, not only a spiritual one.** Core symbols "were
  associated with medieval Indian royalty and served to emphasize the Diwan's supreme religious and
  administrative position" (p. 255), the *palki* being "from ancient times traditionally reserved for
  persons of privileged status" (p. 255).
- **The turban carries two opposed functions at once — the chunk's sharpest interpretive move.** It
  "served as a vehicle both for religious legitimacy and for the distribution of Baba Farid's grace"
  (p. 255), with "ambiguous associations with the crown and thereby with royalty" (p. 255). Eaton
  reads the vocabulary itself as the evidence: *pecha* circulating up and down the hierarchy
  "promoted egalitarian feelings of a shared, common tradition", *dastar* "separate[d] Baba Farid's
  legitimate successor from the common crowd" (p. 257), so that "it is in the turban's rich symbolic
  repertoire that we see its capacity to express and sustain values, so central to the shrine, that
  might otherwise appear mutually antagonistic, i.e., a hierarchy of religious status and a
  commonality of cultural inheritance" (p. 257).
- **What the competing origin stories share is the argument, not their content.** "In all of these
  versions we find the common assumption that the turban, used in the proper ritual context, was
  uniquely empowered to endow the wearer with Baba Farid's own spiritual authority" (p. 256). Eaton
  treats the three villager accounts as "versions" and ranks one as "a truncated version of a story
  given in more detail by another villager" (p. 256) — he is reading testimony for its shared
  premise, not adjudicating the events of 1281.
- **The shrine mediates the Book for the illiterate — the essay's closing thesis.** "the shrine of
  Baba Farid in Pakpattan provides a striking example of how Islam, the religion par excellence 'of
  the Book,' has been mediated among common villagers most of whom were illiterate. For them it was
  the shrine, and less so the Book, which manifested the juncture 'where the contrasted poles of
  Heaven and Earth met'" (p. 257; the quoted phrase carries no visible source — see Doubts). "Through
  its elaborate rituals, grand processions and colorful pageantry, the shrine displayed a sense of
  divine magnificence and mercy. It displayed, in short, the Court of God" (p. 257).
- **And the limit he sets on that thesis.** "Not that Baba Farid himself was confused with God. This
  would of course have been blasphemous idolatry, as was fully understood by one old cultivator"
  (p. 257) — the devotee's own orthodoxy is his evidence. The saint is instead "a 'friend in court,'
  as it were, who represented his interests there. This 'friend in court,' this special pleader, was
  Baba Farid" (p. 257).
- **Two conceptions of order, contrasted then deliberately un-contrasted.** "In the Court of Man, the
  world is held together not by an unseen Presence, but by principles, or 'laws,' which are whatever
  men state them to be" (p. 257). "The case examined here might therefore appear to have represented
  a clash of two conceptual systems. But did they really clash? As we have seen, the District Court
  in the end merely ratified what it deemed to be the customary practice with regard to succession at
  the shrine, and then declared its judgment to be in conformity with Islamic Law" (p. 258). The
  colonial court is thus described as a ratifier of existing shrine custom under an Islamic-law label
  — this is Eaton's reading of the verdict, and the verdict's own text quoted here is only the
  passage on revelation (p. 258, n. 69).
- **Devotee indifference as the final evidence.** "To the masses of devotees in Pakpattan, however,
  the little drama unfolding in the district courthouse in Montgomery was apparently irrelevant to
  their religious concerns" (p. 258); "In the last analysis, the Court of God quite transcended the
  Court of Man in the eyes of the shrine's common devotees" (p. 258). The whole weight of this
  conclusion rests on one murid's quoted sentence (p. 258, n. 70), and Eaton marks it as inference —
  "this sentiment seems to suggest".
- **The Bengal essay's thesis, as stated in its first paragraph.** "A striking feature of Islam in
  pre-modern Bengal is the cleavage that emerged between a folk Bengali variant, which was built upon
  indigenous roots, and a variant practiced and patronized primarily by urban-dwelling, ashr[a]f
  classes. This divide lay behind the nineteenth century reform movement and contributed to the
  twentieth century upheavals that led first to the inclusion of eastern Bengal in the state of
  Pakistan, and ultimately to its secession from that state" (p. 261). Period: sixteenth to
  eighteenth centuries, chosen because evidence for "both ashraf and non-ashraf communities is
  especially well-documented" (p. 261). Two comparative puzzles are set: "why and how Islam became
  the dominant religious tradition in Bengal but not in upper India… and in the eastern, but not the
  western, portion of the Bengal delta" (p. 261).
- **Indianization of the Mughal ruling class, argued from behaviour under duress.** "by the time they
  conquered Bengal in the late sixteenth century they had already assimilated the political
  traditions of north India, a process accelerated by Akbar's policy of admitting Rajputs into his
  ruling class. In fact, they had become virtual Rajputs themselves" (p. 262). The evidence is
  *jahar* [jauhar] among Muslim officers and a preference for Ayurvedic over Yunani medicine, and the
  inference is explicitly methodological: "Reliance on Indian systems of medical theory in the face of
  fatal illness, and on Rajput customs when faced with imm[i]nent annihilation in battle—both of them
  life-threatening situations—suggests the degree to which Indian, and especially Rajput, values had
  penetrated Mughal culture by the early seventeenth century" (p. 262).
- **A theory of decay read out of Abu'l-fazl, and tied to later colonial thought.** "In effect, we
  have here a theory of socio-political decay: an enervating climate corrupts men, corrupted men ruin
  sovereign domains and, implicitly, ruined domains pave the way for conquest by more virile, 'manly'
  races" (p. 263); it "at once recalls similar views later adopted by British colonial officials"
  (p. 263, with Robert Orme, 1763, at n. 4). The theory is Eaton's reconstruction; the *bulghak-khana*
  phrase is Abu'l-fazl's (p. 262).
- **Ruler/ruled framed in fiscal terms.** "Mughal officials thus saw themselves as the land's natural
  rulers, distinguished from Bengalis not only as tax-receivers as opposed to tax-payers, but as north
  Indian fighting men as opposed to docile fishermen" (p. 264). From Manrique's tripartite population
  he draws that "Muslims were, by definition, foreigners to the land", and a Bengali Muslim was
  "conceptually impossible" to the urban ruling class he met (p. 264).
- **Three features of ashraf religion, stated as the essay's framework.** "(a) a special link with
  the pan-Indian Chishti order, (b) a conceptual separation of religion and state, and (c) a
  disinclination to convert Bengalis to Islam" (p. 264).
- **Which saints the ashraf used — the argument that matters for shrine economics.** Nathan's Allah
  "mediated his blessings to believers through the agency of saints. These, however, were not the
  village pirs who played such important roles in the world of rural Bengalis, but shaikhs belonging
  to the Chishti order of Sufism, the order most clearly associated with Mughal, and before that,
  Tughlu[q] imperialism" (p. 265). Two shrine systems, socially stratified, and the imperial one is
  the one with "considerable tax-free land" (p. 265).
- **Religion/state separation read off the built environment and the sacred geography.** Dhaka's
  mosques "bear the stuccoed stamp of their north Indian patrons, and seem intended more to display
  imperial power than to inspire piety" (p. 266); Gaur and Pandua became sacred centres precisely
  because they were "denied any political significance under the Mughals" (p. 266).
- **Non-intervention as policy, with the Ottoman comparison as the control case.** "Unlike the
  contemporary Ottoman Empire where non-Muslim military recruits were converted to Islam as part of
  their assimilation into the ruling class, in India non-Muslims were given full admission into the
  Mughal officer corps as non-Muslims" (p. 267); the binding agent "was not a common religion, then,
  but the ideology of 'salt'" (p. 267). "The invocation of a Hindu deity in this political ritual
  shows that unlike the early Sultans of Bengal, Mughal officials did not patronize Islam as a state
  religion" (p. 267), and "Bengal's rulers maintained a strictly non-interventionist position in
  religious matters, despite pressure from local religious functionaries (mu[l]l[a]s) and Sufis to
  support Islam over other religions" (p. 268) — with the 1679–81 jizya episode named as the
  exception (p. 268, n. 20).
- **A Muslim judge enforcing Hindu custom, used as the strongest case.** The 1640 Naraingarh judge,
  "himself a Bengali Muslim", asked "Art thou not, as it seems, a Bengali and a Musalman…? How then
  didst thou dare in a Hindu district to kill a living thing?", explained that at the conquest
  "sixty-six years earlier… Akbar had given his word 'that he and his successors would let [Bengalis]
  live under their own laws and customs: he therefore allowed no breach of them'", and had the accused
  whipped (p. 268, n. 22). Eaton: "The larger point, of course, is that the Mughals were determined
  not to allow religion to interfere with their administration of Bengal" (p. 268). Note the
  structural echo of the Pakpattan verdict: in both, the state ratifies local custom rather than
  imposing religious law (pp. 258, 268).

- **"Mauj Darya" collides with an archive row and must not be attached to it.** This chunk's figure

  **shrine-of-mauj-darya-bukhari** is Hazrat Meeran Muhammad Shah Bukhari (Mauj Darya) of Edward
  Road, Anarkali, Lahore, **died 1604** — a different man, city, century and tomb. All the material
  here belongs to **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar**. Recorded as a flagged non-match, consistent
  with chunk 015's note.
- **Mithankot appears only inside a source title.** "Journal of Captain C. M. Wade's Voyage from
  Lodiana to Mithankot by the River Satlaj…" (p. 254, n. 57) is a citation, not a reference to the
  shrine at Kot Mithan. **Do not attach mithankot-kot-mithan** to this chunk.
- **Baba Farid's death year disagrees with the archive row.** The chunk gives "Farid al-Din
  Shakarganj (d. 1265)" (p. 265); the archive gives 1266. Chunk 015 also read 1265 (p. 218). The book
  is internally consistent, so this is a live archive-vs-source discrepancy for a human, not an OCR
  artefact.

  1930s depositions describe, but its footnote is the *Punjab District Gazetteers. Montgomery
  District* of 1884 (p. 254, n. 56), while the next sentence dates the European account to "a century
