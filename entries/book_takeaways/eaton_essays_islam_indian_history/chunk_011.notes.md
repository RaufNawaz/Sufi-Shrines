## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- **The Baburi Masjid** — its destruction named again as the concrete event that "colonial
  discourse" analysis could not explain, along with "that incident's historical antecedents"
  (p. 155).
- **Bengal, the Mughal conquest** — Abu'l-fazl's passage was composed in 1579, "just four years
  after Mughal armies had marched down the Gangetic plain and swept away the last dynasty of
  independent sultans in Bengal" (p. 163). Robert Orme's comparable passage was written in 1763,
  "three years after the English East India Company's conquest of the same region" (p. 163).
- **Vijayanagara** — "In the year 1565… the imperial capital of Vijayanagara had been physically
  destroyed by a coalition of sultanates occupying the northern Deccan: Bijapur, Ahmadnagar, and
  Golconda" (p. 164). Its "imperial mystique would persist for centuries and serve to legitimate
  several successor kingdoms in the extreme south", but "the fate of the empire was so tightly
  intertwined with that of the city that the imperial polity never overcame the city's
  destruction" (p. 164). The *Rayavacakamu* was composed in a successor state's court and
  "constantly invoked the memory of that city" (p. 164).
- **Krishna Deva Raya** — Vijayanagara emperor, "(r. 1509–1529)"; the *Rayavacakamu* "situates
  itself" in his reign though written around 1595 (p. 164).
- **Abu'l-fazl 'Allami** — "the chief advisor, promoter and imperial theorist for the Mughal
  Emperor Akbar" (p. 163); *Akbar-nama*, ed. Abdur Rahim (Calcutta, 1873–87), 3:290, tr. Beveridge
  3:427 (p. 163, n. 60).

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **What a Mughal provincial annexation consisted of, in his own summary** — in newly conquered
  Bengal, "Imperial officers were busy setting up a new administrative apparatus, subduing local
  landholders, coopting the former ruling class, dispatching their own revenue agents into the
  countryside, and collecting local knowledge about military and revenue affairs" (p. 163). He
  reads the timing of Abu'l-fazl's text against this: it "should at once alert us to the nexus
  between power and knowledge" (p. 163).
- **The judicial repertoire attributed to the Deccan sultans by the Telugu text** — punishment
  "in front of their audience hall by slicing them in two at the waist, by cutting them apart with
  saws, by tying them up in gunny sacks and beating them with iron maces, by flogging them with
  strings with sharp horn tips, by tying them in bundles and letting them die in the hot sun, by
  dismembering their feet, hands, ears, or noses, or by crushing them to death under the feet of
  elephants" (p. 165). This is the text's claim, reported by Eaton from Wagoner's translation
  (p. 165, n. 63), not his own finding about Deccan practice.
- **Diet as the text's explanation of character** — the sultans' "dissolution and barbarism is said
  to have been their love of wine and opium", against Brahmans "who, thanks to their diet of rice,
  which they take with salt and sambar, did not suffer from such pride or malice" (p. 165).
- **The research infrastructure that emptied out** — "the 1980s and 1990s saw a sharp drop from
  levels of earlier decades in the number of historians who applied for support or for permission
  to conduct research out in the *mufassal*—that is, in district archives, local libraries, private
  collections, *zamindari* records, etc. Most ended up in London, and a few in national or state
  archives in India, studying colonial records that were then subjected to discourse analysis"
  (p. 161). The one number he gives: "Of the 118 proposals submitted in the fall of 1996 to the
  American Institute of Indian Studies, the principal U.S. institution supporting American
  scholarly research in India, all but a handful were crowded into the nineteenth or twentieth
  century" (p. 161, n. 57).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Postcoloniality re-privileged the British period in Indian time — the argument most directly
  usable by this archive.** "the postcolonialists' understanding of Indian time envisioned a linear
  periodization that, surpassing the crudest Orientalist schemes, privileged the European imperial
  Self. Even the Orientalists' triadic formulation of 'Hindu-Islamic-British,' while blatantly
  essentialist and statist in nature, had never gone so far as to privilege any one of these
  'periods' such that the other two were seen in reference to it. But the notion of
  'postcoloniality' situated all Indian time in reference to the British imperial period: time was
  either precolonial, colonial, or postcolonial" (p. 158). The consequence for vocabulary: "Today,
  when one speaks of the 'colonial' period of Indian history, we all 'know' that it is the British
  colonial empire that is intended, as if there had never been any empire in India before
  Britain's" (pp. 158–159), and "because 'colonialism' has become synonymous with but one
  historical imperial formation, it became terminologically impossible to bring other such
  formations into the picture" (p. 159). In a footnote he presses the etymology: colonialism from
  *colonus*, "farmer", imperialism from *imperium*, "the right to rule over", a distinction "clear
  enough to the ancient Romans, who, after all, practiced both forms of political control"
  (p. 159, n. 47).
- **The mechanism he proposes for that outcome.** It "appear[s] to have been rooted in the
  post-modernist belief that discourse not only precedes essence, but underpins, shapes, and
  determines it"; given "the tight fit between discourse and power that lay at the heart of this
  line of thinking, inasmuch as the advent of the British colonial discourse coincided historically
  with the advent of its carriers, it followed that the history of India began epistemologically,
  and hence conceptually, with the British themselves" — so that earlier India "presented a merely
  flat, two-dimensional screen, a period in which things might well have happened, but nothing
  could or did change in any meaningful way" (p. 159). He quotes Gyan Prakash as the plainest
  statement of it: "'History and colonialism… arose together in India. As India was introduced to
  history, it was also stripped of a meaningful past; it became a historyless society brought
  i[nto] the age of History'" (p. 159, n. 48). The reductio he draws: "The logic of post-modernism
  and postcolonialism thus ended up restating the old Orientalist position that for millennia India
  slumbered, awaiting its tryst with destiny when the English East India Company would arrive and
  give definition to this sleeping civilization" (p. 159).
- **Pre-British institutional change is what gets annihilated.** "ancient Indian institutions that
  had experienced enormous transformations during the many centuries preceding the advent of
  British rule either got washed out as timeless 'tradition' or interpreted as inventions of the
  Raj" — and "by extending the logic of 'imagined communities' and 'the invention of tradition' to
  its ultimate conclusion, post-modernist and postcolonialist critiques effectively annihilated all
  history and all historical process that preceded the advent of those who did the imagining and
  inventing, or anyway, of those whose imaginings or inventions were politically significant—the
  British and their native collaborators" (p. 160). He names Dirks's caste claim as the instance:
  caste, "shaped and refashioned by colonial British rulers as an instrument of domination, was
  'more a product of rule than a predecessor of it'" (p. 160, n. 51), and Dirks's "'much of what we
  now recognize as culture was produced by the colonial encounter;' 'culture,' he notes, 'is a
  colonial formation'" (p. 160, n. 50).
- **The result is 1950s modernisation theory with new labels.** "these critiques resolved into a
  simple model of Indian history strikingly reminiscent of the 1950s and 1960s, the heyday of
  modernization theory. A binary temporal division that in Walt Rostow's era had opposed
  'tradition' and 'modernity' was now replaced by, and effectively identified with, a binary
  division opposing the 'precolonial' and the 'colonial'" (p. 160).
- **His positive claim, and the evidence he brings for it: the "colonial discourse" is older than
  the colonisers and is not European.** Section IV's programme: "a careful reading of pre-British
  historical data can turn up historical continuities where post-modernist and postcolonial
  scholarship… perceives only discontinuities", and can reveal "the antecedents to what has thus
  far passed for the British 'colonial discourse'" (pp. 162–163). Evidence, text 1: Abu'l-fazl,
  1579 — "'The country of Bengal,' wrote Abu'l-fazl, is a land where, owing to the climate's
  favo[u]ring the base, the dust of dissension is always rising. From the wickedness of men
  families have decayed, and dominions [have been] ruined. Hence in old writings it was called
  *Bulghakkhana* [i.e., "house of turbulence"]'" (p. 163). Eaton's reading of it: "a remarkable
  theory of political devolution and decay: an enervating climate corrupts men, and corrupted men
  ruin sovereign domains, thereby implicitly preparing the way for conquest by stronger,
  uncorrupted outsiders" (p. 163). Evidence, text 2: Robert Orme, 1763, on "the languor peculiar to
  the unelastic atmosphere of the climate", by which "the natives of Bengal are still of weaker
  frame and more enervated disposition than those of any other province" (pp. 163–164, n. 61). The
  conclusion: "elements of such a discourse were rooted not in Europe but in India itself, and
  specifically in Mughal culture as articulated in or with reference to the imperial province of
  Bengal… men like Orme appear to have appropriated and assimilated values and attitudes that were
  already present in India… If, then, we are talking about any sort of colonial discourse, it is a
  Mughal and not a European discourse that we have here. More accurately, perhaps, what we have is
  a generic discourse of imperialism, quite independent of this or that ethnic identity or imperial
  tradition" (p. 164).
- **The naming argument repeated on a Hindu source — the same finding as the *turushka* point of
  chunk 010, now from the other side.** Of the *Rayavacakamu*'s author on the three sultanates:
  "Significantly, he never refers to the rulers of these states as Muslims, which they were, but as
  'Turks'" (p. 165). His three conclusions from the pair of texts: "the stereotypes that emerge in
  both texts were not of religious communities, but of regional or linguistic groups. Abu'l-fazl
  never wrote of 'Hindus' in Bengal; nor did the *Rayavacakamu* speak of the 'Muslims' of the
  Deccan. Clearly, codes of cultural marking prevalent in the late sixteenth century differed
  greatly from what would appear during British imperial rule"; second, "the corrupted behavior of
  each group was taken not as an inherent or unalterable quality, but simply as a function of their
  geography or diet"; third, both texts "exhibit cultural stereotypes that are today commonly
  associated with Orientalism, or with a European 'colonial discourse'" though they appear "long
  before the rise of European power in India" (p. 165). Against this he sets Partha Chatterjee's
  claim that late nineteenth-century nationalist histories stereotyping Muslims as "'fanatical,
  bigoted, warlike, dissolute, and cruel'" grew out of European scholarship produced under British
  rule (p. 166, n. 65) — and answers that the sixteenth-century texts "suggest a need for a more
  flexible conception of discourse than is provided by 'colonial discourse' or 'invention of
  tradition' models, both of which share a hopelessly shallow sense of temporal depth. Clearly,
  British rulers borrowed and built upon the ideas, stereotypes, and values of pre-British Indian
  rulers, just as the latter had borrowed and built upon those of their own political predecessors"
  (p. 166).
- **The concession he makes, which is the economically load-bearing one.** "it would be wrong to
  conclude that a common imperial discourse effaced any and all qualitative differences between the
  British Raj and earlier Indian empires, such as the Mughals or Vijayanagara. Enormous differences
  distinguished the Raj from its predecessors, **most importantly its association with capitalism,
  which triggered profound social, economic, and political transformations that were quite without
  precedent in Indian history**", citing Ranajit Guha, *Rule of Property for Bengal* (1963)
  (p. 166, n. 67). So the continuity thesis is a claim about discourse and legitimation, not about
  political economy, and he says so himself.
- **The essentialism charge, turned on the critics.** "scholars who decried the essentialism
  Europeans imputed to the 'Orient,' or to colonial India, failed to see the same sort of
  essentialism that they themselves were imputing to 'Europe,' or to European thought, as in one of
  Edward Said's formulations of Orientalism as a monolithic ideology, coherent and all-pervasive,
  extending from Homer to Henry Kissinger" (p. 155). Chakrabarty is his example of the resulting
  wobble: in 1992 he granted that "'any idea of a homogeneous, uncontested "Europe" dissolves under
  analysis'", then in the next sentence that "'a certain version of "Europe," reified and
  celebrated… continues to dominate the discourse of history. Analysis does not make it go away'" —
  so, "Like Prakash, straddling his two horses, Chakrabarty moves uncertainly between two
  contradictory positions: at one moment analysis dissolves Europe, and at another it does not
  dissolve Europe" (pp. 155–156).
- **Why postcolonialism took over, in his account: an academic sociology, not an argument.** The
  term "actually dates to the early 1970s, when it appeared in the context of political theory",
  but its later use "seems to have been related to changing configurations in the sociology of
  Western academe in the 1980s, and in particular, to the arrival in Euro-American universities of
  non-Western scholars of culture and cultural history"; "In their uprooted and culturally
  alienated state, many such 'diasporic' intellectuals repudiated post-modernism's tradition of
  decentredness and vigorously reaffirmed notions of centredness, difference, and cultural
  authenticity", some subscribing to "an *a priori* and enduring Indian community, a
  *Gemeinschaft*, that had been suppressed by British colonialism… a sentiment traceable to
  nineteenth century European romanticism" (p. 156). He endorses Appiah's harsher label in a note:
  "'Postcoloniality… is the condition of what we might ungenerously call a comprador
  intelligentsia: of a relatively small, Western-style, Western-trained, group of writers and
  thinkers who mediate the trade in cultural commodities of world capitalism at the periphery'"
  (p. 156, n. 36). And such notions "found an especially warm reception in an American intellectual
  climate suffused with identity politics, quests for ethnic authenticity, and the celebration of
  diversity and multiculturalism" (p. 157).
- **The Kipling–Dirks pairing completed** (the chapter's two epigraphs, from chunk 010). "It was in
  1889, at the height of the British Raj, that Rudyard Kipling penned his infamous observation
  that 'East is East and West is West, and never the twain shall meet'. A century later… the
  intellectual offspring of Edward Said gave new life to Kipling's sentiments by declaring and
  celebrating the same totalizing opposition, as in Nicholas Dirks's 1989 formulation" — "The
  difference was that while both Kipling and Dirks saw 'East' and 'West' as absolutely opposed, the
  'East' in the postcolonialist view is understood as the creation of the 'West,' and more
  particularly, as the product of a European 'colonial discourse'" (pp. 157–158). The whole shift
  "injected into the intellectual climate winds that were distinctly conservative, Eurocentric, and
  ultimately neo-colonial" (p. 158), and "such a conceptual dichotomy is what had facilitated and
  justified European imperialism in India in the first place" (p. 157).
- **The irony stated twice.** Subaltern Studies, "a movement originally launched in an attempt to
  recover India's history from Colonial, Nationalist, and Marxist metanarratives, was ultimately
  taken over by an intellectual movement which… ended up reaffirming the overwhelming centrality of
  the British intrusion in India" (p. 160); and its practitioners, "in academe's current spirit of
  political correctness and anti-imperialist rhetoric… who seem to have thought they were exposing
  the wicked ends and means of British domination were instead placing the entire explanatory
  weight of India's long history and complex socio-cultural institutions on a European discursive
  formulation that deprived Indians themselves of agency or the ability to make their own history"
  (p. 161).
- **The defence of fieldwork, and the "armchair" parallel.** Mallon is quoted for the cause: "'It is
  hard to return to the archive or the field after engaging in a post-modern critique of the
  transparency of the enterprise. If we are no longer looking for "truth" as irrefutable, clearly
  knowable information, what are we looking for?'" (p. 161). Eaton's own position: "to do Indian
  history one has to visit the Indian hinterland, which means doing what good historians have
  always done: developing a 'nose' for new materials and imaginatively reconstructing the past
  through critical engagement with those materials", where discourse analysis instead "enable[d]
  scholars to cast new interpretive spins on data that not only were already published (and which
  were, conveniently, in English), but were relatively accessible. For Americans, this then made it
  feasible not to go to India or to undertake original field research, while providing unlimited
  opportunities for armchair theorizing" (p. 162). The sting: "a telling feature of classical
  Orientalist scholarship on India had been precisely its detached, armchair quality. Never having
  been to India themselves, and greatly dependent on normative textual materials published in
  Europe, Orientalist scholars like James Mill, Karl Marx, or Max Weber… were innocent of the kind
  of ground-level data that might better have informed their views of the peoples whom they so
  grandly theorized" (p. 162). Bryan Palmer, in a note: "'If discourse is indeed the new
  interpretive key, all of that mucking around in original sources is hardly necessary. A few key
  "texts" will suffice, and their creative "reading" will offer up a history untainted by costly,
  often uncomfortable, research trips'" (p. 162, n. 58).
- **Enumeration as the making of communities — a note, not his own argument, but the one nearest to
  the archive's categories.** Breckenridge and van der Veer, summarising Arjun Appadurai: "'The
  "imagined communities" of Hindu and Muslim nationalism… were produced in the colonial *imaginaire*
  as "enumerated communities"'" (p. 160, n. 52). Eaton cites it as an instance of the logic he is
  criticising, i.e. he does **not** endorse it.
- **The balance sheet that closes the chapter.** In favour: "nobody could object to the usefulness
  of sensitizing new generations of scholars to the complex webs of power relations in which texts
  are always enmeshed, or to considering the possibilities of different readings of texts, even of
  the same texts, according to factors of class, gender, and race, as well as political power.
  Careful historians have always done these things" (p. 166). Against: "a reinsertion of the British
  Raj to the centre stage of Indian history, a neo-colonial vision of an essentialized India and
  Europe locked in implacable binary opposition, an ahistorical and structuralist methodology
  incapable of explaining historical particularity, and an understanding of discourse and culture
  that renders independent South Asia ensnared in Orientalist discursive residues and the region's
  pre-British history befogged in timeless 'tradition'—opaque, distant, and inaccessible" (p. 167).
- Supporting voices he assembles in the notes, all against colonial-discourse analysis: Richard
  Price — "'If meaning is understood as a never-ending series of discursive codes… then the notion
  of change as a historical process… is both moot and unimportant'" (p. 155, n. 32); David Ludden —
  historians of modern South Asia "'seem deeply committed to an all embracing dichotomy between
  India and Europe, and seven volumes of *Subaltern Studies* offer perspectives—"from below"—on
  Indian civilisation as it is defined inside this opposition'" (p. 157, n. 41); Ella Shohat — the
  "post-colonial" "'implies a narrative of progression in which colonialism remains the central
  point of reference in a march of time'" (p. 158, n. 45); Sumit Sarkar — both the Orientalist and
  the discourse-analytic readings of early nineteenth-century Bengal assumed "'total rupture or
  *tabula rasa*, with colonialism completely remoulding such indigenous structures [as caste,
  gender, or class]'" (pp. 159–160, n. 49); Dane Kennedy on the Eurocentrism of colonial-encounter
  literary studies (p. 161, n. 53); McClintock, that "postcolonial" obscures "the continuing
  imperial presence of the United States or its clients in places like Cuba, Chile, Panama, Kuwait,
  Arabia, Afghanistan, or the West Bank" (p. 158, n. 44); Terence Ranger revisiting "the invention
  of tradition" (p. 166, n. 66).
