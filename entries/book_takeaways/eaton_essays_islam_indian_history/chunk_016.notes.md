## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar** (as "the shrine of Baba Farid", "Shaikh Farid al-Din Ganj-i
  Shakar", the shrine at Pakpattan / Ajudhan):
  - **How long the saint was actually there.** "Baba Farid resided in Ajudhan only sixteen or
    twenty-four years" (p. 228, n. 43, citing *Siyar al-auliya'*, 63). Eaton uses this against the
    clan conversion claims: it is "a very short time span for the many eponymous clan founders or
    maliks who are claimed to have met him actually to have been there" (p. 228).
  - **The shrine outlasts the saint as the agent.** "The shrine, on the other hand, has been there
    all along, sustaining the powerful baraka of the saint through its line of diwans" (p. 228).
  - **The renaming.** "by the sixteenth century the very name of the city containing the shrine,
    Ajudhan, became known as Pakpattan ('the holy ferry'), in honour of Baba Farid's memory"
    (p. 228). This is Eaton's explanation of why clans remember the saint rather than a particular
    diwan (p. 228).
  - **Royal visits, dated.** "in October 1398 Tim[u]r, amidst his plundering of northern India,
    took the time to visit the shrine" (p. 228, n. 45, citing *Malfuzat-i Timuri*, abridged trans.
    in Elliot and Dowson, 3:421). "Akbar, in March 1571, opened his sixteenth regnal year in
    Pakpattan, where he implored strength at Baba Farid's shrine" (p. 229, n. 46, citing Abu
    al-Fadl 'Allami, *Akbarnama*, trans. H. Beveridge (Delhi, n.d.), 2:525–6). Eaton generalizes:
    "No major ruler passed by the area without showing deference to its spiritual power" (p. 228).
  - **Shah Jahan's 1629 *farman* — the single most quotable endowment document in the chunk.**
    "in 1629 Shah Jahan issued a farman indicating precisely what his and his predecessor's policy
    was vis-a-vis the shrine" (p. 229). Quoted in full on the page; the operative terms are that
    "The sacred town of Pak Pattan with all its dependencies is by old agreement held in grant from
    the preceding emperors for the 'Langar' expenses of the shrine", held "by Shekh Mohammad,
    Sujjadah Nasheen of the shrine, a descendant of the Baba"; that "the proceeds thereof are
    applied to his own maintenance and to that of the Durveshes and Khadims attached thereto, as
    well as to the feed of Travellers and the repair and adornment of the building"; and the order
    "Continue the whole 'Muhal' [i.e., pargana] in endowment to the shrine." (p. 229, n. 47, citing
    **West Pakistan Board of Revenue, Lahore. File 131/6/24/24**). Note the four stated heads of
    expenditure — custodian, staff, travellers' food, fabric repair — and that the grant is
    described as pre-existing ("by old agreement… from the preceding emperors").
  - **What the shrine gave the state in return: revenue collection.** "In return for this royal
    patronage, the diwans of Pakpattan performed several functions for the Mughal government. Above
    all, on certain tracts of land they received the government's share of all crops on which
    revenue was levied in kind, whereas the tax on cash crops such as cotton, indigo, or tobacco
    had to be paid in cash and went straight to revenue officials without passing the diwan"
    (p. 229, n. 48, citing *Punjab District Gazetteers*, vol. 18-A, Montgomery District, 1933
    (Lahore, 1935), 38).
  - **The incentive that follows, stated as a mechanism.** "It was therefore in the diwan's
    interest as a de facto chaudh[ri], first, that agriculture be expanded at the expense of
    pastoralism, for the diwan derived no cattle tax from the barr clans, and secondly, that food
    crops be sown instead of cash crops" (p. 229). Eaton's inference is flagged by him as
    unsupported: "Although there is no corroborating evidence to this effect, these circumstances
    suggest that the diwans might have been promoters of peasant agriculture, and might explain the
    Jawahir-i Farid['s] seeming approval of peasant agriculture as a way of life for the shrine's
    dependent clans" (p. 229). The *Jawahir-i Faridi* passage he means enumerates the shrine's
    *murid* clans as cultivators: "so these clans—Adhank, Valank, and Sipan—are all farmers in
    Pakpattan… The Baritis are originally Jats.… They live in Pakpattan and practice agriculture"
    (p. 229, n. 49, citing *Jawahir-i Faridi*, 397).
  - **The custodian's title is a revenue title.** "the special title of Baba Farid's chief
    successor, a personage who at other shrines was designated simply sajada nishin ('one who sits
    on the prayer carpet'), at Pakpattan was and is 'diwan,' a term taken directly from the lexicon
    of Indo-Islamic royal courts, and possibly alluding to the man's revenue-collecting function
    mentioned above" (p. 230). "possibly" is Eaton's. Also: "the very word for 'shrine' used in the
    subcontinent, 'dargah,' is also the word for a royal court" (p. 230).
  - **The descendants became a landholding caste.** "The darweshes and khadims (i.e., 'servants')
    mentioned in Shah Jahan's farman as receiving royal support comprised the many hundreds of
    lineal descendants of Baba Farid living in the Pakpattan region. So numerous were these
    descendants that they literally formed a separate zamindar caste in the area, the 'Chishti'
    caste, possessing both economic privileges and ritual status vis-a-vis the local clans"
    (p. 230). A colonial revenue source is quoted on their relation to labour: the Chishtis were
    "a semi-religious Mussalman tribe" having "considerable local influence," who "are not working
    agriculturalists, but depend for cultivation entirely on tenants" (p. 230, n. 52, citing
    Patrick J. Fagan, *Assessment Report for Pakpattan Tahsil* (Lahore, 1896), 50 — the running
    text calls it "An 1897 British Assessment Report"; see Doubts). Eaton extends it backwards as a
    probability: "It is probable that the Chishtis enjoyed the same sort of proprietary rights in
    relation to their tenant clients from a very early date" (p. 230).
  - **The proliferation of descendants had a name.** "It was on account of this vast proliferation
    of descendants that even in the seventeenth century Baba Farid himself was called 'the Second
    Adam'" (p. 230, n. 51, citing G. A. Storey, *Persian Literature, a Bio-bibliographical Survey*
    (London, 1927–71), 2:986).
  - **Allegiance was sworn to the family, not the saint.** "the clans swore allegiance not to Baba
    Farid but to his family—i.e., the Chishti caste of Pakpattan" (p. 230), on the *Jawahir-i
    Faridi*: "The Khokhars, Bhattis, Dhudhis, and Hans are found in the environs of Pakpattan, and
    all the clans take bai'at with this family and have become murids" (p. 230, n. 53, citing
    *Jawahir-i Faridi*, 323); elsewhere "these clans serve the progeny of Baba Farid" (p. 231,
    n. 54, citing ibid., 396, with "Emphasis added" by Eaton).
  - **What *bai'at* meant in practice.** "bai'at means a compact of allegiance, which in early
    Sufism meant spiritual allegiance only, but which among the unlettered Jat clans carried
    political as well as ethical obligations" (p. 231) — and, in one *Jawahir-i Faridi* passage,
    military ones: the clans who became Muslim from Baba Farid's time "are the possessors of
    dignity in the environs of Pakpattan. They can place ten thousand cavalry and foot soldiers in
    his [Baba Farid's] service, and have complete faith in Baba Farid and his descendants, and are
    their murids" (p. 231, n. 55, citing ibid., 397–8). Eaton dates the account to Jahangir's reign
    and notes the levy was not called on then: "we do not hear that the diwan's Jat murids were
    actually called upon to do battle in service of the shrine" (p. 231).
  - **The shrine as an armed territorial power, 1757–1810 — with the dates and the outcome.**
    "in 1757 Diwan 'Abd as-Subhan (1752–66) gathered an army of his Jat murids, attacked the raja
    of Bikaner, and thereby expanded the shrine's territorial holdings for the first time to the
    east of the Sutlej" (p. 231, n. 56, citing *Montgomery District Gazetteer* (1933), 38).
    "Supported mainly by his Wattu murids, the diwan successfully defended a Sikh attack on
    Pakpattan around 1776 in which Hira Singh was killed, and then pursued the retreating Sikhs
    with four thousand cavalry, killing a great number of them" (p. 231, nn. 57–58, citing ibid.,
    35, and Syad Muhammad Latif, *History of the Punjab* (Lahore, n.d.), 312). The attacker was
    "the expanding Sikh power to the north, in particular the Nakkai mi[s]l headed by Hira Singh"
    (p. 231).
  - **The endowment destroyed, and what replaced it — a precise figure.** "In 1810 the shrine's
    extensive holdings were seized by Ranjit Singh, and the diwans of Pakpattan, their brief period
    of political independence now at an end, reverted to their former status of political
    intermediaries" (p. 231). The footnote quantifies the loss: "Ranjit Singh allowed the diwan and
    his retainers only Rs. 1,000 a year for their maintenance, derived from the town duties of
    Pakpattan, in addition to a fourth share of four small villages nearby" (p. 231, n. 59, citing
    *Journal of the Asiatic Society of Bengal*, 6 (1837): 193). Eaton's gloss: "it was under much
    worse terms than under the Mughals" (p. 231, n. 59).
  - **Marriage alliances, counted from a dated source.** "The Jawahir-i Farid[i] not only lists the
    clans that, as of 1623, had entered into a bride-giving relationship with the diwan and his
    family, but even names the groom and the bride's father in such alliances" (p. 232). The
    counts as printed: of thirteen Khokhar–shrine alliances, "seven of the Khokhar brides were
    daughters of clan m[a]liks, or chiefs", and "three brides went to the diwans themselves and six
    went to sons of various diwans"; the earliest was "a Khokhar bride given to a son of Diwan
    Ahmad Shah, who was diwan of the shrine from 1452 to 1474" (p. 232, n. 60, citing *Jawahir-i
    Farid[i]*, 323–4). "Of five exchanges involving the Bhattis, two brides were daughters of
    Bhatti chiefs and three grooms were sons of diwans, the earliest alliance dating back to the
    late 1400s" (p. 232, n. 61). "Of four alliances mentioned between the diwan's family and the
    Hans and Dhudhi tribes, one involved a chief's daughter and two others involved
    granddaughters, and on the other side one alliance involved a diwan and the other three, sons
    of diwans" (p. 232, n. 62).
  - **The direction of bride-giving is the patronage relation.** "the latter groups gave their
    daughters to the diwans and their immediate family, whereas Chishti daughters were evidently
    kept within the caste"; "client-patron relations among castes are often structured by the
    direction of bride-giving, the bride-giving groups normally being clients of the bride-receiving
    group" (p. 232). Eaton's comparison: "the kinship relations of the diwan and the clans
    immediately call to mind those of the Mughal court and its subordinate Rajput clans, except
    that in the case of the diwans religious as well as political patronage was involved" (p. 232).
  - **The shrine's internal hierarchy, ranked.** "the adab of the shrine, like that of the court of
    Delhi, also established and sustained a hierarchic principle: in descending rank there was the
    diw[a]n, the diw[a]n's family, the khalifas and shrine functionaries, the Chishti caste, the
    clan leaders, and the common Jat agriculturalists" (p. 236). And: the code "sorted, arranged,
    and held such peoples in a graded hierarchy that has persisted for centuries" (p. 236). No end
    date is given for "has persisted" (not stated).
  - **The turban's several simultaneous meanings.** "the tying of the turban (dast[a]r
    ba[n]d[i]) possessed a great symbolic repertoire: it defined relations of kinship between the
    shrine and subordinate clans, it symbolically conferred legitimacy on actual rulers in Delhi,
    and it conferred spiritual discipleship at the shrine itself" (p. 236). Earlier on the same
    point: "the dastar band[i] ceremony… has obvious parallels with a coronation ceremony" (p. 230).
  - **The Gate of Paradise.** "Another aspect of the shrine's adab was its carefully defined
    formula for achieving religious transcendence, namely, the practice of passing through its Gate
    of Paradise (bihish[ti] [darwaza — the term is garbled in the text layer, see Doubts]) enabling
    all who did so to ritually enter paradise" (p. 236). This is the same rite chunk 015 recorded
    as the *shisha darwaza* opened at the 'urs (chunk 015, p. 219); the two names are not
    reconciled here (not stated).
  - **Its place among Indian Muslim holy places.** the tombs "of Mu'in ad-Din Chishti at Ajmer,
    Nizam ad-Din Auliya' at Delhi, and Baba Farid at Pakpattan—became the first Muslim holy places
    within India", which meant "that South Asian Muslims were no longer compelled to look
    exclusively to the Middle East for spiritual inspiration" (p. 236). Footnote: "early
    hagiographic manuals declar[e] that a certain number of pilgrimages to certain Sufi shrines in
    India would be equivalent in moral value to a single pilgrimage to Mecca" (p. 236, n. 68); no
    manual is named and no number given (not stated).
  - **The 1935–38 succession litigation — the archive-relevant fact that a custodianship dispute
    generated a mass documentary record.** "In December 1934, Sa'id Muhammad, who was the religious
    head of the shrine of Baba Farid, died, leaving behind him his eleven-year-old son, Qutb
    al-Din, as his chosen successor. Only four months later, however, a local group of devotees at
    the shrine, who like Sa'id Muhammad were genealogical descendants of Baba Farid, filed a legal
    plaint in the nearby District Court in which they not only disputed Qutb al-Din's claim to the
    shrine's leadership, but substituted a rival claimant, Ghulam Ras[u]l, for this post" (p. 238).
    The record: "Between 6 April 1935, when Ghulam Rasul filed his plaint, and 2 December 1938,
    when a District Court judge decided the case in favour of the defendant, Qutb al-Din, no less
    than 159 oral depositions were filed on behalf of the plaintiff and 172 on behalf of the
    defendant, covering in all over 836 typed pages. In addition, both sides filed nearly three
    hundred documents with the Court" (p. 238). The deponents were "for the most part… local
    peasant-devotees from the district of Montgomery (now Sahiwal), Bahawalpur and Lyallpur"
    (p. 238). Source of the record: **Lahore High Court, Regular First Appeal No. 93 of 1939:
    *Dewan Ghulam Rasul vs. Ghulam Qutab-ud-din*, 5 vols (Lahore, 1940)** (p. 239, n. 3).
  - **The custodian's titles, as the depositions have them.** "Called the sajada-nishin,
    ga[dd]-nishin, or most commonly the 'Diwan,' the shrine's religious leader was a direct lineal
    descendant of the saint and was believed to be Baba Farid's living representative" (p. 239).
    "Both the Diwan and the shrine itself, a magnificent marble edifice constructed in the
    fourteenth century, are perceived to be carriers of Baba Farid's baraka" (p. 239) — see Doubts
    on which building this is. "Hence both the Diwan and the shrine functioned as intermediaries
    for an intermediary, as on-going vehicles of the saint's mediative power" (p. 239).
  - ***Nazrana* — the inward cash flow, in the devotees' own words.** "the murids made donations,
    usually cash contributions, that were called nazra[na]" (p. 239). A cultivator from Lyallpur
    District: "We give nazranas to Dewan Sahib out of love, respect and regard for the Pir and to
    invoke his blessings for the good of the murid" (p. 239, n. 2, citing the appeal record,
    2:239). The transaction: "murids regularly made religious vows or 'contracts' with Baba Farid,
    as a mediator between them and God, in hopes of receiving any number of concrete favours, such
    as female fertility, good crops, relief from illness, etc." (p. 239).
  - **Donations were earmarked by the donor.** "Though the vow was made with the saint, the
    donations went to one of the saint's on-going representatives, namely, either the Diwan or the
    shrine as a whole… Some murids testified that, having made the pious visit to Pakpattan, they
    would 'earmark' their nazrana to go either in support of the shrine's public kitchen, or to the
    Diwan. Others gave their nazrana to the Diwan when he visited them in their villages. The
    amount of nazr[a]na varied, of course, with the means of the devotee" (p. 239, n. 4, citing the
    appeal record, 1:55; 2:28, 239). No amounts are given (not stated).
  - **The *langar*, located and quantified.** "Located in the shrine's courtyard just opposite Baba
    Farid's tomb, the langar was a vast operation, daily providing physical sustenance to the many
    pilgrims who came to pay respects and devotions at the shrine. One former worker in the kitchen
    estimated that between 240 and 320 [l]bs. (i.e. 3 or 4 mans) of wheat were consumed there every
    day, along with commensurate amounts of da[l], or split pea" (p. 240, n. 5, citing the appeal
    record, 2:256). This is a witness estimate in a lawsuit, not the shrine's accounts, and no
    figure for pilgrims served is given (not stated).
  - **The outward flow in kind: sugar and turbans, controlled by the Diwan alone.** "Murids also
    received religious goods of a mor[e] symbolic sort, consisting of sugar or turbans, distributed
    only by the Diwan, and only on the occasion of particularly important shrine rituals at which
    he officiated" (p. 240, n. 6, citing the appeal record, 1:[ ]33 — folio garbled). "the
    distribution of sugar represented the outward flow of Baba Farid's bounty and blessings", and
    linked Diwan and murids to "their spiritual ancestor, Baba Farid, whose best-known epithet,
    Ganj-i Shakar, or 'treasury of sugar,' was associated with legends concerning the saint's
    severe austerities" (p. 240). Turbans "likewise served to connect murids to the Diwan, and both
    of these parties to Baba Farid himself, whose spiritual authority at a very early period in the
    history of the shrine's cult came to be represented by his turban" (p. 240).
  - **The ritual repertoire is dated to the generation after the saint.** The *Jawahir-i Faridi*,
    "an important collection of the lives of Baba Farid's spiritual and lineal descendants completed
    in 1623, records that all the major rituals associated with the shrine, including those dealing
    with Baba Farid's turban, had become instituted in the generation following the saint's death,
    that is, in the late thirteenth century" (p. 240, n. 8, citing *Jawahir-i Faridi*, comp. 'Ali
    Asghar Chishti (Lahore, 1883–4), 298–300). Same source and folios as chunk 015, p. 219, n. 11 —
    consistent between the two essays.
  - **The saint's devotees, described in scale without a number.** "this saint has remained for many
    centuries an immensely popular 'invisible friend' among millions of villagers inhabiting the
    southwestern Punjab" (p. 238); "Like any other of Baba Farid's millions of devotees, Timur
    implored the saint's intercession with God for the attainment of worldly concerns" (p. 228,
    n. 45). "Millions" is Eaton's own characterization; no count or source for it is given
    (not stated).

- **samadhi-of-maharaja-ranjit-singh** — **figure-level match only, and a thin one; see Doubts.**
  Ranjit Singh appears here solely as the power that ended the Pakpattan shrine's endowment: he
  seized "the shrine's extensive holdings" in 1810 and allowed the diwan and his retainers "only
  Rs. 1,000 a year for their maintenance, derived from the town duties of Pakpattan, in addition to
  a fourth share of four small villages nearby" (p. 231 and n. 59). Nothing is said about the
  Samadhi, Lahore, or his death (not stated).

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The shrine's own economic model, named by Eaton: an exchange.** The essay's section heading is
  "**THE SHRINE AND THE EXCHANGE OF RELIGIOUS GOODS**" (p. 239), and the structure is stated in
  patron–client terms: "an exchange of 'religious goods' between the shrine's patrons and its
  clients—the chief patron being the Diwan, and the clients being the masses of murids" (p. 239).
  Inward flow: *nazrana*, "usually cash contributions" (p. 239). Outward flow: "the opposite flow,
  from the shrine outward to the devotees, took place in several ways" — the *langar*, sugar, and
  turbans (pp. 239–240).
- **Endowment structure, 1629**: the whole *pargana* ("Muhal") of Pak Pattan "with all its
  dependencies", in grant from previous emperors, designated for *langar* expenses, and applied to
  four heads: the *sajjada nashin*'s maintenance, the *darweshes* and *khadims*, "the feed of
  Travellers", and "the repair and adornment of the building" (p. 229).
- **Revenue farming as the custodian's function**: the government's in-kind crop share on certain
  tracts collected by the diwan; cash crops bypassing him entirely (p. 229). The diwan is described
  as "a de facto chaudh[ri]" (p. 229).
- **A cattle economy outside the tax net**: "the diwan derived no cattle tax from the barr clans"
  (p. 229) — which is why, on Eaton's reading, the shrine's fiscal interest lay with settled
  agriculture.
- **Landholding by the saint's descendants**: the "Chishti" *zamindar* caste, hundreds strong,
  cultivating through tenants rather than themselves, with "both economic privileges and ritual
  status vis-a-vis the local clans" (p. 230).
- **Military service as a component of discipleship**: *bai'at* carrying "political as well as
  ethical obligations" and, in one passage, a claimed levy of "ten thousand cavalry and foot
  soldiers" (p. 231); actually mobilized in 1757 and c. 1776, the latter with "four thousand
  cavalry" in pursuit (p. 231).
- **Territorial expansion by the shrine itself**: holdings extended east of the Sutlej for the first
  time in 1757 (p. 231).
- **Expropriation and its replacement**: 1810 seizure by Ranjit Singh; the substitute settlement was
  Rs. 1,000 a year from Pakpattan's town duties plus a quarter share of four small villages
  (p. 231, n. 59).
- **Marriage as patronage infrastructure**: counted alliances with Khokhar (13), Bhatti (5), Hans
  and Dhudhi (4 between them) as of 1623, asymmetric in direction — clans give brides, Chishtis do
  not (p. 232).
- **Langar operations, 1930s**: courtyard site opposite the tomb; "between 240 and 320 [l]bs. (i.e.
  3 or 4 mans) of wheat… every day, along with commensurate amounts of da[l]" (p. 240).
- **Donor earmarking**: devotees could direct *nazrana* to the public kitchen or to the Diwan
  personally; the Diwan also collected on village tours (p. 239).
- **Distributions in kind, monopolized by the custodian**: sugar and turbans, "distributed only by
  the Diwan, and only on the occasion of particularly important shrine rituals at which he
  officiated" (p. 240).
- **Ritual calendar as institution**: the shrine "displayed throughout the ceremonies and
  celebrations that marked its liturgical calendar the pageantry of both the court of God and the
  court of Delhi" (p. 228). No individual festival dates are given in this chunk (not stated).
- **Institutional dating**: all major rituals, including the turban rites, instituted "in the

- **Titles of the custodianship**: *sajada nishin* elsewhere, *diwan* at Pakpattan (p. 230); and in

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The sugar epithet, as an 1884 gazetteer tells it.** "'By continuous fasting,' noted a British
  gazetteer in 1884, 'his body is said to have become so pure that whatever he put into his mouth
  to allay the cravings of hunger, even earth and stones, was immediately changed into sugar, whence
  his name Shakar-Ganj, or sugar store.'" The same source gives a variant: "the saint, when hungry,
  used to tie a wooden cake (chappati) or a branch of wooden dates to his stomach, and that this
  composed his sole nourishment for thirty years" (p. 240, n. 7). Reported by Eaton as the
  gazetteer's report, not endorsed.
- **Baba Farid converts Ray Siyal.** "According to both the shrine's hagiographic accounts and the
  earliest known history of the clan, the Siyals of Jhang District were introduced to Islam by Baba
  Farid himself, who converted Ray Siyal, the clan's founder. After this event, according to these
  accounts, all Siy[a]ls presumably, were Muslim" (p. 233). Eaton reports this as the accounts'
  claim and then refutes it with Table 9.2.
- **The frock and turban given to the Siyal chief**, sent "to settle and populate the Chenab-Ravi
  area" — from "the hagiographic literature" (p. 230, n. 50, *Jawahir-i Faridi*, 324).
- **Passing through the Gate of Paradise**, "enabling all who did so to ritually enter paradise" —
  described as part of the shrine's *adab* (p. 236).
- **The turban as vessel of the saint's authority** — "whose spiritual authority at a very early
  period in the history of the shrine's cult came to be represented by his turban" (p. 240, n. 8
  attributing the institution of the turban rituals to the *Jawahir-i Faridi*).
- **Equivalence of Indian pilgrimage to the hajj** — "early hagiographic manuals declar[e] that a
  certain number of pilgrimages to certain Sufi shrines in India would be equivalent in moral value
  to a single pilgrimage to Mecca" (p. 236, n. 68); the manuals are unnamed (not stated).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The essay's central move: the converting agent was the institution, not the man.** "If one were
  to hypothesize that the agent of the clans' conversion, instead of Baba Farid himself, was the
  shrine of Baba Farid as a highly complex religious and social institution, a number of problems
  fade" (p. 228). This is the positive claim chunk 015 recorded as outstanding at p. 227, and it is
  offered as a hypothesis, with the clans' own claims salvaged rather than discarded: "we need not,
  because of these hagiographic and chronological problems, dismiss outright the claims of the
  clans" (p. 228).
- **Why the clans nonetheless name the saint.** Because the shrine and the saint were fully
  identified — the renaming of Ajudhan to Pakpattan is his evidence — "it would hardly be surprising
  that the clans, in reconstructing the story of their own conversion to Islam, should recall the
  name of the saint himself and not that of any particular diwan" (p. 228).
- **Nonreligious patronage as the mechanism of religious incorporation — the chunk's thesis for the
  economics reader.** "throughout the period when Jat groups moved up the riverine region and on to
  the barr country, there were several nonreligious ways in which the shrine patronized the clans
  and thus integrated them into its wide orbit of social and political influence, paving the way for
  the Jats' gradual integration into its ritual and religious structure" (p. 228). The order of
  causation is explicit: material and political patronage first, ritual and religious integration
  after. The four ties he then documents in sequence are economic (pp. 229–230), political and
  military (p. 231), kinship (p. 232), and ritual (pp. 232–235): "Paralleling these economic,
  political, and kinship ties between the shrine and its neighboring clans, the latter gradually
  became integrated into the shrine's ritual functionings" (p. 232).
- **The "theatre-state" formulation, borrowed from Geertz.** "In Clifford Geertz's terms, the shrine
  provided the tribes with a tiny 'theatre-state' of their own; that is, it displayed throughout the
  ceremonies and celebrations that marked its liturgical calendar the pageantry of both the court of
  God and the court of Delhi, albeit on a microcosmic scale" (p. 228) — the completion of the
  "theatre versus Scripture" image from p. 216 (chunk 015).
- **What the clans got from that, stated as a political good.** "The shrine thus gave clan leaders
  and their followers not only access to Islam, but the honour of participating in the reflected
  splendour of the Sultanate or Mughal courts without actually being directly subservient to the
  authorities in Delhi" (p. 228) — i.e. prestige without subordination, which is Eaton's account of
  why chiefs bought in.
- **A two-way intermediary.** "As an intermediary institution in both a religious and a political
  sense, the shrine of Baba Farid was itself patronized by Delhi" (p. 228), and "As the Delhi court
  patronized the shrine, so also the shrine patronized the agricultural clans, in some ways even
  mimicking the symbols of the larger court" (pp. 229–230). Eaton's summary at p. 228: "since the
  third diwan, the Delhi court lavished the revenues of towns and villages in support of the shrine
  and its attendants" — see Doubts on the "third diwan" against chunk 015's Ibn Battuta evidence.
- **Courtly vocabulary read as evidence of function, not just imitation.** *dargah* as royal court,
  *diwan* as a revenue office, *dastar bandi* as coronation (p. 230) — with the *diwan* title
  "possibly alluding to the man's revenue-collecting function" (p. 230), which links the symbolic
  argument back to the fiscal one.
- **Conversion was slow, unconscious, and not en bloc — argued from a quantitative index.** "at no
  time, whether in Baba Farid's day or later, were the tribal murids of the shrine converted to
  Islam en bloc. On the contrary, the conversion process seems to have been remarkably slow"
  (pp. 232–233). The index is Bulliet's: "the frequency with which Muslim given names were bestowed
  on males", justified by Bulliet's own reasoning that naming "is an act that reflects, usually
  unconsciously, their view of the society around them at that particular point in time" (p. 233,
  n. 63, citing Bulliet, 'Conversion to Islam and the Emergence of Muslim Society in Iran', in
  N. Levtzion, ed., *Conversion to Islam* (New York, 1979), 43). The result: Muslim names appear
  only "in the early fifteenth century", rise "from 10.24 per cent of the total to 39.21 per cent"
  by the early seventeenth century, reach parity "about the middle of that century", become
  "clearly preponderant (81.81 per cent)" in the early eighteenth, and Punjabi secular names
  disappear entirely by the early nineteenth (pp. 233–234). Conclusion: "The whole conversion
  process thus involved a period from the sixth to the nineteenth generation, or from the late
  fourteenth to the early nineteenth centuries" (p. 234).
- **He states the generalization as conditional.** "If these data on the Siyals are at all
  indicative of the conversion pattern for the other clans that had taken bai'at with Baba Farid's
  shrine, or for that matter with any other shrine, then we may conclude that religious conversion
  among Punjabi clans was very slow indeed—not only slow, but probably unconscious as well"
  (p. 234). The "if" and the "probably" are his.
- **Why unconsciousness is plausible — an argument about categories, not belief.** "This was, after
  all, a period long before either British census officials or zealous reformers began urging
  Indians to place themselves into sharply differentiated religious categories" (p. 234).
- **What devotees took themselves to be: clients and sponsors, not adherents of a text.** "murids of
  Baba Farid's shrine probably saw themselves less in terms of adherents of the Book and more in
  terms of clients and sponsors of a theater-shrine that displayed the wondrous baraka of its saint
  through its pageantry, festivals, and ceremonies" (p. 235) — the direct answer to the question the
  essay opened with at p. 215 (chunk 015).
- **A colonial witness used, and its bias marked.** Eaton quotes Miles Irving, 1911: "To the
  ordinary Montgomery cattle-thief who comes once a year to Pakpattan to obtain remission for the
  enormities of the past twelve months, Baba Farid is the mediator by whose merits he obtains
  forgiveness, assurance of which he obtains through the presence in the flesh of the descendant of
  the saint" (p. 235). He then discounts the framing: "It was, of course, Irving's
  imperial-administrative viewpoint that caused him to judge most clans of the barr country as
  'cattle-thieves' and their actions as 'enormities'" (p. 235, n. 66). Note the substantive datum
  inside the quotation — an annual visit, and forgiveness mediated by a living descendant.
- **The shrine's historical function, stated as a formula.** "In both the Sultanate and Mughal
  periods a long tradition of economic, political, and social patronage by the diwans had absorbed
  into the shrine's orbit of ritual influence groups which, as former pastoralists who had only
  recently achieved a settled way of life, had not formerly been integrated into anything
  approaching urban culture. It was the shrine's historical function to incorporate local systems of
  culture into a larger cultural system, to connect rustic clans politically with Delhi and
  religiously with Islam" (p. 235).
- **Not conversion from Hinduism, but conversion instead of Sanskritization.** "This process,
  however, did not involve for newly incorporated groups a change from a Hindu to a Muslim identity,
  for at the time of the clans' first contact with Baba Farid's shrine these groups had not yet
  become integrated into the Hindu ritual or social structure" (p. 235). The positive mechanism is
  put in status terms: "Although the precise nature of the Jats' pre-Muslim religion is as yet
  unclear, they seem to have had a deep-rooted tradition of social egalitarianism. Hence their rise
  in status from low Sudras to agrarian zamindars could more easily find ideological/ritual
  expression in Islam than within the highly stratified Hindu social system via the process of
  'Sanskritization'" (p. 235). "as yet unclear" and "seem to" are his hedges. The Sikh parallel is
  attributed to Irfan Habib, not claimed by Eaton (p. 235, n. 67).
- **The conclusion qualifies the whole essay.** "Although I have argued that the shrine of Baba
  Farid integrated local systems of culture into a larger one, the shrine nonetheless remained a
  local manifestation of that larger culture" (pp. 235–236) — and the larger culture is the Chishti
  order's precedence at Delhi, which made Ajmer, Delhi and Pakpattan "the first Muslim holy places
  within India" (p. 236). "Shrines like that of Baba Farid made a universal culture system available
  to local groups, enabling such groups to transcend their local microcosms" (p. 236).
- **Adab of the shrine set beside adab of the court — the volume's original theme.** "there evolved
  a distinctive code of conduct, or adab, of the shrines, just as medieval Indo-Muslim culture had
  evolved an equally distinctive adab of the court, i.e., a highly elaborated code of etiquette and
  pageantry that both dazzled and integrated into its structure the subjects of the kingdom"
  (p. 236). At Pakpattan the *adab* "comprised the whole set of rituals and symbols that became
  institutionalized almost immediately after Baba Farid's death and that served progressively to
  assimilate various groups into its social and religious world" (p. 236).
- **Integration and stratification are the same act — an important qualification of the welfare
  reading.** "Even while integrating diverse peoples into a common religious culture, the shrine's
  highly elaborated code of conduct sorted, arranged, and held such peoples in a graded hierarchy
  that has persisted for centuries" (p. 236). The shrine is not described as a leveller.
- **Essay 10's stated gap in the literature.** "Many scholars of Islamic civilization have made this
  observation in a general way. But what has been lacking is any careful study of how Islam after
  the thirteenth century was mediated to whole societies and was sustained by any one of these tomb
  cults; and, above all, how Islam and the cult were viewed from the local perspective, that is, by
  those believers who were integrated into the religious and social world of one of these shrines"
  (pp. 237–238). The essay's aim is "analysing the local perceptions of devotees" (p. 238).
- **The comparative frame for essay 10: Peter Brown on Latin Christianity.** Brown's cult of saints
  grew "in the great cemeteries that lay outside the cities of the Roman world", the tombs
  privileged as places "where the contrasted poles of Heaven and Earth met" (p. 237). Eaton draws
  the parallel explicitly: "Looking at post-thirteenth century Islam as it was naturalized beyond
  the Arab world, one finds a strikingly similar phenomenon. Here, too, there arose a cult of
  saints—many cults, actually—which had the effect of softening if not erasing the stark boundary
  separating Heaven and Earth as posited by Islam in its formal or legalistic sense" (p. 237). The
  comparison is Eaton's; Brown writes only about Christianity.
- **A methodological windfall, and Eaton says why.** "Just as the immediate beneficiaries of legal
  disputes are lawyers, historians are perhaps the ultimate beneficiaries" (p. 238) — the succession
  suit forced hundreds of peasant devotees to testify about shrine practice, producing "a remarkable
  sampling of the shrine's local 'constituency', documenting how the latter viewed the shrine's
  relationship to God, to Islamic Law, to the British government that ultimately ruled them, and to
  their own daily lives" (p. 238). **For an archive built on field testimony this is a model source
  type: litigation as an accidental oral-history corpus.**
- **An assumption Eaton flags himself.** "The past tense is used here and in the following discussion
  because the data on which the discussion is based belong to the period 1935–8. It is fair to
  assume, however, that the most general aspects of the shrine as described here hold as true for
  the present day as for the 1930s" (p. 238, unnumbered footnote). That assumption is asserted, not
  evidenced — and "the present day" means the essay's 1982 original (p. 237), not the 2000/2003
  volume.
- **Intermediaries all the way down.** "both the Diwan and the shrine functioned as intermediaries
  for an intermediary, as on-going vehicles of the saint's mediative power" (p. 239) — the mechanism
  by which a hereditary custodian monetizes a dead saint's *baraka*, stated by Eaton in structural
  rather than polemical terms.
