# eaton_essays_islam_indian_history — chunk 007 — pp. 98–110

The end of Chapter Three (Calicut: Nikitin's drift toward Islam, the Portuguese at Calicut,
summary) and the opening of **Chapter Four, "Temple Desecration and Indo-Muslim States"** — the
most directly useful chapter so far for an archive that holds temples and shrines side by side.

Three things in this chunk matter most. **The 1455 Persian inscription over the tomb-shrine of
Abdullah Shah Changal at Dhar** is quoted at length: a shrine foundation legend with martyred
Sufis thrown into a well, a temple turned into a mosque, a raja converted, a neglected grave, and
a sultan's rebuilding — and Eaton uses it to show that a claim of temple destruction can be a
**community's origin story composed four hundred years after the event**, not a report of it.
**Elliot and Dowson's 1849 *History of India as Told by its Own Historians* is identified as the
source of most modern temple-destruction claims, with its editor's own stated colonial motive
quoted from his preface.** And **Vasco da Gama prays before a Hindu image at Calicut taking it
for the Virgin** — the sharpest instance of the observer-bias thesis, and a description of a
working temple by a hostile observer who did not know what he was looking at.

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- **The tomb-shrine of Abdullah Shah Changal at Dhar, Madhya Pradesh** (formerly the capital of
  Malwa), with an inscription "dated 1455, found over the doorway" — "a 42-verse Persian *ghazal*"
  (p. 108). Its saint "broke the images of the false deities, and turned the idol-temple into a
  mosque"; the temple destruction is placed in the reign of **Raja Bhoja**, "a renowned Paramara
  king who had ruled over the region from 1010 to 1053" (p. 108). Rebuilt by **Sultan Mahmud
  Khalaji** ("the Khilji king Mahmud Shah", styled "'Alau-d-din Wad-dunya Abu'l-Muzaffar"), whose
  works "included a strong vault, a mosque, and a caravan-sarai" (p. 109). Source: G. Yazdani, ed.
  and trans., "The Inscription of the Tomb of 'Abdullah Shah Changal at Dhar", *Epigraphia
  Indo-Moslemica* (1909), 1–5 (p. 109, n. 7).
- **The great Shiva temple in Thali, Calicut** — "an enormous hewn-stone Hindu temple, probably the
  great Shiva temple in Thali in the heart of the city", which da Gama entered "through a bronze
  door", ascending "the temple's stone steps" (p. 100). See Practices for its interior.
- **The fort at Diu**, "still standing", whose "massive walls and fortified towers reflect the
  Portuguese intention of achieving and enforcing a monopoly on the maritime export of Indian
  merchandise" (p. 104).
- **Lamghan**, east of Kabul, attacked by the Ghaznavid Sultan **Sabuktigin (r. 977–97) in 986**,
  when he "attacked and defeated the Hindu Shahi raja **who controlled the region between Kabul and
  northwest Punjab**". 'Utbi, the sultan's son's personal secretary: "'He conquered it and set fire
  to the places in its vicinity which were inhabited by infidels, and demolishing the idol-temples,
  he established Islam in them'" (p. 110, citing 'Utbi, *Tarikh-i Yamini*, in Elliot and Dowson
  2:22, with a thirteenth-century Persian translation made in 1206). **The earliest datable
  desecration in the essay, and its theatre includes northwest Punjab.**
- **Ahmad bin Majid**, the Arab master pilot who, "According to one tradition", guided da Gama from
  Milindi to Calicut — "not only among the most skillful Arab pilots of the age but a scholar who
  had written many learned treatises on the art of navigating the Indian Ocean" (p. 99; Tibbetts's
  translation of the *Kitab al-Fawa'id* cited at n. 32).
- **Pedro Cabral's mission of 1500** — see Practices for the massacre and bombardment.
- **Afanasy Nikitin**, who "did adopt a Muslim name, **Khwaja Yusuf Khurasani**, under which he
  apparently attempted to pass as a Muslim", and who kept the Ramazan fast with Muslims (p. 98).

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The inside of a working Hindu temple at Calicut in 1498, as described by men who thought it a
  church.** Da Gama and his attendants "approached the image of a Hindu goddess, probably Kali or
  Lakshmi. Supposing it to be a statue of the Virgin Mary, they offered prayers. Seeing **Brahman
  priests in the temple's precincts**, the Portuguese took these to be church servants and
  compared the Brahman's **sacred threads** with the stoles of their own deacons. Along the walls
  were **painted images of Hindu deities, some with teeth protruding an inch from their mouths,
  and having four or five arms**. These da Gama and his fellows mistook for Christian saints"
  (p. 100, citing Ravenstein, *Journal of Vasco da Gama*, 52–5). A dated eyewitness description of
  temple sculpture, priesthood and wall painting.
- **Shrine patronage as a royal act, recorded on the shrine itself.** In the Dhar inscription, the
  saint's grave "although a renowned place of pilgrimage, had suffered from neglect" — "'Graves
  from their oldness became leveled (to the ground), (and) there remained no mount on any grave.
  There was also (no place) for retirement, wherein the distressed darvish could take rest.
  Thereupon the king of the world gave the order that this top of Tur [Mount Sinai] be built anew…
  he built afresh this old structure, and this house with its enclosure again became new.'" Eaton:
  "His great act was to **patronize the cult of 'Abdullah Shah by (re)building his shrine** which,
  we are told at the end of the text, **included a strong vault, a mosque, and a caravan-sarai**.
  The inscription closes by offering a prayer that the soul of the benevolent sultan may last until
  Judgment Day and that his empire may last in perpetuity" (pp. 109). **A complete shrine-building
  programme, a stated motive, and the reciprocal prayer — all in one epigraph.**
- **A pilgrimage described in the inscription's own words**: "'Now this tomb since those old days
  has been the famous pilgrimage-place of a world'" (pp. 108–109). And, of the pre-shrine site,
  "'Now this (burial place and) grave of martyrs remained a trace of those holy and pious people'"
  (p. 108) — martyrs' graves as the origin of the site, before the saint.
- **Gift-giving as the mechanism of access at an Indian court, twice botched.** Da Gama's gifts —
  "striped cloth, four red hoods, six hats, four strings of coral, sugar, honey, oil, and six
  washbasins" — "provoked laughter from the court's Muslim officials, who advised the captain that
  such gifts were **unworthy of the poorest merchant from Mecca**" (p. 102). Eaton's gloss: "the
  custom of gift giving in Indian courts was fundamental to any sort of fruitful interaction since
  **gifts reflected a petitioner's respect for a patron as well as his seriousness of purpose**"
  (p. 103). The whole audience is transcribed as dialogue (pp. 102–103), including the raja's
  "'What is it you have come to discover, stones or men?'"
- **Court protocol observed in detail.** The raja "was seated beneath a gilded canopy, 'reclining
  upon a couch covered with a cloth of green velvet'. He was chewing **betel nut** and pitching the
  husks into a golden spittoon measuring sixteen inches at its mouth"; da Gama "did not [approach],
  supposing that only **the betel servant** was permitted that privilege"; the king "served
  everybody bananas", "a novelty to the Europeans", which "provoked a royal smile"; and the Muslim
  courtiers "stood in court in the king's attendance", as under Cheng Ho (pp. 101–102). Da Gama was
  "kept waiting in an antechamber for four hours" on the third day (p. 102).
- **Port duties as the raja's revenue, and the Portuguese refusal to pay them.** Cabral in 1500
  "was told to refuse to pay the **high port duties normally demanded by the raja**" (p. 104).
- **The 1500 massacre at Calicut, with numbers.** Cabral "captured a loaded Arab ship as it was
  putting out to sea. This led to a riot on shore that resulted in bloodshed among both Muslims and
  Portuguese. At this, Cabral ordered that **ten Arab ships be seized and plundered, and their
  crews, numbering more than five hundred men, massacred**. He burned the unloaded ships and the
  next day subjected the entire city to a withering bombardment of cannon fire, which, according to
  a Portuguese eyewitness, killed 'an endless number of people'" (p. 104). His instructions had
  been to take hostages before landing, to declare himself not "'a merchant like the others'", to
  refuse the port duties, "to seize, plunder, and destroy any Arab ships encountered at sea, and to
  ask the raja, whom the Portuguese still considered a fellow Christian, **to expel all Muslim
  ships from his harbour**" (pp. 103–104).
- **The Muslim merchants' warning to the raja, which Eaton says came true.** "the city's Muslims
  had already advised him that the Portuguese were thieves. Once the newcomers were admitted to his
  country, they warned, **no more ships would visit from Mecca or anywhere else**… they would take
  from him, so that his country would be ruined. In fact, this is what happened" (p. 103).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The Dhar shrine's foundation legend, in the inscription's own three movements** (pp. 108–109).
  Before the saint: "'a few persons had arrived before him at this desolate and ruined place. When
  the muazzin raised the morning cry like the trumpet-call for the intoxicated *sufis*, the infidels
  (made an attack from) every wall (?) and each of them rushed with the sword and knife. At last
  they (the infidels) wounded those men of religion, and **after killing them concealed (them) in a
  well**.'" Then the saint: "'When the time came that the sun of Reality should shine in this dark
  and gloomy night, **this lion-man came from the centre of religion to this old temple with a large
  force**. He broke the images of the false deities, and turned the idol-temple into a mosque. **When
  Rai Bhoj saw this, through wisdom he embraced Islam with the family of all brave warriors.**'"
  Then the shrine's neglect and royal restoration. Eaton's reading of the middle movement: the
  Hero's deeds "collectively avenged the martyred Sufis and, most importantly, served to
  (re)establish Islam in the region" (p. 109).
- **A legend frozen by writing, dated.** "the text presents a richly textured legend elaborated
  over many generations of oral transmission until 1455, when the story of 'Abdullah Shah Changal
  and his deeds in Malwa **became frozen in the written word** that we have before us" (p. 109).
  The same literacy-fixes-tradition argument as the Ao *Tsungrem* case, here applied to a shrine
  legend.

## Arguments and interpretations

- **A shrine inscription is not a newspaper — Eaton's central methodological correction, worked on
  a single document.** Against Sita Ram Goel, who "accepts the inscription's reference to temple
  destruction more or less at face value, as though it were a contemporary newspaper account
  reporting an objective fact": "**Unlike Goel, however, the text is concerned not with documenting
  an instance of temple destruction, but with narrating and celebrating the fabulous career of
  'Abdullah Shah Changal, the saint who lies buried at the site of the tomb**" (p. 108). Then the
  dating argument: "Although Indo-Muslim epigraphs are typically recorded near in time to the
  events they describe, **the present one is hardly contemporary, as it was composed some four
  hundred years after the events to which it refers**" (p. 109). And the symmetry argument, which
  is the decisive move: "Whether or not any temple was actually destroyed four hundred years before
  this narrative was committed to writing, we cannot know with certainty. However, **it would seem
  no more likely that such a desecration had actually occurred than that the renowned Raja Bhoja
  had been converted to Islam, which the text also claims**" (p. 110). The conclusion he draws is
  double: "**caution is necessary in interpreting claims made in Indo-Muslim literary sources to
  instances of temple desecration**. It also illustrates **the central role that temple desecration
  played in the remembered past of an Indo-Muslim state or community**" (p. 110) — i.e. the claim is
  evidence about memory, not about masonry.
- **Elliot and Dowson named as the channel, with the editor's motive in his own words.** "Much of
  the contemporary evidence on temple desecration cited by Hindu nationalists is found in Persian
  materials translated and published during the rise of British hegemony in India. Especially
  influential has been the eight-volume *History of India as Told by its Own Historians*, first
  published in 1849 and edited by Sir Henry M. Elliot, who oversaw the bulk of the translations,
  with the help of John Dowson. But Elliot, **keen to contrast what he understood as the justice and
  efficiency of British rule with the cruelty and despotism of the Muslim rulers** who had preceded
  that rule, was anything but sympathetic to the 'Muhammadan' period" (pp. 106–107). Elliot's
  preface is quoted — "'The common people must have been plunged into the lowest depths of
  wretchedness and despondency… of idols mutilated, of temples razed, of forcible conversions and
  marriages… show us that this picture is not overcharged'" — as is his stated hope that the
  translations "'will make our native subjects more sensible of the immense advantages accruing to
  them under the mildness and the equity of our rule'" (p. 107). **Any archive citing a
  temple-desecration report from Elliot and Dowson should carry this provenance.**
- **Mohammad Habib on the long effect of that historiography.** "'The peaceful Indian Mussalman,
  descended beyond doubt from Hindu ancestors, was dressed up in the garb of a foreign barbarian,
  as a breaker of temples, and an eater of beef, and declared to be a military colonist in the land
  where he had lived for about thirty or forty centuries… The result of it is seen in the
  communalistic atmosphere of India [today]'" (p. 107, from Nizami, ed., *Collected Works of
  Professor Mohammad Habib*, 1974, 1:12).
- **The essay's questions, stated as a research programme.** "what temples were in fact desecrated
  in India's pre-modern history? When, and by whom? How, and for what purpose? And above all, what
  might any of this say about the relationship between religion and politics in pre-modern India?"
  — asked because "**few professional historians have engaged the issue, even though it is a
  properly historical one**", and because "many in India today are looking to the past to justify
  or condemn public policy with respect to religious monuments" (p. 106). The method has been
  "selective translations of pre-modern Persian chronicles, together with a selective use of
  epigraphic data" (p. 108); one of Goel's chapters is titled "'From the Horse's Mouth'" (p. 108).
- **The pre-1192 pattern distinguished from what came after.** "It is well known that, during the
  two centuries before 1192, which was when an indigenous Indo-Muslim state and community first
  appeared in north India, Persianized Turks **systematically raided and looted major urban centers
  of South Asia, sacking temples and hauling immense loads of movable property to power bases in
  eastern Afghanistan**" (p. 110). The 986 Lamghan campaign "commenced" the pattern. Note that
  Eaton is setting up a contrast between **raiding for movable wealth from an external base** and
  the later practice of indigenous Indo-Muslim states — the distinction the rest of the chapter
  turns on.
- **The Calicut summary: comparison is what makes any one account interpretable.** "the behaviour
  of Calicut's fifteenth-century visitors was shaped by their impressions of the city and its
  inhabitants, as well as by the visitors' individual cultural backgrounds. What is more, **it is by
  comparing and contrasting the different foreigners' experiences with each other—placing each one
  in relief, profiled against the others—that we can best explain the behaviour of any one of
  them**" (p. 104). The four readings are then restated: Calicut as one node in a Muslim commercial
  network (Razzaq), as a disorienting Muslim city (Nikitin), as a tributary of the Ming (Cheng Ho),
  as a Christian city (the Portuguese).
- **The chiasmus he builds the essay toward.** Of the Chinese: "**Although they came as rulers,
  they ultimately behaved like merchants. The reverse was the case with the Portuguese, who came as
  merchants but in the end behaved like rulers**" (p. 105). And the causal claim attached to it:
  "the Portuguese binary vision of the world as an arena of Muslim–Christian conflict not only
  caused them to misread the Hindu raja as a Christian prince. It also **hastened their adoption of
  an aggressive policy of achieving a commercial monopoly by means of armed force**" (p. 105).
- **What the Portuguese actually accomplished, carefully limited.** "Although the Portuguese had no
  means of colonizing India—or even of launching sustained offensives against its Muslim sultanates,
  Vijayanagara, or later the Mughal empire—by blasting their way into commercial harbours,
  establishing armed enclaves along the western shore, and making grandiose claims of sovereignty,
  **they established the mental framework that would lead other Europeans to dominion**" (p. 105).
  Neither da Gama nor Ma Huan "'discovered' India, any more than Columbus discovered America"
  (p. 105); footnote 47 adds that Norse explorers reached America "in the early eleventh century and
  possibly, too… Irish monks in the sixth", which is why Columbus's return "provoked so little
  stir in western and northern Europe" (p. 105).
- **"One sees only what one wishes to see", demonstrated and then generalised.** The anonymous
  sailor who recorded da Gama's "Christians and spices" reply wrote in the same breath "'that the
  city of Calecut is inhabited by Christians'"; "Convinced that they had found what they had long
  sought, da Gama's party looked at Calicut and saw nothing but Christianity, **although in fact it
  had not a trace of Christian influence**. The drama that followed the Portuguese arrival makes
  sense only in light of this remarkable misunderstanding" (p. 99). Eaton then states the rule:
  "To the Chinese eunuchs… the non-Confucian world was divided into two principal groups,
  Buddhists and Muslims. Hence when they reached Calicut, they saw Buddhists and Muslims. The
  Portuguese… also conceived the world as divided into two principal groups, Christians and
  Muslims, and when they reached Calicut that is what they saw" (pp. 100–101) — and even the
  Chinese, remembered there from eighty years before, "were considered by da Gama's men to have
  been Christians", though possibly "Germans" or "Russians" (p. 101). "**Hindus were understood to
  be Christians, as were the Chinese, who might also have been Germans, or maybe Russians**"
  (p. 101).
- **Nikitin's religious drift read as evidence of Islamic cultural dominance.** "the pronounced
  Islamic element in his speech, seen in the Arabic or Persian names for God, suggests that **Islam
  was the dominant cultural system in the places he visited and among the groups he came to
  know**", so that "Nikitin's narrative confirms that of Abdul Razzaq" (p. 98). His own advice to
  Russians: "'those of you who want to go to the land of India must **leave their faith in Russia
  and invoke Mohammed** and only then go to the land of Hindustan'" (p. 98), and his invocations run
  "'Allah, Khuda, O God, Tangri!'" — "a man who, uprooted from his familiar and comfortable
  culture, was in the process of reorienting himself according to whatever was at hand" (p. 98).

## Doubts

- **No archive row is touched by this chunk.** The Dhar tomb-shrine is in Madhya Pradesh, India,
  and has no archive row; it should not be mapped, but the inscription is the best model in the
  book so far for reading a shrine's own foundation epigraph, and is worth citing in any
  methodological note about the archive's sources.
- **The Dhar inscription's own uncertainties are marked in the translation and must be kept.** The
  text as quoted carries "(1 have heard)", "(made an attack from) every wall (?)", "(them)",
  "(burial place and)", "(no place)", "(re)establish", "(re)building" — Yazdani's and Eaton's
  parenthetical supplements and doubts. **Quote them with the brackets.** Also note Eaton's own
  query "'centre of religion' (Mecca?)" (p. 109) — the saint's place of origin is not stated.
- **Whose reign the destruction is placed in is itself contested in the sources cited.** Eaton
  gives Raja Bhoja "who had ruled… from 1010 to 1053", but footnote 6 records that "Goel does,
  however, consider it more likely that the event took place during the reign of **Raja Bhoja II in
  the late thirteenth century** than during that of Raja Bhoja I in the eleventh century" (p. 108).
  Two candidate kings, two centuries apart, and Eaton's four-hundred-year gap is computed from the
  eleventh-century dating.
- **The deity da Gama prayed to is not identified**: "the image of a Hindu goddess, probably Kali
  or Lakshmi", in a temple "probably the great Shiva temple in Thali" (p. 100). Both are Eaton's
  inferences, flagged as such, and should stay flagged.
- **The Calicut audience dialogue is a modern reconstruction from a Portuguese source**
  (Ravenstein, 62–3, p. 102), set out by Eaton as a script. It is the Portuguese account of what
  was said through interpreters neither party trusted; do not treat the raja's lines as his words.
- Text-layer damage in the Elliot passage and around it (pp. 107–108) is heavy: "who overs:" for
  "who oversaw"; "Ellio ," for "Elliot"; "immense of agvantages" for "immense advantages"; "our
  tule" for "our rule"; "the garb of aforeign barbarian"; "of India epi." where Habib's word
  (probably "today") is lost; and at p. 110 a sentence is destroyed — "constructed their eae
  Central … course, the deecdon of a temple", where **the subject of that sentence is
  unrecoverable** and only its sense (the community constructed its past, with temple destruction
  central to it) survives via the following paragraph. A human must re-read pp. 107–110 from the
  PDF before quoting Elliot or Habib exactly.
- Footnote numbers are badly mangled in the Nikitin section (pp. 98): "#8", "2?", "39", "*N Ibid
  935lt" — the last citation is unrecoverable. Similarly "*)" and "MW Ibid.) 58-604" (p. 102),
  "44 'Thid., 49" (p. 99), "4)" and "“2" (p. 103).
- The running head at p. 107 prints the page number as "3" instead of 95 — a stray from the text
  layer, not a page in the book. The essay's printed pages run 94 onward against PDF pp. 106 onward,
  so the +8 offset noted in chunk 001 is confirmed to +12 here (printed 96 = PDF 108); **the offset
  is not constant across the volume** because of the unnumbered section dividers. Use PDF indices.
