# eaton_essays_islam_indian_history — chunk 004 — pp. 55–69

The close of Chapter One and most of Chapter Two, "Comparative History as World History:
Religious Conversion in Modern India" — a case study of Naga Christianization, 1881–1990. **No
South Asian shrine material and no archive row appears in this chunk.** Its use to the archive is
one thing only, but a substantial one: this is where Eaton sets out **the theory of conversion he
applies to Punjab and Bengal**, and he states it here more explicitly than in the case studies —
Spiro's definition of religion, John Smail's "creative adaptation", Robin Horton's intellectualist
two-tier cosmology, and the proposal that **changes in the naming patterns of humans and of
superhuman beings are the most reliable indices of religious change**. That last is a method
directly applicable to shrine and saint nomenclature.

The Naga case is also, in his own framing, a controlled comparison whose lesson is negative:
proselytising effort does not predict conversion.

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- **Molungyimsen**, the separate all-Christian Ao village founded by Rev. E. W. Clark in **1876**
  after his Sunday-rest rule clashed with communal work rhythms; Clark remained there until
  **1911** (p. 66). **Impur**, the station founded further inland by missionaries who rejected
  Clark's compromises; **the Kohima Mission**, established **1878** by C. D. King in Angami
  country, "some 85 miles southwest of Impur". These two were the mission centres "Down to 1954,
  when foreign missionaries departed the Naga Hills" (p. 66).
- **The morung** — "the village guard-house and lodge where young men of the same exogamous clan
  slept before they married"; located near the village gates, "a morung was also the focal point
  for the elaborate sequence of feasts that marked the Nagas' ritual year and which served as a
  channel of social mobility, since such feasts raised a sponsor's position in the eyes of his
  peers" (pp. 68–69). Male converts were forbidden to live in it.
- **Ibn Battuta**, concluded: an estimated **73,000 miles** across North and West Africa, West
  Asia, Central Asia, India, Southeast Asia and China, "much further and… many more places than
  did Marco Polo", who "died in 1324, the year before Ibn Battuta embarked from Morocco"
  (p. 55). Among the things he recorded: "the altercations between rival Sufi brotherhoods in
  Anatolia over which one would have the honor of hosting him" (p. 55) — a datable instance of
  competitive hospitality between orders.
- British administrative dates for the Naga Hills: Assam annexed **1842**; an English arbitrating
  officer accepted "By the 1870s"; a political officer established at **Kohima in 1878**; the Ao
  country under administration **by 1889**; the Sema country "in the early 1900s" (p. 64).

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **A three-way funding arrangement for village schools, itemised — a usable model of how
  religious institutions were financed under the Raj.** "villages desiring a school provided the
  building and the rice for a teacher, the Government paid the teacher's salary and financed the
  printing of textbooks, while the missionaries wrote the texts and trained the teachers at Kohima
  or Impur" (p. 67). The mission then paid its teachers "in addition to their government salary,
  a sum of about **$4 a month** to preach in addition to doing his school work" (p. 68), and by
  1923 "the mission ran **208 schools**, chiefly primary, serving **5,438 pupils**" (p. 68, n. 18).
  The senior Kohima missionary's own accounting in 1937: "The history of many a church in Assam
  can be traced back to an investment of say, **forty dollars per year** and the placement of a
  Christian teacher in an otherwise unevangelized village" (p. 68).
- **Sunday attendance made a condition of school credit** — missionaries "circumvented the teacher
  training schools' secular façade (necessitated by the fact of partial government support) by
  counting Sundays as one of the required school days, so that students had to attend church
  services to get credit for regular school attendance" (p. 68). By 1923 the Baptist Foreign
  Mission Society could note "that 'the government can secure the service of practically none but
  Christians as teachers'" (p. 68).
- **Naga sacrificial practice and its specialists.** From E. A. Gait in the 1891 census: a "vague
  but very general belief in some one omnipotent being, who is well disposed towards men, and whom
  therefore there is no necessity for propitiating. Then come a number of evil spirits… To these,
  therefore, sacrifices must be offered. These malevolent spirits are sylvan deities, spirits of
  the trees, the rocks and streams, and sometimes also of the tribal ancestors" (pp. 63–64, citing
  *Census of India, 1891*, Assam, 1:93). Eaton adds that the spirits were appeased "in the form of
  sacrifices of pigs, fowl or other living things", requiring "the services of village specialists,
  some interacting with those spirits who could affect the village as a whole, and others with
  spirits affecting individuals" (p. 64).
- **Material markers of conversion imposed by the mission**: Assamese jacket and body cloth for
  early converts, later "European dress—long shirts, mauve coats, khaki shorts, or white blouses
  imported from the plains"; a prohibition on living in the morung; and a strict ban on rice beer,
  "which was central to traditional feasts", such that non-drinking, a district officer noted in
  1931, "'is often regarded as the outstanding mark of a Christian'" (pp. 68–69). Note the state's
  counter-pressure: in **1925** the government "refused to allow the mission to start work in a new
  area until it agreed not to impose Western clothing on converts" (p. 69, n. 27).
- **Census and baptismal registers as two disagreeing instruments.** Table 2.2 sets the Christian
  share of each group in 1971 by baptismal record against the census: **Ao 44.07 vs 98.1 per cent;
  Sema 43.4 vs 77.4; Angami 12.0 vs 40** (p. 61, source Puthuvail T. Philip, *The Growth of Baptist
  Churches in Nagaland*, 2nd ed., 1983, pp. 78, 92, 110). A more than twofold divergence between
  formal affiliation and self-ascription, in a case Eaton calls one of the best documented — worth
  remembering wherever an archive counts adherents.
- **Christian population of Nagaland by census, 1881–1981** (Table 2.1, p. 60): 1881, 94,380 total
  and **3** Christians (0.003 per cent); 1891, 122,867 and 231 (0.18); 1901, 102,402 and 601 (0.58);
  1921, 160,960 and 8,734 (5.4); 1931, 178,844 and 22,908 (12.8); 1941, 189,641 and 34,000 (17.9);
  1951, 205,950 and 93,423 (45.7); 1961, 369,200 and 195,588 (52.9); 1971, 516,449 and 344,798
  (66.7); 1981, 774,930 and 621,571 (80.2); 1990, **90.0 per cent** ("an approximate figure and
  not an official census figure"). Sources are given census by census. The 1911 row is damaged —
  see Doubts.
- **Missionary effort quantified against outcome** (Table 2.3, p. 61, from a 1972 Bethel
  Theological Seminary thesis): **Ao — 170 total missionary years, 1876–1954; Sema — 2 missionaries,
  1948–1955; Angami — 1880–1955.** Two cells are corrupt. The point the table serves is in the
  prose: "The Ao Nagas, who were heavily proselytized between the 1880s and the 1950s, led all
  other Naga groups in converting… On the other hand the Angami Nagas, who were also heavily
  proselytized in the same period, responded at a far slower rate. Conversely, **the Sema Nagas,
  who were virtually ignored by missionaries in this period, like the Aos readily took on a
  Christian identity**" (p. 59).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **"Creative adaptation" — the formulation that governs Eaton's whole treatment of conversion.**
  "we should adopt the perspective of the society actually undergoing change and see conversion not
  as passive acceptance of a monolithic, outside essence, but as '**creative adaptation**' of the
  unfamiliar to what is already familiar, **a process in which the former may change to suit the
  latter**" (p. 58). He credits the phrase to John R. W. Smail, quoted in n. 1: "remembering that
  the essence of acculturation is the acceptance of change by the acculturating group—and hence
  that there can be no question, in the last analysis, of forced culture change" (p. 58, quoting
  *Journal of Southeast Asian History* 2, no. 2, July 1961, p. 91). The formulation "in effect
  inverts the modern Western lay understanding of religious conversion, informed by generations of
  institutionalized Protestant effort, which tends to look at the process from the standpoint of
  the agent—i.e., the 'missionary'… rather than from that of the society undergoing change. All
  of this raises the interesting question of **who, in the meeting of two cultures, is actually
  changing whom**" (p. 58).
- **The molasses image — his standing refusal of diffusionism.** Conversion can no longer be
  conceived "in terms of the 'spread' of an essentialized tradition from point A to point
  B—typically, from metropolis to periphery—as though it were a substance, **like molasses or
  lava, flowing outward from some central point, engulfing and incorporating all that it passes
  over while itself remaining unchanged**" (p. 58).
- **A definition of religion, and from it a method of measurement.** Adopting Melford Spiro's
  definition — "an institution consisting of culturally patterned interaction with culturally
  postulated superhuman beings" — "it follows that a society-wide conversion movement would involve
  a change in the identities of both the humans involved and the superhuman beings with whom they
  interact. If this is so, **changes in the naming patterns of both humans and superhumans, in
  addition to self-ascription as reflected in census data, would appear to be our most reliable
  indices of religious change**" (p. 58). Directly transferable to the naming of saints, sites and
  deities in a shrine archive.
- **Horton's intellectualist theory, summarised as the mechanism of conversion.** Indigenous
  cosmologies have "two tiers: an upper tier consisting of a high god who rules the macrocosm…
  and a lower tier consisting of numerous lesser superhuman beings who watch over and participate
  in a community's immediate natural and social worlds", the high god being "vaguely elaborated and
  seldom approached". "With increasing contact with a world beyond their immediate microcosm,
  however, people tend to give greater attention to their high god, who is understood to control
  that larger world" — and "if Islamic or Christian influences happen to be '**in the air**' when a
  community is widening its social contacts, their natural shift of attention to a high god can
  take the form of conversion to one of those world religions… Such an outcome is especially
  likely when the Christian or Islamic high god is **accommodated within the indigenous
  cosmology**—for example, when that god is identified with the indigenous high god" (pp. 61–62,
  citing Horton in *Africa* 1971 and 1975 and *Patterns of Thought in Africa and the West*, 1993).
  Eaton's gloss: "the intellectualist theory **removes from centre stage the role of religious
  proselytizers**, focusing instead on the social and cosmological dynamics experienced by the host
  populations themselves", and Horton in 1993: "'the phenomenon of "conversion" can only be
  understood if we put the initial emphasis, not on the incoming religious messages, but rather on
  the indigenous religious frameworks and on the challenges they face from massive flows of novel
  experience'" (p. 62).
- **The Naga evidence refutes both "colonialism" and "missionary agency" as explanations.** "Nagas
  as a whole converted most dramatically **only after the dismantling of the colonial state and the
  expulsion of foreign missionaries** by the newly independent Government of India… Therefore,
  blanket explanations focusing on monolithic abstractions like 'colonialism' or on activities of
  the missionaries alone—as though these were independent, explanatory variables—clearly will not
  do" (p. 60). The differential outcomes among Ao, Angami and Sema make the same point (p. 59).
  Footnote 5 names the opposing position he is arguing against: Jean and John Comaroff's *Of
  Revelation and Revolution* and *Ethnography and the Historical Imagination*, which focus "more on
  the agency of missionaries and factors like 'discourses of the imperial imagination' than on the
  changing cosmologies of the people actually undergoing conversion" (p. 60, n. 5).
- **The case for comparison over place-bound history.** "To the comparativist, the topic, the
  problem, or the process is the focus of attention, **not the place**"; comparativists are "well
  positioned to help free the profession from conventions that have bedeviled so much
  history-writing in the past, in particular, the convention of tying history to nation-states or
  civilizations" (p. 57).
- **Why hill peoples remained outside the text-based traditions — the same frontier logic as the
  Punjab and Bengal essays.** "By the mid-nineteenth century, most of the peoples of South Asia had
  been assimilated into one or another of the region's great text-based religious traditions,
  whether Jain, Buddhist, Hindu, Sikh, or Islamic. Over the preceding centuries, as they penetrated
  societies living in India's great alluvial plains, these religious traditions carried with them,
  or associated themselves with, **the characteristic traits of plains culture, such as urban life,
  social stratification, irrigated rice cultivation, a cash economy, centralized political
  authority, a stable priesthood, and a literary tradition**. By the time India came under British
  colonial rule… many peoples inhabiting the outlying, forested hill tracts of the subcontinent
  still remained untouched, or lightly touched, by such text-based religious traditions" (p. 62).
  The Nagas' religion was correspondingly "very locality-specific… highly elaborated with respect
  to the immediate, localized microcosm in which villagers lived", their horizon narrowed by
  stockaded villages, institutionalised inter-village warfare and head-hunting (p. 63).
- **Colonial religious policy was not of one mind, and Eaton shows the split with dates.** Captain
  Johnstone, the first district officer, recalled advising in 1875 "'that the Nagas had no religion;
  that they were highly intelligent and capable of receiving civilization; that with it they would
  want a religion, and that **we might just as well give them our own**, and make them in that way
  a source of strength… a large population of Christian hill-men between Assam and Burmah, would
  be a valuable prop to the State'" (pp. 64, 66). Against that, "unlike the missionaries, district
  officers, interested in maintaining peace and security, wished to avoid interfering with Naga
  culture, especially religion. After all, Englishmen in India had **bitter memories of the 1857
  uprising**, which had followed a period of aggressive British interference in Indian culture in
  the name of 'reform'" (p. 67).
- **An unintended linguistic consequence of scripture translation.** Mission linguists "reduced the
  various Naga languages to written forms and built up the first written literature Naga peoples
  possessed. This in turn simplified the linguistic landscape, for by selecting from among many
  dialects of a language just one for reduction to Roman script, **the missionaries doomed most
  other dialects to oblivion**" (p. 67).
- **Conversion as demanded was total, not confessional.** "in practice they called for a total
  cultural transformation", and "Most foreign missionaries in the Naga Hills were Prohibitionist
  American Baptists who understood religion in strongly moral terms and consequently saw conversion
  as a conscious moral turn-about, of which markers like Western dress or abstinence from alcohol
  were outward signs" (pp. 68–69). One missionary is quoted as having grasped the cost — Tanquist
  on "the 'cleavages and animosities incident to the spread of Christianity among people who are
  closely knit together in village life and **whose very village customs constitute their old
  religion**'" (p. 69, n. 31).
- **Dar al-Islam as a lived unity, in the Ibn Battuta coda.** "Ibn Battuta, in his intercontinental
  wanderings, moved through a single cultural universe in which he felt utterly at home… his book
  conveys a self-assured tone in which the cultural unity of Dar al-Islam, from Spain to China, was
  not even an issue. It was simply taken for granted. **This was a world in which a judge learned
  in the Sacred Law could expect to find employment serving the Muslim community wherever he went**"
  (p. 55).
- **The nation-state named as an alien category for Islamic history.** "the nation-state, Muslim or
  otherwise, is itself a very recent European political category having no roots in Islamic
  history. Indeed, as the political expression of an ethnic community, the nation-state concept is
  fundamentally hostile to the Islamic vision of the umma" — the umma having been "split asunder
  into modern nation-states—the long-term legacy of the French Revolution" (p. 56). And the
  chapter's closing claim: "Chronologically, Islamic history is the link between the ancient and
  modern worlds. Spatially… Islamic civilization grew to become history's first bridge connecting
  the agrarian belt stretching from Gibraltar to China" (p. 56).
