# eaton_essays_islam_indian_history — chunk 019 — pp. 269–283

Continuous argumentative text, not apparatus: the middle and end of the essay "**Who are the Bengal
Muslims?**" (running head on every page, e.g. p. 269), printed pp. 257–271. The chunk runs from the
Mughal "hands-off policy" on conversion (p. 269), through the ecology and economics of the eastward
migration of the Ganges (pp. 270–274), the land-grant/forest-pioneer mechanism and the mosques and
tomb cults it produced (pp. 275–277), the "culture of literacy" (p. 277), the global silver argument
(p. 278), and then three Bengali literary texts read as evidence — the *Candi-Mangala*
(pp. 278–280), the *Sekasubhodaya* (pp. 280–281) and the *Nabi-Bamsa* (pp. 282–283). It breaks off
mid-sentence on p. 283 in a quotation from a 1908 gazetteer.

every row in `shrine_index.tsv` is a site in present-day Pakistan. The "Shrines and figures in the

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- **Shaikh Jalal al-Din Tabrizi** — hero of the sixteenth-century *Sekasubhodaya*, "a figure said to
  have come from somewhere west of Bengal", instructed by Pradhanpurusa ("'Great Person,' i.e., God")
  to go to "the eastern country", meet Raja Lakshmana Sena and build a "'house of God' (devasadana),
  or mosque" (p. 280). He reaches Pandua walking on the Ganges with magical shoes, converts the king
  by a miracle, clears the donated site "of demons", settles cultivators and issues them "formal
  documents of settlement" (p. 281). Eaton: "we should not hope to recover in this text the
  historical 'Shaikh Tabrizi'" (p. 281). **See Doubts on the name.**
- **"Zafar Mian"** — in Mukundaram's *Candi-Mangala* (c. 1590) the leader of the Muslim forest
  pioneers, coming "From the West… Together with twenty-two thousand men", his followers holding
  "Sulaimani beads in their hands" and chanting "the names of their pir and the Prophet" (p. 279);
  "evidently the chieftain or the organizer of the Muslim workmen", and the *pir* whose name they
  chanted "quite possibly was Zafar Mian himself" (p. 280).
- **Zafar Khan** — "the historical pioneer who in 1298 patronized the constructions of a madrasa at
  Tribeni, not distant from Mukundaram's home in Burdwan"; Eaton suggests "Zafar Mian" may be "a
  hazy memory" of him (p. 280, n. 45, citing *Inscriptions of Bengal*, 4:18–21).
- **Shah Zain al-'Abidin** — recipient of the earliest of the Chittagong grants, "dated 2 September
  1666", of "166.4 acres of jungle land", required to bring it into cultivation and to "assiduously
  pray for the survival of the powerful state" (p. 275, n. 37, citing Chittagong District
  Collectorate Record Room, "Kanun Daimer Nathi", No. 1, bundle 59, case no. 3863). No shrine or
  tomb is recorded for him (not stated).
- **Islam Khan**, Mughal governor — "is known to have discouraged the conversion of Bengalis and on
  one occasion actually punished one of his officers for allowing it to happen" (p. 269).
- **Tuqmaq Khan** — in 1609, in "the present Bogra region", defeated **Raja Ray**, "the landholder
  (zamindar) of Shahzadpur", then took the defeated raja's son as personal servant "and at the same
  time converted him to Islam"; the governor "punished Tuqmaq Khan by transferring him from his
  jagir" (p. 269, n. 23, citing Mirza Nathan, *Baharistan*, text fol. 10b; trans. 1:32).
- **'Isa Khan** and the "'twelve chieftains' (bara bhiiyan [*bara bhuiyan*])" — powerful zamindars
  losing the balance of power to Mughal imperial authorities around 1599 (p. 269).
- **Mukundaram**, poet of Burdwan — author of the *Candi-Mangala* / *Kavikankana Candi*, c. 1590
  (pp. 270, 279); also the source, "[a]round 1590", for "the highly elaborated caste society that by
  that time had appeared in western Bengal" (p. 270, n. 27).
- **Kalaketu** — the hunter-king of the *Candi-Mangala*, entrusted by the goddess **Chandi** with
  "temporal sovereignty over her forest kingdom on the condition that he, as king, renounce the
  violent career of hunting and bring peace on earth by promoting her cult" (p. 279).
- **Saiyid Sultan** — author of the *Nabi-Bamsa*, "[c]omposed in the Chittagong region and also
  dating to the late sixteenth century" (p. 282).
- **Vijaya Gupta** — writing in 1494 in the Barisal region, quoted on "a teacher of the Qadi named
  Khilis" who told the fearful to "Write (extracts) from the book and hang it down the neck"
  (p. 277, n. 42, in Abdul Karim's translation).
- **Lakshmana Sena** — "the Hindu king defeated by the Turks in 1204" (p. 280); donor of the forest
  land for Shaikh Tabrizi's mosque (p. 281).
- **Pandua** — the Senas' capital, reached by Shaikh Tabrizi (p. 281); also listed among the
  northwestern-delta capitals of "earlier Muslim rulers of Bengal" ("i.e., Gaur, Pandua, Tanda",
  p. 274) and as the second-largest of ancient Bengal's royal palaces at 13,186,800 square feet
  (p. 270, n. 26). The chunk does not comment on the double use (not stated).
- **Dhaka** — where communities of Muslim cultivators were "first reported… in 1599" (p. 269); the
  Mughal provincial capital "planted… in the heart of the eastern delta" in the early seventeenth
  century (p. 274); site of a 1913 village survey (p. 283).
- **Sylhet** — "eastern Sylhet about 150 inches" of rain (p. 270); the 1898 recollection of village
  foundation practice comes from "a Muslim gentleman of northern Sylhet" (p. 276, n. 39).
- **Chittagong** and **Satgaon** — the two seaports from which rice was exported (p. 274); Chittagong
  also the source of the land-grant records used throughout (p. 275, nn. 37, 41).
- **Sondwip Island**, "off Bengal's southeastern coast" — where, in the *Nabi-Bamsa*, Adam "made his
  first earthly appearance" (p. 282).
- **Pabna District** — where "[p]resent-day Muslim cultivators" identify the earth's soil as the
  source of Adam's power (p. 283, n. 52, citing John P. Thorp, "Masters of Earth", Ph.D. diss.,
  University of Chicago, 1978, 40–54).

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The land grant as the founding instrument of religious institutions — the chunk's central
  economic mechanism.** The Mughals "granted favourable or even tax-free tenures of land to
  industrious individuals who were expected to clear and bring into cultivation undeveloped forest
  tracts. The policy was intended to promote the emergence of local communities that would be both
  economically productive and politically loyal. Every recipient of such grants, Hindu or Muslim, was
  required to support his dependent clients and to pray for the long life of the Mughal state"
  (p. 275).
- **The grant's religious condition, and perpetual endowment out of local produce.** "one of the
  conditions for obtaining a grant was to build on the land a mosque or temple, to be supported in
  perpetuity out of the wealth produced on site" (p. 276).
- **The grant categories are named, and sorted by the cultural universe they built.** Grants to Hindu
  institutions — "(e.g., brahmottar, devottar, vishnottar, sivottar)" — "tended to integrate local
  communities into a Hindu-ordered cultural universe, whereas grants authorizing the establishment of
  mosques or shrines tended to integrate such communities into an Islamic-ordered cultural universe.
  Subsequent demographic patterns evolved from these earlier processes" (p. 276).
- **Who received them decided the outcome.** "Since most pioneers were Muslims… mosques comprised the
  majority of institutions established, with the result that the dominant mode of piety that evolved
  on East Bengal's economic frontier was Islamic" (p. 276).
- **Scale of the documentation.** "Hundreds of Mughal records dating from the mid-seventeenth century
  down to the advent of British power in 1760 document these pioneers' steady push into virgin jungle
  and their recruitment of local peoples to clear the jungle and bring the land into rice cultivation"
  (p. 275, n. 38, citing Eaton, *Rise of Islam*, ch. 9). The earliest individual grant cited is
  2 September 1666, of 166.4 acres (p. 275, n. 37).
- **Architecture of the frontier mosque.** "the mosques themselves were not architecturally comparable
  with the great stone or brick religious monuments that the Mughals built in the cities. They were,
  rather, humble structures built of thatching and bamboo" (p. 276). No dimensions or building dates
  are given (not stated).
- **Personnel carried on the endowment, outliving the founder.** "long after the founding pioneer
  died, the mosque he had built would continue to diffuse Islamic religious ideals amongst local
  communities, since Qur'an readers, callers to prayer, and preachers were also supported in
  perpetuity according to terms specified in the foundational grants" (p. 276). "Contemporary
  government documents confirm that Qur'an readers were attached to rural mosques and shrines as part
  of their endowments" (p. 277), titled "Quran-khwani or tilawat" (p. 277, n. 41, citing three
  Chittagong "Kanun Daimer Nathi" cases).
- **How a tomb cult began, in Eaton's account.** "many pioneers who had obtained the land grants,
  mobilized labour, and founded these institutions passed into subsequent memory as powerful pirs. In
  not a few cases, tomb cults grew up on their gravesites" (pp. 276–277).
- **Density of the institutions.** "the hundreds of tiny mosques and shrines that sprang up along the
  eastern frontier" (p. 277). No count is given (not stated).
- **Village foundation practice as recalled in 1898.** "whenever a new village was founded, a temple
  to the goddess Kali was built if the founding landlord were a Sakta Hindu, and a temple to Vishnu if
  he were a Vaishnava. If a majority of the village were Vaishnava, they would build a shrine
  (a@khra [*akhra*]) to Radha and Krishna. If the area were infested with snakes, the patron deity was
  the snake goddess Manasa, and if the village were founded by Muslims, a shrine to some Muslim pir
  would be established" — reported by a Muslim gentleman of northern Sylhet at a time when "the
  colonization of some of the Sylhet forest was still within living memory" (p. 276, n. 39, citing
  P. N. Bhattacharjee, "Folkcustom and Folklore of the Sylhet District of India", *Man in India* 10/1
  (January–March 1930), 133).
- **Amulet practice, attested in 1494.** The Qadi's teacher Khilis: "why are you afraid of demons…
  when you have got the religious books. Write (extracts) from the book and hang it down the neck. If
  then also the demons (implying snakes) bite, I shall be held responsible" (p. 277, n. 42).
- **The literary model of shrine patronage, stated as a model.** Of the *Sekasubhodaya*: "the text
  presents us with a model of patronage—a mosque linked economically with the hinterland and
  politically with the state—that was fundamental to the historical expansion of Muslim agrarian
  civilization throughout the delta" (p. 281). Its division of labour: "the former donates forest
  land for the mosque while the latter performs the ritual feats necessary to establish the
  institution and invites local people to settle a formerly forested land"; "[t]he shaikh issued
  formal documents of settlement to these men, who now cultivated the fields whose income would be
  used to support the mosque" (p. 281).
- **Revenue as an ecological index.** Mughal revenue demand between 1595 and 1659 "jumped by 117 per
  cent in the delta's most ecologically active southeastern region, and by 97 per cent in the
  northeast… by only 54 per cent in the less active southwest, whereas in the ecologically moribund
  northwest it actually declined by 13 per cent" — "since such figures were based on the capacity of
  the land to produce grain" (p. 274, n. 33, citing Eaton, *The Rise of Islam and the Bengal Frontier
  1204–1760* (New Delhi: Oxford University Press, 1994), 198–9).
- **Rainfall, as the measure of the forest obstacle.** "Today, West Bengal gets about 55 inches of
  rain annually, whereas central and eastern Bengal get 60 inches to 95 inches, with the mouth of the
  Meghna receiving from 100 inches to 120 inches and eastern Sylhet about 150 inches" (p. 270, n. 28,
  citing Spate and Learmonth, *India and Pakistan*, 3rd edn (London: Methuen, 1967), 575), with the
  qualifier "Assuming this climatic pattern held in ancient times" (p. 270).
- **Urban scale as the measure of pre-conquest stratification.** Morrison's comparative palace areas
  in square feet: "Pundranagara, 22,555,000; Pandua, 13,186,800; Gaur, 10,000,000; Kotivarasha,
  2,700,000; Vikrampur, 810,000; and Devaparvata (at Lalmai), 360,000", the four largest in Varendra
  or northwestern Bengal (p. 270, n. 26, citing Morrison, *Lalmai, a Cultural Center of Early Bengal*
  (Seattle: University of Washington Press, 1974), 124).
- **The river's movement, dated from maps and travellers.** The Ganges spilled eastward through "the
  Bhairab, the Mathabhanga, the Garai-Madhumati, the Arialkhan—until finally, in the late sixteenth
  century, it linked up with the Padma"; "European maps dated 1548, 1615, 1660, and 1779 clearly show
  this riverine movement" (p. 271, nn. 30–32). In 1567 Cesare Federici "noted that ships 'were unable
  to sail north of Satgaon (near modern Calcutta) on the old Ganges'"; "seven years later
  Abu'l-fazl… recorded that the Ganges had divided into two branches at Tanda"; "[b]y 1666… it had
  become altogether unnavigable" (p. 271, n. 31).
- **Export economy.** By the late sixteenth century "Bengal was producing so much surplus grain that
  rice emerged as an important export crop, which had never before happened", shipped from Chittagong
  and Satgaon "as far west as Goa and as far east as the Moluccas"; eastward rice export "declined
  after about 1670" but rice "remained cheap and abundant throughout the seventeenth century and well
  into the eighteenth" (p. 274, n. 34). Cotton textiles were "the delta's principal export commodity
  since at least the late fifteenth century, and a major one since at least the tenth", and by the end
  of the seventeenth century Bengal "had emerged as Europe's single most important supplier of goods
  in all of Asia" (pp. 274–275, n. 35, citing Om Prakash (Princeton, 1985), 75).
- **Silver and credit.** Merchants "poured into the delta substantial amounts of silver which, minted
  into currency, fueled the booming agrarian frontier by monetizing the local economy" (p. 275, n. 36,
  citing Eaton, *Rise of Islam*, 200–7); "Hindu financiers advanced capital to Muslim pioneers, who in
  turn organized local labour for cutting forested regions and founded mosques around which new
  agrarian communities coalesced" (p. 278).
- **The occupational shift the grants produced.** "local communities formerly engaged mainly in
  hunting, fishing, or shifting agriculture began devoting more time to full-time wet-rice peasant
  agriculture" (pp. 275–276).
- **Nineteenth- and twentieth-century observations of the result.** A 1913 village survey in Dhaka
  District found Muslims there "'entirely fall upon agriculture as their only source of income, and
  unless driven to the last stage of starvation they never hire themselves for any kind of service,
  which is looked upon with contempt on their part'" (p. 283, n. 53, citing Dhaka District
  Collectorate, Record Room, Mauza Notes, Rupganj, vol. 1). A sentence introducing "the gazetteer for"
  1908 begins on p. 283 and is cut off by the chunk boundary.
- **Dated first appearances of the community itself.** Muslim cultivators "first reported in the Dhaka
  region in 1599", "in the Noakhali region in the 1630s, and in the Rangpur region in the 1660s"
  (p. 269, nn. 24–25).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The heron and the fish** — Shaikh Jalal al-Din Tabrizi challenged Raja Lakshmana Sena "to cause a
  nearby heron to release a fish caught in its bill. When Lakshmana Sena declined, the shaikh merely
  glanced at the bird, which at once dropped the fish", after which the king "asked for the shaikh's
  grace (prasid [*prasad*]) and vowed to remain his steadfast devotee" — from the sixteenth-century
  *Sekasubhodaya* (p. 281, n. 47).
- **Walking on the Ganges** — "Walking on the Ganges River with his magical shoes, Shaikh Tabrizi
  reached the Senas' capital at Pandua" (p. 281, *Sekasubhodaya*).
- **Clearing a site of demons** — the shaikh "prepared the site by clearing the area of demons and
  offering handfuls of holy water to Pradhanpurusa, to the Himalayas, and to various other
  personages" (p. 281, *Sekasubhodaya*).
- **The goddess's commission to Kalaketu** — Chandi entrusts him with sovereignty over her forest
  kingdom if he renounces hunting and promotes her cult (p. 279); in a related passage she "orders the
  poem's hero, Kalaketu, to sell a valuable ring and use the money thus obtained to clear the forest
  so that a city may be built in her honour" (p. 278, n. 43, *Candi-Mangala*).
- **The tiger flees the axe** — "Hearing the sound of the axe, / The tiger became apprehensive and ran
  away, roaring" (p. 279, *Candi-Mangala*, quoted by Eaton).
- **Adam as first cultivator** — in Saiyid Sultan's *Nabi-Bamsa*, Adam appears first on Sondwip
  Island, is sent by Gabriel to build "the original Ka'ba" at Mecca, and is then given "a plough, a
  yoke, two bulls, and seed" with the words "'Niranjan [God] has commanded that agriculture will be
  your destiny'" (pp. 282–283, nn. 49–50).
- **Abraham the forest-clearer** — "Born and raised in a forest, Abraham is said to have travelled to
  Palestine, where he attracted tribes from nearby lands, mobilized local labour to cut down the
  forest, and built a holy place, Jerusalem's Temple, where prayers were offered to Niranjan"
  (p. 283, n. 51, *Nabi-Bamsa*).
- **Muhammad foretold in the Vedas** — "Already in the four Vedas, the poet states, God ('Karta') had
  given witness to the certain coming of Muhammad's prophetic mission" (p. 282, n. 48, *Nabi-Bamsa*).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The paradox the essay is built on.** "although Muslim regimes had ruled over Bengal since the
  early thirteenth century, [a] noticeable community of Muslim cultivators did not emerge there until
  the late sixteenth century, under a regime that did nothing to encourage the conversion of Bengalis
  to Islam and in fact opposed such conversions" (p. 269). The evidence for the opposition is the 1609
  Tuqmaq Khan case: "Clearly, the Governor did not view government service as a reward for conversion
  to Islam; to the contrary, in this instance the man responsible for causing the conversion was
  censured and transferred" (p. 269); and it was not Islam Khan alone but "'the other officers of the
  State,'" "suggesting that the hands-off policy was a general one" (p. 269). This rules out state
  sponsorship as the mechanism of conversion before he supplies his own.
- **Geography of the outcome is the clue to its cause.** "It is significant that the areas where
  communities of Muslim cultivators were first noticed—Dhaka, Noakhali, Rangpur—are located in the
  eastern half of the Bengal delta, and not the western delta. The reasons for this appear related to
  the extraordinary economic growth that the eastern delta was then experiencing relative to the west"
  (p. 269).
- **Why the west was different: two obstacles, then a stratified society.** "Two major obstacles
  inhibited the advance of Brahmanical society into the eastern delta: heavy forestation, and an
  absence of direct riverine contact with upper India" (p. 270); "prior to the Turkish conquest of
  1204, western Bengal had become far more deeply penetrated by Indo-Aryan civilization generally, and
  in particular by Brahman settlement and the diffusion of Brahmanic notions of hierarchical social
  organization and caste specialization" (p. 270). The inference from archaeology is explicit:
  "Greater urbanization suggests greater occupational specialization and social stratification"
  (p. 270), with Morrison's palace areas as the measure (p. 270, n. 26) and Mukundaram's c. 1590 caste
  lists as the western terminus — "No such evidence exists for the east" (p. 270).
- **Ecology as prime mover.** The Ganges's eastward migration "carried with it the epicentre of
  Bengali civilization, since its annual flooding deposited the immense loads of silt that made
  possible the cultivation of wet rice, which in turn could sustain ever larger concentrations of
  population" (p. 274); the revenue jumps of 1595–1659 are offered as the measurable trace, because
  the assessment tracked "the capacity of the land to produce grain" (p. 274).
- **Conjuncture, not coincidence.** "the merger of the Ganges with the Padma occurred at the very
  moment that the whole of Bengal was absorbed into one of the largest imperial systems ever seen in
  South Asia—the Mughal Empire under Akbar" (p. 274), and unlike earlier Muslim rulers the Mughals
  "planted their provincial capital in the heart of the eastern delta, Dhaka", so that for the first
  time eastern Bengal "became the focus of concerted and rapid political and economic development"
  (p. 274).
- **Islamization as an unintended consequence of land policy — the chunk's thesis sentence.**
  "although the Mughal government does not appear to have intended to Islamize the East Bengali
  countryside, such an outcome nonetheless resulted from its land policies" (pp. 277–278).
- **Conversion without conversion agents: religion arrived with the plough.** Because rural eastern
  Bengalis "had not yet been integrated into a rigidly-structured caste society informed by
  Brahmanical notions of hierarchy and order… they were not yet 'Hindu,'" and so "in much of the
  eastern delta rice agriculture and Islam were introduced simultaneously and grew together, both of
  them focused on these humble mosques" (p. 276).
- **Literacy as a competitive advantage of the new cult.** Papermaking, "[t]raceable to the fifteenth
  century and unmistakably identified with Islamic civilization", "fostered attitudes that endowed
  the written word with an authority qualitatively different from oral authority", spreading "a
  'culture of literacy'… far beyond the Mughal state's bureaucratic sector or the delta's urban
  centres"; "the culture of literacy endowed the cult of Allah with a kind of authority—that of the
  unchangeable written word—that the delta's preliterate forest cults had theretofore lacked"
  (p. 277). Eaton's summary: "In the east, then, Islam came to be understood as a religion, not only
  of the axe and the plough, but also of the book" (p. 277). The linguistic evidence offered is that
  "the ordinary Bengali for 'paper' (Adgaj [*kagaj*]) and 'pen' (kalam) are both Perso-Arabic loan
  words" (p. 277).
- **The world-system argument, stated as irony.** From 1574 the region "was also becoming integrated
  economically with the whole world, since silver originally mined in South or Central America and
  shipped to Spain ultimately ended up fueling the eastward push of Bengal's economic frontier"; "All
  of this fostered a kind of cultural authority that was in the first instance Mughal, but ultimately
  Islamic. Ironically enough, Europe's early modern economic expansion in the 'New World' contributed
  to the growth of Islam in the 'Old World'" (p. 278).
- **A correction Eaton makes to the literature, in a footnote.** "The notion that the influx of silver
  was primarily a European and maritime phenomenon has been vastly overstated in the literature. Even
  as late as the mid-eighteenth century, Asian traders—especially Gujaratis, Armenians, and
  Punjabis—played a more important role in Bengal's commercial economy than did Europeans" (p. 275,
  n. 35, citing Sushil Chaudhury, *From Prosperity to Decline* (New Delhi: Manohar, 1995)).
- **What was new was not forest clearing but its attribution to holy men.** "deltaic peoples had been
  transforming forested lands to rice fields long before the Mughal age. What was new from at least
  the sixteenth century on, however, was that this process had become particularly associated with
  Muslim holy men, or perhaps more accurately, with industrious and capable forest pioneers
  subsequently identified as holymen" (p. 278).
- **Saints as compressed history — Eaton's theory of why these cults endured.** "In popular memory,
  some of these men swelled into vivid mythico-historical figures, saints whose lives served as
  metaphors for the expansion of both religion and agriculture. They have endured precisely because,
  in the collective folk memory, their careers captured and telescoped a complex historical
  socioreligious process whereby a land originally forested and non-Muslim became arable and
  predominantly Muslim" (p. 278). This licenses his use of literature alongside documents: "one finds
  evidence of medieval Bengal's socio-economic and religious transformations not only in Mughal
  revenue documents, but also in contemporary Bengali literature" (p. 278).
- **How he reads the *Candi-Mangala*.** The poem is "a grand epic dramatizing the process of
  civilization-building in the Bengal delta, and more concretely, the push of rice-cultivating
  civilization into virgin forest" (p. 279). Its royal model is "unambiguously Hindu" — Kalaketu a
  raja, the cultivators accepting betel-nut from his mouth in an act "drawing directly on the Hindu
  ritual of devotion performed for a deity, that is, puja" — "Yet the principal pioneers responsible
  for clearing the forest… were Muslims" (p. 279). The Muslim pioneers carry "three interrelated
  themes: (a) subduing a tiger, that is, taming Bengal's untamed wilderness, (b) clearing the jungle…
  and (c) establishing markets, that is, introducing commerce and a cash economy into a theretofore
  undeveloped hinterland" (p. 280); their westerly origin "suggest[s] origins in upper India or
  beyond, in contrast to the aboriginals who came from the north and the harvesters who came from the
  south" (p. 280).
- **His explicit limit on the literary evidence.** "Although the narrative cannot be understood as an
  eyewitness account, it probably had some basis in what was happening in Mukundaram's own day. Even
  if there had been no historical 'Zafar Mian,' the poet was clearly familiar with the theme of
  thousands of Muslims entering and transforming the forests under the leadership of capable
  chieftains or charismatic pirs" (p. 280). The same move for the *Sekasubhodaya*: "both men represent
  metaphors for changes experienced by people all over the delta, and in particular, the gradual
  cultural shift—well under way by the sixteenth century—from a Bengali Hindu world to a Bengali
  Muslim world" (p. 281). Note the distinction he keeps: the *texts* are evidence of a pattern, not of
  their protagonists' existence.
- **Presenting the new in the guise of the familiar.** The *Sekasubhodaya* belongs to the
  *mangala-kavya* genre, "which typically glorified a particular deity", but its hero "is not a
  traditional Bengali deity, but Shaikh Jalal al-Din Tabrizi" (p. 280); the shaikh radiates
  *tapahprabhab*, gives *prasad*, consecrates the mosque by "a ritual program consistent with that of
  a temple", and his "patron deity, 'Allah,' was given the generic and hence portable name
  Pradhanpurusa, 'Great Person'" (p. 281). Eaton's conclusion is that the literary texts "confirm the
  evidence of such change found in administrative documents of the period" (p. 281).
- **Against calling the *Nabi-Bamsa* syncretic.** The poem treats Brahma, Vishnu, Siva, Rama and
  Krishna "as successive prophets of God, followed in turn by Adam, Noah, Abraham, Moses, Jesus, and
  Muhammad", fostering "the claim that Islam was the heir, not only to Judaism and Christianity, but
  also to the religious traditions of pre-Muslim Bengal" (p. 282); "rather than repudiating those
  older religious traditions, Saiyid Sultan's epic served to connect Islam with Bengal's socioreligious
  past, or at least with that part of it represented in the high textual tradition of the Brahmans"
  (p. 282). But: "it would be wrong to characterize the work as merely 'syncretic'; on fundamental
  points of theology, the poet clearly drew on Judeo-Islamic and not on Indic thought" — the
  *nabi*/*avatara* interchange carries "the Judeo-Islamic 'once-only' conception of prophethood", and
  cosmic time is not cyclical but a sequence of corruptions and renewed prophecy "culminating in the
  last and most perfect nabi/avatara, Muhammad" (p. 282).
- **The fit between scripture and social reality, which is his closing move.** Abraham's "sylvan
  origins, his recruitment of nearby tribesmen, his leadership in clearing the forest, and his
  building a house of prayer—mirrored rather precisely the careers of the hundreds of pioneers who,
  during the sixteenth to eighteenth centuries, had been given state land-grants for the purpose of
  mobilizing local clients in the Bengali countryside for just such activities" (p. 283). "Here, then,
  was a remarkable fit between social reality and religious thought. To be a good Muslim, so it was
  believed, one must cultivate the earth, as Adam did" (p. 283).
- **A carefully limited claim about origins.** Eaton observes that "Adam's career as a tiller of the
  soil is also found in the Book of Genesis (3:23)", that "such an association is not made in the
  Qur'an", and therefore that "the perception of Adam as the first cultivator, and of his cultivating
  the earth at the command of God, may therefore be a uniquely Bengali variant" — "may", not is
  (p. 283, n. 52).
