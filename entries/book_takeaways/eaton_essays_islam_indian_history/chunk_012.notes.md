# eaton_essays_islam_indian_history — chunk 012 — pp. 171–183

The opening two-thirds of Chapter Six, "The Articulation of Islamic Space in the Medieval Deccan",
footnoted as "Reprinted from Irene A. Bierman, ed., *Islam on the Margins* (Los Angles [*sic*]:
Center for Near Eastern Studies, UCLA, 2000)" (p. 171). Three of its sections: "The 'Maginot Line'
of Deccan Historiography" (p. 171), "Structural Similarities between Bahmani and Vijayanagara
States" (pp. 172–178), "Sacred Space and the Bahmani Sultanate" (pp. 178–183), and the first
paragraphs of "Sacred Space and the Vijayanagara Kingdom" (p. 183), where the chunk breaks
mid-sentence.

**No archive row appears in this chunk.** Its value to the archive is a mechanism rather than a
fact: pp. 180–183 give Eaton's fullest statement of *why* a saint's tomb inside the subcontinent
was a political asset, how *wilayat* (a shaikh's spiritual jurisdiction) functioned as a claim over
territory that removed it from the category of plunderable land, and what a new sultan paid, in
weighed gold and silver, to attach himself to a shrine at his coronation (p. 182). That is
directly the "what welfare functions do shrines produce" question, argued for Chishti shrines in
the Deccan and Delhi rather than for Punjab. See Doubts for two name collisions that must not be
mistaken for archive matches.

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- **Nizam al-Din Auliya (d. 1325)**, "the greatest Chishti shaikh of fourteenth century Delhi"
  (p. 180); "the former spiritual sovereign of North India's Tughluq Empire" (p. 182). His death in
  1325 is the pivot of 'Isami's account of Delhi's decline (p. 181).
- **Burhan al-Din Gharib (d. 1337)**, a leading disciple of Nizam al-Din, who "moved from Delhi to
  the Tughluqs' Deccan capital of Daulatabad" in **1329** (p. 181); his shrine is "located in
  Khuldabad in the northern Deccan" (p. 182). Nizam al-Din "had granted Burhan al-Din spiritual
  sovereignty (*wilayat*) over the Deccan plateau" (p. 182). **Not the Burhan-ud-din of the archive
  — see Doubts.**
- **Shaikh Zain al-Din Shirazi (d. 1369)**, Burhan al-Din's leading disciple, to whom "the
  spiritual protection of Daulatabad, and by implication all Deccan territories under the city's
  jurisdiction, passed" in 1337; "the Deccan's principal Chishti shaikh at the time of the Bahmani
  revolution of 1347", who "played a pivotal role in transforming a rebel state to a legitimate
  Islamic kingdom" (p. 181).
- **Shah Ni'mat Allah Kirmani**, "Iran's most eminent shaikh of the day", in a late-fourteenth
  century encounter with **Timur** (p. 182). **Not a Kirmani of the archive — see Doubts.**
- **Sultan Hasan Bahman Shah (r. 1347–58)**, founder of the Bahmani Sultanate, capital
  **Gulbarga** (pp. 180, 182).
- **Muhammad bin Tughluq (r. 1325–51)**, who "openly displayed disdain fo[r] the Deccan and its
  distinctive culture" (p. 177); around 1333 at Daulatabad he "refused to use local Deccani water
  and insisted instead on using water hand-carried to Daulatabad from the Ganges River, a forty
  days' journey to the north" (p. 177, n. 20; repeated p. 180).
- **Balban**, "the future Sultan of Delhi", who in **1247** advocated raiding Indian states to fund
  defence against the Mongols (pp. 178–179, n. 23).
- **Minhaj Siraj Juzjani**, "India's most prominent historian of the age", writing in **1260**
  (p. 179).
- **'Abd al-Malik 'Isami**, "the earliest panegyrist at the Bahmani court" (p. 181).
- **Vijayanagara rulers**: **Marappa**, "one of the five brothers who launched the Vijayanagara
  state", styled *hindu-r[a]ya-suratrana* in **1347** (p. 175); **Harihara I and Bukka**, praised
  with the same title in **1354** (p. 176); **Devaraya II (1425–46)**, who "more than any other
  monarch was responsible for the assimilation of Muslim cavalry and troops, together with
  Turko-Persian modes of warfare, into Vijayanagara's military system"; **Krishna Deva Raya
  (1509–29)**, "the most famous and powerful of all Vijayanagara kings", who "reigned during the
  period of Vijayanagara's most extensive cultural exchanges with the sultanates of the northern
  Deccan" (p. 176).
- **Kapaya Nayak**, a Telugu chieftain who rebelled against the Delhi Sultanate in the eastern
  Deccan and in **1367** styled himself "'Andhra suratr[a]na,' or 'Sultan of Andhra'" (p. 176).
- **Pampa's *tirtha*** on the southern bank of the **Tungabhadra River**, a river-goddess cult site
  attested "as early as the seventh century", "where passing chieftains would halt and make votive
  offerings during military campaigns"; first temple by the ninth century, "dedicated evidently to
  this goddess"; donations to **Mahakala Deva**, "the violent aspect of Siva, associated with
  destruction and death", from the early eleventh century; by the twelfth century a temple complex
  to **Virupaksa**, "who represented Siva's more universal and benign aspect" (p. 183). The chunk
  breaks here.
- **Mongol dates as given**: Genghis Khan reached the Indus in **1221**; Mongol armies threatened
  Delhi in **1297, 1299, 1303 and 1327**; in **1258** armies under Hülegü Khan "sacked Baghdad,
  executed the Abbasid caliph, and abolished the office of the caliphate" (pp. 178–179).

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **A coronation gift to a shrine, weighed.** On his accession at Gulbarga, Hasan Bahman Shah
  "ordered a gift of five *man* (200 lbs.) of gold and ten *man* (400 lbs.) of silver to be given
  to the shrine of Burhan al-Din Gharib, located in Khuldabad in the northern Deccan" (p. 182,
  citing Firishta, *Tarikh-i Firishta*, Lucknow edn, 1:27). Eaton's reading of the transfer is
  strategic, not pious: "The new sultan's substantial gift to the Khuldabad shrine was politically
  astute, since it acknowledged not only Burhan al-Din Gharib, but that shaikh's own master, Nizam
  al-Din Auliya" (p. 182). **The pound equivalents are the text's own glosses on *man*, i.e. 40 lbs
  to the *man*.**
- **The tomb as located capital.** "Unlike other Sufi orders of the day, the tomb sites of whose
  major shaikhs were located in distant Central Asia or West Asia, the tombs and shrines of the
  major Chishti shaikhs were located within the subcontinent. This focus on India gave shaikhs of
  the Chishti order, among all members of India's formal or informal Muslim establishment, the best
  claims to being both legitimately Islamic and authentically Indian" (p. 180).
- **Shaikhs as an instrument of state expansion.** "as first the Khalaji and then the Tughluq
  sultans of Delhi expanded militarily over the subcontinent, it was Chishti shaikhs who
  accompanied imperial governors and future rulers to India's far-flung corners, there to
  indigenize and legitimate new, satellite Indo-Muslim polities" (p. 180).
- **Jurisdiction as inheritable.** *Wilayat* over Daulatabad "and by implication all Deccan
  territories under the city's jurisdiction" passed from Burhan al-Din to Zain al-Din on the
  former's death in 1337 (p. 181) — a transfer of territorial spiritual office by discipleship, not
  by descent, in this account.
- **A relic used as a sovereignty token.** Per 'Isami, "the very robe worn by the Prophet Muhammad
  on the night he ascended to Paradise—a robe subsequently passed on through twenty-three
  generations of holymen until it was finally received by Zain al-Din—was bestowed upon the poet's
  own royal patron and founder of the Bahmani state, Sultan Hasan Bahman Shah" (pp. 181–182).
- **Land tenure tied to troops, on both sides of the Krishna.** The *iqta'* "was introduced to
  northern India in the eleventh century by Persianized Turks under the later Ghaznavids", and by
  the late thirteenth century its principles "had seeped southward into the eastern Deccan, where
  kings of the Kakatiya dynasty employed an analogous institution called *nayamkara*" (pp. 174–175).
  "Both terms referred to a distinctive mode of land tenure that linked a state's revenue system to
  its armed forces. Specifically, nobles were given land assignments commensurate in area and
  productive capacity with the size of cavalry or other military units that they were expected to
  maintain" (p. 175). The *iqta'* reached the northern Deccan with "the earliest Bahmani sultans" in
  the mid-fourteenth century, and "from about the same time kings in Vijayanagara began issuing
  *nayamkara* grants, with an especially large number of them appearing in the sixteenth century"
  (p. 175). Wagoner calls these tenures "'an Indic adaptation of the *iqta'* system'" (p. 175).
- **The grant-making described by a chronicler.** Saiyid 'Ali Tabataba, whose *Burhan-i ma'athir*
  was completed **1595–6**, recorded that in 1347, "immediately after chasing the last Tughluq
  troops out of the Deccan, the first Bahmani sultan began naming his closest confederates to the
  highest posts of state": "Each of the kingdom's amirs, vazirs, and nameworthy cavalrymen, having
  been distinguished with a *jagir* and *muqassa* according to his own rank, received an *iqta'* in
  towns and *parganas*, and he maintained troops, attendants, and equipage that were proportionate
  to those [lands]" (p. 175, n. 11; Eaton's own translation, so noted).
- **Court dress and gift circulation as legitimation goods.** Garments "adopted and distributed by
  the court of Vijayanagara, served to define political legitimacy in that kingdom, just as they did
  in the northern sultanates": "the high, conical cap of brocaded fabric, known in Telugu as
  *k[u]layi*, a term derived from the Persian *kul[ah]*, and a long tunic known in Telugu as
  *kabayi*, derived from the Arabic *[q]aba*" (p. 174). The itemised gift lists, after Wagoner
  (p. 174, n. 7): Vijayanagara court — "cap, ornamental shirt, necklace, pearl earrings, yellow
  shawl, musk, and betel"; northern sultanates — "sash, tunic, turban, and turban band"; Orissa
  kingdom — "turban, gold necklace, pearl earring, shirt, sash, musk, and betel".
- **Temples built like mosques.** Eaton asks what one would otherwise make "of Hindu landholders
  (*zamindars*) who built temples architecturally indistinguishable from mosques sponsored by their
  Muslim overlords" (p. 173) — asserted as an instance, with no site named.
- **Political dates**: Vijayanagara founded 1346, the Bahmani Kingdom "the next year", 1347
  (p. 177); Kulke, quoted, gives "the 'festival of victory' in the year 1346 and the existence of a
  new capital and new imperial titles in the year 1368" as the "major steps" (p. 177, n. 19).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **Nizam al-Din Auliya's prediction, told of the Bahmani founder.** Having met Muhammad bin
  Tughluq at his hospice while the future Hasan Bahman Shah "was waiting outside", the shaikh "is
  said to have remarked, 'One sultan has left my door; another is waiting there'" (p. 180, citing
  Tabataba and Firishta). Eaton treats it as a genre convention, not an event: "the theme, common in
  thirteenth and fourteenth century Indo-Persian literature, of a Sufi shaikh predicting future
  kingship for some civilian, with such 'prediction' actually serving as a rhetorical form of royal
  appointment" (p. 180).
- **'Isami's doctrine of saintly protection**, quoted: "It is well known… that the existence of the
  world is bound up closely with that of the men of faith", and "In every country there is a man of
  piety who keeps it going and well. Although there might be a monarch in every country, yet it is
  actually under the protection of a *fakir*… The monarchs are, to all appearance, the head of the
  State, but it is the *fakirs* who avert the impending calamities" (p. 181).
- **Delhi's ruin and Daulatabad's prosperity as saintly effects**, per 'Isami: with Nizam al-Din's
  death in 1325 "the city and empire of Delhi sank to an irredeemable state of desolation, tyranny,
  and turmoil", whereas Daulatabad "prospered mightily as a result of the immigrant's presence"
  after Burhan al-Din Gharib settled there in 1329 (p. 181). Reported as the poet's view.
- **The Prophet's ascension robe**, passed "through twenty-three generations of holymen" to Zain
  al-Din and then to Hasan Bahman Shah — attributed to 'Isami (pp. 181–182).
- **Shah Ni'mat Allah Kirmani to Timur**: "'My state is a world without end,' said the shaikh to the
  emperor, 'while yours extends from Khatay [i.e. Cathay] to Shiraz'" (p. 182; Persian given in
  n. 31 as "Mulk-i man 'alamist bi payan; va an-i t[u] khatast ta Shiraz").

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The chunk's target: the Krishna River as a "Maginot Line".** North of it, "from the fourteenth
  through the late seventeenth centuries, lay the Bahmani Kingdom and its successor sultanates—all
  of them ruled by Muslims—and to the south lay the Vijayanagara Kingdom, often characterized as a
  'Hindu bulwark' against Muslim encroachments from the north" (p. 172). His two questions: "To
  what extent is this 'Maginot Line' of the Deccan a product of modern-day tensions, reinforced
  perhaps by an academic division of labour according to which people who study Persian and Urdu
  work north of the Krishna, and those who study Sanskrit or the Dravidian languages work south of
  that river? Conversely, to what extent did such a line actually exist in the period to which it
  is supposed to refer…?" (p. 172). **The self-criticism of the discipline's own division of labour
  is worth noting for an archive built on Urdu and Persian sources.**
- **Against explaining sacred space by colonial discourse.** Constructions of sacred space "are
  hardly new. Nor can they be explained entirely or even mainly in terms of a nineteenth century
  'colonial discourse,' of 'Orientalism,' or of backward projections of twentieth century
  nationalist movements or present-day geopolitical conflicts. Rather, the cultural construction of
  sacred space extends deep in time" (p. 171). Destructions, constructions and wars "provided
  screens onto which subsequent generations of hagiographers or chroniclers, themselves writing
  well before the eighteenth century, projected their own vision of how history happened, or at
  least, ought to have happened" (p. 171). Hence: "the project for historians today has as much to
  do with unravelling complicated historiographies as it does with writing histories. Perhaps one
  cannot even separate the two" (p. 171). **This is the same anti-colonial-discourse position as
  chunk 010's attack on Subalternism, here applied to sacred sites rather than to events.**
- **Hodgson's "Islamicate" is the organising concept, quoted and then extended.** Hodgson (d. 1968)
  wrote that "by the sixteenth century, most of the East Christian[,] Hindu, and Theravada Buddhist
  peoples found themselves more or less enclaved in an Islamicate world where Muslim standards of
  taste commonly made their way even into independent kingdoms, like Hindu Vijayanagara or Norman
  Sicily" (p. 172). Eaton's gloss: beyond predominantly Muslim areas "there lay a transregional,
  cosmopolitan cultural zone that, even if not populated by large numbers of Muslims, was defined
  and inspired by the political, material, literary, and aesthetic vision of Islam", and 'Islamicate'
  is opposed to 'Islamic', "a term properly restricted to the specifically religious dimensions of
  the Muslim community's collective culture" (pp. 172–173). Eaton's own claim: the notion "holds the
  key, I believe, to understanding the complex range of exchanges that occurred between
  Turko-Persian and Indic cultures in medieval South Asia" (p. 173). A related formulation from
  Voll is noted — an 'Islamic world-system' resting on "a 'mutually intelligible discourse among all
  who identify themselves as Muslims within the Dar al-Islam'" (p. 172, n. 3) — and attributed to
  Voll, not adopted.
- **The evidence for structural similarity, in four blocks.** (1) *Moral economy*: Fakhr al-Din Razi
  of Herat (d. 1209) — "Wealth is gathered from the subjects; The subjects are made servants by
  justice; Justice is the axis of the prosperity of the world" — set beside the Telugu *Badde-niti*,
  "composed in the Andhra country in the twelfth or thirteenth century" — "To acquire wealth[,] make
  the people prosper. To make the people prosper: justice is the means… They say that justice is the
  treasury of kings" (p. 173). Eaton: "Both extracts postulate a conceptual linkage between wealth,
  subjects, and justice, forming thereby a unified, coherent political ideology. Since no comparable
  statement can be found in classical Sanskrit texts on governance, it would appear that such a
  conception of a state's proper moral economy became current in the subcontinent only after the
  rise of Indo-Turkish rule in north India" (p. 173) — and because the maxim was requoted by
  sixteenth century Vijayanagara writers, "one may assume that the ideology continued to inform the
  practice of statecraft south of the Krishna throughout the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries,
  just as it did the sultanates to the north" (p. 174). **Note the argument is an argument from
  silence in Sanskrit plus a dating claim — see Doubts.** (2) *Material culture*, the shared
  garments above (p. 174). (3) *Political economy*, *iqta'* and *nayamkara* above (pp. 174–175).
  (4) *Titulature*: from 1347 Vijayanagara kings were "regularly styled *su[lt]an*, or with some
  variation of the formula 'Sultan of/among the Indian rajas'" (pp. 175–176).
- **Why a Hindu king could call himself sultan — the "Church"/"State" claim.** "Whereas religious
  leadership of Sunni Muslims was invested formally in the caliph, and informally in local Sufi
  shaikhs, political leadership of both Muslims and non-Muslims resided in the head of state, or
  sultan, who was understood as the *de facto* sovereign over the entire population of his
  sultanate. Because early Indo-Muslim political culture thus sharply separated 'Church' and
  'State,' it was not uncommon for both Muslim and Hindu rulers aspiring to political grandeur…to
  style themselves 'sultan'. This was, after all, the most powerful and prestigious term available
  in the political vocabulary of the medieval Islamicate world" (p. 176).
- **The earliest self-description as "Hindu", and what the word then meant** — in a footnote, so
  easily missed: of the 1354 Harihara/Bukka inscriptions, "these are the earliest known instances in
  which Indians referred to themselves as 'Hindu'. At this time, the term seems to have had a
  geographical and ethnic meaning—that is, 'a native of India'—and not the religious meaning that it
  would acquire later" (p. 176, n. 15). Consistent with chunk 010's *turushka* argument (p. 139).
- **Both states as Tughluq successor states.** "The usual interpretation of Vijayanagara's origins,
  heavily inflected by modern communalist biases, is that the state emerged as an expression of
  Hindu resistance to the Islamic religion as carried south by the sultans of Delhi… This view,
  which effectively maps a 'Maginot Line' along Vijayanagara's northern frontier formed by the
  Krishna River, misses the important point that the Vijayanagara and Bahmani states did not arise
  out of opposition to each other, but rather emerged about the same time…for essentially the same
  reasons" — both "revolutionary regimes that had evolved out of armed resistance to a common,
  northern, imperial power—the Delhi Sultanate" (p. 177). Evidence offered: Telugu chieftains who had
  already overthrown the Tughluqs helped found the Bahmani state (p. 177); prior Delhi service
  "helped confer political authority and legitimacy on the founders of [b]oth" states, so "both
  polities may properly be understood as Tughluq successor states in the Deccan", as shown by "their
  bold claims to being sultan, a powerful title to which the Tughluq emperor had staked sole claim
  in India" (p. 178); and the earliest Sanskrit sources on Vijayanagara's founding "make no claim
  that the brothers had converted to Islam while in Delhi or were 'reconverted' to Hinduism upon
  their return to the Deccan", "[c]ontrary to later, communalist, interpretations" (p. 178, n. 22).
- **Regional, not religious, chauvinism.** Of the Ganges-water story: "Without arguing that the
  sultan actually believed in the sanctity of Ganges water, one can certainly detect in his behavior
  a north Indian chauvinism vis-a-vis the south… Religion was not an issue here; but regional
  chauvinism certainly was" (p. 177, n. 20).
- **Deccan invasion explained by Mongol pressure — a security-financing argument.** Delhi's rulers
  "well understood that resources were necessary in order to defend north India from Mongol
  invasions", Balban having "advocated raiding Indian states for precisely this purpose" (p. 178);
  so "one can see the Delhi Sultanate's invasions of the Deccan, which began in the late thirteenth
  century when the Mongol threat to north India intensified, as a continuation of an earlier policy
  of looting Indian cities beyond the Sultanate's frontiers in order to defend itself from Central
  Asian attackers" (p. 179). He credits the idea: "This idea was first suggested by Carl Ernst,
  *Eternal Garden*" (p. 179, n. 24). Balban's own 1247 words are quoted at length — raiding so
  "that booty may fall into the hands of the troops of Islam, and means to repel the infidel Mughals
  [Mongols], in the shape of wealth, may be amassed" (p. 179, n. 23).
- **The geo-religious layer above the strategic one.** After 1258, "thoughtful Muslims of the later
  thirteenth century had come to see India as destined to play a very special role for Islam in the
  post-Mongol world. In their view, only India, now deemed the 'focus' of the world-wide Muslim
  community, could save the Islamic enterprise from certain calamity" — resting on Minhaj's 1260
  observation that with Mongol conquest "the authority of the Muhammadan religion departed from
  those regions, which became the seat of paganism, [while] the kingdom of Hindustan… became the
  focus of the people of Islam, and orbit of the possessors of religion" (p. 179; the emphasis is
  Eaton's, so marked). Conclusion: the Deccan invasions "must be seen in this larger, geo-religious
  context" (p. 180). **Distinguish: the "focus of Islam" claim is Minhaj's; the causal use of it is
  Eaton's.**
- **Why Chishtis, and not other orders — the core mechanism for this archive.** With the caliphate
  abolished and north India "deemed the 'focus of the peoples of Islam,' Sufi shaikhs belonging to
  the Chishti order had emerged as the preeminent patrons of Indo-Muslim kingship" (p. 180). The
  stated reason is the geography of their tombs (quoted under Practices): Indian tombs gave them
  "the best claims to being both legitimately Islamic and authentically Indian" (p. 180). Note the
  direction of patronage in his sentence — the shaikhs are "patrons of…kingship", not clients of it.
- **Dual sovereignty: *wilayat* leased out as *hukumat*.** "it was implicitly assumed that
  spiritually powerful Sufi shaikhs were the only genuinely valid sovereigns over this world; but,
  so that they might concentrate on purely spiritual concerns…these same shaikhs were thought to
  have leased out political sovereignty (*hukumat*) to kings, who were thus charged with the messy
  and very worldly business of administration, taxation, warfare, and so forth" (pp. 180–181). This
  was "a variation on the sort of dual sovereignty that had earlier emerged in the Iranian Plateau
  in the eleventh and twelfth centuries, where Turkish strongmen held *de facto* political power as
  'sultans' while the caliph in Baghdad held juridical and religious authority. By the fourteenth
  century, however, it was now Sufi shaikhs who lent juridical and religious legitimacy to
  Indo-Muslim states, since no functioning caliph had existed since 1258" (p. 181). The doctrine is
  evidenced from 'Isami, for whom "history itself was but the working out of divine will as mediated
  by spiritually powerful shaikhs, especially those of the Chishti order" (p. 181) — **'Isami's
  theology; Eaton's use of it as evidence of an assumption current at the Bahmani court.**
- **Two conceptions of space, and the practical correction.** "Whereas *hukumat*, royal authority,
  was always limited in reach, and never coincided with the entire Muslim world—far less with the
  entire planet—the spiritual sovereignty of Sufis, *wilayat*, was theoretically unlimited in
  territorial extent, and hence far greater than the worldly sovereignty of sultans" (p. 182). But:
  "In practice, however, the domain of a Sufi's jurisdiction, or *wilayat*, was just as limited as
  the territorial reach of his royal patron's worldly domain (*hukumat*), if not more so" (p. 183).
- **What inclusion in a *wilayat* did — the mechanism stated most sharply.** "Nonetheless, the
  inclusion of any territory within the *wilayat* of a powerful Sufi shaikh was always charged with
  political significance, since it meant that such territory could not be viewed—as the Deccan
  formerly had been—as land ready for plunder, like a ripe plum… whereas Khalaji and Tughluq
  conquests in the Deccan lacked a moral basis and were undertaken for plunder pure and simple, the
  extension of the Sufis' notion of *wilayat* into the region lent moral legitimacy to the planting
  of subsequent Indo-Muslim states there. To borrow from the rhetoric of classical Islam, Sufi
  shaikhs—first in Delhi, then in the Deccan—could transform yesterday's Abode of War, *Dar
  al-Harb*, into today's Abode of Peace, or *Dar al-Isl[a]m*, thereby bringing about an internally
  coherent basis for the legitimate transplanting of Indo-Muslim rule and civilization from region
  to region within South Asia" (p. 183). **The claim is an institutional one about extractive
  incentives: a saint's jurisdiction reclassified land from plunderable to governable. That is a
  statable welfare mechanism — a shrine changing the tax-and-raid regime over its surrounding
  territory — and it is argued from chronicle and hagiography, not from revenue data.** Eaton adds
  a colonial coda in a footnote: the plunder view "was also the view later adopted by servants of
  the eighteenth century East India Company, when Englishmen plundered the wealth of Bengal for no
  nobler an ideal than that of 'shaking the pagoda tree'" (p. 183, n. 32).
- **Where the two halves of the Deccan do diverge: sacred space, and a class location for it.** The
  differences between the kingdoms "sprang mainly from the contrasting notions of sacred space that
  were constructed by contemporary ideologues representing propertied classes on the two sides of
  the Krishna" (p. 178). Vijayanagara's "political origins were tied to the same anti-Tughluq
  revolutions that produced the Bahmani state", but "its religious origins had very different
  roots" — traced by recent research to a river-goddess cult from the seventh century (p. 183). The
  comparison is cut off by the chunk boundary.
- **Huntington as foil.** "For people like Samuel Huntington the Dardanelles forms a deep,
  dangerous, and impermeable civilizational frontier demarcating 'Western Civilization' from the
  world of Islam" (p. 171, citing "The Clash of Civilizations?", *Foreign Affairs*, June 1993,
  22–49); the Krishna is offered as the Indianist equivalent (p. 172).

## Doubts

- **Two name collisions that are not archive matches, recorded so no later pass makes one.**
  (a) The chunk's **Burhan al-Din Gharib** died **1337**, moved to Daulatabad in 1329, and is
  buried at **Khuldabad in the northern Deccan** (pp. 181–182). The archive's **langer-makhdoom**
  row is Makhdoom Burhan-ud-din at Chiniot, Punjab, died 1245. Different man, different century,
  different country; the chunk says nothing about the Chiniot figure. (b) **Shah Ni'mat Allah
  Kirmani** is placed in **Iran** in the late fourteenth century (p. 182); the archive's Kirmani
  nisba belongs to Daud Bandagi Kirmani (**shergarh**, d. 1575) and to the Dawood Bandgi Kirmani
  affiliation recorded for **darbar-abul-muali-qadri**. The chunk gives no link between them.
- **The Sanskrit-silence argument at p. 173 should be handled carefully if cited.** The *Badde-niti*
  verse is dated "in the twelfth or thirteenth century, that is, before the appearance of Turkish
  Muslims there in the early fourteenth century" (p. 173), yet Eaton concludes that the moral-economy
  conception "became current in the subcontinent only after the rise of Indo-Turkish rule in north
  India" (p. 173). The two are reconcilable only if the diffusion ran from north India after 1192
  into a Telugu text of the twelfth-or-thirteenth century, and the chunk does not spell that out or
  narrow the Telugu dating. The negative premise ("no comparable statement can be found in classical
  Sanskrit texts on governance") is asserted without citation.
- **The 1347 *suratrana* inscription is itself contested in Eaton's own footnote.** Vasundhara
  Filliozat "has doubts about the authenticity of this inscription on the grounds that it lacks a
  royal seal and that the minister mentioned in it, who is also mentioned in a 1391 inscription,
  would not likely have served for so long" (pp. 175–176, n. 14). Eaton reports the doubt and still
  uses the 1347 date in the body text (p. 175). Any use of "1347, the very year that the Bahmani
  sultanate was established" should carry the caveat.
- **A chronicle's distance from its event, in two places.** Tabataba's account of the 1347 *iqta'*
  grants comes from a work "completed…in 1595–6" (p. 175, n. 11), i.e. roughly 250 years later, and
  the 200 lbs/400 lbs coronation gift comes from Firishta (p. 182), later still. Kashani's
  *Ghara'ib al-karamat*, by contrast, is dated to **1340** by a disciple of Burhan al-Din, "just
  several years after his master's death" (p. 182, n. 30). The figures should not be quoted as
  recorded amounts without the source's date.
- **Text-layer artefacts that touch names or terms above** (no `[OCR?]` flags exist in this book;
  these are my readings of the PDF's own text layer, and each should be checked against the PDF
  before the word is printed): "hindu-mya-suratrana" for *hindu-raya-suratrana* and "swhan" for
  *sultan* (pp. 175, 176); "Andhra: suratnina" for *Andhra suratrana* (p. 176); "Azdlayi" and
  "kudh" for *kulayi* and *kulah*, "gaba" for *qaba* (p. 174); "mayamkara" for *nayamkara* and
  "igta" for *iqta'* throughout (pp. 174–175); "hukimat"/"hukumat" and "wiliyat"/"wilayat" both
  spellings within a page (pp. 181–182); "Dar al-Iskim" for *Dar al-Islam* (p. 183);
  "Vidyiranyakilajiana" for *Vidyaranyakalajnana* (p. 178, n. 22); "Futwhu's Salatin" and "Tsami"
  for 'Isami's *Futuh al-salatin* (pp. 181–182); "Tabagat-i Nasir" for *Tabaqat-i Nasiri* (p. 179);
  "Epigraphia India, X1V" for *Epigraphia Indica* XIV (p. 176, n. 17); "whorkeeps" for "who keeps"
  (p. 181); "disdain fog the Deccan" for "disdain for" (p. 177); "doth the Bahmani and the
  Vijayanagara states" for "both" (p. 178); "Los Angles" for Los Angeles (p. 171); "most of the East
  Christian Hindu, and Theravada Buddhist peoples" is missing a comma after "Christian" in the
  Hodgson quotation (p. 172) — **that quotation must be checked before it is reproduced.**
- **Footnote numbers spliced into the running text** at p. 172 ("For most modern historians of 1
  Samuel P. Huntington…") and a stray quote mark for a footnote marker at p. 171 ("are hardly
  new."Nor can they"). Section headings arrive with the running heads and page numbers interleaved
  ("160 ¢ Essays on Islam and Indian History"), so line order around a page break is not reliable.
- **One citation is impossible as printed**: Raverty's translation of the *Tabaqat-i Nasiri* is
  cited as "2:369-900" for the 1260 passage (p. 179, n. 25) — a 531-page range. Flagged, not
  corrected. The Tehran edition of the same work is dated "1363 A.H." (p. 179, n. 23), which is
  ambiguous between Hijri (1944) and Iranian solar (1984).
- **The chunk breaks mid-sentence at p. 183**, inside the first paragraphs of "Sacred Space and the
  Vijayanagara Kingdom": "a temple complex dedicated to Virupaksa, who represented Siva's more
  universal and benign aspect," with no predicate. The Vijayanagara half of the chapter's central
  comparison is therefore not in this chunk, and no conclusion of the chapter should be drawn from
  it alone.
- The gift-list attributions at p. 174, n. 7 (Vijayanagara / northern sultanates / Orissa) are
  summarised from Wagoner by Eaton without page-by-page attribution of each item; the note cites
  "Wagoner, *Tidings*, 106, 116, 149" for all three lists together.
