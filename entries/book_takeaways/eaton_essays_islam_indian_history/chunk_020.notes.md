## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Land grants as the engine of shrine-building on a frontier — the load-bearing mechanism of the
  chunk.** "in the Mughal period, Bengal's agrarian and political frontiers had collapsed into one.
  From Sylhet through Chittagong, the government fused the political goal of deepening its authority
  among dependent clients rooted on the land with the economic goal of expanding the state's arable
  land area" (p. 286).
- **How the grant produced the institution.** "This was achieved by issuing grants aiming at the
  agricultural development of the forested hinterland, most of whose recipients were petty mu/las
  [*mullas*], pilgrims returned from Mecca, preachers, charismatic pirs, and local chieftains seeking
  tax-free land. These men oversaw, or undertook to oversee, the clearing of forests and the
  construction of mosques or shrines, which in turn became the nuclei for the diffusion of Islamic
  ideals along the agrarian frontier" (p. 286). **Tax-free land is named as the recipients' motive**
  (p. 286). No grant, acreage, date or revenue figure is given in this chunk (not stated).
- **The shrine as an economic as well as religious centre** — "the local communities that fell under
  the economic and religious influence of these institutions" (p. 286); the two are stated together,
  not separated.
- **The plough as the social boundary.** "What served most profoundly to distinguish Bengali Muslim
  cultivators from ashraf classes… was the plough. Whereas farmers defined their Muslim identity
  around cultivating the soil, the ashraf disdained the plough and refused to touch it" (p. 284).
  Evidence: a 1901 survey among Muslims of Nadia District — "'the Ashrafs will not adopt cultivation
  for their living. They consider cultivation to be a degraded occupation and they shun it for that
  reason'" (p. 284); Risley in the Census of the same year (p. 284); and the 1960 Comilla study
  (p. 284, n.).
- **Occupational identity denied as convert identity.** The Khulna gazetteer: the Muslim masses
  "'do not, however, know or admit that they are the descendants of converts to Islam; according to
  them they are the tillers of the soil, while the Ashraf do not cultivate the land with their own
  hands'" (p. 284).
- **Wet rice as the economic base the religion grew on.** "the natural population growth that
  accompanied the diffusion or the intensification of wet rice agriculture and the production of
  surplus food grains" (p. 287); hence "a remarkable congruence between a socioeconomic system geared
  to the production of wet rice and a religious ideology that conferred special meaning on agrarian
  life" (p. 287).
- **State obstruction of Islamization, recorded as a fact of administration**: "some Mughal officers
  even opposed the Islamization of native Bengalis" who engaged in agriculture (p. 285).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The essay's governing thesis about religion itself.** "Over the past fifteen centuries, Islam has
  been continuously redefined, reinterpreted, and contested, as competing social groups have risen or
  fallen in prominence and influence. To the historian, the challenge is to identify those groups
  and, by situating them in their unique historical contexts, to determine how they constructed the
  religion in the particular way they did" (p. 285).
- **The vocabulary of orthodoxy is rejected as analysis.** "it becomes unproductive, or simply wrong,
  to speak of one group's understanding of Islam as 'orthodox' and another's as 'unorthodox,' or of
  one variant as 'fundamentalist' and another as 'syncretic,' or whatever. Such rhetorical labels may
  help in identifying and sorting out competing social classes in a given historical situation, or in
  determining who is on whose side in a particular debate. But as analytical tools they are quite
  useless" (p. 285). **This is the chunk's most transferable claim for archive use** — it is a general
  methodological position, not a Bengal-specific one.
- **Against "expansion of Islam" as a model.** "it would be wrong to view Islam as a monolithic
  essence that simply 'expanded' across space, time, and social class, in the process assimilating
  great numbers of people into a single framework of piety" (p. 285). The replacement model is a
  succession of "dominant carrieis [*carriers*], spokesmen, or representatives" (p. 285).
- **The periodized succession of carriers, stated as the essay's summary of its own findings**
  (p. 285): thirteenth century — Islam "associated with the ruling ethos of the delta's Turkish
  conquerors", persisting in the cities "sustained especially by Sufi shaikhs of the Chishti order";
  then the Mughal conquest brings "a new elite class of ashraf Muslims… typically administrators,
  soldiers, mystics, scholars, or long-distance merchants", for whom "a rich tradition of Persian art
  and literature served to mediate and inform Islamic piety, which most of them subordinated to the
  secular ethos of Mughal imperialism"; then "By the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries… owing
  principally to phenomenal levels of agrarian and demographic expansion in East Bengal, the dominant
  carriers of Islamic civilization in the delta were no longer the urban ashraf, but peasant
  cultivators of the eastern frontier, who in extraordinary ways had assimilated Islam to their
  agrarian worldview."
- **"Conversion" rejected as a category for premodern Bengal.** "In the context of premodern Bengal,
  then, it would seem inappropriate to speak of the 'conversion' of 'Hindus' to Islam. What one
  finds, rather, is an expanding agrarian civilization, whose cultural counterpart was the growth of
  the cult of Allah" (p. 287), composed of three named processes: "(a) the eastward movement and
  settlement of colonizers from points west, (b) the incorporation of frontier tribal peoples into
  the expanding agrarian civilization, and (c) the natural population growth that accompanied the
  diffusion or the intensification of wet rice agriculture" (p. 287).
- **The mechanism he puts in conversion's place: "seepage".** Islam was not perceived "as alien, or as
  a closed, exclusive system to be accepted or rejected as a whole"; the frontier was "a fluid context
  in which Islamic superhuman agencies, typically identified with local superhuman agencies, gradually
  seeped into local cosmologies that were themselves dynamic. This 'seepage' occurred over such a long
  period of time that one can at no point identify a specific moment of 'conversion,' or any single
  moment when peoples saw themselves as having made a dramatic break with the past" (p. 286). He
  flags the anachronism explicitly: "Although today one habitually thinks of world religions as
  self-contained and complete systems with well-defined borders, such a static or fixed understanding
  does not apply to Bengal's premodern frontier" (p. 286).
- **Who called Islam foreign, and why that is a historiographical fact rather than a finding.** "the
  cultivating classes never seem to have regarded it as 'foreign'—even though some Muslim and Hindu
  literati and foreign observers did, and still do" (p. 286); the footnote attributes the persistence
  to Manrique's 1629 categories anticipating "later observers who, informed… by normative
  understandings of what constitutes proper Islam, had difficulty understanding how Bengal's 'natives'
  could also be 'Moors'", and then to "British imperialists and both Hindu and Muslim reformers, each
  for their own reasons, stress[ing] Islam's 'foreignness'" in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries
  (p. 286, n. 58, referring the reader on to Joya Chatterji, "The Bengali Muslim: A Contradiction in
  Terms? An Overview of the Debate on Bengali Muslim Identity," *Comparative Studies of South Asia,
  Africa, and the Middle East* 16/2 (1996), 16–24).
- **The immigrant elite compared to the East India Company — Eaton's own analogy.** "the bulk of the
  Turks, Afghans, Iranians or Arabs who had migrated to India from the eleventh century onward no more
  saw themselves taking up agriculture than did English servants of the East India Company. Like the
  British, foreign-born Muslims saw themselves as having come to India to administer a vast empire
  whose wealth they would appropriate, and not to participate with Indians as fellow cultivators"
  (pp. 284–285).
- **Colonial-era evidence used for the premodern period.** The 1901/1908/1911 gazetteer and census
  material (p. 284) and the 1960 village study (p. 284, n.) are carried as evidence about ashraf
  practice generally, with the footnote's own hedge: "These turn-of-the-century observations are
  echoed in more modern studies of Bengali Muslim society" (p. 284, n.). The extension backward is
  Eaton's, not the sources'.
- **Closing evaluative judgement.** "It is testimony to the vitality of Islam—and one of the clues to
  its success as a world religion—that its adherents in Bengal were so creative in accommodating local
  socio-cultural realities with the norms of the religion" (p. 287). This is the book's last sentence
  of running prose.
