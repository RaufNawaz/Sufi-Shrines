# schimmel_pain_and_grace — chunk 005 — pp. 79–95

**This chunk is Part I (Mir Dard), mid-chapter, and it is almost entirely doctrinal, institutional and
genealogical.** It carries the end of the section on man as the locus of the divine names, the whole
of Dard's claim to be the Prophet's true vicegerent, his two lists of self-bestowed names, then two
new section headings: `THE ASCETIC AND 'THE WORLD'.` (p. 87, folio 85) and `OLD AGE` (p. 94,
folio 92). **The archive yield is nil and that is recorded as an explicit negative below; the
silsila, khanqah, discipleship, hereditary-succession, samaʿ and Delhi-famine material is dense and
is recorded in full.**

**The measured folio regime holds on every page of this chunk, without exception.** Every one of the
seventeen pages prints its number in the running header: verso heads read `78 KHWAJA MIR DARD OF
DELHI` (number first), recto heads read `DARD'S LIFE AND TEACHING 77` (number last). PDF p. 79
carries printed `77`; PDF p. 95 carries printed `93`; folio = PDF − 2 is unbroken across pp. 79–95.
**No page in this chunk needs `folio not stated`.** One header is itself damaged — p. 92 prints
`90 a KHWAJA MIR DARD OF DELHI`, with a stray `a`[OCR?] between the number and the title — but the
numeral `90` is clean and gives folio 90 as expected.

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **No archive row is informed by this chunk.** Not one of the 169 rows is named, and no shrine, tomb,
  darbar, ʿurs or custodianship in present-day Pakistan is described anywhere in pp. 79–95. The only

## Practices, institutions, economy

**The explicit negative first: this chunk says nothing whatever about ʿurs, mela, langar, offerings,
votive gifts, sajjada nashin, Auqaf, endowment, waqf, inam, land grant, revenue, visitor numbers,
shrine disputes, state involvement in a shrine, or architecture and building dates.** All
**(not stated)**. What it does carry is a khanqah succession, a daily tomb-attendance routine, a
doctrine legitimating hereditary spiritual office, two Naqshbandi technical practices, music at the
shaikh's house, and one famine mortality figure. Those follow.

- **Convent succession, stated as a bare fact of office.** "Mir Alam, who lived for a long time in
  Bengal, became leader of the convent after his uncle Athar" (p. 93, folio 91). **This is the
  chunk's only statement of institutional succession, and it runs brother-then-son, not
  father-to-son.** The convent is not named, not located and not dated, and Dard's own tenure of it is
  not stated here — all **(not stated)**. The word used is "convent"; elsewhere the same establishment
  is "his paternal dargah" and "the batt al-ma' mur[OCR?] [bayt al-maʿmūr] of his Pir" (p. 89,
  folio 87).
- **The hereditary claim to spiritual office, argued as doctrine.** This is the chunk's most
  consequential institutional passage for the project. "real vicegerency of the Prophet is conditioned
  by proximity of time—the time of the khulafa? ar-rashidin[OCR?] [khulafāʾ ar-rāshidīn] was finished,
  as Muhammad had predicted, thirty years after his death, when Ali was murdered in 661. However, for
  the saints such a proximity of time is not necessary. He himself is the first and true vicegerent of
  his father, the Prince of the Muhamma-dans" (p. 80, folio 78). **A rule is stated for caliphs and
  then suspended for saints, and the vacated office is filled by a son.**
- **And the same claim put as a general law of fathers and sons.** "to obey the sire who is 'the place
  of manifestation of the names 'Creator' and 'Lord?'[OCR?] is essen-tially the same as to obey Him
  ... just as he who obeys the Prophet obeys God; for the Prophet is the spiritual father, and that is
  why his pure wives are called the mothers of the faithful, and the father is the vicegerent of the
  Prophet, ... especially when the father is a sincere Muhammadan and also a spiritual guide, for
  thanks to the grace of God a rotten and unworthy son never comes to such an excellent father"
  (pp. 92–93, folios 90–91). **The last clause is a hereditary-succession warrant in one sentence: an
  excellent shaikh cannot produce an unworthy heir.** Schimmel's own summary: "the father is, for the
  son, the manifestation of Di-vine mercy, and the son is, for the father, a Divine gift (wahb)"
  (p. 93, folio 91).
- **Investiture regalia, itemised in Dard's own Arabic.** "he inclined on his invented throne called
  al-mi' yar[OCR?] [al-miʿyār], and 'Orna-ment of the prayer-niche and the pulpit,' and put on his
  God-be-stowed crown and lifted his Muhammadan banner and called the faith-ful with special
  invitation to the pure Muhammadanism" (pp. 81–82, folios 79–80, citing "(IK 504 f.)"[OCR?]).
  **A throne with a proper name, a crown, a banner and the mihrab-and-minbar title — and he "made him
  sit on the throne of vicegerency of his father, as heritage and in realization" (p. 81,
  folio 79).**
- **The banner as a physical object with a stated military symbolism.** "As to the 'Muhammadan banner'
  it plays an important role in the symbolism of his father's book, where it is described as a kind of
  lance; 'When some brave hero takes it in his hand, and a number of people with sword and shield
  surround him, he can alone and on his own, gain victory over them'" (pp. 83–84, folios 81–82, citing
  "(NA I 322, cf. 834, II 868)").
- **Music at the shaikh's house, as a recurring practice.** "David—the alleged author of the Psalms—is,
  in Islamic tra-dition, gifted with unusual musical powers which manifest them-selves in Dard in his
  fondness of music and the constant visits of masters of music to his house" (p. 82, folio 80).
  **"the constant visits of masters of music to his house" is the chunk's one statement of samaʿ as
  an institution.** It is anticipated in the investiture text itself: "and God softened the heart of
  the unfeeling before him and sent to him people of melodies by virtue of the Davidic sanctity"
  (p. 80, folio 78). **No majlis, no schedule, no performers named and no payment** — all
  **(not stated)**.
- **Two named Naqshbandi practices, with the order named and the count of its principles given.** "In
  true Naqshbandi fashion (which is a development of earlier mystical theories) Dard tried to practice
  safar dar watan, 'journey in one's home,' one of the eight principles on the Path" (p. 89,
  folio 87); and "another important aspect of Naqshbandi training, the khalwat dar anjuman, 'seclusion
  in the multitude,'" (p. 90, folio 88). Dard's own gloss on the first: "it means, first, that the
  wayfarer leaves his lower and bad qualities, reaches works of obedience, and acquires praise-worthy
  qualities in his interior journey. This results in the mys-tic's return to God and his constant
  raising through the various ranks of existence, and eventually leads him to the place of Uni-ty,
  where his true homeland is" (p. 89, folio 87, citing "(K 468)").
- **The mujaddidi school named, and its standing claim reported.** "members of the mujaddidi school
  often claimed, and still claim, that they have passed the delusive state of wahdat al-wujid[OCR?]
  [waḥdat al-wujūd] and have reached the much superior state of wahdat ash-shuhud" (p. 84, folio 82).
  **Schimmel writes this in the present tense — a claim still live at the time of writing.**
- **The order Dard's descendants supported, named as a movement with leaders.** "the followers of the
  tariga Muhammadtyya[OCR?] [ṭarīqa Muḥammadiyya] under Ahmad of Bareilly and Ismail Shahid in their
  struggle against the Sikh" (p. 93, folio 91). The same order is earlier "the larger chain which he
  considers typical of the tariga Muham-madtyya[OCR?]" (p. 83, folio 81), and its hallmark attribute
  is named: "Muhammad, Exis-tence, i.e., the most comprehensive attribute (which is the hall-mark of
  the tariga Muhammadtyya[OCR?], as Nasir held)" (p. 83, folio 81).
- **Dard's refusal of the standard institutional labels.** "his repeated claims that he is neither a
  Sufi nor a molla, but simply a 'pure Muhammadan'" (p. 84, folio 82). **He declines both the shrine
  vocabulary and the ʿulama vocabulary.**
- **And his attack on the shrine shaikh as a tradesman.** The 99 names give him "the strength to speak
  whatever is in his heart, so 'that no shop-keeper-shaikh could explain the reality with this
  correctness, not any contemptible ecstatic could utter words of such truthful-ness'" (p. 87,
  folio 85). **"shop-keeper-shaikh" is the chunk's one economic idiom for religious authority, and it
  is pejorative.**
- **Mass initiation of kin, as a single transaction.** "I took all my children and the family of the
  Qibla of Both Worlds under my protection and made them enter the rank of a sincere Muhammadan, and
  made them pass before the eyes of his Excellency and this High Person accepted them with the view of
  grace, and I brought all my family and children and the relatives of the Qibla of Both Worlds
  according to their different ranks and took them till resurrection un-der his protection" (p. 94,
  folio 92, citing "(K 418)"). **"according to their different ranks" — the family is graded, and the
  grant runs "till resurrection".**
- **Disciples counted as family — fictive kinship as recruitment.** "at present, the author asserts, a
  number of friends and dis-ciples who have studied his and his father's books have come, and he has
  brought them into the Divine presence and considered them his own family according to the prophetic
  tradition 'Who walks on my way these are my family'" (p. 94, folio 92). **The qualification is
  textual — they "have studied his and his father's books" — so book-reading is the entry
  condition.** **No number is given** — **(not stated)**.
- **The lineage is claimed as sayyid descent, and used to extend a liturgical benefit.** "since lové
  of the family of the Prophet is a duty for every believer, it includes his own family (being true
  sayyids) too—every blessing uttered for Muhammad and his family works also upon his family" (p. 92,
  folio 90). **A claim of descent converted into a claim on every believer's prayers.**
- **Daily tomb attendance as a year-long discipline, with a named hour-frame.** "for about a year, used
  to spend all day, from before the morning prayer till after the night prayer, at his father's tomb,
  without taking with him any friend or child" (p. 88, folio 86). **Dawn-to-night, alone, for a year —
  and the chunk says these visits "were the only interruption of his daily routine" (p. 89,
  folio 87).**
- **Delhi's famine of 1782, with a mortality estimate.** "The relevant sentence may have been written
  about 1196/1782 when the whole of Delhi territory was afflicted by a famine which 'swept away
  something like a third to a half of the rural population'" (p. 88, folio 86). **A dual-calendar date
  and a one-third-to-one-half rural mortality figure — the only quantity of any kind in this chunk.
  The quotation's source is not printed on the page** — **(not stated)** — see Doubts.
- **The shaikh as refuge in disorder, and the limit of it.** "it seems that he, too, was troubled by
  the unlucky inhabitants of the town who sought shelter and help with him" (p. 88, folio 86); and
  "Late in his work (D 270) he once more speaks of the destructions and how people sought shelter"
  (p. 88, folio 86). His remedy is textual, not material: "What behoves them is to follow the path of
  God and the Muhammadan path, so that they may pluck the roses of inner blessings from the rosegarden
  of their company and listen to the lamentation of the Nightingale and understand his books" (p. 88,
  folio 86). Schimmel's verdict: "To realize this ideal was probably difficult for the poverty
  stricken, hungry and helpless inhabitants of Delhi!" (p. 88, folio 86).
- **What he prayed against, itemised — a list of the risks a shrine town faced.** "He prays that God
  may preserve the unhappy population and not allow foreign armies to enter the town, and keep the
  imhabitants[OCR?] [inhabitants] free from affliction and pillage and from difficulties in earning
  their lives" (p. 88, folio 86). **Invasion, pillage and loss of livelihood, in that order.**
- **Dhikr of the Divine Names as a named practice with a stated purpose.** "The dhtkr[OCR?] [dhikr] of
  the Divine Names, mentioned NA II 108, meant for Dard to 'acquire the Divine names' (K 217), as it
  was said takhallagu bt-akhlag Allah[OCR?] [takhallaqū bi-akhlāq Allāh], Acquire the qualities of
  God" (p. 87, folio 85, footnote 16).
- **Limb-dissolution during dhikr, reported as a repeated Indian phenomenon.** "A num-ber of Muslim
  saints in India had the ability of dissolving their limbs so that spectators sometimes were horrified
  by this sight" (p. 83, folio 81). **"spectators" implies an audience at the practice; none is
  described** — **(not stated)**.
- **Marriage doctrine in the father's teaching, including a wife's prostration.** "Muhammad Nasir,
  too, held that the wife should regard her husband as the representative of the Lord, according to the
  Prophetic tradition: 'If it were permis-sable to prostrate before anyone but God, I would say that
  the wives should prostrate before their husbands'" (p. 91, folio 89, citing "(NA I 578)"). And a
  wedding-night allegory: "the virgin recognizes, in the moment of consummation of mar-riage, in her
  husband the qualities of Tremendous Majesty (jalal) instead of those of Mercy (jamal) to which she
  was used before" (p. 91, folio 89, citing "(NA I 560)").
- **Women admitted to the Path, in the father's teaching.** "his father had spoken with high regard of
  the pious and God-seeking women who entered the Muhammadan Path and will gain their reward already
  in this world" (p. 91, folio 89, citing "(NA II 344)"); "What shall I say about the pious women? For
  some of them have progressed so far that they have surpassed many men in learning and mystical
  knowledge and love and charity ..."; "He was sure that they, too, will be blessed with the vision of
  God in the other world (NA I 832), an idea not generally accepted by Muslim theologians" (p. 91,
  folio 89). **No woman disciple is named** — **(not stated)** — though three women of the family
  are named at p. 93, folio 91.
- **Dress as a marker of inward state, in a sermon to men.** "Once he ad-monishes men in a longish
  sermon (K 523) not to overdress, 'for outward decoration is a sign of inward depravity'" (p. 90,
  folio 88).
- **Composition dated by the author's age, twice, with no calendar year attached.** "he was in his
  late fifties when he began to compose the Néla[OCR?] [Nāla]" (p. 94, folio 92); and "These thoughts
  moved him, in his 62nd year, when he began to write down Sham' ~ mahfil[OCR?] [Shamʿ-i maḥfil]; for
  at this age there is no time for long hope (tu/-i amal)[OCR?] [ṭūl-i amal]" (p. 95, folio 93, citing
  "(Intr. cf. P 35)"). **The chunk gives no birth year and no death year for Dard** — **(not
  stated)** — so neither work can be dated from this chunk alone.
- **The books that structure the whole account, as the chunk names them.** "Dard's spiritual diaries,
  as we may call his Four Risdlas[OCR?] [Risālas]" (p. 87, folio 85); "the 'I/m ul-kitab"[OCR?]
  [ʿIlm ul-kitāb] (p. 91, folio 89); the "Néla"[OCR?] [Nāla] and "Sham' ~ mahfil"[OCR?]
  [Shamʿ-i maḥfil] (pp. 94–95, folios 92–93); the father's "Nala-yi 'Andalib" (p. 87, folio 85); and
  Firaq's "Matkhana-y1[OCR?] [Maikhāna-yi] Dard", "the only comprehensive description of Dard and his
  family" (p. 93, folio 91).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

All of these are reported by Schimmel as material from Dard's own writings or from "the family
tradition"; the attributions below are hers.

- **The father's knife at his son's throat.** "his father put the knife on his throat in one of the
  states of being drawn near to God in the beginning of his way, with the intention of slaughtering
  him for God, and God accepted him well, and thus he is really one who has been slaughtered by God and
  remained safe in the outward form" (p. 81, folio 79). **Schimmel does not accept a reading and
  offers four:** "Did his father, in a state of mystical rapture, act like Abraham and was willing to
  sacrifice his most beloved son at the beginning of the Muham-madan Path? Does it refer to the
  sufferings which young Dard experienced when his father had his first visions? Or is this a kind of
  initiation-act which is reminiscent of the initiation in many a primitive society, and especially in
  Shamanism? Or is it only the experience Dard had while his father was graced with the vision of
  Imam Hasan? In any case, something unusual must have hap-pened, when the boy was still very young"
  (p. 82, folio 80). **Attributed to Dard's own text (the "Ishmaelian sanctity" passage), suppressed
  by Firaq.**
- **The father's good tiding over the living dead man.** "as his father gave him the good tiding 'Who
  has not seen a dead person wandering around on earth may look at this son of mine who lives through
  me and through me he moves'" (p. 81, folio 79).
- **The nightly dissolution and reassembly of his body.** "he breathes through Israfilian breath and
  loosens the parts of his body and collects them every time, and he sleeps and awakes every day and
  is drawn toward death every time by Azrailian attraction" (p. 81, folio 79). Schimmel: "the passage
  that his limbs were loosened and put together every day reminds us, once more, of shamanistic
  practices" (p. 83, folio 81).
- **Knowledge without books and eating without causes.** "he knows through Gabrielic help without
  mediation of sciences written in books, and he eats with Michaelian help without outward secondary
  causes" (p. 81, folio 79).
- **Pir Chhata's limbs proclaiming God's name.** "'Somebody saw (during the dhikr) that the limbs of
  this love-slain man fell apart, and each of them with a separate tongue proclaimed the name of God'"
  (p. 83, folio 81, footnote 15, attributed to "M. ASzam[OCR?] [Aʿzam] Thattawi, Tuhfat at-tahirin,
  p. 159").
- **God's direct address, and Dard's investiture in a vision.** "He spoke to me: O Vicegerent of God
  and o Sign (aya) of God, verily I have witnessed your state of servantship ('ubédiyya)[OCR?]
  [ʿubūdiyya]; now you wit-ness my Divinity, for you are My servant and he whom I have ac-cepted and
  whom My prophet has accepted" (p. 84, folio 82). And: "O 'Abdallah, o who knows God ('arif
  billdh)[OCR?] [ʿārif bi-llāh], verily I have made you a place of manifestation comprising all My
  manifesta-tions, so bring now My signs to all my creatures. And I have called you the Utmost Sum
  Total (fam' )[OCR?] [jamʿ] and the Muhammadan Sum Total, and who obeys you obeys God and the
  Prophet" (p. 84, folio 82, cited "(cf. P. 8)"[OCR?]). Schimmel's framing: "After this report of his
  vision in which he was invested as the true successor of the Prophet" (p. 85, folio 83).
- **The intercession chain that carried his whole family to God.** "he spon-sored them under his fold
  and brought them in the presence of the most holy Prophet, and he took all of them in the fold of his
  inter-cession and brought them in the palace of Divine proximity and wanted intercession for them.
  From there it was (divinely) directed: 'They have been honored by Sincere Muhammadanism and belong
  with their faith and their confidence into the group of Our servants who have been brought near and
  upon We have mercy and forgive them ...'" (p. 94, folio 92, citing "(K 418)").
- **Jinns among the disciples.** "the family tradition counts even some jinns among Mir Dard's
  disciples, who had been con-verted by his son Alam in far-away Bengal and then stayed in his house"
  (p. 94, folio 92, citing "(F 143)"). **Attributed explicitly to "the family tradition" and
  introduced with "It may be mentioned at random".**
- **Ali as the dot beneath the letter.** "who is, according to his own statement, the dot beneath the
  letter &[OCR?] of the basmala": "All mysteries of the Divine speech are in the Glorious Quran, and
  all the mysteries of the Quran are in the Strat al-Fdatiha[OCR?] [Sūrat al-Fātiḥa], and all
  mysteries of the Fdtzha[OCR?] [Fātiḥa] are in the formula Bismillah, and all the mysteries of the
  Basmala are in the b of Bismillah, and all the mysteries of the b are in the dot beneath the
  6[OCR?] [b], and I am this dot beneath the b" (p. 92, folio 90, citing "(K 75)"). **Attributed by
  Dard to Ali's own statement.**
- **The locusts with no true leader.** "his strange Persian quatrain about the swarms of thronging
  locusts which, however, disappear because they have no true leader" (p. 88, folio 86, citing
  "(P 94)"). **Schimmel offers the dating as a question, not a fact: "May we perhaps date to this
  period …?"**

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **Schimmel's central claim in this chunk: Dard's doctrine of man drives him to language he would
  otherwise reject.** "Such claims lead him in his poetry sometimes to expressions which we would
  expect rather from faithful followers of the theory of Unity of Being" (p. 79, folio 77), and he
  "reaches what seems like unihibited[OCR?] [uninhibited] subjectivism" (p. 79, folio 77) — this
  despite "his aversion to the dangerous wujudzyya[OCR?] [wujūdiyya]-theories" (p. 84, folio 82).
  **She is arguing an inconsistency between his polemics and his verse.**
- **She reads the vicegerency claim as a dynastic argument.** Dard "is the first and true vicegerent
  of his father" and his title "First of the Muhammadans points both to the proximity in time and in
  space" (p. 80, folio 78); he was "made … sit on the throne of vicegerency of his father, as heritage
  and in realization" (p. 81, folio 79). **Schimmel notes the code-switch that marks the claim's
  weight: "Then he writes in Arabic, as always when he conveys his deepest secrets" (p. 80,
  folio 78), and again "He begins in Arabic, for God talked to him, and such an audition is possible
  only in the language of the Quran" (p. 84, folio 82).**
- **She reads the prophet-list as a psychology.** "This description contains a large number of
  allusions to the qualities of the Quranic prophets who stand for the various psychological qualities
  of man, which are perfectly integrated in the Prophet Muhammad" (p. 82, folio 80), and each is
  mapped: David to music and to softening iron and hearts, Solomon to rule, Abraham to "the
  sublimation of the fire of wrath and lower qualities", Joseph to dream interpretation (p. 82,
  folio 80).
- **She compares Dard's list with his father's and finds them diverge.** Muhammad Nasir "though not
  completely logical,—connects seven or nine attributes of God with the great prophets as it is not
  rare in later Sufism: Adam manifests the Divine Will or … Creativity; Jesus, Life; Abraham,
  Knowledge; Noah, Power; David, Hearing; Jacob, Seeing; and Muhammad, Exis-tence"; and "Dard's list
  is more comprehensive and varied, and only in a few cases con-forms to the list of his father"
  (p. 83, folio 81, citing "(NA I 243, 259 and II 652 f.)").
- **She proposes shamanism as a comparative frame, twice, and hedges it both times** — "reminds us,
  once more, of shamanistic practices" (p. 83, folio 81) and "a kind of initiation-act which is
  reminiscent of the initiation in many a primitive society, and especially in Shamanism?" (p. 82,
  folio 80), the second as a question.
- **She admits she cannot place the throne and crown.** "it is diffi-cult to discern where the throne
  and the crown belong which the mystic had received from the Lord and which made him the real
  representative of Him and His prophet on earth—although here influences of the gayyumiyya[OCR?]
  [qayyūmiyya] doctrine seem to be close at hand" (p. 83, folio 81). **An explicit
  unresolved point in her own argument.**
- **She reads the names as psychology and as symptom.** "Dard's 99 names—as well as his attributive
  names—are psycho-logically highly interesting. They are put together apparently partly according to
  reasons of harmony, for many of them rhyme. Arabic and Persian expressions alternate without fixed
  rules, and one is surprised to find a large number of names which are used, otherwise, only for God
  himself" (p. 86, folio 84). And: "In Dard's case they may also point to the problems that puzzled
  him for his childhood to his last day: the search for identity" (p. 87, folio 85). She also gives
  the practice a lineage: "The idea of surrounding a human being with a large number of honorific
  names is definitely inherited from his father who has, in the Nala-yi 'Andalib, invented long chains
  of titles for his heroes … and heroines … and even negative attributes for the evil forces" (p. 87,
  folio 85).
- **She treats Firaq's omission as evidence about hagiography, not about Dard.** "it seems typical of
  later hagiography that Firaq … skips this detail of the 'Ismailian sanctity' from his integral
  translation of the pas-sage" (p. 82, folio 80). **A named case of a family biographer censoring a
  founder's own text.**
- **She argues Dard's misogynist paragraph is formulaic rather than felt, and reasons from the
  evidence.** "Was this traditional Islamic view the real opinion of a man who spoke with such deep
  love of his venerable, pious mother?" (p. 91, folio 89); "his prejorative[OCR?] [pejorative] remark
  about women was, like some other paragraphs of the 'I/m ul-kitab[OCR?] [ʿIlm ul-kitāb], probably
  merely theoretical" (p. 91, folio 89), against which she sets his own later outburst on loving wife
  and children (p. 91, folio 89).
- **She reads a hadith in the father's work as Hinduising.** "a hadith which reminds us rather of Hindu
  ideals of marriage where the husband is, in fact, the representative of Divine power" (p. 91,
  folio 89). **The chunk's only Hindu–Muslim comparison, and it is Schimmel's own inference, not a
  reported practice.**
- **She holds Dard's family feeling to be untypical of Sufism, and names the contrast order.** "If one
  remembers the harsh words of some early Sufis against the fetters of family life, the love of
  family and children, or reads how leaders of the Chishtiyya order in India were notorious for the
  disinterest in their families one understands that Mir Dard's attitude is by no means typical of a
  Sufi" (p. 92, folio 90).
- **She reads Dard's political silence as a characteristic, not an accident.** "Dard's spiritual
  diaries … only rarely speak of the afflictions which his hometown had to under-go almost every year"
  (p. 87, folio 85); "As little as Mir Dard speaks about the terrible events in the political life in
  Delhi during the third quarter of the 18th cen-tury, as silent is he about other facts of daily
  life" (p. 90, folio 88); "In general, the afflic-tions that came over Dehli did not disturb him too
  much; they rather proved to him that real life is found only in the Divine Presence" (p. 89,
  folio 87).
- **She dates a poetic term by political history.** "gazd-1 ftrang[OCR?], 'European prison,' an
  expression that had become popular after the first attacks by the Portuguese on Indian harbors in
  the mid-16th century. Nasir Andalib had fre-quently used this word and since the Europeans were
  connected, in some strange way of poetical thinking, with colorful paintings, Dard connects the
  multicolored show of this world with a Euro-pean prison into which the soul-birds have fallen"
  (p. 90, folio 88, citing "(S 61)").
- **She reads old-age imagery through a poetic convention.** "This image can be better understood when
  one thinks of the general poetical idea that old age resembles the morning. As for the candle, it
  has to be ex-tinguished at dawn" (p. 95, folio 93); "the longer the more he sees in the whiteness of
  dawn a reminder of the shroud" (p. 95, folio 93).
- **She marks two of her own datings as conjectural.** "The relevant sentence may have been written
  about 1196/1782" (p. 88, folio 86) and "May we perhaps date to this period his strange Persian
  quatrain …?" (p. 88, folio 86). **Both are her hypotheses, not the book's assertions.**

## Citable passages

- On hereditary spiritual office: "a rotten and unworthy son never comes to such an excellent father"
  (p. 93, folio 91).
- On the shrine shaikh as tradesman: "no shop-keeper-shaikh could explain the reality with this
  correctness" (p. 87, folio 85).
- On the khanqah as a whole world: "he constantly lived in the batt al-ma' mur[OCR?]
  [bayt al-maʿmūr] of his Pir, i.e., in his paternal dargah" (p. 89, folio 87) — with the maxim he
  quotes for it, "Who is in one place is everywhere, and who is everywhere is nowhere" (p. 89,
  folio 87).
- On music at the shaikh's house: "his fondness of music and the constant visits of masters of music
  to his house" (p. 82, folio 80).
- On the 1782 famine: a famine which "swept away something like a third to a half of the rural
  population" (p. 88, folio 86).

**Archive-mapping doubt: "Pir Chhata in Sind".** The footnote at p. 83 (folio 81) names "Pir Chhata

`shrine-of-pir-chhatal-shah-noorani` ("Shrine of Pir Chhatal Shah Noorani", principal figure "Pir

Magsi District, Balochistan. The chunk gives no dates, no shrine and no sub-provincial location, so
there is nothing further to test the identification against. **Left under "Other saints, sites and
events" pending a human decision.** No second archive row competes for the name.

**Interim place-name convention: nothing to apply it to here.** The chunk contains no bare place name
that would touch an archive row under either ruling. Its place names are Delhi, Bengal, Istanbul,
Najaf, Aden, Bareilly and "Sind" as a region; none corresponds to a row, and the one Pakistan-side
saint-bearing mention is handled in the paragraph above. **No reversibility line is needed for this
chunk.** (Noted explicitly so the consolidator does not read the absence as an oversight.)

**The father's six names, and whether the chunk itself identifies them.** The chunk supplies the
links for four of them — "the list of his father" immediately after quoting "Muhammad Nasir" (p. 83,
folio 81); "his father Andalib" (p. 92, folio 90); "Nasir Andalib" (p. 90, folio 88); "the venerable
Nightingale" whose lamentation generates "Dard" (p. 93, folio 91) — so Muhammad Nasir = Nasir Andalib
= Andalib = the Nightingale is stated, not inferred. **But the chunk never glosses "the 'Qibla of the
Worlds'" (p. 87, folio 85) or "the Qibla of Both Worlds" (p. 94, folio 92)**, and I have not assumed
either refers to the father, though the Delhi "burial garden" at p. 87 (folio 85) sits beside the
father's tomb material at pp. 88–89 (folios 86–87). **A human should settle whether these two
epithets denote the father, the Prophet, or two different figures** — the reading changes what the
"burial garden … in Delhi" is.

**Dard's sons: an unresolved tangle across two pages.** At p. 92 (folio 90) "his own son" is named
"Mir Muhammad Diya un-Nasir". At p. 93 (folio 91) the three surviving children are "Alam, Barati
Begum, and Zinat un-Nisa Begum", and "Mir Alam" becomes convent head. **The chunk never says whether
"Alam" and "Mir Muhammad Diya un-Nasir" are the same man.** Two further antecedents in the same
sentence are ambiguous: "His son, Mir Muhammad Bakhsh died during his father's lifetime" — by word
order "his" is Mir Alam's, but this is not stated; and "a daughter, Amani Begum" — whose daughter is
not stated. **Do not build a genealogy from this chunk without checking the printed page.**

**"Ishmaelian" vs "'Ismailian'" — the same episode spelled two ways.** The investiture passage says
"he gained the Ishmaelian sanctity" (p. 81, folio 79); Schimmel's commentary two pages later calls the
same passage "this detail of the 'Ismailian sanctity'" (p. 82, folio 80). **Both forms are as printed
and neither is an OCR artifact I can detect.** It may be the book's own inconsistency; a human should
confirm.

**The famine mortality quotation has no visible source.** "swept away something like a third to a half
of the rural population" (p. 88, folio 86) is in quotation marks but no footnote or citation for it
appears in the chunk text — **(not stated)**. Since this is the only quantity in the chunk and the
kind of figure the project will want to cite, **the source must be found before it is used.**

**Dard has no dates anywhere in this chunk.** No birth year, no death year, no ʿurs, no hijri date for
him personally. His age is given twice ("in his late fifties", "in his 62nd year", pp. 94–95,
folios 92–93) and those are the only anchors. **Nothing about his dates may be supplied from outside
this chunk.**

**No archive material, stated once more for the consolidator.** pp. 79–95 contribute nothing to any of
the 169 rows. Their value to the project is cross-cutting: silsila (Naqshbandi, mujaddidi, ṭarīqa
