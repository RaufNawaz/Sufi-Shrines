# schimmel_pain_and_grace — chunk 003 — pp. 42–59

**This chunk is the biographical core of Part I.** It runs inside the chapter section headed
`DARD'S LIFE AND TEACHING` (the running head on every recto, e.g. p. 43, folio 41) and, at p. 55
(folio 53), opens a new section headed `MUSIC AND LITERATURE`. Its subject throughout is **Khwaja
Mir Dard of Delhi** and his father **Muhammad Nasir Andalib**: childhood, education, the founding
and naming of the **tariqa Muhammadiyya**, the family compound in Delhi, the composition of the
*Nala-yi Andalib*, Andalib's death and tomb, Dard's succession as head of the path, and his
contested love of music and *samaʿ*.

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

### Khwaja Mir Dard of Delhi

- **His earliest religious interest, and Schimmel's warning about the evidence.** "Mir Dard claims
  that he was interested in religious problems already in his early childhood"; "He may have been
  three or four years old when he was puzzled, as many children of this age, by the question of his
  own existence"; but he "sees here already the specific religious interest which was to
  characterize him throughout his life" in "a back-projection which is typical of old age" (p. 42,
  folio 40). "Firaq, as usual, embellishes the story with touching details and dates it back to
  Dard's second year (F 116 f.)" (p. 42, folio 40). **No birth year or death year for Mir Dard is
  printed anywhere in this chunk** — **(not stated)**.
- **The household that dealt with the crying child, named by relationship only.** "his 'noble
  mother,' his grandmother, and his paternal aunt gathered and discussed the matter"; "tenderly they
  blew prayers and four gud[OCR?] [qul] (Sura 112) upon him" (p. 42, folio 40). His "nurse and the
  other servants became perplexed and worried and tried all kinds of remedies on him" (p. 42,
  folio 40). **None of these women is named** — **(not stated)**.
- **His father is titled, not named, in the childhood account** — "the blessed ears of the Venerable
  Qibla of the Worlds (i.e., his father)" (p. 42, folio 40); the boy "rubbed my head and eyes on his
  necessarily fortunate feet" and asked "who I am and why have I been born and why do I live and
  whence, and where, and who is my creator" (pp. 42–43, folios 40–41). "His father then slowly began
  to introduce him into mystical theology" (p. 43, folio 41), and the question "'Who am I?'" "never
  ceased occupying his mind" to "his last verses, his last sighs (see. p. 97 f.)" (p. 43, folio 41).
- **His formal education, in his own words.** "In the middle of my youth I studied the formal
  sciences such as fundamentals of faith, and logics, and elements of law, and Sufism, and others as
  much as necessary, but I did not rub my foot in this path like the Mollas who cling to the husk,
  or the Sufis who do not understand and not see ... (D 216)" (p. 43, folio 41). Schimmel adds that
  his studies included "Rumi's Mathnawi" (p. 43, folio 41).
- **He was not a Quran-memoriser.** "He did not become a hafiz—but: God threw upon my heart clear
  signs (dyat[OCR?] [āyāt]) though I do not know the Quran by heart (K 65)" (p. 43, folio 41).
- **His father's eight-day seclusion, and the revelation that founded the path.** "It must have been
  during this period that his father was granted a revelation which was destined to reform Muslim
  mysticism," told in "the 'Jim ul-kitab"[OCR?] [ʿIlm ul-kitāb] (85): the father "remained silent
  for seven days and nights and never turned to this world of humanity"; "We young boys attended the
  place at the time of ritual prayer so that he might lead us—but if I should write what events
  happened to us servants in those days when every time the door of the cell remained closed …"
  (p. 43, folio 41). "But when God eventually on the eighth day sent Muhammad Nasir back to the
  world of humanity" he raised his son from the ground and "kissed my forehead" (p. 44, folio 42).
- **He was the order's first murid.** "Thus he was the first to offer allegiance to this new path
  which Muhammad Nasir gave to the world" (p. 44, folio 42).
- **The naming of the order, in the father's reported words.** "If my intention had been such, I
  would name the tariga[OCR?] [tarīqa] after my own name, as the others do. But all of us are
  children, lost in the sea of identity and drowned in one ocean. Our name is the name of Muhammad,
  and our sign is the sign of Muhammad … One must call this order the tariga Muhammadiyya, the
  Muhammadan path. It is exactly the path of Muhammad, and we have not added anything to it. Our
  conduct is the conduct of the Prophet, and our way the Muhammadan way." (p. 44, folio 42)
  **A named order that refuses an eponym — the point is made explicitly against "the others".**
- **His own rank in the order, written "35 years later".** "the grace and acceptance of my venerable
  master Andalib has made this nothing more than all and made him the First of the Muhammadans, and
  the top-rose of the bouquet … (D 254)" (p. 44, folio 42); he calls himself "less than a straw in
  his own view" and "a weak grass in the rosegarden of the Muhammadan path" (p. 44, folio 42).
- **Two honorifics for his position.** "Dard became, thus, 'the threshold of the Muhammadan court,'
  or, with an expression usually used for the relation between the Prophet and Ali ibn Abi Talib,
  'the door of the town of knowledge' (K 587)" (pp. 44–45, folios 42–43).
- **Schimmel's dating of the founding event, and what she accepts from Firaq.** "Since the tariga
  Muhammadtyya[OCR?] [Muhammadiyya] is the focal point in Muhammad Nasir's main work, which was
  written in 1740, the event must have taken place sometime in the mid-thirties of the 18th
  century. Firaq is certainly right when he gives Dard's age at this event as being 13 years"
  (p. 45, folio 43). **Because no birth year is printed in this chunk, "13 years" cannot be
  converted to a date here.**
- **His first book, with age, genre and calendar setting.** "The first fruit of Dard's new interest
  in religion is his booklet Asrar as-salat, 'Mysteries of Ritual Prayer,' which he composed at the
  age of 15 during his seclusion in the month of Ramadan" (p. 45, folio 43).
- **His marriage, with both ages and an explicit gap in the sources.** "Shortly afterwards, at the
  age of about sixteen, Khwaja Mir Dard got married to a young lady of twelve (whose name, however,
  is not given by the sources; F 187), for, as his father says, 'a married dervish will be a hundred
  times superior to a single dervish' (NA I 895)" (p. 45, folio 43). **The wife is unnamed** —
  **(not stated)** — and the book says so itself.
- **The disputed army service, and the one self-description of his worldly phase.** "A number of
  later biographies mention that Mir Dard served temporarily in the Imperial army, but that may be
  due to a confusion of his and his father's biographies, for neither he himself, nor Firaq specify
  his occupations" (p. 45, folio 43). His own account: "a short while I was in the dress of
  wordly-mindedness and in the position of a man of the world; and when I began some work I looked
  up the lucky and unlucky days … And whatever we possessed—jdgir[OCR?] [jāgīr] and high office—I
  considered a royal fortune. I tried to recommend myself to the great amirs of the Emperor and
  those who were close to the ruler … (K 473)" (p. 45, folio 43). **"jdgir"[OCR?] and "high office"
  are the only statement of the family's material position in the chunk.**
- **Taking the dervish habit, dated two ways.** "youth was still there when I drew.off my hand from
  this passing and unstable world, and at the age of 29, I put on the garment of the dervishes …
  (N 289)" (p. 49, folio 47); "That was in 1750" (p. 50, folio 48). Earlier he "'made run the mount
  of negligence in the arena of passion and lust'" (p. 49, folio 47).
- **Service in the khanqah before formal initiation, in his own list of duties** — "even before
  officially joining his father's tariga he devoted himself to the service of the convent, as he
  tells later (N 49)": "I served the brethren of the path and exercised the path and the writing
  down of a book and tried to be useful to people and to sweep the court of the 'Qibla of both
  worlds' and in the instruction and education of brethren and children and completion of assemblies
  and invitations of people and showing the people the way as far as I could" (p. 50, folio 48).
- **His poetic training and circle.** "He studied poetry with Khan-i Arzu, the 'Aristotle of Urdu
  literature' in the early 18th century" (p. 50, folio 48). He joined his father's monthly
  "mushd'ira"[OCR?] [mushāʿira] "(meeting of poets)" (p. 51, folio 49) and "became friendly with the
  future master of Urdu love-poetry, Mir Taqi Mir, who was a few years junior to him" (p. 51,
  folio 49).
- **The waridat: a dated corpus with a number.** "It happened during these years, between Dard's
  entering the order and his father's death (1749 to 1758) that he was granted a number of
  inspirations. They were Persian verses and short sentences of wisdom in concise rhymed prose
  called waridat, 'things coming.'" "Mir Dard had the opportunity to read these wartdat[OCR?]
  [wāridāt]—111 in number—to his father who very much appreciated them." (p. 51, folio 49)
  **The bracketed span "(1749 to 1758)" conflicts with two other dates in the chunk — see Doubts.**
- **The letter-pun on his own filiation.** "Praised be God who helped (nasara) his servant and made
  him now the dot of the inside of the n, as he was previously the dot beneath the 6[OCR?] [b]" and
  "Alif is the divine connection, and b the blessing (baraka) of Muhammad, and n the help of Nasir.
  When they are connected in special equality and combined in a particular way, then the form of
  tbn[OCR?] [ibn] 'son' results from the gathering of those lofty roots; (#67)[OCR?], in which there
  is contained what is contained, and who is a secret of his father, to show openly the Muhammadan
  Path (K 60)" (p. 52, folio 50). Schimmel: "Dard claims here, as elsewhere, that he stands in the
  same relation to his father Nasir as Ali, the 'door of the town of knowledge,' stood in relation
  to the Prophet" (p. 52, folio 50).
- **The relationship continues after the father's death, stated as doctrine.** "Even though he has
  disappeared from the outward eye, yet in the inward actions there is … not a single hair's breadth
  interruption in the relation with his presence … 'Peace be upon you, o people of the tombs, and if
  God will we shall reach you' … 'Verily the saints of God do not die' … (K 636)" (pp. 52–53,
  folios 50–51). His "lamentation about the loss of his parents (K 556)" turns him back "to Him who
  is kinder than seventy loving mothers and more merciful than the affectionate father" (p. 53,
  folio 51).
- **The audition of three promises, and its dating.** "A few years after his father's death he was
  consoled by an audition as he tells in one of the last paragraphs of his last book (S 329), about
  twenty years after the actual event," the words "'translated' into Persian though otherwise the
  auditions are given in Arabic" (p. 53, folio 51). See `## Legends and miracles (karamat)`.
- **His age when he wrote those lines, given as a figure** — "he had reached the age of his father
  (66 lunar years)" (p. 54, folio 52). **Neither man's birth year is given here** — **(not
  stated)**.
- **He succeeded his father as head of the order.** "After Muhammad Nasir's death, Mir Dard became
  his successor as the leader of the Muhammadan Path" (p. 55, folio 53). "He 'supported himself on
  the trust in God and did not lift his foot from his place,' as his friend Mir Taqi Mir says. He
  never left Delhi in spite of all misfortunes that came over the city" (p. 55, folio 53).
- **His silsila is named explicitly, and so is his one departure from it.** "In his mystical
  instructions, he strictly followed the rules of the Naqshbandi mujaddidi school … Likewise, his
  poetry is filled with classical expressions of Naqshbandi mysticism. In one point, however, he did
  not follow the tradition of the oruer[OCR?] [order], and that is his love of music; music and
  samaʿ are prohibited as religious expressions by the Mujaddidi school." (p. 55, folio 53)
- **His rare tract on music.** "his booklet Hurmat al-ghind[OCR?] [Hurmat al-ghinā], 'Veneration of
  Music,' printed in 1843, is extremely rare" (p. 55, folio 53). **The printed English gloss is
  Schimmel's and is recorded as printed — see Doubts.**
- **His defence of the musical sessions, in his own words.** "My samaʿ is from God, and God is every
  time witness that the singers come from themselves and sing whenever they want; not that I would
  call them and would consider it like worship to listen to them, as others do; but I do not refuse
  such an act. However, I do not do it myself, and my creed is that of the masters (ie., the
  Naqshbandiyya) … for I have not given a fatwd[OCR?] [fatwā] that this should be licite, and I have
  not built the mystical path upon samaʿ … (N 35)" (pp. 55–56, folios 53–54). A second, longer
  defence classes music with the exact sciences: "Verily the science of music is most subtle and
  belongs among the mathematical sciences, and influences the soul … But I do not consider it as bad
  as the husk-mollas suppose (it to be) … (A 77)" (p. 56, folio 54).
- **The attacks on him are dated.** "These attacks must have been particularly strong in the early
  1770's, for Dard had noted down a few days earlier another sentence about those colleagues who
  weave like spiders the web of reproach and blame because of his inclination to music, although his
  creed is perfectly sound (N 25)" (p. 56, folio 54). "We do not know, however, the source of this
  criticism" (p. 55, folio 53) — **(not stated)**.
- **His practical musicianship, on Firaq's authority.** "he had, as we are told, an amazing voice and
  an enchanting way of singing Indian rags and ragnis[OCR?] [raginis] (F 148), an art, which he
  practiced, however, only in the company of some experts in music" (p. 56, folio 54); he "had
  studied books on classical Indian music (even though he was probably not acquainted with Sanskrit,
  as this author claims, F 147)" (p. 56, folio 54).
- **His musical imagery, itemised by the book.** the "ney, the reed flute" (P 23, P 122); the
  "gdnin"[OCR?] [qānūn] "from whose different tunes the connoisseur may understand his
  magams"[OCR?] [maqāms] — "a clever pun on magam which means both 'musical mode' and 'mystical
  station' (P 82)" (pp. 57–58, folios 55–56); "every parda ('veil,' and 'mode')" and "every tune
  (magdm[OCR?] [maqām])" (S 24, p. 58, folio 56); "rupak tala" / "rupak tal", the seven-beat
  Hindustani cycle whose "first beat that is not accented" (p. 58, folio 56); the world "replete
  with confused sounds like a kettledrum (P 77)" (p. 58, folio 56); the "'painted tanboura' without
  sound" (p. 59, folio 57); "his self-portrait as the bell of the caravan (U 121)" (p. 59,
  folio 57).
- **His Urdu output, quantified.** "the small collection of little more than a thousand Urdu lines";
  "He became the undisputed master of mystical Urdu poetry, and is probably the only poet who has
  achieved a complete blending of mysticism and verse" (p. 59, folio 57).

### Muhammad Nasir Andalib (Dard's father)

- **His death date, in both calendars, and his burial place.** "Muhammad Nasir Andalib died on
  2. Shaʿ ban 1172/2.4.1759 and was buried in the graveyard near Turkoman Gate at a place which the
  Prophet himself had indicated to Mir Dard (F 96), for, as the quatrain on his tombstone says: He
  was the foot-print of the Lord of 'If thou hadst not been' (e.g., Muhammad)." (pp. 51–52,
  folios 49–50) **A tombstone inscription, quoted; the grave site chosen by prophetic indication.**
- **His tomb as Schimmel found it in 1975.** "his father's tomb had no roof or dome so as to be
  exposed to the weather" (p. 54, folio 52); in her own footnote, "I visited the tomb in September
  1975; it is indeed open, and besides Andalib, Mir Dard, his son Alam and his brother Athar are
  buried there; two small :ombs[OCR?] [tombs] in the modest enclosure have no names. The ground is
  clay, and the location is situated ona little hill-that may account for its 'coolness'." (p. 54,
  folio 52, footnote). **Four named burials in one enclosure plus two anonymous graves — the fullest
  description of a burial site in the chunk.**
- **His spiritual guide is named, with a date** — "the death of Muhammad Nasir's spiritual guide,
  Muhammad Zubair, in February 1740" (p. 46, folio 44). **Elsewhere the chunk calls Shah Gulshan
  "Andalib's mystical master" (p. 47, folio 45) and "his father's master" (p. 57, folio 55) — see
  Doubts.**
- **His magnum opus, dated and measured.** "The book which came thus into existence, and comprises
  in its present print two volumes of more than 900 pages folio size each, was completed in
  1153/1741. It was called Nala-yt[OCR?] [Nala-yi] ʿAndalib, 'The Lamentation of the Nightingale,'
  and its chronogram is formed from the words nala-yi ʿandalib gulshan-t mast[OCR?] [gulshan-i
  mā-st], 'The Lamentation of the Nightingale is our Rosegarden' (pun on the name 'Gulshan',
  Andalib's mystical master)" (pp. 46–47, folios 44–45). "(The story itself begins, though, only
  after ca. 600 pages)" (p. 47, folio 45).
- **How the book was composed: an oral recital in Hindi/Urdu, then Persian, with his son as
  scribe.** He "told this story in Hindi language during three nights to them" (p. 46, folio 44);
  "The story which Andalib told to his friends in the language of Delhi, 'Hindi' or 'Urdu,' was
  later on elaborated 'without difficulty in the Persian language.'" (p. 46, folio 44); "And the
  happy and fortunate Khwaja Mir who is my middle son … took paper and pen into his right hand and
  sat besides me and wrote down letter by letter (NA I 3)" (p. 46, folio 44). "At rare occasions
  Bedar, one of Andalib's disciples in poetry served as secretary instead of Dard" (p. 46,
  folio 44). **Dard is identified here as the middle son.**
- **What the book contains, as Schimmel lists it.** "the pious king and the wise merchant, 'Jupiter'
  and 'Sun,' musicians and dancers, the Hindu ruler converted to Islam, rebellion in the Deccan,
  white elephants and hunting parties; but every allegory is meant to explain the 'pure Muhammadan
  Path,' the 'behavior of the Muhammadans' (NAI 813[OCR?])" (p. 47, folio 45); "the various schools
  of Sufism (NA I 789 ff., 882 f.)", "the different madhhabs of Islam or Shiʿ a imamology (NA II
  583)", "the minutest details of ritual purity (Na I 446-49), fasting (NA I 842, II 217)" (p. 47,
  folio 45); the nightingale "emerges as a symbol of the Prophet of Islam (NA II 712)" (p. 47,
  folio 45).
- **His Hindu material, recorded because the chunk marks it as unusual.** "Sometimes Andalib inserts
  Hindi dhoras[OCR?] [dohras?] into the story, and of special interest for the historian of
  religions are his allusions to Hindu philosophy and Hindu customs. He thus discusses in detail
  Yoga practices, which he considers to be a remarkable achievement but they are, of course, much
  inferior to the experiences the pious Muslim can reach through meditation, and by attaching
  himself completely to the Prophet." (p. 47, folio 45)
- **His position on wahdat al-wujud and on Hallaj, set against his son's.** "Dard's antipathy against
  the representatives of the theories of wahdat al-wujud can be understood from his father's attacks
  against this group of Sufis (cf. NA I 622, 797, 808 and more). Muhammad Nasir, however, is not as
  critical of Hallaj's famous word 'I am the absolute Truth' as his son; in fact, he even uses it in
  a charming story of an enamored gazelle who imagines himself to be a human being and calls 'I am a
  man' just as Mansur called 'I am God' (NA I 132)." (p. 47, folio 45)
- **His own rule for reading his allegories.** "If, for instance, somebody calls a brave man in
  praising him a lion, nobody will look for claws and tail in him (NA II 900)", and "love of the
  idol is only a symbol of being caught by the love of the lightful and shapeful manifestation of
  the Lord (NA II 902)" (p. 48, folio 46).
- **The book as a divination instrument.** "Andalib was absolutely certain that his book was
  extremely valuable, and recommended it for tafa ul[OCR?] [tafaʾʾul], 'future-telling' (NA 902);
  his son, even more, stressed the divine quality of the Nala (K 434) which yields marvellous
  predictions of the future" (p. 48, folio 46) — the same being claimed for Dard's own
  "lm ul-kitab"[OCR?] [ʿIlm ul-kitāb] (pp. 48–49, folios 46–47).
- **The fate of the autograph, and the book's use inside the family.** "Firaq recalls from his
  childhood (F 89) that the Nala-yt ʿAndalib, whose original manuscript was destroyed during the
  Mutiny 1857, used to be read to the members of the family: the ladies, all acquainted with
  Persian, told their children the touching stories of the Rose and the Nightingale until they were
  able to understand their deeper meaning." (p. 49, folio 47) **A dated manuscript loss (1857) and a
  domestic, female-led transmission practice.**
- **His second book, and where the manuscript is.** "the Risdla-yt hush-afzd"[OCR?] [Risāla-yi
  hūsh-afzā], "a book on 'mystical chess' which Muhammad Nasir composed to distract some of his
  disciples from chess playing" (p. 49, folio 47); in the footnote, "Andalib's Risdla-yt
  hishafzd[OCR?] is found in the Library of the University of the Punjab, Lahore, written ina rather
  illegible shikasta" (p. 49, folio 47, footnote). **Mir Dard "never mentions" it** (p. 49,
  folio 47).
- **His inventions and his cane, which became a family relic.** "Muhammad Nasir must have invented a
  number of strange things (F 92), like the 'wandering tent,' and the 'bath for everywhere,' and the
  'candle without tears' and similar items. His famous cane, called an-Nasiri, with the word Nasir
  engraved in it in an artistic tughrd[OCR?] [tughrā], was for a long while in the family. Later,
  this peculiar type of tughraé was used by the members of the family for the sake of blessing
  (M 93 f)." (p. 49, folio 47) **An object of baraka transmitted inside the lineage — see
  `## Practices, institutions, economy`.**
- **He was "also fond of music"** (p. 57, folio 55), and this is offered as part of the explanation
  of his son's famous sessions.

### Other named figures

- **Mihrparwar Begum** — "Aurangzeb's daughter-in-law", who at the sack of Delhi "offered shelter to
  Muhammad Nasir and Mir Dard, for she was afraid lest the houses outside the rampart suffer even
  more than the imperial town", and who afterwards "built a nice compound for them" (pp. 45–46,
  folios 43–44). **The chunk's one named patron.**
- **Zinat un-Nisa** — "Aurangzeb's daughter", builder of "The mosque Zinat al-masdjid[OCR?]", "a
  favorite place where mystical poets used to meet" (p. 50, folio 48).
- **Muhammad Zubair** — Muhammad Nasir's "spiritual guide", died "in February 1740" (p. 46,
  folio 44). No place of burial, no silsila title given — **(not stated)**.
- **Shah Gulshan** — "Andalib's mystical master" (p. 47, folio 45), whose "instructions" governed the
  monthly poets' meeting (p. 51, folio 49); "his father's master, Shah Gulshan had been called the
  'second Khusrau'" (p. 57, folio 55).
- **Imam Hasan** — "The saintly spirit who had descended upon Muhammad Nasir was that of the Imam
  Hasan, the grandson of the Prophet (K 85), (who is, by the way, also regarded as the first
  gutb[OCR?] [qutb], 'Pole' in the Shadhiliyya order)" (p. 44, folio 42).
- **Firaq** — the family's romanticising biographer, cited throughout (F 26, 32, 89, 91, 92, 96, 116,
  147, 148, 187, 46) and repeatedly discounted; "In Firaq's romanticizing account of this event"
  (p. 44, folio 42).
- **Athar** — Dard's brother, author of a "Mathnawi about the family-saga" from which Firaq's
  account is "taken" (p. 44, folio 42); glossed as "('result')" in the audition (p. 53, folio 51);
  buried in the same enclosure (p. 54, folio 52, footnote).
- **Alam** — Dard's son, buried in the same enclosure (p. 54, folio 52, footnote). Nothing else is
  said of him — **(not stated)**.
- **Bedar** — "one of Andalib's disciples in poetry", occasional secretary (p. 46, folio 44).
- **Mir Taqi Mir** — "the future master of Urdu love-poetry", "a few years junior" to Dard; in his

## Practices, institutions, economy

**This chunk is rich in khanqah, discipleship and patronage material and almost empty of shrine
economy.** Explicit negatives first: **the chunk names no sajjada nashin, no Auqaf or state
department, no langar or free kitchen, no offerings or cash donations, no endowment deed, no land
grant to a shrine, no revenue figure, no dispute over custody and no building date for any tomb.**
All of those are **(not stated)**. The one word touching endowed property is Dard's own "jdgir and
high office" (p. 45, folio 43), and that is family property under the empire, not shrine property.

- **A princess endows a khanqah complex, itemised room by room.** "the princess built a nice
  compound for them, comprising of houses, a mosque, a meditation room and a large space for musical
  gatherings, the meetings for the celebration of ʿurs, and gatherings of poets. This compound in
  Chélun ka kucha remained in the hand of Dard's family for a long time; its last traces disappeared
  after partition." (p. 46, folio 44) **This is the single most important institutional sentence in
  the chunk: a named patron, a built complex with four functional spaces, a stated ʿurs function,
  hereditary family possession over "a long time", and a terminal date given only as "after
  partition".**
- **The one mention of ʿurs in the chunk is that function of the compound's assembly hall** (p. 46,
  folio 44). **No ʿurs date, no ʿurs of any named saint, and no ʿurs ritual are described** —
  **(not stated)**.
- **The residence that survived the sack of 1739, and the reason given.** The mystics first "refused
  the offer, and 'thanks to the Muhammadan banner and the benevolence of Nasir' their place in
  Bardmda ki nala remained outside the reach of the looting soldiers" (pp. 45–46, folios 43–44).
  **Protection of a saintly household is credited to the order's own banner and the shaikh's
  benevolence.**
- **A monthly poets' assembly held in a named mosque, on a fixed day, by the shaikh's rule.** "In
  this mosque Muhammad Nasir Andalib, faithful to Shah Gulshan's instructions, used to arrange a
  mushdʿira (meeting of poets) on the 15th day of every month, his son accompanying him" (pp. 50–51,
  folios 48–49). **A calendar observance with a named venue and an inherited instruction behind
  it.**
- **The right to convene that assembly is transmitted by permission, not inheritance.** "After
  Andalib's death, the organization of mushaʿiras was disrupted and Mir asked his friend Dard for
  the permission to continue the tradition, which permission was granted" (p. 51, folio 49).
  **An explicit licensing act by the head of the order.**
- **A monthly commemorative samaʿ for the founder, with a day, a size and an etiquette.** "Mir Dard,
  therefore, arranged for the memory of his father a samaʿ -meeting on every second and
  26thday[OCR?] [26th day] of each month, to which thousands of pious listeners flocked, enjoying
  the nightly music, kneeling as etiquette would have it, on both knees in perfect quietude.
  (F 46)." (p. 57, folio 55) **Two fixed days a month, a crowd figure ("thousands"), a night
  timetable and a prescribed posture — the fullest description of a recurring observance in the
  chunk. The figure is Firaq's.**
- **Musical sessions in the shaikh's house, and the fact that they were contested inside the
  order.** "the musical sessions in his house were famous" (p. 57, folio 55); "music and samaʿ are
  prohibited as religious expressions by the Mujaddidi school" (p. 55, folio 53); "some of his pious
  colleagues did not approve of his inclination to music" (p. 55, folio 53). **An internal
  regulatory conflict over ritual practice, dated to "the early 1770's"** (p. 56, folio 54).
- **Succession to the headship of an order, stated plainly.** "After Muhammad Nasir's death, Mir Dard
  became his successor as the leader of the Muhammadan Path" (p. 55, folio 53). **No installation
  rite, no turban, no gaddi and no contest is described** — **(not stated)**.
- **Initiation described as an act of allegiance to a living shaikh who is also the father.** Dard
  "was the first to offer allegiance to this new path" (p. 44, folio 42); his father "gave to the
  world" the order and refused to name it after himself (p. 44, folio 42).
- **Mass recruitment by an emotional recital, with the mechanism named.** After Muhammad Zubair's
  death, Andalib "told this story in Hindi language during three nights"; "all those who listened to
  it were deeply impressed and started crying and weeping"; "Some of them even became enraptured and
  fell in ecstacy on the ground, and many of them entered the tariga Muhammadtyya" (p. 46,
  folio 44). **Grief-recital in the vernacular as a recruitment event.**
- **Recruitment by miracle, with the same result.** After the rescue in Bengal, "most of the
  travellers immediately entered the Muhammadan Path (K 146)!" (p. 54, folio 52).
- **The duties of a servant of the convent, listed by the servant himself** — sweeping "the court of
  the 'Qibla of both worlds'", "the instruction and education of brethren and children", "completion
  of assemblies and invitations of people", and "showing the people the way" (p. 50, folio 48).
  **Building maintenance, teaching, hospitality and guidance, in one sentence.**
- **A relic used for blessing, and its transmission inside the family.** The cane "an-Nasiri" with
  the founder's name in "an artistic tughrd" "was for a long while in the family", and "this
  peculiar type of tughraé was used by the members of the family for the sake of blessing (M 93 f)"
  (p. 49, folio 47). **A relic, a reproducible calligraphic form, and a family monopoly on its
  blessing use.**
- **Tomb visitation practice, as the chunk describes it in passing.** Those "who visited it and
  kissed its threshold were refreshed even during the hottest days of summer" (p. 54, folio 52); and
  Schimmel's own aside, "Everyone who has visited Indian shrines in summer at high noon, walking
  barefoot through the precincts will gratefully acknowledge Muhammad Nasir's kindness towards his
  disciples" (p. 54, folio 52). **Threshold-kissing and barefoot circulation, the latter as a
  general statement about Indian shrines, not about this tomb.**
- **The tomb is unroofed, and the chunk says so twice.** "his father's tomb had no roof or dome so as
  to be exposed to the weather" (p. 54, folio 52); "it is indeed open … The ground is clay, and the
  location is situated ona little hill" (p. 54, folio 52, footnote). **No enclosure wall date, no
  builder and no custodian** — **(not stated)**; the enclosure is called only "the modest
  enclosure".
- **Grave clustering in one family enclosure.** "besides Andalib, Mir Dard, his son Alam and his
  brother Athar are buried there; two small :ombs in the modest enclosure have no names" (p. 54,
  folio 52, footnote). **Four generations of one household in a single unroofed plot.**
- **A domestic, women-led reading practice around the founder's book.** "the ladies, all acquainted
  with Persian, told their children the touching stories of the Rose and the Nightingale until they
  were able to understand their deeper meaning" (p. 49, folio 47).
- **A book used for divination.** Andalib "recommended it for tafa ul, 'future-telling'" and Dard
  held the same of his own book (p. 48, folio 46).
- **A healing gesture recorded as still current when the book was written.** "tenderly they blew
  prayers and four gud (Sura 112) upon him (even today it is common to utter some prayers and
  'breathe' over the sick part of the body)" (p. 42, folio 40).
- **Manifestation of the shaikh at a distance, said to be current practice.** "Such a manifestation
  for healing or consoling purposes is by no means unusual even today in Naqshbandi circles"
  (pp. 54–55, folios 52–53); and "cases of 'inspired' writing in Turkey happen even today" (p. 51,
  folio 49).
- **Marriage as an ascetic policy.** "'a married dervish will be a hundred times superior to a single
  dervish' (NA I 895)" (p. 45, folio 43).
- **Migration of cultural producers as an economic fact.** Delhi's "misfortunes and afflictions which
  caused most of the poets, musicians and scholars to wander East in search of livelihood" — Dard
  "never left Delhi" (p. 55, folio 53). **A livelihood-driven migration, with the shaikh's
  immobility set against it.**
- **A dated destruction of a religious manuscript.** the autograph of the Nala "was destroyed during
  the Mutiny 1857" (p. 49, folio 47).
- **Astrology in the pre-initiation life.** "when I began some work I looked up the lucky and unlucky
  days and in a certain way hoped from the sky for help" (p. 45, folio 43).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

Schimmel reports all of these at a distance; the attributions below are hers, and she twice states
the family's own scepticism — "Neither Muhammad Nasir nor his son did think much of
miracle-mongery" (p. 54, folio 52).

- **A saint's spirit descends on the founder.** "The saintly spirit who had descended upon Muhammad
  Nasir was that of the Imam Hasan, the grandson of the Prophet (K 85)" (p. 44, folio 42); in
  Firaq's version "he addressed him, saying: 'My dear grandfather has sent me specially to you so
  that I may fill you completely with interior knowledge and sanctity' (F 26)" (p. 44, folio 42).
  Attributed to Firaq and, behind him, to "Athar's Mathnawi about the family-saga".
- **Instant fame and a first healing miracle at 13, both rejected by the author.** "we doubt that the
  boy's fame as a saint immediately after his allegiance to his father was miraculously made known
  to the inhabitants of Delhi, and that he performed his first healing miracle the very next day
  (F 32)" (p. 45, folio 43).
- **The Prophet chooses the founder's grave.** Andalib "was buried in the graveyard near Turkoman
  Gate at a place which the Prophet himself had indicated to Mir Dard (F 96)" (p. 51, folio 49).
- **The temperate tomb.** "twelve years after Muhammad Nasir's death, the place had remained always
  pleasant for the visitors: cool in summer and warm in winter, so that those who visited it and
  kissed its threshold were refreshed even during the hottest days of summer, when everywhere else
  the earth was burned" (p. 54, folio 52, on Mir Dard's authority, K 495). **Schimmel gives a
  natural explanation in her footnote: "The ground is clay, and the location is situated ona little
  hill-that may account for its 'coolness'."** (p. 54, folio 52, footnote)
- **The knight in Bengal.** "Once a disciple of Muhammad Nasir travelled through Bengal when his
  caravan was attacked by highway robbers. Suddenly, a most beautiful knight appeared; he frightened
  the robbers so much that they ran away … The hero was Muhammad Nasir who, though in Delhi, had
  heard the prayer of his disciple and helped him" (p. 54, folio 52, citing K 146). Schimmel:
  "Miracles of this kind are frequently attested in Muslim hagiography and even in modern oral
  traditions" (p. 54, folio 52). **Dard "tells only one other miracle connected with his father,
  although he says in passing that there are many"** (p. 54, folio 52).
- **The audition of three promises (S 329), quoted as Dard gives it** — "O Pain of my Beloved! (Or:
  O my beloved Dard!) and Lamentation of my Nightingale, we shall give you good tidings about three
  great and important things": (1) "we shall make your resurrection … so much in the real form of
  your great father and high spiritual guide that absolutely nobody from among family and
  foreigners … shall see a difference between you"; (2) "we shall inform you beforehand about the
  year of your departure from the world"; (3) "many clear miracles and strong supernatural powers
  shall appear from you, and numberless witnesses and clear evidences shall come so frequently and
  without interruption that involuntarily and indispensably friend and rival will acknowledge the
  stand of your right and title, and will obey and follow you" (pp. 53–54, folios 51–52).
  **The third promise is a claim to authority enforced by miracle — the chunk's clearest statement
  of karamat as legitimation.** Schimmel: "This divine promise was almost fulfilled, when Dard wrote
  these lines" (p. 54, folio 52).
- **Unbroken communion with the dead father.** "in the inward actions there is … not a single hair's
  breadth interruption in the relation with his presence. Nay, but every day the approximation
  became closer, and the company in speaking and hearing became better than is feasible in this
  visible world" (p. 52, folio 50, K 636), argued from "'Verily the saints of God do not die'"
  (p. 52, folio 50).
- **Prophecy fulfilled in a poet's career.** Andalib "had foretold him that he would become mir
  majlis, 'master of the assembly,' and these words proved true" (p. 51, folio 49).
- **Objects and books with power.** the cane's "tughrd" used "for the sake of blessing" (p. 49,
  folio 47); the Nala recommended "for tafa ul, 'future-telling'" and said to yield "marvellous
  predictions of the future" (p. 48, folio 46).
- **Invented marvels attributed to the founder.** "the 'wandering tent,' and the 'bath for
  everywhere,' and the 'candle without tears' and similar items", "If we believe Firaq" (p. 49,
  folio 47).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **Schimmel reads Dard's autobiography as retrospective construction.** His account of infant
  metaphysical anxiety is "(of course, in a back-projection which is typical of old age)" (p. 42,
  folio 40). **This is her governing methodological move in the chunk.**
- **She grades her sources explicitly.** Firaq "as usual, embellishes the story with touching
  details" (p. 42, folio 40) and offers a "romanticizing account" (p. 44, folio 42); yet "Firaq is
  certainly right when he gives Dard's age at this event as being 13 years" (p. 45, folio 43), and
  "We may accept Firaq's statement that he had studied books on classical Indian music" while
  rejecting the claim that he knew Sanskrit (p. 56, folio 54). **Accept-in-part, reject-in-part,
  item by item.**
- **She dates the founding revelation by the date of the book that reports it.** "Since the tariga
  Muhammadtyya is the focal point in Muhammad Nasir's main work, which was written in 1740, the
  event must have taken place sometime in the mid-thirties of the 18th century" (p. 45, folio 43).
  **An inference from textual evidence, and she marks it as one ("must have taken place").**
- **She explains a biographical claim away as source confusion.** The army service "may be due to a
  confusion of his and his father's biographies" (p. 45, folio 43).
- **She reads the Nala-yi Andalib as a period document rather than a mystical one.** "The modern
  reader will find, in its sweet style tinged with melancholia, a strange reflection of the
  vicissitudes of time under which Delhi and its unlucky inhabitants smarted in those years"
  (p. 49, folio 47); and she admits the gap between her own response and Dard's: "For the Western
  reader who may enjoy some of the charming allegories, it is not easy to understand why Mir Dard
  called his father's work 'the billowing of the Divine wisdom ... inspired from God' (K 38)"
  (pp. 47–48, folios 45–46).
- **She sets the son's rigorism against the father's latitude** on wahdat al-wujud and on Hallaj
  (p. 47, folio 45) and on music, where the son breaks with the school (p. 55, folio 53). **The
  book's comparative engine in Part I is father against son.**
- **She explains Mujaddidi hostility to samaʿ historically, not doctrinally.** "One can understand
  this sober attitude of the 'orthodox' tariga better when remembering how the rulers of Delhi after
  Aurangzeb, especially Muhammad Shah, took to music and indulged in doubtful pleasures; but it is
  well known that from the beginning of Sufi history most theoreticians of mysticism have rightly
  suspected that the samaʿ sessions were only a pretext for many would-be mystics to reach a not
  always spiritual rapture." (p. 55, folio 53)
- **She is openly sceptical of Dard's self-exculpation on music.** His confession "seems somewhat
  amusing and (at least for a modern reader) not completely convincing" (p. 55, folio 53), and:
  "Dard's logic is, of course, infallible—since he does not call the musicians, and they still
  continue coming, it must be God's order—why, then, should he oppose it?" (p. 57, folio 55).
- **She explains the musical sessions by lineage rather than by temperament** — the master of the
  master, Shah Gulshan, was "the 'second Khusrau'", and Andalib too "was fond of music" (p. 57,
  folio 55). **A silsila-based explanation of a ritual anomaly.**
- **She claims a specific literary achievement for Dard.** "He became the undisputed master of
  mystical Urdu poetry, and is probably the only poet who has achieved a complete blending of
  mysticism and verse" (p. 59, folio 57), and his musical symbolism "is more personal due to his
  being a practicing musician" (p. 57, folio 55).
- **She notes her own fieldwork as evidence.** "I visited the tomb in September 1975" (p. 54,
  folio 52, footnote), and uses it to give a physical explanation of a miracle.
- **She uses living practice as comparative evidence** — "even today it is common to utter some
  prayers and 'breathe' over the sick part of the body" (p. 42, folio 40); "by no means unusual even
  today in Naqshbandi circles" (p. 54, folio 52); "cases of 'inspired' writing in Turkey happen even
  today" (p. 51, folio 49). **Recorded because the archive may want the method as much as the
  content.**

## Doubts

**Archive mapping.**

- **Nothing in this chunk maps to an archive row, and the negative is deliberate.** Mir Dard's
  Delhi, his father's grave near Turkoman Gate, the Chélun ka kucha compound and the Zinat
  al-masdjid mosque are all outside the archive's Pakistan scope.
- **The Naqshbandi-Mujaddidi identification is refused as a mapping.** "he strictly followed the
  rules of the Naqshbandi mujaddidi school" (p. 55, folio 53) matches the silsila field of two
  archive rows — `mohra-sharif-khanqah` and `ganj-e-inayat-sarkar` — and of the Mujaddidi tradition
  generally. **The chunk names neither shrine, neither saint and no connection whatever, so no id is
  claimed.** Flagged only so the consolidator does not later mistake the silsila for evidence.
- **Interim convention line (carried forward from the sorley run).** Bare place names in this chunk
  take no archive id and are listed under "Other saints, sites and events". If Rauf rules the other
  way, **no single archive row is waiting for any of them**: the only Pakistani place named is
  "Lahore" (p. 49, folio 47, footnote), and it appears solely as the city of "the Library of the
  University of the Punjab", which would not resolve to any one of the archive's many Lahore rows
  (`data-darbar`, `shah-jamal`, `madho-lal-hussain`, `bibi-pak-daman` and the rest). "the Panjab"
  (p. 50, folio 48) is a region, not a site. Recording it this way keeps the decision reversible.

**Internal contradictions and gaps.**

- **The order-entry and death dates do not agree across three pages.** Dard put on "the garment of
  the dervishes" at 29 (p. 49, folio 47), and "That was in 1750" (p. 50, folio 48); but the waridat
  are dated "between Dard's entering the order and his father's death (1749 to 1758)" (p. 51,
  folio 49); and Andalib's death is given as "2. Shaʿ ban 1172/2.4.1759" (p. 51, folio 49).
  **1749 vs 1750 for the entry, and 1758 vs 1759 for the death — both must be checked against the
  book's own chronology before either enters an entry.**
- **Muhammad Nasir's master is named twice, differently.** "Muhammad Nasir's spiritual guide,
  Muhammad Zubair" (p. 46, folio 44) against "Andalib's mystical master" Gulshan (p. 47, folio 45)
  and "his father's master, Shah Gulshan" (p. 57, folio 55). **The chunk never reconciles them and
  never says whether one succeeded the other.** Do not build a silsila from this chunk alone.
- **The father's name is inverted once** — "the 'son' of Nasir Muhammad" (p. 52, folio 50), against
  "Muhammad Nasir" everywhere else. Could be the book's own slip or OCR transposition.
- **No birth or death year for Mir Dard appears in this chunk at all**, so "13 years" (p. 45,
  folio 43), "the age of 15" (p. 45, folio 43), "about sixteen" (p. 45, folio 43), "the age of 29"
  (p. 49, folio 47) and "the age of his father (66 lunar years)" (p. 54, folio 52) cannot be
  converted to dates here. **The book's own dates for Dard are in the front matter and in chunk 002
  and must be supplied from there, not from general knowledge.**
- **"Hurmat al-ghind"[OCR?] is glossed "'Veneration of Music'" (p. 55, folio 53).** The printed
  English gloss is recorded as printed. **A reader may want to query it against the Arabic title,
  but the note must not silently substitute another translation.**
- **The chunk opens mid-quotation.** The passage ending "(K 636)" at p. 42 (folio 40) begins in
  chunk 002, so its subject — Dard or his father — cannot be fixed from this chunk. The same
  citation "(K 636)" is used again at p. 53 (folio 51) for a different passage.
- **The 1757 looting of Delhi is attributed to "the Afghan 'friends;'" without a named commander**
  (p. 51, folio 49) — **(not stated)**; likewise "The useless king of Delhi" who was "deposed and
  blinded by his vizier" is not named (p. 50, folio 48).
- **The block quotation at p. 50 (folio 48) on the 1753 civil war is inside inverted commas but
  carries no source** — "'when the two cities of Delhi were held by the rival factions and fighting
  went on... for six months, paralysed the Empire ...'". **The citation is absent in the chunk.**
- **Footnote numbering is inconsistent in the OCR**: the footnotes on pp. 49 and 54 (folios 47, 52)
  open with a stray asterisk or apostrophe, while pp. 55 and 58 (folios 53, 56) print "5" and "6".
  The chunk therefore cannot be used to number this chapter's footnotes.
