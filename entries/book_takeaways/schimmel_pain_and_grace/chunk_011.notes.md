# schimmel_pain_and_grace — chunk 011 — pp. 193–213

**What this chunk is.** A continuous run inside Part II's chapter on Sufi and Yogi material in the
Risalo, then a verse-by-verse commentary on **Sur Yaman Kalyan**. The running head alternates
`SUFIS AND YOGIS IN SHAH ABDUL LATIF’S POEMS` on rectos and `SHAH ABDUL LATIF OF BHIT` on versos
(apparatus, not quoted as content below). **The folio regime the run brief gives holds on every one
of the twenty-one pages: folio = PDF index, offset 0.** Every page 193–213 prints its number in the
running header — verso number-first (`194 SHAH ABDUL LATIF OF BHIT`, p. 194), recto number-last
(`SUFIS AND YOGIS IN SHAH ABDUL LATIF’S POEMS 193`, p. 193) — **and no page in this chunk lacks a
printed number**, so nothing here is cited as "folio not stated".

**Archive value.** This is not a biography chunk: there is no ʿurs, no langar, no custodian and no
endowment in it. What it does carry, and carries densely, is **a network of named Sindhi and Punjabi
saint-poets, each of whom is the principal figure of an archive row** — Shah Abdul Karim, Makhdum
Nuh of Hala, Abdur Rahim Girhori, Sachal Sarmast, Muhammad Zaman Lanwari, Sultan Bahu, Bullhe Shah
— set by Schimmel into a single textual tradition around Shah Abdul Latif, with dates printed for
several of them. **Two name-collisions in this chunk look like archive rows and are not** (Zakariya;
"Lal") and **one archive-looking place name is a mystical term, not a place** (la-makan). All three
are handled under `## Doubts` and deliberately left unmapped.

## Practices, institutions, economy

**THE EXPLICIT NEGATIVE, which this chunk requires: there is no ʿurs, no mela, no langar, no
offerings, no votive practice, no sajjada nashin or custodian of any kind, no Auqaf, no endowment, no
land or revenue, no visitor numbers, no dispute, no state involvement and no building or architecture
of any kind anywhere in pp. 193–213 (folios 193–213).** The institutional content below is of three
other kinds only: performance rules for the Risalo, the pir–murid relation as a metaphor, and the
textual-transmission apparatus of the Risalo itself.

- **A performance rule for a Sur, with time of day and intended effect.** "Sur Yaman Kalyan is a
  derivation from Kalyan, and should be sung in the evening; it is meant to bring peace to the soul"
  (p. 195, folio 195). **The single most practice-relevant sentence in the chunk: the Risalo is sung,
  and its Surs are calendared to hours.**
- **The `wa’i`[OCR?] [waʾi] as the closing movement of every chapter.** Each chapter of the Sur is
  analysed as verses plus a `wa’i` — "I, (1-20, `wa 72.`[OCR?])" (p. 195, folio 195); "In Chapter II,
  (1-19, `wa 72,`[OCR?])" (p. 196, folio 196); "in the last verse before the `wda’i`[OCR?]" (p. 196,
  folio 196); "The `wai`[OCR?], then, describes the arrival of the beloved" (pp. 198–199,
  folios 198–199); "This topic is carried over into the `wd ’i`[OCR?], too" (p. 197, folio 197);
  "Chapter IV (1-23, `wa’i`)" (p. 199, folio 199). **The numerals attached to these markers are
  OCR-damaged throughout — see `## Doubts`.**
- **Instruments named inside a quoted verse.** "Those, whose body is a rosary, the soul a bead of the
  rosary, the heart a tanboura— They play on the strings of the secret of untty" (p. 194, folio 194).
  **A rosary and a tanboura: the two objects of Risalo performance named in this chunk.**
- **The Yogis' assembly hall, and its living counterpart in the Sindhi house.** The word
  `6tdaq`[OCR?] [ōtāq], "'sitting room,'" is "always used in the `Risdlé`[OCR?] to describe the
  Divine presence: it is Punhun's place in the Sassui circle, Mehar's place in the Sohni tale, and is
  also the high hall where the Yogis meet and inspire those who have come to listen to their songs
  (`Ram. IX 3-13`) as it is now the part of the compound where honored male guests are received by
  the host" (p. 207, folio 207). **An assembly of ascetics with an audience that has come to listen —
  the closest thing in this chunk to a devotional gathering — plus a gendered hospitality rule stated
  in the present tense ("honored male guests").** "The connection of the word `d6tag`[OCR?] with the
  dwelling place of the ruler is spiritualized in Shah Abdul Latif's verse" (p. 207, folio 207).
- **The murshid–murid relation, entirely as metaphor.** "the mystical guide is the blacksmith who
  knows the qualities of the raw ore and works upon it until he transforms it into valuable steel.
  Likewise, the spiritual guide cleans his disciple from rust, puts him into the fire of suffering so
  that he matures, hardens him with cold water, and eventually polishes him until he reaches… a
  highly brilliant quality" (p. 198, folio 198). And "The true guides put poison into the cup and make
  it circulate"; "The perfect mystical guide is the wine-seller who has poison-like wine" (p. 199,
  folio 199). **No named pir, no named murid, no shrine, no institution** — **(not stated)**.
  Schimmel notes the blacksmith image "is not very common in traditional imagery" and that Shah uses
  it "in two more places, once, very similar to this verse, in `Raémakali`[OCR?] (see p. 227)"
  (p. 198, folio 198).
- **The Sufi cap as an affiliation badge, and its repudiation.** "The Sufi cap, often mentioned as the
  distinctive mark of the mystic, and varying in shape and color according to the affiliation of the
  Sufi, was quite early regarded by the advanced mystic with some mistrust" (p. 203, folio 203);
  "In Shah's verse, too, the cap appears as a sign of self-confidence, perhaps even of hypocrisy.
  Such a cap should be thrown away" (p. 203, folio 203). The quoted verse: "You wish to be called
  Sufi! To the Sufis (something) like this is not fitting, Cut the headgear, take and cast it in
  fire!" (p. 203, folio 203, v. 6). **A material marker of silsila membership, and a Sindhi text
  telling the disciple to burn it.**
- **Two named modes of dhikr, contrasted as a technique.** "The contrast of `jali` and `khafi`,
  hidden and open, in line 1 and 2 points to the various kinds of recollection" (p. 208, folio 208);
  "just as the `dhikr khafi`, the silent recollection, runs through the veins in every breathing of
  the mystic, thus the Sufi himself, completely transformed into recollection, is hidden in the world
  and yet keeps it alive" (p. 200, folio 200). The Yogis too "'seek the magnificent Lord with khafi,'
  e.g., silent `dhtkr`[OCR?]" (p. 200, folio 200). And a maxim of the trade: "as the Sufis repeatedly
  said: 'One hour of thinking is better than 70 years of worship'" (p. 208, folio 208).
- **A caste fact stated in the present tense.** "It should be remembered that the `kalal` or
  wine-distiller is considered, in parts of Indo-Pakistan, a member of the most despised caste"
  (p. 199, folio 199). **An occupational-caste ranking, given as current at the time of writing.**
- **The madrasa syllabus as an institution, named by its three books.** "All the three are required by
  those who seek `‘i/m`[OCR?] [ʿilm], outward knowledge, but this kind of knowledge, as indeed all
  studying, is not required in the way of love" (p. 202, folio 202); "Sindhi and Panjabi mystical
  poets have regarded the three books mentioned in its first line as symbols of useless bookishness"
  (p. 202, folio 202); the Kafiya was the poem "according to which children in Indian madrasas were
  instructed in Arabic" (p. 202, folio 202). **A named curriculum, a named clientele (children), and
  a documented Sindhi-Punjabi tradition of mocking it.**
- **The Hanafi madhhab's regional dominance.** "the Hanafi law school (d. 767) which was, and still
  is, prevalent in Indo-Pakistan" (p. 201, folio 201); and the term `la-kuft`[OCR?] [la-kufi] "meant
  probably first 'not belonging to Abu Hanifa's madhhab' and was then used to designate some one
  without affiliation to a particular religious rite. For the lover, the differences of the legal
  schools are completely unimportant" (p. 202, folio 202).
- **The Risalo's editions and commentaries, treated as competing institutions.** Four distinct textual
  authorities are named and set against one another: **the Trumpp edition** — "the Trumpp edition of
  `Shah j6 Risalo`[OCR?] [Shah jo Risalo] adds here" the Kanz-Quduri-Kafiya verse (p. 202,
  folio 202); **the Hyderabad edition** — "The Hyderabad edition has this verse, with a long line of
  similar verses, at the end of Yaman Kalyan, v. 41-44" (p. 202, folio 202, footnote 13) and "the
  Hyderabad edition has a different reading of the initial part of each verse, which caused Dr. N.A.
  Baloch's interpretation in his article 'Shah Abdul Latif and Rumi', 1973" (p. 206, folio 206,
  footnote 17); **the commentary and the Urdu translation** — "Yaman means, as the commentator
  states, 'allusion, symbol'" (p. 195, folio 195), "thus the commentary" (p. 200, folio 200), "The
  Urdu translation says that he has made his body a mirror" (p. 203, folio 203), "'Difficult winding
  paths' follows the commentary and the Urdu translation; the grammatical construction is not clear"
  (p. 210, folio 210); and **the editor** — "The editor explains the expression as `farqé`[OCR?]
  [farqi], 'distinction, separation;' but it really means 'kufic'" (p. 201, folio 201). **Jotwani's
  translation is also contested: "Jotwani translates 'ether' instead of 'breath of life'"** (p. 200,
  folio 200, footnote 9).
- **A nineteenth-century Sindhi manuscript in private hands, and a hadith variant recorded only from
  it.** "Here, the version of the famous tradition which I found in a 19th century manuscript of
  Sindhi mystical poetry would find its place: 'Who knows himself through `fand`[OCR?] [fana],
  "annihilation," knows his Lord through `bagd`[OCR?] [baqa], "remaining in him."' This elegant
  formulation may have been common among the Sindhi Sufis" (p. 212, folio 212, footnote 23 giving the
  owner and place). **Provenance for a text the archive cannot find elsewhere.**
- **Verse-count and structure data for Sur Yaman Kalyan**, useful if the archive ever needs the
  Risalo's shape: Chapter I "(1-20)" (p. 195, folio 195), Chapter II "(1-19)" (p. 196, folio 196),
  Chapter III "(1-27)" (p. 198, folio 198), Chapter IV "(1-23)" (p. 199, folio 199), and Chapter V
  running at least to v. 29 (p. 213, folio 213). "This meditation about wounds and suffering, about
  nightly weeping and the recollection of the friend extends over 19 verses" (p. 196, folio 196).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

Attributed below exactly as the book attributes them; Schimmel reports each as legend or anecdote and
in two cases explicitly declines the reading.

- **The saint who knew only the alif — told of Shah Abdul Latif himself.** "Many legends tell that a
  saint was illiterate and knew only the alif: that is said about the Turkish folk poet Yunus Emre,
  the Panjabi poet Bullhe Shah, and our Abdul Latif as well" (p. 208, folio 208). **Attributed to
  "many legends"; three saints share it.**
- **Shah Abdul Karim taught the syllable OM by an ascetic.** "an anecdote relates that Latif's
  ancestor, Shah Abdul Karim, was taught 'the one crooked word', e.g., OM, by an ascetic in
  Ahmadabad" (p. 213, folio 213). **Attributed to "an anecdote"; Schimmel uses it only to reject a
  Hindu reading of alif-mim: "this seems too far fetched, and has no parallels in Shah Latif's
  poetry" (p. 213, folio 213).**
- **Hallaj executed for divulging the secret of union.** "for he was executed, as legend has it, for
  openly proclaiming the secret of loving union" (p. 197, folio 197).
- **The saint who kissed a hanged thief's feet.** Attar "says about Junaid of Bagdad, in the
  Musibatnama, however, about Shibli, that he kissed the feet of a thief who had been hanged, for
  this man was perfect in his profession and had to pay for his perfection with his life" (p. 197,
  folio 197). **Attributed to Attar, and Schimmel notes the two versions name two different saints.**
- **Zakariya sawed inside a tree, and made a martyr of love in Sindhi folk-poetry.** "according to
  Islamic legends, was killed when he sought refuge in a hollow tree which was, then, sawed at the
  order of his enemy, the Jewish king; he becomes one of the 'martyrs of love' in Sindhi
  folk-poetry" (p. 199, folio 199). **Attributed to "Islamic legends".**
- **Satan as the perfect lover, cursed for outward disobedience.** "Satan, 'more monotheist than God
  Himself,' refused to fall down before the created Adam, faithful to God's order to prostrate one's
  self only before Him. Satan was cursed because of his outward disobedience which involved, however,
  his inward loving obedience" (pp. 210–211, folios 210–211), with the rebellion "according to
  Hallaj… meant 'to declare God Holy'" (p. 211, folio 211, footnote 20 citing "Hallaj, Diwan,
  `muq.`[OCR?] Nr. 28-29"). **Schimmel records the majority view against it: "most interpreters have
  seen in Satan's refusal… an act of onesightedness" (p. 211, folio 211), and notes "there are also
  negative descriptions of Satan in Shah's poetry (see Asa IV 16, 17)" (p. 211, folio 211).**
- **The iron that cries "I am the fire!"** — Rumi "describes the experience of mystical union with the
  tale of the log of iron in the fire: eventually, the iron calls out 'I am the fire!', although its
  essence is still different from, and can never be the same as fire" (p. 198, folio 198).
- **The Chinese and the Rumi painters.** "while the Chinese painters in this tale decorated their
  walls with incredibly beautiful forms and colors, the Rumi painters just polished the marble wall,
  and when the curtain was lifted, their white, polished wall reflected the multitude of colorful
  Chinese pictures" (p. 205, folio 205). **Attributed to the Mathnawi via Lilaram Watanmal — see
  `## Arguments and interpretations`.**
- **The lame ant and the moon.** "As for the lame ant which wants to climb on a hair to the moon, it
  is known to Attar as a symbol of utter helplessness" (p. 203, folio 203), from the proverbial
  verse "It `1s`[OCR?] as though a lame ant, fallen into a pit, would regard the sky" (p. 202,
  folio 202).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Schimmel's method for the chunk, stated openly.** She selects Yaman Kalyan and Ramakali for full
  analysis because they "contain a comparatively greater number of mystical technical terms" (p. 195,
  folio 195), and warns against over-reading: "the language of both chapters, as of the other parts
  of the `Risald`[OCR?], sometimes poses serious problems to the interpreter because of its density,
  and because sometimes the poet seems to sacrifice grammatical clarity for the sake of an elegant
  rhyme or alliteration" (p. 195, folio 195). She allows deliberate obscurity: "we may not be
  mistaken to see at least in some of his verses examples of that paradoxical language by which
  mystics all over the world tried to hide their ideas rather than reveal them" (p. 195, folio 195).
- **Her verdict on the Risalo as a source for Sufi doctrine.** "The only more technical description of
  some Sufi terms, namely of the stages of Divine manifestations, is found in Sur Ramakali" (p. 195,
  folio 195); and "But the picture of the Man of God, as it emerges from his sketchy verses, is
  impressive enough, and explains many of the images used in the other chapters" (p. 195, folio 195).
  **"sketchy verses" is her own characterisation of the Risalo's doctrinal content.**
- **Her disagreement with Lilaram Watanmal, named and argued both ways.** "Shah Latif gives his answer
  in a series of verses in which he refers to Rumi, the great model of mystical thought. That seems,
  at least in the light of Shah's sources, the easiest explanation of the following sequence of
  verses, all of which contain the word Rumi. Lilaram Watanmal. however, sees in these verses
  allusions to the content of the story of the Chinese and the Rumi painters as told in the Mathnawi…
  That would make, again, perfect sense" (p. 205, folio 205, footnote 16 citing "`1.`[OCR?] [L.]
  Watanmal, Life of Shah Abdul Latif, p. 58"). Her conclusion is deliberately undecided: "This
  ambiguity lends a peculiar charm to the following verses" (pp. 205–206, folios 205–206).
- **Her correction of the Risalo's editor on `la-kuft`.** "The editor explains the expression as
  `farqé`[OCR?] [farqi], 'distinction, separation;' but it really means 'kufic' and is, as such, the
  epithet of Abu Hanifa of Kufa" (p. 201, folio 201), and she reconstructs the term's history — "the
  term `la-kuft`[OCR?] meant probably first 'not belonging to Abu Hanifa's madhhab' and was then used
  to designate some one without affiliation to a particular religious rite" (p. 202, folio 202).
- **Her argument that an edition inserted a verse.** "We assume that out of this logic the Trumpp
  edition of `Shah j6 Risalo`[OCR?] adds here" the Kanz-Quduri-Kafiya verse (p. 202, folio 202), with
  the Hyderabad edition placing it elsewhere entirely, "at the end of Yaman Kalyan, v. 41-44"
  (p. 202, folio 202, footnote 13). **A concrete textual-transmission claim: the same verse sits in
  two places in two editions.**
- **Her attribution of a genre's invention to Shah Abdul Karim.** "It was probably Shah Latif's
  ancestor Abdul Karim who invented this type of poetry, which became very popular in later times"
  (p. 202, folio 202), with Sachal Sarmast and Bedil of Rohri given as its continuators (p. 202,
  folio 202). **A three-generation Sindhi literary genealogy, hedged with "probably".**
- **Her Hallajian satanology line through Sind.** "still, the Hallajian theories lived under the
  surface and now and then appeared in ecstatic expressions like that of Sarmad, of our Shah Latif,
  or his younger compatriot, Sachal Sarmast, until they were once more brought into a fascinating
  system by Muhammad Iqbal" (p. 211, folio 211). **Four named figures in one transmission chain; the
  chunk's most explicit statement of Shah Latif's doctrinal descent.**
- **Her dating of a Sindhi loanword to a dynasty, and the inference drawn from it.** The word `6tdaq`
  "points to the Turkish influence that was prevalent in Sind from 1520 onward" (p. 207, folio 207),
  and she reads its spiritualisation as evidence of the poet's theology: it "expresses well his idea
  of God as the King and Lord par excellence" (p. 207, folio 207).
- **Her comparison of Shah Latif against Ghalib and against Persian-Turkish precedent, always to Shah
  Latif's simplicity.** Of the alif-i saiqal speculation: "Shah Abdul Latif, however, does not enter
  into such subtle speculations" (p. 198, folio 198). Of the moth and candle: "is taken up here, but
  is less elaborate than in comparable Persian or Turkish mystical poetry" (p. 198, folio 198).
- **Her cross-religious framing, stated rather than hedged.** The pearl motif "is the one precious
  pearl of the Gospel" (p. 204, folio 204); the iron-in-fire motif "goes back to the classical Sufi
  tradition, as it was known before in Christian mystical theology from at least the writings of
  Origines onward" (p. 198, folio 198, footnote 6 citing "E. Underhill, 'Mysticism,' p. 421 for
  Christian use of the image from Origines to Jacob Boehme"); the world-as-phantasmagory passage: "We
  may think of the Indian concept of `mdya`[OCR?] [maya], but even an orthodox Naqshbandi mystic in
  Sind, like Girhori, quotes the Arabic saying `ad-dunyd sthr`[OCR?]" (p. 205, folio 205); and "It is
  also common in the mystical tradition outside, or besides, Islam in India: Kabir's poetry offers
  fine examples of the same imagery" (p. 213, folio 213).
- **Her doctrinal summary of the Path in the Risalo**, given as the chunk's frame: "Complete severing
  of relations, `gat at-ta‘allug`[OCR?] [qat' at-ta'alluq], that is what the Yogis in Ramakali and
  the hero of `Sur Sératht`[OCR?] [Sur Sorath] experience" (p. 193, folio 193); "One has first to
  give up the world, then the otherworld… and eventually even to give up the 'giving up,' `tark
  at-tark`" (p. 193, folio 193); and after "'dying before death' he may reach the goal of the
  mystics, `fand`[OCR?] [fana], annihilation in God" (p. 193, folio 193).
- **Her claim about the spiritual élite, which is also a social claim.** "The Yogis, says Shah Latif,
  are no companions for weak people; they belong to the spiritual élite whose ways are much too high
  for the ordinary believers, who would never be able to undergo the hardships of the path. After
  all, only those in whose pre-eternal fate such a love is written will enter the desert, or dare
  swim in the dangerous river" (p. 194, folio 194).
- **Her note that Shah Latif sanctions abandoning ritual prayer at the highest stage.** "In this
  state, where the Sufi has lost his self, even the ritual prayer is no longer required, nay, has
  become impossible (`Asa I 11, 12`)" (p. 194, folio 194), with Hallaj's verse "That the ritual
  prayer of the lovers is infidelity" quoted in support (p. 194, folio 194). **A doctrinally sharp
  attribution that a shrine entry should quote only with its context.**
- **Her own earlier publication is cited as where the full apparatus sits** — "Cf. A. Schimmel,
  '`Sah`[OCR?] [Šāh] ‘Abdul Latif's Beschreibung des wahren Sufi', in: Festschrift Fritz Meier,
  where a great number of footnotes and comparisons are given" (p. 195, folio 195, footnote 2).
