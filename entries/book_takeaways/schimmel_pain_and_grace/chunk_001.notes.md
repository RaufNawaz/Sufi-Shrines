# schimmel_pain_and_grace — chunk 001 — pp. 1–20

**This chunk is front matter plus the book's whole opening chapter.** It contains the `FOREWORD`
(pp. 1–3), the `ABBREVIATIONS` page (p. 4), and `CHAPTER ONE / THE INDIAN SCENE IN THE EIGHTEENTH
CENTURY` from p. 5 to the end of the chunk at p. 20, where the chapter is still running. It is
scene-setting: a survey of Indian Muslim political, religious and literary history from the Arab
conquest of Sind to 1857, with the Sufi orders as its spine. **It is not a shrine chapter.** Only
one tomb in the archive's territory is named as a living cult site (Ali Hujwiri's at Lahore, p. 8,
folio 4); the other archive figures appear as poets and masters, with no shrine, ʿurs, custodian or
endowment attached to any of them. **Part I's subject, Mir Dard, is introduced here as "the saint
of Delhi" (p. 1) — outside this archive's territory, and this chunk yields no archive mapping for
him.** See the explicit negative under `## Practices, institutions, economy`.

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- **Khwaja Mir Dard (1721-1785)** — Part I's subject, introduced as "the saint of Delhi and first
  truly mystical poet of the Urdu language" (p. 1, folio not stated). "it is rare that a mystic offers
  his readers such a deep insight into his spiritual formation as does Mir Dard" (p. 1); he "has never
  attracted a scholar in the West, and only a few Urdu writers, who mainly discuss his poetry, not his
  theology" (p. 2, folio xii); his name means "Dard, 'Pain'" (p. 2, folio xii); he is one of "the 'four
  pillars of Urdu'" and "the mystic, who has in his turn written the most perfect Urdu mystical verses"
  (p. 18, folio 14); he is one of the "three leading mystics" of Delhi with Shah Waliullah and Mazhar
  Janjanan (pp. 18–19, folios 14–15). **He is a Delhi figure throughout and takes no archive id.**
- **Muhammad Nasir Andalib, Mir Dard's father** — "Shah Gulshan and his disciple Muhammad Nasir
  Andalib, Mir Dard's father, were prominent members of the group who encouraged the development of
  Urdu poetry" (p. 18, folio 14); he was "composing his `"Nala-y: 'Andalib"[OCR?]` [Nala-yi ʿAndalib]
  in mourning Pir Muhammad Zubair, the last qayyum" at the time of Nadir Shah's invasion (p. 20,
  folio 16).
- **Shah Gulshan** — "a Naqshbandi mystic and poetical preceptor" at Delhi shortly after 1700, through

  Shah Gulshan and Khan-i Arzu (d. 1756)".
- **Shah Waliullah of Delhi** — the chunk's fullest single portrait, and all of it Delhi. "the mystic,

  did not hesitate to invite Ahmad Shah `"Durrani Abt dali"[OCR?]` [Durrani Abdali] to fight the

  helped in the compilation of the `"Fatawa-yt 'Alamgiri"[OCR?]` [Fatawa-yi ʿAlamgiri]" (p. 19,
  folio 15); his terminology "had to be explained by his grandson Ismail Shahid in a book called
  `"Abagat"[OCR?]` ['Abaqat]" (p. 19, folio 15); his main Arabic work

## Practices, institutions, economy

**This chunk has no ʿurs, no mela, no langar, no offerings, no sajjada nashin or gaddi succession, no
Auqaf, no endowment, no land grant, no revenue, no visitor figure, no dispute and no shrine building
date anywhere in it.** All of those are **(not stated)**. What it does have is the institutional
frame — the order, the pir, the disciple's notebook, and the political weight of spiritual
authority — plus two sentences on shrine-going as a mass practice.

- **The order and the pir as institutions, with the term given in the book's own spelling.** "In the
  12th and 13th centuries, mystical orders and brother-hoods crystallized out of formerly loosely
  united groups of dis-ciples, who were introduced into the spiritualization of life by their
  religious guide, the `"shatkh"[OCR?]` [shaikh] or pir. The first representatives of such
  brotherhoods reached India in the beginning of the 13th century." (p. 8, folio 4)
- **Spiritual authority turning into temporal power — the chunk's central institutional claim.** "the
  Mus-lim saints rarely completely agreed with the politics of the ruling classes or the way of life of
  the feudal lords,—although, in later times, the borderline between the highly influential pir and the
  feudal lord was sometimes blurred, for spiritual influence not rarely resulted in political power."
  (pp. 8–9, folios 4–5) **This is the one sentence in the chunk an economics-of-religion entry can use
  directly.**
- **Shrine-going as a mass practice with a stated material motive.** Bullhe Shah's imagery could be
  "sung by the large masses who would flock to the shrines of the saints, hoping for material and
  spiritual help in this time of deepest depression" (p. 17, folio 13). **"material and spiritual help"
  is the book's own pairing.** **No shrine is named, no figures are given** — **(not stated)**.
- **A tomb as a continuing pilgrimage site.** Ali Hujwiri's "tomb in Lahore is still a place of
  pilgrimage for the people" (p. 8, folio 4). **The only living cult site in the chunk.**
- **The malfuzat as the orders' documentary output, and Schimmel's use of it as a social source.** "The
  disciples wrote down the `"malfiizat"[OCR?]` [malfuzat], the words and sayings of their masters. They
  allow us some insight into the spiritual, and also the social life of the Middle Ages, and complete
  thus the outlook of the official chronicles" (p. 8, folio 4). **A methodological statement about
  shrine-produced texts as economic and social evidence.**
- **Conversion as the orders' work rather than the state's.** "Without the help of the religious
  specialists, the rulers would scarcely have been able to maintain their rule over the vast country
  with its various racial and linguistic groups. The decisive force was, in the Islamization of India,
  however, not the activity of theologians and specialists in Islamic law and tradi-tions who were
  sometimes attached to the court, but rather that of the mystics who had settled in the Subcontinent
  as early as the 11th century" (pp. 7–8, folios 3–4). **A named mechanism: Mu'inuddin Chishti's
  preaching "won over considerable numbers of Hindus to Islam" (p. 8, folio 4).**
- **The orders as patrons of literature and music.** "The orders, who helped in Islamizing the country,
  contributed also to the development of literature and, partly, music: mystical poetry in Persian, and
  later in the regional languages, was inspired from their centers" (p. 8, folio 4). And the
  vernacular's institutional cause: "the mystics had played a decisive role in developing the language,
  since they needed a medium for conveying their message to the large masses who were neither able to
  follow the theological Arabic nor the admin-istrative and literary Persian" (p. 15, folio 11).
- **Mystical vocabulary as shared social property.** "Almost every poet and writer was connected in
  some way with a mystical order. Thus, the whole mystical vocabulary, as developed during centuries,
  was practically common stock for all members of the society, down to the lowest strata" (pp. 11–12,
  folios 7–8).
- **The Naqshbandi order in court politics, as a working account of how a silsila gained influence.**
  "This remarkable self-consciousness of the Naqshbandi leaders, together with their skill in politics
  made them more and more influential in India. Ahmad Sirhindi's successors and followers successfully
  worked to penetrate into the court circles. Shah Jahan's second son, Au-rangzeb Alamgir, lent his ear
  to their advice" (p. 11, folio 7); "More than three decades after his death, members of the Sirhindi
  family still con-tinued working behind the political scene, until the fourth
  `"gay-yum"[OCR?]` [qayyum], Pir Muhammad Zubair, died" (p. 11, folio 7). Also the earlier limit:
  "The activity of the Naqshbandis in the Subcontinent was lim-ited during the first century of Mughal
  rule. More emotional orders played a greater role" (p. 10, folio 6); and the two Central Asian
  branches at Farghana "were to play a de-cisive political role in the 17th and 18th centuries" (p. 10,
  folio 6).
- **The Naqshbandiyya's spread into Sindh, stated without a single site.** "The
  `"Nagshbandiyya"[OCR?]` [Naqshbandiyya] also spread in other parts of India, for instance in the
  Lower Indus Valley." (p. 11, folio 7) **No khanqah, no shrine, no family and no date** —
  **(not stated)**. This is the chunk's only institutional statement touching the archive's Sindhi
  Naqshbandi territory.
- **Order fragmentation.** "The other orders continued their activities; some of them had split up,
  forming numerous sub-groups." (p. 11, folio 7)
- **State restriction of music and art under Aurangzeb, twice.** "During Aurangzeb's reign, which
  lasted nearly half a century (1658-1707), music and fine arts were restricted" (p. 11, folio 7);
  "Miniature painting, too, slowly lost its glamour and music was forbidden" (p. 15, folio 11).
- **Poetical and musical assemblies as an institution of the Delhi mystics.** "In poet-ical meetings
  the forms and figures of Urdu poetry were discus-sed, new words adopted, awkward expressions
  discarded" (p. 18, folio 14); Mir Hasan "attended the poetical sessions of Mir Dard" (p. 18,
  folio 14); Shah Alam II "attended the musical meetings of Mir Dard" (p. 18, folio 14). **An emperor
  in a mystic's assembly is the chunk's one datum on court–shrine contact.**
- **State legal production under a named patron.** "the important collection of legal deci-sions
  according to Hanafi law compiled during the rule of Aurang-zeb Alamgir,
  `"faétdwd-yi 'Alamgiri"[OCR?]` [Fatawa-yi ʿAlamgiri], a comprehensive work in whose preparation a
  great number of excellent scholars were in-volved" (p. 12, folio 8).
- **A reformer's attack on shrine-going, fiefs and religious professionals — the chunk's one hostile
  account of the shrine economy.** Shah Waliullah "attacks the scholars who have given up studying the
  Prophetic traditions but rather learn unnecessary things like grammar and Greek philosophy; the false
  Sufis; the perverted preachers; the kings who ought to fight for the exten-sion, or at least the
  defense of Islam; the grandees who could not care less when wine shops, gambling dens or brothels are
  installed in the realm of their fiefs; soldiers who shave their beards and think only of amusements;
  artisans who indulge in drinking, adul-tery, and visits to allegedly sacred places" (pp. 20,
  folio 16). And: "he heavily attacks those Sufis who talk about 'what they think is truth,' seduce
  people by miracle mongering" (p. 20, folio 16). **"visits to allegedly sacred places" and "miracle
  mongering" are the two shrine practices condemned; no site is named** — **(not stated)**.
- **Waliullah's programme, in his own terms.** "Waliullah's interest lay in the purification of the
  Muslim community for a better life here, and even more in the Otherworld" (p. 20, folio 16); "he
  worked to help the Muslims of Delhi and all India by his writings and actions" (p. 19, folio 15); "To
  live with the Quran, as it had been done by the Sufis of yore, was his ideal. A true Sufi has to live
  exclusively on the Quran and the Prophetic tradi-tion" (p. 20, folio 16); he translated the Quran
  into Persian "in order to bring people back to the original text, and avoid the useless study of too
  many commentaries" (p. 20, folio 16).
- **Non-shrine architecture, dated.** "in about 1200, Delhi—with the magnificent building of the Qutb
  Minar--became the capital of the Muslim rulers" (p. 6, folio 2); "The mid-16th and early 17th
  centuries are those periods when Muslim architecture from Adrianopel and Istanbul to Delhi and Agra
  reached its apex" (p. 6, folio 2). **No shrine, tomb or mosque in the archive's territory is dated
  anywhere in the chunk** — **(not stated)**.
- **Destruction and demography of a capital, as a hard figure.** Nadir Shah "invaded the country and
  eventually loot-ed Delhi in 1739; about 30,000 people were killed. The once splendid city never fully
  recovered from this blow" (p. 7, folio 3). **"about 30,000" is the only population figure in the
  chunk.**

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

The chunk reports no karamat at any shrine. What it does report is a set of charismatic claims made
by, or for, two Delhi-area Naqshbandi masters, and Schimmel's own scepticism about them.

- **Ahmad Sirhindi's claim to be the qayyum, and Schimmel's verdict on it.** "the claims of saintship
  and charismatic leader-ship which Ahmad Sirhindi uttered, are expressions of such a fantastic
  self-consciousness that he, with his theories of the res-toration of the first m of Muhammad's name,
  and his claim to be the `"gayyum"[OCR?]` [qayyum] by far surpasses the limits of what we would
  nor-mally call 'orthodox Islam.' He saw himself and three of his descendants as divinely invested
  beings, higher even than the qutb, the 'Pole' of the traditional mystical hierarchy, and des-tined to
  guide the Muslim people as gayyum." (p. 11, folio 7) **The hierarchy term "the qutb, the 'Pole'" is
  the chunk's only piece of Sufi cosmology.**
- **Shah Waliullah's revelations and investitures, as the book lists them.** "as numerous revelations
  had told him, he had become the vicegerent of the Prophet 'in blaming.' Any-thing but modest, he
  claimed to have been granted the highest possible ranks in the religious hierarchy, and been taught
  hikma, wisdom, which is, in his theories, the last step before Prophet-hood: that he was promised to
  enter Paradise without reckoning is almost commonplace with later Muslim mystics. Shah Waliul-lah
  was certain that God had bestowed upon him the 'robe of `"mujaddidtyya"[OCR?]` [mujaddidiyya]' e.g.,
  of renewing the Islamic faith and restoring it to its pristine purity, but he even claimed to have
  been invested with the robe of `"hagqdniyya"[OCR?]` [haqqaniyya], the state of Divine Truth,' which
  deprived him of all logical understanding, so that he experienced 'how God talked upon my tongue.'"
  (p. 19, folio 15, with footnote 1 citing
  `"At-tahfhimat al-ilahiyya"[OCR?]` [At-tafhimat al-ilahiyya] "II 53, 145.")
- **The Prophet's Nightly Journey as the ceiling of mystical attainment.** "'I am His servant'—that is
  the highest station man can reach, for it is the station of the Prophet during his Nightly Journey
  (cf. Sura 17/1)" (p. 10, folio 6).
- **The Prophet as the Perfect Man.** "even the most ardent defenders of wahdat al-wujud agreed that
  the person of the Prophet Muhammad was the locus of the manifestations of the Divine Names, the
  'Perfect Man' par excellence, the highest model of humanity whom to imitate is the first and foremost
  duty of the believer" (p. 10, folio 6).
- **A literary, not a hagiographic, marvel — recorded because it is the chunk's only wonder attached to
  a place in the archive's region.** "'Agra and Lahore of Great Moghul,' which, as Milton says, were
  shown at the beginning of creation to the wonder-stricken Adam, kept some of their old charm" (p. 6,
  folio 2). **Attributed to Milton, not to any Indian tradition.**

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The book's founding complaint, stated in its first sentence.** "Eighteenth-century Islam is
  something that is usually neglected by the historians of religion. Histories of Sufism in general end
  with the thirteenth, at the best the `"fitteenth"[OCR?]` [fifteenth] century; the follow-ing period
  is, then, shortly classified as decadent." (p. 1, folio not stated)
- **The historiographical culprit is named.** "there were many decadent mystics and would-be Sufis as
  they are criticized in Hijazi's satire which, published by A. J. Arberry, has apparently given the
  impression that most of them were so de-generate as to be almost outside the pale of Islam, or else,
  that their works were nothing but epigonic repetitions of what had been said better in previous
  centuries." (p. 1, folio not stated)
- **The counter-thesis, with its geographical range.** "However, mystical thought and poetry flourished
  during the eighteenth century from Morocco to Islamic India." (p. 1, folio not stated) And its
  consequence for the next century: "Without studying the works of the mystical writers during the
  pre-modern period it will be difficult to appreciate the developments that set in, during the
  following century, for the rejuvenation and reform of Islam." (p. 1, folio not stated)
- **A claim about the Delhi school's afterlife.** "It became clear that the Delhi school of mysticism
  contributed more to the formation of Indo-Muslim modernist thought and poetry than is usually
  realized." (p. 1, folio not stated)
- **Method: the poets' own words, no closed system.** "Poets who write in such 'esoteric' languages as
  Sindhi, Urdu, and Persian are liable to be misquoted by those who cannot read their works in the
  original" (p. 2, folio xii); "I have tried to rely largely upon the poets' own words without
  introducing too many parallels or attempting at develop-ing a closed system. My intention was to
  offer a picture of two of the most fascinating Islamic mystics in eighteenth-century India, of two
  men who stand before us as representatives of various trends inside Indian Islam at a crucial point
  of its history." (p. 2, folio xii)
- **The book's thesis in one clause, from the title gloss.** The title "sums up the central ideas of
  both writers, ideas which they have expressed in various forms, i.e., that suffering is the
  prere-quisite of spiritual bliss, and that the Divine grace will descend upon those who rejoice in
  the tribulations which they undergo while travelling through the constantly shifting sand dunes of
  this created world, hoping for the city of God at the end of the road." (pp. 2–3, folios xii–xiii)
- **The shared Quranic frame for both mystics' withdrawal.** "Both receded into the depths of their own
  hearts, closing their eyes to the outward world at a time when 'the world' seemed to illustrate the
  truth of the Quranic sentence: 'Everything is perish-ing save His Face' (Sura 28/88)." (p. 2,
  folio xii)
- **The Foreword is dated and signed.** "1. Advent 1975 ANNEMARIE SCHIMMEL" (p. 3, folio xiii).
- **The chapter's framing judgement on the century, and Schimmel's refusal to leave it there.** "In the
  tides of history, the eighteenth century is usually con-sidered to be the time of lowest ebb for the
  Islamic peoples between the Balkans and Bengal. Almost no trace of the past glory of the Islamic
  Empires was visible any longer" (p. 5, folio not stated); against which, at the chapter's literary
  turn: "The situation in the literary field does, by no means, reflect the deplorable state of the
  Indian Muslims. On the contrary, this 18th century is amazingly fertile in poetical expressions in
  all the languages of Muslim India." (p. 16, folio 12)
- **The Europe-versus-Islam contrast, and the point of contact.** "the historian sees the glory of
  Europe who had gained her stand in world history and was just passing through the Age of Reason";
  "a time in which the borders of an outlook centering on Christian Europe opened and the East was, for
  the first time, considered worthy of an objective study" (p. 5, folio not stated); "The general
  political and social climate in Western Europe brought forth a new world-consciousness, but also an
  economic and political trend to expansion. It was only this latter aspect of 18th century Europe with
  which the Muslim countries were con-fronted." (p. 5, folio not stated)
- **The period label, and its poetic reading.** "It was, indeed, the 'twilight of the Mughals.'" (p. 6,
  folio 2) And: "When Mir Dard, in his poetry and prose, often speaks of the 'rose garden of
  manifestation of Divine power,' one should not forget that in Persian poetical language the red rose
  is connected not only with the idea of happiness or joy and love, but likewise with blood and
  wounds." (p. 6, folio 2)
- **Sind's continuity as a religious centre, asserted for the whole period since 711.** "the Arabs
  conquered Sind, the southern part of present day Paki-stan, in 711, and this province never ceased to
  be a center of Islamic studies and a seat of Islamic mysticism." (p. 6, folio 2) **A direct claim
  about the archive's Sindh territory, with no site attached.**
- **Indian Islamic history as a religious and not only a political history.** "The history of Indian
  Islam is, however, not only a history of political facts, of conquests and wars, of expansion and
  break-down, but is a spiritual history as well. It is the history of the century-long conflict
  between the Islamic concept of tauhid, strict monotheism, and Hinduism in its different
  manifestations which constituted, in the eyes of the pious Muslims, the very essence of idolatry and
  polytheism which had been condemned by the Quran." (p. 7, folio 3)
- **Ibn Arabi's system and its popular reduction.** His work "taught the Muslims the idea of wahdat
  al-wujud, `"-he essential Unity of Being"[OCR?]` [the essential Unity of Being], according to which
  God and the creation can be understood as two aspects of one Reality. Thus, the central Islamic dogma
  of God's unity was overstressed and expanded into a Weltanschauung which has been called either
  'pantheistic' or 'monistic.' Both terms, how-ever, do not give full justice to Ibn Arabi's
  complicated system of thought." (p. 9, folio 5) "The poets and, through them, the large masses
  interpreted his system in the simplified short sentence hama ost, 'Everything is He.'" (p. 9,
  folio 5) **Dated: the work "became known in the early 15th century" (p. 9, folio 5).**
- **Unity of Being as the bridge to Hinduism, and the orthodox reaction.** "The idea of an all
  embracing unity made some Muslim mystics discover similar thoughts in the religious systems of their
  Hindu neighbors" (p. 9, folio 5); "The more orthodox circles did not approve of such a dan-gerous
  interpretation of Islam which seemed to blur the borders between the two religious communities"
  (pp. 9–10, folios 5–6).
- **Schimmel's definition of Sufi 'tolerance' — a qualified one.** "The 'tolerance' of Islamic
  mysticism consists of its embracing all religions under the crown of that final revelation which was
  granted to Muhammad." (p. 10, folio 6) Preceded by: "Islam remained for all of them the last and most
  comprehensive Divine revelation which comprises in itself, and hence abolishes, the laws brought by
  every previous Prophet." (p. 10, folio 6)
- **The Naqshbandi counter-doctrine, with both formulas given.** "It is characteristic of the situation
  in 16th century India that the protest against the widespread theories of 'Everything is He' was
  launched again by a mystical order, namely the Naqshband-iyya" (p. 10, folio 6). Sirhindi's position:
  a doctrine "which excludes a 'Unity of Being' but admits of the possibility of 'Unity of Vision,'
  `"wahdat ash-shuhid"[OCR?]` [wahdat ash-shuhud]. l.e., in the moments of highest bliss the mystic
  eye-witnesses absolute Unity but knows that no essential union be-tween creature and Creator is
  possible, for 'The Lord is Lord, and the servant servant,' as the formulation of this school holds"
  (p. 10, folio 6); "The central thesis is not `"Aama ost"[OCR?]` [hama ost], 'Everything is He,' but
  hama az ost, 'Everything is from Him'--a formulation which is, indeed, much closer not only to
  orthodox Islam but to the theo-ries of unity as professed by the early mystics" (pp. 10–11,
  folios 6–7).
- **Mysticism as the survival mechanism of an afflicted population — the chapter's central
  interpretative claim.** "Poetry and prose written during this period can be understood and enjoyed
  only with a certain knowl-edge of its mystical background; for whether the population of the unhappy
  country would accept tribulations patiently in the feeling that 'Everything is from Him,' or would
  believe in the all embracing Unity of Being which showed its strange manifesta-tions in suffering and
  pain—the mystical way was, for most of them, the one source of strength which enabled them to survive
  during the afflictions which were showered upon Northern India in the 18th century." (p. 12, folio 8)
- **The literary geography of the period, language by language.** "literature, and especially po-etry,
  remained alive in India. It flourished at the courts, but in its vernacular form it was the delight
  of the illiterate villagers" (p. 12, folio 8); "the longer and more regional languages like Sindhi,
  Panjabi, and Pashto de-veloped fascinating poetical forms, while Urdu inherited the Per-sian refined
  tradition" (p. 12, folio 8); "The most important language for literary works, however, was still
  Persian, the official language of the Muslim rulers" (p. 13, folio 9).
- **The diagnosis of the 'Indian style' and its exhaustion.** The poets "had to invent new figures of
  speech; for the outward forms of the `"Per-slan"[OCR?]` [Persian] poem, the gasida and the ghazal
  with their monorhyme and their strict metrical rules as well as the `"mathnaw?"[OCR?]` [mathnawi],
  used for didac-tic and narrative poetry, remained untouched. However, the images were partly broken
  and put together in surprising patterns like the glass pieces in a kaleidoscope" (p. 14, folio 10);
  "Instead of the images of classical poetry which had in themselves a certain logic,
  pseudo-philosophical concepts, and artificial images became common" (p. 14, folio 10); "Still,
  Persian poetry had reached a kind of impasse; Persian prose remained, perhaps, alive longer" (p. 15,
  folio 11).
- **Schimmel reads the poetry as unknowing prophecy, and says so as a question.** "the poets were, at
  heart, nostalgic in the dazzling outward beauty of the 'Indian summer.' Were they homesick for Iran,
  or did they anticipate the gathering of those dark clouds whose lightning was to destroy the harvest
  of this whole super-refined culture? Or was it simply a convention that now words like 'shroud,'
  'breaking,' 'blistered feet,' 'firestricken nest,' etc., were used much more frequently than in
  previous times" (pp. 14–15, folios 10–11). **Posed as alternatives, not settled.**
- **The rise of Urdu, and the paradox Schimmel makes of it.** "One of the surprises in Indian cultural
  history around 1700 is, that members of the 'anti-artistic' Naqshbandiyya order were in-strumental in
  the development of a new literary medium which was, after Aurangzeb's death, to supercede Persian and
  to be-come the typical language of Indian Muslims: that is Urdu." (p. 15, folio 11) And the demand
  side: "Apparently, the Muslims of Northern India, after the breakdown at Aurangzeb's death in 1707,
  needed a new me-dium of expression which even the man in the bazaar would understand. Thus, all of a
  sudden, a remarkable literature in Urdu emerged in the first half of the 18th century in Delhi."
  (p. 16, folio 12)
- **The two regions the book will treat, named and justified.** "In spite of the overall activity of
  writers, theologians, and mystics throughout Muslim India, two areas can be singled out as
  particularly interesting in the history of mystical poetry in the Subcontinent. They are, as is to be
  expected, the capital Delhi with its long history, and the Lower Indus Valley. Although both areas
  had to suffer under the breakdown of the Mughal Empire, and the invasions from their Western
  neighbors, they produced an amazing amount of mystical writings and poetry." (pp. 17–18,
  folios 13–14) **This sentence is the book's two-part structure stated in advance.**
- **Panjabi poetry's golden age dated against invasion.** "The golden age of Muslim Panjabi poetry is
  the late 17th and the 18th cen-tury, e.g., the time when the province was invaded one time after the
  other by Mahrattas, Sikhs, and, in between, by the liberating Muslim armies." (p. 17, folio 13) And
  the lost corpus: "Numerous folk songs and ballads existed but were never commit-ted to writing."
  (p. 16, folio 12)
- **The three Delhi masters, and an admitted gap in the record.** "Delhi was also the place where three
  leading mystics lived and worked simultaneously, each of them claiming highest spiritual experiences,
  and trying in his own environment to ameliorate the situation of the afflicted Muslims of Northwest
  India. All of them were related in some way to the Naqshbandi order which, as we saw, was most
  influential among the poets, too. It is regret-table that we have barely any information about the
  relations among these three great masters, e.g., Shah Waliullah, Mazhar Janjanan, and Mir Dard."
  (pp. 18–19, folios 14–15) **Recorded because it is a gap the book itself marks.**
- **Waliullah's irenicism read out of the political situation.** "Shah Waliul-lah's attempts to bridge
  the gap between the different madhhabs in Islam, as well as the tension between the adherents of
  wahdat al-wujud and `"wahdat ash-shuhid"[OCR?]` [wahdat ash-shuhud] can be understood from the
  situation in the country; in this respect he was a true mystical leader who relied upon the old Sufi
  maxim which is often quoted in his works: Our signs are different, but Thy beauty is one, and
  everything points to that Beauty." (p. 20, folio 16) With his recast parable: "Maulana Rumi's parable
  of the blind and the elephant was trans-formed by him into a story of blind men touching a tree, for
  truth is a big tree from which each person can grasp only one leaf, one branch, the bark, or one
  fruit." (p. 20, folio 16)

**pp. 2 and 3 do print numbers**, in lower-case roman, in the running headers (`XU FOREWORD` and
`FOREWORD XIII`). I have cited them as `(p. 2, folio xii)` and `(p. 3, folio xiii)` on the primary

the roman front-matter folios count as folios for this archive, and whether the measured table needs

fifteen numbered pages, no break.

**Three mappings rest on thin evidence and a human should confirm or strike them.**
- **garh-maharaja-shorkot** for "Sultan Bahu": the chunk names him only as a Panjabi poet with a death
  year of 1691 and a verse form. **No location, no shrine, no silsila.** Name plus death year is the
  whole basis.
- **mausoleum-of-waris-shah** for "Warith Shah": the chunk gives no death year, no place and no shrine —

- **mazar-e-iqbal** for "Muhammad Iqbal": the mention is purely literary — his debt to Bedil, and
  Schimmel's own scholarly route to Mir Dard. **The chunk says nothing about his death, Lahore or his

**Explicit negative, recorded as instructed: Mir Dard yields nothing for this archive.** He is
introduced as "the saint of Delhi" (p. 1, folio not stated) and every fact about him in this chunk is
a Delhi fact — his father, his poetical and musical assemblies, his friendship with Mir Taqi Mir, his
place among the 'four pillars of Urdu'. **No tomb, burial place or shrine of his is mentioned anywhere
in the chunk**, and no archive row is claimed for him. The same holds for Shah Waliullah, Mazhar
Janjanan, Shah Gulshan, Muhammad Nasir Andalib, Ahmad Sirhindi, Pir Muhammad Zubair, Nizamuddin
Auliya, Mu'inuddin Chishti and Gisudaraz, all of whom sit outside this archive's territory.
