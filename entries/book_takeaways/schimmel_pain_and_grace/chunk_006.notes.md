## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Fasting, and the order's position on it.** "Mir Dard is reported to have rigorously kept his
  fast, until the 'fingernail of the crescent' opens the closed mouth of the fasting servant
  (P 91/92); he even added to the prescribed ways of fasting, as modern leaders of the Naqshbandiyya,
  too, ascribe great importance to rigid fasting, though otherwise this order does not begin the
  instruction with 'breaking the lower self' of the novice but rather with purifying and educating his
  heart" (p. 96, folio 94). **A stated difference of pedagogy between the Naqshbandiyya and other
  orders, on the record, from Schimmel.**
- **A named fast with a stated duration.** "Firaq. as several others, mentions a certain kind of
  fasting, called "dirh faga"[OCR?] [dirh faqa], which consisted of abstaining from food and
  drinking for 21 nights and days (F 121)" (p. 96, folio 94). **The name is OCR-damaged and the
  duration, "21 nights and days", is not** — see `## Doubts`.
- **Feeding guests in the mosque at the family's expense.** "a plate, dreamt of for days, was carried
  away by the father to feed the guests in the mosque instead of his family ..." (p. 96, folio 94).
  **The closest thing in this chunk to a langar economy: charitable feeding of guests at a mosque,
  funded out of the household.** The word langar does not appear — **(not stated)**.
- **Refusal of princely patronage, stated as a prohibition from God.** "and my Lord did not allow me
  to go to the doors of the princes and the sultan, and I belong to the 'benchers' (ahli suffa) of
  poverty which was the pride of our Prophet ... "1[OCR?] [I] belong to his family which God has
  purified" (p. 97, folio 95, from the Arabic paragraph at K 605). **This is the chunk's single most
  important statement for the economics of religion: a saint's income is defined by what he refuses
  to solicit.**
- **The counterweight, on the same page: a reigning emperor at his samaʿ.** "It is told that he, in
  his youth, asked Muhammad Shah, the ruler, not to disturb him by his visits (F 121) though he later
  did not mind the presence of Shah Alam II in his musical sessions" (p. 97, folio 95). **Musical
  sessions are stated as a regular institution of his, with imperial attendance, and the book marks a
  change of policy over his lifetime ("though he later did not mind").** No further detail on the
  sessions — no venue, no frequency, no personnel, no payment — **(not stated)**.
- **Sayyid descent claimed as the ground of rank.** "God is the friend of the faithful and it is He
  who made me one of the sayyids" (p. 97, folio 95); "he is closest to his ancestor, the Prophet,
  because of the high rank of poverty which brings him under the protection of God" (p. 97,
  folio 95); and of Gilani and Naqshband, "both Gilani and Naqshband were sayyids" (p. 99, folio 97).
  Schimmel's summary of the doctrine and of its exclusivity: "the highest vision and complete
  detachment from this world is granted especially to members of the family of the Prophet ... an
  approximation which can be inherited (as he thinks, exclusively) by the Prophet's offspring"
  (pp. 99–100, folios 97–98). **Hereditary charisma as an explicit, argued position, with Schimmel's
  own parenthesis marking it as Dard's claim.**
- **Poverty (faqr) as a ranked status, not a condition.** "Mir Dard proudly speaks of his poverty
  ("fagr"[OCR?] [faqr])" (p. 96, folio 94); elite poverty is "an equivalent of fana, 'annihilation,'
  and permanent remaining in God, and is 'a rank among the ranks of proximity'" (p. 96, folio 94);
  "His poverty is not bankruptcy (U 90) but rather the kingdom of absolute peace of mind; when one
  longs no more for the king's crown nor for the beggar's cap" (p. 97, folio 95).
- **Greed named as the governing vice of the political class.** "The greatest evil is hirs, 'greed', a
  word which occurs often in Dard's later prose and his poetry" (p. 98, folio 96); "Dard had more
  than enough opportunities to watch the greed of the politicians who struggled in Delhi with every
  kind of intrigue, hatred, and meanness" (p. 98, folio 96). His instruction to disciples: "so that
  the spider of negligence does not weave the warp and woof of the thought of expecting livelihood in
  your mind, and the fly of greed never sits on the gracious table of inward collection (jam' iyat)
  (D 200)" (p. 98, folio 96). **"the thought of expecting livelihood" is the target — an explicit
  discipline against soliciting income.**
- **The market in poetry, and the migration of poets out of Delhi.** "He was free from this kind of
  greed, but free also from the greed which caused most of his contemporary poets to leave Delhi for
  a safer place where they could better sell their poetry" (p. 98, folio 96). **A stated out-migration
  of literary labour from Delhi on economic grounds, with Dard as the exception.**
- **Delhi's economy as a dating argument.** "Besides, the economic and political situation in Delhi in
  the 1780's was certainly not such that a man could easily enjoy life and get fatter" (p. 96,
  folio 94). And: "for north western India it was such a nightmare that even the hope for the
  morning-light of eternity at the end of this night was no longer expressed in verses full of hope"
  (p. 103, folio 101).
- **The patronage economy of poetry, described as a system.** The poet "must live in the society—even
  though this society be, as mostly for an Oriental poet, the environment of feudal circles, of kings
  and wealthy amirs, who could pay for his praise, or pay off his satires" (p. 106, folio 104). "he
  was as much a member of the society as any artisan and craftsman, and poetry was indeed—as in our
  Middle Ages—considered an art and profession which could be studied like anything else" (p. 106,
  folio 104). The training is itemised: "not only metrics, prosody and the art of versification but
  also a number of other arts and sciences, from astrology (to cast his master's horoscope in
  ingenious verses) to medicine (to congratulate him in proper terms when he took the 'bath of
  recovering.')" (p. 106, folio 104). **Praise and satire are both priced; the poet is placed with
  the artisans; the curriculum is set by the patron's needs.**
- **Tomb-visiting defended doctrinally, with the theological warrant given.** "Dard's constant
  preoccupation with death and annihilation—so typical of classical Sufism—resulted in his high
  evaluation of a common custom in popular Islam, e.g., visiting of tombs, since 'tombs are so to
  speak the footprint of those who have gone the way of annihilation' (A 162)" (p. 102, folio 100).
  "In his "'agida"[OCR?] [ʿaqida], Dard faithfully defends the reality of the eschatological
  instrumentarium" (p. 102, folio 100).
- **The prescribed liturgy at a family grave, and its stated social effect.** "it is a sign of love
  that people should visit the tombs of their deceased family members, and recite there the fatiha
  and other efficient prayers so that God may also grant them a good end thanks to this good deed.
  For such a visit is an atonement for sins, and people may become more thoughtful about their own
  end: big people would become more tender and gracious toward the small, and the small would
  understand that their happiness lies in serving the great people (A 247)" (p. 102, folio 100).
  **A named recitation (the fatiha), an atonement function, and a frankly hierarchical social
  justification — grave-visiting as a school of deference.**
- **Tomb upkeep and death-anniversary gatherings, prescribed as duties.** "The faithful should also
  look after the tombs and keep them well and urge others to visit the tombs of the great, i.e., of
  the pious and saints, and they should gather to remember the anniversary of their death" (p. 102,
  folio 100). **This is the chunk's ʿurs material: an annual death-anniversary assembly and an
  obligation of maintenance, both stated as general duties. No shrine is named, no date is given, the
  word ʿurs is not used, and no custodian is identified** — all **(not stated)**.
- **Intercession by the entombed dead, and the sanction for neglecting them.** "Only those without
  insight do not consider such visits necessary or do not find spiritual happiness and bliss in such
  an act, for they do not see any difference between stone and brick, "cr"[OCR?] [or] between Ka'ba
  and church. How could they, then, hope to find the intercession of those whom they have neglected?
  (S 117)" (p. 102, folio 100). In verse: "The one who has no living heart / Does not come to our
  tomb ... (P 39)" (p. 102, folio 100), and "You must pass by the tomb of us, the strangers— / As
  long as we were alive, we passed through your street "(p 46)"[OCR?] [(P 46)]" (pp. 102–103,
  folios 100–101). **Visiting is framed as a reciprocal obligation between the living and the dead,
  with intercession as the return on it.**
- **Lighting a candle at a saint's tomb, named as a practice that may lapse.** "Even if the
  destroying Time does not light a candle at the tomb of those great men, still, every morning and
  evening, or rather permanently, a confidence burns in the grief of those pure people. God Almighty
  is always their companion and friend in the tomb, and the independent heart of their friends is the
  candle of their grave" (p. 104, folio 102, from S 80). **A physical tomb-lamp practice is assumed
  as the normal case, and its absence is treated as a loss to be compensated.**
- **Sohbat named as a defining Naqshbandi institution.** "To speak words of guidance and to write
  books—that was, in Dard's opinion, the noblest occupation of the wise—sohbat, spiritual
  conversation between master and disciple, and its fixation in writing belong to the most important
  aspects of the Naqshbandi tradition" (p. 114, folio 112). **The clearest statement in the chunk
  about how authority passes in this order: by conversation, and by writing that conversation down.**
- **Books as the order's reproductive institution.** "Books are, for Dard, spiritual children, and a
  gnostic without a book is like a man without children, and a work which is absurdly unconnected, is
  like a child with a bad character (K 592)" (p. 114, folio 112). "Just as children preserve the
  qualities of their ancestors, books preserve the memory of those who have written them, and others
  are inspired by them" (p. 111, folio 109); "To read somebody's books is like keeping company with
  him" (p. 111, folio 109). **Textual descent is put on the same footing as biological descent, and
  reading is equated with sohbat.**
- **The teaching circle, and the treatise named after it.** "the title of his last treatise,
  "Sham '+4 mahfil"[OCR?] [Shamʿ-i mahfil], 'Candle of the Assembly,' alludes to his own function as
  the speaker who illuminates the circle of adepts with his radiant words" (p. 111, folio 109). Dard
  on the same duty: "those who are spiritually enlightened are always prone to explain their
  experiences with the purpose of guiding people the right path (D 241)" (p. 109, folio 107); and he
  "will compose treatises of advice and exhortation" (p. 105, folio not stated).
- **Calligraphy as an institution grown from scriptural reverence.** "The reverence for the written
  word is deeply rooted in the mind of the Muslims from the days of the Prophet; out of this
  reverence the art of calligraphy developed and became one of the distinguishing features of Islamic
  culture" (p. 112, folio 110).
- **The 18th-century Indian book trade, described as derivative.** "Mir Dard was well aware of the
  uninspiring character of many of the theological, philosophical, and even mystical books which were
  studied in 18th century India; commentaries and supercommentaries, extracts and new commentaries
  were composed for the classical works, without adding anything new to the old contents" (p. 112,
  folio 110). Dard's own indictment, in the piece "Warid No. 99 with the title: 'About the usefulness
  of good books' (K 591)": "The compositions ("mu 7allafat"[OCR?] [muʾallafat]) of most of the
  saints and gnostics who have collected books and treatises and call them their 'original books'
  ("musannafdt"[OCR?] [musannafat]) are such only by name, which is used metaphorically" (p. 112,
  folio 110). **A named piece with a number — Warid No. 99 — and a technical vocabulary of book
  production: tasnif (original work) against "ta lif"[OCR?] [taʾlif] (compilation) (p. 113,
  folio 111).**
- **A merit economy that extends to copyists and readers.** "on Doomsday, the ink of the writers of
  such noble books will be equal to the blood of the martyrs; he relies here upon a hadith which was
  often quoted in the Middle Ages "im"[OCR?] [in] order to highlight the importance of studies and
  writing" (p. 113, folio 111); and "the writers and those who copy the books full of blessings and
  the readers and those who look into them are also comprised in the blessing and take part in the
  heavenly reward" allotted "to the martyr-like authors (K 591)" (p. 114, folio 112). **Four grades
  of participant — author, copyist, reader, browser — all drawing on one stock of merit.**

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

All of these are reported by Schimmel as material from Dard's biographers or from tradition, with her
own hedges preserved.

- **The biographers' ascetic wonders, with Schimmel's caution attached.** The poet is one "about whose
  ascetic practices his biographers tell amazing things" (p. 96, folio 94), and she immediately
  qualifies: "To what extent these stories are historically true, is difficult to prove: they conform
  to the traditional ascetic ideal but must contain at least some truth" (p. 96, folio 94).
- **A foreknown death, read numerologically.** "He considered the promised time of his death a
  spiritual grace, since 66 is the numerical value of the word 'Allah.'" (p. 104, folio 102) — and he
  died "at the age of sixtysix lunar years—that means, exactly at the age his father had reached"
  (p. 104, folio 102). **Attributed to Dard's own reading of his death, not to a later tradition.**
- **The overwhelming vision that ends his youth.** "In this expectation he had spent his youth until
  he was blessed with the overwhelming vision of Divine reality in which he, then, lived throughout
  his ripe age" (p. 98, folio 96).
- **Gilani's shathiyya.** "'My foot is on the neck of every saint'" — uttered "perhaps in the
  effervescence of such a state" (p. 99, folio 97). Schimmel's "perhaps" is hers.
- **The theopathic sayings as a class.** "the so-called shathiyat, theopathic expressions which some
  of the great Sufis had uttered in the state of ecstasy, proclaiming their union with God in open
  words" — "'I am the Absolute Truth' or 'Glory be to me'"; later generations held that these mystics
  "had not yet reached the perfect state of union", "but 'when the water boils it becomes silent' and
  'when the torrent reaches the sea it no longer roars'" (p. 108, folio 106).
- **The privileged hadith of the Prophet's solitude with God.** "the Prophetic tradition 'I have a
  time with God to which even Gabriel has no access' is the central truth" (p. 99, folio 97), quoted
  in shorter form inside Dard's own passage as "'I have a time with God?'" (p. 99, folio 97).
- **Books written by dictation of the Holy Ghost.** A true original work "cannot be completed unless
  the Holy Ghost (i.e., Gabriel) helps, and (then) a perfect application of 'Verily He has sent it
  down on your heart' will be reached with God's permission" (p. 113, folio 111); the inspired
  writers "only write down what God makes rain into their hearts" (A 222), which Schimmel reads as
  showing the ideal book "should be written under inspiration and is not produced by the efforts of
  human intellect" (p. 113, folio 111).
- **Intercession by neglected saints, as a threat.** "How could they, then, hope to find the
  intercession of those whom they have neglected? (S 117)" (p. 102, folio 100).
- **Life as sleep, death as waking.** "according to the Prophetic tradition 'Human beings are asleep,
  and when they die they awake'" (p. 103, folio 101).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **Schimmel dates the asceticism partly by Delhi's economy, not by hagiography.** Against the
  biographers' wonders she sets: "Besides, the economic and political situation in Delhi in the
  1780's was certainly not such that a man could easily enjoy life and get fatter" (p. 96, folio 94).
  **An economic argument used as corroborating evidence for a saint's asceticism.**
- **She places Dard's faqr in a long lineage and then in a specific one.** "contentment was the major
  quality of the ascetically minded mystics from the very beginning of Sufi history" (p. 96,
  folio 94); and his elite poverty is "as for Attar, an equivalent of fana" (p. 96, folio 94).
- **She marks Dard's hereditary exclusivism as his, with a parenthesis.** "an approximation which can
  be inherited (as he thinks, exclusively) by the Prophet's offspring" (p. 100, folio 98). **The
  "(as he thinks, exclusively)" is Schimmel distancing herself from the claim.**
- **She reads Dard as anti-wujudi, and judges the argument to fail.** "Dard strictly denies the
  possibility of essential union of man and God, which would mean that man is part of God. Man is
  created in time and can never become God even though all Divine names are manifested through him.
  This is the idea which he defends against the
  "wujudt-mystics"[OCR?] [wujudi-mystics]. Sometimes, though, his argumentation passes the limits of
  logical expression" (p. 101, folio 99). **The last sentence is her verdict, not his.**
- **She sets up the "Who am I?" material as an anticipation of modern egoism.** "His poetry touches
  the same chord and sometimes reaches expressions which we would rather expect in a modern 'poet of
  the Ego' than in a mystic who is supposed to lose his self in constant contemplation, if not union,
  of God" (p. 99, folio 97); the resolution she gives is doctrinal: "selfknowledge belongs only to
  God ... Classical Sufism had already held that the right to say 'I' belongs only to God; Dard, like
  many mystics, expands this idea" (p. 101, folio 99).
- **She claims a line of influence from Dard to Ghalib.** The dust-and-heart idea "was certainly not
  new in Islamic poetry, but has, in its Urdu expression, influenced Ghalib's famous ghazal with the
  rhyme-word "hoga in"[OCR?] [hoga], 'have been'" (p. 103, folio 101). **An influence claim, hedged
  on originality but not on transmission.**
- **She reads the bezel imagery as an Ibn Arabi allusion.** "The image of the bezel is very frequent
  in mystical poetry (certainly not without allusions to Ibn Arabi's
  "Fusis al-htkam"[OCR?] [Fusus al-hikam], 'Bezels of Wisdom')" (p. 100, folio 98).
- **The chapter's governing problem, stated as two competing maxims.** "'He who knows God becomes
  silent' (= 'his tongue becomes short') and 'He who knows God becomes talkative' (= 'his tongue
  becomes long')" (p. 105, folio not stated), with the question put directly: "how do mysticism (and,
  we may add, religious experience in general) and poetry go together? Is it possible to combine
  them, or is such a combination illicit in the eyes of both mystics and poets?" (p. 105, folio not
  stated).
- **Her answer for Dard: he resolves it by ranking the book-holder highest.** "Mir Dard has taken his
  stand in this question. More than many other pious Muslims, he is a great lover and defender of
  the art of speaking and writing" (p. 109, folio 107); Dard's own words: "The rank of such 'men of
  heart' who are, like the prophets, 'possessors of a book' is higher ...", against the view that
  silent saints hold "a special proximity to the Divine Essence" (p. 109, folio 107, K 577). "'To
  possess a book' means for Mir Dard, good Muslim as he is, the highest rank of a prophet or a saint"
  (p. 112, folio 110).
- **She rates Dard's doctrine of the Word as exceptional among mystics.** "not many among the mystics
  have expressed this essential faith in the Word as primary Divine revelation and highest possible
  manifestation of Divine power in the world as indefatigably as did Dard" (p. 110, folio 108); and
  his identification of the Quranic trust with speech, "Speech is a Divine trust which He has
  especially granted His vicegerent (K 518)", set against the readings of the trust as love, as moral
  choice, and Iqbal's as individuation (p. 110, folio 108).
- **She argues the poetry problem was framed by defenders of revelation, not by mystics.** "the
  problem of how to combine religion and poetry was discussed in the Islamic environment not from
  the viewpoint of the mystic, but rather from the viewpoint of the defender of prophetic inspiration
  and divinely revealed law" (p. 106, folio 104); and "It seems as if the Muslim mystics,
  particularly in the Persian speaking area, did not give much thought to the problem of how to
  express their experiences and feelings in poetical form. On the contrary, their output in poetry is
  much larger than that of most non-mystical writers" (p. 108, folio 106).
- **She flags one anti-poetry hadith as possibly spurious, in the text itself.** "often quoted
  his—alleged?—verdict against the poets: 'If somebody's belly were filled with pus until it bursts
  it would be better for him than that it were filled with poetry.'" (p. 107, folio 105) **The
  question mark is Schimmel's.**
- **She credits Islam with creating the book-religion category.** "One should never forget that it
  was Islam that brought into existence the distinction of book-religion and religion without
  scripture—a distinction which still forms an important category in the history of religions"
  (p. 112, folio 110).
- **Two comparative moves outside Islam, both explicit.** Persian, Turkish and Urdu poets "can be
  compared to the contemporaries of the Mughal court poets, the learned English Metaphysical Poets"
  (p. 106, folio 104); and Dard's book-and-child passage recalls "John Donne's comparison of man and
  book, in his Devotions, though in the opposite way" (p. 114, folio 112).
- **Provenance note on this chapter, printed by the author.** "A German version of this chapter was
  published in the 'Festgabe deutscher Iranisten zur 2500-Jahrfeier Irans'; the English text,
  however, does not follow the German exactly" (p. 105, folio not stated, footnote 1).
