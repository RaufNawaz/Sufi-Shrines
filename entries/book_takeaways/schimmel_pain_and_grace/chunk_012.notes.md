# schimmel_pain_and_grace — chunk 012 — pp. 214–235

**This chunk is Schimmel's verse-by-verse commentary on the ascetic Surs, and it is archive-rich for
places rather than for shrines.** It runs continuously inside the chapter whose running header reads
`SUFIS AND YOGIS IN SHAH ABDUL LATIF'S POEMS` on every recto, and it covers the close of Sur Yaman
Kalyan (chapters VI, VII, VIII, pp. 216–219, folios 216–219) and then the whole of **Sur Ramakali,
chapters I to IX** (pp. 219–235, folios 219–235), ending on the elegiac Sur Marui passage at p. 235
(folio 235). **The named pilgrimage goal of the Sur — Hinglaj — recurs four times (pp. 221, 223, 234,
folios 221, 223, 234), and Lahut, Qalat, Kabul, Kashmir and Jonagarh[OCR?] are all named as points on
the Yogis' route.**

**The folio regime holds at offset 0 for every page in this chunk.** Every one of pp. 214–235 prints
its number in the running header — verso as `214 SHAH ABDUL LATIF OF BHIT`, recto as
`SUFIS AND YOGIS IN SHAH ABDUL LATIF'S POEMS 215` — so folio = PDF page throughout, and **no page in
this chunk lacks a printed number**. There is no chapter-opening leaf here. Two recto headers are
themselves OCR-damaged in the saint's name (`LATIE'S` at p. 215, `LATIEF'S` at p. 233) while the
folio digits stay legible; that is apparatus damage only and is inventoried under `## Doubts`.

**Nothing in this chunk describes the shrine at Bhit, or any shrine building, ʿurs, custodian or
endowment anywhere.** The mapping to `bhit-bhit-shah` below rests on the principal figure — the book
calls him "Shah Abdul Latif", "Shah Latif", "Shah" and, in one verse signature, "Abdul Latif"
(p. 232, folio 232) and "Sayyid" (pp. 225, 228, folios 225, 228) — not on any statement about his
tomb.

## Practices, institutions, economy

**This chunk is about ascetic discipline, not about shrine institutions, and the negative is worth
stating plainly: there is no ʿurs, no mela, no fair, no langar, no offerings, no sajjada nashin or
gadinishin, no Auqaf, no endowment, no land, no revenue, no visitor numbers, no dispute, no state
involvement and no building date anywhere in pp. 214–235 (folios 214–235).** The institutional
material below is all about the person of the ascetic and the discipline of the order.

- **Pilgrimage to a named goal, as a repeated motif with verse numbers.** The caravan of Swamis "has
  come to Qalat (23), and from there takes the way to Hinglaj (28 ff)" (p. 221, folio 221); the
  journey to Hinglaj recurs at Ramakali III 10 (p. 223, folio 223) and at Ramakali IX 20 ff. (p. 234,
  folio 234). **A route with two named points and no dates, no numbers and no calendar.**
- **The interior pilgrimage as the author's counter-doctrine.** "Yogis make the pilgrimage, they have
  reached divine essence (uluhiyyat)" while "sitting, the head put on their knees" (p. 227,
  folio 227); "True journey is safar dar watan, interior journey" (p. 230, folio 230). **A stated
  devaluation of physical pilgrimage inside a Sur that names a pilgrimage place.**
- **Forty-day seclusion (chilla) as a measured quantity.** "Just as forty times forty days [seclusion]
  [ts precious |" (p. 215, folio 215); "The body a mosque, the mind a cell, have I made forty (days'
  seclusion) / Why not (rather) worship the One all the eight watches (24 hours)?" (p. 215,
  folio 215); Schimmel's gloss, "forty times forty days of seclusion, which are required for the
  adept" (p. 215, folio 215). **A named duration, a named alternative — the eight watches — and an
  explicit preference for continuous worship over the term of retreat.**
- **Dhikr as the continuous practice, with its physical technique.** "This theme of dhikr,
  recollection" (p. 217, folio 217); "the name of Allah, which the Yogis recollect in their
  never-ending silent dhtkr[OCR?] [dhikr]" (p. 226, folio 226); and the breath discipline — "He sees
  them 'constantly keeping their breath' on the eleventh day, which may simply mean 'silent,' but may
  also be an allusion to the habs-i dam, the long periods of keeping one's breath in medita-tion,
  which was common with the Sufis in later times and may have developed under the influence of Indian
  Yoga practices" (p. 223, folio 223). **A named technique with an attributed Indian origin — the
  chunk's one claim of practical borrowing between the traditions.**
- **The meditation posture, described twice as a rubric.** "(The position for meditation is to put the
  head on the knees)" (p. 219, folio 219); "The knees of the true saints are a prayer niche because
  they constantly put their heads on their knees in the position of medi-tation, and sanctify their
  whole body" (p. 226, folio 226).
- **The body replacing the built place of worship — repeated three times and worth recording as an
  institutional claim.** "The body a mosque, the mind a cell" (p. 215, folio 215); "The image of the
  body as a mosque is repeated in the description of the Yogis" (p. 215, folio 215); "4. Those, whose
  knees are a prayer niche, their body is a mosque, / Making the heart the place of the qibla, they
  have made circumambulation around the body" (p. 226, folio 226); "5. The friend's face ts[OCR?] the
  mihrab, the whole world a mosque" (p. 226, folio 226). **Circumambulation and qibla are relocated
  from a building to a body.**
- **Company (sohbat) as the pillar of transmission.** "The term sohbat, 'company,' may be taken as the
  technical term for the company of master and disciple, one of the pillars of Sufi education"
  (p. 228, folio 228); "The importance of right company belongs to the central teachings of Sufism"
  (p. 219, folio 219); "Who wants to learn love should learn it in the company of those who have true
  knowledge of love" (pp. 217–218, folios 217–218); "The wai emphasizes the search for the right
  company" (p. 219, folio 219). **Also the anti-institutional verse: "24. Put into your interior a
  mufti (ze. keep your conscience awake), / so that you do not need the Qadi" (p. 219, folio 219),
  glossed to "a hadith 'Ask your heart for a legal decision'". A named office — the Qadi — declared
  dispensable.**
- **Mendicancy refused: the chunk's only statement touching shrine or faqir economy, and it is a
  negative.** Shah Abdul Latif praises the Kaparis "'who have no greed, tam' , for food, ta' am (7)'"
  and "8. Dying from hunger they ask not alms from anyone" (pp. 233–234, folios 233–234). **No alms,
  no offerings, no charity institution — the ascetics are described as outside any such economy.**
- **Fasting and festival set against each other.** "Hunger is a great festivity to the saints (9): the
  contrast between hunger and the word 'id, the festival at the end of the month of fasting, is
  extremely well chosen" (p. 234, folio 234). **The only festival named in the chunk is ʿid, and it is
  named as a figure of speech, not as an observance at any place.**
- **Genealogy and lineage explicitly denied to the ascetics.** "They have no genealogy, neither father
  nor mother nor family tree (13)" (p. 234, folio 234). **Set against the descent-based authority of
  the Sindhi shrine families, this is a pointed absence; the chunk does not draw the comparison.**
- **Renunciation of ritual purity, stated with the technical term.** "they do not perform the ritual
  ablution (mash, a word which technically means the wiping of the shoes with water)" (p. 234,
  folio 234).
- **The assembly room where the Yogis performed — the one built or bounded space named in the chunk.**
  "Of special interest is the description of the dtdg[OCR?], the assembly room where the beloved Yogis
  have formerly been sitting to enthrall the audience by their songs" (p. 234, folio 234). **The chunk
  does not locate it, name its owner or say whether it survived** — **(not stated)**. See `## Doubts`
  on the reading of the term.
- **Musical performance as the mechanism of religious effect.** "the emphasis laid on the musical
  activities of the Yogis. Their whistles are all gold, and the listener is killed by the fiddle (19),
  or even by the 'knife of the fiddle' (22)" (pp. 221–222, folios 221–222); the wāʾī "speaks of various
  instruments and praises those who play them; even the murli, the snake charmers' pipe, has found a
  place here" (p. 222, folio 222); "yesterday they have played the pipe" (p. 229, folio 229). **A named
  instrument, a named performing group and a stated audience effect — but no place, occasion or
  payment** — **(not stated)**.
- **Repetition counts for prayer formulas, as the book states the rule.** "This hadith is often used to
  justify the odd numbers as used in the repetition of prayers or religious formulas, which are often
  recommended: one has to do something three, seven, 41, 99 times" (p. 233, folio 233). **Four specific
  counts printed.**
- **An Arabic proverb the chunk dates and localises — the one piece of eighteenth-century Sindhi
  circulation evidence here.** "we may see in this verse an application of a current Arabic saying
  which was very popular in 18th century Sind: al-bala' lil-wala? ka l-lahab lt? dh-dhahab[OCR?],
  'Affliction is for saintliness the same as are flames for the gold'" (p. 222, folio 222). **"very
  popular in 18th century Sind" is Schimmel's assertion and carries no source in the chunk.**
- **Concealment as a rule for saints, given a theological reason.** The Yogis "'are hid-den from the
  people'" and "This is indeed true for the veritable saints: they should be hidden like a bride, for
  God is jealous of them, and docs not want them to display too much of the secret of union" (p. 222,
  folio 222); "'concealed they wander among the people'—this last sentence forms the refrain of the
  whole Chapter II" (p. 222, folio 222). **A stated norm against public display of sanctity.**
- **The four-plane cosmology as a datable Sufi doctrine.** "The four-partition of the planes of divine
  manifestations is perfectly in accordance with the models worked out by the Sufis from the 13th
  century onward" (p. 229, folio 229), the planes being "nasut", "malakit"[OCR?], "jabarut", "lahut"
  and "hahut" (p. 229, folio 229). **A dating claim — "from the 13th century onward" — for a doctrine
  found in an eighteenth-century Sindhi text.**

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

The chunk reports these as traditions, hadith or Sufi lore; the attributions below are the book's.

- **The Yogis burn a place by ascetic heat.** They "by virtue of their tapas 'burnt Jonagarh'[OCR?]
  (Kar. I19)"[OCR?] (p. 219, folio 219), and Schimmel extends it — "the impressive description of their
  burning the forest of the lower instincts which, thus, is turned into a waste desert (Khah. III 9
  f.)" (pp. 219–220, folios 219–220).
- **Lam-alif as one letter, on a hadith the book itself calls alleged.** "lam-alif could even be called
  a single letter in an alleged hadith, on which a_ sober scholar like Qalqashandi in 15th century
  Egypt still relies" (p. 214, folio 214). **The book marks it "alleged".**
- **The dog of the Seven Sleepers sanctified by company.** "just as the dog of the Seven Sleepers
  became sanctified because he faithfully kept company with his saintly masters" (p. 219, folio 219).
- **Moses swooning and the Prophet not — the miracle of steady vision.** "'Moses fell down swooning'
  (Sura 7/139)—the groups of Yogts[OCR?] [Yogis] burn" (p. 224, folio 224), glossed "contrary to Moses
  who fainted at the sight of the Burn-ing Bush" (p. 225, folio 225), with Jamali Kanboh's verse —
  "Moses fainted at a single manifestation of the attributes; / Thou seest the manifestation of the
  Essence, and still smilest" (p. 225, folio 225).
- **Ibn Arabi's vision of the letter h.** "It was this manifestation of the divine A[OCR?] [h] which Ibn
  Arabi saw in a vision: the highest revelation of God who revealed Himself once through the Qura-nic
  word is possible not through a picture, but through the let-ter" (p. 230, folio 230).
- **Rumi's worshippers annihilated by the takbir.** "Rumi has described in a famous passage of the
  Math-nawi how those who prayed behind a great saint were, so to speak, sacrificing themselves when
  speaking the words Allahu Akbar" (p. 226, folio 226).
- **Hadith qudsi on the friends of God.** "as God had promised in a hadith qudsi: 'Verily my friends are
  under my domes,' or: 'under my cloak.'" (p. 225, folio 225).
- **The martyr of chaste love.** "Did not the Prophet say 'Who loves and remains chaste and dies, dies
  as a martyr?'" (p. 217, folio 217), placed alongside "the connection with the heroes of Kerbela"
  (p. 217, folio 217). **Kerbela is named only as a comparison, with no pilgrimage attached** —
  **(not stated)**.
- **The man in the Tigris who would not be saved.** The early-Sufi tawakkul story, told in full, ending
  "'What have I to do with wanting?'. He left his fate in the hands of God who knew whether he was to
  live or to die" (p. 231, folio 231).
- **Hallaj and the gallows as the pattern of the lover's death.** "Whosoever has put his feet in the
  arena of love has already given his head to the gallows, as the poet says with a clear reference to
  Hallaj" (p. 217, folio 217); Hallaj quoted — "I became all hearts, which ask thee to wound them?"
  (p. 217, folio 217, footnote 27 citing "Hallaj, Diwan, mug.[OCR?] Nr. 36.").

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Schimmel's governing claim for Sur Ramakali: the Hindu ascetics are the Risalo's model of
  perfection.** "The ideal of the mystical seeker is even clearer described in Sur Ramakali, where the
  Yogis are praised as the perfected human beings" (p. 219, folio 219); "The true Sanyasis are shown
  here as having reached the highest level of religious experience" (p. 225, folio 225).
- **And her judgement on which chapter matters most.** "The fifth chapter of Ramakali is without doubt
  the most inter-esting part of this Sur, just as the fifth chapter of Yaman Kalyan offered the finest
  description of the true Sufi" (p. 224, folio 224).
- **The East as the Risalo's spiritual homeland, and the genealogy she gives it.** "East is, for Shah,
  the eternal spiritual homeland, which is alluded to also in the very name of Sur Purab, 'East'. He
  follows the old spiritual tradition of the Ex Oriente lux-type" (p. 220, folio 220), traced through
  Suhrawardi Maqtul, Kabir, the Rosicrucians and the German romantics (p. 220, folio 220) — **and she
  concedes the alternative: "As much as Suhrawardi's philoso-phy was known among the Indo-Muslim
  intellectuals there may have been other sources to Shah Latif's imagery as well"** (p. 220,
  folio 220).
- **The "feudal" beloved as a borrowed topos with a local illustration.** "The imagery is derived from
  the classical image of the beloved as a cruel Turkish warrior … Such descriptions of the beloved
  'Turk' are a topos in medieval Persian poetry, and occur in the Subcontinent from the days of Amir
  Khusrau" (p. 216, folio 216); "It was easy to utilize this 'feudal' imagery in mystical context,
  since the suffering of the lover is the central topic of Sufi poetry in the Indo-Persian and Turkish
  tradition" (p. 216, folio 216).
- **A formal-history claim, stated as a first.** Ramakali II "is the first literary example of a genre
  of folksongs that had been popular in Indo-Pakistan for many centuries" (p. 222, folio 222).
- **Her caution where the text will not yield.** "16. … the Yogis from the sphere of divine power,
  jabarut, have played on the road (?)" (p. 230, folio 230); "The first line with its threefold
  repetition of the word /d/[OCR?] in the meaning of 'red', ruby', and 'Beloved' = God, is difficult to
  trans-late" (p. 231, folio 231); and the footnote "The verses 22 and 23 are grammatically not clear"
  (p. 232, folio 232, footnote 36). **Three explicit admissions of untranslatability in one Sur.**
- **Competing Hindu readings recorded rather than resolved.** On 'adam: "The Hindu interpreters explain
  it as sw7j[OCR?], 'Peace inexpres-sible'" (p. 231, folio 231); "Another ex-planation, proposed by Dr.
  Jotwani, would be that they are dumbfounded by the knowledge of the 'whirling', which would mean, in
  Hindu interpretation, the churning of the milk-ocean" (p. 232, folio 232).
- **On a theologically dangerous term she chooses the safe reading.** "Although the term 'descending',
  hall[OCR?] [hulul], in the last line seems to allude to the heretic theory of Aulul[OCR?] [hulul], the
  indwelling of God in man, it here rather means that God enters the human heart and man becomes aware
  of this presence Divine in himself" (p. 226, folio 226).
- **An alternative reading offered and then preferred on grounds of the next verse.** On the
  forty-times-forty verse: "Only by loving meditation in the seclusion the Beloved can be found, not by
  intellectual efforts. Or else: the vision of the Beloved is as precious as forty times forty days of
  seclusion … This interpretation would lead logically to the next verse" (p. 215, folio 215).
- **A philological explanation preferred over a doctrinal one.** "The word 'skele-ton' haddhi, is
  probably used for the sake of alliteration instead of the usual Arabic jifa, 'dead body,' which
  occurs in the oft-quoted hadith of the world as dead body and its seekers as dogs" (p. 230,
  folio 230).
- **The anti-bookish doctrine she traces to named predecessors.** "The study of many books is useless,
  for only one thing is necessary for man's salvation, and that is the knowledge of Allah" (p. 214,
  folio 214), with Attar — "To make the heart white is better than to make paper black" — and "according
  to Molla Jami (d. 1492), the Sufi should strive to lift the veils, not, to collect books" (p. 214,
  folio 214), and Yunus Emre in Turkey (p. 214, folio 214).

   *Sanyasis* (p. 225), "Stnats" for *Sinais* (pp. 224–225), "Rzsald"/"Raémakali" for *Risalo*/*Ramakali*
