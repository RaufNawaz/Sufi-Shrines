# schimmel_pain_and_grace — chunk 009 — pp. 154–172

**This is the Risalo chunk.** It runs from the tail of Schimmel's discussion of Shah Abdul Latif's
literary affiliations (p. 154) through a Sur-by-Sur walk-through of the whole *Risalo* (pp. 154–161),
a history of Sindhi mystical verse before him (pp. 161–162), and a long section on his language,
his Rumi borrowings and his everyday imagery (pp. 162–172). It breaks off mid-sentence at the foot
of p. 172. **The archive-relevant yield is concentrated and specific**: the Yogis' pilgrimage from
Ganji Takar to Hinglaj (p. 159), the votive cloth on trees at Sindhi shrines (p. 168), Shah Abdul
Karim of Bulri with dates and a verse count (p. 161), the Kalhoro prince's Mathnawi gift (p. 165),
and a poet named "Miyan Shah ‘Inat"[OCR?] [ʿInat] "from Nasrpur" who could answer to two archive
rows (p. 162).

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Votive cloth on trees at Sindhi shrines, stated as a practice current at the time of writing.**
  "We watch the wife of the seafaring merchant who binds pieces of cloth on sacred trees and pours
  fragrant otto and oil on the waves of the Indus to secure her husband's safe return (Sam. V 2):
  still in our day the trees near Sindhi shrines are decorated with many pieces of cloth, preferably
  red, in the shape of little flags or tiny cradles, which are attached there for the sake of a vow,
  as it is usual almost everywhere in the Islamic world" (p. 168, folio 168). **This is the chunk's
  single most usable institutional statement: a conditional vow, a physical ex-voto with its colour
  and two named shapes ("little flags or tiny cradles"), a river offering of "otto and oil", and an
  explicit claim of continuity from the poet's century to Schimmel's own. No shrine is named and no
  custodian is identified** — **(not stated)**.
- **Pilgrimage on foot by ascetics, with an origin, a destination and a stated hardship.** The Yogis
  "walk from the village of Ganji Takar near present-day Hyderabad to the sacred mountain Hinglaj (an
  ancient Shiva sanctuary in Balochistan) and suffer every possible discomfort" (p. 159, folio 159).
  **No season, calendar date, numbers or organisers are given** — **(not stated)**.
- **Sufis as the makers of the literary vernacular, and the mystical concert as the setting.** "It
  seems that in Sind, as in other parts of the Muslim world, the Sufis are responsible for the
  development of the spoken language into a literary one" (p. 161, folio 161); the first Sindhi verses
  were used "during the sessions of mystical concerts when music and dancing were in full swing",
  "shortly after 1530" (p. 161, folio 161). **A dated institutional practice — the samaʿ assembly at
  Burhanpur — producing a literary consequence.**
- **Mathnawi recitation as an organised devotional activity in Sind, across orders.** "In Sind there
  were many pious who, by their recitation of the Mathnawi, ‘kept the marketplace of union warm’, and
  Rumi's work was quoted by stern Naqshbandis as much as by members of the more emotional orders"
  (p. 165, folio 165). **Two named constituencies, "stern Naqshbandis" and "the more emotional
  orders", and a metaphor of a marketplace.**
- **A book as an instrument of princely reconciliation with a saint.** Rumi's Mathnawi "served the
  Kalhoro prince to gain back the sympathies of Shah Abdul Latif" (p. 165, folio 165). **Patronage
  through a manuscript gift; the prince is unnamed here** — **(not stated)**.
- **The Qadiri order's arrival in Sind, dated.** "a Sufi of the Qadiri order which was introduced in
  the Indus Valley in the late 15th century" (p. 162, folio 162). **The chunk's only statement of a
  silsila's introduction, and its only silsila attribution of any kind.**
- **Textual institutions: editions, manuscripts and their disagreements.** "the excellent Bombay
  edition" with "thirty chapters, Sur" (p. 154, folio 154); "the Hyderabad edition, 1974" with a
  different and longer sequence of which "Some of the chapters are not found in the other editions"
  (p. 154, folio 154, footnote 5); Trumpp's working manuscript "hopelessly confused" (p. 159,
  folio 159); Shah ‘Inat's collection "the first to be arranged in 19 Surs, and was therefore called
  a Risalo" (p. 162, folio 162); and Shah Abdul Karim's "Persian malfuzat" preserving "93 verses in
  Sindhi" (p. 161, folio 161). **A malfuzat, two printed editions, a defective manuscript and a
  19-Sur precursor — the documentary base a shrine entry would have to cite.**
- **Occupations named as the source of the Risalo's technical vocabulary** — "seafaring, fishing,
  milking, churning, spinning and hunting" (p. 164, folio 164) — plus "the spinning of cotton, one of
  the most important occupations of women" (p. 160, folio 160), camel breeding ("typical for a poet
  who lived in a camel breeding society", p. 155, folio 155), buffalo grazing (p. 156, folio 156),
  pottery (p. 171, folio 171) and the Indus boat trade (p. 171, folio 171).
- **Maritime trade as the Risalo describes it.** "The various stages of sailing to Ceylon are
  described—a true ‘passage to more than India’—from which the lucky merchants may return with pearls
  and spices. But, alas! today only a few faithful dealers in jewels are found, and most of them offer
  cheap beads instead of precious pearls" (p. 155, folio 155); the boat's "cargo should be prayers
  (Sr. VI.11) so that it may reach safely Port Aden" (p. 155, folio 155).
- **A textile economy with a named town.** "Mitthi in the Thar desert is still famous for the
  handwoven and hand-dyed fabrics, in which a dark red and brown are most conspicuous; the area
  produces also beautiful flat-woven rugs" (p. 169, folio 169, footnote 27).
- **An explicit negative, as the protocol requires: this chunk says nothing at all about ʿurs, mela,
  langar, custodianship (sajjada nashin, gaddi nashin, Auqaf or family), endowments, land grants,
  revenue, visitor numbers, disputes, state involvement, architecture or building dates at any named
  shrine.** The only offerings in the chunk are the votive cloth and river oil at p. 168 and, inside
  a poem, the gold a girl promises a crow (p. 172, folio 172); the only pilgrimage is the Yogis' walk
  to Hinglaj (p. 159, folio 159); the only tombs are Mumal's literary graveyard "outside Kak"
  (p. 172, folio 172). Nothing in this chunk describes the shrine at Bhit itself.

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

The chunk is literary, not hagiographic: the marvels in it belong to the folk romances the Surs
retell, and Schimmel presents them as tales, not as karamat of a saint.

- **Sohni's death in the river, with the substituted vessel.** "One night her sister-in-law replaces
  the jar, which she uses as sort of a swimming vest, by a vessel of unbaked clay, and she dies in the
  whirling waves" (p. 156, folio 156); the mystical reading is that "the vessel of her body breaks"
  and she "will be forever united with the friend through death" (p. 156, folio 156).
- **Sassui's dying pursuit, with the animals joining her grief.** "she runs in despair through desert
  and forests where blue snakes and other frightening creatures live (Abri II 9)"; "Even the wild
  animals and trees and birds begin to share her grief and cry with her. Eventually she perishes on
  the road" (p. 156, folio 156).
- **The snake-killing Yogis.** Snakes are "symbols of the devil from which, as Shah says elsewhere,
  the peacock-like Yogis keep aloof, or rather, kill it (Karayil II)"[OCR?] [Karaʾil] (p. 156,
  folio 156).
- **The king who gave his head to a singer.** "King Diyaj"[OCR?] "offered his head to the minstrel
  who had enchanted him by his music" (p. 158, folio 158); in the full tale the bard "was destined to
  kill him according to his horoscope" (p. 159, folio 159).
- **Mumal's lethal beauty and the graveyard it filled.** She "kills so many longing strangers that the
  tombs of her sacrifices form a whole graveyard outside Kak, (Mum. II 2)" (p. 172, folio 172).
- **Morirro and the whale.** "brave Morirro … slayed the whale that had killed his six brothers"
  (p. 158, folio 158).
- **Khizr-type immortality does not appear in this chunk**; the marvels of the birds are literary —
  the thirty birds who "are the Simurgh" (p. 167, folio 167) and the swan that "dives only into the
  clearest water in order to find a pearl" (p. 167, folio 167).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Schimmel's central claim about the Risalo's method**: Shah Latif does not retell the romances but
  freezes them — he "singles out some particularly impressive moments to develop his teachings about
  suffering and love so that even very worldly heroes and heroines are transformed completely into
  symbols of the Godsecking"[OCR?] "soul" (p. 154, folio 154). She repeats the mechanism at each Sur:
  the camel is "the symbol of the lower soul which has to be tamed" (p. 155, folio 155); Lila shows
  "how the queen has to become a slave in order to be accepted by the Lord" (p. 157, folio 157);
  Sassui "becomes for Shah the parable of the seeker on the mystical path" (p. 156, folio 156).
- **Her claim about influence and its limits.** Hindu origin is granted for "some of the stories";
  "some Balochi influence" is admitted; **Urdu is explicitly excluded** — "there was certainly no
  relation with Urdu poetry which was just emerging in northern India during Shah's lifetime";
  Panjabi parallels are "of course, conspicuous" (p. 154, folio 154). The linguistic bridge is
  named: "Many later Sindhi Sufi poets, mainly those of Baloch origin, used also the northern dialect
  of Sindhi, Siraiki, which forms the transition to Panjabi" (p. 154, folio 154).
- **Her judgement on the gender asymmetry of the Risalo, stated flatly as a criticism.** Of Mumal
  Rano: "A modern reader would expect that Rano would need purification after his mistake. But it is
  always the woman who has to suffer. And thus, Mumal had to be fitted into Shah Abdul Latif's
  general imagery, which means, that she had to represent the searching soul" (p. 172, folio 172).
- **She grants the poet "defects" in the classical sense.** "Shah Abdul Latif is not free from such
  defects either, if they can be called ‘defects’ in the sense of classical poetry", the instance
  being the oyster-and-April-rain image put in the mouth of a Thar desert woman (p. 171, folio 171).
- **She rehabilitates Trumpp partially while quoting his verdict in full.** Trumpp's introduction
  calls the Sorathi tale a picture of "Hinduism in its complete decomposition and deepest moral
  decline" and "a picture of crime and absolute obtusity, nothing else", and claims "how much even the
  strong monotheistic deism of Islam has become modified and corrupted in Sind, as in other parts of
  India, by its contact with Hinduism" (p. 158, folio 158). Schimmel's reply: "Part of Trumpp's
  criticism, however, is born from the fact that his manuscript of the Rzsdl6"[OCR?] "was hopelessly
  confused" (p. 159, folio 159) — but she concedes "even the unprejudiced reader will have some
  difficulties in appreciating the description of the generous ruler who offers his head to a bard"
  (p. 159, folio 159). **Trumpp's passage is a primary document of colonial-era hostility to Sindhi
  shrine culture and is worth citing as such.**
- **Her claim about the folk-song substratum and women's voices.** "One should never forget that his
  poetry, as that of his predecessors, grew out of folk songs; and folk poetry, particularly that sung
  by women, has a peculiar style, using many terms of endearment which are otherwise rarely found"
  (p. 162, folio 162); the waʾi of Sur Marui I is "reminiscent of some bridal songs which the recently
  married woman sings when she longs for her paternal home" (p. 163, folio 163), and the
  letter-writing topos "occurs also often in wedding songs" (p. 172, folio 172).
- **Her philological arguments about the text's difficulty**, which bear on how any quotation from the
  Risalo should be handled: the plural for the beloved ("‘the friends’ or other plural
  constructions"), compared to "the Persian use of ishan ‘they’ as honorific title for a mystic,
  especially in the Central Asian Naqshbandi tradition" (p. 162, folio 162); rhyme following "the
  actual pronunciation, not the Arabic writing system" (p. 163, folio 163); "Charming wrong
  etymologies", as "when the sailing boat, ghurab, is connected with gharib, ‘foreign, forlorn,’" and
  "andhdkar"[OCR?] [andhakar] "is spelled hindukdr"[OCR?] [hindukar]": a reminiscence of the equation
  Hindu- = black" (pp. 163–164, folios 163–164).
- **Her claim for Rumi's subcontinental reach**, which frames the Kalhoro Mathnawi gift: "Indeed,
  Rumi's influence on the Indo-Pakistani subcontinent cannot be overrated"; commentaries exist "not
  only in numerous Persian works composed during the Muslim rule (even illustrated copies of this
  ‘Quran in the Persian tongue’ were produced during the sultanate period in Delhi), but also in
  numerous indigenous idioms" (p. 164, folio 164).
- **Hindu material inside a Muslim poet's work, stated without embarrassment.** "Pre-Islamic ideas are
  natural in literature that draws upon Hindu tales; thus Sassui is sometimes called a banbhan (Abri
  I 10), a ‘Brahmin girl,’ or is praised because she is becoming a yogini (Abri III 10); the heroines
  also speak of the funeral pyre (thus Sam. I 8)" (p. 164, folio 164). Also: "From the world of Hindu
  legend the poet returns to Islamic subjects in the following two Surs" (p. 159, folio 159), and of
  Sorathi, "a cruel Hindu story is transformed into a praise of the heavenly harmony" (p. 166,
  folio 166). **For the archive this is the book's evidence that Yogi and Shiva material sits inside
  the canonical text of a Muslim shrine.**
