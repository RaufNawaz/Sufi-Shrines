# schimmel_pain_and_grace — chunk 014 — pp. 255–275

**This chunk is the end of the book's argument and the start of its apparatus.** It closes the
chapter on the Islamic background of Shah Abdul Latif's poetry (pp. 255–262), then gives in full the
book's concluding chapter, printed as `CHAPTER EIGHT` / `TO SUM UP` (p. 263, folio not stated) and
running to p. 269 (folio 269), and then opens the apparatus with a printed `APPENDIX` /
`KHWAJA MIR DARD` (p. 270, folio not stated), which runs to the end of the chunk at p. 275
(folio 275). **The apparatus here is not what the queue hoped for: it is a continuous sequence of
German verse translations of Mir Dard's poetry, with no siglum column, no abbreviations list and no
bibliography.** See `## Bibliography and apparatus` for what it does and does not resolve.

**The zero offset holds on every page in this chunk that prints a number.** Read off the running
header: p. 255 prints `255` (recto, number last), p. 256 prints `256` (verso, number first), and the
pattern is unbroken through p. 269. In the apparatus, p. 271 prints `271` (recto) and p. 272 prints
`272` (verso). **Two pages in this chunk print no number in a running head and are cited by PDF
index only**: p. 263 (the chapter-opening leaf for `CHAPTER EIGHT`) and p. 270 (the appendix-opening
leaf). **On two further pages the number survives in the OCR stream at the foot of the page rather
than in the head** — p. 273 and p. 275 — and both agree with the zero offset; they are cited as
`(p. 273, folio 273)` and `(p. 275, folio 275)`. The running header on p. 257 is itself OCR-damaged
in the poet's name; it is apparatus and is not quoted here, but the damage is inventoried under
`## Doubts` because it is the one place a folio-measurement check could be misread.

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The hereditary succession of a Sufi order, and what it costs — the single most useful
  institutional passage in the chunk.** "This happened, of course, when the leadership of an order
  became hereditary, although in these cases the original strength of the mystical experience is
  usually lost in the sequence of a few generations, as can be witnessed in many 'saintly' families.
  Still, the baraka, the blessing power, of the founder saint is venerated by the masses even in a
  late descendant." (p. 265, folio 265) **A general model: charisma decays, the veneration and
  therefore the income do not. No family, shrine or place is named to it** — **(not stated)**.
- **The pir–murid tie stated as a kinship formula.** Dard "experienced the truth of the saying 'The
  murid is the son of the shatkh'"[OCR?] [shaikh] " in a twofold way, being both son and disciple"
  (p. 265, folio 265). **"murid" and "shaikh" are the only institutional role-terms used in the
  chunk apart from "Pir" at p. 268 (folio 268).**
- **Father-to-son transmission on the mystical path as a historical trend, with an early
  counter-case.** "While in the early Chishti tradition in India the relations between the rigidly
  ascetic fathers and their children were apparently not very close, in later Sufism, the son often
  followed his father on the mystical path and continued the tradition." (p. 265, folio 265)
  Schimmel proposes it as unfinished research: "It would be worth while to study the father-son
  relation in the history of Sufism, as it would be rewarding to find out how many Sufis entered the
  Path under the influence of their pious mothers." (p. 265, folio 265)
- **A named order-succession event, given as fact.** Sultan Walad "eventually handed over the
  leadership of the order to his father's third friend, Husamuddin Chelebi" (p. 265, folio 265).
  **A succession passing outside the family line, in a chunk that otherwise argues the opposite
  tendency.**
- **A communal ritual for rain, named in Arabic and located outside the settlement.** "In order to
  implore God's help in times of drought a special ritual prayer, the salat al-tstisqa"[OCR?]
  [salat al-istisqa] ", was performed by the community outside the town" (p. 257, folio 257).
  **The only congregational rite described in the chunk, and it is not shrine-based.**
- **Grain prices, hoarding and the poor, inside the poetry.** "he prays that there may be plentiful
  rain so that the grain becomes cheaper, and the people happier; but he curses the greedy hoarding
  merchants who are unhappy when the poor are eased (IV 16)"[OCR?] (p. 258, folio 258).
- **The rain economy as the ground of the imagery.** "To compare Divine grace to rain is a common
  topic in oriental cultures, where every drop of rain meant new life for the steppes" (p. 257,
  folio 257); the villager's ploughing and the herdsman's cattle open Sur Sarang (p. 258,
  folio 258); "the cattle quenches its thirst, the buffaloes have full udders (IV 5)"[OCR?]
  (p. 258, folio 258).
- **Poverty described as a material condition, twice.** "The poor women feel cold without quilts, for
  the northern wind blows through the holes of their thatched huts, and they wait for the husband to
  cover them." (p. 259, folio 259) And of the audience: Sufism "offered them some consolation in
  their miserable lives" (p. 269, folio 269).
- **Transmission of religious poetry through labour, not through institutions.** "They could sing
  Shah Abdul Latif's poetry when ploughing or going out for fishing, picking cotton or spinning it"
  (p. 269, folio 269). **Four occupations; the vehicle is work, not a shrine assembly.**
- **Patronage as a poetic commonplace, not an observed practice** — "a court poet sees his
  macenas"[OCR?] [maecenas] " showering gifts upon his admirers" (p. 257, folio 257). Recorded only
  because it is the chunk's one reference to religious or literary patronage, and it is a simile.
- **Dard's own institutional activity, named in one clause** — "his activities as an instructor of
  poets and mystics connected him with 'the world'" (p. 267, folio 267). **No khanqah, no
  endowment, no stipend** — **(not stated)**.
- **EXPLICIT NEGATIVE, and it covers most of the protocol's institutional field.** **This chunk says

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **Rain-miracles as a genre marker for sainthood** — "in many legends the power of a hidden saint
  becomes revealed when God answers his prayer for rain" (p. 257, folio 257). Attributed to "many
  legends", with no saint named. **The chunk's only karama of the ordinary shrine kind.**
- **Schimmel's methodological verdict on hagiography, which is itself a finding about the sources the
  archive uses** — "the later biographies of the saints as composed by many a pious soul approach the
  problems only from the outside and tend to add much unhistorical material in order to wrap the
  venerated master's personality in a gown richly embroidered with legends and miracles" (p. 264,
  folio 264). **Stated as her own judgement, not reported.**
- **Baraka surviving in an unworthy descendant** — "the baraka, the blessing power, of the founder
  saint is venerated by the masses even in a late descendant" (p. 265, folio 265). Attributed to
  "the masses", and Schimmel does not endorse the power, only the veneration.
- **The hadith qudsi of Ahmad without the mim** — God spoke: "Ana Ahmad bila mim, 'Iam Ahmad without
  the letter m, e.g., Ahad, One.'" (p. 260, folio 260). With the numerological gloss: "The letter m
  with the numerical value of 40, represents in later Sufi interpretation the forty stages between
  man and God" (p. 260, folio 260). **Schimmel dates the interpretation: Rumi "does not yet mention
  this interpretation" (p. 260, folio 260).**
- **The Prophet's two Prophetic traditions used as mystical warrants** — the vision "'two bows lengths
  or closer'" during which "'his eye did not rove'" (p. 261, folio 261), and "'My eyes are asleep,
  but my heart is awake'" (p. 261, folio 261). Attributed to the Prophet and cited to the Risalo.
- **The Prophet's tent and crowd on doomsday, from the poetry** — "Other poems speak of Muhammad's
  tent on doomsday (Sar. II wa i)"[OCR?] ", or of the whole crowd of people that will cling to him:
  / In the front will be Mustafa, in the rear the people will march (BS. Il wa’? 2)"[OCR?] (p. 256,
  folio 256).
- **Ali as the lion killing the dragon** — "as he is sometimes depicted in popular painting as lion
  who kills the dragon 'lower soul,' (Bil. Til 1, 4)"[OCR?] (p. 261, folio 261). **A statement about
  devotional images, not about a shrine.**
- **All creation weeping for Husain** — Sur Kedaro "tells in heartrending short cries the tragic fate
  of the beloved grandson of the Prophet about whom all creation wept" (p. 262, folio 262).
- **Dard's progressive identification with the spirits of prophets** — "We can follow him almost step
  by step on his way through the 'intoxication' of early days and the identification with the spirits
  of various prophets until he becomes the true representative of Muhammad the Prophet on earth,
  called by God … to reform the rotten Muslim society" (p. 264, folio 264). Reported from Dard's own
  autobiography; Schimmel notes he lays "emphasis … upon the mystical graces by which God
  distinguished him before everyone else" (p. 265, folio 265).
- **Ibn Arabi invested with supernatural powers** — "the way in which he was invested with
  supernatural powers" (p. 264, folio 264). Attributed to Ibn Arabi's own remarks.

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The book's central answer on the Risalo's religion: it is Islamic, and strongly so.** "the Islamic
  contents of the Risalo, which, it scems to me, are undeniably strong"; "the God of the Rusald"[OCR?]
  [Risalo] " is a living and loving God; not a philosophical or mystical principle, but rather the
  Creator, the Merciful and Majestic" (p. 262, folio 262). **This is the load-bearing claim of the
  whole part, and it is put as the chapter's last sentence.**
- **But intercession is not the whole of it.** "The intercession of the Prophet as praised in Sur
  Sdrang"[OCR?] [Sarang] " occurs time and again in the Risald"[OCR?] [Risalo] ". However, it would
  be wrong to assume that only this aspect of the Prophet is described in Shah's verse. The Sindhi
  poet is well conversant with the high mystical tradition" (p. 260, folio 260). **Schimmel argues
  against reading the poet as a purely popular devotionalist.**
- **A methodological warning the archive should carry forward: do not read the poetry as
  topography.** "Shah's geographical remarks should not always be taken at face value" (p. 256,
  folio 256); and "The final songs, in fact, do not always exactly sum up the contents of the chapter
  which they follow" (p. 256, folio 256). **This is a direct caution against using Risalo place names
  as itinerary evidence.**
- **Schimmel contests a Sindhi interpretive tradition by name.** "I wonder if one can interpret, as
  M.U. Daudpota does, the verse … as pertaining to the Prophet; the learned scholar thinks, as many
  interpreters may have thought as well, that the haqiga muhammadtyya"[OCR?] [haqiqa muhammadiyya]
  " … serves as a kind of mediator" (pp. 260–261, folios 260–261); her verdict: "the reference seems
  to apply to God" (p. 261, folio 261). **She places Girhori and Dard on the interpreters' side and
  Shah Abdul Latif outside it (p. 261, folio 261) — a claim that separates Shah from the Naqshbandi
  reading of the Prophet.**
- **Two priority claims for Shah Abdul Latif**, both hedged: the first marthiya in Sindhi ("probably",
  p. 261, folio 261; "it seems that Shah Abdul Latif is the first", p. 262, folio 262), and the first
  extended 'geographical' description of divine mercy ("found here for the first time in a longer
  description", p. 259, folio 259).
- **A priority claim for Urdu, attributed to another figure** — the first marthiya in Urdu is credited
  to Muhammad-Quli Qutbshah, d. 1620 (p. 261, folio 261), and its classical form to Sauda, d. 1781
  (p. 262, folio 262). **A dated genre history the consolidator can use.**
- **Marthiya as political consolation.** The Lucknow poets' threnodies described Kerbela's sufferings
  "thus consoling the Indian Muslims who were smarting under different afflictions, brought upon them
  by the rule of the infidels" (p. 262, folio 262). **A functional account of devotional poetry as
  compensation under colonial rule; Schimmel's own framing.**
- **The two poets as a typology, which is the book's final thesis.** "Mir Dard and Shah Abdul Latif
  constitute two complementary aspects of Islamic mysticism and mystical poetry, in general and on
  the Indian scene particularly. One is the urban, sophisticated, and highly intelligent trend as it
  had developed in the areas under Persian cultural influence … The other one is the rural, simple
  and unassuming Sufism which uses the whole repertoire of inherited forms with great ease without
  burdening the images with too many theoretical interpretations." (p. 269, folio 269)
- **And the thesis that they nonetheless converge.** "our two 18th century mystics in India start from
  the same premises and reach the same goal: they are firm believers in God's unity and in the
  greatness of the Prophet. They use almost the same favorite Quranic verses and Prophetic
  traditions" (p. 269, folio 269) — named as "'Whithersoever ye turn there is the Face of God'
  (Sura 2/109)"[OCR?] and "'Who knows himself knows his Lord.'" (p. 269, folio 269).
- **Both are placed in a shared historical moment, twice.** "Both Mir Dard and Shah Abdul Latif are
  poets of the twilight of Muslim India. Their approach to the experience of mystical love and gnosis
  is as different as are the sunsets in their native provinces, and still, they very much belong to
  the same spiritual country." (p. 263, folio not stated)
- **The book's account of eighteenth-century Muslim self-doubt, which is its political argument.**
  Dard is "a perfect example of 18th century high-cultured Indian Muslims: confronted with new
  developments on the political and social level, they had to learn the bitter truth that they were
  no longer the rulers of a vast and glorious empire, but rather had to fight against heavy odds and
  to lead unsuccessful wars against interior and exterior enemies under whose blows the glory of
  Mughal India was crushed" (p. 266, folio 266); and "Mir Dard seems to be the first to express in
  clear statements the problem of self-identification which was to trouble the Muslims (not only of
  India) for the next two centuries" (p. 266, folio 266). **Schimmel says flatly that "Shah Abdul
  Latif apparently had no problems of this kind" (p. 267, folio 267)** — a claim that Sind's
  eighteenth-century crisis did not register in his verse.
- **Rain as divine mercy, traced as a genealogy of the image.** From the Quran ("Sura 13/13 f.; cf.
  30/24"[OCR?]; "Sura 30/48"[OCR?]) through Rumi's spring poems to the etymological point: "Rain is
  called in some of the Islamic languages, particularly in Turkish and Persian, rahmat, 'mercy,'
  derived from Sura 27/64"[OCR?] ", where the clouds are described as messengers of God's mercy"
  (pp. 257, folios 257). **The chain Quran → rahmat → the Prophet as "mercy for the worlds" → Sur
  Sarang is the chunk's tightest piece of argument.**
- **A comparative judgement that the nature-observation is unusual for India.** "Shah Abdul Latif's
  talent to develop religious topics out of a fine realistic observation of nature is, to some
  extent, reminiscent of Maulana Rumi, but is a rare quality among the urban mystical poets of India"
  (p. 260, folio 260). And the German term is used for it: his rain descriptions "are closer to
  Erlebnislyrtk"[OCR?] [Erlebnislyrik] " in the Western sense than any other part of Shah Abdul
  Latif's poetry. And yet, the whole imagery serves mainly to extol the Prophet" (p. 256, folio 256).
- **Shah placed against Kabir and against Christian pietism.** Kabir's final goal "is complete
  annihilation as final salvation" whereas "Shah's mysticism of suffering contains, in itself, a more
  positive attitude" (p. 267, folio 267); "His imagery, and his way of describing the path of the
  lover is often reminiscent of Christian pietist hymns in the same 18th century" (p. 268,
  folio 268). **Schimmel's formulation of the doctrinal difference: he "experiences the positive
  aspect of fand"[OCR?] [fana] ", 'annihilation,' e.g., baqd"[OCR?] [baqa] ", remaining in God"
  (p. 268, folio 268).**
- **The inward turn as the shared doctrine, with the technical vocabulary given.** "man's true journey
  is not fulfilled by walking on the outward narrow path, tariga"[OCR?] [tariqa] ", as it branches off
  from the highroad called shari‘a, 'divine law,' but by the pilgrimage into one's own heart. Dard may
  call this journey with the technical term safar dar watan" (p. 263, folio not stated). **A claim
  that shari'a, tariqa and the interior path are ordered, and that physical pilgrimage is not the
  point — relevant to any reading of shrine visiting in these poets.**
- **Dard's tauhid, and servantship as the goal.** "Dard's work is an explanation of what he calls
  tauhid-1 muhammadi"[OCR?] [tauhid-i muhammadi] ", the true profession of faith according to the
  Prophet's teaching … Servantship is the highest goal man can aspire to reach so that he may become
  similar to 'God's slave' par excellence, the Prophet." (p. 268, folio 268)
- **A judgement on Dard's relation to his father's book, stated as the author's own.** "the modern
  student of Sufism has to admit that Dard definitely improved his father's work. Even though it is
  not exactly a pleasure to read 'Ilm ul-kitab, it is certainly more concise and realistic than the
  diffuse Na@la-yi ‘Andalib"[OCR?] [Nala-yi 'Andalib] (p. 266, folio 266). **A blunt evaluative
  verdict on both books.**
- **Mystical autobiography as a rare genre — a source-critical claim.** "Mystical autobiographies of
  this extent are rare in Islam. To gain a picture of a saint's development we have to rely in the
  early period upon the scattered sayings of the Sufis as they were collected in more or less correct
  form by their followers" (p. 264, folio 264). **Directly relevant to the archive's problem of
  sources for saints' lives.**
- **Dard's intellectualism as a class and regional symptom.** "His tendency to explain every spiritual
  experience, every movement of the soul, in a highly technicized language is typical of a person who
  grew up in the North Indian urban mystical tradition and tried to intellectually control his inner
  life as a follower of the Naqshbandi school" (p. 266, folio 266). And the anti-Shia point: "his
  attitude may have been acceptable to his Shia compatriots who otherwise disliked the anti-Shia of
  the Nagshbandtyya mujaddidiya"[OCR?] [Naqshbandiyya mujaddidiyya] (p. 268, folio 268).
- **The Yogis as a case for the historian of religion.** Shah's "attempt to explain, in Quranic terms,
  the high rank of the Yogis is revealing for the historian of religion" (p. 267, folio 267); the
  Yogis are said to "represent" the true lovers of God in the Prophet's vision verse (p. 261,
  folio 261). **The chunk's only material on non-Muslim religious specialists in the Risalo.**
- **A persistence claim about Sufi families in the Subcontinent.** Dard and Shah "seem to represent a
  tendency that can still be found among the Sufi families of the Subcontinent, as it existed also in
  early centuries in Iran" (p. 269, folio 269). **"still" places the observation at the time of
  writing; no family and no shrine is named** — **(not stated)**.

**Archive mapping doubts.**

- **`dargah-of-khwaja-muhammad-zaman-luari-sharif` — mapped, but on a name form the archive does not
  use.** The book prints "Muhammad Zaman Lanwari" (p. 261, folio 261); the archive row reads
  "Khwaja Muhammad Zaman (Sultan al-Aulia)" at "Luari Sharif". `Lanwari` / `Luari` is the whole of
  the evidence — the chunk gives no place, no date, no title and no order for him, and calls him only
  "his master". **This is not obvious OCR damage (it does not fit any measured class), so I have not
  flagged the word itself; it may be Schimmel's own transliteration. A human should confirm the
  identification before this mapping is used in an entry.**
- **`shrine-of-makhdoom-abdul-rahim-girhori` — mapped, but he sits on the unruled line.** "Abdur
  Rahim Girhori" appears in this chunk only as the holder of an interpretation, and a work called
  "Kalam-i Girhori" is cited in the footnote — which is close to the "named purely as an author or
  literary reference" case that earlier chunks of this book raised and that is awaiting Rauf's
  ruling. **I mapped him because the chunk gives him more than authorship: an order
  ("Nagqshbandi"[OCR?]), a named master, and a relative date ("Shah's younger contemporary").** If
  Rauf rules that an interpretive or authorial mention does not take an id, move this bullet to
  "Other saints, sites and events" — nothing else in the notes depends on it.
- **`mazar-of-bulleh-shah` — deliberately NOT mapped.** "Bullhé Shah" appears once, in footnote 15
  (p. 260, folio 260), purely as a poet whose Kulliyat uses the same hadith. No shrine, tomb, place,
  date or observance. Under the interim convention he is listed under "Other saints, sites and
  events". **If the ruling reverses, the row he takes is `mazar-of-bulleh-shah`.**
- **Bare place names, deliberately NOT mapped**, under the interim convention. The ones that touch an
  archive row if the ruling reverses: **"the Thar Parkar"** (p. 259, folio 259) would touch
  `nagarparkar-jain-temples-nagarparkar-cultural-landscape`, `churrio-jabal-durga-mata-temple` and
  `gori-temple-gori-jo-mandar`, all of which sit in that region — and it could not be resolved
  between them from this chunk in any case, because the chunk names no site in it. **"Delhi"**
  (p. 259, folio 259 and pp. 260, 262, 266) would touch nothing in the archive, which has no Delhi
  row. **"Kech"** (p. 268, folio 268), **"Girnar"**, **"Jaisalmer"**, **"Bikaner"** (p. 259,
  folio 259) and **"Kerbela"** (pp. 261, 262, 268) touch no row. None of these has a shrine, tomb,
  saint, ʿurs or custodian attached to it in this chunk.
- **No appearance of Hinglaj in this chunk**, so the empty-id index defect is not triggered here.
- **None of the known gaps appears** — Makli Hill, the Chaukandi tombs and Ganji Takar are not named
  anywhere in pp. 255–275. **No new candidate row arises from this chunk either**: every figure and
  site in it is either already an archive row, a non-Subcontinental comparandum, or a bare place
  name. The one borderline case is Mir Dard himself, who is the book's co-subject and has no archive
  row; **this chunk gives no tomb, shrine or burial place for him**, so there is nothing to build a
  row on.
- **"Shah Inat" / "Mian Shah Inat" / "Shah Inayat of Jhok" does not appear in this chunk**, so the
  unresolved `dargah-roza-sufi-shah-inayat-shaheed` / `shrine-of-shah-inayat-qadiri` question is not
  advanced here.

**Folio doubts.** p. 263 and p. 270 print no number in a running head (chapter- and

its final letters, which is the one place in the chunk where a folio check could be misread; the
number itself, `257`, is clean.** Headers are apparatus and are not quoted as content anywhere above.

**One judgement flagged for the consolidator.** Schimmel reports at p. 268 (folio 268) that Shah
Abdul Latif "is related to have called himself 'something between Sunni and Shii Islam'" — reported
tradition, not an attested statement, and she supplies the technical label
"tash-ayyu‘ hasan"[OCR?] herself. **The same tradition is reported, in different words and with a
different source, in the other book on this saint already in the queue. The consolidator should set
the two reports side by side rather than merging them, because neither book gives a primary source
for it.**
