# schimmel_pain_and_grace — chunk 010 — pp. 173–192

**This chunk is literary-critical, not documentary, and the archive must be told so plainly.** It
carries the close of the chapter whose running header reads `SHAH ABDUL LATIF'S LIFE AND TEACHING`
(pp. 173–189) and the opening of the next, printed as `CHAPTER SIX / SUFIS AND YOGIS IN SHAH ABDUL
LATIF'S POETRY` (p. 190, folio not stated). **Not one date, place of burial, tomb, ʿurs, custodian
or endowment belonging to Shah Abdul Latif appears in these twenty pages.** What the chunk does
supply, and what makes it archive-relevant in spite of that, is (a) the book's flat statement that
Shah Abdul Latif belonged to no silsila (p. 191, folio 191), (b) the book's flat statement that the
Risalo names no order and no shrine (p. 190, folio not stated), (c) the folk-romance cycles tied to
named places — Bhambhore, Kech, Mount Pabb, Malir, Kinjhar lake, Mount Hinglaj — and (d) two
one-line mentions of archive saints, Shah Inayat of Jhok and Sachal Sarmast.

**Folio: offset 0 holds unbroken.** Every page from p. 173 to p. 192 prints its number in the
running header in the pattern the run measured — verso number-first (`174 SHAH ABDUL LATIF OF
BHIT`), recto number-last (`SHAH ABDUL LATIE'S[OCR?] [LATIF'S] LIFE AND TEACHING 173`) — and in
every case the printed number equals the PDF index. **The single exception is p. 190, the
chapter-opening leaf, which prints no number and is cited below as `(p. 190, folio not stated)`.**
Note also that the running header changes wording at the chapter break, from `... POETRY` in the
chapter title on p. 190 to `... POEMS` in the header on p. 191; both are apparatus and neither is
quoted here as content.

**"Bhit" does not occur in the body text of this chunk at all** — it appears only inside the verso
running header, which is apparatus. Recorded as an explicit negative because a consolidator
scanning for the shrine village will otherwise assume it is here.

## Practices, institutions, economy

**This chunk has almost no institutional content, and that absence is itself the finding.** There is
**no ʿurs, no mela, no langar, no offering, no sajjada nashin or gadinishin, no Auqaf, no
endowment, no land grant, no revenue figure, no visitor figure, no dispute, no state involvement and
no architecture or building date anywhere in pp. 173–192** — all **(not stated)**. What follows is
everything the chunk does carry that touches practice or social structure.

- **The Risalo's silence on orders and sanctuaries, stated as a fact about the text.** "None of the
  great tarigas[OCR?] [tariqas] is mentioned, nor any of the sanctuaries to which the inhabitants of
  Sind flocked at many occasions" (p. 190, folio not stated). **The subordinate clause is itself
  evidence that flocking to sanctuaries was normal in the poet's Sind — the author asserts the
  practice while denying its presence in the poems.**
- **The pir's methods described as physically harsh, from classical Sufism and then from the
  poetry.** "We know from classical Sufism that the Pir's methods were often harsh and even cruel,
  particularly for those who were used to a more tender way of education" (p. 180, folio 180). The
  poetic images that follow are occupational and, taken together, are a small inventory of Sindhi
  trades: the guide as washerman "who mistreats the laundry with his stick (BS II 11), a sight well
  known to every inhabitant of India and Pakis-tan"; as "a dyer, who first casts the dirty soul into
  a decolorization chemical to clean it from spots of sin and colors of attachment, and then puts it
  into the vat with the true color (Kohy. IV 10)"; as "a blacksmith who puts the iron ore into the
  furnace to melt and purify it, then mistreats it with a hammer, bellows, and cold water until it
  becomes fine and useful steel (YK I 17)" (pp. 180–181, folios 180–181); and as "the elixir by
  whose touch iron is transformed into gold (Pirbh. I 25)" (p. 181, folio 181).
- **Caste and social stratum, stated as fact about Sind and not only about the poem.** "The Mohana
  traditionally belonged to the lowest social strata in Sind, and their women were noted for beauty
  and not too strict morals" (p. 175, folio 175); Nuri is "the fisher maid (mohani)" "belonging to
  the low gandri caste" (p. 175, folio 175) and "an insignificant, low caste girl" (p. 176,
  folio 176). **The book's one ethnographic generalisation about a named Sindhi community in this
  chunk.** The rank mismatches of the other cycles are at p. 174 (folio 174).
- **Ritual purity as the frame for the pir's work.** "For 'without the beloved, Sohni is unclean,'
  and his company is for her like a ritual bath that takes away impurity (najdsat[OCR?] [najasat])
  from a woman (Sohni. Mut. 2,3)" (p. 180, folio 180). **The comparison is to women's ritual bathing;
  the chunk names no site or tank for it** — **(not stated)**.
- **Sacrifice at shrines, named as a live comparison.** "all lovers are sacrificed like animals
  slaughtered at the festival of pilgrimage", and "who would not think of the Blut-und-Wunden poetry
  of the Christian Church, if one does not prefer to turn to sacrifices at Indian temples and
  shrines?" (p. 182, folio 182). **The festival is not named and no temple or shrine is named** —
  **(not stated)** — but the author asserts animal sacrifice as a practice at "Indian temples and
  shrines" in general. **This is the chunk's only sentence about ritual offering at a shrine of any
  kind.**
- **Fasting and feast as a comparative pair.** "for them, hunger is a feast, just as for ordinary
  people fastbreaking is the feast par excellence" (p. 181, folio 181). **The fast is not named** —
  **(not stated)**.
- **Prostration to the husband reported as a religiously sanctioned domestic practice, and as a
  living interpretive habit.** "the tradition that it is almost women's duty to prostrate themselves
  before their husbands, a tradition which modern Sindhi interpreters of the Risal6[OCR?] [Risalo]
  also quote with great delight" (p. 173, folio 173). **An observation about present-day Sindhi
  commentary, undated and unattributed to any named commentator** — **(not stated)**.

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

**No karamat of Shah Abdul Latif is told in this chunk** — **(not stated)**. The marvellous material
here belongs to others and is reported as legend, with the author marking it as such.

- **Hallaj's saying reinterpreted against legend.** "Did he not ecstatically say: Ana 'l-haqq, '1
  am the absolute Truth'? But this word (which was not, as legend has it, an enthusiastic cry but
  the quintessence of his thinking and feeling) made him also the model case of the lover who
  divulged the secret of love and therefore had to be punished" (p. 183, folio 183). **The author
  explicitly rejects the legendary framing.**
- **Shibli's dream of Hallaj's death** — "in which God proclaimed that He, or His beauty, would be
  the bloodmoney for those martyred on the Path" (p. 185, folio 185); attributed to Shibli, and
  applied to Sassui, who "is consoled with these words (Abri XI 6, Hus. IX 20 f)".
- **Hallaj cast bound into the ocean.** The description "was used by Hallaj to depict Satan's sad
  fate and was then applied by later generations to himself", and appears "in an almost verbatim
  translation in Asa IV 38-39": "God cast him into the ocean, the hands tied-- / And said to him:
  Beware lest you become wet!" (p. 185, folio 185).
- **The Princess with the fish-smell.** "The story of Nurt[OCR?] [Nuri] Tamachi as told in
  Kaéméd[OCR?] [Kamod] may contain a reminiscence of the Indian tale of the Princess with the
  fish-smell" (p. 175, folio 175), footnoted to Eliade. Offered as one of two readings, the other
  being the Persian "King and Beggar" topos.
- **The king's throne as kibriya.** "Nowhere else in the Risalo ts[OCR?] [is] the character of the
  king as representative of God expressed as clearly as in Kamdd[OCR?] [Kamod], where the Quranic
  words about Allah are applied to him, whose throne is kibrvyd[OCR?] [kibriya], 'Divine Grandeur'"
  (p. 175, folio 175). **A sacralisation of kingship attributed to the poem, not to a shrine.**
- **The falcon returning to the Sultan's fist** — given as what "Rumi would say" (p. 176,
  folio 176), footnoted to "Diwdan-i[OCR?] [Diwan-i] Kabir … Nr. 1353, Mathnawi II 1131 ff."
- **The blind men and the elephant** — "as he says in the story of the blind and the elephant (Asa
  ITI[OCR?] [Asa III] 26 ff); but the leaders, endowed with insight (basirat) see the elephant in
  reality, ft'/-hagigat[OCR?] [fi'l-haqiqat]" (p. 186, folio 186).
- **Rumi's Sufi who would not look at the spring gardens** — "(it was originally Rabi'a
  al- Adawiyya) who, lost in meditation, did not look at the gardens in spring, for 'the garden and
  flowers are inside'" (p. 186, folio 186).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **The chunk's central thesis: Indian bridal mysticism is a distinct regional type, and Shah Abdul
  Latif is its perfect case.** The argument is built by elimination across three traditions — Arabic
  ("In the poetical language of early Sufis of the Arabic speaking world, such as Dhu 'n-Nun and
  Hallaj, there is no trace of worldly love"), Persian ("the two lovers are generally imagined as
  male; the allusions to the fresh 'green' down of the young moon-like friend and similar features
  leave no doubt about the sex of the beloved object"), and Persian-Turkish Zulaikha material — then
  landed: "In the Indian tradition, however, the soul is always represented as a seeking female, and
  the beloved, or husband, represents God; the seeker-poet identifies himself with the virahini, the
  longing young wife, of the folk-songs" (p. 173, folio 173). Conclusion: "this crude symbolism is
  refined in the Indian tradition, of which Shah Abdul Latif's poems offer a perfect example"
  (p. 173, folio 173).
- **A genealogy of the type, with a geography and a date range.** The imagery "permeates likewise the
  Muslim poetry in the native languages, beginning from early Ismaili poetry in Sind and Gujrat to
  the first examples of mystical Urdu writings in the 15th and 16th centuries in Southern India, and
  it became the central topic of Sindhi and Panjabi folk mysticism" (p. 173, folio 173). **Ismaili
  Sind is placed at the head of the line — a claim about religious history in the archive's
  geography.**
- **The grammatical explanation of the female soul, given and then deprecated.** "in early ascetic
  Sufism the lower soul, nafs, is usually seen if not as an animal then as a woman; since the noun
  'nafs' is feminine, this equation was easy, and belongs to the favorite images even in high
  Persian poetry. But this crude symbolism is refined in the Indian tradition" (p. 173, folio 173).
- **The heroines read as stages of one soul, with Quranic anchors.** nafs ammara "which incites to
  evil (Sura 12/53)" (p. 181, folio 181); nafs lawwama, "'blaming,' (Sura 75/2)" for Lila (p. 187,
  folio 187); "nafs mutma?inna[OCR?] [mutma'inna], 'Soul at peace' (Sura 89/27)" for Nuri (p. 175,
  folio 175) and for the camel (p. 178, folio 178). **Schimmel's summary of the whole Risalo:** "Shah
  Abdul Latif dwells particularly on one aspect of this age-old story, e.g., the transformation and
  sublimation of the lower soul, the nafs ammara, into the higher soul, mutma ?inna[OCR?]
  [mutma'inna]. All his heroines undergo this process" (p. 187, folio 187).
- **Hallaj as the decisive foreign influence on Sindhi poetry, argued from a dated journey.** "we may
  assume that the image of Hallaj, who had wandered through the Indus Valley in 905, has impressed
  the poets of this part of the Islamic world even more than elsewhere" (p. 183, folio 183);
  "wherever in this poetry the gallows are mentioned, we can see the shadow of Hallaj" (p. 184,
  folio 184). **Note the hedge, "we may assume" — this is offered as inference, not documentation.**
- **A graded comparison of Shah Abdul Latif with Sachal Sarmast on outspokenness**, with Shah Latif
  the more cautious of the two (pp. 184–185, folios 184–185). **The most citable comparative claim
  in the chunk for the relation between two archive shrines' poets.**
- **The parallel with German Pietism and Christian passion devotion, pressed hard and repeatedly.**
  "Sometimes one feels like reading a Sindhi translation of Shah's contemporary, the Christian
  mystic G. Tersteegen's (1697-1769) hymn" (p. 181, folio 181); "it would not be difficult to bring
  close parallels to Shah's 'path of suffering,' as it had been developed by the Sufis in Hallaj's
  succession, from Christian hymns about the ¢tmitatio[OCR?] [imitatio] Christt[OCR?] [Christi] in
  meckness[OCR?] [meekness] and glad acceptance of pain" (p. 182, folio 182); "who would not think
  of the Blut-und-Wunden poetry of the Christian Church" (p. 182, folio 182). **A methodological
  stance worth flagging: the author reads Sindhi folk mysticism through European devotional
  categories throughout this chunk.**
- **Shah Abdul Latif placed in an Indo-Persian court-poetic milieu for his cruelty imagery.** The
  blunt-knife conceit is "an idea often repeated by the court poets of India during the 17th and 18th
  centuries, who indulged in descriptions of this kind, as remained very much alive even during the
  19th century in Ghalib's Urdu verse" (p. 182, folio 182).
- **A claim about rural versus courtly reception.** The mirror image "is frequent in traditional
  Persian mystical poetry, and belongs to Dard's central symbols, it was apparently less popular in
  a rural environment" (p. 187, folio 187). **The author explains a Sindhi absence by the audience's
  social setting — hedged with "apparently".**
- **The individualism thesis, stated flatly** (p. 187, folio 187, quoted in full above). **Set
  against the uwaisi statement at p. 191 (folio 191) and the "no tariqas, no sanctuaries" statement
  at p. 190 (folio not stated), these three passages make one consistent argument: the Risalo is not
  an institutional document.**
