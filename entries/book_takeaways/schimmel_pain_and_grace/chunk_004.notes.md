## Practices, institutions, economy

**Explicit negative first, because the chunk is unusually clean on this point: there is no ʿurs, no
mela, no langar, no offering or nazrana, no sajjada nashin or gadinishin, no Auqaf or state
administration, no endowment, waqf, inam or land grant, no revenue, no visitor numbers, no shrine
dispute, no architecture and no building date anywhere in pp. 60–78 (folios 58–76)** — all **(not
stated)**. **There is also no samaʿ, no music, no qawwali and no dance**: the one phrase that looks
like it, "sing tunes which ought not to be sung (K 284)", is glossed by the book itself as
divulgence of doctrine, not as performance — "as the shari'a prohibits 'real' wine, thus the tariga
forbids the 'spiritual wine,' e.g., the divulgence of the subtleties of mystical union (K 183)"
(p. 76, folio 74). What the chunk does carry is the internal institutional apparatus of a
Naqshbandi house in Delhi, and it carries it densely.

- **Succession to the father's office, stated as the start of Dard's working life.** "From the day
  Mir Dard succeeded his father he had been deeply involved in writing books for guidance and in
  teaching people" (p. 62, folio 60). **No installation ceremony, no date, no investiture object
  and no rival claimant** — **(not stated)**.
- **The office of shaikh as an office, and its scarcity.** "The shatkh[OCR?] [shaikh] is in his group
  like the prophet in his people (K 60)", quoted by Dard as a hadith, and "the office of
  shatkh[OCR?] [shaikh] is vicegerency of the prophecy, and thus not available to every saint
  (K 451)" (p. 72, folio 70). Dard's own claim to it: "in one of his last notes (S 255) he praises
  God who has led him and put him on the seat of conveying the message and spiritual directorship
  which he has adorned with clearest explanation and finest description" (p. 63, folio 61).
- **Initiation is compulsory; there is no unmediated access.** "for initiation is the basis for
  successful progress in the tariga[OCR?] [tarīqa]. … Direct illumination is impossible—either it
  happens after the initiation or the mystic, after his first experience, seeks a master to guide
  him further (K 638)" (p. 72, folio 70). And the principle behind it, in Dard's words: "God's
  custom is to bring spiritual bounty from the living to the living" (p. 72, folio 70).
- **Complete surrender of the murid to the shaikh, in the classical formula.** "As moderate a mystic
  as Ghazzali had stressed the importance of complete surrender to the shazkh[OCR?] [shaikh] so that
  the murid would be like the corpse in the hand of the washerman" (p. 72, folio 70). Schimmel's
  verdict: "Dard underlines the importance of the pir more than his predecessors" (p. 72, folio 70).
- **Sohbat as the working mechanism of Naqshbandi instruction — and books as its substitute.** "One
  of the requirements of the path, especially among the Naqshbandis, is sohbat, the conversation
  with the master, which guarantees not only a perpetual control of the disciple's progress but the
  constant flow of spiritual energy from the shatkh[OCR?] [shaikh] to the murid. For according to
  the hadith, 'Religion consists in giving good advice.' In rare cases the perusal of the master's
  books can be substituted for oral sohbat (K 499; cf. 431)." (p. 75, folio 73) **The substitution
  of text for presence is stated by the book as an exception, "In rare cases".**
- **Veneration of the pir as the test of membership.** "the lowest of the Muhammadans, has been
  created for the sake of venerating his pir, and for the relation of faith. Thus, everybody who
  venerates the pir and has faith is my soul and the beloved of my heart (N 117)" (p. 73, folio 71).
  And the rank Dard claims above all others: "higher than he who has reached baga' in the Prophet is
  he who has found baqd?[OCR?] [baqāʾ] in the shatkh[OCR?] [shaikh], for he has completed the whole
  circle" (p. 73, folio 71), with the polemic that follows: "But we have surpassed this rank!
  (K 116)" (p. 73, folio 71).
- **The assembly (mahfil) as the venue of the teaching, and the emperor in it.** "we did not keep
  quiet in the assemblies that tongue of resplendent explanation of the candle of the assembly, and
  we have delineated the complete rules of the lofty Muhammadan Path" (pp. 62–63, folios 60–61,
  quoting D 336); "a large number of people have made the sweet remembrance of my words the
  sweetmeat of their assemblies" (p. 62, folio 60, from A 311, dated by Schimmel "after
  1193/1779"); "Sham' mahfil is, as its name indicates, meant for the edification of the assembly
  of readers and listeners" (p. 70, folio 68); and **Shah Alam II "used to attend some of the
  meetings in Dard's house"** (p. 71, folio 69). **This last is the chunk's only statement of royal
  attendance, and no gift, grant or payment accompanies it** — **(not stated)**.
- **A household naming rule extended to the servants.** "Muhammad Nasir himself had, as Firaq tells,
  suggested that every member of his house should bear a name connected with 'Nasir' for the sake
  of blessing; thus, even the servants were called Nasir Quli or Nasir Bakhsh. It was believed that
  by this sacred name the members of the family would always remain victorious and successful; every
  letter which began with the formula Huwa an-Nasir, 'He is the Victorious' was supposed to yield a
  positive result for the writer (F 92)." (p. 64, folio 62) **The only mention of household servants
  in the chunk, and the only statement of a family-wide ritual practice.**
- **"Religious shopkeepers": the book's report of Dard's attack on the shaikhs of his own day.**
  "Dervishdom does not consist of astrology and geomancy (K 447), but many of the contemporary
  shaikhs are nothing but 'religious shopkeepers' (K 445)." (p. 76, folio 74) **This is the chunk's
  sharpest statement on the market in sanctity, and it is Dard's, reported by Schimmel.**
- **Tashayyukh — false claim to spiritual leadership — named as a recognised abuse.** "the dangerous
  mode of tashayyukh, i.e., to behave like a self-made mystical leader which he blames often in
  others, is, as he asserts, not found in him (S 244)" (p. 63, folio 61). With it, professional
  rivalry: "apparently he, too, had to suffer from envious colleagues (A 335)", and he "knows the
  'scorpion-natured hypocrites' too well to be worried by their behavior" (p. 63, folio 61).
- **Livelihood against trust in God — the chunk's one piece of household economics.** "Perfect trust
  in God, tawakkul, is basic for the disciple, but if he experiences mental discomfort during his
  attempt to rely absolutely upon God's gifts, he had better earn his livelihood and follow the
  Prophet's advice: / Bind your camel's knees notwithstanding your trust in God (K 241, 243)"
  (pp. 74–75, folios 72–73). **The disciple is told to work rather than to depend.**
- **Detailed regulation of consumption.** "Eating, drinking, dressing are subject to detailed rules
  (K 521ff.; cf. the classical handbooks on Sufi education)" (p. 74, folio 72). **The rules
  themselves are not reproduced** — **(not stated)**.
- **Ritual obligation kept at the centre.** "Dard, following his father's fundamentalism, sees in the
  targa[OCR?] [tarīqa] Muhammadtyya nothing but the perfect reliance upon the Quran and Prophetic
  tradition. He stresses, thus, the importance of the five pillars of faith; among them, prayer and
  fasting are, as for most mystics, central." (p. 74, folio 72)
- **The book trade: copyists, length and binding.** "Although, as he complains, the copyists and
  writers of his time do not like to write long books he urges them to keep the two short books in
  one binding and not to separate them from each other (p. 71-72)" (p. 69, folio 67). **A named
  constraint imposed on an author by the scribal market.**
- **Circulation of a shaikh's sayings by note-taking.** "in the Ah, the author complains of those
  with hard hearts whose cold thoughts are not impressed by his sighs, though many people want to
  note them down and relate them to their friends (A 180)" (p. 70, folio 68). And Dard's own
  complaint of fame: "After becoming the everyday talk of élite and ordinary people and becoming
  famous in the world, I have become for myself a strange pain; I have become a headache (cf. P 54)"
  (p. 62, folio 60).
- **The physical scale and dating of the œuvre, as an institution of textual production.** The 111
  "wdriddét"[OCR?] [wāridāt] "developed into the 648 big pages of the 'J/lm ul-kitdb[OCR?] ['Ilm
  ul-kitāb] (called by some admirers 'the greatest ocean of Sufism')"; "he wrote it down almost 12
  years after his father's death, when he was about fifty lunar years old (K 473)" (p. 64,
  folio 62). "Their number is 111:—110 is the numerical value of the name Ali, his ancestor, and the
  odd number is added 'since God is an odd number and loves odd number' (K 97)" (pp. 63–64,
  folios 61–62). **The four later books each contain exactly 341 paragraphs**: "the Nala-yi dard has
  341 nala 'lamentations' the Ah-i sard comprises 341 ah, 'sighs,' the Dard-i dil, 341 dard,
  'pains,' and the Sham' mahfil, 341 nur, 'light.' The number 341 was chosen because it is the
  numerical value of the word Nasir, so that Dard's mystical connection with his father and guide is
  once more established." (p. 69, folio 67) **Dates, all dual hijri/CE as printed**: Nala-yi dard
  begun "1190/1776 (N 3)" (p. 68, folio 66); Ah-i sard "1193/1779" (p. 69, folio 67); the last two
  books begun "in 1195/1781" (p. 70, folio 68); Sham' mahfil finished "in the beginning of 1199/the
  last days of 1784" (p. 71, folio 69). "Dard's Persian Diwan was collected from the poetry
  scattered in his prose work, and is not an independent book" (p. 71, folio 69).
- **The invocation that opens every chapter, and the merit attached to reading the name.** Each
  chapter of the 'Ilm ul-kitab "begins with the invocation 'Yd Nasir,'[OCR?] [Yā Nāsir] alluding to
  his father's name", "so that everybody who reads the books and risdlas[OCR?] [risālas] and
  corapositions[OCR?] [compositions] and writings should first see this famous name … and all the
  sins and rebellions of us, the sinners, become wiped out and forgiven through the spiritual power
  of his name (S 17)" (p. 64, folio 62). **An intercessory benefit attached to a printed name — the
  closest thing in the chunk to a shrine-style transaction.**

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

The chunk reports these as Dard's own doctrine or as beliefs of his house; Schimmel does not
endorse them and does not call any of them karamat.

- **Miracles affirmed as real but treated as something to be hidden.** "Miracles will occur to the
  wayfarer; they are real and true, but / miracles are the menstruation of men (K 449), / for the
  friends of a king will never divulge the secrets he entrusts to them." (p. 76, folio 74)
- **Four kinds of unveiling (kashf), with their fruits.** "(1) kashf kauni, as the result of
  asceticism, pious actions and purification of the lower soul; it becomes manifest in dreams and
  clairvoyance (2) kashf iléhi[OCR?] [kashf ilāhī], a fruit of constant worship and polishing of the
  heart, which results in the knowledge of the world of spirits and cardiognosy. (3) kashf
  'agli[OCR?] [ʿaqlī], which can be reached by polishing the moral faculties, and can be experienced
  by the philosophers as well. (4) kashf imani is the fruit of perfect faith … then he is blessed by
  direct divine addresses; he talks with the angels, meets the spirits of the prophets, sees the
  Night of Might and the blessings of Ramadan in human form in the 'dalam al-mithal[OCR?]
  [ʿālam al-mithāl] ... (K 443)" (pp. 75–76, folios 73–74).
- **Occult technique disowned.** "Dervishdom does not consist of astrology and geomancy (K 447)"
  (p. 76, folio 74).
- **Uwais al-Qarani initiated by the Prophet at a distance** (p. 72, folio 70) — quoted above.
- **The Prophet living, present and helping after death.** "the holy essence and the purified spirit
  of the most eminent Repository of Guidance, the Shelter of Prophethood, whose firm religion is
  living and standing till doomsday, is in every moment and every wink with us, the Muhammadans, in
  a presence without how, and alive in spiritual life"; "inspite of having left this perishable
  world and having entered the world of spirits he is conversant with the state of this world and is
  all the time present and viewing and preserving and helping (ndsir)[OCR?] [nāsir]" (p. 67,
  folio 65). **He "eyewitnessed the world of barzakh and resurrection"** (p. 67, folio 65).
- **The efficacy of the name an-Nasir as a formula.** "every letter which began with the formula Huwa
  an-Nasir, 'He is the Victorious' was supposed to yield a positive result for the writer (F 92)";
  "It was believed that by this sacred name the members of the family would always remain victorious
  and successful" (p. 64, folio 62).
- **A coincidence the book itself marks as strange.** Dard "had just reached the age of 66 lunar
  years and died shortly afterwards—strangely enough, his Wariddt[OCR?] [Wāridāt], too, had been
  completed a few days before his father passed away at the age of 66 lunar years" (p. 71,
  folio 69).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **Schimmel's central claim about this part of the book: Dard's doctrine of `baqāʾ fi 'sh-shaikh` is
  an innovation produced by one unrepeatable relationship.** "I think we can not blame those mystics
  who would not acknowledge the new spiritual rank of bagd? fi'sh-shatkh[OCR?] [baqāʾ fi
  'sh-shaikh] which Mir Dard defended, for this was born out of his own personal experience in his
  relation to his father, a relation which nobody else could possibly imitate or experience."
  (p. 73, folio 71) She sets it out as a reversal of the classical order — the threefold annihilation
  "fand? fi ?sh-shaikh"[OCR?] [fanāʾ fi 'sh-shaikh], "fand? j2 r-rasil"[OCR?] [fanāʾ fi 'r-rasūl]
  and "fana' fi Allah" culminating in "baga' billah" (p. 72, folio 70), with Dard's own words:
  "Fané?[OCR?] [Fanāʾ] in God is directed toward God, and baqdé?[OCR?] [baqāʾ] in God is directed
  toward creation, and one calls the most perfect wayfarer him who comes down more than others …
  for the end is the return to the beginning ... (K 115)" (p. 72, folio 70).
- **She classes Dard's theology as orthodox and scholastic, not ecstatic.** "Dard was a thinker in
  the Hanafi Ash'ari tradition—his 'aqida (p. 72 f.) fits exactly into the well-known type of
  orthodox Muslim creeds … He follows also the nominalist way of the Ash' arite school by declaring
  that good is what God has declared to be good." (p. 66, folio 64) And on his chapter on love:
  "love is for him nothing but a state somewhat more comprehensive than faith; the definition of
  love as 'obedience,' typical of the early orthodox stance, is maintained" (pp. 66–67,
  folios 64–65).
- **She reads the tariqa Muhammadiyya as the organising claim of Dard's life.** "this tariga
  Muhammadtyya is, indeed, the center of Dard's preaching and life. In his view, kalam, philosophy
  and mystical knowledge are merely servants of the tariga Muhammadtyya, which is higher and subtler
  than anything else (K 157)" (p. 73, folio 71), argued from "the classical tripartition
  shari'a-tarigqa- hagigqa"[OCR?] [sharīʿa–tarīqa–haqīqa] (p. 73, folio 71).
- **She marks the exclusivism of Dard's position as unusual even among the Muhammadiyya movements.**
  After naming the Idrisiya-Sanusiyya and Ahmad Brelwi she writes "however, it will be difficult to
  find a statement like Dard's words: / Humanity consists of Muhammadanism, / so that someone who is
  not a 'Muhammadan' remains outside the fold of the faithful; he is, so to speak, not a human being
  at all, but intrinsically worse than an animal" (pp. 73–74, folios 71–72).
- **She contrasts wahdat al-wujud and wahdat ash-shuhud through Dard's own comparison of two medical
  systems.** "the followers of the wahdat ash-shuhid[OCR?] [ash-shuhūd] are like the physicians who
  follow the Greek medical system, and usually help people, or at least do not harm them, whereas
  the other group is comparable to Indian physicians who may be able to heal a man immediately but
  also to kill him quickly—as most of them do indeed, as he sarcastically adds (K 183 f.)" (p. 77,
  folio 75). And Dard's objection to popular monism: he "accuses in harsh words the broad masses who
  repeat the idea that 'Everything is He'—every little Yogi has the mouth full of these ideas, and
  here hes[OCR?] [lies] the major danger: everyone who talks about wahdat al-wujid[OCR?] [al-wujūd]
  without having reached the experience (which is, in itself, legitimate) is a heretic" (p. 77,
  folio 75). Dard's preferred formula is "the good Naqshbandi formula Hama az ost, 'Everything is
  from Him'" (p. 77, folio 75). Schimmel adds her own aside: "The intellectualist approach of many
  interpreters of Ibn Arabi and his doctrine is indeed a problem for the historian of Sufism even in
  our day" (p. 77, folio 75).
- **Her literary judgements, which a consolidator should keep as judgements.** Of the 'Ilm ul-kitab:
  "The Western reader will certainly enjoy the original waridat; their long winded commentaries in
  sometimes abstruse formulations are less enjoyable" (p. 65, folio 63); "The 'Ilm ul-kitab is a
  strange book" (p. 64, folio 62). Of the later books: "the Ah-1 sard[OCR?] [Āh-i sard] seems to me
  even more perfect as a work of art" (p. 70, folio 68); "The last two rtsalas[OCR?] [risālas] are
  less attractive than the first two; the sentences become more involved, the paragraphs longer and
  more tedious" (p. 70, folio 68); and on Dard's imagery, "the unusual way of applying highly
  poetical images to very abstract contents puzzles the reader more than is the case in earlier
  treatises" (p. 71, folio 69). She calls the Nala "a kind of spiritual diary" (p. 68, folio 66) and
  the first two books "a kind of devotional book interspersed with verse" (p. 69, folio 67).
- **She reads Dard's later work as softening.** "Dard, this stern defender of the difference of
  'abd, the human servant, and rabb, the Divine Lord; who so often has attacked the representatives
  of sukr, 'intoxication' appears in these later works more mellowed, and softened by the fire of
  Divine love. He now uses sometimes the symbolism of the lover and beloved, and even speaks of his
  own 'intoxication.'" (p. 70, folio 68)
- **She notes the near-total absence of politics from Dard's writing as a problem needing
  explanation** — "only a few words are devoted to the world and what is in it. This is all the more
  surprising as the time after Nasir Andalib's death was characterized by major events in the
  political field" (p. 71, folio 69) — and supplies the Delhi chronology quoted above in its place.
- **She treats the pun on the father's name as a deliberate conflation.** "It is indeed a fine pun on
  the name of his father who is here, as in a number of similar cases, brought into such a close
  union with the Prophet that the reader is confused as to whom the reference belongs." (p. 68,
  folio 66)
- **A dating argument from material culture, in a footnote.** The hourglass image "must have become
  fashionable shortly before 1600", evidenced from Ibrahim Adilshah of Bijapur, the Jihangir-period
  court poets and the Bichitr painting "dated 1625" (p. 60, folio 58, footnote 1).
