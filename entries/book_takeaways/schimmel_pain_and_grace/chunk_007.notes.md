## Practices, institutions, economy

**No ʿurs, no mela, no langar, no offerings, no custodianship, no sajjada nashin, no Auqaf, no
endowment, no land, no revenue, no visitor figures, no building dates and no architecture appear
anywhere in this chunk.** That is the explicit negative. What the chunk does contain is an economy
of religious speech — who may be paid for words, and on what terms — plus the internal discipline of
a Naqshbandi household. Both are recorded here because the project wants them.

- **Poetry for hire, refused, and the refusal itemised.** Dard: "I have not been drowned and consumed
  myself in this work with careful observances, and have never praised or satirized anybody, and have
  not said anything according to the order of a person ..." (p. 115, folio 113, cited "(K 91)").
  **Three refusals: panegyric, satire, and composition to commission.**
- **The conditions under which poetry is licit, stated as a rule.** "Poetry is not such a perfection
  that a human being should make it his profession and be proud of it, but it is one of the human
  skills, under the condition that it is not conditioned by searching gifts and running about from
  place to place, and that one does not utter panegyrics and satires for the sake of worldly reward,
  for then it is a sort of beggary and implies greed and meanness of the lower soul ..." (p. 115,
  folio 113, cited "(N 28)"). **This is the chunk's central economic statement: itinerant
  gift-seeking and reward-panegyric are named as "beggary"; the word is Dard's, as the book has it.**
  Schimmel dates the statement to "a Jater period of his life" (p. 115, folio 113) — no year —
  **(not stated)**.
- **The counter-motive Dard does admit to, and Schimmel's reading of it.** "towards the end of his
  life, the idea of a certain fame which he had acquired during his lifetime and which, as he hoped,
  would also continue after his death, can be sensed behind some of his most modest words" (p. 126,
  folio 124). Against which Dard condemns exactly this in others: poetry "of sensual passion which
  appears with the intention of becoming famous and the wish that one's memory should remain alive
  even after one's death, and similar other worldly intentions, like the word of the poets and the
  scholars" (p. 126, folio 124, cited "(D 3)").
- **Teaching poetry as an institution run from a private house.** "he alludes here to the poetical
  instruction which he gave throughout his life to a large number of Urdu poets" (p. 116,
  folio 114); "we must suppose that, in this field, he followed exactly the classical methods of
  instructing the disciples in the rules of poetry and correcting their verses" (p. 126, folio 124);
  and the institutional claim outright: "In his house the theoretical foundations of Urdu poetry were
  established in the mid 18th century" (p. 126, folio 124). **A dated institution — "the mid
  18th century" — with a physical seat, "his house", and no other location named.**
- **The pedagogical technique, in Dard's own joke.** "as he smilingly says in an Urdu ghazal that ‘it
  would be possible to write another ghazal if you can change the rhyme-word'—and then continues
  with the next ghazal with a slight variation of the rhyme" (p. 126, folio 124).
- **Instructional verse as a mass-production genre, and the market it served.** "Dard alludes here to
  the popular way of composing long rhyming pieces about the miracles of the prophets, the details of
  faith and religious law, and about more or less abstruse problems of mystical theology for the sake
  of instruction, just as a number of rhymed treatises on logic and Arabic grammar have served
  through centuries for the instruction of students" (p. 124, folio 122). Dard's own verdict on such
  work: "it is useful because it is easy to remember and to memorize, but it has nothing in common
  with poetry but rhyme and meter ..." (p. 124, folio 122, cited "(K 288)").
- **A stratified market in religious song, and Dard's contempt for its lower end.** The book states
  it as a social fact: "the passage shows also how proud Dard was of his high education and art, and
  how little he thought of the numerous mystics of low birth who lived all around the country and
  attracted people with their simple songs" (p. 123, folio 121). **Competition for an audience
  between a learned urban shaikh and unlettered rural singers, stated in the author's own words.**
- **Inspiration ranked by degree — a hierarchy of authority expressed as a hierarchy of speech.**
  "those who live still in the ‘proximity of sainthood' are in a somewhat shadelike state, whereas
  those who have reached the ‘proximity of prophetic perfections' are blessed with inspirations
  without mediation of any visible means; but the inspirations of those who have attained to the
  ‘Pure Muhammadiyya' are beyond the seeing and understanding even of the aforementioned groups of
  saints" (p. 123, folio 121). And the operative rule: "at least the inspiration of saints with
  knowledge and practice in poetry will be more true and better by degrees than the inspirations of
  unscholarly and unpoetical saints" (p. 123, folio 121, cited "(K 149)"). **Education is made a
  determinant of spiritual rank — a claim with obvious institutional consequences.**
- **The named source-text for the doctrine.** "Dard in his Warid No. 16, ‘Inspired word about the
  expression of topics concerning God's Unity in poetical metaphors'" (p. 122, folio 120, cited
  "(K 148 f.)"); and "Warid No. 110" for the dhikr technique (p. 133, folio 131, cited "(K 637)").
- **Congregational obligation set as a household rule by the father.** "One of the rules for the
  ‘Pure Muhammadans' as set by his father, was to pray the five ritual prayers in the company of the
  faithful" (p. 130, folio 128).
- **Supererogatory night prayer, named and evaluated.** "the nightly prayer (tahajjud) was always
  cherished by the mystics who found here the opportunity of conversing with the Divine beloved in
  the solitude of the night" — "being recommended in the Quran, though not a binding duty on the
  believers" (p. 130, folio 128). Dard's own practice: "he sighs also that every night he hopes to
  spend all night in prayers" and "we beat the head every evening on the stone of striving" (p. 130,
  folio 128, cited "(D 232)").
- **Petitionary prayer as a transaction, and the one transactional practice the chunk names — and
  forbids.** "It is wrong, says Dard, to recite the "fattha"[OCR?] [fatiha] for worldly purposes;
  when reciting this prayer with uplifted hands one should do it without any hope for an answer or
  being granted a wish" (p. 131, folio 129, cited "(S 220)"). **This is the closest the chunk comes
  to shrine-side ritual economics: the fatiha recited for a worldly object, explicitly disallowed.**
- **The rule against abandoning petition, and the rank it protects.** "To ask nothing from God and
  not to pray is a zeal which is out of place but which occurs sometimes with the noble because they
  suspect that they have understood the Truth in their way; but it is not fitting for the rank of
  servantship" (p. 131, folio 129, cited "(A 92)"). And the mean between the two: "the most perfect
  are free from both ways and are without will in praying and not-praying ..." (p. 131, folio 129).
- **What Dard's people actually pray for, counted by frequency.** "It should be noted that the number
  of prayers for forgiveness, in which he recurs upon God's eternal grace, are more frequently found
  than contemplative and philosophical prayers … their goal is not union, as for the ‘intoxicated'
  mystics, but forgiveness and, at the most, vision of eternal beauty" (p. 132, folio 130). **A
  distributional claim about a devotional corpus, the only quantified statement in the chunk.**
- **Forms of address catalogued from the prayers.** "from the frequently used ‘O my God!'
  "(2lahi)"[OCR?] [ilahi] to expressions like ‘Absolute Existence!'"; "poetical words ‘Sun of the Sky
  of Lordliness' and ‘Cupbearer of the goblet of the Grace of Existence' (A 119)"; "God as ‘Coverer
  of faults' stands beside the ‘Sacred Valley of Absolute Existence' (A 123) or ‘Spring of the
  Rosegarden of Divine transcendence (tanzih)' (A 192)"; "Yet the simple "il@hi"[OCR?] [ilahi] or
  khudawanda, ‘O Lord!' are much important in the whole picture of his prayer-life" (p. 130,
  folio 128).
- **Dhikr as the central act, and its Naqshbandi technique set out in full.** "for the mystics the
  central act of worship was "dAzkr"[OCR?] [dhikr], the constant recollection of God" (p. 132,
  folio 130); "Constant "dhzkr"[OCR?] [dhikr] is for him the very center of religious life … the
  safest way that leads to presence or vision" (p. 132, folio 130, cited "(K 624 f.)"). The technique:
  "Faithful to the Naqshbandi tradition Dard teaches the dhikr of the five
  "lata if"[OCR?] [lataʾif] (K 112), the spiritual centers of man" — "the
  "dhzkr qgalbi"[OCR?] [dhikr qalbi], located in the heart, at the left side of the breast,
  pronounced in love and longing, the dhikr ruhi, performed at the right side of the breast in
  quietude and tranquillity, the "dhikr strri"[OCR?] [dhikr sirri], pronounced in intimacy, close to
  the left side of the breast, the dhikr khafawi, performed close to the right corner of the breast
  and connected with absence and extinction of the self, and the
  "dhtkr akhfawi"[OCR?] [dhikr akhfawi], in the center of the breast, which is the sign of
  annihilation and consummation", then "extended to the brain in perfect contentment (dhikr nafsi,
  connected with the "nafs gaddisa"[OCR?] [nafs qaddisa], the sanctified soul)" and finally
  permeating "the whole being ("haqgiga insantyya"[OCR?] [haqiqa insaniyya]), body and soul … this
  is the dhikr sultani" (p. 133, folio 131). **Seven named stages of dhikr, each with a bodily
  location. The chunk gives no lodge, no khanqah and no assembly at which this was performed** —
  **(not stated)**.
- **The prescribed daily office of the Divine names.** "one of the most important aspects of
  religious life for the Sincere Muhammadan is to recite the Divine names every morning and evening,
  to meditate upon their meanings, and, in case of need, invoke the name which is fitting for that
  peculiar occasion" (p. 132, folio 130, cited "(K 201)").
- **The formulae taught, and their escalation.** "when one says
  "/é iléha illad Alléh"[OCR?] [la ilaha illa Allah], There is no deity save God, one should think in
  one's heart "ld ma‘ bida illd Allah"[OCR?] [la maʿbuda illa Allah], There is no object of worship
  save Him, and "/é magqstda illd Allah"[OCR?] [la maqsuda illa Allah], There is no object of desire
  save Him" (p. 132, folio 130, cited "(K 624)"); and further, "the innermost of the innermost is
  "la maujida illaé Allah"[OCR?] [la maujuda illa Allah], There is nothing existent save God"
  (p. 133, folio 131, cited "(K 132)").
- **Training, not reading, as the qualification — with a professional analogy.** "constant practice
  is required to attain the highest stage of dhikr, which is a medicine for the soul—the physician,
  too, does not learn from books but has to practice his art" (p. 133, folio 131).
- **The visualisation exercise Andalib taught, described as a graded progression.** "(The adept) sees
  the blessed figure of the word Allah in the color of light written on the tablet of his heart and
  the mirror of his imagination … whenever he finds himself in the middle of the rank of alif and
  "/ém"[OCR?] [lam], he must proceed and take his place between the two lems, and then walk from
  there, and bring himself between the lem and the ha; and with high ambition he leaves this place
  too and sees himself in the middle of the ringlet of "the had"[OCR?] [the ha]" (p. 133, folio 131).
  **The passage breaks off at the chunk boundary.**

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

**No karamat are told of Dard in this chunk, and no miracle at any tomb or shrine.** What the chunk
carries instead are claims of inspiration and one reported charisma, all attributed as the book
attributes them.

- **Dard's own claim that his writing is dictated, not composed.** "when I want to write anything of
  the things that come into my heart, a bewilderment of the Essence and Majesty and an incredible
  overpowering appears, so that the tongue of the pen becomes bound and the hand of writing remains
  back; but when I sit idly and quietly the endless rain of Divine realities and gnostic problems,
  fresh and obscure, rains from the cloud of mercy upon my heart" (p. 119, folio 117, cited
  "(D 341)"). The book dates it: "Shortly before his death, when the treatise Dard-i dil was just
  completed" (p. 119, folio 117) — **no year is given** — **(not stated)**. Schimmel's own frame:
  "it shows his perfect passivity in receiving the message" (p. 120, folio 118).
- **Automatic composition, reported of Ibn al-Farid and of Rumi.** Ibn al-Farid "composed his highly
  sophisticated poetry in the state of ‘absence'" (p. 120, folio 118); Rumi wrote only when the
  beloved's vision moved him (p. 120, folio 118). Both attributed as things "it is said".
- **Living inspiration witnessed by the author among Turkish mystics** — the "wāridāt or doghush,
  ‘born things,' which overcame them without their own knowledge" (p. 122, folio 120). **Reported as
  the author's own eyewitness observation, not as tradition.**
- **Dard's claim that the whole of his body professes faith.** "each limb, each hair of his is a
  witness of God's creative power and God's Unity"; "from every root of my hair it made grow an
  index-finger for beckoning to the direction of the One Real", the term being
  "angusht-1 shahadat"[OCR?] [angusht-i shahadat], containing "shahddat"[OCR?] [shahādat],
  "‘profession of faith'" (p. 119, folio 117, cited "(S 3)" and "(P 63)"). And: "Like the candle,
  each limb of mine has been transformed into a tongue" (p. 119, folio 117, cited "(P 41)").
- **The claim that inspired poetry is inimitable, on the model of the Quran's own challenge.**
  "nobody can imitate the poetry and words of those divinely inspired masters, and ‘the flag "Bring
  a Sura like it" (Sura 2/21) appears at every place, where such a word is recited ...'" — on which
  Schimmel comments: "This is, indeed, a rather daring comparison of inspired poetry with the words
  of the Holy Quran!" (p. 125, folio 123, cited "(D 5)").
- **The hadith of the two fingers, used to license the poet-as-pen image.** "reminding the pious
  reader of the well known Prophetic saying that man is between two fingers of the Merciful who turns
  him wherever He wants" (p. 121, folio 119).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Schimmel's governing claim for this chapter: Dard denied being a poet while making the highest
  possible claim for poetry.** He "had to declare once more that he was not a ‘poet' and rather
  followed the Divine word of ‘We did not teach him poetry' (Sura 36/69)" (p. 116, folio 114, cited
  "(D 138)"), yet "poetry is a high art" and those who slight it act "out of ignorance" (p. 115,
  folio 113, cited "(S 327)"). **The book treats the contradiction as the chapter's subject, not as
  a defect.**
- **Dard's two-class theory of poetry, reconstructed by Schimmel from four passages.** Neither
  instructional versifiers nor pure technicians qualify: the saints' didactic verse is "mostly
  deprived of charm and poetical measure and form" (p. 124, folio 122, cited "(K 288)"); and "The
  poems which come from the master poets who are not acquainted with the Reality and which show
  purely perfection of poetical technique—those are mostly completely made of metaphors and are bare
  of any reality, (they are) only exaggerations and lies ..." (p. 124, folio 122). Only the
  "‘disciples of the Merciful'" — "‘those who are annihilated in the whirlpool of annihilation in
  God'" (p. 123, folio 121, cited "(A 222)") — hold both graces. **Schimmel endorses part of this:
  "Dard is perfectly right with his criticism" (p. 124, folio 122); "here we will agree with Dard"
  (p. 123, folio 121).**
- **The word-and-deed test applied to both classes.** "For both groups, but even more for the pure
  poets, the Quranic dictum about those ‘who say what they do not do' is valid, and it seems not
  without inner reason that Dard comes back to the statement about such a discrepancy several times:
  the acts of the saints go farther than their poetical expression, and the way of the poets does not
  conform to their sayings" (p. 124, folio 122, cited "(A 53)" and "(A 51, cf. 292)").
- **Schimmel's biographical inference about Dard's enemies.** "Dard with such words alludes to his own
  situation—he, the mystic and poet, had probably to suffer from the dislike of the dry scholars of
  his age and perhaps also from some of his colleagues in the Naqshbandi order" (p. 125, folio 123).
  **Marked as inference by "probably" and "perhaps"; the chunk names no opponent** — **(not
  stated)**.
- **The three sources of Dard's boldest claims, named as a combination.** "the combination of his
  perfect identification with his father-"shatkh"[OCR?] [shaikh], his close relationship with the
  Prophet in his quality of being ‘the first of the Muhammadans' and the fact that he is a true
  sayyid, sometimes leads him to rather daring comparisons and claims" (p. 117, folio 115).
  **Descent ("a true sayyid"), succession to the father, and a special relation to the Prophet — the
  three authority claims, stated together in one sentence.**
- **Schimmel's judgement that Dard suppressed the worldly half of his own career.** "Dard, however,
  never mentions the ‘worldly' side of his poetical activity which is considered, by modern critics,
  perhaps the most important aspect of his life" (p. 126, folio 124); "His mystical theories do not
  explain his way of teaching poetry" (p. 126, folio 124). **An argument from silence, made
  explicit.**
- **A comparative claim about inspiration and social class.** "it is, however, natural that the forms
  and the contents of these inspired poems should vary according to the spiritual rank and the
  educational and cultural background of the mystic concerned" — the Adana blacksmith against "Mir
  Dard's inspired poems and prose-pieces" which "bear everywhere the stamp of his highly
  sophisticated education" (p. 122, folio 120). **Schimmel also notes that Dard wrote classical
  Persian poetry "sometimes, without inspiration" (p. 122, folio 120) — an admission that undercuts
  the inspiration doctrine.**
- **The formal consequence Schimmel draws from inspiration: disorder.** "The uncontrollable
  experience of inspiration is the reason why Dard's compositions are without any visible outward
  order—both the Waridat and the Lamentations came without intention, ‘so that even sometimes poems
  came in inversed order and earlier paragraphs contain the ends of a ghazal, or vice versa'"
  (p. 121, folio 119, cited "(N 337)"). **A textual-criticism point with direct bearing on how the
  works can be used as evidence.**
- **Dard's cooperative theory of authorship, and its Ibn Arabi source.** "This act is a cooperation
  between Lord and servant, for the Lord who is a necessary individuation is in the rank of the
  writer, and the servant who is a contingent individuation is in the role of the pen, and neither
  from the pen alone comes a picture into existence, nor does it appear from the writer alone"
  (p. 120, folio 118), closing with the Fusus line "If He were not and we were not, / Whatever is
  would, then, be not" (p. 120, folio 118).
- **Poetic inspiration folded into the hidden-treasure cosmology.** "according to his theories,
  poetical inspiration is one of the aspects of the famous Divine saying, ‘I was a hidden treasure
  and wanted to be known'" (pp. 120–121, folios 118–119, cited "(K 98)"); "He can also use man as a
  pen which writes down His eternal words which never end" (p. 121, folio 119), Dard citing "‘If the
  sea were ink...' (Sura 18/109)".
- **Schimmel's note that Dard came close to the doctrine he attacked.** The formula
  "la maujuda illa Allah" is "a formulation which brings him very close to the utterances of the
  representatives of wahdat al-wujud, whom he otherwise attacked so mercilessly" (p. 133, folio 131).
  **Compare Dard's own disavowal of pantheism early in the chunk: his first poems showed
  "restlessness and inclination towards all-embracing (pantheistic) mysticism; nevertheless, evil
  drunkenness and shathiyat have never come upon his tongue ..." (p. 115, folio 113, cited
  "(K 400)").** **A contradiction the book states on both sides.**
- **The claim that the ‘Ilm ul-kitab ranks immediately below scripture.** "He considered his own
  productions, especially the ‘Ilm ul-kitab, to be only second to the revealed books" (p. 127,
  folio 125), with the Arabic address to disciples: "God made all the other books of the mystical
  path and Sufism and ethics unnecessary for you by its signs ("dydt"[OCR?] [āyāt]), and completed
  your religion" (p. 127, folio 125, cited "(K 543)"). **A claim that one khanqah's book displaces
  the whole Sufi curriculum.**
- **The chapter-four thesis, stated in Latin.** "Lex orandi lex credendi—this saying is valid for
  Muslims as much as for the faithful in other religions. Few documents in the spiritual history of
  Islam are as revealing as are the various kinds of prayers" (p. 128, folio not stated). **Prayer
  is proposed as the primary evidence for belief — a methodological commitment.**
- **Prayer as divine gift rather than human act.** "Prayer is sometimes given, a Divine grant, not a
  human activity. Dard follows here the traditional mystic view, so beautifully expressed by Rumi and
  others" (p. 131, folio 129), on the strength of the plane-tree passage, of which Schimmel notes the
  imagery is conventional: "the comparison of the leaves of the plane-tree to human hands is almost
  as old as Persian lyrical poetry" (p. 131, folio 129, cited "(S 118)"). The passage itself makes
  renunciation of petition the mark of rank: "the high ambition ("(Aimmat)"[OCR?] [himmat]) of the
  saints is, like the stature of the naked free cypress, freedom from the garments of every
  borrowing"; "those great people do not open the door of wish from their own side" (p. 131,
  folio 129).
- **The literary-sociological aside on prayer poetry.** "Islamic literature is replete with prayer
  poems in simple words, invented by mystic bards in the Anatolian plains or nomads in the highlands
  of Afghanistan"; "numerous unknown poets translated the hope of the masses in Muhammad's
  intercession at Doomsday into unsophisticated folk songs, voicing the never ending cry for
  forgiveness of sins" (p. 129, folio 127). **The nearest thing in the chunk to popular religion as
  a subject.**

## Doubts

**No transcriber flags exist in this text: the book was OCR'd with tesseract and no human read it
through.** Every `[OCR?]` above is my own mark, placed on the printed form as the OCR gives it, with
my reading in square brackets after it. **37 `[OCR?]` marks were added.** The damage classes in this
chunk, inventoried once:

- **Lost macrons and long-vowel letters mangled into `d`, `t`, `1`, `z`, `s`.** This is by far the
  commonest class and it attacks technical vocabulary and titles: "dhzkr"/"dAzkr"/"dhtkr" for
  *dhikr*; "wdriddt" for *wāridāt*; "Risdla"/"Risdlas" for *Risāla(s)*; "Ihyd ‘ultim ad-din";
  "shahddat"; "dydt"; "illd"/"illad"/"illaé" for *illā*; "magqstda"; "maujida" for *maujūda*;
  "insantyya" for *insaniyya*; "angusht-1" for *angusht-i*; "il@hi" and "(2lahi)" for *ilāhī*;
  "fattha" for *fātiha*; "(Aimmat)" for *himmat*; "(iftiqar) 1s" for "is" (p. 130, folio 128).
- **q → g.** Confirmed several times: "Magamat" for *Maqāmāt*; "Taugq" for *Tauq*; "qgalbi" for
  *qalbi*; "gaddisa" for *qaddisa*; "haqgiga" for *haqīqa*; and probably "ilgd@>" for *ilqāʾ* —
  **this last is the one reading I am least sure of, and it carries doctrinal weight**, since it is
  the word for the mode of inspiration Dard accepts for saints, "only by inspiration
  ("ilgd@>"[OCR?] [ilqāʾ]) of the Merciful" (p. 126, folio 124, cited "(D 3)"). A human should check
  whether the printed word is *ilqāʾ* or *ilhām*.
- **ʿayn and hamza dropped or replaced by `?`.** "‘A? isha" for *ʿAʾisha*; "Ibn Ata? Allah" for
  *Ibn ʿAtaʾ Allāh*; "lata if" for *lataʾif*; "Mu‘ adh" broken by a space.
- **Initial capitals and letterforms confused: I/J/l, Ii/l, F/f.** "‘Jim ul-kitab" and
  "‘Jlm ul-kitab" for *ʿIlm ul-kitāb* (the same title is printed correctly as "‘Ilm ul-kitab" at
  p. 127, folio 125, which is how I read the corrupt forms); "/é" and "/ém" for *lā* and *lām*;
  "Fusiis" for *Fuṣūṣ*; "Rimi" for *Rūmī*; "shatkh" for *shaikh*; "the had" for *the ha*; "‘Il'" for
  the pronoun "I" (p. 129, folio 127).
- **Citation numerals run together or broken.** "(M11727)" (p. 120, folio 118) should be a Mathnawi
  book-and-line reference and cannot be reconstructed from the chunk — **a human must check the
  printed page.** Compare the clean "(M II 493 f.)" on the same page.
- **Stray punctuation inside names.** "Muhammad. himself" (p. 122, folio 120); "Divine' love"
  (p. 122, folio 120).
- **Ordinary word-level noise that does not affect any fact above** and is therefore not flagged
  inline: "tums" for *turns*, "most if it" for *most of it*, "a Jater period", "unaquainted",
  "individations", "mytho]-ogy", "charme", "diamant", "unexplicable", "strri" (p. 133, folio 131),
  and a stray "|" for "I" twice at p. 120, folio 118.
