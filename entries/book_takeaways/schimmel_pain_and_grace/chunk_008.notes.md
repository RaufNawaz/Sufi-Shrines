  given.** "besides a few dry facts we know about him merely some legends of more or less authentic
  character" (p. 151, folio not stated). **No karamat, no miracle and no legend about him appears
  anywhere in this chunk** — **(not stated)** — which is worth recording, since the chunk is the
  opening of his whole section.
- **A conversation between God and Dard, reported as Dard's own record of a divine promise.** "he
  reminds God of His promise when He once addressed him: 'O My servant, I have created you with my
  hand and blessed be the Lord, the best of creators!' … And He said: 'By My mercy, I forgive you, and
  again forgive you and again forgive you and who follows you and who follows those who follow you
  until the Day of Resurrection …'" (p. 147, folio 145, cited "(S 249)"). Schimmel introduces it as "A
  typical example" of Dard's Arabic prayers and calls the class "such expressions of spiritual pride"
  (pp. 147, folio 145). **Attributed to Dard's own book, not to a hagiographer, and the forgiveness is
  extended to his followers and their followers — a claim with institutional force.**
- **A prayer for light transmitted from the Prophet.** "the last word that is noted down in his books
  is the Arabic prayer for light which was transmitted from the Prophet and elaborated by Ghazzali and
  many other mystical writers. A few days before his death he wrote:" — then the prayer, "O God, make
  in my heart a light and in my soul a light …" (pp. 134–135, folios 132–133, cited "(S 341)").
  **Recorded here because the death-bed framing is the chunk's one hagiographic gesture toward Dard.**

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **Dard's theology is read as light-mysticism inherited from his father and defended as Quranic.**
  "He remained faithful to the idea that God should be best described as Light, this Quaranic
  description being much more fitting than strange pseudo-philosophical expressions which have no
  foundation in the Quran and the tradition" (p. 134, folio 132). His self-image: "the candle of the
  assembly of those with heart" (p. 134, folio 132).
- **Dard is placed against the Unity-of-Being debate of his own lifetime, and read as seeking a middle
  way.** "Dard tries to find a via media between the two theories the adherents of which fought with
  each other during his life-time": "O God, the confession of Unity of Being is a kind of bad
  behaviour (bi-adabi), and the open show of Unity of Vision is a crooked discourse—the best speech is
  the word: 'There is no God but God' (D 223)" (p. 141, folio 139). **Schimmel's repeated caution:**
  "We should not take such utterances at surface value and understand them as expressions of
  pantheistic views" (p. 141, folio 139); "It does not involve a pantheistic view of all-embracing
  unity" (p. 140, folio 138). And his sectarian targets: "Dard was very much against pantheists,
  self-styled Sufis, and other people whom he considered dangerous for the fabric of Islamic religious
  life" (p. 142, folio 140), "the wujudi-mystics whom he disliked so deeply" (p. 139, folio 137).
- **Dard's detachment from the political catastrophe of Delhi is named as strange, twice.**
  "Considering that Mir Dard lived through the darkest time in the history of his beloved hometown
  Delhi, we understand that he, at times, asked the meaning of God's acting like that with the
  Muslims" (p. 139, folio 137); "It is strange to see that Dard remained, in a certain way, untouched
  by the catastrophes and sufferings around him" (p. 139, folio 137). The prayer she cites in evidence
  is dated only as "a prayer in the midst of destruction" (p. 139, folio 137). **No event, siege,
  invader or year is named** — **(not stated)**.
- **Dard's imagery is argued to be wholly inherited, and its handling to be Indian.** "Dard's whole
  imagery consists more or less of the images inherited from his predecessors in Persian poetry. His
  prayers, too, are permeated by combinations of traditional images, which are, however, often twisted
  in the way typical of the Indian Style in poetry and applied in surprising turns to the Sincere
  Muhammadans" (p. 143, folio 141).
- **A distinction between Dard's Persian and Arabic prayers, with a judgement attached.** "In his
  Persian prayers, Dard sometimes seems to be completely carried away by the plays of words … His
  Arabic prayers, as they are usually scattered through 'J/m ul-kitab[OCR?] ['Ilm ul-kitāb], are in
  general more traditional; they develop out of inherited prayer formulas and are connected with
  Quranic verses. More frequently than in the Persian prayers, where Dard the poet is sometimes
  stronger than Dard the theologian, we find prayers for forgiveness for his family" (p. 147,
  folio 145).
- **Intercession is identified as the centre of Dard's hope, with his father added to the Prophet.**
  "it seems that the center of his hope is the intercession of the Prophet; though not to be derived
  clearly from the Quran, this idea had found its expression very early in the traditions, and became
  the center of general Muslim piety" (pp. 137–138, folios 135–136); "In Dard's case, the problem of
  intercession becomes slightly complicated because he usually adds the hope for his father's help to
  the normal expressions about intercession" (p. 138, folio 136). **A saint's son making his father a
  second intercessor is a shrine-relevant claim, and Schimmel marks it as unusual.**
- **A late turn toward love-symbolism, dated.** "only towards the end of his life, in Dard-1
  dil[OCR?] [Dard-i dil], the mystic turns more frequently to that love-symbolism which he otherwise
  tried to avoid in his prose"; "In these prayers from the early 1780's, God appears to him as the
  real goal of search and the 'ascertained' (tahgigi[OCR?] [taḥqīqī]) beloved" (p. 142, folio 140).
- **Comparative method stated openly across religions.** Rumi's Sun of Tabriz is set beside "Arjuna's
  vision of Krishna whose real being is consuming fire" (p. 138, folio 136); the shadow-play image is
  traced through Attar, Umar ibn al-Farid, Turkish mystical writers and "those in Muslim India"
  (p. 144, folio 142); the weaving image is compared to "the numerous examples in history of
  religions" (p. 144, folio 142). Latin theological tags are used untranslated: "the Fascinans and the
  Trernendum[OCR?] [Tremendum]" (p. 138, folio 136), "God as the dulcis hospes animae who enters the
  broken heart of man secretly" (p. 148, folio 146).
- **The great danger with a great poet, stated as the reason the reception history matters.** "there
  is always the danger that great poetry will be interpreted by every reader according to his own
  feelings, and also according to certain opportunist or even political tendencies; and since
  contradictions cannot be excluded in the verse of a great poet, let alone a religious, or mystically
  minded, poet, everyone finds in him what he has sought" (p. 153, folio 153). **This is the chunk's
  strongest methodological claim and it applies directly to shrine-saint material.**
- **The Risalo's difficulty is used to qualify all of the scholarship on it.** "the style of the
  Risalé[OCR?] [Risālo] is extremely difficult and offers problems even to those whose mother tongue
  is the complicated and melodious Sindhi" (p. 152, folio 152), with the author's own caveat that her
  informants "sometimes differed considerably" and that "other translators may as well reach different
  conclusions" (p. 152, folio 152, footnote 2).
