# schimmel_pain_and_grace — chunk 002 — pp. 21–41

**This chunk is the hinge of the volume.** It closes Chapter One ("THE INDIAN SCENE IN THE
EIGHTEENTH CENTURY", running to p. 31, folio 27), passes the Part I title page
`KHWAJA MIR DARD OF DELHI / (1721-1785)` (p. 32, folio not stated), and opens
`CHAPTER TWO / DARD'S LIFE AND TEACHING` (p. 33, folio not stated), which then runs to the end of
the chunk. **Its first eleven pages are the richest Sindhi-shrine material this chunk has**: the
chapter's closing survey of eighteenth-century Sind names nine archive figures, gives Shah Abdul
Latif's compressed life, and carries the chunk's single strongest passage for the project's
interests — Shah Waliullah's condemnation of tomb pilgrimage (p. 21, folio 17). **Its last nine
pages are Delhi genealogy** — Dard's paternal line, his father Muhammad Nasir Andalib, and the
Naqshbandi *qayyum*s — and yield almost nothing mappable, as expected.

**Folio, read off the running headers.** Every body page in the chunk carries its printed number in
the header line, and both regimes check out against the measured table. In the −4 regime: p. 21 heads
`THE INDIAN SCENE IN THE EIGHTEENTH CENTURY 17`, p. 22 heads `18 THE INDIAN SCENE IN THE EIGHTEENTH
CENTURY`, and the sequence is unbroken to p. 31 (folio 27). In the −2 regime: p. 34 heads
`32 KHWAJA MIR DARD OF DELHI`, p. 35 heads `DARD'S LIFE AND TEACHING 33`, unbroken to p. 41
(folio 39). **Two pages print no number and are cited by PDF index only**: p. 32 (the Part I title,
which carries nothing but the part title and Dard's dates) and p. 33 (the chapter opening). The
folio sequence therefore jumps from 27 to 32 — see `## Doubts`. Running headers are treated as
apparatus throughout and are never quoted as content.

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **bhit-bhit-shah** ("Shah Abdul Latif of Bhit", also "Shah Abdul Latif", "Shah Latif", "Latif"):
  **his dates are given once, parenthetically, with no hedge at all** — "It is Shah Abdul Latif of
  Bhit (1689-1752) who died ten years before Shah Waliullah left this valley of tears" (p. 24,
  folio 20). **The book states them flatly, without the "not established with complete certainty"
  qualification other accounts attach to them.**
- **bhit-bhit-shah**: **he is dated against two other men in the same sentence-run, not against

  death year is nowhere printed in this chunk** — **(not stated)**.
- **bhit-bhit-shah**: **his standing, in the book's own superlative** — "the greatest mystical poet

- **bhit-bhit-shah**: **a dated inscription on the tomb, located on the fabric.** "One of the Persian

- **bhit-bhit-shah**: **the illiteracy claim, named to its source and rejected.** "Mir Ali Shir Qani'
  claims in his two major historical works that the mystical poet was ummi, 'illiterate;' but that

  poems, and decorated with Arabic quotations, or allusion to Persian mystical poetry" (p. 29,
  folio 25). **Schimmel's rejection is her own, stated as an argument from the text of the poems.**
- **bhit-bhit-shah**: **his musicianship, with an instrument invented.** "Shah Latif was an excellent
  musician, who even invented a new type of tambura (drone instrument), and whose blending of
  traditional Indian raga and Sindhi folk tunes enhances the beauty of his verses which ought to be
  sung, not analyzed, to enjoy their full charm" (p. 29, folio 25). **A named instrument and a

- **bhit-bhit-shah**: **the Risalo characterised, and a negative about his prose.** "Shah Abdul
  Latif's Risdld6"[OCR?] [Risalo] ", written in Indian meters, contains a storehouse of legends,
  tales, and Sufi topics; but he never tried to rationalize or to systematize his thoughts, and it

- **bhit-bhit-shah**: **the closing image the book assigns to him** — he "would have probably thought

- **dargah-roza-sufi-shah-inayat-shaheed** (the book calls him "Shah Inayat of Jhok" and "Shah

  figure in this chunk, and it is an account of a shrine movement destroyed by the state.** "The
  first obvious sign of the struggle for power in the Lower Indus Valley becomes visible in 1717,

  presented as caused by them.**
- **dargah-roza-sufi-shah-inayat-shaheed**: **the siege, the surrender and the execution, dated to

- **dargah-roza-sufi-shah-inayat-shaheed**: **his afterlife as a subject of cross-community poetry,

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- **Khwaja Mir Dard, his grave and its neglect** — the project's Delhi material, recorded in full.
  "Khwaja Mir Dard is buried in a small graveyard inside Delhi near Turkoman gate in what is now a
  slum area. Few people know the place, fewer visit it. And still, the man who has found his last
  resting place in this modest tomb was, in his time, one of the great mystical leaders of Delhi,
  and was, at the same time, the first to write mystical verse in Urdu. Those, who visit his tomb to
  recite a fatiha will probably more admire his poetical heritage than his achievements in mystical
  theology, but it is mainly the latter aspect of his work that concerns us here" (p. 33, folio not
  stated). **A located grave ("a small graveyard inside Delhi near Turkoman gate"), a stated
  condition ("what is now a slum area", "this modest tomb"), a named ritual act performed by visitors
  ("to recite a fatiha") and an explicit statement of low visitation ("Few people know the place,
  fewer visit it").** His dates are printed only on the part title: `KHWAJA MIR DARD OF DELHI /
  (1721-1785)` (p. 32, folio not stated); his birth is dated "19. Dhu *1-Qa'da 1133/13.9.1721"[OCR?]
  [Dhu 'l-Qa'da 1133/13.9.1721] (p. 39, folio 37). **The chunk does not narrate his death.**
- **Muhammad Nasir Andalib, Dard's father and his mystical master** — the chunk's second Delhi
  figure and the founder of the order. He is "both real father and mystical leader, and to whom he
  owes his whole spiritual formation" (p. 34, folio 32), and the founder of "the tariqga
  Muhammadtyya"[OCR?] [tariqa Muhammadiyya] (p. 34, folio 32). Born "25. Sha' ban 1105/24.4
  1691"[OCR?] [24.4.1691] (p. 35, folio 33), with a birth chronogram — "warith-i 'ilm-i imamain wa
  'Ali, the heir of the knowledge of the two Imams (i.e., Baha 'uddin Naqshband and Abdul Qadir
  Gilani) and of Ali" — and the description "a 'saint, possessed with a book' (K 137)" (p. 35,
  folio 33). He "studied religious and profane sciences with his father and his grandfather (F 23)",
  joined the Imperial army, and "left the military service in the time of Muhammad Shah, i.e., after
  1719, to devote himself to a religious life and perfect poverty" (p. 35, folio 33). "As most
  members of the North Indian aristocracy, he was a Hanafi by creed, and adopted the Nagshi
  mujaddidi tariga"[OCR?] [Naqshi mujaddidi tariqa] "(D 66)" (p. 35, folio 33). His masters: "Pir
  Muhammad Zubair and Shaikh Sa' dullah Gulshan" (p. 36, folio 34). His main work is "the Nala-yi
  'Andalib", "which seems to consist, at least in some parts, of an autobiography" (p. 35, folio 33).
  **The chunk gives no death date and no burial place for him** — **(not stated)**.
- **Muhammad Nasir's veneration in the street, and the door of his khanqah** — "people came to kiss
  his feet when he rode on horseback through the streets of Delhi, whether they knew him or not"
  (p. 41, folio 39); and, quoted from Dard, "Though the door of guidance and the gate of visits for
  the sincere friends was permanently open, he used to sit constantly in the nest of 'solitude in
  the multitude'" (p. 41, folio 39). **A named practice — "khalwat dar anjuman, 'solitude in the
  multitude'" — described as the way a master handles a permanent stream of visitors.**
- **Shah Waliullah** — the chunk's anti-shrine voice, and the most important figure in it for the
  project. He calls false Sufis "karamat furushan, 'sellers of miracles'" and "the 'highwaymen and
  robbers of religion,'" and regards Sufi books, "'which may have a wonderful effect on the elect,
  to be deadly poison for the ordinary people,'" and "would have liked to declare as infidels those
  who perform pilgrimages to sacred places: 'That is a sin, greater than murder and adultery.'"
  (p. 21, folio 17). His prophetology "is sometimes almost foreshadowing the 'naturism' of Sir Sayyid
  Ahmad Khan more than a century later", and he "even explains miracles like the splitting of the
  moon (Sura 54/1) as a natural phenomenon" (p. 21, folio 17). He "appears to us as a strange figure
  in the history of Islamic mysticism, combining great pride in his visions and his role as the true
  reformer of a rotten society with an extremely complicated web of thoughts"; "He wrote poetry in
  Persian and Arabic, and a few verses in Urdu … His many faceted character still awaits his
  biographers" (pp. 21–22, folios 17–18). **No dates of any kind are given for him in this chunk** —
  **(not stated)**.
- **Ajmer and "the tomb of Salar Mas' ud"[OCR?]** — the two shrines Shah Waliullah names, quoted in
  full from his own text: "All those who go to Ajmer or to the tomb of Salar Mas' ud"[OCR?] "or
  similar places for something they want from there: that is a sin, greater than murder or adultery.
  Is he not like someone who worships handmade things, or like those who called Allat and al-Uzza?
  But I cannot declare them clearly as infidels because there is no text from the lawgiver in this
  peculiar case." (p. 21, folio 17, footnote 4, cited "Id. 11 35."[OCR?] [Id. II 35]). Schimmel
  prefaces it: "The text is interesting enough to deserve quotation" (p. 21, folio 17, footnote 4).
  **Neither shrine is in Pakistan and neither is an archive row; the passage is recorded here because
  it is the chunk's clearest statement of the instrumental logic of shrine visiting — going "for
  something they want from there" — and of the eighteenth-century case against it.**
- **The "'Indian Wahhabis'" label and the destruction of shrines in Arabia** — "Utterances like this
  may be one of the reasons why the followers of his school were later often branded as 'Indian
  Wahhabis,' since the Wahhabis in the Arabian peninsula mercilessly destroyed all places of
  saint-worship" (p. 21, folio 17).
- **Mazhar Janjanan** — the chunk's second Delhi Naqshbandi. "Waliullah's friend and colleague …
  who was highly respected in the Delhi community, and perhaps even better known to many of the
  pious in the capital"; "This 'saintly, purified, dervish-like, scholarly, perfect, famous,
  incomparable person,' as Mir calls him, followed more closely the normal Naqshbandi line" (p. 22,
  folio 18). "Born in 1699 in Agra as son of one of Aurangzeb's courtiers, Mazhar became the
  disciple of Nur Muhammad Bada 'uni, a Naqshbandi saint, at the age of 18. His master was a
  disciple of Muhammad Ma' sum's khalifa. Then, the young man sought the instruction of Sa' dullah
  Gulshan who, however, had entrusted his followers to Pir Muhammad Zubair, the gayyum"[OCR?]
  [qayyum] ". Muhammad Zubair, in turn, sent Mazhar to Hafiz Sa' dullah. Mazhar claimed also to be
  initiated, partly in wwais?"[OCR?] [uwaisi] "succession, into the other orders. According to his
  own words, 'he began to sweep the dervish lodges at the age of thirty,' that is shortly after
  Gulshan's death in 1728" (p. 22, folio 18). **A four-master initiation chain, a claim of uwaisi
  initiation into other orders, and a self-description of menial service in "the dervish lodges".**
- **Mazhar Janjanan's death, dated and violent** — "His uncompromising attitude even caused his
  death: at the age of 82 in 1781, he was killed when ridiculing a Muharram procession" (p. 22,
  folio 18). **A named saint killed at a Muharram procession, with a year and an age.** His
  anti-Shia position is specified: he "went so far as to compose a defense of the Omayyad ruler Mu'
  awiya, whose son Yazid is cursed by all Shiites as the murderer of the Prophet's grandson Husain
  in Kerbela; the Delhi mystic, however, wanted this just and successful ruler to be treated like
  any other companion of the Prophet" (p. 22, folio 18).
- **Mazhar Janjanan's order and its size** — "Mazhar concentrated upon the leadership of his tariqa,
  the Shamsiyya Mazhariyya, which seems to have flourished in Delhi and environment, for not less
  than 49 of his khalifas are known by name" (p. 23, folio 19). **A countable figure for an order's
  deputies — 49 — and a stated geographical range.** Also: he "was, in his youth, enraptured by the
  beauty of the young talented poet Taban, was extremely strict in keeping his ritual duties; he
  once remarks that one should pray not less than 60 rak'as (prayer units) during 24 hours; that is
  more than three times the prescribed number"; and "he married a perfectly intolerable woman in
  order to be polished by living with her; his letters sometimes point to his matrimonial
  difficulties" (p. 23, folio 19). On Hindus he "did not consider the Hindus as sheer mushrikin,
  'heathen,' but rather as followers of some sort of monotheism; but he, of course, maintained that
  Muhammad's revelation had put an end to all previous religions" (p. 23, folio 19, footnoted to
  "Y. Friedmann's article 'Medieval Muslim Views of Indian Religions', in: JAOS 95/2, 1975").
- **Thana 'ullah Panipati**[OCR?] [Thana'ullah] — Mazhar's khalifa at Panipat and the link between

## Practices, institutions, economy

**This chunk's institutional value lies in two places: the eighteenth-century reformist attack on
shrine practice, and three concrete transactions involving shrines — tomb dust as tribute, a
tomb-dome inscription, and an 'urs held in a private house.**

- **The anti-pilgrimage argument, in the reformer's own words and with the shrines named.** Shah
  Waliullah "would have liked to declare as infidels those who perform pilgrimages to sacred places:
  'That is a sin, greater than murder and adultery.'" (p. 21, folio 17), and the footnote gives the
  full text: "All those who go to Ajmer or to the tomb of Salar Mas' ud"[OCR?] "or similar places for
  something they want from there: that is a sin, greater than murder or adultery. Is he not like
  someone who worships handmade things, or like those who called Allat and al-Uzza? But I cannot
  declare them clearly as infidels because there is no text from the lawgiver in this peculiar case."
  (p. 21, folio 17, footnote 4). **Note the transactional framing — people go "for something they
  want from there" — and note the limit the reformer sets on his own ruling: no takfir, "because
  there is no text from the lawgiver".**
- **The commercial vocabulary of the attack on saints.** False Sufis are "karamat furushan, 'sellers
  of miracles'" and "the 'highwaymen and robbers of religion,'" in Waliullah's words, while "his
  colleague Mir Dard uses the expression 'shopkeeper saints' for these characters, whom he even calls
  'pig-natured.'" (p. 21, folio 17) **Both reformers reach for market metaphors, independently —
  directly usable for the project's economics-of-religion framing.**
- **Sufi books as a restricted good.** Waliullah's "reformatory zeal … leads him to even regard the
  Sufi books, 'which may have a wonderful effect on the elect, to be deadly poison for the ordinary
  people,'" (p. 21, folio 17). **A stated two-tier readership.**
- **Tomb dust as tribute, valued above all Sind's products.** Divan Gidumal, "the minister of the
  Kalhoro princes", offered Ahmad Shah Abdali "two bags which, as he said, / contain the most
  valuable of Sind's gifts; they contain the holy dust from the tombs of numerous saints and Pirs of
  Sind." (p. 25, folio 21) **Shrine-derived material used as a diplomatic payment by a state
  minister.**
- **A shrine movement recruiting agricultural labour, and destroyed for it.** Shah Inayat of Jhok's
  "successful mystical preaching attracted many disciples from the mystical centers of the country,
  but also laborers from the neighboring big farms led his adversaries to denounce him at court"
  (p. 25, folio 21); "Shah Inayat was besieged in his Khanqah Jhok, and had to surrender to the
  overwhelming army that came from various places in Sind and Hind. He was executed in January 1718"
  (p. 25, folio 21). **A khanqah drawing labour off neighbouring large farms, and an armed coalition
  assembled against it — the clearest case in the chunk of a shrine as an economic threat.**
- **The 'urs, once, and not at a shrine.** Dard's maternal grandfather "died on 2. Jumada II
  1156/25.7.1743, at the eve of the celebration of an 'urs for his late wife, when a large number of
  noble guests had assembled in his house" (p. 39, folio 37). **The only 'urs in the chunk: held in a
  private house, for a wife rather than a saint, with a large assembly of noble guests.**
- **Muharram as a contested observance, in three places.** Makhdum Muhammad Hashim "attacks the
  Muharram celebrations, which were celebrated on Makli Hill" (p. 27, folio 23); Mazhar Janjanan "was
  killed when ridiculing a Muharram procession" at the age of 82 in 1781 (p. 22, folio 18); and
  "Earlier in that century, Shah Waliullah too had protested against the celebrations of the Ashura
  Day" (p. 22, folio 18). **A named observance at a named burial hill, a reformer killed at a
  procession, and a third reformer's protest — the same target in Sind and Delhi.**
- **Reform with state backing.** Makhdum Hashim fought "with the support of the Kalhora princes
  relentlessly … against the innovations that had spoiled Islam in his days" (p. 26, folio 22).
  **Princely support named explicitly for an anti-innovation campaign.**
- **A dated eighteenth-century account of festivals, music and dance at a burial hill.** Qani's
  "Maklindma"[OCR?] [Maklinama] gives "his notes about the festivities, the sessions of music and
  mystical dance, as they were held there in constant succession", from which "many of the buildings
  which are no longer existent, can be reconstructed from his remarks" (p. 27, folio 23). **A named
  source for lost buildings and for a continuous festival calendar on Makli Hill, by a writer dated
  (1725-1789).**
- **The ban on music and sama', and the jizya, both routed through a Sufi office.** Muhammad Ma'sum
  "is considered to be responsible for Aurangzeb's re-imposure of the jizya on the Hindus and to have
  been instrumental in forbidding music and samd'"[OCR?] [sama'] (p. 36, folio 34). **State
  regulation of shrine practice and of a discriminatory tax, both attributed to a mystical
  office-holder's influence.**
- **The office of qayyum, described as a claim to universal jurisdiction.** "'is the dignitary upon
  whom the whole order of existence depends, and under whose control are all Names, Attributes, and
  things actual and potential. All things, whether they belong to the past, the present, or the
  future, up to the throne of God and the heavens with the signs of the zodiak are under his shadow.
  He is the vicar of God on earth.' 'The Absolute bestowes upon him a special essence, called mauhub,
  given, on which depends the subsistence of the Universe.'" Even "the gutb"[OCR?] [qutb], "otherwise
  considered in Sufism to be the highest representative of the mystical hierarchy, is under his rule,
  nay, is his servant" (p. 36, folio 34, footnoted to "J. Subhan, 'Sufism,' and S. M. Ikram, 'Radi
  Kauthar,'"[OCR?] and to "Ihsan's Raudat al-qayyumiyya … the ms. is found in the Library of the
  Asiatic Society of Bengal"). **A named office claiming supremacy over the whole Sufi hierarchy,
  with its basic text still in manuscript.**
- **The qayyum's political and ceremonial power in Delhi.** Pir Muhammad Zubair "sided with Prince
  Mu' azzam against Prince A' zam"; his protégé "eventually succeeded in ascending the throne as
  Shah Alam Bahadur"; he "probably continued playing an important role in the endless struggles and
  feudal strifes which caused the final disintegration of the Mughal Empire"; and "Firaq's
  description (as usual, greatly exaggerated) tells how he was surrounded by large crowds when he
  rode through Delhi in pompous style, and how the princes spread velvet and brocade under his feet
  when he entered the Lal Qila" (p. 37, folio 35). **Crowds, a ceremonial entry into the fort, and
  cloth laid under a Sufi's feet by princes — recorded with the author's own caveat about
  exaggeration.**
- **Quantified devotional labour.** Muhammad Zubair "is credited with having uttered the profession
  of faith 'There is no God but God' 24,000 times during the 12 hours of the day; further, he
  repeated the name of God 15,000 times a day, retaining his breath according to the traditional
  Naqshbandi fashion" (p. 36, folio 34); Mazhar Janjanan "once remarks that one should pray not less
  than 60 rak'as (prayer units) during 24 hours; that is more than three times the prescribed
  number" (p. 23, folio 19). **Countable ritual outputs, with the second explicitly benchmarked
  against the legal minimum.**
- **An order's deputies counted.** Of Mazhar's "tariqa, the Shamsiyya Mazhariyya, which seems to have
  flourished in Delhi and environment, for not less than 49 of his khalifas are known by name"
  (p. 23, folio 19). **The chunk's only countable measure of an order's institutional reach.**
- **Initiation from a dead saint's spirit, treated as valid.** Muhammad Mu'in's "Risdla-yt
  uwaistyya"[OCR?] [Risala-yi uwaisiyya] concerns "the mystical introduction into the Path without
  the guidance of a living master: the initiation through the spirit of a deceased saint or through
  the Prophetsaint Khidr was considered valid in most Sufi circles" (p. 26, folio 22); Mazhar too
  "claimed also to be initiated, partly in wwais?"[OCR?] [uwaisi] "succession, into the other orders"
  (p. 22, folio 18). **This is the doctrinal mechanism by which a tomb can confer authority without a
  living custodian — record it for the custodianship line of enquiry.**
- **Sayyid status as a present-day asset, observed by the author.** "Mir Dard's greatest source of
  pride was the fact that he was a sayyid from both sides. (Even today Indian sayyid families may
  consider themselves as absolutely superior to any other human being and will tell the visitor how
  fortunate he is to touch the threshold of a descendant of the holy Prophet.)" (p. 40, folio 38)
  **A twentieth-century field observation, in parentheses, about the transaction between a sayyid
  household and a visitor.** Alongside it, Sorley quoted on the "'almost incredible veneration for
  sayyids as such and a great accretion of superstitious practices in the belief of the multitude'"
  (p. 28, folio 24).
- **Veneration in the street.** "people came to kiss his feet when he rode on horseback through the
  streets of Delhi, whether they knew him or not" (p. 41, folio 39).
- **Spiritual conversation as an institution.** "This spiritual conversation, sohbat, is regarded in
  the Naqshbandi tradition as one of the most important facets of mystical life. On the other hand it
  has deeply influenced the whole culture of Muslims in the later Middle Ages so that Mughal culture
  has been even called a 'culture of refined conversationalism.' To listen to the master, to be
  guided by his words, and to learn how to behave properly, to understand the hierarchy of values and
  the spiritual hierarchy among human beings was one of the most important educational contributions
  of the Naqshbandi order, rather of the mystical fraternities in general to Muslim cultural life"
  (p. 41, folio 39). **An order's output described as education in hierarchy — and the counter-term
  "bi-adab, 'without etiquette, ill-mannered,'" as Dard's "expression of deepest abhorrence"
  (p. 41, folio 39).**
- **"khalwat dar anjuman" as the management of a permanent visitor flow.** "Though the door of
  guidance and the gate of visits for the sincere friends was permanently open, he used to sit
  constantly in the nest of 'solitude in the multitude;'" (p. 41, folio 39); the practice "means that
  the spirit of the mystic rests so completely in God that it cannot be disturbed by those who seek
  his company and guidance" (p. 41, folio 39).
- **Religious publishing and schooling.** "Makhdum Hashim's Sindhi Tafsir Hashimi was usually the
  first book given to children in the Quran schools, and belongs to the first Sindhi books ever
  printed" (p. 27, folio 23); Makhdum Hashim produced "more than 300 works" (p. 26, folio 22); "Some
  of Dard's works are practically non-existent, like Wagi'dt-i Dard"[OCR?] [Waqi'at-i Dard] "and
  S6z-i dil"[OCR?] [Soz-i dil], "which were published by the Matba'a Ansari from 1309/1891 onwards …
  the first edition was prepared in 1847 at the request of Dr. Alois Sprenger by Maulana Sahba
  71"[OCR?] [Sahba'i] (p. 33, folio not stated). **A named press, a dated print run, and a colonial
  officer commissioning the first edition of a Sufi's poetry in 1847.**
- **Loss of a shrine figure's library, dated.** Girhori's books were "destroyed during the Afghan
  raids in Upper Sind in 1781" (p. 28, folio 24).
- **Conversion asserted without numbers.** Qani' on Makhdum Hashim: "In his time appeared so to speak
  a renovation of Islam, and every month some Hindus found salvation from the meanness of infidelity
  thanks to his laudable striving." (p. 27, folio 23) **"some Hindus", "every month" — a rate with no
  magnitude.**
- **Hindu participation in Muslim shrine religion, stated four ways.** "a number of Hindus became
  devoted disciples of Muslim saints and did not hesitate to compose religious poetry in honor of
  Muslim saints and even of the Prophet of Islam" (p. 28, folio 24); Shaikh Tahir / Lal Udero "is
  venerated by members of both communities" (p. 28, folio 24); Shah Inayat's death "has inspired many
  Sufi poets, Hindus and Muslims alike, to write touching poetry" (pp. 25–26, folios 21–22); and "the
  Hindu Bedraban"[OCR?] "Khushgu" is named among Shah Gulshan's disciples in Delhi (p. 38, folio 36).
  Against this, Mazhar "did not consider the Hindus as sheer mushrikin, 'heathen,' but rather as
  followers of some sort of monotheism" (p. 23, folio 19), while Girhori died attacking "the Shiva
  idol in Hathungo" (p. 28, folio 24).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

The book reports each of these at a distance; the attributions below are hers.

- **Miracles surrounding a qayyum's birth** — Muhammad Zubair "was born in 1093/1682; his birth was
  surrounded, like that of his ancestors, by miracles" (p. 36, folio 34). **Attributed to no source
  and not endorsed.**
- **A chronogram as a birth omen** — the chronogram of Muhammad Nasir's birth, "warith-i 'ilm-i
  imamain wa 'Ali, the heir of the knowledge of the two Imams … and of Ali", and the description of
  him as "a 'saint, possessed with a book' (K 137)" (p. 35, folio 33).
- **Muhammad Nasir's vision of Imam Hasan** — reported only in order to date and correct a story
  about it: "The story that Gulshan did not accept Muhammad Nasir as disciple in mysticism but only in
  poetry because Nasir was superior to him due to his famous vision of Imam Hasan is historically
  incorrect, for Shah Gulshan died in 1728, and Muhammad Nasir's vision took place only in the
  mid-thirties (cf. F 34 f.)" (p. 37, folio 35). **A miracle story refuted on chronological grounds —
  the author's method in miniature.**
- **Miracles attributed to Shah Gulshan, and his own limits** — "though some miracles are attributed
  to him one of his biographers holds that he could not always stand the 'Divine moments' and
  therefore mostly devoted himself to poetry" (p. 38, folio 36). **Attributed to "one of his
  biographers", unnamed.**
- **Signs of holiness in a child** — "Sayyid Mir Muhammadi, who had shown remarkable signs of
  holiness already in his childhood, died on 5. Rabi' II 1163/15.3.1750 at the age of 19" (p. 39,
  folio 37).
- **Dard's recognition of saintliness by sight** — "'he recognized the light of saintliness in the
  face of one of Mazhar's disciples' (F 142)", reported as "a friendly remark about Mazhar … found in
  a later source" (p. 24, folio 20).
- **Shaikh Tahir / Lal Udero's ecstatic states** — "about whose ecstatic 'states' strange legends
  are told" (p. 28, folio 24). **The legends themselves are not given** — **(not stated)**.
- **125,000 saints on Makli Hill** — "the famous Makli Hill near Thatta (the last resting place of
  125,000 saints, as legend has it)" (p. 26, folio 22). **The book marks it as legend in the same
  breath.**
- **Khidr as an initiator** — "the initiation through the spirit of a deceased saint or through the
  Prophetsaint Khidr was considered valid in most Sufi circles" (p. 26, folio 22).
- **Girhori's martyrdom, sought and obtained** — he "wanted to be a true martyr, shedding his blood
  for the defense of the true faith. Thus, he set out with 72 companions to destroy the Shiva idol in
  Hathungo, District Khairpur, and was killed there as he had hoped" (p. 28, folio 24).
- **The splitting of the moon, naturalised** — Waliullah "now and then even explains miracles like
  the splitting of the moon (Sura 54/1) as a natural phenomenon" (p. 21, folio 17). **Recorded here
  as a miracle the chunk reports being explained away.**

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **The chunk's organising thesis: political catastrophe produced religious literature.** "Thus, the
  18th century, politically perhaps the most saddening phase of Indo-Muslim civilization, proves to
  be the most fertile period in terms of religious literature—very similar to the situatio.. in the
  13th century, when the larger part of the Islamic Empire was devastated by the Mongol hordes, and
  yet the greatest mystical poetry and theory was produced between Cairo and India: it is as though a
  strange balance of power produces such effects" (pp. 30–31, folios 26–27). **Stated as a speculation
  ("it is as though"), and applied to Sind as it was to Delhi: "Just as the new spirit of Urdu poetry
  developed in 18th century Delhi, while the leading mystics looked for ways to lead their followers
  out of the darkness of the political situation, thus in Sind, too, the 18th century can be called
  the most important period for the formation of mystical poetry and prose" (pp. 30–31,
  folios 26–27).**
- **The three Delhi mystics are comparable and must be treated separately first.** "It is difficult
  to judge the relations of Mir Dard with his two senior compatriots. He never mentions any of them
  by name in his writings, nor do they mention him or his father" (p. 24, folio 20); "Before detailed
  studies and comparisons of the three mystics and their theories are possible, one has to discuss
  them one by one. Their approach to life and religion is largely the same" (p. 24, folio 20).
  **A stated negative finding — no mutual citation between the three — used to justify the book's
  structure.**
- **What the three shared, itemised.** "All the three alike stressed the importance of the unmitigated
  Divine Law as the center of Muslim life; all the three attacked philosophy and fake Sufism, and
  refused to be called 'Sufis,' a term that had deteriorated during their days even more than before;
  all the three were poets in Persian and Urdu, and Waliullah also in Arabic" (p. 24, folio 20).
  **The refusal of the name "Sufi" by all three reformers is a finding worth carrying into any entry
  about eighteenth-century shrine criticism.** And how they differ: "while he has become the center
  of interest lately for his religious reforms and his political engagement, and Mazhar remains the
  model of a true Naqshbandi leader, Mir Dard, the youngest of them, is considered the most successful
  mystical poet in the Urdu language, and regarded himself as the elected leader of his community"
  (p. 24, folio 20).
- **Waliullah's socio-geographical theory of why orders differ.** "Prophecy, according to his view,
  shows itself in different ways by polishing the innate qualities of a people as beautifully as
  possible. The innate character of the nation is the raw material on which the Prophet works, be it
  a candle or a clot of earth. This kind of socio-geographical explanation of the various activities
  of the Prophets is for him also the reason why the mystical orders differ in their approach: the
  Turks, living in a cold climate, hence stronger in their 'bestial' powers, needed and indeed
  produced a tartga"[OCR?] [tariqa] "like the Naqshbandiyya, which is best for crushing the lower
  instincts" (p. 21, folio 17, cited "Id. 1 28-34."[OCR?] [Id. I 28-34]). **An eighteenth-century
  climatic-determinist account of why silsilas differ — the author reports it without endorsement.**
- **Schimmel's own addition to Sorley on Hindu-Muslim mystical understanding.** To Sorley's analysis
  "we may add that the mystical understanding between the two great communities may perhaps have been
  facilitated by the activities of the Ismaili missionaries who came to Sind in the 14th and 15th
  centuries, after an Ismaili kingdom had existed in Multan around 900" (p. 28, folio 24). **Marked
  as a hypothesis — "may perhaps have been facilitated".**
- **Girhori is not to be generalised from.** "It would be wrong, however, to regard Girhori as a
  typical exponent of Sindhi Sufism" (p. 28, folio 24). **An explicit warning against taking a
  violent iconoclast as representative.**
- **Firaq as a source is repeatedly discounted.** "His descendant Firaq describes in his usual
  romantic style …" (p. 35, folio 33); "Firaq's description (as usual, greatly exaggerated) tells
  how …" (p. 37, folio 35); and on Muhammad Nasir's asceticism, "though we need not take too
  literally the detailed descriptions of his fasting and other ascetic achievements" (p. 35,
  folio 33). **The book's family-hagiography source is flagged as unreliable three times in three
  pages.**
- **Qani' as a source is discounted once, on a specific point.** His claim "that the mystical poet was
  ummi, 'illiterate;'" is "impossible to accept when one enters into the complicated web of thoughts
  displayed in the poems" (p. 29, folio 25) — though Qani' is otherwise called "one of the most
  important writers in the Persian language in India during the 18th century" (p. 27, folio 23).
- **The state of scholarship on Dard, stated as a gap.** "Besides some articles by the present writer
  (see Bibliography) there are barely studies on Mir Dard available" (p. 33, folio not stated); and
  the Dard-to-Sir Sayyid line "would be an interesting study that might contribute much to our
  knowledge of Indian Muslim revivalist movements" (p. 33, folio not stated). **Two research gaps the
  author names herself.**
- **Sirhindi's qayyumiyya compared to pantheism.** "Sirhindi's theory of gayyumiyya"[OCR?]
  [qayyumiyya] "surpasses by far the claims of most of the pantheistic mystics with the only, though
  important, difference that he does not speak, as did the pantheists, of union with God. Still, not
  many of the wujwdi"[OCR?] [wujudi] "mystics have claimed to be the depositary of Divine Mercy and
  to rule the universe" (p. 36, folio 34).
- **A hypothesis about Dard's debt to qayyum doctrine, explicitly left open.** "It is possible that
  some ideas which recur in Dard's work, especially those explaining the high spiritual rank of his
  father and the mystical graces bestowed upon himself, may have developed under the influence of
  some of the gayyumzyya"[OCR?] [qayyumiyya] "doctrines. But this assumption would need further
  clarification" (p. 37, folio 35).
- **The Naqshbandi turn under Aurangzeb explained.** Khwaja Muhammad Tahir "was granted by Aurangzeb
  high offices; for the religious mentality had turned, at that point of history, in favor of the
  Naqshbandiyya; the time of 'intoxication,' the dream of a mystical religion which might bridge the
  gap between Hinduism and Islam was over" (p. 34, folio 32). **A periodisation of Mughal religious
  policy in one sentence.**
