# schimmel_pain_and_grace — chunk 013 — pp. 236–254

**This chunk is one complete argumentative unit: `CHAPTER SEVEN / THE ISLAMIC BACKGROUND OF /
SHAH ABDUL LATIF'S POETRY` (p. 236, folio not stated), running unbroken to p. 254 and beyond the
chunk boundary** (p. 254 ends mid-sentence, "that a great part of Islamic prophetology has developed
out of this very conviction, that the"). It is **verse commentary, not biography or shrine
description**: Schimmel indexes the Quranic verses, hadith and Arabic proverbs quoted in the

biographical date, no shrine, no tomb, no ʿurs and no custodian anywhere in the body text of this
chunk** — see `## Practices, institutions, economy` for the explicit negative.

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **bhit-bhit-shah** (the book calls him "Shah Abdul Latif", "Shah Latif", "Shah" and once "Shah
  Sahib"): **the whole chunk is about his religion, and its thesis is that that religion is Islamic
  and not a syncretism.** The chapter opens by naming the reading it opposes — "The way Shah Abdul
  Latif unites his descriptions of Sufis and Yogis by explaining the wondrous life of the Hindu
  ascetics with Quranic terminology has induced many of his Hindu admirers and interpreters to regard
  his poetry as a perfect blending of the two religious traditions of India" (p. 236, folio not
  stated). **No dates, no family, no shrine and no silsila are given for him anywhere in this
  chunk** — all **(not stated)**.
- **bhit-bhit-shah**: **his Quranic range is stated as unusually broad, and that breadth is itself
  the evidence.** "A survey of the Quranic verses which are quoted in their original text shows a
  remarkable breadth of variety, and contrary to many other mystical poets who composed their
  ecstatic songs in the Islamic folk tongues Shah Latif does not concentrate upon a few central
  Quranic verses which have been interpreted as keywords for mystical theories time and again"
  (p. 237, folio 237).
- **bhit-bhit-shah**: **his placement in the history of Sufism, stated twice as archaism.** "Shah
  Abdul Latif is faithful to the pristine ascetic outlook of early Sufism in his powerful, poetic
  meditations about death and resurrection" (p. 237, folio 237); and on his names for God, "With this
  approach is he in full harmony though not with most of his contemporaries, but with the Sufis of
  old" (p. 238, folio 238).
- **bhit-bhit-shah**: **a structural claim about where the Islamic material sits in the poems.** "It
  seems typical that most of these allusions are inserted into the final part of each chapter, e.g.,
  the wa i, where Shah liked to express his truly Islamic feelings" (p. 238, folio 238) — "wa i" is
  printed in this chunk as "wa ’?", "wa ’i", "wai", "wa?i", "wd ’i", "wa °7" and "wd ?i"[OCR?] [the
  wāʾī]. A second distributional claim: "It is interesting that the Quranic verses which speak of the
  Light of God are all found in the more 'technical,' less folkloristic chapters" (p. 240, folio 240).
- **bhit-bhit-shah**: **his naming of God, and the resulting theology of unity.** "who is this God
  who is addressed in so many different terms as Beloved, Sweetheart, Master, Lord, the Near One"
  (p. 238, folio 238); "One is almost tempted to say that he, fond of the Most Beautiful Names of
  God, has invented new names for Him whom no name can properly describe" (p. 238, folio 238). The
  profession of faith is "the basso ostinato of the Risalo, beginning in the very first verse of Sur
  Kalyan and underlying all the other chapters" (p. 239, folio 239). God is called "the
  khaliqg"[OCR?] [khaliq], the Creator, "who can produce man out of a drop of sperm (natfo)" ("Pirbh.
  wa ’7 II"[OCR?]) (p. 239, folio 239), and "sattar, the one who covers sins" (p. 240, folio 240).
- **bhit-bhit-shah**: **a negative that Schimmel makes load-bearing — the monist formula he avoids.**

- **bhit-bhit-shah**: **his hadith repertoire is small and conservative, stated as a comparative

## Practices, institutions, economy

**Explicit negative, which is the main finding of this heading for this chunk: the chunk says
nothing whatever about ʿurs, mela, langar, offerings, custodianship (sajjada nashin, gadinishin,
Auqaf or family), endowments, land, revenue, visitor numbers, disputes, state involvement,
architecture or building dates.** It is verse commentary. The word "Bhit" occurs in this chunk only
in the verso running header, which is apparatus, and never in the body text. The few institutional
items the chunk does carry are religious-educational rather than economic:

- **Dhikr as a taught discipline, with the textual warrant named but the technique withheld.** "One
  central aspect of mystical education is mentioned several times in the Risal6é[OCR?] [Risalo]: that
  is man's duty to recollect God often"; the Divine promise "'Remember me and I will remember you'
  (Sura 2/147) … forms the basis for the theories of dhtkr[OCR?] [dhikr] in Sufi education ("Kal. II
  13, Sar. I wa ’i")" (p. 246, folio 246). "The practice of dhtkr[OCR?] [dhikr] has inspired Shah
  Abdul Latif to a whole chapter in the Rzsdlo[OCR?] [Risalo], although he never mentions any
  technicalities of the various stages and forms of the recollection, but rather takes dhikr as a
  constant occupation of the soul" (p. 246, folio 246). **A whole Sur is identified as a dhikr
  chapter but is not named here** — **(not stated)**.
- **Tauba as the entry requirement of the path.** "traditions about the necessity of repentance,
  tauba … 'Hurry with the repentance before death' is mentioned ("Asa III wa i"), however, without
  its usual continuation 'and hurry with the prayer before the prescribed hour has passed.' Tauba is
  the first step on the mystical path" (p. 248, folio 248).
- **The murshid as the boat's pilot — the chunk's one statement of the guide's institutional
  function.** "the lower instincts may deprive the boat of the soul of its precious merchandise when
  the mystical guide, as representative of the Prophet, does not guide it safely through the ocean of
  this world" (p. 248, folio 248). **"as representative of the Prophet" is the only warrant the chunk
  gives for a pir's authority.**
- **Transmission of devotion to the Prophet through school texts and household song.** "His
  veneration was taught in orthodox works like Makhdum Muhammad Hashim's Qut al-‘dshiqin[OCR?] [Qut
  al-ʿashiqin], 'Nurture of the Lovers,' and was imbibed by the children who learned even in
  lullabies something about the lofty qualities of Muhammad, and were instructed to follow his
  example even to the smallest detail" (pp. 253–254, folios 253–254). **A named schoolbook, a named
  domestic genre and a stated pedagogy — the closest the chunk comes to an institution.**
- **Prostration marks as a devotional sign read off the body.** "That implies a constant occupation
  with prayer so that the heroes of Kerbela are praised as those 'whose marks [from prostration] are
  on their faces' (Sura 48/29) ("Ked. V 10")" (p. 245, folio 245).
- **Music as a category of the Risalo's organisation.** "he … mentions it twice in tales connected
  with the power of music, e.g., in Raémakali[OCR?] [Ramakali] and Phirbati" (p. 250, folio 250).
- **The one economic image in the chunk is figurative** — the plundered boat and "its precious
  merchandise" (p. 248, folio 248) — and the one historical economic reference is "the years of
  poverty and misery that came over the country time and again" (p. 241, folio 241), given without
  date, place or figure — **(not stated)**.

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

**All of these are reported by Schimmel as what the poetry or the tradition says; none is asserted
as fact, and none attaches to a shrine.**

- **The elephant's prostration before Abdul Mutallib** — the chunk's one full karama. Of "Sur
  Sdrang[OCR?] [Sarang] (III wa 7)": "contains a lovely poem in maulud-type, e.g., a description of
  the miracles before and after Muhammad's birth: The elephant immediately performed a prostration
  when he saw the light on [Abdul] Mutallib's forehead" (p. 254, folio 254).
- **The Prophet's miracles, and Schimmel's parenthetical denial that they matter.** "The stories of
  his miracles (although he himself did not care at all for miracles, his only miracle being the
  message of the clear Arabic Quran) were known everywhere, and it sufficed to allude to them just
  with a little side-remark, to awaken the whole context in the listener's mind" (p. 254, folio 254).
- **The Prophet's intercession at Doomsday.** "in this terrible hour every prophet will ask for his
  own salvation; only Muhammad, model of the generous 'man of God' (fatd[OCR?] [fata], jawanmard)
  will ask that his community be saved" (p. 254, folio 254).
- **Sassui mistakes the angel of death for a messenger of her beloved**, and the same with Munkar and
  Nakir ("Abri VIII 8") (p. 237, folio 237). Schimmel adds the genre note: "Stories of a saint's
  meeting with the angel of death are not rare in classical Sufism" (p. 237, folio 237).
- **Hidden saints under God's domes** — "Shah Latif also quotes the famous hadith quds?[OCR?] [qudsi]
  in which God describes his hidden friends: 'Verily, my friends are under my domes' (gababi[OCR?]
  [qababi], or, as he has it with a common mistake: 'under my cloak', gabd i[OCR?] [qabaʾi]) ("Asa IV
  wa ’i"). This saying occurs often from the 10th century onward to designate the true saints, who
  are hidden from the eyes of the masses, protected by God who alone knows them and conceals them out
  of jealousy" (p. 249, folio 249). **A doctrine of concealed sanctity, and the q→g damage class in
  its clearest instance.**

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **The chapter's thesis, stated at the outset and confirmed at the end.** Against the Hindu
  interpreters' "perfect blending of the two religious traditions of India" (p. 236, folio not
  stated), Schimmel endorses Brohi — "If Shah for this high flight is indebted to any personality or
  to any book, it is Muhammad and, again, the Quran" (p. 236, folio not stated) — and Sorley — "the
  religion of the Resalo[OCR?] [Risalo] is thoroughly Islamic" (p. 252, folio 252). Her own
  formulation: "Shah Abdul Latif's description of God's qualities fits perfectly into the normal Sufi
  tradition" (p. 242, folio 242).
- **The diagnosis she offers for the Hindu readings, extended to the neighbouring Panjabi
  tradition.** "The tendency of interpreting into the poetry of Sufi saints too much of Vedantic
  philosophy is also visible in studies about the neighboring tradition, that of Panjabi mystical
  poetry, when studied by Hindu authors" (p. 236, folio not stated). **A claim about a whole
  scholarly literature, with no title cited** — **(not stated)**.
- **Her refusal of Watanmal's theology, quoted at length and then rejected.** Watanmal: "They try to
  make their belief in the prophet Mahommad consistent with their Sufi doctrines, an attempt in which
  they fail most ignominiously …"; "the Sufis can not, consistently with their pantheistic doctrines,
  say that the prophet Mahommad is the only medium of salvation …" (p. 252, folio 252); and his three
  hypotheses for Shah's "un-Sufi verses", ending "It may be that Shah Latif wanted to lead the minds
  of his followers slowly and gradually into higher Sufism by allowing them to believe first in their
  prophet" (p. 253, folio 253). Schimmel: "The author, after having thus told the Sufi what he ought
  and ought not believe" (p. 252, folio 252) and "These patronizing remarks are certainly far off the
  mark. Whosoever has studied Sufi poetry and prose knows that it was just the Sufis who developed
  the love of the Prophet in various forms among the people" (p. 253, folio 253).
- **Her general claim about the Prophet's place in Islam, which is the chunk's broadest thesis.** "it
  is the love of the Prophet which distinguishes the Muslim from every other religious community"
  (p. 253, folio 253), and "everyone shares in the feelings of admiration and trust, whether one
  interprets him as the intercessor, the Insén Kamil[OCR?] [Insan Kamil], the social or political
  reformer, the most loving human being" (p. 253, folio 253). She adds a regional fact: "the poetry
  written in honor of the Prophet in the Indus Valley is amazingly large" (p. 253, folio 253).
- **Three places where she marks her own reading as uncertain — record these as hedges, not
  findings.** On the Upanishad parallel to Sura 2/258: "We may ask ourselves whether or not Shah
  Latif thought, with this quotation in the context of a Hindu topic, of the Upanishad expression
  according to which men are led from the darkness towards the light. Yet, the idea is so general
  that it may be sheer coincidence" (pp. 240–241, folios 240–241). On the tresses: "described as
  'like [the letter] Kap[OCR?] [Kaf]; it may be licit, although perhaps too far-fetched, to see here
  an allusion to the letter Kaf by which creation began" (p. 243, folio 243). And she stops short
  altogether: "Some Muslim scholars have tried to go deeper into Shah Latif's Sindhi verse in order
  to discover more Quranic allusions; but in certain cases different interpretations are possible;
  therefore, we do not venture into this difficult matter" (p. 247, folio 247). **Also: "We may
  assume that many more allusions to Quranic verses are likely to be found in the Risalo" (p. 246,
  folio 246) — the index is explicitly incomplete.**
- **The "Quranization of memory" as her method for undetectable allusions, with a worked example.**
  "the Quran was so everpresent in his mind that he could allude to certain verses and events without
  mentioning them expressts verbiss[OCR?] [expressis verbis]. A typical example of this style seems
  to be the wa’?[OCR?] in Sarirag I with its rhyme -an, which inevitably leads the listener back to
  the Quranic model with this rhyme, e.g., the Surat ar-Rahman (Sura 55)" (pp. 246–247, folios
  246–247). **An argument from rhyme, not from quotation.**
- **A distributional argument she raises as a question and leaves open.** "Is it an accident that
  Shah never alludes to this hadith qudsi in his stories about the loving women, but mentions it
  twice in tales connected with the power of music, e.g., in Raémakali[OCR?] [Ramakali] and Phirbati?
  A similar distribution of a famous hadith occurs once more in the Resalo[OCR?] [Risalo]" (p. 250,
  folio 250).
- **The alast-covenant as the organising doctrine of the heroines' fidelity.** "his … favorite
  Quranic allusion is that of the pre-eternal covenant (Sura 7/171) Alastu bi-rabbikum, 'Am I not
  your Lord?' … by their answer bala, 'Yes,' they accepted all the affliction, bald[OCR?] [balaʾ],
  that will be showered upon them during their earthly lives" (p. 243, folio 243). "Marui had
  promised their friends in Malir to remain faithful to them, as the soul has promised the Lord to
  acknowledge His rule till Doomsday" (p. 243, folio 243); Sohni "knows that this pre-eternal love
  has bound her to Mehanwal, long before the souls were created: how, then, should she not swim
  towards him and die in the flood?" (p. 243, folio 243).
- **Fate as the explanation of the heroines' conduct — a determinism she draws out of the Tablet and
  Pen imagery.** "The imagery of Tablet and Pen is found in many places in the Risal6[OCR?] [Risalo]
  and belongs to the most important aspects of Shah's teachings. He knows that union cannot be
  achieved through human force" (p. 244, folio 244); Sohni's "strange behavior by crossing the river
  in cold winter days is again ascribed to her Fate" (p. 244, folio 244); "The qgismat[OCR?]
  [qismat], the inevitable fate, is a strong fetter ("Mumal I 8-9")" (p. 244, folio 244).
- **The nafs and the "greater jihad" as the chunk's reading of the heroic material.** "theirs is not
  the war against outward enemies, but rather the greater jrhdd[OCR?] [jihad], the Holy War against
  the lower self, the dangerous nafs, which is according to the Prophet's word, 'man's greatest
  enemy'" (p. 245, folio 245); "here, the nafs is compared to a majusi, an infidel Zoroastrian, who
  has to be slain" ("Bil. III 2, 12 ff.") (p. 248, folio 248).
- **The masculine-seeker proverb, dated and placed.** "the saying about talib al-maula which, though
  not a Prophetic hadith, had become one of the favorite proverbs in Indian Sufism: 'The seeker of
  this world is feminine, the seeker of the Otherworld is a catamite, and the seeker of the Lord is
  masculine.' This sentence, originating in 13th century India, once more praises the real 'man,' who
  can also be outwardly, like Rabi‘a or Sohni, of feminine sex" (pp. 247–248, folios 247–248).
- **The ascetic hadith she treats as diagnostic of Shah's archaism.** "The ascetic background of
  early Sufism, which otherwise seems to have almost disappeared among the mystical poets of Iran,
  Turkey, and India, is once more recalled by Shah Latif's use of the tradition 'The world is a
  rotting carcass, and those who seek it are dogs,' which occurs twice ("Sar. III wa ’7, Ma‘ dh I
  9")" (p. 247, folio 247).
