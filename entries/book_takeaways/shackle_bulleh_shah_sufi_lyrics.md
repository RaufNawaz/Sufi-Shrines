# Sufi Lyrics — takeaways

*Source: Bullhe Shah; Christopher Shackle (tr.), *Sufi Lyrics* (Murty Classical Library of India,
Volume 1; Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard University Press, 2015; new edition 2021). 209 pages,
transcribed `text_layer`; provenance in `out/ocr/shackle_bulleh_shah_sufi_lyrics/`. Page references
are PDF page indices; folio = printed page number where known. Reviewed: no.*

## What this book is

A scholarly English translation of 157 Panjabi *kāfī*s attributed to Bullhe Shah, with a
twenty-page introduction, an apparatus of endnotes keyed poem by poem, a glossary and a
bibliography. Shackle writes as an editor and translator rather than as a hagiographer: the
introduction is explicitly corrective ("Most of these interpretations may be shown to rely upon
often highly selective readings of his poetry, which are used to support already assumed
ideological positions", pp. 12–13, folios xi–xii), and the notes run a textual-critical apparatus
over six named Panjabi editions whose disagreements are recorded rather than smoothed. For this
archive the book's value sits in three places: the introduction (pp. 8–25, folios vii–xxiv), the
Notes to the Translation (pp. 154–196, folios 130–171) and the Glossary (pp. 198–201, folios
173–176). The 157 lyrics themselves (pp. 28–150, folios 3–125) name no shrine, tomb, ʿurs,
custodian, endowment or building date anywhere: the two shrine references in the whole verse
corpus are "On Monday I go to the shrine and pray to the prophets and saints" (p. 90, folio 65)
and "I search for you at the gate of the shrine, but you get up and flee" (p. 144, folio 119),
and neither names a shrine.

### mazar-of-bulleh-shah — Mazar of Bulleh Shah

The book's subject, present on nearly every page of it. The book's spelling throughout the
introduction and notes is **"Bullhe Shah"**, never "Bulleh Shah"; inside the lyrics he signs
himself **"Bullha"**, **"Bullha Shah"**, **"Bullhe"** and **"Bullhe Shah"** interchangeably, and
the volume never explains the variation (pp. 42–150, folios 17–125; the variation is sharpest at
pp. 51–52, folios 26–27, where both forms stand two pages apart). The bibliography adds seven
further romanizations from other people's title pages: "Bullhe Shāh", "Bulhe Shāh", "Bulleh Shah",
"Bullah Shah", "Bulleh Shaah", "Bullheshāh", "Bullah" (p. 202, folio 177).

- **Saint**
  - *Name, title and signature, explained.* "The title 'Bullhe Shah,' by which the poet is commonly
    known, is the honorific form proper to a Sayyid descendant of the prophet Muhammad. His usual
    poetic signature, 'Bullha,' is the familiar form of his given name, Abdullah." (p. 8, folio
    vii). The spelling convention is the book's own: "These Eng­lish [text-layer?] spellings
    re­flect [text-layer?] the way his name is normally written in Panjabi, where a final 'h' is
    not pronounced" (p. 8, folio vii). The Glossary confirms the given name — "ABDULLAH
    (‘Abdullāh) name of the prophet Muhammad's father; also the given name of Bullhe Shah."
    (p. 198, folio 173). A note records a wordplay used as evidence of authorship: "There is a
    wordplay between cullhā, 'hearth,' and the name Bullha." (p. 179, folio 154, n. 2 on 86).
  - *Death year — the book gives it three different ways and does not reduce them to one.*
    "Bullhe Shah (d. 1758)" (p. 8, folio vii); the tomb chronogram, "It bears a Persian chronogram
    that gives the year of his death as A.H. 1171 according to the Muslim calendar, equivalent to
    1757 or 1758 C.E. (the later year is somewhat more probable)." (pp. 8–9, folios vii–viii);
    and the Library of Congress data as printed, with the book's own question marks and macron,
    "Bullhe Shāh, 1680?-1758?, author." (p. 5, folio not stated). **A birth year appears only in
    that CIP line, and only as "1680?".** Kept side by side; the archive row carries 1757, i.e.
    against the book's own stated preference. See Doubts.
  - *The biographical record is thin, and the book says so.* "Bullhe Shah (d. 1758) has long been
    rightly regarded as the greatest master of the mystical Sufi lyric in Panjabi, but remarkably
    few details of his life can be reliably established. Neither the exact date nor the precise
    place of his birth is known for certain." (p. 8, folio vii); "With so little known of his life,
    he came to be seen as, among other things, an exponent of universal spiritual truths owing as
    much to Vedanta as to Sufism" (p. 12, folio xi).
  - *Dating, stated in passing elsewhere.* He is "an eighteenth-century Muslim poet" (p. 189, folio
    164, n. 3 on 133); the only other dating anywhere in the back matter is inside a third party's
    title, "the Eighteenth-Century Panjabi Sūfī Poet Bullhe Śāh" (Matringe 1992, p. 203,
    folio 178).
  - *Hometown, named as Kasur.* "S suggests that the phrase 'citadel of knowledge' (koṭ ilam de)
    refers to Bullhe Shah's hometown of Kasur, which was famed for the learning of the local
    Islamic scholar Maulana Ghulam Murtaza, called 'The Master of the Panjab' (maḳhdūm-e panjāb)."
    (p. 155, folio 130); Glossary, "Kasur (Qasūr) hometown of Bullhe Shah." (p. 199, folio 174).
    A second note places another toponym "in District Kasur" without naming it (p. 165, folio 140).
    **Kasur is the only place the book attaches to him.**
  - *Caste and descent, stated as a Sayyid against his master's Arain.* "The discrepancy between
    Bullhe Shah's own high status as a Sayyid and the lower rank of Shah Inayat as an Arain is a
    prominent theme of the poet's hagiography. See also 24, 70, 108, 151." (p. 165, folio 140);
    "the familiar theme of the disparity in outward status between the Sayyid poet and his revered
    master from the lower-ranking Arain caste" (p. 183, folio 158, n. 1 on 108); "Sayyids are all
    descended from the Prophet through his son-in-law Ali, the husband of his daughter Fatima."
    (p. 183, folio 158, n. 2 on 108). In the poems he repudiates the title outright: "May anyone
    who calls us a Sayyid suffer the punishments of hell. Swings are set up in paradise for anyone
    who calls us an Arain." (p. 113, folio 88), in a poem where "Bullha's1 sisters and his
    brothers' wives" reproach him — "stop clinging to the Arain's skirt. Why have you brought
    disgrace on the family of the Prophet and the descendants of Ali?" (p. 113, folio 88).
  - *Order — the book contradicts itself and both statements are kept.* The Glossary says "qadiri
    (Qādirī) Sufi order with which Shah Inayat and Bullhe Shah were affiliated." (p. 199, folio
    174). Against that, the introduction's endnote 18 attaches only the Shattari, and only to the
    master, not to the poet: the doctrine of self-obliteration in the master "(Ar. fanā fī
    'l-shaiḳh) was given particular importance within the Shattari order with which Shah Inayat was
    affiliated" (p. 24, folio xxiii) — and chunk 001 records that **no silsila, order or lineage is
    claimed for Bullhe Shah anywhere in the introduction**, (not stated). The archive row's
    "Abdullah Shah Qadri" is therefore half supported: the book gives "Abdullah" as his given name
    (p. 8, folio vii; p. 198, folio 173) and gives "Qadiri" only in the Glossary line above.
  - *Allegiance, stated as a choice against the local scholar.* "Rather than study with this great
    scholar, Bullhe Shah gave his allegiance to Shah Inayat, from whom he received the word
    (kalmā) of his Sufi teaching." (p. 155, folio 130).
  - *No descendants.* "Bullhe Shah remained unmarried and left no direct descendants." (p. 8, folio
    vii). This sits directly against the book's own general statement that shrines "were themselves
    typically administered by a master's lineal descendants" (p. 11, folio x) — recorded as the
    book has it, not reconciled.
  - *He calls himself a fakir, unlettered and beaten.* "Bullhe Shah is a fakir of the lord; he
    disturbs those who are awake" (p. 79, folio 54); "Bullhe Shah is a poor fakir; he has departed,
    proclaiming to the sound of the drum" (p. 141, folio 116); "'Dunce'1 is my name, oh. The mullah
    taught me the lesson, but I could not get beyond alif.2 I suffered a beating with his slippers"
    (p. 102, folio 77), glossed at "The word 'dunce' (kuṭīcal)… with the sense of 'a child who is
    regularly beaten for poor performance in class.'" (p. 180, folio 155, n. 1 on 92).
  - *He refuses every confessional, communal and regional placing of himself.* "Bullha is not a
    Shia or a Sunni, neither a learned scholar nor a magician or philosopher." (p. 46, folio 21);
    "I am not a Hindu, nor a Muslim. I have forsaken pride and become unsullied. I am not a Sunni,
    nor a Shia. I have adopted the path of peace toward all." (p. 58, folio 33); "Bullha,1 what do
    I know about who I am? … I am neither an Arab nor from Lahore, nor an Indian from the city of
    Nagaur.4 I am not a Hindu, nor a Turk5 from Peshawar. Nor do I live in Nadaun.6" (p. 112, folio
    87); "I am not twice-born, nor am I thrice-born.4 I do not recognize pure and impure." (p. 131,
    folio 106).
  - *He speaks in a woman's voice throughout.* "I, Bullha, am the slave girl of the lord" (p. 110,
    folio 85); "I1 am a poor sweeper girl from the establishment of the true lord" (p. 130, folio
    105); "Ranjha is a yogi and I am his yogini" (p. 142, folio 117).
  - *Language choice is thematic, and the book says so.* "Like other kāfīs on Hindu themes, this
    poem is written in Hindi, the characteristic language of the yogis. Compare 15, 20, 66, 113."
    (p. 155, folio 130); "The Hindu imagery of this poem is typically reinforced by a preponderance
    of Hindi vocabulary." (p. 184, folio 159, n. 1 on 113); and of kāfī 35, "The language is fairly
    standard Urdu, and it lacks the usual refrain of a kāfī. It is actually a ghazal in the hazaj
    meter of four feet." (p. 166, folio 141).
  - *A named Persian model for one of the famous kāfīs.* "It has often been observed this famous
    kāfī is modeled on Rumi's great Persian ghazal beginning cih tadbīr ai musalmānān kih man
    ḳhud-rā namīdānam… 'What can I do, oh Muslims, for I do not know myself. I am not a Christian
    nor a Jew, not a Zoroastrian nor a Muslim.'" (p. 167, folio 142). Separately, "This is a unique
    reference in Bullhe Shah's poetry to the great Sufi teacher and poet Jalal ud Din Rumi (d.
    1273)" (p. 177, folio 152, n. 4 on 75).
  - *The poems the translator ranks highest.* kāfī 106 — "The natural appeal of this kāfī across
    religious and national boundaries has helped it become probably the best known of all Bullhe
    Shah's lyrics." (p. 183, folio 158); kāfī 119 — "one of Bullhe Shah's most famous compositions"
    (p. 185, folio 160); kāfī 145 — "This is one of Bullhe Shah's most famous kāfīs." (p. 193,
    folio 168); kāfī 105 — "This very famous kāfī is best known by this second verse of the refrain
    ending with the syllables thaīā thaīā, which are used by teachers of dancing to mark the rhythm
    of the steps." (p. 182, folio 157).
- **Shrine / Darbar**
  - *The tomb and its location — the book's one direct statement.* "His later life was apparently
    spent in Kasur, which is the site of his tomb. It bears a Persian chronogram that gives the
    year of his death as A.H. 1171 according to the Muslim calendar, equivalent to 1757 or 1758
    C.E." (pp. 8–9, folios vii–viii). **No street, quarter, patron, building date, ʿurs, sajjada
    nashin, custodian or endowment is given anywhere in the volume** — all (not stated). The text
    of the chronogram is (not stated); it is the only inscription named in the book.
  - *The present-day fabric, secondhand and in an endnote.* Note 2 cites "Rama Krishna 1938: 40–46
    and Quraeshi 2009: 241–258, which is illustrated with photographs showing the radically
    modernized appearance of Bullhe Shah's shrine in Kasur today." (p. 23, folio xxii). This is the
    volume's **only** statement about the built shrine; it is Shackle reporting what another
    author's photographs show, and it gives no date, agency or description of the modernization.
  - *The two registers on Kasur do not quite agree.* p. 8 (folio vii) calls Kasur "the site of his
    tomb"; the Glossary (p. 199, folio 174) and the note at p. 155 (folio 130) call Kasur only his
    "hometown" and attach no mazar, ʿurs or custodian to it. Both are recorded; neither is
    reconciled by the book.
- **Practices & Events**
  - *Dancing is named as his practice and as what brought him back to his master.* "Bullha, let us
    go and sit at the gate of Shah Inayat, who made me dress in green and red.4 When I started
    dancing, I found my way to him" (p. 111, folio 86; also quoted at p. 19, folio xviii); "Bullha,
    the lord has made me dance, and the sound is very loud" (p. 115, folio 90); "I have stripped
    off my blanket and dance, making him hurl astonished looks at me." (p. 52, folio 27); "Bullha,
    it is indeed a strange thing for a woman to start dancing with her veil on." (p. 51, folio 26).
    The reproach is quoted too: "Satan makes her dance in the courtyard. Talk to her and make her
    stop."1 (p. 116, folio 91).
  - *His verse is composed for performance.* "The main lyrical form is a strophic poem with a
    refrain, called kāfī, which is designed for singing in qawwali and also lends itself to solo
    singing by amateur devotees and professional performers." (p. 13, folio xii); the signature
    verse is a fixed formal feature — "The final verse of the kāfī is regularly marked by the
    poet's signature." (p. 14, folio xiii).
  - *Present-day circulation across communal lines.* "whether through performances by well-known
    Sikh or Muslim sing-ers or through the popular selections issued by Indian and Pakistani
    publishers" (p. 9, folio viii).
- **Legends & Miracles**
  - *The dancing-girl episode, and the book hedges it every time it tells it.* "The poetry also
    bears out the gist of the most popular tale of Bullhe Shah, of how he once fell out of favor
    with his guide, **allegedly** for being too outspoken in his criticisms of formal Islam, and
    was dismissed from his company. Since Bullhe Shah knew of Shah Inayat's fondness for music and
    dancing, he **is then said to have** gone away and taken lessons with a dancing girl, then
    reappeared in Lahore one day, himself dressed as a dancing girl. There he danced and sang in
    front of Shah Inayat, who was won over by his performance and readmitted him to his favor"
    (pp. 18–19, folios xvii–xviii). In the notes it is attributed to the readership, not to the
    translator: "The most popular understanding of the verse is that it refers to an actual
    incident in which Bullhe Shah, having fallen out with Shah Inayat, dressed up as a dancing girl
    and regained his master's favor with his performance. Compare 5, 77, 126, 149." (p. 182, folio
    157, n. 4 on 105); "the famous story of Bullhe Shah dressing up as a dancing girl to win Shah
    Inayat's favor" (p. 187, folio 162, n. 1 on 126); "The verse is sometimes taken to be a direct
    reference to the story" (p. 194, folio 169, n. 3 on 149); "The reference is to the famous
    episode in which Bullhe Shah disguised himself as a dancing girl to regain the affection of
    Shah Inayat. See 105." (p. 156, folio 131).
  - *The modern iconography of the dancing saint.* "The image of Bullhe Shah dancing is a popular
    one in modern iconography; see Frembgen 2006: 106–107." (p. 24, folio xxiii).
  - **No miracle of any kind is attributed to him anywhere in the volume.** The dancing-girl tale
    is the only hagiographic narrative about him in the book, and the translator diagnoses the
    hagiography as a cause of textual growth rather than as evidence — see Significance.
- **Significance**
  - *The political setting of his lifetime.* "He lived at a time of great social upheaval in
    Panjab,1 as the central authority of the declining Mughal empire came under increasing
    challenge from armed rebellion by the Sikhs and from local notables establishing their
    auton-omy." (p. 8, folio vii); "these unsettled conditions are occasionally re­flected
    [text-layer?] in the poetry" but "its main focus is on his spiritual guide" (p. 8, folio vii).
    The notes gloss the same: "The social turmoil attendant on the internal uprisings of the Sikhs
    against the authority of the Mughals, which is the unusual subject of this kāfī, is interpreted
    as a manifestation of the mysterious workings of the divine beloved." (pp. 155–156, folios
    130–131).
  - *A rupture with the master, dated by circumstance and not by year.* "The allusive verses of
    this kāfī appear to refer to a separation of Bullhe Shah from Shah Inayat due to the troubled
    conditions then prevailing in Panjab." (p. 165, folio 140); in the poems, "With the departure
    of Inayat Qadiri,1 my story has again become difficult" (p. 104, folio 79). The only
    chronological marker the poems give is an era the book supplies in a note: "The twelfth century
    of the Hijri era corresponds to the eighteenth century C.E. (1100 A.H. = 1688 C.E.)."
    (pp. 165–166, folios 140–141), glossing "The twelfth century has come with gaping mouth."
    (p. 52, folio 27).
  - *Hagiography is diagnosed as a cause of textual growth — the translator's explicit argument.*
    "It seems likely that this kāfī is a later hagiographic elaboration than 31 of the familiar
    theme of the disparity in outward status between the Sayyid poet and his revered master from
    the lower-ranking Arain caste. Its genuineness is anyway open to question on stylistic grounds"
    (p. 183, folio 158, n. 1 on 108); and of another poem, "It has not been included in any of the
    subsequent standard editions of Bullhe Shah's poetry, unlike the similarly sourced 108, which
    fulfills a more satisfying hagiographic need." (p. 176, folio 151, n. 1 on 72). **This bears
    directly on how the dancing-girl story may be used.**
  - *Corrective purpose.* Twentieth-century readings were "increas-ingly in­flu­enced [text-layer?]
    by the nationalist thinking prevalent among the new middle class of the colonial period"; he
    "became subject to a va­ri­ety [text-layer?] of interpreta-tions.11 … The present volume may
    help correct the biases that have too often continued to distort un­der­stand­ings
    [text-layer?] of Bullhe Shah." (pp. 12–13, folios xi–xii). The four readings listed: "an
    exponent of universal spiritual truths owing as much to Vedanta as to Sufism; an authen-tic
    spokesman of the Panjabi folk tradition; a social revolu-tionary; or simply a Romantic poet
    avant la lettre.12" (p. 12, folio xi).
  - *Against reading him as a social revolutionary — an explicit proportion argument.* "Bullhe
    Shah's critique of the social upheavals in early eigh­teenth-century [text-layer?] Panjab,
    which is so interesting to many modern readers, represents only a very small, if vividly
    expressed, part of his poetry. Like his occasional pungent attacks on the dishonesty of
    contemporary religious special-ists, verses expressing social critique are quite subsidiary in
    quantity to the poems of admonition designed to arouse a very Islamic sense of the
    inevitability of death" (pp. 15–16, folios xiv–xv); the didactic poetry is in turn
    "subsidi-ary to the mystical vision that is its principal theme" (pp. 16–17, folios xv–xvi).
  - *Textual authenticity.* "Absolute authenticity in the precise wording of any poem generally
    agreed to be by Bullhe Shah is hardly to be expected… But while its boundaries cannot be
    de­fined [text-layer?] with absolute preci-sion,14 the corpus that has come down to us provides
    ample evidence of a powerfully coherent poetical and mystical imagination." (p. 14, folio
    xiii). The corpus size: "The present volume includes 157 of these lyrics, although the
    uncertain transmission of the text makes it dif  ­fi­cult [text-layer?] to be sure of the exact
    number of authentic items." (p. 13, folio xii).
  - *The editions he was constituted from.* Nine Panjabi editions, 1889–2008, and eleven English
    versions, 1940–2012, are listed under "Editions and Translations" (p. 202, folio 177): Ruhtaki
    1889 *Qānūn-e ‘ishq* (Lahore), Qasuri 1896 *Kāfīhā-e Hazrat Bullhe Shāh* (Qasur), Ubirai 1930
    (Lahore), Faqir 1960 *Kulliyāt-e Bullhe Shāh* (Lahore), Ahmad 1976 (Lahore), Sabir 1991
    *Bullhe Shāh: Mukammal Kāfiyāñ* (Lahore), Khan 1992 (Lahore), Singh and Singh 2004 (Delhi),
    Jagtar 2008 (Chandigarh); among the English, Rafat 1982 *Bulleh Shah: A Selection Rendered into
    English Verse* (Lahore), Puri and Shangari 1986 *The Love-Intoxicated Iconoclast* (Dera Baba
    Jaimal Singh), Duggal 1996 (New Delhi), Ghaffar 2005 (Lahore), Smith 2012 (Campbells Creek).
    The secondary studies of him: Kohli 1987 (p. 203, folio 178), Matringe 1992 (p. 203, folio
    178), Rinehart 1996 and `[text-layer?]` "   . 1999." [———. 1999.] "The Portable Bullhe Shah:
    Biography, Categorization, and Authorship in the Study of Punjabi Sufi Poetry" (p. 204, folio
    179), Usborne 1982 (p. 205, folio 180). The introduction's verdict on that literature: studies
    of him in Eng­lish [text-layer?], "**none of which is at all comprehensive or very critical**"
    (p. 24, folio xxiii).

### shrine-of-shah-inayat-qadiri — Shrine of Shah Inayat Qadiri

The book's second subject. Its forms are "Shah Inayat", "Inayat", "Inayat Qadiri", "our guide",
"his human guide", "the master" and, in the Glossary, "INAYAT (Ar. ‘ināyat 'favor') Bullhe Shah's
spiritual guide Shah Inayat." (p. 199, folio 174). **The book never prints the compound "Shah
Inayat Qadiri", never names Mozang and never mentions a tomb, a shrine, an ʿurs or a successor for
him** — see Doubts before any of this enters an entry.

- **Saint**
  - *Death year, given once.* The poetry's "main focus is on his spiritual guide, noted Sufi master
    Shah Inayat (d. 1728). Bullhe's repeated references to Shah Inayat testify to the passionate
    quality of his devotion to his master.2" (p. 8, folio vii). It is the only date the book gives
    him.
  - *Placed at Lahore, explicitly and twice.* "The city that is the seat of royal authority in
    Panjab (hence called taḳhat lahor) is here celebrated as the home of Shah Inayat. Compare 121,
    123." (p. 185, folio 160, n. 1 on 120); in the poems, "Baghdad is the place of the Pir of
    Pirs;4 our guide is in royal Lahore" (p. 122, folio 97), in the same poem and within four lines
    of "Shah Inayat has set out his hooks, and without being seen he tugs the line" (p. 122, folio
    97). The dancing-girl story is also set there: Bullhe Shah "reappeared in Lahore one day,
    himself dressed as a dancing girl. There he danced and sang in front of Shah Inayat" (p. 19,
    folio xviii).
  - *Order — the book contradicts itself, and both statements stand.* Qadiri: "Shah Inayat was
    affiliated to the Qadiri order of Sufis. Compare 126." (p. 180, folio 155, n. 1 on 94);
    "qadiri (Qādirī) Sufi order with which Shah Inayat and Bullhe Shah were affiliated." (p. 199,
    folio 174); and in the verse, "Come, Inayat Qadiri,5 my heart desires you" (p. 127, folio 102),
    "With the departure of Inayat Qadiri,1 my story has again become difficult" (p. 104, folio 79).
    Shattari: the doctrine of self-obliteration in the master "(Ar. fanā fī 'l-shaiḳh) was given
    particular importance within the Shattari order with which Shah Inayat was affiliated; compare
    Ahmad 2012: 151–154." (p. 24, folio xxiii). **These two are not reconciled anywhere in the
    volume**, and the Shattari statement is the only order named in the whole introduction. Both
    are recorded; see Doubts.
  - *Caste, stated three times.* "arain (Arāīñ) the caste of vegetable gardeners to which Shah
    Inayat belonged." (p. 198, folio 173); "his revered master from the lower-ranking Arain caste"
    (p. 183, folio 158); "Just as Ranjha was despised by Hir's family, so too was Shah Inayat
    disparaged for being a member of the Arain caste. See 31." (p. 195, folio 170, n. 4 on 151);
    and "the lower rank of Shah Inayat as an Arain" (p. 165, folio 140).
  - *He wrote in Persian.* "Perhaps a general reference to the teaching of Shah Inayat, not
    necessarily to his written Persian treatises." (p. 180, folio 155, n. 2 on 91). The treatises
    are neither named nor dated — (not stated).
  - *The name itself is glossed and played on.* "There is a play on the literal meaning, 'grace,'
    of the name (ināit, Ar. ‘ināyat) of Bullhe Shah's master, Shah Inayat." (p. 173, folio 148,
    n. 7 on 59). An epithet is identified for him — "Oh you with a great family" (vaḍ parvāriā),

  through performances by well-known Sikh or Muslim sing-ers" (p. 9, folio viii).
- **Significance — the book's argument that Panjabi Sufi poetry and Sikh hymnody are two distinct
  traditions, separately transmitted, and asymmetrically self-sufficient.** "In the case of
  Panjabi, these two ­genres [text-layer?] of religious poetry are represented by two distinct
  traditions,3 which have been quite separately preserved and transmitted." (pp. 9–10, folios
  viii–ix). The shared feature is "their common preference for popular poetic styles that draw upon
  folk-songs rather than the learned poetry of the courts.4" (p. 10, folio ix) — but the asymmetry
  is the argument: the Gurus' hymns "may to a considerable extent be legitimately understood in
  their own terms", whereas Panjabi Sufi poetry "is hardly to be understood properly without wider
  reference to the larger religious and literary traditions by which it is so intimately informed"
  (p. 10, folio ix). Against that, the Sufi lyrics "were never systematically preserved by an
  or­ga­nized [text-layer?] faith community, and they were for the most part rec­orded
  [text-layer?] and assembled in printed collections only in the late nineteenth century." (p. 10,
  folio ix) — i.e. the Ādi Granth's canonicity is the contrast that explains the Sufi corpus's
  textual instability.
- **A Sikh category named by an eighteenth-century Muslim poet, and the book calls it unusual.**
  "This mention by an eighteenth-century Muslim poet of the distinctive 'path' (panth) of the Sikhs
  as distinct from both Muslims and Hindus is very unusual" — so unusual that it raises "the
  suspicion that the verse may be a later interpolation" (p. 189, folio 164, n. 3 on 133).

### Samāʿ, qawwali and the shrine economy

- **The single most archive-usable passage in the book, and it is general rather than about any
  named site.** "Persian ghazals were sung in musical performances (called samā‘) that were a
  central part of ritual practice and usually took place at the shrines constructed around the
  tombs of former saints, which were themselves typically administered by a master's lineal
  descendants. They employed the specialist professional musicians known as qawwals. Their strongly
  rhythmic style of performance (called qawwali) has become a well-recog-nized form of world music
  while continuing to be practiced in its original setting.8" (p. 11, folio x). The endnote sends
  the reader to "Qureshi 1986; for the local context of Sufi music and its performers, see Pannke
  1999" (p. 24, folio xxiii), listed in full at p. 204 (folio 179) and p. 203 (folio 178).
- **Annual shrine festivals, with wrestling and sports.** "The image of the contest (chinj) is
  taken from the practice of putting on shows of wrestling and other sports at shrines on the
  occasion of their annual festivals. It is here developed to describe what happens to the soul
  after death." (p. 195, folio 170, n. 1 on 152). **The word ʿurs is never used in this book**; it
  says "annual festivals".
- **The kāfī is built for that setting.** "The main lyrical form is a strophic poem with a refrain,
  called kāfī, which is designed for singing in qawwali and also lends itself to solo singing by
  amateur devotees and professional performers." (p. 13, folio xii). Performance also damages the
  text: "the well-known liberties taken by professional sing-ers and the uncertainties of the
  textual transmission" (p. 14, folio xiii).
- **Shrine visitation on a named weekday — the only such practice in the 157 lyrics, and no shrine
  is named.** "On Monday I go to the shrine and pray to the prophets and saints. When I gain a
  vision of the beloved, I adorn myself" (p. 90, folio 65), inside a weekday scheme: "Friday brings
  a special delight… Monday1 gave us pains; on Tuesday we gained no understanding. We were
  sacrificed to love on Saturday. Wednesday2 removed our awareness" (p. 90, folio 65). The other
  shrine reference in the corpus is "I search for you at the gate of the shrine, but you get up and
  flee." (p. 144, folio 119).

### Custodianship, clerical ranks and their incomes

- **Lineal-descendant custodianship, stated as the norm** (p. 11, folio x, quoted above) — and set
  against the book's own statement that this poet "remained unmarried and left no direct
  descendants" (p. 8, folio vii).
- **The ranks, and what they are paid.** "A qazi is a religious judge, who ranks higher than the
  humble mullah attached to a local mosque. Qazis are regularly castigated for their greed and
  willingness to take bribes." (p. 161, folio 136); "Mullahs are paid a small fee to recite in
  Arabic when butchers slaughter an animal in the prescribed halal fashion by cutting its throat."
  (p. 161, folio 136); "The writing of religious formulae to be worn on the body as protective
  amulets (tāvīz) is a recognized skill of Muslim religious specialists." (p. 161, folio 136).
- **Life-cycle ritual as a fee-taking trade.** "Here the 'robbers' (ṭhag) are the religious
  specialists who create the rituals surrounding birth and death and live off the fees they earn
  from them." (p. 158, folio 133).
- **The pilgrimage economy, stated flatly as exploitation.** "The prestige gained from their
  performance of the hajj, or pilgrimage to Mecca, is exploited by the hajjis on their return in
  order to increase the offerings of the pious." (pp. 160–161, folios 135–136). The poems put it
  more bluntly: "Many become Hajjis and come wearing blue garments. They sell the Haj and live on
  the coins they collect, but who can possibly be pleased by this?" (p. 44, folio 19).
- **Brahman ritual economy, itemised — the densest such passage in the verse.** "The Brahmans2 have
  come to scare their patrons. They make them anxious by telling them how their ancestors suffer"
  (p. 79, folio 54); the demand in quoted speech, "'Your ancestors are in pain. Make offerings of
  raw sugar, rice, and cloth. Undo your shirt and put on your sacred thread.' Then they are quickly
  given a handful of Ganges water" (pp. 79–80, folios 54–55); and the verdict, "This is not how
  suffering is removed, with cash, rupees, vessels, and cattle" (p. 80, folio 55).
- **The learned classes as an economy living off the poor**, poem 26 (pp. 45–46, folios 20–21):
  "Reading and reading, you became a Shaikh of Shaikhs, who filled his belly and slept his fill";
  "from your house you devise tricky questions of the law. You eat by plundering the foolish";
  "you become a mullah, then a qazi, but God is content without all this learning. Your greed
  increases day by day, and your pious intent is set on profit"; "Holy men are arrested and thieves
  are released". Shackle calls these "his occasional pungent attacks on the dishonesty of
  contemporary religious special-ists" (p. 15, folio xiv).

- **The certificate of exemption from forced labour — the chunk's densest institutional passage,
  spoken by a sweeper girl.** "I1 am a poor sweeper girl from the establishment of the true lord.
  With the fan of attention and the broom of knowledge I keep sweeping up lust and anger… The qazi
  knows, the governor knows, I have a certificate freeing me from forced labor." (p. 130, folio
  105); "If I take hold of my fan and make greed fly away, I am exempted from being taxed… I have
  made a pile of grain6 from my gleanings… Bullha, if I am owned by the lord, I am released from
  forced labor" (p. 131, folio 106). The speaker's status is glossed: "As a sweeper girl of the
  untouchable Chuhra caste, she is reckoned to be neither a Hindu nor a Muslim." (p. 189, folio
  164, n. 3 on 131).
- **Agricultural labour under a revenue establishment.** "The master of this safflower is harsh,
  and his accountant is cruel. This safflower has four officers.2 They demand a hefty sum… I have
  filled my basket… The merchants have come to stay." (p. 128, folio 103).
- **Tax, tolls and collectors.** "The constables who pursued us have gone off to pay in our taxes."
  (p. 146, folio 121); "At the crossing place the constables will demand the toll, so say: what
  good deeds will you have to show?" (p. 55, folio 30); "The constables pursued us there, so we
  came here." (p. 78, folio 53); and the metaphor made explicit, "The relentless emissaries of
  death are compared to tax-collectors' agents." (p. 195, folio 170, n. 2 on 152).
- **Money, weights and credit.** "A maund (maṇ) is a large measure of weight, equivalent to 80
  pounds." (p. 166, folio 141); "Cowrie shells were used as the smallest units of currency."
  (p. 181, folio 156, n. 3 on 98); "Before the introduction of pockets and purses, money was kept
  tied in the hem of a garment." (p. 166, folio 141); "Literally, '100,000,' i.e., a large reward."
  (p. 191, folio 166, n. 1 on 139); usury in the verse, "On whatever you give, you charge an extra
  25 percent." (p. 101, folio 76).
- **Peddlers and rural credit, read as a figure for saints.** "The 'traders' (vaṇjāre) are the
  peddlers who supplied the village women with their jewelry, and are here as often elsewhere used
  to symbolize the saints who bear the uniquely valuable message of all-demanding love for the
  divine. Compare 70, 117." (p. 166, folio 141). The ruby market of poem 36 works the same ground
  transaction by transaction (pp. 53–54, folios 28–29): "'Oh brother ruby man, tell me what the
  price is.' 'If you have come to buy rubies, let your head be removed from your body.'"

### Women's work, marriage and dowry

- **The spinning party as a village institution.** "The spinning party (attaṇ) is where the girls
  of a village traditionally gathered together to spin. Compare 70." (p. 165, folio 140); "These
  parties (trinjaṇāñ) are where the girls of the village gather to spin and to gossip." (p. 175,
  folio 150, n. 1 on 70). It is also where the saint is talked about: "Let us go and see that wild
  one, of whom there is so much talk in the spinning parties.1" (p. 84, folio 59).
- **Spinning as the governing metaphor of a rightly conducted life.** "The use of spinning as a
  metaphor for the correct conduct of life is here developed with an abundance of terms referring
  to different parts of the spinning wheel." (p. 166, folio 141); the vocabulary itself runs across
  four lyrics (pp. 54–55, 68–70; folios 29–30, 43–45) — wheel shaft, spindle, washer, connecting
  string, leather straps, driving band, cotton rolls, carding, hanks, warp.
- **Named and graded cloth.** "cauñsī and paiñsī are woven from 400 and 500 threads respectively,
  khaddar and dhotar are types of coarse cloth, and malmal and khāsā are varieties of fine muslin."
  (p. 164, folio 139); in the verse, "All the different kinds of cloth—cauñsī, paiñsī, khaddar,
  dhotar, malmal, and ḳhāsā—are made of the same yarn." (p. 50, folio 25).
- **The marriage economy, itemised by the notes.** The dowry — the bride "needs to prepare the
  clothes that she will take with her as her dowry (dāj) when she leaves for her husband's house"
  (p. 154, folio not stated), and the dāj "is frequently used by Bullhe Shah to symbolize the need
  to build up a store of rightly guided acts before the inevitable approach of death" (p. 154,
  folio not stated); the departure ceremony — "The wedding culminates in the ceremony (muklāvā) of
  the bride being taken away from her parents' home to her husband's house, where she will spend
  the rest of her life." (p. 154, folio not stated); the palanquin (ḍolī) (p. 165, folio 140); the
  countdown by untied knots (ganḍhīñ) (p. 169, folio 144); the bride's colour, "Bright red (sūhā)
  is the color that symbolizes the joyful state of a bride." (p. 160, folio 135); the pre-wedding
  seclusion, "māñjhā… the custom of dressing the bride in old clothes and confining her to the
  house for some days before her marriage" (p. 182, folio 157, n. 2 on 104); the bridegroom's
  anointing (p. 186, folio 161, n. 8 on 122) and his diadem and forehead mark (p. 179, folio 154,
  n. 3 on 81); kin terms, "Literally, 'father's elder and younger brothers' (kiā cāce kiā tāe), the
  core kin group of the patrilineal family." (p. 156, folio 131).
- **Marriage under compulsion before a qazi.** "Chuchak called for the qazi and sat him down…
  Forcibly, the marriage ceremony was recited and the ritual was performed." (p. 123, folio 98);
  the qazi performing a forced marriage at the father's instance (pp. 186, 190–191; folios 161,
  165–166).

- "arain (Arāīñ) the caste of vegetable gardeners" (p. 198, folio 173); "Sayyids are all descended
  from the Prophet through his son-in-law Ali" (p. 183, folio 158); "Here 'twice-born' (do jarme)

  folio 152, n. 3 on 76); "khera (Kheṛā) name of the tribe into which Hir was forcibly married."
  (p. 199, folio 174).
- "'Turk' was a standard term in medieval India for 'Muslim,' as opposed to Hindu. Compare 20, 40,
  78, 106, 131." (p. 158, folio 133); Glossary, "Turk the equivalent of "Muslim," as opposed to
  "Hindu."" (p. 200, folio 175); "Clothes dyed blue were particularly associated with Muslims in

### Sufi vocabulary and offices as the book glosses them

- "PIR (P. Pīr) Sufi spiritual guide." (p. 199, folio 174); "shaikh (Shaiḳh) title given to Muslim
  men of learning; also used by Sufis as the equivalent of Pir." (p. 200, folio 175); "shah (Shāh)
  honorific title, placed before names of saints and after names of Sayyids." (p. 200, folio 175).
- The four stages of the path, given twice: "'Reality' (sacc, Pers. haqīqat) is the third stage on
  the Sufi path, reached after the law (sharī‘at) and the way (tarīqat)." (pp. 175–176, folios
  150–151); "The verse describes the four stages on the Sufi path. The law (Ar. sharī‘at, P.
  sharhā) is followed by the way (tarīqat), then reality (haqīqat), and finally gnosis (ma‘rifat)."
  (p. 178, folio 153, n. 7 on 78).
- "The word 'adept' (Ar. ‘ārif) is a Sufi term, like 'fakir' (Ar. faqīr)." (p. 158, folio 133); "A
  technical term of Sufism (Ar. fanā fī ’llāh), used to describe the obliteration of the self in
  the divine." (p. 169, folio 144); "By 'inspirational knowledge' (Pers. ‘ilm-e ladunnī) is meant
  the mystical science of Sufism." (p. 161, folio 136); "'Real love' (Pers. ‘ishq-e haqīqī) is the
  mystical love of God as the true reality, which is exalted in Sufism above the human love called
  'apparent love' (Pers. ‘ishq-e majāzī)." (p. 159, folio 134); "The Sufi doctrine of 'peace toward
  all' (Pers. ṣulḥ-e kull) was also adopted in India as a definition of Akbar's policy of religious

  saying (Ar. mūtū qabla an tamūtū)." (p. 167, folio 142).
- The Sufi gathering place against the mosque: "The 'tavern' (ḳharābāt), literally, the ruins where
  drinkers hang out, is a key term in the language of the Persian ghazal. It denotes the place where

  (p. 189, folio 164, n. 2 on 135); "In the language of the Persian ghazal, the 'Magian elder'
  (Pers. pīr-e muġhāñ) is the master who runs the tavern where the lovers drink the wine of love."
  (p. 190, folio 165, n. 5 on 136); the distiller "(kalāl) is the equivalent of the cupbearer, who
  dispenses the wine that banishes all thoughts of ritual observance." (p. 175, folio 150, n. 5
  on 67).
- The fakir's dress: the alfī, "the shroudlike sleeveless garment worn by fakirs, so called because
  it is straight in shape like the letter alif" (p. 166, folio 141); "Stains do not show on the
  blanket worn by a fakir, which is dyed a dark color." (p. 172, folio 147).
- **The orders have a documentary base only in the bibliography.** Ahmad, Qazi Moinuddin. 2012.
  *History of the Shattari Silsilah*. Delhi: Idarah-i-Adabiyat-i-Delli, and Bilgrami, Fatima Zehra.
  2005. *History of the Qadiri Order in India (16th–18th Century)*. Delhi: Idarah-i Adabiyat-i

### Ritual, mosque and prayer

- "A mihrab (Ar. miḥrāb) is the niche in the wall of a mosque that marks the Qibla, the direction
  of the Kaaba toward which those offering prayer must face." (p. 156, folio 131); "Qibla the
  direction of Mecca, faced during Muslim prayers." (p. 200, folio 175); "Kaaba (Ar. Ka‘ba) the
  shrine in Mecca that is the goal of the hajj." (p. 199, folio 174).
- "The verse describes the ritual observances of Muslim prayer, beginning with the muezzin's call…
  The preliminary formulation of righteous intent (niyyat) is followed by prostration, which is
  performed facing the mihrab" (p. 187, folio 162, n. 4 on 125); ablution, "the use of the whole
  well rather than the more modest amount of water normally used for ritual ablution (vuzū) before
  prayer" (p. 187, folio 162, n. 3 on 125).
- "The shape of the mark on the forehead that is made by frequent pious prostration in prayer is
  here compared to that of a mihrab." (p. 160, folio 135) — piety as visible social capital.
- "the Muslim dietary requirement to eat only halal meat, which has been properly slaughtered with
  the beast being bled after its throat has been cut, while avoiding the flesh of animals that have
  died a natural death (Pers. murdār)." (p. 159, folio 134).
- "On the day of judgment, the prophet Muhammad will intercede for those who have followed the
  commandments of Islam." (p. 167, folio 142); "sirat (Sirāt) the narrow bridge that leads to
  paradise." (p. 200, folio 175).
- The one named festival in the corpus is Hindu, kept with Islamic formulae: "There is a
  deliberately shocking juxtaposition of the pious Muslim formula 'In the name of God' (Ar.
  bi’smi’llāh)… with the Hindu spring festival of Holi." (pp. 168–169, folios 143–144); "The tube
  (pickārī) used to squirt colored dye at other people during the celebration of Holi." (p. 169,
  folio 144); in the verse, "I will play Holi, after saying bismillāh." (p. 62, folio 37).
- The calendar is glossed once and only seasonally: "savan (Sāvaṇ) the month of the rainy season
  corresponding to July–August." (p. 200, folio 175). **No ʿurs, mela or festival date appears
  anywhere in the volume.**

- "The expression here refers to the first night after death." (p. 188, folio 163, n. 6 on 126);

  avoiding falling into the torments of hell." (p. 185, folio 160, n. 9 on 119); "This represents
  the actions (Ar. a‘māl) that are the product of life on earth and upon which the soul will be

- The burial ground outside the settlement: "You will find your permanent abode dwelling in the

### The book's own arguments

- **Sufism in India is not a syncretic exception.** "This means un­der­stand­ing [text-layer?] that
  Sufism in India is no exception to the general rule that Sufism is and always has been an
  integral part of Islam." (p. 10, folio ix); "The core expression of this vision is again very
  Islamic, relying upon the repeated citation of a number of Qur'anic phrases and other sacred
  sayings in their original Arabic wording" (p. 17, folio xvi). The notes bear it out with sura and
  verse citations at Qurʼan 7.143, 6.32, 2.18, 2.117, 2.115, 15.26, 7.172, 15.29, 50.16, 57.4,
  51.20–21, 17.70, 2.152-3, 2.138, 112.2 (pp. 157–163, 169; folios 132–138, 144), and "This poem is
  remarkable for its deliberate clustering of Qurʼanic and other Arabic phrases" (p. 162, folio
  137).
- **Persian, not Panjabi, is the frame.** "While the use of local languages by Sufi poets has
  helped to ensure their continuing popularity across religious bound-aries today, it should be
  remembered that their activity took place within a literary culture whose principal language was
  Persian.9" (p. 11, folio x). The institutional history behind it: "By the ninth and tenth
  centuries, Sufis were already well estab-lished in Baghdad and other cities of the Middle East."
  (p. 10, folio ix); "A strong Sufi presence was rapidly established with the arrival of charismatic
  fig­ures [text-layer?] associated with the main Sufi orders." (p. 11, folio x).
- **Print, and the colonial break that produced it.** "Only with the British conquest of Panjab in
  the 1840s did the literary culture of the Sufi tradition come to be overlaid by new patterns of
  modernity. Persian was quite rapidly replaced as the language of education, administra-tion, and
  elite literature by Urdu and Eng­lish [text-layer?]" (pp. 11–12, folios x–xi).
- **The recension is built from six named editions whose disagreements are recorded.** "The opening
  of this short poem makes it an obvious choice as the first kāfī in the Persian-script editions F
  and S. But it is not attested in the earlier editions R and Q." (p. 157, folio 132); "This kāfī is
  not included in R. All subsequent editions have taken this poem from Q, but some doubts must
  attach to its authenticity." (p. 166, folio 141); "This remarkable depiction of the strife in
  contemporary society as a set of disputes between different kinds of snacks and sweets is first
  recorded in Q. It is absent from R, and its authenticity has sometimes been questioned." (p. 170,
  folio 145); "This poem is not in R, and is omitted from PS." (p. 173, folio 148); "S omits this
  verse." (p. 193, folio 168). The sigla expand to F = Faqir 1960, J = Jagtar 2008, PS = Puri and
  Shangari 1986, Q = Qasuri 1896, R = Ruhtaki 1889, S = Sabir 1991 (Abbreviations, p. 152, folio
  not stated; expansions at p. 202, folio 177).
- **Oral transmission argued from the textual evidence.** "All editions follow R in printing kāfīs
  49 and 50 as separate poems… these two short poems are otherwise so similar that they are probably
  variants of a single original that diverged in the oral tradition." (p. 170, folio 145); "It seems
  likely that they are variants of a single original that have been separately recorded in R from
  the oral tradition." (p. 188, folio 163, n. 1 on 131); irregular rhyme used as evidence, "The
  rhyme scheme of this kāfī is very irregular, suggesting that the text has been unreliably
  transmitted." (p. 176, folio 151, n. 1 on 74).
- **Three passages called possible later interpolations**, on three different grounds: the
  Hir–Ranjha narrative material in 122 (p. 186, folio 161, n. 1 on 122); the invitation to Baha ud
  Din Zakariya's tomb (p. 186, folio 161, n. 5 on 122); and the Sikh *panth* verse (p. 189, folio
  164, n. 3 on 133).
- **The translator marks his own guesses, repeatedly.** "This is a tentative interpretation of the
  Panjabi text (bhogaṇ karan adāīñ)." (p. 156, folio 131); "the word translated as 'flood'
  (jagātar) in the original appears corrupt. Any translation must therefore be guesswork." (p. 156,
  folio 131); "the sense of the verse is obscure" (p. 178, folio 153); "The sense is unclear."
  (p. 165, folio 140); "It is not clear who exactly is meant here." (p. 189, folio 164); "the whole
  verse is somewhat obscure" (p. 180, folio 155).
- **He adjudicates between predecessors and rejects one on grounds of anachronistic doctrine.** "PS
  explains as, 'I have become immortal in Your love and it is only my mind which has been eliminated
  forever,' but this owes more to Radhasoami than to Sufi ideas." (p. 165, folio 140); "The phrase
  'both heaps' (doveñ thok) is better understood with PS as 'this world and the world to come,'
  rather than with S as the two words of the formula lā ilāha." (p. 167, folio 142).
- **He corrects a received scriptural attribution.** Of lā tataḥarraku: "Although it seems to be
  understood as scriptural, it does not actually occur in the text of the Qurʼan." (p. 171, folio
  146).
- **Letter-mysticism read as the structural key.** alif as "the first letter of the Arabic alphabet
  and the first letter of the word 'Allah'", identical in shape with the sign for 1 (Ar. aḥad), "as
  opposed to the second letter, be… which stands for the world of duality." (p. 157, folio 132);
  "In the Arabic script, Ahad 'the One,' i.e., God, is virtually identical with Ahmad, i.e., the
  prophet Muhammad. Only the letter mīm in its very small medial form (-m-) marks the difference"
  (p. 162, folio 137); "the removal of the initial letter ‘ain from anā ‘arab… results in anā rabb,
  'I am the lord.'" (p. 162, folio 137); "The small dot that distinguishes letters in the
  Perso-Arabic script symbolizes the concentrated essence of mystical reality." (p. 160, folio 135).
- **Where the poems level religious difference.** "From the astronomical perspective of Sahadev, the
  geographical locations on earth of Lanka and Mecca are identical, proving the meaninglessness of
  their rival associations with Hindu and Muslim identity." (p. 159, folio 134); "his central
  perception of the meaninglessness of outward diversity inspired those memorable poems on the
  essential unity of 'Turk' and 'Hindu' that have acquired such a special sig­nifi­cance
  [text-layer?] in modern South Asia" (p. 18, folio xvii) — set against the warning that "fuller
  un­der­stand­ings [text-layer?] depend upon proper attention being given to those differences
  between bhakti and Sufi poetry, which, despite sig­nifi­cant [text-layer?] commonalities, are by
  no means identical in their fundamental presuppositions" (p. 9, folio viii).
- **The translation policy, and its stated loss.** "The present translation tries only to convey the
  sense of the poetry in a consistent style of plain Eng­lish [text-layer?] prose that aims for a
  middle path between off-put­ting [text-layer?] formality and jarring colloquialism… Italics are
  used to mark Bullhe Shah's citation of Qur'anic verses and other Arabic sayings.28" (p. 23, folio
  xxii); note 28, "The translations of Qur'anic verses are based on Yusuf Ali 1977." (p. 25, folio
  xxiv). The loss: "the formal unity generated in the original language by the rhyme scheme
  announced in the refrain is hardly apparent in the Eng­lish [text-layer?] prose translations
  presented in this book" (p. 15, folio xiv).

## Doubts and review points

### 1. Damage: the inventory, and the exemption test

*This book is a `text_layer` route. Sixty-seven `[text-layer?]` flags were left across the seven
chunks and **no `[illegible]` flags**; every flag has been carried above onto the word it was
attached to, and none has been quietly repaired to make a quotation read better.*

**The total-and-mechanical exemption does not apply to any class in this book.** The one class that
would qualify — `fi`/`fl`/`ff` ligature loss, a total and mechanical mapping — **does not occur in
this volume at all**, and all seven chunks record the negative explicitly (pp. 29, 42–74 passim,
75–108 passim, 110–134, 154–172, 173–196, 200–205). Of the classes that do occur, class 1 is the
closest candidate and still fails the test: a bare soft hyphen deletes mechanically, but the same
tokens carry spurious spaces of variable length (`suf­f ering`, `dif  ­fi­cult`,
`dif       ­fi­culties`, `af ­fi li­a­tions`, `in­ side`), and a space inside a word cannot be told
mechanically from a real word-space. Class 2 fails for the converse reason: the book genuinely
hyphenates ("off-put­ting", "well-recog-nized"), so a line-break hyphen is not reversible token by
token. Classes 3–7 are plainly irreversible. **Every affected word therefore keeps its inline flag
as well as this inventory, per convention (b).**

**One further inline hazard that is not damage.** Superscript endnote markers extract as bare
digits fused to the adjacent word throughout the lyrics — `do?1`, `Sikandar?5`, `Yusuf,6`,
`Sulaiman,8`, `Alif  1`, `Panjab,1`, `master.2`, `Inayat Qadiri,1`, `Shah Shams2`, `Zakariya's4`,
`Bahaval Haq.5`, `Mecca.2`, `sayings. 28`. **A digit fused to a word is a note number, not part of
a name or a figure**, and nothing above may be read as a date or an AH year on that basis. In one
place the marker is separated from its word by a space instead — `ġhain 2` (p. 86, folio 61) —
where a reader could take the 2 for part of the word. Italics are also lost by the text layer, so
the extent of scriptural and Persian tags ("Then there is the face of God 1" p. 109; "Am I not your
lord?  4" p. 128; "We are nearer 1" p. 137; "Let it be, and it was.3" p. 122; "I am God" pp. 113,
138; "Arise by my permission" p. 140) was inferred from the endnote markers and sense, not read off
the page.

### 5. Structure, pagination and pages to re-render

- **The folio rule.** Arabic folio = PDF − 25 and it was verified page by page across every
  numbered page of pp. 28–205 without a single exception. Roman folios run vii (p. 8) to xxiv
  (p. 25) at PDF − 1. **The run sheet's front-matter rule is wrong for PDF 26–27**: the roman
  sequence stops at xxiv on p. 25, p. 26 is the half-title `Sufi Lyrics` and p. 27 is blank, both
  unnumbered, and p. 28 prints 3. Correct reading: roman i–xxiv = PDF 2–25 (printed only from
  vii/p. 8); arabic 1–180 = PDF 26–205. Blank or unnumbered pages: pp. 1–7, 26, 27, 151, 152, 153,
  154, 197, 206–209. **p. 153 is additionally blank**, a small correction to the run brief.
- **Pages to re-render as images before anything from them is quoted**: **p. 193** (the damaged
  Arabic alif); **p. 198** (headword capitalisation, the two stray soft hyphens, and whether "a

  poem; note numbers run down the left margin and **restart at 1 under every poem number**; the
  folio sits alone at the page foot. p. 173 (folio 148) shows all three within twelve lines.

  that convention before it is read as a page.

  Gurmukhi or Shahmukhi appears in pp. 1–209; the only non-Latin characters are transliteration
  diacritics and the short Arabic phrases in the letter-mysticism notes. Two things cut against an
