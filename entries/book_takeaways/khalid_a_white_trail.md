## What this book is

Haroon Khalid's *A White Trail: A journey into the heart of Pakistan's religious minorities* (westland ltd, 2013; "First e-book edition: 2013") is reportage: one chapter per festival or site, written from festivals the author attended c. 2010–2012. The chapters cover Hindu festivals at Multan (Holi), Bahawalnagar (Navratri), Katas (Shivratri), Ram Thamman (Baisakhi) and Lahore (Valmiki's birthday, Krishna Janmashtami); the Christian pilgrimage at Maryabad and two Lahore cathedrals; Zoroastrian Navroz and a Baha'i celebration in Lahore; and Sikh festivals at Hassan Abdal, Nankana Sahib, Dera Sahib and Ranjit Singh's samadhi; an Afterword closes the book. The book grew out of a CAP Minority Project that "officially began in September 2010", with "over two hundred oral history interviews" (pp. 10–11, folio not stated). It is written against a "Pakistani historiography" that "discards Hindu and Sikh narratives" (p. 9, folio not stated). The author says it is "not a list of tortures" and that it records syncretism and young people reviving traditions (p. 13, folio not stated). He also states its limits: his Hindu interviews were all from Lahore and all with lower-caste Hindus, and "My accounts are merely a few snapshots" (pp. 233–234, folio not stated). Reliability: the text comes from a born-digital e-book text layer and is essentially undamaged. There are only a few fused words and footnote markers, and the introduction (pp. 9–11) has sentences spliced out of order in the e-book itself. There are no printed folios anywhere, so every reference is a PDF page with "folio not stated". The author's note says plainly that the names of minority members have been changed and that "in some instances I have taken the liberty to stray from strictly speaking, non-fiction" (p. 15, folio not stated). He also says some off-record interviews "have been presented in that framework [of the festival] for the purpose of understanding" (p. 12, folio not stated). So any informant's name may be a pseudonym, and a scene may not be strictly factual. What an informant says and what the book itself says are kept apart throughout.

### gurudwara-janam-asthan-nankana-sahib — Gurudwara Janam Asthan Nankana Sahib

The book's "Gurdwara Janamasthan" (printed "Janamathsan" twice on p. 192), at Nankana Sahib: "the main Gurdwara of the city where Nanak was born" (p. 183, folio not stated); "the most famous place here", where the one main road of Nankana Sahib "begins or ends" (p. 186, folio not stated). Registers: reportage of three festivals the author attended (Guru Gobind Singh's birthday and Lohri, January 2011; Sikh New Year), plus Nadeem's visit at Guru Nanak's birthday (year not stated).

- **Saint**
  - "Gurdwara Janamasthan is situated where he was born" (p. 222, folio not stated). "This room, where the Granth is being recited, is the room where Nanak was born." (p. 189, folio not stated); "Rajbir is standing inside the room where Guru Nanak was born" (p. 213, folio not stated).
  - A well with an iron railing; its board reads "This well was part of Guru Nanak's house." (p. 189, folio not stated).
- **Shrine / Darbar**
  - Exterior and approach: a "tall minaret right in front of Gurdwara Janamasthan", "the tallest in the city", with spiral stairs and an unpainted grey mosque (p. 187, folio not stated). The Railway station "is opposite the Gurdwara Janamasthan" (p. 227, folio not stated); Gurdwara Tambu Sahib is "a few hundred metres away" (p. 223, folio not stated). "Near the Gurdwara Janamasthan, next to the office of the Nazim, the former elected head of the district, is the Government College rechristened recently as Guru Nanak Government College" (p. 172, folio not stated).
  - Gates (Lohri visit, January 2011): the main gate closed, only a small door open; "two police officials sitting inside" let the non-Sikh visitors pass on Kartar's word (p. 187, folio not stated). Gates (Nadeem, Guru Nanak's birthday, returning "after seven years"): the main gate "was earlier made of iron bars, which permitted one to witness the activities inside. Now it is one big closed structure barring any view"; the two smaller flanking gates closed; "The wall has been raised and protected by a barb wire on the top"; police "with guns stand at the gate, on top of walls and all the other higher structures at the complex"; a new side entrance some distance from the main gate (p. 220, folio not stated). Contrast, Guru Gobind Singh's birthday night: "The guard at the entrance is too cold to check for any suspicious people trying to enter" (p. 177, folio not stated).
  - Inside: buildings on the right "where visitors stay when they come for the larger events", a small garden with two walkways and street lights (p. 187, folio not stated); shoes handed to a volunteer child in "a bar cage", matched by numbered coins ("It reads '22'") (pp. 187–188, folio not stated); a water niche for washing feet, two sinks (p. 188, folio not stated).
  - "The Gurdwara is two steps higher from the ground. It has a gilded door, with depictions of Guru Nanak and scenes from a Langar"; two framed pictures in the passage, "Baba Fareed Ganjshakar" in a green shawl and "Baba Guru Nanak" in saffron; a railing divides in and out passages, "useful when there are huge crowds" (p. 188, folio not stated). A Nishan Sahib with candles (p. 189, folio not stated).
  - A "historical tree, next to the portico" with a plaque: "Sikh Martyrdom" – "20th February 1921 at Gurdwara Janamasthan, Nankana Sahib." (p. 189, folio not stated). A second portico with a glass room "marks the spot where the martyrs from the deadly day of 20th February 1921 were cremated in one big pile"; its "golden palanquin was gifted to the Gurdwara by the former Chief Minister of Indian Punjab, Captain Amarinder Singh" (p. 191, folio not stated). Two paintings in the glass room: Baba Nanak with "Bhai Mardana and Bhai Bala" (Mardana playing the rubab, Bala holding the fly-whisk), and the boy Nanak shaded by a cobra (p. 191, folio not stated).
  - Rooms in the courtyard "allotted to Sikh pilgrims at the time of major festivals"; a room behind the shrine is the Granth's, with a bed, an air-conditioner "turned on during the summers for the Granth" and a winter blanket; "No other activity can take place in this room" (pp. 190–191, folio not stated).
  - At Guru Nanak's birthday: "several blocks of hostels here, constructed during various eras" (p. 228, folio not stated); a separate building of communal washrooms left of the main shrine, with stalls for laundry, public telephones, juices and eatables (p. 228, folio not stated); "Only the pool at this Gurdwara is filled with water out of all the Gurdwaras of the city", deepening at the centre, police at its entrance (p. 228, folio not stated); a web of lights over the façade; a banner at the main entrance with Guru Nanak on one side, Guru Gobind Singh on the other and the other Gurus between (p. 228, folio not stated); the offices of the Gurdwara administration (p. 229, folio not stated).
  - "the newly created Langar Hall", the last building before the boundary wall, "beyond which lies the Guru Nanak Government College"; a wall with barbwire and "a large brown gate, which is a recent addition", passable only by officials (p. 229, folio not stated); a locked room beside the washing area "stores raw material for Langar" (pp. 229–230, folio not stated). Two entrances to the courtyard of the main shrine (front, and right side facing the Langar Hall) (p. 230, folio not stated).
  - On a poster at Panja Sahib of "all the functional Gurdwaras of Pakistan": "a large picture of Gurdwara Janamasthan in the middle, a long yellow building with a white outline" (p. 166, folio not stated).
- **Practices & Events**
  - Personnel (informant names may be pseudonyms): "Lalit Singh, a young man in his late thirties, the head Granthi at the Gurdwara"; "Lalit's family has been living here since the late seventies"; he "is considered to be the most learned (religious) person in this Sikh circle" and "leads the community prayers most of the time" (p. 177, folio not stated). "At 70, Rajbir is one of the oldest employees of the Gurdwara Janamasthan. He moved here in 1978, following a passionate speech by Dr. Jagjit Singh Chauhan" (p. 213, folio not stated). Kartar's father "moved from Tirat Valley (Tribal Area) after the 1971 Indo-Pak war and settled here, inside Gurdwara Janamasthan" (p. 183, folio not stated).
  - Guru Gobind Singh's birthday night (reportage): people walk to the gurdwara in near-freezing fog (p. 177, folio not stated); Lalit Singh plays tabla on the stage "next to the shrine", with two female vocalists, one on harmonium; teenage girls sing a song to Guru Gobind Singh; the evening "is dedicated to the children", who prostrate to the Granth Sahib and memorize lines (p. 177, folio not stated); children wear white and "the 5 Ks for this special day, even though most of them have not been baptized as yet", turbans orange, blue or black by group (p. 178, folio not stated). Surjeet conducts the children's show — recitation "from the holy book, the Guru Granth Sahib", songs to "the bravery of Guru Gobind Singh", a fourteen-year-old girl's poem that the young are "cutting their hair and their turbans are being replaced with caps"; Udham calls "Jo bole so nihal!", the audience answers "Sat Sri Akal!" (p. 178, folio not stated); it runs to "eleven in the night" (p. 179, folio not stated).
  - Lohri (reportage): the procession goes from Tamboo Sahib to "the main Gurdwara – Gurdwara Janamasthan" (pp. 185–186, folio not stated); "About a hundred people" hear kirtan in a small room with a portico (p. 189, folio not stated); after an hour of kirtan Lalit Singh, "the head Granthi of the shrine", leads the communal prayer; the Granth is carried on an elderly man's head around the shrine to its bedroom and covered with a blanket, which "marks the end of the prayer and the beginning of the Lohri festival" (p. 191, folio not stated); the community gathers at "a flame in front of the main building" in the outer courtyard, "an integral part of the Lohri celebration" (pp. 191–192, folio not stated).
  - Sikh New Year (reportage): "Carrying a fly-whisk, Rajbir Singh stands in front of the palanquin that holds the Granth Sahib", concluding "the bhog, the communal prayer"; "Hundreds of devotees stand outside, following his lead" (p. 213, folio not stated). The community moves "to the other pavilion that contains the golden palanquin", where in "the glass chamber, a young boy is reading the last pages of the Granth. He is the final reader of the Akhand Path" (p. 214, folio not stated). The audience: "Pathan Sikhs, based in Nankana Sahib and clean shaven Sindhi devotees", "about three hundred people, half from each community" (p. 214, folio not stated). According to "42 year old Ajay Kumar from Sukkur, Sindh": "We are Sikhs but not Singhs"; they came "to hold prayers in memories of Baba Thara Singh", "a group of more than hundred people", and arranged the concluding Akhand Path, the Langar and the sweets and biscuits "as prasad" (p. 214, folio not stated). That night six Muslim qawwals perform "at Gurdwara Janamasthan" (p. 218, folio not stated).
  - Guru Nanak's birthday (Nadeem's visit, year not stated): two queues, women frisked by female police in a small enclosure, men checked in the open and passing "two other security checks" (p. 220, folio not stated); a police official checks Nadeem's national identity card: "No Muslims allowed"; Nadeem: "But I came a few years ago" — no reply (pp. 220–221, folio not stated); barefoot before the main gate he raises his hand "in the direction of the shrine": "I will just say hello to the Baba from here then. Good bye, Baba Ji." (p. 221, folio not stated). [Side by side: "after seven years" (p. 220) and "a few years ago" (p. 221), as printed.]
  - Same festival: a Geo van and media cars broadcast live by satellite (p. 221, folio not stated); "The main focus of the pilgrims is Gurdwara Janamasthan", with more police inside than at the entrance (p. 227, folio not stated); "Everyone has been allotted a pass, even representatives from the media" (p. 228, folio not stated); a blue bus decorated with yellow flowers for the Nagar Kirtan round "the green belt at the centre of the complex"; tents for traders "from other parts of the country" (p. 228, folio not stated); Meena Kumari, 54, sells eatables under "Block 1, a residential hostel", bought "in bulk from Mardan" (pp. 228–229, folio not stated); T-shirt stalls: "Never Forget 1984," "Singh is King," "Raj Karega Khalsa" (p. 229, folio not stated).
  - Langar: food served "around the clock" — "Like Gurdwara Tambu Sahib" (p. 229, folio not stated); the floor wet with "water, food, tea, and mud"; "Most of the volunteers are local Pathan Sikhs"; police patrol it (p. 229, folio not stated); volunteers "performing sewa" wash utensils in two large tubs (pp. 229–230, folio not stated).
  - The courtyard covered with tents and carpets where pilgrims sleep: "The majority of these pilgrims are locals from Khyber Pakhthunkhwa and Sindh, however there are a few Indian families as well" (p. 230, folio not stated).
  - Kirtan from a stage next to the shrine by three men in white shalwar-kameez who "appear to be Muslims"; a screen shows the proceedings, broadcast live through the internet "and viewed by millions of people world over. This is the first time it is being done from here." (p. 230, folio not stated).
  - Darshan: a queue behind "an orange robe"; devotees prostrate ("prostate", as printed) before the Guru Granth Sahib, go clockwise, enter by a small door on the left, fan the fly-whisk over the Book, leave by a door on the right and receive "sujji ka halwa" as Prasad (p. 230, folio not stated).
  - Nagar Kirtan: according to a shopkeeper, the round of the city "hasn't happened for two years now. There is a security threat. They will take out a small procession within the precincts of the Gurdwara, where it is safe" (p. 221, folio not stated). After the Akhand Path a decorated pickup with a dhol-wala distributes juice packets (p. 231, folio not stated); ten Sikh men in "two lines of five each" lead — front row in white cholas and saffron turbans with the Nishan Sahib, the row behind in saffron cholas and blue turbans with kirpans (pp. 231–232, folio not stated); the bus carries the Guru Granth Sahib in "a golden palanquin", devotees on the main gate throw red rose petals, and its halt at the starting point "concludes the Nagar Kirtan, marking an end to the religious rituals of the festival" (p. 232, folio not stated).
  - 1921 (book's historical prose): about two hundred unarmed Sikhs, gathered at Gurdwara Tamboo Sahib, protested "the control of the Hindu priests (mahants) at major Gurdwaras like Janamasthan" and accused its mahant "Narayan Das" of corruption, "alleged to have sold parts of the vast property connected with the Gurdwara" (pp. 189–190, folio not stated); Narayan Das was guarded by "Muslim Pathans from the tribal areas" and joined by local Hindu supporters (p. 190, folio not stated). Led by "Bhai Daleep Singh and Bhai Lachman Singh", the Sikhs entered and were fired on, "killing about a hundred people"; Lachman Singh "was hung upside down on this tree and burnt"; Daleep Singh "was thrown inside a brick kiln, which once used to exist within the premises of the Gurdwaras"; the British government acted against Narayan Das and accomplices, some "sentenced to death", others to life at "Kala Pani (Andaman Islands)"; the event "formed the basis of a larger movement" to take the major Gurdwaras from the mahants (p. 190, folio not stated).
- **Significance**
  - Kartar (informant) on the minaret: "It is a show of strength. They want to tell us who the real inhabitants of Nankana Sahib are." (p. 187, folio not stated).
  - The author on the qawwals: the performance "doesn't promise to revive" the Muslim kirtan tradition "but is still a tribute to the years of interaction between the two communities at Sikh Gurdwaras" (p. 218, folio not stated).

### gurdwara-panja-sahib — Gurdwara Panja Sahib

The book's "Gurdwara Panja Sahib" at "Hassan Abdal" (archive "Hasan Abdal"); chapter "BAIAISAKHI [text-layer?] [BAISAKHI] AT HASSAN ABDAL", pp. 157–171, listed in the contents as "Baisakhi at Hassan Abdal" (p. 5, folio not stated). "Surrounded by mountains on one side and the entire city of Hassan Abdal on the other, the Gurdwara Panja Sahib is one of the most important Sikh shrines in the country" (p. 157, folio not stated). Reportage of Baisakhi, "13th April 2011" (p. 158, folio not stated).

- **Saint**
  - "A few pilgrims visit all year round to pay their respects to the shrine associated with the founder of the Sikh religion, Guru Nanak (1469-1539)" (p. 157, folio not stated).
  - The book's account of the origin: "About five hundred years ago, on the same date, the 1st day of Vaisakh, Guru Nanak came here" with "Bhai Mardana – a Muslim, and Bhai Bala – a Hindu"; the spot "was not a city at that time"; he settled "under a peepal tree, the very spot where the Gurdwara was built later" (p. 159, folio not stated). Mardana was sent three times up the mountain to Wali Qandhari for water (p. 159, folio not stated). The name: "Panja meaning palm, in this case Nanak's palm" (p. 161, folio not stated).
- **Shrine / Darbar**
  - Building: "The main shrine is a splendid grey building adorned with three white and yellow domes on the top"; colourful cloths tied to a railing around it; double-storey buildings on all four sides "contain rooms for the pilgrims to stay" (p. 159, folio not stated). "The façade of the building is pink", the inside closed off by an iron gate (p. 158, folio not stated). "There are four entrances into the main shrine, one from all sides, symbolizing the openness of the religion" (p. 167, folio not stated).
  - Building history, side by side: "This place became a site of devotion after the miracle of Nanak. It was then extended during the tenure of the Sikh ruler of Punjab, Maharaja Ranjit Singh (1780-1839)" (p. 159, folio not stated) / the fading frescoes are "part of the original complex built by Maharaja Ranjit Singh" (p. 167, folio not stated).
  - Interior: "decorated with gaudy glass patterns put up recently by the government of Pakistan as renovation work to cover the fading frescoes"; a wooden palanquin in the centre, a young Pathan-Sikh reading the Guru Granth Sahib, pilgrims resting (p. 167, folio not stated).
  - The pool: "The sacred pool said to have been created after the miracle of Nanak lies in the shadow of the main shrine", "turquoise coloured" (p. 160, folio not stated); white marble stairs bearing donors' names, two inscriptions quoted: "Kulvinder Kaur, Malkit Kaur, Canada Maple ridge (1100), 11.3.2007, Pray for family" and "Jaspreet Singh Atwal (Jesse), Sept 1, 1991 to May 10, 2008, s/o Sadhu Singh Atwal, Seattle, USA" (p. 160, folio not stated). "Water still gushes into this pool through a stream, flowing into the complex from under the protective walls recently raised by the Government of Pakistan"; "Stairs constructed recently" lead to the stream (p. 161, folio not stated); the stream emerges behind the residential quarters "and then flows out of the complex" (p. 170, folio not stated).
  - The rock: facing the main shrine, inside the pool, "in a small niche whose protective arch is made of ivory, there is a grey stone" partly immersed, with "a black mark in the middle of the rock that looks like a hand print"; a window on each side lets women offer devotion; "A marble plaque next to it describes its significance in Gurmukhi" (pp. 160–161, folio not stated).
  - Kirtan hall behind the main shrine: at its entrances "statues of two Sikh soldiers stand guard holding spears, dressed up in yellow clothes and blue turbans"; the stage has "a picture of this particular Gurdwara in the centre and that of Guru Gobind Singh on the top left corner"; behind the reader "a picture of Guru Gobind Singh collecting money for Langar" (p. 169, folio not stated). A staircase leads underground to "two spacious corridors" where pilgrims without rooms live (p. 169, folio not stated).
  - "The Langar Hall is a newly built double storey construction, situated behind these residential quarters" (p. 170, folio not stated). An alley ends in an open space "surrounded by more buildings for the residence of the pilgrims"; a garden "covered by tents provides makeshift rooms" (pp. 166–167, folio not stated).
  - Setting: Hassan Abdal "is a historic city which has managed to retain its character", narrow curved streets with wooden balconies, "Situated on hillocks"; "A few Hindu temples are still present here … but are now being used as improvised houses or in some cases wandering grounds for stray animals"; "The remains of a Mughal garden in a dilapidated state still survive facing the Gurdwara", referred to in the "Ain-i-Akbari" written "during the reign of the third Mughal Emperor, Akbar (1542-1605) by Abu-Fazl" (p. 157, folio not stated).
  - Resident community: "home to about fifty to sixty Sikh families, who have moved here in the last few years from the tribal areas due to the rise of insurgency there, while the rest of the city is Muslim"; most "only know Pashtu"; they "are mostly confined within the boundaries of the Gurdwara, protected by police officials, tall walls and barbed wires on all sides" (p. 157, folio not stated). Refugee Sikh families from the tribal areas "moved to Peshawar, Hassan Abdal and Nankana Sahib, and started living at Gurdwaras" (p. 175, folio not stated).
- **Practices & Events**
  - Baisakhi: "For three days and nights in the month of April, everything changes here"; streets cordoned off, "no Muslim is allowed to venture near the Gurdwara"; local schools shut and classrooms "converted into hostels for the pilgrims" (p. 157, folio not stated). Pilgrims spend "13th, 14th and 15th of April", then most go on "to the other holy Sikh shrines"; "thousands of turbaned Sikhs" in the market, "most of them Indian Punjabis" (p. 157, folio not stated). "Most of the Sikhs here are fair skinned and blue eyed Pathans, from the regions of Khyber-Pakthunkhwa and tribal areas and they are more alien to this place than the Indian Punjabi Sikhs"; a third category, clean-shaven "Hindus from the different regions of Pakistan" (p. 158, folio not stated). Some walk the tar road "without shoes as an expression of extreme respect" (p. 158, folio not stated).
  - Numbers: "According to local media reports, about seven thousand pilgrims have arrived for the festival this year- about two thousand from India and the rest from within Pakistan" (p. 158, folio not stated); "more than two hundred people" in the entry line (p. 158, folio not stated).
  - Security and exclusion: a police officer checks "everyone's security cards"; "Even until last year" turbans or head covers sufficed and those who "looked more 'Muslim'" were barred if Muslim; "This year however, the security has been intensified"; "Even till recently, Muslims were allowed to pay their respects to the shrine of Baba Guru Nanak, much in line with the Sikh philosophy but not anymore"; all get a security card "from the 'Special Branch'" (p. 158, folio not stated); a TV crew threatening to telecast its treatment is let through (p. 158, folio not stated). Anyone is allowed inside a Gurdwara "according to the tradition, a practice that doesn't exist in Pakistan anymore though" (p. 167, folio not stated).
  - Kirtan with harmonium and tabla in Punjabi, heard across the complex (p. 159, folio not stated); it "usually includes verses from the Sikh holy book, the Guru Granth Sahib", a tradition going "back to Nanak's Muslim devotee, Bhai Mardana" (p. 160, folio not stated); notes and coins offered to the musicians (p. 169, folio not stated).
  - At the pool: men stand praying in it, an old man swims, children on the stairs "reluctant, perhaps due to the sacredness of the water" (p. 160, folio not stated); volunteers pass buckets of pool water to wash "the white marble floor of the courtyard" — "a sacred duty called sewa" — and cook and serve "at the Langar Hall" (p. 160, folio not stated). Devotees queue for "the darshan of the stone", place a hand on the mark while pouring water, some kiss it (pp. 160–161, folio not stated). Photo caption: "A pilgrim placing his hand on the hand print of Guru Nanak on the sacred rock at Gurdwara Panja Sahib, Hassan Abdal on the occasion of Baisakhi." (p. 109, folio not stated).
  - Surjeet Singh, "35 year old", a "Pathan-Sikh ... from the city of Peshawar" (family from "a small village in the Khyber Agency"), chases boys out of the pool — "This is no place to play!" — guarding it as "his way of performing sewa"; his shirt reads "Never forget 1984" with "Sant Jarnail Singh Bhindranwale" (pp. 161–162, folio not stated); "This shirt represents the passion of Baisakhi" (p. 164, folio not stated).
  - Akhand Path: devotees fan the Granth with a fly-whisk, "a tradition that comes from Bhai Bala, the Hindu devotee of Guru Nanak"; "the final chapters of the Granth Sahib are being read", marking "the conclusion of Akhand Path" (p. 168, folio not stated).
  - Nagar Kirtan: Avtar Kaur, "55 year old", from "the Indian city of Amritsar": "I have always wanted to come to Pakistan. This is the land of Guru Nanak" (pp. 168–169, folio not stated); "here I have heard that the procession will not be allowed to leave the premises of this Gurdwara. That is sad" (p. 169, folio not stated). The book: "Till a few years ago, the procession marched around the city of Hassan Abdal as local Muslim onlookers ... looked on"; now, "due to the growing threat from the Islamic extremists", it "remains within the protected confines of the Gurdwara" (p. 169, folio not stated).
  - Accommodation: "Usually, foreign pilgrims are accommodated in rooms, while Pakistanis are allowed to settle wherever there is space" (p. 169, folio not stated). Old Indian pilgrims in the corridors recall "the Khalistan days of insurgency": Harvinder Singh ("65 year old"; shop in "Lajpat Nagar (Delhi)" burnt), Jasdeep Singh ("54 years old"), Virinder Kaur ("55 years old", from "a village near Amritsar District") (p. 170, folio not stated).
  - Langar: Sikh boys serve "doodh-soda" at a stall; volunteers guard the full Langar Hall (p. 170, folio not stated).
  - Trade: "'Never forget 1984' is a popular t-shirt here, being sold in a stall behind the main shrine" by "a young dark boy from the southern part of the country"; a woman in a sari bargains — "Two hundred only" … "One-fifty. Final price." — "I don't understand this currency" (pp. 165–166, folio not stated). CDs "Sikh Gurdwaras in Pakistan" — the author calls it "a government sanctioned propaganda documentary"; a poster of "all the functional Gurdwaras of Pakistan"; "Ravinder Singh's latest collection of kirtan" (p. 166, folio not stated); a man throws nimko into the crowd from behind an iron-bar gate (p. 166, folio not stated). Vendors sell "head covers with Khalsa symbols, plates for aarti, posters of Hindu deities and Sikh Gurus"; "Most of these vendors are Pakistanis who travel all over the country throughout the year attending Hindu and Sikh festivals" (pp. 166–167, folio not stated). ### katas-raj-temples — Katas Raj Temples

The book's "Killa Katas" (chapter "SHIVRATRI AT KILLA KATAS"; contents "Shivratri at Killa Katas", p. 5, folio not stated), also "Katas Raj or Killa Katas" / "Katas" (p. 45, folio not stated), "Situated near the small town of Kalar Kahar" (p. 45, folio not stated). Reportage of Shivratri, "2nd March 2011" (p. 47, folio not stated).

- **Saint**
  - The Hindus believe the pond was created from a tear Shiva shed when "Shiva's … wife Sati dead" (according to the guide Shakeel) (p. 43, folio not stated). Shakeel tells tourists a story of Ganesh as "the son of the Hindu God, Shiva" (p. 42, folio not stated).
  - Guru Nanak "(1469-1539) also came here, as it was a popular destination for ascetics at that time" — footnote 3: "Pg 174, Historical Sikh Shrines by Iqbal Qaiser" (p. 45, folio not stated; footnote p. 53, folio not stated).
- **Shrine / Darbar**
  - The complex: "a natural pond, fossils dating back to millions of years, ancient caves, an unexcavated Buddhist stupa, Hindu temples said to be thousands of years old, and a university which attracted scholars from other parts of the world"; "a holy site for thousands of years" (p. 45, folio not stated); "several small temples built around the pond, dedicated to the different Hindu deities"; "an iron fence protects the complex" with "only one entrance", though one can enter by "climbing the mound" (p. 45, folio not stated).
  - The pond: "turquoise", "round"; a rock separates a small section of clear water "only three feet deep"; "Ancient buildings stand at the edge of the deeper end"; "The pond is clearly the lifeline of this complex. Numerous myths have developed around it" (p. 43, folio not stated). Photo caption: "The historical temple of Katas Raj with its emerald pool." (p. 106, folio not stated).
  - The Shiva shrine "next to the pool": "a small light brown building, with a small dome at the top", "a wooden door, covered with floral decorations", a marble floor, at the centre "the Shivling"; "a small room, which can only accommodate about ten people" (p. 52, folio not stated).
  - The temples: "the three tall cone shaped structures"; dating side by side — Shakeel: "from the time of the Mahabharata", built by the exiled Pandavas, "confirmed by a blue board put up by the authorities here, noting the date of Mahabharata to be around 1500 BCE" / the author: "Experts say that these temples were constructed between the 7th and 10th century CE, when this area was under the influence of the Kashmiri kingdom" (p. 44, folio not stated).
  - "the haveli of Hari Singh Nalwa, a general in the army of Maharaja Ranjit Singh (1780-1839)", "the tallest here, standing like a cube with a small dome at the top"; "The artwork has been recently renovated with depictions of Hindu deities on the walls", including Ganesh and Krishna "playing the flute while he flirts with the maidens" (p. 42, folio not stated).
  - Next to the haveli "a Buddhist stupa that has been partially excavated"; Shakeel dates it "from the 3rd century BC, from the time of Taxila"; "More than half of the structure is still underground" (p. 43, folio not stated). [Side by side: "partially excavated" (p. 43) / "an unexcavated Buddhist stupa" (p. 45).]
  - On a mound across the road an "ancient staircase" leads to "a newly constructed single storey rectangular structure" Shakeel calls "the site of a world famous ancient Hindu university" (p. 44, folio not stated); this building, built "a few years ago", "acts as the Archaeology Department office" (p. 45, folio not stated).
  - Restoration history (book's historical prose): "Major renovation work of the temple was undertaken by the Department in 2005": pond cleaned, buildings "painted and restored", "blue boards were raised" (p. 45, folio not stated). "This temple was abandoned as a result of partition", buildings "falling into disrepair and the pond being polluted"; "a few cement factories opened in the surrounding area and started sucking the water out from here" (pp. 45–46, folio not stated). No one guarded it; "Several phone numbers and names are still engraved" on the walls; a few Hindus visited "pretending to be tourists" (p. 46, folio not stated). Prem Gupta, first visiting in 2003: "There were boys jumping from the windows and alleys of temples into this sacred pond. They would leave juice boxes and chips wrappers in the water" (p. 46, folio not stated). "Things changed for the shrine in 2005, when the veteran Indian politician L.K Advani came here and expressed displeasure"; the Government of Pakistan "started renovation work" (p. 46, folio not stated).
  - Staff: Shakeel, "a 25 year old graduate" from Kalar Kahar, employed "In the past five years"; "The Archaeology Department appointed him and a few other people as guides for the location when it was renovated" (pp. 42–43, folio not stated); he ends up "with a good tip" (p. 43, folio not stated); a female photographer gives him "a thousand rupee note" (p. 44, folio not stated).
  - Facilities: "a small building, the only restaurant in the area"; "Just a few years ago, this place would have refused to serve these people"; "untouchability, at least here, has receded"; "the only washroom the pilgrims can use", closing "at eleven in the night" (pp. 48–49, folio not stated). "There is no electricity"; according to Haroon Sarab Diyal, "The authorities have not paid the bill of this temple for the past three months … Ever since the Indians have stopped coming, they have become negligent again. The temple is falling into disrepair" (pp. 49–50, folio not stated).
- **Practices & Events**
  - Before partition "a popular religious site for the Hindus"; "Thousands of pilgrims used to descend here from faraway places on the occasion of Shivratri" (p. 45, folio not stated).
  - Indian pilgrimage: "In the subsequent year" after 2005 the government invited Indian pilgrims for Shivratri; "About three hundred pilgrims came, which became national news"; they stayed "for three days" with security, shelter and "free food", "at the building at the site of the ancient university", the area "cordoned off"; "This continued until 2008"; after the Mumbai attacks ("between the 26th and 29th of November 2008") "Pilgrims didn't come to Katas for Shivratri in 2009" (p. 46, folio not stated).
  - 2010: Hindu organizations wrote to the Archaeology Department, ETPB, Police and Ministry of Minorities and "did not receive any response"; "About two thousand Hindus came to Katas from various cities of Pakistan unannounced, including Lahore, Rahim Yar Khan, Peshawar and Nowshera" (pp. 46–47, folio not stated).
  - 2011 (reportage): pilgrims prepare for Shivratri night, "for which about two thousand pilgrims would pour in from different parts of the country" (p. 42, folio not stated); Hindu children "who have come from Gujranawala" play on the pond steps (p. 42, folio not stated); Gupta's banner "'Welcome Yatrees (pilgrims)'" with a picture of Lord Shiva; a canopy inside the complex (p. 47, folio not stated); only "five officials" of police at the gate — "an indication of the scant importance that Hindu pilgrims are accorded" (author's judgement) (p. 47, folio not stated); an NCA professor and students are stopped — "Muslims are not allowed inside today," says a police official — while Hindu boys enter with a "Maha Shivratri" tag (pp. 47–48, folio not stated); pilgrims from Peshawar "in two large buses", about a hundred, led by Haroon Sarab Diyal, more from Nowshera, Rahim Yaar Khan and Bahawalpur (p. 49, folio not stated); "There are about a thousand people" once all buses arrive [side by side with the "about two thousand" expected, p. 42]; a DSP first refuses to open the department hall, citing "the security conditions of the country", then opens one hall for women, children and the elderly (pp. 50–51, folio not stated).
  - Next morning Archaeology Department officials "find their offices invaded and the temples full of sleeping pilgrims"; junior officials throw luggage, Langar utensils and rice onto the road (pp. 52–53, folio not stated). Pilgrims pour pond water on their heads as "a substitute to performing a ritual ablution or ashnan" because "It is too cold to take a bath" (p. 53, folio not stated).
- **Legends & Miracles**
  - Shakeel: "No one knows how deep it is … There are poisonous lizards and snakes living inside"; the pond is "in the shape of an eye", from Shiva's tear; "Another tear fall in Ajmer Sharif, India, where there is another ancient pond" (p. 43, folio not stated). - **Significance**

### gurdwara-dera-sahib — Gurdwara Dera Sahib

The book's "Gurdwara Dera Sahib, Lahore" (contents chapter "Commemoration of Guru Arjan's martyrdom at Gurdwara Dera Sahib, Lahore", p. 6, folio not stated; chapter opens p. 195), facing Lahore Fort beside the Badshahi Mosque. Reportage of the commemoration, "6th June 2011" (p. 196, folio not stated).

- **Saint**
  - Book's historical prose: "Outside the Alamgiri gate of the Lahore fort, the Guru was forced to sit in a cauldron of boiling water. Burning sand was poured on him"; "On the fifth day of the torture, the Guru requested a bath in the River Ravi, flowing next to the fort"; "Guru Arjan (1563-1606)" took a dip and did not emerge; "Jahangir, who ordered the Guru's assassination" (p. 195, folio not stated). He was assassinated "in Lahore, where his now Gurdwara stands by the name of Gurdwara Dera Sahib" (p. 198, folio not stated).
- **Shrine / Darbar**
  - "Facing the fort is a small yellow building with a golden dome. A thick yellow wall protects it"; street vendors at the wall base; "teeming with visitors on any given day"; the Badshahi Mosque adjacent (p. 195, folio not stated; building unnamed on this page). The garden: "This is where the Sikh ruler of Punjab, Maharaja Ranjit Singh, used to meet his subjects. A Mughal baradari (a pavilion with twelve pillars) stands in the middle of the garden"; "All of these three places are famous with the tourists"; "A giant gateway leading into the yellow building from here facing the garden is permanently locked" (p. 196, folio not stated).
  - Inside: a final security check before the courtyard; the inner structure the same yellow as the wall; "A couple of rooms at the entrance have been taken over by the security officials" (p. 197, folio not stated). The Nishan Sahib "stands at the edge of the courtyard" on "a circular platform about five feet from the ground" (p. 197, folio not stated). A small room "with windows on all sides, protected by iron bars"; in the centre "a small bulge which is covered by a velvet cloth ... This is the main attraction; the samadhi of Guru Arjan" (pp. 197–198, folio not stated).
  - Building history (book's historical prose): "a small structure was raised at the spot by his son, Guru Hargobind to mark the samadhi of the Guru and it is still present there to this day"; "The present structure was first erected by Maharaja Ranjit Singh with further extensions made by the Sikh community in the early years of the twentieth century" (p. 200, folio not stated).
  - Management: "After partition, the Gurdwara came under the control of the Pakistan Government" through the ETPB; "the ETPB regularly undertakes renovation work here"; "In 1996, rooms were built in the complex" for pilgrims "from India and other parts of Pakistan"; "This new hostel was named after Mian Meer" (p. 200, folio not stated).
  - The Langar Hall, reached by "A small door from the samadhi of Guru Arjan", is "divided into two sections", mats "in four lines" (p. 201, folio not stated).
- **Practices & Events**
  - Access: "Today, this place is deserted": the only road "is cordoned off by the police"; a barricade "fortified with barbwire"; only people "carrying special cards, which have been made for them by the Evacuee Trust Property Board (ETPB)" may pass; "Many local tourists return disappointed"; a metal detector door reads 'Out' while the 'In' gate is shut (p. 196, folio not stated). On regular days "hundreds of local visitors" peek in; the security men's answer: "If you are a Muslim, you are not allowed." (p. 196, folio not stated). Male and female police search people, bags pass "through an X-ray machine" (p. 197, folio not stated).
  - Pilgrims: "About three hundred pilgrims from India have come to Lahore for this event, while several hundred have come from other parts of the country"; the Indians stay "for three days", then some return "while others will visit sacred shrines in Nankana Sahib and Hassan Abdal" (p. 196, folio not stated). Most pilgrims are Sikhs "bearing the 5 Ks", a few clean-shaven "Nanak Panthi Sikhs" from "South Punjab and Sindh" (p. 197, folio not stated). The lawn is "a temporary camp for pilgrims who do not have rooms", "primarily Pathan-Sikh families who have come from Nankana Sahib, Hassan Abdal, Peshawar and the Tribal Areas"; Indian pilgrims and "other important guests" have rooms (p. 201, folio not stated).

At the conclusion of Akhand Path "bhog (community prayer) is performed"; "Waheguru"; "the entire community prostates [as printed] to the living Guru, the Granth Sahib"; Sikh boys serve cold milk, juice and "freshly cut water melons" (p. 201, folio not stated).
  - Langar: prepared by volunteers and employees, "an overwhelming majority of who seem to be low-caste Nanak Panthis from Southern Punjab" (p. 201, folio not stated).
  - Informants (names may be pseudonyms): Kartar Singh Kartar, "temporarily residing at this Gurdwara for his education and being given free accommodation and food at the shrine", jokes "I am a langur (a species of monkey)" (p. 196, folio not stated), "the most highly qualified Pakistani Sikh", "to do my PhD in the Punjabi language" (p. 202, folio not stated); "32 year old Dr. Manoj Singh", "the first MBBS doctor from the Pakistani Sikh community", "serving at the Ganga Ram Hospital" (pp. 201–202, folio not stated); "25 year old Premjeet Singh, the first Pakistani Sikh to have been commissioned into the Pakistan Rangers", allotted a room "because of his social standing", shared "with five other family members" (p. 202, folio not stated).
- **Legends & Miracles**

### samadhi-of-maharaja-ranjit-singh — Samadhi of Maharaja Ranjit Singh

The book's "samadhi of Maharaja Ranjit Singh", Lahore, across "a big lawn" from Dera Sahib; contents chapter "Commemoration of Maharaja Ranjit Singh's death anniversary at Lahore" (p. 6, folio not stated), begun p. 205. Reportage of the death anniversary, "29th June 2011" (p. 206, folio not stated). Bullets that only name Ranjit Singh (person-level) are compacted under Saint.

- **Saint**
  - Book's historical prose: "Starting from a small fief around Gujranwala ... the Maharaja marched triumphantly into Lahore in 1799"; "In 1801, Ranjit Singh was crowned as the Ruler of Punjab by Sahib Singh Bedi, a descendant of Guru Nanak"; his empire "extended from FATA (Federally Administered Tribal Areas) to Kashmir to Multan in the South and the River Sutlej"; he made a deal with the British (p. 207, folio not stated).
  - "Like the third Mughal Emperor Akbar, Maharaja Ranjit Singh was a secular ruler"; he "constructed a mosque at a mohalla called Gul Begum in Lahore in memory of his Muslim wife"; "Another Muslim wife of his, Jind Kaur, ordered the construction of a library" at Data Darbar; Muslim generals "General Mohammad Sultan, General Elahi Baksh and General Ghaus Mohammad Khan"; "Fakir Azizudin" held the offices of "Prime Minister and the Foreign Minister" (p. 207, folio not stated). The pavilion, which now stands in the middle of the ground, was part of the structure of Emperor Jahangir's tomb" (p. 207, folio not stated).
  - "There is a view that Ranjit was a true follower of Guru Gobind Singh" (p. 208, folio not stated). Credited with "the construction and renovation of many Gurdwaras all over Punjab"; stability "which lasted a good forty years"; "The British took over soon after his death" (pp. 208–209, folio not stated). "In 1802, he employed a Nihang by the name of Akali Phula Singh to capture the city of Amritsar"; Phula Singh "brought two to three thousand Nihangs into the state army" (p. 210, folio not stated).
  - Person-level mentions elsewhere: "Maharaja Ranjit Singh (1780-1839), the erstwhile ruler of Punjab", whose general Hari Singh Nalwa has a haveli at Katas (p. 42, folio not stated); Panja Sahib "extended" in his tenure (p. 159, folio not stated) and its frescoes "part of the original complex built by Maharaja Ranjit Singh" (p. 167, folio not stated); "During his reign, Maharaja Ranjit Singh captured the Pathan regions of Khyber-Pakhthunkwa and the tribal areas, as a result of which several people converted to Sikhism" (pp. 161–162, folio not stated); he "first erected" the present Dera Sahib structure (p. 200, folio not stated); gurdwara properties "most of which were allotted to the shrines at the time of Ranjit Singh" (p. 203, folio not stated).
- **Shrine / Darbar**
  - Facing the Dera Sahib: "a beautiful white building, adorned with detailed frescoes and other art work ... a grand structure compared to the modest building dedicated to Guru Arjan"; "A police official sits on top of the samadhi with his gun in front of him" (p. 201, folio not stated). "Just behind the samadhi of Guru Arjan, a splendid building, white in colour, stands four floors from the ground with a small dome on the top"; the Badshahi Masjid's minaret behind; "The Hazuri Bagh is situated in the middle of these monuments, with a single storey pavilion placed symmetrically at the center of the garden while four lanes lead towards it" (p. 205, folio not stated). [Side by side: the samadhi "facing" Dera Sahib across a lawn (p. 201) / "Just behind the samadhi of Guru Arjan" (p. 205).]
  - The samadhi "stands on top of another single storey building, painted yellow", in a courtyard "with rooms for the pilgrims on its sides" (p. 206, folio not stated). "Two loud speakers have been placed on top of the samadhi, facing the direction of the ground, erected opposite the mosque and the city, perhaps as a respect to the Muslim religion, a respect that is only expected of the religious minorities" (author's remark) (p. 206, folio not stated).
  - The hall: "a palanquin in the centre, constructed on a raised platform with its pillars made of white marble" (p. 206, folio not stated); it "marks the spot where the last remains of the Maharaja were buried. His cremation took place in the ground accompanying the building" (p. 209, folio not stated). "On a regular day, a portrait of the Maharaja is placed at the palanquin, dressed in a white robe, sporting a long white beard", next to that of "his youngest son, Dalip Singh, who also happened to be the last Maharaja of Punjab"; "Today, the portraits have been removed" (p. 209, folio not stated).
  - The dome "decorated with coloured glass" (green, orange, red) in geometric patterns; niches with frescoes; balcony walls show court scenes and "religious drawings from Hindu mythology", with "depictions of Krishna, Ganesh, Ram and Sita"; the paintings "have faded", some "disappeared all together", and people "have noted down their names and numbers thereby disfiguring it" (p. 209, folio not stated).

### gurdwara-tambo-sahib — Gurdwara Tambo Sahib

The book's "Gurdwara Tamboo Sahib" (pp. 181–190) and "Gurdwara Tambu Sahib" (pp. 107, 222–229), Nankana Sahib, "a few hundred metres away from Gurdwara Janamasthan" (p. 223, folio not stated). Reportage of Lohri (January 2011) and Guru Nanak's birthday (year not stated).

- **Shrine / Darbar**
  - The tree "is spread out like a tent", "an ancient tree, called Waan in Punjabi", "protected by a boundary fence" with one gate; shoes off to enter; "The word tamboo comes from the tent like cover of this tree. Tamboo means tent in Punjabi." (p. 181, folio not stated).
  - Behind the tree "a small mango coloured building with a small dome on the top"; a board "Gurdwara Tamboo Sahib."; the saffron Nishan Sahib in the courtyard (p. 181, folio not stated).   - Grounds: a single-storey building used by farmers, a tractor, "vast land laying vacant on both sides of the shrine which is used for agriculture", "A tall barb-wired wall" on all sides; one gate from the main market; a road board in "English, Shahmukhi ... and Gurmukhi"; a second blue gate "kept permanently locked for security reasons" (pp. 181–182, folio not stated). Inside: "the Thara Sahib (a wooden pavilion which hosts the Granth Sahib)"; two swords in blue velvet sheaths (p. 185, folio not stated).
  - "The area belonging to Gurdwara Tambu Sahib is the second largest in the city, making it the second most popular destination after Janamasthan"; the ground is "big enough to host an international cricket match"; a newly built triple-storey residential hall by the entrance, two larger residential buildings from devotees' donations, "a single storey Langar Hall" (p. 223, folio not stated).

  - "In the future, the Government of Pakistan plans to construct a Guru Nanak University in the vicinity of this complex", a "persistent demand of the expat Sikh community" (pp. 223–224, folio not stated).
- **Practices & Events**
  - Lohri (reportage): Kartar Singh Kartar tears a saffron cloth from inside the Gurdwara and each person ties a piece to the tree; his Muslim friends tie cloth too but keep their hands at their sides (p. 181, folio not stated). The procession then goes to Janamasthan (p. 185, folio not stated).
  - Guru Nanak's birthday: "Most of the pilgrims from the developed countries stay at Gurdwara Tambu Sahib" (p. 223, folio not stated); Shaheed Hall is the "first choice of pilgrims from developed countries", now housing many Indian pilgrims as the former have reduced (pp. 224–225, folio not stated). Security "lax" compared to Janamasthan: metal detectors and a few guards, but "fairly easy to walk in unnoticed" (p. 223, folio not stated). Food served around the clock (p. 229, folio not stated).

## Cross-cutting material

*Merged from the Pass-1 "Practices, institutions, economy", "Legends and miracles" and "Arguments and interpretations" notes; duplicates collapsed, all pages kept. Registers: **reportage** = the author's own observation at the festival; **informant** = what a named person told him (the p. 15 note says names were changed, so any name may be a pseudonym); **book's historical prose** = background the author supplies; **author's argument**. Site names are the book's own. Material about a province in general is under "Province-level material", not here.*

### The book's method, sources and stated limits
- (book's prose) The CAP Minority Project "officially began in September 2010"; "over two hundred oral history interviews" in two years; "about fifty narratives" from long structured interviews, plus short interviews at festivals (pp. 10–11, folio not stated). Some off-record interviews "have been presented in that framework [of the festival] for the purpose of understanding" (p. 12, folio not stated). Names of minority members are changed; "in some instances I have taken the liberty to stray from strictly speaking, non-fiction" (p. 15, folio not stated).
- (author's argument) The title comes from "the white part of the Pakistani flag, which is said to represent the minorities"; the book "aims to freeze the religious minorities of these areas, in this time warp" (pp. 11–12, folio not stated). It goes "beyond the narrowly defined agenda of religious persecution": it is "not a list of tortures", and it records youth reviving traditions and "syncretism, where Muslims take part in Hindu and Sikh festivals" (p. 13, folio not stated).
- (author's argument, Afterword) The book is "about the experience of being a minority in Pakistan", "fraught with danger"; gaining minorities' trust "took some doing" (p. 233, folio not stated). Stated limits: his Hindu interviews were all from Lahore and all with lower-caste Hindus; "My accounts are merely a few snapshots" (pp. 233–234, folio not stated); his Christian interview pool, "due to its different socio-economic conditions was much more diverse than the Hindu group" (p. 234, folio not stated). "one of the basic purposes of this book is to highlight that the experiences of the different religious minorities in Pakistan which cannot be generalized into one" (sentence as printed) (p. 234, folio not stated).

### Demography as the book states it
- (book's prose) Muslims "about 97%"; of the 3% non-Muslims "a large chunk belongs to the Ahmadiyya community"; "In 1947 ... the population of the non-Muslims was about 30% (without including the Ahmadiyya community)", most of whom "have migrated or converted to Islam" (p. 12, folio not stated).
- (book's prose) "according to the census of 1998, the Hindu minority is the largest minority in the country, with Christians in the second place" (p. 87, folio not stated). For where each community lives, by province, see "Province-level material".

### State bodies: Awqaf/Auqaf, the ETPB, the Archaeology Department
- (book's prose) "The Awqaf Department was created during the tenure of General Ayub Khan in 1959, to control, administer and collect revenues from shrines all over the country" (p. 82, folio not stated).
- (book's prose) "Evacuee Trust Property Board (ETPB, – an organization that looks after the property of non-Muslim shrines. It is also referred to as Hindu Awqaf or Wakf)" (p. 46, folio not stated); "created to take care of non-Muslim temples and shrines in the country" (pp. 196, 200, folio not stated).
- (informant: Devi) The Prahlad temple at Multan is under the "Awqaf department"; a High Court stay order was issued on the RAS Society's petition (p. 24, folio not stated).
- (book's prose) The ETPB has controlled Dera Sahib since partition, issues the security cards there and renovates it regularly (pp. 196, 200, folio not stated). Gurdwara property is "about 25,00,000 acres according to an official at the ETPB", mostly allotted "at the time of Ranjit Singh" (pp. 203–204, folio not stated).
- (informant: Bhagat Advit Bhatti, the Pandit of the Neela Gumbad Valmiki temple, Lahore) "Ever since the celebrations at this temple have increased in fervour, the authorities want to take over the administration of the shrine"; he is being "'harassed' and 'threatened' by the authorities to give away control", and his family "has been affiliated with the governing of the temple for generations" (p. 82, folio not stated). "There is vast property connected with the temple ... shops and residential houses around the temple". After "the creation of Pakistan" his father let the authorities "have control over the shops outside of the temple and collect revenue from there, as a contribution of the Valmikis to the Jinnah fund" (pp. 82–83, folio not stated). (book's prose) "Even though the Jinnah fund no longer exists, the authorities still collect the revenues from the shops and the houses, now several thousands of rupees given the prime location", while the Hindu community "continues to live in poverty"; the Pandit says: "We don't even know how much money they collect from our properties" (p. 83, folio not stated).
- (reportage) At Katas, guides were appointed by "The Archaeology Department" when the site was renovated, and the haveli's artwork was "recently renovated" (pp. 42–43, folio not stated).
- (reportage) Government of Pakistan works at Panja Sahib: protective walls "recently raised by the Government of Pakistan" (p. 161, folio not stated); glass decoration "put up recently by the government of Pakistan as renovation work" (p. 167, folio not stated); a "government sanctioned" documentary (p. 166, folio not stated).

### Sikh bodies, gurdwara administration and the calendar dispute
- (book's prose) "All the affairs of Gurdwaras in Pakistan, including arranging festivals, employment, cleanliness, property, renovation etc. are managed by the Pakistan Sikh Gurdwara Prabandhak Committee (PSGPC) that functions under the purview of the ETPB"; it was "formed in 1999" and collects festival revenue, "an amount that runs into several hundreds of thousands of rupees" (p. 203, folio not stated). Before 1999 "the Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee (SGPC), based in Amritsar, used to take the collection at the festivals back to India" (p. 203, folio not stated).
- (informant: Madan Singh, a former PSGPC head) "The PSGPC are cronies of the ETPB dominated by the Muslim community"; "Even the priest (Granthis) and other employees are appointed by the government"; he resigned because "they would not let me work independently" (p. 203, folio not stated). (book's prose) There is "growing resentment" over the PSGPC's handling of "activities, revenues, and property", with allegations of "Gurdwaras being rented out to fill the personal coffers of the officials" (pp. 203–204, folio not stated).
- (informant: Iqbal Qaiser) "Only sixteen out of one hundred and thirty-five historical Gurdwaras are functional in Pakistan"; "The rest are being used as houses, farms, stables, or isolated make-out spots for villagers" (p. 166, folio not stated).
- (book's prose) The calendar dispute: Sikhs used the "Bikrami calendar", whose festival dates shifted every year on the "Georgian calendar" (as printed) (p. 216, folio not stated). Pal Singh Purewal, "a Canadian Sikh", made a solar calendar and presented it "in 1998"; "In 2003 … it was implemented by the SGPC" (pp. 216–217, folio not stated). "It is believed that under the pressure of the Indian government, in 2010 the SGPC approved amendments", which Purewal rejected; Pakistani Sikhs such as Kartar call the move a "sellout". Kartar says: "The Indian government has banned this event all over India", and that it is celebrated "in places like England, America and Canada" (p. 217, folio not stated).
- (book's prose) The Khalistan period: Pakistan's government "openly came out in support of Khalistan as revenge for what had happened in East Pakistan in 1971"; the struggle "gained popularity between the early seventies and late eighties"; banned expatriate Sikhs came to Pakistan for festivals, and there were political speeches after the ceremonies; "That era defines the trajectory of the Sikh community in Pakistan" (pp. 213–214, folio not stated).

### December 1992 and other attacks; encroachment on sites
- (informant: Bishna Ram) The pre-partition Valmiki idol of the Neela Gumbad temple was "designed by students of the Punjab University"; it and a white-marble Krishna were "smashed and destroyed ... by a mob protesting the desecration of the Babri Mosque in India"; "The entire temple was burned down" (p. 67, folio not stated). (book's prose and informant: the Pandit) The attack was "On the 7th of December 1992", by "thousands of them, carrying weapons, including guns"; "the temple had been knocked down and then burned. For days the fire raged" (pp. 68–69, folio not stated). A wrestling arena in the courtyard "was removed when the temple was renovated after the destruction of 1992" (p. 68, folio not stated); the community collected funds and "Within a year or so after the attack, a brand new construction came up" (p. 70, folio not stated). For the Babri demolition date the book gives elsewhere ("6th of December 1992") and its aftermath in Punjab and Sindh, see "Province-level material" (p. 22).
- (book's prose) Sitla Mandir, Lahore: its pool was believed to cure "people with all sorts of diseases" and was visited by mothers "every Thursday" and by "Many Muslims"; it has been replaced by a community park, and the rest of the site is apartments. One is occupied by "Madrassa Noor-ul-Quran", whose maulvi's family moved in after partition and "became the owner of the property by default". "In December 1992" the maulvi instigated an attack and a bulldozer was brought; a boy hammering the cone fell and died and "was given the title of Shaheed"; a plate reading "Ya Allah" now rests in a niche (pp. 69–70, folio not stated).
- (informant: Ahmad Gill) Driving through the mob, he was saved when "four Christian boys emerged and shielded the car"; "I later realized that they were angels" (p. 122, folio not stated). (author's argument) The 1997 reconstruction left "the traumatic components of the attack and underlining prejudices ... unattended" (p. 125, folio not stated). The 2005 and 2009 attacks in Punjab are under "Province-level material".
- (author's argument) After Salman Taseer's assassination "The myth of the silent liberal majority had burst"; the demand has shifted from scrapping the blasphemy laws to their "wrong manipulation" (p. 129, folio not stated).
- (reportage) At Ram Thamman the "orthodox section of the village" has used "the force of the authorities" to curb the festival, removing loudspeakers "from the stalls and Maut ka kuan"; the festival "has managed to thrive" (p. 65, folio not stated). "A few Muslims from the village feel that having food from the donation of the Hindu community is unacceptable" (p. 64, folio not stated).

### Festivals and practice: Hindu
- (book's prose) "Hindu festivals are not officially recognized in Pakistan, so Hindus working at offices have to ask for special holidays"; Charan's five-day leave was refused (p. 35, folio not stated).
- Holi, Multan (reportage, with informant Devi): the Holika burning, about midnight on the eve of Holi in March, is "primarily an all-women's event"; "Only married women take part in this ceremony" (per Devi). About "thirty people" took part. Women circle a pyre of hay and sticks singing devotional songs, pour oil from steel glasses and wind a thread around it; "After seven rounds" a man lights it, and the hay leaves "bright pink ash" (pp. 18–19, folio not stated). "Halwa and berries are offered as prasad"; Devi marks foreheads with vermillion: "This will protect them from evil" (pp. 17–19, folio not stated). Urdu graffiti reads "'Diwali Mubarak'" (p. 18, folio not stated). On Holi day a dhol-wala plays on a roof. Bhang "mixed in almond-milk" would normally be drunk, but the boys "resort to drinking alcohol". Non-Muslims may buy alcohol on a government "permit card" at "a lower price", and some "sell it to their Muslim clients at exorbitant prices" (p. 23, folio not stated). Most women watch: "This is Holi with a tinge of Muslim piety" (p. 25, folio not stated). For matka-breaking, human pyramids ("ten boys" at the base, four lighter boys above, with Durga Mata chants) break matkas of coloured water with a stone (pp. 26–27, folio not stated). In the Christian colony there are buckets of water and dry colours ("orange, red, blue and purple powder"), houses are opened to all, and flirting and public affection "not socially permissible under normal circumstances" are allowed (p. 28, folio not stated).
- Navratri, Bahawalnagar (reportage; visit "16th October 2010", p. 33): Raza and "ten other boys" cleared garbage, collected donations door to door, bought the Durga idol and organised Navratri with a Bahawalpur band. It was "the first time after partition that a Hindu religious festival had been celebrated on such a scale at the Akaliyan Mohalla" (p. 31, folio not stated). According to Raza there were "pilgrims pouring in from as far as Karachi. This is one of the largest Hindu gatherings in the country" (p. 33, folio not stated), and "more than two thousand pilgrims this year"; "Since the past five years, the festival of Navratri has emerged as the most popular event here" (p. 35, folio not stated). Chunris are bought from a vendor and presented to Durga's idol, "an important rite" (p. 34, folio not stated), then "entrusted to the priests, who later make use of it" (p. 37, folio not stated). The langar used five cauldrons; Raza: "The pilgrims give donations, which help us bear the expenses"; "A few years ago, they constructed a Langar hall inside the compound of the shrine, which can accommodate several hundred people at one time". There is no shelter, so pilgrims share locals' houses (p. 35, folio not stated). Rites: aarti and silent prayer. The goddess's clothes are changed (red to green) with attendees and "male priests" sent out; a fire is kept "lit at all times"; protection threads and vermillion are given, and a woman "would then ask for some money" (pp. 38–39, folio not stated). There is "a twenty feet tall figure of Durga Mata cut out of a cardboard" (p. 38, folio not stated). The Kunwari procession: three girls "not older than fourteen in this case" (p. 33, folio not stated), the youngest "about ten years old", dressed as brides and carried in palanquins from about eleven at night. They enter the temple "At about midnight" (pp. 39–40, folio not stated); "This is the last rite of the festival" (p. 41, folio not stated). Raza: "We do not burn Dusshera [as printed] here because of space issues" (p. 41, folio not stated).
- Vows (informant: Charan Das, 42, a peon "at the DIG of Police Office in Multan"): if given a job, he would go without shoes, wear an ankle bangle for a year and cycle to the shrine (p. 34, folio not stated).
- Shivratri 2011, Katas (reportage, with informant Gupta): "After Langar women would take the mehndi of Shiv to the temple, as sisters and sister-in-laws do so for the grooms"; "we have organized four other marriages here", funded "by the contributions of the Sangat (the Hindu community present)" (p. 51, folio not stated). For the langar, "Lentils would be cooked for dinner along with tea", and "Dinner is served at eleven in the night" on "Two white plastic sheets"; for some it begins a fast broken "at noon tomorrow" (p. 51, folio not stated). In the procession, mehndi plates hold "oil lamps and lumps of henna", and a shawl is held by four women with "no one standing underneath it". Elder women place mehndi "at the base of the deity and perform the aarti"; Diyal "pours [milk] onto the Shivling" (pp. 51–52, folio not stated). There were bhajans and a band from Peshawar playing "popular Indian songs" (p. 51, folio not stated). Stalls sold "small idols, pictures of deities, CDs of bhajan recordings, toys for children, bindiyas and mangal sutras" (pp. 52–53, folio not stated).
- Baisakhi, Ram Thamman (reportage; visit "14th April 2011", p. 56). Attendance: "According to Police Intelligence Reports in the archives of the Punjab Secretariat at Lahore, about thirty-five thousand people attended this festival in the year, 1946. This was also the last time Baisakhi was celebrated on such a scale" (p. 56, folio not stated). Today it is "attracting thousands of people" (p. 56, folio not stated); "According to the locals, more than a thousand people come here during these three days" (p. 60, folio not stated). Calendar: it "begins on the 13th of April, which corresponds with the first of Baisakhi according to the local calendar"; "The third and the last day is reserved for women only" (p. 60, folio not stated). At the fair there is a "Maut ka kuan" ('Well of Death') motorbike act and "About a dozen eunuchs, or Khwaja Saras" dancing (pp. 54–55, folio not stated); stalls spread "over an area of nearly two kilometres", with Babas selling rings and lockets ("Ya-Ali", "Love", "Friendship") (p. 60, folio not stated). (informant: Abdul Razzak) He has come for "the past twenty-five years" as "the third generation from his family" selling bangles; "In the earlier days we used to come and stay here for an entire week". Earlier, "Famous wrestlers" and "Popular folk theatre companies" performed "Heer-Ranjha, Sohni-Mahiwal and Raja Rasalu" (p. 61, folio not stated). Eight boys perform Dandiya Raas with dhol; a green chadar with Quranic verses in golden thread, "brought by the group as a gift to the Saint", collects money as "a gift for the performers"; "all contributions are voluntary". Ghulam Ali, 22, says: "There is no invitation for such festivals. People come out of their devotion" (pp. 62–63, folio not stated). Families cut wheat on the village outskirts (p. 63, folio not stated). In 2011 Bhagat Advit Bhatti's delegation of "three hundred people" arrived "on the night of 12th April", prayed before sunrise "at the temple of Kali Mata" and performed "an ablution (ashnan)"; "This is the first year after partition that Hindus have performed a prayer here"; "The door of the temple was opened by Hussain". "For now the Hindus perform the religious rites, while the Muslims take part in the festivities" (p. 64, folio not stated).
- Valmiki's birthday, Neela Gumbad, Lahore (reportage, 2011): preparations began "two days in advance". On "the 21st of October" Azad Chaudary, 45, a Balmiki who became Christian at 22, practised a Mohammad Rafi bhajan with his son Yashwa; Musharraf Ali, 35, a Muslim flute player whose father was a Balmiki, attended (p. 72, folio not stated). The crowd was "Muslims and Christians, most of whom have Balmiki roots and Balmikis"; there was a vegetarian langar of puri and channa (pp. 72–73, folio not stated). Prayer was "At about seven in the evening", with the call "Bolo Shri Valmiki Maharaj ki" / "Jai" and halwa as prasad (p. 73, folio not stated). The final performance was by Zohaib, 30, a Muslim sarangi player of the sixth generation (pp. 74–75, folio not stated). The temple itself faces "a narrow alley, once known as the Valmiki Street", has "a triangle-shaped orange flag with a golden border", "an essential feature of all Valmiki temples", and has no guards (p. 66, folio not stated). ### The author's arguments on history, nation and religion
- "Pakistani historiography discards Hindu and Sikh narratives from its discourse and where ever they are mentioned, they are always mentioned negatively" (p. 9, folio not stated). On the Prahlad temple and the Zakariya shrine, the history of syncretism "is not unconsciously ignored but deliberately eliminated to ensure the conformity of history to the dominant nationalistic discourse" (p. 20, folio not stated). Multan's Hindus are "strangers in their own home"; "they are the true descendants of this land" (p. 21, folio not stated).
- By calling Hindus followers of the book, "Al-Beruni raises their status"; "in contemporary Pakistan, where nationalism is premised upon hatred for Hindus, such a claim would not only be shunned but also taken offence to" (p. 45, folio not stated).
- Dating by brick size at Ram Thamman: the bricks "are smaller than the ones preferred by the British", so the structures predate the British arrival (p. 57, folio not stated).
- "nothing is more political in Pakistan than religion. The creation of the country was based on religious identity" (p. 151, folio not stated). "The political and religious realities here don't allow any blur in religious distinctions"; "The strict compartmentalization of religious practices and identity of which Pakistan is a product of leaves no room for the historical religious syncretism that was once part of this land's religious traditions" (p. 167, folio not stated). "The tradition of making idols and other religious items practised in this part of the world for thousands of years died an untimely death after the partition" (p. 167, folio not stated). Hassan Abdal's Muslim onlookers are "too insecure about their own religious identity to join them like they used to before partition" (p. 169, folio not stated).

### (5) Internal contradictions and attribution doubts (kept side by side)

- **Shanti Nagar:** the Quran pages were found "On the night of 5th February 1997" (p. 121), yet the chapter ends on "the events of 6th February 1997" (p. 125). This may be the discovery and then the attack the next morning; the book does not say.
- **Guru Arjan vs Ranjit Singh commemorations:** "6th June 2011" (p. 196) and "29th June 2011" are called "twelve days after" and "Separated by twelve days" (pp. 206–207). The printed dates are 23 days apart.
- **Guru Nanak festival, Nankana:** it is said to fall in "the last days of November", but in 2011 it ran from the 8th to the 10th of November (p. 222).
- **Guru Arjan's number:** "the fifth Sikh Guru" (p. 162) vs "the fourth Sikh Guru" (p. 168).
- **Katas dating:** Shakeel and the authorities' blue board say Mahabharata, "around 1500 BCE", while "Experts say that these temples were constructed between the 7th and 10th century CE" (p. 44).
- **Raza's timeline:** Raza is "35", was "about 20 at that time", and the room is dated "Fifteen years ago" (p. 30). Yet the book says he is "talking about the late eighties" under Zia "(1978-88)" (p. 30).
- **Maryabad distances:** Faisalabad is "A hundred kilometres away" (p. 85) vs "150 kms. away" (p. 88). The Okara route is "more than one hundred and fifty kilometres" (p. 85), with Lahore "one hundred and twenty kilometres from Okara" (p. 85) and Lahore "about 70 kms." from Maryabad (p. 88).
- **Sadaf:** she is "a 73 year old woman" (p. 138) in a scene dated "21st March 2011" (p. 137), yet she "was born after partition" (p. 138). Farah is "Desai" (p. 136) while her sister is "Dhodi" (p. 138).
- **Hazrat Bab:** his body was moved "sixty years after his death" by Abdul Baha "in 1909", but the execution is dated "1850" (59 years). He is called "the founder of the Baha'i religion" (p. 146), while Bahaullah is the messiah whose followers "came to be known as Baha'is" (p. 148).
- **Baisakhi at Panja Sahib:** the Sikhs are "most of them Indian Punjabis" (p. 157) vs "Most of the Sikhs here are ... Pathans" (p. 158). "here" may mean the resident families.

Whether "Lahore Cathedral" is the "Cathedral Church of Resurrection" (p. 105) is not stated. "the Roman Cathedral" (p. 105) is unclear.
- **Acronym:** "Pakistan Congress for Equality and Harmony (PPCEH)" (pp. 116, 120). The acronym does not match the name.
