## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The turn-system for shrine offerings — the single most useful economic definition in the
  chunk.** "ban 'turn' to collect shrine offerings which are shared by the descendants of a saint"
  and "bdriddr 'holder of a turn' to collect shrine offerings" (both p. 262, folio 242).
- **Shares within a performing group, and a recent change to them.** "The double share for the
  leader is a relatively recent practice running counter to the traditional brddri [text-layer?:
  birādarī] rule of equal shares, a development regretted but practised by all Nizamuddin Qawwals
  and by other hereditary Qawwali groups as well" (note 20, p. 255, folio 235). The unit itself is
  defined at p. 262 (folio 242): "brddri 'community of brothers', endogamous patrilineal community
  usually sharing a hereditary service profession".
- **The collective first song and its take.** "This, at Nizamuddin Auliya and other shrines,
  appropriately gets to benefit the entire group of shrine performers, since they sing the first
  song collectively and all share equally in its 'take'" (note 28, p. 255, folio 235). The practice
  is defined twice in the appendix: "mushtar kd gdnd 'mixed singing', communal singing in which any
  member of the community of shrine musicians has a right and duty to participate" and "panchdyati
  gdne 'communal songs', the ritual Qawwali songs that are sung communally, mainly Qaul and Rang"
  (both p. 259, folio 239).
- **Turn-taking between groups at an assembly.** "ban kd gdnd 'singing by turn' so that individual
  performing groups follow one another during a Qawwali assembly (syn: parti ka gdnd)" (p. 256,
  folio 236); "parti Qawwali performing 'party', headed by a lead singer (or possibly two); modern
  equivalent of chauki" and "chauki a performing group of Qawwals named after the leader or his
  ancestor" (pp. 257, 260; folios 237, 240).
- **Propitiatory offering.** "Occasionally, an offering is also made for the specific purpose of
  diverting possible evil from the sheikh. Such a propitiatory offering (sadqd) is a traditional
  Indo-Muslim practice and includes a standard gesture of circling the offering money above the head
  of the recipient" (note 26, p. 255, folio 235).
- **The saint's anniversary as the occasion for a special repertoire.** "There is a small number of
  ritually used hymns that customarily follow the obligatory ones on special ritual occasions,
  principally the anniversary of a saint. Prominent examples among these are a second Qaul, also in
  Arabic (La tamdfi), as well as some Persian and Hindi songs (for example the Persian ghazal
  Bakhubi, or the Hindi songs Aj badhdvd and Aj tond…)" (note 25, p. 255, folio 235).
- **The obligatory hymn.** "qaul 'saying'; the basic ritual song of Sufism obligatory as the Qawwali
  occasion's opening or closing hymn; the text is based on a saying of Prophet Muhammad; several
  less important hymns are also called qaul" (p. 259, folio 239).
- **Basant.** "basant 'spring festival' and its performance ritual" (p. 256, folio 236) and "basant
  'spring festival', one of the annual rituals at Nizamuddin Auliya" (p. 262, folio 242); its song
  is named at p. 256 (folio 236), "the basant song Phul rahi sarson".
- **Tomb orientation — architecture, stated as a rule.** "Muslim tombs are always oriented toward
  the Kaaba: the body, lying on its back, has the face turned to the right, so as to face in the
  direction of Mekka. The tomb itself is aligned with the body; and the entrance opening is placed
  at the foot end. In India, then, all tombs open toward the South, and have a North-South
  orientation, usually with a mosque to their West" (note 24, p. 255, folio 235).
- **Death during samaʿ, twice, with sources.** "On 20 February 1983, Sufi Abdul Karim Sumar died
  during the 'urs assembly of Baba Ahmad Shah in Karachi, during the recital of a verse by Jami (as
  reported in the newspapers, Dawn, 21 Feb. 1983, and Nawae Waqt, 22 Feb. and 3 March 1983). Maulana
  Muhammad Husain of Allahabad died likewise in Ajmer about 60 years ago, on a famous verse by the
  Chishti saint Abdul Quddus Gangohi (Ajoo, 1983)" (note 27, p. 255, folio 235). **The dates here
  are inside a body sentence and are treated as printed.**
- **Discipleship.** "bai'at 'vow of allegiance', bond of spiritual allegiance and discipleship
  between a murid and his pir" (p. 262, folio 242); "buzurgdn-e-dln [text-layer?: buzurgān-e-dīn]
  'the venerable elders of religion', the panoply of Sufi saints" (p. 262, folio 242).
- **Two named assemblies from the fieldwork**, given only as labels here: "This sama' assembly is
  the Chilla Mahfil, analysed in Chapter Six (Performance No. i [text-layer?: No. 1])" and "This
  assembly is the Hujra Mahfil, analysed in part in Chapter Six (Performance No. 2)" (notes 1–2,
  p. 254, folio 234).
- **Commercialisation and instruments.** The harmonium "was introduced into India by European
  missionaries. Appreciated as early as the seventeenth century (Fryer 1909, Vol. 2, p. 103), the
  instrument is used to accompany all light classical singing, despite continuing resistance from
  purists" (note 17, p. 254, folio 234). The dholak is "barrel-shaped double-headed wooden drum in
  standard use for Qawwali; also common in folk music" (p. 257, folio 237). The modern commercial
  genre gets its own entries: "filml qawwali 'film Qawwali', popularized and secularized Qawwali
  genre initially developed in film music" and "bdzdrigdne 'songs of the bazar'; somewhat derogatory
  term for the category of popular song genres (also: filmi gdne)" (pp. 257, 256; folios 237, 236).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The book's four-part thesis about musical meaning is stated on one page and should be read as a
  block** (p. 248, folio 228). (i) "Qawwali music articulates the ideology of Sufism and conveys the
  meaning of both its structure and its dynamic. That meaning is built into the very structure of
  Qawwali music". (ii) "At the same time, Qawwali music articulates the social reality of Sufism…
  permeated with social meaning at various levels, both manifest, where congruent, and latent, where
  contradictory with Sufi ideology." (iii) "Qawwali music conveys to its listeners affirmation of
  traditional structures, whether ideological or social." (iv) "the same music also concurrently
  articulates, and even promotes, individual seif-assertion [text-layer?: self-assertion]", which
  "may even transcend the confines of traditional structures, as long as this happens within the
  context of the Qawwali event".
- She leaves the consequence of (iii) against (iv) open as a question rather than answering it:
  "Does this meaning endow Qawwali music with the capacity to act as a medium of change, with the
  potential to transform traditional structures even as it serves to articulate them?" (pp. 248–249,
  folios 228–229).
- **The structure of the musical code.** Two characteristics: "the limited range of specific meaning
  options assigned to each individual feature. More often than not, there are only two such options
  or alternatives - for example, a complex vs. a simple musical metre conveying high vs. low status";
  and "the presence of more than one meaning variable operating simultaneously" (p. 249, folio 229).
  The consequence she draws: intensity is produced by redundancy — "the maximum being reached when
  all channels 'say the same thing'" (p. 249, folio 229).
- **An equivalence claim, and a diagnostic one.** "a relation of equivalence obtains between the
  nature of the musical code and the nature of the contextual meaning it serves to convey"; and the
  very features that show it "are also the ones that render Qawwali distinct from other closely
  related musical codes in the Indo-Pakistani region" (p. 249, folio 229).
- **The central socio-economic argument.** "the limitations inherent in the traditional Qawwali code
  correspond perfecdy [text-layer?: perfectly] to the limited role which the Qawwal, its maker,
  actually plays in shaping the traditional context of performance"; the variable structure "is
  controlled ultimately not by the musician but by the listener, although indirecdy [text-layer?:
  indirectly]" (p. 250, folio 230). Hence: "Social dependence thus means musical dependence"
  (p. 250, folio 230).
- **She names and tests her own method's bias.** "Has it then, in fact, been a fallacious Western
  premise of this analysis to focus on the artist as the agent of musical creativity and to consider
  him the catalyst of the musical event…?" — answered by conceding that "the artist's musical
  choices are largely predicated on what he perceives to be the audience's will", and that building
  the analysis on the performer "is ultimately no more than an operational choice" (p. 250, folio
  230). She raises "whether any single vantage point can generate knowledge that is more than a
  point of view" and "whether the dynamic behind human action, even that of one individual, can be
  grasped across cultural, social and intellectual boundaries" (p. 250, folio 230). **Her stated
  answer is methodological and personal: "to complement analysis with analogy, the analogy of
  experience. From my own perspective as another music-maker"** (p. 250, folio 230).
- **Power and its containment — the chapter's sharpest claim.** Contextual constraints "become
  themselves the very subject of his musical utterance", so that "issues of spiritual and
  socio-economic priority, of dominance and submission, of hierarchical order and individual
  assertion, of conformity and creativity, are being negotiated audibly, in the language of music"
  (p. 251, folio 231). Because music, unlike speech, "is not normally subject to correction by
  anyone other than the musician", control must be indirect: "The only way to control the Qawwal's
  musical power over his audience is to give his audience religiously legitimized social and
  economic power over him", enforced "through the presence of a strong authority structure and
  through strict rules of censure for defying it" (p. 251, folio 231). The resolution: "the amount
  of control imposed upon the musician here is in direct proportion to the amount of power invested
  in the musical communication" (p. 251, folio 231).
- **A methodological claim about where musical meaning lives.** "the semantic content, even that of
  music, appears to be found outside music, for the dynamic that ultimately motivates the
  context-to-music input can only be understood with reference to the socio-cultural framework"
  (p. 252, folio 232) — offered as the justification for a music analysis having "to delve so
  extensively into the contextual and background dimensions".
- **Comparative programme.** The approach "could serve as a pilot study, if not a prototype, for the
  analysis of other Indian and Pakistani performance traditions" (p. 252, folio 232), drawing on the
  "'cultural performance' tradition central to social and cultural life (Singer 1972)"; and could
  refine "the concept of an Indie [text-layer?: Indic] music area… analogous with the existing
  concept of an Indie language area (Emenau 1956)" (p. 252, folio 232), toward "a truly Indie
  ethnomusicology" (p. 253, folio 233).
- **Closing claim.** The Qawwali discourse "connects the abstract with the concrete, the emotional
  with the social, and the normative with the idiosyncratic", which "points unequivocally to the
  musical process as the dynamic locus where musical meaning is articulated and makes its unique
  impact" (p. 253, folio 233).
- **Where the Conclusion says the questions came from.** It closes chapter 8 by citing its own
  earlier pages: the tables "explain and illustrate 'how Qawwali is put together into an actual
  musical sequence' as required in Chapter 2 (p. 75)", and answer "the question posed at the outset
  (p. 5): how Qawwali, the music, articulates with Qawwali, its context of performance" (p. 245,
  folio 225). **Both "(p. 75)" and "(p. 5)" are printed folios** — they are folios 75 and 5, i.e.
  PDF pp. 95 and 25 by folio + 20.
- **Table 41 is in ordinary type and survives legibly** (p. 246, folio 226): "Table 41 Qawwali
  Performance Model: Context Input Summarized / 'Qawwali music varies according to context'", with
  three columns, "Text dimension", "Music dimension", "Musical presentation", and rows "Choice of
  Prelude, Introductory Verse and Song" and "Sequencing of Song". Its cells read "ace. to status"
  [acc. to status], "ace. to identity", "ace. to state", "ace. to selective focus" — the four
  semantic referents named in the running text as "status", "identity", "state" and "selective
  focus" (pp. 245–246, folios 225–226).

  glossary entries. I have not chosen between them; both are recorded above as printed.

  **Two are Ghulam Farid Sabri, a modern concert qawwal** (p. 250, folio 230; note 19, p. 254, folio

  1. **Music-notation and table-plate noise — one full page.** p. 247 (folio 227) is the Table 42
     plate and is almost entirely broken glyphs and floating fragments (`z UJ`, `a PUJ z`, `<   u`,
     `OS o <`, `rhythi nversi`). **Not deciphered, not reconstructed, nothing lifted from it.** Its
     caption, running header and folio are all illegible; the folio is supplied by the rule. Table 41
     on p. 246 (folio 226), by contrast, is ordinary type and survives well enough to quote.
  2. **The ā→d class, firing in italics and therefore producing two spellings of one term.**
     Confirmed again here, and the appendix is the best place in the book to see it: `asthdyi`
     (p. 256, folio 236) against `asthdyl` for the same term twice on p. 260 (folio 240); `lai kd
     halqd` (p. 258) against `takrdr ka halqd` (p. 260); `naghmd` (pp. 258–259, folios 238–239).
     **`girah` prints clean in the appendix** ("girah 'knot'", p. 257, folio 237; and inside the
     parknd entry, p. 259, folio 239) — so the damaged form `girdh` recorded elsewhere in the book
     is the italic variant, not the printed headword. Dozens of further headwords carry the class:
     `dldp`, `antard`, `dddrd`, `bdjd`, `barhdnd`, `tardnd`, `takrdr`, `chdl`, `charhd`, `utrd`,
     `dwdz`, `chilld`, `sadqd`, `brddri`, `silsilds`, `thekds`, `dohd`.

  4. **The tt/tl→d class in ordinary English prose**, two clear instances on one page: `perfecdy`
     [perfectly] and `indirecdy` [indirectly] (p. 250, folio 230). Also `litde`-type damage is
     absent elsewhere in the chunk.
  5. **The c→e class**, five instances, all on the same word: `Indie` for *Indic* at pp. 252 (twice),

     "kamdtaka". Also `ace. to` for *acc. to* throughout Table 41 (p. 246, folio 226).
  6. **A candidate ELEVENTH substitution the coordinator should rule on: l → i.** Three independent
     instances, in three different registers: `seif-assertion` for *self-assertion* in the

     list of ten and it fires in body prose, so it is not an italic-only effect.** Flagged rather
     than added to the list.
  7. **A candidate TWELFTH: initial retroflex ṭ → l in italic headwords.** `labia` is the glossary's
     headword for *tabla* — "labia paired drum in standard use for classical music" (p. 260, folio

     for *tappa*, "lappa light classical vocal genre" (p. 260, folio 240). **Consequence for the
     project's own searching: a grep for "tabla" in this book returns nothing from the appendix.**
  8. **A candidate THIRTEENTH: n → ri/rii in italic.** `purdrii dhun, tarz` (p. 259, folio 239) and
