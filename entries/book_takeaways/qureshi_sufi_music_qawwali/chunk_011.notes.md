## Practices, institutions, economy

This is the substance of the chunk: the etiquette of the assembly and the flow of money through it.

**Who has authority in the assembly.**
- The assembly **leader** can over-rule the performer's chosen song at the last moment: "he knows that
  his choice can always be over-ruled at the last moment by a direct request from a spiritual notable,
  for which he must be prepared" (p. 215, folio 195).
- Requests are ranked by the requester's standing. A request judged "just right" (`sahih`) may be sung
  at once without introductions; "As for requests by insignificant listeners, they are fulfilled only
  if the choice seems right to the performer; otherwise he ignores them" (p. 215, folio 195).
- The leader controls the clock: a shortage of time is "indicated, either by signals from the leader,
  or caused by delays preceding the performance", and the performer may then drop the Prelude and even
  the Introductory Verse (p. 215, folio 195).
- A "conservative assembly leader" may require the traditional thematic hierarchy to be repeated, in
  which case "a first ruba'i praising the Prophet will satisfy the formal requirement" and frees the
  performer for a more rewarding song (p. 216, folio 196).
- A leader "giving precedence to his common audience" is one of the two reasons for switching the
  Introductory Verse from Farsi down to Hindi or Urdu; the other is an audience not responding to
  Farsi, "an indication that sophisticated Sufis are missing" (p. 216, folio 196).
- The leader validates ecstasy bodily: the `takrdr` [text-layer?: takrār] repetition must be continued
  "especially once a state of ecstasy is validated by the leader through his gesture of standing up,
  thus causing the entire audience to rise, as happened in Performance No. 2" (p. 225, folio 205). The
  moment is named idiomatically — "Up to the point when 'the mahfil stands up' to acknowledge ecstasy,
  there is some freedom of movement for the performer, at least theoretically" (p. 225, folio 205).
- The leader's arm outranks the performer's momentum: "a simple movement of the leader's hand can send
  him off the stage even after the start of the new song, as happened to Meraj in a Qawwali Hall
  assembly (see p. 105 above) with many visiting Qawwals present" (p. 226, folio 206; the "p. 105" is a
  printed folio — see Doubts).
- The leader may also police a listener's ecstasy: performers "heartily approve of some leaders' tactics
  ensuring that such a person is not allowed to keep the floor, or later to stay in the leader's embrace
  too long" (p. 225, folio 205). The worked instance is the 1976 ʿurs assembly in Khwaja Hasan Sani's
  own `hujrd`, "when an ecstatic adept embraced the exalted `diwdn` of Ajmer … and was, on the leader's
  signal, pulled away to be led back toward his seat at the 'lower' end of the audience" (pp. 225–226,
  folios 205–206). **The audience is thus seated by rank, with a 'lower' end** (p. 226, folio 206).

**How money moves.**
- Offerings are the immediate, visible register of a song's success: if the song choice "is at all
  fortuitous, this impact will be expressed immediately in the form of at least a few offerings made by
  those who wish at this time to link themselves to the message theme or identity of the song" (p. 218,
  folio 198).
- The performer holds a line in repetition **until the money has physically arrived**: repeat statements
  (`dohrdnd` [text-layer?: dohrānā]) "are kept up at least until all offerings have reached their
  destination. This is important because it provides even the listener who is last in line with the
  opportunity to make his offering to the sound of the statement that inspired it" (p. 219, folio 199).
  A second reason is stagecraft: it "ensures that all commotion caused by the offering gestures has
  ceased, and nothing will distract the audience from the impact of the next statement" (p. 219, folio
  199).
- Offerings made to someone other than the leader must be passed on to him before the performer moves
  on, "even though this process is a spiritual formality which need not be tied to the statement that
  initially inspired the offering" (p. 219, folio 199).
- **Offerings circulate as status display among a saint's descendants.** "There are gatherings where the
  presence of several descendants of a saint causes a great expansion of this formality, for by passing
  offerings, once received, to each other rather than `direcdy` [text-layer?: directly] to the leader,
  these descendants demonstrate mutual respect and validate each other's spiritual status before the
  larger audience" (p. 219, folio 199). The performers' verdict on it is flat: "often what looks like
  much offering money is just 'one rupee making the rounds' (`ek rupyd chdroh tarafghumtd hai`
  [text-layer?: ek rupyā chāron taraf ghūmtā hai]), but when it reaches the performer 'it's still only
  one' (`to ek hi hai` [text-layer?: to ek hī hai])" (p. 219, folio 199). **This is the only sum of
  money named anywhere in the chunk; no fees, shares or totals are given — (not stated).**
- **The pick-up arrangement is described as an institution, and it differs shrine by shrine.** "In the
  few shrines where the leader provides one of his men for the task the performer has nothing to do with
  the matter, but in most assemblies it is he who designates one of his singers to look after the
  transfer of offerings from the leader to the performer" (p. 223, folio 203).
- The handling is ritualised: the designated man "must be continually on the alert, presenting himself
  to the leader as soon as one or several offerings are deposited, so that he may receive them, which he
  does by respectfully bowing and raising the money to his forehead before taking it to the performer's
  harmonium" (p. 223, folio 203).
- A rule of decorum governs the leader's lap: "Unless the leader wishes it otherwise, the performer will
  see to it that money is never left lying in front of the leader (this is also the method followed by
  the leader's own carrier)" (p. 223, folio 203).
- The arrangement is double-edged. "Most performers consider this process an appropriate means of
  reminding the audience of the importance of offerings, but it can also waste precious performance
  time, at the point when a verse line has yielded all its offerings, and the performer would like to
  introduce the next line without any distracting commotion"; hence "the pick-up man is always alert,
  and he swiftly moves in on the offerings as they are made, while the lead performer sees to it that
  the line is repeated just long enough to see the money reach him" (p. 223, folio 203).
- **Order of giving follows status.** "Some offerings are now likely to come from the common audience,
  who often tend to follow behind special listeners in their responses" (p. 223, folio 203). The same
  cascade is charted in Table 29, option b): multiple repetition makes "Focal listeners respond more
  strongly, impressing others who then offer" (p. 217, folio 197).
- **A doctrinal limit on what may be paid for.** A purely musical insertion (`tan`) "will not bring
  offerings directly - for it would not be in keeping with Sufi tenets to reward a tune without words -
  but the reward will come at the appropriate moment when the connection with the second verse line is
  made" (p. 224, folio 204).
- **`nisbati` verses carry an obligation to give.** "verses invoking the link between devotee and saint
  or spiritual leader (so-called `nisbati` - 'connectional' or 'linking' verses), have a more or less
  guaranteed appeal which requires an offering response from devotees so as to activate the link
  element. Such verses are of course never omitted, even if limited time requires the song to be
  shortened" (p. 225, folio 205).
- **Invoking the saint's name is a recognised money-move, and a leader may resent it.** "the leader may
  show displeasure at too many repeated appeals to the saint's name, for he considers this too obvious a
  ploy for compelling the devotees to confirm their allegiance by an offering" (p. 221, folio 201). The
  same trade-off is charted at Table 29 option c): multiple repetition alternating the line with the
  saint's name on his anniversary makes the "Common audience responds, offers, but leader may be
  displeased, dampening atmosphere/enthusiasm" (p. 217, folio 197).
- **Displeasure has a price.** "To be scolded or set right by the leader not only affects the performer's
  standing adversely, it also disturbs the atmosphere of the assembly which in the end is likely to
  reduce his income from offerings" (p. 225, folio 205).
- **Time is the performer's scarce good.** "Except where he enjoys privileged access or is the only one
  to sing, a performer is primarily concerned with using his performance time to his best advantage. An
  insignificant person's ecstatic state can constitute a significant loss of precious time" (p. 225,
  folio 205). He conserves it by an "instant switch from one unit of structure to another", ending a
  song "without any cadential signal, simply by starting in on the opening line of the next one, often
  before quite finishing the last line of the previous song" (p. 226, folio 206) — and where his turn
  may be cut off he "tries to eliminate any structural clues to finality - such as slowing down or
  intoning a melodic or rhythmic cadence - so that the assembly leader may not anticipate the impending
  end" (p. 226, folio 206).
- **A calculated risk against the leader, for a rich listener.** There are "moments when he is willing to
  risk that displeasure, for example on seeing an opportunity for a high reward, in the person of a rich
  devotee with a particular preference which he would rather satisfy. The reward to be gained may be
  great, but it is never guaranteed, so that the risk is real" (pp. 224–225, folios 204–205).
- **An unrepeatable windfall.** When an Introductory Verse itself turns into a song, "Performers consider
  such an occurrence a windfall, because, of course, offerings are invariably part of such a positive
  response" — "'This song came to us as a bonus' (`ieh chlz muft men mil gayi` [text-layer?: yeh chīz
  muft men mil gayī]), said Meraj after this happened to him with the Masnavi (Repertoire No. 4) in no
  less a place than the great 'urs assembly at Ajmer in 1974, and the ecstatic response of several
  listeners had brought the entire gathering to its feet (`sari mahfil khari hogayi` [text-layer?: sārī
  mahfil kharī hogayī])" (p. 216, folio 196).
- **A lost connection is a lost collection.** Of leading a `girah` back into the song: "If this climactic
  connection cannot happen, a valuable opportunity for offerings is lost" (p. 222, folio 202).

**Occasions and ritual settings named.**
- The ʿurs appears three times: "the great 'urs assembly at Ajmer in 1974" (p. 216, folio 196);
  "Nizamuddin Auliya's 'urs in 1976" (p. 216, folio 196); and "an 'urs assembly (1976) in Khwaja Hasan
  Sani's own `hujrd`" (p. 225, folio 205). A "saint's anniversary" is also named generically as the
  occasion that makes a saint-addressed or mystical-love song more rewarding than one addressing the
  Prophet (p. 216, folio 196) and as the setting for Table 29's option c) (p. 217, folio 197).
- Other settings named: "the main celebrational assembly" (p. 213, folio 193) and "a Qawwali Hall
  assembly … with many visiting Qawwals present" (p. 226, folio 206).
- The `zikr` is the ritual anchor of the Prelude: a spiritually sophisticated audience, or a performer
  wanting "to project a conservative spiritual image", gets a `naghmd` [text-layer?: naghma] built
  entirely "on the anapaestic rhythm derived from the `zikr Allahu`, tracing with it the traditional
  melodic outline of the `naghmd-e-Quddusi` [text-layer?: naghma-e-Quddusi] (Repertoire No. `lib`
  [text-layer?: No. 11b])" (p. 213, folio 193). Whatever version is used, "the Prelude serves to suggest
  a `zikr`-like experience to the audience" (p. 213, folio 193).

**Language hierarchy as a social instrument.**
- The Introductory Verse "to evoke a higher, but never a lower, status than the song proper. Thus a Farsi
  song can only be introduced by a Farsi verse, a classical Hindi song may be preceded by a verse in
  Farsi, but not normally in Urdu, while for an Urdu song verses from all three languages are
  acceptable" (p. 215, folio 195).
- "Farsi verses are favoured for introductions because they evoke the classical Sufi saints and the entire
  symbolic realm of mysticism", and "a verse in Farsi will make its impression even on an unlettered
  crowd, giving them enough of a taste of high Sufism without boring them with an entire song they do not
  understand" (p. 215, folio 195).
- Worked example: in Performance No. 2 "Meraj chose the Farsi verse `Shud dilam` (Repertoire No. 8) to
  introduce the Hindi song `Kachh jagmag` (Repertoire No. 5)" (p. 215, folio 195).

**Censure, propriety and the limits of a performer's licence.**
- Stringing `girah` verses together is "what Sufis and even Qawwals contemptuously term `tukbandi`
  ('string-along' of verses)"; breaking the song's "chain or `silsild` [text-layer?: silsila]" to "forge
  links of his own" is "highly presumptuous" even where topically appropriate (p. 222, folio 202).
- A salvage operation that buries the song text under long Inserts is "a 'hodge-podge' (`khichri`,
  literally: a dish of rice and lentils mixed together)", "disdained by good performers and censured by
  Sufis, for both uphold the integrity of the song message in its given sequence as one of the
  fundamentals of Qawwali singing" (p. 226, folio 206).
- A popular-style musical insertion invites criticism; a "clearly 'classical' choice such as a phrase from
  a famous raga, or a brief passage using classical solmization syllables (see Table 3), is usually
  appreciated as a sophisticated enhancement by the leader and his assembly" (p. 224, folio 204).
- A leader or spiritual notable may reject a textual version outright, and the performer must yield on the
  spot: "Even when the performer knows his own version to be the standard one, he must then immediately
  correct his performance by changing words or glossing over the offensive text unit as soon as he is
  alerted to the demand" (p. 226, folio 206). "A performer may resent the 'disturbance' caused by such
  arbitrary demands, but whether they arise from a Sufi's self-assertion or his spiritual need, he meets
  them to the best of his ability, as is his task" (p. 226, folio 206).
- An Inserted Verse "runs the risk of inviting displeasure, especially from a discriminating leader or a
  Sufi poet in the audience, since it represents the performer's own choice" (p. 221, folio 201).
- When a listener of standing suggests a verse for insertion, the performer complies gladly, because "its
  author, and probably others respecting his spiritual stature, are likely to assert their link to this
  message by an offering" (p. 221, folio 201).

**Reading the audience and escalating intensity.**
- The performer has "no more than moments to size up" the assembly before beginning (p. 213, folio 193);
  his single decision fixes "song theme, association or author, language and style", the "tune, rhythmic
  framework and ritual association", any "durational or speed limitations - in case of an inexorably short
  'turn'", and the "presentational style" (p. 213, folio 193).
- The genre grants a stay of execution: "the Qawwali song genre is structured in just such a way as to
  allow the performer an initial time of grace in order to observe, test or 'try out' his audience",
  through the Introductory Verse (`ruba'i`) and the Instrumental Prelude (`naghmd`) (p. 213, folio 193).
- The modern Prelude is preferred by audiences "because it has a smart beat" (`kyuhkeh is men thdp wdp
  zard thlk dtd hai` [text-layer?: kyunkeh is men thāp wāp zarā thīk ātā hai]) (p. 213, folio 193); for a
  "substantial common audience" the performer has his accompanists "liven up his tune with strongly
  accentuated drumbeats or handclaps" (p. 213, folio 193).
- During the Prelude he "scan[s] the audience for any subtle signals that could indicate song preference"
  and "by looking around solicitously, especially at the leader and other important personages, he also
  declares himself ready to comply with any direct requests" (pp. 213, 215; folios 193, 195).
- Failure to read the room is escalated through further verses: "Very rarely a performer proceeds to a
  third, even a fourth `rubai`; such an extended introduction invariably reflects his uncertainty" —
  Meraj at Gulbarga extended the Farsi `ruba'i Shud dilam` (Repertoire No. 8b), moved to Urdu, then to a
  third `ruba'i` in Hindi, "while switching themes from ecstatic (`rinddna` [text-layer?: rindāna]) to
  link-related (`nisbati`)" (p. 215, folio 195).
- Total failure leaves only intuition: when he "can't figure things out" (`kuchh samajh men nahih dyd`
  [text-layer?: kuchh samajh men nahīn āyā]) "he simply picks whatever he feels like singing at that
  moment (`jo man men ae` [text-layer?: jo man men āe]) and trusts in the saint's blessing (`karam`) - it
  may just succeed; conversely, the performance may have to be written off as a loss" (p. 216, folio 196).
- Signs he watches for: "strong swaying, head shaking or other rhythmic movements" (p. 224, folio 204);
  "enthusiastic gestures or exclamations" (p. 216, folio 196); "a signalling gesture or even … a direct
  verbal statement" of disapproval (p. 224, folio 204).
- Once a listener is strongly moved, any interruption will "'disturb' (`distarb karega` [text-layer?:
  disturb karegā])", and "even the most appropriate text Insert will only 'sting the aroused devotee
  greatly' (`wahdh to usko girah chhubegi bahut` [text-layer?: wahān to usko girah chubhegī bahut])" —
  Meraj's own comment on a thwarted `girah` in Performance No. 2 (p. 224, folio 204).
- Ecstatic arousal converts a chanted verse into a metred song: "mere repetition of the verse chanted
  without drumbeat will not suffice, for this state calls for the intense rhythmic repetition of `takrdr`"
  (p. 216, folio 196). The same conversion inside a `girah` "may require considerable rhythmic skill",
  since the drum's metre is that of the principal song and "may not naturally fit with the verse rhythm
  (that is, the poetic metre) of the Insert" (p. 221, folio 201).
- Intensifiers listed: "extra weight on accented beats by drum or handclaps, clapping 'double' on
  half-beats, or increasing the tempo very gradually"; they are "meant to be used in situations of intense
  arousal or ecstasy", but a performer may deploy them opportunistically "hoping that offerings may
  result", and "'Actions', too, may sometimes be used for that purpose, provided, of course, they are not
  censured by the leader as being too worldly" (p. 224, folio 204).
- The technical vocabulary of building a line: "to open the [salient concluding] line" (`misra' kholnd`
  [text-layer?: misra' kholnā]) (p. 218, folio 198); repeat statements (`dohrdnd`) (p. 218, folio 198);
  multiple repetition (`takrdr`) (p. 220, folio 200); 'encirclement of repetition' (`takrdr kd halqd`
  [text-layer?: takrār kā halqa]), in which "appropriate fillers - such as saints' titles or exclamations
  - highlight the transition from one repeat statement to the next one, with filler and text unit
  contrasted by solo-group responsory" (p. 225, folio 205).
- Solo/group division is itself a claim to authority: "The opening line of the song he intones solo, thus
  presenting its message in absolute clarity as well as drawing attention to his own leadership of the
  performing group" (p. 218, folio 198).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The author's governing claim for the section: performance choice is a reading of the assembly, made in
  moments.** "What has taken paragraphs to outline can obviously take the performer no more than moments to
  size up before he begins his presentation. Thus, on the basis of both structural and setting factors, he
  arrives at a performance choice for his first Qawwali item" (p. 213, folio 193).
- **The genre is built to buy the performer time to diagnose his audience.** The Introductory Verse and
  Instrumental Prelude exist structurally "to allow the performer an initial time of grace in order to
  observe, test or 'try out' his audience" (p. 213, folio 193) — the Introductory Verse specifically
  "representing, for the listeners, a sort of preview of what is to follow in the song. The performer, in
  turn, has the opportunity of testing their reaction to his choice of theme and language" (p. 215, folio
  195).
- **Language hierarchy is treated as a social diagnostic, not merely a convention:** an audience that fails
  to respond to Farsi is read as one in which "sophisticated Sufis are missing" (p. 216, folio 196).
- **Repetition is analysed as an economic instrument, not only an expressive one.** The author's repeated
  move in this chunk is to explain a musical choice by the offering it is designed to secure or to protect:
  repeats held "at least until all offerings have reached their destination" (p. 219, folio 199); `nisbati`
  verses that "requir[e] an offering response … so as to activate the link element" (p. 225, folio 205); an
  Insert whose lost climax means "a valuable opportunity for offerings is lost" (p. 222, folio 202).
- **She marks a limit the economy itself respects:** a tune without words cannot be rewarded, "for it would
  not be in keeping with Sufi tenets" (p. 224, folio 204) — i.e. the offering is attached to the *message*,
  not to the music as such.
- **Intensification is presented as initiated by the performer, not merely elicited from him.** "At this
  point the performer is obviously using musical intensifiers in order to evoke intensification of his
  listeners' state, rather than only responding to such states - indeed there may not yet be much to respond
  to" (p. 220, folio 200).
- **Authority is asymmetric and the author says so plainly:** "the assembly's leader takes seriously any
  manifestation of arousal - as indeed he must - even if it comes from a low-status or junior listener; the
  performer therefore had better do likewise" (p. 225, folio 205).
- **Structural analysis of the verse line as the unit of communication.** The performer "focusses on the
  most easily comprehensible text unit, the verse line. Using it as the standard unit of communication, he
  follows a general pattern of repeating each verse line one or two times" (p. 218, folio 198); and he takes
  "special care to convey the crucial connection between the initial 'statement' portion (one or more verse
  lines) and the final 'answer' portion (concluding line)" (p. 218, folio 198). The claim is that the
  second, concluding line is the arousal engine: "With the exception of an impressive opening statement of a
  song, it is the second, concluding line of a verse which, most of all, can be expected to generate
  arousal" (p. 223, folio 203).
- **The closing methodological statement of the chapter section, and the hinge to what follows.** "This
  completes a generalized picture of the performance process, focussing on one complete unit of performance,
  a song, but placing it within the larger context of a performer's 'turn' and, ultimately, that of an
  entire Qawwali event. Having thus informed the reader ethnographically how Qawwali music in performance is
  context-sensitive in a remarkably formal and observable way, the ground is prepared for dealing with the
  process of performance analytically so as to make sense out of the rule system at work" (p. 227, folio
  207).
- **The thesis stated at its most compact:** "What has emerged so far is a multi-faceted process in which
  the performer uses music to express context and also to manipulate it. In decision after decision he
  responds instantaneously to a multiplicity of contextual variables and articulates his response by means
  of a multiplicity of musical variables" (p. 227, folio 207); and "Clearly this surface diversity is
  governed by principles or rules which enable the performer to make each musical choice with reference to
  each particular contextual factor instantly and with consistency" (p. 227, folio 207).
