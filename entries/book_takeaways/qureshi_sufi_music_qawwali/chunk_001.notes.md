## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The ecstatic must not be cut off, on pain of death** — reported by the author as the performers'
  own understanding, in her account of a night sama' at the 'urs: "one significant phrase of the song
  so moves an elderly Sufi that he cannot contain himself; he rises and begins a dance of ecstasy.
  For the performers this signals a moment of extreme responsibility, for unless the ecstatic person
  continues to hear the phrase that so moved him, he may die" (p. 24, folio 4).
- **The 'urs as the saint's union with God**, not his death: "the commemoration of his own final union
  with God on the anniversary of his death" (p. 21, folio 1).
- **Amir Khusrau as "the founding father of Qawwali"**, whose "verses and tunes form the core of
  [the] sama' ritual to this day" (p. 21, folio 1) — the book's attribution, given without evidence
  in this chunk.
- **Colour as a marker of lineage:** "A prominent Sheikh at the centre, draped in the orange colour
  of India's highest saintly lineage" (p. 23, folio 3). The lineage is not named (not stated).
- **Blessing and reward linked:** as the singers comply with the ecstatic, they are "hoping that upon
  this spiritual blessing the material reward of offerings will follow" (p. 24, folio 4).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **The book's governing hypothesis, stated at the end of the chunk:** "Qawwali musical sound will
  vary with variation in its context of performance - or, put more simply: Qawwali music is
  context-sensitive" (p. 25, folio 5).
- **The three orders of question** the study sets itself: "questions about sound, its structure and
  sequencing in the Qawwali musical idiom; questions about norms and behaviour in the Qawwali ritual;
  and questions about the relationship between musical and non-musical action in the performance of
  Qawwali" — reduced to one: "how does Qawwali musical sound become meaningful outside itself?"
  (p. 25, folio 5).
- **Method: musicology first, anthropology for the rest.** "Musicology, therefore, is the proper
  starting point for introducing an anthropological perspective to the analysis of musical sound"
  (p. 16, folio xiv); but musicological analysis "will not… account for the sequencing of the musical
  structure", and to do so "requires tools which only anthropology can supply" (p. 16, folio xiv).
  The aim is "a context-sensitive grammar that would enable a musically literate reader to understand
  how variation in the performance of Qawwali is generated" (p. 15, folio xiii).
- **Her positioning in the discipline, with names:** the study "belongs with those which situate music
  within a holistic paradigm focussing on musical performance (for example, Frisbie, Seeger, Feld and
  Stone)" but differs in that "its primary focus is on the musical sound idiom, an approach that
  ethnomusicologists have so far tended to reserve for art music traditions (for example, Powers,
  Malm, Becker)" (p. 16, folio xiv). Intellectual debts to "Harold Powers" and "Michael Asch"
  (p. 17, folio xv).
- **Qawwali is defined against its commercial form:** "not the popular version of Qawwali adapted for
  entertainment in clubs and on the screen, but the authentic spiritual song that transports the
  mystic toward union with God" (p. 21, folio 1).
- **Three definitions the book gives of Qawwali from its participants' side:** "To the Sufi
  participant, Qawwali is 'a method of worship' and 'a means of spiritual advancement'; it is also 'a
  feast for the soul'. To the performer it is mainly a musical genre 'with its distinct character for
  worship'. To the observer, finally, Qawwali is above all music performed very obviously with
  continual reference to its context; it is 'music in context' par excellence" (p. 15, folio xiii).
- **Qawwali as ritual:** "a gathering for the purpose of realizing ideals of Islamic mysticism through
  the ritual of 'listening to music', or sama'"; the music supplies "a powerful rhythm suggesting the
  ceaseless repetition of God's name (zikr)" (p. 15, folio xiii).
- **Gender, stated as fact by the author:** "Given the fact that Sufism is an all-male tradition, and
  Qawwali music the province of very low-status hereditary performers, I as a Western (for which read
  'high-status') female should have been in a double bind. That the opposite was true is due
  principally to the kindness and eclecticism that characterizes the Sufi community" (p. 17,
  folio xv). Compare the Introduction, where the sama' draws "men - and, rarely, women - from all
  walks of life" (p. 21, folio 1). **Flagged for the consolidator: this is an authorial
  generalisation, not a reported finding.**
- **The 1995 retrospect on her own method.** "Twenty years later questions of subjectivity and
  representation pervade the academy, problematizing authorial power, interpretation, and even 'close
  readings' of music. Decentered paradigms diminish confidence in the scholarly process, and
  postcolonial consciousness challenges any certainty about the cross-cultural understanding on which
  this study is based" — answered, not retracted: "it continues to be meaningful to explore and
  'translate' highly particular musical practices in an open process of negotiation across cultural
  and individual differences" (p. 14, folio xii).
- **The repertoire is not the author's selection.** "The choice and order of presentation of the
  following repertoire is based on a consensus of significance by Sufis at the Nizamuddin shrine"
  (p. 13, folio xi).
- **Her stated transliteration system** — directly relevant to the damage in this text layer.
  "Consistency in transliteration is based on the use of a single comprehensive reference source which
  covers vocabulary of both Perso-Arabic and Hindi-Sanskrit derivation: Platts's Dictionary of Urdu
  and Classical Hindi (1970). The only phonetic addition is the symbol a [text-layer?: a superscript
  or schwa symbol that did not extract] for the short vowel which is inserted, in singing, between
  consonants, after a long syllable" (p. 19, folio xvii). Terms "are therefore presented in accordance
  with Indo-Pakistani Sufi usage and pronunciation; this includes standard Islamic terms and refers
  particularly to Persian forms of Arabic words" (p. 19, folio xvii). **None of that system survives
  in this text layer: see Doubts, class 2.**
- **Script policy, stated and consequential.** "Urdu rather than Devanagari script is purposely used
  for the Hindi selections, because they are part of an entirely Urdu-speaking and -writing community
  which uses Persian rather than Devanagari script for Sufi poetry in Farsi, Urdu and Hindi (for
  example Naghmat-e-Sama n.d., Idris Khan 1973, K. Nizami 1973a and P. Nizami 1975)" (p. 19,
  folio xvii). Translations "were made in collaboration with Dr Saleem Qureshi; however, I assume full
  responsibility for them" (p. 19, folio xvii). **The Urdu script the author says she uses does not
  survive anywhere in this text layer; see Doubts.**
