## Practices, institutions, economy

**This is the substance of the chunk.** The material below is Qureshi's account of the dargah as an
economic institution and of the Qawwal as a service professional inside it.

### The dargah as an institution

- Imperial patronage founded the endowment system: "the imperial rulers were generous in granting
  property endowments to such Sufi establishments, particularly the shrines built around the graves of
  their founders" (p. 111, folio 91).
- Four orders entered India "during the thirteenth and up to the sixteenth century": "the Chishtiya and
  the Suhrawardiya, followed by the Naqshbandiya and the Qadriya" (p. 111, folio 91).
- The Chishtiya "is the earliest and the one spread most widely throughout India; in fact, it has in the
  main remained an Indian and Pakistani order with little presence outside the subcontinent" (p. 111,
  folio 91).
- **The decisive structural change is the shift from spiritual to hereditary succession**: "came an
  expansion in the reckoning of Sufi lineages from the principle of purely spiritual descent to the
  inclusion of familial descent from a founder saint", giving "the physical descendants of saints the
  hereditary right to control and manage the endowments, both spiritual and material, of their
  ancestor" (p. 112, folio 92). Thus "a dargah, or Sufi shrine, whether endowed with property or not,
  came to be controlled by a legitimate representative of the saint buried there, whose right is based
  on familial rather than spiritual descent" (p. 112, folio 92).
- **Succession is contested by design**: "Theoretically, there is one single successor to the leadership
  of the saint and his tomb, the sajjdddnashin or gaddinashin [text-layer?: sajjādanashīn /
  gaddīnashīn] ('the one sitting on the [saint's]' prayer mat or throne), but at most large shrines at
  least two if not more descendants often claim this right" (p. 112, folio 92). At major Chishti shrines
  "such as Ajmer or Nizamuddin Auliya, entire communities of such representatives have a hereditary
  share in managing the shrine's spiritual and material benefits" (p. 112, folio 92).
- **What the descendants receive and control**: "Individually or collectively, they receive the income
  or revenue from shrine endowments" (p. 112, folio 92); they distribute "material tokens, especially in
  the form of amulets (tdwiz) [text-layer?: taʿwīz]"; each acts as "the agent {wakil} [text-layer?:
  wakīl] of the Sufi devotees"; and "Most important of all, he is entitled to accept on behalf of the
  saint the propitiatory offerings of the devotees" (p. 112, folio 92).
- **Service professionals are hereditary but dependent**: "professionals providing menial services such
  as sweeping the shrine or cooking food which devotees wish to give away to the needy in the name of
  the saint", and "the Qawwali performers who are indispensable to the performance of sama' [text-layer?:
  samāʿ] assemblies as well as shrine rituals" (p. 112, folio 92). "Notwithstanding their superior
  professional skill and service, the Qawwals, along with all other service professionals, belong to the
  servants of the dargah and stand in a servile or 'client' relationship of dependence to the shrine
  descendants" (p. 112, folio 92).
- **The author's own economic summary**: "At the level of a self-contained institution the Sufi dargah is
  a quasi-feudal establishment in which a hereditary appropriating class of saindy [text-layer?:
  saintly] representatives controls the resources, whether they be property revenue or offerings
  received from devotees" (p. 113, folio 93); and "Viewed in relation to its larger socio-economic base,
  however, the dargah is ultimately an institudon [text-layer?: institution] of patronage, which is
  received either directly or indirectly" (p. 113, folio 93).
- **Patronage is exchanged for legitimation**: "In return for this worldly support the Sufi dargahs have
  historically provided religious legitimization for the Muslim ruling elites, especially vis a vis the
  general subject population" (p. 113, folio 93).
- **Twentieth-century decline of the support base**: under British rule "Sufi establishments continued to
  operate with relative stability (apart from some interruptions such as the 1856 Mutiny)" (p. 113,
  folio 93). After independence "their traditional socio-economic support base has weakened
  considerably, due to the lack of Muslim political dominance, and, more generally, the recent
  decimation of the feudal-based Muslim establishment which was caused by land reforms on one side and
  migration to Pakistan on the other" (p. 113, folio 93). The compensating shift: "individual patronage
  from among the larger public has assumed more importance for Sufi institutions, including patronage
  from the non-Muslim elite" (p. 113, folio 93).
- **Pakistan, specifically** — the chunk's one substantive passage on the archive's country: "In
  Pakistan, on the other hand, a newly evolving elite structure with a strong immigrant component
  (including sheikhs and Qawwals who had themselves migrated from Sufi centres in India), has provided
  support for Sufism and its practice in the major cities. In addition, Pakistan's linguistic regions
  have their own traditions of Sufism and sama'; these are rooted in local shrines and often linked to
  the local feudal establishment. Pakistan's search for an Islamic cultural identity has provided all
  kinds of sama music, but particularly Qawwali, with new opportunities for institutional patronage of a
  secular nature" (pp. 113–114, folios 93–94). **No Pakistani shrine, region or city is named** in this
  passage — all (not stated). The author's forecast: "the generational and economic ties that united
  Sufis, Qawwals and devotees around a major shrine are weakening. The result is a potential
  transformation, both of Sufi practice and of Qawwali music" (p. 114, folio 94).

### Custodianship at Nizamuddin Auliya: the pīrzāde

- Collective, not single, custody: the shrine "is managed and controlled collectively by a group of
  descendants or pirzdde (sing, pirzddd, offspring of a/ur) [text-layer?: pīrzāde, sing. pīrzāda,
  offspring of a pīr]; they live in the walled 'village' (bastl) [text-layer?: bastī] outside the
  shrine" (p. 114, folio 94).
- Descent: "Tracing their descent from the saint's nephew or sister (he himself never married), the
  pirzade are organized into three operative family groups which share the income of shrine offerings
  (see Plate 4) according to a complicated system of week-long 'turns' (bdri; the holder of a turn is
  called bdrlddr; see Dehlavi 1964 for details, also Jeffries 1979)" [text-layer?: bārī / bārīdār]
  (p. 115, folio 95).
- **The turn is worked at the tomb itself.** Plate 4 is captioned "Pirzdde, descendants of the saint
  Nizamuddin Auliya, take their turn (ban) [text-layer?: bārī] watching the tomb and accepting
  offerings from devotees" (p. 114, folio 94).
- A second, parallel income stream is regional clientage: "Individually, they act as agents or advocates
  {waklt} [text-layer?: wakīl] to families of devotees on the basis of their regional affiliation, and
  receive their personal offerings at the shrine" (p. 115, folio 95).
- Three men stand at the head, each claiming sole succession: "Most prominent among these are three
  leading figures, one of each family group, each of whom claims to be the sole legitimate successor of
  the saint (sajjdddnashin) [text-layer?: sajjādanashīn]. Each has a personal following of disciples or
  devotees and uses one or more shrine cells to minister to them (see Plate 5)" (p. 115, folio 95).
- **The two patrons own purpose-built Qawwali halls**: "The leaders of two families have been the
  principal patrons of Qawwali occasions; they each own a hall situated just outside the shrine and
  built expressly for large public Qawwali performances during 'urs celebrations. At the time of writing
  it was the well-known Pir Zamin Nizami who controlled the Urs Mahal {'urs palace), while Khwaja Hasan
  Sani Nizami was managing Qawwali Hall alongside the tomb of his own father, Khwaja Hasan Nizami"
  (p. 115, folio 95).
- **No endowment at all — the whole economy is offerings**: "A special aspect of the Nizamuddin shrine is
  its total lack of any property endowments. The only income, therefore, even for thepirzdde
  [text-layer?: the pīrzāde], comes from offerings and gifts" (p. 116, folio 96).
- The political substitute for endowment, in a secular capital: "there is a great political potential to
  be utilized in this capital city of a nation state, professing a secularism which is, in part,
  expressed through the official patronage of minority religions. Officials and functionaries therefore
  constitute important sources of direct and indirect support, as do foreign Muslim visitors" (p. 116,
  folio 96).
- **The offering transaction, stated plainly**: "visiting devotees can also benefit Qawwali performers
  who may be singing in front of the tomb at the right time to receive their offerings, but it is the
  pirzdde who have direct access to their donations" (p. 116, folio 96). And the shrine's own dependency
  runs the same way: the service professionals "stand in a dependent client relationship to the pirzade,
  insofar as the pirzade control the shrine and all its activities and manage the devotees, who are the
  only source of income for all" (p. 115, folio 95).

### The Qawwal Bachche: hereditary right, record book, earnings

- Origin and name: "At Nizamuddin Auliya, the hereditary Qawwal community traces its descent to the
  original Qawwali singers who are said to have been trained by Amir Khusrau himself, and who are known
  as Qawwal Bachche, 'the (original) Qawwal Offspring'" (p. 119, folio 99).
- **The record book and the token payment — the chunk's sharpest economic detail.** "Their affiliation
  with Nizamuddin Auliya is enshrined in an historical entitlement (mohtdd) from the shrine's income,
  which they value most highly and record carefully, even though it amounts to no more than a few paisas
  (see Plate 7)" (p. 119, folio 99). Plate 7 is captioned "The Qawwal Bachche's record book of mohtdd,
  token payments confirming their hereditary right to perform at the Nizamuddin Auliya shrine" (p. 119,
  folio 99). On `mohtdd` see Doubts — the word carries the ā→d damage but the position of the damaged
  letter is not recoverable, so **the term should be verified against the page image before it is quoted
  in an entry**.
- Size and composition: "Numbering 23 adult male members at the time of writing, the Qawwal Bachche come
  from several family groups (khdnddn) [text-layer?: khāndān], identified by their four towns of origin
  near Delhi (Dasna, Sikandarabad, Khurja, Hapur), and collectively belong to the same brddri
  [text-layer?: birādarī]" (p. 119, folio 99).
- The brādarī as a corporate body: "Qawwals are organized into brddris (literally, bradri is a community
  of brothers), endogamous patrilineal communities defined in accordance with a common local origin and
  subject to a governing body of elders (panch). All male members call each other bhdyl (brother)
  [text-layer?: bhāyī] and women normally do not seclude themselves from male bradri members, though
  otherwise female seclusion is standard practice among Qawwals" (p. 117, folio 97). Marriage: "a
  preference for marriage within the kin group, including cross and parallel cousin marriage" (p. 117,
  folio 97).
- **Women are excluded from performance outright**: "Boys are instructed by their male elders, for women
  have no part in Qawwali singing at any stage" (p. 118, folio 98).
- **Hereditary right is conditional, not absolute**: "At the shrine of their affiliation they have a
  hereditary right to the performance opportunities for Qawwali generated and controlled by their
  patron, but they are in turn obligated to provide their performing services whenever needed, otherwise
  their patrons can admit outside performers into the hereditary performer group (as may happen if the
  group cannot fulfil its ritual singing obligations)" (p. 117, folio 97).
- **Two earnings regimes.** "there are two kinds of singing engagements, mushtarkd gdnd or panchayati
  gdnd ('mixed' or 'communal' singing) and bdrikdgdnd or parti kd gdnd ('singing by turn' or 'party
  singing')" [text-layer?: mushtarka gānā / panchāyatī gānā; bārī kā gānā / pārtī kā gānā] (p. 120,
  folio 100). Communal singing "covers all the singing which is done directly in front of the shrine
  sanctuary, that is, all ritual days, as well as Thursday and Friday singing (see Plate 9)" (p. 120,
  folio 100). Plate 9 is captioned "Panchdyatl gdnd in front of the Nizamuddin Auliya tomb,
  commemorating the anniversary of the death of Hazrat Ali" (p. 121, folio 101).
- **The share rule, stated exactly**: "The earnings from all communal singing are distributed equally,
  but strictly to those present at the time of receipt, so that Meraj becomes eligible for a share from
  the moment he sits down to sing, even if just before that a big offering has come in" (p. 120, folio
  100).
- **Mistrust of the collector is institutionalized in an idiom**: the performers calculate shares
  instantly "for self-protection, lest the money 'get lost in the pick-up man's fingers' {ungliyoh ke
  bhul bhulayye ban j die haih, literally: his fingers turn into a maze)" [text-layer?: ungliyoṅ ke
  bhūl-bhulaiyye ban jāte haiṅ] (p. 120, folio 100).
- **A different rule at the big anniversaries**: "all those who are present on the first evening are
  entitled to a share, even though with the many and simultaneous Qawwali events everyone cannot, or
  need not, be present everywhere, as long as all ritual singing is carried out properly" (p. 120, folio
  100). "Old men with failing voices are subject to this right too, and at the time of writing even the
  deaf-mute son of a senior performer!" (p. 120, folio 100).
- Attendance economics on ordinary days: "only those with no other engagements - mostly the old men -
  come to the shrine on ordinary days (sddhe din) [text-layer?: sāde din], but on days with scheduled
  performances the entire group attends, though in varying numbers, depending on earning prospects and
  personal need" (p. 120, folio 100).
- **Internal competition for the harmonium**: "leading members, or a shrine representative present,
  assign on the spot who should be 'on the harmonium' (lead the performance), but there may be
  competition to 'push down' (dabdnd) [text-layer?: dabānā] a colleague out of antagonism, or just for
  fun" (p. 120, folio 100).
- **Party singing is the real income**, and it is allocated by turn: Meraj, "like the three other parties
  in the brddri, has an assured 'turn' to perform in the various anniversary occasions led by the
  principal representatives at the shrine. Normally he is entitled to first place after the ritual
  singing, but that can be superseded on special occasions" (p. 120, folio 100). His rights extend
  outward: "a customary right to sing at various other annual assemblies or mahfils in and near Delhi,
  as well as in shrines around Hyderabad where his ancestor Tan Ras Khan had settled after the 1856
  Mutiny" (p. 120, folio 100).
- **The assembly leader is the gatekeeper of the hereditary right**: "Such rights, however, are subject
  to validation by the assembly leader, which entitles, but also obligates, the performer to attend"
  (p. 120, folio 100). At the level of a whole shrine: "for major Qawwali assemblies, and during the
  'urs in particular, outside performers from nearby, and sometimes from far-off shrines, visit the
  shrine so that assembly leaders can draw from a pool of performers" (p. 123, folio 103).
- **Patron cultivation**: "Meraj needs to cultivate his patron who will favour him over his colleagues
  and pass any private performing work to him (kdm dildnd) [text-layer?: kām dilānā]. As a special
  service, Meraj offers a weekly performance at the tomb of his patron's father" (p. 122, folio 102).
- **How a lead singer is chosen** — literacy and memory over voice: "it is because of his intelligent
  understanding and remarkable memory for Sufi poetry that his father chose to train him as a lead
  singer and 'gave him the harmonium' (bdjd de did, signifying musical leadership) [text-layer?: bājā de
  diyā]. His brother had a far superior singing voice but was made an accompanist, playing the drum, for
  he could never remember or understand verses well" (p. 122, folio 102).
- **Attendance on the patron as professional training**: "personal association or 'attendance' (sohbat)
  at the teaching circle of Sufi patrons is indeed an essential means for a Qawwal to acquire a
  background knowledge of Sufism and its cultural expression, especially the literary dimension of Sufi
  poetry, which requires a literate tutor" (p. 117, folio 97). It stops short of discipleship: "He may
  even cement the contact with a link of discipleship, but that is in no way a necessary concomitant of
  what both Qawwal and Sufi consider a professional rather than a personal or spiritual tie" (p. 118,
  folio 98).
- Second competence required of a Qawwal: "a Qawwal is not considered capable of performing on his own
  until he has gained an understanding of what he describes as 'the up and down of the gathering'
  (mahfilke nasheb-o-fardz) [text-layer?: mahfil ke nasheb-o-farāz]" (p. 118, folio 98).

### ʿUrs, calendar and assembly types

- **Dates of the two great anniversaries at Nizamuddin Auliya**: "The primary formal activities of the
  shrine are the two anniversaries of Nizamuddin Auliya and Amir Khusrau which fall exacdy
  [text-layer?: exactly] six Islamic months apart on the seventeenth of the Islamic months of
  Rabi-us-Sani and Rajab respectively. Each is celebrated for over five days" (p. 115, folio 95).
  **The book does not assign an era or a Common-Era equivalent: (not stated).**
- What the ʿurs involves: "numerous Qawwali events, together with ritual offerings to the saint in the
  form of special food blessed and dedicated and distributed publicly (tabarruk)" (p. 116, folio 96).
- **The melā and the crowd**: "The 'urs celebrations, along with the adjacent fair (meld) [text-layer?:
  melā], attract hundreds from Delhi and outside, in addition to the steady number of poor people who
  live in and around the shrine, benefiting from food, offerings and charity" (p. 116, folio 96). The
  author cites "Census of India [1961], 1966" for visitor numbers (p. 116, folio 96) — **no figure is
  printed in the chunk itself: (not stated).**
- Who attends: "representatives from other important shrines or from smaller local tombs, and spiritual
  guides who will in turn be sought by their disciples"; "Disciples and devotees of high social standing,
  some visiting from distant centres, others representing local worldly authority"; and "large numbers
  of local visitors attend a major 'urs much like a fair and take in Qawwali as well" (p. 116, folio 96).
- **Other annual events**: "Basant, actually the Hindu spring festival, when an event from the saint's
  life is celebrated with special songs by Khusrau", plus the anniversaries of Hazrat Ali and Baba Farid
  and the martyrdom of Imam Husain (p. 116, folio 96).
- **The general calendar of Qawwali**: the ʿurs, "the commemoration of a saint's final union (wisdl)
  [text-layer?: wisāl] with God on his death day ('urs)", is "the prime raison d'etre for holding
  Qawwali assemblies"; also "the saint's monthly or weekly death days"; and "Qawwali is performed weekly
  on Thursday, the day for the remembrance of the dead in Islam, or on Friday, the day of congregational
  prayer" (p. 123, folio 103).
- **A rare workload figure**: "An important shrine may require its performers to sing at about 50
  assemblies in a year, around half of them major ones" (p. 123, folio 103).
- Range of ʿurs scale: "from a single Qawwali occasion led by the representative of a small shrine, or by
  a sheikh with a group of disciples, to a week-long succession of many assemblies held by different
  spiritual personages for different audiences among large numbers of pilgrims at a major shrine"
  (p. 123, folio 103).
- The major ʿurs assembly: held "to celebrate the saints' union with God with Qawwali, and also to
  commemorate his death by the appropriate ritual for the dead (qui, khalam)" [text-layer?: qul, khatam
  — see Doubts, the book prints two spellings]; "Located near the actual tomb or otherwise close to the
  saint, they are held during either day or night and can last from half an hour to all night long"
  (p. 123, folio 103).
- **Four categories of occasion at Nizamuddin Auliya** (p. 124–125, folios 104–105): (1) the intimate
  mahfil-e-khās [printed `mahfil-e-khds`], "really at the heart of Sufi Qawwali at Nizamuddin Auliya",
  held by both major representatives during the ʿurs, "relatively short and held during daytime", where
  "Only choice Qawwals are admitted"; (2) "The major, celebrational assembly held in a large public
  place", "the mainstay of every 'urs celebration", "held at night and last for hours, their sound
  spread wide by loudspeakers"; (3) "the Major Shrine Ritual, a large assembly held in the shrine
  compound facing the sanctuary… the official death ritual of the 'urs called qui or khatam"; (4) "the
  minor ritual and non-ritual Qawwali occasion", "of least significance as performance occasions".
- **Women and the qul**: "the qui attracts large numbers of devotees - including women, who are permitted
  in the shrine compound. There is a set time for the qui, in the late morning, and its duration is
  limited, although no one person is in control of this event, due to the peculiar situation of
  collective representational rights shared between pirzade" (p. 125, folio 105).
- A third assembly type, not shrine-based: "the gathering of disciples around their sheikh or plr
  [text-layer?: pīr]", held at the shrine when "disciples of one sheikh visit the shrine for an occasion
  like the 'urs" — "Such an assembly may be very small, but the spiritual bond between those present is
  particularly intense" (pp. 124–125, folios 104–105).
- **Outside the shrines**: "it is established Sufi sheikhs or saintly descendants who hold Qawwali
  assemblies for their followers, with varying regularity but guided by the same basic pattern of their
  saint's commemorative days. For this they normally rely on performers attached to nearby shrines"
  (p. 123, folio 103).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

The chunk carries no karāmāt as such. Three traditions are reported, each with the author's own hedging
retained:

- The Qawwal Bachche "trace their descent to the original Qawwali singers **who are said to have been
  trained by Amir Khusrau himself**" (p. 119, folio 99).
- The cell in which Pīr Zamin Nizami holds his dawn assembly is "the small cell **where the saint is said
  to have taught and meditated**" (p. 124, folio 104).
- The Chilla is the gallery "**where Nizamuddin's Sheikh, the saint Fariduddin Ganj-e-Shakar, is said to
  have performed** a forty-day seclusion of chilla" (p. 124, folio 104).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The book's central structural claim** is that the dargah reproduces the surrounding social order:
  "the central institution of Sufism in India, the dargah, clearly reflects the larger socio-economic
  structure of traditional Indo-Muslim society" (pp. 112–113, folios 92–93).
- **Power, not religion, is the axis of Muslim social ranking in India**: "What links the entire Muslim
  social structure together and distinguishes it from Hindu caste society is that power, both political
  and economic, constitutes the primary criterion of status differentiation. Religion can at most play a
  legitimizing role; in addition, religious stature can serve to enhance social status" (p. 111, folio
  91). The two strata are "the ashrdf (sing, sharif) [text-layer?: ashrāf, sing. sharīf], 'noble' or
  'well-born' Muslims claiming foreign descent and holding political or economic power, especially land"
  and "the 'low-born' Muslims… grouped into occupational classes or 'castes' (zdt [text-layer?: zāt])"
  (p. 111, folio 91).
- **Bribery read as a survival of the courtier pattern**: "Bribery, though universally condemned in
  recent times, can be seen as a natural concomitant of this pattern: it represents the material link of
  an offering between client and patron" (p. 111, folio 91). **This is the author's analytic bridge to
  the shrine offering**, and it is the one place the two are put side by side.
- **Sufism legitimized Muslim rule and then mirrored it**: "Indeed, it came to represent and legitimize
  that rule, and in time Sufi practices and institutions came close to mirroring the social structural
  pattern of that rule" (p. 111, folio 91).
- **The ideological account of the performer's low status, and the author's correction of it**: "the
  performer of Sufi music occupies a totally insignificant position; this is so despite the obvious fact
  that he has a key function in the central ritual of Sufism. The ideological explanation of this
  apparent incongruity is that Sufism admits music into the samd assembly only as a medium for spiritual
  advancement, to be achieved strictly through listening, while making music per se is considered
  against Islamic tenets. But this ideological distinction between listening to music and performing it
  needs also to be seen in its socio-economic manifestation" (p. 116, folio 96).
- **The underlying class opposition**: "the more fundamental opposition between two general classes of
  society: those who control resources and those who produce either resources or services" (p. 116,
  folio 96).
- **The methodological claim for the whole book**: "the Qawwali assembly provides a prototype context for
  a formal 'acting out' of the structural and process-related features of Indian Sufism, since it is the
  one formal setting in which the entire Sufi community is represented" (p. 113, folio 93).
- **Two-level analysis announced**: "The analyis [text-layer?: analysis] will deal first with the
  Qawwali occasion at the level of its manifest religious function as realized in concept, setting and
  procedure. To complement this detailed outline, the entire structure will then be considered at the
  level of the latent social and economic function being served by it at the same time" (p. 125, folio
  105).
- **Knowledge of the ritual is unevenly distributed**: "For informed Sufis and scholars their knowledge
  of Qawwali structure takes the form of a fully developed scheme, while others, including most
  performers, have a more limited access to this knowledge" (p. 125, folio 105); and "The social and
  economic dimensions of the Qawwali ritual are of course not conceptualized formally, nor are they
  often made explicit even on inquiring, but Qawwali participants do acknowledge them, performers and
  devotees more than Sufi sheikhs" (p. 126, folio 106).
- **The version presented is Chishti**: "the particular version presented here most closely represents
  the teaching of the Chishti lineage, principal exponent of Qawwali in India" (p. 125, folio 105).
- **The two Sufi terms for the occasion** — the chunk's closing argument, cut off mid-sentence: "one is
  the mahfil-e-sama or 'gathering for listening', the other the darbdr-e-auliyd [text-layer?:
  darbār-e-auliyā] or 'royal court of saints'" (p. 126, folio 106).
