## Practices, institutions, economy

- **A shrine as a complete Qawwali economy.** "A major Sufi shrine is such a domain, for it is the
  locus of Qawwali performances of every kind, of a stable group of performers with a standard core
  repertoire and a predictable audience complex - in short, a local universe of Qawwali" (p. 33,
  folio 13).
- **Shrines as a linked network, not isolated sites.** "Both spiritual ties and actual encounters
  through pilgrimage and travel link shrines and urban centres across the enure [text-layer?:
  entire] subcontinent into one extended Sufi community" (p. 33, folio 13).
- **Repertoire follows the network.** "networks of related shrines share repertoires oriented to
  particular saintly lineages, repertoires which also reflect regional musical styles, especially
  where rural audiences abound. Within shrine networks, each shrine of some import has its own
  special songs" (p. 34, folio 14).
- **Songs that cannot travel.** "Not movable are musical settings of ritual songs or of songs with a
  special shrine association" (p. 39, folio 19) — a stated tie between repertoire and place.
- **Hereditary shrine performers.** The Qawwal Bachche are "die [text-layer?: the] shrine's
  hereditary performers" (p. 33, folio 13); the Qaul tune has "been carefully preserved and passed on
  in an unbroken succession of hereditary shrine performers" (p. 42, folio 22).
- **Ritual sequence at the shrine.** "This is the basic ritual song of Sufism in India; indeed one can
  call it the Opening - or Closing - Hymn of Qawwali. At Nizamuddin Auliya no Qawwali event can start
  any other way, while elsewhere in India and Pakistan the Qaul serves as a conclusion" (p. 41, folio
  21).
- **Patronage and the performers' economic condition.** "(Ultimately it all boiled down to the fact
  that traditional Qawwali performers are in dire need of patrons today.)" (p. 32, folio 12).
- **Teaching as a payment relation.** Learning happens "as a student (shdgird [text-layer?: shāgird])
  who is formally taught by a master (ustad [text-layer?: ustād]) in return for allegiance, made
  manifest through remuneration and personal service" (p. 32, folio 12); the author converted "the
  requirement for service into generous financial reward" and took lessons from more than one teacher
  (p. 32, folio 12).
- **Patron–client distortion of fieldwork.** "my position as a patron resulted at times in my 'client'
  not wishing to answer my question in the negative" (p. 32, folio 12).
- **Transmission is oral, not by lesson.** "Qawwals believe in learning by ear and use formal teaching
  mainly to help out their unmusical youngsters" (p. 32, folio 12).
- **Scale of the corpus.** "I recorded 433 performances of 261 songs with 179 different tunes"; the
  repertoire "is performed and transmitted by hundreds of Qawwals at different local Sufi centres (I
  recorded 83 performing groups at fifteen centres)"; and "I recorded over 20 versions of the two best
  known Qawwali songs" (p. 34, folio 14). **All these digits stand in body text and are treated as
  printed** (damage class 3).
- **Per-song recording counts.** "Twenty-one performances of the Qaul by the Qawwal Bachche were
  recorded - varying in duration from a few minutes to almost one hour" (p. 42, folio 22); of
  Chashm-e-maste, "five other performances… were recorded, four sung by Meraj Ahmad and one by a
  hereditary performer from outside, singing at Nizamuddin Auliya" (p. 45, folio 25).
- **Videotaping at the shrine was tolerated.** "I videotaped Qawwali performances singlehandedly,
  toward the end of my field research, by which time I had become a competent observer and part of
  the scene, and this intrusive procedure was generally accepted by all concerned" (p. 33, folio 13).
- **The author's position as a foreign woman.** "the anomaly of being a foreign female of obvious
  prosperity (evidenced by my having time and money for this research, modest outward appearance
  notwithstanding) made it quite acceptable to convert the requirement for service into generous
  financial reward" (p. 32, folio 12).
- **Instrumentation.** "the instrumental accompaniment on dholak and harmonium is not considered
  integral to the song" and is therefore left out of the transcriptions (p. 40, folio 20).
- **Architecture, in passing.** The assembly disperses over "the marble tomb now silent" (p. 37, folio
  17) — the only physical detail of any shrine in the chunk; the shrine is not named at that point.

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **Attributed to "all Sufis in India":** "it is Amir Khusrau who set this hadis to music, extending
  it with zikr-like phrases… in Farsi which today are only partly intelligible" (p. 42, folio 22).
  The book reports this as the community's attribution, not as its own finding.
- **Attributed to Sufi doctrine:** the Qaul "expresses a basic Sufi tenet, that the principle of
  spiritual succession in Sufism was instituted by the Prophet himself, as recorded in one of his
  sayings (hadis)" (p. 41, folio 21).
- **Attributed to the Qawwal Bachche:** the Qaul is set "to a version of raga shudh kalydn
  [text-layer?: shudh kalyān] which the Qawwal Bachche consider authentic, since the raga is likely
  to have changed over time while the Qawwali hymn tune has been carefully preserved" (p. 42, folio
  22).
- **Attributed as a claim of textual fidelity:** the transcribed Qaul "is identical for all Qawwal
  Bachche and recognized throughout India and Pakistan as the one that most authentically represents
  the original by Amir Khusrau" (p. 42, folio 22).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The threefold task.** "(a) to analyse the sound idiom of Qawwali as a self-contained rule system
  for generating music in performance; (b) to identify the context of performance… so that (c) the
  performance context can be related to the music in a way that will identify the contextual input
  into the musical sound" (p. 26, folio 6).
- **Two unresolved disciplinary issues** are named as blocking this: how to analyse a sound idiom "in
  terms appropriate to its properties and structure", and how to relate context to sound system from
  a perspective "compatible with both domains" (p. 26, folio 6).
- **Culture-specific analysis is required.** Practising ethnomusicologists "discovered what
  anthropologists (Sturtevant 1964) and linguists (Pike 1972) had postulated earlier: namely that
  music is based on culture-specific principles and must be analysed according to the culture's own
  criteria" (p. 26, folio 6).
- **Indic music theory is the chosen metalanguage.** "Indian musical theory, as codified from the
  fifth century onward, has its own, long-established, principles for describing and analysing
  musical sound" (p. 27, folio 7); the author adopts "this theoretical framework of Indie
  [text-layer?: Indic] musicology which I consider an appropriate tool for analysing Qawwali" (p. 28,
  folio 8).
- **But Qawwali is not art music.** "Qawwali must be analysed as a distinct musical idiom which cannot
  be subsumed within Indian art music" (p. 28, folio 8); the safeguard is "careful elicitation and
  verification procedures based on intensive musical study and participation" (p. 28, folio 8).
- **Elicitation is unusually easy here** because "Qawwali musicians are hereditary performers with a
  tradition of teaching and talking about their music in terms compatible with art music; Qawwali
  musical conceptions are thus available literally for the asking" (p. 28, folio 8).
- **Critique of context-based ethnomusicology.** Such studies show "inability to deal satisfactorily
  with musical sound" (p. 28, folio 8), and "an analysis of musical performance based on context
  alone leaves a gap in the analysis of musical sound" (p. 29, folio 9). The remedy is "a synthesis
  between the approaches inspired by musicology and anthropology" (p. 29, folio 9).
- **Qawwali as a 'cultural performance'.** "Qawwali in fact constitutes what Indie [text-layer?:
  Indic] anthropology identifies as a 'cultural performance' (Singer 1972: I48f. [text-layer?:
  148f.])… because it presents 'encapsulated expressions' of its forms and values" (p. 29, folio 9).
  The author notes it is "curiously" rare for this approach to be applied to Indian performance
  traditions (p. 29, folio 9).
- **Four stated assumptions** ground the model: analyse the observable; the conceptual domain is
  distinct from behaviour and "a norm is not a practice"; music too has conceptual and behavioural
  dimensions; and "Process means making structure operational" (pp. 30, folio 10). The fourth leads
  to the key methodological choice: "The key to understanding musical sound in its process of
  performance is to analyse it from the vantage point of the performer" (p. 30, folio 10).
- **Table 1, "Table i [text-layer?: Table 1] Analytical Model"** (p. 31, folio 11): background
  dimensions — "Ideological system", "Symbolic system", "Socio-economic setting", "Performer's
  identity", "Performer's vantage point (self-interest)" — set against "Context of performance"
  (OCCASION STRUCTURE → EVENT PROCESS) and "Medium of performance" (MUSIC STRUCTURE → SONG PROCESS),
  with structure "opera tionalized [text-layer?: operationalized] = process" and the event analysed as
  "Audience ←→ interaction ←→ Performer" (p. 31, folio 11).
- **The three-step plan maps onto the book's three Parts.** Part I = the musical idiom; Part II = the
  performance context, introduced by four background dimensions — "the Sufi ideology which provides
  the rationale and function of Qawwali, the symbolic system of mystical poetry used as Qawwali texts,
  the socio-economic setting of Sufism within which Qawwali is practised, and the social and
  professional identity of the performer"; Part III = the performance process (pp. 31–32, folios
  11–12).
- **The stated hypothesis and end goal.** The aim is "a context-sensitive grammar of Qawwali music",
  which "should account for the total process of musical production in Qawwali and make possible the
  testing of the initial hypothesis: Qawwali sound will vary with variation in the context of Qawwali
  performance" (p. 32, folio 12).
- **A named elicitation trap.** The author distinguishes "'yes, this is done' - yeh hold hai
  [text-layer?: yeh hotā hai] -, meaning standard practice, versus 'yes, this is done as well' - yeh
  bhi hold hai - meaning that it is not done but conceivably could be, since I had raised the
  question" (p. 32, folio 12).
- **Verification by feedback interview.** Conclusions were checked with participants against video,
  "through what Stone and Stone (1981) term the feedback interview", and tested "against data not
  included in the analysis" (p. 33, folio 13).
- **Why a single shrine and a single performer.** The single-shrine choice is defended by Nizamuddin
  Auliya being "a centre of Qawwali" and the seat of Khusrau's "personal legacy" (p. 33, folio 13);
  the single-performer choice, by the claim that "the performance dimension of this music… can only be
  understood through the concrete music-making of the individual performer" (p. 34, folio 14).
- **Repertoire typology.** Any one Qawwal Bachche performer knows four song types: "(a) Songs
  specifically associated with Nizamuddin Auliya, including ritual songs and Amir Khusrau
  compositions"; "(b) Sufi classics known to Sufis and Qawwals all over India"; "(c) Songs that form
  part of the performer's personal repertoire"; "(d) Songs with popular success, added to the
  repertoire to keep up with the trend of the day" (pp. 39–40, folios 19–20). Meraj Ahmad "is
  extremely well-versed in types (a) and (b)… He excels in type (c)… but has kept type (d) to a
  minimum" (p. 40, folio 20).
- **Tune categories in the performers' own words.** "'old' tunes (purdni dhuneh, purdni bandisheh
  [text-layer?: purānī dhuneh, purānī bandisheh])"; "'special' (makhsus, kkds [text-layer?: makhsūs,
  khās]) settings"; "'typical Qawwal tunes' {Qawwali ki thet dhuneh}"; "tunes for common use ('dm
  dhuneh [text-layer?: ʿām dhuneh])"; and "'tunes of nowadays' (djkal ki dhuneh [text-layer?: ājkal kī
  dhuneh])" (p. 39, folio 19).
- **New tunes are taken, not composed.** Nizamuddin Auliya Qawwals are "always on the lookout for new
  tunes, 'picking them up' (urdnd - to snatch [text-layer?: uṛānā]) from listening to performances,
  or, more rarely, making them up" (p. 39, folio 19); recordings have "helped generate a new musical
  repertoire" (p. 39, folio 19).
- **Transcription policy is normative, not acoustic.** "The aim is to represent Qawwali as a normative
  musical structure rather than an acoustic event (the cassette provides that dimension)" (p. 40,
  folio 20).
- **Why the Qaul takes so much repetition.** "the entire textual meaning is contained in the first two
  lines, so that the remainder is rarely repeated more than cursorily"; alternative tune versions
  "enable him to raise the intensity level by raising the pitch level of this low-register asthdyl
  [text-layer?: asthāyī] tune"; and "Amplifying insertions (girahs) are much used… every performer at
  Nizamuddin Auliya has in his memory a stock of appropriate girahs" (p. 42, folio 22).
- **A regional split in improvisation practice.** "Nizamuddin performers prefer melodic improvisation,
  while performers elsewhere also use rhythmic improvisation to the Farsi syllables" (p. 42, folio
  22).
- **Metre creates room for takrār.** In Farsi ghazal settings the musical realization "renders the
  final long syllables of every rhythmic phrase into extended durational values. This extra duration
  permits the lead performer to insert word calls or even a fast repetition of the preceding text
  phrase… particularly suited to varied takrdr [text-layer?: takrār] repetition" (p. 44, folio 24).
- **Musical metre named.** Example 1 is "set to 8/8 kaharvd [text-layer?: kahārvā]" (p. 41, folio 21);
  example 2 uses "verse metre rami [text-layer?: reading uncertain — see Doubts] and musical metre 8/8
  (qawwali kd thekd [text-layer?: qawwālī kā thekā])" (p. 44, folio 24).
