## Practices, institutions, economy

Nothing in this chunk concerns ʿurs, mela, langar, offerings, custodianship, endowment, land, revenue,
visitor numbers, disputes, state involvement or architecture. What it does document in detail is the
craft and the working constraints of the Qawwal — what the performers do, what they must do, and what
they call it. Recorded here because the consolidator will want it under practice.

**The ensemble and its instruments.**
- The texture has three components: a melodic line "vocalized by one or more singers"; the musical
  metre "articulated on the drum"; and the pitch outline "constantly reinforced on the portable
  harmonium" (p. 78, folio 58).
- The drum is "traditionally and normally the double-barrelled dholak, though the tabla, standard drum
  of classical music, is used by some Qawwals today" (p. 78, folio 58).
- The harmonium "also serves for playing the Instrumental Prelude (naghmd) [text-layer?: naghma]"; it
  is "A relatively recent instrument in the Indian vocal ensemble" yet "completely established in the
  Qawwali tradition, replacing less satisfactory predecessors like the sitar" (p. 78, folio 58;
  footnote 17 is attached here).
- "The entire ensemble is controlled by the lead singer who generally plays the harmonium himself"
  (p. 78, folio 58).

**Drumming and clapping technique, and why it is chosen.**
- Two accentual techniques emphasize stressed beats: "One is handclapping, the other a drumming
  technique that uses mainly open-hand or flat-hand strokes (thdp, thapiyd) [text-layer?: thāp,
  thapiyā]" (p. 80, folio 60).
- The technique "is compatible with the dholak, the standard Qawwali drum (see Stewart 1974: 31 ff.)
  and even when the tabla is used, Qawwali performers play it with flat hand strokes {thdp se)
  [text-layer?: thāp se], for the tabla's standard fingered technique (chutki se) is considered to
  have no effect on listeners" (p. 80, folio 60).
- Rhythm is also articulated "percussively by drumming and clapping" as a dimension separate from
  melody (p. 73, folio 53; footnote 16 is attached to "drumming").

**Vocal practice and the priority of the word.**
- "Qawwals themselves usually refer to their singing as speaking (bolnd) [text-layer?: bolnā], and
  their major concern is to make the words understood (bol samjhdnd) [bol samjhānā]" (p. 81, folio 61).
- "all singing is carried out at a high dynamic level and with strong, even exaggerated enunciation of
  consonants" (p. 81, folio 61).
- The ideal Qawwal voice "is considered to be loud or full (bhari hul, moti) [text-layer?: bharī huī,
  moṭī], a voice with life (jdn) [jān] and strength (zor), rather than one that is very melodious
  (surild) [surīlā] or modulated (kids ki)" — the last term is damaged past reading; see Doubts (p. 81,
  folio 61).
- "For additional volume, the solo voice is reinforced by group singing", and clarity and volume come
  together "through the responsorial ensemble structure in which salient statements of any text unit
  are made by the soloist and repeated by the group" (p. 81, folio 61).
- "group alternation permits continuous singing, without instrumental interludes, thus preserving what
  is of primary importance in Qawwali: an uninterrupted verbal communication" (p. 81, folio 61).

**Ritual effect: zikr, raqs and the heartbeat.**
- A strong rhythmic framework and "an emphatic stress pattern or pulse (zarb) - often compared with the
  heartbeat (also called zarb) by Qawwals and Sufis alike - are considered essential for the soul to
  become moved (qalb jdri hojdnd) [text-layer?: qalb jārī ho jānā]" (p. 80, folio 60).
- "the recurring beat is to suggest the continuous repetition of God's name (zikr), and to guide the
  Sufi's movement in ecstatic dancing (raqs)" (p. 80, folio 60).
- "Qawwals agree that with the downbeat of the drum the listener's head moves in silent repetition of
  God's name (zikr), and the listener's foot moves in the dance of ecstasy (raqs). Even the recurring
  drum beat alone may cause ecstasy" (p. 80, folio 60).
- The Instrumental Prelude "is based on the reiteration of a zikr rhythm" (p. 80, folio 60), and its
  underlying text formula is given in Table 13 as "'Allahu' repeated (implied only)" (p. 89, folio 69).
  Ex. 11's surviving caption glosses the rhythm: it "implies Alldhu [Allāhu], that is, 'God Is'"
  (p. 80, folio 60).
- Tempo is used ritually: "gradually increasing speed of delivery… in Qawwali it specifically serves
  the intensification of spiritual emotion and is used selectively for this purpose. Conversely, a
  decrease in speed implies the relaxation of intense emotion, especially at the very end of a song"
  (p. 80, folio 60).

**Serving the assembly: the performer's obligation to the listeners.**
- "The Qawwali performer must be able to repeat, amplify, rearrange or even omit any part of the song
  text in immediate response to the changing requirements of his listeners" (p. 83, folio 63).
- This "presupposes total flexibility of the Qawwali musical structure, both as to the structural units
  and as to their manipulation" (p. 83, folio 63); sections are "further divisible into shorter musical
  units in order to accommodate even the shortest meaningful text unit that may require to be isolated
  and repeated" (p. 83, folio 63).
- Repetition rule of thumb in performance: "a message must be heard at least twice in order to be
  internalized, and it needs to be presented in segments corresponding in duration to one
  message-phrase"; accordingly "the standard unit of repetition is the line set to one tune section"
  (p. 90, folio 70).
- Line length modifies the rule: for "a very short verse line two complementary lines are sung in
  succession and then repeated"; "A very long verse line, conversely, is usually divisible in half by a
  caesura, and each half line is repeated separately at first" (p. 90, folio 70).
- A saint's name is used as a performance signal: "a non-musical method of signalling a text phrase out
  of sequence consists of prefacing it with a short appellation of a saint's name, set to a raised pitch
  also anticipating repetition" (p. 90, folio 70), tabulated as "word call (saint, appellation), added
  to text line where possible, signals alert, suggesting recurrence or repeat" (p. 90, folio 70).

**Repertoire units the performers add to a song.**
- "Two performance units may be added to the song. One is an Introduction (ruba'i) preceding the song
  to indicate the type of poem that is to follow (Repertoire No. 8). The other is an Insert (girak)
  [text-layer?: girah] introduced during the course of a song to amplify the meaning of a verse line
  (see Repertoire Nos. 9, 10)" (p. 88, folio 68).
- "Both are verse units ranging from one to four couplets. Musically, they are rendered as 'recitatives'
  in an unmetred declamatory vocal style, without motivic patterning or reference to a musical metre"
  (p. 88, folio 68) — and in the recitative the drum "may play softly" (Table 11, p. 88, folio 68).
- "Finally, an Instrumental Prelude (naghrna) [text-layer?: naghma], played solo on the harmonium, may
  preface the sung performance (Repertoire No. 11)" (p. 88, folio 68).
- Performers' names for these: "Inserted Verses, called 'knots' (girah), Introductory Verses, called
  'quatrains' (ruba'i) or - for Hindi verses - 'couplets' (dohd) [dohā], verse lines (mism) [misra'],
  and short text phrases called 'something said' (bol)" (p. 85, folio 65).

**The performers' own vocabulary of manipulation.** "'to repeat' (dohrdnd) [dohrānā], 'intensive
repetition' (takrdr) [takrār], 'to amplify' (barhdnd) [baṛhānā], and 'to leave out' (chhoma)
[chhoṛnā] - all these terms being applicable primarily to text units" (p. 85, folio 65). For the
melodic joining of units the performers "really have no terminology beyond a general expression: 'to
make the connection, to harmonize parts' (mel kamd) [mel karnā]" (p. 85, folio 65). Qawwals also name
the poem's rhythmic pattern (wazan), the poetic metre (zamln) [zamīn], the tune (dhun, printed dkun,
bandish, tarz) and the rhythmic framework (tkekd) [thekā] (p. 81, folio 61), and call a tune that
carries a given poetic metre a "'pattern tune' (pattern dhun)" (p. 81, folio 61).

**A practice the book marks as non-standard.** Visual presentation — "occasional gestures by the lead
singer - mainly raising the hand to point or wave to a salient text phrase (see Plate 13)" — is
"marginal to the Qawwali idiom in every sense"; "Because its consistent execution is seen to interfere
with the spiritual purpose of Qawwali… its practice is not standard; rather it characterizes the
popular, filmi qawwali (see Table 2)" (p. 83, folio 63). Table 6 flags the same row "* = controversial,
non-standard feature" (p. 79, folio 59).


## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

**The governing claim: Qawwali's musical distinctiveness is generated by its religious function.**
- "Qawwali music has special features which render it distinct from all other musical idioms of the
  region. These features are directly linked to the basic raison d'etre of Qawwali music: its religious
  function and context of use" (p. 78, folio 58).
- The method follows from it: "To isolate and understand the logic behind these features requires
  bringing into the music analysis the functional or contextual constraints that generate them at
  different levels" (p. 78, folio 58).
- And the warrant is the performers' own testimony: "The basis for such a claim is that Qawwali
  participants themselves identify the musical features of Qawwali in terms of their association with
  its function; indeed, it is this very association which makes such features distinctive to them"
  (p. 78, folio 58).

**The function, defined in three components.** "The function of Qawwali music in accordance with its
place in the ideology of Sufism is to serve the presentation of mystical poetry in order to arouse
mystical emotion in an assembly of listeners with spiritual needs that are both diverse and changing"
(p. 79, folio 59). Its "Three basic components": "( I ) [text-layer?: (1)] arousing (2) through texts
(3) diverse listeners" (p. 79, folio 59), restated as numbered propositions: "i. [text-layer?: 1.]
Qawwali serves to generate spiritual arousal. 2. Qawwali serves to convey a text message of mystical
poetry. 3. Qawwali serves to satisfy listeners' diverse and changing spiritual requirements" (p. 80,
folio 60). Table 6 maps each component onto its "Musical execution: distinctive features" (p. 79,
folio 59).

**Argument 1 — arousal dictates the metre.** Because the framework must be strongly pulsed, "Qawwali
music favours a durational framework consisting of metres with simple and regular durational patterns,
clearly articulated by vigorous and recurring stresses. This means that nearly all Qawwali metres are
short and composed of two simple groupings, of equal or near-equal duration (for example, the 3 + 3
dddrd [dādrā] or the 4 + 4 kaharva)" (p. 80, folio 60). And "all rhythmic groupings begin with a
stressed beat, even in the event where more complicated metres are used which formally exempt some
groupings from stress (for example 3 + 4 rupak, 4 + 4 + 4 + 4 tintal or 2 + 3 + 2 + 3 jhaptdl
[jhaptāl])" (p. 80, folio 60).

**Argument 2 — the text governs the music, more deeply than melody or rhythm.**
- "Qawwali formal structure is dependent on textual structure in a more fundamental way than are either
  melody or rhythm, since its very framework, the Qawwali song, is defined by the textual unit of
  performance, the poem" (p. 76, folio 56).
- Poetic metre drives musical rhythm: "the long-short arrangement of the poetic metre… forms the basis
  for the long-short arrangement of Qawwali melody, whether in a literal 2: 1 proportion (one long = two
  short), or in various asymmetrical arrangements" (p. 81, folio 61); and "the musical metre represents,
  or at least fits the structure of, the poetic metre" (p. 82, folio 62).
- Rhyme drives sectioning: "Rhyming lines, which are normally in a concluding or refrain position, are
  set to the principal section of the tune, the lower-pitched asthdyi [asthāyī], which musically suggests
  stability and conclusiveness"; non-rhyming lines go to "the higher-pitched antard [antarā] which
  musically suggests an excursion into new territory" (p. 82, folio 62). "the final line of a verse is
  always an asthdyi, and the semantically most significant preceding line - initial or penultimate - is
  always an antard" (p. 82, folio 62), so the contrast "serves to underscore the complementarity between
  rhyming and non-rhyming lines, or 'statement' and 'answer'" (p. 82, folio 62).
- Poetic form even constrains pitch, "albeit in an indirect way, by means of their influence on musical
  form and duration"; a caesura in the verse line "is often expressed melodically by complementary
  contouring", and parallelism "serves to mark the points of structure between verse lines through what
  amounts to a musical end rhyme or 'cadence'" (p. 82, folio 62; footnote 18 attached).
- The evidence Qureshi rests this on is lexical: "Performers express the structural dominance of the poem
  by their very vocabulary, identifying formal structure and elements of Qawwali music by poetic rather
  than musical terms, even where standard musical terms exist and are known to them" — tune section is
  called 'verse line' (misra'), the refrain 'salient word phrase' (bol), and the two sections 'first line'
  (misra 'ula) and 'second line' (sani misra), with singing them called "'saying [it] as first' (ula
  bolnd) and 'saying [it] as second' (sani bolnd)" (p. 83, folio 63).

**Argument 3 — directional pitch movement is the syntax, and it is an original identification.** The
three principles of formal construction are repetition ("simple reiteration (dohrdnd), multiple
repetition (takrdr) and recurrence (no term)"), registral contrast, and "the connection of units by
means of directional pitch movement" (p. 77, folio 57). Qureshi then claims priority for the third:
"All three are standard structuring principles of North Indian music, but unlike the first two, the
principle of directional pitch movement is not generally identified in writings on Indian music. Nor
indeed do Qawwali performers themselves abstract it verbally, even though it is clearly a prominent
feature of formal structuring in Qawwali music" (p. 77, folio 57). Its work is semantic as well as
connective: it operates "'allomorphically' to indicate what type of text unit is to follow" (p. 84,
folio 64), chiefly through alternative endings, which "function much like the first and second endings
in Western musical form, but there is no implication of conclusion for either ending" (p. 84, folio 64).
The conclusion: "In short, directional movement is the prime syntactical principle of the Qawwali idiom"
(p. 85, folio 65).

**Argument 4 — pitch is subordinated to duration and form.** The chunk opens on this: melodic structuring
"is minimal in a great many Qawwali tunes, because very strong durational and formal patterning dominates
Qawwali music"; declamatory passages may be inserted "where melodic structuring is limited to orientation
around tonal centres and motivic patterning is largely absent, due to other structuring priorities"
(p. 73, folio 53).

**Argument 5 — regional style is a rhythmic, not a melodic, variable.** The two principal regions "can be
distinguished mainly by their rhythmic setting or 'gait' {chal)", Uttar Pradesh quantitative, Panjab
accentual, with a matching difference in the drum setting (theke ki bandish) "corresponding with the
respective drumming traditions of each region" (p. 73, folio 53).

**Argument 6 — the analysis yields a generative grammar, but the blueprint can only be abstract.** "The
analysis of Qawwali music is now complete… The result is a musical grammar that can account for the
structure of Qawwali." Yet "For Qawwali music such a blueprint exists only in the abstract, since in
performance Qawwali song is, by definition, structured to serve audience needs, as manifested musically
in its features of structural flexibility"; what exists instead is "a 'roadmap' (to borrow a popular
musicians' term)… based on the sequential structure of the song text" (p. 85, folio 65).

**Argument 7 — a caution against over-reading performers' terminology.** Performers "recognize and can
identify the procedure of connecting parts appropriately, but they take it for granted as an integral
part of Qawwali singing - a potent reminder that musical reality extends beyond verbal categories"
(p. 85, folio 65).

**The analytical apparatus, for the consolidator's reference.** Duration is founded on "the concept of
rhythm (lai)", realized in "musical metres (thekd) [thekā] which are composed of a set number of pulses
(mdtrd) [mātrā] organized additively into groupings with an equal or unequal number of pulses"; "Nearly
all Qawwali metres consist of two such groupings, each of which is marked with an initial stress", the
commonest being 4+4 (kaharvd, qawwali kd thekd), 3+3 (dddrd) and 3+4 (pashto, rupak) (p. 73, folio 53).
Table 4 sets these out as 8/8 kaharvā/qawwālī, 6/8 dadra and 7/8 pashto/rupak, with the infrequent metres
10/8 jhaptāl, 14/8 chachar, 16/8 tīntāl and 12/8 ektāl (p. 74, folio 54; the digits in that list are
damaged, see Doubts). Pitch duration has primary units "'short' = one pulse long" and "'long' = two
pulses long", subsidiary "'extended long'" and "'divided short'", plus a "pause of one or more pulses
duration (waqfd) [waqfa]" (p. 74, folio 54). Form runs Item of performance (chiz) > tune (dhun, tarz) >
sections asthāyī (also mukhrā) and antarā with their extensions, on "basic formal scheme"
"||:A(+Aᵉ) (asthdyi) :B(+Bᵉ) (antard): || :A(+Aᵉ) asthdyi :" (p. 75, folio 55), described in prose as "a
loose A-B-A frame" (p. 78, folio 58). Tables 7–10 then nest poem/tune, verse/tune, line/section and
phrase/motive (pp. 86–87, folios 66–67), Tables 11–13 do the same for the adjunct items and the naghma
(pp. 88–89, folios 68–69), and Table 14 gives the sequencing principles (p. 90, folio 70).
