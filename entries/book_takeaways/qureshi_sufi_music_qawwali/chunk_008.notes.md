## Practices, institutions, economy

**The offering transaction, step by step.** This is the chunk's densest material; each step is separately
page-referenced.

- **Step 1 — the devotee bows and offers.** Plate 14: "A devotee bows to make an offering during the 'urs
  of Abdus Salam in Delhi" (p. 144, folio 124). The gesture is read as "formal social interaction with
  superiors which is based to some extent on Islamic precedents, but above all on the Indo-Islamic
  imperial court tradition"; its implication is "principally that of deference, which is indicated by
  bowing as well as by the presentation of a gift that serves as a material token of submission, while
  also implying a request for beneficence" (p. 145, folio 125).
- **Step 1a — the roles the gesture fixes.** "the offerer puts himself in the position of supplicant, a
  'taker', whereas the spiritual superior, while the recipient of a gift, is nevertheless himself the
  benefactor or 'giver', as indicated by the gesture with which he accepts the offering from above"
  (p. 145, folio 125).
- **Step 2 — the leader accepts.** Plate 15: "Ahmad Mian, the assembly leader and `sajjddanashin`
  [text-layer?: sajjādanashīn] of the dargah of Abdus Salam, accepts an offering" (p. 144, folio 124).
  "The recipient of the offering accepts it on behalf of the spiritual power he represents" (p. 145,
  folio 125).
- **Step 2a — prostration after offering.** Plate 16: "A disciple prostrating himself before his sheikh,
  Ahmad Mian of the dargah of Abdus Salam, after making an offering" (p. 144, folio 124).
- **Step 3 — upward re-presentation through the hierarchy.** "unless he occupies the highest spiritual
  status in the assembly, he too is required to present the offering to a spiritual superior, most
  likely the leader; thus he articulates his own place in the spiritual hierarchy and thereby validates
  the structural universe of the royal court of saints" (p. 145, folio 125).
- **Step 3a — the loop when the leader himself offers.** "Very rarely, even a leader may wish to express
  his personal devotion or submission to the divine power whose blessing he enjoys. This he may do by
  making an offering of his own to any other saint's representative, who will then be obliged to present
  it back to him as the spiritually highest person present (see Performance No. 1)" (p. 145, folio 125).
- **Step 4 — acceptance converts the gift into money.** "Once accepted by him, however, their purpose of
  articulating a spiritual relationship is completed and they now become money to be given away, in
  keeping with the Islamic tradition of turning religious tributes into charity. In this particular
  situation the money serves to remunerate the performers for their service" (p. 146, folio 126).
- **Step 4a — the justification cited for paying the performer.** "the recipient of a precious gift
  brought to him by the donor's servant must reward the servant for bringing it, even though this reward
  has no relationship to the gift itself (Idris Khan 1973:5)" (p. 146, folio 126).
- **Step 5 — the pick-up.** "Either at the end of their performance, or more often as every offering is
  accepted by the leader, one of the Qawwals — or an assistant of the leader — goes to receive the money"
  (pp. 146–147, folios 126–127). Plate 17: "The assembly leader turns over the offering money to the
  performers" (p. 146, folio 126).
- **Step 6 — the reverse gesture.** "The standard gesture of turning over the money to the performer is
  the reverse of the offering: the leader drops the money into the extended palm of the performer, thus
  always maintaining the formal distinction between the giver and the taker (see Plate 17)" (p. 147,
  folio 127).
- **Step 7 — the carry-back.** "The taker raises the money to his eyes in a gesture of partaking of the
  blessing conferred on it, and returns, ideally by walking backwards, to place the money on the
  harmonium or next to the lead singer (see Plate 18)" (p. 147, folio 127). Plate 18: "The Qawwal's
  pick-up man deposits the offering money on the harmonium while the singing continues" (p. 147, folio
  127).
- **Step 8 — what the leader has done.** "The leader, then, plays an essential part in managing the
  transformation of the offering from a token of spiritual deference into a payment for service"
  (p. 147, folio 127). Plate 19 photographs the same conversion: "A rupee note about to be transformed
  from offering to pay, as it passes from the devotee to the performer via the sheikh" (p. 158, folio 138).
- **The circuit as the book diagrams it.** "Table 27 Performer's Earning Process" sets out four stations
  (p. 158, folio 138): "Performer / sings Qawwali songs according to needs of listeners" → "Songs" →
  "Listeners / make offerings to activate spiritual link" → "money offering" → "Leader/Head of Sufi
  hierarchy / accepts offerings and authorizes conversion into pay for performers" → "money (pay)" →
  back to the performer.

**Who controls the money.**

- "the leader exercises de facto control over the offering transaction; after all, the performer gets
  his reward only by the leader's leave" (pp. 150–151, folios 130–131).
- Historical change stated: "In past times, assembly leaders exercised total control over the economic
  dimension by retaining the offering and rewarding the performers later at their discretion — an
  expression of a more solid feudal tie between them. But even today, the leader retains his economic
  position of power as a redistributive agent" (p. 150, folio 130). **No period, reign or date is given
  for "in past times" (not stated).**
- The donor's freedom: "the spiritual meaning of the offering makes it equally legitimate for him to
  offer much or nothing" (p. 150, folio 130).
- "The leader thus has the additional function of rewarding the performers" (p. 146, folio 126).

**Offering as money, patronage and status.**

- Access is means-tested in fact if not in rule: "an offering is itself the means for linking up with a
  spiritual superior in the assembly. This means, however, is available only to those who have money.
  While Sufis do not consider this to be an actual rule, it is nevertheless a general fact" — superseded
  only in ecstasy, when "even a penniless devotee may 'meet the sheikh'" and "the lowliest ecstatic
  person, if he is so moved, may even embrace or kiss the highest spiritual personage present" (p. 145,
  folio 125).
- "its built-in social ingredient is the public giving away of money, a worldly asset… the social
  gesture par excellence for expressing high status and a position of patronage" (p. 149, folio 129).
- The norm and its sanction: "it is the accepted social norm that listeners of high worldly status
  should be seen offering generously and in accord with their status, while failing to offer is
  considered to result in a loss of face" (p. 149, folio 129).
- Two social requirements on "special" listeners: "the obligation ('noblesse oblige') for them, as
  patrons, to share in the support of those who provide them with service, that is the singers", and
  "the formal requirement for guests at the royal court — be it of king or saint — to present themselves
  at the beginning to the presiding personage, expressing their deference by a token offering
  {`nazrdna` [text-layer?: nazrāna] in its secular meaning)" (p. 149, folio 129).
- Timing of that second requirement: "during the first song there is often a rush of offerings to the
  leader (see Performance No. 1)" (p. 149, folio 129); and structurally, "The only point in the assembly
  where offerings are certain to be made is during the obligatory hymns, especially where they introduce
  the performance" (p. 145, folio 125).
- Cost as a barrier to attendance: "For devotees with social status, limited financial ability can
  actually operate to limit their attendance at Qawwali assemblies" (p. 149, folio 129).
- Low-status offering as a bid for patronage: "their limited offerings also serve the highlighting of
  their social as well as their spiritual dependence"; and "Performers in particular, while they are not
  singing, often make use of this means of being 'recognized' by the assembly leader or another
  spiritual person" (pp. 149–150, folios 129–130).
- Older, non-monetary form of the offering: "today's money offerings also symbolize what in the past is
  said to have been a spontaneous giving away of anything a Sufi had access to when overcome by mystical
  emotion, including the very clothes on his body" (p. 145, folio 125).
- The sacrifice formula quoted for it: "`muhabbat kl qurbdnl men jan-o-mal dend` [text-layer?: muhabbat
  kī qurbānī men jān-o-māl denā], literally: 'to give life and possessions in the sacrifice of love'"
  (p. 145, folio 125).

**The performer's economic position.**

- "a performer sings for money and will operate not only on the spiritual but also on the material plane
  of making a living, so that spiritual requirements may be superseded by material needs" (p. 150, folio
  130).
- The Sufi view of that, quoted: "'that's the Qawwal's nature, pulling money (`paise luraknd`
  [text-layer?: paise luraknā]) out of people'" — "but they consider control essential to maintain the
  spiritual priority" (p. 150, folio 130).
- Three aspects said to set Qawwali apart from other performance occasions (p. 150, folio 130):
  (1) "the performer depends entirely on the whim of his listeners as to whether he will be rewarded and
  with how much", the inconsistency being extreme "from the fact that the donation is motivated by
  spiritual emotion, rather than the donor's desire to reward the performer"; (2) "the money is offered
  purely as spiritual — and social — currency, so to speak, and in a sense becomes a thing of material
  value only once the non-material transaction is completed"; (3) "the performer's success in creating
  favourable spiritual conditions hinges primarily on his conforming to standards and requirements of
  those with spiritual authority, not just on his appealing to wealthy listeners directly".
- The windfall is attributed to blessing, not generosity: "an economic windfall for the performers — or
  its absence — is seen in terms not of human generosity but of `karam` or divine blessing" (p. 150,
  folio 130); "he can only explain an economic windfall (or its absence) in terms of 'divine blessing'
  {`karam`)" (p. 150, folio 130).
- Indirect remuneration: "his remuneration does not come to him as a direct reward for a good
  performance. Rather, it is the indirect material result of what is essentially a non-material
  transaction: the symbolic expression of the spiritual link between a Sufi and his spiritual superior —
  a transaction not involving the performer at all" (p. 158, folio 138).
- The performer's two factors: "the audience composition with its offering potential, and the authority
  structure that controls his access to these offerings (the leader)" (p. 159, folio 139); he "considers
  himself the natural beneficiary of these offerings" (p. 159, folio 139).
- Sanction for misreading the hierarchy: "should the performer disregard this authority structure by
  catering to a rich devotee over the need of a materially less rewarding spiritual notable, there is
  potential for conflict and censure (see for example Performance No. 2)" (p. 159, folio 139).
- The Qawwal's own formula: "'I am a complete Qawwal … looking equally to spiritual benefice and to
  money.'" (p. 159, folio 139).

**Patronage, access and hereditary right.**

- "the performer stands in the position of a client dependent on patronage, whether it is a permanent
  personal patron-client tie with a saint's representative of his home shrine (as between Meraj and
  Khwaja Hasan Sani…), or the temporary patronage sought or obtained from the leader of a particular
  Qawwali assembly (as between Meraj and Khalil Mian…)" (pp. 156–157, folios 136–137).
- Hereditary shrine right, and its limit: "the hereditary right performers attached to a shrine have to
  ritual performance occasions — as Meraj has at Nizamuddin Auliya — but since these rights are held
  communally by the performing lineage (`brddri` [text-layer?: birādarī]), their benefit for the
  individual performer is limited" (p. 157, folio 137).
- The leader's control over access and process: "Even where the performer has a hereditary right of
  access, the leader can have him time and structure the performance according to his wishes. And where
  he controls the performer's very access — as he does in almost all situations — everything the
  performer does, including his very act of performing, can be liable to the leader's guidance,
  correction or censure" (p. 157, folio 137).
- Competition: "in most assemblies he is only one of several performers, any of whom may easily replace
  him. This pits him against his colleagues — or lineage brothers — in direct competition" (p. 157, folio
  137).
- Status of the performer: "identified with a low social class without assets, 'a despised lot'", with
  the exception of "a `nawdb`'s [text-layer?: nawāb's] young son" performing at "the 'urs of Kakori in
  1976" (p. 157, folio 137); and Qawwals who claim inherited or assigned spiritual status have those
  claims "quite categorically denied by Sufis" (p. 157, folio 137).

**Conduct of the assembly (the leader's management).**

- "it is the function of the leader to control and mediate their often highly individualized and
  unpredictable occurrence", covering both emotional expression and structure (p. 145, folio 125).
- "Emotional expression is monitored indirectly by a calm and composed attitude on the part of the
  leader… he serves as a spiritual anchor for the feelings of everyone else." Overt acts: "calming a
  devotee by placing a hand upon his back or head, during an offering encounter, or even by reciprocating
  an embrace" (p. 146, folio 126).
- Standing and sitting: "it is the leader who gives recognition to the ecstatic state by rising, out of
  respect for the divine blessing it represents, whereupon everyone in the audience does likewise…
  when he perceives the state subsiding, he chooses the right moment to sit down, and thus to have the
  entire gathering return to a sitting position" (p. 146, folio 126).
- `takrdr` [text-layer?: takrār] — the leader "ensures that the performer provides appropriate `takrdr`
  repetitions" (p. 146, folio 126); defined as repeating "incessantly and briskly the particular song
  segment that inspired the state… the one characteristic of Qawwali music understood and expected by
  every participant in a Qawwali occasion" (p. 147, folio 127).
- Policing a long ecstasy by a low-status listener: "the leader may well signal a supporter to have him
  pulled away gently, sometimes even before he can take the liberty" — the reason given is "after all,
  he is keeping higher-status listeners standing" (p. 149, folio 129).
- Who dances: "it is elderly devotees of lower status who are most likely to be seen dancing ecstatically,
  whereas high-status listeners, including saintly representatives with high social and religious
  standing, very rarely 'stand up' (`khqre hogaye` [text-layer?: khaṛe hogaye]) to go into ecstatic
  movement" (p. 149, folio 129).
- Seating and rank: "listeners with a high status are invariably seated and treated with deference,
  while low-status or service class listeners are relegated to insignificance, as long as they do not
  exhibit outstanding spiritual achievement" (p. 148, folio 128).
- The ensemble: the lead singer holds "the onus for handling the entire performance"; his "'companions'
  (`sdthi` [text-layer?: sāthī]) share the same basic musical and contextual knowledge, but he alone is
  required to opera tionalize it in terms of strategy, and his authority is paramount. Within a party, I
  have never heard anyone argue with his leader, though leaders do so in the context of a communal
  performance" (p. 160, folio 140).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **A saint dies and revives on alternate lines of a couplet.** Attributed by the book as "The account of
  the saint Qutabuddin Bakhtiyar-e-Kaki" (p. 147, folio 127), introduced by "Frequently cited examples of
  mystics who died in ecstasy during a Qawwali assembly include great saints of the past, but also
  documented cases of present-day Sufis", with note 27 attached (p. 147, folio 127). The couplet is "by
  the Persian mystic Ahmad Jam": "`Kushtagan-e-khanjar-e-taslim ra` / `Har zaman az khaib`
  [text-layer?: ghaib, per the book's own facing rendering "The Unseen"] `jan-e-dlgar` [text-layer?:
  jān-e-dīgar] `ast`", glossed by the book as "For the martyrs of the dagger of submission / The Unseen
  brings a new life every moment" (p. 148, folio 128). The account: "As the Qawwals repeated every first
  line, the saint fell down dead, but on every second line, he rose to life again. This alternation
  continued for many hours to several days, but the singing could not stop, until finally the Sufis
  present had the performers end on the first line to allow the saint to rest in final union with his
  Beloved" (p. 148, folio 128).
- **The belief that grounds the practice of `takrdr`:** "Sufis believe that this repetition is essential
  to sustain the ecstatic state and bring it to completion; for if the salient song portion is
  discontinued prematurely, the ecstatic person may die" (p. 147, folio 127).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The offering has two simultaneous meanings, and the material one is kept implicit.** The chunk's
  governing claim about the economy: "that dimension is recognized, and accepted by implication, but kept
  in check by allowing only an exclusively spiritual conception to operate at the explicit level"
  (p. 150, folio 130). Correspondingly the performer "can pursue this material goal only by implication,
  so to speak, and his financial success ultimately derives from 'scoring' at the spiritual level"
  (p. 159, folio 139), and "earning money from the audience, important as it may be for the performer,
  has to remain an implicit goal, to be achieved only in conjunction with, or through, the explicit goal
  of evoking spiritual benefices for this audience" (p. 158, folio 138).
- **There is no fixed sequence of audience responses.** "No sequencing is implied in the offering
  response per se" (p. 145, folio 125); "In the absence of a sequential structure of audience responses,
  it is the function of the leader to control and mediate" (p. 145, folio 125).
- **Two response modes, ranked.** "the formal offering is clearly the more complex in its implication,
  for it involves interaction between all categories of participants, actually articulating their
  inter-relation. Emotional self-expression, on the other hand, is considered the primary response, as
  the immediate indicator of mystical arousal which provides the dynamic for the offering, and indeed
  for the entire proceedings" (p. 145, folio 125).
- **Sufi ideology and Indo-Muslim social structure are congruent, and hierarchy is the hinge.** "the
  basic congruence between norms of Indo-Muslim social structure and concepts of Sufi ideology… The most
  prominent principle of social organization thus incorporated in the Qawwali occasion is that of
  hierarchical structuring", reflected "in the `darbar-e-auliyd` [text-layer?: darbār-e-auliyā] concept
  of the royal court of saints" (p. 148, folio 128; the same term is printed `darbdr-e-auliyd` at p. 159,
  folio 139 — see Doubts).
- **A structural exclusion:** "the position accorded to the performer, whose identity as a type of
  religious functionary is nevertheless subsumed within the traditional identity of the service
  professional, so that a performer, almost by definition, cannot also be a Sufi" (p. 148, folio 128).
- **Restraint as a status marker.** "restraint and controlled behaviour are characteristic of high
  status, so that even among Sufis or spiritual personages the frequent and expansive expression of
  strong arousal, especially ecstasy, is associated with lower status — just as are conspicuous items of
  characteristic Sufi clothing. Leading Sufis, then, validate their status with self-control" (p. 149,
  folio 129).
- **Spiritual norms supersede social ones only up to a point.** "the basic rules of interaction between
  juniors and seniors, or low and high, will not be contravened altogether — they do, after all,
  constitute the very foundation of the Sufi assembly" (p. 149, folio 129).
- **Ecstasy is a stage that low status can use and high status must avoid.** "The socially prominent
  devotee, therefore, tends to avoid reaching a state in which he may indulge in any eccentric behaviour
  contradictory with his social image. For the devotee who lacks social standing, on the other hand,
  reaching such a state gives him momentary prominence" (pp. 148–149, folios 128–129).
- **Closing claim of Part II — context, not music, supplies the motive force.** "the contextual dimension
  of the Qawwali assembly also contains the motivational dynamic for making a Qawwali event happen,
  including the music; in other words, this motivational dynamic is ideological, social or economic; it
  is not intrinsically musical" (p. 151, folio 131).
- **The two analytical problems Part III sets itself** (p. 155, folio 135): "having to deal with an
  interaction between two domains which are totally different from each other qualitatively", and
  "having to analyse process, an ongoing dynamic, by means of a procedure — conventional analysis — which
  operates by segmenting its object". The proposed solution is to find "common denominators" that
  "generate some form of equivalence between musical and contextual variables", so that "context
  variables can be 'plugged' into the music, and context constraints can thus become part of a musical
  grammar".
- **The analysis is built on the performer, and the author says why.** "Among Qawwali participants, only
  the performer knows and makes the music, hence such an analysis can only be built on him" (p. 156,
  folio 136); and explicitly not because he is dominant — "the performer is accorded the least possible
  significance, considering his indispensable role. In fact, it appears that he and his actions are
  controlled by the audience. But that control is variable… therefore his actions cannot be predicted on
  the basis of audience actions. Herein lies his position as an originator of strategy" (p. 156, folio
  136).
- **The performer's spiritual goal, in his own terms.** "His explicit goal is to evoke spiritual
  enhancement among the listeners, leading to mystical arousal and even ecstasy"; the career high point
  is "when a great Sufi or leader went into deep ecstasy during their performance"; yet "the performer
  knows his role is simply that of a mouthpiece, and consistently denies having any personal share in the
  impact generated by his performance" (p. 157, folio 137).
- **Conformity, not self-expression.** "performers invariably use this freedom to attempt as close a
  compliance with their audience's wishes as possible — rather than to 'express themselves' or innovate"
  (p. 159, folio 139); the idiom itself "reflects this dual requirement, of conformity on one hand and
  flexibility on the other", leaving "two broad, principal areas of choice": the choice of song, and "the
  internal structuring of the song in performance" (p. 160, folio 140).
- **Three levels of abstraction, mapped to chapters 6, 7 and 8** (p. 161, folio 141): description of
  particular events from videotape ("videotaped performances provide an accurate record of both Qawwali
  sound and audience behaviour, which is indispensable for transcription and subsequent review by
  participants"); a generalized picture "by charting a song performance as a performer experiences it,
  but 'in the abstract'"; and the analysis proper, which "replaces concrete options and relationships
  with abstract principles of meaning (semantic referents)".
- **A stated limit on the source material, and a fieldwork position.** "the vital domain of the
  performer's strategies is not easily accessible to an outsider": a foreign listener is "classed" a
  potential patron, so the performer will not divulge "strategies not congruent with the conception of
  Qawwali as spiritual in nature"; a foreign musician is a potential competitor and "is not likely to be
  let in on anything that may be considered secrets of the trade". The author adds: "In this respect only,
  being a woman and therefore a misfit vis a vis these all-male categories was probably a help in setting
  me, as investigator, apart professionally, while enabling me to establish a family relationship" — the
  chunk ends here, mid-sentence (p. 161, folio 141).
