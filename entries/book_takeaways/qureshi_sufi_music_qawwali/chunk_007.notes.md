## Practices, institutions, economy

**Who may convene an assembly, and on whose authority.**
- "The first and foremost aspect of setting is that the assembly must be in the charge of a spiritual
  authority, whether in the person of a spiritual guide (sheikh) or a saint personified by his
  representative" (p. 129, folio 109).
- The office is named: "The entire proceedings are in the care of this spiritual leader or 'chief of
  the assembly' (mir-e-mahfif [text-layer?: mīr-e-maḥfil]), and he is responsible for the spiritual
  welfare of the participants" (p. 129, folio 109). **Table 20 on the same page prints the same term
  as "(mir-e-mahfil)"** — both forms recorded, neither chosen (p. 129, folio 109).
- "the leader gives the gathering its character, hence assemblies are often identified by their
  leader" (p. 129, folio 109).
- Precedence can be displaced by a visitor: a representative of "a saint higher in status than the
  patron saint of the occasion" may attend, "will be recognized as the ceremonial head of the
  assembly (see below, p. 144)" — a **printed-folio** cross-reference, not a PDF page — "and the
  leader regains charge of the assembly by his leave, while he may assert his own authority through
  the leader" (p. 130, folio 110).

**Sponsorship, and the line between money and authority.**
- "It is also possible for a devotee of social prominence to sponsor a Qawwali occasion in
  association with a spiritual leader, usually his own guide. Sponsorship, in this case, implies
  material backing only - for instance, making a location available - while the authority over the
  assembly is placed entirely in the hands of the spiritual leader" (p. 130, folio 110).

**Patronage and the status economy of the audience.**
- "Persons of high socio-economic status are, in fact, an important audience component, especially
  since devotees from this class have traditionally been the worldly patrons of the Sufi divines,
  (see above, p. 90 f.)" — again a **printed folio** (p. 131, folio 111).
- "Indeed, a partnership between spiritual and feudal lords exists historically and conceptually;
  this is manifested in the paired idioms that group together the 'saintly' and the 'wealthy' as in
  fuqard aur umard (fuqard = plural oifaqlr [text-layer?: plural of faqīr], saintly mendicant; umard
  = plural of amir, wealthy leader)" (p. 131, folio 111); note 22 is attached here.
- The resulting classification of assemblies is itself economic: "a 'special assembly' (khds mahfil
  [text-layer?: khāṣ maḥfil] or mahfil-e-khas) is one consisting only of special people and implying
  a limited number of listeners, while a 'common assembly' ('am mahfil or mahfil-e- am) is one
  attended by common people as well, which, given their relative number, implies a large audience"
  (p. 131, folio 111). The contrast terms are "'special' or 'noble' (khds, sharif)" against "'common'
  or even 'lowly' ('am,zalil)" (p. 131, folio 111).
- Table 20 (p. 129, folio 109) grades participants: spiritual-status holders ("mashdikh / fuqard /
  darwesh / sufi" [text-layer?: mashāikh, fuqarā, darwesh, ṣūfī]) above worldly-status holders
  ("umard, rausd" [text-layer?: umarā, ruʾasā]) — "devotee with wealth", "devotee with (official)
  power" — above "poor devotee", "non-religious young men" and "'common people'" of "minimal status".

**Who is admitted, and who is excluded.**
- "the assembly is open to serious devotees who are in a spiritual frame of mind and ritually pure
  (bd-wuzu [text-layer?: bā-wuzū], that is, having performed ritual ablutions)" (pp. 130–131, folios
  110–111).
- "Women, as well as young boys, are specifically excluded because of the temptation which their
  presence constitutes. This rule is generally enforced; for women in particular, a separate
  enclosure may be provided, in keeping with Indo-Muslim social custom" (p. 131, folio 111).
- Against that: "there is an ideological commitment in Indian Sufism to accept all comers, which in
  effect means that no-one is to be prevented from attending a Qawwali assembly - exceptionally not
  even a woman (as is evidenced by this study)" (p. 131, folio 111).
- Seniority: "the very term for an old or senior person (buzurg) is also the term of reference for
  saints, individually (buzurg) or collectively (buzurgdn-e-din [text-layer?: buzurgān-e-dīn])" (p.
  131, folio 111); against it the "'young men's category' (naujawdn tabqd [text-layer?: naujawān
  ṭabqa])" is "the least significant component of a Qawwali audience" (p. 131, folio 111).

**The occasion calendar (Table 21, p. 130, folio 110).**
- "a) Occasion for Qawwali / saint's anniversary: death day / special life event / saint's special
  day: monthly death day / weekly special day / opportune day in Islam: day of remembering the dead
  (Thursday) / day of weekly prayer (Friday)".
- In the prose: "the primary occasion for holding a Qawwali assembly is one invoking mystical union.
  Foremost among these is the day of a saint's final union with God ('urs), followed by other

- The ʿurs has a terminal ritual: "Of all times, the early morning hours are felt to have a special

  {qui [text-layer?: Qul] or khatam)" (p. 132, folio 112).
- Plate 12 documents that ritual at Delhi: "The saint's spiritual genealogy (shijra) is recited
  during the Qui [text-layer?: Qul], major anniversary ritual of the 'urs of Nizamuddin Auliya held

**Time and duration.**
- Table 21d (p. 130, folio 110) fixes the day by prayer: "fajr / dawn", "zohr / mid-day (flexible
  time)", "asr / mid-afternoon (flexible time)", "maghrib / sunset", "isha / after nightfall
  (flexible time)"; assemblies "fixed by ritual requirement ('A- 1 hour long [text-layer?: ½–1 hour
  long])" precede zohr or maghrib; "extended/open-ended (over 4 hours long)" follow fajr or isha;
  "limited in duration (1-2 hours long)" fall between the prayers.
- "Substantial Qawwali assemblies are often held during the earlier part of the day when no prayer is
  scheduled - this is a preferred time for anniversary assemblies at some shrines. But the favourite
  time for substantial assemblies is the night, when no interruption threatens, continuing as late as
  early morning prayer" (p. 132, folio 112).
- "Four to five hours are quite standard for the duration of a Qawwali occasion not limited by ritual
  requirements" (p. 133, folio 113); ritual-bound events "generally last from one to two hours" (p.
  132, folio 112).

**The performers as an occupational group, and the hereditary economy of shrine attachment.**
- Table 20 grades them by shrine tie: "Performers (qawwat [text-layer?: qawwāl]) / senior saint's
  shrine affiliation / junior saint's shrine affiliation / no shrine affiliation", against "hereditary
  pedigree (maurusi [text-layer?: maurūsī])" or "no pedigree ('dm qawwal [text-layer?: ʿām qawwāl])",
  and places them as "participants outside and beneath audience status categories" (p. 129, folio
  109).
- "it is only as a category of service professionals that the performers are part of the Qawwali
  setting, not as individuals" (p. 132, folio 112); "that presence is ensured by the assembly leader
  who also controls the appearance of a particular performing group" (pp. 131–132, folios 111–112).
- "since their interest in the assembly is recognized as being professional rather than spiritual,
  the performers are not expected to have a devotee's disposition or training. Even those who are
  formally attached to the Sufi hierarchy by a discipleship bond are not considered to be Sufis or
  devotees" (p. 132, folio 112); note 23 is attached here.
- Table 23 distinguishes two performing bodies by their relation to the shrine: obligatory and
  customary ritual songs ("rang-qaul, panchdyatl gdne [text-layer?: panchāyatī gāne]") are executed by
  the "communal group of local shrine (panchdyatl, mushtar [text-layer?: panchāyatī, mushtarak])",
  while "songs freely chosen" are executed by "private 'parties' (parti, chaukl [text-layer?: pārṭī,
  chaukī])" (p. 136, folio 116).

**Decorum, seating and the display of rank.**
- "The highest place is assigned to the saint presiding over the assembly, either represented by his
  tomb, if the assembly is held at his own shrine or darbar (court), or else by his gaddi (throne),
  in the form of the seat occupied by his most exalted representative who controls the event.
  Directly opposite is the space set apart for the performers, who thus principally face and address
  the 'throne'" (p. 134, folio 114).
- "If the assembly is held at a saint's tomb, the row facing in the direction of the Holy Kaaba (see
  Table 22) is reserved for the leading saintly representatives, including the leader himself" (p.
  134, folio 114); note 24 attaches.
- "The leader will ensure that special or prominent listeners are given prominent seating at the
  front near the gaddl [text-layer?: gaddī]; very rarely is he required to relegate a presumptuous
  commoner to the back" (p. 134, folio 114).
- Dress: "The classical dress of Sufi saints or sheikhs (see Plate 11), characterized by a turban
  (sdfd [text-layer?: sāfā]), a long cloak (khnqd [text-layer?: khirqa]) and usually a long scarf
  draped over the shoulders (galdaoni)", still marks exalted standing, but high-ranking Sufis prefer
  plain colouring or "the traditional formal apparel of the Muslim secular elite, adorned by a scarf
  that marks their particular saint's identity (see Plates 12, 25)" (p. 134, folio 114).
- **A status marker worth an entry:** "Conspicuous Sufi dress, on the other hand, is generally worn
  by representatives of minor shrines" (p. 134, folio 114).
- Posture: the ideal is "kneeling while sitting on the heels, preferably with the right foot crossed
  over the left one, arms dropped by the sides, head bowed - the classical devotional posture of
  submission in Islam, prescribed for the prayer ritual (dozdnu [text-layer?: dozānū], see Plate 25)"
  (pp. 134–135, folios 114–115); "spiritual leaders sit for hours without moving during an assembly"
  (p. 135, folio 115).

**The order of service (Table 23, p. 136, folio 116, with the prose at pp. 135–137).**
- "The religious cast of the Sufi assembly is formally expressed in the fact that it 'begins and ends

- Table 23: "I Koranic recitation (duration: 5-15 minutes) — Leader; a) prayer for the dead (fdtehd

**The offering transaction — the chunk's central economic material (p. 143, folio 123, with Table
26).**
- The encounter is formalized and named: "the external format of encountering the saint or 'meeting
  the Sheikh' (sheikh se milna) is constant. In essence, it consists of making a formal offering
  {nazrdnd [text-layer?: nazrāna]}" (p. 143, folio 123).
- The act: "in its standard form the devotee rises to approach the spiritual superior, bows down
  (with unbent legs) or kneels before him, and extends on his open palms - right hand over left - an
  offering of money, usually a single note (see Plate 14). This note is lightly picked up or touched
  by the recipient as a gesture of acceptance and placed on the floor before him (see Plate 15)" (p.
  143, folio 123).
- The return: "The donor may respond to the blessing of the sheikh's touch by touching his forehead
  or by raising his palms to his eyes. If he is a personal disciple, he may well kiss the hand or
  knee of his guide, or the ground before him ... or he may prolong the encounter by remaining in a
  prostrated position, indicating ultimate submission (see Plate 16). When returning to his seat, he
  may further show extreme deference by retreating backward so as not to turn his back to the saint"
  (p. 143, folio 123).
- Table 26 grades the offering by what it expresses: "a) Formal offering (nazrdnd, nazar): standard
  presentation : bow to offer; presentation expressing need for spiritual indicator : offer joindy
  [text-layer?: jointly] with senior person; presentation expressing submission, respect : 'kiss' or
  touch feet (qadambosT [text-layer?: qadambosī]); presentation expressing submission, devotion :
  'prostrate' (sajdd [text-layer?: sajda]); presentation expressing aroused mystical love : embrace
  (gala milna)". **A second, distinct payment class follows and the chunk breaks inside it:** "b)
  Propitiatory offering (sadqd [text-layer?: ṣadqa]) / to avert evil from sheikh" (p. 143, folio 123)
  — the entry has no further text before the chunk ends.
- The recipients of deference, and so of the offering, are enumerated: "these include descendants of
  saints, foremost among them the leader who represents the patron saint of the assembly, but also a
  personal spiritual guide or even the very threshold of the saint's tomb, locus of his resting
  place" (p. 143, folio 123).
- The author frames the offering as solicitation of a flow: "Implied in the Sufi's activating the
  spiritual bond with a saint or his own guide is also an active solicitation of the divine benefices
  that flow from God through the spiritual chain of Sufism" (p. 143, folio 123).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- Attributed by the author to Sufis in general: "According to Sufis, the primary precedent for this
  power of musical sound was set at the time of Creation, when the beauty of God's voice transported
  the human soul into a state of divine ecstasy (zvajd [text-layer?: wajd])" (p. 127, folio 107);
  note 21 attaches.
- Attributed to Amir Khusrau's poem: "the thirteenth-century poetic vision of Amir Khusrau ... Nami
  ddnam che manzil bud ... where the poet finds himself transported in an ideal assembly of ecstatic
  Sufi saints, exalted by the Prophet's presence and presided over by God himself" (p. 128, folio
  108). The author calls it "no better charter for this conception" — presented as a vision, not as
  an event.
- Attributed as "A much-quoted precedent": "when Khusrau danced in ecstasy at his sheikh's assembly,
  the saint immediately restrained this free expression, for the disciple was still a courtier and
  committed to worldly pursuits" (p. 141, folio 121).
- Attributed as "A much-quoted incident from the life of the saint Nizamuddin Auliya": "the saint,
  attending his own Qawwali assembly, suddenly rose in respect, motivated not by inspiring mystical
  song, but by the sight of a dog outside the door that resembled the animal he used to see by the
  house of his own spiritual guide, the saint Baba Farid" (p. 143, folio 123).

## Arguments and interpretations

- The chunk's organizing claim is that the occasion is conceived twice over, and that the two
  conceptions generate its structure: "In its totality, both as mahfil-e-sama' and as darbdr-e-auliyd,
  the conception of the Qawwali occasion may be termed an occasion for Sufi devotees to experience
  mystical arousal within the framework of the Sufi spiritual hierarchy, through the medium of
  mystical songs performed by professional functionaries" (p. 128, folio 108).
- **Three premises about the medium** (p. 127, folio 107): the power of the word, "based on the
  primacy of God's word in the Koran"; the power of repetition, "particularly rhythmic repetition as
  practised in zikr"; and the power of "musical sound (ghind [text-layer?: ghinā], song, music, or
  achchhi dwdz [text-layer?: achchhī āwāz], melodious, pleasing sound, voice) ... to stir the soul
  (takrik-e-qalb [text-layer?: taḥrīk-e-qalb]), and to arouse emotions of love to the point of
  ecstasy". Sufism thereby "utilizes this power of music as a means for spiritual progress (ruhdni
  taraqqi kd ek zariyd [text-layer?: rūḥānī taraqqī kā ek zarīʿa])".
- The corollary the author draws is a regulatory one: because music "can also stir emotions of love
  toward profane purposes", "Sufi music is to be assigned a religious character through text choice
  and the invocation of zikr, and through rules of style and presentation avoiding profane
  association" (p. 127, folio 107).
- **Two premises about arousal** (pp. 127–128, folios 107–108): listening is individual and intuitive,
  so the setting must offer "the utmost flexibility and scope for self-expansion"; and individual
  emotion "finds fulfilment through his link with the spiritual hierarchy", so arousal "must take
  place within the frame of reference of the Sufi hierarchy".
- **Two premises of the Indo-Muslim social order** (p. 128, folio 108): "inner reality is confirmed in
  outward manifestation", and "formal rules are required to govern individual expression". From these
  the author derives the court model: "the Qawwali occasion as an assembly of divines has come to be
  conceptualized in terms of its equivalent in the worldly authority structure: the royal court of
  Muslim rulers and its formal etiquette", with the consequence that "spiritual status does not
  contradict worldly status".
- **The classical categories are judged insufficient**: Sufis discuss setting "with reference to three
  standard categories applied to the sama in classical Sufism (after Al Ghazzali; see Ghazali 1979,
  Book II, Ch. 9, Rahman 1971): zamdn, or time, makdn, abode and akhwan, participants. While these
  categories continue to be relevant for the proper regulation of sama assemblies, they are too
  limited to cover all aspects of this actual setting" (p. 129, folio 109).
- **An analytical observation on seating**: "Given the fact that the Qawwali assembly is a performance
  occasion, it is significant that the seating order does not facilitate the listeners' focus on the
  performers. This reflects the purpose of the assembly - to promote for the listeners an inner
  concentration on the mystical quest, with the help of the Qawwali as a medium only" (p. 134, folio
  114). She adds that "in some mystical traditions the devotees are not even to raise their eyes
  toward the performers (for example in the Abu Ulahl silsild [text-layer?: reading uncertain; see
  Doubts])" and that "Only the leader faces the Qawwals directly, for he is required to control them
  thus" (p. 134, folio 114).
- **A three-stage model of arousal, with a fourth stage supplied by the author**: the states are
  ranked "along a continuum of increasing intensity, as summarized in Table 24" (p. 139, folio 119) —
  stage I "Activated devotional attitude / Enthusiasm (kaif) / Incipient or mild arousal (halki
  kaifiyat)", stage II "Deeply moved, overcome with spiritual emotion (ruhdni kaifiyat) / Intense
  spiritual experience (ruhdni laraqqi [text-layer?: rūḥānī taraqqī]) / Strong arousal (did darje ki
  kaifiyat)", stage III "Transported, self obliterated (beqdbu, behdl) / Trance, ecstasy (behoshi,
  wajd, hdl)", self-control "absent" only at III (Table 24, p. 139, folio 119). The neutral stage is
  the author's own addition: "The stage of normal consciousness is generally taken for granted;
  nevertheless, seen analytically, it is clearly part of the conceptual scheme and must therefore be
  included in an analytical perspective" (p. 140, folio 120).
- **Expressive response treated as a sign system**: the repertoire "in fact constitutes a languge
  [text-layer?: language] of gestural and sound expressions. Analogous to verbal language in its
  signifying function, its meaningful units are essentially signs. Structurally, they are limited in
  number and syntactical manipulability. Semantically, they are characterized by a low degree of
  specificity and a highly evocative content" (pp. 141–142, folios 121–122), rooted in "the general
  idiom of kinesic expression which has been standard in Indo-Muslim elite society" (p. 142, folio
  122). The author compares the first stage of response to "the musha'ira ('poetic symposium'; see
  Qureshi 1969: 430 ff.)" — **her own earlier work** (p. 142, folio 122).
- **Two complementary modes of response**, mapped onto the two conceptions: "one comprises the
  expression of the Sufi's spiritual state; the other the expression of his attachment to the
  spiritual hierarchy ... the first makes reference to the individual's experience of mystical song as
  conceptualized in the mahfil-e-samd', the other to his presence in the saintly gathering as
  conceptualized in the darbdr-e-auhyd [text-layer?: darbār-e-auliyā]" (p. 140, folio 120). The
  second "is governed by rather more formal rules" (p. 140, folio 120).
- **Ecstasy is socially licensed, not merely felt**: "conspicuous or unrestrained self-expression on
  the part of the uninitiated or spiritually less committed is frowned upon" (p. 141, folio 121), and
  "it is ultimately up to the spiritual superior, be it the ecstatic person's own guide or the
  assembly leader, to assess his state and guide him through it, be it by facilitating his expression
  or by restraining him" (p. 141, folio 121).
- Table 25 (p. 141, folio 121) sorts the gestures into what is "specific to Sufism" ("bow head", "join
  hands", "prostrate (sajdd)", "hand on chest (symbolizes image of Sheikh in heart)", "rub face, touch
  eyes") and what is "common to Indo-Muslim cultural expression" ("sway (jhumnd)", "tap rhythmically",
  "raise arm, hand (hath batdnd, hath uthdnd)", "exclaim (dwdz nikdlnd)", "involuntary movement, twitch
  (harkat)"), then grades strong arousal "in order of increasing intensity": "weeping (riqqat, girid,
  rond)", "shout (chlkh, slightly pejorative: huhd)", "stand up (khara hond)", "dance (raqs)", "fall
  down, roll, toss about (lotnd)", **"die (wisdl [text-layer?: wiṣāl])"**.
