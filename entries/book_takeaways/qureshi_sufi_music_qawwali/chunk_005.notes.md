## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The Part II epigraph frames the whole section as one about form and hierarchy**: "Adab muhabbat ka
  pahla qanna hai" / "The first step in the quest for Love is proper Form" (p. 97, folio 77). The
  romanized `qanna` is damaged; see Doubts.
- **The material realities of shrine life, stated outright in the Part II preface**: "living within
  this world of Sufism also brings to the seeker's awareness the social and material realities of a
  Sufi community centred around a saintly shrine: the authority of sheikhs and subordination of
  musicians, the competition among both for material survival and rewards, and the primacy of
  hierarchy and status both spiritual and worldly" (p. 97, folio 77).
- **Silsila and discipleship.** The silsilās are "the spiritual 'chains' or lineages of those who
  followed the Way and received illumination from God, which in turn empowered them to be guides
  (sheikh, pir) to other seekers or disciples (murid)"; "Discipleship (bai'at) links the devotee to
  this genealogy of spiritual power: through his pir to the great saints of the past, to Hazrat Ali …
  then to Prophet Muhammad himself and ultimately to God" (p. 100, folio 80).
- **Spiritual brotherhood**: "the resulting relationship of spiritual 'brotherhood' which links the
  disciples of one spiritual guide, as encapsulated in the expression pirbhdyi [text-layer?:
  *pīrbhāyī*] or 'brother-in-pzr' [text-layer?: *'brother-in-pīr'*] — quite analogous in meaning to
  'brother-in-Christ'. Indeed, this bond is considered fundamental to the concept of the Sufi
  community" (p. 101, folio 81).
- **The turn to shrines (ta'ifa phase), the chunk's single most shrine-relevant passage**: "Later
  Sufism has also come to emphasize nearness to these saints and their power in spatial terms, at the
  abode of their final union with God, that is, their tomb. Accordingly, Sufism in India (Eaton 1978)
  as elsewhere (Geertz 1968, Crapanzano 1973) has in recent centuries shifted its focus of orientation
  to saintly shrines, their founder-saints and spiritual power" (p. 101, folio 81).
- **Barkat**: "In this ta' ifa phase of Sufism (Trimingham 1 971), the devotional veneration of saints
  as spiritual intermediaries and mediators of divine benefice (barkat) becomes an integral and
  central part of Sufi practice, in addition to personal discipleship and lineage links" (p. 101,
  folio 81).
- **Maqām and ḥāl**, quoting Arberry: "'The maqdm [text-layer?: *maqām*] is a stage of spiritual
  attainment which is the result of the mystic's personal effort and endeavour, whereas the hdl
  [text-layer?: *ḥāl*] is a spiritual mood depending not upon the mystic but upon God' (Arberry 1950:
  75)" (p. 100, folio 80); the states are "God's gift of 'states' (ahwdl, pi. of hdl) of nearness,
  leading ultimately to union (wisdl) with God" (p. 100, folio 80).
- **Zikr**: "the reciting or 'recollection' of God's name (zikr)"; "'the constant recollection of God'
  (Schimmel 1975: 84), consists of the repetition — silent or voiced — of divine names or religious
  formulae. Its particular form and emphasis are part of the teaching tradition of the various Sufi
  orders, and it is often practised collectively in special gatherings led by a spiritual leader"
  (p. 102, folio 82).
- **Sama' as a contested practice, and the orders that refuse it**: "zikr is sanctioned by the Koran
  (Sura 33:40 and 13:28)", but "samd has always remained a theologically controversial practice";
  "Orders with a more orthodox orientation, like the Naqshbandiya or the Suhrawardiya prohibit its use
  altogether or compromise by permitting mystical songs unaccompanied by instruments" (p. 102,
  folio 82).
- **Band samāʿ**: "at both shrines a special samd assembly called band samd ('closed' samd) continues
  to be held, where the usual instruments are silent (Hussaini 1970)" (p. 102, folio 82) — the two
  shrines being Nizamuddin Auliya's and Gesudaraz's.
- **Wajd**: "the mainstream of Sufi tradition in India and Pakistan accords importance to samd as the
  context for a Sufi's attaining zvajd [text-layer?: *wajd*], the ecstasy of what means literally
  'finding' God" (p. 102, folio 82).
- **The division of roles at the assembly**: "a conceptual separation exists between the Sufi listener
  of the sama message and the singer who is causing the message to be sounded. The one is termed Sufi
  or, collectively, mashaikh (pi. of sheikh), the other Qawwal, meaning literally 'the one who says',
  or 'the singer of a verbal message' — the message being Qawwali (derived from qaul, that is,
  'saying' in Arabic)" (p. 103, folio 83).
- **Repertoire tied to particular shrines**: "The Hindi repertoire of Qawwali texts is strongly
  localized, often being associated with specific shrines of saints" (p. 104, folio 84); and the
  content category "Poems with a focus on a local saint express a variety of associational links, most
  of all devotion to the particular saint and his shrine and reference to its ritual practice" (p. 106,
  folio 86).
- **Patronage and state, the socio-economic frame** (section C, still opening when the chunk ends):
  "In the Indian subcontinent Sufism, and with it Qawwali, took root during the thirteenth century,
  within the socio-cultural framework instituted by Muslim rule, and under its patronage and
  protection (see Ahmed 1963, K. A. Nizami 1957, 1974, Rizvi 1978)" (p. 110, folio 90). "Through a
  series of dynasties ruling from the eleventh to the eighteenth century Muslim rule imposed a
  centralized agrarian bureaucracy over an existing feudal economy, in which a rigid caste system had
  been operating" (p. 110, folio 90); local rulers gained autonomy "especially on the decline of
  Mughal power (seventeenth to eighteenth century)" (p. 110, folio 90).
- **Nazr, the offering**: hierarchical relationships "were governed by formalized codes of behaviour
  (adab), and elaborate court ritual. At the core of both lay the articulation of submission and
  allegiance to a superior by means of a gift or offering (nazr), which, once accepted, entailed an
  obligation to confer benefice on the donor, thus reaffirming the link between inferior and superior
  members of the elite hierarchy (Ashraf 1970: 72 ff.)" (p. 110, folio 90).
- **The status of the performer class**: the "'lower' services, or shagird peshd ('serving
  professions', menials), comprising specialized crafts, artisan skills and personal services, were
  provided by hereditary professional specialists belonging to Hindu occupational castes or, more
  often, to their equivalent Muslim convert groups", related to the elite "as in the traditional
  patron-client or jajmani system" (p. 110, folio 90). The "'higher' services of administration,
  culture, religion and personal retainership were provided by groups drawn mainly from Muslims of
  foreign descent" (p. 110, folio 90).
- **Orders and their spread**: "It is through the great Sufi orders which were founded during the
  twelfth and thirteenth centuries (Trimingham 1971) that Sufi teachings and culture spread into India
  (Rizvi 1978, Schimmel 1975)" (p. 99, folio 79).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **The musical grammar cannot generate a performance, and this is the hinge of the book.** "the
  obvious shortcoming of the model — its inability to generate even one song in the concrete —
  suggests the need for a different level of inquiry" (p. 92, folio 72); the grammar "can list the
  choices by which Qawwali musical structure is rendered flexible and its units manipulable, but it
  cannot provide a programme for using those choices" (p. 93, folio 73); "How to use this flexibility
  is not a matter of structure at all, but of process: the process of performance" (p. 93, folio 73).
  Her evidence is Example 17, two transcribed openings of *Tori surat* and *Kachh jagmag* by the same
  performer, yielding "two irregularly patterned musical sequences based on structuring choices for
  which a purely musical rationale is apparently lacking" (p. 93, folio 73). The conclusion: "To
  understand this process and its dynamic, however, is nothing less than to understand the Qawwali
  performance context in its entirety" (p. 95, folio 75).
- **Flexibility is tied explicitly to the audience's spiritual needs**: flexibility of structure and
  manipulability of units are "assigned special significance as a distinctive feature and tied
  explicitly to the functional constraint of satisfying the changing spiritual needs of the audience"
  (p. 93, folio 73).
- **The Qawwali occasion is analysed as a religious ritual**: "In accordance with its manifestly
  spiritual function the Qawwali occasion is analysed as a religious ritual, with norms of setting and
  procedure which are informed by a set of relevant concepts" (p. 97, folio 77).
- **Her sources for the ideology chapter, stated**: "The primary sources for this expose are Sufi
  informants, and the secondary source is the literature that best formalizes their ideological frame
  of reference" (p. 99, folio 79).
- **Indic Sufism's roots and periodization**: "Indo-Pakistani Sufism is rooted in the classical
  tradition of Islamic mysticism as it developed in the Arab and Persian culture area between the
  ninth and eleventh centuries, and was codified in the writings of the major Sufi masters by the
  twelfth century (Nicholson 1962, Arberry 1950, Milson 1975)" (p. 99, folio 79).
- **Sufism as both response to and emanation from orthodoxy**: "Sufi ideology is a response to
  orthodox Islam, at the same time emanating from its very tenets" (p. 99, folio 79).
- **Hierarchy as the organizing principle of Indic Sufism**: "Indie [text-layer?: *Indic*] Sufism in
  particular conceptualizes divine power and man's relationship to it very much in hierarchical terms:
  there are degrees of nearness to that power which are reckoned according to the principle of
  spiritual descent from the great Sufi masters or saints (auliyd, pi. otwali) [text-layer?: *auliyā,
  pl. of walī*] of the past" (p. 100, folio 80).
- **Why sama' matters more in India: the Hindu environment.** "there is, in Indo-Pakistani Sufism, yet
  another contributing factor to the importance accorded to the sama' concept: the ubiquitous presence
  of religious music in Hinduism … early Indian Sufis recognized that to spread Islamic mysticism in a
  Hindu environment justified giving special emphasis to sama' (Begg i960)" (p. 103, folio 83).
  Qureshi immediately qualifies it: "While there may be some element of apology in this justification,
  the general Sufi interpretation … is to accord special significance to music as a means for giving a
  more universal reach to Islam" (p. 103, folio 83).
- **A prestige hierarchy among the three Qawwali languages.** Farsi "represents Sufism par excellence"
  and "still enjoys a high spiritual and cultural prestige" (p. 104, folio 84); Hindi is "the second
  'classical' language of Indian Sufism" and "close to Farsi in spiritual prestige", and in India today
  "some ascendancy is given to Hindi by Sufis who wish to broaden the appeal of Sufism beyond the
  Muslim community" (pp. 104–105, folios 84–85); Urdu ranks lowest — "Because Urdu Poetry is no more
  than two centuries old and has little direct association with Sufi saints, it has less spiritual
  prestige than either Farsi or Hindi" (p. 105, folio 85). **Pakistan is named as the place where Urdu
  Qawwali predominates**: "Because Urdu predominates in Pakistan as the national language, Qawwali
  there abounds in both types of Urdu poetry" (p. 105, folio 85), and "there is, furthermore, a recent
  trend in Pakistan toward incorporating more Arabic phraseology into Qawwali poetry" (p. 105,
  folio 85).
- **The ghazal as the vehicle of mystical experience**: "The ghazal form has been considered an ideal
  vehicle for mystical experience (Schimmel 1975 162): the repetitive monorhyme or radlf [text-layer?:
  *radīf*] can serve to reiterate a central word phrase or concept, in the manner of the zikr
  principle" (p. 106, folio 86).
- **The socio-economic argument that opens section C**: "the somewhat marginal place which Sufism and
  Qawwali occupy in today's Muslim society — both in India and Pakistan — can in no way account for the
  power the Qawwali assembly still has as a socio-religious metaphor. The historical development of
  Sufism within Indo-Muslim society accounts for the social and economic base of all Sufi
  institutions, including that of Qawwali" (p. 110, folio 90). She adds that participants' "awareness
  of the purely socio-economic and political implications rarely becomes manifest" (p. 110, folio 90).
- **Tables of ordinary type that survived and are usable**: Table 15 "The Song: Application of
  Directional Melodic Adjustment" and Table 16 "Adjunct Items: Sequencing Principles" (p. 91,
  folio 71); Table 17 "Adjunct Items: Application of Directional Melodic Movement" (p. 92, folio 72);
  Table 18 "Hierarchy of Sufism: the Chishti Silsild" (p. 101, folio 81); Table 19 "Qawwali Poetic
  Metre", listing ten Farsi–Urdu metres in order of decreasing frequency (Hazaj 2, Rami 3, Muzdri,
  Mutaddrik 2, Mutaddrik 3, Rami 1, Kdmil 4, Mutaqdrib 1, Rami 2, Mujtass) and two Hindi metres
  (p. 109, folio 89).

- **`qanna` in the Part II epigraph is damaged and I have not silently repaired it.** The romanized
  line reads "Adab muhabbat ka pahla qanna hai" against the book's own translation "The first step in
  the quest for Love is proper Form" (p. 97, folio 77). "Step" suggests *qadam*, but **the book prints
  no Urdu or Persian script anywhere for this line** (measured: zero Arabic-range characters in the
  whole book), so there is nothing to check the romanization against. Left as printed; a human should
  verify against a clean copy before the line is quoted in an entry.
- **Music-notation noise, inventoried once and not deciphered.** Staff-notation plates and their
  underlaid syllables produce pure noise on **pp. 92–95 (folios 72–75)** — Example 16 "Melodic cadence
  as conclusion" (p. 92, folio 72), Example 17 (a) *Tori surat* and (b) *Kachh jagmag* (pp. 93–94,
  folios 73–74), and a trailing fragment on p. 95 (folio 75) — and there is further non-prose noise at
  the head of p. 99 (folio 79) and around the Plate 3 caption on p. 100 (folio 80), where the
  photographed engraved tablet yields strings such as `Vc£JV^^>€c k'W'©`. **Nothing was lifted from
  any of these: no lyric, no name, no date in this file comes from a notation plate or from the Plate 3
  image.** Per the standing instruction they are not reconstructed and individual tokens are not
  flagged.
- **The internal structure of Table 18 did not survive, only its text.** The table's key prints two
  distinct link types, `direct spiritual link` and `several spiritual generations` (p. 101, folio 81),
  but **the connecting rules, brackets and arrows did not extract**, so which relation joins any two
  names is unrecoverable from this text layer. The column arrangement is legible enough to place
  Ala'uddin Sabir and Nizamuddin Auliya side by side and Nasiruddin and Amir Khusrau side by side, but
  **this is inferred from the line layout of the OCR, not from a surviving rule.** A rendered image of
  p. 101 should be consulted before any lineage claim stronger than "appears in the Chishti table" is
  written into an entry.
- **`shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar`: the death year AGREES with the archive.** Table 18 prints
  `d. 1266, Pakpattan` (p. 101, folio 81); the archive row's `figure_died` is 1266 and its location is
  Pakpattan. **Nothing for the coordinator to reconcile.** The other Table 18 death years (1236, 1237,
  1291, 1325 twice, 1356, 1422) belong to saints who are not archive principal figures, so no
  comparison arises.
- **`data-darbar` rests on four author-only citations** (`Hujwiri 1970: 404`, p. 102, folio 82;
  `Hujwiri 1970: Ch. 25` and `Al Hujwiri` and `Hujwiri 1970: 417`, p. 103, folio 83). The id is
  assigned under the ruling of 18 Sep 2026, but **the consolidator should weigh it as a
  bibliographic-source hit, not a shrine hit**: the chunk gives no Lahore, no Data Ganj Bakhsh, no
  tomb and no death year, only "eleventh-century". Nothing here can be written into the `data-darbar`
  entry as a fact about the shrine; it is evidence that Qureshi's doctrine of sama' is built on the
  *Kashf al-Mahjub* — though **the book does not name that work in this chunk**, giving only
  "Hujwiri 1970".
- **Interim convention (a), bare toponyms: this chunk returns empty for the archive.** The toponyms in
  Table 18 (Ajmer, Delhi, Pakpattan, Piran-e-Kalyar, Gulbarga, p. 101, folio 81) are burial places
  attached to named saints, so the convention does not apply to them; of the five only **Pakpattan**
  corresponds to an archive row and it is already folded into `shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar`. The
  remaining place-words in the chunk — India, Pakistan, the Indian subcontinent, the Arab and Persian
  culture area, the Islamic realm, Mekka, Kashi (Benares) (p. 107, folio 87) — **match no archive row
  in any field**, so a reversal of the interim ruling would change nothing in this chunk. Recorded so
  the check is not redone.
- **Hinglaj does not appear anywhere in pp. 91–110 (folios 71–90).** The archive defect — the row
  `Shaktipeeth Shri Hinglaj Mata Mandir` carries an **empty id cell** in `shrine_index.tsv` — is
  therefore not triggered here, and **no id has been invented for it.** Stated only so the next reader
  knows the check was made.

- **Internal contradiction: the part is numbered `Part 1` on its half-title but called "Part II" in
  its own text.** The half-title prints `Part 1 / The performance context: the Qawwali occasion`
  (p. 97, folio 77), while the facing prose on the same page reads "a detailed consideration of the
  structure of the Qawwali assembly constitutes the core of Part II" (p. 97, folio 77). Given that the
  chunk also contains the end of chapter 2 and that the running header of Part II's chapter 3 is "The
  performance context: the Qawwali occasion", **the half-title is almost certainly a damaged `Part II`
  and the roman numeral lost a stroke.** Recorded rather than repaired.
