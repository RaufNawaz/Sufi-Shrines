## Practices, institutions, economy

**The named participants of the Chilla Mahfil** (this is the chunk's first-order material):

- **Meraj Ahmad** — "The performer is Meraj Ahmad who also contributed to the evaluation of his
  performances" (p. 163, folio 143). At this event he is "the sole performer" and sings three songs
  (p. 164, folio 144); "the Qawwali performance at this event is to be managed exclusively by Meraj
  (see Plate 22)" (p. 164, folio 144).
- **Iqbal Ahmad**, his brother — Plate 22 shows "Meraj Ahmad with his brother Iqbal Ahmad and two
  future Qawwals, awaiting the start of the Chilla Mahfil" (p. 166, folio 146); at the close "his
  brother Iqbal, not needing any separate prompting, instantly contributes a threefold closing
  candence (tiyd [tiyā]) on the dholak" (p. 187, folio 167).
- **Khwaja Hasan Sani Nizami**, the assembly leader — he instituted the assembly: "As instituted by
  Khwaja Hasan Sani Nizami (see p. 95 above)" (p. 164, folio 144). Attendance is limited to devotees
  "all connected in some personal way with the leader Khwaja Hasan Sani" (p. 164, folio 144). He
  recites the Dud [duʿā] and the Fdtehd [Fātiḥa] (p. 165, folio 145). Meraj knows he "never gives
  free expression to his mystical emotion" (p. 173, folio 153). He accepts an offering "in the name
  of his saint" (p. 185, folio 165), and it is his remark to the gaddinashin that signals the end of
  the performance (p. 187, folio 167).
- **Syed Haleem Chishti** — "a gaddlnashin [gaddīnashīn] (senior representative)" of the Ajmer saint;
  "a special friend of Khwaja Hasan Sani. A regular visitor at Nizamuddin Auliya anniversaries, he is
  well known there for his spiritual sophistication and receptive sensitivity to Qawwali, as well as
  for his generosity" (p. 164, folio 144).
- **Syed Zainul Abedin** — "a first-time visitor, young and newly installed as the titular
  representative {diwdn [dīwān], see p. 92 above) of the Ajmer saint after his father's death. This
  means that he formally heads the hierarchy at this assembly, and indeed at any Qawwali assembly in
  India; he is therefore the final recipient of all offerings, even though in personal standing and
  seniority, Syed Haleem Chishti is far above him" (p. 164, folio 144).
- **Anwar Sabri** — "a well-known and venerable Sufi poet who is always part of Khwaja Hasan Sani's
  special audience" (p. 164, folio 144); Plate 26 names him "Sufi poet Allama Anwar Sabri outside the
  Chilla" (p. 167, folio 147). He receives an offering during the Masnavi (p. 173, folio 153).
- **Ashur Mian** — recites the Shijrd [shijra] (Genealogy) (p. 165, folio 145); Plate 25 (p. 167,
  folio 147).
- **Pasha**, the leader's elder brother — receives a one-rupee offering "in recognition of his
  chronological seniority" (p. 174, folio 154). The leader's **younger brother (M)** passes offerings
  (p. 185, folio 165) and initiates the Pakistani visitor into the offering hierarchy (p. 187, folio
  167).
- **The author's husband** — "Also present, as expected, is the author's husband whose literary
  orientation Meraj knows and appreciates, as well as his invariable generosity which Meraj knows to
  be tied to the author's study with him" (pp. 164–165, folios 144–145).
- **Unnamed but recurrent**: "the Canadian patron", "the American Sufi convert (F)", "a prosperous
  devotee (E)", "a local devotee", "a young devotee", "the pick-up man", "the senior accompanist",
  "a few religious old men who have no particular standing other than their age" (pp. 164–165, 173,
  175, 184–185, 187; folios 144–145, 153, 155, 164–165, 167).

**Calendar, timing and institutional setting of the ʿurs:**

- "The Chilla Mahfil is held on the second day of the 'urs of both Nizamuddin Auliya and Amir Khusrau;
  this is the actual death day of both: 'the auspicious seventeenth' (sattarvih sharif [sattarvīṁ
  sharīf]), that is, the seventeenth day of the Islamic months of Rabi-us-Sani and Rajab respectively
  (see p. 95 f. above)" (p. 164, folio 144).
- "this assembly is timed to precede the anniversary ritual {qui [qul], khatam) which is held at the
  sanctuary after 1 1 a.m. [11 a.m.] on that day; accordingly, it is expected to begin at about 10
  a.m. and to last for at most one hour, so that the participants can return to the shrine in time
  for the ritual (see p. 105 above)" (p. 164, folio 144).
- Attendance: "his audience of nearly 30 (see Plate 24)" (p. 164, folio 144); "Due to the somewhat
  remote location of the Chilla … only a limited number of committed devotees attend" (p. 164, folio
  144). It is "the first formal assembly of the 'urs" and "its duration is limited - especially
  today, due to a late start" (p. 165, folio 145).

**The programme as printed** (p. 165, folio 145; starred items were omitted from the transcription):
`I Recitation: Qua at*` [text-layer?: the book glosses it "(Koranic passages)"; the printed form is

**Economy of the assembly — offerings:**

- Amounts are given twice only. During the Rang, "An even greater number (20 persons) offers a total
  of 51 rupees, so that it all takes more time" (p. 172, folio 152). During the Masnavi's third
  statement, "only one rupee is offered which the leader in turn offers formally to his elder
  brother, Pasha" (p. 174, folio 154).
- The offering route is hierarchical and terminates with the performer's assistant: an offering is
  "passed up the hierarchy, to Khwaja Hasan Sani, the leader, to Syed Haleem Chishti, the gaddinashin
  of Ajmer, and finally to Zainul Abedin, the diwdn [dīwān] of Ajmer, so that his pick-up man can
  receive it and deposit it on the harmonium" (p. 184, folio 164). The diwan is "the final recipient
  of all offerings" (p. 164, folio 144). One departure from that route is recorded: the gaddinashin
  gives an offering to the leader "showing his utter devotion to the saint Nizamuddin Auliya (rather
  than passing it on to the diwdn of Ajmer, as would be standard procedure)" (p. 185, folio 165).
- The performer's economic calculus is stated openly. Meraj must "curtail the duration of the ritual
  hymns, so that enough time remains for the more profitable 'party' singing" (p. 165, folio 145). He
  normally has his senior accompanist lead the communal hymns "so that he himself appears as leader
  only at the start of his own party singing, and uninformed listeners may not have the impression
  that Meraj already led and received offerings during the ritual hymns, which would reduce the
  impact of his party singing" (p. 165, folio 145). He breaks that rule today because the leader, and
  earlier Anwar Sabri, "expressed annoyance at the unsatisfactory vocal quality of the senior
  accompanist, a rather old man (cf. cassette 1 (a) and (b))", an annoyance which "had at the time
  resulted in Meraj losing his initial turn and being relegated to the end of a large Qawwali
  assembly" (p. 165, folio 145).
- Money-changing during the performance is itself a coded signal of intent to offer: "three listeners
  signal to the Qawwals, two to change money - which indicates their wish to make more offerings"
  (p. 173, folio 153); "one senior devotee changing money, an indication of intended offerings"
  (p. 173, folio 153).
- Food: "A young devotee moves the tray of food offerings to be distributed later, but that is no
  concern of Meraj" (p. 174, folio 154).
- The shrine as court: the Pakistani visitor had failed to show "his deference to the saint at whose
  darbdr [darbār] (royal court) he is a visitor", until the leader's brother gave him an offering to
  pass on, "thus initiating the Pakistani visitor's participation into the hierarchical presentation
  of respect" (p. 187, folio 167).

**Bodily and gestural responses recorded as institutional behaviour:** bowing the head and joining
the palms; swaying; weeping; tapping to the drum rhythm; calling out or verbalizing the text;
kneeling before a superior and being reciprocated by the touching of feet; touching the wall of the
saint's abode; and prostration — "demonstrating his devotion and allegiance by a prostration
(qadambosi; cf. Table 26)" (p. 184, folio 164; also pp. 173, 175, 185, 187; folios 153, 155, 165,
167).

## Arguments and interpretations

- The chapter's stated purpose: "This chapter aims at presenting the ethnographic evidence for the
  way in which the Qawwali performance process works, in the form of two actual Qawwali performances"
  (p. 163, folio 143).
- The author's two purpose-built notations. "I developed two methods of transcribing and interpreting
  these video recordings: the videograph and the videochart" (p. 163, folio 143). "The videograph
  (see pp. 148 ff.) provides an accurate visual record of audience behaviour as it occurs in response
  to the ongoing song performance and also supplies the temporal axis for plotting that behaviour.
  The more interpretative videochart (see pp. 181 ff.) traces the interaction between the musician's
  ongoing performance decisions and the audience responses as he perceives them, along with the
  resulting song sequence" (p. 163, folio 143). The two are "complementary in emphasis: the one
  (videograph) effectively portraying the complexity of multiple audience responses, the other
  (videochart) focussing on the interactional dynamic of the performance" (pp. 163–164, folios
  143–144).
- Method of the graphs: song units run along a horizontal axis where "one unit of the musical metre
  of the song corresponds to a specific graph length"; "the listeners are lined up vertically in
  accordance with their status position - rather than the seating order of the assembly, although
  that too has some relevance"; and "The timing of these responses is accurate to the metric unit or
  half unit" (p. 172, folio 152).
- Two status hierarchies are distinguished in the audience: a "spiritual status hierarchy" (patron,
  gaddinashin, diwan, Sufi poet, saints' descendants) and a "worldly status category" (well-to-do
  devotees, Bombay businessmen, the author's husband, local devotees) (pp. 164–165, folios 144–145).
- Ritual obligation shapes the opening: "During the Qaul it is incumbent on the spiritual leaders to
  pay their respect to the Sufi hierarchy, by making offerings to the highest representatives present
  as well as by honouring each other" (p. 172, folio 152).
- Language competence is treated as a determinant of response: Meraj "is missing out on responses
  from those listeners who do not understand Farsi, though they are not many" (p. 174, folio 154);
  two listeners "not knowing Farsi, have understood the saint's name and make their first offering on
  it" (p. 175, folio 155). He chooses the Hindi song to "complement the Farsi poem with the more
  direct appeal of Hindi" (p. 175, folio 155).
- The author's most sustained analytic set-piece is the *girah* lead-back (pp. 184–186, folios
  164–166): the insert's tune "does not correspond to the melodic frame of a girah", because "A girah
  must at the end descend from a high pitch so as to lead back into the main song, whereas the last
  line of every Masnavi couplet starts low and actually rises in pitch" (p. 185, folio 165). When
  Meraj starts the descent, "the gaddinashin, as soon as he hears the high pitch, moves his head and
  finger disapprovingly", which Meraj "immediately - and correctly - interprets … as a signal not to
  conclude the Insert" (p. 185, folio 165); he reverses the melodic direction, and "No sooner has the
  direction of the tune changed upward, than the gaddinashln nods approvingly, having instantly
  understood Meraj's intended compliance with his wish" (p. 186, folio 166). The author draws the
  cost: "a truly effective link-up between Insert and main song has been thwarted. On the other hand,
  Meraj has helped sustain the arousal of one of the spiritual leaders of the assembly" (p. 186,
  folio 166).
- The performer also discounts responses he judges inauthentic: of the Rudauli descendant, "he has
  been expressing himself almost continuously, yet he has not focussed his enthusiasm on any part of
  the song, nor validated his link with an offering even once. Obviously his behaviour is not in
  keeping with his status and spiritual identity" (p. 174, folio 154); by the end Meraj "disregards
  [him] entirely as not evincing any spiritual basis" (p. 185, folio 165).
- Fieldwork ethics, closing chapter 5: access "at the personal level (see Plate 20 - a male
  investigator would not have access to families due to women's seclusion)"; "I consider my
  informants' sharing of performance strategies as a special gift"; and the author says she tried to
  avoid "sharing in the attitude of contempt which not only upper-class listeners but - by their own
  implication - even performers themselves often evince toward the Qawwali metier" (p. 162, folio
  142).
