  - He is named a third time as the likeness under which the Beloved is figured, in the song *Kachh

  - The author's own characterization of the song that carries the first two mentions: it "most

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Hereditary transmission.** *Tori surat* "is part of the Qawwal Bachche's special heritage", and is
  "widely sung within the entire Chishti silsild [text-layer?: *silsila*]" (p. 47, folio 27). The
  silsila is here a circuit of musical transmission, not an initiatic claim.
- **Authenticity as a claim about a version.** "The present version of Meraj Ahmad is standard for all
  Nizamuddin Auliya performers and may be considered the most authentic extant today" (p. 47, folio
  27); of the Masnavi, "Meraj's version presented here is considered standard" (p. 50, folio 30).
- **Performance at the shrine.** Of *Batufail-e-ddman-e-Murtazd*: "This song is known to all
  Nizamuddin Qawwals, but it is definitely Meraj's song, and he always leads its performances at the
  shrine" (p. 55, folio 35). **This is the chunk's only use of the word "shrine".**
- **Hereditary versus amateur performers.** "three hereditary Qawwals outside Nizamuddin Auliya"
  (p. 47, folio 27); "a performance by a hereditary Qawwal from Hyderabad" (p. 50, folio 30); the
  tune of one song "is heard in a variety of versions… One of these is by the young amateur performer,
  Iftekhar Amrohvi…, who simplified the original tune considerably — due to his limited musical ability
  and training rather than compositional originality" (p. 58, folio 38); the song "is also favoured by
  amateur performers" (p. 58, folio 38).
- **Boy singers and kinship in the performing family.** A melodious tune with no strong spiritual
  text-phrases makes a song "a favourite solo for young boy Qawwals who can thus display a nice voice
  without having to be prepared for extensive takrdr [text-layer?: *takrār*] repetition which they
  would not have the experience to provide. Hence, among Nizamuddin Auliya performers, I most often
  heard this song sung by a young boy, Chand (brother of Meraj's wife)" (p. 58, folio 38).
- **Repertoire graded by audience.** *Kachh jagmag* is "part of Meraj's personal repertoire. Considered
  an 'old' song, it is currently little heard, but Meraj likes to revive it for 'special' listeners"
  (p. 52, folio 32). *Kisi ko kuchh* "is extremely popular, appreciated by 'common' listeners. But its
  simple appeal can cut across all audience levels; hence Meraj has learned it" (p. 58, folio 38). The
  metre of *Batufail* can be switched to "an 'easy' or 'light' 8/8 metre… depending on the type of
  listener before him" (p. 55, folio 35).
- **The adjunct repertoire as an institution of performance.** "the Introductory Verse (rubai)
  introduces a song and then the Inserted Verse (girah) elaborates portions within a song, while the
  Prelude (naghmd) serves to introduce the entire performance. All three are characterized by an
  improvisational quality" (p. 58, folio 38).
- **Instrumental preludes are recent.** "Instrumental Preludes are a relatively recent addition to
  Qawwali", articulating the zikr phrase 'Allahu'; the modern, faster naghmd has displaced the
  "Naghmd-e-Quddusi" of the sama' assemblies (p. 65, folio 45).
- **Qawwali for the unlettered.** Famous Farsi verses "have inspired later Sufi poets to elucidate
  them with additional verse lines, often in Urdu or Hindi, to clarify their meaning to unlettered
  devotees (lazmin)" (p. 62, folio 42; the term is damaged — see Doubts).
- **The fieldwork corpus, item by item** — the only quantities in the chunk, all in body prose:
  *Tori surat* "was recorded in seven performances, four sung by Meraj Ahmad, and the rest by three
  hereditary Qawwals outside Nizamuddin Auliya" (p. 47, folio 27); the Masnavi recordings "include a
  demonstration by Meraj as well as a performance by a hereditary Qawwal from Hyderabad, and four
  performances using the Masnavi tune in recitative form" (p. 50, folio 30); "Two versions of this
  song were recorded at Nizamuddin Auliya, both sung by Meraj Ahmad" (p. 52, folio 32); *Batufail*
  "was recorded in two performances at Nizamuddin Auliya, as well as one demonstration, all by Meraj
  Ahmad" (p. 55, folio 35); "A total of nine performances of this song were recorded: one each of
  versions 1 and 3, three each of versions 2 and 4, and one more performance by a Nizamuddin Auliya
  Qawwal" (p. 58, folio 38); "Shud dilam shefta was recorded in a total of four performances, all by
  Meraj" (p. 61, folio 41); "The five recorded performances of this girah include two by Aziz Warsi
  and three by Meraj Ahmad" (p. 63, folio 43); of preludes, "Preludes recorded by him and other
  Qawwals are too numerous and too varied to be listed" (p. 65, folio 45).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The composition of the Chilla couplet, attributed by the author without a source.** "Its first
  line was composed by Nizamuddin Auliya himself, while observing from the Chilla… how Hindu
  worshippers bathed in river Jumna: 'Every people has its right path, its faith, and its focus of
  worship'. Amir Khusrau, who was with him, at once completed the couplet with a verse line which has
  come to represent a poetic statement of the Sufi creed: 'I focus my worship on the tilted cap of my
  Beloved.'" The book adds that this final line is "known to every Sufi in India" (p. 62, folio 42).

  the book's own hedge).

  non-Indian or Persian origin, if not the original setting of Rumi's Masnavi itself" — the author
  reports this as their view and does not endorse it (p. 49, folio 29).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **Qawwali is placed inside the North Indian musical language, and the placement is explicitly
  trans-national.** "Qawwali forms part of the musical language of Northern India and Pakistan which
  is codified in the Hindustani classical musical tradition", and four "basic features of musical
  organization and presentation place Qawwali firmly within this North Indian frame of reference":
  a seven-step tonal framework, an additive metric framework, compositions with repeatable sections
  differentiated by register, and an ensemble centred on one melodic line (p. 66, folio 46).
- **Qawwali is "song", between raga music and chant.** Classical music is "characterized by the
  primacy of music over text"; chant "by the primacy of the word over its musical delivery"; song
  falls in between, with text primary, instrumental accompaniment present, and form following the
  text (pp. 66–67, folios 46–47). This is set out as Table 2, "Musical Categories", which puts
  *qawwali* under POPULAR (*filmi bdzdri*) alongside *filmi qawwali*, while the light-classical column
  holds *dhurpad*, *khaydl*, *tardnd*, *asthdyi* (p. 67, folio 47).
- **Within song, Qawwali "most closely fits the rubric of 'light classical', along with other
  supra-local song genres that are the preserve of specialist performers"**, which gives it
  flexibility to borrow both classical technique and popular and folk material (p. 67, folio 47).
  Note the tension with Table 2 on the same page — see Doubts.
- **Performers' categories are the analytic starting point.** "Table 3 presents my analytical summary
  of Qawwali pitch, with reference to performers' concepts which are explained in the Musical
  Vocabulary for Qawwali"; "Qawwals rarely refer to this pitch framework in abstraction, except as a
  teaching tool, but they are cognizant of it" (p. 67, folio 47). Where a performers' term does not
  exist the table says so explicitly: directionality, parallelism and motifs are each marked "(no
  term)" (p. 68, folio 48).
- **The Masnavi tune carries a specific association with classical Sufism.** "here, more than in any
  other Qawwali song, the musical setting itself has a very specific association with classical
  Sufism" (p. 49, folio 29); and because of its "'high stature' (uhcha maqdm [text-layer?: *ūnchā
  maqām*], in Meraj's words) the Masnavi tune is favoured as a setting for other Farsi poems with the
  same metre to lend them its enhancing power" (p. 50, folio 30). A tune is thus treated as a bearer
  of prestige transferable to other texts.
- **The girah as semantic ambush.** The final Khusrau line "has a great potential impact as an Insert,
  suddenly adding its own depth of meaning to a related word or phrase in the main song"; here it is
  hooked to the word *kajkulahe* in the song *Chashm-e-maste*, so that the high pitch register of the
  final line "makes its textual message stand out musically: the effect is that of a 'punch line'"
  (pp. 62–63, folios 42–43).
- **Song type tracks religious address.** "The only na't — or Qawwali addressing the Prophet — among
  these examples is a song recently composed by a Panjabi Qawwal, Rahmat Khan. The poem belongs to the
  more popular type of religious praise song, straightforward in meaning as well as in expression,
  or, as one Nizamuddin Auliya performer puts it, 'totally obvious' (ek dam khuld [text-layer?: *ek
  dam khulā*])" (p. 57, folio 37); its rhythm exemplifies "the 'Panjabi style'" (p. 58, folio 38).
- **Takrar repetition is tied to a song's single-addressee structure.** "Because this song is
  exclusively oriented toward one spiritual personage, it particularly well exemplifies the use of
  ra£rar [text-layer?: *takrār*] repetition in which appellations of Ali are sung in responsorial
  alternation with text phrases, in the classical takrdr style which Meraj masters well" (p. 55,
  folio 35).
- **Melody is classified by degree of raga-consistency.** A fully consistent motivic setting yields "a
  raga-like melody"; the book names *Bakhubl* in raga *shdhdnd*, "the Basant song Phul rahl sarsoh in
  raga bahdr", the Masnavi as a tradition-specific setting, and *Tori Surat* as "recognizable as
  classical ragas but not identified" (p. 72, folio 52). Most Qawwali tunes have only limited motivic
  consistency, including tunes "adapted from devotional folk song (Kachh jagmag), or from popular
  song" (p. 72, folio 52). Favoured classical ragas are listed as "kdfi, shdhdnd, bahdr, bdgeshri and
  jaijaivanti" (p. 69, folio 49).
