## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The occasion and its date.** The Hujra Mahfil is held "late at night on the eighteenth of
  Rabi-us-Sani; the third day of the 'urs celebrations" (p. 195, folio 175), and "begins at midnight,
  after devotees and performer have taken part in a large public assembly in the Urs Mahal hall"
  (p. 196, folio 176). The assembly is annual: "Every year these disciples travel to Delhi" (p. 195,
  folio 175).
- **Who may perform, by location.** "Because of its location at the shrine, the Qawwal Bachche have a
  first right to perform at this assembly. Among them it is Meraj who, by customary right, functions
  as the principal performer, although the assembly leader also gives occasional 'turns' to visiting
  performers" (p. 195, folio 175).
- **Hereditary performing sequence at shrines — the key institutional claim of the chunk.** "While the
  leader has ultimate control over performance turns, there is, at most shrines at least, a customary
  sequence of those performing groups which have a special attachment to the shrine; and this sequence
  is maintained from generation to generation, always under the name of the original head of the group
  (see pp. 99 f. above). Such turns always occur at the beginning of the occasion" (p. 211, folio 191).
  "pp. 99 f." are printed folios = PDF pp. 119 f.
- **How an outsider gets a turn.** "Outside performers obtain their 'turn' at an assembly by first
  paying their respects to the leader, so as to get 'on his list'. Once the list is drawn up, a
  performer learns of his place in the proceedings usually by being told which performer will sing
  before him" (p. 211, folio 191). The customary allowance is "the usual norm of two songs per turn …
  although the leader can always shorten or extend that limit during the assembly" (p. 211, folio 191).
- **Offices present at the assembly.** The "mir-e-mahfil or leader of their assembly is Khalil Mian,
  who is spiritually most senior among them, and has himself the authority to act as a spiritual guide
  (khildfat) [text-layer?: khilāfat]" (p. 195, folio 175). "The only senior person from the Nizamuddin
  Auliya shrine community is Miskin Shah, who, in the absence of a descendant, takes on their role in
  an unofficial way" (p. 195, folio 175). The disciples are "all plrbhdyi [text-layer?: pīrbhāī],
  brothers through their plr [text-layer?: pīr]" (p. 195, folio 175).
- **The vacancy is what creates Miskin Shah's leverage.** "Miskin Shah - in the absence of any
  recognized descendants representing the local saint - seizes the rare opportunity to assert personal
  control over the Qawwals. His command to insert a single verse line … complicates but also enhances
  the song presentation" (p. 200, folio 180).
- **The circulation of an offering, from the VIDEOCHART.** "Local Sufi (S) receives offering, presents
  it to another senior disciple who offers it to L" and then "L offers same nazrdnd [text-layer?:
  nazrānā] formally to S who rises in respect, so that both pass it to performer" (p. 201, folio 181).
  Later: "D, shaking head, searches pocket, stands up with a shout and makes offering to L, bowing
  low", and "L passes offering to S who hands it on to performers" (p. 203, folio 183).
- **The performer sings through the offering.** "Keeps line going during offering sequence. While
  receiving offering with humility, his brother takes opportunity to lead, initiating another
  statement of verse before offering sequence complete" (p. 201, folio 181).
- **Offerings as income, and their absence as a signal.** "Listeners with worldly status are of very
  special value to the performer, since normally they constitute the main source of offerings in a
  Qawwali event" (p. 209, folio 189). Conversely on the chart: "Expects nothing more, since not even S
  has made an offering in appreciation of his insert" / "No offering indicated" (p. 206, folio 186);
  and in the prose, "Expecting nothing more in emotional response or in offerings, Meraj decides to end
  the song" (p. 200, folio 180).
- **Money divides the repertoire.** Meraj "sometimes resents having to comply where he considers the
  initiator's status insufficient and where no financially beneficial response is forthcoming" (p. 200,
  folio 180).
- **Party singing versus community singing — how earnings are split.** Performing either "as an
  individual leading his group, doing 'party singing' … or as a member of his performing brddri
  [text-layer?: birādarī] doing 'community singing' or 'mixed singing'". In the first case "he
  controls the singing and also the earnings"; in the second "the control over both singing and
  earnings is shared, and leadership is usually established on the spot" (p. 210, folio 190).
- **A strategy of withholding.** A party leader "will plan to keep the communally sung ritual songs
  short, in order to save for himself and his group both performance time and his listeners' offering
  money, rather than to have more income distributed among the greater number of community members",
  and "may even go so far as to avoid taking a lead position there … instead of wasting it when the
  reward for it will be dissipated to his entire brddri - a strategy Meraj follows regularly at
  Nizamuddin Auliya (but could not in Performance No. 1; see p. 145 above)" (p. 211, folio 191).
  "p. 145" is a printed folio = PDF p. 165.
- **Reading a predecessor's takings.** "At one level this can be gleaned from the amount of money the
  performer sees his predecessor gather at the end of his performance, as he is about to replace him"
  (p. 212, folio 192).
- **Language ranked by spiritual status of the gathering.** "To express sophistication, Farsi is
  superior, followed by classical Hindi, preferably in the form of a sophisticated poetic text. But
  Urdu, at least theoretically, has no place in a satna' [text-layer?: samāʿ] gathering of high
  spiritual status" (p. 208, folio 188).
- **Shrine architecture as an audience filter.** "Since locale size determines audience capacity, it is
  clear that a small listening area, even in a public shrine, will accommodate few listeners beyond the
  leader's immediate circle … A large public hall or shrine courtyard, on the other hand, is expected
  to attract a sizeable common audience" (p. 210, folio 190). Time of day does the same work: "the
  performer can only expect night performances to be frequented by large common audiences" (p. 210,
  folio 190).
- **The shrine poor, explicitly excluded.** "Of course there is always the destitute component of any
  common audience, especially in assemblies held at shrines where beggars and mendicants abound. These,
  however, are hardly given any consideration, either by leaders or by performers" (p. 210, folio 190).
- **Rank among listeners maps onto shrine rank.** "a representative of a senior saintly lineage or a
  major shrine establishment will be acknowledged in the performer's strategy in the same way"; whereas
  "lesser spiritual leaders or representatives - lower-class sheikhs or representatives of minor
  shrines - the performer usually does not plan to cater to them specifically" (p. 209, folio 189).
- **The prescribed thematic order, honoured in name only.** "The customary thematic sequence of praising
  God, the Prophet, and then saints … performers do not feel particularly bound by this rule, although
  they too pay lip service to it and respect it as a principle. Songs praising God are hardly ever heard
  at all" (p. 211, folio 191).
- **Ritual songs tied to place and occasion.** "those specific songs prescribed for the ritual portions
  of such assemblies (for example Qaul, Repertoire No. 1, and Rang in Performance No. 1)" (p. 210,
  folio 190).
- **Ecstasy and the performer's duty to sustain it.** On the disciple's raqs, "Meraj now complies with
  the requirements of this state of ecstasy (kaifiyat) by providing takrdr [text-layer?: takrār]
  repetitions of the same line and closely monitoring the needs of the ecstatic Sufi" (p. 197, folio
  177); on the chart, "Fulfils duty to accompany ecstatic state for its duration by repeating A
  amplified by B intermittently" (pp. 203–204, folios 183–184). "But even while locked in embrace, the
  ecstatic Sufi continues his dancing movements, so that the performer is required to continue his
  takrdr of the second line until the state finally subsides" (p. 200, folio 180).
- **The assembly stands for an ecstatic, and sits to end him.** "until he is dancing ecstatically (raqs)
  while the other listeners stand in reverence" (p. 197, folio 177); "L and S rise in recognition of D's
  state … Everyone follows L's lead and stands up" (p. 203, folio 183); and to close, "At this Khalil
  Miyan sits down and the rest follow his example, whereupon Meraj immediately terminates the verse"
  (p. 200, folio 180) — on the chart, "L sits down and all the other devotees follow suit" (p. 205,
  folio 185).
- **A senior Sufi can override the performer mid-line.** Meraj tries to insert a Farsi girah "But he is
  immediately stopped by Miskin Shah, the senior Sufi of the shrine, and made to return to repeating
  the song line" (p. 197, folio 177); on the chart, "S immediately signals performers to stop Insert and
  continue repeating A2, giving priority to D's aroused state", and the performer "Instantly obeys S"
  (p. 203, folio 183).
- **The embrace.** "the ecstatic disciple's raqs culminates in an embrace of Khalil Miyan, the leader"
  (p. 197, folio 177); the plate caption reads "Ecstasy culminates in the embrace of the leader who
  holds the disciple securely while encouraging the performers" (p. 199, folio 179).
- **A Hindu verse line accepted into a Sufi assembly.** The line Miskin Shah forces in is one
  "originating in Hindu devotional poetry", and it "fits the song in both meaning and form" (p. 200,
  folio 180).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The chapter's stated aim.** "The aim is to consider the performer's strategies by moving with him
  through an entire song performance, and thus to ascertain how contextual variables serve him as cues
  for his musical options at every stage" (p. 207, folio 187).
- **Performer's priorities reorder the shared categories.** The performer shares "a notion of the
  general structure of the Qawwali occasion (Chapter 4) … At the same time, his vantage point imposes
  particular priorities on his view of these categories, so that logically an outline of performer
  strategies must be structured in accordance with those priorities" (p. 207, folio 187).
- **The leader is assessed spiritually first, materially second.** "the performer must above all know
  his spiritual status and identity - which saintly lineage he belongs to, and whether he is a saint's
  familial descendant or even his recognized representative, or whether he is a recognized spiritual
  guide of some standing" (p. 207, folio 187). Where a rich patron "lacks spiritual status himself, the
  spiritual superior to whom the leader is linked becomes the spiritual focus for the Qawwal" (p. 208,
  folio 188).
- **Nisbat: three ways a song can be linked to a saint.** "This link (nisbat) may be thematic, in the
  form of praise or supplication, and it may also reside in a song's ritual association with the saint
  or his shrine, or in the poet's identity as a disciple or saint of the lineage. Thus, Torisurat
  [text-layer?: Tori surat] (Repertoire No. 3) articulates all three links" (p. 208, folio 188).
- **Occasion and place both generate the associational link.** Fixed setting factors affect "the
  thematic focus of the event through occasion and place, to the extent that these set up an
  associational link with a saint - be it through his anniversary (occasion) or his proximity (place)"
  (p. 210, folio 190).
- **Audience taxonomy.** Performers divide audiences "into the same categories as do other participants
  of Qawwali, both as to the fundamental division into the 'special' and 'common' audience components
  (Table 21), and, within the special category, as to the distinction between those with spiritual and
  worldly status, that is, the 'saintly' and the 'wealthy'" (p. 209, folio 189).
- **Money can outrank the pir.** "Such a situation can lead the performer to the point of superseding
  the preference of the spiritual leadership, as by the choice, for example, of a song theme to suit a
  puritan devotee's orientation, or of a simplistic text to please an unlettered donor" (p. 209, folio
  189).
- **A composite image is now the norm, so the performer hedges.** "Since the majority of leaders today
  prefer a composite image, projecting both sophistication and popularity (due to the patronage
  situation; see Chapter 3), the performer's safest strategy is to plan on staying on a middle ground
  and let the immediate performance situation influence his final decision on the spot" (p. 209,
  folio 189).
- **Nothing is decided in advance.** The performer's "conviction, confirmed by experience, that nothing
  is ever certain, that every situation is different and unpredictable, so that it can best be assessed
  on the spot when the entire constellation of factors is at its most apperceivable" (p. 212, folio 192).
- **Silsila as a rule of programming.** "this procedure conforms to the Sufi guideline for thematic
  continuity which is emphasized by both performers and Sufi listeners, stating that once a link
  (silsild) [text-layer?: silsilā] is established, it should not be broken" (p. 212, folio 192) — and
  it is "quite freely disregarded" where the audience shows no preference (p. 212, folio 192).
- **A complete performance is a sum of turns.** "it is of importance to realize that a complete Qawwali
  performance consists of the sum total of different individual performances. Therefore, in specific
  musical terms, each performer's concern and strategic input is of necessity limited to his own 'turn'"
  (p. 211, folio 191).
- **The videochart's rationale.** It "conveys in its very format the polarized dynamic operating between
  the two processes - one musical, one contextual - which go on interacting with each other during the
  performance: the musician continues to sing, and the audience responses continue to inform his
  perception" (p. 195, folio 175); it is "slightly more interpretative than the videograph" and
  summarizes audience detail "from the interactional perspective of the musician" (p. 195, folio 175).
- **Methodological note: the author and her husband are inside the event they record.** "in the doorway
  where I am operating my videocamera, he has also noted my husband, an important personal patron at
  the present time, who had earlier asked him for an old song, Kachh jagmag jagmag howat hai" (p. 196,
  folio 176). The same person is later called "his Canadian patron": "This was the case when Meraj
  chose Kachh jagmag (Repertoire No. 5) to satisfy his Canadian patron (Performance No. 2)" (p. 209,
  folio 189). The song choice analysed through the whole chapter is therefore one the author's own
  household commissioned. See Doubts.
