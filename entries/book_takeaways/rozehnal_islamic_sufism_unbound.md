## What this book is

A contemporary ethnography of a living Sufi order, and the only book in this corpus written from
inside a twenty-first-century Pakistani silsila as it actually operates. The author states his
subject plainly: "This book is a study of a living Sufi tradition. Drawing on both textual and
ethnographic materials, I trace the identity politics and ritual practices of a contemporary South
Asian Sufi order (silsila) through the contexts of late colonial India and postcolonial Pakistan."
(p. 12, folio not stated) The order is the **Chishti Sabiri** silsila, tracked through three
twentieth-century teaching shaykhs — Muhammad Zauqi Shah (d. 1951), Shahidullah Faridi (d. 1978,
an English convert born Lennard) and Wahid Bakhsh Sial Rabbani — and through their living disciples
in Lahore, Karachi, Islamabad, Bahawalpur, Allahabad (southern Punjab) and Kuala Lumpur. Fieldwork
is dated and located by the book itself: "I spent the academic year of 1995–1996 as a graduate
student studying Urdu in Lahore, Pakistan" and "In Pakistan, I was based in the cultural capital of
Lahore… I also traveled frequently to Islamabad, Bahawalpur, Allahabad (southern Punjab), and
Karachi to interact with the Chishti Sabiri disciples" (p. 21, folio 10; p. 26, folio 15).
Interview datelines run from 2000 through 2002.

For this archive the book's value is threefold and unusual. **First**, it contains the corpus's
fullest first-hand account of a single shrine disaster: the stampede at the shrine of Baba Farid
ad-Din Ganj-i Shakkar in Pakpattan on the night of 1 April 2001, which the author witnessed —
"I too was there that night" (p. 37, folio 27) — and which he reconstructs from the state's
account, the custodians' rebuttal, the press, and the disciples' own theology of it. **Second**, it
is a detailed record of how one order *uses* shrines: which ones it visits and which it forbids,
what it does at the tomb, where it houses its people, how its ʿurs calendar is built, who controls
the qawwals, and where women may and may not go. **Third**, it is a study of Sufi publishing,
translation and media as an economy — bookstalls in shrine lanes, Urdu and English print runs,
cassettes, websites and email discipleship.

The author's stance is declared and reflexive: "I acknowledge my own positioning as a white,
middle-class, American male of Christian background" (p. 22, folio 11); "As an academic outsider, I
claim no direct knowledge of the inner states and experiences that are the essence and goal… of
Sufi practice" (p. 23, folio 12); "while it is not a hagiography, it is narrated with humility,
gratitude, and profound respect" (p. 23, folio 12). His governing thesis is that these disciples
"have thus shaped their own alternative modernity —an identity that is simultaneously Muslim,
mystic, and modern" (p. 20, folio 9), and his methodological slogan is "Sufism (like the broader
category of 'Islam') is best understood as a verb rather than a noun." (p. 25, folio 14)

### shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar — Shrine of Fariduddin Ganjshakar, Pakpattan

The book's central shrine, present in **fifteen of the eighteen chunks** and the subject of its
whole first chapter. The author's forms of the name, all as printed: "Baba Farid ad-Din Mas'ud Ganj-i
Shakkar" (p. 8, folio not stated; p. 29, folio not stated), "Baba Farid ad-Din Ganj-i Shakkar"
(p. 26, folio 15), "Farid al-Din Ganj-i Shakkar" (p. 111, folio 102), "Baba Farid Shakar Ganj"
(p. 220, folio 212), "Shaikh Farid-ud Din Ganj-i Shakar" in two cited book titles (p. 241,
folio 234; p. 272, folio 266), "Baba Farid Shakarganj" in a 1911 article title (p. 242, folio 235),
and in the disciples' own address "Hazrat Baba Sahab" / "Baba Sahab" (p. 76, folio 66; p. 80,
folio 70). The pilgrims' shout at the ʿurs is "Ya Farid, Haq Farid!" (p. 36, folio 26) and
"Farid! Farid!" (p. 36, folio 26).

  - **Birth date, and his own silsila as a chain, are (not stated) anywhere in the book.** He is
    placed only by position: link **19** of the forty-three-link shajara Chishti Sabiri disciples
    "recite … in a ritual liturgy" — "19. Farid ad-Din Mas'ud Ganj-i Shakkar (d. 1265)", standing
    between "18. Qutb ad-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki (d. 1237)" and "20. 'Ala ad-Din 'Ali Ahmad Sabir
    (d. 1291), eponymous founder of the Chishti Sabiri order" (p. 15, folio 4). Elsewhere the only
    affiliation given is the adjective "the Chishti saint" (p. 130, folio 121) and "his renowned
    thirteenth-century predecessor" (p. 175, folio 167).
  - **Rank, in the book's own words.** "Pakistan's most famous Sufi saint" (p. 26, folio 15); "one of
    South Asia's most renowned Sufi saints" (p. 29, folio not stated); "the preeminent saint of
    Pakistan's Punjab" (p. 36, folio 26); "the patron saint of Pakistan" (p. 215, folio 207); "one of
    the Chishti order's most renowned saints" (pp. 157–158, folios 149–150). For the order he is "the
    patron saint" of the silsila (p. 76, folio 66), and "the contemporary silsila places the tomb of
    Shaykh Baba Farid ad-Din Mas'ud Ganj-i Shakkar in Pakpattan at the center of Sufi sacred
    geography in Pakistan" (p. 206, folio 198).
  - **Twelve years of suluk.** "several murids highlighted the symbolic significance of twelve years,
    noting that the saints Baba Farid Ganj-i Shakkar and 'Ali Ahmad Sabir both underwent twelve years
    of spiritual training before completing suluk" (p. 81, folio 71, interview September 2, 2001,
    Karachi).
  - **He is a poet whose own Persian verse is sung at his ʿurs**: "The three hour evening sama'
    session was filled with ancient Hindi songs of Amir Khusrau and the Persian songs of Hazrat Baba
    Farid himself" (p. 232, folio 224).
  - **As author.** Wahid Bakhsh Sial Rabbani "published numerous Urdu translations of classical
    Persian biographical texts, including the discourses of Farid al-Din Ganj-i Shakkar, Maqam-i Ganj
    Shakkar" (p. 111, folio 102). The Chishti Sabiri book about him is Rabbani's *Maqam-i Ganj-i
    Shakkar* (Lahore: al-Faisal Publishers, 1994) (p. 250, folio 243; p. 264, folio 258) — **the
    single most-cited Rabbani title in the endnotes**, carrying the order's overview of zikr at
    420–425 (p. 258, folio 251), its defence of ziyarat and ʿurs at 340–364 (p. 260, folio 253), the
    ʿurs hadith at 38 (p. 260, folio 253), a long treatment of sama' at 365–416 (p. 261, folio 254),
    and the wahdat al-wujud / wahdat ash-shuhud doctrine at 212–231 (p. 251, folio 244). **What that
    book argues about Baba Farid is (not stated).**
  - **Secondary literature the book points to, with its own citations.** Richard M. Eaton, "The
    Political and Religious Authority of the Shrine of Baba Farid," in *Moral Conduct and Authority*,
    ed. Barbara Daly Metcalf (Berkeley: University of California Press, 1984) — cited once at pages

- **Shrine / Darbar**
  - **Place and distance.** "The regional shrine complex in the small pilgrimage town of
    Pakpattan—120 miles southwest of Lahore—has for centuries served as a central marker in the
    regional history of Punjab" (p. 35, folio 25). The figure caption is "The Shrine of Baba Farid
    ad-Din Mas'ud Ganj-i Shakkar (d. 1265), Pakpattan, Pakistan" (p. 8, folio not stated; p. 35,
    folio 25). Called "the rural tomb complex in Pakpattan" (p. 48, folio 38) and "a rural Sufi
    shrine in Pakistan" (p. 158, folio 150).
  - **Antiquity — two figures, both the book's.** "Since the thirteenth century, the tomb of the
    saint, Baba Farid, has linked the local population to a wider universe of Islamic piety and
    Chishti Sufi spiritual hierarchy" (p. 35, folio 25); the author writes of "Pakpattan's
    seven-hundred-year history" (p. 242, folio 235); a disciple speaks of
    "a six-hundred-and-fifty-year-old tradition" of custodianship broken by the Awqaf takeover
    (p. 41, folio 31). **No
    building date, no architect and no foundation year anywhere: (not stated).**
  - **Standing after Partition.** With pilgrimage to "the major Chishti shrines in India—including
    Ajmer Sharif, Kalyar Sharif, and Nizam ad-Din in Delhi" made "prohibitively difficult for
    Pakistani Muslims" by "new passports and the redrawing of local maps", "Pakpattan now stands as
    the unrivaled center of a distinctly Pakistani Sufism" (p. 36, folio 26).
  - **Communal composition, as the book frames it.** "In the absence of other religious
    communities—the Hindu, Jain, or Sikh 'Other'—Pakpattan is no longer a site of intercommunal
    conflict or cooperation. Instead, the shrine is now marked as exclusively Muslim space"
    (pp. 35–36, folios 25–26). Against that, the 1833 account it quotes has "Numbers of pilgrims,
    both Hindus and Mussalmans" coming to the shrine (p. 36, folio 26). **Both are kept.**
  - **The bihishti darwaza (Gate of Heaven).** "the narrow Gate of Heaven (bihishti darwaza)"; "This
    famous southern door of the saint's tomb remains closed throughout the year until the evening of
    the anniversary when it is opened by the Diwan, a direct lineal descendant of the saint and the
    shrine's official representative (sajjada nishin)" (p. 36, folio 26). Passing through it is "a
    direct, salvational encounter with Baba Farid's living grace (baraka), a ritual reenactment of
    the saint's eternal union ('urs, or 'marriage') with God" (p. 36, folio 26). Its dimensions come
    from an 1833 European account the book quotes: "The doorway is about two feet wide . . . the
    apartment itself is not capable of containing thirty people crowded together" (p. 36, folio 26) —
    **the visitor is not named; the year is printed "1833"**. The index carries `bihishti darwaza
    (Gate of Heaven) 26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 212 … see also Pakpattan Sharif` (p. 275, folio not stated).
  - **Other fabric named.** "the narrow stairwell at the extreme north end of the shrine, a
    passageway typically reserved for the Diwan and his personal guests" (pp. 36–37, folios 26–27);
    "the northern gate" by which the order's party customarily leaves (p. 38, folio 28); "a large
    stage in the center of the courtyard" (pp. 37–38, folios 27–28); "the inner sanctum of the tomb"
    (p. 219, folio 211); "the dargah" (p. 218, folio 210); "a room adjacent to the shrine" for sama'
    (p. 225, folio 217); and "a separate indoor room… in private compounds near the shrines"
    (p. 224, folio 216). **No measurements, no dome, no dates: (not stated).**
  - **Crowding is an architectural fact of the site.** "This is especially true at Pakpattan where
    thousands of pilgrims are pressed into an extremely cramped area. As a result, murids generally
    avoid lingering in the shrine for extended periods of time" (p. 217, folio 209).
  - **Bookstalls as shrine economy.** "The silsila's books are also found in the myriad bookstalls

- **The stampede of 1 April 2001 — the book's fullest single subject**
  - **The event, in the book's opening sentence.** "On the night of April 1, 2001 more than one
    hundred Pakistani men lost their lives, crushed to death in a mob stampede at the tomb of one of
    South Asia's most renowned Sufi saints: Baba Farid ad-Din Mas'ud Ganj-i Shakkar (d. 1265)"
    (p. 29, folio not stated). The author was present: "I too was there that night, accompanying a
    select group of Chishti Sabiri Sufi disciples (murids)" (p. 37, folio 27).
  - **Date and calendar.** It fell "Late in the night of April 1, 2001—the fifth day of Muharram, the
    first month of the Muslim lunar calendar", "the eve of Baba Farid's death anniversary ('urs)",
    when "tens of thousands of devotees inundated the provincial town of Pakpattan" (p. 36,
    folio 26).
  - **Death toll — contradictory within the book, and kept so.** "more than one hundred Pakistani
    men" (p. 29, folio not stated); "over a hundred devotees" (p. 26, folio 15); a German disciple's
    "more than one hundred people who breathed their last there" (p. 41, folio 31); against the
    reporter Tahir Jehangir in *The Friday Times*: "The newspapers reported over forty, but I can
    vouch for the fact that there were many more" (p. 37, folio 27).
  - **Mechanics of the crush, as the author reconstructs it.** Victims "ascended the narrow stairwell
    at the extreme north end of the shrine"; "an impatient crowd of several thousand onlookers broke
    through a weak police cordon and descended upon them from above"; "a police contingent posted at
    the entrance point made the fateful decision to close and lock the doors, sealing off the only
    means of escape" (pp. 36–37, folios 26–27). "Pilgrims had become martyrs, crushed to death."
    (p. 37, folio 27)
  - **Two flatly opposed attributions of blame, kept side by side as the book keeps them.** The
    state's: "the Diwan had delayed the public ceremonies and the opening of the bihishti darwaza by
    four hours while bickering over the annual payment allotted to him from the Awqaf Department",
    and the next day "the lead article in the national Urdu newspaper Jang quoted the district
    commissioner who insisted that this financial quarrel precipitated the crisis" (p. 39, folio 29);
    "Local politicians and police attributed the chaos, confusion, and deaths to the greed of the
    local shrine authorities and the lawlessness of an unruly, uneducated mob" (p. 48, folio 38). The
    custodians': Badr Moeenuddin, writing in the English daily *The Nation*, "blames poor crowd
    control and security arrangements as the underlying cause of the stampede", noting "the tragedy
    took place some two hours after the Diwan's arrival for the door-opening ceremony" (p. 39,
    folio 29); "The heirs of Pakpattan's holy man, by contrast, condemned these same administrators
    for overturning established crowd control policies and shirking responsibility" (p. 48,
    folio 38).
  - **The named victim is not named.** "A fourteen-year-old boy from the group was not so fortunate,
    however, perishing amid the melee . . . He was the son of a prominent devotee and, significantly,
    the great-grandson of the prominent Chishti Sabiri master, Shaykh Muhammad Zauqi Shah
    (1877–1951)" (p. 38, folio 28); "a member of a sayyid family (i.e., a lineal descendant of the
    Prophet Muhammad)" (p. 42, folio 32); "a promising young boy among their ranks" (p. 234, folio

    injuries to their own living master and several prominent devotees" (p. 48, folio 38) — the
    injured master is Shaykh Siraj, treated by a Malaysian disciple who is a medical doctor (p. 43,
    folio 33).
  - **The order's reading of the deaths.** "With a firm belief that nothing accidental happens in
    proximity to a saint's tomb, murids reasoned that the loss of life must be sacralized and
    understood in terms of martyrdom (shahadat)" (p. 41, folio 31); losses were read "as a ritual
    sacrifice demanded by the saint" (p. 48, folio 38); "Murids now commemorate the 'marriage'
    ('urs) of both the saint and the young boy with the God. In both cases, death has been
    transformed into martyrdom" (p. 234, folio not stated). This "diverges sharply from the
    rationalist interpretation of the Pakistani state" (p. 234, folio not stated).
  - **Portents.** "many disciples recalled strange circumstances and portents of impending disaster";
    "A number of disciples narrated dreams that they experienced in the days leading up to the 'urs:
    visions of Chishti Sabiri shaykhs, the death of family members and, in the case of one female

    scale of the tragedy of 2001 seem to be unprecedented in Pakpattan's seven-hundred-year history.

- **Practices and events**
  - **The ʿurs: month given, day given, year never fixed.** It is "the annual death anniversary
    ('urs)" (p. 157, folio 149); "the Muharram 'urs of Baba Farid in Pakpattan" (p. 232, folio 224);
    "the annual pilgrimage for the Muharram 'urs at Pakpattan" (p. 234, folio not stated). In the
    order's printed ritual calendar, "measured according to the Muslim lunar calendar", it is item 2:
    "**Baba Farid Ganj-i Shakkar (5 Muharram): at the saint's tomb in Pakpattan**" (p. 215,
    folio 207). The 2001 stampede fell on "the fifth day of Muharram" (p. 36, folio 26), consistent
    with that. It is a "weeklong 'urs retreat" (p. 217, folio 209).
  - **Pakpattan carries two of the order's four ʿurs, because Partition moved one there.** The full
    printed cycle: "1. Khwaja Mu'in ad-Din Chishti Ajmeri (6 Rajab): at the shrine of Baba Farid
    Ganj-i Shakkar in Pakpattan; 2. Baba Farid Ganj-i Shakkar (5 Muharram): at the saint's tomb in
    Pakpattan; 3. Shahidullah Faridi (17 Ramadan): at the saint's tomb in Karachi; 4. Wahid Bakhsh
    Sial Rabbani (20 Dhu al-Qa'da): at the saint's tomb in Allahabad" (p. 215, folio 207). The reason
    is stated: "After 1947, Pakistani disciples could no longer easily travel to the major Sufi
    shrines in India—including the tomb of Mu'in ad-Din Chishti in Ajmer, central Rajasthan. In
    response, the silsila transferred the 'urs of Muin ad-Din to the shrine of Baba Farid in
    Pakpattan" (p. 215, folio 207). **The spelling shifts from "Mu'in ad-Din" to "Muin ad-Din" inside
    that one sentence; see Doubts.**
  - **What the Diwan distributes at the ʿurs.** "the Diwan mounted a large stage in the center of the
    courtyard to distribute saffron-soaked strips of cloth known as pechas to the assembled masses"
    (pp. 37–38, folios 27–28).
  - **The order's own conduct at the tomb, observed.** Male disciples, "Dressed in white shalwar
    kamiz and following the strict rules of etiquette and decorum (adab)", "formed a series of
    straight rows parallel to the main door of the saint's tomb" and "sat or stood in quiet
    contemplation (muraqaba) as the female disciples looked down from the rooftop balcony above"
    (p. 37, folio 27). At the ʿurs "most murids offer communal prayers inside the dargah" (p. 218,
    folio 210); group muraqaba facing the tomb "is a regular practice, for example, in Pakpattan
    where following communal prayers disciples sit together in silent contemplation, facing the
    saint's tomb" (p. 218, folio 210). **Zikr is deliberately suppressed here:** "Disciples generally
    avoid zikr in Karachi and Pakpattan altogether, though it is performed at the 'urs in Allahabad"
    (p. 218, folio 210), explained by a senior disciple as "At Pakpattan we do not do anything extra
    because Baba Sahab's influence is so great and so powerful" (p. 218, folio 210).
  - **Women's access is the most restricted of the order's three Pakistani shrines.** A female
    disciple from Peshawar: "It is not like this at Pakpattan Sharif . . . At Pakpattan Sharif women
    cannot even enter [the inner sanctum of the tomb]. The most you can do is touch and kiss the
    threshold" (p. 219, folio 211), contrasted with "Hazrat Wahid Bakhsh's mazar [in Allahabad]"

- **Legends and miracles (karamat)**
  - **Salvation through the doorway**, in the 1833 account: "all who pass through this doorway are
    considered saved from the fires of perdition"; "A superlative heaven is allotted to those who are
    first to enter the tomb on the day mentioned" (p. 36, folio 26).
  - **Pakpattan grants every request** — what the Malaysian disciple was told before going: "I was
    told that in Pakpattan nobody's requests are rejected, so I packed my bags and followed the

  - **A collapse and illumination inside the shrine at the ʿurs, 1979.** "Suddenly, I received some
    illuminations [kashfiyyat]. I fell down … I sensed that I was unconscious, but I could still hear

    Captain Wahid Bakhsh's hut, outside the shrine"; "Captain Sahab came and blew his breath on me"

    concentration. You are open to these things, but you don't have a filter. A shaykh is a filter."

    three decades ago" (p. 158, folio 150). **He also met, inside the shrine, "a dervish, an English
    dervish, walking around inside the shrine, searching for a shaykh"** — unnamed, and not connected
    by the book to Shahidullah Faridi (p. 157, folio 149); see Doubts.
  - **The dream at his shrine that authorised a shaykh, 1955.** Shahidullah "had a vivid dream in
    which his status as khalifa was confirmed by the patron saint himself" (p. 76, folio 66); the
    dream is dated in the narration to "the ninth of Muharram", with **no year attached to that
    phrase** (p. 76, folio 66). In a disciple's telling: "He saw a vision that he was standing in the
    shrine (darbar) of Hazrat Baba Sahab, may God have mercy upon him"; "Baba Sahab himself was

    your disciples (murids). Make us your disciples!'" (pp. 76–77, folios 66–67), and Baba Sahab
    orders "You make these people murids!", Shahidullah objects "My shaykh has not allowed me", and
    the dead Zauqi Shah appears and answers "Of course I've allowed you!" (pp. 76–77, folios 66–67;
    narrated May 21, 2001, Karachi). The author's framing: "This paradigmatic experience is not
    recorded in any text, but during my interviews I heard it narrated—with slight variations—on
    numerous occasions" (p. 76, folio 66).
  - **The saint gives a living shaykh his name.** "Soon after he officially dropped his surname,
    Lennard, and adopted the moniker 'Faridi' to mark his special relationship with the saint in
    Pakpattan" (p. 77, folio 67, interview September 2, 2001, Karachi); the book calls Baba Farid
    Shahidullah Faridi's "namesake" (p. 79, folio 69).
  - **He met his own wife in a dream**, reported as a hagiographic parallel drawn by disciples:
    "Several disciples recounted a similar story about the saint Baba Farid Ganj-i Shakkar who also
    met his future wife in a prescient dream" (p. 79, folio 69).
  - **He claims a child as his daughter.** A closed door to Zauqi Shah's room "was a sign that some
    saint, or even the Holy Prophet… was present there"; the child Ammi Jan opened it and "the
    saintly person with him was sitting there and said, 'No, no. Let her come. She is my daughter.'
    And then he put her on his lap. Afterward Hazrat Sahab told Ammi Jan that the man was Baba Sahab
    [the saint, Farid ad-Din]" (p. 80, folio 70). The author: "While this intriguing hagiographic
    anecdote is not recorded in any text, it too is now integrated into the contemporary silsila's
    collective memory" (p. 80, folio 70). Her later ziyarat: "Whenever she would visit his mazar
    [Pakpattan Sharif], there was a wonderful scene. People would gather, and there were such
    blessings. She attracted a lot of women; no queen could receive such a reception" (p. 80,
    folio 70, interview April 29, 2001, Bahawalpur). **The bracketed "[Pakpattan Sharif]" is the
    book's own editorial insertion.**
  - **He speaks from the tomb.** "Once I was passing along the street near Baba Sahab [the tomb of
    Baba Farid ad-Din Ganj-i Shakkar in Pakpattan]" — Wahid Bakhsh thought, "'Baba Sahab has not left
    a large number of books for us to read.' At that moment a voice came to me; it was the voice of
    Baba Sahab. He said, 'You are my books!'" (p. 174, folio 166, narrated April 30, 2001,
    Bahawalpur). The author's gloss: the episode "conveys a simple but clear message", illuminating
    "the superiority of experiential wisdom over abstract, theoretical book knowledge" (p. 175,
    folio 167).
  - **Knowledge at the gate surpasses the universities.** Of Shahidullah Faridi at the bihishti
    darwaza: "Hazrat Shahidullah Faridi was at the shrine of Baba Farid Shakar Ganj [at Pakpattan].
    It was the time of the 'urs, and people were coming out of the Gate of Heaven [bihishti
    darwaza]"; "These are the real universities, these places. Real knowledge is given to you through
    the attention of the saint, through inspiration." (p. 220, folio 212)
  - **He appears in a woman's visions**, in a list of Maqsuda Begam's "spiritual encounters with the
    Prophet Muhammad, Khizr, Jesus, Baba Farid ad-Din Ganj-i Shakkar, Muhammad Ali Jinnah, Shah Sayyid
    Waris Hasan, and Zauqi Shah, among others" (p. 93, folio 83).
  - **He is one of five figures contemplated daily.** Murids perform muraqaba-i shaykh on "Hazrat
    Khwaja Mu'in ad-Din Chishti Ajmeri, Baba Farid ad-Din, al-Hujwiri, Muhammad Zauqi Shah, and Shah
    Sayyid Waris Hasan for fifteen to thirty minutes on a daily basis" (p. 206, folio 198).
- **Significance**
  - "Sufi shrines such as Pakpattan serve as vital centers of popular piety—nodal points of local
    identity and culture." (p. 47, folio 37) "contemporary Chishti Sabiri disciples champion and

  - **Richard Eaton on the shrine, quoted at length by the book:** "The shrine of Baba Farid in
    Pakpattan provides a striking example of how Islam, the religion par excellence 'of the Book,'
    has been in one instance mediated among common villagers most of whom were illiterate" — the
    shrine "displayed, in short, the Court of God", and Baba Farid was the devotee's "friend in
    court" (p. 35, folio 25).
  - **Index locators for the person**, printed folios 4, 15, 19, 25–6, 32, 66–7, 69–70, 71, 83, 114,

### data-darbar — Data Darbar (ʿAli Hujwiri, Data Ganj Bakhsh), Lahore

The book's second shrine, present in eight chunks, and the only shrine outside its own silsila that
the order permits its disciples to visit. The author's forms of the name, all as printed:
"'Ali Hujwiri (d. 1072)" (p. 72, folio 62), "al-Hujwiri" (p. 111, folio 102),
"Sayyid 'Ali al-Hujwiri" (p. 123, folio 114), "Sayyid 'Ali ibn 'Uthman al-Hujwiri (d. 1074)"
(p. 171, folio 163; p. 206, folio 198; p. 263, folio not stated), "Hujwiri, 'Ali (d. 1074)" in
the index (p. 276, folio 270), and in the disciples'
address "Hazrat Data Sahab", "Data Sahab", "Data Ganj Bakhsh" (p. 207, folio 199).

- **Saint**
  - **Death year — the book prints 1072 once and 1074 four times, and the archive row records 1072.**
    "'Ali Hujwiri (d. 1072)" (p. 72, folio 62); "(d. 1074)" at p. 171 folio 163, p. 206 folio 198,
    p. 263 folio not stated, and in the index at p. 276 folio 270. **Digits survive in this text
    layer and "1074" sits inside parentheses, not glued to a word, so it is not an endnote number:
    the discrepancy is the book's own.** All are kept; see Doubts.
  - **The moniker is glossed by the book.** "As the saint's moniker, Data Ganj Bakhsh ('The Lavish
    Giver'), suggests al-Hujwiri's tomb is renowned as a place of intense power—an open and
    accessible outlet for spiritual grace." (p. 207, folio 199)
  - **Silsila — never stated in the book's own voice: (not stated).** What the book gives instead is
    an honorary affiliation: "al-Hujwiri is remembered as an honorary Chishti Sabiri, and his shrine
    is a focus of local ritual practice." (p. 207, folio 199) Wahid Bakhsh's letter puts the same
    thing carefully: "Although Hazrat Data Sahab, may God have mercy upon him, is not in the line of
    our silsila directly, he is great enough to know what you need. Moreover, he is very much
    associated with the masha'ikh of our silsila. He is very kind to us." (p. 207, folio 199)
  - **He is one of five figures contemplated daily** in muraqaba-i shaykh, with Mu'in ad-Din Chishti
    Ajmeri, Baba Farid ad-Din, Muhammad Zauqi Shah and Shah Sayyid Waris Hasan, "for fifteen to
    thirty minutes on a daily basis" (p. 206, folio 198).
  - **As author — the book's dominant register for him.** *Kashf al-mahjub* is called "the famous
    Persian treatise on Sufism written by the eleventh-century master 'Ali Hujwiri (d. 1072)" (p. 72,
    folio 62), "al-Hujwiri's famous tome titled Kashf al-mahjub [The Unveiling of the Concealed]"
    (p. 111, folio 102), and "the Persian classic by al-Hujwiri" (p. 173, folio 165). He heads a list
    of five premodern authors whose "writings … are especially popular" among today's disciples, with
    al-Ghazali (d. 1111), Ibn al-'Arabi (d. 1240), al-Suhrawardi (d. 1234) and Rumi (d. 1273)
    (p. 171, folio 163), and appears again among "Imam Ghazali, Ibn Arabi, Junaid Baghdadi, Jalal
    ad-Din Rumi, and Sayyid 'Ali al-Hujwiri" (p. 123, folio 114). **The book's title glosses for the
    same work differ: "[The Unveiling of the Secrets]" (p. 72, folio 62) against "[The Unveiling of
    the Concealed]" (p. 111, folio 102) — both are the book's own brackets.**
  - **His book's place in the order's prescribed reading order is third of four:** after Wahid
    Bakhsh's *Islamic Sufism* ("the primer and we all read that") and *A Guide for Spiritual
    Aspirants*, and before *Immense Power Potential of Pakistan* — "Then you go still deeper with
    Kashf al-mahjub [a translation of the Persian classic by al-Hujwiri]. That delves into all the
    deeper levels of Sufism" (p. 173, folio 165, senior Malaysian disciple, October 1, 2001, Kuala
    Lumpur), the whole run being "like the journey itself: from fana' … to baqa'" (p. 173,
    folio 165).
  - **Three editions of his book in the Chishti Sabiri economy, all Wahid Bakhsh's.** Ch 2 n. 74:
    "Wahid Bakhsh Sial Rabbani translated Kashf al-mahjub from its original Persian into Urdu. See
    Sayyid 'Ali ibn 'Uthman al-Hujwiri, Kashf al-mahjub, Urdu translation from Persian with
    commentary by Wahid Bakhsh Sial Rabbani (Lahore: al-Faisal Publishers, 1995). The text… has also
    recently been translated into English and published by disciples in Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia. See
    Sayyid 'Ali ibn 'Uthman al-Hujwiri, The Kashful Mahjub ('Unveiling the Veiled'): The Earliest
    Persian Treatise on Sufism, English translation with commentary by Wahid Bakhsh Sial Rabbani
    (Kuala Lumpur: A.S. Noordeen, 1997)" (p. 246, folio 239) — **an Urdu and an English version by
    the same shaykh, two years and two cities apart.** A third entry, filed under its title with **no
    author named**, is Reynold A. Nicholson's translation: "The Kashf al-Mahjub: The Oldest Persian
    Treatise on Sufism. Translated by Reynold A. Nicholson. Leiden: E. J. Brill, 1911." (p. 264,
    folio 258). It was Nicholson's translation, not the Persian, that is credited with sparking
    Shahidullah Faridi's interest in Sufism (p. 72, folio 62) — **the edition and year of the copy he
    read are (not stated)**.
- **Shrine / Darbar**
  - **Place, given once, in one clause with the death year.** "the shrine of Sayyid 'Ali ibn 'Uthman
    al-Hujwiri (d. 1074) in the old city of Lahore" (p. 206, folio 198). Elsewhere it is "the tomb
    complex of his patron saint 'Ali Hujwiri" (p. 75, folio 65), "Hujwiri's shrine" (p. 75, folio 65)
    and "al-Hujwiri's tomb" (p. 207, folio 199). **The book never uses the name "Data Darbar"**, and
    the epithet "Data Ganj Bakhsh" appears only as the saint's moniker (p. 207, folio 199), never as
    the shrine's name: (not stated). **No architecture, no dome, no building date, no custodian, no
    Auqaf presence and no visitor figure anywhere: all (not stated).**
  - **A burial inside the tomb complex, and its economy.** Shahidullah Faridi's elder brother Faruq
    Ahmad "died in Lahore on February 13, 1945 after a prolonged bout of pneumonia. The details of
    his tragic death and burial within the tomb complex of his patron saint 'Ali Hujwiri are outlined
    in Tarbiyat al-'ushshaq" (p. 75, folio 65). **Burial in the compound had a price the mourners
    could not pay:** "According to the text, there were insufficient funds to fulfill Faruq Ahmad's
    desire to be buried in the compound of Hujwiri's shrine, and an alternative location was hastily
    arranged. As the burial was about to commence, however, a stranger suddenly arrived and announced
    that he would" (p. 75, folio 65) — the quotation ends there. **The sentence is cut by a chunk
    boundary and what the stranger did is not in the notes: (not stated).** The outcome is
    nonetheless stated flatly eight
    chapters later: the brothers "converted to Islam after reading al-Hujwiri's classic text Kashf
    al-mahjub" and "to this day, Faruq Ahmed lies buried in a small, subterranean room within his
    patron saint's tomb complex" (p. 207, folio 199) — **note the spelling "Faruq Ahmed" here against
    "Faruq Ahmad" at p. 75.** A related detail: workers later moving his grave "discovered that his
    body was still fresh and had not decomposed—one of the well-known tropes of sainthood", and
    "Faruq Ahmad's tomb was indeed left undisturbed" (p. 76, folio 66). The funeral itself is located
    at Lahore: "Throughout the time of the funeral, a light rain fell from that cloud of mercy only
    above that spot in Lahore" (p. 75, folio 65).
- **Practices and events**
  - **The order's single named exception to its own rule.** "Do not go to any mazar which is not in
    our silsila" (p. 207, folio 199), and: "A notable exception is made, however, for the shrine of
    Sayyid 'Ali ibn 'Uthman al-Hujwiri (d. 1074) in the old city of Lahore" (p. 206, folio 198).
  - **A weekly pilgrimage pattern, with days.** "Disciples who live in Lahore regularly visit
    al-Hujwiri's shrine on **Thursday evenings** before attending the communal halqa-i zikr, and they
    often repeat the journey on **Sundays** as well." (p. 207, folio 199) **Who founded it:** "it was
    Shaykh Wahid Bakhsh who established this weekly pilgrimage, instigating a new ritual practice
    that Chishti Sabiris in Lahore continue today" (p. 208, folio 200).
  - **The order does NOT attend the ʿurs.** A senior Pakistani disciple: "Murids do not go to the
    mazar [of al-Hujwiri] collectively. They go privately or as a family, but not as a group. We do
    not even go for the 'urs [death anniver-sary]. We only go for one-day attendance." (p. 208,
    folio 200) **No ʿurs date, month or duration for this shrine anywhere in the book: (not
    stated).**
  - **A ritual differentiation recorded as the disciples' own.** "When we go to shaykhs other than
    our silsila, we recite Fatiha twice… But if you go to Data Sahab, you recite it only once because
    you are in his silsila. This is done out of respect." (p. 208, folio 200)
  - **What a disciple does at the tomb**, a female disciple's routine: "You should say, in your
    heart, 'I am sitting in front of Data Sahab. Data Sahab is sitting there, and I am sitting here.'
    Then you just close your eyes, and take long, slow, deep breaths… When I go to Data Sahab I sit
    there for ten to fifteen minutes of meditation and receive an answer to all my problems."
    (p. 208, folio 200, October 31, 2000, Lahore)
- **Legends and miracles (karamat)**
  - **The grant of South Asia at this tomb.** "According to tradition Mu'in ad-Din Chishti Ajmeri
    (d. 1236), the first Indian Chishti master, was granted the dominion (wilayat) of all of South
    Asia, for himself and his successors, at al-Hujwiri's tomb." (p. 207, folio 199)
  - **Data Sahab appears and speaks.** "When you visualize Data Sahab, you see Data Sahab. Many times
    he will appear to you and give you messages." (p. 208, folio 200) Murids "compare the spiritual
    energy (faizan) that radiates from the saint's tomb during muraqaba to an intense electric
    current surging through the body" (p. 208, folio 200); contemplation there is "an intimate
    conversation between the Sufi disciple and the patron saint, alive in his tomb" (p. 207,
    folio 199).
- **Significance**
  - **The other Lahore shrines, as the order sees them — and none is named.** "There are many mazars
    of unknown shaykhs in Lahore. Some of them are qalandars [a roaming sect of antinomian Sufis] and
    can only give their visitors what they have—that is, they make them qalandars as well. In our
    silsila we avoid this." (p. 207, folio 199) **No such mazar is named, so none is mapped from this
    sentence.**
  - **Index locators**, printed folios 62, 65–6, 102, 114, 163, 165, 198, 199–201 (p. 276,
    folio 270). **No `see also` is printed for him**, and he is not cross-referenced from `shrines`,
    `mazars` or `ziyarat` (pp. 280–281, folios 274–275).

### golra-sharif — Golra Sharif (Pir Sayyid Meher Ali Shah), Islamabad

Three mentions, all short, but one of them names both the man and the place.

- **The firmest mention.** Ch 3 n. 13, first sentence: "On Sayyid Mehr Ali Shah of Golra, see
  especially Gilmartin, Empire and Islam, 58–59" (p. 249, folio 242). **This is the only place in the
  book that attaches him to Golra.** The note sits in a run naming three figures the author treats as
  the print-era precedents for Chishti Sabiri publishing — Mehr Ali Shah, Khwaja Hasan Nizami and
  Jama'at 'Ali Shah.
- **As a reformist comparison case, with dates and order.** "Other notable twentieth-century South
  Asian Sufi masters have pursued similar reformist agendas—the Chishti Nizami masters Sayyid Mehr
  Ali Shah **(1856–1937)** and Khwaja Hasan Nizami (1878–1955), for example, as well as the
  Naqshbandi activist, Pir Jama'at 'Ali Shah (1841–1951)." (p. 103, folio 94) Order as printed:
  **"Chishti Nizami"**. **The printed death year 1937 matches the archive row's `figure_died`
  exactly** — the only positive date agreement this book supplies for any Punjab or Islamabad row.
- **As a Punjabi poet in the qawwals' repertoire.** "Qawwals move fluidly between linguistic
  registers: from the Persian verses of Jami, Hafiz, and Rumi, to the Punjabi poetry of Mehr 'Ali
  Shah and Ghulam Farid, and the classical Hindi of Amir Khusrau, among others" (p. 229, folio 221).
- **Nothing else.** No shrine, no ʿurs, no custodian, no Rawalpindi/Islamabad location, no silsila
  chain: all **(not stated)**. The book spells him "Mehr Ali Shah" and "Mehr 'Ali Shah"; the archive
  row spells him "Meher Ali Shah" — recorded so the consolidator does not read them as two people.

### mithankot-kot-mithan — Mithankot (Kot Mithan), Khwaja Ghulam Farid

Three mentions. He is a **Primary Source author of the Chishti Sabiri canon**, not merely a literary
reference — which makes this the strongest of the book's short mappings after Golra.

- **The bibliography entry, with a death year that matches the archive exactly.** `Farid, Khwaja
  Ghulam (d. 1901). Maqabis al-majalis (compiled 1893–1901). Urdu translation from Persian by Wahid
  Bakhsh Sial Rabbani. Lahore: al-Faisal, 1979/1993.` (p. 263, folio not stated). **The printed death
  year 1901 matches the archive row's `figure_died`**, and the compilation window "1893–1901" is
  printed in full.
- **The body text gives a birth year the bibliography does not.** "the shaykh's lengthy introduction
  to the discourses (malfuzat) of the nineteenth-century Chishti master Ghulam Farid **(1844–1901)**
  titled Maqabis al-majalis [The Lessons of the Spiritual Assemblies]" (p. 223, folio 215). The dates
  and the word "Chishti" are the book's own.
- **A twentieth-century shaykh introduced his nineteenth-century text.** "See also Wahid Bakhsh's
  introduction to Khwaja Ghulam Farid (compiled 1893–1901), Maqabis al-majalis, Urdu translation from
  Persian by Wahid Bakhsh Sial Rabbani (Lahore: al-Faisal, 1979/1993), 239–242." (p. 261, folio 254)
  A chapter of the same work is named: "The Taste of Sama'" (Zauq-i sama'), at 131–212 — **but that
  endnote cites the work under a different author surname, "Faridi"** (p. 261, folio 254); see Doubts.

## Cross-cutting material

*This is the largest section in the file, because the book is an ethnography of a working order and
most of what it knows about shrines is institutional rather than hagiographic. Page references are

### Practices, institutions, economy

#### What a shrine is and what it does, in the book's general voice

- **Shrines as the everyday face of Sufism, and as women's space.** "At the level of everyday social
  practice, Sufism is often equated with 'popular' worship at shrines. In South Asia, the tombs of
  Sufi saints provide an alternative outlet for piety and pilgrimage, especially for women who are
  often marginalized from the public, gendered space of the mosque (figure I.1)." (p. 13, folio 2)
  The author's own caption for that photograph: "Here women dance in a state of ecstasy in front of
  the saint's tomb while a group of musicians (qawwals) perform to a crowd of pilgrims." (p. 13,
  folio 2) Source line: "Photograph by Robert Rozehnal" (p. 13, folio 2).
- **How dargahs come into being, and the one line on shrine economy.** "The posthumous reputation of
  many Chishti shaykhs often led to the development of elaborate shrine complexes (dargahs) where
  devotees continued to flock in search of spiritual power to alleviate their worldly troubles. As
  loci of sacred geography and **fonts for public social welfare**, these regional shrines continue
  to thrive as pilgrimage sites and symbols of local Muslim culture and identity." (p. 14, folio 3)
- **Khanaqah functions, historically.** Chishti shaykhs "established centers of learning and hospices
  (khanaqahs) that offered food and shelter for the wayfarers and mendicants who survived on their
  charity, solace for the pilgrims who visited them for spiritual blessings (baraka), and intensive
  spiritual training for their select disciples." (p. 14, folio 3)
- **A branch characteristic that cuts against shrine-building.** Sabiri shaykhs "stuck to more rural
  locales, made fewer public appearances, trained fewer devotees, wrote fewer books, and generally
  **avoided the construction of large shrine complexes (dargahs)**. As a result, much of the
  silsila's early history is elusive and opaque, shrouded in legend." (p. 14, folio 3)
- **The saint as a renewable source.** "For the faithful who flock to Sufi shrines on pilgrimage, the
  wali Allah represents a tangible and renewable source of blessing" (p. 53, folio 43); the Sufi
  saint "is unbound from the laws of nature by virtue of his intimacy with God" and is "empowered to
  perform miracles … empowered to mediate the course of destiny" (p. 53, folio 43, quoting Vincent
  Cornell). "The friends of God continue to progress even after death. There is no limit to the
  divine essence [dhat], so there is no limit to progress" — Zauqi Shah (p. 255, folio 248); and a
  disciple's version: "The Sufi saint is like a sword unsheathed upon his death… In life they must
  strictly follow the shari'a. But once they have passed the threshold, God puts no limits on their
  powers" (October 22, 2000, Lahore) (p. 169, folio 161).
- **The book's own index glosses**, which are its working definitions: "sajjada nishin (hereditary
  shrine custodian)" (p. 279, folio 273); "'urs (marriage; death anniversary of a Sufi saint)"
  (p. 280, folio 274); "ziyarat (pilgrimage to Sufi shrines)" (p. 281, folio 275); "mazars (Sufi
  tombs) … see also dargahs; shrines" (p. 277, folio 271); "karamat (saintly miracles) … see also
  miracles" (p. 277, folio 271).

#### The state, the Auqaf and shrine governance

- **The legal instrument, dated.** "The state's hegemony over religious spaces and local religious
  leaders was formally institutionalized with the passage of the **West Pakistan Waqf Properties
  Ordinance of 1959**. This act provided legal precedent for the state to appropriate control over
  religious endowments and to manage shrines, mosques, and other properties dedicated to religious
  purposes" (p. 33, folio 23). Its result: "a further dissolution of the independence and political
  power of both the hereditary Sufi families and the local 'ulama" (pp. 33–34, folios 23–24).
- **What a custodial family says the takeover did.** A disciple writes that custodians were "coerced
  into surrendering the management of the shrine" to the Awqaf Department, an act that "dragged an
  institution of an essentially spiritual nature for reasons of revenue collection onto the mundane
  plane of government administration" (p. 41, folio 31), breaking "a six-hundred-and-fifty-year-old
  tradition" (p. 41, folio 31).
- **British colonial shrine policy.** Ewing, quoted: "The hereditary saints [sajjada nishin] were
  seen primarily in terms of their economic power. British policy, especially in the Punjab and Sind,
  was to treat them in essentially the same way that landlords and tribal leaders were treated"
  (p. 33, folio 23). Gilmartin, paraphrased: under colonial rule the shrine's position "as a hinge
  between the culture of the locality and the larger Muslim community" held while "the role of the
  saint's living heirs was increasingly politicized" (p. 33, folio 23). And, directly: "At times, the
  British interfered directly in the administration of local shrines such as `Pakattan`
  [text-layer?: Pakpattan], going so far as to mediate succession disputes in colonial courts"
  (p. 33, folio 23). Further reading the book names: Sarah F. D. Ansari, *Sufi Saints and State
  Power: The Pirs of Sind, 1843–1947* (Lahore: Vanguard Books, 1992); David Gilmartin, *Empire and
  Islam: Punjab and the Making of Pakistan* (Berkeley, 1988), 39–72, 205–224 (p. 241, folio 234);
  Katherine P. Ewing, "The Politics of Sufism: Redefining the Saints of Pakistan," *Journal of Asian
  Studies* XLII, No. 2 (February 1983): 253 (p. 241, folio 234).
- **Shrines as instruments of rural development policy.** Nasr, quoted: "By taking over the
  management of the shrines, state leaders were able to use them to propagate a new interpretation of
  Sufism and rural Islam as compatible with development . . . rural religious festivals were used as
  venues for agricultural and industrial fairs. By becoming the keeper of shrines, the state was able
  to find a presence in rural areas, which was otherwise closed to it by the landed elite" (p. 34,
  folio 24).
- **Sufi leaders as state agents.** "Sufi leaders are co-opted into the ideology and institutions of
  the state as political agents and power brokers, mediators in regionalized networks of local
  identity and power" (p. 47, folio 37).
- **The limit of state capture.** Sufi knowledge "survived and thrived beyond the purview of the
  state" because it is "Communicated orally via the intimate master-disciple (pir-murid)
  relationship and experienced primarily in private ritual arenas" (p. 34, folio 24).
- **Crowd control as a state function.** Pilgrims "waited for hours in the long lines weaving through
  the town" (p. 36, folio 26); on police conduct, pilgrims "are frightened of the police lathi-charge
  so at the slightest chance they tend to make a dash to the entrance" (p. 39, folio 29); police were
  "already stretched thin by the state's concerted efforts to prevent sectarian violence throughout
  the district during the month of Muharram" (p. 39, folio 29).
- **The state destroys shrines elsewhere in the order's world.** In Malaysia, "instead of celebrating
  they destroy the mazars. One of the largest ones, in fact, was demolished by the state government"
  (p. 211, folio 203); murids there "maintain a low profile" and "keep quiet because of the
  government" (p. 211, folio 203).
- **Election arithmetic, which the book uses to date the Islamist ascendancy.** "The national
  elections of October 2002"; the Muttahida Majlis-i Amal (MMA) captured "19 percent of the seats in
  the National Assembly, a controlling majority of the Provincial Assembly of the Northwest Frontier
  Province, and an upper hand as the largest party in Baluchistan" (p. 32, folio 22).
- **The order's own political proposal on shrines.** Wahid Bakhsh: "Our constitution should permit
  all sects to practice religion the way they want. The Government should enforce only those clauses
  on which all the sects have consensus… Peripheral or controversial issues like **visits to the
  shrines, sama' … ta'ziya … and milad** … should be left to the individual sects" (p. 128,
  folio 119). **"public celebration's" is printed with the stray apostrophe in that passage; see
  Doubts.**

#### ʿUrs: definition, calendar, crowds and economy

- **The book's definition.** "'Urs literally means 'marriage.' In the context of Sufi practice, it
  serves as a metaphor for the wali Allah's final union with God." (p. 214, folio 206) Its hadith
  warrant, as Wahid Bakhsh gives it through Hajji Imdad Allah: "Sleep with the sleep of a bridegroom
  ('arus)." (p. 260, folio 253) And its benefit: "because the departed soul himself is the recipient
  of enhanced favor and blessings characteristic of this occasion, he is in a bountiful mood and
  bestows more and more favors on his people" (p. 214, folio 206).
- **The economic character, stated generally.** "Throughout South Asia, 'urs celebrations draw
  massive crowds to the shrines of Sufi saints. **These festivals combine religious fervor, public
  spectacle, and a bustling economic exchange.**" (p. 214, folio 206) "The qawwals represent just a
  single node in a vast web of economic networks and minimarkets that surround the 'urs pilgrimage
  and its public rituals" (p. 229, folio 221).
- **Offerings named, and repudiated by this order.** "The festive part of 'urs has existed for a very
  long time, with people offering ritual gifts [**nazrana**], flowers, and cloths to cover the grave
  [**chadar**]. But the kind of attendance we have is not for nazrana. It is only for the company of
  the shakyh [text-layer?: shaykh] at the mazar." (p. 216, folio 208)
- **Cost of attendance, as an economic fact.** "They [Chishti Sabiri shaykhs] encourage people who
  fly from Canada, or even Jeddah to come and attend the 'urs. It is that valuable. People are
  encouraged to part with a lot of money." (p. 216, folio 208) "Many disciples accept significant
  personal and financial sacrifice in order to participate in these annual events." (p. 216,
  folio 208)
- **The order's annual cycle is four ʿurs, and the book calls it a portable lodge.** "Four times a
  year, disciples travel and live together en masse for short but intensive periods of spiritual
  immersion, **creating a serial khanaqah** during the annual 'urs pilgrimages" (p. 233, folio 225);
  "For today's Chishti Sabiris, 'urs effectively serves as a serial khanaqah." (p. 215, folio 207)
  The retreats are "weeklong" (p. 217, folio 209); "Everyone receives a short refresher course, six
  or seven days" (p. 216, folio 208); "It's like going to a monastery and staying there for a week"
  (p. 215, folio 207); "The living conditions are austere but comfortable" (p. 217, folio 209).
  Attendance is constrained by work — "I missed three or four 'urs because of my job" (March 22,
  2001, Islamabad) (p. 176, folio 168).
- **Dress and ablution before entering any shrine.** "Before entering any shrine, murids perform
  ablutions and dress in clean clothes. Both men and women typically wear simple, white shalwar
  kamiz. The men cover their heads with a skull cap or a modest head covering; the women cover their
  heads with a scarf, or dupatta." (p. 217, folio 209)
- **Gender segregation at the ʿurs, with a warning that it varies.** "The Chishti Sabiri living
  quarters and ritual spaces are segregated by gender during 'urs. While men and women travel
  together, once they reach the shrine they separate for the duration of the spiritual retreat. **The
  access for women at the mazar varies according to location.**" (p. 219, folio 211)
- **A madmen-and-clowns theory of crowd behaviour at shrines.** Wahid Bakhsh: "they are there to
  counterbalance the enormous torrents of blessings, charms and fascinations which, if left
  unimpaired by the clowns, would turn people mad by their intensity." (p. 217, folio 209)
- **Partition as the cause of a changed ritual geography.** "The redrawing of South Asia's
  geopolitical boundaries in 1947 reconstituted the nation and citizenship, mandating new maps and
  new passports. With the emergence of an independent (and Islamic) Pakistan, the absence of the
  Jain, Sikh, and Hindu communities profoundly altered traditional cultural practices and local
  religious identities." (p. 215, folio 207)

#### Ziyarat: the rules of visiting a tomb

- **Prescribed in print by a shaykh.** Wahid Bakhsh in *Islamic Sufism*: "The first and foremost duty
  of the visitor is to have a bath, change clothes, use perfumes and enter the shrine with love and
  reverence for the saint… Next he has to recite the Fatiha… and sit down facing the grave with
  closed eyes… one must sit, or stand if there is no room to sit, for about **half an hour** with
  full attention focused on him." (pp. 206–207, folios 198–199)
- **Ziyarat defended against Islamist attack, and conditioned.** Wahid Bakhsh "comments on the
  efficacy of visiting the graves of deceased spiritual masters (ziyarat)––a ritual act widely
  condemned by Islamists. Here he evokes numerous hadith in defense of the practice" (p. 119,
  folio 110); "Visiting shrines, Wahid Bakhsh maintains, is a powerful catalyst to spiritual
  development but only when complemented by a vigilant adherence to discipline and decorum (adab)"
  (p. 119, folio 110). In his own words: "The amount of inspiration you get from shaykh of the mazar
  very much depends on the effort which you put in at home during the year … **work at home and
  inspiration at the shrine are interdependent and supplement each other**" (p. 119, folio 110).
- **Blessing is stronger at the tomb than at home.** "In both places you receive faizan, blessings
  come down. But the blessings you get at the mazar itself you do not receive at your house. It is
  much stronger at the mazar because the Shaykh intensifies it" (p. 231, folio 223).
- **The order's insularity is the book's key finding about its shrine practice.** "As a rule, Chishti
  Sabiri murids are **discouraged from visiting the tombs of Sufi saints outside the silsila**.
  Though they acknowledge that any saint's tomb is potentially a conduit for Divine inspiration,
  Chishti Sabiri attitudes toward sacred space are remarkably insular." (p. 207, folio 199) The sole
  named exception is al-Hujwiri's shrine in Lahore.
- **A three-node sacred geography, then a fourth overseas.** Pakpattan at the centre, with "The
  growing shrine complexes of Shahidullah Faridi in Karachi and Wahid Bakhsh Sial Rabbani in
  Allahabad… now marked as Chishti Sabiri domain as well" (p. 206, folio 198); and "the expansion of
  Chishti Sabiri spiritual domain is not limited to Pakistan… a Sufi shrine in Malaysia now provides
  an alternative outlet for pilgrimage and worship" (p. 209, folio 201).
- **Deobandi fatwas against shrine visiting are a live conflict**, met with the argument that the
  fatwa "is for those who are completely illiterate" and does not bind "the people of distinction
  [khawas]" (p. 213, folio 205). A murid's historical explanation: "At the time, the British were
  trying to exploit Sufism, saying that it was not Islamic. So in the original school of Deoband,
  they said, 'No, do not go to mazars because the Hindus also go there.'" (p. 214, folio 206) And the
  order's own placement: "Today we go to the Sufi shrines [mazars] like the Barelwis, but we are
  neither Deobandis nor Barelwis. We simply are following the way of our spiritual masters" (May 21,
  2001, Karachi) (p. 46, folio 36).
- **Burial beside a master as a sold or allotted plot.** "An adjacent graveyard contains plots for
  silsila members who wish to be buried near their spiritual master" (p. 78, folio 68); and at an
  unnamed shrine, the practice of arranging to "provide a plot within the mazar" (p. 76, folio 66).
  **Graves are moved for infrastructure:** "In interviews, I was told that when an underground
  parking garage was constructed at the shrine during the 1980s, many such tombs were exhumed and
  relocated" (p. 76, folio 66) — **the shrine is not named: (not stated); see Doubts.** A single
  grave can be kept by one person rather than an institution: Faruq Ahmad's tomb "is maintained by an
  elderly devotee and frequently visited by Chishti Sabiri murids" (p. 76, folio 66).

#### Sama' and qawwali: where the money and the control are

- **Where and when.** "The Chishti Sabiri musical assemblies are usually held on the night of a Sufi
  saint's death anniversary, most often within an enclosed ritual space adjacent to the tomb"
  (p. 224, folio 216), with access restricted: "With rare exception, only a select, inner circle of
  disciples are allowed to participate." (p. 224, folio 216)
- **Performers and instruments.** "the singers—professional musicians known as qawwals
  ('reciters')—perform poetic verses (kalam), accompanied by the rhythm of hand clapping, drums
  (tabla), and the harmonium" (p. 224, folio 216).
- **Seating is hierarchy.** "This spatial layout places the Sufi master at the very center of the
  ritual action, even as it confines the singers to the periphery" (p. 225, folio 217); "The most
  senior murids position themselves nearest the shaykh, while others take seats in the front or back
  of the rows according to their relative status" (p. 225, folio 217). "Female disciples typically
  sit together in rows directly behind the shaykh, divided off from the central space by a thin,
  white sheet" (p. 225, folio 217) — "physically present, but sequestered, silent, and invisible",
  which the author marks as "in sharp contrast to the dynamics of everyday experience where many
  women play integral roles in the Chishti Sabiri order's social and ritual life" (p. 225, folio 217).
- **Payment is routed through the shaykh — the sharpest economic rule in the book.** Zauqi Shah's
  rules for the assembly: "Whatever gifts they give [for the qawwals] should be presented in front of
  the master of the assembly [i.e., the shaykh]. **It is against etiquette to give it directly to the
  qawwals**"; also, everyone performs ablutions and sits cross-legged, no looking around, no talking
  or prayer beads, those in ecstasy stay seated and if one stands all stand, and "those who have
  reached perfection are exempt from these rules" (p. 226, folio 218).
- **The qawwal's paradoxical standing.** "An accomplished qawwal is valued—and financially
  rewarded—for his musical and poetic talents" (p. 229, folio 221), yet "As paid service
  professionals, however, they are simultaneously (and paradoxically) held in low esteem" (p. 229,
  folio 221). Qureshi is quoted on why: "the only way to control the Qawwal's musical power over his
  audience is to give his audience religiously legitimized social and economic power over him"
  (p. 229, folio 221). From a murid: "They must come to us for the blessings of our shaykhs and not
  for the money. **They never get much money from us**" (p. 230, folio 222); and, from the other
  side, "The qawwals only perform for money. They only want to play the songs that people will pay
  money to hear" (p. 230, folio 222).
- **Policing of performers, and expulsion.** "Loud drumming and theatrical gestures are discouraged …
  pandering to popular tastes by playing 'film qawwali' is strictly prohibited. **Singers who violate
  these rules are immediately ushered out of the assembly and not invited to return**" (p. 230,
  folio 222). Senior murids "assist the shaykh in controlling the mahfil-i sama', selecting and
  guiding the qawwals' performance" (p. 230, folio 222), so that "Chishti Sabiris effectively
  maintain a monopoly as the gatekeepers of tradition" (p. 231, folio 223).
- **Programme order, tied to the stages of suluk.** Qawwali "should commence with poems in honor of
  the Prophet [na't sharif]", then "songs in praise of the saints of the [Chishti Sabiri] order
  [manqabat]", then "words of love", then kalam of annihilation [muraqaba-i fana'], then kalam of
  servitude; the singers must not go back a stage and must repeat a couplet that has sent a salik
  into wajd until the state passes (pp. 227–228, folios 219–220). The stake is stated: if the couplet
  is not repeated "he could die under certain circumstances" (p. 228, folio 220), and Zauqi Shah's
  warning "invokes the memory of Shaykh Qutb ad-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki (d. 1235)… who died in a state of
  prolonged spiritual rapture while listening to sama'" (p. 228, folio 220).
- **Languages of the repertoire.** Persian, Punjabi and classical Hindi (p. 229, folio 221); "Apart
  from Farsi, you have kalam in Saraiki, Hindi, Sindhi, and Punjabi. **Urdu has not been selected by
  our saints for this purpose.** In our mahfil-i sama', the qawwals are directed to focus on Farsi"
  (p. 229, folio 221).
- **Sama' distinguished from qawwali as a public genre.** "Qawwali is not sama'. With these Nusrat
  Fateh 'Ali Khan types there is always lots of talk about wine" (p. 230, folio 222); the author's
  own simile is "the mahfil-i sama' resembles a form of spiritual jazz—fluid, open, and responsive to
  audience feedback and participation" (p. 229, folio 221). And the shari'a defence: "In our mahfil-i
  sama' there is nothing that anyone could object to, even Wahhabis. We always keep shari'a intact."
  (p. 225, folio 217)
- **ʿUrs by cassette, for those who cannot travel.** Malaysian disciples "gather together in private
  homes in Kuala Lumpur … and, most intriguingly, listen to taped recordings of sama'" (p. 231,
  folio 223); "We play qawwali on the tape cassette. It is fantastic!" (p. 231, folio 223).
- **Qawwali against a state prohibition, 1951.** During the Hajj "the shaykh managed to arrange for a
  qawwali performance in a private home within the sacred precincts of Mecca—a practice prohibited by
  anti-Sufi, Wahhabi doctrine" (pp. 67–68, folios 57–58); "In Saudi Arabia, qawwali is prohibited,
  but Allah Almighty arranged for qawwali inside the noble sanctuary [haram sharif] in Mecca" (p. 68,
  folio 58).

#### The khanaqah is gone; what replaced it

- **Three generations of shaykhs kept no hospice.** "Unlike his Chishti ancestors, Zauqi Shah never
  lived in a traditional khanaqah… Instead, he met privately with disciples in his modest home in
  Karachi or traveled to meet them in person. Under his leadership the silsila's collective
  gatherings were limited to weekly zikr sessions (also in the Zauqi Shah's home), along with the
  annual pilgrimages to local shrines for the death anniversaries ('urs) of prominent Sufi saints"
  (p. 63, folio 53); "Just as Zauqi Shah before him, he never lived in a khanaqah. Instead, he held
  weekly zikr sessions and met individually with disciples at his home near the central train station
  in Karachi" (p. 78, folio 68); "Like Zauqi Shah and Shahidullah Faridi before him, Wahid Bakhsh
  never maintained a permanent hospice (khanaqah). In fact, he rarely stayed in one place for more
  than a few weeks" (p. 90, folio 80).
- **The institution is treated as defunct, by the disciples themselves.** Asked "if the khanaqah was
  an institution that could function in the modern world," a senior Pakistani male disciple answered,
  "I think that the saints have concluded that it is not possible now—because of the requirements of
  the times, the requirements of jobs, with everyone living such a fast pace of life" (p. 183,
  folio 175). The author: "the days of the traditional khanaqah—the premodern Sufi lodge memorialized
  in classical texts—have come and gone" (p. 183, folio 175).
- **The domestic substitute, described.** After Partition, Zauqi Shah's household and "several
  murids, their families shared a humble two-bedroom flat on the upper floors of a building near the
  main railway station"; "These cramped living quarters doubled as a space for spiritual practices,
  including the weekly performance of communal zikr" (p. 76, folio 66). Today, "Many murids have
  arranged a private meditation chamber in their homes" (p. 201, folio 193).
- **The halqa-i zikr, the order's weekly assembly.** Held "On Thursday evenings" (p. 201, folio 193).
  Venues are all private homes — Karachi (Shaykh Siraj 'Ali Muhammad, "a large zikr circle in a
  private home near the tomb complex of Shahidullah Faridi"), Lahore (a senior male murid's home, and
  separately a female disciple's women's session), Islamabad (discontinued), Kuala Lumpur (rotating
  hosts) — and **no shrine, khanqah or institutional building is named as a zikr venue** (p. 201,
  folio 193). Dress and seating: "clean clothes—typically white shalwar kamiz. Men wear a white
  prayer cap (topi) and women a thin head covering (dupatta)… disciples sit with legs crossed in
  ordered rows on the floor. The ritual space is gendered, with women sitting in rows behind the men.
  The lights are dimmed" (p. 201, folio 193). Order of service: maghrib prayers, then "the imam
  (prayer leader) turns from the qibla (prayer niche) to face the audience, and the zikr immediately
  begins," as call and response (p. 201, folio 193). **Duration and the ban on hospitality:** "The
  entire halqa-i zikr typically lasts about half an hour" (p. 203, folio 195); "That is why serving
  any food or even a cup of tea is strictly forbidden, because some people then might come just to
  socialize" (August 26, 2001, Lahore) (p. 203, folio 195) — **there is no langar.** And it has
  shrunk: "the length of halqa has shortened. It used to last well over an hour. Traditionally it has
  always held on Thursdays, except for the month of Ramadan when it used to be every day… The shaykhs
  also used to hold a stick" (September 1, 2001, Karachi) (p. 204, folio 196).
- **Langar appears exactly once, and at an Indian shrine.** "At Ajmer Sharif they often worked in the
  langar, serving food and water to impoverished pilgrims" (p. 74, folio 64, interview March 24,
  2001, Islamabad). **No langar at any Pakistani shrine is described anywhere in this book: (not
  stated).**

#### Zikr, muraqaba and the daily regimen

- **The written rule-book.** On receiving bay'at a disciple "receives a copy of the silsila's
  official handbook," ***Shajara tayyaba ma'a awrad aur waza'if***, compiled by Zauqi Shah; it "is
  distributed to all disciples and is **not available for public consumption**" (p. 194, folio 186).
  It contains the shajara "Beginning with the current teaching shaykh … back to the Prophet
  Muhammad" — one copy of it in verse — a list of general precepts (hidayat-i 'am) covering "the
  dietary practices, rules of decorum (adab), and supererogatory prayers (nawafil) required of each
  murid", and suras, du'a and durud for specific occasions (p. 194, folio 186). The daily round is
  "devotional exercises (awrad-i rozana), recitations for Friday prayers (ma'mulat-i jum'a
  al-mubarak), and annual recitations tied to the Muslim lunar calendar (ma'mulat-i salana)" (p. 195,
  folio 187).
- **Prescribed counts, as printed.** Zikr-i ism-i dhat begins "with five hundred, and then extend it
  up to as many as ten or twelve thousand… the minimum is reciting one thousand in a sitting. Most
  people do up to six or seven thousand, because that takes about half an hour" (p. 198, folio 190).
  The *Shajara tayyaba* sequence: "After a hundred strikes… Do this two hundred times. Then strike
  the heart four hundred times with only 'illa'llah.' After that recite 'Allahu Allah' six hundred
  times… Repeat this through the entire rosary [tasbih] [one thousand two hundred repetitions]. If
  time is short… recite 'Allah' with the voice onto the heart five hundred times." (p. 199, folio 191)
  **Every numeral in this passage should be checked against the page before it is quoted.**
- **Habs-i dam.** "complete submersion in water for prolonged periods of time, a method that is said
  to cool the 'heat' generated by intense zikr… known as a particular specialty of past Chishti
  Sabiri spiritual masters"; "generally, habs-i dam is performed while submerged in water during the
  winter… begins with the recitation of 'Allah' twenty one times and then gradually increases"
  (p. 199, folio 191). The author raises, cautiously, "a possible connection with Hindu yogic
  practices" through the concept of spiritual heat (tapas) (p. 199, folio 191).
- **A denominational marker.** Chishti Sabiri zikr is vocal and loud: "This distinguishes Chishti
  Sabiris from their Naqshbandi counterparts who typically practice a silent, internal form of zikr."
  (p. 198, folio 190) It is held at arm's length from music: "Although it is not considered 'music'
  per se, there is a definite rhythm and lyricism to Chishti Sabiri vocal zikr (zikr-i lisani). As an
  oral and aural performance, it resembles the art of Qur'an recitation (tajwid)." (p. 198, folio 190)
- **Muraqaba-i shaykh.** Daily contemplation of five figures — Mu'in ad-Din Chishti Ajmeri, Baba
  Farid ad-Din, al-Hujwiri, Muhammad Zauqi Shah and Shah Sayyid Waris Hasan — "for fifteen to thirty
  minutes on a daily basis" (p. 206, folio 198). At shrines it is performed in groups facing the
  tomb, and "Many attend weekly halqa-i zikr sessions and regularly visit local shrines in groups for
  muraqaba" (p. 233, folio 225).
- **Istikhara.** "One such technique known as istikhara involves the recitation of a litany of special
  prayers in prescribed locations––often in a mosque or **the vicinity of a saint's shrine**––with
  the expectation of an answering dream" (p. 190, folio 182). The rite, from Shahidullah Faridi's
  *Malfuzat-i shaykh*: "After the evening prayer, one should declare the intention [niyyat] … Recite
  Durud Sharif … before and after, and recite Du'a-i Qunut … seven times. Then go to sleep" (p. 191,
  folio 183). "Chishti Sabiri murids typically perform istikhara on **Thursday or Sunday evenings**",
  and it is held "particularly useful for guidance and clarity regarding such important matters as
  marriage, education, financial matters, and employment" (p. 191, folio 183).
- **The time budget of suluk.** "A serious person on the Sufi path is recommended to do spiritual
  work [mujahada] four to five hours a day, in addition to their job and daily routine—two to three
  hours in the morning after morning prayers and one to two hours after evening prayers" (p. 193,
  folio 185); many "sit until ishraq", which "In winter especially … is very close to office time"
  (p. 194, folio 186).
- **Daily practice is now solitary, and the book calls this a break.** "In daily practice, Chisthi
  Sabiris perform the majority of their spiritual exercises (awrad) in isolation" (p. 192, folio 184;
  the book prints "Chisthi" — see Doubts); "Suluk is much more isolated now … We don't ask each
  other, 'Do you do zikr 5,000 times, 7,000 times, or 10,000 times a day?' … But the shaykh knows
  what every murid is doing" (pp. 192–193, folios 184–185). The author: this is "a significant break
  from Chishti Sabiri tradition, a necessary concession to the demands of twenty-first-century life"
  (p. 193, folio 185).
- **Chilla.** "His malfuzat recounts two forty-day spiritual retreats (chilla) in particular, one in
  Delhi at the mazar of Nizam ad-Din Awliya' and another in Kalyar Sharif" (p. 63, folio 53).

#### Initiation, membership, women, numbers

- **Bay'at.** "the ritual ceremony that marks formal initiation into the order," by which "the Sufi
  aspirant is transformed into a murid" (p. 153, folio 145); "The ceremony itself is simple yet
  symbolic. With prayers and invocations, the novice acknowledges the absolute authority of the
  shaykh and pledges obedience and submission to him" (p. 153, folio 145). Its precedent: "In
  pre-Islamic Arab society, bay'at was the traditional way of pledging allegiance to a tribal chief"
  (p. 153, folio 145), and it "recalls the public oath of allegiance that Muhammad's followers
  pledged to him at Hudaybiya in 628" (p. 153, folio 145). It can be taken by post — Malaysian
  disciples "took bay'at by letter… long before they had even met the shaykh in person" (p. 156,
  folio 148) — or in a dream: "According to the shaykhs, it is the same thing" (p. 187, folio 179).
- **Four silsilas at once.** "The masters of our order give initiation in four silsilas … 'I take
  bay'at at the hands of my shaykh in the Chishtiyya Sabiriyya and Nizamiyya, Suhrawardiyya,
  Qadiriyya, Naqshbandiyya.' … The teachings [and practices] are that of the Sabiriyaa silsila, but
  we receive the blessings [barakat] of all four silsilas" (p. 154, folio 146; printed `Sabiriyaa`).
- **Exclusivity after initiation.** "Once they have taken bay'at, disciples are strongly discouraged
  from seeking spiritual advice outside the insulated boundaries of the silsila" (p. 154, folio 146);
  before initiation "it is alright to visit other silsilas though, and other shaykhs" (p. 154,
  folio 146). "You can only receive spiritual blessing ( faiz) from your own silsila" (p. 155,
  folio 147; **the space inside the parenthesis is as printed**).
- **The order keeps no register and no public count.** "The silisla [text-layer?: silsila] does not
  maintain public murid registers, and, as disciples note, Chishti Sabiri shaykhs as a rule never
  openly talk about their followers" (p. 140, folio 132). Such numbers as exist: murids "certainly
  number in the thousands" (p. 140, folio 132); "an elite inner circle of **several hundred**
  disciples" (p. 141, folio 133); "a core group of **nearly one hundred** Malaysian disciples"
  (p. 142, folio 134); and of Shahidullah's own following, "While no official records were ever
  maintained, Shahidullah's formal disciples are said to have numbered in the thousands" (p. 77,
  folio 67). The author's warning: "it is extremely misleading to measure the order's impact by
  counting heads" (p. 140, folio 132).
- **Composition.** Mostly Pakistani, with "a significant and growing contingent of foreigners as well:
  Malaysians, South Africans, and Europeans" (p. 140, folio 132); ethnically "primarily . . . ethnic
  Punjabis and Sindhis, along with a smaller representation of Pathans from the Northwest Frontier
  Province", with **Urdu** "the standard medium of exchange among Pakistani disciples" (p. 142,
  folio 134); the inner core "mostly educated, middle-class, urban professionals" and "predominantly
  Sunni", with "several female Shi'a murids" (p. 141, folio 133); occupations "educators, engineers,
  publishers, bankers, computer programmers, medical professionals, technocrats, and businessmen"
  (p. 142, folio 134).
- **Women in the order — a strong positive finding, with a contrary one beside it.** "The silsila is
  distinguished from many other Sufi orders by the numbers and prominence of its female disciples.
  Women are full participants in all the order's ritual activities, and select senior female
  disciples are renowned for their advanced spiritual status and authority" (p. 140, folio 132);
  "Chishti Sabiri shaykhs are fully accessible to women, and treat their female disciples as the
  spiritual equals of their male counterparts" (p. 140, folio 132); Shahidullah's will names senior
  female disciples and asserts that "If the Chishti Sabiri tradition allowed … he would have named
  them his successors" (p. 77, folio 67). **Against this:** "In Tarbiyat al-'ushshaq, Muhammad Zauqi
  Shah asserts that neither khilafat nor prophecy is possible for women" (p. 247, folio 240); women
  at the sama' are "sequestered, silent, and invisible" (p. 225, folio 217); and "On several separate
  occasions, female murids voiced feelings of alienation and marginalization, frustrated with what
  they perceived as entrenched patriarchal practices within both the silsila and society at large"
  (p. 177, folio 169). **All three are kept.**
- **Shaykhs said to be closing ranks.** Disciples "suggest that the immense challenge of integrating
  suluk into modern life has also prompted Chishti Sabiri shaykhs to close ranks, restricting the
  numbers of disciples" (p. 193, folio 185).
- **Internal friction, reported.** "I heard numerous disciples complain about backbiting, petty
  squabbling, and competition among their peers" (p. 177, folio 169). Shahidullah's *wasyat nama*
  legislates against it: "Any kind of selfish behavior, grouping, envy, quarreling, or not talking to
  one another is against the etiquette of society and must be abstained from strictly" (p. 178,
  folio 170).
- **Adab as the order's regulating institution**, governing speech ("the adab is that you should
  really not initiate a discussion", March 16, 2001, Lahore, p. 167, folio 159), the body (standing
  when a shaykh enters, sitting physically lower, not pointing the soles of the feet, no laughter, no
  direct eye contact — pp. 167–168, folios 159–160), and ritual purity before audience (p. 168,
  folio 160). "To a large extent, an individual's standing in the community is measured by her/his
  knowledge of and adherence to these rules of ritual etiquette" (p. 226, folio 218).

#### Publishing, print, cassettes and the internet as an economy

- **Scale and self-financing.** "At present, some fifty different texts are produced and distributed
  from a number of publishing houses" (p. 130, folio 121). "**Typically, no more than a thousand
  copies of each text are printed at a time. Any profits from the sale of books are immediately
  rolled back into the system, making the production process a largely self-sustaining enterprise.
  Remarkably, no resources are committed to advertising**" (p. 131, folio 122); "But marketing is not
  considered helpful for spiritual development" (October 3, 2002, Lahore) (p. 132, folio 123).
  Pricing doctrine, from the disciple who runs Mahfil-i Zauqiyya: "We print them not with the
  intention of making money, but to make them available to people at the least possible cost so that
  they can benefit from this as much as possible" (May 21, 2001, Karachi) (p. 131, folio 122).
- **The constraint on the market.** "Given Pakistan's alarmingly low literacy rates—**less than half
  of the adult population**—the potential reading public is extremely limited and largely determined
  by education and class" (p. 130, folio 121).
- **Distribution runs through shrine bazaars.** The silsila's books are sold in "the myriad bookstalls
  that flank the country's major Sufi shrines", most prominently in the lanes around Baba Farid's
  tomb complex in Pakpattan (p. 130, folio 121), and "are now easily found in the Urdu bookstores of
  Karachi and Lahore" (p. 111, folio 102).
- **The Malaysian press and the website.** A.S. Noordeen Publishers of Kuala Lumpur "has published
  four books: an English version of Wahid Bakhsh's translation of Kashf al-mahjub, a partial
  translation of the malfuzat of Shaykh Sayyid Shah Waris Hasan, an edition of Shahidullah Faridi's
  Inner Aspects of Faith, and Islamic Sufism" (pp. 132–133, folios 123–124). Since 1996 the Malaysian
  disciples "have operated a silsila Web site (http://www.moonovermedina.com). Entitled Moon Over
  Medina: A Sufi Bookstore" (p. 133, folio 124). Its traffic and economics, from the manager: "When I
  started there were about ten to fifteen visitors a day … Now it is up to **forty people a day,
  about 1,200 a month**. But of those, only a few buy books"; "I do not accept credit cards, and that
  is the problem"; "**It doesn't make any money, just enough to cover the fees**" (pp. 133–134,
  folios 124–125).
- **Digital discipleship.** "a significant number of murids also find 'virtual guidance' in
  cyberspace," the shaykh advising "via e-mail" (p. 166, folio 158) — "This new form of digital
  discipleship is yet another striking example of the silsila's fluid incorporation of modern
  technology" (p. 166, folio 158).
- **Letters as the order's guidance institution, and as a circulated genre.** "much of his interaction
  with disciples came in the form of letter writing. A large portion of his daily routine, in fact,
  was occupied with this correspondence" (p. 92, folio 82); the letters "address an immense range of
  material… dream interpretation, prescriptions for spiritual practices, and practical advice on
  personal and family matters (marriage, divorce, finances, children's education, etc.)" (p. 92,
  folio 82); "every time we sent a letter to him, we knew that a reply would come within three weeks"
  (September 30, 2001, Kuala Lumpur) (p. 93, folio 83). They are then photocopied and passed on: "The
  circulation of personal letters is, in fact, a relatively common practice among disciples on both
  sides of the Indian Ocean" (p. 174, folio 166).
- **The library at a tomb complex.** "Among volumes of classical tafsir…, fiqh…, and Sufi poetry in
  Arabic, Persian, Urdu, Saraiki, and Punjabi are thousands of letters from Wahid Bakhsh'
  [text-layer?: Bakhsh's] murids. They are stacked in piles and stored in large bags." (p. 92,
  folio 82)
- **Nineteenth-century print as background**, quoted from Francis Robinson: "By the 1820's, Muslim
  reformist leaders in the Indian subcontinent were busily printing tracts. By the 1830s the first
  Muslim newspapers were being printed. By the 1870s, editions of the Qur'an, and other religious
  books, were selling in tens of thousands. In the last thirty years of the century, over seven
  hundred newspapers and magazines in Urdu were started." (pp. 100–101, folios 91–92) Print reached
  South Asian Muslims "not until the nineteenth century, 400 years after Gutenberg's invention of
  moveable type" (p. 100, folio 91). Muhsin Mahdi is cited for the claim that "aside from state
  governments Sufis were in fact the main patrons of printing in Muslim countries during the
  nineteenth century" (p. 101, folio 92). Ernst's phrase for the result is "a mass marketing of
  Sufism on an unprecedented scale" (p. 102, folio 93).
- **Named periodicals of the order and its neighbours.** *The Sufi Path*, the order's English journal,
  Islamabad, fourteen issues by the time of writing (p. 130, folio 121); *Risala-yi anwar
  al-sufiyya*, monthly, which "played a vital role in the propagation and popularity of the teachings
  of the Naqshbandi master, Jama'at 'Ali Shah" (p. 102, folio 93); *al-Haq* and *al-Wakil* (p. 105,
  folio 96); *Anwar al-Quds*, "published in Bombay from October 1925 to February 1927" (p. 106,
  folio 97); *The Islamic Review*, London, 1933 (p. 106, folio 97). Non-Sufi press used by the order:
  the Delhi Urdu paper *Manshur* (June 20, June 27, July 4, 1943), the Allahabad paper *Onward*
  (June 14 and 21) (p. 107, folio 98), the Karachi paper *Dawn* (1945–1946) and *The People's Voice*
  (from December 1947) (p. 108, folio 99).

#### Madrasas, education and welfare

- **Madrasas as welfare institutions, in a disciple's economic account.** Disciples "view the madrasa
  as an important symbol of Muslim identity" and as "repositories of religious learning and social
  welfare centers", but "do not typically attend madrasas themselves" (p. 145, folio 137). "Madrasas
  are popular because the have-nots of the community send their children there for food, shelter, and
  education. There is a steady stream of students coming in. They cannot do anything else. Either
  they work as tea stall boys or auto shop boys" (pp. 145–146, folios 137–138). The counter-practice:
  "Chishti Sabiri shaykhs typically encourage their **non-Pakistani** disciples to study for a short
  period in a madrasa" (p. 146, folio 138).
- **Their own children go elsewhere.** "disciples typically encourage their own sons and daughters to
  pursue a secular education while maintaining their religious devotions", several in "Western
  universities", taking "advanced degrees in engineering, computer science, accounting, and finance,
  both at home and abroad" (p. 145, folio 137).
- **Donations and welfare, as a shaykh's doctrine.** "Zauqi Shah's biographers also note… that the
  shaykh recognized the importance of accepting donations and handling money in the interest of
  social welfare" (p. 68, folio 58), and in a lecture: "The status of those saints [awliya' Allah]
  who have worldly possessions and wealth is very high. Their powers are so strong that they can
  perform both their spiritual and worldly duties" (p. 69, folio 59). Against that, *Sirat-i Zauqi*
  has him following Hajji Imdad Allah in "insisting that dervishes should avoid directly asking for
  assistance" (p. 68, folio 58). **Both are kept.** And the premodern norm the book quotes from Bruce
  Lawrence: the shaykh, "Poor and unmindful of worldly possessions, he must yet be receptive to large
  donations of money and be able to dispense them quickly for the benefit of those in need" (p. 55,
  folio 45).
- **The amulet trade, refused.** Shahidullah "had little regard for those who came to him only with
  worldly problems … and discouraged disciples from sending anyone to him merely for amulets (tawiz)"
  (p. 78, folio 68).
- **Wealth refused.** His father "arranged to purchase a thousand Morris Minor taxis so that
  Shahidullah might start a new business and generate a large income"; "Shahidullah then distributed
  the taxis to those in need around the city, keeping nothing for himself" (p. 83, folio 73).
- **Shaykhs in state employment.** Wahid Bakhsh was "commissioned in the British Indian Army in 1933",
  attended Dehra Dun "from 1935 to 1937", served "as a captain in the Bahawalpur State Army",
  "retired from military service altogether in 1946", joined the Civil Secretariat as "the personal
  assistant to the prime minister of the state of Bahawalpur", and "In 1955 he was transferred to the
  West Pakistan Secretariat in Lahore where he worked as a civil servant until his retirement in
  1968" (pp. 87–88, folios 77–78). The Nawab of Bahawalpur "secured a position for them as captains
  in the Bahawalpur State Army" in September 1939, which "allowed the brothers to commit themselves
  to their spiritual disciplines while avoiding conscription in the British Army" (p. 74, folio 64).
- **A territorial grant of spiritual jurisdiction, in a will.** Shahidullah's will: Wahid Bakhsh "has
  initiated halqa zikr and guidance in Bahawalpur … I counsel that he should spread the silsila in
  this place only and not move from place to place as that will affect his work" (p. 90, folio 80) —
  which the author reads as delineating "the southern Punjab as Wahid Bakhsh's personal spiritual
  domain (wilayat)" (p. 90, folio 80). Succession was made public by turban: "Hazrat Sahab brought a
  turban (dastar) and tied it on his [Wahid Bakhsh's] head" at the 1975 announcement (p. 90,
  folio 80).
- **Access across religion and class.** *Sirat-i Zauqi*: "Hazrat Shah Sahab's … circle of followers
  included people from every religion and social class. There were no restrictions against Muslims,
  Hindus, Parsis, or Christians… nor did he demand that non-Muslims convert" (p. 63, folio 53); "He
  would allow everyone, even foreigners, a person of any religion, to sit in his zikrs." (p. 92,
  folio 82, interview Bahawalpur, April 28, 2001)

### Arguments and interpretations

- **The book's thesis.** Chishti Sabiris "have thus shaped their own alternative modernity —an
  identity that is simultaneously Muslim, mystic, and modern." (p. 20, folio 9) The title is
  explained three ways: the order's narrative "is unbound from the prevailing story of the nation and
  its religious roots", "increasingly unbound from its historical moorings in South Asia", and
  "contemporary Chishti Sabiris have effectively unbound the trajectory of their own tradition"
  (p. 24, folio 13).
- **Sufism is a verb.** "This book argues that Sufism (like the broader category of 'Islam') is best
  understood as a verb rather than a noun." (p. 25, folio 14) "Furthermore, there is no Sufism
  without Sufis." (p. 25, folio 14) And against the shrine-only picture: "Yet the story of Sufism in
  South Asia does not end with saints and shrines." (p. 13, folio 2)
- **Modernity decentred.** "This book aims to decenter modernity. In my view, modernity is neither
  monolithic nor hegemonic—it has no metanarrative, no universal form, no singular function." (p. 18,
  folio 7) Talal Asad: "Modernity is a project—or rather, a series of interlinked projects—that
  certain people in power seek to achieve." (p. 18, folio 7) Katherine Ewing: "modernity itself is
  not a single force but rather the temporary conjunction of practices and ideologies that have
  diverse sources and divergent trajectories." (p. 20, folio 9) "Nevertheless, I employ the term
  because the Chishti Sabiris I study do." (p. 18, folio 7)
- **Tradition is contested.** Via Muhammad Qasim Zaman: "appeals to tradition are not necessarily a
  way of opposing change but can equally facilitate change; that what passes for tradition is, not
  infrequently, of quite recent vintage…" (p. 17, folio 6), summarised by the author as "Tradition,
  in short, is up for grabs." (p. 17, folio 6)
- **The attack on Sufism is modern, not traditional.** "The increasing attacks on and widespread
  marginalization of Sufism by both secular nationalists and Islamists are a distinctly modern
  phenomenon, however. In fact, the attack on Sufism runs counter to Islamic intellectual history and
  pervasive cultural norms" (p. 32, folio 22). "For conservative Islamist critics who reject any
  intermediaries between human beings and God, the cult of Sufi saints is blasphemous idolatry and
  the worship at shrines corrupt superstition." (p. 13, folio 2)
- **Field criticism, via Carl Ernst.** "Sufism" is "itself an invention of late eighteenth-century
  European Orientalist scholarship" (p. 24, folio 13), typically cast as "an abstract mystical
  philosophy inscribed in 'classical' texts; a thinly veiled political ideology; or an ossified
  relic, a once dynamic tradition now devolved into popular tomb cults." (p. 24, folio 13) Scholars
  and fundamentalists converge in sharing "a 'Golden Age' view of history, which lauds safely dead
  'classical' Sufis while scorning more recent examples of the tradition." (p. 24, folio 13) The
  disciplinary split: Islamicists "tend to focus on the literary texts of select premodern Sufi
  masters", anthropologists on "'objectively' visible phenomena such as saintly charisma and the
  popular ritual practices at Sufi shrines." (p. 25, folio 14)
- **Named predecessors and the author's criticism of each** (all p. 25, folio 14): Katherine Ewing,
  *Arguing Sainthood* — "her ethnography gives less attention to Sufi ritual practices"; Pnina
  Werbner on a transnational Pakistani Naqshbandi order — "glosses over the essential core of Sufi
  practice: the master-disciple teaching relationship"; Arthur Buehler — "focuses largely on
  premodern texts and lacks a nuanced theoretical framework"; Carl W. Ernst and Bruce B. Lawrence,
  *Sufi Martyrs of Love* — "the most relevant contribution", "an unparalleled account of the Chishti
  order", but it "focuses predominantly on premodern shaykhs within the Chishti Nizami lineage, with
  less attention to the Sabiri subbranch."
- **The Pakpattan chapter's argument.** Its stated purpose is to use the stampede to "explore a
  broader issue: how Sufism functions as a lightening rod in the public debates over Islamic identity
  and authority in today's Pakistan" (p. 29, folio not stated), under the framing "Faqir or faker?"
  Its finding is that state and custodians differed on blame but shared a frame — "both camps viewed
  the tragedy as the result of administrative mismanagement—a breakdown in logistics, communication,
  and the allocation of resources" (p. 40, folio 30) — and that "What is missing from both accounts
  are the experiences and views of actual participants" (p. 40, folio 30). The disciples' sacralizing
  account "stands in marked contrast to the logic of the modernist, rationalist state. In my view, it
  can also be read as a statement of resistance, a counternarrative to the hegemony of the state"
  (p. 40, folio 30), and it "marks the shrine—and the legacy of its patron saint, Baba Farid—as the
  rightful inheritance of the contemporary Chishti Sabiri order." (p. 44, folio 34)
- **Sainthood is made, not found.** "Holy persons are born, live, and die, but they are made into
  saints… Sainthood is negotiated in dynamic public discourses and then memorialized in both oral
  narratives and hagiographical texts." (p. 96, folio 86) Three governing assertions: "1. Sainthood
  blurs the boundaries between history and myth"; "2. Sainthood is simultaneously paradigmatic and
  protean" — introducing the book's own coinage, "**a paradigm I refer to as the hagiographical
  habitus**"; "3. Sainthood is socially constructed", so that "If disciples fail to recognize and
  publicize their master's piety, the aspiring saint is doomed to historical anonymity" (pp. 51–52,
  folios 41–42).
- **A hagiography observed in the making.** Wahid Bakhsh's life story is "preserved in a nexus of oral
  narratives"; "It is safe to assume that in time Wahid Bakhsh's teachings and life experiences will
  also be permanently recorded for posterity in a formal hagiography. But **for the moment he remains
  a saint under construction**" (p. 86, folio 76). The author declines to adjudicate miracle claims —
  "It is of course impossible to objectively verify such accounts" (p. 81, folio 71) — while insisting
  on their social force (p. 82, folio 72).
- **Private dreams become collective property.** The recurring thesis: "a private dream is transformed
  into public knowledge and then recirculated through a complex network of storytelling to reaffirm
  the Chishti Sabiri hagiographical habitus" (p. 90, folio 80). "In Chishti Sabiri practice, dreams
  change lives." (p. 186, folio 178) Adab requires that dreams be told only to the shaykh (p. 188,
  folio 180).
- **Miracles as an alternative episteme, and as something to suppress.** Miracle stories "suggest that
  a Sufi saint's powers trump the wisdom and technological wonders of Western science and medicine…
  such miraculous anecdotes mark Sufism as an alternative episteme—a parallel system of inner,
  esoteric knowledge with real power and efficacy in the mundane world" (p. 66, folio 56). Yet the
  order's own doctrine suppresses them: "Unveilings [kashf] and saintly miracles [karamat] are
  insignificant matters." (p. 65, folio 55) "But the awliya' Allah [Friends of God] do not like to
  indulge in miracles because miracles cause distraction and block the process of progress." (p. 94,
  folio 84) And Thanawi, quoted: "Never be deceived by these powers" (p. 149, folio 141).
- **Three breaks from the premodern Chishti paradigm** are asserted: the university (Aligarh "likely
  made Zauqi Shah the first Chishti master to be educated in a modern university. This distinction
  marks a major deviation from the premodern paradigm of Indo-Muslim sainthood", p. 59, folio 49 —
  **note the hedge "likely"**); openness to outsiders (p. 63, folio 53); and political engagement,
  which "deviate[s] sharply from the traditional, premodern pattern of Chishti leadership that
  favored withdrawal from public, political life" (p. 69, folio 59).
- **Print turned Sufism into a public institution.** "In effect, Sufism was transformed from an
  esoteric community based exclusively on the oral instruction between master and disciple into a
  public institution." (p. 102, folio 93) Ernst, quoted: "the media permit Sufism to be contested and
  defended in the public sphere as one ideology alongside others." (p. 102, folio 93) Muslim print
  was defensive in origin — "a weapon in the defense of the faith against the polemical attacks of
  such Hindu reformists groups such as the Arya Samaj, as well as the campaigns of British colonial
  administrators and Christian missionaries." (p. 101, folio 92)
- **A middle ground between modernists, 'ulama and Islamists.** The order's shaykhs "embrace science,
  educational reform, and independent legal reasoning (ijtihad)" and "champion egalitarianism, civil
  liberties, and socioeconomic justice", yet "warn against the wholesale adoption of Western
  political models of liberal democracy"; they deny the 'ulama's interpretive monopoly, grounding
  authority in "ma'rifa… rather than 'ilm"; and they "critique the Islamists' exclusivist and literal
  interpretations of scripture", lamenting "the spread of Wahhabi doctrine and the rise of sectarian
  identities" (pp. 103–104, folios 94–95). The writing project "does not necessarily represent a
  rejection of modernity per se, but can be seen as a reconstruction of modernity according to
  Islamic models and motifs." (p. 104, folio 95)
- **Pakistani nationalism as doctrine.** The three shaykhs "were ardent Pakistani nationalists… This
  too distinguishes them from their Islamist and 'ulama peers, many of whom resisted the independence
  movement and lamented Partition"; invoking "the famous hadith, 'Love of the homeland is part of
  one's faith' (hubb al-watan min al-iman), the shaykhs' writings sacralize Pakistan as nothing less
  than a Divine reward. At the same time, they define the silsila as a sacred moral community
  antecedent to the nation-state." (p. 105, folio 96) The Conclusions restate it: the shaykhs
  "envisioned Pakistan as a tabula rasa", portraying "Sufi masters as the true heirs to the Prophet
  and, by extension, the true spiritual founders of Pakistan" (p. 236, folio 229). "In Wahid Bakhsh's
  alternative reading of history, Pakistan is rightfully an Islamic republic rooted in Sufi piety."
  (p. 122, folio 113) Chishti Sabiris "champion Pakistan
  as the teleological culmination of Islamic history" (p. 24, folio 13).
- **Sufi ritual as technology of the self.** "Borrowing from Foucault, it is useful to describe Sufi
  ritual practices as 'technologies of the self'" (p. 182, folio 174); "The locus and focus of Sufi
  ritual techniques is the physical body … understanding comes only from doing" (p. 182, folio 174).
  Mujahada is glossed against jihad: "The word mujahada (spiritual 'striving') comes from the same
  Arabic root as jihad" (p. 182, folio 174).
- **Adaptation as ijtihad.** "Embracing a form of ijtihad—independent reasoning—today's Chishti Sabiri
  order has developed a range of practical strategies to negotiate change and accommodate Sufism to
  the ground-level contingencies of everyday life in the twenty-first century" (p. 184, folio 176).
  The disciples' own formula: "Our silsila represents compatibility with modern life" (p. 184,
  folio 176). The central modern test is din versus dunya: "Chishti Sabiris view this struggle to
  combine din and dunya as the most important test of a Sufi's piety and faith" (p. 194, folio 186);
  murids "are hyperaware of the clock" (p. 193, folio 185).
- **The two "nows," via Dipesh Chakrabarty.** Disciples "live within two interdependent spheres: a
  'historical now' and a 'sacred now,'" the first "measured by the calendar and the clock," the
  second one "positivist history simply does not have the vocabulary to explain or the tools to
  measure" (p. 179, folio 171).
- **Ambivalence about Sufi identity.** Ewing, quoted: "Many are drawn to Sufism and yet avoid
  identifying themselves as Sufis" (p. 177, folio 169); the author concludes that "a pervasive and
  deep-seated ambivalence about Sufi identity underlies daily life in contemporary, postcolonial
  Pakistan and Malaysia" (p. 177, folio 169). "Murids are noticeably wary of announcing their Sufi
  affiliations to strangers… In private—and among their fellow disciples—however, Chishti Sabiris
  wear their silsila affiliation with immense pride" (p. 178, folio 170).
- **The order's double refusal, politically.** Chishti Sabiris criticise "the Ahl-i Hadith, Wahhabis,
  Jama'at-i Islami, Tablighi Jama'at, and a variety of pro-Taliban religious parties" (p. 46,
  folio 36) and also "avoid association with the pro-Sufi Barelwi movement", steering "between the
  doctrinal conservatism of the contemporary Deoband movement and the populism of the Barelwis"
  (p. 46, folio 36); they "remain wary of all political labels and actively avoid direct involvement
  in any political party or movement" (p. 46, folio 36).
- **The chapter-1 generalisation about Pakistan.** "Sufism remains a malleable and controversial
  signifier within the combative discursive landscape of twenty-first-century Pakistan" (p. 47,
  folio 37); "In today's Pakistan, Islam itself is argued and debated. And when it comes to Sufism,
  lines are drawn and sides are chosen" (p. 47, folio 37).

### 4. Structural and evidential limits of the book itself

- **Every informant is anonymous, by agreement.** "At the request of senior figures in the silsila,
  however, I have withheld the names of individual respondents in the interest of privacy and
  anonymity." (p. 26, folio 15) Informants are identified only by nationality, gender, seniority and
  interview date. **No quotation in this file can ever be attached to a named person**, and the
  fourteen-year-old who died at Pakpattan is never named either.
- **Three named shrines carry the order's ritual life; the rest are unnamed.** The author's fieldwork
  included "numerous pilgrimages in the company of murids to Sufi shrine complexes in Pakistan for
  the annual death anniversaries ('urs) of celebrated Chishti saints" (p. 141, folio 133) — **which
  shrines and which saints are (not stated).** This is the single most consequential omission in the
  book for this archive.
- **No langar, no offerings figure, no visitor count, no endowment figure and no building date is
  given for any Pakistani shrine.** The only langar in the book is at Ajmer Sharif in India (p. 74,
  folio 64); the only waqf is at Pulau Besar in Malaysia (p. 210, folio 202); the only architectural
  building history is also Pulau Besar's (p. 209, folio 201); crowd sizes are given only as "tens of
  thousands" (p. 36, folio 26) and "thousands" (p. 217, folio 209). **These are explicit negatives,
  not gaps in these notes.**
- **Four endnotes give the same interview date and place** — October 6, 2001, Kuala Lumpur —
  for four different personal letters (p. 242 folio 235; p. 254 folio 247; p. 255 folio 248; p. 256
  folio 249). It may be one long interview or a repeated locator; the book does not say.
- **Eaton's article is cited at two irreconcilable page ranges** — "44–61" in Ch 1 n. 16 (p. 241,
  folio 234) and "333–356" in the Bibliography (p. 267, folio 261), for the same chapter in the same
  edited volume. **One of them is wrong and the book does not reconcile them.**
- **Khwaja Ghulam Farid's *Maqabis al-majalis* is cited under two different author surnames** —
  "Khwaja Ghulam Farid" and "Faridi" — in adjacent endnotes on the same page (p. 261, folio 254).
  **This is an internal inconsistency, not two works.**
- **Index locators are the book's own printed folios, never PDF pages**, and **none has been
  converted anywhere in this file.** The same applies to every page span printed in the endnotes
  (e.g. "29–47", "420–425", "340–364", "365–416", "192", "70", "412", "746", "689", "239–242",
  "131–212", "257–261", "58–59"): they belong to other works or to this book's own printed
  pagination. **No index or endnote locator has become a `(p. N)`.**
- **The archive material is front-loaded and back-loaded.** Chunks 009 and 012 contain **no archive-id
  bullets at all**, and chunk 010's and chunk 011's are thin. The order's own shrines — which have no
  archive rows — carry most of the book's middle.
- **Chunk 004's account of the burial at Data Darbar is cut mid-sentence** by a chunk boundary:
  "a stranger suddenly arrived and announced that he would" (p. 75, folio 65). What the stranger did
  is **not in the notes**. The outcome is nonetheless given at p. 207, folio 199 (Faruq Ahmad does
  lie buried in the tomb complex), but **the mechanism by which the money was found is lost** and
  should be recovered from the page before the story is told in an entry.
- **Nothing in this book is written in Urdu, Persian or Arabic script.** The author transliterates
  throughout and says so (p. 26, folio 15), so **no original-script quotation can be supplied from
  this source**, and the transliteration policy is his own: "In transliterating the shaykhs' names, I
  have followed the precedent established by the Chishti Sabiri order's English publications: Zauqi,
  e.g., with a 'z' rather than a 'dh'; and 'Shahidullah' as opposed to 'Shahid Allah'" (p. 243,
  folio 236). **That statement should govern how names taken from this book are normalised.**
