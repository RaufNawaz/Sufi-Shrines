# shackle_risalo_shah_abdul_latif — chunk 004 — pp. 119–156

Four surs of the Risalo itself, in English translation only: **Sur 8, running head "sasui abiri"**,
verses 11–99V (pp. 119–133); **Sur 9 "Ma‘zuri"**, verses 1–56V (pp. 134–142); **Sur 10 "Desi"**,
verses 1–61V (pp. 143–154); **Sur 11 "Kohiyari"**, verses 1–11 (pp. 155–156, cut off mid-sur).
Sur 8 begins before this chunk and Sur 11 continues past it. Titles and numbers are given exactly
as printed — the chunk itself says nothing about what a "sur" is or what these names denote.

**No shrine, tomb, ʿurs, custodian or endowment is mentioned anywhere in these 38 pages.** The
entire range is the Sasui–Punhun cycle. Its value to the archive is what the verse shows of
wayfaring, ascetics, camel caravans, washermen and sound, and the doctrinal positions the poet
states in his own voice — not shrine facts, of which there are none.

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **`bhit-bhit-shah`** (the poet, named inside the verse as "Latif", "Abdul Latif" and "Shah"):
  the only contact this chunk makes with any archive row is the signature line. **Three forms
  alternate, all three within Sur 8 alone**: "Gird your loins, says Latif" (p. 119, v. 13);
  "There, says Abdul Latif, those who are left on their own must make great eforts
  [text-layer?]" (p. 120, v. 17); "Everybody's destination lies there, says Shah" (p. 124, v. 46).
  The long closing verse of Sur 8 uses the full form — "Sisters, says Abdul Latif, my darling
  beloved has come" (p. 133, v. 99V) — as does that of Sur 10, "Sisters, says Abdul Latif, the
  son of Ari will come to me" (p. 154, v. 61V), while Sur 9's closing verse opens "Oh friend,
  Abdul Latif's well-being lies in trouble and humiliation" (p. 142, v. 56V).
- **Nothing else.** This chunk gives no date, no place of burial, no lineage, no order, no
  patron and no biographical statement whatever about Shah Abdul Latif, and does not name Bhit
  or Bhit Shah. (not stated)

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

**Literary personae of the poems, not historical persons with shrines.**

- **Sasui** — the heroine of all four surs, addressed throughout as "girl", "slave girl",
  "wretched girl", "poor woman". The text also calls her **"the washergirl"** (pp. 123, 134, 149,
  154) and twice **"the Brahman girl"** (pp. 134, 135). She sets out from Bhambhor on foot after
  Punhun, and dies on the way (pp. 119–154).
- **Punhun / Hôt Punhun / Prince Punhun** — the beloved, a Baloch of Kech, carried off by his
  kinsmen with their camels by night (pp. 119–154). The book's spelling keeps the circumflex on
  **"Hôt"** throughout; the plural "Hôts" appears once (p. 139, v. 31).
- **Ari Jam / Ari / "the son of Ari" / "the Ariyani" / "the Arichos"** — named repeatedly and
  never explained in this range: "the court of Ari Jam" (p. 126, v. 58), "the courtyard of Ari
  Jam" (p. 132, v. 91), "Alas, the cruel Arichos have taken my husband away. They brought
  messages from Ari for Punhun" (p. 148, v. 30), "The Ariyani is the support of the helpless"
  (p. 152, v. 49).
- **Lila** — named once, in a simile: "She was robbed like Lila, who exchanged her lover for
  gems.4" (p. 135, v. 12). Footnote 4 is not in this range.
- **"Peacock"** — capitalised as a proper name and carrying a footnote marker: "We are like the
  fea [text-layer?] that clings to Peacock's2 ear" (p. 134, v. 4). The note is absent; the
  referent cannot be recovered here.
- **Places named in the verse**, with no gloss anywhere in this range: **Bhambhor** (departure
  point, pp. 119, 123, 129, 144, 150, 152, 155), **Kech** (destination, pp. 119, 123, 134, 136,
  147, 149, 152), **the Harho** (pp. 119, 123, 129, 136, 152), **the Vankar** (pp. 119–122),
  **the Vindar** (pp. 122, 124, 126, 132, 152, 153, 154), **the Pab** — "the whole journey across
  the Pab" (p. 130, v. 80) and "Early in the morning I will leave Mount Pab behind me" (p. 151,
  v. 43), **the Hab** (p. 128, v. 68), and **"the hills Kanbho and Karo"** (p. 151, v. 43).
- **Sindh** is the one region named as such: "Sindh would not have heard of her, but now she is
  famous in many other lands" (p. 135, v. 7).
- **Peoples and a language**: the Baloch, the Kechis, the Hôts, and **the Jats** — "since I
  became connected by marriage to the Jats,15" (p. 153, v. 57); **Balochi** is named as the
  kinsmen's speech (p. 146, v. 17).
- **Superhuman figures named**: **Azrael** (p. 128, v. 71; p. 141, v. 48), **Munkir and Nakir**
  (p. 129, v. 72), **Satan** (p. 146, v. 15). **"Lord Ali"** is named as the speaker of a saying
  (p. 145, v. 14). Eschatological terms used: "the Day of the Covenant" (p. 145, v. 14), "the
  day of judgment" and "doomsday" (p. 149, v. 31), "the day of resurrection" (p. 152, v. 51).

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Becoming a jogi, with its two visible marks.** "Today, mother, I will wash my clothes and
  color them with ochre. I will become a yogi, mother, do not try to stop me. For the sake of my
  Hôt Baloch, I will wear large rings in my ears.10" (p. 122, v. 33). Ochre-dyed cloth and large
  ear-rings are the whole of what the verse specifies; footnote 10 is not in this range.
- **Washermen as an occupational group, a quarter of a town and a status.** "Friends, I sufered
  [text-layer?] when I lived in the quarter of the washermen" (p. 149, v. 34); "We are washermen,
  the servants of Prince Punhun. The Hôt is used to musk; I smell of soap" (p. 149, v. 35);
  "Wretched as I am, Punhun became a washerman for my sake" (p. 149, v. 36). The rank inversion
  is then made explicit: "Together with the washermen, Punhun holds clothes in his hand to wash
  them. Then there came a messenger from Ari Jam, saying: 'Oh perfect one, it is not your task to
  pound the clothes.'" (p. 150, v. 37).
- **The spinning party** as a women's gathering with a reputation to fear: "May none of the girls
  in the spinning party expose my husband" (p. 149, v. 35).
- **Caravan economy, itemised.** "A splendid caravan came in fair array from Kech. The camels
  wore bells and had beautiful aigrettes on their necks" (p. 147, v. 22); "On their saddlecloths
  were decorations and thousands of diamonds" (p. 147, v. 23); "Their camels were decorated with
  bells and tassels and ornaments" (p. 133, v. 99V); "The guests brought their riding camels and
  made them sit in the campground" (p. 147, v. 24); "Having loaded up their goods, they leave for
  their own land" (p. 144, v. 7).
- **Camel age used as a measure of a route's difficulty** — a herder's unit, not a traveller's.
  "She looks and goes along paths where a fve-year-old [text-layer?] camel is scared and cannot
  go, and passes where four-year-olds cannot proceed… difcult [text-layer?] routes that
  full-grown beasts fnd [text-layer?] hard" (p. 146, v. 18).
- **Distance and routes.** Travel is reckoned in **leagues**: "Everyone travels hundreds of
  leagues" (p. 130, v. 81), "On my journey to the Harho I must travel many leagues" (p. 136,
  v. 18). Of the destination: "So many wretched women have died near Kech. **Twenty paths lead
  there**; how do I know the one they took?" (p. 147, v. 21).
- **Travel on foot, and barefoot, as a marked practice.** "for God's sake be content with those
  who travel on foot" (p. 126, v. 55); "as I make my way toward my beloved, I will not wear any
  kind of shoe" (p. 137, v. 21); "**Shoes are worn by those who love their feet.** For the sake
  of her beloved, Sasui has given up all these conventions" (p. 137, v. 22). Against these, the
  counter-instruction: "Go to your Hôt with your heart, do not travel on foot" (p. 121, v. 25),
  "Give up traveling on foot and forget about sitting" (p. 130, v. 79).
- **Women's labour named**: filling water bags — "Be his humble slave, fll [text-layer?] his
  water bags" (p. 140, v. 40), "At dawn my companions fll [text-layer?] the water bags in their
  hands" (p. 146, v. 16), "I will labor for my beloved and bring him water from the well"
  (p. 154, v. 61V); grinding and cooking — "I will grind grain and cook it if you take me with
  you" (p. 128, v. 68).
- **Customary hospitality owed to a husband's kin, and the shame of failing it.** "I did not get
  up before them and prepare fne [text-layer?] food. **Nor did I assemble my companions to sing
  songs for them. I could not perform the customs of our realm.**" (p. 122, v. 34). Song for
  guests is here a duty of the household, not an entertainment.
- **Sound and instruments.** "Do not make a sound like an instrument; listen and let duality slip
  away" (p. 131, v. 90); "The cut reed utters sad sounds,10 the slain Sasui loudly laments"
  (p. 141, v. 45); "A cry arose in the wilderness, like the sound of the fddle [text-layer?].
  People thought it was made by a woman, but it was the sound of love" (p. 142, v. 55). Two bird
  cries are used the same way: "like the cry of the koil.12" and "like the cry of the crane"
  (p. 142, vv. 53–54).
- **Dress and ornament, and their renunciation.** "With vermilion in the parting of her hair and
  kohl on her eyes, she lost the chance to be with him" (p. 135, v. 12); "blessed are those who
  are bare of ornaments" and "set out unadorned, all of you" (p. 135, vv. 8–10); "She who wore
  fne [text-layer?] clothes lost the chance to be with him" (p. 135, v. 11). Elsewhere she wears
  "a skirt of silk" (p. 142, v. 51) and "rejoices in her robe of martyrdom" (p. 125, v. 54).
- **Nothing in this range on ʿurs or mela, langar, offerings, sajjada nashin or other
  custodianship, Auqaf, endowment, land, revenue, visitor numbers, disputes, state involvement,
  architecture or any building date.** (not stated)

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

*These are marvels narrated inside the poems. The book does not present them as karamat of a
historical person, and attributes them to no one.*

- The mountain yields to love: "Rocks become level ground for those who roam in search of the
  beloved" (p. 134, v. 1); "Sasui crossed the mountains that confounded heroes. **The great peak
  was leveled fat [text-layer?] by love**" (p. 150, v. 42).
- Wild animals spare her because of her tie to Punhun: "They are no match for the washergirl,3
  they are well aware who her beloved is. They know of her relationship, says Shah. They might
  not have held back, but they were afected [text-layer?] by her ties to him" (pp. 134–135, v. 6).
- The landscape mourns her death: "the mountains beat their breasts in mourning for the sad
  girl. The deer in the desert weep over her death" (p. 140, v. 41); "The trees and vegetation in
  the mountains utter loud cries over the sad girl" (p. 140, v. 43).
- Birds direct her: "she keeps asking the birds for directions. They take pity on her and tell
  her about the trees that grow there" (p. 139, v. 32).
- She meets the angels of death on the road and questions them about Punhun: "Azrael21 came and
  wakened Sasui as she slept. She thought that Punhun had sent his man to her" (p. 128, v. 71);
  "When she saw Munkir and Nakir,22 she arose before them and asked about Punhun: 'Brothers, did
  my beloved's company pass by this spot?'" (p. 129, v. 72).

## Arguments and interpretations

*A primary source: these are the poet's own judgements, stated in his voice or in Sasui's.*

- **The central doctrine of the range: the beloved is within, and the journey is the error.**
  "The man of the hills8 is not where you thought, you foolish girl. **Do not travel to the hills,
  the Vankar is inside you.** Have nothing to do with strangers, but ask yourself where the
  beloved is" (p. 120, v. 20); "Sasui, search all the corners of your house. Do not go far to
  look, the beloved is inside" (p. 120, v. 21); "Roaming about is not the way to gain awareness of
  him" (p. 121, v. 22); "his resting place is inside you" (p. 121, v. 23); "Why go to the Vankar,
  why not search for him here?" (p. 121, v. 24); "The beloved is nearer to you than your eyes; do
  not call him distant" (p. 119, v. 13). The same point put as a refusal of intermediaries: "No
  one goes to a shop to look for Punhun" (p. 125, v. 49) and "why do you look for a messenger to
  tell you about him?" (p. 125, v. 50). **Note the tension this creates with the rest of the
  cycle**, which is a sustained praise of setting out on foot — see Doubts.
- **Annihilation of the self in the beloved, stated as an identity.** "When I entered into myself
  and talked with my soul, there was no mountain in the land and no desire for the Kechis.
  **I myself became Punhun, while I sufered [text-layer?] as Sasui**" (p. 124, v. 43); "I myself
  became Punhun, the veil of Sasui disappeared" (p. 124, v. 44); "I came to the beloved and lost
  my own identity. Without seeing the beloved, knowledge is not of the least use" (p. 124, v. 47);
  "If you once give up your existence, you get near to the beloved" (p. 124, v. 48). And the
  obstacle named: "It is your self that blocks you from him" (p. 125, v. 50); "Our connections
  with Bhambhor15 block us from the beloved" (p. 124, v. 46).
- **Dying before death.** "Die today before your death7 in order to be exonerated" (p. 137,
  v. 24); "Die while you are still alive, Sasui, before the time appointed for your death. Do not
  shun the company of those who have sacrifced [text-layer?] their souls on the path" (p. 138,
  v. 25); "**Those who die before their death never die at all.** Those who live before their
  life in the world to come will live forever" (p. 138, v. 26); "Death, come to me, so that I may
  follow you" (p. 138, v. 28).
- **Poverty and "not" as the equipment of the path.** "The Hôt is remote from those tied to
  existence; he is close to nonexistence. **The beloved is gained by those who are loaded with
  'not.'5**" (p. 136, v. 13); "take the dagger of denial and strike the mule of the lower self.
  Sell, says Shah, all the baggage of desires" (p. 136, v. 14); "The Hôt will never be with those
  who carry baggage" (p. 136, v. 15); "Arise and take 'not' with you, and be delivered to Kech
  with nothing" (p. 136, v. 16); "She did not get there with goods, but reached the Hôt with
  ecstasy" (p. 136, v. 17).
- **The necessity of a guide — the clearest institutional claim in the chunk.** "**He who has no
  guide, has Satan for a guide;5** without him there is only gloom. She who travels alone is led
  astray by pride. He who travels on the mystic path without a guide [is like one who sails on
  the sea without a boat],6 this is not a good saying. Without him, millions have gone astray"
  (pp. 145–146, v. 15). Consistent with it: "She traveled without a guide and did not reach her
  goal" (p. 129, v. 77); "Oh guide, come to the path of those who have no family or tribe"
  (p. 120, v. 17); "Be our guide and let those who travel on foot advance" (p. 137, v. 20). The
  square brackets in the first quotation are the book's own, not the transcriber's.
- **Predestination, stated flatly and more than once.** "Whether you stride out or take small
  steps, the least thing that is written in your fate cannot be lost" (p. 139, v. 33); "The fate
  written on the forehead can in no way be changed. Whatever the beloved has written on your
  tablet will assuredly come to pass" (p. 139, v. 34); "Is destiny in the hands of camels and
  camel riders? Who is this poor girl to act against what is ordained?" (p. 144, v. 6); "From the
  Day of the Covenant I have been destined to be his" (p. 145, v. 14); "I am drawn by destiny;
  why else would anyone travel over the rocks?" (p. 156, v. 8). Set against this, she assigns
  blame to herself: "It was your fault, but you blame the Kechis" (p. 156, v. 7) and "The fault
  lies in my fate, let no one speak against Punhun" (p. 154, v. 59).
- **Suffering figured as dyeing — the metaphor runs across two surs.** "Through being dyed in the
  suferings [text-layer?] of her journey, she has become fully colored, says Latif" (p. 128,
  v. 70); "Dyed in the way, you will become a precious color, says Latif" (p. 133, v. 98). And as
  cooking: "Be properly cooked, says Shah, as you roast upon the rocks" (p. 128, v. 70); "For the
  Kechi she gave her body a roasting" (p. 139, v. 35).
- **Love confers renown — a claim about reputation, worth noting in an archive about repute.**
  "Because of my relationship with the mighty one, I have become famous in foreign lands.
  Otherwise who would the Brahman girl be, and who would she belong to? Sindh would not have
  heard of her, but now she is famous in many other lands" (p. 135, v. 7).
- **Scripture and tradition quoted inside the verse, each carrying a footnote marker whose note
  is not in this range.** Only one is attributed in the text: "**Lord Ali said: Whoever looks for
  a thing and makes an efort [text-layer?] will fnd [text-layer?] it.4 This Tradition is still my
  support**" (p. 145, v. 14). The others are unattributed here: "He created man in his own
  image14" (p. 124, v. 45); "I saw nothing, all I saw was God.16" (p. 124, v. 48); "We are closer
  to him than his jugular vein; 18" (p. 125, v. 50); "God encompasses everything 19" (p. 125,
  v. 51); "When Be and it was 3 was uttered, Punhun took my soul" (p. 145, v. 14); "you have not
  heard of Die,8 so why cut of [text-layer?] your head?" (p. 138, v. 29).
- **Hearing, not speaking, as the discipline.** "Become all ears, the Kechis are speaking. Do not
  say anything, they are talking. **What they mean may be heard from their silence.** Sit near to
  them, listen, and acquire passion" (p. 131, v. 89); "They have learned no other words, all they
  say is: 'Flee from self.'" (p. 131, v. 90).

## Doubts

- **No archive row is informed by this chunk beyond the poet's signature.** The surs in pp.
  119–156 say nothing about any shrine, tomb, ʿurs, custodian, endowment or building — nothing
  about Bhit or Bhit Shah, and nothing about Shah Abdul Latif's life or death. `bhit-bhit-shah`
  is touched only by the three self-naming forms recorded above, which are facts about the text's
  attribution, not about the site.
- **The original Sindhi is not in this range at all.** Every one of the 38 PDF pages carries
  English translation only, under running heads "risalo" (odd pages) and "8 || sasui abiri" /
  "9 || ma‘zuri" / "10 || desi" / "11 || kohiyari" (even pages). The printed page numbers at the
  foot run **92 to 129 consecutively with no gap**, one per PDF page, so no facing page is missing
  from the extraction — the facing original simply does not sit opposite the translation here.
  **A human must check the PDF for where the Sindhi text is printed** before any date, name or
  place from this book is quoted in the original script.
- **Printed-page offset, for conversion:** PDF p. 119 = printed p. 92, and the offset of 27 holds
  across the whole chunk (PDF p. 156 = printed p. 129). All page references above are PDF indices,
  per the protocol; no `[folio N]` lines appear.
- **Every footnote in this range is a marker without a note.** Roughly 24 markers in Sur 8, 12 in
  Sur 9, 15 in Sur 10 and 1 in Sur 11 point to endnotes that are not in these pages. The notes
  that would identify **Harho, Vankar, Vindar, Pab, Hab, Kanbho, Karo, "Peacock", "the foragers",
  Daulat, Lila, the Jats** and every quoted scriptural phrase are therefore unavailable. **Do not
  treat any place-name in this chunk as identified with a real location by the translator** — in
  this range he identifies none of them.
- **Systematic text-layer damage: the fi / fl / ff / ffi ligatures are dropped throughout.**
  "sufer", "sufering", "suferings" (suffer…), "fxed", "fnd", "fnery", "fulfll", "fre", "fckle",
  "frst", "fed" (for "fled", p. 123, v. 42), "flled", "efort", "eforts", "efect", "diferent",
  "defant", "difcult", "difculties", "sacrifced", "aficted", "fnished", "fve-year-old", "ffth",
  "efaced", "fea", "fesh", "fight", "fat", "defles", "infict", "fll", "fy", "ferce", "fddle",
  "cut of", "gone of". Every quotation above keeps the damaged form with `[text-layer?]` attached.
  This is mechanical and affects no meaning, **except at p. 123, v. 42, where "When I fed from
  Bhambhor" is ambiguous between "fled" and "fed"** — context favours "fled" but that is inference,
  not the printed text.
- **Verse numbers sit on the opposite side of the text on even and odd pages.** On odd PDF pages
  the number precedes the verse; on even pages it follows it at the right margin (e.g. "The trees
  of the Vankar are tall… 17", p. 120). Anyone re-parsing these chunks mechanically must expect
  both orders.
- **The last verse of each sur carries a "V" after its number** — 99V (p. 133), 56V (p. 142), 61V
  (p. 154) — and is set in longer lines than the verses before it. **The chunk nowhere says what
  "V" stands for.** Do not expand it.
- **An internal contradiction in the story as printed.** Sur 9 v. 2 says Sasui reached the beloved
  only by dying — "Woman, it was after you died that you got to be with the beloved" (p. 134) —
  and Sur 9 vv. 41–45 narrate her death and the mourning for her (pp. 140–141). But Sur 10 v. 27
  ends "At last her husband came back to her, and her journey turned out well.9" (p. 148), and the
  closing verses of Surs 8 and 10 announce his arrival: "my darling beloved has come" (p. 133,
  v. 99V), "the son of Ari will come to me" (p. 154, v. 61V). Footnote 9 is attached to precisely
  the contradictory line and is not in this range. Reported as printed, not reconciled.
- **Repeated opening lines are the book's refrain structure, not duplicated text.** vv. 27–28
  (p. 121) both begin "Do not let yourself forget your love, you wretched girl"; vv. 87–88
  (p. 131) both "Since my beloved went, I have no peace"; vv. 15–16 (p. 136) both "Those who
  traveled light got across the Harho"; vv. 49–52 (pp. 141–142) all "She strides along and climbs
  the trees"; vv. 53–55 (p. 142) all "A cry arose in the wilderness"; vv. 47–50 (pp. 151–152) all
  "The hills are hard and the journey is long". A consolidation pass should not read these as an
  extraction fault.
- **Speaker attribution is often unrecoverable.** Quotation marks frequently open without closing
  in this text layer — v. 68 (p. 128) opens a speech that closes only at the end of v. 69 (p. 128);
  v. 84 (p. 131) opens one that runs to v. 88; v. 30 (p. 148) and v. 35 (p. 149) open marks with
  no visible close. Whether a given line is Sasui's, the poet's or her friends' is therefore
  sometimes a guess, and has been left as one.
- **Two spellings to preserve rather than normalise:** "Hôt" with a circumflex throughout, and
  "Ma‘zuri" with a turned comma. Neither is damage.
- **One place-name ambiguity worth flagging for Pass 2:** "the Vankar" is treated as a region to
  be crossed (pp. 119–121) and also as the name of the interior — "the Vankar is inside you"
  (p. 120, v. 20). The chunk never says which sense is primary, and the note that would settle it
  is absent.
