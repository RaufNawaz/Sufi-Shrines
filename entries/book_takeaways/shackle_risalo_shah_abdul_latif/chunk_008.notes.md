## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The section titles as printed, with no gloss anywhere.** `25 Kapaiti` (p. 277), `26 Piribhati`
  (p. 281), `27 Sorath` (p. 285), `28 Dahar` (p. 293), `29 Bilaval` (p. 302), `30 Kedaro` (p. 309),
  and `24 || barvo sindhi` from the running head (p. 276). **The chunk nowhere states what these
  titles denote.** Record the names; do not infer the category.
- **The spinning economy of section 25, itemised.** The wheel and its parts — "Your spinning wheel's
  support posts have become detached, and its driving band has become slack" (p. 277); "Turn the
  broken wheel until the new one is fxed" (p. 278); "The wheels are dismantled, and where have the
  spinners gone?" (p. 279); "The bands on their spinning wheels are slack, and their huts are closed"
  (p. 279). Carding and cotton balls — "The yarn of those who card it in secret is valuable"
  (p. 279); "They carded a quarter of what had been carefully cleaned" (p. 280); "The cotton balls of
  even the best girls lie around on the ground" (p. 279).
- **The dealer as inspector and price-setter.** "So long as you are spinning, do not turn your wheel
  by yourself. Otherwise, the dealer may spot a defect in this thread of yours" (p. 277); "Every
  spinner is approved according to the thread that they have spun" (p. 277); "Then you may smile when
  you are called by the dealer, and get a better price" (p. 278); "the all-powerful dealer does not
  reject the coarse thread that others spin" (p. 279); "People ofer them jewels for their thread,
  but they demand an even higher price" (p. 279).
- **Weighing, and exemption from weighing, as the mark of favour.** "Those who have spun coarse
  thread with love in their hearts sell it to the cotton dealers without its being weighed" (p. 278);
  "Their thread gets sold without being put on the scales and weighed" (p. 279); against "As soon as
  they weighed the warp, many defects emerged. Summoning the spinner, they questioned her in secret"
  (p. 280).
- **The spinning place and the bazaar as sites.** "To make a proft, they come early in the morning
  to the spinning place" (pp. 278–279); "Yesterday they spun and spun, but they have not come into
  the spinning area today" (p. 279); "Those cotton plants have gone, and so have the spinners. The
  bazaars seem desolate without them, and my heart is grieved" (p. 280).
- **Minstrelsy as a trade with its own equipment and discipline (sections 26 and 27).** "To keep his
  harp hanging on a hook is not the behavior of a bard" (p. 281); "You sleep the whole night through,
  using your instrument as a pillow" (p. 281); "With their instruments on their shoulders, they look
  for a path across the wilderness" (p. 281); "Fix your instrument and replace its strings with
  silver ones" (p. 283); "The bard tied tassels and bells on his instrument" (p. 285); "I have put my
  instrument on my shoulder, in search of the way to you" (p. 283).
- **Hereditary status against performance-based reward, stated in the same book.** "Gifts are not
  bestowed according to caste; it is performance that is rewarded" (p. 282); "'I am a hereditary
  minstrel,' he said. The king replied: 'Let me hear something from your heritage'" (p. 287).
- **Royal patronage, largesse and its protocol.** "The descendant of Ronjho opened his caskets in the
  night and took out pearls. The minstrels gathered to collect them and fll their pots" (p. 282);
  "He gave great gifts and took the goods of the base. The lord bestowed presents of gold on fools"
  (p. 284); "Let me sacrifce lakhs, says Latif, and place them at your feet" (p. 287); "Get in the
  palanquin, minstrel, and come up. The handsome king asks for you in his apartments" (p. 287);
  "Bards do not enter palaces without some good end in view… That is why rulers honor bards"
  (p. 290); and the refusal — "He did not take bags of coins, and he rejected jewels" (p. 286).
- **Begging as a regulated relation to one door.** "Prostrate yourself every day at the threshold of
  the giver. Oh minstrel, never remove your lips from it for an instant" (p. 283); "Oh minstrel, beg
  from the one who gives every day. The doors of the world are false, minstrel" (p. 283); "'Oh
  minstrel, why did you leave my door and beg from others? That is why you have sufered days of
  hardship'" (pp. 282–283); "Be happy with whatever is placed in your bowl" (p. 290); "They receive
  alms to match their bowls" (p. 302).
- **The pīr as the institution a destitute man attaches himself to** — the one use of the word in
  this chunk, printed with a macron and footnoted: "Here he has obtained clothes, bread, and a fne
  place with the pīr" (p. 306); "He sits and says, 'I will not go far from my pīr'" (p. 306); and
  "Look at Vagand, sitting at the giver's door" (p. 307).
- **Tribute and exemption from it, twice in section 29.** "The Sumiro women who entered Abro's
  protection would not pay tribute" (p. 303); "No tribute is paid by the destitute women who have
  taken refuge with Abro" (pp. 305–306).
- **Tax collectors leaving with the water (section 28).** "In reality the old riverbed has dried up,
  and only ak bushes grow on its banks. The brave traders have lost their strength, the water has

- **The river economy and its collapse.** "The boatmen frst realized that the old riverbed would not

  herdsmen who give life to the destitute" (pp. 299–300).
- **Muharram observed: the new moon, the absent Imams, and black clothes from the dyer.** "The sight

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- Bijal's playing brings buildings down: "The minstrel entered the palace with his magical
  instrument. When he touched the strings, fortresses fell down" (p. 288); earlier, "The music its
  strings played caused a tumult throughout the city. The maidservants were confused, the ladies
  cried out" (p. 286).
- The instrument as the vehicle of divine speech: "The string does not play music but vibrates with
  the sound of the divine mystery. Everyone says it is the instrument that sounds, but it is the
  hands of the player that make the music" (p. 290).
- Iron turned to gold by a glance, twice in the same section: "You are the philosopher's stone, I am
  the iron. If you just glance at me, I am turned to gold" (p. 282); repeated at p. 283.
- The king's self-beheading, and its aftermath: "His essential light became apparent to Diyach. The
  sight made him take out his knife and stab it into his skull" (p. 291); "They arrange the hair on
  the king's head and give it to the minstrel" (p. 291); "Sorath is dead, there is peace, the prince
  has pitched his tents in heaven" (p. 292); "He found favor before the gate of God" (p. 292).
- Bards seeing the divine in the patron: "Bowing down, they see the light of divine manifestation
  through their master's light. Moonbeams shine in the frame of the handsome king" (p. 290).
- Two men becoming one at the moment the secret passes: "then both of them became one" (p. 286);
  "then the king and the minstrel became one" (p. 286).
- The thorn tree addressed as a surviving witness: "Thorn tree, what size were you when the old
  riverbed was in full food?" (p. 293).
- God reversing the natures of things: "You make leaves sink to the bottom and stones foat safely
  across" (p. 295).
- A bird carries news from Karbala to the Prophet's tomb: "At dawn a bird came swiftly from Karbala.
  It arrived at the tomb of the Prophet and loudly cried: 'I have seen the fashing of steel'"
  (p. 313).
- Cosmic response to the battle: "The earth shook and trembled, and there was uproar in the heavens"
  (p. 309); "Fate has caused the clouds to be flled with concern. On seeing the harsh fate of Lord
  Husain, the prophets cried bitterly. The angels wept, and heaven and earth trembled. The sound of
  weeping flls the highest heaven" (p. 316).
- The blood of Husain as the price of forgiveness: "His mother wiped away the dust of Karbala. Ali
  wiped away the blood that fowed from his wounds. The creator forgave all sins in exchange for his
  blood" (p. 315). And "The houris in heaven garland the martyrs with fowers" (p. 315).
- Jakhiro as a direct creation and an intercessor: "God himself created Jakhiro and gave him form"
  (p. 304); "Like the ocean in food, he drowns them in the deep water of his generosity" (p. 304);
  "If he is transported today, he will rain drops of gold" (p. 306).
- Muhammad's intercession at the resurrection: "Pitching his tent in the burning of the
  resurrection, he will set it up for sinners. The generous one will avert the fate prescribed for
  sinners. He will come at the time for help, and he will change the color of musk" (p. 308).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

*The translator adds nothing in this range — no headnote, no visible footnote text. What follows is
the poet's or the speaker's own judgement, as printed.*

- **Intention is what the market of God prices, not the quality of the work** — stated as a matched
  pair: "Those who have spun fne thread with ill will in their hearts get none of it approved by the
  dealers"; "Those who have spun coarse thread with love in their hearts sell it to the cotton
  dealers without its being weighed" (p. 278).
- **Reward is detached from birth and skill alike.** "Gifts are not bestowed according to caste; it
  is performance that is rewarded" (p. 282), beside "You may not have learned anything of singing,
  yet the king is pleased" (p. 282) and "Become ignorant and beg, forget all your knowledge"
  (p. 282). The chunk asserts all three and reconciles none.
- **The giver's gift, not the beggar's need, is the measure** — the closing judgement of the Sorath
  exchange: "All are quite ready to cut of their heads and ofer them, oh Diyach. But the gift you
  give is the ultimate yardstick for beggars" (p. 289); and the king's own account, "For true being
  lies in selfessness and nonexistence" (p. 289).
- **Word tested against deed, as a general claim about people.** "With their tongues everyone calls
  themselves a true friend. This part is easy, but when action is called for, one fnds out what they
  are like" (p. 275); "Men's sincerity has changed and is no longer in keen demand. Everyone eats
  men's fesh now… there will be only one truly sincere man" (p. 275).
- **Martyrdom construed as a pre-eternal compact and as grace, not as calamity.** "The compact of
  being slaughtered was made with the Imams at the beginning of time" (p. 309); "The harshness of
  martyrdom is all a sign of the beloved's grace. Mystics understand the mystery of the business of
  Karbala" (p. 309); "This was not just a battle, but a manifestation of God's love" (p. 309).
- **God's affliction of those he loves, left unexplained on purpose.** "He lets his beloved friends
  be slain, and lets those he loves be killed. He causes those who are especially dear to him to
  sufer. God the absolute has no care, but does what he wishes. In all this there is some profound
  mystery" (p. 310).
- **Unarmed courage ranked above armed** — a ranking, not a lament: "The warriors who put on armor at
  the time of the battle still have the desire to live. Those who fght unarmed deserve the name of
  hero" (p. 314); "Not all those who are present at the time of the battle are brave" (p. 313).
- **Grief for Husain made a condition of forgiveness.** "Those whose hearts contain no grief for
  Hasan and Lord Husain will never be forgiven by the creator, who is the omnipotent lord" (p. 316).
- **Jakhiro placed above the prophets, with no gloss offered.** "He is the leader of all the prophets
  sent to the world; preeminent is his glory" (p. 305), immediately followed by the footnoted tag
  "There was the distance of two bow-lengths or less between them" and "It is the grace of the lord
  that he has brought me such a guide" (p. 305). Whether a patron, the Prophet or both are meant is
  (not stated) — see Doubts.
- **This world judged a way-station, twice.** "This place you think is home is just somewhere for a
  midday nap" (p. 296); "The corrupted did not taste the milk but turned toward the froth. For the
  sake of this world they lost the world to come, and they were desolate" (p. 297).
- **Birds set above people as models of love.** "They move in focks, and their love for each other
  is unbroken. See how birds show each other much sweeter love than people" (p. 298).
- **The hunter condemned as the type of the separator** — a moral verdict, not a scene: "Hunter, may
  you die and may your snare be destroyed. For yesterday you came between the lovers and separated
  them" (p. 299).

## Doubts

- **No archive row is informed by this chunk, and no archive id is used.** The range is Risalo verse
  plus one acknowledgments page. **Bhit, Bhit Shah and the shrine of Shah Abdul Latif are not
  mentioned anywhere in it**, and the poet's self-naming (pp. 276, 279–284, 287, 290, 292, 296,
  301–303, 307, 308) carries no fact about him as a person, a death or a site — so nothing here maps
  to `bhit-bhit-shah`. This is the expected result for a poetry range and is not a failure of the
  chunk.
- **"Lakho", "Lakho Phulani" and the mare "Lakhi" (p. 300) must not be matched to
  `shrine-of-lakhi-shah-saddar`** (principal figure "Syed Shah Sadaruddin Lakyari (Lakhi Shah
  Saddar)"). The chunk's Lakho is a mounted raider of Kachchh with no shrine, grave, silsila or
  sanctity attached. The similarity of the names is the whole of the resemblance.
- **"the lord of Las Bela" (p. 282) must not be mapped to `chandragup-baba-chandragup`**, whose
  index location reads "Hingol National Park, Lasbela District, Balochistan". The chunk names a
  patron king called Sapar and no site, temple or shrine in Las Bela at all.
- **No footnote text appears anywhere in this chunk**, only superscript markers: none visible in the
  section 24 tail or in section 25; **1–8** in section 26 (pp. 281–284), **1–10** in section 27
  (pp. 285–292), **1–14** in section 28 (pp. 293–301), **1–13** in section 29 (pp. 302–308), and
  **1–11** in section 30 (pp. 309–315). Everything that hangs on a note is unreadable from here:
  who Sapar, Choto, Ronjho, Anirai, the Jasodhos, the Patihal, Hatim, Rahu, Alauddin, Rebari and Hur
  are; what "ak bushes", "Vihag", "the wealthy herdsmen", "Thursday oferings", "Kotri", "pīr",
  "bridegrooms", "indigo" and "the celebration" are glossed as; and the source of every scripture tag
  listed under Practices.
- **The back matter expected in this range is not here.** After section 30 ends at verse 44V (p. 316)
  there is a blank page (p. 317), the ACKNOWLEDGMENTS (p. 318) and another blank (p. 319). **The
  chunk does not state that section 30 is the last section of the Risalo** — it simply stops — and no
  notes, glossary, bibliography or index falls inside it.
- **Section 24's title is known here only from a running head.** The chunk opens mid-section at verse
  18 (p. 275) with no section head; the title `24 || barvo sindhi` comes from the running head on
  p. 276. Verses 1–17 of that section are in the previous chunk.
- **"Anirai" is used in two incompatible senses and both are footnoted.** At p. 289 he is someone to
  whom a promise must be kept; at p. 304 he is the type of the worthless ruler ("all other rulers are
  like Anirai"). Do not assert that one figure is meant.
- **"Sorath" is both the section title and a queen's name** (pp. 285, 286, 291, 292), and "Sorath is
  dead," at p. 292 carries footnote 10. Which is intended at that line cannot be settled from this
  chunk.
- **"He is the leader of all the prophets sent to the world" (p. 305) is said of Jakhiro** in a
  section that elsewhere treats him as a Samo chief and lord of Kachchh (p. 304). A human must read
  footnotes 5–10 of section 29 before this line enters an entry.
- **Hasan's absence from Karbala is asserted flatly and footnoted** — "Hasan was not with Husain, who
  had no helper or support" (p. 313) — with no explanation in the chunk. Report as the book prints
  it; add nothing.
- **No transcriber flags appear in this chunk.** There is no `[OCR?]` and no `[illegible]` anywhere in
  the range, and no `[folio N]` lines; the route was text_layer, so nothing has been marked up.
- **No date, year or era of any kind appears anywhere in this chunk** — not in the verse and not on
  the Acknowledgments page (p. 318). "(not stated)" for every chronology.
- **No Sindhi original text appears, in script or transliteration.** Printed page numbers run 248

  word printed with a diacritic anywhere here), **Vihag** (p. 283), **ak** bushes (p. 293),

  **Hatim** (p. 304), **Anirai** (pp. 289, 304), and the section titles **Barvo Sindhi, Kapaiti,
  Piribhati, Sorath, Dahar, Bilaval, Kedaro**.

- **Four lines where the damage changes the sense and the PDF must be consulted before quoting.**
  (1) "when the old riverbed was in full food" (p. 293), "the time of that water in food" (p. 294)
  and "Like the ocean in food" (p. 304) — *flood* is a reading, not what is printed. (2) "He is sick
  but delights in being infected by his afiction" (p. 307) — "infected" may stand for *infected* or

  forgiven" (p. 301) — ungrammatical as it stands. (4) "Vagand has return, foul and failing to pray"

- **Verse numbers alternate sides of the page and so attach to the wrong line in the text layer.** On

  should         7", p. 278). Verse numbering above was reconstructed on that basis and should be
  spot-checked against the PDF.
- **The suffix "V" on the closing verse of every section here is unexplained** (25V, 21V, 20V, 39V,
  56V, 35V, 44V). The chunk nowhere says what "V" marks.
- **Speaker attribution is unreliable in several places.** Quotation marks open and close unevenly
  across the Sorath dialogue (pp. 285–291) and in section 30 (pp. 311–313), so which lines are the
  king's, the minstrel's, the women's or the poet's cannot always be settled from this text layer.
