## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **bhit-bhit-shah** (the book's names: the saint "Shah Abdul Latif", "Shah Latif", once "Shah
  Abdul Lati[f] Bhittai"; the place "Bhit", "Bhitshah", "the Bhitshah shrine"):
  - The Risālo is "a large collection of Sindhi lyrical poetry by the eighteenth-century Suf
    [text-layer?] poet Shah Abdul Latif (1689–1752) of Bhit, near Hyderabad in modern-day Pakistan"
    (p. 8). Those are the dates the introduction gives, unhedged.
  - The book's own Library of Congress record hedges both: "‘Abd al-Laṭīf, Shah, approximately
    1689-approximately 1752, author" (p. 5). The two statements sit four pages apart — see Doubts.
  - "The honorifc [text-layer?] title Shah indicates his status as a Sayyid claiming direct descent
    from the prophet Muhammad" (pp. 8–9).
  - He "belonged to one of the many lineages of hereditary Suf [text-layer?] saints established in
    the countryside of Sindh, where they have always enjoyed great prestige and power as pīrs or
    holy men with a special spiritual authority" (p. 8).
  - **Silsila: the book says there was none.** "unlike many of the pīrs of Sindh or leading Sufs
    [text-layer?] in other parts of South Asia, he was not formally afliated [text-layer?] in a
    chain of spiritual descent to any of the great Suf [text-layer?] orders. He is therefore
    generally classed as an Uvaisi Suf [text-layer?], the term given to those whose spiritual
    initiation comes directly from divine inspiration without any saintly human intermediary"
    (p. 9). Shackle prefaces this with "It appears that" — the hedge is his.
  - Family and formation: "a member of the rural elite who were trained in the Islamic sciences as
    transmitted through Arabic and Persian, the standard languages of education" (p. 9); his
    great-great-grandfather was Shah Abdul Karim (d. 1623) of Bulri (p. 9, and see next entry); his
    older contemporary Shah Inat Rizvi (d. 1711) of Nasarpur was another pīr he "is also known to
    have been in contact with" (p. 9).
  - Travel: "The wide-ranging references in Risālo to many diferent [text-layer?] locations in and
    around Sindh **support the claim** that Shah Latif traveled extensively as a young man" (p. 10)
    — the book presents this as inference from the poetry, not as documented itinerary.
  - **Bhit and the name Bhitshah**: "he later settled in the desert near Hyderabad in lower Sindh at
    Bhit, a place now known in his honor as Bhitshah ('Shah's Dune')" (p. 10).
  - **The tomb and its patron**: Bhitshah "is the site of the magnifcent [text-layer?] tomb
    constructed in his honor by the local ruler Ghulam Shah Kalhoro" (p. 10). No construction date
    and no dates for the ruler are given (not stated).
  - **Death date as recorded at the site**: "where the date of his death is recorded as 14 Safar
    a.h. 1166 (1752 CE)" (p. 10). The Hijri form is printed in Latin transliteration only; no
    Arabic-script or Sindhi-script date appears anywhere in this chunk.
  - **Succession**: "Although he left no son to assume responsibility for the Bhitshah shrine, his
    disciples ensured that it became the center of his cult" (p. 10). No sajjada nashin, no
    custodial family, no Auqaf, no modern administration (not stated).
  - **The shrine's musical tradition is attributed to the poet himself**: the cult included "the
    elaborate tradition of musical performance of his poetry **that he himself had devised**"
    (p. 10).
  - **The Ganj**: the cult also preserved "the collection of poetry by Shah Latif and others that
    was carefully assembled in the historic manuscript known as the Ganj, dated a.h. 1207
    (1792 CE)" (p. 10). The book does not say in this chunk where the Ganj is held (not stated).
  - His three constant books: "All the sources agree that he kept three books with him as his
    primary sources of continual inspiration: the Qur'an, from whose verses he so frequently quotes
    in Arabic; Rumi's great Persian Masnavī; and the Sindhi verses of his ancestor Shah Karim"
    (p. 23).
  - As a young man "Shah Latif **is believed** to have spent time with" the wandering yogis who
    crossed Sindh on pilgrimage to Hinglaj (p. 20) — the hedge is the book's.
  - Poetic signature: the verses name him variously in the third person — "says Abdul Latif"
    (pp. 30, 36, 39), "says Latif" (pp. 33, 35, 36), "says Shah" (p. 35).
  - The cover of this edition is described as an "Illustration based on the Sufi Shrine of Shah
    Abdul Lati [text-layer?] Bhittai in Pakistan" (p. 5) — the only place in the chunk the epithet
    "Bhittai" is used, and the only reference to the shrine's physical appearance.
  - Colonial-era notice of him: "Richard Burton's notice of Shah Latif in his classic frsthand
    [text-layer?] account of premodern Sindh (Burton 1851: 81–84)" (p. 26, n. 10).
  - **Urs: not stated.** Nothing in this chunk gives an urs date, a festival, pilgrim numbers,
    offerings, langar, endowment or land. The only recurring practice recorded at the site is the

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- **Shah Inat Rizvi (d. 1711) of Nasarpur** — a pīr, Shah Latif's older contemporary and a known
  contact, "the author of a longer collection of Sindhi poetry strikingly similar in scope to the
  far more famous Risālo" (p. 9); a small selection translated in Allana 1996, and the "excellent
  pioneering edition by Baloch (1963)" whose findings "have yet to be refected [text-layer?] in the
  English critical literature" (p. 25, n. 3).
- **Ghulam Shah Kalhoro** — "the local ruler" who built the tomb at Bhitshah; no dates, no titles,
  no further description (p. 10).
- **The shrine of the goddess at Hinglaj, Balochistan** — the destination of "the wandering yogis
  who traversed Sindh during their pilgrimage to the shrine of the goddess at Hinglaj in
  Balochistan", the subject of three surs ("Ramakali", "Khahori", "Purab") (p. 20). The book treats
  this as a real pilgrimage route through Sindh, not a poetic invention. See Doubts on mapping.
- **Mansur al-Hallaj (d. 922)** — "the famous martyr", named as one of the early Sufis of Baghdad
  who used Arabic (p. 11).
- **Jalal ud Din Rumi (d. 1273)** — greatest of the Persian Sufi poets, author of the Masnavī,
  "sometimes called 'the Qur'an in the Persian language'"; "known to have been a primary source of
  inspiration for Shah Latif" (pp. 11–12), and named inside the verse itself (p. 16, verse 2.71).
- **Thatta** — "the Mughal capital of Thatta and other urban centers in Sindh", where the educated
  elite wrote the "now forgotten Persian poetry" (p. 25, n. 7, citing Sadarangani 1987).
- **The Suhravardis and Qadiris** — named as "the main Suf [text-layer?] orders" with which the
  charismatic figures who established a Sufi presence across Sindh were often associated (p. 12).
- **Imam Husain and Karbala** — the sur "Kedaro" is set "in the desert of Iraq, where Imam Husain
  was killed at the battle of Karbala in 680" (p. 22).
- **Literary personae of the Risālo — poem-figures, not historical persons with shrines.** The book
  introduces them as heroines and heroes of "local romantic legends" that "are loosely set in the
  pre-Mughal period when Sindh was ruled by the Muslim Rajput dynasties of Sumiros and Samos"
  (p. 17, spellings as printed):
  - **Suhini** — drowns in the Indus crossing to her beloved, the buffalo herder **Sahar**, when her
    sister-in-law substitutes an unfired pot for her float (p. 17); a **Dam** is named in the verse
    as someone she refuses to look at, unexplained (p. 17, verse 7.91).
  - **Sasui** — "the delicately reared girl from the southern Sindhi city of Bhambhor", whose
    beloved the Baloch prince **Punhun** was abducted by his kinsmen; she tracks him "across Las
    Bela to the west of Sindh" and dies in the wilderness (p. 18). "As the greatest of all the
    heroines, Sasui has no fewer than fve [text-layer?] surs devoted to her" (p. 18).
  - **Lila, Chanesar, Kaunru** — Lila trades a night of her royal husband Chanesar with her rival
    Kaunru for a necklace (p. 19).
  - **Mumal, Rano (the Rajput prince Mendhiro)** — the enchantress Mumal "has used sorcery to
    destroy all the suitors who were lured to her magic palace of Kak" (p. 19).
  - **Marui and the ruler Umar** — a girl "belonging to the Maru tribe of desert nomads" abducted by
    Umar and "held in luxurious confnement [text-layer?] in his fortress at Umarkot in eastern
    Sindh"; her verse asks that her corpse be taken "to Malir" (pp. 19–20, verse 15.44).
  - **Nuri and Prince Tamachi** — "the fsher [text-layer?] girl Nuri from the Kinjhar lake in lower
    Sindh" is made his principal queen; the one happy ending (p. 20).
  - **Sorath, Rai Diyach of Junagarh in Gujarat, and the minstrel Bijal** — Rai Diyach gives his head
    when Bijal asks for it as a reward for his performance (p. 22).
  - **Sapar Khan of Las Bela** — "famously munifcent [text-layer?]", a symbol of perfect beneficence
    in "Piribhati" (p. 22).
  - **Lakho** — "the brave bandit", in "Dahar" (p. 22).
  - **Jadam Jakhiro** — "the Sindhi ruler", his "legendary generosity and chivalry" celebrated in
    "Bilaval" (p. 22).
  - **Vagand** — "the poet's disciple", mocked in "Bilaval" "for his laziness and greed" (p. 22).
    The only named disciple in the chunk.
  - Collective term: "'the seven heroines' (S. sat sūrmiyūṉ), viz. Suhini, Sasui, Lila, Mumal,
    Marui, Nuri, and Sorath" (p. 26, n. 21).
- **Places named as poetic setting rather than as sites**: the river Indus, whose shifting course
  has made an area "now made desolate" that was formerly prosperous (p. 22); Jaisalmer, reached by
  the rains in verse 22.14 (p. 21); Aden, Gujarat and Lanka as the destinations of Sindhi sea
  voyages (p. 16); "a whirlpool near Karachi" where a family of fishermen battle a sea monster in
  "Ghatu" (p. 20).

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Shrine musicians as the medium of Sufi transmission**: besides teaching "within the circle of
  disciples formed around a pīr, the Suf [text-layer?] message was also transmitted to a wider
  audience through poetry sung by musicians attached to the Suf [text-layer?] shrines constructed
  around the tombs of saints" (p. 12).
- **Clerical opposition to that music, named by office**: "Despite the disapproval of music in the
  clerical Islam upheld by the mullahs and qazis, the singing of mystical lyrics gained popularity
  with the increasing use of local languages for poetry that coincided with the decline of Mughal
  authority during the eighteenth century" (p. 12).
- **Hereditary pīr lineages as a standing institution of rural Sindh** — "they have always enjoyed
  great prestige and power as pīrs or holy men with a special spiritual authority" (p. 8); their
  distribution in Sindh is referred to Ansari 1992: 9–35 (p. 25, n. 8).
- **A shrine cult sustained without a biological heir** — disciples, not a family line, carried
  Bhitshah (p. 10). This is the only custodianship arrangement the chunk describes.
- **Manuscript-making as a shrine activity** — the Ganj, a.h. 1207 (1792 CE), assembled poetry "by
  Shah Latif and others" within the cult (p. 10).
- **Oral composition and disciple transcription** — "Shah Latif's verses were frst [text-layer?]
  extemporized orally in speech or song, then unsystematically recorded in writing by disciples"
  (p. 10).
- **Hindu long-distance trade out of Sindh** — "the long-distance sea voyages annually undertaken by
  the Hindu traders of Sindh to locations like Aden, Gujarat, or Lanka", and the wives "left behind
  while their husbands are absent on business" (p. 16). Presented as the real practice behind the
  surs "Sirirag" and "Samundi".
- **Yogi pilgrimage traffic across Sindh** to Hinglaj in Balochistan, with Shah Latif said to have
  spent time among them (p. 20).
- **Colonial language and education policy, 1843 onward** — "Only with the British conquest of Sindh
  in 1843 did the literary culture of the Suf [text-layer?] tradition come to be overlaid by the new
  patterns of modernity"; Persian was "quite rapidly replaced as the language of education,
  administration, and elite literature by English and by Sindhi, whose development was actively
  encouraged by the colonial authorities in Bombay" (p. 13). Because Sindh was administered from
  Bombay rather than Calcutta, "Urdu was not installed as the immediate successor of Persian, as
  happened in Panjab" (p. 25, n. 9).
- **The Risālo enters the examination system** — "Risālo was pressed into service for the
  examinations prescribed by the new education syllabus" (p. 13).
- **1947 and the loss of the Hindu scholarly community** — Hindu scholars had produced "much of the
  new secondary literature in English or Sindhi prose on Shah Latif's life and poetry"; this
  "continued until after independence in 1947, when the mass emigration of the Hindu population from
  Pakistan to India took place" (p. 13).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **Uvaisi initiation** — his spiritual initiation is classed as coming "directly from divine
  inspiration without any saintly human intermediary" (p. 9). Shackle reports this as the standard
  classification, introduced by "It appears that", not as verified fact.
- **The Sunni/Shia anecdote** — "When Shah Latif was asked if he was Sunni or Shia, he frst
  [text-layer?] replied that he was in between the two, and when told there was nothing in between,
  he gave the perfect Suf [text-layer?] response by stating, 'I am that nothing'" (pp. 22–23).
  Shackle calls it "an interesting anecdote" and sources it to Advani 1970: 32 (p. 26, n. 29).
- **Time among the yogis** — "As a young man, Shah Latif is believed to have spent time with these
  yogis" (p. 20); the belief is reported, not endorsed.
- No karamat in the ordinary sense — no healing, no intercession, no miracle at the tomb — appears
  anywhere in this chunk (not stated).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The biography is hagiographic and thin, and Shackle says so at the outset.** "The largely
  hagiographic accounts of his life are of the usual limited use in constructing a fully detailed
  biography" (p. 9). Every life-fact in the introduction should be read against that sentence.
- **Against the "unlettered genius" reading.** "It would however be misleading to see his poetry as
  the entirely original product of an unlettered genius" (p. 9). His evidence is threefold: the
  rural elite's training in the Islamic sciences through Arabic and Persian; Shah Abdul Karim's
  pioneering Sindhi poems a century earlier; and Shah Inat Rizvi's near-contemporary collection
  "strikingly similar in scope". Conclusion: "There was, therefore, already an established culture
  in Sindh of vernacular Suf [text-layer?] poetry, although this tends to be overshadowed by Shah
  Latif's unique reputation in the literary histories" (p. 9).
- **The Risālo has no authorial text — it accreted.** "The Risālo emerged gradually from various
  collections of the verses Shah Latif had produced on many diferent [text-layer?] occasions"
  (p. 10), after oral extemporization and unsystematic transcription by disciples. The shrine cult
  is what then fixed it, in the Ganj of a.h. 1207 (1792 CE), forty-odd years after the recorded
  death date of a.h. 1166 (p. 10).
- **On the title.** Risālo derives "from the Arabic risāla 'treatise' (typically one written on an
  Islamic topic, often in prose), which is itself cognate with the word rasūl 'apostle' used as a
  title of the prophet Muhammad. The common loose translation of Risālo as 'the Message' thus
  conveys an appropriate sense of the poetry's uniquely inspired character" (p. 8).
- **Sufism as integral to Islam, stated as a corrective.** Appreciating the Risālo "frst
  [text-layer?] means understanding that Sufsm [text-layer?] in India is no exception to the general
  rule that Sufsm [text-layer?] is and always has been an integral part of Islam"; the Sufis'
  emphasis on spiritual understanding distinguished them from "the legalistic constructions of the
  orthodox scholars", but "they equally found their core inspiration in the message of the Qur'an
  and the example of the prophet Muhammad as recorded in the Traditions known as Hadith" (p. 11).
- **A three-cornered critique of the existing scholarship** (pp. 13–14). (i) Colonial-era and Indian
  scholarship: Indian scholars "naturally tend to view Shah Latif as one of the many great premodern
  poet saints who helped to construct the national identity of modern India, and to detect the
  particular inspiration of the Vedanta in his exposition of universal spiritual truths". (ii)
  Pakistani scholarship: "very large", "mostly written in Sindhi or in Urdu and so has had rather
  less impact on international understanding", with interpretations "tied to local preoccupations,
  as when Shah Latif is too narrowly seen as an authentic spokesman of the Sindhi folk tradition or
  as an advocate of Sindhi nationalism" (p. 13, citing Syed 1996 at p. 26, n. 13). (iii) His own
  corrective: "the common emphasis upon the Islamic character of Shah Latif's poetry" (p. 14),
  credited in English above all to Schimmel 1976 (p. 26, n. 14).
- **The authenticity of "Kedaro", and the editorial evidence behind the doubt.** Its "clear
  alignment… with the world of Shia mythology has raised questions about its authenticity, which is
  however generally maintained with some qualifcation [text-layer?]" (p. 22). The evidence, in his
  endnote: "It is omitted without comment in Kazi 1961. In Baloch 2012: 417–425 it is placed
  separately, immediately following the main body of surs now generally agreed to be fully
  authentic, and preceding a variety of extra surs that were present in earlier editions. These
  notably include a certainly inauthentic Hir and a Dhol Marui that extend the geographical coverage
  of the Risālo to Panjab and to Rajasthan" (p. 26, n. 28). A worked example of what counts as
  spurious in the Risālo's transmission: geographical over-reach beyond Sindh.
- **An editorial intervention of his own**, stated as such: "'Sorath' hardly deals with the princess
  after whom the sur is named, and is accordingly placed separately in our numerical sequence"
  (p. 26, n. 21) — i.e. his chapter order is not simply inherited.
- **The Risālo as "Qur'an in the Sindhi language" — offered as a possible reading, twice hedged.**
  The contrast in verse 7.77 between "baita, the ordinary Sindhi word for poems, and āyatūṉ, the
  Arabic word for verses of the Qur'an, **might be seen as** an indirect claim for the status of the
  Risālo as a 'Qur'an in the Sindhi language' comparable to the classic defnition [text-layer?] of
  the Masnavī as a 'Qur'an in the Persian language'. Equally, **it might well be said** the Risālo is
  one of those very rare instances in the literary history of South Asia of a genuinely integral
  Indo-Islamic creation" (p. 24; compared to the Avadhi premākhyān at p. 27, n. 32).
- **Why the manner is hard to characterize.** The Sunni/Shia "ambiguity may be the fundamental
  reason why — in contrast to the relative ease with which the formal structures of Shah Latif's
  poetry, even the capacious matter of its content, may be defned [text-layer?] — it is so much
  harder to pin down the distinctive manner of its expression"; "Shah Latif proclaims the same truth
  as other more outspoken Sufs [text-layer?] while disclaiming the need always to speak of it so
  openly" (p. 23).
- **Form, described precisely** (p. 14): the bait, "commonly known by the Arabic word bait ('verse,'
  plural abyāt)", in earlier Sindhi Sufi poetry "generally identical with the dohā"; Shah Latif's
  bait "more usually contains three or more lines, in which the order of one or more of the
  half-lines may be reversed, with the rhyme coming in the middle as in the Hindi soraṭhā"; "The
  meter is fairly free, with a strict syllabic count not always being maintained, but the poetic
  structure of the half-lines is tightened by Shah Abdul Latif's systematic use of strongly marked
  alliteration in each half-line"; "The poet's signature is typically tied into the verse with
  alliteration." And the vāī, "a close relative of the kāfī", consisting of "a varying number of
  monorhymed single verses, preceded by a refrain repeated after each verse".
- **Translation method, declared** (pp. 24–25): "a consistent style of plain English prose that aims
  to steer a middle path between of-putting [text-layer?] formality and jarring colloquialism"; no
  imitation of rhyme, alliteration only "where this occurred naturally"; "the underlying syntax of
  the verses has been maintained, with a full stop marking the end of an individual line and a comma
  being used where appropriate to indicate the half-line caesura"; italics mark Qur'anic and other
  Arabic and Persian quotations, with Qur'anic translations "based on Yusuf Ali 1977" (p. 27, n. 33).
  He recommends reading each sur's first endnote before the chapter (p. 25).
- **His own bibliography of first resort**, for anyone citing this book: "The two best studies in
  English are Sorley 1966 and Schimmel 1976. See also Advani 1970, Baloch 1972, Jhangiani 1987,
  Lalwani 1978, Mirza 1980, Sayed 1988" (p. 25, n. 1); for Sindh's Sufi culture, "Boivin 2015 is a
  most helpful guide", with "Rizvi 1978–1983… the standard general account of the Suf [text-layer?]
  orders in India" (p. 25, n. 8); the "major early edition of the Risālo by Gurbakhshani [1923–1931]
  1979" (p. 26, n. 11).
- **Bibliographic status of this volume, from its own front matter**: "This is an English only
  edition of Risalo by Shah Abdul Latif, published by Havard [text-layer?] University Press in 2018.
  The Sindhi text has been taken out and **the introduction has been slightly edited**" (p. 5). The
  introduction summarized above is therefore an edited version of the 2018 one; "First published in
  Murty Classical Library of India, Volume 18, Harvard University Press, 2018" (p. 5), copyright
  "© 2018 by the President and Fellows of Harvard College" (p. 5), title page "2025" (p. 4).

## Doubts

- **Hinglaj cannot be mapped, and the reason is in the index.** The book states that yogis crossed
  Sindh on pilgrimage "to the shrine of the goddess at Hinglaj in Balochistan" (p. 20). The archive
  index has a row named "Shaktipeeth Shri Hinglaj Mata Mandir" (Hingol Balochistan National Park)
  **whose id column is empty**, so there is no valid id to map to; and `chandragup-baba-chandragup`
  is a different site in the same park (Hingol National Park, Lasbela) that this chunk never names.
  I have therefore placed Hinglaj under "Other saints, sites and events". **A human must decide
  whether that index row should carry an id** — until it does, this book's clearest Balochistan
  reference cannot be cited into the archive.
- **Bulleh Shah's death year contradicts the archive row.** The book prints "Bullhe Shah (d. 1758)"
  (p. 12); the archive index records 1757 for `mazar-of-bulleh-shah`. Reported, not reconciled.
- **The Shah Abdul Karim mapping is to the person, not the site.** The chunk gives only "Shah Abdul
  Karim (d. 1623) of Bulri in southern Sindh" (p. 9) and says nothing about a tomb, shrine or urs
  there. The archive row `shrine-of-shah-abdul-karim-bulri` (Bulri Shah Karim, Tando Muhammad Khan
  District) matches on name, death year and place-name, which is why I mapped it — but the book
  contributes biography and literary history here, not shrine evidence. A reviewer who wants a
  stricter standard should demote these bullets.
- **The poet's dates are given two ways within five pages.** The introduction prints "(1689–1752)"
