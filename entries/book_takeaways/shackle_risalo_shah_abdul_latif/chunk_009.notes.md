# shackle_risalo_shah_abdul_latif — chunk 009 — pp. 320–340

## What this chunk is

**Back matter, not Risalo text.** The whole range is the translator's **NOTES** apparatus, opening
with the heading `NOTES` (p. 320) and running under the head `notes` thereafter. It is organised by
sur: a short unnumbered **headnote** to each sur (musical mode, story, thematic structure, secondary
literature), followed by **numbered endnotes** keyed to verse numbers. The range covers surs
**1 Kalyan** (p. 320) through **27 Sorath**, breaking mid-note at note 4 of sur 27 (p. 340). Printed
page numbers run 293 to 313 (printed page = PDF page − 27). There is **no verse translation, no
Sindhi text in script except two Arabic words in note 27.4 (p. 340), and no index, glossary or
bibliography** in this range. The notes are definitional and interpretive, so the entries below are
short glosses and the translator's own headnote claims; they carry no shrine narrative, because the
apparatus contains none.

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Musical modes as the organising principle of the Risalo, with performance times.** Kalyan is "a
  musical mode associated with the late evening or early morning, which is used to express devotional
  themes" (p. 320); "Yaman Kalyan is a musical variant of Kalyan" (p. 321); "Asa is a musical mode
  that is performed at dawn" (p. 322); Desi "the name of a well-known musical mode" (p. 329);
  "Sirirag (Skt. Śrīrāga-) is the name of a well-known Indian musical mode" (p. 323); "Kamod is a
  musical mode associated with feelings of joy" (p. 333); "Sarang (S. sārangu) is both the name of a
  musical mode associated with the rainy season and a word meaning 'the rains'" (p. 337); "The
  familiar Indian mode Prabhati (S. piribhātī, Hindi prabhātī) is sung before dawn (S. piribhāti)"
  (p. 339); and "a musical mode performed in the early morning (S. vihāgu; compare Hindi bihāgṛā)"
  (p. 340).
- **The formal units of the text are named and glossed.** The **sur**, subdivided into sections —
  "The three sections (dāstān) of this sur are defned with unusual clarity by their subject matter"
  (p. 320); the **vāī** and the **abyāt** — "The exceptional inclusion here of several vāīs is
  intended as a demonstration of the way vāīs are used to punctuate sequences of abyāt throughout the
  Risālo" (p. 320); and the poet's verse called **baita** against the Qur'an's **āyatūṉ** (p. 326).
- **Patronage and the poet–performer relationship.** "A reference to the practice of a poet sharing
  the refreshments served to him with the singer who has performed his poetry" (p. 321). Against it,
  Sapar's patronage of musicians (p. 340) and the musician Bijal who "successfully demanded the
  latter's head as a reward for his performance" (p. 340).
- **Zikr, in two forms.** The formula *lā ilāha illā'llāh* "is recited with inhalation and exhalation
  of the breath in the Suf meditative exercise called zikr" (p. 321); and "the silent zikr, or
  internal repetition of the name of God, as opposed to the 'spoken formula' of the next line"
  (p. 321). One further practice is glossed only as "A demanding exercise of Suf spiritual
  discipline" (p. 322).
- **The four stages of the Sufi path, itemised.** "the four ascending stages on the Suf path, in
  which the Law (Ar. sharī‘at) is followed by the mystical Way (Ar. tarīqat), leading in turn to the
  higher levels of Reality (Ar. haqīqat) and Gnosis (Ar. ma‘rifat). Compare 3.51, 7.32" (p. 320),
  cross-referenced twice more (pp. 323, 325).
- **The shaikh as a requirement, stated in two Arabic sayings left untranslated**: "Ar. man lā
  shaikhun lahu fa-shaikhuhu 'l-shaiṭānu (Suf saying)" and "Ar. bilā shaikhin yamshī fī 'l-ṭarīqi
  [ka-man yamshī fī 'l-baḥri bilā safīnatin] (Suf saying)" (p. 329).
- **Hindu women's vow practice in the trading season, described as custom.** "It was the custom for
  the wives of the Hindu traders of Sindh to make oferings to the water deity for their husbands'
  safe return" (p. 324); "The making of a vow is marked by lighting lamps and by tying ribbons on
  trees" (p. 324); and "The Hindu festival of lamps celebrated in the autumn, which marked the
  beginning of the trading season" (p. 324).
- **Yogi initiation, dress and camp discipline.** "The 'split-ear' order of yogis whose initiation
  involves splitting the cartilage of the ear in order to insert the large wooden rings by which they
  are distinguished" (p. 335); "Yogis wear ochre-colored clothes and have their ears split to
  accommodate their large earrings" (p. 328); "From the bonfres that yogis light wherever they stay"
  (p. 336) and "A burning bonfre is the sign of a yogi's camping place" (p. 337); "Cotton quilts
  (S. ruliyūṉ) are associated with the poor" (p. 336); "The mantras that the yogis utter" (p. 336);
  and the foragers, "The yogis (S. khāhoṛī) who live on whatever they can fnd in the wilderness"
  (p. 329), "the yogis who roamed the wilderness gathering the vegetation that was their sole diet"
  (p. 336).
- **Yogi pilgrimage geography as a circuit.** The homeland "is regularly said to lie east, i.e., in
  the Gangetic valley where so many of the sacred sites of Hinduism are located" (p. 334); "Hind
  denotes the Gangetic region, as opposed to Sindh and the Indus valley" (p. 336); the sur's name
  "(S. pūrabu 'the east') relates to the Gangetic region that is the home of the yogis" (p. 337); a
  hill in District Hyderabad is "the starting point of the pilgrimage to Hinglaj" (p. 336); plus
  "Another remote destination that the yogis make pilgrimage to" (p. 336), unnamed.
- **Yogi instruments.** "The small animal horn (S. siṅī), which is the characteristic instrument of
  the yogis. Compare 18.118V" (p. 334); the bowed instrument "played with a bow, somewhat similar to
  the sārangī" (p. 335); "the double shawm (S. murilī), a wind instrument constructed with a gourd"
  (p. 336); and the comparative judgement that a "fne vāī in praise of the yogi's horn contrasts it
  with the inferior qualities of the various instruments mentioned in other surs of the Risālo"
  (p. 336). Against these the **rebab** "stands for the joys of music and pleasure, as opposed to the
  austerities of the pious life" (p. 326).
- **Hindu and Muslim ritual set side by side in sur 18's glosses.** "the ritual ablution (S. vuzū)
  performed before the Muslim prayers" (p. 336); "Instead of the Arabic call to prayer (S. bāngu)
  they hear the pre-Islamic sound of the sacred Hindu syllable (Skt. oṃ)" (pp. 336, printed across
  the page break at 335–336); and the **mihrab**, "The niche in the wall of a mosque marking the
  direction of Mecca toward which worshipers ofer prayer" (p. 335).
- **Caste as an institution, named and ranked.** "The various castes of fshermen, named in the sur as
  Gandiri, Mangar, Me, and Muhano, were traditionally ranked as the lowest of the low in Sindhi
  society, but Tamachi's favor promotes Nuri above his royal queens" (p. 333); the promotion is
  glossed literally as "made the Me girl a full human being" (S. māṛihū kayo me)" (p. 334), and its
  social limit as "the ladies of the court have no social interaction with the humble circles in which
  Nuri moves" (p. 334). For Sasui: "who belonged to the washerman caste (S. parīṭi ) but was born a
  Brahman" (p. 328). Slave naming: "Daulat, literally 'Prosperity,' the kind of name given to a slave
  girl" (p. 329).
- **Marriage and funeral ritual.** "a wedding ritual, in which it was believed to be unlucky if the
  groom's feet were not placed straight upon the bride's when he entered her parental home" (p. 331);
  and a measuring implement "Needed to measure out the grave that is to be dug" (p. 339).
- **Maritime trade and its hazards.** Sur 5 "uses the imagery of the sea voyages undertaken by the
  traders of Sindh as a symbol for human life" (p. 323); "Portuguese pirates had an evil reputation
  in the seas around Sindh" (p. 323); diving gear — "I.e., wearing diving masks" (p. 323); sur 6
  "(S. sāmūṉḍī 'sailor') is based on the theme of traders sailing away on voyages for business"
  (p. 324).
- **Fishing and its market.** Sur 17 "deals with the fate of a family of expert fshermen (S. ghātū)"
  (p. 334); "The verse pictures the collapse of the local fsh market, including the fshmongers and
  the tax collectors, following the loss of the fshermen" (p. 334), and credit is taken on "In order
  to carry on dealing with the fshmongers in the market" (p. 334).
- **Pastoral and agrarian economy.** "the pastoral tribes who grazed their bufaloes in the pastures
  watered by the Indus" (p. 324); "Camels are harnessed to turn the circular presses used to crush
  oil seeds" (p. 323); "The tribe of camel drivers" (p. 330) and "S. pārīsī, i.e., Balochi, the
  semi-Persian argot of the camel-herding tribes (S. jata)" (p. 329); "Temporary alluvial islands
  (S. beṭu) are regularly formed in the middle of the river" (p. 325).
- **Grain hoarding quantified.** "Meaning that the hoarders would make a 300 percent proft" (p. 338)
  — the only figure of any kind in the chunk.
- **Textile work as women's institution and as economy.** "The spinning place (S. ātaṇu) is where
  the young women gather to spin and enjoy each other's company" (p. 333); sur 25 "(S. kāpāitī
  'spinner')… deals with the popular theme of girls engaged in spinning", with the reward when "the
  thread they have spun is fnally assessed at its true price by the divine dealer in cotton"
  (p. 339); dyes — "The natural dye used by the nomads to color their clothes" (p. 333) and a
  substance "Used as a fxative in dyeing cloth" (p. 330).
- **Desert diet against courtly food.** The Maru dish is "A simple dish made from desert plants, as
  opposed to the richness of pulao, made from meat and rice" (p. 333); the well is monopolised "When
  the well is monopolized by men and their animals" (p. 333); foraged plants are "Gathered with great
  difculty" and "symbolize spiritual knowledge that is to be gleaned only with a comparable efort"
  (p. 336).
- **Calendar.** "The frst month of the rainy season in the Indian calendar, corresponding to
  July–August" (p. 338); a dye note on Eid — "For the Eid festival" (p. 333); the autumn festival of
  lamps opening the trading season (p. 324).
- **Editions and scholarship named.** Kazi 1961 and Baloch 2012 for the placement of a vāī (p. 326);
  Shackle 2022: 388–400 for the Suhini legend (p. 324); Shackle 1985 for the Panjabi *Sassī Punnūṉ*
  by Hasham Shah (p. 327); Shackle 2015 for Bullhe Shah (p. 339); "the illuminating study of this
  sur in Schimmel 1976: 219–235" (p. 334); and "a pioneering scholarly study in German (Trumpp
  1863), mainly focused on its linguistic features" (p. 340).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **A saint's enchanted milk sets the Suhini romance in motion**: her passion "was frst ignited when
  Sahar, also called Mehar… unwittingly gave her a cup of milk infused by a saint with the magical
  power to inspire love" (p. 325), cross-referenced as "the cup of enchanted milk that frst made
  Suhini fall in love with Mehar" (p. 325). The saint is not named (not stated).
- **The unfired pot and Suhini's drowning**: she "regularly crosses the Indus… using an earthen pot
  as a foat, until a member of her husband's family fnds out and substitutes an unfred pot… she
  drowns in the raging waters of the Indus" (p. 325).
- **Joint burial of Sasui and Punhun, twice**: the shepherd "digs the grave in which Punhun will
  also be buried when he eventually comes in search of her" (p. 328); and "When Punhun died after
  fnding Sasui's corpse, the lovers were buried together" (p. 331).
- **The crocodile of Kalachi**: "the six brothers of the fsherman Moriro were killed by a monstrous
  crocodile that lived in the whirlpool of Kalachi… and how Moriro then contrived the monster's
  destruction" (p. 334), reported by the translator as "a local legend".
- **Yogis destroy the snakes of Junagarh by magic**: "a legend in which the snakes that infested the
  jungle surrounding Junagarh were destroyed by yogis with their magical powers" (p. 337).
- **The enchanted palace of Kak**: Mumal "used to lure lovers to their death. When Rano succeeded in
  overcoming her enchantments, Mumal fell in love with him" (p. 331); a later action "destroyed the
  enchantment that had been placed over Kak" (p. 332).
- **The oyster and the pearl, given as popular belief**: "According to popular belief, the oyster
  does not drink water from the ocean or river where it lives. It waits instead for clouds to appear
  in the sky, when it opens to drink the raindrops that it lives on" (p. 333); "Pearls are thought to
  be produced by the hunger and thirst the oyster sufers in the sea" (p. 333).
- **The primal covenant as a scene**: "Ar. alastu bi-rabbikum (Qur'an 7.172), the question addressed
  by God to the unborn souls on the day of creation" (pp. 320–321), and again with the answer —
  "'Am I not your lord?' They said, 'Yes, [we so testify]'" (p. 326).
- **Satan's fall re-read as fidelity**: "His apparent rebellion is explained by Sufs as a
  manifestation of his refusal to accept any authority besides God" (p. 322).
- **The reckoning at death**: "the soul will be held to account for the record of its previous life
  that has been maintained by the angels Munkir and Nakir" (p. 326); "The recording angels Munkir and
  Nakir maintain a complete account of a person's life, which is presented to them at the moment of
  death" (p. 320).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **Shah Latif is read as a deliberately indirect poet, and this is the chunk's strongest
  interpretive claim.** Of *anā 'l-ḥaqq*: "The oblique reference to this notorious expression, so
  frequently invoked by other Suf poets like Bullhe Shah and Sachal Sarmast, is a telling
  illustration of Shah Latif's preference for the indirect expression of a profoundly Suf
  understanding of the world" (p. 326).
- **Every romance is read allegorically, and the translator says so explicitly each time.** Suhini —
  "Suhini's courage is upheld as an example to all true seekers of the divine, as opposed to the
  insincere who rely on mechanical aids to cross the river… the vast river… symbolizes the ocean of
  existence that every soul must traverse" (p. 325); Sasui — the story serves "as a powerful image
  for the devoted pursuit of the divine beloved" (p. 328); Lila Chanesar — "Through the tacit
  equation of Chanesar with God, the theme of the divine jealousy of rival objects of worship is
  also developed" (p. 331); Ghatu — "The story naturally lends itself to interpretation as an
  allegory of the struggle against the power of the lower self" (p. 334); Sorath — "Rai Diyach here
  represents the Suf seeker who is required to give up everything, even his life" (p. 340); and the
  camel of sur 4 "personifes the lower self (Ar. nafs) with its stubborn resistance to the spiritual
  life" (p. 323).
- **A nativist reading of sur 15 is flagged as available rather than asserted**: "The sur also allows
  for a nativist interpretation that exalts the authenticity of the values and landscape of Sindh"
  (p. 332). Relatedly, "The fnal invocation of God's blessings on Sindh is one of the most often
  quoted verses of the Risālo" (p. 338).
- **The Arabic register of sur 15 is read as an ethnographic parallel**: "The many Arabic quotations
  in this section suggest a parallel between the Marus (here actually called S. i‘rābiyuni) and the
  noble Bedouin of Arabia" (p. 332).
- **Sur 18's religious mixing is called remarkable in the author's own voice**: "The multiple
  religious references in this verse are remarkable for their blurring of conventional boundaries"
  (p. 335); "As elsewhere, Shah Latif's description of the yogis' freedom from narrow religious
  constraints moves naturally from a Hindu to an Islamic frame of reference" (p. 336). The same note
  set records that Ram is "used as the common Hindu name for God, although yogis are
  characteristically associated with Shiv" (p. 336).
- **The Sindhi Suhini differs from the Panjabi version usually cited**: "The Sindhi version, which is
  rather diferent from the Panjabi story cited in most modern descriptions, is set among the pastoral
  tribes who grazed their bufaloes in the pastures watered by the Indus" (p. 324).
- **The translator records disagreement among editors and his own uncertainty, four times.** On the
  placement of sur 3 — "Asa is a musical mode that is performed at dawn, and in most editions this
  sur is placed toward the end of the Risālo. But its contents are similar in character to those of
  the two opening surs" (p. 322); "This is a riddling verse variously explained by diferent editors"
  (p. 324); "The compressed expression is not entirely clear" (p. 322); "This seems to be the meaning
  of this highly condensed half-line" (p. 334); and hedged glosses at "Apparently referring to the
  double shawm" (p. 336), "Here the sense seems to be that merely holding a horn is not necessarily a
  sign of the true yogi" (p. 336), and "This is variously interpreted to indicate the mystery of
  humility, or the special character of man, formed from clay" (p. 326).
- **Wordplay and alliteration are treated as evidence the translation loses.** "The original
  alliteration is between S. sikaṇu 'desire' and S. sūrī 'the gallows'" (p. 322); "There is wordplay
  between Ar. fī'l-ḥaqīqat 'in reality' and fīl 'elephant'" (p. 323); "the close verbal similarity in
  Sindhi between mīṉhuṉ 'rain' and nīṉhuṉ 'love'" (p. 338); and register — "Literally, those respond
  with the polite jīu 'yes,' rather than the rude baudu 'yeah?'" (p. 322).
- **Formal features are given technical explanations**: verses 11.1–2 "are macaronics that include
  several Arabic words besides this longer phrase" (p. 330); the rhyme of a vāī "involves the use of
  numerous Arabic loanwords, which in turn generate some unusual images" (p. 326); and one note
  reaches for Arabic grammar — the assimilation rule "so that, e.g., Ar. ja‘ala laka becomes
  ja‘allaka" (p. 327).
- **Speaker attribution is supplied by the translator where the verse leaves it open**, which
  matters for anyone quoting the poem: "the quotation marks indicate the words of the woman who has
  been left behind" (p. 324); "This and the following verses are to be understood as laments by one
  of the dead fshermen's wives" (p. 334); "The speaker here is the poet himself" (p. 333); "The last
  line is addressed to God" (p. 328); "The mountain passes reply to Sasui's question" (p. 331).
- **A gloss defends the Sufis against condemnation**: "Since he is omnipresent, why should the Sufs
  who reveal this be condemned?" (p. 328).
- **Formal religion is judged from inside the notes**: "The practitioners of formal religion fail to
  understand the spiritual signifcance of scripture" (p. 321).
- **One note concedes a sur's title figure is not its subject**: "Sorath is not the heroine of this
  sur, which instead focuses on the demand for supreme self-sacrifce on the part of Rai Diyach"
  (p. 340).

## Doubts

- **The archive mapping in this chunk is thin and is all biographical, not topographical.** Four ids
  are touched — `bhit-bhit-shah`, `dargah-roza-sufi-shah-inayat-shaheed`, `mazar-of-bulleh-shah`,
  `shrine-of-sachal-sarmast` — and in **every case the chunk names a person, not a shrine**. Only
  `bhit-bhit-shah` gets a place-name at all, and "Bhit" is given there as a residence (p. 338). Any
  entry using these bullets must not let the archive row's own location or death year leak into a
  citation of this page.
- **Bullhe Shah and Sachal Sarmast are mapped on the strength of a single comparative aside each**
  (pp. 326, 339). A human may prefer to demote both to "figures not in the archive", since the
  chunk's interest in them is purely as parallel poets. The spelling printed is **"Bullhe Shah"**,
  not the archive's "Bulleh Shah"; the archive names the figure "Bulleh Shah (Abdullah Shah Qadri)"
  and the chunk gives no second name, so the identification rests on the name alone.
- **Hinglaj cannot be cited to an archive id.** The index row named *Shaktipeeth Shri Hinglaj Mata
  Mandir* has an **empty id column** — the only such row — so there is no id to bold, exactly as
  chunk 006 found. Note also that the naming is split across two pages: the descriptive gloss "A
  remote site in Balochistan, sacred to the goddess (S. nānī)" (p. 335) does not itself print the
  word Hinglaj, which appears only at p. 336. A human must decide whether that row gets an id.
- **"Lahut" (p. 335) must not be mapped to `shah-noorani-shrine-syed-bilawal-shah-noorani`.** The
  chunk calls Lahut "a small place in **Sindh** on the yogis' pilgrimage route", while the archive
  row places Lahoot Lamakan valley in **Khuzdar District, Balochistan**. The book also offers the
  alternative that *lāhūtī* denotes a sect and not a place at all. Two readings, two provinces: flag,
  do not pick.
- **The unnamed hill in "District Hyderabad in southern Sindh" (p. 336)** is a pilgrimage
  starting-point and therefore the kind of site the archive collects, but the note gives no name.
  Unplaceable from here.
- **Umarkot (pp. 332, 338) must not be mapped to `umarkot-amarkot-shiv-mandir`.** The chunk names
  only "his fortress of Umarkot" and "the city of Umarkot"; no temple, and no religious site of any
  kind there.
- **Junagarh, Dwarka, Medina, Kech, Bhambhor, Kak and Las Bela are all outside the index** and are
  filed as such. `chandragup-baba-chandragup` sits in the Lasbela district the chunk repeatedly names
  (pp. 327, 328, 330, 337, 340), but Chandragup itself is never mentioned and no mapping is implied.
- **"Ali, the nephew of the Prophet" (p. 335) is what the book prints.** Quoted as it stands; the
  relationship term is the translator's, and a human should check it against the printed page before
  the sentence is quoted in an entry.
- **The jalālī/jamālī glosses are printed in the order "devotees of divine beauty (Ar. jalālī) and
  those of divine majesty (Ar. jamālī)" (p. 334).** Whether that pairing is the book's intent, a
  printing slip or an extraction transposition cannot be settled from this text layer. Do not quote
  the note as a definition without checking the page.
- **Section heading vs. body spelling conflict: "4 Khambhat" is the heading, and the first word of
  its headnote is "Khanbat"** (p. 323). Both are in the chunk; neither is corrected here.
- **Two notes on the same page both cite Qur'an 39.53 with different Arabic** (p. 330, notes 5 and
  6). Not resolvable from here; noted in case it is a slip.
- **Untranslated Arabic and Persian throughout.** A large number of notes give only "Ar. …" or
  "Pers. …" with a Qur'an, Hadith or "(Suf saying)" citation and **no English gloss** — e.g. the
  shaikh sayings (p. 329), "Ar. fānī fī 'llāhi" (p. 322), "Ar. inna auliyāī taḥta qabāī (Hadith)"
  (p. 323), "Ar. ashhadu" (p. 323). Anything that depends on those phrases cannot be read from this
  chunk.
- **The chunk is keyed to verses it does not contain.** Every numbered note points at a sur-and-verse
  reference (1.47, 7.63, 18.118V, and so on) whose text is in other chunks; the cross-references
  "see introductory note to 30" (p. 330) and "See 27" (p. 336) point past the end of this chunk.
  Pairing a gloss with its verse is a Pass 2 job.
- **Unnamed referents.** Several notes gloss a name without repeating it and so cannot be resolved
  here: "The master revered by most orders of yogis" (p. 336) — **no name is given, and none should
  be supplied**; "One of the names of Satan" (p. 321) and "A name of the devil" (p. 328); "A common
  desert shrub", "A desert plant with poisonous milky juice", "A fragrant plant", "A variety of
  sandal" (p. 323); "The bird that brings messages from the beloved" (p. 324); "A place in Sindh"
  (p. 338); "A place in Balochistan, to the west of Sindh" (p. 337); "Epithet of Punhun" (p. 330);
  "A royal title" (p. 332); "Epithet of Sapar" and "Another epithet of Sapar" (p. 340).
- **No folio numbers exist in this book**, so every reference above is a PDF page. The printed page
  numbers visible in this range are 293 (p. 320) through 313 (p. 340), i.e. printed = PDF − 27, the
  same offset chunk 006 recorded.
- **Systematic text-layer damage: the fi/fl ligatures and the doubled f are lost throughout, and
  "Suf"/"Sufs"/"Sufsm" for "Sufi"/"Sufis"/"Sufism" affects the single most frequent term in the
  chunk.** Per instruction these are **not marked inline and not corrected**, and every quotation
  above is reproduced as the text layer stands. Damaged words that appear inside quotations used
  above include: Suf, Sufs, Sufsm, Hanaf, fdā, fnal, fnally, fne, fnd, fnding, fnds, frst, fsh,
  fshermen, fsherman, fshergirl, fshmongers, fre, fercely, fash, fash-, flls, fowing, foat, fute,
  defned, refected, inficts, inficted, sufer, sufers, sufering, suferings, ofer, oferings, of (for
  "off"), diferent, difculty, efort, signifcance, infection, artifcial, safron, proft, munifcence,
  benefcence, infnite, infested, self-sacrifce, superfcially, bufaloes, bufalo, unfred, fve, ffth,
  fame, yaġhfru, and the Arabic transliteration "yafrru" (p. 324). Note that **chunk 006 marked such
  words inline with `[text-layer?]` and this file does not**, on instruction; a consolidator must not
  read the absence of markers here as a claim that the words are sound.
- **Two quotations where the damage could change the sense and the PDF should be consulted**: "Shah
  Latif's fnal composition" (p. 326), where "fnal" is unambiguous in context but printed damaged;
  and "The fnal short vowels marking infection (Ar. i‘rāb, here the rhyme word)" (p. 327), where
  "infection" is the term of Arabic grammar and the intended word is not recoverable from the text
  layer alone.
- **Layout damage on p. 327.** From note 22 onward the indentation of the note bodies collapses and
  the text sits flush left for the rest of that page and the start of sur 8's headnote; nothing
  appears to be lost, but the note-number-to-body alignment on that page should be checked.
- **The chunk ends mid-note.** Note 4 of sur 27 breaks at "the word Ahad ("احد the One," i.e., God,
  is distinguished from the name Ahmad (احمد), i.e., the" (p. 340). This is also the only place in
  the chunk where Arabic script survives the extraction, and it is printed with the parenthesis and
  quotation marks displaced around the script. Its completion is in the next chunk.
