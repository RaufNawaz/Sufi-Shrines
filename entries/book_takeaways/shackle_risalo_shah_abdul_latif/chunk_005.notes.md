# shackle_risalo_shah_abdul_latif — chunk 005 — pp. 157–196

Continuous translated text of the Risalo, running from the middle of **Sur Kohiyari** (sur 11)
through **Sur Husaini** (12), **Sur Lila Chanesar** (13), **Sur Mumal Rano** (14) and into
**Sur Marui** (15), which breaks off mid-verse at the chunk's end. **No shrine, tomb, urs,
custodian or endowment of any archive site is mentioned anywhere in these forty pages.** The
chunk's value to the archive is what the verse shows of wayfaring, jogis, caste, music and
craft, and a short list of citable lines — set out below. The only trace of the archive's
expected row is the poet's own signature inside the verses.

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **`bhit-bhit-shah`** (the poet signs himself "Abdul Latif", "Latif" and "Shah"): this chunk
  contains **no statement of any kind about the shrine at Bhit, about Bhit Shah, or about the
  poet's life, death or burial**. What it does carry is his signature line inside the verse,
  in three forms: "Sisters, says Abdul Latif, such is the guide" (p. 162), "Sisters, says Abdul
  Latif, may my beloved show mercy to me" (p. 177), "I have come, beloved, says Abdul Latif, and
  entered your door" (p. 183), "Sisters, Abdul Latif says that the supreme lord will show his
  grace" (p. 194); "says Latif" (pp. 174, 186, 187, 189, 194, 195, 196); "says Shah" (pp. 163,
  173, 186). The signature falls in the closing or near-closing verse of each sur. Nothing beyond
  this attaches the chunk to the archive row — see Doubts.

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

All of the following are **personae and place-names of the poems**, recorded as the book prints
them. None is presented by the chunk as a historical person with a shrine.

- **Sasui** — the grieving heroine of Sur Kohiyari and Sur Husaini; she searches the mountains for
  Punhun after he is taken from Bhambhor (pp. 157–177). Called "the Baloch's slave girl" (p. 158),
  "The Brahman girl" (pp. 165, 168), and "The washergirl" who "left her village in a passion"
  (p. 174). "Hundreds and thousands of Sasuis roam in search of their Hôt beloveds" (p. 164).
  She dies on the road: "The lord of Kech acted as the pallbearer of Sasui's corpse… The son of
  Ari brought her from the mountain passes, says Latif. The wretched girl was granted a place at
  his feet." (p. 174, with note marker 11). That burial at the beloved's feet is the nearest the
  chunk comes to a grave; it is stated of a poem's heroine, not of a site.
- **Punhun** — the Baloch beloved, also "Hôt Punhun" (p. 158), "Ari Jam" (pp. 158, 160, 167, 174),
  "Ari Punhun" (p. 176), "Punhun Jam" (p. 167), "the peerless Ari" (p. 166), "the mountain lord"
  (p. 158), "That wise mountain dweller" (p. 160). He is removed by force: "The Baloch took my
  husband Punhun Jam on a journey by force, although they had seemed so friendly in Bhambhor"
  (p. 167).
- **Lila** — heroine of Sur Lila Chanesar, who exchanged her husband for a necklace: "You exchanged
  for the jewels the lord who is the delight of your eyes" (p. 178); "When Lila saw the treasure,
  she slipped into selfsh [text-layer?] thoughts" (p. 180). She calls herself "the leading lady in
  Chanesar's kingdom" (pp. 180, 181).
- **Chanesar** — "Prince Chanesar" (pp. 178, 181, 182), also addressed as **Dasaro** (pp. 181, 182,
  183); "The king is a mighty ruler and very jealous. The lands that lie in every direction are in
  awe of Prince Chanesar" (p. 178); "Chanesar is a skilled appraiser who discovers secret thoughts
  and examines hearts" (p. 180).
- **Kaunru** — the rival who won Chanesar by the necklace: "Kaunru's trickery beat me" (p. 178);
  "Chanesar withdrew his afections [text-layer?] and turned to the maidservant" (p. 179); "Kaunru
  sleeps in the same quilts, occupying that fair abode" (p. 180).
- **Mumal** — "the Gujar" girl of Kak, heroine of Sur Mumal Rano: "Wanton Mumal slays the hunting
  princes. She has made many learned pandits and pīrs tremble" (p. 185); "There are steel axes in
  the pupils of the Gujar's eyes" (p. 185).
- **Rano** — the Sodho prince, also called **Mendhiro** (pp. 187, 188, 190, 191, 192): "No one is
  as beautiful in appearance as Rano, the Sodho is fairer than all" (p. 187); "The arrow fred
  [text-layer?] by Mendhiro has struck her in the head" (p. 187). Also "The four friends, the Sodho
  huntsmen" who "rode swiftly on toward Kak" and "did not return" (p. 186).
- **Marui** — heroine of Sur Marui, held captive and longing for her people: "Oh Sumiro, why did
  you put my body in chains? Lord Hamir has acted cruelly in chaining my body" (p. 195); "I am
  confned [text-layer?] in the prison of water" (p. 195); "Umar, may your hands grant freedom to
  the shepherds" (p. 195); "I would return to my native place and see the land of Malir" (p. 196).
- **Husain**, and an unnamed woman who first mourned him — "It was the lady12 who frst
  [text-layer?] mourned in Husaini for Husain. Afterward the world came to learn about it"
  (p. 176). The woman is identified only by the note marker 12, whose note is outside this chunk.
- **Ahmad** — invoked at the hour of death: "Oh Ahmad, come there at the time of difculty
  [text-layer?]" (p. 194).
- Places named, as printed: **the Pab** (mountains, pp. 157, 162, 172); **Kech** and its people the
  **Kechi** (pp. 158, 160, 162, 164, 166, 167, 171, 174, 175, 176); **Bhambhor** (pp. 165, 166,
  167, 168, 176); **Las Bela**, reached by "crossing over the Manban" (p. 167); **the Vankar**,
  which Sasui enters (p. 163); **Kak**, with its "landing places", "red-painted palace" and
  gardens (pp. 184–194); **the Ludano** (pp. 185, 190, 194); **the Dhat** (pp. 188, 190);
  **Malir** (p. 196); **the Harho** (pp. 164, 166, 172, unglossed — see Doubts).
- Peoples and groups named: the **Baloch** and **Hôt** (throughout pp. 157–177); the **Arichos**
  / **Aricho** (pp. 158, 159); the **Sodho** (pp. 186–194); the **Gujar** (pp. 185, 187); the
  **Marus** (pp. 195, 196); "Ranas, Rajputs, and Mumals" and "lords and Hamirs" (p. 194).

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The surs are printed as numbered musical modes, and the chunk gives five names as printed**:
  "11 || kohiyari" (running head, pp. 158, 160, 162); "12 Husaini" (heading, p. 163) with running
  head "12 || husaini"; "13 Lila Chanesar" (heading, p. 178), "13 || lila chanesar"; "14 Mumal
  Rano" (heading, p. 184), "14 || mumal rano"; "15 Marui" (heading, p. 195), "15 || marui"
  (p. 196). Verses are numbered within each sur.
- **The mode Husaini is named inside its own poem, and tied to mourning for Husain.** "Mother, I
  have been slain by the sound of Husaini.5" (p. 168); "It was the lady12 who frst [text-layer?]
  mourned in Husaini for Husain. Afterward the world came to learn about it" (p. 176). A
  statement, in the verse itself, that the mode carries a mourning practice.
- **Instruments of a royal reception**: "Drums and tabors and pipes would welcome me" (p. 180);
  "I was seated in the midst of the company with drums and tabors and pipes" (p. 181). No other
  instrument is named in the chunk.
- **Jogis, fakirs and ascetics — the fullest material in the chunk, at the opening of Sur Mumal
  Rano.** "Yesterday we met a yogi,1 a wandering beggar. The master had a ring of cloth on his head
  and a fne [text-layer?] necklace around his neck. Our heart was wounded by the fakir when he
  appeared before us" (p. 184). The chunk uses **yogi**, **fakir**, **ascetic** and **master**
  for the same figure across pp. 184–193. Further details as printed: "a yogi, shining like the
  moon" (p. 184); "a yogi whose body was covered with clay. Wearing a green shawl around his
  shoulders, the master had a necklace of gold" (p. 184); "The ascetic went into the desert in a
  state of ecstasy" (p. 184); "A fragrance like that of attar came from his forehead. The ascetic
  showed us the place where he had been dyed red" (p. 184); "the jewelry of love" (p. 185);
  "dear yogi who wears a loincloth" (p. 185); "the yogis who charm snakes" (p. 185); "He slipped
  of [text-layer?] leaving his staf [text-layer?] behind,6" (p. 189).
- **Ascetic indifference stated as a test, twice over**: "Kak did not detain the ascetics, its
  palaces did not beguile them. They were not trapped by the snares of the ladies or their
  maidservants. The yogis paid no attention to the lovely women" (p. 187); "Kak did not detain the
  ascetics, its riches did not beguile them. Being in a lofty spiritual state, they caused
  sufering [text-layer?] to the lovely women" (pp. 187–188). And conversely, lovers become
  ascetics: "To see them, says Latif, they have become ascetics. The yogis come to Kak, dyed red
  by love" (p. 187).
- **Antinomian statement about religion**: "If you seek the beloved, turn your back on the practice
  of abstinence. Those who have seen the Baloch have distanced themselves from all religion"
  (p. 171).
- **Religious specialists named as a class, twice, and both times as subject to a higher power**:
  "She has made many learned pandits and pīrs tremble" (p. 185); "The learned, pundits and pīrs,
  even lords and Hamirs9 will die" (p. 194). Note the two spellings — see Doubts.
- **Graves as a place one is told to go and see**: "Go and see the foreigners' graves on the banks
  by Kak" (p. 185).
- **Wayfaring: the caravan, the guide, and the robbers.** "Do not become separated from the caravan,
  for the company is going to climb the mountain passes. Otherwise you will be left behind and be
  unable to fnd [text-layer?] the way they have gone" (p. 171); "I asked the experts about the
  rocks and the path's ups and downs. Those who go alone fall prey to bandits. Without a guide,
  this difcult [text-layer?] land does not become easy" (pp. 175–176); "There are hundreds and
  thousands of robbers in the wilderness. Oh you who travel on foot, take a guide with you in the
  desert" (p. 176); "In the passes there are bushes that draw blood, and there are enemies on my
  path" (p. 161). The waterless stage: "There is no water, the journey is long, in front of me are
  nothing but sand and wasteland" (p. 163).
- **Camel transport and the men who run it**: "the whole tribe of camel men" (p. 164); "The camel
  men did not do what Ari Jam had said" (p. 174); "Their camels passed into Las Bela, crossing
  over the Manban.4" (p. 167); "his camels have become highwaymen" (p. 169); "leading a train of
  camels" (p. 176); "The foragers said: 'Search for him, the way to Kech stretches far ahead'"
  (p. 174).
- **Spinning, carding and the spinning party**: "Do not card a cotton ball for me, mother. Kick the
  spinning wheel and throw the rolls of yarn in the water. The mountain dweller for whom I spun has
  gone to Kech" (p. 164); "Their lovely appearance stops anything being said in the spinning party"
  (p. 186).
- **Cloth printing and dyeing, including the mordant**: "The girls learned to print on cloth, making
  Punhun their pattern" (p. 166); "First he plunges me into alum,4 then he colors me with dye"
  (p. 159); "Millions are dyed as red as betel juice with love for Mumal" (p. 186).
- **Other trades named**: the washergirl (p. 174); the blacksmith and his quench — "For once put out
  this fre [text-layer?] with a shower of water, like the blacksmith does.8" (p. 172); shepherds,
  addressed directly as a source of route information — "I ask you, shepherd,3 about my beloved"
  (p. 159), "the shepherd would be shattered" (p. 160), "may your hands grant freedom to the
  shepherds.6" (p. 195); nomads and Bedouin — "Without the Bedouin I have to sufer [text-layer?]
  troubles here" (p. 196), "I pine for the nomads" (p. 196).
- **Agricultural and industrial furniture of the verse**: an irrigation waterwheel, "they have
  fooded [text-layer?] so much that the waterwheel is submerged" (p. 169); kilns, "whole kilns of
  noses on their faces,8" (p. 193); the garden produce of Kak, "walnuts, grapes, fne [text-layer?]
  sandal trees, and lotus fowers [text-layer?]" (p. 186).
- **Luxury goods and household scent, itemised**: "The green shawls they wore were like betel
  leaves. They had made their bodies fresh with attar and ambergris. Their braided hair was
  impregnated with musk and sandal. The lovely woman's ears were decorated with silver and gold"
  (p. 186); "The golden-colored Sodho women play with silver. Incense sticks burn in their reception
  rooms; their divans are scented with musk. Ewers of perfumed water are poured out in their
  bathrooms" (p. 187); "braided hair is always perfumed with jasmine oil" (p. 186); "The Sodho
  prince cleverly threw down a betel nut.4" (p. 186).
- **Caste as a social fact the poems turn on.** Sasui's caste is the obstacle: "It was in Bhambhor
  that he came to know about my caste.3" (p. 166); "Mother, my caste is a disgrace to the people of
  Kech" (p. 166); "My neighbors did not hide my disgrace. They revealed my caste to gain favor with
  Punhun" (p. 168); "Let news of my caste not reach Kech. Otherwise Punhun may be ashamed of me
  before his people" (p. 175); "I am base-born and unworthy of the Baloch tribe" (p. 174). Against
  that, at the close of Sur Mumal Rano: "Why ask about caste or tribe? Anyone who has come is
  acceptable" (p. 194).
- **Servitude, rank and the inner apartments**: "Whatever I am, I am the Baloch's slave girl…
  I am less even than the slippers he wears" (p. 158); "I was greeted in the inner apartments by
  maids, slaves, and attendants" (p. 181).
- **Kin obligation and marriage**: "If I was their kinswoman, I should have complained about my
  brothers-in-law" (p. 166); "I saw that the groom's feet were crooked at the time of the
  covering.2" (p. 179) — a wedding rite named only as "the covering", with note marker 2 whose
  note lies outside the chunk; "Wrap the hem of your garment humbly around your neck and embrace
  poverty" (p. 182), repeated as "the hem of the shawl that is humbly wrapped around my neck"
  (p. 183) — a gesture of supplication; swings, "When I sat on swings3 I had no awareness"
  (p. 181).
- **Punishment and death imagery drawn from practice**: "She who is wedded to the Baloch will fnd
  [text-layer?] her body up on the scafold [text-layer?], as she weeps tears of blood" (p. 170);
  "In his fury he swiftly sentenced his happy brides to rejection" (p. 182).
- **Divination and message-sending**: "In search of you I have fown [text-layer?] many crows from
  Kak" (p. 188); letters between kin across the desert, "Who will bring me letters from them?"
  (p. 196), "How can I write letters that fate seizes and rips in half?" (p. 196); the traveler as
  courier, "Traveler, give this message to my beloved from the Dhat" (p. 190).
- **Timekeeping by watch and by star**: "Yesterday we met a yogi, one watch after sunrise" (p. 184);
  "As I stood I saw the stars rise; they have all now set" (p. 188); "The Pleiades have set, the
  triple stars of Orion have risen. Rano has not come tonight; the time that he was due has passed"
  (p. 188).
- **Scripture quoted inside the verse**, printed in italics in the source and each carrying a note
  marker whose note is outside this chunk: "Do not despair of the mercy of God 5 is what the beloved
  himself said. Indeed God forgives all sins,6 this is a true sign" (p. 162); "It is truly said that
  All living creatures must taste death.10" (p. 194); "When Am I not your lord? fell on my ears,
  then and there I said with my heart, They said 'Yes.' 1" (p. 195); "the time of Be and it was.2"
  and "There was no Be and it was" (p. 195); "The pen dried after writing what was to happen,5 and
  no alteration to that is possible" (p. 195). Also "You will think a great deal about the Sodho on
  the day of resurrection" (p. 189) and "All things will return to their origin; 8" (p. 196).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

No miracle is attributed to a historical saint anywhere in the chunk. The following marvels are
stated of the poems' personae, as the book states them.

- Weeping dissolves stone: "the tears on the cheeks of those who grieve do not dry up. They dissolve
  the stones of the Pab" (p. 157).
- The wild animals grieve at Sasui's story: "When they heard her story, the wild animals were
  grieved" (p. 157); "Wherever I spend the night, the wild animals are my friends" (p. 167).
- Cosmology stated as fact in the verse: "The mountains are the pegs of the earth,2 the beloved is
  the one who fxes [text-layer?] them" (p. 157).
- Speech that would unmake the landscape: "If I revealed only a little of my true condition, the
  wild animals would be struck dumb and the rocks would be split, the trees would be burned and no
  fresh vegetation would grow" (p. 160); and again, "No rocks would remain in place and the
  mountains would all be burned up" (p. 160).
- The yogi's light and fragrance: "The yogi's face was flled [text-layer?] with a light like that of
  the sun at dawn. A fragrance like that of attar came from his forehead" (p. 184); "The master shed
  a light like that of the full moon. Darkness was removed by the yogi's light" (p. 193); "The whole
  land was made fragrant by the scent of musk. It was from there that the perfect master came"
  (p. 193).
- The burning of Kak: "Kak is consumed, the trees have gone, my heart burns" (p. 189); "Kak is
  consumed, the trees have gone, and the red-painted palace is burned down" (pp. 189–190); and
  Sasui's own act, "I have set fre [text-layer?] to Bhambhor and have come to you" (p. 176).
- Presence in absence, stated as paradox: "Do not be downcast, I am not far away from you. Though it
  may appear I am far from you, we are actually together."9 (p. 173).

## Doubts

- **No archive row is informed by this chunk.** Forty pages of translated verse containing no
  shrine, no tomb, no urs or mela, no custodian, no endowment and no building date. Treat a
  consolidation bullet drawn from here as literary or social context only.
- **`bhit-bhit-shah` is not supported by this chunk except through the book's authorship.** The
  poet's signature is printed as "Abdul Latif", "Latif" and "Shah" (pp. 162, 163, 173, 174, 177,
  183, 186, 187, 189, 194, 195, 196). The words "Bhittai", "Bhit" and "Bhit Shah" appear nowhere
  in the chunk, and nothing here states where he lived, died or is buried. The identification with
  the archive row rests on the title page, not on these pages.
- **The expected facing original is not here.** There is no Sindhi in Perso-Arabic script and no
  transliteration anywhere in pp. 157–196 — English translation only, with alternating running
  heads ("risalo" on one page, "12 || husaini" and the like on the next) and verse numbers set on
  the left of one page and the right of the next. Whether the facing original pages are absent from
  the PDF or merely absent from its text layer **cannot be determined from the chunk**, and a human
  should check the PDF before anyone records that this edition prints no original.
- **No translator's headnote or note text falls in this range.** Endnote markers are present and
  numbered afresh within each sur — e.g. "the pegs of the earth,2" (p. 157), "I ask you,
  shepherd,3" (p. 159), "plunges me into alum,4" (p. 159), "Husaini.5" (p. 168), "the lady12"
  (p. 176), "the time of the covering.2" (p. 179), "the pair of them4" (p. 183), "a yogi,1"
  (p. 184), "the Sodho huntsmen,3" (p. 186), "Hamirs9" (p. 194), "the red lac9" (p. 196) — but
  **every note they point to lies outside the chunk**, so each gloss (including the identity of
  "the lady" who mourned Husain, and the meaning of "the covering") is unavailable here.
- **Systematic text-layer damage: the `fi`/`fl`/`ff` ligatures are dropped throughout.** Quoted as
  printed above and flagged `[text-layer?]` at each occurrence in the body. Representative list:
  "suferings" (p. 157), "fxes"/"fnd" (p. 157), "afected" (p. 158), "diferent" (p. 161), "of
  track" for "off track" (p. 161), "aficted" (p. 161), "fee Bhambhor" for "flee Bhambhor"
  (p. 165), "bonfre"/"fres"/"fames" (p. 164), "fooded" (p. 169), "scafold" (p. 170), "inficted"
  (pp. 171, 178), "difcult"/"difculty" (pp. 176, 194), "sacrifced" (pp. 170, 190), "crucifed"
  (p. 191), "selfood" for "selfhood" (p. 191), "firting" (p. 182), "fulflled"/"flled" (pp. 168,
  184), "he slipped of leaving his staf behind" (p. 189), "went of" (p. 191), "fown many crows"
  (p. 188). **Do not repair these when quoting; quote as printed.** Note that diacritics survive
  intact ("Hôt", "pīrs"), which points to a font-mapping fault rather than general damage.
- **One dropped word, not a ligature**: "In the future do no leave this beggar, oh guide" (p. 194)
  — printed "do no", presumably "do not", but that is a guess and is recorded as printed.
- **Two spellings of the same word within nine pages**: "pandits" (p. 185) and "pundits" (p. 194).
  Prefer neither without checking the print.
- **The verse-number suffix "V" is unexplained by the chunk.** Four verses are numbered 42V
  (p. 162), 97V (p. 176), 30V (p. 183) and 64V (p. 194). Each is the last verse of its sur, each is
  set in longer lines than the rest, and each closes with the poet's signature. The chunk gives no
  key to the letter, so do not gloss it.
- **Speech marking is unreliable in this chunk.** Closing quotation marks appear with no opening
  mark in view at pp. 157 ("…when he abandoned me.”"), 161 ("…among the Baloch.”") and elsewhere;
  where a verse is first-person speech, it is often not possible to tell from this chunk where the
  speech begins or who is speaking. Attribute a quotation to "the poem" rather than to a named
  speaker unless the verse names them.
- **Three words are used in the chunk without any gloss and cannot be identified from it**: "the
  Harho", which appears as something cursed alongside the Hôt (p. 164), as something with a "lord"
  (p. 166), and as something that can be melted with blood (p. 172) — place, tribe or person is
  undetermined; "the Vankar", which Sasui "enters" (p. 163); and "the Arichos"/"the Aricho"
  (pp. 158, 159), apparently the Baloch party or its people.
- **Chunk boundaries cut two verses.** The chunk opens mid-verse at p. 157 ("been abandoned by the
  one they love…"), the first numbered verse being 12 of Sur Kohiyari; and it ends mid-verse at
  p. 196, Sur Marui verse 11 breaking off at "They have removed the red lac9 from". Neither part
  verse should be quoted from this chunk alone.
- **Page-number offset, recorded so nobody re-derives it**: every page in this chunk carries the
  book's printed page number in its footer, and it runs a constant **27 below** the PDF page index
  — PDF p. 157 = printed 130, p. 170 = 143, p. 190 = 163, p. 196 = 169. The chunk carries no
  `[folio N]` lines, so page references above are given in the `(p. N)` form as PDF indices.
- **One archive-adjacent toponym was considered and rejected**: "Las Bela" appears as a point on the
  camels' route (p. 167), and the archive holds a row located in Lasbela District
  (`chandragup-baba-chandragup`). The chunk says nothing that connects the two, and no mapping was
  made.
