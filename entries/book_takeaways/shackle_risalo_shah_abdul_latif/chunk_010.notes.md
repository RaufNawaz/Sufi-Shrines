# shackle_risalo_shah_abdul_latif — chunk 010 — pp. 341–351

## What this chunk is

The book's back matter, not Risalo text and not research prose. Four parts in sequence: the **tail of
the translator's endnotes**, covering sur `28 Dahar` (pp. 341–342), `29 Bilaval` (pp. 342–343) and
`30 Kedaro` (pp. 343–344), each with a short unnumbered headnote followed by numbered notes; a
blank page (p. 345); the **GLOSSARY**, a single two-column list of names running `ahmad` to `yazid`
(p. 346); a blank page (p. 347); and the **BIBLIOGRAPHY**, divided into "Editions and Translations"
(pp. 348–349) and "Other Sources" (pp. 349–351). The running head is `notes` (pp. 341–344) then
`bibliography` (pp. 349–351). **The endnotes gloss verses that are not in this chunk**, so most
notes are comments on lines that cannot be read here. A bibliography entry is evidence that a book
exists, not evidence of what it says, and nothing below is drawn from a title.

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **bhit-bhit-shah** (Bhit): note 12 to sur 29 reads in full "A place near Shah Latif's residence at
  Bhit." (p. 343). This is the chunk's only statement locating the poet, and it gives **Bhit** as his
  "residence"; the place the note actually glosses is in a verse outside this chunk and so is **(not
  stated)** here. Nothing in the chunk describes a shrine, tomb or grave at Bhit.
- **bhit-bhit-shah** (Bhitshah): the imprints in the bibliography record a publishing body at
  Bhitshah under two English names — "Hyderabad: Shah Abdul Latif Bhitshah Saqafati Markaz Kameti"
  (p. 348) and "Bhitshah Cultural Centre Committee" (pp. 348, 350), also given as "Shah Abdul Latif
  Bhitshah Cultural Centre Committee" (p. 348) and as "Bhitshah, Hyderabad" for a place of
  publication (p. 350). This is imprint evidence of an institution bearing the site's name; the
  chunk says nothing about its relation to the shrine.

## Practices, institutions, economy

- Offerings at shrines, as a dated practice: note 11 to sur 28 reads "The day on which oferings are
  traditionally presented at Suf shrines." (p. 342). **Which day is not stated in the note** — it
  glosses a verse outside this chunk.
- "The annual Ashura festival of Muharram 10, which celebrates the martyrdom of Imam Husain"
  (p. 344), and Husain's party setting out "from Medina at the beginning of Muharram, the frst month
  of the Hijri year" (p. 343).
- The three sur headnotes each identify a **musical mode**: "Dahar is a Sindhi musical mode"
  (p. 341); "Bilaval is a well-known Indian musical mode" (p. 342); "Kedaro (S. keḏāro, connected
  with Skt. kedāra- 'feld') is the name of a musical mode associated with martial themes" (p. 343).
- A dried river course as a lost economy: the Sindhi word **ḍhoru**, "the dried-up course of a
  former branch of the Indus, once the site of bustling trade but now reclaimed by the desert where
  only thorn trees grow, following one of the many shifts in the historic route of the river"; "The
  Jasodhos were the former lords of this territory" (p. 341).

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- **Hinglaj and Ganjo** — the glossary gives "hinglaj (Hinglāj) Pilgrimage site in Balochistan" and

- **Mansur al-Hallaj** — "mansur (Mansūr) The great Suf saint Mansūr al-Hallāj, who was martyred in
  Baghdad" (p. 346).
- **Karbala, Husain and Yazid** — "the battle fought in 680 at Karbala in Iraq between Husain, the
  grandson of the Prophet, and the army of the caliph Yazid" (p. 343); the glossary adds "yazid
  (Yazīd) Leader of the army that defeated Husain at Karbala" (p. 346). Named in the Karbala notes:
  Hasan, who "had died shortly before the battle" (p. 344); Hasan's son **Qasim**, "who was married
  to his cousin, Husain's daughter Fatima Kubra, just before the battle" (p. 344); **Hur**, "a senior
  ofcer in Yazid's army", whose arrival "to join Husain's forces is a much celebrated incident"
  (p. 344); and the treachery of "the inhabitants of Kufa, who frst promised Husain their support,
  then betrayed him on the battlefeld" (p. 344).
- **Jakhiro** — "a Rajput ruler of medieval Sindh famous for his bravery and generosity", addressed
  by "the epithet Rahu (the 'upholder of the realm'); his alternative name, Abro; his title, Jadam;
  and his tribal afiation as 'the Samo'" (p. 342). A verse refers to the invasion by "the Muslim
  emperor of Delhi, Sultan Alauddin (d. 1316)", resisted by Jakhiro, "who took under his protection
  the women of his fellow Rajputs, the Sumiros" (p. 342).
- **Vagand** — "Shah Latif's disgraceful but devoted disciple", the subject of sur 29's final section
  (29.25–34), verses that "demonstrate a sharp satirical humor otherwise absent in the Risālo"
  (p. 342); the notes set "Vagand's hopeless condition when he relies on his own eforts" against
  "the bounty he receives from his pīr" (p. 343).
- **Lakho Phulani** — "a legendary bandit from the heroic age of Sindh, who is remembered for his
  fearless attacks on the ruling Jarejo Samos. Here he represents the irresistible challenge of
  death" (p. 342).
- **Place-names glossed in the glossary, with the whole of what is said of each** (p. 346):
  "bhambhor City in southern Sindh where Sasui lived"; "dhat (Ḍhaṭ) The homeland of the Marus in the
  Thar desert to the east of Sindh"; "malir (Malīr) Homeland of the Marus"; "junagarh (Jhūnāgaṛh)
  Capital of a kingdom in Gujarat" (the endnotes add "The royal fort of Junagarh", p. 341); "kech
  Punhun's home country"; "las bela (Las Ḇela) Coastal area bordering southwest Sindh"; "pab (Pab)
  Range of hills in southwest Sindh"; "harho (Hāṛho) Mountain range in Las Bela"; "vankar (Vaṇkār) A
  wooded area in Las Bela"; and "savan (Sāvaṇ) The month of the rainy season corresponding to
  July–August".
- **Anirai** — named as "the ruler who was the rival of Diyach", with the reader referred to the
  introductory note to sur 27, which is not in this chunk (p. 343).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- The bird of the Karbala cycle: "The appeal conveyed by the bird to the tomb of the Prophet in
  Medina is one of the traditional legends associated with the story of Karbala" (p. 344) — given by
  the translator as a traditional legend, not as fact.
- The crane: "The crane (S. kūṉja) comes to Sindh as a winter migrant. Its plaintive cry is thought
  to be an expression of pain caused by its separation from the fock" (p. 341) — attributed to
  belief ("is thought to be"), as a "traditional Indian poetic image".

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **Sur 30 Kedaro's authenticity is disputed among editors, and the translator names the evidence**:
  "The exceptional character of the sur has nevertheless caused some editors to have reservations
  about its authenticity. It is accordingly omitted without explanation from Kazi 1961 and is placed
  immediately before the set of apocryphal surs included at the end of Baloch 2012" (p. 343). The
  translator's own explanation is confessional breadth: "While the Husain cult is particularly
  associated with Shia Islam (Hyder 2006), the fgure of the martyred imam has always had a far wider
  appeal. It therefore is not too surprising that this overtly Shia material should be included in
  the Suf context of the Risālo (Schimmel 1979)" (p. 343).
- **A panegyric read through a human ruler**: the verses in praise of Jakhiro are "to be understood
  as forming an extended panegyric to the prophet Muhammad", in keeping with "the conventions of
  Indian praise poetry" (p. 342). The same substitution is asserted for Sasui's prince: "As often,
  the prince loved by Sasui stands for the divine beloved" (p. 341).
- **A Persian text is named as the origin of the South Asian Karbala literature**: "a very large body
  of devotional literature in South Asia, starting with the hugely popular Rauzat ul Shuhadā
  composed in Persian around 1500 by Husain Va'iz Kashif" (p. 343).

## Doubts

- **Only one archive id is mapped, `bhit-bhit-shah`, and it rests on a single endnote** (p. 343) plus
  imprint names (pp. 348, 350). Neither is a statement about the shrine. A human should decide
  whether an eleven-word note glossing an unread verse is worth citing in the entry at all.
- **Hinglaj (p. 346) again cannot be mapped.** `shrine_index.tsv` carries a row named *Shaktipeeth
  Shri Hinglaj Mata Mandir* whose **id column is empty** — the only such row in the index — so there
  is no id to put in bold, and the glossary lines for `hinglaj` and `ganjo` are filed under "Other
  saints, sites and events" instead. This repeats the position recorded in the notes for chunk 006.
- **Sachal Sarmast and Shah Inat are named only in book titles** (p. 349). `shrine-of-sachal-sarmast`
  is **not** mapped: a title is evidence a book exists, not evidence about a shrine. "Shah Inat" is
  not matched to any row.
- **"Bhit" and "Bhitshah" are used as separate spellings** — "Shah Latif's residence at Bhit"
  (p. 343) against the imprint "Bhitshah" (pp. 348, 350) and the title "Shāh Abdul Latīf of Bhit"
  (p. 351). The chunk nowhere states that they are the same place. The archive row is
  `bhit-bhit-shah`, which carries both, but the identification is not made by this chunk.
- **The endnotes are unreadable as evidence without their verses.** Every numbered note comments on a
  line of the Risalo that lies outside this chunk, and some notes name nothing at all ("I.e., the
  world.", p. 341; "In ritual preparation for eating.", p. 342; "A common plant growing wild in the
  Indus valley.", p. 341; "A legendary Arab chieftain who is proverbial for his generosity throughout
  Islamic literature.", p. 343, which does not give the chieftain's name). Do not build a fact on a
  note alone.
