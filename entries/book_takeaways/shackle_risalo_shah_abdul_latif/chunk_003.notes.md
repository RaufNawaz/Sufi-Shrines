# shackle_risalo_shah_abdul_latif — chunk 003 — pp. 81–118

Continuous text of the Risalo itself, no apparatus: the close of **Sur Sirirag** (verses 30–67V,
pp. 81–88), the whole of **Sur Samundi** (verses 1–46V, pp. 89–95), the whole of **Sur Suhini**
(verses 1–118V, pp. 96–116), and the opening of **Sur Sasui Abiri** (verses 1–10, pp. 117–118).
Sur titles as printed in the running heads and section heads: `5 || sirirag` (p. 82),
`6    Samundi` (p. 89), `7     Suhini` (p. 96), `8    Sasui Abiri` (p. 117).

**No shrine, tomb, ʿurs, khanqah, sajjada nashin, endowment or place in Sindh other than the
Indus is named anywhere in this chunk**, and the chunk carries **no facing original text and no
transliteration** — English translation only. Its value to the archive is therefore entirely in
what the verse shows of the maritime, mercantile, pastoral and devotional world it is set in, and
in the poet's own statements about his poetry. See Doubts before using anything here as a fact
about a site.

The printed page number is set at the foot of each page: PDF p. 81 carries printed page **54** and
PDF p. 118 carries printed page **91**, and the offset of 27 holds unbroken across every page of
this chunk. Page references below are PDF page indices, as the protocol requires.

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **`bhit-bhit-shah`** (the chunk names him only by the signature formulae "says Latif" and
  "says Shah"): **the verse signs itself inside the poem, in two interchangeable forms.**
  "says Latif" at pp. 81, 82, 85, 99, 104, 105, 112; "says Shah" at pp. 93, 97, 106, 107, 113.
  Both occur inside Sur Suhini within a few pages of each other — "Through divine favor she
  crossed the waves, says Latif" (p. 99) against "Love destroys the force of the current, says
  Shah" (p. 99) — so the two are used of the same speaking voice in the same sur. The running
  head of every verso page is `risalo` (pp. 81, 83, 85, 87, 89, 91, 93, 95, 99, 101, 103, 105,
  107, 109, 111, 113, 115, 117).
- **`bhit-bhit-shah`**: **the poet's own account of what his poems are** — "What you consider to
  be poems are divine verses. They direct the mind toward the beloved." (p. 109, Suhini 77). This
  is the only first-person statement about the nature of the work in the chunk.
- **Nothing else in this chunk attaches to the row.** The shrine, its location, its saint's dates,
  his order and his burial are all **(not stated)**. The mapping itself is a judgement made on the
  signature lines alone — see Doubts.

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- **Suhini** — heroine of Sur Suhini, a **literary figure**, a woman who crosses the Indus at night
  on an unbaked clay pot to reach her beloved and drowns (pp. 96–116). Named in the text as
  "Suhini" throughout; "the Brahman girl" is used of Sasui, not of her (p. 118).
- **Sahar** and **Mehar** — the names of Suhini's beloved, used interchangeably across the sur
  (Sahar: pp. 96, 99, 101, 105, 106, 110, 113, 114; Mehar: pp. 97, 98, 103, 104, 105, 113, 116).
  Mehar is a herdsman: "the chief of the herdsmen has shown me the straight path" (p. 97),
  "May Mehar never die, and may his byre never be bare. May the hair of the heifers' herdsman
  never be twisted" (p. 104), "the milk drinker" (p. 114). Sahar is identified with the sea:
  "Sahar is the same as Suhini, and it is Sahar who is the sea" (p. 99).
- **Dam** — named as Suhini's husband and set against Mehar: "My husband, Dam, is false and foul;
  in my heart I hold Mehar" (p. 97); "Ten times a day Dam taunts me" (p. 100); "She is sick when
  with Dam, but healthy with the herdsman" (p. 115).
- **Todi** — a name used of the heroine in three consecutive verses, with an unexplained endnote
  marker: "From the very frst [text-layer?], Todi5 was a seeker of the lord" (p. 101); "From the
  beginning Todi was favored by love" (p. 101); "Abandon that love which makes you happy, Todi"
  (p. 101). The chunk does not say what the name is.
- **Sasui** and **Punhun** — **literary figures**, the heroine and hero of Sur Sasui Abiri
  (pp. 117–118). "When she was near Punhun, Sasui did not understand… The Brahman girl2 lost her
  false sense of self among the Baloch.3" (p. 118). Punhun is also named once inside Sur Suhini:
  "Thoughts of the beloved are everywhere, and Punhun is present everywhere" (p. 113).
- **Mansur** — invoked twice as the type of the executed lover: "They all make thousands of
  Mansurs; which ones will you hang?" (p. 113); "The whole land is Mansur; how many of him will
  you slaughter?" (p. 113). No further identification is given in the chunk.
- **Azazil** — "Do not go near Azazil.4" (p. 118), with an endnote marker whose note is not in
  this chunk.
- **The Prophet** — twice, in a maritime context: sailors "made oferings [text-layer?] to the holy
  Prophet, that he might in his mercy preserve the boats" (p. 85); "By the grace of the Prophet,
  they were not stopped by the customs ofcers [text-layer?]" (p. 86).
- **Places named as sailing destinations, as printed, all in English only**: **Porbandar** — "Lord,
  deliver your company to Porbandar.2" (p. 81); **Aden** — "Remembering God, they have got back
  from Aden.4" (p. 82) and "your boat will not be lost before it gets to Aden" (p. 87);
  **Lanka** — "Wealthy sailors have returned from ransacking Lanka.6" (p. 94) and "'Lanka, Lanka'
  is all that those who have set out for Lanka can think of. When they hear of Lanka's gold, the
  sailors have no peace" (p. 94); **Kech** — "Friendless one, do not send reproaches to Kech"
  (p. 118).
- **The Indus** — the only river named, and named repeatedly: "A mighty wind blows and the Indus
  is turbulent" (p. 86); "What they desire are the pearls of the Indus" (p. 94); "Both banks of
  the wild Indus are threatening" (p. 103); "If you leap into the wild Indus" (p. 105); "those who
  have experienced the Indus refuse to rest on its islands" (p. 111); "In the Indus she came to
  know that it was unbaked" (p. 112).
- **"Frankish pirates"** — "The pilots are not in their former place; Frankish pirates3 have taken
  over" (p. 81). No date, place or further identification in the chunk.

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **A working port economy, itemised in the verse.** Boats are oiled to be made sound ("Oil the
  timbers of your boat and make it sound", p. 82); the carpenter's pegs rot (p. 81); traders "cast
  anchor weights into the waters" (p. 81); sails are "mended… on the harbor quays. When these were
  patched and fxed [text-layer?], they erected the masts. At sea they few [text-layer?] their fags
  [text-layer?]" (p. 92); crews "sought news from the master mariners" before sailing (p. 93);
  a sailor "who keeps watch delivers reports to the captain" (p. 82) and pilots deliver "fearful
  reports" (p. 81). "The fags [text-layer?] of the rich merchants look dazzling in every
  direction" (p. 91).
- **Cargo and the goods of the trade**, in one list: "They put cloves, cardamoms, fne
  [text-layer?] clothes, and lustrous pearls aboard. They placed valuable stores in its bottom. In
  its rigging… they tied foral [text-layer?] decorations" (p. 85). Elsewhere: musk, ambergris
  (p. 106), pearls of the Indus preferred to gold by "Major traders" (p. 94), Lanka's gold (p. 94).
- **Customs are levied and can be escaped.** "By the grace of the Prophet, they were not stopped
  by the customs ofcers [text-layer?]. The sailors who voyaged afar have entered their own
  country" (p. 86).
- **Passage is paid for and the poor are left on the shore.** "I have no money on me to pay for my
  passage. Boatman, tell me how I can get to my beloved" (p. 93); "The travelers had nothing on
  them, nor did the boatmen take them on board for nothing. They remained on the shore all day
  long until the sun set" (p. 93).
- **The gem and jewellery trade as a described occupation.** Jewellers "put a loupe in their eye
  and assess all of them. They determine the value of a pearl's quality from its circumference"
  (p. 84); the dealer's "expert eye" is what secures the value (p. 84); an earlier generation
  "used to pierce diamonds and rubies", and where they worked "blacksmiths now hammer base
  metals" (pp. 83–84); "Glass is bought and jewels are rejected" (p. 84); a misvalued gold is put
  "next to a brass pot" (p. 83).
- **A sailing calendar keyed to wind, festival and month.** "They left when the north wind was
  blowing, and they return on the spring breeze" (p. 92); "As soon as the north wind blows, my
  beloved talks about going" (p. 95); "Seeing that it is Diwali,7 the sailors prepare their sails"
  (p. 95); a voyage of a full year — "He has returned in the twelfth month since he started"
  (p. 95); and a departure the day after arrival, "after spending only twenty-four hours with me"
  (p. 95). **Savan** is the season of flood: "Everyone enters the water in Savan; she is happy in
  winter" (p. 102); "The days of Savan will not be here forever. By tomorrow your foods
  [text-layer?] will subside" (p. 102); "the rains of Savan" (p. 116).
- **Homecoming as festival.** "Mother, the sailors have returned, making a fne [text-layer?]
  display" (p. 91); "For those whose courtyards they enter, today is a happy Eid" (p. 92).
- **Women's votive practice at the waterside — the densest ritual cluster in the chunk, and all of
  it performed by the wives of traders, not at a shrine.** "The traders' wives stand at the landing
  place and make oferings [text-layer?]. They bring musk and ofer [text-layer?] it to the ocean.3"
  (p. 93). "She lights lamps on water and dry land, she ties fags [text-layer?] on trees and
  plants,4 saying: 'Oh God, my hope is that my husband may come.'" (p. 93). And the obligation
  stated as a rule: "She who performs pilgrimage to the water and does not ofer [text-layer?]
  lamps should not desire her husband's return" (p. 93). Vows are counted and redeemed: "I have
  made thousands of vows for your return, my husband" (p. 91); "I give to others the precious gems
  that I vowed to my beloved" (p. 92).
- **Music and instruments named.** The **rebab**, twice: "The instrument plays in my being, the
  rebab dwells in my spirit" (p. 97); "There is nothing in piety, nothing in the rebab.19" (p. 116).
  **Bells** are the guiding sound of the Suhini sur: "the bells stir my whole body. How can I
  disclose to all and sundry the love that their clappers have aroused?" (p. 103); "She is guided
  in midstream by the tinkling of the bells" (p. 103); "The sound of the bells in the river
  thickets arouses me" (p. 103); "I heard the sound of the bells on the far bank as I slept"
  (p. 103). Each sur is itself titled as a mode — see the list at the head of these notes.
- **The evening call to prayer marks the hour of setting out.** "Crows sit crouched in the trees as
  the day draws to its close. When she hears the evening call to prayer, she goes to seek the spots
  where her dear Sahar dwells" (p. 96).
- **The four-stage Sufi path, given in English.** "First learn the lesson of the Law, Suhini. The
  truth of Reality far surpasses the Way. It is Gnosis7 that is the real task of lovers" (p. 102).
- **The guide (murshid) as an institution the poems assume.** "Mother, with humbly folded hands I
  will go to my guide" (p. 95); "Oh my guide, give me your hand, and rescue me from the deep"
  (p. 99); "On your way toward the other side, humbly cling to the guide's stirrup" (p. 116).
- **Alms and the duty to the poor, stated negatively.** "In your house you gathered riches, which
  came up to your knees. It is called carrion10 in the Traditions… You did not share it with the
  poor or fear the wrath of God" (p. 88). Also "It is the time for speedy repentance; this is true
  awareness" (p. 87).
- **Scripture and hadith quoted inside the verse**, each carrying an endnote marker whose note is
  outside this chunk: "Every soul shall have a taste of death:8" (p. 87); "On that day brother will
  fee [text-layer?] from brother 9 is what it says in scripture" (p. 88); "the Traditions" on
  carrion (p. 88); "Do not despair of God's mercy 4" (p. 98); "Be and it was,12" (p. 106); the
  covenant "Am I not your lord?" answered "Yes" (p. 107, and again pp. 107 twice); "the recording
  angels15" (p. 109).
- **The pastoral economy of the far bank.** "The herds grazed the pasture, then crossed the river
  and came to an island.10" (p. 104); "Safe and happy, the bufaloes [text-layer?] will get across"
  (p. 104); the byre and the heifers' herdsman (p. 104).
- **The potter's craft, and the difference between a fired and an unfired pot, treated as
  technical.** "Suhini was happy when she saw the designs drawn by the potter. The water washed
  away the pattern and the glaze could not withstand the impact… In the Indus she came to know that
  it was unbaked" (pp. 111–112); "Take with you a fred [text-layer?] pot with fne [text-layer?]
  designs. Return the unfred [text-layer?] pot straight to the potter" (p. 112).
- **Burial and the grave.** "Oh forgetful one, do not forget the terror of the grave's walls"
  (p. 88). And a funeral described entirely in birds: "The herons are her pallbearers and the
  current is her bier. The cranes that live on the eyots ofer [text-layer?] their shoulders to
  carry her" (p. 109). The day of resurrection is twice invoked as a court of appeal: "Oh river, I
  shall complain against you on the day of resurrection" (p. 110); "If my beloved comes to me on
  the day of resurrection, that is quite near" (p. 113).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

All of the following are **narrated by the poems as the story of a literary heroine**, not
attributed by the book to any saint or site.

- **Suhini is dismembered by the river's creatures**: "The alligator grasps her leg, the cayman has
  her head. Her bracelets mingle with the mud, the current grabs her hair. Countless creatures
  cling closely to her, and river monsters maul her body. Whole schools of fsh [text-layer?]
  surround her, as Suhini is severed limb from limb" (p. 97).
- **And yet she crosses, by divine favour**: "Through divine favor she crossed the waves, says
  Latif" (p. 99); "Suhini crosses safely, unafected [text-layer?] by the whirlpools" (p. 101).
  The chunk holds both her destruction and her safe crossing without reconciling them — see Doubts.
- **Her crossing is placed before creation.** "Sahar's beauty preceded the writing of fate. There
  was no Be and it was,12 nor any other idea. Suhini's song came before the angels were created"
  (p. 106); "Suhini found her entry point and crossed over the river to Mehar before the time of
  the covenant" (p. 107); "I have been married to Mehar from the time of the covenant" (p. 107).
- **Union is completed after death**: "After she was dead, she was enfolded by the waves and taken
  to Mehar" (p. 115); "It was not the river, or a pond, or a lake, that drowned Suhini. Even after
  death, she went to Mehar with longing in her eyes" (p. 115); "she was doubly rewarded through
  drowning" (pp. 105–106).
- **A drink of milk drives her mad**: "It was the drink of milk he gave her that made her crazy.11"
  (p. 105); "She drank a draught of love from Mehar.8 The taste of that drink intoxicated her"
  (p. 104).
- **Reeds save the drowning, or drown with them**: "Reeds are deservedly well known for saving
  people. Either they give support to those who are drowning or they go with them in the current"
  (p. 111).
- **The desert mourns Punhun and the animals die of grief**: "The birds cried after the departure
  of Punhun… In their great grief the beasts accepted death. The desert itself cried out in longing
  for Punhun" (p. 118).
- **A jewel worth more broken than whole**: "Touched by a woman, the jewel was broken in its box.
  When perfect, says Shah, it was worth thousands. Later, when broken, its value was more than a
  billion.5" (p. 84).

## Arguments and interpretations

This chunk carries **no translator's headnote, introduction or note text** — only the sur numbers
and titles and the endnote reference markers. What follows is therefore the primary source's own
judgements, in its own voice.

- **The poet's claim for his own verse**: "What you consider to be poems are divine verses. They
  direct the mind toward the beloved" (p. 109).
- **Identity of lover, beloved and medium asserted as a riddle, not argued**: "Sahar is the same as
  Suhini, and it is Sahar who is the sea. This mystery is magical, this puzzle is profound"
  (p. 99).
- **A repeated and deliberate paradox: aids to crossing must be refused.** "For all who feel the
  force of love, a raft is only a handicap" (p. 98); "Do not set your hand on the beloved's raft as
  you drown" (p. 110); "Even if you are drowning, do not touch the beloved's raft… go with the
  current against you. Step in the direction where there is no safety hut" (pp. 110–111); "If you
  drown in preparation for dry land, then go ahead and drown. Do not gather straw and thorn and
  sticks to make a raft" (p. 111). Against which the same sur says: "You must depart to the other
  side; construct your raft for the deep water" (p. 111), and Sur Sirirag says throughout that the
  prudent sailor who takes "strong equipment" and "the advice of the experts" is the one who
  crosses (pp. 81, 85). The contradiction is the chunk's, not a transcription problem.
- **Predestination stated flatly and more than once**: "How can anyone turn back what has been
  ordained in the book of fate?" (p. 107); "It is not the reed or the pen that is to blame. Fate
  was written where no arm can reach. To whom should I complain when destiny moved the pen?"
  (p. 114); "This is divinely ordained; why else would anyone enter the whirlpools?" (p. 108).
  Set beside a plain moral determinism at the end of Sur Sirirag: "The good perform good deeds,
  the evil commit evil. They each behave in accordance with their natures" (p. 82).
- **Attainment is rare**: "Only a few among millions experience the valley of oneness. Many are
  swept away by the delusion of the river" (p. 107).
- **Poverty as the mark of the true giver**: "There are harbors in every land, but precious gems
  are not found on sandy shores. Priceless jewels are given by those who are dressed like fakirs"
  (p. 93).
- **Annihilation of self given a grammatical simile** — worth noting because it is the chunk's only
  technical metaphor: "The self is subdued in union, like the infectional [text-layer?] vowels in
  assimilation.22" (p. 116).

## Doubts

- **This chunk contains no archive material about any shrine.** No shrine, tomb, ʿurs, khanqah,
  darbar, custodian, endowment, building date or settlement in Sindh is named anywhere in pp. 81–118;
  the only toponyms are Porbandar, Aden, Lanka, Kech and the Indus. Nothing here can support a
  factual statement about `bhit-bhit-shah` as a site.
- **The mapping to `bhit-bhit-shah` rests on the signature formulae alone.** The chunk contains
  "says Latif" and "says Shah" and the running head `risalo`; it never gives the poet's full name,
  never says he is Shah Abdul Latif, and never mentions Bhit or Bhit Shah. Given the book slug the
  identification is very likely right, but **it is an inference from the book's title page, which
  is not in this chunk** — a human should confirm it against the front matter before any entry
  cites these pages as the poet's own words.
- **No original script and no transliteration anywhere in the chunk.** A Murty volume normally
  prints the original facing the translation; this chunk's pages carry only the English, with the
  printed page numbers running 54–91. Either the original pages fall outside this chunk's page
  range, or the transcription dropped them. **Every name and place above is therefore given in
  English only, which the protocol's "quote the original phrase" rule cannot be satisfied for.**
  Worth checking the PDF before assuming the Sindhi is absent from the book.
- **Systematic text-layer damage: the `fi`, `fl` and `ff` ligatures are dropped throughout.**
  "fxed", "fnd", "fne", "frst", "fsh", "fred"/"unfred", "flled", "fow"/"fowed"/"foods", "fre"/
  "fres", "foat", "fags", "few" (for "flew"), "fee" (for "flee"), "foral", "difcult"/"difculty",
  "ofer"/"oferings", "sufering", "ofcers", "bufaloes", "efort", "proft", "unafected",
  "infectional", "inficted", and "set of"/"cast of"/"gets of"/"of course" for the "off" forms.
  This is one mechanical fault, not a series of misprints, but **it has been carried onto every
  word quoted above with a `[text-layer?]` flag** and must not be silently repaired in an entry.
- **Sahar and Mehar are never explicitly said to be the same person.** They are used
  interchangeably of Suhini's beloved within single verses (pp. 104, 113, 116) and the sur ends
  addressing both; but p. 99 says "Sahar is the same as Suhini, and it is Sahar who is the sea",
  which cuts across the identification. Do not resolve this; the chunk does not.
- **"Todi" (p. 101, verses 29–31) is unexplained.** It appears to name the heroine, carries endnote
  marker 5, and occurs nowhere else in the chunk. It could be a variant name, a second figure, or a
  musical mode; **the chunk does not say**, and the endnote text is not in this chunk.
- **"Punhun" appears inside Sur Suhini** (p. 113, verse 98), one sur before the Sasui cycle opens
  at p. 117. Whether this is a deliberate cross-reference or an editorial placement is not stated.
- **Endnote markers appear on at least thirty passages** (Sirirag 2–10, Samundi 1–7, Suhini 1–22,
  Sasui Abiri 1–4) **but no note text falls in this chunk.** Every gloss that would identify
  Porbandar, Aden, Lanka, Kech, the Frankish pirates, Mansur, Azazil, the scriptural quotations,
  the "drink of milk", "eighty eyes", the island of p. 104 or the meaning of "Todi" is therefore
  **(not stated)** here and must be taken from the endnotes chunk.
- **The suffix "V" on verse numbers 67V (p. 87), 46V (p. 95) and 118V (p. 116)** marks the long
  closing lyric of each sur in this edition's layout, but **the chunk nowhere explains the
  convention**; it is recorded as printed and not expanded.
- **Sur Sirirag begins mid-sur**: this chunk opens at verse 30 (p. 81), so verses 1–29 of that sur
  are in an earlier chunk. **Sur Sasui Abiri ends mid-sur**: the chunk stops at verse 10 (p. 118).
- **Internal contradiction within Sur Suhini, not a transcription fault**: she is "severed limb
  from limb" (p. 97) and she "crosses safely, unafected [text-layer?] by the whirlpools" (p. 101);
  she is told to build a raft (p. 111) and told never to touch one (pp. 110–111). Reported as the
  book has it.
- **"Later, when broken, its value was more than a billion" (p. 84)** reads as either a deliberate
  paradox or a translation/printing problem, and carries endnote marker 5. Flagged rather than
  resolved.
- **"I am the one whom eighty eyes cannot fnd [text-layer?] when I am hidden" (p. 84)** — "eighty
  eyes" is unglossed in the chunk (endnote marker 6) and should not be paraphrased.
