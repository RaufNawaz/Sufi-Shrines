# shackle_risalo_shah_abdul_latif — chunk 006 — pp. 197–233

Four numbered and titled sections of the Risalo, printed as English verse only: the tail of
**15 Marui** (from mid-verse 11 to verse 108V, pp. 197–214), **16 Kamod** complete (vv. 1–27V,
pp. 215–219), **17 Ghatu** complete (vv. 1–13V, pp. 220–222), and **18 Ramakali** from verse 1 to
a mid-sentence break in verse 59 (pp. 223–233). The running head alternates `risalo` with the
section head — `15 || marui`, `16 || kamod`, `17 || ghatu`, `18 || ramakali` (pp. 197–233). **The
chunk carries no translator's prose at all**: no headnote to any of the four, and no footnote
texts, only superscript markers. **No archive row is touched by this chunk** — see Doubts.

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- **Marui** — the captive heroine and speaker of most of section 15, **a persona of the poem, not
  presented here as a historical person and with no shrine mentioned**. A girl of the Marus, taken
  to Umarkot and held in chains in a fort/palace: "I am chained in the fort, and my heart is
  distressed in the palace" (p. 208); "Around my neck are collars of iron, I wear fetters and
  chains" (p. 203). She is released at the end: "You are released from captivity, so burn your
  chains" (p. 214).
- **Umar / Sumiro / Hamir** — the ruler who holds her, addressed by all three names, once combined:
  "Oh Umar Sumiro, what is Eid for you is a time of mourning for us" (p. 199); "Lord Sumiro, do
  not try to persuade me to break my chastity. I will go there in a few days, oh Hamir" (p. 202);
  "In this state, oh Hamir, may I return to my homeland thickets" (p. 197). The chunk does not say
  whether Hamir is the same figure — see Doubts. Verse 105 credits him with restraint: "From the
  very beginning, Umar, chastity was prescribed in your destiny. So you did not violate the honor
  of Marui, who was reared by the Marus" (p. 214).
- **Malir** — Marui's homeland, herding country, with a river: "May I die after bathing with the
  Maru in the Malir river" (p. 212); "The Marus of Malir have been martyred by their desire to see
  me" (p. 199); "Go to Malir and discover the honor of the nomads" (p. 205).
- **Umarkot** — the place of her captivity: "Now that I have come to Umarkot, I will do nothing
  wrong" (pp. 198–199); "By coming to Umarkot she has brought disgrace upon herself" (p. 202). No
  building other than "fort", "palace", "mansions" and "cell" is named there. See Doubts on the
  archive's Umerkot row.
- **The Thar and the Dhat** — "she has disgraced the women of the Thar" (p. 197); "cover me with
  the shawl I wore in the Dhat" (p. 197); "In the dunes of the Dhat the Marus have put up many
  huts" (p. 208).
- **Nuri** — the fishergirl of section 16, "a Gandiri [text-layer?] fshergirl [text-layer?] who is
  full of faults" and "a fshergirl [text-layer?] of the Me caste" (p. 215), also called "this
  Mangar girl" (p. 215). A literary figure; no shrine or grave is mentioned.
- **Tamachi, the Samo prince** — "You are Tamachi, lord of the landing place" (p. 215); he marries
  Nuri, "tied the wedding band on her arm" (p. 217), and "waves the royal peacock fan over her"
  (p. 217).
- **Lake Kinjhar** — the setting of section 16: "Whether they are from Lake Kinjhar or from far
  away, they have all been favored by him" (p. 216); "the north wind blows and makes Lake Kinjhar
  rock gently like a swing" (p. 217); "Bravo for the women born by the Kinjhar lake" (p. 217).
- **Kalachi** — the whirlpool of section 17, and the Indus: "Those who entered the waters were
  drowned in the Indus" (p. 220); "No one has ever returned from Kalachi" (p. 220); "The whirlpool
  of Kalachi has a force that sweeps away anyone who enters it" (p. 220).
- **Hinglaj and Dwarka** — named together as destinations of the yogis of section 18: "The naked
  ones have gone to Hinglaj⁸ to behold the goddess. The devotees of Shiv rejoice at the sight of
  Dwarka.⁹ Their guide is Ali¹⁰" (p. 225). **Hinglaj may correspond to a row in the archive index
  that carries no id — see Doubts; it is listed here rather than in the archive section for that
  reason.**
- **Kabul** — a yogi destination and the site of a lodge: "Some painful thought drives them toward
  Kabul.¹⁴ The yogis were talking yesterday about a lodge there" (p. 229).
- **Mount Sinai, Mecca, the Kaaba** — used as figures for the ascetic body: "The knees of the
  sannyasis are like Mount Sinai.¹⁹" (p. 231); "Their hearts point the direction to Mecca, their
  bodies circumambulate the Kaaba" (p. 232).
- **Ram, Shiv, "the goddess", Ali** — named within section 18: "Their souls are entwined in secret
  with Ram.¹²" (p. 227); "Ram always dwells in their hearts" (p. 227); "Day and night their gaze
  is fxed [text-layer?] on the goddess.¹⁸" (p. 231).

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The section titles as printed.** Numbered and named: `15 || marui` (running head, p. 198),
  `16 Kamod` (p. 215), `17 Ghatu` (p. 220), `18 Ramakali` (p. 223). **The chunk nowhere states
  what these titles denote** — no headnote, no gloss. Record the names; do not infer the category.
- **Instruments named.** The yogis' **horns**: "Their horns³ are all made of gold, but regard their
  detachment and do not speak of their wealth" (p. 223); "The masters use their horns to consume
  the self" (p. 224); "The masters carry their horns on their shoulders" (p. 229). The
  **surando**: "I have understood the secrets of their community from the sound of their
  surando.⁶" (p. 224); "I am slain by their surando" (p. 224); "The surandos of the ascetics are no
  longer heard at dawn" (p. 224). A **lute**: "She is a queen among queens, like the main string on
  a lute" (p. 217).
- **Ascetic discipline, itemised across the ten-day sequence and after.** Matted braids and
  topknots, worn hair-strings, dust-coated clothes (p. 226); kindling fire in the lodges from
  "sweepings and straw" (p. 226); leaning on **crutches** (p. 226); rubbing **ashes** from the fire
  on their bodies and "Begging for a little grain" (p. 227); washing their clothes on the seventh
  day and standing "With folded arms… before God the unseen" (p. 227); going "from place to place"
  on the eighth (p. 227); completing pilgrimages and entering "the sacred enclosure" on the
  eleventh, "They have permanently kept silent" (p. 228); "The yogis all long for this pilgrimage"
  on the twelfth (p. 228).
- **Ear-splitting and the marks of the order.** "The Kapat yogis¹³ have their ears pierced and slit
  for earrings. As lovers, they sit forever facing the north wind. They fast and mortify their
  bodies" (p. 228). Also "seal your mouth with rings. The ears you split countless times have made
  no diference [text-layer?] to you. Abandon your sheet and put on bits of leather, shoes are not
  suitable for you" (p. 230); "Why get your ears split if you cannot endure the cold?" (pp. 230–231);
  "Yogi, you have got your ears split, do not become a woman" (p. 231).
- **The lodge as an institution, and its emptying.** "When I see the lodge where they stayed I am
  overcome by grief" (p. 224); "'Alas, alas!' I cry in their lodge" (p. 225); "The lodge where they
  stay is nonexistence" (p. 225); "Master, your shelter stands before me like a thorn.¹⁵ Oh naked
  one, why did you build it if you were going to leave it?" (p. 229).
- **Mendicancy policed from inside.** "Go and beg from the band of yogis who understand but say
  that they do not" (p. 229), against "Yogis who search for food are false. Accursed are the
  wretches who look after their bellies" (p. 231).
- **Islamic ritual vocabulary transferred onto the ascetic body.** "Their knees are a mihrab,²⁰ and
  their bodies are a mosque… Proclaiming the divine reality, they have renounced the body" (p. 232);
  "The beloved's face is their mihrab,²¹ the entire world is their mosque. They have given up the
  Qur'an, and the tablet telling right from wrong" (p. 232); "They have nothing to do with
  unbelievers, and they do not have Islam in their minds" (pp. 232–233).
- **Qur'anic tags carried in the verse, each marked by a footnote whose text is not in this chunk.**
  "ever since the primal covenant of Am I not¹³ or even before that. He does not beget nor is he
  begotten,¹⁴" (p. 206); "There is nothing like him,¹⁵" (p. 206); "nothing will happen to us except
  what God has decreed.¹¹" (p. 201); and in section 16, "He does not give birth, nor is he born³ is
  the mark of his nobility and justice" (p. 218).
- **Tax and exemption, in three separate sections.** "let my kinsfolk be exempted from tax" (p. 215);
  "In their country there is no check or hindrance, nor any revenue tax" (p. 205); "The lake has
  dried up, and the tax collectors have left" (p. 221); and Nuri "has been excused from the fshing
  [text-layer?] gear, the landing places, and the boats" (p. 217).
- **Alms and royal largesse.** Tamachi's closing verse: "He gave treasures to the humble folk, like
  a frst [text-layer?] fruits ofering [text-layer?]. He took out pearls and scattered them like
  cowries at the fsh [text-layer?] stall… He gave gold away as alms and made a sport of silver…
  He made oferings [text-layer?] of turquoises to the fakirs" (p. 219), and "The blind and the lame
  came, when the generous one invited them" (p. 219).
- **Pastoral and textile economy of the Marus.** Camel-herding — "Umar, give the order for me to
  become a herder and to graze the camels on leaves" (p. 197); shearing and spinning — "they shear
  the soft wool from the sheep's tails… free from sorrows they spin the wool at their husbands'
  side. In the desert even the young sucklings give wool from their backs. Precious shawls of high
  value are woven. As they starch the shawls, the women say: 'Marui is needed in Malir'" (p. 204);
  blankets "dyed with lac" held "more beautiful than shawls" and wool "better than silk and
  brocade" (p. 197); patching and darning as a discipline of honour (pp. 197, 203).
- **Ornament as a marker of allegiance.** Glass bangles against gold — "The Maru women wear glass
  bangles on their wrists, taking pride in what most people are ashamed of. We wear black threads
  around our wrists; for us, gold is a cause of mourning" (p. 198). **Betrothal threads and
  betrothal oil**: "The Maru's betrothal threads are like gold to me… Blessed is the thread of the
  blanket that my kinsfolk gave to me" (p. 198); "I use the Maru's betrothal oil on my hair, I have
  no use for yours" (p. 198). Refusal to oil or braid the hair is repeatedly the sign of her state
  (pp. 197, 199, 200).
- **Wells and water-drawing as women's work, timed and measured.** "They dig wells in the desert and
  water their goats. At dawn the women draw water from wells **sixty fathoms deep**. Pouring the
  water into buckets, they cry out with joy" (p. 207); "The women get up at midnight because the
  water lies deep in the ground. The heedless ones get no opportunity during the day.¹⁶ It was my
  bad luck that they snatched me from the well.¹⁷" (p. 207); "On both sides of the well the ropes
  dangle idly" (p. 207); "How wonderful those brackish wells of my native land were, from which I
  used to draw water" (p. 206); churning pots at dawn (p. 206).
- **Foraging and the desert diet.** "Every day, oh Sumiro, they gather and cook food from the
  jungle. They pile up heaps of dried grass… From the lanb [text-layer?] grass, says Latif, they
  extract grains of rice to cook. Oh Umar, they do not consider pulao to be as good as their
  **ārāṛī**.¹²" (p. 204); "They bring branches of berries they have picked and put them on to
  cook. The foragers who frequent the thickets are not thin or weak" (p. 204); "People are feeding
  on the wild fruits of the desert" (p. 209).
- **Seasonal migration keyed to the rains.** "the southwest wind has returned… his cattle tracks and
  pens. The beautiful huts of my husband will be there beside the dunes" (p. 204); "The Marus go out
  in the rainy season, turning toward the borderlands between the river and the desert" (p. 206);
  "They set up their huts where the rain falls" (p. 207); "they drive their focks [text-layer?] back
  to the thickets. They drive the kids to the lower pastures, grazing them on the plains" (p. 208);
  "It is their mark that they travel with their huts and baggage" (p. 209). The **tāro birds¹⁸**
  cry "The rains are falling, come back" (p. 208).
- **Fishing economy, in sections 16 and 17.** Baskets and trays of fish, scales for weighing, the
  cutting and selling of fish (pp. 215–216); "landing places" and boats (pp. 215, 217); "fishing
  spears" (p. 220), nets, hooks and rafts (pp. 220–222); "Use large strong sea nets made with
  colored twine" (pp. 221–222); a fish stall and a quayside (p. 219); the market's collapse —
  "There are heaps of sand where the fshermen [text-layer?] looked for fsh [text-layer?]. Thousands
  of fshmongers [text-layer?] have been ruined" (p. 221); and credit — "I have had to borrow fsh
  [text-layer?].³ God bring back the fshermen [text-layer?]… do not let me be ashamed before the
  traders" (p. 221).
- **Caste and status, stated plainly and then crossed.** "I am a fshergirl [text-layer?] of the Me
  caste" (p. 215); "They are dark, ugly, base, and in no way attractive" (p. 215); the prior social
  separation — "They did not go together to marriage pavilions or to funerals, nor did they
  participate in weddings.²" (p. 218); and the crossing — "He favored the fshergirl [text-layer?]
  and took her with him in his carriage" (p. 216); "He took the fshergirl [text-layer?] into his
  carriage and raised her status.¹" (p. 218). Against which section 18 sets: "How can you ask them
  about their caste?" (p. 229).
- **Captivity described as an apparatus.** "Around my neck are collars of iron, I wear fetters and
  chains. There are shackles on my feet, and the room is nailed on the inside. There are lookouts in
  the courtyard, and the ministers are on guard. I am unhappy in my cell" (p. 203).
- **Burial in the home ground, asked for five times.** "If I die in this place, take my body to
  Malir" (p. 202); "Pour the cool earth of the desert over her dead body. Once my life is over, take
  my corpse to Malir" (p. 202); "May I be buried in the desert with the Marus who live there"
  (p. 203); "make my grave with the desert nomads. Let me smell the fragrance of the creepers of my
  ancestral land" (p. 203); and "I reckon the dust of my native land to be the equal of musk"
  (p. 212).
- **Eid named as a festival that divides ruler from ruled.** "Oh Umar Sumiro, what is Eid for you is
  a time of mourning for us. The sad creatures have forgotten joy and the pleasures of buying
  things.¹⁰" (p. 199).
- **The rosary.** "With your heart smoking with love, turn the beads of the rosary in your mind"
  (p. 229).
- **The guru as the authority the yogi answers to.** "They have found the path of the guru, says
  Latif" (p. 227); "They who fnd [text-layer?] union with the guru are exalted" (p. 228); "Then,
  master, you will not be faulted before the guru" (p. 230).
- **The poet names himself in three forms within this range** — "says Latif" (pp. 197, 200, 204,
  210, 213, 214, 227, 228, 231), "says Shah" (pp. 204, 208, 225, 226, 227) and "says Abdul Latif"
  (pp. 219, 222). The chunk offers no comment on the variation.

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- The ascetics leave the ground: "Consuming their ego, they have gone, and their feet did not touch
  the ground" (p. 225) — said in the voice of the poem's speaker, of the yogis.
- "The masters have been turned into gold" (p. 226); "They have brought signs of some great realm"
  (p. 227).
- Resurrection conditional on burial at home: "I will be restored to life after death if my body
  gets to Malir" (p. 203), repeated at the next verse — "I will be restored to life after death if
  my corpse goes to Malir" (p. 203).
- The whirlpool of Kalachi as a place no one survives — "No one has ever returned from Kalachi"
  (p. 220) — and the sailors' answer to it: "In their search they fell into the whirlpool as they
  explored the deep water. With happy faces, the sailors slew the crocodile" (p. 221). The closing
  verse holds both outcomes at once: "Perhaps the great fsh [text-layer?] has caught them… Sisters,
  Abdul Latif says, they will all cross over the deep water" (p. 222).
- The yogis "constructed their refuge place on the eddying waters of nonexistence. The silent ones
  used their divine knowledge to churn the whirlpool" (p. 233).
- The oyster and the pearl, offered as a lesson rather than a marvel: "The oyster is born in the
  ocean, its trust is in the cloud. The mollusk does not drink salt water, nor does it taste sweet
  water. It produces a pearl because it sufers [text-layer?] in the deep.²⁰" (p. 211); "Oh my
  girlfriends, you should all learn the lesson of faithfulness from the oysters" (p. 211).
- "They have been nailed in my heart without a blacksmith" (pp. 200–201).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

*The translator adds nothing in this range — no headnote, no visible footnote text. What follows is
the poet's or the speaker's own judgement, as printed.*

- **A local community is cited inside the poem as warrant for the story's truth**: "Tamachi was
  commanded to favor Nuri. He took the fshergirl [text-layer?] into his carriage and raised her
  status.¹ **The Kinjhar people say this whole story is true**" (p. 218). The chunk attributes the
  claim to the Kinjhar people, not to the poet or the translator.
- **The test of a true yogi is total severance, stated four times in the same formula.** "If you
  think of becoming a yogi,¹⁶ then break all ties" (p. 229, twice); "then control your mind and
  destroy it within" (p. 229); "then drink the cup of nonbeing" (p. 230); "then seal your mouth with
  rings" (p. 230). And the criterion: "How can those who maintain the least connection with the
  world be called true ascetics?" (p. 228).
- **The poet sets the yogis outside the confessional categories, in his own voice.** "For what
  purpose do the yogis follow this path? Their hearts are not set on hell, nor do they desire
  paradise. They have nothing to do with unbelievers, and they do not have Islam in their minds"
  (pp. 232–233). Beside it, the same figures are given "Ali" as guide on a pilgrimage to Hinglaj and
  Dwarka (p. 225) and hearts in which "Ram always dwells" (p. 227). **The chunk states both and
  reconciles neither.**
- **The speaker disqualifies herself from yoga**, which is a judgement about eligibility rather than
  practice: "Yoga is proper for yogis, and it is yogis whom yoga suits… Ah, alas for me that I did
  not learn yoga" (p. 230); "You are not worthy of yoga, why do you talk about it? There is only one
  path to the beloved; you journey over diferent [text-layer?] country" (p. 230).
- **Poverty judged superior to wealth, as an explicit comparison rather than a lament** — "Their
  cloth woven from wool is better than silk and brocade" (p. 197); "For someone who has rejected
  silver, crores of rupees are like straw" (p. 198); "It is not the way of the Marus to exchange
  their kinsmen for gold" (p. 198); "I will not change the huts that I love for a palace" (p. 199);
  and the needle verse, "I do not think kingship can be compared with the needle that covers the
  naked but itself is bare. Be born again if you would know the worth of the needle" (p. 206).
- **Fate assigned to God without complaint, in the middle of the complaint**: "There is no one
  besides God the almighty, it is from him that ill fortune comes… This is the place of forgiveness.
  For Marui good and bad luck are the same" (p. 201).
- **The ruler who turns predator has no forum of appeal** — a political judgement stated twice:
  "When the ruler we rely on becomes a robber, how can the Marus live in the desert, either now or
  in the future?"; "When he who is our support becomes a robber, at whose door can the poor Marus
  complain?" (p. 212).

## Doubts

- **No archive row is informed by this chunk, and no archive id is used.** The range is Risalo text
  only. **Bhit, Bhit Shah and the shrine of Shah Abdul Latif are not mentioned anywhere in it**, so
  nothing here maps to `bhit-bhit-shah`. This is the expected result for a poetry range and is not
  a failure of the chunk.
- **Hinglaj (p. 225) is the one place-name in this chunk that may correspond to an archive row, and
  that row cannot be cited.** `shrine_index.tsv` carries a row named *Shaktipeeth Shri Hinglaj Mata
  Mandir* (Hingol Balochistan National Park, Road، Asha Pura, Pakistan) whose **id column is
  empty** — it is the only such row in the index. With no id in column 1 there is no valid mapping
  to make, so the Hinglaj line is filed under "Other saints, sites and events" instead. **A human
  must decide whether that row gets an id before this citation can enter an entry.** Note also that
  `chandragup-baba-chandragup` is located in the same national park; the chunk does not mention
  Chandragup and no mapping to it is implied.
- **Umarkot (pp. 199, 202) must not be mapped to `umarkot-amarkot-shiv-mandir`.** The chunk names
  only a fort, a palace, mansions and a cell there, and says nothing of a temple. The shared
  place-name is the whole of the resemblance.
- **No Sindhi original text appears in this chunk**, in script or in transliteration. The printed
  page numbers run consecutively 170 to 206 across PDF pp. 197–233 (printed page = PDF page − 27),
  so no facing original page falls inside this range. Whether the edition prints facing Sindhi
  elsewhere cannot be determined here. The only non-English forms preserved are **ārāṛī** (p. 204),
  **tāro** (p. 208), **surando** (p. 224), **Kapat** (p. 228), and "lanb grass" (p. 204).
- **No footnote text is in this chunk.** Markers run 10–22 in section 15, 1–3 in 16, 1–3 in 17, and
  1–21 in 18. Everything that hangs on a note — what *ārāṛī*, *tāro*, *surando*, *Kapat yogis*,
  "Am I not", Hinglaj, Dwarka, Ali, Ram, and *mihrab* are glossed as, and the source of each
  Qur'anic tag — is unreadable from here.
- **The suffix "V" on three verse numbers is unexplained.** Verses **108V** (p. 214), **27V**
  (p. 219) and **13V** (p. 222) are each the last of their section and each printed as a run of
  short lines rather than as a block. The chunk nowhere says what "V" marks.
- **Umar, Sumiro and Hamir may or may not be one person.** All three are used in direct address
  within the same section, and "Oh Umar Sumiro" (p. 199) joins two of them; "oh Hamir" appears at
  pp. 197 and 202 with no introduction. Do not assert an identification from this chunk.
- **The poet's self-naming varies** — "says Latif", "says Shah", "says Abdul Latif" — within these
  four sections (see Practices). Whether this reflects the original, the translator's practice, or
  both is not stated here.
- **Speaker attribution cannot be reconstructed from the quotation marks.** Closing quotes appear
  without openers at several points (pp. 199, 205, 211, 214), so which long runs of section 15 are
  Marui's direct speech and which are the poet's narration is unreliable in this text layer.
- **Systematic text-layer damage: the fi/fl ligatures and the doubled f are lost throughout.**
  Quoted as printed above and flagged on the word: "fne", "fsh"/"fshergirl"/"fshermen"/
  "fshmongers"/"fsherfolk", "fowers", "sufer"/"sufering", "ofer"/"ofering", "fll"/"flled",
  "fesh", "focks", "frst", "ffth", "fourishing", "diferent", "infcted", "confnement", "proft",
  "fight", "foat", "overfow", "fxed", "fnd", "fne needle", and "of" for "off" ("took of the
  clothes", p. 197; "Do not take of your blanket", p. 208; "sailed of", p. 221; "be of with you",
  p. 231; "They break of the red fowers", p. 205). **These are not corrected here.**
- **Two lines where the damage changes the sense and the PDF must be consulted** (both p. 219, in
  Tamachi's closing verse): "like a frst fruits ofering" — "first fruits" is a reading, not what is
  printed; and "He put foulness away and poured out foods of fragrance", where "foods" could be
  *floods* or *foods* and both are coherent. Do not quote either line without checking the page.
- **"lanb grass" (p. 204) and "Gandiri" (p. 215) cannot be told apart from text-layer damage.**
  Either may be the book's own spelling of a Sindhi term or a mangling of one. Quoted as printed.
- **Section 18 breaks mid-sentence at the chunk boundary**: verse 59 ends "By day their bodies are
  in" (p. 233). Its completion is in the next chunk.
