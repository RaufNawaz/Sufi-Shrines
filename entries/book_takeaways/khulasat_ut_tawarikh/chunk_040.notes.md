# khulasat_ut_tawarikh — chunk 040 — pp. 498–508

This chunk is read from p. 508 down to p. 498. It opens mid-way through suba Kashmir, which carries on from p. 509 outside this chunk (pp. 508–506). That part covers saffron cultivation, sacred springs and temples, the sarkar of Pakhli, produce and food, the routes in, and the suba's extent and revenue. A new section headed "صوبه دار الملک کابل" (suba Kabul, the royal seat) starts on p. 506 and runs to p. 501. It covers Kabul city and its gardens and royal tombs, then the tumans: Daman-i Koh, called "پنجاب کابل"; Ghorband; Zuhak and Bamiyan; Ghazni; Lohgadh[OCR?]; Lamghan; Jalalabad; Bajaur; Swat; and "بنگرام[OCR?]", "known as Peshawar". After the tumans come Peshawar and the jogis' temple "کورکهتری", the Nikshab tuman, climate, fruits, crop shares and languages. The section ends with the Hazara and Afghan peoples, the three roads from Peshawar to Kabul, Kabul as "دروازه هند", and the suba's extent and revenue. The geography closes on p. 501 with a transition: the rulers of Hindustan come next. P. 500 opens the king-lists under "فرمان روایان هندوستان از ابتدای راجه جدهشتر پاندوان" (the rulers of Hindustan from Raja Yudhishthira of the Pandavas), drawn from the Mahabharata. They tell the birth of Dhritarashtra, Pandu and Bidur through Vyasa, Pandu's curse, renunciation and death, and the births of the Pandavas and Kauravas. The chunk breaks off on Bhimsen's strength (p. 498), and the text continues on p. 497, outside this chunk.

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **gorakhnath-goraknath-temple** (the book's "کورکهتری معبد جوگیان", "Kor Khatri, the temple of the jogis", near Peshawar):
  - "در نزدیکے آن کورکهتری معبد جوگیان مشهور بود در عهد شاهجهان بادشاه منهدم شده": near it (Peshawar), Kor Khatri, the jogis' temple, was famous; it was demolished in the reign of Shah Jahan Badshah (p. 503).
  - The same sentence goes on: "امروز تیج[OCR?] تیرتهه بس دل کشای است". Jogis, sannyasis, bairagis and other dervishes have built pleasing buildings around a spring-fed tank ("در حواشی تالاب که چشمه سار است") and live there (p. 503).

- **dargah-pir-ratan-nath-jee**, **gorakhnath-goraknath-temple**, **gurdwara-bhai-beba-singh**, **gurdwara-bhai-joga-singh**, **kali-bari-mandir**, **panj-tirath**, **rahman-baba-mausoleum-rehman-baba-shrine**, **shrine-of-akhund-darweza-baba**, **shrine-of-mian-umar-baba-chamkani**, **wadpagga-sharif** (the book's "پشاور" / "بلده پشاور"):
  - "پشاور شهر قدیم است که در کتب متقدمین پرشاور و فرشاور نیز نویسند": Peshawar is an ancient city, written "Parshawar" and "Farshawar" in the books of earlier writers (p. 503).
  - "از بلده پشاور سه راه بکابل میرود": three roads run from the city of Peshawar to Kabul (pp. 502–501).
    - The first is the Nikshab road ("راه نیکشاب"), with many hard defiles and a long distance; armies pass with difficulty (p. 502).
    - The second is the "کهرپه[OCR?]" road, which joins the highway at Jalalabad. It has narrow defiles, hard passes and little water, and Afghan raids ("دست بردے[OCR?] افغانان") harm travellers (p. 502).
    - The third is "راه علی مسجد است و خیبر". It runs 18 kos ("هژده کروه") from the spring of Jamshid, "known as Jamrud", to "دهیکه[OCR?] کنار نیلاب". The Khyber pass is hard going for two kos. This road is nonetheless easier than the others and is the one armies and caravans use. The book then gives 30 kos without hills from "دکه[OCR?]" to "ننله[OCR?]", and 40 kos, with passes that are not very hard, from "ننله[OCR?]" to Kabul (p. 501).
  - "تومان بنگرام[OCR?] ان مشهور به پشاور جانب هندوستان است": the tuman of Bangram[OCR?], "known as Peshawar", lies on the Hindustan side (p. 504).
    - Its grapes, peaches and melons are like Turan's, while its heat, cold, seasons and rain are like Hindustan's (p. 504).
    - Its rice "سکهد اس[OCR?]" is better than Bengal's, and crops and grain are plentiful (p. 504).
    - "تمام[OCR?] این تومان بنگاه افغانان مهمند و غیر ذلک است": the whole tuman is the home of the Mohmand Afghans and others, who "طریق مال گذاری و رعیت گیری ... دارند", i.e. they pay revenue and are subjects (pp. 504–503).

- **panj-tirath** (the book's "راجه پاند", Raja Pandu; person-level):
  - **Birth and accession.** "راجه بچهتر برج[OCR?]", grandfather ("جد") of the Pandavas, died leaving no son. The nobles turned to "سوامی بیاس", and through him three sons were born to the raja's wives. The second wife turned yellow at the sight of the sage, so her son "بدنش تمام زرد بود ... نامش پاند گذاشتند یعنی زرد رنگ" (his whole body was yellow; they named him Pand, that is, yellow-coloured). Dhritarashtra was blind and Bidur was a slave-girl's son, so rule passed to the middle son, Pandu (p. 500).
  - **Reign and the curse.** He overcame his enemies, conquered the surrounding lands, and was "شکاردوست و عشرت طلب" (fond of hunting and pleasure). He shot a mating deer that was really an ascetic ("عابدی بود مرتاض") in deer form. Dying, the ascetic cursed him: "تو هم در وقت صحبت همخوابه خویش همخوابه مرگ شوی" (p. 500).
  - **Renunciation.** Knowing he would die in union with a woman, he gave up rule ("ترک جهانبانی") for worship in the wilderness, but grieved at having no children (p. 499).
  - **His argument to Kunti.** He told his senior wife Kunti that "هر کس که بی اولاد بمیرد بدوزخ می رود". He said their religion allows a childless man to have a son through a Brahman, and that "پدر من بی اولاد رحلت نمود بصلاح بزرگان توالد من و برادران من از بیاس دیو گردید" (p. 499).
  - **The sons.** Kunti refused any stranger but used a spell learned from an ascetic to summon beings "از عالم ملکوت". She bore "جدهشتر", then "بهیم سین", then "ارجن", and Pandu's second wife bore the twins "نکل و سهدیو". Pandu lived with these sons in the wilderness, while his blind elder brother Dhritarashtra ran affairs at "هستناپور" (p. 499).
  - **Death.** He died in the wilderness "بموجب دعای عابد بسبب صحبت زن", and "زنان خوردش بهمراهی آن بسوخت" (his younger wife/wives, printed plural, burned with him) (p. 498).
  - **The sons accepted.** Ascetics brought the five sons and the senior wife to Hastinapur. Duryodhana objected that they could not be the raja's sons, since he had given up women. A voice from the unseen declared "این ها پاندوان هستند یعنی پسران راجه پاند", born "بذرایع ملایکه" (through angels), and the people accepted them (p. 498).

  - In a cave in "تبت کلان" (Great Tibet) there is an ice image called "امرنات[OCR?]", held a great place of worship. When the moon comes out of conjunction, an ice bubble appears and grows daily, reaching "بده گز الهی" in fifteen days. It shrinks as the moon wanes, and by the month's end nothing is left. "وان را پیکر مهادیو دانند و برامد کارها را دست مایه دانند" (it is held to be the image of Mahadev and a means of having one's wishes fulfilled) (p. 508).

- **churrio-jabal-durga-mata-temple** (the book's "درگا", Durga; person-level, in suba Kashmir):
  - A stone temple on the banks of a river "پدمنی[OCR?]" that comes from "ولایت دکدو[OCR?]" (editor's footnote variant "دار دو[OCR?]"). It is called "اسمارو[OCR?]", "بدرگا منسوب است و بس بزرگ دانند" (ascribed to Durga and held in great veneration). "در هر اشتمی[OCR?] شکل مچهه[OCR?] بجنبش در اید" (p. 507).
  - "پاهنال[OCR?](۱) بتخانه ایست منسوب بدرگا": an idol-temple ascribed to Durga, used for ordeals and omens (p. 508).

    - In a dispute where the right is hard to tell, each party is given a bird or a goat, and both animals are poisoned. The animal of the party in the right lives, and the other dies (p. 508).

The first is "در زمان سابق که کابل از والیئے هند بیرون بود" (former times, when Kabul was outside the ruler of Hind's hands). ## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Suba Kabul revenue:** "هشت سرکار مشتمل بر سی و شش تومان و دوازده کرور و شصت و پنج لک و بیست هزار دام داخل این صوبه است" (p. 501).
- **Suba Kabul extent:**
  - Length "از اتک بنارس تا هندوکوه صد و پنجاه[OCR?] کروه" (p. 501).
  - Breadth "از قراباغ قندهار تا چغتان سرا صد کروه" (p. 501).
  - Bounded east by "دریاے سنده" (p. 501).
- **Kabul as a frontier charge:** "اگرچه این صوبه چندان حاصل ندارد". The ruler of Hindustan sends "مبلغ معتدبه" for the army there to keep the frontier of Turan and Iran safe (p. 501).
- **Kabul crop shares and tumans:**
  - Spring barley pays a third. "از نبات دلمی[OCR?]" pays a tenth. Grapes and "نور[OCR?]" pay no cash. "حاصل سردرختی معاف است" (orchard produce is exempt). Safflower flower pays nothing, and its seed a third (p. 503).
  - As in Samarqand and Bukhara, a pargana with its villages is called a "تومان" (p. 503).
- **Kabul languages:** "یازده زبان" (eleven languages), listed (p. 503).
- **Kabul Mughals:** the Mughals near Kabul "رعیت گیری و مال گذاری کنند" (are subjects and pay revenue) (p. 503).
- **Afghans and the roads:**
  - Afghans do not submit to imperial amirs ("بامراے بادشاهے نیایش نکنند"). They hold the highway, take a sum "بطور انعام" from the subadar, and take "کوشی[OCR?] بطریق راهداری" per head of horse, camel and ox from travellers (p. 502).
  - They also rob caravans and enslave and sell travellers (p. 502).
- **Suba Kashmir revenue:** "چهل و شش محال مشتمل بر دوازده کرور و شصت و دو لک و هشتاد و پنج هزار دام و ده[OCR?] هزار و چهارصد پرگنی داخل این صوبه است" (p. 506).
- **Kashmir assessment:** "در تمام صوبه عمل غله بخشی سه توده[OCR?] ضبط زمین و داد و ستد زر و سیم رسم نیست". Dues are measured "بخروار" (p. 506).
- **Kashmir extent:** "از قنبر[OCR?] تا دریاے کشن گنگ صد و بست کروه عرض هشتاد کروه". Its boundaries include "ولایت[OCR?] گکهران" to the west-south (p. 506).
- **Kashmir routes:** "بست و شش راه از هندوستان". The roads of "بهنبر[OCR?] و پکلی" are the best, and armies mostly enter by "پیر پنجال" (p. 506).
- **Sarkar Pakhli:** "سرکار پکلی داخل این صوبه است", 35 kos long and 25 kos wide (p. 507).
- **Kashmir saffron:** sown at the end of "فرودی" and through "اردی بهشت". One sowing flowers for six years (p. 508).
- **Pilgrimage practice in Kashmir:**
  - Many give their lives to fire around Nila Nag ("درگرداو بسیاری رخت هستی باتش در دهند") (p. 507).
  - At sowing time, milk is offered into the saffron spring at Zibun[OCR?] as an omen (p. 508).
  - "فراوان مردم هجوم اورند" (many people throng) at Shukarnag[OCR?] on the day it flows (p. 508).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- The author calls the Mahabharata "تاریخی است بزرگ تر و از جمیع صحف معتبرتر" (a greater history and more reliable than all books). He says that, by Hindu histories, rule of Hindustan lay with the Pandavas and Kauravas from the creation (p. 500).
- The author reports the judgement of "خردمندان" that Kabul is "دروازه هند", and argues that uniting Hind and Kabul under one ruler secured Punjab and all Hind (p. 501).
- The author reports the Afghans' and the Hazaras' own claims of descent as theirs ("میشمارند" / "قرار دهند") (p. 502). He gives his own judgement: "ادم دزد در ممالک دیگر کم خواهد بود و افغانان تمامی ادم دزد هستند" (p. 502).
- The author writes in the first person about Kabul women: "بعضی زنان را دیدم" (I saw some women). He says they had left one husband for another and taken up to fifteen or twenty husbands in their lives (p. 503).
- The author dates Kabul's founding "بموجب تواریخ" relative to the writing of "این نسخه" (p. 506).

## Doubts

- **Reading order and edges.** The text runs p. 508 → p. 498. P. 508 continues from p. 509 and p. 498 runs on into p. 497, both outside this chunk. Section breaks fall as the brief describes: Kashmir ends and Kabul begins on p. 506, and the geography gives way to the king-lists at pp. 501/500.
- **gorakhnath-goraknath-temple is mapped through the row's location_short ("Gor Khatri area, Peshawar"), not through its principal figure.** The book names "کورکهتری" as a "معبد جوگیان" (jogis' temple) demolished under Shah Jahan. It never names Gorakhnath. Whether the demolished temple is the row's temple is for the consolidator.
- **"امروز تیج[OCR?] تیرتهه" (p. 503) is not read here.** The book does not make clear whether this "today" clause describes the Kor Khatri site or a different place by a spring-fed tank. The first word is flagged and has not been repaired. Because a Peshawar row is named Panj Tirath, the reading of this word matters to **panj-tirath**, so a reviewer should check the lithograph. No site-level mapping has been made on its strength. Panj-tirath carries the passage only through the place-level Peshawar bullet.
- **The Peshawar bullet is place-level** (ruling (a)). It is a general description of the city, its roads and the "بنگرام[OCR?]" tuman, and names none of the ten rows' sites. Two spellings of the Khyber-road station appear, "دهیکه[OCR?]" and "دکه[OCR?]" (p. 501). They are not equated here.
- **panj-tirath (Raja Pandu) is person-level.** The chunk gives the Mahabharata story of Pandu and says nothing about Peshawar or any site linked to him. The grandfather's name "بچهتر برج[OCR?]" is flagged. "زنان خوردش" (p. 498) is printed in the plural, after the book has named only two wives, and is not resolved here.
- **The 8 Shiva rows and churrio-jabal-durga-mata-temple are person-level** (many-row and single-row person rules). Every Mahadev and Durga passage here concerns temples and springs in suba Kashmir, none of them an archive site. Weigh accordingly. The site names are flagged: "نوسر[OCR?]", "امرنات[OCR?]", "اسمارو[OCR?]", "پاهنال[OCR?]". Editor's footnote (۱) on "پاهنال[OCR?]" and (۲) on "پیتلهام[OCR?]" are illegible ("covered by the source stamp", p. 508).
- **sharada-peeth is not mapped.** The chunk never names Sharada. The Durga temple "اسمارو[OCR?]" on the river "پدمنی[OCR?]" (p. 507) is in suba Kashmir, and its printed name is flagged. A reviewer should check the lithograph before anything is attached to the AJK row. The chunk itself makes no identification.
- **The Multan bullet is place-level and only directional.** Both mentions (pp. 502, 503) use Multan as a boundary or direction for Afghan lands and the Nikshab tuman. Nothing is said about Multan itself, so weigh it as minimal. It is mapped only because ruling (a) requires it.
- **The PROVINCE punjab bullet is province-level; weigh accordingly.** "ولایت پنجاب" is the book's own region, unbounded in this chunk and not the modern province. Neither period is dated. The connective "انان[OCR?] زانیکه" is flagged.
- **No province bullet for suba Kabul or suba Kashmir.** Suba Kabul includes Peshawar, Swat, Bajaur and the Khyber road, but the book does not place them in any unit the map calls KP (brief: Peshawar is a toponym). Suba Kashmir is the Mughal suba. The map's Kashmir province is "azad kashmir", with one row, and the chunk never names that row's site. Pakhli, "بهنبر[OCR?]", Gilgit and "ولایت[OCR?] گکهران" are not in toponym_map.txt and take no row.
