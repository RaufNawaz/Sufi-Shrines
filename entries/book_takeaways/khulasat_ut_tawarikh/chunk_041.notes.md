# khulasat_ut_tawarikh — chunk 041 — pp. 509–519

This chunk is part of the book's geography of the Mughal subas. Read in the book's order (p. 519 down to p. 509), it covers the later part of suba Lahore and then the opening of suba Kashmir. It begins mid-sentence on p. 519 at the end of the succession of Baba Nanak's successors. It then describes Baba Nanak's followers and Achal near Batala with its six-day fair. The author says he includes this because Batala is his home town (pp. 519–518). The book then goes doab by doab. It covers Kangra/Nagarkot and Jwalamukhi, and then Sialkot: its history, crafts, scholars, the tomb of Imam Ali al-Haq, Sultan Sarwar's Dhonkal[OCR?] and Purmandal[OCR?]. Next come the river Deg, Jammu and Sodhra, Gujrat and Shah Daula, and the salt mountain with its mines, the Kahyala pool and Tilla (pp. 518–513). A transition, "چون شمه از اماکن مشهوره پنج دوابه بتحریر درامد حقیقت شش دریاے این صوبه ... بتسطیر دراوردن ضرور است" (now that some of the famous places of the five doabs are written, the six rivers of this suba must be set down) (p. 513), introduces the Sutlej, Beas, Ravi, Chenab, Bihat and Sind. These sections include Shah Burhan's tomb, Hir and Ranjha, and Attock with the Jalaliya rock (pp. 513–510). The suba ends with a general account beginning "بالجمله صوبه لاهور ولایتی است خوش آب و هوا" ("in sum, suba Lahore is a country of good water and air"), covering produce, wells, mines, size, borders and revenue (pp. 510–509). The heading "صوبه دل پذیر کشمیر" then opens Kashmir (p. 509), which breaks off mid-sentence. The suba Lahore heading and its opening pages, and the rest of Kashmir, are in the neighbouring chunks.

## Shrines and figures in the archive

- **shrine-of-hazrat-shah-daula-daryai** (the book's "زبده اولیاے شاه دولا" / "شاه دولا", at Gujrat — the saint):
  - He settled in the qasba of Gujrat ("دران قصبه استقامت اختیار کرده") and built tanks, wells and mosques ("تالاب ها و چاهها و مساجد احداث نموده"). He bridged ("قنطره بست") the Chenab, which came down from the hills and used to damage the town. This "موجب ازدیاد ابادی و افزونی رونق گشت" (made the town grow in people and prosperity) (p. 515).
  - "گویند" (it is said): at first he was the slave ("غلام") of "کهابدهره[OCR?]", a resident of Sialkot. He loved the faqirs "باب الله[OCR?]" and served "حضرت میان سیدنا(۱)" devotedly. The editor's footnote gives the variant "سیدنام[OCR?]" (p. 515).
  - When Mian Sayyidna was about to die, he cast "نظر فیض اثر" (a grace-bearing glance) on Shah Daula. Shah Daula's state changed and "بانوار عرفان شبستان باطن روشن گشت" (his inner self was lit with the lights of gnosis). He left Sialkot and settled in Gujrat (p. 515).
  - "از بس که روشن دل بود خزاین غیب نمودار مے گشت": because his heart was so illumined, treasures of the unseen became manifest to him (p. 515).
  - He built buildings and bridges in many places. The book singles out a bridge on the highway ("درشاهراه") over the river Deg, five kos from Eminabad toward Lahore ("پنجگروهی امن اباد جانب لاهور"). It was built so solidly "که از احدی صاحب دولتان چنین عمارت متین نشود" (that no man of wealth could build so firm a structure) (p. 515). The Deg's course later passes "پایان پل شاه دولا که برشاهراه واقع است" (below Shah Daula's bridge on the highway) (p. 516).
  - In his lifetime "عالم عالم خلایق" (throngs upon throngs of people) came from every quarter for ziyarat. They offered "از نقد و جنس فراوان نذورات" (plentiful nazrs in cash and kind). He gave visitors and other petitioners more than they brought, and his daily generosity was such that "سخاوت تمام عمر حاتم عشر عشیر ان نشود" (Hatim's lifetime of generosity would not come to a hundredth of it) (p. 515).
  - His death: "بالآخر درسنه هفدهم[OCR?] عالمگیری بعالم بقا رحلت فرمود" (he died in the seventeenth[OCR?] regnal year of Alamgir) (p. 515).
  - His tomb: "درنزدیکی ان شهر مزاران [read: مزار آن] بزرگوار زیارتگاه طبقات خلایق است". Near that city, the tomb of that venerable one is a place of visitation for people of every class (pp. 515–514).

- **shrine-of-hazrat-shah-daula-daryai** (the book's "گجرات", the town; Gujrat's only archive row):
  - "در دوابه چونهت گجرات قصبه ایست": Gujrat is a qasba in the Chaj doab, settled ("اباد شده") under Muhammad Akbar Badshah. Villages were taken out of pargana Sialkot to make it a separate pargana. "از ابتدا این قصبه چندان رونق نداشت" (at first it was not prosperous) until Shah Daula settled there (p. 515).
  - "انقصبه پیشگاه هرگونه مردم و جامع اجناس هر دیار و اشیاے نادره روزگار است": the town draws every kind of people and gathers goods from every land (p. 514).
  - Rare swords and jamdhars are made there, and "کار چکن زیاده از سیالکوت می شود" (more embroidery is done there than in Sialkot) (p. 514).
  - "درین دیار اسپ عراقی اساپیداے شود": Iraqi(-like) horses are bred in this region, some worth up to "ده هزار روپیه" (p. 514).

- **shrine-of-imam-ali-ul-haq** (the book's "امام علی الحق"):
  - "دران خطه دل کشا خوابگاه امام علی الحق خلف امام زین العابدین است": in that pleasant region (Sialkot) is the resting place of Imam Ali al-Haq, "son of Imam Zain al-Abidin" (p. 516).
  - "گویند" (it is said): he came to Hindustan with many Muslims from Arabia ("از عرب") "بقصد جهاد" (for jihad). By chance they reached "بسیالکوت[OCR?]", fought the Hindus and "درجه شهادت یافتند" (were martyred) (p. 516).
  - "اکنون مزار مظهر انوار ایشان زیارتگاه صغار و کبار است": now his tomb, "a manifestation of lights", is a place of pilgrimage for small and great (p. 516).

- **allo-mahar**, **gurdwara-babay-de-ber**, **jagannath-temple-sialkot**, **shahwala-teja-singh-mandir**, **shrine-of-imam-ali-ul-haq** (the book's "سیالکوت", also "شلکوت(۱)" and "سالکوت"):
  - "در دوابه رچنا شهر متقدمین سیالکوت است": in the Rachna doab lies the ancient city of Sialkot (p. 517).
  - It was called "شلکوت" (editor's footnote (1) is [illegible]). Its founding is attributed to "راجه شل[OCR?] طغائ[OCR?] پاندوان". The city and the raja are mentioned in the Mahabharat, "که از تصنیف ان قریب پنجهزار سال میگذرد" (composed about five thousand years ago) (p. 517).
  - It is also called "سالکوت" and attributed to "راجه سالباهن". "قلعه پخته از و یادگار است": a brick fort is his memorial (p. 517).
  - "درزمان سابق دار الحکومت ولایت پنجاب بود و دو سه کروه آبادی داشت": formerly it was the seat of government of Punjab, with two or three kos of habitation. Now it is known as Sialkot, and "از جمیع قصبات اینصوبه درابادی افزون است" (it is the most populous of all the qasbas of this suba) (p. 517).
  - On his fifth campaign, "درسنه پانصد و هشتاد(۲) هجری", Sultan Shihab al-Din Ghori failed to take Lahore. He came to Sialkot, repaired and rebuilt the old fort, and left his army there (p. 517). Editor's footnotes: (2) "[illegible] پانصد[OCR?] و هشتاد[OCR?] [illegible]"; (3) "[illegible]".
  - "راجه مانسنگه اکبرشاهی", faujdar of Jammu and jagirdar of Sialkot, repaired the fort and improved the city. Then "صفدرخان جهانگیری", also faujdar of Jammu and holding this pargana in jagir, rebuilt the fort and its tower. Most later governors made repairs too (p. 517).
  - "عمارت قانونگویان قوم بدهیره[OCR?] و بعضی مردم دیگر بغایت مطبوع و دل کشا است": the houses of the qanungos of the Budhera[OCR?] community, and of some others, are very fine (p. 517).
  - Paper: good paper is made, especially "کاغذ مانسنگهی و هم حریری و خاصه جهانگیری". It is white, durable and sent to other regions (p. 517).
  - Embroidery: karchikan in silk and kalabatun. The book lists items including "فوطه و سوزنی و عدشقه[OCR?] و دستارخوان و خوانپوش". It says "هر سال قریب لکهه روپیه از کارچکن دوزی به بیع و شرا می آید" (about a lakh of rupees of embroidery is traded each year) and that it goes all over the world (p. 517).
  - Jamdhar, katari and barchhi are well made (p. 517).
  - Gardens surround the city, especially "باغ نظر محمد بهوته[OCR?]", with every kind of fruit (p. 517).
  - The stream "ریک[OCR?]" rises in "کوه جمو" (the Jammu hills) and flows below the city. Ten kos beyond, "زمین همین[OCR?] میشود"; it takes the name "کهیلری[OCR?]" and spreads out until it loses its name (p. 517).
  - When the stream floods in the rains, "جمیع مردم سیالکوت از اکابر و اصاغر" (all the people of Sialkot, great and small) play in the water joyfully, naked except for a loincloth and carrying leather water-skins. Anyone from the city who is far away remembers this water-play with pleasure (pp. 517–516).
  - "دار العلوم و جامع علما و معدن فضل و مسکن فضلاست": the city is a seat of learning (p. 516).
    - Under Akbar, "مولاناے کمال الدین حسین", "خان مرزبان کشمیر رنجیده" (having taken offence, apparently with the Khan who ruled Kashmir), came to Sialkot "درسنه نهصد و هفتاد و یک هجری" and established teaching there (p. 516).
    - Under Shah Jahan, "مولوی عبدالحکیم" spread the sciences further and wrote glosses ("حاشیه") on some books. Students came to his "مدرسه متبرکه" from far and near (p. 516).
    - After him, his second[OCR?] son "مولوی عبدالله" ran the madrasa. He was called "امام وقت" and died "درسنه ۲۶[OCR?](۱) عالمگیری" (editor's footnote: "سنه ۲۶[OCR?]") (p. 516).
  - Sialkot as a reference point:
    - Dhonkal[OCR?] is 12 kos from Sialkot (see sakhi-sarwar) and Purmandal[OCR?] is 15 kos (see Other) (p. 516).
    - The Chenab passes "دیهات پرگنه سیالکوت" (the villages of pargana Sialkot) (p. 512).
    - Gujrat's villages were taken out of "پرگنه سیالکوت" (p. 515).
    - Shah Daula's first master lived in Sialkot (p. 515).
    - Gujrat produces more embroidery than Sialkot (p. 514).

- **sakhi-sarwar** (the book's "سلطان سرور"):
  - "دوازده کروه سیالکوت دهونکل[OCR?] مکانی است منسوب سلطان سرور": twelve kos from Sialkot, Dhonkal[OCR?] is a place associated with Sultan Sarwar (p. 516).
  - It is "دایما زیارتگاه خلایق" (always a place of pilgrimage). In summer people come from every region and offer nazrs ("نذورات می گذرانند"), and "تا دو ماه دران مکان هجوم بسیار می شود" (for two months the place is very crowded) (p. 516).

- **langer-makhdoom** (the book's "شاه برهان", in the city "چندتوت[OCR?]" [read perhaps: چنیوت]):
  - On the Chenab's course, after Hazara: "نزدیک شهر چندتوت[OCR?] که شهر متقدمین است ازمیان دو کوهچه میگذرد و تماشاے عجایب است" (near the ancient city the river passes between two hillocks, a wonderful sight) (p. 511).
  - "درین شهر مزار شاه برهان که بولایت شهرت داشت و خلایق کثیر اعتقاد دارند واقع است": the tomb of Shah Burhan is in this city. He was famous for his sainthood, and many people believe in him (p. 511).

- **tilla-jogian** (the book's "تله(۲)[OCR?]", the "ریاضت[OCR?] کده بالناتهه[OCR?] جوگی"):
  - "بالاے همین کوه بهفت کروهے قلعه رهتاس": on top of this same (salt) mountain, seven kos from Rohtas fort, is the place of austerity of "بالناتهه[OCR?] جوگی", called "تله[OCR?]" (editor's footnote variant: "بله[OCR?]"). It lies "چهار کروه بلند" (four kos high) (p. 514).
  - On the appointed days, especially "روز شیورات که روزه است منسوب بمهادیو[OCR?]" (Shivrat, a fast associated with Mahadev), at the end of winter ("دربرامد[OCR?] زمستان"), "خلایق کثیر و طوایف جوگیان" (crowds of people and bands of jogis) gather there "و نمایش گری بکاری برند" (and put on displays) (pp. 514–513).

- **tilla-jogian** (the Salt Range: the book's salt mountain, "کوه جوده", on which it places Tilla):
  - In the Sind Sagar doab ("دوابه سندساگر"), rock salt ("نمک سنگ") is dug from the foot of the hills near Shamsabad. It is famous above all other salts and is called "نمک سندهه[OCR?]" because it is produced in that doab (p. 514).
  - "تمام کوه از نمک واقع شده که طول آن صد کروه متجاوز نشان مے دهند": the whole mountain is salt, said to be more than a hundred kos long (p. 514).
  - "درظفرنامه و اکبرنامه ان راکوه جوده نوشته اند" (the Zafarnama and the Akbarnama call it Koh-i Jud). Jud was "رئیس قوم جنجوهه" (a chief of the Janjuha). His descendants still live and rule in the parganas "کرچاک[OCR?] و نندنه[OCR?] و کهیاله(۱)[OCR?]" at the foot of the hills. The editor's variant for the last is "کهتاز[OCR?]" (p. 514).
  - Salt mining (p. 514):
    - Miners called "الاشه کش[OCR?]" dig tunnels "زیاده از دو صد سیصد درعه[OCR?] عمیق" (more than two or three hundred cubits[OCR?] deep).
    - Each man goes in naked, with a lamp in his hand and a pick on his shoulder. He cuts a lump of about "سه من" (three man) of salt and carries it out on his back. He is paid by the officials ("از ناظمان این امر اجرت گرفته"), and the men feel no fear.
  - "بحکمت الهی" (by divine wisdom) the tunnels are not hot in summer or cold in winter (p. 514).
  - "کهوهره[OCR?] و کهیوره[OCR?]" are two large tunnels near Shamsabad that yield "هر سال چندان لکهه من نمک" (so many lakhs of man of salt every year). Their revenue, together with that of other places, "بسرکار بادشاهی ضبط می شود" (is collected by the imperial government) (p. 514).
  - Craftsmen make trays, dishes, covers and lamp-stands out of salt. The "گچ شیرین" (sweet gypsum) found next to it is used for whitewash inside the houses of the great. The stone is also made into dishes and "انجوره[OCR?]" (p. 514).
  - Nearby, in the bounds of Kahyala, is a pool, "کهنتا[OCR?] چهه[OCR?] کولابی[OCR?]", whose depth no one can tell (p. 514):
    - It is held to be an ancient temple. On great days, such as the sun's entry into Aries, Hindus crowd there "بقصد تغسل[OCR?]" (to bathe).
    - They believe the earth has two eyes. The right eye is the pool of "بهکر[OCR?]" near Ajmer and the left eye is this pool.

- **gurdwara-babay-de-ber**, **gurdwara-balila-sahib-bal-lila-sahib**, **gurdwara-chakki-sahib**, **gurdwara-choa-sahib**, **gurdwara-darbar-sahib-kartarpur**, **gurdwara-khoohi-bhai-lalo-bhai-lalo-di-khooi**, **gurdwara-malji-sahib**, **gurdwara-panja-sahib**, **gurdwara-patti-sahib**, **gurdwara-pehli-patshahi-jind-pir-sukkur**, **gurdwara-rori-sahib**, **gurdwara-sach-khand-sahib**, **gurdwara-sacha-sauda**, **gurdwara-sahib-saidpur-guru-nanak-dev-ji**, **gurdwara-sri-kiara-sahib**, **gurdwara-sri-tilganji-sahib**, **gurdwara-tambo-sahib**, **gurudwara-janam-asthan-nankana-sahib** (the book's "بابا نانک"; the book names no site of his):
  - "از مریدان و معتقدان بابا نانک اکثرے صاحب حال و مقبول المقال و اهل ریاضات و مستجاب الدعوات می باشند": most of Baba Nanak's disciples and devotees are men of spiritual states and austerities, whose word is accepted and whose prayers are answered (p. 519).
  - "خلاصه عبادت اینطایفه مطالعه اشعار مرشد خویش است که بسرود و نغمه میگویند": in sum, this group's worship is reading their master's verses, which they sing (p. 519).
  - Many have put away worldly ties. Kin and stranger, friend and foe are the same to them, and they live "با دوستان یکرنگ و با دشمنان بی جنگ" (sincere with friends, without quarrel with enemies) (p. 519).

## Province-level material

- PROVINCE punjab (book's word: "صوبه لاهور" / "این صوبه"; also "پنجاب" in "پنج دریاے پنجاب" and "اهل پنجاب"; refers to the author's time, c. 1107 AH, a Mughal suba):
  - The suba is organised by doabs. The book describes "اماکن مشهوره پنج دوابه" (the famous places of the five doabs) and then "شش دریاے این صوبه که برین پنج دوابه حایل است" (the six rivers of this suba that bound these five doabs) (p. 513).
  - Doab names as printed:
    - "بست جالندهر و نهروال[OCR?]" (Sutlej–Beas) (p. 513);
    - "باری مانجهه" (Beas–Ravi) (p. 513);
    - "چنا[OCR?]" (Ravi–Chenab) (p. 512); "رچنا" for Sialkot (p. 517);
    - "چنتهه[OCR?]" (Chenab–Bihat) (p. 511); "چونهت" for Gujrat (p. 515);
    - "سند ساگر" (Bihat–Sind) (p. 511).
  - "پنج دریاے پنجاب" (the five rivers of Punjab) join the Indus beyond Multan in the Baloch country. From there the whole stream is called Sind and becomes "بحر زخار[OCR?]" (a surging sea) (p. 510). The combined Sutlej–Beas likewise ends in the Indus, "که راوی و چناب و بهت نیز درو واصل است" (into which the Ravi, Chenab and Bihat also flow) (p. 513).
  - "بالجمله صوبه لاهور ولایتی است خوش آب و هوا": summers are very hot and winters cold (p. 510). Produce:
    - melons and grapes like those of Iran and Turan, and mangoes like Hindustan's;
    - rice better than Bengal's and sugarcane better than the Deccan's.
  - Irrigation (p. 510):
    - "اکثر مدار اب یاری[OCR?] چاه است": irrigation is mostly from wells.
    - Craftsmen of old devised a wheel of "سیصد و شصت چوب" (360 pieces of wood) and more than a hundred small pots. A pair of oxen turns it, and each turn lifts "چندین صد من اب" (several hundred man of water).
    - Kharif and rabi cultivation and cheap grain depend on rain.
  - Gold is washed from the sand in places, especially on "دریاے بیاه و بهت" (the Beas and the Bihat). The northern hills have mines of silver, copper, "روئین[OCR?]" and "جسد[OCR?]"; the miners profit, "و محصول و بسرکار بادشاهی دهند" (and pay the tax to the imperial government) (p. 510).
  - Size: "طول این صوبه از اب ستلج تا دریاے سند صد و هشتاد کروه" (length, from the Sutlej to the Indus, 180 kos). "عرض از بهیره تا چوکهندی[OCR?] هشتاد و هفت کروه" (breadth, from Bhera to Chaukhandi[OCR?], 87 kos) (p. 510).
  - Borders: "شرق رویه سهرند و غرب سو ملتان و شمال کشمیر و جنوب بیپاپور[OCR?]" (Sirhind to the east, Multan to the west, Kashmir to the north, Bipapur[OCR?] to the south) (p. 510).
  - Revenue: "پنج سرکار بعضے" (p. 510) / "پنج دوابه مشتمل بر سیصد و شانزده محال و هشتاد و نه کرور و سی و سه لک و هفتاد هزار دام داخل این صوبه است". That is: five sarkars, in the five doabs, with three hundred and sixteen mahals and eighty-nine kror, thirty-three lakh, seventy thousand dams, make up this suba (p. 509).
  - "اهل پنجاب" (the people of Punjab) compose wonderful verses and designs on the love of Hir and Ranjha and sing them as songs (pp. 512–511).

- **Pilgrimage and offerings at Pakistani sites:**

  - Imam Ali al-Haq's tomb at Sialkot is a ziyaratgah "صغار و کبار" (of small and great) (p. 516).
  - At Dhonkal[OCR?], Sultan Sarwar's place, there is a summer gathering of two months with nazrs (p. 516).
  - Shah Burhan's tomb: "خلایق کثیر اعتقاد دارند" (many people believe in him) (p. 511).
  - At Tilla, crowds of people and jogis gather on Shivrat (pp. 514–513).
  - Hindus bathe at the Kahyala pool at the sun's entry into Aries (p. 514).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- Shah Daula: the dying Mian Sayyidna's glance changes his state and lights his inner self with gnosis (p. 515).
- Shah Daula: "خزاین غیب نمودار مے گشت" (treasures of the unseen became manifest to him) (p. 515). The book offers this as the source of his giving more than he received.
- Salt mines: "بحکمت الهی" the tunnels are neither hot in summer nor cold in winter (p. 514).
- Kahyala pool: no one knows its depth. It is the earth's left eye; the right eye is the pool of "بهکر[OCR?]" near Ajmer (p. 514). This is Hindu belief, as the book reports it.
- Jalaliya rock at Attock: the common people say a holy man named "جلالیه" is buried on top of it. The author prefers the Akbar story (pp. 511–510; see Arguments).
- Achal: some of the sick are cured "بیامن دعاے خدا اندیشان" (through the blessing of the prayers of the God-minded) (p. 518).
- Nagarkot: a tongue cut off as a vow grows back in hours or days. Some cut off their heads, and when companions set the heads back "بحکمت الهی از سر نو زندگی یابند" (they live again by divine wisdom) (pp. 518–517). Outside Pakistan.
- Jwalamukhi: fire comes out of the ground (p. 517). Outside Pakistan.
- Kashmir: near "سنده براری[OCR?]" a spring is dry for six months. When farmers pray and sacrifice sheep and goats on a fixed day, "بقدرت الهی اب بر جوشد" (by divine power the water gushes) and waters five villages. The water of "گوگرناگ[OCR?]" fills the hungry and makes the full hungry (p. 509).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **The Jalaliya rock.** The author reports the popular explanation of the name ("برزبان عوام الناس است"). He sets against it what he has heard ("وجه تسمیه اینچنان استماع یافته"). An Afghan named Jalaliya was notorious under Akbar. When the royal jewel-boat broke on the rock, the emperor said "این سنگ هم جلالیه گردید" (this rock too has turned Jalaliya), and "سخن بادشاهان بادشاه سخن است" (kings' words are the king of words), so the name stuck (pp. 511–510).
- **Sialkot's founding.** The author gives two traditions side by side without choosing: Shalkot/Raja Shal[OCR?], citing the Mahabharat, and Salkot/Raja Salbahan (p. 517).
- **Koh-i Jud.** The author cites "ظفرنامه و اکبرنامه" for the name and explains it from Jud, a Janjuha chief (p. 514).
- **The Indus.** The author admits its source is "ظاهر نیست" (not known) and gives the travellers' report (p. 511).
- **The Chenab.** The author gives the Hindi-book name "چندر بهاگا" and makes the pun "آب چناب و آب حیات تجنیس خطی" (the two names look alike in writing) (p. 512).
- **Baba Nanak's followers.** The author's own judgement is that their devotion to their leader is rarely found among other groups (p. 519).
- **The author's stance on Batala.** He writes at length about Batala and its fair "چون زاد و بوم نگارنده این نسخه دل کشاے بتاله است" (because the author's native place is Batala) (p. 518).
- **Editor, not author.** The p. 519 footnote says "سنه هجری با سنه جلوس مطابقت[OCR?] ندارد سنه هفدهم جلوس عالمگیری موافق سنه ۸۴[OCR?] - ۸۵[OCR?] هجری می باشد". That is, the Hijri year given for Tegh Bahadur's death does not match the regnal year, and in the editor's view regnal year 17 corresponds to Hijri [10]84–85[OCR?] (p. 519).

## Citable passages

- "درایام حیات ان ولایت پناه عالم عالم خلایق از اطراف گیتی بزیارت می آمد": "In the lifetime of that refuge of sainthood, throngs upon throngs came from every quarter of the world for ziyarat." (Shah Daula) (p. 515)
- "دران خطه دل کشا خوابگاه امام علی الحق خلف امام زین العابدین است": "In that delightful region is the resting place of Imam Ali al-Haq, son of Imam Zain al-Abidin." (p. 516)
- "دوازده کروه سیالکوت دهونکل[OCR?] مکانی است منسوب سلطان سرور": "Twelve kos from Sialkot, Dhonkal[OCR?] is a place associated with Sultan Sarwar." (p. 516)
- "درین شهر مزار شاه برهان که بولایت شهرت داشت و خلایق کثیر اعتقاد دارند واقع است": "In this city is the tomb of Shah Burhan, famed for sainthood, in whom many people have faith." (p. 511)
- "اگر شخصی نیم شبی وارد شود و اسم بابا نانک درمیان ارد ... او را برادر و دوست انگاشته": "If someone arrives at midnight and speaks the name of Baba Nanak … they treat him as brother and friend." (p. 519)

## Doubts

- **Chunk boundaries and order.**
  - The chunk opens mid-sentence at p. 519 ("...رشید سی و هفت سال سجاده آراے گردید"). The heading and opening of suba Lahore, Lahore city, Batala and the start of the Guru succession are all in chunk 042 (p. 520 and above).
  - It ends mid-sentence in Kashmir at p. 509. This chunk holds the OPENING of suba Kashmir (heading on p. 509), not its end: read high to low, Kashmir follows Lahore. The coordinator's description of "the end of suba Kashmir" does not fit the text.
  - All page joins read coherently high → low. The one rough join is p. 510/509, "پنج سرکار بعضے | پنج دوابه مشتمل بر...", where a word may be missing. The revenue sentence is quoted as printed.
- **Guru Nanak, 18 ids (person-level).** The attachment is person-level, not site-specific: the chunk names no Nanak site, only "بابا نانک", his followers and their practices (p. 519).
- **Guru Hargobind, 6 ids (person-level).**
  - The only place named for him is Kiratpur, which has no row (p. 513). The attachment is person-level.
  - The 37-year incumbent's name falls on p. 520, so the chunk itself never says it was Hargobind. Only the following clause connects Har Rai's succession to "جد بزرگوار" (his grandfather) and to Hargobind.
- **gurdwara-dash-mesh-pita.** This rests on a name-form match the chunk does not make. The book writes "گورو گوبند راے" / "گوروگوبند رائے", son of Tegh Bahadur, while the row's figure is "Guru Gobind Singh". The consolidator should confirm or drop it. The row's site (Sukkur) is not named.
- **sakhi-sarwar.** This is person-level. The book names "سلطان سرور" (clearly a saint's name here, not the word "joy"), but the place it describes is Dhonkal[OCR?], 12 kos from Sialkot. That is not the row's site at Sakhi Sarwar/Dera Ghazi Khan, which the chunk never names. Weigh accordingly; this material may belong to a separate Dhonkal site.
- **langer-makhdoom: held identity question, plus a damaged toponym.**
  - The book's "شاه برهان" is mapped to the row's "Makhdoom Burhan-ud-din" by name.
  - The city is printed "چندتوت[OCR?]". The reading "چنیوت" (Chiniot) is mine and is offered only because of the flag.
  - The facts about the city (ancient, two hillocks) are place-level.
- **shrine-of-imam-ali-ul-haq.**
  - "خلف امام زین العابدین" is printed as the book gives it.
  - The account is framed as "گویند" (it is said).
  - "بسیالکوت[OCR?]" is flagged.
  - The verb "یافتند" is plural: the martyrdom is the whole band's.
- **shrine-of-hazrat-shah-daula-daryai.**
  - Flagged readings: "هفدهم[OCR?]" (regnal year of death), "کهابدهره[OCR?]" (his first master), "باب الله[OCR?]", and "میان سیدنا(۱)" (variant "سیدنام[OCR?]").
  - "مزاران بزرگوار" is unflagged in print. My reading, "مزار آن بزرگوار", is offered in brackets.
  - The book spells the name "شاه دولا".
  - The Gujrat town bullet carries the same single id and is place-level.
  - "گجرات" here is clearly the Punjab town in the Chaj doab with Shah Daula, not suba Gujarat.
- **Regnal year 17.** Both Shah Daula's death (p. 515) and Tegh Bahadur's execution (p. 519) are dated "هفدهم عالمگیری". The editor's p. 519 footnote, giving regnal 17 as "۸۴[OCR?] - ۸۵[OCR?]" Hijri, is the editor's equation for Tegh Bahadur only. It is noted here and not applied to Shah Daula. No date is converted.
- **Other dates as printed, none converted:**
  - Ghori: "درسنه پانصد و هشتاد(۲) هجری", with footnote (2) partly illegible: "[illegible] پانصد[OCR?] و هشتاد[OCR?] [illegible]" (p. 517).
  - Kamal al-Din Husain: "نهصد و هفتاد و یک هجری" (p. 516).
  - Abd Allah: "۲۶[OCR?](۱) عالمگیری" (p. 516).
  - Tegh Bahadur: "۸۱[OCR?](۱) هجری مطابق هفدهم عالمگیری" (p. 519).
  - The Mahabharat: "قریب پنجهزار سال" (p. 517).
- **Toponym attachments, all place-level (ruling (a)):**
  - Lahore (35), Multan (8), Sialkot (5) and Gujrat (1).
  - Eminabad (3; the book writes "امن اباد").
  - Jhelum (2), Rohtas fort (1), the Salt Range (1, via the book's "همین کوه" link from Tilla), Jhang (1), Khushab (1) and Siwistan → Sehwan Sharif (2).
  - Several are bare locators on a river's course. Rohtas fort, Khushab and Siwistan get only one mention each.
  - Jhang and Khushab match the rows only at district level: garh-maharaja-shorkot is at Garh Maharaja (Shorkot), and amb-temples-amb-sharif is on Sakesar.
  - "ولایت سیوستان" is a region, not the town of Sehwan.
  - The Multan border "غرب سو ملتان" may mean suba Multan rather than the city.
- **Homonyms NOT mapped:**
  - "شمس آباد": the book places it on the Bihat between Nandana and Bhera, at the foot of the salt hills, so it is not the Okara row `shamsabad`.
  - "قصبه شاهپور تابع قصبه نورپور": on the Ravi in the hills, so not the Shahpur of `shah-yousuf`.
  - "فرید اباد": a pargana on the Ravi/Deg, not Baba Farid.
  - "دولت اباد": a pargana on the Deg.
  - "هزاره": a place on the Chenab, not the Hazara region.
  - "جلالیه": the Attock rock and an Afghan bandit, not Jalaluddin of Uch.
- **Not mapped, for the consolidator to check:**
  - **The Kahyala pool (p. 514)** is regarded as an ancient temple where Hindus bathe at the sun's entry into Aries and as the earth's "left eye". Its name, "کهنتا[OCR?] چهه[OCR?] کولابی[OCR?]", is damaged; the editor's variant for "کهیاله" is "کهتاز[OCR?]". The chunk does not name Katas or Choa Saidanshah, so `katas-raj-temples` is NOT mapped. The material sits under the Salt Range bullet.
  - **Mahadev (Shiva, principal figure of 8 rows)** is named only for specific non-archive sites: Chamba (p. 512), Purmandal[OCR?] in Jammu (p. 516) and Achal via "سوام کارتک خلف مهادیو" (p. 519). He also appears in the Shivrat festival at Tilla (p. 514), which is recorded under tilla-jogian. Under the named-site branch, the 8 Shiva ids are not applied. Flagged in case the consolidator reads the many-row rule more widely.
  - **"بهوانی"** (Nagarkot p. 518; Trikuta p. 512) is not equated by the chunk with any archive figure (Durga or Kali); not mapped.
  - **Gorakhnath** is not named. Tilla's figure in the book is "بالناتهه[OCR?] جوگی", flagged, and the site name "تله[OCR?]" has the variant "بله[OCR?]".
  - **The Shalamar garden.** `toponym_map.txt` maps "shalimar gardens" to madho-lal-hussain, because that row is "near" the gardens. Following the brief, a named garden that is not itself a row goes under Other and is not mapped.
  - **Waris Shah** is not named in the Hir–Ranjha passages; mausoleum-of-waris-shah is not mapped.
  - **Pakhli and Dhantur[OCR?]** are on the Indus/Bihat course. The toponym map has no key for either, so they are not mapped to the Mansehra rows.
  - **Bhakkar** has no map key, and **Thatta** has no row.
  - **"حدود بلوچان"** (the Baloch country on the Indus) is not treated as the province of Balochistan.
- **Province-level, weigh accordingly.** This is a Mughal suba ("صوبه لاهور"), not the modern province's boundaries; the book's own unit is used. The suba as described includes places outside Pakistan: Batala, the Jalandhar doab, the Kangra and Jammu hills, Sirhind. No suba extent beyond the book's own figures is supplied. Kashmir takes no province: the material is the Srinagar valley, and Sharada is not named.
- **Kamal al-Din Husain's clause (p. 516).** "مولاناے کمال الدین حسین خان مرزبان کشمیر رنجیده" has no preposition. Whether "خان" is part of his name or "the Khan, ruler of Kashmir" with whom he was offended is not settled here.
