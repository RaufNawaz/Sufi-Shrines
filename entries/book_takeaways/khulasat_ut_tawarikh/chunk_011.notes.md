# khulasat_ut_tawarikh — chunk 011 — pp. 129–142

The book reads from the highest page down. In that order the chunk runs from p. 142 to p. 129, all inside one reign. The ruler is called "حضرت خاقان زمان" throughout, and the chunk itself names him "جهانگیر شاه" / "شاه جهانگیر" on Nur Jahan's seal and coin (p. 139). It opens with a new heading, "بیان درآمدن نور جهان بیگم زوجه شیر افگن خان بحجره[OCR?] حرم سرائے قدسی" (pp. 142–139). This section covers Sher Afgan's career (Qandahar, Multan, the Thatta campaign, Bengal), his killing of Qutb al-Din Khan and his own death, Ghiyas Beg's family, Nur Jahan's entry into the harem in regnal year six, and her hold on the state. Then come:
- "در بیان باز آمدن خان عالم از ایلچی گری ایران" (pp. 139–138);
- "نهضت موکب والائے مقدس بسیر و شکار گجرات یعنی احمدآباد" (pp. 138–137), with Khair al-Nisa Begam's garden of paper leaves and wax fruit;
- "ولادت باسعادت سلطان محمد اورنگ زیب پسر[OCR?] بادشاه زاده سلطان خرم" (pp. 137–136).

"در بیان مقرر کردن منار و چاه و درختان در شاهراه و عمارات جهانگیرآباد و لاهور" (pp. 136–135) covers the Akbarabad–Lahore highway, the founding of Jahangirabad (tied to Shaikh Salim's prayer), and the royal palace at Lahore. "در بیان تعریف و مذمت تنباکو و منع گشتن آن بموجب حکم والا" (pp. 135–133) covers the spread and banning of tobacco; offenders were paraded in Lahore. The chunk ends with two sections:
- "در بیان بعضے از سوانح بدائع" (pp. 133–131): strange births, a bearded woman, a lion and a jogi, Hakim Ali's underwater room, lightning-iron from Jalandhar, and the emperor's visit to a wonder-working dervish at Mathura;
- "رسیدن جماعة بازی گران بنگاله[OCR?] بحضور مقدس و تماشای انواع حیرت افزا" (pp. 131–129), the Bengal jugglers' tricks. It breaks off mid-sentence at "هرگاه" (p. 129).

  - **Tobacco offenders punished in the city.** Farmans went to governors ordering an end to the "بدعت" of smoking and to its sale. Then "اکثری[OCR?] راکه با وجود صدور حکم جرات بکشیدن آن نمودند در شهر لاهور تشهیر فرمودند بلکه بعضے را لب بریدند": many who dared to smoke despite the order were paraded through the city of Lahore, and some had their lips cut. Still they "عبرت پذیر نشدند و اجتناب نورزیدند", and the practice grew day by day (p. 133).
  - **The highway to Lahore.** "در سال چهاردهم[OCR?] جلوس والا از اکبرآباد تا لاهور در شاهراه": the emperor ordered three things along the road:
    - a tall minar at every kos ("هر کروه[OCR?]") to guide travellers;
    - a baked-brick well "در دو[OCR?] کروه";
    - two rows of trees for shade and fruit.
    The officials built the minars and wells and planted fruit trees (p. 136).
  - **The royal palace.** "همدرین سال دولتخانه سرکار والا در دارالسلطنت لاهور مشتمل بر اقسام نشیمن دل کشا و انواع اماکن فرح افزا بعمارات متین تعمیر یافت و هشت لک[OCR?] روپیه خرچ گردید": the same year the royal palace in Lahore, with many pleasant seats and buildings, was built in solid masonry at a cost of eight lakh[OCR?] rupees. A ruba'i praising the building follows (p. 135). The section heading calls these "عمارات جهانگیرآباد و لاهور" (p. 136).

- **prahladpuri-temple**, **shrine-of-bahauddin-zakariya**, **shrine-of-hafiz-muhammad-jamal-multani**, **shrine-of-hazrat-shah-ali-akbar-shah-ali-akbar-shamsi**, **shrine-of-shah-rukn-e-alam**, **shrine-of-shah-shams-ud-din-sabzwari**, **shrine-of-shah-yusaf-gardez**, **shrine-of-syed-musa-pak** (the book's "ملتان"):
  - Ali Quli Istajlu (later Sher Afgan) came to Hindustan by way of Qandahar. "چون بملتان رسید اورا بخانخانان میرزا خان که متوجه فتح تهته بود ملاقات دست[OCR?] داد": on reaching Multan he met the Khankhanan Mirza Khan, who was then bound for the conquest of Thatta. The Khankhanan reported him to court and enrolled him in absentia (p. 142). The book says nothing about the city itself. See Doubts.

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

**The wonder-working dervish at Mathura (pp. 132–131)**
- The emperor went "درخطه دل کشائے متهرا" to see a "درویش خدا اندیش" who is not named. "بعضے مردم اورا از باریافتگان درگاه الوهیت دانسته اکثر خوارق[OCR?] بیان میکردند و بعضے بسحر[OCR?] و جادو نسبت میدادند": some held him to be one admitted to the divine court and told many wonders of him, while others put it down to sorcery (p. 132).
- **The gold.** It was evening. After the dervish finished his prayer, five other dervishes came and stood beside him, "که مرشد انها بود". They raised their hands in supplication "بمبدأے[OCR?] فیاض", and gold rained on them from the air: "قریب هفتصد[OCR?] اشرفی طلا مرتبه بمرتبه باریده" (p. 132).
- The dervish gave half the ashrafis to the emperor and told him to keep them in his treasury: "گاهے کمی نخواهد شد". He shared the other half among "درویشان حواشی خود". The emperor took his leave (p. 132).
- **The hand-kiss.** On the way back the emperor thought "صد حیف که بدرویش دست بوس دست نکردم": what a pity he had not kissed the dervish's hand. At that moment "خادم[OCR?] آن درویش کامل" arrived and said "دست بوس شما بدرویش رسید": your hand-kiss has reached the dervish (pp. 132–131).
- **The verdict.** The emperor asked whether this should be counted as "کشف و کرامات", as "سحر و جادو", or as "تسخیر اجنه". "آخر الامر تحقیق پیوست که این همه از نتایج و فور عبادات و حصول کمالات است": in the end it was established that all of it came from abundant worship and attained perfections (p. 131). A ruba'i follows: what reaches "بصاحب دل" becomes a jewel (p. 131).

**Shaikh Salim**
- "شیخ سلیم درویش که از دعائے ان مستجاب الدعوات آنحضرت سعادت ولادت یافته[OCR?]": the emperor was granted birth through the prayer of Shaikh Salim, called "مستجاب الدعوات" (pp. 136–135).
- In his princehood the emperor founded "در پنجاب ششینو[OCR?] پور نام دیهی متصل ساهوملی[OCR?]", named "بنام نامی", that is, after himself. "بمناسبت[OCR?] اسم[OCR?]" the shaikh's name, he was known as "بسلطان[OCR?] ششینو[OCR?]" (pp. 136–135). See Jahangirabad below.
- "قطب الدین خان ولد شیخ سلیم چشتی" was the governor of Bengal killed by Sher Afgan (p. 142). The chunk does not say whether this Shaikh Salim is the one of p. 136. See Doubts.

**Other religious figures**
- "نوبتے درویشی[OCR?] شیر لال خان نام قوی هیکل تربیت کرده از نظر گذرانیدند": a powerful lion named Lal Khan, raised by a "درویشی[OCR?]", was presented to the emperor (p. 133).
- "جمعے از جوگیان نیز محو تماشائے بودند": jogis were among the crowd watching the lion. The lion went to "یک جوگی که برهنه بود" "بطریق ملاعبت[OCR?] نه بطرز غضب", treating him as it would its mate, then let him go, to general wonder (p. 133).
- Among tobacco users the book lists "صلحا و زهاد" and "فقرا" (p. 135).
- Nur Jahan is praised as "در خداجوئی و خدا طلبی درویش کردار" (p. 140).

**Nur Jahan and Sher Afgan (pp. 142–139)**
- **Sher Afgan's origin.** "این شیر افگن علی قلی استجلو نام داشت و سفرچی شاه اسمعیل خلف[OCR?] شاه عباس صفوی والی ایران بود" (p. 142).
- **His rise.** After Shah Ismail's death he came "از راه قندهار" to Hindustan "در زمان[OCR?] خلافت حضرت بادشاه خلد آرامگاه", and met the Khankhanan at Multan (above). He showed "ترددات نمایان" in the Thatta campaign. "بعد فتح تهته" he received a fitting mansab at the Khankhanan's request. "در عهد سلطنت حضرت بادشاه بشیر افگن مخاطب گشت و در صوبه بنگاله جاگیر یافت" (p. 142).
- **His death.** He was by nature "بفتنه[OCR?] جوئی و شورش طلبی مفطور". Qutb al-Din Khan, sent to govern Bengal, was told to keep him if he stayed loyal and otherwise send him to court, and "سزا[OCR?] رساند" if he would not come (p. 142).
  - At "بردوان", where Sher Afgan held a jagir, Sher Afgan came out to meet the governor. The governor's men crowded round him.
  - He drew his sword and struck Qutb al-Din in the belly, and Qutb al-Din died on the spot.
  - Qutb al-Din's men then killed Sher Afgan "بقصاص خون آقائے خویش" (p. 142).
- **Ghiyas Beg's family.** Ghiyas Beg was "الملقب[OCR?] باعتمادالدوله", son of "خواجه محمد شریف طهرانی" (p. 142).
  - The Khwaja had been diwan-begi to "محمد خان تکلو حاکم هرات". That Muhammad Khan served Humayun when Humayun went to Iraq, and Shah Tahmasp's farman on the hospitality, "که در اکبر نامه داخل شده", is addressed to him. The sentence on the Khwaja's later career breaks at two `[illegible]` gaps (p. 142).
  - After the Khwaja's death his sons Ghiyas Beg and Khwaja Tahir came to Hindustan. A second daughter was born at Qandahar. At "فتح پور سیکری" they entered the service of "حضرت بادشاه جنت آرامگاه", and Ghiyas Beg became "دیوان بیوتات" (p. 141).
  - The Qandahar-born daughter was married to Sher Afgan. After his death the officials of suba Bengal sent her to court, as ordered (p. 141).
- **Nur Jahan.** "در سال ششم[OCR?] جلوس والا داخل حرم سرائے گردید نخستین نور محل خطاب یافت و بعد آن به نور جهان مخاطب گشت" (p. 141).
  - The book praises her beauty, wisdom and generosity and quotes two of her verses, on a comet ("ستاره ذوذنب") and on the Eid moon (p. 140).
  - "تمامی امور سلطنت و مهام مملکت بقبضه اقتدار و کف اختیار نور جهان بیگم درآمد": all affairs of state passed into her control.
  - The emperor said "من سلطنت را به نور جهان بیگم ارزانی داشتم غیر از یک سیر[OCR?] شراب و نیم سیر[OCR?] گوشت مرا هیچ نمی[OCR?] باید".
  - She sat in the jharokha, and the amirs came to her for kornish and obeyed her orders (pp. 140–139).

**Khan Alam's embassy to Iran (pp. 139–138)**
- He returned "در سال دهم[OCR?] جلوس والا" with Zanbil Beg, the Shah's envoy. The Shah's favour to him was such that a full account "محمول بر اغراق می شود". The Shah visited his lodging, and on his departure came by night to make excuses. His mansab was raised (pp. 139–138).

**Gujarat, i.e. Ahmadabad (pp. 138–137; outside Pakistan, no row)**
- The emperor went "بسیر و شکار گجرات" "و سال دوازدهم[OCR?] جلوس والا" and halted at Ahmadabad. The climate disagreed with him, but "دریائے شور", "سے[OCR?] کروه[OCR?]" from Ahmadabad, lifted his spirits (p. 138).
- Khair al-Nisa Begam, daughter of the Khankhanan, hosted him in "باغ خانخانان متصل گجرات". It was autumn, so she had artisans make leaves and flowers of coloured paper and fruit of wax. The emperor reached out to pick them before he realised. He praised her and increased her jagir and reward (pp. 138–137).

**Birth of Aurangzeb (pp. 137–136)**
- Sultan Khurram had sons by "ممتاز بانو بیگم بنت آصف خان":
  - Dara Shikoh, born "نوزدهم[OCR?] صفر سال دهم[OCR?] جلوس والا";
  - Shuja, born "چهارم[OCR?] جمادی الاول سال یازدهم[OCR?]".
- Muhammad Aurangzeb was born "هنگام مراجعت از گجرات در مقام حوالی موضع دهود[OCR?]" (p. 137). For the date as printed, see Doubts.
- Celebrations, a horoscope cast by astrologers, and a chronogram "از لفظ آفتاب عالمتاب" (pp. 137–136).

**Jahangirabad, "در پنجاب" (pp. 136–135; no row)**
- The village "ششینو[OCR?] پور" near "ساهوملی[OCR?]" had some buildings, and its surroundings were made a hunting ground (pp. 136–135).
- "درینولا" it was made a separate pargana named Jahangirabad, and villages were added "از پرگنات چهار[OCR?] دهات". It was given in jagir to "سکندر سلے[OCR?] قراول", who by order laid foundations for a palace, a large tank and a tall minar (p. 135).
- After him ("سکندر لی[OCR?]") it was given in jagir to Amanat Khan, who took charge of finishing the building. "یک لک[OCR?] و پنجاه هزار[OCR?] روپیه" were spent (p. 135).

**Wonders at court (pp. 133–129)**
- **Births at Akbarabad.** A woman in Akbarabad bore triplet girls, then a boy and two girls, all living. A goldsmith's wife gave birth after "دوازده[OCR?] ماه", "هیزده[OCR?] ماه" and "دوسال[OCR?]" of pregnancy (p. 133).
- **The bearded woman.** A gardener's daughter with a thick beard was examined in private by order and found to be wholly a woman (p. 133).
- **The lions.** Lions were released under the jharokha and "در باغ جهروکه[OCR?]", and bred there (pp. 133–132).
- **Hakim Ali's underwater room.** Hakim Ali built a room under the water of a tank in his house, kept dry by a talisman. The emperor went inside, and Hakim Ali was raised to "دوهزاری[OCR?]" (p. 132).
- **Lightning-iron at Jalandhar.** Lightning struck "در دیهی از دیهات جالندهر تعلقه پنجاب" and scorched a "دوازده[OCR?] درعه[OCR?]" plot. "محمد سعید حکیم جالندهر" had it dug, and at "پنج و شش[OCR?] درعه[OCR?]" a red-hot piece of iron came out (p. 132).
  - The iron was sent to court. "استاد داؤد آهنگر" mixed three parts lightning-iron with one part other iron and made two swords, a dagger and a knife (p. 132).
- **The Bengal jugglers (pp. 131–129).** Their tricks included:
  - seeds that grew at once into fruiting trees, whose fruit the company ate, then sank back into the earth (p. 131);
  - a mirror that lit the night, reportedly seen from ten days' journey away (p. 130);
  - seven men singing with closed mouths; arrows hung and lit in the air, and arrows joined in mid-air (p. 130);
  - a pot that boiled without fire and fed about a hundred platters; a fountain on dry ground (p. 130);
  - human towers of sixty and forty men; a man cut apart and restored behind a curtain (p. 129);
  - the rope trick: a man climbs to fight enemies in the sky and his limbs fall; his wife burns herself with them; he comes down alive, and she reappears alive (p. 129);
  - fighting cocks drawn from an empty bag (p. 129).
  - The author remarks "این قدر قوت از حد قیاس بشری افزونست" (p. 129).

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **A saint's prayer and a royal birth.** The emperor's birth is credited to "دعائے" Shaikh Salim, "مستجاب الدعوات". The emperor's popular name came from the shaikh's name, and a village was named from it, later the pargana of Jahangirabad (pp. 136–135).
- **A dervish circle.** A murshid with five dervishes prayed together after the evening prayer. The murshid shared the gold that fell among "درویشان حواشی خود" and gave half to the emperor (p. 132). The court's test of the wonder named three categories: "کشف و کرامات", "سحر و جادو", and "تسخیر اجنه" (p. 131).
- **Tobacco (pp. 135–133).**
  - It came "از جزایر فرنگ". "حکما و اطبا" used it in medicines and prescribed the smoke for some illnesses (p. 135).
  - At first it was little imported. Later farmers across Hind and Hindustan grew it "عالم عالم[OCR?]" and "حاصلات آن را بر اجناس دیگر تفوق می بخشیدند[OCR?]" (p. 135).
  - Its price rose ("نرخش[OCR?] گران تر"). All ranks smoked, including "صلحا و زهاد" and "فقرا", and it became the best thing to offer a guest (p. 135).
  - The emperor forbade it (p. 134). Farmans went to governors against its smoking and sale, and offenders were paraded in Lahore and some had their lips cut (p. 133).
- **Roads and buildings.**
  - The Akbarabad–Lahore highway got kos minars, wells and tree rows "در سال چهاردهم[OCR?] جلوس والا". The tree rows are credited to "شیر خان افغان" as their inventor (p. 136).
  - Jahangirabad: pargana, jagir, palace, tank and minar, at a cost of "یک لک[OCR?] و پنجاه هزار[OCR?] روپیه" (p. 135).
  - The Lahore palace cost "هشت لک[OCR?] روپیه" (p. 135).
- **Nur Jahan's authority (p. 139).**
  - Farmans to the amirs carried the tughra "حکم علیه عالیه مهد علیا نور جهان بیگم بادشاه". The seal verse was "نور جهان گشت بحکم الله / همدم و همراز جهانگیر شاه".
  - "اگرچه خطبه بنام بیگم نبود اما سکه باین نقش منقش گردید": the khutba was not read in her name, but coins were struck with the verse "بحکم شاه جهانگیر یافت صد زیور / بنام نور جهان بادشاه بیگم زر".
- **Offices and grants.**
  - I'timad al-Daula received "بمنصب وکالت کل". Abu'l-Hasan, her elder brother, received the title I'tiqad Khan and the post of mir saman, and later the title Asaf Khan. Slaves and eunuchs received the titles khan and tarkhan (p. 139).
  - Ghiyas Beg had been "دیوان بیوتات" (p. 141). Hakim Ali was raised to "دوهزاری[OCR?]" (p. 132).
  - Sher Afgan held a jagir in suba Bengal (p. 142). Khair al-Nisa Begam's jagir was increased (p. 137).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The unnamed dervish of Mathura** (pp. 132–131):
  - gold, "قریب هفتصد[OCR?] اشرفی", rained from the air in answer to his and his five disciples' supplication;
  - his promise that the half kept in the treasury "گاهے کمی نخواهد شد";
  - his knowledge of the emperor's unspoken regret about the hand-kiss.
  - The book reports the split opinion ("بعضے ... خوارق[OCR?] بیان میکردند و بعضے بسحر[OCR?] و جادو نسبت میدادند") and the final finding that it came "از نتایج و فور عبادات و حصول کمالات".

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **On Sher Afgan's death.** After praising the widow's beauty, the author writes "درین صورت عجب نیست که شیر افگن بموجب امر والا بقتل رسیده باشد": it is no wonder that Sher Afgan was killed by royal order (p. 141). Yet the narrative on p. 142 has him killed by Qutb al-Din Khan's men in revenge.
- **On Ghiyas Beg.** "در کار کشائے اهل حاجات و رشوت گیری[OCR?] بے نظیر بود" (p. 141).
- **On Nur Jahan's power.** "رفته رفته کار بجائے رسید که از بادشاه جز نامی نماند" (p. 140).
- **On Khan Alam.** "ایلچی گری که او کرده دیگرے نکرده باشد در ایران اهل هند را نیک نام کرده آمد" (p. 138).
- **On tobacco.** The author writes a mock eulogy of tobacco, then turns: "استغفر الله چه می گویم و چه می نویسم". He lists its harms to body and soul (p. 134) and notes that punishment did not deter people (p. 133).
- **On the highway trees.** "اگرچه رسته درختان در شاهراه اختراع شیر خان افغان است اما در عهد دولت حضرت خاقان زمان رواجے تازه یافت" (p. 136).

**The rulers**

**Lahore (place-level; weigh accordingly)**

  - punishments carried out "در شهر لاهور" (p. 133);
  - the palace in "دارالسلطنت لاهور" (p. 135);
  - the highway, where Lahore is only the terminus of a road (p. 136).
