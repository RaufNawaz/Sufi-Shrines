# khulasat_ut_tawarikh — chunk 015 — pp. 183–195

The book reads from the highest page down. In that order the chunk opens mid-narrative in the reign of the ruler the book calls "خاقان زمان" / "حضرت خاقانی". First come the affairs of Badakhshan and Kabul (p. 195): Sulaiman Mirza and Shahrukh Mirza are driven out of Badakhshan, a pretender Muhammad Zaman rises, and Qasim Khan, governor of Kabul, is killed. Then Abdullah Khan of Turan sends a letter of apology through "مولانا ئے حسینی", who dies at court (p. 194). The section "در بیان تسخیر ولایت دلکشائے کشمیر جنت تاثیر" (pp. 193–194) narrates the expedition against Yusuf Khan of Kashmir and his son Yaqub Khan by way of Pakhli, the submission of the Kashmiris, and the assignment of saffron, silk and hunting birds to the imperial establishment. A long digression follows on the earlier history of Kashmir "از بعضے تواریخ" (pp. 186–193): the rajas, Shah Mir's rise and the coming of Islam, then a list of sultans with reign lengths from Shams al-Din to Yusuf Shah. It includes Sikandar "بت شکن" and the Mahadev temple, Zain al-Abidin and the jogis, the Chak rulers, Mirza Haidar, and the scholar "مولانا ئے کمال" who settled at Sialkot (p. 187). The narrative returns to Qasim Khan's campaign, the annexation in "۹۹۳[OCR?]", and the governorship of Mirza Yusuf Khan (pp. 185–186). The chunk ends with the visit to Kashmir in regnal year thirty-four: the Pir Panjal road, a set-piece description of Kashmir in prose and verse, the Bahat river and the Dal lake with its floating gardens, and the Kashmiris' way of life. It ends mid-sentence at "و پوشش" (p. 183).

  - **Maulana Kamal at Sialkot.** In the reign of Husain Khan (Chak) of Kashmir, "افضل الفضلا و اکمل العلما مولانا ئے کمال که در ان زمان از فضیلت درویشی مشهور [illegible] الاکناف بود" left Kashmir, "در سیالکوت رسیده بدرس مقید گشت". That is, the most excellent of scholars, famed in his time for the virtue of dervishhood, came to Sialkot and devoted himself to teaching (p. 187).
  - There he became famous "در هندوستان[OCR?] بفضایل[OCR?] کمالات خداشناسی و ایزد پرستی": for the virtues and perfections of knowing and worshipping God (p. 187).
  - No tomb, date of death or silsila is given for him (not stated).
  - **Shahi Khan at Sialkot (passing).** In the reign of Sultan Ali Shah of Kashmir, his brother Shahi Khan (later Sultan Zain al-Abidin) "از کشمیر برامده بسیالکوت رسید". After reaching Sialkot he joined "جسرته[OCR?] کهوکهر[OCR?]", who had escaped Timur's custody "در پنجاب", and with him marched against Ali Shah (p. 191). Nothing further is said about the town.

## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

**Holy men, legends and religious sites (Kashmir and elsewhere, no rows)**
- **"مولانا ئے حسینی"**, Abdullah Khan of Turan's envoy. He carried a letter of apology for his master's son's "بے ادبی", and "بعد رسیدن بدرگاه ... بابتلائے[OCR?] امتلا درگذشت" (died of a surfeit / repletion after reaching the court). The emperor answered the letter all the same (p. 194).
- **The Mahadev temple "در نزدیکی کشمیر".** Sultan Sikandar "بت شکن" pulled it down. A tablet came out of it with Hindi writing saying that after "یکهزار و یکصد سال" one named Sikandar would destroy the temple. The sultan regretted it greatly, wishing the tablet had been on the door so that he would not have destroyed it "و قول منجمان باطل نشدی" (p. 192). See Legends.
- **Sikandar's policy.** He "اکثر برهمنان را بعد انداختن بتخانها[OCR?] یا بزور[OCR?] مسلمان کرد", which is why he was called "بت شکن" (p. 192). Under Zain al-Abidin the exiled Brahmans returned "و در معابد و مقامات خویش قرار گرفتند". Those converted by force reverted, and "قشقه کشیدن و زنان سوختن و بت پرستیدن" were revived (p. 191).
- **The jogis and Zain al-Abidin.** The sultan "جماعت جوگیان را احترام بسیار می کرد و در اکتساب علوم انها[OCR?] میکوشید". That, says the book, is why he favoured the Hindus (p. 191). See Legends for the soul-transfer story (pp. 190–191).
- **The tank on the "کپرتل[OCR?]" route,** "ساخته حکماے پیشین", where a drum or trumpet call brings great snow and rain (p. 186). See Legends.
- **"پیر پنجال[OCR?]" and "رتن پنجال[OCR?]"**: mountains on the Kashmir road, not saints (p. 185).
- **Islam in Kashmir.** The book says it was divinely willed "که ازان ولایت حکومت هنود منقطع گردد و دین اسلام رواج یابد" (p. 193). Under Shah Mir, crowned as Sultan Shams al-Din, "از ابتدائے [illegible] رواج اسلام گردید" (p. 192). Queen "کوتا دیوی" / "کونتا دیوی" accepted Islam and married Shah Mir (p. 193).

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Revenue of Kashmir.** After the conquest, "بالکل زعفران و ابریشم و جانوران شکاری که خلاصه محصول آن ولایت است در سرکار والا مقرر گردید": all the saffron, silk and hunting animals, "the essence of that country's produce", were assigned to the imperial establishment (p. 193).
- **Sovereignty marks.** The book records "سکه و خطبه" changing hands repeatedly: under Mirza Haidar, then Ali Shah, then Qasim Khan in Srinagar, then the conquest (pp. 186, 187, 188, 193).
- **Marriage tribute.** Husain Khan sent his daughter "با تحف و هدایای لایقه" to the emperor (p. 187). Ali Shah sent his daughter to "شاهزاده سلیم" (p. 187).
- **Grant.** Muhammad Hakim Mirza gave Sulaiman Mirza "چند مواضع از تومان لمغان بسیورغال" (p. 195).
- **Road-building.** For the year-34 visit "چندین هزار خارا تراش آهنین بازو و سنجار[OCR?] قوی دست" cut stones and cleared trees to make the road (p. 184).
- **The Dal lake gardens.** Kashmiris lay large timbers on the water, bind them with ropes, throw earth on them and plant gardens. They make enough land this way to farm. "بعضے عیار پیشه همچنین زمین را دزدیده می برند": tricksters steal such land, and the saying about stealing a garden and land comes from here (p. 183).
- **Kashmiri food.** "خورش دائمی[OCR?] انها خشکه نرم[OCR?] بے نمک از برنج است": their usual food is soft, unsalted cooked rice. Eating it hot is not the custom; they keep rice cooked at night "درون[OCR?] دیگے[OCR?]" and eat it (p. 183).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The tablet in the Mahadev temple.** It foretold its destruction by one named Sikandar after "یکهزار و یکصد سال" (p. 192).
- **Headless bodies.** In the battle of Ali Shah and Shahi Khan, "گویند که چند قالب بے سر در ان جنگ گاه بر خاسته بحرکت در آمد". The book adds, as "قول اهل هند", that when ten thousand are killed one headless body moves (p. 191). The same is said of the fighting during the Kashghar invasion of "سنه ۹۳۹[OCR?]" (p. 188).
- **The jogi who took the sultan's body.** Zain al-Abidin fell ill and was near death. A jogi came to his bedside (p. 191), and when the sultan's soul departed, "جوگی بعلم خلع بدن که میدانست" put his own soul into the sultan's body and rose. The courtiers saw the sultan well and the jogi dead, and the jogi's disciple kept his body. The sultan's body, with the jogi's soul in it, went on ruling, "و ازین جهت[OCR?] رعایت دین هندوان کرده" (p. 190).
- **The tank of the ancient sages.** On the "کپرتل[OCR?]" road, a drum sounded at the tank brings snow and rain. When Qasim Khan's army sounded its drum, snow, rain and hail fell, the cold struck the troops, and many animals died (p. 186).
- **Omen of the child sultan.** Sultan Muhammad Shah, aged seven at accession, ignored gold, silver, arms and cloth set before him and took up a bow. Those present read this as a sign of his greatness and manliness (p. 190).
- **Kashmir's air.** Perhaps "مسیحا[OCR?]" was raised in that land, to revive the dead by his miracle, and Khizr nourished in its water and air, so that endless life ("عمر ابد[OCR?]") is his lot (p. 184). This is a rhetorical figure, not a claim.

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **The author's sources.** He says the history of Kashmir comes "از بعضے تواریخ" (p. 193). The editor's footnotes check the reign lengths against "تاریخ فرشته جلد دوم" and "طبقات اکبری" and give variants (pp. 188–193), for example "راجه سیه دیو" / "راجه سری دیو" for Sahdev and "کوکتا[OCR?] دیوی" for Kota Devi (p. 193).
- **On the Kashmiris.** For all of Kashmir's beauty, "کشمیریان بد معاش زبون زیست هستند" (p. 183). Some of them practise "فریب و تزویر جبلی" (p. 188).
- **On Sikandar.** "سلطان خیلی متعصب بود" (p. 192).
- **On Zain al-Abidin.** He was "عدالت ورز و انصاف[OCR?] دوست", and army and subjects were content with him (p. 191).
- **On Abd al-Momin's request.** When his insolent letter sank with the envoy's boat, people said it was perhaps by the emperor's own sign ("باشاره انحضرت"), "و عجب نیست که چنین بوقوع آید" (p. 195).

## Doubts

**Reading order and edges**
- The chunk runs from p. 195 down to p. 183. It opens mid-narrative with "تعیین کرد و ولایت بدخشان از تصرف ایشان بر آورده" (p. 195). It ends mid-sentence at "و پوشش" (p. 183), which presumably continues in the next chunk. All page joins read through.
- A library stamp at the foot of p. 187 is not text.
- **The ruler is never named in the chunk.** He is "خاقان زمان", "حضرت خاقانی" or "آن حضرت". Calling him Akbar rests on the brief's map of the book, not on this chunk.

**Sialkot (place-level, weigh accordingly)**
- The five Sialkot ids are attached place-level by toponym_map's "sialkot". The book names none of the rows: no Imam Ali-ul-Haq, no temple, no gurdwara.
- The Maulana Kamal material is about a scholar-dervish who settled and taught there, which is the only thing the book says about life at Sialkot. Shahi Khan's arrival is a bare "reached Sialkot" and is kept as a passing sub-bullet only.
- "مشهور [illegible] الاکناف" has a gap, which is not filled. "هندوستان[OCR?]" and "بفضایل[OCR?]" are flagged.
- Maulana Kamal is not identified further by the book. No row has him as principal figure.

**Lahore not mapped**
- Lahore appears three times, all in passing: Humayun's arrival after defeat (p. 188), Fath Shah dying "بطرف لاهور" (p. 190), and the 97-kos distance to Kashmir (p. 184). None says anything about the city itself, so per wave-1 point 3 they stay under Other with no ids.
- A consolidator may judge the 97-kos road measurement worth a place-level line.

**Hasan Abdal**
- The coordinator flagged Hasan Abdal for this chunk, but "حسن ابدال" does not occur on pp. 183–195 as transcribed. Nothing is mapped to gurdwara-panja-sahib.

**Province bullet (province-level, weigh accordingly)**
- "پنجاب" is the book's word, as a governorship ("حاکم پنجاب") and as a region. These are not the modern province's boundaries; the book does not define the unit.
- Jammu, Rajauri, Bhimber, Pakhli, Kishtwar and Tibet are not assigned to any province. The book does not place them in Punjab.

**Traps avoided**
- "پیر پنجال" is a mountain (p. 185), not a pir.
- The Mahadev temple is a deity at a particular non-archive site "در نزدیکی کشمیر", so per wave-1 point 1 it takes none of Shiva's 8 rows. Sharada is not named, so sharada-peeth is not attached.
- "شمس" in "شمس چک" and "سلطان شمس الدین" is a ruler's name, not the Multan Shams.
- "مسیحا" and "خضر" (p. 184) are poetic figures.
- "کمال" in "مولانا ئے کمال" is treated as a name because the book gives it as his name; it is not shrine-of-baba-shah-kamal.
