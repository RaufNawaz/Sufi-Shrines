## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Shattari contemplative method as the precondition of the vision.** Kugle ties the ascension to a
  named technique: "this was precisely the goal of the Shattari Sufi method of contemplation, which
  presumably brought the young Muhammad Ghawth to the threshold of this experience" (p. 162, folio
  141). The goal is glossed in the same sentence as to "delve into one's own nature, observing its
  components with illuminating clarity, comprehending them layer after layer, and transcending them
  until arriving at one's own refined essence" (p. 162, folio 141).
- **Retreat and hermitage.** Khidr says "I came a few times to your hermitage, but I never found room
  there" (p. 163, folio 142), and the hermitage is placed "along the banks of the Ganges" (p. 163,
  folio 142). Kugle: "Muhammad Ghawth evidently learned of this greatest name of God during his
  meditation retreat and ascetic introspection, which fueled his leap of imagination." (p. 166,
  folio 145). **The retreat is given no duration, date or building: (not stated).**
- **Order competition for imperial patronage.** "The Shattari order in the time of Muhammad Ghawth
  was in competition with the Naqshbandi order for influence in the new Mughal empire, for the
  founding Mughal, Babur, had been affiliated with Naqshbandi masters before coming to South Asia.
  Therefore, it is of significance that in Ghawth's vision Ali and Abu Bakr appear together."
  (p. 164, folio 143). This is the chunk's one institutional-political argument and its evidence is
  the joint appearance of the two figures in the vision.
- **Naqshbandi lineage claim.** Reported as doctrine, not as this book's own: Ali is "the anchor of
  all Sufi lineages (except the Naqshbandi lineage with its lineage through Abu Bakr and its
  anti-`Shfi[text-layer?]` [Shīʿī] stance)" (p. 164, folio 143).
- **Muhammad Ghawth's writings.** One book is named with its title and its contents: "In his book
  'Key to the Treasuries' (Kalid al-Makhazin), Muhammad Ghawth drew a diagram of concentric circles
  that graphs how Ahmad emanated from Ahad and how Muhammad then emanated from Ahmad, while through
  Muhammad the whole universe come into being." (p. 177, folio 156, note 33). **No date, place of
  composition, language or manuscript for `Kalid al-Makhazin` is given: (not stated)**, and **the
  diagram itself is not reproduced in this chunk** — the sentence describes it, no figure appears.
- **Teaching practice and a transmitted maxim.** "While teaching his disciples, he had often used
  this saying, 'Your very being is a sin incomparable to any other sin.' His followers among the
  Shattaris were fond of quoting a similar saying, 'The very existence of the worshiper is a veil
  obstructing witness of the One worshiped.'" (p. 178, folio 157, note 34). The only statement in
  the chunk about a living circle of followers; **no khanqah, no place, no numbers: (not stated).**
- **South Asian devotional singing.** A verse is cited as a living genre, unattributed to any poet:
  "As a verse in South Asian Sufi devotional singing phrases it, 'In the garden of God, Muhammad is
  the rose, while Ali is the redness and Hussayn is the fragrance.'" (p. 169, folio 148). **No poet,
  language, region or shrine is named: (not stated).** This is the closest the chunk comes to shrine
  practice and it is not tied to any site.
- **Manuscript provenance of the two plates.** Both miniatures are located in one collection and
  manuscript: "(British Library: India Office Islamic Collection, 3243), `fol,` `io6b[text-layer?]`
  [fol. 106b]" (p. 168, folio 147) and "(British Library: India Office Islamic Collection, 3243),
  `fols. loob-ioia[text-layer?]` [fols. 100b–101a]" (p. 176, folio 155). Both "Painted in India,
  nineteenth century".
- **The plate pages themselves.** pp. 167 and 175 (folios not stated) carry no extractable label,

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- The ascension itself, narrated by Muhammad Ghawth in the first person and presented by Kugle as a
  quoted "Ascension Narrative": the elements of earth, water and air cooperate, and the figure of
  fire, after hesitation, "not only cooperates but is also redeemed by bowing low to Muhammad
  Ghawth" (p. 162, folio 141).
- Placing his foot on the eye of the figure of fire — "He lowered his head further in sorrow, and I
  placed my foot upon his eye and ascended" (p. 163, folio 142) — which Kugle reads through the
  Persian idiom "to place another's foot on your head or eyes (qadam dar sar nihadan)", an
  expression of humility (p. 162, folio 141).
- Recognition of Khidr, whom he had met before at his hermitage but had been unable to see, Khidr
  having had no order "to appear to you in visible form back then" (p. 163, folio 142).
- Angels of the second sphere greeting him: "O great Ghawth! It's been several thousand years since
  we've been seeing, reflected in the divine omnipotence, that you would be passing by this route."
  (pp. 163–164, folios 142–143).
- **The release of Harut and Marut** at his unspoken request: "These two imprisoned angels will
  suffer suspension no more, nor will they continue to be punished with suffocating smoke; they are
  released from now until the Day of Judgment." (p. 164, folio 143). Attributed to "a voice" that
  answers his thought before he speaks it.
- The Persian queen taught the greatest name of God, who "uttered it and ascended to heaven, and God
  transformed her into a star" — given by Kugle as folk-tale material, note 26 (p. 166, folio 145).
- Enoch's intercession for the two angels refused, proved by his inability to see them after praying
  (p. 166, folio 145), the episode ascribed to the "Wonders of Creation" and closing "God alone
  knows their real state!" (p. 168, folio 147, note 27).
- Heaven and hell seen in one place: "I saw one form with two different inherent essences… 'Here the
  essences of heaven and hell are brought into existence in one single place.'" (p. 170, folio 149).
- A hole bored from the fifth sphere to the grave of the forgiven: "From this sphere down to the
  grave of that person, God bores a hole so that they can each gaze up and catch a glimpse of what
  heaven will be like." (p. 170, folio 149).
- Speaking animal-shaped spirits perching on his hands and feet, who declare "we discovered that
  Muhammad Ghawth would be passing along this route. We wanted to see you!" (pp. 170–171, folios
  149–150).
- The place no angel or spirit has entered, opened to him by a voice: he witnesses "the spirits of
  several hundred thousand saints who were not connected to any bodily form", among whom "there were
  seven saints who were so lofty in their rank that no other saints could ever compare with them"
  and of whom two "desired to benefit from my spiritual emanations" (p. 171, folio 150).
- The registers of Might carried by angels of the sixth sphere, "which will consume the people
  consigned to the flames… they record all the `peoples deeds`" (p. 171, folio 150).
- Eve teaching the meaning of divine unity in a chamber "formed from the purest substance of divine
  wrath" (p. 171, folio 150), and her doctrine of the women of hell as "the People of divine Might"
  and "the messengers who will be sent to the people punished in hell fire" (p. 172, folio 151).
- The Prophet Muhammad at the Throne: "In my community, there are seven people who have arrived to
  this point beyond all points." (p. 176, folio 155).
- Ghawth blasted unconscious by the sight of Azra'il and revived (p. 176, folio 155).
- The divine summons punning on the Prophet's name: "You who are Ahmad without the
  `mlm[text-layer?]` [mīm], come up to the divine Throne!" (p. 177, folio 156).
- The angels' protest at the final stage: "There is a sin incomparable to any other sin that
  accompanies this young man unawares!" (p. 178, folio 157), followed by the effacement of ego and
  the verses `You see them enter into the true relationship with God (Q 110:2)` and `God is with you
  all wherever you may be` `(Q5?:4)[text-layer?]` (p. 178, folio 157; **the second citation's
  numerals are destroyed and no reading is offered**).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Ascent is inward, not merely upward.** "These are elemental spheres that not only mark the
  atmospheres between the surface of earth and the seven heavens but also represent the four elements
  that compose the human body. Therefore ascending upward is also delving inward, through bodily
  materiality and into the spiritual potency of the human heart." (p. 162, folio 141). This is the
  chapter's controlling claim in the chunk, and it is Kugle's own.
- **The ascension inverts Adam's creation.** Adam was made from earth mixed with water, given spirit,
  kindled into motion, confronted the fiery ego of Iblis and "descended into the routine world";
  "Muhammad Ghawth was reversing this process." (p. 162, folio 141).
- **Ghawth's hesitation before fire is read psychologically, and Kugle marks the reading as
  conjecture.** "Perhaps Muhammad Ghawth hesitated to confront the figure of fire, fearing that he
  was Iblis or one of his minions. Or perhaps, in a more subtle insight, he hesitated before
  confronting the image of his own ego" (p. 162, folio 141). Both readings are offered as "perhaps".
- **Sectarian politics read out of a visionary detail.** The joint appearance of Ali and Abu Bakr is
  argued to be significant against the Shattari–Naqshbandi contest for Mughal influence (p. 164,
  folio 143). The evidence is the vision's own casting, not any external document cited here.
- **Harut and Marut are not a digression but the chapter's hinge.** "What seems like a strange
  diversion in the Ascension Narrative is actually a key element, for the two angels' story helps
  explain the perils of embodiment and reveals the means for human beings to `ascend!`" (p. 165,
  folio 144). The mechanism is specified: "Embedded in the story of Harut and Marut is a crucial
  detail: not only did they teach human beings harmful sorcery, but they also taught them the
  beneficial 'greatest name of God' (`ism allah al-aczam[text-layer?]` [ism allāh al-aʿẓam])"
  (p. 166, folio 145) — "The angels' descent provided the means for Muhammad Ghawth's ascent, by
  means of the secret name of God. His rise could free them from the consequences of their fall"
  (p. 166, folio 145).
- **Embodiment generates passion through physical heat.** "Their story illustrates the power of
  embodiment in the flesh, which generates an internal heat through consumption and digestion, heat
  that drives the passions and lustful urges." (p. 165, folio 144). "They present an allegory of
  Gnostic imagination in which the divine spirit is imprisoned in the human flesh." (pp. 165–166,
  folios 144–145).
- **Why he hears the angels but does not see them.** Kugle derives this from the Enoch precedent:
  "Following on the footsteps of Enoch in ascending, Muhammad Ghawth hears their plea. This explains
  why he hears them but does not see them, unlike so many of `this other encounters[text-layer?]`
  [his other encounters] with spiritual beings that are intensely visual." (p. 168, folio 147).
- **Harut and Marut as the inverse of the rainbow.** In the "Wonders of Creation" hierarchy, "the
  story of Harut and Marut plays a special role, for they are celestial beings trapped in the dark
  fastness underground. They are the reverse image of the rainbow, which bridges the distance from
  earth to the heavens." (p. 168, folio 147). The treatise's structure is described as running from
  celestial phenomena (`'uluwiyat]`) down to terrestrial things (`safaliyat`) and "the different
  parts of human anatomy" (p. 168, folio 147).
- **Potential to kinetic energy.** "he turns the potential energy of Adam's descent into the kinetic
  energy of becoming the archetypal man, the complete human being
  (`al-iman al-kamity[text-layer?]` [al-iman al-kamil)])" (p. 168, folio 147), with the term
  repeated as "Muslims expressed these images as al-iman al-kamil, the complete human being"
  (p. 168, folio 147). Kugle traces the microcosm–macrocosm image to "Stoic philosophy as well as
  the Vedic myth of Purusha" (p. 168, folio 147).
- **Only the Prophet actualizes the complete human being.** "The human being alone has the potential
  to reflect all God's names and qualities and thereby unify them; however, the Prophet Muhammad
  alone, as the complete human being, actualizes this potential. Those who follow him among Muslim
  saints can, in harmony with his primordial example, also fulfill their potential." (p. 169, folio
  148).
- **Heaven and hell as two alienations.** "This vision confirms the intuition of many Sufis that both
  hell and heaven represent states of alienation from God: one is alienation through divine wrath,
  the other alienation through divine beauty. They appear to be distinct in the discourse of the
  prophets and their scriptures that warn about one and promise the other, yet in reality this
  appearance deceives." (p. 170, folio 149).
- **Mercy inside wrath.** "Muhammad Ghawth's vision confirms the intuition of many Sufis, that within
  the wrath of punishment lays a hidden mercy that will eventually unfold from within it and
  overwhelm what seems on the surface like wrath; they took very seriously a
  `hadith qudsl[text-layer?]` [hadith qudsī] in which God is reported to say, 'My mercy surpasses my
  wrath.'" (p. 172, folio 151), with the Ibn Arabi passage at note 30 as support.
- **The vision as a method of escaping literalist controversy over misogynist hadith.** "However,
  Muhammad Ghawth turns that image on its head! In his vision, the women in hell are the disciples
  of Eve and are manifestations of God's might in the form of wrath." (p. 172, folio 151);
  "`Manyjurists[text-layer?]` [Many jurists] and ascetics took such reports at face value and claimed
  that women by nature veered toward rebelliousness and caused social discord." (p. 172, folio 151);
  and the conclusion, "Muhammad Ghawth did not have to struggle with affirming or denying the outward
  words of reports like this, for his vision allowed him to grasp their inner meaning, which might be
  completely contradictory to an outer, literalist reading. Why argue with literalist scholars and
  hadith experts over the status of Muslim women when he can call upon Eve, the mother of believers?"
  (p. 173, folio 152). Kugle contrasts this route explicitly with Riffat Hassan's (p. 173, folio 152).
- **The vision of the divine names is wahdat al-wujud.** "This vision of the divine names as
  archetypes expresses the cosmology known as wahdat al-wujud, or 'the unity of being.' From a
  creaturely point of view, God created the world. But from a mystical point of view, there is no
  being except God's being." (pp. 174–176, folios 153–155) — "In that they reflect God, all phenomena
  are divine, but in that they are material they pass away into nonbeing. The human being is the most
  complete manifestation of the divine… selected to be God's vice-regent in the world." (p. 176,
  folio 155).
- **The Ahmad–Ahad pun as a doctrine of continuity.** "With this subtle pun, the divine voice denotes
  the continuity between a person, the soul, and the divine source. If Muhammad without one m is
  Ahmad, then Ahmad without another m is Ahad, hinting at continuity between the Prophet and the name
  of God, through which the universe was created." (p. 177, folio 156, note 32).
- **The grammatical climax: selflessness registered in pronouns.** "At this point, the narrative
  signifies selflessness in the eclipse of the first-person subject 'I,' replaced by the third-person
  pronoun 'he.'" — and, because Persian has no gendered or animate pronoun distinction, "'He' could
  be either figure: the gazer or the reflection of that gaze in the mirror. This deep ambiguity is a
  special feature of the Persian language and accounts for its poetic force." (p. 178, folio 157).
  Kugle treats this as evidence internal to the text's language, and it is the chunk's closing move.

## Doubts

**Damage inventory (text_layer route — the source carries no transcriber flags at all; every mark
above is this reader's, on scan-derived extraction).** Classes measured in pp. 162–178:

1. **Letter-for-digit in running headers.** `14!` [141] (p. 162), `I5O` [150] (p. 171), `15!` [151]
   (p. 172): `!`→1 and `I`/`O`→1/0, matching this book's measured substitution set. Letter-spacing
   (`B O D Y   R E F I N E D`) is header formatting, not content. The remaining twelve headers in
   the range extract as clean digits.
2. **Destroyed Qur'an citations — no reading offered.** `(Qyaa)` at p. 162 (folio 141) for the Iblis
   verse, and `(Q5?:4)` at p. 178 (folio 157) for "God is with you all wherever you may be". Both
   carry reported facts (which verse) and both are irrecoverable from the chunk; **neither is
   guessed.** `(Q 2:102)` (p. 165, folio 144) and `(Q 110:2)` (p. 178, folio 157) extract intact.
3. **Dropped ʿayn/hamza and lost macrons in transliteration.** `Shfa` [Shīʿa] twice and `Shfi`

   `hadith qudsl` [hadith qudsī] (p. 172, folio 151, against clean `hadith qudsi` at p. 177, folio
   156); `Qazwlnl` [Qazwīnī] (p. 168, folio 147).
4. **Superscript ʿayn extracted as `c`.** `ism allah al-aczam` [ism allāh al-aʿẓam] (p. 166, folio
   145); `cilm-i dim o cilm-i ladum` (p. 174, folio 153) — the first pair reads [ʿilm-i dīn], but
   **no reading is offered for `ladum`**, whose final syllables are destroyed. Kugle's English gloss
   in the same sentence is intact: "the archetypes of religious knowledge `andintuitive` knowledge".
5. **`b`→`h` and `h`→`H` inside titles**, the same substitution class reported at p. 65 of this
   book: `Makbluqat` [Makhluqat] (p. 168, folio 147) and `MakHuqat` [Makhluqat] (p. 176, folio 155),

7. **Commas for periods.** `ms,` for `ms.` and `fol,` for `fol.` in the p. 168 caption (folio 147),

**Internal contradictions and things a human must check.**

- **Kugle restates the vision's paradox with the terms swapped.** The narrative says "I saw one form
  with two different inherent essences" and "this form with two equal essences" (p. 170, folio 149),
  but Kugle's commentary on the same page renders it "'one essence with two equal forms.'" (p. 170,
  folio 149) — in quotation marks, as if quoting. **Form and essence are transposed between the
  quoted narrative and the commentary's quotation of it.** The contradiction is on one page and is
  not an extraction artefact (both readings are clean); it needs checking against the printed page
  before either phrasing is quoted in an entry.
- **Eve's authority-of-beauty speech and Kugle's gloss diverge in emphasis.** Eve says the women
  "each wish to raise people up from the fires of hell into the realm of pure divine might" (p. 172,
  folio 151); Kugle glosses this as "Through these women, men in hell might slowly work through their
  'punishment' and realize the true worship of God." (p. 172, folio 151). The narrative's agents are
  the women; the gloss's are the men. Recorded, not resolved.
- **A block quotation is printed twice.** "Hearing this explanation, I stood there stunned and amazed
  at my surroundings…" closes p. 173 (folio 152) and opens p. 174 (folio 153); likewise "Then with
  Abu Bakr and Ali, I began to ascend up to the limit of the third heaven." closes and reopens across
  pp. 164 (folio 143), and "They explained, 'Here the essences of heaven and hell…'" across p. 170
  (folio 149). These are the book's own overlapping re-quotations for continuity, **not** duplicated
  extraction; do not treat the repeats as separate statements.
- **Two pages genuinely print no running header: pp. 167 and 175 (folios not stated).** Both are
  unnumbered full-page plates, identified from the `opposite:` captions on pp. 168 and 176. Neither
  carries any extractable text. Reported as the task requires; **no folio is asserted for either.**
- **No page in pp. 162–178 printed a header contradicting `PDF − 21`.** All fifteen headers agree.
- **The plate images are not described.** Only their printed captions are reported. Anything about
  what the miniatures depict beyond the caption wording must come from the page, not from here.
