## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Initiation without a living shaikh.** Abd al-Haqq Rudawli "took initiation directly from the
  long-dead Shaykh Sabir in a visionary encounter and began to spread his teachings as the Sabiri
  branch of the Chishti community" (p. 261, folio 240) — the chunk's one initiation event, and it
  bypasses a living chain entirely. His motive for the graveyard years is given as dissatisfaction
  "that any living person could fully explicate the secrets of Sufi teaching" (pp. 262–263, folios
  241–242).
- **Multiple affiliation, and a dormant order kept on the books.** "We can observe this fluorescence
  of Islamic appropriations of Yoga in both the Sabiri-Chishti and the Shattari Sufi communities …
  The former community was Hajji `Imdadullahs[text-layer?]` [Imdadullah's] main allegiance, while the
  latter was a community into which he also had an initiation even if it was largely inactive in South
  Asia by the nineteenth century" (p. 264, folio 243). **A nineteenth-century shaikh holding a live
  initiation in an order with no functioning institutional presence — useful for the field map on how
  affiliation and institution come apart.**
- **Succession to a shaikh's office, twice named.** Abd al-Quddus was "the primary successor of Shaykh
  Abd al-Haqq Rudawli" (p. 264, folio 243); the hagiographer of the *Anwar al-ʿUyun* "was uniquely
  qualified to do so, since he was the shaykh's own grandson and major successor in the Sabiri-Chishti
  lineage" (p. 262, folio 241). **Descent and office coincide in one person** — the chunk states both
  facts of the same man without comment.
- **Residence at a tomb as apprenticeship.** "The author of these pages stayed at the devotional
  center and tomb of Shaykh Abd al-Haqq for a long time. I pursued intense spiritual exercises and
  long struggles, crying and wailing in trial, burning with hunger and thirst" (p. 262, folio 241) —
  the shrine functioning as a training institution. **The chunk gives the site no name, no location,
  no endowment and no custodian**: **(not stated)**.
- **Burial as devotional practice, and the "inverted prayer."** "While preserving this practice,
  Shaykh Abd al-Haqq actually practiced a spiritual exercise of premature burial. He had his living
  body buried as if dead, and after practicing breath control, ecstatic contemplation, and severe
  bodily discipline for days on end, he would emerge from the earth still alive" (p. 261, folio 240).
  Kugle reads it institutionally: "This use of burial as devotional practice hints at important
  connections with Hatha Yoga as a form of ritual discipline of the body" (p. 262, folio 241).
- **A prescribed posture regime with a counted dhikr.** From the "Shattari Treatise": "Sit
  cross-legged, just as the Yogis do. Raise your eyes and face to the heavens. Pronounce the following
  words of meditation (dhikr) one thousand times or more" (p. 263, folio 242); "There are eighty-four
  types of meditation [postures], and in each one they say there is a different benefit. As for the
  author of this book, I have chosen to practice just one of these eighty-four different postures"
  (p. 264, folio 243). The syllables are printed `ya hi hu, ya hi hu` (p. 263, folio 242) and
  `u hi hi` (p. 263, folio 242) — see Doubts.
- **Breath discipline as the mechanism.** Abd al-Haqq's "teachings stressed absolute control over the
  body through concentrating on the breath" (p. 261, folio 240); "the actual technique of both is
  similar, to reverse the breathing process by holding the breath and turning it upside down and inside
  out" (p. 266, folio 245); Abd al-Quddus "directs the practitioner to stay engaged in 'the activity of
  breath-control. In the language of the Jogis they call it khuvangam, which means "the snake" as it is
  breath-control for the snake.'" (pp. 266–267, folios 245–246; chapter 5 note 45).
- **The five headings of a ritual manual, i.e. the curriculum of a khanqah's devotional day.** "The
  five precious essences are meditations (adhkar), practices (ashghal), litanies (awrad), exercises
  `(riyadat][text-layer?]` and contemplative disciplines `(muraqabatf[text-layer?]` [(muraqabat)]"
  (p. 270, folio 249).
- **Print, lithography and translation as the transmission apparatus.** The figure's source is a
  lithograph: "litho. (Lucknow: `Matba" Fakhr al-Matabf[text-layer?]`, n.d.), 12" (p. 271, folio 250).
  The book works throughout from **an Urdu translation with an editor and a translator, both of whom
  intervene**: "Imdadullah's Urdu translator places special emphasis on this unstated foundation of
  Imdadullah's project" (p. 274, folio 253; chapter 5 note 56), and "The editor of the Urdu translation
  of 'The Brilliance of Hearts' penned an insightful marginal note to clarify this passage" (p. 276,
  folio 255; chapter 5 note 59). **The manual's purpose is stated as generational transmission**:
  "Hajji Imdadullah composed his ritual manual to ensure the transmission of devotional rituals (and
  the bodily disciplines that were their underlying foundation) to a new generation" (p. 276, folio
  255).
- **Sainthood redefined as an office — the chunk's most quotable institutional move.** "Imdadullah's
  primary concern was to maintain the validity of wilaya, or sainthood; this meant asserting that the
  Sufis were still the vanguard of Islamic piety, the cornerstone of community life, and the pivot of
  cosmic order" (p. 274, folio 253). Sainthood grants no exemption: "this elevated state does not grant
  a saint special dispensation or excuse from social obligations and legal responsibility … 'Having
  become supported on the seat of guidance, pointing out the path for fellow seekers of the Truth, his
  wilaya and mushaykha will stay sound and valid.'" (p. 276, folio 255; chapter 5 note 58). Then the
  shift: "The final sentence of this passage introduces a new term just after mentioning wilaya: that
  is mushaykha, or mastery" (p. 276, folio 255), and "This paradigm shift in imagining selflessness,
  from wilaya to mushaykha, is subtle; yet it had become pervasive by the nineteenth century" (p. 276,
  folio 255).
- **What "extending the commands of God" is said to cover, i.e. the shaikh's working portfolio.**
  "Such activity could include juridical decisions based on Islamic law, teaching prophetic traditions
  to a new generation, or addressing the material and political needs of the Muslim community in
  general" (p. 276, folio 255).
- **Political and economic setting, stated once and with a date.** "As the political and economic
  superstructure of the Mughal empire shrank and, by 1859, crumbled under British domination,
  Imdadullah explored alternative styles of devotion and rhetoric of leadership" (p. 274, folio 253).
  The digits of `1859` print clean. **Migration is not narrated in this chunk** — no hijra, no Mecca,
  no Thana Bhawan, no 1857 rising: all **(not stated)**.
- **Sharia conformity imposed after persecution.** "Like later generations Shattari Sufis, after the
  persecution of Muhammad Ghawth, the Sabiri-Chishti Sufis of Abd al-Quddus Gangohi's community
  emphasized that ecstatic inner experience must be enclosed by an outer casing of social and
  religious behavior conforming to the sharia. This is the Sabiri tradition that Imdadullah inherited"
  (p. 274, folio 253).
- **Absent institutional material, recorded as absent so the consolidator does not look for it here:**
  no ʿurs or mela, no langar, no offerings or cash income, no sajjada-nashin, no Auqaf or state

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **Initiation from the dead.** Abd al-Haqq Rudawli "took initiation directly from the long-dead
  Shaykh Sabir in a visionary encounter" (p. 261, folio 240) — reported by Kugle as the tradition's own
  account of how the Sabiri branch was revived.
- **Six months buried alive.** Attributed to the hagiography *Anwar al-ʿUyun*, written by Abd
  al-Quddus, Abd al-Haqq's grandson and successor: "He remained buried underground for six months,
  passing through each realm of the inner worlds, seeking the presence of God in each, such that his
  eyes were lined with the beautiful mascara of *His gaze did not turn aside nor go too far* (Q 53:17).
  In each realm, he would say to himself, 'O Ahmad, this world is not worthy of worship, so wake up now
  and move on!'" (p. 263, folio 242; chapter 5 note 35).
- **Hearing the Quran without ear or tongue.** "He heard the sound of *Then know that there is no god
  but I* (Q 16:2). Without voice and without pronunciation, without ear and without tongue he heard it.
  He passed out of himself … He lost consciousness of himself and came up out of the grave" (p. 263,
  folio 242; chapter 5 note 36).
- **Emergence as ascension.** "The hagiographic account specifies that 'he stood out of the sweetness
  of oblivion and joy of intimacy, and attained completeness in the ascending steps of union
  `(mi'raj-i wisal}[text-layer?]` [(miʿraj-i wisal)]'" (p. 263, folio 242; chapter 5 note 37).
- **The grandson's own vision, in the hagiography's preface.** "Then the nightingale of his soul raised
  its melody from within the garden of the beloved, and the beloved friend became united with him, in
  one voice and one conscience" (p. 262, folio 241).
- **A Yogi defeated and converted.** Muʿin al-Din, on arriving in Rajasthan, was "challenged by a
  formidably ascetic Yogi" who after a contest of devotions "was forced to submit to the more lofty
  authority" and "'converted to Islam'" (p. 268, folio 247; chapter 5 note 47). **Kugle presents this
  as a hagiographic type, not as an event**: "Hagiographies usually depict Yogis as losers in a
  struggle with a stalwart Sufi whose illuminated heart wins them over in the end" (p. 268, folio 247).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The chapter's thesis about Imdadullah's method: not repetition but redeployment.** "While cAbd
  al-Quddus gave Yogic techniques of bodily engagement and meditation a new narrative frame, Hajji
  Imdadullah three centuries later redeployed the body imagery altogether. He focused on the heart as
  the anatomical stage where the Sufi drama of union with the divine is played out" (p. 269, folio 248).
- **"Conversion of narrative", Kugle's own coinage and the chunk's central claim.** "Scholars in South
  Asian Islamic history are very familiar with 'narratives of conversion' to Islam; however, the case
  here is 'conversion of narrative.'" (p. 267, folio 246). He extends it against the standard reading:
  "Many historians have seen such hagiographic conversion narratives as allegories of the political
  conquest of Muslim military rulers over an ever expanding stretch of South Asia. Yet none have yet
  considered them to refer figuratively to the 'conversion of narrative.'" (p. 268, folio 247).
- **An explicit correction of Simon Digby.** "Yet Digby's explanatory term 'allegorize' needs to be
  sharpened and made more specific. The difference between Yogic and Sabiri-Chishti exercises is
  primarily a matter of narrative framework: Abd al-Quddus distilled Yogic techniques into a narrative
  of personal love mysticism, whereas such references to union with a personal deity do not play a
  central role in Yogic teaching at all" (p. 265, folio 244).
- **The anatomical argument: same technique, different organ.** "Although both Sufi techniques and
  Yogic techniques of bodily discipline emphasize the retention of breath, the effect of this breath
  retention manifests in different anatomical passages. In Yogic practice breath retention affects the
  passage of vital fluid through the spine, while in Sufi practice breath retention purifies the heart"
  (p. 266, folio 245), and "As is clear in the Amrtakunda, Yogic teaching largely bypasses the heart as
  the locus of devotional exercise" (p. 266, folio 245).
- **A stated sixteenth-century textualisation.** "Their research reveals that in the sixteenth century
  there was a revival of interest in Yoga among Sufis in South Asia, or at least a new emphasis on
  recording in texts the techniques of practice that were passed on orally from master to disciple"
  (p. 264, folio 243). **The hedge is the author's own** and should be carried with the claim.
- **The chain of textual mediation Kugle reconstructs.** "Imdadullah arrived at this cardiac synthesis
  through the textual mediation of `Al-Jawahir al-Khamsa[text-layer?]`" of Muhammad Ghawth (p. 270,
  folio 249), whose version of the hadith qudsi "anatomically places the presence of God not simply in
  the human body but rather explicitly in the organ of the heart" (p. 270, folio 249), the hadith
  itself deriving from the briefer version "originally quoted by Abd al-Quddus" (p. 270, folio 249) —
  a three-step genealogy Abd al-Quddus → Muhammad Ghawth → Imdadullah, stated as "continuing the
  conversion of narratives begun by Abd al-Quddus and pressed forward by Muhammad Ghawth Gwaliyori"
  (p. 272, folio 251).
- **The hadith qudsi as the load-bearing element, in three escalating versions.** Abd al-Quddus's
  Hindi couplet — "Within the flesh there is a heart, where my lord takes residence" — paraphrasing
  "a hadith qudsi in which Muhammad said that God says, 'In the body of Adam there is a lump of flesh,
  and in that lump of flesh there is a secret, and in that secret am I.'" (p. 269, folio 248; chapter 5
  note 50), which Abd al-Quddus locates "inside the body near the navel" (p. 269, folio 248); Muhammad
  Ghawth's fuller version — "'In the body of each human being there is a knot of flesh, and in this
  knot is a moral heart, and in this heart is a conscience, and in this conscience is a secret, and in
  this secret, there I am.'" (p. 270, folio 249; chapter 5 note 51); and Imdadullah's, "a much more
  elaborate version that anatomically locates this fleshly seat … as the human heart" (p. 269, folio
  248).
- **Two images fused: kundalini and the Satanic serpent in the blood.** "Imdadullah further juxtaposes
  two images, one from `Indie[text-layer?]` [Indic] sources and one from Khurasani sources, and unites
  the two in the heart" (p. 272, folio 251); "With the same motion through which dhikr transforms the
  physical body, like the positive energy of the released kundalini, dhikr also creates the durable
  foundation for apt ethic actions by dispelling self-centeredness, which can be imagined
  metaphorically as repelling of the negative energy of Shaytan in the bloodstream" (p. 272, folio 251).
- **Kugle's stated physiology of dhikr.** "The devotee first controls the breathing process; this
  control generates internal heat, which clarifies the blood and melts the fatty `plague[text-layer?]`
  [plaque] that clogs the arteries; this exercise gives the heart a new flexibility and gentleness that
  dislodges the barbs of the serpent of temptation that coils around the heart" (p. 269, folio 248).
- **Sufism as heart, sharia as body.** "In Imdadullah's metaphor, the heart is the center of the body,
  and without a sincere heart the body will fall into corruption. But the heart cannot stand alone and
  needs an encasing body to enliven it … It is as if Sufism is the heart while the sharia is the outer
  body. Dhikr is medicine to keep the heart pure and harmonious" (pp. 273–274, folios 252–253).
- **Imdadullah as a transitional figure who "embodies contradictions."** "Hajji Imdadullah lived on the
  cusp of great and accelerating changes encompassing the wielding of power, the organization of
  society, and the hegemony of concepts. From this vantage point, he is a transitional figure in the
  religious history of Muslims in South Asia, and in transition, he embodies contradictions" (p. 274,
  folio 253). He "linked his Sufi devotion to new ascendant discourses, such as medical anatomy,
  growing sharia-mindedness, and emphasis on rigorous outward upholding of the legal injunctions of the
  Qur'an" (p. 274, folio 253).
- **An explicit methodological warning against reading ritual as empty form.** "The continuity of
  expression with prior devotional tradition does not mean that `Imdadullah's` works simply repeat
  earlier theories and formulations. To assume this would be to accept modernist condemnations of
  ritual as empty gestures and ritual practitioners as traditionalists alienated from the true meaning
  of their actions" (p. 274, folio 253; chapter 5 note 55).
- **"Metaphysiology" as Kugle's analytical term.** "The text itself is not about metaphysics but rather
  about metaphysiology: that is, how the human body and especially the heart can be trained to adopt
  postures and motions that have transcendent ethical effects so that the body itself can become a
  mirror for a just cosmic order" (p. 275, folio 254).
- **Two maxims bracketing the manual.** "The first is a Delphic maxim: those persons who know their
  selves know God. And the second is a Qur'anic maxim: those who surrender their selves serve God"
  (p. 275, folio 254; chapter 5 note 57); Imdadullah "bases the bodily training … upon the former
  maxim; yet he aims these same ritual techniques toward achieving the latter, activist maxim.
  Retraining one's body techniques can unite knowledge and service" (p. 275, folio 254).
- **A dating claim about Sufi institutions.** Nullifying the self and "sparking the state of wilaya
  have constituted the goal of Sufi communities since they were instituted as formal organizations in
  the twelfth century" (p. 275, folio 254).
- **The bridge-building reading of Sufi–Yogi exchange.** "On the potentially violent fault line
  between Islamic and `Indie[text-layer?]` [Indic] communities, Abd al-Quddus tried to build a bridge
  by rendering homologous the bodily disciplines and ritual practices present in both religious
  traditions" (p. 267, folio 246); and Sufis "sought not only to enrich and intensify the bodily
  disciplines of their ritual tradition but also to build a bridge of communication between the
  religious elites of the Muslim community and those of other, non-Muslim `Indie[text-layer?]` [Indic]
  communities" (p. 268, folio 247).
- **A reading of a hagiography's title as doctrine.** "The phrase 'hidden from sight' usually describes
  pearls, whose luster is buried in the flesh of the oyster and whose beauty is hidden from sight by
  the ugly material of its shell" (p. 262, folio 241); and, of the Earthquake sura, "he claims that
  earth is a metaphor for the human body, for Adam was created of its clay and dust" (p. 262, folio
  241).

## Doubts

**The one archive mapping, and why it needs review.**
- **`shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar` is mapped on the name "Shaykh Farid al-Din" alone** (p. 261,
  folio 240; p. 264, folio 243). The supporting evidence in the chunk is threefold and no more: the
  personal name Farid al-Din; the Chishti lineage, since both Shaykh Sabir and Nizam al-Din are called
  his "followers" and "branch[es] of the Chishti masters" (p. 261, folio 240); and a retreat located
  "in the Punjab" (p. 264, folio 243). **The book nowhere prints Masʿud, Ganj-i Shakar, Ganjshakar,
  Pakpattan, a death year, or any reference to his tomb.** The archive row's own designation is "Baba
  Fariduddin Mas'ud Ganjshakar", Pakpattan. The mapping is therefore an inference from name plus
  lineage plus region, and **a human should confirm it before any of this reaches the Pakpattan
  entry**. If it is rejected, both bullets move under "Other saints, sites and events" unchanged.
- **The archive holds no second Farid al-Din row**, so this is not a two-row ambiguity; it is a
  single-row identification of uncertain strength.

**Refused candidates, recorded with reasons.**
- **`shrine-of-shah-inayat-qadiri` refused.** The chunk names "Burhan al-Din Shattari" and his
  "Shattari Treatise" (p. 263, folio 242), the "Shattari Sufi community" (p. 270, folio 249) and
  "Muhammad Ghawth Gwaliyori, the Shattari master" (p. 270, folio 249). The row's silsila cell reads
  "Qadri Shattari", but **an order name alone does not license a row**, and the precedent refusing this
  exact row on a bare Shattari mention is followed here.
- **`gorakhnath-goraknath-temple` and `tilla-jogian` both refused.** The chunk speaks of "a
  Natha-panti Yogi" (p. 268, folio 247) and of "the ordinary muladvara of `Natha-panthi[text-layer?]`
  [Yogic] teaching" (p. 267, folio 246), and of Yogis throughout. **Guru Gorakhnath is not named, no
  temple, tomb, cave or site is attached, and Natha-panth is a tradition name, not a person** — the
  same category as an order name.
- **All eight-plus Shiva rows refused** (`amb-temples-amb-sharif`, `bhagnari-mandir`, `katas-raj-
  temples`, `shahwala-teja-singh-mandir`, `shiv-mandir-chiti-ghati`,
  `shree-ratneshwar-mahadev-temple-karachi`, `umarkot-amarkot-shiv-mandir`,
  `chandragup-baba-chandragup`). "siva" occurs once, inside Digby's synopsis of Hatha Yogic
  physiology — the kundalini "is persuaded to rise through … the spine until it attains union with
  siva, the force responsible for the production of bindu-rasa in the skull" (p. 266, folio 245). **It
  is a cosmological force in an anatomical scheme, not a deity at a site**, and the name would match
  many rows at once; per the convention it is named here rather than assigned.
- **The `Shaktipeeth Shri Hinglaj Mata Mandir` row (empty id cell) is not triggered and no id is
  invented.** "sakti" appears once, only as a synonym for the coiled force — "a dormant force,
  symbolized as a coiled serpent called kundalini and also sakti" (p. 266, folio 245). Hinglaj, Mata
  and the Goddess as such are absent from the chunk.
- **`bibi-pak-daman` refused.** "a woman master named Bibi Fatima" (p. 262, folio 241) is a teacher of
  Abd al-Haqq Rudawli in fifteenth-century north India; the row's principal figure is "The six Bibis
  (Bibi Pak Daman)" of Lahore. **Shared honorific only, no place, no date, no tomb** — the same
  category as the shared-nisba refusal recorded by an earlier worker.
- **Bare-toponym convention, reversibility note.** No toponym in this chunk is attached to a shrine,
  tomb, saint's grave, ʿurs or custodian, and the only one that would take an id if the bare-toponym
  ruling reverses is **"the Punjab"** (p. 264, folio 243) — and it would take it only as a region, with
  no archive row corresponding to a region. **Delhi, Bihar, Gangoh, Rajasthan, Khurasan and Lucknow
  correspond to no archive row at all**, so the reversal would change nothing for them.

**Internal inconsistencies in the book as printed, not damage.**
- **The Yogic text is spelled two ways**: `Amrtakunda` at pp. 265, 266, 268, 272 (folios 244, 245, 247,

  book's, not the text layer's** — but a human transcribing these syllables into an entry should treat
