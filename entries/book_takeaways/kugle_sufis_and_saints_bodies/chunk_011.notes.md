## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Initiation, itemised in four requirements** — "Muhammad Ghawth told Wajih al-Din that initiation
  consisted of four things: taking his hand in commitment, wearing distinctively Shattari clothes,
  listening avidly to ecstatic music sessions with poetry and dance, and saying his evening prayers
  late" (p. 187, folio 166). The dress requirement was dropped "after pausing in meditation" because
  Wajih al-Din already wore "the dress of the Chishtis" (p. 187, folio 166).
- **The reason given for the late evening prayers is intercessory** — "to dedicate their efficacy
  toward releasing the fallen angels, Harut and Marut, whom he had seen in his ascension visions"
  (p. 187, folio 166).
- **Reliance rather than renunciation** — Ghawth "did not ask him to give up teaching the religious
  sciences to which he devoted his life but rather to give up relying on them", asking reliance
  instead on "the saints[text-layer?] [saint's] alchemical touch, which could turn a leaden soul into
  selfless gold" (p. 187, folio 166).
- **Khanqah construction and its funding** — "With the financial and political support of these
  disciples, Ghawth was able to build a house and devotional center (khanqaty[text-layer?] [khanqah)]
  in Ahmadabad" (p. 188, folio 167). **No date and no cost: (not stated).** The khanqah contained a
  mosque used for forty-day retreats (p. 189, folio 168) and was entered "by a small rear door"
  (p. 190, folio 169).
- **Madrassa and marriage alliance** — "Muhammad Ghawth's four sons became students in his madrassa,
  and the two families began to intermarry" (p. 187, folio 166); Wajih al-Din "began to induct his
  family members into the fold of Shattari disciples" (pp. 187–188, folios 166–167).
- **Astrological prayer as a marketable technique** — the prayers and meditations of Al-Jawahar
  al-Khamsa ("The Five Precious Essences") are "calibrated to constellation movements and planetary
  influences", the technique "known as dawat-i asma wa taskhlr[text-layer?] [taskhīr] or 'invoking
  the divine names and subjecting the world to their influences'" (p. 182, folio 161).
- **Sama and its rejection** — Ali Muttaqi "attended sama sessions of poetry rhythm,[text-layer?]
  [poetry, rhythm,] and music, which could lead those in attendance into states of ecstasy", and
  "rejected this musical style of devotion" (p. 186, folio 165).
- **State office and the machinery of denunciation** — the sultan "named him'Enforcer of the
  Sharia'"[text-layer?] ["Enforcer of the Sharia"] (p. 185, folio 164), rendered at p. 191, folio 170
  as "the 'Enforcer of the Sharia' (hakim-i sharc}[text-layer?] [hakim-i sharʿ)]"; the scholars
  "gathered together and prepared a declaration (mabdar[text-layer?])" (p. 184, folio 163);
  signatures were collected from "All the respected religious leaders, both Sufi and scholarly… with
  the exception of two" (p. 188, folio 167); two successive affirmations were drafted and refused
  (pp. 191–192, folios 170–171); the sanction sought was that Ghawth "be judged an infidel and liable
  for punishment" (p. 188, folio 167), and the penitential act demanded was to "wash the paper clean
  of ink" in public — "if he has to wash it off in front of all the Muslims and publicly swears not
  to teach books about such speculative claims" (p. 192, folio 171).
- **Surveillance** — "A scholar sent his son into the khanqah to spy on Muhammad Ghawth and determine
  the true nature of his teachings" (p. 188, folio 167).
- **The flight** — "moving the whole court with him from Ahmadabad to Mahmudabad" for a hunt; "After
  midnight… Muhammad Ghawth and some followers like Wajih al-Din had disappeared from the city,
  heading toward Bharoch" (pp. 193–194, folios 172–173); Ali Muttaqi asked Mu'ayyad Khan "to detain
  him and send him back to Ahmadabad in chains" (p. 194, folio 173).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

All of the following are presented by Kugle as **Ghawth's own first-person written claim** in the
Ascension Narrative, or as hagiographic report, not as the author's assertion.

- Arrival at the Throne: "When he arrived at the threshold of the divine Throne, he sat down at the
  zenith of power beyond cause", all prophets, saints and angels "fell into a profound prostration
  and were eclipsed, virtually obliterated", with the verse Q 55:28 manifesting (p. 179, folio 158).
  Kugle notes the narrative "can continue only in the third person" at this point (p. 179, folio 158).
- The dialogue and the vision of origins: "I witnessed the essence of that being that was prior to
  any primordial origin… I then saw within every form its own origin and its own destiny, interwoven"
  (p. 179, folio 158).
- The descent through emanations and the meeting with the Prophet, who says "The first step of the
  Prophet is the last step of the saint"; Ali and Abu Bakr escort him back, "one on each side"; he
  sees "my former companion [Khidr]"; then "After taking just three steps, I reached the cot in my
  hermitage" (p. 180, folio 159).
- **Time compression as the miracle's proof** — his neighbours dispute whether he ever rose, one
  insisting "He hasn't gone anywhere—he's just been meditating", while Ghawth reflects, "I've been
  away from what seems like years while these two are arguing over whether I have stood up or
  remained sitting just one moment ago!" (p. 181, folio 160).
- **The angels' testimony of a previous, disembodied presence at the Prophet's own ascension** — "On
  the day that the Prophet Muhammad ascended along this route, there were with him some saints, and
  you were one of them. However, at that time, you were in the form of pure spirit. In contrast, this
  time you are fully attached to your body!" (pp. 190–191, folios 169–170).
- **Political karamat** — "He refused to endorse reigning sultan of Delhi, the Afghan Ibrahim Lodhi,
  and called upon God to remove him from power. Within a year, in 1526, the Turkic Mughals under the
  leadership of Babur overran the Lodi Afghans forces" (p. 183, folio 162); and the prediction, made
  in exile, "How quickly the turn of beautiful names will return!" that Humayun would regain power
  (pp. 183–184, folios 162–163).
- **The eye-signal** — Wajih al-Din "gave his shaykh a signal (ishara)… He communicated through the
  corner of his eye a secret message that could not be spoken and that even contradicted his speech
  at the same moment" (p. 190, folio 169). Kugle treats this as duplicity, not karamat.

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **The ascension's rhetorical shape is an exact inversion of itself** — "his descent from the
  heavens, alighting on the mountain, lapsing back into sleep, and then waking into his routine life
  mirror the process of his ascension" (p. 181, folio 160).
- **The closing modesty is not what carried** — the "first step of the Prophet" formula makes it "a
  secondhand ascension (and therefore a decent ascension)", but "this modest point is not what really
  lasts in the minds of those who read this Ascension Narrative or in the ears of those who might
  have heard of it orally in Ghawth's own time" (p. 182, folio 161).
- **Kugle's causal claim about the source of the cult, stated against the obvious reading** — the
  reputed ability to bend the world to his will through astrological invocation "is most likely the
  real cause of Ghawth's fame and popularity", while the ascension "gave credence and justification to
  his reputation as a saint whose prayers have potent efficacy" (p. 182, folio 161). Selflessness
  made him "a conduit for divine will to act in the material and social world… therefore a source of
  blessing and success, whether one's goal was mystical journeying or political conquest" (p. 182,
  folio 161).
- **Throne-witness converted into throne-legitimation** — "Muhammad Ghawth's witness to the divine
  throne beyond the bounds of material existence may have given him a special authority to fill
  worldly thrones in Delhi", and "Mughal rulers hoped that Muhammad Ghawth's popularity and his
  association with God's throne might give justification to their own claim to the throne at Delhi"
  (p. 183, folio 162). Kugle explicitly defers the full argument: "This is not the place to
  illustrate how Muhammad Ghawth and his coalition of Shattari Sufis supported the Mughal rise to
  power, as I have told that fascinating story elsewhere" (p. 183, folio 162).
- **The philological core of the chapter's thesis** — "In Arabic and Persian, the word for witnessing
  with one's own eyes (mu-shahada) is derived from the same root letters as bearing witness to the
  truth by stating one's belief (shahada)" (p. 184, folio 163); jurists and theological scholars
  "patrol that boundary limit" and "reserve the right to monitor and question the sincerity of
  Muslims' bearing witness" (p. 184, folio 163).
- **Persecution as the archive** — "Much of the information that is available about Muhammad Ghawth
  is due to the persecution campaigns that raged against him" (p. 184, folio 163). Kugle gives three
  reasons for the length of his treatment: it "says much about vision and its relation to the
  embodied self: Was it seen in waking consciousness or in dreaming sleep?"; "it shows us Sufis in
  conflict with one another over the nature of the body, its capacity to misinform the ego, and its
  relationship to the sharia"; and it is "probably the most detailed record in late medieval and
  early modern times about a Sufi's persecution" (pp. 184–185, folios 163–164).
- **A revision of Carl Ernst** — Ernst "had first explored this episode in an insightful article on
  persecution and circumspection, declaring it an incident about which one can say very little for
  sure; however, this precious document now makes this incident one that may be the most detailed
  account yet of a Sufi persecution", and it "questions the ways this struggle has been distorted and
  downplayed in the standard hagiographic accounts of all the figures involved" (p. 187, folio 166).
- **Two opposite reactions to one change** — both men "were dissatisfied with the Sufi style of
  devotion that the Chishti order had promoted and that dominated Islamic culture throughout South
  Asia during the centuries of rule by the Delhi sultanate (1175-1526 C.E.)"; Ghawth "advocated a
  completely new Sufi order in South Asia, the Shattari order", while Ali Muttaqi "settled on
  a'juridical Sufi"[text-layer?] [a "juridical Sufi"] pattern (tasawwuf al-usuliyyin) that eschewed
  partisanship toward any Sufi order" (p. 185, folio 164).
- **Contradiction under pressure, named as such** — "Under the pressure of persecution, Muhammad
  Ghawth appears to contradict his own written account" by conceding the visions were seen in sleep
  (p. 190, folio 169), the concession being prompted by Wajih al-Din's question whether they were
  "visions seen in sleep (ru'iya o waqica}[text-layer?] [waqiʿa)])" (p. 190, folio 169).
- **Hagiography read against the letters** — "Most hagiographic literature portrays Wajih al-Din as a
  mediator who tried to calm the angry Ali Muttaqi. However, in his own letters, Ali Muttaqi presents
  himself as the official mediator to both sides of the conflict, while Wajih al-Din comes across as
  a partisan willing to cheat by saying one thing with his lips while signaling something else with
  his eyes" (p. 192, folio 171).
- **The legal logic of the acquittal attempt** — Wajih al-Din's defence rests on outward acts ("there
  is no visible trace of infidelity in his actions: he says his five prayers each day in
  congregation"), on the ninety-nine-to-one benefit-of-the-doubt rule, and on the exemption of the
  ecstatic from legal responsibility (p. 189, folio 168); the sultan accepts it — "With 'no visible
  trace' of heretical action, how could they judge him a heretic, for jurists judge people by their
  outer actions observed by reliable witnesses" (p. 190, folio 169).
- **Cosmology as the substantive charge** — in Kalid al-Makhazin "Ghawth gave a systematic
  explication of the levels of the cosmos, asserting that each level had emanated from the divine and
  they will be reabsorbed into the divine by the end of time" (p. 192, folio 171); the two "clearest
  charges" were claiming "that God takes material form" and that "God's existence and worldly
  existence are in continuum" (p. 192, folio 171).
- **Emanation as the frame of the descent** — the cosmic levels "are clearly emanations of God, from
  the rarified source of pure being, through the universal intellect, through the spiritual reality
  of Muhammad as the primordial human being, then through further emanations of increasing
  materiality" (p. 180, folio 159); Persian Sufis "appropriated the mirror imagery of Ibn Arabi and
  heightened its intensity as a story of loving desire" (p. 180, folio 159); al-Zahi is quoted on the
  eye as "a means of achieving union between inner, hidden desire and its expression through subtle
  hints and symbolic allusions" (p. 179, folio 158).
