## Shrines and figures in the archive

  `'Madho Lai'`[text-layer?] ['Madho Lal'] Hussayn): **the hagiographic source Kugle quotes through
  the Lahore chapter is named here, in the notes to Chapter Four, and this is the first time it is
  named anywhere the pipeline has read.** Chapter Four note 3 opens
  "Mahmud ibn Muhammad Pir, Haqiqat al-Fuqara' (ms.), 72-73." (p. 326, folio 305, **ch. 4 n. 3**);

- **madho-lal-hussain**: **the source described, in Chapter Four note 6** —
  "Mahmud ibn Muhammad Pir, Haqiqat al-Fuqara', fol. 30. This hagiographic poem was composed about
  1662, just sixty-two years after Shah Hussayn's death. The author's father had been a companion of
  Shah Hussayn's, while the author himself was a close attendant of his successor, Madho."
  (p. 326, folio 305, **ch. 4 n. 6**). So: a verse hagiography, a manuscript, cited both by page
  (72-73) and by folio (fol. 30), composed **"about 1662"**, by a man one generation removed from
  the saint and attached to Madho. **Kugle does not state Shah Hussayn's death year here**:
  **(not stated)** — the two figures in the sentence imply one, but the book does not print it and
  it is not computed here (RULE 2).
- **madho-lal-hussain**: **where Kugle's own earlier publication of this material can be found** —

  Poetic Biography `of'Madho Lai'`[text-layer?] [of 'Madho Lal'] Hussayn," in Vanita and Kidwai,

  [Al-Kattani], Salwat, 2:17, cites fatwas of Ahmad `al-Wansharls!`[text-layer?] in `al-Mi'yar`

  checked (see Doubts).
- **Mawlid and the politics of descent** — "Cornell, Realm of the Saint, 341-42, discusses this

  (p. 319, folio 298, **ch. 1 n. 24**); and the adoption of the practice by "Sultan Yusuf ibn
  `Ya'qub`[text-layer?] ibn Abd al-Haqq `al-Marlnl`[text-layer?] [al-Marini]", which "had begun in
  Egypt roughly a century earlier" (p. 319, folio 298, **ch. 1 n. 23**).

  `al-'Imranl`[text-layer?] [al-'Imrani], had been the first assigned leader of the shurafa' by
  order of Abu Salim ibn `Abl`[text-layer?] [Abi] al-Hasan `al-Marlnl`[text-layer?] [al-Marini]"
  (p. 319, folio 298, **ch. 1 n. 27**).
- **Verse inscribed on shrine fabric** — "the poet who adorned the wall of al-Khalwa al-Qadiriyya
  with his verses" (p. 319, folio 298, **ch. 1 n. 39**).
- **Shrine demolition as programme** — the conclusion's charge against the Wahhabis: they
  "must cover living bodies with regulations and limitations while demolishing the covering of

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **Wahhabism is a doctrine of disenchantment.** "Ibn Abd al-Wahhab's primary text, as amplified and
  applied by his commentators, demonstrates that Wahhabi doctrine is one of disenchantment"
  (p. 309, folio 288); it "bitterly denounces the places and people in whom the majority of Muslims
  find access to the sacred canopy" — "holy people, whether prophets or saints, and the burial sites
  of their bones, through which materiality becomes suffused with spirituality"
  (pp. 309–310, folios 288–289).
- **The prohibition of images fails on its own terms.** Kugle follows al-Zahi: "the image of the body
  becomes present in the rigidity of the juridical position against it, beyond the margins that it
  demarcates for itself… exceeding, to a great extent, the possibility of burying
  (`iqbar`[text-layer?]) the proofs of its presence." (p. 309, folio 288, cited to conclusion n. 48).
- **The evidence offered for Wahhabi motivation, and its limits, stated by the author himself.**
  "The commentator Abd al-Rahman admits that he joined the Sufis but tasted only disappointment and
  failure before turning in compensation to the Wahhabi movement. This is one man's perspective, but
  we cannot attribute a dynamic movement to just a personal grudge, for people have joined the
  Wahhabis with differing motivations." (p. 309, folio 288, cited to conclusion n. 49). A rare
  self-limiting move worth quoting when this book is used as authority.
- **The "Islamic imaginaire" as the thing under attack.** "his attack on the sacred bones of the
  buried body is an attack on the imaginaire of medieval and early modern Islamic societies" —
  a cosmos of "hierarchal levels of value linking the material world to the sacred realm beyond, as
  mediated and connected through the human image" (p. 310, folio 289, cited to conclusion n. 50);
  it "is expressed most profoundly by Sufis and is articulated most lastingly by Islamic art and
  architecture", and "still persists among Muslims who have not given in to the disenchantment
  imposed by colonial modernism from without and wrought by Wahhabi critique from within"
  (p. 310, folio 289).
- **The contrast, stated succinctly by the author.** "Sufis embrace the ambiguity of embodiment and
  channel it to achieve access to the sacred realm that is beyond the limit of materiality. In
  contrast, Wahhabis denounce the ambiguity of embodiment and strive to achieve radical purity by
  banishing or burying images of the body as well as actual bodies of opponents."
  (p. 311, folio 290).
- **A political periodisation.** "Serving the needs of the Saudi monarchy and its oil economy has
  moderated the radical core of Wahhabi ideology; however, in contemporary times after the Cold War
  world order has broken down, the radical core of Wahhabi ideology has resurfaced with a vengeance.
  Al-Qaeda, the Taliban, and other splinter groups of Wahhabi-inspired militants express this
  ideology's most violent inner drive." (p. 311, folio 290, cited to conclusion n. 52); and
  "cultural jihad by prohibiting body images in other ways: suppressing women, denouncing Sufis, or
  reviling as idolatry technologies of visual communication (like photography, cinema, and
  television)" (p. 311, folio 290).
- **The rainbow against the sword.** Noah's rainbow as the sign of reenchantment: "The wooden
  dwelling of his ark could, in later times, give rise to the ark of the covenant, the temple
  sanctuary, and the tomb shrines of saints as buildings of hope where death becomes transmuted into
  a promise of renewed life." (p. 312, folio 291); "Rather than the rainbow, their sign is the
  sword." (p. 312, folio 291).
- **The book's own statement of what it is for.** "this study asserts that the Sufi tradition offers
  a valuable archive of experiences, practices, and worldviews that value the human body, defend its
  integrity, and strive to comprehend human embodiment in an integral way" (p. 313, folio 292); and
  the concession preceding it, that "many contemporary Muslims may view Sufism as sorely antiquated,
  overly traditionalist, too authoritarian, or suspiciously heterodox" (p. 313, folio 292).
- **What the conclusion does NOT do.** pp. 309–315 contain the whole remainder of the conclusion,
  and **they contain no restatement of the book's five cases, no return to the Lahore chapter, and
  no mention of Shah Hussayn, Madho, Lahore or any Lahore shrine.** The conclusion ends instead on
  Nakhshabi and the heart. See Doubts — this closes the search opened by the two earlier workers.
- **A methodological note worth recording**, since it bears on how this book cites Sufi texts:
  "This printed version has been compared against several manuscript versions of the same narrative
  and has in several places been corrected in favor of expressions found in the manuscripts"
  (p. 323, folio 302, **ch. 3 n. 15**, on Ghawth, `Awrad-i Ghawthiyya`).
- **An explicit limit on the evidence**, in the same vein: "There is no other copy of this letter, to
  the extent of my knowledge." (p. 325, folio 304, **ch. 3 n. 64**).
- **A hadith attribution the author refuses.** "Muhammad Ghawth relates this saying,
  `al-nisa' juba'il al-shaytan`[text-layer?], as if it were a hadith attributed to the Prophet.
  However, it does not appear in any of the six authoritative Sunni hadith collections. It may come
  from a rare source or could be an aphorism popularly attributed to the Prophet."
  (p. 324, folio 303, **ch. 3 n. 29**).
