## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Incense at tombs.** "Incense is usually burning in the shrines over saints' tombs, and its heady
  fragrance infuses all objects in the vicinity" (p. 96, folio 75). "Burning it increases its
  potency, spreads its effect, and intensifies its durable traces" (p. 96, folio 75). Incense burners
  "played a dramatic sensory role in the yearly procession to the tomb of Mawlay Idris" (p. 96, folio
  75, note 47) — the chunk's only reference to an annual procession, and it gives **no date, no month
  and no name for the procession: (not stated)**.
- **Custodianship, and women's access.** The chunk's single most citable institutional statement:
  "Although the shrines around their tombs serve as gathering places for women as well as men, the
  custodians are all men, and men alone are allowed in the inner chamber, where one can touch the
  tomb's cloth covering" (p. 104, folio 83). Note the three distinct facts — women admitted to the
  shrine, excluded from the inner chamber, and the cloth covering as the object of touch. **Kugle
  says this of the Fes shrines of chapter 1, not of South Asia**; it is recorded here as comparative
  framing, not as a statement about any archive row.
- **Royal patronage as the price of a shrine.** "While materially dependent on rulers for the vast
  financial outlay that such building projects require, the tomb shrine is never totally determined
  by the rulers' intentions" (p. 99, folio 78). "Both Waghorne and Asad remind us that it is through
  the material surface of the saint, the tomb and shrine that can be built above the bodily remains
  of his bones, that rulers can forge a social image of the saint for posterity" (p. 99, folio 78).
- **Royal ziyara and the building episode, in the book's own words.** Abdullah "desired to visit Imam
  Idris ibn Idris", and his court objected: "How can it be fitting for you to visit Imam Idris when
  you have constructed nothing in Fes, such as what the Marinid rulers constructed of mosques and
  madrassas and such beautiful buildings and edifices that remain as testament to them? But look how
  they covered the resting place of Imam Idris with just a roof low to the ground, no grander than
  the roof over [the tomb of] the master, Muhammad ibn Abbad!" (p. 98, folio 77, note 48).
- **The building itself.** "Shamed by his courtiers, the ruler of the new Sa'dian dynasty ordered
  that a new roof be raised over the tomb of Mawlay Idris to enclose three tiled `courtyards."It`
  `[text-layer?]` [courtyards. "It] is said that only after the construction was complete in the most
  beautiful form and perfect design did he come into the presence of Mawlay Idris with great humility
  and respect."" (p. 98, folio 77, note 49). "In this way, the shrine around Mawlay Idris's tomb took
  the form that it currently has today (despite continuous repairs, enlargements, and elaborations)"
  (p. 99, folio 78). **No building cost, no endowment, no waqf, no revenue grant and no visitor
  figure is given anywhere in this chunk: all (not stated).**
- **Public appropriation after building.** "Once built, it is accessible to the public. People can
  appropriate it for their own needs and extract from it empowerment for their own intentions"
  (p. 99, folio 78).
- **The shrine as spectacle — `mashhad`.** "When a shrine is built around the tomb, it becomes a
  *mashhad*, `"z[text-layer?]` [a] place that provides a view for witnessing." The tomb becomes a
  theater or spectacle" (p. 100, folio 79). "The building, crowds and rituals make evident the hidden
  body of the holy person and the life she or he lived" (p. 100, folio 79). The amplifying media are
  named as "architecture, decorative art, devotional music, religious ritual, and social
  interaction" (p. 100, folio 79).

- **The Sufi hospice as women's refuge.** The Nakhshabi tale's heroine "left her 'house of discord'

  always in practice" (p. 107, folio 86, note 4).
- **Chronology of the formative legal texts.** The puritan regime of gender control "was inscribed in
  Islamic juridical texts and moralistic treatises (in the formative period from the ninth to the
  twelfth century C.E.)" (p. 107, folio 86).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **Kugle's definition of karamat, as the book gives it:** saints' "actions are seen as the actions of
  God channeled through them: this is exactly how Muslim authors define miracles, or *karamat*,
  literally acts of 'noble generosity.'" (p. 97, folio 76).
- **The standard repertoire of saintly miracle, per Kugle:** saints "are perceived to provide generous
  sustenance in times of scarcity, water in times of drought, and protection in times of crisis"
  (p. 97, folio 76).
- **The epistemological rule Kugle sets for miracle reports:** "It is ultimately unimportant for an
  outside observer to judge whether miracles happen or not. They are perceived to happen, and miracle
  stories are told as part of a `peoples[text-layer?]` [people's] self-understanding" (p. 97, folio
  76).
- **Abu Ghalib's rapture** — his "spiritual state flooding over into others when he abandoned himself
  to rapture", cited from "the hagiographic record presented above" (p. 96, folio 75), i.e. from an
  earlier chunk, not narrated here.
- **The rediscovery of the body of Idris** — reported as the event around which chapter 1 turns, with
  no narrative detail in this chunk (p. 97, folio 76).
- **The woman who feigned death** — Nakhshabi's tale: "Tired from `thei[text-layer?]` [their]
  overweening competitive machismo, she even feigned death, but they revived her in order to continue
  fighting with each other over who would possess her through marriage" (p. 105, folio 84, note 2).
  Attributed by Kugle to Nakhshabi, not to a hagiographic collection.
- **Eve created from Adam's rib** — attributed by Kugle to Ibn Kathir, who "seamlessly interweaves
  citations of the Qur'an ... with traditions from the early Islamic community (such as the detail
  about her being created from a rib and her name meaning 'created from something living')" (pp.
  111–112, folios 90–91, note 10). Kugle's own verdict on the detail: it is "as found in Jewish and
  Christian tradition, as if it were found in the Qur'an, which it is not" (p. 112, folio 91).
- **The serpent and the seven fruits** — attributed to al-Kisa'i: "Then Eve advanced toward that
  tree... and plucked seven fruits from seven branches. One fruit she ate, one fruit she saved, and
  five other fruits she went and gave to Adam" (p. 112, folio 91, note 12).
- **Eve's punishment** — "The 'stories of the prophets' genre continues to assert that for this crime
  Eve and her female descendants are 'punished' with menstruation, labor pains, and lack of
  intellect" (p. 113, folio 92, note 13). Attributed by Kugle to the genre, not endorsed.
- **The consecration of Mary before birth** — quoted from the Qur'an: "Once the wife of'Imran called
  out, 'My Lord, I dedicate to you what is in my belly as a consecrated offering!'" (p. 113, folio 92,
  cited `((^3:23)[text-layer?]` [(Q 3:23)]).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **Bones, children and incense are one argument, not three.** "[T]he idea of physical perpetuation of
  spiritual power is reflected also in the idea of genealogical perpetuation of spiritual power. Just
  as the holiness of saints inheres in their bones, their holiness can inhere in the children and
  families they leave behind. This paradoxical relationship between spiritual power and material
  objects is best reconciled in incense" (p. 96, folio 75). Incense works as the model case because
  "its sensory presence in its aroma only grows more intense when its solid material form is
  destroyed" (p. 96, folio 75).
- **Religion as binding.** The intense potency felt at a tomb "is the more general immaterial
  connection between people that is social, cultural, and religious, a connection that binds people
  together into a whole, like ligaments bind the bones and the organs that depend on them into a whole
  body. This image uncovers one of the primary meanings of the word 'religion' as derived from
  *re-ligare*, a binding together again" (p. 96, folio 75). Tombs "provide 'pegs' to preserve the
  order of the human cosmos" (p. 96, folio 75).
- **Material props are necessary, not incidental** — the explicit thesis of the chapter's close:
  "material props are required to achieve this immaterial effervescence of communal harmony and
  social unity. Material props must give space a meaningful form through the architecture and urban
  geography. Time, too, must be shaped into meaningful divisions through the material trappings of
  ritual and performance of devotion" (p. 96, folio 75).
- **The saint as mirror.** "It is as if the personality of the saint becomes transparent, like glass.
  When set against the material of the body, which is like metal, the saint can take on the reflective
  qualities of a mirror. The social public can see its most cherished values and aspirations reflected
  in a saint" (p. 97, folio 76). Hence "miracles and the saints that channel them have a political
  potency, especially during times of political instability" (p. 97, folio 76). Restated at the
  chapter's conclusion: "The `saints[text-layer?]` [saint's] body acts as a mirror for the religious
  virtues around which society can adhere and upon which political rulers can establish their
  authority" (p. 99, folio 78).
- **A shrine is architectural evidence of a political alliance.** The building over Idris's tomb
  "gives evidence of a revolution—a revolution not only in the urban geography of Fes but also in
  spiritual thought and dynastic politics within the city. It gives architectural evidence of an
  alliance that was growing in Morocco between Sufi communities and sharifian notables" (p. 97, folio
  76). This is the chunk's methodological claim that a building can be read as a political document,
  and it is the argument most transferable to the archive's Pakistan rows.
- **Tomb patronage as legitimation by a resented new dynasty.** "So he turned to the tomb of Mawlay
  Idris to provide the spiritual authority to rebind the body politic together with himself as its
  head" (p. 98, folio 77). Kugle reads the courtiers' speech as an argument about competitive display:
  they "compared the shrine that the Marinids built over Mawlay Idris with the low-roofed and humble
  structure they had built over Ibn Abbad in order to encourage the new Sa'dian ruler to outshine
  them and raise the status of Mawlay Idris as a symbol of their new hold on Fes, far above the
  archetypically Marinid personality of Ibn Abbad" (p. 98, folio 77). The two tombs' divergent fates
  are presented as the outcome of that competition (p. 99, folio 78).
- **"The surface" defended against a Victorian inheritance.** Via Mills and Waghorne: the "'vital
  surfaces' of objects (and of holy persons like South Asian divine kings or Pirs [saints]) have been
  neglected by religious studies and by anthropology as a result of Victorian bowdlerization of
  sanctity and the concomitant devaluation of magic ... 'The very point of the sacred or the divine is
  its suspension in time and space; in other words, its material extensions.'" (p. 99, folio 78,
  notes 50–51).
- **The shrine as theophany.** "The shrine manifests the miracle-producing permeation of God through
  the material world—it is a theophany, in Eliade's terms, an irruption of the sacred into the profane
  through a socially created division in space and time" (p. 100, folio 79).
- **Tomb-building as a stage in early modern world history.** "The building of Mawlay Idris's tomb
  marked an upsurge of patriotism, religious fervor, and political reorganization that led Moroccans
  to defend their Atlantic and Mediterranean coasts from assaults by the Portuguese and Spanish"
  (p. 100, folio 79); Islamic resistance "pushed the Portuguese and Spanish farther afield: around
  the southern tip of Africa into the Indian Ocean and, with more devastating consequences, across
  the Atlantic to the American continents" (p. 100, folio 79). This is a strong causal claim made in
  a single sentence with no cited evidence — flagged under Doubts.
- **The tomb as an entity between object and presence.** "In this sense, the tomb is a phenomenon that
  straddles the binary distinction between 'inanimate object' and 'animate presence.'" (p. 104, folio
  83).
- **Chapter 1's shrines were men's institutions, and that is the pivot into chapter 2.** "In the
  previous chapter, on the bones of Mawlay Idris al- Azhar and other saints, the protagonists were all
  male. ... In particular, the political function of the tomb is manifestly patriarchal, serving as it
  does to justify a monarchy based upon patrilineal genealogy, from a prophet-king, Muhammad, through
  a saint-king, Idris, to his descendants" (p. 104, folio 83).
- **Hagiography as a posthumous and growing record.** "Most saints' stories (in the genre of
  hagiography) are recorded after they die and grow embellished after their death with the fortunes of
  their followers or descendants. We can chart their posthumous career through the object of their
  tombs, their architectural space and the social relations that are established and renewed through
  it" (p. 104, folio 83). This is a source-criticism rule the archive can apply to its own Urdu
  hagiographies.
- **Women saints' stories are "doubly complex."** "[T]hey present not only how holy women overcome
  routine limitations to become saints but also how they overcome the routine limitations their
  society places upon women" (p. 104, folio 83). The chapter's stated programme: "We will ask how
  female saints negotiated the patriarchal order of Islamic public space and perhaps reveal something
  of the limits of that order" (p. 104, folio 83).
- **Why living saints matter more than posthumous ones for this argument.** "In contrast to Mawlay
  Idris, some stories of saints reveal more about them as living social persons and illuminate how
  they came to be regarded as saints while still alive" (p. 105, folio 84).
- **Patriarchy defined (Turner, quoted).** "Patriarchy is 'a system of political authority, based upon
  the household, in which dominant property-owning males control and regulate the lives of subordinate
  members of the household, regardless of their sex or age. . . . Young males are as much
  `subjec[text-layer?]` [subject] to patriarchal authority as women . . . this primitive system of
  patriarchy became linked eventually to monarchical power, since there was an obvious parallel between
  the authority of husbands and that of the monarch over his people. In turn, this husband/monarch
  system of power was related to divine fatherly rule on the part of a benevolent God.'" (pp.
  105–107, folios 84 and 86, note 3). The household → monarchy → God chain is what lets Kugle connect
  chapter 1's dynastic politics to chapter 2's gendered body.
- **The Wahhabis did not invent it.** "The Wahhabis certainly did not invent this regime of puritan
  patriarchal control ... Rather, the Wahhabis interpret these texts more rigidly than other Muslims,
  apply the norms of gender hierarchy more zealously" (p. 107, folio 86).
- **Mind–body dualism works against women.** "Systems that denigrate the human body and seek
  perfection or something beyond it always depend on an imbalance of power between genders. Women are
  more closely associated with the body in patriarchal systems and thus are the victims of family
  control, social inequality, and moral censure when the body in general is denigrated" (p. 108,
  folio 87).
- **The belly is a cultural, not an anatomical, category.** "The belly is an ambiguous region, defined
  not by a particular organ but by a cultural concatenation of images" (p. 103, folio 82); "the womb
  becomes just one site in a network of connected organs and functions through which women are closely
  associated with nourishment and sustenance" (p. 104, folio 83).
- **The Qur'an's ambiguity is the argument's hinge.** "As in most questions put to the Qur'an, the
  answer is ambiguous" (p. 102, folio not stated); "the Qur'an is highly ambiguous about Eve's status
  and her mode of creation" (p. 108, folio 87). Kugle's own conclusion: "The view of Islamic theology
  shaped by patriarchy is not necessarily the view of the Qur'an as scripture in critical dialogue
  with patriarchy" (p. 113, folio 92).
- **Rahman/rahim.** "the name of God, the merciful One (`rabman[text-layer?]` [raḥmān]), echoes the
  word for the womb (`rabim[text-layer?]` [raḥim])" (p. 103, folio 82) — an etymological argument the
  chunk uses to ground maternal reverence.
- **Grammatical gender as evidence.** Self or soul (*nafs*) is feminine, mate (*zawj*) masculine, earth
  (*ard*) feminine, spirit (*ruh*) either; therefore "it becomes very difficult to assert that the
  first soul created was a gendered male being, Adam, from whom a gendered female being, Eve, was
  created in a subsequent, subsidiary, or derivative act of creation" (p. 110, folio 89). Sells is
  quoted against "the language of 'he-God and he-man'" (p. 110, folio 89, note 9).
- **Wadud's two readings, and Kugle's use of them.** Both possible readings of *God created you all
  from one single self then made from it its mate* yield the same conclusion: "In either case, the
  interpretive conclusion would be that Eve and Adam are equal in their proximity to God and share
  the same potential for humane development and moral worth" (p. 109, folio 88, note 8).
- **The fictive etymology of Hawwa'.** "Male interpreters in the classical period created a fictive
  etymology for her name ... the etymology they assert is only one of several possible, and it
  contains a judgment about Eve's ontological state" (p. 112, folio 91). Kugle's counter-etymology:
  "that Eve rather than Adam is more closely linked to 'something living,' for she allows other living
  beings come into existence" (p. 112, folio 91).
- **The dual pronoun as the Qur'an's own correction.** "In contrast, the Qur'an always recounts the
  narrative of their eating of the forbidden tree and its consequences with the dual pronoun,
  specifying that both acted together" (p. 112, folio 91).
- **Mary, not Eve, is the Qur'anic exemplar.** "Although the Qur'an does not explicitly blame Eve, as
  the prototype of the female body and sexuality, it also does not hold her up as an exemplary woman.
  That role is reserved for Mary, the mother of Jesus" (p. 113, folio 92). Mary's status: "her
  conversational interaction with God's angel is as full as that of holy men who are granted the
  status of prophet" (p. 113, folio 92). **The chunk breaks off here.**
