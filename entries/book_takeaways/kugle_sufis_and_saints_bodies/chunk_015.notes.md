## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Initiation as a bodily transaction between guide and disciple.** The manual's first chapter is
  titled "Exposition of Detailed Conditions of Recitations, Exercises and Contemplations of the
  Chishti Masters," and "shows how a spiritual guide (murshid) should embark on teaching a disciple
  (murid) how to recite the names of God in meditation" (p. 247, folio 226). The prescribed procedure:
  "The spiritual guide should set his own heart facing [the `disciples[text-layer?]` **[disciple's]**]
  heart, and apply the rhythmic beat of the essential name of God to [the disciple's] heart. … Hurl
  one hundred and one such rhythmic beats, until desire and the heat of recitation affect the other's
  heart, and it begins to move with the rhythmic movement of recitation." (p. 247, folio 226, chapter
  5 endnote 6) **The number is printed as words, "one hundred and one," not digits.**
- **Graded assignment of practice.** "After this, the disciple should be given a recitation appropriate
  to his or her level of experience, and the disciple will become occupied in the exercises that the
  guide instructs." (p. 247, folio 226) Kugle glosses this as a physician's practice: "Like a doctor,
  he prescribes different variations of recitation for different kinds of disease" (p. 249, folio 228).
- **Succession and the tokens of a shaikh's office.** Authority is delegated in life ("Near the end of
  his life, Shaykh Farid al-Din delegated his authority to a young follower"), and confirmed by
  physical transfer — "his staff, sandals, and other tokens of his authority" sent to Delhi in 1265
  (p. 260, folio 239). The competing claim about who was passed over, and why, is recorded as partisan:
  "Followers of Nizam al-Din insist that Shaykh Sabir was passed over for succession in the Sufi order
  precisely because of his spiritual power." (p. 260, folio 239)
- **A branch's lineage of initiation as an institutional fact.** "It is to `Mucin[text-layer?]`
  **[Muʿin]** al-Din that the Sabiri branch of the Chishti community, to which Imdadullah belonged,
  traces its lineage of spiritual initiation." (p. 258, folio 237) The order's name is likewise traced:
  the Chishtis "took its name from the Khwajagan-i Chisht, the spiritual masters of Chisht" (p. 257,
  folio 236).
- **Circulation and readership of a devotional manual.** "It is a ritual manual that circulated widely
  in the nineteenth century and continues to find an active audience among South Asian Muslims today."
  (p. 247, folio 226, chapter 5 endnote 5) **No print figures, no publisher, no price, no language of
  publication are given: (not stated).**
- **Transmission before manuals: orality and secrecy, and the apologetic burden.** "Before this,
  masters passed such instruction secretly and orally to their disciples, while written treatises were
  mainly intended to defend the practice of meditation as Islamic by showing its theoretical roots in
  the Quran and prophetic example (`surma)[text-layer?]` **[sunna)]**. For this reason, the author goes
  to some pains to justify his setting these practices down in writing." (p. 253, folio 232, chapter 5
  endnote 17) "Imdadullah did not have to apologize, since the saint from Awrangabad had already
  cleared the way for such ritual manuals almost a century and a half before." (p. 253, folio 232)
- **Music and audition as institutional marks of the order.** The Chishti synthesis involves "`Indie`
  `[text-layer?]` **[Indic]** forms of rapturous musical devotion" (p. 257, folio 236); the community's
  characteristic teachings include "refinement through poetry and music" (p. 258, folio 237); Nizam
  al-Din "perfected the Chishti style of musical devotion, poetic interpretation of religion, and ardent
  love mysticism" (p. 260, folio 239). In the manual itself, dhikr is "repeated aloud or silently, with
  motion of the tongue or with motion of the head, along with drumming rhythm, instrumental melody, or
  in contemplative silence" (p. 250, folio 229). **The words samaʿ and qawwali do not appear in this
  chunk.**
- **Retreat as a timed institution.** The forty-day `chilla` is named and its duration given —
  "often continued as an isolated retreat (`chillaj[text-layer?]` **[chilla)]** for forty days in
  succession" (p. 260, folio 239). Retreat is also the disposal of a rival: Sabir was sent "out to the
  wilderness" (p. 260, folio 239).
- **Breath and posture, prescribed in detail.** The chapter of the manual on breath is titled
  "Exhaling and Inhaling and Recitation of God's Essential Name (Allah)" (p. 250, folio 229). Its
  sequence, as quoted: raise the hands and recite a formula "three or five or six times"; "Then sit
  down [cross-legged] and take your right foot's big toe and those near it, and with these toes firmly
  compress the kimas artery [behind the left knee]. Keep both hands on your knees while facing in the
  direction of Mecca (`q i b l a j[text-layer?]` **[qibla)]**" (p. 250, folio 229, chapter 5 endnote
  12). Elsewhere the same artery is placed differently — "the kimas artery, an artery that lies within
  the left knee" (p. 248, folio 227) — see Doubts.
- **The graded typology of recitation, as printed.** "Recitation of the tongue is called body-centered
  recitation (`jismfj[text-layer?]` **[jismī)]**, while thinking is called self-centered recitation
  (`nafsfj[text-layer?]` **[nafsī)]**, whereas contemplation is called heart-centered recitation
  (`qalbf[text-layer?]` **[qalbī]**), and witnessing is called spirit-centered recitation
  (`rtihf[text-layer?]` **[rūḥī]**). Finally clearly beholding divinity face to face is called the
  recitation of the inner secret (sirri)" (p. 250, folio 229, chapter 5 endnote 11).
- **Breath retention as a named technique.** "rigorously 'retaining' one's own breath
  (`habs-i dam][text-layer?]` **[habs-i dam)]**" (p. 251, folio 230); repentance plus "effort in

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **Khannas in the body of Adam.** Attributed by Kugle to the Chishti Sufi Nizam al-Din Awliya in
  Chapter 1: "a parable of how Shaytan's young son Khannas entered into the body of Adam and
  `all this children[text-layer?]` **[all his children]**" (p. 251, folio 230). Kugle notes
  "Imdadullah does not quote this story" (p. 251, folio 230).
- **Satan lodged in the human stomach since the Fall.** Quoted from al-Tirmidhi: "when Satan seduced
  man and made him eat of the tree, Satan found access to his stomach by means of the morsel man ate in
  obedience to him. So Satan settled in his stomach, and since that day what is in man's stomach stinks
  because of Satan's filth." (p. 256, folio 235 → p. 257, folio 236, chapter 5 endnote 25)
- **Shaytan as a serpentine dragon coiled around the heart.** Quoted from Imdadullah: "Shaytan appears
  like a serpentine dragon, and its snakelike head is noxiously poisonous and has pain-inflicting
  barbs." (p. 252, folio 231, chapter 5 endnote 14) And: "envision that the scissors of 'no god' seize
  and capture Shaytan's breath" (p. 252, folio 231, chapter 5 endnote 15).
- **al-Tirmidhi's own recorded ascension.** In his autobiography, as Kugle quotes it: "It was as if
  something happened in my heart and I became happy and took delight in i t . . . until the sky with
  its stars and its moon came down close to the earth. … It seemed to me that I was close to the
  location of God's Throne." (p. 255, folio 234, chapter 5 endnote 22)
- **Farid al-Din's spiritual heat.** "With them, he unleashed an inner spiritual heat and force that
  could either propel or singe others, depending upon the sincerity of their approach toward him."
  (p. 260, folio 239) Attributed by Kugle to Farid al-Din's ascetic practice, without a source named in
  the sentence itself.
- **Shaykh Sabir's miracle-working, and its institutional cost.** "His ability to channel this force
  into performing miracles was so audacious that Shaykh Farid al-Din was wary of granting him
  leadership of the community" (p. 260, folio 239); his power is likened to "the Yogic concept of tapas,
  the inner force awakened by devotional penance" (p. 260, folio 239).
- **Dissolution of the reciter's body into recitation.** "sometimes the whole body will be found to be
  reciting holistically and all the limbs and organs will consort in fellowship with the inner heart.
  This recitation is known as the recitation of combined and united limbs." (p. 249, folio 228, chapter
  5 endnote 9) Presented by Kugle as Imdadullah's instruction, not as a miracle claim.

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **Ritual manuals are a neglected genre, and the neglect is a methodological loss.** "Manuals of
  ritual devotion have received very little attention by scholars in Islamic and Sufi studies. However,
  in the context of Sufi imaginings of the human body, ritual manuals take on a new importance."
  (p. 246, folio 225)
- **Kugle deploys Talal Asad against the Victorian definition of ritual.** Late-nineteenth-century
  Western scholars "with Victorian and Protestant biases, defined ritual as empty habitual action
  through which an objective observer could interpret the practitioner (as one would a text)"; this
  "effectively effaces the practitioners and empties them of subjective authenticity. On the other
  hand, it authorizes and empowers the anthropological analyst" (p. 246, folio 225, chapter 5 endnote
  4). Against it: "prior to the categorization of the modern science of anthropology, ritual was a
  script to be performed through rigor and discipline in order to enact or embody some virtue" (p. 246,
  folio 225).
- **The positive claim: meaning is embodied, not encoded.** "Asad's primary insight is that religious
  meaning does not usually manifest as a system external to those who venerate it through ritual, thus
  inviting analysis to decode it; rather, religious meaning is embodied as virtue, and virtue is
  enacted through the ritual of bodily performance, within the limits and training of authoritative
  disciplines." (p. 247, folio 226)
- **"The Brilliance of Hearts" is offered as a test case.** "Hajji Imdadullah's 'The Brilliance of
  Hearts' illustrates Asad's theoretical critiques, for it is a true ritual manual in the premodern
  sense of the term. It lays out methods of training, scripts for proper recitation, and directories
  for the apt performance of actions that will transform the person who undergoes their rigor."
  (p. 247, folio 226)
- **Dying to the self is read as the acquisition of a habitus.** "By advocating that one die to the
  self, mystics loosen the habitual bonds that bind the self to the socially constituted body … In
  short, mystics offer a method of acquiring a whole new bodily habitus, a new ground of being, driven
  not by selfish desires but rather by the embodiment of virtues." (p. 246, folio 225)
- **Dhikr's elements substitute for the body's.** "In this definition of dhikr, the elements of ritual
  recitation come to stand in for the elements of the physical body. Its rhythm first imitates the beat
  of the heart, then becomes integrated with it, and finally replaces it" (p. 249, folio 228).
- **A textual genealogy, dated and ranked.** The manual "is based upon a past model, the text of
  Shaykh Nizam al-Din Awrangabadi (died 1729 C.E.), whose composition Nizam al-Qulub, 'The Harmonic
  Order of Hearts,' is the first Chishti work on how to perform recollection of God's names" (p. 253,
  folio 232). Kugle reads the titles as a deliberate echo: "Imdadullah named his ritual
  `manuar'The Brilliance of Hearts"[text-layer?]` **[manual "The Brilliance of Hearts"]** in clear
  continuity with prior 'The Harmonic Order of Hearts'" (p. 253, folio 232).
- **The tradition is short but the image is ancient.** "The textual tradition from which Imdadullah's
  ritual manual grows is not terribly old. However, as he invokes the serpent of evil within the blood
  stream, he participates in a tradition that is much, much older." (p. 253, folio 232) The earliest
  instance offered is al-Hakim al-Tirmidhi (p. 253, folio 232).
- **Imdadullah's medical metaphor is read as modern.** "This is his spiritual medicine for ethical
  atherosclerosis, the clogging of the cardiac arteries with `fiberofatty[text-layer?]`
  **[fibro-fatty / "fiber of fatty"; no confident reading]** plaque, as the prime metaphor for
  spiritual murk." (p. 253, folio 232)
- **al-Tirmidhi's mysticism is read as a Gnostic–Qurʾanic weave.** "He wove a specifically Islamic
  form of mysticism from the strands of Gnostic tradition that were still vividly current in his home
  region of Balkh" (p. 254, folio 233); "By presenting the divine attributes as realms of light,
  al-Tirmidhi harks back to Gnostic models" (p. 255, folio 234); "The anxiety that humankind is a light
  nature hidden in matter is of ancient Gnostic origin, though al-Tirmidhi sees it as providing an
  ethical urgency for the moral life that is completely consistent with the
  `Qur'aris[text-layer?]` **[Qur'an's]** message." (p. 256, folio 235) Also read as Neoplatonic:
  the light realms "emerge one from the other in descending manifestations that suggest a Neoplatonic
  system of emanations" (p. 254, folio 233).
- **Hell is immanent in anatomy.** "In this way, al-Tirmidhi sees the reality of hellfire as completely
  immanent in the personality—it is a moral reality played out through the drama of human anatomy and
  the struggle for mastery between different organs of the body." (p. 257, folio 236)
- **A regional typology of early Sufism.** "Al-Tirmidhi was an early exponent of the style of Sufism
  that became known as 'Khurasani,' which developed in northern Iran and Central Asia. It emphasized
  that Satan the tempter dwelled in the bloodstream … In contrast, the Sufism that developed in Iraq,
  which emphasized subtle intellectual theories and urbane civility, saw Satan as a mere pawn of God's
  master plan" (p. 257, folio 236). "The Khurasani Sufi masters were more rugged, more athletic in
  their ascetic exercises, and more pessimistic in their assessment of intellect and reason's power to
  temper the lusts of the body." (p. 257, folio 236)
- **The Chishtis are derived from the Khurasani line, and their motto given.** The community's
  characteristic teachings are "'to be stern with oneself' with rigorous austerities and bodily
  discipline 'while being gentle with all others' in passionately gentle service to others" (p. 257,
  folio 236 → p. 258, folio 237).
- **The Sabiris are distinguished within the order by their bodily rigour.** "More than other branches
  of the Chishti community, the Sabiris maintained a more rigorous and physical emphasis on the body,
  between the extremes of ecstatic rapture and stern control." (p. 259, folio 238) And earlier: "From
  his sayings and example Sabiris absorbed the ancient teaching that evil resided in the body, yet they
  also had a more optimistic assessment of the body's capacity for good." (p. 258, folio 237)
- **An attribution openly called apocryphal, and defended as evidence anyway.** "Although the
  attribution of this epistle to `Mucin[text-layer?]` **[Muʿin]** al-Din Chishti is most likely
  apocryphal, it is important that Chishti Sufis saw spiritualized anatomy as the core of their
  mystical devotion, taught by the saint who first established their community." (p. 259, folio 238)
- **Cross-chapter comparison with Christian women mystics.** "Like the female Christian mystics
  discussed in Chapter 2, Hajji Imdadullah imagines the forces of temptation, seduction, and evil to be
  in the shape of a serpent. However, rather than asserting that the serpent is external to the human
  person, he pictures these forces to be internal to the human body." (p. 252, folio 231)
- **Body as double mirror.** "Just as the human body is a microcosmic mirror of the social world, it is
  also a mirror of the celestial world. In the human body are realms analogous to satanic, selfish,
  angelic, and divine forces." (p. 250, folio 229)
