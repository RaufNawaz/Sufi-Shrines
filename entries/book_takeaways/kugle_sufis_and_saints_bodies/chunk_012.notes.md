## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Royal patronage of a tomb, with a building date.** "Although Akbar had a magnificent shrine built
  over his tomb, Muhammad Ghawth never achieved the level of popularity and political influence that
  he enjoyed during the reign of `Humayan[text-layer?]` [Humayun], Akbar's father"
  (p. 195, folio 174); the plate caption dates the tomb "built around 1565 during the reign of the
  Mughal emperor Akbar" (p. 196, folio 175). The chunk's clearest statement of the pattern the
  archive wants — imperial construction over a saint's grave, with the saint's own political standing
  falling as the building rises.
- **Mosque-building by a saint at his own base.** Muhammad Ghawth lived at Ahmadabad "training
  disciples and building a communal mosque in the city" (p. 195, folio 174; chapter 3 note 71).
- **A madrassa as a refuge and a base.** Wajih al-Din "left his madrassa" for Bharoch and later
  "resumed his teaching" at Ahmadabad (p. 195, folio 174); the persecution "compelled Ghawth's
  followers to publicly ascribe to the sharia and take shelter in the orthodoxy of the madrassa
  setting" (p. 203, folio 182; chapter 4 note 2).
- **A shrine used as overnight lodging by a mendicant.** Shah Hussayn returned "at night to stay in
  the shrine of Lahore's patron saint, `cAli Hujwiri[text-layer?]` [ʿAli Hujwiri] (known as Data Ganj
  Bakhsh)" (p. 206, folio 185).
- **A tomb reported as a live pilgrimage place at the time of writing.** Shah Jamal, "whose tomb is
  still a popular pilgrimage place" (p. 207, folio 186). **No visitor numbers, no ʿurs date, no
  custodian, no offerings: all (not stated).**
- **Court commissioning of a saint's biography.** Prince Salim "ordered one of his courtiers, Bahar
  Khan, to attend to Shah Hussayn daily and write down his teachings and doings to preserve for
  posterity. This work is now lost" (p. 206, folio 185; chapter 4 note 5) — patronage of the record
  rather than of a building, and its loss.
- **Policing and public punishment at Lahore.** The square before the Red Fort hosted executions of
  "rebels against the Mughal emperor, highway robbers, and tax evaders" (p. 203, folio 182); the
  officer is titled **Kotwal** — "the police chief, `Kotwal Malik All[text-layer?]` [Malik Ali]" — and
  "had been looking forward to personally administering the punishment" (p. 204, folio 183). The book
  flags as a question for the chapter "what the status of corporal punishment and impaling is in
  Islamic law (as practiced in Mughal South Asia)" (p. 204, folio 183).
- **Basant and Holi as a public festival with suspended hierarchy.** "This holiday of frolic and play
  was perhaps the only time that two such different people could meet and speak. All conventions can
  be broken, all hierarchies can be overturned, and all restraints can be flouted. People of all
  classes can mingle, and separation between men and women is temporarily suspended"
  (p. 210, folio 189). In the verse: "Everyone gathers together that day without a care / the
  honorable mingle freely with the shameless" (p. 211, folio 190; chapter 4 note 14). **The chunk
  does not attach the festival to any shrine, does not date it in the calendar beyond "spring", and
  names no organiser: all (not stated).**
- **Wine, music and street dance as public practice.** "In addition to drinking wine in public, Shah
  Hussayn took to singing and playing musical instruments while dancing in the streets with his band
  of devoted friends and followers" (p. 207, folio 186); the Nakhshabi passage on wine notes it is
  "prohibited by our religion and has been the subject of many proclamations by the leaders"
  (p. 207, folio 186; chapter 4 note 8).
- **Kingmaking, sainthood and the right to rule.** "Ali Muttaqi also asserted that it is the scholars
  (and especially Sufis who are scholars and jurists by training, loyalty, and social role) who
  uphold the law, and it is the law that establishes the right to rule. Nobody should act as
  kingmaker by claiming sainthood by ineffable experiences" (p. 198, folio 177).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The chains that would not hold.** "As the police drag him into the square, the chains keep falling
  off his wrists and ankles" (p. 204, folio 183) — the book attributes the scene to "a biographical
  poem on the Sufi's life" (p. 204, folio 183; chapter 4 note 3) and hedges the crowd's reaction with
  "Perhaps … perhaps".
- **The child in the Prophet's court, and the plucked beard.** The book introduces it as "An
  apocryphal story" (p. 207, folio 186). Hassu teased him, "for I have never seen you in the court of
  the Prophet [Muhammad]!"; the next day, "when Muhammad held his court in the spirit world, with all
  the prophets and saints in attendance including Shaykh Hassu, a child appeared", sat in the
  Prophet's lap, passed round the assembly, and "naughtily plucked some hairs from Shaykh Hassu's
  beard"; challenged again, "Shah Hussayn silently produced before him a hair from his very own
  beard", and Hassu conceded, "So it was you, was it? Ah well, it was as a child that you got the
  better of me" (pp. 207–208, folios 186 and 187 ["l&J", text-layer?]; chapter 4 note 9). The book
  reads the plucking as a symbolic act: the beard "is an outward sign of piety for Muslim men and a
  way of signifying one's reputation" (p. 208, folio 187 ["l&J", text-layer?]), and the act was "often
  used as a form of criminal punishment and public shame against adult men of high social status"
  (p. 208, folio 187 ["l&J", text-layer?]).
- **The hadith of Satan's throne**, circulated by Ali Muttaqi against Ghawth's ascension: "Satan has a

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **The persecution's record simply stops, and the author says so.** "The unique and detailed record of
  this persecution ends here. No other letters by Ali Muttaqi and his allies exist to reveal how the
  high tensions were resolved. It is unclear how long Muhammad Ghawth spent in hiding in Bharoch or
  why the nobles of that city did not act" (p. 195, folio 174). The reconstruction that follows is
  explicitly conjecture: "we can conjecture that the persecutors saw…", "They might have been
  angered…", "Ali Muttaqi may have perceived…" (p. 196, folio 175).
- **The structural reading of the conflict.** "Beneath all these narrative details, Ali Muttaqi opposed
  Muhammad Ghawth because of a structural issue: he claimed religious legitimacy through a
  "transcendental connection" to God that threatened the historically validated forms of Islamic
  legitimacy" (p. 196, folio 175; chapter 3 note 72) — authority by "personal experience … rather than
  through religious learning or socially legitimized channels of authority" (p. 196, folio 175).
- **Two theologies of the body, set against each other.** "The body is not made sacred and powerful by
  being left behind, outstripped by speculative vision. Rather, it is made sacred and powerful by
  being disciplined and constrained by practical actions that imitate the exemplary pattern of the
  Prophet `(sunnaj.[text-layer?]` [(sunna).]" (p. 198, folio 177). Against it: for Ghawth, "leaving
  the body behind and following one's vision into the heavens … was an empowering experience, granting
  him both spiritual insight and worldly might" (p. 200, folio 179).
- **Premodern psychoanalysis, offered as such.** Of the sultan's washing: "his waste of water relates
  to the blood he spilled in coming to power. His washing (which might be termed
  obsessive-compulsive today) was the outward manifestation of inward anxieties that hindered him from
  exerting mature political power. In this incident of premodern psychoanalysis, Ali Muttaqi addressed
  the obsessive symptom" (p. 199, folio 178). The author concedes the archive's silence: "What strange
  doubts and misgivings could lead the young sultan to hand washing of Macbethian proportions the
  historical chronicles do not specify" (p. 199, folio 178).
- **The chapter's stated method, and its claim to novelty.** "We will apply recent insights from gender
  and queer theory to illumine Shah Hussayn's unconventional life through his own Punjabi poetry and
  untouched sources from the archives of Sufi hagiographies in Persian and Urdu"
  (p. 204, folio 183); "Up to this point, queer theory has hardly interacted with Islamic studies.
  This chapter is one small step in that direction" (p. 205, folio 184). The author's compressed
  definition: "If feminism is the radical notion that women are human beings, then queer theory is the
  radical notion that heterosexuality is not normal" (p. 204, folio 183).
- **Sanctity through transgression, as the chapter's thesis about the body.** "His bodily comportment
  had great theological significance, for he saw the body in all its postures as spiritually vivid,
  and through sensual pleasures he crafted a very rebellious notion of morality as "spiritual play""
  (p. 203, folio 182); "the intimate acts of sexual play were not just personal for Shah Hussayn but
  were integral to his whole approach to Sufi spiritual cultivation" (p. 206, folio 185).
- **The lips as the chapter's organ, against the eyes of chapter 3.** "the lips reveal the spiritual
  secrets of saliva, which represents an intimacy that boldly breaks all routine social and even
  legal boundaries" (p. 203, folio 182); in weeping "the eyes lose their visual acuity and take on the
  character of the lips" (p. 210, folio 189).
- **The author names modern evasiveness as a problem in the literature.** The relationship "has caused
  modern writers to be equivocal and evasive, a concern to which we turn later in this chapter"
  (p. 208, folio 187 ["l&J", text-layer?]) — a forward reference to material outside this chunk.
- **The Shattari aftermath.** The persecution "did work indirectly … As a result, Muhammad Ghawth's
  followers in the Shattari community walked a fine line between outer conformity to orthodox Sunni
  norms and inwardly radical contemplative practices. However, not all Mughal-era Sufis walked such a
  fine line" (p. 203, folio 182; chapter 4 note 2) — the hinge sentence by which the book passes from
  Gujarat to Lahore.

## Doubts

**Header and folio.**
- **The run brief is wrong for two pages of this range.** It states that pp. 201, 202 and 208 print no
  running header. **pp. 201 and 208 do print one**, damaged: `ISO` (= 180) and `l&J` (= 187). Only
  **p. 202** is genuinely headerless, and it is the chapter 4 opener. This is the same failure mode
  the brief describes for the earlier measurement pass — damaged headers counted as blanks — recurring
  inside the brief itself. A human should correct the range's headerless count from three to one.
- **`l&J` on p. 208 uses two glyphs outside the measured substitution set.** `&` in the position of 8
  and `J` in the position of 7 are not among the documented substitutions (`O`/`o`→0, `I`/`l`/`!`→1,
  `Q`/`g`→9, `$`/`S`→5). The reading 187 is given because it equals `PDF − 21` and because the first
  glyph `l`→1 is documented; the second and third glyphs are inferred from position, not from
  evidence. Treat folio 187 as probable, not measured.
- **No printed header anywhere in pp. 195–211 contradicts `PDF − 21`.** All fourteen legible headers
  and both damaged ones agree with it.
- **p. 202's folio is not asserted.** The chapter 4 opener prints no header and the contents table is
  not in this chunk, so every fact from that page is cited `(p. 202, folio not stated)` — including
  the chapter title, Shah Hussayn's dates and the `Lahor` spelling, which are the chunk's three most
  load-bearing facts. A human checking the contents table can supply the folio; this worker will not.

**Mapping.**
- **`madho-lal-hussain` — the book's silsila contradicts the archive row.** Kugle calls Shah Hussayn
  "a Sufi of the Qadiri community" (p. 202, folio not stated) and says "He also joined the Qadiri Sufi
  lineage at a young age" (p. 206, folio 185), while also labelling him a **"Qalandar"** and
  **antinomian (be-shar')** (p. 203, folio 182). The archive row records a different affiliation. The
  two are not reconciled here and both should stand side by side in the entry rather than one being
  chosen.
- **`madho-lal-hussain` — the chunk never writes the compound name.** It has "Shah Hussayn" and,
  separately, "a Brahmin youth named Madho"; it does not join them into "Madho Lal Hussain" or use
  the form "Madho Lal", and it does not mention a shared tomb. The row is mapped on Shah Hussayn as
  its principal figure and on Madho as the named beloved, not on the compound.
- **`shah-jamal` — mapped on thin evidence, and reversibly.** The chunk gives the bare name "Shah
  Jamal" with a tomb that "is still a popular pilgrimage place" (p. 207, folio 186) and no location,
  no dates, no full name and no silsila. The Lahore in the sentence is predicated of his disciple
  Hassu Tell, not of the tomb. The archive contains exactly one Shah Jamal row and it is in Lahore,
  which is why the mapping is made — but this is precisely the "Shah Jamal of Lahore vs. another Shah
  Jamal" case the protocol names, and a human should confirm before a fact from this page enters the
  entry.
- **Krishna — refused, two candidate rows.** "the Hindu god Krishna" (p. 210, folio 189) is the
  principal figure of **two** archive rows, `krishna-mandir-ravi-road` (Lahore) and
  `krishna-mandir-kabari-bazar` (Rawalpindi). The chunk attaches him to neither temple, to no city,
  and to nothing but the meaning of Holi. Per the protocol the name is **not** assigned to either row;
  it is recorded under "Other saints, sites and events" with both candidates named here.
- **The Red Fort in Lahore — bare toponym, refused, with its candidate named.** "the high walls of the
  Red Fort in Lahore" and the square before it (p. 203, folio 182) carry no shrine, tomb, saint, ʿurs
  or custodian. Under interim convention (a) it takes no id. **If that ruling reverses, the row it
  would take is `loh-temple-lava-temple` (Loh Temple / Lava Temple, Lahore Fort, near Alamgiri
  Gate)**, with `samadhi-of-maharaja-ranjit-singh` (Hazuri Bagh, near Lahore Fort) as a second
  candidate — and the existence of two candidates is itself a reason to leave it unmapped now. Note
  also that the chunk says **"Red Fort"**, and does not say the fort is the Lahore Fort of those rows:
  **(not stated)**.
- **Lahore standing alone — bare toponym, refused, and no single candidate can be named.** At pp. 203,
  204, 206, 208 and 210 the city is named with nothing attached. The archive holds roughly twenty
  Lahore rows and the chunk supplies no basis for choosing among them, so the instruction to name the
  candidate row cannot be met for these instances. This is the case the protocol itself gives as the
  reason convention (a) exists: "Lahore" on its own tells an entry nothing about any of the archive's
  Lahore rows. Recorded here so the next reader sees the check was made rather than skipped.
- **Hinglaj does not arise in this chunk.** Neither Hinglaj, Hingol, Lasbela nor any Balochistan place
  is named anywhere in pp. 195–211, so the empty-id row `Shaktipeeth Shri Hinglaj Mata Mandir` is not
  triggered here. Stated only so the standing flag is visibly checked.
- **Rows deliberately refused, with reasons.** `data-darbar` is mapped, but no other Lahore row is:
  Miani Sahib, Bibi Pak Daman, Mauj Darya, Mian Mir, Shah Chiragh, Shah Kamal, Shah Inayat Qadiri,
  Peer Makki, Zanjani Sahib, Mazar-e-Iqbal and the Lahore gurdwaras are **not named in this chunk**.
  Nothing in it should be read as bearing on them. **`shrine-of-shah-inayat-qadiri` in particular is
  not triggered**: the "Shah Hussayn" of this chapter and the Shah Inayat of that row are distinct
  names and the chunk never mentions the latter.
- **Abd al-Qadir Jilani** (p. 195, folio 174) is named as the rightful holder of the title "Ghawth
  al-Thaqalayn" and is **not an archive row's principal figure**; he takes no id.
