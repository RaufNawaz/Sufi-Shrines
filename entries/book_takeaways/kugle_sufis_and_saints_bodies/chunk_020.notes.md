- **madho-lal-hussain**: **the origin of the compound name, stated flatly and correcting a common
  error** — "This nickname has led some writers to falsely conclude that Madho's full name was
  '`Madho Lai[text-layer?]` [Madho Lal].' In fact, Hussayn's nickname was 'Lai Hussayn' alluding to
  his penchant for wearing only red clothing. After he fell in love, his lover's name was added to
  his own, resulting in 'Madho Lai Hussayn.'" (ch. 4 n. 24, p. 328, folio 307).
- **madho-lal-hussain**: **Hussayn's trade and unmarried state, in his own verse as Kugle quotes it** —

  Nor was any matrimony ceremony held for him / He was neither a householder nor a traveler / He was
  neither a believer nor an infidel / He is now just as he was." Sourced to "Alhaq, Forgotten Vision,
  2:230" (ch. 4 n. 26, p. 328, folio 307).
- **madho-lal-hussain**: **caste and lineage — Kugle rejects the Rajput claim.** "New Muslims
  (naw-musalman) from weaver castes converted to join the Islamic community in Punjab slightly later
  than in Hindustan… The castes of weavers who converted to Islam in the medieval centuries had most
  likely been Shudras. Biographers like Ramakrishna have asserted that Shah Hussayn came from a
  Rajput or Kayasth clan than had converted to Islam, and this assertion is based on some later Urdu
  sources that claim that his family belonged to the Dahda Rajput clan. However, these castes did not
  engage in weaving… The stories of Hussayn's 'noble' lineage are later accretions to make his image
  more acceptable to society, as are the stories of Madho's 'conversion' to Islam." (ch. 4 n. 52,
  p. 329, folio 308; the sentence completes at the top of the same page's second column).
- **madho-lal-hussain**: **Hussayn placed in a named devotional lineage** — "In his assertion that
  there were more commonalities than differences between Islamic and Hindu devotion, Shah Hussayn
  belongs to the tradition of Kabir and Guru Nanak. Like them, he found vernacular and folk poetry to
  be the best vehicle to expression this fusion of Sufi and bhakti ideals" (ch. 4 n. 51, p. 329,
  folio 308). "to expression" is as printed. On Guru Nanak see Doubts.
- **madho-lal-hussain**: **Hussayn identifies with Hir against Ranjha in two poems.** "In another poem,
  Shah Hussayn refers to his red tattered clothes as the dress of a bride, like the heroine
  `Hlr[text-layer?]` [Hir], who longs for her ideal bridegroom, Ranjha. 'To hell with your white
  garb/ better is the rag of the faqir / only she is wedded who stands up/ to dance in his courtyard

  I myself became Ranjha / May all the people call me Ranjha and not Hlr anymore / I have finally
  found the beloved I sought for so long / It was after meeting the sadhus, says Hussayn/ that the

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Dargahs as manuscript repositories.** The bibliography's unpublished-manuscript section names
  three shrines as holding institutions, alongside libraries: "Ahmadabad: Pir Mohammedshah Dargah,
  152, fols. 1-15" and "Ahmadabad: Pir Mohammadshah Dargah, 1503,1216, and 1217"; "Chishti,
  `Rashld[text-layer?]` [Rashid] al-Din Lala. Mukhbir al-Awliya'. Ahmadabad: Nasir Bagh Dargah."
  (all p. 338, folio not stated); and "Delhi: Shah Abul Khayr Dargah, 21 Tasawwuf, fols. 1-9"
  (p. 339, folio 318). **A shrine functioning as an archive is an institutional fact the archive can
  use.** Note the internal inconsistency "Pir Mohammedshah" / "Pir Mohammadshah" on one page.
- **Lahore's Punjab University Library holds two of the book's Panjabi-Sufi manuscripts** —
  "Mahmud ibn Muhammad Pir. Haqiqat al-Fuqam. Lahore: Punjab University Library, 3253/248 Farsi" and
  "Khweshgi, Abdullah. Maarij al-Wilaya. Lahore: Punjab University Library, H-25/7765 Farsi"
  (p. 338, folio not stated); a third, "`Ghayat al-Ma'mulffl -Suluk wal -Wusul[text-layer?]`
  [Ghayat al-Ma'mul fi al-Suluk wa al-Wusul]. Lahore: Punjab University Library, 4862/1842,
  fols. 9-11" (p. 339, folio 318).
- **Sufi bodily practice as institutionalised technique.** Imdadullah's *Diya' al-Qulub* is cited

  "'On the Instruction of Recitation'" (n. 6), "'Beneficial Moral Results of Recitation'" (n. 8),
  "'Exposition of Pronounced Recitation by Exhaling and Inhaling'" (n. 12), "'Discourse on Recitation

  religious academy of Hyderabad, the Madrassa Nizamiyya." (ch. 5 n. 64, p. 333, folio 312), with the
  Deoband Academy's role "to the communal consciousness of Indian Muslims under colonial rule"
  referred to Metcalf, Kucukcan and Mansurnoor.

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **A wine-and-embrace initiation of Madho by Shah Hussayn, as Kugle quotes it from Nur Ahmad
  Chishti's *Tahqiqat-i Chishti* (not from the primary source, and given by Kugle as an example of
  censorship, not as his own account)** — "So the saint, while drinking wine, embraced Madho and made
  him arrive at the truth (wasl bi-haqq), making Madho into a complete and perfected saint himself
  without any rigors or strenuous labors." Kugle comments: "The erotic encounter of kissing and
  embracing is erased; the only trace of a sexual element is the term 'arriving' (wasl), which can
  mean arriving at a place, arriving into the loving intimacy of a lover, or engaging in a sexual
  union until arriving at a climax." (ch. 4 n. 22, p. 327, folio 306).
- **Shaykh Makhu's cave-retreat and the gender-changed beloved** — see Other saints above; the Urdu
  translation of *Gulzar-i Abrar* turns the young man Hansu into "a beautiful young woman" (ch. 4
  n. 25, pp. 327–328, folios 306–307). Attributed by Kugle to the published translation, against the
  unpublished Persian.
- **An etymology of the shahada as a physical object** — "In Arabic script, the word 'no' (la) looks
  like a pair of scissors, with two blades attached at the bottom and opening upward. Sufis often
  compare 'No god but God' to scissors or a broom, based on its calligraphic appearance" (ch. 5
  n. 15, p. 331, folio 310). Attributed to Sufi usage generally, with Schimmel cited.

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **Kugle's central source-critical claim in the Lahore chapter, stated in a note rather than the
  text**: "Modern Urdu sources have filtered out vivid erotic information from this Persian source
  that they supposedly translate and transmit." (ch. 4 n. 22, p. 327, folio 306). He demonstrates it
  three ways: the Babupur scene compressed to one line in *Tahqiqat-i Chishti*; the dancing charge
  "elided entirely" in the same work's translation of the list of offences (n. 57); and *Khazinat
  al-Asfya* of 1865, where "Madho is mentioned only once, in an accusation against Hussayn that is
  never confirmed."
- **Kugle argues the erasure begins early and is not only colonial**, but that Victorian morality
  intensified it — "It reflects the liberality of Shah Hussayn's era that mention of his
  homosexuality is not given much importance, in contrast to Victorian mannerisms of the nineteenth-
  and twentieth-century Urdu reports that suppress mentioning his sexuality at all." (ch. 4 nn. 57–58,
  pp. 329–330, folios 308–309).
- **Kugle charges a named scholar with suppressing a manuscript she had access to** — "It is clear
  that Ramakrishna knew of the existence of Haqiqat al-Fuqara' but chose not to consult it, although
  she did consult other manuscripts at the same archive. This provides an example of how 'queer
  themes' in Islamic culture are suppressed and marginalized by the mentality and intention of
  researchers." (ch. 4 n. 29, p. 328, folio 307), quoting her conclusion: "as far as poetry can help
  us, we can find no immoral flaw in Lai Hussayn's love for Madho."
- **He rejects "un-natural" as an analytic category** — "'Un-natural' is Ramakrishna's term; it smacks
  of Victorian British morality and has no analytic value. She acknowledges that, to these two
  medieval Punjabis at least, their sexual relation seemed natural enough." (ch. 4 n. 28, p. 328,
  folio 307).
- **A caste argument used against hagiographic genealogy** (ch. 4 n. 52, p. 329, folio 308): weaving
  castes were "most likely… Shudras", "conversion rarely affected a caste's means of livelihood", so
  the Rajput/Kayasth and Dahda Rajput claims are "later accretions".
- **On Sufi–Yogic exchange**, Kugle points to a literature rather than arguing it here — "See Rizvi,
  'Sufis and Natha Yogis,' and Carl Ernst, 'Chishti Meditation Practices,' in Lewisohn and Morgan,
  Late Classical Persianate Sufism, 344-59; 'Situating Sufism and Yoga'; and 'Islamization of Yoga.'
  In addition, see contributions on Bengali Sufi-Yogic interactions in the scholarship of Muhammad
  Enamul Haq and Tarafdar." (ch. 5 n. 40, p. 332, folio 311). He also flags textual instability:
  "There is considerable difference between the two manuscripts of the same text, and Carl Ernst has
  undertaken a comparative translation of them both." (ch. 5 n. 27, p. 331, folio 310).
- **He suspects the Chishti breath-syllables may be pure sound** — "It may even be that the syllables
  did not make semantic sense but were purely sound mantras to provide rhythmic control of the
  breath." (ch. 5 n. 39, p. 332, folio 311), with a stemma: the "Shattari Treatise" → "The Harmonic
  Order of Hearts" → Imdadullah's "The Brilliance of Hearts".
- **He reads Wahhabism as a body-theology, not a purism** — "Wahhabis' literalist reading of scripture
  and refusal to interpret through reason, analogy, or metaphor forces them (like their
  `HanballI`/`Hanball[text-layer?]` [Hanbali] predecessors) to insist that God has body parts: the
  hands, feet, face, and seat that are mentioned in the Qur'an." (Conclusion n. 48, p. 335, folio
  314); and he endorses reading them as Khariji rather than "puritans" or Islamic "protestants"
  (n. 8, p. 333, folio 312).
- **He treats the Wahhabi use of pre-Islamic Arab lore as tendentious** — "Ibn Abd al-Wahhab rather
  predictably neglects to mention that they are preserved in Ibn `al-Kalbl[text-layer?]` [al-Kalbi]'s
  works, preferring simply to say that they were related by Ibn Abbas." (Conclusion n. 31, pp. 334–335,
  folios 313–314).
- **A medical aside offered without a source** — "Arterial clogging begins at around age 5 and
  eventually develops into the leading cause of death in adults, especially in the modern situations
  of overconsumption." (ch. 5 n. 16, p. 331, folio 310). No citation is given: **(not stated)**.
