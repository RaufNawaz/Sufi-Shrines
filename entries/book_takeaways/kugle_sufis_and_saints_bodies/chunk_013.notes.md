## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Tomb and grave.** Hussayn "had prepared his own grave next to their home" (p. 219, folio 198);
  Madho "returned to Lahore to live at the tomb of Hussayn for thirty-five more years" and "upon his
  death, Madho was buried directly beside Hussayn" (p. 219, folio 198). The grave's own construction,
  patron, cost and date are **(not stated)**.
- **Tombstone.** Madho's Hindu given name "is inscribed upon his tombstone today" (p. 219, folio 198).
  Reported in the present tense as a modern observation; the wording of the inscription is
  **(not stated)**.
- **Succession.** Madho "become[text-layer?] [became] the spiritual successor of Hussayn's saintly
  authority" (p. 219, folio 198). **This is the only succession statement in the range.** No later
  successor, no sajjada nashin, no custodial family, no Auqaf or state department, no dispute over
  custody, and no ʿurs: all **(not stated)**.
- **Initiation as an institution.** Hussayn "did initiate many companions in his playful brand of
  mystical devotion" (p. 217, folio 196); the initiation of Madho is described as a discrete event
  the poet "narrates … only in relation to their "initiation," implying that it was a one-time
  instance of ritual union" (p. 216, folio 195). The transmission medium named is the lips and a
  shared cup — "Many Sufis held that a kiss on the lips between men was the vehicle for transmission
  of sacred power, rather than a routine expression of sexual desire." (p. 215, folio 194; chapter 4
  note 20), and the tradition "that mystical insight can be passed on through bodily contact,
  especially through a shared drink or saliva" (p. 216, folio 195).
- **Residence.** Hussayn and Madho "lived together in complete intimacy" (p. 217, folio 196) at
  Baghabanpur (p. 215, folio 194); the earlier private meeting was "in a hut in the village of
  Babupur, far from Lahore" (p. 212, folio 191). Followers were resident enough to watch "through the
  keyhole" (p. 215, folio 194).
- **Dress and appearance as markers.** "Shah Hussayn's red clothes and his face shaved of facial hair
  marked him as a Qalandar." (p. 220, folio 199). The wider trope: "The ecstatic Sufi who has
  renounced all concern for social convention already wears stained clothes" (p. 214, folio 193).
- **Occupational and caste economy.** Hussayn "belonged to a community of weavers, who had as a caste
  converted to Islam only a few generations before" (p. 228, folio 207; chapter 4 note 52). Qalandar
  economics as the book frames it: "They renounce productive work, refuse reproduction of the family,
  and resist the labor it takes to conform to religious custom and law. Those who oppose Qalandars see
  their lives as waste—wasting their time, living as parasites off productive society" (p. 221,
  folio 200).
- **Patronage.** "New Muslims" supported and patronised by "Rajput noble families", who in turn
  supported "the growing Mughal empire in the sixteenth century"; "Mughal royalty provided religious
  justification for their rule through forms of synthesis of Islamic Sufi and Indie bhakti devotion,
  in court life, literature, music, and dance as well as in devotional communities." (p. 228,
  folio 207). **None of this is attached to Hussayn's own shrine or to any grant.**
- **State and policing.** Hussayn "confronted the police chief and other critics" (p. 225, folio 204);
  "We will see later how Mughal authorities received his street antics." (p. 228, folio 207). The
  Qalandar precedent: Sufi leaders "urged political leaders to suppress them by force" and urged the
  Mongols "to execute the "deviant renunciants."" (p. 220, folio 199).
- **Manuscript custody and access.** The primary poem survives as an "unpublished original manuscript
  at Punjab University in Lahore" (p. 218, folio 197; chapter 4 note 27), and Kugle's explanation of
  the scholarly silence is one of access: writers "have been unable to access the records that display
  it" (p. 218, folio 197).
- **Women's domestic economy as poetic material** — the kafis turn on the choice "to spin and weave
  dutifully in the hope of finding a husband or to abandon the bounded courtyard of respectability",
  and on dower: "She needs to earn the marriage, for her family will need to pay the dower to a
  groom." (pp. 223–224, folios 202–203).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **Haridwar in one step.** "Madho defied them and stayed back in Lahore, but Hussayn, in a miracle,
  took him from Lahore to Haridwar in one step." (p. 212, folio 191; chapter 4 note 15). Kugle reads
  it as a trace of settlement: "This miracle may contain hints at reconciliation between Hussayn and
  Madho's family" (p. 212, folio 191).
- **Khidr and the drink from the palm.** "Shah Hussayn himself is reported to have met the perennial
  spiritual guide Khidr in his boyhood. Khidr poured out some water from his jug into his own palm,
  and Hussayn drank from his hand. This transmission of spiritual blessing allowed him to finish
  memorizing the Qur'an with ease and eventually pushed him toward sainthood in his adult life."
  (p. 216, folio 195; chapter 4 note 23). Attributed by the book as a report ("is reported to have").
- **The river austerity.** "He used to stand up to his waist in the Ravi River, lips blue from cold,
  while reciting the Qur'an all day." (p. 221, folio 200) — given as "the portrait of Shah Hussayn as
  an ascetic Sufi before his flash of insight", i.e. as the hagiographic picture of the period before
  his opening, not as a miracle.
- **The keyhole and the followers' doubts.** "some of Hussayn's followers observed their sexual play
  through the keyhole and were troubled for a time by doubts about its propriety. The poet defends
  their sexual play as a "spiritual initiation" in which Hussayn passed on to Madho the spark of
  divine love through touch" (p. 215, folio 194).
- **Kugle's own characterisation of the source's genre.** "Although this account is clearly
  hagiographic, leaving no doubt that Shah Hussayn is a saint and will prevail, it describes this
  seduction in delightfully realistic tones." (p. 213, folio 192).
- **Ras Khan's instantaneous conversion**, from Hindu bhakti hagiographies: "Upon gazing at their image
  of Krishna, the dark-skinned beautiful cowherd with his flute raised provocatively to his pursed
  lips, Ras Khan was immediately and profoundly converted." (p. 227, folio 206; chapter 4 note 48).
- **A later hagiographic claim Kugle rejects.** "Later biographers claim that Madho converted to Islam
  at the hand of Hussayn"; Kugle calls it "another manifestation of the "missionary position" that

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The central claim of the chunk.** "It forces us to acknowledge that Shah Hussayn's sexual
  orientation was queer: if using the modern term "gay" to describe him seems anachronistic for a
  premodern and non-Western personality, "queer" is a more open label for his deliberate
  unconventionality." (p. 217, folio 196). Kugle's evidence is the poetic biography's explicitness
  plus the cohabitation and the absence of marriage (pp. 217–218, folios 196–197).
- **On the historiography.** "Most modern scholarship has shown a deliberate misrecognition of the
  queer erotic dimension of Shah Hussayn's relationship" (p. 217, folio 196); writers "either fail to
  mention the possibility or apologetically assert that such love was surely "platonic." Sometimes
  they even translate the gender of the beloved surreptitiously, transforming a young man into a young
  woman" (p. 217, folio 196; chapter 4 note 25). "The personality of Shah Hussayn is unique in
  offering vivid evidence that this Sufi's love affair with a younger man was not only erotic and
  openly sexual but to a large extent accepted as such by his contemporaries." (p. 217, folio 196).
- **Against Lajwanti Ramakrishna**, named as author of "the definitive book on Shah Hussayn and his
  poetry": she treats the homoerotic relation as rumour — "many people had become suspicious of the
  un-natural relationship" (p. 218, folio 197; chapter 4 note 28) — writes that "Hussayn tried for
  sixteen years to possess the lad", speculates that Madho moved in "not for love but only for
  shelter, since he had been "excommunicated" by his parents and the Brahmin community after having
  eaten with Hussayn and his companions", and claims a Sufi's love for a young man is of "foreign
  origin" from Persia and "is opposed to the Indian concept of Divine Love". Kugle: "Ramakrishna's
  whole biography of Shah Hussayn and Madho is an exercise in bad faith" (p. 218, folio 197; chapter 4
  note 29), and he objects that Hussayn "was not `interesting[text-layer?]` [interested] in possessing
  Madho but only in loving him" (p. 218, folio 197).
- **Against Shuja Alhaq**, "more frank in his assessment": Alhaq `writes/'Hussayn[text-layer?]`
  [writes, "Hussayn] became enamored of his [Madho's] beauty at the first sight of him" but dates the
  attraction "probably in the later years" of Hussayn's life, "thereby erasing the element of
  eroticism and mutual commitment between the two" (p. 219, folio 198; chapter 4 note 30).
- **Scholars Kugle endorses.** "Some contemporary Western scholars, mainly literary experts, have
  moved beyond moralism and see Shah Hussayn's sexuality as part of his literary talent; among these
  scholars are Anna Suvorova and Christopher Shackle." (p. 220, folio 199; chapter 4 note 33). Yogi
  Sikand, "a younger Indian scholar", in a gay and lesbian journal "has reclaimed Shah Hussayn as a
  gay Sufi": "It is possible and indeed very likely that Hussayn's greatly unconventional involvement
  with Madho had a deep impact on his own thinking, his mystical poetry and most all his religious
  life." (p. 220, folio 199; chapter 4 note 34).
- **Kugle's caution against presentism.** "There is certainly merit in delving into the archives of the
  Islamic past for figures who might have new importance in the identity politics of the present.
  However, it must be stressed here that although Shah Hussayn may have been gay … as he was a
  Qalander, his personality was also drawing on popular currents of religious devotion." (p. 220,
  folio 199).
- **Qalandars via Karamustafa and Ewing.** "Ahmed Karamustafa calls [them] "deviant renunciants" in
  his perceptive study of their movement" (p. 220, folio 199); "Katherine Ewing, for instance, calls
  them "the abject" created by the ideological formation of the Sunni-Sufi world order", quoting her:
  ""by operating as an inverted mirror that challenges the background understandings of daily life,
  the qalandar potentially exposes to scrutiny basic issues of subjective experience for the ordinary
  person … [yet paradoxically] functions as a foundation on which the lawful Muslim subject rests… .
  The qalandar `is'not-Me.'[text-layer?]` [is "not-Me."]"" (p. 221, folio 200; chapter 4 note 36).
  "Skillfully `usingjulia[text-layer?]` [using Julia] Kristeva's theoretical term "abject," she
  documents the Qalandars' persistent critique of Islamic moralism into the present, in Pakistan as
  elsewhere in South Asia." (p. 221, folio 200).
- **The theory of play.** "The fundamental root of the conflict between Qalandars and conventional
  Sufis is over the importance of "play."" (p. 221, folio 200); Qalandars counter bourgeois work with
  "holy waste" (p. 221, folio 200). The Qur'anic verse *The life of the world is nothing but play and
  pleasurable distraction* is read as bearing "two contradictory meanings" (p. 221, folio 200). Kugle
  then imports Johan Huizinga's five formal elements of play (p. 225, folio 204; chapter 4 note 44),
  quoting him — "Ritual grafts itself upon it; but the primary thing is and remains play." (p. 226,
  folio 205; chapter 4 note 45) — and concludes that Huizinga "offers us a framework through which to
  understand his erotic playfulness as a form of spiritual cultivation, rather than just an individual
  expression of sexual orientation (which it also most likely was)." (p. 226, folio 205). Eliade's
  *homo religioso* and Huizinga's *homo ludens* are set against each other (p. 225, folio 204), and
  David Kinsley's *The Divine Player* is cited for Krishna devotion as play (p. 226, folio 205).
- **The synthesis argument, and its limit.** "Shah Hussayn is a Muslim but in a very Hindu context,
  and his form of sexual-spiritual play draws on both religious contexts." (p. 226, folio 205); "In
  this commitment to the spirituality of play, Shah Hussayn found a Qur'anic equivalent to the bhakti
  practice of ras-lila" (p. 228, folio 207). But: "Shah Hussayn was not merely a bhakta in Islamic
  disguise, therefore easily ejected from the community of Muslims. Rather, he draws upon persistent
  themes in the Islamic Sufi environment" (p. 228, folio 207). The argument is cut off mid-sentence at
  the chunk's end.
- **On stained clothes as a poetic register.** Kugle distinguishes "the chaste register of the
  tear-stained cloth" (Mir Dard) from "the ecstatic register of the wine-stained cloth" (Hafiz), and
  reads Hussayn's line about accusations as trading on the ambivalence (p. 214, folio 193; chapter 4
  note 18).
- **On the South Asian Sufi pattern generally.** "the Sufi mystical tradition among the Muslims of
  South Asia is rife with stories of saintly persons "falling in love" with young men", whose
  chroniclers "normally remain silent about whether such friendships actually had a sexual dimension";
  "These loves usually transgressed the boundaries of age, class, ethnicity, and even religious
  persuasion." (p. 217, folio 196).
