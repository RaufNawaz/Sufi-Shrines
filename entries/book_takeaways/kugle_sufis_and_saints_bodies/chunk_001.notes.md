## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Shrine custodianship as an office with almost no material apparatus** — the Borabanda custodian's
  "office" is "a bare room", his "uniform" a T-shirt and lungī, and "the only trappings of power
  that he had were the yellow shawl … whose faded brilliance displayed his allegiance to the
  Chishti way, and an intangible air of authority born from experience" (p. 12, folio not stated).
  Useful to the archive as a description of how sajjada-type authority is signalled by cloth colour
  rather than wealth — though the book does not use the word *sajjada nashin* here: **(not stated)**.
- **Tomb-covering as recurring practice** — "New cloth covering for the tomb of Mawlay Idris
  al-Azhar in Fas 54" (p. 9, folio not stated). The chunk gives no date, donor or interval for the
  renewal: **(not stated)**.
- **The shrine as sanctuary from a husband** — "A woman takes refuge from her husband by fleeing to
  a graveyard containing the tomb of a Muslim saint 85" (p. 9, folio not stated). A plate caption
  only; the episode itself is outside this chunk.
- **Tomb architecture as the subject of a chapter** — Chapter 1 analyses "a tomb shrine built over
  the long-dead but still-present body of the patron saint of Fas" (p. 27, folio 6), and the
  chapters together show "not just the architectural presence of dead bodies but also a range of
  other manifestations" (p. 27, folio 6).
- **The tariqa as a cross-class institution and a network** — "Membership in a tariqa brings
  together individuals from many levels of society, including members of the scholarly
  `andjuristic[text-layer?] [and juristic]` class as well as members of craft guilds and mercantile
  networks" (p. 27, folio 6). This is the chunk's one economic claim about Sufi institutions.
- **Saints as political office-holders** — "in premodern times saints were figures central to
  religious life in Islamic societies, in which they often played the role of political leaders and
  moral exemplars" (p. 22, folio 1); each analysis "focuses on the very public nature of saints'
  bodies and their importance in political events" (p. 27, folio 6).
- **The book's own funding and archives, useful for the grant dossier** — written "while supported
  by a fellowship from the International Institute for the Study of Islam in the Modern World at the

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **karamat defined, and attributed to the Sufi communities rather than asserted by the author** —
  followers "learned from them by imitating their example and admiring their supranormal acts of
  generosity and insight (karamat)" (p. 26, folio 5, with note 1).
- **The skin testifying against its owner at the judgment** — the Qur'anic passage the book takes as
  its epigraph and first image: "You cannot hide / Now that your hearing and your sight / And your
  skin bears witness against you. —Qur'an 41:22" (p. 22, folio 1), expanded on the next page: the
  skins "answer, \"God has caused us to speak, as God causes all things to speak for God created
  you in the first instance and you revert inevitably to God!\" (Q 41:19-21)" (p. 23, folio 2).
- **Adam's name derived from skin** — "the same name in Arabic is linked to adim, a skin or surface.
  The linguistic logic is that dust is the surface of the earth, the skin of the earth, from which
  Adam was made" (p. 23, folio 2). Given as Kugle's own etymological reading, not as a report of
  a community's belief.
- **Mystical ascension as a contested miracle** — the mi'raj plate "The Prophet Muhammad ascending
  up through the seven heavenly spheres 128" (p. 9, folio not stated) and the gloss "the controversy
  of bodies in mystical ascension in Chapter 3" (p. 27, folio 6). The controversy itself is not
  narrated here.

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **Subject and stance stated in the first sentence** — "This book discusses Islamic images of the
  human body from the distinct perspective of Sufi understandings of Islam. In particular, it
  examines the role of saints and their bodies in Sufi communities" (p. 22, folio 1).
- **Sufism's aim is set against theology and law, which is the book's organising contrast** — it
  "aims not just to understand God, like theological discourse, or to obey God, like legal
  discourse, but also to love and be loved by God" (p. 22, folio 1).
- **A four-discourse genealogy out of the ascetic reaction to empire.** Theology, law and mysticism
  "emerged in roughly the same era", their advocates interacting "sometimes in cooperation and
  sometimes in competition", and all vying with "a fourth discourse, that of philosophy adopted
  from Hellenism" (p. 22, folio 1). The ascetic movement split three ways: "Moderates … reacted to
  imperial prerogative by creating Islamic legal discourse"; "Radicals … belittled practical action
  in the world in favor of other-worldly devotion"; "Revolutionaries … revolted politically against
  the imperial elite, as in the Khariji and `Shfi[text-layer?] [Shīʿi]` movements" (p. 24, folio 3),
  summed up as "When ascetic scrupulousness and empire cooperated, they created Islamic law. When
  ascetic righteous anger and empire clashed, they gave rise to revolt" (pp. 24–25, folios 3–4).
- **Sufism as the refusal of deferred eschatology.** "Sufis denied the deferral of \"the next
  world\" (al-Akhira)"; jurists "accepted this gap of deferred time", theologians "as an ontological
  gulf"; Sufis asked "Why try to plug the gap with systems of law or doctrines of belief when one
  can plunge into the depths of the gap itself?" (p. 25, folio 4). The doctrinal stake is named:
  anything less "was admitting that the phenomenal world has an independent autonomy in separation
  from God, which limits God's nature as \"the One real,\" al-Haqq" (p. 25, folio 4).
- **The causal claim that carries the whole book: immanence produces a valuing of the body.** "It is
  this affirmation of God's immanence and fascination with God's presence that causes Sufis to value
  the body in ways fuller and deeper than other Muslim authorities" (p. 25, folio 4).
- **Sainthood defined through love, not status.** Saints are "the Friends of God (awliya' allah),
  whom God leads through the intuition of selfless love into states of knowledge that are beyond
  book learning or rational discourse" (p. 26, folio 5); they are "those given such power through
  their intimate love (wilaya)" and "their life stories are the proof of divine favor" (p. 26, folio
  5). The community of love emerges "from the hidden recesses of society with no regard to outward
  status, ethnic pedigree, or rational book learning" (p. 26, folio 5).
- **An explicit objection to translating *tariqa* as "order".** "Western scholars often translate
  tariqa into English as a \"Sufi order\" … However, this use of \"order\" is too reminiscent of
  Catholic monastic orders, from which a Sufi `tarlqa[text-layer?] [tarīqa]` differs in many crucial
  ways, not least in their approach to the body and sexuality. Literally, the tariqa is a \"way\""
  (p. 26, folio 5). Its double nature is then given: "In its structure, it is a lineage,
  representing the transmission of mystical initiation from the Prophet Muhammad through a chain of
  saints"; "In its function, the tariqa offers a distinct method of initiation, recitation, and
  religious exercises" (p. 27, folio 6).
- **Method: hagiography treated as an archive, with its genres enumerated.** "this study draws its
  data from the archives of Sufi literature of many genres. In particular it explores hagiographic
  texts"; hagiographies "incorporate fields of saintly biography, family genealogy, manuals of
  ritual, guides to pilgrimage, accounts of sacred architecture, interpretation of scripture, and
  assertion of community norms, all amply leavened with poetry" (p. 28, folio 7). Directly useful
  to this archive's own source criticism.
- **Why Sufism rather than law or gender** — Sufism "invites comparison in ways more open and
  accessible to contemporary readers"; Sufis "engage common concerns of suffering and anxiety … and
  the terror of confronting death in ways more profound than theologians or jurists"; and as mystics
  they "stress the immediacy of religious experience with, through, and in the body (dhawq, or
  tasting intuitively)" (p. 28, folio 7).
- **A periodisation of "popular Islam" with a named end point.** "Sufism encompasses what might be
  called \"popular Islam\" in the period from the eleventh century until the middle of the
  nineteenth century, when Sufis were increasingly displaced or brazenly assaulted by extremist
  Wahhabi or literalist Salafi interpretations of Islam, a conflict that features prominently in the
  concluding chapter of this book" (p. 28, folio 7).
- **The comparative frame, and a stated divergence from Geertz.** Bringing together "the far west of
  the Mediterranean and the wide east of the Indian Ocean echoes Clifford Geertz's aspiration in his
  popular Islam Observed, which did more than any other study to popularize an anthropology of
  Islamic culture"; but "This study however, diverges from Geertz's message considerably in its
  treatment of saints and Sufi mysticism and even in its assessment of modernity" (pp. 28–29, folios
  7–8).
- **A deliberately modest claim, and the reason given for it.** "This study makes no grand claims
  about Muslims and the human body or the historical evolution of Islamic discourses about the body.
  Its aim is much more modest and tentative (necessarily so, considering the paucity of studies in
  this field)" (p. 29, folio 8). The preface repeats it: "So this book is incomplete, and necessarily
  so. It is an initial exploration into a vast subject" (p. 13, folio xii).
- **The polemical target is Orientalism.** The study "refutes the Orientalist assertion that Islam is
  essentially a rigidly abstract and ascetically rigorous religion, uniquely disengaged from the
  corporal, without ritual depth, and devoid of spiritual vigor" (p. 29, folio 8).
- **The bodies were chosen for range, and the range is stated bluntly.** "They include noble and
  plebian bodies, female and male bodies, living and dead bodies, heterosexual and queer bodies,
  conventionally clothed and transvestite bodies, and even naked bodies", some of them "far beyond
  the bounds of what is routinely thought of as \"Islamic,\" either by `todays[text-layer?]
  [today's]` increasingly rigid norms or even by the much more flexible standards of premodern
  Islamic societies" (p. 29, folio 8).
- **The one-organ-per-chapter device defended as hermeneutic, not reductive.** "the thrust of this
  book is entirely the opposite: it asserts the holistic nature of the human body as a cultural
  vehicle that integrates physicality, mentality, and cosmology. The decision to focus on a single
  body part is purely hermeneutic" (pp. 29–30, folios 8–9), and it "follows the discourse of the
  Qur'an, which tends to address the body in its parts, allowing parts to stand in for the whole,
  even to the point of presenting various body parts as having their own voice" (p. 30, folio 9).
- **A critique of the existing field, and of why women's dress dominates it.** "It is surprising how
  little has been written on Islamic conceptions of the body. Islamicists tend to focus on law when
  dealing with the body, specifically the legal regulations that deal with women's bodies" (p. 30,
  folio 9); "Muslim women's bodies have emerged as the site of contention and the gauge of change,
  whether as the object of the colonial gaze, the goal of secularist reforms, the concern of
  traditionalist reaction, or the target of fundamentalist resurgence" (p. 30, folio 9). The
  literature on dress is "a symptom of a deeper rift": "In times of political crisis, bodies are no
  longer accepted as a natural given but rather seen as highly charged with symbolic, social, and
  ethical significance" (p. 30, folio 9).
- **A caveat on the provenance of his own theory.** "Though such insights come from Western
  scholarship, they allow us to circle back to the Qur'an and its narratives about Adam's breath
  with new insights" (p. 30, folio 9).
- **The foreword's argument, which is the series' and not Kugle's** — the study of Islam "is often
  marred by an overly fractured approach that frames Islam as the polar opposite of what
  \"Westerners\" are supposed to represent and advocate" (p. 10, folio not stated); the twin goals
  are "(`I[text-layer?] [1]`) to locate Islam in multiple pasts across several geo-linguistic,
  sociocultural frontiers, and (2) to open up a new kind of interaction between humanists and social
  scientists" (p. 11, folio x); "Because Muslim networks have been understudied, they have also been
  undervalued" (p. 11, folio x).

## Doubts

- **The `madho-lal-hussain` mapping rests on a name and nothing else, and should be treated as
  provisional until a later chunk confirms it.** The book writes **"Shah Hussayn"**; the archive row
  names its principal figure "Shah Hussain (Madho Lal Hussain)" of Baghbanpura, Lahore. In pp. 1–30
  Kugle gives **no** location, tomb, death year, order, companion or language for his Shah Hussayn —
  only "the Mughal Era" (p. 14, folio xiii). Under the 18 September 2026 ruling a principal figure
  takes the row's id on an author-or-reference mention, so the id is recorded; but the identification
  itself is mine, not the book's, and Chapter 4 (PDF pp. 202 ff.) must be read before any fact is
  carried into the entry. **No second archive row is a candidate** — `madho-lal-hussain` is the only
  row in shrine_index.tsv whose principal figure is a Shah Hussain — so the doubt is whether the
  book's figure is the archive's at all, not which of two rows he is.
- **The plate "Two men engaged in anal sex 216" is placed in Chapter 4 by my arithmetic, not by the
  book.** The illustrations list gives only the page; the assignment follows from the contents'
  chapter openings at 181 and 221 (p. 8, folio not stated). If a later chunk shows the plate falling
  in a different chapter, drop the association.
- **Bare toponyms, written reversibly under the interim convention.** "Pakistan" (p. 13, folio xii;
  p. 28, folio 7) is a country name with no shrine, tomb, saint, ʿurs or custodian attached, so it
  takes no id and sits under "Other saints, sites and events". **If the bare-toponym ruling reverses
  it would still take no row**, since no archive row is a country; the same holds for Morocco and
  India. **No Pakistani settlement or district is named anywhere in pp. 1–30**, so this chunk raises
  no Lahore-row or Sindh-row toponym question at all. Hinglaj does not appear, and no id was
  invented for it.
- **Internal discrepancy: "six saints" against five chapters.** The book says "it explores the
  hagiographic accounts of six saints" (p. 29, folio 8), but the contents list five saint-named
  chapters — Mawlay Idris, Sayyida Amina, Muhammad Ghawth, Shah Hussayn, Hajji Imdadullah (p. 8,
  folio not stated). The preface's list of organs has six — "the breath, bones, belly, eyes, lips,
  and heart" (p. 13, folio xii) — and "breath" belongs to the introduction's Adam material rather
  than to a chapter. **The book does not say whether Adam is the sixth: (not stated).** Do not
  resolve this from outside the text.
