## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Women custodians, and a tomb chamber run by women for women.** "In the small chamber that houses
  her tomb, three custodians (who were all women) attend the tomb, guide visitors (who are
  overwhelmingly women as well), and distribute objects that can convey the blessings of the site."
  (p. 120, folio 99) "The sacred pre-cinct of her tomb is a de facto feminine space through which men
  may pass but seldom linger." (p. 120, folio 99) The author, a man, "caused quite a bit of tension"
  by lingering, "praying a little and observing a lot"; the custodians "were surprised and somewhat
  troubled that a man would spend time there" (p. 120, folio 99).
- **What the tomb draws.** "To this day her tomb is a gathering place for devotees, singers, and
  common people seeking divine blessing and healing." (p. 120, folio 99) **No visitor numbers, no
  ʿurs or annual festival, no endowment, no revenue and no state involvement are stated for this
  tomb**: all **(not stated)**.
- **Food placed on the tomb itself.** "The women custodians had placed on the actual tomb a variety
  of foods, including a bowl of raw olive oil in which were soaking red chili peppers and other whole
  spices. In this way, the wom an's tomb became a sort of nourishing dining table." (p. 120, folio
  99) Kugle marks this as "something I had not noticed at the tomb of male saints" (p. 120, folio
  99).
- **What visitors do and take away.** "The women visitors and pilgrims would dip their fingers in the
  oil to anoint their forehead or hair, in addition eating candies distributed by the custodians.
  They would carry away from the tomb candles and small chunks of incense to be burned at home."
  (p. 120, folio 99)
- **A definition of *baraka* the archive can cite.** The food objects "represented nourishment, good
  health, and well-being (all cultural meanings of the term baraka, or blessing); they were vehicles
  that could absorb spiritual energy from the tomb and its concealed saintly presence while
  transmitting it to visitors and pilgrims." (p. 120, folio 99) The objects are "both material and
  immaterial: they are inanimate and tactile but can be transformed into light, fragrance, taste,
  and heat" (p. 120, folio 99).
- **Local substance as ritual substance.** "Olive oil and chili peppers are especially potent in
  Tunisia as both symbol and substance; they are the basic ingredients of harissa, eaten with bread
  at every meal." Oil "stands in for bread as the basic source of nutritional cuisine," and is "far
  more versatile than bread for ritual symbolism, for it can be poured, drunk, rubbed, or burned"
  (pp. 120–121, folios 99–100).
- **An unvisited tomb, stated as such.** Of Sayyida Amina: "We can gain access to her not through
  her tomb (which is little known and not visited) but through the stories originally told and later
  recorded about her" (p. 121, folio 100). **The tomb's location is not stated.**
- **A rediscovered body founding a claim to authority.** The rediscovery of Mawlay Idris's body and
  the building of his tomb is given as the mechanism by which "the sharifian clans that claimed
  genealogical descent from him gained increasing authority" (p. 125, folio 104).
- **Hereditary juridical office, and women's access to it.** The Ibn al-Qadi moved to Fes "to take up
  official positions in the Marinid bureaucracy"; Amina's father Ahmad "was a judge, as his
  forefathers had been"; and "His daughter, Amina, might have become a female jurist herself, as was
  possible in Marinid-era Fes." (p. 121, folio 100)
- **Legal and familial sanction.** Amina's reputation as Ali al-Sanhaji's "servant and his bed
  companion" "did cause legal action against him and familial action against her" (p. 122, folio
  101). Her family "acted swiftly to deny her access to Ali al-Sanhaji by locking her in her home,"
  and "The whole clan of Ibn al-Qadi came from Meknes to try to take her back. [As she resisted,]
  they ended up locking her in a room and confining her with iron chains." (pp. 123–124, folios
  102–103)
- **Legal exemption for the "divinely distracted."** *Majdhubs* "challenged social norms and legal
  rules, comparable to the 'holy fools' of medieval Christendom. They were often exempt from
  punishments applied to people whose 'reason' was intact." (p. 121, folio 100) The term is glossed:
  "Jadhaba literally means 'to draw out, pull out, or strain.' A majdhub, as the passive recipient of
  the action, is one whose spirit has been drawn forcibly to God and whose reason is thus under
  strain" (p. 121, folio 100).
- **Family wealth spent on a saint.** "Sayyida Amina spent freely of her family's wealth and
  squandered its pious reputation to allow Ali al-Sanhaji to abandon himself to this antinomian
  practice." (p. 122, folio 101) She waited for him in the marketplace "with a bowl full of olive oil
  to offer him as a token of admiration to soil himself and his clothes in public" (p. 122, folio
  101).
- **Payment for political power.** Abu Rawayin's formula to the rich and to rulers: "Quickly, buy
  from me your fortune and you won't come to ruin!" — "If they paid, he would say, 'You are safe.'
  But if they refused, he would pronounce, 'You are cut off,' or 'You are killed,' and shortly that
  would happen." (p. 127, folio 106) To the `Sa'dian` leader: "Purchase Fes [the capital] from me for
  five hundred dinars!" (p. 128, folio 107) — **the only money sum in the chunk, and its digits are
  spelled out in words, undamaged.**
- **Urban topography used as a pulpit.** Ali al-Sanhaji "mounted a central point in the traffic
  arteries of Fes, a bridge that spans the river and connects the two halves of the city" and turned
  in a circle there, shouting "Get out, you children of the Banu Marin tribe By God, we will not let
  you remain in our land!" (p. 126, folio 105). Abu Rawayin "went to the town of Qasr Kutama and
  climbed the minaret of the central mosque, as if to proclaim his body ... to be the axis connecting
  heaven to the social world on earth" (p. 127, folio 106).
- **Sufi orders and the state.** "many Sufi organizations and sharifian families combined forces to
  wage a defensive jihad" and "called for the overthrow of the Wattasid rulers because they could
  not effectively defend Morocco from European assaults" (pp. 125–126, folios 104–105). Ali
  al-Sanhaji had earlier "tacitly acknowledged the right of Marinid and Wattasid aristocracy to rule,
  even as he encouraged them to distribute their wealth to the poor" (p. 125, folio 104).
- **Dynastic chronology as the book gives it**, quoted rather than reconstructed: "Only twenty-five
  years later, in 1465, these sharifian nobles led a popular uprising that put the Marinid sultan to
  death; only after six years of fighting were the Wattasid nobles (related to the Marinids by tribal
  connections) able to retake the capitol of Fes" (p. 125, folio 104); the Portuguese "were attacking
  coastal cities of Morocco as part of their naval colonization and were soon joined by the Spanish"
  (p. 125, folio 104); "the `Sa'dian` forces conquered Meknes and turned to assault Fes" (p. 128,
  folio 107); the "`Sa'dian` conquest of Fes in 1549" (p. 128, folio 107). The printed form "capitol"
  is as printed.
- **No langar, no ʿurs, no mela, no sajjada nashin, no waqf or Auqaf body, no land grant, no
  architecture or building date other than the undated tomb of Mawlay Idris, and no anti-tomb
  polemic appears anywhere in pp. 114–128**: all **(not stated)**. The nearest thing to a
  custodianship institution is the three women custodians at Manuba (p. 120, folio 99).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **Saint Margaret and the dragon**, from "A thirteenth-century Italian story": "There appeared
  before her [Saint Margaret] a great dragon, who was seeking to devour her ... placing his tongue
  under her feet, he thus swallowed her. But Margaret, when she perceived that he wanted to swallow
  her, armed herself with the sign of the cross. Consequently the dragon, by virtue of the cross,
  burst open, and the virgin issued forth unharmed." (p. 116, folio 95) Attributed by Kugle to the
  Christian hagiographic tradition, not endorsed.
- **The serpent at the bed of a holy virgin**, attributed to "Elizabeth `Petroff's[text-layer?]`
  research": one holy virgin "wrapped her clothes around her feet and tied them with a belt, so that
  the serpent couldn't enter from below, at her feet, and get to her nude body." (p. 117, folio 96)
- **Stigmata as a female miracle**: "only for women did the stigmatic wounds bleed periodically"
  (p. 118, folio 97) — attributed to Caroline Walker Bynum.
- **Ali al-Sanhaji's trance and clairvoyance**, quoted from the hagiography: "States of possession
  seized him at all times.... He would give reports on unseen matters and tell those who met him
  about their secret affairs." (p. 122, folio 101)
- **Amina feeds her share to a dog**, quoted from the hagiography: "One day her family cooked a
  chicken and gave her share to her. She gestured to a dog that was there with her at the time,
  saying 'If this bitch were the dog of my Lord [Ali al-Sanhaji], I would give my portion to her!'
  Then she stretched out her hand and fed the chicken to that dog." (p. 123, folio 102)
- **Majnun and Layla's dog**, as Nakhshabi tells it: "when a dog came out of Layla's house, Majnun
  worshiped the ground it walked on. People said,'O Majnun, what insanity is this?' He replied, 'You
  ignorant people, do you realize from whose house this dog is coming?'" (p. 123, folio 102)
- **The falling chains**, quoted from the hagiography: Ali al-Sanhaji appeared "standing in the
  middle of the house, calling to her 'O Amina!' He was not stopped by any limits, neither by walls
  nor by chains. When he called to her she answered,'Yes, my lord?' He ordered to her leave the room
  and her chains suddenly fell away from her ankles, and she walked out of the house with him while
  all her family watched, unable to stop them. Since that day, the people left her to do as she
  liked." (p. 124, folio 103) Kugle frames this as "ways that are stock images in hagiography"
  (p. 123, folio 102).
- **Sayyida Fatima's flight**: "She used to fly or teleport from her home in Fes to the middle of the
  Sahara with fresh food and medicine to care for him," an ability that "came from her selfless
  devotion to her husband: 'by undertaking to uphold the duties to Allah that are laid upon me in
  serving my husband.'" (p. 125, folio 104)
- **Amina's jihad wounds**: "She would wake up in bed covered with miraculous wounds, 'as if she had
  been participating in the jihad.'" (p. 126, folio 105) This is the chapter's central karama and is
  quoted from the hagiography, with a footnote marker `38`.
- **Abu Rawayin's pronouncements take effect**: his curses were followed by ruin or death "shortly"
  (p. 127, folio 106); and his shouting "I favor the Portuguese!" one day and "I favor the Muslims!"
  the next "reflected who was winning in the wars at that particular time" (p. 127, folio 106).
- **Abu Rawayin and the taking of Fes**: he "pronounced, 'Then you won't take Fes this year,'" and
  after being paid "is credited with opening the way for the `Sa'dian` conquest of Fes in 1549"
  (p. 128, folio 107).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **The chapter's stated hypothesis, deliberately limited.** "One assertion of this chapter is that
  nourishment and food are particularly potent symbols through which Islamic culture defines the
  bodies of women saints." Kugle then names two possible over-generalizations — that the dynamic is
  "common to all patriarchal societies" and "common to all women and not limited to women saints" —
  and declines both: "This chapter explores the limited hypothesis and leaves other researchers to
  define the horizons of its generality." (p. 121, folio 100)
- **The method is explicitly material and tactile.** Because Amina's own tomb is unvisited, "this
  chapter will begin an archaeology of her personality in a different site, the tomb of a woman saint
  who is publicly renowned" (p. 119, folio 98), and access is claimed "conceptually and tactilely, to
  both kinds of women saints thro ugh 'inanimate objects' associated with them: not the tomb itself
  but the edible foodstuff present there" (pp. 119–120, folios 98–99).
- **A field-shaping complaint.** "This scholarship on Christian Europe is very subtle and
  theoretically astute. In contrast, the scholarship on Muslim women saints is quite limited. This is
  not for lack of vivid examples and lively personalities." (p. 119, folio 98) He concedes "it is
  doubtful that Muslim holy women ever played such a dominant role as Christian holy women did in
  the high medieval period" (p. 119, folio 98), and observes that women saints' names survive "while
  the details of their lives are forgotten or subsumed into the background of the stories of saints
  who were men" (p. 119, folio 98).
- **On the Qur'an.** "The Qur'anic depictio[text-layer?] [depiction] of women and their bodies is
  surely more ambivalent and subtler than the Islamic exegetical and legal traditions that claim to
  be scripture's best interpreters." (p. 114, folio 93) The symmetry he draws: "Jesus was born
  through Mary but not of her, while Eve was created of Adam but not through him." (p. 114, folio 93)
  Qur'anic citation as printed: "In God's opinion Jesus is like Adam—God created him from dust then
  pronounced 'Be!' and he was (Q 3:59)" (p. 114, folio 93).
- **Womb blood and breast milk as one physiology.** "The female belly was imagined as an organ that
  fused blood and milk, both fluids that flowed beyond the boundaries of her body to sustain and
  nourish others." (p. 114, folio 93) Evidenced from Bynum — "All medieval biologists thought the
  mother's blood fed the child in the womb and then, transmuted into breast milk, fed the baby
  outside the womb as well. For example, a fourteenth-century surgeon wrote that milk is blood twice
  cooked." — and from "One of the Arab texts most frequently used by Western doctors": "milk is
  formed from menstrual blood" (pp. 114–115, folios 93–94, footnote `15`). "Some medieval anatomists
  postulated that the womb and breasts were connected by a blood vessel" (p. 115, folio 94).
- **The belly as the hinge between gluttony and lust.** "Hellenic and Roman moralists often used 'the
  belly' as a metaphor for the genitalia, linking the urge to eat with the urge for sexual pleasure."
  (p. 115, folio 94, footnote `16`) Quoted at length: "The belly is a key topic of these discussions.
  It becomes a catchword for a lifestyle controlled by the desires, and is thus to be considered a
  topos." (p. 115, folio 94, footnote `17`)
- **Philo's three-souled body as the source of the patriarchal reading.** "a rational soul in the
  head, a spiritual soul in the chest, and lustful soul in the belly," with "prudence for the
  reasoning head, courage for the spiritual and passionate chest, and self-mastery and control for
  the lustful belly" (pp. 115–116, folios 94–95, footnote `18`). Kugle's claim: Philo "makes clear
  what lies implicit in all patriarchal societies: that the sin of fleshly desire is personified in
  women's bodies" (p. 116, folio 95), and "Philo's interpretive strategy enshrined patriarchal
  judgments about the corporal nature of women, implying their consequent lack of reason and excess
  of passion." (p. 116, folio 95)
- **The Petroff–Bynum disagreement, set out as a disagreement.** Petroff reads women mystics'
  somatic austerities as evidence of confinement: "In medieval thought, women were bodies (men were
  characterized as mind or spirit), and bodies were dangerous"; the exceptional few "did not break
  the confines of their sisters; rather, the exceptional few ... reinforced the very circumscribed
  norm for women in society as a whole." (p. 117, folio 96, footnote `22`) Bynum reads the same
  evidence as a turning point: "female spirituality in the same period [1200 to 1500] was specially
  somatic—so much so that the emergence of certain bizarre miracles characteristic of women may
  actually mark a turning point in the history of the body in the West." (p. 118, folio 97, footnote
  `23`) Kugle glosses Bynum as seeing "a considerable step forward in uniting the soul and body in
  Christianity," an integral unity "sustained until it dissolved under the influence of Descartes"
  (p. 118, folio 97).
- **Bakhtin's grotesque body, adopted with a caveat.** Quoted: "The grotesque body is not separated
  from the rest of the world. It is not a closed, completed unity; it is unfinished, outgrows itself,
  transgresses its own limits." (p. 118, folio 97, footnote `25`) Kugle's caveat: "Clearly Bakhtin had
  in mind more than womens bodies ... he also included under it clowns, monsters, satyrs, and freaks"
  and "Bakhtin's emphasis on 'protuberances' may confuse the issue here"; what he keeps is "his
  emphasis on 'open bodies' that, through their orifices, meet the world and flow into it" (p. 119,
  folio 98).
- **Two models of female sainthood, contrasted.** Fatima's story "acts as a prototype for other women
  saints. This is the model of female sainthood that complements official male roles, as saints or
  scholars, in a patriarchal social order," achieved "through domestic duties, not in spite of them"
  — "through connection to a male relative who is a saint, through 'devoted service' to a husband, or
  through caring for others in the generous provision of cooked food" (pp. 124–125, folios 103–104).
  Amina's story "is an aberration: it is longer and more detailed than others, and it diverges from
  others in having Amina break patterns of women's domesticity most blatantly" (p. 124, folio 103).
- **Why the transgressive story survived.** "One might assume that the male hagiographers who
  recorded these stories would conveniently forget her. The opposite is the case." The reason given:
  "the political potency of her 'mad holiness'" and "the political force of her body as it became
  exposed to the publics gaze" (p. 125, folio 104). She "was held up along with other majdhub
  characters as prime examples of 'popular Islam.'" (p. 125, folio 104)
- **Food refusal as social rupture.** "The ritual use of foodstuff is, from a utilitarian or ascetic
  point of view, a grand waste. If consuming food in the customary manner is one of the basic ways to
  solidify and replicate social bonds and values, then wasting food is a very visceral way of
  breaking social conventions." (p. 122, folio 101) On the dog: Amina "not only rejected the family
  ties that the reciprocal sharing of food manifests but also gave the food to a dog, a potent symbol
  of ritual pollution in the urban Muslim landscape." (p. 123, folio 102) And: "Amina turned the
  'waste' of food into a powerful display of the excesses of sainthood." (p. 122, folio 101)
- **The inverted stigmata — the chapter's strongest claim.** "This is a remarkable inversion of the
  Catholic iconography of the stigmata.... In Amina's case, the bleeding of a woman does not embody
  and display the wounds of Jesus, the incarnate God. Rather, her bleeding becomes a sublime
  miraculous wound that marks her off as an embodiment of the Moroccan people as a whole, injured in
  fights with the invading European Catholics." (p. 126, folio 105) The general point: "Women saints
  were able to reverse the social dynamic of bleeding: rather than having it be hidden as private,
  shame-inducing, and a mark of inferiority, they were able to display this holy bleeding as public,
  pride-invoking, and a mark of their claim to religious leadership." (p. 126, folio 105)
- **The political logic of the *majdhub*.** "The logic of the majdhub is that he or she has lost
  grasp of the rational faculties, such that his or her body is no longer the locus of a rational
  person bound by law, responsibility, and social custom. The bodies of such individuals become
  'empty' of these social meanings and can stand as a symbol of something greater, either the social
  whole or the workings of divine destiny." (pp. 126–127, folios 105–106) The parallel Kugle draws
  with dynastic change: "Just as the `Sa'dian` leaders were asking Moroccans to shed their legal
  obligation to obey legitimate authority ... the majdhub coalition was asking Moroccans to shed
  their trust in rationality." (p. 126, folio 105) And: "The 'emptied' personality of the
  maj~dhub[text-layer?] [majdhub] became a veritable barometer for political and military changes."
  (p. 127, folio 106)
- **Gendered miracle repertoires.** "Abu Rawayin's miracles came through his speech, while Amina's
  miracles came through her body. Abu Rawayin issued threats, while Amina displayed bleeding
  wounds." (p. 128, folio 107) Supported with Elizabeth Robertson, that "sanctity is not a gender
  neutral concept" and that hagiographic accounts "assume a woman's essential, inescapable
  corporeality" (p. 128, folio 107, footnote `45`). Kugle then refuses the obvious conclusion: "But
  was Amina silenced by the gendered nature of her body? We could turn the tables and ask, Which type
  of miracle was more 'popular' and effective in supporting the jihad?" — answering that Abu
  Rawayin's words "gained him access to elites," whereas "Amina's wounds did not gain her access to
  rulers and military leaders, but they did circulate in public" and "would be a powerful indictment
  against the Wattasid state" (p. 128, folio 107).
- **The double transgression.** Petroff quoted: "The women saints of the Middle Ages were
  transgressors, rule-breakers, flouters of boundaries, and yet they were also saints.... Women
  saints, it seems to me, were doubly transgressors—first, by their nature as saints and, second, by
  thei[text-layer?] [their] nature as women." (p. 124, folio 103, footnote `35`) Kugle's own
  sharpening: "the real transgression was 'to go public' and enter the public arena of preaching,
  teaching, exhorting, and being visible." (p. 124, folio 103)
