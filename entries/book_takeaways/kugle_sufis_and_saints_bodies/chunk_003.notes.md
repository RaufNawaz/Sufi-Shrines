## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Samāʿ as the setting of Nakhshabi's questions about the body.** "In his story cycle, a princess
  asks her parrot a series of questions that arise directly from Chishti ritual gatherings to listen
  to devotional poetry set to music: 'How did the movements of dance
  `origi-nate:1[text-layer?]` [originate?] How can the state of ecstasy be brought about? What
  substance is the human made of which is as heavy as lead and yet soft, and how can such an erect
  figure lose consciousness? How can the body which walks so gracefully become hypnotized by
  chanting?'" (p. 48, folio 27). The parrot's reply, on music as sustenance of the spirit, is at
  Citable passages.
- **The forty-day retreat, as an institution and as a reading discipline.** "Nakhshabi divides his
  composition into forty parts, each dedicated to a body part. Forty is the number of spiritual
  completion in Islam, and Sufi meditation retreats typically last for forty days" (p. 49, folio 28);
  "Reading it over a period of forty days, he hints, will incite in one's heart the same intense
  passion and love that a Sufi retreat might, and the object of contemplation in this spiritual
  transformation should be one's own body" (p. 49, folio 28). A forty-day kneading of Adam's clay is
  the cosmological parallel: "the image of God gathering the earth and kneading it to leaven it for
  forty days between God's two hands" (p. 51, folio 30).
- **Sacred power in places, objects and ritual — the chunk's central statement for shrine practice.**
  "It is a universal characteristic of religions that the sacred lodges in physical places: in caves
  or mountains, in temples that imitate the shape of caves or mountains, in stones or springs or
  `statutes[text-layer?]` [statues] that can be approached through ritual, touched through devotion,
  and through which sacred power can be channeled into more routine social needs" (p. 50, folio 29,
  with a footnote marker 42). The author then says the Sufis went further: "Sufis pushed this logic to
  an iconoclastic extreme and claimed that the divine fully manifests only in human beauty" (pp. 50–51,
  folios 29–30).
- **Custodianship as the human vocation, in the book's own vocabulary.** "As human beings, we are
  charged with responsibility (`khilafaj[text-layer?]` [khilāfa], the trust of responsible
  custodianship" (p. 53, folio 32), glossed from Q 33:72 and from the Sufi formula "we are called to
  'take on the qualities of God' (`takhallaqu bi-akhlaq illah`)" (p. 53, folio 32). Gesu-Daraz's
  version: God "seated Adam upon the throne of responsibility (`khilafa`) and established him there
  firmly" (p. 54, folio 33).
- **Greater and lesser jihad, as the Sufi discipline set against externalised enemies.** "Upon
  returning from a battle against the Muslim's enemies, the Prophet said, 'We have returned from the
  lesser jihad to the greater jihad.' When he said this, the Prophet's companions asked him what he
  meant. He replied, 'The lesser jihad is to fight with swords, but the greater jihad is to counter
  the enemy here,' as he held his hands to either side of his own chest" (p. 57, folio 36). The book
  gives no source or collection for the hadith here: **(not stated)**.
- **The doctrinal limit on bodily sanctity — the anti-incarnationist rule.** "Cautious Sufis warned
  that belief that 'God created the Adam in God's image' should never devolve into incarnationism or
  claiming that God resides in a human body (`hulul`)" (p. 60, folio 39). This is the one
  institutional guard-rail the chunk places on the whole argument about sacred bodies.
- **Bones as the reason a body can be venerated after death.** "Bones are a constant reminder of
  death, for we see them only after the rest of the body has decayed. However, bones are also a
  constant reminder that we persist and that our personality is durable even beyond the portal of
  death, since bones remain intact long after other tissues have rotted away" (p. 65, folio 44).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **Method: the part stands for the whole, and the licence is Qurʾanic rhetoric.** The author adopts
  Nakhshabi's stated procedure — "it suffices for me to mention certain noble bodily organs and some
  exalted body parts, because when some part is cited the whole body is present in the indication"
  (p. 49, folio 28) — and says so in the first person: "We can take Nakhshabi as our literary Sufi
  guide and follow his example as he explains his own method of evoking the holistic body through its
  distinct parts. We can also appreciate his justification of this method through analyzing the
  rhetorical strategy of the Qur'anic discourse, and we will adopt this strategy to guide us in our
  subsequent explorations" (p. 49, folio 28).
- **The historical claim about Sufi attitudes to the body.** "Such an attitude became increasingly
  characteristic of the Sufi tradition once it grew in maturity beyond its roots in the ascetic
  movement of the early Islamic community" (p. 50, folio 29); "Sufis came to see the body not as the
  enemy to be opposed by strenuous ascetic effort (though ascetic exercises continued to play a key
  role in Sufism) but, more subtly, as a sign of the creator, or rather as a whole constellation of
  signs" (p. 50, folio 29); the body is "the locus of the manifestation of God's names and
  attributes—a theophany of the highest order" (p. 50, folio 29).
- **The central paradox the book is built on.** "It is material, therefore ephemeral, limited in
  space, fragile, even brittle; however, it is material infused with spirit and is therefore eternal,
  unbounded by space" (p. 51, folio 30); "the body is matter that acts as the locus for the
  manifestation of the divine spirit in the world. We are clay infused with light, matter that
  crackles with the sparks of the energy of life" (p. 51, folio 30). The triad the author reads out
  of the clay image: transcendence, a "personal" divinity, and immanence — "God must come to our own
  level, placing lips to the dust, so to speak, in a life-giving connection more intimate than a
  kiss" (p. 51, folio 30).
- **Intuitive against rational knowledge.** Sufis read God's teaching Adam "all the names and
  qualities (`Q2:3i[text-layer?]`" [Q 2:31] as "intuitive paradigmatic knowledge that is given
  through reflection (`ma'rifa`) rather than rational practical knowledge that is learned through
  observation (`cilm[text-layer?]`" [ʿilm] (p. 54, folio 33). The author adds that this knowledge is
  "embedded in human nature and enmeshed in the human body, which comes to the light of awareness
  only through human experience, specifically the failure of our reliance on egoistic autonomy and
  our acceptance of our brokenness in humility" (p. 54, folio 33).
- **The book's strongest polemical claim: interiorising Iblis is what separates Sufis from the
  mainstream, and it has social consequences.** "So far in this Qur'anic interpretation, conventional
  Muslim theologians and Sufis can travel together. However, Sufis diverge from the mainstream by
  emphasizing the interiority of Iblis" (p. 57, folio 36); Satan is "a force that can be avoided only
  by introspection, thereby resisting the puritan impulse to project his dark light onto the external
  world in a dynamic of hatred—hatred of despised social groups, religious others, or the human body
  itself" (p. 57, folio 36); "Sufis countered the socially powerful groups, like jurists and ascetics,
  who also addressed, with different solutions, the sense that something is disquieting about the
  human body" (p. 57, folio 36).
- **Iblis as ego and as misapplied reason.** "He is the being who first said '!' [I] in
  contradistinction to God! He is the spark of ego run amok" (p. 55, folio 34); "Reason and
  intelligence fueled his jealousy—an intelligence that has run wild and leaps into arrogance"
  (p. 56, folio 35); "This is the wrong conclusion, based on accurate observation but warped by
  egotistical reason" (p. 56, folio 35). The exposure of nakedness is read psychologically as well as
  sexually: "Iblis sparked in them a fundamental dissociation between self and body, an alienation
  from the body through which we assert that the self is autonomous, independent, even immortal"
  (p. 56, folio 35).
- **The optimism is explicitly balanced, not contradicted.** "The optimistic Sufi assessment of the
  human body, as the place of manifestation of God's names and qualities, is therefore balanced by a
  more pessimistic Sufi assessment that the tempter dwells in the human body, through which
  temptation whispers" (p. 60, folio 39).
- **Genre argument: a South Asian devotional form inside a Persian text.** "In this Persian
  composition, he adopts a particularly South Asian poetic genre for religious devotion: the
  head-to-foot description of the divine body. He calls to mind the genre of Hindu devotional poetry,
  the `anubhava`, an erotic devotional enjoyment that moves from foot to head, describing the idol of
  God in sensual detail" (p. 60, folio 39); Nakhshabi instead uses "the Persian poetic genre of
  `sarapa`, consisting of a head-to-toe description of the beloved, in a list of rhyming couplets"
  (p. 60, folio 39, quoted at pp. 60–61, folios 39–40). His aim is "not to arouse amorous passion
  directed toward sensual pleasure but to arouse ethical passion directed to sublimating simple lust
  to spark spiritual insight" (p. 61, folio 40).
- **The author's declared break with his source's decorum, and the reason he gives.** Nakhshabi
  abstains "from mentioning the lowlier parts of the body, from among those organs that cannot be
  mentioned with honor" (p. 62, folio 41); against this, "In contrast to Nakhshabi, this study has no
  shame, coming as it does during the ongoing sexual revolution in the West, and will mention body
  parts with shameless abandon" (p. 62, folio 41), and "So in this study, if God created it, we will
  mention it!" (p. 62, folio 41). The justification: "in confronting the body's parts and functions
  that are obscured by politeness (those that inspire squeamish discomfort, shame, horror, or
  disgust), we gain real insight into the body's vexing complexity" (p. 62, folio 41).
- **The book's own plan of chapters, stated here.** "We will need such boldness as we begin our
  exploration of Sufi images of the body in earnest, beginning in the next chapter with bones,
  followed by the belly, eyes, lips, and heart, and concluding with the disintegration or
  revitalization of the body under threat of death" (p. 62, folio 41). Useful for placing later
  chunks.
- **The body–world homology, and its mythological genealogy.** "This homology between the world and
  the body is common in ancient mythologies. Often the world is imagined as the body of a cosmic
  giant, the first primal man, who sacrifices himself and whose divided body constitutes the created
  world: the dome of the sky is his skull, the earth his flesh, precious minerals his teeth, rivers
  his blood, and mountains his bones" (p. 65, folio 44, footnote marker 1); "The bones in the human
  body are like the mountains of the earth—they give it its characteristic shape while anchoring its
  surface deep within its structural foundation" (p. 65, folio 44). The chapter then turns to Islam's
  reservation about this, and the chunk ends mid-sentence: "As a radical monotheism, Islam shies away
  from this outright anthro-" (p. 65, folio 44).
- **Bones chosen as the Qurʾan's preferred proof of resurrection.** "It is not surprising that bones
  are the metaphor to which the Qur'an turns insistently to preach the doctrine of resurrection"
  (p. 65, folio 44); the Qurʾan "links the One who firmed up the bones in each gelatinous fetal body
  in the first instance to the One who will restore life to them after their flesh has putrefied into
  liquid and drained away" (p. 65, folio 44).
