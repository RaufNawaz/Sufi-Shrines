## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The Shattari lineage and its claim to method.** "The Shattari community was defined by a series
  of Sufi teachers, originally of Khurasani origin, who branched out as an independent growth from
  the Kubrawi lineage" (p. 152, folio 131). "Under Muhammad Ghawth's leadership, the Shattari
  community became strong during the period of Mughal ascendancy and spread throughout South Asia"
  (p. 152, folio 131). "In their heyday, the Shattari masters were quite audacious in boasting that
  their 'swift-paced' spiritual method (the literal meaning of shattar) was not just distinct from
  the methods of all other Sufi lineages but effectively superior" (p. 152, folio 131).
- **The devotional technique itself, quoted from "A major disciple of Muhammad Ghawth"** —
  "Repeating the essential name of God is the most important method of religious discipline.
  Recitation of the name God should be directed toward your own chest, so that you come to understand
  that recitation of this divine name is actually addressing your own essential self" (p. 156, folio
  135). The disciple is **not named**: **(not stated)**.
- **Antinomian framing of ritual custom.** "the Shattaris claimed to follow religious custom (sunna)
  without paying any attention to custom" (p. 156, folio 135); "Anyone distracted by formalities and
  external norms will never achieve intimacy with God!" (p. 156, folio 135).
- **Retreat practice, extreme fasting and sleeplessness as institution.** "engaging in rigorous
  asceticism and intense meditation. He records that he spent up to six months without sleeping at
  night or being heedless during the day" (p. 155, folio 134); "increasing his isolation and rigor
  while eating little or nothing" (p. 155, folio 134); "he completely stopped eating and his body
  grew so thin that 'if the wind was blowing behind him he would go three or four times faster than a
  normal person'" (p. 157, folio 136). The retreat is named in the primary text: "a place on a remote
  mountain called 'the retreat'" (p. 158, folio 137), with "four companions who were staying with me
  there; two were intimate friends of mine and two were just acquaintances" (p. 160, folio 139).
- **Service novitiate.** The young Ghawth "set to work carrying wood and working in the kitchen under
  the care of his master" before being named "his spiritual successor" (pp. 154–155, folios 133–134).
- **Pilgrimage to a living saint.** "He began to gain local renown at this time, and people addressed
  him with the title 'Ghawth.' People began to climb the mountain to seek him out and to come up from
  the settled lands to live with him." (p. 155, folio 134)
- **Saint and state.** "Muhammad Ghawth returned from his ascension to become a kingmaker with the
  ability to shift the opportunity to rule from one dynastic leader to another. He not only advocated
  a new literary style and a new Sufi order but also supported a new political dynasty the Mughal
  dynasty that was just beginning its program of conquest and governance in South Asia." (p. 154,
  folio 133) His opponents "consistently attacked his ascension experience and the uses he put it to
  in furthering the struggle of the Mughals to control the body politic" (p. 154, folio 133).
- **The source base Kugle declares for the chapter** — "the literary narrative of the ascension
  itself, which is presented for the first time in a complete English translation", from "a
  lithograph print in South India"; "a spiritual biography of the Sufi's early life by one of his
  followers, in a Persian manuscript"; and "the better-known record of political figures who mention
  Muhammad Ghawth's role in historical events" (p. 154, folio 133). Ghawth's "original writings in
  Arabic and Persian" survive (p. 154, folio 133).
- **Manuscript provenance of the one plate in this chunk** (p. 149, folio not stated): "Miniature
  painting of the Prophet Muhammad ascending from Jerusalem up through the seven heavenly spheres
  with his winged steed, Buraq, to meet the other prophets and angels of each sphere. Painted in
  North India in 1808, From Muhammad Rafi `Kha[text-layer?]` [Khan?], Hamla-yi Haydari, ms, (Paris:
  Bibliotheque nationale de France, Suppl, Persian 1030), fol, 35b," — the commas throughout stand
  for printed periods. The shelfmark digits `1030` and the folio `35b` are undamaged; **there is no
  museum accession number in this caption** and none is guessed.
- **Cross-tradition teaching.** "He taught the Sanskrit text `Amrta-Kunda[text-layer?]`
  [Amṛta-Kuṇḍa; macrons and subscript dots not carried by the text layer] ('Pool of the Nectar of
  Life') on the principles of hatha yoga and may have translated it into Persian and Arabic, about
  which we will learn in Chapter 5" (p. 154, folio 133). **No yogi, Nath figure, math or site is
  named** in connection with it: **(not stated)**.

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **Abu Saʿid Abu'l-Khayr bears another's beating on his own body** — attributed by Kugle to Nizam
  al-Din Awliya's teaching session as recorded by Amir Hasan Sijzi: a leather maker beat an animal,
  the shaykh cried "Ah!" "with such pathos that is seemed as though he had been the one beaten", a
  skeptic objected, and "Shaykh Abu `Sacid[text-layer?]` [Saʿid] disrobed and the signs of that
  leather belt appeared on his blessed back!" (pp. 148–150, folios 127–129).
- **Muhammad Ghawth's bodily ascension, 1526** — "Muhammad Ghawth insists that he ascended bodily,
  with the permission of the Prophet, and returned to write an eyewitness account" (p. 152, folio
  131); dated "in the year 1526" (p. 157, folio 136). Attributed to Ghawth's own first-person
  narrative, which Kugle translates.
- **Initiation by the long dead.** "Muhammad Ghawth fell unconscious, and in a vision he was taken to
  Arab lands to meet Shaykh Bayazid Bistami… This is just the first of a long series of mystic
  initiations that were granted to Ghawth by long-dead saints and prophets through visionary
  experiences that he called spiritual disclosures (`mukashifat[text-layer?]` [mukāshafāt])"
  (p. 155, folio 134).
- **Instantaneous travel.** "Muhammad Ghawth crosses vast distances instantaneously, as if space had
  folded upon itself or had freed him from its bounds completely: he arrives at a destination without
  having taken a single step." (p. 155, folio 134) Kugle glosses it as Ghawth's own imagining, not as
  an event he endorses.
- **A body governed by the wind** — so thin that with the wind behind him he moved "three or four
  times faster than a normal person, but if the wind was blowing against him, he could hardly take a
  single step" (p. 157, folio 136), so that "Even his basic bodily movements were not under his
  control but governed by the movement of the winds" (p. 157, folio 136).
- **The voiceless voice and the appearance of Khidr** — "there suddenly arose from the peak of the
  mountain a strange roaring, as if a storm wind were hurtling down the mountain… A voiceless voice
  began to speak" (p. 158, folio 137); on recovering consciousness, "`![text-layer?]` [I] saw a man
  standing over me" (p. 158, folio 137); "He appeared as a young man, all clothed in green" (p. 159,
  folio 138), and told him "Tonight will be the night of your ascension" (p. 160, folio 139).
- **The creation takes human form and is climbed.** "I saw that all the inanimate objects, all the
  plants, and all the animals had taken the form of human beings who stood staring up at the sky!
  When I took another step, they all melted into the shape of one single man" who said "I am the
  earth" (p. 160, folio 139). Then water (p. 161, folio 140), then "the sphere of air" (p. 161, folio
  140), then fire — "fierce, wild and crimson colored, with his head bare and a clutch of
  thunderbolts in his hand" (p. 161, folio 140) — each offering head, shoulder or eyes as a step,
  the ascent ending "beneath the heavenly sphere of the moon" (p. 161, folio 140).
- **The fire-sphere's plea and its refusal** — fire asks that Ghawth's tread dissipate its fear of
  punishment for Iblis; Ghawth "became aware of a message" and answered "You are the guardian of the
  inner sanctuary of divine Might, yet there is no guarantee of your safety from punishment."
  (p. 161, folio 140)

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Vision is the sense through which transcendence is experienced, and the Prophet's ascension is
  its paradigm.** "Vision is the one sense that allows us to experience transcending the body"
  (p. 146, folio 125); "the visionary experience of the Prophet Muhammad confirmed and supported his
  auditory reception of revelation" (p. 146, folio 125); the Qur'an "weaves these two capacities of
  the body's sense perception together" so that acceptance depends "on the veracity of the messenger"
  (p. 146, folio 125).
- **The bodily/spiritual controversy is doctrinally positioned, not merely metaphysical.** "Dogmatic
  theologians have often insisted that the night journey and ascension were in and with the body in
  order to heighten their miraculous nature and set them up as a criterion for defining who is a true
  believer"; "More philosophically inclined theologians have speculated that the journey was out of
  body"; "Sufis assert that the ascension was both bodily and spiritual in order to assert that the
  ascension was not just a miracle granted to the Prophet but also an archetypal experience to be
  emulated by all believers" (p. 148, folio 127). "Sunni orthodoxy eventually settled on the decision
  that it was bodily to heighten its status as a miracle and set the Prophet apart from others"
  (p. 150, folio 129).
- **Poetry as the Sunni Sufi safety device.** "To avoid appearing to infringe on the superiority of
  the Prophet, Sufis normally were carefully circumspect, referring to experiences of ascension
  indirectly through metaphor or poetic innuendo" (p. 150, folio 129); "Most Sunni Sufis resort to
  poetry to express self-transcendence… letting the poetic resonance of words create the image of
  transcendence" (p. 151, folio 130). **"However, talking about the Prophet's ascension could be a
  dangerous enterprise."** (p. 150, folio 129)
- **The chapter's pivot: Ghawth defies that convention.** "In contrast, Muhammad Ghawth was not
  cautious. He broke with this pattern, as he advocated a new and relatively unknown Sufi order on
  South Asian soil." (p. 151, folio 130) "Muhammad Ghawth's narrative strategy renders the ineffable
  experience of confronting absolute being, or God, in first-person prose. He needs to, since he is
  ostensibly offering a firsthand account of his personal experience rather than some literary
  creation, philosophical exposition, or mystical parable." (p. 152, folio 131)
- **Kugle reads the ascension as an interior, not an astronomical, itinerary.** In outward form it
  "presents a passage up through the layers of the astrological cosmos, measured by the orbits,
  stars, and planets"; but "it becomes apparent as he reaches its climax that this passage is
  essentially an ascension through the existential levels of his own being, as he dissolves into pure
  being" (p. 152, folio 131).
- **The organ of the chapter is the eye, and the metaphor is "tissue."** "His calling his record a
  'tissue' highlights for us the importance of the body in his discourse, for as his physical body in
  its gross manifestation as material begins to fade, his personality becomes concentrated in the
  organ of his eyes" (p. 153, folio 132); "In this paradox of extraordinary vision, Muhammad Ghawth
  puts aside the limitation of the individual body and takes on the authority of the body politic"
  (p. 153, folio 132).
- **A direct correction of the existing scholarship.** "Modern scholars of South Asian Islamic
  history have seen him as an example of 'pantheism' or 'syncretic' religion" and "many scholars
  assume that Muhammad Ghawth suffered persecution for crossing the boundaries of religious
  communalism. However, close examination of his persecution shows that those Muslim scholars and
  Sufis who opposed him consistently attacked his ascension experience and the uses he put it to in
  furthering the struggle of the Mughals to control the body politic." (p. 154, folio 133) This is the
  chunk's sharpest historiographical claim.
- **Chishti versus Shattari, stated as a contrast of doctrines of the body.** "In contrast with
  Chishti teachings about common believers' devotional ascent that integrates spirit and body in
  selfless giving to others, Muhammad Ghawth insists that he ascended bodily" (p. 152, folio 131).
- **Shattari cosmology as descent rather than ascent.** "The Shattari disciple does not conceive of
  spiritual development as passing through a series of arduous stages but rather apprehends that one
  has descended downward into worldly existence from an initial and fundamental state of
  nondivergence from the One who creates." (p. 156, folio 135)
- **A methodological caution Kugle applies to his own source.** "The language of his description
  might even be called hallucinogenic, though that runs the risk of denying its authenticity."
  (p. 157, folio 136)

## Doubts

- **Folio relation: no contradiction found.** Every page in pp. 146–161 except p. 149 prints a
  running header, and every one of those fifteen headers agrees with `PDF − 21` once the letter
  substitutions are applied. **p. 149 genuinely prints no header** — it is the full-page plate page
  and carries only the caption and the two apparatus lines; it is cited `(p. 149, folio not stated)`.
  Three headers were damaged and were read through: `I2Q`→129 (p. 150), `I3O`→130 (p. 151), `I4O`→140
  (p. 161), all matching the measured substitutions `I`→1, `O`→0, `Q`→9.
- **The brief's illustration expectation does not match this chunk.** The illustrations list was said
  to place "The fallen angels Harut and Marut" and "Angels in human form" inside this range. **The
  only plate in pp. 146–161 is at p. 149** and it is the Buraq ascension miniature quoted above;
  neither Harut and Marut nor Angels in human form appears. If those captions are at *printed* 146
  and 154, they fall at PDF 167 and 175, i.e. in a later chunk. A human should confirm which reading
  of the illustrations list is right before anyone treats those plates as missing.
- **`shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar` rests on a bare name.** The mapping is made under the ruling of
  18 September 2026: Farid al-Din Ganj-i Shakar is an archive row's principal figure and is named in
  the chunk, so the row takes the bullet even though the mention is a single clause inside a quoted
  oath (p. 159, folio 138). **Nothing about a shrine, Pakpattan, a date or a silsila is present**, and
  the consolidator should not let the row's own attributes leak back into this page reference.
  "Ajhodani" is a nisba inside the name, not a free-standing toponym, so the interim bare-toponym
  convention does not apply to it; if a future ruling treats a nisba as a toponym, the row this
  bullet would move to is still `shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar`, so the mapping is stable either
  way.
- **Refused candidate: `data-darbar`.** The oath swears "by the Closeness of Ali, the friend of God"
  (p. 159, folio 138). That row's principal figure is "Hazrat Ali ibn Usman al-Hujwiri (Data Ganj
  Bakhsh)". **Refused**: the chunk gives no nisba, no "Hujwiri", no "Data Ganj Bakhsh" and no Lahore,
  and positions this Ali immediately after "the Light of Muhammad, the beloved of God" in a sequence
  of prophets and earliest figures, which reads as Ali ibn Abi Talib. Recorded rather than mapped.
- **Refused candidate: the Chishti-Nizami rows, for Nizam al-Din Awliya.** He is the second most
  prominent figure in the chunk (pp. 148, 150–152) but **is not the principal figure of any of the
  169 rows**. `khawaja-feroz-ud-din-gharib-nawaz` ("Chishti Nizamia Qadria") and
  `darbar-hazrat-khawaja-shah-muhammad-sulaiman-taunsvi-r-a` ("Chishti-Nizami") carry his name only
  in their *silsila* field, not as principal figure, so neither takes him. **Refused on both.** If
  the archive ever adds a Nizam al-Din Awliya row, every page cited for him in this chunk is
  material for it.
- **Refused candidates for the yoga material.** "the Sanskrit text Amrta-Kunda … on the principles of
