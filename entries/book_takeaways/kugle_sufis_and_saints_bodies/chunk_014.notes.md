## Practices, institutions, economy

- **`Sama` as the vehicle of a doctrine** — the "oneness of being" "was popularized in South Asia
  through `sama`[text-layer?] [samāʿ], or listening to love poetry in a devotional setting that
  might erupt into ecstatic gesture or rapturous dance; such ritual and the attitude behind it were
  clearly in harmony with the bhakti movement" (pp. 230–231, folios 209–210).
- **`Shahid-bazi`, "playing the witness game"** — "Such a practice is called, in Persian and Urdu,
  `shahid-bazl`[text-layer?] [shahid-bazi], or 'playing the witness game.' It has a long history in
  Persianate Sufism as a sign of surrender and renunciation" (p. 230, folio 209); "The Sufi practice
  of playing the witness game was worshiping the beauty of God as it might manifest in any created
  form." (p. 230, folio 209) "Playing the witness game was so popular in South Asia by the sixteenth
  century that a vocal minority of cautious and juridical Sufis began to write against it." (p. 231,
  folio 210)
- **Annual observance at the tomb** — "a riotous celebration on the anniversary of his death,
  accompanied by frenzied drumming, ecstatic dance, and blazing fires" (p. 241, folio 220). The word
  ʿurs is not used; no date, duration, attendance figure, offering, langar or revenue is given:
  **(not stated)**.
- **Custodianship** — "the custodians of his tomb" (p. 241, folio 220), unnamed and untitled; the
  book's only statement about them is that they "seem to have forgotten the erotic epic of his life
  story and the sexual nature of his love for Madho."
- **Patronage by Mughal nobles** — "The admiration of Mughal nobles protects him from local censure,
  and reciprocally, he becomes a saint who protects Mughal rule." (p. 241, folio 220) "Nobles began
  to patronize him, seeking blessings for their various endeavors." (p. 241, folio 220) No grants,
  land or endowments are named.
- **State offices in Lahore, as the chunk names them** — the `kotwal` or police chief (p. 233, folio
  212); "the chief judge" (p. 233, folio 212); the `Shaykh al-Islam`, "chief religious scholar and
  jurist of the realm" (p. 239, folio 218); the `Qadi`, at whose discretion `ta'zir` penalties are
  set (p. 238, folio 217); and the emperor's court, Lahore being "a seasonal capital city and home
  of the Mughal court during its perennial rotation" (p. 233, folio 212).
- **Legal apparatus** — "The most famous medieval collection of legal decisions in South Asia was
  the `Fatawa-yi Tatar-Khaniyya`, compiled in the fourteenth century." (p. 235, folio 214) "Crimes
  of a sexual nature are found in 'Kitab al-Hudud' ('Chapter on Exemplary Punishments')." (p. 235,
  folio 214) The two categories are defined in the text: "`hadd` (exemplary punishment of a fixed
  form dictated by scripture) or `tazlr`[text-layer?] [taʿzīr] (discretionary punishment of a
  flexible form determined by the will of authorities)" (p. 235, folio 214).
- **Impaling as an extra-judicial state practice** — "we find that medieval rulers in South Asia did
  apply impaling as punishment, beyond the bounds of the `sharia` and outside the jurisdiction of
  religious courts. They applied it to rebels and upstarts as a form of military punishment, meant
  to be public, horrifying, and deterrent to further rebellion. However, impaling was never applied
  to interpersonal crimes, civil cases, or sexual indiscretions." (pp. 238–239, folios 217–218)
- **A ruling on hajj, and an allegation about zakat** — the Shaykh al-Islam's ruling "that the hajj
  to Mecca was no longer obligatory for all Muslims" (p. 240, folio 219), and the allegation that he
  avoided "paying `zakat`, obligatory alms, on the wealth that he accumulated" (p. 240, folio 219).
- **Ritual discipline of the body (chapter 5)** — "Hajji Imdadullah created a ritual that would
  discipline the body. Imdadullah's ritual manual focuses on the heart." (p. 245, folio 224)
  "Imdadullah suggests meditations linked to physical movements to create a concentration that
  centers consciousness on the heart, specifically on rectifying the heart. The results are not
  merely spiritual or ethical but anatomically physical." (p. 245, folio 224) The instruction quoted
  and cut off mid-sentence: "Repeat three times 'God give me light, bestow upon me light, increase
  for me the light, and make me myself light.' … As you allow your body to lighten and feel at ease,
  imagine you have died, and gather all your attention from head to" (p. 245, folio 224).
- **Public plate apparatus** — a plate caption occupies the head of p. 237 (folio 216), ahead of the
  body text; see Doubts for the damaged accession string.

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The chains that would not hold** — "They picture Shah Hussayn confronting the police chief with a
  miracle: the chains kept falling from his ankles no matter how the police chief struggled to
  secure them." (p. 239, folio 218) Attributed by the book to "The hagiographic sources", unnamed.
- **The police chief's death** — "In the end, the police chief himself is impaled to death upon the
  command of the emperor for having failed him in some unrelated matter." (p. 239, folio 218)
  Presented by the book as the hagiographic resolution, "This violent end to one who threatens a
  saint" (p. 239, folio 218).
- **Wine turned to water at Akbar's court** — "The royal inquisition centered on whether Shah
  Hussayn drank wine in public and whether he was miraculously able to turn wine into milk, water,
  tea, or punch." … "According to the hagiographic record of his life, Shah Hussayn was granted a
  miracle, which turned the `emperors`[text-layer?] [emperor's] goblet of wine into water and other
  beverages in his presence, convincing the emperor of the `saints`[text-layer?] [saint's]
  authenticity despite his evident eccentricity." (p. 240, folio 219)
- **Shaykh Bahlul's reversal** — his journey to Lahore to reprove Hussayn, his recognition on seeing
  him "in ripped red clothes, dancing and drinking wine", and his blessing (p. 232, folio 211).
  Offered by the book as a pattern-setting episode, not as a wonder.
- **Amir Khusro's dance in women's clothes before Nizam al-Din Awliyāʾ** (p. 230, folio 209) and
  **Musa Sada Sohag's permanent assumption of women's clothes and bangles** (p. 230, folio 209),
  both flagged as "hagiographic stories".
- **The hadith Imdadullah invokes** — "God is reported to have said, 'My earth and my heaven do not
  encompass me, but the heart of my faithful servant does encompass me.'" (p. 245, folio 224)

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- **Shah Hussayn as a point of contact between traditions** — "By exploring these countercultural
  currents in the Sufi tradition, we can better understand how Shah Hussayn's personality is a point
  of contact between Iranian and `Indie`[text-layer?] [Indic], between Islamic and Hindu, between
  Sufi and bhakti." (p. 229, folio 208)
- **Inversion of norms as supplication** — "Anthropologists have often described how the inversion
  of social norms is a form of supplication or a way of requesting closeness to a source of power."
  (p. 229, folio 208) Kugle's summary of Hammoudi: "A male disciple could approach a male spiritual
  guide only in one of two ways: as seducer in a homoerotic social role that inverts sexual norms,
  or as a subservient effeminate in a feminine social role that inverts gender norms." (p. 229,
  folio 208, chapter 4 note 53)
- **Two distinct arguments, explicitly separated** — "The relationship of gender transgression and
  sainthood presented here consists of two intermeshed but distinct arguments." (p. 229, folio 208)
  Kugle's conclusion on their relation: "In this way, transgender behavior and homoerotic behavior
  are closely linked but are not reducible to each other." (p. 229, folio 208)
- **Where Shah Hussayn differs from Musa Sada Sohag** — "In contrast, Shah Hussayn did not
  consistently invert gender roles but rather inverted sexuality roles through same-sex eroticism."
  (p. 230, folio 209)
- **The doctrinal underpinning** — "Their poetry is completely informed by `wahdat al-wujud`, the

## Doubts

- **Folio census: no discrepancy, and one page with no header.** Every page in pp. 229–245 prints a
  running header except **p. 242**, the display page of chapter 5, which prints the chapter number
  `5`, the title `BODY REVIVED`, the subtitle `The Heart of Hajji Imdadullah` and a Qur'anic epigraph
  in place of a header. It is cited `(p. 242, folio not stated)` throughout and no folio is predicted
  for it. Printed folio 221 therefore does not appear anywhere in this chunk. All sixteen other pages
  agree with `PDF − 21`; folios 211–218 and 222–224 are printed in undamaged digits, which is the
  strongest internal confirmation of the relation available in this range.
- **The archive row's death year is not corroborated here.** The archive records `1599` for Shah
  Hussain; this chunk gives no year of death at all, only "the anniversary of his death" (p. 241,
  folio 220). Nothing in pp. 229–245 may be cited for the date, and the two must not be merged.
- **Silsila contradiction, kept open.** The archive row `madho-lal-hussain` records `Malamati`. This
  chunk ascribes to him "a long tradition in Sufism of Qalandar rebelliousness" (p. 229, folio 208)
  and states no silsila. A `Qadiri` ascription reported from elsewhere in this book does not occur in
  this range. Three ascriptions are therefore in play across the book's own pages and the archive's
  own cell; **none is reconciled here** and a human must decide what an entry says.
- **Krishna: two candidate rows, neither chosen.** "Krishna's dance of bewildering love, the
  `ras-lila`" (p. 231, folio 210). Krishna is the principal figure of **both**
  `krishna-mandir-kabari-bazar` (Kabari Bazar, Rawalpindi) and `krishna-mandir-ravi-road` (Ravi Road,
  Lahore). The chunk names no temple, city or festival that would distinguish them, so **no id is
  assigned** and the fact is parked under "Other saints, sites and events". Note that the ruled
  person-convention would otherwise pull it in; it is the two-row ambiguity, not the
  literary-reference status, that blocks the mapping.
- **"Qalandar" is a category word here, not a person.** `lal-shahbaz-qalandar` (Lal Shahbaz Qalandar,
  Sehwan) and `shrine-of-qalandar-baba-auliya` (Karachi) are **refused**: the chunk uses "Qalandar
  rebelliousness" (p. 229, folio 208) of a tradition, and neither saint is named.
- **`darbar-malik-ahmad-ayaz` refused.** The chunk's "Kotwal Malik `CAH`[text-layer?] [ʿAli]"
  (p. 233, folio 212) / "the police chief, Malik Ali" (p. 239, folio 218) is a Mughal-era police
  chief of Lahore under Akbar; the archive row's principal figure is Malik Ahmad Ayaz, described
  there as slave of Mahmud Ghaznavi and governor of Lahore. Shared element is the title "Malik" and
  the city only. **Not mapped.**
