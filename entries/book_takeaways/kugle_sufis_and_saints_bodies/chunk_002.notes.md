## Practices, institutions, economy

Nothing institutional, economic or shrine-specific. **No ʿurs, mela, langar, offering, custodianship,
endowment, land, revenue, visitor number, dispute, state involvement or building date occurs anywhere
in pp. 31–47** — all **(not stated)**. What the chunk does supply is a typology of *religious bodily
practice* in the abstract, which an entry could use only as framing, never as evidence about a site:

- Ascetic practice, as the extreme of the consciousness Kugle calls restraint: "extreme fasting,
  penitential self-mortification, or other forms of sensory deprivation" (p. 43, folio 22); and "In
  far more moderate ways, restraint is the basis of any ritual activity, like prayer or recitation,
  that focuses awareness away from sensuality of the body" (p. 43, folio 22). Its furthest religious
  extreme "takes the form of contemplation in which the body is held totally still and under complete
  control and intimate surveillance by the ego" (p. 44, folio 23).
- Merit-earning practice, as the extreme of engagement: "activities to earn merit, in which the body
  is focused and extended into acts (ritual or social or artistic) that accrue merit in the next world"
  (p. 45, folio 24).
- Ecstatic and musical practice, as the extreme of rapture: "experience of the body in spontaneity
  and total exertion, such as in play (in pure games, sports, drama, or musical performance) and
  especially in dance" (p. 45, folio 24); at its extreme "ecstatic experiences, such as possession,
  in which the ego is so intimately suffused with the body that it temporary loses self-consciousness
  or self-recognition and is understood to have become united with divinity, possessed by a spirit or
  divine being" (p. 45, folio 24). "temporary loses" is as printed.
- Martyrdom, as the extreme of release: "self-willed death or active surrender to martyrdom, giving
  up life either for the betterment of the social group (thanatomania) or upon the demand of a
  divinity (self-sacrifice)" (p. 46, folio 25).
- Regeneration, a fifth overlapping state, is given in maintenance terms — "to consume nourishment;
  find protection from danger; maintain the body through excretion, hygiene, and restoration of
  health; and rest in safety" (p. 46, folio 25).
- Tantric ritual is named once, as a comparative instance of restraint: people "engage in sex for
  pleasure but withhold themselves from orgasm in order to practice restraint and sublimation of
  sexual urges, as in Tantric ritual" (p. 43, folio 22). **No tradition, place or lineage is attached
  to it: (not stated).**

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

- The chunk's historiographical claim: the body became "a crucial analytic category" in "the past two
  decades of 'postmodern' intellectual debate" (p. 31, folio 10), and Kugle reads this through
  Strathern, quoted at length — "Embodiment is, therefore, a new humanism, not exactly soteriological
  but one that is intended to bring us back to ourselves. It is, put simply, a reaction against
  disembodiment." (p. 31, folio 10). Strathern calls it "a muted universalism" in anthropology and
  social theory (p. 31, folio 10).
- Kugle states his own limit on the literature explicitly: "This study delves into this archive in
  only the briefest way, as many other, more skillful scholars have focused whole books on the issue"
  (p. 31, folio 10).
- The genealogy he accepts: "Marcel Mauss and Mary Douglas were the first scholars to have shown us
  how basic are conceptions of the human body to religious systems of meaning" (p. 31, folio 10);
  Mauss built on Durkheim, "his teacher and father-in-law", in writings of the
  `19305[text-layer?]` [1930s] (p. 31, folio 10). Mauss's key move for Kugle is "habitus", which
  "'does not designate those metaphysical habitudes (acquired abilities or faculties) … [but rather]
  the techniques and work of collective and individual practical reason'" (pp. 31–32, folios 10–11),
  and the claim that "there is no natural body" (p. 32, folio 11), bodily action having an "effective
  aim" and a "religious or symbolic aim" (p. 32, folio 11).
- Douglas is presented as Mauss's successor: `Douglass[text-layer?] [Douglas's] first major work on
  the body, Purity and Danger, consequently focused on religious data, Jewish pollution laws, to
  explore how the human body acts as a metaphor for social order" (p. 32, folio 11); her second,
  Natural Symbols, is where she argues "the physical body is not a biological given that is prior to
  socialization but rather is already understood as a physical body through social categories"
  (p. 32, folio 11). Her strongest claim, as Kugle renders it: "the human body is the central
  symbolic resource in all religious systems despite their great variety precisely because the body's
  shape, its divisions, and its privations are universal" (p. 33, folio 12).
- Bryan Turner's four problems every society must solve — reproduction, "the regulation and control
  of a population through politics", "the restraint of desire through the elaboration of mores and
  morality", and "the representation of bodies to each other" — are given as the refinement of
  Douglas in `The Body and Society`, in the `19805[text-layer?]` [1980s] (p. 33, folio 12).
- The philosophical stake is anti-Cartesian throughout: studies of social theory "critique and
  overcome the Cartesian dualism between body and mind, which Descartes had adopted from Christian
  thought and valorized in European philosophy" (p. 33, folio 12); Merleau-Ponty supplies the body as
  "a 'point of view upon the world'" (p. 33, folio 12) and bodily spatiality as "the very condition
  for the coming into being of a meaningful world" (p. 34, folio 13); Bourdieu supplies "the mind is
  a metaphor of the world of objects" from his Algerian Berber research and adapts "habitus" from
  Mauss (p. 34, folio 13). Kugle rejects both Plato's vehicle metaphor and Descartes' subsidiary mind
  (p. 34, folio 13).
- **The chunk's central polemic — Islam's absence from the embodiment literature.** "scholars
  contributing to the new literature on religion and the body seem to shy away from Islam; worse,
  many do not recognize that this leaves their writing with serious gaps" (p. 35, folio 14), and "It
  is as if Western scholarship has surrendered Islam to Wahhabi puritans to define as a uniquely
  'disembodied' religion" (p. 35, folio 14) — a problem "we will explore in the conclusion" (p. 35,
  folio 14). His exhibit is the three-volume French collection `Fragments for a History of the Human
  Body`, whose editors are quoted asking "what kind of body do these same Greeks, Christians, Jews or
  Chinese endow themselves with—or attempt to acquire—given the power they attribute to the divine?"
  (pp. 35–36, folios 14–15), and whose essays span "Greek, Jewish, Hindu, Daoist, Gnostic, Orthodox
  Christian, Latin Christian, and Japanese Buddhist cultures" — "However, there is no mention of
  Islam" (p. 36, folio 15).
- His graded verdicts on the Islamic-studies literature, in his own words: Talal Asad "particularly
  acute in forcing anthropology of the body into questions of comparison between Islam and
  Christianity, focusing on discipline"; "Less persuasive is the ethnographic work of Fuad Khuri,"
  faulted because "Khuri assumes that Arabic culture defines Islamic culture and that contemporary
  Arabic culture offers a timeless access to what is essentially Islamic"; "More promising is the
  work of Marion Katz"; and Pnina Werbner and Helene Basu, though they "pushed the limits of past
  Islamic scholarship with their study of the performance of charisma through body practices," produce
  a study that "becomes amorphous and abstract" (p. 36, folio 15). **Kugle states his own method
  against theirs**: "This present work will try to follow their hints while maintaining a more
  concrete focus on discrete case examples in people known as saints and the hagiographic texts that
  present them to the public" (p. 36, folio 15) — the closest the chunk comes to the archive's
  subject matter.
- He credits women for the field's progress: "Recent advances in studying embodiment in Islamic
  culture are due largely to the work of female scholars, who use, sometimes explicitly and sometimes
  only implicitly feminist theory" (pp. 36–37, folios 15–16), and states the book's own contention —
  "extending the study of embodiment into Islamic studies is crucial, not only for women but for men"
  (p. 37, folio 16). His success condition is deliberately modest: "If this study achieves nothing
  more than this, it will have been successful" (p. 37, folio 16).
- **Al-Zahi's four-dimension scheme**, restated by Kugle "translated into English from the Arabic"
  (p. 38, folio 17) and built "around the Arabic root letters j-s-d, which provide the basic structure
  of the simple `wordjasad[text-layer?]` [word *jasad*], the body" (p. 38, folio 17). The four, as
  printed: "jasad, or corps; `jasadl[text-layer?]` [jasadi], or corporal; `jasadam[text-layer?]`
  [jasadani], or corporeal; `andjasadaniyya[text-layer?]` [and jasadaniyya], or corporeality"
  (p. 38, folio 17), imagined as concentric circles with the physical body at the core (p. 38,
  folio 17). Al-Zahi's own summary is quoted: "The body is the a priori material while the bodily is
  an almost ethnographic description of the daily postures of the body … Finally corporeality is the
  higher practices of the body and the appearance of its interpretations" (p. 40, folio 19). Kugle
  adds a caution against reading the diagram hierarchically: "the outermost edge feeds back into the
  center; the physical body as a biological organism could not survive without the social, cultural,
  and symbolic dimensions that surround and permeate it. The whole is not reducible to its parts"
  (p. 41, folio 20).
- **The Chebel–al-Zahi dispute**, which Kugle adopts as his own. Chebel is quoted: "The vast majority
  of references to the body in Islam are to the body parts rather than to the whole body. Therefore
  the Muslim world gives importance only to the heart, or the hand, or the eyes, or the sexual organ"
  (pp. 41–42, folios 20–21). Al-Zahi's reply is quoted in full, asking whether Islamic imagination
  is really absent: "Does the imagination exhibited by the foundational Islamic texts cancel out the
  innovative developments opened by the Muslim theologians, philosophers and Sufis?" (p. 42,
  folio 21).
- **Kugle's own thesis in this chunk, and the one with the widest implications**: "Islamic discourse
  focuses on body parts in a limited vision only when Islam is reduced to the Islamic legal discourse,
  which is all too common among Western analysts and among some Muslims" (p. 42, folio 21); "In
  contrast to Islamic law, Sufi discourse, which is just as 'Islamic' as legal discourse … is rich
  with discussions of the body" (p. 42, folio 21); and, reformist, "Islamic law actually needs
  insights from Sufism to reform its legal structure, especially around issues of human rights, civil
  equality, and respect for ethical inviolability of individual bodies. This book focuses on Sufism
  and the body, in part, to foster that necessary dialogue" (p. 42, folio 21).
- **Kugle's own addition to al-Zahi** — four states of *consciousness* of the body, crossing
  al-Zahi's four dimensions "like four quadrants of the circular diagram" (p. 43, folio 22): restraint
  ("being against the body"), engagement ("being through the body"), rapture ("being with the body")
  and release ("being in the body"), with a fifth overlapping state, regeneration ("being for the
  body") (pp. 43–46, folios 22–25). "Restraint and engagement both stress the ego's distance from and
  transcendence above the body; in contrast, the states of rapture and release emphasize the ego's
  intimacy with and immanence in the body" (p. 43, folio 22). He concedes a limit on the
  anti-Cartesian case: "Contemporary phenomenological studies of embodiment can insist that a
  Cartesian split is an epiphenomenon rather than an ontological reality, but such a split … is a
  persistent feature of human experience" (p. 42, folio 21).
- The scheme's pay-off, in his words: it "offers us a way to understand how the body can be a
  biological organism, an expressive medium, a source of psychological yearnings, and a symbol for
  religious coherence" and helps "understand the variety of religious responses to the body, from
  ritual engagement to ascetic restraint, from ecstatic rapture to self-abandonment in martyrdom"
  (p. 47, folio 26).
- A political-historical aside worth keeping: "appeal to the purported supremacy of reason has
  reinforced the imperialist domination of Europe and America, based on theories of racial
  superiority, over the Asian and African worlds", and "Racial anthropology fell into disuse only
  because the Nazis applied its ideas and techniques to other European bodies, rather than to
  'primitive' or 'oriental' bodies that were subject to European colonial domination" (p. 35,
  folio 14). The search for universal values intensified "after decolonization (in the period after
  World War II through the `19605[text-layer?]` [1960s]) and more immediately after the dissolution
  of Cold War ideologies" (p. 35, folio 14).
