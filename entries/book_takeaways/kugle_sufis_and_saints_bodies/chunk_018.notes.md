## Practices, institutions, economy

Everything under this heading is reported *by the book* from Wahhabi sources that condemn it, except
where marked as Kugle's own. That framing must survive into any entry that uses it.

- **The condemned physical furniture of a shrine, itemised** — the commentator Abd al-Rahman: "It is
  especially the praise of their [holy peoples] graves that causes this exaggeration that leads to
  worshiping them, like building domes over them, covering them with cloths, lighting lamps nearby,
  and the establishing of custodians" (p. 307, folio 286). **This is the chunk's most directly usable
  passage for the archive: dome, cloth, lamp and custodian named together as the constituents of a
  functioning tomb shrine.**
- **Offerings and shrine revenue** — continuing the same sentence: "Satan stays close by them to call
  the people to worship them with all types of dedicated offerings and the money accrues to them"
  (p. 307, folio 286). The chunk gives **no sums, no shares and no named recipient institution: all
  (not stated).**
- **Grave architecture judged unlawful** — the commentator "charges that any grave marked as a body
  is not Islamic, writing that Islam acknowledges only those graves over which is built nothing, upon
  which is written nothing, which are not covered with cloths of silk or other substances" (p. 308,
  folio 287). Enclosures are named too: graves "around which are built those enclosures (maqasir)"
  (p. 308, folio 287).
- **Visitation, circumambulation, and contact with grave dirt** — from Sulayman's refutation, the
  practices over which Wahhabis called other Muslims infidels: "especially those who visit the graves
  of ancestors or holy people, who circumambulate the graves, who wipe themselves with dirt from the
  graves, or take blessing from objects associated with graves" (p. 301, folio 280).
- **Local tomb visitation as a substitute for the hajj** — Kugle's own statement, and a comparative
  claim the archive can use: believers "compensate for their inability to travel there with
  visitation to the local tombs of saints" (p. 299, folio 278).
- **Prostration at a saint's threshold** — of Ahmad Badawi's devotees: "Among them are some who
  prostrated on the threshold of his presence [at his tomb]" (p. 306, folio 285). The bracketed
  gloss is Kugle's.
- **Carrying a stone from the Kaʿba and circumambulating it elsewhere** — Ibn al-Kalbi: "No one left
  Mecca without carrying away with him a stone from the stones of the Sacred House as a token of
  reverence to it.... Wherever he settled, he would erect that stone and circumambulate it in the
  same manner that he used to circumambulate the `Kacba[text-layer?]` [Kaʿba]" (p. 304, folio 283,
  note 35 of the Conclusion). **A portable-relic practice, reported as pre-Islamic Arab custom.**
- **Dhikr, as described by a hostile witness** — the Wahhabi editor: "Some of the Sufis go beyond the
  bounds of insolence in claiming that the Prophet is present in their assemblies of uproar and hand
  clapping.... In these assemblies of noise and nonsense that they call remembrance of God (dhikr),
  they spread a white cloth in the center of their circle for the Prophet and his vice-regents to sit
  upon" (p. 300, folio 279, note 25 of the Conclusion). The same passage attacks the economics of
  Sufi retreat: "They waste their lives in isolated retreats, muttering and droning babble, and spend
  all their wealth on the Satanic deceivers who swindle them
  (`al-dajjal In al-mushcawidhin[text-layer?]`), who beguile them with all this out of greed"
  (p. 300, folio 279).
- **Amulets, sacred trees and stones, animal sacrifice** — the folk practices Ibn Abd al-Wahhab
  condemns first: "wearing a brass ring to ward off disease, wearing a thread amulet to ward off
  calamity, wearing charms on the body to gain specific benefits, visiting sacred trees and stones to
  gain blessing, or sacrificing an animal to any power other than God" (p. 298, folio 277, note 20 of
  the Conclusion).
- **Vows, consecration, and the two petitionary practices** — "he argues that it is equally shirk to
  consecrate or dedicate anything to other than God, to take a vow with any power other than God, to
  seek protection (`istiadha][text-layer?]` [istiʿadha]) or sustenance (istighatha) with anyone other
  than God. This gross associationism (shirk akbar) amounts to blatant infidelity (kufr)" (p. 298,
  folio 277, note 21 of the Conclusion).
- **Istighatha defined, and its early precedent** — "which means to appoint someone as the mediator
  between those in need and God, who provides sustenance, usually in the form of rain during drought
  or food during famine. Members of the early Islamic community called upon the Prophet to pray to
  God for rain" (p. 298, folio 277). **Rain-petition at a holy person's intercession, with a claimed
  precedent in the Prophet's own community.**
- **Intercession (shafaʿa) and the means (wasila)** — the hadith Sunnis and Sufis rely on: "my
  intercession (`shafaty[text-layer?]` [shafaʿa]) is made available on the day of reckoning to
  whoever petitions God [to bless and sanctify my name] so that I might be granted the means (wasila)
  to reach intimacy with God" (p. 299, folio 278, note 23 of the Conclusion).
- **Intent as the criterion** — Kugle's institutional point: "Turning to spiritual authorities through
  such practices is quite ambiguous and difficult to judge, since the practices depend upon the
  practitioner's intent (`niyyaj[text-layer?]` [niyya])" (p. 298, folio 277).
- **Praying over tombs as Qur'anic** — Kugle charges that Ibn Abd al-Wahhab "did not deal plainly
  with the `Qur'aris[text-layer?]` [Qur'an's] affirming of the practice of praying over tombs
  (*Q 9:83-84*)" (p. 300, folio 279).
- **Statues erected over graves, and circumambulation of them** — Ibn al-Kalbi's account: "he carved
  five statues depicting the images of their departed relatives and erected them over their graves.
  The relatives would visit the graves marked by these images, paying respect to them by walking
  around the statues" (p. 303, folio 282, note 32 of the Conclusion).
- **State suppression, then and now** — the movement "destroyed the tomb shrines that were the central
  meeting places of Sufis and the architectural focus of their spiritual geography" (p. 301,
  folio 280); today it "bans Sufi literature, forbids visitors from lingering near the tombs they
  have not razed, and arrests people on charges of 'practicing Sufism'" (pp. 301–302, folios
  280–281).
- **Legal consequence of the doctrine** — the eighteen-point list ends with "advice to us to destroy
  those people who are pig-headedly obstinate (`mutantfin[text-layer?]`)" (p. 305, folio 284, note 37
  of the Conclusion), and Kugle reads the list as making Sufis "apostates who can be legally killed"
  and their "blood and property... therefore forfeit" (pp. 305, 307, folios 284, 286).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

All four are reported at second or third hand and are attributed here exactly as the book attributes
them.

- **Ahmad Badawi's powers, as alleged by his devotees and reported by a hostile Wahhabi commentator**
  — "they believed that he had power over destiny in the universe, to extinguish fires and rescue the
  drowning; so they treat him as if he had divinity and lordship and knowledge of the unseen. They
  believed that he could hear them and answer them from distant locations" (pp. 306–307, folios
  285–286, note 42 of the Conclusion).
- **The Prophet's presence in the dhikr assembly** — claimed, per the Wahhabi editor, by "Some of the
  Sufis", who hold "that the Prophet is present in their assemblies of uproar and hand clapping",
  and of whom "Some of them even claim that the Prophet pervades the entire universe" (p. 300,
  folio 279, note 25). Ibn Idris's followers describe their dhikr in their own words as
  "accompanied by a visible manifestation when you see the Prophet himself" (p. 300, folio 279).
- **Satan's inspiration of the first idols, attributed to Ibn Abbas** — "These are the names of holy
  men of the people of Noah. Once they were destroyed, Satan inspired in the minds of the people of
  `Noah/[text-layer?]`Erect in their places of assembly where they used to gather some constructions
  and call them by their names.' So they did this, but without worshiping [them]. Then after those
  [people] perished and the knowledge was forgotten, they [the constructions] were worshiped"
  (p. 303, folio 282, note 30 of the Conclusion). Kugle calls this "an obscure interpretation"
  (p. 302, folio 281) and "a report about Ibn Abbas" that Ibn Abd al-Wahhab "allegedly" relies on
  (p. 302, folio 281).
- **The first grave, and the origin of pilgrimage** — Ibn al-Kalbi's tradition as Kugle glosses it:
  "the first human body provided the raw material for the first grave, the first graven image, and
  the first gravel path of pilgrimage and circumambulation" (p. 303, folio 282), the grave being
  Adam's, "in a cave on the mountain on which he had alighted on earth, on the island of Sarandip
  (Sri Lanka)" (p. 303, folio 282).
- **The son of Cain's pious warning** — "'O ye who are bereaved! Shall I make unto you statues after
  the image of your departed relatives? I can readily do that, although I cannot impart life to
  them'" (p. 303, folio 282, note 32).

## Arguments and interpretations (academic books)

This is the chunk's substance, and the heading the conclusion exists to fill.

- **Wahhabi ideology as an attack on history.** "Past forms of Islam were a threat to be discarded,
  dismantled, or dismembered in order to make way for the Utopian advent of pure Islam. It is as if
  no Muslim had ever lived out the religion of Islam since the `Prophets[text-layer?]` [Prophet's]
  immediate followers passed away, until Ibn Abd al-Wahhab came upon the scene" (p. 294, folio 273).
  Kugle adds a charge against his own discipline: "So powerful has this ideology been that even some
  insightful Western scholars have accepted it as the essential form of Islam that defines the
  religion" (p. 294, folio 273, note 10 of the Conclusion).
- **Wahhabi ideology as an attack on reason, and the impossibility of its own claim.** "Just as the
  Wahhabis despised history, they distrusted reason"; in fact "Ibn Abd al-Wahhab rejected all the
  schools of Islamic law and all the processes of juridical reasoning and community consensus upon
  which they were based", and "Although the `Wahab-bis[text-layer?]` [Wahhabis] claimed to champion
  Sunni orthodoxy, in fact they rejected the theological schools upon which the Sunni consensus had
  been built" (p. 294, folio 273). Kugle's rebuttal is flat: "Of course, this is impossible. All
  reading requires reason and interpretation, even one that disavows rational interpretation"
  (p. 294, folio 273). The single retained tool was "hadith, traditional reports about what the
  Prophet Muhammad is alleged to have said or done" (p. 294, folio 273, note 11), and Kugle turns
  that against itself: "writing down hadith was just as much an innovation in religion as legal
  reasoning, discursive theology, or mystical allegory (in fact, all these interpretive traditions
  took form in roughly the same era, the two or three centuries after the Prophet Muhammad's death)"
  (p. 294, folio 273).
- **What filled the gap.** "Tribal chauvinism, Arab nationalism, and patriarchy were all pre-rational
  and informal sources for their ideals and to a great extent shaped the results of their
  rhetorically 'purist' interpretation" (p. 294, folio 273).
- **Reduction as method.** Under the sub-heading `Body Image and Idolatry`: Ibn Abd al-Wahhab "was a
  genius as a reductionist. He reduced complexity into simplicity and reduced subtle shades of
  difference into polar binaries. Such is the power of ideology. He reduced Muslim Sufis into
  non-Islamic pagans, reduced their complex devotion into 'worship of tombs,' and reduced their
  mystical path, their way of finding signs of God in the created world and the human body, to simple
  idolatry" (p. 294, folio 273). The reduction extends to form: "he even reduced writing from
  discursive prose into a list of statements" (p. 295, folio 274, note 12 of the Conclusion).
- **The theology Kugle extracts from the doctrine.** Belief is combined "with a deeply pessimistic
  assessment of human nature, for any action outside of immediate conformity is 'perversion'
  (`itbac al-hawa'}[text-layer?]`)"; "any change in Islamic practice or thought is 'deviation'
  (bid'a)"; "any trust in human conscience or the human body is 'hypocrisy'
  (`zandaqa'j[text-layer?]`)" (p. 295, folio 274). The group is "the one saved community" (al-firqa
  al-najiya), whose members "will have access to heaven without divine judgment" (p. 295, folio 274,
  note 14 of the Conclusion). Kugle's own conclusion: "Although he does not state so explicitly, Ibn
  Abd al-Wahhab's vision of belief, action, and judgment pictures a very limited role for God, for
  his is a God who understands no ambiguity, admits no subtlety, allows no intercession, judges
  without allowing purgation, and acts without mercy" (p. 295, folio 274). He also traces "a
  puritanical attitude characteristic of the Khariji movement" (p. 295, folio 274).
- **The Sufi counter-position, stated as doctrine about bodies.** Sufis "hold that interior states of
  faith and belief are primary over outer actions, such that restraining from judging others is the
  best way to avoid being judged" (p. 296, folio 275). They hold "that the Prophet continues to be
  present in the world. The `Prophets[text-layer?]` [Prophet's] essence is light that is eternal
  within and beyond the body, his presence is felt closely at his tomb, and his charismatic intuition
  is continually perpetuated in his followers, in those `shurafa'` who emulate his character and in
  those saints who exemplify his virtues" (p. 296, folio 275). **This is the book's own three-term
  model of how sacred power persists — essence, tomb, living successors — and it is stated here in
  one sentence.**
- **The two definitions of an idol.** "Sufis also demean the worshiping of idols, but they have a
  radically different understanding of what constitutes an 'idol'" (p. 296, folio 275); "Despite his
  contempt for the worship of objects as idols, Nakhshabi and other Sufis assert that the tombs of
  prophets and saints are not at all lifeless and therefore undermine the Wahhabi critique" (p. 296,
  folio 275); and, decisively, "For Sufis, to follow one's egoistic urges is to worship an idol—in
  their mystical and psychologically penetrating view, the only real idolatry is selfishness"
  (p. 296, folio 275).
- **The contest over a Qur'anic term.** Ibn Abd al-Wahhab "appropriates the term awliya' allah, 'the
  Friends of God'"; the term "is found in the Qur'an, so in fairness all Muslims can refer to it and
  must take account of its use. However, by the time of Ibn Abd al-Wahhab, the term had become, for
  the majority of Muslims, saturated with the meaning that Sufi activities generate" (p. 296,
  folio 275). His redefinition: "people are the lordly friends [of God] due to their keeping free of
  associating anything with God" (p. 297, folio 276, note 17 of the Conclusion). Kugle notes the
  Wahhabi editor then supplies "an explanatory note of brutal clarity" declaring Sufis "actually the
  friends of the tempter (`awliya shaytan][text-layer?]`)" (p. 297, folio 276, note 18), and argues
  these words "are clearly intended by Ibn Abd al-Wahhab himself", whereas in his own text "Ibn Abd
  al-Wahhab avoids making statements like this that smack of opinion and builds his argument more
  gradually through a pastiche of hadith and Qur'an citations" (p. 297, folio 276).
- **The argument "begins to circle obsessively around the human body."** Its three elements are
  "praise for Abraham, suspicion of Jews and Christians, and spite for pagans" (p. 297, folio 276,
  note 19 of the Conclusion), built on *Q 16:120*, *Q 9:31* and *Q 2:165* (p. 297, folio 276). Kugle:
  "It is a tantalizing argument, since these three elements are shared by many Muslims and have been
  around since the time of Muhammad himself. However, Ibn Abd al-Wahhab derives conclusions from
  these three points that no Muslim theologian had drawn before, with absolutism and virulence that
  justified violence" (pp. 297–298, folios 276–277).
- **Bodies as the target.** "Having warned his audience against taking bodies as God, whether they are
  material forms in the shape of bodies, celestial bodies, the corpus of tradition, or the bodies of
  religious authorities, he begins to detail the bodily practices that perpetrate the sin of
  associating others with God" (p. 298, folio 277).
- **Kugle's defence of mediation as intrinsic to mysticism.** "Islamic mysticism is based upon the goal
  of experiencing God's presence directly, a goal only attainable through the guidance, mediation,
  and help of a holy person. By placing trust in such persons, one's shaykh or spiritual guide, and
  turning to them with deference and respect as channels through which God's protection and nurture
  come, one can hope to internalize virtues, overcome selfishness, and render one's heart open to
  experiences of God's compassionate presence" (p. 298, folio 277). Against this, "Ibn Abd al-Wahhab
  ignores this and reduces people's intentions to the crassest level: in his view, people seek
  protection or sustenance from God through another person only to gain material benefit or avoid
  harm" (p. 298, folio 277).
- **Sainthood as constitutive of Islam, not an accretion.** "Such examples of early companions being
  singled out as holy people reveal how Muslim saints are an integral part of the Islamic religion
  and perpetuate the `Prophets[text-layer?]` [Prophet's] charisma in his community, despite Ibn Abd
  al-Wahhab's accusation that this is shirk that invalidates one's Islam" (p. 299, folio 278).
  **This is the chunk's strongest general claim in the archive's favour.** Kugle also ties the two
  issues together: "the power of living saints to mediate the sacred realm and the power of the
  Prophet to intercede with God are two integrally related issues" (p. 299, folio 278), and calls the
  continuing authority of "living holy people, who mediate between the devoted public and God's
  presence", a topic "linked to the continuing spiritual authority of the Prophet Muhammad himself"
  (p. 299, folio 278).
- **"Exaggeration" defined — the book's theory of contact-blessing and relic power.** Glossing
  al-ghuluw fi al-salihin, Kugle writes: "By exaggeration, he means devotion in which the righteous
  holy people are believed to be so powerful that their presence exceeds the bounds of their bodies
  so that their blessing transmits to people close to them or objects they have touched, their
  personalities can be present in places far beyond their physical limitations in space, and their
  presence can persist long after the mortal duration of their bodies" (p. 300, folio 279). **Three
  distinct mechanisms — contact transmission, distant presence, posthumous persistence — named in one
  sentence; this is the formulation to quote in any relic or barakat discussion.** The Arabic is
  printed `(al-ghuluwfi al-salihinj[text-layer?]` with a lost space and a damaged closing paren
  (p. 300, folio 279).
- **The reply to Peskes, and Kugle's statement of his own method.** "Why the theory that inspired such
  destruction should be decoupled from the actual destruction is not clear; this study's focus on the
  human body should make it clear that these two dimensions of Wahhabi activity are fundamentally
  unified. One cannot separate an ideology's image of the body from its practices regulating the
  body, especially when the ideology advocates revolutionary change and violence against its
  opponents" (p. 301, folio 280). He adds that Peskes's "analytical operation" cannot exonerate the
  movement "when his movement, from its earliest inception in the oases in central Arabia, destroyed
  the tomb shrines that were the central meeting places of Sufis and the architectural focus of their
  spiritual geography" (p. 301, folio 280). **The phrase "the architectural focus of their spiritual
  geography" is the chunk's most compact statement of what a shrine is for.**
- **The thesis restated.** "Contrary to Peskes, this study maintains that the Wahhabi movement is
  anti-Sufi in its ideological marrow. Of course the movement has changed over time in reaction to
  historical contingencies, especially in morphing from a local rebellion into a durable militant
  minority into a ruling regime. However, the bare bones of its ideology have remained remarkably
  constant" (p. 302, folio 281).
- **The refutation from within the family, and what it proves.** Of Sulayman's text Kugle writes that
  it shows the Wahhabis' takfir was "actually the breaking of consensus (`kharq al-ijma[text-layer?]`)
  in order for Wahhabis to claim that their leaders have the right to rule authoritatively", and that
  "Such early insight into the movement by a family member... reveals the integral connection between
  body practices, opposition to Sufism, and the authority to rule in rebellion against established
  norms" (p. 301, folio 280).
- **The book's own theory of the substitute body.** Opening the sub-heading `Holy People and Sacred
  Power`: "We have seen above how those who believe that prophets and saints act as channels for
  sacred power to permeate the world perceive in them an image that exceeds the body. Upon their
  death, this devoted public creates a likeness of the body, or a substitute for it, in a material
  replica of the image it held dear. This, Ibn Abd al-Wahhab asserts, is the origin of idolatry"
  (p. 302, folio 281). **Kugle states the mechanism as his own description and attributes only the
  verdict to his opponent.**
- **Two readings of the same tradition, contrasted as a point of method.** "Although Ibn al-Kalbi
  relates this tradition as the origin of idolatry and calls such idolatrous worship of images
  'disbelief,' he does so in a very neutral way" (p. 303, folio 282); "Ibn al-Kalbi hints that
  degeneration into idolatry is inherent in human nature, as the natural consequence of our drive to
  memorialize the dead and represent in material form our hope for transcendent continuity beyond the
  mortal body" (p. 303, folio 282); "Clearly, Ibn al-Kalbi relates these traditions about idolatry
  with affection, as a part of the Arab and Semitic past that reveals deep human yearnings and
  frailties" (p. 304, folio 283). Against this: "Ibn Abd al-Wahhab cites the same tradition about the
  idols of Noah's people but with none of the warmth and affection of the folkloric philosopher Ibn
  al-Kalbi. Rather, Ibn Abd al-Wahhab's list of points is as stark as the conclusions he extracts
  from this story" (p. 304, folio 283).
- **Grammar as ideology.** "As Ibn Abd al-Wahhab extracts his list of points from the story, he
  dispenses even with verbs: the story is not 'analyzed' or 'interpreted' so that it 'reveals'
  information or 'conveys' knowledge. Verbs would imply human agency in the interpreter, so Ibn Abd
  al-Wahhab elides them, allowing his list of points to stand, in starkly self-revelatory reality, as
  a literal and incontrovertible reading of the text" (pp. 304–305, folios 283–284). **A method
  observation about reading a primary source, transferable to the archive's own sources.**
- **The eighteen-point list, quoted in full across pp. 304–305 (folios 283–284, notes 36 and 37 of the
  Conclusion).** The two points that bear on shrines are numbered `n.[text-layer?]` [11] — "the harm
  brought by gathering at a tomb (`al-cakuf cala al-qabr[text-layer?]`) even for a righteous action" —
  and `12.` — "the prohibition against idols and likenesses (tamathil) and the wisdom of eradicating
  them" (p. 304, folio 283). Point `2.` asserts "knowledge that the first instance of shirk in the
  world was in the form of righteous people" (p. 304, folio 283). Point `15.` and `16.` turn on
  intercession: "clarification that such people [who worshiped idols] intended intercession
  (shafa'a)" and "their opinion that the religious scholars who shaped the images (`suwar][text-layer?]`)
  had intended them for this purpose" (p. 305, folio 284).
- **Kugle's reading of the list's silence.** "Even though Sufis are not named explicitly, the points in
  the end of this list constitute a judgment that Sufis are idolators, are like Christians, are not
  Muslims, and are therefore apostates who can be legally killed" (p. 305, folio 284) — and on its
  juridical character, "Such a justification of violence against fellow Muslims constitutes the most
  radical type of fatwa imaginable, made even more radical by its eschewing any dependence on Islamic
  law or established norms of juridical reasoning" (p. 305, folio 284).
- **The elasticity of "idol."** On Abd al-Rahman's definition — "The word idol (wathn) includes
  comprehensively every object of worship (ma'bud) that is not God, whether that object of worship is
  a tomb (qabr) or memorial (mashhad) or image (sura) or anything else" (p. 306, folio 285, note 40 of
  the Conclusion) — Kugle comments: "the commentator reveals the elasticity of Wahhabi interpretations
  of idols. They are not just the statues of gods or deities. Any image can become an idol, especially
  those images that reflect the body, whether in a lifelike manner, a symbolically abstract manner, or
  in an architecturally monumental manner" (p. 306, folio 285). **The third of those three manners is
  the shrine building itself.**
- **Reduction of a region to a cult, read politically.** "In this commentary, Sufi devotion is
  explicitly reduced to mere saint worship, and rituals at a tomb become idolatry" (p. 306, folio 285);
  and on the choice of Egypt, Iraq and Syria: "The `commentators[text-layer?]` [commentator's] choice
  of regions to criticize is crucial.... These three regions were key areas where Arabs were loyal to
  Ottoman rule. This theological rant not only questions their political loyalty and resistance to the
  Wahhabi political revolt but also judges them as infidels who cannot be granted the status of real
  Muslims and whose blood and property are therefore forfeit" (p. 307, folio 286).
- **The concluding diagnosis: asceticism and dirt.** "In this impassioned prose, which builds an
  impressive edifice of vitriol upon the bare bones of Ibn `cAbd[text-layer?]` al-Wahhab's list of
  points, the commentator reveals an ascetic urge. It is the ascetic denial of spiritual value to the
  human body that informs the whole Wahhabi movement and explains its vehement opposition to Sufism,
  `Shfism[text-layer?]` [Shiʿism], and the folk practices of Islam" (p. 308, folio 287, note 46 of the
  Conclusion). Then: "Wahhabis' ascetic extremism comes down to an obsession with dirt.... They want
  to bury the body with no marker or memorial. Yet at the same time they are horrified by dirt in the
  form of filth and pollution. This reveals a fundamental discomfort with the body, which was created
  from dirt, continually defiles itself, and will inevitably revert to putrefaction" (p. 308,
  folio 287). The positive claim underneath it: the body is "the consummate creation of God and the
  dwelling place of God's 'breath of the merciful One' (`nafas a/-rafoman[text-layer?]`)", and
  "they cannot see the human body as an assembly of the signs of God" (p. 308, folio 287).
- **The comparison to Descartes, cut off at the chunk's end.** "In this sense, their approach to the
  body approximates that of Cartesian modernism"; "Descartes attempted to gain distance from the body
  in order to achieve intellectual certainty (and along the way to find clarity about religious
  quandaries like the nature of the soul); in contrast, the Wahhabis' quest is for religious cer-"
  (p. 308, folio 287) — **the sentence breaks at the chunk boundary and its completion is in
  chunk 019.**
- **Statements of the book's own limits: largely (not stated) here.** The chunk names its method twice
  — "this study's focus on the human body" (p. 301, folio 280) and "this study maintains" (p. 302,
  folio 281) — and cross-refers to `Chapter i[text-layer?]` [Chapter 1] (p. 308, folio 287) and to
  Chapter 3 ("the same story with which Chapter 3 began", p. 302, folio 281). **It does not state the
  book's geographical scope, does not enumerate the five case studies, does not name its source
  languages, and offers no statement of evidentiary limitation: all (not stated) in pp. 294–308.**
  A worker looking for the book's self-assessment must look outside this chunk.
