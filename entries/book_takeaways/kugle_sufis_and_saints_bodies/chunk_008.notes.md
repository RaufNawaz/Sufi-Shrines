## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Tomb-feeding at Manuba.** Sayyida A'isha al-Manubiyya's tomb "is converted into a dining room
  table loaded with food through which her blessing is transmitted to the many women (and few men)
  who still come to visit her" (p. 131, folio 110). A living practice, described in the present
  tense; the book gives no day, festival or ʿurs date for it: **(not stated)**.
- **Oral custodianship as a source.** The oral stories of Lalla Awish are "still told about her by
  the caretakers of her tomb" and are set against "the scant written material about her life"
  (pp. 130–131, folios 109–110). Custodians here function as the tradition's archive.
- **Burial of women inside a male saint's tomb shrine as the sign of propriety.** For the wives and
  daughters of male saints "there is little substance to their biographies or record of their
  miracles; it is enough to highlight their family relationship to a male saint and note their burial
  within his tomb shrine, for such a position is emblematic of their remaining in the enclosing walls
  of family propriety" (p. 136, folio 115).
- **Zawiya founding dates and buildings.** Al-Shadhili "founded his first zawiya, or devotional
  center, in Tunis in 1227"; "The cave to which he would retreat for devotions (maqam) still exists
  on a mountaintop outside the city" (p. 135, folio 114). On moving east "He took many close followers
  to Alexandria, in Egypt, where he built a new devotional center, while leaving other followers to
  perpetuate the community in Tunis" (p. 135, folio 114).
- **The Zawiya Fasiyya as institution.** Abd al-Rahman's "disciples built the Zawiya Fasiyya, the
  Sufi Brotherhood of Fes, which became one of the most prominent and productive Sufi organizations
  in early modern Fes" (p. 138, folio 117). "These Sufi scholars built a zawiya that was both an
  architectural site and a social institution. They recorded stories that would root the zawiya in
  the social and religious life of Fes" (p. 140, folio 119).
- **Lineage as the instrument of institutional exclusion.** "Amina was very important to him
  personally and spiritually, but she had no place in the patriarchal and authoritative construction
  of his lineage" (p. 138, folio 117); "Only when Amina died did Abd al-Rahman take on the full share
  of Ali al-Sanhaji's spiritual authority for himself" (p. 138, folio 117); "at the level of
  institutional records … Amina is mentioned only after Abd al-Rahman al-Majdhub and is presented as
  his 'servant'" (p. 140, folio 119).
- **Lineage creation out of unaffiliated saints.** "Before Abd al-Rahman, hagiographic sources had
  characterized these majdhub figures as saints with no explicit lineage or formal system of teaching
  and passing on mystical intuition. However, they characterized Abd al-Rahman as 'one of the axial
  saints' who began a new powerful lineage" (p. 138, folio 117); he "transformed a ragtag alliance of
  majdhub characters (with their antinomian and even anarchic practices) into an organized Sufi
  lineage" around which "he could build an organized institution (tariqa)" (p. 138, folio 117).
- **Scholarly labour of the zawiya.** Its members "became leading historians, genealogists,
  researchers, and copyists who contributed immensely to Fes's archives, and they included some later
  members of Sayyida Amina's own Ibn al-Qadi clan" (p. 140, folio 119). Manuscript recovery continues:
  al-Kattani's collection "remains the standard reference, even now that scholars in Moroccan
  universities are editing and publishing earlier hagiographic collections that had remained for
  centuries in manuscript form" (p. 141, folio 120).
- **Juristic prosecution of a woman saint through the ruler.** The jurists "To the sultan of Tunisia,
  in the Hafsid dynasty … brought charges 'against this girl who mixes freely with men, which is not
  allowed in the `sharia`'" (p. 134, folio 113, chapter 2 note 56). The outcome is not recorded:
  "While not telling us exactly how she avoided outright persecution, the hagiographic records give
  us hints" — chiefly that "Many of the jurists and scholars who initially attacked her became her
  devotees and followers" (p. 134, folio 113).
- **Dynastic politics of allegiance.** Saints of "the jihad along the northern coast could withdraw
  their allegiance from the Wattasid rulers of Fes. And that is increasingly what happened, with
  drastic results as the Sa'dian forces pushed north to conquer Fes" (p. 129, folio 108).
- **Manuscript archive location named.** The chapter's evidence on Sayyida A'isha comes from
  "hagiographic manuscripts archived in Tunis" (p. 131, folio 110).
- **No ʿurs, no mela, no langar, no offerings, no endowment, no land, no revenue, no visitor figure
  and no dispute over custodianship is reported anywhere in pp. 129–145** — the institutional material
  here is textual and lineage-based, not economic: all **(not stated)**.

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- Lalla Awish "affirms the intimate relation of women's saintliness with food, as one of her miracles
  involves passing on mystical knowledge to a man through investing a piece of bread with her saliva
  and leaving it for him to eat" (p. 131, folio 110) — attributed by Kugle to the stories told of her.
- Sayyida A'isha al-Manubiyya spoke from inside her mother's belly when her mother "confronted a
  lion": "Don't be frightened, Mother, for you are protected by the preserving power of God!"
  (p. 131, folio 110, chapter 2 note 51) — from the hagiography by Ibn Sabbagh.
- Khidr "appeared to her in the streets of Manuba when she was a girl twelve years old … in the form
  of a young boy", saying "Get your father to fix your engagement right away or I will take you
  myself … I've been waiting for you for three-thousand years!" (p. 132, folio 111, chapter 2
  note 52) — hagiographic.
- She told the cousin to whom her father hastily engaged her, "If you love to take me [in marriage],
  then you will die, as God wills"; "Everybody around her laughed. But in the middle of the night, he
  died" (p. 132, folio 111, chapter 2 note 52). Kugle calls this "her first evident miracle, a
  prognosticative warning" that "secured her freedom from marriage" (p. 132, folio 111).
- She claimed visions of the dead: "I have seen `Rabfa`[text-layer?] [Rabiʿa] al-Adawiyya [of Basra]
  and she said to me 'I serve you!'" (p. 132, folio 111, chapter 2 note 53); "She saw Abd al-Qadir
  al-Jilani and Junayd among many others" (p. 132, folio 111).
- Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani is reported telling her, "The saints are silver but you are gold. If a saint
  is silver, nothing can be more beautiful than it except gold!" (p. 134, folio 113).
- She "claimed to have seen and spoken with al-Shadhili, and from him she 'absorbed his virtues of
  knowledge and patience'" (p. 134, folio 113, chapter 2 note 57).
- Abd al-Rahman al-Majdhub's investiture as axial saint: Ali al-Sanhaji "grabbed him as he was
  sitting at one of the gates of the mosque the Qarawiyyin and jerked him around", after which "Abd
  al-Rahman saw Ali al-Sanhaji approach his belly, insert his finger in his navel, and lift him up as
  all the saints gathered around them. Ali al-Sanhaji said, 'Whoever loves me, accept this [man]!'"
  (p. 138, folio 117, chapter 2 note 63) — recounted, the book says, by Abd al-Rahman himself "in very
  dramatic terms".
- Ali al-Sanhaji is described as "giving 'spiritual birth' to his followers, like Abd al-Rahman
  al-Majdhub, twirling them upon his finger by their navels as if he could play out the function of
  the umbilical cord" (p. 142, folio 121).
- Of the Prophet Muhammad: he "is reported to have sensed the oncoming of revelation as an intense
  buzzing or a penetrating resonance like a bell's reverberation and comprehended the precise words as
  the speech of an angel, Gabriel" (pp. 144–145, folio not stated / folio 124); "Muhammad saw the
  angel before hearing the angel's message" (p. 145, folio 124), and vision "played the dominant role
  in the miraculous journey that confirmed Muhammad's commission as God's messenger: his ascension
  into the heavens" (p. 145, folio 124).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Three hagiographic strategies for a woman who is "manifestly not a 'good woman'".** The narrative
  "confronts a universal problem: to explain how a woman who is manifestly not a 'good woman'
  according to patriarchal social values is nonetheless a 'saintly woman'" (p. 129, folio 108). The
  three themes: relation "to a wider family structure in which a male member is considered a saint
  (either a father, husband, or son)"; a "'spiritual marriage' to a divinely blessed figure and the
  consequent rejection of a routine marriage"; and being "said to not really be women at all but
  rather to have become 'men' through their spiritual aspiration and fortitude" (pp. 129–130,
  folios 108–109, chapter 2 note 46).
- **Enclosure of the female body as the patriarchal mechanism.** "Patriarchal societies are
  threatened by the 'openness' of women's bodies and display a social obsession to enclose them in
  order to preserve male control … with family honor and genetic purity that, when enforced, becomes
  legal imperative, moral injunction, and violent indignation" (p. 129, folio 108).
- **Jadhb as an escape hatch from gender.** "Strangely, the majdhub is allowed to trespass the
  boundaries of patriarchal gender roles, as if he or she were a 'raw' human being who has not yet
  been 'cooked' into social gendered roles" (p. 130, folio 109); a female majdhub "is allowed to
  abandon household responsibilities, deferential modesty, and domestic confinement", so "it is not
  surprising to find strong women saints in a majdhub guise" (p. 130, folio 109).
- **A'isha al-Manubiyya as Amina's prototype.** "As an ecstatic woman saint who rebelled against the
  norms of feminine behavior, Sayyida A'isha is probably the strongest prototype for Sayyida Amina
  bint Ibn al-Qadi. This is true despite the difference noted above and even though the former is not
  mentioned by name in the latter's hagiography" (p. 136, folio 115) — an argument from structure, not
  from textual citation, and Kugle says so.
- **Amina inverts the family-saint pattern.** "Sayyida Amina does not at all conform to this pattern.
  In fact, she inverts it! The male members of her family were known as jurists, from whom she
  manifestly broke, while after her death some members of her family would become prominent Sufis,
  following upon her lead rather than leading her to follow" (p. 136, folio 115).
- **A'isha contested her ordinariness, not her gender.** "It was not her feminine gender that she
  protested but her ordinariness within that gender category" (p. 134, folio 113); she "coined new
  locutions to put her ambiguous gender status into language", claiming to be "the female master of
  pious men (`sayyidat al-rijal}`[text-layer?])" and "the beauty of the brothers
  (`zlnat al-ikhwan`[text-layer?] [zīnat al-ikhwān])", "subverting the inherent patriarchy that
  defines Sufi communities as a 'brotherhood'" (p. 134, folio 113).
- **Initiation direct from God, not from a silsila.** Kugle stresses that "Sayyida A'isha never
  admits to having learned Sufi mysticism from any man or woman, living or long passed away, not even
  from the mysterious Khidr" (p. 132, folio 111): "I have not taken this mystical Way as an
  inheritance from anyone. Rather, I have taken it as a gift directly from my Lord" (p. 133,
  folio 112, chapter 2 note 55). Al-Shadhili "is the only contemporaneous man to whom she makes
  deferential reference" (p. 134, folio 113).
- **Mary as the archetype.** "Mary is the closest that a woman comes to being a prophet in the
  Qur'an, so it is no surprise that Sayyida A'isha sees Mary has the archetype through which she is
  spiritually charged" (p. 133, folio 112); "Mary had to contend with a patriarchal society that
  accused her of being a whore after her miraculous pregnancy (Q 19:27-28)" (p. 133, folio 112).
- **The Tunisian branch preserved a more activist Shadhili style.** "al-Shadhili's original followers
  in Tunisia remained active and may have preserved a more charismatic and politically activist style
  of Sufi devotion close to the spirit of the movement's founder" — against the Egyptian branch's
  "subtly intellectual mysticism steeped in Islamic law" (p. 135, folio 114). Note the hedge "may
  have".
- **Separate treatment produced more memory, not less.** "Devotees of Sayyida A'isha place her among
  his 'forty friends.' However, the hagiographer Ibn Sabbagh does not include her … Ironically, by
  treating her separately rather than including her in this group of closest disciples, the
  hagiographer devotes more pages to her memory than to others" (p. 135, folio 114).
- **The popular/official dichotomy rejected.** Examining how Amina's story was recorded "throws into
  doubt common preconceptions about the dichotomy between 'popular Islam' and 'official' or
  'scholarly Islam'", "a persistent pattern among contemporary scholars in sociology and anthropology,
  from Alfred Bel through Ernest Gellner and Clifford Geertz, though it has lately come under
  critique" (p. 137, folio 116, chapter 2 note 60). Conclusion: "one cannot insist on a binary
  division between 'popular religion' and 'official religion' in pre-modern Fes … Any such binary
  distinction, if asserted in general, will inevitably be found to blur and blend in concrete
  historical cases" (p. 141, folio 120).
- **Interdependence is not equality.** "This complex interplay of forces reveals the interdependence
  of male and female religious leaders in Sufism in pre-modern Fes; however this interdependence
  should not be mistaken as equality or parity of social power" (p. 141, folio 120). "It is as if the
  revolutionary forces of her female holy madness were channeled and preserved by men, fueling the
  establishment of a thoroughly patriarchal institution" (p. 141, folio 120).
- **The chapter's methodological proposal on gender in sainthood, and its critique of Mernissi.**
  "Men become saints by tapping 'feminine' qualities that are normally hidden or repressed in men,
  while women become saints by tapping 'masculine' qualities within themselves that ordinary women do
  not actively manifest. Taking this as an approach to studying gender in sainthood would go beyond
  the simple search for women examples and role models, an approach of many feminist scholars such as
  Fatima Mernissi" (p. 142, folio 121, chapter 2 note 71). Forward reference: "we will examine this
  question through queer theory in Chapter 4" (p. 142, folio 121).
- **Poetry as evidence of ambivalence.** Abd al-Rahman's verse ("The market of womankind is only loss
  and chance"; "This world can be called a she-camel") is read as expressing "the ascetic logic that
  this world of material goods and desires is like a woman" and "Abd al-Rahman's deep ambivalence
  toward 'woman,' who, against his will and better judgment, plays such a prominent role in his
  spiritual inheritance and training" (p. 139, folio 118, chapter 2 note 65).
- **Chapter 3's opening thesis: ocularcentrism, shared.** "Western culture shares with Islamic culture
  an ocularcentrism" (p. 144, folio not stated), via Synnott's list of sight-words (chapter 3 note 1).
  The face as second body, via al-Zahi: "By 'beautifying the face' al-Zahi refers especially to
  darkening the rim of the eyes with kohl" (p. 145, folio 124, chapter 3 note 2).
- **Vision over hearing, carefully qualified.** "It must be admitted that the Qur'an is first and
  foremost an aural event, a revelation that was heard, then spoken, then recited", citing Sells's
  "Sound, Spirit and Gender in the Qur'an" (p. 145, folio 124, chapter 3 note 3); nonetheless
  "vision, when aided by views from a great height and unobstructed vantage point, has clarity and
  detail that outstrip even hearing in power" (p. 144, folio not stated), and "vision alone among the
  senses seems to grow more powerful from great heights" (p. 145, folio 124).
