## Other saints, sites and events (not in the archive)

- **Mawlay Idris al-Azhar** — the saint of chapter 1, "he and his father were the first kings of
  Morocco and were sharifian descendants of the Prophet" (p. 82, folio 61). **The chunk gives no
  death date for him**: **(not stated)**. What it gives instead is a dispute over where his body
  was. "Most historians of medieval Fes thought that Mawlay Idris al-Azhar's body was buried next to
  his father's in the small city of Walili (or Volubilis in Latin)" (p. 83, folio 62). "A medieval
  historian of Fes who was writing before 1341 claims that Mawlay Idris al-Azhar died and was buried
  in Fes, yet he also notes an older and divergent tradition (recorded by al-Burnusi) that the saint
  died in Walili and was buried there next to his father's tomb" (p. 83, folio 62, note 21) — **that
  historian is not named**: **(not stated)**. `Al-Jazna3![text-layer?]`, "a historian of the same age
  but writing slightly later, glosses over these conflicting traditions and does not document Mawlay
  Idris's place of burial" (pp. 83–84, folios 62–63, note 22).
- **Mawlay Idris I (the father)** — named only as the father and predecessor: he "had taken this city
  [Walili] as his administrative capital when he first arrived in Morocco, a city established by
  Roman, Byzantine, and Visigothic rulers" (p. 83, folio 62). "Although Mawlay Idris the son founded
  Fes as the new capital, the city's construction was hardly finished by the time of his death"

- **Abu Ghalib** — the chunk's second saint and the one it documents most fully. "the shrine of a
  saintly man, whose name was Abu Ghalib (died in `isgg[text-layer?]`)" (p. 90, folio 69, note 37).
  **The printed death year is destroyed in the text layer and no reading is offered here** — see
  Doubts; do not let a year enter any entry from this page. "Although Abu Ghalib is not a
  historically documented personage, his hagiographic story is crucial" (p. 90, folio 69). He was "a
  grandson of one of these returned shurafa'", "a doctor, a surgeon or bloodletter (hajjam, or in
  some accounts hakim)", who "developed a reputation (at least posthumously) for pious ecstasy and
  generosity to the Sufis, who gathered at his home", "converted his ancestral home into a Sufi
  gathering place `(zawiyaj[text-layer?]` [zawiya)], and when he died, he was buried on the spot"
  (p. 90, folio 69). He "descended from the Idrisi family of shurafa', who descended from Mawlay
  Idris" (p. 90, folio 69).
- **Fes** — the setting throughout; "an intellectual, religious, and commercial center" under the
  first two Berber dynasties (p. 82, folio 61), later "the imperial capital and central hub of

- **Masjid `al-Shurafa3[text-layer?]`** [al-Shurafaʾ] — the mosque in the oldest quarter of Fes in

## Practices, institutions, economy

- **The rediscovery, twice dated to the same year and cleanly printed both times.** "once the body of
  Idris was miraculously 'rediscovered' in 1438, in the waning years of the Marinid dynasty" (p. 82,
  folio 61); "In 1438, the lost tomb of their saintly ancestor, Mawlay Idris, was 'rediscovered' in
  the center of Fes, securing the institutional power of this branch of the al-Juti clan" (p. 85,
  folio 64). **`1438` is undamaged on both pages** — the one firm date in the chunk.
- **Building the shrine.** "The Marinid rulers were quickly enjoined to support the building of a
  proper shrine, mosque, and tomb for the site" (p. 85, folio 64). The authenticators "confirmed
  that a mortuary building (darih) should be erected to commemorate the burial site" (p. 86, folio
  65, note 30). No architect, no cost and no completion date: **(not stated)**.
- **Sanctuary over a marketplace, and the expulsion that came with it.** "In deference to its
  holiness, the entire surroundings of the tomb were designated as a 'sanctuary' in the care of the
  sharifian protectors. This area included the Qaysariyya, in which cloth, gold, spices, and other
  valuable commodities were traded." (p. 85, folio 64) "By a `trump[text-layer?]` of sacred
  geography, the market was cleared of Jewish merchants and landowners who had grown in power over
  several generations and was arrogated by the `al-lmrani[text-layer?]` family." (p. 85, folio 64)
  "By this move, the shurafa' claimed to embody the axis of Fes, controlling the overlapping arenas
  of sacred sites, urban space, and economic networks." (p. 85, folio 64)
- **Shoe removal as an ordered practice.** The chief minister and the Qarawiyyin prayer leader
  "ordered that shoes be taken off around the site so that no misstep might impinge upon his honor"
  (p. 86, folio 65, note 30).
- **Authentication procedure.** "The leader of the shurafa' immediately called upon the chief minister
  and the prayer leader of the Qarawiyyin mosque. They arrived at the site to authenticate the body."
  (p. 86, folio 65, note 29) The bones were identified by the custodian himself: `CAH
  al-lmrani[text-layer?]` [ʿAli al-Imrani] "identified the bones as those of his ancestor, Mawlay
  Idris" (p. 86, folio 65).
- **Epigraphy as title deed.** "Because there was no structure that could be identified as a previous
  tomb, he had a plaque installed, inscribed with the name of the interred saint. 'Words were carved
  into a stone on the wall, certifying that this was the actual tomb. Indeed, the tomb was there, and
  these words were inscribed so that one could bear eyewitness to the man never forgotten in the
  traditions told by trustworthy narrators.'" (p. 86, folio 65, note 28)
- **State money for the tomb, and the hailing of the minister.** "As he stood over the site of Mawlay
  Idris's body to grant money for the reconstruction of a tomb, the minister of state was hailed as
  'the preserver of love for the shurafa', ever ready to show forth their exalted status and
  establish their glory, always desiring to revive the example of their glorious ancestor [the
  Prophet Muhammad].'" (p. 89, folio 68, note 36)
- **A state office for the descendants of the Prophet.** "Marinid rulers recognized a single person as
  `natfb al-shurafa'[text-layer?]` [naqib al-shurafaʾ] / 'the representative of the descendants of
  the Prophet,' and gave him an official administrative role. In addition, the shurafa' took leading
  roles in many other official rituals and new religious celebrations patronized by the court."
  (p. 84, folio 63)
- **Court patronage of the Prophet's birthday.** The Marinids "patronized the celebration of the
  birthday of the Prophet Muhammad" (pp. 84–85, folios 63–64, note 23); "It was the occasion for
  grand ceremonial processions in which the shurafa' played a leading role, raising their social
  status considerably throughout the period of Marinid rule" (p. 85, folio 64, note 24).
- **Madrassa endowment as a legitimating strategy.** "the Marinid dynasty's policy of building Islamic
  colleges (madrassas) closely around it, colleges that brought the devoted, intelligent, and
  ambitious from all over northern Morocco to Fes to study Islamic theology and law in order to take
  positions in government-administered courts and schools" (p. 83, folio 62). The Marinids
  "introduced into Morocco the institution of the madrassa from Egypt and the eastern Islamic world"
  (p. 84, folio 63). The Qarawiyyin "served not only as the official site for the sultan's attendance
  at Friday communal prayers but also as the college's lecture hall" (p. 83, folio 62).
- **A woman's endowment founds the city's central mosque.** "built by the property endowment of a
  noble woman, Fatima al-Fihri" (p. 83, folio 62). **The chunk says nothing about women's access to
  either tomb**: **(not stated)** — worth flagging for the archive's women's-access question, since
  this chapter's only named woman is a founding endower, not a visitor.
- **A perpetual land grant funding a hospital, inside an urban inventory.** The Reservoir of Justice
  "counted more than one hundred thousand residents in the walled city, living in 89,036 private
  homes, with 240 places of convenience and purification (public restrooms), 80 public fountains fed
  from springs, 93 public baths, 136 public bread ovens, 437 hostels for travelers, visiting
  merchants, and the homeless, 400 government-run paper-making factories, and `i[text-layer?]` [1]
  hospital funded by land granted in perpetuity" (p. 83, folio 62). **The numerals 89,036 / 240 / 80
  / 93 / 136 / 437 / 400 are undamaged; only the hospital count is corrupt** — see Doubts.
- **Shrine revenue assigned by state decree to a custodian clan, as compensation for displacement.**
  "The Idrisi shurafa' who settled at al-Sirawiyyin were reinforced by another contingent of
  shurafa', from the al-Talibi clan (a branch of the powerful al-Juti `al-lmrani[text-layer?]`
  family). Marinid rulers had forcibly moved these `shuraja[text-layer?]` [shurafaʾ] from their
  ancestral stronghold in the center of Fes. In recompense, the Marinids decreed that the financial
  proceeds from Abu Ghalib's tomb and charitable contributions to its founder would go to the coffers
  of the al-Talibi shurafa'." (p. 91, folio 70) "Through this arrangement, a branch of the sharifian
  al-Juti clan became the promoters and caretakers of Abu Ghalib's tomb and reinforced the story of
  his original sharifian genealogy." (p. 91, folio 70)
- **Offerings following pilgrims, and who banked them.** "As devotees flooded in, so did pious
  contributions, and the money went to enrich the al-Talibi clan of Imrani shurafa', who may have
  acted as sponsors for the newly arrived Qadiris." (p. 93, folio 72)
- **The tomb as healing station.** "his tomb gained wide renown as a center of healing, especially of
  open sores or wounds. People sought the dirt of Abu Ghalib's grave or the water from the fountain
  at the tomb for blessing and cure" (p. 91, folio 70). Both a **dust** and a **water** medium, and a
  **fountain at the tomb** as built infrastructure.
- **The death anniversary used as a free-clinic day.** "The mawsim of Abu Ghalib, the celebration of
  the anniversary of his death, was an occasion for free circumcision of children whose families were
  too poor to provide the ceremony. The importance of this tomb, however, went beyond free health
  care." (p. 91, folio 70) The word the book uses for the anniversary festival is **mawsim**, not
  ʿurs.
- **A juristic dispute over grave dust.** "So many people were collecting earth from Abu Ghalib's
  graveside to use as medicine that the practice sparked a juristic debate on whether it was legal in
  religious law. The Qadiri clans claimed that the practice was legal, based on traditional reports
  that early Muslims used earth from the tomb of the Prophet's uncle, Hamza, for healing." (pp. 92–93,
  folios 71–72, note 42) **The chunk does not say who argued the other side, or how the debate
  ended**: **(not stated)**.
- **The zawiya: founder, buildings, and treasury money.** "These Sufis built a zawiya at the site of
  Abu Ghalib's tomb. The zawiya consisted of a set of buildings, near the tomb, that would house
  devotees. A Qadiri Sufi master, Muhammad al-Zaytuni, was likely the zawiya's founder, and he
  certainly enlarged the compound with money extracted from the Marinid rulers." (p. 93, folio 72)
- **Charity to animals as a funded practice, and the zawiya's popular name.** "The zawiya became known
  as 'Lover of Cats' `(Bu'1-Qutut[text-layer?]` [Buʾl-Qutut]) because of the many cats that lived on
  the premises, which charitable donations may have been used to feed." (p. 93, folio 72)
- **Weekly samaʿ on Thursday night into Friday.** Abu Ghalib "would… attend with them sessions of
  devotional music and poetry `(sama][text-layer?]` [samaʿ)] whenever he had time. Listening, he
  would sway in a trance and dance in ecstasy. Then he began to practice such sessions at his home,
  with whoever would follow him there, **every Thursday night and into the day on Friday**. Those who
  were wont to listen to such music would gather at his house and keep him company, and he grew
  famous in Fes for these devotions." (pp. 94–95, folios 73–74, note 45) "Slowly, his home grew into
  a zawiya where they would live together." (p. 95, folio 74)
- **Devotional method at the zawiya.** "In ritual devotion, meditative discipline, and listening to
  love poetry sung to rapturous music, the Qadiri Sufis hoped to set aside their minds and open their
  hearts to the presence of 'the other,' whether people, cats, or God", striving "to attain ecstatic
  insight into the unity of being `(wahdat al-wujud}[text-layer?]` [wahdat al-wujud)])" (p. 94,
  folio 73).
- **Affiliation by blood versus affiliation by initiation, as a live institutional distinction.** "In
  Fes, Qadiri could refer to those with direct family ties to Abd al-Qadir, which makes them
  sharifian notables, or it could refer to those with spiritual ties through Sufi initiation leading
  back to Abd al-Qadir." (p. 90, folio 69) "In Fes, there were Sufi Qadiris in addition to the
  biological Qadiris. The Sufis who claimed Abd al-Qadir as their patron entered into a cooperative
  arrangement with the shurafa' who claimed him as their genealogical ancestor. Although the Sufi
  Qadiris did not boast a blood relationship with the dead saint, many of them cultivated intimate
  spiritual ties with him: they saw him in visions or took initiation direction from his hand."
  (p. 93, folio 72)
- **No cult before a located body.** "those who insisted that Mawlay Idris was present in Fes could
  not specify the exact place of burial, and therefore there was no cult of pilgrimage and visitation
  to the site" (p. 85, folio 64). And, for the long Marinid centuries, "nobody missed the fact that
  there was no shrine there and no tomb of Mawlay Idris" (p. 83, folio 62).
- **A shrine's present-day decline, stated flatly.** "Today, the tomb of Abu Ghalib still exists, but
  it is rarely visited. It no longer functions as the location of a zawiya." (p. 95, folio 74)
- **Sufis and shurafa' as military actors.** With the sultan failing at Tangier, "it was left to
  various Sufi groups and shurafa' to lead troops in the city's defense" (p. 86, folio 65).
- **Shrine prestige converted into rebellion within a generation.** "The same `shuraja[text-layer?]`
  [shurafaʾ] whom the Marinid officials lauded and graced with a grand tomb shrine would rebel
  against their rule only twenty-five years later, bringing the Marinid dynasty to an ignoble end and
  heralding the rise of a new dynastic ideology, the legitimacy of sharifian rulers, which would
  dominate Moroccan politics until the present." (p. 89, folio 68) **The book gives the interval, not
  the year**: the year of the revolt is **(not stated)** and is not derived here.

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The rediscovery framed as divine disclosure**, in the words of the anonymous history of the
  Maghrib: "Nobody had been able to pinpoint the exact site of the burial, until that moment when
  Allah willed its appearance, as a sign of compassion and grace to this divinely appointed community
  of faith. So it was agreed to test the foundation of the `qiUa[text-layer?]` [qibla] wall [in the
  Masjid `al-Shurafa3[text-layer?]`] to the right side [of the mihrab]. Within the foundation of the
  wall they found the tomb [sic]. But it was impossible to repair or restore the building of the
  tomb, for almost nothing remained. However, the bones of the deceased [Mawlay Idris] remained there
  as they were, untouched by earthly decay." (pp. 85–86, folios 64–65, note 26) The `[sic]` is the
  book's own.
- **Incorruption of the body**, attributed by Kugle to unnamed sources: "some sources recall that the
  body of Mawlay Idris was uncovered intact and unchanged since the day of its burial, rather than
  just suggested by the presence of bones" (p. 87, folio 66, note 32) — and Ahmad al-Fasi "defended
  the rumor that Mawlay Idris's body was found uncorrupted by decay, even after 'six-hundred and
  thirty-two years, six months and twenty-one days in the ground'" (p. 87, folio 66). **The duration
  is printed in words, not numerals, and is undamaged.**
- **A proverbial guarantee of incorruption for prophets, scholars and Sufis**, in al-Fasi's words:
  "This is a proverbial expression, just as we say, 'The earth does not encroach upon the flesh of
  the prophets `(al-ard la yadu cala luhum al-anbiya')','[text-layer?]` upon them be peace. The same
  is said of religious scholars who practiced righteousness and Sufi sages." (p. 87, folio 66,
  note 33)
- **A contemporary Egyptian parallel, reported by al-Fasi as news**: "Just this year, a jurist in
  Egypt published a book in which he reported finding the body of saint, when one wall of tomb
  collapsed, in a pure condition like the day he was buried. If this is true of a common saint, just
  imagine the state of incorruptibility of one who joins together virtues too numerous to count, like
  Imam Idris and his father!" (p. 87, folio 66, note 33) **The jurist, the saint and the year are all
  (not stated).**
- **Abu Ghalib's posthumous practice exceeding his living one**: "his miracles as a dead man are more
  than his miracles while living" (p. 91, folio 70, note 38) — "Abu Ghalib seems to have pursued his
  profession even after death" (p. 90, folio 69).
- **Abd al-Qadir as a raiser of the dead, in a Qadiri family historian's words**: "Jesus was known for
  reviving the dead and giving them new life. Likewise, Abd al-Qadir was known for having miracles
  flow through his hands that could revive the dead. Nobody else could compare with them in healing
  the sick, except for Abu Ghalib, the saintly descendant of Mawlay Idris of Fes. He would heal people
  with his blessings and cures during his life, and also after his death as people visited him and
  prayed over his tomb." (p. 92, folio 71, note 40)
- **The petition-poem to Abu Ghalib**, composed by al-Juti al-Imrani clansmen: "Abu Ghalib, I call you
  seeking healing and ease / for you are a doctor for every disease. / You answer the one who prays
  for success / So sustain me from your loftiness with a fragrant breeze / Do not seal me out, by
  your beneficent might / for I am weak, while your saintly power will amaze." (p. 91, folio 70,
  note 39)
- **The Marinid sultan's failure read against a miracle**: "It was certainly a time when a miracle was
  needed… The uncovering of the body of their saintly ancestor in the center of Fes seemed to confirm
  the victory of the defensive jihad against the Iberians led by the shurafa'." (p. 86, folio 65)
- **Al-Shibli and the cat**, "a popular hagiographic story" told as a post-mortem dream: God forgave
  him not for "the good works he had done, the prayers he had performed, his fasting and pilgrimages"
  but because "you found a small cat made weak by the cold creeping from wall to wall because of the
  great cold and ice, and out of pity you took it and put it inside a fur you were wearing so as to
  protect it from the pains of the cold? Because of the mercy you showed that cat I have had mercy on
  you." (pp. 93–94, folios 72–73, note 43)
- **The surviving proverb**: "Whoever loves the master Abu Ghalib loves him by loving his cats"
  (p. 95, folio 74, note 46) — now the saint's chief remaining trace, "not for his tomb, its healing
  dust, or the raptures that used to happen there" (p. 95, folio 74).

## Arguments and interpretations

- **The chapter's governing thesis in this chunk**: the tomb is to be read "as a work in progress
  rather than a fait accompli", which "will affirm the critical connection between bones, blood, and
  power in the formation of the body politic" (p. 81, folio 60).
- **Sharifian legitimism is dated, not perennial.** "What these rulers and the hagiographer who
  defended their right to rule conveniently ignore, however, is that this ideology of sharifian right
  to rule was a rather new invention in late medieval Morocco. If Mawlay Idris al-Azhar had asserted
  something similar, his ideas died with him." (p. 82, folio 61) Before it, "Morocco was ruled by
  sultans, not by a commander of the faithful. The sultans were Berber chieftains who engineered
  various ideologies of rule as upholders of Islamic law and theology but never claimed right to rule
  as Arab descendants of the Prophet." (p. 82, folio 61)
- **Titles as genealogical claims.** The Alawis "claimed not just the practical title 'powerful
  leader' (sultan) like other Islamic rulers of their day but also 'commander of the faithful'
  `(amir al-muminln)[text-layer?]` [amir al-muʾminin], which had been granted especially to Ali when
  he was the ruler of the early Islamic community" (p. 82, folio 61). Their "right to rule, we might
  say ran in the blood that flowed from the marrow of their bones" (p. 82, folio 61).
- **Political weakness, not piety, produced the claim to the body.** "The Marinid rulers, Islamic
  scholars, and medieval historians of Fes did not need his royal and saintly body in Fes. It was
  only as their rule grew weak and internal dissent gave rise to competitors who challenged their
  authority that the claim arose that his body was located in Fes." (p. 84, folio 63)
- **A contested urban centre.** "despite the saintly energy of Mawlay Idris's body and the popular
  devotion to this tomb, his building is not quite the center of Fes. Or rather, we can say that it is
  a contested question about where the center exactly is and what edifice occupies that priority
  position. This is because Mawlay Idris's tomb was not always there." (p. 82, folio 61) Its "relative
  newness explains why it stands in strange competition for a central position against the
  Qarawiyyin" (p. 82, folio 61).
- **Legitimacy by proxy.** The Marinids, "as Berber strongmen originally from Algeria, they had no
  claim of legitimacy that inhered in their bloodline or piety (the 'body' and 'knowledge' alluded to
  above in the Qur'anic narrative about King Saul). Rather, they claimed the right to rule by
  upholding Islamic law, sponsoring Islamic scholarship in madrassa institutions, and publicly
  honoring the Prophet Muhammad." (p. 84, folio 63) "The Marinid rulers rationalized the status of
  these clans and engineered an official position for them as a pillar of the state." (p. 84, folio 63)
- **The rediscovery interrogated as an event.** "This account leaves tantalizing obscurities as to the
  agency and intent behind this incident. Who was in charge of the 'rediscovery' and why were they
  digging up the foundation of a wall of the `mosque:1[text-layer?]` [mosque?] How is it that a tomb
  was uncovered and needed to be restored when there admittedly was no tomb there in the first place?
  Answers to these questions point to the critical political importance of this 'rediscovery' of a
  tomb that had never existed before." (p. 86, folio 65)
- **Kugle's deliberate refusal to adjudicate the miracle.** "Whether Mawlay Idris al-Azhar s tomb was
  'discovered' by serendipitous interaction, supernatural intervention, or scheming invention, its new
  marking as a sacred space proved overwhelmingly popular." (p. 87, folio 66) And again: "Whether his
  body was discovered by chance or revealed by stealth, whether what remained were bones or the intact
  flesh, once Mawlay Idris's burial place was identified and his tomb built, the Marinid rulers and
  Sufis loyal to their regime were forced to acknowledge sharifian political might." (p. 89, folio 68)
  **The forgery allegation is reported, not endorsed**: "Some Western scholars have found these
  circumstances to be so serendipitous as to be unbelievable and have alleged that the sharifian
  leader, Ali `al-Tmrani[text-layer?]` [al-Imrani], staged the miracle by having his ancestors body
  exhumed from his fathers tomb near Walili and reburied in the center of Fes." (pp. 86–87, folios
  65–66, note 31) **Those scholars are not named in the body text**: **(not stated)**.
- **Incorrupt body as political prophecy.** The intact body "suggested to the devoted public not just
  that the Moroccan polity would persist against the Iberians, like bones persist against the steady
  moist pressure of decay, but that it would actually overcome them, for the body of its founder and
  patron never decayed" (p. 87, folio 66).
- **Al-Fasi's philological argument, against his own shrine's epigraphy.** He read the plaque's
  "bones" as synecdoche: "it does not mean the bone `(cazm)[text-layer?]` [ʿazm] in distinction from
  the flesh `(labm)[text-layer?]` [lahm] of the body, as one who is ignorant of Arabic linguistics
  and grammatical use of words might erroneously imagine" (p. 87, folio 66, note 33); "the poet spoke
  the name of a body part to signify the whole of the body… for bones are the foundation that support
  the body `(diamat al-jism)[text-layer?]` [diʿamat al-jism]. If one says the bones are together…
  then one means that the body remains intact, for its foundation `(ad)[text-layer?]` remains intact"
  (p. 88, folio 67, note 34). Kugle's summary of the manoeuvre: "the author wrote bones but meant the
  flesh and the whole body" (p. 88, folio 67), reached "even if he had to contradict the explicit
  words of the plaque that marks his tomb, a plaque that mentions that the saints 'bones' were found"
  (p. 87, folio 66).
- **Bodily wholeness as political wholeness.** "Just as the shurafa' were leading a war in the north
  of Morocco to keep the political body from being dismembered or disintegrating, our hagiographer
  pictures the saints body as whole, untouched by decomposition and unsullied by the soil that veils
  it from view." (p. 88, folio 67) "This cultural obsession with the wholeness of the body as the
  foundation for moral righteousness and political cohesiveness is common to other cultures." (p. 88,
  folio 67) Comparative support from Berquist, whose Israelite body "mediated a persons involvement
  with the rest of society and it symbolically represented social cohesion" (p. 88, folio 67, note 35);
  "These dimensions of the whole male body applied with equal vividness to Islamic societies, and the
  hagiographer Ahmad al-Fasi extended their importance into the realm of the dead with Mawlay Idris's
  body, for as a saint he is not really to be considered gone. Rather, his body remains a marker of
  the potent boundary between this world and the next world, ensuring that the society that centers
  itself upon him will cohere, remain politically viable, fertile, and strong." (pp. 88–89, folios
  67–68)
- **The method statement for the chunk's second half.** "The body of Idris was discovered at a key
  moment, and his tomb was built suddenly. Its establishment was spectacularly successful, inspired a
  revolution, and changed the sacred geography of the city entirely. Though its success was without
  precedent, its design as a tomb shrine did have precedent. Searching Fes for other tombs that
  evolved more slowly, I found the tomb of another sharifian saint from the Idrisi family, which had
  evolved slowly into a political pivot point for its custodians and the surrounding community. It
  gained full prominence in the generation before the discovery of Mawlay Idris al-Azhar." (p. 89,
  folio 68)
- **The causal hypothesis, stated twice in near-identical words.** "It may have been the success of
  these shurafa' in promoting a tomb as a source of financial clout, religious reputation, and social
  status that inspired the sharifian leader Ali `al-lmrani[text-layer?]` to 'rediscover' the body of
  Mawlay Idris al-Azhar in his own neighborhood and construct a tomb shrine around it" (p. 89, folio
  68); "It may have been the success of these shurafa' in promoting a tomb as a source of clout that
  inspired the sharifian leader Ali al-lmrani to find the body of Mawlay Idris" (p. 91, folio 70).
  **Kugle marks it as conjecture both times ("It may have been").**
- **A tomb as a genealogical bridge for immigrants.** "The Qadiris cleverly used the tomb of Abu
  Ghalib to bridge the apparent gulf in genealogy and status" (p. 90, folio 69); "Although Abu Ghalib
  is not a historically documented personage, his hagiographic story is crucial, for through it the
  Qadiri `shuraja[text-layer?]` [shurafaʾ] allied with Idrisi shurafa' of Fes and integrated
  themselves into the city's power structure" (p. 90, folio 69). "The arrival of the Qadiris and
  their alliance with the Imrani shurafa' was crucial to the success of Abu Ghalib's tomb. They
  crafted out of the sketchy biography of Abu Ghalib a mythic story in which the saint Abu Ghalib
  mirrored the personality of their own pious ancestor, Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani." (pp. 91–92, folios
  70–71) Kugle judges the borrowed prestige fair: "This is not an unjustified boast from an al-Qadiri
  descendant, for in the ecstatic poetry ascribed to him, Abd al-Qadir himself boasts of his ability
  to heal like Jesus." (p. 92, folio 71)
- **How to handle an origin story the author thinks is false.** "The later story of Abu Ghalib having
  instituted a zawiya at his home is most likely a retroactive explanation of the institution's
  origins; the story should not be discarded as apocryphal, however, for it paints a lively picture of
  such devotional practice there." (p. 94, folio 73) A reading of the hagiography as a self-portrait
  of its tellers follows: "In this story, we can discern traces of the Qadiri shurafa' themselves.
  They came to Fes uninvited as refugees, but by building alliances with more established Idrisi
  shurafa' they were able to found devotional centers across the city." (p. 95, folio 74)
- **Contact relics, theorised.** "the proverb hints at the contagiousness of sacredness and the love
  it inspires. Objects close to a person are infused with that persons aura, so a holy persons
  clothes, artifacts, tomb, and everything found near him or her radiates the holiness of that person,
  even long after her or his death." (p. 95, folio 74)
- **The unattributed epigraph-quotation the chunk opens inside**, on shrine power in space and time:
  "Theoretically, the spiritual power which the [saint] possesses is unlimited, in the sense that
  disciples should not have the impertinence to imagine its limitations. Devotional activity centers
  power in the [shrine] (space-wise) and reinvigorates it at the annual [commemorative celebration of
  the death anniversary] (time-wise). The ritual structures which focus the power in exemplary spaces
  and moments also survive the mortal demise of the [saint] and celebrate his incorporation into the
  chain of divinely illuminated souls, united with God, through which direct experience of divine
  blessing can be obtained." (p. 81, folio 60, note 18) **The bracketed substitutions are the book's
  own, and the source is named before the chunk begins, so it is (not stated) here** — this is the
  chunk's most directly transferable statement about shrines generally and the consolidator should
  recover its attribution from chunk 004.

## Doubts

- **The chunk maps zero archive rows. This is a deliberate negative, not an omission.** Chapter 1 is
  set in Fes; no shrine, saint, tomb, festival, custodian or toponym in pp. 81–95 is in Pakistan. The
  consolidator should not expect an archive-id subsection from this chunk, and should not treat the
  absence as a failed pass.
- **Folio rule versus the briefing, and the page wins.** The run brief states that pp. 81, 82 and 91
  print no running header. **All three print one.** They print `6O` (p. 81), `6l` (p. 82) and `7O`
  (p. 91) — that is, 60, 61 and 70 with letter O for zero and lowercase l for 1. Every one is exactly
  PDF − 21, so the offset is confirmed at 15 of 15 pages with zero exceptions and **no contradiction
  of the offset itself anywhere in this chunk**. **The discrepancy is in the headerless-page census,
  not in the offset.** A human should re-run that census tolerant of `O`/`0`, `l`/`1`, `i`/`1` and
  `$`/`5` substitutions in the header digits: if the same pattern holds across the book, the figure
  of "135 pages print no running header" is inflated, and pages currently cited as "folio not stated"
  may in fact print a recoverable folio. **Nothing in this chunk is cited as "folio not stated".**
- **Abu Ghalib's death year is destroyed and must not be filled.** p. 90, folio 69 prints "(died in
  `isgg`)". The four characters are a digit run rendered as letters and **no reading is offered here**
  — `i` is a known substitute for `1` in this text layer (compare "`i` hospital" on p. 83), and `s`
  and `g` plausibly stand for other digits, but any reconstruction would be invention. A human must
  check the printed page or the PDF image before any year for Abu Ghalib enters an entry. Note that
  the book itself says he "is not a historically documented personage" (p. 90, folio 69), so the
  hagiographic date may be worth little even once read.
- **`i` for `1` in the Reservoir of Justice inventory.** "and `i` hospital funded by land granted in
  perpetuity" (p. 83, folio 62). The reading `1` is near-certain from the singular noun "hospital",
  but it is marked rather than corrected. The other numerals in the same sentence — 89,036; 240; 80;
  93; 136; 437; 400 — and the 9,082 stores on p. 84, folio 63, are undamaged.
- **`trump` on p. 85, folio 64** — "By a `trump` of sacred geography". Quoted as printed. It may be
  the printed word, or a text-layer corruption of something like "triumph" or "trumping". A human
  should check the page before the phrase is quoted in an entry.
- **The al-Tirmidhi / Tirmizi name collision, considered and refused.** The chunk quotes "a Sufi named
  al-Hakim al-Tirmidhi", described as "one of al-Shibli s contemporaries" and therefore placed in the
  Baghdad milieu of al-Hallaj (pp. 93–94, folios 72–73). The archive row
  `shrine-of-pir-baba-syed-ali-tirmizi` has as its principal figure Syed Ali Tirmizi (Pir Baba), died
  1583, Buner District, KPK. **These are plainly different people** — a different given name, a
  different century as the book positions him, and no shrine, tomb or location given for al-Hakim
  al-Tirmidhi at all. **No id assigned.** Recorded here only so the shared nisba is not later
  mistaken for a match.
- **Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani is named repeatedly but is not an archive principal figure**, so the
  author-only ruling of 18 Sep 2026 does not fire. Several archive rows carry a Qadiri or
