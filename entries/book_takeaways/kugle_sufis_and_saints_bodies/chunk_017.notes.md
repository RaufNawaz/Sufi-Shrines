## Practices, institutions, economy

- **Public shrine-building as a function of the saint's standing in society** — "The idea that saints
  act as the pillars of society allowed for massive public building of tomb shrines and encouraged
  the inclusive cultic activity that maintained their sacredness" (p. 288, folio 267). This is the
  chunk's most directly usable statement for any archive entry on shrine patronage, though it names
  no shrine.
- **The shrine as shelter for marginalized people** — "It also served as an umbrella for the
  activities of otherwise marginalized people, such as women, ethnic minorities, the rare individuals
  whose expression of sexuality differed from the norm, or the fringe individuals who altered gender
  roles. Their difference from the numerical majority of Muslims could be transformed from a
  threatening difference into a difference that marked the presence of the sacred" (p. 288, folio
  267).
- **Ritual technique: audible then silent dhikr, with breath retention** — "The practitioner should
  begin this ritual by reciting with the tongue, aloud in the presence of the `egoself`. Only when
  practitioners habituate to audible dhikr should they move on to the more potent form of silent dhikr
  and breath retentions, for bodily habits form the basic foundation for achieving union with God"
  (p. 283, folio 262).
- **Breath retention reframed as `batim mfraj[text-layer?]` [batin miʿraj], "internal ascension"** —
  "Imdadullah frames the retention of breath as fueling the devotees `batim mfraj[text-layer?]`,
  'internal ascension' from the world toward God" (p. 278, folio 257; chapter 5's note 61).
- **`salat-i ma'qusi`, inverted prayer, suppressed as a physical technique** — "Imdadullah does not
  present the inverted prayer as a technique in itself. Rather, he mentions it as an allegory for the
  beneficial moral results of less extreme forms of physical exercise through recitation" (p. 278,
  folio 257). Recast as "any skillful means by which one imagines oneself constantly at the moment of
  death" (p. 279, folio 258).
- **The saint's prescribed civic functions** — "building institutions for education or charity, being
  a mediator in social crises, and upholding fairness in market practices and equality in wealth"
  (pp. 279–280, folios 258–259). A direct statement of what Kugle takes the activist saint's economic
  role to be.
- **Lineage-boasting discouraged** — "Imdadullah urged that Muslim saints desist from boasting about
  their particular lineage of masters, for these were forms that divided them. Through his devotional
  ritual, Imdadullah aimed to unite the various Sufi lineages and their distinct techniques"
  (p. 280, folio 259). He "regretted 'the fact that the various modes and behaviors of the Path have
  become innumerable' by his own generation" (p. 280, folio 259).
- **Hereditary succession ranked lowest** — "he ranked those Sufis who upheld the sharia, the
  criterion for self-mastery, above those who were absorbed in ecstatic music or poetry (and certainly
  above those who had simply inherited Sufi positions from their forefathers)" (p. 279, folio 258).
  Directly relevant to any archive row with a hereditary sajjada nashin, though no shrine is named.
- **Deoband's institutional shift away from Sufi ritual** — "These institutions, preeminently the
  Deoband Academy and its branch academies, stressed the importance of studying hadith and issuing
  fatwas in shaping the devotional life of their adherents. Although many of the early Deobandis
  displayed the self-surrender and fellow service advocated by Imdadullah as the highest level of
  sanctity, few of them publicly referred to Imdadullah's ritual manual as the foundation of such
  virtuous behavior" (p. 284, folio 263). The consequence: "marginalizing Sufi devotional rituals to
  the realm of private belief" (p. 284, folio 263).
- **Wahhabi destruction of shrines as political method** — "As a theological means to wage their
  political rebellion, Wahhabis attacked Sufism and saints' tombs" (p. 292, folio 271). "Wahhabis
  decried devotional ritual at tombs of saints and prophets as idolatry, rather than patronizing them
  as communication with spiritual powers and ultimately communion with God" (p. 292, folio 271).
- **Ibn Idris's community prayer and litanies** — "The most characteristic prayer of their community
  was: There is no God but God and Muhammad is the Prophet of God in every glance and breath, to the
  number only comprehended by God" (p. 290, folio 269; conclusion note 5). In a letter he "counseled
  another major disciple to recite each day special litanies that he provided 'to purify and
  illuminate the heart and to attract the divine influences'" (p. 291, folio 270; conclusion note 7).
- **British administration of religious law** — "the British colonial administration claimed
  legitimacy by justly administering Islamic law to Muslims and Hindu law to Hindus", which "many
  Muslims were not convinced" could preserve the sharia "under the suzerainty of the British"
  (pp. 280–281, folios 259–260; chapter 5's note 63).

## Legends and miracles (karamat)

- **The Prophet's night journey, invoked as the model for the ritual ascent** — the devotee "ascends
  through the layers of the self that are analogous to the layers of the `hearts` organ, just as the
  Prophet Muhammad had ascended physically through the layers of heaven on his night journey to the
  divine throne" (p. 278, folio 257). Kugle's framing: Imdadullah "opted for a metaphor focused on
  the person of the Prophet and supported by hadith traditions" (p. 278, folio 257).
- **Muhammad Ghawth's claim of bodily ascension, reported as a claim** — "as Muhammad Ghawth had
  claimed to do" (p. 278, folio 257), and explicitly denied by Imdadullah (p. 278, folio 257).
- **ʿAʾisha on the chapter "He Frowned"** — "The Prophet Muhammad's wife, A'isha, said that if any
  chapter of the Qur'an could be wiped out, Muhammad had wished that it would be this short chapter"
  (p. 286, folio not stated). Attributed by Kugle to A'isha, not asserted.
- **The "great universal revelation" as a bodily event** — Ibn Idris on "the great universal
  revelation `(fatty[text-layer?]` which is the self-displaying of the most sublime power and the
  overflowing of the manifestation of the greatest glory on all parts of the body, including the hair,
  the skin, the bones and the flesh" (p. 291, folio 270; conclusion note 7). The bracketed Arabic term
  is destroyed; **no reading is offered**.
- **The medieval reputation of meditative discipline** — "Sufis in South Asia extolled the mystical,
  anatomical, and almost magical effects of meditative discipline. Their surrounding society accorded
  great social authority and political power to adepts who could master such effects" (p. 281, folio
  260). Reported by Kugle as the historical reputation, not endorsed.

## Arguments and interpretations

- **Ritual is not a symbol to be read but a discipline that remakes the body.** Kugle enlists Asad:
  "Symbols call for interpretation, and even as interpretative criteria are extended, so
  interpretations can be multiplied. Disciplinary practices, on the other hand, cannot be varied so
  easily, because learning to develop moral capabilities is not the same thing as learning to invent
  representations" (p. 277, folio 256; chapter 5's note 60). Kugle's own gloss: the goal "is rather to
  tear down one's bodily habitus that had been silently learned since childhood and reconstruct in its
  place a new moral body with heightened symbolic significance" (p. 277, folio 256).
- **Imdadullah concealed his own innovation as a bid for authority.** "Imdadullah carefully concealed
  the subtle reworking of tradition that is evident in a close intertextual analysis of his ritual
  manual. He did this to heighten his own claim to authority, not as an innovative author but as a
  doctor prescribing time-honored remedies for spiritual disease and social decay" (p. 277, folio
  256).
- **The saint as physician of a collapsing polity.** "Imdadullah was undoubtedly a spiritual surgeon
  in a body politic that was on the verge of collapse under the forces of European colonization"
  (p. 277, folio 256); his concern "forced him to put himself forward as a spiritual guide with
  ritual tools that could reshape society and shore up political collapse" (p. 277, folio 256).
- **Moderation and suppression as the two mechanisms of Imdadullah's revision.** Ascension is the
  example of moderation, inverted prayer the example of suppression (p. 278, folio 257). "Commensurate
  with this new emphasis on the sharia, Imdadullah moderates or suppresses the more extreme
  athletic-ascetic qualities of these techniques, although he carefully preserves the bodily rigor
  associated with them" (p. 278, folio 257).
- **Old content, new rhetorical frame.** "Earlier Sufis had advocated these techniques as means to
  achieve purity through bodily rigor and spiritual ecstasy, while Imdadullah advocates them as means
  to achieve to purity through anatomical knowledge and spiritual insight" (p. 279, folio 258).
  Medieval Sufis "saw no reason to justify or explain them at all" (p. 279, folio 258), whereas
  Imdadullah argues they lead to "'death before death'" and allow "the apt performance of
  `surma[text-layer?]` [sunna]" (p. 279, folio 258).
- **Self-mastery, not sainthood, is the summit — and it is a political office.** "Hajji Imdadullah
  claimed that self-mastery, rather than just sainthood, was the highest level of sanctity. Those who
  achieved self-mastery could exercise social and political authority in a selfless and virtuous
  manner" (p. 279, folio 258). "His rhetoric of self-mastery presents the fully realized saint not as
  reclusive or emotive but rather as embodying the life of the political body of his community"
  (p. 279, folio 258).
- **Saints stepped into the vacancy left by the Mughal emperor.** "It is as if the Mughal emperor
  could no longer represent the axis of Muslim public order in South Asia, so that Muslim saints had
  to step in and assert authority as social and even political leaders in a decentralized network
  rooted in diverse places that could not be easily eliminated or co-opted" (p. 280, folio 259). The
  urgency is given concretely: "the British had blinded the last Mughal emperor and banished him to
  Burma after hanging for treason most of the men of his royal family" (p. 280, folio 259).
- **Heart-centred ritual as a claim on the body politic.** "Imdadullah's discourse about the heart as
  the center of ritual devotion was to place the saint at the heart of the body politic. This was a
  bid not only for his personal authority as an activist saint but also for the authority of the
  Islamic reassertion that his followers were conducting" (p. 281, folio 260).
- **A three-stage history of the body in Sufi thought.** Early Khurasani (al-Tirmidhi): the body's
  operations as "the root of evil in an almost Manichaean way" (p. 281, folio 260); Shattari and
  Chishti Sufis of South Asia "recast this image in a far more optimistic framework, recommending
  techniques that can not only discipline the body but also actually transform its physiology"
  (p. 281, folio 260); Imdadullah "retains this optimism that the body can be fully transformed but
  couches this optimism in the socially conscious rhetoric and sharia-mindedness that was gaining
  ascendance during his age", focusing "upon the 'rectified heart' as the locus for mystical
  experience, religious authority and social reform" (p. 281, folio 260).
- **The mosque-in-the-heart metaphor, and the axis of three sites.** "if the building is firm and
  beautiful, then virtues, like worshipers, will come on their own volition and inhabit such a space,
  endowing it with sanctity" (p. 282, folio 261). "The heart of the true believer is the real,
  internal `Kacba[text-layer?]` [Kaʿba], of which the stone shrine in Mecca is a reminder" (p. 282,
  folio 261). "Sufis imagine an ascending axis of three symbolic sites that reflect each other in
  different planes of the cosmos: the heart in the center of the human body, the `Kacba[text-layer?]`
  in the center of the world, and the throne of God in the center of the cosmos" (p. 282, folio 261).
  Directly applicable to any entry that describes a shrine as a "house of God" — "just as a mosque or
  shrine is imagined to be `a"house[text-layer?]` [a "house] of God' in which divinity dwells,
  despite the immateriality of God" (p. 283, folio 262).
- **Four levels of the manual.** "It is conservative ... The manual is also creative ... His manual is
  also transformative ... Imdadullah's manual works at a fourth level as well — it is 'reformist' in
  struggling to reassert for modern times that mystical experience and sainthood are central pillars
  in social welfare and political cohesion" (pp. 283–284, folios 262–263).
- **No external revival without interior exploration.** "According to Imdadullah, there could be no
  revival of the external topography of religion (for the purpose of political autonomy or social
  cohesion) without an intrepid and far more frightening exploration of the interior of religion as
  spiritual anatomy" (p. 284, folio 263).
- **THE CONCLUSION'S CENTRAL THESIS, stated on its first page.** "This concluding chapter focuses on
  tombs and graves as the site of intra-Muslim debate over the nature of the body. It highlights both
  grave threats and the hope of renewed life, which are the twin poles between which Sufis chart
  their course in our modern period" (p. 286, folio not stated).
- **The saints are the circulatory system of the Sufi ideal.** "At the heart of this many-leveled
  structure of aims and ideals are the Muslim saints. As we have seen in these chapters, stories about
  saints are the arteries that run through all these levels, and virtue is the nutrient that they
  strive to provide to each part of the structure" (p. 287, folio 266).
- **The Sufi-infused ideal was a symptom of strength, not decline.** "Despite the shrillness of the
  naysayers, this Sufi-infused ideal vision thrived while Islamic societies were strong. It was an
  expression of that strength, in its acceptance of diversity, practical flexibility, and powerful
  union of aristocratic and popular cultures" (p. 288, folio 267). Its three classes of opponent are
  enumerated: "the small minority of rationalist theologians who could not accept the phenomenon of
  mystical experience, the vocal party jurists who were skeptical of the propriety of some Sufi
  practices ... and the rare ruler who suspected that the popularity of saints might threaten his
  power" (pp. 287–288, folios 266–267).
- **The book's own method, stated explicitly — descriptive, not prescriptive.** "The saints' stories
  presented here illustrate the wide variety of experiences that channeled into the institution of
  sainthood. They were chosen to descriptively celebrate that diversity rather than to prescriptively
  argue what is the true pattern of Islamic sainthood" (p. 288, folio 267). This is the closest the
  chunk comes to a statement about the five cases as a set.
- **The book's period and its two chronological brackets.** "These stories span the wide chronological
  terrain of the 'early modern period' (from the mid-fifteenth to the mid-nineteenth century)"
  (p. 288, folio 267), bracketed by Mawlay Idris at the start and, "By the time of Hajji Imdadullah in
  the mid-nineteenth century, the cracks in Islamic polities had split wide open" (p. 288, folio 267).
- **The conclusion's stated puzzle: why Sufis went quiet.** "Yet Sufi communities still exist today
  and in fact constitute an Islamic global network; however, they are seemingly quiet and hard to
  detect, in contrast to their dominant and vivid social roles in early modern Islamic societies. This
  concluding chapter ventures to explain this partial retreat by Sufis from public life as well as
  their persistence by exploring the Wahhabi movement and its conception of the body that is basically
  the image of the body buried and gone" (p. 288, folio 267).
- **"Neo-Sufism" rejected as a label.** Some scholars "label such movements in the nineteenth century
  as 'Neo-Sufism'", on the criteria of "a exoteric focus on enforcing the sharia, adoption of jihad
  rhetoric, and concern for `renewingjuridical[text-layer?]` [renewing juridical] independence
  (ijtihad)" (p. 289, folio 268; conclusion note 2). Kugle: "this label may be misleading even as it
  points to important changes" (p. 290, folio 269), because Imdadullah "promoted ritual engagement
  with the body that was almost Yogic in its athletic and imaginative regime, which different
  radically from the prescriptions of Wahhabi movement" (p. 290, folio 269).
- **Ibn Idris is the control case that rules out a regional explanation.** "Lest this be blamed solely
  on his Indian origins or his allegiance to the Chishti Sufi community we can fruitfully compare
  Imdadullah with another influential nineteenth-century Sufi leader who is often labeled 'Neo-Sufi'"
  (p. 290, folio 269). Conclusion: "Such a theological and mystical optimism cannot be attributed to
  Imdadullah's South Asian background or allegiance to the Chishti Sufi community" (p. 291, folio
  270).
- **Surface convergence, sub-surface divergence.** Ibn Idris, like the Wahhabis, "rejected the
  necessity of following the established juridical schools and felt that Muslims needed to refer to
  the original sources of the Qur'an and hadith to revive the sharia", which "has caused some scholars
  to conclude that Ibn Idris himself was 'a pseudo Wahhabi'" (p. 290, folio 269; conclusion note 3).
  Kugle: "However, Ibn Idris was, beneath the surface, very different from the Wahhabis, especially in
  his view of ritual, ethics, and scriptural interpretation" (p. 290, folio 269). Ibn Idris's own
  words on them: "miserable wretches who are bound inflexibly to the externalities of the law. They
  know the details of knowledge and use them to accuse of heresy those who oppose them" (p. 290,
  folio 269; conclusion note 4).
- **Wahhabism as political rebellion first.** "It is commonly understood as a puritan theological
  movement, but it was equally a political rebellion against Ottoman rule in Arabia" (p. 291, folio
  270). "As a political movement, the Wahhabis rebelled against both the local sharifian rulers of
  Mecca and their Ottoman overlords" (p. 292, folio 271).
- **THE CHUNK'S KEY FORMULATION FOR THE ARCHIVE: two opposite ways of resolving political crisis
  through the saint's body.** "The political instability of the advent of the modern period was not
  resolved by finding saints' bodies and building shrines over them but rather by denouncing saints'
  bodies and destroying their shrines and tombs. This iconoclastic Wahhabi urge contrasts starkly
  with the Sufi approach to the human body" (p. 292, folio 271).
- **The tomb-as-idol claim is an anthropology, not just a ritual rule.** "The Wahhabi claim that tombs
  are idols is rooted in a different understanding of the body, one that can be seen as
  disenchantment with the past; it is rooted in a bitter denial that the human body is invested with
  sacred power and acts as the vessel for God's spirit" (p. 292, folio 271).
- **Wahhabi theology as too radical to be called orthodox.** "Although Ibn Abd al-Wahhab claimed to
  uphold Sunni orthodoxy, his theology was far too radical to be called conservatively orthodox"
  (p. 293, folio 272). "He denounced the traditions of Islamic law as religious innovation, dismissed
  discursive theology as rationalist heresy, and discredited Sufi leaders as satanic hypocrites"

- **Takfir as the operative weapon.** "Their most powerful weapon was not military but ideological.

  their lives were forfeit and their property liable to destruction" (p. 293, folio 272).
- **Why the theology worked in a time of collapse.** "it offered the reassurance that this collapse
  was a good sign. If Islamic empires fell, Islamic traditions died, and Islamic worldviews collapsed,
