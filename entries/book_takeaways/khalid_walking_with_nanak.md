## What this book is

A book in two alternating registers: a present-day travelogue in which the author visits gurdwaras, temples and Sufi shrines in Pakistan with his mentor Iqbal Qaiser (Jhaman, Farooqabad/Chuharkana, Nankana Sahib, Eminabad, Tibba Haji Deen, Kanganpur, Sialkot, Deepalpur, Karachi, Katas Raj, Rohtas, Hassan Abdal, Uch, Kartarpur), and the AUTHOR'S RECONSTRUCTION of Guru Nanak's life as novelised scenes built from the janamsakhis and footnoted secondary works (Dhillon, Cunningham, Grewal, Gandhi and others, pp. 86–100), closing with back matter that includes a chronology (pp. 312–313) and entries on each Guru (pp. 314–317). The author writes as a self-described "product of a post-9/11 world" (p. 279, folio 253) and "as an anthropologist" (pp. 280–281), sceptical of miracle stories and of institutionalised religion, valuing oral tradition over verification, and openly doubting his own "biases" (p. 176, folio 150); he holds that Nanak was "influenced immensely by Islam" (p. 37, folio 19). Reliability: tesseract OCR of a scanned letterpress copy carrying a `[source stamp: www.sikhbookclub.com]` watermark on nearly every page, with letter confusions ("Augaf" for Auqaf, "Igbal" for Iqbal), heavy column-scrambling on many pages and several running headers that disagree with the folio rule; the Chronology page (p. 312, folio 286) is lost entirely and p. 314 has lost lines (see Doubts).

### gurdwara-darbar-sahib-kartarpur — Gurdwara Darbar Sahib Kartarpur

The book's "Kartarpur Sahib", "about an hour away from the city of Narowal", on the Ravi (p. 302, folio 276); once printed "Kartarpur Sahih" [OCR?] [Kartarpur Sahib] (p. 18, folio not stated). Other Kartarpurs in the book (Hargobind's, Harkrishan's, Har Rai's) are not identified with this row — see "Figures and sites not in the archive".

- **Saint** (Nanak's settlement here)
  - "Having spent twenty-four years of his life travelling, Nanak had finally settled down at a place called Kartarpur on the banks of the Ravi. Here he had started working on a piece of land that was given to him by one of his devotees"; he was joined by "his parents and also his wife and children"; devotees moved there; "Using Kartarpur as his base, Nanak now started travelling to nearby regions" (p. 288, folio 262). "He sent for his family from Talwindi [OCR?] [Talwandi] and started living at this small village called Kartarpur" (p. 296, folio 270).
  - "It had now been seventeen years that Nanak had been leading a sedentary life. Hundreds of devotees from neighbouring villages and beyond came to visit him every day"; kirtan every evening; "eventually a new hall was constructed which could host the huge gathering. This place was reserved for the evening prayers and was referred to as a Gurdwara by Nanak, where the Ultimate Guru resided" (p. 298, folio 272).
  - "The story of Nanak, the Gurus and us, converges at Kartarpur Sahih [OCR?] [Kartarpur Sahib] where Guru Nanak lay on his deathbed, breathing life into a new religi[on]" (p. 18, folio not stated; this line alone does not say which Kartarpur).
  - Back matter: "After his travels he settled at Kartarpur"; Guru Angad "became a disciple of Guru Nanak after he had settled at Kartarpur Sahib" (p. 314, folio 288).
  - Author's reconstruction: Nanak lives "in a simple hut that was built of wood and hay" and walks each dawn "towards the Ravi that flowed close to his hut", bathing there "for the past seventeen years" (p. 293, folio 267); he wishes he could have buried Mardana at "Kartarpur, his final abode" (damaged passage) (p. 293, folio 267); at the evening gathering Sulakhni and other women "prepared for the langar which concluded the evening", and Nanak tells her "that Muslims and even untouchables would be welcome to the langar", which at first "repulsed her" (p. 296, folio 270).
- **Shrine / Darbar** (travelogue, with Iqbal Qaiser, "a Sunday morning", year not stated)
  - Approach: "This is the border area; hence the roads are in a bad condition"; "a mud track towards the Ravi, which still flows next to the shrine"; on his first visit Iqbal Qaiser "had to walk from the main road to the gurdwara", "a road of about three kilometres", through partly flooded rice paddies (p. 302, folio 276).
  - At the gate policemen "sitting on a charpoy" drink tea; an old Muslim man and his wife "from a neighbouring village" walk in "without any trouble": "This was Kartarpur Sahib, the final resting abode of Guru Nanak and here, Muslims were allowed to enter." (p. 302, folio 276). A policeman recognises Iqbal Qaiser and waves the car in (pp. 302–303, folios 276–277).
  - "The main shrine was protected by a newly constructed wall." By the parking, "what appeared to be an airplane missile preserved in a glass case" with a marble plaque (see Legends); next to it "a well, covered with marble", whose plaque says it "was the well that was used by Guru Nanak to water his fields"; sugarcane and a peasant working the fields nearby (p. 303, folio 277).
  - Visitors take shoes off and cover heads "with skullcaps that were placed next to the entrance"; "The main structure was in the middle of a courtyard, while around it there were rooms meant to be used to house pilgrims." (p. 303, folio 277)
  - "the glass door that led into the main shrine, where the samadhi of Nanak was situated" was locked; "The main building was constructed around the samadhi, whereas the grave was on one side of the building" (p. 305, folio 279).
  - The grave: "There was a grave next to the main shrine", in the open; its description is OCR-damaged ("There was no tombonsiendgas [OCR?] its occupier"); "there was 2 [OCR?] [a] couplet etched on it" (p. 304, folio 278). Inderjit: "There is a grave of Nanak next to his samadhi" (p. 307, folio 281). The author puts it to Iqbal Qaiser that the grave might be Bhai Mardana's, "next to the samadhi of his Guru" (p. 304, folio 278) — see gurdwara-rori-sahib.
  - Iqbal Qaiser: "the original building was destroyed in a flood several years ago and this new one was built later"; the author: "The grave of Nanak became a casualty of the growing Muslim-Sikh antagonism." (p. 305, folio 279)
  - Iqbal Qaiser (partly OCR-damaged): on his first visit "this had not become a shrine [as] it is today"; it "used to be a centre for smugglers because of its proximity to the border. Drug addicts too had established a place for themselves" (pp. 307–308, folios 281–282).
  - Inderjit: "We established this gurdwara in 2001 … This is wher[e] [OCR?] we renovated the structure. We also prepared our first langar here at that time." (p. 308, folio 282). Works under way: "Right now there was only the shrine and [a] few rooms which were reserved for the caretakers. Soon there would be a langar hall as well as a pool" (OCR line order scrambled) (p. 309, folio not stated).
  - Architecture: Inderjit — "A lot of Sikhs from England and Canada have complained that the architecture of the shrine is not, strictly speaking, Sikh … it has a lot of features of Muslim architecture"; its arches "were plain. This was a feature of Muslim architecture. Sikh arches were usually three-cusped." (p. 309, folio not stated)
  - The border lies "towards the east, across the river"; the Ravi "sometimes crosses into India while at other points it comes back to Pakistan. Otherwise, the river is parched throughout the year"; had Radcliffe erred "by only a few centimetres, the shrine of Kartarpur Sahib would have ended up in India" (p. 307, folio 281).
- **Practices & Events**
  - Custodian: the Granthi "nowadays" is "Inderjit", who is "in the camps"; a young man, "probably a government official deputed here and most probably a Muslim" in the author's guess, says "No photography" (p. 305, folio 279). Inderjit's shed "had been constructed recently with the help of the Sikh Diaspora", with a double bed, a sofa set and a television showing Geo news of floods (pp. 305–306, folios 279–280).
  - Inderjit's family "belonged to a village in Faisalabad district. They weren't Sikhs but Nanak Panthi Hindus"; his garb "was that of a baptized Sikh, the Khalsa of Guru Gobind Singh"; at Partition his grandfather, on his way to India, was stopped "near the border by the Muslims of their village", who promised him security, and lived peacefully "till the war of 1965"; "In 1965, his family moved to Nankana"; he declined to discuss that period (p. 306, folio 280). The author: Inderjit "was now a government official and lived under its protection"; "the Pakistani establishment which regularly monitors the activities of Sikhs in Pakistan" (pp. 306–307, folios 280–281).
  - Muslim devotion: the Muslim couple pray at the grave; the man [OCR-damaged verb] "towards the feet of the grave, while the woman touched it with her hand and passed it over her head, blessing herself" (p. 304, folio 278). Inderjit: local Muslims "come here regularly and offer their fateha (first verse of the Quran, used as a prayer) just like they would at any Muslim shrine", and regard Nanak as a "[s]aint" (OCR "int,'") (p. 307, folio 281). Iqbal Qaiser: locals still came to the grave, presented offerings and "clean[ed] the shrine as a service", a tradition "Prevalent even before Partition" (pp. 307–308, folios 281–282).
  - Langar, per Inderjit: "rich Muslim families of the neighbouring villages provide us with monetary support to cook langar. This is their offering to the shrine"; "The wood that we require for cooking langar is provided to us by the Rangers"; Muslims at first "were hesitant to eat food cooked by Sikhs", but "Today, more Muslims eat food here compared to Sikhs." (p. 308, folio 282)
  - Across the border, behind a fence, "Indian pilgrims, who gather there to perform darshan of the shrine of Guru Nanak" through binoculars; "Hundreds of people gather there every day" (p. 308, folio 282).
  - "There are talks to declare this region a peace corridor", under which Indian pilgrims would enter "without a visa"; "The Pakistani government has given its approval to the Proposal; however the Indian government is not ready to implement the deal", saying it "would be used b[y] [OCR?] terrorists" — speaker apparently Iqbal Qaiser, OCR damaged (pp. 308–309, folio 282 / folio not stated).
- **Legends & Miracles**
  - The plaque by the parking: the missile "was dropped here by the Indian forces in 1971 but due to the miracle of Guru Nanak, it did not blow up" (p. 303, folio 277).
  - Local Muslims' "myth", as Inderjit tells it: "the river floods every twenty odd years and comes right up to the shrine. This is the river paying tribute to the shrine of Nanak." (p. 307, folio 281)
  - Inderjit: a "giant snake" "bigger than two humans combined"; snakes found in the fields are placed next to the shrine: "After paying their respects they return. All they want to do is to catch a sight of the shrine." (p. 309, folio not stated)
- **Significance**
  - The author: "Had Nanak supervised the construction of his own shrine, would he have allowed Muslim influences in the building, which the Sikh Diaspora now complained against?" (p. 310, folio 284)

### gurdwara-panja-sahib — Gurdwara Panja Sahib

The book's "Panja Sahib", "Gurdwara of Panja Sahib", at "Hassan Abdal" (archive "Hasan Abdal"). The only archive row in Hassan Abdal, so the town's place-level material is filed here.

- **Saint**
  - Named only as a destination: pilgrim tourists "come to Nankana Sahib and Hassan Abdal" and also visit Sacha Sauda (p. 79, folio 61).
  - Author's reconstruction: Naju tells Hari Singh that Nanak "came to Hassan Abdal and spent a few days here", as "recorded in the Janamsakhis of Bhai Bala"; Hari Singh's Granthi confirms that "on the 1 [OCR?] [1st] of Vaisakh, Nanak had visited Hassan Abdal as is recorded in the Janamsakhis of Bala" (p. 263, folio 237); local tradition in the scene has Nanak staying a few days under "a peepal tree on the outskirts of the city" (p. 264, folio 238).
- **Shrine / Darbar**
  - Travelogue: "The Gurdwara of Panja Sahib is i[n] [OCR?] the plain below us" the mountain of Wali Qandhari (p. 267, folio 241); Iqbal Qaiser looks down on "the grand Gurdwara of Panja Sahib" (p. 269, folio 243). "The gurdwara that was [built in the] nineteenth century was behind me" (words lost in OCR) (p. 271, folio 245).
  - The pool, as the legend's speaker tells it (speaker partly lost in OCR): citing "Salman Rashid's book The Salt Range and the Potohar Plateau", "this pool of water next to the gurdwara" is thousands of years old and was "considered sacred even in the Buddhist era"; "The Chinese traveller Hiuen Tsang, in the 7th century CE" reportedly saw it as "a sacred lake", a dip in which was believed to cure diseases; "After a gurdwara was built here during the tenure 4 [OCR?] [of] Maharaja Ranjit Singh", the pool "remained sacred but wa[s] given a different iconography", based on Nanak's miracles (p. 270, folio 244).
  - A poster of "the Gurdwara Panja Sahib" hangs in the Manora gurdwara in Karachi, with another of Nanak "stopping with his hand, a ro[c]k hurled towards him from the top of a mountain where the Muslim saint was" (p. 228, folio 202).
  - Hassan Abdal in the author's reconstruction (chapter 18 "The Legend"): Naju walks from "the street of the temple into the street of the Imambargah", past "the old main bazaar"; "This was the connecting town between Punjab and the Khyber"; the town was "abandoned by its citizens" at the Sikh advance; Hari Singh Nalwa camps on the Grand Trunk Road outside; the city "was on a small mountain" (pp. 258–259, folios 232–233); the residents leave for "the town of Attock" (p. 260, folio 234); from the peepal tree Hari Singh could see "gardens that had been constructed by Emperor Jahangir" (pp. 264–265, folios 238–239).
- **Practices & Events** (travelogue)
  - At "Baisakhi", when thousands "descend upon this shrine, several also travel up to this Muslim shrine" of Wali Qandhari (p. 267, folio 241); the festival calendar there records "Baisakh[i]" [OCR?], which "coincides with the [festival] at the shrine of Guru Nanak at the bottom of the mountain" (p. 269, folio 243; OCR heavily damaged).
  - A pilgrim "in his sixties" in a "saffron headscarf" put his hand on the mark — "his hand too fit the hand [of] Nanak perfectly. Miracle!" — placed "a ten rupee note" on the rock, kissed it, poured water from the pool on it and cleaned it; a queue of devotees; "mysterious hands emerged from the windows [fl]anking the rock"; women "placed money o[n] top of the rock" (p. 271, folio 245).
  - It was "Baisakhi and the festival was only beginning", with Sikh pilgrims "all here to commemorate [the] formation of the Khalsa" (p. 271, folio 245).
- **Legends & Miracles**
  - The standard legend (told in reply to Anam; speaker partly lost in OCR): Nanak "arrived here along with Mardana"; they sat "under a peepal tre[e]" where the "gurdwara now stands"; Mardana climbs three times to ask the hermit Wali Qandhari for water and is refused, the third time asking "in the name of God"; Nanak "removed a stone from the ground and water started to gush out"; the Wali's water disappears and he "hurled a rock at Nanak and Mardana, which Nanak stopped with his hand. The print of that hand remained on the rock" (pp. 269–270, folios 243–244). Wali Qandhari is the hermit who "felt jealous of Guru Nanak" (p. 270, folio 244).
  - Iqbal Qaiser's doubt: "if a rock was thrown from here, how could it possibly reach" the gurdwara, and why did Nanak "need[ed] to stop [it with his] hand"? (p. 269, folio 243; OCR damaged)
  - The author's counter-narrative, as a novelised scene (AUTHOR'S RECONSTRUCTION): on "the eve of the Sikh invasion", with news of "Hari Singh Nalwa's approach", only the fakir "Naju" stays and is found "sitting at the shrine" (p. 260, folio 234); a month earlier Naju had seen "a Muslim mason called Kamma" carve the outline of his own hand into a rock, "like a signature that he was leaving for posterity" (pp. 262–263, folios 236–237); Kamma "had been responsible for creating numerous carvings in the temples of Hassan Abdal", his grandfather having converted to Islam "To avoid the jaziya tax that the Mughal Emperor Aurangzeb had reinitiated" (p. 262, folio 236); Naju gives Hari Singh the rock as bearing "the hand print of your Guru Nanak" (p. 263, folio 237); Hari Singh says "See how the hand of Nanak fits my hand perfectly", kisses it and places gold coins on it; "Even while it was at the camp, the rock turned into a shrine" for "Muslims, Hindus and Sikhs" (p. 264, folio 238); Hari Singh orders "the rock to be placed there, under the tree, and for a shrine to be [built] to commemorate the spot where Nanak and Mardana had rested" (p. 265, folio 239).
  - The author at the rock: he placed his hand on the print, "at that moment genuinely believing", yet "I knew this was a fictitious story dreamed up by a man called Naju the fakir to save his life, while the handprint [was chiselled] [OCR?] by a man called Kamma" (p. 271, folio 245).

### gurdwara-sacha-sauda — Gurdwara Sacha Sauda

At the town the book calls "Farooqabad", historical name "Chuhrkhana" / "Chuharkhana" (pp. 63, 70–71, folios 45, 52–53). The nearby "Gurdwara Sachkand" is a different building and is NOT gurdwara-sach-khand-sahib (Shikarpur) — see "Figures and sites not in the archive". The gurdwara visited on pp. 80–83 is not named on those pages; it is attached here because the author calls the visit "while we were at Chuhrkhana" (p. 92, folio 74).

- **Saint** (the Sacha Sauda episode)
  - Footnote: "This incident is known as Sacha Sauda (honest trade) and is one of the most important events from Nanak's early life" — Nanak spending his father's money on food for ascetics (p. 66, folio 48).
  - Author's reconstruction: Mehta Kalu gives the "eighteen-year-old Nanak" "the hefty amount of twenty rupees" to visit "the market town of Chuharkhana about twelve kos ( forty-five kilometres) from here" (p. 63, folio 45); in "a jungle on the way" Nanak meets naked ascetics with matted hair, "Their leader was a wise man called Santrain", a group which "refers to itself as Nirbani" (p. 65, folio not stated); Santrain refuses money as "against his religion", so "when I reached Chuharkha [OCR?] [Chuharkhana] ... I bought food for the ascetics ... That was my honest trade"; Mehta Kalu slaps him and Rai Bular offers to adopt him (p. 66, folio 48). The scene also refers to "the naked ascetics in the jungle of Chuhrkhana" (p. 61, folio 43), who "did not beg for food" (p. 62, folio 44).
  - Travelogue: Iqbal Qaiser — "It was under these trees that Nanak discoursed with the leader of the yogis" (p. 80, folio 62); "This gurdwara was constructed to commemorate the event" (p. 81, folio 63). A local Muslim storyteller at Gurdwara Sachkand says the yogis Nanak fed were meditating "on top of a mound. The mound is where the big gurdwara stands" (p. 74, folio 56).
- **Shrine / Darbar** (travelogue)
  - "At a little distance from the main road, was the Gurdwara Sacha Sauda. It was a splendid structure, recently renovated", within "a protective wall covered with barbed wire on top" (p. 71, folio 53).
  - Layout: through a portal into a courtyard "at the cent[re] of which stood a white gurdwara"; beside it "a few waan trees enclosed by a small boundary"; gurdwara, courtyard and trees "situated on an ancient mound, which was the highest point in and around the city" (p. 80, folio 62); the trees stand "in the middle of a marbled courtyard", "the last remnants of the jungle that used to exist here" (p. 81, folio 63).
  - Interior: "a wooden palanquin in which the Guru Granth Sahib was placed", before it "a couple of swords" (p. 81, folio 63). A "new sukhasan" behind the gurdwara: carpeted, walls "done up in wood", "a split air-conditioner" (off "as there was no electricity"), a carved wooden bed; asked if it was made in "Armritsar [OCR?] [Amritsar]", Boota Singh said "It was made in Nankana Sahib" (p. 81, folio 63).
  - Former state, per Iqbal Qaiser: "I saw this gurdwara for the first time in 1993 when I was researching my book", with "Ilyas Ghuman"; "Buffaloes were tied in the courtyard while drug addicts and gamblers had occupied the rest of the space" (p. 79, folio 61).
  - Renovation, per Iqbal Qaiser: a local man "referred to as Shah" ("I don't remember his real name") tried to keep the place sacred and was beaten by the addicts and gamblers; Qaiser and Ghuman took him to hospital and filed a police report; the issue reached "a few national newspapers"; "the Department of Augaf [OCR?] [Auqaf]" came, "appointed him the official guard of this place", and "In subsequent years, the gurdwara was renovated" (p. 79, folio 61). He affirms ("Yes.") that he "played a major part in the renovation of this gurdwara, like in Shaheed Ganj" (p. 79, folio 61).
  - After Iqbal Qaiser's book "a debate raged about the renovation of a number of gurdwaras, one of which was the Gurdwara Sacha Sauda. Once the gurdwara was constructed, this new road also had to be made to connect it to Nankana Sahib" (p. 85, folio 67); the road, "constructed to facilitate Sikh pilgrims", takes "only about twenty minutes"; before it pilgrims "had to travel to Sheikupura and then head towards Chuhrkhana" (p. 85, folio 67). The author to Boota Singh: "Sacha Sauda is already prominent" (p. 83, folio 65).
- **Practices & Events** (travelogue)
  - At the gate "two security men sitting inside the gate while a policeman sat next to them with his gun resting on his shoulder"; Iqbal Qaiser says they are "new boys" who do not recognise him (p. 79, folio 61). A boy at the gate would not let them in until the guard phoned Boota Singh (p. 80, folio 62).
  - "Boota Singh was the name of the sewadg [OCR?] [sewadar] … appointed here by the Augaf Departmen [OCR?] [Auqaf Department]"; "about forty years old", "wearing a green shalwar kameez with a green turban", with his youngest son, "about ten years old"; "extremely shy" (p. 80, folio 62); "I have been working here for the past twelve years" (p. 83, folio 65). Iqbal Qaiser has "always come here with a jatha (a group of Sikhs)" whom Boota Singh shows round (p. 80, folio 62).
  - Iqbal Qaiser "took out a ten rupee note and placed it there", walked round the palanquin and fanned the Guru Granth Sahib with a "flying-whisk"; the author did the same (p. 81, folio 63). Boota Singh left the sukhasan backwards so his back never faced the Guru Granth Sahib, and the visitors did likewise (p. 82, folio 64).
  - "one of the major shrines that is visited by pilgrim tourists who come to Nankana Sahib and Hassan Abdal" (p. 79, folio 61).
  - The town: driving in "the monsoon season" during "Ramzan", Iqbal Qaiser gives the new name "Farooqabad" and the old "Chuhrkhana", says "This is still a mandi", and puts it "About forty-five kilometres, I think" from Nankana Sahib "in a straight line" (p. 70, folio 52). In "the Zia era" a resident, "Farooq Dogar", was elected MP "when we had non-party based elections in the eighties", supported the military dictator, was "killed over a personal feud", and the town was renamed "from Chuhrkhana to Faroogabad [OCR?] [Farooqabad] in his honour"; "Chuhr" is two bangles worn at the start and end of regular bangles, "a Sanskrit word which means to lead" (p. 71, folio 53).
- **Legends & Miracles**
  - "According to legend", yogis "were hanging from the trees while there were others who were sitting within an empty trunk when Nanak passed through" (p. 81, folio 63; OCR partly garbled).

### gurdwara-babay-nanki — Gurdwara Babay Nanki

The book's "Gurdwara Janamasthan Bebe Nanaki" / "Gurdwara Bebe Nanaki" at the village of Dera Chahal, "on the Bedian road" (apparently the "Gurdwara Dera Chahal" of p. 43); the person Bebe Nanaki (also "Bebe Nanki", once "Bey" [OCR?] [Bebe]).

- **Saint** (Bebe Nanaki)
  - "Gurdwara Bebe Nanaki is narned [OCR?] [named] after Guru Nanak's elder sister. According to tradition, she was born in a house that was situated where the gurdwara now stands"; Mata Tripta "belonged to this historical village of Dera Chahal, located on the Bedian road, now flanked between DHA Phase Five, Six and Seven"; "According to tradition, Nanak was named after his sister Nanaki" (p. 34, folio 16).
  - Author's reconstruction: five months pregnant, Tripta goes to "her maternal home, a village called Dera Chahal at a little distance from Lahore"; "about six years after their wedding" a daughter is born, named Nanaki at her grandmother's suggestion "since the child was born in her maternal house, Nanaka in Punjabi" (p. 29, folio 11).
  - Author's reconstruction: she brings her father "a glass of hot milk mixed with almonds" and defends her brother — "He is a very sensible person" (p. 63, folio 45); "Our Nanak is nos [OCR?] [not] luke [OCR?] [like] ... people. He is sensitive and observant" (p. 64, folio 46). She "got married and moved to Sultanpur" (p. 66, folio 48); she arranged his marriage, having "already identified a girl", Sulakhni (p. 67, folio 49). Her husband "Jai Ram" was well established "in the court of Nawab Daulat Khan, the Gov eT [OCR?] [Governor] of Sultanpuy [OCR?] [Sultanpur]" (p. 67, folio 49; p. 68, folio 50); Nanak went to live with them (p. 68, folio 50) — he "had been sent to Sultanpur by his father on the request of Bey [OCR?] [Bebe] Nanaki and her husband" (p. 106, folio 88).
  - Nanak later "decided to visit his sister, Nanaki, at Sultanpur", stopping at Dera Chahal, "Jaman" and "Ghavind;" [OCR?] [Ghavindi] (p. 54, folio 36); in Iqbal Qaiser's narration he was going "to visit his sister, brother-in-law and his friend Nawab Daulat Khan Lodbi [OCR?] [Lodhi]" (p. 55, folio 37).
  - Author's reconstruction: "Bebe Nanki was particularly sympathetic towards Sulakhni" (p. 294, folio not stated); she "decided to look after her eldest son, Sri Chand [printed 'Sai Chand' [OCR?]]"; "Bebe Nanki did not have a child of her own, which made the decision to temporarily adopt" him easier; Sri Chand followed "the spiritual pursuits of his father, encouraged by Bebe Nanki" (p. 295, folio 269).
- **Shrine / Darbar** (travelogue, "the month of June", leaving at six in the morning, "about two hours here" — p. 40, folio 22)
  - "The white dome of the gurdwara was visible from afar"; the village is ringed by new houses of former agriculturists who sold land to developers (p. 33, folio 15).
  - "A saffron flag rose from behind the wall testifying that there were members of the Sikh community living here"; behind it "a pool of water where buffaloes wallowed. It was once the sacred pool of this shrine. Next to it was a mosque which was being expanded" (p. 34, folio 16).
  - Iqbal Qaiser: the area "used to be the langar hall"; "Now it is a lawn with a few rooms around it" (p. 35, folio 17). Asked whether it stands "on the exact spot where Nanak's maternal family home used to be", he said "Yes"; the gurdwara "was one big hall with a small [OCR? "omall"] balcony opening towards the centre of the hall from the first floor" (p. 38, folio 20).
  - "Since this was Nanak's maternal home he often came here. Later, his followers built a gurdwara here to mark the spot"; Maharaja Ranjit Singh spent on renovating and expanding many gurdwaras "including this one" and attached land to them (p. 34, folio 16).
  - Renovation, per Iqbal Qaiser: "There was a lot of pressure on the Government of Pakistan from Sikhs living in Canada, England and America to renovate this gurdwara after my book came out"; "It was in a horrible state when Meraj Khalid ordered its renovation. Even then, only its external structure was spruced up. The gurdwara remained shut to the Sikh community. Only recently has the government allowed pilgrims to visit this gurdwara when they come to Pakistan for festivals. Now on those occasions, prayers are offered here" (p. 33, folio 15). Malik Meraj Khalid, "born in 1916 to a poor agricultural family of this village", PPP, twice "National Speaker", once Chief Minister of Punjab and "between November 1996 and February 1997" interim Prime Minister: "One of his first orders as the Prime Minister was the approval of funds to renovate this historical gurdwara at his village", "though he was not Sikh" (pp. 33–34, folios 15–16).
- **Practices & Events**
  - The land case: Ghulab Singh, "the first Sikh police officer in the revamped traffic police of Lahore", filed a case against ETPB in the Lahore High Court "for the alleged sale of agricultural land associated with Gurdwara Janamasthan Bebe Nanaki"; a few days before the visit the Supreme Court of Pakistan "took cognisance of the case" and asked the construction companies "to pay Rupees 986 million as surety while the hearings proceeded" (p. 35, folio 17). He stays here when in Lahore; his official bike was in the courtyard (p. 35, folio 17). "Behind the once-sacred pool now occupied by buffaloes is the agricultural land in dispute"; a new apartment block was visible from the window (p. 36, folio 18).
  - A young priest, Daleep Singh, led them in (p. 35, folio 17); he alleged documents prove ETPB corruption and that "Asif Hashmi (former head of the ETPB) has earned billions in his tenure of five years"; the author adds "In April 2016 Asif Hashmi was arrested in Dubai" (p. 35, folio 17). A mosque's loudspeaker sermon drowned the conversation (p. 36, folio 18).
  - Daleep bowed before the Guru Granth Sahib "placed in a wooden Thara Sahib"; the author and Iqbal Qaiser did not bow (p. 37, folio 19).
  - Iqbal Qaiser: Bhai Bannu, sent by Guru Arjan to Lahore to bind the Adi Granth, "came and stayed at this gurdwara" and prepared another copy "without the Guru's permission", with additional verses the Guru rejected; "one of the movements also started from this particular gurdwara" (p. 39, folio 21).
- **Legends & Miracles**
  - In the Sachkand storyteller's version of the mud-into-sugar story, "when his sister opened the bags on the mules she found that the mud had actually been changed into sugar and gur" (p. 75, folio 57).

### gurdwara-babay-de-ber — Gurdwara Babay De Ber

The book's "Baba Bair (the shrine of the Berry saint)", Sialkot; the book never prints "Babay de Ber" — the identification is by name and place (Pass-1 judgement).

- **Saint** (Nanak at the berry tree — author's reconstruction)
  - After walking from Eminabad, Nanak "spotted a [OCR?] berry tree not far from the city and decided to rest under it"; "There were a few scattered houses and shops around" (p. 185, folio 159).
  - A young Hindu woman with her daughter ties cloth to a branch and circles the tree chanting "Vedic prayers"; her community thinks trees are sacred because "holy spirits resided in them", and she prays "for a son" (pp. 185–186, folio 159 / folio not stated).
  - Nanak and Maula Karar talk "under the tree" all evening; Mardana is sent out and returns to "the berry tree" (pp. 187–188, folios 161–162).
- **Shrine / Darbar** (travelogue)
  - Iqbal Qaiser asks a traffic policeman "where is Baba Bair (the shrine of the Berry saint)?"; he points "away from the ancient city of Sialkot", to a place "on top of a mound" (pp. 193–194, folios 167–168); they drive "through the narrow streets of this mohalla (locality) which is now known as the mohalla of Baba Bair" (p. 194, folio 168). Author's diary: "Everybody seemed to know where Baba Bair is but do they also know that it is called Baba Bair because of Guru Nanak?" (p. 194, folio 168)
  - A board at the entrance "noted that the gurdwara was renovated in 2012 by the Augaf [OCR?] [Auqaf] Department"; new tiles lay "on one side, rotting away"; the floor "had recently been completed and the building refurbished, but the gurdwara was far from ready to become functional" (p. 194, folio 168). He had been told it "was now function[OCR?]" through "a few Sikhs living in Nankana Sahib", and found it "quite different" (p. 194, folio 168).
  - "an open structure", "a veranda" whose pillars hold up the roof, and "only one [room] in the centre of the building where the Guru Granth Sahib w[as kept?] [OCR?]"; "The berry tree was behind the building" (p. 194, folio 168).
  - The tree: "a thick tree, bending towards the ground. It looked ancient, if judged by its size." Under it "a long grave … covered with a red sheet with Quranic verses on it"; Iqbal Qaiser: "This grave is new"; "a red flag at the entrance" (p. 195, folio 169).
  - After Partition the gurdwara was abandoned but the place, "particularly the tree", stayed sacred through Nanak; eventually a grave "of this non-existent saint was constructed"; when Iqbal Qaiser last came for his book "there was no such grave" (p. 196, folio 170).
  - Graffiti "'Ya Allah' and 'Ya Muhammad'" on a wall, Gurmukhi text wiped clean and the slogans "scribbled with a piece of coal"; "the building had been Islamised"; a "newly built mosque next [to] the shrine" (p. 196, folio 170).
  - The shrine of Hamza Ghous lies in an old graveyard not far from "the berry tree under which Nanak sat" (p. 199, folio 173).
- **Practices & Events** (travelogue)
  - Men "looking at us suspiciously"; one group "preparing bhang (cannabis)", another "in another corner was smoking dope"; under the tree "where Nanak and Mardana sat" a few men "playing cards, most probably gambling, but no money was visible as gambling is illegal in Pakistan" (p. 195, folio 169).
  - A man of "about thirty years", apparently "hoping for a tip", says it was a gurdwara "abandoned in 1947", calls the pictures of Hindu deities "frescoes", and points out floor tiles carrying "names of Sikh devotees who had contributed towards the initial construction of this gurdwara" (p. 195, folio 169).
- **Legends & Miracles**
  - The same man: the grave is "the grave of Pir Bairi (the saint of berries)", "Very old", and "Guru Nanak also came to this saint and stayed with him … Guru Nanak realised that Pir Bairi was a powerful saint" (p. 195, folio 169); the author calls it "an apocryphal story".
- **Significance**
  - The author: perhaps the tree was sacred before Nanak came, Nanak becoming one of the reasons; "Now this gurdwara has become a shrine to Baba Bair with the red flag at the entrance as his symbol" (p. 196, folio 170).

### gurdwara-rori-sahib — Gurdwara Rori Sahib

The Eminabad gurdwara, and the person Bhai Mardana (principal figure). **The book's other "Gurdwara Rori Sahib", at Jhaman, is NOT this row**; Jhaman appears below only where Mardana is named there.

- **Saint** (Bhai Mardana)
  - Named as Nanak's "Muslim companion" (p. 16, folio not stated; p. 54, folio 36); "Bhai Bala and Bhai Mardana, a Hindu and a Muslim respectively and both companions of Nanak" (p. 95, folio 77); his poetry is in the Guru Granth Sahib (p. 82, folio 64; p. 317, folio 291 — "poetry of several non-Sikh saints including Baba Farid, Bhai Mardana and Bhagat Kabir").
  - Author's reconstruction: "a Muslim Mirasi (a community of genealogists and bards)", Nanak's best friend from Talwindi, who joined him at Sultanpur and "had ignited within Nanak a passion fo[r] music"; Nanak's family "regarded Mardana as an untouchable Mirasi" (p. 106, folio 88). He grew up travelling with his clan, singing the bravery of local chieftains for gifts, and knew "folk stories of Hindu deities and Muslim saints" (pp. 120–121, folios 102–103); he worried that "music was un-Islamic, a criticism that came from the more puritanical sects of Islam" (p. 122, folio 104). As a younger man he "would roam the cities and villages of Punjab, singing folk songs and legends", Puran Bhagat's among the most popular (p. 184, folio 158); he wanted to see Puran Bhagat's temple at Sialkot "not due to his religious convictions but because of the memory associated with the legend" (p. 185, folio 159).
  - Author's reconstruction of the friendship: he regards Nanak "as his mentor and Guru" but the relationship "was not constrained by formalities" and he "could sometimes blackmail his friend" (p. 123, folio 105); "He had accepted Nanak as his eternal guru … For Nanak, Mardana was a friend and an equal, never a devotee" (p. 187, folio 161); he wore "a white chola", so a shopkeeper took him for "a fakir or a dervish" (p. 187, folio 161); Kamal calls him "a Mirasi bard" who "plays the rubab while Nanak sings" (p. 143, folio 125); Nanak describes him as "born in the house of a Muslim Mirasi family" (p. 243, folio 217). Further scenes: weeping at a song he thinks "directed against him" (p. 125, folio 107); noticing "a lot of similarity between the symbolism of Nanak and Sheikh Farid" (p. 141, folio 123); at Baba Farid's shrine (pp. 147–148, folios 129–130); at Kanganpur "under a berry tree" tuning his rubab, then stoned with Nanak (pp. 162–164, folios 144–146); the truth-and-untruth errand to Maula Karar at Sialkot (pp. 187–188, folios 161–162); at Deepalpur hoping "they will have langar for us", worrying over Nanak's "exhaustion and homesickness", recoiling from Nuri the leper (pp. 207–209, folios 181–183); waiting on the mountain at Katas Raj and resolving, after "a long trip to the East and South", to stop travelling because "separation from family" had taken its toll (pp. 236–238, folios 210–211 / folio not stated); feeling "like a young man again" on the northern journey, "the soul of a wanderer" (p. 239, folio 213).
  - At Jhaman (not this site): "It is believed that Nanak, along with his companion Mardana, rested here under a tree. Nanak made a rubab for his friend out of the branches"; the rubab "has acquired a particular significance in the Sikh tradition because of its association with Mardana" (p. 48, folio 30). At the Ghavindi shrine "two plinths meant to represent the seats of Guru Nanak and Bhai Mardana. Nanak's was higher than Mardana's" (p. 54, folio 36). In Kanganpur, paintings on the back wall of an old shop whose text "confirmed that they were of Guru Nanak and Bhai Mardana" (p. 164, folio 146). A Manora poster shows "Guru Nanak, Mardana and Wali Gandhari" (p. 228, folio 202).
  - Iqbal Qaiser's Janamsakhi reading: Nanak "is stripped of all his human characteristics", while Mardana is prey to human weaknesses — the two "probably used as symbols for religious and worldly life" (p. 56, folio 38).
  - Death and grave: "Mardana had passed away in Baghdad while they were on the way back from the Hajj. Nanak did not have a fixed companion anymore" (p. 288, folio 262); in the reconstruction Nanak weeps "for his friend Mardana, who had passed away several years ago in Baghdad" (damaged) (p. 293, folio 267). The author: "no grave marks the spot where Mardana was buried. According to one story, he passed away in Baghdad but there is another story which says that Mardana returned to Punjab with Nanak and died here in Kartarpur. It was, however, his last wish that no shrine be constructed over his grave"; he asks whether the grave beside Nanak's samadhi at Kartarpur may be "the grave of Bhai Mardana" and the no-shrine story "added later on" (p. 304, folio 278). "If there was one person besides Mardana who understood the poetry of Nanak as Nanak himself did, then it was Bhai Lehna" (damaged) (p. 299, folio 273).
- **Shrine / Darbar** (travelogue, Eminabad)
  - Named as nearby, with "members of the Sikh comm[unity]" residing there (p. 116, folio 98). The gatekeeper knew Iqbal Qaiser; empty grounds either side of the path; "a few police officials lazed on a charpoy" (pp. 116–117, folios 98–99).
  - The doorway is "a splendid construction. A verse from the Guru Granth Sahib was carved on top of it with an artistic effect of bricks." Iqbal Qaiser says the designer was "Bhai Ram Singh", Principal of "Mayo School of Arts in Lahore", who designed "the Indian gallery in Buckingham Palace" and came from a family of carpenters, creating "the effect of woodcutting from the use of bricks"; "It was constructed around 1922-23. Before that, it was a small shrine. Only this gateway was constructed by Bhai Ram Singh." (p. 117, folio 99)
  - Shoes off at the portal; "The main shrine was an ordinary room which too was locked"; beside the gateway a small courtyard with several rooms where "A few Pathan Sikh children were playing" — Iqbal Qaiser: "They are the caretakers of this Sikh gurdwara. They have moved here from Nankana Sahib." (p. 117, folio 99)
  - A structure in the ground beside the gurdwara, buffaloes wallowing round it in pools: "That was also a part of the gurdwara. It must have been the [OCR?] hall." The protective wall is recent (line damaged: "It was onl[y] … a few years ago") (p. 118, folio 100).
  - Former state, per Qaiser: "Oh it was horrible. The structure was standing as it is but there [was] no protection. A bazaar for the sale of buffaloes used to be set up [at] the premises of the gurdwara." Present: "It was after my book was published that this gurdwara came into the limelight. Now it is fully restored. There are security officials here at all times and Sikh pilgrims [come] here every year." (p. 118, folio 100)
- **Legends & Miracles**
  - The name, per Iqbal Qaiser: this is not where Nanak worked the grindstone but "the place where he was picked up by the guards", "a small mound outside Eminabad where there Were Baby, [OCR?] pebbles, hence the name Rori Sahib"; "Nanak was sitting here with Mardana at the time of his arrest." (p. 118, folio 100)
  - Iqbal Qaiser's Ghavindi story: Mardana, told not to beg, came back hungry from a family celebrating a son's birth; the child "died by an act of God"; the family sought Nanak's forgiveness (p. 56, folio 38).
  - In the Panja Sahib legend, Mardana climbs three times to Wali Qandhari for water (pp. 269–270, folios 243–244); in the author's Hassan Abdal scene Hari Singh orders a shrine "to commemorate the spot where Nanak and Mardana had rested" (p. 265, folio 239).

### shrine-of-fariduddin-ganjshakar — Shrine of Fariduddin Ganjshakar

The book's "Baba Farid", "Sheikh Farid", "Sheikh Farid-ud-din of Pakpattan", title "Shakarganj". The Tibba Haji Deen material (birthplace, ancestors' graves, childhood well) is on this row by the person rule; it is **not** the Pakpattan shrine.

- **Saint**
  - Baba Farid "was born in the year 1173 CE" at the village of Tibba Haji Deen (formerly Kothewal) (p. 150, folio 132). "It had been three hundred years since the death of the great Sufi" at the time of Nanak's visit (reconstruction) (p. 142, folio 124).
  - Pakpattan "had once been known as Ajodhan, but its name was changed to Pakpattan after the head of the Chistiyan si/si/a [OCR?] [silsila] (religious order), Baba Farid, left Delhi and settled here"; "a small insignificant city", next to which "on top of an abandoned mound Baba Farid used to teach his students"; the Sutlej "snaked through" it (p. 141, folio 123). "Baba Farid moved from Delhi to Pakpattan because he did not want to associate with the rulers there. His madrassa was similar to poorly funded public schools, where the students could not even afford a proper meal" (p. 282, folio 256).
  - "Baba Farid is considered to be the first poet of the Punjabi language"; he "wrote for the people in a language that they could understand", when the elite used Persian and Arabic; he "first uses the symbolism of a wife being abandoned by her husband" for devotee and deity (p. 153, folio 135). "The Chistiyan school of thought in Sufism, of which Baba Farid was once the head, is one of the most syncretic sects of Islam"; its hallmark is qawwali, "the Muslim version of bhajan" (p. 153, folio 135).
  - Chistiyan is "a small city which was established by Baba Farid's son and named after his school of thought, according to legend" (p. 154, folio not stated).
  - Poems quoted in the reconstructed scenes: "I did not sleep with my love tonight / And every bit of my body aches", "Sheikh Farid's most popular qawwali" (p. 138, folio 120); "If someone smites your face, O Farid …" (p. 147, folio 129); "You did not launch your boat …" (p. 148, folio 130).
  - Author's reconstruction of his descendants: Sheikh Ibrahim, "the twelfth descendant of the great Sheikh Farid-ud-din of Pakpattan" (p. 141, folio 123), "the twelfth successor of Baba Farid" (p. 142, folio 124), holding the position of Diwan, which he found frustrating; he disapproved of the myths made from Farid's life (p. 146, folio 128) and "abhorred the institutionalisation of spirituality as was being practised in the name of his family" (p. 142, folio 124); he "took immense pride in the fact that his ancestor Baba Farid was the best Punjabi poet ever" (p. 143, folio 125).
- **Shrine / Darbar**
  - Travelogue, Pakpattan: "The shrine of Baba Farid is the focal point of the city of Pakpattan"; roads lead to it; it is the highest point of the area; it was "an abandoned mound, probably carrying within it the secrets of an ancient city, when Farid moved here with his students. Now it is a shrine spread over a vast tract of land"; shops on the approach are all named after Baba Farid or "his title Shakarganj" (p. 154, folio not stated).
  - "the shrine of Sheikh Farid at Pakpattan, one of the biggest centres of the Sufi Chistiyan school of thought in all of South Asia" (p. 143, folio 125).
  - Author's reconstruction, Nanak's era: the city became "a major trading city, with the shrine at the centre", and "the power of his descendants" grew until they "became major feudal lords and political players of the region" (p. 142, folio 124); the shrine "was in one corner" of the courtyard, beside it a tomb "which contained the graves of the descendants of the saint", with "several stalls around" (damaged) (p. 142, folio 124); the shrine sits on a mound larger than the one where Nanak sat (p. 146, folio 128).
  - Tibba Haji Deen (travelogue): the birthplace house "is preserved", its courtyard holding graves of the saint's ancestors "back seven generations"; "Baba Farid's parents are also buried here"; Ghulam Shabbir's family "has been serving this shrine for the past seven generations", passing down magical stories of the saint and his ancestors (p. 150, folio 132). Nearby "another shrine dedicated to the childhood of Baba Farid": a well within a house under an iron cage, on whose railing devotees tie thread; a caretaker draws and serves the water (p. 150, folio 132). "The shrine at Tiba Haji Deen was older than the shrine of Baba Farid at Pakpattan, but it was less important. The importance, in this case, was determined by the number of followers each shrine had" (p. 145, folio 127).
  - Tibba Nanaksar is "about five kilometres from the shrine of Baba Farid" (p. 154, folio not stated); Iqbal Qaiser says pressure "even by the Diwan of the shrine of Baba Farid" led the government to renovate the Tibba Nanaksar gurdwara (p. 156, folio 138); a descendant of Baba Farid, "Baba Fateh Ullah Shah Noori Chisti", has a shrine there around which a mosque was built (p. 155, folio 137).
- **Practices & Events**
  - His poetry "was still sung by qawwals at the shrine and by fakirs in the cities of Punjab" (reconstruction, p. 142, folio 124); qawwal devotees came "with the Diwan of Chawali Mashaikh" (p. 138, folio 120).
  - "Once Baba Farid found a place in the Guru Granth Sahib, his personality and shrine became holy for the followers of Guru Nanak as well. Every year, hundreds of Sikhs and Hindus travel to Pakpattan to pay homage to the shrine of Baba Farid" (p. 153, folio 135).
  - Named among Sufi shrines ("Baba Farid's at Pakpattan") that have "come under attack" in "a wave of Islamic purism" (p. 278, folio 252).
  - Depicted on the banner at Jalaludin's shrine at Uch, "in a simple white dress with a green shawl on his shoulders" (p. 282, folio 256); named at Uch as one of three saints said to have meditated in the mosque rooms, which the author thinks apocryphal (p. 283, folio not stated).
- **Legends & Miracles**
  - The childhood well: "Because of his presence, this well and its water became sacred" (p. 150, folio 132).
- **Significance**
  - Named among the "spiritual teachers" whose poetry the Guru Granth Sahib contains (p. 82, folio 64; p. 317, folio 291). The author: Ibrahim's scribe compiled "all the poems of Baba Farid" and handed them to Nanak; "It was through this channel that the poems of Baba Farid found their place in the Guru Granth Sahib"; before that they passed orally, "it was through Guru Nanak that they were preserved for the first time in written form" (p. 149, folio 131).
  - In the author's reconstruction Nanak praises "the use of simple vernacular" in Farid's poetry and calls him "the true follower of the Prophet Muhammad (PBUH)" (p. 147, folio 129); narrates Farid's poetry and talks at night to "the imaginary Bhagat Kabir and Farid" (p. 208, folio 182); heads "for Pakpattan to meet his friend Sheikh Ibrahim" (p. 209, folio 183); on his first visit to the shrine notices a few dervish "far from the message of the saint", whose message "for Nanak captured the true essence of religion" (p. 288, folio 262). The author: Nanak "like Farid, also chose Punjabi as his medium of expression" and was "impressed by the fact that Baba Farid lived a life of simplicity and shunned violence" (p. 153, folio 135).
  - Baba Farid is named among Punjabi poets ridiculed by the educated elite of Punjab (p. 128, folio 110) and among the figures who have "become a part of" the author's existence (p. 151, folio 133).
  - Bibliography: "Sri Guru Granth Sahib Raags 1 ndex——Author: Baba Sheikh Farid-—-SearchGurbani. com" (accessed "12 Feb. 2014") (p. 311, folio 285).

### gurdwara-dash-mesh-pita — Gurdwara Dash Mesh Pita

Guru Gobind Singh (also "Gobind", "Gobind Rai (Later Gobind Singh)" [OCR?]). All material is person-level biography; the book says nothing about this row's Sukkur site.

- **Saint**
  - Back matter: "10. Guru Gobind Singh (1666-1708, Guru from 1675 to 1708)"; appointed heir by Guru Tegh Bahadur "Before heading to Delhi"; "Known as a warrior and a poet"; "He founded the Khalsa (The Pure Ones) in 1699, giving the Sikh community its distinct attire and identity"; "He saw the death of all of his sons during his lifetime at the hands of the Mughal authorities"; completed the Granth and "appointed it as the next Guru for eternity" (p. 317, folio 291). Chronology: "1675 - Guru Gobind Singh is appointed as the next Guru"; "1705 — All the sons of Guru Gobind Singh are assassinated by the Mughal forces"; "1708 - Guru Granth Sahib is declared to be the living Guru tor [OCR?] [for] eternity by Guru Gobind Singh"; "1708 - Guru Gobind Singh is assassinated by a Pathan horse trader" (p. 313, folio 287).
  - "Guru Gobind Singh had become Guru at the age of nine in the year 1675. He was assassinated at the hands of Pathan horse traders in the yea[r] 1708" (p. 98, folio 80).
  - Jaita recovered Tegh Bahadur's body and "presented it to Guru Gobind Singh at Anandpur, who then cremated the body" (p. 132, folio 114); it was "actually the young Gobind Singh" who urged his father to aid the Pandits (pp. 132–133); he was "much more militaristically inclined than his father" (p. 137, folio 119).
  - After "the assassination of his father Guru Tegh Bahadur", "The gravest threat came from his uncle Ram Rai, who was the disgruntled son of Guru Har Rai"; Ram Rai approached "Emperor Aurangzeb"; the Punjab chiefs "surrounded Guru Gobind's abode in Anandpur" (p. 96, folio 78).
  - "On the first of Vaisakh, 1699 (14 April 1699)", at "Anandpur, a city that had been founded by his father", five volunteers offered their heads — "in reality, it was a goat's blood" — "the Panj Pyare or the five beloved"; "the five Ks (Aangi [OCR?] [kangi], kesh, kirpan, kachcha and hada [OCR?] [kada])" (p. 97, folio 79). "when Guru Gobind Singh founded the Khalsa, he ordered his followers to keep a kirpan with them at all times" (p. 133, folio not stated).
  - "He lived in a regal tent"; "in command of a standing army, whose weapons were procured through the offerings of devotees or through raids upon neighbouring villages"; "a war drum"; "referred to as Sacha Padshah, the True King, and his court was referred to as Sacha Darbar, the True Court"; fond of war exercises and hunting; "adept in Persian, Arabic and Sanskrit" (p. 99, folio 81). He "loved war epics, used to hunt regularly and took an active part in military exercises"; Hargobind's sword passed to him, and he "can be seen as the true descendant of Guru Hargobind" (p. 233, folio 207). The offering of weapons at the Guru's court "was a practice that was started by Guru Hargobind but then reinforced by Guru Gobind Singh" (footnote, p. 99, folio 81).
  - "The Masand system was abolished by Guru Gobind Singh when he established the Khalwa [OCR?] [Khalsa]", for which "devout Sikhs believe he purged Sikhism [OCR? 'Sikhisay'] of corruption" (p. 182, folio 156); "The system of Masands was abolished by Guru Gobind Singh" (p. 257, folio not stated); he "finally abolished" it "because of the corruption that had seeped into it" (p. 315, folio 289). ### katas-raj-temples — Katas Raj Temples

- **Shrine / Darbar**
  - Travelogue: the author and his wife Anam, "driving back to Islamabad after a weekend in Lahore", stop "to see Nanak's Gurdwara"; he had been "several times" but never seen it; "A few years ago" he came "to document the festival of Maha Shivratri" and the local guide said "there was no such building here" (p. 245, folio 219). "historical records clearly state Nanak visited this sacred site"; Iqbal Qaiser "had seen and photographed the place for his book" (p. 245, folio 219).
  - Location: "only a few kilometres from the citof [OCR?] [city of] Kallar Kahar on the Motorway"; the National Highway Authority was widening the single-lane road; "Cement factories dotting this area" had lobbied intensely for it (p. 245, folio 219).
  - The complex: "the haveli of Hari Sigh [OCR?] [Singh] Nalwa", behind it "the Kashmiri temples dedicated to s),., [OCR?] [Shiva]" "built in the seventh and eighth century"; a "half excavated stupa ... at least four thousand years old"; beside it "the temple dedicated to Ram"; "The sacred pond, believed to be the tear of Lord Shiva" at the base; across the road "the offices of the Archaeology Department" and "a small hotel" (p. 246, folio 220).
  - The pond "had recently been renovated" and was "brimming with water", without the garbage of "the last time"; "Muslim boys" were diving into it from rooftops (p. 246, folio 220). The water level "had started to fall" because "the cement factories around this area were sucking the groundwater"; after writers and journalists wrote about it "the government woke from its slumber"; "The water level has now been restored" (p. 246, folio 220).
  - Nanak's gurdwara: a guide insisted "There is no gurdwara here"; by phone "Igbal [OCR?] [Iqbal] Qaiser" said "It is next to the fort", the only fort being one "built by Hari singh Nalwa" (p. 247, folio 221). It "was at a little distance from the complex. It was not renovated as the rest of the complex was"; the path "had been neglected"; "a big building, with several pillars that once supported a roof"; "The roof was now gone"; "a staircase leading nowhere"; "bigger than I had expected"; the guide: "We never knew what this building was" (p. 247, folio 221).
  - Author's reconstruction (Nanak–Mardana scene, "the annual festival of Shivratri", "a cold February night"): Katas Raj "the holiest of holy temples in Punjab", "Situated on the slope of the mountain", "a collection of temples located around a sacred pond"; the Shiva temple "at the edge of the pool" was "the most important temple here", with temples "dedicated to the goddesses Kali and Durga" (p. 236, folio 210); "a small cave where a few yogis, devotees of Lord Shiva, sat in meditative postures", pilgrims climbing to temples above (p. 237, folio 211).
- **Practices & Events**
  - In the reconstruction: "Pilgrims from hundreds of kilometres away came here to purify their lives"; devotees bathe the Shiva shrine "with milk", others sing his praises; items placed in the pond are carried off by a [stream] that "disappeared into the mount[ains]" (damaged) (p. 236, folio 210). Nanak sits "inside the temple amongst the devotees"; Mardana stays on the adjacent mountain because "being a Muslim, Mardana would not have been allowed inside such a sacred Hindu space" (p. 237, folio 211). The temple "was about fifty kos from Talwindi" [OCR?] [Talwandi]; yogis Nanak had met there had spent time at Katas (p. 237, folio 211).
  - In an earlier reconstruction, Tripta and her husband, back from Tilla Jogian, "stopped at Katas Raj, where both of them took a dip in the holy pond" (p. 21, folio 3).
- **Legends & Miracles**
  - The pool, "constructed in the shape of an eye", "was created when Lord Shiva shed a tear after his wife Sati passed away, or so the story goes"; the tear "could wash away the gravest of sins" (p. 236, folio 210); "considered to have been filled up by a tear drop of Lord Shiva" (p. 21, folio 3).
  - "It is believed that these temples were constructed by the Pandavs [OCR?] brothers when they were sent on a thirteen year exile" after the Kauravas took the throne; "In the Mahabharata these temples are mentioned as sacred spaces" (p. 236, folio 210).

### gurdwara-khoohi-bhai-lalo-bhai-lalo-di-khooi — Gurdwara Khoohi Bhai Lalo (Bhai Lalo di Khooi)

- **Shrine / Darbar** (travelogue)

  - Iqbal Qaiser: "This is how the gurdwara is at all times" (locked); the author guesses the keys are "probably with members of the Sikh comm[unity]" at the nearby Gurdwara Rori Sahib (p. 116, folio 98).

### shri-varun-dev-mandir — Shri Varun Dev Mandir

Attached by Varuna (person-level) and the book's "Manora" island (place-level; Manora's only row). The book does not name this temple; it describes a Hindu temple and two gurdwaras on Manora.

- **Shrine / Darbar**

"A large portion of the island is under the control of the Navy, which monitors the sea regularly to ward off infiltration from India"; guards and check posts (p. 227, folio not stated); Navy patrols on the beach road (p. 229, folio not stated).
  - The Hindu temple: "a tall conical structure constructed right on the beach"; in summer the waters "probably" reach its base; the author "didn't know how old this temple was" and imagines it built by "the indigenous community" and renovated; access "cordoned off", he climbed the fence; on the cone "the face of a Hindu deity" he could not identify; the room "locked"; floor and walls tiled with "Krishna and Radha and Ram and Sita" — "All the faces had been wiped clean" (p. 229, folio not stated).

  - Second gurdwara: "a small single storey structure painted orange", a "Khalsa flag" beside it, the Khalsa symbol on the windows; "recently renovated", the Granth at the centre, pictures of Guru Gobind Singh and the Golden Temple; built "to honour the memory of Guru Nanak", now "incorporated into the beliefs of the Khalsa" (p. 230, folio 204).
  - Author's reconstruction of Nanak's time: "The temple of Varuna was located within a cave in these cli[ffs], on [the] shore", sea water reaching the caves at tide; stone steps from the sea and "another set of stairs for devotees arriving by land"; two rooms, lamps in rock niches each "lit for a particular ship that had set sail"; the captain lights one for his ship (p. 218, folio 192).

  - Custodian of the second: "Rajesh", "a practising Sikh with a turban and a long beard"; "Rajesh's family once belonged to that sect of Hindus [the independent followers of Nanak]. Today, they refer to themselves as Sikhs" (p. 230, folio 204).

- **Legends & Miracles**
  - The pandit in the reconstructed scene: there is no idol — Varuna "resides in the waves and winds of the ocean", "occasionally manifests himself by riding on a giant fish", once meditated in these caves, appears "even today" to the "pure-hearted"; "This is not just a shrine but his actual home" (p. 218, folio 192).
- **Significance**
  - The author: later Sikh writers placed the sea-god temple at Clifton, but that was "on the mainland, so Nanak travelling there on a boat makes no sense"; he "would have needed to travel on a boat to come to the island of Manora"; "We will never know for sure" (p. 229, folio not stated).

### gurdwara-shaheed-ganj-singh-singhnian — Gurdwara Shaheed Ganj Singh Singhnian

The book's "Gurdwara Shaheed Ganj", Naulakha Bazaar, Lahore. All told by Iqbal Qaiser as his first-person account.

- **Shrine / Darbar**
  - "No, this is not the walled city of Lahore. This [is] outside Delhi gate. This area is known as Naulakha Bazaar"; "Prince Dara ... shikob [OCR?] [Shikoh] had his Naulakha palace here" (p. 76, folio 58; p. 77, folio 59). He first told the author the story "when we drove around the railway station" (p. 76, folio 58).
  - "an old issue that started about two centuries a v0 [OCR?] [ago]": a vacant plot "where there was a small mosque" from which "the Qazi would announce punishment to criminals"; "The mosque was small and was never used as a place of worship"; under Maharaja Ranjit Singh the Sikhs "placed the Guru Granth Sahib inside it, converting it into a gurdwara. The Muslims could not do anything then" (pp. 76–77, folios 58–59).
  - British era: Muslims applied in court for its return; "The Lahore High Court" asked for a living witness who had seen it used as a mosque, found none, and "decided in favour of the Sikhs"; the Sikhs razed the building, leading to "rioting between Muslims and Sikhs", which Qaiser blames on British "divide and rule"; "Muhammad Ali Jinnah and Allama Igbal [OCR?] [Iqbal] got involved"; he has seen a photograph of Jinnah addressing a crowd "at the Badshahi Mosque at the time of this riot"; "the gurdwara remained a gurdwara" (p. 77, folio 59).
  - After Partition, with "the Augaf [OCR?] [Auqaf] Department" created, the Lahore High Court decided it "would remain a gurdwara" — "Around 1957-58" (p. 77, folio 59; p. 78, folio 60); "again in the late 1980s" petitioners sought review; the judge would convert it if they produced "even one person who had prayed at the mosque"; none was produced (p. 78, folio 60).
  - "In about 1994-95, the Kar Sewa Committee of England" sought permission to renovate; the government agreed; "Avtar Singh Panghedha" asked Iqbal Qaiser to supervise construction, and "all the money, the contracts ... went through me" (p. 78, folio 60). When newspapers reported it, "the Muslims tried once again to create an issue"; an ironsmith named "Rana" persuaded local traders the gurdwara "would attract foreign pilgrims", and the traders of Naulakha Bazaar became stakeholders; "this gurdwara was eventually constructed in 2004" (p. 78, folio 60).
- **Saint** (the martyrs)
  - "Thousands of them, men, women and children alike were slaughtered and thrown into a well here on the orders of the Governor of Lahore, Moin-ul-Mulk also known as Meer Manu"; a room was built to hold prisoners before execution (p. 77, folio 59). During construction "skeletons of children and adults would emerge", which he identifies as the Sikhs killed on Meer Manu's orders (p. 78, folio 60).
- **Significance**
  - Afterwards "the Government of Pakistan woke up to the potential of Sikh pilgrimage tourism" and invited the Sikh community abroad "to invest in the renovation of any Sikh gurdwara in the country" (p. 79, folio 61). At Sachkand the author and Qaiser name it as the feared outcome: "we could have a situation similar to Gurdwara Shaheed Ganj here?" — "Precisely." (p. 76, folio 58). Qaiser's renovation role is compared to Sacha Sauda (p. 79, folio 61).

### Guru Nanak (person-level: 18 rows)

- **Significance** (the author's arguments)

  - Method: "The only evidence we have is found within the Sikh religion and they are full of stories of miracles"; Iqbal Qaiser: "read what Nanak wrote at these places and try to get into his mind" (p. 286, folio 260). The author: "my depiction of Nanak in this book is not an accurate version of Nanak" but what he wants Nanak "to be like" (pp. 229–230, folio not stated / folio 204); the Nanak narrative "part of the book is fictionalised" (p. 17, folio not stated). ### The author's arguments: oral tradition, folklore and historicity
- Oral history matters more in South Asia than the British-derived preference for written records; folk tales record ancient mounds before archaeologists (Shahjamal, Taxila) (pp. 31–32, folios 13–14).
- Folklore over verification: a Muslim shrine claiming Nanak's visit has significance "irrespective of historical veracity [OCR? "vera, ity"]" given Partition (pp. 37–38); a storyteller's version "was different from the one that is recorded in the Janamsakhis", but historical accuracy "was not a concern" (p. 75, folio 57).
- Naulakha: the story "might not give us an accurate account", but Dhillon's claim "reinforces my perception that Mata probably visited this mound where there must have been a temple" (p. 32, folio 14); the mound was likely an ancient temple sacred to Hindus and Muslims (most Muslims there "were Hindu converts anyway"), converted to a Muslim shrine after Partition, and Naulakha Hazari "must have been a Malamati Sufi" (pp. 32–33, folios 14–15).
- Janamsakhis: collected "[s]ixty-five [OCR?] years after Nanak's death" by devotees (line damaged), with "several miracles" "inserted" (p. 112, folio 94). "The story of Sachkand and Sacha Sauda seem to merge with each other. The former, it seems, is an extension of the latter. Even though the Janamsakhis relate them as two different incidents"; "it is impossible to determine the historicity of either event" (p. 72, folio 54).
- Doubted encounters: Malik Bhago "too tyrannical to allow Nanak to leave unharmed" (p. 112, folio 94); the Babur meeting, given the Baburnama's silence and "That was not the way of kings" (p. 114, folio 96), though in Sikh tradition it is seen "as the beginning" of hostility between the Mughal emperors and the later Gurus (p. 115, folio 97; badly damaged). Iqbal Qaiser: Nanak "must have been an ordinary person", the meeting stories "later constructions by his devotees" (p. 116, folio 98); on Kanganpur, "It is likely that this incident never took place", while the author calls the stigma "harsh on the part of Nanak" (p. 159, folio 141).
- Miracles and legend: Nanak "a rationalist" (p. 112, folio 94); his greatness lies in poetry, not miracles (p. 199, folio 173); the Panja Sahib rock story is "fictitious", yet "such is the power of imagination [in] religion that I forgot all my rationality" (p. 271, folio 245); the handprint legend is "yet another story in which Nanak is depicted as competing with a Muslim saint", driving home "that 'our' saint is stronger than 'your' saint" (p. 270, folio 244; speaker of the passage damaged — author or Iqbal Qaiser).
- Pir Bairi: "This is an apocryphal story. I have seen quite a few shrines around the country to know how to tell a real story from a fabricated one" [OCR?]; the first of "one of two possibilities" is that "this tree was already considered sacred in the local folk religion of [the] region when Nanak came here", berry and peepal being trees "still revered in the Islamic folk religion" [OCR?] (p. 195, folio 169; the second possibility is cut off); "the truth lies somewhere in the middle of these two stories" (p. 196, folio 170).
- Farid and Nanak: oral tradition "presented" them "as contemporaries, not in the dimension of their time period but because of the similarity of their philosophies" (p. 153, folio 135).
- Chronology as test: from the architecture he "had no doubt that Nanak and Hamza Ghous could not have been contemporaries" (p. 198, folio 172), the oval/long dome "pre-Mughal", resembling Delhi Sultanate buildings, a hole atop older domes being "a technique adopte[d] to facilitate the return of the soul to God" (p. 198, folio 172); Bibi Jiwindi's tomb (1494) is contemporary with Nanak's visit, but Jalaludin and Zakariya are thirteenth-century, so the meeting stories fail (pp. 285–286).
- Karachi: against the pandit, the Clifton temple is the one Nanak visited, relying on Iqbal Qaiser (p. 217, folio 191); later, Manora, not Clifton, better fits the Sikh record of a boat to the sea-god temple — "We will never know for sure" (p. 229); local Hindus and Sikhs uniformly deny Nanak visited the southern coast (pp. 224, 227, 228). He projects himself onto Nanak; his depiction "is not an accurate version" (pp. 229–230).
- Travel writing compresses "hours" into "a thousand words or so", so readers "associate romanticism with travel" (p. 192, folio 166).

### The author's arguments: Nanak, the later Gurus and institutionalisation
- The book contrasts Nanak's philosophy with "the politicisation of the religious philosophy of Sikhism as the institutio[n of] Guruhood rose" (p. 18, folio not stated); "Nanak's philosophy is centred on the concept of redefining the sacred and the profane" (p. 82, folio 64).
- Nanak "influenced immensely by Islam", Islamic culture then being "at its zenith" (p. 37, folio 19; similar on the middle course, p. 53, folio 35).
- It is "contradictory that Sikhism today has become [an] institutionalised religion"; "the subsequent Gurus missed the exsence [OCR?] [essence] of Nanak's movement when they institutionalised the seligion [OCR?] [religion]" (pp. 92–93, folios 74–75); ironic that the Guruship became feudal, "because Sikhism is seen as a reaction against the feudal land [grant system?] [OCR?] of Punjab by a lot of historians" (p. 182, folio 156).
- Nanak and Gobind Singh "have played the most important roles"; under Gobind "Sikhism … emerged as an independent religion, separate from Islam and Hinduism" (pp. 94–95, folios 76–77); Gobind's "exclusivist approach … can be understood in the political context of the era" (pp. 96–97, folios 78–79). Gandhi and Abdul Ghaffar Khan "personified what had been stated by Nanak" (p. 95, folio 77).
- The Adi Granth's compilation is central to "a separate Sikh identity" (p. 256, folio 230); Punjabi, "this language of peasants was given the status of a holy language after Sikhism as a religion was institutionalised" (p. 153, folio 135).
- Khalsa identity is absorbing the independent Nanak-followers and erasing distinctions among the Gurus (p. 230).
- Succession: following P. S. Grewal, Ram Das's appointment is linked to caste — he was "relieved" Jetha was Sodhi (p. 272, folio 246) — and to property and the "feudalisatio[n]" of Guruhood (p. 274, folio 248); Grewal (quoted): Amar Das's Goindwal pilgrimage and charnamrit were "departure[s] from earlier Sikh doctrine" (pp. 291–292).

### The author's arguments: Mughal–Sikh history
- Mughal–Sikh incidents "were stripped of their political contexts and presented as a fight between religions" (pp. 96–97, folios 78–79).
- Arjan: the Sikh account (Chandu Lal's connivance) set against Jahangir's own text, which names Khusro and is silent on Chandu Lal; Arjan was caught in Mughal succession politics (pp. 253–255, folios 227–229); Sikh sources on Prithi Chand are "biased against him" (p. 254, folio not stated).
- Hargobind's militarisation: critics see a departure from the early Gurus; it "would be unfair to critic[ise]" without the context of Arjan's martyrdom (pp. 233–234); "The reality… is much more complex" — he reconciled with Jahangir, and the Sikh version "is quiet about the politics of the Guru" (p. 234, folio 208).
- Tegh Bahadur: of the oppressor–oppressed view, "The reality was a little more complicated", citing Cunningham on "the machinations of Ram Rai" (pp. 134–136). - Historiography: Pakistani nationalist historians say a religious divide always existed, the Indian side blames the British; the author reads Nanak's struggle as evidence that identities existed then (p. 266, folio 240).

### (5) Internal contradictions and attribution doubts (kept side by side)

- **The rock shrine near Nankana:** the reconstruction makes the rock "the feet of Kali mata" (p. 24); the author later concludes the shrine was Durga's (p. 32). The Auqaf booklet places the saint before Nanak's birth; the author holds the site became a Muslim shrine after Partition (p. 33).
- **After the Sacha Sauda episode:** the reconstruction has Nanak hiding "in the growe of wax trees outside town" near home (p. 64, folio 46); the Sachkand storyteller has him found "sitting in the jungle at this spot", near Chuhrkhana (p. 74, folio 56; p. 75, folio 57). The ascetics are "a jungle on the way" to Chuharkhana (reconstruction, p. 65) vs "on top of a mound ... where the big gurdwara stands" (storyteller, p. 74, folio 56).
- **Mud/sugar legend:** a trader's sugar turns to mud and back (footnote, p. 71, folio 53); Nanak's mud turns to sugar (storyteller, p. 75, folio 57); the reconstruction has no miracle.
- Iqbal Qaiser's "The Muslims in this village were responsible for all those atrocities on Hindus and Sikhs" (p. 76, folio 58) sits oddly after his praise of the same local Muslims — quoted as printed, possibly OCR-damaged or ironic.
- Boota Singh's family moving "to Nankana Sahib" (Iqbal Qaiser's question, p. 83) vs Boota Singh saying only he is "from the village of Nena Kot" while working at the Chuhrkhana gurdwara.
- Farid and Nanak: the reconstruction has Nanak meet Farid's descendant Ibrahim; the author and Iqbal Qaiser stress they were not contemporaries ("three hundred years", p. 152) — consistent, noted.
- Tegh Bahadur called Hargobind's "SON" (p. 233); Tegh Bahadur's "grandsons (Guru Gobind Singh's sons)" martyred (p. 273) — both as printed.
- Uch: Nanak visited "sometime in the late fifteenth century" (p. 285) vs the reconstruction placing the visit after he settled at Kartarpur following twenty-four years of travel (p. 288); no year for either.
- **Mardana's grave:** death in Baghdad vs Kartarpur, plus the author's own speculation — nothing settled (chunk 014).
- **Har Rai (p. 316):** "supported the former" (Aurangzeb), yet Dara Shikoh "became his devotee" and Har Rai "promised" his force "to Dara Shikoh against his brother".
- The Clifton temple: Vijesh's 150 vs 500 years; the pandit denies any Nanak visit (pp. 216–217). The book calls it formerly "a famous temple dedicated to the sea god".
- **Speaker doubts:** the legend passage at pp. 269–270 (Salman Rashid and Hiuen Tsang claims, Ranjit Singh dating, "our saint / your saint") may be Iqbal Qaiser's or the author's — "You should read Salman Rashid's book" suggests direct speech; the "November 2008" line (p. 49); Iqbal Qaiser's "centre for smugglers" speech (p. 307) and the peace-corridor lines (pp. 308–309) are partly garbled, attribution is the Pass-1 worker's reading.
